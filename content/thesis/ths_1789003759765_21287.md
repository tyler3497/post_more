# The Geometry of Algorithmic Fairness: Impossibility Theorems, Post-Processing Corrections, and Pareto Frontiers of Group-Fair Classification

## Abstract

Group fairness in machine learning is usually expressed as statistical constraints on a classifier's predictions conditional on a protected attribute, yet the most prominent definitions — calibration, equalized odds, and demographic parity — are mutually incompatible except under degenerate conditions. This thesis develops a geometric account of algorithmic fairness in which each fairness criterion is a level set or affine subspace in the space of joint distributions over predictions, labels, and group membership; impossibility theorems then appear as the geometric fact that these subspaces fail to intersect when group base rates differ. We prove the Kleinberg–Mullainathan–Raghavan trade-off for risk scores and the Chouldechova result for binary classifiers, characterize post-processing corrections — group-specific thresholds and randomized decisions derived from ROC geometry and linear programming — as projections onto fairness level sets, and analyze the resulting accuracy–fairness trade-off as a Pareto frontier in a multi-objective optimization problem. Illustrations on COMPAS data and synthetic Gaussian models verify the theory's quantitative predictions, including the *levelling-down* phenomenon. We close by delineating the limits of oblivious statistical fairness: causality, dynamics, and normative choice.

## 1. Introduction

The deployment of machine-learned classifiers in domains protected by anti-discrimination law — credit, hiring, criminal justice, medicine — has forced an old philosophical question into the language of statistics: *what does it mean for a decision procedure to be fair to different groups?* Nearly all proposed criteria are *oblivious* measures: they depend only on the joint distribution of $(\hat{Y}, Y, A)$, not on any interpretation of the features $X$ [2].

This obliviousness makes fairness auditable without opening the black box — but it is also the source of the field's central difficulty: several individually plausible oblivious criteria cannot hold simultaneously. The canonical instance is the 2016 COMPAS dispute: ProPublica found Black defendants' false-positive rate roughly twice that of white defendants [5]; the vendor replied the instrument was *calibrated* across groups. Both claims were arithmetically correct — when base rates differ, calibration and error-rate balance are *provably incompatible* short of perfect prediction [1][5].

The key move of this thesis is to treat fairness criteria as *subspaces* — affine constraints — in the simplex of joint distributions over $(\hat{Y}, Y, A)$. A criterion carves out a level set; satisfying two means finding a point in the intersection of two level sets; an impossibility theorem proves this intersection empty except in degenerate cases. Post-processing corrections are *projections* onto a fairness level set along the accuracy gradient, and the menu of achievable trade-offs is the *Pareto frontier* over accuracy and fairness violation. This vocabulary explains why the impossibility is structural, why randomized post-processing is optimal [2], why the trade-off is a smooth curve [7], and why it can exhibit *levelling down* [4][8].

The remainder of this thesis is organized as follows. Section 2 fixes notation and defines the principal fairness criteria. Section 3 develops the methodology: impossibility theorems, post-processing corrections, and the Pareto-frontier formalism. Section 4 deepens each geometrically; Section 5 gives proof sketches and empirical illustrations; Section 6 discusses limitations; Section 7 concludes.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 Notation and setting

We consider binary labels $Y \in \{0,1\}$, a binary protected attribute $A \in \{0,1\}$, and either binary predictions $\hat{Y}$ or continuous risk scores $R \in [0,1]$ thresholded to yield $\hat{Y}$.

For a group $a$, the *base rate* (prevalence) is $p_a = P(Y = 1 \mid A = a)$. The standard confusion-matrix quantities for group $a$ are:

- **True positive rate** (recall, sensitivity): $\mathrm{TPR}_a = P(\hat{Y} = 1 \mid Y = 1, A = a)$
- **False positive rate**: $\mathrm{FPR}_a = P(\hat{Y} = 1 \mid Y = 0, A = a)$
- **Positive predictive value** (precision): $\mathrm{PPV}_a = P(Y = 1 \mid \hat{Y} = 1, A = a)$
- **False negative rate**: $\mathrm{FNR}_a = 1 - \mathrm{TPR}_a$

A *risk score* $R$ is **calibrated within groups** if $P(Y = 1 \mid R = s, A = a) = s$ for every score $s$ and group $a$.

### 2.2 The principal fairness criteria

The dominant group-fairness definitions [6][8], each an oblivious constraint on the joint distribution, are:

| Criterion | Formal requirement | Intuition |
|---|---|---|
| **Demographic parity** | $P(\hat{Y}=1 \mid A=0) = P(\hat{Y}=1 \mid A=1)$ | Equal selection rates |
| **Equalized odds** | $\hat{Y} \perp\!\!\!\perp A \mid Y$: $\mathrm{TPR}_0=\mathrm{TPR}_1$ and $\mathrm{FPR}_0=\mathrm{FPR}_1$ | Equal error rates among positives and negatives |
| **Equal opportunity** | $\mathrm{TPR}_0 = \mathrm{TPR}_1$ | Equal recall (a relaxation of equalized odds) [2] |
| **Predictive parity** | $\mathrm{PPV}_0 = \mathrm{PPV}_1$ | Equal precision |
| **Calibration** | $P(Y=1 \mid R=s, A=a) = s$ for all $a$ | Scores mean what they say, in every group |
| **Balance (positive)** | $E[R \mid Y=1, A=0] = E[R \mid Y=1, A=1]$ | Equal mean score among positives [1] |
| **Balance (negative)** | $E[R \mid Y=0, A=0] = E[R \mid Y=0, A=1]$ | Equal mean score among negatives [1] |
| **Anti-classification** | $\hat{Y}$ does not use $A$ or its proxies | Process fairness (contested) [8] |

Each row is a *level set* — typically an affine subspace — in the space of achievable confusion matrices; the impossibility results of Section 3 concern their *intersections*.

### 2.3 The COMPAS motivating example

The ProPublica investigation of COMPAS [5] reported, at a representative operating threshold:

| Quantity | Black defendants | White defendants |
|---|---|---|
| Base rate $p_a$ | 51.4% | 39.4% |
| PPV (predictive parity) | 63.0% | 59.1% |
| False positive rate | 44.9% | 23.5% |
| False negative rate | 28.0% | 47.7% |
| Overall accuracy | 63.8% | 67.0% |

Predictive parity held approximately while error rates differed nearly twofold — not an accident of COMPAS's engineering but a *consequence of the differing base rates*.

## 3. Methodology

Our methodology has three components: (i) impossibility theorems via confusion-matrix algebra; (ii) post-processing corrections as a linear program over ROC convex hulls; (iii) Pareto-frontier analysis of the accuracy–fairness trade-off.

### 3.1 The Kleinberg–Mullainathan–Raghavan impossibility theorem

Kleinberg, Mullainathan, and Raghavan [1] work with *risk scores* $R$ rather than binary decisions, and ask which of three natural properties a score can satisfy simultaneously across groups:

1. **Calibration within groups**: $P(Y=1 \mid R=s, A=a) = s$ for all $s, a$.
2. **Balance for the positive class**: $E[R \mid Y=1, A=0] = E[R \mid Y=1, A=1]$.
3. **Balance for the negative class**: $E[R \mid Y=0, A=0] = E[R \mid Y=0, A=1]$ — equal mean scores among non-recidivists.

> **Theorem 1 (Kleinberg–Mullainathan–Raghavan, ITCS 2017 [1]).** *For two groups with different base rates $p_0 \neq p_1$, no risk score can simultaneously satisfy calibration within groups, balance for the positive class, and balance for the negative class, unless the score is a perfect predictor ($R = Y$ almost surely) — a degenerate case. Moreover, approximately satisfying all three conditions approximately forces the data into an approximate version of one of these degenerate cases.*

The proof (Section 5.1) is algebraic: calibration pins each group's mean score to its base rate while the balance conditions constrain the same means from the other side, yielding an inconsistent system when $p_0 \neq p_1$. The approximate version shows the conflict is robust.

### 3.2 The Chouldechova formulation for binary classifiers

Chouldechova [5] proved the analogue for *thresholded* binary classifiers:

> **Theorem 2 (Chouldechova, Big Data 2017 [5]).** *If the base rates differ across groups ($p_0 \neq p_1$) and the classifier is not perfect, then calibration (predictive parity), equal false-positive rates, and equal false-negative rates cannot hold simultaneously.*

The mechanism is a single identity. By Bayes' rule, for each group $a$,
$$\mathrm{PPV}_a = \frac{\mathrm{TPR}_a \cdot p_a}{\mathrm{TPR}_a \cdot p_a + \mathrm{FPR}_a \cdot (1 - p_a)}.$$
If $\mathrm{TPR}$ and $\mathrm{FPR}$ are equal across groups but $p_0 \neq p_1$, then $\mathrm{PPV}_0 \neq \mathrm{PPV}_1$ necessarily.

### 3.3 Post-processing corrections: the Hardt–Price–Srebro framework

Given an *already trained* score $R$, Hardt, Price, and Srebro [2] showed how to derive a new (possibly randomized) binary predictor $\tilde{Y}$ that satisfies equalized odds or equal opportunity while maximizing accuracy — *without retraining*. The construction is purely geometric:

1. For each group $a$, compute the ROC curve of $R$: achievable $(\mathrm{FPR}, \mathrm{TPR})$ pairs as the threshold varies.
2. Take the **convex hull** of each ROC curve; randomizing between two thresholds attains any point in the hull, convexifying the feasible set.
3. Choose a common operating point in the *intersection* of the two hulls, where $\mathrm{FPR}_0 = \mathrm{FPR}_1$ and $\mathrm{TPR}_0 = \mathrm{TPR}_1$.
4. Maximize expected accuracy — a **linear function** of the operating point — over this intersection: a linear program with a closed-form solution.

The equal-opportunity variant (equal TPR only) is analogous, with a one-dimensional feasibility set. The accuracy cost falls on the decision maker, incentivizing better classifiers [2]; the method is implemented in FairLearn's `ThresholdOptimizer` [9].

Pleiss et al. [3] showed calibration is compatible only with a *single* error constraint, and that any algorithm satisfying this relaxation is *no better than randomly flipping a fraction of predictions*: the calibration and error-balance level sets intersect only along a segment toward the random classifier, so the correction dilutes signal rather than creating information.

### 3.4 The Pareto-frontier formalism

Once exact fairness is recognized as generally unattainable jointly with other desiderata, the natural object of study is the *trade-off surface*. We formulate group-fair classification as a bi-objective problem:

$$\min_{\theta} \mathcal{L}(\theta) \quad \text{s.t.} \quad \mathcal{V}(\theta) \le \epsilon,$$
which traces the frontier as $\epsilon$ varies.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 The geometry of the impossibility triangle

Consider three affine subspaces of the joint-distribution simplex: the **calibration subspace** $\mathcal{C}$ ($Y \perp\!\!\!\perp A \mid \hat{Y}$), the **separation subspace** $\mathcal{S}$ ($\hat{Y} \perp\!\!\!\perp A \mid Y$), and the **independence subspace** $\mathcal{I}$ ($\hat{Y} \perp\!\!\!\perp A$). Each is non-empty for a single group; impossibility is the demand that one classifier satisfy all three pairwise intersections at once.

The Bayes identity of Section 3.2 shows $\mathcal{C} \cap \mathcal{S}$ is empty for $p_0 \neq p_1$ away from perfection: two hyperplanes whose offsets depend on the base rate, i.e., parallel but displaced slices — the linear-algebra picture of an inconsistent system $Ax = b$. The triangle metaphor: the three criteria are pairwise compatible only in restricted settings, the triple intersection is generically empty, and each vertex pulls toward a different degenerate extreme (perfect prediction, constant prediction, or equal base rates).

### 4.2 Post-processing as projection onto fairness level sets

The Hardt–Price–Srebro correction [2] is a **projection**: achievable $(\mathrm{FPR},\mathrm{TPR})$ pairs form each group's ROC curve, randomized rules fill its convex hull $\mathcal{H}_a$, and the equalized-odds feasible set is the convex polygon $\mathcal{H}_0 \cap \mathcal{H}_1$. The accuracy-maximizing point in this intersection is the projection of the unconstrained optimum onto the feasible polygon.

Randomization's role is to *convexify*: without it the feasible set is a union of two curves whose intersection may be empty and the fair optimum may not exist; with it, the problem is a linear program with a guaranteed optimum. Fairness is thus a *geometric regularization* — restriction to a convex subset — and its accuracy cost is the duality gap between the unconstrained and constrained optima.

Pleiss et al.'s result [3] is the calibration analogue: the calibration level set meets the equal-FNR level set only along a segment toward the random classifier, so "calibrated and error-balanced" post-processing is *equivalent to partial randomization* — it dilutes signal rather than creating information.

### 4.3 Pareto frontiers: the marginal price of fairness

Decision makers typically demand *bounded* unfairness: $\min_\theta \mathcal{L}(\theta)$ subject to $\mathcal{V}(\theta) \le \epsilon$ traces the frontier as $\epsilon$ varies. Under mild conditions the achievable region is convex and the frontier a continuous, decreasing, convex curve [7]; its slope — the Lagrange multiplier, the *marginal price of fairness* — is the accuracy cost of the last unit of fairness purchased.

Three regimes appear: **free fairness** (flat segment near the unconstrained optimum [2]); **linear trade-off** (constant slope, post-processing thresholds sliding along ROC hulls); and **steep degradation** as $\epsilon \to 0$, where exact fairness demands heavy randomization [3].

The frontier is thus the honest answer to "what does fairness cost here": a single number (the accuracy of the exactly-fair classifier) is one endpoint of a curve, and the curve itself is the object a stakeholder should see before choosing an operating point.

### 4.4 Levelling down and the ethics of constrained optima

Strictly egalitarian constraints exhibit *levelling down*: the optimal fair classifier can be worse for *every* group than some unfair alternative. Middleton et al. [4] show hard equalized-odds or demographic-parity constraints frequently reduce true-positive rates for *both* groups.

Corbett-Davies et al. [8] prove a structural version: the standard fairness definitions typically select *strongly Pareto-dominated* policies. In their college-admissions case study, enforcing classification parity simultaneously reduced student-body diversity *and* academic preparedness relative to policies explicitly optimizing those outcomes — fairness constraints harming the very groups they protect.

> **Theorem (informal, Corbett-Davies et al. [8]).** *For generic joint distributions over features, labels, and group membership, any policy satisfying anti-classification, classification parity, or calibration-as-decision-rule is strongly Pareto-dominated: there exists another feasible policy that improves the decision maker's utility and every group's outcome simultaneously.*

Geometrically, levelling down occurs when the fairness level set cuts through the interior of the achievable region rather than touching its Pareto boundary: the "price of fairness" is then paid in group welfare — the very currency the constraint was meant to protect. *Axiomatic* fairness, imposed without reference to consequences, is a poor substitute for *consequential* fairness: optimizing outcomes directly with the frontier as decision aid [8].

## 5. Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 Proof sketch of the KMR impossibility

We sketch [1]. Let $p_a = P(Y=1\mid A=a)$, and write $\mu_a^+ = E[R\mid Y=1,A=a]$, $\mu_a^- = E[R\mid Y=0,A=a]$, $\mu_a = E[R\mid A=a]$.

*Step 1.* By total expectation and calibration, the mean score in group $a$ equals the base rate: $\mu_a = E[E[Y \mid R, A=a]] = E[Y \mid A=a] = p_a$.
*Step 2.* Decompose by label: $\mu_a = p_a \mu_a^+ + (1-p_a)\mu_a^-$.
*Step 3.* Balance asserts $\mu_0^+ = \mu_1^+ =: \mu^+$ and $\mu_0^- = \mu_1^- =: \mu^-$, so $p_a = p_a \mu^+ + (1-p_a)\mu^-$ for $a \in \{0,1\}$.
*Step 4.* Subtracting gives $(p_0-p_1) = (p_0-p_1)(\mu^+-\mu^-)$; if $p_0 \neq p_1$ then $\mu^+ - \mu^- = 1$, forcing $\mu^+ = 1$, $\mu^- = 0$ since $R \in [0,1]$ — *perfect prediction*. The approximate theorem shows this is stable: near-satisfaction forces near-equal base rates or a near-perfect score. ∎

### 5.2 Worked numerical illustration: COMPAS

Verifying the Bayes coupling on the Section 2.3 statistics: for Black defendants ($p = 0.514$, $\mathrm{TPR} = 0.720$, $\mathrm{FPR} = 0.449$):
$$\mathrm{PPV} = \frac{0.720 \times 0.514}{0.720 \times 0.514 + 0.449 \times 0.486} = \frac{0.370}{0.370 + 0.218} \approx 0.629$$
(reported 63.0%). For white defendants ($p = 0.394$, $\mathrm{TPR} = 0.523$, $\mathrm{FPR} = 0.235$):
$$\mathrm{PPV} = \frac{0.523 \times 0.394}{0.523 \times 0.394 + 0.235 \times 0.606} = \frac{0.206}{0.206 + 0.142} \approx 0.591$$
(reported 59.1%).

Forcing equal error rates — say $\mathrm{TPR} = 0.62$, $\mathrm{FPR} = 0.34$ for both groups — at fixed base rates gives
$$\mathrm{PPV}_{\text{Black}} \approx 0.659, \qquad \mathrm{PPV}_{\text{White}} \approx 0.542.$$
Predictive parity is destroyed: a 12-point PPV gap opens *as a mathematical consequence* of equalizing the error rates. No threshold, retraining, or reweighting avoids this — the content of Theorem 2, and why the ProPublica/Northpointe dispute was unresolvable on its own terms.

### 5.3 Tracing the Pareto frontier on synthetic data

The following Python code generates two Gaussian groups with different base rates, sweeps group-specific thresholds (the post-processing family of [2]), and traces the accuracy vs. equalized-odds-violation frontier.

```python
import numpy as np
from sklearn.linear_model import LogisticRegression

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)

def make_group(n, base_rate, mean_shift):
    y = rng.binomial(1, base_rate, size=n)
    x = rng.normal(loc=np.where(y == 1, 1.0, -1.0) + mean_shift, scale=1.0, size=(n, 4))
    return x, y

X0, y0 = make_group(6000, 0.55, 0.0)
X1, y1 = make_group(2000, 0.35, 0.6)
X = np.vstack([X0, X1]); y = np.concatenate([y0, y1])
A = np.concatenate([np.zeros(6000, dtype=int), np.ones(2000, dtype=int)])

clf = LogisticRegression(max_iter=1000).fit(X, y)
scores = clf.predict_proba(X)[:, 1]

def rates(mask, thr):
    pred = scores[mask] >= thr
    yt = y[mask]
    tpr = pred[yt == 1].mean() if (yt == 1).any() else 0.0
    fpr = pred[yt == 0].mean() if (yt == 0).any() else 0.0
    return tpr, fpr

frontier = []
for t0 in np.linspace(0.05, 0.95, 37):
    for t1 in np.linspace(0.05, 0.95, 37):
        m0, m1 = A == 0, A == 1
        tpr0, fpr0 = rates(m0, t0)
        tpr1, fpr1 = rates(m1, t1)
        acc = ((scores[m0] >= t0) == y[m0]).mean() * m0.mean() \
            + ((scores[m1] >= t1) == y[m1]).mean() * m1.mean()
        viol = abs(tpr0 - tpr1) + abs(fpr0 - fpr1)   # equalized-odds gap
        frontier.append((1 - acc, viol, t0, t1))

# Pareto filter: keep points not dominated in (error, violation)
pts = np.array([(e, v) for e, v, _, _ in frontier])
pareto = [p for p in frontier
          if not np.any((pts[:, 0] <= p[0]) & (pts[:, 1] <= p[1])
                        & ((pts[:, 0] < p[0]) | (pts[:, 1] < p[1])))]
pareto.sort(key=lambda p: p[1])
for err, viol, t0, t1 in pareto[::max(1, len(pareto) // 8)][:8]:
    print(f"violation={viol:.3f}  error={err:.3f}  thresholds=({t0:.2f}, {t1:.2f})")
```

Typical output reproduces the three regimes of Section 4.3: free fairness, a linear middle segment, and steep degradation near exact equalized odds — the impossibility theorems rendered as a price schedule.

The equalized-odds gap $\mathcal{V}_{EO}$ is cheap to audit at serving time:

```rust
/// Equalized-odds gap |ΔTPR| + |ΔFPR| from (tp, fp, tn, fn) counts.
pub fn equalized_odds_gap(g0: (u64, u64, u64, u64), g1: (u64, u64, u64, u64)) -> f64 {
    let rates = |(tp, fp, _tn, fn): (u64, u64, u64, u64)| {
        let tpr = tp as f64 / (tp + fn).max(1) as f64;
        let fpr = fp as f64 / (fp + _tn).max(1) as f64;
        (tpr, fpr)
    };
    let (tpr0, fpr0) = rates(g0);
    let (tpr1, fpr1) = rates(g1);
    (tpr0 - tpr1).abs() + (fpr0 - fpr1).abs()
}
```

## 6. Limitations

The geometric account developed here is powerful but bounded, and its boundaries matter.

**Obliviousness is a ceiling, not a foundation.** Oblivious measures cannot distinguish disparities caused by the classifier from those inherited from the data [2]; causal notions of fairness require structural assumptions the impossibility theorems do not address.

**Base rates are not metaphysical.** The theorems fix $p_a$, but decisions *change* base rates — and statically "fair" policies can harm protected groups dynamically [6], a dimension the snapshot frontier misses.

**The normative gap.** Nothing in the mathematics selects *which* criterion to enforce: calibration, separation, and independence encode different moral theories, and the theorems prove they conflict without adjudicating between them. Choosing a frontier operating point is a *policy* decision; presenting the frontier honestly — levelling-down regions marked — is the most the formalism can contribute [8].

---

## 7. Conclusion

The central results of algorithmic fairness — the impossibility theorems [1][5], post-processing corrections [2][3], and Pareto-frontier analysis [7] — form *one geometric story*: fairness criteria are affine level sets; impossibility is the emptiness of their intersection under unequal base rates; post-processing is projection onto a fairness level set; and the trade-off is the Pareto frontier, priced by its slope.

Three consequences follow. First, *audit all three vertices of the triangle*: the theorems guarantee unreported criteria disagree when base rates differ. Second, *trace the frontier*: stakeholders should see the whole price schedule — levelling-down regions included — before choosing. Third, *treat axiomatic fairness with suspicion*: consequence-blind constraints can select Pareto-dominated policies [4][8]; the honest alternative is optimizing outcomes directly with the frontier as decision aid.

The geometry cannot resolve the normative question — no theorem tells a lender or a judge which criterion to want. It makes the *structure* of the choice visible: a triangle that cannot close, a projection that cannot create information, a frontier that cannot be wished away. Fairness is not a property a classifier has but a trade-off a society negotiates.
