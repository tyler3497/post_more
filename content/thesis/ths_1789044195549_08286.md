# Continuous-Depth Learning with Neural Ordinary Differential Equations: From the Adjoint Sensitivity Method to Flow-Based Generative Modeling

## Abstract

Deep residual networks discretize depth: each layer applies one explicit Euler step of a hidden-state update, *h*ₜ₊₁ = *h*ₜ + *f*(*h*ₜ, θₜ). This paper treats the limit where layer depth becomes a continuous variable and parameterizes the *derivative* of the hidden state, d*h*(t)/dt = *f*(*h*(t), t, θ), computing the network output by solving this ordinary differential equation with a black-box numerical solver. The central technical contribution is a memory-efficient training procedure based on the adjoint sensitivity method: rather than backpropagating through the solver's internal operations at *O(N)* memory cost, gradients are obtained by solving a second, augmented ODE backward in time, yielding *O(1)* memory complexity regardless of the number of function evaluations. Applied to density estimation, the same continuous viewpoint yields continuous normalizing flows, in which the instantaneous change-of-variables formula reduces the log-determinant-Jacobian computation to a single trace operation; combined with Hutchinson's stochastic trace estimator, this enables scalable exact-likelihood generative models with unrestricted architectures (FFJORD). We survey the theory of the adjoint method, the trace-based likelihood, and their applications to irregularly-sampled time series, and discuss limitations including stiffness, trajectory-crossing constraints, and the cost of adaptive stepping.

## 1 Introduction

The *depth* of a neural network is ordinarily a hyperparameter: one chooses an integer number of layers, each performing a discrete transformation. Residual networks reframed depth as a *residual* update, *h*ₜ₊₁ = *h*ₜ + *f*(*h*ₜ, θₜ) [4], and a long-standing observation notes that this update is precisely one explicit Euler step of an ordinary differential equation. The Neural Ordinary Differential Equation (Neural ODE) framework of Chen et al. takes this observation seriously as an architectural principle rather than a curiosity: instead of specifying a discrete sequence of hidden layers, one specifies the continuous-time dynamics of the hidden state with a neural network, and hands the integration to a black-box ODE solver [1].

This shift has three profound consequences:

1. **Adaptive computation.** A black-box solver chooses its own step sizes and number of function evaluations (NFE) to meet an error tolerance, so computation is allocated adaptively: simple inputs are integrated in a handful of steps, hard inputs in many.
2. **Memory-efficient training.** The adjoint sensitivity method computes gradients by solving a second ODE backward in time, giving *constant* memory cost with respect to depth — a property no discrete backpropagation scheme enjoys.
3. **Continuous normalizing flows.** When the ODE's vector field is invertible-by-construction (flows of a differential equation are always invertible), the model becomes a normalizing flow whose change-of-variables term collapses from an *O(D³)* determinant to an *O(D)* trace, enabling exact-likelihood generative modeling with free-form architectures [2].

> **Theorem (Adjoint sensitivity):** Let *z*(t) solve d*z*/dt = *f*(*z*(t), t, θ) with loss *L*(*z*(t₁)). Define the adjoint *a*(t) = ∂*L*/∂*z*(t). Then *a*(t) satisfies d*a*/dt = −*a*(t)ᵀ ∂*f*/∂*z*, and d*L*/dθ = −∫ₜ₁ᵗ⁰ *a*(t)ᵀ ∂*f*/∂θ dt — a computation requiring no storage of intermediate solver states [1].

The remainder of this work develops this theory from first principles: the continuous-depth viewpoint, the adjoint method and its relationship to optimal control, continuous normalizing flows and stochastic trace estimation, and empirical results across classification, density estimation, and irregular time series. We conclude with a candid discussion of limitations.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 From residual blocks to continuous dynamics

A residual network with *L* layers computes *h*ₜ₊₁ = *h*ₜ + *f*(*h*ₜ, θₜ) for t = 0, …, *L* − 1 [4]. If we interpret t as a continuous variable and absorb the layer-dependent parameters into a time-dependent function θ(t), the formal limit Δt → 0 yields the ODE d*h*/dt = *f*(*h*(t), t, θ). Neural ODEs make this limit concrete by *sharing* a single parameterized function *f*(·, θ) across the entire trajectory and evaluating *h*(t₁) = *h*(t₀) + ∫ *f*(*h*(t), t, θ) dt with an adaptive solver such as Dormand–Prince [1].

Several properties distinguish this from merely sharing weights across residual blocks:

- **Evaluation is adaptive.** The solver increases NFE for inputs whose trajectories are numerically stiff and decreases it for trivial ones — a per-example form of adaptive compute.
- **Reversibility comes free.** ODE solvers can integrate backward in time, so the forward trajectory can be recomputed from its endpoint rather than stored.
- **Homeomorphism constraint.** Flows of ODEs with Lipschitz-continuous *f* are *homeomorphisms*: distinct trajectories cannot cross. The network cannot, in one continuous sweep, map two distinct inputs to the same output or tear the input manifold — a topological restriction that forces augmented architectures for some tasks.

### 2.2 Classical sensitivity analysis and optimal control

The adjoint sensitivity method is not new to machine learning. Pontryagin and collaborators developed adjoint equations in the 1960s for *optimal control*: given a dynamical system and a cost functional, the adjoint state encodes how the cost varies with the system state, and its backward-in-time evolution follows from the same calculus that yields Pontryagin's maximum principle [6]. Reverse-mode automatic differentiation is the discrete-time special case of this idea. What Chen et al. contributed was the *computational realization* for neural differential equations — casting backpropagation through a black-box solver as an augmented ODE initial-value problem that modern autograd frameworks can solve without unrolling [1].

### 2.3 Normalizing flows and the change of variables

A normalizing flow defines a density *p*ₓ on data *x* by pushing a base density *p*𝓏 through an invertible map *g*: log *p*ₓ(*x*) = log *p*𝓏(*z*) − log|det ∂*g*/∂*z*|. Discrete flows (NICE, Real NVP, Glow) achieve cheap determinants by *restricting architectures*: coupling layers yield triangular Jacobians, rank-one weight updates yield matrix-determinant-lemma tricks [2]. These restrictions are the price of tractability. The continuous analogue removes them: because d*z*(t)/dt = *f*(*z*(t), t, θ) defines a diffeomorphism for every Lipschitz *f*, *any* neural network can serve as the vector field, and the instantaneous change of variables

> d log *p*(*z*(t)) / dt = −tr(∂*f*/∂*z*)

replaces the determinant with a trace [1]. The price moves from architecture to computation: the trace still costs *O(D²)* naively, and adaptive solvers can be slower than a fixed forward pass.

### 2.4 Hutchinson's trace estimator

Hutchinson observed in 1990 that tr(*A*) = **E**[εᵀ *A* ε] for any random vector ε with **E**[εεᵀ] = *I* — for instance, Rademacher (±1) noise [5]. A vector–Jacobian product εᵀ(∂*f*/∂*z*) is exactly what reverse-mode autograd computes in *O(D)* time, so the trace of the Jacobian is estimated in a *single* backward pass with *unbiased* stochasticity. Grathwohl et al. recognized that this estimator plugs directly into the continuous change-of-variables formula, yielding FFJORD: a flow-based generative model with unrestricted vector fields, exact likelihood *in expectation*, and one-pass sampling [2].
## 3 Methodology

We present the complete mathematical and computational pipeline: the augmented adjoint ODE, its numerical implementation in autograd frameworks, and the trace-based density evaluation used for generative modeling.

### 3.1 The augmented adjoint system

Given the forward IVP d*z*/dt = *f*(*z*(t), t, θ), *z*(t₀) = *z*₀, and a scalar loss *L* = *L*(*z*(t₁)), we define the adjoint *a*(t) := ∂*L*/∂*z*(t) and augment the state with the parameter and time adjoints. Chen et al. show that the quantity *s*(t) = [*z*(t), *a*(t), ∂*L*/∂θ(t)] satisfies a single augmented ODE [1]:

```
d/dt [ z(t)        ]   [ f(z(t), t, θ)                  ]
d/dt [ a(t)        ] = [ -a(t)^T ∂f/∂z                  ]
d/dt [ ∂L/∂θ(t)    ]   [ -a(t)^T ∂f/∂θ                  ]
```

with initial condition at t₁ of [*z*(t₁), ∂*L*/∂*z*(t₁), 0], integrated *backward* to t₀. The gradients ∂*L*/∂*z*(t₀) and ∂*L*/∂θ emerge as the final adjoint states. Crucially:

1. **No trajectory is stored.** The forward states *z*(t) are reconstructed on the fly by re-integrating from *z*(t₁) backward (or, in practice, by caching checkpoints), so memory is *O(1)* in the number of solver steps rather than *O(NFE)*.
2. **One extra vector–Jacobian product per step.** The term *a*ᵀ∂*f*/∂*z* is computed by autograd as a VJP, matching the per-step cost of forward evaluation up to a constant factor.
3. **The solver is a black box.** Any adaptive method — Dormand–Prince (dopri5), Adams, or implicit stiff solvers — can be used, because the method never differentiates through the solver's internal step logic.

In practice, the `torchdiffeq` library implements both `odeint` (direct backpropagation through a fixed-step solver) and `odeint_adjoint` (the adjoint method), and a typical training loop is:

```python
import torch
from torchdiffeq import odeint_adjoint

class ODEFunc(torch.nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, dim):
        super().__init__()
        self.net = torch.nn.Sequential(
            torch.nn.Linear(dim + 1, 64),  # +1 for time t
            torch.nn.Tanh(),
            torch.nn.Linear(64, dim),
        )
    def forward(self, t, z):
        # time is concatenated so f is explicitly time-dependent
        return self.net(torch.cat([z, t.expand(z.shape[0], 1)], dim=1))

f = ODEFunc(dim=64)
z0 = encoder(x)                                  # (B, 64)
zt = odeint_adjoint(f, z0, torch.tensor([0., 1.]))  # (2, B, 64)
logits = classifier(zt[-1])
loss = torch.nn.functional.cross_entropy(logits, y)
loss.backward()  # O(1) memory via the adjoint ODE
optimizer.step()
```

The `+1` time input is essential: without explicit time dependence, the vector field is autonomous and the model loses expressive power, since the same transformation would apply at every "depth" [1].

### 3.2 Density evaluation via the instantaneous change of variables

For a continuous normalizing flow, the state is augmented once more with the log-density. Defining Δlog *p*(t) := log *p*(*z*(t)) − log *p*(*z*(t₀)), the instantaneous change-of-variables theorem gives the coupled system [1]:

| Quantity | Forward ODE |
|---|---|
| State *z*(t) | d*z*/dt = *f*(*z*(t), t, θ) |
| Log-density Δlog *p* | dΔlog *p*/dt = −tr(∂*f*/∂*z*) |
| Loss gradient | via adjoint (Section 3.1) |

Naively computing tr(∂*f*/∂*z*) costs *O(D²)* (forming the full Jacobian). FFJORD replaces it with Hutchinson's estimator: sample ε ~ Rademacher (±1)ᴰ and compute εᵀ(∂*f*/∂*z*)ε by a single VJP — an *unbiased* estimate of the trace in *O(D)* time [2]. During training, the stochasticity acts as a mild regularizer; at test time, the exact trace (or a many-sample Monte Carlo estimate) can be computed for precise likelihood evaluation.

The key architectural freedoms unlocked by this construction are worth enumerating:

- **No invertibility constraints.** Every Lipschitz vector field defines a diffeomorphism, so *concatsquash* layers, dense MLPs, and convolutional blocks can all serve as *f* without triangular-Jacobian engineering.
- **Exact likelihood.** Unlike VAEs (which maximize an ELBO) or GANs (which offer no likelihood at all), CNFs compute log *p*ₓ(*x*) directly — the estimator is unbiased, so the expected gradient of the estimated loss equals the gradient of the true loss.
- **One-pass sampling.** Drawing *x* = *z*(t₁) from *z*₀ ~ *p*𝓏 requires a single forward solve, versus the *D*-pass sequential sampling of autoregressive flows.

### 3.3 Continuous-time latent variable models

For irregularly-sampled time series {(tᵢ, *x*ᵢ)}, Rubanova et al. replace the discrete RNN hidden update with a continuous one [3]. An encoder (an ODE-RNN run backward over observations) produces a posterior *q*(*z*₀ | data) over the initial latent state; the latent trajectory *z*(t) then evolves under a learned ODE; and a decoder emits observations *p*(*x*ᵢ | *z*(tᵢ)). Because time is continuous, observations at *arbitrary* timestamps are handled naturally — the solver simply integrates to each requested tᵢ — whereas discrete RNNs must impute or bin the data. Training maximizes an ELBO whose KL term and reconstruction term are both evaluated along the continuous trajectory.
## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The adjoint method as continuous-time backpropagation

To understand why the adjoint method works, consider ordinary backpropagation as the discretization of the adjoint ODE. In a discrete network *h*ₜ₊₁ = *h*ₜ + ε*f*(*h*ₜ), the chain rule gives ∂*L*/∂*h*ₜ = ∂*L*/∂*h*ₜ₊₁ · (*I* + ε∂*f*/∂*h*). Rearranging, (∂*L*/∂*h*ₜ − ∂*L*/∂*h*ₜ₊₁)/ε = −(∂*L*/∂*h*ₜ₊₁)ᵀ∂*f*/∂*h*; taking ε → 0 yields exactly d*a*/dt = −*a*ᵀ∂*f*/∂*h*. Backpropagation *is* the Euler discretization of the adjoint equation. The adjoint method simply solves the continuous equation with an adaptive solver instead of the Euler scheme — a perspective that unifies deep learning's workhorse algorithm with six decades of optimal-control theory [6][1].

This unity has a practical corollary: the backward pass costs roughly the same number of function evaluations as the forward pass (the adjoint vector field involves one VJP, comparable in cost to one evaluation of *f*), and numerical error in the backward solve is controlled by the same tolerance machinery as the forward solve.

### 4.2 Why the trace replaces the determinant

The instantaneous change of variables follows from Jacobi's formula for the derivative of a determinant. For the discrete flow *z*ₜ₊ε = *z*ₜ + ε*f*(*z*ₜ), the Jacobian is *I* + ε∂*f*/∂*z*, and log det(*I* + ε*A*) = ε·tr(*A*) + *O*(ε²). Accumulating over infinitesimal steps, the *O*(ε²) terms vanish and the total log-volume change becomes the time integral of the trace:

> log *p*(*z*(t₁)) = log *p*(*z*(t₀)) − ∫ₜ₀ᵗ¹ tr(∂*f*/∂*z*(t)) dt.

Intuitively, the trace measures the *divergence* of the vector field — how much it locally expands or contracts volume — and integrating the divergence along the trajectory accounts for all volume change. This is Liouville's theorem from statistical mechanics, applied to learned dynamics [1].

### 4.3 Hutchinson's estimator: unbiased gradients through noise

Replacing the exact trace with εᵀ(∂*f*/∂*z*)ε introduces stochasticity into the loss, but the estimator is *unbiased*: **E**ε[εᵀ*A*ε] = tr(*A*) whenever **E**[εεᵀ] = *I*. Since the gradient operator is linear, the *expected* gradient of the stochastic loss equals the gradient of the true loss — stochastic gradient descent remains valid [2][5]. FFJORD's authors use Rademacher noise (entries ±1 with equal probability) because it minimizes variance among zero-mean unit-covariance distributions, and they report that the resulting gradient noise is comparable in magnitude to ordinary minibatch noise, so training is stable without variance-reduction machinery.

### 4.4 Stiffness, trajectory crossing, and the homeomorphism barrier

The elegance of the adjoint method does not eliminate numerical realities. Three failure modes recur:

1. **Stiffness.** If *f* has large Lipschitz constant along some directions, explicit solvers (dopri5) take tiny steps and NFE explodes. Implicit solvers handle stiffness but require solving nonlinear systems per step — costly in high dimensions. In practice, learned vector fields often *become* stiff during training, and monitoring NFE is essential.
2. **Numerical drift in the adjoint.** The backward solve reconstructs *z*(t) from *z*(t₁); numerical error accumulates, and the reconstructed trajectory can diverge from the forward one, injecting gradient error. Tight tolerances mitigate this at the cost of more NFE. Recent work on memory-efficient discrete-adjoint variants shows the continuous adjoint's gradients can disagree with exact backprop-through-solver gradients at finite step sizes [8].
3. **The homeomorphism barrier.** Because ODE flows cannot cross trajectories, a Neural ODE cannot represent functions that map two distinct inputs to one output — such as the two-circles or concentric-rings classification boundaries — *unless* the state space is augmented with extra dimensions (a trick analogous to adding channels in discrete networks) [1]. Augmented Neural ODEs, which evolve [*z*(t); *a*(t)] in a higher-dimensional space, recover universal approximation at modest extra cost.

## 5 Empirical Results / Proofs

The original Neural ODE paper evaluates three regimes, each demonstrating a distinct virtue of continuous depth [1].

**MNIST classification.** An ODE-Net (downsampling convolutions, then a single ODE block in place of a ResNet stack, then a linear classifier) reaches approximately 0.60% test error with ~0.22M parameters, versus a ResNet's ~0.41% with ~0.60M parameters — comparable accuracy with roughly one-third the parameters. Forward NFE averaged ~44 with the adjoint backward pass roughly doubling that. The experiment's point is not raw accuracy but *parameter efficiency*: continuous depth lets one small vector-field network do the work of many discrete layers.

**Continuous normalizing flows on toy densities.** On the two-circles and two-moons datasets, CNFs learn vector fields that transport a Gaussian base into intricate multimodal targets — transformations no coupling-layer flow with comparable capacity achieves, because the free-form Jacobian permits arbitrary local stretching and rotation.

**Latent ODEs on irregular time series.** On a synthetic spirals dataset with sparse observations, Latent ODEs extrapolate trajectories far beyond the observed window where RNN baselines diverge. On the PhysioNet ICU dataset (irregularly sampled vital signs, mortality prediction), Latent ODEs achieve approximately 0.85 AUC versus ~0.81 for RNN baselines, demonstrating that continuous time is not a luxury but a modeling necessity when timestamps are irregular [3].

**FFJORD density estimation.** FFJORD scales the CNF idea to image data: on MNIST it achieves 0.99 bits/dim and on CIFAR-10 3.40 bits/dim (test), state-of-the-art among exact-likelihood models with efficient sampling at the time — accomplished with unrestricted convolutional vector fields that no discrete flow architecture could tractably invert [2].

*Sketch of the adjoint theorem's proof.* Consider the Lagrangian ℒ = *L*(*z*(t₁)) − ∫ *a*(t)ᵀ(d*z*/dt − *f*) dt. Variation with respect to *z*(t) and integration by parts yields the adjoint ODE d*a*/dt = −*a*ᵀ∂*f*/∂*z* with terminal condition *a*(t₁) = ∂*L*/∂*z*(t₁); variation with respect to θ yields the parameter-gradient integral. This is the standard Pontryagin derivation, reproduced in Appendix B of [1] and in every optimal-control text [6].

## 6 Limitations

**Computational cost.** Adaptive solvers are slower per epoch than fixed discrete layers, often by 2–5×, and NFE can grow during training as vector fields stiffen. The *O(1)* memory claim is real but trades memory for recomputation: the backward pass re-solves the forward trajectory, so wall-clock cost roughly doubles relative to backprop-through-solver.

**Gradient fidelity.** The continuous adjoint is exact only in the limit of vanishing solver tolerance; at practical tolerances, its gradients can differ from the true gradients of the discretized forward pass, occasionally destabilizing optimization. Discrete-adjoint (differentiate-then-discretize swapped) variants fix fidelity at the cost of memory [8].

**Topological expressivity.** Homeomorphism constraints force state augmentation for non-injective maps; augmented ODEs partially address this but reintroduce architectural choices the framework was meant to eliminate.

**Likelihood estimation variance.** FFJORD's unbiased trace estimator has variance that grows with dimension and Jacobian norm; single-sample estimates can make validation likelihoods noisy, requiring multi-sample evaluation for reliable model comparison [2].

**Tuning burden.** Solver choice (dopri5 vs. Adams vs. implicit), tolerances (rtol/atol), and time-augmentation are new hyperparameters with sharp effects on both accuracy and speed — a tuning surface unfamiliar to most practitioners.

## 7 Conclusion

Neural ordinary differential equations reframe depth from a discrete architectural choice into a continuous physical process, and in doing so recover — with modern computational force — the adjoint methods of classical optimal control. The adjoint sensitivity method's *O(1)* memory training, the instantaneous change-of-variables formula's reduction of determinants to traces, and Hutchinson's estimator's reduction of traces to vector–Jacobian products form a coherent ladder of ideas: each step replaces an intractable exact computation with a cheaper equivalent, culminating in FFJORD's exact-likelihood generative models with unrestricted architectures and one-pass sampling. Empirically, the framework has proven its worth where continuity is intrinsic — irregular time series, adaptive computation, and flow-based density estimation — while remaining slower and fussier than discrete networks for standard supervised tasks. The lasting contribution may be conceptual: the recognition that backpropagation is the Euler discretization of a continuous adjoint equation, and that the space between layers was always differentiable. Future work on stiff solvers, discrete-adjoint hybrids, and augmented state spaces continues to close the gap between the elegance of the theory and the demands of practice.
