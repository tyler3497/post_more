# Conflict-Driven Clause Learning in Modern SAT Solvers: VSIDS Branching, LBD Clause Quality, Two-Watched Literals, Restarts, and DRAT Certification

## Abstract

The propositional satisfiability problem (SAT) is the canonical NP-complete decision problem, yet modern conflict-driven clause learning (CDCL) solvers routinely decide industrial formulas with millions of variables and clauses. This thesis presents a unified technical treatment of the CDCL architecture that displaced classical DPLL search: Boolean constraint propagation via the two-watched-literal scheme, decision heuristics centered on VSIDS activity scores and phase saving, first-unique-implication-point conflict analysis producing asserting clauses, learned-clause database management governed by literals-blocks-distance (LBD) quality estimates, aggressive restart policies, inprocessing simplification, and DRAT proof logging for independently checkable unsatisfiability certificates. We formalize each component, state its key invariants as theorems, analyze asymptotic costs, and connect the engineering to proof complexity: a CDCL run corresponds to a resolution refutation, and with restarts the method can simulate general resolution exponentially more compactly than restart-free variants. The result is both a practical blueprint and a theoretical map of why CDCL dominates automated reasoning.

## 1. Introduction

Fifty years after Cook's theorem established propositional satisfiability as NP-complete [9], SAT solvers have become the workhorse of formal verification, planning, cryptography, and electronic design automation. The decisive algorithmic break came not from asymptotically better worst-case bounds — none exist under standard complexity assumptions — but from *conflict-driven clause learning* (CDCL), a search paradigm that turns every dead end into reusable knowledge.

The lineage is well documented [4][6]. DPLL (1962) combined backtracking with unit propagation but forgot every conflict it backtracked past. GRASP (1996) introduced clause learning and non-chronological backtracking. Chaff (2001) showed the *engineering* matters as much as the learning: VSIDS branching and two-watched literals yielded order-of-magnitude speedups [9]. MiniSat (2003) distilled CDCL to a minimal extensible core [2]; Glucose (2009) added LBD quality measurement and aggressive deletion, winning gold in the UNSAT category of the 2009 SAT Competition [1][7].

This thesis dissects the modern CDCL solver into six interacting subsystems and treats each with the precision it deserves:

1. **Boolean constraint propagation (BCP)** via two-watched literals — the inner loop, consuming the overwhelming majority of runtime.
2. **Branching heuristics** — VSIDS activity scores, decay, and phase saving, which choose the next decision variable and polarity.
3. **Conflict analysis** — implication graphs, the first-unique-implication-point (1UIP) scheme, and clause minimization.
4. **Clause database management** — LBD-based quality estimation, glue clauses, and periodic deletion.
5. **Restarts and inprocessing** — restart schedules and formula simplification during search.
6. **Proof logging** — DRAT certificates that make UNSAT answers independently verifiable.

Our contribution is a self-contained synthesis: formal invariants, cost analyses, proof-complexity connections, and a candid account of the method's limits.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 Propositional Satisfiability in CNF

A *literal* is a Boolean variable $x$ or its negation $\lnot x$. A *clause* is a disjunction of literals; a *CNF formula* $F$ is a conjunction of clauses. An *assignment* $\sigma$ maps variables to $\{\top, \bot\}$. $F$ is *satisfiable* if some $\sigma$ satisfies every clause; otherwise it is *unsatisfiable*.

CDCL maintains a *trail*: the sequence of assignments, partitioned into *decision levels*. Level $0$ holds pre-decision assignments; each decision opens a new level, and the literals it implies by unit propagation share that level. A clause is *unit* under $\sigma$ if all but one literal is falsified and the survivor is unassigned; *conflicting* if all its literals are falsified.

### 2.2 From DPLL to CDCL

DPLL explores a binary search tree: decide, propagate, and on conflict backtrack chronologically. Its weakness is amnesia — the same conflict region is rediscovered repeatedly. CDCL replaces this with *conflict analysis*: each conflict yields a *learned clause* (a logical consequence of $F$) added to the database, and search backtracks *non-chronologically* to the level the learned clause dictates [3][6].

> **Theorem: Resolution soundness of clause learning.** Every clause learned by CDCL conflict analysis is derivable from the input formula by a sequence of resolution steps, hence is a logical consequence of $F$. *Proof sketch.* Conflict analysis resolves the conflicting clause with reason clauses along the implication graph; each resolution step is sound, so the final learned clause is entailed by $F$. ∎

The central theoretical lens is *proof complexity*: an UNSAT run of CDCL implicitly constructs a resolution refutation of $F$. Understanding CDCL is therefore understanding which resolution proofs it can find efficiently — and which families (pigeonhole, parity) defeat it.

### 2.3 The CDCL Main Loop

The algorithm can be stated compactly: **preprocess**; **decide** (branching heuristic picks a variable and polarity at a new decision level); **propagate** (BCP to fixpoint or conflict); **analyze** (derive a 1UIP learned clause and its backjump level); **backjump** (the learned clause becomes unit and forces a new implication); periodically **restart** (keeping learned clauses) and **reduce** (deleting low-quality ones). Termination: SAT when all variables are assigned, UNSAT on conflict at level $0$.

Steps 2–5 dominate; step 3 alone typically consumes 80–90% of runtime, which is why the two-watched-literal scheme (§4.1) is the single most important data structure in the solver [6][9].

## 3. Methodology

We analyze CDCL as a composition of six subsystems with explicit interfaces, following the architecture distilled by MiniSat [2] and extended by Glucose [1]:

| Subsystem | Input | Output | Key invariant |
|---|---|---|---|
| BCP (2WL) | new assignment | implications or conflict | watch invariant (§4.1) |
| Branching (VSIDS) | unassigned variables | decision literal | heap-ordered activity |
| Conflict analysis (1UIP) | conflicting clause, trail | learned clause + backjump level | asserting clause (§4.3) |
| Clause DB management | learned clauses + LBD | retained subset | glue clauses never deleted |
| Restarts | conflict/LBD statistics | restart decision | schedule (Luby / dynamic) |
| DRAT logging | clause additions/deletions | proof file | RAT checkable |

Our method is threefold. *First*, we formalize each subsystem's contract and prove its core invariant. *Second*, we analyze asymptotic costs and the empirical constants that matter (cache behavior, watcher-list length). *Third*, we connect the design to proof complexity and measured behavior from the literature [1][5][7].

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Two-Watched Literals and Boolean Constraint Propagation

Naive BCP visits every clause touching an assigned variable — prohibitively expensive when formulas contain millions of clauses. The two-watched-literal (2WL) scheme, introduced in Chaff [9], watches only *two* literals per clause and visits a clause only when one of its watched literals becomes false.

The scheme rests on a single invariant:

> **Theorem: Watch invariant.** For every non-satisfied clause $C$, its two watched literals are never both false unless $C$ is unit or conflicting. Equivalently, if neither watched literal is false, no propagation or conflict can be derived from $C$ yet.

*Why it works.* Suppose watched literal $\ell_1$ becomes false. The solver scans $C$ for a non-false replacement $\ell'$; if one exists, the watch moves. Otherwise all other literals are false: if $\ell_2$ is unassigned, $C$ is unit and implies $\ell_2$; if $\ell_2$ is false, $C$ is conflicting. The engineering payoff: on backtrack, watches are *never undone* — an unassigned watched literal still satisfies the invariant — making undo $O(1)$ per assignment.

Modern solvers add *blocker literals* [1][7]: each watch-list entry caches a literal that would satisfy the clause, letting propagation skip the visit in the common case. The C-style core loop is:

```c
/* propagate literal p; returns conflicting clause or NULL */
Clause *propagate(Solver *S, Lit p) {
    WatchList *ws = &S->watches[neg(p)];
    for (int i = 0, j = 0; i < ws->size; i++) {
        Watcher w = ws->data[i];
        if (w.blocker != UNDEF && value(S, w.blocker) == TRUE) {
            ws->data[j++] = w; continue;   /* blocker: skip clause */
        }
        Clause *c = w.clause;
        /* ensure the false literal is c->lits[1] */
        if (c->lits[0] == neg(p)) { c->lits[0] = c->lits[1]; c->lits[1] = neg(p); }
        /* try to find a new watch */
        int k;
        for (k = 2; k < c->size; k++)
            if (value(S, c->lits[k]) != FALSE) break;
        if (k < c->size) {
            c->lits[1] = c->lits[k]; c->lits[k] = neg(p);
            add_watch(S, c->lits[1], w);    /* move watch */
        } else {
            ws->data[j++] = w;
            if (value(S, c->lits[0]) == FALSE) return c;  /* conflict */
            enqueue(S, c->lits[0], c);                    /* unit: imply */
        }
    }
    ws->size = j;
    return NULL;
}
```

Amortized cost is effectively constant per propagation in practice: a clause is scanned only when a watched literal is falsified, and each scan either relocates a watch or derives a unit/conflict. Since BCP dominates runtime, this lazy structure — visiting clauses only when forced — is the decisive reason CDCL scales to industrial instances [6][9].

### 4.2 VSIDS Branching, Decay, and Phase Saving

*Which variable to branch on next* determines the shape of the entire search tree. The Variable State Independent Decaying Sum (VSIDS) heuristic, introduced in Chaff [9], scores variables by their recent involvement in conflicts:

- **Bump**: when a clause is learned, increment the activity of every variable in the learned clause (and every variable resolved during analysis) by `var_inc` [2].
- **Decay**: periodically multiply `var_inc` by $1/\alpha$ ($\alpha \approx 0.95$), so recent conflicts outweigh ancient ones.
- **Decide**: pick the unassigned variable of maximum activity via a binary heap.

```python
def vsids_decide(solver):
    while solver.heap:
        v = solver.heap.pop_max()          # highest activity
        if solver.value[v] is None:        # still unassigned
            phase = solver.saved_phase[v]  # phase saving
            return (v, phase)
    return None                            # all assigned: SAT
```

MiniSat's adaptation [2] is worth noting: Chaff scored *literals*, while MiniSat scores *variables* and pairs the heap with *phase saving* (Pipatsrisawat and Darwiche, 2007) — caching each variable's last assigned polarity and reusing it at decision time [6]. On industrial instances this is remarkably effective because consecutive conflicts revisit the same region of the search space, so saved phases reconstruct the previous partial assignment almost for free.

*Why VSIDS works* is subtler than its simplicity suggests: it exploits the *community structure* of industrial formulas, where variables in the same community co-occur in conflicts, so bumping focuses search inside the currently active community [5]. The decay implements exponential recency weighting, keeping the heuristic adaptive.

### 4.3 First-UIP Conflict Analysis and Clause Minimization

When BCP derives a conflict, the solver reconstructs the *implication graph*: nodes are assigned literals, edges run from the literals of a reason clause to the literal they implied, and two sinks represent the conflicting literal and its negation [3][5].

A *unique implication point (UIP)* at the conflict level is a node dominating both conflict sinks — every path from the decision literal to the conflict passes through it. The *first UIP* (1UIP) is the one closest to the conflict. The 1UIP *cut* partitions the graph into a reason side and a conflict side; negating the literals on the reason side of the cut yields the learned clause [3].

> **Theorem: Asserting clause property.** The 1UIP learned clause contains exactly one literal from the current decision level. After backjumping to the second-highest decision level among its literals, the clause is unit and immediately implies the negation of that literal.

*Proof sketch.* By construction the cut separates the 1UIP from the conflict, so precisely one conflict-level literal (the 1UIP's negation) appears in the clause. All other literals sit at strictly lower levels; backjumping to the maximum of those levels falsifies them all, leaving the single current-level literal unassigned — hence unit. ∎

Zhang et al. compared several learning schemes empirically and found 1UIP dominant [9]: it produces short, highly constraining clauses whose backjump is typically far — often dozens of levels at once.

Learned clauses can be *minimized*: MiniSat's recursive minimization [2] removes literals implied by the rest of the clause via the existing reason graph (a form of self-subsuming resolution), routinely shrinking clauses by 20–40% at modest cost — and shorter clauses propagate more often.

```haskell
-- Sketch: 1UIP conflict analysis over an implication graph
analyze :: Trail -> Clause -> (Clause, DecisionLevel)
analyze trail confl = go confl (conflictLevel trail)
  where
    go clause lvl
      | countAtLevel lvl clause == 1  = (minimize clause, backjumpLevel clause)
      | otherwise = go (resolve clause (reasonOf (lastAssigned trail lvl))) lvl
```

### 4.4 LBD Clause Quality and Learned-Clause Database Management

Clause learning has a dark side: a solver can learn *millions* of clauses, and as Mitchell observed, BCP then becomes impractical even when memory is plentiful — the watcher lists grow so long that propagation grinds to a halt [5]. Solvers must therefore *forget*: periodically delete a large fraction of learned clauses. The question is *which*.

Early solvers used clause *activity* (bump on use, decay over time), mirroring VSIDS. Audemard and Simon's breakthrough [1] was the **literals blocks distance**:

> **Definition (LBD).** The LBD of a learned clause $C$ is the number of distinct decision levels occurring among its literals, measured at learning time.

A clause with small LBD ties together few blocks of propagated literals — it expresses a strong, local dependency. *Glue clauses* with $\mathrm{LBD} \le 2$ are so valuable that Glucose keeps them **forever**. Empirically, LBD predicts future usefulness far better than clause length or activity [1][7].

Glucose's database policy [1][7] is deliberately aggressive, and the PLOS ONE study [5] confirms the mechanism experimentally: deleting learned clauses is not merely a memory optimization but a *propagation-speed* necessity, and the deletion heuristic measurably shifts the runtime distribution.

| Policy element | MiniSat-style (activity) | Glucose (LBD) |
|---|---|---|
| Quality metric | clause activity, bumped on conflict participation | LBD at learning time (lower is better) |
| Protected clauses | none (all eligible) | glue clauses (LBD ≤ 2) kept permanently |
| Reduction trigger | geometric growth from \|F\|/3 conflicts | 20000 learned clauses initially (+500 per reduction) |
| Reduction amount | remove half by activity | remove half by LBD |
| Restart coupling | independent | restarts driven by LBD moving average |

### 4.5 Restart Policies, Inprocessing, and DRAT Proof Logging

**Restarts.** Gomes, Selman, and Kautz showed that randomized restarts combat heavy-tailed runtime distributions [6]; Baptista and Marques-Silva, and then zChaff, made them aggressive (tens of conflicts). Two schedules dominate today:

- **Luby sequence** ($1,1,2,1,1,2,4,\dots$ scaled by a base conflict count): near-optimal for Las Vegas algorithms (Luby, Sinclair, Zuckerman, 1993), used as a robust fallback [6][8].
- **Glucose dynamic restarts**: restart when the moving average of recent learned-clause LBDs exceeds a factor of the global average — i.e., when learning quality degrades [1][7].

Restarts are *not* free of theory: Pipatsrisawat and Darwiche proved CDCL with restarts can simulate general resolution, and Atserias, Fichte, and Thurley showed restarts yield exponential separations over restart-free CDCL — the schedule is part of the proof system, not just a heuristic [6].

**Inprocessing.** Modern solvers simplify the formula *during* search: bounded variable elimination, vivification, failed-literal probing, and subsumption [6][8] — each preserving satisfiability and, with care, the DRAT proof.

Since 2014 the SAT competition requires UNSAT solvers to emit checkable proofs. The standard is **DRAT** — *Deletion Resolution Asymmetric Tautology* (Wetzman, Heule, and Biere, 2014) [10]: the solver logs every learned-clause addition and deletion, and the checker (`drat-trim`) verifies each addition has the RAT property via a polynomial-time unit-propagation test.

> **Theorem: DRAT checkability.** Verifying a DRAT refutation of $F$ takes time polynomial in the size of the proof. If the checker accepts, $F$ is unsatisfiable.

This closes the trust gap: the solver is an untrusted, highly optimized artifact, but its UNSAT verdicts are certified by a small independent checker — essential when solvers verify hardware and cryptographic primitives.

## 5. Empirical Results and Proof-Theoretic Analysis

### 5.1 Measured Impact

Three quantitative facts anchor CDCL's dominance, drawn from the competition and literature record:

1. **Chaff (2001)** outperformed the best contemporary solvers (GRASP, SATO) by one to two orders of magnitude on industrial benchmarks, establishing VSIDS + 2WL as the new baseline [9].
2. **Glucose (2009)** won the gold medal in the UNSAT category of the SAT Competition (127 instances) and silver overall, on the strength of LBD-based deletion and dynamic restarts [1][7].
3. **Proof-logging overhead** is modest: DRAT emission typically costs well under 20% of solving time, and `drat-trim` verification is routinely faster than solving itself [10]. The MiniSat lineage [2] adds a software-engineering result: a compact extensible core can match far larger solvers, which is why MiniSat became the substrate for dozens of research solvers.

### 5.2 Proof Complexity: What CDCL Can and Cannot Prove Efficiently

Every UNSAT CDCL run without restarts corresponds to a *tree-like* resolution refutation; with restarts and clause learning, the correspondence lifts toward *general* (DAG-like) resolution [6]. The key theoretical landmarks:

- **Beame, Kautz, and Sabharwal (2004)** formalized clause learning as a proof system, showing it polynomially simulates regular resolution variants.
- **Pipatsrisawat and Darwiche (2011)** proved CDCL with restarts and suitable branching can simulate *general* resolution.
- **Atserias, Fichte, and Thurley (2011)** separated the models: formulas exist where CDCL with many restarts is exponentially faster than without — the restart schedule is proof-theoretically load-bearing.

Conversely, CDCL inherits resolution's lower bounds: pigeonhole-principle and Tseitin (parity) formulas require exponentially long resolution refutations, so no CDCL configuration decides them efficiently — a *theorem*, not an engineering gap.

### 5.3 Soundness and Completeness

> **Theorem: CDCL is sound and complete.** If the solver reports SAT, the returned assignment satisfies $F$; if it reports UNSAT, $F$ is unsatisfiable; it always terminates.

*Proof sketch.* SAT: the solver reports SAT only when every clause is satisfied under the final trail. UNSAT: learned clauses are resolution consequences (§2.2), so the empty clause at level $0$ certifies unsatisfiability. Termination: only finitely many distinct clauses exist over $n$ variables, each conflict learns a new one, and deletions never remove already-derived consequences — hence a level-0 conflict is eventually forced. ∎

## 6. Limitations

Intellectual honesty requires enumerating where CDCL fails or strains:

1. **Proof-complexity barriers.** Families with exponential resolution lower bounds — pigeonhole, clique-coloring, Tseitin formulas — defeat every CDCL solver regardless of heuristics (§5.2); parity constraints arise naturally in cryptography.
2. **Heuristic brittleness.** VSIDS is superb on industrial instances with community structure but can be misled on crafted/random formulas where conflict locality is absent; no branching heuristic dominates all benchmark families [5].
3. **Restart sensitivity.** Overly aggressive restarts discard search progress (mitigated by phase saving); overly lazy restarts get trapped in heavy tails. The optimal schedule is instance-dependent and only heuristically approximated [6].
4. **Proof-logging cost on huge proofs.** While typically modest, DRAT proofs of hard UNSAT instances can reach tens of gigabytes, and checking becomes I/O-bound — a practical bottleneck for certification pipelines [10].
5. **Inprocessing soundness risk.** Every simplification must preserve the DRAT proof; bugs in vivification or BVE have historically produced *wrong* UNSAT verdicts — which is why independent checking is mandatory, not optional.
6. **Parallel scaling.** Portfolio and divide-and-conquer solvers scale sublinearly; clause sharing between workers remains incompletely solved, and deterministic parallelism is still elusive.

---

## 7. Conclusion

CDCL's triumph is a triumph of *co-designed* heuristics: two-watched literals make propagation cheap enough to learn aggressively; VSIDS focuses branching where conflicts occur; 1UIP analysis converts each conflict into an asserting clause that backjumps far; LBD identifies the learned clauses worth keeping; restarts escape heavy tails; inprocessing shrinks the problem mid-flight; and DRAT logging makes the whole edifice auditable. Ablation studies consistently show that removing any one of them collapses performance on industrial benchmarks [1][5][7].

The theoretical picture is equally coherent: CDCL is resolution made practical, with restarts lifting it toward the full power of general resolution and proof-complexity lower bounds marking exactly where it must fail. Future progress lies at the boundaries identified in §6: proof systems beyond resolution for parity-heavy formulas and certified inprocessing that never outruns the checker.

Three decades after GRASP first refused to forget a conflict, the lesson endures: in combinatorial search, *what you learn from failure matters more than how fast you fail* — provided you can propagate, branch, and restart fast enough to learn at scale.
