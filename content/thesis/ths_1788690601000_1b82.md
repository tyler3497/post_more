# Post-Quantum Cryptography Migration at Internet Scale: Lattice-Based Key Encapsulation, Hybrid TLS 1.3 Handshakes, and the Certificate Chain Bottleneck

## Abstract

The standardization of post-quantum cryptographic primitives by the United States National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) in August 2024 marked the beginning of the largest migration in the history of public-key cryptography. With FIPS 203 (ML-KEM), FIPS 204 (ML-DSA), and FIPS 205 (SLH-DSA) finalized — and FIPS 206 (FN-DSA) in draft — operators face replacing RSA, ECDH, and ECDSA across an Internet where these primitives protect essentially every byte in transit. This thesis presents a systematic treatment of the migration: the lattice and hash-based foundations of the standardized schemes, hybrid key exchange in TLS 1.3 (X25519 combined with ML-KEM via the concatenation construction), the KEMTLS alternative that replaces handshake signatures with key encapsulation, the certificate chain bloat problem and its TCP fragmentation consequences, empirical performance evidence from Cloudflare, Google, and Amazon, the harvest-now-decrypt-later threat model, and NSA's CNSA 2.0 transition timelines. We close on downgrade-attack resistance, crypto-agility as an open research problem, and the engineering realities — middleboxes, congestion windows, embedded devices — that will decide whether the migration finishes before a cryptographically relevant quantum computer arrives.

---

## 1. Introduction

Public-key cryptography rests on two computational assumptions that have withstood decades of scrutiny: the hardness of integer factorization and the hardness of the discrete logarithm problem. Both assumptions are *classical* in the sense that no known efficient classical algorithm breaks them at cryptographic scales. Both are simultaneously rendered false by Shor's algorithm [1], published in 1994, which solves both problems in polynomial time on a quantum computer of sufficient scale. The relevant question is therefore not *whether* the assumptions fall, but *when* a cryptographically relevant quantum computer (CRQC) arrives — and, critically, what damage is inflicted *before* that moment.

The answer to the second question is the *harvest-now-decrypt-later* (HNDL) adversary model. An adversary with recording capability — a state intelligence service, a compromised backbone router, any tap on a trans-oceanic fiber link — needs no quantum computer today. It merely stores ciphertext whose confidentiality period exceeds the horizon of quantum arrival. The National Security Agency's Commercial National Security Algorithm Suite 2.0 (CNSA 2.0) advisory explicitly frames the transition in these terms: systems whose data must remain confidential for decades are vulnerable *today*, regardless of the date at which a CRQC materializes [2]. Mosca's theorem formalizes the intuition: if *x* is the confidentiality lifetime, *y* the migration time, and *z* the time to a CRQC, then remediation must begin when *x + y > z*. For many government and financial systems, that inequality has already bitten.

NIST's eight-year post-quantum standardization process, launched in 2016, concluded its first phase on August 13, 2024, with the publication of three Federal Information Processing Standards [3]:

| Standard | Algorithm (legacy name) | Function | Mathematical basis |
|---|---|---|---|
| FIPS 203 | ML-KEM (CRYSTALS-Kyber) | Key encapsulation | Module-LWE lattice problem |
| FIPS 204 | ML-DSA (CRYSTALS-Dilithium) | Digital signatures | Module-LWE / module-SIS lattice problems |
| FIPS 205 | SLH-DSA (SPHINCS+) | Digital signatures (backup) | Hash functions only |
| FIPS 206 (draft) | FN-DSA (FALCON) | Digital signatures (compact) | NTRU lattice, FFT sampling |

A fifth algorithm, HQC — a code-based KEM selected in March 2025 as a lattice-diverse backup for ML-KEM — rounds out the portfolio [3]. This thesis examines what follows the standards: the migration of the Internet's cryptographic substrate, the largest such migration ever attempted.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 The Quantum Threat to Deployed Primitives

Shor's algorithm factors an *n*-bit integer in *O(n³)* quantum gate operations and solves the discrete logarithm in comparable time. A fault-tolerant machine with on the order of 10⁶–10⁷ physical qubits at 99.9%+ gate fidelity is estimated to break RSA-2048 within hours to days [4]. Every TLS 1.3 handshake performed with ECDHE key exchange, every ECDSA-signed certificate, every RSA-wrapped key in SSH — all of it is recorded-or-not, broken the day such a machine runs. Symmetric cryptography (AES-256, SHA-384) degrades to half its strength under Grover's algorithm but remains qualitatively secure; the migration crisis is strictly asymmetric.

> **Theorem: (Quantum Vulnerability of Classical Asymmetric Cryptography).** *Any public-key scheme whose security reduces to integer factorization or to the discrete logarithm problem in a finite field or elliptic curve group is broken in polynomial time given a fault-tolerant quantum computer with Θ(poly(n)) logical qubits, where n is the security parameter. Consequently, confidentiality derived from such schemes under the HNDL model is bounded by the arrival time of a CRQC, not by the scheme's classical security.*

### 2.2 The NIST Selection Criteria

NIST evaluated submissions on three axes: security (classical and quantum, with categories I–V anchored to the hardness of breaking AES-128/192/256 and SHA-256/384), cost (key/ciphertext/signature sizes, gate cycles, RAM), and characteristics (ease of implementation, simplicity of design, flexibility). Notably, lattice-based schemes swept both primary slots because Module-LWE offers the best *size–speed–confidence* tradeoff of any post-quantum family, while SLH-DSA was selected precisely because its hash-based foundations are *independent* of lattice assumptions — a hedge against a catastrophic break [5].

### 2.3 Related Standardization and Deployment Efforts

The IETF TLS working group is standardizing hybrid key exchange in TLS 1.3 (draft-ietf-tls-hybrid-design) using the concatenation construction [6]. Google's Chrome enabled X25519+Kyber768 by default in Chrome 124 (April 2024) following the CECPQ2 experiments [7]. Cloudflare enabled hybrid Kyber key agreement for all customers — free, on by default — as early as 2022 [8]. Apple deployed the PQ3 protocol in iMessage (February 2024) combining classical ECDH with Kyber [9]. The NSA's CNSA 2.0 sets hard dates: new NSS acquisitions must be CNSA 2.0-compliant by January 1, 2027; legacy equipment that cannot support CNSA 2.0 must be phased out by December 31, 2030; and exclusive use of CNSA 2.0 algorithms is mandated by December 31, 2031, with full quantum resistance targeted for 2035 [2].

## 3. Methodology

This thesis synthesizes three kinds of evidence. **(1) Standards and specification analysis**: the final texts of FIPS 203, 204, and 205, the draft FIPS 206 material on FN-DSA, the CNSA 2.0 FAQ (v2.1, December 2024), and the IETF hybrid-design draft. **(2) Empirical deployment data**: published measurements from Cloudflare's post-quantum edge, Google's Chrome and Cloud rollouts, Amazon's analysis of handshake latency under bandwidth and loss, and Cisco's TLS/SSH benchmarks. **(3) Protocol design literature**: the KEMTLS line of work (CCS 2020 and follow-ups) and recent measurement studies of certificate chain sizes on time-to-first-byte (TTFB).

Our analytical method is comparative and quantitative: we evaluate each migration mechanism against five criteria — *security assurance* (does it preserve classical security if the PQC component fails?), *bandwidth cost* (handshake bytes added), *latency impact* (extra round trips under TCP slow start), *deployment friction* (middlebox behavior, fragmentation), and *agility* (how easily the algorithm set can change). We do not run new experiments; the deployed Internet is itself the experiment, and we cite its results.

```python
# Mosca's migration inequality: when must remediation begin?
def migration_urgency(confidentiality_years: int, migration_years: int, crqc_years: int) -> str:
    if confidentiality_years + migration_years > crqc_years:
        return "MIGRATE NOW: data lifetime outlives the quantum horizon"
    return "monitor"
# For CNSA 2.0 targets: x=30 (long-lived secrets), y=10 (migration), z=15 (est. CRQC)
print(migration_urgency(30, 10, 15))
```

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Module-LWE: The Lattice Foundation of ML-KEM and ML-DSA

Both FIPS 203 and FIPS 204 reduce their security to the *Module Learning With Errors* (Module-LWE) problem. Let *R_q = Z_q[x]/(x^n + 1)* be the cyclotomic ring with *n = 256* and *q = 3329*. In Module-LWE over module rank *k*, the adversary is given a random matrix **A** ∈ *R_q^{k×k}* and a vector **t** = **As** + **e**, where **s**, **e** are sampled from a centered binomial (small-norm) distribution, and must distinguish (**A**, **t**) from (**A**, **u**) with **u** uniform.

The geometry is what matters. Each sample defines a noisy linear equation; finding **s** is equivalent to finding the lattice point closest to **t** in a *q*-ary lattice — the *bounded-distance decoding* problem. The best known attacks are lattice reduction (BKZ with block size β) combined with sieving, whose cost grows as roughly *2^{0.292β}*. ML-KEM-768 targets NIST security category III (≈ AES-192 strength) with a public key of **1,184 bytes** and ciphertext of **1,088 bytes** — compact by post-quantum standards and the reason Kyber was selected as the primary KEM [5].

ML-DSA rests on the same Module-LWE assumption plus Module-SIS (short integer solution), using the Fiat–Shamir-with-aborts paradigm of Lyubashevsky: sample a masking vector **y**, compute the challenge *c = H(**Ay**, msg)*, respond **z** = **y** + *c***s**₁, and *abort* (resample) unless **z** is short and independent of the secret — a rejection-sampling step that is the scheme's signature subtlety and its main implementation pitfall. Parameter sets ML-DSA-44/65/87 give signatures of **2,420 / 3,309 / 4,627 bytes** respectively [10].

### 4.2 Hybrid Key Exchange in TLS 1.3: X25519 + ML-KEM

The central deployment insight of the migration is the *hybrid* construction: run a classical key exchange and a post-quantum KEM side by side, and combine their shared secrets so that the result is secure if *either* component is secure. The IETF draft specifies the concatenation approach [6]: the client's `KeyShareEntry` carries the concatenation of the X25519 ephemeral public key (32 bytes) and the ML-KEM public key (1,184 bytes for ML-KEM-768); the server responds with its X25519 share concatenated with the KEM ciphertext (1,088 bytes). Both shared secrets feed the TLS 1.3 key schedule, so breaking the handshake requires breaking *both* primitives.

Google's rollout is the canonical data point. After the CECPQ1/CECPQ2 experiments (X25519 combined with HRSS, an NTRU variant, and later with SIKE as CECPQ2b — the latter abandoned after SIKE's catastrophic classical break in 2022 [7]), Chrome 116 shipped X25519Kyber768 behind a flag, and Chrome 124 enabled it by default. Cloudflare's measurements of the earlier deployment showed essentially no user-visible latency regression for typical broadband users [8]. The lesson generalizes: hybrid key exchange adds roughly 2.2 KB to the handshake — large relative to 32-byte X25519 shares, but negligible relative to typical page weights.

> **Theorem: (Hybrid Security Composition).** *Let Π₁ be a key-exchange protocol secure under assumption A₁ and Π₂ a KEM secure under assumption A₂. The hybrid protocol that derives its session key via KDF(ss₁ ∥ ss₂) is secure if at least one of A₁, A₂ holds, provided the KDF is a dual-PRF. In particular, X25519+ML-KEM in TLS 1.3 is classically as secure as X25519 alone and quantum-secure if Module-LWE holds [6].*

Downgrade resistance follows from TLS 1.3's transcript hashing: the `supported_groups` extension negotiates the hybrid group, and the Finished messages authenticate the entire transcript, so a network adversary cannot strip the post-quantum component without detection [6].

### 4.3 KEMTLS: Authenticating Without Signatures

Post-quantum *signatures* are the painful half of the migration. An ML-DSA-65 signature (3,309 bytes) dwarfs an ECDSA signature (64 bytes); SLH-DSA's 7,856-byte signatures are worse still. Schwabe et al.'s **KEMTLS** (CCS 2020) asks a radical question: what if the handshake uses no signatures at all? [11]

The construction: the server's long-term identity key in its certificate is a *KEM* public key, not a signing key. The client encapsulates to it; only the holder of the corresponding private key can decapsulate and thus derive the handshake keys — an *implicit* authentication proof, replacing TLS 1.3's explicit handshake signature. Certificate authorities still sign certificates (there is no escaping signatures in the trust anchor), but the online handshake signature — the latency-critical, per-connection operation — disappears. The result: a Kyber512+Dilithium-II-equivalent handshake shrinks from ~15 KB to ~8.3 KB, and KEM operations are computationally far cheaper than lattice signing [11]. On embedded platforms (Cortex-M4), KEMTLS reduces handshake time by up to **38%**, lowers peak memory, and saves traffic volume [12].

The price is one additional round trip before the server can send *authenticated* application data, and slightly weaker downgrade resilience and forward secrecy during the handshake itself — both fully recovered once the handshake completes [11]. KEMTLS-PDK, a variant using pre-distributed public keys (pinned in apps or embedded devices), recovers the round trip and is strictly more bandwidth-efficient than signed-KEM TLS [13]. KEMTLS is not standardized and is unlikely to displace TLS 1.3 in the web PKI, but it is the strongest evidence that the signature-size problem has architectural — not merely incremental — solutions.

### 4.4 The Certificate Chain Bottleneck: Bloat, Fragmentation, and Slow Start

Authentication in the web PKI means certificates, and certificates carry public keys *and* signatures — both of which balloon under PQC. Consider a typical three-certificate chain (leaf, intermediate, root) signed with ML-DSA-44: each certificate contributes a ~1,312-byte public key and a ~2,420-byte signature, yielding chains of roughly **11–16 KB** including X.509 metadata and OCSP data [14]. Falcon-512 (future FN-DSA), with 897-byte public keys and ~666-byte signatures, compresses the same chain to ~6.5 KB — comfortably below critical thresholds [14].

The threshold that matters is the TCP initial congestion window (initcwnd, typically 10 segments ≈ 14.6 KB, often raised to 20). When the server's certificate flight exceeds initcwnd, the handshake cannot complete in a single round trip: the server must wait for ACKs before sending the remainder, adding a full RTT. Amazon's measurements show that a 16 KB ML-DSA chain takes nearly twice as long as an 8 KB chain at the 90th percentile on 1 Mbps links [15]. A dedicated arXiv study of PQC chain sizes on time-to-first-byte in CDN deployments confirms that certificate sizes grow 5–20× over classical and directly inflate RTT counts past MTU and flight-capacity thresholds [16]. Mitigations under active study include omitting intermediate CA certificates (cached by clients), Merkle-tree certificates (Chrome/Cloudflare experiments with the IETF PLANTS working group [17]), and algorithm mixing across chain levels — e.g., Dilithium for fast-signing leaves, Falcon for compact intermediates [14].

Worse than latency is *failure*. A field study across eight TLS stacks found that middleboxes — firewalls, load balancers, transparent proxies — that assumed handshake messages fit in a single TCP segment silently drop the enlarged post-quantum flights. A standard HAProxy TCP-passthrough configuration required a two-line fix to buffer complete handshakes; without it, hybrid connections failed 100% of the time, and clients with classical fallback silently *downgraded* — precisely the outcome the migration exists to prevent [18].

### 4.5 Threat Model, Timelines, and Crypto-Agility

The migration is driven by the HNDL adversary, but its schedule is set by bureaucracy. CNSA 2.0's phased deadlines — software/firmware signing by 2025–2030, network equipment by 2026–2030, browsers and cloud by 2025–2033, operating systems by 2027–2033 — create a demand signal that vendors cannot ignore: any NSS procurement after January 1, 2027 must be CNSA 2.0-compliant [2]. Notably, the v2.1 FAQ disqualifies pre-standard Kyber/Dilithium implementations: only final FIPS 203/204 conformance counts, forcing rebuilds of early prototypes [19].

> **Theorem: (Agility Necessity).** *No fixed algorithm suite can be assumed permanent against an adversary with a CRQC and an unbounded cryptanalytic future. Therefore any migration architecture must support algorithm replacement without protocol redesign — i.e., crypto-agility is not a feature but a precondition of the migration's success.*

Crypto-agility remains the open problem. The X.509, TLS, and CMS ecosystems encode algorithm identifiers in dozens of places; hybrid identifiers multiply the combinatorial surface (every classical × PQC pair needs a code point). The IETF's hybrid-design draft explicitly punts pure-PQC groups to future documents [6]. Composite certificates, algorithm-negotiation frameworks, and automated inventory tooling (cryptographic bill of materials) are active research areas, but no deployed PKI today can rotate its signature algorithm in less than years. The sobering corollary: the migration to ML-KEM/ML-DSA must itself be built to be migrated away from.

## 5. Empirical Results and Proofs

We consolidate the quantitative record from published large-scale measurements:

| Deployment / study | Configuration | Measured effect |
|---|---|---|
| Google Chrome 116→124 | X25519Kyber768 default-on | No user-visible latency regression on broadband; protects all TLS traffic against HNDL [7] |
| Cloudflare edge | X25519Kyber512/768Draft00, free default | Hybrid handshake negotiates at scale; security reduces to X25519 if Kyber breaks [8] |
| Amazon (TLS 1.3 latency study) | 16 KB ML-DSA chain vs 8 KB chain, 1 Mbps | 90th-percentile handshake time ~2×; small transfers (<10–20 KB) most affected [15] |
| Cisco TLS/SSH benchmarks | P256+NTRU-HRSS hybrid vs classical | Median handshake only 1–2 ms slower; ~13 KB extra data, extra TCP round trips under congestion control |
| KEMTLS on Cortex-M4 (WolfSSL) | KEMTLS vs PQ-TLS 1.3, LTE-M/NB-IoT | Handshake time −38%, lower peak memory, reduced traffic [12] |
| CDN TTFB study (arXiv) | PQC chain sizes, Zeek/NCSA data | Chain sizes 5–20× classical; MTU/flight-capacity exceedance inflates RTT and TTFB [16] |
| Apple iMessage PQ3 | Kyber + classical ECDH, periodic rekeying | Level-3 quantum resistance claim for messaging key exchange [9] |

The theoretical proofs underpinning deployment are equally settled for the standardized core: Module-LWE enjoys worst-case to average-case reductions from approximate shortest-vector problems on module lattices [5]; the TLS 1.3 hybrid construction is proven secure in the multi-stage key-exchange model with dual-PRF key derivation [6]; KEMTLS carries a standard-model proof [11]. The residual proof gap is *compositional*: the security of the full ecosystem — hybrid negotiation, composite certificates, fallback logic, middlebox behavior — has no unified formal model, and the silent-downgrade failure mode documented in [18] is precisely the kind of emergent behavior such a model would need to capture.

```rust
// Hybrid shared-secret derivation (draft-ietf-tls-hybrid-design, concatenation approach)
// client:  key_exchange = x25519_pk || mlkem_pk   (32 + 1184 bytes)
// server:  key_exchange = x25519_share || mlkem_ct (32 + 1088 bytes)
fn hybrid_handshake_secret(x25519_ss: &[u8; 32], mlkem_ss: &[u8; 32]) -> [u8; 32] {
    // Both inputs feed the TLS 1.3 key schedule; breaking the
    // handshake requires breaking BOTH components (dual-PRF KDF).
    let mut input = [0u8; 64];
    input[..32].copy_from_slice(x25519_ss);
    input[32..].copy_from_slice(mlkem_ss);
    hkdf_sha256_extract(&input) // handshake secret
}
```

## 6. Limitations

This analysis is bounded in several ways. **First**, the security estimates for lattice schemes rest on the concrete hardness of BKZ-β with sieving, whose cost models (the "core-SVP" methodology) are heuristic extrapolations, not theorems; the history of SIKE — selected for round 4 and then broken classically weeks later — counsels humility about all hardness assumptions [3]. **Second**, FN-DSA (FALCON) remains a draft standard; its floating-point Gaussian sampling is notoriously difficult to implement in constant time, and side-channel vulnerabilities in deployed Falcon implementations are a live concern [20]. **Third**, our performance synthesis inherits the limitations of its sources: most measurements use x86-64 servers and broadband networks, while the hardest deployment environments — NB-IoT radios, satellite links, 8-bit microcontrollers — are underrepresented. **Fourth**, we do not model the *economic* cost of migration: certificate re-issuance at web scale, HSM firmware upgrades, and FIPS 140-3 re-validation of every module touching the new algorithms represent a bill measured in the tens of billions of dollars, and cost is the binding constraint on the CNSA 2.0 schedule, not mathematics. **Fifth**, the pure-PQC end state (no hybrids) has no finalized TLS code points and no deployment experience; every production system today is hybrid, and the security and operational properties of the eventual pure-PQC Internet are extrapolated, not observed.

## 7. Conclusion

The post-quantum migration is the largest cryptographic transition ever attempted: replacing the asymmetric primitives underlying TLS, SSH, IPsec, S/MIME, code signing, and the web PKI within roughly a decade. The standards are done — FIPS 203, 204, and 205 finalized in August 2024, FIPS 206 in draft, HQC selected as the code-based hedge — and the deployment machinery is moving: hybrid X25519+ML-KEM key exchange is default-on in Chrome, free and default at Cloudflare, and rolling out across Google Cloud; CNSA 2.0 gives the transition legal teeth with procurement deadlines beginning January 2027.

Three findings emerge from the evidence. **(1) Key exchange is solved as an engineering problem.** Hybrid concatenation adds ~2.2 KB and negligible latency; the dual-PRF composition theorem guarantees no regression in classical security. **(2) Authentication is the bottleneck.** Post-quantum signatures inflate certificate chains past TCP congestion-window thresholds, adding round trips and — through middlebox fragility — silent downgrade failures; KEMTLS shows that architectural alternatives exist, but the web PKI will live with large signatures for years. **(3) Agility is the real deliverable.** The migration's success should be measured not by whether ML-KEM survives — it may not, and SIKE's fate proves that — but by whether the infrastructure built to deploy it can deploy its successor. A migration architecture that cannot itself be migrated is a bridge to nowhere.

The harvest-now-decrypt-later adversary does not wait for standards bodies. Every TLS session recorded today with classical key exchange is a liability against the quantum horizon. The migration has begun; the physics of TCP congestion windows and the politics of procurement timelines will decide whether it finishes in time.
