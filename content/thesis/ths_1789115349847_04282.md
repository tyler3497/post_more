# Quantum Machine Learning: Quantum Kernels, Barren Plateaus, Dequantization, and the Debate over Quantum Advantage

## Abstract

Quantum machine learning (QML) promises to harness exponentially large Hilbert spaces for learning tasks that resist classical computation, yet the field remains divided over where — and whether — genuine quantum advantage exists. This thesis presents a unified analysis of four pillars of the debate: (i) quantum kernel methods, which embed data in quantum feature spaces and estimate inner products inaccessible to classical machines; (ii) barren plateaus, the exponentially vanishing gradients that render many parameterized quantum circuits untrainable at scale; (iii) dequantization, Ewin Tang's program of quantum-inspired classical algorithms that collapsed several claimed exponential speedups; and (iv) provable separations, where carefully constructed learning tasks admit rigorous quantum advantages under standard cryptographic assumptions. Synthesizing results from Havlíček et al. [2], McClean et al. [3], Cerezo et al. [4], Tang [5], Liu, Arunachalam, and Temme [6], Huang et al. [7], and Schuld [1], we argue that QML's advantage is real but narrow: it survives only where data encoding is classically hard, cost functions are local, and noise is tamed.

## 1 Introduction

Machine learning and quantum computing are the two most capital-intensive technologies in contemporary computer science, and their intersection has generated a literature that is simultaneously exuberant and uneasy. The exuberance is easy to understand: an *n*-qubit quantum computer manipulates states in a 2^n-dimensional Hilbert space, offering an exponentially large feature space essentially for free. The unease is empirical: despite more than a decade of proposals, no broadly accepted demonstration of quantum advantage on a real-world learning task has survived scrutiny [1][7].

This thesis argues that the confusion is not merely sociological but structural: three distinct phenomena pull the field in opposing directions. **Quantum kernels** and related feature-map methods supply the most credible route to advantage, because they translate the entire expressive power of a quantum device into a single, well-understood mathematical object: the kernel function [1][2]. **Barren plateaus** supply the most serious obstacle to the dominant alternative, variational training of parameterized quantum circuits, by proving that gradients vanish exponentially in the number of qubits for wide classes of ansätze [3][4]. **Dequantization** supplies the historical corrective: a series of results showing that several celebrated quantum machine learning algorithms were never faster than classical algorithms at all, once the right classical data-access model is adopted [5].

Our contribution is to organize these threads into a single framework and identify the precise conditions under which each applies: Schuld's unification theorem [1] collapses the zoo of variational models onto kernel methods; the barren-plateau literature converges on a locality principle [3][4]; dequantization's reach is bounded by its data-access assumptions [5]; and provable advantage exists but lives in cryptographic neighborhoods far from practical data [6][7].

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Parameterized Quantum Circuits and Variational Learning

A **parameterized quantum circuit** (PQC) is a unitary V(**θ**) composed of fixed gates and gates whose rotation angles **θ** = (θ_1, …, θ_M) are tunable. A variational algorithm prepares the state |ψ(**θ**)⟩ = V(**θ**)|0⟩, estimates an expectation value C(**θ**) = ⟨ψ(**θ**)|O|ψ(**θ**)⟩ of some observable O, and uses a classical optimizer to minimize C. This hybrid loop underlies the variational quantum eigensolver (VQE), the quantum approximate optimization algorithm (QAOA), and most near-term quantum neural networks (QNNs) [3].

The *hardware-efficient ansatz* popularized in the NISQ era alternates layers of single-qubit rotations with fixed entangling gates:

> **Definition (Hardware-efficient ansatz).** An *L*-layer ansatz on *n* qubits has the form V(**θ**) = ∏_{ℓ=1}^{L} W_ℓ · ∏_{ℓ=1}^{L} ⊗_{j=1}^{n} R_{σ_j}(θ_{ℓ,j}), where W_ℓ are fixed entangling unitaries (e.g., ladders of CNOT gates) and R_{σ} are Pauli rotations with angles θ_{ℓ,j}.

Training proceeds by gradient estimation — analytically via the **parameter-shift rule** [1], or numerically — and gradient descent on **θ**.

### 2.2 Kernel Methods and Quantum Feature Maps

Classical kernel methods avoid explicit feature vectors by working only with inner products k(x, x′) = ⟨φ(x), φ(x′)⟩ in a high-dimensional feature space. The **quantum feature map** replaces φ with a data-encoding circuit:

k(x, x′) = |⟨φ(x)|φ(x′)⟩|²,   |φ(x)⟩ = U(x)|0⟩^⊗n

The kernel is estimated on quantum hardware by measuring the overlap of two prepared states — for example, via the **Loschmidt echo** circuit U†(x′)U(x)|0⟩ and reading the all-zero probability, or via a SWAP test. A classical support vector machine then consumes the kernel Gram matrix K_{ij} = k(x_i, x_j) and trains a max-margin classifier [2].

Havlíček et al. demonstrated this pipeline experimentally on a superconducting processor, using a **ZZ feature map** — single-qubit rotations encoding individual features interleaved with entangling gates encoding pairwise products — and achieving competitive accuracy on synthetic datasets [2]. Their work also introduced the *variational classifier*, which classifies directly from a trained PQC, and the *quantum kernel estimator*, which separates kernel estimation from convex optimization.

### 2.3 The Trainability Question

Two questions dominate the theory of QML: **expressivity** (can the model represent the target function? PQCs with data re-uploading are universal approximators with Fourier spectra growing with encoding depth [1]) and **trainability** (can optimization find the parameters? Often no — see barren plateaus). Expressivity without trainability is vacuous: a model class that contains the answer but cannot be navigated is useless in practice.

## 3 Methodology

This thesis is a synthesis and comparative analysis rather than an experimental report. Our methodology has three components.

**Analytical unification.** We adopt Schuld's framework [1], which shows that supervised quantum models — variational classifiers, quantum neural networks, and quantum kernel methods — are instances of a single mathematical structure: linear models in the Hilbert space of density matrices, trained either variationally or by convex kernel optimization. This lets us compare trainability and advantage claims on common ground.

**Scaling analysis.** For each phenomenon we extract the asymptotic scaling law in the number of qubits *n*, the depth *L*, and the dataset size *M*:

| Phenomenon | Key scaling law | Regime of validity |
|---|---|---|
| Barren plateau (random ansatz) | Var[∂_k C] ∈ O(2^−n) | circuits forming 2-designs [3] |
| Local-cost trainability | Var[∂_k C] ∈ Ω(1/poly(n)) | depth L ∈ O(log n), local observables [4] |
| Quantum kernel estimation | O(M²/ε²) circuit evaluations | additive error ε per entry [2][6] |
| Dequantized recommendation | O(poly(k) polylog(mn)) | SQ access, rank-k matrix [5] |
| Discrete-log separation | exp. gap, classical ≈ random guessing | fault-tolerant quantum access [6] |

**Evidence hierarchy.** We rank claims by rigor: *provable separations* (conditional on standard hardness assumptions) outrank *complexity-theoretic arguments*, which outrank *heuristic benchmarks*, which outrank *small-qubit hardware demonstrations*. Much of the field's confusion comes from comparing evidence across levels without acknowledging the shift.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Quantum Kernels and the Feature-Space Program

The kernel perspective is QML's most disciplined idea. Schuld's central result [1] is a representation theorem: any supervised quantum model that encodes data via a fixed unitary U(x), applies a trainable unitary V(**θ**), and measures an observable M produces a decision function of the form

f(x) = Tr[ρ(x) Γ(**θ**)],   ρ(x) = U(x)|0⟩⟨0|U†(x),

which is linear in the data-dependent state ρ(x). Optimizing **θ** variationally therefore searches a subset of the function class accessible to the *quantum kernel method*, which optimizes over the full reproducing kernel Hilbert space via the representer theorem. Consequences follow immediately:

- **Convexity:** kernel training is a convex quadratic program with a global optimum; variational training is non-convex and can stall [1].
- **Encoding primacy:** because the kernel depends only on the feature map x ↦ ρ(x), *the data encoding — not the trainable ansatz — is the ingredient that can separate quantum models from classical ones* [1][7].
- **Fidelity vs. projected kernels:** the fidelity kernel k(x,x′) = Tr[ρ(x)ρ(x′)] can suffer **exponential concentration** toward a fixed value as *n* grows, washing out all data dependence; projected quantum kernels, which first reduce ρ(x) to local subsystems, provably resist this concentration [7].

> **Theorem (Quantum models as kernel methods, informal [1]).** For every variational quantum classifier with fixed data encoding there exists a kernel-based classifier using the kernel k(x, x′) = Tr[ρ(x)ρ(x′)] whose training optimum is at least as good. Hence any advantage of variational models over kernel methods is impossible; advantage must come from the encoding itself.

Huang et al. made this encoding-centric view quantitative through the **geometric difference** between quantum and classical kernels, showing that quantum advantage in learning requires the label function to have large norm in the quantum RKHS while being nearly orthogonal to the classical one — and crucially, that this advantage *persists* only when data or encodings are themselves classically inaccessible [7]. Their work explains why quantum kernels excel on engineered datasets (e.g., those built from classically hard functions) yet struggle on standard benchmarks: on "classical" data, a well-chosen classical kernel already spans the needed function space.

A minimal implementation of fidelity-kernel estimation illustrates the pipeline:

```python
import numpy as np

def zz_feature_map(x, reps=2):
    """Return unitary U(x) as matrix for n=2 ZZ feature map (toy)."""
    n = len(x)
    U = np.eye(2 ** n, dtype=complex)
    for _ in range(reps):
        for i, xi in enumerate(x):            # single-qubit rotations
            U = rot_z(xi, i, n) @ U
        for i in range(n - 1):                # pairwise ZZ entanglement
            U = zz_gate(x[i] * x[i + 1], i, i + 1, n) @ U
    return U

def fidelity_kernel(X, reps=2):
    """Gram matrix K_ij = |<0|U(x_j)^dagger U(x_i)|0>|^2."""
    Us = [zz_feature_map(x, reps) for x in X]
    M = len(X)
    K = np.zeros((M, M))
    for i in range(M):
        for j in range(i, M):
            amp = (Us[j].conj().T @ Us[i])[0, 0]
            K[i, j] = K[j, i] = abs(amp) ** 2
    return K
```

On hardware, each entry costs O(1/ε²) shots; the M² entries dominate the budget, motivating **Nyström** and **random-feature** approximations.

### 4.2 Barren Plateaus: Vanishing Gradients and Cost-Function Locality

If kernels are QML's most disciplined idea, barren plateaus are its most sobering theorem. McClean et al. proved that for PQCs whose blocks approximate unitary **2-designs** — essentially, random circuits — the gradient of a typical cost function concentrates around zero [3]:

> **Theorem (Barren plateaus [3]).** Let C(**θ**) = Tr[O V(**θ**)|0⟩⟨0|V†(**θ**)] with V a layered ansatz whose blocks form 2-designs. Then 𝔼[∂_k C] = 0 and Var[∂_k C] ∈ O(2^−n). Distinguishing the gradient direction from statistical noise requires measurement precision exponential in *n*.

Intuition: random circuits scramble information across the full Hilbert space (a manifestation of **concentration of measure**, closely related to Levy's lemma), so any fixed direction in parameter space perturbs the cost by an exponentially small amount. The result is independent of the optimizer — gradient descent, natural gradients, and gradient-free methods all face the same flat landscape.

Cerezo et al. refined the picture by showing that trainability depends on the **cost function**, not just the ansatz [4]:

- **Global costs** (e.g., fidelity with a target state, or ⟨Z^⊗n⟩) produce barren plateaus *even for shallow circuits* of constant depth.
- **Local costs** (sums of few-body observables) yield gradients that vanish at worst polynomially, provided the depth is O(log n).

> **Theorem (Cost-function-dependent plateaus [4]).** For an alternating layered ansatz of depth O(log n) with local 2-design blocks, Var[∂_k C] ∈ Ω(1/poly(n)) when C is a local observable, while Var[∂_k C] ∈ O(2^−n) when C is global — regardless of depth.

Additional mechanisms compound the problem: **noise-induced barren plateaus**, where uncorrected hardware noise flattens the landscape exponentially in depth even for local costs [4]; **entanglement-induced plateaus**, where excessive entanglement between visible and hidden subsystems hides gradients; and **expressibility-induced plateaus**, where highly expressive ansätze are precisely those that form 2-designs. Mitigations form an active subfield:

1. **Local cost functions** with shallow, O(log n)-depth ansätze [4].
2. **Structured ansätze** with inductive bias — e.g., quantum convolutional neural networks provably avoid plateaus under random initialization.
3. **Smart initialization**: identity-block or layerwise strategies that begin training in a non-scrambled region.
4. **Parameter correlation** and reduced effective dimension, trading expressivity for gradient signal.
5. **Kernel methods themselves**, which sidestep variational optimization entirely [1].

### 4.3 Dequantization and the Quantum-Inspired Counteroffensive

The most dramatic reversals in QML's history came not from hardware but from classical algorithms. In 2018, Ewin Tang **dequantized** the quantum recommendation-system algorithm of Kerenidis and Prakash — one of the flagship examples of exponential quantum speedup in machine learning — by exhibiting a classical algorithm with matching polylogarithmic runtime [5]:

> **Theorem (Dequantized recommendation [5]).** Given sample-and-query (SQ) access to a preference matrix A ∈ ℝ^{m×n} of rank k, there exists a classical algorithm producing recommendations in O(poly(k) polylog(m, n)) time, matching the quantum algorithm up to polynomial factors.

The mechanism is the **ℓ²-norm sampling** primitive: where the quantum algorithm assumes the ability to prepare the quantum state |A⟩ ∝ Σ_{ij} A_{ij}|i⟩|j⟩ in polylogarithmic time (via QRAM-like oracles), Tang assumed the classical ability to sample rows with probability proportional to their squared norms and query individual entries — and showed this suffices. A wave of follow-ups dequantized quantum PCA, quantum clustering, and portions of the quantum singular-value-transformation toolkit applied to low-rank linear algebra [5].

Three lessons deserve emphasis:

- **Dequantization is an oracle-relative statement.** It compares quantum state-preparation access against classical SQ access. If SQ access is unrealistically strong for a given application — and in practice, building the required sampling data structures costs O(nnz(A)) preprocessing — the comparison flatters the classical side.
- **Low rank is load-bearing.** All dequantization results to date assume the input matrix is (approximately) low-rank. Quantum algorithms retain provable advantages for high-rank or full-rank linear algebra under standard input models.
- **The damage was to specific claims, not to the field.** Dequantization refuted the proposition that *these particular* quantum ML algorithms were exponentially faster. It did not touch kernel methods on classically hard feature maps [2][6], nor separations based on cryptographic hardness [6].

Fair accounting therefore treats dequantization as a *discipline device*: any claimed quantum ML speedup must now specify its input model, and "quantum-inspired" classical algorithms are a standing control experiment against which new quantum proposals must be tested.

### 4.4 Provable Advantage: Discrete-Logarithm Separations and the Role of Data

Against this skeptical backdrop, Liu, Arunachalam, and Temme constructed the first *rigorous* quantum speedup for supervised learning with purely classical data access [6]. The construction is cryptographic: data points are group elements, and labels are computed from discrete logarithms.

> **Theorem (Rigorous quantum speedup [6]).** There exists a family of classification tasks such that (a) a quantum kernel method using a fault-tolerant quantum computer achieves high accuracy with polynomial resources, while (b) no classical learner achieves accuracy better than random guessing by more than an inverse-polynomial margin, assuming the classical hardness of the discrete logarithm problem. The separation is robust to additive errors in kernel estimation.

The quantum kernel encodes the group structure via a feature map whose overlaps are computable only with Shor-like subroutines; the classical learner sees what is effectively noise. Huang et al. generalized the lesson: quantum advantage in learning is fundamentally about **data** — whether the data-generating process or the optimal feature map is classically intractable [7]. On data produced by classical processes and encoded by classical means, quantum models enjoy no generic advantage; on data with quantum origins (quantum simulation outputs, quantum sensor readings) or cryptographic structure, advantage is provable.

This yields a practitioner checklist: (1) is the encoding circuit classically simulable? If yes, a classical surrogate likely matches the quantum model [7]; (2) is the cost local and the ansatz shallow? If no, expect plateaus [3][4]; (3) does a dequantized classical algorithm exist under realistic data access? If yes, the quantum proposal adds nothing [5]; (4) does the task embed a classically hard function? Only then is provable advantage on the table [6].

## 5 Empirical Results and Formal Guarantees

We now consolidate the quantitative evidence across the four pillars.

**Barren plateaus, numerically.** Cerezo et al. simulated quantum autoencoders up to *n* = 100 qubits, confirming the theory: global-cost gradient variances decay as ≈ 2^−n across all depths, while local-cost variances decay polynomially for O(log n)-depth circuits [4]. The crossover is sharp — replacing a global observable with a local one at fixed ansatz depth restores trainability by orders of magnitude.

**Kernel experiments.** Havlíček et al.'s two-qubit superconducting experiments achieved near-perfect classification on engineered datasets with both the variational classifier and the kernel estimator, with the kernel estimator exhibiting superior noise robustness because convex optimization tolerates kernel-entry errors [2]. Subsequent studies on up to 20+ qubits confirm that kernel-target alignment predicts generalization, and that **bandwidth tuning** of the feature map controls the expressivity–generalization tradeoff [7].

**Dequantization benchmarks.** Implementations of Tang's algorithm confirm the polylogarithmic scaling in matrix dimensions for fixed rank; the practical crossover where the quantum-inspired method beats dense SVD occurs only at enormous sizes, underscoring that dequantization is primarily a *complexity-theoretic* victory [5].

**Provable separation, concretely.** Liu et al.'s discrete-logarithm construction requires a fault-tolerant quantum computer and polynomially many kernel evaluations, each robust to O(1/poly(n)) additive error — errors that finite sampling naturally satisfies [6]. No classical algorithm can do better than guessing under the DLP assumption, a gap far stronger than any heuristic benchmark.

| Claim | Evidence level | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| Quantum kernels beat classical kernels generically | heuristic benchmarks | **False** — only on classically hard encodings [7] |
| Variational QNNs train at scale with random init | theory + numerics | **False** — barren plateaus [3][4] |
| Quantum recommendation was exponentially faster | dequantization | **False** — matched classically [5] |
| Quantum advantage in supervised learning exists | provable separation | **True** — conditional on DLP hardness [6] |
| Data encoding determines advantage | theory | **True** — Schuld [1], Huang et al. [7] |

## 6 Limitations

This analysis has boundaries that must be stated plainly.

**Fault tolerance gap.** The only rigorous separations [6] assume fault-tolerant quantum computers. All near-term QML — kernels on NISQ devices, variational classifiers — remains heuristic, and noise-induced barren plateaus [4] may erase even heuristic advantages before error correction arrives.

**Kernel concentration.** Fidelity quantum kernels concentrate exponentially with qubit count for typical global encodings, rendering the Gram matrix near-identity and the model untrainable — a kernel analogue of barren plateaus. Projected kernels and bandwidth tuning mitigate this but reintroduce classical simulability concerns: a kernel tame enough to avoid concentration may be tame enough to simulate [7].

**Dequantization's moving target.** New quantum algorithms invite new dequantizations. The SQ-access model debate is unresolved: critics argue it grants classical algorithms oracular powers no real database provides, while defenders note quantum state preparation is equally oracular. Advantage claims must therefore be *input-model explicit* — a standard the field still applies inconsistently.

**Data bottleneck.** Quantum advantage requires classically hard data or encodings [7], but most commercially valuable datasets are classical and low-dimensional. Quantum-native data (from quantum simulation or sensing) is the natural habitat, yet that literature is comparatively thin, and our supervised-learning conclusions do not automatically transfer to unsupervised, generative, or reinforcement settings.

---

## 7 Conclusion

Quantum machine learning is neither the revolution its early literature promised nor the mirage its critics allege. It is a field whose central questions have, remarkably, been answered — in the negative for generic claims, in the affirmative for carefully scoped ones.

The answers, in brief: **quantum kernels** provide the right mathematical frame, and Schuld's unification [1] shows the encoding is everything; **barren plateaus** [3][4] prove generic variational training cannot scale, with cost-function locality as the decisive variable; **dequantization** [5] proved several flagship speedups were artifacts of generous quantum input models; and **provable separations** [6][7] establish that quantum advantage in learning is real — but lives where classical computation cannot follow.

The research program this implies is narrower and healthier than the one it replaces: design encodings that are classically hard yet physically realizable; train with local costs or convex kernel methods rather than deep variational circuits; benchmark every quantum proposal against its dequantized classical twin; and target data with quantum origins. Pursued this way, QML's advantage is not a matter of faith but of construction — conditional, provable, and increasingly within reach of fault-tolerant hardware.
