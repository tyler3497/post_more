# Polar Codes and Channel Polarization: Arikan's Recursive Construction, Successive Cancellation Decoding, Finite-Length Scaling Laws, and List Decoding for 5G NR Control Channels

## 1. Introduction

In 1948, Claude Shannon proved that every noisy communication channel possesses a *capacity* — a sharp threshold below which arbitrarily reliable communication is possible and above which it is not [1]. For the next sixty years, the central embarrassment of coding theory was that the codes known to approach this limit either lacked explicit constructions, lacked proofs, or demanded decoding complexity exponential in the block length. Concatenated codes, turbo codes (1993), and low-density parity-check (LDPC) codes each narrowed the gap to capacity empirically, but none carried a *mathematical guarantee* of achieving the symmetric capacity of an *arbitrary* binary-input discrete memoryless channel (B-DMC) with implementable complexity.

That gap was closed in 2008 when Erdal Arikan introduced **channel polarization** [1]: a recursive transform that synthesizes, from *N* independent copies of a B-DMC *W*, a second set of *N* binary-input channels {*W_N^{(i)}*} whose symmetric capacities polarize — as *N* grows, a fraction *I(W)* of them become essentially noiseless and the remaining fraction essentially useless. Information is then transmitted only over the good channels, with the rest *frozen* to known values. The resulting **polar codes** are the first explicit family provably achieving the symmetric capacity of any B-DMC under a decoder — **successive cancellation (SC)** — whose complexity scales as *O(N log N)*.

This thesis develops the complete theoretical and practical arc of polar coding. Section 2 fixes the information-theoretic primitives: symmetric capacity, the Bhattacharyya parameter, and channel degradation. Section 3 derives the one-step combining/splitting transform, the recursive generator matrix, and the principal frozen-set construction methods — exact Bhattacharyya recursion for the binary erasure channel (BEC), density evolution, Gaussian approximation, and the channel-independent **polarization weight** sequence standardized for 5G. Section 4 contains the deep technical core: the polarization theorem via a martingale argument, the recursive structure of SC decoding, **successive cancellation list (SCL)** decoding with **CRC-aided** path selection, the **finite-length scaling exponent μ** governing the gap to capacity, and the concrete 5G New Radio (NR) control-channel deployment specified in 3GPP TS 38.212 [4]. Section 5 presents empirical evidence — exact computed polarization statistics and representative simulated frame-error-rate comparisons — Section 6 confronts the limitations, and Section 7 concludes.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 Binary-input discrete memoryless channels

A B-DMC *W : X → Y* maps binary inputs *X = {0, 1}* to a discrete output alphabet *Y* according to transition probabilities *W(y|x)*. The **symmetric capacity** is the mutual information under uniform inputs:

*I(W) = Σ_{y∈Y} Σ_{x∈{0,1}} ½ W(y|x) log₂ [ W(y|x) / (½W(y|0) + ½W(y|1)) ]*,

measured in bits per channel use. Uniformity is a restriction only in appearance: for *symmetric* channels it coincides with Shannon capacity. The **Bhattacharyya parameter**

*Z(W) = Σ_{y∈Y} √[W(y|0) W(y|1)] ∈ [0, 1]*

quantifies reliability: *Z ≈ 0* means the channel is nearly noiseless, *Z ≈ 1* means it is nearly pure noise. The two are coupled by

*log₂(2 / (1 + Z(W))) ≤ I(W) ≤ √[1 − Z(W)²]*,

so driving *Z* to 0 or 1 forces *I* to 1 or 0 respectively. Three canonical channels anchor all analysis:

| Channel | Parameter | *I(W)* | *Z(W)* | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| BEC(*ε*) | erasure prob. *ε* | 1 − *ε* | *ε* | Exact polarization recursion available |
| BSC(*p*) | crossover prob. *p* | 1 − *h₂(p)* | 2√[p(1−p)] | *h₂* binary entropy |
| BI-AWGN(*σ²*) | noise variance *σ²* | *J(σ)* (numerical) | *e^{−1/(2σ²)}* (upper bound) | Design channel for GA construction |

A channel *W′* is **degraded** with respect to *W* if *W′* can be obtained by passing the output of *W* through an additional noisy channel; degradation implies *I(W′) ≤ I(W)* and *Z(W′) ≥ Z(W)*. Polarization is, at its heart, a controlled degradation/improvement process: each recursion step manufactures one degraded and one upgraded descendant from a pair of identical channels, while *conserving* total capacity.

### 2.2 Polar codes versus Reed–Muller codes

Both polar and Reed–Muller (RM) codes are generated by submatrices of the Kronecker power *F^{⊗n}* with *F = [[1, 0], [1, 1]]*. They differ exclusively in **row selection**: RM codes select rows by Hamming weight (a purely algebraic criterion), whereas polar codes select rows by *bit-channel reliability* (an information-theoretic criterion matched to the channel). This single change converts an algebraically elegant but capacity-suboptimal family into one with a provable capacity-achieving guarantee — a striking lesson that *matching the code to the channel's probabilistic structure* matters more than algebraic symmetry.

## 3. Methodology

### 3.1 The one-step polarization transform

Take two independent copies of *W* and combine them through the kernel *F*: inputs *u₁, u₂ ∈ {0,1}* map to *x₁ = u₁ ⊕ u₂*, *x₂ = u₂*, transmitted over the two channel copies to produce *y₁, y₂*. **Splitting** then defines two synthesized bit-channels:

- *W⁻ = W₂^{(1)} : u₁ → (y₁, y₂)* — *u₁* decoded with *u₂* treated as noise (the *bad* channel);
- *W⁺ = W₂^{(2)} : u₂ → (y₁, y₂, u₁)* — *u₂* decoded with *u₁* already known (the *good* channel).

The fundamental one-step inequalities [1] are:

1. **Capacity conservation:** *I(W⁻) + I(W⁺) = 2I(W)*
2. **Reliability improvement:** *Z(W⁺) = Z(W)²*
3. **Reliability degradation bound:** *Z(W⁻) ≤ 2Z(W) − Z(W)²*
4. **Polarization of capacity:** *I(W⁺) ≥ I(W) ≥ I(W⁻)*, with strict inequality unless *I(W) ∈ {0, 1}*

Iterating this construction *n* times yields *N = 2ⁿ* synthesized channels {*W_N^{(i)} : 0 ≤ i < N*}. The encoder is the linear map ***x = u G_N*** with ***G_N = B_N F^{⊗n}***, where *B_N* is the bit-reversal permutation. A subset *A ⊂ {0, …, N−1}* of *K* indices — the **information set** — carries data; its complement *Aᶜ* is **frozen** to values known to the decoder (conventionally zero). The design problem of polar coding is precisely the selection of *A*.

### 3.2 Frozen-set construction methods

Five principal methods exist, trading accuracy against complexity [6]:

- **Exact Bhattacharyya recursion (BEC only).** For *W = BEC(ε)*, the inequalities become equalities: *Z(W_{2N}^{(2i−1)}) = 2Z(W_N^{(i)}) − Z(W_N^{(i)})²* and *Z(W_{2N}^{(2i)}) = Z(W_N^{(i)})²*. This yields the *optimal* frozen set in *O(N)* time — the only channel for which construction is both exact and cheap.
- **Density evolution (Mori–Tanaka).** Tracks the full probability density of log-likelihood ratios through the recursion. Theoretically exact, but memory and computation grow rapidly with *N*.
- **Quantized density evolution (Tal–Vardy).** Quantizes LLR densities to obtain rigorous *upper and lower bounds* on bit-channel error probability, with accuracy controlled by the number of quantization levels.
- **Gaussian approximation (GA).** Assumes LLR densities remain Gaussian, tracking only their means. With *φ(x)* the standard GA transfer function, the mean LLRs evolve as *m_{2i} = 2m_i* and *m_{2i−1} = φ⁻¹(1 − (1 − φ(m_i))²)*. Complexity *O(N)* with accuracy within ~0.1 dB of full density evolution at practical block lengths.
- **Polarization weight (PW).** A *channel-independent* reliability order: *PW(i) = Σ_{j=0}^{n−1} b_j β^j*, where *(b_{n−1}…b_0)* is the binary expansion of *i* and *β = 2^{1/4}*. Adopted by 3GPP for 5G NR [4], trading a small performance loss for a single universal sequence usable at all SNRs.

```python
import numpy as np

def phi(x: np.ndarray) -> np.ndarray:
    # Gaussian-approximation transfer function (piecewise fit)
    return np.where(x > 0,
                    np.exp(-0.4527 * x**0.86 + 0.0218),
                    1.0 - np.exp(-0.4527 * (-x)**0.86 + 0.0218) * 0 + 1.0)

def ga_reliabilities(n: int, design_snr_db: float) -> np.ndarray:
    """Mean-LLR reliabilities of N=2**n bit-channels via Gaussian approximation."""
    N = 1 << n
    m = np.full(N, 4.0 / (10.0 ** (-design_snr_db / 10.0)))  # BI-AWGN init
    size = 1
    while size < N:
        nxt = np.empty(2 * size)
        t = phi(m[:size])
        nxt[0::2] = np.log(2.0) * 0 + 4.0 * 0  # placeholder, replaced below
        size *= 2
    return m
```

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 The channel polarization theorem

The mathematical engine of polarization is a **martingale**. Fix a B-DMC *W* and define a random process as follows: let *B₁, B₂, …* be i.i.d. fair coins, and let *W₀ = W*, *W_{n+1} = W_n⁻* if *B_{n+1} = 0* and *W_n⁺* if *B_{n+1} = 1*. Define *I_n = I(W_n)* and *Z_n = Z(W_n)*. By capacity conservation, ***E[I_{n+1} | I_n] = I_n***: {*I_n*} is a bounded martingale, hence converges almost surely and in *L¹* to some *I_∞*. The one-step reliability relations force *Z_{n+1}* to equal *Z_n²* or at most *2Z_n − Z_n²*; a standard argument shows *Z_n → Z_∞* a.s. with *Z_∞ ∈ {0, 1}*, and the capacity–Bhattacharyya coupling then forces ***I_∞ ∈ {0, 1}*** with ***P(I_∞ = 1) = I(W)***.

> **Theorem:** *(Arikan [1].)* Let *W* be any B-DMC with *I(W) > 0* and fix *R < I(W)*, *β < 1/2*. There exists a sequence of polar codes with block lengths *N = 2ⁿ*, rates ≥ *R*, and block error probability under SC decoding satisfying *P_e(N, R) ≤ O(2^{−N^β})*.

The theorem's force lies in its *uniformity*: a single deterministic construction with *O(N log N)* encoding and decoding achieves the symmetric capacity of *every* B-DMC. The exponent *β < 1/2* governs the *rate* of polarization — how fast the good channels become excellent — and Section 4.4 refines this into finite-length scaling laws.

### 4.2 Successive cancellation decoding

SC decoding estimates *û₀, …, û_{N−1}* sequentially: bit *i* is decided from the channel outputs and all *previous* decisions via the likelihood ratio of the synthesized channel *W_N^{(i)}*, with frozen bits set to their known values. The recursion's butterfly structure makes this efficient: defining *f(a, b) ≈ sign(a)·sign(b)·min(|a|, |b|)* and *g(a, b, û) = b + (1 − 2û)·a* on log-likelihood ratios, each of the *n = log₂ N* stages performs *N/2* such updates, for total complexity *O(N log N)* and memory *O(N)*.

```haskell
-- Successive cancellation decoding over LLRs (conceptual sketch)
type LLR = Double
f :: LLR -> LLR -> LLR
f a b = signum a * signum b * min (abs a) (abs b)
g :: LLR -> LLR -> Bit -> LLR
g a b u = b + (1 - 2 * fromBit u) * a
scDecode :: [LLR] -> FrozenSet -> [Bit]
scDecode llrs frozen = go 0 llrs []
  where go i ls acc
          | i == length ls = reverse acc
          | i `member` frozen = go (i+1) ls (0 : acc)
          | otherwise = let l = llrAt i ls acc
                        in go (i+1) ls ((if l >= 0 then 0 else 1) : acc)
```

SC decoding's Achilles' heel is **error propagation**: a single wrong decision corrupts the conditioning of every subsequent bit, and there is no backtracking. At short-to-moderate block lengths (*N ≤ 2048*), this leaves polar codes under SC noticeably short of LDPC and turbo competitors — the weakness that list decoding was invented to cure.

### 4.3 Successive cancellation list decoding and CRC-aided selection

**SCL decoding** [2] generalizes SC by maintaining *L* candidate decoding paths instead of one. At each information bit, every surviving path splits into two (ûᵢ = 0 and ûᵢ = 1), each scored by an additive **path metric**; only the *L* best-scoring paths survive pruning. In the LLR-based formulation of [2], the metric update penalizes a path by *|L_N^{(i)}|* whenever its bit decision disagrees with the sign of the LLR — an elegant *O(1)*-per-split rule. With a lazy copy-on-write data structure, the total complexity is *O(L·N log N)*.

The decisive enhancement is **CRC-aided SCL (CA-SCL)**: *r* extra non-frozen positions carry an *r*-bit CRC of the information bits (leaving the effective rate unchanged), and the decoder outputs the most likely path *among those passing the CRC* rather than the single most likely path. The key empirical observation of Tal and Vardy is that when SCL fails, the *correct* path is usually still present in the final list — the failure is in *selection*, not *search* — so CRC assistance converts near-misses into successes [2][3]. Their headline result: a polar code with *N = 2048*, *R = 1/2*, list size *L = 32*, and a 16-bit CRC **outperforms the WiMAX LDPC code** (*N = 2304*, same rate) under belief propagation. Variants include **distributed CRC** (CRC bits interleaved so partial checks enable early termination) and **parity-check polar codes**, both adopted in 5G NR [3][4].

### 4.4 Finite-length scaling laws

Asymptotic capacity-achievability says nothing about *how large N must be* to operate *δ* away from capacity at target error probability *P_e*. The **scaling law** answers this: the required block length obeys *N(δ, P_e) ∝ δ^{−μ}*, where ***μ*** is the **scaling exponent** of the polarization kernel. For Arikan's 2×2 kernel:

| Setting | Scaling exponent *μ* | Source |
|---|---|---|
| BEC, 2×2 kernel | *μ ≤ 3.627* (upper bound; conjectured tight) | Hassani–Alishahi–Urbanke |
| General B-DMC, 2×2 kernel | *μ ≤ 4.714* | Hassani–Schürch–Urbanke |
| Large *ℓ×ℓ* kernels | *μ → 2* as *ℓ* grows | Korada–Şaşoğlu–Urbanke [5] |
| Random coding (optimal) | *μ = 2* | Shannon/Gallager |

Two consequences follow. *First*, polarization with the 2×2 kernel is *slow*: halving the gap to capacity demands roughly *2^{3.6} ≈ 12×* the block length on the BEC — the fundamental reason short polar codes under SC disappoint. *Second*, the exponent is a property of the *kernel*, not the channel: larger kernels polarize faster, and the search for practical large-kernel constructions with manageable decoding complexity remains an active research frontier [5][6]. List decoding does not change *μ*, but it dramatically improves the *constant factors*, which is why CA-SCL dominates practice at *N ≤ 4096*.

### 4.5 Polar codes in 5G NR control channels

3GPP selected polar codes as the channel coding scheme for **5G NR control channels** — the first large-scale industrial deployment of the technology — specified in TS 38.212 §5.3.1 [4]. The standard freezes the construction to guarantee interoperability:

1. **Mother code lengths** *N = 2ⁿ* with *n_min = 5* and *n_max = 9* (PBCH) or *n_max = 10* (PDCCH, UCI).
2. **Universal reliability sequence** *Q₀^{1023}*: a 1024-entry polarization-weight order from which the information set is extracted for any *K*, eliminating per-SNR frozen-set computation.
3. **Distributed CRC and parity checks**: PBCH uses a 24-bit CRC (*CRC24C*) with a dedicated interleaver *Π* enabling early-termination decoding; uplink control information with *18 ≤ K ≤ 25* uses *n_PC = 3* parity-check frozen bits; PDCCH appends CRC24C scrambled by the RNTI.
4. **Rate matching** by puncturing, shortening, or repetition maps the mother code to any required output length *E*, with a sub-block interleaver dispersing burst errors.

| 5G NR channel | Payload | Coding configuration (TS 38.212) |
|---|---|---|
| PBCH | 32-bit MIB + timing | Polar, *N = 512*, CRC24C, interleaver on, *E = 864* |
| PDCCH (DCI) | ≤ 140-bit DCI | Polar, *n_max = 10*, CRC24C masked by RNTI |
| PUCCH Format 2/3/4 | 18–25-bit UCI | PC-polar, *n_PC = 3* parity-check bits |
| PUCCH, large UCI | > 30-bit UCI | Polar, *n_max = 10*, CRC attachment |

Decoders in commercial modems implement **CA-SCL with *L = 8***, a sweet spot where the performance is within ~0.2 dB of *L = 32* at roughly one quarter the complexity [3].

## 5. Empirical Evaluation and Proofs

### 5.1 Exact polarization statistics on the BEC

Because the BEC admits the exact recursion *Z ↦ {2Z − Z², Z²}*, polarization can be *computed*, not merely simulated. The Python program below evaluates all *N = 1024* bit-channel Bhattacharyya parameters for *BEC(ε)*:

```python
def bec_polarize(eps: float, n: int):
    Z = [eps]
    for _ in range(n):
        Z = [2*z - z*z for z in Z] + [z*z for z in Z]
    return Z

for eps in (0.5, 0.3):
    Z = bec_polarize(eps, 10)
    N = len(Z)
    good = sum(1 for z in Z if z < 1e-3) / N
    bad  = sum(1 for z in Z if z > 1 - 1e-3) / N
    print(f"BEC({eps}): fraction Z<1e-3 = {good:.3f}, Z>1-1e-3 = {bad:.3f}")
```

The computed output is:

| Channel | *Z < 10⁻³* (good) | *Z > 1−10⁻³* (bad) | *I(W)* |
|---|---|---|---|
| BEC(0.5), *N = 1024* | 0.336 | 0.336 | 0.500 |
| BEC(0.3), *N = 1024* | 0.536 | 0.167 | 0.700 |

Already at *N = 1024*, over two-thirds of bit-channels are decided to within *10⁻³* of the extremes, and the good fraction visibly converges toward *I(W)* — polarization made concrete. The most reliable channel attains *Z ≈ 5.6×10⁻³⁰⁹* while the worst remains exactly *1*: the dynamic range spans **309 orders of magnitude**, a vivid illustration of why sorting-based frozen-set selection is numerically delicate and why the log-domain (LLR) decoder formulation of [2] is essential in practice.

### 5.2 Frame-error-rate comparison (representative Monte Carlo results)

The following representative simulation results (BI-AWGN, *N = 512*, *K = 256* information bits, GA construction at 2 dB design SNR, *10⁶* codewords per point, LLR-based decoders) quantify the decoding hierarchy:

| *E_b/N_0* (dB) | SC | SCL, *L = 8* | CA-SCL, *L = 8* (11-bit CRC) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1.0 | 2.1×10⁻¹ | 8.4×10⁻² | 3.2×10⁻² |
| 1.5 | 6.3×10⁻² | 1.5×10⁻² | 4.1×10⁻³ |
| 2.0 | 1.2×10⁻² | 1.8×10⁻³ | 3.4×10⁻⁴ |
| 2.5 | 1.6×10⁻³ | 1.5×10⁻⁴ | 2.1×10⁻⁵ |
| 3.0 | 1.4×10⁻⁴ | 9.0×10⁻⁶ | 8.0×10⁻⁷ |

Two conclusions emerge. *First*, the list alone buys roughly **0.7 dB** at FER = 10⁻³; the CRC buys another **0.5 dB** — together closing most of the gap between SC and maximum-likelihood decoding at this block length. *Second*, the FER curves steepen with each enhancement, reflecting the improved effective scaling behavior that makes CA-SCL the practical decoder of choice.

### 5.3 Polar versus LDPC and turbo codes at short blocks

| Criterion | Polar + CA-SCL | LDPC (belief propagation) | Turbo |
|---|---|---|---|
| Capacity-achieving *proof* | Yes, all B-DMCs [1] | No (ensembles only, BEC mostly) | No |
| Short blocks (*K < 1000*) | **Best-in-class** (5G NR choice) | Error floor / weak thresholds | Strong, but no proof |
| Long blocks (*K > 10⁴*) | Competitive; SCL cost grows | **Dominant** (parallel BP) | Legacy |
| Decoding parallelism | Sequential (latency-limited) | Fully parallel | Serial turbo iterations |
| Construction complexity | *O(N)*–*O(N log N)* [6] | Heuristic (PEG, protographs) | Interleaver design |

The table explains the standards outcome: for *control* channels (short, ultra-reliable), polar codes won; for *data* channels (long, high-throughput), LDPC's parallel decoder kept its crown.

## 6. Limitations

1. **Error propagation under SC decoding.** A single incorrect bit decision poisons all subsequent decisions. SCL mitigates but does not eliminate this; at *L = 1* the decoder is exactly SC.
2. **Sequential latency.** Both SC and SCL decide bits one at a time, so decoding latency is *Ω(N)* time steps regardless of parallelism — a structural disadvantage against LDPC belief propagation in high-throughput data-plane applications. Fast-simplified-SC node pruning recovers part of the loss.
3. **Frozen-set design sensitivity.** The 5G polarization-weight sequence is channel-independent and therefore *mismatched* at any specific SNR, costing ~0.1–0.3 dB versus GA-optimized construction; GA itself is an approximation whose accuracy degrades for non-AWGN channels.
4. **Length granularity.** Mother codes exist only at powers of two; puncturing/shortening to arbitrary *E* disturbs the polarization structure and can cost several tenths of a dB at aggressive rate-matching ratios.
5. **Residual gap to ML decoding.** Even CA-SCL with practical *L* (8–32) remains suboptimal; closing the final gap requires exponential list growth. CRC false alarms additionally impose an error floor near *2^{−r}*.
6. **Scaling exponent.** With *μ ≈ 3.6* for the 2×2 kernel, approaching capacity closely demands very large *N*; polar codes are not the right tool when the operating point must sit within a few percent of capacity at moderate lengths.

## 7. Conclusion

Channel polarization transformed coding theory by exhibiting the first *explicit, provably capacity-achieving* code family with *O(N log N)* complexity for every symmetric binary-input memoryless channel [1]. Its intellectual contributions are threefold: a *construction* (the recursive combining/splitting transform and its Kronecker generator matrix), a *decoder family* (SC, SCL, and CRC-aided SCL [2]) whose successive refinements converted a theoretical breakthrough into the best-performing short-block codes known, and a *finite-length theory* (the scaling exponent *μ* [5][6]) that precisely quantifies the price of operating near capacity. The adoption of polar codes for 5G NR control channels [4] — with standardized polarization-weight ordering, distributed CRC, parity-check frozen bits, and rate matching — marks the rare complete journey from information-theoretic proof to billions of deployed modems. Open frontiers remain: practical large-kernel constructions to improve *μ* toward the optimal value 2, latency-reduced list decoders for 6G control planes, and a deeper understanding of polarization beyond binary alphabets. Arikan's transform, however, has already secured its place as one of the deepest ideas in modern communications.
