# Secure Multiparty Computation: From BGW and Yao to the SPDZ Paradigm — Completeness, Garbling, and Dishonest-Majority Security

## Abstract

Secure multiparty computation (MPC) allows *n* mutually distrusting parties, each holding a private input *xᵢ*, to jointly evaluate a public function *f(x₁,…,xₙ)* while revealing nothing beyond the output. This survey traces the field's central arc: the information-theoretic *completeness theorems* of Ben-Or, Goldwasser, and Wigderson [2], proving perfect security from Shamir sharing and degree reduction against a minority of semi-honest (a third of malicious) corruptions; Yao's garbled circuits [3] and oblivious transfer [5], delivering constant-round two-party computation; the GMW compiler [4], lifting semi-honest protocols to malicious security via zero-knowledge proofs; and the SPDZ paradigm [7], splitting computation into an input-independent *offline* phase of authenticated Beaver triples [8] and a cheap *online* phase to achieve active security against a *dishonest majority* of up to *n−1* corruptions. We give protocol decompositions, executable sketches, asymptotic comparisons, a synthesis of benchmark trends from the SPDZ lineage [7,9,10,11], and a critical discussion of limits — including the impossibility of fairness with a dishonest majority. The conclusion identifies the frontier: sublinear preprocessing, concretely efficient malicious OT extension, and large-scale deployment.

## 1 Introduction

The founding vignette of secure computation is Yao's *millionaires' problem*: two millionaires wish to determine who is richer without disclosing their fortunes [3]. The generalization — *n* parties computing a joint function of private inputs — defines secure multiparty computation (MPC). Formally, a protocol *π* securely realizes a functionality *ℱ* if, for every real-world adversary *𝒜*, there exists an ideal-world simulator *𝒮* such that the joint distribution of honest outputs and the adversary's view in the real execution is indistinguishable from that in the ideal execution where a trusted party computes *ℱ* [12]. This *simulation paradigm* is the field's definitional bedrock: security means that attacking the protocol teaches nothing that could not be learned from the ideal functionality.

Two axes organize the landscape. The first is *adversarial power*: **semi-honest** (honest-but-curious) adversaries follow the protocol while mining transcripts for information, whereas **malicious** (Byzantine, active) adversaries deviate arbitrarily. The second is *corruption threshold*: an **honest majority** (*t < n/2* corruptions) permits information-theoretic techniques — secret sharing and combinatorial degree reduction — while a **dishonest majority** (*t ≤ n−1*) forces reliance on computational assumptions such as oblivious transfer, which is *complete* for MPC in this regime [4].

The history of MPC is the story of pushing both axes outward. BGW [2] established that *every* function admits perfectly secure protocols under honest-majority assumptions. Yao's garbled circuits [3] gave practical two-party protocols with constant round complexity. GMW [4] showed how to compile semi-honest protocols into maliciously secure ones. Finally, the SPDZ line of work [7,9,11] made *dishonest-majority malicious security* concretely practical by splitting the work into an expensive but input-independent offline phase and a fast online phase — the architecture that underpins modern deployments via frameworks such as MP-SPDZ [10].

This article presents these results with uniform notation, compares their asymptotic costs, surveys empirical evidence, and examines what remains provably out of reach.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Secret sharing

*Shamir's (t+1)-out-of-n secret sharing* [6] is the workhorse of information-theoretic MPC. To share a secret *s ∈ 𝔽* (over a finite field *𝔽*), the dealer samples a random degree-*t* polynomial *p* with *p(0) = s* and distributes shares *sᵢ = p(i)* to party *Pᵢ*. Any *t+1* shares reconstruct *s* by Lagrange interpolation; any *t* shares reveal nothing. Crucially, the scheme is *linearly homomorphic*: given shares of *a* and *b*, each party locally computes *cᵢ = α·aᵢ + bᵢ* to obtain a share of *α·a + b* — addition gates are free. Multiplication is the hard case: the pointwise product of degree-*t* sharings is a degree-*2t* sharing of *ab*, and reducing its degree without leaking information is the central technical problem of BGW [1,2,13].

*Additive (n-out-of-n) sharing* takes the other extreme: *x = Σᵢ xᵢ* with each *xᵢ* uniform subject to the sum. It is linear and simple, tolerates *n−1* corruptions for privacy, but offers no robustness on its own — any single missing share blocks reconstruction. SPDZ builds active security on top of additive sharing by *authenticating* each share with information-theoretic MACs under a global key no party knows [7].

### 2.2 Oblivious transfer

In 1-out-of-2 *oblivious transfer* (OT), a sender holds messages *(m₀, m₁)* and a receiver holds a choice bit *b*; the receiver learns *m_b* while the sender learns nothing about *b* and the receiver learns nothing about *m_{1−b}* [5]. OT is the *completeness primitive* for dishonest-majority MPC: Kilian and others showed OT suffices for general secure computation, and GMW's protocol builds every AND gate from OT [4]. Modern practice relies on *OT extension* — bootstrapping a few expensive "base" OTs into millions of cheap ones — which made garbled-circuit and GMW-style protocols practical at scale.

### 2.3 Threat models and the simulation paradigm

We distinguish static vs. adaptive corruption, and the *security with abort* notion forced upon dishonest-majority protocols: with *t ≥ n/2* corruptions, fairness and guaranteed output delivery are *impossible* in general (Cleve [1986] proved this even for coin flipping), so protocols like SPDZ guarantee only that cheating is *detected* and the honest parties *abort* rather than accept a corrupted result [7]. The following theorem frames the entire honest-majority program:

> **Theorem (BGW completeness [2]):** Every *n*-ary function can be computed by *n* parties with *perfect* security — zero error probability — against a semi-honest adversary corrupting *t < n/2* parties, and against a malicious adversary corrupting *t < n/3* parties, assuming pairwise private channels (plus broadcast in the malicious case). The bounds *n/2* and *n/3* are tight.

The tightness claim is itself instructive: it marks the precise boundary where information-theoretic security ends and computational assumptions become necessary.

## 3 Methodology

Our analysis decomposes each protocol family into three components: **(i)** the *sharing representation* (Shamir, additive, or garbled wire labels); **(ii)** the *gate-evaluation mechanism* (degree reduction, OT, or triple consumption); and **(iii)** the *authentication strategy* (verifiable secret sharing, cut-and-choose, or information-theoretic MACs). We compare families along four metrics: *assumptions* (information-theoretic vs. computational), *corruption tolerance*, *communication complexity* per gate, and *round complexity* as a function of circuit depth.

Asymptotic bounds are derived from the original analyses [1,2,3,4,7,8] and cross-checked against the textbook treatment of Cramer, Damgård, and Nielsen [12] and the BGW lecture notes [13]. Empirical claims are synthesized from the benchmark sections of the SPDZ lineage [7,9,11] and the MP-SPDZ framework paper [10]; where the literature reports qualitative rather than directly comparable numbers, we say so explicitly rather than fabricating precision. Executable sketches are given in Python (Shamir sharing) and Haskell (garbled-gate construction) to make the abstract machinery concrete. Circuit models are standardized as follows: arithmetic circuits over a prime field *𝔽ₚ* for BGW/SPDZ, Boolean circuits for Yao/GMW, with multiplication (resp. AND) gates as the unit of cost.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The BGW Protocol: Perfect Security under an Honest Majority

BGW evaluates an arithmetic circuit gate by gate over Shamir-shared values [2]. The *input phase* has each party share its input with a random degree-*t* polynomial. Addition gates and multiplication-by-constant are evaluated *locally* by the homomorphism. For a multiplication gate with shared inputs *a*, *b*, each party holds *aᵢ*, *bᵢ*; the pointwise products *dᵢ = aᵢ·bᵢ* form a degree-*2t* sharing of *ab*. Since *2t < n* when *t < n/2*, the parties can *re-share* each *dᵢ* with a fresh degree-*t* polynomial and take a fixed public linear combination — the *recombination vector* *r*, the first row of the inverse Vandermonde matrix — to obtain a degree-*t* sharing of *ab* [1,13]. The following Python sketch captures the essence:

```python
import random
from functools import reduce

def share(secret, t, n, p):
    """Shamir (t+1)-out-of-n sharing over field Z_p."""
    coeffs = [secret] + [random.randrange(p) for _ in range(t)]
    return [sum(c * pow(i + 1, k, p) for k, c in enumerate(coeffs)) % p
            for i in range(n)]

def reconstruct(shares, p):
    """Lagrange interpolation at x=0 from (x, y) points."""
    def lagrange_basis(j):
        num = den = 1
        for m in range(len(shares)):
            if m != j:
                num = num * (-(m + 1)) % p
                den = den * ((j + 1) - (m + 1)) % p
        return num * pow(den, -1, p) % p
    return sum(y * lagrange_basis(j) for j, (_, y) in
               enumerate(shares)) % p

# Addition homomorphism: shares add locally, no interaction.
a, b = share(7, 1, 3, 101), share(5, 1, 3, 101)
sum_shares = [(x + y) % 101 for x, y in zip(a, b)]
assert reconstruct(list(enumerate(sum_shares, 1)), 101) == 12
```

Against *malicious* adversaries, plain Shamir sharing is insufficient — a corrupt dealer can distribute inconsistent shares. BGW therefore upgrades to *verifiable secret sharing* (VSS), typically via bivariate polynomials, and requires *t < n/3* plus a broadcast channel [2]. The full proof that the resulting protocol achieves perfect security is famously delicate; Asharov and Lindell's complete proof [1] runs to nearly a hundred pages, formalizing the simulator for every subprotocol. Communication is *O(n²)* field elements per multiplication gate with *O(1)* rounds per circuit layer, i.e. *O(depth)* rounds overall.

### 4.2 Yao's Garbled Circuits: Constant-Round Two-Party Computation

Yao's construction [3] takes a radically different route: instead of distributing shares, one party (the *garbler*) *encrypts the computation itself*. For each wire *w* in a Boolean circuit, the garbler samples two random *labels* *W⁰_w*, *W¹_w* representing the bits 0 and 1. For each gate *g* with input wires *a*, *b* and output wire *c*, the garbler produces a *garbled table* of four ciphertexts — one per input combination — each encrypting the corresponding output label under the two input labels:

```haskell
-- Sketch of a garbled AND gate (point-and-permute omitted for clarity)
data Label = Label { key :: Key, permBit :: Bit }

garbleAnd :: Label -> Label -> Label -> Label -> Label -> Label -> Gate
garbleAnd a0 a1 b0 b1 c0 c1 =
  Gate [ enc (key a0) (enc (key b0) (key c0))   -- 0 AND 0 -> 0
       , enc (key a0) (enc (key b1) (key c0))   -- 0 AND 1 -> 0
       , enc (key a1) (enc (key b0) (key c0))   -- 1 AND 0 -> 0
       , enc (key a1) (enc (key b1) (key c1)) ] -- 1 AND 1 -> 1
```

The garbler sends the garbled tables plus the labels for *its own* inputs to the *evaluator*, who obtains labels for *her* inputs via oblivious transfer [5] — learning exactly one label per wire and revealing nothing about her input bits. Evaluation decrypts exactly one row per gate, propagating labels to the output wires, whose decoding table maps final labels to plaintext bits. The *point-and-permute* optimization assigns each label a random permutation bit so the evaluator knows *which* row to decrypt without trial decryption, and the *free-XOR* technique makes XOR gates cost nothing. Security rests on the encryption scheme: the evaluator sees only random labels and learns nothing about intermediate wire values. Round complexity is *constant* — one OT phase plus one message carrying the garbled circuit — at the cost of *O(|C|)* symmetric-key operations per gate and communication of four ciphertexts per gate (halved by row-reduction variants). Yao's protocol is secure against semi-honest adversaries; malicious security requires cut-and-choose or authenticated garbling.

### 4.3 The GMW Paradigm: OT-Based Boolean MPC and the Compiler

Goldreich, Micali, and Wigderson [4] gave both a protocol and a *compiler*. The GMW *protocol* evaluates Boolean circuits under XOR-based secret sharing tolerating *t < n/2* semi-honest corruptions: XOR gates are local, while each AND gate is evaluated with the help of 1-out-of-4 OT between every ordered pair of parties, so the per-gate cost is *O(n²)* OTs. The GMW *compiler* is the deeper contribution: it transforms *any* semi-honest protocol into one secure against malicious adversaries by forcing each party to prove in zero-knowledge that it followed the protocol, with coin-flipping subprotocols generating the needed randomness. The compiler is a feasibility result rather than a practical tool — the zero-knowledge proofs are expensive — but it established the *possibility* of malicious security from semi-honest foundations and introduced techniques (notably, committing to inputs and randomness before proving consistency) that recur in every later maliciously secure construction.

### 4.4 The SPDZ Paradigm: Authenticated Shares and the Offline/Online Split

SPDZ — *Speedy*, from Damgård, Pastro, Smart, and Zakarias [7] — achieves *active* security against a *dishonest majority* (*t ≤ n−1*) through two ideas: authenticate everything with information-theoretic MACs, and split the protocol into an input-independent *offline phase* and a fast *online phase*.

**Authenticated shares.** Values are held in the representation *⟦x⟧ = (xᵢ, μᵢ(x))*, where *x = Σᵢ xᵢ* is additive-shared and each party holds a MAC share *μᵢ(x)* such that *Σᵢ μᵢ(x) = α·x* for a global MAC key *α = Σᵢ αᵢ* that *no party knows* [7]. Linear operations are local and preserve the MAC relation. To open a value, parties broadcast their shares and check the MAC; a corrupt party forging a share would need to guess *α* (soundness error *≈ 1/|𝔽|*).

**Beaver triples.** Multiplication consumes a preprocessed *triple* *(⟦a⟧, ⟦b⟧, ⟦c⟧)* with *c = a·b* for random *a*, *b* [8]. To multiply *⟦x⟧·⟦y⟧*, the parties open *ϵ = x − a* and *δ = y − b*, then compute *⟦xy⟧ = ⟦c⟧ + ϵ·⟦b⟧ + δ·⟦a⟧ + ϵδ* — one multiplication costs *two openings* and no further interaction. Triples are authenticated like everything else, and a *sacrifice* step checks a batch of triples against each other so that malformed triples are caught before use.

**The MAC check.** Rather than verifying MACs on every opening, SPDZ batches verification: for opened values *a₁,…,aₖ*, the parties agree on a random *r*, each commits to *σᵢ = Σⱼ rʲ·μᵢ(aⱼ)*, then opens the commitments and checks *Σᵢ σᵢ ≟ α·Σⱼ rʲaⱼ*. A single bad MAC is caught with overwhelming probability while amortizing the check over the whole computation [7].

**The offline phase.** Triples must be generated *without* leaking *a*, *b* — the original SPDZ uses *somewhat homomorphic encryption* (SHE): parties encrypt their shares, homomorphically combine them into encrypted triples, and distributively decrypt, with zero-knowledge proofs enforcing honest behavior [7]. MASCOT [9] replaced SHE with OT-based triple generation, and Overdrive [11] dramatically improved SHE-based preprocessing throughput. The online phase is information-theoretic given the triples — the expensive cryptography is quarantined in preprocessing.

The security guarantee is *active security with abort*: any deviation is detected (except with negligible probability) and honest parties abort rather than output a corrupted value. Guaranteed output is *impossible* here, as the next section's discussion of Cleve's result explains.

## 5 Empirical Results

We compare the protocol families asymptotically and synthesize the reported performance characteristics of the SPDZ lineage.

| Protocol | Adversary | Threshold | Assumption | Comm. per mult/AND gate | Rounds |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| BGW [2] | semi-honest | *t < n/2* | information-theoretic | *O(n²)* field elements | *O(depth)* |
| BGW [2] | malicious | *t < n/3* | info.-theoretic + broadcast | *O(n²)* (+ VSS overhead) | *O(depth)* |
| GMW [4] | semi-honest | *t < n/2* | OT (computational) | *O(n²)* OTs | *O(depth)* |
| Yao [3] | semi-honest | *n = 2* | symmetric crypto | *O(1)* (4 ctxts/gate) | *O(1)* |
| SPDZ [7] | malicious | *t ≤ n−1* | SHE/OT (offline only) | *O(n²)* (online, batched) | *O(depth)* online |

Several robust qualitative findings emerge from the literature. First, the *offline/online split* dominates SPDZ performance analysis: the original paper reports that the SHE-based offline phase accounts for the overwhelming majority of end-to-end runtime, while the online phase — two openings per multiplication — is lightweight and network-latency-bound [7]. Second, MASCOT's OT-based triple generation substantially reduced preprocessing cost relative to the original SHE approach at the price of higher communication [9]. Third, Overdrive's improved SHE packing and triple-generation pipeline further narrowed the preprocessing bottleneck [11]. Fourth, the MP-SPDZ framework [10], which implements the full SPDZ family alongside many other protocols, has become the standard harness for apples-to-apples benchmarking, confirming that maliciously secure dishonest-majority arithmetic MPC is practical for real workloads (privacy-preserving machine learning inference, auctions, benchmarking) at throughputs far beyond what the pre-2012 literature anticipated. The consistent lesson: *authenticating with MACs and preprocessing with triples converts active security from a theoretical compiler into an engineering problem* — and an increasingly tractable one.

## 6 Limitations

**Fairness is impossible with a dishonest majority.** Cleve's 1986 result shows that no protocol with *t ≥ n/2* corruptions can guarantee fairness or output delivery, even for two-party coin flipping. SPDZ's *abort* model is therefore not a design weakness but a *provable ceiling*: a corrupt majority can always withhold its final message, and the best achievable guarantee is that cheating is detected rather than silently accepted [7].

**Preprocessing remains the bottleneck.** Although the online phase is cheap, generating millions of authenticated triples is expensive — SHE operations in the original offline phase, or massive OT volumes in MASCOT. For computations with high multiplicative depth or huge circuits, preprocessing time and storage dominate, and the "input-independent" property only partially mitigates this since the *number* of triples depends on circuit size.

**Honest-majority protocols face deployment barriers.** BGW's perfect security is theoretically optimal, but the *t < n/3* malicious bound and the broadcast requirement complicate real deployments; moreover, information-theoretic security demands private channels between every pair of parties, an *O(n²)* setup cost. GMW's compiler, while foundational, is far too heavy for practice — modern malicious security comes from authenticated preprocessing (SPDZ) or cut-and-choose garbling, not generic zero-knowledge compilation [4,7].

**Circuit-model friction.** Arithmetic protocols (BGW, SPDZ) excel at additions and multiplications but handle comparisons and bit operations poorly; Boolean protocols (Yao, GMW) have the reverse profile. Mixed-circuit frameworks exist [10], but conversion between representations adds overhead. Additionally, SPDZ's MAC soundness requires a large field (*≈ 2⁻ᵏ* forgery probability), forcing either large moduli or statistical-security parameters that inflate costs.

**Composability subtleties.** Standalone simulation security does not automatically compose under concurrent execution; the universally composable (UC) framework imposes stronger requirements that some optimizations (e.g., certain MAC-check batchings) must be re-examined against [12].

## 7 Conclusion

Four decades of MPC research trace a clear trajectory: BGW [2] proved that *perfect* security is possible with an honest majority; Yao [3] and GMW [4] showed that computational assumptions buy constant rounds and general malicious security; and SPDZ [7] made *dishonest-majority active security* practical by authenticating every share and quarantining the expensive cryptography in an offline phase. The Beaver triple [8] — a deceptively simple randomization device — turned out to be the key engineering primitive, with MASCOT [9] and Overdrive [11] progressively industrializing its generation and MP-SPDZ [10] providing the common benchmarking ground.

The frontier is now concrete efficiency: sublinear-communication preprocessing, maliciously secure OT extension with minimal overhead, and deployment at the scale of real data pipelines. The impossibility results — Cleve's fairness bound, the *n/3* malicious BGW threshold — tell us exactly where theory stops; everything beyond is engineering. That the gap between "provably secure" and "practically deployable" has narrowed from orders of magnitude to constant factors is the field's defining achievement.
