# Entropy-Optimal Learned Lossy Image Coding: Hyperprior Variational Autoencoders with Autoregressive Contexts and Asymmetric Numeral Systems at Sub-0.1 Bits per Pixel

## Abstract

Learned lossy image compression has matured from a proof of concept for variational autoencoders into a genuine competitor of hand-engineered codecs, achieving state-of-the-art rate–distortion (R–D) performance on perceptual metrics and approaching the best conventional standards on mean squared error. This thesis presents a unified, mathematically rigorous treatment of the modern learned-compression pipeline: nonlinear analysis and synthesis transforms, factorized and hierarchical (hyperprior) entropy models, autoregressive context models for conditional probability estimation, rate–distortion optimization through Lagrangian relaxation, and bit-exact entropy coding with asymmetric numeral systems (ANS). We derive the R–D objective from the variational evidence lower bound, show the scale hyperprior captures spatial dependencies that factorized priors cannot express, and analyze ANS — near-Shannon rates at Huffman-like throughput — against the decoding-time cost of autoregressive contexts. Joint hyperprior–autoregressive models outperform BPG on both PSNR and MS-SSIM, with compelling reconstructions below 0.1 bits per pixel [1][2][4].

## 1. Introduction

Lossy image compression is one of the oldest problems in information theory and among the most practically consequential: every photograph uploaded, streamed frame, and satellite image transmitted must be reduced to a fraction of its raw size with imperceptible loss of fidelity. For three decades, progress was driven by hand-engineered transform coding — JPEG's discrete cosine transform, JPEG 2000's wavelets, and HEVC-intra derivatives such as BPG — combined with context-adaptive entropy coding. These codecs decompose compression into *transform*, *quantization*, and *entropy coding*, each optimized in isolation against a fixed mathematical model of natural image statistics.

End-to-end learned compression [1][2][3] rejects this decomposition: a neural network jointly learns the nonlinear analysis transform, the latent probability model, and quantization behavior, all optimized against the single scalar objective that matters — the rate–distortion Lagrangian. The pivotal observation [1] is that this pipeline is formally a *variational autoencoder*: the rate term is the cross-entropy of the approximate posterior under the prior, and distortion is the negative log-likelihood of the reconstruction.

This thesis addresses the central architectural question that followed: **what is the correct entropy model for the quantized latent representation?** A fully factorized prior [3] assumes independence across latent dimensions and leaves exploitable statistical structure on the table. The *scale hyperprior* [1] introduces auxiliary latent variables — side information in the classical sense — that condition the scale of a Gaussian prior per latent element, capturing the empirical observation that neighboring latent coefficients tend to vary together in magnitude. Minnen et al. [2] showed that hyperpriors and *autoregressive context models*, which predict each latent coefficient from its already-decoded spatial neighbors, are complementary: their combination yields a 15.8% average file-size reduction over the previous learned state of the art and beats BPG on both PSNR and MS-SSIM. Cheng et al. [4] further generalized the conditional distributions to Gaussian mixtures and added attention modules, closing the remaining gap to VVC on PSNR.

The scope of this work is the complete stack: from the information-theoretic foundations of the R–D Lagrangian, through the hierarchy of entropy models, to the final bitstream, where *asymmetric numeral systems* (ANS) [5] — Duda's stack-based entropy coder that marries the throughput of Huffman coding to the near-Shannon efficiency of arithmetic coding — converts predicted probabilities into bits. Our running design target is aggressive but concrete: high-fidelity reconstruction at **0.1 bits per pixel and below**, a regime where classical codecs collapse and learned models' perceptual advantages are most pronounced.

Our contributions: (i) learned compression derived as variational inference with exact R–D semantics; (ii) comparative analysis of factorized, hyperprior, and autoregressive entropy models with complexity accounting; (iii) ANS as the natural entropy-coding backend; (iv) benchmarks against classical standards; (v) an honest account of limitations separating prototypes from deployable codecs.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 Transform coding and rate–distortion theory

Classical lossy compression follows Shannon's source-coding theorem in its rate–distortion form. For a source $X$ and a distortion measure $d(x, \hat{x})$, the rate–distortion function

$$
R(D) = \min_{p(\hat{x} \mid x):\; \mathbb{E}[d(X,\hat{X})] \le D} I(X; \hat{X})
$$

gives the minimum achievable expected code length (in bits) at distortion at most $D$ [6]. No codec can beat $R(D)$; a codec's quality is measured by its distance from this unattainable frontier. Practical codecs approximate the frontier via *transform coding*: an invertible-ish transform concentrates signal energy into few coefficients (energy compaction), scalar quantization discards precision, and entropy coding exploits the resulting non-uniform symbol distribution.

JPEG, JPEG 2000, and BPG share the block diagram *predict → transform → quantize → entropy-code*, differing mainly in transforms; their entropy coders' adaptation is hard-wired by committee. Learned compression keeps the diagram but makes every block differentiable and data-driven.

### 2.2 Variational autoencoders as compression models

A variational autoencoder defines a generative model $p(x, y) = p(x \mid y)\, p(y)$ with latent $y$, and an approximate posterior $q(y \mid x)$, trained to maximize the evidence lower bound (ELBO):

$$
\log p(x) \ge \mathbb{E}_{q(y\mid x)}\!\left[\log p(x \mid y)\right] - D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\left(q(y \mid x)\,\|\,p(y)\right).
$$

Ballé, Laparra, and Simoncelli [3] observed that for image compression this decomposition is *exactly* the codec's accounting. If $q(y \mid x)$ is deterministic up to additive uniform quantization noise, and $\hat{y} = \mathrm{round}(y)$ is the quantized latent, then:

- The **distortion** $D = \mathbb{E}[\|x - g(\hat{y})\|^2]$ is the negative log-likelihood under a Gaussian observation model.
- The **rate** $R = \mathbb{E}[-\log_2 p(\hat{y})]$ is the cross-entropy of the quantized latents under the learned prior — the expected bitstream length.

Training therefore minimizes the **rate–distortion Lagrangian**

$$
\mathcal{L}(\lambda) = R + \lambda D,
$$

where $\lambda$ is the Lagrange multiplier tracing out the R–D curve. Varying $\lambda$ over a discrete set of models (or conditioning a single model on $\lambda$) yields the operational R–D frontier [1][2]. During training, the non-differentiable rounding is replaced by additive uniform noise, $ \tilde{y} = y + u $, $u \sim \mathcal{U}(-\tfrac{1}{2}, \tfrac{1}{2})$, which makes the expected rate differentiable while matching the true quantized distribution at integer centers — the density $p_{\tilde{y}}$ convolved with the uniform noise equals the probability mass of $\hat{y}$ [3].

### 2.3 Entropy coding: from Huffman to ANS

Given predicted symbol probabilities, the final step — converting symbols to bits — is *entropy coding*. The classical choices are:

1. **Huffman coding**: optimal among prefix codes, but restricted to code lengths that are integers; its redundancy can approach 1 bit per symbol for skewed distributions.
2. **Arithmetic / range coding**: encodes an entire sequence into a single fractional interval, achieving expected length within 2 bits of the entropy of the whole sequence — essentially optimal — but requires maintaining interval bounds with multiplications and is comparatively slow.

**Asymmetric numeral systems (ANS)** [5] ends this trade-off: it maintains a *single* natural-number state $s$, mapping $s \mapsto s' \approx s/p_x$ so each symbol costs $\approx -\log_2 p_x$ bits. The tabled variant (tANS) precomputes transitions into a kilobyte-scale automaton, decoding ~50% faster than Huffman at arithmetic-coding rates [5][7]. ANS is stack-like (LIFO), which interacts with autoregressive contexts (§4.5). Its adoption — Zstandard, LZFSE, JPEG XL, JPEG AI — testifies to its practical dominance [7].

## 3. Methodology

### 3.1 Model family

The canonical architecture [1][2][3][4]: the **analysis transform** $g_a(x; \phi)$ maps $x$ to latents $y$ via strided convolutions with *generalized divisive normalization* (GDN) [3]; $\hat{y} = \mathrm{round}(y)$; the **synthesis transform** $g_s(\hat{y}; \theta)$ reconstructs $\hat{x}$.

The entropy model assigns a probability mass to every element $\hat{y}_i$. We study a hierarchy:

| Model | Prior form | Reference |
|---|---|---|
| Factorized prior | $p(\hat{y}) = \prod_i p_i(\hat{y}_i)$, one learned univariate density per channel | Ballé et al. 2017 [3] |
| Scale hyperprior | $p(\hat{y} \mid \hat{z}) = \prod_i \mathcal{N}(\hat{y}_i; 0, \sigma_i^2(\hat{z}))$, $\hat{z} = \mathrm{round}(h_a(y))$ | Ballé et al. 2018 [1] |
| Mean–scale hyperprior | $\mathcal{N}(\hat{y}_i; \mu_i(\hat{z}), \sigma_i^2(\hat{z}))$ | Minnen et al. 2018 [2] |
| + Autoregressive context | $p(\hat{y}_i \mid \hat{z}, \hat{y}_{<i})$ via masked 5×5 convolutions | Minnen et al. 2018 [2] |
| + Gaussian mixture + attention | $\sum_k \pi_{k,i}\, \mathcal{N}(\hat{y}_i; \mu_{k,i}, \sigma_{k,i}^2)$ | Cheng et al. 2020 [4] |

### 3.2 The hyperprior mechanism

The hyperprior introduces a second, smaller autoencoder operating in *latent space*: $z = h_a(y)$, quantized to $\hat{z}$, with its own learned factorized prior $p(\hat{z})$. The hyper-synthesis transform $h_s(\hat{z})$ outputs, for each spatial–channel position $i$ of $\hat{y}$, the parameters of its conditional distribution — a scale $\sigma_i$ (scale hyperprior) or a mean–scale pair $(\mu_i, \sigma_i)$ (mean–scale hyperprior). The total rate decomposes as

$$
R = \underbrace{\mathbb{E}[-\log_2 p(\hat{z})]}_{R_z\ \text{(side information)}} \;+\; \underbrace{\mathbb{E}[-\log_2 p(\hat{y} \mid \hat{z})]}_{R_{y\mid z}\ \text{(conditional latents)}}.
$$

End-to-end training *learns the optimal balance* between side-information cost $R_z$ and the entropy reduction it buys in $R_{y\mid z}$ [1] — the learned analogue of classical side information (e.g., HEVC prediction-mode signaling), discovered from data rather than designed.

### 3.3 Training objective and quantization surrogate

The full training objective for the joint hyperprior–autoregressive model is

$$
\mathcal{L}(\lambda) = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim p_{\text{data}}}\!\left[-\log_2 p(\hat{z}) - \log_2 p(\hat{y} \mid \hat{z}, \hat{y}_{<i}) + \lambda\, \|x - g_s(\hat{y})\|_2^2\right],
$$

with rounding replaced by uniform-noise injection during training. Models train on 256×256 Vimeo-90K patches (Adam) over a $\lambda$ ladder covering 0.05–1.0 bpp [2]; evaluation on the 24 Kodak images reports bpp, PSNR (RGB), and MS-SSIM in dB.

### 3.4 Discretization of the conditional Gaussian

The continuous density $p(y_i \mid \hat{z})$ must become a probability *mass* on integers; following [1] we convolve with the unit uniform:

$$
P(\hat{y}_i = k \mid \hat{z}) = \int_{k-\frac{1}{2}}^{k+\frac{1}{2}} \mathcal{N}(t; \mu_i, \sigma_i^2)\, dt = \Phi\!\left(\tfrac{k+\frac{1}{2}-\mu_i}{\sigma_i}\right) - \Phi\!\left(\tfrac{k-\frac{1}{2}-\mu_i}{\sigma_i}\right),
$$

where $\Phi$ is the standard Gaussian CDF. Training-time density and test-time PMF then coincide exactly, so the optimized rate is the *true* expected bit rate.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Rate–distortion optimization as Lagrangian relaxation

The constrained problem $\min R$ s.t. $D \le D_0$ is non-convex in the parameters of deep transforms, so we solve its Lagrangian relaxation $\min_\phi R(\phi) + \lambda D(\phi)$ for fixed $\lambda$. Each $\lambda$ yields one point on the convex hull of the achievable R–D region; sweeping $\lambda$ traces the operational frontier. Three observations deserve emphasis:

1. **The multiplier is the price of fidelity.** Because transforms are re-optimized per $\lambda$, each point is the *envelope* over architectures — which is why learned codecs can cross the curves of fixed codecs.
2. **Perceptual losses reshape the frontier's meaning.** Training with MS-SSIM optimizes a different $R(D)$ than MSE; cross-metric comparisons are category errors. Learned models lead MS-SSIM frontiers earlier than PSNR ones [1].
> **Theorem:** *For the uniform-noise training surrogate, the expected rate term equals the expected code length of the rounded latents under the discretized prior.* Formally, with $\tilde{y} = y + \mathcal{U}(-\tfrac12,\tfrac12)$ and $P(\hat{y}) = (p * \mathcal{U})(\hat{y})$, we have $\mathbb{E}[-\log_2 p_{\tilde{y}}(\tilde{y})] = H(\hat{y}) + D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P_{\hat{y}} \| P)$, so minimizing the surrogate rate minimizes an upper bound on the true entropy, tight when the learned prior matches the empirical latent distribution.

### 4.2 Factorized priors and their information-theoretic ceiling

The factorized prior [3] models each latent channel with an independent univariate density — computationally ideal, since all $\hat{y}_i$ entropy-code in parallel with static tables. But natural images induce strong *spatial* dependencies in $y$. By the chain rule,

$$
H(\hat{y}) = \sum_i H(\hat{y}_i) - \sum_i I(\hat{y}_i; \hat{y}_{\setminus i} \mid \text{others}) \le \sum_i H(\hat{y}_i),
$$

with strict inequality whenever dependencies exist. The gap — unexploited total correlation — costs roughly 10–20% excess rate on Kodak [1]. The factorized prior is a *ceiling on simplicity, not performance*: the baseline every conditional model must beat, and the right choice for latency-critical decoding.

### 4.3 The scale hyperprior: side information as a prior on entropy-model parameters

The scale hyperprior's [1] key insight is statistical: conditioned on local image content, latent coefficients are approximately Gaussian, but their *variance* varies spatially — smooth sky regions produce near-zero coefficients while textured regions produce large ones. A single global prior cannot express this; a per-location scale can. The hyper-latent $\hat{z}$ (typically 192 channels at 1/64 spatial resolution, i.e., ~1/64 the size of $\hat{y}$) encodes a low-resolution *variance map*, and $h_s$ upsamples it to per-coefficient scales $\sigma_i$.

Information-theoretically:

$$
H(\hat{y}) \;\to\; H(\hat{z}) + H(\hat{y} \mid \hat{z}),
$$

profitable whenever $I(\hat{y}; \hat{z}) > H(\hat{z})$. Empirically the scale hyperprior cuts ~15% rate over the factorized model at matched PSNR — the first learned model to surpass all published ANN-based methods on PSNR [1]; the mean–scale extension [2] adds $\mu_i$ prediction for a further ~5%.

### 4.4 Autoregressive context models and the decode-time complexity trade-off

The autoregressive context model [2] predicts each $\hat{y}_i$ from its *causal* spatial neighbors $\hat{y}_{<i}$ using masked convolutions (typically 5×5), producing context features $c_i$ that are concatenated with the hyperprior output before parameter prediction:

$$
p(\hat{y}_i \mid \hat{z}, \hat{y}_{<i}) = \mathrm{GMM}\!\left(\hat{y}_i;\, \pi(\hat{z}, c_i),\, \mu(\hat{z}, c_i),\, \sigma(\hat{z}, c_i)\right).
$$

The hyperprior captures *global, low-frequency* scale structure; the context model captures *local, high-frequency* dependencies (e.g., the continuation of an edge across adjacent coefficients). Minnen et al. [2] demonstrated they are **complementary**: the joint model outperforms either alone, achieving the first learned-codec victory over BPG on both PSNR and MS-SSIM, with bitstreams 8.4% smaller than BPG and 59.8% smaller than JPEG on Kodak.

The cost is *serial decoding*: $\hat{y}_i$ cannot be decoded until $\hat{y}_{<i}$ are known, turning an embarrassingly parallel workload into a sequential scan over $\sim$12k spatial positions per channel. Throughput drops by one to two orders of magnitude relative to the hyperprior-only model. Mitigations include:

Mitigations include channel-wise autoregression (parallelism over space), checkerboard/wavefront schedules (halving the critical path), and hybrid deployment (hyperprior-only for real-time decode, joint for archival encode). The fundamental tension: *every nat of conditional entropy saved by a richer context is paid for in decoding latency.*

### 4.5 Asymmetric numeral systems: stack-based entropy coding at Shannon efficiency

Given the per-symbol PMFs $P(\hat{y}_i \mid \cdot)$ from the entropy model, ANS [5] converts them to bits. The range variant (rANS) maintains state $s$ and encodes symbol $x$ with quantized probability $p_x = f_x / M$ via

```python
def rans_encode_step(s: int, x: int, freq: list[int], M: int, renorm_out: list[int]) -> int:
    # freq[x]: quantized symbol frequency, sum(freq) == M (power of two)
    while s >= (1 << 23) * M // freq[x]:   # renormalization: emit bits
        renorm_out.append(s & 1)
        s >>= 1
    s = (s // freq[x]) * M + (s % freq[x]) + sum(freq[:x])
    return s
```

Decoding inverts the mapping with a single division/modulo per symbol — no interval arithmetic, no multiplications in the fast path — and emits symbols in **reverse** order (LIFO). The expected code length satisfies

$$
\mathbb{E}[L] \le H(X) + \frac{\log_2 s_{\max}}{n} + \epsilon_{\text{quant}},
$$

where the overhead vanishes as $1/n$ with sequence length $n$ and $\epsilon_{\text{quant}}$ is the probability-quantization loss (negligible for $M \ge 2^{12}$) [5][7]. In practice, learned codecs using rANS or range coding achieve within ~1% of the cross-entropy rate — the entropy model, not the coder, dominates the rate.

The LIFO discipline is pure bookkeeping for autoregressive contexts: the encoder emits symbols back-to-front with no rate consequence [8]. tANS replaces arithmetic with table lookups at Huffman-competitive throughput [5] — the configuration of choice at sub-0.1 bpp, where PMFs are extremely sharp (most mass on $\hat{y}_i = 0$).

We evaluate the ladder — Factorized [3], Scale Hyperprior [1], Mean–Scale [2], Joint [2], Cheng GMM+attention [4] — against JPEG, JPEG 2000, WebP, and BPG (4:4:4) on the 24 Kodak images, following [1][2]. Rate in bpp; distortion in PSNR (RGB) and MS-SSIM (dB).

**First**, the joint model is the first learned codec to beat BPG on *both* PSNR and MS-SSIM [2]. **Second**, on MS-SSIM the learned models lead by ~2 dB even at 0.1 bpp. **Third**, at 0.1 bpp learned reconstructions stay recognizable and artifact-light where JPEG shows severe blocking. BD-rate over 0.05–0.8 bpp: the joint model saves ~13% vs. BPG and ~60% vs. JPEG at matched PSNR, consistent with [2].

### 5.3 Ablation: where do the bits go?

At $\lambda$ tuned for 0.15 bpp on Kodak, a typical bit allocation is:

| Component | Share of bitstream | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| $\hat{z}$ side information | 3–6% | Grows slowly with rate |
| $\hat{y}$ conditional on hyperprior only | ~72% | Dominant term |
| Additional saving from AR context | −8 to −12% | Relative to hyperprior-only |
| ANS/range coder overhead | <1% | Vs. cross-entropy |

The hyperprior's variance maps are interpretable: high $\sigma_i$ at texture and edges, low $\sigma_i$ at smooth gradients — the network rediscovers spatially adaptive bit allocation from data alone.

### 5.4 Proof sketch: optimality of the Lagrangian frontier

> **Theorem:** *Let $\mathcal{F}$ be the class of achievable $(R, D)$ pairs for a fixed architecture family. The lower convex envelope of $\mathcal{F}$ is exactly the set of solutions to $\min R + \lambda D$ as $\lambda$ ranges over $(0, \infty)$.*

*Proof sketch.* Each $\lambda$-minimizer supports the R–D epigraph with a line of slope $-1/\lambda$; no achievable point can lie below it. Conversely every envelope point is a supporting point for some slope. ∎ Corollary: the upper envelope over a $\lambda$ grid is the operational frontier.

## 6. Limitations

1. **Decode complexity.** Autoregressive contexts serialize decoding; even hyperprior-only models need GPU-class hardware to match hand-optimized CPU codecs. Real-time 4K decode remains out of reach for the best variants [2].
2. **Model proliferation.** The $\lambda$-ladder multiplies storage costs; conditional variable-rate models [2] mitigate this at a 2–5% BD-rate penalty.
3. **Domain shift.** Models trained on photographic data degrade on screen captures, medical imagery, and line art, where classical codecs' weaker assumptions are more robust.
4. **Metric dependence.** PSNR-optimal and MS-SSIM-optimal models differ; "state of the art" must name the distortion [1].
5. **Ecosystem.** No standard bitstream syntax, no hardware decoders, no clear patent landscape — adoption barriers independent of R–D performance.
6. **Quantization mismatch.** The uniform-noise surrogate is exact only at integer centers; learned quantization needs biased straight-through estimators.

## 7. Conclusion

Learned lossy image compression is *variational inference with a bitstream attached*: analysis transform as encoder, entropy model as prior, rate as cross-entropy, distortion as log-likelihood. The field's history is a progressive enrichment of the prior — factorized [3], scale hyperprior [1], mean–scale and autoregressive [2], Gaussian mixtures with attention [4] — each step converting unexploited dependence into rate savings, each paid for in decoding complexity. ANS [5] closes the loop at near-Shannon efficiency and Huffman-like speed.

The verdict is unambiguous: joint hyperprior–autoregressive models outperform BPG at matched distortion, with their largest perceptual advantages in the sub-0.1 bpp regime [2][4]. What remains is engineering: parallelizable contexts, variable-rate conditioning, integer arithmetic, standardized bitstreams.
