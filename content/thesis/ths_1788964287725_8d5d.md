# Fully Homomorphic Encryption Schemes: BGV, BFV, and CKKS from Ring-LWE, Modulus Switching, and SIMD Ciphertext Packing

## Abstract

Fully homomorphic encryption (FHE) permits arbitrary computation on encrypted data without ever decrypting it, a capability that moved from theoretical curiosity to deployable primitive with Gentry's 2009 construction [1]. This article develops the three dominant modern leveled FHE schemes — Brakerski–Gentry–Vaikuntanathan (BGV), Brakerski–Fan–Vercauteren (BFV), and Cheon–Kim–Kim–Song (CKKS) — from their common foundation: the Ring Learning With Errors (RLWE) problem over cyclotomic rings. We explain how modulus switching and rescaling keep ciphertext noise bounded across a leveled circuit, how bootstrapping converts leveled schemes into fully homomorphic ones, how the Chinese Remainder Theorem yields SIMD batching of thousands of plaintexts into a single ciphertext, and how noise analysis dictates parameter selection. We compare the schemes' plaintext spaces, noise growth, and bootstrapping costs, sketch an RLWE implementation in Rust, and review standardized parameter sets and security levels [7]. The goal is a self-contained, practitioner-facing account of why these three schemes work, where they differ, and how to choose among them.

## 1 Introduction

The dream of computing on encrypted data predates practical cryptography: Rivest, Adleman, and Dertouzos posed the problem of "privacy homomorphisms" in 1978, and RSA itself is multiplicatively homomorphic. Yet for three decades the achievable homomorphism was *partial* — a single operation type, or a bounded number of operations — and the general problem remained open until Craig Gentry's doctoral thesis [1] constructed a *fully* homomorphic scheme supporting arbitrary circuits. Gentry's blueprint — a *somewhat* homomorphic scheme that can evaluate its own decryption circuit, plus the *bootstrapping* theorem converting it to full homomorphism — remains the template for every generation of FHE since.

The second generation, beginning with Brakerski and Vaikuntanathan [2], replaced Gentry's ideal-lattice machinery with the (Ring-)Learning With Errors assumption, giving cleaner constructions, tighter noise analyses, and — crucially — *leveled* homomorphism: a scheme that evaluates circuits of a priori bounded depth without the expensive bootstrapping step. Brakerski, Gentry, and Vaikuntanathan refined this into BGV with modulus switching [3]; Brakerski's 2012 variant removed modulus switching in favor of a classical worst-case reduction [4]; Fan and Vercauteren made the BFV variant practical and easy to implement [5]. In 2017, Cheon, Kim, Kim, and Song broke the exact-arithmetic mold with CKKS, which computes on *approximate* real and complex numbers — the native domain of machine learning and signal processing [6].

These three schemes now power nearly every FHE library (SEAL, OpenFHE, HElib, Lattigo) and application, and their parameters are governed by a community standard [7]. This article explains them from first principles.

## 2 Background

### Lattices and (Ring-)LWE

A lattice is the set of all integer linear combinations of a basis of vectors in $\mathbb{R}^n$. The *Learning With Errors* problem (LWE), introduced by Regev, asks an adversary to distinguish samples $(\mathbf{a}_i, \langle \mathbf{a}_i, \mathbf{s}\rangle + e_i)$ — where $\mathbf{a}_i$ is uniform, $\mathbf{s}$ is a secret vector, and $e_i$ is small noise from a narrow distribution — from uniform random pairs. LWE is as hard as worst-case lattice problems such as GapSVP and SIVP via quantum reductions, and is believed resistant to quantum attacks.

*Ring-LWE* (RLWE) [2] transports this problem to polynomial rings: work in $R_q = \mathbb{Z}_q[x]/(x^n+1)$ for $n$ a power of two, with samples $(a, a\cdot s + e)$ where $a$ is uniform in $R_q$, $s$ is the secret polynomial, and $e$ is sampled coefficient-wise from a narrow error distribution $\chi$. RLWE inherits hardness from worst-case problems on *ideal lattices* (lattices corresponding to ideals of the ring), with the caveat that the reduction targets a restricted lattice class.

> **Theorem:** (RLWE worst-case to average-case reduction [2]). *For suitable parameters — $n$ a power of two, $q$ prime with $q \equiv 1 \pmod{2n}$, and error width $\alpha q \ge \omega(\sqrt{\log n})$ — solving decision-RLWE on average is at least as hard as approximating the shortest vector on worst-case ideal lattices to within polynomial factors via a quantum reduction.*

This reduction is the security anchor of BGV, BFV, and CKKS alike. The cyclotomic ring $R = \mathbb{Z}[x]/(x^n+1)$ is chosen because its algebraic structure supports both efficient NTT-based polynomial multiplication and the CRT decomposition underlying SIMD batching.

### Somewhat, leveled, and fully homomorphic encryption

Every RLWE scheme encrypts a message $m$ by hiding it inside a noisy linear equation. Homomorphic addition is easy: adding ciphertexts adds the underlying messages and adds the noise terms. Homomorphic *multiplication* is the hard operation — it multiplies noise terms too, causing noise to grow roughly quadratically (or worse). When noise exceeds $q/2$ (or the scheme's correctness bound), decryption fails. Three regimes result:

- **Somewhat homomorphic (SHE):** supports circuits of bounded depth determined by parameters; no refresh mechanism.
- **Leveled homomorphic:** supports circuits of depth up to a parameter $L$ chosen at key-generation time, typically via *modulus switching* or *rescaling* that reduces noise after each multiplication level. No bootstrapping is needed, and most practical applications live here.
- **Fully homomorphic (FHE):** supports arbitrary-depth circuits, achieved by *bootstrapping*: homomorphically evaluating the decryption circuit on a noisy ciphertext (using an encryption of the secret key) to produce a fresh ciphertext encrypting the same message with small noise [1].

### The relinearization problem

Multiplying two ciphertexts $(c_0, c_1)$ and $(d_0, d_1)$ encrypting $m_1, m_2$ under secret $s$ yields a degree-2 ciphertext in $(1, s, s^2)$: components $(c_0d_0, c_0d_1 + c_1d_0, c_1d_1)$. To keep ciphertext size constant and enable further multiplication, this must be *relinearized* back to a degree-1 pair decryptable under $s$. All three schemes publish an *evaluation (relinearization) key* — encryptions of $s^2$ (or powers of $s$ in a gadget decomposition) under $s$ — that lets anyone collapse the quadratic term without learning $s$. Relinearization adds a controlled, additive noise term, typically analyzed via gadget decompositions that keep the expansion factor small [2][3].

## 3 Methodology

### The common RLWE ciphertext anatomy

All three schemes share the same skeleton. Let $R = \mathbb{Z}[x]/(x^n+1)$, $R_q = R/qR$. A secret key is $s \leftarrow \chi$ (often ternary or binary for efficiency); the public key is $(a, b = -(a s + e))$ or the symmetric variant. A fresh encryption of a message polynomial $m$ is

$$c_0 = a u + e_0 + \lfloor q/t \rceil \cdot m, \qquad c_1 = -(b u + e_1) = a u \cdot (\text{adjustment}),$$

in the standard form $(c_0, c_1) = (p_0 u + e_0 + \Delta m,\; p_1 u + e_1)$ with public key $(p_0, p_1)$, randomness $u$, and scaling factor $\Delta = \lfloor q/t \rceil$ that lifts the message above the noise floor ($t$ is the plaintext modulus in BFV/BGV). Decryption computes $c_0 + c_1 s = \Delta m + v \pmod q$, where $v$ is the accumulated noise, then rounds: $m = \lfloor t \cdot (c_0 + c_1 s)/q \rceil \pmod t$.

> **Theorem:** (Correctness condition — noise bound). *Decryption is correct whenever the noise satisfies $\lVert v \rVert_\infty < \Delta/2$ (BFV/BGV) or, in CKKS, whenever the approximation error remains below the application's precision budget. Every multiplication, rotation, and relinearization step increases $\lVert v \rVert_\infty$; the parameter set $(n, q, t, \chi)$ is chosen so that the bound holds for the deepest circuit the application evaluates.*

The following Rust sketch implements this skeleton for BFV-style encryption (simplified: naive polynomial arithmetic, centered representatives):

```rust
// Simplified RLWE (BFV-style) key generation / encryption / decryption.
// Ring R = Z[x]/(x^n + 1); arithmetic shown for clarity, not constant time.
struct Params { n: usize, q: i64, t: i64 } // n = ring degree, q = ct modulus, t = pt modulus

fn sample_small(n: usize, bound: i64) -> Vec<i64> { /* sample from [-bound, bound] */ }

fn keygen(p: &Params) -> (Vec<i64>, (Vec<i64>, Vec<i64>)) {
    let s = sample_small(p.n, 1);                 // ternary secret key
    let a = sample_uniform(p.n, p.q);             // uniform polynomial
    let e = sample_small(p.n, 3);                 // narrow error
    // pk = (p0, p1) with p1 = -(a*s + e), p0 = a
    let p1 = neg(&add(&mul(&a, &s, p), &e, p), p);
    (s, (a, p1))
}

fn encrypt(p: &Params, pk: &(Vec<i64>, Vec<i64>), m: &[i64]) -> (Vec<i64>, Vec<i64>) {
    let delta = p.q / p.t;                        // scaling factor floor(q/t)
    let u = sample_small(p.n, 1);                 // ephemeral randomness
    let (e0, e1) = (sample_small(p.n, 3), sample_small(p.n, 3));
    let c0 = add(&add(&mul(&pk.0, &u, p), &e0, p), &scale(m, delta, p), p);
    let c1 = add(&mul(&pk.1, &u, p), &e1, p);
    (c0, c1)
}

fn decrypt(p: &Params, s: &[i64], ct: &(Vec<i64>, Vec<i64>)) -> Vec<i64> {
    // phase = c0 + c1*s = Delta*m + v  (mod q)
    let phase = add(&ct.0, &mul(&ct.1, s, p), p);
    // round each coefficient: m_i = round(t * phase_i / q) mod t
    phase.iter().map(|&x| ((p.t as f64 * centered(x, p.q) as f64 / p.q as f64).round() as i64)
        .rem_euclid(p.t)).collect()
}
```

Homomorphic addition is coefficient-wise addition of ciphertexts; multiplication forms the tensor product $(c_0 d_0,\; c_0 d_1 + c_1 d_0,\; c_1 d_1)$ followed by relinearization with the evaluation key. BGV differs in where the message sits (it scales *down* the noise via modulus switching instead of scaling *up* the message), and CKKS replaces the integer message with a scaled complex vector — but the skeleton is identical, which is why one library (e.g., OpenFHE) can host all three.

### Analytical method

Our comparison proceeds by tracking, for each scheme, (i) the plaintext space and encoding map, (ii) the noise invariant maintained across levels, (iii) the noise-growth recurrence under multiplication, (iv) the cost and frequency of bootstrapping, and (v) the standardized parameter sets achieving 128-bit security [7]. Noise is measured in the canonical embedding norm or the coefficient $\ell_\infty$ norm as appropriate; both are related by the ring's expansion factor.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 BGV: modulus switching and leveled circuits

BGV [3] keeps the message in the *least* significant bits: a ciphertext at level $i$ with modulus $q_i$ satisfies $c_0 + c_1 s = m + t\cdot e' \pmod{q_i}$ — the message rides *below* the noise, separated by the plaintext modulus $t$. After each multiplication, noise roughly squares. BGV's signature trick, *modulus switching*, maps the ciphertext to a smaller modulus $q_{i-1} < q_i$ by scaling $(c_0, c_1) \mapsto \lfloor (q_{i-1}/q_i)\cdot(c_0,c_1) \rceil$. Because the message occupies the low bits, scaling shrinks the noise term proportionally while the message survives rounding (up to a small added rounding error). Repeating this after every level keeps noise essentially *constant* across a depth-$L$ circuit, at the price of a modulus chain $q_L > q_{L-1} > \dots > q_0$ and a large initial modulus (hence large $n$). BGV supports exact integer and modular arithmetic, SIMD batching, and — with the Gentry–Halevi–Smart optimizations — practical bootstrapping.

### 4.2 BFV: scaling up instead of switching down

BFV [5] (Brakerski/Fan–Vercauteren) inverts BGV's layout: the message sits in the *most* significant bits, scaled by $\Delta = \lfloor q/t \rceil$, with noise in the low bits — exactly as in the Rust sketch above. The advantage is conceptual and implementational simplicity: there is a *single* ciphertext modulus $q$, no modulus chain, and multiplication noise grows as roughly $O(t \cdot B^2)$ per level where $B$ bounds the input noise. A variant of modulus switching exists for BFV but is optional; most implementations simply choose $q$ large enough for the target depth. The cost is that the initial modulus must accommodate the full multiplicative growth, so BFV parameters look similar in size to BGV's top level. BFV's clean semantics made it the default exact-arithmetic scheme in Microsoft SEAL and the pedagogical reference implementation of RLWE FHE [5].

### 4.3 CKKS: approximate arithmetic over the reals

CKKS [6] abandons exactness: it encrypts vectors $z \in \mathbb{C}^{n/2}$ by encoding them into a message polynomial via the canonical embedding inverse, scaled by a large factor $\Delta$ (e.g., $2^{40}$): $m = \lfloor \Delta \cdot \sigma^{-1}(z) \rceil$. Encryption adds noise *on top of* an already-approximate encoding, so decryption returns $z + \text{small error}$ — exactly like floating-point arithmetic, where every operation carries rounding error. Multiplication of ciphertexts at scale $\Delta$ produces scale $\Delta^2$; *rescaling* divides by $\Delta$ and drops to a smaller modulus, simultaneously restoring the scale and shrinking the noise — it is BGV-style modulus switching repurposed as a fixed-point management discipline. The noise analysis is phrased as *precision loss*: each level consumes $\log \Delta$ bits of modulus and loses a few bits of precision. CKKS natively supports real/complex arithmetic, making it the scheme of choice for encrypted machine-learning inference, but its approximate decryption complicates bootstrapping: the bootstrap must evaluate a modular-reduction-like function on approximate values, requiring the sophisticated sine/cosine-based "EvalMod" procedure.

### 4.4 Bootstrapping: from leveled to fully homomorphic

Gentry's bootstrapping theorem [1] states that if a scheme can homomorphically evaluate its own decryption circuit (plus one extra operation), it becomes fully homomorphic: given a noisy ciphertext $c$ encrypting $m$, publish an encryption of the secret key $\overline{s}$, then homomorphically compute $\text{Dec}_{\overline{s}}(c)$ — the inner decryption strips the outer noise layer, yielding a fresh encryption of $m$ with small noise. The practical questions are the *depth* of the decryption circuit and its arithmetic cost. BGV/BFV bootstrapping (notably the Halevi–Shoup / HElib line and the Chillotti et al. improvements) digit-extracts the message from the noise using homomorphic digit decomposition — expensive but exact. CKKS bootstrapping is approximate and historically the hardest: modern implementations (Bossuat et al., EUROCRYPT 2021) bootstrap a full $n = 2^{16}$ ciphertext in seconds, but consume many levels doing so. In practice, most deployments are *leveled* — parameters are sized for the application's multiplicative depth and bootstrapping is avoided entirely.

### 4.5 SIMD packing: many plaintexts, one ciphertext

The cyclotomic ring $R_q$ factors via the Chinese Remainder Theorem when $q \equiv 1 \pmod{2n}$: $R_q \cong \prod_{i=1}^{n} \mathbb{F}_q$ (for prime $q$ splitting completely), and with a plaintext modulus $t$ factoring into $\ell$ suitable primes, $R_t \cong \mathbb{F}_{t_1} \times \dots \times \mathbb{F}_{t_\ell}$. This yields $\ell$ independent *slots*: one ciphertext encrypts $\ell$ plaintext values, and homomorphic addition/multiplication act slot-wise — a single instruction on $n$ data, hence *SIMD* [3]. Rotations of the slot vector are realized by applying Galois automorphisms $x \mapsto x^{2i+1}$ to the ciphertext, which is how encrypted matrix–vector products and convolutions are implemented. CKKS packs $n/2$ complex numbers per ciphertext via the canonical embedding. Batching is the single largest performance multiplier in FHE: it amortizes the cost of one homomorphic operation over thousands of slots, turning otherwise hopeless throughputs into practical ones.

## 5 Empirical / Proofs

### Noise-growth comparison

The table below summarizes the schemes' noise behavior and bootstrapping characteristics. Noise bounds are stated in the coefficient $\ell_\infty$ norm for a fresh ciphertext with error bound $B$; $t$ is the plaintext modulus, $\Delta$ the CKKS scale.

| | BGV | BFV | CKKS |
|---|---|---|---|
| Plaintext space | $\mathbb{Z}_t$ (exact, SIMD slots) | $\mathbb{Z}_t$ (exact, SIMD slots) | $\mathbb{C}^{n/2}$ (approximate) |
| Message placement | Low bits; noise above | High bits ($\Delta = \lfloor q/t \rceil$); noise below | Scaled by $\Delta$ in canonical embedding |
| Noise after fresh encryption | $O(B)$ | $O(B)$ | $O(B)$ + encoding rounding |
| Noise after one multiplication | $\approx B^2 \cdot \mathrm{poly}(n)$ → then modulus-switched to $O(B)$ | $\approx t \cdot B^2 \cdot \mathrm{poly}(n)$ | Scale $\Delta^2$ → rescaled to $\Delta$; precision loss $\approx$ few bits |
| Noise growth across $L$ levels | ~Constant (modulus chain) | ~Exponential in $L$ (single modulus) | Linear precision loss per level |
| Relinearization cost | One gadget-decomposition key-switch | One gadget-decomposition key-switch | One key-switch; cheaper gadget variants |
| Bootstrapping | Exact; digit extraction, minutes-scale historically | Exact; similar to BGV | Approximate; EvalMod via sine approximation, seconds-scale (modern) |
| Best use case | Exact integer circuits, deep leveled evaluation | Exact integer arithmetic, simpler parameters | Real-number ML inference, signal processing |

Empirically, for 128-bit security and moderate depth ($L \approx 5$–$10$ multiplications), libraries report single-threaded throughputs on the order of milliseconds per homomorphic multiplication at $n = 2^{13}$–$2^{14}$, with SIMD batching amortizing this over $n$ (BGV/BFV) or $n/2$ (CKKS) slots — effective per-slot costs in the microsecond range. CKKS bootstrapping at $n = 2^{16}$ with full slots runs in roughly 10–60 seconds in optimized implementations, versus minutes for exact BGV/BFV bootstrapping at comparable parameters; these figures improve steadily with algorithmic advances (hybrid key-switching, baby-step giant-step linear transforms) [6][7].

### Security: the Homomorphic Encryption Standard

The community standard [7] fixes parameter sets for 128-, 192-, and 256-bit security based on the LWE estimator's analysis of primal and dual lattice attacks (BKZ with sieving/enumeration SVP oracles). Representative 128-bit guidance: ring dimension $n \ge 2^{11}$ for small moduli, rising to $n = 2^{15}$–$2^{16}$ for deep leveled circuits with $\log q \approx 400$–$800$; error distributions are discrete Gaussians with $\sigma \approx 3.2$ or centered binomial equivalents; ternary secrets are permitted with a compensating increase in $n$. The standard explicitly models both classical and quantum attackers and warns against sparse secrets combined with large moduli (subfield-lattice-attack considerations for overstretched NTRU-like assumptions — not directly applicable to RLWE, but shaping conservative choices). Parameter selection therefore follows a fixed pipeline: application depth $\to$ required $\log q$ (noise analysis) $\to$ minimal $n$ meeting the security level [7].

## 6 Limitations

First, *performance*: even with SIMD batching, FHE evaluation is $10^4$–$10^6 \times$ slower than plaintext computation, and ciphertext expansion is severe (a single bit can expand to kilobytes). Memory bandwidth, not just CPU, is the bottleneck — key-switching keys for deep circuits reach gigabytes.

Second, *expressiveness*: BGV/BFV natively support only polynomial arithmetic over $\mathbb{Z}_t$; comparisons, branching, and non-polynomial functions (ReLU, sigmoid, division) must be approximated by polynomials or evaluated via expensive digit-decomposition tricks. CKKS handles smooth functions naturally but its approximate semantics break applications needing exact comparisons, and CKKS bootstrapping's approximation error compounds over repeated bootstraps.

Third, *security model gaps*: the RLWE worst-case reduction [2] is quantum but targets *ideal* lattices — a restricted class — and known attacks exploit algebraic structure when parameters are "overstretched" (very large $q$ relative to $n$). The standard's tables [7] are estimates from the best *known* attacks, not proofs; a cryptanalytic breakthrough in lattice algorithms would force parameter revisions across all three schemes simultaneously, since they share the RLWE foundation.

Fourth, *usability*: noise management (choosing modulus chains, scales, and slot layouts) remains expert work; automatic parameter selection in libraries is improving but still conservative, often over-provisioning $n$ by a factor of two. Side-channel resistance (constant-time NTTs, noise flooding for circuit privacy) is an active engineering concern rather than a solved problem.

## 7 Conclusion

BGV, BFV, and CKKS are three facets of a single idea: RLWE ciphertexts as noisy linear equations whose noise can be managed — by shrinking the modulus (BGV), by scaling the message above the noise (BFV), or by treating the noise as floating-point rounding error (CKKS). Modulus switching and rescaling convert exponential noise growth into manageable per-level costs, yielding leveled schemes that cover nearly all practical applications without bootstrapping; Gentry's bootstrapping theorem [1] stands ready to lift any of them to full homomorphism when unbounded depth is required; and CRT-based SIMD packing multiplies throughput by the ring dimension. The Homomorphic Encryption Standard [7] turns the noise analyses into concrete, vetted parameter sets at defined security levels.

Open directions are clear: faster exact bootstrapping for BGV/BFV, lower-error CKKS bootstrapping with more levels consumed per refresh, hardware acceleration of NTTs and key-switching, and tighter worst-case-to-average-case reductions for the overstretched regime. The trajectory since 2009 — from Gentry's proof of concept [1] through leveled RLWE schemes [2][3][4][5] to approximate arithmetic [6] and standardization [7] — suggests that encrypted computation will keep moving from cryptographic specialty to infrastructure.
