# Algebraic Effect Handlers: Operational Semantics, Evidence-Passing Compilation in Koka, and Effect Typing in OCaml 5

## Abstract

## 1. Introduction

Computational effects — mutable state, exceptions, input/output, nondeterminism, concurrency — have historically been handled by an unruly menagerie of language mechanisms: monad transformers, exception hierarchies, `async`/`await` sugar, coroutines, and generators. Each mechanism embeds its own semantics into the compiler, and combining them requires non-trivial plumbing. Algebraic effects, introduced by Plotkin and Power [10] and extended with handlers by Plotkin and Pretnar [2], propose a single unifying abstraction: an *effect* is declared as a signature of operations from an algebraic theory; a *handler* interprets those operations by providing, for each, a clause with access to the *delimited continuation* of the operation call.

The consequences are far-reaching. Exceptions, state, generators, probabilistic choice, cooperative threading, and backtracking all become instances of one construct [2]. Performing an operation captures the evaluation context up to the nearest enclosing handler and passes it to the clause as a resumable continuation — a form of *delimited control* [4].

But compact semantics do not imply cheap implementation: resuming a continuation may reinstall the handler, clone stack segments, or allocate heap objects per operation. Three systems embody three distinct answers:

1. **Koka (Microsoft Research)** compiles row-typed algebraic effects via *type-directed selective CPS translation*, transforming only the effectful fragments of a program [3].
2. **Evidence passing** (Xie et al. [3]) restricts handlers to *scoped resumptions* and passes handler evidence explicitly, enabling in-place execution of tail-resumptive operations and constant-offset handler dispatch.
3. **OCaml 5** retrofits effect handlers onto an industrial runtime using *fibers* (segmented stacks) with one-shot continuations, preserving backwards compatibility with a mean 1% overhead on code that never uses handlers [5].

This thesis contributes: a formalization of deep vs. shallow handler semantics with reduction rules; an account of row-polymorphic effect typing with scoped labels; a cost-model comparison of the three implementation strategies; an analysis of evidence passing with Perceus reference counting [7]; and an evaluation of OCaml 5's fiber-based handlers on the Multicore OCaml runtime [8].

## 2. Background

### 2.1 Algebraic operations and generic effects

Plotkin and Power observed that computational effects arise uniformly from *operations* subject to *equations* [10]. The theory of state is given by operations `get : 1 ⇝ S` and `put : S ⇝ 1` with equations such as `put(s); put(s') = put(s')` and `put(s); get = put(s); return s`. The theory of exceptions is given by `raise : 0 ⇝ E` (an operation of *arity zero* — it never returns). The *free model* of the theory induces the computational monad for the effect: for state, the state monad; for exceptions, the exception monad.

A signature is a family of operation symbols `op : α ⇝ β`, where `α` is the *parameter* type and `β` the *arity* — the set of possible outcomes. A *generic effect* is a function `op : α → β` whose invocation performs the operation and resumes with the result; Plotkin and Power proved giving a generic effect is equivalent to giving an algebraic operation [10].

### 2.2 Handlers as homomorphisms

The innovation of Plotkin and Pretnar [2] is to give exception *handling* an algebraic treatment, and then to generalize it. A handler for a theory *T* yields a *model* of *T*: it interprets each operation symbol as an actual function. The handling construct

```
handle M with { opᵢ(x, k) ↦ Nᵢ }ᵢ | return x ↦ N_ret
```

then denotes the unique homomorphism from the free model into the model given by the handler. Intuitively: running `M` builds a tree of operation calls; the handler folds over this tree, replacing each operation node with its clause and threading the *continuation* `k` through the clause body.

> **Theorem (Handling as homomorphism [2]):** Each handler yields a model of the corresponding algebraic theory, and each handling construct yields the homomorphism induced by the universal property of the free model.

This semantic picture yields the operational semantics almost for free: to handle `M`, evaluate it until it either returns a value `V` (apply the `return` clause) or performs an operation `op` with parameter `V` not handled by any inner handler (apply the clause for `op`, binding the continuation `k` to the captured evaluation context).

### 2.3 Delimited continuations

Handler dispatch is a form of *delimited control*. This connects effect handlers to multi-prompt delimited continuations: an effect label acts as a prompt, `perform op` as a control operator capturing the continuation up to the nearest prompt for that label, and the handler clause as the code receiving it [4]. The entire cost model of handlers reduces to the cost model of capturing, representing, and resuming delimited continuations.

### 2.4 Deep versus shallow handlers

Two handler disciplines dominate the literature. **Deep handlers** (the original Plotkin–Pretnar semantics) *reinstall* the handler around the continuation: resuming `k x` re-handles further operations with the *same* handler. **Shallow handlers** treat the continuation *as-is*: resuming proceeds without the handler — a *case split* rather than a *fold* [6].

Deep handlers express the common recursive patterns (state, exceptions, nondeterminism) directly; shallow handlers expose individual steps, useful for streaming and staged interpretation. Hillerström and Lindley proved the two disciplines inter-simulate: deep handlers are simulable by shallow handlers plus general recursion (up to congruence), and shallow handlers by deep handlers (up to administrative reduction) [6]. A language may therefore implement one discipline primitively and derive the other.

### 2.5 Row-based effect typing

Unrestricted handlers threaten type soundness and modularity: which operations may a computation perform, and who handles them? Koka's answer is *row-polymorphic effect types* [3]. A function type carries an effect row, e.g.

```
map : (a -> e b, list<a>) -> e list<b>
```

where `e` is an effect variable standing for "whatever effects the argument may have". Rows support extension (`⟨exn, div | μ⟩`) and, crucially, *scoped labels*: the same label may appear multiple times in a row, with inner occurrences shadowing outer ones, so that masking and local handling compose. Effect inference unifies rows much like Hindley–Milner unifies types, with the row tail variable playing the role of the flexible part.

## 3. Methodology

Our investigation combines formal development with implementation archaeology across three systems. We proceed as follows:

1. **Formal calculus.** A call-by-value fine-grain calculus with deep and shallow handling constructs, row-polymorphic effect types, small-step semantics, reduction rules, and deep–shallow simulation theorems.
2. **Compilation strategies.** Koka's selective CPS [3], the evidence-passing translation [4] with scoped resumptions, and capability-passing style [9].
3. **Runtime analysis.** Perceus reference counting [7] under scoped resumptions, and OCaml 5's fiber implementation [5] on the Multicore OCaml runtime [8].
4. **Empirical comparison.** Published benchmarks of handler dispatch, continuation capture, and throughput versus monadic encodings.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Operational Semantics: Operation Clause Dispatch

We work in a fine-grain call-by-value calculus where computations are distinguished from values. The syntax of handlers is:

```haskell
-- handler with operation clauses and a return clause
handle M with
  { op₁(x, k) ↦ N₁
  ; ...
  ; opₙ(x, k) ↦ Nₙ
  ; return x  ↦ Nᵣ }
```

Evaluation contexts `E` are *effectful* contexts that may pass through `handle` frames; pure contexts `F` may not. The central reduction rule is **perform**:

> **Rule (Perform — deep handler dispatch):** If `E` is an effectful evaluation context whose nearest enclosing handler frame handles `op` with clause `op(x, k) ↦ N`, and no handler frame inside `E` handles `op`, then
>
> `handle E[perform op V] with H  ⟶  N[V/x, (λy. handle E[y] with H)/k]`
>
> where `H` is the handler. The continuation `k` is the captured context `E` *rewrapped* with the handler — this rewrapping is precisely what makes the handler *deep*.

The return rule is simpler:

> **Rule (Return):** `handle (return V) with H  ⟶  Nᵣ[V/x]` where `return x ↦ Nᵣ ∈ H`.

For **shallow** handlers (`handle†`), the perform rule is identical except the continuation is *not* rewrapped:

> **Rule (Perform — shallow):** `handle† E[perform op V] with H  ⟶  N[V/x, (λy. E[y])/k]`.

The difference is a single rewrap, but its consequences are profound. Deep handlers implement *folds* over the computation tree — resuming reinstalls the handler, so a state handler's `get` clause needs no explicit recursion. A shallow handler handles one operation and then *falls through*: the programmer must reinstall the handler explicitly.

> **Theorem (Deep–shallow inter-simulation [6]):** Deep handlers with general recursion simulate shallow handlers up to congruence, and shallow handlers simulate deep handlers up to administrative reduction. Both translations are local and type-preserving.

OCaml 5 implements deep handlers primitively; shallow behavior is encoded by not resuming under the handler.

Operationally, dispatch requires finding the nearest enclosing handler for the performed operation — a dynamic, stack-walking search whose cost is a central concern of every implementation strategy in §4.3–§4.5. Note also the *one-shot* restriction adopted by OCaml 5: continuations may be resumed at most once, which rules out backtracking-style multi-shot use but enables the efficient fiber representation.

| Discipline | Continuation rewrapped? | Expresses | Primitive in |
|---|---|---|---|
| Deep | Yes — handler reinstalled | Folds: state, exceptions, nondeterminism | OCaml 5, Koka, Eff |
| Shallow | No — bare context | Case splits: streaming, staging | Links IR, Frank |

### 4.2 Row-Polymorphic Effect Types with Scoped Labels

Koka's type system tracks effects as *rows* [3]. An effect row is an unordered collection of effect labels with a tail variable:

```
e ::= ⟨l₁, …, lₙ | μ⟩ | ⟨⟩ | μ
```

A function that may raise exceptions and diverge has type `() → ⟨exn, div⟩ int`. Row polymorphism makes higher-order functions effect-generic: `map : ((a → e b), list<a>) → e list<b>` works for any row `e`.

The subtlety is *duplicate labels*. Consider nested state handlers: the inner `run_state` handles `st`, but the outer computation may also use `st` — with a different store type. Koka resolves this with **scoped labels** [3]: labels in a row are ordered by scope, and a handler for `st` captures only the *innermost* occurrence. The type of the handler reflects which instance it discharges:

```haskell
-- Koka-style: the state effect is parameterized by heap h and scoped
runState : (h : heap, action : () -> <st<h> | e> a) -> e (a, h)
```

### 4.3 Evidence-Passing Compilation

The naive compilation of handlers — capture the continuation as a heap object, walk the stack for the handler — is expensive. Koka's original strategy is *type-directed selective CPS* [3]: the compiler uses effect rows to CPS-transform only effectful code, leaving pure code in direct style. But CPS transformation is global and complicates interfacing with foreign runtimes.

**Evidence passing** (Xie et al. [4]) takes a different route. The key restriction is *scoped resumptions*: resumptions are *affine* and *scoped* — the continuation cannot escape the clause. Under this restriction, the translation passes the handler itself as *evidence* — an explicit runtime parameter — to every operation.

Because evidence is passed explicitly, **no runtime search for the handler is needed**: an operation is a direct call through the evidence vector, each effect label at a **constant offset** [4]. And because resumptions are scoped, **tail-resumptive operations execute in place** — no capture of the evaluation context is needed.

The translation is proven sound and coherent: elaboration from the source calculus `F` to the evidence calculus `Fᵉᵛ` preserves typing and semantics, and the subsequent monadic translation into plain call-by-value lambda calculus (`Fᵛ`, based on multi-prompt semantics) is coherent [4]. The restriction to scoped resumptions is the price: fully general multi-shot continuations (e.g., backtracking search resuming one continuation twice) are excluded. All *standard* effects — state, exceptions, iterators, async/await — remain expressible, and the restriction buys local reasoning: a clause cannot observe or duplicate the continuation beyond its scope.

The compilation pipeline — source to typed calculus `F`, to evidence calculus `Fᵉᵛ`, to plain lambda calculus `Fᵛ` via multi-prompt semantics, to the C/JS backend — is proven coherent at every arrow, so optimizations at any level are semantics-preserving [4]. A related design, **capability-passing style** [9], passes lexically scoped handler references instead of evidence vectors, enabling compile-time specialization.

### 4.4 Delimited Continuations and Multi-Prompt Control

Every handler implementation is, at bottom, an implementation of *delimited continuations*. The handler frame is a *prompt*; performing an operation captures the continuation up to the nearest prompt for that effect label. The multi-prompt aspect matters: with several effects in play, each handler installs its own prompt, and dispatch selects the prompt by label.

The cost model: capturing a continuation is O(1) (allocate a small object pointing at the fiber); resuming is O(1) (pointer swing); the expensive case is *deep* stacks with many live frames, where cache effects dominate — but no copying occurs. Contrast this with a CPS translation, where *every* effectful call allocates closures, or with a naive "copy the stack" implementation, where capture is O(stack size).

A subtle point: OCaml's handlers are *deep* and *untyped* with respect to effects (there is no effect system; unhandled effects raise `Unhandled`). The type system tracks only that `perform` and `continue` agree on types via the effect declaration `effect E : t`. Effect safety is dynamic, not static — the opposite trade-off from Koka.

### 4.5 Perceus Reference Counting Interplay

Koka's backend uses **Perceus** [7]: precise, compile-time-inserted reference counting (`dup`/`drop`) with reuse analysis, yielding *garbage-free* execution — objects are freed at the exact moment their last reference dies. Perceus's soundness proof is carried out in a linear resource calculus over a functional core language with *explicit control flow*.

Here is the crux: reference-counting transformations are *unsound* under general effect handlers. If a continuation can be resumed zero times, twice, or escape its handler, the liveness of captured variables is unpredictable — a `drop` at a statically determined last use may fire while a suspended continuation still references the value [4].

Scoped resumptions resolve this. Because a resumption is affine and cannot escape the clause, the continuation's lifetime is statically bounded by the clause body, and the standard reference-counting transformations — `dup` on share, `drop` at last use, drop specialization, reuse analysis — remain sound [4]. After elaboration to `Fᵉᵛ` and then to plain lambda calculus, handler frames are ordinary data and Perceus applies unchanged — including to handler frames, evidence vectors, and continuation objects, which are reclaimed deterministically with no GC pauses.

## 5. Empirical Results and Formal Analysis

### 5.1 Cost-model comparison

| Strategy | Handler dispatch | Continuation capture | Resume | Needs effect types? | Multi-shot? |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Naive (search + copy stack) | O(handlers) walk | O(stack) copy | O(stack) copy | No | Yes |
| Koka selective CPS [3] | Direct (CPS'd) | Closure alloc | Function call | Yes (rows) | Via CPS |
| Evidence passing [4] | Constant-offset load | *None* (in-place, tail-resumptive) | Direct call | Yes (rows) | No (scoped) |
| OCaml 5 fibers [5] | Fiber switch | O(1) object alloc | O(1) pointer swing | No | No (one-shot) |
| Free monad encoding | Pattern match | Tree node alloc | Tree fold | Via monad | Yes |

The table makes the trade-offs explicit. Evidence passing dominates for *tail-resumptive* operations — the common case — by eliminating capture entirely. OCaml 5 fibers dominate for *general* one-shot control by making capture O(1) without type-system cooperation. CPS is the most general but pays closure allocation per effectful call.

### 5.2 Published measurements

OCaml 5's retrofitting imposes a mean **1% overhead** on handler-free macro benchmarks, with fiber allocation on effect-heavy microbenchmarks competitive against hand-written callback code [5]. Evidence-translated Koka matches or beats its selective-CPS baseline, with the largest wins on tail-resumptive workloads where continuation capture disappears entirely [4]. Free-monad encodings of the same benchmarks are typically 2–10× slower due to per-operation tree allocation; `mtl`-style transformer stacks recover some performance but sacrifice modularity.

### 5.3 Formal results

> **Theorem (Soundness of evidence translation [3]):** The elaboration `⟦·⟧ : F → Fᵉᵛ` is type-preserving, and evaluation is preserved: if `e ⟶* v` in `F` then `⟦e⟧ ⟶* ⟦v⟧` in `Fᵉᵛ`. The subsequent monadic translation `Fᵉᵛ → Fᵛ` into plain call-by-value lambda calculus with multi-prompt control is *coherent*: different elaboration choices yield contextually equivalent terms.

> **Proof sketch:** By logical relations. Scoped resumptions guarantee the continuation is used affinely within the clause's dynamic extent, so the evidence vector faithfully represents the dynamic handler stack — the vector at any point coincides exactly with the enclosing handlers. Coherence follows because evidence is uniquely determined by the typing derivation's effect rows. ∎

> **Theorem (Perceus soundness under handlers [5, 7]):** The Perceus `dup`/`drop` insertion and reuse analysis are sound for programs elaborated through the evidence translation. Garbage-freedom is preserved: every allocated object is freed at its last use, including handler frames and evidence vectors.

The second theorem is the payoff of the whole design: it is *because* resumptions are scoped that the linear resource calculus underlying Perceus extends to handler code. Without the restriction, the liveness analysis at the heart of Perceus would be unsound, and Koka would need a tracing collector for effectful code.

## 6. Limitations and Open Problems

**Expressivity of scoped resumptions.** The evidence-passing restriction rules out genuinely multi-shot continuations: backtracking search, general coroutines yielding repeatedly from non-tail positions, and some logic-programming encodings. Whether a *graded* discipline — affine by default, multi-shot where explicitly requested — can recover full expressivity without sacrificing the common case remains open.

**Effect typing vs. advanced language features.** Row-polymorphic effect systems interact delicately with higher-rank polymorphism, GADTs, and modular abstraction. Sivaramakrishnan et al. note that the search for a type-and-effect system combining well with polymorphism, modularity, and generativity is still open [5]; OCaml 5 sidestepped it entirely by going untyped.

**Deep-handler space behavior.** Deep handlers reinstall the handler on every resumption, which can retain handler frames (and their environments) longer than necessary — a form of space leak absent in shallow encodings. The shallow-as-deep translation [6] shows this is not fundamental, but no production system performs it automatically.

**Resource management.** Handlers and *linear* resources (file handles, locks) interact subtly: a handler that discards its continuation leaks resources acquired inside it. A fully general, typed account of resource safety under handlers remains ongoing work.

**Performance cliffs.** One-shot continuations in OCaml 5 raise on double resume *at runtime*; evidence passing rejects such programs *statically* but rejects some valid ones too. Neither system gives a gradual story.

---

## 7. Conclusion

The deepest lesson is methodological: *restrictions enable optimizations*. Scoped resumptions look like a weakening of handlers, but they are what make evidence passing coherent, reference counting sound, and local reasoning possible. The future of the field lies in mapping this restriction landscape precisely — finding the weakest discipline that still admits efficient compilation — and in closing the gap between Koka's static effect safety and OCaml's dynamic pragmatism.
