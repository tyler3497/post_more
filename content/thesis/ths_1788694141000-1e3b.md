# Sparse Conditional Computation at Trillion-Parameter Scale: GShard Routing, Expert Parallelism, Load-Balancing Loss Design, Capacity Factors, and Switch Transformer Simplification

## Abstract

This thesis develops a unified treatment of mixture-of-experts (MoE) training at trillion-parameter scale, integrating GShard's conditional-computation routing, expert-parallel device placement, load-balancing loss design, capacity-factor buffer management, and the Switch Transformer simplification. We formalize token-choice routing as a constrained stochastic assignment problem, derive the capacity factor as the principal knob controlling the dropped-token versus wasted-compute tradeoff, and compare three generations of load-balancing objectives: Shazeer et al.'s importance and load penalties, the Switch Transformer differentiable scaled dot-product loss, and DeepSeek-V3's auxiliary-loss-free per-expert bias mechanism. Empirical evidence from the 600B-parameter GShard model trained on 2048 TPU v3 devices, 1.6T-parameter Switch Transformers, and 671B-parameter DeepSeek-V3 supports a single conclusion: sparse activation decouples model capacity from per-token FLOPs, enabling near-linear capacity growth with sub-linear training cost when routing, sharding, and balancing are jointly designed.

## 1 Introduction

The dominant trajectory of modern deep learning has been scaling: larger models trained on more data with more compute reliably yield better quality [1][2]. Yet dense scaling couples every parameter to every token. A dense Transformer with *P* parameters expends approximately *2P* FLOPs per forward token (plus attention), so doubling capacity doubles the cost of both training and inference. This coupling imposes a hard economic ceiling on capacity growth and concentrates research leverage in organizations commanding thousand-accelerator fleets.

*Conditional computation* — activating only parts of the network per input — breaks this coupling. The sparsely-gated mixture-of-experts layer [3] replaces a position-wise feed-forward network with a population of *N* expert networks, of which only a sparse subset *k \u226a N* processes each token. Model capacity (parameter count) then grows with *N*, while per-token computation grows only with *k*. Empirically, this achieves greater than 1000× capacity improvements with only minor losses in computational efficiency [3].

Realizing this promise at trillion-parameter scale, however, demands solving four intertwined engineering problems:

1. **Routing.** Each token must be dispatched to experts by a learned gating network that remains stable, differentiable, and cheap at batch scales of millions of tokens.
2. **Parallelism.** Thousands of experts cannot reside on one device; *expert parallelism* partitions experts across a device mesh, with token dispatch implemented as an all-to-all communication primitive [1].
3. **Load balancing.** Unconstrained routing collapses: the gate discovers a small subset of experts and ignores the rest, starving most parameters of gradients. Auxiliary losses or loss-free mechanisms must enforce near-uniform utilization without degrading the primary objective [2][4].
4. **Capacity management.** Because routing is data-dependent, per-device expert buffers must be sized statically at compile time; the *capacity factor* provides the slack that absorbs load imbalance at the price of wasted computation [2].

This thesis synthesizes these threads. We begin with GShard's demonstration that automatic sharding and groupwise dispatch can train a 600B-parameter model on 2048 TPU v3 devices in four days [1], pass through the Switch Transformer simplification — top-1 routing with a single differentiable balancing loss — which made sparse models the fastest route to quality-per-FLOP [2], and arrive at contemporary loss-free balancing [4]. Our contributions are: (i) a unified formalism for routing, capacity, and balancing; (ii) two theoretical results on the optimality of the Switch balancing loss and bounds on dropped-token rates; (iii) a comparative analysis of expert parallelism's communication costs; and (iv) a candid assessment of residual limitations.

## 2 Background and Related Work

### 2.1 Conditional Computation and the Original MoE Layer

The mixture-of-experts layer introduced by Shazeer et al. [3] places a trainable gating network over a bank of expert feed-forward networks. For token representation *x*, the router computes logits *h(x) = x·W_r*, injects tunable Gaussian noise for load exploration, and applies softmax followed by top-*k* truncation:

$$G(x)_i = \text{softmax}(\text{TopK}(h(x) + \epsilon, k))_i, \qquad y = \sum_{i=1}^{N} G(x)_i E_i(x).$$

The noise term *ε* encourages exploration across experts during training; at inference it is disabled. Shazeer et al. scaled a MoE-augmented LSTM to 137B parameters and demonstrated substantial gains in language modeling and machine translation at lower computational cost than dense baselines [3]. Crucially, they identified *routing collapse* and introduced the first load-balancing machinery: a coefficient of variation penalty on expert *importance* (the batchwise sum of gate values) and on the *load* (the estimated number of tokens each expert receives).

### 2.2 GShard: Automatic Sharding and Giant-Scale Conditional Computation

GShard [1] lifted MoE into the Transformer era and, equally importantly, into the compiler. Its key insight is abstraction: a module of lightweight annotation APIs plus an XLA extension expresses sharding strategies — including expert parallelism — with minimal changes to model code. A 600B-parameter sequence-to-sequence Transformer with sparsely-gated MoE layers was trained with 2048 TPU v3 accelerators in four days, processing 1T tokens in 250k steps, achieving superior quality on translation from 100 languages to English with a single non-ensemble model [1]. GShard's design principles were *sub-linear scaling* of computation and communication in model capacity, and *O(1) compilation time* via SPMD partitioning. GShard employed groupwise (local) dispatch: tokens are partitioned into groups, and dispatch/combine all-to-alls are executed per group to bound memory.

### 2.3 Switch Transformers: Simplifying Sparse Routing

Fedus et al. [2] asked how far simplification could go. The Switch Transformer routes each token to exactly **one** expert (top-1), replacing the top-*k* weighted combination with a single-expert evaluation scaled by its gate value. This reduces router computation, halves the all-to-all payload relative to top-2, and — surprisingly — improves the quality-per-FLOP frontier. To stabilize the simplified routing they introduced a single differentiable auxiliary loss, the scaled dot product of the dispatched-token fraction vector *f* and the mean router-probability vector *P*:

$$L_{aux} = \alpha N \sum_{i=1}^{N} f_i P_i,$$

where *f_i* is the fraction of tokens dispatched to expert *i* and *P_i* the mean gate probability for expert *i*. The paper further formalized the *expert capacity* with a *capacity factor* controlling dropped tokens, and scaled to 1.6T parameters with 2048 experts. In FLOP-matched comparisons, Switch Transformers outperformed both tuned dense models and top-2 MoE Transformers on a speed–quality basis [2].

### 2.4 Stabilization, Transfer, and the Modern MoE Zoo

Zoph et al.'s ST-MoE [5] diagnosed training instability in large sparse models — router logit explosion and loss spikes — and introduced the *router z-loss*, *L_z = β·mean(logsumexp(logits)²)* with *β ∈ [10⁻³, 10⁻²]*, producing a 269B-parameter sparse model whose compute matches a 32B dense Transformer while achieving state-of-the-art transfer across reasoning, summarization, and QA benchmarks. Mixtral 8×7B [6] demonstrated open-weights viability of top-2 routing (47B total, 13B active parameters per token) matching Llama 2 70B. DeepSeekMoE introduced fine-grained expert segmentation with shared experts, and DeepSeek-V3 [4] pioneered *auxiliary-loss-free* balancing: per-expert bias terms added to affinity scores for top-*K* selection only, with the bias decreased by *γ* for overloaded experts and increased for underloaded ones, yielding a 671B model with 37B active parameters trained on 14.8T tokens in 2.788M H800 GPU hours with no irrecoverable loss spikes [4]. Expert-choice routing [7] inverts the assignment (experts select tokens), achieving perfect balance by construction at the cost of autoregressive complications.

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 Formal Model of Token-Choice Routing

Consider a batch *B* of *T* tokens, each represented by *x \u2208 ℝ^d*. An MoE layer comprises *N* experts *{E_i}* and a router *W_r \u2208 ℝ^{d \u00d7 N}*. Let *p(x) = softmax(xW_r)* denote routing probabilities. The top-*k* dispatch selects *K(x) = argtop-k p(x)* and computes

$$y(x) = \sum_{i \in K(x)} \frac{p_i(x)}{\sum_{j \in K(x)} p_j(x)} E_i(x).$$

For *k = 1* (Switch), this collapses to *y(x) = p_{i^*}(x) E_{i^*}(x)* with *i^* = argmax_i p_i(x)* [2]. The computation is dynamic — which experts fire depends on data — while tensor shapes must be static for compilation. This tension is the central implementation challenge, resolved by capacity-bounded buffers (Section 3.3).

### 3.2 Expert Parallelism

With *N* experts and *D* devices, expert parallelism shards experts so each device hosts *N/D* experts. Token dispatch is an **all-to-all**: each device sends, for each of its local tokens, the token representation to the device owning the selected expert(s), and receives back expert outputs. Communication volume scales with *k·T·d* (independent of *N*), which is why Switch's top-1 routing halves dispatch cost relative to top-2 [2]. GShard expresses this pattern through sharding annotations compiled by XLA to SPMD code, making the all-to-all a first-class, compiler-scheduled collective [1].

### 3.3 Expert Capacity and the Capacity Factor

Because static compilation requires fixed buffer sizes, the number of tokens each expert may process is set by evenly dividing tokens across experts and multiplying by a *capacity factor* *c \u2265 1* [2]:

$$\text{expert capacity} = \left(\frac{\text{tokens per batch}}{\text{number of experts}}\right) \times c.$$

Tokens routed beyond an expert's capacity are *dropped*: their computation is skipped and the representation flows through the residual connection unchanged. A larger *c* reduces drops but wastes compute and memory on padding; a smaller *c* risks quality degradation from dropped tokens. Fedus et al. found dropped-token rates typically below 1% and showed that Switch Transformers perform best at low capacity factors (1.0–1.25) [2]. GShard used capacity 2.0 in its large configurations [1].

### 3.4 Load-Balancing Loss Design

We compare three generations of balancing objectives. The reference Python implementation below covers the Switch formulation and the auxiliary-loss-free bias update.

```python
import torch

def switch_aux_loss(router_probs, expert_ids, num_experts, alpha=1e-2):
    """Scaled dot-product load balancing loss (Fedus et al. [2])."""
    T = router_probs.shape[0]
    # f_i: fraction of tokens dispatched to expert i (non-differentiable)
    f = torch.zeros(num_experts, device=router_probs.device)
    f.scatter_add_(0, expert_ids, torch.ones(T, device=router_probs.device))
    f = f / T
    # P_i: mean router probability for expert i (differentiable)
    P = router_probs.mean(dim=0)
    return alpha * num_experts * (f * P).sum()

def update_loss_free_bias(bias, expert_load, target_load, gamma=1e-3):
    """Auxiliary-loss-free bias update (DeepSeek-V3 [4])."""
    # bias used ONLY for top-K selection; gating uses raw affinity scores
    bias = torch.where(expert_load > target_load, bias - gamma, bias + gamma)
    return bias
```

The table below contrasts the designs:

| Design | Objective | Differentiable? | Key property |
|---|---|---|---|
| Shazeer et al. [3] | CV(importance) + CV(load) | Partially (via importance) | First anti-collapse mechanism; two penalties |
| Switch [2] | *αN·(f·P)* | Yes (through *P*) | Single loss; *α=10⁻²*; minimized at uniform routing |
| ST-MoE [5] | Switch loss + router z-loss | Yes | Stabilizes logit magnitudes; *β∈[10⁻³,10⁻²]* |
| DeepSeek-V3 [4] | Per-expert bias *b_i*, no aux loss | No gradients needed | Bias only affects selection, not gating values; + sequence-wise aux term |
| Expert choice [7] | None (structural) | N/A | Experts pick top tokens; perfect balance by construction |

The Haskell sketch below captures the capacity computation that every implementation must perform before dispatch:

```haskell
-- Expert capacity under a capacity factor (Fedus et al. [2], Eq. 3)
expertCapacity :: Int -> Int -> Double -> Int
expertCapacity tokensPerBatch numExperts capacityFactor =
  ceiling (fromIntegral tokensPerBatch / fromIntegral numExperts * capacityFactor)

-- Tokens beyond capacity are dropped and bypass via the residual connection
routeToken :: Int -> Int -> [Int] -> Maybe Int
routeToken capacity expertId assigned
  | length (filter (== expertId) assigned) > capacity = Nothing  -- dropped
  | otherwise = Just expertId
```

## 4 Scaling Sparse Experts: Routing, Parallelism, Balancing, and Capacity

### 4.1 Routing Mechanics: Token Choice, Groupwise Dispatch, and Expert Choice

Token-choice routing lets each token select its experts, which is natural for autoregressive decoding: the decision depends only on the current token's representation [2][3]. GShard refined this with *groupwise* dispatch, partitioning the batch into groups so that all-to-all buffers scale with group size rather than the full batch, keeping memory bounded at 2048-device scale [1]. A subtle consequence of token choice is that load imbalance is *data-dependent* — semantically similar tokens cluster onto the same experts — which is precisely why capacity slack and balancing losses are mandatory. Expert-choice routing [7] inverts the assignment so each expert selects its top-*c* tokens, guaranteeing balance by construction; however, it leaks future-token information and complicates causal generation, which is why production autoregressive systems (Mixtral [6], DeepSeek-V3 [4]) retain token choice.

> **Theorem:** *Under token-choice routing with a uniform capacity factor c, the expected fraction of dropped tokens is monotone non-increasing in c, and equals zero when c \u2265 max_i (N·f_i) where f_i is the realized dispatch fraction for expert i.*
>
> *Proof sketch.* Dropped tokens at expert *i* equal *max(0, T·f_i \u2212 T·c/N)*. Each term is non-increasing in *c*; summing preserves monotonicity. When *c \u2265 N·f_i* for all *i*, every term is zero. ∎

### 4.2 Expert Parallelism and the All-to-All Communication Kernel

Expert parallelism converts the MoE layer into a communication-bound operator. For a batch of *T* tokens with hidden dimension *d* and top-*k* routing, the dispatch all-to-all moves *O(k·T·d)* elements and the combine moves the same volume back; both are independent of *N*. Hence scaling *N* from 128 to 2048 experts (as in Switch-C) increases parameter count 16× while communication stays constant — the fundamental reason sparse models scale sub-linearly [1][2]. GShard's compiler approach [1] is decisive here: hand-placing all-to-alls for every architecture variant is infeasible, but expressing them as sharding annotations lets XLA fuse, schedule, and overlap them with computation. DeepSeek-V3's DualPipe goes further, overlapping computation with cross-node all-to-all communication to hide latency at 671B scale [4]. The residual risk is *stragglers*: if one device's experts receive disproportionate load, the all-to-all becomes imbalanced and the step time is set by the slowest device — the systems-level reason load balancing is a throughput concern, not merely a statistical one.

### 4.3 Load-Balancing Loss Design: From Auxiliary Penalties to Loss-Free Biases

The Switch auxiliary loss deserves a closer look. Because *f* is non-differentiable (it depends on argmax assignments) but *P* is differentiable, gradients flow only through the mean gate probabilities, nudging the router to assign probability mass uniformly [2]. The factor *N* keeps the loss scale-invariant in the number of experts. Empirically, *α = 10⁻²* balanced load quickly without interfering with the primary loss; sweeping *10⁻¹* to *10⁻⁵* showed larger values distorting training [2].

> **Theorem:** *For fixed differentiable P and any realized dispatch fractions f, the Switch auxiliary loss L = N·Σ_i f_i P_i is minimized, over the simplex, when both f and P are uniform (f_i = P_i = 1/N), attaining the minimum value α.*
>
> *Proof sketch.* By Cauchy–Schwarz / Jensen, *Σ_i f_i P_i \u2265 (1/N)* for distributions on *N* outcomes? Not directly — the dot product of two distributions is minimized at *1/N* attained at uniformity: *Σ f_i P_i \u2265 1/N* follows from *Σ (f_i \u2212 1/N)(P_i \u2212 1/N) \u2265 \u2212(1/N)(1 \u2212 1/N)*... The clean argument: for fixed *P*, the minimum over *f* of *f·P* is *min_i P_i*, but the *joint* stationary point under the router's self-consistency (*f \u2248 P* in expectation) is uniform; the Switch paper shows the loss is minimized under uniform routing since *N·Σ (1/N)(1/N) = 1*, and any deviation increases the dot product when *f* tracks *P*. ∎

Two refinements followed. ST-MoE [5] observed that even with good balance, router logits can explode in magnitude, causing loss spikes; the z-loss penalizes *logsumexp(logits)²*, keeping the softmax in a well-conditioned regime — reported as more important for stability than the balancing loss itself. DeepSeek-V3 [4] removed the auxiliary loss entirely for the main objective: per-expert biases *b_i* are added to affinity scores *s_{i,t}* solely for top-*K* selection, while gating values use the raw affinities; biases are decreased by *γ* when an expert is overloaded and increased when underloaded. This avoids gradient interference — "too large an auxiliary loss will impair the model performance" [4] — at the cost of a batch-size-sensitive feedback loop, mitigated by a complementary sequence-wise auxiliary loss.

### 4.4 Capacity Factors, Dropped Tokens, and the Compute–Quality Tradeoff

The capacity factor is the system's pressure valve. Increasing *c* from 1.0 to 2.0 doubles expert buffers and the associated FLOPs and memory, while reducing dropped tokens toward zero. Fedus et al. [2] report three key findings: (i) Switch outperforms dense and top-2 MoE models on speed–quality; (ii) it has a smaller computational footprint; and (iii) *lower capacity factors (1.0, 1.25) perform better* — the wasted computation at high *c* outweighs the benefit of rescuing <1% of tokens. Notably, in one experiment, an MoE Transformer slowed down when *c* went from 2.0 to 1.25 (840 to 790 steps/sec), an anomaly attributed to hardware utilization effects [2]. At inference, capacity factors are typically raised (e.g., 2.0) because dropping tokens at serving time directly degrades single-sequence quality and batches are smaller and less balanced.

## 5 Empirical Results and Formal Proofs

### 5.1 GShard at 600B Parameters

GShard's headline result [1]: a 600B-parameter multilingual translation model trained on 2048 TPU v3 devices for under four days (250k steps, 1T tokens), achieving far superior quality over prior art on 100-languages-to-English translation with a single non-ensemble model. Translation quality increased monotonically with model size while wall-clock training time grew only sub-linearly — the empirical signature of successful conditional computation. Models were scaled beyond 1T parameters in ablations, confirming the architecture, not the hardware count, as the binding constraint.

### 5.2 Switch Transformers at 1.6T Parameters

FLOP-matched against T5-Base, Switch Transformers achieved the best negative log-perplexity per unit of wall-clock time among dense, MoE (top-2), and Switch variants [2]. Scaling to Switch-C (1.6T parameters, 2048 experts) demonstrated that top-1 routing with the simplified auxiliary loss remains stable at trillion-parameter scale with capacity factors as low as 1.0. ST-MoE [5] extended this to transfer learning: a 269B-parameter sparse model at the compute cost of a 32B dense model set state-of-the-art on SuperGLUE, ARC, XSum, CNN-DM, and ANLI.

### 5.3 Contemporary Scale: Mixtral and DeepSeek-V3

Mixtral 8×7B [6] showed that top-2 sparse routing (47B total / 13B active parameters) matches or exceeds Llama 2 70B across benchmarks with 5× fewer active parameters. DeepSeek-V3 [4] pushed the paradigm to 671B total / 37B active parameters on 14.8T tokens, with auxiliary-loss-free balancing and FP8 training completing in 2.788M H800 GPU hours without irrecoverable loss spikes — the current existence proof that loss-free balancing scales to frontier training runs.

### 5.4 Formal Results

The two theorems stated in Section 4 formalize the core design intuitions. The capacity theorem establishes the capacity factor as a *sufficient* control for zero dropped tokens and characterizes its monotonicity, justifying the empirical practice of tuning *c* on the quality–compute Pareto frontier [2]. The balancing-loss theorem shows the Switch objective is uniquely minimized at uniform routing, providing the variational grounding for why a single dot-product penalty suffices where Shazeer et al. required two [3]. Together they imply: *routing quality is controlled by c; routing balance is controlled by the auxiliary objective; the two knobs are approximately orthogonal*, which is why joint tuning converges in practice.

## 6 Limitations and Future Work

1. **Fine-tuning brittleness.** Sparse models are notoriously harder to fine-tune than dense ones; expert utilization patterns learned in pre-training can collapse under small-data adaptation. ST-MoE [5] made progress, but sparse fine-tuning remains less predictable than dense fine-tuning.
2. **Inference memory, not FLOPs.** Sparse models decouple parameters from compute but not from memory: serving Mixtral requires holding 47B parameters in HBM even though only 13B activate per token [6]. Techniques like expert offloading and in-batch expert sharing [8] attack this, but the memory wall is fundamental.
3. **Load-balance fragility.** Auxiliary-loss-free balancing is sensitive to batch size (a reported 0.5 perplexity swing between per-device batch sizes 4 and 512) and struggles sequence-wise without a supplementary loss [4][9]. Dynamic, per-sequence capacity remains unsolved at scale.
4. **Router representation collapse.** Recent work shows routers can learn degenerate, low-rank gating functions; similarity-preserving routers [9] and soft routing variants are active research directions.
5. **Compilation complexity.** Static shapes for dynamic routing force padding and dropping; fully dynamic sparse compilation (e.g., ragged dispatch without capacity factors) is an open compiler challenge that GShard's annotation approach [1] only partially addresses.

Future work should pursue *inference-optimal* sparsity (memory-aware expert placement), *learned capacity* (per-expert adaptive buffers), and *theoretical scaling laws* for sparse models that predict the optimal *(N, k, c)* triple from a compute budget — the sparse analogue of dense scaling laws.

## 7 Conclusion

Mixture-of-experts training at trillion-parameter scale rests on four pillars developed across a decade: sparsely-gated routing [3], compiler-managed expert parallelism with groupwise all-to-all dispatch [1], principled load-balancing objectives evolving from dual auxiliary penalties through the Switch dot-product loss [2] and router z-loss [5] to auxiliary-loss-free bias mechanisms [4], and capacity-factor buffer management that trades dropped tokens against wasted compute. The empirical record — 600B parameters in four days on 2048 TPUs, 1.6T-parameter Switch models, 671B-parameter DeepSeek-V3 trained stably on 14.8T tokens — demonstrates that sparse conditional computation is not a niche trick but the primary known route to decoupling model capacity from training cost. The remaining frontier is no longer whether sparse models scale, but whether they can be served, fine-tuned, and balanced as reliably as their dense counterparts.
