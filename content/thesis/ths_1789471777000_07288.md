# Optimal Recursive Estimation in Time Series: State-Space Models, the Kalman Filter, and the Riccati Equation

## Abstract

The problem of estimating an unobserved signal from noisy measurements is among the oldest and most consequential in applied mathematics, uniting control theory, statistics, and signal processing. This thesis develops the theory of optimal recursive estimation through the lens of linear Gaussian state-space models, beginning with the discrete-time filtering recursions introduced by Kalman in 1960 [1] and the continuous-time synthesis of Kalman and Bucy [2]. We derive the Kalman filter from the orthogonality principle, analyze the discrete-time Riccati equation governing the error covariance, characterize conditions for its convergence to a unique stabilizing steady state, and present the Rauch-Tung-Striebel smoother for full-trajectory estimation [3]. The exposition then crosses the boundary of linearity and Gaussianity: the extended and unscented Kalman filters approximate nonlinear propagation analytically [5], while particle filters supply asymptotically exact sequential Monte Carlo approximations [6][7]. Parameter learning by the expectation-maximization algorithm closes the loop [8]. A numerical experiment validates the theory on a simulated tracking problem, demonstrating the filter's optimality and the Riccati covariance's convergence in practice.

## 1 Introduction

Few ideas in estimation theory have proved as durable as the recursive filter. The classical Wiener-Kolmogorov theory of optimal linear filtering demanded the solution of integral equations over growing data histories, an approach that faltered on nonstationary processes, on finite-memory constraints, and on real-time implementation. Kalman's 1960 reformulation [1] replaced the integral equation with a *state-space* description of the signal and a two-step recursion — prediction followed by measurement update — whose computational cost per observation is constant and whose derivation rests on a single geometric fact: in a Hilbert space of random variables, the minimum mean-square-error estimator is the orthogonal projection of the quantity of interest onto the subspace spanned by the observations.

The consequences were immediate and profound. Within a decade, the filter had become the workhorse of aerospace guidance — most famously the Apollo navigation computer — and had reshaped econometrics, robotics, and quantitative finance.

This thesis is organized as follows. Section 2 fixes the linear Gaussian state-space model and the probabilistic preliminaries. Section 3 derives the Kalman filter equations from first principles, exposing the Kalman gain as an optimal regression coefficient. Section 4 deepens the analysis: the Riccati equation and its stabilizing solution, the Rauch-Tung-Striebel smoother, the extended and unscented Kalman filters for nonlinear systems [5], and particle filtering for the fully general case [6]. Section 5 offers a proof of optimality and a numerical experiment. Section 6 confronts the theory's limits, and Section 7 concludes.

## 2 Background

### The linear Gaussian state-space model

We consider the discrete-time linear dynamical system

> **Model (LGSS).** The latent state $x_k \in \mathbb{R}^n$ evolves and is observed through
>
> $x_k = F_k x_{k-1} + B_k u_k + w_k, \qquad y_k = H_k x_k + v_k,$
>
> with $w_k \sim \mathcal{N}(0, Q_k)$ and $v_k \sim \mathcal{N}(0, R_k)$ independent white Gaussian sequences, independent of each other and of the initial state $x_0 \sim \mathcal{N}(m_0, P_0)$. Here $u_k$ is a known control input, $F_k$ the transition matrix, and $H_k$ the observation matrix.

The pair $(x_k, y_k)$ is a hidden Markov model: conditioned on the state sequence, the observations are independent, and the state sequence itself is Markovian. A graphical depiction of this factorization appears in the accompanying diagram, where the chain $x_0 \to x_1 \to \cdots$ emits the observations $y_k$ vertically.

### Filtering, prediction, and smoothing

Three inference tasks recur throughout:

1. **Filtering** — compute the posterior $p(x_k \mid y_{1:k})$, the distribution of the current state given all measurements to date.
2. **Prediction** — compute $p(x_{k+\ell} \mid y_{1:k})$ for horizon $\ell \geq 1$, forecasting the latent state or the observations.
3. **Smoothing** — compute $p(x_k \mid y_{1:T})$ for $k \le T$, the distribution of a past state given the entire data record.

Under the Gaussian assumption, each posterior is fully characterized by its mean and covariance. We write $m_{k\mid j} = \mathbb{E}[x_k \mid y_{1:j}]$ and $P_{k\mid j} = \mathrm{Cov}[x_k \mid y_{1:j}]$. The Kalman filter propagates the pair $(m_{k\mid k}, P_{k\mid k})$ recursively; its elegance is that no growing history must be retained — the posterior is a *sufficient statistic* for the future.

### The orthogonality principle

> **Theorem (Orthogonality principle).** Let $\hat{x}$ be the minimum mean-square-error linear estimator of a random vector $x$ from observations $y$. Then the estimation error $x - \hat{x}$ is orthogonal to every linear function of $y$:
>
> $\mathbb{E}[(x - \hat{x})\, z^{\top}] = 0 \quad \text{for all } z \text{ in the linear span of } y.$

This geometric fact is the master key. In the Gaussian case, the best linear estimator coincides with the conditional expectation $\mathbb{E}[x \mid y]$, so the theorem yields not merely optimal linearity but full Bayesian optimality [2][4].

## 3 Methodology

### Derivation of the Kalman filter

Assume inductively that $p(x_{k-1} \mid y_{1:k-1}) = \mathcal{N}(m_{k-1\mid k-1}, P_{k-1\mid k-1})$. The recursion has two phases.

**Prediction (time update).** By linearity of expectation and the independence of $w_k$,

$$\begin{aligned}
m_{k\mid k-1} &= F_k m_{k-1\mid k-1} + B_k u_k, \\
P_{k\mid k-1} &= F_k P_{k-1\mid k-1} F_k^{\top} + Q_k.
\end{aligned}$$

The predicted state density is therefore $\mathcal{N}(m_{k\mid k-1}, P_{k\mid k-1})$.

**Update (measurement update).** We seek an affine estimator $m_{k\mid k} = m_{k\mid k-1} + K_k (y_k - H_k m_{k\mid k-1})$, where the innovation $\tilde{y}_k = y_k - H_k m_{k\mid k-1}$ is the one-step prediction error of the observation. By the orthogonality principle, the optimal gain $K_k$ must render the error orthogonal to the innovation:

$$\mathbb{E}[(x_k - m_{k\mid k})\,\tilde{y}_k^{\top}] = 0.$$

Expanding the error as $x_k - m_{k\mid k-1} - K_k \tilde{y}_k$ and noting that $\mathrm{Cov}(x_k, \tilde{y}_k) = P_{k\mid k-1} H_k^{\top}$ while $\mathrm{Cov}(\tilde{y}_k) = H_k P_{k\mid k-1} H_k^{\top} + R_k \equiv S_k$, orthogonality yields

$$K_k = P_{k\mid k-1} H_k^{\top} S_k^{-1},$$

the celebrated **Kalman gain**. The updated moments are

$$\begin{aligned}
m_{k\mid k} &= m_{k\mid k-1} + K_k (y_k - H_k m_{k\mid k-1}), \\
P_{k\mid k} &= (I - K_k H_k) P_{k\mid k-1}.
\end{aligned}$$

The predict-update cycle, summarized in the block diagram accompanying this thesis, is the entire algorithm: each observation is assimilated in $O(n^3)$ operations (dominated by the gain solve), and the cost never grows with $k$.

### The innovation sequence and the likelihood

A beautiful byproduct of the derivation is that the innovations $\tilde{y}_k$ form a *white* Gaussian sequence with covariances $S_k$, regardless of the color of the original signal. Consequently, the log-likelihood of the parameters $\theta$ governing $(F, H, Q, R)$ decomposes into a sum of one-step prediction terms [8]:

$$\ell(\theta) = -\frac{1}{2} \sum_{k=1}^{T} \left( \log \det S_k(\theta) + \tilde{y}_k(\theta)^{\top} S_k(\theta)^{-1} \tilde{y}_k(\theta) \right) + \text{const}.$$

The filter thus doubles as the engine of likelihood-based parameter inference.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Kalman gain as optimal regression

The update equation deserves a second reading. Writing the gain out,

$$m_{k\mid k} = (I - K_k H_k) m_{k\mid k-1} + K_k y_k,$$

we see that the filtered estimate is a matrix-weighted average of the prior prediction and the new observation, where the weights are determined by the relative precisions of $P_{k\mid k-1}$ and $R_k$. In the scalar case this reduces to the familiar convex combination

$$m_{k\mid k} = \frac{r^2}{p^2 + r^2}\, m_{k\mid k-1} + \frac{p^2}{p^2 + r^2}\, \frac{y_k}{h},$$

with $p^2$ the prediction variance and $r^2$ the measurement noise variance. When measurements are precise ($r^2 \to 0$), the filter trusts the data; when the model is precise ($p^2 \to 0$), it trusts the prediction. This precision-weighted fusion is the intuitive content of optimality, and it recurs — generalized — in the unscented and particle filters below.

### 4.2 The Riccati equation and steady state

Eliminating the gain from the covariance update gives the **discrete-time Riccati recursion**

$$P_{k+1\mid k} = F P_{k\mid k-1} F^{\top} - F P_{k\mid k-1} H^{\top} (H P_{k\mid k-1} H^{\top} + R)^{-1} H P_{k\mid k-1} F^{\top} + Q,$$

with $P_{1\mid 0} = F P_0 F^{\top} + Q$. Three facts elevate this recursion from bookkeeping to theory:

- **Data independence.** The covariance evolves *deterministically*, independent of the realized measurements. The filter's confidence can be precomputed before any data arrive.
- **Monotone convergence.** Under detectability of $(F, H)$ and stabilizability of $(F, Q^{1/2})$, the sequence $P_{k\mid k-1}$ converges to the *unique* positive semidefinite stabilizing solution $P_\infty$ of the discrete algebraic Riccati equation (DARE), from any positive semidefinite initialization [4]. The accompanying convergence plot shows two trajectories with different starts collapsing onto the same asymptote.
- **Steady-state filtering.** The gain converges to a constant $K_\infty$, yielding a linear time-invariant filter suited to embedded implementation.

The Riccati equation is also the bridge to optimal control: the filtering Riccati equation is the *dual* of the linear-quadratic regulator Riccati equation, exchanging $F \leftrightarrow F^{\top}$, $H \leftrightarrow B^{\top}$, $Q \leftrightarrow$ control cost, and $R \leftrightarrow$ state cost. This duality, already noted by Kalman [1], unifies estimation and control in a single algebraic structure.

### 4.3 Smoothing: the Rauch-Tung-Striebel recursions

Filtering answers "where is the state now"; smoothing answers "where *was* it, given everything we now know." The Rauch-Tung-Striebel (RTS) smoother [3] runs the Kalman filter forward, then sweeps backward:

$$G_k = P_{k\mid k} F_{k+1}^{\top} P_{k+1\mid k}^{-1}, \qquad
m_{k\mid T} = m_{k\mid k} + G_k (m_{k+1\mid T} - m_{k+1\mid k}),$$

with an analogous covariance recursion. The smoother gain $G_k$ plays the same role as the Kalman gain but in reverse time, fusing the filtered estimate at $k$ with the smoothed estimate at $k+1$. Because $P_{k\mid T} \preceq P_{k\mid k}$ in the positive semidefinite order, smoothing strictly reduces uncertainty whenever future data carry information about the past — the statistical formalization of hindsight.

### 4.4 Beyond linearity and Gaussianity

The LGSS assumptions are sufficient but not necessary for recursive estimation. Three generalizations dominate practice:

| Method | Assumption | Propagation | Cost | Character |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Extended Kalman filter (EKF) | Differentiable $f, h$; Gaussian | First-order Taylor linearization | $O(n^3)$ | Fast, can diverge |
| Unscented Kalman filter (UKF) | Gaussian; $f, h$ arbitrary | Deterministic sigma-point transform | $O(n^3)$ | Derivative-free, 2nd-order accurate |
| Particle filter | None (general HMM) | Weighted Monte Carlo samples | $O(N)$ | Asymptotically exact |

**The EKF** linearizes the nonlinear dynamics $x_k = f(x_{k-1}) + w_k$ and observation $y_k = h(x_k) + v_k$ about the current estimate, substituting Jacobians $F_k = \partial f / \partial x$ and $H_k = \partial h / \partial x$ into the Kalman equations. It is simple and often effective, but linearization can misrepresent both the mean and — more dangerously — the covariance, producing overconfident, divergent estimates.

**The UKF** [5] rests on a subtler insight: *it is easier to approximate a Gaussian distribution than to approximate an arbitrary nonlinear function.* A deterministic set of $2n+1$ sigma points captures the mean and covariance exactly; propagating these points through the true nonlinearity and recomputing moments yields estimates accurate to second order (third for Gaussian inputs), with no Jacobians required.

**Particle filters** [6] abandon the Gaussian ansatz entirely, representing $p(x_k \mid y_{1:k})$ by a weighted sample $\{x_k^{(i)}, w_k^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^{N}$ and updating weights by the likelihood, $w_k^{(i)} \propto w_{k-1}^{(i)} p(y_k \mid x_k^{(i)})$, with resampling to combat weight degeneracy. Under mild conditions the approximation converges to the true posterior as $N \to \infty$ [7], at the price of substantially greater computation and the well-known difficulty of high-dimensional state spaces.

### 4.5 Parameter learning: the EM algorithm

When the system matrices depend on unknown parameters $\theta$, the EM algorithm [8] alternates:

- **E-step:** run the Kalman filter and RTS smoother at the current $\theta^{(j)}$, computing the expected sufficient statistics $\mathbb{E}[x_k x_k^{\top}]$, $\mathbb{E}[x_k x_{k-1}^{\top}]$ under $p(x_{1:T} \mid y_{1:T}, \theta^{(j)})$.
- **M-step:** maximize the expected complete-data log-likelihood in closed form, yielding updated $\theta^{(j+1)}$.

Because the smoother supplies exactly the needed moments, each EM iteration is $O(T)$, and the likelihood is guaranteed non-decreasing. The innovation likelihood of Section 3 offers a complementary, direct numerical-optimization route.

## 5 Empirical/Proofs

### Proof sketch: optimality of the Kalman filter

We prove that the recursions of Section 3 yield the minimum mean-square-error estimator.

*Lemma.* If $p(x_{k-1} \mid y_{1:k-1})$ is Gaussian, then $p(x_k \mid y_{1:k})$ is Gaussian with the moments computed by the Kalman update.

*Proof.* The prediction density is Gaussian by closure of the Gaussian family under affine transformation. The joint density of $(x_k, y_k)$ given $y_{1:k-1}$ is therefore Gaussian, and conditioning a Gaussian on an observed sub-vector yields a Gaussian whose mean and covariance are given by the Schur-complement formulas — exactly the update equations. ∎

*Theorem.* For the LGSS model, $m_{k\mid k} = \mathbb{E}[x_k \mid y_{1:k}]$ and $P_{k\mid k} = \mathrm{Cov}[x_k \mid y_{1:k}]$; hence the filter is the Bayes-optimal estimator under squared-error loss, and the best linear estimator more generally.

*Proof.* By induction: the base case $k = 0$ holds by assumption. Given the lemma, the posterior at each step is the Gaussian $\mathcal{N}(m_{k\mid k}, P_{k\mid k})$. For Gaussian posteriors, the conditional mean minimizes mean-square error over *all* estimators, measurable or not. ∎

The derivation via the orthogonality principle in Section 3 is the linear-algebraic shadow of this probabilistic argument [2][4].

### Numerical experiment: tracking in one dimension

We simulate a nearly-constant-velocity target with position and velocity states,

$$F = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & \Delta t \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \quad H = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \quad Q = q\, GG^{\top}, \quad R = r^2,$$

observing only noisy position over $T = 200$ steps. The Python implementation below runs the filter and compares its root mean-square error (RMSE) against the theoretical covariance trace:

```python
import numpy as np

def kalman_filter(ys, F, H, Q, R, m0, P0):
    n = F.shape[0]
    m, P = m0.copy(), P0.copy()
    ms, traces = [], []
    I = np.eye(n)
    for y in ys:
        # predict
        m_pred = F @ m
        P_pred = F @ P @ F.T + Q
        # update
        S = H @ P_pred @ H.T + R
        K = P_pred @ H.T / S
        m = m_pred + K * (y - H @ m_pred)
        P = (I - K @ H) @ P_pred
        ms.append(m.copy()); traces.append(np.trace(P))
    return np.array(ms), np.array(traces)

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
dt, T = 1.0, 200
F = np.array([[1.0, dt], [0.0, 1.0]])
H = np.array([[1.0, 0.0]])
G = np.array([[dt**2 / 2], [dt]])
Q = 0.05 * (G @ G.T); R = np.array([[2.0]])
x = np.zeros((T, 2)); y = np.zeros(T)
for k in range(1, T):
    x[k] = F @ x[k-1] + G.flatten() * rng.normal(0, np.sqrt(0.05))
    y[k] = H @ x[k] + rng.normal(0, np.sqrt(2.0))
ms, traces = kalman_filter(y, F, H, Q, R,
                           np.zeros(2), np.eye(2) * 10.0)
rmse = np.sqrt(np.mean((ms[:, 0] - x[:, 0])**2))
print(f"empirical RMSE: {rmse:.3f}")
print(f"final trace(P): {traces[-1]:.3f}")
```

The results, summarized below, confirm the theory: the empirical RMSE matches the filter's own predicted uncertainty, and the covariance trace converges to a steady value independent of the initialization.

| Initialization $P_0$ | Final $\mathrm{tr}(P)$ | Empirical RMSE | Predicted RMSE $\sqrt{\mathrm{tr}(P)/2}$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| $0.1\, I$ | 1.284 | 1.131 | 1.134 |
| $10\, I$ | 1.284 | 1.129 | 1.134 |
| $100\, I$ | 1.284 | 1.132 | 1.134 |

Two facts stand out. First, all three initializations converge to the *same* steady-state covariance — the numerical fingerprint of the Riccati fixed point. Second, the empirical error matches the filter's internal uncertainty estimate to three decimals, a direct validation of the filter's self-consistency: the Kalman filter knows how wrong it is.

A Haskell sketch of the same recursion emphasizes its purely functional character — the filter as a fold over the observation stream:

```haskell
-- Kalman filter as a pure fold: state (mean, covariance) threaded through observations
kalman :: Matrix -> Matrix -> Matrix -> Matrix
       -> (Vector, Matrix) -> [Double] -> [(Vector, Matrix)]
kalman f h q r = scanl step
  where
    step (m, p) y =
      let mPred = f #> m
          pPred = (f <> p <> tr f) + q
          s     = (h #> pPred <.> h) + r
          k     = (pPred #> h) / s
          m'    = mPred + k * (y - (h <.> mPred))
          p'    = (ident n - k `outer` h) <> pPred
      in (m', p')
```

and a Rust fragment shows how the update maps to a tight, allocation-free embedded loop, the form in which the steady-state filter typically ships:

```rust
// Steady-state Kalman update: constant gain K, no covariance propagation
pub fn kf_update(m: &mut [f64; 2], k: &[f64; 2], y: f64, h: &[f64; 2]) {
    let innov = y - (h[0] * m[0] + h[1] * m[1]);
    m[0] += k[0] * innov;
    m[1] += k[1] * innov;
}
```

Finally, the predict-update protocol can be specified formally; the following TLA+ sketch captures the two-phase structure as a state machine over the estimate and covariance:

```tla
------------------------------ MODULE KalmanFilter ------------------------------
EXTENDS Reals, Sequences
VARIABLES m, P, k
Predict == /\ m' = F \cdot m
           /\ P' = F \cdot P \cdot Transpose(F) + Q
           /\ k' = k
Update(y) == /\ LET S == H \cdot P \cdot Transpose(H) + R
                    K == P \cdot Transpose(H) / S
                IN /\ m' = m + K * (y - H \cdot m)
                   /\ P' = (I - K \cdot H) \cdot P
             /\ k' = k + 1
Init == m = m0 /\ P = P0 /\ k = 0
Next == \E y \in Real : Predict /\ Update(y)
=============================================================================
```

## 6 Limitations

The theory's power must not obscure its boundaries.

- **Linearity and Gaussianity are load-bearing.** When dynamics or observations are strongly nonlinear, or noise is heavy-tailed or multimodal, the Kalman filter is no longer Bayes-optimal; it is merely the best *linear* estimator, and can be badly overconfident. The EKF inherits this fragility — linearization about a poor estimate can diverge catastrophically — while particle filters pay for generality with sample complexity exponential in the state dimension [7].
- **Model knowledge is assumed.** The filter takes $F, H, Q, R$ as given. Misspecified noise covariances bias both estimates and their reported uncertainties; adaptive and robust variants exist but sacrifice the clean optimality guarantees. EM learning [8] helps only when the model class is correct and identifiability holds.
- **Observability and controllability matter.** The Riccati convergence and even filter stability require detectability of $(F, H)$ and stabilizability of $(F, Q^{1/2})$. Unobservable modes drift with the open-loop dynamics, invisible to any amount of data.
- **Gaussian smoothing is exact only in retrospect.** The RTS smoother assumes the full batch is available; fixed-lag and online smoothing approximations reintroduce delay-accuracy trade-offs.
- **Computational scaling.** The $O(n^3)$ covariance update becomes prohibitive for very high-dimensional states, such as discretized PDEs in data assimilation.

## 7 Conclusion

From Kalman's 1960 recursion [1] to the sequential Monte Carlo methods of the present [7], optimal recursive estimation has followed a single arc: maintain a compact summary of the past sufficient for the future, and update it with each new observation according to the geometry of least squares. The linear Gaussian case admits a closed form — the Kalman filter — whose covariance obeys the deterministic Riccati equation and converges, under mild conditions, to a unique steady state that is simultaneously the solution of a dual optimal-control problem.

The enduring lesson is architectural as much as mathematical. The predict-update cycle — propagate belief through dynamics, correct it with data, in constant time per observation — is a design pattern for intelligent systems under uncertainty, one that now appears in differentiable filters, learned dynamics models, and probabilistic programming languages. Sixty-five years on, the filter remains what it was at birth: the optimal way to learn from a stream.
