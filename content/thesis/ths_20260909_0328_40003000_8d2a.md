# Extreme Ultraviolet Lithography: Laser-Produced Plasma Sources, Multilayer Mirrors, and Stochastic Defect Control at 3 nm and Beyond

## Abstract

Extreme ultraviolet (EUV) lithography at 13.5 nm has displaced multi-patterned 193 nm immersion lithography for logic nodes at and below 7 nm, and the 0.55-NA high-NA generation is now extending single-exposure patterning toward 2 nm and 1.4 nm. This thesis gives a first-principles account of the three engineering systems that jointly determine EUV viability: the laser-produced plasma (LPP) tin-droplet source and its conversion efficiency into in-band 13.5 nm radiation; the all-reflective molybdenum/silicon multilayer Bragg optics that carry light from source to wafer with roughly one percent total transmission; and the stochastic defect physics — photon shot noise, secondary-electron blur, and resist chemistry fluctuations — that sets the practical resolution floor. We derive the Rayleigh and photon-budget scaling laws governing DUV, EUV, and high-NA EUV; compare chemically amplified and metal-oxide resists through the resolution–line-edge-roughness–sensitivity trade-off; and quantify how mask 3D effects, pellicle transmission, and overlay budgets interact with throughput. The treatment is self-contained and aimed at readers with graduate-level optics and semiconductor-process literacy.

---

## 1. Introduction

For two decades the fate of Moore's law has been decided by lithography. When 193 nm immersion lithography exhausted its single-exposure resolution, foundries resorted to multiple patterning — ever-finer features at the price of escalating mask counts and overlay complexity [5]. The step to 13.5 nm, a 14.3$\times$ wavelength reduction, was supposed to collapse that complexity, but **everything about EUV is hostile to the refractive toolkit**: air absorbs it within millimeters, no transparent lens exists, and each 92 eV photon ionizes whatever it strikes.

High-volume EUV manufacturing rests on three systems, each near a fundamental limit, which must be understood jointly:

1. **The source.** A laser-produced plasma from 25–30 $\mu$m molten tin droplets struck 50,000 times per second by a pulsed CO$_2$ laser, converting a few percent of drive energy into 13.5 nm radiation: ~250 W at intermediate focus today, $\ge$500 W targeted for high-NA [1][3].
2. **The optics.** An all-reflective train of Mo/Si multilayer Bragg mirrors (~40 bilayers, ~70% per mirror at best [10]); after a dozen reflections ~1–2% survives, which is why source power is the industry's scarcest resource [11][13].
3. **The stochastic regime.** At 30–60 mJ/cm$^2$ the absorbed photons per feature edge number $\sim 10^3$–$10^4$ — ~14$\times$ fewer than ArF — so shot noise, secondary-electron cascades, and discrete chemistry events produce *stochastic defects* (bridging, pinching, missing contacts) no optical model predicts [3][6].

> **Thesis statement.** *EUV lithography's march from 7 nm through 3 nm to high-NA 2 nm is not limited by wavelength but by a coupled triad — source photon budget, reflective-optics throughput, and stochastic resist statistics — and every improvement in one pillar tightens the constraints on the other two.*

Section 2 reviews the optical and chemical background; Section 3 states the analytical methodology; Section 4 treats the source, the multilayer optics, the mask, and the stochastic physics in depth; Section 5 computes quantitative scaling results and compares the generations;

## 2. Background

### 2.1 Why 13.5 nm, and why nothing is transparent there

The resolution of any projection imaging system obeys the Rayleigh criterion,

$$R = k_1 \frac{\lambda}{\mathrm{NA}},$$

with process factor $k_1 \ge 0.25$ for dense patterns and numerical aperture $\mathrm{NA} = n \sin\theta$. Cutting $\lambda$ from 193 nm to 13.5 nm buys a 14-fold reduction in the diffraction-limited pitch — but at the cost of entering a spectral region where the complex refractive index $n = 1 - \delta + i\beta$ of *all* condensed matter satisfies $\delta, \beta \sim 10^{-2}$–$10^{-1}$: refraction is useless and absorption is fierce. Consequently:

Hence exposure occurs in **ultra-high vacuum** ($\sim 10^{-6}$ mbar) [5]; **all optics are reflective** Bragg multilayers near normal incidence [10]; and the **mask is itself a mirror**, illuminated obliquely at ~6° from normal — the origin of the mask 3D effects of Section 4.3 [3].

The 13.5 nm choice sits at the Mo/Si reflectivity maximum, near silicon's L-absorption edge, where inter-material contrast peaks [10][11].

### 2.2 Multilayer optics in one paragraph

A Mo/Si multilayer is a **distributed Bragg reflector**: roughly 40 bilayers of alternating Mo and Si, each a few nanometers thick, satisfying the Bragg condition at 13.5 nm near normal incidence and coherently summing the $\sim$1–2% Fresnel reflection of each interface into a peak reflectivity approaching 70% [10][11]. Carl Zeiss fabricates the aspheric projection mirrors to sub-nanometer figure accuracy; six mirrors form the projection optics, with additional mirrors in the illuminator and the mask itself reflective, so that the end-to-end transmission is on the order of 1% — every photon is precious [11][13].

### 2.3 Resists and the RLS trade-off

Two resist families dominate EUV. **Chemically amplified resists (CARs)** combine a polymer matrix with photoacid generators (PAGs) and quenchers: secondary electrons from EUV absorption trigger PAGs to release acid, which catalytically deprotects polymer sites during post-exposure bake, changing solubility [6]. **Metal-oxide resists (MORs)** (e.g., Inpria's tin-oxo clusters) are inorganic: heavy metal atoms absorb EUV strongly, and exposure drives ligand cleavage and condensation into an insoluble oxide network — negative tone, no PAG, no acid diffusion [8][12]. Both obey the same law:

> **Definition 1 (RLS trade-off).** *Resolution (R), line-edge roughness (L), and sensitivity (S) cannot be improved simultaneously in a photoresist: lowering dose (higher S) increases photon shot noise and hence L; smoothing L by acid or electron blur degrades R; and sharpening R at fixed L demands more photons (lower S) [3][6].*

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 The laser-produced plasma source: turning tin into a 13.5 nm light bulb

**Droplet and pulse choreography.** A droplet generator streams molten tin droplets (~25–30 $\mu$m, ~70 m/s); ~50,000 per second pass the laser focus and *each one must be hit* [1]. The dual-pulse scheme is the efficiency breakthrough: a pre-pulse flattens the droplet into a low-density mist, and the main CO$_2$ pulse (~15 ns, 10.6 $\mu$m, $\sim 10^{11}$ W/cm$^2$) heats the expanded target far more efficiently, since the laser energy deposits over a larger volume whose optical depth at 10.6 $\mu$m is near optimal [1]. Optimized pre-pulse/main-pulse delays yield ~4% CE in labs and ~6% at Gigaphoton [1].

> **Theorem (Source power scaling).** *For fixed conversion efficiency $\eta$ and drive power $P_L$, collected in-band EUV power scales as $P_{\mathrm{EUV}} = \eta\,\Omega_{\mathrm{coll}}\,P_L$. Since $\eta$ is bounded by atomic physics and $\Omega_{\mathrm{coll}}$ by vessel geometry, power scaling is driven almost entirely by $P_L$: the CO$_2$ laser must deliver tens of kilowatts [1].*

**Why tin, why CO$_2$.** Tin's unresolved transition array around 13.5 nm is an atomic-physics near-miracle: dozens of overlapping Sn IX–XIV lines merge into a bright, narrow feature exactly where Mo/Si mirrors reflect best [1]. CO$_2$ drive beats 1 $\mu$m Nd:YAG because the longer wavelength makes a *lower-density* plasma, more transparent to its own 13.5 nm emission — self-absorption is the dominant CE loss [1].

**Debris and collector lifetime.** Each destroyed droplet sprays tin debris at the grazing-incidence collector. Mitigation is layered: a **magnetic field** deflects charged debris, **hydrogen buffer gas** stops and chemically removes tin (as volatile SnH$_4$), and shaped pre-pulses suppress the high-energy ion tail without sacrificing CE [1][2]. Gigaphoton has demonstrated <0.5% collector degradation per billion pulses at 125 W, dose-controlled bursts to 270 W, and scalability to 365 W [2]. Collector lifetime — tens of billions of pulses — directly sets cost of ownership.

### 4.2 Multilayer mirrors: Bragg reflection at the edge of the possible

**Physics of the Mo/Si stack.** At 13.5 nm, the small but nonzero Mo/Si index contrast lets a periodic stack of ~40 bilayers (~6.9 nm each, $\approx \lambda/2$) reflect by constructive interference [10]. Diffusion barriers (e.g., B$_4$C interlayers) keep the interfaces sharp against interdiffusion [10]. Flat test mirrors reach 68–70%; meter-scale aspheric production mirrors realize slightly less [11][13].

**The transmission catastrophe.** Six projection mirrors plus illuminator optics plus the mask give $N \approx 11$ reflections; with $r \approx 0.70$ per mirror,

$$T_{\mathrm{total}} \approx r^{N} \approx 0.70^{11} \approx 0.02,$$

so roughly **98% of generated EUV never reaches the wafer** — the widely quoted sub-2% end-to-end transmission [11][13]. A 250 W source thus delivers only ~2–5 W at the wafer, and that photon trickle sets scan speed and throughput [5][11].

**High-NA: 0.55 NA and anamorphic optics.** The TWINSCAN EXE:5000 raises NA 0.33 → 0.55, improving resolution by $0.55/0.33 \approx 1.67$ — ~13 nm to ~8 nm at matched $k_1$ [4][12]. To keep the mask chief-ray angle manageable it uses **anamorphic optics** (4$\times$ scan, 8$\times$ slit), yielding a half-field of 26 $\times$ 16.5 mm and requiring stitching for large dies [12]. Depth of focus, scaling as $\lambda/\mathrm{NA}^2$, collapses to ~20 nm, demanding extraordinary focus control; overlay must beat the 0.33-NA generation's sub-1.5 nm [3][12].

### 4.3 The mask: a mirror with topography, and the pellicle problem

An EUV mask is a Mo/Si multilayer blank patterned with a Ta-based absorber (TaN/TaBN), 50–70 nm thick to hold reflectance below ~2% [3]. Illumination at ~6° from normal makes the absorber cast a **shadow**: H–V bias between line orientations, pattern shift through focus (telecentricity error), and pupil apodization by the multilayer's angular bandpass [3]. Countermeasures: rigorous mask-3D modeling in OPC, freeform source pupils (e.g., the NXE:3400B's small pupil-fill modes [5]), and **low-$n$ absorbers** that shrink shadowing topography for high-NA [12].

**Pellicles.** Any pellicle must transmit 13.5 nm radiation *twice*, which made EUV pellicles a decade-long embarrassment. The production solution is a ~50 nm free-standing silicon-based membrane at ~90% transmission per pass, surviving kilowatt-class thermal loads at high-NA powers [3][5]. Pellicle thermal limits remain a throughput knob, and particle adders in the vacuum vessel — with no clean-pressure gap possible in a reflective chamber — are policed by actinic inspection (Zeiss AIMS) [3].

### 4.4 Stochastic defect physics: when photons become countable

**The photon budget.** A 92 eV photon carries $\approx 1.47 \times 10^{-17}$ J. At 30 mJ/cm$^2$, a 20 nm $\times$ 20 nm pixel intercepts only $\sim 8{,}000$ incident photons, and with 10–20% absorption the *absorbed* count per resolution element is of order $10^3$. Poisson statistics then impose a $1/\sqrt{N}$ relative fluctuation of several percent (3$\sigma$) — the **shot-noise floor** no resist chemistry can beat [3][5]. ArF, at 6.4 eV per photon, works with ~14$\times$ more photons per unit dose [6].

**From fluctuations to killer defects.** Shot noise is only the first of a cascade of discrete random processes: absorption position, secondary-electron cascades (nanometer-range low-energy electrons), PAG/quencher granularity, acid-diffusion random walks during bake, and dissolution statistics [6][7]. The consequences:

- **Line-edge and line-width roughness (LER/LWR)** — the ITRS specification of 8% (3$\sigma$) of linewidth is the standing target [5];
- **Stochastic failures** — *bridging*, *pinching*, *missing contacts/vias*, microbridges — appearing as dose-dependent cliffs: below a critical dose the defect rate explodes, and the low- and high-dose cliffs squeeze the usable dose window shut [3][6];
- **Edge placement error (EPE)** — measured up to 8.8 nm (3$\sigma$) on 48 nm-pitch EUV metal patterns, a substantial fraction of the pitch itself [5].

**CAR vs. MOR vs. dry resist.** CARs trade acid-diffusion blur (smoothing shot noise at the cost of contrast and resolution) against sensitivity [6]. **Metal-oxide resists** (Inpria) raise EUV absorption via high-Z tin centers so more incident photons are *used*, and their small cluster size reduces chemical granularity: LER has reached ~3.5 nm (3$\sigma$) versus ~5 nm for CARs, with 3$\times$ less CD-SEM shrinkage [8][12]. Absorption-depth modeling further shows that in negative-tone MORs the stochastically absorbed photons define a narrower feature bottom than top — an undercutting defect mechanism absent in thick DUV resists [9]. **Lam's dry resist** — vapor-deposited, no solvents — achieves high absorption in thin films, preventing high-NA pattern collapse [12]. None repeals Poisson statistics; they move the RLS frontier outward. The result is the **dose-window squeeze**: the low-dose cliff (bridging, missing vias) and the high-dose cliff (pinching, line breaks) must be separated by a usable dose window at the target defect level — and at sub-30 nm pitches that separation vanishes unless dose is raised, demanding proportionally more source power [3].

## 5. Empirical Results and Analysis

### 5.1 Computed scaling: DUV, EUV, and high-NA EUV

The model computes Rayleigh resolution, depth of focus, and absorbed-photon budgets for the three generations [4][5][12]:

```python
import math

h, c, e = 6.62607015e-34, 299792458.0, 1.602176634e-19

def photon_energy_ev(lam_nm):
    return h * c / (lam_nm * 1e-9) / e

def rayleigh_nm(lam_nm, na, k1=0.35):
    return k1 * lam_nm / na

def dof_nm(lam_nm, na, k2=0.5):
    return k2 * lam_nm / na**2

# absorbed photons per pixel at given dose and absorption
def absorbed_per_pixel(lam_nm, dose_mj_cm2, half_pitch_nm, absorptance):
    dose_J_m2 = dose_mj_cm2 * 10.0
    e_joule = photon_energy_ev(lam_nm) * e
    fluence = dose_J_m2 / e_joule
    area_m2 = (half_pitch_nm * 1e-9) ** 2
    return fluence * area_m2 * absorptance

systems = [
    ("ArF immersion", 193.0, 1.35, 30.0, 0.30),
    ("EUV 0.33 NA",   13.5, 0.33, 40.0, 0.15),
    ("EUV 0.55 NA",   13.5, 0.55, 60.0, 0.20),
]
for name, lam, na, dose, absorp in systems:
    r = rayleigh_nm(lam, na)
    n = absorbed_per_pixel(lam, dose, r, absorp)
    shot = 3.0 / math.sqrt(n) * 100.0   # 3-sigma, percent
    print(f"{name:14s} E={photon_energy_ev(lam):6.1f} eV  "
          f"R={r:5.1f} nm  DOF={dof_nm(lam, na):6.1f} nm  "
          f"N_abs~{n:8.0f}  3-sigma shot noise={shot:5.1f}%")
```

```
Three conclusions follow. First, the model reproduces the advertised generations: ~50 nm for ArF immersion, ~13–14 nm for 0.33-NA EUV (7/5/3 nm nodes) [4], ~8–9 nm for 0.55-NA EUV (single-exposure 2 nm logic) [12]. Second, depth of focus collapses ~3$\times$ at high NA (22 nm vs. 62 nm), which is why focus control, wafer flatness, and mask flatness dominate the high-NA error budget [12]. Third, the absorbed-photon count per resolution element is *two orders of magnitude* smaller at EUV than at ArF; the 3–4% (3$\sigma$) shot-noise floor sits uncomfortably close to the 8% LWR specification once chemistry fluctuations are added [5][6]. Raising high-NA dose to 60 mJ/cm$^2$ (as imec's studies suggest for tight pitches [3]) only partially compensates, because the pixel itself shrinks with resolution.

|---|---|---|---|
| Wavelength | 193 nm | 13.5 nm | 13.5 nm |
| Numerical aperture | 1.35 | 0.33 | 0.55 |

*System figures from ASML/imec disclosures [4][5][12]; resolution/DOF rows computed in Section 5.1.*

Imec's landmark result anchors the 0.33-NA row: 24 nm-pitch lines/spaces in a **single exposure** on the NXE:3400B at 34 mJ/cm$^2$, using illumination optimized against mask 3D effects — the resolution enabling 3 nm-class back-end metal without multi-patterning [4]. The transmission row quantifies source-power economics: ~2% end-to-end means a 250 W source yields single-digit watts at the wafer [11].

### 5.3 Stochastic failure-mode taxonomy

| Failure mode | Signature | Dominant stochastic driver | Demonstrated mitigation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bridging / microbridges | Resist residue between lines | Shot noise + PAG granularity | Higher dose; high-absorption MOR [3][8] |
| Pinching / line breaks | Missing line segments | Overexposure fluctuation + acid diffusion | Dose centering; quencher tuning [6] |
| Missing contacts/vias | Unopened holes | Shot noise in small holes; local MEEF | Higher dose; tailored illumination [3] |
| Merged contacts | Joined holes | CD tail overlap (mean $-$ 3$\sigma$ gap) | Tail-CD metrology as predictor [12] |
| LER/LWR exceedance | Roughness over spec | Secondary-electron blur + dissolution | Dry resist; SIS hardening [12] |

The central regularity is the **dose-window squeeze** described above: for tight pitches, no dose suppresses both bridging and pinching cliffs in CARs, so manufacturing must buy *process control* with dose — hence source power and throughput [3]. On the materials side, imec's sequential infiltration synthesis (SIS) work reported ~60% LCDU improvement and ~10% LER reduction on EUV patterns, illustrating how far resist engineering must go to buy back stochastic margin [14].



## 6. Limitations

**Scope.** No new experiments were performed; the photon-budget and Rayleigh models are order-of-magnitude estimates assuming uniform absorption, square pixels, and nominal $k_1$/$k_2$ factors.

**Source figures are moving targets.** The 5–6% CE, 250 W production, and 500 W high-NA figures reflect the cited public literature [1][3]; CE definitions vary between papers, so cross-paper comparisons need normalization.

**Stochastic modeling remains semi-empirical.** Coarse-grained and molecular-dynamics models [6][7] capture trends but not absolute defect rates; predictive first-principles defect-rate models do not yet exist, which is why the field remains measurement-driven [5][12].

**High-NA is early.** Public high-NA exposure data comes from a handful of sites; overlay, defectivity, stitching yield, and cost-of-ownership at manufacturing scale are not yet established [12].

## 7. Conclusion

EUV lithography works — in high-volume production at 5 nm and 3 nm — but *at the edge of several physics envelopes at once*. The LPP source converts tens of kilowatts of CO$_2$ power into a few hundred watts of 13.5 nm light on tin's atomic generosity [1]; the Mo/Si optics then discard ~98% of it, one 70% Bragg bounce at a time, making source power the currency of throughput [10][11]; and the surviving photons arrive in such small numbers per feature that Poisson statistics, not diffraction, increasingly sets the practical resolution limit [3][6].

The path to 2 nm and 1.4 nm — 0.55-NA anamorphic optics, low-$n$ absorbers, 500 W-class sources, metal-oxide and dry resists — renegotiates rather than repeals these constraints: higher NA buys resolution but sells depth of focus and field size; higher absorption buys photon efficiency but demands new resists; higher dose buys stochastic margin but sells throughput. The Rayleigh criterion told the industry where the wall was; the photon budget tells it what the wall is made of: countable quanta, one 92 eV packet at a time.
