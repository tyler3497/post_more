# Transient Execution Mitigations in Crisis: Retbleed, Inception, and Downfall as a Natural Experiment in the Economics of Speculative Defenses

## Abstract

Spectre-BTI mitigations once converged on retpoline as a cheap, purely software defense. Retbleed, Inception, and Downfall collectively dismantled that equilibrium by showing that returns, transiently-trained predictors, and vector data paths all leak across privilege boundaries. This thesis reconstructs the three attacks, measures their mitigations' costs, and argues that speculative-execution defenses are governed less by cryptography than by microeconomics: every defense prices a scarce resource — prediction state, microarchitectural sharing, or raw throughput — and vendors optimize against disclosure risk rather than absolute security. We survey the mechanisms: Retbleed's hijack of return predictions under branch-history conditions, Inception's Training in Transient Execution with PhantomJmp and PhantomCALL, and Downfall's sampling of a shared temporal gather buffer. We tabulate mitigation overheads reported in the literature — retpoline single-digit percent on CPU-bound code, Retbleed-era IBRS switches up to 39%, Downfall microcode up to 50% on gather-heavy paths, Inception IBPB barriers up to 239% — and show why vendors prefer opt-out microcode over hardware redesign.

---

## 1 Introduction

When Kocher et al. disclosed Spectre in January 2018, the industry's most consequential engineering compromise was not the attack but the mitigation [1]. **Spectre Variant 2** — Branch Target Injection (BTI) — permits an attacker to poison the Branch Target Buffer (BTB) so that a victim's indirect branch speculatively executes attacker-chosen code, leaking secrets through a cache side channel. The software response, *retpoline* ("return trampoline"), proposed by Paul Turner, converted every indirect branch into a *return* instruction: since returns were presumed immune to BTB poisoning, the speculative frontier could be fenced with pure software, costing only a few percent on most workloads [2].

Retpoline was elegant precisely because it exploited an architectural asymmetry: the Return Stack Buffer (RSB) that predicts `ret` instructions was believed to be software-controlled and unpoisonable by the BTB training machinery. Three attacks published between 2022 and 2023 — **Retbleed**, **Inception**, and **Downfall** — each invalidate a different half of that asymmetry, and together they expose a deeper pattern: *every speculative-execution defense rests on a microarchitectural assumption, and every assumption is a bet about future reverse engineering.*

This thesis makes three contributions. First, it reconstructs the three attacks with sufficient mechanism-level detail to see why their mitigations are expensive. Second, it tabulates the measured performance costs of the mitigation stack — retpoline, IBRS, eIBRS, IBPB, STIBP, and microcode updates — across workloads. Third, it offers an economic reading: vendors face a repeated game in which each new attack forces a re-pricing of throughput against security, and the observed equilibria (opt-out microcode, default-on-but-toggleable mitigations) are what one would expect from rational risk management under disclosure embargoes, *not* from an attempt to achieve secure speculation.

> **Theorem 1 (Defense completeness):** Any software-only mitigation for transient control-flow hijack must either (a) enumerate every branch-predictor structure that can produce a speculative target, or (b) serialize every speculative control-flow decision. Retbleed, Inception, and Downfall are respectively counterexamples to enumerations that omit returns-as-indirect-branches, transient predictor *writes*, and non-branch data paths.

The remainder of this work is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews the predictor structures and the retpoline defense. Section 3 describes our methodology: a literature synthesis with an explicit threat model and cost model. Section 4 dissects the three attacks. Section 5 formalizes the empirical overhead picture. Section 6 states limitations, and Section 7 concludes.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Spectre-BTI and the predictor state

Modern x86 front-ends decouple *prediction* from *execution*. The BTB maps instruction addresses to predicted targets; the branch history register (BHR) and pattern history table (PHT) supply direction statistics; the RSB supplies return addresses derived from the architectural call stack. Spectre-BTI (CVE-2017-5715) works in three phases [1]:

1. **Training.** The attacker executes branches that collide with the victim's BTB set, installing attacker-chosen targets.
2. **Transient execution.** The victim's indirect branch mispredicts to the poisoned target; the pipeline executes instructions that encode secret-dependent data into the cache.
3. **Exfiltration.** A Flush+Reload or Prime+Probe channel recovers the secret from cache-timing differences.

The defense menu that emerged spans software and hardware:

| Mitigation | Mechanism | Cost center |
|---|---|---|
| retpoline | indirect branch → guarded `ret` | extra instructions, no BTB use |
| IBRS | restrict indirect-branch speculation in kernel | MSR writes per privilege transition |
| eIBRS | IBRS always-on, hardware-isolated | near-zero steady state, hw required |
| IBPB | flush branch-predictor state | thousands of cycles per flush |
| STIBP | isolate sibling-thread predictors | reduced SMT speculation efficiency |
| RSB filling | stuff RSB on context switch | O(RSB depth) per switch |
| Microcode (GDS) | block transient gather results | gather throughput collapse |

### 2.2 The retpoline construction

The canonical retpoline replaces an indirect call with a sequence that can never speculatively execute an attacker-controlled target [2]:

```asm
; retpoline: indirect call through %rax, speculatively safe
call set_up_target        ; architectural: pushes return address
capture_target:
    pause                 ; speculative trap: spins until resolved
    lfence
    jmp capture_target
set_up_target:
    mov %rax, (%rsp)      ; overwrite return address with real target
    ret                   ; architectural return to target;
                          ; speculative path is trapped above
```

The argument for safety was: *the speculative path after `call` predicts a return to `capture_target`, a benign spin loop, because returns are predicted by the RSB, not the attacker-poisonable BTB.* Retbleed is the discovery that this argument is false under specific microarchitectural conditions [3].

### 2.3 Transient execution beyond branches

Branch-predictor attacks are one family. **Microarchitectural Data Sampling (MDS)** — RIDL, Fallout, ZombieLoad — showed that *data* paths (line-fill buffers, load ports) also leak transiently. Downfall belongs to this second family: it does not hijack control flow at all, but samples a shared temporal buffer used by the `gather` instruction [4]. Inception straddles both: it uses control-flow hijack to leak, but its novel primitive is *writing* predictor state inside a transient window [5].

## 3 Methodology

**Threat model.** We consider an unprivileged local attacker able to execute arbitrary user-space code on the victim machine (the "native" model), with extensions to cross-VM leakage where published results exist. The victim is the OS kernel (Retbleed, Inception) or any co-located process or thread sharing a physical core (Downfall). Side-channel readout is assumed via standard cache-timing channels; we do not re-derive channel capacity.

**Evidence base.** We synthesize only peer-reviewed or vendor-confirmed sources: the USENIX Security papers for Retbleed [3] and Inception [5], the MICRO paper on Phantom speculation [6], Intel's security advisory and technical paper for Gather Data Sampling [7][8], and industry performance studies from Red Hat [9], Phoronix [10], and the Linux kernel mailing list. No new experiments are claimed; all quantitative figures are attributed.

**Economic model.** We treat each mitigation as pricing a scarce resource: retpoline prices *instruction overhead*, IBRS prices *privilege-transition latency*, IBPB prices *predictor warm-up*, and Downfall microcode prices *vector throughput*. A vendor chooses mitigations to minimize `E[cost] = p(disclosure) × reputational loss + throughput penalty`, which predicts exactly the observed behavior: ship cheap mitigations by default, reserve expensive ones behind opt-outs, and prefer microcode over silicon respins.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Retbleed: when returns are indirect branches

Retbleed (CVE-2022-29900 on AMD, CVE-2022-29901 on Intel, CVE-2022-28693 on ARM), by Wikner and Razavi, is a Spectre-BTI attack that uses *return* instructions as the speculative gadget — the very instructions retpoline converted everything into [3]. Two insights make it possible:

1. **Returns can be predicted like indirect branches.** On Intel, the authors reverse-engineered the microarchitectural conditions under which the predictor falls back from RSB prediction to BTB-style prediction for `ret`. When a return follows a sufficiently deep call stack and is paired with a *precise branch history*, the predictor serves a BTB entry for the return, and that entry is poisonable by an unprivileged attacker. On AMD Zen 1, Zen 1+, and Zen 2, the condition is even weaker: *any* return can be hijacked regardless of call-stack depth, as long as the attacker chooses the preceding branch destination correctly during poisoning.

2. **Exploitable returns exist in the kernel.** The authors built a dynamic analysis framework that scans the Linux kernel for returns satisfying the hijack conditions and reachable from unprivileged system calls — finding many such gadgets, which makes the attack practical rather than theoretical.

The irony is architectural: retpoline *manufactures* returns at every indirect-branch site, maximizing the attacker's gadget supply. The published PoC leaks kernel memory at ~0.25 KiB/s on affected Intel parts and ~4 KiB/s on AMD [11] (Intel Core 6th–8th gen; AMD Zen 1/1+/2).

> **Theorem 2 (Retbleed subversion):** Let `M` be any mitigation that converts indirect branches into returns while preserving RSB semantics. If the microarchitecture predicts some returns via the BTB under attacker-influencable history, then `M` does not reduce the Spectre-BTI attack surface; it relocates it onto the converted returns.

**Mitigation.** Intel's guidance abandons retpoline on affected parts in favor of **IBRS** (Indirect Branch Restricted Speculation), which restricts speculation of indirect branches in the kernel regardless of branch *type* [12]. Linux 5.18.14 and 5.19 adopted per-CPU IBRS selection; the cost is substantial — Retbleed-era measurements report up to **14% overhead on AMD and 39% on Intel** Linux systems [13]. Windows was already IBRS-based and needed no change — a telling illustration that the cheaper mitigation was the less robust one.

During the Retbleed investigation, the same authors also documented **phantom jumps**: by training the predictor to believe a branch exists at an address where none does, speculative execution can be triggered from *arbitrary instruction boundaries* [11]. Phantom speculation later became the substrate for Inception's TTE-RSB variant (CVE-2022-23825) [6].

### 4.2 Inception: training in transient execution

Inception (CVE-2023-20569), by Trujillo, Wikner, and Razavi, targets all AMD Zen microarchitectures and defeats *every* deployed software and hardware mitigation for transient control-flow hijack, including Automatic IBRS on Zen 4 [5]. Its novelty is not a new leak channel but a new *write* primitive: **Training in Transient Execution (TTE)**.

Classical Spectre-BTI trains the predictor with *architectural* branches executed by the attacker before entering the victim context. Defenses like IBPB flush predictor state at privilege boundaries precisely to sever this training channel. TTE breaks the assumption behind the flush:

- **TTE-BTB:** inside a transient window (opened by any misprediction, including a *phantom* one), the attacker executes branches whose BTB updates *persist* after the transient window is squashed. The kernel then becomes a confused deputy: attacker-trained BTB entries steer the victim's later transient execution.
- **TTE-RSB:** a transiently executed `call` pushes an attacker-controlled address onto the RSB; a later victim `ret` consumes it.
- **PhantomJmp / PhantomCALL:** building on phantom speculation [6], the attacker needs no architectural branch at the training site at all — a *phantom* jump conjures a transient window from a non-branch instruction, and a *recursive* PhantomCALL poisons the RSB arbitrarily deep.

The end-to-end exploit chains these primitives into an infinite transient loop leaking arbitrary kernel memory at **39 bytes/s on AMD Zen 4** despite all mitigations [5]; the artifact is public [14].

> **Theorem 3 (TTE vs. barrier defenses):** Any mitigation that sanitizes predictor state only at architectural privilege transitions is incomplete against an adversary who can commit predictor updates during transient execution, because the transient update is architecturally invisible and therefore unsanitized by construction.

**Mitigation.** The Inception paper's own evaluation shows that the only complete software stop is flushing predictor state on privilege transitions on the affected parts — i.e., IBPB on every kernel entry — at a measured cost of **93.1% to 239.2% overhead** [14]. A complete fix requires hardware changes (discarding transient predictor updates, e.g., speculative predictor structures). AMD's deployed response combines microcode updates with guidance; the performance economics here are stark: no vendor ships a 2–3× slowdown by default, so practical deployments accept residual risk.

### 4.3 Downfall: sampling the shared gather buffer

Downfall (CVE-2022-40982), disclosed by Daniel Moghimi in August 2023 after a one-year embargo, is a different species: it needs no branch misprediction and no victim gadget [4]. The `gather` instruction (AVX2 `vpgather*`, AVX-512) collects scattered memory elements into a vector register. Moghimi discovered that during speculative execution, gather *transiently forwards* stale data from an internal **temporal buffer shared across sibling threads** to dependent instructions — data belonging to other processes, the kernel, or other VMs on the same core.

Two attack variants are described [4]:

1. **Gather Data Sampling (GDS):** a highly practical attack that repeatedly samples the shared buffer, exfiltrating AES keys (128- and 256-bit demonstrated), kernel memory, and printable character streams.
2. **Gather Value Injection (GVI):** turns the leak into a microarchitectural *injection*, poisoning victim computations with attacker-influenced values.

The root cause, in Moghimi's analysis, is not gather per se but the existence of implicitly shared intra-core memory — the vector register file and its staging buffers — that was never designed with cross-context isolation [15]. Affected parts span Intel Skylake through Tiger Lake and Rocket Lake (6th–11th generation Core); Alder Lake, Raptor Lake, and Sapphire Rapids are not affected.

> **Theorem 4 (Sharing vs. isolation):** Any intra-core structure whose contents are a deterministic function of another context's execution, and which is observable through a transient data path, constitutes a cross-context leak regardless of the instruction that exposes it. Patching the instruction (gather) treats the symptom; the buffer sharing is the vulnerability.

**Mitigation.** Intel released microcode that blocks transient results of gather instructions (INTEL-SA-00828), with an **opt-out** via bit 4 of the `IA32_MCU_OPT_CTRL` MSR [7]. Intel's own guidance warns the overhead can reach **50%** where gather sits on the critical path; Red Hat's testing found severe worst-case microbenchmark regressions but low single-digit impact on realistic applications [8][16]. The opt-out bit is the economic tell: vector-heavy customers (HPC, media) are expected to trade the security guarantee for throughput — precisely the rational-vendor equilibrium of Section 3.

### 4.4 The mitigation stack and its price

Stepping back, the post-2022 mitigation stack for a Linux kernel on affected x86 hardware is a palimpsest:

1. **retpoline** everywhere (cheap, defeated for BTI by Retbleed on old parts, still useful defense-in-depth);
2. **IBRS/eIBRS** on privilege boundaries (robust for BTI, expensive per-transition on non-enhanced parts);
3. **IBPB** on context switch for untrusted tasks (very expensive, selectively applied);
4. **STIBP** for SMT isolation (moderate, conditional);
5. **RSB filling** on context switch (cheap insurance);
6. **GDS microcode** for gather (workload-dependent, opt-out available).

Each layer prices a different resource, and each was added *after* an attack repriced the previous equilibrium. The BHI episode of 2022 is instructive: retpoline *plus* eIBRS cost up to **35%** on Tiger Lake [10] — vendors ship the cheapest mitigation that survives public scrutiny.

```c
/* The defender's dilemma, in one syscall path (pseudocode): */
uint64_t handle_syscall(int nr) {
    ibrs_enter();          /* +cycles: MSR write, pipeline drain   */
    ibpb_if_untrusted();   /* +thousands of cycles: predictor flush */
    rsb_fill();            /* +16/32 stores: poisoned RSB insurance */
    uint64_t r = dispatch(nr);  /* retpoline-thunked indirect call  */
    ibrs_exit();
    return r;
    /* security: high. throughput: negotiable. */
}
```

## 5 Empirical Results / Proofs

We consolidate published overhead figures below. Numbers come from different hardware, kernels, and benchmarks — not directly commensurable, but their *magnitudes* tell the economic story.

| Mitigation (context) | Reported overhead | Source |
|---|---|---|
| retpoline, CPU-bound (SPECcpu2006-like) | 1–2% | Red Hat [9] |
| retpoline, kernel-heavy / OLTP | 4–8% | Red Hat [9] |
| Retbleed fix: IBRS instead of retpoline (Intel) | up to 39% | ETH Zurich [13] |
| Retbleed fix: IBRS instead of retpoline (AMD) | up to 14% | ETH Zurich [13] |
| BHI: eIBRS + retpoline (Tiger Lake, OSBench) | 35.6% | Phoronix [10] |
| Downfall GDS microcode, gather-heavy path | up to 50% | Intel [7] |
| Downfall GDS microcode, realistic apps | low single-digit % | Red Hat [16] |
| Inception: IBPB on kernel entry (Zen) | 93.1–239.2% | Artifact eval [14] |
| openSUSE IBRS default vs retpoline | ~21% single-thread | Phoronix [17] |

Three regularities emerge:

1. **The cheap mitigations fall first.** Retpoline (1–8%) was defeated by Retbleed; IBRS-class defenses (14–39%) survive Retbleed but not BHI-without-retpoline; IBPB-class defenses (93–239%) are the only software answer to Inception and are undeployable by default.
2. **Opt-outs track cost.** Downfall's microcode ships an explicit opt-out MSR bit; no vendor proposes default-on IBPB-per-syscall. The market reveals its willingness to pay: tens of percent, not multiples.
3. **Hardware fixes lag by generations.** Downfall is fixed in silicon only from Alder Lake onward — five years after the AVX-era warnings [18] — consistent with a model in which respins are priced against the *next* disclosure, not the current one.

> **Theorem 5 (Mitigation trilemma):** For transient-execution defenses, at most two of the following hold simultaneously: (i) pure software deployment, (ii) negligible performance cost, (iii) completeness against known attack classes. Retpoline offered (i)+(ii); IBRS offers (i)+(iii) at cost; hardware redesign is the only route to all three.

## 6 Limitations

**Measurement incomparability:** overhead figures come from different microarchitectures, kernels, and benchmarks; read Section 5's table as an order-of-magnitude map, not a controlled experiment. **Attacker model narrowness:** the native unprivileged-attacker model overstates risk for hardened multi-tenant clouds with core scheduling, and understates it for SMT-enabled desktops. **Gadget dependence:** Retbleed and Inception both require suitable victim gadgets; published gadget-finding is demonstrated on specific kernel versions, and gadget availability on other OSes and hypervisors is less studied. **Economic model simplicity:** our vendor-incentive model treats disclosure as exogenous and embargoes as fixed; in reality, embargo length, bug-bounty pricing, and coordinated-release politics shape the game. **Forward risk:** the analysis covers attacks disclosed through 2023; the predictor structures of recent heterogeneous and chiplet designs (e.g., shared predictors across chiplets) are largely unexplored in the public literature and may invalidate current mitigations again.

---

## 7 Conclusion

Retbleed showed that returns are branches. Inception showed that transient execution can *write* the predictor, not merely read through it. Downfall showed that the leak need not involve a branch at all. Each attack broke a different assumption that a deployed, performance-tuned mitigation depended on — and each mitigation that followed was more expensive than the one it replaced, from retpoline's single digits to IBRS's tens of percent to IBPB's multiples.

The economic reading is the durable contribution. Vendors do not buy security; they buy *defensibility at acceptable throughput*, repriced at each disclosure. Opt-out microcode, default-off barriers, and generation-lagged silicon fixes are not engineering failures — they are the equilibrium of a game in which the attacker moves by publishing and the defender moves by patching. The only exit from the game is hardware that makes speculation *provably* invisible — speculative predictor structures, principled buffer partitioning, or undo-based designs [19] — and the literature gives no reason to expect it cheaply or soon. Until then, every retpoline is a bet, and Retbleed, Inception, and Downfall are the receipts.
