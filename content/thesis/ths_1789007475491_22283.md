# Memory-Hard Functions: Argon2, scrypt, Balloon Hashing, and the Pebbling Complexity of Password Hashing

## Abstract

Memory-hard functions (MHFs) are the foundation of modern password hashing: they force offline dictionary attackers to spend large amounts of memory per guess, neutralizing the economics of GPU and ASIC brute-force rigs. This thesis surveys the three most important constructions — scrypt (Percival, 2009), Argon2 (Biryukov–Dinu–Khovratovich, Password Hashing Competition winner, RFC 9106), and Balloon hashing (Boneh–Corrigan-Gibbs–Schechter, 2016) — through the lens of pebbling complexity, the combinatorial framework that reduces a function's memory-hardness to the complexity of placing pebbles on its computation graph. We dissect Argon2's lane/slice memory matrix and its data-dependent versus data-independent variants, scrypt's ROMix two-phase design, and Balloon's random sandwich graphs with their proof of sequential-model security. We then review the pebbling reductions of Alwen–Serbinenko and Alwen–Blocki, including area–time and cumulative-complexity lower bounds and the practical attacks they enabled against Argon2i and Balloon. The survey closes with open problems: the parallel-model gap, side-channel versus TMTO trade-offs, and parameter selection in a heterogeneous hardware world.

---

## 1. Introduction

When an authentication database is stolen, iterated hashes like PBKDF2 and bcrypt buy only linear time: each guess costs the attacker a few microseconds of silicon, and custom hardware evaluates billions of candidates per dollar. Memory-hard functions change the cost model by binding each guess to a large working set of memory. Because memory is expensive in area on an ASIC and bandwidth-limited on a GPU, the attacker's *area–time product* per guess — the economically relevant quantity — is driven up toward the square of the defender's memory budget.

Three designs dominate the literature and practice. **Argon2**, designed by Alex Biryukov, Daniel Dinu, and Dmitry Khovratovich, won the Password Hashing Competition in 2015 and was standardized by the IRTF as RFC 9106 [1]. Colin Percival's **scrypt** (2009) introduced the memory-hardness notion explicitly and remains the most deployed memory-hard KDF [2]. **Balloon hashing**, by Boneh, Corrigan-Gibbs, and Schechter, was the first practical construction with *proven* memory-hardness in the random-oracle model while keeping a password-independent access pattern [3]. Underpinning the security analysis of all three is the **pebbling complexity** of their computation graphs — the subject of the reductions by Alwen–Blocki [4] and Alwen–Serbinenko [5], which convert statements about optimal pebbling strategies into statements about optimal hardware attackers.

This thesis proceeds as follows. Section 2 fixes terminology and the attacker model. Section 3 describes the comparative methodology. Section 4 gives a deep technical treatment of each construction and of the pebbling framework. Section 5 collects the concrete theorems, tradeoff bounds, and empirical comparisons. Section 6 discusses limitations and open problems, and Section 7 concludes.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 The offline dictionary attack

A password hash must survive the worst case: the adversary holds the full database of salts and digests and guesses passwords offline, limited only by budget. The relevant metric is not wall-clock time on a defender's laptop but the *amortized cost per guess* on attacker hardware. Two quantities matter:

- **Area–time (AT) complexity**: chip area × evaluation time, the proxy for the dollar cost of an ASIC attack.
- **Cumulative memory complexity (CMC)**: the integral of memory usage over time, a proxy for energy, which Alwen and Blocki argue is at least as important in practice as AT [4].

An ideal MHF with space parameter σ and time parameter τ should force AT complexity Θ(σ²·τ): the attacker must dedicate σ memory for τ time steps, so cost scales quadratically in memory rather than linearly in time [4].

### 2.2 Data-dependent vs. data-independent addressing

A critical design axis is whether the memory access pattern depends on secret data:

- **Data-dependent** (Argon2d, scrypt): reference indices are derived from intermediate block values. This defeats many time–memory tradeoff (TMTO) attacks, since the attacker cannot predict which blocks to keep, but it leaks the password through cache-timing side channels.
- **Data-independent** (Argon2i, Balloon): reference indices are derived from public parameters only. The access pattern is a fixed function of the salt and parameters, so cache-timing observations reveal nothing — but the attacker *knows* the access pattern in advance and can schedule pebbling attacks accordingly.

Functions with data-independent addressing are called **iMHFs** and are exactly those whose computation is a fixed DAG, which is why the pebbling reductions apply to them directly.

---

## 3. Methodology

This survey compares the three constructions along five axes: (i) construction and parameters, (ii) access pattern and side-channel profile, (iii) provable-security status and the computational model in which it holds, (iv) concrete TMTO bounds from the literature, and (v) standardization and deployment. The unifying analytical tool is the pebbling reduction: each iMHF is modeled as a DAG *G* whose labels are computed by a random oracle, and the attacker's cost is lower-bounded by the pebbling complexity of *G* [4][5]. We report bounds in the notation of the original papers (sequential black pebbling unless stated otherwise) and reproduce the concrete tradeoff factors stated in RFC 9106, which are derived from the tradeoff cryptanalysis of Biryukov and Khovratovich [6].

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Argon2: the PHC winner and RFC 9106

Argon2 won the Password Hashing Competition in 2015 [7] and was published as the informational RFC 9106 in September 2021 [1]. It takes a password *P*, salt *S*, parallelism *p*, tag length *T*, memory *m* (in KiB), passes *t*, version *v = 0x13*, optional secret *K* and associated data *X*, and a type *y* ∈ {0, 1, 2}.

**Memory organization.** Argon2 allocates *m′ = 4p·⌊m/4p⌋* blocks of 1024 bytes, arranged as a matrix *B[i][j]* with *p* rows (lanes) and *q = m′/p* columns. For parallelism, the matrix is further divided into *SL = 4* vertical **slices**; the intersection of a lane and a slice is a **segment**, and segments in the same slice are computed in parallel without referencing each other (Figure 0).

![Argon2 memory matrix](public/thesis/ths_1789007475491_22283-0.webp)

**The compression function.** New blocks are computed as *B[i][j] = G(B[i][j−1], B[l][z])*, where *(l, z)* is the reference block. *G* takes two 1024-byte inputs *X, Y*, forms *R = X ⊕ Y*, views *R* as an 8×8 matrix of 16-byte registers, applies a BLAKE2b-derived permutation *P* first rowwise then columnwise, and outputs *Z ⊕ R*. The round function *GB* augments BLAKE2b's *G* with 64-bit multiplications (`a = a + b + 2·trunc(a)·trunc(b)`), deliberately increasing circuit depth for ASICs while costing CPUs almost nothing thanks to pipelining [1].

**The three variants.** The reference block *(l, z)* is chosen from values *J₁, J₂*:

| Variant | *J₁, J₂* derivation | Strength | Weakness |
|---|---|---|---|
| Argon2**d** | first 64 bits of *B[i][j−1]* (data-dependent) | maximal TMTO resistance | cache-timing side channels |
| Argon2**i** | *G*-outputs over a counter *Z = (r, l, sl, m′, t, y)* (data-independent) | side-channel resistant | weaker against TMTO |
| Argon2**id** | Argon2i rule for pass 0, slices 0–1; Argon2d rule otherwise | hybrid | slight side-channel exposure after first half-pass |

The lane index is *l = J₂ mod p*, and the block index *z* is drawn from the eligible set *W* with the non-uniform mapping *|W|(1 − J₁²/2⁶⁴)*, biasing references toward recently computed blocks [1].

**Recommended parameters.** RFC 9106 gives two defaults: the *first recommended option*, Argon2id with *t = 1* and *m = 2 GiB*, for general use; and the *second recommended option*, Argon2id with *t = 3* and *m = 64 MiB*, for memory-constrained environments [1]. The tradeoff analysis behind these choices [6] finds that the best TMTO attack on 1-pass Argon2id gains only a factor of about **2.1** (combined low-storage attack on the first half of memory plus a ranking attack on the second half), and on *t*-pass Argon2id only **1.33** — far below the exponential savings attackers enjoy against non-memory-hard hashes.

### 4.2 scrypt: sequential memory-hardness via ROMix

Percival's scrypt [2], standardized as RFC 7914 [8], wraps PBKDF2-HMAC-SHA256 around a sequential memory-hard core:

```
scrypt(P, S, N, r, p, dkLen) = MFcrypt_{HMAC-SHA256, SMix_r}(P, S, N, p, dkLen)
```

Each of the *p* lanes runs **ROMix**, which has two phases (Figure 1):

1. **Fill:** *X = B*; for *i = 0 … N−1*: *V[i] = X*; *X = BlockMix(X)*.
2. **Lookup:** for *i = 0 … N−1*: *j = Integrify(X) mod N*; *T = X ⊕ V[j]*; *X = BlockMix(T)*,

where *Integrify* interprets the last 64 bytes of *X* as a little-endian integer, and **BlockMix** chains the 8-round Salsa20/8 core across *2r* 64-byte blocks. Memory consumption is *128·N·r* bytes (e.g., *N = 2¹⁴, r = 8* ⇒ 16 MiB); *N* must be a power of two, *r = 8, p = 1* are the recommended defaults, and *p* raises CPU cost without raising memory [2][8].

![scrypt ROMix](public/thesis/ths_1789007475491_22283-1.webp)

> **Theorem (Percival's Conjecture 1, informal).** If no circuit computes Salsa20/8 faster than time *t* and storing *x* bits costs area at least *s·x*, then no circuit evaluates scrypt with expected amortized area–time product per password below *1024·N²·r²·p·s·t* [2].

In words: scrypt's AT complexity is conjectured to scale as Θ(*N²*), the product of its memory (*N* blocks) and its time (*N* steps) — the quadratic ideal.

### 4.3 Balloon hashing: provable security from sandwich graphs

Balloon [3] targets the same goal as Argon2i — a *password-independent* access pattern — but was designed from the start for provable security. The algorithm has three phases:

1. **Expansion:** fill a buffer of *s_cost* blocks by hashing *(counter ‖ password ‖ salt)*.
2. **Mixing:** repeat *t_cost* times: `buf[m] = H(cnt ‖ buf[m] ‖ buf[m−1] ‖ buf[δ random predecessors])`.
3. **Output:** return the last block.

A compact reference implementation of the mixing core is:

```python
def balloon_mix(buf, t_cost, delta, H):
    n, cnt = len(buf), 0
    for _ in range(t_cost):
        for m in range(n):
            prev = buf[(m - 1) % n]
            others = [buf[pseudorandom(cnt + k) % n] for k in range(delta)]
            buf[m] = H(cnt, buf[m], prev, *others)
            cnt += 1
    return buf[-1]
```

The computation graph is a **random sandwich graph**: *s* layers of *n* vertices, with edges from each vertex to its counterpart and to δ pseudorandom vertices in the previous layer (Figure 3). The paper's central technical contribution is a new pebbling lower bound for sandwich graphs, yielding:

> **Theorem (Boneh–Corrigan-Gibbs–Schechter [3], informal).** In the random-oracle model, any *sequential* algorithm evaluating Balloon with space *S* and *S ≪ n* needs time Ω(*n²/S*) — i.e., the space–time product is Ω(*n²*).

The same sandwich-graph techniques were used to give the first random-oracle security proofs for **scrypt** and **Argon2i** as well [3]. The authors are explicit about the model's boundary: the proof covers sequential (single-core) adversaries, and massively parallel machines fall outside it — a gap later work exploited.

### 4.4 Pebbling complexity: the shared analytical engine

All iMHF security arguments rest on the **black pebbling game** on a DAG *G* (Figure 2). A pebble on a vertex means its label is in memory; a pebble may be placed on *v* only when all of *v*'s parents are pebbled (the corresponding oracle queries have been made); pebbles may be removed at any time. A pebbling *P = (P₀, …, P_t)* has time Π_t = *t*, space Π_s = max|*P_i*|, space–time Π_st = Π_t·Π_s, and **cumulative complexity** Π_cc = Σ_i |*P_i*|.

![Pebbling game](public/thesis/ths_1789007475491_22283-2.webp)

Two landmark results structure the field:

> **Theorem (Alwen–Serbinenko [5], informal).** There is a parallel pebbling game whose complexity tightly characterizes the amortized complexity of iMHFs in the parallel random-oracle model; cumulative complexity (CC) is the right measure, and explicit constant-indegree graph families achieve near-maximal CC.

> **Theorem (Alwen–Blocki [4], informal).** For any DAG *G* there is a generic evaluation algorithm whose AT and energy complexity are bounded by a combinatorial property of *G*. Instantiated, this gives upper bounds (i.e., attacks): Catena-Dragonfly and Catena-Butterfly have AT *O(n^1.67)*, and Argon2i has AT *O(n^{7/4} log n)*.

The practical consequence was dramatic: the same paper family produced concrete low-space evaluations of Argon2i, and the Balloon authors themselves implemented an attack evaluating Argon2i in less space than previously claimed possible [3]. A key sufficient condition for strong lower bounds is **depth-robustness** — a graph that retains a long path even after many vertices are deleted — which yields optimal cumulative complexity up to polylogarithmic factors [4].

## 5. Empirical Results and Proofs

| Construction | Year | Access pattern | Proof model | Best known TMTO / pebbling bound | Standard |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| scrypt | 2009 | data-dependent | ROM (sequential), conjectured AT bound [2] | Θ(*N²*) AT (conjectured) | RFC 7914 [8] |
| Argon2d | 2015 | data-dependent | heuristic + tradeoff analysis [6] | ranking attacks limited; side-channel exposed | RFC 9106 [1] |
| Argon2i | 2015 | data-independent | ROM via sandwich techniques [3] | AT *O(n^{7/4} log n)* attack [4] | RFC 9106 [1] |
| Argon2id | 2015 | hybrid | tradeoff analysis [6] | 2.1× (1-pass), 1.33× (t-pass) [1] | RFC 9106 (recommended) [1] |
| Balloon | 2016 | data-independent | ROM, sequential ST = Ω(*n²*) [3] | parallel attacks outside model | — |

Three empirical facts stand out. **First**, the provable guarantees all live in the *sequential* random-oracle model; Alwen and Blocki's parallel pebbling attacks [4] and the subsequent "practical attacks on Argon2i and Balloon hashing" operate precisely in the gap between the sequential model and parallel hardware. **Second**, performance measurements in the Balloon paper show Balloon matching or exceeding the best heuristically secure PHC candidates on commodity CPUs [3], refuting the folk belief that provable security must cost performance. **Third**, the concrete numbers in RFC 9106 remain the most actionable guidance available: with 1-pass Argon2id at 2 GiB, even an optimal TMTO adversary saves only a factor of ~2.1 over the honest evaluator [1].

## 6. Limitations and Open Problems

1. **The parallel-model gap.** Every deployed proof (Balloon, scrypt, Argon2i) assumes a sequential adversary, yet real attackers use GPUs and ASICs with massive parallelism. Closing this gap — proving security in the parallel random-oracle model for a *practical* construction — remains the central open problem; recent work on sustained space complexity and data-dependent MHFs shows progress but relies on expensive combinatorial objects or heuristic pebbling games [9].
2. **Side channels vs. TMTO resistance.** Argon2d's data-dependent addressing is its TMTO strength and its side-channel weakness; Argon2id's hybrid schedule is a compromise, not a resolution. A construction with the TMTO resistance of data-dependent addressing *and* the side-channel profile of data-independent addressing is unknown.
3. **Parameter selection.** RFC 9106's two recommended options [1] are calibrated to 2021-era hardware economics; memory bandwidth, not capacity, increasingly dominates real cost, and no current guidance accounts for it.
4. **Multi-instance amortization.** Password databases contain millions of hashes; most bounds are per-instance, and amortized multi-instance security (beyond the energy-complexity framing of [4]) is still poorly understood.
5. **Quantum adversaries.** Grover-style speedups halve effective preimage resistance but their interaction with *memory*-hardness — where the bottleneck is area, not queries — has no satisfactory treatment.

---

## 7. Conclusion

Memory-hard password hashing has matured from Percival's scrypt conjecture [2] into a discipline with real standards (RFC 7914, RFC 9106 [1][8]), real proofs (Balloon's sandwich-graph analysis [3]), and a real complexity-theoretic foundation in pebbling reductions [4][5]. The arc of the field is a dialectic between proofs and attacks: each TMTO analysis [6] and each pebbling upper bound [4] has forced the next construction to be sharper. The practical upshot is stable — use Argon2id with the RFC 9106 parameters [1] — but the theoretical foundations are not: until a practical construction carries a proof in the *parallel* model, the security of every password database rests partly on the hope that the best known attacks are close to the best possible ones. Proving that hope correct is the open problem that will define the next decade of password hashing.
