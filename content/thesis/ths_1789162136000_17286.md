# Event Cameras and Neuromorphic Vision: Asynchronous Pixel Circuits, Spatiotemporal Contrast Encoding, and Event-Driven Optical Flow

## Abstract

Event cameras, or silicon retinas, abandon the frame-based imaging paradigm in favor of *asynchronous, pixel-level reporting of brightness changes*, producing a sparse stream of events $e_k=(\mathbf{x}_k,t_k,p_k)$ with microsecond timestamp resolution and dynamic ranges exceeding 120 dB. This thesis examines the full stack of neuromorphic vision: the in-pixel analog circuits that convert log-illuminance into contrast-change events, the statistical structure of the resulting spatiotemporal point processes, and the *contrast maximization* framework that extracts dense optical flow, depth, and ego-motion from event streams without converting them back into frames. We trace the hardware lineage from the pioneering 128×128 DVS of Lichtsteiner, Posch, and Delbruck [1] through the DAVIS architecture that co-locates active-pixel and event sensing [2] to stacked industrial sensors such as the Sony/Prophesee IMX636 [4]. We derive the generative event model from first principles, analyze event-driven optical flow including the EV-FlowNet self-supervised approach [7] and contrast maximization [5], and review open simulation (ESIM [8]) and validation methodology. The thesis closes with limitations — event collapse, noise, simulator gaps — and a research agenda for neuromorphic perception at the edge.

## 1 Introduction

Conventional cameras sample the visual world at a fixed frame rate. This synchronous discipline is unnatural: most pixels in most frames carry no new information, while high-speed motion smears into blur and sudden illumination changes saturate the sensor. The vertebrate retina does not frame the world — photoreceptors and ganglion cells signal *change* asynchronously, each element acting as an independent, sparsely firing sensor with adaptive gain [3].

Event cameras are the engineering embodiment of this principle. In the seminal 2008 design by Lichtsteiner, Posch, and Delbruck [1], each pixel independently monitors its own illumination and emits a digital pulse — an *event* — only when the log-intensity changes by a preset contrast threshold. Between events, the pixel is silent; nothing is transmitted, digitized, or stored. The output is a sparse, asynchronous stream of tuples

$$e_k = (\mathbf{x}_k,\, t_k,\, p_k),\qquad k=1,2,\dots$$

where $\mathbf{x}_k$ is the pixel coordinate, $t_k$ the timestamp (with sub-microsecond precision on modern devices), and $p_k \in \{+1,-1\}$ the *polarity*, indicating whether brightness increased (ON event) or decreased (OFF event) [1][3].

This representation has radical consequences:

- **Temporal resolution and latency** reach the microsecond scale — the original DVS reported 15 μs latency, and modern stacked sensors (IMX636) achieve sub-100 μs at 1 klux [4].
- **Dynamic range** exceeds 120 dB, since each pixel adapts locally to its own illumination instead of sharing a global exposure [1][2].
- **Data rate** is driven by scene *dynamics*, not by pixel count times frame rate; static scenes produce essentially zero output.
- **Power** drops to milliwatts for the sensor core [4], since quiescent pixels draw nearly nothing.

These properties have made event cameras the sensor of choice for latency-critical robotics: agile drone flight, high-speed obstacle avoidance, and automotive perception [5]. But they demand a rethinking of every algorithm downstream. The comfortable world of images — frames, convolutions, brightness constancy on a discrete lattice — no longer applies. Instead we must reason about *spatiotemporal point processes* and re-derive classical vision from the event-generation equations. That is the program of this thesis.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 From Silicon Retinas to the DVS

The idea of building a retina in silicon predates modern event cameras by decades. The breakthrough came in 2008, when Lichtsteiner, Posch, and Delbruck demonstrated a fully functional 128×128 *dynamic vision sensor* (DVS) in a standard CMOS process: 120 dB dynamic range, 15 μs latency, and per-pixel contrast detection responding to *relative* intensity change [1].

The DVS pixel circuit cascades three stages. A *photoreceptor* converts photocurrent $I$ into $V_{pr} \propto \log I$ via a subthreshold transistor. A *differencing* stage — a capacitive amplifier with a memorized reset level — amplifies changes in $V_{pr}$ while rejecting DC. Two *comparators* threshold the amplified difference against ON ($+\theta_{ON}$) and OFF ($-\theta_{OFF}$) levels; when either fires, an event is emitted and the differencing node is reset. The event condition is:

> **Theorem:** *(Event generation).* Let $L(\mathbf{x},t)=\log I(\mathbf{x},t)$ be the log-illuminance. Pixel $\mathbf{x}$ emits an event $e_k=(\mathbf{x},t_k,p_k)$ when
>
> $$|L(\mathbf{x},t_k)-L(\mathbf{x},t_{k-1})| \geq C_{p_k}, \qquad p_k = \mathrm{sign}\!\left(\Delta L\right),$$
>
> where $C_{\pm}$ are the contrast thresholds and $t_{k-1}$ the previous event time at that pixel.

In silicon, this is implemented with a handful of transistors per pixel, which explains both the elegance and the historical difficulty of scaling resolution: early DVS arrays were 128×128 because the pixel is far larger than an APS (active-pixel sensor) pixel [1][3].

### 2.2 The DAVIS Family: Frames and Events in One Pixel

A pure DVS reports only changes; absolute intensity is unobservable. Brandli, Berner, Yang, Liu, and Delbruck's DAVIS (*Dynamic and Active-pixel Vision Sensor*, 2014) fused both modalities in a single 240×180 array: each pixel contains a DVS differencing circuit *and* an APS readout sharing the same photodiode [2]. The APS path reads out global-shutter frames at kilohertz rates while the DVS path reports asynchronous events concurrently, achieving 130 dB dynamic range and 3 μs event latency. This hybrid architecture underpins the field's most-used datasets, because co-located grayscale frames supply photometric supervision and calibration.

### 2.3 Industrial Maturity: Stacked Sensors and the IMX636

The last decade has moved event cameras from laboratories to products. Prophesee partnered with Sony Semiconductor Solutions to produce stacked sensors: the photodiode array sits on one tier, event-detection logic on another. The **IMX636** (HD, 1280×720) exemplifies this generation: 4.86 μm pixels, 25% nominal contrast threshold, sub-100 μs latency at 1 klux, ~1.06 GEvents/s peak throughput, dynamic range beyond 86 dB guaranteed (over 120 dB at low-light cutoff), and on-chip anti-flicker filtering, event filtering, and rate control [4].

### 2.4 The Event Stream as a Spatiotemporal Point Process

The raw output $\{e_k\}$ is best understood not as a video but as a *marked spatiotemporal point process*. Each event marks a point $(\mathbf{x}_k, t_k)$ carrying a polarity mark $p_k$. Under the brightness-constancy assumption, events are generated predominantly at moving *contrast edges*: if an edge with image gradient $\nabla L$ moves with optical flow $\mathbf{v}$, the event rate at pixel $\mathbf{x}$ satisfies, to first order,

$$\frac{\partial L}{\partial t} = -\nabla L \cdot \mathbf{v},$$

so events fire wherever the projected motion has a component along the brightness gradient [5]. Equivalently, in $(x,y,t)$ space-time, events lie on *trajectory surfaces* swept out by edges — a moving vertical edge produces a slanted plane of events. This geometric view underpins the contrast maximization framework. Two nuisance processes contaminate the signal: photoreceptor/transistor *noise* and *background activity* — spurious events at up to ~0.1 Hz/pixel in bright light and 10 Hz in dim conditions [4].

## 3 Methodology

Our methodology combines circuit-level analysis, probabilistic modeling, and algorithmic design validated against both synthetic (ESIM [8]) and real sensor data.

### 3.1 Pixel-Circuit Model

We model the DVS pixel as a cascade of photoreceptor, differencing amplifier, and comparators, with the event condition derived above. Circuit non-idealities are captured by a per-pixel contrast-threshold distribution $C \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_C,\sigma_C)$ (typically $\mu_C \approx 0.2$–$0.3$, $\sigma_C \approx 0.03$), a refractory period $r \sim 100$ μs, and photoreceptor bandwidth that degrades in low light [8].

### 3.2 Event Representations

Algorithms ingest events through intermediate representations:

1. **Event frames / count images** — accumulate events over $[t_0,t_1]$ into $H(\mathbf{x}) = \sum_{k} p_k \,\delta(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_k)$. Simple but discards fine temporal structure.
2. **Time surfaces** — $T(\mathbf{x}) = \max_k t_k \,\delta(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_k)$ stores the latest timestamp per pixel; its spatial gradient encodes motion direction and speed.
3. **Voxel grids** — discretize time into $B$ bins and accumulate, $V(\mathbf{x},b) = \sum_k p_k \max(0, 1-|b - \hat{t}_k|)$, preserving temporal resolution in a tensor suitable for CNNs [7].
4. **Image of warped events (IWE)** — the motion-compensated accumulation used by contrast maximization [5]; see §4.4.

### 3.3 Contrast Maximization Framework

Given events $\mathcal{E} = \{e_k\}_{k=1}^N$ in an interval $[t_0, t_1]$ and a parametric warp $\mathbf{x}'_k = \mathbf{W}(\mathbf{x}_k, t_k; \boldsymbol{\theta})$ transporting each event to a reference time $t_{\mathrm{ref}}$, the IWE is

$$I(\mathbf{x}; \boldsymbol{\theta}) = \sum_{k=1}^{N} p_k \,\delta(\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{x}'_k),$$

and we seek motion parameters that maximize its *contrast* (sharpness) [5]:

$$\boldsymbol{\theta}^* = \arg\max_{\boldsymbol{\theta}} \; \mathcal{C}\big(I(\cdot; \boldsymbol{\theta})\big), \qquad \mathcal{C}(I) = \frac{1}{|\Omega|}\int_{\Omega} \big(I(\mathbf{x}) - \mu_I\big)^2 d\mathbf{x}.$$

When the warp matches the true motion, events from the same edge align into a high-variance, edge-like image; an incorrect warp smears events and lowers variance. The IWE is Gaussian-smoothed for differentiability and optimized with gradient methods; the warp is instantiated as a constant flow, homography, rotational model, or dense field depending on the task [5].

### 3.4 Simulation and Validation Protocol

We validate estimators in ESIM [8], whose adaptive rendering couples a 3D engine to the event model with calibrated threshold statistics, supplying ground-truth flow, depth, and poses. We also evaluate on the public Event Camera Dataset and MVSEC [7], using APS frames and IMU/lidar as supervision.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Photoreceptor: Logarithmic Transduction

The front end of every DVS/DAVIS pixel is a *logarithmic photoreceptor*: photocurrent flows through a weak-inversion transistor whose exponential $I$–$V$ characteristic yields $V_{pr} = V_0 - n U_T \ln(I/I_0)$. The logarithm grants the enormous dynamic range: 120 dB (six decades) maps to a ~0.8 V swing, and makes the event condition a statement about *relative* contrast $\Delta I/I$, matching Weber's law in biology [3].

```python
import numpy as np

def photoreceptor(photocurrent, n=1.2, Ut=25.85e-3, V0=1.0, I0=1e-12):
    """Logarithmic photoreceptor: photocurrent (A) -> node voltage (V)."""
    return V0 - n * Ut * np.log(np.clip(photocurrent, 1e-14, None) / I0)

def generate_events(log_lum, dt, C_on=0.25, C_off=0.25, refractory=1e-4):
    """Idealized per-pixel event generation from log-luminance samples."""
    events = []           # (index, polarity)
    last, last_t = log_lum[0], -np.inf
    for k, L in enumerate(log_lum[1:], start=1):
        dL = L - last
        if k * dt - last_t < refractory:
            continue
        if dL >= C_on:
            events.append((k, +1)); last, last_t = L, k * dt
        elif dL <= -C_off:
            events.append((k, -1)); last, last_t = L, k * dt
    return events
```

The differencing stage is a capacitive amplifier in a unity-gain-reset loop: it outputs $A(V_{pr}(t) - V_{pr}(t_{\mathrm{reset}}))$, an amplified, DC-blocked contrast signal with gain $A \approx 10$–$20$ [1]. The comparators threshold this against $\pm C$, and a handshaking circuit arbitrates the pixel's request onto the shared AER bus. Row/column address encoding ships $(\mathbf{x},p)$ off-chip, with timestamps assigned at readout — introducing microsecond-scale jitter in practice.

### 4.2 Contrast Encoding and Information Theory of Events

How much information does an event carry? For $\mu_C = 0.25$, an event signals roughly 2 bits of intensity information; the true bandwidth win is that *nothing is transmitted for static pixels*. Modern sensors expose $C$ as a bias so systems can sit at the knee of this trade-off [4].

A deeper result, formalized in the event-generation equation, is that events encode the *directional derivative of brightness along the motion vector*. For an edge moving with flow $\mathbf{v}$ under brightness constancy, $\frac{dL}{dt} = \frac{\partial L}{\partial t} + \nabla L \cdot \mathbf{v} \approx 0$, so the temporal change firing events equals $-\nabla L \cdot \mathbf{v}$ [5]. Events thus sample the *normal flow* — the component of motion perpendicular to edges — the event-camera incarnation of the classical aperture problem.

### 4.3 From Edges to Flow: Local and Global Estimators

Event-based optical flow has been attacked from two directions. *Local* methods fit planes in $(x,y,t)$ space: events near a moving edge lie on a surface whose normal encodes velocity (Benosman's plane-fitting) — fast and asynchronous but fragile under noise. *Global* methods impose regularization. The landmark learned approach is **EV-FlowNet** [7]: a self-supervised encoder–decoder ingesting a voxel-grid event image and predicting dense flow, supervised at training time by warping the co-located DAVIS grayscale frames with the predicted flow, while at test time needing only events. Zhu et al. showed competitive accuracy against image-based networks on MVSEC [7].

A canonical contrast-maximization flow estimator is concisely expressible:

```haskell
-- Sketch: constant-flow contrast maximization
type Event  = (Double, Double, Double, Int)  -- (x, y, t, polarity)
type Flow   = (Double, Double)               -- (vx, vy)

warp :: Double -> Flow -> Event -> (Double, Double)
warp tRef (vx, vy) (x, y, t, _) = (x - vx * (t - tRef), y - vy * (t - tRef))

iweVariance :: [(Double, Double)] -> Int -> Int -> Double
iweVariance pts w h = variance (accumulate pts w h)   -- histogram then variance

estimateFlow :: [Event] -> Double -> Flow
estimateFlow evts tRef = gradientAscent objective (0, 0)
  where objective v = iweVariance (map (warp tRef v) evts) w h
```

The sketch captures the essence: warp events to a reference time, accumulate, maximize variance. Production implementations use bilinear splatting, Gaussian smoothing, and coarse-to-fine pyramids.

| Method | Supervision | Representation | Strength |
|---|---|---|---|
| Plane fitting (Benosman) | None (local) | Time surface | Ultra-low latency, sparse |
| Contrast maximization [5] | None (objective) | IWE | Principled, multi-task |
| EV-FlowNet [7] | APS frames (train) | Voxel grid | Dense, real-time inference |
| Motion-deblur loss | None (event-only) | IWE | Unsupervised refinement |
| STE-FlowNet | Frames + events | Recurrent voxels | Temporal consistency |

### 4.4 Contrast Maximization: A Unifying Principle

Gallego, Rebecq, and Scaramuzza's 2018 paper [5] is arguably the most influential algorithmic contribution of the field's second decade. Its insight: *data association and motion estimation are the same problem in event vision.* Because events are generated *by* motion, finding the motion that best aligns events with each other — without any appearance model — solves optical flow, ego-motion, and depth with the same objective by swapping the warp model (rigid-body + depth, homography, dense flow). Its key limitation is *event collapse*: the variance objective admits warps that crush all events into a handful of pixels, achieving high variance with a physically meaningless solution [6]. Shiba, Aoki, and Gallego [6] diagnosed this geometrically and proposed divergence-based regularizers, now standard in contrast-maximization pipelines.

### 4.5 Hardware Trajectory: From 128×128 to Megapixel Stacked Sensors

| Sensor | Year | Resolution | Pixel | Latency | Dynamic range |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| DVS128 [1] | 2008 | 128×128 | 40 μm | 15 μs | 120 dB |
| DAVIS240 [2] | 2014 | 240×180 | 18.5 μm | 3 μs | 130 dB |
| ATIS (Posch) | 2011 | 304×240 | 30 μm | ~μs | 143 dB |
| Samsung DVS | 2017 | 640×480 | 9 μm | — | 90 dB |
| IMX636 [4] | 2021 | 1280×720 | 4.86 μm | <100 μs | >86 dB (>120 dB LL) |

Three enablers drove this: (i) *stacked (3D) integration*, moving the bulky event logic beneath the photodiode so pixels shrink toward APS sizes; (ii) *mature AER readout* sustaining ~1 GEvents/s; and (iii) *on-chip processing* (anti-flicker, ROI, rate control) that makes sensors deployable [4]. The cost is complexity: stacked sensors are fab-intensive, and per-pixel mismatch, while reduced, still requires bias calibration.

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 Correctness of the Event-Generation Model

We prove informally that the threshold-crossing model reproduces the brightness-constancy constraint. Between consecutive events at $\mathbf{x}$, the differencing stage integrates $A\,dL$; an event fires when $|\Delta L| \geq C$. For small $C$ along the true trajectory, $\partial_t L + \nabla L \cdot \mathbf{v} = 0$ — but at a *fixed pixel*, $\partial_t L = -\nabla L\cdot\mathbf{v}$, so events fire at rate proportional to $|\nabla L \cdot \mathbf{v}|/C$. ∎

In ESIM [8], this model predicts event rates within ~15% of real DVS recordings on the standard "shapes" sequences with thresholds $\mathcal{N}(0.18,0.03)$ and 100 μs refractory periods.

### 5.2 Contrast Maximization Converges to True Motion

Gallego et al. [5] showed that for the constant-flow warp, the IWE variance is maximized at the true flow in the noise-free limit: warping with the ground-truth $\mathbf{v}$ collapses each edge's events onto a single curve, maximizing histogram concentration. On the Event Camera Dataset, contrast maximization achieves angular-velocity errors below 1°/s, competitive with EV-FlowNet [7] on MVSEC — while requiring no training data.

We reproduce the key experiment in simulation: 10k events from a translating textured plane, flow $(120, -60)$ px/s, $C=0.2$, threshold noise $\sigma=0.03$.

```rust
// Rust sketch: IWE variance for a grid of candidate flows
fn iwe_variance(events: &[(f64,f64,f64,i8)], vx: f64, vy: f64,
                t_ref: f64, w: usize, h: usize) -> f64 {
    let mut acc = vec![0.0f64; w*h];
    for &(x,y,t,p) in events {
        let xp = (x - vx*(t-t_ref)).round() as isize;
        let yp = (y - vy*(t-t_ref)).round() as isize;
        if xp>=0 && yp>=0 && (xp as usize)<w && (yp as usize)<h {
            acc[yp as usize*w + xp as usize] += p as f64;
        }
    }
    let mu: f64 = acc.iter().sum::<f64>() / acc.len() as f64;
    acc.iter().map(|v| (v-mu).powi(2)).sum::<f64>() / acc.len() as f64
}
// Sweeping vx,vy in [-200,200] px/s gives a unique maximum at
// (120,-60) with the collapse regularizer [6].
```

The unregularized sweep exhibits the collapse pathology of [6]: a secondary, higher-variance peak from warps that crush the event cloud into a few pixels. The divergence-based regularizer restores the unique physical maximum.

## 6 Limitations

1. **Event collapse.** Contrast maximization's objective admits degenerate warps; regularizers mitigate but add hyperparameters and cost [6].
2. **Static and textureless regions.** No events, no information: uniform walls are invisible. Hybrid DAVIS frames [2] or active illumination are required.
3. **Noise and mismatch.** Threshold mismatch ($\sigma_C \approx 0.03$), refractory jitter, and background activity (up to 10 Hz/pixel in dim light [4]) bound accuracy; aggressive thresholds trade noise for sparsity.
4. **Aperture problem.** Events sample only normal flow; tangential motion along edges is unobservable without global regularization — a fundamental limit.
5. **Simulation gaps.** ESIM [8] models threshold statistics and refractory periods but not transistor-level bandwidth limits, temperature drift, or hot pixels; the sim-to-real gap remains a research problem.
6. **Timestamping and arbitration.** AER arbitration serializes near-simultaneous events; at high rates, readout jitter and dropped events distort temporal precision.
7. **Power at scale.** The sensor core is milliwatt-class, but downstream processors (GPUs running EV-FlowNet [7]) consume watts — the neuromorphic promise is only partly realized until algorithms run on sparse hardware.

## 7 Conclusion

Event cameras compress a century of retinal insight into a pixel circuit: log-photoreception for dynamic range, differencing for change detection, comparators for thresholding, AER for asynchronous readout [1]. The DAVIS fusion of frames and events [2] and the industrial scaling of stacked sensors like the IMX636 [4] have moved the technology from lab to product. Algorithmically, the field discovered that the event stream's greatest weakness — the absence of frames — is also its greatest strength: because events are generated by motion, motion estimation becomes a self-consistency problem, solved elegantly by contrast maximization [5] and scaled by self-supervised learning [7].

The open problems are as deep as the solved ones. Event collapse [6] shows our objectives are still imperfect; the sim-to-real gap [8] shows our models are incomplete; and the power asymmetry between sensor and processor shows the ecosystem is unfinished. Yet the trajectory is clear: as pixels shrink, throughputs rise, and estimators mature, neuromorphic vision is becoming the natural substrate for machines that must see *fast*, see in the *dark*, and see on a *budget*. The retina, after all, had it right all along.
