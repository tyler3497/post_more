# Single-Cell Perturbation Response Modeling: Causal Optimal Transport, Perturb-seq Screens, and Foundation Models for In Silico Perturbation Prediction

## Abstract

Predicting how an individual cell's transcriptional state changes under genetic or chemical perturbation is central to drug discovery, functional genomics, and precision medicine, yet the single-cell measurement destroys the cell, so counterfactual pairs are never observed. This thesis develops the computational program for this problem: high-throughput pooled Perturb-seq screens generating population-level perturbation-response data [1][2][9]; latent-variable and compositional models learning disentangled perturbation effects (scGen, CPA) [3][4]; graph neural architectures exploiting biological priors to generalize to unseen and combinatorial perturbations (GEARS) [5]; neural optimal transport inferring cell-level counterfactual couplings from unpaired marginals (CellOT) [6]; and generative foundation models pretrained on tens of millions of cells (scGPT, Geneformer) [7][8]. We formalize each family, derive the identifiability conditions for unpaired perturbation-response prediction, compare benchmark results on harmonized datasets [10], and analyze the open challenges — distributional shift, causal confounding, and the combinatorial explosion of multigene perturbations — that define the field's frontier.

## 1. Introduction

The history of genetics is, in large part, the history of perturbation: mutate a gene, observe the phenotype, and infer function. For decades this program operated at the level of whole organisms or bulk populations, averaging away the heterogeneity that is often the very phenomenon of interest. Two revolutions have changed this. First, **single-cell RNA sequencing** (scRNA-seq) resolves the transcriptional state of individual cells, exposing the rich within-population variation — cell types, activation states, stochastic bursts — that bulk assays conflate [8]. Second, **pooled CRISPR screens** allow thousands of perturbations to be delivered and assayed in a single experiment, with each cell carrying a barcode identifying the perturbation it received [1][2].

Their combination, **Perturb-seq**, produces a remarkable and peculiar data type: hundreds of thousands of single-cell transcriptomes labeled with perturbations, drawn from *unpaired* populations — perturbed cells were physically different cells from controls, because sequencing destroys the cell [6]. The fundamental computational question is therefore one of **counterfactual inference from unpaired distributions**: given samples from a control distribution *P₀* and samples from a perturbed distribution *P₁*, can we predict the response of a *specific* unseen cell drawn from *P₀*? And, more ambitiously, can we predict responses to perturbations never performed experimentally — including combinations whose number grows combinatorially with the gene count?

This thesis argues these questions share a coherent mathematical structure spanning optimal transport, causal inference, graph representation learning, and generative modeling. We organize the field into four methodological generations:

1. **Latent-variable encoders** (2019–2022): variational autoencoders that map control and perturbed cells into a shared latent space, enabling *in silico* perturbation by vector arithmetic (scGen [3]) and compositional autoencoders with explicit drug/perturbation embeddings (CPA [4], chemCPA).
2. **Knowledge-informed graph models** (2023–2024): graph neural networks over gene–gene interaction priors that predict transcriptional outcomes of single and multigene perturbations, including genes never perturbed during training (GEARS [5]).
3. **Neural optimal transport** (2022–2024): transport maps learned between unpaired control and perturbed distributions, justified by the *minimal-effort principle* of acute cellular responses (CellOT [6]).
4. **Single-cell foundation models** (2023–2025): transformers pretrained at scale on tens of millions of cells, adapted by fine-tuning or in-context conditioning to perturbation prediction (scGPT [7], Geneformer [8]).

Each generation addresses a distinct facet of the problem — representation, inductive bias, unpaired coupling, and scale — and each rests on assumptions whose violations define its failure modes. The thesis proceeds by formalizing the shared statistical problem, developing each methodology with its theorems and algorithms, and confronting the resulting models with harmonized benchmarks [10].

## 2. Background

### 2.1 The data-generating process

Let a cell be described by a gene-expression vector **x** ∈ ℝ₊ᴳ, where *G* ≈ 20,000 is the number of measured genes (in practice restricted to a highly-variable subset). A single-cell experiment produces a *count matrix* **X** ∈ ℕ^{N×G} of unique molecular identifiers (UMIs). We model each cell as an i.i.d. draw from a distribution over cell states, **x** ∼ *P*, where *P* is a mixture over biological cell types and states corrupted by technical noise (dropout, amplification bias, batch effects).

A **perturbation** *p* (e.g., CRISPR-mediated knockout of gene *g*) induces an *interventional distribution* *P^p* := *P*(**x** | do(*p*)), in Pearl's causal notation. The experimentalist observes:

- *n₀* control cells {**x**ᵢ⁰}ᵢ₌₁ⁿ⁰ ∼ *P₀*,
- *n₁* perturbed cells {**x**ⱼᵖ}ⱼ₌₁ⁿ¹ ∼ *P^p*,

with the crucial fact that these are **unpaired**: there is no index correspondence *i ↔ j*, because sequencing destroys the cell [6]. The two samples are drawn from *different cells*.

### 2.2 Perturb-seq and pooled screens

Perturb-seq (Dixit et al., 2016) pools these experiments: a lentiviral library of sgRNAs barcodes each cell with its perturbation, droplet-based scRNA-seq reads both the transcriptome and the guide barcode, and a single sequencing run assays thousands of perturbations across hundreds of thousands of cells [1]. Scale escalated dramatically: genome-scale screens now profile thousands of gene knockouts in a single assay, enabling systematic maps of *trans* gene regulation [2]. Harmonization efforts collect dozens of such datasets into unified resources with standardized QC and energy-statistic effect-size measures [10].

Key terminology, used throughout:

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Perturbation response** | The distribution *P^p* or a predicted sample from it |
| **Differential expression (DE)** | Genes whose distribution differs significantly between *P₀* and *P^p* |
| **Genetic interaction** | Non-additivity: effect(*p₁ + p₂*) ≠ effect(*p₁*) ⊕ effect(*p₂*) |
| **E-distance** | Energy-statistic distance between two empirical cell populations [10] |
| **Counterfactual prediction** | Predicting **x**ᵖ for a specific observed **x**⁰ |
| **Unpaired** | Control and perturbed cells are distinct physical samples |

### 2.3 The statistical core

All methods below solve variants of one problem. Given empirical control and perturbed distributions, and auxiliary information (perturbation identity, biological graphs, pretrained representations), learn a function *f* such that *f*(**x**⁰, *p*) approximates a draw from *P*(· | do(*p*), **x**⁰) — the **conditional counterfactual distribution** of that cell's response. 

> **Theorem (Identifiability barrier).** *Without further assumptions, the counterfactual coupling between P₀ and P^p is not identifiable from unpaired marginal samples alone. Any joint distribution π on ℝᴳ × ℝᴳ with marginals (P₀, P^p) is compatible with the observations.*

This is the Sklar-type non-identifiability of couplings: data constrain only the marginals. Every method in this thesis therefore injects a structural assumption — a latent-space arithmetic (scGen), a compositional decoder (CPA), a minimal-transport principle (CellOT), a biological graph prior (GEARS), or a pretrained generative prior (scGPT) — that selects one coupling from the unidentified set. The scientific content of each method *is* its assumption.

## 3. Methodology

### 3.1 The shared evaluation scaffold

Before developing the methods, we fix the evaluation scaffold used in §5, following harmonized benchmarks [10]. For a held-out perturbation *p* (genes or combinations never perturbed at training):

1. **Marginal fidelity**: compare predicted population {*f*(**x**ᵢ⁰, *p*)} to the true perturbed sample via **E-distance** (energy statistic), which detects differences in high-dimensional distributions without density estimation [10]:

```python
def e_distance(X: np.ndarray, Y: np.ndarray) -> float:
    """Energy-statistic distance between two cell populations."""
    from scipy.spatial.distance import cdist
    d_xx = cdist(X, X).mean()
    d_yy = cdist(Y, Y).mean()
    d_xy = cdist(X, Y).mean()
    return 2.0 * d_xy - d_xx - d_yy
```

2. **DE-gene fidelity**: Pearson correlation and mean-squared error on the top differentially expressed genes (typically the top 20 or 40 by effect size), computed against the observed mean shift **μ**ᵖ − **μ**⁰.
3. **Genetic-interaction classification**: for combinatorial perturbations, precision at recovering synergistic, suppressive, redundant, and neomorphic interaction subtypes [5].
4. **Cell-level metrics**: where paired-like structure can be approximated (e.g., via optimal-transport coupling of the test set), the fraction of variance of single-cell responses explained.

### 3.2 Architectural taxonomy

We now develop the four methodological families in §4. For orientation:

- **scGen** [3]: VAE + latent arithmetic, δ̄ᵖ = **z̄**ᵖ − **z̄**⁰ applied to new cell types.
- **CPA** [4]: autoencoder with perturbation/dosage embeddings and an adversarial batch classifier; prediction by swapping the perturbation code.
- **GEARS** [5]: dual GNN encoders over a Gene Ontology graph and a co-expression graph, compositional combination, cross-gene decoder.
- **CellOT** [6]: input-convex neural network parameterizing the Brenier potential; the transport map *T* = ∇*u* pushes *P₀* to *P^p* with minimal quadratic cost.
- **scGPT** [7] / **Geneformer** [8]: transformer foundation models pretrained with masked-gene modeling on tens of millions of cells; perturbation prediction by fine-tuning or embedding arithmetic.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Pooled Perturb-seq screens: experimental design and statistical structure

The experimental design of Perturb-seq determines the statistical structure of every downstream model [1]. A pooled library of sgRNAs (each targeting one gene, often with multiple guides per gene and non-targeting controls) is transduced at low multiplicity of infection so that most cells receive a single guide. A guide barcode — a polyadenylated transcript identifying the sgRNA — is captured alongside the cell's mRNA in droplets. After sequencing, each cell is assigned (i) a transcriptome and (ii) a perturbation label, 

Three design quantities govern statistical power, and harmonized resources now publish recommendations for them [10]:

1. **Cells per perturbation**: with ~200 cells per perturbation, mean-shift estimates stabilize; rare-cell-type responses require proportionally more.
2. **Read depth**: deeper sequencing reduces dropout-induced variance but exhibits diminishing returns beyond ~10⁴ UMIs per cell for DE detection.
3. **Guide multiplicity**: combinatorial screens (two guides per cell) grow quadratically; compressed sensing designs (group testing) recover sparse interaction structure with sub-quadratic cell counts.

> **Theorem (Pooling and assignment).** *Under random guide assignment with per-guide transduction probability q ≪ 1, the fraction of cells receiving exactly one guide is approximately q·e^(−q), and guide–cell assignments are exchangeable across cells, so that the perturbed sample for perturbation p is an i.i.d. draw from P^p up to doublet contamination.*

The i.i.d. structure licenses distribution-level modeling; doublet contamination (cells receiving two guides) is the principal violation, addressed by hashing or computational doublet detection [2].

### 4.2 Latent-variable encoders: scGen and compositional perturbation autoencoders

**scGen** (Lotfollahi et al., 2019) trains a variational autoencoder on control and perturbed cells jointly, then performs perturbation prediction by *latent arithmetic* [3]. Let **z** = encoder(**x**). The perturbation effect is estimated as the difference of latent means, δ̄ᵖ = **z̄**ᵖ − **z̄**⁰, and the response of a new control cell is predicted by decoding **z**⁰ + δ̄ᵖ. The key empirical finding: this arithmetic *transfers across cell types* — δ̄ᵖ learned in one cell type predicts responses in another — suggesting that perturbation directions are approximately conserved in the learned latent geometry.

**CPA** (compositional perturbation autoencoder, Lotfollahi et al., 2023) generalizes this by making the perturbation an explicit, learnable input rather than a post-hoc vector [4]. The encoder maps (**x**, *p*, *d*) — expression, perturbation, dosage — to a basal state **z**_basal and perturbation embedding **z**_p; an adversarial classifier removes perturbation information from **z**_basal, enforcing disentanglement; the decoder reconstructs **x̂** = decoder(**z**_basal, **z**_p, *d*). 

> **Theorem (Disentanglement and counterfactual validity).** *If the encoder achieves perfect disentanglement — i.e., **z**_basal ⫫ p — and the decoder is invertible in its basal argument, then decoder(**z**_basal, p′) samples from P(· | do(p′)) for the cell whose basal state is **z**_basal. Imperfect disentanglement leaks perturbation signal into the basal code and biases predictions toward the training perturbations.*



### 4.3 Neural optimal transport: CellOT and the minimal-effort principle

CellOT (Bunne et al., 2023) takes the unpairedness of the data as its starting point rather than as an inconvenience [6].

The biological justification is the **minimal-effort principle**: acute perturbations move cells along a few dimensions of expression space while preserving correlation structure, so the least-action coupling plausibly models the true counterfactual coupling [6].

> **Theorem (Brenier).** *Let P₀ be absolutely continuous and P^p have finite second moment. Then there exists a convex function u: ℝᴳ → ℝ such that T = ∇u uniquely (P₀-a.e.) minimizes ∫ ‖x − T(x)‖² dP₀ over all maps pushing P₀ to P^p.*

Crucially, where scGen/CPA predict *population-level* shifts applied uniformly, CellOT predicts a **cell-specific** response *T*(**x**⁰) — the first method in this thesis to operate genuinely at single-cell resolution.

The assumption's boundary is equally informative: when perturbations are too strong — the pre- and post-distributions differ too drastically — the minimal-effort hypothesis breaks, and OT-based prediction degrades along with every other method [6].

```rust
// Sketch: ICNN transport map training objective (dual Kantorovich form)
fn dual_objective(u: &Icnn, xs: &[Vec<f64>], ys: &[Vec<f64>]) -> f64 {
    // sup_u  E_{x~P0}[u(x)] - E_{y~P1}[u*(y)]  via convex conjugate
    let term0: f64 = xs.iter().map(|x| u.eval(x)).sum::<f64>() / xs.len() as f64;
    let term1: f64 = ys.iter().map(|y| u.conjugate(y)).sum::<f64>() / ys.len() as f64;
    term0 - term1
}
```

### 4.4 Knowledge-informed graph models: GEARS and combinatorial generalization

If OT solves the *unpaired coupling* problem, GEARS (Roohani, Huang & Leskovec, 2024) solves the *unseen perturbation* problem [5].

The architecture has five components [5]:

1. **Gene encoder**: a GNN over the GO-derived gene–gene graph produces gene embeddings capturing functional similarity.
2. **Perturbation encoder**: a GNN over a perturbation-similarity graph (built from co-expression) produces perturbation embeddings.
3. **Compositional module**: perturbation embeddings are summed (order-invariant) to handle multigene sets.
4. **Cross-gene layer**: an MLP over the concatenated per-gene representations models gene–gene coordination.
5. **Gene-specific decoders**: per-gene output heads emit post-perturbation expression.

Trained on Perturb-seq screens of >1,500 perturbations in RPE-1 and K562 cells [2], GEARS reduced MSE on held-out genes by >30% versus baselines and achieved ~40% higher precision classifying genetic-interaction subtypes (synergy, suppression, redundancy, neomorphism) [5].

> **Theorem (Graph-constrained generalization).** *Informally: if the perturbation-response function is L-Lipschitz with respect to the diffusion distance on the gene knowledge graph, then the prediction error for an unseen gene is bounded by L times its graph distance to the nearest seen perturbation. GEARS is an architectural realization of this bound — the GNN smooths perturbation embeddings over graph neighborhoods.*

The limitation is the prior's fidelity: GO annotations are incomplete and biased toward well-studied pathways, so GEARS's inductive bias is strongest precisely where biology is already known, and weakest for uncharacterized genes — the ones whose perturbation is most scientifically valuable (§6).

### 4.5 Foundation models: scGPT, Geneformer, and the scale hypothesis

The fourth generation bets on **scale**: pretrain a transformer on tens of millions of single-cell transcriptomes with a masked-modeling objective, then adapt to perturbation prediction [7][8].

The scale hypothesis has two components:

1. **Representation transfer**: pretraining distills gene–gene co-regulatory structure into embeddings, so that perturbation prediction becomes a low-data fine-tuning problem rather than a from-scratch learning problem.
2. **Zero-shot compositionality**: attention over gene tokens can, in principle, compose known single-gene effects into combinatorial predictions without explicit graph priors.

Recent benchmarking tempers the enthusiasm: rigorous studies find deep models — including foundation models — do not yet consistently outperform simple linear baselines on truly held-out perturbations.

## 5. Empirical Results / Proofs

We synthesize the reported quantitative results across the ten primary sources, evaluated on the scaffold of §3.1.

**Population-level prediction (marginal fidelity).** 

**Unseen single-gene perturbations.** GEARS, trained on ~1,500 Perturb-seq perturbations per cell line, reduced MSE on held-out genes by >30% relative to CPA and to a CellOracle-style linear network-propagation baseline, with the largest gains on genes whose GO neighborhoods were densely perturbed at training [5].

**Combinatorial perturbations and genetic interactions.** GEARS predicted four genetic-interaction subtypes with ~40% higher precision than prior approaches, identifying the strongest interactions at roughly twice the prior rate [5]; rich single-cell phenotypes make interaction geometry — not just scalar viability — observable [9].

**Foundation-model transfer.** scGPT fine-tuned for perturbation prediction outperformed from-scratch baselines on cell-type annotation and batch integration, and showed competitive perturbation-response performance; Geneformer predicted network-level consequences of *in silico* transcription-factor perturbation that were experimentally validated in follow-up assays [7][8].

**Benchmarks and sobriety.** Harmonized resources (44 datasets, uniform QC, E-statistic effect sizes) now make cross-study comparison possible and publish design recommendations: hundreds of cells per perturbation, attention to read depth, and standardized DE testing [10]. The most recent prospective benchmarks deliver a cautionary theorem-in-practice: under strict zero-shot splits, linear baselines remain fiercely competitive, implying that much of the signal in current datasets is additive and low-rank — and that demonstrating genuine non-additive, cell-specific prediction requires evaluation protocols as careful as the models themselves.

## 6. Limitations

**The identifiability ceiling.** The theorem of §2.3 is not a technicality but the field's permanent horizon: unpaired data underdetermine counterfactuals, and every method's predictions are only as credible as its structural assumption. Minimal-effort transport fails for strong perturbations [6]; latent arithmetic fails when perturbation directions are cell-type-specific; graph priors fail for unannotated genes [5]; pretrained priors fail under distribution shift to unseen tissues and diseases.

**Evaluation leakage.** Correlated genes, shared pathways, and cell-type structure make it easy to construct splits that look zero-shot but leak. The recent finding that linear baselines match deep models under strict protocols is best read as a critique of *benchmarks*, not of modeling — but it sets the bar: claims of non-additive or cell-specific prediction must survive prospective, leakage-free evaluation [10].

**Causal confounding.** Perturb-seq measures association under intervention, but technical confounders — cell-cycle state, capture efficiency, batch — covary with perturbation assignment in subtle ways. Disentanglement (CPA) and counterfactual identification frameworks address this only under untestable assumptions [4].

**Combinatorial explosion.** Even GEARS's impressive interaction-subtype results cover a tiny fraction of the 2×10⁸ two-gene space; higher-order combinations are essentially unexplored, and current models' compositional operators (summation of embeddings) are a crude approximation of true epistatic logic [5].

## 7. Conclusion

Single-cell perturbation response modeling has matured from an experimental curiosity into a coherent computational discipline with a shared mathematical core: counterfactual inference from unpaired interventional distributions. Its four methodological generations — latent-variable encoders [3][4], knowledge-informed graph models [5], neural optimal transport [6], and foundation models [7][8] — each contribute an indispensable idea: disentangled compositional representations, graph-structured inductive bias for the combinatorial explosion, principled coupling of unpaired marginals, and scale-driven representation transfer. The experimental substrate — pooled Perturb-seq screens at genome scale [1][2] and harmonized benchmarking resources [10] — has kept pace.

The central open problem is now sharply posed: **cell-specific counterfactual prediction for unseen combinatorial perturbations under leakage-free evaluation**. No existing method occupies that cell of the capability matrix. Progress will likely come from synthesis — transport-theoretic couplings inside pretrained generative models, causal constraints on graph architectures, and benchmarks with clinical-trial rigor. If the last decade taught the field to *measure* perturbation responses at single-cell resolution, the next must teach it to *predict* them with therapeutic-grade reliability.
