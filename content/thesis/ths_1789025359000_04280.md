# MLIR and the Polyhedral Model: Multi-Level Intermediate Representations, Loop Transformation Theory, and the Compilation of Machine Learning Workloads

## Abstract

This thesis examines the convergence of two major advances in compilation technology: Multi-Level Intermediate Representation (MLIR) and the polyhedral model of loop-nest optimization. MLIR, introduced by Lattner et al. at CGO 2021 [1], replaces the monolithic single-IR compiler with a framework in which multiple domain-specific *dialects* coexist and progressively lower toward machine code, enabling reuse of transformation infrastructure across machine learning, scientific computing, and hardware-design domains. The polyhedral model supplies the mathematical machinery — integer sets, affine relations, and schedule functions computed by parametric integer programming [2] — that makes aggressive loop transformations provably legal and systematically searchable, from Feautrier's minimum-delay schedules to the Pluto algorithm's tiling hyperplanes for parallelism and locality [3]. We show how these strands meet inside MLIR's *affine* and *Linalg* dialects, where polyhedral scheduling, tiling, fusion, and vectorization are realized as composable, transformation-oriented rewrites on structured IR [4], and how systems such as Tensor Comprehensions [5] demonstrated the payoff on GPU machine-learning workloads. The synthesis offers a principled path toward portable, high-performance ML compilers.

## 1 Introduction

Modern compilers face a structural dilemma. Machine-learning workloads are expressed at a high level — *tensor graphs, einsum notation, stencil programs* — yet must ultimately execute on hardware whose performance depends on loop order, tile sizes, memory coalescing, and SIMD lane utilization: details that only become visible near the bottom of the compilation stack. Classical single-IR compilers, LLVM prominent among them, force this entire descent through one rigid abstraction, so domain knowledge evaporates early and must be re-inferred by heroic analysis. The result is *software fragmentation*: every hardware vendor and every framework builds its own compiler stack, duplicating analyses, lowering passes, and code generators [1].

Two independent research traditions have attacked opposite ends of this gap. The **polyhedral model**, developed since the late 1980s, treats loop nests as geometric objects — iteration domains are integer polyhedra, dependences are affine relations, and transformations are schedule functions — enabling transformations as complex as loop fusion, skewing, interchange, tiling, and parallelization to be composed into a *single* schedule computed by integer linear programming [2][3]. Meanwhile, **MLIR** provides the compiler-engineering substrate for *multi-level* IRs: an extensible framework in which dialects at many abstraction levels coexist, progressively lower into one another, and share a common pass, pattern-rewriting, and verification infrastructure [1].

This thesis argues that the conjunction is more than coincidental: MLIR's *affine* and *Linalg* dialects are, in effect, a syntactic home for the polyhedral model, and the *transformation-oriented IR design* of modern MLIR tensor compilers [4] is the polyhedral methodology rendered as progressive lowering. We develop the mathematical foundations, show how classical polyhedral scheduling is embodied in MLIR passes, analyze the systems (Tensor Comprehensions [5], Pluto-style schedulers, Polygeist-style raising) that validate the approach on machine-learning workloads, and delineate the open problems that remain.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The Polyhedral Model: Core Abstractions

The polyhedral model applies to *static control parts* (SCoPs): program fragments in which loop bounds, conditionals, and array subscripts are affine functions of enclosing loop indices and symbolic parameters. Such fragments admit an exact mathematical description:

- **Iteration domains.** Each statement $S$ in a loop nest executes over the integer points of a polyhedron: $D_S = \{ \mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{Z}^d \mid A\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{a} \succeq 0 \}$, where the constraint matrix $A$ encodes loop bounds and guards.
- **Dependence relations.** Read-after-write, write-after-read, and write-after-write dependences are represented as affine relations between iteration vectors, e.g. $D_{S \to T} = \{ (\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{y}) \mid \mathbf{x} \in D_S, \mathbf{y} \in D_T, f(\mathbf{x}) = g(\mathbf{y}) \}$, with $f, g$ affine access functions. Exact dependence testing reduces to emptiness checks on such relations.
- **Schedules.** A schedule $\Theta$ maps each dynamic statement instance to a logical timestamp, $\Theta_S: D_S \to \mathbb{Z}^m$, where the timestamp is an affine function of the iteration vector. Loop transformations — interchange, reversal, skewing, fusion, distribution, tiling, shifting — all become *schedule changes*; code is regenerated by scanning the transformed iteration domains with polyhedral code generators such as CLooG.

> **Theorem (Schedule validity via Farkas' lemma):** A multidimensional affine schedule $\Theta$ is legal — it preserves all program dependences — if and only if, for every dependence relation $D_{S \to T}$, the implication $(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{y}) \in D_{S \to T} \Rightarrow \Theta_T(\mathbf{y}) - \Theta_S(\mathbf{x}) \succeq_{\text{lex}} 0$ holds. By Farkas' affine form of the duality theorem, these conditions translate into linear constraints on the schedule coefficients, making the search for *optimal* schedules an integer linear programming problem.

The computational engine behind these analyses is **parametric integer programming**, first implemented in PIP by Feautrier [2], and the **integer set library (isl)** of Verdoolaege, a portable C library manipulating sets and relations of integer points bounded by affine constraints — supporting intersection, union, projection, coalescing, and lexicographic minimization — which serves as the backend of the GCC Graphite and LLVM Polly frameworks [6].

### 2.2 From Feautrier to Pluto: The Scheduling Lineage

The scheduling lineage begins with **Feautrier's algorithm**, which computes *minimum-delay* (minimum-latency) affine schedules for arbitrary nested loops with affine bounds [2]. A decisive refinement came with **Pluto** (Bondhugula et al., 2008): instead of merely minimizing latency, Pluto finds affine transformations that simultaneously expose coarse-grained parallelism and improve temporal data locality, and — critically — that are *tilable*, meaning the transformed loops admit rectangular or space-time tiling for cache hierarchies and distributed memory [3].

| Scheduler | Objective | Technique | Output |
|---|---|---|---|
| Feautrier (1992) | Minimum latency | Parametric integer programming | 1-D affine schedules |
| Pluto (2008) | Parallelism + locality + tilability | ILP on tiling hyperplanes | Multidimensional affine transformations |
| isl-based (2010s) | General legality + objectives | Lexicographic minimization | Schedule trees |

Pluto's core idea is to find *tiling hyperplanes*: affine functions $\phi(\mathbf{x}) = \mathbf{h}\cdot\mathbf{x} + h_0$ whose level sets partition the iteration space into tiles with bounded inter-tile communication. Validity constraints bound the coefficients; objective functions minimize dependence distance (locality) and push dependence-carrying dimensions inward (parallelism). The result is fully automatic C-to-OpenMP parallelization with tiling for locality [3].

### 2.3 MLIR: Dialects, Progressive Lowering, and Rewriting

MLIR [1] restructured compiler IR design around three principles:

1. **Dialects** — namespaces of operations, types, and attributes. A program may mix dialects freely; *affine*, *linalg*, *vector*, *gpu*, *llvm*, *scf*, *arith* are all dialects.
2. **Progressive lowering** — high-level abstractions are decomposed into lower-level ones in stages, so each transformation operates where the relevant information is still present.
3. **Declarative rewriting** — transformations are specified as pattern rewrites (TableGen/ODS, PDL), applied by a greedy or dialect-conversion driver, rather than as hand-written AST visitors.

The IR itself is an *operation-region-block* hierarchy: every operation owns regions, regions contain blocks, blocks hold ordered operation lists with SSA block arguments — a uniform structural skeleton that every dialect shares.

## 3 Methodology

Our method is a systematic mapping between polyhedral theory and MLIR practice, structured in three layers.

**Layer 1 — Formal correspondence.** We identify each polyhedral primitive (iteration domain, dependence relation, affine schedule, tiling hyperplane) with its MLIR counterpart (*affine.for* bounds and affine maps, memory-effect interfaces, schedule-driven loop transforms, and the tiling transformations in the *affine* and *Linalg* dialects). The affine dialect's restriction that bounds and access functions be *semi-affine* maps is precisely the SCoP condition of the polyhedral model.

**Layer 2 — Compilation as schedule search.** We treat the MLIR tensor compilation pipeline — *linalg* → tiling → *affine* loops → *vector* → *llvm* — as a progressive realization of schedule composition: each lowering stage fixes one more schedule dimension, mirroring the layered schedule trees that isl-based polyhedral compilers produce.

**Layer 3 — Empirical grounding.** We anchor the theory in measured systems: Pluto's published PolyBench results [3], Tensor Comprehensions' reported speedups over vendor libraries on ML kernels [5], and the modular code-generation work in MLIR that achieves strong single-threaded CPU performance without systematic autotuning [4]. We also consult the MLIR codegen dialect overview maintained by the LLVM community [7] for the canonical lowering stack.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Dialect Stack as a Schedule Tree

Consider matrix multiplication entering MLIR as a single structured operation:

```mlir
linalg.matmul ins(%A, %B : tensor<128x128xf32>, tensor<128x128xf32>)
              outs(%C : tensor<128x128xf32>) -> tensor<128x128xf32>
```

In polyhedral terms this is a statement $S[i,j,k]$ with iteration domain $D_S = \{ (i,j,k) \mid 0 \le i,j,k < 128 \}$ and a reduction dependence along $k$. Progressive lowering refines this description stage by stage, exactly as a schedule tree refines execution order:

1. **Tiling** (e.g. tile sizes $32 \times 32 \times 32$) introduces outer tile loops and inner point loops — the polyhedral *strip-mine + interchange* realized as a schedule $\Theta$ with block dimensions outermost.
2. **Lowering to affine loops** makes the schedule explicit: `affine.for %ti = 0 to 4`, `%i = 0 to 32`, with access maps `(d0, d1) -> (d0 * 32 + d1)` encoding the blocked index expressions.
3. **Vectorization** maps the innermost schedule dimension to SIMD lanes via the `vector` dialect (`vector<8xf32>`), the polyhedral analog of aligning unit-stride dependences with the vector dimension [4].
4. **Bufferization and LLVM lowering** fix memory placement and emit machine code.

Each stage is legality-checked by the structure of the IR rather than by re-running dependence analysis — the *transformation-oriented IR design* advocated by recent MLIR tensor-compiler work [4], which deliberately avoids legality analyses on low-level IR by constructing abstractions whose decomposition is correct by construction.

### 4.2 Feautrier Scheduling Inside the Affine Dialect

The *affine* dialect is MLIR's syntactic realization of the SCoP: `affine.for` loops accept only bounds given by *affine maps* of enclosing indices and symbols, and `affine.load`/`affine.store` accept only affine access maps. A classic schedule computation can be staged as follows:

```python
# Feautrier-style scheduling sketched with isl (islpy)
import islpy as isl
ctx = isl.Context()
# Iteration domain of a 2-D statement nest
domain = isl.Set("{ S[i, j] : 0 <= i < N and 0 <= j < M }")
# Read-after-write dependence: S[i,j] reads what S[i-1,j] wrote
dep = isl.Map("{ S[i, j] -> S[i-1, j] : 0 < i < N and 0 <= j < M }")
# A valid 1-D schedule: theta(i,j) = i satisfies
# theta(i,j) - theta(i-1,j) = 1 >= 0 for every dependence pair
schedule = isl.Map("{ S[i, j] -> [i] }")
```

Feautrier's contribution [2] was to show that *minimum-delay* schedules are obtained by solving, for the dependence polyhedron, a parametric integer program whose lexicographically minimal solution yields schedule coefficients that are as small as possible — pushing every statement instance as early as dependences allow. Pluto generalized the objective: rather than pure latency, its ILP minimizes a weighted combination of dependence distances along schedule dimensions, subject to tiling-validity constraints that guarantee the resulting hyperplanes admit *tiling* — permutable bands of loops that can be blocked for caches and parallelized [3].

### 4.3 Tiling, Fusion, and Vectorization as One Schedule

In the polyhedral view, the "menu" of loop optimizations is not a menu at all — it is a single object. Given original loops $(i, j)$ over a rectangular domain, tiling by $B_i \times B_j$ is the schedule:

$$\Theta(i, j) = (\lfloor i/B_i \rfloor,\; \lfloor j/B_j \rfloor,\; i \bmod B_i,\; j \bmod B_j)$$

realized in MLIR by splitting one `affine.for` into tile and point loops. Fusion of producer-consumer nests is a schedule that interleaves their statement dimensions; Pluto's scheduler decides fusion vs. distribution automatically through its objective function, balancing the reuse gained by fusion against the parallelism unlocked by distribution [3]. Vectorization aligns the innermost schedule dimension with hardware lanes — a constraint that polyhedral vectorizers (Trifunović et al.; Vasilache et al.; Kong et al.) model by penalizing non-unit strides in the schedule's inner dimensions, precisely the transformation that MLIR's `vector` dialect lowering performs when it converts `affine` loops over contiguous memory into `vector.transfer_read`/`vector.transfer_write` operations [4].

The practical consequence for ML workloads is decisive: a convolution or attention kernel is a *single* SCoP whose optimal execution requires tiling across registers, L1, L2, shared memory, *and* threads — a schedule with a dozen dimensions. Pluto-style ILP finds such schedules automatically [3]; MLIR gives them a home as staged, verifiable dialect rewrites.

### 4.4 Linalg, Named Operations, and the ML Compilation Pipeline

The *Linalg* dialect sits above the affine layer as MLIR's structured tensor abstraction. Its design directly encodes polyhedral wisdom: a `linalg.generic` carries *iterator types* (`parallel`, `reduction`, `window`) and *indexing maps* that declare, up front, the iteration domain structure and the data-parallel vs. reduction character of each dimension — information a polyhedral extractor would otherwise have to rediscover from scalar loops. *Named operations* (`linalg.matmul`, `linalg.conv_2d_nchw_fchw`) go further, packaging domain-specific payload semantics that admit specialized lowering (e.g. to tensor-core instructions) without analysis.

The canonical MLIR codegen pipeline [7] is therefore a polyhedral pipeline in modern dress:

1. `linalg` on tensors → tile + fuse + pad (schedule decisions on the structured domain);
2. bufferization → `linalg` on memrefs / `affine` loops (schedule made explicit);
3. vectorization → `vector` dialect (SIMD/SIMT mapping);
4. `gpu` dialect + `llvm` dialect → machine code.

Systems predating MLIR proved each step in isolation: **Tensor Comprehensions** (2018) combined a Halide-inspired IR for computation with a polyhedral IR for loop structure, JIT-compiling mathematical descriptions of deep-learning DAGs to CUDA with operator fusion and size specialization, reporting up to $4\times$ speedups over NVIDIA libraries on ML kernels and production models at Facebook [5]. **Polygeist** (PACT 2021) closed the loop from the other direction, *raising* C/C++ into polyhedral MLIR so that legacy code can re-enter this pipeline. MLIR's contribution is to make all of these stages share one IR framework, one pattern-rewriting engine, and one verifier [1].

## 5 Empirical Results / Proofs

We summarize the quantitative evidence for the polyhedral-MLIR program in three strands.

**Strand A — Automatic scheduling.** Pluto's evaluation on the PolyBench suite showed that fully automatic affine scheduling with tiling matches or exceeds hand-tuned and autotuned baselines on multicore CPUs, with the scheduler itself running in seconds on kernels with hundreds of statements [3]. Later ILP-based schedulers with richer performance vocabularies (stride optimization, unroll-and-jam cost models, parallelism/reuse trade-offs) matched or beat Pluto on most PolyBench kernels on a 10-core Skylake-X *without* source-level tiling or autotuning, demonstrating that schedule-space search has headroom beyond Pluto's heuristics.

**Strand B — ML workloads.** Tensor Comprehensions reported up to $4\times$ speedup over NVIDIA libraries on kernels relevant to the ML community and on a production Facebook model, attributing the gains to polyhedral operator fusion and specialization for concrete tensor sizes [5]. In MLIR proper, the composable tensor-compiler work [4] demonstrated strong single-threaded CPU code generation from structured abstractions *without* systematic tuning, validating the transformation-oriented design.

**Strand C — A legality proof sketch.** The correctness of the entire edifice rests on the Farkas-lemma characterization of schedule validity (Section 2.1). A compact proof sketch: suppose dependence $(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})$ requires $\Theta(\mathbf{y}) - \Theta(\mathbf{x}) \succeq_{\text{lex}} 0$. Each difference is an affine function $\delta(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}) = \mathbf{c}\cdot(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}) + c_0$ constrained to be nonnegative over the dependence polyhedron $P$. By Farkas' lemma, $\delta \ge 0$ on $P$ iff $\delta$ is a nonnegative combination of the constraints defining $P$ — a *linear* condition on the schedule coefficients $\mathbf{c}$. Hence legality is exactly a system of linear inequalities, solvable by the same ILP machinery that optimizes the objective. Tiling validity adds the constraint that permutable schedule dimensions keep all dependences forward-pointing, which Pluto enforces as additional linear constraints [3].

Complexity caveat: general parametric ILP is exponential in the worst case, but the instances arising from real loop nests are small enough that modern solvers (isl's generalized-basis-reduction ILP, PIP) handle them in milliseconds to seconds.

## 6 Limitations

The synthesis has sharp boundaries, and honesty about them is part of the theory.

1. **The SCoP restriction.** The polyhedral model requires affine bounds and subscripts. Indirect accesses (`A[B[i]]`), data-dependent control flow, and while-loops fall outside the model; MLIR's affine dialect simply *refuses* to represent them, pushing such code to `scf` loops where polyhedral transforms do not apply. Real ML graphs are mostly affine, but sparse tensors, dynamic shapes, and ragged attention patterns stress the boundary.
2. **Schedule-space explosion.** Even with ILP, the space of tilings, fusions, and vectorization choices for a deep schedule tree is vast; Pluto's heuristics and cost models are approximations, and optimal schedules for multi-level memory hierarchies remain an active research problem.
3. **Bufferization and aliasing.** The linalg-on-tensors abstraction assumes functional, alias-free semantics; *bufferization* (choosing in-place vs. copy) is a global optimization with its own legality analysis, and getting it wrong silently changes numerics.
4. **Code-size and compile-time costs.** Polyhedral code generation (CLooG-style scanning) can produce loop nests whose size grows with the complexity of the schedule; MLIR mitigates this with structured ops but does not eliminate it.
5. **Verification gaps.** Schedule validity is provable, but the *lowering* from linalg to affine to vector is trusted compiler code; translation validation for MLIR lowering pipelines is still nascent.

## 7 Conclusion

The polyhedral model gave compiler science its deepest theory of loop transformation: iteration domains as integer sets, dependences as affine relations, and optimizations as schedules computed by integer programming [2][3][6]. MLIR gave compiler engineering its most flexible substrate: dialects at every abstraction level, progressive lowering, and declarative rewriting [1]. Their union — the *affine* and *Linalg* dialects, transformation-oriented lowering pipelines [4], and polyhedral schedulers operating on structured IR — is now the standard route from machine-learning mathematics to high-performance machine code, as demonstrated by Tensor Comprehensions' GPU results [5] and the growing ecosystem of MLIR-based tensor compilers [7].

The research frontier lies in *automation*: learned cost models that guide schedule search, exact handling of sparse and dynamic-shape workloads within affine-like abstractions, and verified lowering that carries the polyhedral legality guarantees all the way to machine code. Feautrier's parametric integer programs and Lattner's dialect infrastructure, born a quarter-century apart, are converging on a single vision — a compiler that understands both the mathematics of the workload and the architecture of the machine, and translates between them provably and automatically.
