# Polyhedral Compilation in MLIR: The Affine Dialect, Integer-Set Scheduling, and Halide-Style Lowering Pipelines

## Abstract

MLIR has re-architected compiler construction around progressively lowered, domain-specific intermediate representations, and its *affine dialect* is the clearest realization of this philosophy: it embeds the polyhedral model — iteration domains as integer sets, dependences as Presburger relations, transformations as affine schedules — directly into SSA-based IR, where the classical toolchain of Pluto, Polly, and the Integer Set Library can operate without leaving the compiler [3][6]. This thesis unifies affine compilation in MLIR, from the syntactic constraints that make `affine.for` analyzable, through exact dependence analysis, to the schedules realizing tiling, skewing, fusion, and parallelization, and finally the lowering pipelines carrying optimized nests to LLVM IR. We formalize the key legality argument — a schedule preserves semantics exactly when it respects the lexicographic order of every dependence polyhedron — and show how the *transform dialect* externalizes Halide-style schedules as first-class IR [2][4]. We close by quantifying the model's fundamental limits: exponential worst cases in integer programming, the parametric-tiling barrier, and the expressiveness ceiling of purely affine control.

## 1 Introduction

For four decades, the most aggressive loop-nest optimizations — cache tiling, loop fusion, skewing, time-skewed wavefront parallelization — lived in research compilers and source-to-source tools, walled off from production optimizers by a representational chasm. Classical production compilers lower loops to unstructured control flow early, discarding the iteration-space structure on which polyhedral transformations depend; recovering it later through *SCoP detection* (as LLVM's Polly does) is fragile, and the recovered representation is foreign to the SSA-based passes that must consume its results [2].

MLIR, introduced by Lattner and colleagues as a multi-level infrastructure for domain-specific computation, attacks this problem at its root [1]. Rather than reconstructing loop structure from flat control flow, MLIR preserves it in dialects whose operations carry exactly the constraints an optimizer needs. The **affine dialect** restricts loop bounds, access subscripts, and branch conditions to affine functions of enclosing induction variables and symbolic parameters. This single restriction is the hinge of everything that follows: it makes iteration domains describable as integer sets, dependences as Presburger relations, and legality checks as integer linear programs — decidable, exact, and mechanizable through libraries such as isl [6].

This thesis develops the theory and engineering of polyhedral compilation as realized in MLIR. Our contributions are expository and synthetic, assembled with the rigor of a survey a practitioner can implement from:

1. **A formal account of the affine dialect** — its ops, its affine-expression grammar, and why its constraints are precisely the ones Presburger arithmetic can decide.
2. **Exact dependence analysis** — dependence polyhedra from access relations and schedules, following Feautrier [5], mechanized in MLIR's affine analysis utilities.
3. **Transformations as schedule rewrites** — tiling, skewing, interchange, and fusion expressed uniformly, with legality reduced to a single lexicographic-positivity condition.
4. **Halide-style scheduling via the transform dialect** — optimization *strategy* as first-class IR: authored, versioned, and autotuned, echoing Halide's separation of algorithm from schedule [4].
5. **The lowering pipeline** — the progressive path from `affine` through `scf` and `vector` to the `llvm` dialect and LLVM IR [2].

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The Polyhedral Model

The polyhedral model represents each dynamic instance of a statement *S* inside a loop nest as an integer point in a *iteration domain* D_S, a (union of) convex polyhedron(s) described by affine inequalities over the loop iterators. A memory access becomes an *access relation* mapping iteration points to the array cells they touch; a *schedule* assigns each iteration a multi-dimensional timestamp in a logical time space. Two statements are fused, tiled, or parallelized by choosing a new schedule, and the transformation is legal when the new schedule preserves the original execution order of every pair of dependent iterations.

The decisive theoretical ingredient is **Presburger arithmetic**: first-order logic over the integers with addition and order. Feautrier showed in 1991 that exact dataflow analysis of array references — determining, for each read, precisely which write produced the value — is expressible and decidable in this logic, giving the first exact dependence analysis for affine programs [5]. Decidability comes at a cost: Presburger decision procedures are worst-case doubly exponential, and practical tools restrict themselves to fragments — existentially quantified conjunctions of affine inequalities — handled by the simplex-based and parametric integer programming algorithms inside isl [6].

### 2.2 Pluto, Polly, and the Classical Toolchain

**Pluto** (Bondhugula et al., 2008) framed automatic parallelization and locality optimization as the search for affine schedules minimizing a linear cost over dependence distances — yielding tiling, fusion, and parallelization from one integer linear program [3]. **Polly** brought the model into LLVM, detecting *static control parts* in LLVM IR and regenerating optimized code — proving polyhedral power attainable in production, while exposing the brittleness of structure recovery and the friction of a non-SSA representation beside SSA passes [2].

MLIR's affine dialect is best understood as the synthesis that resolves these frictions: the IR *is* the polyhedral representation, maintained in analyzable form throughout the pipeline and embedded in SSA via attributes and regions [2].

### 2.3 Halide: Algorithm Distinct from Schedule

Halide (Ragan-Kelley et al., 2013) attacked a neighboring problem — image-processing pipelines — by separating *what* is computed from *how* it is scheduled: tiling, vectorization, parallelization, and storage folding are expressed in a scheduling language applied to a pure functional algorithm [4]. Halide's schedules are hand-authored strategies, later partially automated by autoschedulers. MLIR's transform dialect generalizes this insight to arbitrary loop nests: optimization strategy becomes IR, written once, reused, and autotuned [2].

## 3 Methodology

Our method is analytic and constructive. We define the affine dialect's core operations and their mathematical counterparts, derive dependence polyhedra for a running example, and exhibit the principal transformations as schedule rewrites with explicit legality arguments. We then trace the engineering pipeline — analysis utilities, transformation passes, the transform dialect, and progressive lowering — mapping each theoretical object to its MLIR realization. Empirical claims are drawn from the published record of Pluto, Polly, Halide, and MLIR-based flows.

**Running example.** Throughout, we use a tiled matrix multiplication kernel:

```mlir
affine.for %i = 0 to 2048 {
  affine.for %j = 0 to 2048 {
    affine.for %k = 0 to 2048 {
      %a = affine.load %A[%i, %k] : memref<2048x2048xf64>
      %b = affine.load %B[%k, %j] : memref<2048x2048xf64>
      %c = affine.load %C[%i, %j] : memref<2048x2048xf64>
      %p = arith.mulf %a, %b : f64
      %s = arith.addf %c, %p : f64
      affine.store %s, %C[%i, %j] : memref<2048x2048xf64>
    }
  }
}
```

The iteration domain of the inner statement is the integer set D = { (i, j, k) : all three indices range over the nonnegative integers below 2048 }. Every bound is an affine function of enclosing iterators and constants; every subscript is an affine function of iterators. That is the entire contract of the affine dialect, and everything in this thesis follows from it.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Affine Dialect: Syntax with a Mathematical Shadow

The affine dialect's operations — `affine.for`, `affine.load`, `affine.store`, `affine.apply`, `affine.if`, `affine.parallel`, `affine.vectorize` — look like ordinary loop IR, but each carries a restriction that gives it a *mathematical shadow* in the polyhedral model [1]. Loop bounds must be affine maps of enclosing induction variables and symbols; a *symbol* is a value constant for the duration of the loop nest (a problem size, a tile size), which is why parametric analysis remains possible. Memory accesses must use affine subscripts into `memref` types. The operation `affine.apply` materializes an affine map as a computed value, and `affine.if` guards execution on affine conditions.

An **affine map** is written `(d0, d1)[s0] -> (d0 floordiv 32, d1, d0 mod 32)`: dimensions bound by enclosing loops, symbols standing for runtime constants, and a result tuple of affine expressions admitting floor division and modulo by constants. This grammar is not arbitrary — it is precisely the class of functions whose graphs are Presburger-definable, and therefore the class over which dependence questions are decidable [6].

Two design consequences deserve emphasis. First, the dialect needs no *recovery* phase: unlike Polly, which must detect SCoPs in general control flow, every `affine.for` nest is born analyzable. Second, the dialect *stays* analyzable through transformation: tiling an `affine.for` nest produces more `affine.for` nests [2]. This composability with SSA — polyhedral facts living in attributes and regions beside ordinary SSA values — lets affine passes interoperate with loop-invariant code motion, vectorization, and the rest of the pass manager instead of fighting them.

### 4.2 Dependence Analysis with Presburger Arithmetic

Dependence analysis answers the only question legality ever asks: *which pairs of statement instances must execute in their original relative order?* In the polyhedral model this is computed exactly. For the running example, the store to `C[i, j]` in iteration (i, j, k) and the load of `C[i, j]` in iteration (i, j, k+1) form a read-after-write (flow) dependence; expressed as a relation between source and target iterations, it is the integer set { (i, j, k) -> (i, j, k+1) : indices in range }, a Presburger relation computed by composing access relations with the schedule [5][6].

> **Theorem:** (Exactness of affine dependence analysis). For a program whose control flow, bounds, and access functions are affine in loop iterators and parameters, the set of dependent iteration pairs is exactly representable as a finite union of Presburger relations, and emptiness of any such relation is decidable.

MLIR mechanizes this through its affine analysis utilities, which build on isl-backed machinery to compute dependence information between `affine.load`/`affine.store` operations: for each pair of accesses to the same memref, the analysis forms the dependence polyhedron by intersecting the access relations with the ordering constraints of the original schedule, then tests emptiness via integer linear programming [6]. The classical dependence taxonomy drops out naturally:

| Dependence type | Relation shape | Preserves |
|---|---|---|
| Flow (read-after-write) | write iteration -> later read iteration | producer-consumer order |
| Anti (write-after-read) | read iteration -> later write iteration | value read before overwrite |
| Output (write-after-write) | write iteration -> later write iteration | last-writer-wins semantics |
| Input (read-after-read) | read iteration -> read iteration | never constraining |

Only the first three constrain transformations; input dependences never do. The analysis is *exact* — no may-alias conservatism — because affine subscripts make accessed sets computable rather than approximated [5]. Where the dialect's constraints are violated, the operations simply cannot be expressed in the affine dialect, and analysis degrades gracefully — a property enforced by construction rather than by heuristic.

The practical cost is the integer linear programming at the heart of every dependence query. Each query is a parametric ILP; isl solves these with algorithms that are worst-case exponential but fast on the small, structured instances arising from loop nests [6].

### 4.3 Tiling, Skewing, Interchange, and Fusion as Schedule Rewrites

The deep uniformity of the polyhedral model is that *every* loop transformation is a schedule change, and *every* legality check is the same check. A schedule Theta assigns each statement instance a logical timestamp; the transformation is legal when, for every dependence from instance x to instance y, the timestamp of x precedes that of y lexicographically [3].

> **Theorem:** (Schedule legality). A candidate affine schedule Theta preserves program semantics iff the image of every dependence relation under Theta is lexicographically positive — i.e., every dependent pair executes in its original relative order under the new timestamps.

Consider what this buys for each classical transformation on the running example:

- **Tiling** partitions the iteration space into blocks for cache locality. Tiling by 64 in i and j is the schedule (i, j, k) -> (i floordiv 64, j floordiv 64, i, j, k): outer components enumerate tiles, inner ones enumerate points within a tile. MLIR realizes this in `-affine-tile` and, in the higher-order polyhedral path, by expressing the tiling schedule as an MLIR affine map and generating code through isl [7].
- **Skewing** rewrites (i, j) to (i, i + j), slanting the iteration space so dependences become forward in time — the classic enabler of wavefront parallelization. Note that MLIR's *linalg* rationale explicitly discourages skewing: it often produces complex control flow that harms accelerator performance, better addressed by alternatives such as diamond tiling [2].
- **Interchange** permutes schedule dimensions, e.g. swapping i and j loops for spatial locality of `B` accesses. Legal exactly when no dependence is carried negatively by the swap.
- **Fusion** merges compatible nests to improve temporal locality between producer and consumer — MLIR's `-affine-loop-fusion` pass, driven by dependence analysis.

Pluto's landmark contribution was to observe that all of these — plus parallelization — emerge from a *single* optimization problem: find affine schedules minimizing dependence distances subject to legality, with tileability and parallelism as properties of the resulting schedule [3].

### 4.4 Halide-Style Scheduling via the Transform Dialect

Halide's enduring idea is the separation of the algorithm from its schedule: the same pipeline can be tiled, vectorized, parallelized, and storage-folded in dozens of ways by changing only scheduling directives [4]. MLIR's **transform dialect** generalizes this to arbitrary loop nests, as IR itself. A transform script matches operations — say, every `linalg.matmul` — and applies a sequence of named transformations with explicit parameters:

```mlir
transform.sequence failures(propagate) {
^bb0(%arg0: !transform.any_op):
  %matmul = transform.structured.match ops{["linalg.matmul"]} in %arg0
  %tiled, %loops:2 = transform.structured.tile %matmul [32, 32, 0]
  %vectorized = transform.structured.vectorize %tiled
  transform.yield
}
```

This script *is* the schedule: tile the outer two dimensions at size 32, vectorize the inner computation. Because the strategy is IR, it can be versioned, reused across models, generated by autotuners through MLIR's Python bindings, and composed with polyhedral legality checks rather than replacing them [2]. The affine dialect and the transform dialect thus divide the labor cleanly: the affine dialect guarantees that transformations are *expressible and checkable*, while the transform dialect makes the *choice* of transformations programmable. Halide required a domain-specific language for stencils and pipelines; MLIR's version works on any loop nest the affine dialect can represent, with isl-backed analysis standing in for Halide's bounds inference wherever exactness is required [4][6].

### 4.5 Lowering Pipelines to LLVM

Optimization is only half the story; the optimized nest must reach machine code. MLIR's answer is **progressive lowering**: the affine nest is rewritten through successively lower-level dialects, each step small, local, and verifiable, until LLVM IR emerges [1][2]:

1. **Affine optimization** (`-affine-loop-fusion`, `-affine-tile`, `-affine-parallelize`, `-affine-vectorize`): the polyhedral transformations of Sections 4.2-4.3, all within the affine dialect.
2. **Affine to SCF** (`-lower-affine`): `affine.for` becomes `scf.for`, `affine.load`/`store` become `memref.load`/`store`. Loop structure is preserved but the analyzability contract is discharged — the nest is now ordinary structured control flow.
3. **SCF to control flow** (`-convert-scf-to-cf`), **vector to LLVM** (`-convert-vector-to-llvm`), **memref to LLVM** (`-convert-memref-to-llvm`): each dialect lowers to the next, with the `vector` dialect carrying SIMD intent down to target-specific intrinsics.
4. **LLVM dialect to LLVM IR** (`mlir-translate`), then `llc` or `clang` for native code generation.

The crucial property is that lowering is *staged and inspectable*: `mlir-opt` can dump the IR after every pass, so a miscompiled tiling is debuggable at the level where it was introduced rather than in the wreckage of flat LLVM IR. Contrast this with Polly, where polyhedral optimization happens on recovered SCoPs inside LLVM's pass pipeline and the transformation's input and output are both LLVM IR — powerful, but opaque [2].

There is also a second, higher-level pipeline: many flows start from `linalg` operations on tensors (e.g. `linalg.matmul`), tile and fuse at the structured-op level where legality reasoning is simpler, and lower *into* the affine dialect only when loop-level polyhedral optimization is wanted — or bypass it entirely toward `scf` and `vector` [2]. The affine dialect is one station in a network of lowering paths, not a mandatory tollbooth — exactly the flexibility a multi-level infrastructure is for [1].

## 5 Empirical Results and Formal Guarantees

The empirical record of the polyhedral approach is strong, and the affine dialect inherits it. Pluto demonstrated automatic parallelization and locality optimization competitive with hand-tuned libraries on dense linear algebra, with speedups routinely in the multi-fold range over unoptimized baselines through tiling and fusion alone [3]. Polly showed that these transformations survive contact with a production compiler: on LLVM IR, polyhedral optimization of SCoPs yielded significant speedups on PolyBench kernels, the standard benchmark suite for this literature [2]. Halide's scheduled pipelines matched or exceeded hand-written assembly for image processing, validating the algorithm-schedule separation that MLIR's transform dialect generalizes [4].

For the affine dialect specifically, the published record centers on *correctness-by-construction* and *composability*. Bondhugula's tiling work in MLIR shows that expressing tiling schedules as MLIR affine maps and generating code through isl produces the expected nests with the expected locality behavior, including multi-level cache-plus-register schedules in the spirit of classical BLAS tuning [7]. Equally important: every affine transformation is guarded by the dependence analysis of Section 4.2, so legality is proved, not hoped for — the schedule-legality theorem of Section 4.3 is checked mechanically.

That said, intellectual honesty requires noting what the numbers do *not* show. End-to-end MLIR flows rarely beat vendor libraries (MKL, cuBLAS) on dense kernels, because those libraries encode man-centuries of microarchitectural tuning — prefetch distances, instruction selection, register blocking — below the level any loop transformation reaches. The polyhedral model's victories are in *generality*: it optimizes loop nests no library covers, and it does so automatically and provably [3].

## 6 Limitations

The affine dialect's power is bounded by the same mathematics that enables it, and a clear-eyed account must name the boundaries.

**Algorithmic complexity.** Every dependence query and schedule search bottoms out in integer linear programming — worst-case exponential, though fast on the small, structured instances of real loop nests [6]. Pluto's unified scheduling ILP is the extreme case: one elegant formulation whose solve time grows with the program [3].

**The expressiveness ceiling.** The affine contract excludes data-dependent control flow, indirect accesses, and non-affine subscripts — which is to say, it excludes sparse codes, graph algorithms, and much of modern machine learning's dynamic shapes. Programs outside the contract cannot use the dialect at all. Research directions (dynamic control flow, irregular indexing) exist but remain outside production flows [2].

**Parametric tiling.** When tile sizes are not known at transformation time, tiled address expressions and loop bounds become non-affine or require exponentially complex reasoning; this is a genuine barrier, not an implementation gap, and it is one reason the linalg dialect prefers *data-space* tiling through strided memref views, where complexity stays predictable [2].

**Strategy, not just mechanism.** The affine dialect supplies transformation mechanism and legality checking, but choosing *good* schedules — tile sizes, fusion depths, parallelization granularity — remains a search problem. The transform dialect makes the search programmable and autotunable, but it does not solve it; Halide's autoscheduling literature shows how hard the remainder is [4].

**Skewing and accelerator-hostile control flow.** As the linalg rationale notes, some classical transformations actively harm modern targets: skewing introduces complex control flow that degrades GPU performance, and the polyhedral model's love of general affine schedules can produce code shapes that vectorizers and accelerators dislike [2]. Legality is necessary but not sufficient for performance.

## 7 Conclusion

MLIR's affine dialect is the polyhedral model's coming-of-age in production compilers. By restricting loop IR to exactly the affine fragment that Presburger arithmetic decides, it makes exact dependence analysis and provably legal transformation a standing property of the IR rather than a recovered accident [5][6]. By keeping the IR in analyzable form through every transformation, it composes with SSA-based optimization instead of fighting it [2]. By externalizing strategy into the transform dialect, it generalizes Halide's algorithm-schedule separation to arbitrary loop nests [4]. And by lowering progressively to LLVM, it keeps every step inspectable [1].

The limitations are real — exponential worst cases, the affine expressiveness ceiling, parametric tiling, and unsolved automatic strategy selection — but they are the limitations of the mathematics, shared with every polyhedral system since Feautrier [3][5]. What MLIR contributes is the embedding itself: the polyhedral model as a first-class citizen of a mainstream, multi-level, SSA-based compiler. For anyone building loop optimizers today, the affine dialect is the reference design — the place where forty years of scheduling theory finally compiles.
