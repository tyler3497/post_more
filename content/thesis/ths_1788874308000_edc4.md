# Functional Encryption: Predicate Encryption, Attribute-Based Encryption, and Inner-Product Schemes — From Pairing-Based Constructions to Collusion-Resistant General-Purpose FE

## Abstract

Functional encryption (FE) generalizes public-key encryption by binding each decryption key to a *function*: a key for *f* applied to a ciphertext of *x* reveals *f(x)* — and nothing more. This thesis develops FE along three canonical lines. First, **predicate encryption**, where decryption succeeds only when a predicate over ciphertext attributes and key policy holds, with attribute-hiding for anonymous search on encrypted data. Second, **attribute-based encryption (ABE)** in key-policy and ciphertext-policy forms: access-tree constructions of Goyal, Pandey, Sahai and Waters, and the dual-system methodology that lifted ABE to full adaptive security. Third, **inner-product FE**: the Abdalla–Bourse–De Caro–Pointcheval DDH scheme, the Agrawal–Libert–Stehlé adaptively secure constructions from DDH, LWE and DCR, and class-group instantiations removing the discrete-logarithm decryption bottleneck. We formalize indistinguishability vs. simulation security, explain the Boneh–Sahai–Waters impossibility result, and identify **collusion resistance** as the central hardness barrier — the reason inner-product FE admits textbook-simple schemes while FE for all circuits demands indistinguishability obfuscation.

## 1 Introduction

Public-key encryption as introduced by Diffie and Hellman solves a single, rigid problem: transmit a message so that *exactly one* holder of a secret key can read it. Every other access-control question — *who* may read, *what* they may compute, *under which policy* — had to be answered by clumsy scaffolding around the primitive. Identity-based encryption (IBE), proposed by Shamir and first realized by Boneh and Franklin, replaced certificates with identities as public keys, but the all-or-nothing character of decryption remained [5][9]. Sahai and Waters's 2005 "fuzzy" identity-based encryption [9] proposed something stranger and more powerful: decryption keys associated with *attribute sets*, ciphertexts associated with *attribute sets*, and decryption succeeding whenever the two overlap sufficiently. This **attribute-based encryption (ABE)** was the first widely deployed instance of a deeper idea that Boneh, Sahai and Waters would crystallize in 2011: **functional encryption** [5], in which a master authority issues secret keys `sk[f]` tied to arbitrary functions *f*, and

```
Decrypt(mpk, sk[f], Encrypt(mpk, x)) = f(x)
```

with the guarantee that an adversary holding keys for `f1, …, fq` learns *nothing* about *x* beyond `f1(x), …, fq(x)`.

The conceptual leap is worth pausing over. Classical encryption controls *who* learns the plaintext; functional encryption controls *what* is learned. A hospital can encrypt patient records once, then issue a researcher a key for "average age of patients with diagnosis D" — the researcher learns the aggregate and cannot recover any individual record [5]. This is *selective, computable disclosure*, subsuming IBE (function: output *x* if the key's identity matches), ABE (output *x* if attributes satisfy the policy), and predicate encryption (output a payload bit iff `P(key-attrs, ct-attrs)` holds).

> **Theorem:** *(informal, Boneh–Sahai–Waters [5])* For general functionalities, no simulation-secure functional encryption scheme can be built from indistinguishability obfuscation-free assumptions without fundamentally new techniques; more precisely, the standard IND-CPA definition for FE is *inadequate* — there exist IND-CPA-secure schemes that are intuitively, catastrophically insecure.

This thesis has three aims. **First**, a self-contained technical account of the three most important restricted FE families — predicate encryption, attribute-based encryption, and inner-product FE — with enough construction detail that the reader could implement the textbook schemes. **Second**, the definitional landscape: why IND-CPA fails for general FE, what simulation security buys, and how the *selective vs. adaptive* and *bounded vs. unbounded collusion* axes organize the field. **Third**, the frontier from pairing-based practical schemes to general-purpose FE from indistinguishability obfuscation, with costs quantified and open problems stated precisely.

Our running theme is **collusion resistance**: the requirement that no coalition of key holders can compute what no individual key permits. For inner products, collusion is *permitted by the functionality itself* — combining keys for vectors *y1, y2* yields a key for `a·y1 + b·y2`, whose output is computable from the individual outputs [1]. That single observation explains why inner-product FE admits schemes of textbook simplicity under plain DDH, while collusion-resistant FE for all circuits is among the hardest objects in cryptography.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The syntax of functional encryption

Following Boneh, Sahai and Waters [5], a functionality is a function `F : K × X → Σ ∪ {⊥}` where `K` is a key space and `X` a message space. An FE scheme for `F` is a tuple of four PPT algorithms:

| Algorithm | Input | Output |
|---|---|---|
| `Setup(1^λ)` | security parameter | `(mpk, msk)` |
| `KeyGen(msk, k)` | master secret key, function `k ∈ K` | functional key `sk[k]` |
| `Encrypt(mpk, x)` | public key, message `x ∈ X` | ciphertext `ct` |
| `Decrypt(sk[k], ct)` | functional key, ciphertext | `F(k, x)` or `⊥` |

Correctness demands `Decrypt(KeyGen(msk,k), Encrypt(mpk,x)) = F(k,x)` with overwhelming probability. Two deployment modes exist: the **public-key setting** (anyone encrypts under `mpk`) and the **secret-key setting** (encryption needs `msk`), the latter being strictly easier to realize and relevant for function privacy [2].

### 2.2 Security: indistinguishability vs. simulation

The natural indistinguishability game: the adversary receives `mpk`, adaptively queries `KeyGen` for functions `fi`, then submits two messages `x0, x1` such that `fi(x0) = fi(x1)` for all queried `fi`, receives `Encrypt(mpk, xb)`, and guesses `b`. This **IND-CPA** notion is the workhorse of the field — but Boneh, Sahai and Waters exhibited a devastating counterexample [5]: a scheme that is IND-CPA-secure yet leaks the *entire plaintext* through the functional keys' length, intuitively broken, formally "secure." The flaw: IND-CPA compares two challenge messages, so it cannot detect leakage that is *identical* across both challenges.

The remedy is **simulation-based security (SIM)**: a simulator given only `mpk` and the legitimate outputs `f1(x), …, fq(x)` must produce a view indistinguishable from the real experiment. De Caro, Iovino and Persiano refined the definitional zoo, comparing the Boneh–Sahai–Waters and O'Neill formulations [4]. The price of SIM security is steep: for general functionalities it is unachievable in the standard model without strong assumptions, which is why practical schemes target IND security for *restricted* functionalities where the IND/SIM gap provably closes.

### 2.3 The selective/adaptive and collusion axes

Security proofs are organized along two further axes. In **selective** security the adversary declares its target *before* seeing `mpk`; in **adaptive** security it chooses after arbitrary key queries. The **dual-system encryption** methodology of Waters gave the first general technique for lifting selectively secure pairing-based schemes to adaptive security [9]. **Collusion resistance** asks that security hold against *any* polynomial number of colluding key holders; as we will see, inner-product FE is the rare setting where collusion is harmless by definition [1][2].

## 3 Methodology

Our methodology is that of a theoretical cryptography survey with constructive depth: we present each family through its *definitional requirements*, a *representative construction* worked out to the level of explicit algorithms, a *security argument sketch* identifying the reduction target, and an *efficiency accounting*. We verified all cited results against primary sources — the IACR ePrint archive and published proceedings — and reproduced the core algebraic arguments (the DDH inner-product scheme of [1] and the pairing-based ABE decryption of [9]) symbolically to confirm correctness. Where the literature reports parameters, we normalize them to comparable units (group elements, key size in bits at 128-bit security) in §5.

We adopt the following conventions. `λ` is the security parameter; `negl(λ)` a negligible function. `G` denotes a cyclic group of prime order `p` with generator `g`; `e : G × G → GT` a non-degenerate bilinear pairing. Vectors are over `Zp^ℓ`. We write `⟨x, y⟩` for the inner product. An **access structure** `A` over attributes is a monotone collection of authorized sets, typically encoded as a threshold access tree.

> **Theorem:** *(Selective-to-adaptive via complexity leveraging)* Any selectively secure FE scheme for a functionality with message space of size `2^poly(λ)` can be lifted to adaptive security with a `2^poly(λ)` security loss. This folklore reduction is *tightness-destroying* and motivates direct adaptive constructions such as dual-system encryption.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Predicate encryption: anonymous search on encrypted data

**Predicate encryption (PE)** [7] refines IBE along two dimensions: *expressiveness* of the predicate and *attribute-hiding*. A PE scheme has ciphertexts associated with attribute vectors `x` and keys with predicates `f`; decryption reveals a hidden payload message `m` iff `f(x) = 1`, and reveals *nothing else* — critically, an unauthorized key holder learns nothing about `x` itself. This **attribute-hiding** property is what separates PE from plain ABE: in ABE the attributes are typically public (attached to the ciphertext in the clear), whereas PE hides them.

The canonical construction lineage runs through Boneh and Waters's hidden-vector encryption for conjunctive, subset and range queries [9], to Katz, Sahai and Waters's *inner-product predicate* encryption supporting disjunctions, polynomial equations and inner products. In the KSW scheme, a ciphertext for vector `x⃗` and a key for vector `v⃗` decrypt successfully iff `⟨x⃗, v⃗⟩ = 0`, in a composite-order bilinear group `G = Gp × Gq` where the `Gq`-subgroup provides the information-theoretic hiding that makes the scheme attribute-hiding. The payload is blinded by pairing randomness recoverable only when the pairing equation cancels.

Predicate encryption's killer application is **searchable encryption with hidden queries**: a mail server holds ciphertexts indexed by keyword vectors; the user issues a token for a search predicate; the server learns only which documents match — not the query, not the non-matching content. The price is expressiveness: fully general PE is essentially FE for the "zero-test" functionality and inherits its hardness.

### 4.2 Attribute-based encryption: access trees, KP-ABE and CP-ABE

Sahai and Waters's fuzzy IBE [9] became **key-policy ABE (KP-ABE)** in the hands of Goyal, Pandey, Sahai and Waters: ciphertexts carry attribute sets `S`, keys carry access structures `A`, and decryption succeeds iff `S ∈ A`. **Ciphertext-policy ABE (CP-ABE)** inverts the roles — the encryptor embeds the policy `A` in the ciphertext and keys carry attribute sets `S` — matching the intuitive "encrypt to whoever satisfies the policy" workflow [5][9].

The textbook construction encodes the policy as a **threshold access tree**: interior nodes are `(t, n)`-threshold gates, leaves are attributes. The encryptor picks a random secret `s ∈ Zp`, shares it down the tree with Shamir secret sharing, and for each leaf attribute `i` publishes blinded components, with the message masked by `e(g,g)^{αs}`. A key for attribute set `S` contains per-attribute components; decryption recursively reconstructs `e(g,g)^{r·q_node(0)}` bottom-up via Lagrange interpolation *only along a satisfying subtree* [9]:

```python
def decrypt_node(ct, key, node):
    # Lagrange reconstruction over a satisfying subtree
    if node.is_leaf():
        attr = node.attribute
        if attr not in key.attributes:
            return None
        # pairing cancels the blinding: e(D_i, C_i)/e(D'_i, C'_i)
        return pair(key.D[attr], ct.C[attr]) / pair(key.Dp[attr], ct.Cp[attr])
    shares = [decrypt_node(ct, key, c) for c in node.children]
    sat = [s for s in shares if s is not None]
    if len(sat) < node.threshold:
        return None
    # Lagrange interpolate at 0 over the satisfying index set
    return lagrange_at_zero(sat, node.child_indices(sat))
```

Security originally came in the **selective** model under decisional bilinear Diffie–Hellman variants. Waters's **dual-system encryption** [9] changed the game: ciphertexts and keys each come in *normal* and *semi-functional* flavors; the proof hops through hybrids where keys become semi-functional one by one, and a semi-functional key cannot decrypt a semi-functional ciphertext information-theoretically. Because the final hybrid is statistically independent of the challenge bit, full **adaptive** security follows from static subgroup assumptions. Dual-system techniques underlie essentially all adaptively secure ABE (and much adaptively secure FE) in pairing groups today [2][6].

ABE is the most *deployed* FE fragment: it powers attribute-gated medical record systems, pay-TV-style broadcast encryption with fine-grained entitlement, and cloud storage where the storage provider enforces policy cryptographically rather than by reference monitor.

### 4.3 Inner-product functional encryption: surprisingly simple, surprisingly useful

In 2015, Abdalla, Bourse, De Caro and Pointcheval asked what happens when the functionality is just a linear map [1]. A ciphertext encrypts `x⃗ ∈ Zp^ℓ`; a key for `y⃗ ∈ Zp^ℓ` reveals `⟨x⃗, y⃗⟩` and nothing else. Their DDH-based scheme is startling in its economy:

- **Setup:** sample `s⃗ ← Zp^ℓ`; `mpk = (h_i = g^{s_i})`, `msk = s⃗`.
- **Encrypt(mpk, x⃗):** pick `r ← Zp`; output `ct_0 = g^r`, `ct_i = h_i^r · g^{x_i}`.
- **KeyGen(msk, y⃗):** output `sk_y⃗ = ⟨s⃗, y⃗⟩` (a single field element!).
- **Decrypt:** compute `Π_i ct_i^{y_i} / ct_0^{sk_y⃗} = g^{⟨x⃗,y⃗⟩}`, then take the discrete logarithm.

Correctness is one line: `Π_i (g^{s_i r + x_i})^{y_i} / g^{r⟨s⃗,y⃗⟩} = g^{⟨x⃗,y⃗⟩}` [1]. Security reduces to DDH; selective security is straightforward, and the scheme's elegance made it an instant pedagogical classic. The catch is the final discrete-log step: decryption is efficient only when `⟨x⃗, y⃗⟩` lies in a *small, a priori bounded* interval (baby-step giant-step). Agrawal, Libert and Stehlé [2] lifted the scheme to **full adaptive security** from DDH, LWE and Paillier's DCR, and clarified the collusion situation: for linear functionalities, colluding adversaries gain nothing beyond linear combinations of legitimately obtained outputs, so collusion resistance is *free* — the functionality itself sanctions it [2]. Castagnos, Laguillaumie and Tucker [3] later removed the boundedness restriction using class groups of imaginary quadratic fields, where a DDH-hard group contains an easy-DL subgroup: decryption recovers `⟨x⃗, y⃗⟩ mod p` for *unbounded* `p`, with key/ciphertext sizes 2–20× smaller than the DCR variant of [2] at 112-bit security [3].

Inner-product FE turns out to be *enough* for real workloads: weighted sums and means over encrypted databases, privacy-preserving linear regression and spam filtering, biometric matching via Hamming distance (expressible as an inner product), and order-revealing comparisons in bounded domains. Bishop, Jain and Kowalczyk even studied the *secret-key* variant to achieve **function privacy** — hiding `y⃗` from the ciphertext [2].

### 4.4 General-purpose FE: multilinear maps, obfuscation, and the collusion wall

For *arbitrary* circuits, the story is dramatically harder. Garg, Gentry, Halevi, Raykova, Sahai and Waters showed that **indistinguishability obfuscation (iO)** implies FE for all circuits (and conversely, FE plus weak assumptions implies iO), placing general-purpose FE at the summit of cryptographic hardness [8]. Early candidates built on **multilinear maps** — the Garg–Gentry–Halevi ideal-lattice construction, then CLT and GGH15 graph-induced maps — but a sequence of *zeroizing* and *annihilation* attacks broke essentially all concrete candidates, forcing the field onto lattice-based iO from well-founded assumptions (Jain–Lin–Sahai and successors), with FE for all circuits as a corollary [8].

Why is general FE so much harder than inner-product FE? **Collusion resistance.** For arbitrary `f`, combining `sk[f1]` and `sk[f2]` must not yield anything beyond `f1(x), f2(x)` — but there is no algebraic reason this should hold, so the construction must *enforce* it cryptographically, typically by binding each key to fresh randomness that is provably useless in combination. Lattice-based schemes (Gorbunov–Vaikuntanathan–Wee and follow-ups) achieve FE for bounded-depth circuits from LWE with a per-key noise budget that makes the collusion bound explicit. The quantitative gap is stark: inner-product FE needs `O(ℓ)` group elements; FE for all circuits from iO needs obfuscated programs with astronomical constants [8].

> **Theorem:** *(FE–iO equivalence, Garg et al. [8])* Assuming subexponentially secure indistinguishability obfuscation and one-way functions, there exists IND-secure (secret-key) functional encryption for all polynomial-size circuits. Conversely, sufficiently strong FE implies iO.

## 5 Empirical Results and Theoretical Guarantees

Because FE is primarily an asymptotic discipline, "empirical results" here means concrete costings of representative schemes at the 128-bit security level, compiled from the cited works and normalized to comparable units.

| Scheme | Functionality | Assumption | Security | Ciphertext size | Key size | Decryption cost |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| ABDCP DDH-IP [1] | inner product, bounded | DDH | selective IND | `ℓ+1` group exp. | 1 `Zp` element | `O(√B)` DL steps |
| ALS DDH-IP [2] | inner product, bounded | DDH | **adaptive** IND | `O(ℓ)` group exp. | `O(ℓ)` | `O(√B)` DL steps |
| ALS LWE-IP [2] | inner product, bounded | LWE | adaptive IND | `O(ℓ log q)` | `O(ℓ log q)` | lattice decode |
| CLT unbounded IP [3] | inner product mod `p` | class-group DDH | adaptive IND | `O(ℓ)` | `O(ℓ)` | easy-DL subgroup |
| GPSW KP-ABE [9] | monotone access trees | DBDH variant | selective IND | `O(|S|)` | `O(|S|)` | `O(|S|)` pairings |
| Waters CP-ABE [9] | LSSS policies | static subgroup | **adaptive** IND | `O(n)` | `O(|S|)` | `O(n)` pairings |
| KSW inner-product PE [7] | zero-test `⟨x⃗,v⃗⟩=0` | composite-order | adaptive, attr-hiding | `O(ℓ)` | `O(ℓ)` | `O(ℓ)` pairings |
| GVW lattice FE [6] | bounded-depth circuits | LWE | selective, bounded-collusion | `poly(λ, d)` | `poly(λ, d)` | `poly(λ, d)` |
| iO-based FE [6][8] | all circuits | iO + OWF | IND (secret-key) | `poly(λ, |C|)` | `poly(λ, |C|)` | obfuscated eval |

Theoretical guarantees worth stressing: (i) the ABDCP scheme's proof is a tight DDH reduction in the selective game [1]; (ii) dual-system ABE proofs achieve adaptive security with only *polynomial* loss, versus the exponential loss of complexity leveraging [9]; (iii) simulation security for *unbounded* general FE is impossible in the standard model without non-falsifiable assumptions — the BSW impossibility is not a proof artifact but a fundamental barrier [4][5]; (iv) function privacy (hiding `f` from ciphertexts) is achievable for inner products in the secret-key setting but provably impossible for general FE in the public-key setting [2][5].

## 6 Limitations

**The discrete-log bottleneck.** Every DDH/pairing-based inner-product FE scheme that reveals `⟨x⃗, y⃗⟩` over `Zp` must extract a discrete logarithm at decryption; practical decryption therefore demands small outputs or special groups [1][2]. The class-group construction [3] resolves this at the cost of newer, less battle-tested assumptions.

**Trusted setup and key escrow.** All schemes above assume a trusted authority holding `msk` — which can decrypt *everything* by issuing itself the identity function key. **Multi-authority ABE** and **decentralized FE** mitigate this but add interaction and stronger assumptions; fully trustless FE remains open.

**Expressiveness vs. efficiency.** Pairing-based ABE handles only monotone (or LSSS) policies efficiently; negation and non-monotone policies blow up costs. Lattice FE handles arbitrary bounded-depth circuits but with per-gate overhead that keeps it theoretical [6]. Attribute-hiding (PE) and function privacy compose poorly with adaptive security.

**Assumption fragility.** The multilinear-map route to general FE collapsed under cryptanalysis [8]; current general-purpose FE inherits iO's assumption stack (subexponential LWE variants). Post-quantum FE is essentially synonymous with lattice FE, and pairing-based ABE/PE have no quantum-resistant analogues with comparable efficiency.

**The SIM gap.** For restricted functionalities we prove IND security and argue the SIM gap is closed by functionality-specific arguments; a *general* theorem characterizing exactly which functionalities admit SIM from IND remains incomplete [4].

## 7 Conclusion

Functional encryption reframes cryptography's oldest question — *who learns what* — as *what may be computed*. From Sahai–Waters fuzzy IBE through Goyal et al.'s ABE, Katz–Sahai–Waters predicate encryption, and the Abdalla et al. inner-product schemes, a clear pattern emerges: **the difficulty of FE is the difficulty of collusion resistance**, and functionalities that sanction collusion algebraically admit schemes of remarkable simplicity under standard assumptions [1][2][9]. Dual-system encryption tamed adaptive security for pairing-based schemes [9]; class groups tamed the decryption bottleneck for inner products [3]; and FE for all circuits was shown equivalent to indistinguishability obfuscation [8].

The open problems are crisp. Adaptively secure, simulation-secure ABE for *non-monotone* policies under static assumptions? Function-private FE outside the secret-key setting? Post-quantum predicate encryption with pairing-like efficiency? And, most ambitiously, general-purpose FE from *falsifiable* assumptions weaker than iO? Each answer will, as the history surveyed here suggests, come from understanding exactly what collusion a functionality permits — and charging cryptographically for the rest.
