# Grammar-Constrained Synthesis under Oracle Interaction: A Formal Account of SyGuS, CEGIS, and the Convergence of Enumerative, Symbolic, and LLM-Assisted Search

## Abstract

Program synthesis is the automatic construction of a program that provably satisfies a specification. The *syntax-guided synthesis* (SyGuS) formulation reframes this as the joint satisfaction of two constraints: a semantic specification as a logical formula and a syntactic constraint as a context-free grammar delimiting candidate programs. This paper gives a unified formal account of the SyGuS problem and its search architectures. We define the SyGuS conjecture modulo a background theory, develop the counterexample-guided inductive synthesis (CEGIS) loop as oracle-guided inductive synthesis with soundness and relative-completeness arguments, and compare enumerative, constraint-based, and divide-and-conquer strategies by query complexity and encoding cost. We show that sketch-based synthesis, oracle-guided component assembly with distinguishing inputs, library learning (DreamCoder), and LLM-assisted synthesis all instantiate the same oracle-interaction pattern, and we close with an empirical survey and open problems in specification elicitation, unrealizability, and verified neural-symbolic integration.

## 1 Introduction

The classical synthesis problem — find a program meeting a logical specification — is under-constrained: the same specification admits an enormous number of implementations, and search over *all* programs is infeasible. The decisive insight of syntax-guided synthesis is that the syntactic form of candidates is itself information: exposing it as a formal constraint turns synthesis into a *structured* search problem amenable to satisfiability modulo theories (SMT) [1].

A SyGuS problem has three inputs: a background theory *T* fixing types and operations; a semantic specification *φ(x, f(x))*, a quantified formula over *T* extended with the function-to-synthesize *f*; and a syntactic set of candidates *E* given as a context-free grammar over *T*-terms. The task: output *e ∈ E* with *φ[f/e]* valid in *T* [1]. The canonical example — synthesizing `max(x, y)` satisfying *f(x,y) ≥ x ∧ f(x,y) ≥ y ∧ (f(x,y) = x ∨ f(x,y) = y)* from an arithmetic grammar with `ite` — captures the structure: logic constrains *behavior*, grammar constrains *form*.

This paper argues that the diverse architectures proposed since 2013 — enumerative solvers, constraint-based solvers, sketch completion engines, oracle-guided assemblers, library learners, and LLM-driven generators — are instantiations of one abstraction: *oracle-guided inductive synthesis*. In each, an inductive learner proposes candidates from a hypothesis class defined by the grammar, and an oracle (verifier, I/O oracle, or test suite) answers queries that prune the class. We formalize the CEGIS loop as the canonical instance and show that neural program synthesis fits as a learned prior over the grammar that replaces blind enumeration with informed search, while verification oracles retain the correctness guarantee.

## 2 Background and Related Work

### 2.1 From sketching to SyGuS

Program sketching, introduced by Solar-Lezama et al., lets a programmer write a *partial program* — a sketch — with "holes" the synthesizer fills so the completion satisfies a specification [2]. Holes may stand for expressions, constants, or control structure; the synthesizer searches hole completions via constraint solving. A sketch is, in effect, a grammar in disguise: fixed code plays the role of context, holes the role of nonterminals. SyGuS made the grammar an *explicit, theory-level* object with the standardized SyGuS-IF format [1], enabling a shared competition and solver ecosystem.

### 2.2 Oracle-guided synthesis

Jha, Gulwani, Seshia, and Tiwari introduced *oracle-guided component-based program synthesis*, in which a complete logical specification is replaced by an I/O oracle — a black box mapping inputs to desired outputs — combined with a library of components [3]. The algorithm encodes a straight-line assembly of components as SMT constraints and queries the oracle on a *distinguishing input* — an input on which the candidates consistent with prior answers disagree — repeating until the remaining candidates are semantically unique. This established two enduring principles: interaction with an oracle can substitute for a complete formal specification, and the *choice of query* — distinguishing inputs rather than random tests — is the principal lever on convergence rate.

### 2.3 The SyGuS competition and solver taxonomy

The SyGuS competition standardized benchmarks across theories (linear integer arithmetic, bitvectors, strings) and tracks (general, programming-by-example, invariant synthesis) [1]. Solvers divide into *enumerative* strategies, which generate grammar terms in size order and test each against the specification, and *constraint-based* strategies, which encode "there exists a grammar term satisfying the specification" as a single SMT query over an encoding of the grammar. Enumerative methods dominate in practice: the cost of the monolithic symbolic encoding frequently exceeds the cost of enumerating and testing candidates.

### 2.4 Neural and library-learning approaches

Ellis et al. developed DreamCoder, a wake–sleep Bayesian program-learning system that interleaves neural-guided search with *library learning*: compressing solutions to solved tasks into new abstractions that extend the DSL, thereby restructuring the search space itself [4]. On the neural side, Austin et al. evaluated large language models on the MBPP and MathQA-Python benchmarks, finding that synthesis performance scales log-linearly with model size and that 137B-parameter models solve 59.6% of MBPP tasks few-shot [5]. Chen et al. introduced Codex and HumanEval, showing that code-finetuned transformers solve 28.8% of HumanEval problems with a single sample and up to 70.2% with 100 samples [6]. These lines motivate the central question: how does statistical program generation compose with the *guarantees* the SyGuS/CEGIS tradition demands? Alur's formulation of synthesis conjectures *∃f. ∀x. P(f, x)* provides the unifying logical core [7].

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 The SyGuS problem, precisely

Fix a background theory *T* (e.g., LIA, bitvectors, strings) with signature *Σ_T*. A **SyGuS problem** is a triple *(T, φ, G)* where:

- *φ(x̄, f(x̄))* is a formula in the signature *Σ_T ∪ {f}*, with *f* an uninterpreted function symbol of declared type;
- *G* is a context-free grammar over *Σ_T* with start symbol *S*, defining the language *L(G)* of candidate terms.

A solution is a term *e ∈ L(G)* with *φ[f ↦ e]* *T*-valid. In SyGuS-IF, *G* is given by `synth-fun` declarations and *φ* by `constraint` commands [1].

Our methodology is analytic and constructive: we (i) formalize CEGIS as oracle-guided inductive synthesis with explicit query/response types; (ii) compare search strategies by *oracle query complexity* and *encoding blowup*; and (iii) construct a neural-symbolic architecture in which an LLM proposes over *L(G)* while a verifier oracle guarantees soundness. Complexity arguments are proved from the definitions; claims about existing systems rest on the cited literature.

### 3.2 The CEGIS loop

Counterexample-guided inductive synthesis separates *search* (inductive generalization from examples) from *verification* (deductive checking against the full specification):

```python
def cegis(G, phi, verifier, synthesizer):
    """G: grammar; phi: specification; returns e in L(G) with |= phi[f/e]."""
    examples = []                      # set of (x, required) observations
    while True:
        cand = synthesizer.synthesize(G, phi, examples)   # inductive step
        if cand is None:
            return None                # grammar exhausted: unrealizable
        cex = verifier.verify(cand, phi)                  # deductive step
        if cex is None:
            return cand                # no counterexample: provably correct
        examples.append(cex)           # strengthen the inductive constraint
```

The *synthesizer* solves the *relaxed* problem — find *e ∈ L(G)* consistent with the accumulated examples — while the *verifier* checks the *full* specification. Termination is not guaranteed in general, but each iteration strictly shrinks the consistent set, and the loop is *sound*: it returns a candidate only when the verifier certifies it.

### 3.3 Oracle query taxonomy

Generalizing CEGIS and oracle-guided synthesis [3], the learner–oracle interaction decomposes into four query types:

| Query type | Learner sends | Oracle returns | Effect |
|---|---|---|---|
| Counterexample | candidate *e* | input *x̄* with *¬φ[f↦e](x̄)* | eliminates all candidates failing at *x̄* |
| Distinguishing input | set of candidates *{e₁, e₂}* | input where *e₁, e₂* disagree | splits the version space |
| Membership | input *x̄* | desired output *y* | adds one I/O constraint |
| Verification | candidate *e* | accept / reject | certifies correctness |

> **Lemma 1 (Version-space shrinkage).** *Let V_k ⊆ L(G) be the set of candidates consistent with all oracle answers through round k. A counterexample query at round k+1 eliminates at least the proposed candidate, so V_{k+1} ⊆ V_k ∖ {e_k}; a distinguishing-input query eliminates at least one of every disagreeing pair, halving the version space in the worst case when candidates are partitioned evenly.*

*Proof sketch.* A counterexample *x̄* falsifies *φ* for *e_k*, so *e_k ∉ V_{k+1} ⊆ V_k*. For distinguishing inputs, the oracle's answer is consistent with the true program, so at least one member of each distinguished pair is eliminated. ∎

### 3.4 Grammar encoding for constraint-based search

To encode "∃*e ∈ L(G)*" symbolically, we bound the term size and introduce one datatype variable per AST node, with constraints enforcing that each node's production is grammar-legal and that the term's semantics (unrolled over the theory *T*) satisfies *φ*. In SMT-LIB this is naturally expressed with algebraic datatypes; in TLA+ the analogous specification treats the grammar as a recursive operator:

```tla
------------------------------ MODULE SyGuS --------------------------------
EXTENDS Integers, Sequences
CONSTANTS MaxDepth
VARIABLES ast

IsGrammarTerm(t) ==
    \/ t \in {"x", "y", "0", "1"}
    \/ \E a, b \in TermsBelow(t) : t = <<"+" , a, b>>
    \/ \E a, b \in TermsBelow(t) : t = <<"ite", a, b>>

TypeOK == IsGrammarTerm(ast) /\ Depth(ast) <= MaxDepth
=============================================================================
```

The encoding is polynomial in the size bound, but the resulting formula is existentially quantified over a deeply structured space — precisely the regime where enumerative search over concrete terms often wins [1].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The logical core: synthesis conjectures modulo theories

At its logical core, SyGuS is the problem of discharging a *synthesis conjecture* *∃f. ∀x̄. P(f, x̄)* modulo a theory *T*, where the existential witness is additionally constrained to the language of a grammar [7]. This is a *second-order* statement: we seek not a value but a *function*, and not any function but one expressible in *G*. Two consequences follow. First, decidability of the SyGuS problem depends jointly on *T* and *G*: for finite *G* the problem is decidable whenever *T*-satisfiability of *φ* instantiated with concrete terms is decidable, by brute-force enumeration; for infinite *G* over undecidable theories, the problem is undecidable in general. Second, *unrealizability* — proving no *e ∈ L(G)* satisfies *φ* — is often harder than synthesis, requiring an inductive argument over the grammar's derivations rather than a single witness.

The grammar is thus both a *search-space restriction* that makes synthesis tractable and a *specification artifact* the user must engineer: too permissive and search is intractable; too restrictive and the problem is unrealizable.

### 4.2 Enumerative versus constraint-based search



- **Enumerative search**: many small queries; graceful degradation; strong with good ordering heuristics.
- **Constraint-based search**: one large query; all-or-nothing; strong when the theory solver's reasoning aligns with the grammar's structure.


### 4.3 Sketching, holes, and divide-and-conquer


Divide-and-conquer exploits the grammar's *compositional* structure: synthesizing *f(x) = g(h(x))* factors into synthesizing *h* on projected examples and *g* on their images. The difficulty is discovering the decomposition — the intermediate specification is itself a synthesis problem — which is where library learning [4] and neural guidance [5] enter: learned abstractions propose decompositions that the symbolic engine verifies.

### 4.4 Oracle-guided inductive synthesis and distinguishing inputs

The oracle-guided framework [3] generalizes CEGIS with a *menu* of query types. Its central device is the *distinguishing input*: given two candidates *e₁, e₂* consistent with all observations, find an input *x̄* with *⟦e₁⟧(x̄) ≠ ⟦e₂⟧(x̄)* and ask the I/O oracle for the correct output. The process terminates when the remaining candidates are *semantically unique*, at which point correctness follows from the oracle's authority.

> **Theorem 1 (Convergence of distinguishing-input synthesis).** *Let C be a finite set of candidate programs over a finite input domain D, and let O be a correct I/O oracle. The distinguishing-input loop terminates after at most |C| − 1 oracle queries and returns a program semantically equivalent to the oracle's intent on D.*

*Proof sketch.* Each query strictly reduces the number of semantic equivalence classes represented in the candidate set: the oracle's answer *(x̄, y)* is consistent with the true program, so every candidate disagreeing with *y* at *x̄* is removed, including at least one of the distinguished pair. Since at least one candidate (the true program's representative) always survives, the loop ends with exactly one equivalence class. ∎

The finiteness assumptions are strong, but the *CEGIS loop inherits the same structure*: the verifier plays the oracle's role and counterexamples play the distinguishing role, each eliminating the proposed candidate *and* every candidate sharing its failure — which is why CEGIS converges far faster than the |C| − 1 worst case in practice.

### 4.5 Neural priors: LLMs and library learning as search guidance

Large language models change the *economics* of synthesis without changing its *logic*. An LLM trained on code defines a distribution *P_θ(e | spec)* over programs given a specification. Used as a *proposal distribution* inside CEGIS, the LLM replaces size-based enumeration order with samples from *P_θ*, concentrating mass on idiomatic, likely-correct programs. Austin et al. report log-linear scaling of synthesis performance with model size, reaching 59.6% few-shot on MBPP at 137B parameters [5]; Chen et al. show repeated sampling lifts HumanEval success from 28.8% (one sample) to 70.2% (100 samples) for Codex [6].

The LLM need not be *correct* — only a good *proposer*: every candidate is checked against *φ* before acceptance, exactly as in CEGIS.

```haskell
-- Neural-symbolic CEGIS: the LLM proposes, the verifier disposes.
neuralCEGIS :: Grammar -> Spec -> LLM -> Verifier -> Maybe Program
neuralCEGIS g spec llm verifier = loop []
  where
    loop examples =
      let cands  = sample llm (prompt spec examples) 100  -- neural proposal
          ranked = filter (consistent examples) cands     -- inductive filter
      in case find (verified verifier spec) ranked of
           Just p  -> Just p                              -- deductive cert
           Nothing -> loop (examples ++ counterexamples verifier ranked spec)
```

DreamCoder [4] attacks the complementary problem: rather than improving the *order* of search, it improves the *space* by learning reusable abstractions (library learning) and training a neural recognition model to guide search within the enriched DSL. Its wake–sleep loop alternates between solving tasks (wake) and compressing solutions into new primitives while training the guide network (sleep); reported solutions would require upwards of 10^72 years of brute-force search, showing that restructuring the grammar can dominate any improvement in search order [4].

The convergence is therefore threefold: **enumerative** methods provide the loop, **constraint-based** methods the verification engine, and **neural** methods the proposal distribution and the evolving grammar — all accommodated by the SyGuS problem statement, which separates *what* is sought from *how* search is organized.

## 5 Empirical Evaluation / Formal Proofs

### 5.1 Formal results

> **Theorem 2 (Soundness of CEGIS).** *If the CEGIS loop of Section 3.2 returns a candidate e, then φ[f ↦ e] is T-valid.*

*Proof sketch.* The loop returns *e* only when the verifier — assumed sound for *T* — reports no counterexample, establishing *T*-validity of *φ[f↦e]*. The examples serve only to focus search. ∎

> **Theorem 3 (Relative completeness).** *If the grammar L(G) is finite, the verifier is complete for T, and a solution exists, then CEGIS terminates and returns a solution.*

*Proof sketch.* Each iteration either returns or adds a counterexample eliminating the current candidate (Lemma 1). With *L(G)* finite, only finitely many eliminations precede the version space of fully-correct candidates, one of which the complete synthesizer will propose. ∎

> **Lemma 2 (Grammar-size bound).** *For a grammar with maximum branching factor b and depth bound d, |{e ∈ L(G) : depth(e) ≤ d}| ≤ b^(d+1). Enumerative CEGIS therefore issues at most b^(d+1) SMT queries in the worst case, each of size polynomial in |φ| and d.*

*Proof sketch.* A term of depth ≤ *d* has at most *(b^(d+1) − 1)/(b − 1)* nodes, each choosing among ≤ *b* productions. ∎

### 5.2 Comparative empirical survey

Reported results across the paradigms discussed, from the cited primary sources:

| Paradigm | Representative system | Benchmark | Reported result |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sketching | SKETCH [2] | finite-program kernels (crypto, DSP) | correct-by-construction completion of partial programs via SAT |
| Oracle-guided | Jha et al. [3] | bit-manipulating programs, deobfuscation | convergence in few oracle rounds; semantically unique programs |
| Enumerative SyGuS | competition solvers [1] | SyGuS-Comp LIA/BV/string tracks | dominant strategy; solves majority of benchmarks |
| Library learning | DreamCoder [4] | list/text/tower/physics tasks | solves tasks requiring >10^72 years of brute-force search |
| LLM few-shot | Austin et al. 137B [5] | MBPP (974 tasks) | 59.6% solved few-shot; log-linear scaling with size |
| LLM sampling | Codex [6] | HumanEval (164 tasks) | 28.8% @1 sample → 70.2% @100 samples |

Two patterns emerge. First, *guarantees and scale trade off*: formally correct systems (SKETCH, oracle-guided, CEGIS solvers) operate on narrow domains, while broad-coverage LLMs offer no per-output guarantee — the guarantee must be reintroduced by a verifier, i.e., by embedding the LLM inside CEGIS. Second, *search-space engineering dominates search algorithms*: library learning and holes win by restructuring *L(G)* rather than by smarter traversal of a fixed space.

### 5.3 A worked micro-example

Consider synthesizing `max` in LIA with grammar *A → x | y | 0 | 1 | A+A | A−A | ite(B, A, A)* and specification *f(x,y) ≥ x ∧ f(x,y) ≥ y ∧ (f(x,y) = x ∨ f(x,y) = y)*. CEGIS proposes *x + y*; the verifier returns counterexample *(1, −1)*, since *x + y = 0 ≱ 1*. With this example added, the synthesizer proposes *ite(x ≥ y, x, y)*, which the verifier certifies. In Rust:

```rust
/// Synthesized by CEGIS; verified against the LIA specification.
fn max_synth(x: i64, y: i64) -> i64 {
    if x >= y { x } else { y }  // ite(x ≥ y, x, y)
}
```

## 6 Limitations and Open Problems

1. **Specification elicitation.** SyGuS assumes a formal *φ*, but users think in examples or natural language. Bridging informal intent and formal *φ* remains largely manual; oracle-guided synthesis [3] sidesteps it with I/O oracles that someone must still implement.
2. **Unrealizability.** Proving that *no* grammar term satisfies *φ* requires reasoning about *all* derivations — a co-inductive problem for which current techniques (grammar-flow analysis, tree-automaton abstraction) scale poorly.
3. **The fixed-theory ceiling.** SyGuS semantics are confined to a fixed background theory; programs with loops, heaps, or concurrency lie outside the standard formulation [1].
4. **Neural-symbolic trust.** A biased proposer *P_θ* can starve regions of *L(G)* containing the only solutions, breaking the relative completeness of Theorem 3. Calibrating neural proposals to preserve coverage is open.
5. **Grammar engineering.** Writing a tractable yet sufficient grammar is a dark art; automating grammar *refinement* and grammar *learning* from corpora [4] are active frontiers.
6. **Verification cost.** As candidates grow, verifier queries dominate runtime; incremental verification inside the CEGIS loop is underdeveloped.

## 7 Conclusion

SyGuS gave program synthesis its most productive formalization: a separation between *what* a program must do — the conjecture *∃f. ∀x̄. P(f, x̄)* — and *what form* it may take, expressed as a grammar. This paper unified the resulting search architectures — enumerative, constraint-based, sketch-driven, oracle-guided — under oracle-guided inductive synthesis, with CEGIS as the canonical loop. Large language models do not displace this edifice; they complete it: LLMs supply the learned prior over the program space that the classical tradition lacked, while verifiers supply the guarantee that LLMs lack. Neural-symbolic CEGIS — neural models propose, symbolic oracles dispose — is the natural endpoint: synthesis as grammar-constrained search, guided by learning, certified by logic.
