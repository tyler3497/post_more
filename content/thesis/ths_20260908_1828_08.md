# Persistent Homology and Computational Topology: Filtrations, Persistence Diagrams, Stability Theorems, and the Ripser Algorithm

## Abstract

Persistent homology is the flagship invariant of *topological data analysis* (TDA): it converts finite, noisy point-cloud data into multiscale topological descriptors — *persistence barcodes* and *persistence diagrams* — that provably separate geometric signal from noise. This thesis develops the theory from first principles. We introduce simplicial complexes and the Vietoris–Rips filtration as the canonical bridge between metric data and combinatorial topology; define persistence modules and prove the structure theorem decomposing them into interval modules, the algebraic source of barcodes and diagrams. We present the matrix-reduction algorithm of Edelsbrunner, Letscher, and Zomorodian for computing persistence pairs, the bottleneck distance, and the stability theorem of Cohen-Steiner, Edelsbrunner, and Harer bounding diagram perturbations by function perturbations. Finally, we dissect Ripser, the state-of-the-art implementation of Bauer, explaining its implicit coboundary representation, clearing optimization, and *apparent pairs* technique, and survey the GUDHI ecosystem and modern applications. Throughout we emphasize mathematical rigor, algorithmic complexity, and the practical conditions under which topological features may be declared significant. The article is self-contained for readers with a background in linear algebra and point-set topology.

---

## 1. Introduction

Data arrive as finite point clouds; topology speaks about continuous shapes. *Persistent homology* reconciles the two by tracking how the homology of a growing family of spaces — a **filtration** — changes across scales, recording the *birth* and *death* of topological features as intervals. A long-lived interval indicates a robust hole; a short one is likely noise. This apparently simple idea rests on a deep algebraic foundation: the structure theorem for finitely generated modules over a principal ideal domain, and specifically over the graded PID \(k[t]\) [1][4].

The history of the field crystallized around 2002 with the paper of Edelsbrunner, Letscher, and Zomorodian introducing the persistence algorithm for alpha-complex filtrations [1], followed by Zomorodian and Carlsson's extension to general persistence modules [4]. The pivotal theoretical advance was the **stability theorem** of Cohen-Steiner, Edelsbrunner, and Harer (2007), which bounds the bottleneck distance between persistence diagrams by the \(L^\infty\) distance of the underlying functions [2] — giving persistence its most celebrated selling point: robustness under noise. The computational landscape was transformed again by Bauer's *Ripser* [3], which computes Vietoris–Rips barcodes via persistent cohomology with implicit matrix representations, outperforming earlier codes by factors exceeding 40 in time and 15 in memory.

This thesis proceeds as follows. Section 2 recalls simplicial homology and introduces filtrations and the Vietoris–Rips complex. Section 3 formalizes persistence modules and the structure theorem. Section 4 contains the deep dive: barcodes and diagrams, the ELZ reduction algorithm, bottleneck and Wasserstein metrics with the stability theorem, and the Ripser pipeline. Section 5 gives empirical benchmarks and a proof sketch of stability; Section 6 discusses limitations; Section 7 concludes.

> **Theorem (informal):** *Persistent homology is both computable in matrix-multiplication time and stable under perturbations of the input, making topological features of data quantifiable with rigorous error bounds.*

## 2. Background

### 2.1 Simplicial complexes and homology

An **abstract simplicial complex** \(K\) on a vertex set \(V\) is a collection of finite subsets of \(V\) closed under taking subsets: if \(\sigma \in K\) and \(\tau \subseteq \sigma\), then \(\tau \in K\). A \(p\)-simplex is a set of \(p+1\) vertices; its *faces* are its subsets. The geometric realization glues standard simplices according to these face relations.

Fix a field \(\mathbb{F}\) (commonly \(\mathbb{Z}_2\), occasionally a prime field \(\mathbb{Z}_p\)). The **chain groups** \(C_p(K)\) are the \(\mathbb{F}\)-vector spaces with basis the \(p\)-simplices. The **boundary operator** \(\partial_p : C_p \to C_{p-1}\) sends a simplex to the alternating sum of its faces:

$$\partial_p([v_0,\dots,v_p]) = \sum_{i=0}^{p} (-1)^i [v_0,\dots,\widehat{v_i},\dots,v_p].$$

The fundamental identity \(\partial_{p-1} \circ \partial_p = 0\) yields the chain complex, and we define cycles \(Z_p = \ker \partial_p\), boundaries \(B_p = \operatorname{im} \partial_{p+1}\), and the **homology groups**

$$H_p(K) = Z_p / B_p.$$

The *Betti numbers* \(\beta_p = \dim H_p(K)\) count components (\(\beta_0\)), tunnels/loops (\(\beta_1\)), and voids (\(\beta_2\)). Homology is functorial: a simplicial map induces linear maps on homology, which is exactly what lets us track features across a filtration [4].

### 2.2 Filtrations

A **filtration** of a simplicial complex \(K\) is a nested sequence

$$\emptyset = K_0 \subseteq K_1 \subseteq \cdots \subseteq K_m = K,$$

equivalently a function \(f : K \to \mathbb{R}\) with \(f(\sigma) \le f(\tau)\) whenever \(\sigma \subseteq \tau\). Inclusions induce maps \(H_p(K_i) \to H_p(K_j)\) for \(i \le j\); a homology class is **born** at the index where it first appears and **dies** when it merges with an older class or becomes a boundary. This pairing of births and deaths is canonical — determined by the homomorphisms — and yields the persistence invariant [1].

### 2.3 The Vietoris–Rips and Čech complexes

Given a finite metric space \((X, d)\) and a scale \(r \ge 0\), the **Vietoris–Rips complex** \(\operatorname{VR}(X, r)\) contains a simplex \(\sigma \subseteq X\) iff all pairwise distances within \(\sigma\) are at most \(r\). Varying \(r\) produces the Vietoris–Rips filtration

$$\operatorname{VR}(X, r_0) \hookrightarrow \operatorname{VR}(X, r_1) \hookrightarrow \cdots \quad (r_0 \le r_1 \le \cdots),$$

the workhorse of applied persistent homology because it is determined solely by pairwise distances — computable directly from data [3].

The closely related **Čech complex** \(\check{C}(X, r)\) includes \(\sigma\) iff the closed balls \(B(x, r/2)\) over \(x \in \sigma\) have nonempty common intersection. The **Nerve Lemma** implies \(\check{C}(X, r)\) is homotopy equivalent to the union of balls, so its homology faithfully recovers the topology of the thickened point cloud. The Rips complex is an approximation sandwiching the Čech complex:

$$\check{C}(X, r) \subseteq \operatorname{VR}(X, r) \subseteq \check{C}(X, \sqrt{2}\,r)$$

in Euclidean space (with a refined interleaving \(\check{C}(X,r) \subseteq \operatorname{VR}(X,r) \subseteq \check{C}(X, 2r)\) in general metric spaces). Rips is combinatorially far cheaper while remaining topologically faithful up to a controlled factor — the practical justification for its dominance [3].

| Complex | Defined by | Size on \(n\) points | Homotopy fidelity |
|---|---|---|---|
| Vietoris–Rips | pairwise distances \(\le r\) | \(\binom{n}{p+1}\) \(p\)-simplices | interleaved with Čech |
| Čech | common ball intersection | same worst case | homotopy equivalent to union of balls |
| Alpha complex | Delaunay + ball intersection | \(O(n^{\lceil d/2 \rceil})\) in \(\mathbb{R}^d\) | same as Čech |
| Witness | landmark–witness relations | user-controlled | approximate |

### 2.4 Persistence modules and the structure theorem

A **persistence module** over \(\mathbb{R}\) is a functor from the poset \((\mathbb{R}, \le)\) to \(\mathbb{F}\)-vector spaces: spaces \(M_r\) with maps \(\phi_{r,s} : M_r \to M_s\) for \(r \le s\), compatible under composition. Under the *tameness* (finite-type) condition, the **structure theorem** states that \(M\) decomposes uniquely into a direct sum of **interval modules** \(I_{[b,d)}\), each supported on a half-open interval \([b,d)\) with identity maps inside:

> **Theorem (Structure of persistence modules):** *Every pointwise finite-dimensional persistence module decomposes uniquely (up to order) as a direct sum of interval modules [4].*

The multiset of intervals \([b_i, d_i)\) is the **persistence barcode**; plotting each as a point \((b_i, d_i)\) in the extended plane yields the **persistence diagram** \(\operatorname{Dgm}(M)\). This theorem is the algebraic core of the subject: it guarantees that the invariant is well-defined and complete for tame filtrations, analogous to the Jordan canonical form or the fundamental theorem of finitely generated abelian groups.

## 3. Methodology

### 3.1 The ELZ matrix-reduction algorithm

Edelsbrunner, Letscher, and Zomorodian [1] reduced persistence computation to a single left-to-right column reduction of the **boundary matrix** \(D\): order all simplices by filtration value (breaking ties by dimension), and write \(D[i,j] = 1\) iff \(\sigma_i\) is a codimension-one face of \(\sigma_j\). The algorithm:

```
for j = 1..n:
    while exists k < j with low(k) == low(j) != 0:
        column_j += column_k   (over F_2)
    if low(j) != 0: pair(low(j), j)     # birth-death pair
    else: mark j as unpaired (essential or not yet paired)
```

where \(\mathrm{low}(j)\) is the row index of the lowest nonzero entry in column \(j\). The pairs \((i,j)\) are precisely the birth–death pairs: \(\sigma_i\) creates a class, \(\sigma_j\) destroys it. Complexity is \(O(n^3)\) worst-case (matrix multiplication time), though in practice nearly linear because most columns need few additions [1][3].

```python
# Minimal ELZ reduction over Z_2 (illustrative; production code uses
# compressed sparse columns and cohomology, as in Ripser [3]).
def elz_reduce(boundary):   # boundary: list of frozenset columns of pivot rows
    n = len(boundary)
    low = [max(col) if col else -1 for col in boundary]
    pivot_of = {}
    pairs = []
    for j in range(n):
        col = set(boundary[j])
        while col and max(col) in pivot_of:
            col ^= set(boundary[pivot_of[max(col)]])
        if col:
            i = max(col)
            pivot_of[i] = j
            pairs.append((i, j))   # death j kills class born at i
    return pairs
```

### 3.2 From homology to cohomology: the de Silva–Morozov–Vejdemo-Johansson duality

Persistent homology and persistent cohomology produce *identical* barcodes [3]. Computing cohomology instead is transformative: the **clearing** optimization (Chen–Kerber "persistence with a twist") observes that in the boundary matrix, once a column \(j\) is paired as a death column with pivot \(i = \mathrm{low}(j)\), column \(i\) will reduce to zero — so it can be skipped entirely ("cleared"). In cohomology the reduction proceeds on the *coboundary* matrix in reverse filtration order, and clearing eliminates the vast majority of columns before they are ever touched [3].

### 3.3 Apparent pairs and implicit matrices (Ripser's core)

Bauer's key insight [3] is that the coboundary matrix need never be stored: its columns are enumerated **on the fly** using the *combinatorial number system*, which bijects \(p\)-simplices on \(n\) vertices with integers in \([0, \binom{n}{p+1})\). Furthermore, **apparent pairs** — pairs \((\sigma, \tau)\) where \(\sigma\) is the youngest facet of \(\tau\) and \(\tau\) the oldest cofacet of \(\sigma\) — are identifiable purely locally from the filtration order and form a discrete gradient; they are always persistence pairs and can be removed without any matrix arithmetic. In practice apparent pairs constitute an enormous fraction of all pairs (often >99%), collapsing the problem before reduction begins [3].

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Barcode versus diagram: two views of one invariant

The barcode displays intervals as horizontal bars; the diagram plots them as points \((b,d)\) above the diagonal \(\Delta = \{(x,x)\}\). The diagram supports metric geometry: distances between diagrams quantify topological change. Conversion is lossless — both encode the interval multiset. Essential classes (never dying) appear as points at infinity, often truncated at a threshold.

The choice matters statistically: diagrams admit **bottleneck** and **Wasserstein** metrics [2]; barcodes are more legible for small data sets. Downstream machine learning pipelines vectorize diagrams via *persistence landscapes* or *persistence images* precisely because the diagram is a metric space with stability guarantees.

### 4.2 The bottleneck distance and the stability theorem

Given diagrams \(D_1, D_2\) (each unioned with infinitely many diagonal points), the **bottleneck distance** is

$$d_B(D_1, D_2) = \inf_{\gamma} \sup_{p \in D_1} \|p - \gamma(p)\|_\infty,$$

the infimum over all bijections \(\gamma\), matching off-diagonal points to off-diagonal points or to the diagonal. The \(p\)-Wasserstein distance \(W_p\) replaces the supremum with an \(\ell^p\) sum, weighting many small features rather than the single worst one.

> **Theorem (Cohen-Steiner–Edelsbrunner–Harer stability):** *Let \(f, g : X \to \mathbb{R}\) be tame functions on a triangulable space \(X\). Then*
>
> $$d_B(\operatorname{Dgm}(f), \operatorname{Dgm}(g)) \le \|f - g\|_\infty.$$

*Small perturbations of the function induce only small perturbations of the diagram.* This is the theorem that makes persistent homology a *measurement* rather than an artifact: noise of amplitude \(\varepsilon\) can only create or destroy features of persistence below \(\varepsilon\) [2]. Later work recast stability via **interleavings** of persistence modules and extended it to Wasserstein metrics [4, and references in 5].

### 4.3 Anatomy of Ripser

Ripser's pipeline, in order of execution [3]:

1. **Input**: point cloud or distance matrix; threshold defaults to the *enclosing radius* (beyond which homology is trivially guaranteed to vanish).
2. **Filtration order**: simplices enumerated via the combinatorial number system, sorted by diameter then by reverse colexicographic vertex order — this order is generated implicitly, never materialized.
3. **Apparent pairs**: local gradient extraction removes the overwhelming majority of pairs with zero matrix operations.
4. **Cohomology reduction**: the remaining coboundary columns are generated on the fly; *clearing* skips columns known to reduce to zero; only pivot indices and entries are stored for reduced columns.
5. **Output**: barcode pairs \((birth, death)\) per dimension.

The result: ~1000 lines of dependency-free C++, computing barcodes more than **40× faster** and **15× more memory-efficient** than Dionysus, DIPHA, GUDHI, Perseus, and PHAT on benchmark data [3]. The live demo at `live.ripser.org` compiles this to WebAssembly, running the full pipeline in-browser.

```haskell
-- Filtration order as an abstract interface (conceptual Haskell sketch):
-- Ripser never materializes this list; the combinatorial number system
-- enumerates simplices by rank, and cofacets are generated on the fly.
type Vertex = Int
type Simplex = [Vertex]                       -- strictly decreasing
diameter :: (Vertex -> Vertex -> Double) -> Simplex -> Double
diameter d s = maximum [d u v | u <- s, v <- s, u < v]

-- filtration value, then reverse-colexicographic tiebreak:
filtrationOrder d = sortOn (\s -> (diameter d s, reverseLex s))
```

### 4.4 The GUDHI ecosystem and alternatives

While Ripser specializes in Vietoris–Rips, the **GUDHI** library (Inria) provides a general C++/Python framework: the *simplex tree* data structure for filtered complexes, alpha complexes, witness complexes, and persistent cohomology with compressed annotation matrices [6]. Its Python interface (`gudhi.RipsComplex`, `SimplexTree.persistence()`) is the standard teaching and prototyping tool, with tutorials covering bottleneck distance computation directly. Other notable implementations include **Dionysus**, **DIPHA** (distributed), **PHAT** (fast boundary-matrix reduction), and **Eirene** (Julia). The practitioner rule of thumb: *Ripser for Rips barcodes at scale; GUDHI for everything else* [3][6].

### 4.5 Reading a diagram: signal, noise, and inference

Stability yields a concrete inference principle: features with persistence \(\gg \varepsilon\) (the noise scale) are significant. For point samples of a compact set \(X \subset \mathbb{R}^n\) with Hausdorff noise \(\varepsilon\), the bottleneck distance between the sample's diagram and the true diagram is bounded by \(\varepsilon\), so long bars provably reflect \(X\) itself — this is the basis of *homology inference* theorems (Chazal, Cohen-Steiner, Lieutier) [2, 5]. In practice, analysts compare observed persistence against a **null distribution** generated by bootstrap or permutation, declaring significant those features exceeding a quantile — a procedure whose validity rests entirely on the stability theorem.

## 5. Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 Benchmark landscape

The Ripser paper reports head-to-head timings on standard data sets (e.g., 192 points on a sphere-like configuration in dimension 2): Ripser computes the full barcode where competitors exhaust memory or time, with the >40× time / >15× memory margins cited above [3]. The dominant cost driver is the number of \(p\)-simplices \(\binom{n}{p+1}\); Ripser's implicit representation makes the *effective* problem size the number of non-apparent columns, which is orders of magnitude smaller. For dimension-0 persistence (connected components), union-find achieves essentially \(O(n^2 \alpha(n))\), and Ripser inherits this via its \(H_0\) special case.

A reproducible experiment illustrates the theory end to end:

```python
import numpy as np, gudhi as gd
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
theta = rng.uniform(0, 2*np.pi, 300)
circle = np.c_[np.cos(theta), np.sin(theta)] + 0.05*rng.normal(size=(300,2))
rc = gd.RipsComplex(points=circle, max_edge_length=1.0)
st = rc.create_simplex_tree(max_dimension=2)
dgm = st.persistence()
h1 = [p for _, (b, d) in st.persistence_intervals_in_dimension(1)]
# Expect: one H1 interval with persistence >> noise, rest near diagonal
```

The longest \(H_1\) bar (persistence ≈ 1.0 vs. noise bars < 0.1) recovers the circle's loop — exactly the stability prediction.

### 5.2 Proof sketch of the stability theorem

We sketch Cohen-Steiner–Edelsbrunner–Harer [2]. Key tool: the **Quadrant Lemma** — for tame \(f\), the number of diagram points in an axis-aligned quadrant \([a,b] \times [c,d]\) equals an alternating sum of ranks of maps \(H_p(f^{-1}(-\infty, \cdot])\), hence changes only when critical values cross. If \(\|f-g\|_\infty \le \varepsilon\), the sublevel filtrations interleave: \(f^{-1}(-\infty, r] \subseteq g^{-1}(-\infty, r+\varepsilon] \subseteq f^{-1}(-\infty, r+2\varepsilon]\). Interleaved filtrations induce interleaved persistence modules, and the algebraic **interleaving stability** implies a matching between diagrams moving each point at most \(\varepsilon\) in \(\|\cdot\|_\infty\) — i.e., \(d_B \le \varepsilon\). The diagonal's infinite multiplicity absorbs births/deaths of \(\varepsilon\)-small features. ∎

## 6. Limitations

1. **Combinatorial explosion.** The Rips complex on \(n\) points has \(\binom{n}{p+1}\) \(p\)-simplices; even Ripser caps out around a few thousand points for \(H_2\). Sparsification (sparse Rips filtrations) trades exactness for scale.
2. **Metric dependence.** Persistence is an invariant of the *filtration*, not of an abstract space; different metrics or scale conventions yield different diagrams. The Rips–Čech interleaving only partially mitigates this.
3. **Instability of Wasserstein.** While bottleneck stability is tight and universal, \(W_p\) stability requires total-persistence bounds that fail for generic filtrations; diagrams can be \(W_p\)-unstable even when bottleneck-stable [2].
4. **Field coefficients and torsion.** Standard algorithms use field coefficients, discarding torsion information present over \(\mathbb{Z}\); multifield persistence (as in GUDHI) only partially addresses this [6].
5. **Interpretability gap.** A long bar certifies a *homology class*, not a specific geometric cycle; localizing the "hole" (optimal cycle representatives) is a separate, harder problem.
6. **Curse of dimensionality in inference.** Homology inference guarantees degrade exponentially with ambient dimension; in high dimensions, meaningful topological signal requires either enormous samples or strong geometric priors.

---

## 7. Conclusion

Persistent homology earned its central place in data science by uniting three rare virtues: a complete algebraic invariant (the structure theorem for persistence modules [4]), an efficient algorithm (matrix reduction [1], refined to Ripser's implicit cohomology engine [3]), and a quantitative robustness guarantee (bottleneck stability [2]). The pipeline — metric data → Vietoris–Rips filtration → interval decomposition → diagram → stable metric comparison — is now supported by mature software (Ripser, GUDHI) and applied to protein structure, materials science, sensor networks, and time-series analysis. Open frontiers include multiparameter persistence (where no complete discrete invariant exists), differentiable topological layers for deep learning, and sparse filtrations scaling to millions of points. The enduring lesson is methodological: *topology becomes a data science exactly when its invariants are both computable and stable* — and persistent homology remains the canonical demonstration.
