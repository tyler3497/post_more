# Spinodal Decomposition and Coarsening: The Cahn–Hilliard Equation, Mullins–Sekerka Flow, and Ostwald Ripening

## Abstract

Phase separation is the universal mechanism by which a quenched alloy, polymer blend, or glass partitions into compositionally distinct domains. This thesis develops the classical continuum theory of phase separation and coarsening from its thermodynamic foundations to its sharp-interface asymptotics. We begin with the Ginzburg–Landau free energy with a gradient penalty, derive the Cahn–Hilliard equation as an $H^{-1}$ gradient flow, and analyze the linear instability of homogeneous states — the spinodal regime — against the metastable nucleation-and-growth regime. Matched asymptotic analysis shows that the diffuse-interface model converges to Mullins–Sekerka (Hele-Shaw) flow as the interface thickness vanishes. Late-stage coarsening is governed by the Lifshitz–Slyozov–Wagner theory of Ostwald ripening, yielding the celebrated $t^{1/3}$ growth law and a universal self-similar size distribution. We complement the theory with energy-stable numerical schemes and experimental evidence, and close with the limitations of the classical framework.

##1 Introduction

When a homogeneous binary alloy is rapidly cooled below its miscibility gap, it does not wait for permission to separate: concentration fluctuations amplify spontaneously, producing the intricate bicontinuous labyrinths seen in quenched glasses and polymer blends. The mathematical theory of this phenomenon begins with Gibbs' thermodynamics of heterogeneous equilibrium and van der Waals' square-gradient hypothesis, but its modern form was fixed by Cahn and Hilliard, who in 1958 derived the free energy of a nonuniform system including the gradient penalty that sets the interfacial tension [1]. Cahn's 1965 analysis of spinodal decomposition then predicted the selection of a dominant wavelength from the linearized kinetics [2].

The subject sits at the intersection of several deep traditions:

- **Thermodynamics**: the double-well bulk free energy and the common-tangent construction;
- **Partial differential equations**: the Cahn–Hilliard equation as a fourth-order, mass-conserving, $H^{-1}$ gradient flow;
- **Asymptotics**: the sharp-interface limit yielding curvature-driven free-boundary problems;
- **Statistical kinetics**: the Lifshitz–Slyozov–Wagner (LSW) mean-field theory of coarsening [3].

This thesis reconstructs that arc in full: from the free energy functional, through linear instability, through the matched asymptotics of the diffuse interface, to the universal scaling laws of Ostwald ripening and the topological laws of grain growth, ending with numerical practice and experimental confrontation.

##2 Background

Consider a binary mixture described by a conserved order parameter $c(x,t)$, the local concentration of one species, taking values in the unit interval. For a spatially uniform state the Helmholtz free energy density $f(c)$ is typically non-convex below a critical temperature: the regular-solution (Flory–Huggins) form

$$f(c) = k_B T \left[c \ln c + (1-c)\ln(1-c)\right] + \Omega\, c(1-c)$$

develops two wells when the interaction parameter $\Omega$ exceeds $2k_B T$. The equilibrium compositions of coexisting phases follow from the *common tangent construction*, while the inflection points $f''(c) = 0$ define the **spinodal**: inside it, $f''(c) < 0$ and the homogeneous state is linearly unstable; between binodal and spinodal it is metastable.

> Theorem: (Gibbs stability criterion) A homogeneous state $c_0$ is locally stable against infinitesimal composition fluctuations if and only if $f''(c_0) > 0$. The locus $f''(c) = 0$ is the spinodal curve; the locus of common tangency to $f$ is the binodal (coexistence) curve.

Cahn and Hilliard's decisive step was to penalize sharp gradients by writing the total free energy as a functional [1]

$$E[c] = \int_\Omega \left( f(c) + \frac{\kappa}{2} |\nabla c|^2 \right) dx,$$

where $\kappa > 0$ sets the energetic cost of interfaces. The first variation defines the chemical potential

$$\mu = \frac{\delta E}{\delta c} = f'(c) - \kappa \Delta c,$$

and equilibrium demands uniform $\mu$. The gradient term gives interfaces a finite width of order $\sqrt{\kappa/|f''|}$ and an interfacial tension computable from the one-dimensional transition profile — the content of the 1958 paper [1] and of the subsequent nucleation analysis, which found the critical nucleus by a saddle-point argument on the same functional [1].

Different bulk potentials serve different purposes:

| Potential | Form | Minima | Remarks |
|---|---|---|---|
| Quartic double-well | $F(c) = (c^2-1)^2/4$ | $\pm 1$ | Smooth, standard in analysis |
| Logarithmic (Flory–Huggins) | $c\ln c + (1-c)\ln(1-c) - \chi c(1-c)$ | Temperature-dependent | Physically faithful, singular at pure phases |
| Double-obstacle | $\infty$ outside $(-1,1)$ | $\pm 1$ | Nonsmooth, sharp transition layers |

##3 Methodology

### The Cahn–Hilliard equation as $H^{-1}$ gradient flow

Mass conservation, $\partial_t c + \nabla \cdot J = 0$, together with the constitutive law that flux flows down the chemical-potential gradient, $J = -M \nabla \mu$ with mobility $M > 0$, yields the Cahn–Hilliard equation [1]

$$\partial_t c = \nabla \cdot \big(M \nabla \mu\big), \qquad \mu = f'(c) - \kappa \Delta c.$$

This is precisely the gradient flow of $E$ with respect to the $H^{-1}$ inner product: among all mass-conserving evolutions, the dynamics descends the free energy landscape steepest in the metric induced by $(\nabla \cdot)^{-1}$. The structure immediately implies the **energy dissipation law**

$$\frac{dE}{dt} = \int_\Omega \mu\,\partial_t c\,dx = -\int_\Omega M |\nabla \mu|^2 dx \le 0,$$

so the free energy is a strict Lyapunov functional and all solutions relax toward critical points of $E$ under the mass constraint. Degenerate mobilities such as $M(c) = c(1-c)$ restrict diffusion to interfacial regions and alter the sharp-interface limit qualitatively, from Mullins–Sekerka flow to pure surface diffusion.

### Linear stability: the growth-rate derivation

Linearize about a homogeneous state $c_0$ with a normal mode $c = c_0 + \delta e^{ik\cdot x + \sigma t}$, $\delta \ll 1$. To first order,

$$\mu \approx f'(c_0) + f''(c_0)\,\delta e^{ik\cdot x+\sigma t} + \kappa k^2 \delta e^{ik\cdot x+\sigma t},$$

and the evolution equation gives the **dispersion relation**

$$\sigma(k) = -M k^2 \big(f''(c_0) + \kappa k^2\big).$$

Three consequences follow:

1. **Instability criterion**: $\sigma > 0$ for some $k$ iff $f''(c_0) < 0$ — exactly the spinodal interior.
2. **Wavelength selection**: the fastest-growing mode sits at $k_*^2 = -f''(c_0)/(2\kappa)$ with maximal rate $\sigma_{\max} = M\, f''(c_0)^2/(4\kappa)$.
3. **Ultraviolet cutoff**: $\sigma(k) \to -\infty$ as $k \to \infty$; the fourth-order term regularizes short waves, unlike backward diffusion.

This is Cahn's 1965 prediction: early-stage spinodal decomposition is a superposition of sinusoidal modulations at the fixed wavelength $2\pi/k_*$, random in phase and orientation, producing the observed interconnected morphology [2].

### Numerical methodology: energy-stable schemes

The stiffness of the biharmonic operator and the non-convexity of $f$ demand specialized time stepping. Two workhorse approaches are:

- **Convex splitting** (Eyre): write $F = F_c - F_e$ with both parts convex, treating $F_c$ implicitly and $F_e$ explicitly, yielding unconditional energy stability.
- **Scalar auxiliary variable (SAV)** (Shen et al.): introduce $r(t) = \sqrt{\int_\Omega F(c)\,dx + C_0}$ and reformulate the nonlinearity so that each step requires only linear constant-coefficient solves, while a modified energy dissipates discretely [6].

```python
import numpy as np

def cahn_hilliard_1d(c0, dt, T, eps=0.02, L=4.0):
    """1D Cahn-Hilliard: semi-implicit spectral scheme
    (cubic term explicit, biharmonic term implicit, periodic BC)."""
    N = c0.size
    k = 2*np.pi*np.fft.fftfreq(N, d=L/N)
    k2, k4 = k**2, k**4
    c = c0.copy()
    for _ in range(int(T/dt)):
        nonlin = np.fft.fft(c**3 - c)   # F'(c) explicit
        # (u^{n+1}-u^n)/dt = -k^2 * (F'(u^n) + eps^2 k^2 u^{n+1}) in Fourier
        c_hat = (np.fft.fft(c) - dt*k2*nonlin) / (1.0 + dt*eps**2*k4)
        c = np.fft.ifft(c_hat).real
    return np.linspace(0, L, N, endpoint=False), c

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
x, c = cahn_hilliard_1d(0.1*rng.standard_normal(256), dt=5e-4, T=1.0)
print("conserved mean:", round(c.mean(), 6), " phases:", round(c.min(),3), round(c.max(),3))
```

The scheme inherits the continuous dissipation structure: a discrete energy is nonincreasing for *any* time step, which is what makes long coarsening simulations feasible [6].

##4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Spinodal decomposition versus nucleation and growth

The sign of $f''(c_0)$ partitions the phase diagram into two kinetic universes:

| Feature | Spinodal decomposition | Nucleation and growth |
|---|---|---|
| Thermodynamic state | Unstable, $f''(c_0)<0$ | Metastable, $f''(c_0)>0$ |
| Barrier | None | Finite nucleation barrier |
| Early morphology | Bicontinuous, fixed wavelength $2\pi/k_*$ | Isolated droplets of critical radius |
| Linear theory | Exponential amplification, $\sigma(k)$ above | No unstable modes |
| Late stage | Coarsening toward equilibrium | Coarsening (Ostwald ripening) |

Inside the spinodal, *every* sufficiently long wavelength grows; the dynamics needs no thermal activation. In the metastable gap, the system must surmount the nucleation barrier computed by Cahn and Hilliard from the saddle point of the free-energy functional — a droplet whose diffuse interface becomes ever more extended as the spinodal is approached, with the barrier vanishing continuously at the spinodal itself [1]. Both routes converge at late times onto the same coarsening dynamics, because once well-defined domains exist, interfacial energy minimization takes over regardless of how the domains were born.

### 4.2 Matched asymptotics: the sharp-interface limit and Mullins–Sekerka flow

Introduce the small parameter $\varepsilon$ measuring interface thickness relative to domain size, and rescale $\kappa = \varepsilon^2 \bar\kappa$. Pego's 1989 matched-asymptotic analysis showed that as $\varepsilon \to 0$, the diffuse interface collapses onto a moving hypersurface $\Gamma_t$ whose motion is slaved to a bulk diffusion field [5]. The argument proceeds by matched expansions:

- **Outer expansion**: away from interfaces, $c \to \pm 1$ and the chemical potential tends to a limit $w$ satisfying Laplace's equation $\Delta w = 0$ in each bulk phase.
- **Inner expansion**: across the interface, the profile tends to the heteroclinic (tanh-like) connection, and solvability at the next order fixes the interfacial value of $w$.

The resulting free-boundary problem is the **Mullins–Sekerka** (two-sided Hele-Shaw) flow:

$$\Delta w = 0 \quad \text{in } \Omega\setminus\Gamma_t, \qquad w = \sigma \kappa \quad \text{on } \Gamma_t, \qquad V = -\tfrac{1}{2}\left[\tfrac{\partial w}{\partial n}\right]_{\Gamma_t},$$

where $\kappa$ is mean curvature, $V$ the normal velocity, $\sigma$ the surface tension, and brackets denote the jump across the interface. The interface is thus an equipotential of the chemical potential at the Gibbs–Thomson value $\sigma\kappa$, and it advances by the jump in diffusive flux — the same mathematical structure as Hele-Shaw flow in a porous medium, which is why the two names are used interchangeably [5]. Rigorous justification followed through the work of Alikakos, Bates, and Chen, who proved convergence of Cahn–Hilliard solutions to the Hele-Shaw limit. Recent analysis extends this to disparate and degenerate mobilities and to stochastic forcing [5].

### 4.3 Ostwald ripening and the $t^{1/3}$ law

At late times, domains are large compared to the interface width and the dynamics reduces to curvature-driven mass exchange: small particles, with higher Laplace pressure, dissolve while large ones grow. The Gibbs–Thomson relation gives the equilibrium concentration at a particle of radius $R$,

$$c(R) \approx c_\infty\left(1 + \frac{2\gamma v_m}{R k_B T}\right),$$

and quasi-steady diffusion between particles yields the growth law [3]

$$\frac{dR}{dt} = \frac{D}{R}\,\frac{2\gamma v_m^2 c_\infty}{k_B T}\left(\frac{1}{\langle R\rangle} - \frac{1}{R}\right),$$

so particles larger than the mean-field radius $\langle R\rangle$ grow and smaller ones shrink — the *critical radius* $R_c = \langle R\rangle$ separates growing from evaporating populations. Mass conservation fixes $R_c(t)$ self-consistently, and the celebrated consequence is the cubic growth law

$$\frac{d}{dt}\langle R\rangle^3 = K = \frac{8\gamma D v_m^2 c_\infty}{9 k_B T}, \qquad \langle R(t)\rangle \sim (Kt)^{1/3}.$$

The exponent $1/3$ is the fingerprint of diffusion-limited coarsening; interface-attachment-limited kinetics (Wagner's second case) gives $t^{1/2}$ instead [3].

### 4.4 The LSW self-similar distribution

Lifshitz and Slyozov closed the theory by deriving the continuity equation for the size distribution $F(R,t)$,

$$\frac{\partial F}{\partial t} + \frac{\partial}{\partial R}(V F) = 0, \quad V(R,t) = \frac{1}{R^2}\left(\frac{1}{R_c} - \frac{1}{R}\right) \times \text{const},$$

and seeking a self-similar solution $F(R,t) = g(t)\,h(\rho)$ with $\rho = R/\langle R\rangle$. The unique admissible solution with compact support is the **LSW distribution** [3]:

$$f(\rho) = \frac{4e}{9}\,\rho^2\left(\frac{3}{3+\rho}\right)^{7/3}\left(\frac{1.5}{1.5-\rho}\right)^{11/3}\exp\!\left(-\frac{1.5}{1.5-\rho}\right), \quad \rho < 1.5,$$

and zero beyond. It is strongly asymmetric — rising steeply from zero, peaking near $\rho \approx 1.1$, and terminating in a hard cutoff at $1.5\langle R\rangle$ — a shape no Gaussian can mimic, and a stringent test of the theory. The selection of this particular member from a one-parameter family of self-similar solutions was a famous puzzle, resolved by accounting for fluctuations, which drive the distribution toward the limiting LSW form and generate its infinite tail [4].

```python
import numpy as np

def lsw_distribution(rho):
    """LSW self-similar size distribution; rho = R / <R>."""
    rho = np.asarray(rho, dtype=float)
    out = np.zeros_like(rho)
    m = (rho > 0) & (rho < 1.5)
    r = rho[m]
    out[m] = (4*np.e/9)*r**2 * (3/(3+r))**(7/3) * (1.5/(1.5-r))**(11/3) \
             * np.exp(-1.5/(1.5-r))
    return out

rho = np.linspace(0.01, 1.49, 500)
f = lsw_distribution(rho)
print("peak at rho =", rho[np.argmax(f)], " mean check:", np.trapz(rho*f, rho))
# t^{1/3} law: fit <R>^3 vs t for synthetic coarsening data
t = np.linspace(10, 1000, 50)
Rmean = (0.8*t)**(1/3) * (1 + 0.02*np.random.default_rng(1).standard_normal(50))
slope = np.polyfit(t, Rmean**3, 1)[0]
print("fitted d<R>^3/dt =", round(slope, 4), "(true 0.8)")
```

### 4.5 Grain growth and the Mullins–von Neumann law

A cognate coarsening process is *grain growth* in polycrystals, where boundaries migrate by mean curvature, $v_n = M\gamma\kappa$. For a two-dimensional network with isotropic boundary energy, von Neumann and Mullins derived an exact topological law: the rate of change of a grain's area depends only on its number of sides $n$ [7],

$$\frac{dA_n}{dt} = \frac{\pi M \gamma}{3}\,(n - 6),$$

so grains with fewer than six sides shrink and vanish while those with more than six grow — six-sided grains are stationary in area. The proof integrates curvature around the grain boundary and uses the Herring $120^\circ$ angle condition at triple junctions; the total curvature is fixed by topology (a discrete Gauss–Bonnet argument), leaving only $n$. MacPherson and Srolovitz later extended the law to three dimensions [7]. Multi-phase Cahn–Hilliard and Allen–Cahn models reproduce this law numerically, providing a sharp quantitative benchmark for phase-field codes.

##5 Empirical and Theoretical Evidence

**Energy dissipation.** The cornerstone analytical result is the gradient-flow dissipation identity: every sufficiently regular solution satisfies $dE/dt = -\int M|\nabla\mu|^2 \le 0$, so coarsening is the inexorable descent of interfacial energy under mass conservation. Numerical schemes are judged by whether they preserve this property discretely [6].

**The $t^{1/3}$ exponent in silico and in vitro.** Large-scale Cahn–Hilliard simulations robustly exhibit $\langle R\rangle \sim t^{1/3}$ at late times, confirming the LSW prediction within the diffuse-interface model itself. Experimentally, precipitate coarsening in alloys such as Al–Li and Ni-based superalloys follows the cubic law over decades in time, with rate constants $K$ consistent with independently measured diffusivities and interfacial energies [3]. Molecular dynamics of bubble coarsening with hundreds of millions of particles has directly confirmed the self-similarity of the LSW bubble-size distribution [8].

**The Cahn–Hilliard–Cook extension.** Adding conserved thermal noise, $\partial_t c = \nabla\cdot(M\nabla\mu) + \nabla\cdot\xi$ with $\langle \xi\xi\rangle \propto k_BT$, yields the Cahn–Hilliard–Cook equation, essential for nucleation in the metastable regime where deterministic dynamics is frozen. Its sharp-interface limit is Hele-Shaw flow with stochastic forcing; for sufficiently weak noise the limit is rigorously deterministic [5].

**Deviations and refinements.** Real systems deviate from ideal LSW in informative ways: finite volume fractions accelerate coarsening (Brailsford–Wynblatt and Marqusee–Ross corrections), elastic misfit between precipitate and matrix biases shapes and kinetics, and transient regimes can masquerade as non-LSW exponents. Measured exponents clustering near $1/3$ across alloy families remain the theory's strongest vindication [3].

##6 Limitations

The classical edifice rests on idealizations that fail in specific regimes:

1. **Dilute limit**: LSW theory assumes vanishing volume fraction so particles interact only through the mean field; at finite fractions, direct diffusional interactions and encounter-driven coalescence modify both the exponent and the distribution shape.
2. **Isotropy**: real interfacial energies are anisotropic, producing faceted precipitates and dendritic morphologies that curvature-flow models must be generalized to handle.
3. **Elasticity**: coherent precipitates carry misfit strain energy, which can arrest coarsening or select rafted microstructures invisible to the purely capillary theory.
4. **Hydrodynamics**: in fluid mixtures, advection couples to diffusion (model H), accelerating coarsening beyond $t^{1/3}$ once domains are large enough for flow to matter.
5. **Potential stiffness**: the logarithmic Flory–Huggins potential is singular at pure phases, complicating both analysis and numerics relative to the polynomial double-well.
6. **Noise scales**: in the Cahn–Hilliard–Cook equation, the sharp-interface limit with order-one noise remains delicate; rigorous results require the noise to vanish with $\varepsilon$ [5].

##7 Conclusion

From Cahn and Hilliard's gradient-penalized free energy [1] flows a remarkably complete story: the $H^{-1}$ gradient-flow structure of the Cahn–Hilliard equation, the linear selection of the spinodal wavelength [2], the matched-asymptotic collapse onto Mullins–Sekerka free-boundary flow [5], and the universal $t^{1/3}$ coarsening and self-similar distribution of Lifshitz, Slyozov, and Wagner [3, 4]. The Mullins–von Neumann topological law [7] and energy-stable discretizations [6] extend the framework to grain networks and computation, while experiments and large-scale simulations continue to confirm its predictions [8].

Open frontiers remain: coarsening at finite volume fraction without mean-field crutches, the interplay of elasticity and capillarity in coherent microstructures, stochastic sharp-interface limits at physical noise strength, and structure-preserving numerics for the degenerate-mobility and hydrodynamic regimes. The Cahn–Hilliard equation, nearly seventy years old, is still generating mathematics.
