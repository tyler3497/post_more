The Linux CPU scheduler, long treated as an immutable kernel artifact, has become a programmable surface. This thesis examines two coupled revolutions in Linux scheduling: the Earliest Eligible Virtual Deadline First (EEVDF) scheduler that displaced CFS as the default fair-scheduling algorithm beginning with kernel 6.6, and sched_ext, the extensible scheduling class merged in kernel 6.12 that delegates scheduling decisions to BPF programs loaded from userspace. # sched_ext and the BPF Scheduler Revolution: Pluggable Scheduling Classes in Linux, EEVDF Virtual Deadlines, and Userspace Scheduling Policy

## Abstract

The Linux CPU scheduler, long treated as an immutable kernel artifact, has become a programmable surface. This thesis examines two coupled revolutions in Linux scheduling: the Earliest Eligible Virtual Deadline First (EEVDF) scheduler that displaced CFS as the default fair-scheduling algorithm beginning with kernel 6.6, and sched_ext, the extensible scheduling class merged in kernel 6.12 that delegates scheduling decisions to BPF programs loaded from userspace. We formalize EEVDF's lag accounting, eligibility predicate, and virtual-deadline ordering, proving its bounded-lag fairness guarantee and showing how short-slice, latency-sensitive tasks are preferentially dispatched. We then dissect sched_ext's operational contract - the sched_class hierarchy, the `select_cpu`/`enqueue`/`dequeue`/`dispatch`/`yield` ops, dispatch queues (DSQs), BPF maps as the userspace control plane, and the kernel's integrity watchdogs that restore the default scheduler on any fault. We survey production schedulers built on the framework (scx_lavd, scx_bpfland, scx_rusty, scx_layered), characterize their design trade-offs for gaming, interactive, and server workloads, and evaluate the safety, performance, and expressiveness costs of moving policy out of the kernel.

## 1. Introduction

For nearly two decades, the Completely Fair Scheduler (CFS), merged in Linux 2.6.23 (2007), was the unquestioned arbiter of CPU time on Linux. CFS accumulated an ever-growing pile of heuristics - sleeper fairness bonuses, wakeup preemption tweaks, NUMA balancing patches - each fixing a visible pathology while complicating the whole.

Two developments broke the logjam. First, in 2023, Peter Zijlstra proposed replacing CFS's vruntime machinery with Earliest Eligible Virtual Deadline First (EEVDF), an algorithm tracing to Stoica and Abdel-Wahab's 1995 work on fair queueing [1][3]. EEVDF was merged as an option in kernel 6.6 and became the default fair-scheduling discipline [2][7]. Second, Tejun Heo and collaborators pushed sched_ext onto the mailing lists in late 2022: a new *scheduling class* that implements no policy at all, but invokes BPF programs - loaded at runtime from userspace - to make every interesting scheduling decision. After extended controversy, the sched_ext core was merged in the 6.12 kernel [4][5].

The significance of sched_ext is difficult to overstate. Historically, experimenting with a new Linux scheduler meant maintaining an out-of-tree patch set (BFS, MuQSS) - a burden so heavy that scheduler research on production systems was effectively gated. sched_ext turns a scheduler into a *loadable program*: installed, hot-swapped, and unloaded without rebooting - and the kernel guarantees the system remains schedulable no matter what the BPF program does. On any detected error, stalled runnable task, or SysRq key sequence, the kernel aborts the BPF scheduler and falls back to the built-in classes [5].

This thesis provides a rigorous treatment of both revolutions. Our contributions are:

1. A formal exposition of EEVDF's lag accounting, eligibility predicate, and virtual-deadline ordering, including a proof sketch of its bounded-lag fairness guarantee.
2. A complete dissection of the sched_ext contract: class ordering, the ops table, dispatch queues, and the integrity watchdogs.
3. A comparative analysis of the principal sched_ext schedulers and their workload affinities.
4. An evaluation methodology for latency-sensitive workloads and the safety/performance trade-offs of programmable scheduling.

## 2. Background and Related Work

### 2.1 From CFS to EEVDF

CFS modeled each CPU's runqueue as a red-black tree keyed by virtual runtime (*vruntime*), always dispatching the smallest-vruntime task. The design was elegant, but latency handling was bolted on through heuristics - most notoriously the sleeper bonus, which granted slept tasks a vruntime discount on wakeup. These heuristics interacted poorly with the bursty, phased behavior of modern interactive workloads, motivating Zijlstra's rewrite [1].

EEVDF replaces heuristic latency handling with a *deadline*. Each task carries **lag** - the difference between the CPU time it was entitled to and the time it received (positive means owed CPU, negative means over-consumed) - and a **virtual deadline (VD)**, computed as the task's *eligible time* plus its allotted time slice. A task is **eligible** iff its lag is >= 0; among eligible tasks, the scheduler dispatches the one with the earliest virtual deadline. Shorter slices yield earlier deadlines, so latency-sensitive tasks are preferentially scheduled *without any special-case heuristic* [1][2], and tasks may request slice lengths via `sched_setattr()` [3].

A subtle problem is sleeping tasks: a task that sleeps with negative lag could wake with a large positive lag and monopolize the CPU. EEVDF's answer is *lag decay*: a sleeping task remains on the runqueue in a "deferred dequeue" state so its lag decays over virtual runtime, and long sleepers eventually have their lag reset [3].

### 2.2 The long road to pluggable schedulers

Pluggable schedulers are not a new idea. Con Kolivas's BFS showed a single global runqueue with a fixed 6 ms slice could beat CFS on desktop interactivity, and MuQSS added per-CPU skiplist queues with deadline ordering for multi-core scalability - but both lived permanently out-of-tree [8]. Research systems like Google's ghOSt proved the appetite for userspace policy but paid a steep kernel-userspace communication cost.

sched_ext differs from both lineages. Unlike BFS/MuQSS it requires no kernel patching - it is a *mainline* extension point. Unlike ghOSt it executes policy *inside the kernel* in the BPF virtual machine, invoked synchronously on the scheduling path, with BPF maps as the high-bandwidth shared-memory interface to a userspace control plane [4].

### 2.3 Related BPF infrastructure

sched_ext builds on a decade of BPF maturation: the verifier, BTF for kernel data-structure access, sleepable BPF programs, and per-CPU maps. Scheduler callbacks are BPF programs attached to `sched_ext_ops`, loaded with the standard `bpftool`/`libbpf` toolchain; the upstream sched-ext organization ships production schedulers as Rust+BPF crates [6].

## 3. Methodology

Our analysis is grounded in the primary kernel documentation and patch series [3][5], contemporary LWN kernel coverage [1][2][4], and the open-source sched_ext scheduler corpus [6]. We proceed as follows.

1. **Formal reconstruction.** We reconstruct EEVDF's scheduling rule [1][2][3] and derive its lag-bound guarantee from first principles.
2. **Contract analysis.** We enumerate sched_ext's ops table and class-ordering semantics from the in-kernel documentation added with the merge [5].
3. **Workload characterization.** We classify the design goals of the four principal sched_ext schedulers (scx_lavd, scx_bpfland, scx_rusty, scx_layered) [4][6], mapping each to the workload shapes it targets.
4. **Evaluation framework.** We specify a reproducible measurement methodology for latency-sensitive scheduling evaluation (Section 5), identifying the metrics and confounds any honest comparison must control.

We deliberately avoid presenting single-machine benchmark numbers as universal truths: scheduler behavior is workload- and topology-sensitive, and the honest contribution here is the *framework* for comparison rather than a leaderboard.

## 4. Deep Technical Dive

### 4.1 The sched_class hierarchy and the sched_ext contract

The Linux scheduler core dispatches through a chain of *scheduling classes* ordered by priority. sched_ext inserts `ext_sched_class` above `fair_sched_class`, so the BPF scheduler is consulted before EEVDF whenever the core picks the next task [5].

A BPF scheduler implements its policy by filling in a `struct sched_ext_ops`. The essential callbacks:

| Operation | Invoked when | Contract |
|---|---|---|
| `select_cpu` | a task wakes | optionally choose the CPU it should run on |
| `enqueue` | task becomes runnable | place it on a dispatch queue or internal structure |
| `dequeue` | task stops being runnable | remove it from scheduler state |
| `dispatch` | the CPU needs its next task | return the chosen task, or `NULL` to fall through to the fair class |
| `yield` | task calls `sched_yield()` | demote it behind its peers |
| `set_cpus_allowed` / `update_idle` | affinity changes / idle state transitions | keep placement consistent with constraints |

The key semantic subtlety is the **fall-through**: if `dispatch` returns `NULL`, the core proceeds to `fair_sched_class` as if sched_ext were absent. A BPF scheduler may also call `scx_bpf_switch_all()` from its `init()` op, routing *all* `SCHED_NORMAL`, `SCHED_BATCH`, `SCHED_IDLE`, and `SCHED_EXT` tasks through itself [5].

> **Theorem: (sched_ext integrity).** *System schedulability is invariant under arbitrary BPF scheduler behavior.* The BPF verifier guarantees callbacks terminate and cannot corrupt kernel memory; a watchdog detects stalled runnable tasks and aborts the scheduler; an operator can always force fallback via SysRq-S. On abort, every task reverts to the built-in classes [5].

It converts scheduler development from a kernel-privileged activity into something like writing a userspace driver: mistakes crash the scheduler, not the machine.

### 4.2 EEVDF: lag, eligibility, and virtual deadlines

We now formalize EEVDF. Let `vruntime_i(t)` be task *i*'s virtual runtime and `V(t)` the runqueue's virtual time. The **lag** is `lag_i(t) = V(t) - vruntime_i(t)` (positive: owed CPU; negative: over-consumed). The **eligibility predicate**: task *i* is *eligible* iff `lag_i(t) >= 0`. The **virtual deadline**: `VD_i = eligible_time_i + slice_i`. The **dispatch rule**: dispatch the eligible task with minimum `VD_i`.

Three consequences follow. First, *fairness is structural*: ineligible (negative-lag) tasks cannot run, so no task accumulates unbounded lead over its peers. Second, *latency handling is emergent*: a short-slice task receives an early virtual deadline and preempts longer-slice competitors - the behavior CFS approximated with sleeper bonuses [1][2]. Third, *preemption is deadline-driven*: a waking task with an earlier virtual deadline preempts the current task, with no separate wakeup-preemption heuristic.

The deferred-dequeue mechanism for sleeping tasks deserves emphasis. When a task sleeps, it lingers on the runqueue, marked for deferred dequeue, while its lag decays over virtual runtime - closing the exploit in which a task slept briefly to launder negative lag into a scheduling advantage, a class of gaming that plagued CFS's sleeper fairness for years [3].

### 4.3 Dispatch queues, dispatch paths, and watchdog guarantees

On the sched_ext side, the central abstraction is the **dispatch queue (DSQ)**. Rather than per-CPU runqueues as in CFS/EEVDF, a BPF scheduler organizes runnable tasks into DSQs of its own design: per-CPU for locality, global for work-conserving simplicity (the BFS lineage), or arbitrary groupings (per-cgroup, per-NUMA-node, per-"layer" as in scx_layered). `enqueue` fills DSQs; `dispatch` drains them.

The dispatch path is worth tracing. When a CPU must schedule, the core calls into `ext_sched_class`; the BPF `dispatch` program runs *on that CPU, synchronously*, and either returns a task (performing the context switch, optionally direct-dispatching to a specific idle CPU) or returns `NULL`, in which case the core falls through to EEVDF.

Because `dispatch` runs synchronously on the scheduling path, its cost adds directly to context-switch latency - which is why verifier complexity bounds matter for performance, not just safety. Production schedulers keep dispatch O(1) or O(log n), precomputing placement in `enqueue`/`select_cpu` and leaving `dispatch` as a cheap queue pop.

The watchdog closes the loop: the kernel independently tracks whether runnable tasks make progress, and if a BPF scheduler enqueues tasks but never dispatches them - the scheduling equivalent of deadlock - it aborts the scheduler and restores EEVDF. Scheduler authors cannot wedge the machine by dropping tasks on the floor [5].

### 4.4 Representative schedulers: scx_lavd, scx_bpfland, scx_rusty, scx_layered

The sched_ext ecosystem has already produced a small zoo of schedulers, each embodying a different philosophy [4][6]:- **scx_lavd** (Latency-Aware Virtual Deadline): targets interactivity and gaming, aiming for consistently higher frame rates - the EEVDF idea carried into BPF, with latency awareness in the deadline arithmetic rather than bolted on.
- **scx_bpfland**: a vruntime-based scheduler minimizing interactive response times, exposing tunables such as `--slice-us` (default 5000 us) and `--slice-us-min` (default 500 us) to trade throughput against responsiveness [6].
- **scx_rusty** (and scx_rustland): written in Rust; scx_rustland forwards scheduling *events* to userspace and decides there - an architecture Tejun Heo was initially skeptical of, but which works because the event channel is cheap enough for many workloads [4].
- **scx_layered**: partitions CPUs and tasks into layers with distinct per-layer policies - e.g., aggressive-preemption latency layers alongside long-slice throughput layers - demonstrating *heterogeneous policy* within one machine, something no in-kernel scheduler has offered.

| Scheduler | Primary objective | Queue structure | Policy location |
|---|---|---|---|
| EEVDF (in-kernel) | fair sharing + emergent latency | per-CPU rb-tree by VD | kernel |
| scx_lavd | gaming frame consistency | per-CPU DSQs, deadline-ordered | BPF |
| scx_bpfland | interactive response time | vruntime-ordered DSQs | BPF |
| scx_rusty | throughput + Rust safety | per-CPU DSQs | BPF |
| scx_rustland | policy experimentation | event forwarding | userspace |
| scx_layered | heterogeneous workload classes | layered DSQs | BPF |

### 4.5 Userspace policy injection through BPF maps

The most underappreciated half of sched_ext is the *control plane*. The BPF scheduler runs in the kernel but shares state with userspace through BPF maps: per-task metadata, tunables, statistics, hints. The canonical pattern is a frame-deadline-aware game scheduler: the compositor publishes each render thread's upcoming vblank deadline into a map, and the scheduler boosts threads approaching their deadlines and demotes them afterward - policy *impossible* for a generic in-kernel scheduler, which cannot know what a frame is [4].

This inverts the traditional division of labor. Previously, application knowledge stopped at `nice()` values and cgroup weights - impoverished, one-dimensional hints. With sched_ext, applications expose rich, high-frequency state to the scheduler, and the scheduler can be *co-designed* with the application - at the cost of a distributed system (BPF program, map schema, userspace agent) with its own versioning concerns.

## 5. Empirical Results and Formal Arguments

### 5.1 The EEVDF lag bound

We now prove the central formal claim about EEVDF.

> **Theorem: (EEVDF bounded lag).** *In EEVDF, the lag of any continuously runnable task is bounded: `-slice_max <= lag_i(t) <= slice_max`, where `slice_max` is the maximum time slice in the system.*

*Proof sketch.* A task with `lag_i(t) < 0` is ineligible and cannot be dispatched, while every eligible task has non-negative lag and the scheduler always dispatches an eligible one. As peers run, the runqueue virtual time `V(t)` advances while the ineligible task's vruntime does not, so `lag_i(t) = V(t) - vruntime_i(t)` increases until it reaches zero and the task becomes eligible - dispatched no later than one maximum slice after the earliest-deadline eligible task completes. Symmetrically, an eligible task's positive lag is consumed by dispatching it in virtual-deadline order, so lag cannot exceed one slice in either direction. ∎

The practical import: EEVDF provides *deterministic fairness* - a quantitative bound on how far any task can fall behind - whereas CFS offered only asymptotic fairness modulated by heuristics. Short-slice tasks see better tail latency because their virtual deadlines are early *by construction*; the scheduler never has to guess which tasks are interactive.

### 5.2 An executable model of virtual-deadline selection

The following Python model simulates EEVDF's core decision - selecting the eligible task with the earliest virtual deadline - faithfully reproducing the ordering semantics of [1][2][3] in simplified form:

```python
import heapq

def virtual_deadline(eligible_time, time_slice):
    # VD_i = eligible_time_i + slice_i  (per EEVDF dispatch rule).
    return eligible_time + time_slice

def eevdf_pick(tasks):
    # tasks: list of dicts with name, lag, eligible_time, slice.
    # Returns the name of the task EEVDF would dispatch.
    eligible = [t for t in tasks if t["lag"] >= 0.0]
    if not eligible:
        return None  # all tasks ahead of their share; advance virtual time
    return min(eligible,
               key=lambda t: virtual_deadline(t["eligible_time"], t["slice"]))["name"]

# Three tasks start together; C is latency-sensitive (short slice).
tasks = [
    {"name": "A", "lag": 0.0, "eligible_time": 0.0, "slice": 10.0},
    {"name": "B", "lag": 0.0, "eligible_time": 0.0, "slice": 10.0},
    {"name": "C", "lag": 0.0, "eligible_time": 0.0, "slice": 2.0},
]
print(eevdf_pick(tasks))  # -> 'C': earliest VD (0 + 2.0), no heuristic needed
```

The output is `C`: the short-slice task wins purely through deadline arithmetic - no heuristic needed to *infer* interactivity, unlike CFS's sleeper bonus.

### 5.3 Evaluation methodology for latency-sensitive workloads

Any empirical claim about a sched_ext scheduler must control the confounds that dominate scheduler measurement. We propose the following discipline:

1. **Metrics.** Report *distributions*, not means: p50/p99/p999 of wakeup latency (via `perf sched` or `schbench`) and frame-time percentiles for gaming workloads (the metric scx_lavd optimizes [4]). Tail latency is the entire game; mean-latency comparisons are nearly meaningless.
2. **Co-location.** Evaluate under contention: a latency-sensitive task alone on an idle machine tells you nothing. Use `hackbench`/`schbench` backgrounds plus CPU-bound spinners sized to the machine.
3. **Confound control.** Pin frequency governors (`performance`), explicitly model SMT, control NUMA placement, and report kernel version and scheduler tunables (e.g., scx_bpfland's `--slice-us`) [6]. Results without tunables are not reproducible.
4. **Overhead accounting.** Measure BPF dispatch-path cost directly: context-switch latency with and without the scheduler loaded, and BPF program time via `bpftool prog profile`. A scheduler improving p99 wakeup latency 10% while adding 2 us to every context switch may be a net loss for syscall-heavy workloads.
5. **Fallback verification.** Kill the loader mid-benchmark and verify tasks revert to EEVDF without lost progress - the integrity theorem of Section 4.1 is a *testable* claim [5].

Early community reports are encouraging but preliminary: scx_lavd's developers report consistently higher, more stable frame rates in games [4], and scx_bpfland targets measurable reductions in interactive response time [6]. sched_ext has turned scheduler evaluation from "trust the kernel defaults" into an experimental science anyone can practice - which is itself the revolution.

## 6. Limitations and Open Problems

**Verifier expressiveness.** The verifier bounds what schedulers can compute: no unbounded loops, limited stack, restricted helpers. Sophisticated policies - learned cost models, long-horizon planning - must split across the BPF fast path and a userspace slow path (the scx_rustland pattern [4]), reintroducing communication latency for the hardest decisions.

**Feature parity.** At merge, sched_ext lacked mature cpufreq integration, CPU hotplug, and full cgroup semantics; basic cgroup support landed in 6.12 with more promised later [4]. Work-conserving behavior across DSQs, NUMA-aware placement, and energy-aware scheduling integration remain active development areas.

**The tuning burden.** Programmability exports the kernel developers' hardest problem - choosing good parameters - to every user. scx_bpfland's `--slice-us` knob is the thin end of a wedge: a misconfigured scheduler can do *worse* than the defaults, and the community has not yet developed the autotuning practices that make programmability safe for non-experts [6].

**Security surface.** A compromised BPF scheduler cannot crash the machine (Section 4.1), but within its domain it *can* starve chosen tasks or exfiltrate timing information through scheduling decisions - a subtle side channel. The threat model for multi-tenant machines running third-party schedulers is not yet fully understood.

**Evaluation standards.** The field lacks agreed benchmarks for scheduler comparison; until reproducible, workload-diverse harnesses exist, claims about sched_ext schedulers remain anecdotal.

---

## 7. Conclusion

EEVDF and sched_ext are two answers to the same crisis: the collapse of heuristic scheduler design under its own complexity. EEVDF answers *within* the kernel, replacing CFS's pile of latency heuristics with a single deadline mechanism from which fairness and responsiveness both emerge [1][2][3]. sched_ext answers *outside* it, conceding that no single policy serves games, servers, phones, and HPC clusters, and making policy a loadable, swappable, user-programmable artifact with kernel-enforced safety [4][5].

Their combination is more powerful than either alone. EEVDF is the trustworthy fallback - the integrity theorem's "ground truth" - which makes sched_ext's radical programmability deployable: operators can experiment precisely because failure reverts to a proven default. And sched_ext is the laboratory in which EEVDF's successors will be prototyped and measured, shortening the iteration loop from kernel release cycles to compile-and-load.

The CPU scheduler spent forty years as the operating system's most conservative component. It is now its most programmable. The research agenda - verifier-friendly policy languages, autotuned parameters, heterogeneous multi-policy machines, rigorous evaluation standards - will occupy the field for the next decade.
