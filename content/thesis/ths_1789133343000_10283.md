# Algebraic Effects and Effect Handlers: Row-Polymorphic Typing, Handler Semantics, and Compilation by Evidence Passing

## Abstract

Algebraic effects and their handlers provide a uniform, mathematically grounded treatment of computational effects — exceptions, state, nondeterminism, concurrency, and input/output — in which effectful operations are algebraic constructors and handlers are their interpreters. This thesis develops the theory from its categorical origins in the correspondence between algebraic operations and generic effects, through the introduction of handlers as models of effect theories whose handling construct denotes the unique homomorphism induced by the universal property of the free model. It then examines the central typing discipline that makes effects modular in practice: row-polymorphic effect types, in which effect rows with polymorphic tails support precise, compositional tracking of latent effects and their elimination by handling. Finally, it presents the evidence-passing compilation strategy that turns typed effectful programs into efficient code: handlers are passed explicitly as runtime evidence, operations resolve to constant-offset evidence lookups, and tail-resumptive operations execute in place without capturing continuations. The result is a coherent story from equational theory to machine code, grounded in verified sources and illustrated with formal calculi, typing rules, and reference implementations.

## 1. Introduction

Most languages treat effects — state, exceptions, I/O, nondeterminism — as privileged, hard-wired runtime features. A function that might raise, read, or toss a coin is distinguished from a pure one only by convention or a monadic type threaded by hand. Effects cannot be *defined* by libraries, *combined* without bespoke plumbing, or *reinterpreted* without rewriting the program. Monads compose one effect at a time; combining two needs a hand-built transformer, and the tower resists reinterpretation.

Algebraic effects, in the modern form of Plotkin and Power [3] and Plotkin and Pretnar's handlers [1][2], present an effect as an *equational theory*: a signature of operations plus equations. State is the theory of `get`/`put` with the familiar laws; nondeterminism, of a binary `choose` that is associative, commutative, and idempotent. The *free model* of the theory — terms built from operations modulo the equations — induces exactly the computational monad Moggi proposed for that effect. Effects whose monads arise this way are called *algebraic*; with the notable exception of continuations, every standard effect qualifies [2].

Handlers complete the picture by generalizing exception handlers to arbitrary algebraic effects. A handler supplies, for each operation, a *clause* that interprets it, with access to the *continuation* — the rest of the computation suspended at the point of the operation. Handling a computation amounts to *homomorphically mapping* the free-model term onto another computation. The same mechanism behind `try/catch` also implements backtracking search (resume the continuation twice), cooperative concurrency (resume it later), and transactional rollback. The operation is syntax; the handler is semantics; the resumption is control.

Three problems have driven the field. First, *semantics*: what does handling mean when continuations are resumed zero, one, or many times, and when handlers nest? Second, *typing*: how do we track which effects a computation may perform, compose effectful fragments modularly, and know statically that a handler discharged exactly the effects it claims? Third, *compilation*: how do we implement the expensive machinery — capturing delimited continuations, searching the dynamic extent for the innermost handler — without unacceptable runtime cost?

This thesis addresses all three: §2 recalls the categorical background; §3 sets out the methodology; §4 develops row-polymorphic typing, handler semantics, evidence-passing compilation, and scoped resumptions; §5 states the central theorems and surveys performance evidence; §6 discusses limitations; §7 concludes.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 From monads to algebraic theories

Moggi modeled each notion of computation — partiality, exceptions, state, nondeterminism, continuations — by a strong monad, with effectful programs in the Kleisli category. Plotkin and Power refined this: many such monads are *algebraic*, arising as the free-model monad of an equational theory [3]. An *algebraic operation* such as `get : 1 → S` is a constructor of computations subject to equations, and there is a precise bijection between algebraic operations and *generic effects* — morphisms `1 → T(S)` in the Kleisli category.

The *sum* of theories presents the combination of effects; the tensor, under commutativity conditions, their independent combination. Handlers initially resisted algebraic treatment: the exception monad `− + E` is presented by nullary `raiseₑ` operations with *no equations*. Plotkin and Pretnar's breakthrough [1][2] was to treat the handler itself as the model: each handler determines an algebra for the effect theory, and the handling construct denotes the *unique homomorphism* out of the free model guaranteed by its universal property. Exception handling became the special case of a general construct.

### 2.2 The handler calculus

The untyped core calculus, in the formulation refined by Xie et al. [6], extends call-by-value lambda calculus with `perform op` and `handle h e`. Handlers are records of operation clauses; the operational semantics is given by a single characteristic rule:

> *handle h · E[perform op v] → f v k*, where *k = x. handle h · E · x*

Here `E` is an evaluation context containing no handler for `op` (the `bop` side-condition), `f` is the clause for `op` in `h`, and `k` is the *delimited continuation*: the context `E` captured up to the handling boundary, re-wrapped in the handler. This re-wrapping makes the standard handler *deep*: if the continuation performs `op` again, the same handler intercepts it. A *shallow* handler does not re-wrap; the continuation runs unhandled (useful for threading state as a parameter).

The continuation `k` is first-class within the clause, and the clause may invoke it *zero times* (aborting), *once* (the common case), or *many times* (backtracking). This multi-shot power is the source of both the expressiveness of handlers and their implementation difficulty: any reasoning assuming a continuation runs at most once — notably reference-counting optimizations — is unsound under unrestricted handlers [6].

### 2.3 Languages and systems

The ideas have been embodied in a family of languages. **Eff** (Bauer and Pretnar [4]) was the first language designed around algebraic effects, with first-class effects and handlers. **Koka** (Leijen [5]) introduced *row-polymorphic effect types* with Hindley–Milner-style inference and a type-directed evidence-passing compiler. **Frank** (Lindley et al. [8]) makes handlers the primary abstraction via call-by-push-value. **Multicore OCaml** added one-shot handlers to a mainstream language; **Links** and **Effekt** explored neighboring designs. Kammar, Lindley, and Oury [7] showed handlers subsume monads, arrows, and idioms as special cases.

## 3. Methodology

This thesis is developed as a synthesis of primary sources, formalized through three running artifacts:

1. **A core calculus** `λᵉᶠᶠ` in the style of Plotkin–Pretnar [2] and Xie et al. [6], with explicit `perform`/`handle`/`resume` forms, deep-handler semantics, and a small-step operational semantics over evaluation contexts.
2. **A row-typed extension** following Leijen [5], with effect rows `⟨l₁, …, lₙ | μ⟩`, row unification, and handler typing rules that discharge handled labels from rows.
3. **An evidence-passing translation** `F → Fₑᵥ` following Xie et al. [6] and Xie and Leijen [9], elaborating typed terms into a calculus with explicit evidence-vector parameters, with coherence and semantic preservation proved by reference.

Empirical claims are drawn from the benchmark suites in [6] and [9], described qualitatively. The contribution is expository and synthetic: one account of the pipeline from equational theory to efficient machine code.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Row-polymorphic effect typing

A type-and-effect system answers "what may this computation do?" as part of the type. In the row-polymorphic discipline pioneered by Koka [5], the answer is an *effect row*: a finite mapping from effect labels to signatures, terminated by a *row variable* standing for "any other effects." A function type carries its latent effects:

```
map : ∀α β μ. (α → ⟨μ⟩ β, list α) → ⟨μ⟩ list β
```

The row variable `μ` makes `map` *effect-polymorphic*: instantiable with any row, so `map` works uniformly over pure, stateful, or I/O-performing element functions, and the inferred effect is the union of the rows. Rows support extension `⟨l | ε⟩`, concatenation, and unification — with one subtlety Koka handles with unusual care: *duplicate labels*.

With two state instances `⟨state⟨h₁⟩, state⟨h₂⟩ | μ⟩`, Koka [5] treats rows as ordered and lets a handler discharge the *leftmost* matching label, so residual rows still mention the unhandled instance. This makes effect types *modular*: inferred rows mention only the effects actually used.

The typing rule for handling has the characteristic shape (simplified):

```
Γ ⊢ e : A ! ⟨l | ε⟩      Γ ⊢ h : Handler l A B ε
───────────────────────────────────────────────
Γ ⊢ handle h e : B ! ε
```

The handled computation `e` may perform `l` among other effects `ε`; the handler interprets `l`-operations, transforming result type `A` into `B`; the handled expression's row is `ε` — the label `l` has been *eliminated*. Each operation clause is checked against the operation's signature, with the resumption given the type of the handled continuation:

```
Γ ⊢ f : ∀r. (P → ((R → ⟨l | ε⟩ B) → ⟨l | ε⟩ B)) → ⟨l | ε⟩ B
```

That is, the clause receives the parameter of type `P` and a resumption of type `R → ⟨l | ε⟩ B`, producing a `B` computation that may itself perform `l` (for deep handlers). Deep resumption is well-typed because the continuation's row still contains `l`, which the enclosing `handle` re-discharges.

### 4.2 Handler semantics: deep versus shallow, single- versus multi-shot

Denotationally, a handler is a *model* of the effect theory: each clause gives an interpretation `⟦op⟧ : P × (R → A) → A` for a carrier `A`, and handling is the unique homomorphism from the free model extending the return clause. Two independent axes classify handlers:

| Aspect | Deep handler | Shallow handler |
|---|---|---|
| Resumption wrapping | `k = x. handle h · E · x` (re-handled) | `k = x. E[x]` (runs raw) |
| Repeated operations | Intercepted by same handler | Escape to outer handlers |
| Typical use | State-as-handler, exceptions, I/O | State-as-parameter, single-pass folds |
| Typing of resumption | `(R → ⟨l \| ε⟩ B)` | `(R → ⟨ε⟩ B)` |

The deep handler is the default in Eff, Koka, and the Plotkin–Pretnar calculus; the shallow handler appears in Hillerström and Lindley's work and in Multicore OCaml's `Effect.Shallow`. The choice determines the *meaning* of resumption and hence the handler's reasoning principles.

Orthogonally, a resumption may be invoked any number of times — which is what lets a single `choose` implement depth-first search:

```haskell
-- A nondeterminism handler: resume twice, collect both outcomes.
search :: Free Nondet a -> [a]
search = handle return (\Choose k -> search (k True) ++ search (k False))
```

But multi-shot resumption invalidates any analysis that treats the continuation linearly. The evidence-passing work [6] therefore imposes *scoped resumptions* — continuations that cannot escape the dynamic extent of the handler — recovering tractable reasoning while still expressing every standard effect. Multicore OCaml goes further, enforcing *single-shot* resumption dynamically.

> **Theorem (Universal property of handling; Plotkin–Pretnar [2]).** *Let T be an algebraic effect theory and F(X) its free model on a set X. For every model M of T with carrier A and every map g : X → A, there exists a unique homomorphism φ : F(X) → A extending g. The handling construct denotes this φ, where the handler's operation clauses define the T-algebra structure on A and the return clause defines g.*

This theorem is the semantic bedrock: handlers are not an ad-hoc control operator but the *canonical* way to interpret effectful computations, dual to the way folds are canonical for algebraic data types.

### 4.3 Compilation by evidence passing

The naive implementation — capture the evaluation context, then linearly search the stack for the innermost handler — is correct but slow. Leijen's evidence-passing translation [5] eliminates both costs by making handlers *explicit parameters*.

The idea: every function whose type mentions effects takes an additional *evidence vector* argument — a runtime record, one entry per effect label in scope, each holding the handler's *marker* and *clause record*. Performing an operation compiles to a *constant-offset lookup* into the evidence vector — no stack search — followed by invoking the clause. Because the type system knows the full effect row at each program point, the evidence layout is statically determined.

Concretely, the translation elaborates a typed calculus `F` into `Fₑᵥ`, an extension with explicit evidence parameters [6]:

```
⟦Γ ⊢ e : A ! ε⟧  =  λevv_ε. ⟦e⟧       -- every effectful term takes evidence
⟦perform op v⟧  =  λevv. (evv.op).clause v (λx. …) evv
```

Handling extends the evidence vector: `handle h e` evaluates `⟦e⟧` under `evv` extended with `h`'s entry. The resumption is an ordinary closure over the evidence vector — no stack capture needed.

Three optimizations fall out naturally, and they are the empirical core of [6]:

1. **Tail-resumption optimization.** When a clause invokes its resumption in tail position, no continuation closure is allocated: the clause's result *is* the resumption's result, and control transfers in place. State, reader, writer, and exception handlers are tail-resumptive, so their operations compile to ordinary calls.
2. **Constant-offset evidence indexing.** The linear handler search becomes an array index; duplicate labels get distinct slots.
3. **Selective monadic translation and bind inlining.** Pure functions stay in direct style; only effectful code pays for the translation, and elaboration-introduced binds are inlined away.

Xie and Leijen [9] generalize this pipeline: from algebraic effects, through multi-prompt delimited control, through *yield bubbling*, to a final monadic translation into plain lambda calculus — and hence to C, which the Koka compiler implements in production.

### 4.4 Scoped resumptions and the handler abstraction gap

Evidence passing is sound only if the evidence a clause receives faithfully represents the handlers in scope — which fails if a resumption can escape its handler and be invoked under different evidence. The *scoped resumption* restriction [6] closes this gap: resumptions are second-class within clauses and cannot outlive them. The clause typing of §4.1 enforces this — the resumption's type mentions the handled row and cannot be smuggled into the result type.

This also clarifies *capabilities*: in capability-passing style (Effekt) a capability is an explicit value, whereas in evidence passing the evidence vector *is* the capability table, compiler-managed. Row typing makes this safe: the row tells the compiler exactly which capabilities are in scope.

A residual gap remains for *higher-order operations* — operations whose parameters are themselves computations. Standard algebraicity requires first-order parameters; recent work on `λ^hop` extends row-typed handlers to such operations, but their evidence-passing compilation remains open — see §6.

## 5. Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 Formal results

The metatheory of row-typed effect handlers rests on three pillars, each proved in the cited sources:

> **Theorem (Type soundness).** *In the row-typed handler calculus, well-typed programs do not go wrong: progress — a well-typed closed term is a value or takes a step — and preservation — stepping preserves typing, with the effect row evolving only by handler discharge — hold.*

> **Theorem (Adequacy of the handler semantics).** *The operational rule `handle h · E[perform op v] → f v k` is adequate with respect to the denotational semantics: handling denotes the unique homomorphism of the universal property, and the small-step semantics computes it.*

> **Theorem (Correctness of generalized evidence passing; Xie & Leijen [9]).** *The full pipeline — algebraic effects → multi-prompt delimited control → yield bubbling → monadic translation → C — preserves observational equivalence at each stage.*

Additionally, Kammar, Lindley, and Oury [7] prove that handlers *subsume* monads, applicative functors (idioms), and arrows: each is recovered as a handler over a suitable theory, with the handler laws implying the corresponding categorical laws — the formal content of the claim that handlers are a unifying abstraction.

### 5.2 Performance evidence

The evidence-passing papers report systematic benchmarks [6][9]: effect-intensive microbenchmarks — mutable state, exceptions, generators, backtracking search — compiled through the evidence-passing pipeline to native code, compared against Multicore OCaml handlers and hand-written baselines. The reported findings:

1. **Tail-resumptive operations achieve near-zero overhead.** State, reader, and exception benchmarks with in-place tail resumption perform comparably to hand-written state-passing or native-exception code; the handler abstraction is fully erased.
2. **Evidence indexing eliminates search cost.** Replacing the dynamic handler search with constant-offset evidence lookup removes the linear stack walk, with the largest wins on deeply nested handler stacks.
3. **Full continuations remain expensive but rare.** Non-tail-resumptive operations (generators, backtracking) still capture continuations at a cost in line with other delimited-control implementations; programs pay it only for operations that need it.
4. **End-to-end competitiveness.** The generalized pipeline [9] produces C code competitive with Multicore OCaml and within striking distance of hand-optimized C.

## 6. Limitations

**Higher-order effects.** The algebraic framework natively supports only first-order operations. Higher-order operations (bracketing, catching, parallelism combinators) need extensions such as `λ^hop`, whose evidence-passing compilation remains incomplete.

**Multi-shot reasoning.** Multi-shot continuations (Eff, Koka) complicate resource management: linear types, exception safety, and memory reclamation interact subtly with resuming a continuation twice. Multicore OCaml's single-shot enforcement is pragmatic but rules out backtracking search as a handler.

**Row-type complexity.** Row unification with duplicate labels and masking is an algorithmic and cognitive burden; row-mismatch errors remain notoriously difficult, and the interaction of rows with GADTs, existential instances, and substructural types is only partially understood.

**Verification gap.** Type soundness and translation coherence are proved, but functional verification of handler-based programs (e.g., proving a backtracking handler enumerates all solutions) still relies on bespoke program logics; no widely adopted Hoare logic for handlers exists.

## 7. Conclusion

Algebraic effects and effect handlers close a long-standing gap in the theory of computational effects: they give handlers the same algebraic status that Plotkin and Power gave to operations, interpreting handling as the unique homomorphism out of the free model. Row-polymorphic effect typing turns this mathematics into a practical discipline, tracking latent effects compositionally and discharging them precisely. Evidence-passing compilation turns the typed discipline into efficient code, replacing runtime handler search with static evidence layout and erasing tail-resumptive handlers entirely.

Each stage — the universal property, the row-typing rules, the evidence translation, the multi-prompt elaboration — is both a theorem and an implementation technique. The open problems that remain, chiefly higher-order effects and verification of handler-based programs, are active research fronts rather than defects of the framework. For language designers, the lesson is concrete: effects need not be primitive, handlers need not be slow, and the type system can carry the entire pipeline.

### Appendix: Reference Implementations

The free-model encoding of algebraic effects in Haskell, and the handler-as-interpreter pattern in Rust:

```haskell
-- The free model F(X): computations as operation trees [3].
data Free f a = Pure a | forall x. Op (f x) (x -> Free f a)

-- A handler is a fold: a generator for values plus an algebra for
-- operations -- the unique homomorphism of the universal property [1][2].
handle :: Functor f
       => (a -> r)                         -- return clause
       -> (forall x. f x -> (x -> r) -> r) -- operation clauses + resumption
       -> Free f a -> r
handle gen _   (Pure a)   = gen a
handle gen alg (Op op k)  = alg op (handle gen alg . k)

-- Nondeterminism as a binary algebraic operation.
data Choose x = Choose x x
  deriving Functor

-- Deep handler: the resumption is re-wrapped, so nested `Choose`
-- operations are intercepted again.
collect :: Free Choose a -> [a]
collect = handle (\a -> [a])
                 (\(Choose x y) k -> k x ++ k y)
```

```rust
/// Algebraic effects, deconstructed: an operation tree plus a handler
/// that folds it -- the deep-handler semantics of [1][2].
enum Step {
    Done(String),
    ReadLine(Box<dyn FnOnce(String) -> Step>),
    PrintLine(String, Box<dyn FnOnce() -> Step>),
}

/// The handler: each clause interprets its operation and resumes the
/// continuation recursively -- i.e. a deep handler.
fn run_console(step: Step) -> String {
    match step {
        Step::Done(s) => s,
        Step::ReadLine(k) => {
            let mut buf = String::new();
            std::io::stdin().read_line(&mut buf).expect("io");
            run_console(k(buf.trim().to_string()))
        }
        Step::PrintLine(msg, k) => {
            println!("{msg}");
            run_console(k(()))
        }
    }
}

/// Row polymorphism, informally: `greet` is polymorphic in the tail of
/// its effect row -- the essence of Leijen's row discipline [5].
fn greet(build: impl FnOnce() -> Step) -> Step {
    build()
}
```
