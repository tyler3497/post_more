# Delay-Tolerant Networking for the Solar-System Internet: The Bundle Protocol, Contact Graph Routing, and Licklider Transmission Protocol Reliability

## Abstract

This paper presents a comprehensive analysis of delay-tolerant networking (DTN) as the architectural foundation of the Solar-System Internet. Motivated by one-way propagation delays of up to twenty-two minutes between Earth and Mars, by frequent and predictable link disruptions, and by highly asymmetric data rates, we examine the three pillars of modern deep-space networking: the Bundle Protocol version 7 (RFC 9171), which implements store-carry-forward message switching over a CBOR-encoded bundle layer; Contact Graph Routing (CGR), which computes earliest-arrival paths through time-expanded contact plans with a modified Dijkstra search; and the Licklider Transmission Protocol (RFC 5326), which delivers reliable red-part data across light-time-delayed links using checkpoint and report segments with timers suspended across outages. We analyze the removal of custody transfer in BPv7, the Bundle Protocol Security framework (RFC 9172/9173), and convergence-layer adaptation including TCPCLv4. Operational evidence from NASA's DTN Project, the PACE mission's thirty-four million bundles, and lunar-orbit DTN tests aboard the Korea Pathfinder Lunar Orbiter grounds the analysis in deployed reality.

## 1 Introduction

In 1962, J. C. R. Licklider articulated the vision of an *"Intergalactic Computer Network"* — globally interconnected computers through which anyone could access data and programs from any site [4]. Six decades later, that vision has matured into an active engineering program: NASA's Solar-System Internet initiative, extending internetworking from low Earth orbit to the lunar surface, to Mars, and ultimately to the outer planets [5]. The defining obstacle is physics. Electromagnetic signals propagate at the speed of light, and across interplanetary distances the resulting delays are not merely large — they are *structural*, reshaping every assumption of conventional protocol design.

| Destination | One-way light time | TCP-style handshake (3 RTT) |
|---|---|---|
| Moon | ~1.3 s | ~8 s |
| Mars (closest / farthest) | 3–22 min | 18 min – 2.2 h |
| Jupiter | 33–53 min | 3.3 – 5.3 h |

A three-way handshake to Mars at maximum elongation consumes over two hours *before a single byte of application data moves*; slow-start congestion control, tuned for millisecond feedback, becomes pathological across such bandwidth-delay products. Worse, connectivity is not merely slow but *discontinuous*: spacecraft pass behind planets, ground stations rotate out of view, and solar conjunctions black out links for weeks.

The Delay-Tolerant Networking architecture, formalized by Cerf et al. in RFC 4838 [3] as a generalization of the Interplanetary Internet work, answers with a radical move: **abandon the end-to-end path**. DTN introduces a *bundle layer* — a message-oriented store-carry-forward overlay above the transport layers of the constituent networks. Bundles wait in persistent storage at each node and forward only when the next scheduled, predicted, or opportunistic contact becomes available. Reliability comes from hop-by-hop accountability composed with end-to-end acknowledgment semantics redesigned for partitioned networks.

This paper contributes a precise exposition of Bundle Protocol version 7 [1], including its CBOR wire format and the consequential removal of custody transfer; a formalization of Contact Graph Routing [6] as a time-dependent shortest-path problem with analysis of its properties and recent capacity-aware improvements [7]; and a dissection of the Licklider Transmission Protocol [4], the convergence layer that makes reliable transfer possible over links where one acknowledgment takes the better part of an hour. Theory is connected to deployment throughout: NASA's DTN Project, operational across the Near Space Network and Deep Space Network since January 2026 [5], and the first lunar-orbit DTN experiments aboard the Korea Pathfinder Lunar Orbiter [8].

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The RFC 4838 architecture

RFC 4838, *"Delay-Tolerant Networking Architecture"* (Cerf, Burleigh, Hooke, Torgerson, Durst, Scott, Fall, Weiss, April 2007), defines DTN as a *message-oriented overlay* above the transport — or other — layers of the networks it interconnects [3]. Its central abstractions are:

- **Endpoints and Endpoint Identifiers (EIDs).** Destinations named by URIs such as `dtn://node/service` or `ipn:243.1`, decoupling identity from underlying addresses.
- **The bundle layer.** A new protocol layer whose PDU, the *bundle*, carries application data units across regions with radically different link characteristics.
- **Store-carry-forward.** Nodes *must* possess persistent storage; a bundle may reside at a node for hours or days awaiting the next contact.
- **Convergence layers.** Adapters carrying bundles over TCP, UDP, LTP, CCSDS space links, or even physical media — the bundle layer never assumes a particular transport.

The architecture accommodates *scheduled* contacts (known a priori from orbital mechanics), *predicted* contacts, and *opportunistic* contacts. Deep-space networking lives almost entirely in the first regime — which is precisely what makes deterministic routing feasible.

### 2.2 From BPv6 to BPv7

The original Bundle Protocol, RFC 5050 (2007), was *experimental*. Fifteen years of implementation experience — in JPL's Interplanetary Overlay Network (ION), the DTN2 reference implementation, and flight experiments — motivated a standards-track revision: RFC 9171, *"Bundle Protocol Version 7"* (Burleigh, Fall, Birrane, January 2022) [1]. Its principal departures:

1. **CBOR wire encoding.** All blocks use the Concise Binary Object Representation (RFC 8949), replacing BPv6's bespoke SDNV scheme with deterministic, compact, easily parsed encodings.
2. **Custody transfer removed.** The most consequential change: BPv7 deletes custody transfer entirely, judging its timer and signaling complexity to outweigh its benefits; reliability is instead composed from convergence-layer reliability and end-to-end status reporting [1].
3. **BPSec integration.** Security moved from BPv6's bolt-on protocol into the coherent BPSec framework (RFC 9172) [2], with extension blocks for integrity (BIB) and confidentiality (BCB).
4. **Rationalized extension blocks.** A small core set — payload, previous node, bundle age, hop count — with the remainder reserved or private-use.

> **Theorem:** *Custody removal (informal).* In a store-carry-forward network, hop-by-hop custody provides no reliability guarantee unobtainable from (a) reliable convergence layers per hop composed with (b) end-to-end delivery confirmation — while custody signals add timer-management complexity that grows with path length under intermittent connectivity.

### 2.3 Naming, time, and the deep-space environment

Three environmental facts dominate DTN design for the Solar-System Internet. First, *delay*: one-way light times make interactive protocols impossible. Second, *disruption*: contacts are episodes with known start/end times, data rates, and light-time delays, separated by outages. Third, *asymmetry*: deep-space downlinks routinely exceed uplinks by orders of magnitude, punishing chatty acknowledgment schemes and rewarding the sparse checkpoint/report signaling of LTP.

## 3 Methodology

This paper is an *architectural analysis* grounded in primary standards and deployed systems. Our method has four stages:

1. **Normative analysis.** We extract wire formats, state machines, and algorithms from RFC 9171 [1], RFC 9172 [2], RFC 4838 [3], and RFC 5326 [4], resolving ambiguities against published errata.
2. **Algorithmic formalization.** We restate Contact Graph Routing [6] as a time-dependent shortest-path problem on a time-expanded graph, identifying which classical assumptions (FIFO property, static edge weights) hold and which fail.
3. **Implementation grounding.** We cross-check claims against NASA JPL's ION implementation — the reference flight DTN stack — and the documented operational record [5][8].
4. **Empirical triangulation.** We draw on published mission data (PACE telemetry, KPLO lunar-orbit tests) and peer-reviewed CGR improvements [7] to assess the architecture under real constraints.

Our criteria throughout are *correctness under disruption*, *delay efficiency* (closeness to the physical lower bound), and *resource frugality* at constrained nodes.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Bundle Protocol Version 7: Structure and Semantics

A BPv7 bundle is an ordered sequence of *blocks*: the **primary block** first, the **payload block** last, and any number of **canonical extension blocks** between them [1].

**Primary block fields** (wire order): *version* (7); *bundle processing control flags* (fragment, administrative record, do-not-fragment, status-report requests for reception/forwarding/delivery/deletion); *CRC type* (0 = none, 1 = CRC-16, 2 = CRC-32, computed with the CRC field zeroed); *destination*, *source*, and *report-to* EIDs; *creation timestamp* — a *(dtn-time, sequence-number)* pair with dtn-time counting seconds since the DTN epoch (2000-01-01T00:00:00Z); and *lifetime*, after which the bundle is silently discarded.

**Canonical block format.** Every non-primary block is a CBOR array of 5 elements (no CRC) or 6:

| Field | Description |
|---|---|
| Block type code | 1 = payload; 6 = previous node; 7 = bundle age (ms); 10 = hop count; 192–255 = private/experimental |
| Block number | Unique within the bundle; identifies BPSec targets |
| Block processing control flags | Must-replicate, discard-on-failure, report-if-unprocessable |
| CRC type | As in primary block |
| Block-type-specific data | Definite-length CBOR byte string (the ADU for type 1) |
| CRC | Present iff CRC type nonzero |

Two design decisions deserve emphasis. First, **fragmentation is proactive, not reactive**: a source fragments an ADU into multiple bundles (each carrying fragment offset and total ADU length) when it knows path MTUs or contact volumes in advance — eliminating BPv6's notorious mid-path reactive fragmentation. Second, **bundle-age and hop-count blocks** give downstream nodes loop suppression and lifetime enforcement without trusting the source clock.

```rust
// Illustrative BPv7 primary block assembly (CBOR, RFC 9171 §4.2)
use serde_cbor::Value;

fn primary_block(dst: &str, src: &str, lifetime: u64) -> Vec<u8> {
    let now = std::time::SystemTime::now()
        .duration_since(std::time::UNIX_EPOCH).unwrap().as_secs()
        - 946684800; // DTN epoch: 2000-01-01T00:00:00Z
    let block = Value::Array(vec![
        Value::Integer(7),                 // version
        Value::Integer(0),                 // processing flags
        Value::Integer(2),                 // CRC-32
        Value::Text(dst.into()),           // destination EID
        Value::Text(src.into()),           // source EID
        Value::Text(src.into()),           // report-to EID
        Value::Array(vec![                 // creation timestamp
            Value::Integer(now as i128),
            Value::Integer(0),
        ]),
        Value::Integer(lifetime as i128),  // lifetime (s)
    ]);
    let mut bytes = serde_cbor::to_vec(&block).unwrap();
    bytes.extend_from_slice(&crc32fast::hash(&bytes).to_be_bytes());
    bytes
}
```

### 4.2 Contact Graph Routing: Deterministic Routing Over Scheduled Contacts

Where terrestrial routing discovers topology, deep-space routing *knows* it: orbital mechanics and station schedules determine months in advance when node *A* can transmit to node *B*, at what rate, and with what delay. This knowledge is a **contact plan** — a set of contacts, each a tuple

$$C = (t_{start},\ t_{end},\ X,\ Y,\ R,\ \delta)$$

with transmitter *X*, receiver *Y*, nominal rate *R*, and one-way light time *δ* [6].

CGR casts routing as graph search over a **time-expanded contact graph** [6]:

1. **Root contact.** An artificial contact $C_{root}$ from the local node to itself — infinite capacity, zero light time, spanning the bundle's lifetime. All routes begin here.
2. **Contact vertices.** Each planned contact becomes a vertex; edges connect contacts where the receiver of one is the transmitter of the next *and* the time intervals permit store-and-forward (arrival plus light time precedes the next contact's end).
3. **Modified Dijkstra search.** From $C_{root}$, minimizing *earliest arrival time* at the destination. Traversal cost incorporates waiting (until $t_{start}$), transmission (bundle size / *R*), and propagation *δ*.
4. **Volume annotation.** After route selection, the contact's residual volume is decremented ("booked"), steering lower-priority bundles around saturated contacts. ION's linear model assumes utilization begins at contact start — a simplification known to mis-model long continuous contacts [6].
5. **Per-waypoint re-computation.** CGR is *not* source routing: each node re-runs the search with its own (possibly fresher) plan, letting downstream nodes correct for congestion or updates the source could not foresee.

> **Theorem:** *Earliest-arrival optimality.* Given a complete, accurate contact plan and the FIFO property (departing later cannot arrive earlier — satisfied when waiting is permitted and transmission is non-preemptive), the modified Dijkstra search returns the route minimizing bundle arrival time [6].

```python
import heapq

def cgr_route(contacts, src, dst, bundle_size, t_now, ttl):
    """Modified Dijkstra over a contact plan. Returns (arrival_time, prev)."""
    INF = float("inf")
    best, prev = {}, {}
    pq = [(t_now, src)]
    best[src] = t_now
    while pq:
        t_avail, node = heapq.heappop(pq)
        if t_avail > best[node] or node == dst:
            continue
        for (ts, te, X, Y, rate, owlt) in contacts:
            if X != node:
                continue
            t_tx = bundle_size / rate
            t_depart = max(t_avail, ts)      # wait for contact start
            if t_depart + t_tx > te:
                continue                    # contact too short
            t_arr = t_depart + t_tx + owlt
            if t_arr - t_now > ttl:
                continue                    # bundle would expire
            if t_arr < best.get(Y, INF):
                best[Y] = t_arr
                prev[Y] = (X, ts, te)
                heapq.heappush(pq, (t_arr, Y))
    return best.get(dst, INF), prev
```

Recent research attacks CGR's simplifications directly: an improved formulation recasts routing as the *Feasible Earliest-Arrival Path with Capacity and Buffer constraints (FEAP-CB)*, adding contact-splitting and edge-pruning so that route search itself respects capacity and buffer limits — proving optimality among feasible solutions instead of discovering infeasibility mid-forwarding [7]. This closes a real gap between CGR theory and buffer-constrained flight hardware.

### 4.3 Licklider Transmission Protocol: Reliability Without Conversations

If BPv7 is the network layer of the Solar-System Internet, the **Licklider Transmission Protocol** (LTP, RFC 5326, 2008) [4] is its deep-space transport — named for the visionary of the intergalactic network. Deployed as a *convergence layer* beneath BP, each LTP "block" carries one bundle across a single scheduled space link.

LTP's core insight: a block need not be uniformly reliable. Each block splits into a **red part** (reliable, via acknowledgments and retransmissions) and a **green part** (unreliable, no acknowledgment). Fully reliable transfer simply marks the whole block red; missions trade reliability for efficiency per block — critical telemetry red, bulk science data (protected by its own end-to-end coding) green.

**The reliability dialogue.** Blocks are chopped into MTU-sized *segments* in a sparse, timer-driven exchange [4]:

| Segment | Direction | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| Data segment (DS) | Sender → receiver | Red/green data; may bear CP/EORP/EOB flags |
| Checkpoint (CP) | Sender → receiver | Solicits a reception report |
| Report segment (RS) | Receiver → sender | Claims received/missing red-part ranges |
| Report acknowledgement (RA) | Sender → receiver | Confirms an RS; closes the loop |
| Cancel (CR) / Cancel-ack (CAR) | Either | Aborts a session |

The sender emits red segments, marks selected ones as *checkpoints*, and starts a checkpoint timer. The receiver accumulates red segments and, on a checkpoint or end-of-block, returns a *report segment* enumerating received and missing byte ranges; the sender retransmits only the gaps. Crucially, **all retransmission timers are suspended during link outages** — LTP consumes *link state cues* from below, freezing timers when the link goes dark rather than firing spuriously [4]. Sessions thus span multiple contact episodes: a block begun in one pass resumes in the next, with zero timeout-and-restart pathology.

> **Theorem:** *Outage transparency.* Because checkpoint, report, and cancel timers are suspended — not reset — across disconnections, an outage induces zero spurious retransmissions, and session progress is monotone in cumulative contact time, never wall-clock time [4].

LTP deliberately provides **no congestion control**; rate control belongs to the link layer. On a scheduled deep-space link, capacity is known from the contact plan, and AIMD probing across a 44-minute RTT would be pure overhead. Authentication extensions are specified separately in RFC 5327.

### 4.4 Security and Convergence: BPSec and the Adaptation Layer

Security in a store-carry-forward network faces an unusual threat model: bundles rest in intermediate storage for hours, possibly on other agencies' infrastructure, with no contemporaneous access to a certificate authority [2]. BPSec (RFC 9172, 2022) answers with *object security* instead of channel security, defining two extension block types [2]:

- **Block Integrity Block (BIB)** — an integrity mechanism (e.g., HMAC-SHA-256 under the RFC 9173 default context) over target blocks, computed over plaintext.
- **Block Confidentiality Block (BCB)** — encrypted content replacing plaintext for its targets.

A *security source* applies operations for a *security acceptor*; *waypoint* nodes may add protections but cannot silently strip them. Hop-by-hop authentication is explicitly *not* a supported service — BPSec protects data, not links [2]. RFC 9173's default contexts (BIB-HMAC-SHA2, BCB-AES-GCM) provide an interoperable baseline, while asymmetric COSE-based profiles remain under active standardization.

**Convergence layers** complete the picture. Standardized adapters include **TCPCLv4** (*draft-ietf-dtn-tcpclv4*) — redesigned for BPv7 with TLS support and session keepalives, the workhorse for terrestrial and near-Earth segments; **LTPCL** — binding one bundle to one LTP block for scheduled deep-space links (§4.3); and **UDPCL** — a minimal datagram adapter for high-rate local links. A single bundle can thus traverse a TCPCL mission-operations LAN, an LTPCL Earth–Mars link, and a CCSDS proximity hop, with no layer above the adapters aware of the transitions.

## 5 Empirical Evidence

DTN for deep space is no longer experimental:

1. **NASA's DTN Project — operational.** The multi-center DTN Project completed in January 2026; DTN is now an *operational service* in the Near Space Network and Deep Space Network [5]. The roadmap builds the Solar-System Internet in phases — DTN across SCaN networks, lunar extension via LunaNet, a Mars relay network anchored by a DTN-enabled orbiter planned for 2030 — with DTN embedded in Artemis requirements [5].

2. **PACE: 34 million bundles, 100% success.** In 2024, NASA's PACE mission became the first Class-B mission to use DTN operationally for housekeeping telemetry: **34 million bundles** at **100% success**, with transfers auto-initiated on contact and downlinks gracefully resumed after unplanned disruptions [5] — the strongest evidence for DTN's core claim of automated disruption handling at scale.

3. **KPLO lunar-orbit tests.** The Korea Pathfinder Lunar Orbiter (Danuri) flew a DTN payload (DTNPL) built by KARI, NASA JPL, and ETRI running JPL's ION stack — the first DTN-ION deployment beyond Earth orbit. At ranges up to 1,250,000 km, it exercised LTP, CFDP, and the Bundle Streaming Service Protocol: LTP command messaging, CFDP image transfer, and real-time video streaming [8], validating the full stack in a genuine cislunar delay/disruption environment.

4. **Algorithmic advances.** The FEAP-CB reformulation of CGR [7] proves capacity- and buffer-aware search strictly dominates classical forward-and-react CGR — operationally significant, since flight processors offer kilobytes to megabytes of buffer, not datacenter DRAM.

The measured verdict: DTN's *mechanisms* (store-carry-forward, contact-plan routing, outage-suspending reliability) work as designed in flight; its *algorithms* (notably CGR resource modeling) remain an active frontier.

## 6 Limitations

1. **Contact-plan fidelity.** CGR's optimality assumes complete, accurate plans. Antennas fail, passes are preempted, solar weather intervenes; stale plans route through contacts that never materialize. Per-waypoint re-computation mitigates but does not eliminate the damage, and the *uncertain contact plan* problem is open research [6].
2. **Buffer exhaustion.** Store-carry-forward converts bandwidth scarcity into storage scarcity. A node accepting bundles it cannot forward becomes a black hole; lifetime expiry bounds the damage, but priority inversion in buffers is left to implementations.
3. **Custody's ghost.** Without custody transfer, a hop lacking a reliable convergence layer detects loss only via end-to-end status reports — potentially a full light-time round trip later.
4. **Key management.** BPSec's object-security model is elegant, but cross-agency key distribution, rollover on decade-long missions, and revocation without CA connectivity are unsolved; the asymmetric COSE context remains unstandardized in flight stacks.
5. **No congestion control.** Neither LTP (by design [4]) nor the bundle layer implements congestion control. Fairness on shared scheduled links is operations policy, not protocol mechanism — a fragility that sharpens as cislunar traffic grows under Artemis.
6. **Interoperability debt.** BPv6 and BPv7 do not interoperate on the wire; mixed deployments need translation gateways, and legacy flight systems remain BPv6-based.

## 7 Conclusion

The Solar-System Internet is being built, bundle by bundle. The DTN overlay of RFC 4838 [3] makes a single profound wager: that *time* can be treated as a routable resource. The Bundle Protocol version 7 [1] encodes that wager in CBOR — a block architecture clean enough for flight software, with BPSec [2] protecting data at rest across untrusted custodians. Contact Graph Routing [6] turns orbital mechanics into a routing algorithm, computing earliest-arrival paths through time itself, with recent work [7] making the search respect the physical limits of buffers and contact volumes. And the Licklider Transmission Protocol [4] — named for the man who dreamed the intergalactic network — delivers reliability across links where an acknowledgment outlasts a terrestrial TCP connection, by the simple expedient of refusing to let timers measure outages.

The operational verdict, from PACE's 34 million bundles to lunar-orbit tests [5][8], is that the mechanisms work. The frontier has moved from *whether* store-carry-forward internetworking is possible to *how well*: uncertainty-aware contact plans, buffer-aware routing, post-quantum BPSec contexts, and congestion management for a traffic-rich cislunar economy. Licklider's 1962 vision assumed the network would come to deep space; instead, deep space forced the network to reinvent itself. The Bundle Protocol, contact graphs, and red/green reliability are the shape of that reinvention.
