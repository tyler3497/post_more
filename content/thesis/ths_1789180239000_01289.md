# Verified Compilation with CompCert: Semantic Preservation Proofs, Simulation Relations, Verified Optimization Passes, and the Verified Software Toolchain

## Abstract

CompCert is the landmark formally verified optimizing C compiler: a full pipeline from a large subset of C99 to ARM, AArch64, PowerPC, RISC-V, and x86 assembly, written almost entirely in Coq and accompanied by a machine-checked proof of semantic preservation [1][2]. This thesis analyzes the engineering of verified compilation: a ladder of eleven intermediate languages with formal operational semantics, joined by per-pass forward simulations that compose into an end-to-end backward simulation [3]; the block-structured memory model of Leroy and Blazy that grounds pointer reasoning [4]; translation validation as the proof-effort lever behind graph-coloring register allocation and lazy code motion [6]; the Flocq-based formalization of IEEE-754 floating point [5]; and the Verified Software Toolchain (VST), which layers separation-logic program proofs on CompCert's semantics for foundationally end-to-end guarantees [7][8]. Coq development metrics — on the order of a hundred thousand lines of specification and proof, dozens of verified passes — quantify the cost of this rigor. We close with frontiers: concurrency, link-time composition, and the residual trusted base of extraction and the assembler.

## 1 Introduction

*Compilers are generally assumed to be semantically transparent: the compiled code should behave as prescribed by the semantics of the source program.* That sentence, from Xavier Leroy's landmark Communications of the ACM paper, is both the mission statement and the indictment of an entire field [1]. Compilers — especially optimizing compilers — are among the most complex symbolic-transformation programs ever written, and despite decades of intensive testing, miscompilation bugs are routinely discovered in production compilers, silently corrupting programs whose safety had been painstakingly established by other means. A formally verified compiler is the principled answer: rather than testing the compiler on finitely many inputs, one proves, once and for all and with machine-checked rigor, that *every* compilation preserves the semantics of its source program.

CompCert (*compilateur certifié*) is the field's reference realization of that ambition [1][2]. It compiles **CompCert C**, a large subset of ISO C99, to assembly for five instruction-set architectures (ARM, AArch64, PowerPC, RISC-V, and x86). The compiler is written almost entirely in the specification language of the Coq proof assistant (now Rocq), and its correctness was entirely proved within Coq itself. The development has grown continuously since 2005: floating-point arithmetic with a verified IEEE-754 formalization [5], new back ends, and a verified ecosystem culminating in the Verified Software Toolchain (VST), which connects Hoare-style program proofs of C source code all the way down to the machine code CompCert emits [7][8].

This thesis tours the four pillars of that guarantee: the **ladder of intermediate languages** [9], the **simulation-relation** proof discipline [3], the **verified optimization passes** with translation validation as the effort lever [2][6], and the **Verified Software Toolchain** on CompCert's semantics [7][8].

> **Theorem (CompCert correctness, informal):** *If `transf_c_program p = OK tp`, then every observable behavior of the assembly program `tp` is an observable behavior of the C program `p` — or `p` goes wrong, in which case `tp` may improve on it.* [1]

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Why verifying a compiler is hard

Compiler correctness was studied long before machine-checked proof — Morris (1973) gave one of the first formal treatments — but three realities defeated every attempt to scale pencil-and-paper proofs to production compilers:

- **Language scale.** Real C has pointers, casts, structs, unions, function pointers, and — most treacherously — *undefined behavior*: hundreds of situations the standard leaves unspecified, which compilers exploit aggressively.
- **Optimization scale.** Dozens of interacting transformations, each a research artifact; proving any single one correct against a realistic semantics is a publication-scale effort.
- **Proof scale.** Hundreds of person-months of machine-checked proof that must stay *maintainable* as optimizations and targets are added.

CompCert's answer to all three is *decomposition*: decompose the language gap into small intermediate languages, decompose the correctness argument into per-pass simulations, and decompose cleverness from trust by verifying validators rather than heuristics.

### 2.2 Observable behavior and undefined behavior

CompCert's correctness is stated over **observable behaviors**: *termination* (with a trace of observable events — system calls, volatile accesses — plus an exit code), *divergence* (infinite execution with a possibly infinite trace), or *going wrong* (execution gets stuck: illegal memory access, division by zero, any other undefined behavior). Behaviors are ordered by *improvement*: a program that goes wrong may be improved into one that terminates or diverges — the compiler is explicitly permitted to turn undefined behavior into *anything*.

> **Definition (Semantic preservation):** *Compilation preserves semantics if, for every source `S` compiling to target `T`, every observable behavior `B` of `T` improves on some observable behavior of `S`. If `S` cannot go wrong, `T` exhibits exactly the behaviors of `S`.* [2]

This is the *garbage in, garbage out* stance of the CompCert literature: dead-code elimination may delete a division by zero along a path whose result is unused, turning a diverging source into a terminating target, and the theorem still holds because the source "went wrong" [3]. For *safe* source programs the guarantee is total: the assembly does exactly what the C semantics prescribes.

### 2.3 The trusted computing base

A verified compiler does not eliminate trust; it *concentrates* it. CompCert's TCB comprises the Coq kernel, Coq's extraction to OCaml, the OCaml toolchain, the unverified front-end pieces (the C parser and per-target assembly printers, both deliberately tiny), the assembler and linker, and the hardware. Everything else — every optimization, every code-generation pass — is proved. Understanding this boundary is essential to understanding what "verified" buys (Section 6).

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 Analytical method

We reconstruct, from the primary papers [1][2][4][5][6], the commented Coq development [3], and secondary expositions [9]: (i) the language ladder and its rationale; (ii) the block memory model [4]; (iii) the simulation toolbox and composition theorems; (iv) the verified pass inventory and where translation validation replaces direct proof; (v) the floating-point [5] and VST [7][8] extensions; (vi) quantitative evidence on cost and benefit.

### 3.2 The final theorem, in Coq

The entire development converges on one machine-checked statement:

```coq
Theorem transf_c_program_correct:
  forall p tp,
  transf_c_program p = OK tp ->
  backward_simulation (Csem.semantics p) (Asm.semantics tp).
```

`Csem.semantics p` and `Asm.semantics tp` are labeled transition systems — small-step operational semantics whose labels are observable events. `transf_c_program` composes all passes (each returning `OK` or `Error`); `backward_simulation` is the formal rendering of semantic preservation: every target behavior improves on a source behavior. The proof is by composition: each pass enjoys a forward-simulation theorem, forward simulations compose, and — because the target semantics are deterministic — a forward simulation from `S` to `T` yields a backward simulation from `T` to `S` [3]. The Coq kernel checks it down to the last case split.

### 3.3 Prove the checker, not the heuristic

CompCert's most portable methodological insight is **translation validation** as a proof-effort lever [6]. Wherever an optimization involves genuine algorithmic cleverness (graph-coloring register allocation, lazy code motion, code layout, inlining heuristics), the pass is split:

- an **untrusted oracle**, typically OCaml, making the clever decisions;
- a **verified validator**, written and proved in Coq, that checks the oracle's output and fails compilation if the check fails.

The validator is small and its proof tractable; the oracle bears no proof burden. Pass correctness reduces to *validator* correctness — the reason CompCert ships sophisticated optimizations without a commensurate explosion in proof effort [2][9].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Ladder of Intermediate Languages and the Block Memory Model

**One concern per rung.** CompCert descends through eleven intermediate languages, each differing from its neighbor in exactly one dimension [9]:

| Language | Representation | What changes at this rung |
|---|---|---|
| CompCert C | C AST (large C99 subset) | source language |
| Clight | simplified C, pure expressions | desugaring, explicit temporaries |
| C#minor | Clight + explicit stack data | evaluation order fixed |
| Cminor | low-level statements | structured control → CFG |
| CminorSel | Cminor + machine operators | instruction selection (smart constructors) |
| RTL | CFGs, pseudo-registers, 3-address code | CFG form, infinite temporaries |
| LTL | RTL + locations (registers/slots) | register allocation |
| LTLin | LTL, linearized | branch tunneling, linear layout |
| Linear | LTLin + explicit labels | CFG → linear code with labels |
| Mach | Linear + frames, calling conventions | activation records, frame layout |
| Asm | target assembly (textual) | pretty-printing to assembly syntax |

The front end (CompCert C → RTL) follows Blazy, Dargaye, and Leroy [1]; the back end (RTL → Asm), including the proof-intensive register allocator, is Leroy's JAR paper [2].

**The memory model.** All pointer reasoning rests on the block-structured model of Leroy and Blazy [4]. Memory is a finite collection of **blocks** with bounds; a pointer is a pair `(b, δ)` — block plus byte offset. Typed `load`/`store` operations are guarded by monotonically evolving **permissions**; reading uninitialized memory yields `Vundef`, which propagates through arithmetic — capturing C's undefined behavior inside a tractable semantics.

```c
int f(void) {
  int x = 4 / 0;   /* undefined behavior: goes wrong */
  int y = 41;
  return y + 1;    /* optimizer may delete the division */
}
```

Here `4 / 0` is a stuck state ("goes wrong"), but dead-code elimination can prove `x` unused, so the compiled program returns `42` without dividing — a legal *improvement* of a going-wrong behavior, exactly the latitude the theorem grants [3].

For passes that *reorganize* memory — spilling, frame layout, the Mach construction — the model provides **memory injections** `j` mapping source blocks/offsets to target blocks/offsets, with `Mem.inject j m1 m2` stating the target extends the source accordingly [4]. Simulation relations for these passes are parameterized by injections, extended as new blocks are allocated.

### 4.2 Simulation Relations: Forward, Backward, Measured, and the Diagram Toolbox

A **forward simulation** `R` from `L1` to `L2` relates states so that initial states are related and every `L1` step from a related state is matched by zero or more `L2` steps — emitting matching observable events — reaching a related state. A **backward simulation** is the mirror image. Forward simulation is the natural direction to *prove*; backward simulation is the useful direction for *users*, constraining what the compiled code can do.

> **Theorem (Forward-to-backward, via determinacy):** *If `R` is a forward simulation from `L1` to a deterministic `L2`, then (the converse of) `R` is a backward simulation from `L2` to `L1`.* [3]

The proof is the elegant heart of the methodology: because `L2` is deterministic, the target steps matching a source step are *forced*, so matching replays backwards. All CompCert IRs are deterministic (front-end desugaring resolves C's nondeterminism), which is why the whole edifice is built from forward simulations alone.

**The diagram toolbox.** Not every pass matches steps one-to-one [2][3]:

| Diagram | Shape | Used when |
|---|---|---|
| Lock-step | 1 source step ↔ 1 target step | instruction selection, linearization |
| Plus | 1 source step ↔ 1+ target steps | passes inserting moves/spills |
| Option | 1 source step ↔ 0 or 1 target steps | branch tunneling |
| Star (measured) | 1 source step ↔ 0+ target steps, decreasing measure | dead-code elimination, step-deleting opts |

The **star diagram** is the subtle one: when a pass *deletes* source steps, matching "zero target steps" naively admits infinite stuttering. A well-founded **measure** that strictly decreases on stuttering steps rules out the degenerate case [2]. Its essence is easily stated computationally:

```python
def star_diagram_holds(src_steps, tgt_steps, measure):
    """Star simulation: every stutter strictly decreases the measure."""
    for (s, s_next, t_seq) in zip(src_steps, tgt_steps):
        if not t_seq:  # stuttering: target takes zero steps
            assert measure(s_next) < measure(s), "measure must decrease"
    return True
```

And the lock-step diagram as a TLA⁺ sketch:

```tla
---- MODULE Simulation ----
EXTENDS Naturals
VARIABLES s, t  \* source and target states

R(s, t) == (* matching relation, e.g. memories equal up to injection *)

LockStep ==
  /\ R(s, t)
  /\ [][ s' \in Next_s(s) =>
        \E t' \in Next_t(t) : R(s', t') ]_<<s,t>>
====
```

**Composition.** Forward simulations compose *relationally*: if `R1` simulates `S → M` and `R2` simulates `M → T`, then `R1 ∘ R2` simulates `S → T` [3]. Prove each pass once, compose mechanically, and the end-to-end theorem — backward simulation from composed forward simulation plus target determinacy — falls out.

### 4.3 Verified Optimization Passes: Kildall Analysis, Inlining, and Register Allocation

CompCert is an *optimizing* compiler: constant propagation, CSE, dead-code elimination, inlining, tail-call recognition, branch tunneling, and graph-coloring register allocation all ship with proofs [2][9].

**Dataflow à la Kildall.** Constant propagation and CSE are built on a Kildall fixpoint solver over RTL CFGs, instantiated with CompCert's **abstract value domain**: each pseudo-register maps to `⊤` (unknown), `⊥` (unreachable), or a known constant [2][9]. The simulation proof shows replacing a register use by its known constant preserves the matching relation:

```haskell
data AbsVal = Bot | Const Integer | Top
  deriving (Eq, Show)

join :: AbsVal -> AbsVal -> AbsVal
join Bot x = x
join x Bot = x
join (Const a) (Const b) | a == b = Const a
join _ _ = Top
```

**Inlining and tail calls.** Inlining duplicates callee bodies at call sites; its simulation proof relates the *call-stack* shapes of source and target via injections over the abstract call stack. Tail-call recognition rewrites eligible calls into jumps reusing the caller's frame, proved by a lock-step diagram over Mach states [2].

**Register allocation: the flagship.** Graph-coloring allocation (George–Appel style) is the most algorithmically sophisticated pass, and exactly where *prove the checker* shines [2]. An untrusted OCaml oracle computes an interference-graph coloring; a small Coq **validator** checks it is a proper coloring respecting calling conventions, and only then is the allocation accepted. A bad coloring *fails compilation loudly* rather than miscompiling — heuristic bugs become compile errors.

**Stack layout (Linear → Mach).** The Mach construction allocates one stack block per frame, mapping abstract slots to concrete offsets; the simulation carries a memory injection from Linear to Mach blocks, with every access shown to hit the right offset with the right permissions [2][4].

### 4.4 Translation Validation, Floating Point, and the Verified Software Toolchain

**Verified validators beyond allocation.** Tristan and Leroy's verified validator for **lazy code motion** — a notoriously subtle partial-redundancy elimination — checks that the anticipability/availability dataflow facts claimed by the untrusted optimizer are genuine fixpoints and that the rewritten code respects them; the validator's correctness proof yields the pass's simulation theorem [6]. The lesson is architectural: *push cleverness into untrusted oracles, keep trust in small checkers.*

**Floating point, done right.** Boldo, Jourdan, Leroy, and Melquiond closed CompCert's historic floating-point gap using **Flocq**, a Coq formalization of IEEE-754 (formats, rounding modes, exceptional values), proving that compilation of float operations — from constant folding through instruction selection to SSE2/ARM/VFP instructions — preserves IEEE semantics [5]: essentially the only compiler with a machine-checked proof covering floating-point code generation.

**The Verified Software Toolchain.** CompCert verifies the *compiler*; **VST** verifies the *programs* [7][8]. VST's **Verifiable C** is a higher-order separation logic for Clight, embedded in Coq, for proving functional-correctness specifications of C programs. Verifiable C is proved sound against CompCert's operational semantics — the same semantics the simulation theorems start from — so the two compose into a *foundational* end-to-end guarantee: a VST proof entails the produced assembly satisfies the specification, modulo only the TCB [7].

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

**Scale.** The Coq development is on the order of **a hundred thousand lines** of specification, implementation, and proof, maintained over two decades across five targets [1][3][9]. The proof-to-code ratio — several lines of proof per line of compiler — is the honest price of the guarantee, *contained* by the architecture: a new back end reuses the front and middle ends unchanged.

**Technique economy.** Roughly twenty passes, eleven languages, one simulation proof per pass — yet the *proof techniques* number only a handful: four diagram shapes, memory injections, translation validation. The design document's three themes — the theorem shaped around undefined behavior, one concern per IR, prove the checker not the heuristic — are load-bearing engineering decisions, not slogans [9].

| Dimension | CompCert | Typical unverified optimizer |
|---|---|---|
| Correctness evidence | Machine-checked proof (Coq kernel) | Test suites, fuzzing |
| Miscompilation | Impossible for safe programs (modulo TCB) | Silent wrong-code bugs, regularly found |
| Optimization breadth | Moderate (~20 passes, no vectorization) | Aggressive (100s of passes) |
| Code quality | Roughly GCC `-O1` class | GCC/LLVM `-O2`/`-O3` |
| TCB | Coq kernel, extraction, OCaml, assembler, HW | The entire compiler |

**Performance.** Benchmarks place CompCert-generated code near GCC at `-O1` — typically within tens of percent — while GCC/LLVM at `-O2`/`-O3` pull ahead via loop optimizations and vectorization that CompCert deliberately omits [1]. For critical embedded software, where *correctness dominates performance*, this is the right trade.

**Impact.** CompCert sees production high-assurance use (notably via AbsInt's commercial support) and has seeded a lineage: CompCertTSO (verified compilation under weak memory), CompCertS, the verified-validators program, and VST's growing corpus of verified C software. The memory model [4] and simulation framework [2] are reused far beyond the compiler.

## 6 Limitations

1. **The trusted computing base.** The proof bottoms out at the Coq kernel, extraction, the OCaml toolchain, the C parser, the assembly printer, the assembler/linker, and the hardware. The parser and printer are small but *not* proved; extraction is trusted — the irreducible residue of verified-compiler work.
2. **Undefined behavior is a license, not a guarantee.** For programs with *any* UB — overruns, use-after-free, signed overflow, data races — CompCert promises nothing, and dead-code elimination observably exploits that latitude [3]. The guarantee is total only for UB-free programs, which is why VST's memory-safety proofs are the natural complement [8].
3. **Concurrency.** The forward-to-backward theorem leans on target *determinacy*, at odds with multithreading's nondeterminism. Bender's 2023 reformulation recovers a global backward simulation for threaded programs without major proof rewrites [10]; CompCertTSO tackles x86-TSO — but upstream CompCert remains single-threaded.
4. **Separate compilation and linking.** The theorem is whole-program. Real software links separately compiled units, hand-written assembly, and libraries. Compositional CompCert extends the framework to verified separate compilation, but this remains frontier rather than shipped reality.
5. **Language coverage.** CompCert C is a large but incomplete C99 subset (historically missing `long long`, unstructured `switch`, `goto`/`setjmp`; coverage has grown across releases). Programs outside the subset do not compile.
6. **Optimization ceiling.** No loop-nest optimization, no auto-vectorization, no profile-guided optimization. For compute-bound workloads the gap versus GCC/LLVM `-O3` is real and structural — the price of keeping every transformation inside the proof envelope.

---

## 7 Conclusion

CompCert's enduring contribution is less the compiler than the *method*: decompose compilation until each piece admits a small, checkable argument, then compose the arguments mechanically. The four ideas — **one concern per IR**, **forward simulations from a four-shape diagram toolbox**, **memory injections**, and **translation validation** — form a reusable blueprint since applied to concurrent compilation, weak-memory models, and verified static analysis [2][4][6][9][10]. The Verified Software Toolchain shows the full payoff: when compiler and program-logic semantics are *the same* Coq definitions, a source-level correctness proof becomes a machine-code-level guarantee [7][8].

The open frontiers — concurrency, compositional separate compilation, shrinking the TCB through verified assemblers and linkers, closing the optimization gap without reopening the proof gap — define the next decade of verified compilation. But the central lesson already stands: *you can trust your compiler, if you prove it* — and CompCert shows, in complete machine-checked detail, exactly how.
