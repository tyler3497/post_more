# Discrete Time Crystals: Spontaneous Breaking of Time-Translation Symmetry in Floquet Many-Body Systems, from No-Go Theorems to Quantum Processor Realizations

## Abstract

The discrete time crystal is a nonequilibrium phase of matter that spontaneously breaks the discrete time-translation symmetry of a periodically driven system, exhibiting rigid subharmonic oscillations at an integer multiple of the drive period. This article presents a comprehensive technical treatment: Frank Wilczek's original 2012 proposal of a quantum time crystal and the no-go theorems of Bruno and of Watanabe and Oshikawa that ruled it out in thermal equilibrium; the 2015–2016 resurrection in Floquet systems through many-body localization, due to Khemani, Lazarides, Moessner, and Sondhi and to Else, Bauer, and Nayak; the eigenstate-order formulation that places discrete time crystals alongside conventional phases defined by long-range order; the prethermal route to time crystallinity without disorder; and experimental realizations on trapped-ion chains, nitrogen-vacancy centers, nuclear magnetic resonance platforms, and Google's Sycamore processor. We derive the canonical pi-pulse Floquet spin model, explain why the observed period doubling is rigid rather than fine-tuned, and close with open questions: the fate of Floquet many-body localization in higher dimensions, heating timescales in the thermodynamic limit, and the boundary between genuine eigenstate order and long-lived metastability.

## 1. Introduction

Crystals break spatial translation symmetry spontaneously. A salt crystal selects a discrete lattice out of the continuum of possible positions, and the resulting rigidity — the lattice spacing does not drift when the salt is squeezed or warmed — is the hallmark of a genuine phase of matter. In 2012, Frank Wilczek asked whether an analogous structure could exist in time: a many-body system whose lowest-energy configuration is periodic motion, spontaneously breaking time-translation symmetry in the ground state [1][2]. The proposal ignited a debate that produced a decisive negative answer for equilibrium systems: under broadly applicable assumptions, a quantum time crystal in the ground state or thermal equilibrium is impossible [3][4].

The story did not end there. In 2015, Krzysztof Sacha showed that ultracold atoms bouncing on an oscillating atom mirror can self-organize into motion with twice the drive period [5], and in 2016 Khemani, Lazarides, Moessner, and Sondhi [6] and Else, Bauer, and Nayak [7] established that periodically driven many-body-localized spin systems host a sharp nonequilibrium phase with rigid subharmonic response. The following year, two experiments observed the phenomenon: a chain of ten trapped ytterbium ions [10] and an ensemble of roughly one million nitrogen-vacancy centers in diamond [11]. In 2021–2022 the concept was pushed further with prethermal time crystals without disorder [14] and time-crystalline eigenstate order on a superconducting quantum processor [12].

This article is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews the equilibrium background: spontaneous symmetry breaking, the Wilczek proposal, and the no-go theorems. Section 3 develops the methodology, centered on the canonical Floquet pi-pulse spin model and the numerical diagnostics used to identify the phase. Section 4 is a deep dive into the five pillars of the field: equilibrium no-go results, Floquet many-body-localized time crystals, prethermal time crystals, experimental realizations, and generalizations to higher-order and quasiperiodic time crystals. Section 5 collects the empirical evidence and the proof structure behind eigenstate order; Section 6 confronts the limitations, and Section 7 concludes.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 Spontaneous symmetry breaking and phases of matter

In equilibrium statistical mechanics, a phase is defined by the spontaneous breaking of a symmetry of the Hamiltonian: the ground state or thermal state fails to be invariant under a transformation that leaves the dynamics unchanged. The Ising ferromagnet is the prototype. Crucially, phases are *rigid*: the order parameter is locked to a quantized value and survives small perturbations. The definition extends from ground states to highly excited eigenstates through **eigenstate order**, in which every eigenstate in the spectrum exhibits long-range order, as in the spin-glass phase of a disordered Ising chain.

Time translation is the symmetry generated by the Hamiltonian itself: a time-independent system is invariant under shifts of the time origin. In a stationary state the probability density does not evolve, so continuous time-translation symmetry cannot be broken in equilibrium — the intuition behind the no-go theorems of Section 4.1. Periodically driven systems possess only *discrete* time-translation symmetry, invariance under shifts by multiples of the drive period T. Breaking *this* symmetry is the subject of the discrete time crystal.

### 2.2 Many-body localization

Many-body localization (MBL) is the interacting generalization of Anderson localization. In a strongly disordered, isolated quantum system, local integrals of motion prevent thermalization: an initial product state retains local memory of its initial conditions for arbitrarily long times. MBL is the key ingredient of the Floquet time crystal, because a driven interacting system that thermalizes would simply heat to infinite temperature — the single trivial Floquet-ergodic phase — erasing any temporal order. Disorder, ordinarily the enemy of order, here *protects* it by freezing the dynamics that would otherwise wash out the subharmonic response [6][7].

### 2.3 Floquet theory in brief

For a time-periodic Hamiltonian H(t + T) = H(t), the stroboscopic evolution over one period is governed by the Floquet unitary U(T), whose eigenstates satisfy U(T)|φ_α⟩ = e^{−i ε_α T}|φ_α⟩ with quasienergies defined modulo 2π/T. The Floquet eigenstates play the role of energy eigenstates in equilibrium, and the notion of eigenstate order carries over: a Floquet time crystal is a phase in which the Floquet eigenstates exhibit spatiotemporal long-range order [6][7][8].

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Wilczek's proposal and the equilibrium no-go theorems

Wilczek's 2012 paper "Quantum Time Crystals" [1] considered a ring of particles threaded by magnetic flux, arguing that the ground state could carry persistent periodic motion. A classical counterpart by Shapere and Wilczek [2] exploited a kinetic-energy minimum at nonzero velocity. The response was swift and negative. Bruno [3] showed that the proposed ground-state currents either vanish or correspond to excited states once the calculation is done carefully, and Watanabe and Oshikawa [4] proved a general no-go theorem: in the thermodynamic limit, for systems with short-range interactions, ground states and thermal equilibrium states cannot exhibit long-range order in time.

> **Theorem (Watanabe–Oshikawa, 2015):** For a quantum many-body system with short-range interactions in thermal equilibrium, lim_{|t−t'|→∞} ⟨O(t) O(t')⟩ cannot oscillate persistently; time-translation symmetry cannot be spontaneously broken in equilibrium. [4]

Later refinements clarified the scope: the theorem rules out simultaneous long-range order in space *and* time, leaving narrow loopholes widely regarded as unphysical. The upshot is that equilibrium time crystals, as Wilczek envisioned them, do not exist. The field therefore moved to *driven* systems, where energy is not conserved and the no-go arguments do not apply.

### 4.2 Floquet many-body-localized time crystals

The breakthrough came in 2016 from two directions. Khemani, Lazarides, Moessner, and Sondhi [6] asked whether driven quantum systems admit a sharp notion of a phase at all, and found that disordered Floquet-MBL systems do: besides the trivial Floquet-ergodic phase, they host a "π spin glass" with long-range-ordered Floquet eigenstates paired by half the drive quantum. Else, Bauer, and Nayak [7] gave the operational definition: discrete time-translation symmetry is spontaneously broken if, for short-range-correlated initial states, some local operator's expectation value is *not* T-periodic despite the T-periodicity of the Hamiltonian.

The structure of eigenstate order is worth stating precisely. In the π spin glass, each Floquet eigenstate has a partner related by a global spin flip, with quasienergies split by π/T. A short-range-correlated initial state overlaps both members of each pair, and the cross terms oscillate with period 2T. Because the pairing is a property of *every* eigenstate, the subharmonic response is independent of the initial state: it is a phase of matter, not a property of a special preparation. Von Keyserlingk, Khemani, and Sondhi [8] further proved absolute stability: the spatiotemporal long-range order survives arbitrary weak perturbations respecting the drive periodicity.

A subtle point, emphasized by Else et al. [7], is that the π spin glass is *not* many-body localized in the strictest sense: the complete set of local integrals of motion of conventional MBL cannot coexist with the non-local Floquet eigenstates. The phase is instead localized in a generalized sense — it retains quasi-local conserved quantities while the eigenstates themselves are long-range correlated.

### 4.3 Prethermal time crystals without disorder

Disorder is sufficient for Floquet time crystallinity but not necessary. In 2017, Else, Bauer, and Nayak showed that at high drive frequencies, even *clean* systems exhibit a long-lived prethermal regime governed by an effective static Hamiltonian, which can host time-crystalline order with a lifetime exponentially long in the ratio of drive frequency to local energy scales [13]. The prethermal time crystal is thus a *metastable* phenomenon rather than a true eigenstate-ordered phase — but "metastable" here means lifetimes that can exceed any experimental timescale by many orders of magnitude.

The cleanest demonstration came in 2021, when Kyprianidis et al. observed a prethermal discrete time crystal in a chain of 25 trapped ions *without* disorder [14]. Driving at high frequency, the system is described for an exponentially long prethermal window by an effective Hamiltonian with an emergent symmetry, and the subharmonic response persists for hundreds of cycles. A related experiment by Rovny, Blum, and Barrett [15] found discrete-time-crystal signatures in an *ordered* dipolar system via NMR, further supporting the prethermal picture.

### 4.4 Experimental realizations

**Trapped ions (Zhang et al., 2017).** The Maryland group used a chain of 10 ^171Yb+ ions with long-range Ising interactions mediated by spin-dependent optical dipole forces and programmable disorder from AC Stark shifts [10]. They observed the subharmonic peak at half the drive frequency, mapped its collapse as the pulse imperfection ε increased, and showed that interactions *restore* the rigid response that the non-interacting system cannot sustain — the smoking gun of the many-body phase.

**NV centers in diamond (Choi et al., 2017).** The Harvard group used an ensemble of roughly one million dipolar-coupled NV-center spins at room temperature, a three-dimensional system where strict MBL is not expected [11]. They nevertheless observed long-lived temporal order robust against perturbations, attributing the stability to slow thermalization rather than true localization — demonstrating that time-crystalline signatures are accessible at room temperature in a solid-state platform.

**Superconducting qubits (Mi et al., 2022).** The Google Quantum AI collaboration implemented the Floquet-MBL model on 20 qubits of the Sycamore processor [12]. Going beyond previous experiments, they verified the *defining* criteria of a phase: a time-reversal protocol distinguishing intrinsic thermalization from external decoherence, finite-size scaling toward the thermodynamic limit, robustness over an extended parameter range, and — via quantum typicality with scrambling circuits — the response of effectively all initial states in a single run.

**NMR and other platforms.** Rovny et al. [15] observed discrete-time-crystal signatures in ordered ammonium dihydrogen phosphate via NMR; the QuTech group realized an MBL-DTC in nine ^13C nuclear spins in diamond with lifetimes an order of magnitude longer than the Sycamore demonstration; and dissipative time crystals — where an environment stabilizes rather than destroys the order — were realized with Bose–Einstein condensates in optical cavities.

### 4.5 Generalizations: higher-order and quasiperiodic time crystals

The period doubling (n = 2) is the simplest case. Yao et al. [9] constructed models with period-nT response for general integer n, using clock-like transitions between n-level systems, and mapped the dynamical phase transition at which the crystal melts. Sacha's original atomic proposal [5] showed that even *single-particle* driven systems can exhibit nT responses, though without the many-body rigidity that defines the phase. In 2018, Autti et al. reported a time *quasicrystal* — and its transition to a continuous time crystal — in superfluid helium-3, extending the concept beyond strict periodicity.

### 5.2 Proof sketch: from eigenstate pairing to rigid response

The theoretical core can be summarized as a chain of implications:

1. **Definition.** Discrete time-translation symmetry is spontaneously broken if there exists a local operator O and a short-range-correlated state |ψ⟩ such that ⟨ψ(t)|O|ψ(t)⟩ is not T-periodic.
2. **Eigenstate criterion.** This holds if and only if the Floquet eigenstates cannot all be short-range correlated.
3. **Pairing.** In the π spin glass, eigenstates form pairs split by π/T. Cross terms between paired eigenstates oscillate at exactly half the drive frequency.
4. **Rigidity.** The splitting is enforced by the emergent symmetry and the MBL structure; perturbations preserving the drive period cannot continuously deform it. Hence the subharmonic peak is *locked*, not fine-tuned — the defining property of a phase.

> **Theorem (Absolute stability, von Keyserlingk–Khemani–Sondhi 2016):** Spatiotemporal long-range order in a Floquet-MBL system is stable against arbitrary weak period-preserving perturbations; the discrete time crystal is a genuine phase of matter with a sharp boundary to the trivial Floquet paramagnet. [8]

### 5.3 The dynamical phase transition

Yao et al. [9] mapped the transition at which the crystal melts as the pulse imperfection ε grows: the subharmonic peak height serves as an order parameter, vanishing at a critical ε_c set by the interaction strength. The trapped-ion experiment [10] located this boundary, finding ε_c consistent with the interaction scale — quantitative agreement between theory and observation.

## 6. Limitations

The field's foundations, while impressive, rest on several contested or incomplete pillars.

**The fate of MBL in higher dimensions.** The Floquet-MBL time crystal inherits the controversies of MBL itself. While MBL is well established in one dimension, its stability in two and three dimensions is debated: rare thermal regions may seed "avalanches" that thermalize the system. The NV-center experiment [11] is three-dimensional, so it cannot be a true MBL phase — its order must be prethermal or otherwise metastable. Whether a *thermodynamically stable* Floquet-MBL time crystal exists beyond one dimension remains open.

**Heating and the thermodynamic limit.** A periodically driven interacting system generically absorbs energy from the drive. MBL prevents this, but only if MBL itself is stable; prethermal crystals heat after a time exponential in the drive frequency. All experiments to date observe tens to hundreds of cycles — far from the infinite-time limit in which phases are strictly defined.

**Decoherence versus thermalization.** In open experimental platforms, the observed decay of the subharmonic signal conflates intrinsic thermalization with coupling to the environment. The Sycamore experiment's time-reversal protocol [12] was designed to disentangle the two, but most earlier experiments could not.

**The "trivial" objection.** Critics note that period doubling is ubiquitous — from parametrically driven pendulums to synchronized fireflies — and argue that some observations may reflect synchronized few-body dynamics rather than many-body eigenstate order. The community's answer is the rigidity criterion: only the locked, initial-state-independent, perturbation-resistant response qualifies.

**No-go loopholes and continuous time crystals.** Dissipative and open-system realizations evade the equilibrium no-go theorems by construction, but they describe steady states of driven-dissipative dynamics rather than eigenstate order. Whether these deserve the name "time crystal" or are better classified as synchronized limit cycles remains a live debate.

## 7. Conclusion

The discrete time crystal has traveled from an impossible dream — Wilczek's equilibrium time crystal, ruled out by the no-go theorems [3][4] — to an experimentally established nonequilibrium phase of matter. The pillars are the Floquet-MBL π spin glass of Khemani et al. [6] and Else, Bauer, and Nayak [7], with eigenstate pairing and absolute stability [8]; the prethermal route without disorder [13][14]; and experiments spanning trapped ions [10], diamond spins [11], and superconducting processors [12].

The open questions are as deep as the achievements. Is Floquet-MBL stable in higher dimensions, or is the true time crystal intrinsically one-dimensional? Can heating be postponed indefinitely, or does every driven system eventually thermalize? And can the rigidity of the subharmonic response be turned into technology, from quantum memories to sensors? Whatever the answers, the discrete time crystal has already secured its place: the first fundamentally new phase of matter discovered in the nonequilibrium regime, and the template for the classification of driven quantum matter yet to come.
