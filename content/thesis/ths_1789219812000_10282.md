# DAG-Based Byzantine Fault-Tolerant Consensus: Decoupling Data Dissemination from Ordering in Narwhal, Tusk, Bullshark, and Mysticeti

## Abstract

Classical Byzantine fault-tolerant (BFT) consensus protocols — from PBFT to HotStuff — organize computation around a single leader's proposals, coupling data dissemination with total ordering and throttling throughput to the bandwidth of one replica. DAG-based consensus rejects this coupling. Replicas continuously and concurrently inject vertices into a structured directed acyclic graph (DAG), disseminating payloads at aggregate network speed, while ordering is obtained by interpreting the graph: anchors elected by a shared coin are committed together with their causal histories, with no additional communication beyond DAG construction. This post traces the intellectual arc of the DAG-BFT paradigm from the theoretical foundations of **DAG-Rider** [3] through **Narwhal and Tusk** [1], the partially synchronous and practically optimized **Bullshark** [2], the pipelining and leader-reputation framework of **Shoal** [5] and its high-throughput successor **Shoal++** [6], to the latency-optimal uncertified DAGs of **Mysticeti** [4]. We formalize the commit rules, prove why retrospective ordering preserves safety and liveness, quantify the latency–throughput frontier with published benchmarks, and delineate the remaining limitations: certification overhead, wave-length latency, fairness under censorship, and the certification-versus-robustness trade-off that continues to define the field.

## 1 Introduction

The state-machine replication problem asks a set of *n* replicas, up to *f* of which may be Byzantine, to agree on an ever-growing totally ordered log of client commands. For two decades the field's dominant designs were *leader-based*: a designated leader proposes a block, replicas vote, and a quorum certificate advances a view. PBFT, Tendermint, and the HotStuff family all share this spine, differing chiefly in view-change mechanics and communication complexity.

Leader-based designs suffer a structural bottleneck. The leader must receive, validate, and broadcast every transaction it proposes; system throughput is capped by one replica's bandwidth. Worse, progress hinges on the leader's correctness — a crashed or equivocating leader triggers timeouts, view changes, and the machinery of leader replacement that dominates protocol complexity.

The DAG paradigm, initiated in its modern form by DAG-Rider [3] and industrialized by Narwhal and Tusk [1], restructures the problem along two axes:

1. **Dissemination is democratic.** Every replica proposes vertices in every round in parallel; throughput scales with the *aggregate* bandwidth of the system, and no single replica is a choke point.
2. **Ordering is retrospective.** Once the DAG exists, replicas agree on an order by locally interpreting the graph — electing *anchor* vertices via a shared random coin and committing their causal histories. In the asynchronous variants, consensus adds *zero* extra communication beyond building the DAG [3].

This separation yields striking numbers: Bullshark reports ~125,000 transactions per second at ~2s latency across 50 geo-distributed parties [2]; Mysticeti reaches the theoretical latency lower bound of 3 message rounds while sustaining over 100k TPS [4]; Shoal++ adds a fast direct commit rule, parallel DAGs, and near-optimal latency while preserving throughput scalability [6].

> **Definition: (Byzantine Atomic Broadcast).** A protocol solves Byzantine Atomic Broadcast (BAB) if it guarantees **Agreement** (correct replicas deliver the same sequence), **Validity** (a value broadcast by a correct replica is eventually delivered), **Total order** (delivery order is identical across correct replicas), and **Termination/Liveness** (every value broadcast by a correct replica is eventually delivered by all correct replicas). DAG-based protocols target BAB with *f < n/3*, optimal resilience for the partially synchronous and asynchronous models.

This post is organized as follows. Section 2 situates DAG-BFT in the consensus taxonomy and fixes the network and fault models. Section 3 formalizes the Narwhal dissemination layer and the methodology of wave-based anchor election. Section 4 deep-dives into Tusk's commit rule, Bullshark's partially synchronous optimization, Shoal's pipelining and leader reputation, and Mysticeti's uncertified DAG. Section 5 presents published empirical results and a safety/liveness proof sketch. Section 6 catalogs limitations, and Section 7 concludes.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The leader bottleneck and why it matters

In chained-BFT protocols such as HotStuff, a block carries a quorum certificate (QC) for its parent, so commits pipeline across views. Yet at any instant exactly one leader disseminates payload. Measurements of production deployments routinely show the leader's egress saturating long before the *aggregate* network is stressed; scaling therefore demands *all-to-all* dissemination, not faster single-leader broadcast. Shoal's authors quantify this gap directly: Bullshark on Narwhal orders over 100k TPS, but its DAG construction adds a latency price relative to state-of-the-art leader-based protocols [5].

### 2.2 Network and fault models

DAG-BFT protocols span the classical triad:

| Model | Assumption | Representative DAG protocols |
|---|---|---|
| **Asynchronous** | Messages eventually delivered, no timing bound | DAG-Rider [3], Tusk [1] |
| **Partially synchronous** | Unknown GST; synchrony holds eventually | Bullshark [2], Shoal [5], Shoal++ [6], Mysticeti-C [4] |
| **Synchronous** | Known delay bound Δ | Fast paths / optimistic fast commit rules [4] |

All tolerate *f < n/3* Byzantine faults — the optimal resilience bound for partial synchrony and asynchrony. DAG-Rider is additionally post-quantum safe when instantiated with a threshold-coin that avoids asymmetric assumptions [3].

### 2.3 Quorum certificates and the Narwhal mempool

Narwhal [1] splits each validator into a **primary** and several **workers**. Workers batch transactions and disseminate them; the primary builds the DAG over *headers* (small metadata: worker batch digests, parent links, signatures) and runs reliable broadcast. A header becomes a certified *vertex* once it collects 2f+1 signatures — a **certificate of availability** — guaranteeing the payload is retrievable from at least f+1 honest workers. This certification is the linchpin of all later reasoning: because vertices are certified, any two correct replicas' DAGs eventually contain the same vertices, and *causal histories* are consistent.

> **Definition: (Round-based DAG).** The DAG is partitioned into rounds. Every validator broadcasts at most one vertex per round; a vertex in round *r* references at least 2f+1 vertices from round *r−1*. The *causal history* of a vertex *v* is the transitive closure of its references. A fundamental invariant: if a vertex *v* appears in two correct replicas' local DAGs, their causal histories of *v* are identical.

### 2.4 Waves, anchors, and the shared coin

Ordering protocols group rounds into **waves**. Each wave deterministically or randomly elects a **leader/anchor** vertex; replicas check whether the anchor gathered enough *support* (references) in a subsequent round, commit it if so (ordering its entire causal history by deterministic DFS), and otherwise skip it. Election uses a shared random coin so that Byzantine replicas cannot predict or manipulate anchor selection — fairness follows because every correct replica's vertices are eventually included in some committed causal history [3].

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 The dissemination layer: building the certified DAG

The Narwhal primary executes reliable broadcast per round:

```go
// Simplified primary loop (inspired by Narwhal [1])
for r := 1; ; r++ {
    parents := collect(2*f+1, dag[round(r-1)])   // quorum of prior vertices
    batchDigests := workers.fetchDigests()        // payload availability
    v := Vertex{Round: r, Author: self, Parents: parents, Batches: batchDigests}
    sigs := broadcastAndCollect(v)                 // reliable broadcast
    cert := Certificate{v, sigs}                   // 2f+1 signatures
    dag.insert(cert)                               // certified vertex
}
```

Certification converts the DAG into a *commons*: every correct replica eventually holds the same set of certified vertices, so local interpretation of the graph — rather than further message exchange — suffices for ordering. The Shoal++ correctness proof rests explicitly on this property: `CAUSAL HISTORY(n)` returns the same vector to all replicas *because* all DAG nodes are certified [6].

### 3.2 The ordering layer: anchor election and commit rules

Given the DAG, a wave-based protocol proceeds:

```python
# Wave-based anchor commitment (Tusk/Bullshark style [1,2])
def process_wave(w):
    anchor = shared_coin.elect(w)          # deterministic given committed state
    support = {v for v in dag[round(w,1)] if anchor in v.parents}
    if len(support) >= f + 1:              # Tusk direct commit rule
        order_causal_history(anchor)       # DFS, deterministic tie-break
        garbage_collect(below(w))
    # else: skip anchor; its history may still commit via a later anchor
```

The commit rule's art lies in its *support threshold* and *wave length*: weaker thresholds and shorter waves commit faster but risk divergence under asynchrony; the protocols below navigate this trade-off differently.

### 3.3 What "zero-overhead" means

DAG-Rider's headline claim — ordering with *no extra communication* beyond DAG construction [3] — follows from determinism: the commit rule is a pure function of the local DAG, and because certified vertices are eventually delivered to all correct replicas, every correct replica reaches the same commit decisions. Bullshark inherits this property in its partially synchronous form, which is why its implementation is only ~200 lines atop an existing Narwhal deployment [2].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Tusk: the asynchronous baseline

Tusk [1] layers an asynchronous consensus protocol over Narwhal. It organizes the DAG into waves of **3 rounds** (propose, vote, elect) and simplifies DAG-Rider's commit rule: rather than evaluating support across the whole wave, Tusk checks support *in the round immediately following* the anchor. A leader block commits once it receives **f+1 supports** in the next round — a threshold reachable even when f replicas stay silent. Safety holds because any two quorums of size 2f+1 intersect in at least f+1 replicas, so if one correct replica commits an anchor, every correct replica eventually observes sufficient support for the same anchor; liveness follows from the shared coin's unpredictability and the guarantee that every correct proposal eventually lands in a committed causal history [1].

The cost of asynchrony is latency: waves are long, and randomized election can skip anchors, delaying commits by whole waves. Bullshark attacks exactly this cost.

### 4.2 Bullshark: making DAG-BFT practical

Bullshark [2] keeps the Narwhal DAG but targets the *common synchronous case*: it is an asynchronous BAB protocol optimized for periods of synchrony, plus a standalone partially synchronous variant evaluated against the state of the art. Two changes matter:

1. **Steady-state fast path.** When the network behaves synchronously, anchors commit in as few as **2 rounds** — the protocol exploits timely delivery to shrink wave length rather than waiting for worst-case asynchronous progress.
2. **No view changes.** The partially synchronous variant eliminates the "notoriously complex view-change mechanisms" of leader-based BFT: anchor election proceeds through the DAG itself, so a faulty anchor costs at most one skipped wave, never a protocol reconfiguration [2].

> **Theorem: (Bullshark safety sketch [2]).** If a correct replica commits anchor *a* in wave *w*, then every correct replica eventually commits *a*. *Proof sketch.* Committing *a* requires 2f+1 round-*r* vertices referencing *a*. Any other correct replica's round-*r* quorum of 2f+1 vertices intersects this set in ≥ f+1 vertices; at least one is from a correct replica, whose causal history contains *a*. Deterministic ordering of causal histories then yields identical sequences. ∎

Empirically, the partially synchronous Bullshark achieves **125,000 TPS at ~2s latency with 50 parties** — while the asynchronous state of the art pays a steep ~50% latency premium in the same setting [2].

### 4.3 Shoal and Shoal++: pipelining, reputation, prevalent responsiveness

Vanilla Bullshark suffers a structural latency tax: anchors exist only every few rounds, so a transaction arriving just after an anchor waits nearly a full wave before ordering can even begin. **Shoal** [5] is a protocol-agnostic framework adding three mechanisms atop any Narwhal-based consensus:

- **Pipelining:** every round carries an anchor, so commit opportunities are continuous rather than wave-periodic.
- **Leader reputation:** anchors are drawn from a reputation-weighted set that excludes slow, isolated, or crashed validators, based on the DAG-observed recent behavior of each replica.
- **Prevalent responsiveness:** the combination lets Shoal eliminate timeouts in all but extremely rare cases — strictly stronger than HotStuff's *optimistic responsiveness*, which only removes timeout effects in failure-free synchronous runs [5].

Shoal reports up to **40% latency reduction** failure-free and up to **80%** with failures versus vanilla Bullshark [5]. **Shoal++** [6] pushes further with a *fast direct commit rule* (commit an anchor upon observing 2f+1 uncertified proposals referencing it, without waiting for certification), *multiple anchors per round*, and *parallel DAGs* that stream data like a pipeline without the queuing latency of Narwhal's worker design. Crucially, the fast rule is proved safe by reduction: `FAST_COMMIT(a)` can only return *a*, and 2f+1 referencing proposals imply f+1 correct broadcasts, so the Bullshark logic commits the same anchor everywhere (Lemma 6.1 in [6]).

### 4.4 Mysticeti: the uncertified DAG and the latency floor

Certification is expensive: every vertex requires a round trip of signature collection before it can be referenced. **Mysticeti-C** [4] asks whether certification can be dropped entirely. Validators broadcast blocks through a single message — *no propose/acknowledge/certify phases* — and order emerges from a commit rule synthesized from DAG-Rider's wave-final evaluation and Tusk's next-round check: waves of **3 rounds** with leaders in round 1, committed via a two-stage rule that every block can satisfy without delay. The result is the **first DAG-based protocol to hit the 3-message-round latency lower bound**, achieving **0.5s WAN commit latency at over 100k TPS** [4], and it now powers the Sui blockchain with a reported 4× latency reduction in production.

Dropping certification reintroduces *equivocation*: a Byzantine validator may broadcast conflicting blocks in the same round. Mysticeti's commit rule compensates by requiring stronger, carefully structured support — safety without certificates, at the cost of more intricate reasoning. **Mysticeti-FPC** extends the design with a fast commit path for asset transfers woven directly into the DAG, matching the latency of consensus-free protocols like Zef and FastPay while batching for higher throughput [4].

| Protocol | DAG type | Wave / commit latency | Key innovation |
|---|---|---|---|
| DAG-Rider [3] | Certified, async | 4-round waves, randomized | Zero-overhead ordering; post-quantum safe |
| Tusk [1] | Certified (Narwhal), async | 3-round waves, f+1 next-round support | Simplified commit rule over Narwhal mempool |
| Bullshark [2] | Certified, async + partial-sync variant | 2-round steady state | Optimized for common synchronous case; no view change |
| Shoal [5] | Certified + reputation | Pipelined anchors | Leader reputation; prevalent responsiveness |
| Shoal++ [6] | Certified, parallel DAGs | Fast direct commit | Uncertified-vote fast path; streaming throughput |
| Mysticeti-C [4] | **Uncertified** | **3 message rounds (optimal)** | Threshold clocks; every block committable |

### 4.5 The consensus-free frontier

Not every workload needs total order. Payments and asset transfers are *commutative* for distinct owners, so protocols like FastPay dispense with consensus for the common case, using Byzantine consistent broadcast plus per-account sequence numbers. Mysticeti-FPC [4] internalizes this insight: the fast path is not a separate protocol but a commit mode *within* the DAG, so fast-path and consensus-path transactions share dissemination infrastructure. This convergence — DAG as universal substrate, consensus as one commit rule among several — is arguably the paradigm's deepest lesson.

## 5 Empirical Results / Proofs

### 5.1 Published benchmark summary

| System | Committee | Throughput | Commit latency | Setting |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Narwhal+Tusk [1] | 4–50 validators, WAN | ~46k TPS (4 nodes, 512B tx) | ~464 ms consensus | Asynchronous |
| Bullshark (partial-sync) [2] | 50 parties, WAN | 125,000 TPS | ~2 s | 50-party WAN deployment |
| Shoal+Bullshark [5] | WAN | >100k TPS | −40% vs Bullshark (no failures); −80% (with failures) | Partial synchrony |
| Mysticeti-C [4] | WAN | >100k TPS (v3: >50k) | **0.5 s** (first sub-second WAN BFT commit) | Partial synchrony |
| Shoal++ vs Mysticeti [6] | 100 replicas, 1% message drops | 18k TPS moderate load | Shoal++ ≤1.3× baseline; Mysticeti ~10× spike | Fault injection |

The Shoal++ fault-injection study [6] is the most instructive head-to-head: under crash failures of 33 of 100 replicas, Shoal/Shoal++ swiftly re-elect live anchors via reputation while Bullshark and Mysticeti stall waiting for a timely anchor; under 1% sporadic message drops, Mysticeti's uncertified DAG suffers ~10× latency spikes from critical-path synchronization on missing data, whereas Shoal++'s certified DAG keeps synchronization asynchronous and off the critical path — the clearest empirical statement of the certification trade-off.

### 5.2 Proof sketch: why retrospective ordering is safe

We sketch the core argument shared by the certified-DAG family [1,2,5,6]:

> **Theorem: (Anchor agreement).** In a certified round-based DAG with n = 3f+1, if a correct replica commits anchor *a* of wave *w*, all correct replicas eventually commit *a* in the same position.
> **Proof.** Committing requires a quorum *Q* of 2f+1 vertices in the vote round referencing *a*. Any correct replica eventually obtains its own 2f+1 quorum *Q′* for that round (liveness of reliable broadcast). |Q ∩ Q′| ≥ f+1, so at least one vertex *v* ∈ Q ∩ Q′ is from a correct replica. Correct replicas never equivocate, so *v*'s reference to *a* is visible to all, and *a*'s causal history — identical at all correct replicas by certification — is ordered deterministically. Skipped anchors' histories are subsumed by the next committed anchor's causal history, preserving total order. ∎

Liveness follows from the shared coin: with probability bounded away from zero each wave elects an anchor from a correct replica whose support arrives in time, so commits occur infinitely often [3]. Fairness (every correct proposal eventually ordered) follows because causal histories are committed wholesale.

## 6 Limitations

1. **Certification overhead vs. robustness.** Certification adds a signature-collection round trip per vertex; uncertified DAGs (Mysticeti) remove it but pay with intricate commit rules and fragility under message loss — Shoal++'s experiments show ~10× latency spikes for uncertified designs under 1% drops [6]. There is no free lunch, only a choice of which failure mode to optimize.
2. **Wave-length latency floor.** Even 2-round steady-state commits (Bullshark) exceed the 3-message-round optimum only in the uncertified regime; certified DAGs cannot reach it because certification itself consumes rounds [4].
3. **Censorship and fairness gaps.** Fairness arguments assume anchors' causal histories eventually include all correct proposals, but a Byzantine majority of a round's vertices can delay specific replicas' vertices; leader reputation mitigates slow-anchor censorship [5] but targeted transaction censorship remains an active research area.
4. **Memory and garbage collection.** The DAG grows at aggregate network speed; all protocols require GC watermarks tied to committed waves. A replica that falls behind the GC horizon cannot rejoin without state transfer — checkpointing and epoch-change machinery (as in Mysticeti-FPC [4]) is essential but adds complexity the core papers under-specify.
5. **Evaluation realism.** Published numbers use homogeneous WAN testbeds with well-provisioned validators; adversarial network scheduling, heterogeneous hardware, and smart-contract execution costs (as opposed to raw dissemination) can all erode the reported frontier. The SoK systematization [7] catalogs how rarely DAG systems are compared under identical conditions.

## 7 Conclusion

DAG-based consensus reframes BFT ordering from *leader-driven proposal* to *democratic dissemination plus retrospective interpretation*. Narwhal [1] showed that separating the mempool from consensus unlocks aggregate-bandwidth throughput; Tusk and DAG-Rider [1,3] showed ordering needs no extra communication; Bullshark [2] showed the asynchronous design can be tuned for the synchronous common case without view changes; Shoal and Shoal++ [5,6] showed pipelining, reputation, and fast commit rules close most of the remaining latency gap; Mysticeti [4] showed that dropping certification reaches the latency floor — at a robustness price the field is still quantifying. The emerging synthesis treats the DAG as a universal substrate supporting multiple commit modes, including consensus-free fast paths. Open problems — censorship resistance under adaptive adversaries, principled GC and checkpointing, and honest heterogeneous evaluation — will determine whether the DAG paradigm fully displaces the leader-based designs it was built to surpass.
