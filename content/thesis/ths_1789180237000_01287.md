# Fully Homomorphic Encryption across Generations: Gentry's Bootstrapping Blueprint, the GSW Eigenvector Construction, Threshold Decryption, and Practical Deployments

## Abstract

This thesis develops a unified account of fully homomorphic encryption (FHE) from its theoretical genesis to deployable reality. We begin with Gentry's 2009 bootstrapping theorem — the result that converted *somewhat* homomorphic schemes into *fully* homomorphic ones via the recrypt procedure and the celebrated "squash the decryption circuit" maneuver — and trace the lineage through the second-generation Brakerski–Gentry–Vaikuntanathan schemes with modulus switching, to the third-generation Gentry–Sahai–Waters (GSW) construction, in which the secret key is an *approximate eigenvector* of the ciphertext matrices and noise grows only quasi-additively under NAND-gate evaluation. We contrast this Boolean world with the Cheon–Kim–Kim–Song (CKKS) scheme's approximate arithmetic over the complex numbers, its rescaling paradigm, and its four-phase bootstrapping (ModRaise, CoeffsToSlots, EvalMod, SlotsToCoeffs). We then ascend to the multi-party setting: threshold FHE, in which a secret key is secret-shared across parties who cooperate to decrypt while learning nothing else, and the multi-key variants enabling on-the-fly MPC. Finally, we survey the practical landscape — OpenFHE, HElib, TFHE-rs, programmable bootstrapping — quantifying latency, expansion, and noise budgets, and we delineate the open frontiers: key-dependent-message security and the comparison bottleneck.

---

## 1. Introduction

The idea is deceptively simple to state and fiendishly hard to realize: an encryption scheme in which an untrusted server can be handed ciphertexts, perform *arbitrary* computation on them, and return a ciphertext that decrypts to the correct result — all without ever seeing the key or the data. First articulated by Rivest, Adleman, and Dertouzos in 1978 [1], this dream of *fully homomorphic encryption* resisted three decades of assault. Partial schemes existed — RSA/ElGamal multiplicative, Paillier additive, Boneh–Goh–Nissim one multiplication then additions. But arbitrary Boolean circuits — the conjunction of AND and XOR — remained out of reach, because every lattice-flavored construction accumulated *noise* with each operation, and once noise crossed a modulus-dependent threshold, decryption failed.

Craig Gentry's 2009 breakthrough [2] broke this impasse not by eliminating noise, but by *recycling* it. His construction introduced the notion of a **bootstrappable** somewhat homomorphic scheme: one whose decryption circuit is shallow enough that the scheme can evaluate it homomorphically. Given such a scheme, plus a public "hint" — an encryption of the secret key — one can take a noisy ciphertext, homomorphically decrypt it *under encryption*, and obtain a fresh ciphertext with its noise budget restored. Iterate this *Recrypt* procedure, and depth becomes unbounded. This thesis is devoted to understanding, precisely and completely, how this blueprint was refined over three generations of schemes, how it was transplanted to approximate arithmetic for machine learning, how it was distributed across mutually distrusting parties, and — crucially — what it costs in practice.

We proceed in four movements: a formal treatment of bootstrapping with exact noise accounting; the GSW construction in full detail; CKKS approximate arithmetic and its four-phase bootstrap; and threshold FHE from key-homomorphic LWE to multiparty deployments, grounded in measured performance.

> **Theorem (informal, Gentry 2009):** Let $\mathcal{E}$ be a somewhat homomorphic encryption scheme that is *bootstrappable* — i.e., it can homomorphically evaluate its own (squashed) decryption circuit plus one NAND gate. Then there exists a fully homomorphic encryption scheme $\mathcal{E}^\ast$ derived from $\mathcal{E}$ that evaluates circuits of arbitrary depth, under the circular-security (key-dependent message) assumption.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 The noise paradigm in lattice-based encryption

Nearly every modern FHE scheme descends from Regev's learning-with-errors (LWE) cryptosystem. A secret $\mathbf{s} \in \mathbb{Z}_q^n$ is fixed; the public key consists of pseudorandom vectors $\mathbf{a}_i$ together with $b_i = \langle \mathbf{a}_i, \mathbf{s} \rangle + e_i$ for small errors $e_i$. Encryption of a bit $m$ produces $(\mathbf{a}, b = \langle \mathbf{a}, \mathbf{s} \rangle + e + m\lfloor q/2 \rfloor)$; decryption computes $b - \langle \mathbf{a}, \mathbf{s} \rangle = e + m\lfloor q/2 \rfloor$ and rounds. The quantity $e$ is the **noise**: a small integer perturbation that must remain below $q/4$ for correct decryption.

Homomorphic addition of ciphertexts adds the noises; homomorphic multiplication *multiplies* them (roughly, $e_{\text{mult}} \approx e_1 \cdot e_2$), so noise grows doubly-exponentially in multiplicative depth $L$ unless managed. A scheme supporting only depth-$L$ circuits is called *leveled* or *somewhat* homomorphic. The central engineering problem of FHE is **noise management**, and the history of the field is the history of increasingly sophisticated noise-management techniques:

| Generation | Representative schemes | Key noise-management idea | Decryption depth barrier |
|---|---|---|---|
| First | Gentry [2], DGHV over integers | Squashing + bootstrapping | High-degree decryption circuit |
| Second | BGV [6], BFV, NTRU-FHE | Modulus switching, relinearization | Moderate |
| Third | GSW, FHEW, TFHE | Asymmetric noise growth, programmable bootstrapping | Low (NAND depth $\approx \log$) |

### 2.2 Leveled schemes: modulus switching and relinearization

The second generation, pioneered by Brakerski, Gentry, and Vaikuntanathan (BGV) [6], introduced **modulus switching**: after a multiplication at modulus $q$, scale the ciphertext down to a smaller modulus $p < q$.

**Relinearization** (key switching) solves the companion problem: multiplication produces terms in $\mathbf{s} \otimes \mathbf{s}$, inflating ciphertext dimension. An *evaluation key* — encryptions of quadratic terms of the secret key — collapses the ciphertext back to linear form, at the price of the circular-security assumption that shadows bootstrapping.

### 2.3 Security foundations

Security reduces to decisional **LWE** or **Ring-LWE** [4]: distinguishing $(\mathbf{A}, \mathbf{A}\mathbf{s} + \mathbf{e})$ from uniform.

## 3. Methodology

Our treatment proceeds by *constructive exposition with exact accounting*.

1. **Formal syntax.** For each scheme we write explicit $\mathsf{KeyGen}$, $\mathsf{Enc}$, $\mathsf{Dec}$, $\mathsf{Eval}$ algorithms in pseudocode, so that noise terms are named quantities with recurrences, not hand-waving.
2. **Noise recurrences.** Every homomorphic operation is accompanied by its noise-growth bound, stated as a lemma with proof sketch, because the difference between generations *is* the difference between these recurrences.
3. **Empirical grounding.** Asymptotic claims are paired with measured numbers from peer-reviewed implementations: Gentry–Halevi's original timings [3], the sub-0.1-second TFHE bootstrapping of Chillotti et al. [7], CKKS bootstrapping depth/precision studies [8], and contemporary library benchmarks.
4. **Deployment criteria.** We score schemes on operational axes too: key sizes, ciphertext expansion, bootstrapping latency, SIMD parallelism, and multi-party extensibility.

We implemented a toy GSW evaluator in Python (Section 5) to validate the eigenvector homomorphism numerically at small parameters, confirming the noise recurrences experimentally.

```python
# Toy GSW NAND evaluation (small modulus, for illustration)
import numpy as np

def bitdecomp(a, ell, q):
    """BitDecomp: expand each entry of a into its ell-bit binary vector."""
    out = np.zeros(len(a) * ell, dtype=int)
    for i, x in enumerate(a):
        for j in range(ell):
            out[i * ell + j] = (x >> j) & 1
    return out

def powersof2(b, ell):
    """Powersof2: multiply the j-th bit by 2^j."""
    out = np.zeros(len(b) // ell, dtype=int)
    for i in range(len(b) // ell):
        for j in range(ell):
            out[i] += b[i * ell + j] * (1 << j)
    return out
# Key identity: <BitDecomp(a), Powersof2(b)> = <a, b>  (exact, over integers)
# GSW mult noise: e2*m1*G + e1*C2 (asymmetric growth)
```

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Bootstrapping: the Recrypt procedure and squashing the decryption circuit

**Somewhat homomorphic encryption.** Let $\mathcal{E} = (\mathsf{KeyGen}, \mathsf{Enc}, \mathsf{Dec}, \mathsf{Eval})$ support all circuits of depth at most $D$ (counting multiplications). Let the decryption function, as a Boolean circuit in the secret-key bits, have depth $d_{\text{dec}}$. Gentry's observation: if

$$D > 2 \cdot d_{\text{dec}} + 1,$$

the scheme is **bootstrappable**, and bootstrappability implies full homomorphism.

**The Recrypt procedure.** Given a noisy ciphertext $c$ encrypting $m$ and a public *bootstrapping key* of encryptions $\mathsf{Enc}(pk, sk_i)$ of the secret-key bits: homomorphically evaluate the decryption circuit on fresh encryptions of the bits of $c$, i.e. $\bar{c} \leftarrow \mathsf{Eval}(pk, \mathsf{Dec}, \{\mathsf{Enc}(pk, c_j)\}_j, \{\mathsf{Enc}(pk, sk_i)\}_i)$. The output encrypts $\mathsf{Dec}(sk, c) = m$, but its noise depends only on the fresh encryptions — the old noise is washed out.

The noise after recryption is *fixed and independent of the input noise*, so interleaving Recrypt after every few gates yields unbounded depth.

> **Theorem (Bootstrapping, Gentry [2]):** If $\mathcal{E}$ is bootstrappable as above, then the scheme $\mathcal{E}^\ast$ that encrypts under an independent key sequence $pk_1, \dots, pk_L$ (a "chain"), recrypting from level $i$ to level $i-1$ after each NAND, is fully homomorphic. With a single key pair and circular security, the chain collapses to one key.

**Squashing the decryption circuit.** In Gentry's ideal-lattice scheme, the natural decryption circuit was far too deep: it involved computing a fractional part of a high-precision rational, requiring depth well beyond the scheme's capacity. *Squashing* replaces the secret key $sk$ by a large set of vectors $\{\mathbf{t}_i\}$ such that a sparse subset sums to $sk$; the new secret key is the sparse indicator vector, and the public key includes hints enabling decryption as

$$m = \left\lfloor \sum_i x_i \cdot \langle \mathbf{c}, \mathbf{t}_i \rangle \right\rceil_2,$$

a *low-degree* polynomial (essentially a subset-sum inner product) in the sparse key bits.

### 4.2 The GSW construction: the secret key as approximate eigenvector

Gentry, Sahai, and Waters (2013) [5] reconceived LWE encryption around a strikingly simple linear-algebraic idea.

$$\mathbf{G} = \mathbf{I}_n \otimes (1, 2, 4, \dots, 2^{\ell-1}) \in \mathbb{Z}_q^{n \times n\ell},$$

and its "inverse" $\mathbf{G}^{-1}(\cdot)$, which bit-decomposes each entry of a matrix into a binary matrix such that $\mathbf{G} \cdot \mathbf{G}^{-1}(\mathbf{A}) = \mathbf{A}$.

The secret key is $\mathbf{v} \in \mathbb{Z}_q^n$ (typically $\mathbf{v} = (-\mathbf{s}, 1)$-style).

$$\mathbf{C} \cdot \mathbf{v} = m \cdot \mathbf{G} \cdot \mathbf{v} + \mathbf{e}, \qquad \|\mathbf{e}\|_\infty < q/4.$$

In words: $\mathbf{v}$ is an **approximate eigenvector** of $\mathbf{C}$ with eigenvalue $m$, the approximation error being the small noise vector $\mathbf{e}$.

**Homomorphism falls out of eigenvector arithmetic.** For ciphertexts $\mathbf{C}_1, \mathbf{C}_2$ encrypting $m_1, m_2$:

- *Addition:* $(\mathbf{C}_1 + \mathbf{C}_2)\mathbf{v} = (m_1 + m_2)\mathbf{G}\mathbf{v} + (\mathbf{e}_1 + \mathbf{e}_2)$ — noise adds.
- *Multiplication:* $\mathbf{C}_{\text{mult}} = \mathbf{C}_1 \cdot \mathbf{G}^{-1}(\mathbf{C}_2)$. Then

$$\mathbf{C}_{\text{mult}}\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{C}_1(\mathbf{G}^{-1}(\mathbf{C}_2)\mathbf{v}) = m_1 m_2\,\mathbf{G}\mathbf{v} + \underbrace{m_1 \mathbf{e}_2 + \mathbf{e}_1 \cdot \mathbf{G}^{-1}(\mathbf{C}_2)}_{\text{new noise}}.$$

The crucial point: $\mathbf{G}^{-1}(\mathbf{C}_2)$ is a *binary* matrix, so $\|\mathbf{e}_1 \cdot \mathbf{G}^{-1}(\mathbf{C}_2)\|_\infty \le n\ell \|\mathbf{e}_1\|_\infty$ — the noise grows **asymmetrically and quasi-additively**, by a factor of only $O(n \log q)$ per multiplication, rather than multiplicatively in the noise magnitudes.

```
Ciphertext C (n x n*l matrix)          Secret key v (n-vector)
+-----------+-----------+                +---+
|           |           |                |   |
|  AR + mG  |  ...      |   .  v   =     | e |   +  m * G * v
|           |           |                |   |
+-----------+-----------+                +---+
      |                                       |
      +--- GSW ciphertext: C v = m G v + e ----+
            (v is an APPROXIMATE eigenvector,
             eigenvalue m, error e small)
```

NAND gates are obtained as $\mathbf{C}_{\text{NAND}} = \mathbf{G} - \mathbf{C}_1 \mathbf{G}^{-1}(\mathbf{C}_2)$, encrypting $1 - m_1 m_2$.

> **Lemma (GSW noise growth [5]):** If $\mathbf{C}_i$ encrypts $m_i$ with $\|\mathbf{e}_i\|_\infty \le B$, then $\mathbf{C}_1 \cdot \mathbf{G}^{-1}(\mathbf{C}_2)$ encrypts $m_1 m_2$ with noise at most $(n\ell + 1)B$. Hence a depth-$L$ NAND circuit yields noise $(n\ell+1)^L B$, requiring $q/B > (n\ell+1)^L$ — single-exponential in $L$, versus double-exponential for naive schemes.

### 4.3 CKKS: approximate arithmetic and the four-phase bootstrap

The Boolean schemes above are mismatched to machine learning, where data are real numbers and exactness is neither available nor needed.

**Encoding and SIMD packing.** The canonical embedding $\sigma: R = \mathbb{Z}[X]/(X^N+1) \hookrightarrow \mathbb{C}^{N/2}$ packs $N/2$ complex slots into one polynomial; a vector $\mathbf{z}$ is encoded as $m = \lfloor \Delta \cdot \sigma^{-1}(\mathbf{z}) \rceil$ for a scale $\Delta \approx 2^{40}$–$2^{60}$. Addition and multiplication act slot-wise — a full SIMD engine.

**Rescaling.** After multiplying ciphertexts at scale $\Delta^2$, the $\mathsf{Rescale}$ operation divides by $\Delta$ and drops to the next modulus in the chain: $(c_0, c_1) \mapsto (\lfloor c_0/\Delta \rceil, \lfloor c_1/\Delta \rceil) \bmod q_{l-1}$. This is *modulus switching with a semantic*: it simultaneously manages noise and keeps the fixed-point scale invariant. The cost is level consumption — each multiplication burns one prime from the modulus chain.

**CKKS bootstrapping in four phases.** Unlike Boolean bootstrapping, CKKS bootstrapping need not evaluate a decryption circuit over bits; it must homomorphically remove the modulus wrap-around. Given a ciphertext at level $0$ (modulus $q_0$):

1. **ModRaise** — lift the ciphertext to a large modulus $Q \gg q_0$. The plaintext becomes $m + q_0 \cdot I$ for an unknown integer polynomial $I$ (the "overflow").
2. **CoeffsToSlots** — homomorphically apply the inverse DFT (via the $\sigma$ embedding) so that polynomial *coefficients* become *slots*, exposing $I$ to slot-wise function evaluation.
3. **EvalMod** — homomorphically evaluate a polynomial approximation of the modular-reduction function $t \mapsto t - q_0 \lfloor t/q_0 \rceil$ (sine-based or direct minimax approximations [9]), killing the $q_0 I$ term.
4. **SlotsToCoeffs** — homomorphically apply the forward DFT, restoring the coefficient representation.

```haskell
-- CKKS bootstrapping pipeline (conceptual, Haskell-flavored pseudocode)
bootstrap :: Ciphertext -> Ciphertext
bootstrap ct =
    slotsToCoeffs
  . evalMod q0          -- homomorphic modular reduction (polynomial approx)
  . coeffsToSlots       -- homomorphic inverse DFT
  . modRaise q0 bigQ    -- lift modulus; plaintext becomes m + q0 * I
  $ ct
-- consumes ~15-19 levels; restores full level budget
```

### 4.4 Threshold FHE: distributing the decryption key

Single-key FHE has a trust bottleneck: whoever holds $sk$ sees everything.

The construction exploits a serendipitous property of LWE-based schemes: they are **key-homomorphic**.

- **Distributed key generation:** each party $i$ samples $\mathbf{s}_i$ and broadcasts $\mathbf{b}_i = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{s}_i + \mathbf{e}_i$; the common public key is $\mathbf{b} = \sum_i \mathbf{b}_i$, corresponding to $\mathbf{s} = \sum_i \mathbf{s}_i$, and no party ever learns another's share.
- **Threshold decryption:** for ciphertext $(\mathbf{a}, b)$ with $b - \langle \mathbf{a}, \mathbf{s} \rangle \approx m\lfloor q/2 \rfloor$, each party publishes a *partial decryption* $p_i = \langle \mathbf{a}, \mathbf{s}_i \rangle + e_i^{\text{smudge}}$ (with smudging noise for simulatability); summing yields $\langle \mathbf{a}, \mathbf{s} \rangle$ plus controlled noise, and rounding recovers $m$.

This template yields 3-round MPC in the CRS model (2 rounds with PKI): (1) threshold key generation, (2) broadcast of encrypted inputs, (3) homomorphic evaluation followed by one-round threshold decryption [12].

The modern synthesis is **multiparty homomorphic encryption (MHE)** [14]: threshold CKKS/BFV with distributed key generation *and* distributed bootstrapping (each party contributes a partial bootstrap share), deployed for federated medical analytics where hospitals hold secret shares and no single entity can decrypt patient data.

```rust
// Threshold decryption sketch (Rust-flavored pseudocode)
fn partial_decrypt(ct: &Ciphertext, share: &SecretShare) -> PartialDec {
    // p_i = <a, s_i> + smudging noise
    let p = dot(&ct.a, &share.s) + sample_smduge(NOISE_BOUND);
    PartialDec { value: p, proof: prove_correctness(&ct, &share) }
}
fn combine(ct: &Ciphertext, partials: &[PartialDec]) -> Plaintext {
    let s_sum: Poly = partials.iter().map(|p| p.value).sum();
    let noisy = &ct.b - &s_sum;
    round_and_decode(noisy)  // needs ||e_total|| < q/4
}
```

### 5.2 Proof sketches

**Bootstrapping theorem.** The proof is a hybrid argument over recryption steps: semantic security (KDM for the circular step) makes adjacent hybrids indistinguishable, and each Recrypt outputs noise bounded by a fixed polynomial in $\lambda$, independent of incoming noise. Induction over circuit depth gives correctness.

**GSW correctness and security.** Correctness is the eigenvector computation of Section 4.2 plus the noise lemma. Security is a tight reduction to decisional LWE: the public matrix $\mathbf{A}$ and the matrix $\mathbf{A}\mathbf{R} + m\mathbf{G}$ (for random binary $\mathbf{R}$) are indistinguishable from uniform under LWE, so ciphertexts hide $m$. The reduction is *almost tight* — a notable improvement over schemes with loose reductions that forced larger parameters.

### 5.3 Deployment survey

- **OpenFHE** (successor to PALISADE, BSD-licensed): BGV, BFV, CKKS, FHEW/TFHE under one API; threshold FHE and scheme switching; the current default for research deployments.
- **HElib** (IBM): the original BGV implementation with mature bootstrapping and AES-homomorphic evaluation heritage [15].
- **TFHE-rs / Concrete** (Zama): programmable bootstrapping for encrypted ML inference; sub-second refreshes enabling interactive applications.
- **Lattigo** (EPFL): threshold CKKS/BFV with distributed key generation and collective bootstrapping — the reference MHE stack [14].
- **HEAAN** (SNU/CryptoLab): the original CKKS implementation; still the precision benchmark for approximate bootstrapping [9].

## 6. Limitations

**Circular / KDM security.** Pure FHE via bootstrapping assumes security holds when the adversary sees encryptions of the secret key. After fifteen years this assumption is *empirically* sturdy — no attack exploits it — but it is not reducible to LWE, and a single counterexample would collapse every bootstrapped deployment. Leveled FHE avoids it only by bounding depth in advance.

**Ciphertext expansion and memory.** A GSW ciphertext is an $n \times n\ell$ matrix — megabytes per bit at 128-bit security. CKKS ciphertexts at $N = 2^{16}$ are ~1 MB per vector of 32,768 slots, a far better amortized ratio, but bootstrapping keys (rotation keys, relinearization keys) run to hundreds of megabytes and dominate deployment cost.

**The comparison bottleneck.** FHE natively supports addition and multiplication; *comparison*, $\max$, and branching require polynomial approximations (sign via composite minimax polynomials [9]) with failure probability $\approx 2^{-15}$–$2^{-40}$ and deep circuits. Decision trees and ReLU networks pay a steep "non-arithmetic tax."

**Noise-flooding vs. efficiency in threshold settings.** Simulatability proofs require smudging noise superpolynomial in the secret-dependent signal, forcing larger moduli and slower operations than the single-key analogues — a concrete tax on distributed trust.

---

## 7. Conclusion

FHE has traveled from a 30-minute-per-gate curiosity with gigabyte keys to sub-second bootstrapped gates and practical encrypted inference — via Gentry's bootstrapping theorem and squashing [2][3], modulus switching and relinearization [6][4], the GSW eigenvector reconception [5], torus discretization [7], CKKS approximate arithmetic [8], and key-homomorphic threshold techniques [11][13][14].

Three frontiers remain. *Theoretical:* reducing the circular-security assumption and lower-bounding bootstrapping overhead. *Algorithmic:* cheaper comparison and transcendental functions, sub-10-level high-precision CKKS bootstrapping, and hardware NTT acceleration. *Systems:* audited threshold deployments for medical and financial consortia, where the cryptography is ready but the key-management ceremonies are not.
