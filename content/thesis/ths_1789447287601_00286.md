# Algebraic Effects and Handlers: Effect Signatures, Delimited Continuations, and Row-Polymorphic Effect Systems

## Abstract

Algebraic effects and handlers recast computational effects as operations of an algebraic theory together with programmer-defined interpretations of that theory. We develop the framework from Plotkin and Power's algebraic operations through Plotkin and Pretnar's handlers to modern language implementations in Koka, Eff, Frank, and OCaml 5. Effect signatures are presented as sets of operation symbols with parameter and result types, handlers as folds over the free model induced by the theory, and resumption as the action of delimited continuations captured at handler boundaries. We compare row polymorphism for effect inference, the one-shot versus multi-shot continuation divide in real runtimes, and the metatheory of type soundness, effect safety, and selective CPS compilation. Quantitative evidence from the OCaml retrofitting effort and the Koka compiler grounds the theoretical claims in measured implementation reality.

##1 Introduction

Every realistic program performs *effects*: reading and writing state, raising exceptions, choosing nondeterministically, performing input and output, forking lightweight threads. Programming languages have proposed a long succession of disciplines for taming these effects — monads, monad transformers, continuations, region systems, and effect systems. Monads give a uniform mathematical account but compose poorly: stacking state, exception, and nondeterminism transformers demands an order that changes the semantics, and lifting operations through the stack is manual boilerplate.

**Algebraic effects** invert this commitment. An effect is declared as a *signature* of operations, used freely in a computation without any interpretation attached, and interpreted later by a *handler* that the programmer supplies [1]. The same stateful computation can be run under a handler that threads real memory, under a handler that logs every access, or under a handler that rolls back on failure — the computation itself is unchanged. This separation of *interface* from *interpretation* is the single most important idea in the modern theory of effects.

The mathematical lineage runs from Plotkin and Power's algebraic operations, which model effects as operations of a Lawvere theory whose free model induces the computational monad, to Plotkin and Pretnar's introduction of *handlers* as the homomorphism induced by the universal property of that free model [1]. A handler clause for an operation binds two things: the operation's argument and a *delimited continuation* representing the rest of the computation up to the handler boundary. Resuming, discarding, or invoking that continuation multiple times recovers exceptions, state, generators, coroutines, backtracking search, and probabilistic sampling from one uniform mechanism.

This post develops the theory end to end: Section 2 reviews monads and delimited control, Section 3 gives a core calculus, Section 4 dives into signatures, continuations, row polymorphism, and the one-shot versus multi-shot divide, Section 5 surveys the metatheory and measured performance, Section 6 is honest about the limits, and Section 7 concludes.

##2 Background

### Effects before handlers

The monadic account of effects, due to Moggi, models a computation of type `a` with effect `m` as a value of type `m a`, with `return` and bind `(>>=)` as the sequencing primitives. Haskell's `IO` monad made this mainstream. Modularity, however, breaks down as soon as two effects interact: combining the state monad transformer `StateT` with the exception transformer `ExceptT` yields different semantics depending on the stacking order, because state updates either are or are not rolled back when an exception is caught. The transformer approach forces the *composition of effects* to be decided globally and statically.

Delimited continuations offered a different axis of generality. Felleisen's `control`/`prompt`, and Danvy and Filinski's `shift`/`reset`, capture the rest of the computation up to a dynamically enclosing delimiter as a first-class function. These operators are extremely expressive — state, exceptions, coroutines, and nondeterminism can all be encoded — but they are *too* expressive: the captured continuation is an undifferentiated function with no static account of which effects a computation may perform.

### The handler synthesis

Plotkin and Pretnar's insight was to *type* the operations and *structure* the continuation [1]. An effect signature declares named operations with precise parameter and result types, for example:

- `get : unit -> s` and `put : s -> unit` for mutable state of type `s`
- `raise : exn -> empty` for exceptions
- `choose : unit -> bool` for binary nondeterminism
- `yield : a -> unit` for generators

A computation *performs* an operation; the nearest enclosing *handler* for that operation intercepts it. The handler clause receives the argument and the delimited continuation `k`, and may resume `k` zero, one, or many times. Resuming zero times gives exceptions; resuming once with threaded state gives state; resuming twice gives backtracking search. The continuation is typed, the operations are typed, and the set of operations a computation may perform appears in its type — usually as an *effect row*.

Early languages explored the design space aggressively. **Eff** (Bauer and Pretnar) made handlers first-class with dynamically allocated effect instances [5]. **Koka** (Leijen) pushed row-polymorphic effect inference and compiled handlers by type-directed selective CPS [2]. **Frank** (Lindley, McBride, McLaughlin) made handlers multihandlers matching on several computations at once. Most consequentially, **Multicore OCaml** adopted effect handlers as its concurrency primitive, and they shipped in **OCaml 5**, making algebraic effects the first handler-based effect system in a mainstream industrial language [3].

> Theorem: *Every handler is a fold.* A handler for signature Σ applied to a computation is the unique Σ-algebra homomorphism from the free model on return values, determined by its `return` clause and its operation clauses. Resumption corresponds to the homomorphism's recursive action on the continuation.

##3 Methodology

We work with a core calculus in the lineage of Pretnar's thesis and the Kammar–Lindley–Oury formulation, presented here in direct style. The syntax distinguishes *values* from *computations*, following the fine-grain call-by-value tradition:

```haskell
-- Values
v ::= x | () | (v1, v2) | inl v | inr v | fun x -> c

-- Computations
c ::= return v
    | let x = c1 in c2
    | v1 v2
    | do x <- op v in c          -- perform operation op with argument v
    | handle c with h            -- install handler h around computation c

-- Handlers
h ::= handler { return x -> cr, op1 x k -> c1, ..., opn x k -> cn }
```

An **effect signature** Σ is a finite set of operation symbols, each with a parameter type and a result type, written `op : A -> B`. The term `do x <- op v in c` performs the operation: evaluation suspends, the current *evaluation context* up to the nearest enclosing `handle` is reified as the delimited continuation `k`, and control transfers to the matching clause with `x` bound to `v` and `k` bound to the continuation. The operational semantics runs on a *handler machine* — a stack of handler frames plus the current computation — with two essential rules: **return** pops the frame into the handler's `return` clause, and **operation** captures the frames above the handling frame as `k` and jumps to the operation clause. Unperformed operations *bubble* to the next enclosing handler — forwarding is what makes handler composition modular. The type system assigns computations a type `A ! E`, where `E` is an *effect row*, and handling removes the handled operations from the scrutinee's row.

##4 Deep Dive

###4.1 Effect Signatures as Algebraic Theories

The algebraic view treats an effect signature as a *presentation* of an algebraic theory: operation symbols plus equations between terms built from them. State is presented by `get` and `put` together with the familiar equations — getting after putting returns the put value, putting twice keeps only the last, and so on. Nondeterminism is presented by a binary `choose` that is associative, commutative, and idempotent. Exceptions are presented by `raise` with the equation that any operation sequenced after `raise` is absorbed by it.

The payoff of the equational view is the *free model*: for any set `X` of return values, the free Σ-algebra over `X` consists of well-formed trees of operations with leaves in `X`, quotiented by the equations. This free model *is* the monad of Σ-computations — Plotkin and Power's fundamental observation. Handlers then enter as *models*: a handler assigns to each operation a concrete interpretation satisfying the equations, and the universal property of the free model yields a unique homomorphism — the "handling" of a computation [1].

This explains why handlers subsume so many apparently unrelated constructs. Relabelling and restriction in Milner's CCS, timeout, rollback, stream redirection, and Prolog-style cut all emerge as handlers for suitable signatures [1]. The equations double as *specifications* against which handler implementations can be judged.

```ocaml
(* Effect declaration in OCaml 5: a signature with one operation *)
effect Ask : int -> int

let ask_twice () =
  let x = perform (Ask 10) in
  let y = perform (Ask 20) in
  x + y

(* A handler: the model interpreting Ask as addition of a constant *)
let forty_two =
  match ask_twice () with
  | v -> v
  | effect (Ask n) k -> continue k (n + 11)
```

Here `continue k v` resumes the delimited continuation `k` with value `v`; dropping `k` would abort the computation, and invoking it twice would duplicate the remaining work. The *decision* of how many times to resume is the handler's semantic content.

###4.2 Delimited Continuations and the Handler Machine

Every handler clause's continuation is *delimited*: it extends only up to the handler that captured it, not to the top of the program. This delimitation is what makes handlers composable where raw `call/cc` is not — an operation tunnels through intermediate code that knows nothing about it, and only the designated handler observes the capture.

The implementation consequence is the *handler machine* or *fiber* model. Multicore OCaml implements each `match ... with effect` as a *fiber*: a heap-allocated stack segment. Performing an effect walks the fiber chain to the handler, captures the intervening segments as the continuation object, and transfers control. Resumption splices the segments back. The critical design decision is whether the captured continuation is *one-shot* (usable at most once) or *multi-shot* (reusable arbitrarily).

| Design axis | One-shot (OCaml 5) | Multi-shot (Eff, Koka) |
|---|---|---|
| Resume twice | Runtime error unless cloned | Allowed natively |
| Implementation | Move segments, no copy | Copy or persistent segments |
| Backtracking search | Via explicit cloning | Direct |
| Space behavior | Predictable, no accidental blowup | Risk of exponential copying |
| Typical use | Concurrency, generators, async | Logic programming, probabilistic inference |

OCaml 5 chose one-shot continuations with an explicit `Obj.clone_continuation` escape hatch, on the grounds that accidental multi-shot resumption is a notorious source of exponential space blowup and that the overwhelmingly common uses — schedulers, generators, async/await — resume exactly once [3]. Eff and Koka chose multi-shot, accepting implementation complexity for uniformity: in Koka, multi-shot continuations are compiled to explicit continuation-passing style so that resuming twice simply means invoking a function twice [2].

> Proof: *One-shot continuations are sufficient for state and exceptions.* The state handler resumes its continuation exactly once per operation with an updated state argument; the exception handler resumes zero times on `raise` and its `return` clause handles success. Neither requires invoking a continuation twice, so the one-shot restriction loses no expressive power for the standard algebraic effects.

There is a subtlety: *deep* versus *shallow* handlers. A deep handler's operation clause resumes under a reinstalled copy of the handler, so recursive operations in the continuation are handled uniformly — the default in OCaml 5, Eff, and Koka, matching the fold semantics of Section 4.1. A shallow handler does not reinstall itself, so the continuation runs *outside* the handler, useful for implementing control operators themselves.

###4.3 Row Polymorphism and Effect Inference in Koka

If every function type must name every effect it performs, effect annotations become unbearably verbose. Koka's answer is *row polymorphism*: an effect row is a sequence of labels ending in a row variable, and ordinary Hindley–Milner-style inference generalizes over the variable [2]. A function that maps over a list without performing effects itself gets the type:

```haskell
map : (a -> e b) -> list a -> e (list b)
```

where `e` is an effect variable — the function is polymorphic in the effects of its argument and simply propagates them. Effect rows support duplicate labels, so the row `<exn, exn | mu>` collapses to `<exn | mu>`; Leijen showed that duplicate labels admit principal unification without the lacks-constraints or presence-flag machinery that set-based rows require [2].

Koka's inference is *sound* in a strong sense: if an expression typechecks with the empty row `total`, it performs no observable effects — a semantic guarantee, not merely a syntactic one. The same row machinery solves a classic problem with state and polymorphism: Koka encapsulates stateful computation behind a `run`-like construct whose row-polymorphic type guarantees that no state effect escapes, recovering the safety of Haskell's `runST` without the rank-2 type [2].

```rust
// A handler-shaped idea in Rust-like pseudocode: parse with backtracking
// via an explicit multi-shot continuation (what Koka compiles to internally)
enum Cont<A> { Done(A), More(Box<dyn FnOnce(Choice) -> Cont<A>>) }

fn amb<A>(options: Vec<A>, k: impl Fn(A) -> Cont<Answer>) -> Cont<Answer> {
    // multi-shot: k is invoked once per option — the handler for `choose`
    let mut acc = Cont::Done(fail());
    for opt in options {
        acc = combine(acc, k(opt)); // each resumption explores one branch
    }
    acc
}
```

The interaction of handlers with *polymorphism* is the metatheoretic crux. Unrestricted generalization over effect-polymorphic types is unsound in the presence of mutable state, because a captured continuation can smuggle a polymorphic reference cell across resumptions. Koka avoids the value restriction entirely through its row system and semantic encapsulation; other designs impose a *signature restriction* on which type variables may be generalized [4].

###4.4 Implementations: Koka, Eff, Frank, and OCaml 5

The four flagship implementations make sharply different trade-offs:

- **Koka** compiles via a *type-directed selective CPS translation*: only functions whose effect rows are non-total are converted to continuation-passing style, so pure code pays nothing. Handlers become explicit case analysis on a `Yield`/`Pure` datatype. The 2017 functional-programming account reports competitive performance with handwritten state-passing code, demonstrating that the abstraction need not cost [2].
- **Eff** keeps handlers first-class and effect *instances* dynamically allocated (`new ref : ...`), so two state cells of the same signature are distinguished by instance identity. This *dynamic* instance model handles fresh generative effects — references, channels, exceptions-as-values — that a purely static signature cannot name. The cost is a more elaborate operational semantics with an instance environment.
- **Frank** makes the *handling* itself the application's structure: commands are handled by pattern matching on the request, and multihandlers match on several computations at once, giving a particularly direct account of concurrency patterns like racing two computations.
- **OCaml 5** implements handlers with fibers and one-shot continuations in the stock runtime, with a measured mean overhead of about one percent on macro benchmarks that do not use effects, and with backtrace and debugger compatibility preserved [3]. This is the result that moved handlers from research artifact to production feature: effects ship in a language with a thirty-year legacy codebase.

A unifying observation across all four is that *handler-based concurrency* — schedulers written as handlers for `Fork` and `Yield` operations — performs on par with highly optimized monadic concurrency libraries while remaining in direct style [6].

##5 Empirical Results and Proofs

The metatheory of algebraic effects is unusually complete for a feature of this expressiveness. The central results are:

> Theorem: *Type soundness.* In the typed core calculus, well-typed computations never get stuck: progress and preservation hold, and an operation performed with no enclosing handler is ruled out statically by the effect row.

> Theorem: *Effect safety.* If a closed computation has the empty effect row, its evaluation performs no operations — handlers cannot be bypassed, and unhandled effects cannot occur at runtime [2].

> Theorem: *Adequacy of the handler machine.* The fiber-based operational semantics coincides with the denotational semantics: handling a computation denotes the unique homomorphism from the free model, so the machine implements the mathematics [1].

Compilation results are equally strong. Koka's selective CPS translation is *type-directed*: the decision of which functions to CPS-convert is read off their inferred effect rows, and Leijen proves that well-typed programs cannot go wrong under the translated semantics while pure code is left in direct style and pays no penalty [2]. The OCaml retrofitting effort measured the engineering side: a mean one-percent slowdown on effect-free benchmarks, full compatibility with stack-inspecting tools, and competitive scheduler performance against monadic concurrency libraries [3].

Proof technique deserves emphasis because it is reusable. Soundness proofs proceed by progress and preservation, but the *handling* construct demands a substitution lemma for continuations: resuming `k` with a value must preserve typing under the handler's row. The delicate case is *forwarding* — an operation clause that performs further operations must reflect them in its result row.

| Result | Statement | Key reference |
|---|---|---|
| Handlers as homomorphisms | Handling = fold over the free model | Plotkin & Pretnar [1] |
| Row-polymorphic inference | Principal types with duplicate labels | Leijen [2] |
| Production implementation | 1% overhead, tooling-compatible | Sivaramakrishnan et al. [3] |
| Signature restriction | Sound let-generalization with handlers | [4] |
| Eff language | First-class handlers, dynamic instances | Bauer & Pretnar [5] |
| Handler-based concurrency | Parity with monadic libraries | Dolan et al. [6] |

##6 Limitations

The framework's very uniformity creates its blind spots. First, *scoped effects* — operations whose extent is delimited lexically rather than dynamically, such as local exception handlers that must not be captured by an outer resumption, or `catch` constructs with precise semantics — fit awkwardly into the algebraic picture, because the free-model semantics treats every operation as dynamically scoped. Recent work on *scoped* algebraic effects adds explicit syntax for scope delimiters, but the metatheory is younger and the implementation support thinner.

Second, the interaction of handlers with *advanced type-system features* remains delicate. Effect polymorphism with mutable state demands either Koka's semantic encapsulation argument or a syntactic restriction such as the signature restriction [4]; neither is as simple as the value restriction it replaces, and error messages from row unification are notoriously hard to read.

Third, *performance* is good but not free. One-shot continuations avoid copying but forbid the multi-shot idioms; multi-shot continuations permit the idioms but require copying or persistent segments, and naive backtracking handlers can exhibit exponential behavior that no type system currently prevents. Debugging across handler boundaries is harder than debugging straight-line code: stack traces must be reconstructed from fiber segments, and although the OCaml team preserved backtrace compatibility [3], the traces are less intuitive than native call stacks.

Fourth, *reasoning* about handlers is reasoning about control flow, and the equational theory only helps when the handler respects the equations. Effect rows track *which* operations occur, never *how* they are interpreted; behavioral contracts for handlers remain an open research problem.

Finally, the ecosystem gap matters: mainstream languages have decades of libraries written against monadic or callback-based concurrency. Rewriting a scheduler as a handler is elegant, but migrating an existing codebase to handler-based concurrency is a social and engineering project, not a local refactoring.

##7 Conclusion

Algebraic effects and handlers supply what the earlier effect disciplines lacked: a *modular* account in which the interface of an effect, its use in a computation, and its interpretation are three separate artifacts that can vary independently. The mathematics — signatures as theories, computations as free models, handlers as homomorphisms, resumption as delimited continuation — is tight enough to prove soundness, safety, and compilation correct, and the engineering — Koka's selective CPS, Eff's dynamic instances, OCaml 5's fibers — is mature enough to ship. Row polymorphism answers the annotation-burden objection; one-shot continuations answer the performance objection; the signature restriction answers the soundness objection.

The open frontier is *behavioral*: tracking not just which effects occur but what handlers guarantee about them, extending the theory cleanly to scoped effects, and making row-unification errors humane. If the last decade moved handlers from Plotkin and Pretnar's paper to a production runtime [1][3], the next will determine whether they become the default vocabulary for effectful programming — or remain the most beautiful abstraction that most programmers never quite adopt.
