# Differentiable Earth System Modeling: Neural Subgrid Parameterizations, Conservation-Constrained Emulators, and Adjoint-Driven Hybrid Climate Models

## Abstract

Conventional general circulation models (GCMs) resolve planetary-scale dynamics but represent subgrid processes — deep convection, cloud microphysics, boundary-layer turbulence — through heuristic closures whose structural errors dominate climate-projection uncertainty. This thesis develops a rigorous treatment of **differentiable hybrid Earth system models**: GCM dynamical cores that are end-to-end differentiable, coupled to neural subgrid parameterizations trained on high-resolution storm-resolving simulations. We synthesize three converging lines of work: (i) offline emulation of superparameterized physics, from the pioneering Rasp–Pritchard–Gentine aquaplanet experiments to the ClimSim 5.7-billion-pair benchmark; (ii) architectural enforcement of energy and moisture conservation to machine precision, which provably stabilizes coupled prognostic runs; and (iii) differentiable dynamical cores — culminating in NeuralGCM — that admit adjoint gradients through the resolved flow, enabling optimization of hybrid models against climate statistics rather than instantaneous tendencies. We analyze the offline–online stability gap, the spectral bias of emulators, and the conditions under which hybrid models remain stable for multi-decadal simulations, and we close with a research program for data-driven Earth system modeling grounded in conservation laws and differentiability.

## 1 Introduction

General circulation models discretize the atmosphere and ocean on grids whose cells are *orders of magnitude larger* than the phenomena that control their evolution: convective updrafts occupy hundreds of meters; GCM cells span tens of kilometers [1]. The resulting **closure problem** — how the unresolved subgrid flow back-reacts on resolved scales — has been handled for half a century by *parameterizations*: physically motivated but fundamentally approximate bulk formulae for convection, cloud microphysics, radiation, and turbulence. These parameterizations dominate CMIP climate-sensitivity spread [5].

Two revolutions have converged to break this deadlock. The first is *storm-resolving modeling*: kilometer-scale global simulations such as the DYAMOND intercomparison, in which nine models simulated 40 days of global atmosphere at resolutions below 5 km, explicitly resolving tropical deep convection and thereby removing the need to parameterize it [7]. Such simulations are too expensive for century-scale climate projection, but they are ideal **training data** — high-fidelity ground truth from which cheaper surrogates can learn. The second revolution is *differentiable programming*: frameworks in which PDE solvers expose gradients with respect to inputs, parameters, and even architecture, turning the simulator itself into a learnable, optimizable object [6].

The synthesis is the **differentiable hybrid Earth system model**: a GCM whose resolved dynamics are implemented in a differentiable numerical core, coupled to neural network parameterizations that replace heuristic subgrid closures and are trained — offline on storm-resolving data, or *online* through the differentiable simulator — to reproduce subgrid tendencies while exactly conserving energy and moisture [2][3][4]. This thesis is a self-contained treatment of that synthesis: its mathematical structure, its empirical record, its failure modes, and its open problems.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The subgrid closure problem

In a GCM, each grid column carries a state vector **x** = (T, q, u, v, w, p, ...) — temperature, moisture, winds, pressure — and evolves by the prognostic equations

∂**x**/∂t = D(**x**) + P(**x**),

where D is the *resolved dynamics* (advection, pressure-gradient forces, Coriolis terms) and P is the *subgrid physics*: everything happening below grid scale, projected back onto the resolved state. The closure problem is that P(**x**) is not a function of the resolved state alone — it depends on the unresolved flow — so any parameterization is an approximation, typically a regression of subgrid tendencies against resolved variables plus tuned constants.

**Superparameterization** offered a brute-force alternative: embed a two-dimensional cloud-resolving model inside every GCM column (as in SPCAM), explicitly computing convection. Superparameterized models reproduce the mean climate and variability far better than parameterized ones — but at *~200×* the computational cost, prohibitive for operational use [1].

### 2.2 From superparameterization to emulation

The natural move is to *emulate* the expensive embedded model with a neural network. The recipe, established by Rasp, Pritchard, and Gentine [1] and industrialized by ClimSim [2]:

1. Run a superparameterized model (e.g., SPCAM or E3SM-MMF) and save, at each column and time step, the resolved state **x** and the subgrid tendency **y** = P_SP(**x**).
2. Train a neural network f_θ: **x** → **ŷ** by supervised regression on millions of such pairs.
3. Replace the physics module P with f_θ and run the GCM prognostically, coupling the emulator back to the dynamics.

Rasp et al. demonstrated that this works on an aquaplanet: a deep network trained on SPCAM replaced *all* atmospheric subgrid processes, and the resulting hybrid model ran stably for multiple simulated years, reproducing the mean climate, precipitation extremes, and the equatorial wave spectrum of the reference simulation — while *approximately* conserving energy despite no explicit instruction to do so [1]. ClimSim scaled this paradigm to 5.7 billion input–output pairs from the global E3SM-MMF model, providing deterministic and stochastic baselines and a coupling pipeline for online testing [2].

> Theorem: *A neural emulator that achieves small offline regression error on superparameterized tendencies does not necessarily yield a stable online simulation.* Small systematic biases in the emulator — undetectable in offline metrics — accumulate through the prognostic feedback loop, shifting the simulated climate outside the training manifold and causing drift or catastrophic blowup [1].

The *offline–online gap* is the central difficulty of hybrid climate modeling.

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 The offline emulation pipeline

The standard offline pipeline mirrors ClimSim's design. The training set consists of pairs (**x**ᵢ, **y**ᵢ), where **x**ᵢ ∈ ℝⁿ is the resolved column state (temperature and moisture profiles on ~30–60 vertical levels, surface fluxes, insolation, Coriolis parameter) and **y**ᵢ ∈ ℝᵐ is the subgrid tendency (heating and moistening rates per level, radiative fluxes, precipitation). A multilayer perceptron or residual network is trained with mean-squared error,

ℒ(θ) = (1/N) Σᵢ ‖f_θ(**x**ᵢ) − **y**ᵢ‖²,

often with per-variable normalization so that small-amplitude upper-tropospheric tendencies are not ignored. The trained f_θ is then coupled into the GCM: at each dynamical time step, the physics driver calls the network instead of the parameterization suite.

### 3.2 Differentiability and adjoint gradients

A *differentiable dynamical core* is one in which every operation — spectral transforms, semi-Lagrangian advection, the ODE time stepper — is implemented with automatic differentiation support, so that gradients ∂ℒ/∂θ flow backward through the entire simulation. NeuralGCM realizes this: a spectral dynamical core written in a differentiable framework, coupled to a learned-physics module of per-column neural networks, trained end-to-end with losses on simulated trajectories [3]. Differentiability unlocks three capabilities beyond offline regression [6]:

- **End-to-end training**: backpropagate through *n* coupled time steps so the network learns tendencies that produce correct trajectories, not just correct instantaneous outputs — the solver-in-the-loop philosophy that also accelerated coarse-grid turbulence simulation [5].
- **Objective calibration**: differentiate climate statistics with respect to model parameters, replacing manual tuning of dozens of constants with gradient-based optimization.
- **Adjoint sensitivity analysis**: compute ∂(climate metric)/∂(parameter or forcing) through the chaotic flow, enabling principled attribution and uncertainty quantification.

```haskell
-- Conceptual: adjoint gradient through n coupled hybrid steps
-- x_{t+1} = F(x_t; theta) = dynCore(x_t) + dt * nn(theta, x_t)
adjointLoss :: Params -> [State] -> Scalar
adjointLoss theta targets =
    let xs = scanl (\x _ -> step theta x) x0 [1..n]   -- forward rollout
        residuals = zipWith (\x t -> mse x t) xs targets
    in sum residuals                                 -- grad flows back through F^n
```

Here `grad` applied to `adjointLoss` yields parameter gradients through *n* steps of the coupled hybrid model — the object that distinguishes online training from offline regression.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Conservation-constrained neural parameterizations

A convection scheme is not merely a regression: it must satisfy **analytic conservation laws**. In the absence of convective momentum transport, superparameterized convection conserves two column-integrated quantities — liquid/ice water static energy and total water — and, when the network predicts radiative heating profiles and fluxes, longwave and shortwave radiation impose two further constraints [4]. An unconstrained network violates these by amounts comparable to its regression error, and the violations act as spurious global sources that accumulate over simulated decades.

Beucler et al. [4] introduced a systematic method for enforcing such constraints *in the architecture*: the network predicts a subset of outputs, and the remaining outputs are computed deterministically from the constraint equations, so conservation holds to machine precision by construction — without degrading offline skill, and in fact *reducing* errors on the variables most affected by the constraints.

> Theorem: *Let C(y, x) = 0 be a set of r independent linear conservation constraints on the emulator output y ∈ ℝᵐ. If the network predicts the first m − r outputs freely and the remaining r are solved from C(y, x) = 0, then the coupled hybrid model conserves the constrained quantities to machine precision for every prognostic time step, regardless of the network's weights.*

The practical construction, expressed as a declarative specification:

```tla+
------------------------------ MODULE ConservationLayer ------------------------------
\* Spec: architectural enforcement of column energy and moisture conservation
EXTENDS Reals
VARIABLES x, yRaw          \* x: resolved state, yRaw: unconstrained NN output
CONSTANTS nLevels          \* number of vertical levels

ColEnergy(y) == Sum over levels of (mass(k) * (cp*T_tend(k) + Lv*q_tend(k)))
ColWater(y)  == Sum over levels of (mass(k) * q_tend(k))

\* Conservation layer: solve for boundary fluxes from the constraint residuals
Conserve ==
    /\ yRaw \in Real^(2*nLevels)
    /\ LET dE == ColEnergy(yRaw) IN
       LET dQ == ColWater(yRaw) IN
           y = AdjustFluxes(yRaw, -dE, -dQ)   \* residual absorbed by fluxes
    /\ ColEnergy(y) = 0 /\ ColWater(y) = 0     \* exact by construction
=============================================================================
```

An equivalent Rust implementation sketch of the residual-absorption layer used in production hybrid models:

```rust
/// Enforce column-integrated energy and moisture conservation exactly.
fn conservation_layer(y: &mut [f64], mass: &[f64], cp: f64, lv: f64) {
    let n = mass.len();
    // Column-integrated residuals of the raw NN output
    let d_e: f64 = (0..n).map(|k| mass[k] * (cp * y[k] + lv * y[n + k])).sum();
    let d_q: f64 = (0..n).map(|k| mass[k] * y[n + k]).sum();
    // Absorb residuals into the surface sensible/latent flux predictions
    y[2 * n]     -= d_e / mass.iter().sum::<f64>(); // sensible flux
    y[2 * n + 1] -= d_q / mass.iter().sum::<f64>(); // latent flux
    debug_assert!(column_energy_residual(y, mass, cp, lv).abs() < 1e-12);
    debug_assert!(column_moisture_residual(y, mass).abs() < 1e-12);
}
```

### 4.2 Differentiable dynamical cores and adjoint gradients

The decisive architectural advance of NeuralGCM [3] was making the *dynamical core itself* differentiable: a spectral solver for atmospheric dynamics written so that gradients propagate through advection, the implicit–explicit ODE time stepper, and the learned-physics tendency network simultaneously. This permits training the entire hybrid system on trajectory-level objectives — e.g., matching reanalysis or storm-resolving reference rollouts — rather than on single-step tendency targets.

Mathematically, let **x**ₜ₊₁ = F(**x**ₜ; θ) be one coupled step. Offline training minimizes ‖f_θ(**x**ₜ) − P(**x**ₜ)‖² per step; online (differentiable) training minimizes trajectory losses such as

ℒ_traj(θ) = Σₜ ‖**x**ₜ(θ) − **x**ᵗ_ref‖²,   **x**ₜ₊₁(θ) = F(**x**ₜ(θ); θ),

whose gradient requires the *adjoint* product (∂F/∂**x**)ᵀ through the dynamical core. Gelbrecht et al. [6] argue this capability is the essential ingredient of the next generation of data-informed Earth system models: it converts calibration — historically a manual, expert-driven tuning of dozens of closure parameters — into a well-posed optimization problem, and it lets observational and high-resolution simulation data constrain *combinations* of parameters through their joint effect on simulated climate statistics.

A crucial subtlety: chaotic divergence makes long-horizon adjoints ill-conditioned. Stable online training therefore uses short rollout horizons, stochastic perturbations (ensemble-style losses such as CRPS), and regularization of the learned physics — the toolkit that lets NeuralGCM produce ensemble weather forecasts competitive with ECMWF's operational system [3].

### 4.3 Online stability and the offline–online gap

The empirical record on coupled stability is instructive. Rasp et al.'s aquaplanet hybrids were stable for years, but small changes in training data or input/output vectors produced networks that generated "increasingly unrealistic outputs and causing model blowups" [1]. The mechanism is now well understood: the emulator is trained on states drawn from the *reference* climate's distribution, but once coupled, its own outputs determine the states it will see next — a distribution shift that compounds. An emulator with 1% systematic moistening bias in the deep tropics will, over thousands of coupled steps, humidify the column beyond anything in its training set, at which point its predictions become uncontrolled.

Three complementary mitigations have emerged:

```python
# Online stability probe: couple emulator, integrate, monitor drift
x = reference_state()
for step in range(n_steps):
    x = dyn_core.advance(x, dt)
    y = conservation_layer(neural_param(x), x)
    x = x + dt * y
    assert column_energy_residual(y) < 1e-10, f"blowup at step {step}"
```
1. **Conservation constraints** (§4.1) remove the largest class of systematic drift by forbidding spurious global sources [4].
2. **Online (differentiable) training** exposes the network to its own induced distribution during training, penalizing trajectories that drift [3][5].
3. **Stochastic parameterizations** — predicting distributions rather than deterministic tendencies, as in ClimSim's stochastic baselines — inject variability that mimics the unresolved ensemble and damps the growth of coupled instabilities [2].

| Approach | Offline skill | Online stability | Cost | Key reference |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Deterministic NN (unconstrained) | High | Fragile; blowups common | Low | Rasp et al. 2018 [1] |
| Conservation-constrained NN | High | Stable for years (aquaplanet) | Low | Beucler et al. 2021 [4] |
| ClimSim-scale baselines | High | Mixed; needs coupling pipeline | Medium | Yu et al. 2024 [2] |
| Differentiable hybrid (NeuralGCM) | High | Stable for decades (AMIP) | Medium–High | Kochkov et al. 2024 [3] |

### 4.4 Spectral bias and emulated spectra

Neural networks exhibit a *frequency principle*: they fit low-frequency components of a target function far faster than high-frequency ones, and finite-capacity networks systematically under-represent fine-scale variance. In climate emulation this manifests as **spectral bias in emulated spectra** — the emulator's predicted heating/moistening fields are too smooth, deficient in small-scale variance relative to the cloud-resolving reference. Rasp et al. noted that their hybrid reproduced the equatorial wave spectrum "closely" but not exactly, with degradation at the smallest resolved scales [1].

Why does this matter for climate? Kinetic energy cascades and convective organization transfer variance *upscale*; an emulator that damps fine scales can still shift the large-scale circulation. Mitigations include **stochastic emulators** that model subgrid variance explicitly (ClimSim's stochastic baselines) [2], **spectral losses** penalizing the predicted power spectrum rather than pointwise MSE, and **solver-in-the-loop corrections** that learn discretization corrections at coarse resolution, preserving spectral fidelity by construction [5].

## 5 Empirical Results / Proofs

We now consolidate the empirical record into quantitative claims.

**Claim 1 — Feasibility of full subgrid replacement.** Rasp et al. replaced *all* atmospheric subgrid parameterizations in an aquaplanet GCM with a deep network trained on SPCAM. Prognostic multi-year runs were stable and reproduced the mean climate, precipitation extremes, and the equatorial wave spectrum of the cloud-resolving reference; the network approximately conserved energy without being instructed to, and generalized to new surface-forcing patterns [1].

**Claim 2 — Scale of modern training data.** ClimSim provides 5.7 billion input–output pairs from the global E3SM-MMF superparameterized model, with global coverage over multiple years at high sampling frequency, plus deterministic and stochastic regression baselines and a containerized online-coupling pipeline [2]. This is the largest dataset ever assembled for hybrid ML–physics climate research.

**Claim 3 — Conservation without performance loss.** Beucler et al. proved by construction, and verified on SPCAM emulation, that architectural enforcement of energy, moisture, and radiation conservation holds to machine precision *without degrading* offline skill, and reduces errors on constraint-adjacent outputs [4].

**Claim 4 — Differentiable hybrids at GCM scale.** NeuralGCM combines a differentiable spectral dynamical core with per-column learned physics. It is competitive with state-of-the-art ML weather models at 1–10 days and with ECMWF ensembles at 1–15 days; with prescribed SST it tracks global mean temperature for multiple decades, exhibits realistic tropical cyclone frequency and tracks at 140 km resolution, and runs 8–40× coarser than operational systems for 3–5 orders of magnitude computational savings (70,000 simulated days per 24 hours on a single TPU) [3].

**Claim 5 — Differentiable corrections inside solvers.** Kochkov et al.'s learned-interpolation CFD showed that embedding a neural network *inside* a differentiable solver — correcting convection stencils on coarse grids — achieves 8× coarser grids with ~40× speedup on turbulent flows at Re = 100,000, a template for solver-aware (rather than tendency-regression) climate emulation [5].

**Claim 6 — The generalization boundary.** All surveyed emulators interpolate well within their training manifold but degrade under strong distribution shift: Rasp et al.'s network "struggles to cope with temperatures far outside its training manifold" [1], and NeuralGCM exhibits climate drift under increased-SST AMIP forcing [3]. The training manifold of the present climate does not contain the +4 K world — a fundamental limit on purely data-driven projection.

## 6 Limitations

1. **Distribution shift under climate change.** Emulators trained on present-day climates face out-of-manifold states under strong warming; no offline skill guarantees extrapolation [1][3]. Climate-invariant input transformations mitigate this only partially.
2. **Chaotic adjoint conditioning.** Gradients through long chaotic rollouts explode; online training is currently limited to short horizons and regularized losses. Whether decade-scale adjoint optimization of ESMs is tractable remains open [6].
3. **Spectral bias persists.** Deterministic emulators under-represent fine-scale variance; stochastic and spectral-loss remedies add complexity and can destabilize coupling [2].
4. **Missing processes.** Current hybrids emulate the atmosphere; ocean, sea-ice, land, and biogeochemistry — with their own closure problems and longer timescales — remain largely untouched.
5. **Cost of differentiability.** Reverse-mode differentiation through a GCM multiplies memory and compute; checkpointing and reversible solvers help but add engineering burden [3][6].
6. **Evaluation standards.** The field lacks agreed online-evaluation protocols; offline MSE leaderboards (even at ClimSim scale) can rank models inversely to coupled stability, rewarding the wrong objective [2].

## 7 Conclusion

Differentiable Earth system modeling reframes climate simulation as a *differentiable program*: resolved dynamics implemented in an adjoint-capable core, subgrid physics learned from storm-resolving truth, and conservation laws enforced architecturally rather than hoped for. The empirical arc — from Rasp et al.'s aquaplanet proof of concept [1], through conservation-constrained architectures [4] and the ClimSim data infrastructure [2], to NeuralGCM's differentiable, decades-stable hybrid [3] — demonstrates that the approach is not speculative: it already matches operational systems on weather timescales and tracks climate metrics for decades at a fraction of the cost.

The central theoretical lesson is the **offline–online gap**: regression accuracy on superparameterized tendencies is necessary but not sufficient; stability is a property of the *coupled* dynamical system, and only conservation enforcement, online training through the differentiable core, and stochasticity have been shown to control it. The central practical lesson is that differentiability converts the two hardest problems of climate modeling — closure and calibration — from art into optimization [6].

The research program ahead is clear: extend neural parameterizations to the ocean and cryosphere, develop climate-invariant features that survive strong distribution shift, scale adjoint training to coupled multi-decadal rollouts, and build evaluation protocols that measure what matters — stable, conservative, spectrally faithful climate simulation. Storm-resolving references like DYAMOND [7] provide the ground truth; differentiable programming provides the machinery.
