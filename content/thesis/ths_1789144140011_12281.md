# Decoupling Dissemination from Ordering: DAG-Based Asynchronous Byzantine Consensus from Narwhal/Tusk to Bullshark, with HotStuff as the Partially-Synchronous Counterpoint

## Abstract

Byzantine fault-tolerant state machine replication has fractured into two architectural philosophies: the partially-synchronous, leader-driven lineage of HotStuff [1], and the asynchronous, DAG-based lineage of Narwhal/Tusk [2] and Bullshark [3]. This thesis analyzes the decisive design move of the latter family — the complete decoupling of reliable data dissemination from total ordering — and its consequences for throughput, latency, and liveness under adversarial conditions. We reconstruct HotStuff's chained three-phase commit and linear view-change mechanism, identify the partial-synchrony assumption as the fault line separating the two families, and present the Narwhal directed-acyclic-graph mempool construction in full technical detail: round-based reliable broadcast, 2/3 causality, certificates of availability, and worker-level scale-out. We then examine Tusk's zero-message-overhead asynchronous consensus embedded directly in the DAG, and Bullshark's partially-synchronous dual-path design that recovers two-round steady-state latency while preserving an asynchronous fallback. Drawing on published wide-area measurements, we compare the protocols quantitatively, prove the central safety and liveness theorems of each family, and close by identifying the remaining costs of DAG-based designs: persistent memory pressure, transaction-fairness subtleties, and the irreducible latency floor of randomized asynchrony.

## 1. Introduction

For four decades, Byzantine fault-tolerant (BFT) consensus research has been dominated by a single architectural template inherited from PBFT [7]: a distinguished leader proposes, replicas vote in two or three phases, and a view-change sub-protocol replaces a faulty leader. HotStuff [1] perfected this template, achieving *linear* communication complexity and *responsiveness* — progress at the speed of the actual network rather than a conservative delay bound — through threshold signatures and a pipelined, chained three-phase commit. Yet the template carries two structural wounds. First, the leader is a serialization bottleneck: dissemination throughput is bounded by what a single replica can propose. Second, liveness rests on *partial synchrony*: after an unknown Global Stabilization Time (GST), message delays are bounded. Before GST — or under a sustained denial-of-service attack that makes the network behave as if pre-GST — the protocol stalls [2].

The DAG-based family of protocols, inaugurated by DAG-Rider [6] and brought to production maturity by Narwhal/Tusk [2] and Bullshark [3], attacks both wounds at once. Its central thesis is a *separation of concerns*: reliable dissemination of transactions (the mempool problem) is solved once, asynchronously, by every replica in parallel; total ordering of the disseminated data (the consensus problem) is then solved as a lightweight computation *over the resulting causal graph*. Because dissemination no longer passes through the leader, throughput scales with the aggregate bandwidth of all replicas. Because the mempool tolerates full asynchrony, the system's data plane never stalls even when its ordering plane does.

This thesis is organized as follows. Section 2 fixes the system model, recalls the FLP impossibility [8] and the partial-synchrony model, and derives the precise commit rules of chained HotStuff. Section 3 describes our comparative methodology. Section 4 presents the deep dive: HotStuff's chained protocol as the partially-synchronous counterpoint (§4.1), the Narwhal DAG mempool (§4.2), Tusk's zero-overhead asynchronous ordering (§4.3), and Bullshark's dual-path design (§4.4). Section 5 reports empirical results from published wide-area experiments and proves the core theorems. Section 6 discusses limitations, and Section 7 concludes.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 System model

We consider a static set of *n = 3f + 1* replicas, at most *f* of which may be Byzantine (arbitrarily malicious, but computationally bounded so that digital signatures and threshold signatures are unforgeable). Replicas communicate over authenticated point-to-point links. A *quorum* is any set of *2f + 1* replicas; any two quorums intersect in at least *f + 1* replicas, hence in at least one honest replica — the *quorum intersection* property on which all safety arguments rest.

> **Theorem (Quorum intersection).** In a system of *n = 3f + 1* replicas with at most *f* Byzantine faults, any two quorums of size *2f + 1* intersect in at least one honest replica.

*Proof.* Two sets of *2f + 1* drawn from *3f + 1* elements intersect in at least *(2f+1) + (2f+1) − (3f+1) = f + 1* elements. Since at most *f* are Byzantine, at least one is honest. ∎

### 2.2 The synchrony spectrum and FLP

Three timing models matter. In the *synchronous* model, message delay is bounded by a known Δ. In the *partially synchronous* model, there exists an unknown GST after which delay is bounded by a known Δ. In the *asynchronous* model, no delay bound exists at all. The celebrated FLP result [8] proves that no deterministic protocol solves consensus in the asynchronous model with even one faulty process. Deterministic BFT protocols therefore *must* assume partial synchrony (HotStuff [1], Bullshark's steady state [3]) or inject randomness (Tusk's common coin [2], DAG-Rider [6]).

### 2.3 Chained HotStuff: the partially-synchronous counterpoint

HotStuff's basic protocol has three voting phases — *prepare*, *pre-commit*, *commit* — followed by *decide*. A leader collects *2f + 1* votes per phase into a *quorum certificate* (QC) using threshold signatures, keeping communication linear. The chained (pipelined) variant collapses the protocol into a single repeating propose–vote pattern: each view's proposal extends the previous view's QC, so the three phases of one block overlap with the phases of its successors.

> **Theorem (Chained HotStuff commit rule).** A block *b* proposed in view *v* is committed once a replica observes a *three-chain* *b, b′, b″, b\** — three consecutive blocks each carrying a QC for its parent, plus one further block — where *b′* extends *b*, *b″* extends *b′*, and *b\** extends *b″* with the first two adjacencies consecutive [1].

Safety follows from a locking argument: a replica that votes for a block in a higher view sets its *locked QC* to the highest QC it has seen, and thereafter votes only for proposals extending a chain at least as recent. Two conflicting committed blocks would require two quorums to have voted for incompatible chains above the same lock — impossible by quorum intersection. The price of this elegance is a *three-chain latency*: commitment lags proposal by roughly three leader rotations, and — critically — every block still flows through a single leader.

## 3. Methodology

Our analysis follows a three-part methodology. First, *protocol reconstruction*: we formalize each protocol's message pattern, quorum structure, and commit rule as abstract state machines, enabling apples-to-apples comparison of message complexity, round complexity, and timing assumptions. Second, *quantitative comparison*: we adopt four metrics — peak sustained throughput (transactions/second), commit latency under no faults, latency under *f* crash faults, and per-decision message complexity — and populate them from the peer-reviewed experimental evaluations in [1, 2, 3], restricting ourselves to wide-area (WAN) deployments with 10–50 validators. Third, *proof-theoretic analysis*: we isolate the single lemma on which each family's safety rests (locking for HotStuff, 2/3 causality plus common-coin agreement for the DAG family) and present rigorous sketches. We do not re-run experiments; all numbers are cited from their sources.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 HotStuff's chained three-phase commit in detail

In chained HotStuff, every view *v* has a designated leader *L(v)*, typically rotating round-robin. The leader proposes a block *B_v = (txs, QC_{v−1})* extending the highest QC it knows. Each replica votes once per view if the proposal extends its locked QC; the next leader aggregates *2f + 1* votes into *QC_v* with a single threshold signature. The vote rule in pseudocode:

```rust
fn on_proposal(b: &Block, locked_qc: &QC) -> Option<Vote> {
    // Safety rule: extend the locked chain, liveness rule: extend highest QC
    if b.parent_qc.view >= locked_qc.view {
        let vote = sign(b.hash);
        Some(Vote { block: b.hash, share: vote })
    } else {
        None // conflicting fork: refuse to vote
    }
}
```

Three properties make HotStuff the apex of the leader-based lineage [1, 4, 5]:

1. **Linear view change.** A new leader needs only the highest QC among *2f + 1* NEW-VIEW messages — *O(n)* communication — versus PBFT's *O(n²)* view-change proof assembly [7].
2. **Responsiveness.** After GST, a correct leader drives consensus at the actual network speed *δ*, not the worst-case bound Δ.
3. **Optimistic responsiveness.** Even the view-change path advances at network speed.

The Fast-HotStuff variant [4] additionally hardens the protocol against forking attacks by forward-securing QCs, and DiemBFT later adopted a *two-chain* commit rule for lower latency — at the cost of requiring the leader to wait for the maximal delay before proposing, sacrificing responsiveness [9]. The fundamental limitation remains: the leader serializes *dissemination*, so throughput is capped by one replica's egress bandwidth, and view changes under asynchrony inflate latency sharply.

### 4.2 Narwhal: the DAG mempool

Narwhal's [2] radical move is to remove the leader from the data path entirely. Every validator continuously broadcasts *batches* of transactions, and these broadcasts are woven into a shared directed acyclic graph with the following structure:

- **Rounds.** Execution proceeds in numbered rounds. In each round *r*, every validator reliably broadcasts exactly one *vertex* *v = (author, r, batch digests, parents)*, where *parents* references at least *2f + 1* vertices from round *r − 1*.
- **Certificates of availability.** A vertex is *certified* once *2f + 1* validators acknowledge it; the certificate proves the vertex's data is retrievable from at least *f + 1* honest validators.
- **2/3 causality.** Because every certified vertex references a quorum of the previous round, the DAG satisfies a crucial completeness property:

> **Lemma (Narwhal completeness).** If an honest validator holds a certified vertex *v* at round *r*, then it can eventually obtain the full causal history of *v* — every ancestor vertex — because each certified vertex's data is available from at least *f + 1* honest replicas.

The broadcast itself is asynchronous and needs no leader, no view change, and no timing assumption: validators simply keep adding vertices. Throughput scales by adding *workers* — separate processes per validator that disseminate batches in parallel while the primary orders only small digests. The Python sketch below captures the core loop:

```python
def narwhal_round(r: int, pending: list[Batch]) -> Vertex:
    # Collect 2f+1 certified vertices of round r-1 as parents
    parents = wait_for_quorum_certified(round=r - 1)
    digest = disseminate(pending)          # workers spread data in parallel
    v = Vertex(author=self, round=r, digest=digest, parents=parents)
    broadcast(v)
    # Vertex becomes certified after 2f+1 acknowledgements
    return await_certificate(v)
```

The DAG thus provides *reliable, causally-ordered dissemination with no liveness assumption whatsoever*. Consensus is demoted to the far cheaper problem of agreeing on *which vertices to linearize* — and, crucially, any deterministic function of the DAG (e.g., depth-first traversal from committed anchors) yields the same total order at all honest validators.

### 4.3 Tusk: zero-message-overhead asynchronous consensus

Given the Narwhal DAG, Tusk [2] observes that ordering requires *no additional messages at all*: the consensus logic is a pure local computation over vertices validators already broadcast. Time is divided into *waves* of three rounds. In each wave, a *global perfect coin* (realized by threshold signatures over the wave number) elects a leader vertex from the wave's first round *retrospectively* — after the DAG for the wave is complete, so the adversary cannot adaptively corrupt the leader before election.

> **Theorem (Tusk commit rule).** Let *w* be a wave with rounds *(3w+1, 3w+2, 3w+3)* and elected leader vertex *ℓ* in round *3w+1*. An honest validator commits *ℓ* if at least *f + 1* vertices in round *3w+2* reference *ℓ*. Committed leaders are then ordered by wave number, and each leader's full causal history is linearized deterministically.

*Proof sketch.* If *f + 1* honest vertices in round *3w+2* reference *ℓ*, then by quorum intersection every honest validator's round-*3w+3* vertex references at least one of them, so every honest validator observes support for *ℓ* and commits the same leader. Randomness guarantees that with constant probability per wave the elected leader is referenced by enough honest vertices, giving expected-constant-time liveness despite full asynchrony — sidestepping FLP [8] exactly where FLP says determinism cannot go. ∎

Because Tusk adds zero messages to the mempool traffic, its overhead over raw dissemination is nil; the cost is latency (several DAG rounds per commit) and the cryptographic machinery of the common coin.

### 4.4 Bullshark: recovering partially-synchronous latency with an asynchronous fallback

Tusk pays for asynchrony in latency. Bullshark [3] asks: if the network is *usually* well-behaved, can we commit in two DAG rounds while retaining an asynchronous safety net? Its answer is a *dual-path* protocol. Each wave has four rounds and two distinguished leader slots (rounds 1 and 3) with *predetermined* leaders:

- **Steady-state path.** If the predetermined leader's vertex is referenced by *2f + 1* round-2 vertices, commit it immediately — two DAG rounds, no coin, no waiting.
- **Fallback path.** If a local timeout expires before the steady-state commit (evidence of asynchrony or a faulty leader), validators fall back to electing the round-1 leader via the common coin, Tusk-style, and commit under the asynchronous rule.

Safety across the two paths rests on a single invariant: a steady-state commit of leader *ℓ* requires *2f + 1* references, which by quorum intersection forces every honest validator's fallback computation to also observe *ℓ*'s support. Validators therefore never disagree about which path's decision stands. The TLA⁺ sketch below captures the quorum-intersection core shared by all three DAG protocols:

```tla
------------------------------ MODULE QuorumDAG ------------------------------
EXTENDS Naturals, FiniteSets
CONSTANTS Replicas, F
N == 3 * F + 1
Quorum == {Q \in SUBSET Replicas : Cardinality(Q) >= 2 * F + 1}

\* Safety invariant: conflicting leaders cannot both gather quorum support
SafetyInvariant ==
    \A L1, L2 \in Leaders :
        (L1 /= L2) => ~(\E Q1, Q2 \in Quorum :
            /\ Supports(Q1, L1)
            /\ Supports(Q2, L2)
            /\ \A r \in Q1 \cap Q2 : Honest(r) => ~ByzantineDoubleVote(r))
=============================================================================
```

In production, this design proved decisive: Bullshark (and its pipelined successor Shoal) underpins the Aptos and Sui blockchains, where the steady-state path delivers HotStuff-class latency while the DAG data plane sustains throughput far beyond any single leader.

## 5. Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 Quantitative comparison

The table below consolidates the WAN measurements reported in the primary sources (10–50 validators, 512-byte transactions unless noted).

| Protocol | Timing model | Commit latency (rounds) | WAN throughput | WAN latency | Msgs / decision |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| PBFT [7] | Partial sync | 3 (2 phases + reply) | ~10³ tx/s | ~1 s | *O(n²)* |
| HotStuff [1] | Partial sync | ~3 chained views | ~1,800 tx/s | ~1 s | *O(n)* |
| Narwhal–HotStuff [2] | Partial sync (mempool async) | ~3 views over DAG | **>130,000 tx/s** | **<2 s** | *O(n)* + DAG |
| Tusk [2] | **Asynchronous** | ~7 DAG rounds (waves) | **~160,000 tx/s** | **~3 s** | **0 extra** |
| Bullshark [3] | Partial sync + async fallback | **2** (steady state) | ~10⁵ tx/s class | ~2 s class | *O(n)* + DAG |

Three findings stand out. First, *decoupling dissemination from ordering buys two orders of magnitude*: Narwhal–HotStuff sustains over 130,000 tx/s at under 2 seconds latency where standalone HotStuff manages 1,800 tx/s — and adding workers scales throughput linearly to 600,000 tx/s with no latency increase [2]. Second, *asynchrony is affordable*: Tusk achieves roughly 160,000 tx/s at ~3 s latency with zero consensus messages beyond the DAG itself, about 20× the state-of-the-art asynchronous protocols of its time [2]. Third, *faults discriminate the families*: under *f* crash faults, Narwhal–HotStuff maintains throughput but its latency inflates (view changes stall the ordering plane), whereas Tusk's latency is essentially fault-insensitive because no view change exists [2].

### 5.2 Proof summaries

**HotStuff safety.** Suppose two conflicting blocks *b, w* both commit. Each commit implies a three-chain of QCs; let *qc_b*, *qc_w* be the highest QCs in the two chains. Quorum intersection yields an honest replica that voted in both quorums; its lock monotonicity forces the two chains to be comparable, contradicting conflict. Machine-checked proofs of this argument now exist in Agda [9].

**DAG-family safety.** All three DAG protocols reduce safety to Lemma 4.2 (completeness) plus the commit-rule quorum argument of §4.3: any two honest validators that commit leaders for the same wave commit the *same* leader, and causal histories are linearized by an identical deterministic function, so total orders coincide.

**Liveness.** HotStuff is live after GST with a correct leader (partial synchrony). Tusk is live under full asynchrony with probability 1, via the common coin's constant per-wave success probability. Bullshark is live after GST on the fast path and live under asynchrony on the fallback path — the first DAG protocol to offer both.

## 6. Limitations

The DAG architecture is not free. **Memory pressure** is the most immediate cost: every validator must retain the full causal history of uncommitted vertices, and under sustained asynchrony the DAG grows without bound; production systems add garbage-collection watermarks tied to committed anchors, which reintroduces a (weak) synchrony assumption for liveness of cleanup. **Fairness** is subtler: a Byzantine validator can selectively exclude competitors' vertices from its parents, censoring transactions at the dissemination layer — Narwhal guarantees *availability*, not *inclusion*, and inclusion fairness requires additional mechanisms. **Latency floors** persist: even Bullshark's two-round steady state exceeds HotStuff's optimistic single-leader latency in benign LAN conditions, and Tusk's randomized waves cannot beat the deterministic three-phase minimum. **Complexity** is a genuine engineering tax: implementing reliable broadcast, worker scale-out, coin infrastructure, and dual-path commit logic is an order of magnitude more code than chained HotStuff, with correspondingly larger audit surface [9]. Finally, the empirical record, while strong, rests on experiments with at most dozens of validators; behavior at hundreds of validators under adversarial network scheduling remains largely uncharted.

## 7. Conclusion

The trajectory from HotStuff to Narwhal/Tusk to Bullshark is the story of consensus shedding its most expensive assumption — that ordering and dissemination must share a leader and a clock. HotStuff [1] proved that leader-based BFT can be linear and responsive; Narwhal [2] proved that dissemination need not pass through the leader at all; Tusk proved that ordering over a DAG needs no extra messages and no timing assumption; Bullshark [3] proved that the two regimes can coexist, buying partially-synchronous latency with an asynchronous fallback. The measured payoff — two orders of magnitude in throughput at comparable latency, with graceful degradation under faults — has already reshaped production blockchains. The open frontier is no longer raw throughput but the harder properties DAGs complicate: censorship-resistant inclusion, bounded memory under adversarial asynchrony, and formal verification of the full stack from mempool to commit [9]. The leader is dead as a data-plane bottleneck; the work now is making its replacement trustworthy.
