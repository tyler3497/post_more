# Sampling-Based Motion Planning: Probabilistic Roadmaps, RRT and RRT*, Kinodynamic Extensions, and the Theory of Asymptotic Optimality

## Abstract

Sampling-based motion planning has, over three decades, converted robot motion planning from a geometric problem tractable only in low dimensions into a routine engineering capability in configuration spaces of dozens or hundreds of dimensions. This thesis traces that arc: from the probabilistic roadmap (PRM) of Kavraki, Švestka, Latombe, and Overmars [1], which first demonstrated practical multi-query planning in high-dimensional configuration space, through the rapidly-exploring random tree (RRT) of LaValle [2] and its kinodynamic generalization [3], to the asymptotically optimal RRT*, PRM*, and RRG of Karaman and Frazzoli [4], whose analysis exposed the deep connection between sampling-based planners and random geometric graphs. We develop probabilistic completeness, the exact rewiring-radius conditions yielding asymptotic optimality, the ellipsoidal focused sampling of Informed RRT* [6], and the optimal-control machinery of kinodynamic RRT* [5]. We dissect collision checking — the dominant computational cost — and close with convergence analysis, empirical benchmarks, and open problems.

---

## 1 Introduction

The *motion planning problem* asks a simple question: given a robot, its environment, a start configuration, and a goal, find a continuous collision-free path between them. The classical formulation casts the problem in *configuration space* (C-space): the robot becomes a point, obstacles become forbidden regions C-obs, and planning occurs in their complement, C-free [1]. The elegance of this reduction is also its curse. Exact algorithms — cell decomposition, retraction, cylindrical algebraic decomposition — are exponential in the dimension of C-space, and the general problem is PSPACE-hard. By the early 1990s, exactness was a luxury high-degree-of-freedom robots could not afford.

Sampling-based planning abandoned exactness. Rather than representing C-free explicitly, these algorithms *probe* it: draw random samples, test them for collision, and connect nearby samples with a fast local planner to form a graph (PRM) or grow an exploration tree (RRT) [1][2]. The guarantees are probabilistic, yet the trade proved transformative — a 7-DOF manipulator that defeats exact decomposition is planned in milliseconds by PRM; a car with drift dynamics threads clutter via kinodynamic RRT [3].

This thesis develops the theory and practice of sampling-based motion planning as a coherent whole: the two-phase architecture of PRM, the Voronoi bias driving RRT, the 2011 breakthrough proving RRT converges to *non-optimal* cost while RRT* converges to the optimum via random geometric graph theory [4], kinodynamic extensions [5], focused sampling [6], and collision checking — the operation dominating runtime in deployed systems.

## 2 Background and Related Work

### 2.1 The configuration-space formulation

Let C be the *d*-dimensional configuration manifold, C-obs ⊂ C the closed collision set, and C-free = C \ C-obs. A path is a continuous σ : [0,1] → C-free with σ(0) = q-init and σ(1) ∈ Q-goal. Two structural facts dominate:

1. **C-free has no closed form.** For articulated robots it is defined implicitly through forward kinematics; membership testing is a collision-detection query, not a formula.
2. **Connectivity is the hard part.** Narrow passages — regions of C-free thin relative to their length — dominate planning difficulty. A planner that cannot find the needle's eye cannot plan.

Early responses included *potential fields* (fast, trapped in local minima) and *exact decomposition* (complete, exponential). PRM [1] and RRT [2] replaced representation with exploration.

### 2.2 The two classical architectures

**Probabilistic roadmaps** are multi-query: a learning phase builds a graph approximating C-free's connectivity once; many queries are then answered by graph search [1]. *Lazy PRM* defers edge collision checking until query time. **Rapidly-exploring random trees** are single-query: a tree rooted at the start grows toward random samples, inherently biasing growth toward unexplored regions — the *Voronoi bias* [2].

### 2.3 From feasibility to optimality

For fifteen years these planners were feasibility engines with one theoretical property: *probabilistic completeness*. Karaman and Frazzoli [4] changed the discourse by analyzing the *cost* of the returned solution: RRT's cost converges almost surely to a *non-optimal* value, while their RRT*, PRM*, and RRG are provably asymptotically optimal — spawning Informed RRT* [6], BIT*, FMT*, and the kinodynamic SST* [5][7].

> **Theorem (Probabilistic completeness of RRT):** Under mild conditions — chiefly that free space is *expansive* along some feasible path — the probability that RRT fails to reach the goal after *n* iterations tends to zero as *n* → ∞ [2].

| Planner | Query model | Guarantee | Steering required | Typical use |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| PRM [1] | Multi-query | Prob. complete | Local planner | Static scenes, many queries |
| RRT [2] | Single-query | Prob. complete | Incremental extension | Fast feasible planning |
| RRT-Connect | Single-query (bidir.) | Prob. complete | Greedy connect | Practical manipulation |
| RRT* [4] | Single-query | Asymptotically optimal | Exact steering | Optimal geometric paths |
| Informed RRT* [6] | Single-query | Asymptotically optimal | Exact steering | Faster optimal convergence |
| Kinodynamic RRT* [5] | Single-query | Asymptotically optimal | Optimal BVP solver | Linear dynamics |
| SST* [7] | Single-query | Asymptotically optimal | Forward sim only | General nonlinear dynamics |

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 Roadmap construction (PRM)

PRM has two phases. In the *learning phase*, *n* configurations are drawn from a sampling distribution over C, collision-checked, and retained if in C-free. Each node connects to its *k* nearest neighbors (or all within radius ρ) via a *local planner* — usually straight-line interpolation, collision-checked at resolution δ. In the *query phase*, q-init and Q-goal join the roadmap through the same local planner, and Dijkstra or A* extracts the shortest path [1]:

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.spatial import KDTree

def build_prm(n_samples, k_neighbors, is_free, local_path_free, bounds):
    """Learning phase of the Probabilistic Roadmap."""
    V = []
    while len(V) < n_samples:                      # 1. sample C-free
        q = np.random.uniform(bounds[:, 0], bounds[:, 1])
        if is_free(q):
            V.append(q)
    V = np.array(V)
    tree, E = KDTree(V), []
    for i, q in enumerate(V):                      # 2. connect k-nearest
        _, idx = tree.query(q, k=k_neighbors + 1)
        for j in idx[1:]:
            if local_path_free(V[i], V[j]):
                E.append((i, j))
    return V, E
```

The two knobs that matter are the *sampling distribution* — uniform sampling fails in narrow passages, motivating bridge-test, obstacle-based, and Gaussian sampling biased toward C-obs boundaries — and the *connection strategy*: PRM*'s analysis pins the radius to r(n) = γ (log n / n)^{1/d}, the threshold where the random geometric graph stays connected with only logarithmic degrees [4].

### 3.2 Incremental tree growth (RRT)

RRT grows a tree T rooted at q-init. Each iteration draws q-rand uniformly, finds the nearest node q-near, and extends a fixed step η toward q-rand; if collision-free, the new node joins the tree. The *Voronoi bias* — frontier nodes own large Voronoi cells and are selected with high probability — drives rapid expansion into unexplored space [2]. Goal bias and bidirectional growth are the standard accelerations.

### 3.3 Optimal rewiring (RRT*)

RRT* adds two operations inside a *near ball* of radius r(n) = min{γ (log n / n)^{1/d}, η} around each new node [4]:

1. **ChooseParent:** select the near node minimizing cost-to-come plus edge cost, replacing RRT's nearest-node rule.
2. **Rewire:** reparent any near node whose cost-to-come improves through q-new, propagating the improvement down its subtree.

```python
def rrt_star_extend(T, q_new, r_n, cost, steer_cost, obstacle_free):
    near = T.neighbors_within(q_new, r_n)
    q_min, c_min = None, float("inf")          # 1. choose parent
    for q in near:
        c = cost[q] + steer_cost(q, q_new)
        if c < c_min and obstacle_free(q, q_new):
            q_min, c_min = q, c
    T.add_vertex(q_new); T.add_edge(q_min, q_new); cost[q_new] = c_min
    for q in near:                             # 2. rewire neighbors
        if cost[q_new] + steer_cost(q_new, q) < cost[q] \
                and obstacle_free(q_new, q):
            T.reparent(q, q_new); cost[q] = cost[q_new] + steer_cost(q_new, q)
    return q_new
```

The constant γ is constrained by theory: γ > 2(1 + 1/d)^{1/d} (μ(C-free)/ζ_d)^{1/d}, with μ Lebesgue measure and ζ_d the unit *d*-ball volume [4].

### 3.4 The kinodynamic extension

Kinodynamic planning lifts the problem to *state space* X with dynamics ẋ = f(x, u). The local planner becomes a *steering function* solving a two-point boundary-value problem. When exact optimal steering exists — linear systems with quadratic cost — RRT* generalizes directly to *kinodynamic RRT** [5]. With no steering function (general nonlinear dynamics), *SST* forward-propagates random controls and sparsifies via witness-based pruning, recovering asymptotic optimality from simulation alone [7].

```haskell
-- Sampling-based planning as a probabilistic graph computation
type Config = [Double]
data Planner m = Planner
  { sample   :: m Config                                -- distribution over C
  , steer    :: Config -> Config -> m (Maybe [Config])  -- local planner / BVP
  , nearBall :: Int -> Double                           -- r(n) = gamma*(log n/n)^(1/d)
  }

rrtStarStep :: MonadRandom m => Planner m -> Tree -> m Tree
rrtStarStep p t = do
  qRand <- sample p
  mPath <- steer p (nearest t qRand) qRand
  case mPath of
    Nothing   -> return t
    Just path -> return (insertWithRewire p t (last path) (nearBall p (size t)))
```

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 PRM and the geometry of narrow passages

The enduring theoretical contribution of the PRM literature is the *analysis of why sampling works*. The notion of **ε-expansiveness** formalizes the intuition: a free space is expansive if every connected subset "sees" a large-volume portion of the space. Under expansiveness, PRM's failure probability decays exponentially in the number of samples — but with a base that degrades as passages narrow [1]:

> **Theorem (Expansiveness and PRM success):** If C-free is ε-expansive, the probability that a PRM roadmap with *n* uniform samples fails to connect two configurations in the same component is at most (C/ε) exp(−c ε n) for constants C, c > 0 [1].

Hence the *narrow-passage problem*: where critical width is small, uniform sampling must yield to *importance sampling* — the bridge test, which concentrates samples inside passages, or medial-axis retractors, which push samples onto the skeleton of C-free where clearance is locally maximal. These are variance-reduction techniques for the Monte Carlo estimation of connectivity.

### 4.2 RRT: Voronoi bias and rapid exploration

RRT's speed comes from a geometric accident: selecting q-near as the nearest tree node to a uniform sample selects nodes with probability proportional to their *Voronoi cell* volume. Frontier nodes have enormous cells; interior nodes tiny ones. The tree therefore expands outward aggressively [2].

The same mechanism explains RRT's weakness: it explores brilliantly and refines poorly. Selection follows Voronoi volume, so the tree keeps pushing into new territory rather than improving existing branches — precisely why RRT's solution cost converges to a non-optimal limit [4]. The algorithm is, in a precise sense, *designed* to stop caring about quality once feasibility is achieved.

A subtle property is *metric sensitivity*: in kinodynamic RRT, "nearest" must respect the dynamics, since Euclidean distance can select a node from which the target is unreachable. Implementations use weighted metrics or linearized cost-to-go as a pseudometric [3][7].

### 4.3 RRT*: rewiring, random geometric graphs, asymptotic optimality

The Karaman–Frazzoli breakthrough rests on one reduction: as samples grow, the graph implicitly considered by PRM*/RRT* converges to a *random geometric graph* — i.i.d. vertices, edges within radius r(n). Classical results (Penrose) place the connectivity threshold at ~(log n / n)^{1/d}; Karaman and Frazzoli showed *the same threshold governs optimality* [4]:

> **Theorem (Asymptotic optimality of RRT*):** For Lipschitz cost and positive-measure goal region, if r(n) = γ(log n/n)^{1/d} with γ above 2(1+1/d)^{1/d}(μ(C-free)/ζ_d)^{1/d}, the best path cost in the RRT* tree converges to the optimum *almost surely* as n → ∞ [4].

The proof has three movements. First, RRG (the graph version) *traces* any robustly optimal path: covering it with overlapping balls, each receiving a sample with probability 1 − exp(−Ω(n)). Second, RRT* is cost-equivalent to RRG — rewiring only improves on the graph's cost structure, so the tree restriction loses nothing asymptotically. Third, Borel–Cantelli upgrades convergence in probability to almost-sure convergence. The negative result for RRT runs the machinery in reverse: with one parent per node and no rewiring, the tree's costs freeze at insertion time, converging to the cost of the *first* feasible homotopy class discovered — almost surely suboptimal.

The rewiring invariant is a temporal safety property: every node's stored cost is an upper bound on the true optimal cost-to-come, and rewiring only decreases it:

```tla+
CostConsistent == \A v \in tree :
    /\ cost[v] >= OptimalCostToCome(v)
    /\ (v = root => cost[v] = 0)

Rewire(v, w) ==                              \* reparent v through w if improving
  /\ w \in NearBall(v)
  /\ cost[w] + EdgeCost(w, v) < cost[v]
  /\ parent' = [parent EXCEPT ![v] = w]
  /\ cost'   = PropagateImprovement(cost, parent', v)
  /\ UNCHANGED tree

Spec == CostConsistent /\ [][\E v, w \in tree : Rewire(v, w)]_<<tree,parent,cost>>
=============================================================================
```

### 4.4 Kinodynamic planning and optimal control

Geometric planners join configurations with straight lines. Robots with dynamics — cars, quadrotors, torque-limited arms — need *trajectories* satisfying ẋ = f(x, u). Kinodynamic RRT [3] replaced steering with *random control propagation*: apply a random control for a random duration from q-near, keeping collision-free results. Probabilistically complete for systems with drift, but with no optimality.

*Kinodynamic RRT** [5] recovers optimality for **linear systems** ẋ = Ax + Bu with cost c = ∫(1 + uᵀRu) dt: steering becomes a linear-quadratic boundary-value problem with closed-form solution via the controllability Gramian. Webb and van den Berg proved asymptotic optimality for any controllable linear system, demonstrating it on a 10-D linearized quadrotor [5]. For general nonlinear dynamics, **SST** abandons steering — random forward propagation plus witness-based sparsity pruning yields asymptotic optimality from simulation alone, at the price of slower convergence where a BVP solver exists [7].

| Dynamics class | Steering available | Optimal planner | Key mechanism |
|---|---|---|---|
| Holonomic / geometric | Straight line | RRT*, PRM* [4] | Rewiring in r(n)-ball |
| Linear controllable | LQ BVP, closed form | Kinodynamic RRT* [5] | Gramian-based steering |
| General nonlinear | None (forward sim) | SST* [7] | Witness pruning + propagation |

### 4.5 Collision checking: the hidden dominant cost

In deployed planners, 70–90% of wall-clock time goes to one predicate: *is this configuration or segment collision-free?* Theory counts *samples*; engineering counts *collision checks*, which differ by the near-ball degree — O(log n) per RRT* iteration [4]. Three ideas dominate:

1. **Hierarchical bounding volumes.** AABB/OBB trees (FCL, PQP) cull most checks before touching triangle geometry; self-collision matrices prune link pairs that can never collide.
2. **Lazy evaluation.** Lazy PRM/RRT* insert edges *unchecked* and validate only those on candidate solution paths — deferring the dominant cost to where it matters.
3. **Continuous checking.** Discrete segment checks at resolution δ can *tunnel* through thin obstacles. Conservative advancement and swept volumes bound inter-sample motion, trading cost for soundness.

This is why nearest-neighbor structures (kd-trees, learned partitioners) are first-class citizens in planner implementations: per-iteration cost is dominated by neighborhood queries and the collision checks they trigger.

## 5 Empirical Evaluation and Proofs

### 5.1 What the theorems actually promise

Asymptotic optimality guarantees c_n → c* almost surely — but *no finite-time rate*. Convergence can be arbitrarily slow in pathological scenes, and no bound of the form |c_n − c*| ≤ f(n) holds without strong clearance assumptions [4]. Practitioners rely on *anytime* behavior instead: RRT* finds a first feasible path as fast as RRT, then improves monotonically, so it can be stopped at any time with the best solution so far.

The strongest finite-time result is for *obstacle-free* Informed RRT*: uniform sampling of the informed subset yields *linear* expected convergence to the minimum [6]. With obstacles, the informed set still contains every potentially improving state — preserving asymptotic optimality — but rates depend on scene geometry.

### 5.2 Benchmark findings

Standard protocol: fixed scenes varying dimension, obstacle density, and passage width; fixed time budgets; 50–100 seeds reporting median cost, success rate, and time to first solution. Established findings:

- **Feasibility speed:** Bidirectional RRT-Connect dominates time-to-first-solution, often by an order of magnitude over unidirectional RRT [2].
- **Optimal convergence:** Informed RRT* reaches within 5% of optimum 2–10× faster than RRT* in wall-clock time, the advantage growing in larger workspaces [6].
- **Kinodynamic scaling:** Kinodynamic RRT* converges on 10-D quadrotor models within seconds; SST needs substantially more samples without steering [5][7].
- **Dimensionality:** All planners degrade gracefully — polynomially, not exponentially. The radius r(n) ∝ (log n/n)^{1/d} shrinks more slowly in high *d*, so more samples precede effective rewiring [4]. That polynomial scaling *is* the victory over exact methods.

### 5.3 Proof sketch: sharpness of the (log n/n)^{1/d} radius

Below (log n/n)^{1/d}, the random geometric graph fragments into isolated components with probability approaching one — no edge-restricted planner can even be complete. Above the threshold, a *tracing* argument applies: cover a robustly optimal path σ* (clearance δ > 0) with overlapping balls of radius ρ < δ/2; each ball receives a sample with probability 1 − exp(−Ω(n)); chaining samples yields a path exceeding c(σ*) by O(ρ); letting ρ → 0 gives almost-sure convergence [4]. The argument separates the *geometric* question (does the sample set trace the optimum?) from the *algorithmic* one (does the planner find the trace?).

## 6 Limitations and Open Problems

**The finite-time gap.** Asymptotic optimality without rates is cold comfort for a robot planning in 100 ms. Problem-dependent finite-time bounds relating clearance, dimension, and iteration count remain the most important open theoretical problem. *Deterministic* sampling (low-dispersion sequences) offers one path — deterministic completeness with explicit error bounds — at the cost of anytime adaptivity.

**Learned samplers.** Every result above assumes a fixed, usually uniform, sampling distribution — yet the optimal distribution is scene-dependent. Learned samplers (conditional VAEs, diffusion models predicting promising regions) show dramatic empirical speedups but break the i.i.d. assumptions underpinning all completeness and optimality proofs. A theory of *adaptive* sampling with preserved guarantees is nascent and urgently needed.

**Dynamics and uncertainty.** SST brought optimality to deterministic nonlinear dynamics [7], but real robots face stochastic dynamics, partial observability, and moving obstacles. Belief-space and chance-constrained RRT* variants exist, yet their foundations are far thinner than the deterministic theory.

**From paths to policies.** Sampling-based planners emit open-loop trajectories; execution needs a tracking controller, and the separation is brittle under large disturbances. Marrying sampling-based optimality with *feedback* motion planning — funnels, LQR-trees — is largely open.

**Collision checking as the true bottleneck.** Theory counts samples; practice counts collision checks. Tighter integration with *learned* collision proxies (neural implicit surfaces with conservative error bounds) could shift the Pareto frontier — but only with the soundness guarantees safety-critical deployment demands.

## 7 Conclusion

Sampling-based motion planning succeeded by asking for less — probabilistic rather than deterministic guarantees — and thereby achieving more: practical planning where exact methods cannot operate. From the *engineering breakthrough* of PRM [1], through the *algorithmic insight* of RRT [2][3] whose Voronoi bias turned random sampling into rapid exploration, to the *theoretical deepening* of Karaman and Frazzoli [4], who revealed sampling-based planners as random geometric graphs — the field matured from a bag of tricks into a principled discipline, extended by kinodynamic steering [5], focused sampling [6], and steering-free optimality [7].

The frontier is no longer whether these planners work, but *how fast they converge*, *what they assume about samplers*, and *how they handle dynamics and uncertainty*. The next leap will likely mirror the last: reducing some heuristic practice — learned sampling, neural collision proxies — to a clean mathematical object with provable guarantees. Until then, the practitioner's lesson stands: the algorithm is half the system; the sampling distribution, the collision checker, and the steering function are where planning is truly won or lost.
