# The Post-Quantum Cryptography Transition: MLWE Hardness and Module-Lattice Constructions, the FIPS 203/204/205 Standards Suite, Code- and Hash-Based Alternatives, and Hybrid TLS 1.3 Deployment

## Abstract

On August 13, 2024, NIST finalized FIPS 203 (ML-KEM), FIPS 204 (ML-DSA), and FIPS 205 (SLH-DSA), concluding an eight-year post-quantum standardization effort. This thesis gives a rigorous, deployment-oriented treatment of the migration from number-theoretic to quantum-resistant asymmetric cryptography. We develop the Module Learning With Errors (MLWE) problem from Regev's original LWE through Ring-LWE to the module-lattice setting, explaining the worst-case-to-average-case reductions that underpin lattice-based schemes. We dissect the Kyber-to-ML-KEM key-encapsulation mechanism, including its Fujisaki-Okamoto transform to IND-CCA security, and the Dilithium-to-ML-DSA Fiat-Shamir-with-aborts signature scheme with its uniform-sampling, constant-time design. We contrast these with stateless hash-based SLH-DSA and code-based alternatives (Classic McEliece, BIKE, HQC), and analyze hybrid TLS 1.3 key exchange (X25519MLKEM768) as the practical vehicle against harvest-now-decrypt-later attacks. Benchmarks, concrete attack costs, and the limitations of each family are treated quantitatively.

## 1. Introduction

The security of nearly all deployed public-key cryptography rests on two problems: integer factorization and the discrete logarithm. Shor's polynomial-time quantum algorithm for both problems [1] converts this foundation from bedrock to quicksand: a cryptographically relevant quantum computer (CRQC) would collapse RSA, Diffie-Hellman, ECDSA, and EdDSA in hours. The threat is not purely futuristic. Adversaries practice *harvest now, decrypt later* — recording today's encrypted traffic for retrospective decryption once a CRQC exists — so data with long-lived confidentiality (state secrets, health records, intellectual property) is already exposed.

NIST's response, launched in 2016, was an open international competition that evaluated 82 submissions over three rounds [7]. On July 5, 2022, four winners were announced: CRYSTALS-Kyber for key encapsulation, and CRYSTALS-Dilithium, FALCON, and SPHINCS+ for signatures. Two years of hardening later, on **August 13, 2024**, the first three standards became final:

- **FIPS 203** — Module-Lattice-Based Key-Encapsulation Mechanism Standard (ML-KEM), derived from CRYSTALS-Kyber [7];
- **FIPS 204** — Module-Lattice-Based Digital Signature Standard (ML-DSA), derived from CRYSTALS-Dilithium [8];
- **FIPS 205** — Stateless Hash-Based Digital Signature Standard (SLH-DSA), derived from SPHINCS+ [9].

A fourth round continues for key-encapsulation diversity: on March 11, 2025, NIST selected the code-based **HQC** as the backup KEM to ML-KEM [14], while Classic McEliece and BIKE remain instructive alternatives. FALCON is expected to become FIPS 206 (FN-DSA).

This thesis is organized around three questions: *why should we believe lattices are hard?* (Section 4.1), *how do the standardized constructions actually work?* (Sections 4.2–4.4), and *what are the alternatives and how do we deploy them?* (Sections 4.5–6).

## 2. Background

### 2.1 The Learning With Errors problem

Regev's LWE problem [2] is deceptively simple. Fix integers *n* (dimension), *q* (modulus), and an error distribution χ over Z_q. Given a matrix **A** ← Z_q^{m×n} and **b** = **A s** + **e** (mod *q*) with **e** ← χ^m, recover the secret **s**. The *search* variant asks for **s**; the *decision* variant asks to distinguish (**A**, **b**) from uniform. Without the error term the problem is linear algebra; with it, it becomes a bounded-distance decoding problem in a random *q*-ary lattice — and, crucially, Regev proved it **as hard in the average case as approximating worst-case lattice problems** such as GapSVP and SIVP to within polynomial factors:

> **Theorem (Regev [2]):** If there exists an efficient algorithm solving decision-LWE with modulus *q* = poly(*n*), then there exists an efficient quantum algorithm approximating GapSVP and SIVP on *any n*-dimensional lattice to within Õ(*nq*) factors.

This worst-case-to-average-case reduction is the intellectual crown jewel of lattice cryptography: breaking a deployed LWE instance implies breaking the hardest instances of a class.

### 2.2 Ring and module variants

Plain LWE carries quadratic key sizes (a public key is an *m* × *n* matrix), motivating algebraic structure. **Ring-LWE** [3] replaces Z_q^n with the cyclotomic ring R_q = Z_q[X]/(X^n + 1), compressing keys by a factor of *n* while preserving reductions against worst-case problems on *ideal* lattices. **Module-LWE** generalizes further: secrets and errors are vectors over R_q^k, for a module rank *k*. Langlois and Stehlé [4] proved that Module-LWE over rank-*k* modules reduces to worst-case problems on module lattices of rank ≥ *k*, smoothly interpolating between LWE (*k* = 1 over Z_q... more precisely the ring is Z) and Ring-LWE (*k* = 1 over R_q). MLWE is the assumption underlying both Kyber and Dilithium, and hence ML-KEM and ML-DSA.

The complementary **Module-SIS** (Short Integer Solution) problem — find a short nonzero **z** with **A z** = 0 mod *q* — underlies unforgeability of Dilithium/ML-DSA signatures.

### 2.3 The Number Theoretic Transform

Polynomial multiplication in R_q dominates runtime. With *n* = 256 and moduli chosen so that X^256 + 1 splits completely — *q* = 3329 (Kyber) and *q* = 8380417 (Dilithium) — the Number Theoretic Transform gives quasi-linear Õ(*n* log *n*) multiplication, and is the target of nearly all implementation optimization [6].

| Notation | Meaning |
|---|---|
| R_q | Z_q[X]/(X^256 + 1), the base ring |
| η | bound on centered binomial error/secret distributions |
| IND-CCA | indistinguishability under adaptive chosen-ciphertext attack |
| QROM | quantum random oracle model |
| Core SVP | cost unit: one call to an SVP oracle in the relevant dimension |

## 3. Methodology

Our analysis proceeds in four layers:

1. **Definitions and reductions.** We restate the standardized schemes from their academic specifications [5, 6, 10] and trace each security claim to its underlying problem (MLWE, Module-SIS, hash-function properties).
2. **Concrete security.** We translate asymptotic reductions into attack costs using the Core SVP methodology: the best known primal and dual lattice attacks (BKZ with sieving) are costed in Core SVP units, and NIST security categories (1, 2, 3, 5) map to AES-128/192/256 and SHA3-256 equivalents.
3. **Parameter accounting.** Sizes, failure probabilities, and operation counts are taken from the final FIPS documents [7, 8, 9], not drafts.
4. **Deployment analysis.** We evaluate migration vehicles — hybrid key exchange in TLS 1.3 [12, 13] and X.509 composite approaches — against protocol constraints (handshake size, fragmentation, HSM support).

```python
# Toy illustration: LWE sample generation over a small modulus
import random
q, n, m = 97, 8, 16
s = [random.randrange(q) for _ in range(n)]          # secret
A = [[random.randrange(q) for _ in range(n)] for _ in range(m)]
def sample_noise():                                  # tiny discrete error
    return random.choice([-2, -1, 0, 1, 2])
b = [(sum(A[i][j]*s[j] for j in range(n)) + sample_noise()) % q
     for i in range(m)]                              # b = A s + e (mod q)
```

The snippet above is pedagogical: real schemes use centered binomial or Gaussian noise, moduli of thousands to millions, and dimensions in the hundreds — but the algebraic skeleton is identical.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Module-LWE: the hardness foundation

The Module-LWE assumption states that for A ← R_q^{k×k} uniform, s ← χ^{k} short, and e ← χ^{k} short, the pair (A, **t** = A**s** + **e**) is computationally indistinguishable from uniform. Two attack families matter:

- **Primal attacks** embed the LWE instance in a lattice of dimension ≈ *kn* and run BKZ to recover the short error vector. Cost is dominated by the SVP oracle dimension β; modern estimates use the Core SVP model with sieving at 2^{0.292β} classical / 2^{0.265β} quantum operations.
- **Dual attacks** distinguish (A, t) from uniform by finding short vectors in the dual lattice, requiring many samples.

For ML-KEM-768 (*k* = 3, *n* = 256, *q* = 3329, η = 2), the best-known attacks cost on the order of 2^{180}–2^{215} Core SVP operations, conservatively mapped to NIST **Category 3** (192-bit classical security, ≈ AES-192 key search) [5, 7]. ML-KEM-512 targets Category 1 and ML-KEM-1024 Category 5. The analysis assumes lattice reduction behaves as predicted by the Geometric Series Assumption and that no structural attack exploits the ring structure — a bet hedged by keeping *k* ≥ 2 and avoiding overly aggressive parameters.

### 4.2 Kyber → ML-KEM (FIPS 203)

Kyber [5] is most naturally understood as a noisy ElGamal over module lattices. The CPA-secure public-key encryption at its core:

- **KeyGen:** A ← R_q^{k×k} (expanded from a seed), s, e ← short; pk = (A, **t** = A**s** + **e**), sk = **s**.
- **Encrypt(pk, m):** sample r, e_1, e_2 short; **u** = A^T**r** + **e_1**, v = **t**^T**r** + e_2 + ⌈q/2⌋·m; compress (**u**, v) to save bandwidth.
- **Decrypt:** m ≈ v − **s**^T**u**, rounding away the accumulated noise.

The Fujisaki–Okamoto transform then converts this CPA-PKE into an IND-CCA-secure KEM: encapsulation encrypts a random message *m*, derives the shared secret K = H(m, H(pk)), and — critically — the decapsulation algorithm **re-encrypts** the recovered *m* and rejects on mismatch, defeating chosen-ciphertext attacks in both the classical and quantum random oracle models [5].

| Parameter set | Module rank *k* | Public key | Ciphertext | NIST category |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| ML-KEM-512 | 2 | 800 B | 768 B | 1 |
| ML-KEM-768 | 3 | 1184 B | 1080 B | 3 |
| ML-KEM-1024 | 4 | 1568 B | 1568 B | 5 |

All share *n* = 256, *q* = 3329, and a 32-byte shared secret. ML-KEM-768 is the recommended default, balancing Category-3 security against handshake sizes that fit comfortably in a TLS ClientHello.

### 4.3 Dilithium → ML-DSA (FIPS 204)

Dilithium [6] builds on the Fiat–Shamir-with-aborts paradigm. Key generation samples short **s**_1, **s**_2 and publishes **t** = A**s**_1 + **s**_2 (an MLWE instance, hiding the secret). Signing samples a masking vector **y** uniformly, computes **w** = A**y**, hashes (**w**, message) to a sparse challenge polynomial *c*, and forms **z** = **y** + c**s**_1. Rejection sampling then restarts unless **z** and the low bits of **w** − c**s**_2 stay within bounds — the "aborts" that make the signature distribution independent of the secret.

Two design decisions were decisive for standardization. First, Dilithium uses **uniform sampling only**: no discrete Gaussians, whose timing side channels had broken earlier lattice signatures [6]. Second, every operation is straightforwardly constant-time, including the NTT over *q* = 8380417.

| Parameter set | (*k*, ℓ) | Public key | Signature | Security |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| ML-DSA-44 | (4, 4) | 1312 B | 2420 B | Category 2 |
| ML-DSA-65 | (6, 5) | 1952 B | 3309 B | Category 3 |
| ML-DSA-87 | (8, 7) | 2592 B | 4627 B | Category 5 |

Security rests on three problems jointly: MLWE (key recovery), Module-SIS (forgery via short vectors), and the newer SelfTargetMSIS assumption, whose quantum hardness was subsequently reduced to MLWE in the QROM. ML-DSA-65 is the recommended general-purpose choice.

### 4.4 SPHINCS+ → SLH-DSA (FIPS 205)

SLH-DSA [9] is deliberately different mathematics — a hedge in case lattices fail. It is a *stateless* hash-based signature built from three primitives [10]:

1. **WOTS+** — a one-time signature from hash chains;
2. **FORS** — a few-time signature ("forest of random subsets") that signs message digests;
3. **XMSS hypertree** — layers of Merkle trees whose leaves are WOTS+ instances, chaining FORS public keys up to a single root.

Signing picks a random hypertree leaf (statelessness: no counter to maintain, unlike stateful XMSS), signs the digest with FORS, then authenticates the FORS key up the hypertree with WOTS+ signatures and Merkle paths. Security reduces *only* to properties of the underlying hash function (second-preimage resistance, pseudorandomness) — no number theory, no lattices.

The price is size and speed: signatures range from ≈ 7.9 KB (128s) to ≈ 49.9 KB (256f), with signing 10–100× slower than ML-DSA. Twelve parameter sets (128/192/256 × small/fast × SHA2/SHAKE) let applications trade size against speed. SLH-DSA is standardized explicitly as the **backup** signature scheme [9] — slower and larger, but resting on the most conservative assumption available.

### 4.5 Code-based alternatives: McEliece, BIKE, HQC

Code-based cryptography predates lattices by decades: McEliece's 1978 system hides a binary Goppa code behind a random-looking generator matrix **G'** = **S G P**. Encryption adds a weight-*t* error vector; the legitimate receiver uses the hidden Goppa decoder to remove it. The security assumption — decoding a random linear code (syndrome decoding) — has resisted 45+ years of cryptanalysis, the longest track record in post-quantum cryptography [11]. Classic McEliece (the Niederreiter dual variant) offers perfect correctness and blazing-fast encryption, but public keys of 261 KB to 1.3 MB:

| Parameter set | Public key | Ciphertext | Security |
|---|---|---|---|
| mceliece348864 | 261,120 B | 96 B | Category 1 |
| mceliece460896 | 524,160 B | 156 B | Category 3 |
| mceliece8192128 | 1,357,824 B | 208 B | Category 5 |

The fourth NIST round (BIKE, Classic McEliece, HQC — after SIKE was classically broken in August 2022) concluded with **HQC selected on March 11, 2025** as the backup KEM [14], a structured-code scheme with keys an order of magnitude smaller than McEliece. A draft HQC standard is expected in 2026 with finalization in 2027.

## 5. Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 Size and performance comparison

Representative figures on a modern 64-bit CPU (reference/optimized C implementations):

| Scheme | Role | Public key | Signature/Ciphertext | Keygen | Sign/Encaps | Verify/Decaps |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| X25519 (classical) | KEM | 32 B | 32 B | — | ~0.02 ms | ~0.05 ms |
| ML-KEM-768 | KEM | 1184 B | 1080 B | ~0.03 ms | ~0.04 ms | ~0.05 ms |
| ML-DSA-65 | SIG | 1952 B | 3309 B | ~0.05 ms | ~0.15 ms | ~0.07 ms |
| SLH-DSA-128s | SIG | 32 B | 7856 B | ~0.1 ms | ~10 ms | ~1 ms |
| mceliece348864 | KEM | 261 KB | 96 B | ~100 ms | ~0.03 ms | ~0.3 ms |

Two facts stand out. First, lattice operations are *fast* — ML-KEM-768 encapsulates in tens of microseconds, competitive with X25519. Second, the migration cost is bandwidth, not CPU: a TLS handshake carrying ML-KEM-768 plus an ML-DSA-65 certificate chain moves roughly 10–15 KB instead of ~1 KB.

### 5.2 Proof sketches

> **Theorem (Kyber IND-CCA [5]):** The ML-KEM KEM is IND-CCA secure in the (quantum) random oracle model if the Module-LWE problem is hard for the chosen parameters.

*Sketch.* The FO transform is tight: any CCA adversary distinguishing real from random session keys either breaks the CPA security of the underlying PKE (reducing to decision-MLWE) or finds a hash collision. The re-encryption check makes decryption queries useless without the randomness used at encapsulation.

> **Theorem (SPHINCS+ EU-CMA [10]):** The hypertree construction is existentially unforgeable if the tweakable hash functions are second-preimage resistant and pseudorandom, with a security loss linear in the number of FORS instances.

*Sketch.* A forgery must either forge a WOTS+ one-time signature on some hypertree node (breaking the hash chain), forge FORS on the message digest, or find a second preimage for a Merkle node. The hypertree composes these bounds multiplicatively across layers.

### 5.3 Hybrid TLS 1.3: the deployment vehicle

Pure-PQC migration is risky while implementations are young, so the IETF standardized **hybrid key exchange**: concatenate a classical and a post-quantum share, deriving security from *either* component [13]. The adopted groups are X25519MLKEM768 (codepoint 0x11EC), SecP256r1MLKEM768 (0x11EB), and SecP384r1MLKEM1024 [12], with the combiner simply concatenating the ML-KEM ciphertext and the ECDH share into the TLS 1.3 `key_exchange` field:

```rust
// Wire layout of an X25519MLKEM768 key share (draft-ietf-tls-ecdhe-mlkem)
encaps_key   = mlkem_pk   || x25519_pk;    // 1184 + 32  = 1216 bytes
ciphertext   = mlkem_ct   || x25519_eph;   // 1080 + 32  = 1112 bytes
shared_secret = mlkem_ss  || x25519_ss;    // 32   + 32   = 64 bytes
```

This hybrid was already battle-tested at scale: Cloudflare and Google deployed X25519Kyber768 (the draft-00 predecessor) across production traffic in 2023–2024 [12], exercising fragmentation handling for the ~1.2 KB key shares before the final standard landed.

## 6. Limitations

- **Bandwidth inflation.** ML-DSA certificates are 20–100× larger than ECDSA; large chains trigger TCP retransmission and middlebox fragmentation bugs observed during the hybrid-TLS experiments [12].
- **Side channels.** Constant-time code is necessary but not sufficient: Dilithium's signing is probabilistic (rejection sampling), and power/EM analysis of the NTT remains an active attack surface. Masked implementations exist but carry significant overhead [6].
- **New assumptions.** ML-DSA's SelfTargetMSIS is younger than MLWE/MSIS; SLH-DSA's conservative hash assumption is precisely why it exists as a backup.
- **Code-based bulk.** Classic McEliece's 1 MB keys exclude it from TLS but suit static deployments (firmware signing, long-lived VPNs). HQC's standardization completes no earlier than 2027 [14].
- **Migration inertia.** X.509, HSMs, FIPS 140-3 validation, and embedded stacks turn over on decade timescales; crypto-agility — algorithm identifiers negotiated at runtime, as TLS 1.3's `supported_groups` already provides — is the actual deliverable of this transition, not any single algorithm.
- **Harvest-now-decrypt-later asymmetry.** Key exchange must migrate *before* CRQCs; signatures can migrate *after*, since a future forgery cannot rewrite a verified past — but code-signing and PKI roots have decade-long trust horizons that demand early movement.

## 7. Conclusion

The August 2024 finalization of FIPS 203/204/205 [7, 8, 9] marks the end of the beginning, not the end, of the post-quantum transition. Module lattices won the first standards round on the strength of Regev-style reductions [2, 4], two decades of cryptanalysis, and engineering that makes ML-KEM-768 as fast as X25519 at the cost of kilobytes. Hash-based SLH-DSA provides assumption diversity at the cost of size and speed; code-based HQC will provide KEM diversity by 2027; hybrid TLS 1.3 [12, 13] provides the deployment ramp. The deepest lesson is architectural: the transition's success is measured not by whether Module-LWE survives the next thirty years, but by whether our protocols can swap algorithms in thirty days when it doesn't. Crypto-agility — negotiated, hybrid, and validated — is the standard that matters most.
