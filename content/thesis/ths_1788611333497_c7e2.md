# Chained-Phase Optimistic BFT State Machine Replication: HotStuff-2's Two-Chain Commit Rule, Threshold-Signature Linearity, Pacemaker Liveness under Partial Synchrony, and Comparative Analysis with PBFT, Tendermint, and Narwhal–Bullshark

## Abstract

This thesis presents a rigorous treatment of pipelined Byzantine fault tolerant state machine replication (BFT SMR) through the lens of HotStuff-2, the two-phase refinement of the HotStuff protocol family. We formalize the transition from HotStuff's three-phase chained commit discipline—prepare, pre-commit, commit, with a three-consecutive-view commit rule—to HotStuff-2's two-phase regime and its two-chain commit rule, proving that two phases suffice for responsive, linearly-communicating BFT under partial synchrony. Central mechanisms are analyzed in depth: *optimistic responsiveness*, whereby a correct leader drives consensus at actual network speed; linear authenticator complexity via *(n−f)-out-of-n* threshold signatures aggregated into quorum certificates (QCs); the *pacemaker* abstraction that synchronizes view progression after the global stabilization time (GST) to guarantee liveness; and the *locked-QC* voting discipline that preserves safety across view changes. We prove safety by quorum intersection and liveness by pacemaker induction, quantify latency in network hops, and compare empirically and analytically against PBFT, Tendermint, Fast-HotStuff, and Narwhal–Bullshark DAG-based BFT. The analysis establishes HotStuff-2 as the current simplicity frontier for partially synchronous leader-based BFT while identifying its residual worst-case quadratic view-change cost, forking-attack throughput degradation, and the throughput-versus-latency trade-off relative to DAG-based designs.

## 1. Introduction

Byzantine fault tolerant state machine replication (BFT SMR) is the foundational primitive of permissioned blockchains and high-assurance distributed services: *n = 3f + 1* replicas, up to *f* of which may behave arbitrarily, must agree on a single totally ordered log of client commands and execute them deterministically [4]. The classical reference point, PBFT (Castro and Liskov, 1999), demonstrated that BFT could be practical—its replicated NFS service ran within 3% of an unreplicated one—but at the cost of *O(n²)* all-to-all messaging per phase and a notoriously intricate view-change sub-protocol whose worst-case cost and engineering complexity became the field's central pain point [4].

Two decades of research have attacked this cost along three axes: **communication complexity** (reducing per-decision authenticators from quadratic to linear), **responsiveness** (committing at the speed of the actual network rather than a conservative timeout bound), and **leader-rotation discipline** (making leader failover cheap enough to rotate leaders every view, thereby achieving chain quality and fairness for blockchain settings). HotStuff (Yin et al., 2019) was the first partially synchronous protocol to achieve *both* linearity and responsiveness simultaneously, via threshold-signature quorum certificates and a chained phase structure that folds view change into the normal case [1]. HotStuff-2 (Malkhi and Nayak, 2023) then removed an entire phase, showing that *two phases are enough for BFT after all*—achieving a two-phase commit regime within a view while preserving optimistic linearity, responsiveness, and *O(n²)* worst-case communication [2][3].

This thesis reconstructs the full technical argument. Our contributions are:

1. A unified formalization of **chained HotStuff** (three-phase, three-chain commit) and **HotStuff-2** (two-phase, two-chain commit), with explicit pseudocode and a TLA⁺ specification of the voting rule.
2. A proof of **safety** via quorum intersection and the locked-QC invariant, and a proof of **liveness** via the pacemaker abstraction under partial synchrony.
3. A quantitative **latency accounting** in network hops and a communication-complexity analysis distinguishing optimistic, view-change, and worst-case regimes.
4. An **empirical synthesis** drawing on published measurements: HotStuff at 100+ replicas versus BFT-SMaRt [1], HotStuff-2 versus HotStuff head-to-head implementations [5], the HotStuff/Tendermint bottleneck decomposition [6], and forking-attack throughput collapse in Fast-HotStuff [7].
5. A systematic **comparison** with PBFT [4], Tendermint, and Narwhal–Bullshark DAG-based BFT [8], locating HotStuff-2 on the simplicity–throughput–latency Pareto frontier.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 System model

We adopt the standard partially synchronous model of Dwork, Lynch, and Stockmeyer: there exists an unknown *global stabilization time* (GST) after which message delays between correct replicas are bounded by a known Δ, but before GST the network may be fully asynchronous [1][2]. Up to *f* of *n = 3f + 1* replicas are Byzantine. Safety (agreement and validity) must hold in all executions; liveness (termination) is required only after GST. Clients submit commands; a command is *committed* when a correct replica executes it, and clients learn commitment from *f + 1* matching replies.

> **Theorem (Quorum intersection):** In a system with *n = 3f + 1* replicas, any two quorums of size *n − f = 2f + 1* intersect in at least *f + 1* replicas, hence in at least one correct replica. This is the combinatorial bedrock of every safety argument below.

### 2.2 PBFT: the three-phase baseline

PBFT proceeds in *views*, each with a designated primary. The normal case has three phases—**pre-prepare**, **prepare**, **commit**—each requiring a quorum of *2f + 1* matching messages, with all-to-all broadcast in the latter two phases yielding *O(n²)* authenticators per decision [4]. The view-change protocol is the expensive part: on primary failure, replicas exchange view-change messages carrying their prepared state, and the new primary must assemble an *O(n²)*-sized proof to justify its proposal. PBFT also lacks *responsiveness* in the strict sense used here: although its normal case advances at network speed, its timeout machinery and the quadratic view change dominate at scale.

### 2.3 Tendermint: gossip-based round structure

Tendermint (Buchman, 2016) organizes consensus into *heights* and *rounds*, each round having propose, prevote, and precommit steps with *gossip* (all-to-all) dissemination, hence *O(n²)* communication per round even in the good case. Its locking rule—a replica locks on a value after seeing *2f + 1* prevotes (a *polka*) and only unlocks for a higher-round polka—is the direct ancestor of HotStuff's locked-QC discipline. Tendermint's commit additionally waits a fixed `TimeoutCommit` δ, sacrificing responsiveness for simplicity. Detailed bottleneck analysis shows these structural choices (gossip, fixed commit delay) account for most of Tendermint's latency and bandwidth overhead relative to HotStuff [6].

### 2.4 Threshold signatures and quorum certificates

A *(k, n)*-threshold signature scheme lets any *k* of *n* replicas produce signature *shares* on a message *m* such that any *k* shares combine into a single compact signature verifiable under one public key, while fewer than *k* shares reveal nothing [1]. HotStuff sets *k = n − f = 2f + 1*. A **quorum certificate (QC)** for a block *b* is such a combined signature over the tuple *(view, b.hash)*. Crucially, a QC is *O(1)* in size regardless of *n*, so a leader can *prove* quorum support to all replicas with a single broadcast: this converts PBFT's *O(n²)* phase into an *O(n)* star (replicas → leader → replicas) and is the entire basis of HotStuff's linear authenticator complexity [1][3].

## 3. Methodology

Our method is analytic and synthetic rather than experimental: we reconstruct the protocols from their primary sources [1][2][3], formalize the commit rules, prove safety and liveness from first principles, and integrate published empirical results [1][5][6][7] into a unified quantitative picture. We use three formal instruments:

- **Python pseudocode** for the chained commit-rule logic and QC formation, precise enough to be transliterated into an implementation.
- **TLA⁺** for the replica voting rule, exposing the exact guard conditions that enforce the locked-QC invariant.
- **Latency accounting in network hops**, measuring the longest causal chain of messages from client submission to finality confirmation, following the hop-counting methodology of the HotStuff-2 exposition [3].

We compare protocols along five dimensions: phases per view, commit rule, optimistic communication complexity, worst-case communication complexity, and responsiveness. All claims are tied to numbered references.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Chained HotStuff: three phases, pipelined

Basic HotStuff has three phases per view—**prepare**, **pre-commit**, **commit**—each collecting *n − f* votes into a QC. Chained HotStuff observes that the three phases are structurally identical (collect votes, form QC, broadcast) and *pipelines* them: the leader of view *v + 1* performs the pre-commit phase of view *v*'s proposal *while simultaneously* running its own prepare phase, by chaining its proposal to the QC it just formed. Each view therefore advances three in-flight proposals by one phase each [1].

Replicas maintain two QCs: `genericQC` (highest QC seen) and `lockedQC` (highest QC of a two-chain). The voting rule is: vote for a proposal *b* carrying `justify` only if `b` extends `lockedQC` (i.e., `justify.view ≥ lockedQC.view` and ancestry links through). The commit rule is the **three-chain rule**: commit *b* when three blocks *b, b′, b″* with *consecutive* view numbers form a chain, each certified. In code:

```python
def on_receive_proposal(b):
    # b.justify is a QC from the previous view
    assert verify_qc(b.justify)
    if b extends locked_qc:          # voting rule: never abandon a lock
        send_vote(leader(view + 1), sign(b))
        generic_qc = max(generic_qc, b.justify)
        if forms_one_chain(b):       # prepare phase of b succeeded
            generic_qc = b.justify
        if forms_two_chain(b):       # pre-commit phase of b succeeded
            locked_qc = b.justify
        if forms_three_chain(b):     # commit phase of b succeeded
            commit(b)                # execute and reply to client
```

The elegance is that *view change is free*: the leader rotates every view by round-robin, and the new leader simply extends the highest QC it knows. There is no separate view-change message pattern with *O(n²)* proofs as in PBFT; a failed leader merely means its view produces no QC, and the chain skips it [1][3].

### 4.2 HotStuff-2: two phases suffice

HotStuff-2's key insight is that the pre-commit phase exists only to solve one problem: **ensuring liveness during a view change**—a new leader must learn the highest locked QC so it does not propose a conflicting block that correct replicas, locked on the old QC, will refuse to vote for [2][3]. HotStuff solves this with an extra phase that locks replicas *before* the view ends. HotStuff-2 instead requires the *next leader to wait*: before proposing in view *v*, the leader collects `NEW-VIEW` messages carrying the highest QC from *n − f* replicas, and extends the maximum. Because any locked QC is held by at least *f + 1* correct replicas and the leader hears from *n − f* replicas, quorum intersection guarantees the leader learns every QC that could be locked [2].

With that wait in place, the protocol collapses to **two phases per view**: propose/vote → prepare-QC, then vote on the prepare-QC → commit-QC. The commit rule becomes the **two-chain rule**: a block *b* is committed when two consecutive views certify *b* and its child—equivalently, when a replica observes a commit-QC (a QC over a prepare-QC) for *b*. In the failure-free case this yields finality within **7 network hops** (client → leader → replicas → leader → replicas → leader → replicas → client), and the *expected* client wait for the next proposal is a single network delay due to pipelining [3].

```tla+
\* HotStuff-2 replica voting rule (simplified)
VoteFor(b) ==
    /\ VerifyQC(b.justify)
    /\ b.justify.view >= lockedQC.view   \* never vote against a lock
    /\ b.view = currentView
    /\ SendVote(Leader(currentView + 1), ShareSign(b))
    /\ genericQC' = MaxQC(genericQC, b.justify)
    /\ IF FormsTwoChain(b)
       THEN /\ lockedQC' = b.justify     \* prepare-QC locks replicas
            /\ committed' = committed \cup {b.ancestor}
       ELSE UNCHANGED <<lockedQC, committed>>
```

The trade is explicit and quantified: HotStuff-2 adds one half-phase of leader waiting (a linear `NEW-VIEW` collection) in *every* view, including the good case, but deletes an entire voting phase from the critical path. The authors prove this is **optimal**: no responsive BFT protocol with *O(n²)* worst-case communication can use fewer than two phases [2]. Head-to-head implementations confirm the payoff—HotStuff-2's codebase is substantially more compact and its efficiency superior, particularly at low Byzantine ratios [5].

### 4.3 Optimistic responsiveness and linear communication

*Responsiveness* (in the sense of [1]) means a correct leader drives consensus at the pace of *actual* network delay δ_actual rather than the worst-case bound Δ. Both HotStuff and HotStuff-2 are responsive in the good case: phases complete as soon as *n − f* votes arrive, with no timeout-gated steps on the critical path (unlike Tendermint's `TimeoutCommit`) [1][3][6]. *Optimistic* linearity means communication is *O(n)* per view when the leader is correct—*n − f* vote messages inbound, one QC broadcast outbound—while the worst case (cascading faulty leaders) degrades to *O(n²)*, because each of up to *f* consecutive faulty leaders can trigger a linear view-change attempt before a correct leader synchronizes the system [2][3]. The following table summarizes the protocol landscape:

| Protocol | Phases / view | Commit rule | Optimistic comm. | Worst-case comm. | Responsive |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| PBFT [4] | 3 (pre-prepare, prepare, commit) | 2f+1 commits | *O(n²)* | *O(n²)* view change | Partial |
| Tendermint | 3 (propose, prevote, precommit) + δ wait | 2f+1 precommits | *O(n²)* (gossip) | *O(n²)* | No (fixed δ) |
| HotStuff [1] | 3 chained (prepare, pre-commit, commit) | Three-chain | *O(n)* | *O(n²)* | Yes |
| HotStuff-2 [2] | 2 chained (prepare, commit) + leader wait | Two-chain | *O(n)* | *O(n²)* | Yes |
| Fast-HotStuff [7] | 2 (aggregated votes) | Two-chain + direct votes | *O(n)* | *O(n²)* | Yes |
| Narwhal–Bullshark [8] | DAG rounds + anchor commits | 2f+1 DAG votes / anchor | *O(n²)* (amortized) | *O(n²)* | Partial |

### 4.4 The pacemaker: liveness under partial synchrony

Safety in HotStuff-2 never depends on timing, but liveness does: after GST, correct replicas must *overlap* in the same view with a correct leader long enough for two phases to complete. The **pacemaker** is the abstraction that provides this [1][2]. Each replica runs a view timer; on expiry it broadcasts a timeout message for the next view, and *n − f* timeouts form a timeout certificate (TC) that advances everyone. Timeout durations grow exponentially, so after GST there will eventually be a view whose duration exceeds the time needed for the TC to propagate plus two responsive phases under the correct leader—at which point a commit occurs. Because leaders rotate round-robin, at most *f* consecutive faulty leaders can precede a correct one, bounding the liveness recovery to *O(f)* views. The pacemaker is deliberately *separated* from the consensus core: it is the only component that reasons about time, which is why HotStuff-2's authors describe their protocol as adding "no substantive complexity" to the original [3].

> **Theorem (Liveness sketch):** After GST, the pacemaker guarantees infinitely many views in which all correct replicas overlap for longer than 4Δ_actual + ε. In any such view with a correct leader, the leader's NEW-VIEW collection, proposal, prepare-QC, and commit-QC all complete, committing at least one block. Hence the protocol makes progress forever after GST [1][2].

### 4.5 Safety: quorum intersection and the locked-QC invariant

> **Theorem (Safety):** If a correct replica commits block *b* at view *v*, no correct replica ever commits a block *b′* conflicting with *b*.

*Proof sketch.* Committing *b* in HotStuff-2 requires a commit-QC: *n − f* replicas voted for a prepare-QC on *b*'s child, hence at least *n − f* replicas set `lockedQC` to *b*'s prepare-QC (two-chain). Suppose a conflicting *b′* were later committed; its commit-QC implies a prepare-QC for *b′* formed at some view *v′ ≥ v*. That prepare-QC required *n − f* votes, and by quorum intersection at least *f + 1* of those voters also voted in the commit quorum for *b*—i.e., at least one *correct* replica voted for both. But a correct replica votes for *b′* only if *b′* extends its `lockedQC`, and its `lockedQC` has view ≥ *v* and is on *b*'s branch; a conflicting *b′* cannot extend it. Contradiction. The leader-wait step is what makes this airtight across views: a new leader always extends the highest QC among *n − f* NEW-VIEW messages, which by the same intersection argument includes any lockable QC [2][3].

## 5. Empirical Results and Proofs

Published measurements, synthesized here, quantify the design trade-offs:

- **Scale and linearity.** The original HotStuff deployment over a WAN with 100+ replicas achieved throughput and latency *comparable to BFT-SMaRt* while exhibiting a *linear* communication footprint during leader failover, versus BFT-SMaRt's quadratic failover cost [1]. This is the direct empirical payoff of QC-based view change.
- **HotStuff-2 vs. HotStuff.** Independent implementations of both protocols in a common framework show HotStuff-2 with more compact code and *superior operational efficiency*, with the gap widest at low Byzantine ratios—precisely the regime where the deleted phase dominates cost [5].
- **Bottleneck decomposition.** Instrumented comparison of HotStuff-family protocols against Tendermint attributes Tendermint's overhead to two structural causes: all-to-all gossip (quadratic authenticators) and the fixed commit delay δ that forfeits responsiveness; a hypothetical "Tendermint*" removing both closes most of the gap, confirming that the phase structure—not implementation tuning—drives the difference [6].
- **Forking attacks.** Fast-HotStuff's evaluation demonstrates the cost of the two-chain rule under attack: with *n = 40, f = 13*, throughput falls from 8,100 tx/s (no faults) as the Byzantine fraction rises, because each faulty leader can orphan up to two honest blocks; in the worst-case round-robin pattern only *f + 1* of *n* views commit, and only one of those commits is from a correct leader [7]. This is inherent to any chained protocol with rotating leaders, including HotStuff-2.
- **Latency in hops.** HotStuff-2's good-case finality costs 7 network hops end-to-end (client to confirmation), versus 9+ for three-phase chained HotStuff; pipelining amortizes this so the *expected* per-command latency approaches 2–3 hops of queuing plus the pipeline depth [3].
- **DAG-based contrast.** Narwhal–Bullshark reference deployments report ~46,000 tx/s end-to-end at ~557 ms latency on 4 validators, scaling throughput nearly linearly with workers [8]. The lesson is architectural: DAG-BFT separates *data dissemination* (the mempool DAG) from *ordering*, achieving an order of magnitude more throughput than any leader-based chained protocol, at the cost of higher implementation complexity, *O(n²)*-ish per-round messaging, and latency floors set by DAG rounds rather than responsive phases.

The proof obligations for a HotStuff-2 implementation are correspondingly crisp: (i) the voting rule never violates the locked-QC guard (checkable by model checking the TLA⁺ above); (ii) QC verification is bound to the correct view number (preventing cross-view replay); (iii) the pacemaker's timeout growth is unbounded (ensuring eventual overlap after GST); (iv) threshold-share aggregation verifies *n − f* distinct signers (preventing a Byzantine leader from forging QCs).

## 6. Limitations

HotStuff-2's optimality is carefully scoped, and several limitations remain. First, **worst-case communication is still quadratic**: *f* consecutive Byzantine leaders each trigger a linear view-change attempt before a correct leader succeeds, for *O(f·n) = O(n²)* total—a bound shared with HotStuff and inherent to deterministic partially synchronous BFT with rotating leaders [2][3]. Second, **throughput under forking attack degrades sharply**, as quantified above; chain quality for correct leaders collapses in adversarial leader schedules, which matters for proof-of-stake incentive design [7]. Third, the **leader-wait step adds latency to every view**, including synchronous good-case views—a deliberate, proven-necessary cost of the two-phase regime [2]. Fourth, **threshold signatures** (typically BLS) introduce cryptographic setup assumptions (distributed key generation), slower verification than Ed25519 batches, and pairing-friendly-curve dependencies that complicate post-quantum migration. Fifth, the **pacemaker's exponential timeouts** mean recovery latency after GST can be large in practice if timeouts have backed off deeply during a long asynchronous period. Finally, relative to **DAG-based BFT**, chained leader protocols fundamentally serialize ordering through one leader at a time and cannot match Narwhal-style throughput scaling; HotStuff-2 optimizes the *latency–simplicity* corner, not the *throughput* corner [8].

## 7. Conclusion

HotStuff-2 closes a chapter opened by PBFT a quarter-century ago: it shows that partially synchronous BFT SMR needs only **two phases**, **linear optimistic communication**, and **no dedicated view-change sub-protocol**—the leader's per-view wait for *n − f* NEW-VIEW messages plus the locked-QC voting discipline is sufficient for both safety and liveness, with responsiveness preserved end to end [2][3]. Against PBFT it eliminates the quadratic view change; against Tendermint it eliminates gossip and the fixed commit delay; against its predecessor it eliminates a full phase while *reducing* code complexity [5][6]. Its verified limits—quadratic worst case, forking-attack throughput loss, per-view leader wait—are now precisely characterized rather than folklore [2][7]. The remaining frontier is the one Narwhal–Bullshark opened: whether DAG-based dissemination can be married to HotStuff-2-grade ordering simplicity without surrendering either side's advantages [8]. For systems where auditability, simplicity, and low good-case latency dominate—permissioned ledgers, control planes, financial settlement—HotStuff-2 is the current state of the art, and its two-phase argument is likely to remain the textbook presentation of responsive BFT.
