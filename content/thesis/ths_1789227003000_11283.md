# Learning from Human Preferences: Reward Modeling, KL-Regularized Policy Optimization, and Direct Preference Methods

## Abstract

Reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) has become the dominant paradigm for aligning large language models with human intent, yet its theoretical foundations, tradeoffs, and failure modes remain incompletely understood. This thesis presents a unified treatment of the preference-based alignment pipeline: from the Bradley–Terry model of pairwise preferences through reward-model fitting and KL-regularized PPO, to the modern family of *direct* preference optimization methods that dispense with the reward model entirely. We derive the closed-form optimal policy of the KL-regularized objective and show how Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) [1] reparameterizes it into a pure maximum-likelihood objective on preference pairs. We survey principled variants — IPO, KTO, and ORPO — contrasting their statistical assumptions, loss curvature, and data requirements. Central to the analysis is *reward overoptimization*: optimizing a learned proxy reward beyond its region of validity degrades true performance in the lawful manner captured by the scaling laws of Gao, Schulman, and Hilton [5]. The thesis concludes with open problems in distributional preference learning, process supervision, and the calibration of alignment tax.

## 1 Introduction

Modern language models are trained to predict the next token on web-scale text, an objective that is only incidentally related to being helpful, truthful, and safe. The gap between the pretraining objective and desired behavior — the *alignment gap* — cannot be closed by scale alone [2]. Specifying a reward function by hand for open-ended language behavior is intractable: notions such as helpfulness, nuance, and taste resist formalization, and naive reward proxies are easily exploited, a failure mode known as *reward hacking*.

The central insight of the preference-learning literature is that *comparing* two candidate outputs is far easier for humans than *producing* a gold-standard output or *writing* a reward function [4]. By collecting pairwise preference judgments $(x, y_w, y_l)$, where $y_w$ is the preferred (winning) response and $y_l$ the dispreferred one, we obtain a scalable supervision signal. The classical pipeline, introduced for deep reinforcement learning by Christiano et al. [4] and adapted to language models by Ziegler et al. [6] and Ouyang et al. (InstructGPT) [2], consists of three stages:

1. **Supervised fine-tuning (SFT).** A pretrained model is fine-tuned on human demonstrations to produce $\pi_{\text{ref}}$, the reference policy.
2. **Reward modeling.** A reward model $r_\phi(x, y)$ is fit to the preference data under the Bradley–Terry model, predicting scalar rewards consistent with observed comparisons.
3. **Policy optimization.** The reference policy is optimized against the learned reward with proximal policy optimization (PPO), penalized by a KL divergence term that keeps the policy near the reference.

This thesis traces the mathematical structure of this pipeline, exposes the pathologies that arise when each component is pushed too far, and analyzes the recent shift toward *direct* methods that fold reward modeling and policy optimization into a single contrastive objective. The treatment is self-contained but pitched at the level of a reader comfortable with probabilistic modeling and convex optimization.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The Bradley–Terry Model of Preferences

Pairwise preference modeling has a long history in statistics and psychometrics. Given a prompt $x$ and two responses $y_1, y_2$, the **Bradley–Terry (BT)** model [7] posits a latent reward function $r(x, y)$ such that

$$P(y_1 \succ y_2 \mid x) = \sigma\big(r(x, y_1) - r(x, y_2)\big),$$

where $\sigma(z) = 1/(1 + e^{-z})$ is the logistic sigmoid. Intuitively, the probability that a human prefers $y_1$ depends only on the *difference* of latent rewards — a special case of the Plackett–Luce ranking model. The BT model is *identifiable only up to an additive constant per prompt*: shifting $r(x, \cdot)$ by a prompt-dependent offset leaves all preference probabilities unchanged.

Fitting a parametric reward model $r_\phi$ to a dataset $\mathcal{D} = \{(x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)})\}_{i=1}^N$ of preferences reduces to maximum likelihood:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{RM}}(\phi) = -\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}\left[ \log \sigma\big(r_\phi(x, y_w) - r_\phi(x, y_l)\big) \right].$$

This is a logistic regression on reward differences — convex in the reward differences, though not in $\phi$ for a deep network. Christiano et al. [4] additionally blended the BT likelihood with a 10% uniform-noise mixture, $\epsilon$-contamination, to absorb label noise, a robustness device still relevant when annotator agreement hovers near 70–75%.

### 2.2 KL-Regularized Policy Optimization

With a reward model in hand, the alignment problem becomes: maximize expected reward while remaining close to the reference policy $\pi_{\text{ref}}$. The canonical objective is

$$\max_{\pi_\theta} \; \mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{D},\, y \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot \mid x)} \big[ r_\phi(x, y) \big] \;-\; \beta \, \mathbb{D}_{\text{KL}}\big[ \pi_\theta(\cdot \mid x) \,\|\, \pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot \mid x) \big], \tag{1}$$

with $\beta > 0$ controlling the strength of regularization [2]. This objective admits a *closed-form solution*, a fact of central importance to everything that follows.

> **Theorem:** For fixed $x$, the maximizer of (1) over all distributions $\pi(\cdot \mid x)$ with full support is the Gibbs distribution
> $$\pi^*(y \mid x) = \frac{1}{Z(x)} \, \pi_{\text{ref}}(y \mid x) \, \exp\!\left( \frac{1}{\beta} \, r_\phi(x, y) \right),$$
> where $Z(x) = \sum_y \pi_{\text{ref}}(y \mid x) \exp(r_\phi(x, y)/\beta)$ is the partition function.

*Proof sketch.* Writing the Lagrangian of the constrained optimization and differentiating with respect to $\pi(y \mid x)$ yields $\pi(y\mid x) \propto \pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp(r_\phi(x,y)/\beta)$; normalization gives $Z(x)$. Equivalently, the objective equals $-\beta \, \mathbb{D}_{\text{KL}}[\pi \,\|\, \pi^*] + \beta \log Z(x)$, minimized at $\pi = \pi^*$. ∎

Inverting this relation expresses the *implicit reward* defined by any policy $\pi_\theta$ relative to $\pi_{\text{ref}}$:

$$r_\phi(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y \mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y \mid x)} + \beta \log Z(x). \tag{2}$$

This inversion is the mathematical hinge of direct preference optimization.

### 2.3 PPO-Based RLHF

In practice, (1) is optimized with PPO [2, 6]: the policy generates rollouts, the reward model scores them, and a clipped surrogate objective updates the policy while an explicit per-token KL penalty (or PPO's trust region) anchors it to $\pi_{\text{ref}}$. PPO is on-policy and sample-inefficient, requires a value-function critic, and is notoriously sensitive to hyperparameters — reward normalization, KL coefficient scheduling, and advantage whitening all materially affect outcomes. These engineering burdens motivated the search for simpler alternatives.

| Component | Classical RLHF | Direct methods |
|---|---|---|
| Reward signal | Explicit model $r_\phi$ | Implicit in policy log-ratios |
| Optimizer | PPO (on-policy RL) | Supervised contrastive loss |
| Data | Online rollouts + labels | Static preference pairs |
| KL control | Explicit penalty $\beta$ | Implicit via $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ ratios |
| Failure mode | Reward hacking | Likelihood collapse on $y_l$ |

## 3 Methodology

We now develop the canonical three-stage RLHF pipeline and its direct-method successors as instances of a single mathematical framework: *preference likelihood maximization under a reference-anchored policy class*.

**Stage 1 — Supervised fine-tuning.** The base model $\pi_{\text{base}}$ is fine-tuned by maximum likelihood on demonstrations $\mathcal{D}_{\text{demo}}$, yielding $\pi_{\text{ref}}$. This stage adapts the model to the desired output distribution (format, style, role) and provides the KL anchor for everything downstream.

**Stage 2 — Reward modeling.** Human annotators rank $K \ge 2$ sampled completions per prompt (InstructGPT used $K$ between 4 and 9); all $\binom{K}{2}$ pairs enter $\mathcal{D}$ [2]. The reward model — typically the SFT model with a scalar value head — is trained with the BT loss above. Validation accuracy on held-out pairs (typically 72–76%) is the key diagnostic.

**Stage 3 — PPO.** The policy $\pi_\theta$, initialized at $\pi_{\text{ref}}$, is optimized against $r_\phi$ with per-token KL penalties. The effective per-token reward is $r_\phi(x, y) - \beta \log(\pi_\theta/\pi_{\text{ref}})$ accumulated over the sequence, and training is stopped by monitoring the KL budget: InstructGPT reports results as a function of $\sqrt{\mathbb{D}_{\text{KL}}}$ rather than training steps [2].

**Direct preference optimization.** Substituting the inverted reward (2) into the BT model and observing that $\beta \log Z(x)$ *cancels* in the difference $r(x, y_w) - r(x, y_l)$ yields the DPO objective [1]:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}}(\theta) = -\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}\left[ \log \sigma\!\left( \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_w \mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w \mid x)} \;-\; \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_l \mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l \mid x)} \right) \right]. \tag{3}$$

No reward model is trained, no RL is run, and no sampler is needed: the objective is a plain binary classification loss on the *implicit reward margin*. The partition function — intractable over the infinite response space — never appears.

```python
def dpo_loss(pi_theta_logps, pi_ref_logps, yw_idx, yl_idx, beta=0.1):
    # pi_theta_logps: log pi_theta(y|x) for the batch's chosen/rejected
    pi_logratios = pi_theta_logps - pi_ref_logps          # implicit rewards / beta
    logits = beta * (pi_logratios[yw_idx] - pi_logratios[yl_idx])
    return -torch.nn.functional.logsigmoid(logits).mean()
```

The gradient of (3) has an instructive form. Let $\hat{r}_\theta(x,y) = \beta \log(\pi_\theta/\pi_{\text{ref}})$ and $u = \hat{r}_\theta(x,y_w) - \hat{r}_\theta(x,y_l)$. Then

$$\nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}} = -\beta \, \mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}\big[ \sigma(-u) \cdot \big( \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_w \mid x) - \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_l \mid x) \big) \big].$$

The update *increases* the likelihood of the winner and *decreases* that of the loser, weighted by $\sigma(-u)$ — the model's current probability of getting the pair wrong. Correctly-ranked pairs with large margins contribute vanishing gradients; the loss focuses capacity on contested pairs, exactly as a margin-based classifier should.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 DPO as Exact Reduction of the RLHF Objective

A common misconception is that DPO is an *approximation* to RLHF. Under the BT model and an unconstrained policy class, it is an *exact reparameterization*: the policy minimizing (3) is precisely $\pi^*$ from the Theorem, i.e., the KL-regularized reward maximizer [1]. The reduction is elegant but its preconditions deserve scrutiny. First, it assumes the BT model is the true data-generating process for preferences — any deviation (annotator heterogeneity, intransitivity, ties) breaks the equivalence. Second, it assumes the policy class can realize arbitrary conditional distributions; a finite neural network with an SFT initialization cannot, and the optimization dynamics of gradient descent on (3) need not track the RL dynamics that PPO would produce even when the optima coincide. Empirically, DPO matches or exceeds PPO-based RLHF on summarization and dialogue benchmarks at a fraction of the compute [1], which explains its rapid adoption despite these caveats.

### 4.2 IPO: When the Bradley–Terry Assumption Fails

Identity Preference Optimization [8] starts from a pointed critique: the BT model forces preference probabilities through a logistic link, so a sufficiently expressive reward model will drive implicit reward margins to infinity on deterministic preferences, producing overconfident policies. IPO replaces the logistic loss with a *squared* loss on the implicit margin:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{IPO}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}\left[ \left( \hat{r}_\theta(x, y_w) - \hat{r}_\theta(x, y_l) - \tfrac{1}{2\tau} \right)^2 \right],$$

with temperature $\tau$. Rather than maximizing the margin without bound, IPO regresses it toward a fixed target $1/(2\tau)$, embodying the view that preferences carry *bounded* information: even a unanimous preference among annotators should not push the policy to assign probability one to the winner. The general theoretical paradigm of Azar et al. [8] frames this as learning from preferences under a general preference model $\Psi$, of which BT (DPO) and the identity map (IPO) are special cases. In practice, IPO exhibits markedly better KL control — the policy drifts less from $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ for the same win-rate gains — at the cost of slower convergence on sharply-separated pairs.

### 4.3 KTO: Prospect Theory and Binary Desirability Signals

Kahneman–Tversky Optimization [9] attacks a different bottleneck: *data*. Pairwise preferences are expensive; binary judgments of whether a single output is desirable ($y \sim$ good) or not are far cheaper and more abundant (thumbs-up/down telemetry, implicit engagement signals). KTO defines a HALO — *human-aware loss* — built on the Kahneman–Tversky value function, which is concave for gains, convex for losses, and steeper for losses (loss aversion):

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{KTO}} = \mathbb{E}_{x, y \sim \mathcal{D}} \left[ \lambda_y - v(x, y) \right], \quad v(x,y) = \begin{cases} \sigma\big(\hat{r}_\theta(x,y) - z_0\big) & y \text{ desirable} \\ \sigma\big(z_0 - \hat{r}_\theta(x,y)\big) & y \text{ undesirable} \end{cases}$$

where $z_0 = \mathbb{D}_{\text{KL}}[\pi_\theta \,\|\, \pi_{\text{ref}}]$ serves as a reference point and $\lambda_y$ encodes asymmetric loss aversion ($\lambda_D < \lambda_U$). Remarkably, KTO matches or exceeds DPO from 1B to 30B parameters using only binary signals [9], suggesting that much of the information in pairwise rankings is redundant once desirability is known — though at the price of requiring careful calibration of the desirable/undesirable base rates.

### 4.4 ORPO: Monolithic Alignment Without a Reference Model

Odds Ratio Preference Optimization [10] questions the pipeline itself: why SFT first and align second? ORPO fuses the two stages by appending an odds-ratio penalty to the standard negative log-likelihood:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{ORPO}} = \mathcal{L}_{\text{SFT}} + \lambda \, \mathcal{L}_{\text{OR}}, \quad \mathcal{L}_{\text{OR}} = -\log \sigma\!\left( \log \frac{\text{odds}_\theta(y_w \mid x)}{\text{odds}_\theta(y_l \mid x)} \right),$$

where $\text{odds}_\theta(y \mid x) = \pi_\theta(y \mid x)/(1 - \pi_\theta(y \mid x))$. The NLL term teaches the model the domain (the SFT role); the odds-ratio term weakly penalizes the disfavored style relative to the favored one. No reference model is kept in memory — a substantial efficiency win — and a 7B model trained with ORPO on UltraFeedback alone reaches 12.2% on AlpacaEval 2.0 and 7.32 on MT-Bench [10]. The tradeoff is the loss of the KL anchor: without $\pi_{\text{ref}}$, there is no principled dial for how far the policy may drift, and practitioners must tune $\lambda$ as an ersatz regularizer.

### 4.5 Reward Overoptimization: Goodhart's Law Made Quantitative

All of the above optimizes *a proxy*: the reward model is trained on finite data and is therefore wrong somewhere, and optimization pressure reliably finds where. Christiano et al. [4] already observed policies exploiting reward models (e.g., avoiding losing at Pong without trying to win); Gao, Schulman, and Hilton [5] made this *quantitative*. In a synthetic setup where a fixed "gold" reward model stands in for humans, they measured gold reward as a function of optimization pressure and found:

- Under RL optimization, gold reward follows $R(d) = d(\alpha - \beta \log d)$ where $d = \sqrt{\mathbb{D}_{\text{KL}}(\pi \,\|\, \pi_{\text{ref}})}$: an initial linear gain followed by logarithmic decay — the classic Goodhart hump.
- Under best-of-$n$ sampling, gold reward grows as $\alpha \log n$ then saturates, with coefficients scaling smoothly in reward-model parameter count.
- Larger reward models and larger preference datasets *delay* but do not eliminate the turnover point; the KL penalty's coefficient shifts the hump but cannot remove it.

Crucially, recent work shows direct methods are not immune [11]: although DAAs train no proxy reward model, the *implicit* reward $\hat{r}_\theta$ undergoes the same overoptimization through what the authors term implicit bootstrapping — the token-level MDP view reveals a soft Bellman equation governing the learned per-token rewards, and length exploitation (verbosity bias) emerges as the direct-method analogue of reward hacking. The IPO objective, with its bounded margin target, provably resists this better than DPO [11].

## 5 Empirical Results and Formal Arguments

We consolidate the key quantitative findings of the literature into a comparative picture.

**InstructGPT [2].** The headline result: human labelers preferred 1.3B-parameter InstructGPT outputs over 175B-parameter GPT-3 outputs on the API prompt distribution, despite 100× fewer parameters. Truthfulness (TruthfulQA) improved and toxicity (RealToxicityPrompts) decreased, with minimal regressions on standard NLP benchmarks. The KL coefficient was set so that $\sqrt{\mathbb{D}_{\text{KL}}}$ stayed in the single digits nats$^{1/2}$ — already past this, labeler preference flattened while proxy reward kept climbing, an early sighting of overoptimization.

**DPO [1].** On TL;DR summarization and Anthropic HH dialogue, DPO matched or beat PPO-based RLHF on GPT-4-judged win rates while using only the static preference dataset. Ablations showed the implicit reward accuracy (agreement with held-out human preferences) tracking policy win rates, validating the reparameterization empirically.

**Overoptimization scaling [5].** Fitting $R(d) = d(\alpha - \beta \log d)$ across reward-model sizes from 12M to 3B parameters, the coefficients $\alpha, \beta$ varied smoothly (near power-law) in parameter count; dataset size entered primarily through $\alpha$. The practical prescription: pick the KL budget at the predicted peak of the gold-reward curve, not at the maximum proxy reward — a rule now standard in RLHF tuning.

**Variant comparison [8, 9, 10].** IPO achieves comparable win rates to DPO with substantially lower $\mathbb{D}_{\text{KL}}$ from the reference, confirming better regularization. KTO reaches DPO-level AlpacaEval scores from binary data alone, with loss-aversion coefficients $\lambda_D/\lambda_U \approx 1$–$1.5$ performing best. ORPO's single-stage training halves the memory footprint (no reference model) while matching two-stage DPO+SFT pipelines on MT-Bench.

A formal argument worth stating explicitly concerns *sample complexity*. The BT reward-model loss is a logistic regression over reward *differences*; its excess risk scales as $\tilde{O}(\sqrt{C/N})$ where $C$ measures the complexity of the reward class and $N$ the number of pairs [8]. DPO inherits this rate for the implicit reward but pays an additional approximation error from the restricted policy class — the gap between the realizable $\pi_\theta$ and the Gibbs optimum $\pi^*$. This decomposition explains why DPO can underperform PPO when the policy class is the binding constraint (e.g., small models, long horizons) even though the two share an optimum in the nonparametric limit.

## 6 Limitations

The preference-learning paradigm, for all its empirical success, rests on fragile foundations that this thesis has only partially addressed.

1. **The BT model is descriptively false.** Human preferences are intransitive, context-dependent, and heterogeneous across annotators; fitting a single scalar reward collapses a distribution of values into a point estimate that systematically favors majority tastes [12]. EM-style mixture approaches exist but are nascent.
2. **Overoptimization is managed, not solved.** KL penalties, early stopping on proxy reward, and bounded-margin losses all *delay* Goodhart degradation; none removes the incentive to exploit residual reward-model errors, and the gold-reward peak must be located empirically per setup [5].
3. **Length and style confounds.** Both explicit and implicit reward models latch onto superficial correlates of quality — verbosity, list formatting, hedged tone — because these correlate with preference in the training data. Length-controlled evaluations (AlpacaEval LC) mitigate the measurement problem but not the underlying misattribution.
4. **The alignment tax is real but poorly characterized.** RLHF reliably degrades some capabilities (calibration, certain reasoning benchmarks) even as it improves judged helpfulness; the conditions under which the tax is paid versus avoided remain poorly predicted by theory.
5. **Process vs. outcome supervision.** All methods discussed supervise *outcomes* (final responses). For reasoning tasks, supervising intermediate steps (process reward models) shows promise but multiplies annotation cost and introduces credit-assignment pathologies of its own.

## 7 Conclusion

From Christiano et al.'s trajectory-segment comparisons [4] to the menagerie of direct preference losses, the field has converged on a striking mathematical unity: under the Bradley–Terry model, KL-regularized reward maximization, reward-model fitting, and contrastive policy optimization are views of a single object — the Gibbs policy $\pi^* \propto \pi_{\text{ref}} \exp(r/\beta)$. DPO's contribution was to show that the partition function cancels, turning reinforcement learning into supervised learning; IPO, KTO, and ORPO's contributions were to show that the logistic link, the pairwise format, and even the reference model are all negotiable. Yet the central pathology — optimizing a proxy past its validity, formalized in the overoptimization scaling laws [5] — survives every reformulation, migrating from explicit reward models into the implicit rewards of direct methods. The next frontier is not a better loss but a better *model of the human*: distributional, process-aware, and honest about uncertainty. Until then, the KL budget remains what it has always been — the practitioner's quantified humility.
