# Probabilistic Programming with Hamiltonian Monte Carlo: NUTS Tree Doubling, Mass Matrix Adaptation, Divergent Transitions, and NumPyro-Style JAX-Vectorized Inference

## Abstract

Hamiltonian Monte Carlo (HMC) exploits the geometry of the target distribution by simulating Hamiltonian dynamics on an augmented phase space, proposing distant, low-autocorrelation transitions that defeat the random-walk behavior of classical Metropolis–Hastings. Its Achilles' heel has always been *tuning*: the leapfrog step size ε and trajectory length L must be matched to the local curvature of the posterior, a task that is infeasible by hand in the thousand-dimensional, multiscale models that define modern Bayesian practice. This thesis develops the complete modern machinery that made HMC the default inference engine of probabilistic programming. We derive the symplectic leapfrog integrator and its volume-preserving, reversible properties; reconstruct the No-U-Turn Sampler (NUTS) of Hoffman and Gelman [1] — its recursive binary-tree doubling, slice-based candidate weighting, and the U-turn stopping criterion that yields reversibility without a fixed L; analyze primal–dual averaging for step-size adaptation [7] and diagonal/dense mass-matrix (metric) adaptation during warmup; explain divergent transitions as failures of geometric ergodicity on funnels, and how they diagnose biased inference [4]; and show how NumPyro-style JAX implementations vectorize and JIT-compile the entire NUTS recursion — including tree building — into XLA kernels for GPU/TPU throughput [5]. We close with empirical benchmarks, a catalog of pathologies, and the open frontier of within-orbit local adaptation [3].

## 1 Introduction

The success of probabilistic programming — the vision that a user writes down a generative model as code and receives posterior inference for free — rests almost entirely on the availability of *black-box, self-tuning* inference engines. For continuous latent-variable models, that engine is Hamiltonian Monte Carlo, and its practical embodiment is the No-U-Turn Sampler. Systems such as Stan, PyMC, and NumPyro have carried Bayesian modeling into thousands of scientific domains precisely because their default sampler requires no user tuning: warmup adapts the step size, the mass matrix, and the trajectory length automatically, and the diagnostics it emits (divergences, treedepth saturation, E-BFMI) tell the modeler when the geometry of the posterior has defeated the algorithm rather than silently returning biased draws.

This is a remarkable achievement, because the underlying numerics are delicate. HMC replaces the blind random walk of Metropolis with *informed* deterministic motion: it endows each parameter with a fictitious momentum, defines a Hamiltonian as the sum of kinetic and potential energy, and integrates Hamilton's equations to glide along level sets of the target density. A single proposal can traverse the entire typical set — in the best case, the chain mixes in O(d^{1/4}) gradient evaluations for a d-dimensional Gaussian, versus O(d) for random-walk Metropolis [7]. But this efficiency is hostage to two tuning parameters. The leapfrog step size ε controls numerical stability: too large and the integrator explodes, too small and the sampler crawls. The trajectory length L = ε·T controls exploration: too short and HMC degenerates to a random walk, too long and trajectories loop back on themselves, wasting gradient evaluations on already-visited territory.

> **Theorem:** (Neal, 2011) For a d-dimensional target with log-density satisfying global Lipschitz conditions, optimally tuned HMC with randomized trajectory length requires O(d^{1/4}) leapfrog steps per effectively independent sample, versus O(d) for optimally tuned random-walk Metropolis and O(d^{1/3}) for Metropolis-adjusted Langevin (MALA).

The No-U-Turn Sampler [1] eliminated L as a tuning parameter through a deceptively simple idea: simulate the Hamiltonian trajectory *both* forward and backward in time, doubling the path length recursively, and stop when the trajectory begins to "turn back" toward its starting point — the U-turn criterion — while preserving the detailed-balance reversibility required for convergence. Step-size adaptation via primal–dual averaging and metric (mass-matrix) adaptation via regularized sample covariance during warmup eliminated ε and the kinetic-energy geometry as well. What remains is a sampler that is nearly tuning-free in practice — yet still fails, in informative and diagnosable ways, on the pathological geometries (funnels, heavy tails, strong multimodality) that real hierarchical models produce.

This thesis proceeds as follows. Section 2 establishes the Hamiltonian formalism, the leapfrog integrator, and the Metropolis correction. Section 3 reconstructs NUTS: slice sampling, binary tree doubling, the U-turn criterion, and biased progressive sampling. Section 4 goes deep on the four subsystems named in the title — tree doubling, mass-matrix adaptation, divergences, and JAX-vectorized inference. Section 5 presents empirical evaluations: scaling laws, the Neal funnel benchmark, and divergence diagnostics. Section 6 catalogs limitations. Section 7 concludes with the frontier, including within-orbit step-size adaptation [3].

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Hamiltonian dynamics for sampling

Let the target density be π(q) ∝ exp(−V(q)) on ℝ^d, where V(q) = −log π(q) is the *potential energy*. HMC augments the parameter vector q with a momentum vector p ∈ ℝ^d, defining the joint *canonical* distribution

π(q, p) ∝ exp(−H(q, p)),  H(q, p) = V(q) + K(p),

with kinetic energy K(p) = ½ pᵀ M⁻¹ p for a symmetric positive-definite *mass matrix* M. Marginally over p, q follows π; conditionally, p ∼ Normal(0, M) independently of q. The Hamiltonian generates dynamics via

dq/dt = ∂H/∂p = M⁻¹p,   dp/dt = −∂H/∂q = −∇V(q).

Three properties make this flow useful for MCMC [7]: **energy conservation** (dH/dt = 0 along exact trajectories, so proposals stay on the typical set); **volume preservation** (the flow is a symplectomorphism — Liouville's theorem — so no Jacobian correction is needed); and **time reversibility** (negating p and running the flow backward returns to the start; combined with momentum resampling, this yields detailed balance).

### 2.2 The leapfrog (Störmer–Verlet) integrator

Exact Hamiltonian flow is unavailable for non-Gaussian targets, so HMC discretizes it with the *leapfrog integrator*, a second-order symplectic method — half-step momentum, full-step position, half-step momentum. Leapfrog inherits volume preservation and reversibility exactly; it loses only energy conservation, accumulating error O(ε²) per step. A Metropolis–Hastings accept/reject step with acceptance probability min(1, exp(H_old − H_new)) corrects the discretization bias and preserves π exactly. In practice ε is chosen so that the average acceptance probability sits near the theoretically optimal δ ≈ 0.65–0.80 for HMC [2], a target the primal–dual averaging adaptor enforces automatically.

> **Theorem:** (Hairer, Lubich & Wanner) The leapfrog integrator is symplectic and symmetric (time-reversible). Its modified Hamiltonian H̃ = H + ε²H₂ + ε⁴H₄ + ⋯ is conserved exactly, so energy error remains bounded (oscillatory) rather than drifting — the property that lets HMC trajectories run for hundreds of steps without leaving the typical set.

### 2.3 Why trajectory length matters

A fixed integration time T = Lε that is too short yields proposals highly correlated with the current state (random-walk behavior); one that is too long wastes computation as the trajectory curves back through already-explored regions. Worse, the *optimal* T varies across the typical set in multiscale posteriors. This is the problem NUTS was built to solve.

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 NUTS: the slice-sampling reformulation

NUTS [1] begins by introducing a *slice variable* u ∼ Uniform(0, exp(−H(q₀, p₀))), which renders the conditional distribution over (q, p) uniform over the *slice* { (q,p) : H(q,p) ≤ −log u }. The sampler's job reduces to: *explore the slice along the Hamiltonian trajectory and pick a point (nearly) uniformly from the visited states that lie in the slice.*

Because exact reversibility is required, NUTS cannot simply integrate forward until a stopping rule fires. Instead it builds a *balanced binary tree* of leapfrog states by recursive doubling: at each doubling, it extends the trajectory either forward or backward with probability ½. The tree is grown until the *U-turn criterion* fires:

**(θ⁺ − θ⁻) · r⁻ < 0  or  (θ⁺ − θ⁻) · r⁺ < 0,**

where θ⁻, θ⁺ are the leftmost/rightmost positions and r⁻, r⁺ the corresponding momenta. Geometrically, this is the time derivative of half the squared distance between the endpoints — the trajectory has begun to retrace toward its start, and further doubling would waste gradients.

### 3.2 Biased progressive sampling and the efficient NUTS

The original NUTS sampled uniformly from all visited slice states, which requires storing the whole trajectory. The *efficient* NUTS used in Stan and NumPyro instead samples *progressively*: when a new subtree is added, the sampler moves to a state in the new subtree with probability proportional to its slice weight, biased toward the *later* states. This "biased progressive" scheme preserves correctness while using O(1) memory and favoring distant proposals. A *max treedepth* (default 10, i.e., 2¹⁰ = 1024 leapfrog steps) caps pathological trajectories; hitting it is itself a diagnostic warning.

### 3.3 Warmup: the three adaptation windows

Stan-style warmup divides iterations into three phases [6]:

| Phase | Purpose | Mechanism |
|---|---|---|
| *Initial buffer* (~75 iters) | Reach the typical set | Dual averaging only |
| *Windowed adaptation* (geometric windows) | Learn metric + step size | Welford covariance → mass matrix; dual averaging → ε |
| *Terminal buffer* (~50 iters) | Freeze, fine-tune ε | Dual averaging only |

The **mass matrix** M is estimated from the sample covariance of warmup draws with shrinkage regularization toward a diagonal (or identity) to guarantee positive definiteness. A diagonal M rescales each parameter by its marginal variance — a poor-man's preconditioner; a dense M additionally rotates into the posterior's correlation structure, at O(d²) memory and O(d²) per-leapfrog cost. Dual averaging drives the average acceptance statistic ᾱ toward the target δ (default 0.8 in Stan, 0.65–0.8 elsewhere). Shrinkage toward μ and 1/√t averaging give the vanishing-adaptation property MCMC theory requires.

### 3.4 Divergences: definition and diagnosis

A *divergent transition* is declared when the energy error along a leapfrog step exceeds a threshold (default `max_delta_h = 1000` in Stan): |H(q,p) − H(q₀,p₀)| > 1000. This is a heuristic tripwire for trajectories that have gone numerically unstable — the integrator has flown off toward infinity because local curvature defeated the step size. Crucially, divergences *cluster where the sampler fails to explore*: in Neal's funnel, they concentrate in the narrow neck, and posterior means computed from the chain are then biased because the neck is under-sampled [4]. The diagnostic protocol is:

1. **Locate** divergences in parameter space (pairs plots) — clustering indicates a geometric pathology, scattering suggests mere bad luck.
2. **Reduce ε** (raise `adapt_delta` toward 1): if divergences vanish, geometric ergodicity held and the model is fine; if they persist, the geometry is genuinely hostile.
3. **Reparameterize**: the non-centered parameterization is the canonical cure for funnels.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Tree doubling under the hood: reversibility by construction

The subtle genius of NUTS is that the doubling construction makes the *set of visited states* independent of the direction choices in a way that preserves reversibility. The key invariant, proved in [1, Theorem 1], is that the collection of all states ever *considered* as the next sample — the candidate set C — together with the stopping decision, is symmetric under reversal of the trajectory: starting from any state in C and running the same random doubling sequence would produce the same tree. Because the final sample is drawn (progressively) from C with weights proportional to slice membership, detailed balance holds with respect to the uniform distribution on the slice, hence π.

The U-turn criterion deserves a closer look. When either endpoint's momentum points back along the span (θ⁺ − θ⁻), the path's endpoints are approaching each other and the trajectory is "turning." Stopping here yields, empirically, the *locally longest* non-retracing path — typically within a small factor of the oracle-optimal fixed integration time, with zero tuning [2].

But tree doubling is not free. The sampler evaluates the gradient at *every* leapfrog node, including nodes in subtrees that trigger the stopping rule and are then discarded — the "wasted simulation" critique [2]. The efficient (iterative, biased-progressive) NUTS mitigates this by sampling during construction rather than after. The max-treedepth cutoff introduces a subtle bias in extreme cases, but chains that routinely saturate treedepth 10 are waving a red flag about step size or geometry anyway.

### 4.2 Mass-matrix adaptation: preconditioning the geometry

The mass matrix M defines the kinetic energy, hence the geometry in which Hamiltonian trajectories are "straight lines." Choosing M ≈ Cov_π[q]⁻¹ — the inverse posterior covariance — transforms the target toward isotropy, where a single ε works uniformly and trajectories mix rapidly. This is *preconditioning* in the MCMC sense, and warmup is where it is learned.

Stan implements a three-stage windowed schedule [6]. Within each adaptation window, Welford's online algorithm accumulates the sample mean and covariance of the (unconstrained) parameters; at the window boundary, the mass matrix is set to a regularized, shrinkage-toward-identity estimate for numerical safety. Two estimators are standard:

- **Diagonal (`diag_e`)**: M = diag(σ̂₁², …, σ̂_d²) — O(d) memory, O(d) per leapfrog step; rescales but does not decorrelate.
- **Dense (`dense_e`)**: full Σ̂ — O(d²) memory, captures correlations; essential for strongly correlated posteriors (e.g., hierarchical models with correlated group effects), but the Cholesky factor and triangular solves add real cost per step.

A common failure mode is *too-short warmup*: the mass matrix is estimated from pre-convergence draws, the metric is wrong, and sampling mixes poorly despite healthy-looking diagnostics — the remedy is simply more warmup, or initializing from the posterior mode.

### 4.3 Divergent transitions: when the integrator meets the funnel

The canonical pathology is *Neal's funnel*:

v ∼ Normal(0, 3),   x_i | v ∼ Normal(0, e^{v/2}),  i = 1…n.

The marginal of v has a wide "mouth" and an exponentially narrow "neck" where curvature grows as e^{−v}. No single step size ε can serve both: ε tuned for the mouth is catastrophically large for the neck, where one leapfrog step overshoots the high-curvature region and the energy error explodes — a *divergence*. The sampler then under-explores the neck, and since the neck contains most of the probability mass of v's left tail, estimates of E[v] and tail quantiles are biased [4].

This is why divergences are the most important diagnostic in the Stan ecosystem: they are a *computable certificate of non-ergodicity*. The theory [4] frames it as loss of *geometric* ergodicity — the Hamiltonian flow on the funnel's neck has regions where the integrator cannot resolve the dynamics at any fixed ε, so the chain's stationary distribution differs from π in exactly the region the divergences flag. The prescribed remedies form a ladder:

1. `adapt_delta → 1` (e.g., 0.95 → 0.99): shrinks ε; eliminates divergences *only if* geometric ergodicity holds at smaller ε.
2. **Non-centered reparameterization**: x_i = e^{v/2}·z_i with z_i ∼ Normal(0,1) — moves the funnel's curvature into a deterministic transform, giving the sampler a near-Gaussian geometry.
3. **Model revision**: if divergences persist after (1)–(2), the model itself is likely weakly identified; more informative priors or a different likelihood are indicated.

Empirically, the pairs plot of (v, x₁) with divergences overlaid in red is the single most informative figure in applied Bayesian workflow: divergences hugging the funnel's neck scream "reparameterize," while scattered divergences whisper "increase adapt_delta" [4].

### 4.4 NumPyro-style JAX-vectorized inference: NUTS as an XLA kernel

NumPyro [5] reimplemented NUTS around a single insight: JAX's function transformations compose. `grad` gives exact gradients of the model log-density; `jit` compiles the leapfrog step — and, crucially, the *entire tree-building loop* — into XLA; `vmap`/`pmap` vectorize across chains and devices. The recursive `BuildTree` of [1] cannot be JIT-compiled directly (dynamic recursion, variable tree sizes), so NumPyro uses an *iterative* NUTS formulation: the doubling loop is a `lax.while_loop`/`lax.scan` over a fixed maximum depth with masked updates, and progressive sampling maintains running candidate state in accumulators. The whole transition becomes one compiled program with no Python overhead per leapfrog step.

The performance consequences are structural: **throughput** — hundreds of chains run in a single `vmap`, saturating GPU/TPU; **memory** — the iterative formulation holds O(1) state regardless of treedepth; **composability** — the sampler is just a JAX-transformed function, composing with `jit`-compiled models and neural-network likelihoods.

```python
import jax, jax.numpy as jnp
import numpyro
import numpyro.distributions as dist
from numpyro.infer import MCMC, NUTS

def funnel(n=10):
    v = numpyro.sample("v", dist.Normal(0., 3.))
    with numpyro.plate("x", n):
        numpyro.sample("x", dist.Normal(0., jnp.exp(v / 2)))

kernel = NUTS(funnel, target_accept_prob=0.9, dense_mass=False,
              max_tree_depth=10)
mcmc = MCMC(kernel, num_warmup=1000, num_samples=2000,
            num_chains=4, chain_method="vectorized")  # vmap over chains
mcmc.run(jax.random.PRNGKey(0), n=10)
mcmc.print_summary()  # r_hat, n_eff per parameter
```

Note `chain_method="vectorized"`: all four chains' NUTS transitions execute as one vmapped XLA program — the modern realization of "inference as a compiled kernel." MixedHMC extends the same machinery to discrete latents, and HMCECS subsamples data per iteration for tall-data regimes [5].

## 5 Empirical Evaluation / Proofs

### 5.1 Scaling: HMC vs. NUTS vs. random-walk Metropolis

The theoretical scaling hierarchy [7] is borne out empirically on Gaussian targets. NUTS pays a modest constant-factor overhead for tree doubling relative to oracle-tuned HMC, but requires no oracle — the comparison that matters [2]. On the Stan benchmark suite (diamonds, earnings, radon hierarchies), NUTS with diagonal mass matrix achieves min-ESS/sec within 2× of hand-tuned HMC on every model, and beats it on multiscale models where no single L is adequate.

### 5.2 The funnel benchmark: divergences detect bias

Fitting the centered funnel (n = 9, v ∼ Normal(0,3)) with defaults yields the textbook result [4]: several hundred divergent transitions, all clustered at v < −2 in the neck; posterior mean of v biased upward by ~0.3 (true: 0); R̂ nominal on non-divergent chains, masking the bias. Raising `adapt_delta` to 0.99 reduces but does not eliminate divergences — the signature of genuine geometric pathology. The non-centered reparameterization (x_i = e^{v/2} z_i) eliminates divergences entirely at default settings, recovers E[v] ≈ 0, and roughly doubles ESS/sec.

### 5.3 Correctness proofs: the NUTS invariant

The central theoretical result [1, Theorem 1]: *NUTS leaves π invariant.* The slice augmentation preserves π; conditional on u, the doubling procedure's candidate set and stopping rule are invariant under trajectory reversal (direction choices are symmetric and checks depend only on visited states plus endpoints); progressive sampling draws from the candidate set with slice weights, a valid update on the uniform slice distribution; momentum resampling preserves the Gaussian marginal. Ergodicity follows from momentum resampling giving positive probability of reaching any positive-measure set, under standard regularity conditions on V.

### 5.4 JAX vectorization: measured speedups

NumPyro's benchmarks [5] on hierarchical logistic regression (d ≈ 4000) show the iterative JIT-compiled NUTS running ~10–100× faster than equivalent PyMC3/Theano implementations on GPU, with the gap widening in d: Python-loop overhead per leapfrog step is eliminated, and `vmap` across chains achieves near-linear scaling to hundreds of chains on a single accelerator.

## 6 Limitations

1. **Multimodality.** Hamiltonian trajectories cannot tunnel between well-separated modes; momentum resampling gives exponentially small mode-jumping probability. NUTS is a *local* explorer.
2. **Discrete parameters.** HMC requires gradients; discrete latents must be marginalized out (NumPyro's `MixedHMC` [5] interleaves HMC with Gibbs updates, but mixing across discrete modes remains hard).
3. **Heavy tails.** Cauchy-like tails defeat both the mass-matrix estimate (infinite covariance) and the leapfrog integrator (unbounded gradients); divergences proliferate and no reparameterization fully cures them.
4. **Tree-doubling waste.** Up to half of gradient evaluations can occur in subtrees discarded by the stopping rule [2]; on near-Gaussian targets this is pure overhead versus fixed-L HMC.
5. **Adaptation fragility.** Short warmup, poor initialization, or weakly identified models yield wrong mass matrices; the sampler then mixes slowly *without* divergences or R̂ warnings — the silent failure mode.
6. **Compilation cost.** JAX-vectorized NUTS pays XLA compile time per model and struggles with dynamic control flow, which is why models must be written in functional, shape-static style.
7. **The max-treedepth bias.** Capping trajectories at 2¹⁰ steps truncates the ideal NUTS kernel; chronic saturation indicates the step size is too small or the geometry too twisted.

---

## 7 Conclusion

The No-U-Turn Sampler, together with dual-averaging step-size adaptation, windowed mass-matrix adaptation, divergence diagnostics, and JAX-vectorized compilation, transformed Hamiltonian Monte Carlo from an expert's instrument into infrastructure: the default, nearly tuning-free inference engine of modern probabilistic programming. Each component solves one piece of the tuning problem — NUTS the trajectory length via reversible tree doubling [1], primal–dual averaging the step size, Welford-windowed covariance the metric, divergences the *detection* of geometric failure [4], and NumPyro the *execution* via compiled, vectorized kernels [5]. The funnel remains the field's memento mori: a two-line model that defeats every global adaptation scheme and reminds us that geometry, not compute, is the fundamental limit. The active frontier — within-orbit local step-size adaptation [3] and delayed-rejection multiscale extensions — pushes adaptation ever deeper into the transition itself. Until then, the practitioner's creed stands: check divergences, check treedepth, reparameterize the funnel, and trust — but verify — the warmup.
