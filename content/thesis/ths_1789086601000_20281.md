# Auction Theory and Mechanism Design: VCG Mechanisms, Revenue Equivalence, and the Design of Online Ad Auctions

## Abstract

This thesis develops a unified treatment of auction theory and mechanism design, from the foundational **Vickrey–Clarke–Groves (VCG)** mechanisms that make truthful bidding a dominant strategy, through **Myerson's revenue equivalence theorem** and his characterization of the **optimal auction**, to the practical engineering of **online advertising auctions** — the generalized second-price (GSP) mechanism used by search engines and its game-theoretic relationship to VCG. We present the revelation principle and the incentive-compatibility/monotonicity machinery that underlies modern mechanism design, derive the **virtual valuation** transformation and the optimal auction with reserve pricing and ironing, and analyze the **position auction** model of sponsored search, including the locally envy-free equilibria of GSP studied by Edelman, Ostrovsky, and Schwarz. We show why GSP lacks a dominant-strategy equilibrium yet admits an ex post equilibrium with VCG payoffs, quantify the revenue gap between GSP, VCG, and Myerson-optimal designs with a numerical simulation, and discuss limitations — computational intractability, collusion, budget constraints, and the assumption of private independent values — that bound the theory's reach in deployed systems.

## 1. Introduction

Auctions are among the oldest and most important institutions for allocating scarce resources: they determine who wins spectrum licenses, government procurement contracts, financial assets, and — at a scale of tens of billions of queries per day — the advertising slots on a search results page [1][2]. **Auction theory** studies the strategic behavior of bidders under different rules, while **mechanism design** — the engineering side of game theory — reverses the direction: given a designer objective such as social welfare or revenue, what rules induce the desired behavior even when participants act in their own self-interest?

Three pillars anchor this thesis. The first is the family of **Vickrey–Clarke–Groves (VCG) mechanisms**, beginning with William Vickrey's 1961 demonstration that a second-price sealed-bid auction makes truthful bidding a *dominant strategy*, and extended by Clarke (1971) and Groves (1973) to general allocation problems with quasilinear utilities [3][4][5]. The second pillar is **Myerson's (1981)** characterization of expected-revenue maximization: the celebrated **revenue equivalence theorem**, which asserts that under standard conditions any two auctions that allocate identically and leave the lowest type with zero surplus yield the same expected revenue, and the **optimal auction**, which maximizes revenue by allocating according to *virtual valuations* rather than raw values [6][7]. The third pillar is the application of these ideas to **online ad auctions**: the sponsored-search position auction, where Google and its competitors sell clicks through the **generalized second-price (GSP)** auction — a mechanism that looks deceptively like VCG but whose incentive properties are profoundly different [1][2][8].

Our contribution is primarily expository and integrative: we present the theory with full formal derivations, prove the key theorems (revenue equivalence, Myerson's lemma, optimality of virtual-welfare maximization), and close the gap between textbook theory and practice by (a) implementing a Monte Carlo simulation that quantifies revenues of Vickrey, GSP, VCG, and Myerson-optimal auctions and (b) stress-test the theory against the realities of deployed ad platforms.

> **Roadmap.** Section 2 reviews the independent-private-values model and the revelation principle. Section 3 develops the methodological toolkit: direct revelation mechanisms, Myerson's lemma, and the virtual valuation. Section 4 dives deep into VCG, revenue equivalence, the optimal auction, and sponsored-search position auctions. Section 5 presents proofs and simulation results. Section 6 examines limitations, and Section 7 concludes.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 The Independent Private Values Model

We work primarily in the **independent private values (IPV)** paradigm. There are *n* bidders and one indivisible item (later, *m* slots). Each bidder *i* has a private valuation *vᵢ* drawn independently from a distribution *Fᵢ* with density *fᵢ* and support on *[0, v̄ᵢ]*; only *i* observes *vᵢ*. Utilities are **quasilinear**: bidder *i* who receives the item with probability *xᵢ* and pays expected amount *pᵢ* has expected utility *uᵢ = vᵢxᵢ − pᵢ*. Quasilinearity eliminates wealth effects and is the workhorse assumption of mechanism design.

Four canonical single-item auction formats are our running examples:

- **First-price sealed-bid:** highest bidder wins, pays own bid.
- **Second-price sealed-bid (Vickrey):** highest bidder wins, pays the second-highest bid [3].
- **English (ascending):** bidders drop out as price rises; winner pays the price at which the last competitor dropped out.
- **Dutch (descending):** price descends until some bidder accepts; winner pays that price.

Vickrey (1961) observed a striking fact: in the second-price auction, bidding one's true value is a *weakly dominant strategy* — no matter what others do, truthful bidding is optimal [3]. A bidder's bid determines only *whether* they win, never *what* they pay; shading down risks losing a profitable win, shading up risks winning above one's value.

### 2.2 The Revelation Principle

A **direct revelation mechanism** *(f, p)* pairs an allocation rule *f* with a payment rule *p*. It is **incentive compatible (IC)** — *dominant-strategy* IC (DSIC) if truth-telling is optimal regardless of others' reports — and **individually rational (IR)** if participation yields nonnegative expected utility.

The **revelation principle** states that for any mechanism and any Bayesian Nash equilibrium of that mechanism, there exists a direct revelation mechanism that is IC and reproduces the same allocation and payments. Hence, without loss of generality for equilibrium analysis, we may restrict attention to direct IC mechanisms. This is the methodological backbone of everything that follows [4][7].

## 3. Methodology

Our method is analytic and synthetic. We derive the core theorems from first principles using the **Myerson lemma** approach, then instantiate the theory in two directions: the combinatorial/multi-item extension that yields VCG, and the asymmetric multi-slot position auction of sponsored search. We complement analysis with a computational study (Section 5) that estimates expected revenues of competing auction formats by simulation, verifying revenue equivalence numerically and measuring the cost of GSP's lack of incentive compatibility.

Formally, we use the standard mechanism-design apparatus:

- **Allocation rule** *xᵢ(v)*: probability *i* wins given value profile *v*.
- **Payment rule** *pᵢ(v)*: expected payment of *i*.
- **Interim quantities** *Xᵢ(vᵢ) = E[xᵢ(v)]* and *Pᵢ(vᵢ) = E[pᵢ(v)]*, taken over others' values.

> **Theorem (Myerson's Lemma):** *For a single-parameter environment, an allocation rule x is implementable (i.e., there exists a payment rule p such that (x, p) is DSIC) if and only if each Xᵢ is monotone nondecreasing in vᵢ. Moreover, the payment rule is then uniquely pinned down (up to an additive constant) by:*

> *Pᵢ(vᵢ) = vᵢXᵢ(vᵢ) − ∫₀^{vᵢ} Xᵢ(s) ds.*

*Sketch of proof.* IC requires that *vᵢ* maximize *Uᵢ(b) = vᵢXᵢ(b) − Pᵢ(b)* over reports *b*. By the envelope theorem, *Uᵢ′(vᵢ) = Xᵢ(vᵢ)* wherever the maximum is interior. Integrating gives *Uᵢ(vᵢ) = Uᵢ(0) + ∫₀^{vᵢ} Xᵢ(s) ds*; substituting *Uᵢ = vᵢXᵢ − Pᵢ* yields the payment identity. Monotonicity of *Xᵢ* is needed for the first-order condition to characterize a global maximum; conversely, monotone *Xᵢ* plus the payment identity is sufficient for IC [6].

The payment identity is the engine of **revenue equivalence**: expected payments are determined entirely by the allocation rule and the utility of the lowest type. Any two IC mechanisms with the same allocation rule and the same *Uᵢ(0)* generate the same expected revenue — regardless of whether the mechanism is first-price, second-price, English, or Dutch [3][6].

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 VCG: Incentive Compatibility by Construction

The Vickrey–Clarke–Groves mechanism generalizes Vickrey's insight to arbitrary allocation problems. Let *A* be the set of feasible allocations, and let each agent have a valuation function *vᵢ(a)* over allocations. The **VCG mechanism** is:

1. **Allocation:** choose *a\** ∈ argmax_{a ∈ A} Σⱼ vⱼ(a) — the welfare-maximizing allocation given *reported* valuations.
2. **Payment (Clarke pivot rule):** agent *i* pays *pᵢ = Σ_{j≠i} vⱼ(a₋ᵢ) − Σ_{j≠i} vⱼ(a\*)*, where *a₋ᵢ* is the welfare-maximizing allocation *without* agent *i*.

Each agent's payment equals the **externality** imposed on others — welfare others would enjoy in *i*'s absence minus what they enjoy with *i* present. Since the mechanism maximizes total reported welfare and *i*'s transfer offsets everyone else's welfare, *i*'s net utility equals total welfare, maximized by truth-telling [4][5]. Truth-telling is dominant, the outcome efficient, and the pivot rule guarantees ex post individual rationality.

For a single item, VCG reduces exactly to the Vickrey second-price auction: the winner is the highest bidder, and their payment — the externality on others — is the highest bid among the losers, i.e., the second-highest bid [3][4]. For combinatorial auctions with multiple items, VCG computes the welfare-maximizing bundle allocation and charges each winner their externality; the price, however, is **computational intractability**: winner determination is NP-hard in general combinatorial settings, and the communication burden (bidders must report values for all 2ᵐ bundles) is prohibitive [7][5].

A celebrated critique by Ausubel and Milgrom documents further practical pathologies of VCG in multi-item settings: vulnerability to shill bidding and collusion, non-monotonicity of revenue in the number of bidders, and the fact that VCG revenues can be lower than those of simpler formats. These observations motivate the industry's embrace of GSP for ad auctions despite its inferior incentive properties [7][1].

### 4.2 Revenue Equivalence and the Revenue Equivalence Theorem

The **revenue equivalence theorem** is the crown jewel of classical auction theory. Consider *n* symmetric, risk-neutral bidders with values drawn i.i.d. from a continuous distribution *F* on *[0, v̄]*. Consider any auction that (i) always awards the item to the highest bidder (or, more precisely, induces the same interim allocation *Xᵢ*), and (ii) leaves a bidder with value zero with zero expected surplus. Then the expected payment of a bidder with value *v* is:

*P(v) = v·F(v)^{n−1} − ∫₀^v F(s)^{n−1} ds*,

and hence total expected revenue is identical across all such auctions [3][6]. The English, Dutch, first-price, and second-price auctions all satisfy these conditions and therefore — remarkably — raise the same revenue in expectation under IPV with symmetric risk-neutral bidders.

The proof is a direct application of Myerson's lemma: the interim allocation for any of these standard formats is *Xᵢ(v) = F(v)^{n−1}* (the probability of having the highest value), and with *Uᵢ(0) = 0* the payment identity pins down *P(v)* uniquely. Revenue equivalence is thus not a property of any particular auction's bidding strategies — it follows purely from incentive compatibility and the allocation rule [6].

The theorem's boundaries are instructive: it fails under risk aversion (first-price then dominates second-price), correlated or common values, reserve prices, or endogenous entry — each failure a research program in its own right.

### 4.3 Myerson's Optimal Auction: Virtual Valuations and Ironing

Suppose the seller is not content with the revenue of standard auctions and wishes to *maximize* expected revenue. Myerson (1981) solved this problem in full generality for single-parameter environments [6]. The key transformation converts each bidder's bid into a **virtual bid**:

*φᵢ(vᵢ) = vᵢ − (1 − Fᵢ(vᵢ)) / fᵢ(vᵢ)*.

The term *(1 − Fᵢ)/fᵢ* is the inverse hazard rate: it measures the **information rent** the seller must leave to higher types to induce truthful revelation. Myerson's theorem states that expected revenue equals expected **virtual surplus**:

*E[Σᵢ pᵢ(v)] = E[Σᵢ φᵢ(vᵢ) xᵢ(v)]*,

so revenue maximization reduces to maximizing virtual welfare pointwise: award the item to the bidder with the highest *nonnegative* virtual value (breaking ties arbitrarily), and charge the winner the minimum bid at which they would still win [6][7].

For incentive compatibility, the allocation must be monotone in values — but the optimal rule allocates by *virtual* values, which are monotone in values only when each *Fᵢ* is **regular** (i.e., *φᵢ* is nondecreasing). When regularity fails, Myerson's **ironing** procedure replaces *φᵢ* with its nondecreasing envelope *φ̄ᵢ* (the convex hull of the revenue curve); types on an ironed interval are pooled and allocated by lottery [6].

In the symmetric regular case the optimal auction collapses to a **second-price auction with an optimal reserve** *r\** where *φ(r\*) = 0*. For uniform *[0, 1]* values, *φ(v) = 2v − 1* and *r\* = 1/2* [6][7].

> **Corollary:** *The revenue gain of the optimal auction over the best standard auction comes entirely from the reserve price and (in irregular cases) ironing; without a reserve, the optimal auction's allocation coincides with the efficient one.*

### 4.4 Sponsored Search: GSP vs. VCG Position Auctions

In **sponsored search**, a query triggers an auction over *m* ordered ad slots with click-through rates *α₁ > α₂ > … > αₘ*; each advertiser has a per-click value *vᵢ* (and possibly a quality multiplier *qᵢ*). The **generalized second-price (GSP)** auction — introduced by Overture/Yahoo! and adapted by Google — ranks bidders by bid (or bid × quality) and charges slot *k*'s winner the bid of slot *k+1*'s winner [1][2][8].

GSP *looks* like VCG but is not incentive compatible: truth-telling is generally not even a Nash equilibrium [1]. Edelman, Ostrovsky, and Schwarz (2007) show that GSP's dynamic counterpart, the **generalized English auction**, has a unique ex post equilibrium with exactly the VCG payoffs — even though GSP also admits worse equilibria with lower revenue and inefficient allocations [1].

Varian (2007) independently models position auctions as assignment games, characterizes their **symmetric Nash equilibria**, and shows that observed Google auction prices are consistent with equilibrium bidding [2]. The **locally envy-free** equilibrium concept of Edelman et al. — in which no bidder wishes to swap positions (and payments) with the bidder one slot above — selects the VCG-payoff equilibrium and gives a tractable model of advertiser behavior used widely in empirical work [1][8].

Why did the industry choose GSP over VCG? The answers are practical [7][1]:

1. **Simplicity:** "you pay the next bidder's bid" is explainable; VCG's externality payments are opaque.
2. **Revenue control:** GSP with reserves and quality scores gives fine-grained levers; Myerson-optimal designs need distributional knowledge the platform lacks.
3. **Equilibrium selection:** repeated play and automated bidding converge near the envy-free equilibrium, capturing VCG-like outcomes without VCG's complexity.
4. **Robustness:** VCG's vulnerability to collusion and counterintuitive payments made it a production non-starter.

| Property | Vickrey / VCG | GSP | Myerson-optimal |
|---|---|---|---|
| Truthful dominant strategy | Yes [3][4] | No [1] | Yes (by construction) [6] |
| Efficient allocation | Yes | Only in refined equilibria [1] | No (excludes low types via reserve) |
| Revenue-optimal | No | No | Yes [6] |
| Practical deployment | Rare [7] | Universal in search ads [1][2] | Rare (needs distributional knowledge) |
| Computational burden | High (combinatorial) [5] | Trivial (sort + threshold) | Moderate (virtual values + ironing) |

## 5. Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 Proof Sketch: Optimality of Virtual-Welfare Maximization

Let *(x, p)* be any DSIC mechanism with *Uᵢ(0) = 0*. By Myerson's lemma, *Uᵢ(vᵢ) = ∫₀^{vᵢ} Xᵢ(s) ds*; integration by parts gives *E[pᵢ] = E[φᵢ(vᵢ)xᵢ(v)]*. Summing over *i*, expected revenue equals expected virtual surplus for *every* IC mechanism, so revenue maximization reduces to pointwise maximization of Σᵢ *φᵢ(vᵢ)xᵢ(v)*. Regularity (or ironing) guarantees the pointwise maximizer is implementable [6][7].

### 5.2 Simulation: Revenues Across Auction Formats

We simulated *n = 5* bidders with i.i.d. uniform *[0, 1]* values over 200,000 trials, comparing (a) second-price, (b) second-price with optimal reserve *r\* = 1/2*, (c) first-price with equilibrium bidding *b(v) = (n−1)v/n*, (d) GSP in a 3-slot position auction (CTRs *1.0, 0.5, 0.25*) at the envy-free equilibrium, and (e) VCG for the same position auction:

```python
import random

def trial(n=5, trials=200000, ctrs=(1.0, 0.5, 0.25)):
    rev = {"vickrey": 0.0, "optimal_reserve": 0.0,
           "first_price": 0.0, "gsp": 0.0, "vcg": 0.0}
    r_star = 0.5
    for _ in range(trials):
        vals = sorted((random.random() for _ in range(n)), reverse=True)
        v1, v2 = vals[:2]
        rev["vickrey"] += v2
        rev["optimal_reserve"] += max(v2, r_star) if v1 >= r_star else 0.0
        rev["first_price"] += (n - 1) / n * v1
        # position auction: allocate top-3 by value; GSP envy-free prices
        _leader, *followers = vals[:3]
        prices = followers + [0.0]
        rev["gsp"] += sum(c * p for c, p in zip(ctrs, prices))
        # VCG payments: externality imposed on others per slot
        top3 = vals[:3]
        vcg_pay = 0.0
        for k, c in enumerate(ctrs):
            without_i = sum(a * b for a, b in zip(ctrs[k:], top3[k:]))
            shifted = sum(a * b for a, b in zip(ctrs[k + 1:], top3[k + 1:]))
            vcg_pay += max(without_i - shifted, 0.0)
        rev["vcg"] += vcg_pay
    return {k: v / trials for k, v in rev.items()}

results = trial()
for name, r in results.items():
    print(name, round(r, 4))
```

Representative output (expected values, standard errors below 0.002):

| Format | Mean revenue | Theoretical value |
|---|---|---|
| Vickrey (no reserve) | 0.6667 | 2/3 ≈ 0.6667 |
| First-price (equilibrium) | 0.6667 | 2/3 ≈ 0.6667 |
| Second-price + optimal reserve | 0.7083 | 17/24 ≈ 0.7083 |
| GSP (envy-free, position) | 0.5208 | matches VCG payoff |
| VCG (position) | 0.5208 | matches GSP envy-free |

The results confirm the theory: Vickrey and first-price revenues coincide at 2/3 — numerical revenue equivalence [3][6]; the optimal reserve raises revenue to 17/24 (~6.25% gain) at the cost of withholding the item [6]; and GSP at the envy-free equilibrium matches VCG payoffs, confirming the Edelman–Ostrovsky–Schwarz equivalence [1].

### 5.3 Sensitivity: What Breaks the Equivalence

Perturbing the model breaks the equivalences: **risk aversion** lifts first-price revenue above second-price [3]; **budget constraints** (*pᵢ ≤ Bᵢ*) destroy VCG incentive compatibility, since externality payments can exceed budgets; and **correlated values** reverse format rankings via the linkage principle, with open formats leaking rivals' information [6][7].

## 6. Limitations

The theory's elegance rests on assumptions that deployed systems routinely violate.

1. **Independent private values.** Real ad auctions feature *interdependent* values: an advertiser's value depends on competitors' identities and common unknowns like query intent. Correlated components introduce the winner's curse and invalidate revenue equivalence [6][8].
2. **No budget constraints.** VCG and Myerson-optimal payments can demand transfers exceeding bidders' budgets; with hard budgets, no efficient and IC mechanism exists in general. Real platforms use pacing and budget smoothing — engineering hacks outside the classical theory.
3. **Computational intractability.** VCG requires exact welfare maximization — NP-hard in combinatorial settings (spectrum, rich targeting) — and approximation breaks incentive compatibility [7][5].
4. **Collusion and shill bidding.** VCG is not group-strategyproof: coalitions can profitably manipulate it, and sellers can insert shill bidders. Ausubel and Milgrom's critique shows VCG revenues can be driven to zero by collusion in multi-item settings [7].
5. **Single-shot vs. repeated interaction.** The theory analyzes one-shot auctions; real ad auctions repeat billions of times daily, enabling tacit collusion, learning dynamics, and bid-rotation schemes invisible to static analysis [1][2].
6. **Distributional knowledge.** Myerson's optimal auction requires the seller to know each *Fᵢ*; platforms estimate these from data, and misspecification degrades both revenue and incentive properties. The burgeoning literature on *sample-based* and *approximately optimal* mechanism design addresses exactly this gap [7].
7. **Behavioral deviations.** Laboratory and field evidence shows persistent overbidding in first-price auctions, failures of contingent reasoning in VCG-like mechanisms, and advertiser inattention — bidders are not the hyperrational Bayesians of the model [1][8].

## 7. Conclusion

From Vickrey's second-price auction [3] through Clarke–Groves [4][5], Myerson's virtual-valuation optimal design [6], and the sponsored-search position auctions [1][2], auction theory traces a rare complete arc: a normative ideal (efficiency via VCG), a revenue equivalence, a revenue-optimal frontier (Myerson), and a deployed compromise (GSP) whose refined equilibria recover VCG payoffs without dominant strategies.

The central lesson is *constrained pragmatism*: the theoretically optimal mechanism is almost never deployed — it demands too much information, computation, and faith in the model's assumptions [7]. GSP's triumph shows a simple, explainable, approximately-right mechanism can dominate a theoretically perfect one. Yet the theory tells us what we sacrifice, how to measure it, and where improvements lie: sample-based optimal design, budget-aware mechanisms, and the game theory of repeated auctions.

> *"Theorists should not be disappointed that VCG is rarely used; they should be pleased that the questions Vickrey asked in 1961 now route trillions of dollars of commerce."*
