# The ZGC and Shenandoah Low-Latency Collectors: Load Barriers, Colored Pointers, and Concurrent Compaction ## Abstract Low-latency managed runtimes must reconcile two antagonistic demands: bounded pause times and compacting memory layout. This thesis compares the two dominant OpenJDK solutions — **ZGC**, built on *colored pointers* and *load barriers*, and **Shenandoah**, the region-based concurrent compacting collector built on *Brooks forwarding pointers* and *write barriers*. We trace each from first principles: how ZGC encodes mark, remap, and finalization state into the metadata bits of a 64-bit pointer and recovers the raw address through *heap multi-mapping*, and how Shenandoah attaches a forwarding word to every object header so concurrent evacuation remains correct under mutator writes. We formalize the load-barrier and Brooks-pointer correctness invariants as theorems, quantify the engineering trade-offs — ZGC's ~4% throughput overhead on SPECjbb2015 versus Shenandoah's per-object forwarding-word space tax — and review measured pause distributions from production deployments. The study concludes that both collectors deliver sub-millisecond to single-digit-millisecond pauses independent of heap size, but embody complementary philosophies: *metadata in the pointer* versus *metadata in the object*, with direct consequences for barrier cost, NUMA locality, and generational evolution. ## 1. Introduction The latency problem in automatic memory management is older than the Java Virtual Machine itself. Tracing collectors from Baker's *incremental* and *copying* schemes of 1978 [1] through Appel, Ellis, and Li's *real-time concurrent* design [2] have pursued the same elusive target: *pause times that do not scale with heap size*. For two decades the production frontier was G1, whose region-based, partially concurrent collection still concentrated compaction into mixed-collection pauses that degraded as the heap grew. The failure mode is well understood: when a collector must stop all mutator threads to rewrite the object graph, the pause is a function of the live set, and the live set is a function of the business. Two projects broke this coupling within OpenJDK, and both shipped as production collectors. ZGC, originally delivered as an experimental collector in JDK 11 [3] and promoted to production in JDK 15 [4], guarantees — as an engineering goal — pause times of a *few milliseconds*, independent of heap and live-set size, over heaps from 8 MB to 16 TB. Shenandoah, integrated experimentally in JDK 12 and contributed by Red Hat, targets the same regime through a different primitive. Both are concurrent, region-based, compacting, and NUMA-aware, yet their correctness mechanisms are nearly dual: - **ZGC** performs its concurrent work behind a *load barrier*: every reference loaded by the mutator passes through a color check, and relocation proceeds concurrently because stale pointers remain *usable*. - **Shenandoah** performs its concurrent work behind a *write barrier* and the *Brooks pointer*: every object carries a forwarding word, and all accesses resolve through it, so evacuation can proceed while mutators write. This thesis compares the two designs at the level of mechanisms, invariants, and measured behavior. Our central claim is that the choice between them is the choice between *storing GC metadata in the reference* (colored pointers) and *storing GC metadata in the object* (Brooks pointers) — and that this single decision propagates through barrier cost, address-space consumption, and the feasibility of generational layering. ## 2. Background and Related Work Garbage collection design space is conventionally decomposed along three axes: *what is reclaimed* (mark-sweep vs. copy/compact), *when collection happens* (stop-the-world vs. concurrent/incremental), and *how the mutator is kept safe* (barrier design). The classic concurrent collectors — Dijkstra's on-the-fly scheme, Steele's parallel variant, and Boehm–Demers–Weiser conservative scanning — all preserved correctness during concurrent marking by combining snapshot-at-the-beginning or incremental-update barriers with write interception. Compaction, however, resisted concurrency: moving an object while a mutator might dereference the old copy demands either *globally stopping the world* to update references or a *decoupling mechanism* that lets old and new copies coexist. Two decoupling mechanisms preceded the collectors studied here. *Brooks forwarding pointers* (1978), proposed by Rodney Brooks in the context of parallel copying collection, attach to every object a word that always names the most current copy; mutators resolve through it, so a collector may evacuate objects while the program runs, atomically swinging the forwarding word with compare-and-swap. *Colored pointers* — first demonstrated at scale by the Azul C4 collector's loaded-value barrier [5] and by SAP's *Pauseless* design — instead steal unused bits of a 64-bit address to carry GC state, letting a *load barrier* detect and repair stale references at dereference time. ZGC generalizes the Azul approach to the OpenJDK HotSpot runtime, adding *multi-mapped memory*: the same physical pages are mapped into several virtual address ranges, so "unmasking" a colored pointer costs nothing more than choosing which virtual alias to dereference [6]. Shenandoah, by contrast, generalizes Brooks' design to a fully concurrent regional collector with a write barrier that enforces the *to-space invariant* ("all writes happen into the to-space copy") [7]. Both collectors therefore sit in the lineage of Dijkstra/Steele/Baker rather than breaking from it: the novelty is engineering these abstractions at commodity scale with hardware-assisted cheap barriers. Contemporary alternatives sharpen the comparison. C4 on Azul's Vega appliances remains the latency leader but requires proprietary hardware cooperation; Go's concurrent collector achieves sub-millisecond pauses with a hybrid write barrier but performs *no compaction at all*; .NET's Server GC offers concurrent background compaction only on workstations. ZGC and Shenandoah are the only fully general-purpose, compacting, concurrent collectors available on stock OpenJDK. --- ## 3. Methodology This thesis is a *comparative architecture study* grounded in primary engineering sources rather than a new empirical campaign. Our method has three components. 1. **Primary-source reconstruction.** We reconstruct each collector's mechanisms from the OpenJDK design documents — JEP 333 for ZGC [3], the ZGC generational design notes [8], and the Shenandoah wiki's implementation overview [9] — cross-checked against the HotSpot source layout they describe and the technical talks of the lead engineers (Liden's ZGC slide deck [6]; Shipilev's Shenandoah series [7]). 2. **Invariant formalization.** For each correctness-critical mechanism (the ZGC load barrier, the Shenandoah Brooks-pointer update protocol) we state the key invariant as a theorem and sketch a proof. This lets the two designs be compared *as protocols* rather than as code, which is the level at which the engineering trade-offs are visible. 3. **Empirical synthesis.** We collect published pause-time measurements and barrier-overhead figures from the same sources and from independent evaluations, and we reproduce the decisive micro-behavior — barrier fast-path cost and evacuation write-storm avoidance — in small synthetic models written in Python and Rust. These models are illustrative, not competitive benchmarks; they exist to make the invariants executable. > **Theorem (Barrier-correctness criterion):** A concurrent compacting collector is correct iff every reference observed by the mutator *eventually* resolves to the canonical (to-space) copy of its object, and no mutator operation writes through a stale (from-space) reference. > > **Proof sketch:** Decompose correctness into (a) *reachability preservation* — the collector never reclaims an object reachable from a mutator-visible root — and (b) *access safety* — every read sees the latest field values, every write lands in the canonical copy. (a) follows from snapshot-at-the-beginning marking if the barrier covers all reference stores; (b) is what the load-barrier (ZGC) or Brooks-pointer (Shenandoah) repair provides. Both collectors satisfy the criterion by construction, as shown in §§4.2 and 4.4. ∎ ## 4. Deep Dive ### 4.1 ZGC's Colored Pointers and Multi-Mapped Memory ZGC encodes per-reference GC metadata in the pointer itself. On 64-bit platforms only the low 42 bits (in the classic non-generational layout) address the heap; the high bits carry *color* information. On x86-64, the canonical layout reserved the top bits as follows: | Bits (from MSB) | Width | Meaning | |---|---|---| | 63–46 | 18 | Unused (sign extension zone) | | 45–44 | 2 | Unused | | 43–42 | 2 | Finalizable / color extension | | 41 | 1 | Remapped | | 40 | 1 | Marked1 | | 39 | 1 | Marked0 | | 38–0 | 39 | Object offset within a 512 GB view (16 TB heap across views) | The three color bits select among *virtual address views* of the same physical heap. ZGC maps the entire heap three times into the process address space [6]: - `0x00000C0000000000` — the **Marked0** view (used during marking cycle 0), - `0x0000080000000000` — the **Marked1** view (cycle 1, alternating), - `0x0000100000000000` — the **Remapped** view (references already relocated), - `0x0000140000000000` — the **colorless** base view (the true address). Because the views are *aliases of identical physical pages*, converting a colored pointer to a usable address is a matter of **address arithmetic rather than data movement**: masking the color bits yields a virtual address in the remapped view, and the MMU resolves it to the same physical page. The load barrier therefore has a *fast path of a handful of machine instructions* — on x86-64, a test of the color bits against the current global phase followed by a masked dereference — and measured overhead of roughly **4% on SPECjbb2015** [6]. This is the central ZGC insight: *pointer metadata is free to test, and views are free to switch*. Generational ZGC (JEP 439, delivered in JDK 21) refines this layout. Because multi-mapping made heap-size accounting confusing (the same memory appeared three times in `ps` output), the generational design drops multi-mapping and moves the metadata into the **low-order bits** of the pointer, keeping colorless pointers in registers and the hardware stack and translating only at heap-resident reference boundaries [8]. A single shift instruction can then simultaneously test and strip the metadata. The design space thus spans a spectrum from *many virtual views* to *dense in-pointer metadata* — both instances of the same principle. ```rust /// ZGC-style colored pointer: decode color bits and strip to raw address. /// Mirrors the fast-path logic of the HotSpot x86-64 load barrier. const MARKED0: u64 = 1 << 39; const MARKED1: u64 = 1 << 40; const REMAPPED: u64 = 1 << 41; const COLOR_MASK: u64 = MARKED0 | MARKED1 | REMAPPED; #[inline(always)] fn zgc_load_barrier(oop: u64, good_mask: u64) -> u64 { // Fast path: pointer already has a "good" color for this phase. if oop & !good_mask == 0 { return oop & !COLOR_MASK; // strip color, dereference via remapped view } slow_path_remap(oop) // mark/relocate/remap, then heal the reference } fn slow_path_remap(oop: u64) -> u64 { // Concurrent mark/relocate logic elided; returns healed pointer. oop & !COLOR_MASK } ``` ### 4.2 The ZGC Load Barrier and Concurrent Relocation ZGC's collection cycle is *mark → relocate → remap*, all largely concurrent. Marking is striped and parallel; relocation selects sparsely-populated regions as the *relocation set* and copies live objects to fresh regions while mutators run; remapping lazily heals stale references as they are loaded. The only stop-the-world phases are *root scanning* (pause mark start, pause mark end, pause relocate start) — bounded by the root set, not the heap. > **Theorem (ZGC load-barrier safety):** During concurrent relocation, any mutator load of a reference resolves to an object whose fields reflect all writes that happened-before the load. > > **Proof:** Consider a load of reference *r* pointing to object *O* in the relocation set. If *O* has already been evacuated, *r* carries a stale color; the barrier's slow path detects the color mismatch, consults the off-heap forwarding table (populated by the relocating thread), and heals *r* to the new copy before the load completes. If *O* has not yet been evacuated, the barrier may itself perform the relocation (mutator-assisted relocation), guaranteeing that the subsequent load reads the to-space copy. Because the forwarding table is written before the from-space pages are reused, there is no window in which a stale reference resolves to reclaimed memory. ∎ Two engineering details make this practical. First, **forwarding tables live off-heap**, so no forwarding words pollute object layout and old pages can be reused immediately after evacuation — there is no separate mark-compact fallback for full GC [6]. Second, **relocation is NUMA-aware**: pages are allocated and evacuated with node affinity, so the compacting collector does not silently destroy memory locality on multi-socket machines. ### 4.3 Shenandoah's Brooks Pointers and the To-Space Invariant Shenandoah takes the dual approach: the metadata lives *in the object*, not in the pointer. Every Java object carries an extra header word — the **Brooks forwarding pointer** — which always names the most current (to-space) copy of the object [7]. When the collector evacuates an object, it copies the payload to a to-region and atomically swings the forwarding word with a CAS; reads and writes issued against the from-space copy are transparently redirected. The correctness contract is the **to-space invariant**: > **Theorem (To-space invariant):** Under Shenandoah's barrier protocol, every mutator write lands in the to-space copy of its target object, and every mutator read observes the to-space copy. > > **Proof:** All object accesses are compiled through the Brooks indirection: the barrier loads the forwarding word *before* touching fields. Writes additionally pass a write barrier that (a) resolves the to-space copy, (b) performs the store there, and (c) enqueues the reference update if needed for SATB marking. Evacuation proceeds by copying and CAS-installing the forwarding word; because mutators never address the from-copy directly, a concurrent copy cannot be observed mid-flight, and the CAS guarantees a single winner for the canonical copy. Reference updating later rewrites heap references to point at to-space directly, but correctness never depends on it having completed. ∎ Notably, Shenandoah's original design **evacuates on write, not on read** [7]: reading an object in a from-region does *not* trigger evacuation. This is deliberate — a "read storm" in which every load paid a copy would make latency bursty and unpredictable. Evacuation is driven by GC threads selecting collection-set regions plus mutator assists on the write path. The price of the Brooks pointer is explicit: **one extra word per object**, roughly 10–20% space overhead depending on average object size, plus a **write barrier on every reference store**. Since Java programs execute far more loads than stores, Shenandoah's steady-state throughput overhead is typically *lower* than ZGC's load-barrier overhead — but its worst-case pause structure differs, as §5 discusses. ### 4.4 The Shenandoah Concurrent Cycle and Barrier Design A full Shenandoah cycle interleaves five stop-the-world pauses — each O(root set), not O(heap) — with concurrent phases [9]: 1. **Pause Init Mark** — prepare heap, scan roots. 2. **Concurrent Marking** — SATB trace of the reachable graph. 3. **Pause Final Mark** — drain queues, choose the collection set, pre-evacuate roots. 4. **Concurrent Cleanup** — reclaim immediately-garbage regions. 5. **Concurrent Evacuation** — copy live objects out of the collection set (the phase unique among OpenJDK collectors). 6. **Pause Init Update Refs / Concurrent Update References / Pause Final Update Refs** — rewrite all references to evacuated objects, then recycle collection-set regions. ```python # Illustrative model of the Shenandoah evacuation protocol. # Shows the Brooks-pointer CAS and the to-space invariant under # concurrent mutator writes (simplified; no real threading here). import threading class ShenandoahObject: def __init__(self, fields): self.fields = dict(fields) self.fwd = self # Brooks pointer: initially self-referential def evacuate(obj, lock): """GC thread: copy to to-space, swing forwarding word exactly once.""" with lock: if obj.fwd is not obj: # lost the race: someone evacuated first return obj.fwd clone = ShenandoahObject(obj.fields) clone.fwd = clone obj.fwd = clone # atomic publish of the to-space copy return clone def write_barrier(obj, key, value): """Mutator write: always resolve through the Brooks pointer.""" target = obj.fwd # to-space invariant: writes land here target.fields[key] = value def read_barrier(obj, key): """Mutator read: select the to-space copy.""" return obj.fwd.fields[key] ``` Under allocation pressure beyond the collector's ability to keep up, Shenandoah can *degenerate* to a stop-the-world full collection (logged as `Degenerated GC`) — a safety valve ZGC's design avoids needing because relocation never blocks allocation in the same way. This degenerate path is the principal tail-latency risk of the Shenandoah design. --- ## 5. Empirical Results and Proofs Published measurements, all from primary sources or their direct engineering reports, converge on a consistent picture: | Metric | ZGC (non-generational) | Shenandoah | |---|---|---| | Typical max pause | < 1 ms – few ms [3][6] | < 1 ms – few ms [7][9] | | Pause scaling | Independent of heap, live set, root set (goal) | Independent of heap/live set; O(root set) | | Heap range | 8 MB – 16 TB [3] | Tested to ~100 GB+ live [7] | | Barrier overhead | ~4% on SPECjbb2015 [6] | Write-barrier dominated; lower steady-state | | Space overhead | Forwarding tables + multi-mapped VM | ~1 word/object (Brooks pointer) | | Full GC fallback | None (relocation covers it) [6] | Degenerated STW GC under extreme pressure [9] | Three results deserve emphasis. **First**, the pause distributions: ZGC's published SPECjbb2015 runs show max pauses measured in the *hundreds of microseconds*, with the stop-the-world phases (root scanning) dominating; Shenandoah's LRUFragger runs on a 100 GB heap with ~80 GB live data show all five pauses in the *sub-millisecond* range even while concurrent evacuation moves gigabytes [7]. **Second**, the barrier economics invert intuition: although loads outnumber stores, ZGC's load-barrier fast path is so cheap (a bit-test and masked dereference) that its measured throughput cost is only ~4%, while Shenandoah pays its write barrier on every store plus the per-object forwarding word. **Third**, generational layering arrived asymmetrically: ZGC gained a generational mode in JDK 21 (JEP 439) [8] precisely because its colored-pointer scheme could be re-laid with low-bit metadata; Shenandoah's generational mode (JDK 24, JEP 475) required reworking the Brooks-pointer protocol around generations. The formal results of §§4.2–4.4 are *safety* proofs, not liveness proofs. Both collectors additionally require *progress* arguments: ZGC must show relocation keeps pace with allocation (its heuristics size the relocation set from allocation rate); Shenandoah must show the concurrent phases complete before the heap exhausts (its pacer throttles allocation). These are engineering control problems rather than theorems, and they are where production incidents concentrate. --- ## 6. Limitations and Threats to Validity No honest comparison can present either collector as a solved problem. We enumerate the principal limitations. - **ZGC's address-space appetite.** Multi-mapping triples the *virtual* footprint of the heap (though not physical), confusing capacity tooling and capping the heap at 16 TB in the classic layout. Generational ZGC removes multi-mapping but pays with more complex barrier code and the colored/colorless translation at heap boundaries [8]. - **Load-barrier ubiquity.** ZGC's barrier fires on *every reference load*, including in tight loops the JIT cannot always de-barrier. The ~4% figure is a benchmark average; pointer-chasing workloads can see higher. - **Shenandoah's space tax is structural.** The Brooks word cannot be optimized away for small objects; a workload of tiny objects pays the full word per object indefinitely, unlike ZGC's per-pointer metadata which costs address bits rather than heap bytes. - **Degenerate GC.** Shenandoah's STW fallback under extreme allocation pressure is a genuine tail-latency hazard for latency-SLO workloads; ZGC has no equivalent cliff, only graceful throughput degradation. - **Root-set pauses.** Both collectors retain O(root-set) pauses. Applications with enormous thread counts or JNI-heavy root sets can observe pauses that violate the "few milliseconds" goal regardless of heap size. - **Evaluation bias.** Most published numbers come from the collectors' own authors on favorable workloads (SPECjbb2015, LRUFragger). Independent, adversarial benchmarking across diverse production shapes remains sparse — a threat to the external validity of every figure in §5. - **NUMA asymmetry.** Both collectors claim NUMA awareness, but on multi-socket machines with imbalanced allocation, cross-node evacuation traffic can reintroduce latency the models ignore. ## 7. Conclusion ZGC and Shenandoah represent the two canonical answers to concurrent compaction on commodity hardware, and their duality is instructive. **ZGC stores GC metadata in the reference**: colored pointers plus heap multi-mapping make stale references *harmless* rather than *impossible*, and a cheap load barrier heals them lazily. **Shenandoah stores GC metadata in the object**: the Brooks forwarding word makes the to-space copy *authoritative at all times*, and a write barrier keeps it so. The first design buys tighter pause guarantees at the cost of a load barrier on every dereference and a profligate virtual address layout; the second buys lower steady-state overhead at the cost of a per-object space tax and a degenerate full-GC fallback. The field is converging rather than diverging: generational modes have arrived for both (JDK 21 for ZGC, JDK 24 for Shenandoah), barrier fast paths keep shrinking with compiler cooperation, and the remaining pauses are increasingly dominated by root scanning — a problem neither colored pointers nor Brooks pointers address. The next frontier is not a better barrier but *root-set independence*: sub-millisecond pauses that hold regardless of heap, live set, *and* root set. Whichever collector reaches that goal first will do so standing on the invariants proved here. ## References [1] H. G.
