# Quorum Certificates, View Changes, and Pacemakers: A Deep Technical Treatment of PBFT, HotStuff, and Tendermint in Byzantine Fault Tolerance

## Abstract

Byzantine fault-tolerant state machine replication turns *n = 3f + 1* replicas, up to *f* of them arbitrarily malicious, into a single linearizable service. This thesis unifies three landmark protocols — Castro and Liskov's PBFT (1999), Yin et al.'s HotStuff (2019), and Buchman's Tendermint (2016/2018) — through the shared mechanisms of *quorum certificates*, *view changes*, and *synchronizers* (pacemakers). We reconstruct PBFT's three-phase normal path and its quadratic, bug-prone view-change machinery; HotStuff's pipelined prepare/pre-commit/commit core with constant-size threshold-signature quorum certificates, linear view changes, and a modular pacemaker restoring responsiveness after GST; and Tendermint's rotating-proposer two-phase scheme with *polka*-based locking, timeout-gated rounds, and gossip dissemination defeating the hidden-lock hazard. Central results include the quorum-intersection safety argument, HotStuff's 2-chain and 3-chain commit rules, Tendermint's locked-on-precommit invariant, and the partial-synchrony liveness arguments via doubling timers and pacemakers. Annotated Python, Haskell, Rust, and TLA⁺ specifications accompany an empirical synthesis of reported performance figures, closing with limitations in setup trust, timer fragility, and proof-of-stake-specific hazards.

## 1. Introduction

The Byzantine Generals problem, formalized by Lamport, Shostak, and Pease, asks whether loyal generals can reach agreement when traitors send conflicting messages. State machine replication (SMR) elevates this to systems practice: *n* replicas must execute the same deterministic sequence of client requests despite up to *f* Byzantine replicas. The canonical bound is *n ≥ 3f + 1* in the partially synchronous model — messages eventually arrive within an unknown bound Δ after an unknown Global Stabilization Time (GST). This is the right model for the Internet: weaker than synchrony, yet strong enough for liveness, while safety must hold *even under asynchrony*.

Practical Byzantine Fault Tolerance (PBFT) [2] was the first protocol to make this bound *practical*: a replicated NFS service only 3% slower than unreplicated NFS, via a stable-leader three-phase protocol (pre-prepare, prepare, commit) plus a view-change subprotocol electing a new primary on suspicion of failure. PBFT's normal path is cheap, but its view change is O(n²) in communication and O(n³) in authenticators — notoriously bug-prone, and the epicenter of replication complexity for two decades.

HotStuff [1] reframes the design space around *quorum certificates* (QCs): a leader collects *n − f* threshold-signature shares into a single constant-size certificate. Every phase then shares one communication pattern (leader broadcast, votes to leader), yielding *responsiveness* (progress at actual network speed δ, not Δ) and *linearity* (O(n) per view, including view changes) simultaneously. View change becomes an ordinary phase; liveness is isolated in a modular *pacemaker*. Its chained variant pipelines decisions, with a three-chain commit rule finalizing blocks at network speed.

Tendermint [3][4] targets proof-of-stake blockchains with stake-weighted rotating validators: a two-phase protocol (prevote, precommit) with an explicit locking rule (lock on *polka*, i.e., *2f + 1* prevotes) and timeout-gated rounds defeating the *hidden lock* problem. Gossip-based vote dissemination and round-based rotation make leader failover trivial — rotation *is* the normal path — at the cost of O(n²) gossip traffic and Δ-gated responsiveness.

This thesis unifies these protocols through the three mechanisms that determine their character — **quorum certificates**, **view changes**, and **synchronizers/pacemakers** — then proves the key invariants, reconstructs the commit rules, and synthesizes reported performance.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 System model and the 3f + 1 bound

We assume *n* replicas on authenticated point-to-point links, up to *f* Byzantine (arbitrary, possibly colluding). The network is *partially synchronous*: after an unknown GST, every message between correct replicas arrives within an unknown Δ; before GST, delays are arbitrary, and safety must hold regardless.

> **Theorem:** No deterministic protocol solves Byzantine agreement with *n ≤ 3f* in partial synchrony.

The standard proof partitions replicas into three groups of size ≤ *f* and constructs indistinguishable executions. With *n = 3f + 1*, any two quorums of size *n − f = 2f + 1* intersect in at least

|*Q₁* ∩ *Q₂*| ≥ |*Q₁*| + |*Q₂*| − *n* = (2f + 1) + (2f + 1) − (3f + 1) = *f + 1*

replicas, of which at least one is correct. This intersection property is the *sole* combinatorial fact on which all three protocols' safety rests.

Key derived facts:

- A *quorum* is any set of *n − f = 2f + 1* replicas; equivalently, *2f + 1* votes.
- A *quorum certificate (QC)* is a cryptographic object attesting that a quorum voted for a value in a view/round — implicit in PBFT (a set of *2f + 1* signed messages), explicit and constant-size in HotStuff via threshold signatures.
- Liveness additionally requires eventual synchrony and a correct leader (non-faulty, post-GST, timers exceeding Δ).

### 2.2 Authenticators and threshold signatures

PBFT uses *authenticators*: vectors of MACs, one per recipient, over pairwise symmetric keys. Multicasting an authenticator costs O(n) MACs; a *2f + 1*-message collection is O(n²)-sized — the root of PBFT's quadratic view-change cost.

HotStuff replaces authenticators with **threshold signatures** (e.g., BLS). A *(t, n)*-threshold scheme lets any *t = n − f* replicas each produce a *signature share*; any *t* shares combine into a single constant-size signature verifiable under one aggregate public key. BLS signatures support non-interactive aggregation (shares are group elements; combining is point addition), at the cost of a trusted or distributed (DKG) setup — revisited in Section 6.

The QC abstraction then becomes a constant-size `QC(v, view) = (view, value v, σ)` where σ proves *2f + 1* distinct replicas signed `(view, v)` — making HotStuff's phases O(n) in messages *and* bits: linearity.

### 2.3 Partial synchrony, responsiveness, and the pacemaker

*Responsiveness* (crystallized by the HotStuff paper) means a correct leader drives consensus at actual network delay δ, not the worst-case bound Δ. PBFT's normal path is responsive; its view change is not. Tendermint is deliberately *non-responsive*: fixed timeouts collect gossip, trading latency for simplicity and rotation robustness.

A *pacemaker* (*synchronizer* in earlier literature) is a module that, after GST, co-locates correct replicas in one view with a correct leader long enough for QC formation. HotStuff's insight is *separating* safety from liveness: voting/commit rules guarantee safety in asynchrony, while any correct pacemaker (e.g., doubling timeouts) guarantees liveness. PBFT and Tendermint intertwine timer logic with the core protocol instead.

## 3. Methodology

We reconstruct the three protocols in a common notation, cross-checked against the primary sources and the formal Tendermint specification [3]:

1. **Faithful reconstruction** in each protocol's own phase structure (PBFT: pre-prepare/prepare/commit + view-change/new-view [2]; HotStuff: prepare/pre-commit/commit/decide with NEW-VIEW [1]; Tendermint: propose/prevote/precommit across heights and rounds [3][4]).
2. **Common-metric comparison** — normal-case and view-change complexity, authenticator cost, responsiveness, commit latency, rotation cost (Table 1).
3. **Executable specification** — Python (PBFT normal path), Haskell (QC algebra, Tendermint locking), Rust (BLS share aggregation), TLA⁺ (HotStuff commit rule). Pedagogical distillations, not production code.

### 3.1 PBFT: the three-phase normal path

With stable primary *p = v mod n* in view *v*:

1. **PRE-PREPARE.** Primary multicasts `⟨PRE-PREPARE, v, seq, D(m)⟩`, assigning a sequence number.
2. **PREPARE.** Each backup multicasts `⟨PREPARE, v, seq, D(m), i⟩` on acceptance. Replica *i* is *prepared* once it holds the pre-prepare plus *2f* matching prepares (*2f + 1* total) [2].
3. **COMMIT.** Each prepared replica multicasts `⟨COMMIT, v, seq, D(m), i⟩`. On *2f + 1* matching commits, *i* executes *m* and replies; the client accepts after *f + 1* matching replies.

```python
# PBFT replica: normal-path message accounting (pedagogical)
class PBFTReplica:
    def __init__(self, rid, n, f):
        self.rid, self.n, self.f = rid, n, f
        self.prepared, self.committed = {}, {}
        self.log = []

    def on_pre_prepare(self, view, seq, digest, primary):
        if primary != view % self.n: return
        if not self._in_watermark(seq): return
        self._multicast(("PREPARE", view, seq, digest, self.rid))

    def on_prepare(self, view, seq, digest, prepares):
        key = (view, seq, digest)
        if len(prepares.get(key, set())) >= 2 * self.f:   # + own vote => 2f+1
            self.prepared[key] = True
            self._multicast(("COMMIT", view, seq, digest, self.rid))

    def on_commit(self, view, seq, digest, commits):
        key = (view, seq, digest)
        if len(commits.get(key, set())) >= 2 * self.f + 1:
            self.committed[key] = True
            self.execute(seq, digest)
```

The trouble begins at **view change**: on suspecting the primary, replica *i* multicasts `⟨VIEW-CHANGE, v+1, P⟩` bundling its prepared certificates. The new primary collects *2f + 1* view-change messages — each O(n)-sized — and broadcasts a `NEW-VIEW` of size O(n²), each backup verifying at O(n²) authenticator cost: O(n³) total, O(n²) with signature batching. This view-change logic is the part of PBFT most often cited as bug-prone [1][6].

### 3.2 HotStuff: pipelined phases over quorum certificates

HotStuff restructures every phase into one pattern: **leader → all** broadcasts a proposal carrying the parent QC; **all → leader** send votes (threshold-signature shares) to the *next* view's leader, which aggregates *n − f* shares into a QC.

The four logical phases are *prepare*, *pre-commit*, *commit*, *decide*, but they pipeline: the QC formed in view *v*'s prepare phase serves as the pre-commit QC of view *v + 1*, and so on. A block *B* carries `B.qc` (the QC justifying its parent); a replica votes for *B* iff the safe-node rule holds — `B.view > lockedQC.view` implies `B.qc.view ≥ lockedQC.view` — updating `lockedQC` on pre-commit QC formation and `prepareQC` on prepare QC formation.

**View change as a phase.** On timer expiry, *i* sends `NEW-VIEW` with its highest `prepareQC` to the next leader, which proposes extending the maximum-view QC among *n − f* NEW-VIEW messages. NEW-VIEW messages are O(1)-size and the proposal is an O(n) broadcast, so the entire view change is **O(n)** — the cost of a normal phase [1].

**Pacemaker.** Liveness is delegated to a `Pacemaker` module. The paper proves *any* pacemaker achieving eventual view synchronization (all correct replicas co-located in a view with a correct leader) yields liveness, while safety is pacemaker-independent. The reference pacemaker doubles timeouts per view, guaranteeing eventual overlap post-GST.

### 3.3 Tendermint: rotating proposers, locks, and timeouts

Tendermint structures consensus per *(height h, round r)* with stake-weighted rotating proposer `proposer(h, r)`. Each round has three timeout-gated steps:

1. **Propose.** Proposer broadcasts `⟨PROPOSAL, h, r, v, vr⟩` (*vr = −1* for a new value, else the round of an observed polka). Validators wait `timeoutPropose`.
2. **Prevote.** Validators gossip `⟨PREVOTE, h, r, v/nil⟩`. On *2f + 1* prevotes for *v* (a *polka*), a validator *locks* (`lockedValue`, `lockedRound`); then waits `timeoutPrevote`.
3. **Precommit.** Validators gossip `⟨PRECOMMIT, h, r, v/nil⟩`. On *2f + 1* precommits for *v*, the validator commits *v* at height *h* and advances; otherwise, after `timeoutPrecommit`, round *r + 1* begins.

```haskell
-- Tendermint locking, algebraically
data Step = Propose | Prevote | Precommit
data Vote = VoteFor Block | VoteNil

-- A correct validator never precommits against its lock without
-- a polka for the new value at a HIGHER round.
canPrecommit :: ValidatorState -> Block -> Round -> Bool
canPrecommit st b r =
    lockedValue st == Nothing
    || lockedValue st == Just b
    || polkaRound st b > lockedRound st
```

The locking rule plus timeout-gating solves the **hidden lock problem** [4]: a slow validator might hold the highest lock unknown to the new proposer, which then proposes a lock-violating value and stalls liveness. Tendermint's fix is temporal: `timeoutPropose` forces the proposer to wait a configured bound so that, post-GST, gossip from *all* correct validators arrives first. This is why Tendermint sacrifices responsiveness: the timeout is load-bearing for liveness.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 The algebra of quorum certificates

A QC is a *transferable proof*: embeddable in proposals, chainable across views, verifiable by light clients in O(1). HotStuff chains `qc(Bᵥ₋₁)` inside *Bᵥ*, so one message proposes transactions, certifies the parent, and advances the grandparent's phases — one block finalized per leader broadcast in steady state.

```rust
// BLS threshold-share aggregation into a HotStuff QC
struct QuorumCertificate {
    view: u64,
    block_hash: [u8; 32],
    phase: Phase,
    agg_sig: Signature,
}

fn form_qc(view: u64, hash: [u8; 32], phase: Phase,
           shares: &[(usize, Signature)], threshold: usize)
           -> Option<QuorumCertificate> {
    if shares.len() < threshold { return None; }
    // BLS aggregation is non-interactive point addition over G2
    let agg = shares.iter().map(|(_, s)| s)
        .fold(Signature::identity(), |a, s| a.aggregate(s));
    Some(QuorumCertificate { view, block_hash: hash, phase, agg_sig: agg })
}
```

A subtle point: threshold aggregation is secure only if shares bind the exact message `(view, block_hash, phase)` — otherwise a faulty leader could transplant shares across phases. HotStuff binds the phase into the signed payload.

### 4.2 HotStuff's commit rules: two-chain and three-chain

HotStuff defines finality through *chains* of QCs: a *k-chain* is QCs for *B₁ ← B₂ ← … ← Bₖ* in consecutive views *v, …, v+k−1*.

> **Theorem (HotStuff safety):** If a correct replica commits *B* (observes a 3-chain over *B* in consecutive views), no conflicting block *B′* can ever gather a QC in any view.

*Proof sketch.* Committing *B* means *2f + 1* replicas formed a pre-commit QC for *B*'s child and set `lockedQC` to *B*'s prepare QC (view *v*). Any QC for conflicting *B′* in view *w > v* needs *2f + 1* votes; by quorum intersection, some correct replica *r* voted in both quorums. But *r* votes for *B′* only if `B′.qc.view ≥ v` — and the only QC of view *v* is on *B*'s branch, contradiction. ∎

The **two-chain** variant (Jolteon/DiemBFT, HotStuff-1 [7]) commits on two consecutive QCs with an extra voting constraint, trading one phase of latency for a more delicate liveness argument. HotStuff-2 showed responsive two-phase consensus is possible via optimistic voting.

### 4.3 Tendermint locking and the hidden-lock hazard

Tendermint's safety invariant is the **lock**: once a correct validator observes a polka for *v* at round *r*, it sets `lockedValue := v`, `lockedRound := r`, and thereafter prevotes/precommits only *v* (or nil) unless it sees a polka for *v′ ≠ v* at round *r′ > r*. The *proposal rule* (proposer attaches its highest known *vr*) ensures a correct proposer never asks locked validators to violate their locks.

The locking rule plus timeout-gating solves the **hidden lock problem** [4]: a slow validator might hold the highest lock unknown to the new proposer, which then proposes a lock-violating value and stalls liveness. Tendermint's fix is temporal: `timeoutPropose` forces the proposer to wait a configured bound so that, post-GST, gossip from *all* correct validators arrives first. This is why Tendermint sacrifices responsiveness: the timeout is load-bearing for liveness.

Formally, Amoussou-Guenou et al. [4] prove Tendermint satisfies one-shot consensus per height and repeated consensus across heights in eventually synchronous systems, and additionally prove its reward mechanism is *not* fair — though a small protocol twist restores eventual fairness.

### 4.4 The pacemaker: liveness as a separate concern

HotStuff's pacemaker abstraction is arguably its most influential conceptual export (adopted by DiemBFT, Jolteon, Narwhal-based mempools). The reference pacemaker doubles timeouts per view, guaranteeing that after GST some view has a correct leader lasting longer than the Δ needed to collect NEW-VIEW messages, propose, and drive three phases. Crucially, *no timer appears in the safety proof*: replicas vote on QCs and locks alone, never on elapsed time — unlike PBFT, where view-change timers interleave with safety-critical state, and Tendermint, where timeouts gate every step.

Modern pacemakers (e.g., the *Fever* synchronizer, Cogsworth/Naor–Keidar) achieve expected O(δ) view synchronization with O(n²) worst-case messages.

### 4.5 Threshold signatures: setup, aggregation, and accountability

HotStuff's linearity rests on the threshold-signature setup:

- **Key generation.** A trusted dealer (or DKG) splits a master secret into *n* shares with threshold *t = 2f + 1*; replica *i* holds *skᵢ*, the group publishes *pk* and per-replica verification keys.
- **Signing & aggregation.** Replica *i* produces share *σᵢ = Sign(skᵢ, m)* on the phase-bound message *m*; any *t* valid shares combine into σ verifiable in one pairing: *Verify(pk, m, σ) = true*.

Accountability bonus: with *t = 2f + 1*, a QC proves *at least f + 1 correct* replicas signed. The cost: DKG setup rounds, proactive share refresh, and ms-scale pairing verification.

## 5. Empirical Evaluation and Proofs

### 5.1 Safety proofs, reconstructed

**PBFT.** *Prepared* requires *2f + 1* matching PREPAREs; two conflicting prepared values in the same view/sequence would need quorums with disjoint correct members — impossible. Across views, the *2f + 1* VIEW-CHANGE messages intersect any committed request's COMMIT quorum, so the new primary's *P*-set contains the committed value and the new view re-proposes it [2].

**HotStuff.** Via the 3-chain theorem in §4.2; the full paper [1] extends this to the pipelined variant and proves the voting predicate necessary and sufficient for the lock invariant.

**Tendermint.** If *2f + 1* precommits for *v* at height *h* exist, ≥ *f + 1* correct validators hold `lockedValue = v`. Any future polka for *v′ ≠ v* needs *2f + 1* prevotes, intersecting the locked set in a correct validator that prevotes only *v* (or nil) absent a higher-round polka — and a higher-round polka for *v′* would itself need those locked validators' prevotes, a circular impossibility formalized in [4].

### 5.2 Liveness arguments

All three rely on eventual synchrony plus correct-leader views:

- **PBFT:** exponential-backoff view-change timers guarantee some view with a correct primary lasting > Δ; its NEW-VIEW re-drives pending requests.
- **HotStuff:** the pacemaker delivers synchronized views with correct leaders; the leader's proposal extends the highest QC, satisfying every correct replica's voting predicate, so three phases complete within the view.
- **Tendermint:** per-round proposer rotation guarantees a correct proposer infinitely often; post-GST timeouts let the proposer learn the highest lock and drive prevote→precommit→decision.

### 5.3 Reported performance synthesis

| System | Setup | Reported result | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| PBFT (BFS) | 4 replicas, LAN | ~3% slower than unreplicated NFS; >10× faster than prior BFT | [2] |
| HotStuff | 100+ replicas, WAN | throughput/latency ≈ BFT-SMaRt; linear vs quadratic view change | [1] |
| Tendermint/Cosmos | ~125–175 validators, global | ~1 s block time, ~10k TPS ceiling (app-limited) | [3][4] |
| DiemBFT (HotStuff var.) | 100 validators | ~150 ms p50 latency intra-region at low load | Diem tech papers |

Caveats: these numbers span 25 years of hardware and incomparable workloads; they convey *scaling behavior* — linear vs quadratic view change, timeout-gated vs responsive latency — not a winner.

## 6. Limitations

1. **Quadratic corners remain.** Tendermint's gossip is O(n²) per round; PBFT's view change is O(n²)–O(n³) in authenticators. Only HotStuff is O(n) on all paths, and only under the threshold-signature trust assumption.
2. **Threshold setup trust.** HotStuff needs DKG or a trusted dealer; compromise of *f + 1* shares forges QCs. Proactive refresh adds real operational complexity.
3. **Timer fragility.** PBFT's and Tendermint's liveness depends on timeouts relative to unknown Δ — misconfiguration causes view-change storms or permanent round-skipping. HotStuff isolates this in the pacemaker but does not eliminate it.
4. **Tendermint's PoS-specific hazards.** Nothing-at-stake, long-range attacks, and stake concentration (a *2f + 1* stake quorum may be few entities) lie outside the *3f + 1* model; weak-subjectivity checkpoints are required in practice.
5. **Responsiveness vs. rotation.** HotStuff is responsive but needs a correct pacemaker; Tendermint rotates trivially but waits out timeouts every round. No protocol here is simultaneously responsive, rotation-trivial, and setup-free — HotStuff-2 narrows but has not closed this gap.
6. **Verification coverage.** Mechanized proofs exist for Tendermint's core [4] and PBFT fragments, but full mechanized safety+liveness for pipelined HotStuff with an abstract pacemaker remains partial.

---

## 7. Conclusion

PBFT, HotStuff, and Tendermint are three answers to one question — how to replace a faulty leader without forking the log — differing in how they *reify agreement* (implicit vote sets vs. constant-size QCs vs. gossiped polkas), *transfer authority* (heavyweight view change vs. view-change-as-a-phase vs. per-round rotation), and *recover liveness* (intertwined timers vs. modular pacemaker vs. timeout-gated rounds). The quorum-intersection lemma underwrites all three safety stories; the frontier is liveness — responsive, linear, rotation-friendly synchronizers — and reducing the trust assumptions on which linearity depends. For practitioners: PBFT's lineage for small stable-membership clusters; HotStuff's for large high-throughput deployments where linear view change matters; Tendermint's for stake-weighted, rotation-heavy blockchains where operational simplicity outweighs timeout-gated latency.
