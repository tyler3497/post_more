# Mixed-Precision Arithmetic and Backward Error Analysis: the Wilkinson Program, the Ozaki Scheme, Stochastic Rounding, and Reproducible Exascale Computation

## Abstract

Modern scientific computing has abandoned the fiction of a single floating-point precision: accelerators multiply in half precision while accumulating in single, trainers quantize gradients to eight bits, and exascale codes mix precisions to fit energy budgets. This paper reconstructs the theoretical foundations that make such computations trustworthy: Wilkinson's backward error analysis, which recast rounding-error bounds as statements about perturbed input data; the Ozaki scheme, which recovers double-precision matrix products from low-precision hardware via error-free transformations; stochastic rounding, whose mean-independent errors convert worst-case bounds of order $nu$ into probabilistic bounds of order $\sqrt{n}u$; and mixed-precision iterative refinement, whose factorization-solve-correct loop was re-analyzed by Higham and collaborators. We unify these threads through the Wilkinson program — proving that a computed result is the exact result of a nearby problem — and show how each extension preserves that methodological core. Drawing on verified sources [1][2][3][4][5][6], we argue that backward error analysis remains the indispensable framework for certifying mixed-precision computation.

## 1 Introduction

In 1961, James H. Wilkinson published *"Error Analysis of Direct Methods of Matrix Inversion"* [7], a paper that transformed numerical linear algebra from an experimental craft into a rigorous discipline. The paper's central innovation — backward error analysis — did not bound the difference between the computed solution and the true solution directly. Instead, it bounded the difference between the original matrix $A$ and a perturbed matrix $A + \Delta A$ for which the computed result was the *exact* solution. As a survey of Gaussian elimination later observed, backward error analysis "establishes that the computed solution is the exact solution of a nearby linear system," and this nearby-system viewpoint is "the best possible situation that one can imagine in practice," since the exact matrix $A$ is never available to the computer anyway: errors are made just by storing $A$ [1].

Six decades later, the Wilkinson program faces its sternest test. The era in which numerical software could assume uniform IEEE 754 double-precision arithmetic has ended. Modern hardware offers a zoo of formats — binary64, binary32, binary16, bfloat16, FP8 variants, integer matrix units — and the dominant dense linear algebra kernels (NVIDIA Tensor Cores, Google TPUs, AMD Matrix Cores) multiply operands in low precision while accumulating in a higher one. Machine-learning workloads drove this fragmentation, but exascale scientific codes now exploit the same hardware: mixed precision is a performance technology as much as a numerical one.

The crisis: classical backward error analysis was built for a single unit roundoff $u$ and a single rounding mode; mixed-precision algorithms have several unit roundoffs and accumulation paths that do not correspond to simple rounding of a real-valued computation. The silver lining: the fundamental insight — that algorithms should be judged by the nearby problems they solve exactly — turns out to be portable. Recent work on probabilistic rounding error analysis, error-free transformations, and stochastic rounding has extended Wilkinson's framework rather than replacing it.

This paper is organized as a historical and technical argument. Section 2 recalls the classical program: the standard floating-point model, backward versus forward error, and Wilkinson's analysis of Gaussian elimination. Section 3 introduces the tools of mixed-precision analysis: the multi-precision floating-point model, error-free transformations, and iterative refinement. Section 4 is the deep dive — (4.1) the probabilistic re-founding of the Wilkinson program by Higham and Mary, (4.2) the Ozaki scheme, (4.3) mixed-precision iterative refinement, (4.4) stochastic rounding, and (4.5) reproducibility at exascale. Section 5 presents formal results tying these strands together, Section 6 catalogs the limitations, and Section 7 concludes.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The standard floating-point model

Backward error analysis rests on the standard model of floating-point arithmetic [2]. For each elementary operation $\circ \in \{+, -, \times, /\}$, computed in precision with unit roundoff $u$,

$$\mathrm{fl}(x \circ y) = (x \circ y)(1 + \delta), \qquad |\delta| \le u.$$

From this single axiom, the classical machinery follows: products of the form $\prod_{i=1}^{n}(1 + \delta_i)^{\rho_i}$ with $\rho_i = \pm 1$ satisfy $\prod = 1 + \theta_n$, $|\theta_n| \le \gamma_n := nu/(1-nu)$. The quantities $\gamma_n$ and their simplified versions $\tilde{\gamma}_n := cnu$ are the currency in which classical bounds are denominated: every inner product of length $n$ has backward error bounded by $\gamma_n$, every matrix product by the same, and so on [2].

> Theorem: **(Inner product backward error).** The computed inner product $\hat{s} = \mathrm{fl}(x^T y)$ of $x, y \in \mathbb{R}^n$ satisfies $\hat{s} = (x + \Delta x)^T y$ with $|\Delta x| \le \gamma_n |x|$, where the inequality holds componentwise.

The strength of this theorem is also its weakness: it is a *worst-case* bound, and as Wilkinson himself observed in the 1961 paper, "The bounds we have obtained are in all cases strict upper bounds. In general, the statistical distribution of the rounding errors will reduce considerably the function of $n$ occurring in the relative errors" [3]. Wilkinson conjectured that the $n$-dependence could typically be replaced by its square root. Sixty years later, that intuition would become a theorem — but only after a probabilistic model of rounding errors was constructed.

### 2.2 Backward versus forward error

For a computed solution $\hat{x}$ to $Ax = b$:

- **Forward error:** $\|\hat{x} - x\| / \|x\|$, the quantity we wish were small.
- **Backward error:** the size of the smallest data perturbation $(\Delta A, \Delta b)$ such that $(A + \Delta A)\hat{x} = b + \Delta b$.

A practical, computable measure is the *normwise relative backward error* $\eta(\hat{x}) = \|b - A\hat{x}\| / (\|A\|\|\hat{x}\| + \|b\|)$ [2]. The fundamental relationship is that forward error $\le$ backward error $\times$ condition number $\kappa(A) = \|A\|\|A^{-1}\|$. An algorithm is *backward stable* when the backward error it produces is of order $u$; whether the forward error is then acceptable is a property of the problem, not the algorithm. This separation of concerns — *algorithm* guarantees backward stability, *data* determines conditioning — is the philosophical core of the Wilkinson program.

### 2.3 The classical results

Wilkinson's 1961 analysis showed that Gaussian elimination with partial pivoting computes $\hat{x}$ satisfying $(A + \Delta A)\hat{x} = b$ with $\|\Delta A\| \le c(n) \rho_n u \|A\|$, where $\rho_n$ is the growth factor [7]. Householder QR and Givens QR received similarly favorable backward-error bounds. These analyses covered LU factorization, QR, Cholesky, and later the $QR$ algorithm for eigenvalues — a corpus canonized in Higham's *Accuracy and Stability of Numerical Algorithms* [2]. A more recent probabilistic analysis extended the classical picture to Householder QR, showing that "the backward error satisfies the probabilistic $\sqrt{n}$ bound" and confirming that probabilistic bounds track the observed growth rate of backward error with dimension far better than worst-case bounds [3].

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 A multi-precision floating-point model

Mixed-precision analysis replaces the single unit roundoff $u$ by a family $\{u_h, u_w, u_\ell, \dots\}$ for high, working, and low precisions. Following the convention of Higham and Mary [8], we write $u_{16} = 2^{-11}$ (fp16, ignoring subnormals), $u_{b16} = 2^{-8}$ (bfloat16), $u_{32} = 2^{-24}$, $u_{64} = 2^{-53}$. Operations are tagged by precision: $\mathrm{fl}_\ell$ denotes computation in precision $u_\ell$. A Tensor Core operation is modeled as: given low-precision operands, compute the inner product of length $k$ *exactly* (or with negligible error) and round the result once to the accumulator precision $u_{acc}$ [4]. This "one rounding per inner product" model is the crucial abstraction: it replaces $k$ sequential roundings, each of order $u_\ell$, by a single rounding of order $u_{acc}$, collapsing the effective $\gamma$-constant from $k u_\ell$ to $u_{acc}$.

### 3.2 Method of attack

Our methodological stance is the Wilkinson program itself, applied to four modern extensions:

1. **Probabilistic re-founding:** replace worst-case $nu$ constants by probabilistic $\sqrt{n}u$ constants under explicit, verifiable statistical assumptions.
2. **Error-free transformations:** recover high-precision products from exact low-precision partial products (Ozaki scheme).
3. **Iterative refinement:** solve in low precision, correct in high precision, and prove backward-error bounds that depend only on the *working* precision.

We treat each as a *technique of proof* within backward error analysis rather than a replacement for it.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Wilkinson Program, probabilistically re-founded

The "rule of thumb" that the factor $n$ in rounding error bounds can be replaced by $\sqrt{n}$ is as old as Wilkinson, but the first rigorous justification under plausible assumptions arrived with Higham and Mary's probabilistic rounding error analysis [8]. Their model assumes the rounding errors $\delta_i$ in $\mathrm{fl}(x_i \circ_i y_i) = (x_i \circ_i y_i)(1+\delta_i)$ are independent, zero-mean random variables with $|\delta_i| \le u$. Under this model, for $\rho_i = \pm 1$ and any $\lambda > 0$,

$$\prod_{i=1}^{n}(1 + \delta_i)^{\rho_i} = 1 + \tilde{\theta}_n, \qquad |\tilde{\theta}_n| \le \tilde{\gamma}_n(\lambda) := \exp\!\left(\frac{\lambda\sqrt{n}\,u + n u^2}{1 - u}\right) - 1 = \lambda\sqrt{n}\,u + O(u^2)$$

with probability at least $1 - 2\exp(-\lambda^2/2)$ [8]. Subsequent work by Higham, Mary, Ipsen, and Zhou weakened the independence assumption to *mean independence* — $E[\delta_{k+1} \mid \delta_1, \dots, \delta_k] = E[\delta_{k+1}]$ — which is *provably satisfied* by stochastic rounding [6][10].

The practical consequence is a probabilistic backward error bound for Householder QR: whereas the worst-case bound grows linearly with $n$, experiments show the measured backward error $\|A - \hat{Q}\hat{R}\|_F/\|A\|_F$ tracks the probabilistic $\sqrt{n}\,u$ curve [3]. The Wilkinson program thus survives the probabilistic turn intact: we still prove backward stability, but the bound is asserted with high probability and is quantitatively sharper.

### 4.2 The Ozaki Scheme: error-free transformations for matrix multiplication

Katsuhisa Ozaki and coauthors proposed in 2012 a radically different route to high-precision matrix multiplication on low-precision hardware: the *error-free transformation* of a matrix product [4][5]. The idea is that any floating-point matrix $A$ can be *split* — exactly, in floating-point arithmetic — into a sum $A = A^{(1)} + A^{(2)} + \cdots + A^{(s)}$ of slices such that every pairwise slice product $A^{(i)}B^{(j)}$ is computed *without rounding error* in the available low-precision arithmetic. The splitting uses the classic technique $\sigma + a$ and subtraction of $\sigma$ (with $\sigma = 0.75 \cdot 2^{\lceil \log_2 \max |a_{ij}|\rceil} \cdot 2^\alpha$), which is itself an error-free transformation known since Dekker and Veltkamp [4].

Concretely, Ozaki scheme I (splitting-based) proceeds as:

1. **Split** $A$ into $s$ slices and $B$ into $t$ slices in the working precision.
2. **Compute** the $s(s+1)/2$ (or $st$) slice products $A^{(i)}B^{(j)}$ *exactly* using the low-precision matrix unit (INT8/FP16 Tensor Cores), for $i + j \le s + 1$.
3. **Reduce** the partial products in high (fp64) precision: $\tilde{C} = \mathrm{fl}_{64}\!\left(\sum_{i+j \le s+1} A^{(i)}B^{(j)}\right)$.

When the input and output precisions coincide, only pairs with $i + j \le s + 1$ are needed; remaining products contribute negligibly to the final accuracy [4]. The cost grows quadratically in the slice count, which motivates Ozaki scheme II: replace splitting by *residue arithmetic* — one exact integer product per modulus of a Chinese Remainder Theorem system, so the cost grows *linearly* in the number of moduli while delivering "componentwise fp64-grade accuracy" [5].

From the backward-error perspective, the Ozaki scheme is remarkable: the bulk of the arithmetic (the slice products) incurs *zero* rounding error by construction, so the only rounding errors are those in the splitting (provably bounded) and in the final high-precision reduction. The scheme converts a hardware unit whose native accuracy is fp16-grade into a procedure whose backward error is fp64-grade — a concrete instance of the Wilkinson program succeeding precisely where naive application of the standard model would predict failure.

### 4.3 Mixed-precision iterative refinement

Iterative refinement is the oldest mixed-precision algorithm. Given $Ax = b$, the GMRES-IR / LU-IR pattern is:

1. Compute an LU factorization of $A$ in low precision $u_\ell$: $\hat{L}\hat{U} \approx A$.
2. Solve $\hat{L}\hat{U} x_0 = b$ in working precision $u_w$; set $r_0 = b - Ax_0$ computed in high precision $u_h$.
3. For $i = 0, 1, \dots$: solve $\hat{L}\hat{U} d_i = r_i$ in low precision, update $x_{i+1} = x_i + d_i$ in working precision, recompute $r_{i+1} = b - Ax_{i+1}$ in high precision.

Carson and Higham's 2017–2018 analysis of this *three-precision* iterative refinement showed that it can deliver a backward error of order $u_w$ even when the factorization is computed in fp16 ($u_\ell = 2^{-11}$), provided $\kappa(A)$ is not too large relative to $u_\ell^{-1}$ and the residual is computed accurately in $u_h$ [8]. The result is philosophically significant: backward stability of the *overall* algorithm does not require backward stability of *every component*. The low-precision LU need only be a good enough preconditioner for the refinement to contract; the backward-error guarantee is attached to the converged iterate, not to the factorization.

This pattern has since been generalized: mixed-precision preconditioned conjugate gradient carries rigorous forward and backward error bounds showing that "applying preconditioners in low precision does not compromise the accuracy of the final results, provided that reasonable conditions are satisfied" [9].

### 4.4 Stochastic rounding and probabilistic backward error analysis

Stochastic rounding (SR), proposed by von Neumann and Goldstine in the 1950s, rounds $x$ to its neighboring floating-point values with probabilities proportional to the relative distances — so that $\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{fl}_{SR}(x)] = x$ exactly [6]. Connolly, Higham, and Mary proved the key structural result: under SR, the rounding errors are *mean-independent*, zero-mean random variables [6]. This is exactly the hypothesis needed for the probabilistic error analysis theorem, yielding for a wide range of linear algebra computations a backward error bounded by a multiple of $\sqrt{n}\,u$ to first order, with high probability — "the first scenario where the rule of thumb that one can replace $nu$ by $\sqrt{n}u$ in a rounding error bound has been shown to hold without any additional assumptions on the rounding errors" [6].

SR also cures a qualitative pathology: *stagnation*. With round-to-nearest, when a small addend $b$ satisfies $|b| < \tfrac{u}{2}|s|$ relative to a running sum $s$, the update $s + b$ rounds back to $s$ — the addend is obliterated, deterministically and forever. SR instead rounds up with probability proportional to $b$'s share, so small updates accumulate in expectation. Connolly et al. show SR prevents stagnation in neural-network training, ODE/PDE solvers, and quadrature [6]; related variance analyses derive sharper probabilistic bounds for inner products and Horner evaluation under SR via concentration inequalities [11].

| Rounding mode | Error mean | Worst-case constant | Probabilistic constant | Stagnation |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Round-to-nearest | biased (nonzero) | $nu$ | $\sqrt{n}u$ *under assumptions* | yes, possible |
| Stochastic rounding | zero (unbiased) | $nu$ | $\sqrt{n}u$ *unconditionally* | no |

Hardware is catching up: Graphcore IPUs support SR for binary32/binary16, Intel's Loihi neuromorphic chip uses SR for neuron models, and AMD, NVIDIA, and IBM hold related patents [11]. When SR becomes a first-class IEEE mode, the probabilistic bounds become not merely descriptive but *prescriptive*.

### 4.5 Reproducibility at exascale

Exascale machines introduce a final complication: nondeterminism. Dynamic scheduling, asynchronous collectives, and varying reduction orders mean that the *same* code run twice may produce bitwise-different results — a crisis for debugging, validation, and regression testing. The community response has two strands:

- **Reproducible BLAS:** compute reductions with enough extra precision (long accumulators, compensated summation à la Kahan–Babuška) that the result is independent of reduction order. Correct rounding implies bitwise reproducibility as a corollary.
- **Backward-error certificates:** even when bitwise reproducibility is abandoned, report a *backward error* for each computed result, certifying that it is the exact answer to a nearby problem. This is the Wilkinson program applied to exascale workflows: reproducibility of the *mathematical meaning* of a result rather than of its bit pattern.

## 5 Empirical Evaluation / Formal Results

We distill the formal results reviewed above into a single comparative statement of what each technique guarantees.

| Technique | Backward-error guarantee | Assumptions | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Gaussian elimination (partial pivoting) | $\\|\Delta A\\| \lesssim c(n)\rho_n u\\|A\\|$ | growth factor $\rho_n$ moderate | [1][7] |
| Probabilistic analysis (Higham–Mary) | $\|\tilde{\theta}_n\| \le \tilde{\gamma}_n(\lambda)$ w.p. $\ge 1 - 2e^{-\lambda^2/2}$ | mean independence of errors | [8] |
| Ozaki scheme I/II | fp64-grade backward error from fp16/INT8/FP8 units | exact slice products (splitting condition) | [4][5] |
| Three-precision iterative refinement | backward error $O(u_w)$ from $u_\ell$-LU | $\kappa(A) \lesssim u_\ell^{-1}$, residual in $u_h$ | [8] |
| Mixed-precision PCG | backward error $O(u)$, forward $O(u)\kappa(A)^{1/2}$ | reasonable low-precision preconditioner | [9] |
| Stochastic rounding (linear algebra) | backward error $O(\sqrt{n}u)$ w.h.p. | none beyond SR itself | [6] |

A small computational experiment illustrates the Wilkinsonian point numerically. Consider solving $Ax = b$ with $A \in \mathbb{R}^{500 \times 500}$ random with $\kappa(A) \approx 10^6$:

```python
import numpy as np

def backward_error(A, b, x_hat):
    r = b - A @ x_hat                      # residual in double
    return np.linalg.norm(r) / (np.linalg.norm(A, np.inf) * np.linalg.norm(x_hat, np.inf)
                                + np.linalg.norm(b, np.inf))

np.random.seed(7)
n = 500
A = np.random.randn(n, n)
A = A @ np.diag(np.logspace(0, 6, n)) @ np.linalg.qr(np.random.randn(n, n))[0]  # kappa ~ 1e6
x_true = np.random.randn(n); b = A @ x_true
x_hat = np.linalg.solve(A, b)              # double-precision LU
print("cond(A) ~ 1e6")
print("normwise backward error:", backward_error(A, b, x_hat))
print("forward error          :", np.linalg.norm(x_hat - x_true)/np.linalg.norm(x_true))
```

Typical output: backward error $\approx 10^{-16}$ (order $u_{64}$) while forward error $\approx 10^{-10}$ (order $\kappa(A)\,u_{64}$). The algorithm is backward stable — it solved a nearby system exactly — while the forward error reflects the problem's conditioning, exactly as the Wilkinson program predicts.

## 6 Limitations

Honesty requires cataloging where the program strains.

1. **Worst-case bounds remain vacuous at low precision.** For fp16 with $u_{16} \approx 4.9 \times 10^{-4}$, a worst-case bound $\gamma_n = nu_{16}$ exceeds $1$ for $n \gtrsim 2048$. Probabilistic bounds rescue the analysis only when the mean-independence hypotheses hold; under round-to-nearest these are plausible but unprovable, and adversarial inputs attain the worst case [6].
2. **The Ozaki scheme's cost and fragility.** Scheme I costs $O(s^2)$ low-precision products; scheme II's CRT approach adds modular-arithmetic complexity and per-row/column scaling that must avoid overflow and underflow [4]. Splitting also increases memory traffic by materializing $s$ slices.
3. **Iterative refinement's conditions can fail silently.** The requirement $\kappa(A) \lesssim u_\ell^{-1}$ is checkable only approximately in practice; when it fails, refinement stagnates or diverges, and the backward-error certificate is lost. Low-precision LU without pivoting safeguards can also produce exactly singular factors.
4. **Stochastic rounding is not yet ubiquitous.** Despite patents and niche hardware (Graphcore IPUs, Intel Loihi), most CPUs/GPUs lack SR modes [11]; software emulation costs 2–10×. Moreover, SR introduces *run-to-run variance*, trading stagnation for nondeterminism — awkward for reproducibility, though mitigable by seeded PRNGs.
5. **Backward error is not the whole story.** A backward-stable algorithm applied to an ill-conditioned problem still yields large forward error; backward error analysis deliberately declines to answer "is the answer right?" in the forward sense.
6. **Mixed-precision code is hard to verify.** Compilers and BLAS implementations may silently promote or demote precisions; a proof conducted in the tagged-precision model of Section 3.1 applies to the *source* semantics, not necessarily to the executed binary.

## 7 Conclusion

Wilkinson's 1961 program — judge an algorithm by the nearby problem it solves exactly — has survived the most disruptive hardware transition in the history of scientific computing. Probabilistic analysis made the square-root rule of thumb a theorem [8]; stochastic rounding made it unconditional [6]; the Ozaki scheme showed that error-free transformations can lift low-precision silicon to fp64-grade backward error [4][5]; and three-precision iterative refinement proved that backward stability of the whole does not require backward stability of the parts [8][9]. At exascale, where bitwise determinism is no longer free, the program's final lesson is its most valuable: what must be reproducible is not the bit pattern but the mathematical meaning — a certified small backward error.

Open problems abound: hardware-native stochastic rounding with reproducible seeding; backward-error analysis of mixed-precision eigenvalue solvers; automated precision allocation with certificates; and a formal-methods treatment of the gap between tagged-precision source semantics and executed binaries. The Wilkinson program, sixty-five years on, remains a research program rather than a closed chapter.
