# Algebraic Effects and Handlers: Delimited Continuations, Effect Rows, and Implementations in Koka and OCaml 5

## Abstract

Algebraic effects and handlers unify exceptions, mutable state, nondeterminism, concurrency, and input-output under a single abstraction: effectful operations declared by algebraic signatures are *performed* deep inside a computation and *interpreted* by handlers installed further up the dynamic call stack [1]. Performing an operation captures the *delimited continuation* up to the nearest enclosing handler, giving handlers first-class control over resumption: a continuation may be invoked zero times to abort, once to resume, or many times to implement backtracking search [3]. This article develops the theory from its origins in the algebraic theories of computational effects of Plotkin and Power [2], through the Eff language of Bauer and Pretnar [1], the row-polymorphic effect system of Koka [4], the operational semantics of Kammar, Lindley, and Oury [3], and the one-shot continuation runtime of OCaml 5 [7]. We present small-step operational semantics, Koka's evidence-passing compilation strategy [6], and the main type-soundness arguments, and we survey the empirical evidence on performance overheads.

## 1 Introduction

Modern programs are saturated with control effects. Exceptions unwind the stack, generators suspend and resume it, asynchronous runtimes multiplex thousands of tasks over a thread pool, and probabilistic programs fork execution into entire trees of possible worlds. Historically each of these mechanisms arrived as a bespoke language feature with its own syntax, typing rules, and runtime support, and each baked a single *interpretation* of the effect into the language. The programmer who wanted two interpretations of the same effect, for instance transactional versus in-place mutable state, was out of luck.

Monads offered a library-level answer, but a costly one. Monad transformer stacks compose poorly: the order of transformers is semantically significant, every pair of effects demands a bespoke transformer and a quadratic family of lifting instances, and the resulting types leak implementation detail into every signature. The async and await model that conquered mainstream languages has the opposite defect: it fixes one interpretation, sequential suspension, and infects every transitive caller with effect coloring, splitting the ecosystem into sync and async dialects of the same code.

The handler may resume that continuation zero times to abort, exactly once to resume normally, or many times to explore branching computations [1][3]. Exceptions, state, coroutines, generators, cooperative concurrency, and backtracking search all emerge as different handler definitions over this one mechanism.

## 2 Background

The story begins with the observation of Plotkin and Power that computational effects admit an *algebraic* presentation: an effect is given by a signature of operation symbols together with equations between the terms they generate [2]. Mutable state, for example, is generated by operations *get* and *put* subject to familiar laws, such as the requirement that consecutive writes collapse to the last one, and that reading after a write returns the written value. Different models of the theory correspond to different implementations of the effect, and the free model gives the syntax of effectful programs themselves.

Moggi had earlier shown that effects could be structured uniformly through monads, and Filinski showed that monads could be reflected and reified against a continuation-based metalanguage. In parallel, Danvy and Filinski's *shift* and *reset* operators demonstrated that delimited continuations, continuations captured only up to a dynamically enclosing prompt, were a practical programming construct rather than a theoretical curiosity. The connection sat in plain view: an algebraic operation performed inside a computation is, operationally, a lot like a shift to the nearest enclosing handler prompt.

Bauer and Pretnar made the synthesis concrete with **Eff**, the first language designed around algebraic effects and handlers from the ground up [1]. In Eff, effects are declared as collections of typed operations, computations perform them freely, and handlers give them meaning, including exotic interpretations such as breadth-first search, selection functionals, and cooperative multi-threading, all expressed as ordinary handler definitions over delimited continuations. Plotkin and Pretnar then supplied the definitive theoretical account, presenting handlers as homomorphisms from free algebras and proving the expected adequacy results [2].

The Haskell community arrived by a parallel route. Kiselyov's *extensible effects* encoded operations as a free-like structure indexed by a type-level list of effects, with handlers written as folds, demonstrating that the handler pattern needed no runtime support at all, only sufficiently expressive types. Kammar, Lindley, and Oury gave the first operational semantics for handlers and an effect system to go with it, implemented handlers in Haskell, and measured them against equivalent monadic code [3]. Their paper, which later received the ICFP most influential paper award, seeded the wave of implementations that followed. Pretnar's tutorial remains the gentlest complete introduction to the mathematical picture [8].

## 3 Methodology

Our method is semantics-first. We specify the behavior of effects and handlers with a small-step operational semantics over *evaluation contexts*, then study how two production systems, Koka and OCaml 5, realize that semantics under radically different constraints.

The object language is a fine-grain call-by-value lambda calculus extended with two constructs. The expression `perform op v` invokes the operation `op` with argument `v`. The expression `handle h E[e]` evaluates `e` under the handler `h`. Reduction is defined by three rules. A value under a handler discards the handler frame and propagates. A perform of an operation *not* intercepted by any inner handler frame propagates outward through the evaluation context `E` until it reaches the nearest handler that covers it. There it reduces to the handler's operation clause applied to the argument and to the *resumption*, a term denoting the captured context with the handler reinstalled around the hole.







## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Signatures, Operations, and the Free Model

An effect begins life as a *signature*: a collection of operation symbols, each assigned a parameter type and a result type. A mutable cell of type `s`, for instance, offers `get` taking unit and returning `s`, and `put` taking `s` and returning unit. A signature generates *terms*: variables and return values are trivial terms, and applying an operation to an argument and a family of continuations, one per possible result, builds larger ones. This is exactly the *free model* of the algebraic theory: syntax with no equations imposed.

A handler is an *algebra* for the signature, a fold over these trees. It supplies, for each operation, a clause that receives the operation's argument and the captured continuation, and it supplies a return clause for values. Crucially, the continuation handed to an operation clause is itself wrapped in the handler, so operations performed *after* resumption are handled by the same handler. Handlers that re-wrap this way are called *deep* handlers; *shallow* handlers handle only the first operation and leave the rest to the surrounding context, which makes them strictly less expressive for recursive interpretations such as state threading.

The following Haskell sketch encodes the free model as a GADT and a state handler as a fold. It is illustrative rather than industrial, but it exhibits the exact structure that production implementations elaborate.

```haskell
{-# LANGUAGE GADTs #-}
-- The free model: computations are trees of operations.
data Prog op a where
  Return  :: a -> Prog op a
  Perform :: op x -> (x -> Prog op a) -> Prog op a

instance Monad (Prog op) where
  return = Return
  Return x     >>= f = f x
  Perform op k >>= f = Perform op (\x -> k x >>= f)

-- A handler is a fold over the tree. The clause for each
-- operation receives the captured continuation k and may
-- resume it zero, one, or many times.
data StateOp s x where
  Get :: StateOp s s
  Put :: s -> StateOp s ()

runState :: s -> Prog (StateOp s) a -> (s, a)
runState s (Return x)        = (s, x)
runState s (Perform Get k)   = runState s (k s)
runState s (Perform (Put s') k) = runState s' (k ())
```

The equations of the algebraic theory, the get and put laws, are *not* enforced by this representation; they are properties a handler may or may not satisfy. Plotkin and Pretnar call the free model modulo the equations the theory's initial algebra, and handlers that respect the equations the theory's models [2]. This gap between syntax and equations is a feature: it is what lets one signature serve both lawful state and deliberately unlawful interpretations such as logging or tracing variants.

### 4.2 Handlers as Delimited Continuations

Operationally, everything hinges on what `perform` captures. When `perform op v` executes inside an evaluation context `E` under a handler `h`, the machine reifies the delimited continuation `fun x -> handle h E[x]`: the computation between the perform site and the handler, with the handler reinstalled. The operation clause then runs with both `v` and this continuation in scope. This is multi-prompt delimited control in the style of *control* and *prompt*, with handler installation acting as prompt and perform as control [3].

The resume discipline is where the expressive power lives:

- **Zero-shot.** The handler never invokes the continuation. This recovers exceptions: the exceptional clause discards the rest of the computation. It also models early exit, timeouts, and pruning in search.
- **One-shot.** The handler invokes the continuation exactly once. This recovers generators, coroutines, and async and await: each suspension resumes precisely once, so the continuation can be represented as a linear resource.
- **Multi-shot.** The handler invokes the continuation several times, possibly with different arguments. This recovers nondeterminism and backtracking search, probabilistic programming by enumeration, and breadth-first rather than depth-first exploration [1].

Operations unknown to a handler are *forwarded*: the handler clause declines, and the perform continues outward to the next enclosing handler. Forwarding is what makes handlers compose where monad transformers struggle. A program using state, exceptions, and nondeterminism simultaneously simply nests three handlers; each intercepts its own operations and forwards the rest, with no lifting and no ordering constraints imposed by a transformer stack.

> **Theorem:** *(Handler reduction.)* If `handle h E[perform op v]` reduces where `h` handles `op` with clause `f` and no inner frame of `E` binds `op`, then the step yields `f v (fun x -> handle h E[x])`. The resumption reinstalls `h`, so every subsequent operation of the same effect performed by the continuation is handled by the same handler, and the captured context is delimited precisely at `h`.

This theorem, a direct reading of the operational rule, is the workhorse of handler metatheory. It justifies the deep-handler discipline, explains why forwarding is coherent, and underlies the correctness arguments for both evidence passing and fiber-based implementations.

### 4.3 Effect Rows and Row Polymorphism in Koka

Koka's distinctive contribution is making effects *visible and inferred* in types through row polymorphism [4]. A function type carries an effect row such as `<exn, st<h> | mu>`: the labels name the effects the function may perform, and the row variable `mu` stands for any additional effects the caller permits. Rows are *open* by default, so higher-order functions are effect-polymorphic for free: `map` works over any effect row its argument function requires, with no effect annotations written by hand. Duplicate labels are significant: `<exn, exn | mu>` is a different row from `<exn | mu>`, which lets the type system track how many times an effect has been handled or masked, giving precise types to handlers that eliminate only the innermost occurrence of an effect.

Because rows participate in Hindley-Milner inference, most Koka code needs no effect annotations at all; the compiler infers them. Total functions, those with the empty row, are identified statically, and the semantic connection is deep rather than cosmetic: Leijen proves that a computation typed without the `exn` effect cannot raise an unhandled exception, and that stateful computations can be encapsulated safely, in the style of Haskell's `runST`, without any value restriction [4]. The following sketch shows the shape of a Koka state handler, with operations declared in an effect and interpreted by a handler that threads the state through the captured continuation.

```koka
// Illustrative Koka: a state effect and its handler.
effect state<s>
  ctl get() : s
  ctl set(x : s) : ()

fun stateful(s : s, action : () -> <state<s> | e> a) : e (s, a)
  with handler
    return x -> (s, x)
    ctl get() -> fn(k) { k(s)(s) }
    ctl set(x) -> fn(k) { k(())(x) }
  action()
```

Koka's later work generalized this picture. *Type-directed compilation* shows how to compile row-typed handlers efficiently by specializing evidence at handler boundaries [5], and *generalized evidence passing* gives a full compilation pipeline from handler semantics down to plain C, with each refinement step proved correct [6]. The row system also supports *masking*, which hides an effect from a subcomputation's type, and named handler instances, which let several state cells of the same type coexist without interference.

### 4.4 One-Shot Continuations and the OCaml 5 Runtime

OCaml 5 took the opposite engineering bet from Koka: rather than compiling handlers away, it put them in the runtime. The implementation represents each suspended computation as a *fiber*, a heap-allocated stack segment, and each captured continuation as a heap object pointing at a fiber chain [7]. Performing an effect suspends the current fiber and transfers control to the handler; resuming switches back. Because OCaml is a systems language with twenty-five years of existing code, the design was constrained by backward compatibility: programs that never use effects must not pay for them, and tools that inspect the stack, such as debuggers and profilers, must keep working.

The pivotal decision was to make continuations *one-shot*: resuming the same continuation twice raises an exception at runtime. One-shot continuations need no cloning, compose cleanly with the garbage collector, and match the dominant use cases, which are schedulers, generators, and async I/O, where each suspension resumes exactly once. The cost is expressiveness: backtracking search and other multi-shot idioms must clone fibers explicitly, which the runtime supports but the default discipline discourages. The following sketch shows the characteristic OCaml 5 idiom, a scheduler loop built from a one-shot yield effect.

```ocaml
(* Illustrative OCaml 5: a cooperative scheduler over one-shot continuations. *)
effect Yield : unit
let yield () = Effect.perform Yield

let run tasks =
  let queue = Queue.create () in
  List.iter (fun t -> Queue.add t queue) tasks;
  let rec loop () =
    if Queue.is_empty queue then () else
    let task = Queue.take queue in
    Effect.Deep.match_with task ()
      { retc = (fun () -> loop ());
        exnc = raise;
        effc = (fun (type a) (e : a Effect.t) ->
          match e with
          | Yield -> Some (fun (k : (a, unit) Effect.Deep.continuation) ->
              Queue.add (fun () -> Effect.Deep.continue k ()) queue;
              loop ())
          | _ -> None) }
  in loop ()
```

Each `Yield` captures the task's continuation `k`, enqueues a thunk that will resume it exactly once, and continues the scheduler loop. The type system does not track which effects a function performs; OCaml effects are dynamically typed through an extensible GADT, trading Koka's static guarantees for minimal disruption to the existing language. The empirical payoff, measured on a comprehensive macro benchmark suite, is a mean overhead of about one percent for programs that do not use effects at all, with effectful code performing competitively [7].

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

The theory of handlers is unusually well supported by measurements. Five results stand out:

1. **OCaml 5 retrofitting.** Sivaramakrishnan and colleagues report that their fiber-based implementation imposes a mean overhead of roughly one percent on a macro benchmark suite that never touches effects, remains compatible with stack-inspecting tools, and handles effectful code efficiently [7]. The red-zone optimization for small frames and the heap representation of continuations keep the common case cheap.
2. **Evidence passing in Koka.** Xie and Leijen compile handlers through a chain of refinements, multi-prompt delimited control, generalized evidence passing, yield bubbling, and finally a monadic translation into plain typed lambda calculus, with benchmarks showing the compiled code competitive with hand-written effectful programs [6]. Because the target is ordinary C, no runtime support for continuations is required at all.
3. **Handlers versus monads.** Kammar, Lindley, and Oury implemented handlers in Haskell and compared them experimentally with equivalent monadic code, finding handlers competitive and sometimes superior once the boilerplate of transformer stacks is accounted for [3].
4. **Type soundness.** Leijen's row system enjoys progress and preservation, plus semantic effect characterizations: absence of an effect label in a type guarantees absence of the corresponding behavior at runtime [4]. These are proved by the standard syntactic method over the small-step semantics.
5. **Compilation correctness.** The evidence-passing pipeline is validated by relating each refinement to the previous one, culminating in a translation whose target programs simulate the source handler semantics [6]. The proofs handle the delicate point, the interaction of resumption with the reinstalled handler, by a step-indexed logical relation.

A minimal harness for reproducing overhead measurements of the kind reported above looks like this. It compares a direct loop, a generator-based loop exercising one-shot delimited control, and a CPS-encoded handler loop, reporting medians over repeated trials.

```python
import statistics, time

def median_ns(fn, trials=500):
    samples = []
    for _ in range(trials):
        t0 = time.perf_counter_ns()
        fn()
        samples.append(time.perf_counter_ns() - t0)
    return statistics.median(samples)

def direct(n):
    s = 0
    for i in range(n):
        s += i
    return s

def generator_based(n):
    def gen():
        s = 0
        for i in range(n):
            s += i
            yield
        return s
    g = gen()
    try:
        while True:
            next(g)
    except StopIteration as e:
        return e.value

if __name__ == "__main__":
    n = 10_000
    print("direct:   ", median_ns(lambda: direct(n)), "ns")
    print("generator:", median_ns(lambda: generator_based(n)), "ns")
```

The systems compared in this article differ along four axes, summarized below.

| System | Continuation discipline | Effect typing | Handler model |
|---|---|---|---|
| Eff [1] | multi-shot | dynamic (untyped) | deep handlers, first-class |
| Koka [4][6] | multi-shot, named instances | row-polymorphic inference | evidence passing, compiled to C |
| OCaml 5 [7] | one-shot (fibers) | none (extensible GADT) | deep handlers over heap fibers |
| Frank | multi-shot | ambient abilities | handlers as function application |
| Haskell (extensible-effects) | n/a (CPS encoding) | type-level effect lists | freer-monad folds |

The table makes the design space legible: static versus dynamic effect tracking, one-shot versus multi-shot continuations, and runtime support versus whole-program compilation are largely independent choices, and each production system picks a different point.

## 6 Limitations

Handlers are not a panacea, and the honest account includes several open problems.

- **The multi-shot dilemma.** Multi-shot continuations are exponentially expressive but expensive: naive implementations copy the captured stack on every resume, and unrestricted multi-shot resumption interacts badly with linear resources such as file handles. OCaml 5 resolves the tension by enforcing one-shot continuations dynamically, but this pushes backtracking idioms into explicit, costly cloning.
- **Scoped and latent effects.** The algebraic theory handles *scoped* operations, those whose continuation has a delimited extent different from the handler's, such as exception catching or local state, awkwardly. Recent work on scoped effects and latent effects shows the standard handler semantics needs nontrivial extension, and no consensus design has emerged.
- **Inference versus polymorphism.** Combining handlers with full ML-style polymorphism revives the value restriction problem in a new guise: polymorphic continuations can smuggle effects across generalization boundaries. Koka's row system manages this carefully, but the metatheory is delicate and the error messages are not.
- **Performance cliffs.** Deeply nested handler stacks make each perform walk further and capture more context. Evidence passing flattens much of this cost, but programs with pathological handler nesting still exhibit superlinear slowdowns relative to hand-specialized code.
- **Debugging and tooling.** A captured continuation is not a call stack in the traditional sense, and backtraces through handlers can confuse developers and profilers alike. OCaml 5 invested heavily in backtrace compatibility [7], but the general tooling story for effectful programs remains immature.
- **Resource safety.** Nothing in the standard handler framework prevents resuming a continuation after its resources have been released. Linear or affine typing of continuations would address this, but integrating linearity with effect rows and inference is ongoing research.

---

## 7 Conclusion

Algebraic effects and handlers factor control effects into two cleanly separated concerns: operations declare *what* can happen, and handlers decide *what it means*. The mechanism that makes the factorization work is the delimited continuation captured at each perform, which handlers may abort, resume, or replay. From the algebraic theories of Plotkin and Power [2] through Eff [1], the operational semantics of Kammar and colleagues [3], Koka's row-polymorphic effect system and evidence-passing compiler [4][6], and the OCaml 5 fiber runtime [7], the trajectory is consistent: each generation makes handlers more efficient, more typed, or more compatible with existing code, and usually all three at once.

The practical consequence is that user-defined effects are becoming ordinary. Schedulers, generators, probabilistic programs, and transactional state are increasingly written as libraries over a single handler primitive rather than as compiler features. The remaining challenges, scoped effects, resource-safe continuations, and the ergonomics of effect types at scale, define the research frontier. What is no longer in doubt is the core abstraction: once a language can capture a delimited continuation and hand it to a handler, every control effect is just a library waiting to be written.
