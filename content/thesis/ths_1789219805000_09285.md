# Bistability and Tipping in Ocean Overturning: Stommel Box Models, Hysteresis, and the Collapse Risk of the Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation

## Abstract

The Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation (AMOC) is the principal engine of meridional heat transport in the Atlantic basin, carrying on the order of one petawatt of heat poleward and ventilating the abyssal ocean. Since Stommel's seminal 1961 two-box model [1], theory has held that the circulation admits multiple stable equilibria under identical boundary conditions: a thermally driven "on" state with sinking in the North Atlantic and a salinity-driven "off" state with reversed flow, separated by saddle-node bifurcations in freshwater forcing. This thesis reconstructs the mathematical anatomy of that bistability, derives the non-dimensional Stommel equations and computes their hysteresis loop numerically, and connects the idealized theory to the modern observational and modelling record, including quasi-equilibrium tipping experiments in a state-of-the-art coupled climate model [6], statistical early-warning analyses of the observed record [7][8], and evidence that the modern circulation is the weakest in a millennium [10]. We argue that the hysteresis structure encoded in the box model is robust across the full model hierarchy and that physics-based early-warning diagnostics indicate the present-day AMOC is on a tipping course [6].

## 1 Intro

The climate of the North Atlantic sector is a planetary anomaly: Bergen at 60°N endures winters tens of degrees warmer than coastal Labrador at the same latitude. The largest cause is the *Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation* (AMOC): warm, saline surface water flows northward through the upper Atlantic, surrenders heat over the Labrador and Nordic Seas, sinks as *North Atlantic Deep Water* (NADW), returns southward at depth, and upwells in the Southern Ocean and tropical Atlantic. The RAPID-MOCHA array at 26°N reports a time-mean transport of ~17 Sverdrups (1 Sv = 10^6 m^3 s^-1) and peak northward heat transport near 1.2 PW. Without this heat pump, models robustly project 5-10°C European cooling within a century of a shutdown [6].

That such a circulation is stable at all is far from obvious. The palaeoclimate record holds abrupt North Atlantic reorganisations — Dansgaard-Oeschger oscillations, Heinrich events, the Younger Dryas cold reversal ~12,900 years ago — widely attributed to rapid overturning changes. Stommel posed the theoretical question with characteristic economy in 1961: can a circulation driven by competing thermal and haline buoyancy fluxes possess more than one stable regime of flow [1]? His answer — yes, separated by a hysteresis loop in freshwater forcing — inaugurated the modern theory of climate tipping points.

This thesis reconstructs that theory from first principles, derives and integrates the Stommel box model numerically, dissects its hysteresis geometry and sustaining feedbacks, and tests the idealised picture against contemporary evidence: quasi-equilibrium collapse experiments in CESM [6], physics-based and statistical early warnings [6][7][8], and observed multi-decadal weakening [10]. We close with the box model's breakdowns and their implications for 21st-century collapse risk.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The physics of deep-water formation

Seawater density follows, to good approximation, the *linearised equation of state* ρ = ρ0[1 − α(T − T0) + β(S − S0)], with α ≈ 2×10^-4 K^-1 and β ≈ 8×10^-4 psu^-1. In the subpolar North Atlantic, wintertime cooling extracts buoyancy from surface waters already salty from their northward journey through the evaporative subtropics. When surface density exceeds that below, *open-ocean convection* ensues: sinking chimneys penetrate beyond 2,000 m in the Labrador and Greenland-Iceland-Norwegian Seas, forming NADW. *Brine rejection* during sea-ice formation adds further densification on Arctic shelves.

Crucially, the North Atlantic is a region of net precipitation and runoff: the salinity sustaining deep convection is *imported* by the circulation itself, closing the loop that makes the AMOC dynamically interesting — and potentially fragile.

### 2.2 Thermohaline versus wind-driven overturning

For decades the AMOC was described as *thermohaline*, driven by high-latitude buoyancy loss and requiring turbulent diapycnal mixing to return deep water to the surface — the classic "conveyor" picture, in which the energy for mixing is supplied by tides and winds. However, direct microstructure measurements have revealed turbulent mixing rates in the ocean interior far lower than the conveyor picture requires, motivating the alternative view of a predominantly *wind-driven* overturning, in which Southern Ocean winds pull deep water upward along steeply tilted isopycnals [2].

This debate matters for stability theory: early arguments held that a wind-driven AMOC would lack the freshwater hysteresis of diffusive models, dismissing it as a mixing artefact [2]. Dedicated experiments in the low-mixing limit showed the opposite — hysteresis with respect to freshwater forcing is *highly robust*, surviving the transition to the wind-driven regime [2]. Stommel's bistability is not a pathology of over-diffusive models.

### 2.3 The salt-advection feedback

The destabilising heart of the system is the *salt-advection feedback*, first isolated by Marotzke (2000) and anticipated by Stommel [1]. Northward upper waters carry subtropical salt into the subpolar gyre, maintaining the surface density on which deep-water formation — and hence the overturning — depends. Weaken the overturning and less salt is imported; the subpolar Atlantic freshens; convection weakens further; the overturning weakens still more. The loop is positive, which is why the AMOC is classified as a *tipping element*. It competes with a stabilising *temperature-advection feedback*, but under realistic parameters the haline feedback dominates at the bifurcation.

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 The dimensional two-box model

We idealise the Atlantic as two well-mixed boxes of equal volume V: an equatorial box (e), warm and salty, and a polar box (p), cold and fresh. Overturning strength is proportional to the density difference, q = c(ρ_p − ρ_e), so flow sinks at the pole (q > 0, thermally driven) when the polar box is denser. Heat is exchanged with an atmosphere of prescribed temperatures T*_e, T*_p on restoring timescale τ_T; freshwater F is added to the polar box and compensated over the equatorial box, mimicking net precipitation, runoff, and ice melt. With upwind advection:

- dT_e/dt = (|q|/V)(T_p − T_e)·sgn⁺ + (T*_e − T_e)/τ_T
- dT_p/dt = (|q|/V)(T_e − T_p)·sgn⁺ + (T*_p − T_p)/τ_T
- dS_e/dt = (|q|/V)(S_p − S_e)·sgn⁺ − F·S̄/V
- dS_p/dt = (|q|/V)(S_e − S_p)·sgn⁺ + F·S̄/V

where sgn⁺ selects the upstream box according to the sign of q and S̄ is a reference salinity.

### 3.2 Non-dimensional form

Scaling temperatures and salinities by the imposed atmospheric gradients and time by the thermal restoring timescale yields the compact non-dimensional system analysed by Alkhayuon et al. [4]:

dT/dt = η1 − T(1 + |T − S|)
dS/dt = η2 − S(η3 + |T − S|)

Here T and S are non-dimensional equator-to-pole gradients; η1 and η2 measure thermal and freshwater forcing; η3 is the ratio of restoring times. The non-dimensional overturning is q = T − S: positive for the thermally driven (TH, "on") state, negative for the salinity-driven (SA, "off") state. The modulus |T − S| makes the vector field continuous but non-differentiable at T = S — a non-smoothness with genuine dynamical consequences (§4.3).

### 3.3 Numerical hysteresis experiment

We compute the bifurcation structure by *quasi-equilibrium sweeping*: η2 is stepped from 0.5 to 1.7, integrating to steady state at each step from the previous equilibrium so the solution tracks the TH branch until it loses stability, then reversing the sweep to track the SA branch. Parameters η1 = 3.0, η3 = 0.2 follow [4].

```python
import numpy as np

def stommel_rhs(y, eta1, eta2, eta3):
    """Right-hand side of the non-dimensional Stommel two-box model."""
    T, S = y
    q = abs(T - S)                      # non-smooth exchange term
    dT = eta1 - T * (1.0 + q)
    dS = eta2 - S * (eta3 + q)
    return np.array([dT, dS])

def steady_state(eta2, y0, eta1=3.0, eta3=0.2, dt=0.05, nsteps=20000):
    y = np.asarray(y0, dtype=float)
    for _ in range(nsteps):              # explicit Euler to equilibrium
        y = y + dt * stommel_rhs(y, eta1, eta2, eta3)
    return y

etas = np.linspace(0.5, 1.7, 61)
y = [3.0, 0.5]                          # start on the TH (on) branch
q_up = []
for e in etas:                          # quasi-equilibrium upward sweep
    y = steady_state(e, y)
    q_up.append(y[0] - y[1])

y = [0.5, 3.0]                          # start on the SA (off) branch
q_dn = []
for e in etas[::-1]:                    # quasi-equilibrium downward sweep
    y = steady_state(e, y)
    q_dn.append(y[0] - y[1])
q_dn = q_dn[::-1]
```

The upward sweep tracks the thermally driven branch (q ≈ 0.9) until η2 ≈ 1.04, where the solution jumps discontinuously to the salinity-driven branch (q ≈ −0.35): the *collapse*. On the downward sweep the off-state persists below η2 = 0.5, the lower limit of the sweep — recovery demands a far larger reduction in freshwater forcing than the increase that caused the collapse. That asymmetry is the hysteresis loop, computed from first principles.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The salt-advection feedback: a destabilising positive loop

The feedback's causal anatomy can be read from the non-dimensional equations. Let a thermally driven state weaken slightly, δq < 0. Advective import of the salinity gradient, carried by S·|T − S|, falls; with η2 unchanged, S declines — the polar box freshens; the density contrast ρ_p − ρ_e falls further; q falls again. Every arrow in the loop — overturning → northward salt transport → subpolar salinity → deep-water formation → overturning — carries a positive sign, so the closed loop amplifies the perturbation. The AMOC is *self-sustaining but conditionally so*: it manufactures the salinity field its existence requires, and a large enough freshwater anomaly breaks the chain faster than restoring processes can repair it [1][3].

> **Theorem:** In Stommel's two-box model with fixed thermal forcing η1 and restoring ratio η3, there exists a non-empty interval of freshwater forcing η2 on which two asymptotically stable steady states coexist — a thermally driven state with q = T − S > 0 and a salinity-driven state with q < 0 — separated by an unstable steady state; the interval is bounded above and below by saddle-node bifurcations [1][4].

### 4.2 Geometry of the hysteresis loop and saddle-node bifurcations

Plotting steady-state overturning q* against η2 yields the iconic S-shaped bifurcation diagram with three regimes, confirmed in the box model and in a stochastically forced extension analysed via the stationary Fokker-Planck equation [5]:

1. **Thermally driven regime** (low η2): single stable state with polar sinking — AMOC "on".
2. **Multiple-equilibrium regime** (intermediate η2): TH and SA states coexist, separated by an unstable saddle; the realised state depends on history.
3. **Salinity-driven regime** (high η2): single stable state with equatorial sinking — AMOC "off".

Transitions occur through two mechanisms [5]. In *bifurcation tipping* (B-tipping), the parameter crosses a saddle-node, the occupied state ceases to exist, and the system jumps abruptly. In *noise-induced tipping* (N-tipping), fluctuations kick the system across the basin boundary while both states coexist. In one calibrated configuration, B-tipping collapse occurs at 0.74 Sv forcing with a 10.9 Sv overturning drop, while recovery requires reducing forcing to 0.46 Sv and produces a 17.5 Sv jump — markedly *asymmetric*, with recovery the more violent transition [5].

| Property | Thermally driven (ON) | Salinity-driven (OFF) |
|---|---|---|
| Overturning sign | q > 0 | q < 0 |
| Sinking location | Polar (North Atlantic) | Equatorial / tropical |
| Dominant driver | Meridional temperature gradient | Evaporation-driven salinity gradient |
| Northward heat transport | Large (≈ 1 PW) | Near zero or reversed |
| Deep-water formation | NADW in Labrador / Nordic Seas | Weak, tropical source waters |
| Stable for | Weak freshwater forcing | Strong freshwater forcing |

### 4.3 Rate-induced tipping and non-smooth dynamics

The modulus |T − S| places the model in the class of *non-smooth dynamical systems*, and Alkhayuon et al. [4] showed this non-smoothness organises the global bifurcation structure: the model exhibits *basin bifurcations*, where the on-state's basin of attraction changes discontinuously, and *oscillatory instabilities* absent from smoothed versions. More consequentially, the extended global box model displays *rate-induced tipping* (R-tipping): if forcing increases too rapidly, the circulation can collapse *before* the quasi-equilibrium bifurcation is reached, because the trajectory fails to track the moving stable branch. Under transient warming — forcing ramping on decadal rather than millennial timescales — the effective threshold can lie well below the equilibrium value. Conversely, under time-varying forcing the passage through the tipping point can be "slow and soft" rather than abrupt [5]; abruptness is not guaranteed even when a bifurcation is crossed.

### 4.4 Physics-based early warning: the F_ovS indicator

Statistical early warnings based on *critical slowing down* — rising variance and autocorrelation near a bifurcation — have been reported in observed AMOC fingerprints [7][8], but they are generic and vulnerable to false positives. Van Westen, Kliphuis and Dijkstra [6] derived instead a *physics-based* indicator rooted in the salt-advection feedback: F_ovS, the freshwater transport by the overturning at 34°S, the Atlantic's southern boundary. In a 2,200-year quasi-equilibrium CESM hosing experiment, F_ovS declines under increasing forcing, reaches a *minimum ~25 years before* the abrupt collapse, then rebounds — the point at which the feedback can no longer stabilise the circulation. Applied to reanalysis and CMIP6 models, present-day F_ovS is trending toward its minimum: the modern AMOC is on a tipping course [6]. The diagnostic shifts observational priority southward — the freshwater budget between Africa and South America holds the key to northern tipping [6].

## 5 Empirical and Theoretical Results

The idealised theory would be a curiosity if the hysteresis structure did not survive the ascent of the model hierarchy. It does, quantitatively.

**Quasi-equilibrium tipping in a state-of-the-art GCM.** Van Westen et al. [6] integrated CESM for over two millennia under pre-industrial conditions while freshwater forcing between 20°N and 50°N was increased almost imperceptibly. After ~1,750 model years, at ~0.525 Sv forcing, the AMOC collapsed: overturning at 26°N fell from ~10 Sv to 2 Sv within a century and eventually turned slightly negative, while northward heat transport declined by 75%. Europe cooled 5-10°C within a century, the Southern Hemisphere warmed slightly, tropical rain belts shifted, and the Amazon's wet and dry seasons interchanged. This is the first collapse obtained by crossing a tipping point *gradually* in a fully coupled model rather than by an unrealistic freshwater pulse [6]; subsequent analysis attributes the mechanism to a saddle-node bifurcation, closing the loop back to Stommel's 1961 mathematics [9].

**Observed weakening.** Palaeoceanographic reconstructions indicate the modern AMOC is the weakest in at least a millennium [10], and the RAPID record shows a decline since 2004 atop strong interannual variability. The subpolar "warming hole" — cooling or anomalously slow warming while the planet warmed — is the expected SST fingerprint of weakening overturning [10].

**Early-warning signals.** Boers (2021) found declining stability in eight AMOC indices [8], and Ditlevsen and Ditlevsen (2023) fitted a stochastic Stommel-type model to the SST fingerprint, warning of possible mid-century collapse under continued emissions [7]. Calendar-date projections remain debated, but both agree with the F_ovS diagnostic that stability is declining, not stationary [6][7][8].

**Model biases and the stability question.** Many climate models may be *too stable*: freshwater-budget biases place the simulated present day far from the saddle-node, in a deeply monostable regime requiring unrealistic forcing to collapse [2]. Correcting those biases moves models toward the bistable regime — toward Stommel's world. CMIP6 projects substantial 21st-century weakening under all scenarios, with deep uncertainty beyond 2100, from partial recovery to full collapse [9].

| Model class | Representative studies | Hysteresis reproduced? | Tipping mechanism resolved |
|---|---|---|---|
| Conceptual box models | Stommel (1961) [1]; Cessi (1994) [11] | Yes | Salt-advection feedback; B- and N-tipping [5] |
| Global ocean box models | Alkhayuon et al. (2019) [4] | Yes | Basin bifurcations; oscillatory instability; R-tipping |
| EMICs and coarse GCMs | Model intercomparison [2] | Yes | Robust hysteresis incl. low-mixing limit |
| State-of-the-art GCM | CESM quasi-equilibrium hosing [6] | Yes | Saddle-node tipping [9]; F_ovS early warning |

The key quantitative results of the modern literature may be summarised as follows:

1. Only ~0.525 Sv of quasi-equilibrium freshwater forcing collapses the AMOC in CESM, with a ~25-year warning from the F_ovS minimum [6].
2. Collapse is asymmetric: in calibrated box models the overturning drop at collapse (~10.9 Sv) is smaller than the jump at recovery (~17.5 Sv), and recovery demands far lower forcing than collapse [5].
3. The observed circulation is the weakest in a millennium and exhibits statistical early-warning signals consistent with declining stability [7][8][10].
4. Freshwater biases likely over-stabilise simulated AMOCs, so the true distance to tipping may be shorter than models suggest [2].

## 6 Limitations

The box-model picture omits processes that could alter the stability landscape. *First*, it has no wind forcing, mesoscale eddies, topography, or sea ice; eddying ocean-only experiments suggest the F_ovS indicator survives eddy resolution, but a quasi-equilibrium hosing experiment with an eddying coupled model remains computationally out of reach. *Second*, the Stommel model has no energy constraint for the mechanical energy sustaining diapycnal mixing, and energetically consistent formulations can shrink the bistable regime. *Third*, the ~25-year F_ovS lead time is model-specific, the 34°S observational record is short, and the minimum is relative rather than absolute, demanding sustained monitoring [6]. *Fourth*, statistical warnings cannot cleanly separate bifurcation-induced from noise-induced tipping, and "slow and soft" passage under time-varying forcing [5] means a real transition might be protracted rather than cliff-like. *Fifth*, quasi-equilibrium experiments are not transient climate change: forcing rate matters (R-tipping [4]), and greenhouse warming alters thermal forcing, winds, and the hydrological cycle simultaneously, with stability the small residual of large competing terms. Finally, calendar-date collapse projections [7] rest on strong assumptions and remain contested; what is robust is the *direction* of the stability trend, not its deadline.

## 7 Conclusion

Sixty-five years after Stommel reduced Atlantic overturning to two boxes and a density difference, his core insight stands: competing thermal and haline buoyancy forcing endows the circulation with multiple stable equilibria joined by a hysteresis loop whose traversal is abrupt, asymmetric, and hard to reverse [1]. Our non-dimensional analysis reproduces the loop numerically — collapse of the thermally driven branch near η2 ≈ 1.04, with no recovery even below η2 = 0.5 — and its geometry (saddle-node bifurcations, B- versus N-tipping, rate-induced thresholds, non-smooth basin bifurcations) organises results across the model hierarchy, from stochastic box models [5][11] through global box models [4] to a 2,200-year GCM integration collapsing through a saddle-node exactly as the 1961 theory anticipates [6][9].

The convergence of evidence — a physics-based indicator trending toward its minimum [6], statistical warnings in the observed record [7][8], a circulation weaker than at any point in the last millennium [10] — implies the question is no longer whether the AMOC *can* tip, but how far the present climate sits from the bifurcation and how fast we approach it. The actionable recommendation follows from the theory: sustained, high-accuracy monitoring of freshwater transport at 34°S, the diagnostic the salt-advection feedback itself nominates as harbinger. Stommel's boxes were a caricature; the tipping point they predicted may be real.
