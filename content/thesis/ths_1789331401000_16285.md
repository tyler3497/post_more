# Polar Codes and Channel Polarization: Arikan's Construction, Successive-Cancellation List Decoding, and 5G Adoption

## Abstract

In 2009, Erdal Arikan introduced polar codes — the first explicitly constructed family of codes provably achieving the symmetric capacity of every binary-input discrete memoryless channel under a low-complexity decoder. The mechanism, *channel polarization*, recursively synthesizes virtual channels that polarize to nearly perfect or nearly useless extremes, with the fraction of perfect channels tending to the channel capacity. This thesis presents Arikan's construction via the 2×2 kernel and its Kronecker powers, the Bhattacharyya-parameter machinery governing construction, and successive-cancellation decoding in *O(N log N)* time. It then develops the successive-cancellation list decoder of Tal and Vardy and CRC-aided decoding, which transformed polar codes from an asymptotic curiosity into a finite-length competitor to LDPC and turbo codes. We survey the error-exponent theory, larger polarization kernels, and the 2016–2018 adoption of polar codes for 5G New Radio control channels under 3GPP TS 38.212 — the first industrial deployment of a Shannon capacity-achieving construction.

## 1 Introduction

Shannon's 1948 channel coding theorem is one of the great existence proofs of engineering: every discrete memoryless channel with capacity *C* admits codes of any rate below *C* whose error probability can be driven to zero. Yet for six decades the theorem's proof was non-constructive. In 2009, Erdal Arikan closed that gap. **Polar codes** are the first explicitly constructed family of codes provably achieving the symmetric capacity of every binary-input discrete memoryless channel, with both encoding and decoding complexity of order *N log N* [1]. The mechanism behind them — *channel polarization* — is arguably the most original idea in coding theory since Shannon himself. Starting from *N* identical copies of a channel *W*, a recursive combining-and-splitting transform synthesizes *N* new virtual channels that, as *N* grows, polarize: a fraction tending to *I(W)* becomes nearly perfect, while the rest become nearly useless. Coding then reduces to sending information through the good channels and freezing the bad ones.

This thesis develops the subject from first principles. Section 2 fixes the channel model and the Bhattacharyya reliability measure. Section 3 describes our expository methodology. Section 4 gives the deep technical core: the 2×2 kernel, the polarization process and its speed, successive-cancellation decoding, the Tal–Vardy list decoder with CRC aiding [4], and the exponent theory of larger kernels [3]. Section 5 states the main theorems with proof sketches and surveys the empirical record, including the 2016–2018 decision by 3GPP to adopt polar codes for 5G New Radio control channels [6][7]. Section 6 confronts the limitations — finite-length weakness of plain successive cancellation, construction complexity, non-universality, and decoding latency — and Section 7 concludes.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The binary-input discrete memoryless channel

A **binary-input discrete memoryless channel** (B-DMC) *W* is specified by finite input alphabet {0, 1}, a finite (or continuous) output alphabet *Y*, and transition probabilities *W(y|x)*. The channel is *symmetric* when a permutation of the output alphabet maps the *x = 0* distribution onto the *x = 1* distribution. The **symmetric capacity** is

$$I(W) = \sum_{y}\sum_{x \in \{0,1\}} \tfrac{1}{2}\, W(y|x)\, \log_2 \frac{W(y|x)}{\tfrac{1}{2}W(y|0) + \tfrac{1}{2}W(y|1)},$$

the mutual information under uniform input — the highest rate achievable with equiprobable signaling [1].

The second workhorse quantity is the **Bhattacharyya parameter**

$$Z(W) = \sum_{y \in \mathcal{Y}} \sqrt{W(y|0)\,W(y|1)},$$

which lives in the unit interval. *Z(W)* is an upper bound on the maximum-likelihood error probability of a single use of *W*; it tends to 0 exactly when the channel is nearly noiseless and to 1 exactly when it is nearly useless. Arikan's construction uses *Z* as the reliability ordering: virtual channels with small *Z* carry data, those with large *Z* are frozen.

### 2.2 The landscape before polarization

Three traditions preceded polar codes:

- **Algebraic codes** (Reed–Solomon, BCH): strong distance guarantees, but decoding complexity and rates ill-suited to soft-decision capacity approach.
- **Convolutional and turbo codes**: turbo codes (1993) stunned the field by approaching capacity with iterative decoding, and they carried 3G/4G data channels — but no capacity-achieving proof and a persistent error floor.
- **LDPC codes**: rediscovered in the mid-1990s, capacity-approaching under belief propagation with excellent thresholds, adopted for DVB-S2, Wi-Fi, and 5G data channels — again without a general provable construction at manageable complexity.

Polar codes entered as the first family combining *explicit construction*, *provable capacity achievement*, and *O(N log N)* encoding and decoding [1].

## 3 Methodology

This thesis is expository and synthetic rather than experimental: our "method" is a structured reconstruction of the theory from primary sources, with each technical claim anchored to a verifiable reference.

1. **Reconstruction of the combining transform.** We present Arikan's channel combining and splitting operations exactly as in [1], including the conservation identities that make polarization inevitable rather than accidental.
2. **The polarization process.** We formalize the Bhattacharyya random process, its supermartingale property, and the almost-sure convergence to the extremes — the probabilistic engine of the theory — following [1] and the rate refinement of [2].
3. **Decoding as inference.** We derive successive-cancellation decoding as recursive likelihood-ratio computation, then extend it to the Tal–Vardy list decoder and CRC-aided decoding [4], whose capacity-achieving property under list decoding was later formalized in [5].
4. **Exponent and kernel theory.** We summarize the Korada–Şaşoğlu–Urbanke characterization of polarization exponents and the impossibility result for small kernels [3].
5. **Standardization record.** For the 5G account we rely on the 3GPP meeting record and specification TS 38.212 as surveyed in [6], cross-checked against an open reference implementation of the 5G polar chain [7].

All inline citations [n] refer to the numbered References; every reference is cited at least once.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Two-by-Two Kernel and Recursive Channel Synthesis

The entire edifice rests on one elementary operation. Take two independent copies of a B-DMC *W* and combine them as

$$W_2(y_1, y_2 \mid u_1, u_2) = W(y_1 \mid u_1 \oplus u_2)\, W(y_2 \mid u_2),$$

where ⊕ is modulo-2 addition. Now *split* the combined channel back into two virtual channels by decoding order: the first sees *u_1* with *(y_1, y_2)* as output while treating *u_2* as noise,

$$W_2^{(1)}(y_1, y_2 \mid u_1) = \sum_{u_2} \tfrac{1}{2}\, W_2(y_1, y_2 \mid u_1, u_2),$$

and the second sees *u_2* with *(y_1, y_2, u_1)* as output, i.e., with *u_1* already known. Three facts, all elementary, change everything [1]:

- **Conservation:** *I(W_2^{(1)}) + I(W_2^{(2)}) = 2I(W)*. No capacity is created or destroyed.
- **Strict improvement (for non-extremal W):** *I(W_2^{(1)}) ≤ I(W) ≤ I(W_2^{(2)})*, with strict inequality unless *I(W)* is already 0 or 1. One virtual channel is *worse* than the original, one *better*.
- **Recursion:** applying the same transform to pairs of *W_2^{(i)}* channels yields *W_4^{(i)}*, and iterating *n* times yields *N = 2^n* virtual channels *W_N^{(i)}*.

> **Theorem (Channel polarization, Arikan [1]):** For any B-DMC *W* and any δ in the open unit interval, as *N = 2^n → ∞* through powers of two, the fraction of indices *i* with *I(W_N^{(i)})* exceeding *1 − δ* tends to *I(W)*, and the fraction with *I(W_N^{(i)})* below *δ* tends to *1 − I(W)*.

The encoder is the Kronecker power of the 2×2 kernel whose rows are (1, 0) and (1, 1): with bit-reversal permutation *B_N*,

$$G_N = B_N F^{\otimes n}, \qquad x_1^N = u_1^N G_N.$$

The Python fragment below builds the transform directly, showing how little machinery encoding requires:

```python
import numpy as np

def kronecker_power(F, n):
    """Return F^{otimes n}, the n-fold Kronecker power of the 2x2 kernel."""
    G = np.array([[1]])
    for _ in range(n):
        G = np.kron(G, F)
    return G % 2

def bit_reversal_permutation(n):
    N = 1 << n
    perm = [int(f"{i:0{n}b}"[::-1], 2) for i in range(N)]
    return perm

def polar_encode(u, frozen):
    """Encode info vector u (frozen positions preset to 0) via G_N."""
    n = int(np.log2(len(u)))
    F = np.array([[1, 0], [1, 1]])
    G = kronecker_power(F, n)
    perm = bit_reversal_permutation(n)
    return (u @ G[perm, :]) % 2
```

Choosing the information set *A* as the *K* indices with smallest *Z(W_N^{(i)})* and freezing the complement to zero yields a polar code of rate *R = K/N*. The whole construction is deterministic given *W* [1].

### 4.2 The Bhattacharyya Process and the Speed of Polarization

Why must the recursion polarize rather than merely spread? Track the Bhattacharyya parameter through one combining step. For the upgraded channel the relation is exact — *Z(W^+) = Z(W)^2* — while for the degraded channel *Z(W^-) ≤ 2Z(W) − Z(W)^2*, with equality for erasure channels [1]. Now define a random process: let *B_1, B_2, …* be fair coin flips and set *Z_0 = Z(W)*, *Z_{n+1} = Z_n^2* if *B_{n+1} = 1* and *Z_{n+1} ∈ [Z_n, 2Z_n − Z_n^2]* otherwise. This process is a bounded **supermartingale**, so it converges almost surely, and the only possible limit points are the fixed points of squaring: 0 and 1. Conservation of capacity pins down the probabilities: *Z_n → 0* with probability *I(W)* and *Z_n → 1* with probability *1 − I(W)*.

The *speed* of this convergence governs finite-length performance. Arikan and Telatar sharpened the original *O(N^{−1/4})* block-error bound dramatically [2]:

> **Theorem (Rate of polarization, Arikan–Telatar [2]):** For any B-DMC *W* with *I(W) > 0*, any rate *R < I(W)*, and any *β < 1/2*, there exist polar codes of blocklength *N* and rate at least *R* whose block-error probability under successive-cancellation decoding satisfies *P_e ≤ 2^{−N^β}* for all sufficiently large *N*.

The constant *1/2* is the **polarization exponent** of the 2×2 kernel — no accident but a property of the kernel itself. Korada, Şaşoğlu, and Urbanke characterized the exponent of *any* ℓ×ℓ kernel and proved a striking impossibility: **no kernel smaller than 15×15 has exponent exceeding 1/2**, while BCH-based constructions achieve exponents arbitrarily close to 1 for large sizes [3]. The 2×2 kernel is thus optimal among all small kernels, which partly explains why the simplest possible construction survived all challengers.

### 4.3 Successive-Cancellation Decoding

The decoder mirrors the encoder's recursion. Given channel log-likelihood ratios *L_i = log(W(y_i|0)/W(y_i|1))*, the decoder estimates *û_1, …, û_N* in order, using already-decided bits as known side information. The two computational kernels on the factor graph are the *boxplus* operation

$$f(a, b) = \mathrm{sign}(a)\,\mathrm{sign}(b)\,\min(|a|, |b|) \approx 2\,\mathrm{atanh}\!\left(\tanh\tfrac{a}{2}\tanh\tfrac{b}{2}\right)$$

for the degraded (minus) branch and

$$g(a, b, \hat{u}_s) = (-1)^{\hat{u}_s} a + b$$

for the upgraded (plus) branch, where *û_s* is the previously decided partial sum. The decision rule is

$$\hat{u}_i = \begin{cases} 0, & i \notin \mathcal{A}\ (\text{frozen}), \\ 0, & i \in \mathcal{A}\ \text{and}\ L_N^{(i)} \ge 0, \\ 1, & \text{otherwise}. \end{cases}$$

Each of the *N* bit-channels costs *O(log N)* likelihood computations, for total *O(N log N)* time and *O(N)* memory [1]. The block-error probability is bounded by the sum of Bhattacharyya parameters over the information set, *P_e ≤ Σ_{i∈A} Z(W_N^{(i)})* — the analytic handle that makes every later performance claim provable rather than merely simulated.

The recursion has a clean functional formulation; in Haskell the decoder is a fold over the polarization tree:

```haskell
-- LLR-domain successive cancellation over the polar factor tree.
data PTree = Leaf Int Double        -- bit index and channel LLR
           | Node PTree PTree

boxplus :: Double -> Double -> Double
boxplus a b = signum a * signum b * min (abs a) (abs b)

gNode :: Double -> Double -> Int -> Double
gNode a b uhat = (if uhat == 0 then 1 else -1) * a + b

scDecode :: PTree -> [Int] -> ([Int], PTree)
scDecode (Leaf i llr) frozen
  | i `elem` frozen = ([0], Leaf i llr)
  | otherwise       = ([if llr >= 0 then 0 else 1], Leaf i llr)
scDecode (Node l r) frozen =
  let (uL, l') = scDecode l frozen
      partial  = map (`mod` 2) uL          -- propagate partial sums
      (uR, r') = scDecode (inject partial r) frozen
  in (uL ++ uR, Node l' r')
  where inject _ t = t  -- partial sums enter the g-kernel (elided)
```

A single wrong hard decision propagates, since later likelihoods condition on it — at short blocklengths this error propagation makes plain SC mediocre, the gap list decoding was invented to close [4].

### 4.4 Successive-Cancellation List Decoding and CRC Aiding

Tal and Vardy generalized successive cancellation from a single greedy path to a **beam search** over the code tree [4]. At each information bit the decoder splits every surviving path in two, scores each extension by a path metric derived from the bit-channel LLRs, and prunes back to the *L* best paths. Frozen bits prune automatically. The complexity is *O(LN log N)* with an efficient lazy-copy implementation, and for moderate *L* the decoder is effectively maximum-likelihood: the correct path survives with overwhelming probability whenever *any* near-ML decoder could find it.

The decisive practical ingredient is **CRC aiding**. Concatenate an outer cyclic-redundancy-check code: after list decoding, discard every candidate failing the CRC and select the most likely survivor. The CRC acts as a genie that identifies the correct path among the list, and the finite-length gains are dramatic — polar-CRC codes under list decoding were shown to outperform the LDPC codes of the WiMAX standard [4], a result that single-handedly moved polar codes from theory seminars into standards meetings. Later work formalized the picture: precoded polar codes, the family including CRC-precoded constructions, provably achieve symmetric capacity under successive-cancellation list decoding [5].

| Decoder | Complexity | Finite-length quality | Provable capacity |
|---|---|---|---|
| Successive cancellation (SC) | *O(N log N)* | Mediocre at short *N* | Yes [1] |
| SC list, size *L* (SCL) | *O(LN log N)* | Near-ML for moderate *L* | Yes (precoded) [5] |
| CRC-aided SCL | *O(LN log N)* + CRC | Excellent; beats WiMAX LDPC [4] | Via precoding [5] |
| Belief propagation | *O(IN log N)*, *I* iterations | Limited gain over SC on AWGN | No general proof |

The Rust-style sketch below shows the heart of list decoding — the path-metric update that replaces SC's hard decision:

```rust
/// Extend one decoding path with a hypothesized bit value.
/// `llr` is the bit-channel LLR; the metric penalizes disagreement.
fn extend_path(path: &mut Path, bit: u8, llr: f64) {
    // Path metric: accumulate penalty when the hypothesized bit
    // contradicts the sign of the LLR (Tal--Vardy metric).
    let penalty = if (llr >= 0.0) == (bit == 0) { 0.0 } else { llr.abs() };
    path.metric += penalty;
    path.bits.push(bit);
}

/// Prune 2L extended paths back to the L smallest metrics.
fn prune(paths: &mut Vec<Path>, l: usize) {
    paths.sort_by(|a, b| a.metric.partial_cmp(&b.metric).unwrap());
    paths.truncate(l);
}
```

### 4.5 Exponents, Larger Kernels, and the Road to 5G

Three theoretical threads converged to make polar codes deployable. First, the exponent theory [3] showed the 2×2 kernel is optimal among small kernels, licensing the industry to standardize the simplest construction. Second, list decoding with CRC [4][5] closed the finite-length gap to turbo and LDPC codes. Third, systematic encoding, simplified decoders, and hardware-friendly list architectures drove implementation cost down to competitive levels [4].

The standardization outcome was historic. In 3GPP RAN1 deliberations through 2016–2018, **polar codes were selected for the 5G New Radio control channels** — the physical broadcast channel (PBCH), the physical downlink control channel (PDCCH), and uplink control information (PUCCH) — while LDPC took the data channels [6]. The specification, 3GPP TS 38.212, defines a complete polar coding chain: distributed CRC (D-CRC) bits interleaved with information for early termination, parity-check (PC) frozen bits derived from a length-5 shift register, a sub-block interleaver, and rate matching by puncturing, shortening, or repetition [6][7]. Representative parameters from the standard: PBCH uses blocklength 56 including a 24-bit CRC with *N_max = 512*; DCI on PDCCH supports information sizes up to *A_max = 140*; PUCCH segmentation applies for large payloads up to *A_max = 1706* [6]. This was the first industrial deployment of a provably capacity-achieving code family — a direct line from Arikan's 2008 preprint [1] to silicon in every 5G handset.

## 5 Empirical Evidence and Proofs

### 5.1 Proof sketches of the main theorems

**Polarization (sketch).** The Bhattacharyya process *{Z_n}* is a bounded supermartingale, hence convergent a.s. to some *Z_∞*. Squaring is the only exact recursion (*Z → Z^2* on the plus branch), so *Z_∞* satisfies *Z_∞ = Z_∞^2* a.s., forcing *Z_∞ ∈ {0, 1}*. Because total symmetric capacity is conserved at every combining step, *E[1 − Z_∞]*-type accounting gives *P(Z_∞ = 0) = I(W)*. Translating the process back to indices yields the polarization theorem [1].

**Rate of polarization (sketch).** Arikan and Telatar analyze the speed at which *Z_n* collapses by studying the process *Z_n^{1/2^n}*-style scalings and large-deviation behavior near the extremes; the exponent *1/2* emerges from the linearization of the recursion around 0 and 1. The union bound *P_e ≤ Σ_{i∈A} Z(W_N^{(i)})* then converts per-channel reliability into the block-error guarantee *2^{−N^β}*, *β < 1/2* [2].

**Capacity under list decoding (sketch).** The formalization in [5] shows that successive-cancellation list decoding with unbounded list contains the SC decoder's decisions as a special case and that precoded polar codes — including CRC-precoded ones — inherit the polarization argument, hence achieve *I(W)* under SCL as well.

### 5.2 The empirical record

- **Near-ML at moderate list sizes.** Simulations in [4] show SCL with *L = 32* essentially coinciding with maximum-likelihood bounds near blocklength 2048, while plain SC lags by more than a decibel — the CRC providing the largest single gain.
- **Beating incumbents.** Polar-CRC under list decoding outperformed the WiMAX LDPC code in [4], with independent studies confirming competitiveness against LTE turbo codes at short control-channel blocklengths — precisely the regime 5G control channels occupy.
- **Standardization-grade link simulations.** The 3GPP record in [6] documents extensive BLER-vs-SNR simulations across PBCH, PDCCH, and PUCCH formats that drove the selection of distributed CRC, PC bits, and the interleaver; the open reference chain in [7] reproduces the TS 38.212 encoder and SCL decoder bit-exactly.
- **Hardware reality.** List decoders with *L = 8* — the typical 5G operating point — have been synthesized at multi-Gb/s throughputs, confirming that *O(LN log N)* complexity is practical, not merely asymptotic [4][6].

## 6 Limitations

1. **Finite-length weakness of plain SC.** The capacity theorem is asymptotic, and the *1/2* exponent means convergence is slow: at *N = 1024* and below, uncoded-list SC decoding is uncompetitive. Everything practical about polar codes depends on the CRC-plus-list machinery [4], which the original theory did not include.
2. **List decoding cost and latency.** *O(LN log N)* with *L = 8–32* is affordable but not free, and the serial bit-by-bit nature of SCL resists parallelization; throughput-focused designs resort to partitioned, fast-simplified, or stack variants with their own trade-offs.
3. **Construction complexity.** The information set depends on the channel, and exact reliability computation is intractable, so practice uses density evolution or Gaussian approximation — each with its own finite-length mismatch loss.
4. **Non-universality.** A polar code constructed for one B-DMC is not guaranteed for another; the information sets differ. Compound-capacity constructions exist (e.g., staircase and alignment schemes [5]–style universality results), but they inflate blocklength and complexity.
5. **Power-of-two blocklengths.** Arbitrary lengths require puncturing or shortening, which perturbs the polarization structure; 5G rate matching handles this, but not without design effort [6][7].

None of these limitations has blocked deployment, but each is an active research frontier.

## 7 Conclusion

Channel polarization is that rare thing in engineering science: a genuinely new phenomenon, discovered rather than invented, with a proof that fits on a few pages and consequences that reached cellular base stations within a decade. Arikan's transform [1] turned the vague hope of "capacity-achieving codes with practical decoding" into an explicit *O(N log N)* reality; the rate analysis [2] and exponent theory [3] mapped its fundamental limits; Tal and Vardy's list decoder with CRC aiding [4][5] made it competitive at the blocklengths that matter; and 3GPP's adoption for 5G NR control channels [6][7] made it the first Shannon-theory-optimal code family ever deployed at scale.

The deeper lesson is methodological: Shannon proved codes *exist*, turbo and LDPC practitioners found codes that *work*, and Arikan showed how to *construct* codes that provably achieve capacity — with the community then rediscovering, through list decoding and CRC concatenation, the engineering pragmatism that turns a theorem into a product.
