# Sieve Methods in Analytic Number Theory: Brun's Pure Sieve, the Selberg Sieve, the Parity Barrier, and Bounded Gaps Between Primes

![Filtration diagram for Brun's pure sieve, crossing out multiples of small primes up to a sifting level](public/thesis/ths_1789198146000_04286-0.webp)

## Abstract

Sieve methods constitute the principal analytic instrument for detecting primes in thin sequences, replacing the unavailable exact Möbius inversion by upper and lower approximants of controllable remainder. This survey develops the three classical architectures — the combinatorial **Brun sieve** (1915), the quadratic **Selberg sieve** (1947), and the large sieve — derives the fundamental lemma in its modern form, and analyzes Selberg's *parity barrier*, the theorem that no sieve can distinguish integers with an even number of prime factors from those with an odd number, which blocks direct sieving routes to the twin prime conjecture. The obstruction is then traced through its circumvention: the Bombieri–Vinogradov theorem and Zhang's refinement of the level of distribution for smooth moduli power the Goldston–Pintz–Yıldırım method to its conditional limit; Yitang Zhang's 2013 breakthrough established the first finite bound $\liminf(p_{n+1}-p_n)\le 70\,000\,000$, refined by Polymath8a to $4680$, by Maynard to $600$, and by Polymath8b to $246$, with the sieve-theoretic optimum $H_1\le 6$ conditional on the generalized Elliott–Halberstam conjecture. We present the governing heuristics, the exact statements, the remainder architecture, and the remaining limitations of the sieve program.

## 1 Intro

The *sieve of Eratosthenes* (ca. 200 BCE) is the oldest known prime-detecting algorithm: write the integers up to $x$, then successively cross out the multiples of each prime $p \le \sqrt{x}$. What survives is precisely the set of primes in the interval. For more than two millennia this procedure was a *computational* recipe, not an analytic tool; the breakthrough that converted it into one was due to Viggo Brun, who in 1915 realized that *truncating* the inclusion–exclusion expansion of the sieve process yields controllable upper and lower bounds for the sifted set. Brun's 1915 paper founded combinatorial sieve theory, showing that crude, finitely truncated inclusion–exclusion could produce theorems of genuine depth [1].

The program advanced in three great waves. First, the **combinatorial sieves** of Brun, Buchstab, and their successors, culminating in the modern *fundamental lemma* of sieve theory, which states that inclusion–exclusion truncated at an optimal point behaves, up to small relative error, like the natural probabilistic heuristic. Second, the **Selberg sieve** of 1947: Atle Selberg observed that the weights applied to each surviving integer could be chosen by *optimizing a quadratic form*, producing the sharpest known upper bounds for the sifted set [1]. Third, the **large sieve** of Linnik and Rényi, recast by Bombieri and Montgomery as an inequality about trigonometric polynomials, which turned equidistribution of primes in arithmetic progressions into viable input for sieves — the *level of distribution* machinery of Bombieri and Vinogradov.

This article surveys the full architecture: the formal definition of the sifted set and its main term, Brun's pure sieve and the fundamental lemma, Selberg's weights and their optimality, the parity barrier that fundamentally limits all sieves, and the modern climax — the proof that prime gaps are bounded, via the work of Goldston, Pintz, and Yıldırım [6], Zhang [2], Maynard [3], and the Polymath8 collaborations [4][5].

## 2 Background

Let $\mathcal{A}$ be a finite sequence of integers (the *sequence to be sifted*), $\mathcal{P}$ a set of primes (usually all primes, or the primes in some interval), and $z\ge 2$ a *sifting level*. Define

$$S(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{P},z) = \#\{a \in \mathcal{A} : (a, P(z)) = 1\},\qquad P(z) = \prod_{\substack{p < z\\ p \in \mathcal{P}}} p.$$

The sifted set $S(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{P},z)$ counts the elements of $\mathcal{A}$ with no prime factor below $z$. Taking $\mathcal{A} = \{1, \dots, x\}$ and $z = \sqrt{x}$ gives $S = \pi(x) - \pi(\sqrt{x}) + 1$: *exact* prime detection. The difficulty is that exact detection by inclusion–exclusion,

$$S(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{P},z) = \sum_{d \mid P(z)} \mu(d)\, |\mathcal{A}_d|, \qquad \mathcal{A}_d = \{a \in \mathcal{A} : d \mid a\},$$

requires summing over $2^{\pi(z)}$ divisors — impossible for large $z$. Every sieve method is therefore a strategy for *approximating* this Möbius sum with controlled error. The standard decomposition writes $|\mathcal{A}_d| = X\,\omega(d)/d + r_d$, where $X$ approximates $|\mathcal{A}|$, the multiplicative function $\omega$ captures the *density* of the sequence modulo $d$ (with $0 \le \omega(p) < p$), and $r_d$ is the *remainder*. The sieve problem is to choose signed weights $\lambda_d$ supported on $d < D$ (the *level of the sieve*) so that

$$S(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{P},z) \approx X \sum_{d \mid P(z)} \lambda_d \frac{\omega(d)}{d} + \sum_d \lambda_d r_d,$$

with the remainder sum small. The central tension: the *main term* wants $z$ large while the *remainder* wants $D$ small; the art of the sieve is choosing weights that reconcile the two.

Key terminology used throughout this survey:

| Symbol | Meaning |
|---|---|
| $S(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{P},z)$ | Sifted set: elements of $\mathcal{A}$ with no prime factor in $\mathcal{P}$ below $z$ |
| $P(z)$ | Product of the sifting primes $p < z$ |
| $D$ | Sieve level: support bound for the weights $\lambda_d$ |
| $s = \log D / \log z$ | Sifting ratio; controls the quality of all estimates |
| $\omega(d)$ | Local density function, multiplicative, $0\le \omega(p)<p$ |
| $V(z) = \prod_{p<z}(1-\omega(p)/p)$ | Expected surviving proportion (the main-term factor) |
| $X$ | Approximation to $\|\mathcal{A}\|$ |
| $r_d$ | Remainder: $|\mathcal{A}_d| - X\omega(d)/d$ |

The *dimension* $\kappa$ of a sieve is defined by the average behavior $\sum_{p<z} \omega(p)\log p / p \sim \kappa \log z$; the linear sieve ($\kappa = 1$) governs twin-prime-like problems, and its extremal functions $F, f$ are the celebrated solutions of the Buchstab-type differential–difference system [1]. The *fundamental lemma* asserts, roughly, that for $s$ large the sifted set satisfies $S = XV(z)(1 + O(e^{-s}))$ — inclusion–exclusion truncated optimally mimics the naive probabilistic prediction $XV(z)$.

## 3 Methodology

The three great sieve architectures differ in how they choose the weights $\lambda_d$:

1. **Combinatorial (Brun) sieves.** Truncate the inclusion–exclusion sum directly, using only the terms with $\omega(d) = \nu$ below an even cutoff $r$ (upper bound) or an odd cutoff (lower bound). The weights are explicit, $\lambda_d \in \{-1, 0, 1\}$, and the error is controlled by the *tail* of the binomial expansion. Advantage: genuine lower bounds. Disadvantage: the main term loses a positive proportion — the *Brun deficit*.
2. **The Selberg sieve.** Choose the weights to *minimize* the quadratic form $Q = \sum_{d_1, d_2} \lambda_{d_1}\lambda_{d_2}\, \omega([d_1,d_2])/[d_1,d_2]$ subject to $\lambda_1 = 1$. The optimum is explicit: $\lambda_d = \mu(d)\rho_d$ with $\rho_d$ a truncated divisor sum. Advantage: the sharpest possible upper bound from this diagonal approach, with a clean main term. Disadvantage: *only* an upper bound — the method is intrinsically one-sided.
3. **The large sieve and dispersion methods.** Bound the remainder $\sum |\lambda_d r_d|$ using mean-value estimates over moduli — the Bombieri–Vinogradov theorem and its refinements. These are not sieves per se but the *engine room* that extends the admissible level $D$ and hence the sifting ratio $s$.

A minimal computational model of Brun's pure sieve illustrates the truncation principle:

```python
def brun_pure_sieve(limit: int, z: int, r: int) -> int:
    """Upper bound for pi(limit) via Brun's pure sieve with truncation r."""
    import math
    primes = [p for p in range(2, z) if all(p % q for q in range(2, int(p**0.5) + 1))]
    X = limit
    total = X  # d = 1 term
    # truncated inclusion-exclusion: sum over squarefree d with omega(d) <= r
    def rec(idx: int, d: int, omega: int, sign: int) -> int:
        if omega > r or idx == len(primes):
            return 0
        acc = 0
        for i in range(idx, len(primes)):
            dd = d * primes[i]
            if dd > limit:
                break
            acc += sign * (X // dd) + rec(i + 1, dd, omega + 1, -sign)
        return acc
    return X + rec(0, 1, 0, -1)
```

Choosing $r$ *even* gives an upper bound and $r$ *odd* a lower bound — the parity of truncation governing all combinatorial sieves; the GPY multidimensional sieve generalizes this architecture to prime $k$-tuples [6].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Brun's Pure Sieve and the Fundamental Lemma

Brun's original idea was disarmingly simple: in the inclusion–exclusion expansion of $S(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{P},z)$, *discard* all terms with $\omega(d) > r$ for a fixed even $r$. Since the terms alternate in sign and decrease (for the classical sequences), the partial sum over $\omega(d) \le r$ with $r$ even is an *upper* bound, and with $r$ odd a *lower* bound. The error is bounded by the first omitted term — the *tail* of the expansion. Optimizing $r$ against the sifting ratio $s = \log D/\log z$ gives the **fundamental lemma**: $S(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{P},z) = XV(z)(1+O(e^{-s/2}))$ for $s$ bounded away from the critical point [1].

> **Theorem:** *(Brun, 1915).* Let $P_2(x)$ count integers $n \le x$ with $\Omega(n) \le 2$. Then the number of twin almost-primes satisfies $\#\{p \le x : p+2 \in P_2\} \gg x/(\log x)^2$. In particular, $\sum_{p,\,p+2\ \text{prime}} 1/p$ converges — Brun's constant.

Brun's theorem on twin almost-primes was the first sieve result on prime pairs, and it remains the template: the sieve cannot produce the primes themselves, but it *can* produce almost-primes in abundance. Every later refinement — including Chen's theorem that $p+2$ is infinitely often prime-or-product-of-two-primes — is a sharpening of this Brun-type lower bound [1].

### 4.2 The Selberg Sieve and the Optimal Quadratic Form

Selberg's 1947 innovation began from the observation that $S(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{P},z) \le \sum_{a \in \mathcal{A}} \bigl(\sum_{d \mid (a, P(z))} \lambda_d\bigr)^2$ for *any* choice of real weights $\lambda_d$ with $\lambda_1 = 1$, since the inner sum is $1$ when $(a, P(z)) = 1$ and the square is nonnegative regardless. Expanding the square and summing over $a$ gives

$$S \le X \sum_{d_1, d_2} \lambda_{d_1}\lambda_{d_2}\frac{\omega([d_1,d_2])}{[d_1,d_2]} + \sum_{d_1,d_2} |\lambda_{d_1}\lambda_{d_2}|\, |r_{[d_1,d_2]}|.$$

Minimizing the main-term quadratic form over $\lambda_d$ supported on $d < \sqrt{D}$ yields the closed-form optimum $\lambda_d = \mu(d)\rho_d/\rho_1$ where $\rho_d = \sum_{d \mid m < \sqrt{D}} \omega(m)/m$, and the minimal value

$$S(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{P},z) \le \frac{X}{G(z)} + \text{remainder},\qquad G(z) = \sum_{\substack{d < \sqrt{D}\\ d \mid P(z)}} \frac{\omega(d)}{d}\prod_{p\mid d}\Bigl(1-\frac{\omega(p)}{p}\Bigr)^{-1},$$

with $G(z)^{-1} \sim e^{-\gamma}V(z)/\log z$ in the linear case — the familiar Selberg upper bound, sharp up to the constant and the natural barrier of the method. This diagonalization makes the Selberg sieve the default *upper-bound* machine: it is essentially the best bound obtainable from second-moment information [1].

![Optimization of the Selberg quadratic form: optimal weights lambda_d on the divisor lattice](public/thesis/ths_1789198146000_04286-1.webp)

The weight computation in Haskell:

```haskell
-- Selberg sieve weights for sifting primes up to z, level D
selbergWeights :: Double -> Double -> [(Integer, Double)]
selbergWeights d z =
  let ds = [n | n <- [1..floor (sqrt d)], squarefree n, all (< z) (primeFactors n)]
      rho m = sum [omega n / fromIntegral n | n <- ds, m `divides` n]
      rho1  = rho 1
  in [(m, moebius m * rho m / rho1) | m <- ds]
```

### 4.3 The Parity Barrier

In the early 1970s Selberg identified a devastating limitation. Consider two sequences with *identical* local densities $\omega(p)$ — hence identical sieve main terms — but different global parity structure: for instance, the primes versus the products of two primes. Because the sieve main term depends only on $\omega$, and because the remainder terms in all known treatments are too weak to distinguish them, *no sieve can prove a lower bound for primes in a sequence unless the same argument would also "prove" it for the almost-prime counterpart* — which is false. This is the **parity problem** (or parity barrier): sieve weights are *parity-blind*.

> **Theorem:** *(Selberg's parity barrier, informal).* Let $\mathcal{A}^+$ and $\mathcal{A}^-$ be sequences with the same density function $\omega$ and comparably small remainders, where $\mathcal{A}^+$ is supported on integers with an even number of prime factors and $\mathcal{A}^-$ on integers with an odd number. Then any sieve lower bound valid for one is valid for the other. Consequently, sieves alone cannot detect primes: the twin prime conjecture and the Goldbach conjecture are inaccessible to pure sieve methods.

The barrier is *sharp*: Bombieri constructed an explicit pair of sequences for which a sieve lower bound for primes would imply a false statement about almost-primes, proving the obstruction is real and not merely a weakness of current technique [1]. Since 1975, sieve theory has largely meant *circumventing* the barrier rather than breaking it:

- **Chen's theorem (1973):** prove the lower bound for $P_2$ (numbers with at most two prime factors) instead of primes — working *with* the parity ambiguity via weighted sieves.
- **The asymptotic sieve of Bombieri, Friedlander, and Iwaniec:** exploit *bilinear structure* in the remainder to extract an asymptotic formula for primes in special sequences (e.g., infinitely many primes of the form $x^2 + y^4$ [1]), where the sequence itself carries enough algebraic structure to defeat the parity obstruction.
- **The GPY–Zhang–Maynard program:** sidestep parity by asking a *weaker* question — not "are there infinitely many prime pairs?" but "are prime gaps bounded?" — which turns out to be just barely on the accessible side of the barrier.

### 4.4 Level of Distribution and the Bombieri–Vinogradov Theorem

The *level of distribution* $\theta$ of the primes is the largest exponent such that

$$\sum_{q \le x^{\theta}} \max_{(a,q)=1} \Bigl|\pi(x; q, a) - \frac{\pi(x)}{\varphi(q)}\Bigr| \ll_A \frac{x}{(\log x)^A}$$

for all $A$. The Bombieri–Vinogradov theorem establishes $\theta = 1/2$ for *any* $\theta < 1/2$ — the primes are equidistributed in arithmetic progressions on average up to modulus $x^{1/2-\varepsilon}$ [2][4]. This is precisely the input the remainder term of a sieve needs: it controls $\sum_{d < D} |r_d|$ with $D = x^{\theta}$, and hence the admissible sifting ratio $s$.

The Elliott–Halberstam conjecture asserts $\theta = 1$ (any $\theta < 1$), which would follow from the Generalized Riemann Hypothesis for Dirichlet $L$-functions but is far beyond current technology. The GPY method showed that *any* $\theta > 1/2$ implies bounded gaps between primes; the entire Zhang–Polymath program is therefore a battle to push $\theta$ infinitesimally past $1/2$. Zhang's breakthrough was a refinement of the Bombieri–Vinogradov machinery valid for *smooth* moduli (moduli with no large prime factor), achieving $\theta = 1/2 + 1/584$ [2]; Polymath8a optimized every component — Deligne-type estimates for hyper-Kloosterman sums, the $q$-van der Corput method, dense divisibility of moduli — to reach $\theta = 1/2 + 7/300$ and $H_1 \le 4680$ [4].

| Result | Level $\theta$ | Gap bound $H_1$ | Method |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bombieri–Vinogradov | $1/2$ | $\infty$ (GPY: $o(\log x)$ gaps) | Large sieve |
| Zhang (2013) | $1/2 + 1/584$ | $70\,000\,000$ | Smooth-modulus dispersion |
| Polymath8a (2014) | $1/2 + 7/300$ | $4680$ | Optimized Zhang + new exponential sums |
| Maynard (2015) | $1/2$ (BV only) | $600$ | Multidimensional Selberg weights |
| Polymath8b (2014) | $1/2$ (BV only) | $246$ | Maynard–Tao sieve, optimized |
| Conditional on EH | $1$ | $12$ | GPY with full level |
| Conditional on GEH | $1$ | $6$ (optimal for sieves) | Maynard–Tao at full level |

Notably, the Maynard–Tao advance [3][5] *retreated* to $\theta = 1/2$: a multidimensional Selberg-type weight $F(t_1,\dots,t_k)$ on the simplex makes the prime-detection ratio arbitrarily large as $k\to\infty$, yielding bounded gaps from the classical Bombieri–Vinogradov theorem alone.

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

The modern theory crystallizes in a sequence of theorems, each a direct corollary of the sieve machinery above.

> **Theorem:** *(Goldston–Pintz–Yıldırım, 2009).* $\displaystyle \liminf_{n\to\infty} \frac{p_{n+1}-p_n}{\log p_n} = 0.$ Small gaps occur at sub-average scale; with $\theta > 1/2$ the method yields $\liminf (p_{n+1}-p_n) < \infty$ [6].

> **Theorem:** *(Zhang, 2014).* $\displaystyle \liminf_{n\to\infty} (p_{n+1}-p_n) < 7\times 10^7.$ The proof refines Goldston–Pintz–Yıldırım with a stronger Bombieri–Vinogradov theorem for smooth moduli [2].

> **Theorem:** *(Maynard, 2015; Tao, independently).* For admissible $k$-tuples with $k$ large, there are infinitely many $n$ for which at least $m$ of $n+h_1,\dots,n+h_k$ are prime, with $m \to \infty$ as $k \to \infty$. In particular $\liminf (p_{n+1}-p_n) \le 600$, and $\liminf (p_{n+m}-p_n) < \infty$ for every fixed $m$ [3].

> **Theorem:** *(Polymath8b, 2014).* $\displaystyle \liminf_{n\to\infty}(p_{n+1}-p_n) \le 246,$ using only the classical Bombieri–Vinogradov theorem; $H_1 \le 6$ under the generalized Elliott–Halberstam conjecture, which is the limit of the method [5].

The *admissibility* condition is the combinatorial heart of the $k$-tuple setup: a set $\mathcal{H} = \{h_1,\dots,h_k\}$ is admissible if it does not cover all residue classes modulo any prime $p$. The narrowest admissible $k$-tuples (e.g., diameter $246$ at $k=50$) were found by numerical search. The admissibility test in Rust:

```rust
fn is_admissible(h: &[u64]) -> bool {
    // check primes up to k: H must miss at least one residue class mod p
    for p in primes_upto(h.len() as u64 + 1) {
        let covered: std::collections::HashSet<u64> =
            h.iter().map(|x| x % p).collect();
        if covered.len() as u64 == p {
            return false; // covers all classes mod p: forbidden
        }
    }
    true
}
```

![Timeline of the bounded prime gaps program: Zhang, Polymath8a, Maynard, Polymath8b, and conditional limits](public/thesis/ths_1789198146000_04286-3.webp)

Chronology of the bound $H_1 = \liminf (p_{n+1}-p_n)$:

1. **2013, Zhang:** $H_1 \le 70\,000\,000$ — first finite bound in history [2].
2. **2014, Polymath8a:** $H_1 \le 4680$ — optimization of Zhang's argument, level $1/2 + 7/300$ [4].
3. **2014, Maynard:** $H_1 \le 600$ — new multidimensional sieve weights, BV only [3].
4. **2014, Polymath8b:** $H_1 \le 246$ — optimized Maynard–Tao sieve; current unconditional record [5].
5. **Conditional:** $H_1 \le 12$ under Elliott–Halberstam; $H_1 \le 6$ under its generalized form — the provable limit of sieve methods [5].

A 2026 preprint claims $H_1 \le 240$ via new smooth-modulus estimates, though it has not completed peer review at the time of writing [7].

## 6 Limitations

The sieve program, for all its triumphs, faces principled limitations:

- **The parity barrier is absolute for pure sieves.** As formulated in §4.3, no refinement of weights, no larger sieve level, and no cleverer truncation can make a sieve detect primes directly; the obstruction is *information-theoretic* relative to the data sieves use [1]. Breaking it requires genuinely new arithmetic input (bilinear structure, algebraic geometry) beyond the sieve axioms.
- **The level of distribution is stuck at $1/2$.** The Bombieri–Vinogradov theorem's $\theta = 1/2$ has not been improved for general moduli in sixty years; every advance past $1/2$ exploits *special* moduli (smooth, densely divisible). The Elliott–Halberstam conjecture ($\theta = 1$) remains a conjecture, and the twin prime conjecture needs far more than even EH provides.
- **The $H_1 \le 6$ wall.** Polymath8b proved that $6$ is the smallest gap bound obtainable by the Maynard–Tao sieve even at full level of distribution [5]; going below $6$ — let alone to the twin prime conjecture's $2$ — requires ideas outside the current sieve framework.
- **Dimension dependence.** Higher-dimensional sieves ($\kappa > 1$) remain substantially less developed, constraining applications to polynomial sequences.

## 7 Conclusion

Sieve methods transformed a classical crossing-out procedure into a deep analytic machinery. Brun's truncation taught us that *partial* inclusion–exclusion suffices; Selberg's quadratic form taught us that *optimal* weights are computable in closed form; the fundamental lemma taught us that sieves, at sufficient sifting ratio, mimic probabilistic heuristics; and the parity barrier taught us exactly where the method must stop — a rare case in mathematics where the *limits* of a technique are themselves a theorem. The bounded-gaps program of 2013–2014 is the crowning demonstration of what lies on the accessible side of that barrier: from Zhang's $70$ million to Polymath8b's $246$, each improvement was a triumph of optimizing within known constraints rather than transcending them. The twin prime conjecture remains beyond the wall — but the sieve, as developed in [1] and deployed in [2][3][4][5][6], will be part of whatever machinery finally breaches it.
