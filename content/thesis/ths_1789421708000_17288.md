# Dislocation Theory in Crystalline Solids: Burgers Vectors, the Peierls–Nabarro Stress, and Strain-Gradient Plasticity

## Abstract

Dislocations are the elementary carriers of plastic deformation in crystalline solids, yet turning that picture into a predictive theory of strength has occupied nearly a century of physics, mathematics, and mechanics. This thesis develops dislocation theory from first principles: the Volterra construction and the Burgers vector as a topological invariant of the crystal lattice; the elastic stress fields of screw and edge dislocations and the Peach–Koehler configurational force; the Peierls–Nabarro model, which replaces the singular dislocation core with a disregistry field and predicts a finite lattice resistance — the Peierls stress — as the stress required to translate the core by one atomic spacing; and the modern synthesis of strain-gradient plasticity, in which geometrically necessary dislocations and an intrinsic material length scale explain the size-dependent strengthening observed in torsion, bending, and indentation at the micron scale. We derive the central formulas, confront the theory with experiment and density-functional calculations, and delineate precisely where the classical picture breaks down.

## 1 Introduction

The paradox of crystal strength — *theoretical* versus *observed* — is among the oldest quantitative embarrassments of materials physics. A perfect crystal sheared along a close-packed plane should resist with a stress of order *μ*/30, where *μ* is the shear modulus; real crystals yield at stresses three to four orders of magnitude smaller [4]. The resolution, proposed independently by Orowan, Polanyi, and Taylor in 1934, is that plasticity does not occur by the rigid sliding of entire atomic planes but by the propagation of line defects — **dislocations** — whose motion requires breaking only a handful of atomic bonds at a time [4]. Dislocation glide explains work hardening, the Bauschinger effect, creep, fatigue crack initiation, and the size-dependent strengthening of small structures.

Yet the dislocation is a peculiar object: a *topological* defect that is simultaneously an *elastic* singularity, an *atomistic* core structure, and a *dynamic* entity subject to drag and thermal activation.

This thesis is organized as an ascent through length scales. **Sections 2–5** climb from Burgers-vector kinematics through the Peierls–Nabarro core and dislocation line mechanics to strain-gradient plasticity; **Sections 6–7** confront the limitations and conclude.

> **Theorem: (Scope of the thesis).** *Every result below is stated in the language of continuum dislocation theory with atomistic input entering only through the generalized stacking-fault energy surface. No claim is made about core structures that require explicit electronic degrees of freedom.*

## 2 Kinematic Foundations: The Volterra Construction and the Burgers Vector

### The Volterra cut-and-weld procedure

Vito Volterra, in 1907, classified the ways a multiply connected elastic body can be deformed while keeping every material element locally stress-free. His construction is disarmingly simple: take a perfect crystal, make a cut along a half-plane, displace the two lips of the cut relative to one another by a fixed vector, and weld the material back together. The result is a line defect — a **Volterra dislocation** — running along the edge of the cut. Six elementary distortions are possible: three translations and three rotations of the lips. The translational ones are the *edge*, *screw*, and *mixed* dislocations of materials science; the rotational ones are *disclinations*, the dislocations' less famous cousins.

The construction makes one fact vivid: a dislocation is a property of the *global* lattice arrangement, not of any single atom — the signature of a topological defect, demanding a topological measure of its strength.

### The Burgers circuit

Consider a closed lattice walk encircling the line: in a perfect crystal it closes on itself, but around a dislocation it finishes one lattice spacing from its start. The vector from the finish to the start is the **Burgers vector** **b**. Formally, for any contour *L* enclosing the line with the line direction **ξ**,

```
b = ∮_L du
```

where **u** is the displacement field, and the integral is the total jump of displacement around the loop [3]. The Burgers vector is *conserved*: identical for every enclosing contour, unchanging along the line, and obeying a Kirchhoff-like node rule (**b₁ + b₂ = b₃**) at junctions.

An **edge** dislocation has **b** perpendicular to **ξ** (the classic extra half-plane of atoms); a **screw** dislocation has **b** parallel to **ξ** (the lattice becomes a helicoid, like a spiral parking ramp); a **mixed** dislocation interpolates between the two. The magnitude *b* is typically one lattice spacing — for instance, *a*/2 along the ⟨110⟩ direction in face-centered cubic metals [4] — and it quantizes plastic shear: when the line sweeps area *A*, the crystal shears by *bA*/*V*.

The following table summarizes the geometric characters:

| Character | Relation of **b** to **ξ** | Lattice picture | Glide plane constraint |
|---|---|---|---|
| Edge | **b** ⊥ **ξ** | Extra half-plane of atoms | Fixed: **b** × **ξ** plane |
| Screw | **b** ∥ **ξ** | Helical ramp of atoms | Any plane containing **b** |
| Mixed | Oblique | Combination | Fixed: **b** × **ξ** plane |

A compact way to internalize the Burgers circuit is to implement it. The following Haskell sketch computes the closure failure of a lattice walk around a simulated edge dislocation, illustrating that **b** is a property of the walk's topology, not of its detailed shape:

```haskell
-- Burgers circuit on a square lattice with an edge dislocation core at origin.
-- The extra half-plane occupies x == 0, y > 0; steps crossing it are sheared.
type Vec = (Int, Int)

shearStep :: Vec -> Vec -> Vec
shearStep (x, y) (dx, dy)
  | x == 0 && y >= 0 && dx > 0 = (x + dx, y + dy + 1)  -- slip across the cut
  | otherwise                  = (x + dx, y + dy)

burgersVector :: [Vec] -> Vec -> Vec
burgersVector steps start =
  let finish = foldl shearStep start steps
  in (fst start - fst finish, snd start - snd finish)  -- closure failure

-- Any closed walk encircling the core returns b = (0, -1): one lattice spacing.
circuit :: [Vec]
circuit = replicate 6 (1,0) ++ replicate 6 (0,1)
       ++ replicate 6 (-1,0) ++ replicate 6 (0,-1)
```

Varying the walk leaves the result unchanged: that invariance *is* the topological content of the Burgers vector [3].

## 3 The Peierls–Nabarro Model: Misfit Energy and the Peierls Stress

### The problem with the Volterra dislocation

The Volterra construction has an embarrassing feature: the elastic stress field of a dislocation diverges as 1/*r* at the core, and the elastic energy density diverges as 1/*r*². The continuum theory predicts infinite stresses where the defect actually lives. Peierls (1940) and Nabarro (1947) repaired this by treating the crystal as *two elastic half-spaces* joined across the slip plane, with atomic discreteness represented by a nonlinear **misfit energy** penalizing relative sliding [1][2].

### The model

Consider a straight edge dislocation along the *x*₂ axis, gliding in the *x*₁–*x*₃ slip plane, with Burgers vector *b* along *x*₁. Let *φ*(*x*₁) be the **disregistry** — the relative displacement of the upper half-crystal with respect to the lower one across the slip plane, with *φ* → 0 far to the left and *φ* → *b* far to the right; the core is the transition region [1].

The total energy is the sum of a long-range elastic part and a local misfit part:

```
E[φ] = E_elastic[φ] + E_misfit[φ]
```

with

```
E_misfit[φ] = ∫ W(φ(x)) dx,      W(φ) = (μ b² / 4π² d) · (1 − cos(2πφ/b))
```

where *W* is the sinusoidal **interplanar potential**, *μ* the shear modulus, and *d* the interplanar spacing [1][4]. The elastic energy is the standard linear-elastic energy of the displacement field *U* whose trace on the slip plane equals *φ* [2].

Minimizing *E* yields the celebrated Peierls–Nabarro disregistry profile,

```
φ(x) = (b/π) · arctan(x / ζ),       ζ = d / (2(1 − ν))   (edge)
```

an arctangent kink of **half-width** *ζ* interpolating between the two perfect registries. The core is no longer singular: it is spread over a width set by the competition between elastic stiffness (which favors a wide, gentle transition) and misfit energy (which favors a narrow one) [1].

### The Peierls stress

Now translate the entire profile rigidly by a fraction of a lattice spacing. The elastic energy is translation-invariant, but the misfit energy is not: the sinusoidal potential *W* presents a periodic landscape of **Peierls valleys** separated by barriers of atomic-scale period. The dislocation must climb these barriers to move, and the maximum slope of the misfit energy per unit length defines the **Peierls stress** — the intrinsic lattice resistance to dislocation motion at zero temperature [4]:

```
τ_P = (2μ / (1 − ν)) · exp(−2πζ / b)     (edge)
τ_P =  2μ · exp(−2πζ / b)                (screw, ζ = d/2)
```

Because the core half-width *ζ* is typically comparable to *b*, the Peierls stress is exponentially small — of order 10⁻⁴*μ* to 10⁻³*μ* in close-packed metals — yet exponentially *sensitive* to core width. Narrow cores (covalent crystals, BCC metals at low temperature) give enormous Peierls stresses and brittle behavior; wide cores (FCC metals, dissociated partials) give tiny ones and ductility [4].

> **Theorem: (Peierls–Nabarro barrier).** *For the sinusoidal misfit potential, the static equilibrium disregistry is the arctangent kink above, and the stress required for rigid translation of the core satisfies τ_P ∝ exp(−2πζ/b). In particular, τ_P → 0 as ζ/b → ∞: a sufficiently delocalized core experiences no lattice friction.*

Density-functional calculations of the generalized stacking-fault energy surface *γ*(**u**) confirm the qualitative picture while correcting the numbers [4]: the Peierls–Nabarro model remains the standard bridge between *ab initio* *γ*-surfaces and mesoscale yield-strength predictions [1][4].

A minimal numerical estimate is instructive:

```python
import math

def peierls_stress(mu, nu, d, b, character="edge"):
    """Peierls-Nabarro lattice resistance (isotropic estimate)."""
    if character == "edge":
        zeta = d / (2.0 * (1.0 - nu))
        return (2.0 * mu / (1.0 - nu)) * math.exp(-2.0 * math.pi * zeta / b)
    zeta = d / 2.0
    return 2.0 * mu * math.exp(-2.0 * math.pi * zeta / b)

# Copper-ish parameters: mu = 48 GPa, nu = 0.34, d/b ~ 0.8
mu, nu = 48e9, 0.34
for ch in ("screw", "edge"):
    tp = peierls_stress(mu, nu, 0.8, 1.0, ch)
    print(f"{ch:6s}: tau_P = {tp/1e6:8.2f} MPa  ({tp/mu:.2e} mu)")
```

## 4 Stress Fields, Line Mechanics, and Collective Behavior

### 4.1 The Peach–Koehler force and dislocation mobility

How does an applied stress push on a dislocation? The answer is the **Peach–Koehler formula**: a dislocation line with Burgers vector **b** and local tangent **ξ** in a stress field **σ** experiences a force per unit length

```
f = (σ · b) × ξ
```

directed perpendicular to the line, in the glide plane. A resolved shear stress *τ* on the slip system drives the line with *f* = *τb* per unit length, opposed by the Peierls barrier, phonon drag, and the dislocation forest [3].

The PN framework extends rigorously from statics to dynamics: the phase-field limit yields an evolution law for overdamped glide driven by the Peach–Koehler force [2].

### 4.2 Line energy, line tension, and the self-force

For a straight dislocation in an isotropic crystal, the elastic energy per unit length stored between a core cutoff *r*₀ and an outer radius *R* is

```
E = (μ b² / 4π) · ln(R / r₀)            (screw)
E = (μ b² / 4π(1 − ν)) · ln(R / r₀)     (edge)
```

The logarithm is characteristic: most of the energy lives far from the core, which is why dislocations interact over long ranges and why the line-tension approximation — treating the dislocation as an elastic string with tension *T* ≈ *αμb*², *α* of order unity — is so useful [3]. Line tension governs Orowan bowing between obstacles, loop stability, and the self-force straightening curved segments.

### 4.3 Stacking faults, partial dislocations, and dissociation

In close-packed crystals the perfect Burgers vector is not always the cheapest option. A dislocation with **b** of the a/2⟨110⟩ type in an FCC lattice can lower its elastic energy — which scales as *b*², Frank's rule — by **dissociating** into two Shockley partials of a/6⟨211⟩ type, bound together by a ribbon of **stacking fault** of energy *γ* per unit area. The equilibrium separation *d* ∝ *μb*²/*γ* balances the partials' mutual repulsion against the fault's surface tension [4]. The *γ*-surface computed from density-functional theory thus controls not only the Peierls stress of Section 3 but the entire topology of slip [4].

### 4.4 Forest hardening and the Taylor relation

Real crystals contain dislocation *populations*, not isolated lines. A gliding dislocation must cut through the **forest** of dislocations threading its slip plane; each intersection creates a jog and costs energy. Taylor (1934) argued that the flow stress should scale with the square root of the total dislocation density *ρ*,

```
τ = α μ b √ρ,
```

with *α* a dimensionless constant of order 0.2–0.5 encoding the average obstacle strength [5]. This **Taylor relation** is among the most robust laws in metallurgy, and the microscopic anchor of the strain-gradient theories in Section 5 [5][7].

## 5 Strain-Gradient Plasticity: Geometrically Necessary Dislocations and Intrinsic Length Scales

### The failure of classical plasticity at small scales

Classical continuum plasticity has no length scale: its constitutive laws relate stress to strain and strain history alone. Dimensional analysis then predicts that a geometrically self-similar experiment — say, indenting with a cone of fixed angle — should give the same hardness at every depth. Experiment disagrees spectacularly: micro- and nano-indentation hardness *increases* as the indent shrinks, thin wires strengthen in torsion as their diameter falls, and thin beams strengthen in bending [6][7]. These size effects live at the micron scale — precisely where the dislocation spacing becomes comparable to the specimen dimensions [6].

### Geometrically necessary dislocations

The missing physics is the **geometrically necessary dislocation** (GND). Where plastic strain varies in space — in bending, torsion, or under an indenter — the lattice curves to accommodate the gradient, requiring extra half-planes of atoms: GNDs whose density is fixed by the deformation geometry.

```
ρ_G = ηᵖ / b,
```

where *η*ᵖ is the effective plastic strain gradient [5][7]. Inserting *ρ* = *ρ_S* + *ρ_G* into the Taylor relation yields a flow stress that *grows* as gradients steepen — the observed size effect [5].

MSG theory derives this from a multiscale argument linking the Taylor law at the microscale to the strain gradient at the mesoscale via an intrinsic length *l* of a few microns [5][7]. The Nix–Gao indentation law,

```
H / H₀ = √(1 + h* / h),
```

with *h** proportional to *l*, reproduces the measured depth dependence of hardness with no adjustable physics beyond the dislocation picture [7].

### Phenomenological and higher-order formulations

Parallel to MSG, Fleck and Hutchinson built a **phenomenological** strain-gradient theory on couple-stress foundations: the effective stress Σ combines the conventional von Mises stress with the couple stress *m* scaled by a material length, Σ² = σₑ² + *l*⁻²*m*ₑ² [6][7]. This version successfully rationalizes the strengthening of thin wires in torsion (with *l* ≈ 3 μm for copper) and thin beams in bending (with *l* ≈ 5 μm for nickel) [7]. Conventional mechanism-based theories (CMSG) removed the higher-order stresses while retaining gradient effects, enabling practical finite-element implementation [6].

The GND density required to accommodate a prescribed lattice curvature — the kinematic heart of every strain-gradient theory — is readily computed:

```rust
/// Geometrically necessary dislocation density from lattice curvature.
/// kappa: curvature tensor magnitude (1/length); b: Burgers vector magnitude.
fn gnd_density(kappa: f64, b: f64) -> f64 {
    // Nye's relation in scalar form: rho_G = kappa / b
    kappa / b
}

fn main() {
    let b = 0.286e-9; // Burgers vector of Al, meters
    // Bending a 10-micron foil to 1% surface strain needs curvature ~ 2e3 m^-1
    let kappa = 2.0e3;
    let rho_g = gnd_density(kappa, b);
    println!("rho_G = {:.2e} m^-2", rho_g); // ~7e12 m^-2: comparable to heavily-worked SSD densities
}
```

## 6 Limitations, Open Problems, and Frontiers

No layer of this edifice is complete. The principal gaps:

1. **The core problem persists.** The Peierls–Nabarro model smears the core but retains the sinusoidal misfit guess; real *γ*-surfaces are anisotropic and multi-welled, and phenomena such as core reconstruction in BCC metals lie entirely outside the PN picture [4].

2. **Cross-slip and climb are bolted on.** The Peach–Koehler force describes glide; thermally activated cross-slip of screw dislocations and vacancy-mediated climb require separate kinetic laws with their own activation parameters. A unified mobility law spanning glide, cross-slip, and climb does not exist [3].

3. **The Taylor relation is mean-field.** Forest hardening treats obstacles as a homogeneous density; real dislocation networks form cells, walls, and avalanches with long-range correlations. Discrete dislocation dynamics captures this at the cost of losing the analytic simplicity that makes the Taylor law useful [5].

4. **Strain-gradient theories proliferate.** Phenomenological, mechanism-based, higher-order, and conventional variants make different predictions for boundary layers and passivation effects, and the extra boundary conditions of higher-order theories remain physically ambiguous [6][8]. The community has not converged on a canonical formulation.

5. **Length scales are not constants.** The intrinsic length *l* extracted from torsion differs from that extracted from indentation; it evolves with deformation and depends on grain size, temperature, and loading mode [7].

These are not defects of the exposition but of the science — and they define the research frontier: concurrent atomistic–continuum coupling for cores, data-driven mobility laws, and gradient theories with evolving length scales [2][8].

## 7 Conclusion

From Volterra's cut to the strain gradient, dislocation theory is a study in how a single topological idea ramifies across scales. The **Burgers vector** gives the kinematics: a conserved lattice invariant that quantizes plastic shear. The **Peierls–Nabarro model** gives the core: a smooth disregistry field whose translation costs the **Peierls stress**, exponentially small in the core width, explaining why close-packed metals are soft and covalent crystals brittle. **Line mechanics** — the Peach–Koehler force, line tension, partial dissociation, Taylor forest hardening — gives the mesoscale rules of engagement. And **strain-gradient plasticity** closes the loop to the macroscale: where plastic strain varies rapidly, geometrically necessary dislocations accumulate, an intrinsic length scale enters the constitutive law, and small specimens strengthen exactly as observed [1][2][3][4][5][6][7][8].

The theory remains gloriously unfinished. Every quantitative prediction still leans on parameters — core widths, *γ*-surfaces, drag coefficients, length scales — that must be measured or computed elsewhere. Taylor's 1934 moral stands: *crystals are weak because their defects are mobile, and understanding that mobility, defect by defect and scale by scale, is understanding strength itself.*
