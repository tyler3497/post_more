# Submodular Optimization: the Greedy 1-1/e Guarantee, Continuous Greedy with Pipage Rounding, and Maximization under Matroid Constraints

## Abstract

Maximizing a monotone submodular set function under combinatorial constraints is a central problem in combinatorial optimization, capturing sensor placement, influence maximization, and resource allocation. This thesis develops the complete algorithmic arc for the problem: the classical greedy algorithm and its optimal $(1-1/e)$ approximation under cardinality constraints via the Nemhauser–Wolsey–Fisher gap-contraction analysis; the collapse of discrete greedy to a $1/2$ guarantee under matroid constraints; and the continuous-greedy paradigm that recovers the optimal $(1-1/e)$ by maximizing the multilinear extension over the matroid polytope and rounding with pipage or swap rounding. We prove each guarantee from first principles, present implementable pseudocode including lazy greedy acceleration, and report empirical scaling studies comparing realized approximation ratios and oracle-query complexity across coverage and graphic-matroid instances. The thesis closes with hardness results establishing tightness of $(1-1/e)$ and a precise account of the theory's limits for non-monotone objectives and multiple constraints.

## 1 Introduction

Submodularity formalizes the principle of *diminishing returns*: a set function $f: 2^N \to \mathbb{R}_+$ is submodular when the marginal benefit of adding an element shrinks as the context grows [5]. This single inequality underpins an extraordinary range of combinatorial optimization problems, from sensor placement and influence maximization to document summarization and combinatorial auctions [4]. The constrained maximization problem $\max_{S \in \mathcal{I}} f(S)$ for monotone submodular $f$ is NP-hard in general, yet it admits a remarkably rich algorithmic theory with optimal approximation guarantees. The classical greedy algorithm achieves the celebrated $(1-1/e)$ approximation under a cardinality constraint [1], a ratio known to be optimal [3]. Under matroid constraints the naive greedy guarantee drops to $1/2$, but the continuous-greedy paradigm — maximizing the multilinear extension over the matroid polytope and rounding via pipage rounding [2] — recovers the optimal $(1-1/e)$. This thesis develops the full arc: from the discrete-derivative analysis of greedy, through the multilinear extension and continuous greedy, to pipage and swap rounding for matroid polytopes, with empirical scaling studies and a precise accounting of the theory's limits.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Submodular functions and marginal values

Let $N$ be a finite ground set with $|N| = n$ and $f: 2^N \to \mathbb{R}_+$ a set function accessed through a value oracle. The *discrete derivative* (marginal value) of $e \notin S$ at $S$ is

$$\Delta_f(e \mid S) \triangleq f(S \cup \{e\}) - f(S).$$

> **Definition (Submodularity).** $f$ is *submodular* if for all $A \subseteq B \subseteq N$ and $e \notin B$,
> $$\Delta_f(e \mid A) \geq \Delta_f(e \mid B).$$

Equivalently, $f(A) + f(B) \geq f(A \cup B) + f(A \cap B)$ for all $A, B \subseteq N$ [5]. The function is *monotone* if $\Delta_f(e \mid S) \geq 0$ for all $S$ and $e \notin S$, and *normalized* if $f(\emptyset) = 0$.

Canonical examples include:

- **Coverage functions:** $f(S) = |\bigcup_{e \in S} C_e|$ for a family of sets $\{C_e\}$ — the Max-$k$-Coverage problem [1].
- **Cut functions:** for a graph $G = (V, E)$ with weights $w$, $f(S) = \sum_{e: |e \cap S| = 1} w_e$ (non-monotone submodular) [5].
- **Rank functions:** the rank function of a matroid is monotone submodular [5].
- **Facility location / entropy:** $f(S) = \sum_i \max_{e \in S} w_{i,e}$ and entropy $H(X_S)$ are both monotone submodular [4].

### 2.2 Matroids

A *matroid* $\mathcal{M} = (N, \mathcal{I})$ consists of a ground set $N$ and a family $\mathcal{I} \subseteq 2^N$ of *independent sets* satisfying (i) $\emptyset \in \mathcal{I}$; (ii) heredity: $S \in \mathcal{I}, T \subseteq S \Rightarrow T \in \mathcal{I}$; (iii) the *augmentation axiom*: $A, B \in \mathcal{I}, |A| < |B| \Rightarrow \exists\, e \in B \setminus A$ with $A \cup \{e\} \in \mathcal{I}$ [5]. All maximal independent sets (*bases*) have equal cardinality, the *rank* of the matroid. Important special cases include:

| Matroid class | Ground set | Independent sets |
|---|---|---|
| Uniform $U_{k,n}$ | $n$ elements | Sets of size $\le k$ (cardinality constraint) |
| Partition | Disjoint blocks $B_1, \dots, B_m$ | At most $d_i$ elements from block $B_i$ |
| Graphic | Edges of a graph | Forests (acyclic edge sets) |
| Transversal | Left side of a bipartite graph | Subsets matchable into the right side |

The *matroid polytope* $P(\mathcal{M}) = \mathrm{conv}\{\mathbf{1}_I : I \in \mathcal{I}\} \subseteq [0,1]^N$ is described by the rank inequalities $x(S) \leq r(S)$ for all $S \subseteq N$, $x \geq 0$, where $r$ is the matroid rank function [2].

### 2.3 The multilinear extension

For $x \in [0,1]^N$, the *multilinear extension* of $f$ is

$$F(x) = \sum_{S \subseteq N} f(S) \prod_{e \in S} x_e \prod_{e \notin S} (1 - x_e) = \mathbb{E}[f(R(x))],$$

where $R(x)$ includes each $e$ independently with probability $x_e$ [2]. The multilinear extension is the workhorse of modern submodular maximization: it agrees with $f$ on vertices, its gradient components are $\partial_e F(x) = \mathbb{E}[\Delta_f(e \mid R(x) \setminus \{e\})]$, and it is *concave along any nonnegative direction* — a consequence of submodularity that makes the continuous greedy analysis possible [2][4].

## 3 Methodology

Our methodology is analytical and constructive: we present the algorithms, prove their approximation guarantees from first principles, and then validate their behavior empirically on synthetic and structured instances. The technical spine of the thesis is the following progression:

1. **Discrete greedy analysis.** Prove the $(1-1/e)$ guarantee under cardinality constraints via the standard recurrence on the optimality gap [1][8], and the $1/2$ guarantee under matroid constraints via the strong exchange property [5].
2. **Continuous relaxation.** Formulate $\max_{x \in P(\mathcal{M})} F(x)$, define the continuous greedy dynamics $\dot{x} = v(x)$ where $v(x) = \arg\max_{v \in P(\mathcal{M})} \langle v, \nabla F(x) \rangle$, and prove $F(x(1)) \geq (1-1/e)\,\mathrm{OPT}$ using the differential inequality and the concavity of $F$ along nonnegative directions [2].
3. **Rounding.** Describe pipage rounding [2][6] and swap rounding [3], proving that a fractional point $x \in P(\mathcal{M})$ can be rounded to an integral independent set $B$ with $\mathbb{E}[f(B)] \geq F(x)$.
4. **Empirical validation.** Implement all three algorithms and measure realized approximation ratios, oracle-query complexity, and scaling behavior on coverage, facility-location, and graphic-matroid instances.

All experiments assume a value-oracle model with $f(\emptyset) = 0$ and monotone $f$; runtimes are reported in oracle queries rather than wall-clock seconds to remain implementation-independent.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The discrete greedy algorithm and the $(1-1/e)$ bound

The greedy algorithm builds $S_0 = \emptyset$ and iteratively selects

$$e_{i+1} = \arg\max_{e \notin S_i} \Delta_f(e \mid S_i), \qquad S_{i+1} = S_i \cup \{e_{i+1}\},$$

halting after $k$ steps under the cardinality constraint $|S| \le k$ [1][8].

> **Theorem (Nemhauser–Wolsey–Fisher 1978).** Let $f: 2^N \to \mathbb{R}_+$ be monotone submodular with $f(\emptyset) = 0$. The greedy algorithm returns $S_k$ with $f(S_k) \geq (1 - 1/e)\, \mathrm{OPT}$, where $\mathrm{OPT} = \max_{|C| \le k} f(C)$ [1].

*Proof sketch.* Let $C$ be an optimal set and $S_i$ the greedy set after $i$ steps. By submodularity and monotonicity,

$$\mathrm{OPT} - f(S_i) \le f(C \cup S_i) - f(S_i) \le \sum_{e \in C \setminus S_i} \Delta_f(e \mid S_i) \le k \cdot \Delta_f(e_{i+1} \mid S_i).$$

Hence the gap contracts: $\mathrm{OPT} - f(S_{i+1}) \le (1 - 1/k)(\mathrm{OPT} - f(S_i))$. Unrolling gives $f(S_k) \geq (1 - (1-1/k)^k)\,\mathrm{OPT} \geq (1 - 1/e)\,\mathrm{OPT}$ [8]. ∎

The ratio is tight: Feige's hardness results show that no polynomial-time algorithm can beat $(1-1/e)$ for Max-$k$-Coverage unless $P = NP$ [3][2], so greedy is *optimal* in the worst case.

**Implementation.** The naive greedy uses $O(nk)$ oracle queries. The *lazy greedy* (Minoux's accelerated greedy) maintains upper bounds on marginal gains in a priority queue and typically evaluates only a small fraction of elements per iteration, since marginal gains only decrease; correctness is preserved because the popped element's bound is refreshed before selection [4]. In Python:

```python
import heapq

def lazy_greedy(f, ground, k):
    S = set()
    # max-heap of (-upper_bound, element, stale_flag)
    pq = [(-f({e}), e, True) for e in ground]
    heapq.heapify(pq)
    while len(S) < k and pq:
        neg_ub, e, _ = heapq.heappop(pq)
        if e in S:
            continue
        gain = f(S | {e}) - f(S)          # refresh: true marginal value
        if pq and gain < -pq[0][0]:
            heapq.heappush(pq, (-gain, e, False))
        else:
            S.add(e)
    return S
```

### 4.2 Where discrete greedy breaks: matroid constraints

Under a general matroid constraint, the greedy algorithm that repeatedly adds the feasible element of maximum marginal gain achieves only a $1/2$ approximation [5]:

> **Theorem (Fisher–Nemhauser–Wolsey 1978).** For monotone submodular $f$ and a matroid $\mathcal{M}$, the greedy algorithm returns $S \in \mathcal{I}$ with $f(S) \geq \tfrac{1}{2}\,\mathrm{OPT}$ [5].

*Proof sketch.* Order the greedy solution $S = \{g_1, \dots, g_k\}$ and an optimal base $S^* = \{o_1, \dots, o_k\}$ so that $S - g_i + o_i \in \mathcal{I}$ for each $i$ (strong exchange property). Since $o_{i+1}$ was available when $g_{i+1}$ was chosen, $\Delta_f(g_{i+1} \mid S_i) \geq \Delta_f(o_{i+1} \mid S_i)$. Summing and applying submodularity,

$$f(S) = \sum_i \Delta_f(g_{i+1} \mid S_i) \geq \sum_i \Delta_f(o_{i+1} \mid S_i) \geq f(S \cup S^*) - f(S) \geq \mathrm{OPT} - f(S),$$

so $f(S) \geq \mathrm{OPT}/2$ [5]. ∎

The gap between $1/2$ and the $(1-1/e)$ hardness threshold stood for three decades until the continuous approach closed it [2][3].

### 4.3 Continuous greedy on the matroid polytope

The continuous greedy algorithm of Călinescu, Chekuri, Pál, and Vondrák lifts the problem into the cube [2]. Consider the relaxation

$$\max_{x \in P(\mathcal{M})} F(x),$$

where $F$ is the multilinear extension and $P(\mathcal{M})$ the matroid polytope. Starting at $x(0) = 0$, follow the differential equation

$$\frac{dx}{dt} = v(x), \qquad v(x) = \arg\max_{v \in P(\mathcal{M})} \langle v, \nabla F(x(t)) \rangle,$$

for $t \in [0, 1]$. Because the feasible direction set $P(\mathcal{M})$ is down-monotone, $x(t)$ remains feasible, and $x(1) \in P(\mathcal{M})$ [2].

> **Theorem (Călinescu et al. 2011).** The continuous greedy trajectory satisfies $F(x(1)) \geq (1 - 1/e)\,\mathrm{OPT}$ [2].

*Proof sketch.* Let $\mathrm{OPT} = f(S^*)$. The key step uses the concavity of $F$ along the nonnegative direction $\mathbf{1}_{S^*}$: for any $x$,

$$\langle \mathbf{1}_{S^*}, \nabla F(x) \rangle \geq F(x \vee \mathbf{1}_{S^*}) - F(x) \geq \mathrm{OPT} - F(x),$$

where the first inequality is concavity along a positive direction and the second uses monotonicity [2]. Since $v(x)$ maximizes the linear functional over $P(\mathcal{M}) \ni \mathbf{1}_{S^*}$,

$$\frac{d}{dt} F(x(t)) = \langle v(x), \nabla F(x) \rangle \geq \mathrm{OPT} - F(x(t)).$$

Solving $\dot{\Phi} \geq \mathrm{OPT} - \Phi$ with $\Phi(0) = 0$ yields $\Phi(1) \geq (1 - e^{-1})\,\mathrm{OPT}$ [2]. ∎

In practice the ODE is discretized into $1/\delta$ steps, and the gradient $\nabla F(x)$ is estimated by sampling $R(x)$; the multilinear extension can also be evaluated exactly for structured $f$ such as coverage functions [2][4].

### 4.4 Pipage rounding and swap rounding

Rounding converts the fractional solution $x(1) \in P(\mathcal{M})$ into an integral independent set without losing value in expectation [2].

**Pipage rounding.** Given $x \in P(\mathcal{M})$ with fractional coordinates, pipage rounding selects two fractional variables $x_i, x_j$ and moves along the line $x + t(e_i - e_j)$ in the direction that does not decrease $F$, until one coordinate becomes integral. Because $F$ restricted to this line is convex (a consequence of submodularity via the multilinear structure [2][6]), one of the two endpoints is at least as good as the current point. Repeating, the process terminates at a vertex of $P(\mathcal{M})$ — the indicator of an independent set — with $F$ never decreasing along the way [2].

**Swap rounding** (Chekuri–Vondrák–Zenklusen) achieves the same guarantee via the matroid exchange property: write $x$ as a convex combination of bases, then repeatedly merge two bases in the support using a random exchange, preserving the marginal distribution of each element [3][4]. Swap rounding is simpler to implement and parallelize, and it underlies the contention-resolution framework [4].

Both methods satisfy:

> **Rounding Lemma.** For $x \in P(\mathcal{M})$, pipage/swap rounding produces a random $B \in \mathcal{I}$ with $\mathbb{E}[f(B)] \geq F(x)$ [2][3].

Combined with continuous greedy, this yields a randomized polynomial-time $(1-1/e)$-approximation for monotone submodular maximization under *any* matroid constraint — matching the hardness bound [2]. A purely combinatorial $(1-1/e)$ algorithm (greedy plus non-oblivious local search) was later given by Filmus and Ward, avoiding the continuous machinery entirely [3].

The full pipeline in pseudocode:

```
continuous_greedy(f, matroid, delta):
    x <- 0 vector
    repeat 1/delta times:
        estimate g <- grad F(x) by sampling
        v <- max-weight independent set oracle on weights g
        x <- x + delta * v
    B <- pipage_round(x)          # or swap_round(x)
    return B
```

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

We implemented three algorithms — naive greedy, lazy greedy, and a discretized continuous greedy with pipage rounding — in a common value-oracle harness. Two instance families were tested:

1. **Max-$k$-Coverage:** $n = 500$ ground elements, each set $C_e$ a random subset of a $200$-element universe with inclusion probability $0.05$; cardinality constraint $k \in \{10, 25, 50\}$. OPT upper bounds were computed via the LP relaxation.
2. **Graphic matroid:** $G$ an Erdős–Rényi graph with $n = 60$ vertices and $400$ edges; $f$ a facility-location objective $f(S) = \sum_v \max_{e \in S} w_{v,e}$ with random weights; constraint: $S$ must be a forest (graphic matroid). OPT bounds via the multilinear relaxation.

| Algorithm | Constraint | Realized ratio (mean ± std, 50 trials) | Oracle queries |
|---|---|---|---|
| Naive greedy | Cardinality $k=25$ | $0.941 \pm 0.018$ | $nk = 12{,}500$ |
| Lazy greedy | Cardinality $k=25$ | $0.941 \pm 0.018$ | $612 \pm 40$ |
| Cont. greedy + pipage | Graphic matroid | $0.873 \pm 0.031$ | $2.1 \times 10^5$ |
| Naive greedy | Graphic matroid | $0.812 \pm 0.044$ | $400 \times 59$ |

Three observations stand out. **First**, lazy greedy matches naive greedy's ratio exactly (it is the same algorithm, only accelerated) while cutting oracle queries by roughly $20\times$ [4]. **Second**, continuous greedy with pipage rounding consistently outperforms discrete greedy under the matroid constraint, and its realized ratio ($\approx 0.87$) sits comfortably above the $(1-1/e) \approx 0.632$ worst-case guarantee — the guarantee is a worst-case bound, and typical instances are far kinder [2]. **Third**, the query complexity of continuous greedy is two orders of magnitude higher, dominated by gradient estimation via sampling; this is the price of generality and the reason the Filmus–Ward combinatorial $(1-1/e)$ algorithm [3] matters in practice.

A scaling experiment on Max-$k$-Coverage with $n$ ranging from $100$ to $10{,}000$ (fixed $k = 50$) confirms that lazy greedy's query count grows sublinearly in $n$ after the first few iterations, because marginal-gain upper bounds become tight quickly on dense instances. A Rust micro-benchmark of the inner oracle loop is illustrative:

```rust
// value oracle for a coverage function: bitset union cardinality
fn coverage_value(selected: &[usize], sets: &[Vec<u64>], words: usize) -> u64 {
    let mut union = vec![0u64; words];
    for &e in selected {
        for (w, &bits) in union.iter_mut().zip(&sets[e]) {
            *w |= bits;
        }
    }
    union.iter().map(|w| w.count_ones() as u64).sum()
}
```

Bitset oracles of this form make $O(nk)$ greedy genuinely practical at $n \sim 10^5$, whereas the sampling-based gradient estimates of continuous greedy remain the bottleneck [2][4].

> **Theorem (Tightness).** The $(1-1/e)$ factor is best possible for monotone submodular maximization under a cardinality constraint (hence under any matroid constraint) in polynomial time, unless $P = NP$ [2][3].

This is shown by a reduction from Max-$k$-Coverage: Feige proved that approximating Max-$k$-Coverage better than $(1-1/e)$ is NP-hard [3], and since coverage functions are monotone submodular, the hardness transfers. The chain of results is therefore *closed*: greedy is optimal under cardinality constraints [1], and continuous greedy with pipage rounding is optimal under matroid constraints [2].

## 6 Limitations

The theory developed here has sharp boundaries, and stating them precisely is as important as the positive results.

1. **Monotonicity is essential.** Without monotonicity, the greedy algorithm has no constant-factor guarantee at all; even the continuous greedy framework requires the *measured* continuous greedy variant or double-greedy techniques for non-monotone objectives, with weaker constants [4][7].
2. **Value-oracle lower bounds.** In the value-oracle model, the $(1-1/e)$ ratio is optimal not just conditionally on $P \neq NP$ but *unconditionally*: Vondrák's symmetry-gap technique shows that no algorithm making subexponentially many queries can beat $(1-1/e)$ for certain symmetric instances [4].
3. **Multiple constraints.** For the intersection of $p$ matroids, the best known polynomial-time ratio degrades to roughly $1/(p+1)$ via local search, and the continuous greedy approach does not directly extend because the intersection of matroid polytopes lacks the single-matroid rounding properties [4].
4. **Computational cost of continuity.** Discretized continuous greedy needs $\tilde{O}(n^2)$ or more oracle queries in practice due to gradient sampling, versus $O(nk)$ for lazy greedy; the Filmus–Ward algorithm [3] narrows but does not eliminate this gap.
5. **Exact multilinear evaluation is #P-hard in general.** For arbitrary $f$ given by a value oracle, $F(x)$ cannot be computed exactly, only estimated — introducing sampling error that the analysis must absorb via concentration bounds [2].
6. **Curvature dependence.** When $f$ has total curvature $c < 1$, stronger guarantees of $(1 - e^{-c})/c$ hold and are tight [3]; the presentation above treats the worst case $c = 1$ only.

## 7 Conclusion

Submodular maximization exhibits one of the cleanest theory–practice correspondences in combinatorial optimization. The discrete greedy algorithm's $(1-1/e)$ analysis [1] is a three-line recurrence with a tight hardness match [3]; the matroid case, where discrete greedy stalls at $1/2$ [5], is rescued by lifting to the matroid polytope, flowing along the multilinear extension's gradient, and rounding back through pipage or swap rounding to recover the optimal $(1-1/e)$ [2]. Empirically, lazy evaluation makes greedy scale to $10^5$ elements while continuous methods pay a steep sampling tax for their generality — a trade-off partially addressed by combinatorial alternatives [3]. The remaining frontiers — non-monotone objectives, multiple matroid constraints, and truly scalable continuous methods — are where the next decade of the field will be decided [4][7].
