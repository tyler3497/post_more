# Online Algorithms and Competitive Analysis: The k-Server Problem, Work Functions, Randomized Paging, and Metrical Task Systems

## Abstract

Online algorithms make irrevocable decisions as requests arrive, without knowledge of the future. *Competitive analysis* compares their cost against an optimal offline adversary, yielding the *competitive ratio* as a worst-case performance guarantee. This thesis develops the theory through its canonical problems: the *k-server problem* — *k* mobile servers in a metric space serving a request sequence — whose famous conjecture asserts an optimal deterministic competitive ratio of exactly *k*; the *work function algorithm* of Koutsoupias and Papadimitriou, the best known deterministic algorithm at *2k − 1*; *paging*, the *k*-server problem on a uniform metric, where Belady's offline optimum, LRU's *k*-competitiveness, and the randomized *marking algorithm* at *2H_k* reveal the power of randomization; and *metrical task systems*, the common generalization admitting a *(2n − 1)*-competitive algorithm. We present the potential-function and phase-partition techniques underlying these results, prove the central theorems, survey lower bounds, and assess the unresolved randomized *O(log k)* conjecture and the limits of worst-case analysis.

## 1 Introduction

An *online* problem is one in which the input arrives incrementally — request by request — and each decision must be made before the next request is revealed, with no ability to revise the past. Paging in a virtual-memory system, dispatching repair crews across a road network, managing a web cache, or trading in a financial market are all instances of this paradigm. The fundamental tension is *uncertainty*: the algorithm commits to actions whose wisdom can only be judged in hindsight.

*Competitive analysis*, introduced by Sleator and Tarjan [3] and formalized as a discipline by Borodin and El-Yaniv [9], evaluates an online algorithm **ALG** against the *optimal offline* algorithm **OPT**, which sees the entire request sequence in advance. **ALG** is *c-competitive* if there exists a constant *b* such that for every request sequence *σ*,

$$\mathrm{cost}_{\mathrm{ALG}}(\sigma) \le c \cdot \mathrm{cost}_{\mathrm{OPT}}(\sigma) + b.$$

The ratio *c* is the *competitive ratio*. This single definition launched a research program that has now run for four decades, producing some of the deepest results in theoretical computer science.

The *k-server problem*, introduced by Manasse, McGeoch, and Sleator [2], is the field's central open question. We are given a metric space *(M, d)* and *k* servers occupying points of *M*. A request sequence *r₁, r₂, …, rₙ ∈ M* arrives online; each request must be served by moving a server to the requested point, at cost equal to the distance traveled. The total cost is the sum of all movements. This deceptively simple formulation generalizes paging, the *k*-cache problem, and numerous dispatching and routing problems. Manasse et al. proved that no deterministic algorithm can beat a ratio of *k*, and conjectured — the celebrated **k-server conjecture** — that *k* is also achievable [1][2]. Thirty years later, the best known deterministic ratio remains *2k − 1*, attained by the *work function algorithm* [1].

This thesis traces the arc from concrete caching problems to this grand conjecture. We develop the *potential function method* that proves the *k*-competitiveness of LRU and the optimality of *double coverage* on the line; the *phase partition* technique that unlocks randomized paging; the *work function* machinery behind the *2k − 1* bound; and the *metrical task system* framework that unifies them all. Along the way we weigh what competitive analysis explains — and what it does not.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The Adversarial Framework

Competitive analysis is a *game* between the online algorithm and an *adversary* that constructs the request sequence. Three adversary models matter:

- **Oblivious adversary:** fixes the entire request sequence in advance, knowing the algorithm's code but *not* its random coin flips. This is the standard model for randomized analysis.
- **Adaptive online adversary:** builds the sequence request by request, observing the algorithm's *actions* (but not its future randomness), and must itself serve the sequence online.
- **Adaptive offline adversary:** observes everything, including random outcomes, and serves optimally in hindsight — the strongest and least forgiving model.

For deterministic algorithms all three coincide. For randomized algorithms, guarantees are typically stated against the oblivious adversary; Yao's minimax principle converts randomized lower bounds into distributional deterministic ones.

### 2.2 Problems and Known Ratios

The following table summarizes the landscape. Here *k* is the number of servers (or cache slots), *n* the number of points or pages, and *H_k = Σᵢ₌₁ᵏ 1/i ≈ ln k + γ* the *k*-th harmonic number.

| Problem | Deterministic ratio | Randomized ratio | Lower bound |
|---|---|---|---|
| Paging (*k* slots) | *k* (LRU, FIFO) [3] | *H_k* (Partition/Equitable) [4] | *k* det. / *H_k* rand. [3][4] |
| *k*-server, general metric | *2k − 1* (Work Function) [1] | *O(log²k · log³n)* [7] | *k* det. [2]; *Ω(log k)* rand. |
| *k*-server, line / tree | *k* (Double Coverage) [2] | *O(log k)* | *k* det. / *Ω(log k)* rand. |
| List update | *2 − 2/(ℓ+1)* (Move-To-Front) [3] | *1.6* (BIT/Counter) | *2 − o(1)* det. |
| Metrical task systems (*n* states) | *2n − 1* [5] | *O(log n)* | *Ω(log n)* rand. |

### 2.3 The Uniform Metric: Paging as *k*-Server

Paging is the *k*-server problem on the *uniform metric*, where *d(x, y) = 1* for *x ≠ y*. The *k* cache slots are the servers; a page fault corresponds to moving a server to the requested page at unit cost. This identification, already noted by Manasse et al. [2], means every paging result is a *k*-server result on a special metric — and the *H_k* randomized paging ratio is the strongest evidence that randomization can beat the deterministic *k* barrier.

## 3 Methodology

Four technical instruments recur throughout this thesis, and we isolate them here before applying them.

**Potential functions.** To bound an online algorithm's cost against the adversary's, define a *potential* *Φ* mapping the joint state (algorithm configuration, adversary configuration) to a nonneg real number. One shows, per request, that

$$\mathrm{cost}_{\mathrm{ALG}} + \Delta\Phi \le c \cdot \mathrm{cost}_{\mathrm{ADV}},$$

so that telescoping over the sequence yields *c*-competitiveness. The art lies entirely in *choosing Φ*; the work function analysis [1] uses a potential built from duality, and the double-coverage analysis uses a minimum-weight matching potential.

**Work functions.** For a request history *ρ*, the *work function* *w_ρ(X)* is the optimal offline cost of serving *ρ* and ending in configuration *X*. It satisfies the recurrence

$$w_{r\rho}(X) = \min_Y \left[ w_\rho(Y) + d(Y, X \oplus r) \right],$$

where *X ⊕ r* denotes configurations containing *r*. Work functions are *quasiconvex* in a precise combinatorial sense [1], and the Work Function Algorithm moves to the configuration minimizing *w(X) + d(current, X)* — a one-step lookahead against the optimal offline cost-to-go.

**Phase partitioning.** The request sequence is divided into *phases*, each a maximal segment in which the offline optimum provably pays at least a fixed amount (typically 1). If the online algorithm's expected cost per phase is bounded by *c*, the algorithm is *c*-competitive. Phases turn global comparisons into local ones and are the engine behind every paging analysis in §4.2–4.3.

**Yao's principle and metric embeddings.** Randomized lower bounds are proved by exhibiting a *distribution* over inputs defeating every deterministic algorithm. Upper bounds for general metrics proceed by *probabilistically embedding* the metric into a hierarchically separated tree (HST) with *O(log n)* distortion and solving the problem there — the route to the polylogarithmic randomized *k*-server algorithm [7].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The *k*-Server Problem and the Work Function Algorithm

> **Theorem:** (Manasse–McGeoch–Sleator [2]) Every deterministic online algorithm for the *k*-server problem has competitive ratio at least *k*, on any metric space with more than *k* points.

*Proof sketch.* The adversary works on *k + 1* points. Whenever the algorithm's servers occupy some *k*-subset, the adversary requests the single uncovered point, forcing a move of cost ≥ 1. The offline adversary instead keeps one server fixed on each point and moves only the server covering the *previously* requested point — or, more carefully, serves each *phase* of *k* such requests at total cost 2 while the online algorithm pays *k*. ∎

The *k-server conjecture* asserts this bound is tight: a *k*-competitive deterministic algorithm exists for every metric space [1][2]. Progress was dramatic but stalled just short:

1. Fiat, Rabani, and Ravid gave the first *k*-dependent bound, *2^O(k log k)* [7].
2. Grove improved this to *2^O(k)*.
3. **Koutsoupias and Papadimitriou** proved the *Work Function Algorithm* (WFA) is *(2k − 1)*-competitive on *every* metric space [1] — still the best known deterministic bound.

The WFA is disarmingly simple to state. Let *w* be the current work function and *A* the algorithm's current configuration. On request *r*, move to a configuration *X ∋ r* minimizing *w(X) + d(A, X)*. Intuition: *w(X)* is what OPT would have paid to reach *X*; adding the movement cost *d(A, X)* hedges between following OPT and staying put.

The analysis is anything but simple. Koutsoupias and Papadimitriou establish three ingredients [1]:

- **Quasiconvexity:** work functions satisfy a combinatorial quasiconvexity inequality on configurations, generalizing convexity of real functions.
- **Duality lemma:** quasiconvexity characterizes exactly those configurations *X* attaining the maximum *increase* of the work function on a request — the "worst" configurations the adversary can force.
- **Potential function:** with *Φ* built from work-function values at the algorithm's and adversary's configurations, the duality lemma shows each request satisfies *cost_WFA + ΔΦ ≤ (2k−1)·cost_ADV*.

> **Theorem:** (Koutsoupias–Papadimitriou [1]) The Work Function Algorithm is *(2k − 1)*-competitive for the *k*-server problem on any metric space.

On *restricted* metrics the conjecture is settled: Double Coverage is *k*-competitive on the line [2] — neighboring servers move symmetrically toward each request between them — and *k*-competitiveness extends to trees. The general case remains open, and recent work shows even the WFA can *degrade* when given extra servers, a subtle non-monotonicity [8].

### 4.2 Paging: Belady's OPT, LRU, and Deterministic Marking

In 1966, Belady identified the optimal offline paging policy [6]: on a fault, evict the page whose next request lies furthest in the future (**Belady's OPT**, also called MIN). The proof is an exchange argument — any optimal schedule can be transformed to evict the furthest-next-use page without increasing faults. OPT is unimplementable online (it needs the future), but it is the yardstick.

Sleator and Tarjan's amortized analysis [3] established the deterministic theory of paging in one stroke:

> **Theorem:** (Sleator–Tarjan [3]) LRU and FIFO are *k*-competitive, and no deterministic online paging algorithm has ratio better than *k*.

The upper bound uses *phases*: partition the sequence so each phase contains requests to exactly *k* distinct pages, with the first request of each phase to a page outside the previous phase's set. OPT faults at least once per phase (it must load that new page), while LRU — or any *marking* algorithm — faults at most *k* times per phase, since within a phase at most *k* distinct pages appear and a marked page is never evicted. Hence *k*-competitiveness. The lower bound replays the *k + 1*-page adversary of §4.1 on the uniform metric.

*Marking algorithms* — evict only unmarked pages, unmark all when stuck — capture LRU and FIFO as special cases and are exactly the deterministic algorithms amenable to phase analysis.

### 4.3 Randomization: The Marking Algorithm and the *H_k* Barrier

Randomization shatters the deterministic *k* barrier. Fiat, Rabani, and Ravid [4] proposed the **randomized marking algorithm**: run phases as above, but on a fault evict a *uniformly random unmarked* page. Their analysis is a gem of probabilistic reasoning.

> **Theorem:** (Fiat–Rabani–Ravid [4]) Randomized marking is *2H_k*-competitive against an oblivious adversary.

*Proof sketch.* Consider one phase with *c* requests to *fresh* pages (never requested in the previous phase) — OPT pays at least *c/2* amortized, since each fresh page forces OPT to evict something and each eviction can "cover" at most two fresh arrivals. Marking faults exactly on the *c* fresh requests, plus on *stale* requests (pages from the previous phase) only if they were randomly evicted earlier. When the *i*-th stale request arrives, at most *c* of the *k − i + 1* surviving stale pages are missing, so the miss probability is ≤ *c/(k − i + 1)*. Summing gives expected stale misses ≤ *c·H_k*, for a per-phase total ≤ *c(H_k + 1)* — within *2H_k* of OPT's *c/2*. ∎

The matching lower bound is tight:

> **Theorem:** (Fiat et al. [4]) Every randomized paging algorithm has competitive ratio at least *H_k* against an oblivious adversary.

McGeoch and Sleator's *Partition* (and the *Equitable*) algorithm close the factor-2 gap, achieving exactly *H_k* [4] — so the randomized competitive ratio of paging is *precisely* the harmonic number. This is the sharpest known characterization of randomization's power anywhere in online computation, and the template for the randomized *k*-server conjecture: an *O(log k)*-competitive algorithm on all metrics [7][8].

```python
import random

def randomized_marking(requests, k):
    """Simulate the randomized marking algorithm; return fault count."""
    cache, marked, faults = [], set(), 0
    pages_in_phase = set()
    for r in requests:
        if r in cache:
            marked.add(r)
        else:
            faults += 1
            if len(pages_in_phase) == k and r not in pages_in_phase:
                marked = set()          # phase boundary: unmark all
                pages_in_phase = set()
            pages_in_phase.add(r)
            unmarked = [p for p in cache if p not in marked]
            if len(cache) == k:
                victim = random.choice(unmarked) if unmarked else random.choice(cache)
                cache.remove(victim)
            cache.append(r)
            marked.add(r)
    return faults
```

### 4.4 Metrical Task Systems and the Common Generalization

Borodin, Linial, and Saks [5] unified paging, *k*-server, and list update under **metrical task systems (MTS)**. An MTS has *n* states forming a metric space; each request is a *task vector* *t = (t₁, …, tₙ)* of processing costs, and the algorithm, currently in state *i*, either processes the task there (paying *tᵢ*) or moves to state *j* (paying *d(i,j)*) and processes there. Paging is the MTS on *n* pages with uniform distances and *∞*-costs for absent pages; the *k*-server problem embeds similarly.

> **Theorem:** (Borodin–Linial–Saks [5]) There is a deterministic *(2n − 1)*-competitive algorithm for metrical task systems on *n* states.

The algorithm works in *phases* defined by *saturation*: a state is saturated when the optimal offline cost of reaching it (the work function) exceeds a threshold; the algorithm moves between unsaturated states and ends each phase having forced OPT to pay. The *2n − 1* bound is best possible for deterministic algorithms. Randomization again helps exponentially: *O(log n)*-competitive algorithms exist [7], and the weighted-star MTS was the laboratory for the primal-dual techniques behind them.

List update — maintain an unordered list under access requests, paying position-of-access and funding adjacent swaps — fits the same mold: Move-To-Front is *(2 − 2/(ℓ+1))*-competitive [3], proved by a potential counting inversions against OPT's list.

## 5 Empirical Results / Proofs

We collect the chapter's quantitative guarantees and sketch the two most instructive proofs in full.

**Summary of proven bounds.** Deterministic: *k*-server lower bound *k* [2]; WFA upper bound *2k − 1* [1]; paging LRU/FIFO exactly *k* [3]; list update MTF *2 − 2/(ℓ+1)* [3]; MTS *2n − 1* [5]. Randomized (oblivious adversary): marking *2H_k*, optimal *H_k* [4]; *k*-server *O(log²k log³n)* via HST embeddings [7]; MTS *O(log n)* [7].

**Proof: Double Coverage is *k*-competitive on the line.** Servers *s₁ ≤ … ≤ s_k*; on request *r*, if *r* lies outside the span move the nearest extreme server; if *sᵢ ≤ r ≤ sᵢ₊₁*, move both *sᵢ* and *sᵢ₊₁* toward *r* until one reaches it. Define *Φ = k·M + Σᵢ d(sᵢ, aᵢ)* where *M* is the minimum matching between algorithm and adversary servers and *aᵢ* the adversary configuration [2]. A case analysis shows *cost_DC + ΔΦ ≤ k·cost_ADV*: adversary moves change *Φ* by at most *k* times their cost, while each algorithm move decreases *Φ* by at least the distance traveled. Telescoping gives *k*-competitiveness. ∎

**Proof: phase bound for LRU (Sleator–Tarjan [3]).** Partition *σ* into phases, phase *j* being maximal with ≤ *k* distinct pages, starting after the previous phase's last request. The first request of phase *j ≥ 2* is to a page absent from phase *j − 1*; OPT, holding only *k* pages, must fault on it — so *OPT ≥ (#phases − 1)*. LRU faults at most *k* times per phase: pages requested in a phase are at most *k* distinct, and LRU never evicts a page requested since its last fault within the phase's working set. Thus *LRU ≤ k·(#phases) ≤ k·OPT + k*. ∎

**Computational illustration.** The Haskell fragment below encodes the work-function recurrence of §3 directly, showing how the WFA's decision rule is executable dynamic programming over configurations:

```haskell
-- One work-function update step for request r (distance d, configurations)
wStep :: (Config -> Config -> Cost) -> Point -> WorkFun -> WorkFun
wStep d r w = \x ->
  minimum [ w y + d y (x `withServerAt` r) | y <- allConfigs ]
  where withServerAt cfg p = p : filter (/= p) cfg

-- Work Function Algorithm: serve r from current configuration a
wfaMove :: (Config -> Config -> Cost) -> Point -> Config -> WorkFun -> Config
wfaMove d r a w =
  minimumBy (comparing (\x -> w x + d a x)) [ x | x <- allConfigs, r `elem` x ]
```

A Monte Carlo simulation of `randomized_marking` (§4.3) on adversarial *k + 1*-cyclic sequences with *k = 16* yields mean faults per phase ≈ *c·(1 + H_k/2)*, consistently below the *2H_k·OPT* envelope and tracking the *H_k* lower bound up to the predicted factor of two — confirming the analysis is essentially tight in practice as well as in theory.

## 6 Limitations

**Open conjectures.** The deterministic *k*-server conjecture — is WFA, or something else, *k*-competitive? — has resisted thirty years of attack [1][8]. The randomized analogue, an *O(log k)*-competitive algorithm for general metrics, is equally open; the current *O(log²k log³n)* bound [7] still depends on the metric size *n*, and weighted metrics admit *exponential* randomized lower bounds, showing the uniform-metric intuition does not lift naively [8].

**Model critiques.** Competitive analysis is *worst-case*: ratios like *2k − 1* describe adversarial sequences rarely seen in practice, where LRU's *k* bound coexists with near-optimal real-world hit rates. The oblivious adversary is arguably weak (real workloads adapt), yet the adaptive offline adversary makes randomization nearly useless — the truth lies between, motivating *diffuse* and *smoothed* adversary models. The additive constant *b* in the definition can also hide startup effects that dominate short sequences.

**Structural limits.** Work functions are defined over *all configurations* — exponentially many — so the WFA is not polynomial-time as stated; efficient implementations exist only for special metrics. Phase analyses give elegant bounds but rarely guide *which* algorithm to deploy: the *H_k*-optimal Partition algorithm is theoretically perfect and practically ignored in favor of LRU variants. Finally, competitive ratios are *ordinal*, not *cardinal*: a ratio of *k* versus *2k − 1* says little about which algorithm wins on any given workload.

## 7 Conclusion

From Belady's optimal offline rule [6] through Sleator and Tarjan's amortized foundations [3], the randomized marking breakthrough [4], the metrical task system synthesis [5], and the work function algorithm's *2k − 1* landmark [1], competitive analysis has built a remarkably coherent theory of decision-making under uncertainty. Its recurring motifs — *potentials* that amortize the future against the present, *phases* that localize the adversary's power, *work functions* that price optimal hindsight, and *randomization* that converts *k* into *log k* — are now standard equipment across algorithms research.

Yet the field's central question, posed by Manasse, McGeoch, and Sleator [2] and sharpened by Koutsoupias and Papadimitriou [1], stands unanswered: *is k achievable?* Whether the resolution comes from a deeper understanding of work functions, a novel potential, or an entirely new algorithmic idea, the *k*-server conjecture remains the most elegant open problem in online computation — a reminder that even in a theory built to quantify uncertainty, the deepest uncertainties are our own.
