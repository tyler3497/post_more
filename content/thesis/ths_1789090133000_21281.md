# Functional Encryption: Constructions from Standard Assumptions to Indistinguishability Obfuscation, and the Impossibility Results That Bound Them

# Functional Encryption: Constructions from Standard Assumptions to Indistinguishability Obfuscation, and the Impossibility Results That Bound Them

## Abstract

Functional encryption (FE) generalizes public-key encryption by replacing monolithic decryption with fine-grained, function-specific access: a master authority issues secret keys $\mathsf{sk}_f$ for functions $f$, and decryption of a ciphertext $\mathsf{ct} = \mathsf{Enc}(\mathsf{mpk}, x)$ with $\mathsf{sk}_f$ reveals $f(x)$ and provably nothing else about $x$. This paper surveys the full arc of the theory. We begin with the syntax and game-based security formalization of Boneh, Sahai, and Waters [1], then develop the principal construction families: inner-product FE from standard assumptions such as DDH and LWE, where Agrawal, Libert, and Stehlé achieved adaptive security [4]; bounded-collusion FE for general circuits bootstrapped from inner products; and succinct FE for all circuits from indistinguishability obfuscation via the celebrated Garg–Gentry–Halevi–Raykova–Sahai–Waters transformation [2], complemented by the reusable-garbled-circuit paradigm of Goldwasser et al. [7]. We then turn to the negative results that delimit the primitive: the impossibility of simulation-based security for general functionalities, sharpened by the Agrawal–Gorbunov–Vaikuntanathan–Wee lower bound [3] and their unbounded-simulator interpolation, together with the intrinsic tension of function privacy in the public-key setting. We close with open problems on efficiency, quantum resilience, and usable instantiations.

---

## 1. Introduction

Public-key encryption was conceived around an all-or-nothing access model: whoever holds the secret key recovers the entire plaintext, and whoever does not learns nothing. For modern data systems — medical registries queried for aggregate statistics, cloud-hosted classifiers evaluated on private inputs, genomic databases searched for markers — this granularity is precisely wrong. What is needed is *controlled disclosure*: a mechanism that lets an analyst learn exactly the output of an authorized computation on encrypted data, and nothing beyond it.

*Functional encryption* (FE) is the cryptographic formalization of this idea. It was proposed by Sahai and Waters in 2005 [6] as a unifying vision, and given its definitive syntactic and security framework by Boneh, Sahai, and Waters in 2011 [1]. In an FE scheme, a trusted authority runs $\mathsf{Setup}(1^\lambda)$ to obtain a master public key $\mathsf{mpk}$ and a master secret key $\mathsf{msk}$. Encryption is public, as usual: anyone can compute $\mathsf{ct} \leftarrow \mathsf{Enc}(\mathsf{mpk}, x)$. The novelty is key derivation: for any supported function $f$, the authority issues a *function key* $\mathsf{sk}_f \leftarrow \mathsf{KeyGen}(\mathsf{msk}, f)$. Decryption is function evaluation: $\mathsf{Dec}(\mathsf{sk}_f, \mathsf{ct}) = f(x)$. The security requirement is that the *collection* of function keys held by an adversary reveals no more about $x$ than the union of the corresponding outputs — a *collusion resistance* property that is the technical heart of the subject.

This paper traces three threads of the FE literature:

1. **Definitions** — the game-based (IND) and simulation-based (SIM) formalizations, their relationship, and where they diverge.
2. **Constructions** — from selective-security inner-product schemes under DDH/LWE, through adaptive security and bounded-collusion compilers, to FE for all circuits from indistinguishability obfuscation.
3. **Impossibility and barriers** — why simulation security collapses for general functionalities, and why function privacy resists standard-model definitions.

Our treatment is self-contained at the PhD level: definitions are stated precisely, constructions are sketched with their core mechanics, and impossibility proofs are given in sufficient detail to expose the underlying commitment problem.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 The Landscape of Restricted Decryption

FE sits at the apex of a hierarchy of primitives that relax all-or-nothing decryption:

- **Identity-based encryption (IBE):** the function is an equality test $f_{\mathsf{id}}(x) = x$ iff identities match [6].
- **Attribute-based encryption (ABE):** $f$ is a policy over attribute vectors; decryption succeeds iff the policy is satisfied.
- **Predicate / searchable encryption:** keys test conjunctive, subset, and range predicates over encrypted data [5].
- **Inner-product encryption (IPE):** $\mathsf{sk}_{\mathbf{x}}$ decrypts $\mathsf{ct}_{\mathbf{y}}$ to the inner product $\langle \mathbf{x}, \mathbf{y} \rangle$ [4].

Each is an instance of FE for a restricted function class. The ambition of FE is to support *all* polynomial-size circuits while retaining a meaningful, achievable security notion.

### 2.2 Syntax and Correctness

> **Definition (Functional Encryption):** A *functionality* $F$ defined over $(K, X)$ is a function $F: K \times X \to \Sigma \cup \{\bot\}$, where $K$ is a key space, $X$ a plaintext space, and $\Sigma$ an output space [1]. An FE scheme for $F$ is a tuple $\mathsf{FE} = (\mathsf{Setup}, \mathsf{KeyGen}, \mathsf{Enc}, \mathsf{Dec})$ of PPT algorithms:
>
> - $\mathsf{Setup}(1^\lambda) \to (\mathsf{mpk}, \mathsf{msk})$
> - $\mathsf{KeyGen}(\mathsf{msk}, k) \to \mathsf{sk}_k$
> - $\mathsf{Enc}(\mathsf{mpk}, x) \to \mathsf{ct}$
> - $\mathsf{Dec}(\mathsf{mpk}, \mathsf{sk}_k, \mathsf{ct}) \to y \in \Sigma \cup \{\bot\}$
>
> *Correctness:* for all $k, x$ with $F(k,x) \neq \bot$, decryption succeeds with overwhelming probability.

In the special case of *FE for circuits*, $K$ is the set of Boolean circuits of some bounded size and $F(C, x) = C(x)$.

### 2.3 The Security Zoo

The definitional literature offers several notions, partially ordered by strength:

| Notion | Adversary power | Achievability for general circuits |
|---|---|---|
| Selective IND (s-IND) | Declares challenge messages before seeing $\mathsf{mpk}$ | Plausible under standard assumptions for restricted classes |
| Adaptive IND (IND) | Declares challenges after key queries | Achieved from iO [2]; for IPE from DDH/LWE [4] |
| Simulation (SIM) | Requires an ideal-world simulator | **Impossible** for general functionalities [1, 3] |
| USIM (unbounded simulator) | Simulator may run in unbounded time | Interpolates IND and SIM [3] |
| Function privacy | Keys must hide $f$ itself | Impossible for general circuits in public-key setting |

The IND notion is the workhorse. Formally, the IND-CPA game proceeds as follows:

```python
def ind_fe_game(adversary, scheme, lmbda):
    mpk, msk = scheme.setup(1**lmbda)
    # Phase 1: adaptive key queries
    for f in adversary.query_phase1(mpk):
        adversary.receive(scheme.keygen(msk, f))
    # Challenge: adversary submits x0, x1 with the constraint
    # f(x0) == f(x1) for every queried f
    x0, x1 = adversary.challenge(mpk)
    assert all(f(x0) == f(x1) for f in adversary.queried)
    b = random_bit()
    ct = scheme.enc(mpk, x_b)
    # Phase 2: more key queries (subject to the same constraint)
    for f in adversary.query_phase2(mpk, ct):
        assert f(x0) == f(x1)
        adversary.receive(scheme.keygen(msk, f))
    return adversary.guess(ct) == b
```

Security demands $\Pr[\text{game returns } 1] \le \tfrac{1}{2} + \mathsf{negl}(\lambda)$. The constraint $f(x_0) = f(x_1)$ is load-bearing: it encodes the principle that keys may legitimately distinguish the challenge messages *only* through their sanctioned outputs.

## 3. Methodology

Our methodology is a structured synthesis of the primary literature. We proceed in four stages:

1. **Formal extraction:** we isolate the exact syntax, correctness, and security experiments from the definitional papers [1, 3, 8], normalizing notation across sources.
2. **Construction archaeology:** we reconstruct the key mechanisms of each major construction family — dual-system encryption for IPE [4], the iO-plus-NIZK compiler for general circuits [2], and the reusable-garbled-circuit route to succinctness [7] — at the level of their central algebraic or combinatorial ideas.
3. **Impossibility analysis:** we identify the *commitment problem* at the root of every simulation-security impossibility proof, and show how the Agrawal et al. lower bound [3] exploits unbounded collusion.
4. **Empirical grounding:** where implementations exist (inner-product FE libraries), we report concrete cost parameters — ciphertext expansion, decryption bottlenecks, and the discrete-logarithm recovery step — to connect asymptotic claims with deployability.

We distinguish sharply between *theorem statements* taken from the literature, which we cite, and *proof sketches*, which we present in our own reconstruction. All mathematical claims in the sequel are standard results; citations mark their provenance.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 IND versus SIM: Why the Natural Definition Fails

The simulation paradigm — the gold standard inherited from secure computation — demands that whatever an adversary learns from ciphertexts and function keys could also be learned by a simulator given only the legitimate outputs $\{f(x)\}$ and the adversary's key set. For FE this is *impossible* in the standard model for general functionalities, a fact already observed by Boneh, Sahai, and Waters [1] and deepened by O'Neill's definitional analysis [8].

> **Theorem (informal, [1, 3]):** There is no simulation-secure FE scheme for general (even very simple) functionalities in the standard model against non-adaptive adversaries making a single key query, under general assumptions.

The obstruction is a *commitment problem*. Consider the functionality family of pseudorandom functions. A simulator must produce a fake ciphertext before knowing which key the adversary will request. When the adversary later asks for $\mathsf{sk}_{\mathsf{PRF}_k}$, the simulator must "explain" its earlier ciphertext as an encryption consistent with the PRF output on the challenge input — but it committed to a concrete string too early. The real scheme has no such problem: the real ciphertext genuinely encrypts $x$, and the real key genuinely evaluates the PRF.

Agrawal, Gorbunov, Vaikuntanathan, and Wee [3] sharpened this into a full lower bound for a *weak* (non-adaptive) simulation notion, exploiting *unbounded collusions* in an essential way — the first impossibility that needs the adversary to hold many keys. They further proposed **USIM**, simulation with an *unbounded* simulator, which interpolates IND and SIM: an unbounded simulator can break the commitment problem by brute force, recovering a notion that is strictly stronger than IND yet still (barely) achievable. This interpolation revealed deep correlations with barriers in zero-knowledge and multi-party computation [3].

*Practical moral:* do not ask FE for ideal-world guarantees. The achievable target is IND-security with collusion resistance — keys reveal only sanctioned outputs, formalized as indistinguishability under the $f(x_0) = f(x_1)$ constraint.

### 4.2 Inner-Product FE from Standard Assumptions

The inner-product functionality — $\mathsf{sk}_{\mathbf{x}}$ applied to $\mathsf{ct}_{\mathbf{y}}$ yields $\langle \mathbf{x}, \mathbf{y} \rangle$ — is the workhorse of practical FE. It captures weighted sums, statistical moments, and linear classifiers, and it admits constructions from *standard* assumptions.

The modern line begins with Abdalla et al. (PKC 2015), who gave simple DDH- and LWE-based schemes, but only with *selective* security: the adversary must declare $x_0, x_1$ before seeing $\mathsf{mpk}$. Agrawal, Libert, and Stehlé [4] lifted this to *full adaptive* security using hash proof systems with homomorphic properties over the key space, achieving:

- Adaptive IND-security under DDH, LWE, and (novel) Paillier composite-residuosity instantiations [4].
- Inner products over a prime field $\mathbb{Z}_p$ rather than only short integer vectors.
- A compiler showing that prime-field IPE *suffices for bounded-collusion FE for all circuits* [4].

The last point is striking: a seemingly weak linear functionality already bootstraps to general computation, provided the number of colluding keys is bounded. The core technique encodes circuit evaluation as a sequence of inner products with carefully structured vectors, using the field structure to simulate non-linear gates.

The price of inner-product decryption is the *discrete logarithm* step: the scheme recovers $g^{\langle \mathbf{x}, \mathbf{y} \rangle}$ and must extract the exponent, feasible only when the inner product lies in a small range. This is the fundamental efficiency bottleneck of all DDH-based IPE and the reason deployments restrict to small message spaces or use LWE-based variants that avoid it at the cost of larger parameters.

```haskell
-- The inner-product functionality as a pure specification
type Vector = [Integer]

innerProduct :: Vector -> Vector -> Integer
innerProduct xs ys = sum (zipWith (*) xs ys)

-- An FE scheme is correct for this functionality when:
-- dec (keyGen msk f) (enc mpk x) == innerProduct f x
-- and IND-secure when no adversary distinguishes encryptions
-- of x0, x1 with innerProduct f x0 == innerProduct f x1 for all queried f
```

### 4.3 FE for All Circuits from Indistinguishability Obfuscation

The landmark result of Garg, Gentry, Halevi, Raykova, Sahai, and Waters [2] constructs FE for *all* polynomial-size circuits from indistinguishability obfuscation (iO), public-key encryption, and NIZKs. The construction is a masterclass in cryptographic bootstrapping:

1. **iO for $\mathsf{NC}^1$** from a new algebraic assumption over simplified multilinear maps ("multilinear jigsaw puzzles").
2. **iO for $\mathsf{NC}^1$ + FHE (with $\mathsf{NC}^1$ decryption) $\Rightarrow$ iO for all circuits** [2].
3. **iO + PKE + NIZK $\Rightarrow$ FE for all circuits**, with *succinct* ciphertexts — ciphertext size independent of circuit size [2].

The FE construction works by obfuscating a program that, on input an encryption of $x$ and a proof of well-formedness, outputs $C(x)$ for the circuit embedded at setup, while the NIZK prevents malleability attacks that would otherwise let an adversary re-randomize ciphertexts to probe the obfuscated program. Succinctness — ciphertexts that do not grow with $|C|$ — is not a cosmetic property: Goldwasser et al. [7] showed that *succinct* FE is equivalent to *reusable garbled circuits*, a primitive with sweeping applications to delegation and one-time programs.

> **Theorem (GGHRSW [2]):** Assuming sub-exponentially secure iO for $\mathsf{P/poly}$, public-key encryption, and NIZKs, there exists an IND-secure functional encryption scheme for all polynomial-size circuits with succinct ciphertexts and collusion resistance against any polynomial number of key queries.

The caveat, and it is a large one, is that iO itself rests on multilinear-map candidates whose concrete security has been repeatedly undermined. FE for all circuits is therefore *feasibility* rather than *deployability*: it tells us the primitive is not information-theoretically or complexity-theoretically barred, but gives no scheme one could ship.

```rust
// Conceptual shape of the GGHRSW-style FE interface
trait FunctionalEncryption {
    type Mpk; type Msk; type Ct; type Sk;
    fn setup(secparam: usize) -> (Self::Mpk, Self::Msk);
    // sk is bound to a circuit C at key-generation time
    fn keygen(msk: &Self::Msk, circuit: &Circuit) -> Self::Sk;
    fn encrypt(mpk: &Self::Mpk, x: &[bool]) -> Self::Ct;
    // Returns C(x); IND-security: keys reveal nothing beyond this.
    fn decrypt(sk: &Self::Sk, ct: &Self::Ct) -> Vec<bool>;
}
```

### 4.4 The Lower-Bound Frontier: What Cannot Be Built

Beyond simulation impossibility, the literature maps several hard boundaries:

1. **Simulation with bounded collusion still fails.** The AGVW lower bound [3] shows that even non-adaptive simulation security collapses once the adversary may hold many keys, using PRFs to force the commitment problem at scale. This was the first impossibility to *require* unbounded collusion, closing the hope that "a few keys" might be simulatable.

2. **Function privacy is unachievable for expressive classes.** In the public-key setting, an adversary can always encrypt chosen messages and test candidate functions against the public key. Boneh et al. showed that meaningful simulation-based function privacy for general circuits would imply virtual black-box obfuscation — itself long known impossible [3]. The best achievable notions are IND-style, restricted to high-min-entropy function distributions, and even those are confined to simple functionalities like IBE and inner products.

3. **Efficiency barriers.** Any FE for general circuits with *short* keys or *compact* ciphertexts must embed the full power of iO or succinct arguments: the GKP+13 equivalence [7] between succinct FE and reusable garbled circuits means that compressing FE below the iO barrier is as hard as the central open problems of obfuscation.

These results give FE an unusual profile: a primitive whose *existence* is settled (from iO) but whose *instantiation* from standard assumptions is impossible for general circuits, and whose strongest natural security notion is unachievable at all. The viable design space is therefore sharply delineated: IND-security, restricted function classes (inner products, quadratics, bounded collusion), standard assumptions — or general circuits under iO-grade assumptions.

### 4.5 Function Privacy: Hiding the Function in the Key

Standard FE hides the *message* but not the *function*: $\mathsf{sk}_f$ is typically issued in the clear and may leak $f$. *Function-private* FE asks that keys reveal $f$ only through their input-output behavior. The motivation is concrete — a hospital issuing a key for a proprietary diagnostic predicate does not want the predicate itself reverse-engineered from the key.

In the *private-key* setting, function privacy is achievable for useful classes. In the *public-key* setting, the adversary's ability to encrypt arbitrary messages and observe decryptions makes full function hiding impossible for expressive classes, as discussed above. The surviving positive results use IND-style definitions over high-min-entropy function distributions — e.g., Boneh, Raghunathan, and Segev's function-private IBE — and, as shown in [3], any simulation-flavored strengthening for general circuits collapses to VBB obfuscation. Function privacy thus remains one of the sharpest illustrations of FE's definitional fragility: the gap between the intuition ("keys shouldn't leak the function") and any achievable formalization is a theorem, not a failure of imagination.

## 5. Empirical Results and Proofs

FE for general circuits has no meaningful benchmarks — iO-based constructions are asymptotic existence proofs. The empirical content of the field lives in inner-product FE, where multiple implementations exist. The table below consolidates the concrete cost profile of the principal schemes:

| Scheme | Assumption | Security | Ciphertext size | Key size | Decryption bottleneck |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Abdalla et al., PKC 2015 (DDH) | DDH | Selective | $O(n)$ group elements | $O(n)$ | Discrete log over small range |
| Abdalla et al., PKC 2015 (LWE) | LWE | Selective | $O(n \log q)$ | $O(n \log q)$ | Noise flooding, large modulus |
| Agrawal–Libert–Stehlé [4] (DDH) | DDH | **Adaptive** | $O(n)$ group elements | $O(n)$ | Discrete log over small range |
| Agrawal–Libert–Stehlé [4] (LWE) | LWE / ext-LWE | **Adaptive**, mod-$p$ | $O(n \log q)$ | $O(n \log q)$ | Modulus switching, no DLP |
| Agrawal–Libert–Stehlé [4] (Paillier) | Composite residuosity | Adaptive | $O(n)$ mod $N^2$ | $O(n)$ | Paillier decryption per slot |
| GGHRSW [2] (general circuits) | iO + PKE + NIZK | Adaptive IND | Succinct: $\mathsf{poly}(\lambda, |x|)$ | $\mathsf{poly}(\lambda, |C|)$ | Obfuscated evaluation (infeasible) |

Here $n$ is the vector dimension. For DDH-based IPE, encryption of an $n$-dimensional vector costs $n+O(1)$ exponentiations; decryption costs $O(n)$ pairings/exponentiations *plus* a baby-step-giant-step discrete logarithm whose running time is $O(\sqrt{B})$ for output bound $B$ — practical only for $B \lesssim 2^{40}$ even with precomputation. LWE-based schemes avoid the discrete log entirely but pay in parameter size: moduli $q \approx 2^{60}$–$2^{80}$ and dimension blowup from noise flooding push ciphertexts into the hundreds of kilobytes for $n = 100$, versus tens of kilobytes under DDH.

> **Theorem (Bounded-collusion compiler [4]):** Fully secure (adaptive) inner-product FE over a prime field implies bounded-collusion IND-secure FE for all circuits.

*Proof sketch.* Fix a collusion bound $Q$. The construction secret-shares the circuit $C$ into $Q$ randomized components such that any $Q$ function keys jointly reconstruct $C(x)$ but any $Q-1$ reveal nothing. Each component is evaluated through inner products over $\mathbb{Z}_p$ with vectors encoding randomized gate tables; the prime-field structure supports the non-linear gate simulation via polynomial encodings. Adaptive security of the underlying IPE lifts through the compiler because the reduction can program the shares after seeing the adversary's queries. The exponential dependence on $Q$ is the price of generality: this is feasibility for *constant* collusion bounds, not a practical compiler. ∎

For quadratic functionalities (degree-2 polynomials), dedicated constructions achieve linear-size parameters without the exponential blowup, and have been implemented in open-source libraries with encryption throughput in the thousands of ciphertexts per second for $n = 50$ on commodity hardware — the current practical frontier of expressive FE.

## 6. Limitations

We enumerate the principal limitations honestly, since they define the research frontier:

1. **No standard-model FE for general circuits.** Every known construction for all polynomial-size circuits assumes iO or multilinear-map-grade primitives [2]. Under standard assumptions (DDH, LWE, RSA), FE is confined to restricted classes: inner products [4], quadratics, and bounded-collusion compilers with exponential overhead.

2. **Simulation security is unachievable.** The strongest intuitive notion — ideal-world simulation — is provably impossible for general functionalities, even non-adaptively with a single key query [1, 3]. Deployments must settle for IND-security and understand exactly what its $f(x_0) = f(x_1)$ constraint does and does not promise.

3. **The discrete-log bottleneck.** DDH-based inner-product FE, the most efficient family, requires extracting $\langle \mathbf{x}, \mathbf{y} \rangle$ from $g^{\langle \mathbf{x}, \mathbf{y} \rangle}$, restricting outputs to small ranges. This rules out direct use for large-integer arithmetic and forces careful application design.

4. **Function privacy is nearly vacuous in the public-key setting.** Keys for expressive functions cannot hide the function against an adversary who can encrypt and observe [3]. Applications that need proprietary-function hiding must move to the private-key setting or accept IND-style, distribution-restricted guarantees.

5. **Quantum vulnerability of deployed instantiations.** DDH- and Paillier-based IPE fall to Shor's algorithm. LWE-based IPE [4] is plausibly post-quantum, but the adaptive LWE construction relies on *extended* LWE variants whose concrete security is less studied than plain LWE, and parameter selection for 128-bit post-quantum security inflates sizes substantially.

6. **Trusted setup and key escrow.** FE concentrates absolute power in the master authority: $\mathsf{msk}$ decrypts everything. There is no meaningful "trustless" FE without multi-authority extensions, which remain far less developed than their ABE counterparts.

---

## 7. Conclusion

Functional encryption began as a unifying vision — IBE, ABE, and searchable encryption as special cases of a single primitive [1, 6] — and matured into a field with unusual intellectual honesty: its impossibility results are as celebrated as its constructions. The positive arc runs from Boneh and Waters' conjunctive-query systems [5] through adaptive inner-product FE from standard assumptions [4] to the iO-based feasibility of FE for all circuits with succinct ciphertexts [2, 7]. The negative arc runs from the collapse of simulation security [1, 8] through the Agrawal et al. lower bound exploiting unbounded collusion and their USIM interpolation [3], to the reduction of function privacy to virtual black-box obfuscation.

The synthesis is a clear map of the possible. *Achievable today:* IND-secure FE for linear and quadratic functionalities under standard assumptions, with real implementations and understood cost profiles. *Feasible in principle:* IND-secure FE for all circuits from iO-grade assumptions. *Provably impossible:* simulation security for general functionalities, and function privacy for expressive classes in the public-key setting. The open problems that matter are correspondingly concrete: post-quantum IPE with tight parameters, multi-authority FE without trusted setup, and — the grand challenge — basing general FE on assumptions weaker than iO. Until then, FE remains cryptography's most elegant demonstration that the boundary between the possible and the impossible can itself be a theorem.
