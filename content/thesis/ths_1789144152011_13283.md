# Sub-Quadratic Attention: FlashAttention Tiling, KV-Cache Compression, and the Memory Wall of Transformers

## Abstract

The self-attention operator at the heart of the Transformer scales quadratically with sequence length in both time and memory, a complexity that has become the central bottleneck of long-context language modeling. This thesis examines the memory wall from three angles. First, *IO-aware exact attention*: the FlashAttention family (1, 2, 3), which replaces the O(N²) attention matrix with block-wise online-softmax tiling between GPU high-bandwidth memory and on-chip SRAM, cutting memory to O(N) with no approximation. Second, the *KV-cache*, whose footprint grows linearly in length, depth, and batch size: we survey quantization, eviction policies (H2O, StreamingLLM), and multi-query/grouped-query attention. Third, architectural escape hatches: linear attention and selective state-space models such as Mamba, which trade the exact softmax for O(N) recurrent dynamics. We derive the IO-complexity bounds of tiled attention, the closed-form KV-cache memory equation, and the rescaling identities of online softmax, and close with the Pareto frontier governing efficient sequence modeling.

---

## 1. Introduction

The Transformer has become the dominant architecture of sequence modeling, yet its core operator — scaled dot-product attention — carries a cost that is quadratic in sequence length N. For query, key, and value matrices Q, K, V ∈ ℝ^{N×d}, the standard formulation

$$\text{Attention}(Q, K, V) = \text{softmax}\left(\frac{QK^{\top}}{\sqrt{d}}\right)V$$

requires Θ(N²d) floating-point operations and Θ(N²) memory to store the intermediate score matrix S = QKᵀ and probability matrix P = softmax(S) [1]. For decades this cost was tolerable because sequence lengths stayed modest. That era has ended: modern language models are trained and served on contexts of 32K, 128K, and even million-token scale, where the quadratic term dominates every other cost in the system.

Two distinct walls now constrain Transformers. The *training and prefill wall* is a problem of memory bandwidth and capacity: the N×N attention matrix cannot be materialized in HBM at scale. The *inference wall* is the *KV-cache*: during autoregressive decoding, the keys and values of every previous token must be retained to avoid recomputation, producing a cache that grows linearly with sequence length, depth, head count, and batch size. For a LLaMA-2-7B model with batch size 8 and a 32K-token context, the KV-cache alone reaches 128 GB — exceeding the model's own parameters [6].

This thesis surveys the three families of attack on these walls. **Exact IO-aware algorithms**, led by the FlashAttention lineage [1][2][3], keep the mathematics of attention bit-identical while restructuring its execution to respect the GPU memory hierarchy. **KV-cache compression**, including quantization and eviction policies such as H2O [4] and StreamingLLM [5], reduces the serving footprint by discarding or compressing cached state. **Sub-quadratic architectures**, including kernelized linear attention and selective state-space models such as Mamba [7], abandon the softmax entirely in exchange for linear-time recurrent dynamics.

The central claim of this thesis is that the memory wall is not a single barrier but a *stack* of bottlenecks — IO complexity, cache footprint, and asymptotic complexity — each requiring a different instrument, and that the winning designs are those that are co-designed with hardware rather than derived from pure asymptotics.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 The GPU memory hierarchy and the IO model

Modern GPUs expose a steep memory hierarchy. Off-chip HBM offers tens of gigabytes at roughly 2–3.5 TB/s of bandwidth, while on-chip SRAM — shared memory and registers per streaming multiprocessor — offers only tens of megabytes in aggregate at an order of magnitude higher bandwidth. In the *external memory model* (the IO model), the cost of an algorithm is measured not in FLOPs but in the number of elements moved between slow and fast memory. An algorithm that performs few FLOPs but shuttles large tensors through HBM repeatedly is *memory-bound*: its wall-clock time is dictated by bandwidth, not by the tensor cores.

Standard attention is the canonical memory-bound kernel. It reads Q, K, V from HBM, writes the N×N matrices S and P back to HBM, reads them again for the softmax and the multiplication by V, and writes the output. The IO complexity is Θ(Nd + N²): linear terms for the inputs plus a quadratic term for the materialized attention matrix. Because the kernel performs only O(1) FLOPs per byte moved, it saturates HBM bandwidth while leaving the tensor cores idle — the precise pathology that IO-aware algorithms cure.

### 2.2 Autoregressive decoding and the KV-cache

During training and *prefill*, all N query positions are processed in parallel. During *decoding*, tokens are generated one at a time: to emit token t+1, the model needs the keys and values of tokens 1…t. Recomputing them from scratch at every step would cost O(t) per step and O(N²) total; instead, inference engines *cache* them. The KV-cache stores two tensors per layer:

$$\text{KV-cache bytes} = 2 \cdot n_{\text{layers}} \cdot n_{\text{kv\_heads}} \cdot d_{\text{head}} \cdot L \cdot b_{\text{bytes}} \cdot B$$

where L is the sequence length, b_bytes the bytes per element, and B the batch size. For multi-head attention (n_kv_heads = n_heads), this simplifies to 2 · n_layers · d_model · L · b_bytes · B. In fp16 (2 bytes), LLaMA-2-7B (32 layers, d_model = 4096) requires 2 · 32 · 4096 · 2 = 524,288 bytes ≈ 0.5 MB *per token*; a 32K context therefore needs ≈16 GB per request, and a batch of 8 needs 128 GB [6]. Decode is thus memory-bandwidth bound: each step streams the entire cache through HBM to produce a single token, so throughput is governed by cache size divided by bandwidth.

### 2.3 Approximate attention: the road not taken (at scale)

A large literature proposed *approximate* attention — sparsity patterns, low-rank factorizations, kernel approximations — trading model quality for reduced complexity. Dao et al. [1] observe a sobering empirical fact: these methods frequently fail to achieve *wall-clock* speedup despite better asymptotics, because sparse or low-rank kernels have low arithmetic intensity and poor hardware utilization, while often degrading perplexity. The field therefore converged on a different principle: keep the math exact, and optimize the *execution*.

## 3. Methodology

This thesis proceeds by *complexity accounting*: for each technique we derive (i) the asymptotic time and memory complexity, (ii) the IO complexity in the external-memory model where stated in the literature, (iii) the empirical wall-clock or memory outcome reported by the original authors, and (iv) the quality cost, if any. Our comparison framework rests on four axes:

1. **Exactness** — is the attention output bit-identical (in real arithmetic) to standard attention, or an approximation?
2. **Memory scaling** — how does peak memory grow with N: O(N²), O(N), or O(1) per step?
3. **IO complexity** — how many HBM accesses are required, as a function of SRAM size M?
4. **Quality retention** — measured in perplexity or downstream accuracy relative to the dense baseline.

We draw all empirical claims from the primary sources [1]–[7], and we reproduce the key algorithmic identities (online softmax rescaling, the KV-cache equation) in executable form so the reader can verify the arithmetic.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Online softmax: the identity that makes tiling possible

The obstacle to tiling attention is the softmax: it couples every element of a row, seemingly requiring the entire row of scores before any output can be produced. The *online softmax* algorithm of Milakov and Gimelshein (2018) removes this obstacle by maintaining two running statistics per row — the running maximum m and the running normalization sum ℓ — and rescaling partial results as new blocks arrive.

Consider one row-block of scores partitioned as [S⁽¹⁾ S⁽²⁾]. Standard softmax needs the global maximum m = max(rowmax(S⁽¹⁾), rowmax(S⁽²⁾)) and the global sum ℓ = rowsum(e^{S⁽¹⁾−m}) + rowsum(e^{S⁽²⁾−m}) before normalizing. The online variant processes S⁽¹⁾ first, computing local statistics m⁽¹⁾, ℓ⁽¹⁾ and an unnormalized partial output Õ⁽¹⁾ = e^{S⁽¹⁾−m⁽¹⁾}V⁽¹⁾, then *corrects* for the new global maximum when S⁽²⁾ arrives [2]:

$$m^{(2)} = \max(m^{(1)}, \text{rowmax}(S^{(2)}))$$
$$\ell^{(2)} = e^{m^{(1)}-m^{(2)}}\ell^{(1)} + \text{rowsum}(e^{S^{(2)}-m^{(2)}})$$
$$\tilde{O}^{(2)} = \text{diag}(e^{m^{(1)}-m^{(2)}})\tilde{O}^{(1)} + e^{S^{(2)}-m^{(2)}}V^{(2)}, \qquad O = \text{diag}(\ell^{(2)})^{-1}\tilde{O}^{(2)}$$

The rescaling factor e^{m⁽¹⁾−m⁽²⁾} retroactively re-normalizes the first block's contribution to the new maximum. By induction over blocks, the tiled computation is *exactly* equal to the full softmax — in real arithmetic — while never holding more than one S-block in fast memory. This identity is the engine of the entire FlashAttention family.

### 4.2 The FlashAttention lineage: IO-aware exact attention

**FlashAttention (Dao et al., 2022)** [1] applies tiling to the full attention operator. Q is partitioned into row-blocks of size B_r and K, V into column-blocks of size B_c, chosen so that four blocks plus the running statistics fit in SRAM. The outer loop iterates over K/V blocks (loaded once each from HBM), the inner loop over Q blocks; per (Q-block, K/V-block) pair the kernel computes S = QKᵀ in SRAM, applies online softmax, and accumulates into the output block — which is written to HBM only once, at the end. The N×N matrices S and P are *never materialized*. The backward pass recomputes the attention probabilities on the fly from the stored softmax normalization statistics, trading recomputation for memory.

> **Theorem (IO complexity of FlashAttention):** FlashAttention requires Θ(N²d²/M) HBM accesses, where M is the SRAM size — versus Θ(Nd + N²) for standard attention — and Dao et al. prove this is *optimal* over the practical range of SRAM sizes [1]. Memory complexity drops from O(N²) to O(N).

Empirically: 15% end-to-end speedup on BERT-large (MLPerf 1.1 record), 3× on GPT-2, 2.4× on Long-Range Arena, and the first better-than-chance Transformers on Path-X (16K) and Path-256 (64K) [1].

**FlashAttention-2 (Dao, 2023)** [2] reworks parallelism and work partitioning: it parallelizes over the sequence-length dimension in addition to the batch and head dimensions, adjusts the inner/outer loop order so that each thread block processes one Q-block against all K/V blocks (reducing non-matmul FLOPs and improving occupancy), and distributes work to keep all SMs busy even for short sequences. The result is approximately 2× the throughput of FlashAttention-1.

**FlashAttention-3 (Shah et al., 2024)** [3] targets NVIDIA Hopper (H100) hardware, where FlashAttention-2 reached only ~35% utilization. Three techniques close the gap:

- *Producer–consumer asynchrony* via warp specialization: data-movement warps and compute warps are split so Tensor Core execution overlaps TMA (Tensor Memory Accelerator) transfers.
- *Interleaved GEMM and softmax*: a two-stage pipeline hides low-throughput softmax operations (exponentials, multiply-adds) underneath asynchronous WGMMA instructions.
- *FP8* support via block quantization and incoherent processing to tame low-precision error.

FlashAttention-3 achieves 1.5–2.0× over FlashAttention-2 in FP16 (up to 740 TFLOPs/s, 75% utilization) and nearly 1.2 PFLOPs/s in FP8, with 2.6× lower numerical error than baseline FP8 attention [3] — gains from deeper hardware co-design, not new mathematics.

### 4.3 The KV-cache wall: quantization, eviction, and head sharing

Tiled kernels solve the *compute* side of long context; the *serving* side is dominated by the KV-cache equation of §2.2. Three compression families attack it.

**Head sharing (MQA/GQA).** Multi-query attention shares a single key/value head across all query heads; grouped-query attention (GQA) interpolates, using e.g. 8 KV heads for 64 query heads in LLaMA-2-70B — an 8× cache reduction with negligible quality loss. This is now the default in production models, and it composes with everything below.

**Quantization.** KIVI and related schemes quantize keys per-channel and values per-token to 4 or even 2 bits, exploiting the empirical observation that key channels have large-magnitude outliers while value tokens do not. INT4 quantization cuts the cache ~4× with minimal perplexity degradation; INT2 remains an active research frontier.

**Eviction policies.** When the cache must be *bounded*, which tokens may be forgotten? **H2O** (Zhang et al., 2023) [4] observes that a small set of *heavy hitter* tokens accumulates most of the attention mass; it retains recent tokens plus the heavy hitters, formalizing eviction as a *dynamic submodular* maximization with a provable approximation guarantee. With only 20% of the cache retained, H2O improves throughput up to 29× over DeepSpeed Zero-Inference and HuggingFace Accelerate, and reduces latency up to 1.9×, on OPT-6.7B/30B [4]. **StreamingLLM** (Xiao et al., 2023) [5] discovers the *attention sink* phenomenon: initial tokens attract disproportionate attention mass regardless of semantic content, acting as a sink the softmax needs for numerical stability. Keeping just the first few tokens plus a sliding window of recent tokens recovers window attention's performance and enables stable generation to 4M+ tokens — a 22.2× speedup over sliding-window recomputation [5]. **SnapKV** refines the idea with an *observation window*: it uses the last w queries' attention to vote on which past tokens matter, keeping the winners — a hybrid of positional and content-based selection.

Attention mass is *heavy-tailed*: a tiny fraction of positions carries most of the influence, so aggressive eviction is nearly lossless — until structurally critical positions (delimiters, question boundaries) are evicted, which collapses quality regardless of the scoring heuristic; production policies protect them explicitly.

### 4.4 Escape hatches: linear attention and selective state spaces

If the softmax itself is the problem, one may abandon it. **Linear attention** replaces softmax(QKᵀ)V with a kernelized form φ(Q)(φ(K)ᵀV), where the feature map φ admits the associative regrouping (φ(K)ᵀV computed first) — yielding O(Nd²) time and an O(d²) recurrent state instead of a growing cache. Elegant, but the kernel approximation historically trails softmax quality on language, and the d² state can exceed a KV-cache for wide models.

**Selective state-space models** take the recurrent view seriously. A classical SSM evolves a latent state h_t = Āh_{t−1} + B̄x_t with output y_t = Ch_t — linear-time, constant memory, but with *time-invariant* dynamics that cannot do content-based reasoning (the model cannot choose to remember or forget based on the input). **Mamba** (Gu & Dao, 2023) [7] makes the discretization step Δ and the matrices B, C *functions of the input*, giving the recurrence a selection mechanism: the state update becomes data-dependent, recovering attention-like content-based gating. Selectivity breaks the convolutional training view, so Mamba introduces a *hardware-aware parallel scan* — an associative parallel algorithm over the sequence dimension, fused into SRAM-resident kernels in the spirit of FlashAttention. The result: 5× higher inference throughput than Transformers, linear scaling to million-length sequences, and a 3B-parameter model that outperforms same-size Transformers and matches Transformers twice its size [7]. The SSM lineage (S4 → Mamba) thus replays, in recurrent form, the same lesson as FlashAttention: asymptotics are necessary but not sufficient; *hardware-aware algorithms* win.

The following Python program reproduces the two central quantitative identities of this thesis — the KV-cache memory equation and the IO-complexity scaling of tiled versus standard attention — so the reader can verify the arithmetic on any model configuration:

```python
import math

def kv_cache_bytes(n_layers, n_kv_heads, d_head, seq_len, bytes_per_elem=2, batch=1):
    """KV-cache footprint: 2 (K and V) x layers x kv_heads x head_dim x L x precision x batch."""
    return 2 * n_layers * n_kv_heads * d_head * seq_len * bytes_per_elem * batch

def fmt(n):
    for unit in ("B", "KB", "MB", "GB", "TB"):
        if n < 1024:
            return f"{n:.2f} {unit}"
        n /= 1024
    return f"{n:.2f} PB"

# LLaMA-2-7B: 32 layers, 32 kv heads, head dim 128, fp16
for L in (4096, 32768, 131072):
    print(f"L={L:>7,}: {fmt(kv_cache_bytes(32, 32, 128, L))} per request")

# batch=8, 32K context -> the 128 GB figure reported in the literature
print("batch=8, L=32K:", fmt(kv_cache_bytes(32, 32, 128, 32768, batch=8)))

def io_standard(N, d):
    return N * d + N * N          # inputs + materialized N x N matrix

def io_flashattention(N, d, M):
    return (N ** 2 * d ** 2) / M  # Theta(N^2 d^2 / M) HBM accesses

N, d, M = 8192, 128, 20 * 1024**2 // 2  # 20 MB SRAM, fp16 elements
print(f"standard IO ~ {io_standard(N, d):.2e}, tiled IO ~ {io_flashattention(N, d, M):.2e}")
```

Running it confirms the headline numbers: ≈2 GB at 4K tokens, ≈16 GB at 32K tokens per request, and 128 GB at batch 8 — the memory wall, in one line of arithmetic.

## 6. Limitations

Every technique surveyed here pays a price, and an honest accounting must name them.

**Tiled exact attention does not change the FLOP count.** FlashAttention remains Θ(N²d) in work; it converts a memory-bound kernel into a compute-bound one, but for long enough sequences the quadratic FLOPs themselves become the wall — no tiling can fix O(N²) work.

**Eviction is lossy and task-sensitive.** H2O, StreamingLLM, and SnapKV preserve perplexity on language modeling and many benchmarks, but needle-in-a-haystack retrieval — finding one fact buried mid-context — is precisely the workload eviction destroys: the needle is rarely a heavy hitter, a sink, or recent. Compression policies must therefore be matched to the task distribution, and "near-lossless on average" can hide catastrophic worst cases.

**Quantization interacts with outliers.** Key channels exhibit large-magnitude outlier dimensions; naive per-tensor quantization collapses under them, which is why KIVI-style per-channel/per-token schemes are required. INT2 remains brittle, and the interaction of quantization with eviction (quantized heavy hitters) is underexplored.

**Sub-quadratic architectures trade expressivity.** Linear attention's kernel approximation and Mamba's fixed-size recurrent state both impose an information bottleneck: a constant-size state cannot, in principle, implement arbitrary content-addressable lookup over unbounded context. Whether this matters in practice is task-dependent — Mamba matches Transformers on many benchmarks — but the *copying* and *in-context retrieval* weaknesses of recurrent models are well documented, and hybrid attention–SSM architectures are an active compromise.

**Hardware specificity is a double-edged sword.** FlashAttention-3's gains depend on Hopper's WGMMA and TMA; the kernel must be rewritten per architecture generation. IO-awareness buys speed at the cost of portability — a tax the field keeps paying.

---

## 7. Conclusion

The memory wall of Transformers is not one wall but three: the *IO wall* of materializing N×N attention matrices, the *capacity wall* of the linearly growing KV-cache, and the *asymptotic wall* of quadratic work itself. This thesis has traced the instruments built for each. FlashAttention [1][2][3] defeats the IO wall with online-softmax tiling, proving that exactness and efficiency are compatible when the algorithm respects the memory hierarchy — and that each hardware generation repays co-design with another factor of two. KV-cache compression [4][5] defeats the capacity wall by exploiting the heavy-tailed structure of attention mass: most tokens can be forgotten because few tokens matter, though the boundary between the forgettable and the structurally critical must be handled with care. Selective state spaces [7] tunnel under the asymptotic wall entirely, replacing the softmax with input-dependent recurrences that scale linearly — at the cost of exact content-addressability.

The through-line is a design principle: *count bytes moved, not just FLOPs*. The algorithms that survived contact with production — tiled exact attention, grouped queries, sink-aware eviction, hardware-aware scans — are those whose analysis includes the memory hierarchy. The methods surveyed here will not be the last word, but the principle will endure: the fastest algorithm is the one that moves the least data.
