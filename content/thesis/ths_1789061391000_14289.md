# The Preprocessing Paradigm in Maliciously Secure Multiparty Computation: Yao's Garbled Circuits, the GMW Protocol, and the SPDZ Offline–Online Architecture under Dishonest Majority

## Abstract

Secure multiparty computation (MPC) lets mutually distrusting parties evaluate a function over private inputs, revealing nothing beyond the output. This paper surveys the path from Yao's garbled circuits [1] and the GMW completeness theorem [2] to practical maliciously secure MPC. We give a rigorous account of three pillars: (i) **Yao's garbled circuits**, which reduce two-party computation to symmetric cryptography via encrypted truth tables and oblivious transfer; (ii) the **GMW protocol**, which evaluates boolean circuits over secret shares — XOR gates for free, AND gates via oblivious transfer — and whose *compiler* upgrades semi-honest to malicious security with zero-knowledge proofs of correctness; and (iii) the **SPDZ framework** [4], which achieves malicious security against *n−1* corruptions by authenticating every share with an information-theoretic MAC under a global key no party knows, amortising the cost of malice through an **offline/online** split: input-independent Beaver triples [3] are manufactured by heavyweight preprocessing (homomorphic encryption [4], oblivious transfer [5], or lattice packing [6]) and consumed by a near-free, information-theoretic online phase. We analyse the protocols, proofs, asymptotic costs, the MP-SPDZ ecosystem [7], and remaining limitations.

## 1. Introduction

Consider *n* parties *P₁, …, Pₙ*, each holding a private input *xᵢ*, who wish to compute a public function *f(x₁, …, xₙ) = y* without disclosing anything about their inputs beyond what *y* itself reveals. The archetypal instances — two millionaires comparing fortunes without revealing them, hospitals jointly training a disease classifier without pooling patient records, or banks benchmarking fraud statistics without sharing ledgers — all instantiate the same cryptographic task: **secure function evaluation (SFE)** in the two-party case, **secure multiparty computation (MPC)** in general.

Two orthogonal axes organise the design space of MPC protocols. The first is the **adversarial model**: a *semi-honest* adversary follows the protocol but inspects transcripts, while a *malicious* adversary may deviate arbitrarily. The second is the **corruption threshold**: *honest-majority* protocols tolerate fewer than *n/2* corruptions and exploit the honest remainder; *dishonest-majority* protocols survive *n−1* colluding adversaries but, by a classic result, **cannot guarantee fairness or guaranteed output delivery** — a malicious party can always abort after learning the result.

This paper studies the preprocessing paradigm that made malicious, dishonest-majority MPC practical. The narrative unfolds in three movements:

1. **Yao's garbled circuits (1986).** The first general-purpose protocol for two-party computation: encrypt the circuit, let the evaluator decrypt exactly one row per gate, and use oblivious transfer to bind input labels to the evaluator's private bits [1].
2. **The GMW protocol (1987).** The first completeness theorem for MPC: *any* polynomial-time function admits a secure protocol with honest majority, given trapdoor permutations [2]. Its secret-sharing evaluation — linear gates for free, AND gates through oblivious transfer — remains the template for boolean-circuit MPC.
3. **The SPDZ framework (2012–present).** Damgård, Pastro, Smart, and Zakarias showed that malicious security under dishonest majority could be made *practical* by splitting the protocol into an expensive, input-independent **offline phase** and a cheap, information-theoretic **online phase** [4]. Every shared value carries an information-theoretic MAC under a global key no party knows; Beaver multiplication triples [3] make each multiplication cost one opened pair; and a final batched MAC check converts any cheating into a guaranteed abort. Successive preprocessing backends — somewhat-homomorphic-encryption-based [4], OT-based MASCOT [5], and packed Overdrive/LowGear [6] — each cut offline cost by roughly an order of magnitude, while MP-SPDZ [7] turned the whole family into a single programmable framework.

> **Thesis:** *Malicious security is not a tax paid uniformly across a computation; it is a one-time cost amortised over an input-independent preprocessing phase. Once Beaver triples and MAC-authenticated shares are banked, the online phase of dishonest-majority MPC is information-theoretic, constant-round per layer, and fast enough for real applications.*

## 2. Background

### 2.1 The real/ideal paradigm and security definitions

Modern MPC security is defined by comparison with an **ideal world**: parties send inputs to an incorruptible trusted party that computes *f* and returns outputs. A protocol is secure if, for every real-world adversary, a **simulator** in the ideal world produces an indistinguishable view. Security decomposes into **privacy** (nothing learned beyond prescribed outputs), **correctness** (honest parties output *f* on true inputs, up to abort), and **input independence** (corrupt inputs cannot depend on honest ones).

> **Theorem: (Impossibility of fairness under dishonest majority).** *No protocol securely realises general functionalities with guaranteed output delivery (or even fairness) when a strict majority of parties may be corrupt. A rushing adversary can always abort after receiving its output share but before honest parties reconstruct theirs.*

*Proof sketch.* After receiving the last message, one party can compute the output; if it halts instead of sending the final message, the other party is left without output, and no recovery is possible without a trusted third party. ∎

Consequently, dishonest-majority protocols aim for **security with abort**: either all honest parties receive correct outputs or all detect cheating and abort. SPDZ's MAC check is precisely the mechanism that enforces this.

### 2.2 Secret sharing and linear homomorphisms

**Additive secret sharing** distributes random summands *xᵢ* with Σᵢ xᵢ = *x*; it is perfectly hiding against any *n−1* parties and *linearly homomorphic* (⟦x+y⟧, ⟦c·x⟧ computed locally, no interaction). Only **multiplication** demands communication — which is why Beaver triples are the currency of arithmetic MPC.

### 2.3 Oblivious transfer and OT extension

**Oblivious transfer (OT)** is the workhorse of boolean-circuit MPC. In 1-out-of-2 OT, the sender holds messages *(m₀, m₁)*, the receiver holds a choice bit *b*, and the receiver learns *m_b* while the sender learns nothing about *b* and the receiver learns nothing about *m_{1−b}*. OT is *complete* for secure computation: given OT, general MPC follows. Base OTs need public-key operations, but **OT extension** (Ishai–Kilian–Nissim–Petrank, 2003) bootstraps a few base OTs into millions using only symmetric cryptography — the engine that makes Yao and GMW practical.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Yao's garbled circuits: encrypting computation itself

Yao's 1986 paper, *"How to Generate and Exchange Secrets"* [1], introduced two ideas that reshaped cryptography: the millionaires' problem as a motivating functionality, and — presented orally at FOCS and first written down by Goldreich, Micali, and Wigderson [2] — the **garbled circuit**.

Formally, for a gate *g* with input labels *A⁰, A¹, B⁰, B¹* and output labels *C⁰, C¹*, the garbled table is the random permutation of the four double-encryptions *Enc_{A^i, B^j}(C^{g(i,j)})*.

The protocol is **constant-round**: two messages suffice regardless of circuit depth.

*Malicious garbling.* Plain Yao is secure only against semi-honest adversaries: a malicious garbler can construct an incorrect circuit. The standard remedy is **cut-and-choose**: garble *s* circuits, open *s/2* to verify correctness, evaluate the rest and take a majority. The GMW compiler [2] offers an alternative route: prove in zero knowledge that each garbled table is well-formed — historically expensive, but the conceptual ancestor of all "prove honest behaviour" compilers.

### 4.2 GMW: secret-shared circuits and the malicious compiler

Goldreich, Micali, and Wigderson's STOC 1987 paper, *"How to Play Any Mental Game — A Completeness Theorem for Protocols with Honest Majority"* [2], proved something astonishing: assuming trapdoor permutations exist, **every** polynomial-time functionality admits a secure multiparty protocol with honest majority.

- **Input sharing:** each party distributes XOR-shares of its input bits.
- **XOR gates:** computed *locally* — party *i* sets its output share to *aᵢ ⊕ bᵢ*. XOR is free. **NOT gates** likewise.
- **AND gates:** the expensive case, reduced to oblivious transfers. With ⟦a⟧, ⟦b⟧ shared over 𝔽₂, the product *(Σaᵢ)(Σbⱼ) = Σᵢ aᵢbᵢ ⊕ Σ_{i≠j} aᵢbⱼ* needs the diagonal terms (local) plus *n(n−1)* cross-terms, each requiring one OT between a pair of parties. Hence GMW's communication is **Θ(n²) per AND gate** — quadratic in the party count, though each OT is cheap via OT extension.

The **GMW compiler** lifts any semi-honest protocol to malicious security: each party commits to its input and randomness, then attaches a zero-knowledge proof to every message attesting correct computation from the committed values.

| Protocol | Security | Threshold | Circuit | Rounds | Comm. per AND/mult |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Yao [1] | semi-honest (malicious via cut-and-choose) | 2-party | boolean | O(1) | O(κ) bits (2 ctexts, half-gates) |
| GMW [2] | semi-honest (malicious via ZK compiler) | honest majority | boolean | O(depth) | O(n²) OTs |
| SPDZ [4] | **malicious, abort** | **dishonest majority (n−1)** | arithmetic | O(depth), cheap online | 1 triple + 2 openings |

### 4.3 Beaver triples: circuit randomisation

Donald Beaver's CRYPTO 1991 paper, *"Efficient Multiparty Protocols Using Circuit Randomization"* [3], introduced the most useful correlated-randomness object in MPC.

1. Locally compute shares of *ε = x − a* and *δ = y − b*.
2. **Open** ε and δ (reconstruct toward all parties — one round, two field elements per party).
3. Each party locally sets its share of the product:
   ⟦x·y⟧ᵢ = ⟦c⟧ᵢ + ε·⟦b⟧ᵢ + δ·⟦a⟧ᵢ + ε·δ·[i = 1]

> **Theorem: (Beaver multiplication correctness and privacy).** *If (a, b, c) is a valid triple independent of (x, y), the protocol above yields a correct sharing of x·y, and the opened values (ε, δ) are uniformly random and independent of (x, y), hence leak nothing.*

*Proof.* Correctness: *xy = (a+ε)(b+δ) = ab + εb + δa + εδ = c + εb + δa + εδ*. Privacy: *ε = x − a* is a one-time-pad encryption of *x* under uniform *a*. ∎

Beaver's insight decouples the *cryptographic* work (generating triples) from the *input-dependent* work (opening two masked values).

### 4.4 SPDZ: malicious security through authenticated shares

The SPDZ protocol of Damgård, Pastro, Smart, and Zakarias (CRYPTO 2012) [4] achieves the strongest standard security notion — **malicious security against up to n−1 corruptions** — with an online phase that is essentially free.

*Authenticated shares.* Every value *x* is shared as *⟨x⟩ = (⟦x⟧, ⟦m⟧)* where *m = α·x* and the MAC key α is secret-shared and **never reconstructed**. Linear operations preserve the MAC relation locally; multiplication uses a Beaver triple whose components are *also* MAC-authenticated under the same α. The invariant is preserved end to end.

*The MAC check.* Opening a value *⟨x⟩* reveals *x* but does **not** yet verify the MAC. Instead, the protocol accumulates all opened values and performs one **batched check** at the end (or before output):

```python
def mac_check(opened, alpha_shares, r):
    # opened: list of (x, m_shares) for values revealed so far
    # r: public random coefficients from coin-tossing
    s = sum(r_i * x_i for r_i, (x_i, _) in zip(r, opened))
    t_shares = [sum(r_i * m_i for r_i, (_, m_i) in zip(r, opened))]
    # each party computes sigma_i = t_i - alpha_i * s, then commits and opens
    sigma = commit_then_open([t_i - a_i * s for t_i, a_i in zip(t_shares, alpha_shares)])
    assert sigma == 0, "MAC check failed: abort"
```

> **Theorem: (SPDZ MAC soundness).** *If any opened value carried an invalid MAC (m ≠ α·x), the batched check passes with probability at most 2^{−σ}, where σ is the statistical security parameter, provided the coefficients r are sampled after the adversary's messages are fixed.*

*Proof sketch.* The check verifies Σᵢ rᵢ(mᵢ − αxᵢ) = 0. If some pair has *Δᵢ = mᵢ − αxᵢ ≠ 0*, the sum is a non-trivial linear form in the random *rᵢ*, which vanishes with probability ≤ 2^{−σ} over the choice of *r*. The commit-then-open ordering prevents the adversary from choosing its shares after seeing *r*. ∎

This yields **security with abort**: any deviation that corrupts a value is caught with overwhelming probability before outputs are released.

*The offline phase.* Generating authenticated triples is the hard part. The original SPDZ [4] uses **somewhat homomorphic encryption** (SHE, BGV-style): parties encrypt their shares, triples are formed homomorphically under encryption, and distributed decryption with zero-knowledge proofs of correct behaviour yields maliciously secure triples. The cost is steep — public-key operations per triple — which motivated a lineage of faster preprocessing:

| Offline backend | Technique | Reference |
|---|---|---|
| SPDZ [4] | Somewhat homomorphic encryption + ZK proofs | CRYPTO 2012 |
| SPDZ-2 | Covert/active key generation, improved online | CCS 2013 |
| MASCOT [5] | Oblivious transfer (no public-key per triple) | CCS 2016 |
| Overdrive / LowGear [6] | Packed SHE with improved ZK | EUROCRYPT 2018 |
| SPDZ2k | Arithmetic over rings ℤ_{2ᵏ} (CPU-native) | CRYPTO 2018 |

MASCOT [5] replaced homomorphic encryption with OT-based triple generation and reported roughly an order-of-magnitude improvement in preprocessing throughput; Overdrive [6] reclaimed the SHE approach with lattice packing and amortised proofs, pushing the frontier further.

### 4.5 From theory to framework: MP-SPDZ

Marcel Keller's **MP-SPDZ** (CCS 2020) [7] is the engineering culmination of the preprocessing paradigm: a fork of the SPDZ-2 codebase extended to **30+ protocol variants** — honest and dishonest majority, semi-honest and malicious, boolean and arithmetic circuits — behind a single Python-embedded compiler emitting bytecode for a common virtual machine.

## 5. Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 Asymptotic cost model

Let *|C|* be the circuit size (number of multiplication/AND gates), *n* the party count, κ the computational and σ the statistical security parameter. The table below summarises per-gate online costs; offline costs are stated separately because they are input-independent and amortisable.

| Protocol | Online comm. per mult/AND | Online rounds | Offline cost per triple |
|---|---|---|---|
| Yao (half-gates) [1] | 2κ bits (garbler → evaluator) | O(1) total | — (garbling is the cost) |
| GMW (OT-based) [2] | O(n²) OTs | O(depth(C)) | OT extension (amortised ~hashes) |
| SPDZ [4] | 2 field elements opened per party | 1 round per mult layer | SHE ops (expensive) |
| SPDZ+MASCOT [5] | 2 field elements opened per party | 1 round per mult layer | OTs (∼10× cheaper than [4]) |
| SPDZ+Overdrive [6] | 2 field elements opened per party | 1 round per mult layer | packed SHE (further ∼order of magnitude) |

Three observations follow. **First**, the SPDZ online phase is *information-theoretic* and minimal: each multiplication layer costs one broadcast round of two masked field elements per party — independent of κ. **Second**, communication grows linearly in *n*, in contrast to GMW's quadratic pairwise OTs. **Third**, the offline phase dominates total cost, which is why the literature's empirical effort concentrates there: the MASCOT and Overdrive papers are, in effect, triple-factory benchmarks [5, 6], and MP-SPDZ's contribution is making the comparison apples-to-apples across 30+ variants [7].

### 5.2 Security proof structure (SPDZ online phase)

The proof follows the real/ideal paradigm: the simulator extracts corrupt inputs from committed shares, fabricates each multiplication's opened *(ε, δ)* as uniform random values (indistinguishable since *a, b* are uniform and hidden), performs the MAC check honestly (cheating causes abort except with probability 2^{−σ}), and forwards the ideal functionality's output on success — the adversary's view being independent of α. The offline phase is proved separately as a secure realisation of the triple-generation functionality and composed via the UC theorem. This modularity — *prove the triple factory once, get every circuit for free* — is the methodological payoff of the preprocessing paradigm.

### 5.3 Garbled-circuit optimisations: quantitative impact

The garbled-circuit line has its own empirical success story, worth recording because it competes with SPDZ in the two-party setting. Successive optimisations cut per-AND-gate communication from 4κ bits (classical Yao) to 2κ (half-gates), with XOR gates free. Combined with OT extension, modern implementations garble and evaluate **millions of AND gates per second** on commodity hardware [8]. For two parties on high-latency networks, Yao's constant-round profile frequently beats SPDZ's round-per-layer online phase — a reminder that "best protocol" is a function of the network, not just the cryptography.

## 6. Limitations

**No fairness or guaranteed output in dishonest majority.** The impossibility result of Section 2.1 is absolute: SPDZ guarantees *abort*, not delivery. For auctions or contract signing where fairness matters, honest-majority protocols (or trusted hardware / blockchains as fairness anchors) are required.

**Preprocessing remains expensive.** The online phase is cheap only because the offline phase is not: generating millions of authenticated triples for large circuits can take hours and gigabytes of communication even with MASCOT/Overdrive backends [5, 6]. Silent and function-dependent preprocessing are active research aimed at this bottleneck.

**Network dependence.** SPDZ's online phase needs one broadcast round per multiplication layer; on high-latency WANs, round complexity dominates, and Yao's constant-round garbling [1] or BMR-style multi-party garbling may win despite higher bandwidth. GMW's *O(depth)* rounds with quadratic communication make it the weakest of the three on slow networks [2].

**Covert vs. malicious gaps in deployment.** Several deployed "SPDZ-like" systems use *covertly secure* key generation (cheating detected only with some probability) for performance. The security downgrade is rarely surfaced to application developers — a genuine hazard in the MP-SPDZ/SCALE-MAMBA ecosystem [7].

**Side channels and implementation fragility.** The proofs assume idealised primitives; real implementations must resist timing attacks on OT extension, never reuse randomness across triples, and ensure the MAC key α is never reconstructed — a single debugging log of α collapses all security. The MAC check's commit-then-open ordering is load-bearing; implementations that open directly admit selective-failure attacks.

**Arithmetic model mismatch.** SPDZ natively computes over finite fields or rings ℤ_{2ᵏ}; floating-point machine learning must be quantised or emulated, and comparisons/bit-decompositions require expensive bit-level subprotocols (edaBits). GMW/Yao handle boolean logic natively but pay per-bit for arithmetic [1, 2].

## 7. Conclusion

Forty years separate Yao's FOCS 1986 talk [1] from the MP-SPDZ framework [7], yet the trajectory is a single coherent argument. Yao showed that computation itself could be encrypted, reducing two-party security to symmetric cryptography and oblivious transfer. Goldreich, Micali, and Wigderson showed that *any* functionality admits a secure protocol, and gave the compiler that turns honest behaviour into *provable* honest behaviour [2]. Beaver showed that the expensive part of MPC — multiplication — could be front-loaded into input-independent correlated randomness [3]. And Damgård, Pastro, Smart, and Zakarias showed that by authenticating every share under an unknown global key and checking once at the end, malicious security under dishonest majority could be bought almost entirely in the preprocessing phase [4]. MASCOT [5], Overdrive [6], and the MP-SPDZ lineage [7] have since steadily collapsed the cost of that preprocessing.

The preprocessing paradigm is now the default architecture for practical malicious MPC: *bank correlated randomness with heavy cryptography when idle; spend it with information-theoretic operations when the inputs arrive.* Its limits — abort instead of fairness, preprocessing cost, round complexity on slow networks — define the research frontier: silent preprocessing, function-dependent triples, and maliciously secure constant-round protocols. The lesson of the arc from Yao to SPDZ is that in secure computation, the deepest wins come not from faster primitives but from restructuring *when* the work is done.
