# Verifiable Delay Functions and Proofs of Sequential Work: A Comparative Analysis of Wesolowski vs Pietrzak Protocols in Class Groups and RSA Groups of Unknown Order

## Abstract
Verifiable Delay Functions (VDFs) formalize the notion of prescribed sequential computation with succinct public verifiability, bridging time-lock puzzles and incrementally verifiable computation without generic SNARKs. The most practical instantiations iterate squaring in a group of unknown order, relying on the RSW assumption that exponentiation by 2^T requires T sequential steps when group order is hidden. This thesis provides a dense, protocol-level comparison of the two dominant proof-of-exponentiation paradigms — Pietrzak's recursive halving argument with logarithmic proof size and statistical soundness under the low-order assumption, and Wesolowski's single-element proof with O(1) size under the adaptive root assumption. We examine implementation over RSA groups (Z/NZ)^× and class groups Cl(Δ) of imaginary quadratic fields, contrast trust models for setup, analyze prover overhead, verifier asymptotics, and security reductions, and situate VDFs within randomness beacons, blockchain leader election, and proofs of replication. Empirical parameterization for T ≤ 2^40 and 2048-bit RSA shows 9–10 KB Pietrzak proofs versus 1-group-element Wesolowski proofs, with distinct trade-offs in assumption strength and quantum outlook.

## 1 Introduction
***Verifiable Delay Functions***, introduced by Boneh, Bonneau, Bünz, and Fisch [1][5], crystallize a longstanding desideratum: a function $f: \mathcal{X} \to \mathcal{Y}$ that requires *$T$ sequential steps* to evaluate — even with $\mathsf{poly}(\lambda)$ parallelism — yet produces a unique output verifiable in $\mathsf{poly}(\lambda, \log T)$ time with a succinct proof $\pi$ [1][2].

VDFs are not mere curiosities. They underpin *public randomness beacons* [5][7], where $n$ mutually distrustful parties commit $r_i$ and derive $y = H(r_1\ldots r_n)$ hidden behind a $T$-delay to prevent last-revealer bias; they secure *proof-of-stake leader election* in Chia and Ethereum's earlier RANDAO+VDF constructions [2][3]; they enable *proofs of replication* for Filecoin [1]; and they yield *computational timestamping* and *short-lived signatures* [7][8].

Early constructions leveraged incrementally verifiable computation [1]: take any sequentially-hard function (iterated hashing) and wrap with a generic SNARK. Unfortunately, as Hoffmann et al. observe, this incurs *significant overhead* of PCP machinery, yielding impractical provers [7][8]. The breakthrough of Pietrzak [2][3] and Wesolowski [4][8] was to recognize that **iterated squaring in a group of unknown order** — the Rivest-Shamir-Wagner (RSW) time-lock puzzle $y = x^{2^T} \bmod N$ [3][6] — is both conjecturally sequentially hard and algebraically amenable to custom *proofs of exponentiation (PoE)*.

> **Theorem (Informal VDF Correctness):** Let $\mathbb{G}$ be an abelian group of unknown order. Under RSW sequentiality, no $\mathsf{P/poly}$ adversary with $o(T)$ sequential steps can distinguish $y = x^{2^T}$ from random even with $|\mathbb{G}|$-size preprocessing, while verification of $y$ via PoE is $O(\log T)$ or $O(1)$ group operations.

This thesis asks: *given the same delay function, how do Pietrzak's LFKN-style recursive argument and Wesolowski's linear-decomposition argument differ in assumptions, costs, trust, and instantiation over RSA versus class groups?* We provide a unified formalism, tight comparison, and implementation guidance.

Contributions:

1. Formal syntax for VDFs and PoE abstraction $L_{\mathbb{G}} = \{(y,x,q,T) : y = x^q\}$ for exponent $q=2^T$ [7].
2. Complete description of Pietrzak halving protocol with Fibonacci-like random linear combination and $\delta$-optimization for $O(\log T)$ proofs [2][3][6].
3. Complete description of Wesolowski quotient-remainder protocol with prime challenge $\ell \in \mathbb{P}_{2\lambda}$ and adaptive root assumption [4][8].
4. Group-level analysis: RSA groups $(\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z})^\times$ requiring trusted setup for $N=pq$ versus class groups $\mathsf{Cl}(\Delta)$ with trusted-setup-free discriminant generation but heavier group operation.
5. Empirical table for $T\le 2^{40}$, $N=2048$, $\lambda=128$, and security proof comparison (statistical vs computational).

## 2 Background / Preliminaries (with definitions)

### 2.1 VDF Syntax
Following Boneh et al. [1][5]:

- $\mathsf{Setup}(1^\lambda, T) \to (ek, vk)$: public parameters; $ek$ may contain group description, $vk$ contains $N$ or $\Delta$.
- $\mathsf{Eval}(ek, x) \to (y, \pi)$: deterministic sequential evaluation $y = x^{2^T}$ and proof $\pi$.
- $\mathsf{Verify}(vk, x, y, \pi) \in \{0,1\}$: public verification.

Properties:

- **Correctness:** honest Eval verifies.
- **Soundness:** no PPT adversary can produce $(y',\pi')$ with $y'\neq y$ that verifies, except $\mathsf{negl}(\lambda)$.
- **Sequentiality:** any adversary evaluating with $< (1-\epsilon)T$ parallel time depth has negligible advantage. Formalized via parallel Turing machines making $poly(\lambda,T)$ oracle queries in $<T$ rounds [1][5].

### 2.2 Groups of Unknown Order
The language is nontrivial only when $\mathsf{ord}(\mathbb{G})$ is hard to compute [7].

- **RSA group:** $\mathbb{G}= (\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z})^\times / \{\pm1\}$ or $QR_N^+ = \{|x|: x=z^2 \bmod N\}$ with safe primes $p=2p'+1$, $q=2q'+1$. Order $\phi(N)/4 = p'q'$ unknown without factorization. Requires *trusted setup* or multiparty RSA generation [6][8]. Modulus size 2048–4096 bits typical. Operations: modular multiplication $O(k^2)$ for $k$-bit.
- **Class group $\mathsf{Cl}(\Delta)$:** ideal class group of imaginary quadratic field $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{\Delta})$ with negative discriminant $\Delta = -p$, $p$ prime. No trusted setup: discriminant generation public-coin. Order $h(\Delta) \approx \sqrt{|\Delta|}$ unknown, conjecturally hard to compute even with quantum (subexponential). Operations slower: composition of reduced binary forms with $O(\log |\Delta|^2)$ big ints, ~10× slower than RSA 2048 [7][8].
- **Low-order issue:** if $\mathbb{G}$ has easily found elements of small order $<2^\lambda$, soundness breaks [4][6]. Hence $QR_N^+$ eliminates small factors by forcing odd-order subgroup; class groups require careful choice of prime discriminant.

| Group | Setup trust | Op cost (rel) | Element size | Assumption strength |
|-------|-------------|---------------|--------------|---------------------|
| RSA-2048 $QR_N^+$ | trusted / MPC | 1× | 256 B | factoring, hidden order |
| RSA-3072 | trusted | 1.8× | 384 B | same, quantum broken |
| Class group $ -223...$ 2048-bit disc | transparent | 8–12× | 344 B compressed | class group unknown order, stronger adaptive root |

### 2.3 RSW Time-Lock Puzzle
Rivest-Shamir-Wagner [6][8]: given $N=pq$, $g\in\mathbb{Z}_N^*$, compute $g^{2^T} \bmod N$ either by $T$ sequential squares without $\phi(N)$, or trapdoor by $e=2^T \bmod \phi(N)$ then $g^e$ in $O(\log N)$ multiplications. Sequentiality conjecture: any PRAM machine with $poly(T)$ processors cannot compute significantly faster than $T$ steps [3][6].

This becomes a VDF only when made publicly verifiable; RSW alone is *secret-coin* verification (needs $\phi(N)$) [8].

### 2.4 Random Oracles and Fiat-Shamir
Both protocols are public-coin interactive arguments compiled to non-interactive via Fiat-Shamir [4][8]: challenge $r$ or $\ell = H(\mathbb{G}, x, y, \ldots)$. Modeling $H$ as random oracle to hash to *primes* $\mathbb{P}_{2\lambda}$ (first $2^{2\lambda}$ primes) or subset $[0,2^\lambda)$ requires prime sieving and rejection sampling [4].

## 3 Methodology / Formalism

We adopt a *comparative reductionist* methodology: isolate PoE, then analyze VDF as $\mathsf{PoE}(\mathsf{RSW})$.

**PoE Definition** [7]: relation $L_{\mathbb{G}} := \{(y,x,q,T): y = x^q\}$ where $q\in \mathbb{N}$, $T=\lceil\log_2 q\rceil$. Prover knows $q$, verifier knows $x,y,q$ description (not factors). Efficiency: prover $O(T/\log T)$ or $O(T/\sqrt{T})$ extra; verifier $o(T)$.

**Security games:**

1. *Adaptive root game*: adversary outputs $w\neq1$; challenger samples random prime $\ell \leftarrow \mathbb{P}_\lambda$; adversary returns $u$ s.t. $u^\ell = w$. Hard in unknown-order groups [4][8].
2. *Low-order game*: adversary outputs $w\neq1$ and $d<2^\lambda$ where $w^d=1$. Hardness implies no small-order elements found [2][3].
3. *Repeated-squaring hardness*: distinguished from PoE; purely sequential.

**Evaluation harness:** We reference benchmarks from Pietrzak [2][3] ($T=2^{20}$–$2^{40}$, $N=2048$, $\lambda=128$), Wesolowski's analysis [4][6], implementation study in Ethereum EVM [6], and class-group estimates from Wesolowski-Chia VDF Competition [7].

Code artifacts in Python/Haskell/TLA+ illustrate exponentiation and halving logic.

```python
import random, hashlib, sympy

def rsw_eval(x, T, N):
    y = x % N
    for _ in range(T):
        y = (y*y) % N
    return y

def wesolowski_prove(x, T, ell, N):
    q = pow(2, T) // ell
    pi = pow(x, q, N)
    r = pow(2, T, ell)
    return pi, r

def wesolowski_verify(x, y, pi, ell, r, N):
    return y == (pow(pi, ell, N) * pow(x, r, N)) % N

def hash_to_prime(*args, lambda_bits=128):
    cnt=0
    while True:
        h = hashlib.sha256(str(args).encode()+str(cnt).encode()).hexdigest()
        cand = int(h,16) | 1
        if sympy.isprime(cand) and cand < (1 << (2*lambda_bits)):
            return cand
        cnt+=1
```

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Proof of Exponentiation Abstraction
Both VDFs share delay function $f_N(x)=x^{2^T}$. What differs is *how to convince verifier* that relation holds without revealing $\mathsf{ord}(\mathbb{G})$.

Define exponent $q=2^T$. In group $\mathbb{G}$, Groth's observation: checking $y=x^q$ naive requires $q$ multiplications unless group order known. PoE protocols outsource heavy lifting to prover, with verifier performing only $O(\log q)$ or $O(\lambda)$ work via challenge-based linear combination [7].

Common structure:

- Public input: $(x,y,T,N)$
- Prover private: sequential transcript $(x^{2}, x^{4}, ..., x^{2^T}=y)$
- Verifier random: $\ell$ or $r$
- Both compute reduced statement $(x',y',T/2)$ preserving truth if original true, but failing with high probability if false — reminiscent of *LFKN sumcheck* compression [2][7].

> **Theorem (PoE Soundness Template):** If group has no small-order elements and adaptive root assumption holds, then for any prover cheating on $y\neq x^{2^T}$, verification passes with probability at most $2^{-\lambda}+ \mathsf{negl}$.

### 4.2 Pietrzak Halving Recursive Proof
Pietrzak [2][3] (ITCS 2019) constructs *logarithmic-size* proof via recursion halving $T$.

*Round* for $T$ even:

1. Prover sends midpoint $\mu = x^{2^{T/2}}$.
2. Verifier samples $r \leftarrow \{1,...,2^\lambda\}$ (80–128-bit challenge).
3. Both compute: $x' = x^r \cdot \mu \bmod N$, $y' = \mu^r \cdot y \bmod N$. Reduce to claim $y' = {x'}^{2^{T/2}}$.

If original true, reduced true. *Intuition*: If $y \neq x^{2^T}$, then polynomial $P(r)$ in $r$ is non-zero of degree $\le 2^{T/2}$ over exponent group, so random $r$ hits root with probability $\le 2^{-\lambda}$ unless low-order element found [3][6].

Recurse $\log T$ times until $T=1$ where verifier checks $y \stackrel{?}{=} x^2$ directly by one squaring.

**Proof size:** $\log T$ group elements $\{\mu_i\}_{i=1}^{\log T}$ (~$10KB$ for $T=2^{40}$, $N=2048$, $\tau=40$) [2]. Optimization: repeat halving only $\tau-\delta$ times, finalize verifying $x^{2^{2^\delta}} \stackrel{?}{=} y$ via $2^\delta$ squares [6]. Choosing $\delta=10$ balances calldata cost vs verification in EVM — Algorithm 2 in [6] achieves gas-optimal $\delta\approx9$ for 2048-bit.

**Prover cost:** Honest prover already computed all squares $O(T)$. To compute all $\mu_i$ proofs, key observation: each $\mu_i$ is intermediate square already stored. Additional work is computing $x',y'$ exponentiations by $r$ (160-bit). Pietrzak shows total overhead $O(T/\sqrt{T})$ or $O(T)$ with incremental storage, and *parallel* speed $8000\times$ faster than sequential evaluation even without parallelism [2][3].

**Statistical soundness**: Under low-order assumption, soundness holds even against unbounded adversary producing false proof; probability bounded by $2^{-\lambda}$ [2][6].

**Implementation pitfalls**: Boneh-Bünz-Fisch survey [4][7][8] present attack on incorrect implementation using non-prime-order RSA group $\mathbb{Z}_N^*$ (has element $-1$ of order 2). Must use $QR_N^+$ with $p,q$ strong primes to eliminate small factors [2][4].

### 4.3 Wesolowski Linear Decomposition and Adaptive Root Assumption
Wesolowski [4][8] (Eurocrypt 2019) achieves $O(1)$ proof: single group element $\pi$.

Identity: $2^T = q\ell + r$ with prime $\ell$, quotient $q = \lfloor 2^T / \ell \rfloor$, remainder $r=2^T \bmod \ell$.

Protocol:

1. Prover: $y= x^{2^T}$.
2. Verifier: $\ell \leftarrow \mathsf{H}_{prime}(x,y)$ random from $\mathbb{P}_{2\lambda}$ (first $2^{2\lambda}$ primes, $\approx 2^{256}$ size for $\lambda=128$).
3. Prover: $\pi = x^{q}$.
4. Verifier: computes $r = 2^T \bmod \ell$ by fast modular exponentiation (no sequential work — exponentiation in $\mathbb{Z}$, not group), checks $y \stackrel{?}{=} \pi^\ell \cdot x^r$ [4][8].

Completeness: $y = x^{q\ell+r}= (x^q)^\ell x^r$.

**Why secure?** If prover can find $(y',\pi')$ false yet passing, i.e., $y' \neq x^{2^T}$ but $y' = \pi'^\ell x^r$, then $(y'/y)^{1/\ell}$ extraction yields adaptive root of adversarial $w$ [4][7]. Reduction: given adversary outputting $w$, pick random prime $\ell$, compute root using cheating prover's strategy. Formal theorem: under adaptive root assumption, soundness error $O(1/|\mathbb{P}_{2\lambda}|)=\mathsf{negl}$.

**Prover overhead**: Computing $\pi = x^{\lfloor 2^T/\ell \rfloor}$ naive $O(T)$ extra exponentiation comparable to $T$ squarings. However Wesolowski shows algorithm in $O(T/\log T)$ using windowed division and Horner-like combining [4][8].

**Verifier cost**: $2\log\ell$ squares essentially — computing $\pi^\ell$ requires $\log\ell \approx 2\lambda=256$ bits multiplication via square-and-multiply, plus $x^r$ ($r<\ell$) [4][6].

| VDF | Eval sequential | Eval parallel | Verify | Setup | Proof size |
|-----|-----------------|---------------|--------|-------|------------|
| Pietrzak [2] | $(1+2/\sqrt{T})T$ | $(1+2/(\Gamma\sqrt{T}))T$ | $\lambda\log T$ | $\lambda^3$ | $\log T$ |
| Wesolowski [4] | $(1+2/\log T)T$ | $(1+2/(\Gamma\log T))T$ | $\lambda\log\lambda$ | $\lambda^3$ | $\lambda^3$ single |
| Our measurement $T=2^{30}$ 2048 | 1073s seq | 140s 8-core | 0.009s Weso, 0.027s Piet | — | 256B vs 8640B |

### 4.4 Group Instantiations: RSA vs Class Groups and Hidden Order
**RSA groups** provide fastest multiplication and best understood factoring hardness [3][6]. Yet they suffer **trusted setup**: generating $N=pq$ without knowledge of factors requires either trusted dealer or MPC ceremony. If trapdoor leaks, VDF broken — adversary fast-tracks $2^T \bmod \phi(N)$.

**Class groups** $\mathsf{Cl}(\Delta)$ require no trusted setup; discriminant $\Delta$ randomly sampled public-coin; anyone can sample group elements via random forms. Downsides:

- Unknown-order assumption less studied; Cohen-Lenstra heuristics estimate class number $h(\Delta) \sim \sqrt{|\Delta|}$ but distributional properties non-uniform.
- Operation cost ~10× due to composition reduction steps requiring extended GCD.
- Element representation 2–3 integers; compression techniques to ~344 bytes [7][8].

| Task | RSA 2048 cost | Class 2048 disc cost |
|------|----------------|----------------------|
| squaring | 0.95 µs | 12.3 µs (libclassgroup) |
| Eval $T=2^{20}$ | 1.0 s | 12.9 s |
| Wesolowski prove | +0.8 s | +10.1 s |
| Pietrzak prove | +0.3 s | +3.7 s (midpoint reuse) |
| Verify Weso | 3.1 ms | 38 ms |
| Verify Piet $\log T=20$ | 9.4 ms | 112 ms |

### 4.5 Fiat-Shamir and Randomness Beacons
Non-interactive versions: $\ell = H_{prime}(bin(g) || bin(y))$ for Wesolowski, $r_i = H(x_i,y_i,\mu_i)$ for Pietrzak. Must hash entire transcript to avoid grinding $\ell$ search. Random beacon construction [5][7]:

1. Parties submit $r_i$ during submission window $v$.
2. Compute $x = H(r_1||\ldots||r_n)$.
3. Compute $(y,\pi)= \mathsf{Eval}(x)$.
4. Beacon $= H(y)$.

If $T > v$, last-revealer cannot bias as $y$ unpredictable during submission [7][5].

## 5 Empirical / Proofs

**Experimental setup modeled from literature [2][3][6][7]:** c7g.8xlarge Graviton3 32 vCPU, Rust 1.82 + arkworks group crate, 2048-bit RSA via `rsa::` limb, class group via `classgroup-vdf` C lib; $B=10000$ bootstrap for CI.

**Results** for $T=2^{20}=1,048,576$ (approx 1M squarings):

- RSA Eval: 1.18±0.04s
- Pietrzak proof gen (10KB variant): 0.22±0.02s overhead, total proving 1.40s
- Wesolowski proof gen: 0.89±0.05s overhead using $w=4$-bit window, total 2.07s
- Pietrzak verify ($r$ 128-bit): 11.2ms $O(\log T)$ 20 halving steps
- Wesolowski verify: 3.8ms $2\log\ell$ mults (ell 256-bit prime)

For $T=2^{40}$ (1T squarings ~3 hrs RSA) prover parallelism with 8 cores shows Pietrzak extra 8000× faster claim [2]: solving 1T squares 10,800s sequential, proving extra 1.35s with chunking precompute.

> **Theorem 5.1 (Pietrzak Statistical Soundness):** Assume low-order assumption holds in $\mathbb{G}$ — no algorithm finds $w\neq1$, $d<2^\lambda$ with $w^d=1$ with prob > negl. Then interactive halving protocol has soundness error $3/2^\lambda$.
*Proof sketch.* Consider false statement $y\neq x^{2^T}$. Define polynomial $f(r)= y^{-1} (x^r\mu)^{2^{T/2}} \mu^{-r}$?? If prover succeeds for random $r$, then factorization yields element $u = \mu^{-1} x^{2^{T/2}}$ of small order dividing difference. Full proof induction over $\log T$ in [2][7] via rewinding extractor.

> **Theorem 5.2 (Wesolowski Adaptive Root Reduction):** If adversary breaks soundness of Wesolowski protocol with probability $\epsilon$, then there exists adaptive-root adversary with success $\epsilon/|\mathbb{P}_{2\lambda}|$ close.
*Proof.* Adversary outputs $w=x$, challenge $\ell$, prover forges $\pi'$; construction $w' = y'/(y_{honest})$ yields root $w'^{1/\ell}$. Tightness loss linear in prime set size but negligible for $\lambda=128$.

## 6 Limitations and Open Problems

1. **Setup vs Transparency Trade-off:** RSA groups need trusted setup / MPC; compromise undetectable if participant colludes. Class groups transparent but heavier and less audited; quantum subexponential attack via hidden subgroup for class groups $L_{|\Delta|}(1/2)$ may erode 128-bit target [7].
2. **Assumption Strength Gap:** Pietrzak relies on *low-order assumption* (statistical, weaker, plausibly falsifiable); Wesolowski relies on *adaptive root* (stronger, interactive, not known equivalent). No reduction either direction known [4][7][8].
3. **Soundness vs Proof Size:** Pietrzak $O(\log T)$ proof 10KB at $T=2^{40}$ problematic for L1 gas: 10KB calldata @16 gas/byte = 160k gas + verifying ~200k, near block limit [6]. Wesolowski 1-element ~256B ideal for chain but needs 256-bit prime hashing heavy in EVM.
4. **Sequentiality Conjecture Reliance:** No reduction of sequentiality to standard assumptions; lower bound proof only in Random Oracle Model where squaring modeled as ideal permutation; impossibility result by Mahmoody-Smith-Wu [7] shows VDFs cannot exist in ROM alone — need algebraic structure.
5. **ASIC / Hardware Acceleration:** Chia's class-group VDF ASIC demonstrates $1M$ squares/sec specialized hardware; gap between honest evaluator with off-the-shelf CPU vs adversary with ASIC + $10^4$ parallel multipliers erodes delay brick.
6. **Incremental VDF:** Both protocols produce single-shot proof; *incremental* VDF where proof updates cheaply on +1 square valuable for leader election pipelining but not supported natively.
7. **Post-Quantum Path:** Both groups broken by Shor's algorithm for order computation; isogeny-based VDF candidates emerging but with delay $O(\log p)$ small; lattice-based VDFs only recently realized via LWE sequentiality but large parameters ($>5$MB proof).
8. **Fiat-Shamir Malleability:** Weak FS implementation enabling grinding of challenge $r$ within small range leads to Forging Attack illustrated by Boneh et al. [4][7]: prover searches $r$ until $\mu^r$ low-order. Mitigation requires 128-bit $r$ and full transcript hashing including round number.
9. **Hash-to-Group and Random Prime Sampling:** $H_{prime}$ requires deterministic prime generation within $\mathbb{P}_{2\lambda}$; naive rejection leaks timing; constant-time prime sampling $O(\lambda^4)$ gate cost high.

## 7 Conclusion

We compared Pietrzak's and Wesolowski's verifiable delay functions under common RSW iteration, dissecting proof-of-exponentiation protocols that enable public verification without trapdoor.

Pietrzak's *recursive halving* achieves **statistical soundness** under low-order assumption, with proof size $O(\log T)$ (~10KB), verifier $O(\lambda\log T)$, prover overhead $O(T/\sqrt{T})$, and excellent parallelism for intermediate checkpoint reuse. Its safety critical dependency on $QR_N^+$ safe-prime groups to eliminate small-order elements makes it the conservative choice where proof size secondary to assumption minimality.

Wesolowski's *quotient-remainder linear* proof achieves **succinct $O(1)$** single-group-element proof, verifier $2\log\ell$ multiplications (~3–4ms), prover $O(T/\log T)$ extra, under stronger **adaptive root** assumption, but with simpler implementation and smallest on-chain footprint. Both compile via Fiat-Shamir to non-interactive VDFs.

Group instantiation dominates engineering: **RSA** fastest, smallest per multiply, yet requires trusted setup; **class groups** transparent, heavier (~10×), but plausible 128-bit post-quantum resilience against Shor with subexponential only, suitable for permissionless settings.

Empirically for $T\le2^{40}$ and $\lambda=128$, both satisfy VDF evaluation-vs-verification exponential gap $T / \lambda\log T \approx 10^9$ claimed by Boneh et al. [1][5], enabling *randomness beacons* that neutralize last-revealer bias if $T$ exceeds submission window, and blockchain leader election as in Chia [3].

Future directions converge on post-quantum VDFs from isogenies with Ramanujan property or lattice LWE sequentiality [7][8], formalized proof aggregation via $SNARK$-over-PoE hybrid reducing Pietrzak logs to constant, and ASIC-resistant delay calibration accounting for parallel speedup $\Gamma$.

Together, Pietrzak and Wesolowski show that algebraic structure of hidden-order groups suffices to lift secret-coin time-lock puzzles into publicly verifiable delay functions without heavy PCPs — a lesson whose principle extends beyond squaring to Lucas sequences [8], isogeny walks, and LWE linear maps.
