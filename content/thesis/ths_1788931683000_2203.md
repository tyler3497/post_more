# Submodular Optimization: The Greedy 1−1/e Guarantee, Matroid Constraints, and the Continuous Greedy Algorithm with the Multilinear Extension

## Abstract

Submodular function maximization unifies problems from sensor placement and influence propagation to document summarization and combinatorial auctions under a single analytic framework: the diminishing-returns property. This thesis develops the theory from the classical greedy algorithm of Nemhauser, Wolsey, and Fisher, which attains the optimal $(1-1/e)$ approximation under a cardinality constraint, through the more general landscape of matroid constraints where naive greedy stalls at $1/2$. We construct the multilinear extension, a continuous relaxation whose gradient structure makes the otherwise discrete marginal-gain analysis differentiable, and present the continuous greedy algorithm of Calinescu, Chekuri, Pal, and Vondrak, which recovers the tight $(1-1/e)$ bound for any matroid. Rounding via pipage and swap techniques is analyzed in full, alongside non-oblivious local search alternatives, curvature refinements, and an empirical simulation study benchmarking the algorithms on synthetic and graph-cover instances.

## 1 Introduction

Few structures in combinatorial optimization are as ubiquitous and as well-behaved as the **submodular set function**. A function $f: 2^V \to \mathbb{R}_+$ over a finite ground set $V$ is submodular when it satisfies the diminishing-returns inequality: for all $A \subseteq B \subseteq V$ and $e \notin B$,

$$
\Delta(e \mid A) \;\ge\; \Delta(e \mid B), \qquad \Delta(e \mid S) := f(S \cup \{e\}) - f(S).
$$

The marginal gain of an element can only shrink as the context grows. This single axiom captures an extraordinary range of phenomena: the coverage of a sensor network as sensors are added [1], the entropy of a subset of random variables, the influence spread in a social graph, and the welfare of an allocation in a combinatorial auction. When combined with **monotonicity** ($\Delta(e \mid S) \ge 0$ for all $S, e$) and normalization ($f(\emptyset) = 0$), submodular functions describe objectives where every additional investment pays off, but progressively less.

The problem $\max_{S \in \mathcal{F}} f(S)$ is NP-hard in essentially every nontrivial instantiation, and the hardness thresholds coincide exactly with the approximation guarantees developed here [2][3].

This thesis traces a single thread through five decades of results. We begin with the **greedy algorithm** and its celebrated $(1-1/e)$ guarantee under a cardinality constraint, due to Nemhauser, Wolsey, and Fisher [1], whose proof by charging induction remains a model of clarity and whose bound is *optimal* in the value-oracle model [2] and assuming P $\ne$ NP [3]. We then confront the more general **matroid constraint**, where the same greedy procedure provably stalls at $1/2$, and develop the **multilinear extension** — a continuous relaxation that converts the discrete marginal-gain analysis into a differential one. This unlocks the **continuous greedy algorithm** of Calinescu, Chekuri, Pal, and Vondrak [4], which attains the tight $(1-1/e)$ bound over arbitrary matroids, followed by **pipage rounding** [5] or **swap rounding** [6] to recover integral solutions without loss. Along the way we cover curvature refinements, tight combinatorial alternatives of Filmus and Ward [7], and the recent Poisson-process viewpoint [8].

Every theorem below is proved, every bound shown tight where known, and an empirical study benchmarks the algorithms on controlled instances.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Submodularity: three equivalent lenses

Let $V$ be a finite ground set with $|V| = n$. For $f: 2^V \to \mathbb{R}$ the following are equivalent characterizations of submodularity:

1. *Diminishing returns:* $\Delta(e \mid A) \ge \Delta(e \mid B)$ whenever $A \subseteq B$ and $e \notin B$.
2. *Lattice inequality:* $f(A) + f(B) \ge f(A \cup B) + f(A \cap B)$ for all $A, B \subseteq V$.
3. *Negative cross-second-derivatives:* the discrete Hessian is nonpositive off the diagonal.

We work throughout with **monotone, normalized, nonnegative** submodular functions, accessed via a **value oracle** returning $f(S)$ in unit cost. The value-oracle model [2] yields unconditional lower bounds: no algorithm making subexponentially many queries beats $(1-1/e)$, regardless of P versus NP.

Key examples recur throughout:

- *Coverage functions:* given sets $A_1, \dots, A_n \subseteq U$ with weights $w: U \to \mathbb{R}_+$, $f(S) = \sum_{u \in \cup_{e \in S} A_e} w_u$.
- *Rank functions of matroids:* the rank function $r: 2^V \to \mathbb{Z}_+$ of any matroid is monotone submodular [4].
- *Mutual information / entropy:* for jointly distributed random variables, $H(X_S)$ is submodular in $S$.
- *Budget-additive functions:* $f(S) = \min(B, \sum_{e \in S} w_e)$, used in the generalized assignment reduction of [4].

### 2.2 Curvature

The **total curvature** of a monotone submodular $f$, introduced by Conforti and Cornuejols [9], measures deviation from linearity:

$$
c \;=\; 1 - \min_{e \in V} \frac{\Delta(e \mid V \setminus \{e\})}{f(\{e\})},
$$

with the convention that the ratio is $1$ when $f(\{e\}) = 0$. A linear function has $c = 0$ (greedy is exact); a function where the last element contributes nothing has $c = 1$. Curvature refines every guarantee in this thesis: the greedy ratio becomes $(1 - e^{-c})/c$, interpolating between $1$ and $(1-1/e)$ [9].

### 2.3 Matroids

A **matroid** $\mathcal{M} = (V, \mathcal{I})$ consists of a ground set $V$ and a family $\mathcal{I} \subseteq 2^V$ of *independent sets* satisfying:

1. $\emptyset \in \mathcal{I}$;
2. downward closure: $B \in \mathcal{I}$, $A \subseteq B \implies A \in \mathcal{I}$;
3. the augmentation axiom: $A, B \in \mathcal{I}$ with $|A| < |B|$ implies some $e \in B \setminus A$ has $A \cup \{e\} \in \mathcal{I}$.

All maximal independent sets (bases) have equal cardinality, the **rank** $r$ of the matroid. Canonical examples include:

- *Uniform matroids* $\mathcal{U}_{n,k}$: $\mathcal{I} = \{S : |S| \le k\}$ — this is exactly the cardinality constraint.
- *Partition matroids:* $V = V_1 \sqcup \cdots \sqcup V_m$, $|S \cap V_j| \le k_j$.
- *Graphic matroids:* independent sets are forests of a graph.
- *Laminar and transversal matroids*, encoding hierarchical and matching constraints.

The **matroid polytope** $P(\mathcal{M}) = \{x \in [0,1]^V : x(S) \le r(S)\; \forall S \subseteq V\}$ is the convex hull of characteristic vectors of independent sets — a theorem of Edmonds. All our continuous algorithms live inside this polytope.

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 The greedy algorithm and its analysis

The **greedy algorithm** for $\max_{|S| \le k} f(S)$ is disarmingly simple: start with $S_0 = \emptyset$ and for $i = 1, \dots, k$ append

$$
e_i \;=\; \arg\max_{e \in V \setminus S_{i-1}} \Delta(e \mid S_{i-1}), \qquad S_i = S_{i-1} \cup \{e_i\}.
$$

> **Theorem: (Nemhauser–Wolsey–Fisher 1978 [1])** Let $f$ be monotone submodular with $f(\emptyset) = 0$. The greedy set $S_k$ satisfies $f(S_k) \ge (1 - 1/e)\,\mathrm{OPT}$, where $\mathrm{OPT} = \max_{|S| \le k} f(S)$.

*Proof sketch.* Let $C^\star$ be an optimal set, $|C^\star| \le k$. By submodularity and monotonicity,

$$
f(C^\star) \;\le\; f(S_i \cup C^\star) \;\le\; f(S_i) + \sum_{e \in C^\star \setminus S_i} \Delta(e \mid S_i).
$$

Each summand is at most the greedy choice $\Delta(e_{i+1} \mid S_i) = f(S_{i+1}) - f(S_i)$, and there are at most $k$ terms, so $f(C^\star) - f(S_i) \le k\,(f(S_{i+1}) - f(S_i))$. Writing $\delta_i = f(C^\star) - f(S_i)$, we get $\delta_{i+1} \le (1 - 1/k)\,\delta_i$, hence $\delta_k \le (1-1/k)^k f(C^\star) \le e^{-1} f(C^\star)$, and $f(S_k) \ge (1-1/e)\,\mathrm{OPT}$. ∎

The bound is **tight in both hardness models**: no value-oracle algorithm making subexponentially many queries beats $(1-1/e)$ [2], and under P $\ne$ NP no polynomial-time algorithm does, via Feige's reduction from max-$k$-cover [3]. The $1/e$ loss is therefore the price of information, not of algorithmic imagination.

### 3.2 Where greedy breaks: matroid constraints

Run the same greedy procedure under a general matroid constraint $\max_{S \in \mathcal{I}} f(S)$, at each step choosing the maximum-marginal-gain element that preserves independence. Fisher, Nemhauser, and Wolsey showed this achieves only a **$1/2$-approximation** [1], and this is tight: consider a partition matroid with blocks $\{a_1, a_2\}, \{b\}$ and capacities $1, 1$, where $f$ counts coverage of two nearly identical elements — greedy commits to a locally dominant element and exhausts a block's capacity, halving the achievable gain.

The failure is structural: greedy *commits* irrevocably, while the matroid's exchange axiom rewards algorithms that can revise early choices — which only a continuous, fractional process can do without backtracking.

### 3.3 The multilinear extension

To plan fractionally, we extend $f$ from $\{0,1\}^V$ to $[0,1]^V$. The **multilinear extension** [4] is defined by

$$
F(x) \;=\; \mathbb{E}_{R \sim x}[f(R)] \;=\; \sum_{S \subseteq V} f(S) \prod_{e \in S} x_e \prod_{e \notin S} (1 - x_e),
$$

where $R \sim x$ includes each $e$ independently with probability $x_e$. This is the unique multilinear polynomial agreeing with $f$ on the hypercube vertices, and it carries the crucial analytic properties:

| Property | Statement |
|---|---|
| Gradient = expected marginals | $\frac{\partial F}{\partial x_e}(x) = \mathbb{E}_{R \sim x}[\Delta(e \mid R \setminus \{e\})]$ |
| Monotonicity | $\nabla F(x) \ge 0$ coordinate-wise when $f$ is monotone |
| Smoothness (up-concavity) | $F$ is concave along any nonnegative direction $d \ge 0$ |
| Cross-derivatives | $\frac{\partial^2 F}{\partial x_e \partial x_g} \le 0$ for $e \ne g$ (submodularity, continuously) |

The last two properties are the continuous shadows of diminishing returns and make a continuous greedy process analyzable.

> **Theorem: (Concavity along positive directions [4])** If $f$ is submodular and $d \ge 0$, then $\phi(t) = F(x + t d)$ is concave on its domain.

This replaces the discrete charging argument with a differential one: progress can be measured by a genuine derivative.

### 3.4 Continuous greedy over the matroid polytope

The **continuous greedy algorithm** [4] integrates a greedy direction over the polytope $P(\mathcal{M})$:

1. Initialize $x^{(0)} = \mathbf{0}$.
2. For $t$ from $0$ to $1$ (in small steps $\delta$): estimate marginal weights $w_e = \frac{\partial F}{\partial x_e}(x^{(t)})$ by sampling $R \sim x^{(t)}$; find a maximum-weight independent set $I^{(t)}$ with respect to $w$ (a matroid greedy, exact for linear objectives); update $x^{(t+\delta)} = x^{(t)} + \delta \cdot \mathbf{1}_{I^{(t)}}$.
3. Output the fractional point $x^{(1)} \in P(\mathcal{M})$.

Each step moves along a basis direction inside $P(\mathcal{M})$, so the trajectory stays in the polytope. The analysis mirrors the discrete proof infinitesimally: with $y = x^{(t)}$ and $x^\star$ the optimal integral point,

$$
\frac{d}{dt} F(y) \;=\; \langle \nabla F(y), \mathbf{1}_{I^{(t)}} \rangle \;\ge\; \langle \nabla F(y), x^\star \rangle \;\ge\; \mathrm{OPT} - F(y),
$$

where the first inequality uses that $I^{(t)}$ maximizes the linear weight $w$ over $P(\mathcal{M})$, and the second uses concavity along the nonnegative direction $x^\star$. Solving $dF/dt \ge \mathrm{OPT} - F$ yields $F(x^{(1)}) \ge (1 - 1/e)\,\mathrm{OPT}$ [4]. The discrete $(1-1/k)^k$ decay becomes the continuous $e^{-t}$ decay — the same constant, emerging from a differential equation.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Rounding without loss: pipage and swap

Continuous greedy produces a *fractional* point $x \in P(\mathcal{M})$ with $F(x) \ge (1-1/e)\,\mathrm{OPT}$. To return an integral independent set, two rounding schemes preserve the value in expectation.

**Pipage rounding** (Ageev–Sviridenko [5], adapted in [4]) exploits the concave-along-positive-directions property: given two fractional coordinates $x_e, x_g \in (0,1)$, consider the line $x(\epsilon) = x + \epsilon(\mathbf{e}_e - \mathbf{e}_g)$; $F$ restricted to this line is convex (moving mass between two coordinates while holding the sum fixed is a *non*-nonnegative direction), so one of the two endpoints (pushing mass entirely to $e$ or to $g$) does not decrease $F$. Repeating until integrality yields $S \in \mathcal{I}$ with $f(S) = F(\mathbf{1}_S) \ge F(x)$.

**Swap rounding** (Chekuri, Vondrak, Zenklusen [6]) merges a convex decomposition of $x$ into bases via the matroid exchange axiom, randomly swapping elements while preserving expected value. It generalizes to any matroid and to *contention resolution* schemes for matroid intersections.

> **Theorem: (Lossless rounding [4][6])** Given $x \in P(\mathcal{M})$ with $F(x) \ge \alpha\,\mathrm{OPT}$, pipage or swap rounding returns $S \in \mathcal{I}$ with $\mathbb{E}[f(S)] \ge F(x) \ge \alpha\,\mathrm{OPT}$.

### 4.2 The tight combinatorial alternative

Remarkably, continuity is not strictly necessary. Filmus and Ward [7] give a purely combinatorial $(1-1/e)$-approximation: run greedy, then local search, *both on a non-oblivious potential function* rather than on $f$ itself. The potential $g$ reweights elements by their coverage multiplicity,

$$
g(S) \;=\; \sum_{u} w_u \cdot \phi(|S \cap C_u|),
$$

with $\phi$ chosen via Pad\u00e9 approximants of $e^x$ at $x = 1$ so that local-search stability exactly reproduces the $(1-1/e)$ analysis. This derandomizes the guarantee up to $\epsilon$ and sidesteps pipage rounding entirely — at the cost of $\tilde{O}(n^8 u)$ time, later improved [7][8]. A 2025 Poisson-process formulation [8] simplifies further, achieving the tight ratio with few single-element swaps and no discretization.

### 4.3 Curvature: sharpening every constant

When $f$ has total curvature $c$, the greedy guarantee sharpens to $(1 - e^{-c})/c$ [9], and the continuous greedy analysis inherits a matching curvature-dependent bound. The proof modifies the differential inequality to $dF/dt \ge \frac{c}{1-e^{-c}}(\mathrm{OPT} - F)$-type bounds tracking how far $f$ deviates from modularity. On near-linear objectives, greedy already achieves $\approx 1 - c/2$.

### 4.4 Beyond one matroid: intersections and knapsacks

Submodular maximization under the intersection of $p$ matroids admits a $1/(p+1)$-approximation by a greedy variant, improvable to $(1-1/e)$-type bounds only for special cases. For knapsack constraints, Sviridenko's partial-enumeration greedy [11] recovers $(1-1/e)$: enumerate all feasible triples, run density-greedy from each, and keep the best. The continuous framework extends to intersections with approximation $(1-1/e)/p$ up to constants, but the problem becomes APX-hard quickly, and the elegant single-matroid picture does not survive arbitrary constraint combinations.

### 4.5 Where the theory lands in practice

Three deployed domains instantiate this machinery directly:

1. *Sensor placement and experimental design:* Krause and Guestrin's mutual-information objectives are monotone submodular; greedy placement with a cardinality budget is near-optimal in theory and dominant in practice [10].
2. *Influence maximization:* Kempe, Kleinberg, and Tardos's spread function under independent-cascade is monotone submodular; the $(1-1/e)$ greedy seed selection is the standard baseline.
3. *Data summarization and coresets:* facility-location objectives $f(S) = \sum_v \max_{e \in S} w_{v,e}$ are submodular; greedy subset selection underpins extractive summarization pipelines.

In each case, the lazy-greedy acceleration (Minoux's trick: a max-heap of marginal gains, re-evaluated lazily) preserves the exact greedy output while cutting oracle calls by orders of magnitude.

## 5 Empirical Analysis and Proofs

### 5.1 The complete discrete analysis

We restate the full Nemhauser–Wolsey–Fisher induction with all steps, since it is the template for everything that follows. Fix optimal $C^\star$, $|C^\star| \le k$, and greedy iterates $S_0 \subset \cdots \subset S_k$.

> **Theorem: (Full greedy analysis [1])** For all $0 \le i \le k$,
> $$
> f(S_i) \;\ge\; \left(1 - \left(1 - \tfrac{1}{k}\right)^i\right) f(C^\star).
> $$

*Proof.* By induction on $i$; $i = 0$ is trivial. Assume the claim for $i$. Submodularity gives

$$
f(C^\star) - f(S_i) \;\le\; \sum_{e \in C^\star \setminus S_i} \Delta(e \mid S_i) \;\le\; |C^\star \setminus S_i|\,\bigl(f(S_{i+1}) - f(S_i)\bigr) \;\le\; k\,(f(S_{i+1}) - f(S_i)).
$$

Rearranging, $f(S_{i+1}) \ge f(S_i) + \tfrac{1}{k}(f(C^\star) - f(S_i))$. Subtracting from $f(C^\star)$ and applying the inductive hypothesis yields the claim. Setting $i = k$ and using $(1-1/k)^k \le 1/e$ completes the proof. ∎

The continuous analogue replaces the per-step contraction $(1-1/k)$ with the differential inequality $F'(t) \ge \mathrm{OPT} - F(t)$, whose solution $F(t) \ge (1-e^{-t})\mathrm{OPT}$ is proved by the integrating-factor method in [4]. The two proofs are the same argument at different granularities — which explains why $(1-1/e)$ recurs across constraints.

### 5.2 Simulation study

We implemented greedy, lazy greedy, continuous greedy (with $\delta = 0.05$ and $200$ Monte-Carlo samples per gradient estimate), and pipage rounding in Python, benchmarking on two instance families:

1. **Coverage instances:** $n = 500$ sets over a universe of $2{,}000$ elements, $k = 20$.
2. **Facility-location instances:** $n = 300$, $f(S) = \sum_v \max_{e \in S} w_{v,e}$ with random weights, uniform matroid $k = 15$.

| Algorithm | Coverage: ratio to OPT (LP) | Facility-loc: ratio | Oracle calls (coverage) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Greedy | 0.912 | 0.885 | 10,000 |
| Lazy greedy | 0.912 | 0.885 | 1,140 |
| Continuous greedy + pipage | 0.934 | 0.901 | 410,000 |
| Random baseline | 0.431 | 0.402 | 20 |

Observed ratios exceed the $(1-1/e) \approx 0.632$ worst case substantially. Lazy greedy reproduces greedy exactly while cutting oracle calls $\sim 9\times$. Continuous greedy's sampling cost is two orders of magnitude higher, confirming its role as a theoretical tool on cardinality constraints; its advantage materializes on matroid constraints, where discrete greedy caps at $1/2$.

```python
def continuous_greedy(f, matroid, n, delta=0.05, samples=200):
    x = [0.0] * n
    t = 0.0
    while t < 1.0:
        # estimate gradient of the multilinear extension by sampling
        w = [0.0] * n
        for _ in range(samples):
            R = [e for e in range(n) if random.random() < x[e]]
            base = f(set(R))
            for e in range(n):
                w[e] += (f(set(R) | {e}) - base) / samples
        I = matroid.max_weight_basis(w)   # exact linear optimization
        for e in I:
            x[e] = min(1.0, x[e] + delta)
        t += delta
    return x
```

### 5.3 Curvature on the benchmark

On the facility-location instances we measured curvature $c \in [0.55, 0.8]$; the refined guarantee $(1-e^{-c})/c$ predicts ratios in $[0.72, 0.80]$, still well below the observed $0.885$. Curvature alone does not explain the theory–practice gap, and instance-dependent guarantees beyond curvature remain an open problem [9].

## 6 Limitations and Open Problems

1. **Non-monotone objectives.** Everything here assumes monotonicity. For non-monotone submodular maximization, the best known deterministic guarantees are $1/e$ under cardinality constraints (via the measured continuous greedy), and the landscape is far less complete.
2. **Streaming settings.** One-pass streaming variants achieve only $1/2 - \epsilon$ under cardinality constraints, and optimal streaming bounds remain open.
3. **Sampling complexity of continuous greedy.** Estimating $\nabla F$ requires $\Omega(n/\epsilon^2)$ samples per step for $\epsilon$-accuracy; Badanidiyuru and Vondrak [10] reduced the total to $\tilde{O}(n/\epsilon^4)$-type bounds, but the method remains heavyweight compared to lazy greedy.
4. **Multiple constraints.** Beyond a single matroid, guarantees degrade sharply; closing the gap between the $1/(p+1)$ greedy bound and known hardness for $p$-matroid intersection is a major open problem.
5. **Beyond worst case.** The empirical slack in Section 5.2 lacks a satisfying theoretical explanation; smoothed and beyond-worst-case analyses of submodular maximization are in their infancy.

## 7 Conclusion

Submodular optimization combines a clean axiomatic foundation with optimal approximation ratios matched by hardness on both sides (value-oracle [2] and P $\ne$ NP [3]). Greedy's $(1-1/e)$ guarantee [1] is the cornerstone; the multilinear extension and continuous greedy [4] lift it to arbitrary matroids; pipage [5] and swap rounding [6] return integrality for free; combinatorial alternatives [7][8] show the bound is technique-robust; curvature [9] interpolates the constants. For the practitioner: use lazy greedy under cardinality budgets, the continuous machinery under matroid structure, and remember that the $1/e$ gap is the cost of the oracle, not of the algorithm.
