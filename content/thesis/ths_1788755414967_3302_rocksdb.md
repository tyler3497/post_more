# Log-Structured Merge Trees at Scale: Write Amplification, Compaction Policies, and NVMe-Aware Tuning in RocksDB

## Abstract

The log-structured merge tree (LSM-tree) of O'Neil et al. (1996) remains the dominant storage-engine architecture for write-intensive key-value workloads three decades later [1]. RocksDB, Meta's industrial-strength implementation, extends the classic design with multi-level leveled compaction, tiered universal compaction, probabilistic Bloom filters, a partitioned block cache, and an extensive tuning surface of over one hundred interdependent options [7]. This thesis develops a rigorous treatment of the RocksDB storage engine: the write path from write-ahead log through memtable to flush; the compaction-theoretic foundations of leveled versus tiered merge policies; a quantitative analysis of write, read, and space amplification as functions of the size ratio $T$ and level count $L$; the mathematics and engineering of Bloom filters and block caching for point-lookup acceleration; and a tuning methodology for modern NVMe-backed deployments where microsecond-scale I/O latency and multi-queue parallelism invalidate classical HDD-era heuristics. Analytic cost models are validated against published benchmark results, and the limits of the LSM paradigm under scan-heavy and transactional workloads are examined [2][4][5][6][8].

---

## 1. Introduction

Key-value engines must absorb writes at memory speed while serving reads with bounded latency from durable media. The LSM-tree resolves this by **deferring structure**: writes are batched in memory, appended sequentially, and merged lazily in the background — yielding near-sequential write throughput at the price of *write amplification* (repeated re-processing of data) and *read amplification* (multi-path reads across overlapping layers).

RocksDB is arguably the most deployed LSM-tree engine in existence, underpinning systems as diverse as Meta's production databases, CockroachDB, TiDB, Kafka Streams state stores, and numerous blockchain clients [7]. It inherits the architectural skeleton of LevelDB while adding column families, multi-threaded compaction, universal (tiered) compaction, dynamic level sizing, tiered storage, and an extensive observability surface. Yet its performance is notoriously sensitive to configuration: the same engine can differ in throughput by an order of magnitude depending on the choice of compaction policy, filter policy, cache sizing, and I/O threading model [2][6].

This thesis makes four contributions. First, it presents the RocksDB write path — write-ahead log (WAL), mutable and immutable memtables, flush, and the level hierarchy — as a coherent pipeline with precise cost accounting. Second, it derives the amplification bounds for leveled and tiered compaction, showing that leveled compaction incurs $O(L \cdot T)$ write amplification while tiered compaction incurs only $O(T)$, at the cost of strictly worse read and space amplification [5]. Third, it analyzes the auxiliary structures that tame read amplification — Bloom filters with their optimal sizing, and the two-level caching hierarchy of block cache plus OS page cache [7][9]. Fourth, it develops a tuning discipline for NVMe solid-state storage, where the rules of the HDD era (minimize random I/O at all costs) give way to a subtler optimization: exploiting massive queue depth and parallelism while respecting flash write endurance.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 The LSM-tree invariant

An LSM-tree maintains data in a hierarchy of geometrically growing components. The in-memory **memtable** absorbs writes; when full, it is flushed as an immutable sorted run, and background **compactions** merge runs downward through levels $L_0, L_1, \dots, L_k$. Each level $i$ holds at most $T^i$ times the data of $L_0$, where $T$ is the *size ratio* (classically 10) [1][6]. The number of levels required for $N$ entries with page size $B$ and $L_0$ capacity $pg$ pages is:

$$L = \left\lceil \log_T \left( \frac{N}{B \cdot pg} \cdot \frac{T-1}{T} \right) \right\rceil \tag{1}$$

[6]. Within a level, the merge policy determines whether sorted runs are kept disjoint (leveling) or allowed to overlap (tiering) [5].

### 2.2 RocksDB in one paragraph

RocksDB organizes a database into *column families*, each with its own LSM-tree, WAL, and option set. Writes enter the WAL and the active memtable — a concurrent skiplist — simultaneously: the WAL provides crash durability, the memtable provides read-your-writes visibility. When the memtable reaches `write_buffer_size` (default 64 MB), it is frozen, a fresh memtable is installed, and a background flush serializes it into SST files in $L_0$; compactions then propagate data downward. SST files are partitioned into data blocks (default 4 KB, commonly 16–32 KB in production), index blocks, and filter blocks [7][9].

### 2.3 The three amplifications

Every LSM-tree analysis revolves around three dimensionless costs [5][6][8]:

- **Write amplification** ($W_{amp}$): bytes written to storage per byte of user data inserted.
- **Read amplification** ($R_{amp}$): storage blocks (or files) consulted per logical read.
- **Space amplification** ($S_{amp}$): physical bytes occupied per byte of live logical data.

No design minimizes all three simultaneously; the merge policy chooses the trade-off.

## 3. Methodology

Our analysis proceeds on two complementary tracks: *analytic cost modeling* and *empirical validation*.

**Analytic track.** We adopt the steady-state model of the LSM cost literature: leveling is assumed, the tree is full, and insert volume equals delete volume [6]. The Lethe analysis gives the sharp characterization $w_{amp} = \frac{\text{csize}(N^+) - \text{csize}(N)}{\text{csize}(N)}$, yielding $O(L \cdot T)$ for leveling and $O(T)$ for tiering [5]. Read-amplification bounds follow from the count of sorted runs a lookup must consult, moderated by Bloom-filter false-positive probabilities.

**Empirical track.** We ground the models in RocksDB's tuning documentation — bloom filter sizing, block-cache defaults and sharding, compaction threading [7][9] — and in published benchmarks: Autumn's YCSB evaluation against RocksDB and LevelDB [8], the vLSM study of I/O amplification versus tail latency [4], and ArceKV's parameter studies spanning buffers 16–128 MB, caches 128 MB–4 GB, and entries 128 B–2 KB [2]. Numeric predictions come from the analytic models, cross-checked against the order of magnitude of published measurements.

The §4.5 tuning recommendations are validated against NVMe engineering constraints — deep queues, microsecond reads, finite endurance — that qualitatively change which costs dominate.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 The write path: WAL, memtable, and flush

Every `Put`, `Delete`, or `Merge` in RocksDB traverses an identical write pipeline:

1. **WAL append.** The operation is appended to the active WAL segment before becoming visible. WAL writes are sequential and batched; `bytes_per_sync` controls sync frequency, and `manual_wal_flush` amortizes sync cost across commits.
2. **Memtable insert.** The key-value pair, tagged with a monotonically increasing sequence number, is inserted into the active memtable — a lock-free concurrent skiplist with $O(\log n)$ insertion. The sequence number establishes a total order across memtable and SST files, resolving which version of a key is newest during reads and compactions.
3. **Memtable switch and flush.** At `write_buffer_size`, the memtable is frozen as *immutable* and a fresh one installed (`max_write_buffer_number`, default 2, bounds accumulated immutables before writes stall). A background flush serializes the already-sorted skiplist into SST files in $L_0$ and discards the WAL prefix.

The WAL is the durability boundary and the memtable is the performance boundary: enlarging `write_buffer_size` reduces flush frequency but increases recovery time and memory pressure. Production deployments commonly use 128 MB–1 GB memtables, trading recovery time for smoother write throughput [7].

> **Theorem:** *Flush optimality.* Flushing an immutable memtable of $M$ bytes produces exactly $M$ bytes of $L_0$ SST data (plus per-block metadata); no key is rewritten, so the flush phase contributes exactly $1.0$ to write amplification. All additional amplification is attributable to compaction.

### 4.2 Leveled versus tiered compaction: theory and practice

Compaction is the heart of the LSM-tree and the source of its pathologies. RocksDB supports three compaction styles — **level** (leveled), **universal** (tiered), and **FIFO** — of which the first two dominate serious deployments [7].

**Leveled compaction.** Every level below $L_0$ is a single sorted run: SST files within a level have non-overlapping key ranges. A compaction picks one file from level $i$ and merges it with *all overlapping* files in level $i{+}1$, rewriting the union in sorted order. A point lookup therefore consults at most one file per level (modulo Bloom-filter false positives), giving $R_{amp} = O(L)$. The price is paid in rewrites: an entry in level $i$ is re-merged each time level $i{-}1$ fills, on average $T/2$ times:

> **Theorem:** *Amplification bounds (Lethe).* In steady state, the write amplification of leveled compaction is $O(L \cdot T)$ — each entry participates in on average $T/2$ compactions per level — while tiered compaction achieves $O(T)$, each entry being rewritten once per level [5]. Leveled compaction minimizes read and space amplification; tiered compaction minimizes write amplification. No merge policy dominates on all three axes [5][8].

Concretely, with $T = 10$ and $L = 6$, leveled compaction rewrites each byte on the order of 30 times, tiered roughly 6 times — but a tiered point lookup may probe $K \cdot L$ runs instead of $L$.

**Tiered (universal) compaction.** Each level holds a *set* of overlapping sorted runs; when $K$ runs accumulate they are merged into one run at the next level. Write amplification drops to $O(T)$, but read amplification grows to $O(K \cdot L)$ and transient space overhead spikes during merges. RocksDB's *universal compaction* implements this policy with time- or size-based triggers, suited to write-heavy, cold-data workloads such as time-series ingestion and log archival.

**$L_0$ as a hybrid.** RocksDB's $L_0$ is *tiered by construction* — flushed files overlap arbitrarily — while $L_1$ onward is leveled [4]. Modern engines keep a tiered $L_0$ and a leveled $L_1$ of comparable size to reduce level count and compaction-chain length [4]. The $L_0 \to L_1$ compaction, merging overlapping inputs against a non-overlapping target, is frequently the largest contributor to write amplification in production.

| Property | Leveled (`kCompactionStyleLevel`) | Tiered / Universal (`kCompactionStyleUniversal`) |
|---|---|---|
| Write amplification | $O(L \cdot T)$ — high | $O(T)$ — low |
| Point-read amplification | $O(L)$ file probes — low | $O(K \cdot L)$ run probes — high |
| Range-scan cost | $O(L)$ iterators merged | $O(K \cdot L)$ iterators merged |
| Space amplification | $\approx 1 + 1/(T-1)$ — low | $O(T)$ transient — high |
| Best workload | Read-heavy, point lookups | Write-heavy, bulk ingest |
| RocksDB default | **Yes** | Opt-in |

**Compaction scheduling.** RocksDB picks compactions by score — typically `kByCompensatedSize`, prioritizing levels whose size most exceeds target — with `level0_file_num_compaction_trigger` (default 4) gating $L_0$ compaction [2]. The scheduler is the arbiter of the amplification frontier: aggressive scheduling bounds read and space amplification but burns write bandwidth and CPU on merges; lazy scheduling does the reverse. Adaptive schemes such as ArceKV's workload-driven compaction selection have shown that no static schedule is optimal across phases of a single workload [2].

### 4.3 Bloom filters and the block cache: taming read amplification

With $L$ levels to probe, naive point lookups would cost $L$ random I/Os. RocksDB deploys two probabilistic and caching defenses.

**Bloom filters.** Each SST file carries a Bloom filter over its keys (optionally partitioned per data block): a negative answer skips the file; a positive answer costs one I/O. For $m$ bits, $n$ keys, $k$ hash functions, the false-positive rate is $(1 - e^{-kn/m})^k$, minimized at $k = (m/n)\ln 2$. RocksDB's default **10 bits per key yields roughly a 1% false-positive rate** [7]; each doubling of bits per key roughly squares precision. The memory arithmetic is unforgiving — 10 bits per key over one billion keys is ~1.2 GB — hence `optimize_filters_for_hits` (skip last-level filters, cutting filter memory ~10× when most lookups hit) and the newer Ribbon filter [9].

**Block cache.** RocksDB maintains a user-space LRU cache of *uncompressed* data blocks (`NewLRUCache(capacity, shard_bits)`), complemented by the OS page cache holding *compressed* blocks. The layers are not redundant: a block-cache hit avoids decompression, a page-cache hit avoids device I/O [7]. Production practice allocates 40–60% of RAM to the block cache on dedicated hosts, raising `shard_bits` (e.g., to 6, giving 64 shards) when mutex contention appears on parallel NVMe hosts [7]. Critically, index and filter blocks are *not* counted against the block-cache budget by default — budget 10 GB for cache while filters consume 5 GB and RSS reads 15 GB [9]. `cache_index_and_filter_blocks = true` folds them into the cache at the cost of eviction churn; `pin_l0_filter_and_index_blocks_in_cache = true` is the common compromise, since $L_0$ files are consulted by nearly every lookup.

Together, filters and cache convert the $O(L)$ worst-case point lookup into an expected cost of ~1 device I/O for misses and ~0 for hot keys.

### 4.4 Write amplification analysis

We now quantify the end-to-end amplification of a RocksDB deployment under leveled compaction. Let $T = 10$ be the inter-level size ratio, $L$ the number of levels from (1), $B$ the block size, and $f$ the fraction of entries updated (overwrites reduce amplification, since obsolete versions are dropped during merges rather than rewritten indefinitely [5]).

The per-level rewrite factor $T/2$ from [5] gives total write amplification:

$$W_{amp} \approx 1 + \sum_{i=1}^{L} \frac{T}{2}\,(1 - d_i) \approx 1 + \frac{T \cdot L}{2} \cdot (1 - \bar{d}) \tag{2}$$

where $d_i$ is the fraction discarded as obsolete at level $i$ and the leading $1$ is the flush. For a 1 TB dataset with $T = 10$, $L = 6$ under pure insert ($\bar{d} = 0$), (2) predicts $W_{amp} \approx 31$; at 50% overwrite it falls toward ~16. Published studies report tens of × for leveled designs and single-digit × for tiered designs on identical workloads [2][4][5].

```python
def write_amplification(T: float, L: int, discard: float = 0.0) -> float:
    """Analytic model of Eq. (2): leveled-compaction write amplification."""
    return 1.0 + (T * L / 2.0) * (1.0 - discard)

for L, T in [(4, 10), (6, 10), (6, 4), (8, 10)]:
    print(f"L={L} T={T:2d} -> W_amp ~ {write_amplification(T, L):5.1f}x "
          f"(50% overwrite: {write_amplification(T, L, 0.5):5.1f}x)")
```

```text
L=4 T=10 -> W_amp ~  21.0x (50% overwrite:  11.0x)
L=6 T=10 -> W_amp ~  31.0x (50% overwrite:  16.0x)
L=6 T= 4 -> W_amp ~  13.0x (50% overwrite:   7.0x)
L=8 T=10 -> W_amp ~  41.0x (50% overwrite:  21.0x)
```

The table exposes the central lever: **reducing the number of levels dominates**. Every level removed saves $T/2$ rewrites per byte. This is why `dynamic_level_bytes` and large $L_0$/$L_1$ configurations measurably reduce amplification in practice [4][7], and why Autumn's no-merge-first-level design yields disproportionate savings [8].

The endurance corollary is immediate. A drive rated 1 DWPD over 5 years sustains $\approx$ 1,825 full rewrites; a leveled LSM at $W_{amp} = 31$ caps *user-visible* writes at roughly 1/31 DWPD. For write-heavy NVMe deployments this arithmetic, not raw throughput, is frequently the binding constraint — the strongest quantitative argument for universal compaction on flash [4][5].

### 4.5 Tuning for NVMe

Classical LSM tuning assumed HDDs: random I/O was ~100× more expensive than sequential, so every heuristic minimized seeks. NVMe inverts several premises — random 4 KB reads complete in tens of microseconds, queue depths reach 64K, and internal parallelism rewards concurrency — while finite program/erase endurance becomes the binding constraint. The tuning discipline changes accordingly [4][7]:

1. **Parallelize background work.** The defaults (`max_background_compactions = 1`, single flush thread) starve NVMe devices. Raise `max_background_jobs` to 4–8 and enable `max_subcompactions` so large compactions fan out across cores and queues; throughput scales nearly linearly until CPU (decompression, merge-sort) becomes the bottleneck [7].
2. **Bypass the page cache selectively.** With `use_direct_io_for_flush_and_compaction` and `use_direct_reads`, RocksDB issues `O_DIRECT` I/O, avoiding double-buffering between the page cache and the block cache and eliminating page-cache lock contention on many-core hosts. This helps when the block cache is correctly sized; it hurts when the working set exceeds RAM [7].
3. **Reconsider compression.** On HDD, compression was nearly free. On NVMe, decompression competes with merge computation for cores. LZ4 or low-level ZSTD remain worthwhile — they reduce both $W_{amp}$ in bytes and PCIe traffic — but measure: on some hosts, disabling bottommost compression *increases* throughput [7][9].
4. **Grow blocks and readahead.** The 4 KB default block size was chosen for HDD-era random reads; on NVMe, 16–32 KB blocks shrink index and filter metadata roughly linearly with negligible latency penalty [9]. Set `compaction_readahead_size` to ≥ 2 MB so sequential compaction scans fully exploit device prefetching.
5. **Flatten the tree.** Enable `dynamic_level_bytes` and size $L_0$ / `write_buffer_size` generously — each level eliminated removes $T/2$ from $W_{amp}$ per (2) [4][7].
6. **Separate the WAL.** WAL appends are latency-sensitive and strictly sequential; isolating them from compaction storms protects foreground write latency. `recycle_log_file_num` avoids file-creation overhead on the hot path [7].

A representative NVMe-tuned option set [7]:

```cpp
rocksdb::Options opt;
opt.write_buffer_size = 256 << 20;
opt.max_write_buffer_number = 4;
opt.min_write_buffer_number_to_merge = 2;
opt.max_background_jobs = 8;
opt.max_subcompactions = 4;
opt.level0_file_num_compaction_trigger = 4;
opt.dynamic_level_bytes = true;
opt.target_file_size_base = 256 << 20;
rocksdb::BlockBasedTableOptions tbo;
tbo.filter_policy.reset(rocksdb::NewBloomFilterPolicy(10, false));
tbo.block_size = 32 << 10;
tbo.cache_index_and_filter_blocks = false;
tbo.pin_l0_filter_and_index_blocks_in_cache = true;
opt.table_factory.reset(rocksdb::NewBlockBasedTableFactory(tbo));
auto cache = rocksdb::NewLRUCache(8ULL << 30, 6);
opt.use_direct_io_for_flush_and_compaction = true;
opt.use_direct_reads = true;
opt.compaction_readahead_size = 2 << 20;
```

## 5. Empirical Results / Proofs

We validate the analytic framework against published measurements.

**Amplification.** The Lethe characterization ($O(L\cdot T)$ leveled, $O(T)$ tiered) is corroborated across independent studies: ArceKV's evaluation reports leveling achieving the lowest space amplification with only marginal excess over adaptive schemes, while tiered configurations trade that space for substantially lower write I/O [2]; vLSM documents the doubling of I/O amplification when the growth factor is raised from 10 to 20 to shorten compaction chains, confirming the linear dependence on $T$ in (2) [4]; and the Autumn analysis attributes a large fraction of total write amplification to first-level merges, consistent with (2)'s per-level accounting [8].

**Read-path behavior.** Under YCSB workloads, RocksDB with bloom filters and a warmed block cache serves point lookups near the analytic floor: the Autumn evaluation shows read-optimized LSM configurations outperforming LevelDB baselines on read-heavy mixes, with bloom filters eliminating the large majority of unnecessary file probes exactly as the FPR analysis predicts [8]. The tuning guide's rule of thumb — enable filters for point lookups on uncompacted databases — is the operational form of this result [7].

**Proof sketch of the $O(L\cdot T)$ bound.** In steady state with all levels full, a compaction of level $i{-}1$ (capacity $C$) into level $i$ (capacity $T{\cdot}C$) rewrites the overlapping fraction of level $i$ — in expectation $T{\cdot}C/2$ resident bytes, i.e., $T/2$ rewrites per merged byte. Each inserted byte undergoes one such compaction per level across $L$ levels: $L \cdot T/2$ rewrites, i.e., $O(L\cdot T)$. Tiered compaction merges runs without a resident target level, rewriting each byte once per level: $O(T)$ total [5]. ∎

## 6. Limitations

Several caveats apply. First, the steady-state, full-level assumptions behind (1) and (2) rarely hold exactly: skewed keys and time-varying overwrite rates perturb the constants, though not the asymptotics [6]. Second, this thesis treats the LSM-tree as a single-node engine; distributed deployments (CockroachDB, TiDB) layer consensus and sharding atop RocksDB, introducing cross-node amplification the local model does not capture. Third, the NVMe tuning guidance in §4.5 is necessarily heuristic: optimal `max_background_jobs`, block sizes, and compression choices depend on the specific device (PCIe 3.0 vs 5.0), CPU headroom, and workload mix, and must be validated with `db_bench` on the target hardware — the tuning guide itself stresses measurement over rules of thumb [7].

More fundamentally, the LSM-tree is the wrong structure for some workloads, and no tuning repairs that. Scan-heavy analytics pay the $O(L)$ iterator-merge cost on every range query. Serializable transactions need external concurrency control. Delete-heavy workloads accumulate tombstones that ordinary compaction must eventually reclaim — a cost the delete-aware Lethe design addresses [5]. The LSM-tree dominates *write-ingest with point reads*; outside that envelope, B-trees, columnar stores, and learned indexes deserve evaluation.

---

## 7. Conclusion

RocksDB endures as the default embedded storage engine because its costs are exactly characterizable and therefore exactly tunable. The write path contributes a fixed $1.0$ to write amplification; compaction contributes the rest — $O(L \cdot T)$ under leveling, $O(T)$ under tiering — and choosing a policy is choosing which amplification to pay [5]. Bloom filters at ~10 bits per key and a sharded block cache over the OS page cache collapse the $O(L)$ read path to near-unity expected I/O [7][9]. On NVMe, the center of gravity shifts from seek avoidance to parallelism management and endurance budgeting: flatten the tree, parallelize compactions, size blocks up, bypass the page cache deliberately, and measure everything [4][7].
