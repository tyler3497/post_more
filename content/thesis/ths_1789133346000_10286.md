# IC3/PDR and the New Era of Hardware Model Checking: Inductive Invariants, Relative Induction, and Frame Propagation

## Abstract

Property-directed reachability (PDR) — introduced as IC3, *Incremental Construction of Inductive Clauses for Indubitable Correctness*, by Aaron Bradley in 2011 [1] and industrialized by Eén, Mishchenko, and Brayton [2] — redefined SAT-based hardware model checking. Instead of unrolling the transition relation, IC3 builds a sequence of over-approximating *frames* $F_0 \subseteq F_1 \subseteq \cdots \subseteq F_k$, each a conjunction of learned clauses, blocking counterexamples to induction backward while propagating inductive lemmas forward — until a fixed point yields an inductive invariant, or a predecessor chain reaches the initial states, exposing a counterexample. This thesis dissects the three pillars of the algorithm: **relative induction**, the one-step SAT query that admits a clause into a frame; **inductive generalization**, which turns blocked cubes into far-reaching lemmas via literal dropping, counterexample-to-generalization, and lifting; and **frame propagation**, the convergence engine that certifies proofs. We compare IC3/PDR against bounded model checking, k-induction, interpolation, and BDD-based reachability, survey its competition record, and examine its limits in liveness, word-level reasoning, and generalization cost.

---

## 1. Introduction

The safety verification of synchronous hardware asks a deceptively simple question: *starting from the reset states, can the circuit ever reach a state that violates the property?* Given a transition system $M = (I, T)$ over Boolean state variables $V$ and a safety property $P(V)$, we ask whether every reachable state satisfies $P$. The reachable set can be astronomically large, and proving $R^* \subseteq P$ is PSPACE-complete — no explicit enumeration survives contact with industrial designs.

Earlier generations each stalled: **BDD-based** model checking [7] computed exact reachable sets until the diagrams blew up; **bounded model checking (BMC)** [4] unrolled $T$ for $k$ steps and hunted bugs but could never *prove* anything; **Craig interpolation** [6] and **k-induction** [5] recovered partial proving power but inherited unrolling's cost — enormous monolithic SAT queries stressing the solver's global reasoning.

Bradley's IC3 [1], simultaneously recast as *property-directed reachability* by Eén, Mishchenko, and Brayton [2], broke this mold. Its central observation is anthropological as much as algorithmic: *when humans analyze systems, they produce a set of lemmas — typically inductive properties — that together imply the desired property*, each proved relative to previously established lemmas [1]. IC3 mechanizes exactly this. It performs tens of thousands of tiny, *local* SAT queries — each asking only about a single transition step — and accumulates the answers as an incrementally refined inductive invariant. The solver is never asked to reason about an entire execution. This *distribution of responsibility* between search strategy and solver is what the title's "new era" names: since 2011, PDR-family engines have dominated the Hardware Model Checking Competition, and the algorithm has been generalized to software verification, Horn clauses, and SMT theories.

This thesis presents the algorithm from first principles, with unusual attention to its three mechanisms: **relative induction**, the logical condition under which a learned clause may strengthen a frame; **inductive generalization**, the art of converting one blocked state into a lemma excluding exponentially many; and **frame propagation**, the forward-pushing dynamics that detect convergence. We then evaluate IC3/PDR against its predecessors and close with an honest account of where it still struggles.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 Transition Systems and Inductive Invariants

A *safety verification problem* is a triple $(I, T, P)$: $I(V)$ describes initial states; $T(V, V')$ relates current to next states; $P(V)$ is the property. The system is *safe* if no reachable state violates $P$.

The classical certificate of safety is an **inductive invariant**: a formula $F(V)$ such that

1. **Initiation:** $I \Rightarrow F$;
2. **Consecution:** $F \land T \Rightarrow F'$;
3. **Safety:** $F \Rightarrow P$.

If such an $F$ exists, induction over execution length proves safety. Existence is never the problem — in propositional logic, $R^*$ itself always qualifies. The difficulty is *concise representation*: the invariant must be found in manipulable CNF, small enough to check.

### 2.2 The Predecessors

- **BDD-based reachability** [7] computes $R^*$ exactly via symbolic breadth-first search. When variable orderings cooperate it is unbeatable; on wide datapaths or deep pipelines the diagrams explode and the method dies without partial results.
- **Bounded model checking** [4] checks $I \land T_0 \land \cdots \land T_{k-1} \land \lnot P_k$ for satisfiability. Superb at *falsification*, but the formula grows with $k$, the solver must reason about entire executions, and safety is never established.
- **k-induction** [5] proves $P$ for the first $k$ steps and shows any $k$-step $P$-path extends to a $(k{+}1)$-st $P$-state. It works when the property is nearly inductive, but realistic properties need auxiliary invariants — which k-induction cannot discover.
- **Interpolation-based model checking** [6] extracts Craig interpolants from refutations of unrolled formulas, yielding over-approximate reachability sequences. Elegant, but each query is again unrolled, and weak interpolants cause slow convergence or divergence.

> **Theorem:** (Folk characterization of the gap) For every $k \geq 0$ there exist safe systems whose shortest inductive invariant requires auxiliary lemmas that no $k$-induction check can derive from $P$ alone, yet which IC3 discovers through generalization of blocked CTIs. IC3's lemma generation is *strictly* more powerful than fixed-strengthening induction: it synthesizes the strengthening rather than assuming it.

What IC3 contributes is a *proof search* that is incremental, local, and property-directed: every learned clause exists because some concrete threat to the property demanded it.

## 3. Methodology

### 3.1 The Frame Sequence

IC3 maintains **frames** $F_0, F_1, \ldots, F_k$, each in CNF, satisfying [1, 2]:

| # | Invariant | Meaning |
|---|-----------|---------|
| (i) | $F_0 = I$ | First frame is exactly the initial states |
| (ii) | $F_i \Rightarrow F_{i+1}$ for $i < k$ | Frames grow *weaker* (admit at least as many states) |
| (iii) | $F_i \land T \Rightarrow F_{i+1}'$ for $i < k$ | One transition from $F_i$ lands inside $F_{i+1}$ |
| (iv) | $F_i \Rightarrow P$ for $i < k$ | Every frame except possibly the last implies $P$ |

Intuitively, $F_i$ *over-approximates* states reachable in at most $i$ steps. The last frame $F_k$ is the working frontier: the algorithm repeatedly establishes $F_k \Rightarrow P$, and when some state in $F_k$ can reach $\lnot P$ in one step, it *blocks* that threat — recursively if necessary — learning clauses that strengthen the frames.

### 3.2 The Main Loop

The algorithm alternates two phases. In the **blocking phase**, it enumerates cubes $s$ (conjunctions of literals) with $s \models F_k \land T \land \lnot P'$ — *counterexamples to induction* (CTIs) at the frontier — and blocks each: it finds a clause $c$ excluding $s$ that is *inductive relative to* $F_{k-1}$ and adds $c$ to frames $1$ through $k$. If some CTI's predecessor chain reaches $F_0 = I$, a concrete trace is returned. In the **propagation phase**, clauses are pushed forward: a clause $c \in F_i$ inductive relative to $F_i$ joins $F_{i+1}$. If $F_i = F_{i+1}$, then $F_i$ is an inductive invariant and the property is proved.

```python
def ic3(I, T, P):
    """Core IC3/PDR loop (simplified; cf. Bradley [1], Eén et al. [2])."""
    F = [I]                      # F[0] = I; frames are clause sets
    while True:
        # --- Blocking phase: enforce F[k] => P ---
        while sat(F[k] & T & ~P.prime()):
            s = get_model()      # CTI cube: s |= F[k] /\ T /\ ~P'
            if not block(s, k, F, I, T):
                return "UNSAFE", extract_trace()
        F.append(top())          # new frontier frame F[k+1] = True
        # --- Propagation phase: push lemmas forward ---
        for i in range(1, len(F) - 1):
            for c in list(F[i] - F[i + 1]):
                if not sat(F[i] & c & T & ~c.prime()):
                    F[i + 1].add(c)          # c inductive relative to F[i]
            if F[i] == F[i + 1]:
                return "SAFE", And(*F[i])    # inductive invariant found

def block(s, i, F, I, T):
    """Block cube s at frame i; False iff I is reachable (real CEX)."""
    if i == 0:
        return False             # predecessor chain hit initial states
    while sat(F[i-1] & ~s & T & s.prime()):
        p = get_model()          # predecessor of s within F[i-1]
        if not block(p, i - 1, F, I, T):
            return False
    c = generalize(~s, i - 1, F, T)   # minimal inductive clause excluding s
    for j in range(1, i + 1):
        F[j].add(c)
    return True
```

Observe what is *absent*: no unrolling, no monolithic formula, no BDDs — every SAT query mentions $T$ exactly once. The `generalize` subroutine (Section 4.3) is where the algorithm's intelligence lives:

```tla
------------------------------ MODULE FrameInvariants ------------------------------
EXTENDS Naturals
CONSTANTS Init, Next, Prop, K
VARIABLES F  \* F[i] : set of states (frame i), 0 <= i <= K

FrameOK ==
    /\ F[0] = Init
    /\ \A i \in 0..K-1 : F[i] \subseteq F[i+1]       \* weakening
    /\ \A i \in 0..K-1 : F[i] \subseteq Prop          \* safety below frontier
    /\ \A i \in 0..K-1 :                            \* consecution
         \A s \in F[i] : \A t \in Next(s) : t \in F[i+1]

Converged == \E i \in 1..K-1 : F[i] = F[i+1]
\* Converged => F[i] is an inductive invariant proving Prop.
=====================================================================================
```

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Relative Induction: The One-Step Check

The single most important definition in IC3 is *relative inductiveness*. A clause $c$ is **inductive relative to** frame $F_i$ if every successor of an $(F_i \land c)$-state satisfies $c$:

$$F_i \land c \land T \;\Rightarrow\; c'$$

Operationally, the SAT query $\mathrm{sat}\,(F_i \land c \land T \land \lnot c')$ must be **unsatisfiable** [1, 2]. Note the subtlety: $c$ need not be inductive *absolutely* ($c \land T \Rightarrow c'$ is far too strong); it must be inductive *under the assumption* of the accumulated knowledge in $F_i$. This is the distribution of responsibility made formal: each lemma leans on earlier lemmas, exactly as Lemma 5 in a human proof cites Lemmas 1–4.

Because every learned clause passes a relative-induction check, implementations maintain $\mathrm{Clauses}(F_{i-1}) \subseteq \mathrm{Clauses}(F_i)$ — and then the convergence test "$F_i = F_{i+1}$" is a cheap syntactic comparison that *implies* semantic inductiveness of the whole frame [1]. A typical run issues tens of thousands of these one-step queries; each is small (one copy of $T$, a few dozen clauses as assumptions), and incremental solvers answer in milliseconds. The solver does *local* reasoning; the algorithm does *global* strategy. In a systems language, the check is one solver, one copy of $T$, and assumptions:

```rust
/// True iff clause `c` is inductive relative to frame `F_i`.
fn is_relatively_inductive(
    solver: &mut SatSolver,
    frame_lits: &[Lit],   // F_i as unit assumptions
    clause: &[Lit],        // candidate lemma c
    trans: &Cnf,           // T(V, V') -- added ONCE, reused incrementally
) -> bool {
    // Query: F_i /\ c /\ T /\ ~c'.  UNSAT  <=>  relatively inductive.
    let mut assumps = frame_lits.to_vec();
    assumps.extend(clause.iter().copied());                 // c over V
    assumps.extend(clause.iter().map(|l| l.prime().neg()));  // ~c' over V'
    !solver.solve_with_assumptions(trans, &assumps)
}
```

### 4.2 Blocking and Counterexample-to-Generalization

When the blocking phase finds a CTI $s \models F_k \land T \land \lnot P'$, conjoining $\lnot s$ to $F_k$ is insufficient: predecessors of $s$ within $F_{k-1}$ would still reach $\lnot P$ in two steps. `block` therefore recurses — while a predecessor $p \models F_{i-1} \land \lnot s \land T \land s'$ exists, block $p$ at $i-1$ first. In practice this recursion is managed with a priority queue of **proof obligations** $(s, i)$ — "cube $s$ must be excluded from frame $i$" — processed lowest-frame-first [2].

Eén, Mishchenko, and Brayton's crucial addition is **counterexample-to-generalization (CTG)** [2]: when generalizing $s$ fails to be relatively inductive at $F_{i-1}$ — i.e., $\mathrm{sat}(F_{i-1} \land \lnot c \land T \land c')$ yields a predecessor $p$ — the algorithm recursively blocks $p$ (a *counterexample to generalization*) and retries. CTG turns generalization failures into new proof obligations, so the effort spent attempting a strong lemma is never wasted: it either yields the lemma or discovers a genuine predecessor that had to be blocked anyway. In ABC, CTG accounts for much of the performance gap over Bradley's original scheme.

*Termination of blocking* holds because each recursive call strictly decreases the frame index and frame 0 is $I$: either every CTI is excluded by a relatively inductive clause, or some predecessor chain reaches $I$ — and that chain *is* a concrete counterexample trace.

### 4.3 Generalization: From Cubes to Minimal Inductive Clauses

A CTI $s$ is a *cube* — e.g. $x_1 \land \lnot x_3 \land x_7$. Blocking the single state it denotes excludes one of $2^{|V|}$ states: hopeless. **Generalization** negates the cube to a clause $c = \lnot s$ and *drops literals* while preserving relative inductiveness, yielding a short clause excluding an exponentially large set of states that share the "reason" $s$ is dangerous.

The standard *down* loop [1], refined by Hassan, Bradley, and Somenzi [3]:

1. Start with $c := \lnot s$.
2. For each literal $\ell \in c$: tentatively remove it; if $c \setminus \{\ell\}$ remains inductive relative to $F_{i-1}$ (one SAT query), keep it removed; else restore it.
3. Repeat until fixpoint — the result is a *minimal* (locally, not globally minimum) inductive clause.

Hassan et al. [3] showed generalization *quality* dominates overall performance: stronger lemmas mean fewer clauses, shorter proofs, faster convergence. Their improvements include unsat-core seeding (shrink $\lnot s$ with an unsatisfiable core before the drop loop) and better literal orderings. The accumulated arsenal:

| Technique | Idea | Source |
|-----------|------|--------|
| Literal dropping (*down*) | Greedily remove literals while relative inductiveness holds | Bradley [1] |
| Unsat-core seeding | Shrink the initial clause with an unsat core first | Hassan et al. [3] |
| CTG | Recursively block counterexamples to generalization | Eén et al. [2] |
| Ternary simulation / lifting | Generalize the CTI cube before negating | Eén et al. [2]; AVR |
| Multi-frame (EXCTG-style) | Generalize against several frames at once | later PDR literature |

*Why does dropping literals work?* Removing a literal from a clause *weakens* it — a shorter clause excludes *more* states, making it harder to keep inductive. The loop walks from "trivially inductive but useless" ($\lnot s$, excluding one state) toward "maximally general but still inductive", stopping exactly where the next drop would admit a predecessor. Each dropped literal doubles the excluded set: a 5-literal clause carved from a 200-latch design excludes $2^{195}$ states. This exponential leverage is the engine of IC3's scalability.

### 4.4 Frame Propagation and Convergence

Blocking strengthens frames *horizontally*; **propagation** strengthens them *vertically*. After $F_k \Rightarrow P$ is established, every clause $c \in F_i \setminus F_{i+1}$ inductive relative to $F_i$ is pushed into $F_{i+1}$. Propagation is monotone — frames only gain clauses — and serves two purposes: lemmas proved at low frames migrate upward so the frontier inherits the run's accumulated wisdom, and it provides the *termination certificate*. If $F_i = F_{i+1}$, then by invariants (ii)–(iv): initiation ($I = F_0 \Rightarrow F_i$ by weakening), consecution ($F_i \land T \Rightarrow F_{i+1}' = F_i'$), and safety ($F_i \Rightarrow P$) — so $F_i$ *is* an inductive invariant.

Gurfinkel and Ivrii's **"Pushing to the Top"** [8] observed plain propagation is often too timid: a clause may fail to push not because it is false, but because the frame lacks a *supporting* lemma. Their technique uses $k$-induction-style reasoning to push aggressively, learning missing support lemmas on demand — CTG's philosophy again ("a failed proof attempt is a proof obligation in disguise"). Modern implementations also propagate selectively by clause activity and periodically drop subsumed clauses to control memory.

> **Theorem:** (Soundness and completeness of IC3/PDR) If the algorithm returns SAFE, some $F_i = F_{i+1}$, and $\bigwedge F_i$ is an inductive invariant implying $P$ — the system is safe. If it returns UNSAFE, the exhibited predecessor chain is a concrete execution $s_0, \ldots, s_k$ with $s_0 \models I$, $s_k \models \lnot P$ — the system is unsafe. The algorithm always terminates on finite-state systems: each major iteration either extends the frame sequence (progress toward the reachability diameter) or learns a clause (progress in a finite clause space).

The completeness argument is worth savoring: unlike BMC, which can deepen $k$ forever on a safe system, IC3 *must* terminate — the frame sequence cannot grow past the reachability diameter without two frames coinciding, and only finitely many clauses exist over finite $V$. In practice it terminates enormously earlier: the learned invariant is usually tiny compared to $R^*$.

## 5. Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 Against the Predecessors

The table below captures Bradley's central polemic [1]: BMC and interpolation hand the solver *global* problems (whole executions); IC3 hands it *local* ones (single steps).

| Algorithm | Query shape | Proves safety? | Finds bugs? | Solver burden | Characteristic failure |
|-----------|-------------|----------------|-------------|---------------|------------------------|
| BDD reachability [7] | fixpoint over BDDs | yes (exact) | yes | canonical blow-up | diagram explosion |
| BMC [4] | $I \land T^k \land \lnot P_k$ | no | yes (deep) | one huge unrolled formula | no proofs, ever |
| k-induction [5] | base + $k$-step induction | yes, if invariants suffice | yes | $k$-unrolled formulas | cannot invent lemmas |
| Interpolation [6] | unrolled refutation $\to$ interpolant | yes, often | yes | large unrolled queries | weak interpolants diverge |
| **IC3/PDR** [1, 2] | $F_i \land c \land T \land \lnot c'$ | **yes** | **yes** | thousands of *one-step* queries | generalization quality |

Three consequences, each empirically confirmed. **First**, on safe designs BMC is silent by construction while IC3 returns a checkable inductive invariant — initiation, consecution, and safety are three small SAT checks, a property certifying toolchains exploit. **Second**, every lemma is learned in response to a concrete CTI, so proof search is *property-directed*: it never characterizes reachable regions irrelevant to $P$, unlike interpolation's global approximation. **Third**, when IC3 struggles it still yields *partial* results — valid low-frame lemmas — whereas a BDD blow-up yields nothing; the ABC implementation of PDR [2, 9] solved HWMCC instances that defeated every BDD engine, and PDR-family solvers have topped the competition's single-safety tracks since the early 2010s.

### 5.2 The Numbers That Matter

Bradley's original evaluation [1] already showed IC3 competitive with the best contemporary engines on HWMCC benchmarks. Eén et al. [2] demonstrated that implementation details compound into order-of-magnitude speedups: incremental SAT with assumption-based frame solvers, delta encoding of frames (storing only clause *differences* between consecutive frames), the proof-obligation priority queue, and CTG. A typical run shows the signature profile: $10^4$–$10^6$ SAT calls at sub-millisecond median latency, hundreds to thousands of learned clauses, and convergence far below the design's sequential depth — the *invariant* is shallow even when *executions* are deep.

### 5.3 Proof-Theoretic View

IC3 also changed what counts as output: it made the *proof* first-class. A BDD run leaves a gigantic diagram; a BMC run leaves nothing; an IC3 run leaves a short list of clauses — a human-inspectable, machine-checkable inductive invariant. The idea outgrew its birthplace: *generalized* PDR lifts frames to Horn clauses over theories, and IC3-style algorithms now verify software, infer quantified invariants, and drive SMT-based infinite-state reasoning.

## 6. Limitations

Honesty requires the other side of the ledger.

- **Liveness and beyond-safety.** Frames are intrinsically about *invariants*. Liveness ("every request is eventually granted") needs different certificates — ranking functions, fair cycles. Reductions exist (k-liveness, liveness-to-safety), but they are bolt-on, and PDR's dominance does not transfer cleanly.
- **Word-level and arithmetic reasoning.** Bit-blasted PDR speaks bits, not words; a 64-bit multiplier's CNF is enormous and learned clauses cannot see arithmetic structure. Word-level PDR via SMT exists but pays a heavier per-query cost — precisely the cheap-query advantage that makes bit-level PDR fly. Datapaths remain the hardest benchmarks.
- **Generalization is heuristic.** The drop loop finds *locally* minimal clauses, and outcomes depend on literal ordering and seeding. Poor choices can cause clause-count explosion or practical non-convergence despite theoretical termination. Hassan et al. [3] improved the heuristics; they did not eliminate the heuristicity.
- **Deep, narrow counterexamples.** On genuinely violated properties at great depth, PDR must block frame by frame and can be slower than BMC at pure falsification. Portfolio solvers run PDR *and* BMC in parallel for this reason.
- **Clause database growth.** Hard safe instances accumulate tens of thousands of clauses; subsumption and periodic frame reduction help, but memory pressure is real, and the delta-encoded frame solvers of [2] are load-bearing, not ornamental.
- **Infinite-state divergence.** The termination argument relies on a finite clause space. For SMT-based PDR over infinite domains it fails, and widening machinery is required.

None of these has dethroned the algorithm; together they map the active frontier: word-level generalization, liveness-native PDR, learning-guided clause selection, and tighter k-induction-style pushing [8].

## 7. Conclusion

IC3/PDR endures because it aligned the algorithm with the structure of proofs. Bounded model checking asked the solver to swallow executions whole; interpolation asked it to summarize refutations; k-induction asked it to check strengthenings it could not invent. IC3 asks the solver, tens of thousands of times, a question so small it is nearly trivial — *does this one clause survive one step, assuming what we have already proved?* — and assembles the answers into an inductive invariant through disciplined frame mechanics: block backward, generalize ruthlessly, propagate forward, stop at the fixed point.

The three pillars restate as design principles. **Relative induction** teaches that lemmas should be judged against accumulated knowledge, not in a vacuum. **Generalization** teaches that a single counterexample contains an exponential lesson, if one pays a few extra SAT calls to extract it. **Frame propagation** teaches that convergence should be *detected*, not *scheduled* — the algorithm stops the moment its knowledge becomes self-sustaining. The frame sequence remains the clearest known embodiment of machine-discovered induction, and the era Bradley opened in 2011 [1] — industrialized by Eén, Mishchenko, and Brayton the same year [2] — is, by every empirical measure, still underway.
