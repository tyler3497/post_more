# The Neural Tangent Kernel: Infinite-Width Limits, Lazy Training, and the Linearization of Deep Learning

## Abstract

This thesis develops the theory of the *neural tangent kernel* (NTK), the mathematical object that makes gradient-descent training of sufficiently wide neural networks exactly analyzable. We present the foundational result of Jacot, Gabriel, and Hongler: in the infinite-width limit, the empirical NTK converges to a deterministic, time-invariant kernel, and training under gradient flow is equivalent to kernel gradient descent in a reproducing kernel Hilbert space [1]. We examine the linearization of the model about its initialization, the NTK versus NNGP distinction, and the "lazy" versus "rich" training regimes characterized by Chizat, Oyallon, and Bach [3]. Using Yang's Tensor Programs, we show how the NTK limit extends to arbitrary architectures and how parametrizations decide between feature learning and frozen kernels [4][5]. We analyze the linearized predictor dynamics, spectral convergence rates, and empirical evidence for the linearization in finite-width networks [2], and catalog the regime's limitations: lazy training cannot learn hierarchical representations and generalizes worse than fully trained networks [6].

## 1 Introduction

For decades, the training dynamics of neural networks resisted closed-form analysis. The loss landscape is non-convex, the parameter space is high-dimensional, and the evolution of the learned function under gradient descent seemed to require tracking every weight. A central aspiration of deep learning theory has been to find a regime in which this complicated dynamics simplifies — ideally into something *linear* — without losing contact with practical networks.

The neural tangent kernel, introduced by Jacot, Gabriel, and Hongler in 2018, provides exactly such a regime [1]. The key insight is deceptively simple: consider a neural network $f(x; \theta)$ with parameters $\theta$, and define the *empirical NTK* as the inner product of parameter-gradients,

$$\Theta_t(x, x') = \langle \nabla_\theta f_t(x), \nabla_\theta f_t(x') \rangle.$$

Under gradient flow, the network's output evolves according to a differential equation governed by this kernel. The decisive theorem states that as the widths of all hidden layers tend to infinity (under the NTK parametrization), $\Theta_t$ converges to a *deterministic* kernel $\Theta$ that is *constant in time* [1]. Training the network is then exactly equivalent to kernel gradient descent with a fixed kernel — a convex problem in function space, completely solvable in closed form for squared loss.

> **Theorem: (NTK limit, informal)** Under the NTK parametrization and mild regularity conditions, the empirical neural tangent kernel of a deep network converges in probability to a deterministic limiting kernel as all hidden widths tend to infinity, and remains constant throughout gradient-flow training.

This result ignited a research program. Lee et al. showed that wide networks "evolve as linear models under gradient descent" and derived the Gaussian process of predictions at convergence [2]. Chizat, Oyallon, and Bach clarified the general phenomenon of *lazy training*: the kernel regime is not specific to neural networks but arises whenever the parametrization's scaling makes the model behave as its linearization about initialization [3]. Yang's Tensor Programs generalized the NTK limit to arbitrary architectures — recurrent networks, transformers, networks with batch normalization — and, together with Hu, classified parametrizations into those that admit *feature learning* and those trapped in the kernel regime [4][5].

Yet the theory carries a sobering message, already flagged by its founders [3]: lazy training freezes the features at their random initialization, and on high-dimensional problems this is a poor approximation to what makes deep learning work [6]. This thesis presents the full arc — the definition, the limit theorems, the linearized dynamics, the architectural generalizations, the parametrization taxonomy, and the fundamental limitations — as a coherent account of what is arguably the most successful quantitative theory of neural network training ever developed.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 From random networks to Gaussian processes

The infinite-width story begins *before* training. Neal observed in 1996 that a single-hidden-layer network with random weights converges to a Gaussian process (GP) as the width tends to infinity. This was extended to deep networks: at initialization, the preactivations at each layer form Gaussian processes with covariance $\Sigma^{(\ell)}$ defined recursively by

$$\Sigma^{(1)}(x, x') = \tfrac{1}{n_0} x^\top x' + \beta^2, \qquad \Sigma^{(\ell+1)}(x, x') = \mathbb{E}_{(u,v) \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \Sigma^{(\ell)})}[\sigma(u)\sigma(v)] + \beta^2,$$

where the expectation is over the bivariate Gaussian of preactivations [1]. The resulting *neural network Gaussian process* (NNGP) kernel describes the distribution of the network's *outputs* at initialization. The NTK is a different object: it describes the *training dynamics*.

### 2.2 Gradient flow in function space

For a scalar-output network trained under gradient flow $\dot{\theta}_t = -\eta \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}(\theta_t)$, the chain rule gives the evolution of the function itself:

$$\dot{f}_t(x) = -\eta \sum_{i=1}^{n} \Theta_t(x, x_i)\, \partial_{f_t(x_i)} \ell(f_t(x_i), y_i),$$

where $\Theta_t(x,x') = \langle \nabla_\theta f_t(x), \nabla_\theta f_t(x') \rangle$ is the empirical NTK. This is *kernel gradient flow* with a time-varying kernel [1]. The entire difficulty of the non-convex parameter dynamics is compressed into the time-dependence of $\Theta_t$.

### 2.3 Parametrization and scaling

Whether $\Theta_t$ stabilizes depends critically on how the network is parametrized. In the *NTK parametrization*, layer $\ell$ computes $\tilde{\alpha}^{(\ell+1)} = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{n_\ell}} W^{(\ell)} \alpha^{(\ell)} + \beta b^{(\ell)}$ with $W_{ij}^{(\ell)}, b_i^{(\ell)} \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1)$ i.i.d. The $1/\sqrt{n}$ scaling ensures each neuron's contribution to the output is $O(1/\sqrt{n})$ while gradients accumulate coherently; as $n \to \infty$, individual parameters move only $O(1/\sqrt{n})$, and the kernel freezes [1]. The standard parametrization behaves differently at finite width but admits the same infinite-width kernel limit under appropriate learning-rate scaling [2].

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 The recursive NTK construction

The limiting NTK $\Theta^{(L)}$ for a depth-$L$ network is computed alongside the NNGP covariance. With $\dot{\Sigma}^{(\ell)}(x,x') = \mathbb{E}[\dot{\sigma}(u)\dot{\sigma}(v)]$ the covariance of activation derivatives,

$$\Theta^{(1)} = \Sigma^{(1)}, \qquad \Theta^{(\ell+1)}(x,x') = \Sigma^{(\ell+1)}(x,x') + \Theta^{(\ell)}(x,x') \cdot \dot{\Sigma}^{(\ell+1)}(x,x').$$

The first term is the contribution of the top layer's parameters (which equals the NNGP kernel); the second propagates gradient information backward through the layers [1][5]. This recursion makes the NTK *computable* for any architecture admitting a Gaussian description of forward and backward signals.

### 3.2 Linearized training and the GP of predictions

Lee et al. proposed studying the *linearized model* $f_{\text{lin}}(x; \theta) = f(x;\theta_0) + \langle \nabla_\theta f(x;\theta_0), \theta - \theta_0 \rangle$ trained by gradient descent [2]. In the infinite-width limit, the linearization becomes exact: the true network and its linearization follow identical trajectories. For squared loss, the converged predictor is distributed as a Gaussian process whose mean is kernel ridge regression with the NTK and whose covariance involves both the NTK and the NNGP kernel — unifying the Bayesian (NNGP) and optimization (NTK) pictures of infinite-width networks.

### 3.3 Tensor Programs for arbitrary architectures

Yang's Tensor Programs provide a programming-language formalism in which essentially any network computation — forward passes, backpropagation, attention, normalization — is expressed as a straight-line program over large random tensors [5]. A master theorem characterizes the scaling limit of any such program, from which both the NNGP limit and the NTK limit follow as corollaries. Crucially, the framework identifies the *gradient independence assumption* (GIA) — that a weight matrix is independent of its transpose in backprop — as the step requiring justification, and gives a checkable condition (the "Simple GIA Check") under which it is valid [5].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The NTK limit theorem and positive-definiteness

The foundational theorem of Jacot et al. has two parts: (i) at initialization, $\Theta_0 \xrightarrow{p} \Theta$ as widths go to infinity; (ii) during training, $\sup_t \|\Theta_t - \Theta_0\| \xrightarrow{p} 0$ [1]. Part (ii) is the deep one: it requires showing that parameters move so little that the kernel, a smooth function of them, barely changes. The proof exploits the NTK parametrization's $1/\sqrt{n}$ scaling, under which each parameter's total displacement over training is $O(1/\sqrt{n})$.

Positive-definiteness of the limiting kernel on the training data is what guarantees convergence to zero training loss. Jacot et al. proved $\Theta$ is positive definite when inputs lie on the sphere and the nonlinearity is non-polynomial [1] — a condition satisfied by ReLU, tanh, and essentially every activation used in practice.

> **Theorem: (Convergence in the NTK regime)** If the limiting NTK Gram matrix $\Theta(\mathcal{X}, \mathcal{X})$ is positive definite, then gradient flow on the squared loss converges linearly to zero training loss, with rate governed by the smallest eigenvalue $\lambda_{\min}(\Theta)$.

> **Proof:** (Sketch.) With $\Theta_t \to \Theta$ fixed, the residual vector $r_t = f_t(\mathcal{X}) - \mathcal{Y}$ obeys $\dot{r}_t = -\eta\, \Theta\, r_t$, a linear ODE whose solution is $r_t = e^{-\eta \Theta t} r_0$. Positive-definiteness gives $\|r_t\| \le e^{-\eta \lambda_{\min} t} \|r_0\|$. ∎

### 4.2 Spectral dynamics: why early stopping works

Diagonalizing $\Theta = \sum_k \lambda_k v_k v_k^\top$, the residual decomposes into eigencomponents decaying as $e^{-\eta \lambda_k t}$ [1]. Large-eigenvalue directions — the top kernel principal components of the data — are learned first; small-eigenvalue directions are learned slowly and are most vulnerable to label noise. This gives a *theoretical motivation for early stopping*: stopping at time $t$ effectively regularizes by the spectral filter $(1 - e^{-\eta \lambda_k t})$, shrinking the noisy low-eigenvalue components. Empirically, NTK eigenspectra of real data decay rapidly, so most of the signal is captured early [2].

### 4.3 Lazy training: the general phenomenon

Chizat, Oyallon, and Bach showed that the kernel regime is an instance of a far more general phenomenon [3]. For *any* differentiable model $h(\theta)$, rescaling the output as $\alpha h(\theta)$ with large $\alpha$ forces gradient descent to stay in an $O(1/\alpha)$ neighborhood of initialization, where the model is well approximated by its linearization:

$$h_{\text{lin}}(\theta) = h(\theta_0) + \langle \nabla h(\theta_0), \theta - \theta_0 \rangle.$$

Lazy training "essentially solves a kernel regression" with the tangent kernel of $h$ at $\theta_0$ [3]. The crucial conceptual point: laziness is *not* primarily about overparametrization — it is about *scaling*. The NTK parametrization's $1/\sqrt{n}$ output scaling is precisely the choice that makes wide networks lazy. Their experiments further showed that forcing standard convolutional networks into the lazy regime *degrades* performance on vision tasks, suggesting laziness is not the mechanism behind deep learning's practical successes [3].

### 4.4 Feature learning versus kernel regimes: the parametrization taxonomy

The most far-reaching extension is the classification by Yang and Hu of parametrizations into two mutually exclusive classes [4]:

| Parametrization | Width scaling | Infinite-width limit | Features evolve? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Standard (SP) | $W \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1/n)$, lr $\Theta(1)$ | Kernel (NTK) | No |
| NTK (NTP) | $1/\sqrt{n}$ explicit factor | Kernel (NTK) | No |
| Mean-field / $\mu$P | lr $\propto$ width | Feature learning (nonlinear limit) | **Yes** |

> **Theorem: (Kernel vs. feature learning dichotomy)** In the natural space of parametrizations studied by Yang and Hu, any parametrization either admits feature learning in the infinite-width limit or has training dynamics given by kernel gradient descent — but not both [4].

Under Maximal Update Parametrization ($\mu$P), hidden representations evolve by $\Theta(1)$ amounts even as width $\to \infty$, and the limit is described by Tensor Programs as a nontrivial nonlinear dynamics rather than a fixed kernel. Yang and Hu demonstrated on Word2Vec and few-shot Omniglot — tasks that *require* feature learning — that the $\mu$P infinite-width limit outperforms both NTK baselines and finite-width networks [4]. This dichotomy reframes the NTK not as *the* theory of deep learning but as the theory of one of two possible infinite-width behaviors.

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 Linearization holds at practical widths

Lee et al. provided the decisive empirical validation: for networks of widths in the hundreds to thousands, the trajectory of the true nonlinear network and its first-order linearization agree closely throughout training, across architectures (fully-connected, convolutional, residual), optimizers (SGD, Adam), and losses [2]. The agreement is not merely qualitative — test predictions of the linearized model track the full network's predictions with small relative error. This established that the NTK regime is not a purely asymptotic curiosity but a quantitatively predictive model of realistic training.

A minimal experiment illustrating the NTK dynamics can be written directly:

```python
import numpy as np

def ntk_regression(K_train, K_test_train, y, lam=1e-3):
    """Closed-form NTK predictor: kernel ridge regression with the NTK."""
    n = K_train.shape[0]
    alpha = np.linalg.solve(K_train + lam * np.eye(n), y)
    return K_test_train @ alpha

def linearized_dynamics(K, y, f0, eta, steps):
    """Exact gradient-flow trajectory under a fixed NTK (squared loss)."""
    r = f0 - y
    traj = []
    for t in range(steps):
        r = r - eta * (K @ r)   # residual ODE: dr/dt = -eta K r
        traj.append(float(r @ r))
    return traj
```

The second routine is *exact* for infinite-width networks under squared loss [1]: no approximation is involved once $\Theta_t \equiv \Theta$.

### 5.2 NTK versus NNGP as predictors

A large-scale empirical study compared kernel predictors head-to-head [2]. Key findings: (i) NNGP kernels frequently *outperform* NT kernels on image classification, suggesting the Bayesian posterior is a better model of the trained network than the gradient-descent solution; (ii) kernel methods outperform finite-width fully-connected networks but underperform finite-width convolutional networks — the *architecture gap* that lazy training cannot close; (iii) the NTK parametrization outperforms the standard parametrization at finite width; (iv) large learning rates and weight decay *break* the correspondence between finite and infinite networks, pushing training out of the kernel regime and typically improving performance [2].

### 5.3 Exact computation for convolutional architectures

The recursive NTK construction of Section 3.1 was extended from fully-connected networks to convolutional architectures, where the kernel factorizes over spatial locations, enabling exact computation of the convolutional NTK (CNTK) [1][5]. Combined with the closed-form predictor above, this made it possible to evaluate infinite-width convolutional networks on CIFAR-10, achieving the strongest kernel-method classification results of their time and providing a rigorous baseline against which trained finite-width networks could be measured [2].

## 6 Limitations

The NTK theory's very precision makes its boundaries sharp, and they are significant.

**1. No feature learning.** The defining property of the kernel regime — a frozen tangent kernel — is also its defining weakness. Hidden representations do not adapt to the data; the model is a linear method over random features fixed at initialization. On tasks where representation learning matters (vision, language), trained networks substantially outperform their NTK predictors [2][3].

**2. Lazy training is provably suboptimal.** Ghorbani, Mei, and Montanari proved sharp separations: for two-layer networks on anisotropic data, the lazy (NT/RF) predictors have *limited approximation power*, and full nonlinear training achieves significant gains [6]. Random features cannot adapt their representation to the target function's structure — a fundamental, not incidental, deficit.

**3. The kernel regime may not describe practice.** Chizat et al.'s experiments showed performance *degrades* when standard deep convolutional networks are pushed into the lazy regime [3]. Combined with the finding that large learning rates — standard in practice — break the finite/infinite correspondence [2], there is serious doubt that production training lives in the NTK regime at all. The theory may describe a regime adjacent to, rather than coincident with, practical deep learning.

**4. Computational intractability of the exact kernel.** The NTK Gram matrix is $n \times n$ in the dataset size; exact kernel regression costs $O(n^3)$. For ImageNet-scale data this is prohibitive, which is why the theory's empirical footprint is concentrated on CIFAR-scale benchmarks.

**5. Fragility of the limit.** The constancy of $\Theta_t$ requires the NTK parametrization (or equivalent scaling), small learning rates, and gradient flow or small-step GD. Adaptive optimizers, large step sizes, batch normalization subtleties, and cross-entropy dynamics can all violate the assumptions [2][5].

These limitations do not diminish the NTK's achievement — the first exact, quantitative theory of neural network training dynamics — but they redirect the field: toward feature-learning limits ($\mu$P, mean-field), and toward understanding *when* real training is lazy and when it is rich.

---

## 7 Conclusion

The neural tangent kernel transformed deep learning theory by exhibiting a nontrivial regime in which training is *exactly solvable*. From Jacot et al.'s limit theorem [1], through the linearization program of Lee et al. [2] and the lazy-training unification of Chizat, Oyallon, and Bach [3], to Yang's Tensor Programs [5] and the kernel/feature-learning dichotomy of Yang and Hu [4], the NTK program built a complete mathematical picture: wide networks under the right scaling are linear models over a fixed, computable kernel, with training dynamics given by a linear ODE, convergence governed by the kernel spectrum, and predictions described by Gaussian process posteriors.

The theory's lasting value may lie as much in what it rules out as in what it explains. By characterizing precisely what lazy training can and cannot do — and proving its suboptimality where feature learning matters [6] — the NTK literature drew the map on which the next theory must navigate: infinite-width limits that *learn features*, scaling laws that transfer hyperparameters across widths, and a quantitative account of the rich regime in which modern deep learning actually operates. The kernel was the first exact mirror held up to gradient descent; the task now is to grind a mirror for the nonlinear dynamics it revealed by contrast.
