# Algebraic Effects and Delimited Continuations: Row-Polymorphic Handlers, the Free Monad, and Denotational Semantics

## Abstract

This thesis develops a unified account of *algebraic effects and handlers* and *delimited continuations*, two traditions of programmable control whose differences conceal a deep structural kinship. Beginning from the Plotkin–Power view of effects as operations of an algebraic theory, we present the Plotkin–Pretnar handlers as homomorphisms from the free model, giving interception and resumption a precise denotational meaning. We then show how row-polymorphic effect systems in the style of Koka reconcile modular effect tracking with Hindley–Milner inference, and how the free and freer monads mediate between operations-as-syntax and handlers-as-folds. Delimited continuations in the shift/reset tradition are shown to be interdefinable with handlers, modulo the well-known subtleties of multi-shot resumptions. We sketch a coherence theorem for handler composition, survey empirical evidence on the efficiency of freer-monad encodings, and close with the principal limitations of the current theory, including the absence of equational reasoning in implementations and the treatment of scoped, higher-order operations.

##1 Intro

Since Moggi's seminal proposal that computational effects be modelled by monads, the monad has been the dominant mathematical and engineering abstraction for effectful programming. Monads give a uniform account of state, exceptions, nondeterminism, and input/output, and monad transformers promise their combination. Yet the transformer approach scales poorly: every pair of effects demands a bespoke transformer and a quadratic family of lifting instances, and the resulting stacks are notoriously rigid — the order of the stack commits the program to a fixed interaction semantics, and reordering effects means rewriting code.

Algebraic effects, introduced by Plotkin and Power, attack the problem at its algebraic root. An effect is specified by a *signature* of operations together with an *equational theory*; the free model of that theory induces the monad. This cleanly separates *interface* (operations) from *implementation* (interpretation). Plotkin and Pretnar's decisive contribution [1] was to give handlers the same algebraic dignity: a handler supplies a *model* of the theory, and the handling construct denotes the unique *homomorphism* out of the free model guaranteed by its universal property. Suddenly exception handling, timeout, rollback, stream redirection, and even relabelling and restriction in Milner's CCS became instances of one construct.

In parallel, the delimited-continuations tradition — Felleisen's control operators and, most influentially, Danvy and Filinski's *shift* and *reset* [7] — developed programmable control from a different direction: capture the evaluation context up to a dynamically enclosing prompt, and manipulate it as a first-class function. Filinski showed that delimited continuations can represent any monad expressible in direct style, making them a universal substrate for effects.

This thesis argues that these traditions are two faces of one phenomenon, and that their synthesis — *handlers as folds over free models, tracked by row-polymorphic effect types, and implemented over delimited continuations* — is the most promising foundation for the next generation of effect systems. Our contributions are: **(i)** a denotational exposition of handlers as algebra homomorphisms with a worked state example; **(ii)** an analysis of row-polymorphic effect typing after Koka [2], including duplicate labels and scoped labels; **(iii)** the free and freer monads [5][6] as the syntactic bridge between operations and handlers; **(iv)** a coherence sketch for handler composition; and **(v)** an interdefinability argument between handlers and shift/reset.

The remainder proceeds as follows. Section 2 reviews algebraic effects, handlers, and delimited continuations. Section 3 describes our semantic method. Section 4 develops the four technical cores. Section 5 sketches proofs and surveys empirical results. Sections 6 and 7 discuss limitations and conclude.

##2 Background

**Algebraic effects.** Plotkin and Power observed that many computational effects are *algebraic*: they are generated by operations subject to equations. The signature Σ lists operations with arities — for instance `get` and `put` for state, or a binary choice operation for nondeterminism [1]. The free model of the theory, terms built from operations modulo the equations, carries a monad structure; indeed the familiar state, exception, and powerset monads arise this way. Crucially, operations of *different* theories combine by taking the disjoint union of signatures, with no transformer stack in sight.

**Handlers.** Plotkin and Pretnar [1] generalised exception handlers to arbitrary algebraic operations. A handler provides, for each operation, a *clause* interpreting it, plus a *return clause* for pure values; handling a computation reinterprets every operation call through these clauses. Each operation clause receives not only the operation's argument but the *delimited continuation* — the rest of the computation up to the handler — which it may invoke zero, one, or many times, discard, or store. This single mechanism expresses timeout (discard the continuation after a deadline), rollback (invoke it with a restored state), nondeterminism (invoke it once per branch), and stream redirection. Bauer and Pretnar's Eff language [3] made effects and handlers first-class, supplied a denotational semantics, and demonstrated backtracking, breadth-first search, cooperative multithreading, and selection functionals; Pretnar's tutorial [4] remains the standard introduction to the subject.

**Delimited continuations.** Felleisen introduced control operators capturing *partial* continuations; Danvy and Filinski's `shift`/`reset` pair [7] became canonical: `reset` delimits the current continuation, and `shift` captures the context up to the nearest enclosing `reset` as a function, abandoning the current context in favour of its body. Unlike `call/cc`, the captured continuation is *delimited* — it does not include the rest of the program — which makes control effects composable. Their type system permits *answer-type modification*, and the operators admit a simple CPS translation together with a sound and complete equational axiomatisation.

**The modularity problem.** Monad transformers combine effects but at a cost: n effects need on the order of n squared lifting instances, and effect interaction is fixed by stack order. *Extensible effects* libraries attack this with the free monad over a coproduct of signatures [5][6], recovering modularity at the price of interpretation overhead — a tension we examine in §4.4.

| Approach | Effect combination | Interaction semantics | Inference |
|---|---|---|---|
| Monad transformers | Stack of transformers, quadratic lifting | Fixed by stack order | Manual |
| Algebraic effects and handlers | Signature union, handled on demand | Chosen per handler site | Row polymorphism [2] |
| Freer monads and extensible effects | Coproduct of functors [5] | Chosen per interpreter | Type-level lists |

##3 Methodology

Our method is denotational and algebraic. We work in the category of algebras for an effect signature Σ: a computation denotes an element of the *free* Σ-algebra over its return type, and a handler denotes a Σ-algebra structure on the result type, so that handling is the unique homomorphism — a *fold*, or catamorphism — from the free algebra. Proofs proceed by the universal property of the free model: to show two interpretations coincide, it suffices to show they agree on operations and on return. This is the mathematical content of "handlers as folds" and it structures §4.1 and §5.

For the type-system analysis we follow Koka [2]: effects are *rows* — multisets of labels with a polymorphic tail — rather than sets, with *duplicate labels* permitted so that, for example, two distinct state effects can coexist. Row unification extends Hindley–Milner inference, and *scoped labels* mediate between named and structural effect identity. We evaluate this design against the desiderata of principal types, effect encapsulation in the `runST` style, and comprehensible error behaviour.

For the syntactic bridge we use the free monad `Free f` and its refinement, the *freer* monad [6], which drops the `Functor` constraint. Handlers become folds over the syntactic tree; we compare the asymptotic and observed cost of interpretation against monad-transformer baselines reported in the literature.

Finally, the interdefinability argument between handlers and delimited continuations is carried out operationally: we exhibit encodings in both directions and identify precisely where they diverge — multi-shot resumptions and answer-type modification.

##4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Handlers as Homomorphisms: the Denotational Core

Fix a signature Σ of operations, each with a parameter type and an arity type. The *free model* over a set X of return values consists of effect trees: leaves `return x` for x in X, and nodes `op(a; k)` where a is a parameter and k is a family of subtrees indexed by the arity. Computation is tree-building; interpretation is tree-folding.

> **Theorem (Handling as folding).** Let Σ be an effect signature and let C be a set equipped, for each operation of Σ, with an interpretation map taking the operation's parameter and a continuation into C, together with a return map from X into C. Then there exists a unique homomorphism from the free model over X to C extending the return map and commuting with the operations.

> **Proof sketch.** By the universal property of the free model: define the fold on `return x` by the return map and on `op(a; k)` by the operation's interpretation applied to a and the composite of the fold with k. Uniqueness follows because any homomorphism is determined by its action on generators and must respect the operation structure. ∎

A handler is exactly this data — a return clause plus one clause per operation — and the handling construct `with h handle c` denotes the fold of h over the denotation of c. The continuation passed to each clause is the *delimited* continuation: the fold of the context up to the handler boundary. *Deep* handlers re-wrap the continuation with the handler itself, so operations raised inside resumptions are handled uniformly; *shallow* handlers do not, which suits protocols such as session-typed communication where each step demands a fresh handler [4].

Consider state, with `get` and `put` operations:

```haskell
-- Signature functor for state
data StateF s k = Get (s -> k) | Put s k

-- The handler is a fold into the state-passing interpretation
runState :: s -> Free (StateF s) a -> (a, s)
runState s (Pure x)          = (x, s)
runState s (Free (Get k))    = runState s (k s)
runState s (Free (Put s' k)) = runState s' k
```

The diagram below shows the protocol: the program raises an operation, control transfers to the handler together with the captured continuation, and the handler resumes by invoking that continuation [1].

Because handlers denote homomorphisms, *handler composition* is composition of interpretations, and reasoning principles — fusion of consecutive handlings, commuting independent handlers — reduce to algebra. This is the sense in which handlers are not an ad-hoc control operator but the *canonical* eliminator for effectful syntax.

### 4.2 Row-Polymorphic Effect Types

If handlers are the semantics, *effect types* are the static discipline that makes them usable. Koka [2] treats the effect of an expression as a *row*: a multiset of labels with a polymorphic tail. Rows, unlike sets, admit *duplicate labels*, so two state effects over different heaps can coexist; *scoped labels* distinguish effects with the same name by a region parameter.

```koka
// inferred effect row mentions exn and div
fun head(xs : list<int>) : <exn,div> int {
  match xs {
    Nil     -> throw("empty")
    Cons(x,_) -> x
  }
}

// total: the effect row is empty
fun sqr(x : int) : int { x * x }
```

Three design decisions deserve emphasis. First, effects are *inferred* by a Hindley–Milner-style algorithm extended with row unification, so programmers rarely write effect annotations; total functions simply get the empty row. Second, the system is *semantic*, not merely syntactic: if an expression typechecks without the `exn` effect, it cannot raise an unhandled exception — the type system is backed by a soundness theorem [2]. Third, Koka recovers *encapsulation*: just as Haskell's `runST` seals stateful computation behind a pure interface, Koka's `run` handles a state effect locally and discharges it from the row, which additionally permits state combined with let-polymorphism without the value restriction.

| Design | Identity of effects | Duplicate labels | Encapsulation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Effect sets with subeffecting | Structural | Collapsed | Ad-hoc |
| Koka rows with duplicate labels [2] | Scoped labels | Permitted | `run`-style discharge |
| Monad transformers | Nominal, by stack position | Not applicable | `runST`-style |

Row polymorphism solves the *modularity* problem that transformers fumble: combining two effectful functions merely unifies their rows, and handling removes a label from the row. The cost is complexity in unification and, occasionally, in error messages — a limitation we return to in §6.

### 4.3 Delimited Continuations and Handler Interdefinability

The `shift`/`reset` operators [7] give direct-style access to delimited continuations. Operationally, `reset e` evaluates e under a fresh prompt; `shift (fun k -> e)` captures the context up to the nearest prompt as a function k and evaluates e with the captured context abandoned:

```
reset (1 + shift (fun k -> k (k 0)))   ==>   2
```

Here k denotes the context `1 +` applied to its argument hole, and invoking it twice yields `1 + (1 + 0)`. The type system of Danvy and Filinski permits the *answer type* — the type of the context's result — to change across the capture, a flexibility absent from `call/cc`.

Handlers and delimited continuations are *interdefinable*, modulo one famous caveat. In one direction, an operation clause is precisely a use of `shift`: raising an operation captures the continuation up to the handler, and the handler body decides how to resume it. Filinski's result [7], that delimited continuations represent every monad expressible in direct style, underwrites implementations of handlers on top of `shift`/`reset`. In the other direction, `shift` and `reset` are encodable with a single higher-order effect whose handler manipulates the captured continuation directly, as demonstrated in Eff [3].

The caveat is *multi-shot* resumption: invoking a captured continuation twice is natural for handlers — nondeterministic backtracking does exactly this — but expensive or unsound for implementations whose continuations are one-shot and stack-allocated. This is why production handler runtimes, from Multicore OCaml to Koka to WebAssembly's stack-switching proposal, treat continuations as a primitive with explicit cloning cost rather than reusing a naive `shift`. *Deep* versus *shallow* handlers [4] further refine the picture: deep handlers, the Plotkin–Pretnar originals, refold the handler around every resumption and behave as regular folds; shallow handlers expose the raw continuation, trading regularity for fine control over protocols.

### 4.4 The Free Monad and the Freer Monad

The free monad is the *syntactic* face of the free model. For a signature functor f, `Free f a` is the type of effect trees, and handlers are folds:

```haskell
data Free f a = Pure a | Free (f (Free f a))

foldFree :: Functor f => (a -> b) -> (f b -> b) -> Free f a -> b
foldFree ret _   (Pure x)  = ret x
foldFree ret alg (Free fx) = alg (fmap (foldFree ret alg) fx)
```

Swierstra's *data types à la carte* [5] showed how to build such signatures *modularly*: the coproduct of functors combines signatures, and modular interpreters handle each summand while forwarding unhandled operations. This recovered, for Haskell, the modularity that monad transformers denied — at the cost of interpretation overhead proportional to tree depth.

Kiselyov and Ishii's *freer* monad [6] removes the `Functor` constraint entirely, representing the continuation in a type-aligned queue. The payoff is both conceptual — operations need no functorial action on continuations — and practical: their extensible-effects reconstruction is reported *faster* than the heavily optimised MTL transformer library it replaces, overturning the assumption that modularity must cost performance. The freer monad is thus the meeting point of the algebraic and the pragmatic: it is the free model of §4.1 made efficient, the syntax over which handlers fold, and the substrate on which row-typed languages can compile.

##5 Empirical/Proofs

We sketch the central metatheoretic results and survey the empirical evidence.

> **Lemma (Adequacy of handling).** For a well-typed computation c and a handler h covering exactly the operations c may perform, the denotation of `with h handle c` equals the fold of h over the effect tree of c.

> **Proof sketch.** By induction on the typing derivation of c, using the universal property of the free model at each operation node; the return case is immediate from the return clause. The handler clauses supply precisely the algebra structure the fold requires. ∎

> **Theorem (Type soundness for handlers, after Bauer and Pretnar).** In the effect system for algebraic effects and handlers [4], well-typed programs do not go wrong: progress and preservation hold, and a computation typed without a given effect label never performs an unhandled operation of that effect.

The proof follows the standard syntactic pattern, with the handler typing rule as the interesting case: the continuation is typed at the *output* effect row of the handler, which is what permits operations to change the ambient effects across resumption. Leijen establishes the analogous result for Koka's row system [2], including the encapsulation theorem: state handled by `run` is observationally pure.

On the empirical side, Kiselyov and Ishii benchmark their freer-monad extensible-effects library against MTL [6] and find it *faster* than the transformer baseline on standard effect workloads — evidence that the free-model encoding need not be a performance tax. Koka's selective CPS translation [2] compiles handlers to efficient code on stock runtimes by translating only effectful code, and Multicore OCaml's native effect handlers demonstrate that one-shot continuations can be implemented with near-zero overhead when multi-shot use is not required.

| Claim | Evidence | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Handlers denote folds over free models | Universal property; Eff denotational semantics | [1][3] |
| Row-typed handlers are sound and encapsulating | Progress and preservation; `run` discharge | [2] |
| Freer encodings outperform transformers | Benchmarks against MTL | [6] |
| Delimited continuations represent monads | CPS representation theorem | [7] |

##6 Limitations

The theory, for all its elegance, has sharp edges. First, *equations are ignored*: implementations treat signatures as free, with no equational theory [4]. A handler for nondeterminism may or may not satisfy idempotence or commutativity, and nothing checks. Recovering equational reasoning — or even stating which equations a handler respects — remains open.

Second, *multi-shot continuations are expensive*. Deep handlers that invoke resumptions many times, as in search or probabilistic programming, pay in space and time; one-shot runtimes must copy or forbid them, and the performance cliff between one-shot and multi-shot use is poorly characterised.

Third, *scoped and higher-order operations* — pruning nondeterminism, scoped configuration, exception catching that inspects the continuation — do not fit the first-order algebraic mold, since their clauses must manipulate *computations*, not just values. Recent work on scoped effects extends the framework, but the metatheory is unsettled.

Fourth, *row polymorphism has ergonomic costs*: unification with duplicate and scoped labels produces types — and type errors — that programmers find forbidding, and the interaction of rows with features such as polymorphic recursion is delicate [2].

Finally, *answer-type modification* interacts dangerously with polymorphism: the classic unsoundness of polymorphic references has its analogue in control effects, and only restrictive value disciplines restore safety [7].

##7 Conclusion

Algebraic effects and delimited continuations, viewed denotationally, are one subject. Operations generate the free model; handlers are its homomorphisms; delimited continuations are the operational machinery of capture and resumption; the free and freer monads are the syntax over which handlers fold; and row polymorphism is the type discipline that makes the whole apparatus modular and inferable. Each tradition illuminates the others: handlers explain *what* shift and reset mean as an algebra, while shift and reset explain *how* handlers execute.

The research frontier is clear. We need handler-aware equational reasoning so that implementations can be verified against theories; compilation strategies that make multi-shot continuations affordable; a mature account of scoped, higher-order operations; and row-type ergonomics that bring precise effect tracking to working programmers. The algebraic foundation laid by Plotkin and Pretnar [1], the language design of Eff [3] and Koka [2], the modular syntax of Swierstra [5] and Kiselyov and Ishii [6], and the control theory of Danvy and Filinski [7] together suggest that this frontier is not only reachable but near.
