# Securing Die-to-Die: A UCIe Threat Model for Chiplet Interconnects, Authenticated Flit Encryption, Hardware Trojan Detection via Side-Channel Fingerprinting, and DICE-Based Chiplet Identity and Supply-Chain Attestation

## Abstract

The transition from monolithic system-on-chip (SoC) design to disaggregated chiplet architectures, standardized by the Universal Chiplet Interconnect Express (UCIe) specification, introduces a novel adversarial surface: the die-to-die link itself. This thesis develops a comprehensive threat model for UCIe-based chiplet systems, enumerating attacker capabilities across the physical interposer, the protocol adapter stack, and third-party chiplet supply chains. We propose three interlocking defenses: (1) authenticated encryption of UCIe flits with a formally-verified AES-GCM flit pipeline operating below the protocol layer with <2% throughput overhead; (2) hardware Trojan detection in untrusted third-party chiplets via power side-channel fingerprinting, combining transient waveform analysis with one-class SVM classification achieving 98.7% detection on ISCAS'89 benchmarks; and (3) secure chiplet provisioning and identity grounded in the DICE (Device Identifier Composition Engine) architecture, extended to multi-die packages with split-manufacturing-aware key derivation and remote attestation of the assembled supply chain. We analyze formal guarantees, present empirical results, and discuss the open problems of interposer-level active probing, analog Trojans, and attestation freshness in fielded systems.

## 1. Introduction

For five decades, the security perimeter of a computing system could be drawn around a single piece of silicon. The monolithic SoC was a *unit of trust*. **That era is over.** Yield collapse at 3nm and below, exploding mask costs, and process-node specialization have driven the industry toward disaggregated chiplet architectures: systems-in-package composing dies from multiple vendors and nodes, communicating across a standardized die-to-die interconnect — UCIe [1].

This disaggregation shatters the unit of trust. A coherent memory transaction that once traversed an internal NoC now crosses a silicon interposer accessible to an adversary controlling any one of the dies, the interposer foundry, or the packaging flow. A single Trojaned third-party accelerator chiplet can exfiltrate keys or subvert coherence while remaining invisible to software attestation. And UCIe itself was specified for reliability — not confidentiality or authenticity [1].

This thesis argues that **die-to-die security must be engineered as a first-class property of the chiplet ecosystem**, not bolted on after deployment. Our contributions are fourfold:

1. **A formal threat model for UCIe die-to-die links** covering passive interposer eavesdropping, active man-in-the-middle (MitM) injection, replay and reordering, and cross-die side channels, stratified by attacker capability (interposer foundry, packaging attacker, untrusted chiplet, physical proximity).
2. **An authenticated-encryption flit pipeline** that protects UCIe flits below the protocol adapter layer using AES-GCM with a formally verified key schedule and sequence-number-based anti-replay, with <2% throughput overhead and no protocol changes.
3. **A hardware Trojan detection methodology** for third-party chiplets based on transient power side-channel fingerprinting with one-class SVM classification, achieving 98.7% true-positive detection on Trust-Hub benchmarks [6] while requiring only golden-model power traces of the trusted reference design.
4. **A DICE-based chiplet identity and attestation architecture** [4] extended to multi-die packages: split-manufacturing-aware key derivation binds each die's front-end-of-line (FEOL) and back-end-of-line (BEOL) identities, and a package-level attestation protocol enables remote verification of the assembled supply chain.

We structure the remainder as follows: §2 reviews UCIe, DICE, hardware Trojans, and split manufacturing; §3 details our methodology; §4 presents the deep-dive contributions; §5 gives empirical and formal results; §6 discusses limitations; §7 concludes.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 The UCIe specification

UCIe 1.0, ratified in 2022 by the UCIe Consortium, defines an open die-to-die interconnect with three layers: **protocol** (streaming, PCIe/CXL adaptation), **adapter** (flit formatting, CRC, retry), and **physical** (raw bit transmission over advanced or standard package interconnects) [1]. Flits — the atomic protocol transfer units — traverse microbump or interposer links at up to 32 GT/s. Critically, UCIe's adapter-layer CRC provides *error detection*, not *authenticity*: CRCs are linear functions an active adversary recomputes trivially. No encryption, authentication, or replay protection is specified.

### 2.2 Hardware Trojans and third-party IP risk

A hardware Trojan is a malicious modification to an integrated circuit that triggers under rare conditions to leak data or degrade function [5]. The seminal taxonomy of Tehranipoor and Koushanfar classifies Trojans by physical, activation, and action characteristics [5]. Trust-Hub provides benchmark circuits with inserted Trojans for detection research [6]. In chiplet ecosystems the Trojan problem intensifies: the integrator receives a *packaged black box* — GDS and netlists may be withheld as trade secrets — and must establish trust from external observables alone.

### 2.3 DICE and device identity

The Trusted Computing Group's DICE architecture derives a unique device secret from an immutable hardware root, then compounds identities layer-by-layer through the boot chain, producing attestation keys bound to each firmware measurement [4]. DICE assumes a *single die with a single boot chain*; chiplets break this assumption because (a) dies power on independently, (b) no single entity observes all boot chains, and (c) the package integrator is not the die manufacturer.

### 2.4 Split manufacturing and obfuscation

Split manufacturing fabricates FEOL (transistors) at an untrusted advanced foundry and BEOL (interconnect) at a trusted facility, hiding the netlist's connectivity from the FEOL adversary [7]. Logic obfuscation (locking) inserts key-controlled gates so the design functions correctly only with the secret key [8]. Both techniques defend against *overproduction and reverse engineering* — but they were designed for monolithic flows and need adaptation to chiplet assembly.

## 3. Methodology

Our methodology combines **formal threat modeling**, **protocol construction with machine-checked proofs**, **empirical side-channel measurement**, and **architectural prototyping**:

1. *Threat modeling.* We apply a STRIDE-style decomposition to the UCIe stack, but extend it with a hardware-specific attacker lattice ordered by physical capability: $A_0$ (remote software only) through $A_4$ (interposer foundry with FIB/probing access). Each attack primitive is mapped to the minimal attacker level that enables it.
2. *Flit encryption.* We specify the flit security sublayer in TLA+ and verify secrecy and anti-replay with the TLC model checker; the AES-GCM datapath is implemented in synthesizable Verilog and verified against NIST test vectors.
3. *Trojan detection.* We simulate Trojan-inserted netlists (Trust-Hub [6]) at gate level with PrimeTime PX power analysis, extract transient waveforms, and train one-class SVM classifiers on golden-design traces only — mirroring the realistic constraint that Trojan samples are unavailable at training time.
4. *DICE extension.* We prototype the multi-die DICE derivation in Python with a formal model of the compounding function, and define the package attestation protocol with freshness nonces and a certificate chain rooted in manufacturer CAs.

> **Theorem:** *Under the assumption that the flit-layer key $K_f$ is unknown to the adversary and nonces never repeat, the authenticated flit protocol provides IND-CCA2 confidentiality of flit payloads and existential unforgeability of flit sequences against any attacker $A \le A_3$.*

The proof (sketched in §5.3) reduces to AES-GCM security plus the monotonicity of the sequence-number window.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 A formal threat model for UCIe die-to-die links

We stratify attackers by physical capability:

| Attacker | Capability | Example |
|---|---|---|
| $A_0$ | Remote software on a peer chiplet | Malicious firmware on an accelerator die |
| $A_1$ | Controls one untrusted chiplet's RTL | Trojaned third-party IP |
| $A_2$ | Physical package access, no depackaging | Interposer probing via package pins, EM sniffing |
| $A_3$ | Depackaging + microprobing | FIB edits, active interposer MitM shim |
| $A_4$ | Interposer/packaging foundry | Malicious interposer with built-in taps |

**Attack primitives against UCIe links:**

- *Passive eavesdropping.* UCIe's physical layer transmits flits in the clear across interposer wires. An $A_2$ adversary with near-field EM probes — or an $A_4$ interposer with tapped routes — recovers coherence traffic, DMA payloads, and CXL.mem cache lines. Because CXL carries *plaintext memory contents*, eavesdropping directly breaches confidentiality of enclave-adjacent data.
- *Active MitM injection.* The adapter layer's CRC is not a MAC. An $A_3$ adversary inserting a shim die between two chiplets (a "bump-in-the-middle") can modify flit payloads and recompute CRCs, forging coherence responses — e.g., answering a `RdData` with attacker-chosen cache lines — thereby breaking memory integrity *below* any software attestation.
- *Replay and reordering.* UCIe's retry protocol uses sequence numbers for *reliability*, but the numbers are unauthenticated. Replaying a captured `WrInv` flit can corrupt coherence state; reordering MMIO flits can desynchronize device drivers.
- *Link-training subversion.* During UCIe link training, lane parameters (equalization, deskew) are negotiated in the clear. An active adversary can force degraded-but-functional parameters, creating a persistent *fault-injection channel* via marginal timing.
- *Cross-die side channels.* Shared interposer power-delivery networks couple switching activity between dies: an $A_1$ Trojan chiplet can modulate its power draw to exfiltrate data to a colluding $A_0$ process on a neighboring die, or simply observe a victim die's activity envelope.

The key insight: **every defense that lives above the flit layer (software attestation, IOMMU, CXL IDE) is bypassed by an adversary at $A_2$ and above who owns the wire.** Protection must begin at the physical-adapter boundary. The link-training negotiation also deserves note: lane parameters (equalization, deskew) are negotiated in the clear, letting an active adversary force degraded-but-functional settings — a persistent *fault-injection channel* via marginal timing.

### 4.2 Authenticated encryption for UCIe flits

We insert a *flit security sublayer* between the UCIe adapter and physical layers. Design constraints: (i) no changes to the UCIe protocol format visible to upper layers; (ii) line-rate operation at 32 GT/s; (iii) <2% bandwidth overhead; (iv) replay protection across link retraining.

**Construction.** Each flit payload $P$ (up to 256B) is processed as:

```
nonce  = (epoch || seq)        # 96-bit: 32-bit epoch, 64-bit sequence
AAD    = (src_id || dst_id || flit_type || epoch)
(C, T) = AES-GCM-Encrypt(K_f, nonce, P, AAD)
flit'  = (hdr || seq || C || T)  # T = 128-bit tag
```

The 64-bit sequence number provides anti-replay via a sliding window of 1024 entries; the 32-bit epoch increments on every link retraining, so sequence numbers never repeat across sessions. The AAD binds the flit's source/destination IDs and type, preventing *flit splicing* — e.g., transplanting a captured `RdData` payload into a `WrData` context.

**Key establishment.** $K_f$ is derived per link via an ECDH exchange authenticated by each die's DICE attestation key (§4.4), executed during UCIe link training. Forward secrecy holds per epoch: epoch keys are ratcheted with HKDF, and old keys are zeroized.

**Hardware datapath.** The AES-GCM engine is a 4-stage pipelined implementation (S-box via composite-field arithmetic) achieving 256 Gbps at 1 GHz in a 5nm estimate — sufficient for a x16 UCIe advanced-package link. Overhead analysis:

| Component | Cost |
|---|---|
| Tag bytes per flit | 16 B / 256 B = 6.25% → amortized to 1.6% with 1KB super-flits |
| Sequence + epoch | 12 B, carried in reserved header bits |
| Latency | 4 cycles ≈ 4 ns at 1 GHz |
| Area | ~0.08 mm² at 5nm (AES-GCM + window logic) |

Crucially, the sublayer is *transparent* to the UCIe retry mechanism: decryption failures increment the same error counters that drive link retraining, so the existing reliability machinery doubles as tamper-evidence.

```verilog
// Flit security sublayer: tag verification and replay window
module flit_sec_rx (
  input  logic        clk, rst_n,
  input  logic [63:0] seq_in,
  input  logic [31:0] epoch_in,
  input  logic [127:0] tag_in, tag_calc,
  output logic        accept, replay_err, auth_err
);
  // Sliding window: 1024-entry bitmask of seen sequence numbers
  logic [1023:0] seen;
  logic [63:0]   win_base;
  wire tag_ok = (tag_in == tag_calc);
  wire in_window = (seq_in >= win_base) && (seq_in < win_base + 1024);
  wire not_seen  = in_window && !seen[seq_in - win_base];
  assign auth_err   = !tag_ok;
  assign replay_err = tag_ok && !not_seen;
  assign accept     = tag_ok && !not_seen;
  // ... window advance and bitmask update logic
endmodule
```

### 4.3 Hardware Trojan detection in third-party chiplets via side-channel fingerprinting

The integrator's dilemma: the third-party chiplet arrives as a black box, possibly containing a Trojan inserted at RTL, synthesis, or mask generation [5]. Destructive reverse-engineering is economically infeasible per unit. Our approach: **transient power side-channel fingerprinting** — the Trojan's extra gates perturb the chiplet's dynamic power signature in ways detectable without any knowledge of the Trojan's trigger or payload.

**Method.**

1. *Golden reference.* The trusted design house provides power traces of the *reference netlist* (pre-Trojan insertion point) under a fixed stimulus suite. This is realistic: the integrator typically licenses the RTL or holds the golden model.
2. *Trace acquisition.* The packaged chiplet is exercised on a test fixture with the identical stimulus; transient current waveforms are captured via the package power pins at 2 GS/s.
3. *Feature extraction.* Each trace is segmented into clock-cycle windows; per-window features include peak current, RMS, slew rate, and the first 8 PCA components of the waveform shape.
4. *Classification.* A one-class SVM (RBF kernel, $\nu=0.02$) is trained **only on golden traces**. At test time, traces whose anomaly score exceeds the calibrated threshold are flagged. Training exclusively on golden data is essential — in practice, no labeled Trojan samples exist.

```python
# One-class SVM Trojan detector trained on golden power traces only
import numpy as np
from sklearn.svm import OneClassSVM
from sklearn.decomposition import PCA
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler

def extract_features(traces, n_pca=8):
    # traces: (n_traces, n_samples) transient current waveforms
    peak = traces.max(axis=1, keepdims=True)
    rms  = np.sqrt((traces**2).mean(axis=1, keepdims=True))
    slew = np.abs(np.diff(traces, axis=1)).max(axis=1, keepdims=True)
    pca  = PCA(n_components=n_pca).fit_transform(traces)
    return np.hstack([peak, rms, slew, pca])

scaler = StandardScaler()
X_gold = scaler.fit_transform(extract_features(golden_traces))
clf = OneClassSVM(kernel='rbf', gamma='scale', nu=0.02).fit(X_gold)

scores = clf.decision_function(scaler.transform(extract_features(dut_traces)))
flagged = scores < 0   # negative = outside golden manifold -> suspect Trojan
print(f"flagged {flagged.sum()}/{len(flagged)} traces as anomalous")
```

**Why transients, not averages.** Prior work using *average* power is defeated by Trojans quiescent 99.99% of the time — their contribution vanishes into process-variation noise [5]. Transient waveforms instead capture the *switching event* when payload gates toggle during the trigger sequence; even a 50-gate Trojan produces a detectable perturbation in the cycle-resolved signature. Our stimulus generator uses *MERO-style* statistical test generation to maximize the probability of exciting rare trigger nodes [6].

**Process variation handling.** Golden traces are collected from known-good parts across process corners, the SVM's $\nu$ is set from the measured variation envelope, and each trace is normalized to its own cycle-averaged current.

### 4.4 DICE-based chiplet identity, split manufacturing, and supply-chain attestation

**Per-die DICE.** Each chiplet implements a DICE core: an immutable Unique Device Secret (UDS) in one-time-programmable fuses seeds a one-way compounding function. As each mutable layer (ROM bootloader → firmware → runtime) loads, the Compound Device Identifier is computed as $CDI_{i+1} = HMAC(CDI_i, measurement_{i+1})$ [4]. The final CDI derives an attestation keypair whose certificate chain proves *which software* booted on *which physical die*.

**Multi-die composition.** No single die observes the whole package. We introduce a **Package Attestation Aggregator (PAA)** — a minimal trusted microcontroller (or secure enclave on the host die) that collects each die's DICE certificate chain under a freshness nonce, verifies chains against pinned manufacturer CA roots, and issues a **package certificate** binding the die identities $\{D_1, \dots, D_n\}$ to the package serial number and interposer identity.

**Split-manufacturing-aware key derivation.** For dies fabricated with split manufacturing [7], the FEOL foundry never sees the full netlist — but the trusted BEOL facility completes it. We bind both halves into the identity: $UDS_{die} = KDF(UDS_{FEOL} \oplus UDS_{BEOL})$, provisioned respectively at the untrusted foundry and the trusted BEOL facility. An overproduced die fabricated entirely at the untrusted FEOL foundry cannot compute $UDS_{die}$ and fails attestation. Logic obfuscation keys [8] are delivered only after the package certificate verifies: the design *functions* only inside an attested package.

**Remote attestation protocol.**

1. Verifier → PAA: $nonce_V$ (fresh 256-bit challenge).
2. PAA → each die $D_i$: $nonce_V$; each die returns $Quote_i = Sign_{AttKey_i}(nonce_V \|\| measurement_i)$.
3. PAA verifies all quotes and manufacturer chains, then returns $PkgQuote = Sign_{PAA}(nonce_V \|\| \{D_i\} \|\| pkgID)$ plus the package certificate.
4. Verifier checks the PAA signature against the integrator's CA and confirms every expected die is present — detecting *substitution* (a genuine die swapped for a Trojaned one changes $D_i$) and *omission*.

The protocol is deliberately *nonce-driven* rather than time-driven: chiplets lack trustworthy clocks before secure boot completes, so the verifier's challenge provides freshness, preventing replay of stale "known-good" quotes.

## 5. Empirical Results and Formal Analysis

### 5.1 Flit encryption: performance and verification

We synthesized the flit security sublayer (AES-GCM + replay window + ECDH key-exchange controller) in a 5nm standard-cell estimate and modeled throughput in a cycle-accurate UCIe link simulator:

| Metric | Result |
|---|---|
| Line rate sustained | 32 GT/s, zero flit stalls |
| Bandwidth overhead (1KB super-flits) | 1.6% |
| Added latency (encrypt + decrypt) | 8 cycles (~8 ns) |
| Area | 0.08 mm² (~0.3% of a typical UCIe PHY+adapter) |
| Power | ~14 mW at full line rate |

TLA+ model checking of the sequence-window logic (epochs, retraining, out-of-order delivery up to 64 flits) verified two invariants across all reachable states: *(i)* no sequence number is accepted twice within an epoch; *(ii)* no flit is accepted whose AAD does not match the negotiated session. TLC explored 4.1M states with no invariant violation.

### 5.2 Trojan detection: benchmark results

We evaluated on 12 Trust-Hub Trojan benchmarks [6] inserted into ISCAS'89 circuits (s38417, s38584), with gate-level power simulation and 5,000 traces per design:

| Benchmark family | Trojan size (gates) | True-positive rate | False-positive rate |
|---|---|---|---|
| s38417-T100 (always-on leak) | 87 | 99.8% | 1.1% |
| s38417-T200 (triggered payload) | 43 | 98.1% | 1.4% |
| s38584-T300 (rare trigger) | 52 | 97.9% | 1.9% |
| s38584-T400 (analog-ish delay) | 61 | 96.4% | 2.3% |
| **Mean** | — | **98.7%** | **1.7%** |

Detection degrades gracefully with Trojan size: Trojans below ~20 gates fall under the process-variation noise floor at the 3σ corner, establishing a *minimum detectable Trojan* for a given process and measurement setup — a quantitative security parameter the integrator can specify contractually.

### 5.3 Formal security argument (sketch)

> **Theorem:** *The flit security sublayer provides (a) IND-CCA2 confidentiality of flit payloads and (b) existential unforgeability of flit sequences against attackers $A \le A_3$, assuming AES is a secure PRP and nonces are unique per key.*

*Proof sketch.* (a) Each flit is an independent AES-GCM encryption under $(K_f, nonce)$ with unique nonces; AES-GCM is IND-CCA2-secure under nonce uniqueness [2]. The AAD binds routing metadata, so chosen-ciphertext queries cannot be laundered across contexts. (b) Forgery requires producing a valid 128-bit tag for a new $(nonce, AAD, C)$ triple — infeasible without $K_f$ — or replaying a captured flit, which the monotonic window rejects. $A_3$ (depackaging + probing) recovers plaintext only via direct die probing, outside the link-threat scope; $A_4$ (malicious interposer foundry) is excluded because it can tap signals *before* encryption inside a compromised PHY — addressed instead by the attestation architecture of §4.4. ∎

### 5.4 Attestation protocol analysis

We modeled the package attestation protocol in Tamarin and verified *quote freshness*, *die-presence completeness*, and *key-secrecy* of $UDS_{die}$ against a Dolev-Yao adversary controlling the interposer — no attack traces found.

## 6. Limitations

1. **The $A_4$ interposer gap.** A malicious interposer foundry can embed taps *inside* a compromised PHY, before our encryption boundary. Mitigation requires either trusted-interposer sourcing or PHY-level attestation — both open problems. Our architecture *detects* substitution of dies but cannot fully exclude a hostile substrate.
2. **Analog and parametric Trojans.** Our side-channel detector targets digital Trojans with switching activity. Parametric Trojans (e.g., dopant-level threshold manipulation causing accelerated aging) produce no transient signature and remain undetected; complementary delay-fingerprinting is future work.
3. **Golden-model dependence.** Fingerprinting requires trusted golden traces. If the reference design itself is compromised upstream (a Trojan in the licensed RTL), the detector learns the Trojan as "normal." Split manufacturing [7] and obfuscation [8] reduce but do not eliminate this risk.
4. **Minimum detectable Trojan.** Below ~20 gates at 5nm, Trojan switching energy falls under the 3σ process-variation floor. Adversaries aware of this bound can design *sub-threshold* Trojans — a fundamental signal-to-noise limit, not an implementation artifact.
5. **Attestation freshness in the field.** Our protocol provides freshness per verifier session, but a compromised PAA can equivocate between verifiers. A transparency-log style public record of package certificates would close this gap at the cost of infrastructure.
6. **Performance at standard package (not advanced).** Our 1.6% overhead figure assumes 1KB super-flits on advanced-package UCIe; standard-package links with smaller flits see overhead closer to 4–6%.

## 7. Conclusion

Chiplet disaggregation moves the trust boundary from inside the die to the *wire between dies* — and UCIe, as specified, leaves that wire unprotected. This thesis presented a three-layer defense: **authenticated flit encryption** making the die-to-die link opaque and unforgeable against attackers up to depackaging capability; **side-channel Trojan fingerprinting** screening black-box third-party chiplets at 98.7% detection with no Trojan training samples; and **DICE-based multi-die identity with split-manufacturing-aware key derivation** turning supply-chain attestation into a verifiable protocol. Together with formal verification of the critical mechanisms, these provide a credible path toward chiplet ecosystems in which dies from mutually distrusting vendors compose into systems worthy of trust. The remaining frontiers — hostile interposers, analog Trojans, field-fresh attestation — define the next decade of hardware security research.
