# Evolutionary Game Theory: Replicator Dynamics, Evolutionarily Stable Strategies, Stochastic Stability, and the Evolution of Cooperation

## Abstract

This thesis develops the mathematical core of evolutionary game theory: the replicator dynamics introduced by Taylor and Jonker, the evolutionarily stable strategy concept of Maynard Smith and Price, the stochastic stability refinement of Foster and Young and Kandori, Mailath and Rob, and the modern theory of the evolution of cooperation culminating in Nowak's five rules. We derive the replicator equation, characterize ESS through payoff comparisons and invasion barriers, analyze the global structure of dynamics on the strategy simplex, prove the selection of risk-dominant equilibria under perpetual mutation, and derive each of the five cooperation conditions — kin selection, direct reciprocity, indirect reciprocity, network reciprocity, and group selection — from inclusive fitness and payoff-transformation arguments. The synthesis clarifies how static stability, dynamic stability, and stochastic stability relate, and where each fails.

---

## 1 Intro

Classical game theory asks what a *rational* agent should do given the anticipated actions of others. Evolutionary game theory asks a different question: which behaviors *persist* when strategies reproduce in proportion to their success. The shift is decisive. No hyper-rationality is assumed; players may be insects, cells, or boundedly rational humans imitating successful neighbors. Stability is not an equilibrium of beliefs but a *population-level fixed point* that resists invasion [1].

The founding insight, due to Maynard Smith and Price, was that animal conflict could be modeled as a game whose equilibria are immune to mutant invasion rather than to unilateral deviation by a rational optimizer [1]. A decade later, Taylor and Jonker supplied the dynamics: the *replicator equation*, which translates payoff advantages into differential growth rates and turns the static ESS into a dynamically stable attractor [2]. Schuster and Sigmund then showed that these dynamics are mathematically identical to models from population ecology and population genetics, unifying the theory with the rest of evolutionary biology [3].

Three deep problems structure the field and this thesis:

1. **Dynamics.** Given a payoff structure, what does the replicator vector field look like on the strategy simplex, and which rest points attract nearby trajectories?
2. **Selection.** When many equilibria exist, which one does evolution actually select? Stochastic stability answers by injecting perpetual noise into the dynamics and asking which states carry all stationary mass in the vanishing-noise limit [5][6].
3. **Cooperation.** Natural selection favors defectors in any one-shot social dilemma. Yet cooperation built every major transition in evolution. Five distinct mechanisms — each with a precise inequality condition — explain how cooperators can resist invasion [7].

Our contribution is a unified, proof-oriented exposition: every central result is derived from first principles, every condition is stated as a theorem with its assumptions made explicit, and the three strands are shown to be facets of one question — what survives.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Symmetric games and fitness

Consider a symmetric two-player normal-form game with finite pure-strategy set $S = {1, \dots, n}$ and payoff matrix $A = (a_{ij})$, where $a_{ij}$ is the payoff to a player using strategy $i$ against an opponent using strategy $j$. A population state is a vector $x$ in the standard simplex $\Delta = \{x \in \mathbb{R}^n : x_i \geq 0, \sum_i x_i = 1\}$, where $x_i$ is the frequency of strategy $i$. The *fitness* of strategy $i$ against population state $x$ is the expected payoff

$$f_i(x) = (Ax)_i = \sum_j a_{ij} x_j,$$

and the *average fitness* of the population is $\bar{f}(x) = x^\top A x$. Fitness is identified with reproductive success: strategies with above-average fitness increase in frequency.

### 2.2 The Hawk–Dove game and the birth of ESS

Maynard Smith and Price introduced ESS in a model of animal conflict [1]. In the Hawk–Dove game, two animals contest a resource of value $V$; escalated fighting costs $C$:

| | Hawk | Dove |
|---|---|---|
| **Hawk** | $(V-C)/2$ | $V$ |
| **Dove** | $0$ | $V/2$ |

If $V > C$, Hawk is the unique strict equilibrium; if $V < C$, neither pure strategy is stable, and the mixed strategy playing Hawk with probability $V/C$ is the unique ESS. The mixed ESS is the first rigorous explanation of *limited war*: ritualized conflict persists because total escalation is self-defeating.

> **Definition (ESS):** A strategy $\sigma$ is an *evolutionarily stable strategy* if, for every alternative strategy $\tau \neq \sigma$, there exists an invasion barrier $\bar{\epsilon} > 0$ such that
> $$u(\sigma, (1-\epsilon)\sigma + \epsilon\tau) > u(\tau, (1-\epsilon)\sigma + \epsilon\tau) \quad \text{for all } \epsilon \in (0, \bar{\epsilon}).$$

Equivalently, the two conditions hold [1]: (i) $u(\sigma, \sigma) \geq u(\tau, \sigma)$ for all $\tau$ (Nash equilibrium condition), and (ii) if equality holds in (i) for some $\tau \neq \sigma$, then $u(\sigma, \tau) > u(\tau, \tau)$ (stability condition). Every ESS is a Nash equilibrium, but not conversely.

### 2.3 The replicator equation

Let $p_i(t)$ be the frequency of strategy $i$ at time $t$. In discrete generations, offspring inherit strategies proportionally to fitness:

$$p_i' = p_i \cdot \frac{f_i(x)}{\bar{f}(x)}.$$

Passing to continuous time under the assumption of small per-generation change yields the *replicator equation* of Taylor and Jonker [2]:

$$\dot{x}_i = x_i\left(f_i(x) - \bar{f}(x)\right) = x_i\left((Ax)_i - x^\top Ax\right), \quad i = 1, \dots, n.$$

The simplex $\Delta$ is forward invariant, and the qualitative structure — orbits, rest points, stability — is the central object of study. An early success of the theory is the theorem that every ESS is asymptotically stable under the replicator dynamics [2][3].

## 3 Methodology

Our method throughout is *payoff-comparison analysis*: reduce each evolutionary question to inequalities among a small set of expected payoffs, then derive dynamic consequences. The toolkit has four components:

1. **Linearization of the replicator field.** At a rest point $x^*$, the Jacobian of the replicator vector field determines local asymptotic stability via eigenvalue analysis, with the caveat that stability must be assessed on the tangent space of the simplex.
2. **Invasion analysis.** For a resident strategy $\sigma$ and mutant $\tau$, compare $u(\sigma, (1-\epsilon)\sigma + \epsilon\tau)$ against $u(\tau, (1-\epsilon)\sigma + \epsilon\tau)$ as functions of $\epsilon$; the sign of the difference near zero determines uninvadability.
3. **Markov perturbation analysis.** For stochastic stability, model the finite population as a Markov chain with mutation rate $\epsilon$, compute the ergodic distribution $\pi_\epsilon$, and study $\lim_{\epsilon \to 0} \pi_\epsilon$ via the resistance-tree (radius–coradius) technique [5][6].
4. **Payoff transformation.** Each cooperation mechanism is analyzed by transforming the base payoff matrix into an *effective* matrix in which cooperation becomes dominant or stable, then reading off the inequality condition on the transformation parameters [7].

We implement the replicator dynamics numerically to verify phase portraits, and we formalize the mutation-selection process in TLA+ to make the stochastic stability argument machine-checkable in principle.

```python
import numpy as np

def replicator(A, x0, T=20.0, dt=0.01):
    """Integrate the replicator equation dx/dt = x * (Ax - x'Ax)."""
    x = np.asarray(x0, dtype=float)
    n_steps = int(T / dt)
    traj = np.empty((n_steps + 1, len(x)))
    traj[0] = x
    for k in range(n_steps):
        f = A @ x
        fbar = x @ f
        x = x + dt * x * (f - fbar)
        x = np.clip(x, 0.0, 1.0)
        x = x / x.sum()
        traj[k + 1] = x
    return traj

# Hawk-Dove with V=2, C=4: mixed ESS plays Hawk with prob V/C = 0.5
A = np.array([[ (2-4)/2, 2.0],
              [ 0.0,      2/2 ]])
traj = replicator(A, [0.9, 0.1])
print("final state:", traj[-1])  # converges to [0.5, 0.5]
```

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The replicator equation and phase-portrait analysis

The replicator equation is a *selection dynamics*: it preserves the support of $x(0)$ and leaves each face of the simplex invariant. Its rest points are exactly the population states at which all present strategies earn equal fitness. The fundamental classification result states that every *Lyapunov stable* rest point is a Nash equilibrium of the underlying game, and every ESS is asymptotically stable [2][3].

The two-strategy case is fully understood. With strategies $A$ and $B$ and payoff matrix $\begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{pmatrix}$, let $x$ be the frequency of $A$. The dynamics reduce to a single ODE whose sign is governed by the linear function

$$g(x) = (a - c - b + d)x + (b - d),$$

so that $\dot{x} = x(1-x)g(x)$. The interior rest point $x^* = (d-b)/(a-c-b+d)$, when it lies in the open unit interval, is the mixed Nash equilibrium, and its stability is determined by the sign of $g'(x^*) = a - c - b + d$:

| Game class | Sign pattern | Replicator outcome |
|---|---|---|
| Dominance | $a > c,\ b > d$ | $A$ fixates from any interior start |
| Coordination | $a > c,\ d > b$ | bistable; interior rest point repelling |
| Hawk–Dove (anti-coordination) | $c > a,\ b > d$ | globally stable interior rest point |
| Neutral | degenerate | orbits foliate the simplex |

For three or more strategies, the simplex $\Delta$ is a triangle (or higher-dimensional polytope) and the flow field can exhibit cycles, heteroclinic orbits, and even chaotic attractors — the rock–paper–scissors game produces closed periodic orbits around the interior rest point under replicator dynamics, a fact with deep connections to the Lotka–Volterra equations of ecology [3].

> **Theorem (ESS implies asymptotic stability):** If $\sigma$ is an ESS of the symmetric game with payoff matrix $A$, then the population state $x = \sigma$ is an asymptotically stable rest point of the replicator dynamics [2].

The proof uses the Kullback–Leibler divergence $D(x^* \| x) = \sum_i x^*_i \log(x^*_i / x_i)$ as a Lyapunov function: along replicator trajectories, its time derivative is strictly negative in a neighborhood of an ESS. This is the single most important bridge between the static and dynamic theories.

### 4.2 Evolutionarily stable strategies: characterization and invasion barriers

The ESS definition admits a sharp *invasion barrier* formulation. For resident $\sigma$ and mutant $\tau$, define the post-entry fitness difference

$$\Delta(\epsilon) = u(\sigma, x_\epsilon) - u(\tau, x_\epsilon), \quad x_\epsilon = (1-\epsilon)\sigma + \epsilon\tau.$$

Expanding, $\Delta(\epsilon) = \Delta_0 + \epsilon\Delta_1$ where $\Delta_0 = u(\sigma,\sigma) - u(\tau,\sigma)$ and $\Delta_1 = u(\sigma,\tau) - u(\tau,\tau) - \Delta_0$. The ESS conditions are precisely $\Delta_0 \geq 0$, with $\Delta_1 > -\Delta_0$ slackness when $\Delta_0 = 0$. The invasion barrier is $\bar{\epsilon} = \Delta_0 / (\Delta_0 - \Delta_1)$ whenever $\Delta_1 < 0 < \Delta_0$; a mutant entering above this frequency can invade, which is why ESS is a *local* concept.

A crucial subtlety: ESS is defined against *single* mutants. A population playing an ESS can still be invaded by *two* simultaneous mutants, and mixed ESSs are vulnerable to drift along the equilibrium component. Moreover, in asymmetric games (two populations, e.g., buyers and sellers), the natural generalization is that of an *evolutionarily stable strategy pair*, and the characterization becomes more delicate.

```haskell
-- Check the two ESS conditions for a resident mixed strategy
-- against every pure-strategy mutant.
type Payoff = Double
type Matrix = [[Payoff]]

expected :: Matrix -> [Payoff] -> [Payoff] -> Payoff
expected a s t = sum [ si * sum [ tj * (a !! i !! j)
                            | (j, tj) <- zip [0..] t ]
                   | (i, si) <- zip [0..] s ]

isESS :: Matrix -> [Payoff] -> Bool
isESS a sigma = all check [0 .. n - 1]
  where
    n = length a
    check j = let tau = [if i == j then 1.0 else 0.0 | i <- [0..n-1]]
                  d0 = expected a sigma sigma - expected a tau sigma
                  d1 = expected a sigma tau - expected a tau tau
              in d0 > 1e-9 || (abs d0 <= 1e-9 && d1 > 1e-9)
```

### 4.3 Stochastic stability and equilibrium selection

Deterministic dynamics suffer an embarrassment of riches: coordination games have multiple asymptotically stable rest points, and the outcome depends on initial conditions. Foster and Young observed that real populations are perpetually perturbed by mutation and chance events, and that *persistent* noise selects differently from one-shot perturbations [5]. A state is *stochastically stable* if it retains positive weight in the ergodic distribution $\pi_\epsilon$ as the mutation rate $\epsilon$ tends to zero.

Kandori, Mailath and Rob made this operational for finite populations playing symmetric $2 \times 2$ games [6]. In each period, agents best-respond to the previous population state, but with small probability $\epsilon$ they *mutate* — play an arbitrary strategy. This yields an ergodic Markov chain over population compositions. The key theorem:

> **Theorem (KMR selection):** In a symmetric $2 \times 2$ coordination game, the unique stochastically stable equilibrium is the *risk-dominant* equilibrium — the one with the larger basin of attraction under best-response dynamics [6].

The proof technique is the *radius–coradius* argument. The *radius* of an equilibrium is the minimum number of simultaneous mutations needed to escape its basin of attraction; the *coradius* is the maximum, over all other states, of the mutations needed to reach it. If radius exceeds coradius, the equilibrium is stochastically stable. In $2 \times 2$ games, the risk-dominant equilibrium has the deeper basin, hence the larger radius, hence selection.

We formalize the mutation-selection skeleton in TLA+ to expose the exact probabilistic assumptions:

```tla
------------------------------- MODULE KMR -------------------------------
EXTENDS Naturals, FiniteSets
CONSTANTS N,          \* population size
          Epsilon     \* mutation probability (real, in (0,1))
VARIABLES pop         \* pop[i] \in {"A", "B"}: strategy of agent i

BestResponse(i) ==
  LET nA == Cardinality({j \in 1..N : pop[j] = "A"})
      nB == N - nA
      \* coordination payoffs: u(A,A)=3, u(B,B)=2, else 0
      uA == 3 * nA
      uB == 2 * nB
  IN IF uA >= uB THEN "A" ELSE "B"

Mutate(i) == /\ pop' = [pop EXCEPT ![i] = IF @ = "A" THEN "B" ELSE "A"]

Step == \E i \in 1..N :
  \/ Mutate(i)                       \* with probability Epsilon
  \/ pop' = [pop EXCEPT ![i] = BestResponse(i)]  \* best response

Init == pop \in [1..N -> {"A", "B"}]
Spec == Init /\ [][Step]_pop
================================================================================
```


### 4.4 The evolution of cooperation: five mechanisms


| Mechanism | Rule for cooperation | Intuition |
|---|---|---|
| Kin selection | $b/c > 1/r$ | help relatives; $r$ = relatedness |
| Direct reciprocity | $b/c > 1/w$ | repeated encounters; $w$ = continuation probability |
| Indirect reciprocity | $b/c > 1/q$ | reputation; $q$ = probability of knowing reputation |
| Network reciprocity | $b/c > k$ | spatial structure; $k$ = number of neighbors |
| Group selection | $b/c > 1 + n/m$ | $n$ = group size, $m$ = number of groups |






> **Theorem (Five rules):** For each of the five mechanisms above, the stated inequality on $b/c$ is necessary and sufficient (under the mechanism's idealized assumptions) for cooperators to be favored over defectors — i.e., for selection to oppose the one-shot dominance of defection [7].

## 5 Empirical/Proofs

The theory earns its keep through a chain of formal results and their empirical counterparts:

1. **ESS as Nash refinement.** The two-condition characterization (Section 2.2) is proved by expanding $\Delta(\epsilon)$ to first order; the invasion barrier formula follows immediately [1]. Every finite symmetric game possesses at least one *symmetric* Nash equilibrium, and the set of ESS is generically finite.
2. **Dynamic stability.** The Lyapunov proof via relative entropy establishes that ESS implies asymptotic stability under replicator dynamics [2]. The converse fails: asymptotically stable rest points need not be ESS (e.g., in the rock–paper–scissors game the interior rest point is Lyapunov stable but not asymptotically stable, and no ESS exists).
3. **Risk-dominance selection.** The radius–coradius argument proves that KMR dynamics select the risk-dominant equilibrium in $2 \times 2$ games [6]; Young extended the framework to general $n$-player weakly acyclic games using adaptive play with finite memory [5].
4. **Hamilton's rule.** Derived from the Price equation: with relatedness $r$ defined as the regression coefficient of recipient genotype on actor genotype, allele frequency change is proportional to $rb - c$, yielding the rule as an exact first-order condition [4].
5. **Five rules.** Each rule is derived by computing fixation probabilities or ESS conditions in the mechanism's canonical model — e.g., kin selection via inclusive-fitness payoff transformation, direct reciprocity via repeated-game equilibrium analysis, network reciprocity via pair approximation on regular graphs [7].

Empirically, the predictions hold up remarkably well:

- **Animal conflict.** Field studies of ritualized combat — from red deer roaring contests to cichlid mouth-wrestling — match the Hawk–Dove prediction that escalation is limited when injury costs exceed resource value [1].
- **Microbial cooperation.** Experiments with *Pseudomonas* siderophore producers and yeast invertase secretion confirm that spatial structure (network reciprocity) and relatedness (kin selection) quantitatively shift the cooperation threshold as the theory predicts [7].
- **Human behavioral experiments.** Public-goods games with punishment, reputation treatments, and partner-choice manipulations reproduce the $q > c/b$ logic of indirect reciprocity and the group-selection effects of intergroup competition [7].
- **Equilibrium selection in the lab.** Coordination-game experiments consistently show convergence to risk-dominant rather than payoff-dominant equilibria when strategy revision is noisy — a direct behavioral analogue of stochastic stability [6].

## 6 Limitations

The framework's power comes from idealization, and each idealization is a boundary:

- **Infinite populations.** The replicator equation assumes an infinite, well-mixed population. Finite populations introduce demographic stochasticity and fixation probabilities; the Moran process replaces the ODE, and weak-selection approximations ($N \to \infty$ with $N$ times selection intensity fixed) are needed to recover deterministic predictions.
- **Asexual inheritance.** Strategies are treated as faithfully inherited replicators. Sexual recombination, diploid genetics, and genotype–phenotype maps complicate the mapping from game-theoretic strategy to genetic architecture.
- **Local stability concepts.** ESS and asymptotic stability are local. Global dynamics on the simplex can contain multiple attractors, limit cycles, and chaotic sets; the theory says little about which basin a given initial condition falls into without stochastic perturbation.
- **Vanishing-noise limits.** Stochastic stability results concern the $\epsilon \to 0$ limit, but real mutation rates are fixed and small-but-positive. For strictly positive noise, the ergodic distribution may place substantial weight on non-stable states, and convergence can be exponentially slow in population size.
- **Mechanism idealizations.** Each cooperation rule assumes a canonical model — regular graphs, island-model group structure, perfect reputation observation — that real systems only approximate. The inequalities are guides, not laws; overlapping mechanisms interact nonlinearly in ways the five-rules taxonomy does not capture.
- **Equilibrium selection paradoxes.** Selecting the risk-dominant but Pareto-inferior equilibrium is normatively uncomfortable: evolution's answer to "which equilibrium?" can be "the worse one." Whether this is a deep truth about decentralized adaptation or an artifact of the mutation specification remains debated.

## 7 Conclusion

Evolutionary game theory replaces the rational optimizer with the reproducing population and asks what survives. The answer comes in three layers. **Statically**, the evolutionarily stable strategy of Maynard Smith and Price gives a payoff-comparison criterion for uninvadability, with an explicit invasion barrier quantifying its robustness [1]. **Dynamically**, the replicator equation of Taylor and Jonker turns payoff advantages into flow on the strategy simplex, and the ESS emerges as an asymptotically stable attractor via a relative-entropy Lyapunov function [2][3]. **Stochastically**, perpetual mutation reorders the equilibria: Foster and Young, and Kandori, Mailath and Rob, show that the vanishing-noise limit selects the risk-dominant equilibrium — not necessarily the efficient one — through the geometry of basins of attraction [5][6].

The evolution of cooperation then appears not as a paradox but as a catalog: kin selection, direct and indirect reciprocity, network reciprocity, and group selection each transform the effective game so that the cooperation inequalities hold, each derived from the same payoff-comparison machinery [4][7]. Hamilton's rule $rb > c$ is the special case where the transformation is genetic relatedness; the other four are its strategic analogues.

The open frontier is synthesis at scale: multi-mechanism models where spatial structure, reputation, and group competition interact; finite-population stochastic dynamics beyond weak selection; and the application of equilibrium-selection theory to cultural evolution, where imitation and teaching replace genetic inheritance. The mathematics — replicator fields, invasion barriers, resistance trees — will remain the common language.
