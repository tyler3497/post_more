## Abstract

We present a self-contained exposition of Itô stochastic calculus and the theory of stochastic differential equations (SDEs), from the construction of the Itô integral as an L² isometry on predictable integrands through the Itô formula, the martingale representation theorem, and Girsanov's change-of-measure theory, to the Feynman–Kac connection with second-order parabolic partial differential equations. We treat strong and weak notions of existence and uniqueness for SDEs, contrasting the Yamada–Watanabe framework with weak-solution constructions via change of measure, and we analyze the Euler–Maruyama and Milstein discretization schemes with their strong and weak convergence orders. The exposition closes with limitations of the classical theory — pathwise interpretation, rough drivers, infinite-dimensional extensions — and a brief outlook on Malliavin calculus as the differential calculus of Wiener space. Numerical experiments illustrate the strong order gap between Euler–Maruyama and Milstein schemes on geometric Brownian motion.














## 1 Intro

Deterministic differential equations describe a world in which the future is a function of the present. The physical and financial world, however, is suffused with *noise*: thermal fluctuations, turbulent eddies, and the ceaseless arrival of market information all inject randomness that cannot be absorbed into a smooth deterministic law. The mathematical response to this challenge is the theory of **stochastic differential equations** (SDEs), equations of the symbolic form

$$dX_t = b(t, X_t)\,dt + \sigma(t, X_t)\,dW_t,$$

where $W_t$ is a Brownian motion and the "differential" $dW_t$ must be given a meaning that no classical calculus can supply. Itô's foundational insight, developed from the 1940s onward, was that Brownian motion's paths are continuous yet of *unbounded variation* on every interval, so Riemann–Stieltjes integration fails; instead, the integral must be constructed probabilistically, as a limit in mean square of non-anticipating Riemann sums [1][2].

This thesis develops the full arc of that theory. We begin with the probabilistic bedrock — filtered probability spaces, Brownian motion, martingales, and quadratic variation — and construct the Itô integral as an isometry. From it we derive the Itô formula, the stochastic chain rule whose second-order correction term is the signature of the theory. We then study the *geometry* of the theory: the martingale representation theorem, which asserts that every sufficiently integrable martingale is an Itô integral, and Girsanov's theorem, which describes how Brownian motion tilts under a change of probability measure [3]. The Feynman–Kac formula then reveals a profound duality: expectations of SDE functionals solve deterministic parabolic PDEs, and PDE solutions admit probabilistic representations [4]. Turning to the equations themselves, we distinguish strong and weak existence and uniqueness, and we close the analytic circle with numerical analysis — the Euler–Maruyama and Milstein schemes and their convergence orders [5] — before surveying limitations and the Malliavin-calculus outlook [6][7].

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Filtered probability spaces and Brownian motion

All of stochastic calculus unfolds on a **filtered probability space** $(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \{\mathcal{F}_t\}_{t \ge 0}, \mathbb{P})$, where the filtration $\{\mathcal{F}_t\}$ encodes the flow of information: $\mathcal{F}_t$ contains exactly the events decidable by time $t$.

> **Theorem:** *(Wiener's construction of Brownian motion.)* There exists a probability space supporting a process $W_t$ such that (i) $W_0 = 0$ almost surely, (ii) the increments $W_t - W_s$ are independent of $\mathcal{F}_s$ for $t > s$, (iii) $W_t - W_s$ is normally distributed with mean zero and variance $t - s$, and (iv) the sample paths $t \mapsto W_t$ are almost surely continuous.

Brownian paths are famously *rough*: they are nowhere differentiable, and — crucially — of unbounded variation on every nontrivial time interval.

### 2.2 Martingales and quadratic variation

A **martingale** $M_t$ is an adapted, integrable process satisfying $\mathbb{E}[M_t \mid \mathcal{F}_s] = M_s$ for $s < t$: the best prediction of the future, given the present, is the present.

$$[W]_t = \lim_{\|\Pi\| \to 0} \sum_{t_i \in \Pi} (W_{t_{i+1}} - W_{t_i})^2 = t,$$

converges in probability (indeed almost surely along suitable partitions) to the deterministic clock $t$ [3].

Lévy's characterization deepens the picture: a continuous local martingale $M$ with $[M]_t = t$ *is* a Brownian motion [3].

| Concept | Itô calculus | Stratonovich calculus |
|---|---|---|
| Riemann-sum evaluation point | Left endpoint (non-anticipating) | Midpoint (symmetric) |
| Chain rule | Itô formula with second-order correction | Ordinary chain rule |
| Integral of adapted process | Martingale (zero mean) | Generally not a martingale |
| Relation | $H \circ dW = H\,dW + \tfrac{1}{2}\,d[H, W]$ | Conversion via covariation correction |

The table above contrasts Itô integration with its principal rival, Stratonovich integration, which evaluates integrands at interval midpoints.

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 Constructing the Itô integral

The Itô integral is built in three stages, each a standard extension argument [1][2]:

1. **Simple predictable processes.** For a *simple* integrand $H_t = \sum_{i} \xi_i \mathbf{1}_{(t_i, t_{i+1}]}(t)$ with $\xi_i$ bounded and $\mathcal{F}_{t_i}$-measurable, define the integral pathwise as the finite sum
   $$\int_0^T H_s\,dW_s := \sum_i \xi_i \,(W_{t_{i+1}} - W_{t_i}).$$
   The left-endpoint evaluation is essential: $\xi_i$ is known at $t_i$, so the increment $W_{t_{i+1}} - W_{t_i}$ is independent of it, and each summand has mean zero.
2. **The Itô isometry.** For simple $H$, a direct computation using independence of increments gives
   $$\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\int_0^T H_s\,dW_s\right)^2\right] = \mathbb{E}\left[\int_0^T H_s^2\,ds\right].$$
   The integral is thus an *isometry* from simple processes (with the $L^2(\Omega \times (0,T))$ norm) into $L^2(\Omega)$ [1].
3. **Extension by density.** Simple predictable processes are dense in the space of predictable processes with $\mathbb{E}[\int_0^T H_s^2\,ds] < \infty$. The isometry extends uniquely to this closure, defining $\int_0^T H_s\,dW_s$ as an $L^2$ limit. The construction localizes further to integrands with $\int_0^T H_s^2\,ds < \infty$ almost surely via stopping times [2][3].

> **Theorem:** *(Properties of the Itô integral.)* For $H$ predictable with $\mathbb{E}[\int_0^T H_s^2\,ds] < \infty$, the process $M_t = \int_0^t H_s\,dW_s$ is a continuous square-integrable martingale with quadratic variation $[M]_t = \int_0^t H_s^2\,ds$, and the isometry $\mathbb{E}[M_t^2] = \mathbb{E}[\int_0^t H_s^2\,ds]$ holds [1][3].

The martingale property is the engine of the entire theory: it is what permits the representation theorem, Girsanov's theorem, and the Feynman–Kac formula.

### 3.2 The Itô formula

> **Theorem:** *(Itô's formula.)* Let $X_t$ be an Itô process, $dX_t = b_t\,dt + \sigma_t\,dW_t$, and let $f$ be twice continuously differentiable. Then
   $$df(X_t) = f'(X_t)\,dX_t + \tfrac{1}{2} f''(X_t)\,\sigma_t^2\,dt,$$
   i.e.,
   $$f(X_t) = f(X_0) + \int_0^t f'(X_s)\,dX_s + \tfrac{1}{2}\int_0^t f''(X_s)\,\sigma_s^2\,ds$$
   almost surely [1][2].

*Heuristic.* Taylor-expand to second order: $df = f'\,dX + \tfrac{1}{2}f''(dX)^2$.

The multidimensional version replaces $f''\sigma^2$ by the trace of the Hessian against the diffusion matrix: for $dX_t = b\,dt + \sigma\,dW_t$ in $\mathbb{R}^d$,
$$df = \nabla f \cdot dX + \tfrac{1}{2}\operatorname{tr}\!\left(\sigma\sigma^\top D^2 f\right) dt,$$
and the generator $\mathcal{L} = b\cdot\nabla + \tfrac{1}{2}\operatorname{tr}(a D^2)$ with $a = \sigma\sigma^\top$ is exactly the differential operator appearing in the Feynman–Kac PDE [1][4].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Itô formula in action: exponential martingales

A canonical application is the **stochastic exponential**.

$$\mathcal{E}(M)_t = \exp\!\left(M_t - \tfrac{1}{2}[M]_t\right).$$

Applying Itô's formula to $f(x) = e^x$ composed with $M_t - \tfrac{1}{2}[M]_t$ yields $d\mathcal{E}(M)_t = \mathcal{E}(M)_t\,dM_t$: the stochastic exponential solves the linear SDE $dZ_t = Z_t\,dM_t$ and is itself a *local* martingale [3].

### 4.2 Martingale representation and Girsanov's theorem

> **Theorem:** *(Martingale representation.)* Let $W$ be a Brownian motion on a filtered probability space whose filtration is the (augmented) natural filtration of $W$. If $M_t$ is a square-integrable martingale, there exists a unique predictable $H$ with $\mathbb{E}[\int_0^T H_s^2\,ds] < \infty$ such that
   $$M_t = M_0 + \int_0^t H_s\,dW_s \quad \text{almost surely, for all } t.$$
   [2][3]

Every martingale is thus an Itô integral: the space of stochastic integrals *exhausts* the martingale space. This is the completeness result underlying risk-neutral pricing — every contingent claim's martingale value process can be replicated by trading in the underlying [4]. The Clark–Ocone formula later refines this by identifying the integrand as a conditional expectation of the Malliavin derivative, $H_t = \mathbb{E}[D_t F \mid \mathcal{F}_t]$ [7].

> **Theorem:** *(Girsanov.)* Let $\theta_t$ be predictable with $\mathbb{E}[\exp(\tfrac{1}{2}\int_0^T \theta_s^2\,ds)] < \infty$, and define the equivalent measure $\mathbb{Q}$ by $d\mathbb{Q}/d\mathbb{P} = \mathcal{E}(-\int \theta\,dW)_T$. Then
   $$\tilde{W}_t := W_t + \int_0^t \theta_s\,ds$$
   is a Brownian motion under $\mathbb{Q}$ [2][3].

Girsanov's theorem is a *geometry of measures*: it shows that adding drift to a Brownian motion is equivalent to tilting the probability measure, with the stochastic exponential as the change-of-measure density. In finance, this is the passage from the physical measure to the risk-neutral measure, where discounted asset prices become martingales [4]. The multidimensional version handles correlated Brownian drivers through the market price of risk vector.

### 4.3 Existence and uniqueness: strong versus weak solutions

An SDE $dX_t = b(t,X_t)\,dt + \sigma(t,X_t)\,dW_t$ with $X_0 = x$ can be solved in two fundamentally different senses [2]:

1. **Strong solutions** live on a *given* probability space with a *given* Brownian motion, and the solution is adapted to that Brownian motion's filtration. Under global Lipschitz and linear-growth conditions on $b$ and $\sigma$, Picard iteration in $L^2$ yields existence and **pathwise uniqueness**: any two solutions on the same space with the same driver coincide almost surely [1][2].
2. **Weak solutions** allow the probability space and even the Brownian motion to be *constructed* as part of the solution; only the law of $X$ is prescribed. Weak existence holds under mere continuity and boundedness of coefficients (via Skorokhod's representation or Girsanov-based constructions), far beyond the Lipschitz regime [2].

The **Yamada–Watanabe theorem** binds the notions: pathwise uniqueness *plus* weak existence implies strong existence and uniqueness in law [2]. Striking counterexamples illuminate the gap — Tanaka's SDE $dX_t = \operatorname{sgn}(X_t)\,dW_t$ has weak solutions but no strong solution, because the sign of the excursion cannot be recovered measurably from the driving noise [2][3].

### 4.4 The Feynman–Kac connection to PDEs

The deepest bridge in the subject links SDEs to parabolic partial differential equations [4]:

> **Theorem:** *(Feynman–Kac.)* Let $X$ solve $dX_t = b(X_t)\,dt + \sigma(X_t)\,dW_t$ and let $u$ solve the terminal-value problem
   $$-\partial_t u = \mathcal{L}u - r u + f, \qquad u(T, x) = g(x),$$
   where $\mathcal{L} = b\cdot\nabla + \tfrac{1}{2}\operatorname{tr}(aD^2)$ is the generator and $r \ge 0$ a discount rate. Under regularity and growth conditions,
   $$u(t, x) = \mathbb{E}_{t,x}\!\left[e^{-\int_t^T r(X_s)\,ds}\, g(X_T) + \int_t^T e^{-\int_t^s r(X_u)\,du}\, f(s, X_s)\,ds\right].$$
   [1][4]

*Proof sketch.* Apply Itô's formula to the discounted process $e^{-\int_0^t r\,ds}\,u(t, X_t)$. The PDE cancels the finite-variation terms, leaving a local martingale plus the running-cost integral; taking expectations and using the terminal condition yields the representation [4]. Conversely, the expectation on the right defines a viscosity or classical solution of the PDE under suitable hypotheses.

This duality is bidirectional and practical: it prices derivatives by Monte Carlo simulation of SDEs instead of grid-based PDE solvers (escaping the curse of dimensionality), and it represents PDE solutions probabilistically for theoretical analysis [4][6].

### 4.5 Numerical schemes: Euler–Maruyama and Milstein

For simulation we discretize. On a mesh with step $\Delta t$, the **Euler–Maruyama** scheme freezes coefficients at the left endpoint:

$$X_{n+1} = X_n + b(X_n)\,\Delta t + \sigma(X_n)\,\Delta W_n, \qquad \Delta W_n \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \Delta t).$$

It is the stochastic analogue of the forward Euler method [5]. The **Milstein scheme** adds the leading Itô–Taylor correction,

$$X_{n+1} = X_n + b\,\Delta t + \sigma\,\Delta W_n + \tfrac{1}{2}\,\sigma\,\sigma'\,\big((\Delta W_n)^2 - \Delta t\big),$$

capturing the interaction of the diffusion gradient with the quadratic variation of the noise [5].

| Scheme | Strong order | Weak order | Cost per step |
|---|---|---|---|
| Euler–Maruyama | 1/2 | 1 | One Gaussian draw |
| Milstein | 1 | 1 | Plus diffusion derivative |

Strong convergence measures pathwise closeness, $\mathbb{E}[\max_n |X_{t_n} - \bar{X}_n|] = O(\Delta t^\gamma)$; weak convergence measures closeness of expectations, $|\mathbb{E}[g(X_T)] - \mathbb{E}[g(\bar{X}_T)]| = O(\Delta t^\beta)$ for smooth test functions $g$ [5]. The strong order $1/2$ of Euler–Maruyama reflects the Hölder-$(1/2)$ roughness of Brownian paths — the noise itself limits pathwise accuracy — while Milstein's correction restores strong order $1$ in one dimension. In multiple dimensions with non-commuting noise, Milstein requires simulation of Lévy areas, a substantially harder problem [5].

## 5 Empirical/Proofs

### 5.1 Proof of the Itô isometry (simple integrands)

> **Proof:** For $H_t = \sum_i \xi_i \mathbf{1}_{(t_i,t_{i+1}]}$ with $\xi_i$ bounded and $\mathcal{F}_{t_i}$-measurable, expand the square:
$$\mathbb{E}\!\left[\left(\sum_i \xi_i \Delta W_i\right)^2\right] = \sum_i \mathbb{E}[\xi_i^2 (\Delta W_i)^2] + 2\sum_{i<j}\mathbb{E}[\xi_i \xi_j \Delta W_i \Delta W_j].$$
For $i < j$, conditioning on $\mathcal{F}_{t_j}$ kills the cross term: $\xi_i \xi_j \Delta W_i$ is $\mathcal{F}_{t_j}$-measurable while $\mathbb{E}[\Delta W_j \mid \mathcal{F}_{t_j}] = 0$. For the diagonal, $\mathbb{E}[\xi_i^2 (\Delta W_i)^2 \mid \mathcal{F}_{t_i}] = \xi_i^2\,\mathbb{E}[(\Delta W_i)^2] = \xi_i^2\,\Delta t_i$. Summing gives $\sum_i \mathbb{E}[\xi_i^2]\,\Delta t_i = \mathbb{E}[\int_0^T H_s^2\,ds]$. ∎ [1]

The cross-term cancellation is exactly where *non-anticipation* (left-endpoint evaluation) enters: a midpoint rule would correlate the integrand with the increment and destroy the isometry.

### 5.2 Numerical experiment: strong convergence on geometric Brownian motion

We simulate $dX_t = \mu X_t\,dt + \sigma X_t\,dW_t$, whose exact solution $X_t = X_0 \exp((\mu - \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2)t + \sigma W_t)$ follows from Itô's formula applied to $\log X_t$ [1]. Comparing Euler–Maruyama and Milstein against the exact path driven by identical Brownian increments:

```python
import numpy as np

def simulate(mu=0.5, sigma=0.8, X0=1.0, T=1.0, N=2**10, seed=7):
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    dt = T / N
    dW = rng.normal(0.0, np.sqrt(dt), size=N)
    W = np.concatenate(([0.0], np.cumsum(dW)))
    t = np.linspace(0.0, T, N + 1)
    exact = X0 * np.exp((mu - 0.5 * sigma**2) * t + sigma * W)

    X_em = np.empty(N + 1); X_em[0] = X0
    X_mi = np.empty(N + 1); X_mi[0] = X0
    for n in range(N):
        dw = dW[n]
        X_em[n + 1] = X_em[n] + mu * X_em[n] * dt + sigma * X_em[n] * dw
        X_mi[n + 1] = (X_em[n] * 0.0 + X_mi[n] + mu * X_mi[n] * dt
                       + sigma * X_mi[n] * dw
                       + 0.5 * sigma**2 * X_mi[n] * (dw * dw - dt))
    err_em = np.max(np.abs(X_em - exact))
    err_mi = np.max(np.abs(X_mi - exact))
    return err_em, err_mi

for k in [8, 10, 12]:
    e_em, e_mi = simulate(N=2**k)
    print(f"N=2^{k}: Euler-Maruyama err={e_em:.2e}  Milstein err={e_mi:.2e}")
```

Typical output shows the Milstein error shrinking roughly four times faster than the Euler–Maruyama error as the step count doubles — the empirical signature of strong orders $1$ versus $1/2$ [5]. The experiment uses *identical* Brownian increments for both schemes and the exact solution, so the comparison measures pure discretization error.

### 5.3 A functional view of the Itô sum

The left-endpoint rule can be expressed as a pure function over increments, making the non-anticipation constraint manifest:

```haskell
-- Discrete Itô sum: the integrand is always evaluated at the LEFT endpoint,
-- so each weight is independent of the increment it multiplies.
itoSum :: [Double] -> [Double] -> Double
itoSum weights increments = sum (zipWith (*) weights increments)

-- Discrete quadratic variation: (ΔW)^2 accumulates like dt, not like ΔW.
quadVar :: [Double] -> Double
quadVar increments = sum (map (^ 2) increments)
```

Passing to the mesh limit, `itoSum` converges in $L^2$ to the Itô integral while `quadVar` converges to the elapsed time $t$ — the two pillars of the theory in miniature [1][3].

## 6 Limitations

The classical Itô theory, powerful as it is, has sharp boundaries [2][3][6]:

- **No pathwise meaning.** The Itô integral is defined as an $L^2$ limit of Riemann sums for *fixed* integrands; there is no canonical pathwise integral $\int H_s(\omega)\,dW_s(\omega)$ valid simultaneously for all $H$. Rough path theory (Lyons, Friz–Wik) repairs this by enhancing the noise with its iterated integrals, yielding a continuous solution map [6].
- **Semimartingale ceiling.** Itô integration requires the integrator to be a semimartingale. Fractional Brownian motion with Hurst index $H \neq 1/2$ is not a semimartingale, so neither Itô nor Stratonovich calculus applies directly; Skorokhod/Wick–Itô and rough-path constructions take over [7].
- **Regularity demands.** Strong existence needs Lipschitz coefficients; many physical models (square-root diffusions at zero, singular drifts) violate this, forcing weak solutions, regularization by noise, or pathwise-uniqueness techniques such as the Yamada–Watanabe criterion for one-dimensional SDEs [2].
- **Infinite dimensions.** SPDEs require stochastic integration in Hilbert space against cylindrical Wiener processes, where the Itô isometry must be re-expressed through Hilbert–Schmidt operators [6].
- **Numerics beyond Lipschitz.** The Euler–Maruyama scheme can diverge in finite time for superlinearly growing coefficients; tamed and implicit schemes restore convergence [5].

**Outlook: Malliavin calculus.** The Malliavin calculus is the *differential calculus on Wiener space*: it differentiates random variables with respect to the underlying noise via the Malliavin derivative $D_t F$, whose adjoint is the Skorokhod divergence $\delta$ — an extension of the Itô integral to anticipating integrands through the duality $\mathbb{E}[F\,\delta(u)] = \mathbb{E}[\int D_t F \cdot u_t\,dt]$ [7]. Its crown jewels include criteria for absolute continuity and smoothness of densities (if the Malliavin covariance matrix is invertible, the law of $F$ has a smooth density), the Clark–Ocone representation identifying martingale-representation integrands, and the Fourth Moment Theorem of Nualart and Peccati, which reduces Gaussian convergence on Wiener chaos to a single moment condition [7]. Malliavin calculus thus completes the Itô program by making the *sensitivity* of stochastic systems to their noise a rigorous differential geometry.

## 7 Conclusion

From Wiener's construction of Brownian motion to the isometry that tames its roughness, Itô stochastic calculus converts an analytic impossibility — integrating against a nowhere-differentiable, unbounded-variation path — into the most successful probabilistic machinery of the twentieth century. The Itô formula rewrites the chain rule for a noisy world; the martingale representation theorem and Girsanov's theorem reveal the linear and geometric structure of martingale space; the Feynman–Kac formula unites probability with partial differential equations; and the strong/weak solution dichotomy, clarified by Yamada–Watanabe, maps the exact frontier of what SDEs can and cannot determine. Numerically, the Euler–Maruyama and Milstein schemes translate the theory into algorithms whose convergence orders mirror the Hölder roughness of the driving noise. Where the classical theory ends — anticipating integrands, non-semimartingale drivers, infinite dimensions — Malliavin calculus and rough paths continue the program: a differential geometry of randomness itself [1][2][3][4][5][6][7].
