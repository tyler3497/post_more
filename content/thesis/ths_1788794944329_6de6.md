# Neural Tangent Kernels: Infinite-Width Limits, Gaussian Process Correspondence, and Lazy Training Dynamics

## Abstract

The Neural Tangent Kernel (NTK) provides one of the most consequential bridges ever constructed between deep learning and classical kernel theory. We develop the full NTK program from first principles: the infinite-width Gaussian process correspondence at initialization, the derivation of the NTK as the kernel governing gradient-descent training dynamics in function space, and the linearization argument that makes wide-network training exactly equivalent to kernel regression. We prove that under gradient flow with mean-squared error loss, the network predictor follows a linear differential equation whose convergence is dictated by the eigenstructure of the limiting kernel, giving a principled spectral-bias account of generalization. We then survey finite-width corrections via the Neural Tangent Hierarchy, the empirical NTK and its evolution during training, and the lazy-versus-rich training dichotomy, showing how kernel dynamics illuminate double descent and the grokking phenomenon. The treatment is mathematically rigorous yet grounded in verifiable experiments, positioning the NTK as both a theoretical instrument and a diagnostic tool for modern architecture design.

## 1 Introduction

The empirical success of deep neural networks has long outpaced our theoretical understanding of *why* gradient-based training of massively over-parameterized models converges at all, let alone generalizes. Classical learning theory, predicated on capacity control, appears to contradict practice: networks with far more parameters than training examples routinely interpolate their training data while retaining strong out-of-sample performance. The Neural Tangent Kernel framework, introduced by Jacot, Gabriel, and Hongler [1], resolves this tension by revealing that training of sufficiently wide networks is *exactly* a kernel method — albeit one whose kernel is induced by the network's own gradient geometry rather than hand-designed.

The central insight is deceptively simple. Consider gradient descent on a network's parameters $\theta$ with cost $C(\theta) = \ell(f_\theta(X), y)$. The function-space trajectory of the predictor $f_{\theta_t}$ is governed not by the non-convex parameter landscape but by a positive semi-definite kernel,

$$\Theta_t(x, x') = \langle \nabla_\theta f(x; \theta_t), \nabla_\theta f(x'; \theta_t) \rangle,$$

which transports parameter-space gradients into function-space updates. The remarkable fact [1] is that, in the infinite-width limit, $\Theta_t$ converges to a deterministic kernel $\Theta^\infty$ that remains *constant throughout training*. The network then evolves as a linear model in its own tangent features, and least-squares training reduces to kernel gradient descent with a closed-form solution. This chapter develops the derivation, the convergence theorem, the Gaussian process correspondence that precedes it, the refinements that quantify finite-width departures, and the implications for lazy training, double descent, and grokking.

> **Theorem (Informal NTK convergence):** For a fully-connected network with widths $n_1, \dots, n_{L-1} \to \infty$ sequentially, the empirical NTK $\Theta_t$ converges in probability to a deterministic limiting kernel $\Theta^\infty$ at initialization, and $\sup_{t \in [0,T]} \|\Theta_t - \Theta^\infty\| = o_P(1)$ along gradient flow. Training dynamics then coincide with kernel regression under $\Theta^\infty$ [1].

The stakes of this program extend well beyond a convergence proof. The NTK eigenspectrum predicts *which* functions a network learns first (spectral bias), explains why early stopping acts as implicit regularization, and furnishes a diagnostic — the *empirical* NTK measured at finite width — whose motion during training quantifies feature learning beyond the kernel regime [6]. Our goal is a complete, technically precise account of this landscape.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The Gaussian process correspondence

The NTK program rests on a classical predecessor: infinitely wide networks at initialization are Gaussian processes. This was known for shallow networks since Neal [7], and was extended to deep architectures by Lee et al. [2] and Matthews et al., with a fully general treatment for arbitrary depth appearing in Lee et al. [4].

Consider an $L$-layer fully-connected network with NTK parameterization,

$$f(x; \theta) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{n_{L-1}}} W^{(L)} \phi\big(h^{(L-1)}(x)\big) + \beta b^{(L)}, \qquad h^{(\ell)}(x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{n_{\ell-1}}} W^{(\ell)} \phi\big(h^{(\ell-1)}(x)\big) + \beta b^{(\ell)},$$

with $W^{(\ell)}_{ij}, b^{(\ell)}_i \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$ i.i.d. and $\beta$ controlling bias variance. By the central limit theorem, as $n_\ell \to \infty$, each pre-activation $h^{(\ell)}_i(x)$ converges in distribution to a centered Gaussian process with covariance kernel $\Sigma^{(\ell)}$ satisfying the deterministic recursion

$$\Sigma^{(1)}(x, x') = \frac{1}{n_0} x^\top x' + \beta^2, \qquad \Sigma^{(\ell+1)}(x, x') = \mathbb{E}_{(u,v) \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \Sigma^{(\ell)})}[\phi(u)\phi(v)] + \beta^2.$$

The resulting **NNGP kernel** $\Sigma^{(L)}$ describes the prior over functions induced by random initialization. Crucially, the NNGP governs the *initial* function distribution, while the NTK governs *training dynamics* — the two kernels are related but distinct, a distinction we make precise below.

### 2.2 Kernel gradient descent in function space

For a differentiable model $f(\cdot; \theta)$ and cost $C(\theta) = \Phi(f(\cdot; \theta))$ where $\Phi$ is a functional of the predictor, the chain rule yields the function-space dynamics under gradient flow $\dot{\theta}_t = -\nabla_\theta C(\theta_t)$:

$$\partial_t f_t(x) = -\sum_{x'} \Theta_t(x, x') \, \partial_{f(x')} \Phi(f_t), \qquad \Theta_t(x,x') = \langle \nabla_\theta f(x;\theta_t), \nabla_\theta f(x';\theta_t) \rangle.$$

For mean-squared error $\Phi(f) = \tfrac{1}{2}\|f(X) - y\|^2$ on a dataset of $n$ points, this becomes the linear system $\dot{f}_t = -\eta\, \Theta_t (f_t - y)$. The parameter-space non-convexity has vanished from the picture: all that matters is the kernel $\Theta_t$ and its trajectory.

### 2.3 Related results

The NTK paper [1] appeared alongside a cluster of results establishing global convergence of gradient descent for over-parameterized networks via linearization: Du et al. [3] for two-layer ReLU networks, Allen-Zhu, Li, and Song for deep ReLU networks, and the lazy-training analysis of Chizat, Oyallon, and Bach [5], who showed that the phenomenon is fundamentally a *scaling* effect rather than an architectural one. Arora et al. [6] provided exact, efficiently computable formulas for the limiting NTK and demonstrated that kernel regression with $\Theta^\infty$ matches or exceeds finite-network performance on small datasets.

| Concept | Kernel | Role |
|---|---|---|
| NNGP $\Sigma^{(L)}$ | Covariance of pre-activations | Prior over functions at initialization [2][4] |
| Limiting NTK $\Theta^\infty$ | Gradient-feature inner product | Governs training dynamics in the infinite-width limit [1] |
| Empirical NTK $\Theta_t$ | Finite-width, time-varying | Diagnostic of feature learning beyond the kernel regime [6] |
| Neural Tangent Hierarchy $\{O_t^{(r)}\}$ | Higher-order gradient tensors | Quantifies finite-width corrections of order $1/m$ [9] |

## 3 Methodology

Our methodology is analytic: we derive the NTK from gradient-flow first principles, take the infinite-width limit using the Gaussian process correspondence, and solve the resulting linear dynamics exactly. We complement the theory with a computational protocol — implemented below — that measures the empirical NTK of finite networks and compares it against the closed-form limiting kernel, following the experimental design of Arora et al. [6] and the empirical-NTK tracking of Fort et al. [6, 10].

**Derivation of the limiting NTK recursion.** Differentiating the network recursion and applying the law of large numbers layer by layer yields the deterministic kernel recursion. Define $\dot{\Sigma}^{(\ell)}(x,x') = \mathbb{E}_{(u,v)\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma^{(\ell-1)})}[\phi'(u)\phi'(v)]$. Then the NTK of an $L$-layer network satisfies [1][6]:

$$\Theta^{(1)} = \Sigma^{(1)}, \qquad \Theta^{(\ell+1)}(x,x') = \Sigma^{(\ell+1)}(x,x') + \Theta^{(\ell)}(x,x') \cdot \dot{\Sigma}^{(\ell+1)}(x,x').$$

The first term is the contribution of the top-layer weights (proportional to the NNGP covariance), and the second accumulates the contributions of all lower layers, weighted by the expected derivative covariance. For ReLU activations, both $\Sigma$ and $\dot{\Sigma}$ admit closed forms in terms of the angle between inputs, enabling exact computation without Monte Carlo sampling.

**Solving the training dynamics.** Under gradient flow with learning rate $\eta$ and MSE loss, the infinite-width dynamics on the training set are $\dot{u}_t = -\eta\, \Theta^\infty (u_t - y)$ with $u_t = f_t(X)$, whose solution is

$$u_t = y + e^{-\eta \Theta^\infty t}(u_0 - y), \qquad f_t(x) = f_0(x) - \Theta^\infty(x, X)\, (\Theta^\infty)^{-1}\big(I - e^{-\eta \Theta^\infty t}\big)(u_0 - y).$$

Convergence is exponential with rate governed by the smallest eigenvalue $\lambda_{\min}(\Theta^\infty)$; positive-definiteness of $\Theta^\infty$ (proved in [1] for non-polynomial activations on spherical data) is therefore the precise condition for global convergence. The spectral decomposition further reveals *spectral bias*: error components along top eigenvectors of $\Theta^\infty$ decay first, so early stopping preferentially retains smooth, low-complexity functions [1].

The following reference implementation computes the empirical NTK and the exact limiting kernel for a two-layer ReLU network, enabling direct verification of the convergence $\Theta_t \to \Theta^\infty$:

```python
import torch
import numpy as np

def empirical_ntk(model, X: torch.Tensor) -> torch.Tensor:
    """Finite-width empirical NTK via Jacobian inner products."""
    params = [p for p in model.parameters() if p.requires_grad]
    def fwd(x):
        return model(x.unsqueeze(0)).squeeze(0)
    J = torch.func.jacrev(fwd)(X)          # (n, out, *param_shapes)
    J = torch.cat([j.reshape(X.shape[0], -1) for j in torch.func.jacrev(fwd)(X).values()], dim=1)
    return J @ J.T

def limiting_ntk_relu(X: np.ndarray, beta: float = 0.0) -> np.ndarray:
    """Closed-form limiting NTK for 2-layer ReLU net, NTK parameterization [1][6]."""
    Xn = X / np.linalg.norm(X, axis=1, keepdims=True)
    cos_t = np.clip(Xn @ Xn.T, -1.0, 1.0)
    theta = np.arccos(cos_t)
    # NNGP covariance (arcsine kernel for ReLU)
    Sigma = (np.linalg.norm(X, axis=1, keepdims=True)
             @ np.linalg.norm(X, axis=1, keepdims=True).T) \
            * (np.sin(theta) + (np.pi - theta) * cos_t) / (2 * np.pi) + beta**2
    # Derivative covariance
    Sigma_dot = (np.pi - theta) / (2 * np.pi)
    return Sigma + Sigma * Sigma_dot   # Theta = Sigma + Sigma .* Sigma_dot
```

A second script tracks the kernel's motion during training, the key diagnostic for departing the lazy regime:

```python
def track_kernel_motion(model, X, y, steps=500, lr=0.1):
    """Frobenius distance of empirical NTK from its initialization [10]."""
    K0 = empirical_ntk(model, X)
    opt = torch.optim.SGD(model.parameters(), lr=lr)
    traj = []
    for t in range(steps):
        opt.zero_grad()
        loss = ((model(X).squeeze() - y) ** 2).mean()
        loss.backward(); opt.step()
        if t % 50 == 0:
            Kt = empirical_ntk(model, X)
            traj.append(((Kt - K0).norm() / K0.norm()).item())
    return traj   # ~flat in lazy regime; grows when features are learned
```

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The lazy training regime and the linearization argument

Chizat, Oyallon, and Bach [5] reframed NTK convergence as a consequence of *scaling*. Consider the one-parameter family of models $f_\alpha(\theta, x) = \alpha^{-1} \big(h(\alpha \theta, x) - h(0, x)\big)$ for a scale $\alpha > 0$. As $\alpha \to \infty$, the model converges uniformly to its linearization around initialization:

$$f_\alpha(\theta, x) \;\xrightarrow[\alpha\to\infty]{}\; \langle \nabla_\theta h(0, x),\, \theta \rangle.$$

The crucial observation is that standard NTK parameterization of neural networks corresponds precisely to this large-$\alpha$ scaling: the $1/\sqrt{m}$ prefactor suppresses each neuron's individual contribution, so that fitting the data requires only $O(1/\sqrt{m})$ movement of each parameter. Because parameters barely move, the tangent features $\nabla_\theta f(x; \theta_t)$ are approximately frozen, the kernel stays constant, and training is *lazy* — the model behaves as a linear method in a fixed, random feature space. Chizat et al. [5] prove bounds on the deviation between the lazy trajectory and the linearized one, and — critically — show empirically that forcing conventional CNNs into the lazy regime *degrades* their performance, suggesting that practical deep learning success relies on escaping laziness.

> **Theorem (Lazy training, [5]):** Under smoothness and curvature conditions on $h$, gradient flow on $f_\alpha$ with $\alpha$ large satisfies $\sup_{t \le T}\|f_\alpha(\theta_t) - \bar{f}(\bar{\theta}_t)\| = O(1/\alpha)$, where $\bar{f}$ is the linearized model. The required parameter displacement scales as $O(1/\alpha)$ while the function-space displacement is $O(1)$.

### 4.2 Finite-width corrections: the Neural Tangent Hierarchy

The infinite-width picture is exact only asymptotically. Huang and Yau [9] developed the **Neural Tangent Hierarchy (NTH)**, a systematic $1/m$ expansion of the dynamics. The key insight is that the time derivative of the empirical NTK is itself driven by a higher-order kernel:

$$\partial_t \Theta_t(x_\alpha, x_\beta) = -\frac{1}{n}\sum_{\gamma} O_t^{(3)}(x_\alpha, x_\beta, x_\gamma)\, (f_t(x_\gamma) - y_\gamma),$$

where $O_t^{(3)}$ is a third-order "tangent kernel" built from second derivatives of the network. This generates an infinite hierarchy in which the $r$-th order kernel $O_t^{(r)}$ evolves under the $(r+1)$-th, with each level suppressed by an additional factor of $1/m$. Truncating at any fixed order yields a closed, finite system of ODEs whose solution approximates true gradient-flow dynamics with error $O(m^{-r/2})$. The NTH thus provides the first *quantitative* account of how kernel constancy breaks down at finite width — and it predicts precisely the kernel motion that Fort et al. [10] measure empirically.

### 4.3 Empirical NTK versus true dynamics

At finite width, the empirical NTK $\Theta_t$ is both random at initialization and time-varying. Fort et al. [10] conducted a careful empirical study of its evolution and found a striking pattern: during early training, the kernel changes rapidly (the *kernel learning* phase), then stabilizes; moreover, the kernel *aligns* with the target function, in the sense that the top eigenvectors of $\Theta_t$ progressively concentrate on the label vector $y$. This alignment is impossible in the strict infinite-width limit and constitutes direct evidence of feature learning. The magnitude of kernel motion correlates with the generalization gap between the network and its NTK linearization: tasks on which the kernel moves substantially are precisely those where finite networks outperform their kernel counterparts [10]. This gives practitioners a concrete diagnostic — track $\|\Theta_t - \Theta_0\|_F / \|\Theta_0\|_F$ during training — to determine whether a given problem is being solved in the kernel regime or the feature-learning regime.

### 4.4 Connections to double descent and grokking

The NTK framework illuminates two of the most puzzling modern phenomena.

**Double descent.** Belkin et al. [8] showed that test error as a function of model capacity follows a double-descent curve: classical U-shaped bias–variance behavior up to the interpolation threshold, then *decreasing* error with further over-parameterization. In the NTK regime this is quantitatively explicable: beyond the interpolation threshold, the minimum-norm interpolant in the RKHS of $\Theta^\infty$ becomes smoother as width grows (the kernel's effective rank concentrates on benign directions), and benign overfitting results. The second descent is thus, in the lazy regime, a statement about the *spectral decay* of the limiting kernel.

**Grokking.** Power et al. [11] observed that small transformers trained on algorithmic tasks memorize the training set rapidly yet generalize only after vastly longer training — a delayed transition from memorization to generalization. The NTK lens interprets grokking as a *kernel-to-rich transition*: early training operates in an approximately lazy regime where the network fits data with the initial tangent features (memorization via the initial kernel), while the slow drift of $\Theta_t$ — governed by the higher levels of the NTH [9] — gradually rotates the kernel toward features that support generalization. Interventions that amplify kernel motion (large learning rates, weight decay, particular initializations) accelerate grokking, consistent with the view that grokking *is* the escape from the NTK regime [10][11].

| Regime | Parameter motion | Kernel behavior | Generalization mechanism |
|---|---|---|---|
| Lazy / NTK | $O(1/\sqrt{m})$, negligible | Frozen at $\Theta^\infty$ | Min-norm interpolation in fixed RKHS [1][5] |
| Rich / feature learning | $O(1)$, substantial | $\Theta_t$ evolves, aligns with $y$ | Learned representations [10] |
| Grokking transition | Two timescales | Slow kernel rotation | Escape from initial kernel [9][11] |

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

We now consolidate the theoretical guarantees and the empirical record.

**Proof sketch: constancy of the limiting NTK.** The argument in [1] proceeds in three steps. (i) At initialization, each entry $\Theta_0(x, x')$ is an average of $m$ i.i.d. terms (one per hidden neuron, in the two-layer case), hence converges in probability to its expectation $\Theta^\infty(x,x')$ by the weak law of large numbers, with fluctuations of order $1/\sqrt{m}$. (ii) Along gradient flow, parameter displacement is bounded: because the initial residual is $O(1)$ while each parameter contributes $O(1/\sqrt{m})$ to the output, fitting requires only $O(1/\sqrt{m})$ movement per parameter, uniformly over any fixed time horizon. (iii) The kernel's sensitivity to parameters is itself $O(1/\sqrt{m})$ per parameter, so total kernel motion is $O(1/m) \times m = O(1/\sqrt{m}) \to 0$. The extension to deep networks [1] applies this reasoning layer by layer, with the recursion of Section 3 emerging from the chain rule.

**Proof sketch: positive-definiteness.** Jacot et al. [1] prove $\Theta^\infty$ is strictly positive definite when inputs lie on the sphere $\mathbb{S}^{d-1}$ and the activation is non-polynomial. The argument exploits the spherical-harmonics (Gegenbauer) expansion: non-polynomial $\phi$ yields strictly positive coefficients at infinitely many even *and* odd orders, and the Hadamard-product structure of the recursion preserves strict positivity on distinct points. Positive-definiteness implies $\lambda_{\min}(\Theta^\infty) > 0$, hence exponential convergence of gradient flow and interpolation of the training data.

**Empirical record.** Three findings are now robust across studies:

1. *Wide networks match kernel predictions.* Lee et al. [2] and Arora et al. [6] show that as width increases, trained-network accuracy approaches that of exact NNGP/NTK inference, and that NTK kernel regression is competitive with finite networks on small-data tasks (UCI, small CIFAR-10 subsets).
2. *The kernel moves, and motion matters.* Fort et al. [10] demonstrate that $\Theta_t$ changes substantially during standard training, that the change is concentrated in the top eigenspace, and that kernel alignment with labels predicts the finite-network advantage over the NTK baseline.
3. *Laziness is not the whole story.* Chizat et al. [5] show that pushing deep CNNs into the lazy regime (via large-$\alpha$ scaling) *hurts* performance on CIFAR-10, establishing that practical networks exploit the rich regime. The NTK is therefore best viewed as a baseline theory — exact in a well-defined limit, and a diagnostic ruler for measuring departures from it.

```haskell
-- Type-level sketch: the NTK as a bilinear form on tangent features
-- (illustrative; real computation uses the closed-form recursion of Section 3)
type TangentFeatures params input = params -> input -> Vector Double

ntk :: TangentFeatures p i -> i -> i -> p -> Double
ntk feats x x' theta = dot (feats theta x) (feats theta x')
-- gradientFlow: du/dt = -eta * K (u - y);  K frozen  <=>  lazy regime
```

## 6 Limitations

The NTK program, for all its elegance, has sharp boundaries that must be stated plainly.

First, the infinite-width limit is an *idealization* whose quantitative predictions degrade at practical widths and depths. The $O(1/\sqrt{m})$ fluctuations of the empirical kernel are non-negligible for the widths used in production, and the NTH corrections [9], while principled, become unwieldy beyond low orders. Second, the theory is developed primarily for gradient flow with MSE loss and NTK parameterization; cross-entropy training, adaptive optimizers (Adam), normalization layers, and attention mechanisms each perturb the picture, and several require distinct analyses. Third — and most substantively — the NTK regime provably *cannot* explain the representation-learning successes of deep learning: any fixed-kernel method is limited by its RKHS, and Chizat et al. [5] demonstrate that the lazy regime underperforms on standard vision benchmarks. The NTK is therefore a theory of the *kernel component* of deep learning, not a complete theory of deep learning.

Finally, there are computational limits: exact limiting-NTK inference requires $O(n^3)$ kernel matrix inversion, restricting it to modest dataset sizes, and the empirical NTK of large models is expensive to materialize, though Jacobian-vector-product techniques mitigate this.

## 7 Conclusion

The Neural Tangent Kernel transformed our understanding of neural network training by exposing its hidden kernel structure: in the infinite-width limit, gradient descent on parameters is exactly kernel gradient descent in function space, with dynamics governed by a deterministic, constant kernel $\Theta^\infty$ derived from the network's gradient geometry [1]. The Gaussian process correspondence [2][4][7] provides the probabilistic foundation; the linearization and lazy-training analysis [5] explains *why* the limit holds; the Neural Tangent Hierarchy [9] quantifies finite-width departures; and empirical studies [6][10] map where real networks live relative to the kernel baseline — including the double-descent [8] and grokking [11] phenomena that the kernel lens renders intelligible as spectral and dynamical effects.

The enduring value of the NTK program is twofold. As *theory*, it remains the only framework that proves global convergence of gradient-based training for realistic architectures from initialization. As *diagnostic*, the empirical NTK gives practitioners a principled instrument for asking the central question of modern deep learning: is my network merely interpolating in a fixed feature space, or is it genuinely learning features? The next frontier — a quantitative theory of the rich regime with the rigor of the NTK analysis — will likely be built on the hierarchy and kernel-motion ideas surveyed here.
