# From Static Single Assignment to Register Allocation: Algorithms and Engineering in Modern Optimizing Compilers

## Abstract

Program representation and register assignment are the twin load-bearing pillars of the optimizing compiler middle and back end. This thesis unifies their theory and practice: we develop the *Cytron algorithm* for static single assignment (SSA) construction — dominance-frontier φ-function placement followed by dominator-tree renaming — prove its minimality, and show how SSA's single-definition invariant unlocks sparse, scalable global value numbering [1][2]. We then trace the path from SSA through liveness analysis to register allocation, contrasting the NP-complete graph-coloring formulation of Chaitin [6] and its refinements — Briggs's optimistic coloring [5] and George and Appel's iterated register coalescing [4] — with Poletto and Sarkar's linear-time linear scan [3] and LLVM's modern greedy allocator [7]. We prove the classical reduction from graph coloring, present the chordal-graph characterization of SSA interference graphs that makes SSA allocation almost-linear, and explain why classical SSA *elimination* nevertheless remains NP-complete. Empirical results from the literature are analyzed: linear scan delivers code within 12% of aggressive graph coloring at a fraction of the compile-time cost.

## 1. Introduction

Every optimizing compiler is a negotiation between two scarcity regimes. In the *middle end*, the scarce resource is *analysis precision per unit of compile time*: the optimizer must reason globally about data flow across thousands of basic blocks without drowning in bit-vector computations. In the *back end*, the scarce resource is *physical registers*: the allocator must map an unbounded set of temporaries onto a few dozen machine registers while minimizing spill traffic to memory. For three decades, the field's central insight has been that these two negotiations are coupled through the *program representation* itself.

**Static single assignment (SSA)** form — in which every variable is defined exactly once and control-flow merges are reconciled by *φ-functions* — transformed the middle end by replacing dense use–def chains with sparse, explicit def–use edges [1]. **Graph-coloring register allocation** — in which liveness interference becomes a graph and registers become colors — transformed the back end by turning an ad hoc packing problem into a well-studied combinatorial one [6]. And the two are deeply entangled: the interference graphs of strict SSA programs are *chordal* and therefore optimally colorable in linear time, a structural fact that underwrites the modern decoupled "SSA allocation" pipeline [6].

This thesis presents a self-contained development of both subjects and their interface:

1. *SSA construction*: dominance, dominance frontiers, φ-placement, and renaming, with the Cytron algorithm and its minimality theorem.
2. *SSA-enabled optimization*: global value numbering as the canonical sparse consumer of SSA.
3. *Register allocation*: from Chaitin's coloring formulation through Briggs, George–Appel, and the linear scan revolution, to LLVM's production greedy allocator.
4. *The complexity interface*: why allocation is NP-complete in general, polynomial on strict SSA, and NP-complete again after classical SSA destruction.

Our treatment is aimed at the compiler engineer and the theoretically inclined reader alike: every algorithm is presented with enough detail to implement, and every major claim is anchored in the primary literature.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 Dominance and the control-flow graph

All of SSA construction rests on one relation.

> **Definition (Dominance).** A node *d* *dominates* a node *n*, written *d* ⪰ *n*, if every path from *entry* to *n* passes through *d*. Node *d* *strictly dominates* *n* if *d* ⪰ *n* and *d ≠ n*. The *immediate dominator* *idom(n)* is the unique strict dominator of *n* that is dominated by all other strict dominators of *n*.

The immediate-dominator relation forms a tree — the *dominator tree* — rooted at *entry*, computable in near-linear time by the Lengauer–Tarjan algorithm.

From dominance, Cytron et al.

> **Definition (Dominance frontier).** The *dominance frontier* of a node *x*, denoted *DF(x)*, is the set of nodes *y* such that *x* dominates some predecessor of *y* but does not strictly dominate *y*:
>
> *DF(x) = { y ∈ V | ∃p ∈ pred(y): x ⪰ p ∧ ¬(x ≻ y) }.*

The frontier is exactly the "edge" of *x*'s region of guaranteed execution: control paths that *x* dominated have just *escaped* its dominance.

### 2.2 A brief history of the two problems

| Era | Representation | Allocation | Key idea |

## 3. Methodology

Our methodology is *reconstructive and comparative*.

Concretely:

1. **SSA construction** is developed through the two-phase Cytron algorithm: (a) φ-placement via the iterated dominance frontier computed by a worklist algorithm; (b) renaming via a preorder walk of the dominator tree maintaining per-variable version stacks. Minimality is proved: the algorithm inserts a φ-function *iff* one is required [1].
2. **GVN** is developed as the partition-refinement algorithm of Alpern, Wegman, and Zadeck [2], then lifted to SSA, where φ-functions become ordinary operators in the congruence.
3. **Register allocation** is developed in three generations: (i) Chaitin-style coloring with the simplify/select/spill loop and its NP-completeness reduction; (ii) the coalescing lineage — conservative (Briggs/George) criteria and Appel–George iterated coalescing; (iii) linear scan and its production descendant, LLVM's greedy allocator.
4. **Complexity analysis** states and sketches proofs for: allocation ≡ *k*-coloring is NP-complete for *k ≥ 3*; strict-SSA interference graphs are chordal and hence greedily optimally colorable; classical SSA destruction restores NP-completeness.

All algorithms are stated at a level of detail sufficient for direct implementation; the Haskell and Rust sketches in §4 are type-correct in spirit and mirror real compiler code (GHC's, LLVM's) in structure.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Cytron algorithm: dominance frontiers and φ placement

The naïve approach to SSA — insert a φ-function for every variable at every join point, then delete the dead ones — is *O(V²)* in φ-functions and was the reason early SSA prototypes were considered impractical.

- **φ-placement is a join problem.** Where two definitions of *v* at nodes *x, y* can both reach a use, control must merge them. The set of merge points is exactly the *iterated join* *J⁺(Defs(v))*, and Cytron et al. prove the beautiful identity *J⁺(S) = DF⁺(S)* whenever *entry ∈ S* [1]. φ-placement thus reduces to computing iterated dominance frontiers.
- **DF⁺ is computable by worklist.** The following algorithm visits each frontier edge once:

```python
def place_phi_functions(cfg, defs):
    """defs: dict var -> set of blocks containing a definition.
       Returns dict var -> set of blocks needing a phi-function."""
    DF = dominance_frontiers(cfg)          # DF[x]: set of nodes
    phi = {v: set() for v in defs}
    for v, sites in defs.items():
        worklist = list(sites)
        ever_on_wl = set(sites) | set()    # blocks already processed
        has_phi = set()
        while worklist:
            x = worklist.pop()
            for y in DF[x]:
                if y not in has_phi:
                    phi[v].add(y)          # insert v := φ(...) at y
                    has_phi.add(y)
                    if y not in ever_on_wl:
                        ever_on_wl.add(y)  # a phi is itself a definition
                        worklist.append(y)
        return phi
```

The crucial line is the last: a newly placed φ-function *is* a definition of *v*, so it can force further φ-functions downstream — this is the *iteration* in *iterated* dominance frontier.

> Theorem: *(Cytron et al. [1], φ-placement minimality).* The algorithm inserts a φ-function for variable *v* at node *y* **iff** *y ∈ DF⁺(Defs(v))*, which holds **iff** some correct SSA renaming requires a φ-function for *v* at *y*. Hence the constructed SSA form is *minimal*: no φ-function can be removed without breaking the single-assignment property.

Three flavors of SSA refine this picture, differing only in how aggressively they prune:

| Flavor | φ-placement rule | Use case |
|---|---|---|
| *Minimal SSA* | φ at all of *DF⁺(Defs(v))* | Canonical theory; Cytron's output |
| *Pruned SSA* | φ only where *v* is live | Fewer dead φs; needs liveness first |
| *Semi-pruned SSA* | φ at *DF⁺* of blocks where *v* is "non-local" | LLVM's choice: cheap, near-pruned |

LLVM famously constructs *semi-pruned* SSA during IR building (its `mem2reg` pass), trading a small amount of dead φ-code for avoiding a full liveness pre-pass.

### 4.2 Variable renaming and semantic preservation



```haskell
-- | SSA renaming over the dominator tree.  Each variable maps to a
-- stack of version numbers; the top of the stack is the current version.
rename :: DomTree -> Map Var [Int] -> Block -> FreshM (Map Var [Int])
rename domTree stacks block = do
  saved <- forM (defsOf block) $ \v -> do      -- 1. push new versions
    i <- fresh v
    return (v, i : lookupStack v stacks)
  let stacks' = foldr (uncurry insertStack) stacks saved
  forM_ (usesOf block) $ \u ->                -- 2. rewrite uses
    rewriteUse u (head (lookupStack (varOf u) stacks'))
  forM_ (childrenOf domTree block) $ \c -> do -- 3. recurse down the tree
    rename domTree stacks' c
    forM_ (phiArgs c block) $ \(v, arg) ->     -- 4. fill φ operands
      rewritePhiArg v arg (head (lookupStack v stacks'))
  restoreStacks saved                          -- 5. pop on the way back up
```



*Semantic preservation* follows from a simulation argument: each renamed variable *vᵢ* denotes the value of the *i*-th dynamic assignment to *v* along any execution, and each φ-function *v₃ := φ(v₁, v₂)* selects the operand corresponding to the CFG edge actually taken — a case analysis on the last executed predecessor.

### 4.3 Global value numbering on SSA

*Value numbering* detects redundant computations: if two expressions provably compute the same value, one can be eliminated.



```rust
/// Congruence test for SSA value numbering: two instructions are
/// congruent iff their opcodes match and their operands are congruent.
fn congruent(a: &Instr, b: &Instr, class_of: &HashMap<Value, ClassId>) -> bool {
    a.opcode == b.opcode
        && a.operands.len() == b.operands.len()
        && a.operands.iter().zip(&b.operands)
            .all(|(x, y)| class_of[x] == class_of[y])
    // NOTE: Phi nodes participate like any other opcode: φ is just
    // another function symbol in the Herbrand universe.
}
```



```
  before GVN (SSA):                 after GVN:
  b1 = φ(a0, a2)                    b1 = φ(a0, a2)
  c1 = b1 + x0                      c1 = b1 + x0
  ...                               ...
  b2 = φ(a0, a2)   // same merge    b2 = b1        // φ redundant!
  c2 = b2 + x0     // same add      c2 = c1        // add redundant!
```

*Sparse* analyses like this are SSA's raison d'être: because every use names its unique definition, the optimizer walks only the def–use edges it cares about instead of scanning whole basic blocks.

### 4.4 Graph-coloring register allocation: Chaitin, Briggs, and iterated coalescing

*Register allocation* maps temporaries to *k* physical registers.

1. **Liveness analysis** — backward dataflow: `LiveIn(s) = Gen(s) ∪ (LiveOut(s) − Kill(s))`.
2. **Interference graph** — nodes are live ranges; an edge *(u, v)* means *u* and *v* are simultaneously live and must not share a register.
3. **Coalescing** — merge the endpoints of copy instructions when safe, deleting the copy.
4. **Simplify/select** — repeatedly remove a node of degree *< k*, push it on a stack; when stuck, pick a *spill candidate* by a cost heuristic; then pop the stack assigning the lowest available color, spilling nodes whose neighbors exhausted all *k* colors.
5. **Spill code insertion** — insert loads/stores for spilled ranges and *iterate*.

> Theorem: *(Chaitin [6], allocation ≡ coloring).* For fixed schedule and no spilling, deciding whether a program's temporaries can be assigned to *k* registers is polynomially equivalent to *k*-coloring its interference graph, and is therefore **NP-complete for every fixed *k ≥ 3*** (by reduction from graph *k*-colorability).



**Coalescing** — deleting `t := s` copies by merging nodes — is where the engineering gets delicate. Reckless coalescing can turn a *k*-colorable graph into a non-*k*-colorable one (the merged node has the *union* of neighborhoods). Two conservative criteria preserve colorability [4]:

- **Briggs's criterion:** coalesce *u, v* only if the merged node has *< k* neighbors of *significant* degree (degree *≥ k*).
- **George's criterion:** coalesce *u, v* only if every neighbor *t* of *u* either already interferes with *v* or has degree *< k*.



### 4.5 Linear scan and the LLVM greedy allocator



```
intervals sorted by start:      A ▓▓▓▓▓▓▓░░░░
                                B   ▓▓▓▓▓▓▓░░
                                C     ▓▓▓░░░░░░░   <- spilled (longest)
                                D         ▓▓▓▓▓░░
R = 2 registers                 E             ▓▓▓▓
```





**LLVM's modern answer** keeps the interval idea but replaces the single pass with a priority queue. The *greedy allocator* (`RegAllocGreedy.cpp`, LLVM's default since 3.0) [7]:

1. Enqueues virtual registers by *spill weight* (estimated execution frequency × loop depth), not by start position.
2. Assigns registers in priority order using a *live interval union* for interference queries.
3. On conflict, may **evict** an already-colored lower-weight interval back onto the queue rather than spilling immediately.
4. **Splits** live ranges around hot regions (loop-aware splitting) so that a long range spilled in one hot loop doesn't force spills in cold code.
5. Applies **last-chance recoloring**: before spilling, try to recolor the conflicting neighbors.

## 5. Empirical Results / Proofs

### 5.1 Complexity-theoretic results

> Theorem: *(Chaitin [6]; refined in [6].)* Register allocation with spilling allowed, minimizing spill cost, is NP-complete. Even the *decision* version — "can this program be allocated to *k* registers with total spill cost ≤ *C*?" — is NP-complete, and the coloring phase alone is NP-complete for *k ≥ 3*.

*Proof sketch.* Reduce from graph *k*-coloring: given *G = (V, E)*, construct a program with *|V| + 1* variables — one per vertex plus a variable *x* — structured so that the program's interference graph is exactly *G* plus a universal vertex *x* adjacent to everything.

> Theorem: *(Bouchez, Brisk, Hack — via [6].)* The interference graph of a program in *strict* SSA form (each variable defined once, φ-operands treated as parallel copies) is **chordal**: every cycle of length ≥ 4 has a chord.

*Corollary.* Chordal graphs admit *perfect elimination orderings* — computable by maximum-cardinality search in linear time — along which greedy coloring is *optimal*.

> Theorem: *(Pereira & Palsberg [6].)* Register allocation **after classical SSA elimination** — where φ-functions are replaced by parallel copies on predecessor edges — is **NP-complete**, because the inserted copies destroy chordality.

Together these three results draw the exact boundary: SSA's structure *almost* trivializes allocation, but the *destruction* step that real compilers must perform reintroduces the full combinatorial hardness.

### 5.2 Empirical evidence

The two most-cited measurement studies frame the engineering trade-off quantitatively [3][4]:

| Study | Allocator A | Allocator B | Compile time | Code quality |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Poletto & Sarkar [3] | Linear scan | Iterated coalescing [4] | **Several × faster** (even vs. *fast* coloring) | Within **12%** (all but 2 benchmarks) |
| Poletto & Sarkar [3] | Linear scan | Top-*k* hot variables | Comparable | **Several × faster** (linear scan wins hugely) |
| George & Appel [4] | Iterated coalescing | Briggs conservative | Similar | Fewer copies, fewer spills |

A TLA⁺ sketch captures the core invariant that makes linear scan's single pass sound — no two intervals sharing a register may overlap:

```tla
---- MODULE LinearScan ----
EXTENDS Naturals, FiniteSets
CONSTANTS Intervals, K          \* K = number of physical registers
VARIABLES active, assignment
TypeOK == active \subseteq Intervals
         /\ assignment \in [active -> 1..K]
NoOverlap == \A i, j \in active :
               i /= j /\ assignment[i] = assignment[j]
                 => IntervalsEnd[i] < IntervalsStart[j]
                    \/ IntervalsEnd[j] < IntervalsStart[i]
Init == active = {} /\ assignment = <<>>
Next == \E i \in Intervals \ active :   \* next interval by start order
          LET expired == {j \in active : IntervalsEnd[j] < IntervalsStart[i]}
          IN  /\ active' = (active \ expired) \cup {i}
              /\ \E r \in 1..K : (\A j \in active' : j = i \/ assignment[j] /= r)
                                 /\ assignment' = [assignment EXCEPT ![i] = r]
Spec == Init /\ [][Next]_<<active, assignment>>
THEOREM Spec => []NoOverlap
====
```

The invariant `NoOverlap` is exactly the coloring constraint restricted to interval graphs — and interval graphs, like chordal graphs, are *perfect*: greedy coloring by start order is optimal for the interval approximation, which is why linear scan's quality loss comes only from the approximation, never from the coloring step itself.

## 6. Limitations

**SSA is not a universal IR.** Its elegance degrades in several real settings:

1. **Predicated and vector code.** On architectures with predicated execution (ARM, GPUs), a definition under a predicate is a *partial* definition; SSA's total-definition invariant forces either ψ/γ-function extensions (ψ-SSA) or abandoning SSA for those regions. LLVM handles predication late, in machine IR, largely *outside* SSA.
2. **Exception-heavy and irreducible control flow.** Languages with exceptions, `setjmp`/`longjmp`, or irreducible CFGs (goto into loops) complicate dominance: the *DJ-graph* and factored control-flow representations exist precisely because real control flow is not always a clean dominator tree. φ-placement on irreducible graphs is still correct but can produce pathologically many φ-functions.
3. **Memory SSA is a second-class citizen.** Scalar SSA is exact; *memory* SSA (LLVM's `MemorySSA`) is necessarily approximate because aliasing is undecidable. Optimizations that need memory value numbering (store-to-load forwarding across calls) hit the alias-analysis wall, not the SSA wall.
4. **Phase ordering.** SSA enables sparse passes, but passes still run in *some* order, and no order is optimal: GVN before loop-invariant code motion finds different redundancies than after. SSA does not solve phase ordering; it merely makes each phase cheaper.
5. **Allocation after SSA destruction.** As proved in §5, the chordality that makes SSA allocation easy is *destroyed* by φ-elimination, and repairing the damage (copy placement, coalescing of φ-related copies) reintroduces NP-hard subproblems that heuristics must absorb [6].
6. **Compile-time memory.** Minimal SSA is linear in *theory*, but the constant factors — dominator trees, DJ-graphs, per-variable stacks — are real. JITs at `-O0` skip SSA entirely (LLVM builds SSA lazily; V8's Turbofan uses a sea-of-nodes IR instead), accepting weaker optimization for faster startup.

## 7. Conclusion

Static single assignment and register allocation are usually taught as separate chapters — one in the "middle end" half of the course, one in the "back end" half. This thesis has argued they are one story: *the representation determines the algorithm*. Dominance frontiers made SSA construction linear and thereby made sparse global optimization routine [1]; SSA's single-definition invariant made value numbering a partition-refinement problem rather than a dataflow fixpoint [2]; and SSA's chordal interference graphs nearly — but not quite — trivialized register allocation [6].

The practical arc bends from generality toward stratification: Chaitin's coloring for maximum quality [6], Briggs's optimism and George–Appel iteration for production robustness [4][5], Poletto–Sarkar linear scan for compile-time budgets [3], and LLVM's greedy priority-queue allocator [7] as the current synthesis — quality near coloring, speed near linear scan, with splitting and recoloring to close the gap. GCC's IRA/LRA split tells the same story from the RTL side.

Open directions are plentiful: *verified* SSA construction and allocation in proof assistants (CompCert's verified register allocator is a landmark, but its SSA middle end remains unverified); *learned* spill heuristics and ML-guided splitting that outperform hand-tuned cost models; and *quantum* or *dataflow* targets where the register file is not a flat set of *k* colors but a constrained, banked, predicated resource — for which neither Chaitin's reduction nor chordality quite applies. The next thirty years of program representation will likely be as productive as the last.
