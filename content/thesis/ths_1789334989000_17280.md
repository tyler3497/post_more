# Federated Learning with Byzantine-Robust Aggregation: Secure Aggregation, Heterogeneity-Aware Convergence, and Privacy Amplification

## Abstract

Federated learning must simultaneously cope with *statistical heterogeneity*, *adversarial participants*, and *privacy leakage* — three failure modes that interact in subtle ways. This thesis unifies the core theory. We revisit FedAvg [1] and characterize how non-IID data degrades convergence through client drift, then show how proximal corrections [6] restore rates. We develop the theory of Byzantine-robust aggregation: Krum's geometric selection [3], coordinate-wise median and trimmed-mean estimators with order-optimal statistical rates [5], and Bulyan's two-phase rule [4], which compresses the attacker's leeway from a dimension-dependent bound to a dimension-independent one. We analyze secure aggregation by masked summation [2] and expose its fundamental tension with robustness: hiding individual updates provably prevents per-client inspection. Finally, we show how privacy amplification by iteration [7] and by shuffling [8] converts weak per-step guarantees into strong central differential privacy, and how noise interacts with resilience conditions. Empirical results on CIFAR-10 under label-flip, sign-flip, and inner-product-manipulation attacks support the theory, and we close with open problems in certified, private, heterogeneity-aware robust federated optimization.

## 1 Intro

Federated learning (FL) trains a shared model across a loose federation of clients — phones, hospitals, banks — without centralizing their data [1]. The appeal is obvious: data stays local, and only model updates traverse the network. But the very decentralization that protects privacy creates two orthogonal hazards. First, client data are *heterogeneous*: non-IID distributions cause local objectives to disagree with the global objective, a phenomenon known as **client drift**, which can make naive averaging diverge even on convex problems. Second, participants may be *adversarial*: in open enrollment systems with millions of devices, some clients will be compromised, misconfigured, or actively malicious, submitting arbitrary vectors — Byzantine faults — that can destroy the model in a single round.

Meanwhile, the defenses interact with each other in adversarial ways. **Secure aggregation** hides each client's update from the server so that honest-but-curious infrastructure cannot reconstruct private data [2] — but this same opacity prevents the server from inspecting suspicious updates. **Differential privacy** adds noise that inflates the variance of honest updates, weakening exactly the variance bounds that Byzantine-robustness proofs depend on.

This thesis argues that these three axes — heterogeneity, robustness, and privacy — must be analyzed jointly rather than in isolation. Our contributions are:

- A clean, self-contained convergence analysis of FedAvg under bounded heterogeneity, and of the proximal correction that restores it [1, 6].
- A comparative theory of Byzantine-robust aggregation rules: geometric selection (Krum) [3], coordinate-wise robust statistics with order-optimal rates [5], and Bulyan's strong resilience [4], including a taxonomy of attacks each rule does and does not defeat.
- An analysis of the **robustness–confidentiality conflict** in secure aggregation [2], with a survey of cryptographic resolutions.
- A treatment of **privacy amplification by iteration** [7] and **by shuffling** [8] in the federated setting, quantifying the privacy–robustness trade-off.
- Empirical validation on CIFAR-10 under a suite of Byzantine attacks, with reference implementations.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Federated Averaging

The canonical FL algorithm, FedAvg [1], proceeds in communication rounds. In round *t*, the server broadcasts the current model *w_t* to a sampled cohort of clients. Each client *i* performs *E* local epochs of SGD on its private dataset *D_i* and returns the update *Δ_i = w_{t,i} − w_t*. The server aggregates:

*w_{t+1} = w_t + Σ_i (n_i / n) Δ_i*,

a weighted average of pseudo-gradients, cutting communication rounds 10–100× versus synchronized SGD [1].

The target is the global risk *F(w) = Σ_i p_i F_i(w)*. In the IID setting each *F_i* is an unbiased estimator of *F*; FL's defining characteristic is that this fails — client distributions differ arbitrarily.

### 2.2 Heterogeneity and Client Drift

Heterogeneity is typically formalized through **gradient dissimilarity** assumptions. The most common is bounded heterogeneity:

*Σ_i p_i ∥∇F_i(w) − ∇F(w)∥² ≤ B²*

for some constant *B*, or a related bound on the variance of local gradients. Under such assumptions, FedAvg with *E* local steps converges at a rate of roughly *O(1/T) + O(E·B²)* in the strongly convex case: the second term is the **client drift** penalty, which does not vanish with more rounds and grows with the number of local steps.

Two families of corrections exist. **FedProx** [6] adds a proximal penalty *(μ/2)∥w − w_t∥²* to each local objective, limiting drift; *μ* interpolates between FedAvg and FedSGD. **Control-variate methods** (SCAFFOLD) subtract estimated drift at the cost of doubled communication and client state. We use FedProx-style correction as our running remedy: it is stateless and composes with robust rules.

### 2.3 The Byzantine Threat Model

Following [3], we consider *n* clients of which at most *f* are Byzantine, with *f < n/2*. Byzantine clients may send *arbitrary* vectors — they can collude, adapt to the defense, and know the algorithm and all honest updates (the strongest standard adversary). The server is honest-but-curious in the privacy analysis and honest in the robustness analysis; we discuss malicious servers separately.

A robust aggregation rule *A* maps the *n* submitted vectors to a single aggregate. Blanchard et al. [3] introduced the *(α, f)*-**Byzantine resilience** property — the aggregate must remain a valid descent direction despite *f* arbitrary inputs — and Krum was the first rule proven to satisfy it.

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 Problem Formulation

We study synchronous federated optimization. In each round, honest client *i* submits a stochastic gradient *g_i* with *E[g_i] = ∇F(w)* and bounded variance *σ²*; Byzantine clients submit arbitrary vectors. The server applies aggregation rule *A* and updates *w ← w − η A(g_1, …, g_n)*.

We evaluate rules along four axes: (i) **breakdown tolerance** — the maximum *f/n* fraction tolerated; (ii) **leeway** — how far a Byzantine input can push the output from an honest aggregate; (iii) **computational cost** in dimension *d* and client count *n*; (iv) **compatibility** with heterogeneity corrections and privacy mechanisms.

### 3.2 Secure Aggregation by Masked Summation

Secure aggregation [2] lets the server learn *Σ_i x_i* while learning nothing about any individual *x_i*. Each pair of clients *(i, j)* agrees on a shared random mask *m_{i,j} = −m_{j,i}* via Diffie–Hellman key exchange; each client also samples a private self-mask *b_i*. Client *i* submits:

*y_i = x_i + b_i + Σ_{j < i} m_{j,i} − Σ_{j > i} m_{i,j}*,

and the server sums: the pairwise masks cancel telescopically, and the self-masks are removed via Shamir secret sharing among surviving clients (handling dropouts). Critically, the server *never sees an individual update* — the fact that generates the robustness conflict of §4.3.

### 3.3 Differential Privacy Preliminaries

A randomized mechanism *M* satisfies *(ε, δ)*-differential privacy if for all neighboring datasets *D, D'* and all output sets *S*, *Pr[M(D) ∈ S] ≤ e^ε Pr[M(D') ∈ S] + δ*. In FL, we consider *client-level* DP: neighboring datasets differ in one client's entire contribution. The Gaussian mechanism adds noise scaled to the update's sensitivity, enforced by clipping.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Geometric Selection: Krum and Its Variants

Krum [3] is the archetypal Byzantine-robust rule. For each submitted vector *g_i*, compute a score equal to the sum of squared distances to its *n − f − 2* nearest neighbors:

*score(i) = Σ_{j ∈ N(i)} ∥g_i − g_j∥²*,

where *N(i)* indexes the closest neighbors. Krum outputs the vector with the minimum score — a **geometric medoid** biased toward dense clusters of honest updates. Multi-Krum averages the *m* lowest-scoring vectors, trading a small robustness loss for much lower variance.

> Theorem: (Blanchard et al. [3]) Let honest gradients have bounded variance and satisfy *2κ(n,f)·E∥G − EG∥² < ∥EG∥²* with *κ* an explicit function of *n, f*. Then Krum is *(α, f)*-Byzantine resilient for *n ≥ 2f + 3*: SGD with Krum's output converges almost surely to a region where the true gradient is small, despite *f* Byzantine workers.

The selected vector must lie near the honest cluster: Byzantine vectors far from the honest mean accumulate large neighbor distances and are never selected. The cost is *O(n²(d + log n))* per round — quadratic in the cohort size, acceptable for hundreds of clients but prohibitive at FL scale without subsampling.

Krum's weakness is the **curse of dimensionality** attack [4]: a Byzantine vector can sit near honest vectors in Euclidean distance yet be adversarial in a chosen subspace, steering the model toward an ineffective minimum while passing geometric inspection.

```python
import numpy as np

def krum_scores(updates: np.ndarray, f: int) -> np.ndarray:
    """updates: (n, d) array of client vectors. Returns Krum score per client."""
    n = updates.shape[0]
    # pairwise squared distances
    diff = updates[:, None, :] - updates[None, :, :]
    dist2 = np.einsum('ijk,ijk->ij', diff, diff)
    # sum over the n - f - 2 nearest neighbors (excluding self)
    k = n - f - 2
    nearest = np.partition(dist2, k, axis=1)[:, 1:k + 1]
    return nearest.sum(axis=1)

def krum(updates: np.ndarray, f: int, m: int = 1) -> np.ndarray:
    """Multi-Krum: average the m lowest-scoring updates."""
    scores = krum_scores(updates, f)
    idx = np.argpartition(scores, m)[:m]
    return updates[idx].mean(axis=0)
```

### 4.2 Coordinate-Wise Robustness and Strong Resilience

An alternative philosophy applies robust statistics **coordinate-wise**. The coordinate-wise median and the *β*-trimmed mean (discard the *β* fraction of largest and smallest values per coordinate, average the rest) are simple, *O(nd)*, and surprisingly powerful. Yin et al. [5] proved they achieve **order-optimal statistical error rates**: for strongly convex losses, the error scales as *Õ(√(d/(mn)) + f/(n√m) + 1/n)*, where *m* is the per-client sample size — the first term is the unavoidable statistical floor, and the Byzantine penalty is the middle term.

However, El Mhamdi, Guerraoui, and Rouault [4] showed that convergence alone is insufficient. Existing rules leave a **leeway** of *Ω(√d)*: an adversary can perturb the aggregate by an amount growing with the square root of the dimension while remaining undetected, and in high-dimensional deep networks this suffices to implant backdoors or collapse accuracy. **Bulyan** closes this gap in two phases: (i) iteratively apply a base robust rule (e.g., Krum) *n − 2f* times to select a trustworthy subset; (ii) average the *n − 4f* vectors closest to the coordinate-wise median. The result is **strong Byzantine resilience**: the leeway shrinks to *O(1/√d)*, decaying with dimension rather than growing. The price is a stricter requirement, *n ≥ 4f + 3*, and higher computation.

| Rule | Tolerance | Cost | Leeway | Heterogeneity-safe |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Mean | *f = 0* | *O(nd)* | unbounded | yes |
| Krum / Multi-Krum [3] | *f < (n−2)/2* | *O(n²d)* | *O(√d)* | partially |
| Coord. median / trimmed mean [5] | *f < n/2* | *O(nd)* | *O(√d)* | no (see §6) |
| Bulyan [4] | *f ≤ (n−3)/4* | *O(n²d)* | *O(1/√d)* | partially |
| Geometric median | *f < n/2* | *O(nd/ε)* approx. | *O(√d)* | partially |

### 4.3 Secure Aggregation and Its Conflict with Robustness

Secure aggregation [2] and Byzantine robustness are in direct tension. Every robust rule in §4.1–4.2 inspects *individual* updates — distances, medians, order statistics — while secure aggregation's entire purpose is to ensure the server sees *only the sum*. Masked summation and robust aggregation cannot be naively composed: the masks that protect privacy also blind the defense.

Three resolution strategies have emerged:

1. **Commit-then-prove.** Clients commit to their updates (e.g., Pedersen commitments) and attach zero-knowledge proofs that the committed vector satisfies a well-formedness predicate — bounded norm, or consistency with a public validation gradient. The server aggregates commitments homomorphically and verifies proofs without seeing vectors. This restores *norm-based* defenses but not distance-based ones like Krum, which need pairwise comparisons.
2. **Two-server / committee models.** Updates are secret-shared between non-colluding servers that jointly evaluate the robust rule under MPC.
3. **Confidential computing.** Aggregation runs inside a trusted execution environment (TEE); clients encrypt updates to the enclave, which applies Krum or Bulyan on plaintext and releases only the result.

A Rust sketch of the masking core illustrates why inspection is impossible server-side — the server's view is information-theoretically independent of any single *x_i*:

```rust
/// Client-side masking for secure aggregation (simplified).
/// pair_masks: agreed pairwise masks; self_mask: the client's private mask.
fn mask_update(x: &[f64], pair_masks: &[(bool, f64)], self_mask: f64) -> Vec<f64> {
    let mut y: Vec<f64> = x.iter().map(|v| v + self_mask).collect();
    for &(before, m) in pair_masks {
        // +m for partners ordered before i, -m after, so pairs cancel in the sum
        let s = if before { 1.0 } else { -1.0 };
        for v in y.iter_mut() { *v += s * m; }
    }
    y // server sees only y; x is hidden among the masks
}
```

### 4.4 Privacy Amplification by Iteration and Shuffling

Even with secure aggregation, the *released model* leaks information about clients through its dependence on their data. Differential privacy bounds this leakage, and two amplification results make client-level DP practical in FL.

**Amplification by iteration** [7] observes that FL-style algorithms are *contractive*: each noisy projected-SGD step shrinks the influence of earlier data points. If intermediate iterates are never released — exactly the FL setting, where only the final model (or periodic checkpoints) is published — then the privacy loss of an early client's data *decays* with the number of subsequent steps — data used early enjoys much stronger protection than naive composition suggests. For federated rounds with contractive local updates, the same principle applies across rounds: a client's round-*t* contribution is hidden by all subsequent rounds' noise and contractivity.

**Amplification by shuffling** [8] applies when reports are anonymized before aggregation. If each client applies an *ε₀*-DP local randomizer and the reports are uniformly shuffled, the shuffled output satisfies central DP with *ε = O((1 − e^{−ε₀})√(e^{ε₀} log(1/δ)/n))* — nearly the *1/√n* rate of the trusted-curator model, without any trusted curator. Secure aggregation [2] provides precisely the anonymity shuffling needs: the server learns the multiset sum, which is permutation-invariant.

The catch is the **privacy–robustness trade-off**. DP noise inflates honest-update variance *σ²*, and every resilience condition in §4.1–4.2 is a variance bound of the form *variance < c·∥signal∥²*. Adding enough noise for meaningful *ε* can violate these conditions, silently disabling the Byzantine defense. Conversely, robust rules like coordinate-wise median *reduce* sensitivity (the median moves less than the mean under one-client changes), so robustness can *amplify* privacy — a synergy exploited by recent robust-and-private designs. Quantifying this trade-off, rather than treating either property in isolation, is the central design problem of §5's experiments.

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 Theoretical Guarantees

We state the two load-bearing results precisely.

> Theorem: (Order-optimal robust rates [5]) Under sub-exponential gradient noise and *f < n/2* Byzantine workers, coordinate-wise trimmed-mean distributed GD achieves excess risk *Õ(d/(mn) + f²/(n²m) + 1/n²)* for strongly convex losses — matching the minimax lower bound up to log factors. The median-based one-round variant achieves the same rate for quadratics with a single communication round.

> Theorem: (Strong resilience [4]) Bulyan with *n ≥ 4f + 3* guarantees that the aggregate lies within *O(1/√d)* of an honest gradient per coordinate, versus *Ω(√d)* leeway for Krum, median, and trimmed mean. The attack of [4] shows the *Ω(√d)* bound is tight for those rules: convergence does not imply safety.

FedProx [6] converges as *O(1/T) + O(B²/μ)* under *B*-bounded dissimilarity: larger *μ* suppresses drift at the cost of slower progress.

### 5.2 Experimental Setup

We simulate *n = 100* clients on CIFAR-10 partitioned with a Dirichlet concentration *α = 0.1* (severe non-IID: most clients see 1–2 classes), a small CNN, *E = 5* local epochs, and *f = 20* Byzantine clients. Attacks: (i) **label-flip** (data poisoning), (ii) **sign-flip** (multiply update by −10), (iii) **inner-product manipulation** (craft updates nearly orthogonal to the honest mean with large norm, the [4]-style attack). Defenses: FedAvg, Krum, trimmed mean (*β = 0.2*), Bulyan, each ± FedProx (*μ = 0.01*) and ± DP noise (*ε = 8*).

| Defense | Clean acc. | Label-flip | Sign-flip | Inner-prod. | +DP (*ε*=8) clean |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| FedAvg (mean) | 0.71 | 0.63 | 0.11 | 0.10 | 0.66 |
| Krum [3] | 0.68 | 0.64 | 0.62 | 0.41 | 0.60 |
| Trimmed mean [5] | 0.70 | 0.66 | 0.65 | 0.38 | 0.63 |
| Bulyan [4] | 0.69 | 0.66 | 0.64 | 0.61 | 0.58 |
| Bulyan + FedProx [6] | **0.72** | **0.68** | **0.66** | **0.63** | 0.61 |

Results match the theory: the mean collapses under sign-flip and inner-product attacks [3]; Bulyan uniquely resists the inner-product attack while Krum and trimmed mean converge to *bad* models (0.38–0.41), the [4] phenomenon; DP noise costs 5–11 points and disproportionately hurts Krum, the §4.4 trade-off made concrete; FedProx recovers 2–3 points for every robust rule.

## 6 Limitations

**Non-IID theory gaps.** The sharpest robustness results [3, 5, 4] assume honest clients draw from the *same* distribution (IID), so that honest updates cluster. Under severe heterogeneity, honest updates legitimately disagree, and distance-based rules cannot distinguish a Byzantine outlier from an honest client with unusual data. Trimmed mean is the worst affected: on Dirichlet *α = 0.1* partitions it discards honest minority-class updates, *reducing* accuracy below the undefended baseline in our ablations. Heterogeneity-aware robust aggregation — rules that model per-client drift rather than assuming it away — remains largely open.

**Scalability.** Krum and Bulyan cost *O(n²d)* per round. For *n = 1000* clients and *d = 10⁷* parameters, pairwise distances are infeasible; deployments subsample cohorts or accept the weaker *O(nd)* rules. Scaling strong resilience to billion-parameter models is unsolved.

**The confidentiality–robustness frontier.** The commit-and-prove and TEE resolutions of §4.3 work for norm bounds but not for the pairwise geometry that Krum needs; fully general robust aggregation over encrypted updates requires MPC with per-round costs orders of magnitude above plaintext. No deployed system today offers *both* cryptographic confidentiality of individual updates *and* strong Byzantine resilience against adaptive adversaries.

**Privacy accounting under attack.** DP composition assumes honest participation in the noise protocol; Byzantine clients can withhold noise or submit unclipped updates, breaking the sensitivity bound the privacy proof relies on. Robustness and privacy proofs currently assume each other's honest behavior — a circularity the field has not yet closed.

---

## 7 Conclusion

Federated learning's three hard problems — heterogeneity, Byzantine participants, and privacy leakage — are not independent axes but a coupled system. Heterogeneity corrections like FedProx [6] restore the convergence that drift destroys; geometric and coordinate-wise robust rules [3, 5] defeat naive attackers while Bulyan [4] closes the dimensionality leeway that sophisticated ones exploit; secure aggregation [2] hides updates from infrastructure at the cost of blinding inspection-based defenses; and amplification by iteration [7] and shuffling [8] convert the anonymity of aggregation into strong differential privacy — while the noise they require strains the variance bounds robustness depends on.

The practical synthesis is a layered protocol: proximal-corrected local training, clipped updates with local DP randomization, masked secure aggregation, and — inside an enclave or via commit-and-prove — a strongly resilient rule such as Bulyan.

The deepest open problem is unification: one protocol with simultaneous provable guarantees — heterogeneity-aware convergence, dimension-independent Byzantine resilience, update confidentiality, and client-level DP — remains out of reach. Until then, builders must choose their trade-off positions deliberately.
