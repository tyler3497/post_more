# Quantum Error Mitigation on Noisy Intermediate-Scale Devices: Zero-Noise Extrapolation, Probabilistic Error Cancellation, and the Sampling-Overhead Frontier

## Abstract

Quantum error mitigation (QEM) is the principal strategy for extracting reliable expectation values from noisy intermediate-scale quantum (NISQ) processors without the qubit overhead of fault tolerance. This thesis gives a unified treatment of the two dominant QEM families — *zero-noise extrapolation* (ZNE), which amplifies circuit noise via identity insertions, unitary folding, and pulse stretching before extrapolating to the zero-noise limit, and *probabilistic error cancellation* (PEC), which inverts noise channels through quasiprobability decompositions of ideal operations into noisy basis operations at a sampling cost exponential in the circuit fault rate. Both are formalized within the universal $(Q,K)$-protocol framework [2], and the bias–variance economy of mitigated estimators is quantified under fixed measurement-shot budgets. We map the *sampling-overhead frontier*: universal exponential lower bounds in circuit depth and width that render unstructured mitigation asymptotically infeasible, while certifying PEC's optimality for local dephasing noise. Hardware benchmarks, adaptive shot allocation, and noise-learning pipelines ground the theory in practice, and we delineate where mitigation composes usefully with early fault tolerance.

## 1. Introduction

The noisy intermediate-scale quantum era is defined by a mismatch: processors with tens to hundreds of physical qubits and two-qubit gate error rates near $10^{-3}$ can execute circuits far too deep for exact classical simulation, yet far too shallow for fault-tolerant quantum error correction (QEC), which demands thousands of physical qubits per logical qubit [1]. *Quantum error mitigation* (QEM) occupies the space between these regimes. Rather than encoding information redundantly, QEM accepts noisy executions and engineers *classical post-processing* — circuit variants, noise-scaled fits, quasiprobability sampling — to recover reduced-bias estimates of ideal expectation values [1].

Two techniques dominate the modern landscape. **Zero-noise extrapolation** (ZNE) runs the circuit at several artificially amplified noise levels, then extrapolates measured expectation values back to the zero-noise limit [5, 6]. **Probabilistic error cancellation** (PEC) decomposes the ideal noise-free channel into a real linear combination of noisy implementable operations, samples from the associated quasiprobability distribution, and reweights outcomes to cancel noise exactly in expectation [1, 4]. Both share a common currency — *sampling overhead* — and confront a common frontier: fundamental exponential lower bounds on the samples any mitigation protocol must consume as circuits grow [2, 3].

This thesis has three objectives:

1. **Mechanism.** A mathematical account of ZNE (noise scaling, extrapolation, variance amplification) and PEC (quasiprobability decompositions, the $\gamma$-factor, noise learning) in the superoperator formalism of [1].
2. **Frontier.** Exposition of the $(Q,K)$-protocol framework [2] and its exponentially tighter successors [3, 9], proving that sampling overhead is a *law* — any unbiased mitigation of unstructured deep circuits costs exponentially many samples — while PEC saturates this law for dephasing noise.
3. **Practice.** A survey of real deployments: composite strategies on 100+-qubit superconducting processors [5], shot-efficient adaptive allocation under fixed budgets [8], Pauli error propagation reducing PEC overhead [7], and molecular ground-state benchmarks [10].

Section 2 covers noise models and the bias–variance–MSE language. Section 3 introduces the unified $(Q,K)$-protocol methodology. Section 4 is the deep dive: ZNE, PEC, the sampling-overhead frontier, and composition. Section 5 proves the key bounds and reports empirical results. Sections 6 and 7 cover limitations and conclusions.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 Noise channels and the NISQ error budget

A NISQ computation ideally prepares $|\psi\rangle = U|0\rangle^{\otimes n}$ and estimates $\langle O \rangle = \langle\psi|O|\psi\rangle$ for some observable $O$, typically a sum of Pauli strings. In reality each gate $G$ is followed by a noise channel $\mathcal{E}$, a completely positive trace-preserving map. The dominant models are:

- **Depolarizing noise:** $\mathcal{D}_\epsilon(\rho) = (1-\epsilon)\rho + \epsilon\,\mathbb{I}/d$, the workhorse of theoretical analysis [2, 3].
- **Dephasing noise:** $\mathcal{Z}_\epsilon(\rho) = (1-\epsilon)\rho + \epsilon Z\rho Z$, the canonical model for which PEC optimality is proven [2].
- **Amplitude damping / $T_1$ decay:** non-unital noise, physically dominant in superconducting architectures and treated explicitly in the tightened bounds of [3].
- **Pauli (stochastic) noise:** $\mathcal{E}(\rho) = \sum_P p_P P\rho P$, to which coherent errors are *twirled* by randomized compiling before mitigation [1, 5].

In a depth-$L$ layered circuit, local noise drives states exponentially fast toward the maximally mixed state, which is why unmitigated quantum advantage is conjectured to vanish beyond logarithmic depth [3, 9].

### 2.2 Estimation theory: bias, variance, and mean-squared error

With $N$ measurement shots, the empirical mean $\bar{a}$ estimates $\langle O\rangle$ with shot noise $\mathrm{Var}[\bar{a}] = \sigma^2/N$. A mitigation protocol replaces the raw estimator with $\hat{O}_{em}$, governed by the bias–variance decomposition [8]:

$$\mathrm{MSE}[\hat{O}_{em}] \;=\; \underbrace{\big(\mathbb{E}[\hat{O}_{em}] - \langle O\rangle_{\mathrm{ideal}}\big)^2}_{\mathrm{Bias}^2} \;+\; \underbrace{\mathrm{Var}[\hat{O}_{em}]}_{\mathrm{variance}}.$$

Mitigation *reduces bias* but *increases variance*; under a fixed shot budget a protocol is worthwhile only if the MSE — not the bias alone — decreases [8]. Finite-shot *help–harm boundaries* exist where mitigation makes estimates strictly worse [8].

### 2.3 The superoperator picture

Following [1], we vectorize density matrices as $|\rho\rangle\!\rangle$ and channels as matrices $\mathcal{U}$, with $\mathrm{Tr}[O\rho] = \langle\!\langle O|\rho\rangle\!\rangle$. An ideal gate is $\mathcal{U}$, its noisy realization $\tilde{\mathcal{U}} = \mathcal{E}\circ\mathcal{U}$, and every protocol combines statistics of $\{\tilde{\mathcal{U}}_k\}$ into an estimate of $\langle\!\langle O|\mathcal{U}|\rho_0\rangle\!\rangle$.

### 2.4 The mitigation zoo

| Method | Mechanism | Bias | Sampling overhead | Key requirement |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Zero-noise extrapolation (ZNE) | Noise scaling + extrapolation to $\lambda=0$ | Residual (model-dependent) | Variance amplification $\sum_i c_i^2$ | Tunable noise amplification [5, 6] |
| Probabilistic error cancellation (PEC) | Quasiprobability inversion of noise | Zero (exact noise model) | $\gamma^{2\|C\|}$, exponential in fault rate | Accurate noise tomography [1, 4] |
| Readout mitigation | Assignment-matrix inversion | Reduced | Mild (calibration shots) | Stable readout fidelities [5, 8] |
| Symmetry verification | Post-selection on conserved quantities | Reduced | Post-selection discard rate | Known symmetries [1] |
| Virtual distillation | $M$-copy purification via derangement | Exponentially suppressed in $M$ | Exponential in $M$ | Multiple identical copies [1] |
| Clifford data regression | Learned map from Clifford training circuits | Learned | Training-circuit budget | Near-Clifford targets [1] |

## 3. Methodology

### 3.1 The $(Q, K)$-protocol framework

We adopt the universal framework of Takagi et al. [2], which subsumes essentially all known protocols:

> **Definition ($(Q,K)$-protocol).** *A protocol performs $K$ experiments. In experiment $k$, it runs the noisy circuit $Q$ times to prepare a distorted state $\bigotimes_{q=1}^Q \mathcal{E}^{(k)}_q(\psi)$ with possibly experiment-dependent noise strength $\epsilon_k$, measures, and combines all outcomes through classical post-processing into an estimate $e_A$. Quality is captured by maximum bias $b_{\max}$ and maximum estimator spread $\Delta e_{\max}$ — the range of estimator values, which controls variance via Hoeffding-type concentration.*

ZNE is a $(1, K)$-protocol over $K$ noise scales; PEC is a $(1,1)$-protocol whose spread is exactly the $\gamma$-factor [2]. This unification enables *universal* lower bounds: a bound on $\Delta e_{\max}$ for all $(Q,K)$-protocols constrains every scheme at once.

### 3.2 Cost accounting

Cost is measured in *samples*: total circuit executions for target accuracy $\delta$. For spread $\Delta e_{\max}$, Hoeffding's inequality gives $N = O(\Delta e_{\max}^2/\delta^2)$ shots, sufficient and essentially necessary [2]. Overhead is thus multiplicative on the shot budget, and protocols must be compared at *matched total budget* — the discipline of the shot-efficient protocol in [8].

### 3.3 Noise characterization pipeline

PEC and some ZNE variants need a *learned noise model*: gate-set tomography or scalable Pauli–Lindblad learning of per-layer generator rates [1]. Twirling converts coherent errors into the assumed stochastic Pauli form [5]. Model drift between characterization and mitigation is a first-order concern (Section 6).

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Zero-Noise Extrapolation: Noise Scaling and the Extrapolation Calculus

ZNE rests on a simple observation: if the noisy expectation value $E(\lambda) = \mathrm{Tr}[O\,\rho(\lambda)]$ can be evaluated at amplified noise strengths $\lambda \geq 1$, the ideal value $E(0)$ follows by fitting a model $E(\lambda;\theta)$ and evaluating at $\lambda = 0$ [6].

**Noise scaling.** Three strategies dominate. *Identity insertions* [6] replace $G$ by $G\,(G^\dagger G)^n$; the identity factors add hardware noise, scaling effective noise by $\lambda = 2n+1$ with no pulse-level control. *Unitary folding*, $U \to U\,(U^\dagger U)^n$, gives the same $\lambda$; *partial folding* of a random subset reaches non-integer $\lambda \in [1,3]$ [5]. *Pulse stretching* lengthens pulses to increase decoherence exposure — precise but hardware-specific [1].

**Extrapolation models.** Common choices are polynomial $E(\lambda) = E_0 + \sum_{k=1}^{d} a_k \lambda^k$, exponential $E(\lambda) = a + b\,e^{-c\lambda}$, and poly-exponential hybrids. *Richardson extrapolation* is the distinguished linear case: given measurements at scaled points $x_i = c_i x_0$,

$$E(0) \;=\; \sum_i \left(\prod_{k \neq i} \frac{c_k}{c_k - c_i}\right) E(x_i),$$

exactly cancelling the first $d$ orders of a polynomial noise response [6].

**Variance amplification.** With $E_{em} = \sum_i c_i E(x_i)$, $\sum_i c_i = 1$, variance inflates by $\Gamma = \sum_i c_i^2 \geq 1$; Richardson coefficients grow combinatorially, so high-order extrapolation trades bias reduction against a variance explosion:

```python
import numpy as np

def richardson_extrapolate(scales, values):
    """Estimate E(0) from measurements at noise scales >= 1."""
    scales = np.asarray(scales, dtype=float)
    coeffs = np.array([
        np.prod([s_k / (s_k - s_i) for k, s_k in enumerate(scales) if k != i])
        for i, s_i in enumerate(scales)
    ])
    e0 = float(coeffs @ np.asarray(values))
    gamma_var = float(coeffs @ coeffs)  # variance amplification factor
    return e0, gamma_var

# Example: unitary-folding scales [1, 3, 5]
e0, g = richardson_extrapolate([1, 3, 5], [0.81, 0.62, 0.47])
```

**Digital ZNE best practices.** On a 104-qubit chain in the 127-qubit `ibm_washington` processor, Kim et al. [5] show *composite* strategies — readout mitigation plus twirling plus ZNE — drastically outperform ZNE alone, exponential extrapolation wins at large depths (70 two-qubit gates), and small-range $[1, 1.1, 1.2]$ linear extrapolation is the only stable choice near noise saturation. Enhanced variants exploit repetitive structure (e.g., Trotter steps) to learn the model from the circuit's own symmetry.

### 4.2 Probabilistic Error Cancellation: Quasiprobability and Inverse Noise

PEC, introduced by Temme et al., is the only widely used protocol that *fully removes bias* — $\mathrm{Bias}[\hat{O}_{em}] = 0$ — given an exact noise model [1, 4]. Given implementable noisy basis operations $\{\mathcal{B}_j\}$ (learned by tomography), decompose the ideal channel as a *real* linear combination:

$$\mathcal{U} \;=\; \sum_j c_j \,\mathcal{B}_j, \qquad c_j \in \mathbb{R}.$$

With $\gamma = \sum_j |c_j| \geq 1$ and $p_j = |c_j|/\gamma$: sample $j \sim p_j$, apply $\mathcal{B}_j$, measure $O$ to get $a_j$, and form $\hat{e} = \gamma\,\mathrm{sgn}(c_j)\,a_j$. Linearity gives $\mathbb{E}[\hat{e}] = \langle O\rangle_{\mathrm{ideal}}$ exactly, with spread $\Delta e_{\max}^{\mathrm{PEC}} = \gamma$ [2]. The price is variance: overhead $\gamma^2$ *per corrected gate*, i.e. $\gamma^{2|C|}$ — exponential in the circuit fault rate [4]. But $\gamma \to 1$ as noise vanishes, so PEC is asymptotically free on clean hardware.

For Pauli noise the decomposition is explicit and factorizes per layer, with sparse Pauli–Lindblad models making learning tractable at scale [1]. Two refinements reduce the exponent's constant:

- **Reduced-overhead QPDs** (Piveteau et al. [4]): optimized quasiprobability decompositions of two-qubit gates lower per-gate $\gamma$, directly shrinking $\gamma^{2|C|}$.
- **Pauli error propagation / pPEC** [7]: propagating Pauli corrections through the circuit identifies interfering corrections *before* sampling, reducing sampled instances from $4^n$ to $2^n$ (accepted in *Quantum*, 2025).

```haskell
-- | One PEC shot: draw basis op from |c|/gamma, apply, reweight by sign.
pecShot :: [(Double, BasisOp)]    -- ^ (coefficient, noisy basis operation)
        -> (BasisOp -> IO Double)  -- ^ hardware execution returning <O>
        -> IO Double
pecShot decomp run = do
  let gamma = sum (map (abs . fst) decomp)
      dist  = map (\(c, b) -> (abs c / gamma, (signum c, b))) decomp
  (s, b) <- sampleDiscrete dist
  a      <- run b
  return (gamma * s * a)   -- unbiased: E[gamma*sgn(c)*a] = <O>_ideal
```

### 4.3 The Sampling-Overhead Frontier: Fundamental Lower Bounds

The central theoretical result of modern QEM is that exponential sampling cost is *unavoidable* — a law for unstructured circuits, not an engineering gap [2, 3, 9].

> **Theorem (Takagi et al. [2], Thm. 3).** *For an arbitrary $(Q,K)$-error mitigation protocol with maximum bias $b_{\max}$ applied to $n$-qubit circuits with $L$-layer unitaries under local depolarizing noise, the maximum estimator spread is lower bounded by*
>
> $$\Delta e_{\max} \;\geq\; \frac{1 - 2b_{\max}}{\sqrt{2\ln 2}\,\sqrt{nQ}\,K}\left(\frac{1}{1-\epsilon_{\min}}\right)^{L},$$
>
> *where $\epsilon_{\min}$ is the minimum noise strength across the $K$ experiments.*

The proof is information-theoretic: per-layer trace-distance contraction by $(1-\epsilon)$ forces any bounded-bias estimator to resolve exponentially small differences, demanding spread — hence samples — exponential in $L$ [2]. PEC *saturates* this bound for dephasing noise:

> **Theorem (PEC optimality [2]).** *For local dephasing noise $\mathcal{Z}_\epsilon^{\otimes n}$, the optimal PEC cost $\gamma_{\mathrm{opt}} = (1-2\epsilon)^{-n}$ exactly coincides with the universal lower bound for unbiased $(1,1)$-protocols. PEC is therefore the optimal unbiased single-copy mitigation protocol for dephasing noise, at arbitrary qubit number $n$.*

Successors tighten the frontier [3, 9]:

- **Exponentially tighter bounds** (Quek et al., *Nature Physics* 2024 [3]): sample complexity scales as $\exp(n)$, not merely $\exp(L)$, because noise contracts distinguishability *across the circuit width*; limitations onset at nearly *constant* depth. Empirically $\exp(n)$-cost schemes are "pretty much as good as they can get" absent special circuit structure. The analysis extends to *non-unital* noise including $T_1$ decay — the dominant mechanism in superconducting qubits.
- **Unified NISQ limitations** (npj Quantum Information 2025 [9]): for single-copy mitigation of depth-$t$ circuits, any quantum advantage needs runtime $T(n) = 2^{\Omega(|\log \mu|\,t)}$ — simultaneously capturing noise-induced barren plateaus and mitigation's exponential sampling cost, and applying even to *learning-based* mitigation that adapts from prior queries.

The practical reading: mitigation is a *constant-depth technology*. It multiplies the effective depth reachable on NISQ devices, but cannot convert polynomial-depth noisy circuits into reliable computations.

### 4.4 Composition, Hybridization, and the Bias–Variance Economy

The state of the art is *composite mitigation*: Pauli twirling, readout mitigation, dynamical decoupling, symmetry post-selection, and ZNE or PEC on top [5]. Composition is not always additive — post-selection discards shots (raising variance), and twirling changes the effective channel the other methods must learn.

The shot-efficient protocol of [8] makes the economy quantitative. On a 156-qubit IBM Heron processor (`ibm_marrakesh`, median two-qubit error $2.99\times10^{-3}$, median readout error $1.45\times10^{-2}$), the authors fix the *total* shot budget and decompose MSE into bias² and variance for raw estimation, readout mitigation, uniform ZNE, and adaptive shot-allocation ZNE (ASB-ZNE). Sobering findings: adaptive allocation beat uniform allocation in only two of six budgets (mean MSE ratio $2.16$), and standard ZNE *increased* bias versus raw measurement for a bias-sensitive parity observable — the finite-shot *help–harm boundaries* of Richardson extrapolation [8].

A compact calculator makes the frontier concrete:

```rust
/// Sampling overhead for PEC: shots multiplier = gamma^(2 * n_gates).
fn pec_shot_multiplier(gamma_per_gate: f64, n_gates: usize) -> f64 {
    gamma_per_gate.powi(2 * n_gates as i32)
}

fn main() {
    // gamma = 1.02 per gate (excellent hardware), 1000 corrected gates
    let m = pec_shot_multiplier(1.02, 1000);
    println!("shots multiplier: {:.2e}", m); // ~4.3e17: infeasible
    // gamma = 1.001 per gate, 200 gates
    let m2 = pec_shot_multiplier(1.001, 200);
    println!("shots multiplier: {:.2e}", m2); // ~1.5: trivially feasible
}
```

PEC is practical only when the *total* fault rate is $O(1)$; for deep circuits $\gamma^{2|C|}$ exceeds any realistic budget — full PEC on a 100-qubit depth-4000 Trotter circuit would exceed a day of QPU time at the best current error rates.

## 5. Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 Proof sketch of the exponential lower bound

For per-layer depolarizing noise $\mathcal{D}_\epsilon^{\otimes n}$, take orthogonal inputs $\psi, \phi$ with distinguishable ideal outputs. Each layer contracts trace distance by $(1-\epsilon)$, so after $L$ layers $D_{\mathrm{tr}}(\mathcal{E}(\psi), \mathcal{E}(\phi)) \leq (1-\epsilon)^L$ [2]. An estimator with bias $\leq b_{\max}$ on both must output values separated by $\geq 1-2b_{\max}$ while the distributions are $(1-\epsilon)^L$-close; data processing on the classical post-processing forces the spread bound of Theorem 3, and Hoeffding converts spread to samples, $N = \Omega(\Delta e_{\max}^2)$. Reference [3] strengthens the $n$-dependence to a genuine $\exp(n)$ bound via a width-wise distinguishability argument.

### 5.2 Proof sketch of PEC optimality for dephasing

For per-qubit $\mathcal{Z}_\epsilon$, the inverse channel is $\mathcal{Z}_\epsilon^{-1} = \frac{1-\epsilon}{1-2\epsilon}\mathcal{I} - \frac{\epsilon}{1-2\epsilon}\mathcal{Z}$ with per-qubit $\gamma = \frac{1}{1-2\epsilon}$, hence $\gamma_{\mathrm{opt}} = (1-2\epsilon)^{-n}$ [2]. The universal unbiased-$(1,1)$ bound under the same noise evaluates to exactly $(1-2\epsilon)^{-n}$ via the trace-distance ratio $\max_{\psi,\phi} D_{\mathrm{tr}}(\psi,\phi)/D_{\mathrm{tr}}(\mathcal{Z}_\epsilon(\psi),\mathcal{Z}_\epsilon(\phi))$. No unbiased single-copy protocol can do better, and PEC achieves it — a practical protocol that is provably unimprovable.

### 5.3 Empirical benchmarks

| Experiment | Platform / scale | Key result |
|---|---|---|
| Composite digital ZNE [5] | 104-qubit chain, `ibm_washington`, 70 two-qubit gates | Exponential extrapolation wins at depth; small-range linear ZNE only stable option near noise saturation |
| Shot-efficient benchmarking [8] | 156-qubit Heron, `ibm_marrakesh` | Fixed-budget MSE decomposition; ASB-ZNE worse than uniform on average (ratio 2.16); ZNE increased bias for parity observable |
| HCl ground state [10] | Simulator + IBM hardware, contextual-subspace VQE | ZNE via CNOT-root noise amplification recovers ground-state energy at modest depth |
| Enhanced extrapolation | Repetitive-structure circuits | Trotter periodicity improves extrapolation stability vs. generic ansätze |

The pattern is consistent: order-of-magnitude bias reductions at *modest* depth and *matched* budgets, but the variance tax and model-misspecification risk grow with depth, and adaptive allocation does not reliably beat careful uniform allocation [5, 8].

## 6. Limitations

**Fundamental.** The bounds [2, 3, 9] are unconditional within their frameworks: for unstructured circuits under local noise, *every* mitigation protocol pays exponential sampling cost in depth and width. Mitigation cannot substitute for fault tolerance; it is a finite-depth amplifier of NISQ reach.

**ZNE-specific.** Extrapolation is only as good as its model: polynomial, exponential, and Richardson ansätze mis-specify the true $E(\lambda)$ for general noise, leaving residual bias no sampling removes. Near saturation the signal flattens and fits turn unstable [5]. Variance amplification $\Gamma = \sum_i c_i^2$ can exceed the bias reduction, producing help–harm inversions [8].

**PEC-specific.** Exact bias cancellation demands an exact noise model; gate-set tomography scales exponentially, and even Pauli–Lindblad learning suffers *model drift* between characterization and execution. The $\gamma^{2|C|}$ overhead restricts PEC to shallow circuits or very clean gates, and coherent errors must first be twirled — itself an approximation.

**Practical.** Correlated errors, crosstalk, and leakage violate the local-noise assumptions. Readout mitigation assumes a stable tensor-product assignment matrix. Classical overheads — fitting, sampling large QPDs, storing characterization data — are nontrivial at 100+ qubits. The bounds are *worst-case*: structured circuits (symmetries, Clifford-dominated, error-detecting codes) can evade them — where the field's headroom lies [3].

---

## 7. Conclusion

ZNE and PEC are the load-bearing pillars of quantum error mitigation, embodying complementary philosophies: ZNE is *empirical*, learning the noise response from scaled experiments; PEC is *algebraic*, inverting a learned noise model exactly in expectation at exponential sampling cost. The $(Q,K)$ framework [2] unifies them and, with its successors [3, 9], draws the sampling-overhead frontier in permanent ink: unbiased mitigation of unstructured deep circuits costs exponentially many samples, and PEC attains that bound for dephasing noise.

For practitioners: compose techniques — twirling, readout mitigation, dynamical decoupling, symmetry verification — before reaching for ZNE or PEC [5]. Budget shots, not just bias: evaluate protocols by MSE at fixed total cost and respect the help–harm boundaries [8]. Learn noise with Pauli–Lindblad methods and propagate Pauli corrections to shrink quasiprobability overhead [4, 7]. And target the structured regime: the frontier is a theorem about *unstructured* circuits, and the best mitigation research — symmetry-aware, code-assisted, early-fault-tolerant hybrids — gives circuits structure the bounds cannot see.
