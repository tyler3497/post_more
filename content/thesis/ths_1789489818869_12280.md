# Approximate Homomorphic Encryption: The CKKS Scheme, Rescaling, Modulus Switching, and Bootstrapping over Ring-LWE

## Abstract

The CKKS scheme of Cheon, Kim, Kim, and Song broke a long-standing taboo of homomorphic encryption: it abandoned exactness [1]. Rather than decrypting to precisely the value computed, CKKS decrypts to an *approximation* whose error is dominated by the rounding behavior already familiar from floating-point arithmetic. This article develops the scheme from first principles. We present the Ring Learning With Errors hardness assumption, the canonical embedding that packs a vector of complex numbers into a single plaintext polynomial while preserving error geometry, and the rescaling operation that manages noise growth during approximate multiplication by rounding the plaintext itself. We contrast rescaling with the modulus switching of BGV, describe the residue-number-system and NTT machinery that makes CKKS practical, and work through CKKS bootstrapping — CoeffToSlot, EvalMod, and SlotToCoeff — which refreshes exhausted ciphertexts at the cost of approximation error. We compare CKKS against the exact BGV and BFV schemes, discuss parameter selection under the Homomorphic Encryption Standard, and survey applications in encrypted machine learning inference.

## 1 Introduction

Fully homomorphic encryption (FHE) allows arbitrary computation on encrypted data. Since Gentry's 2009 breakthrough, the field's second generation of schemes — BGV and BFV — matured around a single contract: decrypt the ciphertext and recover *exactly* the integer or modular value that the plaintext circuit would have produced [8]. This contract fits exact domains like encrypted databases, but sits awkwardly with scientific computing and machine learning, where floating-point rounding is already the norm.

The CKKS scheme, introduced by Cheon, Kim, Kim, and Song at ASIACRYPT 2017, proposed a heretical simplification: treat the noise that every lattice-based scheme accumulates as *part of the approximation error* rather than as an adversary to be annihilated [1]. A CKKS ciphertext encrypts a vector of complex numbers; homomorphic addition and multiplication return ciphertexts whose decryptions are close to — but not equal to — the true results. The central technical contribution is the **rescaling** procedure, which truncates a ciphertext into a smaller modulus and thereby rounds the plaintext, keeping message magnitudes bounded through deep circuits. The modulus bit-length grows only *linearly* with multiplicative depth [1].

This article is a self-contained technical treatment. Section 2 recalls Learning With Errors, Ring-LWE, and the noise-management techniques of earlier schemes. Section 3 develops CKKS proper: the cyclotomic ring, the canonical embedding and its isometric packing of complex slots, encoding with a scaling factor, and the rescaling operation. Section 4 dives deeper into four topics: the geometry of the canonical embedding, rescaling versus BGV-style modulus switching, the RNS/NTT implementation substrate, and CKKS bootstrapping. Section 5 states correctness and precision results, surveys implementations and encrypted-inference milestones, and Section 6 confronts the scheme's genuine limitations — including a subtle security caveat discovered years after publication [9].

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Learning With Errors and its ring variant

The security of nearly all practical FHE traces to the **Learning With Errors** (LWE) problem introduced by Regev [2]. Given a random matrix **A** over Z_q, a secret vector **s**, and a small error vector **e**, the LWE assumption asserts that the pair (**A**, **b** = **A·s** + **e** mod q) is computationally indistinguishable from uniform. 

**Ring-LWE** specializes this to polynomial rings, replacing matrix-vector products with polynomial multiplication in R_q = (Z_q[X])/(X^N + 1) for N a power of two [2]. A Ring-LWE sample is a pair (a, b) with b = a·s + e mod q, where a is uniform in R_q and s, e are small polynomials. The ring structure buys an efficiency windfall: polynomial multiplication via the Number Theoretic Transform (NTT) runs in quasilinear time, and a single ciphertext carries N coefficients' worth of entropy. Every scheme discussed here — BGV, BFV, and CKKS — instantiates this template with different plaintext encodings and noise-management disciplines.

### 2.2 Generations of FHE and the noise problem

First-generation FHE (Gentry 2009) managed noise with **bootstrapping**: homomorphically evaluating the decryption circuit to refresh a noisy ciphertext. Bootstrapping is asymptotically the key to *fully* homomorphic encryption, but it was long considered prohibitively expensive.

CKKS keeps the leveled skeleton but changes the contract: the "noise" after rescaling is reinterpreted as approximation error, and decryption is only ever approximate. This single conceptual move eliminates the exactness threshold and, as we shall see, makes bootstrapping dramatically cheaper to approximate — though never exact.

### 2.3 SIMD batching

A ciphertext polynomial of degree N can carry many plaintext values at once. In BGV and BFV this uses the Chinese Remainder Theorem: when the plaintext modulus t is chosen so that X^N + 1 splits modulo t, the ring R_t factors into many small fields, each holding one "slot" [8]. CKKS cannot use this trick — its plaintext lives in characteristic zero, where no such splitting exists. Instead it packs via the **canonical embedding**, a complex-analytic map that is the subject of Section 4.1.

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 The cyclotomic ring and the canonical embedding

Fix N = 2^k and R = Z[X]/(X^N + 1), the 2N-th cyclotomic ring. Let ζ_j = exp(πi(2j+1)/N) for j = 0, …, N/2 − 1 be the primitive 2N-th roots of unity with positive imaginary part. The **canonical embedding** is

σ : R → C^{N/2},   σ(m) = (m(ζ_0), m(ζ_1), …, m(ζ_{N/2−1})).

 Crucially, σ is an *isometric* ring homomorphism for the infinity norm: ‖σ(m)‖_∞ = max_j |m(ζ_j)|. Error measured in coefficient space therefore does not blow up when viewed in slot space, and vice versa [1]. This isometry is what makes approximate encryption of complex vectors possible at all: rounding a polynomial's coefficients perturbs each slot by a comparably small amount.

### 3.2 Encoding and decoding

To encrypt z ∈ C^{N/2}, choose a scaling factor Δ (a power of two, e.g. 2^40) and define

- **Encode**: m = Ecd(z; Δ) = ⌊Δ · σ^{−1}(z)⌉ ∈ R, rounding each coefficient to the nearest Gaussian integer;
- **Decode**: Dcd(m; Δ) = σ(m)/Δ ≈ z.

The rounding introduces an error of at most half a unit per coefficient, which the isometry keeps small in every slot. 

```python
import numpy as np

def ckks_encode_toy(z, Delta=2**40):
    """Toy CKKS encoding via the inverse canonical embedding (illustrative only)."""
    n = len(z)
    N = 2 * n
    # primitive 2N-th roots of unity with positive imaginary part
    zeta = np.exp(1j * np.pi * (2 * np.arange(n) + 1) / N)
    V = np.vander(zeta, N, increasing=True)          # Vandermonde of the embedding
    rhs = np.concatenate([z, np.conj(z[::-1])])       # conjugate symmetry
    m_complex = np.linalg.solve(V, rhs)               # sigma^{-1}(z)
    return np.round((Delta * m_complex).real).astype(object)

z = np.array([3.25 - 1.5j, 0.125 + 2.0j, -7.75 + 0j, 1.0 - 1.0j])
m = ckks_encode_toy(z)
print("plaintext coefficients (first 4):", m[:4])
```

### 3.3 Key generation, encryption, decryption

Parameters: ring dimension N, a chain of moduli q_0 < q_1 < … < q_L with q_l = ∏_{i=0}^{l} p_i, and scale Δ ≈ p_i for each level prime. Key generation samples a sparse secret s (ternary or binary, Hamming weight h) and publishes RLWE samples as usual. Encryption of m = Ecd(z; Δ) at top level L produces ct = (b, a) ∈ R_{q_L}^2 with

b = −a·s + m + e  (mod q_L),

and decryption computes m + e′ = b + a·s (mod q_L), then decodes σ(m + e′)/Δ ≈ z. The error e′ aggregates the fresh-encryption noise and every subsequent rounding [1].

### 3.4 Homomorphic operations and rescaling

- **Addition**: ct_add = ct_1 + ct_2 encrypts z_1 + z_2 at unchanged scale Δ.
- **Multiplication**: the tensor product ct_mult = ct_1 ⊗ ct_2 encrypts z_1 ⊙ z_2 (slot-wise) but at scale Δ², with three ring elements. **Relinearization** uses an evaluation key to collapse back to two elements, exactly as in BGV/BFV.
- **Rescaling**: RS_{l→l−1}(ct) = ⌊(q_{l−1}/q_l)·ct⌉ (mod q_{l−1}). Because q_{l−1}/q_l = 1/p_l, this divides *both* the ciphertext and the underlying message by p_l ≈ Δ, restoring scale Δ while consuming one level. The rounding adds an error of magnitude below one per coefficient — negligible next to Δ [1].

> **Lemma (Rescaling).** Let ct at level l encrypt m with scale Δ² and let p_l ≈ Δ. Then RS(ct) encrypts approximately m/p_l with scale Δ, and the canonical-embedding noise norm is divided by p_l up to an additive rounding term bounded by a small constant.

Consequently a depth-L circuit needs log q_L ≈ L·log Δ + O(1): the modulus bit-length grows **linearly** in depth, not exponentially [1]. Ciphertexts also support **rotation** of slots via the automorphisms X ↦ X^{2k+1} composed with key switching, enabling SIMD permutations, inner products, and matrix-vector products.

```haskell
-- Rescaling as functional pseudocode: divide modulus and message together
rescale :: Integer -> Ciphertext -> Ciphertext
rescale p (CT l polys q) = CT (l - 1) polys' (q `div` p)
  where polys' = map (map (roundDiv p)) polys
        roundDiv d x  = (x + d `div` 2) `div` d   -- nearest-integer rounding
```

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The canonical embedding as an isometric packing map

The canonical embedding deserves closer scrutiny, because it is the single design decision that separates CKKS from every exact scheme. In BGV/BFV, batching exploits algebraic splitting: the plaintext ring R_t ≅ ∏ F_i factors into fields, and each factor is a slot. 

CKKS instead works over C. The map σ embeds the integer polynomial ring into complex space by evaluation at roots of unity, and the number of slots is *always* N/2, independent of any parameter choice. The isometry ‖σ(m)‖_∞ = max_j |m(ζ_j)| has a concrete operational meaning: if we perturb coefficients by rounding (encoding) or by RLWE noise (encryption), each slot's value moves by at most the coefficient perturbation times a small, dimension-dependent constant. 

### 4.2 Rescaling versus modulus switching

It is tempting to call CKKS rescaling "just modulus switching," since the formula ⌊(q′/q)·ct⌉ is identical to BGV's. The semantics differ profoundly, and the difference is worth a table.

| Aspect | BGV modulus switching | BFV scale management | CKKS rescaling |
|---|---|---|---|
| Plaintext space | Z_t (exact integers) | Z_t (exact integers) | C^{N/2} (approximate) |
| Operation | ⌊(p/q)·ct⌉ mod p | multiply by t/q, round | ⌊ct/p_l⌉ mod q_{l−1} |
| Message effect | unchanged | unchanged (scale t restored) | **divided by p_l ≈ Δ** |
| Noise effect | shrunk by p/q | absorbed into rounding | shrunk by p_l; becomes approximation error |
| Correctness condition | noise < q/(2t) hard bound | noise < q/(2t) hard bound | error small relative to message (soft) |
| Failure mode | catastrophic wrong decrypt | catastrophic wrong decrypt | graceful precision loss |

In BGV, modulus switching is a *noise-reduction* device that must preserve the message bit-for-bit; if noise ever exceeds the threshold, decryption is simply wrong. In CKKS, rescaling is a *precision-management* device: it deliberately rounds the message, and there is no cliff — only a gradual erosion of significant figures [1]. This is why CKKS can tolerate the accumulated error of bootstrapping (Section 4.4) while BGV/BFV bootstrapping must be exact and is correspondingly far more expensive.

### 4.3 RNS, NTT, and machine-word arithmetic

The original CKKS description uses one giant modulus q_L of hundreds or thousands of bits — unusable with hardware integer units. The **full RNS variant** of Cheon et al. replaces q_l with a product of machine-word primes, q_l = ∏_{i=0}^{l} p_i, each p_i fitting in 50–60 bits and satisfying p_i ≡ 1 (mod 2N) so that NTTs exist modulo each p_i [4]. All polynomial arithmetic then happens residue-by-residue, with NTT-based multiplication in O(N log N) per residue.

Two operations become nontrivial in RNS form:

1. **Rescaling** requires dividing by p_l and dropping that residue — easy, since p_l is literally the last prime in the chain: ⌊ct/p_l⌉ is computed per remaining residue with a small correction for the rounding.
2. **Key switching** (relinearization, rotations) requires lifting a ciphertext from modulus q_l to a larger modulus p·q_l and back, which needs **base conversion**: reconstructing each coefficient's value modulo a new prime from its RNS residues. Fast base-conversion algorithms using precomputed NTT-domain constants make this practical, and the *hybrid* key-switching technique splits the difference between digit-decomposition cost and noise growth [4][6].

```rust
/// NTT-domain multiplication modulo one RNS prime (schematic).
fn ntt_mul(a: &[u64], b: &[u64], p: u64, n: usize) -> Vec<u64> {
    let fa = ntt(a, p, n);            // forward transform of a
    let fb = ntt(b, p, n);            // forward transform of b
    let fc: Vec<u64> = fa.iter().zip(fb.iter())
        .map(|(x, y)| ((x * y) % p))
        .collect();
    intt(&fc, p, n)                   // back to coefficient domain
}
// A full RNS multiplication maps ntt_mul across every prime p_i in the chain.
```

### 4.4 Bootstrapping: CoeffToSlot, EvalMod, SlotToCoeff

A leveled CKKS ciphertext eventually runs out of levels: after L rescalings the modulus is q_0, too small for further multiplication. **Bootstrapping** refreshes it to a large modulus Q without decrypting, enabling unbounded depth [3]. CKKS bootstrapping is conceptually identical to other schemes' — homomorphically evaluate decryption — but approximate throughout, which makes it an order of magnitude cheaper than exact bootstrapping.

Let ct = (b, a) encrypt m at level 0, so b + a·s = m + e (mod q_0) with small m + e. The four stages are:

1. **ModRaise.** Reinterpret the polynomials' coefficients as integers in a larger range and view ct as encrypting the same value modulo Q ≫ q_0. Now ⟨ct, sk⟩ = q_0·I + m + e *over the integers*, where I is a small unknown integer polynomial recording how many times each coefficient wrapped around modulo q_0.
2. **CoeffToSlot.** Apply a homomorphic linear transform — essentially the DFT matrix, implemented with O(log N) rotations and plaintext multiplications — that moves the *coefficients* of the message polynomial into the *slots*. After this step, each slot holds one coefficient t_i = q_0·I_i + m_i + e_i.
3. **EvalMod.** Homomorphically evaluate the modular reduction t ↦ t mod q_0, slot-wise, by a polynomial approximation. The trick of Cheon et al. exploits the scaled sine: iterating g(t) = t − (q_0/2π)·sin(2πt/q_0) converges to the nearest multiple of q_0 offset, so t − K-fold iteration of g approximates t mod q_0 with error decaying exponentially in the number of iterations K [3]. In practice the sine itself is replaced by a minimax polynomial (Taylor or Remez) of modest degree, and K ≈ 8–14 iterations consume the bulk of the bootstrapping levels.
4. **SlotToCoeff.** Apply the inverse homomorphic linear transform, moving the reduced values back into coefficient positions. The result encrypts approximately m + e at a high modulus Q, with fresh level budget — at the cost of the EvalMod approximation error and the consumed levels.

 * Level bookkeeping for leveled CKKS: rescaling consumes levels,
 * bootstrapping restores them. Safety: level never goes negative.

Init == level = L
Rescale == level > 0 /\ level' = level - 1
Bootstrap == level = 0 /\ level' = Lboot

## 5 Empirical Evaluation and Proofs

### 5.1 Correctness and precision theorems

The foundational guarantee of CKKS is not exactness but *bounded precision loss*:

> **Theorem (Approximate correctness [1]).** Let ct be the result of homomorphically evaluating a circuit of multiplicative depth L over CKKS ciphertexts with scale Δ. Then decryption and decoding yield z̃ with ‖z̃ − z‖_∞ ≤ B·2^{−p} for an explicit bound B growing at most linearly in L, where z is the exact plaintext result. In particular, the precision loss relative to unencrypted floating-point evaluation of the same circuit is bounded by approximately one additional bit.

The proof tracks two error sources — the RLWE noise e and the rescaling roundings — through the canonical embedding isometry, showing each rescaling divides the accumulated error by p_l ≈ Δ just as it divides the message. 

### 5.2 Parameter selection and the Homomorphic Encryption Standard

| Ring dimension N | Slots (N/2) | Total log q budget | Typical use |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2^13 (8192) | 4096 | ~218 bits | shallow circuits, demos |
| 2^14 (16384) | 8192 | ~438 bits | medium-depth inference |
| 2^15 (32768) | 16384 | ~881 bits | deep circuits, bootstrapping |
| 2^16 (65536) | 32768 | ~1753 bits | large-scale bootstrapping workloads |

 

### 5.3 Implementations

The reference implementation **HEAAN** (Homomorphic Encryption for Arithmetic of Approximate Numbers) by the scheme's authors demonstrated both the original construction and the first CKKS bootstrapping [3]. Today the ecosystem is broad: **HElib** (Halevi–Shoup) supports CKKS alongside BGV; **OpenFHE** (the successor to PALISADE) is the most widely used open-source library with production-grade RNS-CKKS, hybrid key switching, and bootstrapping [6]; **Lattigo** provides a clean Go implementation; and Microsoft **SEAL** supports CKKS for leveled workloads. 

### 5.4 Encrypted machine learning: from CryptoNets to ImageNet

The canonical application lineage begins with **CryptoNets** (ICML 2016), which showed that neural-network inference could run on encrypted data at all — using an exact leveled scheme over integers, with square activations replacing ReLUs and achieving 99% accuracy on MNIST [7]. CKKS removed the two obstacles that made CryptoNets a laboratory curiosity: exact integer arithmetic forced awkward quantization, and the absence of approximate bootstrapping capped network depth.

## 6 Limitations

**Approximation is a ceiling, not a floor.** CKKS is unsuitable wherever exactness is load-bearing: financial settlement, encrypted search indices, or any computation whose control flow branches on exact equality. 

**A genuine security caveat.** The original security argument targets IND-CPA: ciphertexts leak nothing. But Li and Micciancio showed that CKKS is *not* secure against attackers who observe decryption results (the IND-CPA^D model): because decryption reveals m + e including the noise, a passive observer of enough approximate decryptions can recover the secret key [9]. The mitigation is operational — never share decryption outputs with untrusted parties, or apply noise flooding (adding large fresh noise before decryption) — and the Homomorphic Encryption Standard now documents this explicitly [5]. Any deployment where the decryptor and the evaluator are mutually distrustful must account for it.

**Comparison with exact schemes.** BGV and BFV remain superior for integer workloads: exact decryption, simpler noise analysis, and no IND-CPA^D-style leakage from approximate output [8]. CKKS wins only where the application already tolerates rounding — which, fortunately, describes most of numerical computing and machine learning.

---

## 7 Conclusion

CKKS reframed homomorphic encryption around a pragmatic insight: if the application tolerates approximation, the cryptosystem should too. The canonical embedding gives a geometrically faithful packing of complex vectors into plaintext polynomials; rescaling turns noise management into floating-point-style renormalization with modulus growth linear in depth; the RNS/NTT substrate makes it run on real hardware; and approximate bootstrapping — CoeffToSlot, sine-iteration EvalMod, SlotToCoeff — refreshes ciphertexts at a cost measured in bits of precision rather than prohibitive computation [1][3][4]. Standardized parameters [5], mature libraries [6], and ImageNet-scale encrypted inference [10] mark the scheme's passage from heresy to infrastructure.  For privacy-preserving numerical computation, however, the approximate contract has proven to be exactly the right abstraction.
