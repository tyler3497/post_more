# Algebraic Multigrid Methods: Galerkin Coarsening, Strength of Connection, and Optimal-Complexity Solvers for Unstructured Elliptic Problems

## Abstract

Algebraic multigrid methods construct multilevel solvers from the entries of a sparse matrix alone, delivering mesh-independent convergence for elliptic problems on unstructured grids without geometric information [1][3]. This thesis develops the complete theoretical and algorithmic foundation of AMG: the multigrid principle decomposing error into *smoothing* and *coarse-grid correction*, classical Ruge–Stüben AMG [10] with C/F splitting driven by strength of connection, smoothed-aggregation AMG [4] preserving near-nullspace modes through tentative prolongators, and the Xu–Zikatanov identity [1] giving sharp two-level convergence bounds via the weak approximation property. Compatible relaxation is developed as both a convergence-theory tool and a practical coarsening quality measure. The analysis extends to parallel AMG at scale — hypre's BoomerAMG [9] on hundreds of thousands of cores, NVIDIA AmgX [7] on GPUs, and tensor-core accelerated solvers — identifying the algebraic obstacles to scalable setup and solve phases. Empirical evidence from PyAMG [5] and benchmark suites demonstrates optimal O(n) complexity: three-dimensional Poisson problems with millions of unknowns converge in a dozen V-cycles with operator complexity near 1.3.

## 1 Introduction

The numerical solution of elliptic partial differential equations — Poisson's equation, diffusion with heterogeneous coefficients, linear elasticity — reduces, after discretization by finite differences or finite elements, to a linear system

$$Ax = b,$$

where $A \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$ is a large, sparse, typically symmetric positive definite (SPD) matrix with $n$ ranging from millions to billions of unknowns. Direct factorization costs $\mathcal{O}(n^3)$ in general and suffers catastrophic fill-in for three-dimensional problems; even the best Krylov subspace methods, such as conjugate gradients, require a number of iterations growing with $\sqrt{\kappa(A)}$, where $\kappa$ is the condition number that deteriorates as $\mathcal{O}(h^{-2})$ with mesh refinement [3].

Multigrid methods achieve the theoretical ideal: **optimal complexity** $\mathcal{O}(n)$, with convergence factors bounded independently of the mesh size. Classical *geometric* multigrid exploits a hierarchy of nested meshes, but it fails when the domain is geometrically complex or the problem is purely algebraic — a system of equations with no mesh at all. The founding insight of AMG, pioneered by Brandt, McCormick, and Ruge in the early 1980s and crystallized by Ruge and Stüben in 1987 [10], is that the *hierarchy itself can be built from the matrix*: grid points are identified with unknowns, adjacency with nonzero entries, and the "smoothness" of error is defined algebraically rather than geometrically.

This thesis presents a unified treatment of algebraic multigrid across five themes:

1. **The multigrid principle** — the complementary decomposition of error reduction into smoothing and coarse-grid correction, with two-grid analysis.
2. **Classical (Ruge–Stüben) AMG** — C/F splitting by strength of connection, direct and standard interpolation, and the Galerkin construction of coarse operators.
3. **Smoothed aggregation AMG** — the Vaněk–Mandel–Brezina framework of tentative prolongators and near-nullspace preservation [4].
4. **Convergence theory** — the Xu–Zikatanov identity [1], the weak approximation property, and compatible relaxation as a diagnostic and constructive tool.
5. **Parallel AMG at scale** — hypre BoomerAMG [9], NVIDIA AmgX [7], and the algebraic challenges of GPU and exascale implementations.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Iterative solvers and the need for preconditioning

For SPD $A$, the conjugate gradient (CG) method converges with the error bound

$$\|e^{(k)}\|_A \le 2\left(\frac{\sqrt{\kappa} - 1}{\sqrt{\kappa} + 1}\right)^k \|e^{(0)}\|_A,$$

so the iteration count grows like $\sqrt{\kappa}$. For a second-order elliptic operator discretized with mesh size $h$, $\kappa = \mathcal{O}(h^{-2})$, and doubling resolution quadruples the iteration count. In practice, a single AMG V-cycle used as a CG preconditioner typically yields convergence in roughly **10 iterations** to a relative residual of $10^{-8}$, independent of problem size [5].

### 2.2 The two-grid cycle

A two-grid method combines two complementary mechanisms acting on the error $e^{(k)} = x - x^{(k)}$: **smoothing**, a simple iteration (Gauss–Seidel, damped Jacobi) that rapidly damps oscillatory error but stalls on smooth components, and **coarse-grid correction**, which restricts the residual to a smaller space, solves $A_c e_c = Rr$ there, and prolongates the correction back.

With prolongation $P: \mathbb{R}^{n_c} \to \mathbb{R}^n$, restriction $R: \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^{n_c}$, and coarse matrix $A_c = RAP$, the coarse-grid correction operator is the $A$-orthogonal projection

$$\Pi_c = P A_c^{-1} RA, \qquad T = I - \Pi_c.$$

The two-grid error propagator for $\nu_1$ pre- and $\nu_2$ post-smoothing steps is $E_{TG} = S^{\nu_2}(I - P A_c^{-1} RA) S^{\nu_1}$, and multilevel V-cycles, W-cycles, and F-cycles recurse this construction.

![Multigrid V-cycle and W-cycle grid hierarchy](ths_1788647359309_7cfa-0.webp)

The key algebraic condition for the coarse-grid correction to complement the smoother is the **Galerkin property**: choosing $R = P^T$ and $A_c = P^T A P$ makes the coarse correction the *best* approximation in the range of $P$ with respect to the energy norm. This variational principle is why AMG almost universally adopts the Galerkin construction: coarse problems remain SPD, and it gives the sharpest coarse correction available for a given prolongator [3].

### 2.3 Algebraic smoothness

In geometric multigrid, "smooth" means geometrically smooth; in AMG, the concept must be redefined from the matrix alone. An error is **algebraically smooth** if relaxation converges slowly on it, i.e., $Se \approx e$. For a convergent smoother with $A$-norm contraction, algebraic smoothness is characterized by the energy inequality

$$\|Se\|_A^2 = \|e\|_A^2 - \| (I - S)e \|_{\tilde{M}}^2,$$

where $\tilde{M}$ is the symmetrized smoother. For M-matrices this has a concrete interpretation: after a few Gauss–Seidel sweeps, $Ae \approx 0$, meaning the error varies slowly along *strong connections* — precisely the heuristic classical AMG exploits when deciding which unknowns can interpolate which others [10].

## 3 Methodology

The AMG pipeline consists of a **setup phase** (hierarchy construction) and a **solve phase** (cycling). We summarize the classical algorithm before developing it in depth.

**Setup phase (classical Ruge–Stüben AMG):**

1. Compute strength of connection: $i$ *strongly depends* on $j$ if $-a_{ij} \ge \theta \max_{k \ne i} (-a_{ik})$, $\theta \in (0,1]$ (often $0.25$).
2. Partition unknowns into disjoint $C$ and $F$ sets by a maximal-independent-set coarsening over the strong-connection graph.
3. Build interpolation $P$ (direct or standard formulas) from $C$-point values.
4. Form the coarse operator by the Galerkin product $A_c = P^T A P$ and recurse; the solve phase then smooths, restricts, recurses, prolongates, and smooths again.

The two complexity measures that govern practical efficiency are:

| Quantity | Definition | Target |
|---|---|---|
| Grid complexity $c_G$ | $\sum_\ell n_\ell / n_0$ | $\approx 1.2$–$1.5$ |
| Operator complexity $c_A$ | $\sum_\ell \mathrm{nnz}(A_\ell) / \mathrm{nnz}(A_0)$ | $< 1.5$ |

A well-designed AMG hierarchy coarsens by roughly a factor of four per level in 2D ($c_G \approx 4/3$) while keeping coarse operators sparse ($c_A \lesssim 1.5$); a V-cycle then costs a small multiple of one sparse matrix–vector product on the fine grid [9].

> **Theorem:** (Galerkin optimality) Let $A$ be SPD and $P$ have full column rank. Then $A_c = P^TAP$ is SPD, and the coarse-grid correction $I - P A_c^{-1} P^T A$ is the $A$-orthogonal projection onto $\mathrm{range}(P)^\perp$; hence $\|e - P e_c\|_A = \min_{v_c} \|e - Pv_c\|_A$ [3].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Multigrid Principle: Smoothing, Coarse-Grid Correction, and Two-Grid Analysis

Multigrid's power lies in a single observation: *no single process needs to reduce all error components*. Classical relaxation is a high-frequency killer. For the one-dimensional Poisson problem with damped Jacobi ($S = I - \omega D^{-1} A$), the Fourier modes $\phi_k(x) = \sin(k\pi x)$ have eigenvalues

$$\lambda_k(S) = 1 - 2\omega \sin^2\left(\frac{k\pi h}{2}\right),$$

so with $\omega = 2/3$, high-frequency modes are damped by a factor of at least $1/3$ per sweep, while the smoothest mode is barely touched [3]. This is the **smoothing property**.

![Error spectrum: smoothing damps high frequencies, coarse-grid correction handles the rest](ths_1788647359309_7cfa-2.webp)

Coarse-grid correction does exactly the complementary job. The projection $T = I - P A_c^{-1} P^T A$ annihilates precisely the components in $\mathrm{range}(P)$ while leaving the rest untouched; if $P$ represents smooth functions accurately — the **approximation property** of Hackbusch — then the two-grid convergence factor satisfies $\rho(E_{TG}) \le 1 - \delta < 1$ with $\delta$ independent of mesh size [3]. The multilevel V-cycle preserves this property by recursion; the W-cycle and F-cycle add robustness at modest extra cost. The critical lesson for AMG is that *both* properties must be secured algebraically: the smoother is fixed (Gauss–Seidel or polynomial), so all the design freedom — and all the risk — lives in the coarse spaces, i.e., in $P$.

### 4.2 Classical AMG: C/F Splitting, Strength of Connection, and Interpolation

Classical AMG, due to Ruge and Stüben [10], was developed for M-matrices: nonsingular $A$ with $a_{ii} > 0$, $a_{ij} \le 0$ for $i \ne j$, and $A^{-1} \ge 0$ entrywise. For such matrices, the sign pattern makes the heuristic transparent: algebraically smooth error satisfies $Ae \approx 0$, i.e.,

$$a_{ii} e_i \approx -\sum_{j \ne i} a_{ij} e_j,$$

so $e_i$ is a *weighted average* of its neighbors with weights $-a_{ij}/a_{ii}$. Large weights mark **strong connections** — neighbors whose values genuinely determine $e_i$ — and interpolation from strong neighbors is justified.

![Strength-of-connection graph coarsening: C-points and F-points with strong connections highlighted](ths_1788647359309_7cfa-1.webp)

**Strength of connection.** Point $i$ is said to *strongly depend* on point $j$ if

$$-a_{ij} \ge \theta \max_{k \ne i}(-a_{ik}), \qquad 0 < \theta \le 1,$$

with the canonical choice $\theta = 0.25$. Only strong connections participate in coarsening and interpolation; weak ones are collapsed onto the diagonal [10]. This thresholding is the decisive algebraic act, converting the matrix graph into a *coarsening graph* on which geometric intuition is replaced by a greedy maximal independent set.

**C/F splitting (Ruge–Stüben two-pass algorithm).** Each point $i$ receives a measure $\lambda_i = |S_i^T|$, the number of points strongly influenced by $i$. The first pass greedily selects as $C$-points the unassigned points of largest measure, marking their strong neighbors as $F$-points — producing a *maximal independent set* over strong connections. The second pass enforces interpolation quality: every $F$-point's strong $F$-neighbors must themselves depend strongly on the $C$-set interpolating $i$ (the *C2 condition*), converting additional points to $C$ where needed. The two goals are in tension:

1. **(C1)** $C$ is a maximal independent set — keeping the coarse problem small.
2. **(C2)** Each $F$-point strongly depends on $C$-points that can interpolate it — keeping interpolation accurate.

**Interpolation formulas.** Classical AMG offers three constructions of increasing sophistication: *direct interpolation* (weights splitting $a_{ij}$ over strong/weak $F$-neighbors), *standard interpolation* (extending the stencil to distance-two $C$-points via "twice-removed" formulas), and *multipass interpolation* (for aggressive coarsening where the $C$-neighborhood may be empty) [3].

The resulting method converges with factors of $0.1$–$0.3$ per V-cycle for scalar diffusion on unstructured meshes, with $c_A \approx 1.3$ and $c_G \approx 1.25$ — the gold standard against which all AMG variants are measured [9].

### 4.3 Smoothed Aggregation: Tentative Prolongators and Near-Nullspace Preservation

Smoothed aggregation AMG (SA-AMG), introduced by Vaněk, Mandel, and Brezina [4], abandons C/F splitting entirely. Instead of selecting a subset of unknowns, it *partitions* them into disjoint **aggregates** $\{\mathcal{A}_j\}$, each aggregate becoming one coarse degree of freedom — an agglomeration philosophy that generalizes naturally to systems of PDEs and parallel implementation.

The construction proceeds in three steps:

1. **Tentative prolongator.** Given the near-nullspace modes — vectors the operator nearly annihilates, such as constants for the Laplacian or rigid-body modes for elasticity — assembled into a candidate matrix $B \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n_b}$, restrict $B$ to each aggregate and orthonormalize by QR factorization. This guarantees $\mathrm{range}(B) \subset \mathrm{range}(P_t)$: the tentative prolongator *exactly reproduces* the near-nullspace, so coarse-grid correction cannot be blind to the error components relaxation handles worst.
2. **Prolongator smoothing.** Apply one damped-Jacobi step, $P = (I - \omega D^{-1} A) P_t$, to each column of $P_t$, enlarging basis-function support and improving energy-norm approximation — the method's namesake improvement [4].
3. **Galerkin coarse operator:** $A_c = P^T A P$, recursed.

> **Theorem:** (Vaněk–Brezina–Mandel [4]) For the SA-AMG two-level method, the convergence estimate depends only *polylogarithmically* on the mesh size and requires only a *weak approximation property* for the aggregates — a condition that can be verified computationally *a priori* once the aggregates are known.

The decisive advantage of SA-AMG is its treatment of **systems**: for linear elasticity, the near-nullspace is the six-dimensional space of rigid-body modes (three translations, three rotations), and reproducing all six exactly is what makes SA-AMG succeed where classical scalar AMG fails [8]. Adaptive SA ($\alpha$SA) extends this further by *discovering* near-nullspace modes through relaxation on the homogeneous problem, removing the need for user-supplied candidates [11].

A compact Python illustration of the SA-AMG setup (as exposed by PyAMG [5]) is:

```python
import numpy as np
import pyamg

# 5-point Laplacian on an n x n grid: the model elliptic problem
A = pyamg.gallery.poisson((200, 200), format='csr')
b = np.random.rand(A.shape[0])

ml = pyamg.smoothed_aggregation_solver(A)   # hierarchy built in setup
x = ml.solve(b, tol=1e-10)                  # V-cycle iteration
print(f"relative residual: {np.linalg.norm(b - A @ x)/np.linalg.norm(b):.2e}")
```

PyAMG additionally implements classical Ruge–Stüben solvers, compatible-relaxation coarsening, and strength-of-connection measures (symmetric, evolution, affinity, algebraic distance), making it the standard open-source laboratory for AMG experimentation [5].

### 4.4 Convergence Theory and Parallel AMG: The XZ Identity, Compatible Relaxation, and GPU Solvers

**The Xu–Zikatanov identity.** The definitive algebraic convergence result for symmetric AMG is the XZ identity [1]. For the two-grid method with $A$-convergent smoother inducing the symmetrized operator $\bar{M}$, the exact $A$-norm convergence factor is

$$\|E_{TG}\|_A^2 = 1 - \frac{1}{K}, \qquad K = \max_{v \ne 0} \min_{v_c} \frac{\|v - Pv_c\|_{\bar{M}}^2}{\|v\|_A^2}.$$

> **Theorem:** (Xu–Zikatanov [1]) Under standard SPD assumptions with full-rank $P$ and $A$-convergent smoother, $\|E_{TG}\|_A = \sqrt{1 - 1/K}$ exactly. Uniform convergence $\|E_{TG}\|_A \le \rho < 1$ holds if and only if the **weak approximation property** holds uniformly: $\min_{v_c}\|v - Pv_c\|_{\bar{M}}^2 \le C \|v\|_A^2$.

This reduces the entire question of AMG convergence to a single approximation-theoretic quantity $K$ — the price of approximating arbitrary fine vectors by the coarse space in the smoother-induced norm. Falgout and Vassilevski's sharp two-level theory complements it by characterizing *ideal interpolation* as the minimizer of the convergence bound, guiding practical interpolation design [3].

**Compatible relaxation (CR).** Introduced by Brandt and Ron, compatible relaxation measures how well relaxation converges on the $F$-variables with $C$-variables held fixed:

$$\rho_{CR} = \rho\big((I - M_{FF}^{-1}A_{FF})^{\nu}\big),$$

where the subscript $FF$ denotes the $F$-block of the smoother splitting. Fast CR convergence means the chosen $C$-set captures the slow-to-converge modes — so $\rho_{CR}$ is simultaneously a *quality measure* for any candidate coarsening and a *construction tool* (add to $C$ the points where CR stalls) [12]. PyAMG's compatible-relaxation demo exhibits the canonical behavior: CR-driven coarsening of the 2D Poisson problem yields a mild coarsening-by-two with excellent convergence [5].

**Parallel AMG at scale.** Algebraic coarsening is intrinsically sequential — greedy maximal independent sets create irregular, latency-bound communication. The parallel AMG community has responded on three fronts [8][9]:

- **Parallel coarsening:** hypre's BoomerAMG implements parallel variants — CLJP (parallel maximal independent set with random tie-breaking), PMIS, and HMIS (hybrid) — that recover the quality of sequential Ruge–Stüben coarsening while scaling to *100,000+ cores* with demonstrated weak scalability for 3D diffusion [2].
- **Communication-avoiding smoothers:** Gauss–Seidel's triangular solves serialize across processors; hybrid smoothers (processor-local Gauss–Seidel with Jacobi coupling) and Chebyshev polynomial smoothers — pure SpMVs, no triangular solves — restore scalability, with polynomial smoothers proving competitive even serially due to cache effects [8].
- **GPU acceleration:** NVIDIA AmgX provides Ruge–Stüben and aggregation-based AMG on GPUs with up to **10×** speedups on the solve phase for implicit unstructured simulations [7]; hypre has been restructured with a unified memory model for heterogeneous nodes [2].

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

We report representative, reproducible behavior documented in the AMG literature and the PyAMG test suite [5][8][9].

**Convergence on model problems.** For the 3D Poisson problem with a 27-point stencil (the hypre AMG benchmark [9]), BoomerAMG with HMIS coarsening and hybrid Gauss–Seidel smoothing achieves convergence factors of approximately **0.1–0.2 per V(1,1) cycle**, with $c_G \approx 1.2$ and $c_A \approx 1.3$ — essentially optimal. The PyAMG classical-AMG demo on an unstructured mesh shows a coarsening-by-four with $c_A = 1.327$, $c_G = 1.267$ [5].

**Residual histories.** A Ruge–Stüben V-cycle solve in PyAMG exhibits textbook linear convergence — roughly one digit per two cycles, reaching $4.32 \times 10^{-7}$ at iteration 15 from an initial residual of $5.54 \times 10^{0}$ (full trace in the PyAMG examples [5]):

| Iteration | Relative residual |
|---|---|
| 0 | $5.54 \times 10^{0}$ |
| 3 | $2.96 \times 10^{-1}$ |
| 6 | $1.20 \times 10^{-2}$ |
| 9 | $9.68 \times 10^{-4}$ |
| 12 | $2.94 \times 10^{-5}$ |
| 15 | $4.32 \times 10^{-7}$ |

**AMG as a preconditioner.** Used as a CG preconditioner, a single AMG V-cycle reduces iteration counts from $\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{\kappa})$ — thousands for fine meshes — to a mesh-independent **$\approx$ 10 iterations** for $10^{-8}$ residual reduction on diffusion problems [5].

**Scalability.** hypre's BoomerAMG has demonstrated weak scaling to **100,000 cores** on 3D unstructured problems with billions of unknowns [2]. On GPUs, AmgX reports up to **10× acceleration** of the linear-solve portion of implicit CFD workloads relative to CPU AMG [7].

**Theoretical guarantees.** The XZ identity [1] converts these observations into theorems: for the model SPD problems above, the weak approximation property holds with $K$ bounded independently of $h$, yielding provably mesh-independent two-grid factors.

## 6 Limitations and Open Problems

1. **Beyond SPD elliptic problems.** Classical AMG theory and the XZ identity assume symmetric positive definiteness. Convection-dominated flows, the indefinite Helmholtz equation, and saddle-point systems (Stokes, Maxwell) defeat both the smoother and the coarsening heuristic; specialized variants (AIR, reduction-based AMG, auxiliary-space methods) exist but lack AMG's black-box reliability [6].
2. **Setup-phase cost.** The Galerkin product $P^TAP$ and strength computations can consume *over 80%* of total runtime, and their irregular integer arithmetic parallelizes poorly — the dominant GPU bottleneck [8].
3. **Parameter sensitivity.** The strength threshold $\theta$, interpolation truncation, and aggressive-coarsening levels interact nonlinearly; PyAMG's strength-measure studies show convergence varying dramatically with these choices, and no universal autotuning exists [5].
4. **Two-level vs. multilevel gap.** The XZ identity gives *exact* two-level factors, but multilevel V-cycle theory for classical AMG remains incomplete — uniform two-level convergence does not imply uniform multilevel convergence, as Brandt noted [3].
5. **Coarse-operator densification.** Galerkin products densify on coarse levels for difficult problems (anisotropy, high contrast), inflating $c_A$ and communication; sparsification trades convergence for scalability in ways not yet fully understood [2][6].
6. **Near-nullspace identification.** SA-AMG's guarantees assume the near-nullspace is known; adaptive discovery ($\alpha$SA) adds significant setup cost [11].

## 7 Conclusion

Algebraic multigrid remains the closest the field has come to a *universal* optimal solver for discretized elliptic problems. Its architecture — a fixed smoother paired with a matrix-derived coarse hierarchy — concentrates all design intelligence into three algebraic decisions: **how to coarsen**, **how to interpolate**, and **how to form coarse operators** via the Galerkin variational principle. The Xu–Zikatanov identity and compatible relaxation have transformed these heuristics into a quantitative science, giving exact convergence factors and computable coarsening diagnostics. At scale, hypre and AmgX demonstrate that the algebraic formulation — freed from geometric constraints — maps onto the largest machines and accelerators ever built, albeit with the setup phase as the outstanding challenge. For unstructured elliptic problems today, however, the verdict of four decades stands: *build the hierarchy from the matrix, and the solver will follow*.
