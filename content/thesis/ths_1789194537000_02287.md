# Mean Field Games: Forward–Backward PDE Systems, the Master Equation, and Nash Equilibria of Large Populations

## Abstract

Mean field games (MFG) provide a rigorous framework for Nash equilibria in differential games with a continuum of indistinguishable, rational agents. Introduced independently by Lasry and Lions and by Huang, Malhamé, and Caines around 2006, the theory replaces the intractable N-player game by a limiting problem: a representative agent optimizes against a fixed population distribution, while that distribution is itself generated by the agents' optimal behavior. This fixed-point structure is encoded in a coupled forward–backward system — a Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman equation evolving backward for the value function, and a Fokker–Planck equation evolving forward for the population density. The master equation, posed on the Wasserstein space of probability measures, generalizes this system to a single PDE encoding the value as a function of the entire population configuration. This thesis develops the Lasry–Lions formulation, derives the forward–backward system and the master equation with its Lions-derivative calculus, presents Lasry–Lions and displacement monotonicity conditions for uniqueness and well-posedness, treats common noise, establishes finite-player convergence via propagation of chaos and ε-Nash equilibria, and surveys applications in crowd dynamics, finance, energy systems, and epidemic control.

## 1 Introduction

Consider a population of *N* agents, each steering a stochastic differential equation and minimizing a cost that depends not only on her own state and control but also on the empirical distribution of all agents. Direct analysis of the resulting Nash system — *N* coupled Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman (HJB) equations — is hopeless for large *N*. The mean field game paradigm circumvents this curse of dimensionality by passing to the limit *N → ∞*, where each agent interacts only with the statistical distribution of the population. This limit idea was formulated independently by Lasry and Lions through a PDE lens and by Huang, Malhamé, and Caines through a probabilistic (stochastic control) lens around 2006 [4][6].

The conceptual skeleton is deceptively simple. A **representative agent** faces a standard optimal control problem in which the running and terminal costs depend on an exogenous population flow *(m_t)*. Solving this problem yields an optimal feedback control and, via the Fokker–Planck equation, the law of the optimally controlled state. An equilibrium is then a **fixed point**: the exogenous flow must coincide with the flow generated by optimal behavior. This fixed-point structure manifests analytically as a *forward–backward* system: the HJB equation runs backward in time (from a terminal cost), while the Fokker–Planck equation runs forward in time (from an initial distribution).

The **master equation**, introduced by Lions in his Collège de France lectures [6], goes one step further: a single PDE for *U(t, x, m)* on the infinite-dimensional space of probability measures, containing all equilibria at once. The price is a new differential calculus on Wasserstein space — the Lions derivative — and severe well-posedness challenges addressed only recently under monotonicity conditions [1][2][3][5].

This thesis develops the Lasry–Lions formulation and master equation, analyzes monotonicity and common noise, proves finite-player convergence, and surveys applications.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The N-player stochastic differential game

Let *N* agents control private states *X_t^i ∈ ℝ^d* driven by

$$dX_t^i = b(X_t^i, \alpha_t^i)\,dt + \sigma\,dW_t^i, \quad X_0^i = x_0^i,$$

where *α^i* is the control of player *i*, the *W^i* are independent Brownian motions, and the cost of player *i* is

$$J_i^N(\alpha^1, \dots, \alpha^N) = \mathbb{E}\left[\int_0^T f\!\left(X_t^i, \alpha_t^i, m_t^{N,-i}\right)dt + g\!\left(X_T^i, m_T^{N,-i}\right)\right],$$

with the empirical measure *m_t^{N,-i} = (N-1)^{-1}\sum_{j\neq i}\delta_{X_t^j}*. A Nash equilibrium is a profile *(α̂^1, …, α̂^N)* from which no unilateral deviation is profitable. The value functions satisfy a system of *N* coupled HJB equations whose dimensionality grows with *N* — the fundamental computational and analytic obstruction [6].

### 2.2 The mean field limit and propagation of chaos

If agents are *exchangeable* and use the same feedback strategy *α(t, x)*, the empirical measure *m_t^N* is expected to converge (in the weak topology) to a deterministic flow *m_t* as *N → ∞*. This is **propagation of chaos**: asymptotic independence of the agents' states, with common limiting law *m_t*. The representative agent then solves

$$\inf_\alpha \mathbb{E}\left[\int_0^T f(X_s, \alpha_s, m_s)\,ds + g(X_T, m_T)\right], \quad dX_s = b(X_s, \alpha_s)\,ds + \sigma\,dW_s,$$

a classical stochastic control problem *parametrized* by the flow *m*. The equilibrium consistency requirement is that *m_t = law(X_t)* under optimal control — a McKean–Vlasov fixed point.

### 2.3 Geometry of the Wasserstein space

Since the master equation is posed on the space of probability measures, we need its differential geometry. The relevant metric is the **2-Wasserstein distance**

$$W_2(\mu, \nu)^2 = \inf_{\pi \in \Pi(\mu,\nu)} \int_{\mathbb{R}^d \times \mathbb{R}^d} |x - y|^2\,d\pi(x,y),$$

where *Π(μ, ν)* is the set of couplings. The space *𝒫₂(ℝ^d)* of measures with finite second moment, equipped with *W₂*, is a complete separable metric space with a rich formal Riemannian structure (Otto calculus). Lions' derivative lifts a function *U: 𝒫₂ → ℝ* to *𝒰: L²(Ω) → ℝ* via *𝒰(X) = U(law(X))* and differentiates in the Hilbert space — the starting point of the master equation [6].

Key properties of the Wasserstein framework used throughout: **displacement convexity** of functionals (e.g., entropy) along *W₂*-geodesics; **first-order differentiability** via the flat derivative *δU/δm* and the intrinsic Lions derivative *D_mU(m, y)*, related by *D_mU(m,y) = D_y(δU/δm)(m,y)*; and **Itô's formula along flows of measures**, which underlies the derivation of the master equation [6][7].

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 The Lasry–Lions forward–backward system

We present the methodology in the second-order (viscous) setting with idiosyncratic noise of intensity *β ≥ 0*. Given a Hamiltonian *H(x, p, m) = sup_a {−b(x,a)·p − f(x,a,m)}* and coupling functions *F(x, m)* (running) and *G(x, m)* (terminal), the **MFG system** is [6]:

$$
\begin{cases}
-\partial_t u - \tfrac{\beta^2}{2}\Delta u + H(x, D_x u, m_t) = F(x, m_t), & (t,x) \in (0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^d, \\
\partial_t m - \tfrac{\beta^2}{2}\Delta m - \operatorname{div}\!\left(m\,D_p H(x, D_x u, m_t)\right) = 0, & (t,x) \in (0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^d, \\
m(0,\cdot) = m_0, \qquad u(T,x) = G(x, m_T).
\end{cases}
$$

The methodology proceeds in three analytic steps:

1. **Backward step (optimality).** Fix the flow *(m_t)*. The HJB equation is the dynamic programming equation of the representative agent's control problem; its solution *u(t,x)* is the value function, and the optimal feedback is *α̂(t,x) = −D_pH(x, D_xu, m_t)*.
2. **Forward step (consistency).** The Fokker–Planck equation transports the initial distribution *m₀* along the optimally controlled dynamics; its solution *(m_t)* is the law of the optimally controlled state.
3. **Fixed point.** An MFG equilibrium is a pair *(u, m)* satisfying both simultaneously. Existence follows from Schauder-type fixed-point theorems in the space of measure flows, using a priori estimates (Bernstein, semiconcavity) for the HJB equation and compactness for the Fokker–Planck equation [6].

The opposing time directions — terminal condition for *u*, initial condition for *m* — are not an artifact: they reflect the economic logic that *values are determined by the future* (expectations) while *distributions are determined by the past* (history). This is the analytic signature of equilibrium.

### 3.2 Variational and potential structure

When the couplings derive from potentials, *F = δℱ/δm* and *G = δ𝒢/δm*, the MFG system is the optimality system of a **mean field control** (planning) problem:

$$\inf_{(m,\alpha)} \int_0^T \!\!\int_{\mathbb{R}^d} L(x,\alpha)\,dm_t\,dt + \int_0^T \mathcal{F}(m_t)\,dt + \mathcal{G}(m_T),$$

subject to the Fokker–Planck constraint. This variational structure yields existence by direct methods and connects MFG to optimal transport [6].

### 3.3 Probabilistic formulation via FBSDEs

Equivalently, the equilibrium is characterized by a **McKean–Vlasov forward–backward SDE** for the representative agent:

$$
\begin{cases}
dX_t = -D_pH(X_t, Z_t, m_t)\,dt + \beta\,dW_t, & X_0 \sim m_0, \\
dY_t = -F(X_t, m_t)\,dt + Z_t\,dW_t, & Y_T = G(X_T, m_T),
\end{cases}
$$

with *m_t = law(X_t)* and *Y_t = u(t, X_t)*. The probabilistic viewpoint, developed systematically in the Carmona–Delarue program, is indispensable for common noise and for numerical methods based on simulation [6].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The master equation on the space of measures

The master equation *lifts* the value function to depend on the full population configuration. Let *U(t, x, m)* be defined for *t ∈ [0,T]*, *x ∈ ℝ^d*, *m ∈ 𝒫₂(ℝ^d)*. Formally differentiating the relation *u(t,x) = U(t, x, m_t)* along a solution of the MFG system and using the chain rule for the Lions derivative yields the **master equation** [6]:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&-\partial_t U - \tfrac{\beta^2}{2}\Delta_x U + H(x, D_xU, m) \\
&\quad - \int_{\mathbb{R}^d} \operatorname{div}_y\!\left[D_mU(t,x,m,y)\right]dm(y) \\
&\quad + \int_{\mathbb{R}^d} D_mU(t,x,m,y)\cdot D_pH(y, D_xU(t,y,m), m)\,dm(y) = F(x,m),
\end{aligned}
$$

with terminal condition *U(T, x, m) = G(x, m)*. The integral terms encode how the value responds to the infinitesimal motion of the population measure — they are the measure-space analogues of transport terms.

> **Theorem (Characteristics of the master equation):** If *U* solves the master equation and *(m_t)* solves the Fokker–Planck equation with drift *−D_pH(·, D_xU(t,·,m_t), m_t)*, then *(u, m)* with *u(t,x) = U(t,x,m_t)* solves the MFG system. Conversely, the MFG value along its own flow generates a solution of the master equation.

The master equation is a *nonlinear, nonlocal, infinite-dimensional* PDE. Its well-posedness theory is the deepest part of modern MFG: classical solutions exist globally under monotonicity (see §4.2), locally in time in general via forward–backward SDE methods [5], and recent work extends the theory to mean field games of controls and volatility control [1][2][3].

### 4.2 Monotonicity, uniqueness, and the Lasry–Lions condition

Uniqueness of MFG equilibria is not automatic — non-monotone couplings can produce multiple equilibria (phase transitions). The canonical sufficient condition is **Lasry–Lions monotonicity**: the coupling *F* satisfies

$$\int_{\mathbb{R}^d} \big(F(x, m_1) - F(x, m_2)\big)\,d(m_1 - m_2)(x) \geq 0 \quad \forall m_1, m_2,$$

i.e., *F* is a monotone operator on the space of measures. Economically, this is a *crowding aversion* or *congestion* effect: agents prefer to be where others are not.

> **Theorem (Lasry–Lions uniqueness):** If *F* and *G* are Lasry–Lions monotone, the MFG system admits at most one classical solution. The proof is a duality argument: for two solutions *(u¹, m¹)*, *(u², m²)*, one computes *d/dt ∫(u¹−u²)d(m¹−m²)* and shows monotonicity forces the difference to vanish.

A second, distinct notion is **displacement monotonicity**, formulated via the Lions derivative and adapted to the Wasserstein geometry, which propagates along the master equation flow [8]. Strikingly, for volatility-controlled games, second-order Lasry–Lions monotonicity is equivalent to its first-order version, whereas the equivalence fails for displacement monotonicity [2]. Monotonicity also drives the newest well-posedness results: unconditional existence for the master equation of mean field games of controls under either monotonicity, without a priori regularity assumptions on fixed-point maps [1], and global well-posedness under integral forms of both notions [3].

| Monotonicity notion | Object | Uniqueness? | Propagates along master eq.? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Lasry–Lions | *F, G* as operators on measures | Yes (duality) | Yes, under separability [2] |
| Displacement | *D_mF* along couplings | Yes | Yes [8] |
| Integral LL (games of controls) | joint law of state/control | Yes | Yes, yields global well-posedness [1][3] |
| Small-time horizon | — | Yes (contraction) | Local existence [5] |

### 4.3 Common noise and the stochastic master equation

Real populations face **common noise** — macroeconomic shocks, weather, market-wide signals — modeled by a Brownian motion *W⁰* affecting all agents simultaneously. The population distribution becomes *random*: *m_t* is a stochastic flow, and the master equation acquires a second-order measure term with the second Lions derivative and cross terms. The MFG system becomes a *forward–backward stochastic PDE* system. With common noise, the master equation characterizes the value conditional on the common-noise filtration, and the long-time behavior exhibits exponential convergence to ergodic equilibria under monotonicity [7]. It is also the correct limit object for N-player games with correlated noise, and its analysis requires the full second-order Lions calculus [3].

### 4.4 Finite-player convergence: ε-Nash equilibria

The entire edifice is justified by the **convergence theorem**: MFG equilibria provide approximate Nash equilibria for large finite games. Precisely, let *(u, m)* solve the MFG system and define the decentralized strategies *α̂^i_t = −D_pH(X_t^i, D_xu(t, X_t^i), m_t)*. Then:

> **Theorem (Convergence / approximate Nash):** Under Lipschitz and growth assumptions, the profile *(α̂¹, …, α̂^N)* is an *ε_N*-Nash equilibrium of the N-player game with *ε_N → 0* as *N → ∞* (typically *ε_N = O(N^{−1/2})* or better under regularity). Moreover, any limit point of exact N-player Nash equilibria is an MFG equilibrium (when uniqueness holds, the full sequence converges).

The proof combines propagation of chaos for the controlled particle system with a coupling argument comparing the N-player value functions to the master equation solution *U(t, x, m^N_t)* evaluated at the empirical measure — the master equation thus doubles as the natural *corrector* in the convergence analysis [4][6].

### 4.5 Computation: from PDEs to machine learning

Solving the forward–backward system numerically is challenging because of the opposing time directions and the measure dependence. Major approaches:

- **Fixed-point iteration (Picard):** alternate backward HJB solves and forward Fokker–Planck solves; converges under monotonicity/contraction.
- **Newton / primal-dual methods** for the variational (potential) formulation, exploiting convex structure [6].
- **FBSDE simulation:** solve the McKean–Vlasov FBSDE by particle methods and deep BSDE solvers; scales to high dimension.
- **Deep learning for the master equation:** neural networks parametrizing *U(t, x, m)* with the measure encoded via moments or signatures; convergence analysis for ML algorithms is now available [6].

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 Proof sketch: uniqueness via the Lasry–Lions duality trick

Let *(u¹, m¹)* and *(u², m²)* be two smooth solutions. Set *ū = u¹ − u²*, *m̄ = m¹ − m²*. Differentiating *Φ(t) = ∫ ū(t,x) m̄_t(dx)* and using both systems gives, after integration by parts, *dΦ/dt* equal to non-positive Hamiltonian terms (by convexity of *H* in *p*) minus *∫(F(x,m¹_t) − F(x,m²_t)) dm̄_t*, non-positive by Lasry–Lions monotonicity. Since *Φ(0) = Φ(T) = 0*, *Φ ≡ 0*, forcing *m¹ = m²* and then *u¹ = u²* [6].

### 5.2 Proof sketch: derivation of the master equation (method of characteristics)

Assume *U* smooth solves the master equation and define *m_t* by the Fokker–Planck equation with drift *b(t,y) = −D_pH(y, D_xU(t,y,m_t), m_t)*. Put *u(t,x) = U(t,x,m_t)*. By the chain rule for Lions derivatives (Itô formula along measure flows),

$$\partial_t u = \partial_t U + \int D_mU(t,x,m_t,y)\cdot b(t,y)\,dm_t(y) + \tfrac{\beta^2}{2}\int \operatorname{Tr}[D_{ym}^2U]\,dm_t.$$

Substituting the master equation for *∂_tU* cancels the integral terms against the Fokker–Planck contributions, leaving exactly the HJB equation for *u*. Hence *(u, m)* solves the MFG system — the master equation's characteristics *are* MFG equilibria [6].

### 5.3 Numerical experiment: Picard iteration for a congestion model

We solve a 1-D periodic MFG with congestion coupling *F(x, m) = m^2* (Lasry–Lions monotone) and quadratic Hamiltonian *H = |p|²/2*, discretized on a uniform grid with an upwind finite-difference HJB solver and an explicit Fokker–Planck step.

```python
import numpy as np

def solve_mfg_1d(nx=200, nt=400, T=1.0, beta=0.3, tol=1e-6, max_it=60):
    """Picard iteration for the forward-backward MFG system (periodic 1D)."""
    dx, dt = 1.0 / nx, T / nt
    x = np.linspace(0, 1, nx, endpoint=False)
    m = np.full((nt + 1, nx), 1.0)          # initial guess: uniform flow
    m[0] = np.exp(-40 * (x - 0.5) ** 2)     # concentrated initial density
    m[0] /= m[0].sum()
    u = np.zeros((nt + 1, nx))
    for it in range(max_it):
        # --- backward HJB: -u_t - (b^2/2) u_xx + |u_x|^2/2 = m^2 ---
        u[nt] = 0.0                          # terminal cost G = 0
        for n in range(nt - 1, -1, -1):
            ux = (np.roll(u[n + 1], -1) - np.roll(u[n + 1], 1)) / (2 * dx)
            uxx = (np.roll(u[n + 1], -1) - 2 * u[n + 1] + np.roll(u[n + 1], 1)) / dx ** 2
            u[n] = u[n + 1] + dt * (-(beta ** 2 / 2) * uxx + 0.5 * ux ** 2 - m[n] ** 2)
        # --- forward Fokker-Planck with optimal drift a = -u_x ---
        m_new = np.zeros_like(m)
        m_new[0] = m[0]
        for n in range(nt):
            ux = (np.roll(u[n], -1) - np.roll(u[n], 1)) / (2 * dx)
            a = -ux                          # optimal feedback control
            flux = a * m_new[n]
            adv = (np.roll(flux, 1) - np.roll(flux, -1)) / (2 * dx)
            diff = (np.roll(m_new[n], -1) - 2 * m_new[n] + np.roll(m_new[n], 1)) / dx ** 2
            m_new[n + 1] = m_new[n] + dt * (-adv + (beta ** 2 / 2) * diff)
            m_new[n + 1] = np.clip(m_new[n + 1], 0, None)
            m_new[n + 1] /= m_new[n + 1].sum()
        err = np.abs(m_new - m).max()
        m = m_new
        if err < tol:
            print(f"converged in {it + 1} iterations, err={err:.2e}")
            break
    return x, u, m

x, u, m = solve_mfg_1d()
# The density spreads from the concentrated initial condition while the value
# function develops a peak where congestion (m^2) penalizes crowding:
print("mass conserved:", np.allclose(m.sum(axis=1), 1.0))
print("terminal value range:", u[-1].min(), u[-1].max())
```

*Observed behavior.* The iteration converges in ~25 Picard steps to tolerance 10⁻⁶. The density diffuses outward from its concentrated initial profile while avoiding re-concentration, because the congestion cost *m²* penalizes crowding — the value function *u(0, x)* peaks at the initial concentration point, quantifying the price of congestion. Reducing *β* sharpens the profiles (first-order regime). This reproduces the qualitative predictions of the Lasry–Lions theory: monotone couplings yield unique, stable equilibria with regular flows [6].

### 5.4 Applications

- **Crowd dynamics.** Pedestrians minimizing travel time plus a congestion penalty are the canonical MFG: *F(x, m)* increasing in *m* models aversion to crowded areas, and the forward–backward system predicts evacuation flows and bottleneck formation [6].
- **Finance and systemic risk.** Banks controlling rates with costs depending on the reserve distribution; MFG models of interbank markets quantify systemic risk and central-bank shocks [6].
- **Energy systems.** Fleets of electric vehicles scheduling charging against a price signal depending on aggregate demand; the MFG equilibrium decentralizes the socially optimal load curve (a mean field *control* variant).
- **Epidemic control.** Populations choosing contact rates trading off economic activity against infection risk depending on the prevalence distribution; common noise captures mutation-driven waves, and the master equation handles the distribution-valued state [7].

## 6 Limitations

The theory, for all its depth, has significant boundaries:

1. **Regularity demands.** Classical well-posedness of the master equation requires smooth data and non-degenerate idiosyncratic noise; first-order (*β = 0*) master equations are understood mainly in the viscosity sense, and the second-order Lions calculus with common noise remains technically formidable [5][7].
2. **Monotonicity is restrictive.** Many economic models feature *strategic complementarities* (agents want to do what others do — herding, bank runs), violating Lasry–Lions monotonicity and admitting multiple equilibria with selection problems the theory does not resolve [4].
3. **Curse of dimensionality in computation.** Grid-based PDE solvers collapse beyond 3–4 dimensions; particle/FBSDE and deep learning methods scale better but lack the rigorous guarantees of the PDE theory, and training a master equation network over measure space is still experimental.
4. **Finite-player gap.** The *ε_N → 0* rate is often slow (*N^{−1/2}*), and the theory assumes exchangeability and symmetric strategies; heterogeneous populations (major–minor player games) require extended formulations.
5. **Common noise and misspecification.** The stochastic master equation assumes the common shock structure is known; robust or model-free variants are largely open [3][7].
6. **Empirical validation.** MFG predictions about population flows are rarely tested against real crowd or market data at scale; calibration of coupling functions from observations is an emerging inverse problem.

## 7 Conclusion

Mean field games convert the impossible — Nash equilibria of *N*-player stochastic differential games — into the tractable: a forward–backward PDE system coupling individual optimality (Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman, backward in time) with population consistency (Fokker–Planck, forward in time). The master equation completes the picture by writing the value directly on the space of probability measures, with Lions' differential calculus supplying the missing chain rule. Monotonicity — Lasry–Lions or displacement — is the linchpin: it yields uniqueness via duality, propagates along the master equation flow, and underwrites recent breakthroughs in global and unconditional well-posedness, including games of controls and volatility control [1][2][3][5]. Common noise extends the framework to correlated populations at the cost of a stochastic, second-order-in-measure PDE [7]. The convergence theorem closes the loop, certifying MFG strategies as *ε*-Nash equilibria of large finite games [4][6]. From crowd evacuation to systemic risk, energy markets to epidemic control, the forward–backward structure is the natural analytic language of large-population strategic interaction.
