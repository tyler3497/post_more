# Beyond the Leader: EPaxos, Caesar, and Generalized Consensus in Leaderless Distributed Protocols

## Abstract

The classical state machine replication narrative is a monarchy: a leader sequences commands and followers obey. Leadership concentrates throughput on one machine, taxes distant clients with extra round trips, and turns leader failure into a throughput cliff. This thesis presents a rigorous treatment of the leaderless alternative: state machine replication and generalized consensus without a privileged coordinator. We dissect Egalitarian Paxos (EPaxos), which replaces total order with per-command dependency graphs committed in a single round trip on the fast path; Caesar, which reframes fast decisions as timestamp-predicated waiting; and Lamport's Generalized Consensus, the formal substrate letting non-interfering commands commit in two message delays unconditionally. We compare dependency-graph ordering against strong eventual consistency via conflict-free replicated data types (CRDTs), where commutativity eliminates coordination entirely, and develop correctness arguments — invariant-based safety, quorum intersection, and dependency closure acyclicity — alongside latency lower bounds proving two message delays optimal. We survey empirical evaluations, known bugs and their repairs, and the open problem of execution latency under conflict.

## 1 Intro

Leader-based replication dominates production systems, yet the distinguished leader is simultaneously a performance bottleneck, a latency tax on distant clients, and a single point of failure whose loss stalls progress. Geo-replicated services, where actors span continents and share one database, feel these costs most acutely [1].

Leaderless protocols ask a radical question: what if *no* replica is special? EPaxos [1], Caesar [3], and the generalized consensus family descending from Lamport [6] distribute sequencing across all replicas and commit uncontended commands in a single round trip, substituting a *partial order* for a *total order*: commands that do not interfere need not be ordered at all. Conflicting commands are ordered through dependency graphs, timestamps, or deterministic arbitration, and execution reduces to a topological sort.

This thesis makes three contributions. First, we reconstruct the core mechanisms of EPaxos, Caesar, and Generalized Paxos in a unified vocabulary, exposing where each spends its message delays and where each pays for conflict. Second, we formalize commutativity-based conflict resolution and the precise boundary at which coordination can be dropped altogether — the CRDT regime of strong eventual consistency [5]. Third, we develop correctness arguments: the dependency invariant, quorum intersection requirements, and the two-message-delay lower bound that makes fast paths optimal.

> **Thesis statement:** Leadership is an optimization for the uncontended case that becomes a liability under failure and geographic distribution; dependency-graph and commutativity-based protocols achieve strictly better availability and tail latency, at the cost of execution complexity that grows with the conflict rate.

## 2 Background

**From consensus to generalized consensus.** Traditional consensus agrees on a *sequence* of values. Lamport observed that this is stronger than necessary: state machine replication only requires agreement on a *partially ordered set* of commands, where the order need only relate commands that *interfere* [6]. Two commands interfere if executing them in different orders can produce different results — two writes to the same key, or a read and a write to the same key. Two increments of a counter, or writes to disjoint keys, commute and may be applied in any order. Generalized consensus weakens agreement accordingly: learners may learn different command sequences provided they are equivalent up to permutation of non-interfering commands.

> **Theorem: (Two-delay lower bound).** Any fault-tolerant asynchronous consensus algorithm requires at least two message delays between the issuing of a command and its execution. Moreover, no algorithm can guarantee this bound when two *different, interfering* commands are issued concurrently — but for *non-interfering* concurrent commands, two message delays are achievable *always*, and generalized consensus realizes it.

**Fast Paxos and the collision problem.** Fast Paxos introduced proposers contacting acceptors directly, skipping the leader, with acceptors voting in two message delays. Its weakness is the *collision*: if two proposers propose concurrently and acceptors vote for different values, no quorum agrees and the protocol falls back to a slow classic ballot. Generalized Paxos softens collisions into non-events when the collided commands commute.

**Quorums without leaders.** In leaderless protocols every replica is simultaneously proposer, acceptor, and learner. EPaxos uses fast-path quorums of size *f + floor((f+1)/2)* out of *2f+1* replicas: large enough that any two fast quorums intersect in a way that exposes conflicts, small enough to commit in one round trip. The 2025 repair of EPaxos generalized the admissible region to *n >= max(2e + f - 1, 2f + 1)*, where *e* bounds fast-path failures, and proved this region optimal [2].

| Protocol | Ordering primitive | Fast-path cost | Conflict handling | Quorum size |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Multi-Paxos | Total order via leader | 2 delays (leader-local) | Leader serializes | floor(n/2)+1 |
| EPaxos | Dependency graph | 2 delays | Slow path re-ballot | f + floor((f+1)/2) |
| Caesar | Timestamps + wait | 2 delays | Wait on predecessors | floor(n/2)+1 |
| Gen. Paxos | Command sequences | 2 delays | Merge commuting seqs | floor(n/2)+1 |
| CRDT (op-based) | None (commute) | 1 delay (broadcast) | Not applicable | n/a |

## 3 Methodology

Our method is analytic and reconstructive: we extract the protocol mechanisms from the primary sources [1][2][3][6], restate them in a common formal vocabulary, and prove the key invariants from first principles. We adopt the standard asynchronous crash-fault model: *n = 2f + 1* replicas, at most *f* crash failures.

We define the vocabulary precisely, since much confusion in this area is terminological:

- **Interference.** Commands *c1* and *c2* interfere if there exists a state *s* with *apply(apply(s, c1), c2) != apply(apply(s, c2), c1)*.
- **Dependency.** In EPaxos, *c1* depends on *c2* if they interfere and *c2* was known to the replica that proposed *c1*.

A recurring formal device is the *dependency invariant*: if two interfering commands are both committed, at least one records a dependency on the other. Every correctness argument in this thesis is, at bottom, a proof that some protocol maintains this invariant under quorum intersection, and that execution respects it.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 EPaxos: ordering as graph agreement

EPaxos [1] is the canonical leaderless protocol. Each replica maintains per-instance records holding a command, ballot number, dependency set, and status. To propose command *c*, replica *r* computes *deps(c)* — the committed or proposed commands known to *r* that interfere with *c* — and sends *PreAccept* to a fast-path quorum. Each recipient replies with its own computed dependencies, possibly a superset of what *r* proposed.

The fast path succeeds when all fast-quorum replies return *identical* dependency sets; then *r* broadcasts *Accept* and commits on a majority of replies. If replies disagree, the slow path runs a Paxos-style ballot to agree on the *union* of the returned dependency sets — agreement on a set, which always terminates because the union is well-defined.

Execution is where the elegance exacts its price. Replicas build the graph of committed commands and execute in reverse topological order. But dependency graphs can contain *cycles*: *c1* depends on *c2* while *c2* depends on *c1*, because neither proposer knew of the other. The fix is to execute *strongly connected components* (SCCs) atomically, ordering commands within an SCC deterministically (e.g., by instance id) — the approach made explicit in [4]. The dependency invariant guarantees that every interfering pair shares at least one edge, so all replicas serialize them identically.

The subtle correctness property is *stability* of dependencies: once a command commits with dependency set *D*, no later commit can shrink *D*. The fast quorum size is engineered so that any recovery quorum intersects every fast quorum in enough replicas to recover the committed dependencies — the *e = ceil((f+1)/2)* fast-path failure bound repaired and generalized in [2], which also documents nontrivial bugs in the original recovery procedure and supplies the first rigorously proved replacement.

```python
def epaxos_propose(replica, cmd):
    deps = {c for c in replica.known_commands if interferes(c, cmd)}
    replies = broadcast_preaccept(cmd, deps, fast_quorum(replica))
    if all(r.deps == deps for r in replies):
        broadcast_accept(cmd, deps)   # fast path: one round trip
        return commit(cmd, deps)
    deps = set.union(*(r.deps for r in replies))
    return paxos_ballot(cmd, deps)    # slow path: agree on the union
```

### 4.2 Caesar: consensus by waiting

Caesar [3] starts from the observation that EPaxos's slow path is expensive precisely when the workload conflicts — the case a geo-replicated database cares about most. Its insight is to change *what the fast decision means*. Instead of agreeing on dependency sets, each command is assigned a logical timestamp, and a replica *waits* until all commands with smaller timestamps that could interfere are decided before executing.

Concretely, a proposer broadcasts its command; each recipient replies with a timestamp proposal (its local clock advanced past any seen conflicting command) and the commands it knows that the new command might depend on. The proposer takes the maximum timestamp and broadcasts a *stable* message. A replica delivers a command with timestamp *t* only after it has decided every command with a smaller timestamp — a local wait, not a global agreement round. Where EPaxos takes the slow path on disagreeing dependency sets, Caesar *accepts the disagreement* and lets the wait rule sort it out, because the timestamp order already determines a consistent execution order.

> **Theorem: (Caesar wait-safety).** If every replica executes commands in nondecreasing timestamp order, waiting for all smaller-timestamped commands to be decided, then any two replicas execute interfering commands in the same order.

*Proof sketch.* Timestamps are assigned so that if *c1* is known to the proposer of *c2* and they interfere, then *ts(c1) < ts(c2)*. Interfering commands with distinct timestamps are thus ordered identically everywhere by the wait rule; ties break deterministically by proposer id. The wait terminates because timestamps are well-founded and every proposed command is eventually decided by quorum progress. ∎

The evaluation in [3] on five EC2 geo-replicated sites is the strongest empirical datapoint for the leaderless program: Caesar outperforms EPaxos by up to 1.7x at 30% conflict rates and Multi-Paxos by up to 3.5x — EPaxos pays a full slow-path ballot per conflict, while Caesar pays only local waiting.

### 4.3 Generalized consensus and fast-path quorums

Lamport's Generalized Paxos [6] is the common ancestor. Acceptors vote for *command sequences*; a learner that sees a quorum of votes for sequences learns their greatest lower bound under the "equivalent up to commuting permutations" order. The fast ballot lets proposers bypass the leader entirely, and collisions involving only commuting commands dissolve without a slow ballot.

The MDPI reconstruction [7] clarifies the safety subtlety: a classic ballot's leader must arbitrate among the sequences reported in phase 1b, and with slow acceptors it cannot always tell which sequences were actually learned. The quorum arithmetic must therefore guarantee that any sequence learned on the fast path is *visible* to any subsequent classic ballot's quorum — the same intersection logic behind EPaxos's *2e + f - 1* bound [2]. This is the deep unity of the area: *fast paths are quorum-intersection theorems wearing protocol clothes*.

> **Theorem: (Fast-path quorum bound).** In a system of *n* replicas tolerating *f* crash faults with fast-path tolerance *e*, fast decisions are safe only if *n >= max(2e + f - 1, 2f + 1)*, and this bound is tight.

The bound is proved in [2] by a partition argument: with fewer replicas, an adversary can split the system so that a fast decision is invisible to a recovery quorum, then drive recovery to a conflicting decision. EPaxos's original choice (*n = 2f + 1*, *e = ceil((f+1)/2)*) sits exactly on this boundary.

### 4.4 Commutativity-based conflict resolution

All three protocols exploit the same algebraic fact: *commuting operations need no order*. Three strategies span the design space: **static commutativity** (EPaxos, Caesar), where the interference relation is fixed by the application — simple but conservative, serializing operations that merely *might* conflict; **state-dependent commutativity**, where operations commute in the current state though not in general (increments commute; writing the same value twice commutes); and **semantic resolution**, where genuine conflicts meet a deterministic merge function — last-writer-wins, add-wins/remove-wins sets, or application reconciliation. The third strategy dissolves the boundary between "consensus" and "data type design": if every operation pair either commutes or has a deterministic resolution, *no agreement is needed at all*.

### 4.5 CRDTs vs coordination: when consensus can be avoided

Conflict-free replicated data types [5] are the endpoint of the commutativity program. A state-based CRDT requires states to form a join-semilattice with merge as least upper bound; an operation-based CRDT requires concurrent effectors to commute under causal delivery. Either condition yields *strong eventual consistency*: replicas that have received the same updates converge to the same state *regardless of order*, with no consensus, no leader, and no dependency graph.

The comparison with EPaxos/Caesar is instructive:

- **Consistency strength.** CRDTs provide SEC, strictly weaker than linearizability. A counter CRDT cannot report the exact value "now" — only eventual agreement. Applications needing linearizable reads cannot use CRDTs alone.
- **Latency.** Op-based CRDTs commit in *one* message delay (dissemination) versus two for leaderless consensus — beating even the lower bound of Section 2, because the bound assumes the stronger problem.
- **Expressiveness.** Not every data type admits a convergent commutative design without semantic distortion; the replicated *list* and *graph* types required years of research [5].
- **Composition.** CRDTs compose (maps of CRDTs, sets of CRDTs); consensus protocols do not — each is a monolith.

The practical synthesis, visible in systems like Riak, is *tiered*: use CRDTs where SEC suffices, and reserve EPaxos/Caesar-style coordination for the linearizable core. The open theoretical question is a *characterization theorem*: exactly which data types admit coordination-free implementations at which consistency levels — partial answers exist in the commutativity literature, but a complete algebraic classification remains open.

## 5 Empirical/Proofs

**What the measurements say.** Three empirical claims anchor the literature. First, the original EPaxos evaluation [1] showed uniform load balancing, uninterrupted availability with only a majority reachable, and single-round-trip commits in the common case. Second, the Bipartisan Paxos study [4] benchmarked MultiPaxos, EPaxos, and BPaxos on EC2: at *f = 1*, MultiPaxos peaked near 25–30k commands per second, EPaxos at 30–40k depending on conflict rate, and BPaxos at 70–75k — nearly double EPaxos — showing that *decomposing* the protocol (separating dependency service, acceptors, replicas) matters as much as the algorithm. Third, Caesar's geo-replicated evaluation [3] is the headline result for conflict-heavy workloads: 1.7x over EPaxos at 30% conflicts across five EC2 regions.

**The bug record as proof artifact.** EPaxos's history is a case study in why these protocols resist casual specification. The 2025 EPaxos* paper [2] documents that the original was "very complex, ambiguously specified and suffers from nontrivial bugs," then supplies a simpler recovery algorithm with a rigorous proof. The lesson generalizes: in leaderless protocols, the *recovery path* is the protocol — safety lives in the intersection properties invoked exactly when things go wrong. New designs should carry machine-checked or hand-verified proofs of their recovery procedures as a matter of course.

**Lower bounds: what cannot be beaten.** We collect the bounds that circumscribe the design space:

1. **Two message delays** are necessary for any fault-tolerant command execution [6]; the fast paths of EPaxos, Caesar, and Generalized Paxos all achieve it, so *commit latency* is optimal.
2. **No fast path under concurrent interference**: if two interfering commands are proposed concurrently, no protocol guarantees two-delay commit for both [6].
3. **Quorum size** *n >= max(2e + f - 1, 2f + 1)* is necessary and sufficient for *e*-resilient fast decisions [2].
4. **Execution latency is unbounded in the conflict rate** (see the separation theorem below) — the central open problem of the area.

> **Theorem: (Execution latency separation).** There exist executions in which every command of EPaxos commits on the fast path (two message delays) yet some command's execution is delayed by a chain of *k* sequential dependency resolutions for arbitrarily large *k*.

*Proof sketch.* Construct commands *c1 ... ck* where each *ci+1*, proposed concurrently with *ci*'s commit, observes *ci* as interfering and depends on it. Each commits in two delays, but *ck* cannot execute until *c1 ... c(k-1)* are all committed and ordered before it. ∎

This separation — fast *commit*, slow *execution* — is the honest caveat every leaderless proposal must carry.

## 6 Limitations

The leaderless program has four genuine limitations, and intellectual honesty requires stating them plainly.

**Execution complexity.** Dependency graphs must be maintained, topologically sorted per SCC, and garbage-collected. Under high conflict, graph maintenance dominates — the very regime where leaderless protocols are supposed to win. Caesar replaces graph sorting with potentially long local waits.

**Read-your-writes and linearizable reads.** Leader-based protocols answer linearizable reads at the leader trivially. Leaderless protocols must route reads through a quorum (EPaxos's quorum read leases [1]) or accept weaker read semantics, adding latency to the operation mix that dominates many workloads.

**The conflict cliff.** All measurements agree: leaderless throughput degrades with conflict rate, superlinearly in the worst case because slow paths serialize. A workload a leader would serialize at line rate can collapse a leaderless protocol into repeated recovery ballots.

**Specification fragility.** The EPaxos bug history [2] is a warning: these protocols have large, subtle state spaces, and safety hinges on quorum intersection properties that are easy to mis-state. Verified-protocol tooling (TLA+, Ivy, Coq) should be mandatory.

Finally, CRDTs remind us that the entire debate can sometimes be sidestepped: if the application tolerates strong eventual consistency, coordination — leaderless or otherwise — is pure overhead [5].

## 7 Conclusion

Leaderless and consensus-free protocols redraw distributed coordination around a single insight: *order is only needed where operations interfere, and interference is a property of the workload, not of the system*. EPaxos turned this into dependency graphs with single-round-trip commits [1]; Caesar turned conflict from a voting problem into a waiting problem and won back the contended case [3]; Generalized Paxos supplied the formal foundation and the two-delay lower bound proving the fast path optimal [6]; CRDTs showed that sufficient commutativity eliminates coordination entirely [5].

The monarchy had a good run. The egalitarian parliament is harder to run correctly — but for geo-replicated, failure-prone, conflict-moderate workloads, it is the better government.
