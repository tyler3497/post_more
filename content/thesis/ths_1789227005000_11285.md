# Quantum Key Distribution: From the BB84 Security Proof to Device-Independent and Satellite-Based Networks

## Abstract

Quantum key distribution (QKD) is the only key-exchange technology whose security can be reduced to the laws of quantum mechanics rather than to unproven computational assumptions. This thesis presents a unified, proof-oriented survey of the field: the Bennett–Brassard 1984 (BB84) protocol and its information-theoretic security via the Shor–Preskill entanglement-purification reduction; the decoy-state method that rescues weak-coherent-pulse implementations from photon-number-splitting attacks; measurement-device-independent QKD, which closes every detector side channel by delegating Bell-state measurement to an untrusted relay; device-independent QKD, which derives security solely from the observed violation of a Bell inequality; and satellite-based QKD, exemplified by the Micius experiments that extended secure key exchange to 1,200 km with kilohertz key rates. We formalize key-rate formulas, the 11% quantum-bit-error-rate threshold, composable security, and experimental benchmarks, and we close with the open problems — quantum repeaters, finite-key tightness, and truly loophole-free device independence — that define the road to a global quantum network.

## 1 Introduction

Conventional public-key cryptography rests on a fragile foundation: the *conjectured* hardness of factoring and discrete logarithms. Shor's factoring algorithm implies that a sufficiently large fault-tolerant quantum computer would reduce RSA and elliptic-curve key exchange to polynomial-time exercises. By contrast, the one-time pad, introduced by Shannon, is *information-theoretically* secure — but only if two parties already share a secret key as long as the message. Quantum key distribution solves exactly this bootstrapping problem: it allows two distant parties, Alice and Bob, to establish a shared secret key over an insecure quantum channel plus an authenticated classical channel, with security guaranteed against an eavesdropper, Eve, bounded only by quantum mechanics itself [1].

The field was born in 1984, when Charles Bennett and Gilles Brassard proposed encoding random bits in the polarization states of single photons, chosen from two conjugate bases [1]. Any attempt by Eve to intercept and measure the photons necessarily disturbs them — a consequence of the Heisenberg uncertainty principle and the no-cloning theorem — and this disturbance is detectable as an elevated quantum bit error rate (QBER). The protocol was demonstrated over 30 cm of free space in 1989, and within two decades QKD had matured into commercial systems.

Three conceptual revolutions followed the original proposal:

1. **Rigorous security proofs.** Early security arguments were heuristic. The watershed came with entanglement-based proofs: Lo and Chau reduced entanglement-based QKD to entanglement purification, and Shor and Preskill then showed that BB84 itself is secure, with a key rate tied to the fidelity of an implicit Calderbank–Shor–Steane (CSS) quantum error-correcting code [2].
2. **Practical photon sources.** True single-photon sources remain difficult to engineer; real systems use attenuated lasers (weak coherent pulses), which occasionally emit multiple photons and are vulnerable to the photon-number-splitting (PNS) attack. The *decoy-state* method defeats PNS by randomly varying the pulse intensity, allowing Alice and Bob to bound the single-photon contribution [3][4].
3. **Weakening trust assumptions.** Detector side-channel attacks (blinding, time-shift) showed that imperfect devices break textbook security claims. Measurement-device-independent QKD removes all trust from the detectors [5], while device-independent QKD goes further, basing security only on the violation of a Bell inequality, with no assumptions about the internal workings of the devices at all [6][7]. In parallel, the Micius satellite demonstrated that free-space links from orbit defeat the exponential loss of fiber, bringing QKD to intercontinental distances [8][9].

This thesis develops each of these ideas with the formalism they deserve: protocol definitions, adversary models, key-rate theorems, and the experiments that validate them.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Quantum primitives underlying QKD

Three quantum-mechanical facts do the heavy lifting in every QKD security proof:

- **The no-cloning theorem.** There is no unitary operation mapping an unknown state |ψ⟩ to |ψ⟩ ⊗ |ψ⟩. Eve cannot copy the photons in transit and measure the copy later.
- **Measurement disturbance.** Non-orthogonal states cannot be perfectly distinguished, and any measurement that extracts information about a state in one basis randomizes it in the conjugate basis. In BB84, Eve's intercept-resend attack on a photon prepared in the rectilinear basis, measured by Eve in the diagonal basis, introduces a 25% error rate on the sifted key — easily detected.
- **Bell nonlocality.** For the Clauser–Horne–Shimony–Holt (CHSH) inequality, local hidden-variable theories satisfy S ≤ 2, while quantum mechanics permits S up to the Tsirelson bound 2√2. A measured violation S > 2 certifies, *without any model of the devices*, that Alice's and Bob's outcomes contain intrinsic randomness inaccessible to any third party — the foundation of device-independent security [6].

### 2.2 The BB84 protocol

In the prepare-and-measure BB84 protocol [1], Alice generates two random bit strings: the *data* bits aᵢ and the *basis* choices xᵢ ∈ {+, ×}. She prepares photon i in one of four states:

| Bit aᵢ | Basis + (rectilinear) | Basis × (diagonal) |
|--------|----------------------|--------------------|
| 0 | \|0⟩ (horizontal) | \|+⟩ = (\|0⟩+\|1⟩)/√2 |
| 1 | \|1⟩ (vertical) | \|−⟩ = (\|0⟩−\|1⟩)/√2 |

Bob measures each photon in a randomly chosen basis yᵢ. After transmission, Alice and Bob announce their bases over the authenticated classical channel and discard rounds with xᵢ ≠ yᵢ (*sifting*). They sacrifice a random subset of the remaining rounds to estimate the QBER; if it exceeds a threshold, they abort. The surviving *raw key* then undergoes two classical post-processing stages:

- **Error correction** (e.g., Cascade or LDPC codes), leaking leak_EC ≈ f · h₂(QBER) bits per sifted bit, where h₂ is the binary entropy and f ≥ 1 the efficiency factor;
- **Privacy amplification** by universal hashing, which compresses the corrected key by Eve's estimated information, yielding the final *secret key* whose length is governed by the leftover hash lemma.

### 2.3 Composable security

Modern QKD is analyzed in the *composable* framework: a protocol is ε-secure if the joint state of Alice's key, Bob's key, and Eve's quantum system is ε-close in trace distance to an ideal state in which the key is uniformly random and product with Eve's system. Composability guarantees that the key can be safely used in any cryptographic application, such as one-time-pad encryption. Security proofs must hold against *coherent* (general) attacks, where Eve may entangle a joint probe across all rounds and delay her measurement arbitrarily.

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 Adversary and channel model

Throughout this thesis we grant Eve the maximum power compatible with quantum mechanics: she controls the quantum channel completely, possesses unbounded quantum computation and perfect quantum memory, and may mount collective or coherent attacks. The classical channel is assumed *authenticated* (Eve may listen but not modify — authentication itself requires a short pre-shared key, consumed at a negligible rate). Alice's and Bob's laboratories are private: no information leaks except through the declared channels.

### 3.2 Proof techniques

Four techniques recur across the protocols surveyed:

1. **Entanglement-purification reduction.** Lo and Chau's insight: an entanglement-based protocol in which Alice and Bob distill near-perfect EPR pairs via local operations and classical communication (LOCC) is secure, because a perfect EPR pair is by definition uncorrelated with Eve. Shor and Preskill showed that BB84 *is* such a protocol in disguise [2].
2. **CSS code structure.** Calderbank–Shor–Steane codes correct bit and phase errors independently. Phase-error correction corresponds to privacy amplification, so the quantum protocol reduces to classical error correction plus hashing — no quantum computer required.
3. **Random sampling and de Finetti arguments.** Estimating the phase-error rate from the observed bit-error rate, and reducing coherent attacks to collective ones via permutation symmetry, are the standard tools for finite-key and general-attack proofs.
4. **Smooth min-entropy.** The extractable key length is lower-bounded by the smooth min-entropy of Alice's raw key conditioned on Eve's system, minus the information leaked during error correction — the workhorse inequality of the Renner framework.

### 3.3 Key-rate computation

We illustrate the methodology with a compact numerical experiment: the asymptotic one-way key rate of BB84 under the Shor–Preskill analysis, r(Q) = 1 − 2h₂(Q), as a function of QBER Q, together with a decoy-state GLLP estimate. The following Python script computes the threshold and the rate curve:

```python
import numpy as np

def h2(x):
    """Binary entropy (bits), clipped for numerical stability."""
    x = np.clip(x, 1e-12, 1 - 1e-12)
    return -x * np.log2(x) - (1 - x) * np.log2(1 - x)

def bb84_rate(qber):
    """Shor-Preskill asymptotic one-way key rate per sifted bit."""
    return np.maximum(0.0, 1.0 - 2.0 * h2(qber))

# Locate the threshold where the rate vanishes: 1 - 2*h2(Q) = 0
qs = np.linspace(0, 0.25, 25001)
threshold = qs[np.argmax(bb84_rate(qs) <= 0)]
print(f"One-way BB84 threshold QBER: {threshold:.4f}  (~11%)")

# Decoy-state GLLP estimate with typical parameters
mu = 0.5                      # signal intensity
eta = 10 ** (-0.2 * 50 / 10)  # 50 km fiber at 0.2 dB/km
Y0 = 1e-5                     # dark-count yield
Y1 = eta                      # single-photon yield approx
e1 = 0.01                     # single-photon error rate
Qmu = 1 - np.exp(-mu * eta) + Y0          # signal gain
Q1 = mu * np.exp(-mu) * Y1                # single-photon gain
rate = -Qmu * 1.16 * h2(0.03) + Q1 * (1 - h2(e1))
print(f"Decoy-state asymptotic rate at 50 km: {max(rate,0):.2e} bit/pulse")
```

The script confirms the celebrated **11% threshold**: the one-way key rate vanishes when h₂(Q) = 1/2, i.e., Q ≈ 0.110. Two-way classical post-processing (advantage distillation) pushes the tolerable QBER higher, at the cost of protocol complexity.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Shor–Preskill reduction and the 11% threshold

The Shor–Preskill proof [2] proceeds in three stages. First, consider an entanglement-based protocol: Alice prepares EPR pairs, sends half to Bob, and they perform entanglement purification using a CSS code, measuring check operators to estimate bit-flip and phase-flip error rates. Because CSS codes decouple the two error types, bit-error correction and phase-error correction commute, and the phase-error syndrome need never be announced — phase correction *is* privacy amplification. Second, the measurements defining the CSS code can be pushed before Eve's intervention without changing statistics, converting the entanglement-based protocol into a prepare-and-measure scheme. Third, that scheme is exactly BB84 with random basis choice: the phase-error rate in the key-generating basis equals the bit-error rate in the conjugate basis, which Alice and Bob estimate by random sampling.

> **Theorem (Shor–Preskill [2]).** *BB84 with one-way classical post-processing is unconditionally secure against general attacks, with asymptotic key rate r ≥ 1 − h₂(δ_b) − h₂(δ_p) per sifted bit, where δ_b and δ_p are the bit- and phase-error rates. For a symmetric channel δ_b = δ_p = Q, the rate is positive for Q ≲ 11%.*

The reduction is conceptually profound: it shows that the security of a protocol using only single-qubit preparations and measurements follows from the security of entanglement distillation, with the CSS code acting as the bridge.

### 4.2 Decoy states and the photon-number-splitting attack

Practical BB84 uses phase-randomized weak coherent pulses, whose photon number follows a Poisson distribution P(n) = e^(−μ) μⁿ/n!. A pulse with n ≥ 2 photons is fatal under the *photon-number-splitting* attack: Eve performs a quantum nondemolition photon-number measurement, keeps one photon from every multi-photon pulse, forwards the rest through a lossless channel, and waits for the basis announcement before measuring her stored photon — gaining full information with zero induced error. Without countermeasures, the secure key rate scales as O(η²) with channel transmittance η, collapsing at long distance.

The decoy-state method [3] defeats PNS with a disarmingly simple idea: Alice randomly varies the mean photon number among signal (μ), decoy (ν < μ), and vacuum intensities. Because Eve cannot know which intensity a given pulse belongs to, the yields Yₙ (detection probability given n photons) and error rates eₙ are *identical* across intensities. The observed gains Q_μ = Σₙ e^(−μ)μⁿYₙ/n! for several μ then form linear constraints from which Alice and Bob lower-bound the single-photon yield Y₁ and upper-bound the single-photon error rate e₁. The GLLP key rate follows:

$$R \;\ge\; q\,\Big\{\,-Q_\mu f(E_\mu)\,h_2(E_\mu) \;+\; Q_1\big[\,1 - h_2(e_1)\,\big]\Big\},$$

where q is the sifting factor and Q₁ = μe^(−μ)Y₁. Ma, Qi, Zhao, and Lo showed that a *practical* protocol with only a vacuum and one weak decoy asymptotically approaches the infinite-decoy limit and remains viable beyond 100 km with hours of data [4]. Decoy states restored O(η) scaling and remain the standard for all deployed fiber QKD.

### 4.3 Measurement-device-independent QKD

The most devastating practical attacks targeted not the protocol but the *detectors*: bright-illumination blinding and time-shift attacks gave Eve full key knowledge while the QBER stayed nominal. Measurement-device-independent QKD [5] eliminates this entire attack surface by reversing the architecture. Both Alice and Bob prepare BB84 states (with decoy intensities) and send them to an *untrusted* central relay, Charlie, who performs a Bell-state measurement (BSM) and announces the result. A successful projection onto |ψ⁻⟩ anti-correlates Alice's and Bob's bits; they keep those rounds and proceed as in BB84.

The security intuition is a time-reversed EPR argument: conditioned on Charlie's announcement, Alice's and Bob's systems are projected into an entangled state *regardless of how Charlie obtained his outcome* — even if Charlie *is* Eve. The measurement apparatus may thus be manufactured by the adversary without compromising security; only the sources must be trusted and characterized. Decoy-state analysis carries over to bound the single-photon-pair yield Y₁₁. MDI-QKD doubles the effective channel loss (both arms must transmit), but its immunity to all detector side channels makes it the architecture of choice for star-topology quantum networks.

### 4.4 Device-independent QKD from Bell violation

Device-independent QKD asks how little must be trusted. In the DI scenario, Alice and Bob treat their devices as black boxes with classical inputs (measurement settings) and outputs; the *only* assumption is that the observed statistics violate a Bell inequality and that the laboratories do not leak [6]. Acín, Brunner, Gisin, Massar, Pironio, and Scarani derived the optimal collective attack in this scenario and proved a tight bound on Eve's Holevo information as a function of the CHSH value S:

$$\chi(B{:}E) \;\le\; h_2\!\left(\frac{1 + \sqrt{(S/2)^2 - 1}}{2}\right),$$

so that a larger Bell violation directly translates into a higher key rate [6]. Pironio *et al.* then gave the full security proof against collective attacks, showing that the protocol's key rate is a monotone function of the violation and analyzing the detection loophole [7].

The price of device independence is steep: closing the detection loophole demands overall efficiencies above ~83% for the CHSH inequality (lower with optimized non-maximally entangled states and noisy preprocessing), and key rates remain orders of magnitude below device-dependent protocols. Nevertheless, loophole-free Bell tests and the first proof-of-principle DI-QKD demonstrations establish that cryptography with *zero* device characterization is physically possible — the ultimate vindication of Ekert's 1991 intuition that Bell nonlocality, not trusted hardware, is the true resource.

### 4.5 Satellite QKD: the Micius experiments

Fiber loss (~0.2 dB/km) makes direct-transmission QKD exponentially costly beyond a few hundred kilometers, and quantum repeaters remain experimental. The satellite route exploits a simple asymmetry: most of a satellite-to-ground path is vacuum, where loss is negligible, with atmospheric attenuation confined to the lowest ~10 km. The Chinese Micius satellite (launched 2016) carried this out at scale.

Liao *et al.* implemented decoy-state BB84 on a satellite-to-ground downlink, reporting secure key rates at the kilohertz level over slant ranges up to 1,200 km — roughly *twenty orders of magnitude* higher than what the same-length fiber link could deliver [8]. Yin *et al.* distributed entangled photon pairs from Micius to two ground stations separated by 1,203 km, observing a CHSH violation of S = 2.37 ± 0.09 under strict locality conditions, proving that entanglement — and hence entanglement-based QKD — survives intercontinental-scale free-space channels [9]. A subsequent experiment wove these links into a satellite-relayed intercontinental network connecting ground stations in China and Austria via trusted-node key relay.

The caveat is architectural: the satellite must be trusted (it knows the keys), unless entanglement-based protocols with both ground stations measuring are used. Current research therefore pursues entanglement-based satellite QKD, higher-orbit relays, and ultimately quantum repeaters that would remove the trusted node entirely.

## 5 Empirical Results and Formal Arguments

The central formal result unifying Sections 4.1–4.4 is the key-rate theorem: in the asymptotic limit against collective attacks, the secret key rate per signal is lower-bounded by the Devetak–Winter expression r ≥ I(A:B) − χ(B:E) (for direct reconciliation), with χ(B:E) bounded by the phase-error rate (BB84 [2]), by decoy-estimated single-photon parameters [3][4], by the BSM-conditioned correlations (MDI [5]), or by the Bell violation (DI [6][7]). Finite-key analyses replace Shannon quantities with smooth min-entropies and add statistical-fluctuation penalties, but the hierarchy of assumptions is unchanged.

The following table summarizes the protocol landscape:

| Protocol | Trusted components | Security foundation | Demonstrated reach |
|---|---|---|---|
| BB84 (ideal) [1][2] | Source + detectors | No-cloning; EDP/CSS reduction; QBER ≲ 11% | Lab / metro fiber |
| Decoy-state BB84 [3][4] | Source + detectors | Decoy bounds on Y₁, e₁; O(η) scaling | >100 km fiber, practical |
| Decoy-state MDI-QKD [5] | Sources only | Time-reversed EPR; untrusted BSM relay | Metropolitan networks |
| DI-QKD [6][7] | None (Bell violation only) | χ(B:E) bounded by CHSH value S | Proof-of-principle |
| Satellite decoy-state QKD [8][9] | Satellite + ground terminals | Downlink decoy BB84; entanglement distribution at 1,203 km, S = 2.37 ± 0.09 | 1,200 km, kHz key rates |

Empirically, the field's progress is measured in orders of magnitude: from the 11% one-way QBER threshold [2], to decoy-state key rates viable past 100 km [4], to a satellite link outperforming equivalent fiber by ~10²⁰ [8]. Each step relaxed either a technological requirement (true single photons), a trust requirement (detectors, then all devices), or a distance requirement (fiber loss).

## 6 Limitations

QKD's guarantees are only as strong as their assumptions, and the remaining gaps are significant:

- **Authenticated classical channel.** QKD does not bootstrap trust from nothing; authentication requires a short pre-shared secret. Without it, Eve can mount a man-in-the-middle attack on the classical discussion.
- **Source characterization.** MDI-QKD trusts the sources; Trojan-horse attacks, in which Eve injects bright light and analyzes reflections from Alice's modulators, show that "trusted" must mean carefully characterized and optically isolated.
- **Finite-key effects.** Real deployments exchange finite blocks; statistical fluctuations in parameter estimation substantially reduce the key rate below asymptotic formulas, and composable finite-key proofs remain an active research area.
- **DI-QKD practicality.** The detection efficiencies and Bell-violation margins required for positive key rates confine DI-QKD to heroic laboratory demonstrations for the foreseeable future.
- **Trusted nodes and distance.** Without quantum repeaters, long-haul QKD networks (fiber or satellite) rely on trusted intermediate nodes that hold the key in the clear — a single point of compromise. Satellite QKD additionally requires clear night skies and precise tracking.
- **Side channels beyond the model.** Any degree of freedom not modeled — timing, spectrum, spatial mode — is a potential side channel. Implementation security is a never-ending audit, not a one-time proof.

## 7 Conclusion

Four decades after Bennett and Brassard, quantum key distribution stands as the most mature application of quantum information science: its core protocol enjoys a complete information-theoretic security proof [2]; the decoy-state method made it practical with off-the-shelf lasers [3][4]; measurement-device independence removed the most exploited implementation loophole [5]; device independence showed that even untrusted hardware can be certified by Bell nonlocality alone [6][7]; and satellites carried it to intercontinental distances [8][9]. The through-line is a progressive *weakening of trust* — from trusted devices, to trusted sources, to no trusted devices — paid for in key rate and experimental difficulty.

The open frontier is the quantum internet: quantum repeaters with entanglement swapping and purification that would extend DI-grade security to global distances without trusted nodes; tighter finite-key bounds; and integrated photonic platforms that shrink today's rack-scale systems onto chips. If those arrive, the vision implicit in the 1984 paper — key exchange whose security rests on physics rather than conjecture — will have become planetary infrastructure.
