# Malliavin Calculus: Wiener Chaos Expansions, the Integration-by-Parts Formula, and Density Estimates

## Abstract

Malliavin calculus is an infinite-dimensional differential calculus on Gaussian probability spaces that turns the *integration-by-parts formula* into a machine for proving absolute continuity, regularity, and quantitative bounds for the laws of random variables. This article develops the theory from first principles: isonormal Gaussian processes and the **Wiener chaos decomposition** via Hermite polynomials and multiple Wiener–Itô integrals; the **Malliavin derivative** *D* and its Sobolev spaces; the **divergence operator** *δ* (the Skorohod integral) as the adjoint of *D*; and the **Ornstein–Uhlenbeck generator** *L* with its spectral diagonalization on chaos. We prove the integration-by-parts identity and derive its consequences: explicit density representation formulas, the **Nourdin–Viens** exact density formula avoiding the divergence operator, criteria for nondegeneracy through the Malliavin covariance matrix, Malliavin's probabilistic proof of **Hörmander's hypoellipticity theorem**, and Stein–Malliavin quantitative bounds including the fourth-moment theorem on Wiener chaos. The exposition is self-contained at the graduate level, with complete statements, sharp hypotheses, and pointers to the literature.

##1 Introduction

Classical analysis proves the smoothness of a probability density by differentiating it — but what if the random variable is defined implicitly, as the solution of a stochastic differential equation, or as a supremum of a Gaussian process? Paul Malliavin's 1978 insight was that one can differentiate the *random variable itself* with respect to the underlying noise, producing an infinite-dimensional integration-by-parts formula on Wiener space that plays the role of differential calculus for laws [1]. This *stochastic calculus of variations* bypasses the density entirely: instead of studying *p(x)* directly, one studies the Malliavin derivative *DF*, an *H*-valued random variable measuring the sensitivity of *F* to perturbations of the noise in each direction.

The program rests on three pillars. **First**, the *Wiener chaos expansion* decomposes every square-integrable functional of the noise into an orthogonal series of multiple stochastic integrals, the Gaussian analogue of a Fourier series, with Hermite polynomials as its building blocks. **Second**, the operators *D* (derivative), *δ* (divergence, adjoint of *D*, extending the Itô integral to anticipating integrands), and *L* (the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck generator) satisfy a clean algebraic structure — the commutation relations, the Clark–Ocone representation, and Mehler's formula — that makes computations tractable. **Third**, the *integration-by-parts formula*

> **Theorem (Bismut integration by parts).** *Let F be nondegenerate and smooth in the Malliavin sense. Then its law admits a smooth density p, and for every multi-index α there exist Malliavin-smooth weights H_α such that ∂^α p(x) = E[1_{F > x} H_α].*

This single mechanism yields the existence and regularity of densities for hypoelliptic diffusions [7], explicit two-sided Gaussian density bounds for stochastic partial differential equations [4], and — fused with Stein's method — quantitative central limit theorems on Wiener chaos with optimal rates [3].

This article proceeds as follows. §2 fixes the Gaussian framework and the Wiener chaos expansion. §3 constructs the three fundamental operators and their duality. §4 is the deep dive: chaos-level spectral calculus, the Clark–Ocone formula, Sobolev spaces, and the chain rule. §5 proves the integration-by-parts formula, the Nourdin–Viens density formula, Hörmander's theorem, and the Stein–Malliavin bounds. §6 discusses limitations: nondegeneracy requirements, the Gaussian restriction, and computational cost. §7 concludes with open directions.

##2 Background

### The isonormal Gaussian process

Fix a real separable Hilbert space *H*. An **isonormal Gaussian process** over *H* is a centered Gaussian family *W = {W(h) : h ∈ H}* on a probability space *(Ω, F, P)* with covariance *E[W(h) W(g)] = ⟨h, g⟩_H*. The canonical example takes *H = L²(the unit interval, dt)* and *W(𝟙_{(0,t]}) = B_t*, a standard Brownian motion; we henceforth work with this Brownian model where convenient, identifying *H* with *L²(the unit interval)*. Every statement below is invariant under the choice of *H*, and this abstraction is what lets the theory apply simultaneously to Brownian motion, fractional Brownian motion, and spatially homogeneous Gaussian noises [4].

### Hermite polynomials and multiple integrals

The one-dimensional Hermite polynomials *H_n*, defined by *H_n(x) = (−1)^n e^{x²/2} (d^n/dx^n) e^{−x²/2}*, form an orthogonal basis of *L²(ℝ, γ)* for the standard Gaussian measure *γ*. For *f ∈ H^{⊙n}* (the symmetric tensor power), the **multiple Wiener–Itô integral** *I_n(f)* extends the iterated Itô integral to non-adapted symmetric kernels and satisfies the fundamental **isometry**

*E[I_n(f) I_m(g)] = n! ⟨f̃, g̃⟩_{H^{⊗n}} 1_{n=m}*,

where *f̃* is the symmetrization. The closed linear span *H_n = {I_n(f) : f ∈ H^{⊙n}}* is the **n-th Wiener chaos**, and one has the orthogonal **chaos decomposition**

> **Theorem (Wiener chaos expansion).** *Every F ∈ L²(Ω) admits a unique expansion F = Σ_{n=0}^∞ I_n(f_n) with f_n ∈ H^{⊙n}, and ‖F‖²_{L²} = Σ_{n=0}^∞ n! ‖f_n‖².*

This is the Gaussian analogue of the Fourier series: a complete orthonormal expansion in which differentiation will act diagonally, exactly as the Fourier transform diagonalizes constant-coefficient differential operators [1].

### Why differentiation on Wiener space is hard

Naively differentiating a functional of Brownian paths fails because Brownian paths are nowhere differentiable and the Cameron–Martin directions form a null set. Malliavin's resolution: differentiate only along the reproducing directions *h ∈ H*, i.e., perturb the noise by *εh* and differentiate in *ε*. This yields a *weak* (Sobolev) derivative that is well-defined on a dense class of smooth cylindrical functionals and closable in *L^p* — the entire calculus is then built by closure, mirroring how Sobolev spaces extend classical derivatives [1][2].

##3 Methodology

Our methodology is operator-theoretic. Rather than differentiating densities, we construct three densely defined operators on *L²(Ω)* and exploit their algebraic relations:

1. **The Malliavin derivative** *D : D^{1,2} ⊂ L²(Ω) → L²(Ω; H)*, defined on cylindrical *F = f(W(h_1), …, W(h_n))* by *DF = Σ_{i=1}^n ∂_i f(W(h_1), …, W(h_n)) h_i*, then closed in the graph norm.
2. **The divergence** *δ : Dom(δ) ⊂ L²(Ω; H) → L²(Ω)*, the adjoint of *D*: *E[δ(u) F] = E[⟨DF, u⟩_H]* for *F ∈ D^{1,2}*. On adapted *u*, *δ(u) = ∫_0^T u_t dW_t* is the Itô integral; on anticipating *u* it is the **Skorohod integral**.
3. **The Ornstein–Uhlenbeck generator** *L = −δD*, self-adjoint, diagonal on chaos: *L I_n(f_n) = −n I_n(f_n)*, with semigroup *T_t F = Σ_{n=0}^∞ e^{−nt} I_n(f_n)* (Mehler's formula).

The integration-by-parts identity *E[φ′(F) G] = E[φ(F) H]* for suitable *G, H* is obtained by writing *φ′(F) = ⟨Dφ(F), (DF)/‖DF‖²⟩* via the chain rule and moving *D* across the inner product onto the weight using duality with *δ*. Nondegeneracy — invertibility of the **Malliavin covariance matrix** *γ_F = ⟨DF, DF⟩_H* — is the exact hypothesis that makes division by *‖DF‖²* legitimate. Every density result in §5 is a corollary of this single mechanism, differing only in the choice of weight process [1][8].

##4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Spectral calculus on Wiener chaos

The chaos expansion turns every Malliavin operator into a multiplier on the chaos coefficients, which is why the theory is computable. For *F = Σ_{n=0}^∞ I_n(f_n)*:

- **Derivative:** *D_t I_n(f) = n I_{n−1}(f(·, t))*, so *DF = Σ_{n=1}^∞ n I_{n−1}(f_n)*.
- **Ornstein–Uhlenbeck:** *L F = −Σ_{n=0}^∞ n I_n(f_n)*, and its pseudo-inverse on centered variables is *L^{−1} F = −Σ_{n=1}^∞ n^{−1} I_n(f_n)*.
- **Semigroup:** *T_t F = Σ_{n=0}^∞ e^{−nt} I_n(f_n)*, a contraction semigroup with generator *L*.

The **multiplication formula** *I_n(f) I_m(g) = Σ_{r=0}^{n∧m} r! C(n,r) C(m,r) I_{n+m−2r}(f ⊗_r g)* (with *⊗_r* the *r*-contraction) is the workhorse behind moment computations: it reduces products of chaos variables to chaos expansions, and it is what makes the fourth-moment criterion of §5 provable by elementary contraction estimates [3].

```python
# Chaos coefficient bookkeeping: L^2 norm and OU action on a truncated expansion
import math
def chaos_norm(coeffs):
    """coeffs[n] = kernel f_n; returns sum n! ||f_n||^2."""
    return sum(math.factorial(n) * (fn ** 2).sum() for n, fn in enumerate(coeffs))

def ou_semigroup(coeffs, t):
    """Apply T_t: multiply chaos-n coefficient by exp(-n t)."""
    return [math.exp(-n * t) * fn for n, fn in enumerate(coeffs)]

def pseudo_inverse(coeffs):
    """L^{-1} on centered F: divide chaos-n coefficient by -n."""
    out = [0.0]  # constant (zeroth-chaos) component maps to zero
    out += [fn / (-n) for n, fn in enumerate(coeffs[1:], start=1)]
    return out
```

```haskell
-- Hermite polynomials via the probabilists' recurrence H_{n+1} = x H_n - n H_{n-1}
hermite :: [Double] -> Int -> Double
hermite _ 0 = 1.0
hermite (x:_) 1 = x
hermite xs@(x:_) n = x * hermite xs (n-1) - fromIntegral (n-1) * hermite xs (n-2)

-- Chaos projection weights: the n-th chaos carries L^2 mass n! * ||f_n||^2
chaosMass :: Int -> Double -> Double
chaosMass n kernelNormSq = fromIntegral (product [1..n]) * kernelNormSq
```

### 4.2 The Malliavin derivative and Sobolev spaces

For *p ≥ 1* and *k ≥ 1*, the **Sobolev space** *D^{k,p}* is the closure of smooth cylindrical functionals under

*‖F‖^p_{k,p} = E|F|^p + Σ_{j=1}^k E‖D^j F‖^p_{H^{⊗j}}*.

The operator *D* satisfies the familiar rules of differential calculus in this weak sense:

- **Chain rule:** if *φ* is *C¹* with bounded derivative and *F ∈ D^{1,p}*, then *φ(F) ∈ D^{1,p}* and *Dφ(F) = φ′(F) DF* [1].
- **Product rule:** *D(FG) = F DG + G DF* for *F, G ∈ D^{1,2} ∩ L^∞*.
- **Commutation:** *D_t δ(u) = u_t + δ(D_t u)*, the infinite-dimensional analogue of *∂_i(x_j ·) = δ_{ij} + x_j ∂_i*.

Two canonical computations anchor the intuition. If *F = W(h)* is a single Gaussian, *DF = h* is deterministic. If *F = ∫_0^T u_t dW_t* is an Itô integral, then *D_s F = u_s + ∫_s^T D_s u_t dW_t*: the derivative sees both the integrand's direct dependence and its anticipative sensitivity. The **Clark–Ocone formula** inverts this:

> **Theorem (Clark–Ocone).** *For F ∈ D^{1,2}, F = E[F] + ∫_0^T E[D_t F | F_t] dW_t.*

Every square-integrable functional is thus an Itô integral of the predictable projection of its Malliavin derivative — the martingale representation theorem made explicit and computable [2][8].

### 4.3 The divergence as Skorohod integral

The adjoint relation *E[δ(u) F] = E[⟨DF, u⟩_H]* defines *δ* on *Dom(δ) = {u : |E[⟨DF, u⟩]| ≤ c_u ‖F‖_2}*. For adapted *u ∈ L²(the unit interval × Ω)*, *δ(u)* coincides with the Itô integral; for anticipating *u*, *δ* extends integration beyond the adapted world, which is precisely what the integration-by-parts weights require (they are never adapted). The **Skorohod isometry** *E[δ(u)²] = E‖u‖²_H + E‖Du‖²_{H^{⊗2}}* quantifies the price of anticipation: the extra term *E‖Du‖²* vanishes exactly for adapted integrands. Anticipative calculus — stochastic differential equations with initial conditions depending on the whole path, insider-trading models in finance — is built on this extension [8].

### 4.4 The Ornstein–Uhlenbeck structure

The generator *L = −δD* is the infinite-dimensional Laplacian associated with the Gaussian measure, and *T_t* is the associated heat flow. **Mehler's formula** gives a probabilistic representation: *(T_t F)(ω) = E[F(e^{−t} ω + √(1 − e^{−2t}) ω′)]*, averaging *F* over an independent copy *ω′* — a noise interpolation that contracts toward equilibrium. Hypercontractivity of *T_t* (Nelson's theorem: *‖T_t F‖_q ≤ ‖F‖_p* for *e^{2t} ≥ (q−1)/(p−1)*) implies the **equivalence of all L^p norms on a fixed Wiener chaos**: *‖F‖_q ≤ C(n, p, q) ‖F‖_p* for *F ∈ H_n*. This innocent-looking estimate is load-bearing: it converts the *L²*-computable fourth moment *E[F⁴]* into control of tails, and it is the engine behind the density bounds of §5 [1][3].

| Operator | Domain | Action on chaos *I_n* | Probabilistic meaning |
|---|---|---|---|
| *D* (derivative) | *D^{1,2} → L²(Ω; H)* | *n I_{n−1}(f(·, t))* | Sensitivity to noise perturbation |
| *δ* (divergence) | *Dom(δ) → L²(Ω)* | *(n+1) I_{n+1}(f̃)* | Adjoint; Skorohod integral |
| *L* (OU generator) | *Dom(L) → L²(Ω)* | *−n I_n(f_n)* | Infinite-dim. Laplacian |
| *T_t* (OU semigroup) | *L²(Ω) → L²(Ω)* | *e^{−nt} I_n(f_n)* | Mehler noise interpolation |
| *L^{−1}* (pseudo-inverse) | centered *L² → L²* | *−n^{−1} I_n(f_n)* | Stein kernel ingredient |

##5 Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 The integration-by-parts formula and existence of densities

Let *F ∈ D^∞* (smooth in the Malliavin sense: *F ∈ ∩_{k,p} D^{k,p}*). Its **Malliavin covariance matrix** is *γ_F = ⟨DF, DF⟩_H* (a scalar for real-valued *F*). Call *F* **nondegenerate** if *γ_F > 0* a.s. and *γ_F^{−1} ∈ ∩_{p≥1} L^p*.

> **Theorem (Integration by parts [1]).** *Let F be smooth and nondegenerate. For every multi-index α and every test function φ, there exists a Malliavin-smooth weight H_α(F) such that E[∂^α φ(F)] = E[φ(F) H_α(F)]. The weights are constructed recursively by H_{(i)}(F) = δ(γ_F^{−1} DF) and H_{α} = H_{(α_k)}(composed).*

*Proof sketch.* For *|α| = 1*, the chain rule gives *∂_i φ(F) = ⟨Dφ(F), γ_F^{−1} D F^i⟩* (summing over components in the vector case). Duality with *δ* moves the derivative off *φ(F)*: *E[⟨Dφ(F), u⟩] = E[φ(F) δ(u)]* with *u = γ_F^{−1} DF^i*. Iteration gives higher orders; nondegeneracy guarantees *γ_F^{−1}* has all moments, so the weights stay integrable. ∎

Taking *φ* approximating *1_{(x,∞)}* yields the **density representation** *p(x) = E[1_{F > x} H(F)]*, and differentiating under the expectation gives *∂^α p(x) = E[1_{F > x} H_α(F)]*: the law of *F* has a *C^∞* density, with derivatives bounded by moments of explicit Malliavin weights. No PDE for *p* was ever written down — smoothness emerges purely from the calculus on the underlying noise [1][6].

### 5.2 The Nourdin–Viens exact density formula

A drawback of the classical representation is the divergence operator in the weights, which is difficult to estimate. Nourdin and Viens discovered a formula that eliminates *δ* entirely [4]. For centered *Z ∈ D^{1,2}*, define the **Stein kernel**

*g(Z) = ⟨DZ, −DL^{−1}Z⟩_H ≥ 0*.

Then *E[Z φ(Z)] = E[g(Z) φ′(Z)]* for nice *φ* (Stein's identity on Wiener space), and if *g(Z) > 0* a.s.:

> **Theorem (Nourdin–Viens density formula [4]).** *Under g(Z) > 0 a.s., Z admits a density ρ given exactly by ρ(x) = E[ 1_{Z > x} · δ(−DL^{−1}Z) / g(Z) ].*

Because *L^{−1}* and *D* are both chaos multipliers, *g(Z)* is often explicitly computable — for *Z* in a fixed chaos, *g(Z)* is a polynomial in *Z* — yielding **two-sided density bounds** by estimating *g(Z)* from above and below. Applied to the maximum of a Gaussian process, this produces sharp tail estimates that improve the classical Borell–Sudakov inequality in concrete regimes [4]. For SPDEs with additive noise, the same machinery yields **optimal Gaussian bounds** *c₁ t^{−1/2} exp(−C₁x²/t) ≤ ρ_t(x) ≤ c₂ t^{−1/2} exp(−C₂x²/t)* for the solution's density [4].

```rust
// Stein kernel g(Z) = <DZ, -D L^{-1} Z>: chaos-level computation
// For Z = I_q(f) in the q-th chaos: -D L^{-1} Z = (1/q) DZ, so g(Z) = ||DZ||^2 / q.
fn stein_kernel_chaos(q: usize, d_zt_sq: f64) -> f64 {
    d_zt_sq / (q as f64)
}

fn density_proxy_via_nourdin_viens(
    indicator: f64,      // 1_{Z > x}
    div_term: f64,       // delta(-D L^{-1} Z)
    g: f64,              // Stein kernel, must be > 0
) -> Option<f64> {
    if g > 0.0 { Some(indicator * div_term / g) } else { None }
}
```

### 5.3 Hörmander's hypoellipticity theorem via Malliavin

Consider the Stratonovich SDE *dX_t = V_0(X_t) dt + Σ_{i=1}^m V_i(X_t) ∘ dW^i_t* on ℝ^d. Hörmander's condition asks that the vector fields *V_1, …, V_m* together with all their iterated Lie brackets span ℝ^d at the starting point (the *parabolic* version includes *V_0* in brackets). The PDE proof is notoriously technical; Malliavin's probabilistic proof is conceptually transparent [7]:

1. *X_t* is Malliavin-smooth, and its derivative *D_s X_t = J_{s→t} V(X_s)* is the linearized flow applied to the diffusion coefficients.
2. The Malliavin covariance *γ_t = ∫_0^t (J_{s→t} V)(J_{s→t} V)^⊤ ds* is the controllability Gramian of the linearized system.
3. Hörmander's bracket condition implies a **Norris-type lemma**: if a semimartingale and its quadratic variation are both small, the integrand is small — propagating smallness of *γ_t* down through iterated brackets until it contradicts the spanning condition.
4. Hence *det γ_t^{−1}* has all moments: *X_t* is nondegenerate, and §5.1 gives a *C^∞* density.

This is the crown-jewel application: a deep PDE regularity theorem reduced to nondegeneracy of an explicit random matrix [1][7].

### 5.4 Stein–Malliavin bounds and the fourth-moment theorem

Fusing Malliavin calculus with Stein's method yields quantitative central limit theorems. For centered *F ∈ D^{1,2}* with *E[F²] = 1* and *N ∼ N(0,1)*:

> **Theorem (Stein–Malliavin bound [3]).** *d_{TV}(F, N) ≤ 2 E| 1 − ⟨DF, −DL^{−1}F⟩_H |.*

The right-hand side is exactly *E|1 − g(F)|*: closeness of the Stein kernel to 1 controls closeness to Gaussianity. On a fixed Wiener chaos this collapses to a moment condition — the celebrated **fourth-moment theorem** of Nualart and Peccati [3]:

> **Theorem.** *Let F_n ∈ H_q (q ≥ 2) with E[F_n²] = 1. Then F_n converges in law to N(0,1) if and only if E[F_n⁴] → 3.*

Sufficiency follows from the multiplication formula: *E|1 − q^{−1}‖DF_n‖²|²* expands into contraction norms that vanish exactly when the fourth cumulant *E[F_n⁴] − 3* vanishes, via hypercontractivity. Quantitative versions give Kolmogorov bounds such as *d_{Kol}(F, Z) ≤ √(E[F⁴] − 3) / (√(3q))* up to constants [3]. The theory extends to Gamma and Pearson targets and to multivariate settings, forming the "Stein–Malliavin" program [3].

##6 Limitations

The theory's power comes with sharp boundaries, and honest application requires respecting them.

1. **Nondegeneracy is essential and hard to verify.** Every density result above assumes *γ_F* invertible with inverse moments. For SDEs this is Hörmander's condition — checkable via Lie brackets — but for general functionals (suprema, SPDE solutions, rough-path lifts) proving *(det γ_F)^{−1} ∈ L^p* is often the entire difficulty of a paper [4][6].
2. **Gaussian restriction.** The chaos expansion, Mehler's formula, and hypercontractivity all exploit Gaussian structure. Extensions to Poisson space exist (Nualart–Vives; see [2], Chapters 9–11) with a *difference* operator replacing *D*, but the clean spectral picture partially breaks: the Poisson–OU generator is not diagonalizable by a simple multiplier rule, and Stein kernels become less explicit.
3. **Smoothness requirements vs. irregular coefficients.** The chain rule needs *C¹* test functions and the basic calculus needs Lipschitz coefficients. SDEs with merely bounded measurable drift (the regime of [5]-style applications to irregular drifts) require localized or approximated Malliavin calculus with delicate truncation arguments.
4. **Computational cost.** Malliavin weights like *δ(γ_F^{−1} DF)* involve iterated Skorohod integrals; Monte Carlo evaluation of density representations *p(x) = E[1_{F>x} H]* suffers from the variance of *H*, which can be enormous in degenerate directions. The weights are theoretically explicit but numerically unfriendly without variance reduction [6].
5. **Infinite-dimensional technicalities.** Closability, domains of *δ*, and the distinction between *D^{k,p}* spaces demand genuine functional analysis; formal manipulation of *D* and *δ* without domain checks produces false identities (e.g., applying duality to *u ∉ Dom(δ)*).

##7 Conclusion

Malliavin calculus converts the analytic question "is the law of *F* smooth?" into the probabilistic question "is *F* nondegenerate in the directions of the noise?" — and answers the latter with a computable operator algebra. From the Wiener chaos expansion's diagonalization of *D*, *δ*, and *L*, through the integration-by-parts formula, to the Nourdin–Viens exact density representation, Hörmander's hypoellipticity theorem, and the Stein–Malliavin fourth-moment phenomenon, a single mechanism — differentiate the variable, dualize, divide by the covariance — produces the deepest regularity results in stochastic analysis. Open frontiers include sharp density estimates for rough differential equations driven by fractional noise, Malliavin calculus for singular SPDEs where renormalization meets nondegeneracy, and making Malliavin weights numerically viable for high-dimensional density estimation. The calculus of variations on Wiener space, nearly fifty years on, remains the sharpest tool for seeing the invisible: the fine structure of laws that have no closed form.
