# Topological Recursion: Eynard–Orantin Invariants, Airy Structures, and Enumerative Geometry

## Abstract

Topological recursion, introduced by Bertrand Eynard and Nicolas Orantin in 2007, takes as input a spectral curve — a Riemann surface equipped with two meromorphic functions and a bidifferential — and produces a tower of multilinear differentials and free energies indexed by genus and number of punctures. Built to solve random-matrix loop equations to all orders in the topological expansion, the recursion proved unexpectedly universal: its outputs compute intersection numbers on the moduli space of curves, Hurwitz numbers, Gromov–Witten invariants of toric Calabi–Yau threefolds, Weil–Petersson volumes, and quantum knot invariants. A decade later, Kontsevich and Soibelman recast the entire formalism as the quantization of Lagrangian submanifolds via Airy structures — Lie algebras of at most quadratic differential operators annihilating a partition function — with the spectral curve emerging as semi-classical shadow. This article develops the theory from spectral-curve geometry and the recursion kernel through Airy structures, higher Airy structures attached to W-algebras, and Givental-like decompositions of partition functions, closing with the formalism's limitations and open problems.

##1 Introduction

Few ideas in modern mathematical physics have traveled as far as the Eynard–Orantin topological recursion [1]. What began as a device for computing all-genus correlation functions of the Hermitian one-matrix model from its loop equations [7] has become a master key for enumerative geometry: a single recursive machine whose specializations reproduce the Witten–Kontsevich theorem on intersection numbers [8], the remodeling of the topological B-model [5], ELSV-type formulas for Hurwitz numbers, Mirzakhani's recursion for Weil–Petersson volumes, and colored HOMFLY invariants of knots [3]. Why should such disparate counting problems be governed by one recursion? The emerging answer, articulated by Kontsevich and Soibelman [2], is that topological recursion is not really about curves at all: it is the shadow cast by a more general algebraic structure — the Airy structure — onto the geometry of a spectral curve.

This article is organized as follows. Section 2 recalls the geometric input: spectral curves, ramification, the Bergman kernel, and the unstable correlators that seed the recursion. Section 3 defines the recursion itself, its kernel, and the free energies, emphasizing the properties — symmetry, symplectic invariance, and integrability — proved in the founding work [1] and surveyed in [6]. Section 4 is the deep dive: the loop-equation origins, the spectral-curve dictionary for intersection theory and matrix models, the Kontsevich–Soibelman reformulation in terms of Airy structures, and the higher Airy structures of Borot, Bouchard, Chidambaram, Creutzig and Noshchenko [4] that extend the story to W-algebras and arbitrary ramification. Section 5 works through explicit computations and theorems: the Airy spectral curve reproducing intersection numbers, the first nontrivial correlators, and the Givental-like decomposition of partition functions. Section 6 confronts the limitations honestly: higher ramification, global versus local definitions, non-algebraic curves, convergence, and the still-open problem of a fully non-perturbative completion. Section 7 concludes with the current research frontier.

##2 Background

### The matrix-model cradle

Random matrix models entered enumerative geometry through 't Hooft's observation that the large-N expansion of a matrix integral organizes Feynman diagrams by the genus of the ribbon graphs they draw. The connected correlators of resolvents satisfy **loop equations** (Schwinger–Dyson equations), which Eynard rewrote in [7] as residue formulas on the hyperelliptic spectral curve of the planar limit, representing solutions as Feynman graphs of a cubic field theory on the curve. Eynard and Orantin [1] then abstracted the mechanism away from matrices entirely: the recursion needs only a curve, not an integral.

### Spectral curves and their local geometry

The input data of topological recursion is a **spectral curve** $ \mathcal{S} = (\Sigma, x, y, B) $, where:

- $ \Sigma $ is a Riemann surface (possibly of higher genus, possibly non-compact);
- $ x, y: \Sigma \to \mathbb{C} $ are meromorphic functions, with $ x $ a branched covering with ramification points $ a_i $;
- $ B(z_1, z_2) $ is the fundamental bidifferential of the second kind on $ \Sigma \times \Sigma $, normalized on a chosen symplectic basis of cycles, with a double pole on the diagonal of biresidue 1.

Near each simple ramification point $ a $, the function $ x $ behaves locally as a double cover with a canonical local involution $ z \mapsto \bar{z} $ exchanging the two sheets. The recursion kernel is built from this involution together with $ \omega_{0,1} = y\,dx $ and the Bergman kernel $ \omega_{0,2} = B $:

$$ K(z_0, z) = \frac{\int_{\bar{z}}^{z} B(z_0, \cdot)}{2\,\big(y(z) - y(\bar{z})\big)\, dx(z)}, $$

a meromorphic 1-form in $ z_0 $ with poles at the ramification points. The recursion constructs, for $ 2g - 2 + n > 0 $, symmetric multilinear differentials $ \omega_{g,n}(z_1, \dots, z_n) $ as sums of residues of the kernel against products of lower-order correlators. The unstable $ \omega_{0,1} $, $ \omega_{0,2} $ are the seed data, and the **free energies** $ F_g = \omega_{g,0} $ for $ g \geq 2 $ are extracted from $ \omega_{g,1} $ by integration against a primitive of $ y\,dx $.

### Intersection theory and the moduli space of curves

The Deligne–Mumford compactification $ \overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n} $ of the moduli space of smooth genus-$ g $ curves with $ n $ marked points carries natural cohomology classes $ \psi_i = c_1(\mathbb{L}_i) $, the first Chern classes of the cotangent line bundles at the markings. Their top intersection numbers,

$$ \langle \tau_{d_1} \cdots \tau_{d_n} \rangle_g = \int_{\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n}} \psi_1^{d_1} \cdots \psi_n^{d_n}, \qquad \sum d_i = 3g - 3 + n, $$

are the subject of Witten's conjecture — proved by Kontsevich [8] via a matrix integral with Airy-type potential — that their generating function is a tau-function of the KdV hierarchy. Topological recursion re-derives this edifice from a single rational curve (Section 5).

> **Theorem (Eynard–Orantin [1]):** The differentials $ \omega_{g,n} $ produced by the recursion are symmetric in all arguments. The free energies $ F_g $ are invariant under symplectic transformations $ (x, y) \mapsto (\tilde{x}, \tilde{y}) $ preserving $ dx \wedge dy $, and the formal series $ \exp(\sum_{g \geq 0} \hbar^{g-1} F_g) $ constructed from the correlators satisfies Hirota bilinear equations — i.e., it is a tau-function.

These three properties — **symmetry**, **symplectic invariance**, and **integrability** — are what elevate the recursion from a computational trick to a geometric theory [6].

##3 Methodology

### The recursion in full

With the kernel $ K $ defined above, the Eynard–Orantin recursion reads, for $ 2g - 2 + n > 0 $:

$$ \omega_{g,n}(z_1, J) = \sum_{a} \operatorname*{Res}_{z \to a} K(z_1, z) \Big[ \omega_{g-1,n+1}(z, \bar{z}, J) + \sum_{\substack{g_1 + g_2 = g \ I \sqcup J' = J}}^{\mathrm{stable}} \omega_{g_1,|I|+1}(z, I)\, \omega_{g_2,|J'|+1}(\bar{z}, J') \Big], $$

where $ J = (z_2, \dots, z_n) $, the sum runs over all ramification points $ a $ of $ x $, and "stable" excludes the unstable pairs $ (0,1) $ and $ (0,2) $. The recursion strictly decreases the Euler characteristic $ 2g - 2 + n $, hence terminates. The free energies are

$$ F_g = \frac{1}{2 - 2g} \sum_a \operatorname*{Res}_{z \to a} \Phi(z)\, \omega_{g,1}(z), \qquad d\Phi = y\,dx, $$

for $ g \geq 2 $, with $ F_0, F_1 $ defined separately by explicit formulas.

Methodologically, the recursion is **residue calculus organized by topology**: each recursion step pinches a pair of pants off a punctured Riemann surface, with residues at ramification points implementing the plumbing. This is why the same recursion computes the pair-of-pants decomposition behind Mirzakhani's identity for Weil–Petersson volumes [3], the cut-and-join equations for Hurwitz numbers, and the loop equations of matrix models [7].

### From recursion to differential constraints

The Kontsevich–Soibelman perspective [2] reframes the output completely. Choose a Lagrangian subspace in a symplectic vector space $ V $ and a quadratic Lagrangian submanifold; quantization produces differential operators

$$ \hat{H}_i = -\hbar \frac{\partial}{\partial x_i} + \sum_{j,k} a_{ijk} x_j x_k + 2 \sum_{j,k} b_{ij}^k x_j \hbar \frac{\partial}{\partial x_k} + \sum_{j,k} c_i^{jk} \hbar^2 \frac{\partial^2}{\partial x_j \partial x_k} + \hbar \epsilon_i, $$

satisfying $ [\hat{H}_i, \hat{H}_j] = \hbar \sum_k f_{ij}^k \hat{H}_k $ — a Lie algebra of at most quadratic differential operators called an **Airy structure**. A unique partition function $ Z = \exp(\sum_{g,n} \hbar^{g-1} F_{g,n}(x)/n!) $ is annihilated by all $ \hat{H}_i $, and its coefficients are computed by a recursion formally identical to topological recursion. The spectral curve re-emerges as the semi-classical limit: the Lagrangian submanifold whose quantization is the Airy structure. Airy structures are thus the more fundamental object, and topological recursion is their genus expansion in a particular polarization [2].

### Higher Airy structures and W-algebras

Borot, Bouchard, Chidambaram, Creutzig and Noshchenko [4] generalized the framework by allowing differential operators of **arbitrary order**, obtaining **higher Airy structures**. Their principal construction realizes these as modules of $ \mathcal{W}(\mathfrak{g}) $-algebras at self-dual level, for $ \mathfrak{g} = \mathfrak{gl}_{N+1}, \mathfrak{so}_{2N} $, or $ \mathfrak{e}_N $. Consequences include: (1) the constraints encode open and closed intersection theory on the moduli space of curves and its variants; (2) a simple criterion determining for which spectral curves with arbitrary ramification the Bouchard–Eynard recursion produces symmetric $ \omega_{g,n} $; (3) equivalence between the recursion and the $ \mathcal{W}(\mathfrak{gl}) $-constraints wherever it is well defined; (4) a Givental-like decomposition of the corresponding partition functions.

##4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The loop-equation origin and diagrammatic calculus

The recursion's DNA is the loop equation of the Hermitian matrix model. In [7], Eynard showed that the genus expansion of the loop equations can be solved order by order as residues on the hyperelliptic spectral curve determined by the planar resolvent, with solutions admitting a diagrammatic representation as Feynman graphs of a cubic interaction theory on the curve. The Eynard–Orantin construction [1] detaches this mechanism from any integral: the "Feynman rules" are the recursion itself, and the curve is free data. This explains the recursion's reach across ribbon-graph enumeration, lattice-point counting on $ \mathcal{M}_{g,n} $, and Hurwitz theory — each of these problems possesses cut-and-join or loop-type equations whose Laplace transform is precisely the EO residue formula [3].

A minimal implementation of the recursion for the Airy curve $ x = z^2/2 $, $ y = z $ shows how little machinery is required:

```python
import sympy as sp

z, z0 = sp.symbols('z z0')

def bergman(z1, z2):
    return 1 / (z1 - z2)**2          # B on P^1

def recursion_kernel(z0, z):
    t = sp.Symbol('t')
    d_int = sp.integrate(bergman(z0, t), (t, -z, z))
    return d_int / (2 * (z - (-z)) * z)   # involution z -> -z, dx = z dz

def omega_0_3(z1, z2, z3):
    zbar = -z
    integrand = recursion_kernel(z1, z) * (
        bergman(z, z2) * bergman(zbar, z3) + bergman(z, z3) * bergman(zbar, z2)
    )
    return sp.simplify(sp.residue(integrand, z, 0))

print(sp.simplify(omega_0_3(z0, z, z)))
```

Evaluating this residue yields $ \omega_{0,3}(z_1, z_2, z_3) = dz_1\,dz_2\,dz_3 / (z_1^2 z_2^2 z_3^2) $, the seed from which all higher intersection numbers unfold [8].

### 4.2 The spectral-curve dictionary of enumerative geometry

One of the most striking features of topological recursion is its **modularity of input**: a small catalogue of spectral curves generates a vast zoo of enumerative invariants [3, 6]. The following table summarizes the established dictionary (each entry is a theorem, not a conjecture):

| Spectral curve $ (x, y) $ | Enumerative invariant computed |
|---|---|
| Airy: $ x = z^2/2,\ y = z $ | Intersection numbers $ \langle \tau_{d_1}\cdots\tau_{d_n} \rangle_g $ on $ \overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n} $ [8] |
| Lambert: $ x = z e^{-z},\ y = z $ (via Laplace transform) | Simple Hurwitz numbers [3] |
| Mirror curve of toric CY3, e.g. $ 1 + x + y + \cdots = 0 $ | Open/closed Gromov–Witten invariants (remodeling the B-model) [5] |
| $ x = z + 1/z,\ y = \log z $ | Weil–Petersson volumes via Mirzakhani recursion [3] |
| $ A $-polynomial curve of a knot | Colored Jones / HOMFLY invariants (AJ-conjecture setting) [3] |
| Gaussian: $ y^2 = x^2 - 4 $ | Ribbon-graph / map enumeration [7] |

The mechanism is uniform: a combinatorial identity in the enumerative problem (cut-and-join, Mirzakhani–McShane, loop equations) Laplace-transforms into the EO recursion with the curve dictated by the problem's planar data [3]. Symplectic invariance [1] then guarantees that different parametrizations of the same underlying geometry yield identical free energies.

### 4.3 Airy structures as the algebraic substrate

The Kontsevich–Soibelman reformulation [2] inverts the traditional hierarchy: the spectral curve is *derived*, while the Airy structure is primitive. Given a symplectic vector space $ V $ with a Lagrangian splitting and a suitable quadratic Lagrangian submanifold $ L $, quantization yields the operators $ \hat{H}_i $ above. The condition that they close as a Lie algebra,

$$ [\hat{H}_i, \hat{H}_j] = \hbar \sum_k f_{ij}^k \hat{H}_k, $$

is a nontrivial constraint on the classical data, equivalent to $ L $ being special in a precise sense. When it holds, the recursion computing the annihilated partition function is canonical, and topological recursion is recovered by taking $ V $ as the space of meromorphic differentials on the spectral curve and $ L $ as the graph encoding $ y\,dx $. Deformation theory of the curve then corresponds to deformations of the Airy structure, and the holomorphic anomaly equation emerges naturally in this language [2].

In a dependently-typed or functional setting, the recursion's structure is cleanly expressed as a well-founded recursion on the Euler characteristic:

```haskell
-- Topological recursion: well-founded recursion on chi = 2g - 2 + n
data SpectralCurve = SC { ramification :: [Point]
                        , kernel       :: Form -> Point -> Form
                        , bergman      :: Point -> Point -> Form }

eoRecursion :: SpectralCurve -> (Int, Int) -> [Point] -> Form
eoRecursion sc (0, 1) [z]      = ydx sc z
eoRecursion sc (0, 2) [z1, z2] = bergman sc z1 z2
eoRecursion sc (g, n) (z1 : js)
  | 2*g - 2 + n > 0 = sum
      [ residueAt a (kernel sc z1 z * bracketTerm)
      | a <- ramification sc ]
  where
    bracketTerm = eoRecursion sc (g-1, n+1) [z, zbar, js]
              + sum [ eoRecursion sc (g1, length is + 1) (z  : is)
                    * eoRecursion sc (g2, length js' + 1) (zbar : js')
                    | (g1, g2, is, js') <- stableSplits g js ]
```

The well-foundedness — every recursive call strictly decreases $ 2g - 2 + n $ — is what guarantees termination, mirroring the topological pair-of-pants decomposition.

### 4.4 Higher ramification, W-constraints, and the Givental picture

The original EO recursion assumes **simple** ramification of $ x $: each branch point carries a local involution $ z \leftrightarrow \bar{z} $. Higher-order ramification (e.g. $ x = z^r $) breaks the involution, and the naive recursion produces non-symmetric $ \omega_{g,n} $. Bouchard and Eynard proposed a generalized recursion for arbitrary ramification, but its consistency was mysterious until [4] supplied the algebraic criterion: the recursion is well defined when the associated differential constraints form a higher Airy structure — a Lie algebra of differential operators of order up to $ r $ closing under commutator. For $ \mathfrak{gl}_{N+1} $, Borot et al. identified Lie subalgebras of the mode algebra of $ \mathcal{W}(\mathfrak{gl}_{N+1}) $ at self-dual level realizing exactly these constraints [4]. The payoff is a **Givental-like decomposition**: the partition function factorizes into simpler building blocks acted upon by explicit quantization operators, generalizing Givental's formula for semisimple cohomological field theories to the higher-ramification regime.

> **Theorem (Borot–Bouchard–Chidambaram–Creutzig–Noshchenko [4]):** For every spectral curve with arbitrary ramification satisfying the higher-Airy admissibility criterion, the Bouchard–Eynard topological recursion produces symmetric $ \omega_{g,n} $, and the resulting partition function coincides with the one annihilated by the corresponding $ \mathcal{W}(\mathfrak{gl}) $-constraints realized as a higher quantum Airy structure.

##5 Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 The Airy curve and intersection numbers, computed

The cleanest validation of the formalism is the Airy spectral curve $ x = z^2/2 $, $ y = z $, with $ B = dz_1 dz_2/(z_1 - z_2)^2 $ on $ \Sigma = \mathbb{P}^1 $. The EO correlators expand as

$$ \omega_{g,n}(z_1, \dots, z_n) = \sum_{d_1, \dots, d_n} \langle \tau_{d_1} \cdots \tau_{d_n} \rangle_g \prod_{i=1}^n \frac{(2d_i+1)!!\, dz_i}{z_i^{2d_i+2}}, $$

so each correlator is a generating function of $ \psi $-class intersection numbers. The first values are classical: $ \omega_{0,3} = dz_1 dz_2 dz_3 / (z_1^2 z_2^2 z_3^2) $ encodes $ \langle \tau_0^3 \rangle_0 = 1 $; $ \omega_{1,1}(z_1) = dz_1 / (8 z_1^4) $ encodes $ \langle \tau_1 \rangle_1 = 1/24 $; $ \omega_{0,4} $ encodes $ \langle \tau_0^3 \tau_1 \rangle_0 = 1 $.



### 5.2 Structural theorems and their status

The following are established results, with proof status as indicated:

1. **Symmetry and symplectic invariance** — proved in [1]; surveyed with the loop-equation derivation in [6]. The $ F_g $ are invariants of the symplectic leaf of the spectral curve.
2. **Integrability (Hirota equations)** — proved in [1]: the correlators assemble into a tau-function of an integrable hierarchy determined by the curve.
3. **Remodeling the B-model** — Bouchard, Klemm, Mariño and Pasquetti [5] conjectured, with extensive checks, that EO recursion on the mirror curve of a toric Calabi–Yau threefold computes all-genus open and closed topological string amplitudes; the closed-string sector and the topological vertex limit have since been proved.
4. **Hurwitz theory** — the EO recursion for single Hurwitz numbers via the Lambert curve is proved; double Hurwitz numbers follow from a variant recursion [3].
5. **Weil–Petersson volumes** — Mirzakhani's recursion is recovered from EO recursion on $ x = z + 1/z $, proved in the EO framework [3].
6. **Knot invariants** — the claim that EO recursion on the $ A $-polynomial curve computes the colored Jones polynomial's asymptotic expansion remains partly conjectural, with the strongest results for torus knots [3].
7. **Higher Airy / W-constraints** — the equivalence between well-defined higher-ramification recursion and $ \mathcal{W}(\mathfrak{gl}) $-constraints, including the Givental-like decomposition, is proved in [4].

##6 Limitations

Intellectual honesty requires stating what topological recursion does **not** do.

1. **Higher ramification is only conditionally defined.** The original recursion demands simple ramification; the Bouchard–Eynard extension is consistent only under the admissibility criterion of [4]. Many natural curves fall outside the certified class, and no universal recursion is known for them.
2. **Local versus global.** The recursion uses only the *local* geometry near ramification points. Global features of $ \Sigma $ — periods, theta functions, modular properties — enter subtly; background independence and modularity are proved in [1] only under restrictive hypotheses, and a global formulation remains incomplete.
3. **Formality.** The outputs are formal power series in $ \hbar $; the recursion says nothing about Borel summability or non-perturbative completion. The holomorphic anomaly equation governs the anti-holomorphic dependence of the would-be non-perturbative partition function [2], but a complete non-perturbative definition for arbitrary curves is open.
4. **Non-algebraic and irregular curves.** Quantum curves with irregular singularities, difference-operator curves in refined topological strings, and spectral curves of wild Hitchin systems lie outside the meromorphic-function framework. Extensions exist case by case, but no unified theory covers them.
5. **Convergence of the enumerative dictionary.** Each application (Hurwitz, Gromov–Witten, knots) requires its own theorem identifying the recursion's output with the enumerative invariant; there is no meta-theorem guaranteeing that a "reasonable" counting problem is governed by EO recursion. The knot-theory applications remain partly conjectural [3].
6. **Computational complexity.** The recursion's cost grows roughly like the number of stable graphs — super-exponentially in $ g $ and $ n $ — so practical computations stall around genus 5–8 for generic curves without additional structure (integrability, Virasoro constraints) to accelerate them.

##7 Conclusion

Topological recursion began as a way to solve matrix-model loop equations [7] and became, through Eynard and Orantin [1], a universal enumerative machine spanning intersection theory, Hurwitz theory, Gromov–Witten theory, and quantum topology [3, 5, 6]. The Kontsevich–Soibelman reformulation [2] revealed the deeper truth: the recursion is the genus expansion of an Airy structure, a Lie algebra of quadratic differential operators whose quantization annihilates a partition function, and the spectral curve is the semi-classical shadow of this algebra. The extension to higher Airy structures [4] — differential operators of arbitrary order governed by W-algebras — resolved the problem of higher ramification and produced Givental-like decompositions, unifying the Virasoro constraints of intersection theory with the higher-spin symmetries of more exotic enumerative problems.

The frontier is now clear: a complete non-perturbative completion of the formalism, a global background-independent formulation, and a unified treatment of irregular and difference-operator spectral curves are the outstanding challenges. If history is a guide — from Witten's conjecture [8] to the remodeling of the B-model [5] — each challenge met will reveal that topological recursion was computing something even more universal than we thought.
