# Cost-Based Query Optimization with the Cascades Framework: Memo Groups, Transformation and Implementation Rules, Histogram-Based Cardinality Estimation, and Dynamic Programming Join Ordering

## Abstract

Cost-based query optimization is the mechanism by which declarative relational queries are translated into efficient execution plans. This thesis examines the Cascades framework, Goetz Graefe's 1995 rule-based extensible optimization architecture, as the unifying foundation beneath SQL Server, Greenplum's Orca, and modern extensible optimizers. We develop its four central pillars in depth: the memo as a compact representation of the entire plan search space via equivalence groups; transformation and implementation rules as programmable search-space exploration, including enforcer rules for physical properties; histogram-based cardinality estimation, where equi-depth histograms and their refinements supply the selectivities that dynamic programming join ordering depends upon; and the cost model that makes these components commensurable. We prove the memo's deduplication invariant, formalize rule application as monotone closure over equivalence classes, derive selectivity estimators from first principles, and analyze the DPsize/DPsub join-ordering algorithms. Empirical evidence from the Join Order Benchmark is surveyed to quantify where optimizer errors actually concentrate. We close by identifying Cascades' open problems: correlated predicates, interesting-order explosion, and the emerging role of learned cardinality estimators.

---

## 1. Introduction

The relational data model derives its power from a deliberate separation: users state *what* data they want, and the system decides *how* to compute it. Between declarative SQL and the physical reality of disk pages and CPU caches sits the query optimizer — arguably the most consequential software in any database system. A single query may admit an astronomically large space of logically equivalent plans: different join orders, join algorithms (nested-loop, hash, merge), access paths (sequential scan, index scan), and placements of aggregation and sorting. For a query joining ten relations, the number of distinct bushy join trees alone exceeds 17 billion. The optimizer must navigate this space using only metadata — statistics, schema, and a cost model — because the data cannot be examined at optimization time.

The modern history of this problem begins with System R's access-path selection [2], which introduced dynamic programming over relation subsets and *interesting orders*: physical properties of intermediate results, such as sort order, that make a locally suboptimal subplan globally optimal. The Volcano optimizer generator [3] unified logical rewriting and physical plan generation into one rule-driven search. Cascades, introduced by Goetz Graefe in 1995 [1], resolved Volcano's remaining deficiencies and became the dominant industrial design: it underlies Microsoft SQL Server's optimizer, was reimplemented as the standalone Orca optimizer [7], and its core ideas — the memo, transformation and implementation rules, enforcers, top-down branch-and-bound search — appear in nearly every industrial optimizer built since.

This thesis treats Cascades and its supporting machinery in depth. Section 2 surveys the background (System R, EXODUS, Volcano). Section 3 presents the methodology: the top-down driver, the memo, and the rule framework. Section 4 is the deep dive: memo groups and invariants, the rule taxonomy, histogram-based cardinality estimation, and DP join ordering. Section 5 surveys empirical evidence on optimizer accuracy via the Join Order Benchmark [4]. Section 6 discusses limitations; Section 7 concludes with open directions.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 System R and the Birth of Cost-Based Optimization

Selinger et al.'s 1979 paper [2] remains the most influential contribution to query optimization:

1. **Dynamic programming over relation subsets.** Optimal plans for subsets of size 1 are built first, then size 2, and so on; for each subset *S* and each *r ∈ S*, the optimal plan for *S ∖ {r}* is joined with *r*. This yields optimal *left-deep* trees.
2. **Interesting orders.** A subplan that is not cheapest for its subset may be retained if it produces an interesting order — e.g., sorted on a join key — reducing the cost of a later merge join or grouping step.
3. **Cost model and statistics.** Plan costs were estimated from cardinalities, tuple widths, page counts, and predicate selectivities under simple uniformity assumptions.

System R's optimizer was monolithic: enumeration, rewrites, and physical operators were hard-coded, so adding a new join algorithm required invasive surgery.

### 2.2 EXODUS and Volcano: The Rule-Based Turn

The EXODUS optimizer generator introduced *transformation rules* — pattern-directed rewrites such as join commutativity — applied to a search-space *model*. Volcano [3] unified logical-to-logical transformations and logical-to-physical *implementation* rules in one framework, added *promises* to guide rule application, and introduced the exchange operator for parallelism. But Volcano had deficiencies Cascades would address [1]:

- Rules could not easily manipulate *operator arguments* (e.g., predicate expressions), only operator structure.
- There was no clean mechanism for *enforcers*: operators like Sort whose sole purpose is to establish a required physical property.
- Search guidance was coarse, and the framework offered no path toward parallel search, partially ordered cost metrics, or dynamic plans.

### 2.3 The Cascades Innovation

Cascades restructures search around a **memo** — a compact, deduplicated representation of the entire explored plan space — and a **top-down, demand-driven** driver. The optimizer copies the query into the memo, applies rules to grow it, then extracts the cheapest plan per group under each required physical property. The memo makes the search space itself *data* that rules read and write, rather than control flow a search loop walks. This yields extensibility (new capabilities are new rules), efficiency (duplicates are never stored twice), and a clean separation between search *strategy* and the search *space* [1].

## 3. Methodology

### 3.1 The Top-Down Driver

Cascades optimization is driven by a scheduler over *tasks*:

- **OptimizeGroup(G, requiredProps):** find the cheapest plan for group *G* delivering required physical properties (e.g., sorted on column *x*).
- **OptimizeExpr(E, G, requiredProps):** optimize a particular group expression *E* in *G*.
- **ApplyRule(R, E):** apply transformation or implementation rule *R* to *E*, inserting results into the memo.
- **ExploreGroup(G):** apply transformation rules to generate logically equivalent forms before costing.

Optimization starts with `OptimizeGroup(root, {})` and proceeds depth-first and demand-driven: costing a parent demands optimal costs of its input groups, recursively triggering their optimization. *Branch and bound* prunes aggressively — once a plan for a group under given properties is known with cost *C*, any partial exploration whose lower bound already exceeds *C* is abandoned.

### 3.2 Rules as the Sole Extension Mechanism

Every optimizer capability is a rule, with a **pattern** (operator tree shape with variables), a **guard** (predicate on the binding), a **substitute** (function building new expressions), and a **promise** (priority guiding firing order).

> **Theorem (Rule closure):** Let *M₀* be the initial memo. Let *M_{i+1} = M_i ∪ { substitutes of all applicable rules on M_i }*. The sequence is monotone and, for a finite rule set over a finite operator algebra, reaches a fixpoint *M** — the complete explored search space.

Rule application only *adds* expressions, so exploration is monotone toward the fixpoint while branch-and-bound costing prunes concurrently.

### 3.3 The Cost Model

Costs need only be totally ordered and *composable*: an expression's cost is a function of its operator's local cost plus its input groups' optimal costs. A typical instantiation sums I/O and CPU components:

```sql
-- Conceptual cost formula per operator instance
-- cost(op) = local_cost(op) + Σ cost(best plan of input Gi)
-- local_cost(HashJoin) ≈ build_cost(|outer|) + probe_cost(|inner|)
-- local_cost(IndexScan) ≈ index_pages * random_page_cost + tuples * cpu_tuple_cost
```

The cost model consumes *cardinalities*, estimated per group (Section 4.3) — the group, not the expression, is the unit of estimation, since all logically equivalent expressions in a group produce the same tuples by definition.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Memo: Groups, Group Expressions, and the Deduplication Invariant

Three definitions must be kept rigorously distinct [1]:

- An **expression** is one operator whose inputs are *group references*: `Join(G2, G3)` is one expression.
- A **group** is an equivalence class: all expressions — logical and physical — producing the same logical result.
- A **group expression** is a member of a group: one concrete operator instantiation with group inputs.

Because inputs are group references, a memo with few groups encodes exponentially many plans:

```
memo:  G1 = { Join(G2,G3), Join(G3,G2), HashJoin(G2,G3), MergeJoin(G2,G3) }
        G2 = { Scan(A), IndexScan(A) }       -- groups = equivalence classes
        G3 = { Scan(B) }                     -- members share one cardinality
```

*G1* stands for 4 × 2 × 1 = 8 complete plans in seven stored nodes. Selinger's DP table keyed by relation subset is the special case where the only equivalences are join reorderings; Cascades groups hold *any* logically equivalent expressions, including rewritten predicates.

![Cascades memo structure with groups, group expressions, and shared cardinalities](ths_1788787762176_a3f9-0.webp)

> **Theorem (Memo deduplication invariant):** No two group expressions in the same group are structurally identical. Each distinct logical expression appears in exactly one group, exactly once.

*Proof sketch.* Insertion is guarded by a hash lookup on the structural signature; duplicates are no-ops. Rules only insert, never delete, so the invariant holds inductively from copy-in. ∎

This invariant absorbs the exponential blowup of naive enumeration into *sharing*, and underwrites costing correctness: the cheapest expression in a group under required properties is the optimal plan for that subproblem — the principle of optimality justifying dynamic programming. Practical implementations add **multi-expressions** (implicit redundant variants) and **group merging** when transformations prove two groups equivalent.

| Memo concept | Stores | Shared across members |
|---|---|---|
| Group | equivalence class of expressions | logical properties, cardinality |
| Group expression | one operator + group inputs | winning plan per property set |
| Logical expression | relational operator (Join, Select, …) | — |
| Physical expression | executable operator (HashJoin, …) | cost under each property |

### 4.2 Transformation Rules, Implementation Rules, and Enforcers

Cascades classifies rules into three families [1]:

**Transformation rules** map logical expressions to equivalent logical expressions, growing the logical space:

- *Join commutativity:* `R ⋈ S → S ⋈ R`
- *Join associativity:* `(R ⋈ S) ⋈ T → R ⋈ (S ⋈ T)`
- *Select pushdown:* `σ_p(R ⋈ S) → σ_p(R) ⋈ S` when *p* references only *R*
- *Predicate manipulation:* rewriting operator *arguments* — a Cascades novelty — e.g., splitting conjunctive predicates or deriving transitive equalities.

**Implementation rules** map logical expressions to executable algorithms: `Join → HashJoin / MergeJoin / NestedLoopJoin`; `Scan(R) → IndexScan(R, idx)` when a usable index exists; `GroupBy → HashAgg / SortAgg`.

**Enforcers** insert *glue operators* establishing required physical properties without changing semantics: `EnforceSort(G, keys) → Sort(G, keys)`; `EnforceDist(G, keys) → Exchange/Repartition(G, keys)`. Enforcers elegantly solve the interesting-orders problem in a rule framework: a rule observes that no expression delivers a required property and inserts a Sort above it, handling physical properties uniformly.

![Transformation rule application: join commutativity rewriting within a memo group](ths_1788787762176_a3f9-1.webp)

```haskell
-- Simplified Cascades rule application
applyRule :: Rule -> GroupExpr -> Memo -> Memo
applyRule rule expr memo =
  case match (pattern rule) expr of
    Nothing      -> memo
    Just binding ->
      if guard rule binding
        then foldr insertExpr memo (substitute rule binding)
        else memo

insertExpr :: GroupExpr -> Memo -> Memo
insertExpr e memo =
  case lookupSignature (signature e) memo of
    Just _  -> memo   -- deduplication invariant
    Nothing -> addToGroup e memo
```

**Rule guidance and stratification.** Blind rule application explodes the memo (associativity alone generates the Catalan numbers). Cascades supports rule *promises* ordering rule firing, and production systems *stratify* search into phases — e.g., SQL Server applies space-shrinking rewrites (predicate pushdown, view matching) before space-growing ones (associativity). Timeouts are measured in *transformation counts*, not wall-clock time, so an overloaded machine produces the same plans as an idle one — a crucial determinism property.

### 4.3 Histogram-Based Cardinality Estimation

Every cost computation bottoms out in a cardinality estimate, the dominant source of optimizer error [4]. The workhorse is the **histogram**: a compressed approximation of an attribute's value distribution, maintained per column by the statistics subsystem.

**Equi-depth histograms** partition the domain into *β* buckets of approximately equal tuple count (*N/β*); dense regions get narrow buckets, sparse regions wide ones. For a range predicate `a ≤ x ≤ b`, selectivity sums fractional bucket overlaps:

> **Definition (Equi-depth range selectivity):** Let bucket *i* span *[lᵢ, uᵢ]* with count *cᵢ*. Then *sel(a ≤ x ≤ b) = (1/N) · Σᵢ cᵢ · overlap([a,b],[lᵢ,uᵢ]) / (uᵢ − lᵢ)*, assuming *uniformity within each bucket* — the central modeling assumption.

The optimizer composes primitive selectivities under *attribute value independence* (AVI) and *join uniformity*:

| Predicate form | Estimator |
|---|---|
| `x = const` | `1 / n_distinct`, or MCV frequency if tracked |
| `x < const` | histogram fractional overlap |
| `x = y` (join) | `1 / max(n_distinct(x), n_distinct(y))` |
| `p AND q` | `sel(p) · sel(q)` (independence) |
| `p OR q` | `sel(p) + sel(q) − sel(p)·sel(q)` |

**Refinements** [5] include *V-optimal histograms* (buckets placed to minimize squared error, not equalize depth), *most-common-value lists* (exact heavy-hitter frequencies), *multi-dimensional statistics* attacking AVI, and *join synopses* estimating join selectivities directly.

The fragility is fundamental: selectivity errors *multiply* through a join tree, compounding exponentially with query complexity. Leis et al. [4] show industrial estimators routinely err by orders of magnitude, and these errors — not the cost model or enumeration — dominate poor plans. This is why the memo's design (one cardinality per *group*, shared by all equivalent expressions) matters: it guarantees *consistency* — two plans for the same subresult are costed from the same estimate, so costing cannot contradict itself even when the estimate is wrong.

### 4.4 Dynamic Programming Join Ordering

Join ordering is the combinatorial core of optimization. In Cascades, enumeration is typically DP over groups corresponding to relation subsets, with the classic results carrying over under sharper modern analysis [6]. **DPsize** enumerates subsets by increasing size, trying all splits *S = S₁ ∪ S₂* and retaining the cheapest plan per interesting property; **DPsub** enumerates connected subgraphs and complements directly. Moerkotte and Neumann [6] showed the two variants diverge sharply across query graph shapes — each dominates on one of chain or clique graphs — and derived **DPccp**, which adapts to the query graph by enumerating only connected complement pairs, handling star queries efficiently.

```haskell
-- DP over subsets with physical properties (Cascades-flavored)
bestPlan :: Subset -> PhysProps -> Plan
bestPlan s props = minimumBy cost
  [ joinWith p1 p2 props
  | (s1, s2) <- splits s, connected s1, connected s2
  , p1 <- bestPlan s1 (propsFor s1)
  , p2 <- bestPlan s2 (propsFor s2) ]
  -- enforcer inserted when no candidate delivers `props` natively
```

Bushy DP without cross products is *O(3ⁿ)* on clique graphs, multiplied by the number of relevant physical properties. In practice, optimizers bound the explosion: left-deep-only trees for large *n*, heuristic ordering beyond thresholds (e.g., PostgreSQL's genetic algorithm), and query simplification removing provably useless orderings [6]. A four-relation example illustrates the lattice: subsets of {A,B,C,D} form 15 non-empty nodes; DPsize fills level 1 (cheapest access path per property), then level 2 (both build orders, all join algorithms), then level 3, then the full set — each node's winner built from already-optimal smaller nodes, exactly the principle of optimality the memo generalizes.

## 5. Empirical Evaluation

Optimizers are judged by plan quality, and the landmark study is Leis et al.'s "How Good Are Query Optimizers, Really?" [4], introducing the **Join Order Benchmark (JOB)**: 113 queries over the real-world IMDB dataset with up to 28 joins, designed so that cardinality estimation — not syntax — is the stressor. By selectively replacing estimator components with true cardinalities, the authors disentangled the contributions of estimation, cost modeling, and enumeration:

1. **Cardinality estimation dominates.** All industrial estimators routinely erred by orders of magnitude; substituting true cardinalities improved plan quality more than any other intervention.
2. **Cost models matter less.** Given reasonable cardinalities, even simple cost models selected good plans.
3. **Exhaustive enumeration helps despite bad estimates.** Dynamic programming over the full space beat heuristic enumeration even with poor estimates — a larger space offers more chances to find a robust plan.

These results validate Cascades' bets: invest in a rich, deduplicated search space and exhaustive-ish enumeration with branch and bound, because the binding constraint is the quality of the numbers fed into costing. They also explain the research surge the paper triggered: *learned cardinality estimators* (autoregressive models, sum-product networks), *dynamic plans* that re-optimize at runtime, and robust optimization preferring estimation-insensitive plans.

Industrial evidence aligns: SQL Server's Cascades-derived optimizer and Orca [7] dedicate major engineering to statistics maintenance (auto-update thresholds, filtered statistics, adaptive sampling) because the search machinery is only as good as its inputs. The recent survey of extensible optimizers in practice [8] confirms rule-based extensibility — the Cascades thesis — remains the dominant industrial paradigm, with statistics quality and cloud-scale plan stability as frontier concerns.

## 6. Limitations

**Correlated predicates and AVI.** The independence assumption is the deepest estimation weakness: real predicates correlate (`city='Seattle'` ∧ `state='WA'`), and single-column histograms cannot capture this. Multi-dimensional statistics help but face the curse of dimensionality.

**Interesting-property explosion.** Each group retains a winning plan per *required property*, and properties compose (sort × partitioning × compression). Pruning dominated properties is heuristic, and pathological queries can still blow up the search; stratification mitigates but does not eliminate this.

**Rule authoring complexity.** Rule interactions are subtle — two rules can ping-pong expressions into the memo without the deduplication invariant and guards — promise tuning is black art, and a buggy guard silently shrinks the search space. Debugging demands the extensive tracing Graefe built into Cascades [1].

**Cost model calibration.** Cost units are workload-dependent: a model tuned for spinning disks misranks plans on NVMe, and cloud optimizers must model network transfer and elastic pricing. The framework's agnosticism to the cost function is flexibility and weakness at once.

**Optimization time vs. quality.** Full transformation with branch and bound is expensive; production systems cap it with transformation-count budgets and fall back to heuristics for very large queries. Plans are thus budget-dependent artifacts, albeit deterministic ones.

**Learned components and guarantees.** Learned estimators promise to break the AVI barrier but forfeit determinism and debuggability: a neural estimator can regress silently on distribution shift, and its errors resist diagnosis compared to a stale histogram. Integrating learning while preserving Cascades' invariants is unresolved.

## 7. Conclusion

Cascades endures because it correctly factored query optimization. The memo factors the *search space* into deduplicated equivalence groups, making exponential plan spaces representable. Rules factor *optimizer capabilities* into independently authorable transformations, making the system extensible without invasive change. Enforcers factor *physical properties* into ordinary operators, unifying interesting orders with the rest of the search. The top-down branch-and-bound driver factors *search strategy* away from *search-space construction*, enabling principled pruning.

Three decades of evidence — SQL Server's production optimizer, Orca's reimplementation [7], the Volcano lineage [3], the modern survey literature [8] — confirm this factorization. Yet success has clarified where the real difficulty lies: not in enumeration, which DP and the memo handle well, but in *knowing the cost of things* — cardinality estimation under correlation, cost calibration across heterogeneous hardware, robustness to inevitable errors. The Join Order Benchmark's verdict [4] stands: estimation errors dominate, cost models matter less, exhaustive search over a rich space is worth the expense.

The frontier lies at Cascades' boundaries rather than its core: learned and adaptive estimators that preserve debuggability, dynamic plans that re-optimize when estimates fail, multi-objective cost models for cloud economics, and principled handling of correlated predicates. The memo will likely still be there — deduplicating, sharing, quietly encoding exponentially many plans in a handful of groups — whatever fills those frontiers in.
