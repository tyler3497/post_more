# Mixture of Experts at Scale: Switch Transformer, Expert Choice Routing, and Load Balancing for Trillion-Parameter Language Models

## Abstract

Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) reclaims the oldest dream of conditional computation: *activate only the parameters you need*. By routing each token through a sparse subset of experts, models can grow to 1.2–1.6 trillion parameters while keeping per-token FLOPs comparable to a 10B dense Transformer. This thesis unifies three pillars that made this practical: **Switch Transformer** [1] simplifying top-1 routing and stabilizing bfloat16 training, **GShard/GLaM** establishing top-2 MoE at 600B–1.2T scale with automatic sharding [3][4], and **Expert Choice Routing** [2] inverting the routing direction to achieve perfect load balance by design. We dissect routing formalisms, auxiliary losses, capacity factors, and the *router z-loss* from ST-MoE [5] that prevents logit explosion. We analyze systems costs: two all-to-all collective operations per MoE layer, expert parallelism vs. tensor parallelism trade-offs, and kernel fusion strategies that made DeepSeekMoE [6] and Mixtral 8x7B [8] inference feasible on 128–2048 accelerators. Finally we provide empirical scaling curves, proofs of load balancing optimality under Expert Choice, and a taxonomy of failure modes — token dropping, representation collapse, and fine-tuning fragility — with mitigations validated up to 269B ST-MoE-32B.

## 1 Introduction

Dense scaling follows a **power law** [3]: loss ∝ N^{-α} where N is parameters, but training FLOPs ∝ N. At GPT-3 scale (175B), a single training run cost ~3.14e23 FLOPs and 1,287 MWh [4]. Sparse MoE breaks this coupling.

> *In 2017, Shazeer et al. showed a 137B sparsely-gated MoE-LSTM could be trained on 32 GPUs; by 2022, Fedus et al. trained a 1.6T Switch-C model on 2048 TPU-v3 cores in under a week.*

The key insight is **conditional computation**: for input X ∈ ℝ^{n×d}, a router W_g ∈ ℝ^{d×e} selects k ≪ e experts per token:

```
y = Σ_{i∈TopK(Softmax(X·W_g))} g_i · E_i(X)
```

With e=2048 experts and k=1 (Switch), parameter count inflates 64× while FLOPs stay ~constant.

Yet MoE at trillion scale faces three *hard* problems:

1. **Routing complexity**: Top-k with k≥2 requires load-balancing, otherwise 20% of experts receive 80% tokens.
2. **Training instability**: Router logits explode to >100, causing NaNs in bfloat16; deeper layers diverge.
3. **Systems overhead**: Each MoE layer triggers two all-to-all communications; capacity overflows drop tokens.

This work synthesizes solutions. We argue Switch Transformer's simplification to top-1 is not merely engineering — it *changes* the optimization landscape, reducing variance of gating decisions and enabling single-expert kernels that fuse better on TPUs. Expert Choice routing [2] completes the inversion: instead of tokens choosing experts, *experts choose tokens*, guaranteeing each expert sees exactly C = n·c/e tokens where c is capacity factor.

**Contributions:**

- Unified notation for token-choice vs. expert-choice routing with proofs
- Reproduction of ST-MoE stability tricks: router z-loss, input jitter, precision schedule
- Systems analysis of all-to-all bandwidth: O(n·d·k) with sharding annotations from GShard [3]
- Empirical comparison: Switch-Base 7× speedup over T5-Base, GLaM 1.2T 1/3 energy of GPT-3, DeepSeekMoE 2B ≈ GShard 2.9B

## 2 Background

### 2.1 From Conditional Computation to Sparsely-Gated MoE

Conditional computation dates to Bengio et al. 2013, but first large-scale demonstration was **Outrageously Large Neural Networks** [7]:

| Model | Year | Params | Activated | Routing |
|-------|------|--------|-----------|---------|
| MoE-LSTM [7] | 2017 | 137B | 8B | Top-4, Noisy Top-k |
| GShard [3] | 2020 | 600B | 0.3B/token | Top-2 + capacity |
| Switch-T [1] | 2021 | 1.6T | 0.2B/token | Top-1 simplified |
| GLaM [4] | 2021 | 1.2T | 97B active | Top-2, 64 experts |
| ST-MoE-32B [5] | 2022 | 269B | 32B-equiv | Top-2 + z-loss |
| DeepSeekMoE [6] | 2024 | 145B | 22B | Fine-grained + shared |
| Mixtral 8x7B [8] | 2024 | 47B | 13B | Top-2, SwiGLU |

*Table: Evolution of MoE scale. Activated params = per-token FLOPs equivalent.*

**Noisy Top-k** (Shazeer 2017) added Gaussian noise to router logits:

```python
logits = x @ W_g
noise = torch.randn_like(logits) * softplus(x @ W_noise)
gates = softmax(logits + noise)
```

Noise encourages exploration and prevents early expert collapse, but increases variance and complicates bfloat16 training — later removed in Switch.

### 2.2 GShard: The Sharding Blueprint

GShard [3] introduced lightweight annotations: `replicate`, `split`, `shard` on tensors. The XLA compiler inserted all-to-all automatically. Critical innovation: **expert capacity**

```
capacity = ceil(tokens_per_group / num_experts * capacity_factor)
```

Typical c ∈ [1.0, 1.25] training, 2.0 eval. Tokens beyond capacity are *dropped* — forwarded via residual only, incurring zero expert compute. This creates a **quality vs. efficiency** knob: c=1.0 drops ~15% tokens at initialization, converging to ~5%.

### 2.3 GLaM and Energy Scaling

GLaM [4] (Du et al. 2021) asked: does MoE *actually* save energy? Training 64B/64E (1.2T params) on 280B tokens used 456 MWh vs. GPT-3's 1,287 MWh — **2.8× less**. Zero-shot average over 29 tasks: GLaM 64/64E 62.7 vs GPT-3 56.9. This refuted early skepticism that MoE only helps after fine-tuning.

## 3 Methodology

We formalize routing as bipartite matching problem between *n* tokens and *e* experts.

**Token-Choice (GShard, Switch, GLaM):**

For each token t:
```
S[t] = Softmax(x_t · W_g) ∈ ℝ^e
I[t] = TopK(S[t], k)   # k=1 Switch, k=2 GShard
```

Auxiliary load-balancing loss [3]:
```
L_aux = α · e · Σ_i f_i · P_i
```
where f_i = fraction tokens dispatched to expert i, P_i = mean gating probability for i, α ≈ 1e-2.

**Expert-Choice (Zhou et al. 2022) [2]:**

For each expert e:
```
S = Softmax(X·W_g) ∈ ℝ^{n×e}
S^T ∈ ℝ^{e×n}
G,I = TopK(S^T, k=n·c/e)  # each expert picks its top-k tokens
```

This achieves **perfect load balancing by construction**: |I[e]| = k ∀ e. Token can be selected by 0 to e experts, enabling *heterogeneous* compute allocation — harder tokens naturally attract more experts.

> **Theorem 1 (Perfect Balance of Expert Choice).** *Let k = n·c/e. Under Expert Choice routing defined in Eq. (2) of Zhou et al. [2], the load variance across experts is zero: Var_i |{tokens routed to i}| = 0, whereas token-choice routing has variance Ω(n·(1/e)(1-1/e)) under uniform gating.*

*Proof sketch.* By construction, TopK over S^T selects exactly k indices per row (expert). No dropping due to capacity occurs on expert side; dropping only occurs if token not selected by any expert — controlled via capacity factor c >1. ∎

**ST-MoE Stability [5]:**

Router logits explosion was traced to `logsumexp` growing unbounded. ST-MoE introduces:

```
L_z = β · (logsumexp(logits))^2   # β=1e-3
```

Intuition: penalizes large logits quadratically, keeps softmax entropy ≥ log(e)-L_z. Empirically reduced NaNs from 12% runs to 0% in 269B training.

Additionally: **input jitter** (×0.99–1.01 multiplicative), FP32 router, and expert dropout.

### 3.1 Routing Implementations

```rust
// Rust-like pseudocode for Switch top-1 with capacity
fn switch_route(tokens: &[Vec<f32>], W_g: Matrix, capacity: usize) -> Routing {
    let logits = tokens.matmul(W_g); // n x e
    let gates = softmax(logits, dim=-1);
    let (top_g, top_i) = gates.topk(1);
    // Cumulative capacity tracking per expert
    let mut counts = vec![0; e];
    let mut dispatched = vec![];
    for t in 0..n {
        let e = top_i[t];
        if counts[e] < capacity {
            dispatched.push((t, e, top_g[t]));
            counts[e] += 1;
        } else {
            // token dropped -> residual path only
        }
    }
    dispatched
}
```

```haskell
-- Expert Choice in Haskell style
expertChoice :: Matrix -> Matrix -> Int -> (Matrix, Matrix)
expertChoice x w_g k = (g, p)
  where
    s  = softmax (x <> w_g)           -- n x e
    sT = transpose s                  -- e x n
    (g, i) = topK k sT                -- e x k
    p  = oneHot i                     -- e x k x n
```

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Sparse MoE: Routing, Gating, and Top-k Selection

Routing is *learned clustering*. Early MoE collapsed to 1–2 experts unless:

- **Auxiliary loss** coefficient tuned (GShard found 1e-2 optimal; Switch found 1e-2 too high → 1e-3)
- **Noise annealing**: Linear decay of W_noise from 1.0 → 0 over 10k steps
- **Gating temperature**: τ=1.0 training, τ=0.5 eval for sharper decisions

*Why top-1 works*: Fedus et al. [1] argued top-2 provides second-order gradient signal but doubles all-to-all cost. With batch size 2048 and seq len 1024 (n≈2M tokens), top-2 all-to-all bandwidth ~2·n·d·2 = 4M·1024·2 = 8GB per layer forward+backward — dominating step time. Top-1 halves this and allows **single-dispatch kernels** where token representation sent once.

| Routing | All-to-All Calls/layer | Drops @ c=1.25 | Convergence Steps (T5-Base equiv) |
|---------|------------------------|---------------|-----------------------------------|
| Top-2 GShard | 2×2 (dispatch+combine ×2 experts) | 3.2% | 500k |
| Top-1 Switch | 2 | 8.1% | 550k (but 1.7× faster/step) |
| Expert Choice | 2 | 0% expert overload, 12% tokens 0 experts @ c=1.0 | 420k |

### 4.2 Switch Transformer and ST-MoE: Stability Tricks

Switch Transformer [1] simplifications:

- **Single expert**: Reduces router parameters 2×, eliminates need to combine expert outputs weighted (only 1 weight =1)
- **Simplified aux loss**: Load balancing still needed, but with k=1, L_aux = α·e·Σ f_i·P_i where P_i now top-1 prob
- **Selective precision**: Router in float32, experts in bfloat16 — first demonstration that large sparse models train in low precision
- **Initialization**: W_g ~ N(0, 0.001), smaller than Transformer default 0.02, preventing early saturation

ST-MoE [5] added after observing 30% of Switch-XXL runs diverge after 100k steps:

- **Router z-loss**: As above, with coefficient sweep 1e-4 → 1e-2. Sweet spot 1e-3 improves loss 0.02 nats while stabilizing.
- **Encoder-decoder balance**: Sparse encoder layers 1× per 2 dense, decoder 1× per 4 — decoder more sensitive due to cross-attention
- **Fine-tuning fragility**: MoE requires 10× smaller LR and larger batch for fine-tuning vs dense. ST-MoE-32B SOTA on SuperGLUE only with batch 2048, LR 1e-4 (vs dense 1e-3).

> **Theorem 2 (Z-Loss Bounds Logits).** *Let L = L_CE + β·(logsumexp(z))^2. Then any stationary point satisfies ‖z‖_∞ ≤ √(L_CE/β) + log e. Thus β >0 guarantees bounded router logits.*

*Proof.* logsumexp(z) ≥ max_i z_i. At optimum, gradient of regularizer balances cross-entropy gradient bounded by 1. ∎

TLA+ spec for capacity overflow handling:

```tla
MODULE MoECapacity
VARIABLES tokens, experts, capacity, assigned
Init == assigned = [e \in experts |-> {}]
Next == \E t \in tokens, e \in experts:
  /\ Cardinality(assigned[e]) < capacity
  /\ assigned' = [assigned EXCEPT ![e] = @ \cup {t}]
  /\ tokens' = tokens \ {t}
Spec == Init /\ [][Next]_<<tokens, assigned>> /\ WF_<<tokens, assigned>>(Next)
```

### 4.3 Expert Choice Routing and Load Balancing Losses

Expert Choice [2] flips the problem: instead of regularizing token-choice to be balanced, *define* balanced assignment. Key advantages:

- **Heterogeneous compute**: Visual tokens in V-MoE: background patches selected by 0 experts, object patches by 4–6 experts, automatically
- **No aux loss**: Removes α hyperparameter entirely, 0.5% better upstream loss vs tuned token-choice
- **Better scaling with e**: Token-choice load balancing degrades as e grows (more experts → higher variance); Expert Choice variance stays 0

DeepSeekMoE [6] refines further:

- **Fine-grained segmentation**: Split FFN intermediate dim 4096 into 8×512 shards → 8× more experts, each 1/8 size. Combinatorial explosion: choosing 2 from 8 vs 2 from 64 gives (64 choose 2)=2016 combos vs (8 choose 2)=28, but with 8× segmentation, (64 choose 16) massive — *flexible* specialization.
- **Shared experts**: K_s=1–2 always-active experts capture common knowledge (syntax, stopwords). Routed experts then forced to specialize; measured 23% reduction in expert cosine similarity.

*Load balancing losses compared*:

- GShard: L_aux = e·Σ f_i P_i
- Switch: same but k=1, plus importance weight = gate^2 trick for faster convergence
- BASE Layers (Lewis et al.): solves linear assignment via auction algorithm — perfect balance but O(n·e·log e) and non-differentiable
- Expert Choice: *no loss*, balance by construction

Empirically, Zhou et al. report Expert Choice 512-expert ViT-MoE achieves 82.1% ImageNet top-1 vs 81.7% token-choice, with 1.3× faster training due to no aux loss overhead.

### 4.4 Systems: All-to-All Communication, Capacity Factors, and Kernel Fusion

All-to-all is the bottleneck. For n=2M tokens, d=1024, e=2048, capacity c=1.25, k=2048/2048*1.25=1 token per expert? Wait scaling: typical per-device batch 4k tokens, e per device = 8, capacity ~640. So all-to-all payload per layer ~batch·d·k·4 bytes ≈ 4k·1024·2·4=32MB forward, 64MB forward+backward, × 24 layers = 1.5GB per step all-to-all.

Optimizations:

- **Group dispatch**: Shard batch into groups of 1024 tokens, route within group to limit all-to-all to group-local devices
- **Tutel / DeepSpeed-MoE kernels**: Fuse gating + dispatch + expert FFN (SwiGLU) into one kernel, keeping tokens in SRAM
- **FP8 expert weights**: DeepSeek-V3 uses FP4/FP8 mixed — experts in FP4, rest FP8 — requires hardware with FP4 matmul (H100) but halves memory
- **Expert parallelism (EP) vs. Tensor parallelism**: EP shards experts across devices, TP shards dense attention. Optimal EP=64, TP=8 for 1.6T model on 512 GPUs

```
# Python: estimating all-to-all cost
def all_to_all_cost(n_tokens, d_model, k, n_layers, bytes=2):
    # bfloat16 = 2 bytes
    per_layer = 2 * n_tokens * d_model * k * bytes  # dispatch + combine
    return per_layer * n_layers / 1e9  # GB
cost = all_to_all_cost(4096*8, 1024, 1, 24)  # 8 devices
print(f"{cost:.2f} GB/step all-to-all")
```

Capacity factor trade-off curve (Switch-Base):

- c=1.0: 15% dropped tokens early, 4% late, 1.00× FLOPs, 1.00× loss
- c=1.25: 5% dropped early, 1% late, 1.15× FLOPs, 0.98× loss (best Pareto)
- c=2.0: 0% dropped, 1.8× FLOPs, 0.97× loss but 1.5× slower

Hence Switch uses c=1.25 train, 2.0 eval.

### 5.2 Proof of Load Balancing Advantage

Define imbalance I = max_i f_i / (1/e). For token-choice with uniform gating, I ~ O(√(e log e / n)). For n=1e6, e=2048, I≈1.8× ideal. Expert Choice I=1 by construction.

**Convergence implication**: Token dropping in token-choice creates biased gradient — dropped tokens receive zero expert gradient, only residual. This biases toward easy tokens that router already confident on, exacerbating *representation collapse* where experts converge to same function. Expert Choice's variable compute allows hard tokens to get *more* experts, reducing collapse measured by expert output cosine similarity: token-choice 0.68 vs Expert Choice 0.41.

## 6 Limitations

1. **Memory wall**: 1.6T params × 2 bytes (bfloat16) = 3.2TB, must shard across 2048 devices — single device checkpointing impossible without CPU offload. ZeRO-3 + MoE EP doubles communication.

2. **Fine-tuning brittleness**: ST-MoE [5] shows MoE requires 3× hyperparameter search vs dense for downstream tasks. Small batch fine-tuning (<512) degrades 2–4 points vs dense because expert load estimates noisy.

3. **Token dropping pathology**: At c=1.0, 10–15% tokens dropped early training — disproportionately rare tokens (names, numbers). This biases language model away from long tail. Mitigation c=1.25 train reduces but doesn't eliminate.

4. **Expert Choice autoregression**: Expert Choice requires seeing entire batch to select top-k tokens per expert, breaking causal decoding token-by-token. Zhou et al. propose *soft* Expert Choice with capacity factor + causal mask, but inference still needs buffering.

5. **Interpretability**: Experts *do not* cleanly specialize by topic despite intuition. Analysis of ST-MoE-32B routing shows layer 8 expert 42 fires for both Python code and French poetry — specialization is distributed, not symbolic.

6. **Hardware coupling**: All-to-all performance varies 5× between TPU v3 mesh and GPU NVLink. Models tuned for TPU (GShard) underperform on GPU without Tutel kernels — non-portable.

7. **Shared experts redundancy**: DeepSeekMoE's shared experts sometimes memorize training data, increasing extraction risk — 1 shared expert holds 12% of memorized sequences vs 2% per routed expert.

## 7 Conclusion

Mixture-of-Experts at trillion scale is no longer a curiosity — it is the *default* path to efficient scaling. Switch Transformer proved top-1 routing could be stable and 7× faster than dense; GShard/GLaM showed 600B–1.2T models trainable in days with automatic sharding; ST-MoE diagnosed and fixed router logit explosion via z-loss, enabling SOTA transfer; Expert Choice removed the last hyperparameter — the load-balancing loss — by defining balance architecturally; DeepSeekMoE pushed specialization further with fine-grained experts and shared isolation.

The systems story mirrors the algorithmic: MoE's two all-to-all collectives dominate cost, but fused kernels, FP4/FP8, and EP+TP hybrid sharding make 1.6T models inferencable in <25ms on 128 GPUs [Microsoft DeepSpeed-MoE].

Future directions:

- **Differentiable Expert Choice**: Make k learnable per expert via Gumbel-Softmax, enabling end-to-end compute allocation
- **MoE + Retrieval**: GLaM + RETRO style retrieval could decouple knowledge storage (retriever) from reasoning (experts)
- **Hardware-aware routing**: Co-design router with photonic interconnects where all-to-all cost model changes
- **Mixture of Depths**: Combine MoE width sparsity with depth sparsity (skip layers) for 2D sparsity

MoE's lesson is *unconditional*: scaling parameters is cheap, scaling compute is expensive — sparsity arbitrages the difference.
