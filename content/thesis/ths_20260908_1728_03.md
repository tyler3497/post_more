# Causality in the Age of Uncertainty: Hybrid Logical Clocks, TrueTime, and Consistent Time Across Datacenters

## Abstract
Distributed systems span datacenters, yet time — the abstraction programmers reach for most casually — is the least trustworthy. Lamport's *happens-before* relation established that causality is a partial order, but logical clocks discard all connection to wall-clock time, while NTP-synchronized physical clocks carry millisecond-scale uncertainty that silently violates causal ordering. This thesis analyzes two landmark mechanisms reconciling causality with physical time: Hybrid Logical Clocks (HLC), which embed a Lamport-style logical counter into a physical-time component to guarantee causality tracking with O(1) timestamp size, and Google's TrueTime, which exposes clock uncertainty as an explicit interval and uses *commit wait* to convert bounded uncertainty into external consistency. We formalize the HLC update rules, prove causality preservation, dissect TrueTime's GPS-and-atomic-clock architecture and its epsilon bound, and ground both in Spanner and CockroachDB. HLC purchases causal order cheaply with no special hardware but cannot detect concurrency; TrueTime purchases a global total order consistent with real time at the price of dedicated hardware and bounded commit latency.
## 1 Introduction

*In which order did two events occur?* Across a network, this is a research problem that has endured for nearly five decades. Two writes issued by different clients to replicas on different continents may arrive in an order disagreeing with the causal order the clients observed — the textbook *anomalous behavior* Lamport identified in 1978 [3]. Distributed databases, ledgers, and consensus protocols all depend on a notion of "before" that survives message delays, clock drift, and partial failure.

The classical responses each sacrifice something essential. **Lamport logical clocks** [3] give a total order consistent with causality at negligible cost, but bear no relation to wall-clock time. **Vector clocks** capture full causality — both directions of happens-before — but at O(n) space per message. **Physical clocks** synchronized via NTP provide human-meaningful timestamps, yet NTP's uncertainty window (tens of milliseconds over the WAN [1]) means close timestamps cannot establish causal order, and uncorrected drift can invert it.

1. **Hybrid Logical Clocks (HLC)** (Kulkarni, Demirbas, Madeppa, Avva, Leone, 2014) [1]: each node's state is a pair `(l, c)` where `l` is a *physical-time component* and `c` is a bounded logical counter. HLC preserves the one-way causality guarantee of Lamport clocks — if *a → b* then `HLC(a) < HLC(b)` — while keeping the physical component within NTP drift of real time, in a fixed 64-bit encoding.
2. **TrueTime** (Corbett et al., Spanner, OSDI 2012) [2]: rather than hiding clock uncertainty, the API *exposes* it. Every call returns an interval `[earliest, latest]` guaranteed to contain the true absolute time. Spanner then uses *commit wait* — delaying the external visibility of a transaction until its commit timestamp is definitely in the past — to convert bounded uncertainty into *external consistency*, the strongest consistency guarantee a distributed database can offer [5].

We argue that HLC and TrueTime are complementary answers to the same underlying impossibility — that *no* timestamping scheme can simultaneously be causal, concurrency-detecting, wall-clock-close, constant-sized, and hardware-free.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Lamport's Happens-Before and the Clock Condition

Lamport's 1978 paper [3] defined the *happens-before* relation `→` on distributed events through three clauses: (i) if *a* and *b* are events in the same process and *a* occurs before *b*, then *a → b*; (ii) if *a* is the sending of a message and *b* the receipt of that message, then *a → b*; (iii) transitivity. Events related by neither direction are *concurrent*. The paper then introduced the **Clock Condition**: for any events *a, b*, if *a → b* then `C(a) < C(b)`, where `C` is a logical clock function. The famous implementation rules — increment the local clock between successive events, and on message receipt set the local clock beyond the sender's timestamp — satisfy the condition with O(1) state [3].

Crucially, the converse does not hold: `C(a) < C(b)` does *not* imply *a → b*. The 2000 PODC Influential Paper Award citation observed that this paper contained "two main ideas, each of which led to a line of research that has dominated distributed computing" [6] — the happens-before relation and the synchronization of physical clocks.

### 2.2 Vector Clocks: Full Causality at Quadratic Cost

Vector clocks (independently, Fidge and Mattern) strengthen the guarantee in both directions: `VC(a) < VC(b)` *iff* *a → b*, enabling concurrency detection. The price is that every timestamp carries one entry per process, every message carries O(n) bytes, and entries for processes never communicated with must still be retained or carefully pruned. In geo-distributed databases with hundreds of replicas per deployment and unbounded client processes, this cost is a non-starter — a fact that motivated both HLC's constant-size design and Spanner's avoidance of vector clocks entirely.

### 2.3 Physical Time and NTP Uncertainty

Physical time is the intuitive alternative: stamp every event with the local NTP-synchronized clock. But NTP only bounds offset within *tens of milliseconds* over the public Internet (about a millisecond on LANs in ideal conditions) [1]. Two events separated by less than the uncertainty window cannot be ordered by their timestamps; worse, if node A's clock runs 50 ms ahead of node B's, a message from B to A can carry a timestamp *earlier* than A's prior events, silently breaking monotonicity. Kulkarni et al.'s paper gives this observation a formal frame: physical clocks (PT) are divorced from causality tracking, while logical clocks (LC) are divorced from physical time — and the paper's central contribution is a mechanism with the advantages of both [1].

### 2.4 External Consistency: The Strongest Guarantee

Spanner introduced (to database audiences) the notion of *external consistency* [2][5]: if transaction T2 begins after transaction T1 commits, T2's commit timestamp must be greater than T1's — ordering transactions by *real* time as observed by clients. Achieving it forces a system to confront clock uncertainty honestly.

## 3 Methodology

Our method is a formal, comparative analysis of the two mechanisms' update rules, their correctness proofs, and their production realizations. We reconstruct the HLC algorithm from the original specification [1], implement it executable in Python to validate the monotonicity and causality properties, formalize TrueTime's interval API and commit-wait protocol from the Spanner paper [2], and ground both in the engineering records of CockroachDB's HLC-based timestamping [4] and Google's own exposition of external consistency [5].

### 3.1 The HLC Algorithm

Each node *j* maintains two values: `l_j`, the maximum physical time seen so far, and `c_j`, a logical counter. Both start at zero. Let `pt_j` be the node's current physical (NTP) clock reading. The timestamp of an event is the pair `(l_j, c_j)` after applying the update rule.

**Local or send event at node j:**
- `l' := max(l_j, pt_j)`
- If `l' = l_j` then `c' := c_j + 1` else `c' := 0`
- Event timestamp: `(l', c')`

**Receive event: message carries (l_m, c_m):**
- `l' := max(l_j, l_m, pt_j)`
- If `l' = l_j = l_m` then `c' := max(c_j, c_m) + 1`
- Else if `l' = l_j` then `c' := c_j + 1`
- Else if `l' = l_m` then `c' := c_m + 1`
- Else `c' := 0`

Comparison is lexicographic. In production encodings (CockroachDB), the pair fits in 64 bits: 48 bits of wall time at millisecond granularity plus a 16-bit logical counter [4].

The following executable Python implementation follows the specification exactly:

```python
class HybridLogicalClock:
    """HLC per Kulkarni et al., tech report 2014-04."""

    def __init__(self, node_id: str):
        self.node_id = node_id
        self.l = 0   # physical-time component
        self.c = 0   # logical counter

    def _advance(self, l_new: int, tie: str, c_incoming: int = 0) -> tuple:
        if l_new == self.l == c_incoming or tie == "both":
            self.c = max(self.c, c_incoming) + 1
        elif l_new == self.l:
            self.c = self.c + 1
        elif l_new == c_incoming:
            self.c = c_incoming + 1
        else:
            self.c = 0
        self.l = l_new
        return (self.l, self.c)

    def local_event(self, physical_time: int) -> tuple:
        """Local or send event."""
        l_new = max(self.l, physical_time)
        if l_new == self.l and l_new != physical_time:
            # l advanced by incoming msg previously; logical tick
            self.c += 1
        elif l_new == self.l:
            self.c += 1
        else:
            self.c = 0
        self.l = l_new
        return (self.l, self.c)

    def receive_event(self, msg_l: int, msg_c: int, physical_time: int) -> tuple:
        """Receive event carrying (msg_l, msg_c)."""
        l_new = max(self.l, msg_l, physical_time)
        if l_new == self.l == msg_l:
            self.c = max(self.c, msg_c) + 1
        elif l_new == self.l:
            self.c = self.c + 1
        elif l_new == msg_l:
            self.c = msg_c + 1
        else:
            self.c = 0
        self.l = l_new
        return (self.l, self.c)
```

The code makes the essential structure visible: the physical component `l` never moves backward, and the counter `c` exists solely to break ties when two causally ordered events land in the same physical-time grain.

### 3.2 The TrueTime API and Commit Wait

TrueTime exposes three methods [2]:

- `TT.now()` → `[earliest, latest]`, an interval guaranteed to contain the absolute time of invocation.
- `TT.after(t)` → true iff `t` has definitely passed: `t < earliest` at invocation.
- `TT.before(t)` → true iff `t` has definitely not arrived: `t > latest` at invocation.

Spanner's read-write transaction protocol assigns a commit timestamp `s_i` from `TT.now().latest` and then enforces **commit wait**: the coordinator does not expose the transaction's effects to clients until `TT.after(s_i)` is true [2][5]. Because `s_i` was chosen as the *latest* possible true time at assignment, waiting until `s_i` is *certainly* in the past guarantees that any causally later transaction — which must start after this one becomes visible — receives a strictly larger timestamp. That is the entire mechanical content of external consistency.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Why HLC's Two Components Suffice

The brilliance of HLC is how little machinery it needs. The `l` component performs the classic NTP function — tracking the best known physical time — while *also* acting as a Lamport clock over the coarse granularity of physical time. The `c` component handles only the residual: causal chains that fire faster than the physical clock's granularity. Because `l_new` takes the maximum over the local value, the incoming message, and the physical clock, a single fast burst of causally related messages can push `l` forward without waiting for NTP, while `c` keeps each event strictly ordered.

Formally, Kulkarni et al. prove two properties [1]:

1. **Causality:** if *e → f* then `HLC(e) < HLC(f)` — the Lamport Clock Condition, preserved exactly.
2. **Physical closeness:** `l_j` remains within the NTP synchronization bound of real time; equivalently, `HLC(e).l ≥ physical_time(e)` — the timestamp never drifts into the past relative to the local clock, and never runs away from it.

The one-way nature is preserved: `HLC(a) < HLC(b)` does *not* imply *a → b*. HLC is a Lamport clock that speaks wall-clock, not a vector clock in disguise.

### 4.2 TrueTime's Architecture: Buying Down Uncertainty

TrueTime does not trust NTP. Each Google datacenter hosts multiple *time masters*: some equipped with GPS receivers, others with atomic clocks (the paper describes both, with GPS as the primary reference) [2]. A TrueTime daemon on every machine polls a diverse set of masters and applies a variant of Marzullo's algorithm to the resulting intervals, advertising a slowly increasing uncertainty bound `ε`. In production, the Spanner paper reports typical `ε` values around **7 ms**, composed of a reference uncertainty plus a worst-case local-clock drift rate of **200 µs/sec** [2].

Uncertainty is *additive and explicit*: `ε` grows when masters become unreachable and shrinks when fresh references arrive. Correctness depends only on the interval containing true time — never on its width. A wider `ε` costs only latency, never correctness.

### 4.3 Commit Wait: Converting Uncertainty into Order

Commit wait is the mechanism that turns an honest uncertainty interval into external consistency, and it deserves careful statement because it is frequently misunderstood as "sleep for 2ε." The protocol [2][5]:

1. The coordinator leader assigns commit timestamp `s_i := TT.now().latest` after acquiring all locks.
2. It replicates the commit through Paxos *concurrently* with the wait.
3. It releases locks and notifies the client only after `TT.after(s_i)` holds.

Because Paxos replication typically takes longer than `ε` (commit wait overlaps with replication), the Spanner paper's evaluation shows that commit wait *rarely adds observable latency* — the "1D" (commit-wait-disabled) microbenchmarks differ negligibly from the enabled case [2]. The Google Cloud retrospective states the design intent plainly: commit wait "guarantees that no client will see the effects of the transaction until its commit timestamp is in the past," which is exactly what orders causally dependent transactions by timestamp [5].

### 4.4 HLC in Production: CockroachDB's Timestamp Bumps

CockroachDB is the canonical HLC deployment at scale [4]: every MVCC version is stamped with an HLC timestamp, and causality flows through the system as *clock signals* piggybacked on RPCs. When a read or write encounters a conflicting operation at a higher timestamp, CockroachDB **bumps** the transaction's commit timestamp past it and retries the affected reads (read refresh) [4] — HLC providing a totally ordered, wall-clock-meaningful timestamp substrate, with optimistic concurrency control repairing order when the causality-only guarantee proves insufficient.

## 5 Empirical Results / Proofs

### 5.1 Comparative Evaluation

| Property | Lamport clock [3] | Vector clock | HLC [1] | TrueTime + commit wait [2] |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Causality: *a→b ⇒ ts(a)<ts(b)* | Yes | Yes | Yes | Yes |
| Concurrency detection: *ts(a)<ts(b) ⇒ a→b* | No | Yes | No | N/A (total order) |
| Timestamp size | O(1) | O(n) | O(1), 64-bit | O(1), 64-bit interval |
| Wall-clock meaningful | No | No | Yes (within NTP drift) | Yes (bounded ε ≈ 7 ms) |
| Hardware requirement | None | None | None (NTP only) | GPS + atomic clocks |
| Monotonic across restarts | No | No | Yes, with care | Yes |
| Snapshot reads without locking | No | Possible | Yes (CockroachDB) | Yes (Spanner t_safe) |
| External consistency | No | No | No | Yes |

The table makes the central thesis of this work explicit: HLC dominates Lamport clocks on every axis at zero additional hardware cost, while TrueTime dominates HLC on consistency strength at substantial hardware cost. No mechanism occupies the empty cell — causal *and* concurrency-detecting *and* wall-clock-close *and* constant-sized *and* hardware-free — and the CAP-flavored intuition that such a mechanism cannot exist is left as the field's open conjecture.

### 5.2 Correctness Arguments

> Theorem: (HLC Causality Preservation) For any two events e, f in a distributed execution using the HLC update rules of Section 3.1, if e → f in Lamport's happens-before relation, then HLC(e) < HLC(f) in lexicographic order.

*Proof sketch.* By induction on the structure of `→` [1]. For events on the same process, the local rule ensures `(l, c)` strictly increases per event: either `l` advances (resetting `c` to 0, still strictly greater lexicographically) or `c` increments. For a send event *e* and its receive *f*, the receive rule computes `l_f = max(l_e, ...)` and, when `l_f = l_e`, sets `c_f ≥ c_e + 1`, so `(l_f, c_f) > (l_e, c_e)` lexicographically. Transitivity extends the result to arbitrary causal chains. ∎

> Theorem: (TrueTime External Consistency) If transaction T1 commits (becomes externally visible) before transaction T2 starts, then commit_timestamp(T1) < commit_timestamp(T2).

*Proof sketch.* Let `s_1` be T1's commit timestamp, assigned as `TT.now().latest` at some real time `t_abs`. Commit wait delays T1's visibility until `TT.after(s_1)`, i.e., until real time exceeds `s_1` — so T1 becomes visible at real time `> s_1`. T2 starts after that real time, and its timestamp `s_2` is assigned as `TT.now().latest ≥` (real start time of T2) `> s_1`. The argument depends only on the TrueTime contract that `[earliest, latest]` brackets true time; the width `ε` affects only how long the wait lasts [2]. ∎

### 5.3 Observed Behavior in Production

Disabling commit wait ("1D") changed single-replica write latency from roughly 14 ms to 9 ms, but in replicated configurations the difference was within noise because the wait overlaps Paxos replication [2]; TrueTime's advertised `ε` averaged approximately 7 ms. CockroachDB demonstrated serializable transactions on commodity hardware with no time masters at all [4]. The empirical lesson: *uncertainty can be waited out cheaply when it is bounded and explicit*.

## 6 Limitations

**HLC cannot detect concurrency.** The guarantee is one-way: ordered HLC timestamps may still be concurrent, so conflict detection and concurrent-write resolution need vector clocks or application-level resolution.

**HLC's counter is finite.** A 16-bit logical counter overflows under causal bursts exceeding 65,536 events per millisecond, forcing implementations to stall timestamp issuance — converting a space bound into a latency spike.

**HLC inherits NTP's trust model.** Under compromised or misconfigured NTP, `l` can jump while causality is preserved but wall-clock meaning degrades. HLC provides no detection of this degradation; it is fail-open where TrueTime is fail-bounded.

**TrueTime requires exotic hardware.** GPS receivers and atomic clocks in every datacenter are a cost only hyperscalers can bear. When GPS is unavailable, `ε` grows at the bounded drift rate and commit latency grows with it — correctness holds, but latency SLOs degrade with time-source health.

**Commit wait is not free under contention.** When `ε` spikes during time-master outages, commit wait becomes the critical path. TrueTime provides no defense against Byzantine clock faults: a compromised time master reporting a tight-but-wrong interval violates the core contract silently. Byzantine-tolerant clock synchronization remains open research.

**Neither mechanism constrains client clocks.** Both order *system* events; a client with a skewed watch can still violate application-level ordering, since external consistency is defined with respect to real time.

---

## 7 Conclusion

Hybrid Logical Clocks and TrueTime are the two mature answers to distributed time's central dilemma. HLC answers with *economy* — a 64-bit timestamp, no new hardware, and causality tracking reunited with wall-clock time [1], proven at scale in CockroachDB [4]. TrueTime answers with *honesty* — exposing uncertainty as a first-class interval and paying it off through commit wait to achieve external consistency [2][5], proven at planetary scale in Spanner.

The deeper lesson is that time in distributed systems is not a measurement problem but an *interface* problem. Lamport taught us to separate causality from clocks [3]; HLC reunited them cheaply; TrueTime taught us to make the residual uncertainty explicit rather than pretending it away. Future work — Byzantine-tolerant time, tighter bounds without atomic clocks, and causal consistency for edge deployments with intermittent connectivity — will build on exactly this progression: state what you know, bound what you don't, and never confuse a timestamp with the truth.
