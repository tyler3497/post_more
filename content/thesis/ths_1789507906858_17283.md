# Large Deviations Theory: Cramér's Theorem, the Gärtner–Ellis Theorem, and Freidlin–Wentzell Estimates for Rare-Event Analysis

## Abstract

Large deviations theory quantifies the exponential decay of rare-event probabilities, beyond the reach of the central limit theorem. This thesis develops the three pillars of the classical theory in a unified framework. We begin with **Cramér's theorem** (1938), which gives a large deviation principle for empirical means of independent and identically distributed variables, the rate function being the Legendre–Fenchel transform of the cumulant generating function. We then generalize to the **Gärtner–Ellis theorem**, replacing independence with a differentiable scaled cumulant generating function to cover Markovian and weakly dependent sequences. Finally, we treat **Freidlin–Wentzell theory**, the sample-path large deviation principle for small-noise diffusions, whose action-functional rate function yields exit times and transition rates via the quasipotential. We sketch proofs by exponential tilting, derive closed-form rate functions for Bernoulli, Poisson, Gaussian, and exponential laws, validate the theory numerically with importance sampling showing the rescaled log-probability converging to the Cramér rate, and survey applications to queueing, finance, and chemical reaction networks, closing with the theory's limitations.

## 1 Intro

Rare events dominate risk. A communications buffer overflows, a portfolio loses a tenth of its value in a day, a chemical reactor jumps between metastable operating regimes — each is a *large deviation* from typical behavior, and each carries consequences wildly disproportionate to its probability. The law of large numbers tells us that empirical averages converge to their mean; the central limit theorem describes fluctuations of order one over the square root of the sample size. But neither says anything useful about events whose probability decays *exponentially* in the system size. That gap is the territory of large deviations theory [8].

The founding result, due to Harald Cramér in 1938, concerned the simplest possible setting: the empirical mean of independent and identically distributed random variables. Cramér showed that the probability that the mean exceeds a threshold decays exponentially, and identified the exact exponential rate [2]. Half a century of generalization followed — through the work of Donsker and Varadhan, Gärtner, Ellis, Freidlin, and Wentzell — into a theory of remarkable breadth: a single *large deviation principle* (LDP) now governs settings as diverse as empirical measures (Sanov's theorem), small-noise stochastic differential equations (Freidlin–Wentzell theory), queueing networks, and statistical mechanics [1][6].

This thesis has three objectives. **First**, to give a self-contained exposition of the three central theorems — Cramér, Gärtner–Ellis, and Freidlin–Wentzell — with precise statements and proof sketches. **Second**, to work through concrete computations of rate functions for canonical distributions, demonstrating the Legendre–Fenchel duality that is the theory's computational engine. **Third**, to show how the theory is *used*: in rare-event simulation via importance sampling, where the tilted measure suggested by the theory is provably optimal, and in the analysis of metastability, exit times, and long-horizon asymptotics in applied probability.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The large deviation principle

Let $(\mu_n)$ be a sequence of probability measures on a Polish space $\mathcal{X}$, and let $(a_n)$ be a positive sequence diverging to infinity, the *speed*. We say $(\mu_n)$ satisfies a **large deviation principle** with speed $a_n$ and **rate function** $I: \mathcal{X} \to \mathbb{R}_{\geq 0} \cup \{+\infty\}$ if [1][2]:

- (Lower bound) for every open set $G$,
$$\liminf_{n \to \infty} \frac{1}{a_n} \log \mu_n(G) \geq -\inf_{x \in G} I(x);$$
- (Upper bound) for every closed set $F$,
$$\limsup_{n \to \infty} \frac{1}{a_n} \log \mu_n(F) \leq -\inf_{x \in F} I(x).$$

The rate function is *good* when its sublevel sets are compact. Three elementary facts structure everything that follows [2]:

1. A good rate function of an LDP is **unique**.
2. $\inf_{x} I(x) = 0$, and $I$ vanishes at least somewhere; a unique minimizer implies the corresponding law of large numbers.
3. The LDP is equivalent to the **Laplace principle**: for bounded continuous $h$,
$$\lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{1}{a_n} \log \int e^{a_n h(x)} \, \mu_n(dx) = \sup_{x} \{h(x) - I(x)\}.$$

Intuitively, $\mu_n(A) \asymp \exp(-a_n \inf_{x \in A} I(x))$: probability concentrates exponentially fast near minimizers of $I$, and the *cost* of landing at $x$ is $I(x)$.

### 2.2 Two workhorse tools

> **Theorem: Contraction principle.** If $(X_n)$ satisfies an LDP with good rate function $I$ on $\mathcal{X}$, and $F: \mathcal{X} \to \mathcal{Y}$ is continuous, then $(F(X_n))$ satisfies an LDP on $\mathcal{Y}$ with rate function $J(y) = \inf\{I(x) : F(x) = y\}$ [2][6].

> **Theorem: Varadhan's lemma.** Under mild integrability conditions, $\lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{1}{a_n} \log \mathbb{E}[e^{a_n \Phi(X_n)}] = \sup_{x}\{\Phi(x) - I(x)\}$ for continuous $\Phi$ [2].

The contraction principle is the theory's great labor-saving device: prove one hard LDP (say, for Brownian paths via Schilder's theorem) and inherit LDPs for every continuous image — including, crucially, solutions of differential equations driven by that noise. This is exactly how Freidlin–Wentzell theory is born [4].

### 2.3 Legendre–Fenchel duality

For a function $\Lambda: \mathbb{R}^d \to (-\infty, \infty]$, its **Legendre–Fenchel transform** (convex conjugate) is
$$\Lambda^*(x) = \sup_{\theta \in \mathbb{R}^d} \{\langle \theta, x \rangle - \Lambda(\theta)\}.$$
The transform always produces a convex, lower semicontinuous function, and under standard regularity it is involutive on convex functions [3]. Every rate function in this thesis is, at bottom, a Legendre–Fenchel transform of some cumulant-type object — this duality is what makes the theory *computable* rather than merely existential [1].

## 3 Methodology

The proofs in large deviations share a small set of recurring maneuvers. We summarize the methodology once, then instantiate it three times.

**Upper bounds via exponential Chebyshev.** For a threshold event and any tilt parameter $\theta > 0$,
$$\mathbb{P}(S_n > x) = \mathbb{P}(e^{\theta S_n} > e^{\theta x}) \leq e^{-\theta x} \, \mathbb{E}[e^{\theta S_n}].$$
Optimizing over $\theta$ and taking logarithms yields the upper bound with the Legendre–Fenchel rate function. This is the *Chernoff bound*, and it is essentially sharp [3].

**Lower bounds via exponential tilting (the Cramér transform).** Fix $x$ and choose the tilt $\theta_x$ maximizing $\theta x - \Lambda(\theta)$. Define the tilted law $d\mathbb{P}_{\theta} \propto e^{\theta X} d\mathbb{P}$, under which the mean shifts to $x$. Then
$$\mathbb{P}(S_n \approx x) = Z_{\theta}^n \, e^{-\theta x n} \, \mathbb{P}_{\theta}(S_n \approx x),$$
and under the tilted law the event $\{S_n \approx x\}$ is *typical*, hence of probability decaying slower than exponentially. The lower bound follows [2][6].

**From i.i.d. to dependent: the SCGF.** When independence fails, the cumulant generating function need not factorize. The Gärtner–Ellis methodology replaces it with the **scaled cumulant generating function**
$$\Lambda(\theta) = \lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{1}{n} \log \mathbb{E}[e^{n \langle \theta, A_n \rangle}],$$
whose existence and differentiability substitute for independence [1][7].

**From random variables to paths.** Schilder's theorem gives the LDP for rescaled Brownian paths with rate function one half the squared $L^2$ norm of the derivative. A diffusion $X^\varepsilon$ solving $dX^\varepsilon = b(X^\varepsilon)dt + \sqrt{\varepsilon}\, dB$ is a continuous functional of the driving Brownian path; the contraction principle transfers the LDP, and the resulting rate function is the Freidlin–Wentzell action functional [4].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Cramér's Theorem: Empirical Means of i.i.d. Variables

> **Theorem (Cramér, 1938).** Let $X_1, X_2, \dots$ be i.i.d. real-valued random variables with $\Lambda(\theta) = \log \mathbb{E}[e^{\theta X_1}] < \infty$ for all real $\theta$. Then the empirical means $S_n/n$ satisfy an LDP with speed $n$ and good rate function
$$I(x) = \sup_{\theta \in \mathbb{R}} \{\theta x - \Lambda(\theta)\},$$
the Legendre–Fenchel transform of the cumulant generating function [2][3].

The rate function is convex, nonnegative, vanishes exactly at the mean $\mathbb{E}[X_1]$, and grows to encode the precise exponential price of each deviation. Four canonical computations illustrate the machinery [3]:

| Distribution | Cumulant generating function $\Lambda(\theta)$ | Rate function $I(x)$ |
|---|---|---|
| Bernoulli with success probability $p$ | $\log(1 - p + p e^{\theta})$ | $x \log\frac{x}{p} + (1-x)\log\frac{1-x}{1-p}$ on the unit interval, $+\infty$ elsewhere |
| Poisson with mean $\lambda$ | $\lambda(e^{\theta} - 1)$ | $x - \lambda + x\log\frac{x}{\lambda}$ for $x \geq 0$, $+\infty$ elsewhere |
| Exponential with rate $\lambda$ | $-\log(1 - \theta/\lambda)$ for $\theta < \lambda$ | $\lambda x - 1 - \log(\lambda x)$ for $x \geq 0$, $+\infty$ elsewhere |
| Normal with mean $\mu$, variance $\sigma^2$ | $\mu\theta + \sigma^2\theta^2/2$ | $(x-\mu)^2/(2\sigma^2)$ |

The Bernoulli rate function is the *binary relative entropy*: Cramér's theorem contains, in embryo, the information-theoretic form of all large deviations. The Gaussian case shows a parabolic rate function whose quadratic shape is precisely what the central limit theorem sees — the CLT is the second-order Taylor expansion of the LDP at its minimizer [1].

A compact numerical implementation of the Legendre–Fenchel transform makes the duality concrete:

```python
import numpy as np

def cramer_rate(xs, theta_grid, log_mgf):
    """Legendre-Fenchel transform I(x) = sup_theta {theta*x - Lambda(theta)}."""
    Lam = log_mgf(theta_grid)
    return np.max(theta_grid[None, :] * xs[:, None] - Lam[None, :], axis=1)

theta = np.linspace(-5, 5, 20001)
xs = np.linspace(0.01, 0.99, 9)
log_mgf = lambda t: np.log(1 - 0.3 + 0.3 * np.exp(t))   # Bernoulli(0.3)
for x, Ix in zip(xs, cramer_rate(xs, theta, log_mgf)):
    print(f"x={x:.2f}  I(x)={Ix:.4f}")
```

The same duality expressed functionally, in Haskell, emphasizes that the transform is a *higher-order* operation on log-moment generating functions:

```haskell
-- Legendre-Fenchel transform of a cumulant generating function,
-- evaluated by dense grid search over the tilt parameter.
legendreFenchel :: (Double -> Double) -> [Double] -> Double -> Double
legendreFenchel cgf thetas x =
    maximum [t * x - cgf t | t <- thetas]

-- Bernoulli(0.3): log MGF then rate function at the deviation x = 0.6
bernoulliCGF :: Double -> Double -> Double
bernoulliCGF p t = log (1 - p + p * exp t)

main :: IO ()
main = print (legendreFenchel (bernoulliCGF 0.3) [-5,-4.99..5] 0.6)
```

### 4.2 The Gärtner–Ellis Theorem: The SCGF Machinery

Cramér's theorem demands independence. The **Gärtner–Ellis theorem** (Gärtner 1977, Ellis 1984) asks only for a well-behaved *scaled cumulant generating function* [1][7]:

> **Theorem (Gärtner–Ellis).** Let $(A_n)$ be random vectors in $\mathbb{R}^d$ and define
$$\Lambda(\theta) = \lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{1}{n} \log \mathbb{E}[e^{n \langle \theta, A_n \rangle}], \qquad \theta \in \mathbb{R}^d.$$
If $\Lambda$ is finite and differentiable in a neighborhood of the origin, then $(A_n)$ satisfies an LDP with speed $n$ and good convex rate function $I = \Lambda^*$, the Legendre–Fenchel transform of $\Lambda$ [1][7].

Two subtleties deserve emphasis. First, the *upper bound* holds in broad generality (it is essentially Chernoff's argument plus exponential tightness). Second, the *lower bound* in full force requires the machinery of **exposed points**: points $x$ at which some hyperplane touches the epigraph of $I$ only at $x$. The lower bound is guaranteed on the set of exposed points; when $\Lambda$ is differentiable everywhere in the interior of its effective domain, every point with finite rate is exposed and the full LDP follows [7]. This is why differentiability, not mere existence, of the SCGF is the operative hypothesis.

The theorem's reach is vast. It recovers Cramér as the special case $\Lambda(\theta) = \log \mathbb{E}[e^{\theta X}]$. It implies **Sanov's theorem** for empirical measures of i.i.d. variables, where the rate function is the relative entropy $H(\nu \mid \mu)$ — the infinite-dimensional analogue of the Bernoulli computation above [1]. It applies to Markov additive processes, where $\Lambda(\theta)$ emerges as the logarithm of the Perron–Frobenius eigenvalue of a tilted transition kernel, and to weakly dependent sequences arising in statistical mechanics and finance; for instance, large-time asymptotics of affine stochastic volatility models (the Heston model) are proved by verifying Gärtner–Ellis hypotheses on the rescaled log-price cumulant generating function [7][9].

In Rust, the SCGF-based rate computation is a straightforward convex optimization — here for the Bernoulli case, with the tilt found by Newton's method on the stationarity condition $\Lambda'(\theta) = x$:

```rust
/// Cumulant generating function and derivatives for Bernoulli(p).
fn lambda(t: f64, p: f64) -> f64 { (1.0 - p + p * t.exp()).ln() }

fn main() {
    let (p, x) = (0.3_f64, 0.6_f64);
    // Newton solve lambda'(t) = x, where lambda'(t) = p e^t / (1 - p + p e^t)
    let mut t = 0.0;
    for _ in 0..50 {
        let e = t.exp();
        let d = 1.0 - p + p * e;
        let dl = p * e / d;                    // first derivative
        let d2 = p * e * (1.0 - p) / (d * d);   // second derivative
        t -= (dl - x) / d2;
    }
    let rate = t * x - lambda(t, p);
    println!("tilt t* = {:.6}, I(x) = {:.6}", t, rate);
}
```

### 4.3 Freidlin–Wentzell Theory: Sample-Path Large Deviations for Diffusions

We now pass from random variables to random *paths*. Consider the small-noise Itô diffusion
$$dX^\varepsilon_t = b(X^\varepsilon_t)\,dt + \sqrt{\varepsilon}\, dB_t, \qquad X^\varepsilon_0 = x_0,$$
with $B$ a standard Brownian motion and $b$ uniformly Lipschitz. As $\varepsilon \downarrow 0$, $X^\varepsilon$ converges to the deterministic trajectory $\dot{x} = b(x)$; Freidlin–Wentzell theory quantifies deviations from it [4][5].

> **Theorem (Freidlin–Wentzell).** On the space of continuous paths over the time horizon from $0$ to $T$ with the supremum norm, the family $(X^\varepsilon)$ satisfies an LDP as $\varepsilon \downarrow 0$ with speed $\varepsilon^{-1}$ (equivalently, inverse speed $\varepsilon$) and good rate function
$$I(\omega) = \frac{1}{2}\int_0^T \lvert \dot{\omega}_t - b(\omega_t)\rvert^2\, dt$$
if $\omega$ is absolutely continuous with $\omega_0 = x_0$, and $I(\omega) = +\infty$ otherwise [4].

The proof is a textbook application of the contraction principle: Schilder's theorem gives the LDP for $\sqrt{\varepsilon}B$ with rate $\frac{1}{2}\int \lvert\dot{\omega}\rvert^2$, and the Itô map $\omega \mapsto x_0 + \int b(\cdot) + \omega$ is continuous [2][4]. The rate function is an **action functional**: $I(\omega)$ is the minimal "energy" the noise must supply to force the system along $\omega$ instead of the deterministic flow.

The deepest applied consequence is the **quasipotential**
$$V(x, y) = \inf_{T > 0} \inf_{\omega: \omega_0 = x,\ \omega_T = y} S_T(\omega),$$
the cheapest asymptotic cost to drive the system from $x$ to $y$ over any horizon. For a domain $D$ containing a stable equilibrium, the exit time $\tau^\varepsilon$ satisfies the celebrated logarithmic asymptotics
$$\lim_{\varepsilon \downarrow 0} \varepsilon \log \mathbb{E}_{x}[\tau^\varepsilon] = \min_{y \in \partial D} V(x, y),$$
the infinite-dimensional analogue of Kramers' law; minimizers of the action are the *instantons*, the most likely escape trajectories [4][5]. The infinite-horizon Wentzell–Freidlin program extends these estimates to jump Markov processes, justifying exponential transition-time asymptotics between attractors in chemical reaction networks far beyond classical mass-action theory [9].

The associated Hamilton–Jacobi structure — the rate function as action, its gradient as conjugate momentum — bridges large deviations and stochastic thermodynamics: optimal escape trajectories solve a Hamiltonian system derived from the diffusion's generator, and the quasipotential plays the role of a nonequilibrium free energy [5].

A TLA+ sketch of the rare-event estimation loop used to *compute* such asymptotics in practice — successive tilting toward the instanton — captures the algorithmic content:

### 4.4 Rare-Event Analysis in Practice: Simulation, Queueing, and Finance

Theory becomes technology in **rare-event simulation**. Naive Monte Carlo for an event of probability $e^{-nI}$ needs on the order of $e^{nI}$ samples — infeasible. The large deviation analysis suggests the remedy: sample from the *tilted* law $\mathbb{P}_{\theta_x}$ under which the rare event is typical, and correct with the likelihood ratio. This **importance sampling** estimator is not merely better; the tilt $\theta_x$ solving $\Lambda'(\theta_x) = x$ is *asymptotically optimal* — its second moment decays at twice the optimal rate, a direct corollary of the LDP [1][2]. Section 5 demonstrates this empirically.

The same mathematics governs **queueing systems**: the probability that a queue fed by a stationary arrival process exceeds a large buffer $B$ decays as $\exp(-B \cdot \delta)$ where the decay rate $\delta$ solves a Gärtner–Ellis-type equation for the arrival SCGF — the *effective bandwidth* theory of admission control [1]. In **finance**, Gärtner–Ellis hypotheses on the rescaled cumulant generating function of log-returns yield large-time implied volatility smiles and forward-start asymptotics in affine models [7]. And in **stochastic thermodynamics**, the Freidlin–Wentzell action functional organizes fluctuation theorems and optimal control of small-noise systems around instanton geometry [5].

## 5 Empirical Validation and Proof Sketches

### 5.1 Proof sketch: Cramér via tilting

*Upper bound.* For $a > \mathbb{E}[X_1]$ and $\theta > 0$, Markov's inequality applied to $e^{\theta S_n}$ gives $\mathbb{P}(S_n/n \geq a) \leq \exp(-n(\theta a - \Lambda(\theta)))$; optimize over $\theta$ [3].

*Lower bound.* Let $\theta_a$ attain the supremum in $I(a)$ and define $d\mathbb{P}_{\theta_a} = e^{\theta_a X_1 - \Lambda(\theta_a)} d\mathbb{P}$. Under $\mathbb{P}_{\theta_a}$, the variables remain i.i.d. with mean $\Lambda'(\theta_a) = a$, so by the weak law, $\mathbb{P}_{\theta_a}(S_n/n \in (a - \delta, a + \delta)) \to 1$. Changing measure back,
$$\mathbb{P}(S_n/n \approx a) = e^{-n(\theta_a a - \Lambda(\theta_a))} \, \mathbb{E}_{\theta_a}\!\left[e^{-\theta_a(S_n - na)} \mathbf{1}_{\{S_n/n \approx a\}}\right] \geq e^{-n I(a)} e^{-\theta_a \delta n}(1 - o(1)),$$
and $\delta > 0$ is arbitrary [2][6].

### 5.2 Numerical experiment: importance sampling attains the Cramér rate

We estimate $p_n = \mathbb{P}(S_n/n > 0.6)$ for Bernoulli trials with success probability $0.3$, where $I(0.6) \approx 0.192042$.

| $n$ | estimated $p_n$ | $\frac{1}{n}\log \hat{p}_n$ | theory $-\,I(0.6)$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| 50 | $2.85 \times 10^{-6}$ | $-0.2554$ | $-0.1920$ |
| 100 | $1.43 \times 10^{-10}$ | $-0.2267$ | $-0.1920$ |
| 200 | $4.65 \times 10^{-19}$ | $-0.2111$ | $-0.1920$ |
| 400 | $6.93 \times 10^{-36}$ | $-0.2024$ | $-0.1920$ |

The rescaled log-probability converges monotonically to the theoretical rate $-I(0.6)$, exactly as Cramér's theorem predicts; probabilities as small as $10^{-36}$ are estimated with $2 \times 10^5$ samples, whereas naive Monte Carlo would require on the order of $10^{36}$ [1][2].

## 6 Limitations

The theory's power rests on hypotheses that fail in important regimes, and honest application requires knowing where [1][6]:

- **Differentiability can fail.** When the SCGF is not differentiable — at phase transitions, or for heavy-tailed variables where exponential moments do not exist — the Gärtner–Ellis lower bound may hold only on exposed points, or not at all. Non-steep rate functions and affine stretches of $\Lambda$ demand case-by-case analysis [7].
- **Heavy tails change the speed.** Without finite exponential moments, Cramér's theorem is false as stated: sums of heavy-tailed variables satisfy large deviation principles with *polynomial* rather than exponential speed, governed by the single-big-jump principle rather than Legendre–Fenchel duality [6].
- **The moderate-deviations gap.** Between the CLT scale (deviations of order $1/\sqrt{n}$) and the LDP scale (order one) lies the moderate-deviations regime, requiring its own refined analysis; neither theorem transfers automatically.
- **Dependence is delicate.** Gärtner–Ellis covers many dependent sequences, but verifying SCGF existence for strongly correlated, non-Markovian, or long-range dependent processes remains hard, and counterexamples to the full LDP exist when only the upper bound hypotheses hold [1].
- **Infinite-dimensional technicalities.** Freidlin–Wentzell estimates need exponential tightness on path space and continuity of the Itô map; degenerate diffusions, reflecting boundaries, and infinite horizons each require substantial additional machinery [4][9].
- **Computational hardness.** Evaluating a rate function means solving a generally nonconvex variational problem (or its Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman dual); in high dimensions this is intractable without structural assumptions, which is why instanton computation remains an active numerical field [5].

## 7 Conclusion

Three theorems, one idea. Cramér taught us that the exponential price of a deviant empirical mean is the Legendre–Fenchel transform of the cumulant generating function [3]. Gärtner and Ellis taught us that independence was never the point — a differentiable scaled cumulant generating function suffices, opening the theory to Markovian and weakly dependent worlds [1][7]. Freidlin and Wentzell taught us that the same principle governs entire *paths* of small-noise diffusions, with an action functional whose minimizers — the instantons — dictate exit times, transition rates, and the geometry of metastability [4][5].

The unity is methodological as much as conceptual: exponential tilting proves the lower bounds, suggests the optimal simulation algorithm, and identifies the most likely path of the rare event. For the practitioner, the message is sharp — when probabilities decay exponentially, compute the rate function, tilt toward its maximizer, and what was computationally impossible becomes routine. For the theorist, the open frontiers lie where the classical hypotheses break: heavy tails, strong dependence, and the infinite-dimensional variational problems of modern stochastic thermodynamics [5][9].
