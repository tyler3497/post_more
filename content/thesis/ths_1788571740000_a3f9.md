# The Neural Tangent Kernel: Infinite-Width Linearization, Gradient Flow Dynamics, and Spectral Bias in Overparameterized Networks

## Abstract

The Neural Tangent Kernel (NTK), introduced by Jacot, Gabriel, and Hongler in 2018 [1], provides a rigorous mathematical framework for understanding the training dynamics of overparameterized neural networks. In the infinite-width limit, the evolution of a network's function during gradient descent is governed not by intractable nonconvex parameter dynamics but by a **kernel gradient flow** in function space, where the kernel — the inner product of parameter gradients of the network function — converges to an explicit deterministic limit and remains constant throughout training. This thesis develops the full NTK theory: the NTK parameterization and its scaling, the linearization of wide networks around initialization [2], the equivalence of gradient-descent training to kernel regression, the universality of the framework across architectures via Tensor Programs [4], and the spectral bias governing the learning-rate ordering of eigenfunctions [5]. We derive the governing differential equations, state convergence theorems for gradient flow and discrete gradient descent, characterize finite-width corrections and the lazy-versus-feature-learning dichotomy [3][6], and close with the theory's limitations as a model of modern deep learning practice.

## 1. Introduction

Deep neural networks present a paradox that classical statistical learning theory struggles to resolve: they possess vastly more parameters than training samples, their loss landscapes are nonconvex, and yet gradient-based optimization reliably drives training loss to zero while producing models that generalize. The **Neural Tangent Kernel** theory resolves the optimization half of this paradox by showing that, in the limit of infinite width, the entire training trajectory of a network becomes *linear* in its parameters and *convex* in its function-space representation [1].

The central insight is deceptively simple. Consider a network function $f_\theta : \mathbb{R}^d \to \mathbb{R}$ parameterized by $\theta \in \mathbb{R}^P$. Under continuous-time gradient descent (gradient flow) on the least-squares loss

$$
L(\theta) = \frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n} (f_\theta(x_i) - y_i)^2,
$$

the network's *outputs* on the training set evolve as

$$
\frac{d}{dt} f_t(x_i) = -\sum_{j=1}^{n} \Theta_t(x_i, x_j)\, (f_t(x_j) - y_j),
$$

where the **Neural Tangent Kernel** is defined as

$$
\Theta_t(x, x') = \left\langle \nabla_\theta f_{\theta_t}(x),\, \nabla_\theta f_{\theta_t}(x') \right\rangle = \sum_{p=1}^{P} \partial_{\theta_p} f_{\theta_t}(x)\,\partial_{\theta_p} f_{\theta_t}(x').
$$

The dynamics in *function space* are therefore governed by a kernel gradient flow that is *convex* in the function $f$ — even though the dynamics in *parameter space* are nonconvex in $\theta$. The NTK is random at initialization and generally evolves during training, but the decisive theorem of Jacot et al. [1] states that as hidden widths tend to infinity (under a suitable parameterization), $\Theta_t$ converges in probability to an **explicit deterministic limiting kernel** $\Theta_\infty$ and remains *constant* along the training trajectory. Training then reduces to kernel regression, with a closed-form solution.

This thesis is organized as follows. Section 2 situates the NTK within the classical correspondence between wide networks and Gaussian processes. Section 3 develops the methodology: NTK parameterization, the gradient-flow derivation, and the linearization argument of Lee et al. [2]. Section 4 is the deep dive: the parameterization-scaling dichotomy and lazy training [3], exact kernel-regression solutions, Tensor Programs universality [4], the NTK eigenspectrum and spectral bias [5], and finite-width corrections with the feature-learning frontier [6]. Section 5 presents proof sketches and empirical validation; Section 6 delineates the theory's limitations.

> **Theorem 1 (NTK convergence — informal, Jacot et al. [1]):** *For a fully-connected network with a Lipschitz, non-polynomial activation and data on the unit sphere, the empirical NTK $\Theta_t^{(m)}$ at widths $m \to \infty$ converges in probability to a deterministic positive-definite kernel $\Theta_\infty$, uniformly over compact sets of inputs, and $\sup_{t \in [0,T]} \|\Theta_t^{(m)} - \Theta_\infty\| = o_P(1)$ for any finite horizon $T$.*

## 2. Background

### 2.1 The Gaussian process correspondence

The NTK theory is a dynamical extension of the older, static correspondence between infinitely wide networks and Gaussian processes. Neal (1996) showed that a single-hidden-layer network with i.i.d. Gaussian weights converges to a Gaussian process (GP) as width tends to infinity; this was extended to deep networks by Lee et al. and Matthews et al. [2]. The critical distinction: the GP correspondence describes the network only *at initialization*, while the NTK describes it *during training* — how the function moves.

### 2.2 Classical kernel regression

Given a positive-definite kernel $K$ and training data $\{(x_i, y_i)\}_{i=1}^n$, kernel ridge regression solves

$$
\min_{f \in \mathcal{H}} \sum_{i=1}^{n}(f(x_i) - y_i)^2 + \lambda \|f\|_{\mathcal{H}}^2,
$$

with closed-form solution $f^\star(x) = K(x, X)(K(X,X) + \lambda I)^{-1}y$ via the representer theorem. The NTK theory will show that *gradient descent on an infinitely wide network computes exactly this object* (with $\lambda = 0$) using $K = \Theta_\infty$.

### 2.3 Gradient flow and the chain rule in function space

Under gradient flow $\dot{\theta}_t = -\nabla_\theta L(\theta_t)$, the chain rule gives the function-space dynamics:

$$
\dot{f}_t(x) = \langle \nabla_\theta f_{\theta_t}(x), \dot{\theta}_t \rangle = -\sum_{i=1}^{n} \Theta_t(x, x_i)\,\partial_{f(x_i)} C,
$$

where $C$ is the cost as a functional of $f$. The key structural fact: $\Theta_t$ is positive *semi*-definite by construction (it is a Gram matrix of gradients), so the loss decreases monotonically. The NTK thus acts as a *preconditioner* on the functional gradient.

## 3. Methodology

### 3.1 The NTK parameterization

The convergence of the NTK to a constant limit hinges on parameterization. In the **NTK parameterization**, a fully-connected network of depth $L$ and hidden widths $m$ is written

$$
\begin{aligned}
h^{(1)}(x) &= \frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\,W^{(1)} x + b^{(1)}, \
x^{(\ell)}(x) &= \phi(h^{(\ell)}(x)), \
h^{(\ell+1)}(x) &= \frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\,W^{(\ell+1)} x^{(\ell)}(x) + b^{(\ell+1)},\quad \ell = 1,\dots,L-1,\
f_\theta(x) &= \frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\, w^{(L+1)\top} x^{(L)}(x),
\end{aligned}
$$

with all weights and biases drawn i.i.d. $\mathcal{N}(0,1)$. The $1/\sqrt{m}$ factors ensure that preactivations remain $O(1)$ as $m \to \infty$ *and* that each parameter's contribution to the output is $O(1/\sqrt{m})$. Consequently, moving the output by $O(1)$ requires each of the $m$ parameters to move only by $O(1/\sqrt{m})$ — the network's parameters *barely move*, while its function moves substantially. This is the **lazy training** regime of Chizat and Bach [3]: the model behaves as its first-order Taylor expansion around initialization.

### 3.2 Derivation of the kernel gradient flow

Differentiating the least-squares loss under gradient flow with learning rate $\eta$:

$$
\dot{\theta}_t = -\eta \sum_{i=1}^{n} (f_t(x_i) - y_i)\,\nabla_\theta f_{\theta_t}(x_i).
$$

Applying the chain rule to $f_t(x) = f_{\theta_t}(x)$:

$$
\boxed{\quad \dot{f}_t(x) = -\eta \sum_{i=1}^{n} \Theta_t(x, x_i)\,(f_t(x_i) - y_i) \quad}
$$

This is a *linear* ODE in the vector of training predictions $u(t) = (f_t(x_1),\dots,f_t(x_n))^\top$:

$$
\dot{u}(t) = -\eta\,\Theta_t(X,X)\,(u(t) - y).
$$

**If** $\Theta_t \equiv \Theta_\infty$ is constant, the ODE has the closed-form solution

$$
u(t) - y = e^{-\eta \Theta_\infty(X,X) t}\,(u(0) - y),
$$

and for a test point $x$:

$$
f_t(x) = f_0(x) - \Theta_\infty(x, X)\,\Theta_\infty(X,X)^{-1}\Big(I - e^{-\eta \Theta_\infty(X,X) t}\Big)(f_0(X) - y).
$$

As $t \to \infty$, provided $\Theta_\infty(X,X) \succ 0$, this converges to the kernel-regression interpolant $f_\infty(x) = \Theta_\infty(x,X)\Theta_\infty(X,X)^{-1} y + f_0(x) - \Theta_\infty(x,X)\Theta_\infty(X,X)^{-1}f_0(X)$ — the *minimum-RKHS-norm* interpolant shifted by the initialization [1][2].

### 3.3 The linearization argument

Lee et al. [2] made the linearization rigorous and quantitative. Define the linearized model

$$
f^{\mathrm{lin}}_t(x) = f_{\theta_0}(x) + \langle \nabla_\theta f_{\theta_0}(x),\, \theta_t - \theta_0 \rangle.
$$

Their main result: for widths $m = \Omega(n^4/\lambda_{\min}^4)$ (polynomial in dataset size and inverse minimum NTK eigenvalue), with high probability,

$$
\sup_{t \ge 0} |f_{\theta_t}(x) - f^{\mathrm{lin}}_t(x)| = O(m^{-1/2}),
$$

uniformly on compact sets. Moreover, the parameters satisfy $\|\theta_t - \theta_0\|_2 = O(1/\sqrt{m})$ for all $t$ — movement vanishes as width grows, while the function-space movement stays $O(1)$ because $P \approx m^2$ parameters each contribute $O(1/\sqrt{m})$ through gradients of norm $O(1/\sqrt{m})$:

$$
\|f_t - f_0\| \sim \sqrt{P} \cdot \frac{1}{\sqrt{m}} \cdot \|\theta_t - \theta_0\| = O(1).
$$

This scaling balance is the mathematical heart of lazy training [3].

### 3.4 Empirical NTK computation

The finite-width NTK can be computed exactly via Jacobian contraction:

```python
import torch
from torch.func import functional_call, jacrev, vmap

def empirical_ntk(model, params, x1, x2):
    """Compute the empirical NTK matrix Theta(x1, x2) via per-sample Jacobians."""
    def fnet(single_params, single_x):
        return functional_call(model, single_params, (single_x.unsqueeze(0),)).squeeze(0)
    # Jacobian of outputs w.r.t. flattened parameters, per input
    jac1 = vmap(jacrev(fnet, argnums=0), in_dims=(None, 0))(params, x1)
    jac2 = vmap(jacrev(fnet, argnums=0), in_dims=(None, 0))(params, x2)
    flat1 = torch.cat([j.reshape(x1.shape[0], -1) for j in jac1.values()], dim=1)
    flat2 = torch.cat([j.reshape(x2.shape[0], -1) for j in jac2.values()], dim=1)
    return flat1 @ flat2.T  # (n1, n2) Gram matrix of parameter gradients
```

In practice, libraries such as **Neural Tangents** (built on JAX) compute both the empirical NTK and the analytic $\Theta_\infty$ via the recursion of Section 4.1 [2].

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 The recursion for the limiting NTK

For a fully-connected network, $\Theta_\infty$ admits an explicit layer-wise recursion. Let $\Sigma^{(\ell)}(x,x')$ be the NNGP covariance and $\dot{\Sigma}^{(\ell)}(x,x') = \mathbb{E}_{(u,v)\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\Lambda^{(\ell)})}[\phi'(u)\phi'(v)]$ the derivative covariance, where $\Lambda^{(\ell)}$ is the $2\times2$ covariance of preactivations. Then

$$
\boxed{\quad \Theta^{(\ell+1)}(x,x') = \Theta^{(\ell)}(x,x')\,\dot{\Sigma}^{(\ell+1)}(x,x') + \Sigma^{(\ell+1)}(x,x') \quad}
$$

with $\Theta^{(1)}(x,x') = \Sigma^{(1)}(x,x')$. The first term propagates gradient signal backward through the derivative kernel; the second adds the fresh contribution of layer $\ell+1$'s own weights. For ReLU, both $\Sigma$ and $\dot{\Sigma}$ have closed forms via the arccosine kernels, making $\Theta_\infty$ exactly computable without any network [1].

### 4.2 Lazy training versus feature learning: the scaling dichotomy

Chizat, Oyallon, and Bach [3] showed that lazy training is not fundamentally about *width* but about *scaling*. Consider a model $\alpha\, f(\theta, \cdot)$ with output scale $\alpha$:

- **Lazy (kernel) regime** ($\alpha \to \infty$ or NTK parameterization): the model linearizes; the tangent kernel $\langle \nabla_\theta f, \nabla_\theta f \rangle$ is frozen; training is kernel regression.
- **Rich / mean-field regime** ($\alpha = O(1)$, standard parameterization with $1/m$ output scaling): parameters move by $O(1)$; hidden features *adapt* to data; the tangent kernel *evolves* during training.

Yang and Hu [6] sharpened this into a classification theorem: in their space of parameterizations, any infinite-width limit either admits **feature learning** or has training dynamics given by **kernel gradient descent** — *never both*. The standard parameterization (SP) and NTK parameterization both fall on the kernel side; the **maximal update parameterization** ($\mu$P) was constructed precisely to sit on the feature-learning side while keeping per-layer update magnitudes width-independent. This dichotomy is arguably the most important conceptual export of NTK theory: it tells us the infinite-width limit is *not unique*, and which limit you get is a choice of scaling.

| Regime | Parameterization | Param movement | Kernel during training | Learns features? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Lazy / kernel | NTK param., $1/\sqrt{m}$ | $O(1/\sqrt{m}) \to 0$ | Frozen at $\Theta_\infty$ | No |
| Mean-field | $1/m$ output scaling | $O(1)$ | Evolves (Wasserstein flow) | Yes |
| Maximal update ($\mu$P) | Layer-wise tuned | Width-independent $O(1)$ effect | Evolves | Yes |

### 4.3 Tensor Programs: universality across architectures

A natural objection is that the NTK recursion above is specific to fully-connected nets. Yang's **Tensor Programs** framework [4] removes this limitation entirely. A tensor program is a straight-line program over large random tensors; Yang's Master Theorem characterizes the scaling limit of *any* such program. Consequences include:

1. **GP limits** for RNNs, CNNs, ResNets, Transformers, and batch-normalized networks;
2. A rigorous treatment of the **gradient independence assumption** (GIA) — the heuristic that backpropagated weights can be treated as independent copies — with precise conditions for when it holds and corrections when it fails;
3. The convergence of the NTK at initialization for essentially *any* architecture expressible as a tensor program.

This elevates the NTK from a fully-connected curiosity to a universal descriptor: *every* standard architecture has a well-defined infinite-width NTK, computable (in principle) by mechanical application of the Master Theorem [4].

### 4.4 Spectral bias: learning in eigenfunction order

Perhaps the most empirically consequential prediction of NTK theory concerns *which functions are learned first*. Diagonalize the limiting kernel on the data distribution:

$$
\int \Theta_\infty(x, x')\,\varphi_k(x')\,d\mu(x') = \lambda_k\,\varphi_k(x), \qquad \lambda_1 \ge \lambda_2 \ge \cdots > 0.
$$

Projecting the residual dynamics onto eigenfunctions gives decoupled scalar ODEs:

$$
\boxed{\quad \frac{d}{dt} \langle r_t, \varphi_k \rangle = -\eta\,\lambda_k\,\langle r_t, \varphi_k \rangle \quad\Rightarrow\quad \langle r_t, \varphi_k \rangle = e^{-\eta\lambda_k t}\,\langle r_0, \varphi_k \rangle. \quad}
$$

Components along large-eigenvalue eigenfunctions decay exponentially faster. For data on the sphere $\mathbb{S}^{d-1}$ with dot-product kernels, the eigenfunctions are **spherical harmonics** and eigenvalues decay polynomially with frequency: $\lambda_k \asymp k^{-\beta}$ for a smoothness index $\beta$ determined by the activation (for ReLU networks, $\beta$ relates to the kernel's singularity at $x = x'$). Low-frequency (smooth, global) components are therefore learned *first*; high-frequency (oscillatory, local) components require exponentially longer training [1][5].

This is the NTK *explanation* of the empirically observed **spectral bias** (or *frequency principle*): Rahaman et al. [5] showed via Fourier analysis that deep ReLU networks fit low frequencies before high frequencies, with the $k$-th frequency component's magnitude decaying at least as $O(k^{-2})$. The NTK derivation turns this observation into a theorem about gradient-flow timescales, and immediately suggests a theoretical motivation for **early stopping**: halting training at time $t$ acts as a spectral filter, fitting only eigenfunctions with $\lambda_k \gtrsim 1/(\eta t)$ — an implicit regularization toward smooth functions [1].

### 4.5 Finite-width corrections and the NTK hierarchy

At finite width, the kernel is not frozen. Huang and Yau derived the **NTK hierarchy**: an infinite system of coupled ODEs for the NTK and higher-order tangent kernels

$$
K^{(r)}_t(x_1,\dots,x_r) = \sum_{p} \partial_{\theta_p} f_t(x_1)\cdots\partial_{\theta_p} f_t(x_r),
$$

showing the $r$-th kernel evolves under the $(r{+}1)$-st. Truncating at order $1/m$ yields the leading finite-width correction: the kernel drifts by $O(1/m)$ over $O(1)$ training time. At practical widths, the $O(1/\sqrt{m})$ fluctuations of the empirical NTK around $\Theta_\infty$ are non-negligible, and ensemble averaging over initializations demonstrably closes part of the gap between finite networks and their infinite-width limits [2].

## 5. Empirical Results / Proofs

### 5.1 Proof sketch: constancy of the NTK

The core technical argument (Jacot et al. [1], simplified) proceeds in three steps:

1. **Initialization.** By the law of large numbers over hidden units, $\Theta_0^{(m)}(x,x') \xrightarrow{P} \Theta_\infty(x,x')$, since the NTK is an average of $m$ i.i.d. terms at each layer.
2. **Uniform convergence.** Equicontinuity plus Arzelà–Ascoli upgrades pointwise convergence to uniform convergence on compacts.
3. **Stability during training.** The key estimate: $\|\theta_t - \theta_0\|_2 \le \frac{\|u(0)-y\|_2}{\sqrt{m}\,\lambda_{\min}(\Theta_\infty(X,X))}(1 - e^{-\eta\lambda_{\min}t}) = O(1/\sqrt{m})$. Since $\Theta_t$ is locally Lipschitz in $\theta$ with constant $O(1)$, we get $\|\Theta_t - \Theta_0\| = O(1/\sqrt{m}) \to 0$. The kernel cannot move because the parameters cannot move far enough to move it.

The positive-definiteness of $\Theta_\infty$ on distinct sphere-supported data (for non-polynomial $\phi$) then guarantees $\lambda_{\min} > 0$ and hence *global exponential convergence* of the training loss: $L(t) \le e^{-2\eta\lambda_{\min}t}L(0)$.

### 5.2 Proof sketch: linearization error bound (Lee et al. [2])

Writing $f_t - f^{\mathrm{lin}}_t$ via Taylor's theorem with integral remainder, the remainder is controlled by $\sup_t \|\nabla^2_\theta f_{\theta_t}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\,\|\theta_t - \theta_0\|^2$. Under NTK scaling, $\|\nabla^2 f\|_{\mathrm{op}} = O(1/\sqrt{m})$ while $\|\theta_t - \theta_0\| = O(1)$ — giving remainder $O(1/\sqrt{m})$. Empirically [2], the linearized model's predictions track the true network's to within a few percent even at widths of a few hundred, across MLPs, CNNs, and (with caveats) cross-entropy loss.

### 5.3 Empirical protocol for verifying NTK predictions

A complete empirical check on a synthetic regression task: (1) train a wide MLP ($m = 2048$) under the NTK parameterization; (2) compute $\Theta_\infty$ analytically via Section 4.1; (3) compare test predictions at convergence against the closed-form kernel-regression predictor; (4) project residuals onto top NTK eigenvectors and verify eigenvalue-proportional decay rates. Reported results in [1][2]: agreement within $\sim$1% relative error for the final predictor, with spectral-bias decay confirmed on synthetic and MNIST/CIFAR subsets.

## 6. Limitations

The NTK theory is a landmark, but its limitations as a description of *practical* deep learning are severe:

1. **No feature learning.** The frozen-kernel prediction means hidden representations never adapt to data — yet representation learning is widely believed to be the source of deep learning's power. Chizat et al. [3] showed forcing real CNNs into the lazy regime *degrades* performance, and Yang & Hu [6] proved the infinite-width kernel limit cannot learn features by construction.
2. **Width requirements are astronomical.** Non-asymptotic bounds require $m = \mathrm{poly}(n, 1/\lambda_{\min})$ with large exponents — far beyond practical widths, where the kernel *does* evolve and networks *do* learn features.
3. **The kernel regime can underperform.** On hard tasks, finite networks that escape the lazy regime (via large learning rates, standard parameterization, or $\mu$P) outperform their NTK predictors. The NTK is thus better viewed as a *baseline* — the performance achievable *without* feature learning — than as a full explanation.
4. **Discrete-time and adaptive optimizers.** The cleanest theorems concern gradient *flow*. Discrete GD requires small step sizes; the extension to Adam and other adaptive optimizers needs a nonlinear generalization of the kernel picture (recently addressed in the Tensor Programs IVb line [4]).
5. **Generalization is not explained.** The NTK characterizes *optimization* and the *implicit bias* (minimum-RKHS-norm interpolation), but the RKHS norm governing generalization can be enormous for deep NTKs; the theory does not by itself explain why these interpolants generalize on real data.
6. **Architectural blind spots.** Batch normalization, attention scaling subtleties, and weight tying require careful extensions; naive application of the Master Theorem can mislead when the gradient independence assumption fails [4].

In short: the NTK theory *solves* the optimization mystery of overparameterized networks in a limit where networks provably *cannot do the thing* (feature learning) that many believe makes them work. Its enduring value is as a rigorous reference point — the "null model" of deep learning dynamics — against which richer, feature-learning theories must be measured.

---

## 7. Conclusion

The theory's central equation, $\dot{f}_t = -\eta\,\Theta_\infty\,(f_t - y)$, deserves its place alongside the backpropagation equations as a fundamental description of learning: gradient descent on an infinitely wide network performs kernel regression with a specific, computable kernel; it learns smooth structure before fine detail; and early stopping is spectral filtering. Where the theory ends — at the boundary between lazy kernel dynamics and rich feature learning — the next generation of theory begins. Understanding that boundary, quantitatively and at practical widths, remains the central open problem in the mathematical foundations of deep learning.
