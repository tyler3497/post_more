# In-Network Aggregation for Distributed Deep Learning with P4 Programmable Switches: SwitchML Gradient Reduction, Quantization-Aware Allreduce, and Line-Rate Summation at 100Gbps

## Abstract

Data-parallel deep learning is bottlenecked by the allreduce collective: synchronizing a 500 MB gradient vector across 64 workers on 100 Gbps Ethernet via ring allreduce moves roughly 2 bytes per model byte per worker, saturating fabrics and idling GPUs for up to 70% of iteration time. This thesis develops the theory, architecture, and formal analysis of in-network aggregation — executing the allreduce summation inside the dataplane of a P4 programmable switch. We dissect SwitchML: pool-based streaming aggregation, integer-only dataplane arithmetic via adaptive block-floating-point quantization, and loss-tolerant transport, proving per-worker traffic volume drops from O(n) to O(1) at line rate. We extend the treatment to multi-rack topologies via ATP's decentralized aggregator scheduling and bitmap-based retransmission, and contrast SHARP's fixed-function InfiniBand alternative. We analyze quantization-aware allreduce with error feedback, derive convergence bounds under inexact aggregation, model SRAM and pipeline-stage budgets on Tofino-class hardware, and quantify gains: up to 5.5x end-to-end training speedup and 2.9x over NCCL on microbenchmarks. We close with limitations — integer-only ALUs, failure semantics, multi-tenant isolation — and open problems in floating-point dataplanes and congestion-aware aggregator placement.


## 1. Introduction

Synchronous data-parallel stochastic gradient descent (SGD) dominates the training of large neural networks. Each of *n* workers computes a stochastic gradient on its local minibatch and all workers must agree on the mean gradient before advancing to the next iteration. This consensus is realized by the **allreduce** collective, whose cost grows with the model size and, in many configurations, with the number of workers. Empirical studies report communication occupying 40–80% of wall-clock training time for models such as VGG-16 and BERT at 32–64 GPU scale on 100 Gbps Ethernet [4][5].

> **Theorem (Lower bound intuition):** Any allreduce that routes only through end-hosts must move at least 2(n−1)/n model bytes per worker. In-network aggregation achieves 2 model bytes per worker — matching the bandwidth lower bound of a centralized parameter server — while retaining the *O*(1) per-node simplicity of a symmetric collective.

The key observation is that the allreduce's reduce-scatter phase is a pure elementwise summation. There is no algorithmic reason for this summation to occur at the endpoints: it can occur *inside the network*, at the point where all gradient streams converge — the top-of-rack (ToR) switch. Programmable switches (Barefoot Tofino, Intel IPU-class pipelines) based on the Reconfigurable Match-Action Table (RMT) architecture expose stateful ALUs and tens of megabytes of SRAM at 3.2–25.6 Tbps of aggregate throughput, making them capable of aggregating vector chunks at line rate [7]. SwitchML [1] demonstrated the first practical realization: a co-design of a P4 switch program and an end-host transport that accelerates PyTorch/TensorFlow training by up to 5.5× end-to-end.

This thesis makes four contributions:

1. **A rigorous cost model** of in-network aggregation: per-worker traffic volume, latency, SRAM/stage budgets, and the fixed-point quantization theory that makes integer-only dataplanes exact enough for convergence.
2. **A complete exposition of SwitchML's mechanisms** — pool-based streaming, adaptive block floating point, the loss-recovery protocol with sequence slots, and shadow copies for switch failures — with correctness arguments for at-most-once aggregation semantics.
3. **Extensions to scale-out topologies**: ATP's [2] decentralized multi-rack aggregation with dynamic aggregator allocation and retransmission bitmaps; GRID's [3] gradient-routing formulation; and SHARP's [4] fixed-function alternative.
4. **A convergence analysis of quantization-aware allreduce**, bounding the variance injected by integer dataplane arithmetic and error-feedback compensation, plus an empirical synthesis of reported speedups and a catalog of open problems.

## 2. Background and Related Work

### 2.1 Distributed training and the allreduce bottleneck

In synchronous data-parallel SGD, iteration *t* with *n* workers computes

$$
g_t = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} \nabla F_i(x_t; \xi_{t,i}), \qquad x_{t+1} = x_t - \eta_t g_t,
$$

where $F_i$ is the local loss and $\xi_{t,i}$ the local minibatch. The allreduce implements the summation. The classical *ring allreduce* organizes workers in a ring; each worker sends |g|/n-sized chunks 2(n−1) times, for total per-worker traffic 2(n−1)/n · |g| ≈ 2|g|. At 100 Gbps and |g| = 500 MB (a large language model), each iteration moves ~1 GB per worker — 80 ms of pure communication against ~30 ms of compute, a ratio that worsens as GPUs accelerate [5][6].

**Alternatives.** Parameter servers centralize aggregation but concentrate traffic and add a hop. Hierarchical allreduce reduces volume at the cost of complexity. Gradient compression (quantization to 1–8 bits [5][6], sparsification) reduces bytes but interacts with convergence and is orthogonal to *where* aggregation happens. In-network aggregation is complementary: it reduces *traffic volume*, independent of compression ratio.

### 2.2 Programmable switch dataplanes

Modern programmable switches implement the P4 → RMT pipeline: a parser, *k* match-action stages (typically 12 on Tofino), each with TCAM/SRAM tables and stateful ALUs, then a deparser. Critical constraints for in-network computation [1][7]:

| Resource | Typical budget (Tofino-class) | Implication |
|---|---|---|
| Per-stage SRAM | ~10s of MB total across stages | Cannot hold a full model; must *stream* |
| Stateful ALU ops | add/sub/min/max/bitwise per packet | **Integer arithmetic only** — no floating point |
| Parser depth | ~200–300 B practical packet headers | Vector chunks bounded by MTU |
| Pipeline stages | 12 stages | Aggregation must fit in few stages |
| Line rate | 3.2–25.6 Tbps aggregate | ~1 GHz packet clock; any per-packet logic must be O(1) |

The integer-only restriction is the central design driver: ML gradients are FP32, but switch ALUs add integers. SwitchML's answer is *adaptive block floating point* (§4.3).

### 2.3 Prior and concurrent systems

- **SHARP** (Mellanox/NVIDIA) [4]: fixed-function in-network reduction trees in InfiniBand switches; achieves 2.1× latency reduction for 8 B MPI_Allreduce on 128 hosts, but requires InfiniBand and fixed-function FPUs.
- **ATP** [2]: decentralized in-network aggregation for multi-rack Ethernet; dynamic aggregator scheduling across ToR and spine switches; bitmap-based retransmission for loss resilience.
- **GRID** [3]: formulates gradient routing as an optimization over switch load, balancing aggregation across programmable switches in two-layer topologies.
- **PANAMA / iSwitch**: FPGA-based in-network accelerators; programmable but lower port density than ASIC switches.
- **NetReduce / P4ML**: early prototypes of switch-based aggregation, limited by memory and reliability handling.

## 3. Methodology

Our methodology combines (i) a traffic and resource cost model parameterized by *n* workers, model size |g|, packet chunk size *k*, and pool size *s*; (ii) a quantization error model for block-floating-point dataplane arithmetic with adaptive scaling; (iii) a correctness analysis of the streaming aggregation protocol under packet loss, duplication, and switch failure; and (iv) a convergence analysis of SGD under the resulting inexact-but-bounded aggregation noise. Empirical claims synthesize published measurements from the SwitchML [1], ATP [2], GRID [3], and SHARP [4] evaluations, reproduced here as comparative tables rather than new experiments.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 SwitchML: pool-based streaming aggregation

A complete model update (hundreds of MB) far exceeds the ~50–100 MB of usable on-chip SRAM in a programmable switch, and even if it fit, holding it would serialize iterations. SwitchML therefore *streams* aggregation through a **pool of integer aggregators** — a small array of (value, count) slots resident in register arrays across pipeline stages [1].

**Switch logic (per packet).** Each gradient packet carries `(idx, off, vector)`: `idx` addresses a pool slot, `off` the offset into the model tensor, and `vector` a chunk of *k* int32 elements. On arrival:

```
pool[idx].value += packet.vector      // elementwise integer add, k elements
pool[idx].count += 1
if pool[idx].count == n:              // all workers contributed
    packet.vector = pool[idx].value   // rewrite payload with the sum
    pool[idx].value = 0; pool[idx].count = 0
    multicast(packet)                 // one copy per worker port
else:
    drop(packet)                      // partial sums never leave the switch
```

This is *O*(1) per-packet work and fits in a handful of RMT stages. The end-hosts own all policy: they assign `idx` slots to tensor regions, pipeline chunks so the pool is always full (hiding the aggregation latency), and recycle a slot only after receiving its result. Because only *completed* sums are multicasted, downstream bandwidth sees exactly |g| bytes of results per iteration per worker — the theoretical minimum.

**Volume analysis.** Per iteration, worker *i* sends |g| bytes of gradient chunks and receives |g| bytes of aggregated results: **2|g| bytes total**, independent of *n*. Compare ring allreduce's 2(n−1)/n·|g| and a parameter server's 2|g| (plus an extra network hop). In-network aggregation matches the lower bound *without* concentrating state at a host. For *n* = 64 workers and |g| = 1 GB, this removes ~62 GB of cross-fabric traffic per iteration relative to host-based reduction trees.

### 4.2 Reliability: slots, shadows, and at-most-once semantics

UDP-based transport (SwitchML uses RDMA over Unreliable Connected, or DPDK UDP) admits loss, duplication, and reordering. Double-counting a retransmitted chunk would corrupt the sum — a catastrophic, silent failure mode. SwitchML's protocol provides *at-most-once, at-least-once* aggregation semantics through three mechanisms [1]:

1. **Per-slot sequence numbers.** Each pool slot carries a generation counter. A packet with a stale generation is ignored; only the first arrival of each (slot, generation) contributes to `count`.
2. **Worker-driven retransmission.** End-hosts track which slots have not returned results within a timeout and retransmit the identical chunk. Because the switch logic is idempotent per generation (a duplicate arrives with the same generation and is dropped after the slot advanced), retransmission is safe.
3. **Shadow copies.** To survive *switch* failure mid-aggregation, each slot has a shadow copy on a designated worker. If the switch is declared dead, the shadow replays pending chunks to a backup aggregator. This is the only component that violates pure in-network execution, and it activates only on failure.

> **Theorem:** *If at most one of {switch, shadow worker} fails per iteration and the network delivers each packet at least once within the timeout budget, then every pool slot delivers exactly one correct sum to all n workers.* *Proof sketch:* idempotence per generation prevents double-counting (at-most-once); timeouts plus retransmission guarantee eventual completion (at-least-once); the shadow preserves slot state across switch failure. ∎

ATP [2] generalizes this with per-packet **bitmaps**: each aggregator stores a bitmap of which workers have contributed; retransmitted fragments check the bitmap to decide whether to add or to forward the current partial sum downstream. This handles partial aggregation in multi-level trees where a retransmission may arrive at a switch whose aggregator has already been deallocated.

### 4.3 Quantization-aware allreduce: making integer ALUs sufficient

Switch ALUs cannot add FP32. SwitchML's solution is an *adaptive block-floating-point* representation used only for aggregation; weights and activations remain FP32 everywhere else [1]:

1. **Scaling (host):** for each chunk, workers agree on a scale factor $s = \max |x|$ (or a per-tensor adaptive factor updated with momentum). Each worker transmits $\mathrm{round}(x_i \cdot 2^{31} / s)$ as int32.
2. **Summation (switch):** pure int32 elementwise addition — no rounding, no error.
3. **Descaling (host):** divide the received sum by $2^{31}/s$ and by *n*.

Only the initial rounding introduces error, bounded by $s \cdot 2^{-32}$ per element — far below SGD's intrinsic stochastic noise ($\sigma^2$ from minibatch sampling). SwitchML reports *no measurable convergence degradation* on ResNet-50, VGG-16, and BERT benchmarks [1]. A 16-bit variant halves bandwidth but risks dynamic-range collapse; the 32-bit design uses minimal extra switch resources (same ALU width) and is the recommended operating point.

**Error feedback synergy.** When combined with aggressive quantization (e.g., 8-bit or 1-bit schemes [5][6]), error feedback (EF) compensates bias: workers accumulate the compression residual $e_{t+1} = g_t + e_t - C(g_t + e_t)$ and add it to the next gradient before compression. The switch remains oblivious — it sums whatever integers arrive — so EF composes cleanly with in-network aggregation. Formally, if the quantizer $C$ is a $\delta$-contractive compressor, EF-SGD converges at the uncompressed rate up to a $O(1/\delta)$ transient [6].

### 4.4 Scaling out: ATP, GRID, and hierarchical aggregation

A single ToR switch serves one rack (up to 64 nodes at 100 Gbps). Data-center training spans racks. **ATP** [2] extends in-network aggregation to two-layer (leaf–spine) topologies:

- **Decentralized aggregator scheduling.** Each switch independently manages a pool of aggregators; workers route gradient fragments to the nearest switch with free capacity (their ToR first, spilling to the spine). No central controller sits on the critical path.
- **Partial aggregation forwarding.** A ToR switch aggregates its local workers' fragments, then forwards the *partial sum* (with its worker bitmap) to the spine switch, which completes the global sum and multicasts down. Bitmaps make partial sums composable and loss-recoverable.
- **Loss resilience.** Retransmitted fragments carry sequence numbers; the switch consults the aggregator bitmap to distinguish "already counted" from "new", forwarding current values downstream instead of double-adding.

**GRID** [3] treats the same problem as *gradient routing*: given switch load/capacity ratios, it solves an optimization (via PuLP) that assigns each worker's fragments to aggregation switches minimizing max link utilization — a traffic-engineering view of in-network aggregation. **SHARP** [4] takes the fixed-function route: InfiniBand switches with dedicated reduction trees and FPUs, achieving single-digit-microsecond allreduce latency but requiring an InfiniBand fabric and new ASIC generations to evolve.

| System | Fabric | Aggregation scope | Arithmetic | Fault model | Reported gain |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| SwitchML [1] | Ethernet (P4) | Single rack | int32 + host scaling | Shadow copies, retransmit | 5.5× E2E, 2.9× vs NCCL |
| ATP [2] | Ethernet (P4) | Multi-rack (leaf–spine) | int + bitmaps | Bitmap retransmission | 2–3× vs PS baselines |
| GRID [3] | Ethernet (P4/Tofino) | Multi-switch routing | int32 | Re-routing | ≥3 Gbps min gradient rate |
| SHARP [4] | InfiniBand | Fabric-wide trees | Fixed FPUs | Subnet manager | 2.1× latency (8 B, 128 hosts) |

### 4.5 Line-rate summation at 100 Gbps: pipeline mapping

The claim "line rate" is a hardware claim and must be discharged against the RMT pipeline. Consider a 100 Gbps port, 1500 B packets: ~8.3 Mpps per port; a 32-port switch processes ~266 Mpps. At a 1 GHz pipeline clock this is one packet per ~4 clocks per port — comfortable headroom. The aggregation data path per packet is:

1. **Parse:** extract `(idx, off, vector)` — vector is *k* int32s; *k* = 32 for a 128 B payload, keeping headers under ~200 B.
2. **Stage 1–2:** register read of `pool[idx].value` (SRAM read, 1 stage per array bank; striping *k* elements across banks parallelizes this).
3. **Stage 3:** stateful ALU add of vector into the register; increment count.
4. **Stage 4:** compare count == *n*; on match, rewrite packet payload with the sum, zero the slot, and set the multicast group; else drop.

Four stages of ~12, leaving capacity for the switch's primary forwarding function — the hard requirement that aggregation *not* degrade normal traffic [1]. SRAM budget: a pool of *s* = 1024 slots × 32 int32s × 4 B ≈ 128 KB per bank-striped array — under 1% of a Tofino's SRAM. The binding constraint is not memory but **stage occupancy** and the atomicity of read-modify-write on the counter, which the stateful ALU provides natively.

## 5. Empirical Evaluation and Formal Proofs

### 5.1 Throughput and speedup synthesis

SwitchML's evaluation on an 8-GPU cluster with 10/100 Gbps networks across 8 DNN benchmarks (ResNet-50, VGG-16, AlexNet, BERT, …) reports [1]:

- **Up to 5.5× end-to-end training throughput** vs. baseline collectives on communication-bound models.
- **≥20% improvement** on every benchmarked model, including compute-bound ones where overlap hides most communication.
- **2.9× faster than NCCL** on a communication microbenchmark — the cleanest measure of the collective itself.

ATP shows 2–3× over parameter-server baselines [2]; SHARP cuts 8-byte allreduce latency from 6.01 μs to 2.83 μs on 128 hosts [4].

### 5.2 Convergence under inexact aggregation

Let the switch compute $\hat{s}_t = \sum_i Q(g_{t,i})$ where $Q$ is the int32 rounding quantizer with per-element error bounded by $\varepsilon = s \cdot 2^{-32}$.

> **Theorem:** *Assume each $F_i$ is $L$-smooth, stochastic gradients have variance $\sigma^2$, and $\|g_t\|_\infty \le G$. Then SGD with in-network integer aggregation and step size $\eta \le 1/L$ satisfies*
>
> $$\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1} \mathbb{E}\|\nabla F(x_t)\|^2 \le \frac{2(F(x_0)-F^\star)}{\eta T} + \eta L\left(\frac{\sigma^2}{n} + d\,\varepsilon^2\right).$$
>
> *Proof sketch:* Decompose the update into the exact mean gradient plus zero-mean stochastic noise plus deterministic quantization error bounded by $\sqrt{d}\,\varepsilon$. Standard smooth non-convex analysis absorbs the deterministic term into the $O(\eta)$ residual. With $\varepsilon \approx 2.3\times 10^{-10} G$, the $d\varepsilon^2$ term is ~20 orders of magnitude below $\sigma^2/n$ for practical $d$. ∎

In plain terms: **the integer dataplane is provably irrelevant to convergence**. The dominant error remains minibatch noise — which is why SwitchML's measured accuracy curves are indistinguishable from FP32 baselines [1].

### 5.3 Worked example: packet-level arithmetic

Consider *n* = 8 workers, chunk size *k* = 32 int32s, pool slot 7 assigned to tensor offset 0x1000, scale $s = 0.03125$:

```python
# Host-side quantization (per worker i)
scale = 0.03125
ints_i = [round(x * (2**31) / scale) for x in grad_chunk_i]  # int32 vector
packet = {"idx": 7, "off": 0x1000, "gen": 42, "vector": ints_i}
send(packet)

# Switch dataplane (P4-ish pseudocode, runs at line rate)
# stateful ALU: pool_value[7] += packet.vector  (32 parallel int32 adds)
# stateful ALU: pool_count[7] += 1
# if pool_count[7] == 8: emit multicast with pool_value[7]; reset slot
```

```rust
// Host-side dequantization on receiving the aggregated packet
let scale: f64 = 0.03125;
let n = 8usize;
let mean_grad: Vec<f64> = agg_vector.iter()
    .map(|&v| (v as f64) * scale / (2f64.powi(31) * n as f64))
    .collect();
```

Rounding error per element: at most $0.5 \times s / 2^{31} \approx 7.3 \times 10^{-12}$ — six orders of magnitude below FP32's own $1.2 \times 10^{-7}$ relative precision at unit magnitude. The switch's integer sum is *exact*; all approximation lives in the host-side rounding, exactly as the theorem assumes.

## 6. Limitations and Open Problems

1. **Integer-only dataplanes.** Every design surveyed works around the absence of floating-point ALUs. Adaptive scaling solves it for summation, but *nonlinear* reductions (e.g., quantized max for sparse allreduce, approximate top-k) cannot be expressed. Next-generation pipelines with FP16 ALUs would unlock in-network compression, not just summation.
2. **Single administrative domain.** SwitchML assumes one rack under one operator; ATP assumes a cooperating fabric. In multi-tenant clouds, aggregator pools are a shared, contended resource — noisy-neighbor and isolation semantics are unsolved. GRID's routing optimization [3] is a first step but assumes cooperative tenants.
3. **Failure semantics at scale.** Shadow copies handle single-switch failure, but correlated failures (power, firmware) during a 10,000-GPU run remain unaddressed. Checkpoint-coordinated recovery of in-flight aggregations is an open protocol problem.
4. **Stragglers.** In-network aggregation is barrier-like: the sum completes when the *slowest* worker's chunk arrives. Ring allreduce pipelines around stragglers more gracefully. 5. **Aggregator placement.** Optimally placing aggregators across a fat-tree under dynamic load is NP-hard in general; ATP uses greedy decentralization and GRID uses LP relaxation [2][3]. 6. **Beyond summation.** Allreduce-mean covers SGD, but second-order methods (K-FAC), mixture-of-experts routing, and federated secure aggregation need richer in-network primitives — a P4 library of *verified* collective kernels does not yet exist.

## 7. Conclusion

In-network aggregation reframes the allreduce from an end-host algorithm into a *network service*: the switch, positioned at the natural convergence point of gradient streams, performs the summation that every worker would otherwise redundantly recompute and retransmit. SwitchML proved the concept practical on commodity programmable switches — 5.5× end-to-end speedups with provably negligible quantization error — by co-designing a streaming pool architecture, integer dataplane arithmetic with adaptive block floating point, and a loss-tolerant transport. ATP and GRID extended it across racks and across switches; SHARP showed the fixed-function endpoint of the same idea. The fundamental result is a volume argument: per-worker traffic drops from ~2|g|·(n−1)/n to 2|g|, the information-theoretic minimum, at line rate, in four pipeline stages, using under 1% of switch SRAM. The remaining frontier is not whether switches can sum — they can — but what *else* the dataplane can be trusted to compute: floating-point reductions, straggler-resilient partial collectives, and multi-tenant isolated aggregation. As models grow and fabrics accelerate, the network-as-coprocessor thesis will only strengthen: the cheapest byte is the one never sent, and the fastest sum is the one computed where the packets already meet.
