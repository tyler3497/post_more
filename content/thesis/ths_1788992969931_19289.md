## Abstract

This thesis develops a unified group-theoretic treatment of geometric deep learning, synthesizing the blueprint of Bronstein et al. [1] with group-equivariant convolutions [2], spherical CNNs on homogeneous spaces [3], and the steerable message-passing architectures of Tensor Field Networks [4] and NequIP [5]. We formalize *equivariance* as the commutation of a learned feature map with a symmetry group's action on its domain, and show how the classical convolution theorem generalizes — via representation theory and fiber-bundle geometry — to homogeneous spaces, Riemannian manifolds, and gauge bundles. The central contribution is a self-contained derivation of the E(3)-equivariant message-passing layer: spherical-harmonic filter bases, Clebsch–Gordan tensor products composing the irreducible representations of SO(3), and the constraint that radial filter components depend only on interatomic distances. We review empirical evidence — rotated MNIST, QM9 molecular properties, and NequIP's thousandfold data-efficiency gains — and discuss principled limitations: symmetry mismatch, discretization artifacts on manifolds, and the computational cost of high-order irreps. Symmetry emerges not as a mere inductive bias but as the organizing principle of modern deep architectures, in the spirit of Klein's Erlangen Programme.

## 1. Introduction

The empirical success of deep learning is, in a precise sense, a triumph of *symmetry*. Convolutional neural networks (CNNs) exploit the translation symmetry of images through weight sharing; the convolution layer is translation-equivariant, so shifting the input and then processing it yields the same result as processing first and shifting the feature maps afterward. This property — equivariance, Φ(T·x) = T·Φ(x) — permits statistical strength to be shared across spatial locations, defeating the curse of dimensionality that would otherwise doom learning in high-dimensional pixel space [1].

Yet translations form only one entry in a vast bestiary of symmetries. Molecular configurations live in three-dimensional Euclidean space and carry the full Euclidean group E(3) of rotations and translations; natural images additionally admit planar rotations and reflections; climate data is naturally posed on the sphere S² with symmetry SO(3); and fields on curved surfaces — meshes, protein backbones, vascular networks — are most naturally described as sections of *vector bundles*, where the freedom to choose a local coordinate frame at each point is a *gauge symmetry* [1]. The Geometric Deep Learning (GDL) programme, articulated by Bronstein, Bruna, Cohen, and Veličković in 2021 [1], proposes that all of these architectures are instances of a single blueprint:

> **The GDL Blueprint.** Choose a symmetry group G acting on the input domain Ω. Construct layers that are *G-equivariant*, composed with *G-invariant* pooling and *local* (scale-separating) aggregation. The result is a network whose hypotheses respect, by construction, the symmetries of the data.

This thesis is organized as follows. Section 2 establishes the mathematical background: groups, representations, homogeneous spaces, and fiber bundles. Section 3 presents the methodology — the equivariance constraint and how it determines the admissible linear layers via representation theory. Section 4, the deep dive, works through four concrete realizations: group-equivariant CNNs on discrete groups [2], spherical convolutions on homogeneous spaces [3], the gauge-equivariant formulation on manifolds, and E(3)-equivariant message passing culminating in Tensor Field Networks [4] and NequIP [5]. Section 5 discusses empirical results and theoretical guarantees; Section 6, limitations; and Section 7 concludes.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 Groups and Representations

A *group* (G, ·) is a set equipped with an associative composition, an identity element e, and inverses. A *representation* of G is a homomorphism ρ : G → GL(V), mapping group elements to invertible linear operators on a vector space V, such that ρ(gh) = ρ(g)ρ(h). In deep learning, groups act on data: translations act on images, rotations act on point clouds, and permutations act on sets.

A map Φ : X → Y between spaces carrying group actions (g·x on X, g·y on Y) is **equivariant** if

$$\Phi(g \cdot x) = g \cdot \Phi(x) \quad \forall g \in G, \; x \in X,$$

i.e., the following diagram commutes. When the action on Y is trivial, Φ is **invariant**: Φ(g·x) = Φ(x). Invariance discards the pose information; equivariance *preserves* it in a structured way, allowing downstream layers to consume it [1].

### 2.2 The Five Geometries

Bronstein et al. [1] classify geometric domains into the "5 Gs", each pairing a domain with its symmetry group and natural signal type:

| Domain (G) | Geometry | Symmetry group | Signal type | Canonical architecture |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Grids** | Euclidean space ℝᵈ | Translations T(d) | Images, volumes | CNNs |
| **Groups** | Group G itself | Left action of G | Functions on G | Group-equivariant CNNs [2] |
| **Graphs** | Graph 𝒢 | Automorphisms Aut(𝒢) | Node/edge features | GNNs, Deep Sets |
| **Geodesics** | Manifold ℳ | Isometries Isom(ℳ) | Geodesic signals | Intrinsic mesh CNNs |
| **Gauges** | Vector bundle E → ℳ | Gauge transformations | Sections of E | Gauge-equivariant CNNs |

This taxonomy is not merely descriptive: each row determines the algebraic constraints on admissible layers, as Section 3 shows.

### 2.3 Harmonic Analysis and Irreducible Representations

The *Peter–Weyl theorem* states that the square-integrable functions on a compact group G decompose into a direct sum of its irreducible representations (irreps):

$$L^2(G) \cong \bigoplus_{\pi \in \widehat{G}} \dim(\pi) \cdot V_\pi.$$

For the rotation group SO(3), the irreps are the familiar Wigner D-matrices of dimension 2ℓ+1, with basis functions the *spherical harmonics* Yₘ^ℓ. This decomposition is the engine of all steerable architectures: a filter is expanded in an irrep basis, and equivariance then reduces to *algebraic* constraints on the expansion coefficients [4].

### 2.4 Fiber Bundles and Gauges

A *fiber bundle* π : E → ℳ assigns to each point x ∈ ℳ of a manifold a fiber Eₓ (e.g., a tangent space). A *gauge* is a smooth choice of local frame in each fiber; a *gauge transformation* changes the frames. Physical quantities (tangent vectors, electromagnetic potentials) are described by *sections* of the bundle, and their coordinate representations transform under gauge change by the *representation* of the structure group. Gauge-equivariant networks demand that processing commutes with *arbitrary* local frame changes — a far stronger requirement than global equivariance — and this is precisely the setting of mesh CNNs and the general theory of Cohen, Weiler, and co-workers [1].

## 3. Methodology

### 3.1 The Equivariance Constraint Determines the Layers

The methodological core of GDL is a derivation rather than an invention. Suppose we seek a *linear* map Φ : L²(Ω) → L²(Ω) equivariant to a group G acting transitively on Ω (a homogeneous space). A classical theorem — the *convolution theorem for homogeneous spaces*, developed in the generality of Kondor, Trivedi, and Cohen [1] — states that every such linear map is a *group convolution* with a G-steerable kernel:

$$(\Phi f)(x) = \int_G k(g^{-1} x) \, f(g) \, d\mu(g),$$

where the kernel k must satisfy the *steerability constraint*

$$k(h x) = \rho_{\text{out}}(h) \, k(x) \, \rho_{\text{in}}(h)^{-1} \quad \forall h \in H,$$

with H the stabilizer subgroup of a reference point, and ρin, ρout the input/output representations. This single equation is the design rule of geometric deep learning: *the kernel's transformation law is dictated by the input and output types*.

### 3.2 Blueprint Construction Recipe

Concretely, the GDL blueprint prescribes the following steps [1]:

1. **Identify the domain Ω and its symmetry group G.** The data's natural invariances (translation, rotation, permutation, gauge) fix this choice.
2. **Select input/output feature types** as representations of G (scalars = trivial irrep, vectors = ℓ=1 irrep, tensors = higher irreps).
3. **Solve the steerability constraint** for the kernel basis — typically by expanding k in spherical harmonics or Wigner D-functions and projecting onto the admissible subspace.
4. **Compose**: interleave equivariant linear layers with pointwise nonlinearities (which commute with any group action applied channel-wise) and local aggregation (neighborhood sums, which preserve equivariance when the neighborhood is group-invariant, e.g., radial cutoffs).
5. **Pool invariantly** at the end (global sums, norms of irreps) when the task demands an invariant output.

> **Theorem (Equivariance closure):** The composition of G-equivariant maps is G-equivariant, and any pointwise nonlinearity σ applied identically to each fiber is G-equivariant. Hence a network built from equivariant linear layers, pointwise nonlinearities, and group-invariant neighborhoods is equivariant end to end [1].

### 3.3 From Blueprint to Algorithm

The blueprint is *constructive*: given G and feature types, the kernel basis can be computed in closed form. For the Euclidean group E(3) = ℝ³ ⋊ SO(3) with features in irreps of SO(3), the constraint forces the convolution kernel to factor as a radial function of ‖r‖ times a spherical harmonic of the direction r̂, composed via the Clebsch–Gordan tensor product — the mathematical core of Tensor Field Networks [4], detailed in Section 4.4.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Group-Equivariant CNNs: Lifting the Signal to the Group

Cohen and Welling's *G-CNNs* [2] generalize planar convolution from translations to any discrete group G (e.g., p4: translations plus 90° rotations and reflections). The first layer lifts a planar image f : ℤ² → ℝ to a function on the group,

$$(f \star \psi)(g) = \sum_{y \in \mathbb{Z}^2} f(y) \, \psi(g^{-1} y),$$

and subsequent layers convolve *functions on G with filters on G*:

$$(f \star_G \psi)(g) = \sum_{h \in G} f(h) \, \psi(g^{-1} h).$$

Because the summation ranges over the whole group, the output transforms as $(g \cdot f) \star \psi = g \cdot (f \star \psi)$ — exact equivariance, with negligible computational overhead for discrete groups since G-convolution decomposes into ordinary convolutions over cosets [2]. Empirically, p4-equivariant CNNs achieved state of the art on rotated MNIST and improved CIFAR-10 baselines without augmentation [2]. The conceptual leap is profound: *the feature maps themselves live on the symmetry group*, so pose information is retained in group coordinates rather than discarded.

### 4.2 Spherical CNNs: Convolution on the Homogeneous Space S²

The sphere S² = SO(3)/SO(2) is a homogeneous space of the rotation group. Cohen et al. [3] defined the spherical cross-correlation between a spherical signal f : S² → ℝᴷ and a filter ψ,

$$(f \star \psi)(R) = \int_{S^2} \sum_k f_k(x) \, \psi_k(R^{-1} x) \, dx, \quad R \in SO(3),$$

producing a *function on SO(3)* — again the "lift to the group" pattern. The Fourier transform on SO(3) expands functions in Wigner D-matrices,

$$\hat{f}^\ell_{mn} = \int_{SO(3)} f(R) \, \overline{D^\ell_{mn}(R)} \, dR,$$

and the convolution theorem becomes *blockwise matrix multiplication* in Fourier space, enabling fast implementations via generalized FFTs. On the synthetic spherical MNIST task — digits projected onto the sphere and classified under arbitrary 3D rotation — spherical CNNs dramatically outperformed planar CNNs, which fail catastrophically under out-of-plane rotation [3]. The sphere is the canonical *curved* homogeneous space, and this construction is the bridge from flat G-CNNs to manifold architectures.

### 4.3 Gauge Equivariance: Convolutions on Bundles

On a general Riemannian manifold ℳ (a mesh, a cortical surface, a protein), there is no global group action — the isometry group may be trivial. The relevant symmetry is instead *gauge freedom*: at each point x ∈ ℳ we may choose any orthonormal frame of the tangent space Tₓℳ, and physical signals (e.g., directional curvature descriptors) are represented as vectors in these frames. A gauge transformation g : ℳ → SO(d) changes all frames simultaneously, and a network layer is *gauge-equivariant* if its output transforms by the output representation at every point [1].

The gauge-equivariant convolution (realized in architectures such as GemCNN) takes the form

$$f_{\text{out}}(x) = \int_{T_x\mathcal{M}} k(v) \, \rho_{\text{in}}(g_{x \leftarrow y}) \, f_{\text{in}}(y) \, dv,$$

where the *parallel transport* $g_{x \leftarrow y}$ along the geodesic from y to x aligns the frames before the kernel k — itself constrained by the steerability equation of Section 3.1 — is applied. This is the *most general* formulation in the GDL taxonomy: global equivariance (Section 4.1–4.2) is recovered when the manifold admits a transitive isometry group and a global gauge is fixed [1]. The price of generality is computational: parallel transport on meshes requires geodesic computations, and discretization breaks exact equivariance — a limitation we return to in Section 6.

### 4.4 E(3)-Equivariant Message Passing: Tensor Field Networks and NequIP

The most consequential realization of the blueprint operates on 3D point clouds — molecules, materials, particle systems — with the Euclidean group E(3). *Tensor Field Networks* (TFNs) [4] define, at each layer, a point convolution whose filter is built from spherical harmonics:

$$W^{\ell k}(\mathbf{r}) = \sum_{J=|k-\ell|}^{k+\ell} \varphi^{\ell k}_J(\|\mathbf{r}\|) \sum_{m=-J}^{J} Y^J_m(\hat{\mathbf{r}}) \, Q^{\ell k}_{Jm},$$

where φ are *learned radial functions* (MLPs of the distance), $Y^J_m$ are spherical harmonics of the direction, and $Q^{\ell k}_{Jm}$ are the Clebsch–Gordan coefficients coupling input irrep k and filter irrep J into output irrep ℓ. The layer update,

$$\mathcal{L}^{\ell}_a = \sum_{b \in \mathcal{N}(a)} W^{\ell k}(\mathbf{r}_{ab}) \otimes_{cg} V^{k}_b,$$

sums over neighbors within a radial cutoff — a group-invariant neighborhood — guaranteeing E(3)-equivariance: rotations of the input rotate all tensor features by the corresponding Wigner D-matrices, while the radial functions, depending only on distances, are unaffected [4]. Pointwise nonlinearities must respect the irrep structure (e.g., norm-based gating), since a naïve ReLU on tensor components would break equivariance.

*NequIP* [5] industrialized this construction for interatomic potentials. Where invariant models (SchNet and successors) featurize environments with scalar descriptors, NequIP propagates *geometric tensors* — vectors, quadrupoles — through SE(3)-equivariant convolutions, yielding a far more information-rich representation of atomic environments. The headline result: NequIP matched or exceeded state-of-the-art accuracy on MD17, QM9, and reactive systems with up to *three orders of magnitude less training data* [5], published in *Nature Communications* (2022). The total energy is predicted as an invariant scalar; forces follow as $F = -\nabla E$, automatically conservative and equivariant. This is the blueprint's promise made concrete: *symmetry buys data efficiency*, because the hypothesis class need not learn what the group action already dictates.

A minimal illustration of the irreps machinery, using the e3nn library [6]:

```python
import e3nn.o3 as o3
import torch

# Irreducible representations: 1 scalar (l=0), 1 vector (l=1)

irreps_out = o3.Irreps("1x0e + 1x1o + 1x2e")

# Equivariant linear layer: only CG-allowed paths survive
tp = o3.FullyConnectedTensorProduct(irreps_in, "1x1o", irreps_out)
print(tp)  # weights exist only on Clebsch-Gordan-compatible paths
```

## 5. Empirical Results and Theoretical Guarantees

**Sample complexity.** The theoretical claim — equivariance reduces sample complexity — rests on classical generalization bounds: constraining the hypothesis class to G-equivariant functions shrinks its covering number by a factor exponential in the "size" of the group relative to the domain [1]. Empirically:

- **Rotated MNIST / CIFAR-10:** p4 G-CNNs [2] set the state of the art on rotated MNIST (error ≈ 2.3%) and beat augmentation-trained baselines on CIFAR-10, demonstrating that *architectural* equivariance outperforms *data-augmentation* equivariance when the symmetry is exact.
- **Spherical MNIST:** spherical CNNs [3] achieved near-perfect accuracy under arbitrary SO(3) rotations where planar CNNs degraded to chance — a clean experimental isolation of the value of the correct symmetry group.
- **QM9 / MD17:** TFNs [4] learned molecular energies from small training sets; NequIP [5] reduced data requirements by up to 1000× relative to invariant baselines at matched accuracy, directly validating the data-efficiency thesis on production molecular-dynamics workloads.

**Universality.** A natural question: does the equivariance constraint cost expressivity? For compact groups, *equivariant universal approximation* theorems (e.g., via symmetrization of universal approximators, or the completeness of steerable kernel bases) show that G-equivariant networks are universal *within the class of G-equivariant functions* [1]. The constraint discards only hypotheses that violate the symmetry — precisely the hypotheses we have prior reason to exclude.

**Exactness vs. discretization.** On grids and discrete groups, equivariance is exact (up to boundary effects). On the sphere with band-limited filters, equivariance holds up to the bandlimit; on meshes, parallel-transport discretization introduces gauge-equivariance error that vanishes under refinement [1].

## 6. Limitations

1. **Symmetry mismatch.** Exact equivariance is a *hard* constraint. If the true data-generating process breaks the assumed symmetry — molecules in external fields, images with a canonical "up" — the model cannot fit the asymmetry, and performance degrades relative to unconstrained baselines. *Approximately* equivariant or symmetry-breaking extensions (e.g., learned canonicalization, relaxed steerability) remain an active frontier [1].
2. **Computational cost of irreps.** Clebsch–Gordan tensor products scale as O(L⁶) in the maximum angular momentum L; practical models cap L ≤ 3–4. High-frequency directional information is therefore expensive, and invariant baselines remain cheaper per flop — the data-efficiency gain must be weighed against inference cost [5].
3. **Discretization artifacts.** Gauge-equivariant mesh CNNs inherit the mesh's discretization; exact equivariance holds only in the continuum limit. Quadrature on S² and radial basis truncation similarly introduce small equivariance violations that can accumulate in deep stacks [1, 3].
4. **Group must be known a priori.** The entire framework is *prescriptive*: the symmetry group is specified by the designer. Discovering symmetries from data — the *descriptive* counterpart — lies outside the blueprint and connects to causal representation learning and symmetry-discovery methods.
5. **Nonlinearity design.** Pointwise nonlinearities on tensor features require care (norm gating, tensor-product activations); the elegant simplicity of ReLU does not transfer, complicating optimization and architecture search [4, 6].

## 7. Conclusion

Geometric deep learning reframes architecture design as *applied representation theory*. The convolution — long understood as translation-equivariant weight sharing — is the special case of a general construction: linear layers constrained by the steerability equation, composed with pointwise nonlinearities and invariant pooling, on domains classified by their symmetries. From G-CNNs on discrete groups [2] through spherical convolutions [3], gauge-equivariant message passing on bundles, to the E(3)-equivariant tensor networks that now drive *ab initio* molecular dynamics [4, 5], the trajectory is one of increasing geometric generality purchased with increasingly sophisticated algebra — spherical harmonics, Wigner D-matrices, Clebsch–Gordan coefficients, parallel transport.

The empirical verdict is decisive where the symmetry is exact: orders-of-magnitude gains in data efficiency, robustness to pose variation without augmentation, and physically consistent predictions (conservative forces, covariant tensors) *by construction*. The open challenges — symmetry mismatch, the cost of high-order irreps, discretization, and symmetry discovery — define the next decade of the programme. In Klein's spirit: *geometry is the study of invariants under groups of transformations; deep learning, it turns out, is too*.
