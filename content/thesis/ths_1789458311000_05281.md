# FHEW/TFHE Gate Bootstrapping: Blind Rotation on the Torus, Bootstrapped Boolean Circuits, and the Road to Programmable Functional Bootstrapping

## Abstract

Gate bootstrapping turned the refresh operation of fully homomorphic encryption into the computational primitive itself. We trace the FHEW lineage: Ducas and Micciancio's FHEW evaluates a homomorphic NAND gate and refreshes its output in under a second by running LWE decryption inside a ring-GSW accumulator [1], and Chillotti, Gama, Georgieva and Izabachène's TFHE re-derives the construction over the torus around an asymmetric external product, driving gate latency to about 13 milliseconds and shrinking bootstrapping keys from one gigabyte to 24 megabytes [2][3]. We develop the machinery in full — torus LWE ciphertexts, gadget decomposition, the blind rotation as a homomorphic multiplexer tree, sample extraction and key switching — and show how bootstrapped gates compose into arbitrary Boolean circuits with no depth limit. We then follow the lineage to its natural extension: reprogramming the blind rotation's test polynomial turns the refresh into a lookup-table evaluation, i.e. functional and programmable bootstrapping, which delivers the quantized activations of encrypted neural inference [5][7].

---

## 1 Introduction

Craig Gentry's 2009 breakthrough showed that arbitrary computation on encrypted data is possible, and his *bootstrapping theorem* reduced the problem to a single operation: homomorphically evaluating the decryption circuit to refresh a noisy ciphertext [8]. For the first generation of schemes this refresh was agonizingly slow. The HElib implementation of the Brakerski–Gentry–Vaikuntanathan scheme needed on the order of six minutes to bootstrap a single batch [1], which kept fully homomorphic encryption firmly in the realm of theory.

The decisive break came from Ducas and Micciancio in 2015 [1]. Rather than treating bootstrapping as an occasional, expensive global event, they proposed *gate bootstrapping*: evaluate one binary gate, then immediately refresh the result, so noise never survives beyond a single gate of depth. Their scheme, FHEW, homomorphically computes a NAND gate and refreshes the output in roughly 690 milliseconds — two orders of magnitude faster than HElib. The conceptual shift is as important as the speedup: the NAND gate, refreshed at birth, becomes the *universal computational primitive*, so encrypted computation reduces to evaluating a netlist of bootstrapped gates, with no depth limit and no per-circuit noise analysis.

The core mechanism is a *homomorphic decryption*. An LWE ciphertext (a, b) encrypting a bit x decrypts as b − ⟨a, s⟩. FHEW evaluates this linear function *homomorphically*, inside the accumulator of a ring variant of the GSW cryptosystem, using encryptions of the secret-key bits to steer the computation — the procedure we now call the *blind rotation* [1].

TFHE then re-derived FHEW from cleaner algebraic foundations [2][3]. Chillotti, Gama, Georgieva and Izabachène observed that the internal product of GSW could be replaced by a simpler *external product* between a GSW and an LWE ciphertext. Rewriting FHEW's bootstrapping purely in terms of this external product cut latency from under one second to under one tenth of a second, shrank the bootstrapping key from one gigabyte to 24 megabytes at comparable security, and exposed an *asymmetry* in noise propagation that made the construction composable — with homomorphic automata evaluation, and eventually with functional bootstrapping [2].

This article follows that lineage. Section 2 fixes the torus-based notation: TLWE, TRLWE and TRGSW ciphertexts, gadget decomposition, and the external product. Section 3 presents the gate bootstrapping pipeline as a methodology: modulus switching, blind rotation, sample extraction, and key switching, culminating in the bootstrapped NAND gate. Section 4 dives into the blind rotation as a homomorphic multiplexer tree, the noise asymmetry of the external product, the composition of bootstrapped gates into arbitrary Boolean circuits, and finally the extension to functional bootstrapping. Section 5 reports empirical benchmarks, Section 6 discusses limitations, and Section 7 concludes.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Torus LWE and its ring variants

Let **T** denote the real torus **R**/**Z**, the additive group of reals modulo one. The TFHE lineage works natively over **T** rather than over a finite ring **Z**_q, which lets message encodings and noise live in the same continuous space. A *TLWE* ciphertext encrypting a message μ in **T** under a secret key s in {0,1}^n is a pair

> **Definition 1 (TLWE).** A TLWE sample is c = (a, b) with a uniform in **T**^n and b = ⟨a, s⟩ + e + μ in **T**, where e is a small Gaussian error.

Decryption computes the *phase* φ(c) = b − ⟨a, s⟩ = μ + e and rounds to the nearest valid codeword. For binary messages, μ is chosen in {0, 1/2}: the two codewords sit at opposite points of the torus and tolerate error up to magnitude one quarter [2][3].

The ring analogues lift everything to R = **Z**[X]/(X^N + 1) with N a power of two. A *TRLWE* ciphertext is (a, b) with b = ⟨a, S⟩ + E + μ over the torus polynomials **T**_N[X], and a *TRGSW* ciphertext is a vector of ℓ TRLWE samples encrypting gadget multiples of μ — the torus generalization of the GSW construction [2][4].

| Ciphertext | Plaintext space | Ciphertext space | Secret key space | Role |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| TLWE | **T** | **T**^{n+1} | {0,1}^n | Gate inputs and outputs |
| TRLWE | **T**_N[X] | **T**_N[X]^{k+1} | {0,1}_N[X]^k | Blind-rotation accumulator |
| TRGSW | **Z**_N[X] | vector of TRLWE | {0,1}_N[X]^k | Bootstrapping key material |

### 2.2 Gadget decomposition and the external product

Fix a base B_g and a decomposition length ℓ. The *gadget decomposition* writes any torus element as a signed-digit expansion in powers of the inverse base, introducing a controlled rounding error. The *external product* ⊡ takes a TRGSW ciphertext A of message μ_A and a TRLWE ciphertext b of message μ_b and returns a TRLWE ciphertext of the product μ_A · μ_b [2]:

> **Theorem 1 (External product).** Let A be a TRGSW encryption of μ_A in **Z**_N[X] and b a TRLWE encryption of μ_b in **T**_N[X]. Then A ⊡ b is a TRLWE encryption of μ_A · μ_b, with error variance bounded by a term linear in Var(Err(b)) scaled by the squared norm of μ_A, plus an additive decomposition term independent of μ_b.

The decisive feature is the *asymmetry*: when the TRGSW message has small norm — as it does for an encryption of a single secret-key bit — noise grows essentially *linearly* in the TRLWE operand's noise [2][4]. Chaining n external products therefore grows noise additively, not multiplicatively, which is exactly the regime in which gate-by-gate bootstrapping is affordable.

### 2.3 The NAND gate as universal primitive

Every Boolean function can be expressed with NAND gates alone. Gate bootstrapping exploits this to collapse the distinction between *computing* and *refreshing*: the homomorphic NAND gate of FHEW takes two TLWE ciphertexts, forms the linear combination corresponding to NAND, and bootstraps the result before the noise of even one gate can matter [1]. The TFHE library sustained roughly 50 such gates per second per core, with the multiplexer gate taking about 26 milliseconds [2]. Encrypted computation is just a netlist — no moduli chains, no depth bookkeeping.

## 3 Methodology

The gate bootstrapping pipeline refreshes a noisy TLWE ciphertext in four stages.

### Step 1 — Modulus switching to a small discrete space

The input TLWE ciphertext lives over the continuous torus. Rounding maps it to the discrete ring **Z**_{2N}: b̄ = round(2N·b) and ā_i = round(2N·a_i), turning the phase φ = b − ⟨a, s⟩ into an integer exponent φ̄ up to rounding drift. The exponent space is chosen deliberately: multiplication by X^{φ̄} in **T**_N[X] rotates polynomial coefficients, and the negacyclic relation X^N = −1 turns the second half of the rotation range into negated copies of the first half [2].

### Step 2 — Blind rotation: homomorphic decryption

An accumulator ACC is initialized as the TRLWE encryption of (0, testv), where testv is a *test polynomial* in **T**_N[X]. Given the bootstrapping key BK = (BK_1, …, BK_n) — TRGSW encryptions of the LWE secret bits s_i under a ring key S — the blind rotation computes

> **Theorem 2 (Blind rotation).** Iterating ACC ← BK_i ⊡ (X^{ā_i} · ACC) + (1 − BK_i) ⊡ ACC for i from 1 to n yields a TRLWE encryption of X^{φ̄} · testv, where φ̄ = b̄ − Σ_i ā_i s_i approximates 2N·(μ + e).

In words, the encrypted secret key selects, bit by bit, whether the accumulator is rotated by X^{ā_i}; the composition of all selections rotates it by the encrypted phase itself [1][2] — a homomorphic evaluation of the LWE decryption function, performed *inside the exponent of X*.

### Step 3 — Sample extraction

The rotated accumulator encrypts the polynomial X^{φ̄} · testv. *SampleExtract* pulls out the constant coefficient as a TLWE ciphertext under the ring key S [2][3]. For gate bootstrapping, testv = (1 + X + ⋯ + X^{N−1}) · X^{N/2} · m̂ encodes a step function: after rotation by the encrypted phase, the constant coefficient equals +m̂ or −m̂ depending on the input bit, so extraction returns a TLWE encryption of a signed multiple of the gate's logical output.

### Step 4 — Key switching and the bootstrapped NAND

A key-switching key translates the TLWE ciphertext from the ring key S back to the original LWE key s, returning a fresh ciphertext with fixed, small noise [1][2]. To build the NAND gate, the evaluator first forms the TLWE linear combination c = (0, 5/8) − c_1 − c_2 for input bit-ciphertexts c_1, c_2 — an unrefreshed encryption of NAND(x_1, x_2) with one gate of noise — and then runs the four-stage pipeline on c. The output is a *fresh* encryption of the NAND result, ready to feed the next gate. Bootstrapped AND, OR, XOR and NOT gates are obtained by varying the initial linear combination; the MUX gate composes two bootstrappings [2].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The blind rotation as a homomorphic multiplexer tree

Each blind-rotation iteration is a *controlled multiplexer* on ciphertexts: BK_i encrypts the bit s_i, and the update selects between the rotated and unrotated accumulator according to that bit [2]. Unrolled, the n iterations form a binary selection tree evaluated *homomorphically* — no party ever learns which rotation was taken. The total cost is n external products, each dominated by ℓ forward NTTs over the anticyclic ring, which is why TFHE implementations invest in a dedicated FFT for **Z**[X]/(X^N + 1) with AVX and FMA vectorization [2]. The rounding drift from modulus switching must stay below half a coefficient slot, which couples the ring dimension N, the decomposition parameters, and the input noise [3].

### 4.2 The external product and the asymmetry of noise propagation

The internal product of GSW multiplies two matrix ciphertexts and incurs noise quadratic in the operands' magnitudes. The external product ⊡ instead multiplies a TRGSW ciphertext by a TRLWE ciphertext, and its noise bound reveals a striking asymmetry: the TRGSW operand's error is amplified by the decomposition length and ring dimension, while the TRLWE operand's error is scaled only by the squared norm of the TRGSW message [2][4].

Consequences reach beyond bootstrapping. *Deterministic automata* can be evaluated homomorphically with noise overhead only linear in the input length, because each transition is one external product by a small-norm TRGSW ciphertext [2]. The Chimera framework [4] exploits the same asymmetry to move ciphertexts between the B/FV, TFHE and HEAAN representations over a unified torus plaintext space, choosing per operation the representation with the best noise profile — and using these bridges to predict the accuracy impact of homomorphic noise on deep learning models before any expensive encrypted evaluation [4].

### 4.3 Bootstrapped Boolean circuits: the gate as computer

With the bootstrapped NAND in hand, encrypted computation needs nothing else. The evaluator walks the circuit in topological order; for each gate it forms the prescribed linear combination of its input TLWE ciphertexts and bootstraps the result. Because every gate output is fresh, noise never compounds across depth — a million-gate circuit is exactly as feasible, noise-wise, as a ten-gate one [1][2]. This is the sharp contrast with leveled schemes, where parameters must be provisioned for the maximum multiplicative depth in advance.

Two refinements make the model practical. First, *circuit bootstrapping* converts a TLWE ciphertext back into a TRGSW ciphertext encrypting the same bit, at the cost of a handful of bootstrappings [2][3]; this closes the loop for constructions that need TRGSW inputs downstream, such as feeding a computed bit back into another blind rotation as a selector. Second, the TFHE scheme supports *leveled* operations between bootstraps — linear combinations and a bounded number of external products — so shallow subcircuits can be evaluated without paying a refresh per gate, with bootstrapping reserved for depth cut points [3].

> **Theorem 3 (Gate-bootstrapped circuit evaluation).** Let C be a Boolean circuit of arbitrary depth over bootstrapped NAND gates with per-gate noise bounded by a fixed constant after each refresh. Then homomorphic evaluation of C returns correct decryptions with failure probability bounded by the per-gate failure probability times the number of gates, independent of the circuit's depth.

The theorem's content is the absence of depth dependence: bootstrapping converts a depth-bounded primitive into an unbounded one [1][2].

### 4.4 From gate bootstrapping to functional bootstrapping

Here the lineage takes its most consequential turn. The blind rotation computes X^{φ̄} · testv for *any* test polynomial. Gate bootstrapping uses a step-function testv to recover a multiple of the input bit — but nothing forces this choice. Reprogramming the coefficients of testv to encode an arbitrary lookup table makes the very same rotation *evaluate the table at the encrypted input* while refreshing the noise [5]:

```python
def program_test_polynomial(f, domain, encode, N):
    # the second half follows by negacyclicity: testv[i+N] = -testv[i]
    coeffs = [encode(f(x)) for x in domain]
    coeffs += [negate(c) for c in coeffs]
    return build_polynomial(coeffs[:N])

def programmable_bootstrap(ct, f, bootstrapping_key):
    ct_q = modulus_switch(ct, 2 * N)
    testv = program_test_polynomial(f, DOMAIN, encode, N)
    acc = blind_rotate(testv, ct_q, bootstrapping_key)
    out = sample_extract(acc, position=0)
    return key_switch(out)
```

This is *programmable bootstrapping* (PBS), also called *functional bootstrapping*: the refresh is fused with the evaluation of a univariate function at no extra cost [5]. The constraint is *negacyclicity* — because X^N = −1, only antiperiodic tables fit in one test polynomial, which in the original gate bootstrapping manifests as a sacrificed *padding bit* [5]. Arbitrary tables need domain splitting or the generalized bootstrapping of Chillotti, Ligier, Orfila and Tap, whose *without-padding* PBS removes the padding bit entirely and evaluates several lookup tables per bootstrap at small precision [6].

*Multi-value* functional bootstrapping packs several output bits into one PBS by encoding multiple functions into different coefficient positions [5]. And the 2019 work of Boura, Gama, Georgieva and Jetchev [4] situated functional bootstrapping inside a unified framework covering the B/FV and HEAAN schemes as well, with noise analyses precise enough to *simulate* homomorphic noise on neural network accuracy in plaintext — justifying smaller, faster TFHE parameter sets for encrypted inference [4].

## 5 Empirical Results

### 5.1 Gate bootstrapping benchmarks

The table compares the generations of gate bootstrapping on comparable hardware classes, as reported in the original publications.

| Scheme | Bootstrapping latency | Bootstrapping key size | Security level |
|---|---|---|---|
| FHEW [1] | about 690 ms | about 1 GB | lattice-based, 128-bit class |
| TFHE [2] | under 100 ms (about 13 ms per binary gate) | 24 MB | 110-bit (library default parameters) |
| TFHE journal parameters [3] | tens of ms | tens of MB | 128-bit |

The fifty-fold speedup from FHEW to TFHE at the gate level came from three compounding improvements: the external product replacing the GSW internal product, approximate rather than exact gadget decomposition, and a hand-tuned FFT for the anticyclic ring [2]. The TFHE library evaluated netlists at roughly 50 gates per second per core, the MUX gate taking about 26 milliseconds [2]. Modern Rust implementations in the tfhe-rs lineage push a single programmable bootstrap into the low milliseconds on commodity CPUs [7].

### 5.2 The extension to encrypted neural inference

Programmable bootstrapping turns the gate-bootstrapping engine toward arithmetic. The paper of Chillotti, Joye and Paillier [5] introduced a *discretized* variant of TFHE for quantized networks: linear layers use leveled integer arithmetic, and every nonlinear activation — the sign function of binarized networks, quantized ReLU, or FHE-friendly approximations — is delivered by one PBS, which simultaneously refreshes the noise of the linear layer.

Concretely, their construction targets discretized networks whose signals are restricted to small integer sets, with the sign function as the canonical activation [5]. A single PBS maps each neuron's pre-activation through the activation lookup table and returns a fresh low-noise ciphertext. The reported experiments reached plaintext-comparable accuracy on MNIST-scale benchmarks with end-to-end inference on the order of minutes on a single machine [5].

```rust
// Illustrative pattern (not a verbatim library API): one PBS per activation.
fn dense_layer(ct: &FheCiphertext, weights: &Weights) -> FheCiphertext {
    let preact = ct.linear_combine(weights);   // leveled integer arithmetic
    // PBS: refresh noise AND apply the quantized activation as a LUT.
    preact.programmable_bootstrap(&ACTIVATION_LUT)
}
```

Complementary evidence comes from the simulation methodology of Boura et al. [4], who injected calibrated noise into plaintext networks and found classification accuracy degrading gracefully, and from recent training work [7], which observes that PBS cost grows steeply with the bit width of the encrypted integers. The engineering lesson is consistent: *keep precision small, quantize aggressively, and let one PBS per activation do the nonlinear work*. Production toolchains follow exactly this discipline — Zama's Concrete library exposes PBS as the primitive for non-linear functions over encrypted integers, and ConcreteML compiles quantized models down to circuits where every activation is a PBS [5][7].

## 6 Limitations

**The negacyclic straitjacket.** Only antiperiodic tables fit in a single test polynomial. Functions that violate the symmetry need domain splitting, padding tricks, or the generalized bootstrapping of [6] — each adding latency or parameter cost.

**Precision walls.** PBS latency grows steeply with plaintext bit width [7]. Realistic parameters support 4 to 8 bits of precision per bootstrap; pushing further demands the larger-precision machinery of [6]. Applications must be *designed* around quantization.

**Latency for deep circuits.** Even at milliseconds per gate, large netlists need minutes per evaluation. Throughput scales with parallelism — independent gates batch well across cores and GPUs — but interactive latencies remain out of reach for very large circuits.

**Key material and memory.** Bootstrapping keys of tens of megabytes per key set plus key-switching and circuit-bootstrapping keys add up; multi-precision deployments must manage gigabytes of key material [2][6].

**Security subtleties.** The noise after bootstrapping is *deterministic given the input noise distribution*, and decryption failures — however rare — can leak information in interactive protocols. Parameter sets must keep the failure probability cryptographically negligible, following the entropy-based security analysis of [2][3].

**Composability gaps.** PBS outputs TLWE ciphertexts; feeding results back into further blind rotations needs circuit bootstrapping, which is an order of magnitude more expensive than a plain gate bootstrap [2][3].

## 7 Conclusion

The FHEW lineage made a radical simplification stick: *the refresh is the computer*. FHEW showed that a homomorphically evaluated NAND gate, refreshed at birth, is a universal primitive for encrypted computation [1]. TFHE showed that the external product over the torus makes that primitive fast, compact, and composable [2][3]. Bootstrapped Boolean circuits need no depth provisioning and no noise bookkeeping — just a netlist and a steady gate rate. Programmable bootstrapping then revealed that the same blind rotation can evaluate a lookup table for free, extending the gate paradigm from Boolean circuits to quantized arithmetic and encrypted neural inference [5][6][7].
