# Beyond Succinctness: The Prover–Verifier Tradeoff Frontier in Zero-Knowledge Arguments — PLONK's Polynomial Commitments versus FRI-Based STARKs

## Abstract

Modern zero-knowledge proof systems for verifiable computation cluster around two poles: **polynomial-commitment SNARKs**, exemplified by PLONK, which compress proofs to a few hundred bytes via pairing-based commitments but inherit a structured trusted setup and quantum vulnerability; and **hash-based STARKs**, which replace algebraic commitments with the Fast Reed–Solomon Interactive Oracle Proof of Proximity (FRI), achieving transparency and conjectured post-quantum security at the cost of proofs one to two orders of magnitude larger. This paper gives a unified account of the two families as resolutions of the same tension: reducing verifier work and proof size while containing prover time, setup assumptions, and cryptographic hardness. We dissect PLONK's arithmetization pipeline — circuit, constraint and permutation polynomials, Fiat–Shamir-compiled KZG evaluation proofs — and the FRI commit–query protocol over Reed–Solomon codewords, covering its linear-time prover, logarithmic query complexity, and soundness analysis. We then characterize the Pareto frontier spanned by setup trust, proof size, prover time, verifier time, and quantum resistance, using Groth16, PLONK, STARK, and folding-based IVC as landmarks, and derive rules of thumb for when each construction is optimal for a given deployment envelope.

## 1 Introduction

Succinct non-interactive arguments of knowledge (SNARKs) promised a striking asymmetry: the prover does work polynomial in the circuit size *n*, while the verifier and the proof both scale *polylogarithmically* in *n*. Yet fifteen years after the first practical constructions, deployed systems remain fragmented. On one side stand *pairing-based SNARKs* — Groth16 [4], PLONK [1], and their Halo2-like successors — whose proofs are a handful of elliptic-curve points but whose setups demand a toxic-waste ceremony and whose hardness rests on assumptions broken by Shor's algorithm. On the other side stand *transparent, hash-based STARKs* [3] built on FRI [2], which need no trusted party and survive the quantum transition, but whose proofs are measured in tens or hundreds of kilobytes.

The central thesis of this paper is that this fragmentation is not accidental but reflects a genuine **multi-objective frontier** with no universally dominant point: every known construction is Pareto-optimal along at least one axis — proof size, prover time, verifier time, setup trust, or post-quantum security. We develop the two canonical families in full technical detail — PLONK's universal-SRS polynomial-IOP compiled against KZG commitments, and the FRI-based IOP compiled against Merkle-tree commitments — and place them, alongside Groth16 and folding-based IVC [6], on a single parameterized tradeoff map. The analysis yields concrete guidance: *which* scheme wins depends on measurable deployment parameters (proof-frequency, verifier gas budget, ceremony feasibility, quantum-horizon risk) rather than on any intrinsic superiority.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Arithmetization: from circuits to polynomial identities

Both families begin by converting a computation into a system of algebraic constraints over a finite field **F**, but they differ in the *intermediate representation*.

- **R1CS/QAP arithmetization** (Groth16 lineage): a circuit of *n* gates becomes matrices *A, B, C* and the prover exhibits a witness vector *z* with *Az ◦ Bz = Cz*. The QAP reduction folds all constraints into a single divisibility identity *p(x) = h(x)·t(x)* on a domain *H*.
- **PLONK arithmetization** [1]: gates are indexed by *i ∈ [n]* over a multiplicative subgroup *H = ⟨ω⟩*. Each gate's left, right and output wire values are encoded as polynomials *a(x), b(x), c(x)* interpolating wire values over *H*; selector polynomials *q_L, q_R, q_M, q_O, q_C* encode gate *types*. The constraint identity is

  *q_L(x)·a(x) + q_R(x)·b(x) + q_M(x)·a(x)b(x) + q_O(x)·c(x) + q_C(x) = 0*  ∀ *x ∈ H*,

  checked via a single quotient polynomial *t(x)* vanishing on *H*.

- **AIR arithmetization** (STARK lineage) [3]: a computation becomes an *execution trace* — a table *w: [T] × [w] → F* — with *transition constraints* *C_j(w_t, w_{t+1}) = 0* and *boundary constraints*. The trace is interpolated column-wise into polynomials evaluated on a domain *D* ⊇ *H* of size *β·T* (the *blowup factor β* creates the Reed–Solomon codeword redundancy that FRI will later exploit).

### 2.2 Interactive oracle proofs and polynomial commitments

The modern modular view decomposes a zk-SNARK into two pieces: an **information-theoretic polynomial interactive oracle proof (IOP)** — the prover sends oracles (functions), the verifier queries them at few points — and a **cryptographic compiler** that replaces each oracle with a *commitment scheme* [5]. KZG commitments bind a polynomial to a single group element with constant-size openings; Merkle-tree commitments bind an *evaluation vector* with logarithmic-size openings per query. This is the precise locus where the two families diverge: PLONK's IOP commits to coefficient-space polynomials via KZG, while STARK's IOP commits to evaluation-space Reed–Solomon codewords via Merkle trees, and delegates *low-degree testing* of those codewords to FRI.

### 2.3 Copy constraints and the permutation argument

Wire equality in PLONK — "the left input of gate *i* equals the output of gate *j*" — cannot be expressed by gate-local identities. Following Bayer and Groth, PLONK encodes all copy constraints as a permutation *σ* on *3n* wire positions and proves, via a **grand-product argument**, that the permuted evaluation vector matches the unpermuted one:

∏_{i∈[n]} *(a_i + γ) (b_i + γ) (c_i + γ)* = ∏_{i∈[n]} *(σa_i + γ) (σb_i + γ) (σc_i + γ)*,

accumulated as a running product polynomial *z(x)* whose transition *z(ωx) = z(x)·v(x)* is enforced as an additional constraint identity [1]. The permutation argument is the signature move that makes PLONK's constraint system *universal*: the same circuit layout supports arbitrary wirings through different *σ*.

## 3 Methodology

Our method is a **comparative cryptanalytic reconstruction**: we re-derive both families from a common IOP framework, exposing where each spends its prover and verifier budgets and what hardness it assumes. The evaluation uses:

1. **Asymptotic bookkeeping** — prover time, verifier time, proof size, and round complexity as functions of *n* (gates/constraints) and the security parameter λ.
2. **Concrete-parameter calibration** — representative instantiations (BN254 PLONK with KZG; 64-bit-field STARKs with FRI over ~2^21 domains as in ethSTARK [7]) to ground asymptotic claims in deployed numbers.
3. **Theorem-level comparison** — completeness and soundness statements with their exact error bounds, so that the tradeoff map is anchored in proven guarantees rather than folklore benchmarks.

### 3.1 The common skeleton

Every construction in this paper is an instance of the same five-stage pipeline:

1. **Arithmetize** the computation into polynomial constraint identities.
2. **Commit** to the witness polynomials/codewords (KZG vs. Merkle).
3. **Open** at verifier-chosen points, with zero-knowledge blinding (random multiples of the vanishing polynomial).
4. **Test proximity** to the Reed–Solomon code where required (FRI; KZG-based schemes skip this since degree is enforced by the commitment).
5. **Fiat–Shamir compile** the public-coin protocol into a non-interactive proof with a random oracle.

The SNARK–STARK divide is then precisely the choice made in stages 2 and 4: *algebraic* binding with pairings and trusted setup, or *combinatorial* binding with hashing and transparency.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 PLONK end to end: from circuit to nine curve points

PLONK's prover proceeds in five rounds [1]:

1. **Wire commitments.** Compute *a, b, c* from the witness, add blinding multiples of *Z_H(x) = x^n − 1*, and send KZG commitments *[a]_1, [b]_1, [c]_1*.
2. **Permutation product.** Given challenges *β, γ*, compute the grand-product polynomial *z* accumulating the ratio of permuted to unpermuted wire values; commit *[z]_1*.
3. **Quotient polynomial.** Given challenge *α*, form the constraint polynomial *t(x)* — gate identity plus permutation identity — and split it into degree-*n* chunks *[t_{lo}]_1, [t_{mid}]_1, [t_{hi}]_1* (a quotient of degree < 2n is split to keep each chunk's degree below *n*).
4. **Evaluation.** Given challenge *𝔷*, open *a, b, c, S_{σ1}, S_{σ2}, z* at *𝔷*, and *z* additionally at *𝔷ω*.
5. **Batching.** Given challenge *ν*, batch all openings into one KZG multi-opening proof *W_𝔷* plus one at *𝔷ω*.

The verifier checks a single pairing equation of the form

*e(W_𝔷 + u·W_{𝔷ω}, [x]_2) = e(ν·W_𝔷 + u·ν·W_{𝔷ω} − … , [1]_2)*,

whose batched form verifies all gate, permutation, and evaluation claims simultaneously. Proof size is constant: nine *G₁* elements plus field elements (~400 bytes on BN254).

> **Theorem (PLONK knowledge soundness, informal).** If the KZG scheme is binding and the *q*-SDH assumption holds for the SRS, then PLONK is a *computational* argument of knowledge with soundness error *negl(λ)* in the algebraic group model [1].
>
> *Proof sketch.* Extract the witness polynomials from the algebraic adversary's representations; the batching polynomial *r(x)* vanishes at the challenge only if all claimed openings are correct, and *z(ω) = 1* plus the grand-product identity enforce correct wiring. The Schwartz–Zippel lemma bounds the probability that a false identity survives a random challenge. ∎

### 4.2 FRI: folding Reed–Solomon codewords

FRI [2] solves the subproblem KZG dodges: given an oracle *f₀: L₀ → F* allegedly close to *RS[L₀, K]* (polynomials of degree < *K* evaluated on *L₀*), verify proximity with *O(log N)* queries and a *strictly linear* prover.

**Commit phase.** Write *f(x) = f_even(x²) + x·f_odd(x²)* and define, for a random *α*,

*Fold[f, α](x²) := f_even(x²) + α·f_odd(x²)*.

If *f* has degree < *K*, the fold has degree < *K/2* over the halved domain *L₁ = {x² : x ∈ L₀}* — validity is preserved with probability 1 for honest provers, and a *δ*-far word's fold stays far with high probability over random *α*. Repeating *r = log K* folds yields a constant *f_r*; each layer is Merkle-committed.

**Query phase.** The verifier samples *x₀ ∈ L₀*, and checks consistency *f_{i+1}(x_{i+1}) ≟ Fold[f_i, α_i](x_{i+1})* along the path, each check costing 2 queries to *f_i*. It also checks that the final layer is constant. Repeating *m* times drives soundness error to *(1−δ)^m*.

> **Theorem (FRI soundness, BBHR18).** For code rate *ρ = K/N*, the interactive FRI prover runs in time *< 6N* field operations; the verifier makes *2m log K* queries with arithmetic cost *≤ 21 log N*. Words *δ*-far from the code are rejected with probability at least *min{δ(1−o(1)), δ₀}*, where *δ₀ > 0* depends only on *ρ* [2].
>
> *Proof sketch.* A *commit error* occurs when the prover's committed *f_{i+1}* disagrees with *Fold[f_i, α_i]* at many points; a random *α_i* makes consistent cheating require *f_i* to be close to the code. Inductively, the distance from the code propagates: if *f₀* is *δ*-far, some layer exhibits a large commit error, and the query phase detects it with probability governed by the Johnson bound. Later work (BCI⁺23) tightened this via proximity gaps, removing the dependence on list-decoding radii. ∎

**STARK assembly.** The STARK protocol [3] runs an AIR-based IOP: the prover interpolates the execution trace, computes constraint-composition polynomials *C_i(x)* that vanish on the trace domain iff all transition/boundary constraints hold, commits all evaluations via Merkle trees, and uses FRI (through the DEEP-ALI/DEEP-FRI composition) to prove the composed polynomial is low-degree. Zero-knowledge comes from adding random masking polynomials — unlike KZG blinding, this costs extra trace columns but no trapdoor. ethSTARK [7] instantiated this over 64-bit fields with extension fields for soundness, demonstrating concretely that the transparency premium is paid in *proof size* (~100 KB) rather than in *prover asymptotics*.

### 4.3 The five-dimensional tradeoff map

We score each landmark scheme on five axes: setup trust (**T**), proof size (**P**), prover time (**W_p**), verifier time (**W_v**), and post-quantum security (**Q**). Let *n* be constraints/trace length and λ the security parameter.

| Scheme | Setup (T) | Proof size (P) | Prover time (W_p) | Verifier time (W_v) | PQ-safe (Q) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Groth16 [4] | Trusted, per-circuit | *O_λ(1)* ≈ 200 B | *O(n log n)* | *O_λ(1)*, 3 pairings | ✗ |
| PLONK [1] | Universal, updatable SRS | *O_λ(1)* ≈ 400 B | *O(n log n)* | *O_λ(1)*, ~2 pairings + MSM | ✗ |
| STARK [3] | Transparent | *O(log²n)* ≈ 40–200 KB | *O(n log n)* | *O(log²n)* | ✓ |
| Nova IVC [6] | Transparent | *O(|F|)* native, *O(log n)* compressed | *O(n)* MSM/step | *O(log n)* | ✗ (DLOG) |

No row dominates another: Groth16 minimizes *P* and *W_v* but maximizes setup ceremony cost per circuit; PLONK trades a constant-factor proof-size increase for a one-time universal setup [1]; STARK pays two orders of magnitude in *P* to buy transparency and quantum resistance [3]; Nova avoids SNARK recursion entirely at each IVC step, paying non-succinct native proofs that are then compressed [6]. This is the **Pareto frontier**: moving along it converts one scarce resource into another at rates fixed by the underlying cryptography.

### 4.4 Where the budgets actually go: prover anatomy

A common misconception is that "SNARKs are expensive to prove." The dominant costs differ by family:

- **PLONK/KZG prover:** FFTs for witness interpolation and quotient computation (*O(n log n)* field ops), plus multi-scalar multiplications (MSMs) for the nine commitments — the MSMs dominate concretely on pairing-friendly curves, scaling at roughly *n* curve ops with a large constant.
- **STARK/FRI prover:** trace interpolation and constraint evaluation over the *blowup domain* of size *βT* (typically *β ∈ {4, 8, 16}*), Merkle-tree hashing of all layers, and FRI folding — all *symmetric* primitives. The prover is arithmetically *linear* in *N = βT* [2], and in practice dominated by hashing and memory bandwidth rather than field arithmetic, which is why STARK provers parallelize so well on commodity hardware.
- **Groth16 prover:** QAP witness map plus three MSMs; concretely fastest per constraint among the three, but the per-circuit trusted setup is an *amortized* cost that must be counted for short-lived circuits.

The verifier-side story inverts the ranking: Groth16 verifies in ~1–2 ms with 3 pairings, PLONK in ~3–6 ms, STARK in ~5–15 ms of hashing, and Nova's native verifier is linear in *|F|* unless compressed [6].

```python
# Sketch: the algebraic heart of one FRI round (cf. BBHR18 [2])
def fri_fold(f_evals, alpha, L):
    """f_evals: evaluations of f on coset domain L (x, -x pairs)."""
    assert len(L) % 2 == 0
    out = []
    for x, fx, fnegx in zip(L[::2], f_evals[::2], f_evals[1::2]):
        # f(x) = f_even(x^2) + x * f_odd(x^2)
        f_even = (fx + fnegx) / 2
        f_odd  = (fx - fnegx) / (2 * x)
        out.append(f_even + alpha * f_odd)   # Fold[f, alpha] at x^2
    return out  # evaluations on halved domain L1
```

```rust
// Sketch: PLONK's grand-product accumulation for the permutation argument [1]
fn permutation_product(a: &[F], b: &[F], c: &[F], sigma: &Perm, beta: F, gamma: F) -> Vec<F> {
    let mut z = vec![F::ONE; a.len() + 1];
    for i in 0..a.len() {
        let num = (a[i] + beta * sigma.a(i) + gamma)
                * (b[i] + beta * sigma.b(i) + gamma)
                * (c[i] + beta * sigma.c(i) + gamma);
        let den = (a[i] + beta * S::a(i) + gamma)
                * (b[i] + beta * S::b(i) + gamma)
                * (c[i] + beta * S::c(i) + gamma);
        z[i + 1] = z[i] * num * den.invert().unwrap();
    }
    z // prover shows z(n) == 1 and z(wx) == z(x) * v(x)
}
```

## 5 Empirical Results / Proofs

### 5.1 Concrete calibration points

Anchoring the asymptotics to published instantiations (λ ≈ 100–128 bits):

1. **Proof size.** Groth16: 3 curve points ≈ 192–256 B [4]. PLONK (BN254): 9 *G₁* + 7 field elements ≈ 400–500 B [1]. STARK (ethSTARK parameters, ~2^20 trace): 45–150 KB depending on query count and hash [7]. The *P*-axis spans nearly three orders of magnitude.
2. **Prover time.** For *n = 2^20* constraints: Groth16 ≈ 10–30 s single-threaded; PLONK ≈ 30–90 s (MSM-bound); STARK ≈ 20–60 s (hash-bound, far better multi-core scaling) [3][7]. FRI's *< 6N* field-op bound [2] is the reason STARK provers are not asymptotically worse despite the blowup factor.
3. **Verifier time.** Groth16 ≈ 1.5 ms; PLONK ≈ 3–6 ms; STARK ≈ 5–15 ms; on-chain (EVM) verification: Groth16 ≈ 200k gas, PLONK ≈ 250–300k gas, STARK verification is typically *not* deployed on-chain at these sizes because calldata dominates.

> **Theorem (Frontier characterization, this paper).** Under standard assumptions (q-SDH for KZG [5], collision-resistance of the hash for FRI [2]), for every ε > 0 there exist parameters at which: (i) no transparent scheme achieves proof size *o(log n)*; (ii) no scheme with *O(1)*-size proofs avoids either a structured setup or non-falsifiable assumptions; (iii) prover time *Ω(n log n)* is unavoidable for the FFT-based arithmetizations of [1][4], while *O(n)* is achievable for IOP+FRI provers [2][3].
>
> *Proof sketch.* (i) A transparent verifier must read *Ω(λ)* bits to reach soundness *2^{−λ}* against an unbounded prover, and the Merkle authentication paths add the *log n* factor. (ii) Constant-size proofs with public setup would imply succinct commitments with local opening from falsifiable assumptions only, contradicting known lower bounds for pairing-free groups. (iii) Interpolation over *n* points costs *Ω(n log n)* by the FFT lower bound; FRI's prover avoids interpolation entirely, folding evaluations in linear time [2]. ∎

### 5.2 Decision rules

From the frontier we derive three practical rules:

1. **On-chain verifier with tight gas budget** → pairing-based SNARK. Groth16 if the circuit is fixed and a ceremony is affordable; PLONK if many circuits share one SRS [1][4].
2. **Long-lived or adversarial-setup environments** (no trusted party feasible; quantum horizon < 20 years) → FRI-based STARKs; budget ~100 KB proofs and hash-bound proving [3].
3. **Incremental/streaming computation** (blockchains, IVC) → folding schemes: Nova's per-step prover is two MSMs with *constant* recursion overhead, deferring succinctness to a final compression SNARK [6].

## 6 Limitations

This analysis has four principal limits. **First**, concrete benchmarks are parameter-sensitive: the STARK–PLONK prover crossover depends on field choice (64-bit Goldilocks vs. 256-bit BN254), hash function (Poseidon vs. Blake3), and hardware (MSM accelerators vs. hash throughput), so our calibration numbers are order-of-magnitude guides, not predictions. **Second**, we treat *Nova* [6] only as a frontier landmark; folding schemes introduce their own tradeoff axis (non-succinct native IVC proofs vs. cheap steps) that deserves a dedicated treatment. **Third**, our post-quantum claims rest on the *conjectured* quantum-resistance of hash-based IOPs: FRI's soundness in the quantum random-oracle model is analyzed, but a full QROM proof for the STARK composition remains an active research area [3][7]. **Fourth**, we do not model *prover memory*: FRI's Merkle trees over the blowup domain have a substantially larger memory footprint than KZG's streaming MSMs, which can dominate on memory-constrained provers — a sixth axis we leave for future work.

## 7 Conclusion

PLONK and FRI-based STARKs are best understood not as competitors but as *dual optima* of a single design space. PLONK [1] pushes algebraic structure to its limit: one universal setup, nine curve points, millisecond verification — purchased with pairings, trapdoors, and quantum fragility. FRI [2] and the STARKs built on it [3] push combinatorial structure to its limit: no setup, no trapdoors, conjectured post-quantum soundness, linear-time proving — purchased with logarithmic proof growth and hash-bound constants. The frontier theorem of §5 shows these prices are not implementation artifacts but consequences of the cryptography itself. For the practitioner, the message is liberating: choose the point on the frontier your deployment's scarcest resource dictates — gas, ceremony, transparency, or quantum safety — and the theory guarantees no construction can give you all four at once.
