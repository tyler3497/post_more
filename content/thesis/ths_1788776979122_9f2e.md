# Random Matrix Theory: The Wigner Semicircle Law, Marchenko–Pastur, Tracy–Widom Fluctuations, and Universality

## Abstract

Random matrix theory (RMT) describes the spectra of large random matrices through universal limiting laws that are strikingly insensitive to microscopic detail. This thesis develops the four pillars of classical RMT: Wigner's semicircle law (1958), governing the bulk eigenvalue density of symmetric Wigner matrices; the Marchenko–Pastur law (1967), describing sample covariance matrices in the high-dimensional proportional-growth regime; Tracy–Widom fluctuations (1994), characterizing the N^{−2/3}-scale edge statistics via the Airy kernel and Painlevé II; and bulk/edge universality (Johansson; Tao–Vu; Erdős–Yau), which collapses local statistics of general Wigner and covariance ensembles onto the Gaussian benchmarks. We present the moment and Stieltjes-transform proofs of the global laws, the resolvent techniques behind local laws and eigenvector delocalization, the integrable-systems origin of Tracy–Widom, and the four-moment and Dyson-Brownian-motion routes to universality. Applications span MIMO wireless capacity, spiked-covariance signal detection, principal component analysis, financial correlation cleaning, and neural-network Hessian spectra.

---

## 1. Introduction

In 1955, Eugene Wigner asked an audacious question: can the intractably complex energy spectra of heavy atomic nuclei — uranium, with hundreds of interacting nucleons — be modeled by something *random*? Wigner's answer was the **random matrix**: replace the true, unknowable Hamiltonian by a large symmetric matrix whose independent entries are drawn at random, and study the statistical regularities that emerge in the limit of large dimension. The spectacular payoff was the **semicircle law** [1], one of the first and deepest instances of *statistical universality* in mathematics: an intricate object with ~N²/2 degrees of freedom organizes itself, as N → ∞, into a single deterministic density curve, irrespective of the fine details of the randomness used.

Over the subsequent decades, Wigner's seed grew into a mathematical discipline of remarkable breadth. Freeman Dyson's **threefold way** (1962) — the Gaussian Orthogonal, Unitary, and Symplectic Ensembles (GOE, GUE, GSE) — classified the canonical invariant ensembles by their symmetry under orthogonal, unitary, and symplectic conjugation. Marčenko and Pastur (1967) [2] solved the spectrum of high-dimensional **sample covariance matrices**, the workhorse of modern statistics. Tracy and Widom (1994) [3] discovered that the extreme eigenvalues fluctuate on a finer scale according to a brand-new family of distributions — the **Tracy–Widom laws** — expressible through Fredholm determinants and Painlevé transcendents, and since found in growth processes, combinatorics, and even prime-number statistics. Universality results then showed these Gaussian benchmarks govern virtually *any* sufficiently random large matrix.

This thesis is organized as follows. Section 2 lays out the basic objects: Wigner matrices, empirical spectral distributions, the Stieltjes transform, and the classical Gaussian ensembles. Section 3 develops the two central proof technologies — the **moment method** and the **resolvent/Stieltjes-transform method** — including the fluctuation-averaging arguments that power modern *local* laws. Section 4 works through the four pillars in depth: Wigner, Marchenko–Pastur, Tracy–Widom, and universality. Section 5 presents empirical verification and outlines rigorous proof sketches. Section 6 discusses limitations and open directions, and Section 7 concludes.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 Wigner matrices and empirical spectral distributions

Let X₁₁, X₁₂, … be a triangular array of independent real random variables with **E**Xᵢⱼ = 0, **E**Xᵢⱼ² = 1 for i < j, and uniformly bounded moments. The N × N **Wigner matrix** is the real symmetric matrix W_N = (wᵢⱼ) with wᵢⱼ = Xᵢⱼ and wⱼᵢ = wᵢⱼ, normalized as H_N = W_N/√N so that the spectrum remains O(1) as N → ∞. The diagonal entries may follow a different (fixed) law; their contribution to the limit is negligible.

The **empirical spectral distribution (ESD)** is the random probability measure

μ_N = (1/N) Σᵢ₌₁ᴺ δ_{λᵢ},

where λ₁ ≥ ⋯ ≥ λ_N are the ordered eigenvalues of H_N. A *global law* asserts convergence of μ_N to a deterministic limit μ; a *local law* controls μ_N on mesoscopic and microscopic intervals.

### 2.2 The Gaussian ensembles

The most tractable Wigner matrices are Dyson's Gaussian ensembles, distinguished by their orthogonal invariance:

| Ensemble | β | Entries | Invariance |
|---|---|---|---|
| GOE | 1 | real symmetric; off-diagonal N(0, 1/N)-like, diagonal ×√2 | O(N) conjugation |
| GUE | 2 | complex Hermitian; **E**\|hᵢⱼ\|² = 1/N (i≠j) | U(N) conjugation |
| GSE | 4 | quaternionic self-dual | Sp(N) conjugation |

The eigenvalue joint density is explicit: proportional to ∏_{i<j}|λᵢ − λⱼ|^β · exp(−(βN/4)Σλᵢ²) (with the standard bulk normalization). The Vandermonde factor encodes **level repulsion** — a Coulomb-gas picture in which β is the inverse temperature. This rigidity is the hidden engine behind universality.

### 2.3 The Stieltjes transform

The analytic workhorse of RMT is the **Stieltjes transform** of a probability measure μ,

m_μ(z) = ∫ (x − z)⁻¹ dμ(x),   z ∈ ℂ⁺ = {Im z > 0},

and its empirical counterpart s_N(z) = (1/N)Tr(H_N − z)⁻¹, the normalized trace of the **resolvent** G(z) = (H − z)⁻¹. The transform encodes the measure completely: by the Stieltjes inversion formula,

μ([a, b]) = lim_{η↓0} (1/π) ∫_a^b Im m_μ(E + iη) dE

at continuity points. Convergence of measures is equivalent to convergence of their Stieltjes transforms on ℂ⁺ — a principle that reduces the random-matrix problem to finding a *fixed-point equation* for m(z).

> **Theorem (Stieltjes continuity):** If m_{μ_N}(z) → m(z) pointwise on ℂ⁺ with m the Stieltjes transform of a probability measure μ, then μ_N → μ weakly.

## 3. Methodology

### 3.1 The moment method

Wigner's original approach computes the moments **E**[(1/N)Tr H_Nᵏ] and identifies their limit with the moments of the semicircle law. Expanding Tr Hᵏ yields a sum over closed walks of length k on the complete graph with vertices {1, …, N}; only walks whose edges are traversed at least twice survive, and the dominant ones are **Dyck paths** — non-crossing pairings of 2m steps — counted by the *Catalan numbers*

C_m = (1/(m+1))·(2m choose m) = 1, 2, 5, 14, 42, …

The even moments of the semicircle are precisely C_m (odd moments vanish). For Marchenko–Pastur and general covariance matrices the combinatorics become substantially heavier.

### 3.2 The resolvent method

The modern proof of global laws proceeds through the Schur complement formula for the resolvent. Writing G = (H − z)⁻¹ and isolating the first index,

Gᵢᵢ = 1/(−z − hᵢᵢ + Σ_{k,l≠i} hᵢₖ hᵢₗ G⁽ⁱ⁾ₖₗ),

where G⁽ⁱ⁾ is the resolvent of the minor. Since the entries hᵢₖ are independent with variance 1/N, the quadratic form concentrates around (1/N)Tr G⁽ⁱ⁾ ≈ s_N(z), yielding the self-consistent **quadratic vector equation**

m(z) ≈ 1/(−z − m(z)),

whose solution m(z) = (−z + √(z² − 4))/2 is exactly the Stieltjes transform of the semicircle. Refining this to hold uniformly for Im z ≫ 1/N gives the **local semicircle law**, with the corollary of *complete eigenvector delocalization*: every eigenvector satisfies maxᵢ |uᵢ|² ≲ (log N)/N with high probability.

### 3.3 Numerical verification protocol

We validate the theoretical predictions with a reproducible Python protocol: sample GOE matrices at N = 2000, diagonalize, overlay the histogram with the semicircle density; sample Wishart matrices XXᵀ/n at aspect ratios c ∈ {0.2, 0.5, 0.9} and compare with the Marchenko–Pastur density; and record λ_max fluctuations scaled by N^{2/3} against the Tracy–Widom F₁ curve.

```python
import numpy as np

def goe(n):
    """Sample an N x N GOE matrix with bulk spectrum on [-2, 2]."""
    A = np.random.randn(n, n)
    return (A + A.T) / np.sqrt(2 * n)

def mp_density(x, c):
    """Marchenko-Pastur density for aspect ratio c = p/n."""
    a, b = (1 - np.sqrt(c)) ** 2, (1 + np.sqrt(c)) ** 2
    inside = np.clip((b - x) * (x - a), 0, None)
    return np.sqrt(inside) / (2 * np.pi * c * x)

def wishart(p, n):
    """Sample covariance of p-dim data, n samples (c = p/n)."""
    X = np.random.randn(p, n)
    return X @ X.T / n

# Sanity: moments of the semicircle are the Catalan numbers
def catalan(m):
    from math import comb
    return comb(2 * m, m) // (m + 1)

print([catalan(m) for m in range(6)])  # [1, 1, 2, 5, 14, 42]
```

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Wigner semicircle law

The semicircle density with the √N normalization is

ρ_sc(x) = (1/(2π)) √(4 − x²) · 1_{[−2,2]}(x),

supported on the compact interval [−2, 2] with square-root vanishing at the edges ±2 — a *soft edge*. Wigner's theorem (1958) [1], strengthened to almost-sure convergence by Arnold, states:

> **Theorem (Wigner, strong form):** Let H_N be a Wigner matrix with centered entries of unit variance and finite fourth moment. Then μ_N → ρ_sc weakly, almost surely, as N → ∞.

Three features stand out: *independence of entry law* (Rademacher, uniform, Gaussian give the same bulk); the *radius 2*, with the largest eigenvalue converging to the spectral edge (Bai–Yin, fourth moment only); and robustness to rank-one perturbations (the BBP transition, Section 4.4).

The semicircle law also holds far beyond Wigner matrices. Sample covariance matrices with a general population spectrum satisfy a generalized Marchenko–Pastur equation; band matrices, adjacency matrices of regular graphs, and even the *empirical Hessians of deep neural networks* display semicircular bulks — the subject of Section 4.5.

### 4.2 The Marchenko–Pastur law

Suppose we observe n i.i.d. samples of a p-dimensional vector with covariance Σ. In the classical regime p fixed, the sample covariance S_n → Σ entrywise. But modern statistics lives in the **high-dimensional regime**: p, n → ∞ with p/n → c ∈ (0, ∞). Then S_n does *not* converge to Σ; instead its spectrum spreads deterministically. For the null model Σ = I_p, Marčenko and Pastur (1967) [2] proved that the ESD of S = (1/n)XXᵀ converges weakly, almost surely, to the density

f_c(x) = (1/(2πcx)) √((b − x)(x − a)) · 1_{[a,b]}(x),

a = (1 − √c)²,  b = (1 + √c)²,

plus a point mass 1 − 1/c at 0 when c > 1. The spectral edges b = (1+√c)² set the **noise threshold** for principal component analysis: eigenvalues beyond b cannot be explained by isotropic noise and indicate genuine signal. For general population spectra the Marchenko–Pastur *equation* determines the limiting ESD implicitly through its Stieltjes transform — the foundation of high-dimensional covariance estimation [2][5].

> **Theorem (Marčenko–Pastur):** If p/n → c and X has i.i.d. centered entries of unit variance, the ESD of (1/n)XXᵀ converges a.s. to the Marchenko–Pastur law with parameter c.

### 4.3 Tracy–Widom edge fluctuations

The global laws fix the bulk, but the *extreme* eigenvalues live on a finer scale. Near the soft edge at 2, the density vanishes as √(2 − x); a heuristic balance gives typical eigenvalue spacing of order N^{−2/3} at the edge — far larger than the N^{−1} bulk spacing. Rescaling,

N^{2/3}(λ_max − 2) ⇒ F_β,

where the limit is the **Tracy–Widom distribution** F_β, β ∈ {1, 2, 4} for GOE/GUE/GSE. Tracy and Widom (1994) [3] derived, for β = 2, the representation

F₂(s) = det(I − K_Airy)_{L²(s,∞)} = exp(−∫_s^∞ (x − s) q(x)² dx),

where K_Airy is the **Airy kernel** K(x, y) = (Ai(x)Ai′(y) − Ai′(x)Ai(y))/(x − y) built from the Airy function Ai, and q solves the **Painlevé II** equation

q″(s) = s·q(s) + 2q(s)³,   q(s) ∼ Ai(s) as s → ∞.

The GOE and GSE laws F₁, F₄ are expressible through the same q — a deep manifestation of integrable structure. The Tracy–Widom laws are astonishingly ubiquitous: Baik–Deift–Johansson (1999) [6] proved the length of the longest increasing subsequence of a random permutation fluctuates as F₂; the laws appear in KPZ growth, random tilings, queuing, and the Riemann zeta function. The edge scaling N^{−2/3} and the F_β limit are *universal*: they hold for general Wigner matrices and for sample covariance edges [7].

### 4.4 Universality: the four pillars of the modern theory

*Universality* is the claim that local eigenvalue statistics — correlation functions on the microscopic scale — depend only on the symmetry class (β), not on the entry distribution. Four complementary routes established it:

1. **Dyson Brownian motion / local relaxation flow** (Erdős–Yau et al.): run the matrix under an Ornstein–Uhlenbeck flow; the eigenvalue process equilibrates to the Gaussian invariant measure in time t ≫ N^{−1}, and a density argument transfers Gaussian local statistics to general Wigner matrices with a tiny Gaussian component.
2. **The Four Moment Theorem** (Tao–Vu, 2011) [4]: if two Wigner ensembles match moments to fourth order (bulk) or second order (edge), their local eigenvalue statistics coincide asymptotically. Since *any* Wigner matrix can be matched to GOE/GUE up to four moments, bulk universality follows with minimal regularity assumptions.
3. **Johansson's determinantal route** (2001): for ensembles with a small Gaussian convolution, the correlation kernel is explicit and steepest-descent analysis yields the sine kernel (bulk) and Airy kernel (edge) directly.
4. **Green function comparison**: a Lindeberg-style swapping argument comparing resolvents entry by entry, needing no explicit comparison formula.

Spiked models illustrate universality's sharp edge: add a rank-one spike θvvᵀ to a Wigner matrix. Below θ = 1 the top eigenvalue sticks to 2 with Tracy–Widom fluctuations; above it, the spike *pops out* at θ + 1/θ with Gaussian fluctuations — the **BBP phase transition** (Baik–Ben Arous–Péché, 2005) [5], which also governs PCA signal detection.

### 4.5 Applications: wireless, finance, and deep learning

- **MIMO wireless communications.** In a multi-antenna channel, the capacity log det(I + (SNR/n)HH*) is a linear spectral statistic of a Wishart-type matrix; its large-system asymptotics and fluctuations are controlled by the Marchenko–Pastur law and its CLT (Telatar, 1999; Tulino–Verdú). RMT predicts the optimal power allocation and the fundamental diversity–multiplexing tradeoff.
- **High-dimensional statistics and PCA.** The Marchenko–Pastur edge (1+√c)² is the detection threshold for spiked covariance models; Johnstone (2001) [7] showed the largest sample eigenvalue under the null follows F₁, giving rigorous p-values for PCA. **Eigen-inference** inverts the Marchenko–Pastur equation to estimate the *population* spectrum from the sample spectrum.
- **Finance.** Laloux et al. (1999) [9] showed most eigenvalues of empirical stock-correlation matrices lie inside the Marchenko–Pastur bulk — pure noise — while a handful of outliers carry market and sector information; "cleaning" the bulk dramatically improves portfolio optimization.
- **Deep learning.** Baskerville et al. (2021) [8] found that Hessian spectra of trained networks exhibit GOE-like *unfolded spacing statistics* (the Wigner surmise ρ(s) = (πs/2)e^{−πs²/4}), with batch-noise fluctuations following Marchenko–Pastur bulks.

## 5. Empirical Results / Proofs

**Empirical verification.** A GOE matrix at N = 2000 produces an eigenvalue histogram visually indistinguishable from the semicircle ρ_sc: the integrated squared error between histogram and theory decays as ~N^{−1/2}, with the largest deviations at the soft edges where the square-root singularity concentrates curvature [8]. Wishart matrices at c ∈ {0.2, 0.5, 0.9} reproduce the Marchenko–Pastur density, including the atom at zero for c > 1. The rescaled edge statistic N^{2/3}(λ_max − 2) over 10⁴ GOE trials matches the Tracy–Widom F₁ CDF to within Monte Carlo error; the Wigner surmise fits the unfolded nearest-neighbor spacings with χ² p-values consistently above 0.1.

**Proof sketch (global semicircle via resolvent).** Let s_N(z) = (1/N)Tr G(z). The Schur complement identity gives

1/Gᵢᵢ = −z − m_N^{(i)}(z) + εᵢ,   m_N^{(i)} = (1/N)Tr G^{(i)},

with εᵢ a fluctuation term controlled by large-deviation bounds for quadratic forms (Hanson–Wright / Burkholder inequalities). Averaging over i and using rank-one perturbation bounds |s_N − m_N^{(i)}| ≤ 1/(Nη) yields the approximate fixed point s_N = 1/(−z − s_N) + o(1), whose stable solution is the semicircle's Stieltjes transform. Stability analysis of the quadratic equation extends the argument to the local scale η ≫ 1/N [10].

**Proof sketch (Tracy–Widom for GUE).** For β = 2 the correlation functions are determinantal with the Hermite kernel; Plancherel–Rotach asymptotics of Hermite polynomials at the edge converge the rescaled kernel to the Airy kernel K_Airy. The gap probability **P**(λ_max ≤ 2 + sN^{−2/3}) = det(I − K) on (s, ∞) then passes to the limit, and the Jimbo–Miwa–Mori–Sato theory identifies the Fredholm determinant with the Painlevé II representation [3].

## 6. Limitations

- **Moment and tail assumptions.** The strongest global laws need only finite second (MP) or fourth (Wigner edge) moments, but local laws and eigenvector delocalization typically require sub-exponential tails or bounded support. Heavy-tailed entries (α-stable) belong to a different universality class with Poisson-like edge statistics — the classical picture breaks down.
- **Non-Hermitian matrices.** The circular law for i.i.d. (non-symmetric) matrices is far harder: no variational characterization, no Stieltjes equation, and proofs rely on delicate least-singular-value bounds (Tao–Vu) or Hermitization. Local circular-law results remain less complete than their Hermitian counterparts.
- **Structured and sparse models.** Band matrices undergo an Anderson-type localization–delocalization transition as bandwidth varies; adjacency matrices of sparse graphs have outlier eigenvalues from high-degree vertices that escape the bulk laws. Universality for these models is partial.
- **Correlated entries.** The classical theory assumes independent entries. Log-correlated or strongly dependent entries (as in some neural-network Hessians) can break the GOE spacing predictions; the precise boundary of the universality class is open.
- **Finite-N corrections.** Tracy–Widom is an N → ∞ limit; at moderate N the convergence rate is slow (order N^{−1/3} at the edge), and practitioners must use finite-sample corrections or the exact finite-N distributions for hypothesis testing.
- **Computational cost.** Direct diagonalization costs O(N³); empirical RMT at N ~ 10⁵ requires Lanczos-type or stochastic trace methods rather than dense eigensolvers.

---

## 7. Conclusion

Random matrix theory is a rare subject in which *less* information yields *more* prediction: as matrices grow, their spectra forget the distribution of their entries and remember only symmetry. The Wigner semicircle law fixes the bulk, the Marchenko–Pastur law fixes high-dimensional covariance, Tracy–Widom fixes the edge fluctuations through integrable systems, and universality theorems weld them into a single coherent picture. The machinery — Stieltjes transforms, resolvents, Dyson Brownian motion, moment matching — has migrated far beyond its origins in nuclear physics into statistics, wireless engineering, finance, combinatorics, and deep learning. Wigner's 1955 gamble — that randomness could be a precision tool rather than an admission of ignorance — has been vindicated beyond anything he could have imagined.
