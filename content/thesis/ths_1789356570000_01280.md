# Zero-Knowledge Succinct Proofs: zk-SNARKs, PLONK, FRI-Based STARKs, and Polynomial Commitments

## Abstract

Zero-knowledge succinct non-interactive arguments of knowledge (zk-SNARKs) compress arbitrarily large computations into proofs verifiable in milliseconds, revealing nothing about private inputs. This thesis unifies the two dominant design families — pairing-based systems descended from Groth16, epitomized by the universal SNARK PLONK, and hash-based transparent systems built on the Fast Reed–Solomon Interactive Oracle Proof of Proximity (FRI) that underpin zk-STARKs — through the polynomial-IOP compiler lens: an information-theoretic protocol compiled into a non-interactive argument by a polynomial commitment scheme. We show how one arithmetization instantiates with KZG for 400-byte proofs under a trusted setup or with FRI for 100-kilobyte post-quantum proofs with no trust assumptions. We reconstruct the PLONK permutation argument, the KZG opening check and its batched generalization, the FRI folding round with concrete prover and verifier complexity, and the DEEP-FRI sampling refinement. We prove succinctness of the compiled argument, tabulate asymptotic and concrete costs, and implement a FRI folding prototype in Python, a PLONK permutation accumulator in Haskell, and a KZG verification routine in Rust.

## 1 Introduction

Can a computationally weak verifier *V* be convinced of the correctness of a computation performed by a powerful prover *P*, without redoing the work and without learning anything beyond the statement's validity? The affirmative answer, developed over four decades of complexity theory and industrialized over the last decade, now runs in production blockchains.

A **zero-knowledge succinct non-interactive argument of knowledge** (zk-SNARK) satisfies four properties at once. *Completeness*: honest proofs verify. *Knowledge soundness*: a convincing prover must know a witness, formalized via an extractor. *Succinctness*: proof size and verification time are sublinear in the computation size. *Zero knowledge*: the transcript reveals nothing about the witness beyond the statement's truth. All four are achieved by composing three ingredients:

1. **Arithmetization** — encoding the computation as a statement about polynomials (R1CS, QAP, Plonkish, AIR).
2. **A polynomial interactive oracle proof (IOP)** — an information-theoretic protocol in which the prover sends oracles to polynomials and the verifier makes random evaluation queries [6].
3. **A polynomial commitment scheme** — a cryptographic compiler turning polynomial oracles into short commitments and openings, preserving soundness while adding succinctness and zero knowledge.

The field's history is a progressive modularization of these layers. Pinocchio and Groth16 intertwined all three [1]. PLONK made the split explicit: one universal polynomial IOP compiled with KZG, reusable for any circuit of bounded size [2]. The STARK line replaced the commitment layer with a proximity test to Reed–Solomon codes, eliminating trusted setup at the cost of larger proofs [7][9].

> **Theorem (Informal):** Let Π be a polynomial IOP with r rounds, query complexity q, and soundness error ε, and let C be a binding polynomial commitment scheme with evaluation binding. Compiling Π by committing to each oracle message and answering each query with an opening proof yields proof size and verifier time O(r + q · cost_open), soundness error ε + negl(λ), and zero knowledge via blinding each committed polynomial with random multiples of the vanishing polynomial. [6][3]

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Interactive proofs and the road to succinctness

The IP = PSPACE theorem showed interaction plus randomness exponentially expands what a weak verifier can check. Micali's CS proofs added hashing and the Fiat–Shamir transform, converting public-coin interactive proofs into non-interactive arguments in the random oracle model; Kilian showed PCPs compress with Merkle trees into polylogarithmic-size arguments. Modern SNARKs refine this lineage: an information-theoretic core compiled by pairings, discrete logarithms, or hash functions into something a blockchain verifies.

Zero knowledge, in the simulation paradigm of Goldwasser, Micali, and Rackoff, is achieved in practice by *blinding*: the prover adds random low-degree multiples of the vanishing polynomial, randomizing each committed polynomial over all polynomials consistent with the claimed evaluations.

### 2.2 Arithmetization: from circuits to polynomials

The first design decision in any SNARK is arithmetization:

- **R1CS:** the computation flattens into constraints of the form *u · v = w* over wire vectors. The lingua franca of Groth16 and circom, but each constraint is a multiplication gate, so additions fold in awkwardly.
- **QAP:** Genaro, Gentry, Parno, and Raykova's transform converts R1CS into polynomial divisibility — the prover exhibits A, B, C with A(x)·B(x) − C(x) divisible by a target polynomial T(x), checked at a random point via Schwartz–Zippel. This is Groth16's engine [1].
- **Plonkish:** n gates with three wires (a, b, c), a gate identity q_L·a + q_R·b + q_O·c + q_M·a·b + q_C = 0 selected by selector polynomials, plus a copy constraint equating wired values. Generalizes R1CS with arbitrary degree-2 custom gates; PLONK's native language [2].
- **AIR:** the computation is a state machine whose transition relation is polynomial constraints over a trace table; trace columns interpolate into polynomials. The native language of STARKs [9].

### 2.3 Pairings and the structured reference string

Pairing-based SNARKs use groups G1, G2, GT of prime order with a bilinear map *e: G1 × G2 → GT*. The **KZG commitment** [8] samples a secret τ at setup and publishes powers [1], [τ], [τ²], …, [τ^d] in G1. The commitment is com(f) = [f(τ)]; to prove f(z) = y the prover sends π = [q(τ)] for q(X) = (f(X) − y)/(X − z), and the verifier checks *e(com(f) − [y]₁, [1]₂) = e(π, [τ]₂ − [z]₂)*. Binding reduces to q-SDH; commitment and proof are each one group element regardless of degree.

## 3 Methodology

This thesis proceeds by *reconstruction*: we rebuild each core protocol inside the polynomial-IOP compiler framework and instantiate the algorithms as executable pseudocode. Four steps per system: **arithmetize** (the constraint system and polynomial identity), **IOP** (oracle messages plus query checks, with completeness and soundness), **compile** (substitute KZG or FRI/Merkle commitments, derive proof size and verifier equations, apply Fiat–Shamir), **compare** (prover time, proof size, verifier time, setup, quantum resistance).

We take the **polynomial IOP** as the unit of comparison: PLONK with KZG versus with FRI share the identical IOP and differ only in the commitment layer [2].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Groth16 and the pairing-SNARK baseline

Groth16 is the efficiency gold standard for pairing-based SNARKs [1]. Setup yields a circuit-bound proving and verification key; the proof is exactly three group elements; verification needs three pairings plus a multi-exponentiation over public inputs. On BN254 at 128-bit security the proof is roughly 200 bytes and verification takes a few milliseconds [4].

Its elegance comes from the QAP divisibility check: with R1CS matrices encoded as polynomials u_i, v_i, w_i and witness a_i, the prover forms A(X) = Σ a_i u_i(X), B(X) = Σ a_i v_i(X), C(X) = Σ a_i w_i(X), and shows H(X) = (A(X)B(X) − C(X))/T(X) is a polynomial for the target polynomial T vanishing on the constraint points:

```python
# Groth16 proof elements (schematic, over a pairing-friendly curve)
# alpha, beta, gamma, delta: toxic waste of the circuit-specific setup
A = alpha + sum(a_i * u_i(tau)) + r * delta      # in G1
B = beta  + sum(a_i * v_i(tau)) + s * delta      # in G2
C = (sum(a_i * (beta*u_i(tau) + alpha*v_i(tau) + w_i(tau))) + H(tau)*T(tau)) / delta \
    + A*s + B*r - r*s*delta                        # in G1
# verifier checks: e(A, B) = e(alpha, beta) * e(public_inputs, gamma) * e(C, delta)
```

### 4.2 PLONK: universal SRS and the permutation argument

PLONK separates *what each gate computes* from *how gates are wired* [2]. Wire polynomials a(X), b(X), c(X) interpolate wire values over a multiplicative subgroup H of order n; selector polynomials q_L, q_R, q_O, q_M, q_C choose each gate's operation:

> **Gate identity:** q_L(X)·a(X) + q_R(X)·b(X) + q_O(X)·c(X) + q_M(X)·a(X)·b(X) + q_C(X) = 0 for all X in H.

Wiring is a permutation σ over the 3n wire positions. The **permutation argument** proves the multiset of wire values is invariant under σ, forcing connected wires to carry equal values. Using the Bayer–Groth grand product with challenges β, γ, the accumulator z (with z(1) = 1) satisfies

> **Permutation accumulator:** z(ωX)·(a(X) + βX + γ)(b(X) + βk₁X + γ)(c(X) + βk₂X + γ) = z(X)·(a(X) + βσ₁(X) + γ)(b(X) + βσ₂(X) + γ)(c(X) + βσ₃(X) + γ)

where σ₁, σ₂, σ₃ encode the permutation and k₁, k₂ are coset shifts separating wire domains. Checked at a random point alongside z(ωⁿ) = 1 and the gate identity, the whole circuit reduces to a handful of evaluations — committed once, opened at one challenge point via batched KZG [3].

The prover commits to a, b, c; receives β, γ and commits to z; receives α and commits to the quotient polynomial t; then opens everything at ζ and ζω with one batched KZG proof. The verifier does about ten pairings; proofs are roughly 400–500 bytes [4]. In Haskell, the accumulator is a pure fold:

```haskell
-- PLONK permutation accumulator (schematic over a prime field)
grandProduct :: Field -> Field -> [Field] -> [Field] -> [Field] -> Perm -> [Field]
grandProduct beta gamma as bs cs sigma = scanl step 1 (zip [0..] as `zip` bs `zip` cs)
  where
    step z (i, (a, (b, c))) =
      z * (a + beta * sigmaA i + gamma)
        * (b + beta * sigmaB i + gamma)
        * (c + beta * sigmaC i + gamma)
      / ((a + beta * omegaPow i + gamma)
        * (b + beta * k1 * omegaPow i + gamma)
        * (c + beta * k2 * omegaPow i + gamma))
```

Zero knowledge comes from random multiples of the vanishing polynomial Z_H(X) = Xⁿ − 1 added to the wire and accumulator polynomials [2].

### 4.3 Polynomial commitments: KZG and its batching evolution

The commitment scheme is the compiler's cryptographic half; its choice fixes proof size, setup, and quantum resistance. The design space:

| Scheme | Commitment | Opening | Setup | Assumption |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| KZG [8] | 1 G1 | 1 G1 | trusted, universal | q-SDH |
| KZG batched [3] | 1 G1 | 1 G1 | trusted, universal | q-SDH |
| Inner-product (Bulletproofs) | 1 G | O(log d) | transparent | discrete log |
| FRI / Merkle | 1 hash | O(log² d) hashes | transparent | collision resistance |
| DARK (RSA-based) | 1 group element | O(log d) | transparent | adaptive root |

Boneh, Drake, Fisch, and Gabizon showed one group element can open many polynomials at many points, at the cost of extra G2 operations — the batching PLONK uses to open all its polynomials at ζ and ζω in a single proof [3]. In Rust, verification is the pairing equation transcribed directly:

```rust
// KZG evaluation proof verification (schematic)
fn kzg_verify(com: G1Affine, z: Scalar, y: Scalar, pi: G1Affine,
              srs_g2_1: G2Affine, srs_g2_tau: G2Affine) -> bool {
    // check e(com - [y]1, [1]2) == e(pi, [tau]2 - [z]2)
    let lhs = pairing(&(com - G1Affine::generator() * y), &srs_g2_1);
    let rhs = pairing(&pi, &(srs_g2_tau - G2Affine::generator() * z));
    lhs == rhs
}
```

Binding: two valid openings (z, y₁, π₁), (z, y₂, π₂) with y₁ ≠ y₂ subtract to a non-trivial relation breaking q-SDH, giving evaluation binding [8][3].

### 4.4 FRI, DEEP-FRI, and the STARK lineage

**FRI** tests, given oracle access to f₀: L₀ → F, whether f₀ is close to the Reed–Solomon code of a degree bound — the evaluation of a low-degree polynomial — using only a hash function [7]. Writing f(X) = f_even(X²) + X·f_odd(X²), the prover commits on challenge α to the fold Fold[f, α](Y) = f_even(Y) + α·f_odd(Y) on the squared domain L₁ = {x² : x ∈ L₀}, halving the domain. Folding is *local*: Fold[f, α](x²) needs only f(x) and f(−x), so the verifier checks layer consistency with two queries. After log(N/d) rounds the function is constant and checked directly; the query phase samples x₀ ∈ L₀, walks x_{i+1} = x_i², and verifies each fold.

> **Theorem (FRI complexity [7]):** For block length N, the prover costs below 6·N arithmetic operations; the verifier costs at most 21·log N arithmetic operations with 2·log N queries; words δ-far from the code are rejected with probability at least min{δ·(1 − o(1)), δ₀} for a constant δ₀ depending on the code rate.

One folding round is a dozen lines of Python:

```python
def fri_fold(coset_vals, alpha):
    """One FRI folding round over cosets {x, -x}; returns {x^2: folded}."""
    folded = {}
    for x, fx, fnx in coset_vals:
        f_even = (fx + fnx) / 2
        f_odd = (fx - fnx) / (2 * x)
        folded[x * x] = f_even + alpha * f_odd
    return folded

def fri_commit_phase(f0, domain, rhos):
    layers, oracles = [f0], [merkle_commit(f0)]
    f, L = f0, domain
    for alpha in rhos:                      # Fiat-Shamir challenges
        f = fri_fold(pair_cosets(f, L), alpha)
        L = [x * x for x in L[::2]]
        layers.append(f); oracles.append(merkle_commit(f))
    return layers, oracles
```

**STARKs** assemble FRI into a full system [9]: the computation becomes an AIR, the execution trace interpolates into polynomials, constraints form a composition polynomial, and the ALI protocol reduces everything to one low-degree test that FRI proves. Merkle commitments and hashed challenges make the system *transparent* and plausibly *post-quantum*; the price is proofs of tens to hundreds of kilobytes [4][9].

**DEEP-FRI** sharpens soundness by sampling *outside* the committed domain [11]: the prover opens the polynomial at a random point beyond L₀, forcing commitment to one global low-degree function. This lifts ALI soundness from a small constant below one-eighth to a constant arbitrarily close to 1, sharply cutting the repetitions needed for target security [11].

### 4.5 Recursion, folding, and the modern synthesis

The newest chapter composes proof systems with themselves. **Recursion** verifies a SNARK inside another SNARK's circuit, giving incrementally verifiable computation and aggregation of thousands of proofs into one — via curve cycles (Pasta) or FRI-native recursion (Plonky2). **Folding schemes** (Nova and generalizations) accumulate circuit satisfiability into a running instance checked once at the end. **Lookup arguments** (plookup, Caulk, cq, Lasso) prove wire values belong to a public table, making range checks, bitwise ops, and S-boxes cost O(1) gates [5]. Modern zkEVMs are PLONK-plus-lookups with KZG; modern STARK stacks are AIR-plus-FRI with DEEP sampling.

## 5 Empirical and Theoretical Analysis

### 5.1 Asymptotic comparison

| System | Arithmetization | Prover | Proof size | Verifier | Setup | Post-quantum |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Groth16 [1] | R1CS/QAP | O(n log n) | O(1) — 3 G | O(m) + 3 pairings | trusted, per-circuit | no |
| PLONK + KZG [2] | Plonkish | O(n log n) | O(1) — ~9 G | O(1) — ~10 pairings | trusted, universal | no |
| Sonic [10] | QAP-like | O(n log n) | O(1) | O(1) | trusted, universal | no |
| Bulletproofs | R1CS | O(n) | O(log n) | O(n) | transparent | no |
| STARK (FRI) [9] | AIR | O(n log n) | O(log² n) | O(log² n) | transparent | yes |
| PLONK + FRI | Plonkish | O(n log n) | O(log² n) | O(log² n) | transparent | yes |

The trade-off is legible: pairing-based systems minimize proof size and verifier work at the cost of trusted setup and quantum vulnerability; FRI-based systems remove trust and gain quantum resistance at two to three orders of magnitude more proof bytes [4].

### 5.2 Concrete estimates

For 2²⁰ ≈ one million gates at 128-bit security: **Groth16** proves in ≈ 200 bytes verified in milliseconds, but needs a circuit-specific ceremony [1][4]. **PLONK + KZG** proves in ≈ 400–500 bytes verified with ≈ 10 pairings, on one universal ceremony reused forever [2][4]. **STARK** proves in ≈ 100–200 KB verified in ≈ 10 ms, with no ceremony and post-quantum security [4][9]. **Bulletproofs** prove in ≈ 1.5 KB but verify in linear time — seconds at this scale [4].

### 5.3 Succinctness of the compiled argument

> **Theorem (Compilation preserves succinctness):** A polynomial IOP with r rounds and query complexity q, compiled with a commitment scheme of opening cost c_open, yields a non-interactive argument of proof size O(r·|com| + q·c_open) and verifier time O(r + q·t_open).

*Proof sketch.* Each oracle message becomes a commitment; each query is answered by an opening proof. With KZG, |com| and c_open are O(1), so constant-round IOPs like PLONK's give O(1) proofs. With FRI, each opening is a Merkle path of length O(log N) plus consistency checks, giving O(log² N). Soundness composes additively: IOP error plus binding failure, both negligible. ∎

### 5.4 Worked miniature example

Prove knowledge of x with x³ + x + 5 = 35. In Plonkish form this is two multiplication gates plus additions; selectors pick multiplication for squaring and cubing; σ wires the squaring gate's output into the cubing gate and pins the public output to 35. The prover commits to five small polynomials; the verifier checks the gate and grand-product identities at one random point; the batched KZG opening attests all evaluations at once. The proof is a few hundred bytes for a single-field-element witness — overhead essentially constant, which is precisely succinctness.

## 6 Limitations

**Trusted setup is the pairing world's original sin.** If any ceremony participant retains τ, soundness collapses; updatable ceremonies reduce this to one-honest-contributor, but every relying party must still verify the transcript [2].

**Quantum vulnerability.** Pairing- and discrete-log-based SNARKs fall to Shor's algorithm. Only the hash-based FRI/STARK line plausibly survives, under the stronger assumption that the hash resists quantum adversaries as a random oracle [9].

**Prover scalability.** Quasilinear proving is not fast proving: MSMs and NTTs over hundreds of millions of gates need GPU/FPGA acceleration and tens of gigabytes of RAM. The prover, not the verifier, is the practical bottleneck for zkEVMs and zkML.

**Arithmetization mismatch.** R1CS punishes non-algebraic operations; AIR punishes irregular control flow; Plonkish with lookups handles both better but enlarges the trusted codebase. No arithmetization dominates, and translation between them can cost an order of magnitude in constraints.

**Soundness subtleties.** Fiat–Shamir deployments have failed when challenges omit transcript parts such as public inputs. FRI's soundness analysis needed several revisions before DEEP-FRI stabilized the bounds [11]. Formal verification of SNARK implementations remains nascent.

**Zero-knowledge overhead.** Blinding adds degree and randomness to every committed polynomial; for small circuits it can dominate cost. Validity-only deployments (e.g., rollups) often drop zero knowledge for efficiency.

## 7 Conclusion

Zero-knowledge succinct proofs have converged on a clean architecture: arithmetize, design a polynomial IOP, compile with a polynomial commitment, apply Fiat–Shamir. The two great families are two instantiations of one compiler — PLONK's IOP with KZG gives the smallest proofs under a universal trusted setup, while the STARK IOP with FRI gives transparent post-quantum proofs at larger size [2][9]. The commitment layer, not the arithmetization, is the load-bearing design decision.

Three frontiers define the next decade. **Recursion and folding** turn SNARKs from one-shot arguments into a composable fabric, with proof-carrying data and incrementally verifiable computation entering deployment. **Lookup arguments and custom gates** close the arithmetization gap, making it cheap to prove the operations real programs perform [5]. **Prover acceleration** — hardware, better algorithms, proof splitting — attacks the one cost asymptotics cannot hide.

The methodological lesson: the polynomial-IOP compiler paradigm lets the information-theoretic core and the cryptographic compiler improve independently, with every improvement propagating to all systems built on it. That modularity is why a 2019 paper on permutations [2] and a 2018 paper on Reed–Solomon proximity testing [7] jointly underlie an industry's verification infrastructure.
