# SPDZ Secure Multi-Party Computation: Beaver Triples, MACed Secret Shares, and Actively Secure Protocols

## Abstract

Secure multi-party computation (MPC) enables *n* mutually distrusting parties to evaluate a joint function over private inputs without revealing them. The **SPDZ** protocol family, introduced by Damgård, Pastro, Smart, and Zakarias [1], is the canonical construction achieving **active security against a dishonest majority** — up to *n*−1 corruptions — while keeping the input-dependent online phase information-theoretic and cheap. Its core ingredients are **additive secret sharing authenticated by information-theoretic MACs** under a global key known to no single party, and **Beaver multiplication triples** [2] reducing every product gate to two public openings. Heavy cryptography — somewhat homomorphic encryption or oblivious transfer via MASCOT [3] — is confined to an input-independent preprocessing phase. Later work extended SPDZ to rings modulo 2^k (SPDZ2k [5]), integrated garbled-circuit techniques for Boolean sub-circuits [4], and proved security in the UC framework [6]. This thesis presents the full architecture: the sharing representation, the online protocol, the batch MAC check, OT-based triple generation, the 2^k adaptation, UC simulation arguments, measured performance, and open problems.

## 1 Introduction

Classical results of Yao [10] and Goldreich, Micali, and Wigderson [11] established that any efficiently computable function can be evaluated securely by two or more parties. For decades, however, the strongest adversary model — **active (malicious) corruption of a dishonest majority** — remained the province of theory: protocols required expensive public-key operations *per gate*, rendering large-scale computation impractical. Honest-majority protocols such as BGW [12] tolerate only *t* < *n*/3 (or *t* < *n*/2 with broadcast) corruptions, an assumption untenable when the participants are, say, competing banks or mutually suspicious jurisdictions.

SPDZ broke this impasse with an architectural insight that has since become the standard shape of maliciously secure arithmetic MPC [1]. Rather than authenticating the *computation* with cryptography at every step, SPDZ authenticates the *data*: every secret-shared value carries an information-theoretic MAC under a global key α that is itself secret-shared, so no party (and no coalition of *n*−1 parties) can forge a tag on a modified share except with negligible probability. Multiplication, the only non-linear operation, is outsourced to preprocessed **Beaver triples** — random shared tuples (⟦*a*⟧, ⟦*b*⟧, ⟦*ab*⟧) — so the online phase needs only local arithmetic and two broadcast openings per product gate [2]. The triples, being input-independent, are manufactured in an **offline phase** where expensive cryptography is amortized: the original construction used somewhat homomorphic encryption with zero-knowledge proofs of plaintext knowledge [1], while MASCOT replaced it with oblivious transfer and symmetric-key primitives, accelerating triple generation by more than two orders of magnitude [3].

This thesis dissects SPDZ from first principles. Section 2 recalls additive sharing, Beaver's trick, and the UC framework. Section 3 formalizes the protocol's methodology: the sharing semantics, the MAC algebra, and the two-phase methodology. Section 4 is a deep technical dive into the five pillars of the construction. Section 5 presents empirical performance figures and proof sketches. Section 6 discusses limitations — abort-only security, the preprocessing bottleneck, and the field-versus-ring tension — and Section 7 concludes with the protocol's legacy in modern privacy-preserving analytics.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The MPC Model and Adversary Classes

In secure *n*-party computation, parties *P₁, …, Pₙ* hold private inputs *x₁, …, xₙ* and wish to compute *y* = *f*(*x₁, …, xₙ*) such that each party learns *y* (and nothing else), even when an adversary corrupts a subset of parties. Adversaries are classified along two axes:

- **Semi-honest (passive):** corrupted parties follow the protocol but pool their views to infer secrets.
- **Malicious (active):** corrupted parties deviate arbitrarily — sending malformed messages, aborting early, or substituting inputs.

The corruption threshold matters enormously. A **dishonest majority** (*t* ≥ *n*/2, in the extreme *t* = *n*−1) rules out information-theoretic solutions entirely for general functionalities and, by Cleve's impossibility result [13], rules out *fairness* (guaranteed output delivery) and *guaranteed termination* for most functions. SPDZ therefore targets the strongest achievable notion in this setting: **security with abort** — either every honest party receives the correct output, or the protocol aborts with cheating detected.

### 2.2 Additive Secret Sharing

The simplest linear secret-sharing scheme splits a field element *x* ∈ 𝔽_p into shares *x₁, …, xₙ* with Σᵢ *x*ᵢ = *x*, where each *P*ᵢ holds *x*ᵢ. Any *n*−1 shares reveal nothing about *x*. Linearity is the scheme's superpower: given ⟦*x*⟧ and ⟦*y*⟧ (using ⟦·⟧ to denote a sharing), parties compute ⟦*x*+*y*⟧ and ⟦*c·x*⟧ **locally, with zero communication**, by adding or scaling their own shares. Multiplication of shared values is the hard operation — shares of a product cannot be derived locally — and is precisely the problem Beaver triples solve.

### 2.3 Beaver's Multiplication Trick

Beaver observed in 1991 [2] that a random triple (*a*, *b*, *c*) with *c* = *ab*, secret-shared in advance, reduces secure multiplication to openings of *masked* values. To multiply ⟦*x*⟧·⟦*y*⟧, parties open ε = *x* − *a* and δ = *y* − *b* (revealing nothing, since *a*, *b* are uniform and independent of the inputs), then compute

> **Theorem (Beaver [2]):** Given ⟦*a*⟧, ⟦*b*⟧, ⟦*c*⟧ with *c* = *ab*, the product ⟦*xy*⟧ = ⟦*c*⟧ + ε⟦*b*⟧ + δ⟦*a*⟧ + εδ is a correct sharing of *xy*, obtained with two openings and no further interaction.

The online cost of multiplication is thus constant and input-independent in its structure — the foundation of the preprocessing paradigm.

### 2.4 Universal Composability

Simulation-based security compares a real protocol execution against an *ideal* one in which a trusted functionality 𝓕 evaluates the function. The **UC framework** of Canetti [6] strengthens this by requiring indistinguishability against an *environment* that interacts with the adversary throughout the execution, yielding a **composition theorem**: a protocol proven UC-secure remains secure when composed concurrently with arbitrary other protocols. SPDZ's security is established in the 𝓕_OT-hybrid UC model with abort, the appropriate ideal functionality being arithmetic-circuit evaluation with identifiable abort [1][3].

## 3 Methodology

The SPDZ methodology rests on three design decisions that together tame the dishonest majority.

**Representation.** Every wire value *x* is held as a *MACed* additive sharing ⟦*x*⟧ = ((*x*ᵢ), (*m*ᵢ)): party *P*ᵢ stores a value share *x*ᵢ and a MAC share *m*ᵢ such that Σᵢ *x*ᵢ = *x* and Σᵢ *m*ᵢ = α·*x*, where α ∈ 𝔽_p is a **global MAC key**, itself additively shared and never reconstructed except inside the MAC check. Linear operations act homomorphically on both components: ⟦*x*⟧ + ⟦*y*⟧ adds value shares and MAC shares; *c*·⟦*x*⟧ scales both. Crucially, an adversary holding *n*−1 shares cannot produce a valid MAC share for a forged value share without guessing α — an information-theoretic guarantee independent of computational assumptions [1].

**Two-phase architecture.** The protocol is split into an **offline (preprocessing) phase**, which uses heavy cryptography to produce MACed Beaver triples, random shared values, and the MAC key, and an **online phase**, which consumes these to evaluate the circuit using only cheap field arithmetic and broadcasts. The offline phase is input-independent and can run before inputs are known; its cost is amortized over many evaluations.

**Deferred verification.** Rather than verifying every opened value when it appears, SPDZ accumulates all opened values and performs a **single batch MAC check** before outputs are released (and, in SPDZ2 [14], potentially earlier). The check commits parties to a random linear combination of (value, MAC) pairs, opens α, and verifies consistency — catching any tampering with probability 1 − 2/*p* while adding only O(1) amortized communication.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Authenticated Additive Sharing and the MAC Algebra

Formally, over a prime field 𝔽_p, the sharing ⟦*x*⟧ consists of shares (*x*₁, …, *x*ₙ) and (*m*₁, …, *m*ₙ) with reconstruction *x* = Σ *x*ᵢ and authentication condition Σ *m*ᵢ = α·*x*, where α = Σ αᵢ is the shared global key. The following Python sketch illustrates the representation and its linear homomorphism for three parties:

```python
import random
P = 2**61 - 1  # Mersenne prime, cf. MP-SPDZ's k=61 field

def share(x, n=3):
    """Return MACed additive sharing of x under fresh random key alpha."""
    alpha = random.randrange(P)
    xs = [random.randrange(P) for _ in range(n - 1)]
    xs.append((x - sum(xs)) % P)
    mac = (alpha * x) % P
    ms = [random.randrange(P) for _ in range(n - 1)]
    ms.append((mac - sum(ms)) % P)
    aks = [random.randrange(P) for _ in range(n - 1)]
    aks.append((alpha - sum(aks)) % P)
    return xs, ms, aks

def add(sh1, sh2):
    return ([(a + b) % P for a, b in zip(sh1[0], sh2[0])],
            [(a + b) % P for a, b in zip(sh1[1], sh2[1])])

def check(xs, ms, aks):
    alpha = sum(aks) % P
    x = sum(xs) % P
    return sum(ms) % P == (alpha * x) % P  # True iff untampered
```

Input sharing is itself non-trivial against active adversaries: a corrupt input owner could distribute inconsistent shares. SPDZ handles this by having the dealer broadcast encryptions or by the standard technique of sharing *x* and then MACing it inside the protocol — the MAC check at the end authenticates the entire transcript, so any inconsistency introduced at input time is caught before output release [1].

> **Theorem (MAC unforgeability [1]):** Let ⟦*x*⟧ be a MACed sharing with uniformly random α unknown to the adversary. If the adversary modifies its value share to *x*′ ≠ *x*, then any MAC share *m*′ it produces satisfies Σ*m*′ = α*x*′ with probability at most 1/*p* over the choice of α.

### 4.2 Beaver Triples: Multiplication in the Online Phase

The online multiplication protocol consumes one triple per product gate. Given ⟦*x*⟧, ⟦*y*⟧ and a triple (⟦*a*⟧, ⟦*b*⟧, ⟦*c*⟧) with *c* = *ab*:

| Step | Action | Cost |
|------|--------|------|
| 1 | Each *P*ᵢ computes εᵢ = *x*ᵢ − *a*ᵢ, δᵢ = *y*ᵢ − *b*ᵢ | local |
| 2 | Broadcast εᵢ, δᵢ; reconstruct ε, δ | 2 field elements / party |
| 3 | Compute ⟦*z*⟧ = ⟦*c*⟧ + ε·⟦*b*⟧ + δ·⟦*a*⟧ + εδ | local |
| 4 | MAC shares update identically (linearity) | local |

The MACs ride along for free: because step 3 is a *linear* combination of shared values, each party applies the same combination to its MAC shares, preserving the invariant Σ*m*ᵢ = α*z*. The opened values ε, δ are uniformly random masks, leaking nothing about *x*, *y* [2]. Additions and scalar multiplications cost nothing; the entire online phase is information-theoretic given the triples — no computational assumptions are used after preprocessing.

### 4.3 The Preprocessing Phase: SHE, MASCOT, and Overdrive

Generating MACed triples against an active adversary is the protocol's hard core. Three generations of solutions exist:

1. **SHE-based (original SPDZ [1]).** Parties encrypt shares under a distributed somewhat homomorphic encryption scheme (e.g., BGV), homomorphically compute encrypted products, and distributedly decrypt. Malicious behavior is blocked by zero-knowledge proofs of plaintext knowledge attached to every ciphertext — secure but extremely expensive: the reported throughput was roughly **23.5 multiplications/second** for two parties [3].
2. **OT-based (MASCOT [3]).** Keller, Orsini, and Scholl replaced homomorphic encryption with **oblivious transfer extension** (the KOS protocol [15] and its successors). Multiplication triples are built from correlated OTs: to multiply shared values, parties perform OTs whose choice bits encode one party's share and whose messages encode the other's. Consistency checks — sacrificing a fraction of triples to verify the rest — upgrade semi-honest OT multiplication to malicious security at less than 6× the semi-honest cost. The result: **4,842 multiplications/second** for two parties, a speedup exceeding 200× [3].
3. **Overdrive [7].** Keller, Pastro, and Rotaru revisited SHE with modern lattice packing (LowGear/HighGear), narrowing the gap for settings where OT's communication becomes the bottleneck.

The preprocessing also produces the global MAC key α in shared form and authenticates every triple: each of ⟦*a*⟧, ⟦*b*⟧, ⟦*c*⟧ carries MAC shares, and a **sacrifice step** opens a random linear combination of two triples to verify *c* = *ab*, discarding triples that fail [1][3].

### 4.4 Rings, SPDZ2k, and Garbled-Circuit Techniques

SPDZ natively computes over a prime field 𝔽_p, but real programs compute modulo 2^k — native CPU integer arithmetic. **SPDZ2k** (Cramer, Damgård, Escudero, Scholl, Xing [5]) solved the long-standing open problem of dishonest-majority MPC over the ring ℤ_{2^k}. The obstacle is fundamental: the MAC-forgery argument needs a field, since a zero divisor could annihilate the check. SPDZ2k's elegant fix computes over the larger ring ℤ_{2^{k+s}} with *s* bits of statistical slack: MACs are checked modulo 2^{k+s}, and any forgery succeeds with probability at most 2^{−s}, even though 2 is not invertible [5]. Triple generation reuses MASCOT adapted to the ring setting, and Overdrive2k [9] later provided an SHE-based alternative with improved ciphertext packing. The payoff is concrete: arithmetic maps directly onto 32- and 64-bit machine words, which is decisive for applications like privacy-preserving machine learning [8].

For **Boolean circuits**, the SPDZ family turns to garbled-circuit techniques in the tradition of **Beaver, Micali, and Rogaway (BMR)** [4], whose 1990 construction gave constant-round multi-party garbling. Modern frameworks such as MP-SPDZ [8] implement BMR-style garbled circuits for the dishonest-majority setting and provide *conversions* between SPDZ arithmetic shares and garbled-circuit wire labels, enabling hybrid protocols that evaluate arithmetic-heavy segments with triples and comparison-heavy segments with garbling. The same MACed-sharing discipline — authenticate everything, verify in batch — carries over, and the UC proofs compose across the conversion boundary [6].

The batch MAC check itself can be specified rigorously; the following TLA⁺ fragment captures its commit-then-open structure:

```tla
------------------------------ MODULE MACCheck ------------------------------
EXTENDS Naturals, FiniteSets
CONSTANTS Parties, Field, OpenedVals
VARIABLES committed, alpha_opened, abort

Init == committed = [p \in Parties |-> {}]
        /\ alpha_opened = FALSE /\ abort = FALSE

Commit(p, sigma_p) == /\ ~alpha_opened
                     /\ committed' = [committed EXCEPT ![p] = @ \cup {sigma_p}]
Check == /\ \A p \in Parties : committed[p] /= {}
         /\ alpha_opened' = TRUE
         /\ LET sigma == SumShares(committed)
            IN IF sigma = 0 THEN abort' = FALSE ELSE abort' = TRUE
=============================================================================
```

### 4.5 UC Security: Simulation with Abort

SPDZ's security theorem is stated in Canetti's UC framework [6]: the protocol **UC-realizes the ideal functionality 𝓕_MPC** (arithmetic-circuit evaluation with abort) in the 𝓕_OT-hybrid model against any static malicious adversary corrupting up to *n*−1 parties. The proof's simulator is instructive:

- **Extraction:** During input sharing, the simulator observes the adversary's messages and extracts its effective inputs, forwarding them to 𝓕_MPC.
- **Equivocation:** In the online phase, the simulator plays with dummy (e.g., zero) shares for honest parties; because openings ε, δ are uniformly random masks, the adversary's view is identically distributed to a real run regardless of the true inputs.
- **MAC-check programming:** The simulator forces the batch check to pass exactly when the real protocol would, and triggers the ideal-world abort otherwise. The 2/*p* soundness error appears as the simulation's distinguishing gap — negligible for κ-bit primes [1][3].

Composition via the UC theorem then guarantees that SPDZ remains secure when run concurrently with arbitrary protocols — the property that makes it deployable as a subroutine inside larger privacy-preserving systems [6].

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

The MASCOT paper [3] reports the definitive head-to-head preprocessing comparison (κ = 128-bit statistical security, *n* parties):

| Protocol | Domain | Communication per mult. | Throughput, *n* = 2 (mult/s) |
|----------|--------|------------------------|-------------------------------|
| SPDZ (active, SHE) [1] | 𝔽_p | 215·*n*(*n*−1) kbit | 23.5 |
| SPDZ (covert) | 𝔽_p | 66·*n*(*n*−1) kbit | 204 |
| **MASCOT (active, OT) [3]** | 𝔽_p | **180·*n*(*n*−1) kbit** | **4,842** |
| MASCOT (active, OT) [3] | 𝔽_{2^m} | 180·*n*(*n*−1) kbit | 4,827 |

The online phase, common to all variants, evaluates roughly **millions of gates per second** on LAN networks in MP-SPDZ [8], since each multiplication is two broadcasts of field elements. The MAC check adds a single round of commitments plus one opening of α, amortized over the entire circuit.

**Proof sketch — batch MAC check soundness.** Suppose opened values *a*₁, …, *a*_t were tampered with, so Σ*m*_{j} ≠ α·Σ…; precisely, define the error Δ = Σⱼ *r*ⱼ·(γⱼ − α·*a*ⱼ) where *r*ⱼ are public random coefficients chosen *after* the adversary commits to its shares, and γⱼ are the claimed MACs. If any tampering occurred, Δ is a non-zero linear polynomial in the *r*ⱼ over 𝔽_p; by the Schwartz–Zippel lemma it vanishes with probability at most 1/*p* (the factor of 2 in the 2/*p* bound accounts for the adversary's freedom in the commitment phase) [1]. Hence cheating is caught except with negligible probability, and the protocol aborts before any output is released — no information about honest inputs leaks through the abort itself, because the check's outcome is independent of the secret values.

## 6 Limitations

SPDZ's guarantees come with sharp, well-understood boundaries. **No fairness:** with a dishonest majority, Cleve's impossibility [13] dictates that a corrupt party can always learn the output and then abort before honest parties do; SPDZ achieves *security with abort* and nothing stronger. **Preprocessing dominates cost:** even with MASCOT's 200× speedup, generating triples remains orders of magnitude more expensive than consuming them, and its O(*n*²) communication per triple limits scalability to large committees — an active research frontier (cf. recent constant-online-communication protocols [16]). **The ring/field tension:** SPDZ2k's 2^{k+s} lifting buys native integer arithmetic at the cost of *s* extra bits of communication per value and a more delicate MAC analysis; comparisons and bit-decomposition remain disproportionately expensive, motivating the hybrid garbled-circuit conversions of Section 4.4. **Covert vs. active trade-offs:** deployments that accept deterrence-based (covert) security can cut preprocessing cost substantially [14], but the choice must be deliberate, not accidental. Finally, the UC proof assumes a static adversary and an honest-majority-free setup for OT; adaptive corruptions and weaker setups require additional machinery [6].

---

## 7 Conclusion

SPDZ transformed maliciously secure MPC from a theoretical possibility into an engineering discipline. Its enduring contributions are architectural rather than merely cryptographic: **authenticate the data, not the computation**, via information-theoretic MACs under a shared global key; **separate input-independent preprocessing from the online phase**, so that the cheap part of the protocol is also the part that touches secrets; and **verify in batch**, amortizing the cost of active security to near zero per gate. From SHE-based origins [1] through the OT revolution of MASCOT [3], the ring-arithmetic of SPDZ2k [5], and the BMR-flavored garbled extensions for Boolean logic [4][8], the family has steadily closed the gap between semi-honest and malicious performance — Damgård has noted efficiency gains of roughly three orders of magnitude over the protocol's lifetime, with commercial deployment through ventures such as Partisia and Sepior [17]. The open problems — sublinear preprocessing, large-scale committees, and seamless arithmetic–Boolean hybrids — define the next decade of dishonest-majority MPC.
