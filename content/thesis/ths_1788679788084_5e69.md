# Read Disturbance in Modern DRAM: A Unified Treatment of RowHammer, RowPress, and Their Defenses

## Abstract

Dynamic random-access memory (DRAM) has become one of the most security-critical components of computing, not because of its functional specification but because of its physics. Repeated activation of a single DRAM row corrupts data in physically adjacent rows without ever accessing them—**RowHammer** [1]. More recently, keeping an aggressor row open for an extended duration was shown to induce failures with orders of magnitude fewer activations, a distinct phenomenon termed **RowPress** [2]. This thesis develops a unified treatment of DRAM read disturbance from first principles: the charge-leakage physics of neighboring and passing wordlines, the access-pattern taxonomy from single-sided hammering through many-sided, non-uniform, distance-two-assisted, and long-open patterns, and the mitigation lineage of PARA, Target Row Refresh, DDR5 Refresh Management, and per-row activation counting. We present analytical models of the read-disturbance threshold and the aggressor-row on-time scaling law, characterize the TRR-bypass lineage (TRRespass, Blacksmith, Half-Double, Phoenix), survey exploitation from Project Zero's original privilege escalation through Drammer, RAMBleed, and ZenHammer, and explain why every deployed defense has been bypassed. We close with limitations, open problems, and the constraints any provably secure mitigation must satisfy.

## 1. Introduction

Memory isolation—the guarantee that accessing one memory location cannot alter the contents of another—is a bedrock assumption of operating systems, hypervisors, and language runtimes. DRAM, the dominant main-memory technology, violates this assumption. In 2014, Kim et al. demonstrated that repeatedly opening (*activating*) and closing (*precharging*) a DRAM row tens of thousands of times within a refresh window induces bit flips in adjacent rows on 110 of 129 tested DDR3 modules [1]. Because the aggressor rows can belong to an unprivileged user process while the victim rows belong to the kernel or another process, the phenomenon is a direct breach of memory protection, and Project Zero demonstrated a working privilege-escalation exploit within a year [3].

For nearly a decade, the industry treated RowHammer as an *activation-count* problem: failures were a function of how many times aggressor rows were toggled. This mental model collapsed in 2023 when Luo et al. showed that keeping an aggressor row *open* for a long time—rather than toggling it rapidly—induces bit flips with up to two orders of magnitude fewer activations [2]. The two phenomena share the substrate (charge coupling between adjacent wordlines) but differ in their governing device physics, their flipped-bit directionality, and the mitigation strategies that contain them.

> **Thesis statement:** DRAM read disturbance comprises two physically distinct mechanisms—activation-driven (RowHammer) and open-time-driven (RowPress)—whose interaction with proprietary, undocumented in-DRAM mitigations creates an attack–defense asymmetry: every deployed defense to date has been bypassed by pattern-space search, and provable security requires open, specified, per-row activation accounting.

The contributions of this thesis are:

1. A device-physics model explaining both RowHammer and RowPress through neighboring-wordline and passing-wordline coupling [4].
2. A taxonomy of access patterns: single-sided, double-sided, many-sided (TRRespass), non-uniform/frequency-domain (Blacksmith), distance-two-assisted (Half-Double), and extended-open (RowPress/ScaleDisturb).
3. Analytical models of the *RowHammer threshold* (TRH) and the aggressor-row-on-time (tAggON) scaling law.
4. A systematic account of defenses—PARA, TRR, RFM, PRAC—and the precise mechanisms by which each was bypassed.
5. An evaluation of exploitation from kernel privilege escalation to confidentiality breaks (RAMBleed), mobile compromise (Drammer), and DDR5 exploitation (Phoenix).
6. A statement of the open problems that separate current practice from a provably secure DRAM substrate.

## 2. Background

### DRAM organization and operation

A DRAM chip is organized into *banks*, each a two-dimensional array of cells. A cell is one transistor plus one capacitor (1T1C): charge stored on the capacitor encodes the bit, and the transistor's gate is controlled by a *wordline* shared across a row. Reading a row proceeds in three phases:

1. **Activation (ACT):** the wordline is raised, connecting every cell in the row to its *bitline*; charge sharing perturbs the bitline voltage, and the *sense amplifier* amplifies the deviation to a full logic level, restoring the cell charge.
2. **Row open:** the row remains in the row buffer while column accesses (READ/WRITE) occur. The wordline stays asserted for the entire open interval.
3. **Precharge (PRE):** the wordline is lowered and bitlines are equalized, closing the row.

JEDEC timing parameters bound these phases: tRAS is the minimum ACT-to-PRE interval, tRP the minimum PRE-to-ACT interval, and tREFI (7.8 µs for DDR4) the average refresh interval, with every row refreshed once per tREFW (64 ms) [5]. Cells leak charge continuously and must be refreshed before the weakest cell's retention time expires.

### The RowHammer phenomenon

Kim et al. [1] showed that repeated ACT–PRE cycling of one or two *aggressor rows* accelerates charge leakage in the *victim rows* physically adjacent to them, eventually flipping bits. Their key findings: 110 of 129 DDR3 modules exhibited errors; the earliest vulnerable modules dated to 2010; the minimum activation count to first failure was ~139K for DDR3; and simple *error-correction codes (ECC)* or *probabilistic adjacent row activation (PARA)* could mitigate the effect at modest cost.

Seaborn and Dullien (Project Zero) weaponized the primitive in 2015 [3]: by spraying page-table entries across DRAM and hammering, they induced a flip in a page-table entry's physical-address field, gaining write access to their own page tables and hence to all of physical memory—full kernel privilege escalation from unprivileged code.

### The RowPress phenomenon

RowPress, characterized by Luo et al. on 164 real DDR4 chips [2], keeps the aggressor row open for a long time (tAggON ≫ tRAS) by issuing many column reads to different cache blocks within the row before precharging. The results were striking:

- The *minimum activation count* (ACmin) to first bit flip drops by **1–2 orders of magnitude** as tAggON grows from tRAS to 7.8 µs (one tREFI).
- RowPress bit flips have the **opposite direction** to RowHammer flips (charged→discharged vs. discharged→charged dominance), indicating a distinct physical mechanism.
- Nearly all tested DDR4 chips were vulnerable; the effect scales with technology node.

Combined RowHammer+RowPress patterns induce the first flip up to **46.1% faster** than RowPress alone, and flip *different* bit positions—evidence that the two mechanisms disturb cells through different coupling paths [5].

### Threat model

We assume an attacker who can execute unprivileged code (native or JavaScript), flush cache lines (e.g., via `clflush`), and measure time. The attacker knows the DRAM address mapping only through reverse engineering. Defenses are the in-DRAM mechanisms (TRR, RFM), the memory controller (ECC, increased refresh), and OS-level isolation. The attacker's goal is integrity violation (bit flips in victim data), privilege escalation, or confidentiality breach.

## 3. Methodology

### Device-level characterization

State-of-the-art read-disturbance research follows a two-pronged methodology. **FPGA-based testing infrastructures** (SoftMC, DRAM Bender) issue raw DRAM commands with cycle-accurate timing to bare chips, sweeping activation counts, tAggON, data patterns, and temperature while recording per-cell failures [2][4][5]. **Production-setting studies** (TRRespass, Blacksmith, Half-Double) reverse-engineer the DRAM address mapping on real systems and hammer from user space, synchronizing with refresh to defeat in-DRAM mitigations [6][7][8].

Our analytical treatment synthesizes results from 164 DDR4 chips (RowPress) [2], 84 DDR4 chips (combined patterns) [5], 40 DDR4 devices across four vendors (Blacksmith) [7], 42 DDR4 modules (TRRespass) [6], and 15 DDR5 chips (Phoenix, 2021–2024) [9]. Temperature is controlled or recorded because leakage and thresholds are temperature-sensitive; data patterns use the checkerboard (0xAA/0x55) known to maximize error coverage [1].

### Metrics

- **TRH (RowHammer threshold):** minimum aggressor activations to first bit flip in a victim row under double-sided hammering.
- **ACmin(tAggON):** minimum activations to first flip as a function of aggressor open time—the RowPress scaling law.
- **HCfirst / BER:** hammer count to first failure and bit-error rate for pattern comparison.
- **RDT (read disturbance threshold):** the contemporary unified term for the activation budget a row tolerates [4].

### Modeling approach

We model victim-cell charge as a leaky integrator driven by aggressor activity, distinguishing *neighboring-wordline* (NWL) coupling—cells sharing the active region—and *passing-wordline* (PWL) coupling—wordlines routed through the shallow trench isolation region without forming transistors [4]. RowHammer maps to repeated NWL toggling; RowPress maps to sustained PWL electric-field exposure. The models are validated against the opposite-direction flip signature and the tAggON scaling law.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Device physics: why disturbance happens

A DRAM cell's capacitor leaks through several paths: subthreshold conduction of the access transistor, gate-induced drain leakage (GIDL), and trap-assisted tunneling. An asserted wordline raises the electric field across neighboring cells' access transistors. Two geometries matter [4]:

- **Neighboring wordline (NWL):** the wordline of the adjacent row in the same active region. Toggling it repeatedly (RowHammer) pumps charge out of victim cells through repeated field transients—each ACT–PRE cycle injects a small disturbance, and failures occur when the accumulated loss crosses the sense-amplifier margin before the next refresh.
- **Passing wordline (PWL):** a wordline that passes *over* a victim cell's active region through isolation oxide without contacting it. Holding it high for a long time (RowPress) applies a sustained field that enhances leakage continuously—hence the strong tAggON dependence and the opposite flip direction, since the coupling geometry differs.

> **Theorem:** *For a victim cell with sense margin M and per-event disturbance δ, the number of events to failure scales as M/δ; RowHammer minimizes δ per event but maximizes event rate, while RowPress maximizes δ per event through sustained field exposure, minimizing the event count.* [2][4]

Technology scaling shrinks cell capacitance and wordline pitch simultaneously, increasing coupling while reducing margins—so TRH has fallen from ~139K (DDR3) to ~10K (DDR4) to ~4.8K (LPDDR4) [1][10].

### 4.2 The access-pattern taxonomy

| Pattern | Aggressors | Key idea | Year |
|---|---|---|---|
| Single-sided | 1 adjacent row | Simplest; weakest | 2014 |
| Double-sided | 2 rows sandwiching victim | Maximizes coupling; standard benchmark | 2014 |
| Many-sided (TRRespass) | *n* > 2 rows | Overflows TRR tracking tables | 2020 [6] |
| Non-uniform (Blacksmith) | *n* rows, varied frequency/phase | Evades TRR samplers in the frequency domain | 2022 [7] |
| Half-Double | Far aggressor A (many) + near B (few) | Distance-2 assisted disturbance | 2021 [8] |
| RowPress | Long tAggON | Open-time-driven mechanism | 2023 [2] |
| ScaleDisturb | Asymmetric timing | Temporal asymmetry amplifies disturbance | 2025 [11] |

**TRRespass** [6] observed that in-DRAM TRR tracks only a limited number of aggressor rows; hammering *many* rows (e.g., 9–19-sided patterns) overflows the tracker so that some aggressors are never mitigated, flipping bits on 13 of 42 DDR4 modules. **Blacksmith** [7] found that TRR *samples* aggressor activity only at certain points within each tREFI; by distributing activations non-uniformly across aggressors in the frequency domain, its fuzzer evaded sampling and flipped bits on **all 40** tested DDR4 devices from Samsung, Micron, SK Hynix, and a fourth vendor. **Half-Double** [8] (Google) showed that TRR only refreshes the *immediate* neighbors of a tracked aggressor: hammering row A very many times plus row B a few dozen times flips bits in row C at distance two—outside the mitigation's blast radius. Each bypass exploits the same root cause: the defense's model of the attack is narrower than the physics of the attack.

### 4.3 RowPress: the open-time scaling law

The central quantitative result of RowPress is the ACmin(tAggON) curve [2]. As tAggON increases from tRAS (~36 ns) toward tREFI (7.8 µs):

- ACmin falls by **10×–100×** across vendors; some chips flip with only a few hundred activations.
- The effect saturates near one tREFI because refresh intervenes.
- Bit-flip *direction* inverts relative to RowHammer, and the set of vulnerable cells differs—combined patterns flip bits that neither pure pattern flips [5].

```c
// Canonical RowPress access loop (after Luo et al. [2])
for (iter = 0; iter < NUM_ITER; iter++) {
    for (i = 0; i < NUM_AGGR_ACTS; i++) {
        for (j = 0; j < NUM_READS; j++)   // keep row open long
            *(volatile char *)AGGR1[j];
        for (j = 0; j < NUM_READS; j++)
            *(volatile char *)AGGR2[j];
        for (j = 0; j < NUM_READS; j++) { // evict from cache
            _mm_clflushopt(AGGR1[j]);
            _mm_clflushopt(AGGR2[j]);
        }
        _mm_mfence();
    }
    activate_dummy_rows();  // desynchronize TRR tracking
}
record_bitflips(VICTIM);
```

The practical consequence is severe: any mitigation calibrated purely on activation counts (TRR thresholds, RFM budgets) underestimates the threat by orders of magnitude when the attacker controls row-open time—which, on real memory controllers with open-page policies, the attacker largely does.

### 4.4 The defense lineage and its bypasses

**PARA (2014).** On each row close, with small probability *p*, refresh the adjacent rows. Provably secure if *p* is set from the true TRH, with negligible overhead—but it requires the controller to know adjacency, and vendors never deployed it as specified [1].

**TRR (Target Row Refresh, DDR4 era).** In-DRAM logic tracks frequently activated rows (via counters or sampling) and issues extra refreshes to their neighbors during REF commands. Implementations are proprietary and undocumented. TRRespass overflowed the trackers; Blacksmith evaded the samplers; Half-Double stepped outside the ±1 neighbor radius; U-TRR reverse-engineered the internals and crafted per-device custom patterns flipping bits on 45 modules [12]. The lesson: *security by obscurity fails when the attacker can experiment*—TRR's secrecy delayed but never prevented bypass.

**DDR5: RFM and PRAC.** DDR5 replaces vendor-opaque TRR with architected mechanisms: *Refresh Management (RFM)* counts per-bank activations and, at a threshold (RFMTH), forces the controller to issue refresh-management commands targeting hot regions; *Per-Row Activation Counting (PRAC)* adds exact per-row counters with back-off signaling. Yet **Phoenix** (2025) bypassed DDR5 TRR/RFM by exploiting unsampled refresh intervals, achieving the first RowHammer privilege escalation on a production DDR5 desktop in **109 seconds** across all 15 tested chips (2021–2024) [9]. **ZenHammer** (2024) reverse-engineered AMD address functions and flipped bits on Zen 2/3 DDR4 systems and one Zen 4 DDR5 system [13].

> **Theorem:** *Any mitigation that tracks a bounded set of aggressors, samples activations at bounded rate, or refreshes a bounded neighborhood is bypassable by a pattern that exceeds the bound in the corresponding dimension—count, rate, or distance.* The bypass lineage is a constructive proof.

**ECC.** SECDED ECC corrects single-bit errors per word, but RowHammer induces *multi-bit* failures and ECC itself has been defeated end-to-end: **ECCploit** and **ECC.fail** (2025) demonstrated exploitation on ECC-protected DDR4 servers [14][15]. On-die ECC in DDR5 corrects only single-bit errors *within the die* and is invisible to the controller—it improves yield, not security.

### 4.5 Exploitation case studies

1. **Project Zero (2015)** [3]: PTE spraying + double-sided hammering → kernel privilege escalation on x86-64 Linux. Established RowHammer as a practical exploit primitive.
2. **Drammer (2016)**: deterministic RowHammer on ARM/Android without root or special permissions, escalating to kernel via `mmap` massaging—bringing the attack to mobile.
3. **RAMBleed (2019)**: turned the integrity primitive into a *confidentiality* break, reading OpenSSH secret keys from neighboring rows by observing flip probabilities conditioned on victim data.
4. **SMASH / TRRespass (2020)** [6]: many-sided patterns from JavaScript, bypassing TRR in browsers.
5. **Half-Double (2021)** [8]: distance-two assisted flips, defeating neighbor-refresh defenses.
6. **Blacksmith (2022)** [7]: non-uniform frequency-domain patterns; 40/40 DDR4 devices flipped; vendors confirmed.
7. **ZenHammer (2024)** [13]: AMD-specific; first DDR5 flips on Zen 4.
8. **Phoenix (2025)** [9]: DDR5 RFM/TRR bypass → root in 109 s on production desktop; RSA-2048 SSH key targeting demonstrated.

Each case study widens either the *attacker capability* (JavaScript, network, GPU—see GPUHammer on GDDR6/HBM [10]) or the *victim impact* (integrity → confidentiality → availability).

## 5. Empirical Results and Proofs

We consolidate the quantitative record across the characterization literature:

| Study | Chips | Key result |
|---|---|---|
| Kim et al. 2014 [1] | 129 DDR3 modules | 110 vulnerable; TRH ≈ 139K |
| TRRespass 2020 [6] | 42 DDR4 + 13 LPDDR4 | Bypass on 13/42 DDR4; many-sided patterns |
| U-TRR 2021 [12] | 45 DDR4 modules | Custom per-device patterns; ECC insufficient |
| Half-Double 2021 [8] | Google DDR4 pool | Distance-2 flips with ~dozens of near-aggressor ACTs |
| Blacksmith 2022 [7] | 40 DDR4 (4 vendors) | 40/40 flipped via non-uniform patterns |
| RowPress 2023 [2] | 164 DDR4 | ACmin ↓ 10–100× with tAggON; opposite flip direction |
| Combined RH+RP 2024 [5] | 84 DDR4 | First flip up to 46.1% faster; distinct flip sets |
| VRD 2025 [16] | DDR4/DDR5 | Read-disturbance threshold varies over time per row |
| ZenHammer 2024 [13] | 30 AMD systems | 7/10 Zen 2, 6/10 Zen 3, 1/10 Zen 4 DDR5 |
| Phoenix 2025 [9] | 15 DDR5 (2021–24) | 15/15 bypassed; root in 109 s |

**Analytical model.** Let a victim cell tolerate disturbance budget *B* (in activation-equivalents). Under double-sided RowHammer, each ACT–PRE pair contributes disturbance *d_H*; failure when *N·d_H ≥ B*, i.e., TRH = *B/d_H*. Under RowPress with open time *t*, each activation contributes *d_H + α·t* (sustained-field term); thus

```
ACmin(t) = B / (d_H + α·t)
```

which reproduces the measured hyperbolic decline of ACmin with tAggON [2]. Temperature enters through *B(T)* (leakage ↑ ⇒ *B* ↓) and *α(T)*.

> **Theorem:** *A mitigation is sound iff for every aggressor row r and every victim row v within coupling distance, the mitigation's preventive-refresh rate exceeds the maximum disturbance rate any attacker pattern can apply to v.* PARA with *p ≥* TRH⁻¹·(margin) satisfies this; bounded trackers, samplers, and fixed neighborhoods do not. [1][4]

**Variable read disturbance (VRD)** [16] adds a temporal dimension: a row's threshold varies over time, so one-time profiling underestimates vulnerability—mitigations must use the *minimum* observed threshold with margin, not the mean.

## 6. Limitations and Open Problems

1. **Proprietary opacity.** TRR/RFM implementations remain undocumented; research proceeds by black-box reverse engineering (U-TRR [12]). JEDEC should mandate *specified, testable* mitigation semantics.
2. **Pattern-space completeness.** Blacksmith's fuzzer and TRRespass's many-sided search sample an infinite pattern space; no defense has been *proven* complete against all patterns, only tested against known ones.
3. **RowPress-aware accounting.** RFM/PRAC count activations, not open time. An attacker maximizing tAggON within the activation budget defeats count-based accounting by design [2][11]. Mitigations must bound the *disturbance integral*, not just the count.
4. **Performance–security tension.** Tripling the refresh rate stops Phoenix [9] but costs power and bandwidth; PRAC's back-off signaling stalls the controller. Deployable security needs <1% overhead.
5. **Confidentiality.** RAMBleed showed flips leak data; disturbance-based *read* primitives remain under-explored relative to write primitives.
6. **New substrates.** GPUHammer extends the threat to GDDR6/HBM2e/3 [10]; 3D-stacked and high-bandwidth memories have different coupling geometries needing fresh characterization.
7. **Benign-triggered failures.** As TRH approaches benign workload activation rates, systems may see spontaneous flips or mitigation-induced slowdowns without any attacker [13]—a reliability cliff, not just a security one.

## 7. Conclusion

A decade of research has transformed RowHammer from a curiosity into a standing counterexample to memory isolation, and RowPress has shown the phenomenon is broader and cheaper to trigger than the activation-count model admitted. The empirical record is unambiguous: **every widely deployed mitigation—TRR in its vendor-specific forms, DDR5 refresh management, and ECC—has been bypassed** by patterns that exploit the gap between the defense's model and the device's physics [6][7][8][9]. The path forward is equally clear. Provable security requires (i) exact per-row activation *and* open-time accounting (PRAC extended to disturbance integrals), (ii) specified rather than secret mitigation semantics, (iii) thresholds derived from the minimum observed RDT across temperature, data pattern, and time (accounting for VRD [16]), and (iv) validation against combined RowHammer+RowPress patterns rather than either alone. Until then, DRAM remains a substrate where physics, not privilege rings, decides who can corrupt whose memory.
