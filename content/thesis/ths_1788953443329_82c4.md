# From Squid Axons to Population Codes: The Hodgkin–Huxley Formalism and the Statistical Foundations of Neural Coding

## Abstract

The 1952 Hodgkin–Huxley (HH) papers stand as the most consequential quantitative achievement in cellular neurophysiology: a set of coupled nonlinear differential equations, fitted to voltage-clamp data from the squid giant axon, that predicted the shape, threshold behavior, and propagation velocity of the action potential from first principles of ionic conductance. This thesis reconstructs that achievement — the voltage-clamp experiments, the gating-variable formalism, and cable-theoretic propagation — then traces the two great descendants of the HH program: its mathematical reductions (FitzHugh–Nagumo, integrate-and-fire) and its conceptual complement, the neural coding problem. We develop the statistical machinery of spike-train analysis — rate versus temporal coding, the Poisson hypothesis, tuning curves and population vectors — and derive the Fisher-information and Cramér–Rao bounds that quantify how well populations of neurons can be decoded. We close with modern extensions: stochastic HH models of channel noise and dendritic computation, where single neurons implement multi-layer functions.

*— anon#4821*

## 1. Introduction

Few moments in the history of science match the audacity of what Alan Hodgkin and Andrew Huxley accomplished between 1949 and 1952. Working with the giant axon of the squid *Loligo forbesi* — a single axon up to a millimeter in diameter, large enough to thread with an electrode — they set out to do something that had never been done: to *measure* the ionic currents flowing through an excitable membrane as a function of voltage and time, to *fit* those measurements to a mathematical model, and then to *predict*, by numerical integration performed on a hand-cranked calculator, the full waveform of the propagating action potential. The prediction succeeded [1][2].

The significance of this work extends in two directions. Downward, into the membrane: the HH formalism introduced the **gating variable** — the idea that macroscopic conductance is the product of microscopic, voltage-dependent, statistically independent gating particles — an idea that anticipated the molecular biology of ion channels by decades. Upward, into the brain: once the mechanism of the spike was understood, the question of what the spike *means* became unavoidable. The HH model answers "how does a neuron fire?"; the **neural coding problem** asks "what does the firing say?" [6].

This thesis treats these two problems as one intellectual arc. We begin with the experiments (§2), formalize the mathematics (§3), descend into the deep technical structure of the model and its reductions (§4), subject the coding problem to statistical analysis with information-theoretic guarantees (§5), acknowledge the boundaries of the framework (§6), and conclude with the modern synthesis (§7).

## 2. Background

### 2.1 The excitability problem before 1952

By the late 1940s, electrophysiologists knew that the action potential was an electrical event: the membrane potential of a nerve fiber swings from roughly −60 mV to nearly +50 mV and back within about a millisecond, propagating without decrement along the axon. Bernstein's membrane theory (1902) had proposed that the resting potential arises from selective potassium permeability and that excitation involves a transient breakdown of membrane selectivity, but the ionic identities and kinetics of the currents were unknown. The key experimental obstacle was that in a freely propagating spike, **voltage and current vary together** — one cannot measure a current–voltage relationship when the voltage itself is a moving target.

### 2.2 The voltage clamp

The solution was the **voltage clamp**, developed independently by Cole and by Marmont around 1949. An electronic feedback circuit injects whatever current is needed to hold the membrane potential at a commanded step value; by Kirchhoff's laws, the injected current is then equal and opposite to the ionic current through the membrane. Voltage-clamp experiments on the squid giant axon [1][2] revealed:

1. **Two separable ionic currents.** Replacing extracellular sodium with impermeant choline abolished the early, transient inward current while leaving the late, sustained outward current intact. The early current was carried by **Na⁺**, the late by **K⁺**, with a small residual **leak** current (largely Cl⁻ and others).
2. **Distinct kinetics.** The sodium conductance rose rapidly upon depolarization and then *inactivated* even while depolarization persisted; the potassium conductance rose more slowly and did not inactivate. The two conductances had voltage-dependent steady states and time constants.
3. **Independence of gating.** Shifting the holding potential before a test pulse changed the *amplitude* but not the *time course* of the sodium current, suggesting activation and inactivation were separate processes — the empirical seed of the *m* and *h* variables.

### 2.3 The equivalent circuit

Hodgkin and Huxley represented the membrane as an electrical circuit [1]: a capacitance *Cₘ* (the lipid bilayer) in parallel with three ionic branches, each a voltage-dependent conductance in series with a battery representing the ion's electrochemical driving force:

![Hodgkin–Huxley equivalent circuit: membrane capacitance in parallel with sodium, potassium, and leak conductances, each with its reversal-potential battery](/thesis/ths_1788953443329_82c4-0.webp)

## 3. Methodology

Our methodology is threefold: (i) faithful reconstruction of the HH differential equations with standard parameter values; (ii) analytical reduction to low-dimensional models via timescale separation; (iii) statistical treatment of spike trains as point processes, with decoding bounds derived from estimation theory.

### 3.1 The Hodgkin–Huxley equations

Let *V* be the membrane potential in mV (absolute convention), *Cₘ* = 1 μF/cm² the specific capacitance. The total membrane current is:

$$C_m \frac{dV}{dt} = I_{\mathrm{ext}} - \bar{g}_{Na} m^3 h\, (V - E_{Na}) - \bar{g}_K n^4\, (V - E_K) - g_L\, (V - E_L)$$

The gating variables *m* (Na⁺ activation), *h* (Na⁺ inactivation), and *n* (K⁺ activation) obey first-order kinetics:

$$\frac{dx}{dt} = \alpha_x(V)\,(1 - x) - \beta_x(V)\,x = \frac{x_\infty(V) - x}{\tau_x(V)}, \qquad x \in \{m, h, n\}$$

with the classic voltage-dependent rate functions (shifted to absolute potential) [1][5]:

$$\begin{aligned}
\alpha_m &= \frac{0.1\,(-(V+40))}{\exp(-(V+40)/10) - 1}, & \beta_m &= 4\,\exp(-(V+65)/18) \\
\alpha_h &= 0.07\,\exp(-(V+65)/20), & \beta_h &= \frac{1}{1 + \exp(-(V+35)/10)} \\
\alpha_n &= \frac{0.01\,(-(V+55))}{\exp(-(V+55)/10) - 1}, & \beta_n &= 0.125\,\exp(-(V+65)/80)
\end{aligned}$$

> **Theorem (Gating-particle interpretation):** If a channel opens only when *k* independent gating particles are simultaneously in the permissive state, each with open probability *x(t)*, then the macroscopic open probability is *xᵏ*. The exponents *m³* and *n⁴* thus encode a molecular hypothesis: three activation particles per Na⁺ channel, four per K⁺ channel — remarkably close to the tetrameric structure of real voltage-gated channels discovered decades later.

Standard parameters (squid axon, 6.3 °C):

| Parameter | Value | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| *ḡ_Na* | 120 mS/cm² | Maximal Na⁺ conductance |
| *ḡ_K* | 36 mS/cm² | Maximal K⁺ conductance |
| *g_L* | 0.3 mS/cm² | Leak conductance |
| *E_Na* | +50 mV | Na⁺ reversal potential |
| *E_K* | −77 mV | K⁺ reversal potential |
| *E_L* | −54.4 mV | Leak reversal potential |

A minimal numerical implementation (explicit Euler, Δt = 0.01 ms) reproduces the spike:

```python
import numpy as np

def hh_step(V, m, h, n, I_ext, dt=0.01):
    """One Euler step of the Hodgkin-Huxley model (absolute mV convention)."""
    gNa, gK, gL = 120.0, 36.0, 0.3
    ENa, EK, EL, Cm = 50.0, -77.0, -54.4, 1.0
    def rates(V):
        am = 0.1*(-(V+40))/(np.exp(-(V+40)/10)-1)
        bm = 4.0*np.exp(-(V+65)/18)
        ah = 0.07*np.exp(-(V+65)/20)
        bh = 1.0/(1.0+np.exp(-(V+35)/10))
        an = 0.01*(-(V+55))/(np.exp(-(V+55)/10)-1)
        bn = 0.125*np.exp(-(V+65)/80)
        return (am,bm),(ah,bh),(an,bn)
    (am,bm),(ah,bh),(an,bn) = rates(V)
    m += dt*(am*(1-m) - bm*m)
    h += dt*(ah*(1-h) - bh*h)
    n += dt*(an*(1-n) - bn*n)
    INa = gNa*m**3*h*(V-ENa)
    IK  = gK*n**4*(V-EK)
    IL  = gL*(V-EL)
    V  += dt*(I_ext - INa - IK - IL)/Cm
    return V, m, h, n
```

### 3.2 Cable theory and propagation

For a cylindrical axon of radius *a* and intracellular resistivity *Rᵢ*, axial current flow couples neighboring membrane patches. Adding the axial term to the HH current balance yields the **cable equation**:

$$c_m \frac{\partial V}{\partial t} = \frac{a}{2R_i}\frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial x^2} - \sum_{\mathrm{ion}} g_{\mathrm{ion}}(V - E_{\mathrm{ion}}) + i_{\mathrm{ext}}$$

Hodgkin and Huxley sought traveling-wave solutions *V(x − θt)* and computed a conduction velocity of **θ ≈ 18.8 m/s** at 18.5 °C for the 0.476 mm squid axon — within roughly 10% of the measured ~21 m/s [1]. This was the model's triumphant *a priori* prediction: kinetics measured under voltage clamp in one set of experiments quantitatively predicted propagation in an entirely different regime.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 The gating variables as a dynamical system

The HH model is a four-dimensional nonlinear dynamical system. Its power lies in the *separation of timescales*: at rest, *τₘ* ≈ 0.2 ms ≪ *τₕ*, *τₙ* ≈ 1–5 ms. A depolarizing step triggers a stereotyped sequence visible in simulation:

![Action potential with gating variables m, h, n: fast sodium activation, sodium inactivation, and slower potassium activation](/thesis/ths_1788953443329_82c4-1.webp)

*Figure 2. Simulated action potential (top) and gating variables (bottom). Fast m³ activation drives the upstroke; h inactivation terminates Na⁺ influx; delayed n⁴ activation drives repolarization and the after-hyperpolarization.*

1. **Upstroke:** depolarization explosively increases *m*; regenerative Na⁺ influx (*m³h* large, driving force *V − E_Na* strongly negative) drives *V* toward *E_Na*.
2. **Peak and repolarization:** *h* falls (inactivation) while *n* rises; K⁺ efflux now dominates, driving *V* toward *E_K*.
3. **Refractoriness:** elevated *n* and depressed *h* constitute the relative refractory period — the dynamical memory of the spike.

> **Theorem (Excitability as a threshold phenomenon):** The HH system possesses no true voltage threshold in the mathematical sense — FitzHugh (1961) proved the model has *neither* a strict threshold *nor* all-or-none spikes; apparent threshold behavior emerges from the unstable manifold structure (a quasi-separatrix) of the 4-D phase space [3]. Threshold is a geometric, not an algebraic, property.

### 4.2 Reductions I: the FitzHugh–Nagumo model

FitzHugh (1961) observed that *h + n ≈ constant* during the spike and that *m* is fast enough to be slaved to *V*, collapsing the dynamics onto a two-dimensional **excitable medium** [3]:

$$\begin{aligned}
\frac{dv}{dt} &= v - \tfrac{v^3}{3} - w + I \\
\frac{dw}{dt} &= \varepsilon\,(v + a - b\,w), \qquad 0 < \varepsilon \ll 1
\end{aligned}$$

with canonical parameters *a* = 0.7, *b* = 0.8, *ε* = 0.08. Here *v* is the fast excitation variable and *w* the slow recovery variable. Nagumo, Arimoto, and Yoshizawa (1962) built an equivalent circuit from tunnel diodes, proving that excitability requires no ion channels at all — only a cubic nullcline and timescale separation [4]. The FHN model made **phase-plane analysis** of excitability possible: nullcline intersections classify resting, excitable, and oscillatory regimes, and the work of Rinzel, Ermentrout, and Izhikevich later connected these to bifurcation types (saddle-node vs. Hopf onset of spiking; "Type I" vs. "Type II" excitability).

### 4.3 Reductions II: integrate-and-fire and the road to networks

Collapsing the spike waveform itself to a stereotyped event yields the **leaky integrate-and-fire (LIF)** neuron [5]:

$$\tau_m \frac{dV}{dt} = -(V - E_L) + R_m I(t), \qquad V(t^-) = V_{\mathrm{th}} \Rightarrow V(t^+) = V_{\mathrm{reset}}$$

The LIF neuron discards the ionic mechanism but preserves the essential computational operation — *temporal integration of inputs followed by a thresholded, all-or-none output* — at negligible computational cost. It is the workhorse of large-scale network modeling: Brunel and Hakim (1999) used networks of LIF neurons to show analytically how fast global oscillations (e.g., in the gamma range) emerge from the interplay of synaptic delays and inhibition even at low single-neuron firing rates [7].

| Model | Dimensions | Spike mechanism | Use case |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hodgkin–Huxley | 4 (+ space) | *m³h*/*n⁴* conductances | Biophysical fidelity, channel pharmacology |
| FitzHugh–Nagumo | 2 | Cubic nullcline excursion | Phase-plane analysis, bifurcation theory |
| Integrate-and-fire | 1 | Hard threshold + reset | Large networks, analytical mean-field theory |
| Spike-response / GLM | 0 (statistical) | Stochastic intensity | Fitting to recorded spike trains |

### 4.4 The neural coding problem: rate versus temporal codes

Once the spike is understood as a stereotyped, all-or-none event, the neuron's output reduces to a **point process** — a list of spike times {*tᵢ*}. The neural coding problem asks which features of this list carry information about the stimulus *s* [6]:

- **Rate coding** (Adrian, 1928): information resides in the spike *count* *N* over a window *T*, *r = N/T*. The tuning curve *f(s) = ⟨r|s⟩* is the codebook.
- **Temporal coding**: information resides in precise spike *times* — latency to first spike, inter-spike intervals, spike patterns, or phase relative to an ongoing oscillation — at fixed mean rate.

The debate is not merely philosophical: it determines what the downstream decoder must compute. A rate decoder counts; a temporal decoder must resolve millisecond-scale structure.

### 4.5 Spike-train statistics and the Poisson hypothesis

The simplest statistical model treats spikes as an **inhomogeneous Poisson process** with rate *λ(t|s)*. The Poisson hypothesis makes sharp, testable predictions:

- **Count statistics:** *P(N|λ) = (λT)ᴺe^(−λT)/N!*, so Var[*N*] = E[*N*] — the **Fano factor** *F* = Var/Mean equals 1.
- **Interval statistics:** inter-spike intervals are exponentially distributed; the coefficient of variation CV = σ_ISI/μ_ISI equals 1.

Cortical recordings famously violate the regular-firing prediction of naive integrate-and-fire models: Softky and Koch (1993) showed that cortical neurons fire with CV ≈ 1 (Poisson-like irregularity) even though they receive thousands of inputs whose summed fluctuations should average out [8]. The resolution — balanced excitation and inhibition placing neurons in a fluctuation-driven regime — became a cornerstone of cortical theory and was formalized in the Brunel network models [7]. The Poisson model thus survives not as a mechanistic truth but as a **maximum-entropy null hypothesis**: given only the rate, it is the least structured process consistent with it.

## 5. Empirical Analysis and Theoretical Guarantees

### 5.1 Population coding and the population vector

Single neurons are noisy; populations are precise. For a stimulus *s* (e.g., movement direction) encoded by neurons with tuning curves *fᵢ(s)* and preferred stimuli *sᵢ*, Georgopoulos and colleagues showed that the **population vector**

$$\mathbf{P} = \sum_i r_i \, \mathbf{u}_i, \qquad \mathbf{u}_i = \text{unit vector toward } s_i$$

points in the stimulus direction with accuracy growing as √*N* [11]. Tuning curves thus tile stimulus space, and decoding becomes a problem in statistical estimation.

*Figure 3. The population-coding pipeline: spike rasters from a neural population (left) are summarized by tuning curves fᵢ(s) (center); a decoder such as the population vector combines preferred-direction vectors weighted by firing rates to estimate the stimulus (right).*

### 5.2 Fisher information and the Cramér–Rao bound

For any unbiased estimator *ŝ* of the stimulus, the **Cramér–Rao bound** sets a hard floor on decoding error:

> **Theorem (Cramér–Rao bound for neural decoding):** Let *p(**r**|s)* be the population response distribution. Then for any unbiased estimator *ŝ*(**r**),
>
> $$\mathrm{Var}(\hat{s}) \;\ge\; \frac{1}{J(s)}, \qquad J(s) = \mathbb{E}\left[\left(\frac{\partial \log p(\mathbf{r}|s)}{\partial s}\right)^2\right]$$
>
> where *J(s)* is the **Fisher information**. For independent Poisson neurons with tuning curves *fᵢ(s)*, this takes the closed form
>
> $$J(s) = \sum_{i=1}^{N} \frac{\big(f_i'(s)\big)^2}{f_i(s)}$$

This is a *theoretical guarantee*: no decoder — however clever — can beat 1/*J(s)*. Maximum-likelihood decoders asymptotically saturate the bound, and the bound reveals design principles: Fisher information concentrates where tuning curves are steepest, so narrow tuning helps fine discrimination while broad tuning aids detection — a tradeoff quantified by Zhang and Sejnowski (1999), who showed the Fisher-optimal tuning width depends on decoding time [9]. For correlated populations the bound generalizes with the inverse covariance matrix, and noise correlations can either help or hurt depending on their alignment with the signal direction.

### 5.3 Information-theoretic framing

Beyond estimation error, the **mutual information** *I(S; R)* between stimulus and response quantifies the code's capacity in bits [6]. Key results:

1. Temporal codes can carry information *beyond* the rate: for a time-varying rate *λ(t)*, the information in precise spike times exceeds that in counts alone whenever the stimulus varies faster than the counting window.
2. **Efficient coding** (Barlow, 1961): sensory systems adapt their codes to stimulus statistics, maximizing *I(S; R)* under metabolic constraints — the normative principle linking HH-style biophysics to coding.
3. Spike-train metrics (Victor–Purpura) and the time-rescaling theorem give operational tools to test whether temporal structure carries information the rate decoder misses.

## 6. Limitations

1. **The HH model is deterministic and mean-field.** Real channels open stochastically; with few channels, conductance noise is significant. The **stochastic HH** extension (Fox, 1997) replaces gating ODEs with Langevin equations whose noise amplitude scales as 1/√*N_channels* [10] — essential for small compartments and for explaining spike-time jitter and spontaneous firing.
2. **Point-neuron assumption.** The HH equations describe an isopotential patch. Real neurons are extended dendritic trees; **Rall's cable theory** and modern multi-compartment models show dendrites perform nonlinear integration — Poirazi, Brannon, and Mel (2003) demonstrated a pyramidal neuron implements a two-layer neural network via dendritic subunits [12].
3. **Poisson and independence assumptions.** Real spike trains show refractoriness, bursting, and noise correlations; the Fisher-information analysis above assumes conditional independence, and correlated variability can qualitatively change decoding limits.
4. **The coding problem is underdetermined.** Rate vs. temporal coding remains empirically open and likely task-dependent; the brain may multiplex codes across timescales.
5. **Temperature and preparation specificity.** HH kinetics were fitted at 6.3 °C in squid axon; mammalian channels at 37 °C are an order of magnitude faster, requiring refit rate functions (*Q₁₀* ≈ 3).

---

## 7. Conclusion

The arc from Hodgkin and Huxley's voltage clamp to modern population decoding is one of the great unifications in science. The HH formalism showed that a handful of voltage-dependent conductances, described by first-order gating kinetics, suffice to generate the action potential and predict its propagation — a triumph of quantitative biology [1][2]. Its reductions — FitzHugh–Nagumo's phase planes [3][4], the integrate-and-fire neuron [5][7] — made the mathematics tractable and the networks analyzable. And its conceptual heir, the neural coding problem, replaced the question "how does the spike arise?" with "what does the spike convey?", answering it with the statistical machinery of point processes [6][8], population vectors [11], and the Fisher-information/Cramér–Rao framework that bounds every possible decoder [9].

The modern frontier reunites the two strands: stochastic HH models inject channel noise into the gating variables [10], dendritic computation enriches the single-neuron input–output map [12], and large-scale recordings test coding theories against the simultaneous activity of thousands of neurons. Seventy-four years on, every spike recorded in every laboratory is still, in a precise sense, a Hodgkin–Huxley spike — and every theory of what those spikes mean is still negotiating the terms that the neural coding problem set.
