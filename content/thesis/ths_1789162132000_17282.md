# E-Graphs and Equality Saturation: Compact Representation of Equivalent Programs, Rewrite Saturation, and Cost-Driven Extraction for Compiler Optimization

## Abstract

Equality saturation sidesteps the *phase ordering problem* of compilers — choosing the order in which to apply optimizations — by applying all rewrites simultaneously and retaining every equivalent program in a compact *e-graph*. E-graphs represent exponentially many equivalent terms through maximal sharing: provably equal terms are grouped into *e-classes* of *e-nodes* (function symbols applied to child e-classes). This article presents the theory, algorithms, and engineering of e-graphs and equality saturation in full depth. We trace the congruence-closure foundations of the late 1970s [3], the introduction of equality saturation in the Peggy optimizer [4], and the two key innovations of the modern `egg` library — amortized *rebuilding* and *e-class analyses* [1]. We examine the unification of equality saturation with Datalog in `egglog` [2], the cost-model-driven *extraction* phase that selects optimal terms from saturated e-graphs (greedy and integer-linear-programming formulations [7]), and major applications in floating-point accuracy [6] and DSP vectorization [5], closing with the fundamental limitations of the approach.

## 1 Introduction

A traditional optimizing compiler applies its passes in a fixed pipeline: constant folding, then inlining, then strength reduction, and so on. Each pass *destructively* rewrites the program, committing to a single transformation order. The result is the **phase ordering problem**: applying rewrite A may destroy the opportunity to apply rewrite B, even though B would have yielded the better final program, and no fixed ordering is optimal for all inputs [4]. Superoptimizers and search-based approaches attempt to escape this trap by exploring alternatives, but they typically enumerate programs one at a time, paying exponential cost for every candidate.

**Equality saturation** takes a radically different approach: instead of choosing *which* rewrite to apply, apply *all* of them, *simultaneously*, and keep every result. The enabling data structure is the **e-graph** (equivalence graph). An e-graph compactly represents a *congruence relation* over a set of terms: it stores not one program but an entire equivalence class of programs, with common subexpressions shared maximally. The saturation loop repeatedly (1) finds all matches of rewrite rules in the e-graph (*e-matching*), (2) adds the right-hand sides of matched rules, and (3) merges e-classes that the rules equate, until a fixpoint (saturation) or a timeout is reached. Because rewriting in an e-graph is purely additive — nothing is ever deleted — the phase ordering problem simply vanishes: every rewrite order's outcome is present in the final structure [1].

Once the e-graph is saturated, a final **extraction** phase selects the single best program according to a user-defined *cost model*. The compact representation is crucial here: an e-graph with *n* e-nodes can represent *O(2^n)* distinct terms, yet a bottom-up dynamic program can extract the minimum-cost term in time linear in the size of the e-graph — provided the cost function is *monotone* and the sharing structure is respected [1][7].

This article develops the full picture:

- **§2** — the mathematical foundations: congruence relations, congruence closure, and the e-graph data structure with its representation invariants.
- **§3** — the methodology of equality saturation: the saturation loop, e-matching, rebuilding, and e-class analyses.
- **§4** — a deep dive into the four technical pillars: e-graph representation, amortized rebuilding, e-class analyses, and cost-driven extraction.
- **§5** — empirical results and formal properties: performance data from the `egg` case studies, the ILP formulation of extraction and its complexity.
- **§6** — limitations: non-termination, matching complexity, and the expressive ceiling of syntactic rewriting.
- **§7** — conclusion and outlook, including the `egglog` unification with Datalog.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Congruence relations and congruence closure

Let *T(F)* denote the set of ground terms over a signature *F* of function symbols. A binary relation ≡ on *T(F)* is a **congruence** if it is reflexive, symmetric, transitive, and *monotone* with respect to every function symbol:

> **Theorem:** A relation ≡ on terms is a congruence iff (i) it is an equivalence relation, and (ii) for every *n*-ary *f ∈ F*, if *tᵢ ≡ sᵢ* for all *i*, then *f(t₁,…,tₙ) ≡ f(s₁,…,sₙ)*.

Given a finite set *E* of equations between terms, the **congruence closure** of *E* is the smallest congruence relation containing *E*. Computing congruence closure is the core decision procedure for the quantifier-free theory of equality with uninterpreted function symbols — the workhorse of SMT solvers.

The classical algorithms date to the late 1970s. Nelson and Oppen gave a procedure based on term DAGs with union-find over equivalence classes [3]; Downey, Sethi, and Tarjan improved the bound to *O(n log n)* using hash-consing keyed on successors' equivalence classes — a technique foreshadowing the modern e-graph's hash-cons invariant [3]. These algorithms were built for *theorem proving*: given fixed input equalities, decide whether two terms are congruent.

### 2.2 From congruence closure to equality saturation

Equality saturation repurposes the e-graph from a *decision* procedure into a *search* procedure. The key conceptual shift was made by Tate, Stepp, Tatlock, and Lerner in the **Peggy** Java bytecode optimizer (POPL 2009) [4]. Peggy represents programs as PEGs (Program Expression Graphs) and *E-PEGs* (which add equality edges). Rather than applying optimizations in sequence, Peggy's saturation engine repeatedly dispatches *equality analyses* — pairs *(p, f)* of a trigger pattern *p* and a callback *f* that computes new equalities whenever *p* matches. All discovered equalities are added monotonically; a global *profitability heuristic* then selects the best PEG from the saturated E-PEG. On their benchmarks, Peggy achieved speedups of 10% or more where the Soot baseline produced almost no improvement [4].

1. **Rebuilding** — an amortized invariant-restoration technique that defers congruence maintenance to once per saturation iteration, yielding asymptotic speedups in practice.
2. **E-class analyses** — a general mechanism for attaching domain-specific, lattice-structured data (e.g., constant values, free-variable sets) to e-classes, computed bottom-up during rebuilding.

Subsequently, Zhang et al. unified equality saturation with Datalog in **`egglog`** (PLDI 2023) [2], observing that both are *add-only fixpoint computations over a database of facts*.

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 The e-graph data structure

An **e-graph** consists of:

- **E-classes**: sets of *e-nodes* known to be equivalent. Each e-class has a canonical id maintained by a union-find structure.
- **E-nodes**: terms of the form *f(c₁, …, cₙ)* where *f* is a function symbol and each *cᵢ* is an e-class id (not a subterm — this indirection is what enables sharing).
- **Hash-cons table**: maps each e-node to its e-class, enforcing the invariant that no two *congruent* e-nodes exist in *different* e-classes.

The two representation invariants are [1]:

1. **Hash-cons invariant**: the map from e-node to e-class is injective up to congruence — congruent e-nodes are hash-consed to the *same* e-class.
2. **Congruence invariant**: if *f(c₁,…,cₙ)* and *f(d₁,…,dₙ)* are e-nodes with *cᵢ ≡ dᵢ* for all *i*, they reside in the same e-class.

Together these guarantee that the e-graph represents exactly a congruence relation, and that *find(x) = find(y)* iff *x* and *y* are congruent. Insertion of a new term proceeds bottom-up: insert children, canonicalize their ids, then hash-cons the parent e-node — creating a fresh e-class only if no congruent e-node exists.

### 3.2 The equality saturation loop

The canonical saturation algorithm, in `egg`'s read/write-phase formulation [1], is:

```python
def equality_saturation(expr, rewrites):
    egraph = initial_egraph(expr)
    while not egraph.saturated_or_timeout():
        matches = []
        # READ phase: invariants hold; collect all matches
        for rw in rewrites:
            for (subst, eclass) in egraph.ematch(rw.lhs):
                matches.append((rw, subst, eclass))
        # WRITE phase: invariants may be broken
        for (rw, subst, eclass) in matches:
            rhs_class = egraph.add(rw.rhs.subst(subst))
            egraph.merge(eclass, rhs_class)
        # restore invariants ONCE per iteration
        egraph.rebuild()
    return egraph.extract_best()
```

Three properties of this formulation are worth emphasizing: **additivity** (rewriting never deletes; merges only coarsen the partition, so every term ever added remains represented — the phase ordering problem is eliminated by construction [4]); **order independence** (all matches are collected in the read phase before any are applied, so one iteration's outcome does not depend on rewrite-list order [1]); and **amortized rebuilding** (congruence invariants are restored once per iteration rather than after every merge, asymptotically cheaper when many merges occur per iteration [1]).

### 3.3 E-matching

**E-matching** is the problem of finding all substitutions *σ* such that a pattern *p* matches some term represented in the e-graph — pattern matching *modulo the congruence relation* — and it dominates saturation cost. Classical implementations compile patterns to discrimination trees or adapt the Rete algorithm [4]. `egglog` instead compiles patterns to relational queries over the e-graph viewed as a database, enabling join ordering plus *semi-naïve evaluation* to avoid rediscovering matches across iterations [2]. The worst case is sobering: a pattern with *k* variables can produce *O(nᵏ)* matches, and AC-matching is NP-complete — which is why Diospyros lets users disable AC rules during saturation [5].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Anatomy of an e-graph: sharing and the congruence invariant

Consider the term *(a × 2) / (a ≪ 1)*. In a tree representation this contains two copies of *a* and one *2*. In an e-graph, the initial construction hash-conses aggressively:

```
e-class C_a  = { a }
e-class C_2  = { 2 }
e-class C_m  = { ×(C_a, C_2) }        -- a × 2
e-class C_s  = { ≪(C_a, C_1) }        -- a ≪ 1  (after constant 1 added)
e-class C_d  = { ÷(C_m, C_s) }        -- (a×2)/(a≪1)
```

Now apply the rewrite *x × 2 → x ≪ 1*. E-matching finds *σ = {x ↦ C_a}*; the right-hand side *≪(C_a, C_1)* is added — but hash-consing finds it *already present* in *C_s*, so the merge *C_m ≡ C_s* simply unions two e-classes and creates no new e-node. A guarded rewrite *x / x → 1* (requiring *x ≠ 0*) then merges *C_d* with the e-class of *1*. The whole optimization — strength reduction plus cancellation — emerges from *two merges and zero new e-nodes*.

The following Rust sketch shows how `egg` expresses this [1]:

```rust
define_language! {
    enum Arith {
        Num(i32),
        Var(Symbol),
        "+" = Add([Id; 2]),
        "*" = Mul([Id; 2]),
        "<<" = Shl([Id; 2]),
        "/" = Div([Id; 2]),
    }
}

let rules: &[Rewrite<Arith, ()>] = &[
    rewrite!("mul-two";  "(* ?x 2)" => "(<< ?x 1)"),
    rewrite!("div-self";  "(/ ?x ?x)" => "1"
        if is_not_zero("?x")),
];

let runner = Runner::default()
    .with_expr(&"( / (* a 2) (<< a 1) )".parse().unwrap())
    .run(rules);
let best = Extractor::new(&runner.egraph, AstSize).find_best(runner.roots[0]);
```

Note the *conditional rewrite*: `is_not_zero` is implemented as an **e-class analysis** (§4.3), showing how semantic side conditions integrate with syntactic rewriting.

### 4.2 Rebuilding: amortized invariant restoration

The naïve approach maintains the congruence invariant *eagerly*: after every `merge`, walk the parents of merged classes and re-hash-cons them, recursively merging newly congruent ones. A single iteration may perform *millions* of merges with overlapping parent sets, making total work quadratic in the merge count [1]. **Rebuilding** defers all repair to end-of-iteration: `egg` keeps a worklist of e-classes whose parents may violate the invariants, and `rebuild()` processes it once, re-canonicalizing e-nodes and merging congruent ones (which may enqueue further parents). Each e-node is re-canonicalized only when a child class actually changed, giving near-linear amortized cost per iteration [1].

> **Theorem:** In `egg`'s saturation loop, deferring congruence restoration to a single `rebuild()` per iteration preserves soundness: every equivalence discovered by eager maintenance is discovered by the deferred schedule, possibly one iteration later. Matches are never lost — only delayed — because the read phase of each iteration runs on a fully restored e-graph.

The "possibly one iteration later" caveat is precisely why the read/write phase split matters: e-matching in the read phase always sees valid invariants, so deferred rebuilding cannot cause *missed* matches within an iteration, only matches that materialize in the *next* iteration [1].

### 4.3 E-class analyses: principled domain reasoning

Purely syntactic rewrites cannot express rules like *x / x → 1* safely (it requires *x ≠ 0*) or *constant folding* (*2 + 3 → 5*). Earlier systems handled these with ad-hoc hooks into the saturation engine. `egg`'s **e-class analyses** provide a general, sound mechanism [1]:

An analysis defines a *semilattice* domain *D* with a join operation ⊔, plus:

- `make(egraph, enode) -> D`: compute data for a single e-node from its children's data (bottom-up);
- `merge(&mut D, D) -> DidMerge`: join data when e-classes merge;
- optionally `modify(egraph, eclass)`: add new e-nodes justified by the analysis (e.g., add the constant-folded node).

Because analysis data is maintained *per e-class* and joined on merge, it is always a sound over-approximation of the data of every term in the class. The classic examples:

| Analysis | Domain | Use |
|---|---|---|
| Constant folding | `Option<Constant>` | *2+3 → 5*; guards like *x≠0* |
| Free variables | `Set<Var>` | Capture-avoiding rewrites under binders |
| Cost | `u64` | Extraction (§4.4) |
| Type inference | Type lattice | Typed rewrites |

Analyses compose: `egg` supports a single analysis per e-graph (a limitation lifted in `egglog`, which supports cooperating analyses over the same database [2]).

A Haskell-style specification of the analysis interface clarifies the lattice requirements:

```haskell
class (Lattice d) => Analysis lang d where
  make   :: EGraph lang -> ENode lang -> d
  merge  :: d -> d -> (d, DidMerge)   -- join; report whether changed
  modify :: EGraph lang -> EClassId -> EGraph lang  -- optional
```

*Monotonicity* of `make` in the lattice order is the soundness condition: joining more terms into a class must only move the analysis value *up*, never invalidating derived facts [1].

### 4.4 Extraction: from saturated e-graph to optimal program

After saturation, the e-graph represents a vast set of equivalent programs; **extraction** selects one minimizing a cost function. The standard algorithm is a bottom-up dynamic program [1] (shown in §3's pseudocode pattern): each e-class records its cheapest e-node, iterating to a fixpoint to handle rewrite-introduced cycles. For *monotone, additive* costs (AST size, instruction count), one pass over a topological order suffices on acyclic e-graphs. The subtlety is **sharing**: the extracted term is a DAG, and the cost model must count each shared e-class once, not once per use site.

The greedy DP is not always optimal. When the cost function accounts for *sharing* (a subterm used twice costs once) or global constraints, extraction becomes NP-hard, and the exact formulation is an **integer linear program** [7]:

- Binary variable *xₙ ∈ {0,1}* for each e-node *n* (1 iff *n* is selected);
- Binary variable *y_c ∈ {0,1}* for each e-class *c* (1 iff *c* is "active", i.e., used by the extracted term);
- Constraints: exactly one e-node selected per active e-class; if *n* is selected then all its child e-classes are active; the root e-class is active;
- Objective: minimize *Σₙ cost(n)·xₙ*.

Yang et al. show that the natural greedy algorithm can be arbitrarily suboptimal on sharing-heavy e-graphs, that the LP relaxation has an unbounded integrality gap (fractional solutions "split" activation across e-nodes to share costs), and that a purpose-built ILP encoding nevertheless solves practical instances efficiently [7]. `egg`'s default extractor uses the fast greedy DP; the ILP formulation is available for domains (like hardware synthesis) where optimality justifies solver time.

A TLA+-style sketch of the extraction correctness invariant:

```tla
---- MODULE Extraction ----
EXTENDS Naturals
VARIABLES chosen  \* [eclass -> enode] partial extraction map
TypeOK == chosen \in [EClass -> ENode \cup {None}]
Sound  == \A c \in DOMAIN chosen :
            chosen[c] # None => chosen[c] \in NodesOf(c)
Complete == root \in DOMAIN chosen /\ chosen[root] # None
Optimal(C) == \A t \in TermsOf(root) : Cost(Extract(chosen)) <= Cost(t)
====
```

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 `egg` case studies

Willsey et al. evaluate `egg` on three previously published equality-saturation applications, reimplemented to isolate the contribution of rebuilding and e-class analyses [1]: **Tensat** (TensorFlow graph optimization), where rebuilding turns timeouts into completed saturations on large graphs; **Herbie-style floating-point rewriting**, where analyses express constant folding and interval reasoning that previously required invasive engine modifications [6]; and **Diospyros-style DSP vectorization**, reproducing expert-competitive kernels [5]. The central empirical claim: a *general* e-graph library with the right two mechanisms subsumes a family of bespoke engines, running competitive with or faster than prior specialized implementations in far less code [1].

### 5.2 `egglog` microbenchmarks

Zhang et al. compare `egglog` against `egg` on 100 iterations of saturation over `egg`'s math rewrite suite [2]. Key results:

- `egglogNI` (egglog *without* semi-naïve evaluation) grows the *identical* e-graph as `egg` but finishes **3.34× faster**, attributed to relational e-matching and the query optimizer.
- Full `egglog` with semi-naïve evaluation grows a slightly *larger* e-graph (it avoids redundant matches, changing the fixpoint slightly) with a **9.27× speedup** over `egg`.

These numbers demonstrate that viewing the e-graph as a *database* — rather than a pointer structure — unlocks decades of database optimization for equality saturation [2].

### 5.3 Formal properties

We collect the key metatheoretic facts, each proved or sketched in the cited literature:

> **Theorem (Soundness of saturation):** Every term represented in the e-graph after any number of saturation iterations is provably equal (in the equational theory of the rewrites) to the input term. *Proof sketch.* By induction on iterations: `add` inserts instances of rewrite right-hand sides, and `merge` unions classes only for pairs connected by a rewrite instance or by congruence from such pairs [1][4].

> **Theorem (Non-termination):** Equality saturation does not terminate in general. *Proof sketch.* The rewrite *x → f(x)* strictly grows terms forever; saturation enumerates an infinite sequence of distinct e-classes. Practical systems impose iteration limits, e-node caps, or timeouts [4].

> **Theorem (Extraction complexity):** Optimal extraction with sharing-aware (DAG) costs is NP-hard (reduction from weighted set cover / Steiner variants); the greedy bottom-up DP is optimal only for *tree* cost models without sharing [7].

## 6 Limitations

**Non-termination and blowup.** Equality saturation is a semi-algorithm: rules like *x → x + 0* or AC-expansions grow the e-graph without bound. Iteration limits and node caps make it total in practice but sacrifice completeness — extraction quality depends on the budget [4][1].

**E-matching cost.** Matching dominates runtime; multi-variable patterns and AC rules can explode the match set. Relational e-matching [2] improves constants and avoids redundant work but not the worst case.

**Extraction vs. cost-model expressiveness.** The greedy extractor is optimal only for additive, monotone, tree-like costs. Real objectives — register pressure, scheduling, numerical accuracy — are global; the ILP formulation [7] handles sharing exactly but scales poorly to huge e-graphs, and neither handles non-linear objectives.

**Rewrite authoring.** Saturation shifts the burden from *ordering* rewrites to *writing* them: the rule set must be confluent enough to find good programs yet restrained enough to saturate. Debugging a 200-rule system over a 100k-node e-graph remains a dark art; `egglog`'s Datalog-style rules and provenance help [2].

**Soundness of conditional rules.** Every conditional rewrite's side condition must be a sound e-class analysis (monotone in the lattice order). An unsound `modify` or a non-monotone `make` silently corrupts the congruence — the library cannot check this [1].

## 7 Conclusion

E-graphs turn the phase ordering problem inside out: by representing *all* equivalent programs simultaneously through maximal sharing, equality saturation replaces the fragile art of pass ordering with the robust science of fixpoint computation over congruence relations. The lineage runs from Nelson and Oppen's congruence-closure procedures [3], through Tate et al.'s reformulation as an optimization discipline [4], to `egg`'s rebuilding and e-class analyses [1], and `egglog`'s unification of rewriting with Datalog [2]. Extraction closes the loop, reducing "choose the best program" to a dynamic program or, when sharing matters, an integer linear program [7]. Applications from Herbie [6] to Diospyros [5] show expert-quality results across domains. The frontier is *scale and expressiveness* — but the core insight is stable: **never throw away an equivalent program until you know which one is best.**
