# Securing Interdomain Routing: RPKI Route Origin Validation, BGPsec Path Validation, Route Leak Detection, and Measurement Studies of Global BGP Hijacking Defense

## Abstract

The Border Gateway Protocol (BGP), the Internet's interdomain routing protocol, was designed under the assumption that every participating Autonomous System (AS) is trustworthy [1]. History has repeatedly falsified this assumption: from Pakistan Telecom's accidental hijack of YouTube's prefixes to sophisticated cryptocurrency thefts executed through forged-origin sub-prefix attacks, control-plane manipulation remains a persistent, high-impact threat. This thesis presents a unified analytical and empirical treatment of the modern interdomain routing defense stack. We examine three complementary mechanisms: (i) Resource Public Key Infrastructure (RPKI) route origin validation (ROV), which cryptographically binds prefixes to authorized origin ASes; (ii) BGPsec path validation, which extends cryptographic attestation from prefix origin to the full AS path; and (iii) route-leak detection rooted in the valley-free routing model. We formalize each mechanism's threat model and validation semantics, derive complexity and security properties, and synthesize longitudinal measurement evidence to assess deployment progress and residual vulnerabilities. Our central finding is that ROV is a necessary but strictly insufficient control, that BGPsec's incremental-deployment game converges to zero adoption without coordination, and that the emerging zones-of-trust paradigm offers the most credible near-term path to measurable hijack suppression.

---

## 1. Introduction

BGP is the glue of Internet connectivity: roughly 75,000 autonomous systems exchange reachability information through hundreds of thousands of announced IPv4 and IPv6 prefixes. Yet BGP's UPDATE message carries *claims* rather than *proofs*. A BGP speaker that receives an announcement "AS 64496 originates 192.0.2.0/24 via AS 64501" has no intrinsic basis to believe either the origin claim or the transit chain. The protocol's security posture rests entirely on out-of-band configuration: prefix filters, IRR data, and operator vigilance.

The consequences of this fragility are neither theoretical nor rare. Observable prefix-hijack incidents occur with high frequency somewhere in the global table [2]. The attacker model divides naturally into three classes:

1. **Accidental misorigination.** A misconfiguration causes an AS to originate a prefix it does not own (e.g., announcing a more-specific of a legitimate prefix).
2. **Malicious prefix hijack.** An adversary deliberately announces a victim's prefix—or a more-specific sub-prefix—to redirect traffic for interception, censorship, or denial of service.
3. **Route leak.** An AS violates its intended export policy, propagating a learned route to a party that should never receive it, causing traffic to trombone through unintended paths.

Origin validation and path validation target classes 1 and 2; leak detection targets class 3. No single mechanism covers all three, and cryptographic strength in one dimension can be undercut by operational weakness in another. This thesis develops the argument in four movements: the RPKI trust architecture and ROV semantics (§2–4); BGPsec's cryptographic extension and its deployment economics (§4.2–4.3); route-leak detection and the OTC attribute (§4.4); and measurement evidence quantifying how far the defenses have—and have not—come (§5).

## 2. Background and Related Work

### 2.1 BGP and the Trust Problem

BGP speakers exchange NLRI reachability via UPDATE messages carrying path attributes, the most important being AS_PATH, the sequence of ASes the announcement traversed. Loop detection is syntactic: an AS rejects announcements containing its own number. Nothing validates that the *rightmost* AS truly originates the prefix, or that intermediate ASes authorized forwarding.

### 2.2 Early Proposals

The Secure Inter-Domain Routing (SIDR) working group evaluated a lineage of proposals: S-BGP (Kent et al.), soBGP (White et al.), psBGP, SPV (Secure Path Vector), and Pretty Good BGP (PGBGP). PGBGP is instructive: it avoided cryptography entirely, flagging new prefix–origin bindings as *suspicious* for a quarantine interval. Empirical follow-ups showed that without a reliable attack-free training period, anomaly-detection approaches generate unmanageable false positives and cannot distinguish adversarial from legitimate churn. The lesson—*statistical detection without ground truth is fragile*—motivated the cryptographic turn: RPKI and BGPsec.

### 2.3 RPKI Architecture

RFC 6480 defines the RPKI as a PKI mirroring the address/ASN allocation hierarchy: IANA → RIRs → ISPs → end sites [1]. X.509 certificates carry RFC 3779 extensions binding holders to IPv4/IPv6 prefixes and ASN ranges. A Route Origin Authorization (ROA) is a signed object asserting that a specific AS is authorized to originate specific prefixes, optionally with a *maxLength* attribute extending authorization to more-specific sub-prefixes. ROAs are distributed through a repository system and consumed by relying parties running validators (Routinator, rpki-client, Fort), which emit Validated ROA Payloads (VRPs) to routers over the RPKI-to-Router (RTR) protocol.

## 3. Methodology

Our analysis proceeds along three tracks:

1. **Formal reconstruction.** We restate the ROV validation semantics (RFC 6811) as an explicit decision function, BGPsec's nested signature algebra (RFC 8205), and valley-free routing as a graph-theoretic invariant, so that security claims can be evaluated mechanically.
2. **Threat-model parameterization.** For each mechanism we identify the minimal attacker capabilities required to defeat it (e.g., key compromise vs. protocol downgrade vs. partial-deployment exploitation), following the NIST SP 800-189 Rev. 1 framing of routing threats [6].
3. **Measurement synthesis.** We aggregate published longitudinal measurements—NIST's RPKI monitor, Kentik's NetFlow-annotated ROV adoption analysis, and active experimental studies of sub-prefix hijacks—to ground claims about deployment state and residual attack surface in data rather than aspiration.

We deliberately restrict our claim set to what these sources support; where evidence is thin (notably BGPsec, which has *no* production deployment), we say so explicitly.

## 4. Deep Technical Dive

### 4.1 RPKI Route Origin Validation: Semantics and Formal Properties

The core of ROV is a three-valued classification of every BGP route against the VRP database. RFC 6811 defines:

> **Theorem (Origin Validation Semantics):** Let a route *r* have prefix *p_r*, length *ℓ_r*, and origin AS *a_r*. Let *V* be the set of VRPs, each *v ∈ V* covering prefix *p_v*, maximum length *m_v*, and ASN *a_v*. Define *v* *covers* *r* iff *p_r* is contained in *p_v* with *ℓ_r ≥ ℓ_v*; *v* *matches* *r* iff it covers *r*, *ℓ_r ≤ m_v*, and *a_r = a_v*. Then the validation state *S(r)* is:
> - **Valid** if *∃ v ∈ V* matching *r*;
> - **Invalid** if *∃ v* covering but none matching *r*;
> - **NotFound** otherwise.

This is RFC 6811's Covered/Matched/Valid-Invalid-NotFound trichotomy [2]. Three properties deserve emphasis:

1. **Validity is existential, invalidity universal.** A single matching ROA rescues a route; invalidity requires the *absence* of any match. Operators must therefore enumerate every legitimate origin ASN as a ROA or risk self-induced invalidation.
2. **maxLength is a blunt instrument.** A ROA for 192.0.2.0/23 with maxLength 24 authorizes the AS to originate *any* /24 under the /23—including sub-prefixes the operator may never announce. This creates the *forged-origin sub-prefix hijack*: an attacker announces an unannounced more-specific with the victim's ASN in the origin position, and ROV deems it **Valid**, because the ROA's authorization is indistinguishable from intent. Measurements in 2017 found 84% of prefixes covered by maxLength-bearing ROAs vulnerable to this attack [8].
3. **NotFound is the policy vacuum.** Routes without covering ROAs inherit no cryptographic information; most operators today accept them (fail-open), which preserves connectivity while the ecosystem converges but offers zero protection for the unsigned majority.

The following reference implementation captures the decision logic:

```python
def origin_validate(route, vrps):
    """RFC 6811 origin validation. Returns 'Valid'|'Invalid'|'NotFound'."""
    covered = False
    for v in vrps:
        if not v.covers(route.prefix):
            continue
        covered = True
        if (route.prefix_len <= v.max_length and
                route.origin_as == v.asn):
            return 'Valid'   # existential match
    return 'Invalid' if covered else 'NotFound'
```

Operationally, routers evaluate the state per received UPDATE and apply local policy—typically *drop Invalid*. NIST SP 1800-14 measured convergence overhead of only 2–7% under full ROA coverage, demonstrating that ROV's cost is negligible relative to its protection against accidental misorigination.

### 4.2 BGPsec: Cryptographic Path Validation

ROV answers "is the origin authorized?" but says nothing about the transit path. BGPsec (RFC 8205) closes the gap with a new optional, non-transitive path attribute, **BGPsec_PATH**, carrying one signature segment per AS on the path [3].

The construction is an *iterative* or *nested* signature: AS_i signs (previous signatures, AS_i, target AS_{i+1}, prefix, ...). Concretely, the signature produced by the *k*-th AS covers the Secure_Path of all *k−1* preceding ASes plus the NLRI, binding each hop to the *specific* neighbor it authorizes to re-advertise. Validation requires the recipient to walk the segment list, fetching each AS's BGPsec router certificate from the RPKI and verifying each signature against its predecessor's.

Key design properties:

| Property | Mechanism | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Hop-by-hop authorization | Each AS signs the *target* AS | A forged or spliced AS_PATH fails signature verification |
| Algorithm agility | ECDSA P-256 per RFC 8208 | Compact signatures; verification at wire speed |
| Replay scope | No timestamps; sequence via path position | Path truncation attacks remain possible without ROV pairing |
| Partial deployment | Unsigned segments permitted in migration | Security degrades to ROV-only for unsigned portions |

BGPsec is thus *complementary*, not substitutive: it presupposes the RPKI certificate hierarchy of RFC 6480 and is explicitly intended to be deployed alongside origin validation [3]. Crucially, BGPsec validates that every listed AS authorized the advertisement to the next AS—it does *not* authenticate data-plane forwarding, and it does not bind the origin to a prefix beyond what ROV already provides.

### 4.3 The Incremental Deployment Game

BGPsec's signature algebra is sound; its deployment economics are catastrophic. Consider the game: each AS deciding whether to deploy BGPsec bears full cost (router certificates, key management, signature CPU) while receiving security benefit only proportional to the fraction of *other* ASes that also deploy—a classic positive-externality coordination problem. Worse, partial deployment enables *downgrade confusion*: a BGPsec-aware router receiving an unsigned update cannot distinguish "sender hasn't deployed" from "sender's signature was stripped by an attacker," because the attribute is optional and non-transitive.

> **Theorem (Partial-Deployment Degradation):** In a network where a fraction *α* of ASes validate BGPsec signatures, an attacker controlling one BGPsec-capable AS can inject a path-manipulated announcement that is accepted by every non-validating AS and by validating ASes that treat unsigned segments as permissible. The effective protected fraction is bounded above by the probability that *every* AS on both the legitimate and the attacker's propagation paths validates—vanishingly small for realistic *α ≪ 1*.

This explains the empirical fact: nearly a decade after RFC 8205's publication, BGPsec has **zero production deployment** on the global Internet. The community's response has been the *zones of trust* model: rather than demanding universal cryptographic path validation, a connected region of cooperating ASes enforces ROV plus SAV and leak prevention at its boundary, providing meaningful protection for members even when outsiders do not participate [8]. The zone model accepts weaker formal guarantees in exchange for a deployable incentive structure—a pragmatic concession we return to in §5.

### 4.4 Route Leak Detection: Valley-Free Invariants and the OTC Attribute

Route leaks are policy violations, not cryptographic forgeries: the announcement is *authentic* but *out of scope*. RFC 7908 defines a leak as "the propagation of routing announcement(s) beyond their intended scope," where scope is defined by bilateral business relationships [4].

The foundational model is Gao's: AS relationships are classified as provider-to-customer (p2c), customer-to-provider (c2p), peer-to-peer (p2p), or sibling. The **valley-free** property states that a valid AS path has the form (c2p)* (p2p)? (p2c)*—traffic climbs toward providers, crosses at most one peering link, then descends. Any path containing a "valley" (a descent followed by a climb) violates export policy and is, with high probability, a leak.

Operationalizing this required signaling relationship *roles* explicitly, because inferred relationships are noisy. The **Only-to-Customer (OTC) attribute** [5] standardizes this:

1. When an AS sends a route learned from a provider, peer, or route server to a customer, peer, or provider, it attaches OTC set to its own ASN.
2. A receiving AS configured with role-based policy checks: if the route arrives from a provider/peer/RS *with* OTC set, or the route is sent onward in a way that contradicts the role, the leak is detected and the route's LocalPref is lowered or the route is dropped.

```python
def receiver_leak_check(route, sender_role, local_asn, LEAK_PENALTY):
    """OTC-based route leak detection (receiver side)."""
    otc = route.attributes.get('OTC')
    if otc is not None:
        route.local_pref = LEAK_PENALTY   # deprefer leaked route
        return 'LEAK_SUSPECT'
    if sender_role == 'customer':
        route.attributes['OTC'] = local_asn
    elif sender_role == 'peer' and otc not in (None, route.sender_asn):
        route.attributes['OTC'] = local_asn
    return 'OK'
```

OTC's strength is that it requires no cryptography and no global PKI—only bilateral role configuration on eBGP sessions. Its weakness is identical: without universal role configuration, misconfigured peers simply omit the attribute, and detection degrades to heuristic valley-free analysis with substantial false-positive rates on complex multi-relationship peerings [5].

### 4.5 The RPKI's Own Attack Surface

A sobering measurement literature examines not BGP's insecurity but the *defense infrastructure's*. In 2024, researchers using the CURE fuzzing framework disclosed 18 vulnerabilities across all major RPKI validator implementations—crashes, standard-behavior violations, and, most severely, bugs permitting a network adversary to subvert a validator's trust-anchor configuration and inject forged "valid" routing data. Five CVEs were assigned, one scoring 9.3. This inverts the usual assumption: the validator, the component that *decides* trust, was itself the weakest link. Defense in depth therefore requires validator diversity, RTR transport hardening, and monitoring of the monitor—an operational burden the standards acknowledge but do not automate.

## 5. Empirical Results and Formal Arguments

### 5.1 Adoption Trajectories

The measurement record shows genuine, if uneven, progress on the origin-validation front:

- **May 2023** marked a milestone: over 50% of IPv4 routes in the global table carried ROAs (NIST RPKI monitor); IPv6 crossed the threshold in 2022.
- By **October 2024**, NetFlow-annotated analysis found more than half of BGP routes had ROAs, with roughly three-quarters of *traffic volume* destined to ROA-covered prefixes—the traffic-weighted view being the security-relevant one.
- ROV *enforcement* (dropping Invalids) lags ROA *publication*: a 2021–2023 study found only ~12.3% of tested ASes behaved as if they or all their transit providers consistently performed ROV, with larger ASes disproportionately represented [8].
- Regional skew is stark: October 2023 NIST data showed 63% of prefix-origin pairs RPKI-valid in the RIPE region versus 21% in AFRINIC [8].

### 5.2 Attacks That Evade the Defenses

Recent active experimentation demonstrates the residual gap. Researchers ethically hijacked their own prefixes and showed that *sub-prefix* hijacks—announcing a more-specific of the victim's prefix—propagate globally due to longest-prefix-match preference, and that standard control-plane monitors (RIPE RIS, RouteViews, commercial services) can miss carefully constructed stealthy variants, particularly when the attacker's announcement is engineered to avoid triggering monitoring heuristics [7]. RPKI with maxLength-free ROAs would block these; with maxLength, the forged-origin variant sails through as Valid [8].

### 5.3 Formal Security Summary

| Threat | ROV (RFC 6811) | BGPsec (RFC 8205) | Leak detection (OTC) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Accidental misorigination | **Blocked** (Invalid) | Blocked | N/A |
| Exact-prefix hijack | **Blocked** (Invalid) | Blocked | N/A |
| Sub-prefix hijack (no maxLength) | **Blocked** | Blocked | N/A |
| Sub-prefix hijack (maxLength ROA) | **Evades** (Valid) [8] | Blocked | N/A |
| Forged-origin (victim ASN spoofed) | **Evades** (Valid) | **Blocked** (signature fails) | N/A |
| Path shortening / splicing | Evades | **Blocked** | N/A |
| Route leak (policy violation) | Evades | Evades | **Detected** (if roles configured) |
| Validator compromise | **Evades** | Evades (shared PKI) | N/A |

The table's moral: each mechanism covers a distinct slice of the threat space, and the union still leaves holes where mechanisms' assumptions overlap (shared RPKI trust anchors) or where configuration is absent.

## 6. Limitations and Open Problems

1. **BGPsec's deployment paradox.** No incentive-compatible adoption path has been demonstrated at scale; the zones-of-trust model [8] is the leading alternative but weakens guarantees to "attacks from outside the zone are filtered at the boundary," which is an economic claim, not a cryptographic one.
2. **maxLength hygiene.** Best practice now recommends against maxLength, but legacy ROAs persist; automated ROA linting and RIR-side defaults remain incomplete.
3. **Validator robustness.** The 2024 CURE findings show the trust anchor's software supply chain is immature; continuous differential fuzzing of validators should be a standing operational requirement.
4. **NotFound majority.** Until ROA coverage approaches universality and fail-closed policies become viable, the unsigned majority of prefixes remains unprotected—and fail-closed risks partitioning legitimate but unsigned networks.
5. **Leak detection without roles.** OTC requires bilateral configuration whose absence is undetectable remotely; inferring roles from public data reintroduces the false-positive problem valley-free heuristics never solved.
6. **Data-plane gap.** None of these mechanisms verify that packets actually follow the validated control-plane path; data-plane verification is an orthogonal, unsolved problem.

---

## 7. Conclusion

Interdomain routing security has moved from aspiration to partial reality. RPKI route origin validation—grounded in RFC 6480's trust architecture and RFC 6811's crisp validation semantics—now covers a majority of the global table and demonstrably suppresses accidental misorigination and naive hijacks at negligible convergence cost. Yet the defense stack is incomplete in principled ways: ROV's maxLength semantics admit forged-origin sub-prefix hijacks; BGPsec's elegant nested-signature construction is undeployed because its security game punishes early adopters; route-leak detection via OTC works only where bilateral roles are configured; and the RPKI validators themselves have proven vulnerable to implementation flaws. The measurement record counsels humility: deployment is real, protection is partial, and the residual attack surface is precisely characterized—which is itself progress. The credible near-term agenda is not universal BGPsec but disciplined ROV hygiene (no maxLength, fail-closed where feasible), zones of trust that make partial deployment meaningful, hardened validators, and continued measurement. BGP will never be provably secure end-to-end; it can, however, be made *expensive* to attack—and cost, in the end, is what deters.
