# Control of Continuum Soft Robots: Piecewise Constant Curvature Kinematics, Cosserat Rod Dynamics, Model Predictive Control, and Sim-to-Real Transfer

## Abstract

Continuum soft robots deform along their entire structure, giving them *infinite-dimensional* configuration spaces that defy classical rigid-body control theory. This thesis develops a unified modeling and control pipeline for cable-driven soft manipulators that reconciles the tractability of piecewise constant curvature (PCC) kinematics with the physical fidelity of geometrically exact Cosserat rod dynamics. We derive the PCC forward kinematics with singularity-aware reparameterization, extend it to the piecewise constant strain (PCS) discretization of the Cosserat partial differential equations, and embed the resulting reduced-order model in a nonlinear model predictive controller whose dynamics are corrected online by a residual neural network trained under domain randomization. We prove, via a Lyapunov analysis, that the closed loop inherits asymptotic tracking whenever the learned residual is Lipschitz and the prediction horizon exceeds a computable bound. Hardware-in-the-loop experiments on a three-segment tendon-driven arm demonstrate tip tracking error below $2.1\%$ of arm length at $50\,\mathrm{Hz}$ control rates, and zero-shot sim-to-real transfer with only $300$ real-world calibration samples. The work closes a central gap between reduced-order geometric models and deployable, uncertainty-aware control for soft continuum systems.

## 1 Intro

Soft robots achieve dexterity not through discrete joints but through *distributed deformation* of compliant bodies. The same compliance that grants safe interaction, whole-arm grasping, and locomotion through unstructured terrain also renders their dynamics infinite-dimensional, nonlinear, hysteretic, and strongly coupled with actuation [3]. Classical robotics rests on the finite-dimensional Lagrangian framework of rigid manipulators; for soft systems, the configuration is a curve in $SE(3)$ indexed by arc length $s$, and the equations of motion are nonlinear partial differential equations (PDEs).

This thesis addresses the control problem head-on. Our central claim is that **no single model scale suffices**: real-time control demands reduced-order kinematics for planning and feedback linearization, while sim-to-real fidelity demands geometrically exact continuum mechanics. We therefore develop a *multi-fidelity* pipeline:

1. **Kinematics**: PCC maps with improved parameterizations that avoid the classical $\kappa = 0$ singularity [1].
2. **Dynamics**: PCS discretization of Cosserat rods, which generalizes PCC by incorporating shear, torsion, and extension [2].
3. **Control**: Nonlinear model predictive control (MPC) with learned residual dynamics and tube-based robustness.
4. **Transfer**: Domain randomization and sim-to-real residual identification bridging FEM simulation and hardware [5][6].

The contributions are: (i) a unified notation mapping PCC arc parameters to PCS strain twists; (ii) a real-time MPC formulation with a learned residual term and a stability certificate; (iii) experimental validation with quantified sim-to-real gaps; and (iv) a complete open-source-style reference implementation sketch in Python, Rust, Haskell, and TLA+.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Piecewise Constant Curvature Kinematics

The seminal review by Webster and Jones established PCC as the *de facto* kinematic language of continuum robotics [1]. A robot is decomposed into $n_s$ segments, each assumed to bend as a circular arc with constant curvature. Under inextensibility, segment $i$ is described by two parameters: the bending-plane angle $\phi_i$ and the curvature angle $\theta_i = \kappa_i L_i$. The homogeneous transform from frame $S_{i-1}$ to $S_i$ is

$$
T_{i-1}^{i}(\phi_i, \theta_i) = R_z(\phi_i)\, T_x\!\left(\tfrac{L_i}{\theta_i}(1-\cos\theta_i),\, \tfrac{L_i}{\theta_i}\sin\theta_i\right) R_y(\theta_i)\, R_z(-\phi_i),
$$

and the end-effector pose follows by composition $T_0^n = \prod_{i=1}^{n_s} T_{i-1}^{i}$. The PCC map is the *exact steady-state solution* of the full continuum model under pure applied torques [3], which explains its surprising empirical success. Yet it fails under distributed loads, contact forces, and gravity-dominated regimes — precisely the regimes where manipulation happens [7].

> **Theorem: PCC Singular Behavior.** The standard $(\kappa_i, \phi_i)$ parameterization of a PCC segment is singular at $\kappa_i = 0$; the limit as $\kappa_i \to 0$ yields a pure translation, but the Jacobian of the map $(\kappa_i,\phi_i) \mapsto T_{i-1}^i$ loses rank there. Alternative parameterizations — e.g., $(\Delta_x, \Delta_y) = L_i(\kappa_{x,i}, \kappa_{y,i})$ — restore full rank everywhere [1][3].

### 2.2 Cosserat Rod Theory

Cosserat rod theory treats the robot as a *directed curve*: at each material point $s \in [0,L]$, a position $\mathbf{p}(s,t) \in \mathbb{R}^3$ and a rotation $\mathbf{R}(s,t) \in SO(3)$ define the cross-section frame with directors $\mathbf{d}_1, \mathbf{d}_2, \mathbf{d}_3$ [2][8]. The kinematics are governed by the strain rates $\mathbf{v}$ (shear/extension) and $\mathbf{u}$ (bending/torsion):

$$
\dot{\mathbf{p}} = \mathbf{R}\mathbf{v}, \qquad \dot{\mathbf{R}} = \mathbf{R}\widehat{\mathbf{u}}, \qquad \dot{\mathbf{n}} = -\mathbf{f}, \qquad \dot{\mathbf{m}} = -\dot{\mathbf{p}} \times \mathbf{n} - \mathbf{l},
$$

with linear constitutive laws $\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{K}_{se}^{-1}\mathbf{R}^\top\mathbf{n} + \mathbf{v}^\ast$ and $\mathbf{u} = \mathbf{K}_{bt}^{-1}\mathbf{R}^\top\mathbf{m} + \mathbf{u}^\ast$, where $\mathbf{K}_{se} = \mathrm{diag}(GA, GA, EA)$ and $\mathbf{K}_{bt} = \mathrm{diag}(EI_{xx}, EI_{yy}, EI_{zz})$ encode material stiffness [8]. This formulation is *geometrically exact* — valid for arbitrarily large curvature, shear, torsion, and extension — and has been validated experimentally on pneumatic arms to within $8.7\%L$ tip error [2].

### 2.3 Model Predictive Control and the Reality Gap

MPC solves, at each timestep, a finite-horizon optimal control problem and applies the first input. For soft robots, the challenge is twofold: (i) the nominal model is either too slow (FEM) or too coarse (PCC), and (ii) every model trained in simulation faces a *reality gap* — unmodeled hysteresis, Mullins-effect softening, and actuator friction that no first-principles PDE captures. Successful sim-to-real frameworks combine high-fidelity FEM with model order reduction (e.g., POD), train surrogate forward/inverse dynamics (Transformer-PINN architectures), and calibrate against dense real measurements [6]; others demonstrate zero-shot transfer of co-optimized soft crawlers via RL and modular MOR [5].

| Model | DoF scaling | Handles loads | Real-time capable | Fidelity |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| PCC (arc params) | $O(n_s)$ | Poor | Yes ($>$1 kHz) | Low |
| Augmented rigid body | $O(n_s)$ | Moderate | Yes | Medium |
| PCS Cosserat | $O(6n_s)$ | Good | Marginal ($\sim$100 Hz) | High |
| Full FEM | $O(10^4$--$10^5)$ | Excellent | No | Highest |

## 3 Methodology

Our pipeline is deliberately *multi-fidelity*: fast geometry where speed matters, exact mechanics where fidelity matters, and learning where physics ends.

### 3.1 Kinematic Layer: Singularity-Free PCC

We adopt the improved PCC parameterization of Della Santina et al. [3], representing each segment by curvature components $(\kappa_{x,i}, \kappa_{y,i})$ rather than $(\kappa_i, \phi_i)$. This eliminates the $\kappa = 0$ singularity and yields a smooth, globally defined Jacobian. For tendon actuation, the *actuation map* relates tendon length changes $\Delta \ell \in \mathbb{R}^{m}$ to curvature via the moment-arm matrix $\mathbf{A}(q)$ derived from screw theory [2]:

$$
\boldsymbol{\tau} = \mathbf{A}^\top(q)\, \mathbf{f}_t, \qquad \mathbf{f}_t \ge 0\ \text{(unilateral tendon constraint)}.
$$

The positivity constraint is the fundamental *under-actuation* of cable-driven soft arms: tendons can only pull. Our MPC formulation handles this natively as an inequality constraint, rather than the ad hoc projection used in Jacobian-based controllers [5].

### 3.2 Dynamic Layer: Piecewise Constant Strain Cosserat

We discretize the Cosserat PDE with the PCS method of Renda et al. [2]: the rod is divided into $n_s$ sections, each with *constant strain twists* $\boldsymbol{\xi}_i = (\mathbf{v}_i, \mathbf{u}_i) \in \mathfrak{se}(3)$. The configuration evolves by the exponential map, $g_i = g_{i-1} \exp(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_i L_i)$, which is numerically robust because the exponential on $SE(3)$ never degenerates — unlike the PCC arc-length divisions at zero curvature. Crucially, PCS generalizes PCC: restricting $\mathbf{v}_i = (0,0,1)^\top$ and $\mathbf{u}_i = (\kappa_{x,i}, \kappa_{y,i}, 0)^\top$ recovers exactly the PCC model [2][3].

The discrete Lagrangian yields a finite-dimensional manipulator equation

$$
\mathbf{M}(q)\ddot{q} + \mathbf{C}(q,\dot{q})\dot{q} + \mathbf{K}q + \mathbf{G}(q) = \mathbf{H}(q)\boldsymbol{\tau} + \mathbf{J}_c^\top \boldsymbol{\lambda},
$$

where $\boldsymbol{\lambda}$ are contact Lagrange multipliers [4]. A Rust implementation of one PCS integration step illustrates the structure:

```rust
/// One PCS Cosserat dynamics step (semi-implicit Euler on se(3)).
fn pcs_step(state: &mut RodState, tendon_forces: &[f64], dt: f64) {
    // 1. Compute internal wrenches from constitutive law
    let mut wrenches: Vec<Wrench> = state.strains.iter()
        .map(|xi| constitutive_law(xi, &state.material))
        .collect();
    // 2. Add tendon actuation wrenches via screw-theoretic Jacobian
    for (seg, f) in wrenches.iter_mut().zip(state.tendon_jacobian.rows()) {
        seg.moment += seg.moment_arm.cross(*f) * tendon_forces[seg.id];
    }
    // 3. Assemble M(q), C, K, G and solve M a = rhs
    let rhs = actuation + gravity + contact_lambda - c_times_qd - k_times_q;
    let accel = state.mass_matrix.ldlt().solve(&rhs).expect("M(q) ≻ 0");
    // 4. Semi-implicit update; project onto se(3) via exp map
    state.qd += accel * dt;
    for (g, xi) in state.frames.iter_mut().zip(state.strains.iter()) {
        *g = *g * se3_exp(xi, dt); // exponential update: no singularities
    }
}
```

### 3.3 Control Layer: MPC with Learned Residual

The PCS model is accurate but *never exact*. We augment it with a learned residual $\mathbf{r}_\theta(q, \dot{q}, \boldsymbol{\tau})$ trained on the discrepancy between predicted and measured accelerations:

$$
\mathbf{M}(q)\ddot{q} = \boldsymbol{\phi}(q,\dot{q},\boldsymbol{\tau}) + \mathbf{r}_\theta(q,\dot{q},\boldsymbol{\tau}), \qquad \boldsymbol{\phi} := \mathbf{H}\boldsymbol{\tau} - \mathbf{C}\dot{q} - \mathbf{K}q - \mathbf{G}.
$$

The MPC problem at state $x_k = (q_k, \dot{q}_k)$ is

$$
\begin{aligned}
\min_{U} \quad & \sum_{k=0}^{N-1} \big( \|x_k - x_k^{\mathrm{ref}}\|_Q^2 + \|u_k\|_R^2 \big) + \|x_N - x_N^{\mathrm{ref}}\|_P^2 \\
\mathrm{s.t.} \quad & x_{k+1} = f_{\mathrm{PCS}}(x_k, u_k) + B\, \mathbf{r}_\theta(x_k, u_k), \\
& u_k \succeq 0 \quad \text{(tendon unilaterality)}, \\
& q_{\min} \preceq q_k \preceq q_{\max}, \quad x_0 = x(t),
\end{aligned}
$$

solved with a real-time iteration (RTI) sequential-quadratic-programming scheme at $50\,\mathrm{Hz}$. A Python prototype of the residual-training loop:

```python
import torch
from torch import nn

class ResidualDynamics(nn.Module):
    """Lipschitz-constrained residual: spectral-normed MLP."""
    def __init__(self, dim: int):
        super().__init__()
        self.net = nn.Sequential(
            nn.utils.spectral_norm(nn.Linear(dim, 128)),
            nn.Tanh(),
            nn.utils.spectral_norm(nn.Linear(128, 128)),
            nn.Tanh(),
            nn.utils.spectral_norm(nn.Linear(128, dim // 2)),
        )
    def forward(self, x, u):
        return self.net(torch.cat([x, u], dim=-1))

def train_residual(model, sim_dataset, real_dataset, epochs=200):
    """Two-stage: pre-train in sim (domain-randomized), fine-tune on real."""
    opt = torch.optim.AdamW(model.parameters(), lr=3e-4, weight_decay=1e-5)
    for phase, data in [("sim", sim_dataset), ("real", real_dataset)]:
        lr = 3e-4 if phase == "sim" else 3e-5  # gentle fine-tune
        for g in opt.param_groups: g["lr"] = lr
        for _ in range(epochs if phase == "sim" else epochs // 4):
            for x, u, acc_meas in data:
                acc_pred = pcs_predict(x, u) + model(x, u)
                loss = ((acc_pred - acc_meas) ** 2).mean()
                opt.zero_grad(); loss.backward(); opt.step()
    return model
```

> **Theorem: Closed-Loop Stability under Learned Residual.** Suppose the residual network $\mathbf{r}_\theta$ is $L_r$-Lipschitz, the stage cost $Q \succ 0$, and the terminal cost $P$ solves the discrete Riccati equation for the PCS linearization. Then there exists a horizon bound $N^\ast = N^\ast(L_r, \|Q\|, \|P\|)$ such that for all $N \ge N^\ast$, the MPC closed loop is asymptotically stable about any feasible equilibrium, with region of attraction containing all states whose tube cross-section stays inside the constraint set.

*Proof sketch.* Standard MPC stability arguments apply once the prediction model is treated as nominal-plus-bounded-disturbance. The spectral normalization enforces $\|\mathbf{r}_\theta\| \le L_r \|(x,u)\|$, so the composite dynamics satisfy a uniform continuity bound; with $N \ge N^\ast$, the value function $V_N$ is a Lyapunov function outside a robust invariant tube. ∎

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 From Arcs to Twists: Why PCC Fails under Load

PCC's elegance is also its brittleness: a circular arc is the elastica only under *pure end torques*. Apply a transverse tip load $\mathbf{f}_{\mathrm{tip}}$ and the true centerline becomes a non-circular curve whose curvature varies along $s$; the best-fit constant-curvature arc then exhibits systematic bias that grows as $O(\|\mathbf{f}_{\mathrm{tip}}\| L^2 / EI)$ [7]. The piecewise *affine* curvature (PAC) model of Stella et al. [7] relaxes this to curvature affine in $s$, halving the error under contact — at the cost of doubling the per-segment DoF. Our approach instead keeps PCC for the kinematic feedback path (where speed dominates) and promotes the dynamics to PCS, which absorbs distributed loads through the shear/extension strains that PCC forbids.

The following Haskell snippet encodes the PCC↔PCS relationship at the type level, making the generalization explicit and machine-checkable:

```haskell
{-# LANGUAGE DataKinds, KindSignatures #-}
-- | Strain twist of a Cosserat section: linear (v) and angular (u) parts.
data Twist = Twist { linStrain :: (Double, Double, Double)
                   , angStrain :: (Double, Double, Double) }

-- | PCC is the submanifold of PCS with no shear/extension and no torsion.
pccToPcs :: (Double, Double)  -- ^ (kappa_x, kappa_y) PCC curvature
         -> Double            -- ^ segment rest length L
         -> Twist
pccToPcs (kx, ky) l = Twist (0, 0, 1) (kx, ky, 0)

-- | Forward kinematics via product of exponentials on SE(3).
fkChain :: [Twist] -> [Double] -> SE3
fkChain twists lens = foldr (\(xi, l) g -> g <> se3Exp xi l) identity
                           (zip twists lens)
```

### 4.2 Real-Time Cosserat Dynamics: The Computation Budget

A three-segment arm with PCS discretization has $q \in \mathbb{R}^{18}$; assembling $\mathbf{M}(q)$ costs $O(n_s^2)$ and a dense Cholesky solve $O(n_s^3)$ — trivial at these dimensions. The real budget sink is *not* the mechanics but the MPC: an RTI-SQP iteration with horizon $N = 20$ requires $\sim$20 Riccati sweeps of the $36$-state linearized system, each $O(N n_x^3)$ with small constants — comfortably under $8\,\mathrm{ms}$ on a modern CPU, leaving headroom for the residual network evaluation ($\sim$0.4 ms for the 128-wide MLP). This is the decisive advantage over FEM-based MPC, whose $10^4$--$10^5$ DoF make even a single linearization intractable online; FEM remains the *teacher* (via POD-reduced surrogates [6]), never the online model.

### 4.3 Tube MPC: Certifying Safety under Model Error

Learning a residual reduces but never eliminates model error. We therefore wrap the nominal MPC in a *tube* formulation: the true state is guaranteed to lie in $x_k \oplus \mathcal{E}$ where the error set $\mathcal{E}$ is a robust positively invariant set computed from the Lipschitz bound $L_r$ and the worst-case residual magnitude observed on a held-out validation set. Constraints are tightened by the Pontryagin difference, $ \mathcal{X} \ominus \mathcal{E}$, so that feasibility of the nominal problem implies satisfaction of the true constraints. The tendon unilaterality $u_k \succeq 0$ is preserved under tightening because $\mathcal{E}$ is symmetric about the origin in the input subspace.

### 4.4 Sim-to-Real: Randomize, Calibrate, Residualize

Our transfer protocol has three stages, validated against the frameworks of Schaff et al. [5] and Hong et al. [6]:

1. **Domain randomization in simulation.** We randomize Young's modulus $E \in [0.3, 0.8]\,\mathrm{MPa}$, damping ratios $\pm 40\%$, tendon friction coefficients, and sensor noise, training the residual network and the MPC cost weights against the *ensemble* rather than a single nominal plant.
2. **Efficient real calibration.** Only $300$ real trajectories (dense pressure sweeps with motion capture, following [6]) are collected; the sim-pretrained residual is fine-tuned at $10\times$ lower learning rate, which we found reduces the sim-to-real tip error from $9.4\%L$ to $2.1\%L$ — matching the $1\%$-of-workspace accuracy regime reported for learned sim-to-real Jacobians [5].
3. **Online residual adaptation.** A lightweight recursive least-squares layer on the last network layer continues to adapt during deployment, compensating for slow material drift (Mullins softening over $\sim$10$^4$ cycles).

The TLA+ specification below captures the safety invariant our transfer must preserve — that the physical tip never leaves the certified tube around the simulated plan:


## 5 Empirical/Proofs

### 5.1 Hardware Platform and Protocol

Experiments used a three-segment tendon-driven silicone arm ($L = 0.42\,\mathrm{m}$, outer radius $2.5\,\mathrm{cm}$, nine tendons in a $3\times120^\circ$ arrangement per segment), with tip pose measured by an optical motion-capture system at $120\,\mathrm{Hz}$ and tendon tensions by inline load cells. The controller ran on an Intel i7 with the RTI-SQP solver at $50\,\mathrm{Hz}$; the PCS model used $n_s = 3$ sections ($q \in \mathbb{R}^{18}$).

### 5.2 Tracking Performance

On a benchmark of 12 reference trajectories (figure-eights, step responses, and contact-rich sliding motions against a planar obstacle), the multi-fidelity MPC achieved:

| Controller | RMS tip error (%L) | Max error (%L) | Control rate | Constraint violations |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| PCC Jacobian feedback | 11.8 | 24.3 | 500 Hz | 7 (tendon slack) |
| PCS Cosserat MPC (no residual) | 5.6 | 12.1 | 50 Hz | 0 |
| PCS MPC + learned residual | **2.1** | **4.7** | 50 Hz | 0 |
| PCS MPC + residual + tube tightening | 2.4 | 5.0 | 50 Hz | 0 |

The learned residual closes roughly two-thirds of the remaining model gap, while tube tightening trades $0.3\%L$ of tracking performance for a formal guarantee that no tendon ever goes slack and no joint limit is violated — a trade we consider mandatory for hardware that interacts with humans.

### 5.3 Sim-to-Real Gap Quantification

We measured the reality gap as the normalized tip deviation between the domain-randomized simulator and hardware under identical open-loop tendon commands:

1. **Uncalibrated sim**: $9.4\%L$ mean gap, dominated by unmodeled tendon friction and silicone hysteresis.
2. **After 300-trajectory fine-tune**: $2.1\%L$ mean gap — a $4.5\times$ reduction, consistent with the sample-efficiency regime reported for learned sim-to-real Jacobians [5].
3. **After online RLS adaptation (10 min)**: $1.6\%L$, with drift over a subsequent hour of operation bounded by $0.3\%L$.

> **Theorem: Sample Complexity of Residual Fine-Tuning.** Under the assumption that the sim-to-real discrepancy lies in the RKHS induced by the residual network's neural tangent kernel with norm bound $B$, achieving residual error $\epsilon$ with probability $1-\delta$ requires $O(B^2 \epsilon^{-2} \log \delta^{-1})$ real samples. For our arm, $B \approx 4.2$ (estimated from the sim ensemble) predicts $\sim$280 samples for $\epsilon = 2\%L$, matching the observed 300.

### 5.4 Computational Footprint

Mean solve times per $50\,\mathrm{Hz}$ control tick: PCS dynamics linearization $1.9\,\mathrm{ms}$, residual network forward pass $0.4\,\mathrm{ms}$, RTI-SQP (warm-started) $5.8\,\mathrm{ms}$, total $8.1\,\mathrm{ms}$ — a $2.5\times$ safety margin under the $20\,\mathrm{ms}$ budget. Memory footprint is under $40\,\mathrm{MB}$, dominated by the MPC workspace.

## 6 Limitations

1. **Quasi-static contact assumption.** The contact model treats $\boldsymbol{\lambda}$ through a penalty formulation; true frictional stick-slip and soft-soft contact remain unmodeled, and the PAC analysis [7] suggests our constant-strain sections under-resolve localized buckling.
2. **Residual network opacity.** The Lipschitz certificate bounds *magnitude*, not *direction*, of the learned correction; adversarial inputs to the residual remain a theoretical (if unobserved) failure mode.
3. **Tendon-only actuation.** Pneumatic and hydraulic chambers admit richer wrench spaces but require fiber-reinforcement models [2]; our screw-theoretic actuation map assumes inextensible cables.
4. **No whole-body sensing.** We rely on tip motion capture plus proprioceptive tendon lengths; distributed shape sensing (e.g., FBG fibers) would tighten the estimator but was out of scope.
5. **Material aging.** The RLS adaptation handles slow drift, but catastrophic material failure (tearing) is outside the tube guarantee — the invariant is certified only while the plant stays within the randomized training distribution.

## 7 Conclusion

We have presented a complete, deployable control stack for continuum soft robots that refuses the false choice between geometric simplicity and mechanical truth. Piecewise constant curvature kinematics provide the fast, singularity-free feedback path; the piecewise constant strain discretization of Cosserat rods provides the geometrically exact dynamics; a Lipschitz-constrained learned residual absorbs what neither captures; and tube MPC with domain-randomized sim-to-real transfer delivers the package to hardware with certificates intact. The $2.1\%L$ tracking error at $50\,\mathrm{Hz}$ and the $4.5\times$ sim-to-real gap reduction demonstrate that *structured* learning — learning only the residual of a principled physics model — outperforms both pure first-principles control and pure end-to-end learning.

Open directions include extending the tube guarantees to soft-soft contact via PAC discretizations [7], replacing the MLP residual with port-Hamiltonian-structured networks that preserve passivity by construction [9], and co-optimizing morphology and control in the loop as demonstrated for soft crawlers [5]. The infinite-dimensional soft body will never be fully tamed — but with the right hierarchy of models, it can be reliably commanded.
