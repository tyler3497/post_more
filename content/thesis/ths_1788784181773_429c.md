# ARM Confidential Compute Architecture: Realms, Granule Protection Checks, and the Realm Management Monitor

## Abstract

The Arm Confidential Compute Architecture (CCA) breaks the coupling between *resource management* and *the right of access* in virtualized systems: before CCA, a hypervisor that allocated memory to a virtual machine could also read it. CCA introduces a fourth execution world — the **Realm world** — alongside the Root, Secure, and Non-secure worlds of the Armv9-A Realm Management Extension (RME), and enforces isolation in hardware through **Granule Protection Checks (GPCs)** driven by **Granule Protection Tables (GPTs)** programmed by Root-world firmware. The **Realm Management Monitor (RMM)**, executing at Realm EL2, multiplexes Realm virtual machines: it exposes the *Realm Management Interface (RMI)* to the untrusted host for resource management and the *Realm Services Interface (RSI)* to Realms for measurement, memory-state transitions, and attestation. This thesis analyzes the four-world model, GPT lookup and GPC fault semantics, the RMM object model, the Realm Initial Measurement (RIM) and extensible measurement (REM) scheme, and the CBOR/COSE attestation token flow, grounding each claim in the Arm specifications DEN0137, DDI 0615, and DEN0129 and in the TF-RMM, islet, and Trusted Firmware-A implementations.

---

## 1. Introduction

Confidential computing promises execution on infrastructure the workload does not trust. For two decades Arm answered with TrustZone — a Secure/Non-secure partition guarding secrets from a rich OS — but TrustZone never protected a virtual machine from its own hypervisor, because the privilege hierarchy fused two distinct powers: the power to *manage* a resource and the power to *access* it. The RMM specification states the remedy plainly: "a Realm is a protected execution environment for which this coupling is broken, so that the right to manage resources is separated from the right to access those resources" [1].

CCA makes the separation concrete in three layers. First, the **Realm Management Extension (RME)** adds Realm and Root worlds to Armv9-A, introduces Realm exception levels (R-EL0/R-EL1/R-EL2), and defines the **Granule Protection Check**: every access is validated downstream of translation against a per-granule physical-address-space assignment in **Granule Protection Tables** owned by Root-world firmware [2]. Second, the **Realm Management Monitor (RMM)** at R-EL2 manages Realm VMs — stage-2 translation, vCPU context, interrupts — while validating every request from the untrusted host [1]. Third, an ecosystem (EL3 firmware with an RMM dispatcher, KVM with CCA extensions, guest drivers) turns the architecture into a deployable confidential-VM stack [3][4].

This thesis dissects all three layers: §2 situates CCA against TrustZone, Intel TDX, and AMD SEV-SNP; §3 describes the specification-first methodology; §4 covers the four-world model, GPT/GPC mechanics, the RMM's object model and RMI/RSI interfaces, and measurement and attestation; §5 presents executable-model results and proof sketches; §6 examines limitations; §7 concludes.

## 2. Background and Related Work

### 2.1 The privilege-hierarchy problem

Cloud threat models now treat the hypervisor as *malicious or compromised* — a single point of failure shared by thousands of tenants. Intel answered with **SGX** (process enclaves) and **TDX** (trust domains: confidential VMs with memory encrypted and integrity-protected by the memory controller). AMD's **SEV-SNP** encrypts guest memory with per-guest keys and adds a hardware-checked Reverse Map Table so the hypervisor cannot remap guest pages. CCA is Arm's counterpart, distinguished by enforcing isolation through **physical-address-space tagging rather than memory encryption**: every granule (4/16/64 KiB) is assigned to exactly one of four physical address spaces — Non-secure, Secure, Realm, Root — and the GPC faults any access whose requester PAS mismatches the granule's assignment [2]. DRAM encryption against physical attacks is left to platform engines rather than being architecturally mandated.

### 2.2 TrustZone, EL3 firmware, and the road to RME

In Armv8-A, EL3 hosts the Secure Monitor (typically Trusted Firmware-A's BL31), mediating Secure/Non-secure switches via SMC. RME extends this: EL3 becomes a **Root-world monitor** managing transitions into and out of the Realm world, and TF-A gains an **RMM dispatcher (RMMD)** plus a **GPT library** for initializing and transitioning granules between address spaces [4]. New architectural state includes `GPCCR_EL3` (GPC control), `GPTBR_EL3` (GPT base), and `TLBI PAALLOS` for invalidating cached GPT information [2].

### 2.3 Reference implementations and ecosystem

Three open implementations anchor the ecosystem. **TF-RMM** is the Trusted Firmware reference RMM at R-EL2 [5]. **islet** is an independent Rust RMM with a documented attestation-token path [6]. **Trusted Firmware-A** supplies the EL3 side — GPT library, RMMD dispatcher, and a Test Realm Payload (TRP) implementing an RMI subset for bring-up when no RMM image is provided [4]. The host runs KVM with CCA extensions driving RMI; the guest gains an `arm-cca-guest` driver (`drivers/virt/coco/`) retrieving attestation tokens via configfs-tsm [3][7].

## 3. Methodology

This thesis follows a *specification-first, implementation-cross-checked* methodology:

1. **Primary source analysis.** Normative claims — world encodings, GPT lookup rules, GPC fault classes, RMI/RSI semantics, attestation flows — are traced to the *RMM Specification* (DEN0137) [1], the *RME Supplement* (DDI 0615) [2], and the *RME System Architecture* (DEN0129) [8].
2. **Implementation cross-check.** Deployability claims are verified against TF-RMM [5], islet [6], TF-A's RME support [4], and Linux's CCA documentation [7].
3. **Executable reference model.** A Python model of GPT index decoding (DDI 0615 §4.5.5) and RIM construction is executed; outputs are reported in §5 as validation of the bit-level analysis.
4. **Security analysis.** The threat model of [1] is cast as invariants (delegation confinement, measurement integrity, host-request validation), each examined as a theorem with proof sketch.

Cycle-level benchmarking is out of scope: no production RME silicon was available for measurement (see §6).

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 The four-world exception model

RME replaces the two-world model with **four worlds**:

| World | Typical resident | Exception levels | Trust assumption |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Root** | EL3 firmware (TF-A BL31, RMMD, GPT library) | EL3 | Trusted; owns GPT |
| **Secure** | Trusted OS / trusted apps | S-EL2, S-EL1, S-EL0 | Trusted; unchanged semantics |
| **Realm** | RMM + Realm VMs | R-EL2, R-EL1, R-EL0 | Host untrusted; Realms mutually isolated |
| **Non-secure** | Host OS / hypervisor (KVM) | EL2, EL1, EL0 | Untrusted by Realms |

Only **Root-world** software programs the GPT, and it does so at the host's request *through* RMI calls the RMM validates. The host may ask that a granule be *delegated* to the Realm world, but cannot *read* it once delegated — the GPC enforces this in hardware downstream of stage-1 and stage-2 translation [2]. Entry to the RMM is always via SMC trapped to EL3 and dispatched by RMMD to R-EL2. Interrupts for a Realm pass through the RMM (context save/restore); the GIC itself is largely emulated by the untrusted host, with ITS buffers confined to shared memory [3].

> **Theorem 1 (World separation).** *If GPCs are enabled (`GPCCR_EL3.GPC == 1`) and each granule is assigned exactly one PAS, no Non-secure access can read or write a Realm-PAS granule.*
>
> **Proof sketch.** Every access undergoes a GPC downstream of translation [2, §4.5.1]; a Non-secure access carries the Non-secure PAS and faults on GPI mismatch. Only Root-world software modifies the GPT, and the delegation protocol (§4.4) requires Realm consent for transitions *out of* the Realm PAS, so the host cannot alter the assignment either. ∎

### 4.2 Granule Protection Tables and the Granule Protection Check

The **GPT** is a two-level radix structure in Root-world memory assigning a PAS to every granule in the protected range. Lookup is parameterized by `GPCCR_EL3` fields: **`PPS`** (protected physical address size, 32–52 bits), **`PGS`** (granule size: 4/16/64 KiB), and **`L0GPTSZ`** (level-0 coverage, fixing split point `s`). For a physical address `PA` (DDI 0615 §4.5.5) [2]:

1. `PA[51:t]` (`t` = protected size) is range-checked — upper bits must be zero.
2. `PA[t-1:s]` indexes the **level-0 table**; each entry points to a level-1 table.
3. `PA[s-1:p+4]` indexes the **level-1 table**; `PA[p+3:p]` selects one of sixteen **4-bit GPI fields** packed in the 64-bit L1 entry (`p` = 12/14/16 for 4/16/64 KiB).
4. The GPI encodes Non-secure, Secure, Realm, or Root (with reserved no-access encodings).

The **GPC** runs in the MMU *downstream of translation* — after stage-1/stage-2 walks resolve the PA — and additionally at SMMUs (DMA) and interconnect GPCs [2][9]. Checking the *physical* address post-translation is load-bearing: a malicious hypervisor cannot bypass it via stage-2 manipulation. Four fault classes exist:

| Fault class | Cause |
|---|---|
| Granule Protection Fault (GPF) | Requester PAS forbidden by the GPI |
| GPT walk fault | GPT walk fails (invalid L0/L1 entry) |
| GPT address size fault | PA exceeds the configured protected range |
| Sync External abort on GPT fetch | Memory error fetching GPT entries |

GPT data may be TLB-cached; coherence uses `TLBI PAALLOS` plus `DSB` discipline, and RMM delegation flows mandate invalidation exactly where a granule changes PAS, closing TOCTOU windows [2].

```python
def gpt_decode(pa: int, t: int, s: int, p: int):
    """Decode GPT table indices for a physical address (DDI 0615, 4.5.5)."""
    assert pa >> t == 0, "GPT address size fault: PA exceeds protected range"
    l0_index = (pa >> s) & ((1 << (t - s)) - 1)        # PA[t-1:s]
    l1_index = (pa >> (p + 4)) & ((1 << (s - p - 4)) - 1)  # PA[s-1:p+4]
    gpi_sel  = (pa >> p) & 0xF                          # PA[p+3:p]: nibble
    granule  = pa & ~((1 << p) - 1)                     # granule base
    return {"l0": l0_index, "l1": l1_index, "gpi_nibble": gpi_sel,
            "granule_base": hex(granule)}
```

### 4.3 The Realm Management Monitor: architecture, RMI, and RSI

The RMM is the Realm world's firmware hypervisor at **R-EL2**, owning Realm stage-2 tables and multiplexing CPUs among vCPUs. Its core objects:

- **RD (Realm Descriptor):** per-Realm state — RIM, REMs, RAK handle, IPA width, hash algorithm, lifecycle state.
- **REC (Realm Execution Context):** per-vCPU registers, attestation scratch state, exit reason; plus `REC_aux` granules for RMM-private bookkeeping.
- **RTT (Realm Translation Table):** RMM-owned stage-2 tables mapping Realm IPA to delegated granules, supporting folding and entry-state queries (`RMI_RTT_READ_ENTRY`).

The RMM exposes **two SMCCC SMC interfaces** [1]. **RMI (host → RMM)** drives lifecycle: `RMI_VERSION`, `RMI_GRANULE_DELEGATE`/`UNDELEGATE`, `RMI_REALM_CREATE`/`DESTROY`, `RMI_REC_CREATE`/`DESTROY`, `RMI_RTT_CREATE`/`DESTROY`, `RMI_DATA_CREATE`/`DESTROY` (measured initial memory), `RMI_RTT_MAP_UNPROTECTED` (shared memory), `RMI_REC_ENTER` (run a vCPU). The RMM *validates every call*: granule-state preconditions (`UNDELEGATED` before delegation), IPA bounds, alignment, and ordering (no activation before all initial data is created; no undelegation of mapped granules). Policy — *when* to create, *what* to delegate, *when* to schedule — is the host's; **mechanism and validation are the RMM's** [3].

**RSI (Realm → RMM)** serves the Realm: `RSI_IPA_STATE_SET`/`GET` (transition protected IPA between `RAM` and `EMPTY`, enabling ballooning), `RSI_MEASUREMENT_READ`/`EXTEND`, `RSI_ATTESTATION_TOKEN_INIT`/`CONTINUE`, `RSI_HOST_CALL`, `RSI_REALM_CONFIG`. A third interface, **RHI**, carries guest↔hypervisor traffic (virtio) and deliberately *bypasses* the RMM [3] — the RMM is a protection monitor, not a proxy.

The IPA space is split: the *lower half* is protected (host-invisible; the RMM forbids host modification without Realm cooperation), the *upper half* shared (host MMIO emulation, freely modifiable). The Realm selects halves via the top IPA bit, treated as a page-table attribute (`PROT_NS_SHARED` in Linux); the guest's IPA width is therefore dynamic and negotiated at creation [3][7].

> **Theorem 2 (Host-request validation).** *The RMM never mutates granule PAS or Realm lifecycle state on a host request violating the specification's preconditions.*
>
> **Proof sketch.** By case analysis over RMI pseudocode [1, Part B]: each command begins with explicit checks — granule state, Realm state progression, IPA bounds, TLB-invalidation ordering — returning error statuses (`RMI_ERROR_INPUT`, …) without mutation on violation; Part D flows show every PAS transition guarded by at least one such check. ∎

```rust
/// RMI_GRANULE_DELEGATE: host requests moving a granule into the Realm PAS.
fn rmi_granule_delegate(phys: u64) -> RmiStatus {
    if granule_state(phys) != GranuleState::Undelegated {
        return RmiStatus::ErrorInput;   // precondition fails: no mutation
    }
    el3_gpt_transition(phys, Gpi::Realm); // Root world updates GPT via RMMD
    tlbi_paallos(phys); dsb_sy();         // purge stale cached GPT/TLB data
    set_granule_state(phys, GranuleState::Delegated);
    RmiStatus::Success
}
```

### 4.4 Realm lifecycle, measurement, and the attestation token flow

**Lifecycle.** Construction order is RMM-enforced: delegate granules → `RMI_REALM_CREATE` (allocates RD, generates per-Realm RAK) → create RTTs → `RMI_DATA_CREATE` per initial page (each call *extends the RIM*) → `RMI_REC_CREATE` per vCPU → `RMI_REC_ENTER`. Destruction reverses it; undelegating a still-mapped granule fails, so the host cannot silently reclaim Realm memory [1].

**Measurement.** The **RIM (Realm Initial Measurement)** is a hash (e.g., SHA-512) accumulated by the RMM over every `RMI_DATA_CREATE` payload plus Realm parameters (algorithm ID, IPA width, flags). The **REMs (Realm Extensible Measurements, REM[0..3])** start from a fixed value and are extended at runtime by the Realm via `RSI_MEASUREMENT_EXTEND`, measured-boot style, recording its own boot chain [1][6].

**Attestation.** The RMM produces a **CCA attestation token** in two parts [1][6]: (1) a **realm token** — claims {challenge, RIM, REMs, personalization value, RAK public key}, CBOR-encoded, signed with the per-Realm **RAK** (COSE_Sign1); (2) a **platform token** — platform claims signed by a hardware-rooted key (HES/HUK-derived), binding the RAK to genuine CCA hardware. Retrieval is a resumable RSI flow, since signing exceeds one SMC's budget [1, D1.7]:

1. `RSI_ATTESTATION_TOKEN_INIT(addr, challenge)` — RMM records `(ATTEST_IN_PROGRESS, addr, challenge)` in the REC.
2. Loop `RSI_ATTESTATION_TOKEN_CONTINUE(addr)` until the return differs from `RSI_INCOMPLETE`. A physical interrupt mid-generation saves REC context and exits with reason `IRQ`; the Realm re-issues `CONTINUE` — idempotent by construction. Generation must run on the CPU that initialized it [3].
3. The CBOR token (realm + platform) is at `addr`; the Realm forwards it to the verifier, which checks COSE signatures, challenge freshness, and measurements against reference values.

> **Theorem 3 (Measurement integrity).** *The attested RIM equals the hash of the exact initial image loaded, and the host cannot forge a token for a different image.*
>
> **Proof sketch.** RIM is accumulated solely by the RMM over bytes *as written* into Realm-PAS granules during `RMI_DATA_CREATE` [1]; substituted bytes change the RIM, and the realm token's RAK signature is unforgeable without the RAK private half, which never leaves the RMM/platform boundary. Challenge freshness binds the token to the verifier's session. ∎

## 5. Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 Executing the GPT reference model

The §4.2 model was executed for `PPS` = 44 bits, 4 KiB granules, `s` = 30:

| Input PA | L0 index (`PA[43:30]`) | L1 index (`PA[29:16]`) | GPI nibble (`PA[15:12]`) | Granule base |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| `0xB_0000_1000` | `0x2c` | `0x0` | `0x1` | `0xb00001000` |
| `0xB_0001_F234` | `0x2c` | `0x1` | `0xf` | `0xb0001f000` |
| `0xC_1234_5678` | `0x30` | `0x1234` | `0x5` | `0xc12345000` |
| `0x1_0000_0000_0000` (>44 b) | — | — | — | **GPT address size fault** |

Three specification properties hold mechanically: (i) granules sharing a 1 GiB L0 region share the L0 index and diverge at L1/nibble level, matching the radix design; (ii) nibble selection isolates exactly the addressed granule's 4-bit GPI; (iii) out-of-range addresses are rejected *before* any table walk — the range check precedes the walk, as DDI 0615 §4.5.5 orders it [2].

### 5.2 Executing the RIM construction model

Four deterministic 4 KiB pages were hashed incrementally with SHA-512 alongside Realm parameters (IPA width, flags, algorithm ID). The 64-byte RIM was run-stable, and flipping a single bit of one page changed the digest — the avalanche behavior on which Theorem 3 rests. An `RSI_MEASUREMENT_EXTEND` chain over four boot stages (BL33 → bootloader → kernel → application policy) produced a final REM committing to the *ordered* sequence: swapping two stages changed the REM, confirming order-sensitivity of extend.

### 5.3 Corroboration from running implementations

- **TF-RMM** implements the DEN0137 RMI/RSI surface at R-EL2 and boots Realm VMs under the FVP with TF-A's RMMD [5].
- **islet** independently implements the token path — realm claims (challenge, RIM, REMs, personalization, RAK public key), CBOR encoding, RAK signing, composition with the cached platform token — showing the format is implementable from the specification alone [6].
- **TF-A** supplies the EL3 GPT library, RMMD, and TRP fallback for bring-up [4].
- **Linux** documents the full stack: KVM driving RMI, the guest's `PROT_NS_SHARED` top-bit convention, and the `arm-cca-guest` driver retrieving tokens via configfs-tsm on the initializing CPU [3][7].

Two independent RMMs plus a complete EL3/host/guest stack interoperating on the Arm Fixed Virtual Platform give high confidence the specification is realizable.

## 6. Limitations and Threats to Validity

**DMA and device assignment.** SMMU-translated accesses need GPC coverage too; the SMMUv3 RME supplement defines GPC fault reporting (`SMMU_ROOT_GPF_FAR`) and SMMU GPT configuration [9]. A misprogrammed SMMU GPT base is a full bypass — device isolation is only as strong as Root-world SMMU programming.

**Interrupt and GIC emulation.** The GIC is largely host-emulated [3]. The RMM mediates save/restore and virtual-interrupt injection, but the host observes interrupt *patterns* (a side channel), and the RMM's interrupt state machine is the largest remaining trusted-code surface after memory isolation.

**Side channels and physical attacks.** CCA provides access control, not memory encryption; cold-boot and bus-snooping resistance depends on platform memory-encryption engines that are not architecturally mandated. Cross-Realm microarchitectural channels rely on RMM context-switch hygiene, for which no machine-checked proof exists.

**Maturity.** The RMM specification evolved through beta revisions to 1.0-REL0; TF-A's RME support targets the FVP, and production RME silicon is only now arriving — FVP-verified behavior may diverge from silicon in GPC fault timing and GPT caching.

**Validity of this analysis.** The Python models validate *understanding*, not hardware; the theorems are sketches over specification pseudocode, not machine-checked proofs; implementation claims rest on public repository state, which evolves.

## 7. Conclusion

CCA reframes confidential computing as *physical-address-space governance*. By adding the Realm and Root worlds, tagging every granule in hardware-enforced GPTs, and checking every access downstream of translation, RME reduces the monitor to a small, checkable job: validate host requests, isolate Realms, measure what was loaded, sign evidence of it. The RMI/RSI split separates untrusted policy (host) from trusted mechanism (RMM); the attestation token — RIM plus REMs, challenge-bound, RAK-signed, platform-anchored — closes the loop from hardware root of trust to remote verifier. The executable models here confirm the bit-level mechanics of GPT decoding and measurement; TF-RMM and islet confirm the specification is buildable. Remaining work is the hard engineering: SMMU/GIC hardening, side-channel discipline, silicon validation, and the machine-checked proofs that would promote Theorems 1–3 from sketches to guarantees. CCA does not make the hypervisor trustworthy — it makes the hypervisor *irrelevant* to confidentiality, which is the stronger claim.
