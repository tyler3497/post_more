# Graph Limits: Szemerédi's Regularity Lemma, Graphons, and the Cut Metric

## Abstract

What is the limit of a sequence of finite graphs? For dense graphs, the theory of graph limits gives a complete answer: the limit objects are *graphons* — symmetric measurable functions from the unit square into the unit interval — and convergence is governed by the *cut metric*. This thesis develops the analytic picture in full. We define homomorphism densities and left convergence, state and prove Szemerédi's regularity lemma by the energy-increment method, and present the Frieze–Kannan weak regularity lemma with its low-rank cut approximations. We introduce the cut norm, empirical graphons, and the cut distance obtained by quotienting out measure-preserving relabelings. The central results are proved: the space of graphons modulo weak isomorphism is compact in the cut distance; a graph sequence converges in homomorphism densities if and only if its empirical graphons are Cauchy in the cut metric; and the limit is unique up to weak isomorphism. Applications to the triangle removal lemma, quasi-random graphs, and property testing are given, together with the fundamental limitations: tower-type bounds, collapse in sparse regimes, and hardness of computing the cut distance.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Homomorphism densities and left convergence

For finite simple graphs *F*, *G*, a **homomorphism** is a map φ: *V(F)* → *V(G)* preserving adjacency. The **homomorphism density**

$$t(F, G) = \frac{\text{hom}(F,G)}{|V(G)|^{|V(F)|}}$$

is the probability that a random map is a homomorphism [3]. A sequence *(Gₙ)* with |*V(Gₙ)*| → ∞ is **left-convergent** if *t(F, Gₙ)* converges for every fixed *F*. This asks that all subgraph statistics stabilize — and raises the question of *what* they converge to. The answer is not a graph, but a graphon.

### 2.2 The regularity lemma, stated

For disjoint vertex sets *X*, *Y* with density *d(X, Y)* = *e(X,Y)* / (|*X*|·|*Y*|), the pair (*X*, *Y*) is **ε-regular** if |*d(X′,Y′)* − *d(X,Y)*| ≤ ε for all *X′* ⊆ *X*, *Y′* ⊆ *Y* with |*X′*| ≥ ε|*X*|, |*Y′*| ≥ ε|*Y*|. An ε-regular pair mimics a random bipartite graph of the same density.

> **Theorem:** (Szemerédi's regularity lemma [4]) For every ε > 0 and integer *m* there is *M*(ε, *m*) such that every graph with at least *m* vertices has a partition *V₀* ∪ ⋯ ∪ *Vₖ*, *m* ≤ *k* ≤ *M*, with |*V₀*| ≤ ε*n*, equal-sized remaining parts, and all but at most ε*k*² of the pairs (*Vᵢ*, *Vⱼ*) ε-regular.

Every large graph is thus summarized by a bounded-size weighted "cluster graph" of pair densities. The price is *M*(ε): a tower of exponentials in ε⁻¹, shown essentially optimal by Gowers [8].

### 2.3 The cut norm and empirical graphons

A **graphon** is a symmetric measurable *W* from the unit square into the unit interval. A graph *G* on *n* vertices becomes its **empirical graphon** *W_G*: tile the unit square with an *n* × *n* grid, setting *W_G* ≡ 1 on tile (*i*, *j*) when *ij* ∈ *E(G)*. Graph convergence becomes convergence of gray-scale images [2]. The **cut norm**

$$\|W\|_{\square} = \sup_{S,T} \left| \int_{S \times T} W(x,y)\,dx\,dy \right|$$

measures the largest imbalance of *W* across any rectangular cut. It forgets fine detail while remembering exactly the information relevant to cuts and subgraph densities.

### 2.4 Toward the cut metric

The cut norm compares functions with *fixed* labels, but vertex labels are arbitrary. The **cut distance** takes the infimum over all measure-preserving relabelings of the unit interval, and its completion is the space of graphons modulo **weak isomorphism** [3]. The sections ahead make this precise.

## 3 Methodology

The methodology is *analytic*: translate combinatorial questions into analysis, prove theorems with soft tools, and pull consequences back to finite graphs.

### 3.1 The energy-increment argument

For a partition *P* = {*V₀*, …, *Vₖ*} of an *n*-vertex graph, the **energy** *q(P)* = Σ_{i,j} (|*Vᵢ*||*Vⱼ*|/*n*²) *d(Vᵢ*, *Vⱼ*)² satisfies 0 ≤ *q(P)* ≤ 1. If *P* is not ε-regular, some irregular pair admits witness subsets whose density deviates by more than ε; refining along all witnesses raises the energy by a fixed amount depending only on ε. Termination after O(ε⁻¹)-many steps yields an ε-regular partition — but parts compound at each refinement, producing the tower bound [4][8]. This *bounded potential + definite increment* template recurs throughout the subject.

### 3.2 Weak regularity and low-rank approximation

Frieze and Kannan [6] proved a weaker, far more tractable lemma: every *n*-vertex graph is ε*n*²-close in cut norm to a cluster graph on *k* ≤ 2^{O(ε⁻²)} parts. Equivalently, the adjacency matrix is a sum of O(ε⁻²) rank-one *cut matrices* plus small cut-norm error. The proof increments the squared Frobenius norm of the residual. Exponential rather than tower bounds make this the *algorithmic* version, underlying fast dense Max-Cut approximations [6].

### 3.3 The martingale construction of limits

Lovász and Szegedy [2] built the limit graphon via weak regularity plus a **martingale argument**: refining ε-regular partitions with ε → 0 give step-function approximations forming a martingale with respect to the partition σ-algebras, and martingale convergence yields a measurable limit on the unit square. Finite combinatorics passes to the continuum exactly as Riemann sums pass to integrals.

### 3.4 Factoring out relabelings

For graphons *U*, *W* and measure-preserving φ, write *W^φ(x, y)* = *W*(φ(*x*), φ(*y*)). The **cut distance** δ_□(*U*, *W*) = inf_φ ‖*U* − *W^φ*‖_□ is a pseudometric whose zero-sets are the **weakly isomorphic** pairs: δ_□(*U*, *W*) = 0 iff *t(F, U)* = *t(F, W)* for all *F* [3][5]. The quotient — graphons modulo weak isomorphism — is a **compact metric space** [2]. Convergence notions unified by the theory:

| Notion | Criterion | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Left convergence | *t(F, Gₙ)* converges for all *F* | [3] |
| Metric convergence | *(Gₙ)* Cauchy in δ_□ | [3] |
| Sampling convergence | Random induced-subgraph laws converge | [2] |
| Structural convergence | Statistical-physics ground-state energies converge | [3] |

For dense graphs these are all equivalent [3] — each is convergence of empirical graphons to a limit graphon in the cut metric.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Szemerédi's Regularity Lemma in Full Detail

The energy-increment proof (Section 3.1) terminates because energy is bounded by 1 and each refinement of a non-ε-regular partition adds a fixed ε-dependent increment. The resulting partition has at most a tower-type number of parts — and Gowers showed towers are necessary [8].

> **Theorem:** (Counting lemma) If *F* has *v* vertices and *G* admits an ε-regular partition with cluster graph *R*, then *t(F, G)* is close to *t(F, W_R)*, the density predicted by the step-graphon of *R* — provided ε is small relative to the relevant pair densities.

The counting lemma makes regularity useful: the bounded summary *R* determines all subgraph densities. Its flagship application is the **triangle removal lemma** of Ruzsa and Szemerédi: a graph with *o(n³)* triangles becomes triangle-free after deleting *o(n²)* edges. Proof: regularize, delete edges inside parts, across irregular pairs, and across low-density pairs (*o(n²)* deletions); a surviving triangle would sit in pairwise regular dense parts, where counting forces Ω(*n³*) triangles — contradiction [4]. *Decompose, clean, count*: the template behind the full graph removal lemma and much of extremal combinatorics.

### 4.2 The Weak Regularity Lemma and Low-Rank Approximation

> **Theorem:** (Frieze–Kannan [6]) For every ε > 0, every *n*-vertex graph *G* has a vertex partition into *k* ≤ 2^{O(ε⁻²)} parts whose cluster graph *R* satisfies ‖*A_G* − *A_R*‖_□ ≤ ε*n*².

The adjacency matrix thus splits into O(ε⁻²) rank-one cut matrices plus small cut-norm error — a cousin of the SVD tuned to the cut norm. This powers polynomial-time approximation schemes for dense Max-Cut and related problems: enumerate over the cut matrices, solve a small integer program on the parts, lift the solution [6].

The *W*-random graph construction bridges finite graphs and graphons computationally:

```python
import numpy as np

def w_random_graph(W, n, seed=0):
    """Sample G(n, W): latent x_i uniform, edge (i,j) with prob W(x_i, x_j)."""
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    xs = rng.random(n)
    Wmat = W(xs[:, None], xs[None, :])
    A = (rng.random((n, n)) < Wmat).astype(int)
    return np.triu(A, 1) + np.triu(A, 1).T

def W_sbm(x, y):  # two-block stochastic block model graphon
    return np.where((x < 0.5) == (y < 0.5), 0.8, 0.1)

A = w_random_graph(W_sbm, 400)
print("edge density:", A.sum() / (400 * 399))
```

A theorem of [2][3]: **G**(*n*, *W*) converges almost surely to *W* in the cut metric. Every graphon is the limit of some graph sequence, and random sampling is the canonical construction.

### 4.3 Graphons: Definition, Sampling, and Weak Isomorphism

The homomorphism density extends to graphons by integration:

$$t(F, W) = \int_{(0,1)^{V(F)}} \prod_{ij \in E(F)} W(x_i, x_j)\, dx,$$

recovering *t(F, G)* for *W* = *W_G* [3]. A measure-preserving φ acts by *W^φ(x, y)* = *W*(φ(*x*), φ(*y*)) — relabeling in the continuum. Graphons *U*, *W* are **weakly isomorphic** if they are pullbacks of a common *Z*; equivalently *t(F, U)* = *t(F, W)* ∀*F*; equivalently δ_□(*U*, *W*) = 0 [3][5].

> **Theorem:** (Uniqueness of graph limits [7]) For graphons *U*, *W*: δ_□(*U*, *W*) = 0 ⇔ *t(F, U)* = *t(F, W)* for all *F* ⇔ *U*, *W* weakly isomorphic.

Like uniqueness of real limits of rational sequences, this makes δ_□ a genuine *metric* on the quotient — via the hard moment analysis of [7]. Canonical examples: constant graphons *W* ≡ *p* (limits of *G(n, p)*, i.e. quasi-random sequences); the half-graphon 1_{x+y≥1} (convergent but structured); step-function block-model graphons (community structure). Every graphon is a cut-norm limit of step graphons. In Haskell, empirical graphons are step functions and *t(F, W)* is Monte Carlo integration:

```haskell
type Graphon = Double -> Double -> Double

empiricalGraphon :: [[Int]] -> Graphon
empiricalGraphon adj = \x y ->
  let n = length adj
      i = min (n-1) (floor (x * fromIntegral n))
      j = min (n-1) (floor (y * fromIntegral n))
  in fromIntegral ((adj !! i) !! j)

-- t(F, W) for a {0,1}-valued graphon, by sampling
homDensity :: [(Int,Int)] -> Graphon -> Int -> IO Double
homDensity edges w trials = do
  hits <- fmap sum $ replicateM trials $ do
    xs <- replicateM k (randomRIO (0.0, 1.0))
    return (if all (\(u,v) -> w (xs!!u) (xs!!v) > 0.5) edges then 1 else 0 :: Int)
  return (fromIntegral hits / fromIntegral trials)
  where k = 1 + maximum (map (uncurry max) edges)
```

### 4.4 The Cut Metric: Structure of the Limit Space

> **Theorem:** (Convergence in the cut metric [3]) For graphs *(Gₙ)* with |*V(Gₙ*)| → ∞ and a graphon *W*: *t(F, Gₙ)* → *t(F, W)* ∀*F* ⇔ δ_□(*W_{Gₙ}*, *W*) → 0.

Left convergence *is* metric convergence. With compactness of the quotient space, every dense sequence has a left-convergent subsequence, and limits are unique up to weak isomorphism: the graphon space is the **completion** of finite graphs [2][5]. Densities are quantitatively controlled by the **graphon counting lemma**: for *F* with *e* edges, |*t(F, U)* − *t(F, W)*| ≤ *e*·δ_□(*U*, *W*), proved by telescoping over edges [3]. Hence δ_□-convergence gives all subgraph densities with explicit rates — the basis of *testability*: a constant-size sample of a huge dense graph estimates its triangle density, because sampling distance is controlled by cut distance [3].

| Distance | Between | Label-invariant? | Controls densities? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cut distance δ_□ | Graphs / graphons | Yes | Yes (counting lemma) |
| Cut norm ‖·‖_□ | Fixed-label graphons | No | Yes, directly |
| Sampling distance | Graphs | Yes | Yes, asymptotically |
| Edit distance | Graphs | Yes | Only coarse densities |

The cut distance is the *weakest* distance controlling all homomorphism densities — exactly why it is the right one: it identifies precisely the pairs asymptotically indistinguishable by subgraph statistics.

## 5 Empirical and Proof-Theoretic Core

### 5.1 Compactness

> **Theorem:** (Lovász–Szegedy [2]) Graphons modulo weak isomorphism, with δ_□, form a compact metric space.

Every graph sequence with |*V(Gₙ*)| → ∞ thus has a subsequence converging in δ_□ — the Bolzano–Weierstrass theorem of graph theory. A corollary stressed in [3]: *inhomogeneous quasi-random graphs are completely general* — every dense sequence has a subsequence resembling **G**(*n*, *W*) for some *W*. The models **G**(*n*, *W*), one per graphon, asymptotically cover all dense graphs.

### 5.2 Canonical convergent sequences

**Erdős–Rényi** *G(n, p)* converges a.s. in δ_□ to the constant graphon *W* ≡ *p*; subgraph densities concentrate at *p^{e(F)}*. A sequence is quasi-random (Chung–Graham–Wilson) iff its limit graphon is a.e. constant. The **half-graphs** *Hₙ* (edges *aᵢbⱼ* for *i* ≤ *j*) converge to the half-graphon 1_{x+y≥1} — convergent but not quasi-random, with visible large-scale structure in the gray-scale limit [2].

### 5.3 Testing and estimation

A graph parameter is **testable** if estimable from a small random induced subgraph. Borgs et al. [3] proved every δ_□-continuous parameter is testable, with sample complexity from the modulus of continuity — and conversely. Since *t(F, ·)* is Lipschitz in δ_□, all subgraph densities are testable: large dense graphs are statistically *compressible*. This founded **graphon estimation**: given *G* ~ **G**(*n*, *W*), recover *W* in cut norm, with the unknown latent ordering mirroring the infimum over φ in δ_□; minimax rates and spectral estimators are surveyed in [1]. Diaconis and Janson [9] embed graphons in Aldous–Hoover theory: every exchangeable infinite random graph is a mixture of *W*-random graphs — graphons are the ergodic decomposition of exchangeability.

### 5.4 Algorithmic regularity in practice

Implementations follow the weak lemma [6]: compute a low-rank cut approximation, intersect bipartitions into parts, solve the problem on the small cluster graph, lift the solution. For dense Max-Cut this yields polynomial-time approximation schemes — the infinitary theory made concrete.

## 6 Limitations

### 6.1 Tower-type bounds are unavoidable

Gowers [8] proved that some graphs need tower-type many parts in *every* ε-regular partition: the regularity lemma is purely asymptotic, silent about graphs below astronomical sizes. Quantitative work must use the weak lemma or accept tower dependencies.

### 6.2 The dense regime only

Graphons capture **dense** graphs (Ω(*n*²) edges). For sparse sequences, *W_{Gₙ}* → 0 in cut norm and all densities vanish — the theory trivializes. Sparse limits need different objects: *L^p* graphons, **graphexes**, Benjamini–Schramm local-weak limits. No single compact limit space as clean as the graphon space exists there; the cut metric must be renormalized by edge density to see structure [3].

### 6.3 Hardness and non-constructive limits

Compactness yields limits non-constructively, and computing δ_□ exactly is NP-hard in general (it generalizes quadratic assignment); the infimum over measure-preserving maps is infinite-dimensional. Practice uses the cut *norm* or weak-regularity approximations instead. The uniqueness theorem [7] characterizes limits without constructing the witnessing maps.

### 6.4 Subtleties and blind spots

Graphons live naturally on general probability spaces [5]; weak isomorphism is strictly coarser than a.e. isomorphism, requiring couplings via a third graphon. And δ_□ forgets by design: graphs at tiny cut distance can differ wildly in diameter or chromatic number. The theory measures *statistical* similarity, not isomorphism — powerful where statistics suffice, silent where they do not.

## 7 Conclusion

From Szemerédi's decomposition lemma [4] through low-rank cut approximations [6] to the metric theory of Borgs, Chayes, Lovász, Sós, and Vesztergombi [3] and Lovász–Szegedy [2], graph limits form a complete analytic theory of dense graphs: a compact space of continuum objects in which every dense sequence has convergent subsequences, limits are unique up to weak isomorphism [7], and subgraph counts, cut distance, sampling, and physical energies all agree. The *approximate in cut norm, then count* methodology reshaped extremal combinatorics, property testing, and network statistics. Its edges — tower bounds, sparse collapse, computational hardness — mark where soft analysis meets hard combinatorics. Open directions abound: unifying sparse limit theories, effective uniqueness, practical cut-distance approximation, and graphons in machine learning. The unit square still has secrets to yield.
