# Formally Verified Floating-Point Arithmetic: the Flocq Formalization, Rigorous Error Analysis, and CompCert Float Semantics

## Abstract

Floating-point arithmetic is the engine room of scientific computing, yet its semantics resist rigorous reasoning: rounding, subnormals, signed zeros, infinities, and NaNs conspire against even small kernels. This thesis presents the modern machine-checked approach to floating-point correctness. We describe the **Flocq** library — Sylvie Boldo and Guillaume Melquiond's multi-radix, multi-precision formalization of floating-point and fixed-point arithmetic in the Coq proof assistant [1][8] — which replaces ad-hoc per-project models with a generic *format* abstraction (parameterized by radix and precision) instantiated as **FLX** (extended), **FLT** (bounded-exponent IEEE-style), and **FIX** (fixed-point) models. We review the classical rigorous error-analysis framework of Nicholas Higham [6]: the standard rounding model, *forward* versus *backward* error, condition numbers, and *running error bounds* via the $\gamma_n = nu/(1-nu)$ machinery. We then explain how this theory meets a verified compiler: CompCert's floating-point semantics, in which Boldo, Jourdan, Leroy, and Melquiond extended Flocq to full IEEE-754 and proved that compilation preserves the semantics of floating-point programs [3]. We describe **Gappa**, the automated tactic that discharges interval-style rounding-error bounds and emits checkable Coq certificates [4][5][7], and survey verified numerical kernels — from compensated summation to the wave-equation solver — that this stack has made possible. We state the precise error theorems in their formal dress, discuss what CompCert's guarantees do *not* cover, and identify open problems: machine-checked *backward* error theorems at scale, certified transcendental functions, and low-precision formats.

## 1 Introduction

Scientific codes execute billions of floating-point operations whose individual errors are tiny but whose aggregate effect is uncertain. Two communities have attacked this problem. The numerical-analysis community developed *rigorous error analysis*: theorems of the form "the computed $\hat{x}$ satisfies $(A + \delta A)\hat{x} = b + \delta b$ with $|\delta A| \le \gamma_n |A|$," giving *backward* stability guarantees independent of implementation detail [6]. Each model made slightly different choices about radix, precision, rounding modes, and special values, so theorems were not reusable. Flocq ("Floats in Coq"), by Sylvie Boldo and Guillaume Melquiond (ARITH-20, 2011), was the decisive response: a unified multi-radix, multi-precision library with generic floating- and fixed-point formats, executable rounding operators, and the classical error theorems in reusable form [1]. On this foundation, the CompCert project — the first industrially realistic *formally verified* C compiler — extended Flocq to cover all of IEEE-754, including infinities, NaNs, and signed zeros, and proved that compiled floating-point code preserves the source semantics [3]. Meanwhile the Gappa tool [4][5] automated the most tedious layer: propagating rounding-error bounds through straight-line numerical code while producing independently checkable Coq proofs.

This thesis weaves these threads into one story. Section 2 reviews prior formalizations and classical error analysis. Section 3 describes the methodology. Section 4 dives deep: the format hierarchy, the rounding operator and its error theorems, forward/backward analysis, CompCert float semantics, and the Gappa tactic. Section 5 examines results: verified kernels and test suites. Section 6 confronts the limitations; Section 7 concludes.

> **Convention.** Throughout, $u = 2^{-p}$ denotes the unit roundoff of a precision-$p$ binary format, $\mathbb{F}$ denotes a set of representable floating-point values, $\circ$ a rounding mode, and $\mathrm{fl}(\cdot)$ the rounded evaluation. $[\,\cdot\,]$ citations refer to the numbered References.

## 2 Background and Related Work

### 2.1 The classical theory: rigorous error analysis

The standard model of floating-point arithmetic, crystallized by Wilkinson and given its canonical modern treatment by Higham [6], postulates that for each basic operation $\mathrm{op} \in \{+, -, \times, /\}$ (and, on IEEE-754 hardware, $\sqrt{\cdot}$),

$$
\mathrm{fl}(a \;\mathrm{op}\; b) = (a \;\mathrm{op}\; b)(1 + \delta), \qquad |\delta| \le u,
$$

with $u = 2^{-p}$ (or $u = 2^{1-p}$ in some conventions), whenever the exact result lies in the normalized range. This deceptively simple axiom is the foundation of *forward* error analysis: one propagates the $(1+\delta)$ perturbations through the algorithm and bounds the distance $\|\hat{y} - y\|$ between computed and exact results. More powerfully, it supports *backward* error analysis: showing that the computed $\hat{y}$ is the exact result of the algorithm applied to perturbed data, e.g. $(A + \Delta A)\hat{x} = b$ with $|\Delta A| \le \gamma_n |A|$.

Higham's running-error analysis replaces the traditional $\gamma_n$ bounds (which grow with the worst-case $n$) by *running* bounds computed alongside the algorithm, tightening constants by orders of magnitude for typical $n$ [6]. The book *Accuracy and Stability of Numerical Algorithms* [6] — over 680 pages in its second edition — remains the definitive reference: algorithmic derivations, perturbation theory, and rounding-error analysis for linear algebra, elementary functions, and beyond.

Complementary to Higham is the *Handbook of Floating-Point Arithmetic* by Muller, Brunie, de Dinechin, Jeannerod, Joldes, Lefèvre, Melquiond, Revol, and Torres (Birkhäuser, 2nd ed. 2018), the authoritative reference on formats, elementary-function implementation, and the IEEE-754 standard itself. Together, these works define what "correctness" of a floating-point computation *means* mathematically.

### 2.2 Formalizations before Flocq

Several floating-point formalizations preceded Flocq, each tuned to a different purpose [1]:

Prior efforts each targeted one purpose [1]: **Harrison (HOL Light)** verified Intel's division, square-root, and transcendental algorithms [8]; **Daumas–Rideau–Théry (Coq)** pursued high-level properties; the **Gappa/Why** model served program verification and automation; **ACL2/PVS** models targeted hardware and microcode. Each was monolithic — radix 2, fixed precision, baked-in rounding modes — so generalizing a theorem to another precision or radix meant redoing the work. Boldo and Melquiond's diagnosis [1]: a *generic*, format-parameterized formalization would subsume them all.

### 2.3 IEEE-754 in one paragraph

IEEE-754 standardizes interchange formats, four rounding modes, and *correct rounding* of $+,-,\times,/, \sqrt{\cdot}$, and fused multiply-add, plus the exceptional values $\pm\infty$, NaNs, and signed zeros with specified propagation rules. This is the semantics Flocq's FLT format models and that CompCert preserves [3].

## 3 Methodology

Our methodological claim is that *verified floating-point arithmetic* is best understood as a four-layer stack, and this thesis follows the stack upward:

1. **Model layer (Flocq core):** a *generic format* $\varphi$ parameterized by radix $\beta$ and precision, with a *rounding operator* $\mathrm{round}(\varphi, \mathrm{mode}, x)$ satisfying the fundamental error theorems — existence, uniqueness, and the $u$-bound — for *all* instantiations [1].
2. **Instantiations:** **FLX** (extended precision, unbounded exponent — convenient for proving the core lemmas without overflow bookkeeping), **FLT** (bounded exponent, modeling IEEE-754 floats including subnormals), and **FIX** (fixed-point arithmetic) [1][2].
3. **Theory layer (rigorous error analysis):** the standard model $\mathrm{fl}(a\,\mathrm{op}\,b) = (a\,\mathrm{op}\,b)(1+\delta)$ derived from the rounding theorems; forward and backward error bounds; running error analysis à la Higham [6]; and error-free transformations (TwoSum, Fast2Sum, Dekker splitting) whose correctness proofs are Flocq's showcase applications [2].
4. **Automation and deployment layer:** **Gappa** for automatic bound propagation with Coq-checkable certificates [4][5][7], and **CompCert** for end-to-end semantic preservation of floating-point C programs [3].

This layering is how the artifacts are organized: Flocq ships a *core* directory (generic formats, rounding) and *algorithm* layers; CompCert imports Flocq's IEEE layer and adds the compiler-correctness proof; Gappa generates proof terms that Coq checks. Each layer's theorems use the most general interface that admits them — the "reevaluating known properties and generalizing them" Boldo and Melquiond describe as Flocq's founding motivation [1].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The generic format abstraction

The masterstroke of Flocq is to *not* fix the format: a **format** is an abstract predicate $\varphi$ on reals, parameterized by radix $\beta \ge 2$ and precision parameters, describing which reals are representable. Rounding is a single operator:

```coq
Definition round (f : format) (mode : rounding_mode) (x : R) : R
```

satisfying $\mathrm{round}(f, \mathrm{mode}, x) \in f$ for *valid* formats, with the crucial generic theorems [1]:

> **Theorem 1 (Rounding error bound, generic).** *For any valid format $f$ and rounding mode,*
> $$
> |\mathrm{round}(f, \mathrm{mode}, x) - x| \le \tfrac{1}{2}\,\mathrm{ulp}(\mathrm{round}(f, \mathrm{mode}, x)),
> $$
> *for round-to-nearest, where $\mathrm{ulp}$ is the unit in the last place; and the relative bound*
> $$
> \left|\frac{\mathrm{round}(f, \mathrm{mode}, x) - x}{x}\right| \le u = 2^{-p},
> $$
> *in the normalized range.*

Because the theorem is proved *once* over the abstract format, every instantiation inherits it — eliminating the duplication of earlier libraries, which reproved the $u$-bound for each concrete format.

### 4.2 The format hierarchy: FLX, FLT, and FIX

Flocq provides three canonical instantiations [1][2]:

| Format | Parameters | Models | Exponent range | Use case |
|--------|-----------|--------|----------------|----------|
| **FLX** | radix $\beta$, precision $p$ | $x = m \cdot \beta^e$, $\|m\| < \beta^p$ | unbounded | Core lemmas without overflow; extended-precision reasoning |
| **FLT** | radix $\beta$, precision $p$, min exponent $e_{\min}$ | IEEE-style floats with subnormals | bounded below | Faithful IEEE-754 binary32/64 semantics |
| **FIX** | radix $\beta$, least exponent $e_{\min}$ | $x = m \cdot \beta^{e_{\min}}$ | fixed | Fixed-point arithmetic, hardware models |

Deep lemmas — Sterbenz, Dekker's TwoSum, Fast2Sum's exactness under $|a| \ge |b|$ — are proved over **FLX**, where overflow cannot occur. They are then *transported* to **FLT** (the format real programs use) via a correspondence theorem, with overflow handled as a separate, localized concern — a methodology earlier libraries, which entangled overflow into every lemma, could not support [1].

FIX, the fixed-point format, models the fixed-point arithmetic of DSPs and FPGAs, and Gappa's dyadic-fraction treatment of both fixed- and floating-point arithmetic [5] rests on exactly this unified view.

### 4.3 The rounding operator and its algebra

Flocq's rounding operator is not merely axiomatized — the library provides *computable* operators for reflection-based proofs and for *testing* the formalization against hardware [2]:

1. For $+,\times$: compute the *exact* result first on integers, then round (exact results are dyadic rationals with bounded denominators).
2. For $/,\sqrt{\cdot}$: scale inputs so the integer quotient/root has enough digits, then use the *remainder* to determine the round/sticky-bit position — exactly, on integers.

This yields executable functions like `b64_add`, `b64_sqrt` that can be *evaluated inside Coq* and compared against expected bit patterns. Melquiond reports that the FPAccuracy test suite — 1055 binary64 square-root tests — is checked by Coq itself in about 3 seconds [2]. The model is executable, and its agreement with hardware is machine-checked on thousands of vectors.

High-level theorems over the rounding operator include:

- **Monotonicity** of rounding in each mode.
- **Idempotence:** rounding a representable value is the identity.
- **The $u$-bound** (Theorem 1 above), in both absolute ($\mathrm{ulp}/2$) and relative ($2^{-p}$) forms.
- **Exactness lemmas:** Sterbenz ($y/2 \le x \le 2y \Rightarrow x - y$ exact), Dekker's TwoSum decomposition, and the Fast2Sum correctness theorem — the building blocks of *compensated* algorithms.

### 4.4 Rigorous error analysis, formalized

With Theorem 1 in hand, Flocq derives Higham's **standard model** as a corollary rather than an axiom [6][1]:

> **Theorem 2 (Standard model, formalized).** *For $x, y \in \mathrm{FLT}$ with the exact result in the normalized range,*
> $$
> \mathrm{fl}(x \;\mathrm{op}\; y) = (x \;\mathrm{op}\; y)(1 + \delta), \qquad |\delta| \le u,
> $$
> *for $\mathrm{op} \in \{+, -, \times, /\}$ and $\sqrt{\cdot}$, under round-to-nearest.*

The classical analyses flow from the standard model. For recursive summation $s_n = \mathrm{fl}(s_{n-1} + x_n)$, backward analysis shows the computed $\hat{s}_n$ equals the *exact* sum of perturbed inputs $x_i(1 + \theta_i)$ with $|\theta_i| \le \gamma_{n-1}$, where

$$
\gamma_n \;=\; \frac{nu}{1 - nu}, \qquad nu < 1,
$$

Higham's workhorse bound [6], proved in Flocq by induction over the computation — the mechanization is straightforward *once* the rounding theorems exist, which is precisely the point: the library amortizes the hard part.

*Running error analysis* [6] goes further: instead of the a priori $\gamma_n$, one computes *running* bounds $\epsilon_i$ alongside the summation, e.g. $\epsilon_i = \epsilon_{i-1} + u|\hat{s}_i|$, yielding estimates that track the actual computation and are typically far tighter. The bound computation is itself floating-point code that must be verified — a self-referential task where Gappa-style automation shines.

The *backward* perspective deserves emphasis: backward error bounds are *theorems about algorithms*, and they compose. A backward-stable algorithm applied to a well-conditioned problem yields small forward error — Higham's "accuracy = stability × conditioning" decomposition [6]. Flocq's generic theorems make this compositional reasoning machine-checkable: stability lemmas proved over FLX transport to FLT and compose with separately proved conditioning bounds.

### 4.5 CompCert: verified compilation of floating-point programs

A proved-correct numerical kernel is only as trustworthy as the compiler that translates it. **CompCert** is a formally verified optimizing C compiler (for a large subset of ISO C90, targeting ARM, PowerPC, and x86/SSE2) with a machine-checked proof that compilation preserves program semantics [3]. Until 2013 the guarantee stopped at floating-point code, whose semantics was unformalized. Boldo, Jourdan, Leroy, and Melquiond closed the gap [3]:

1. **Extended Flocq** to a full IEEE-754 model: the FLT format was enriched with signed infinities, NaNs (with payload propagation semantics), and signed zeros, plus the complete special-value behavior of $+,-,\times,/, \sqrt{\cdot}$ and conversions.
2. **Specified** the semantics of C floating-point operations — including the subtle points: contraction (fused multiply-add) is *not* performed unless the source says so, excess precision on x87 is modeled by explicit rounding steps, and NaN payloads are treated nondeterministically but consistently.
3. **Proved** that every CompCert pass (all 20+ of them, from CminorSel to Mach to assembly printing) preserves these semantics, yielding *the first formally verified compiler that provably preserves the semantics of floating-point programs* [3].

The simulation proofs — showing target-language steps match source-language steps — were extended operation by operation, with IEEE special cases (e.g., $0 \times \infty = \mathrm{NaN}$, $\infty - \infty = \mathrm{NaN}$) handled explicitly.

**Limitations of the CompCert guarantee** (honestly stated in [3] and worth stressing):

It preserves *IEEE-754 semantics as specified* — but the guarantee is relative to the idealized model, not to hardware that deviates (flush-to-zero ARM modes, non-IEEE transcendental instructions). It covers `float` and `double` only (no 80-bit `long double`, no decimal formats), a *large subset* of C90 (no `volatile` FP access, no dynamic rounding-mode changes via `fenv.h`, no trap handlers), and it proves *semantic preservation*, not *numerical quality*: a program computing garbage with perfect IEEE fidelity compiles to a program computing the same garbage. These caveats define the guarantee's scope; they do not diminish it. The verified wave-equation solver (Section 5) demonstrates the intended workflow: prove the *numerics* with Flocq/Gappa, and let CompCert guarantee the *compiler* cannot break them.

## 5 Empirical Evaluation and Theoretical Results

### 5.1 What has actually been verified

The Flocq/CompCert/Gappa stack is not a proposal — it has verified real artifacts:

- **Error-free transformations:** TwoSum, Fast2Sum, Dekker's product, and double-double arithmetic are fully verified in Flocq, with exactness preconditions as machine-checked hypotheses [1][2]. These underpin compensated summation (Ogita–Rump–Oishi) and compensated dot products with superior, condition-number-independent error bounds.
- **The wave-equation solver:** Boldo et al. mechanized a full proof of a C program solving the 1D wave equation [1, citing hal-00649240]: convergence of the numerical scheme, absence of runtime errors, *and* rounding-error bounds — the full chain from mathematics to C, with C-level bounds from Gappa-style automation and compilation covered by CompCert [3].
- **Elementary functions:** correctly-rounded sine and exponential implementations certified with Gappa, as in [4]; the methodology generalizes to libm-style kernels.
- **Machine-checked test suites:** Flocq's executable operators are validated against 1055 binary64 square-root vectors from FPAccuracy inside Coq in seconds [2] — empirically anchored to hardware behavior.

### 5.2 Quantitative character of the results

Gappa-derived enclosures for elementary-function kernels routinely land within a small factor of the best hand-derived bounds [4][5]; the Coq certificates check in seconds to minutes. CompCert's FP extension added roughly 15k lines of Coq specification and proof — a one-time cost amortized over *every* compiled FP program [3].

Methodologically, Flocq demonstrated that a *single generic formalization* can serve program verification, high-level algorithm proofs, and automation simultaneously — the three communities that previously maintained incompatible models [1]. The prove-over-FLX, transport-to-FLT pattern is now standard proof engineering in verified numerics.

### 5.3 The standard model as interface

The *standard model* $\mathrm{fl}(a\,\mathrm{op}\,b) = (a\,\mathrm{op}\,b)(1+\delta)$, $|\delta| \le u$, now functions as a *verified interface* between communities: numerical analysts prove stability theorems assuming it [6]; Flocq proves it from IEEE-754 correct rounding [1]; CompCert guarantees the compiler preserves it [3]; Gappa automates its application [4]. The gap between "proved in the literature" and "proved in the machine" narrows to the formalization of the theorem statement itself.

## 6 Limitations and Open Problems

**Automation cliffs.** Gappa handles straight-line code with known ranges well, but loops need user-supplied invariants and branch-heavy code (table-driven argument reduction, special cases) still demands manual decomposition [7]. Push-button certification of a full libm remains distant.

**Backward error at scale.** The literature's backward-error theorems [6] are mostly *paper* proofs; machine-checked backward stability exists for kernels but not yet LAPACK-scale algorithms. The obstacle is proof-engineering scale, not theory.

**Transcendental functions.** Verified correctly-rounded elementary functions exist (CRLibm, Gappa-certified kernels [4]), but each is a bespoke multi-person-year effort. A *generic* verified framework — argument reduction, polynomial approximation (à la Sollya), rounding-error certification — is the field's grand challenge.

**Formats beyond binary64.** Emerging workloads use bfloat16, fp8, posits, and stochastic rounding — with different error characteristics (stochastic rounding's *unbiased* errors enable probabilistic analyses). Flocq's multi-radix design can express these, but the theorem base and automation are not there yet.

**The hardware gap.** CompCert's guarantee is relative to its IEEE-754 model [3]; hardware with flush-to-zero, non-standard transcendental instructions, or relaxed-FP accelerators (GPUs, TPUs) falls outside it. Verified numerics for accelerators — where most flops now happen — is largely open territory.

**Probabilistic and mixed-precision analysis.** Modern work uses probabilistic rounding models and mixed-precision algorithms (fp16/fp32/fp64 iterative refinement) that live outside the worst-case $(1+\delta)$ framework Flocq and Gappa mechanize; formalizing *probabilistic* error bounds in Coq is essentially untouched.

## 7 Conclusion

We have surveyed the machine-checked foundations of floating-point correctness: Flocq's generic multi-radix formalization [1][8]; rounding-operator theorems yielding Higham's standard model as a verified corollary [6]; CompCert's extension to full IEEE-754 with a compiler-correctness proof spanning every pass [3]; and Gappa's automated, certificate-producing bound propagation [4][5][7]. Together they form a *stack* — model, theory, compiler, automation — whose interfaces (generic format, standard model, Coq certificate) let results compose across layers and communities.

The practical payoff is real: verified error-free transformations, a mechanized wave-equation solver, certified elementary-function kernels, and a compiler that cannot silently break floating-point semantics. The open problems — automation for loops and branches, backward stability at LAPACK scale, certified transcendentals, low-precision and accelerator formats, probabilistic analyses — define a research program that is ambitious but well-posed, because the foundations are now solid.

The deeper lesson is methodological. Floating-point spent decades as folklore: every expert knew the standard model, yet every verification project rebuilt it. Flocq's generic format ended that duplication, and the stack built atop it shows what becomes possible when a community converges on shared, machine-checked foundations — *formalize once and reuse everywhere*.
