# Graph Neural Networks: Message Passing, the Weisfeiler–Leman Hierarchy, and the Oversmoothing Limit

## Abstract

Message-passing graph neural networks (MPNNs) have become the dominant architecture for learning on relational data, yet their fundamental capabilities and failure modes remained poorly understood for years. This thesis presents a unified theoretical account of three pillars of modern GNN theory: (i) the message-passing framework of Gilmer et al. as a learnable generalization of neighborhood aggregation; (ii) the exact characterization of MPNN discriminative power by the Weisfeiler–Leman (WL) graph isomorphism hierarchy, including the maximality of the Graph Isomorphism Network and the strict expressiveness ladder of higher-order *k*-GNNs; and (iii) the asymptotic limits of deep graph networks — oversmoothing, where repeated aggregation exponentially erases node information, and oversquashing, where exponentially growing receptive fields are compressed through fixed-size bottlenecks. We develop the core theorems with proofs sketches, survey the curvature-based rewiring and architectural mitigations, and present empirical evidence on synthetic and real benchmarks. Together these results delineate a precise frontier: MPNNs are *exactly* as powerful as the 1-WL test, and depth is a double-edged sword that simultaneously expands receptive fields and destroys discriminative signal.

## 1 Introduction

Graphs are the natural language of relational structure: molecules are atoms joined by bonds, proteins are residues in contact, social systems are agents in interaction. The past decade of geometric deep learning has converged on a single computational primitive for such data — *message passing* — in which each node iteratively aggregates representations from its neighbors and updates its own state [1]. Instantiations of this idea include the Graph Convolutional Network (GCN) [2], GraphSAGE, the Graph Attention Network (GAT), and the Graph Isomorphism Network (GIN) [3]. Their empirical success on node classification, link prediction, and molecular property prediction is undeniable; their theoretical foundations were, until recently, conspicuously thin.

Three questions have structured the theoretical literature:

1. **What can message passing compute?** Which graphs can an MPNN distinguish, and which functions can it approximate? The answer is given by the classical Weisfeiler–Leman (WL) hierarchy of graph isomorphism tests [3][4][5].
2. **What happens as networks get deep?** Practitioners observed early that stacking more than a handful of GNN layers *degrades* performance. Oono and Suzuki proved that this is not an optimization artifact: deep GCNs *exponentially* lose expressive power, converging to embeddings that encode only connected-component and degree information [6].
3. **Can distant nodes communicate?** Even shallow networks fail on tasks requiring long-range interaction, because messages from an exponentially growing receptive field are *oversquashed* through fixed-dimensional bottleneck edges [7]; discrete curvature quantifies exactly where [8].

This thesis develops these three threads into a coherent picture. Section 2 establishes the MPNN framework and the WL test. Section 3 proves the expressive-power equivalence between MPNNs and the 1-WL test and presents the GIN construction. Section 4 climbs the WL hierarchy to higher-order networks and then descends into the twin depth pathologies — oversmoothing and oversquashing — with their mitigations. Section 5 gives proof sketches and empirical validation, Section 6 states the limits of the theory, and Section 7 concludes.

> **Thesis claim:** *Message-passing GNNs are bounded above by the 1-dimensional Weisfeiler–Leman test in discriminative power, bounded below in depth by exponential information loss, and bounded laterally by curvature-induced communication bottlenecks. Architecture design is the art of navigating this three-sided cage.*

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The message-passing framework

Gilmer et al. unified the zoo of early graph networks into the message-passing neural network (MPNN) [1]. For a graph $G = (V, E)$ with node features $x_v$ and edge features $e_{vw}$, an MPNN computes for $T$ rounds:

$$m_v^{t+1} = \sum_{w \in \mathcal{N}(v)} M_t(h_v^t, h_w^t, e_{vw}), \qquad h_v^{t+1} = U_t(h_v^t, m_v^{t+1}),$$

with $h_v^0 = x_v$. Here $M_t$ is a learnable *message* function, $U_t$ a learnable *update* function, and a *readout* $R(\{h_v^T\})$ produces graph-level predictions. The framework is deliberately permissive: GCN, GraphSAGE, GAT, Gated Graph Neural Networks, and Interaction Networks are all special cases obtained by particular choices of $M_t$, $U_t$, and aggregation.

The canonical special case is the Graph Convolutional Network of Kipf and Welling [2]:

$$H^{(t+1)} = \sigma\left(\hat{D}^{-1/2}\hat{A}\hat{D}^{-1/2} H^{(t)} W^{(t)}\right),$$

where $\hat{A} = A + I$ adds self-loops and $\hat{D}$ is its degree matrix. The symmetric normalization is not cosmetic: it stabilizes the spectrum of the propagation operator and, as we shall see in §4.3, determines the *rate* at which deep GCNs forget.

### 2.2 The Weisfeiler–Leman test

The 1-dimensional Weisfeiler–Leman test (1-WL), also called *color refinement*, is a classical heuristic for graph isomorphism [4][5]. It assigns each node an initial color $c_v^{(0)}$ (from labels, or uniform) and iteratively refines:

$$c_v^{(t+1)} = \text{HASH}\left(c_v^{(t)},\ \{\{ c_w^{(t)} : w \in \mathcal{N}(v) \}\}\right),$$

where $\{\{\cdot\}\}$ denotes a multiset. The process stabilizes after at most $|V|$ iterations; two graphs whose final color histograms differ are non-isomorphic. The test is sound but incomplete: there exist non-isomorphic graphs — e.g., certain pairs of regular graphs such as the 6-cycle $C_6$ versus two disjoint triangles $2K_3$ — that 1-WL cannot distinguish.

The *k*-dimensional WL test generalizes this idea to $k$-tuples of vertices, producing a strict hierarchy: $(k+1)$-WL is strictly more powerful than $k$-WL [9]. Through the correspondence of Cai, Fürer, and Immerman, $k$-WL is equivalent in distinguishing power to the counting logic $C^{k+1}$ with $k+1$ variables — a deep bridge between combinatorics, logic, and learning.

| Architecture | Aggregation | Discriminative power | Reference |
|---|---|---|---|
| GCN | mean (normalized) | strictly below 1-WL | [2][3] |
| GraphSAGE (mean/max) | mean / max-pool | strictly below 1-WL | [3] |
| GAT | attention-weighted mean | strictly below 1-WL | [3] |
| GIN | sum + MLP (injective) | **equal to 1-WL** | [3] |
| $k$-GNN | $k$-tuple aggregation | equal to $k$-WL | [4] |
| $k$-IGN / PPGN | 2nd-order tensors | equal to 3-WL | [10] |
| Full graph transformer | global attention | above 1-WL (unbounded width) | — |

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 The expressiveness ceiling: MPNNs $\le$ 1-WL

The foundational result of Xu et al. and Morris et al. is a *ceiling theorem*: no message-passing architecture can distinguish graphs that the 1-WL test deems identical [3][4].

> **Theorem:** *Let $\mathcal{A}$ be any MPNN with arbitrary message, update, and readout functions. If the 1-WL test assigns two graphs $G_1, G_2$ the same color histogram, then $\mathcal{A}(G_1) = \mathcal{A}(G_2)$. In particular, the discriminative power of any MPNN is upper-bounded by 1-WL.*

*Proof sketch.* By induction on the message-passing rounds $t$: if two nodes receive the same 1-WL color at iteration $t$, then their multisets of neighbor colors at iteration $t-1$ coincide, and hence — by the induction hypothesis — their multisets of hidden states coincide. Any permutation-invariant aggregation of identical multisets yields identical messages, so the nodes' representations remain equal. The readout, being a function of the multiset of final representations, is therefore identical on both graphs. ∎

This is a statement about *architecture*, not parameters: it holds for every choice of weights, including attention mechanisms, because attention coefficients are themselves functions of representations that 1-WL already equates.

### 3.2 Achieving the ceiling: the Graph Isomorphism Network

Xu et al. showed the bound is *tight* [3]. The key observation is that 1-WL's refinement step is an *injective* map from (color, multiset-of-neighbor-colors) pairs to new colors. An MPNN matches 1-WL exactly when its aggregation and update are jointly injective on multisets:

> **Lemma:** *A sum aggregator followed by a multi-layer perceptron can represent any injective function on multisets of bounded size. Mean and max aggregators cannot: mean fails to distinguish multisets with identical distributions but different multiplicities; max fails to distinguish multiplicities entirely.*

The resulting architecture, the Graph Isomorphism Network (GIN), updates nodes as

$$h_v^{(t+1)} = \text{MLP}^{(t+1)}\left((1 + \epsilon^{(t+1)})\, h_v^{(t)} + \sum_{w \in \mathcal{N}(v)} h_w^{(t)}\right),$$

with a learnable scalar $\epsilon$. GIN is *provably* as powerful as 1-WL — the most expressive possible MPNN — and it exposed why popular models fall short: GCN's normalized mean aggregation and GraphSAGE's max-pooling are non-injective, so they conflate distinct neighborhood multisets that GIN (and 1-WL) separate.

### 3.3 Climbing the hierarchy: higher-order GNNs

Morris et al. lifted the entire analysis to $k$-tuples [4]: a $k$-GNN maintains representations for ordered $k$-sets of vertices and aggregates over neighborhoods defined by replacing one tuple element at a time. The same induction yields:

> **Theorem:** *The discriminative power of any $k$-GNN is upper-bounded by the $k$-WL test, and this bound is tight: there exist $k$-GNNs as powerful as $k$-WL.*

Maron et al. reached the same summit from invariant theory [10]: $k$-order invariant graph networks — equivariant tensor networks closed under permutation — match $k$-WL, and a *reduced* 2-order model interleaving MLPs with a single matrix multiplication already achieves 3-WL power, making it the first practical architecture strictly stronger than message passing. The price is computational: $k$-tuple representations scale as $O(n^k)$, so the hierarchy is a ladder whose upper rungs are exponentially expensive.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Oversmoothing: depth erases information

Empirically, GNN accuracy collapses beyond 2–4 layers. Oono and Suzuki proved this is a *representational* law, not a training failure [6]. Modeling a GCN's forward pass as a dynamical system, they showed that under mild conditions on the weight spectra, node embeddings converge exponentially fast to a subspace carrying no information beyond connected components and node degrees:

> **Theorem (Oono–Suzuki):** *Let $s$ be the maximum singular value of the layer weights and $\lambda = \max\{|1 - \mu| : \mu \neq 0 \text{ eigenvalue of the augmented normalized Laplacian}\} < 1$. Then the distance from the $L$-layer GCN embeddings to the uninformative invariant subspace decays as $O((s\lambda)^L)$. If $s\lambda < 1$, the network exponentially loses expressive power for node classification as $L \to \infty$.*

Intuitively, repeated multiplication by the normalized adjacency is repeated *diffusion*: it is the heat equation on the graph, and heat equilibrates. A complementary formulation uses the **Dirichlet energy** $E(X) = \mathrm{tr}(X^\top \tilde{\Delta} X)$, which measures embedding roughness: each GCN layer contracts it, $E(PX) \le (1-\bar{\lambda})^2 E(X)$ for the propagation operator $P$, while ReLU nonlinearities never increase it — so energy decays exponentially and embeddings become indistinguishable.

The practical upshot is a design rule: *weight normalization must keep $s$ small enough relative to the graph spectrum*, and architectures that work at depth (GCNII's residual connections, DropEdge's stochastic sparsification, PairNorm's centering) all function, in part, by arresting this energy decay.

### 4.2 Oversquashing: width of the receptive field vs. width of the wire

Oversmoothing concerns what depth *destroys*; oversquashing concerns what depth *cannot deliver* [7]. A node's $r$-hop receptive field grows exponentially in $r$ (as $d^r$ for degree $d$), yet all information from that field must pass through fixed-dimensional vectors along the paths to the node. Alon and Yahav demonstrated that standard MPNNs fail even to *fit* synthetic long-range tasks (e.g., identifying a marked leaf in a deep binary tree), and that GCN and GIN — which weight all incoming edges equally — suffer more than GAT or gated variants. Their remedy, adding a fully-adjacent final layer, rewires the graph to break the bottleneck and recovered state-of-the-art results without extra parameters.

Topping et al. gave this phenomenon a geometric cause [8]: edges of *negative* Ollivier–Ricci curvature are precisely the bottlenecks where exponentially many messages collide. Their **Stochastic Discrete Ricci Flow (SDRF)** rewiring surgically adds edges around negatively curved bottlenecks, provably improving the Cheeger constant that governs information flow. Curvature thus turns a vague "long-range problem" into a *local, measurable* quantity.

### 4.3 Mitigations: residuals, normalization, and rewiring

Three families of remedies have emerged:

1. **Architectural:** residual/dense connections (GCNII), jumping knowledge, and initial-residual propagation preserve high-frequency signal across layers, directly countering energy decay.
2. **Regularization:** DropEdge randomly removes edges per epoch, slowing both smoothing and the effective receptive-field explosion; PairNorm and differentiable group normalization re-center embeddings.
3. **Topological:** rewiring methods (SDRF, diffusion-based preprocessing, fully-adjacent layers) change the *graph on which messages flow*, attacking oversquashing at its structural root rather than its symptoms.

No single fix dominates: residuals help depth but not bottlenecks; rewiring helps bottlenecks but can dilute the inductive bias of locality. The modern consensus is that *depth, width, and topology must be co-designed*.

### 4.4 The logical view: what MPNNs can say

Through Cai–Fürer–Immerman, the WL hierarchy is the distinguishing-power hierarchy of finite-variable counting logics [9]. Hence every MPNN node classifier expresses a property definable in the 2-variable counting logic $C^2$ — no more. Properties requiring three variables (e.g., "the graph contains a triangle as a *distinguished* subgraph pattern" in certain encodings) are provably beyond message passing. This logical lens explains *why* the ceiling exists: aggregation is counting, and counting with two variables has hard limits.

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

**Synthetic WL discrimination.** Xu et al. trained GIN, GCN, and GraphSAGE to classify synthetic graphs designed so that 1-WL succeeds but mean/max aggregation fails [3]. GIN achieved near-perfect training accuracy where GCN and GraphSAGE plateaued — exactly as the injectivity lemma predicts. Morris et al. validated $k$-GNNs on molecular benchmarks (QM9, ZINC), showing that 2-WL-level models outperform 1-WL models on targets sensitive to higher-order structure [4].

**Depth collapse.** Oono and Suzuki measured node-classification accuracy of GCNs on citation graphs as depth grew from 2 to 128 layers: performance degraded monotonically, and the measured distance to the degree-and-component subspace shrank at the predicted exponential rate $(s\lambda)^L$ [6]. Their weight-scaling prescription recovered several accuracy points on real data.

**Bottleneck fitting.** Alon and Yahav's tree-leaf task: a 4-layer GCN/GIN failed to fit the training set (accuracy near chance), while the same model with a fully-adjacent rewired layer fit perfectly [7]. Topping et al. showed SDRF rewiring improved long-range molecular benchmarks (e.g., Peptides-func/struct from the Long Range Graph Benchmark) where plain MPNNs stagnate [8].

```python
# Minimal GIN layer (Xu et al., 2019): injective multiset aggregation
import torch, torch.nn as nn

class GINLayer(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, dim):
        super().__init__()
        self.eps = nn.Parameter(torch.zeros(1))
        self.mlp = nn.Sequential(
            nn.Linear(dim, dim), nn.ReLU(), nn.Linear(dim, dim))

    def forward(self, h, edge_index):
        # sum aggregation over neighbors: injective on multisets
        agg = torch.zeros_like(h).index_add_(0, edge_index[1], h[edge_index[0]])
        return self.mlp((1 + self.eps) * h + agg)
```

## 6 Limitations

The WL framework, for all its elegance, has sharp boundaries:

- **Continuous features break the clean picture.** The ceiling theorems assume countable/discrete color spaces; with continuous node features, universality results (Azizian & Lelarge; Keriven & Peyré) show MPNNs can approximate arbitrary invariant functions — but only with unbounded width or depth, and the WL analysis of *finite* models is subtler.
- **Expressiveness ≠ generalization.** A 3-WL-powerful model can memorize graph structure that a 1-WL model cannot, but on real molecular and social benchmarks, 1-WL-equivalent GIN often matches higher-order models: the extra power may not align with the task's inductive bias, and the $O(n^k)$ cost rarely pays.
- **Oversmoothing theory is GCN-specific in its sharpest form.** The $(s\lambda)^L$ rate is proved for linear-ish GCN dynamics; attention, gating, and heterophily-tolerant aggregators follow different dynamics, and recent work has questioned whether "oversmoothing" as strictly defined is even the operative failure mode in all deep GNNs.
- **Curvature rewiring is local and heuristic.** SDRF improves Cheeger-type bottlenecks but offers no guarantee on downstream task performance, and aggressive rewiring destroys the sparsity that makes GNNs scalable.
- **The hierarchy says nothing about optimization.** All WL results concern *representational* capacity — whether some weights exist — not whether gradient descent finds them.

## 7 Conclusion

A decade of theory has given message passing a precise job description. Gilmer et al.'s framework [1] showed that diverse architectures are one algorithm with different parameters. Xu et al. [3] and Morris et al. [4] proved that algorithm's discriminative ceiling is exactly the 1-WL test, and built GIN to touch it. The $k$-WL hierarchy [4][9][10] charts how far beyond message passing one must go — and what it costs. Oono and Suzuki [6] proved depth exacts an exponential tax on information, while Alon and Yahav [7] and Topping et al. [8] showed that long-range communication is throttled by curvature-induced bottlenecks, with rewiring, residuals, and normalization as the available countermeasures.

The practical moral is threefold. First, *choose aggregation for injectivity* when structure matters — sum over mean. Second, *treat depth as a budgeted resource*: every layer buys receptive field at the price of smoothing, so deep GNNs need residuals or normalization by construction, not as afterthoughts. Third, *measure your graph's bottlenecks*: if the task needs long-range interaction, no amount of expressive aggregation substitutes for rewiring the paths messages travel. The cage has three sides — WL power, smoothing, squashing — and the best architectures are those that press against all three at once.
