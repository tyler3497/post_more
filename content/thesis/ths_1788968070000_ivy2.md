# Mechanized Verification of Distributed Consensus: Decidable Reasoning from EPR to TLA+ and Ivy

## Abstract

Distributed consensus protocols such as Paxos and Raft underpin fault-tolerant storage, coordination services, and blockchains, yet their correctness arguments are notoriously subtle: a single interleaving missed by testing can violate agreement or liveness in production. This thesis presents a unified account of *mechanized* verification for consensus, spanning two complementary traditions. The first is **decidable reasoning**, exemplified by Ivy [1] and the EPR encoding of Paxos [2], which reduce parameterized safety to satisfiability in the Bernays–Schönfinkel fragment [7], discharging proof obligations fully automatically with SMT solvers. The second is **expressive specification**, exemplified by TLA+ [3] and its industrial deployment at Amazon Web Services [6], which trades decidability for modeling fidelity and catches design bugs before implementation. We develop the logical foundations of the effectively propositional (EPR) fragment, explain how protocol symmetry and modular decomposition keep verification conditions decidable, and compare the Ivy, TLA+/TLC, IronFleet [4], and Verdi [5] ecosystems on automation, guarantees, and scale. We argue that the decidable–expressive spectrum is not a dichotomy but a pipeline: TLA+ finds the bugs early, Ivy proves the survivors correct.

## 1. Introduction

Consensus is the *sine qua non* of reliable distributed computing. Any replicated state machine, from Spanner to etcd, rests on a protocol guaranteeing that non-faulty replicas agree on a total order of commands despite crashes, asynchrony, and message loss. The celebrated FLP impossibility result teaches that deterministic consensus is impossible in fully asynchronous systems with one crash failure, so practical protocols — Paxos, Raft, Multi-Paxos, and their descendants — achieve safety *unconditionally* while guaranteeing liveness only under partial synchrony assumptions.

Proving such protocols correct is famously difficult. Lamport's original Paxos paper is celebrated partly because the protocol it describes is *almost* right, and partly because the gap between "almost right" and "right" spans decades of subtle bugs: ballot-number reuse, uncommitted log truncation, and leader-election races that manifest once in millions of executions. Testing and model checking finite configurations catch many errors [6], but they cannot certify a protocol for *all* numbers of replicas, all ballot numbers, and all interleavings — the parameterized verification problem.

Two intellectual traditions have attacked this problem from opposite ends. The **expressive** tradition, led by TLA+ [3], gives engineers a precise, executable mathematics for describing concurrent systems and a model checker (TLC) that explores finite instances exhaustively. Amazon Web Services famously used TLA+ to find deep bugs in DynamoDB, S3, and EBS that years of testing had missed [6]. The **decidable** tradition, led by Ivy [1, 2], restricts the verification logic to fragments where satisfiability is decidable — notably the effectively propositional (EPR) fragment of first-order logic [7] — so that checking an inductive invariant for *arbitrarily many* replicas reduces to an SMT query that a solver answers "unsatisfiable" (safe) or provides a genuine counterexample.

This thesis makes three contributions. *First*, we give a self-contained exposition of the EPR fragment, its finite-model property, and why it is the right decidable core for consensus. *Second*, we explain the two key engineering ideas that make decidable verification practical: **stratified modular decomposition**, which splits a monolithic protocol into layers whose verification conditions stay within the decidable fragment, and **generalization from counterexamples**, which synthesizes inductive invariants automatically. *Third*, we compare the four major ecosystems — Ivy, TLA+/TLC, IronFleet [4], and Verdi [5] — along the axes of automation, guarantees, and engineering cost, and we articulate the pipeline view: specify in TLA+, then prove in Ivy.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 The consensus problem

We work with the standard crash-fault model: a fixed set of *acceptors* (replicas) communicate by message passing over an unreliable asynchronous network; any replica may crash silently. The protocol must satisfy **safety** — *agreement* (no two replicas decide different values), *validity* (a decided value was proposed), and *integrity* — and, under eventual synchrony, **liveness** (termination). Paxos achieves this through a two-phase discipline: *ballots* (totally ordered proposal numbers) impose a discipline on which proposals may be voted for, and a *quorum intersection* property ($\lvert Q_1 \cap Q_2 \rvert \ge 1$ for any two quorums) ensures that a value chosen in one ballot is visible to all later ballots.

The state space is parameterized: correctness must hold for any number $n$ of acceptors, any number of ballots, and any interleaving of sends, receives, and crashes. This is the *parameterized verification problem*, which is undecidable in general even for simple topologies — hence the need for carefully chosen decidable fragments.

### 2.2 TLA+ and finite-state model checking

TLA+ [3] is a formal specification language based on the Temporal Logic of Actions. A system is a state machine: an initial predicate $Init$ and a next-state relation $Next$, typically written as an action formula relating unprimed and primed variables. Safety properties are invariants; TLC, the explicit-state model checker, checks them on finite instances by enumerating reachable states.

```tla
---- MODULE Paxos ----
EXTENDS Integers, FiniteSets
CONSTANTS Acceptors, Quorums, Ballots, Values
VARIABLES msgs, accBallot, accVoted
Phase1b(a) == \E m \in msgs : m.type = "1a" /\ m.bal > accBallot[a]
    /\ msgs' = msgs \cup {[type |-> "1b", bal |-> m.bal,
                           mbal |-> accBallot[a], mval |-> accVoted[a][2],
                           from |-> a]}
    /\ UNCHANGED <<accBallot, accVoted>>
```

TLA+ is *expressive* — essentially full mathematics — which is both its power and its limitation: TLC cannot verify the parameterized system, only instances up to a few replicas and ballots. Yet this has proven enormously valuable industrially [6]: AWS engineers write TLA+ specifications of new features, model-check them, and fix the bugs TLC finds *before* writing production code.

### 2.3 The effectively propositional fragment

The Bernays–Schönfinkel class [7], also called the *effectively propositional* (EPR) fragment, consists of first-order sentences of the form $\exists^* \forall^*\, \varphi$ with $\varphi$ quantifier-free and *no function symbols* (constants are nullary functions and are allowed). EPR has the **finite-model property**: every satisfiable EPR sentence has a model whose universe size is bounded by the number of constants plus the number of existential variables. Consequently, satisfiability is decidable — indeed NEXPTIME-complete — and modern SMT solvers such as Z3 decide it efficiently in practice.

> **Theorem (Decidability of EPR).** Satisfiability of the Bernays–Schönfinkel class is decidable, and every satisfiable sentence has a model of size at most $c + e$, where $c$ is the number of constants and $e$ the number of existentially quantified variables [7].

For consensus, the connection is tantalizing: an inductive invariant $I$ with transition relation $T$ yields the verification condition $I \land T \Rightarrow I'$, i.e., the unsatisfiability of $I \land T \land \lnot I'$. If the protocol model uses only relations (no functions) and the formula is in the right quantifier shape, this is an EPR query — *decidable*, for *unboundedly many* replicas.

### 2.4 Inductive invariants and the verification condition

A predicate $I$ is an *inductive invariant* if (i) $Init \Rightarrow I$ and (ii) $I \land Next \Rightarrow I'$. Safety follows: every reachable state satisfies $I$, and $I \Rightarrow Safe$. The genius of the Ivy approach [1] is to check both conditions as EPR satisfiability queries and, when they fail, to extract from the solver's counterexample a *generalization* — a new conjunct that rules out not just the spurious state but a whole class of them — iterating until the invariant is inductive or a real bug is found.

## 3. Methodology

Our methodology has two strands: a *logical* strand that characterizes exactly which protocol models fall into EPR, and an *engineering* strand that describes how Ivy keeps them there.

### 3.1 Stratified relations

The central technical device is **stratification**. A first-order theory is *stratified* if its sorts can be ordered so that every function symbol $f : s_1 \times \dots \times s_n \to s$ satisfies $s \succ s_i$ for all $i$ — no "cycles" in sort dependencies. Padon et al. [2] showed that the verification conditions of a stratified protocol model lie in a decidable extension of EPR (FAU — the "finite almost uninterpreted" fragment). Concretely, Ivy programs are written with *relations* over *uninterpreted sorts*: `node`, `ballot`, `value`. Ballots need a total order, which Ivy handles through a special *totally ordered* sort with a decidable axiomatization.

The discipline is strict: no unbounded arithmetic, no recursive functions over protocol state, no quantification that escapes the $\exists^*\forall^*$ shape. When a protocol genuinely needs such features (e.g., integer counters in Raft's log indices), the modeler must either abstract them into relations or prove a *cutoff* — but for classic Paxos, the abstraction is exact.

### 3.2 Modular decomposition

Real protocols are too complex for one invariant. Ivy supports **modular decomposition** [2]: the system is split into layers (e.g., *ballot ordering*, *quorum intersection*, *voting*), each verified as an independent module against the abstract interfaces of the others. Each module's verification conditions are small enough to stay decidable and for the solver to discharge quickly. The composition theorem guarantees that the conjunction of module invariants is an invariant of the whole — a assume-guarantee discipline at the level of first-order theories.

### 3.3 Invariant synthesis by generalization

Writing inductive invariants by hand is the traditional bottleneck. Ivy automates it [1]: starting from the safety property, the tool queries Z3 for a counterexample to inductiveness, then *generalizes* the negated counterexample into candidate invariant conjuncts using a syntax-guided search over a grammar of formulas. The key insight is that the generalization is guided by the *vocabulary of the protocol* (its relations and their arities), which keeps candidates in the decidable fragment. On Paxos, this loop discovers the classic quorum-intersection invariant automatically.

```python
# Sketch: checking the inductive step in the EPR fragment with Z3
from z3 import *
node, ballot = DeclareSort('node'), DeclareSort('ballot')
voted = Function('voted', node, ballot, BoolSort())
chosen = Function('chosen', ballot, BoolSort())   # stratified: no cycles
I = ForAll([n, b], Implies(chosen(b), voted(n, b)))  # candidate invariant
T = ...                                            # transition relation (EPR)
query = And(I, T, Not(substitute(I)))              # I /\ T /\ ~I'
print(solver.check(query))  # unsat  =>  inductive step holds
```

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Paxos in EPR: the Padon et al. encoding

The landmark result is *Paxos Made EPR* [2]: the safety of Paxos, for any number of acceptors and ballots, reduces to EPR satisfiability. The encoding is a masterclass in modeling discipline. Nodes, ballots, and values are uninterpreted sorts. The protocol state is a handful of relations: `voted(n, b, v)` (node $n$ voted value $v$ in ballot $b$), `chosen(b, v)`, and the ballot order `le(b1, b2)`. Messages are modeled as *derived* relations — Ivy's actions update relations, and the network is an adversary that can deliver any subset of "sent" messages, which keeps the model finite and the quantifier shape clean.

The safety property is agreement: $\forall b_1, b_2, v_1, v_2.\, chosen(b_1, v_1) \land chosen(b_2, v_2) \Rightarrow v_1 = v_2$. The inductive invariant adds the quorum lemma: if a value is chosen at ballot $b$, then every quorum contains a node that voted for it at some ballot $\le b$, and no higher ballot can choose differently. All of this is $\forall^*$ over relations — squarely in EPR.

### 4.2 Why stratification is the crux

Without stratification, trouble arrives quickly. A seemingly innocent function — say, $maxballot(n)$ returning the highest ballot seen by node $n$ — introduces a function symbol whose *range* sort (ballot) also appears in its *domain*, creating a sort cycle. The resulting verification conditions escape EPR and may be undecidable. The Ivy methodology therefore *bans* such functions, replacing them with relations like $seen(n, b)$ plus axioms. This is not merely a technicality: it is the precise boundary between "the solver always terminates" and "the solver may loop forever." The paper [2] proves that the stratified fragment used by Ivy (FAU) is decidable via a reduction to EPR with a bounded sort ordering.

### 4.3 From safety to liveness

Safety is only half the story: a protocol that never decides is safe but useless. Liveness verification is harder because it involves *eventual* properties ($\Diamond\, decided$) and fairness assumptions about the network. Ivy was originally a safety tool [1], but the methodology extends: by modeling *rounds* explicitly and proving that each round makes progress under a fair scheduler, one can establish termination for the partially synchronous case. The key trick is to phrase liveness as a sequence of *safety-like* obligations — "if the leader of round $r$ is correct and the network is timely, then some node decides by round $r+1$" — each of which is again an EPR query. This decomposition does not capture every liveness argument (Byzantine settings remain out of reach), but it covers the practical core of crash-fault consensus.

### 4.4 TLA+ at scale: the AWS experience

Where Ivy proves, TLA+ *explores*. Newcombe et al. [6] report that AWS teams write TLA+ specifications for critical features of DynamoDB, S3, and EBS, then run TLC on instances with 3–4 nodes. The return on investment is striking: subtle bugs — a lost-update race in DynamoDB's replication, an inconsistency in EBS's volume management — were found *before* code was written, at a fraction of the cost of post-deployment fixes. TLA+'s advantage is *modeling freedom*: engineers write the algorithm as they understand it, with no stratification discipline, and TLC's explicit-state search finds bugs that decidable fragments cannot even express. The limitation is equally clear: TLC verifies instances, not the parameterized protocol, so a clean TLC run is evidence, not proof.

## 5. Empirical Analysis and Theoretical Guarantees

We compare the four ecosystems on a common yardstick: which protocols they have verified, what guarantees they provide, and what they cost the engineer.

| System | Logic / method | Guarantee | Automation | Notable target |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Ivy [1, 2] | EPR / FAU + SMT | Parameterized safety | Fully automatic invariant synthesis | Paxos, Raft, Chord |
| TLA+ / TLC [3, 6] | Unbounded FOL + explicit-state | Instance safety/liveness | Automatic (bounded) | DynamoDB, S3, EBS designs |
| IronFleet [4] | Dafny + Z3 (refinement) | Parameterized safety + liveness, *code-level* | Interactive (proof annotations) | Paxos-based replicated KV store |
| Verdi [5] | Coq (verified transformers) | Parameterized safety, extracted code | Interactive (Coq tactics) | Raft (verified implementation) |

> **Theorem (Paxos Made EPR [2]).** There exists a stratified first-order model of Paxos and an inductive invariant $I$ such that $Init \Rightarrow I$, $I \land Next \Rightarrow I'$, and $I \Rightarrow Agreement$ are all valid in FAU, hence decidable.

Empirically, Ivy verifies Paxos safety in seconds on a laptop, with the invariant synthesized automatically [2]; the TLA+ specification of the same protocol plus TLC on 3 acceptors finds injected bugs within minutes but cannot certify $n = 100$ [6]. IronFleet [4] goes further — it verifies *executable Dafny code* down to the network layer — but requires the engineer to write refinement proofs and ghost state by hand, an effort measured in person-months. Verdi [5] offers a middle path: verify a high-level Raft model in Coq, then apply *verified system transformers* (e.g., crash-recovery wrappers) that preserve the proof, extracting runnable OCaml.

The theoretical guarantee of the decidable approach is crisp: *termination of the verifier*. Because EPR satisfiability is decidable, Ivy's check of a *given* invariant always terminates; the synthesis loop may diverge in principle, but in practice the grammar-guided generalization converges on the protocols studied [1].

## 6. Limitations

The decidable approach has real boundaries, and intellectual honesty demands naming them. *First*, the stratification discipline is a genuine restriction: protocols whose correctness depends on unbounded arithmetic — Raft's log indices, vector clocks, or timeout counters — cannot be modeled exactly and must be abstracted, which can both hide bugs and introduce spurious counterexamples. *Second*, Byzantine faults are largely out of scope: the adversary's ability to equivocate arbitrarily breaks the clean relational models, and no decidable fragment yet captures BFT protocols like PBFT at full fidelity. *Third*, liveness remains second-class: the round-based decomposition of §4.3 handles crash-fault termination but not the subtle timing arguments of real leader election. *Fourth*, Ivy verifies *models*, not *code*: the gap between the Ivy program and the deployed implementation is bridged only by discipline, whereas IronFleet [4] and Verdi [5] verify the artifact that ships. Finally, the synthesis loop, while automatic, is a heuristic — there is no completeness theorem guaranteeing it finds an invariant when one exists in the grammar.

## 7. Conclusion

Mechanized verification of consensus has matured from research curiosity to engineering practice along two complementary paths. **TLA+** [3] brought formal methods to industry by meeting engineers where they are: write the design precisely, let the model checker hunt bugs, fix them before coding [6]. **Ivy** [1, 2] brought decidability to parameterized verification: restrict the logic to EPR [7], stratify the model, decompose the protocol, and let the solver prove safety for *all* configurations automatically. **IronFleet** [4] and **Verdi** [5] close the gap to executable code at the price of interactive proof effort.

The pipeline view we advocate is pragmatic: specify new protocols in TLA+ and model-check finite instances to eliminate design bugs cheaply; then, for the stable core — Paxos, Raft, and their variants — invest in an Ivy model that yields a machine-checked, parameterized safety proof. The decidable–expressive spectrum is not a choice between rivals but a division of labor, and the most reliable distributed systems of the next decade will be built by teams fluent in both.
