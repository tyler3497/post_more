# The GKR Protocol: Interactive Proofs for Bounded-Depth Computation and the Sum-Check Engine

## Abstract

The Goldwasser-Kalai-Rothblum (GKR) protocol converts a layered arithmetic circuit evaluation into an interactive proof whose verifier runs in nearly linear time while the prover remains polynomial-time efficient. At its core lies the sum-check protocol of Lund, Fortnow, Karloff, and Nisan, which reduces a claim about an exponentially large sum to a claim about a single polynomial evaluation. This thesis presents a complete account of the GKR construction: multilinear extensions of gate-value functions, low-degree wiring predicates that encode circuit topology, and the layer-reduction loop that walks a claim from the output layer back to the input layer through iterated sum-check invocations. We analyze the protocol's complexity — quasilinear verifier time, polylogarithmic communication, and polynomial prover time — and survey the engineering advances that made it practical: streaming verifiers that touch the input once in logarithmic space, linear-time provers for regular circuits, and GPU-parallelized implementations. Empirical results, soundness proofs, and the protocol's limitations are discussed in depth.

##1 Introduction

Delegating computation is one of the central problems of modern computing infrastructure. A weak client wishes to outsource a heavy computation to a powerful but *untrusted* server — the canonical cloud-computing scenario — and obtain a guarantee that the returned result is correct, without re-executing the computation itself [2]. Classical complexity theory answered this question in the 1980s and 1990s with spectacular results such as **IP = PSPACE**, but those protocols featured computationally *unbounded* provers and verifiers whose running time dwarfed the computation being verified: they were mathematical marvels with no path to deployment.

The 2008 work of Goldwasser, Kalai, and Rothblum, published in final form in 2015, changed this picture decisively [2]. They introduced *doubly-efficient* interactive proof systems: the honest prover runs in polynomial time — a mere "muggle" — while the verifier runs in nearly linear time. Their protocol, now universally called the **GKR protocol**, handles any language computable by a logspace-uniform family of bounded-depth arithmetic circuits — that is, the class **NC** — and generalizes naturally to circuits of moderate depth. The engine that powers the entire construction is the *sum-check protocol* of Lund, Fortnow, Karloff, and Nisan [1], a deceptively simple interactive procedure that reduces a claim about a sum over exponentially many terms to a claim about a single evaluation of a low-degree polynomial.

This thesis develops the GKR protocol from first principles. We begin with the algebraic machinery of interactive proofs, then dissect the protocol's three pillars: *multilinear extensions* that lift discrete gate values to the continuous algebraic domain, *wiring predicates* that encode circuit topology as low-degree polynomials, and the *layer-reduction loop* that propagates a claim about the circuit's output backward through the circuit, layer by layer, using sum-check as the reduction workhorse. We quantify the protocol's efficiency, trace the engineering lineage that brought it from theory to working code — streaming verifiers, near-linear-time provers, and GPU acceleration — and confront the genuine limitations that remain.

##2 Background

Interactive proof systems were introduced by Goldwasser, Micali, and Rackoff in the 1980s as a probabilistic generalization of the classical notion of proof: a computationally bounded verifier exchanges messages with an all-powerful prover and accepts or rejects with high probability. The landmark results of the era — Lund, Fortnow, Karloff, and Nisan's algebraic protocols for the polynomial hierarchy and Shamir's proof that **IP = PSPACE** — established a revolutionary *algebraization* technique: Boolean formulas are converted into low-degree polynomials over a finite field, and combinatorial claims about satisfiability become algebraic claims about polynomial identities [1].

The sum-check protocol is the crown jewel of this algebraic machinery. Suppose a verifier wishes to confirm the value of

$$H = \sum_{b_1 \in \{0,1\}} \cdots \sum_{b_v \in \{0,1\}} g(b_1, \ldots, b_v)$$

for a *v*-variate polynomial *g* of individual degree at most *d* over a finite field *F*. Direct evaluation requires summing $2^v$ terms. Instead, the prover sends the univariate polynomial $g_1(X_1) = \sum_{b_2,\ldots,b_v} g(X_1, b_2, \ldots, b_v)$, the verifier checks that $g_1(0) + g_1(1)$ equals the claimed sum, samples a random $r_1 \in F$, and the protocol recurses on the remaining variables. After *v* rounds, the verifier need only evaluate *g* at a single random point — a task that is often feasible even when the original sum is not [1].

Soundness rests on the **Schwartz–Zippel lemma**: a nonzero polynomial of total degree *D* over *F* vanishes at a random point with probability at most $D/|F|$. If the prover lies about any round polynomial, the verifier's random challenge exposes the lie with probability at least $1 - d/|F|$ per round, giving total soundness error at most $dv/|F|$ — negligible when the field is exponentially large [1]. The GKR protocol inherits this engine wholesale, applying sum-check not once but once per circuit layer, chaining the reductions together. Thaler's lecture notes give a compact modern exposition of both the sum-check protocol and the GKR construction built on it [7].

##3 Methodology

The GKR protocol operates on *layered arithmetic circuits*: the circuit has *d* layers of gates, layer 0 is the input layer, layer *d* is the output layer, and every gate is either an addition or a multiplication gate of fan-in two, reading its inputs exclusively from the immediately preceding layer. Let $S_i = 2^{s_i}$ be the number of gates at layer *i*, and $S = \sum_i S_i$ the circuit size. Each gate is labeled by a binary string in $\{0,1\}^{s_i}$.

For each layer *i*, define the **gate-value function** $V_i : \{0,1\}^{s_i} \to F$ mapping each gate label to the value computed at that gate on the given input. The prover's ultimate claim is about $V_d$ at the output label — but a claim about a *single point* is not yet in sum-check form. The methodology therefore proceeds in three algebraic steps, repeated per layer:

1. **Arithmetize the wiring.** For each layer *i*, the circuit's topology is captured by two *wiring predicates*, $\text{add}_i(g, b, c)$ and $\text{mul}_i(g, b, c)$, which equal 1 when gate *g* at layer *i* is respectively an addition or multiplication gate with children labeled *b* and *c* at layer $i-1$, and 0 otherwise. Because the circuit family is logspace-uniform, these predicates admit *low-degree extensions* $\widetilde{\text{add}}_i, \widetilde{\text{mul}}_i$ that the verifier can evaluate in polylogarithmic time [2].

2. **Lift values to multilinear extensions.** Each $V_i$ is extended to its unique multilinear extension $\widetilde{V}_i$ over $F^{s_i}$. 

3. **Reduce the layer claim by sum-check.** A claim $\widetilde{V}_i(z) = v$ is rewritten as a sum over the Boolean hypercube of dimension $3s_i$:

$$f_{i,z}(g, b, c) = \widetilde{\text{eq}}(g, z)\,\Big[\widetilde{\text{add}}_i(g,b,c)\big(\widetilde{V}_{i-1}(b) + \widetilde{V}_{i-1}(c)\big) + \widetilde{\text{mul}}_i(g,b,c)\,\widetilde{V}_{i-1}(b)\,\widetilde{V}_{i-1}(c)\Big]$$

where $\widetilde{\text{eq}}$ is the multilinear extension of the equality predicate. Summing $f_{i,z}$ over all Boolean $(g,b,c)$ yields exactly $\widetilde{V}_i(z)$. Applying sum-check to this sum reduces the layer-*i* claim to a claim about $\widetilde{V}_{i-1}$ at a *single random point* $r \in F^{s_{i-1}}$ [2].

A subtlety: the reduction actually produces *two* claims about layer $i-1$ (evaluations at two distinct points arising from the sum-check transcript). A **point-reduction** step draws a random line $\ell(t)$ through these points and reduces both claims to a single claim $\widetilde{V}_{i-1}(\ell(t^\*)) = v^\*$ at a random point on the line, again via a tiny sum-check-like argument. Iterating this loop from layer *d* down to layer 0 leaves a single claim about $\widetilde{V}_0$ — the multilinear extension of the *input* — which the verifier evaluates directly in a streaming pass [3].

> **Theorem:** Let *C* be a logspace-uniform layered arithmetic circuit of size *S* and depth *d* over a field *F*. There is a public-coin interactive proof for the claim that *C* evaluates to a given output in which the prover runs in time $\text{poly}(S)$, the verifier runs in time $O(n \log n + d \log S \cdot \text{polylog}(S))$ where *n* is the input length, communication is $O(d \log S)$, and the soundness error is at most $O(d \log S / |F|)$ [2].

##4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Sum-Check Protocol, Formally

We now examine the sum-check engine with the precision it deserves, following the presentation of Lund et al. [1]. Let $g : F^v \to F$ be a polynomial of degree at most *d* in each variable, and let the claimed sum be $H$. The protocol proceeds in *v* rounds:

1. **Round 1.** The prover sends $g_1(X_1) = \sum_{x_2,\ldots,x_v \in \{0,1\}} g(X_1, x_2, \ldots, x_v)$, a univariate polynomial of degree at most *d*. The verifier checks $g_1(0) + g_1(1) = H$ and rejects otherwise.
2. **Round $j$ (for $2 \le j \le v$).** The verifier samples $r_{j-1} \in F$ uniformly and sends it to the prover. The prover responds with $g_j(X_j) = \sum_{x_{j+1},\ldots,x_v \in \{0,1\}} g(r_1, \ldots, r_{j-1}, X_j, x_{j+1}, \ldots, x_v)$. The verifier checks $g_{j-1}(r_{j-1}) = g_j(0) + g_j(1)$ and rejects on mismatch.
3. **Final check.** The verifier samples $r_v \in F$, evaluates $g(r_1, \ldots, r_v)$ directly (using its oracle or unaided evaluation), and accepts iff $g_v(r_v)$ equals this value.

> **Theorem (Completeness and soundness of sum-check):** If $H$ is the true sum, the honest prover makes the verifier accept with probability 1. If $H$ is incorrect, any prover strategy makes the verifier accept with probability at most $dv/|F|$ [1].

> **Proof sketch:** Suppose the prover's first message $g_1$ differs from the true polynomial $h_1$. Both have degree at most *d*, so they agree on at most *d* points; the verifier's random $r_1$ avoids this agreement set with probability $1 - d/|F|$, in which case the reduced claim about the $(v-1)$-variate sum is already false. Induction over the rounds gives the bound. ∎

The communication cost is $O(dv)$ field elements and the verifier performs only $O(dv)$ field operations plus one evaluation of *g*. The following table summarizes one round's message flow:

| Round | Prover sends | Verifier checks | Verifier sends |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | $g_1(X_1)$, degree ≤ *d* | $g_1(0)+g_1(1) = H$ | $r_1 \in F$ |
| *j* | $g_j(X_j)$, degree ≤ *d* | $g_{j-1}(r_{j-1}) = g_j(0)+g_j(1)$ | $r_j \in F$ |
| final | — | $g_v(r_v) = g(r_1,\ldots,r_v)$ | accept/reject |

The example implementation below, in Python, captures the verifier's side of a single sum-check over a small field, illustrating how little work the verifier performs relative to the $2^v$-term sum:

```python
def sumcheck_verify_round(g_prev_at_r, coeffs, r_prev, claimed_H=None):
    """Verify one sum-check round message given as coefficients of g_j."""
    # coeffs: coefficients of univariate polynomial g_j sent by prover
    # check g_{j-1}(r_{j-1}) == g_j(0) + g_j(1)
    g_j_at_0 = coeffs[0]
    g_j_at_1 = sum(coeffs)          # sum of coefficients = eval at 1
    assert (g_j_at_0 + g_j_at_1) % P == g_prev_at_r % P, "round check failed"
    # sample challenge and bind variable
    r = secrets.randbelow(P)
    g_at_r = sum(c * pow(r, k, P) for k, c in enumerate(coeffs)) % P
    return r, g_at_r
```

### 4.2 Multilinear Extensions and Wiring Predicates

A function $V : \{0,1\}^m \to F$ has a *unique* multilinear extension $\widetilde{V} : F^m \to F$ — the polynomial of degree at most 1 in each variable agreeing with *V* on the Boolean hypercube. It admits the explicit Lagrange-style formula [3]:

$$\widetilde{V}(x_1,\ldots,x_m) = \sum_{b \in \{0,1\}^m} V(b)\,\chi_b(x), \qquad \chi_b(x) = \prod_{k=1}^{m} \big(b_k x_k + (1-b_k)(1-x_k)\big).$$

 A special case is the equality selector $\widetilde{\text{eq}}(x, y) = \prod_{k}(x_k y_k + (1-x_k)(1-y_k))$, which the layer-reduction polynomial uses to *select* the gate label *z* from the sum over all labels *g*.

The wiring predicates $\text{add}_i$ and $\text{mul}_i$ are Boolean functions of $3s_i$ variables. The protocol requires them to have *low-degree extensions* that the verifier can evaluate quickly. This is where **logspace uniformity** enters: the circuit family must be generated by a Turing machine using $O(\log S)$ space, which implies the wiring predicates have small formulas and hence low-degree polynomial extensions evaluable in $\text{polylog}(S)$ time [2]. Intuitively, the verifier cannot afford to read the entire circuit description — that would already cost $\Omega(S)$ — so the topology must be *implicitly* computable.

Key properties exploited by the analysis:

- *Multilinearity bounds the degree.* In the layer polynomial $f_{i,z}$, the term $\widetilde{V}_{i-1}(b)\,\widetilde{V}_{i-1}(c)$ has degree at most 2 in each variable of *b* and *c*; the wiring extensions contribute only polylogarithmic degree. Hence sum-check soundness per layer costs only $O(\log S / |F|)$.
- *Uniqueness pins down the prover.* If the prover claims a false layer value, the multilinear extension is a *specific* nonzero polynomial, and Schwartz–Zippel guarantees the verifier's random point exposes it.
- *Streaming evaluation of the input MLE.* At the end of the reduction, the verifier must compute $\widetilde{V}_0(\omega)$ for a random $\omega$. Cormode, Thaler, and Yi showed this can be done in $O(n \log n)$ time and $O(\log n)$ space with a *single streaming pass* over the input, using the $\chi_b$ expansion directly [3].

### 4.3 Layer Reduction and Point Combination

The heart of GKR is the iterative reduction. Suppose the verifier holds a claim $\widetilde{V}_i(z) = v$ for some layer *i* and point $z \in F^{s_i}$. The identity

$$\widetilde{V}_i(z) = \sum_{g,b,c \in \{0,1\}^{s_i}} f_{i,z}(g,b,c)$$

rewrites the claim as a sum over $3s_i$ Boolean variables, and one sum-check invocation of $3s_i$ rounds reduces it to the claim $f_{i,z}(r_g, r_b, r_c) = v'$ at a random point. Expanding $f_{i,z}$ at this point, the verifier can evaluate everything *except* the two values $\widetilde{V}_{i-1}(r_b)$ and $\widetilde{V}_{i-1}(r_c)$: the wiring extensions and $\widetilde{\text{eq}}$ are computable by the verifier alone. The prover is therefore asked to supply these two values, producing *two* claims about layer $i-1$ [2].

Two claims are one too many — left unchecked, the number of claims would double at each layer and reach $2^d$. The **point-reduction** (or *claim-combination*) step solves this: the verifier draws a random line $\ell : F \to F^{s_{i-1}}$ through $r_b$ and $r_c$, asks the prover for the univariate polynomial $h(t) = \widetilde{V}_{i-1}(\ell(t))$ restricted appropriately, and checks consistency at the two known points before binding *t* to a random $t^\*$. The two claims collapse into one: $\widetilde{V}_{i-1}(\ell(t^\*)) = v^\*$ [2].

The loop therefore maintains the invariant of *exactly one* outstanding claim as it descends from layer *d* to layer 0. The total communication is $O(d \log S)$ field elements and the round complexity is $O(d \log S)$.

##5 Empirical Results and Proofs

We now consolidate the quantitative evidence and give the core soundness argument.

**Benchmark results.** The following table collects representative measurements from the implementation lineage [4][5]:

| System | Benchmark | Prover overhead vs. native | Verifier time | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| CMT implementation [4] | $512 \times 512$ matrix multiplication | $\sim$1000× | seconds | First practical GKR code |

**Soundness of the full protocol.** We sketch the proof that a cheating prover is caught except with probability $O(d \log S / |F|)$ [2].

> **Theorem (GKR soundness):** If the circuit's true output differs from the claimed output, the verifier accepts with probability at most $O(d \log S / |F|)$.

> **Proof sketch:** Consider the first layer *i* (counting down from *d*) at which the prover's claimed value $\widetilde{V}_i(z) = v$ is false while all claims about higher layers were true. The layer polynomial $f_{i,z}$ summed over the Boolean cube then equals the *true* value, differing from the claimed *v*; the prover's first sum-check message must therefore deviate from the honest polynomial. By sum-check soundness [1], the verifier's random challenges catch this deviation except with probability $O(\log S / |F|)$ per layer. The point-reduction step is itself a degree-2 univariate check, adding $O(1/|F|)$ error. A union bound over *d* layers gives $O(d \log S / |F|)$. If the prover instead lies only at the input layer, the verifier's direct evaluation of $\widetilde{V}_0$ catches it with certainty. ∎

**Completeness** is immediate: the honest prover sends the true round polynomials at every step, all verifier checks pass by construction, and the final input-layer claim matches the streamed evaluation [2].

**The streaming-evaluation lemma** [3] deserves emphasis because it is the step most often taken on faith: the verifier's incremental computation of $\sum_b V_0(b)\chi_b(\omega^\*)$ maintains, for each of the $\log n$ variables, the partial products of $\chi_b(\omega^\*)$, updating them in $O(\log n)$ time per input symbol. 

##6 Limitations

Despite its elegance, the GKR protocol faces genuine constraints that circumscribe its applicability:

- **Circuit model rigidity.** The protocol speaks only the language of layered arithmetic circuits over a finite field. Computations rich in comparisons, branching, or bitwise operations must first be *arithmetized*, often at significant blowup in circuit size. Irregular control flow is the enemy of the wiring-predicate machinery.
- **Uniformity and wiring costs.** The verifier's quasilinear runtime depends on evaluating the wiring-predicate extensions $\widetilde{\text{add}}_i, \widetilde{\text{mul}}_i$ in polylogarithmic time. For circuits with irregular wiring, this evaluation can dominate the verifier's work, eroding the asymptotic advantage [2][4].
- **Field-size requirements.** Soundness error scales as $O(d \log S / |F|)$, so the field must be exponentially large in the security parameter. For 128-bit security with deep circuits, this forces large-characteristic fields with expensive arithmetic, and the protocol offers no graceful degradation for small fields.
- **Round complexity and interactivity.** The protocol requires $O(d \log S)$ rounds of interaction. For deep circuits this means thousands of round trips — acceptable for a co-located client and server, but punishing over high-latency networks. Fiat–Shamir-style non-interactive compilation exists only in the random-oracle model or under cryptographic assumptions, departing from the protocol's information-theoretic purity.
- **Prover overhead persists.** Even the best implementations leave the prover 100–500× slower than native evaluation for general circuits [5]. For computations where the server is already saturated, this tax can exceed the value of the proof.
- **No zero-knowledge by default.** The transcript leaks the intermediate round polynomials, which can reveal information about the input. Zero-knowledge variants exist but add substantial cost.
- **Competition from succinct arguments.** Modern SNARKs achieve *constant* proof size and verification time under cryptographic assumptions, whereas GKR's communication grows with depth. 

##7 Conclusion

The GKR protocol stands as the definitive answer to a question that haunted complexity theory for two decades: can the spectacular power of interactive proofs be made available to *realistic* parties — a polynomial-time prover and a nearly-linear-time verifier? By wedding the sum-check engine of Lund, Fortnow, Karloff, and Nisan [1] to a careful arithmetization of layered circuits, Goldwasser, Kalai, and Rothblum showed that the answer is yes for all of logspace-uniform **NC**, with information-theoretic soundness and no cryptographic assumptions [2].

The subsequent engineering story — streaming verifiers that touch the input once [3], linear-time provers for regular circuits [4], GPU acceleration [5], and hybrid architectures [6] — transformed a theoretical breakthrough into running code with measured, published performance. The protocol's intellectual legacy extends further: the sum-check-plus-multilinear-extension pattern now underlies the most efficient modern argument systems, and the doubly-efficient paradigm reshaped how the field thinks about the prover–verifier asymmetry.

Open frontiers remain: taming irregular wiring without verifier blowup, reducing round complexity, and closing the prover-overhead gap for general circuits. But the core lesson endures — *algebraization*, the same technique that proved **IP = PSPACE**, is also the technique that brought interactive proofs down from the complexity-theoretic heavens into the machine room.
