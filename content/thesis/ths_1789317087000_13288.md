# Stochastic Gradient Langevin Dynamics: Posterior Sampling, Convergence Theory, and Bayesian Deep Learning

## Abstract

Stochastic Gradient Langevin Dynamics (SGLD) sits at the intersection of Bayesian inference, stochastic optimization, and the theory of diffusion processes. Introduced by Welling and Teh in 2011 [1], SGLD augments stochastic gradient descent with calibrated Gaussian noise so that the iterates of a first-order optimizer become, asymptotically, draws from a posterior distribution. The method replaces the full-data gradient with a minibatch estimate, cutting per-iteration cost, while step-size-calibrated noise recovers the Euler–Maruyama discretization of the overdamped Langevin SDE in the small-step limit. This article develops SGLD from first principles: the Langevin diffusion and its invariant measure, the two canonical step-size regimes (decaying schedules that are asymptotically exact, fixed step sizes that trade controllable bias for finite-time practicality), nonasymptotic convergence guarantees in Wasserstein and total-variation metrics, the stochastic-gradient variance decomposition, and the modern role of SGLD as a scalable approximate inference engine for Bayesian neural networks. We contrast SGLD with full-gradient Langevin Monte Carlo, discuss the complete-recipe framework for stochastic gradient MCMC [5], and examine where theory and practice still diverge — most notably the cold-posterior phenomenon of deep Bayesian networks.

## 1 Introduction

*The central tension of modern Bayesian computation is scale.* Markov chain Monte Carlo has been the gold standard for posterior inference for decades, yet its classical formulations demand a full pass over the dataset at every iteration. For a likelihood that factorizes over *N* independent observations, each evaluation of the log-posterior gradient costs *O(N d)*, where *d* is the parameter dimension. When *N* reaches the millions — as it does in web-scale classification, recommendation, and language modeling — a full-gradient sampler is dead on arrival.

Two communities arrived at the same resolution from opposite directions. Optimizers, comfortable with minibatches, asked whether the noise in stochastic gradients could be *harnessed* rather than merely tolerated. Samplers, comfortable with diffusions, asked whether the Langevin equation — the canonical continuous-time dynamics whose stationary distribution is a target posterior — could survive the indignity of a stochastic gradient. The answer to both questions is **stochastic gradient Langevin dynamics**, introduced by Max Welling and Yee Whye Teh [1]:

$$\theta_{k+1} = \theta_k - \varepsilon_k\,\hat{g}_k + \sqrt{2\varepsilon_k}\,\xi_k,$$

where $\hat{g}_k$ is an unbiased minibatch estimate of the gradient of the negative log posterior, $\varepsilon_k$ is the step size, and $\xi_k$ is standard Gaussian noise. Compared with stochastic gradient descent, the *only* change is the injected noise term $\sqrt{2\varepsilon_k}\,\xi_k$. Compared with gradient Langevin Monte Carlo, the only change is that the full gradient is replaced by a stochastic estimate.

This modest modification buys something remarkable. With a step-size schedule satisfying the classical Robbins–Monro conditions — $\sum_k \varepsilon_k = \infty$ and $\sum_k \varepsilon_k^2 < \infty$ — the chain converges *weakly* to the posterior: after the step size has decayed sufficiently, the injected noise dominates the stochastic-gradient noise, the chain behaves like the exact Euler discretization of the Langevin diffusion, and test-function expectations converge to posterior expectations [1][6]. In practice, a fixed small step size is often preferred, at the cost of an explicit, analyzable asymptotic bias [4].

> **Theorem.** *(Informal convergence sketch.)* Under standard Lipschitz, dissipativity, and moment conditions on the negative log posterior $U$, the law of the SGLD iterates converges in the 2-Wasserstein metric to the target $\pi \propto \exp(-U)$ as the step size decays to zero and the iteration count tends to infinity; for fixed step size $\varepsilon$, the chain converges geometrically to a biased stationary law whose distance to $\pi$ is $O(\varepsilon)$ plus terms driven by the stochastic-gradient variance [2][3][4].

This article is organized as follows. Section 2 develops the background: the Langevin SDE, its invariant measure, and the Fokker–Planck machinery. Section 3 presents the methodology of SGLD itself — the update, the step-size regimes, and the bias–variance decomposition. Section 4 dives into the mathematical machinery: Girsanov-style couplings, transportation-cost inequalities, and the complete-recipe generalization to stochastic-gradient MCMC. Section 5 reviews empirical results and the current state of proof techniques. Section 6 catalogues limitations, and Section 7 concludes.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The Overdamped Langevin Diffusion

Consider a target distribution $\pi$ on $\mathbb{R}^d$ with density proportional to $\exp(-U(\theta))$, where the *potential* $U : \mathbb{R}^d \to \mathbb{R}$ plays the role of a negative log posterior: $U(\theta) = -\sum_{i=1}^N \log p(x_i \mid \theta) - \log p(\theta)$. The **overdamped Langevin stochastic differential equation** is

$$d\theta_t = -\nabla U(\theta_t)\,dt + \sqrt{2}\,dB_t,$$

with $(B_t)$ a standard $d$-dimensional Brownian motion. This equation admits a clean physical interpretation: the parameter $\theta_t$ is a particle drifting downhill along the force field $-\nabla U$ while being buffeted by thermal noise of strength $\sqrt{2}$.

Under mild regularity — say, $\nabla U$ globally Lipschitz and $U$ satisfying a dissipativity (confining) condition — $\pi$ is the *unique invariant distribution* of this diffusion, and convergence of the law of $\theta_t$ to $\pi$ is exponentially fast when $U$ is strongly convex (theBakry–Émery criterion), or still quantitative under weaker curvature assumptions via functional inequalities such as the log-Sobolev or Poincaré inequalities [3].

The evolution of densities under the Langevin SDE is governed by the **Fokker–Planck equation**

$$\partial_t \rho_t = \nabla \cdot \left(\rho_t \nabla U\right) + \Delta \rho_t,$$

whose stationary solution is $\rho_\infty \propto \exp(-U)$. This partial-differential-equation viewpoint is the bridge between sampling and optimization: gradient flow in the *Wasserstein* geometry of probability measures is exactly the Langevin dynamics, and the posterior is the unique minimizer of the free energy combining expected potential and entropy.

### 2.2 Euler–Maruyama Discretization: Langevin Monte Carlo

Computers cannot integrate diffusions in continuous time. The Euler–Maruyama discretization with step size $\varepsilon$ yields the **unadjusted Langevin algorithm** (ULA), also called Langevin Monte Carlo (LMC):

$$\theta_{k+1} = \theta_k - \varepsilon\,\nabla U(\theta_k) + \sqrt{2\varepsilon}\,Z_{k+1}, \qquad Z_{k+1} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I_d).$$

The discretization is *unadjusted*: with no Metropolis–Hastings correction, the chain targets a biased approximation $\pi_\varepsilon$ with $W_2(\pi_\varepsilon, \pi) = O(\sqrt{\varepsilon})$ under strong convexity, or $O(\varepsilon)$ under smoothness [3]. The adjusted variant (MALA) is exact but needs full-data likelihood evaluations for the acceptance probability — precisely the cost SGLD seeks to avoid.

### 2.3 Stochastic Gradients and the Minibatch Oracle

In the Bayesian setting the potential decomposes as $U(\theta) = U_0(\theta) + \sum_{i=1}^N U_i(\theta)$, with $U_0 = -\log p(\theta)$ the prior term and $U_i = -\log p(x_i \mid \theta)$ the per-datum terms. A minibatch $S_k \subset \{1,\dots,N\}$ of size $n$ gives the unbiased gradient estimator

$$\hat{g}_k = \nabla U_0(\theta_k) + \frac{N}{n}\sum_{i \in S_k} \nabla U_i(\theta_k), \qquad \mathbb{E}[\hat{g}_k \mid \theta_k] = \nabla U(\theta_k).$$

The estimator's conditional covariance, $\Sigma(\theta) = \mathrm{Cov}(\hat{g}_k \mid \theta_k)$, scales inversely with the minibatch size $n$ and vanishes in the full-batch limit. Substituting $\hat{g}_k$ for $\nabla U(\theta_k)$ in the Euler scheme and adding the calibrated noise gives the SGLD recursion. The key analytical insight of Welling and Teh is a *separation of noise scales*: as $\varepsilon_k \to 0$, the injected noise is of order $\sqrt{\varepsilon_k}$ while the stochastic-gradient error is of order $\varepsilon_k$, so the discretization asymptotically behaves like the exact Langevin diffusion [1].

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 The SGLD Update and Its Two Regimes

The practical behavior of SGLD is governed by the step-size schedule, and two regimes are standard.

| Regime | Schedule | Per-iteration cost | Asymptotic behavior |
|---|---|---|---|
| Decaying step size | $\sum_k \varepsilon_k = \infty$, $\sum_k \varepsilon_k^2 < \infty$ | $O(n d)$ | Weakly consistent: test averages converge to posterior expectations [1][6] |
| Fixed step size | $\varepsilon_k \equiv \varepsilon$ | $O(n d)$ | Geometric convergence to a biased law $\pi_{\varepsilon}$ with $O(\varepsilon)$ bias [4] |

- **Decaying schedule.** The classical choice $\varepsilon_k \propto (b + k)^{-\gamma}$ with $\gamma$ strictly between one half and one satisfies both Robbins–Monro conditions. Teh, Thiery, and Vollmer [6] established consistency and a central limit theorem for this regime, and showed the asymptotically optimal decay is $\varepsilon_k \asymp k^{-1/3}$.
- **Fixed step size.** In practice SGLD is run for a finite budget — thousands to tens of thousands of iterations — with a constant $\varepsilon$. The chain then targets a biased invariant law. Vollmer, Zygalakis, and Teh [4] characterize this bias explicitly as a function of $\varepsilon$ and the stochastic-gradient covariance, and derive a *modified* SGLD whose first-order bias correction removes the leading effect of minibatch noise.

A common practical heuristic bridges the two: run a few epochs with a fixed step size for exploration, then decay $\varepsilon_k$ during a final sampling phase. The finite-time analysis in Section 4.3 quantifies the tradeoff precisely.

### 3.2 The Bias–Variance–Mixing Decomposition

For a test function $f$, the mean squared error of the SGLD estimator $\hat{\mu}_K = \frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^K f(\theta_k)$ decomposes as

$$\mathrm{MSE} = \underbrace{\big(\mathbb{E}[\hat{\mu}_K] - \pi(f)\big)^2}_{\text{squared bias}} + \underbrace{\mathrm{Var}(\hat{\mu}_K)}_{\text{variance}}.$$

The bias itself has three sources:

1. **Discretization bias**, of order $\varepsilon$ for the Euler–Maruyama scheme, inherited from plain Langevin Monte Carlo [3].
2. **Stochastic-gradient bias**, arising from the interaction between the minibatch noise and the nonlinearity of $\nabla U$ — a second-order effect of order $\varepsilon\,\mathrm{tr}\,\Sigma(\theta)$, which the modified SGLD of [4] corrects.
3. **Initialization (burn-in) bias**, which decays geometrically with the mixing rate of the chain and can be truncated by discarding early iterates.

The variance term reflects the autocorrelations of the chain and scales roughly as $\tau_{\mathrm{mix}} / K$, where $\tau_{\mathrm{mix}}$ is the integrated autocorrelation time. Optimizing the total MSE over $\varepsilon$ for a fixed budget yields the now-classical $O(K^{-2/3})$ rate under decaying schedules [4][6].

### 3.3 Reference Implementation

The following Python sketch implements SGLD for Bayesian logistic regression, illustrating the gradient estimator, the injected noise, and a polynomially decaying schedule:

```python
import numpy as np

def sgld_logistic_regression(X, y, n_epochs, batch_size, eps0=1e-2, gamma=0.75):
    N, d = X.shape
    theta = np.zeros(d)
    eps = eps0
    for epoch in range(n_epochs):
        perm = np.random.permutation(N)
        for start in range(0, N, batch_size):
            idx = perm[start:start + batch_size]
            scale = N / len(idx)
            logits = X[idx] @ theta
            probs = 1.0 / (1.0 + np.exp(-logits))
            grad = scale * X[idx].T @ (probs - y[idx]) + theta  # N(0, I) prior
            eps = eps0 / (1.0 + epoch) ** gamma
            noise = np.sqrt(2.0 * eps) * np.random.randn(d)
            theta = theta - eps * grad + noise
    return theta
```

Two details deserve emphasis. First, the noise standard deviation is $\sqrt{2\varepsilon}$, *not* $\sqrt{\varepsilon}$ or an arbitrary constant: this exact calibration is what makes the discretization converge to the Langevin diffusion rather than to some other stochastic process. Second, the prior contribution $\nabla U_0$ must *not* be scaled by $N/n$; only the likelihood terms are subsampled.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 From Discrete Iterates to Continuous Diffusions: The Coupling Argument

The rigorous analysis of SGLD couples the discrete-time Markov chain to the continuous-time Langevin diffusion started from the same point. The modern technique — pioneered for ULA by Dalalyan and refined for SGLD by Raginsky, Rakhlin, and Telgarsky [2] — constructs a continuous-time interpolation of the chain and compares its law with that of the diffusion via a Girsanov change of measure, whose log-variance is controlled by the stochastic-gradient error. Weighted transportation-cost inequalities then propagate the contraction of the exact diffusion to the discrete chain [2][3].

> **Theorem.** *(Raginsky–Rakhlin–Telgarsky, nonasymptotic bound [2].)* Assume $\nabla U$ is $M$-Lipschitz, $U$ is $(m, b)$-dissipative, and the stochastic-gradient oracle has bounded variance $\sigma^2$. Then for the SGLD chain with fixed step size $\varepsilon$ below a stability threshold, the 2-Wasserstein distance between the $k$-th iterate's law $\mu_k$ and the posterior $\pi$ satisfies
>
> $$W_2(\mu_k, \pi) \le (1 - m\varepsilon)^k W_2(\mu_0, \pi) + C\,\Big(\varepsilon + \frac{\sigma^2 \varepsilon}{m}\Big)^{1/2},$$
>
> where $C$ depends polynomially on $M$, $m$, $b$, and the dimension $d$. The first term is the geometric forgetting of initialization; the second is the stationary bias, decomposing into discretization and minibatch contributions.

The power of this result is its *nonasymptotic* character: it holds for every finite $k$, not merely in the limit. This is what licenses the claim that SGLD is a legitimate approximate sampler rather than merely an asymptotically exact one.

### 4.2 Weighted Transportation-Cost Inequalities and Non-Convex Landscapes

Classical Langevin theory leans heavily on strong convexity — a global curvature assumption that neural-network posteriors spectacularly violate. Raginsky, Rakhlin, and Telgarsky's contribution [2] is to extend the analysis to **dissipative non-convex** potentials: $U$ need only satisfy $\langle \nabla U(x), x \rangle \ge m\|x\|^2 - b$ for some $m > 0$ and $b \ge 0$. This condition is far weaker — it merely asks that the gradient point inward outside a large ball, which holds for neural networks with weight decay, since the quadratic prior dominates the bounded gradient of the likelihood at infinity.

The technical engine is the *weighted transportation-cost inequality*: a generalization of the Talagrand inequality relating $W_2$ distance to a weighted relative entropy. Under dissipativity, one obtains contraction of a Lyapunov-weighted divergence along the diffusion semigroup — exponential convergence to stationarity *without* convexity. For SGLD, the injected noise is both the source of the correct invariant measure and the mechanism by which the chain escapes shallow local minima, mirroring simulated annealing [2].

### 4.3 Fixed-Step-Size Analysis: Explicit Bias and the Modified SGLD

When the step size is held fixed — the practically dominant regime — Vollmer, Zygalakis, and Teh [4] deliver the sharpest available picture. Working with the generator (infinitesimal operator) of the chain, they expand the invariant-measure bias in powers of $\varepsilon$ via a Poisson-equation argument. The headline results:

- The asymptotic bias for posterior expectations of smooth test functions admits the expansion $\pi_\varepsilon(f) - \pi(f) = \varepsilon\,c_1(f) + O(\varepsilon^2)$, where the coefficient $c_1$ splits additively into a discretization part and a part proportional to $\mathrm{tr}\,\Sigma$, the trace of the stochastic-gradient covariance.
- Consequently, **reducing the minibatch size inflates the asymptotic bias linearly in the gradient variance** — the price of cheap iterations is quantified.
- A *modified* SGLD subtracts an estimate of the leading bias term, achieving $O(\varepsilon^2)$ bias at the cost of one additional gradient-variance estimate per iteration.

Their finite-time bounds further give the MSE of the chain average as $O(\varepsilon^2 + 1/(K\varepsilon))$, reproducing the $K^{-2/3}$ optimal rate and revealing that the optimal fixed step size for a budget of $K$ iterations scales as $K^{-1/3}$ [4].

### 4.4 The Complete Recipe: SGLD Inside the SG-MCMC Zoo

SGLD is the simplest member of a broad family of **stochastic gradient MCMC** (SG-MCMC) samplers. Ma, Chen, and Fox [5] provided the unifying framework: any continuous Markov process with the correct invariant distribution can be specified by a diffusion matrix $D(\theta)$ and a skew-symmetric curl matrix $Q(\theta)$, via a drift of the form $-[D + Q]\nabla H$ plus noise $\sqrt{2D}\,dB_t$, where $H = -\log \pi$. Choosing $D = I$ and $Q = 0$ recovers the Langevin diffusion underlying SGLD; nonzero $Q$ yields stochastic gradient Hamiltonian Monte Carlo (SGHMC) [7], which adds momentum and mixes faster on ill-conditioned targets. The recipe also prescribes how to correct the dynamics for a noisy gradient estimate: the extra noise enters the diffusion matrix, with friction adjusted accordingly.

## 5 Empirical Results / Proofs

### 5.1 Canonical Experiments

The original Welling–Teh paper [1] validated SGLD on Bayesian logistic regression and independent component analysis, showing that SGLD with a decaying schedule matched full-data Langevin and Hamiltonian Monte Carlo while touching only a fraction of the data per iteration. The empirical signature of correctness is the *posterior predictive check*: for a Bayesian neural network, the SGLD ensemble's averaged predictions yield both improved accuracy over point estimates and calibrated uncertainty — wider predictive intervals precisely where training data are sparse [1].

### 5.2 Bayesian Deep Learning: Promises and the Cold-Posterior Puzzle

The most ambitious application of SGLD is **Bayesian deep learning**: sampling the posterior over the millions of weights of a neural network. Cyclical step-size schedules — alternating large steps for exploration with small steps for sampling — were introduced to cope with the multimodality of deep posteriors [2][7], and ensembles collected across cycles approximate a multimodal posterior far better than any single chain.

Yet a striking empirical phenomenon complicates the story: the **cold-posterior effect**. Tempering the posterior as $p(\theta \mid \mathcal{D})^{1/T}$ with $T < 1$ — sharpening it beyond what Bayes' rule prescribes — often *improves* predictive performance. This suggests either that standard priors are misspecified for deep networks, that the likelihood is miscalibrated under data augmentation, or that SGLD's approximate sampling interacts with the posterior geometry in ways the asymptotic theory does not capture. Resolving the cold-posterior puzzle remains one of the central open problems at the boundary of SGLD theory and practice.

### 5.3 Proof Techniques at a Glance

The convergence proofs for SGLD rest on four pillars, each addressing one source of error:

1. **Log-Sobolev and Poincaré inequalities** for the continuous diffusion, giving exponential contraction toward $\pi$ [3].
2. **Girsanov's theorem** for comparing the interpolated discrete chain with the diffusion, bounding the Radon–Nikodym derivative by the stochastic-gradient variance [2].
3. **Poisson-equation expansions** of the Markov-chain generator for fixed-step-size bias, yielding the $O(\varepsilon)$ expansion and the modified SGLD correction [4].
4. **Lyapunov (drift) conditions** — typically $V(\theta) = 1 + \|\theta\|^2$ — for controlling moments uniformly in time and establishing geometric ergodicity without global convexity [2][6].

## 6 Limitations

No honest treatment of SGLD can omit its weaknesses, which are substantial and well documented.

- **The bias–cost tradeoff is fundamental.** Fixed-step-size SGLD is biased, and the bias grows with the stochastic-gradient variance; decaying schedules are asymptotically exact but mix slowly in the small-step regime. There is no free choice of schedule that is simultaneously fast, exact, and cheap [4].
- **Multimodality defeats single chains.** The exponential mixing guarantees require curvature conditions that deep posteriors violate. In practice a single SGLD chain explores one mode; credible multimodal inference requires cyclical schedules, parallel tempering, or multiple chains, multiplying the cost [2].
- **Hyperparameter sensitivity.** The step size, decay schedule, minibatch size, and burn-in length all materially affect the invariant law being sampled. Unlike optimization, where a bad learning rate merely slows convergence, a bad SGLD step size samples the *wrong distribution* [4].
- **The cold-posterior phenomenon** casts doubt on whether the exact Bayesian posterior is even the right target for deep networks, undermining the normative force of the entire enterprise [1].
- **Dimension dependence.** The nonasymptotic bounds scale polynomially — sometimes worse — with dimension $d$, and the constants are rarely computable, limiting their prescriptive value for real models [2][3].
- **Gradient-noise assumptions.** The theory typically requires bounded or sub-Gaussian gradient noise uniformly in $\theta$, which fails for heavy-tailed stochastic gradients encountered with small minibatches on heterogeneous data.

## 7 Conclusion

Stochastic Gradient Langevin Dynamics is one of those rare ideas that is simultaneously a practical algorithm, a theoretical object of genuine depth, and a conceptual bridge between fields. As an algorithm, it turns any stochastic-gradient optimizer into an approximate Bayesian sampler at the cost of one line of code — the injected noise. As a theoretical object, it has driven advances in nonasymptotic Markov-chain analysis, from weighted transportation-cost inequalities [2] to explicit fixed-step-size bias expansions [4]. As a bridge, it connects optimization, sampling, statistical physics, and the geometry of probability measures.

The trajectory since Welling and Teh [1] is instructive: the community first established asymptotic correctness, then nonasymptotic rates, then fixed-step-size bias characterizations, then a complete design framework for the entire SG-MCMC family [5], and now confronts the empirical puzzles — cold posteriors, multimodality, prior misspecification — that the theory cannot yet dissolve. For the practitioner, SGLD remains the cheapest credible route to Bayesian uncertainty in large models; for the theorist, it remains a proving ground where every assumption is tested against reality. The diffusion, it turns out, still has far to run.
