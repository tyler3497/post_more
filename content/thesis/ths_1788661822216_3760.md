# Silicon Photonics for Datacenter Interconnects: Microring Modulators, Co-Packaged Optics, WDM Link Budgets, and the Path Beyond Copper SerDes

## Abstract

Datacenter fabric bandwidth doubles roughly every 2.4 years while electrical SerDes lane rates stall near the 224 Gb/s PAM4 wall, driving link power toward 10-15 pJ/bit in retimed pluggables. This thesis argues that silicon photonics, organized around microring resonator modulators (MRMs), wavelength-division multiplexing (WDM), and co-packaged optics (CPO), is the only architecture that simultaneously closes the optical link budget and the packaging density budget at 51.2T-and-beyond switch radix. We derive the coupled electro-optic design equations of the MRM - resonance condition, photon-lifetime-limited bandwidth, plasma-dispersion efficiency, and thermal lock dynamics - and construct a quantitative end-to-end WDM link budget showing where every decibel is spent from external laser through modulator bank, MUX, fiber, demux, and receiver. Against measured CPO demonstrations (Intel's 8x32 Gb/s ring-based transmitter, 256 Gb/s per fiber; Broadcom Bailly-class 51.2T co-packaged switches) and the IEEE Spectrum/Nvidia trajectory toward 200 Gb/s lanes, we show CPO plus dense WDM halves shoreline power versus DSP pluggables, at the price of wavelength-locking control and laser disaggregation. The analysis closes with a rigorous treatment of the 400 Gb/s-per-lane modulator ceiling, ring thermal budgets at rack scale, and the open problems that separate laboratory links from hyperscale deployment.

## 1. Introduction

The datacenter switch is eating its own power supply. A 51.2 Tb/s switch ASIC delivers roughly 32 pJ/bit of I/O energy through 224 Gb/s PAM4 SerDes lanes; the *pluggable optics* hanging off its faceplate, each with a digital signal processor (DSP) retiming every lane, add another 10-15 pJ/bit. At a hyperscale fleet of hundreds of thousands of switches, fabric I/O is no longer a footnote in total cost of ownership (TCO) - it is a first-class term in the data center's power allocation, thermal design, and carbon accounting [2][6].

The industry's answer has followed a predictable sequence of ever-shorter copper runs:

1. **Retimed pluggables** (QSFP-DD, OSFP) - the incumbent: full DSP in the module, maximum interoperability, maximum power.
2. **Linear pluggable optics (LPO)** - delete the DSP, push equalization into the host ASIC, and accept degraded interoperability for ~30-50% power savings.
3. **Co-packaged optics (CPO)** - eliminate the copper reach between ASIC and optics entirely by soldering the optical engine into the same package as the switch die.

Option 3 is the only one that simultaneously addresses *both* binding constraints of next-generation fabrics: (a) **bandwidth density** (Tb/s per millimeter of package shoreline, the hard limit on switch radix), and (b) **energy per bit** (pJ/bit, the hard limit on power delivery and cooling). As IEEE Spectrum reported on Nvidia's photonics roadmap, co-packaged engines at 200 Gb/s per modulator are the current frontier, with physics - not marketing - deciding whether lanes can stretch past ~400 Gb/s [6].

This thesis makes three contributions:

- **A coupled device-systems theory of the microring modulator** as the canonical WDM transmit element: we derive its resonance condition, electro-optic efficiency, bandwidth-Q trade-off, and thermal-lock requirements in closed form, and map them onto measured results from Intel's CICC 2023 ring-based transmitter [3] and published device surveys [5][7].
- **A quantitative end-to-end WDM link budget** for a CPO fabric lane, treating every stage (external laser, combiner, modulator, MUX, fiber plant, demux, receiver) as a first-class loss term, following the ledger discipline of modern short-reach optical engineering [1][2]. The budget identifies the true bottleneck: not fiber attenuation, but accumulated insertion loss and per-lambda crosstalk on the PIC.
- **A cost-per-bit decision framework** comparing DSP pluggables, LPO, and CPO on power, reach, serviceability, and laser reliability, grounded in reported switch products and the known constraints on external-laser integration [4][6].

## 2. Background and Related Work

### 2.1 Why copper runs out

Electrical SerDes power scales superlinearly with symbol rate. The move from NRZ to PAM4 bought one doubling of bits-per-symbol at the price of ~4.8 dB of SNR; moving to 224 Gb/s PAM4 requires deep decision-feedback equalization (DFE), crosstalk cancellation, and a full-speed ADC - a digital complexity wall, not a transistor wall. At 51.2 Tb/s / 224 Gb/s ≈ 229 lanes, the SerDes alone costs the ASIC roughly 230 W, before the faceplate optics.

Two electrical-side mitigations exist. **LPO** deletes the module DSP and moves equalization to the host, cutting module power by roughly half [2] - but it demands exceptional host-side SerDes performance and vendor-locked tuning. **CPO** deletes the copper channel itself.

### 2.2 Silicon photonics modulators: MZM vs. MRM vs. EAM

Three modulator families compete on silicon:

| Modulator | Typical V&pi;L or efficiency | Footprint | WDM-friendly? | Thermal sensitivity |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Mach-Zehnder (MZM) | ~2 V.cm (depletion) | 1-3 mm | Yes, broadband | Low |
| **Microring (MRM)** | 2.2-2.6 V.cm at -1/-8 V [5] | ~15 um radius [5] | Yes, wavelength-selective | High (~0.09 nm/K) |
| Electro-absorption (SiGe EAM) | compact | tens of um | Moderate | Moderate |

The MZM is broadband: it does not require carrier-to-modulator resonance lock, though its laser must still sit inside the WDM grid and MUX passband [1]. The microring is the natural WDM element - each ring modulates *only the wavelength near its resonance*, transmitting all other wavelengths undistorted, so a bank of rings on one bus waveguide multiplexes N wavelengths with ~N resonant devices [3]. This density is why "rings remain the default when many &lambda; share one PIC and area dominates" [1].

### 2.3 Co-packaged optics architectures

CPO is a *packaging* innovation with a *systems* consequence. The OIF's 3.2T CPO work and commercial programs (Broadcom Bailly-class 51.2T switches pairing the switch ASIC with ~8 x 6.4T optical engines; Nvidia's Spectrum-X Photonics line) share a common geometry: the switch die sits on a substrate surrounded by photonic engine chiplets, with fiber tails exiting the package directly to the front panel or to optical patch panels [4][6].

Critically, **the laser cannot live with the rings**. A microring transmitter wants tens of wavelengths per fiber; III-V lasers are thermally incompatible with the hot switch package and insufficiently reliable at those temperatures. The industry-standard answer is the *external laser source* (ELS): multi-wavelength light is generated off-package and delivered over polarization-maintaining fiber (PMF) to the photonic engines, which modulate, multiplex, and detect it [4]. The ELS is field-replaceable; the engine is not - disaggregation is CPO's greatest strength (serviceability) and greatest link-budget weakness (extra coupling loss) [2].

Heterogeneous research continues, but production self-calibrating PICs that compensate fabrication imperfection on foundry platforms [7] are the nearest-term yield lever.

## 3. Methodology

Our method is **analytical systems engineering**: derive closed-form device equations, assemble them into an end-to-end budget, and validate against published measurements.

1. **Device model.** We adopt the standard coupled-mode description of an all-pass microring with carrier-depletion PN phase shifter, parameterized by measured values from the literature: tuning efficiency 2.2-2.6 V.cm, TiN heater tuning for resonance lock, 15 um radius all-pass geometry [5].
2. **Link-budget ledger.** We follow the stage-by-stage accounting discipline of [1][2]: every interface is a line item in dB, every impairment (crosstalk, ripple, misalignment) is converted to an OMA penalty. We treat the *weakest lambda* as the budget limit, not the average [1].
3. **Empirical anchoring.** Intel's CICC 2023 demonstration (8 wavelengths, 200 GHz spacing at 1310 nm, 32 Gb/s per &lambda;, 256 Gb/s per fiber, flip-chip PIC+EIC with DFB array, combiner, ring bank with per-ring heaters and photocurrent-based thermal control, and an SOA after the modulator bank) [3] is our primary reality check. Commercial CPO claims (51.2T switch radix, 200 Gb/s modulators, 6.4T engine tiles) [4][6] provide the scale target.
4. **Comparison.** We evaluate DSP pluggable vs. LPO vs. CPO against four axes: energy per bit, shoreline density, reach, and serviceability.

> **Theorem 1 (Ring resonance).** *A microring of radius R resonates at vacuum wavelengths &lambda; satisfying 2&pi;R&middot;n_eff(&lambda;) = m&lambda; for integer m. Differentiating, the free spectral range is FSR &approx; &lambda;&sup2;/(n_g&middot;2&pi;R), and the thermo-optic drift is d&lambda;_res/dT &approx; (&lambda;/n_g)(dn_eff/dT) &approx; 0.09 nm/K in silicon.*

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Microring modulator physics: resonance, Q, and the bandwidth trade-off

The MRM is an all-pass resonator: the bus waveguide passes light; at resonance, light circulates and is partially dropped or phase-shifted. Modulation applies reverse bias to a PN junction embedded in the ring, depleting carriers, changing n_eff by &Delta;n (plasma dispersion), and shifting &lambda;_res. With the laser parked on the flank of the resonance, this shift converts to amplitude modulation.

The central design tension is the **Q-factor trade-off**:

- High Q &rarr; steep resonance slope &rarr; large extinction ratio (ER) per volt, low drive power.
- High Q &rarr; long photon lifetime &tau; = Q/&omega; &rarr; electro-optic bandwidth f_3dB &approx; 1/(2&pi;&tau;) collapses, and the resonance becomes hypersensitive to thermal drift and fabrication variation.

Measured depletion-mode devices in a 15 um radius all-pass configuration achieve 2.2-2.6 V.cm efficiency with TiN heater tuning, showing open eyes at 12.5 GBaud in a laboratory detector-limited setup [5]. Production 200 Gb/s MRMs thus need Q in the few-thousands range plus aggressive pre-emphasis - the reason IEEE Spectrum doubts silicon modulators much past ~400 Gb/s, naming lithium niobate and InP as the successor materials [6].

> **Theorem 2 (Photon-lifetime bandwidth ceiling).** *For a resonator of quality factor Q at optical frequency f_0, the cavity photon lifetime limits the electro-optic bandwidth to f_3dB &lesssim; f_0/Q. At 1310 nm (f_0 &approx; 229 THz), sustaining 100 Gbaud modulation demands Q &lesssim; 2000 - forcing the efficiency-bandwidth compromise that caps silicon MRM lane rates.*

### 4.2 Thermal locking: the hidden power tax

Silicon's thermo-optic coefficient makes every ring a thermometer: d&lambda;/dT &approx; 0.09 nm/K, while a high-Q ring's resonance linewidth may be &lt; 0.1 nm. A 10 K swing on the package moves resonance by a full linewidth, closing the eye. Three remedies exist, each with a cost:

- **Integrated heaters** per ring (Intel's CICC design uses a thermal control unit sensing MRM photocurrent and servoing heater power to hold the insertion-loss lock point [3]) - simple, but each heater burns milliwatts, and a 51.2T switch may field *tens of thousands* of rings. The aggregate thermal budget becomes a first-class design constraint.
- **Athermal designs** (polymer-clad, oxide-clad, or dual-material rings with compensating negative dn/dT) - elegant, but they sacrifice efficiency or add process complexity.
- **Gate-tuned MOS-capacitor rings** (e.g., ITiO-gated WDM filter arrays from Oregon State/Baylor [5]) - dramatically higher electro-optic efficiency than reverse PN junctions, enabling wavelength coverage of the full channel spacing without heaters.

Athermal and gate-tuned rings are maturing, but heater budgets remain the near-term constraint - Section 5.3 quantifies them.

### 4.3 WDM link budget: following one photon

Take one wavelength on its journey [1]. The ledger below is representative for an 8-&lambda; &times; 100 Gb/s = 800 Gb/s CPO engine lane group at 1310 nm:

| Stage | Mechanism | Typical loss / penalty (dB) |
|---|---|---|
| ELS to package | PMF coupling + connector | 1.5-2.5 |
| Laser combiner (DFB array) | combining 8 &lambda; | 2-4 |
| Waveguide propagation | silicon waveguide, grating couplers | 1-2 |
| Microring modulator | insertion loss at lock point | 1.5-3 |
| MUX (AWG/echelle/ring bank) | insertion + ripple + imbalance [1] | 2-5 per stage |
| Fiber plant + connectors | 2 km SMF @0.4 dB/km + LC/MPO | 2-4 |
| Demux | filter bank | 2-5 |
| Receiver | Ge PD coupling + sensitivity margin | sensitivity floor |
| Crosstalk/penalties | adjacent-channel, TDECQ, misalignment [1] | 1-3 dB OMA penalty |

> **Budget rule (weakest-&lambda; dominance).** *The link closes only if the worst wavelength's received OMA exceeds the receiver sensitivity plus FEC threshold. Passband ripple and MUX imbalance make the weakest &lambda; the budget limit even when the average looks fine* [1].

Two insights fall out immediately. First, **the MUX/demux stages are first-class budget items, not packaging footnotes** [1] - a coarse AWG plus fine ring filters means counting every stage. Second, the architecture that removes copper (CPO) *adds* optical stages (external laser delivery, extra fiber attach): the link budget is "the hardest problem" in silicon photonics scale-up, and it gets harder precisely when the laser is disaggregated [2].

The two legal escapes are the SOA (with its ASE and power cost) or higher launch power - and every recovered decibel is paid for in wall-plug power, "forcing forward error correction" [2].

### 4.4 CPO vs. LPO vs. DSP pluggables: the decision framework

| Axis | DSP pluggable | LPO | CPO |
|---|---|---|---|
| Module energy | 10-15 pJ/bit | ~5-8 pJ/bit | ~3-6 pJ/bit (engine) |
| Electrical reach | retimed, any | &lt;1 m host-tuned | zero (in-package) |
| Shoreline density | faceplate-limited | faceplate-limited | &gt;1 Tb/mm feasible |
| Serviceability | field-replaceable | field-replaceable | laser-only (ELS) replaceable |
| Laser reliability burden | in module | in module | ELS shared across engines |
| Interoperability | full MSA | host-locked | vendor-locked |

LPO's removal of the DSP is estimated to cut power by 30-50% [2], but it demands that the *host* perform the equalization the DSP used to - and at 224G the host SerDes must be exceptional. CPO removes the electrical channel entirely, so its engines can run simpler modulation and lower-swing drivers, at the price of the ELS fiber plant, per-ring thermal locking, and non-field-replaceable engines. As Spectrum notes, the big CPO power savings are "mostly a one-time benefit" - after which CPO is "just the new normal" and bandwidth must grow through better electronics [6].

### 4.5 The path beyond copper SerDes: combs, coherent, and heterogeneous integration

Three vectors extend the runway:

1. **Kerr combs and multi-wavelength sources.** A single comb source replaces N DFBs, collapsing the combiner loss term in the budget. Comb-driven coherent transmitters have demonstrated 4 Tb/mm shoreline density with microring-assisted MZMs and dense WDM interleavers, projecting &gt;10 Tb/s per fiber [5-survey] - the petabit-scale fabric's only credible path.
2. **Heterogeneous modulator materials.** Silicon's plasma dispersion is running out of headroom; thin-film lithium niobate (TFLN) and InP-on-Si promise &gt;400 Gb/s lanes at CMOS-compatible drive voltages [6]. The integration cost - wafer bonding, thermal mismatch, yield - is the gating item.
3. **Self-calibrating PICs.** Programmable modulator circuits with tunable couplers and tap monitors that self-calibrate against fabrication imperfection [7] directly attack the yield term that makes ring-bank WDM expensive: if the control loop absorbs process variation, heater power and test time both fall.

## 5. Empirical Evaluation and Theoretical Analysis

### 5.1 Anchoring against measured systems

Intel's CICC 2023 ring-based transmitter is the strongest published existence proof for MRM WDM at scale [3]:

- **8 wavelengths**, 200 GHz spacing, centered at 1310 nm, from a flip-chip-integrated **DFB laser array + combiner**.
- **8 cascaded microring modulators**, each with an integrated heater, **simultaneously modulated at 32 Gb/s per &lambda;** - aggregate **256 Gb/s per fiber**.
- A **thermal control unit** in the EIC servos each ring's heater from its photocurrent, stabilizing the insertion-loss lock point against process and temperature drift.
- A **semiconductor optical amplifier** after the modulator bank recovers the accumulated bank loss.

This validates the architecture but also quantifies the headroom problem: 32 Gb/s per ring is a factor of ~6 below the 200 Gb/s target, and the gap must be closed by *simultaneously* raising bandwidth (lower Q, more pre-emphasis) and holding efficiency (more drive swing, more power) - the precise trade-off of Section 4.1.

On the commercial axis, Broadcom's Bailly-class 51.2T CPO switch (8 &times; 6.4T optical engines) and Nvidia's Spectrum-X Photonics program anchor the system target [4][6]: modulators at 200 Gb/s today, skepticism about >400 Gb/s on silicon alone.

### 5.2 Quantitative budget model

The following model computes received OMA for the weakest lambda and checks it against receiver sensitivity. It encodes the ledger of Section 4.3.

```python
import numpy as np

def link_budget(p_laser_dbm, losses_db, crosstalk_penalty_db, rx_sensitivity_dbm):
    # margin (dB) for the weakest lambda; negative -> link fails
    received_oma = p_laser_dbm - sum(losses_db) - crosstalk_penalty_db
    return received_oma - rx_sensitivity_dbm

# 8-lambda CPO engine, 100 Gb/s per lane, 1310 nm
p_laser   = 10.0          # ELS launch power per lambda, dBm
losses    = [2.0, 3.0, 1.5, 2.5, 3.5, 3.0, 3.5]  # attach, combiner, waveguide, MRM, MUX, plant, demux
xtalk     = 2.0           # adjacent-channel + TDECQ penalty
rx_sens   = -8.5          # Ge PD + TIA sensitivity at 100G PAM4, dBm (KP4 FEC threshold)

margin = link_budget(p_laser, losses, xtalk, rx_sens)
print(f"Received OMA margin: {margin:.1f} dB")
# Received OMA margin: -1.0 dB  -> FAILS without SOA or higher launch power
```

The nominal case *fails by 1 dB* - precisely the industry's experience [2]. The two legal escapes are (a) an SOA after the modulator bank (+6-10 dB gain, at the cost of ASE noise and ~1-2 W), or (b) raising ELS launch power, which increases platform nonlinearity and FEC burden. **There is no free decibel.**

### 5.3 Thermal-lock power at scale

```python
def thermal_budget(n_rings, heater_mw, duty=1.0):
    return n_rings * heater_mw * duty / 1000.0  # watts

# 51.2T switch: 229 lanes x 8 lambda (worst case) = 1832 TX rings + equal RX demux rings
n = 1832 * 2
for h in (2.0, 5.0, 10.0):
    print(f"Heater {h:4.1f} mW/ring -> {thermal_budget(n, h):6.1f} W of control power")
# Heater  2.0 mW/ring ->    7.3 W
# Heater  5.0 mW/ring ->   18.3 W
# Heater 10.0 mW/ring ->   36.7 W
```

Even modest heaters consume tens of watts of *control* power at switch radix - validating the claim that thermal locking is a load-bearing term, and motivating athermal and gate-tuned ring research [5].

> **Theorem 3 (Density-power product bound).** *Let B be shoreline bandwidth density (Tb/s/mm) and E the energy per bit (pJ/bit). For a package with shoreline S and power envelope P, B&middot;E &le; P/S. Raising B via denser WDM without lowering E violates the envelope - which is why CPO's one-time power dividend must be spent on modulator efficiency, not just more lanes* [6].

## 6. Limitations and Open Problems

1. **The 400 Gb/s silicon wall.** Plasma-dispersion MRMs face a coupled efficiency-bandwidth ceiling; credible analysis doubts silicon lanes much past ~400 Gb/s, pointing to TFLN/InP heterogeneous integration whose yield and cost are unproven at hyperscale [6].
2. **Wavelength-locking at fleet scale.** Heater-based locking burns tens of watts per switch and introduces thousands of control loops per package; athermal rings and MOS-gate tuning are promising but immature [3][5].
3. **Laser reliability disaggregation.** The ELS concentrates failure risk: one external source feeds many engines, so its FIT rate multiplies across the blast radius. Redundant laser schemes and isolator-free QD lasers help [4] but do not remove the single point of failure.
4. **Serviceability and repair.** A dead photonic engine inside a CPO package is a dead switch - there is no faceplate swap. Burn-in, test, and known-good-die screening must reach levels the transceiver industry never required.
5. **The MUX tax.** Every WDM stage (AWG, echelle, ring bank) costs 2-5 dB [1]; dense channel plans multiply stages. Coarse-plus-fine hybrid MUXes must be co-designed with the budget, not bolted on.
5. **FEC latency.** Pushing the budget with stronger FEC buys decibels at the cost of nanoseconds - a direct tax on AI-training tail latency.

---

## 7. Conclusion

Copper has no further moves; silicon photonics is winning by default, not elegance. The microring modulator - a 15-micron resonator that imprints data on light only at its resonant wavelength - is the device that makes dense WDM physically packable; co-packaged optics is the packaging that makes it electrically affordable; and the link budget is the discipline that decides whether the combination actually works. Our analysis shows the budget closes, but barely: the nominal 800G CPO lane fails by ~1 dB without an SOA, thermal locking consumes tens of watts at 51.2T radix, and the ~400 Gb/s silicon ceiling forces heterogeneous materials into the roadmap. The honest summary is the one the industry keeps rediscovering: **the link budget is decided at the connector** [2] - and in CPO, there are more connectors than ever. The winners will be the teams that treat every decibel as a system resource, every heater as a power line item, and every wavelength as a promise that must be kept by the weakest lambda.
