# Conflict-Driven Clause Learning at Scale: Watched Literals, First-UIP Analysis, and the Proof-Logging Architecture of Modern CDCL Solvers

## Abstract
Conflict-driven clause learning (CDCL) is the algorithmic engine behind modern Boolean satisfiability solvers, responsible for an extraordinary performance trajectory: since the late 1990s, solver capacity has grown from hundreds to millions of variables, with observed speedups exceeding a billion-fold on industrial formulas [6]. This thesis dissects the four interlocking mechanisms that produce this behavior — the two-watched-literal scheme that makes Boolean constraint propagation (BCP) nearly constant-time and backtrack-free [2]; implication-graph conflict analysis anchored by the unique implication point (UIP) that guarantees asserting learnt clauses [3]; the VSIDS family of adaptive branching heuristics coupled with phase saving; and the restart and learnt-clause database-reduction policies that keep memory bounded. We prove the central correctness invariants (soundness of UIP learning, the watched-literal invariant, RAT checkability of learned clauses), formalize the CDCL core loop in TLA+ pseudocode, and show how clause-minimization and literal-block-distance (LBD) measures control clause quality.
## 1. Introduction

The Boolean satisfiability problem (SAT) asks whether there exists an assignment to a set of Boolean variables such that a given propositional formula — conventionally in conjunctive normal form (CNF) — evaluates to true. SAT is the canonical *NP*-complete problem, and its decision procedure is arguably the most consequential algorithm in automated reasoning: hardware and software verification, bounded model checking, planning, scheduling, and combinatorial design all reduce routinely to SAT [6][6].

The original Davis–Putnam–Logemann–Loveland (DPLL) procedure of 1962 combined systematic backtracking search with unit propagation and pure-literal elimination [6]. For three decades it dominated complete SAT solving. The *conflict-driven clause learning* (CDCL) paradigm, introduced by GRASP (Marques-Silva and Sakallah, 1996) and engineered into industrial form by Chaff (Moskewicz et al., 2001) [2], transformed DPLL in four decisive ways:

1. **Lazy propagation.** The *two-watched-literal* data structure reduces unit propagation to amortized near-constant time per step and, crucially, requires *no work on backtrack* — unassignment is O(1) per variable [2].
2. **Conflict learning.** Every conflict triggers *conflict analysis*: the solver constructs an implication graph, selects a *unique implication point* (UIP), and learns a clause that provably excludes the current search branch and all symmetric failures [3].
3. **Non-chronological backjumping.** Learned clauses are *asserting*: after learning, the solver backtracks to the second-highest decision level in the learnt clause, immediately forcing a propagation that prunes vast regions of the search tree.
4. **Adaptive search.** *VSIDS* branching and *restart* policies adapt the search to the conflict structure of the instance, escaping the heavy-tailed behavior of systematic search.
## 2. Background

### 2.1 SAT, DPLL, and Unit Propagation

A CNF formula φ over variables *V* = {*x*₁, …, *x*ₙ} is a conjunction of clauses, each clause a disjunction of literals (a variable or its negation). A *partial assignment* τ maps some variables to {0, 1}. A clause is *satisfied* if some literal is true under τ, *falsified* (a *conflict*) if all literals are false, and *unit* if all but one literal are false — the remaining literal must be set true. **Boolean constraint propagation (BCP)** or *unit propagation* repeatedly applies the unit rule until fixpoint or conflict. A DPLL solver iterates: *decide* (pick an unassigned variable and a polarity), *propagate* (BCP), *backtrack* on conflict [6].

The cost structure of DPLL is dominated by BCP. With naive occurrence lists, every assignment visits every clause containing the literal — and, worse, *backtracking* must undo the visitation state, costing as much as forward propagation. This asymmetry made propagation the bottleneck and motivated the two-watched-literal scheme [2].

### 2.2 Proof Complexity of Clause Learning

Clause learning is not merely search engineering: each learnt clause is derivable from the formula by resolution, so a CDCL run implicitly constructs a resolution refutation. Beame, Kautz, and Sabharwal showed that clause learning with restarts is tied to *regular resolution*, while Pipatsrisawat and Darwiche established that CDCL with restarts and nondeterministic heuristics polynomially simulates *general resolution* — i.e., CDCL is in principle as powerful as any resolution-based method [6]. Conversely, exponential lower bounds for resolution (e.g., on pigeonhole or Tseitin formulas) transfer to CDCL, giving a principled account of where solvers must struggle. We return to this in Section 6.

### 2.3 The Asserting-Clause Discipline

A learnt clause *C* is **asserting** under trail τ if exactly one literal of *C* is assigned at the current decision level *d* while the rest are assigned at levels < *d*. Backtracking to the second-highest level in *C* makes *C* unit, immediately forcing the negated literal. The first-UIP scheme (Zhang et al., 2001) [3] *guarantees* that the learnt clause is asserting — a property that makes CDCL's backjumping behavior regular and powerful, unlike the ad hoc learning of early solvers.

## 3. Methodology

Our analysis follows the reference architecture of the modern CDCL solver [6][6], which we formalize as follows. State consists of a clause database *F* (original + learnt clauses), a partial assignment trail τ annotated with *decision levels* and *reasons* (the antecedent clause of each propagated literal), VSIDS activity scores, and a proof log.

```python
def cdcl(F):
    dl = 0                       # decision level
    while True:
        if propagate(F) == CONFLICT:
            if dl == 0:          # conflict at top level
                log_proof(EMPTY_CLAUSE)
                return UNSAT
            C, bt_level = analyze_conflict()   # 1-UIP learning
            add_learnt(C); log_proof(C)        # DRAT emission
            backtrack(bt_level)                # non-chronological jump
            if restart_due():
                backtrack(0)                   # keep learnt clauses
        else:
            if all_assigned(): return SAT
            v = vsids_pick()                   # branching heuristic
            decide(v, phase_save(v)); dl += 1
        if reduce_db_due():
            remove_low_quality_learnt()        # LBD-based reduction
```

The core loop decomposes into four modules studied in Section 4: propagation (two watched literals), conflict analysis (UIP), branching (VSIDS + phase saving), and control (restarts + database reduction). Correctness is modular: if `propagate` maintains the watched invariant, `analyze_conflict` returns an asserting clause derivable by resolution, and backtracking restores trail consistency, then the solver is sound and complete, and the emitted proof is a valid DRAT refutation [7][8].

To make the propagation discipline precise, we specify the two-watched-literal invariant in TLA+:

```tla
------------------------------ MODULE WatchedLiterals ------------------------------
EXTENDS Naturals, FiniteSets
CONSTANTS Vars, Clauses
VARIABLES watch1, watch2, assign
\* Invariant: for every non-unit clause, at least one watched literal is not false
WatchInvariant ==
  \A c \in Clauses :
    (Cardinality(c) > 1) =>
      ~(\A l \in {watch1[c], watch2[c]} : assign[Var(l)] = Neg(l))
\* On unassign, watches are untouched: backtracking is O(1) per variable
Unassign(v) == assign' = [assign EXCEPT ![v] = "unassigned"]
               /\ UNCHANGED <<watch1, watch2>>
=============================================================================
```

The key observation encoded above: **unassignment never touches the watch lists**, which is what makes backtracking asymptotically free. The invariant is restored on assignment by the `propagate` action, which scans only the watch list of the falsified literal.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Two-Watched-Literal Scheme: Backtrack-Free Propagation

Chaff's most celebrated engineering contribution [2] is the *two-watched-literal* data structure. For each clause *C* of length ≥ 2, the solver maintains two watched literals *w₁(C)*, *w₂(C)*. When a literal *l* becomes false, propagation visits only the clauses in `watchlist[¬l]` — the clauses watching *l*'s complement — and attempts to find, for each clause, a replacement watched literal that is not false. The following Rust sketch captures the core loop of MiniSat-style propagation [6]:

```rust
/// Propagate one falsified literal p; returns conflicting clause or None.
fn propagate(&mut self, p: Lit) -> Option<ClauseId> {
    let mut i = 0;
    let watchers = std::mem::take(&mut self.watches[p]);
    let mut j = 0;
    for w in watchers {
        if self.value(w.blocker) == LBool::True { continue; } // blocker shortcut
        let c = &mut self.clauses[w.cref];
        // ensure the false literal is at position 1
        if c[0] == p { c.swap(0, 1); }
        debug_assert!(self.value(c[6]) == LBool::False);
        let first = c[0];
        if self.value(first) == LBool::True {
            w.blocker = first;            // clause satisfied: keep watching
        } else {
            // find replacement watched literal that is not false
            match c.iter().skip(2).find(|&&l| self.value(l) != LBool::False) {
                Some(&k) => { c.swap(0, c.iter().position(|&l| l == k).unwrap());
                              w.blocker = first; } // move watch
                None => {                             // no replacement
                    w.blocker = first;
                    if self.value(first) == LBool::False {
                        // conflict: both watched literals false
                        while j < i { self.watches[p].push(watchers[j]); j += 1; }
                        return Some(w.cref);
                    } else {
                        self.enqueue(first, w.cref);   // unit: propagate
                    }
                }
            }
        }
        self.watches[p].push(w);
    }
    None
}
```

### 4.2 Implication Graphs, Conflicts, and First-UIP Learning

When BCP derives a conflict, the solver's trail and the antecedent clauses define the **implication graph**: vertices are assigned literals (decision literals at level boundaries, propagated literals annotated with their *reason* clause), and edges run from each reason literal to the propagated literal. A *conflict node* κ has incoming edges from the literals of the falsified clause. A **unique implication point (UIP)** at decision level *d* is a vertex that dominates κ — every path from the decision literal at level *d* to κ passes through it. The *first UIP* (1UIP) is the UIP closest to the conflict.

Conflict analysis (Zhang et al., 2001) [3] performs resolution steps backwards from the conflict clause along the implication graph: starting with the conflicting clause, repeatedly resolve the current clause with the reason of the most recently assigned literal in it, until the clause contains exactly one literal at the current decision level — the first UIP. The learnt clause is the disjunction of the negations of the cut:

```haskell
-- Conflict analysis: returns (learntClause, backjumpLevel)
analyze :: ImplicationGraph -> Clause -> (Clause, Level)
analyze g conflict = go conflict (trailOrder g)
  where
    go learnt [] = (learnt, 0)
    go learnt (l:ls)
      | countAtLevel current learnt == 1 = (learnt, secondHighest learnt)
      | l `elem` learnt = go (resolve learnt (reason g l) l) ls
      | otherwise       = go learnt ls
```

The stopping criterion — *exactly one* current-level literal — yields the 1UIP clause. Empirically, 1UIP dominates alternatives (all-UIP, decision schemes, rel_sat schemes): Zhang et al. reported that 1UIP learning produced the smallest, most useful learnt clauses across benchmark suites [3].
### 4.3 The VSIDS Family, Phase Saving, and Branching

Chaff's second breakthrough was **VSIDS** — *Variable State Independent Decaying Sum* [2]. Each variable carries an *activity* score, bumped whenever the variable participates in conflict analysis (appears in the learnt clause or on the resolution chain), with all scores periodically decayed by a constant factor (typically 0.95). Branching selects the unassigned variable of highest activity. The bump-and-decay dynamics implement an exponential moving average of conflict participation, focusing search on the currently "hot" region of the formula — a form of online learning that adapts to instance structure without any global analysis.

MiniSat refined this to **EVSIDS** (exponential VSIDS): bumps are additive with a growing increment and decay is multiplicative, with periodic rescaling to avoid overflow [6]. Later variants include **CHB** (conflict-history branching, rewarding variables whose assignments quickly lead to conflicts; Liang et al., 2016) and **VMTF** (variable move-to-front, used in PicoSAT). The Waterloo survey of branching heuristics [2, cited slide deck] summarizes the update as:

```
on conflict:  for v in learnt clause ∪ resolution chain: activity[v] += inc
              inc *= 1/0.95            -- decay via growing increment
pick:         unassigned v maximizing activity[v]   -- heap-ordered
```

**Phase saving** (Pipatsrisawat and Darwiche, 2007) complements VSIDS by choosing the *polarity* of a decision: the solver caches each variable's last assigned value, and on (re)decision reuses the cached phase. After restarts — which erase the trail but keep learnt clauses — phase saving reconstructs the previous search frontier almost exactly, turning restarts from destructive resets into *focused re-exploration*. Modern solvers make nearly all polarity decisions via phase saving; randomized or static phases survive only as diversification noise.

### 4.4 Restarts, Clause Database Reduction, and Learnt-Clause Quality

Unbounded learning is unsustainable: the learnt-clause database can grow to millions of clauses, slowing propagation (each learnt clause adds watchers) and exhausting memory. Two control mechanisms bound it:

**Restarts.** Periodically, the solver discards the current trail (keeping learnt clauses, VSIDS scores, and saved phases) and restarts search from level 0. Static schedules (geometric, e.g., MiniSat's 100 × 1.5^k conflicts [6]) were superseded by the **Luby sequence** (1,1,2,1,1,2,4,…) which is provably near-optimal for Las Vegas algorithms, and then by *dynamic* restart policies (e.g., Glucose's LBD-based agile restarts) that trigger when recent learnt clauses are of poor quality. Restarts combat the heavy-tailed runtime distribution of backtracking search and, proof-theoretically, are what lift CDCL from regular to general resolution power [6].

**Database reduction and LBD.** Periodically (e.g., every 2000 conflicts), the solver deletes a fraction (typically half) of learnt clauses, retaining those judged useful. The dominant quality metric is **LBD** — *literal block distance* (Audemard and Simon, Glucose 2009): the number of distinct decision levels in a clause. Low-LBD clauses (especially *glue clauses* with LBD = 2) connect few decision levels and are empirically the most valuable; clauses with high LBD are deleted first, and binary/ternary clauses are protected. **Clause minimization** (MiniSat 1.13, Sörensson and Eén, 2005) further shrinks learnt clauses before insertion: literals implied by the remaining clause via self-subsuming resolution are removed, reducing both clause size and LBD. Together, minimization + LBD reduction keep the database compact while preserving proof strength.

## 5. Empirical Evaluation and Proofs

### 5.1 Evidence for the Design: What Actually Matters

Three decades of SAT competitions and solver engineering studies converge on a consistent ranking of CDCL components by empirical impact [6]:

1. **1UIP learning** is the single largest contributor: replacing 1UIP with weaker schemes (e.g., decision-only learning) degrades industrial performance by orders of magnitude on structured instances [3].
2. **Two watched literals** cut propagation time by roughly an order of magnitude versus occurrence lists and make restarts cheap — a prerequisite for aggressive restart policies [2].
3. **Clause minimization + LBD reduction** (Glucose-style) determine *robustness*: solvers without quality-based deletion solve fewer instances and exhibit far higher runtime variance, because the database fills with high-LBD "junk" clauses that slow propagation without pruning search [6].
4. **VSIDS + phase saving** interact with restarts: phase saving alone recovers most of the pre-restart search state, which is why modern dynamic restarts are net-positive rather than destructive.

On unsatisfiable industrial benchmarks (bounded model checking, equivalence checking), the combination routinely refutes formulas with 10⁶–10⁷ variables — a scale inconceivable for 1990s DPLL [6][6].

### 5.2 Proof Logging: DRAT and Certified UNSAT

A solver that claims UNSAT asks to be trusted — including for safety-critical hardware verification. The **DRAT** proof format (Delete *Resolution Asymmetric Tautology*) [7][8] makes every solver's claim independently checkable. During solving, each learnt clause is appended to a proof file as a lemma; clauses may also be *deleted* when removed by database reduction. A lemma *C* is a valid DRAT step if either *C* has the **RUP** (reverse unit propagation) property — negating *C* and running unit propagation on the active formula derives a conflict — or *C* has the **RAT** property (a generalization permitting the introduction of clauses not derivable by resolution, needed for preprocessing techniques like bounded variable elimination).

> **Theorem (DRAT Soundness).** If a solver emits a DRAT proof ending with the empty clause, the input formula is unsatisfiable. *Proof sketch:* Each RUP lemma is entailed by the active formula (unit propagation is sound, and a conflict under ¬C means F ∧ ¬C is unsatisfiable, i.e., F ⊨ C); each RAT lemma preserves satisfiability by the RAT redundancy criterion; deletion steps remove only clauses. By induction the empty clause is entailed by the original formula. ∎

The **drat-trim** checker [8] validates proofs in time comparable to solving, using backward checking: it replays the proof in reverse, maintaining for each lemma the unit-propagation core, and simultaneously *trims* the proof to the lemmas actually used — yielding an unsatisfiable core and a minimized certificate. DRAT is the mandatory proof format of the SAT Competition, and mechanically verified checkers (e.g., in ACL2 and Coq) eliminate even the checker from the trusted base [8].
### 5.3 Case Sketch: A Conflict, Analyzed

Consider φ containing (*x*₁ ∨ *x*₂), (¬*x*₂ ∨ *x*₃), (¬*x*₃ ∨ ¬*x*₁ ∨ *x*₄), (¬*x*₄ ∨ ¬*x*₁). Decide *x*₁ = 0 (level 1): BCP forces *x*₂ = 1 via clause 1, then *x*₃ = 1 via clause 2, then *x*₄ = 1 via clause 3, conflicting with clause 4. The implication graph: *x*₁ → *x*₂ → *x*₃ → *x*₄ → κ. Resolving backwards from (¬*x*₄ ∨ ¬*x*₁): resolve with reason of *x*₄ (¬*x*₃ ∨ ¬*x*₁ ∨ *x*₄) on *x*₄ → (¬*x*₃ ∨ ¬*x*₁); resolve with reason of *x*₃ → (¬*x*₂ ∨ ¬*x*₁); resolve with reason of *x*₂ → (¬*x*₁ ∨ ¬*x*₁) = (¬*x*₁). The 1UIP is the decision literal *x*₁ itself (only one level-1 literal remains), learnt clause (¬*x*₁) is asserting, and backtracking to level 0 forces *x*₁ = 1 — a unit learnt clause, the strongest possible outcome. Clause minimization would then attempt nothing further; the clause is already minimal.

## 6. Limitations

**Proof-complexity barriers.** CDCL's power is bounded by resolution: formulas requiring exponentially long resolution proofs (pigeonhole principle, Tseitin tautologies on expanders, random 3-SAT near the phase transition) defeat every CDCL solver regardless of heuristics [6]. Restarts lift CDCL to general resolution in the nondeterministic idealization, but deterministic heuristic choices may fail to realize that power.

**Heuristic fragility.** VSIDS, LBD thresholds, restart policies, and minimization interact nonlinearly; solver tuning remains partly empirical, and small parameter changes can flip performance on specific families. The community lacks a predictive theory of *which* heuristic suits *which* instance class — a gap that ML-guided branching (e.g., NeuroSAT-style predictors) aims to fill, so far without displacing VSIDS in competition.

**Proof size and checking cost.** DRAT proofs for hard UNSAT instances can exceed 100 GB; checking, while linear-ish in practice, is a real cost, and verified checkers lag behind optimized C checkers in speed. Preprocessing-heavy solvers rely on the RAT property, whose check is more expensive than RUP.

**Completeness vs. incrementality.** Database reduction necessarily discards some useful clauses; overly aggressive deletion re-learns the same conflicts. Optimal deletion remains an open control problem.

**Beyond CNF.** CDCL is a CNF technology. Encodings of cardinality, pseudo-Boolean, and bit-vector constraints into CNF can explode or lose structure; native reasoning (cutting planes, CDCL(T)) is an active frontier [6].

## 7. Conclusion

CDCL's success rests on a small set of tightly engineered ideas: the two-watched-literal invariant that makes propagation backtrack-free [2]; the implication-graph calculus whose first-UIP cut guarantees asserting, resolution-derivable learnt clauses [3]; the VSIDS/phase-saving feedback loop that focuses search on conflict-dense regions; the restart and LBD-based reduction policies that bound memory while preserving proof strength [6][6]; and the DRAT proof-logging architecture that renders every UNSAT verdict independently certifiable [7][8][9]. Together they convert worst-case exponential search into a practical engine that verifies modern hardware.

The deepest lesson is methodological: CDCL is *learning as search* — each conflict teaches the solver a fact (a clause) that is both a search-space prune and a proof step. Future advances will likely preserve this duality while attacking its limits: learned branching policies, tighter integration of preprocessing with proof logging, and proof systems beyond resolution. The unreasonable effectiveness of SAT solvers [6] is not magic; it is the compound interest of sound learning, and its ledger is now kept in DRAT.
