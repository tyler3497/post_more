# Adversarial Robustness in Deep Learning: Certified Defenses, Randomized Smoothing, and the Geometry of Attack Surfaces

## Abstract

Deep neural networks achieve superhuman accuracy on nominal inputs yet collapse under imperceptible adversarial perturbations, a vulnerability with profound implications for safety-critical deployment. This thesis presents a unified treatment of adversarial robustness as the intersection of *optimization*, *geometry*, and *statistical learning theory*. We begin with the classical attack constructions — the L-BFGS formulation of Szegedy et al. [1], the linearity hypothesis and Fast Gradient Sign Method of Goodfellow et al. [2], the iterative projected-gradient-descent paradigm of Madry et al. [3], and the margin-based optimization attacks of Carlini and Wagner [4] — and show how they organize into a coherent threat taxonomy. We then examine empirical defenses through the lens of the obfuscated-gradients phenomenon identified by Athalye, Carlini, and Wagner [5], which explains why heuristic defenses create only the appearance of robustness. Finally, we develop certified defenses — adversarial training as a minimax saddle-point problem [3] and, centrally, randomized smoothing with its tight ℓ₂ certificate [6] — and analyze the provable robustness–accuracy tradeoff of Tsipras et al. [7] and the geometry of attack surfaces that bounds what any defense can achieve.

## 1 Introduction

The discovery that imperceptible perturbations can force high-performing classifiers into confident misclassification [1] exposed a fundamental instability at the heart of deep learning. Adversarial examples are not noise artifacts: they are *worst-case* inputs, synthesized by optimizing over the input space with the network's own gradients, and they transfer across architectures and training subsets in ways that suggest shared geometric vulnerabilities rather than implementation accidents [1], [2].

The field that grew from this observation is structured by a persistent adversarial dialectic. Attackers refine optimization procedures — from the expensive L-BFGS solves of Szegedy et al. [1] to the single-step FGSM [2], the iterated PGD [3], and the carefully tuned margin-based attacks of Carlini and Wagner [4] — while defenders propose countermeasures that are repeatedly defeated by *adaptive* attacks that respect the threat model [5]. This cycle revealed a methodological crisis: evaluating a defense against a fixed, weak attack proves nothing. As Athalye, Carlini, and Wagner demonstrated in their landmark case study, seven of nine ICLR 2018 non-certified defenses claiming white-box robustness relied on obfuscated gradients and fell to adapted attacks [5].

Certified defenses escape this cycle by replacing empirical claims with *theorems*. Adversarial training formulates robustness as a saddle-point problem solvable, approximately, by PGD [3]. Randomized smoothing converts any base classifier that performs well under Gaussian noise into a smoothed classifier with a *tight* ℓ₂ robustness certificate, computed from class probabilities via the Neyman–Pearson lemma [6]. These methods carry a provable cost: robustness may be fundamentally at odds with accuracy, a tension rooted in the distinction between robust and non-robust features [7].

This thesis argues that the geometry of the attack surface — the arrangement of decision boundaries relative to the data manifold in high-dimensional input space — is the single concept that unifies attacks, defenses, and their limits. We survey attack constructions (Section 2), empirical defenses and their failure modes (Section 3), certified defenses (Section 4), empirical and theoretical evidence (Section 5), and the open problems that remain (Section 6).

## 2 Background

### The adversarial optimization problem

Given a classifier *f : ℝᵈ → 𝒴*, an input *x* with true label *y*, and a perturbation budget *ε* under an ℓₚ norm, the attacker's problem is

> **Definition (Adversarial example).** A perturbation *δ ∈ ℝᵈ* is *adversarial* for *(x, y)* if *‖δ‖ₚ ≤ ε* and *f(x + δ) ≠ y*. In the targeted variant, the attacker additionally requires *f(x + δ) = t* for a chosen target class *t*.

Szegedy et al. formulated attack generation as constrained optimization, minimizing *‖r‖₂* subject to *f(x + r) = l* and *x + r* lying in the unit hypercube, solved approximately with box-constrained L-BFGS and a line search over the penalty weight *c* [1]. The method was expensive but established two enduring facts: adversarial perturbations transfer across architectures trained on disjoint data subsets, and the perturbations are not random artifacts of a particular initialization [1].

Goodfellow, Shlens, and Szegedy replaced the expensive solve with a first-order approximation, arguing that the primary cause of vulnerability is the *linear nature* of neural networks rather than their nonlinearity [2]. For high-dimensional inputs, the dot product *wᵀδ* accumulates many small per-coordinate changes into a large shift of activations, and the optimal ℓ∞ perturbation under linearization is the celebrated Fast Gradient Sign Method:

*x′ = x + ε · sign(∇ₓ J(θ, x, y))* [2].

This explanation also predicted *transferability*: models trained on overlapping data learn similar linear directions, so a perturbation aligned with one model's weight vector perturbs others' similarly [2]. The same paper introduced adversarial training, mixing FGSM examples into the training set with a weighted objective

*J̃(θ, x, y) = αJ(θ, x, y) + (1 − α)J(θ, x + ε·sign(∇ₓJ(θ, x, y)))*,

which reduced MNIST maxout error from 0.94% to 0.84% — a regularization effect, though one weaker than dropout at the time [2].

### The attack zoo

Subsequent work produced a rich taxonomy of attacks, summarized in Table 1. Madry et al. elevated PGD — iterated signed-gradient steps with random restarts inside the ℓ∞ ball — to the status of a *universal first-order adversary*, conjecturing that resistance to PGD implies resistance to essentially all first-order attacks [3]. Carlini and Wagner introduced minimum-distortion attacks for ℓ₀, ℓ₂, and ℓ∞ norms, removing the box constraint via the change of variables *x + δ = ½(tanh(w) + 1)*, optimizing a margin loss on pre-softmax logits

*f(x′) = max(maxᵢ≠ₜ Z(x′)ᵢ − Z(x′)ₜ, −κ)*,

and tuning the distortion–success tradeoff *c* per example by binary search; their attacks defeated defensive distillation with nearly the perturbation cost of attacking an undefended model, proving distillation had merely flattened gradients without moving the decision boundary [4]. Fawzi, Moosavi-Dezfooli, and Frossard provided early theoretical analysis, showing fundamental upper bounds on robustness for linear and quadratic classifiers and a sharp distinction between robustness to random noise and to adversarial perturbations — differing by a factor proportional to *√d*, a striking explanation of why high-dimensional models are fragile [7-adjacent theory; see [6] for the empirical complement].

| Attack | Norms | Iterations | Key idea |
|---|---|---|---|
| L-BFGS [1] | ℓ₂ | ~100s | Minimum-norm targeted solve, box-constrained |
| FGSM [2] | ℓ∞ | 1 | Linearized loss, sign of input gradient |
| PGD [3] | ℓ∞, ℓ₂ | 10–100 | Iterated FGSM + random restarts; universal first-order |
| C&W [4] | l0, l2, l-infinity | ~1000s | Logit-margin objective, tanh change of variables, per-example *c* search |
| DeepFool | ℓ₂ | few | Linearize decision boundary, step to nearest class |
| Square Attack | ℓ∞, ℓ₂ | 1000s | Gradient-free random search; black-box |

*Table 1: A taxonomy of canonical attack algorithms, ordered by increasing optimization sophistication.*

## 3 Methodology

### The minimax formulation of defense

Madry et al. reframed the entire attack–defense problem as a single saddle-point optimization [3]:

> **Theorem (Saddle-point formulation [3]).** Adversarially robust learning is the problem
>
> *min_θ ρ(θ),  ρ(θ) = 𝔼_{(x,y)∼𝒟}[ max_{δ∈𝒮} L(θ, x + δ, y) ]*,
>
> where *𝒮* is the perturbation set (typically *{δ : ‖δ‖∞ ≤ ε}*). The inner maximization is the attack; the outer minimization is the defense.

The inner problem is solved by PGD: from a random start in *𝒮*, iterate

*δ⁽ᵗ⁺¹⁾ = Π_𝒮( δ⁽ᵗ⁾ + α · sign(∇_δ L(θ, x + δ⁽ᵗ⁾, y)) )*,

with projection implemented as two clamps — onto *[−ε, ε]ᵈ* and onto the valid pixel range [3]. The random start is essential: different starts reach comparably high-loss points, which licenses treating PGD's output as a reliable worst-case estimate despite the non-convex inner landscape, and distinguishes PGD from deterministic FGSM [3]. The outer loop is ordinary SGD on the adversarial loss.

### Why most empirical defenses fail: obfuscated gradients

A defense that appears robust against PGD may only be *hiding* from it. Athalye, Carlini, and Wagner identified three forms of **obfuscated gradients** — gradient shattering (non-differentiable operations that break the gradient signal), stochastic gradients (randomization that makes single-sample gradients uninformative), and vanishing/exploding gradients (deep or recurrent computation that destroys the signal) — and developed counter-techniques for each: Backward Pass Differentiable Approximation (BPDA), Expectation Over Transformation (EOT), and reparameterization, respectively [5].

Their case study of ICLR 2018 defenses remains the canonical warning: of the non-certified white-box defenses, seven of nine relied on obfuscated gradients, and the adapted attacks circumvented six completely and one partially *under each paper's original threat model* [5]. The methodological moral is severe: a defense must be evaluated against **adaptive attacks** — attacks designed with full knowledge of the defense — because any fixed evaluation can be silently defeated by a mechanism that merely obscures gradients rather than constraining the decision boundary [4], [5].

### Lipschitz constraints and verification

A parallel line of work bounds robustness *a priori* by constraining the network's Lipschitz constant. If *f* is *L*-Lipschitz in the ℓ₂ norm and the margin between the top two logits exceeds *√2·L·ε*, no ℓ₂ perturbation of radius *ε* can change the prediction. Training with spectral normalization, Parseval constraints, or interval bound propagation (IBP) — which propagates axis-aligned boxes through the network to over-approximate the reachable output set — yields deterministic certificates at the cost of significant clean-accuracy degradation on large-scale datasets.

```python
import torch

def pgd_attack(model, x, y, eps=8/255, alpha=2/255, steps=20):
    """Universal first-order adversary (Madry et al. [3])."""
    delta = torch.empty_like(x).uniform_(-eps, eps)
    for _ in range(steps):
        delta.requires_grad_(True)
        loss = torch.nn.functional.cross_entropy(model(x + delta), y)
        grad = torch.autograd.grad(loss, delta)[0]
        with torch.no_grad():
            delta = delta + alpha * grad.sign()
            delta = torch.clamp(delta, -eps, eps)
            delta = torch.clamp(x + delta, 0, 1) - x
    return (x + delta).detach()
```

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Randomized smoothing: a tight ℓ₂ certificate

Randomized smoothing, developed by Cohen, Rosenfeld, and Kolter, turns *any* classifier *f* that classifies well under Gaussian noise into a smoothed classifier *g* with a provable ℓ₂ robustness guarantee [6]. The construction is disarmingly simple:

*g(x) = argmax_c ℙ_{δ∼𝒩(0,σ²I)}[ f(x + δ) = c ]*.

If, under the noise distribution, the top class *c_A* is returned with probability *p_A* and the runner-up with at most *p_B*, then *g*'s prediction is constant within an ℓ₂ ball whose radius is given by the tight bound [6]:

> **Theorem (Cohen et al. [6]).** Let *Φ⁻¹* be the inverse CDF of the standard normal. Then *g(x + δ) = c_A* for all *‖δ‖₂ < R*, where
>
> *R = (σ/2)·(Φ⁻¹(p_A) − Φ⁻¹(p_B))*,
>
> and this radius is *tight*: no larger radius can be certified from *(p_A, p_B)* alone.

The proof applies the Neyman–Pearson lemma to the hypothesis-testing problem of distinguishing *𝒩(x, σ²I)* from *𝒩(x + δ, σ²I)*; the worst case over all measurable base classifiers consistent with the observed probabilities is attained by a half-space classifier, yielding the Gaussian tail probabilities [6]. Certification is practical: *p_A* and *p_B* are estimated by Monte Carlo sampling with Clopper–Pearson confidence bounds, and the guarantee holds with the stated confidence over the randomness of certification [6].

Empirically, this is the *only* certified defense demonstrated at ImageNet scale: Cohen et al. trained a ResNet-50 under Gaussian augmentation and obtained 49% certified top-1 accuracy under ℓ₂ perturbations of radius 0.5 (= 127/255), where prior certified methods could not scale at all [6]. On CIFAR-10, smoothing delivered higher certified accuracies than competing deterministic approaches, at the cost of clean accuracy that declines as the noise level *σ* grows — the central certified-robustness tradeoff.

### 4.2 The geometry of the certified radius

The certificate's geometry is instructive. *R* grows linearly in *σ* but only logarithmically in the probability margin, so large certificates require the base classifier to be overwhelmingly confident under noise — effectively demanding that the decision boundary lie far from *x* in ℓ₂ distance. In high dimensions, Gaussian noise concentrates on a thin shell of radius *σ√d*, so the smoothed classifier's decision depends on an average over a vast annulus: robustness is purchased by *blurring* the decision function until the blurred boundary is far from typical inputs [6].

This reveals a deep connection to Lipschitz analysis. Salman et al. showed that the smoothed classifier is Lipschitz with constant *√(2/π)/σ*, so the certificate can equivalently be derived from the margin divided by the Lipschitz constant — the two views coincide [6-cited follow-up]. The choice of noise distribution determines the norm: Gaussian noise induces ℓ₂ certificates, while uniform noise on the ℓ∞ cube induces ℓ∞ certificates, and no single noise distribution certifies all norms simultaneously. The geometry of the *attack surface* — which directions from *x* reach the boundary first — thus dictates which smoothing distribution is appropriate, and an attacker aware of the certificate simply operates in the norm the defender did not certify.

### 4.3 The robustness–accuracy tradeoff: robust versus non-robust features

Tsipras, Santurkar, Engstrom, Turner, and Madry proved that the tension between robustness and accuracy can be *inherent*, not merely an artifact of training [7]. In a simple distribution where one feature is robust but weakly predictive and *d − 1* features are non-robust but strongly predictive in aggregate, any classifier achieving high standard accuracy must rely on the non-robust features and is therefore vulnerable; the robust classifier sacrifices accuracy [7].

> **Proposition (Tsipras et al. [7]).** There exist natural data distributions on which no classifier simultaneously achieves high standard accuracy and high adversarial accuracy. The tradeoff is information-theoretic, not algorithmic.

The paper's deeper contribution is representational: robust models learn *fundamentally different features* than standard models — features that align with salient, human-perceptible structure. Adversarial training yields models with interpretable gradients, and maximizing a robust model's class score produces GAN-like feature visualizations without any generative machinery [7]. The tradeoff is therefore a statement about *which* features a learner is allowed to use: standard training exploits brittle high-frequency correlations that are predictive but manipulable, while robust training is forced onto the sparse set of features that survive worst-case perturbation.

### 4.4 Adaptive attacks, detection, and the limits of verification

The arms race did not end with certificates. Adaptive attackers respond to randomized smoothing with *smoothed attacks* — optimizing the expected loss under the noise distribution via EOT — and Salman et al. showed that adversarially training the *base* classifier against such smoothed attacks substantially improves the certified accuracy of the resulting smoothed classifier [6-cited]. Detection-based defenses, which flag adversarial inputs rather than classifying them correctly, face an even harsher standard: an adaptive attacker jointly optimizes misclassification *and* detector evasion, and detectors without certificates provide no more guarantee than the classifiers they monitor [5].

Verification methods — exact or sound over-approximation of the network's reachable set via MILP, SMT, or abstract interpretation — give deterministic guarantees but scale poorly: complete verification is NP-hard in network size, and IBP-style incomplete verifiers pay a steep price in certified accuracy on deep networks. The practical hierarchy is therefore clear: *empirical* defenses (adversarial training) give the best robust accuracy against known attacks but no guarantees; *randomized* certificates give probabilistic guarantees that scale to ImageNet; *deterministic* verification gives absolute guarantees that do not scale [3], [5], [6].

## 5 Empirical Results / Proofs

We consolidate the quantitative record reported across the cited literature.

**Attack effectiveness.** Carlini and Wagner's ℓ₂ attack finds adversarial examples 2–10× closer than prior methods and breaks defensive distillation with 100% success at nearly the perturbation cost of attacking an undefended network [4]. PGD with random restarts reliably estimates the worst case of the inner maximization: on CIFAR-10, a naturally trained ResNet drops from ~95% clean accuracy to ~0% under 20-step PGD at *ε = 8/255*, while a PGD-adversarially trained model retains ~45–50% robust accuracy at the same budget [3].

**Certified performance.** Randomized smoothing on ImageNet: 49% certified top-1 accuracy at ℓ₂ radius 0.5 with *σ = 0.5*, the first and still canonical certified ImageNet result [6]. On CIFAR-10 with *σ = 0.25*, smoothing certifies roughly 60–70% accuracy at radius 0.25–0.5, exceeding IBP-based deterministic certificates on the same architecture [6]. The clean-accuracy cost is monotone in *σ*: each doubling of the noise level buys certificate radius at a cost of several points of standard accuracy.

**The tradeoff, quantified.** Tsipras et al. report that adversarial training on CIFAR-10 at *ε = 8/255* reduces clean accuracy from ~95% to ~85% while raising robust accuracy from ~0% to ~50% — a ten-point tax for robustness [7]. Their synthetic construction proves the gap can be made arbitrarily large in principle, though subsequent work (e.g., on *r*-separated data) argues the tradeoff is not intrinsic to real image distributions, only to the features current training procedures extract [7].

**Proof sketch of the smoothing certificate.** The core is a reduction to hypothesis testing. Suppose *g(x) = c_A*. For any *δ*, distinguishing *𝒩(x, σ²I)* from *𝒩(x+δ, σ²I)* with a test derived from *f* is hardest when *f* is a half-space indicator — by Neyman–Pearson, the likelihood-ratio test is optimal, and for two Gaussians the worst-case class probabilities under perturbation *δ* are *Φ(Φ⁻¹(p_A) − ‖δ‖₂/σ)* and *Φ(Φ⁻¹(p_B) + ‖δ‖₂/σ)*. The smoothed prediction flips only when the second exceeds the first, which rearranges to *‖δ‖₂ ≥ (σ/2)(Φ⁻¹(p_A) − Φ⁻¹(p_B))*, proving tightness: a half-space base classifier attains equality [6].

## 6 Limitations

Certified robustness remains narrow in three dimensions. **Norm specificity:** certificates are stated for a single ℓₚ ball, yet real adversaries are not norm-constrained — semantic perturbations (rotations, lighting, patch attacks) lie outside every ℓₚ threat model, and a defense certified in ℓ₂ may be trivially broken in ℓ∞ [5], [6]. **Scale:** deterministic verification does not reach ImageNet-scale networks, and even randomized smoothing's Monte Carlo certification costs ~10⁴–10⁵ forward passes per input, making deployment-time certification expensive [6]. **The accuracy tax:** every certified method to date sacrifices significant clean accuracy, and the provable tradeoff of [7] suggests this is partly fundamental — though the *r*-separation critique shows the inevitability claim depends on distributional assumptions that real data may violate.

Further, the threat model itself is idealized: white-box access, fixed *ε*, and a static classifier. Real deployments face *adaptive, sequential* adversaries who probe, learn the defense, and shift distributions over time. Detection, ensembling, and randomized defenses without certificates remain valuable heuristically but, per [5], must never be mistaken for guarantees.

## 7 Conclusion

Adversarial robustness has matured from an empirical curiosity [1], [2] into a discipline with theorems. The minimax formulation [3] gives adversarial training a principled objective; the obfuscated-gradients analysis [5] gives the field an immune system against false security; randomized smoothing [6] gives the first scalable certificates; and the robustness–accuracy analysis [7] gives the enterprise its fundamental limit. The unifying object is the geometry of the attack surface: how far, and in which directions, the decision boundary lies from the data. Attacks are algorithms for *finding* the nearest boundary; defenses are procedures for *moving* it; certificates are proofs of *how far* it has moved. The central open question is whether we can learn classifiers whose boundaries are simultaneously far in all relevant geometries and aligned with the features humans use — or whether, as [7] suggests, the features that make deep learning powerful are inseparable from the fragility they induce.
