# The Free Energy Principle: Predictive Coding, Active Inference, and the Variational Bayesian Mechanics of the Brain

## Abstract

The free energy principle (FEP) holds that any self-organizing system that persists over time must minimize the long-term average of surprise — the negative log-probability of its sensory exchanges with the world — or, equivalently, minimize an upper bound on surprise known as variational free energy. This thesis develops the principle from its information-theoretic foundations through its three great operationalizations: predictive coding, in which cortical hierarchies infer hidden causes of sensation by propagating precision-weighted prediction errors; active inference, in which action itself is cast as inference over policies evaluated by expected free energy; and the neural process theories that map these computations onto canonical cortical microcircuits and belief-propagation message passing on factor graphs. We review empirical support from electrophysiology, neuroimaging, and computational psychiatry, derive the core decomposition of free energy into accuracy and complexity terms, and confront the framework's most serious objections: the dark-room problem, worries about falsifiability, and the charge that the principle's universality empties it of empirical content.

## 1 Intro

Few proposals in theoretical neuroscience have been as ambitious — or as contested — as the **free energy principle** introduced by Karl Friston [1]. Its central claim is disarmingly simple: any system that maintains itself as a bounded, persisting entity in a fluctuating environment must occupy a restricted set of states with high probability, and therefore must resist the dispersive tendency of its own dynamics. Cast in probabilistic terms, the system must minimize the *surprise* associated with its sensory states — that is, the negative logarithm of the probability of its exchanges with the world, evaluated under the model of how the world generates those exchanges.

The conceptual lineage of this idea runs deep. Helmholtz's doctrine of *unconscious inference* framed perception as the brain's solution to an inverse problem: sensory data underdetermine their causes, so the nervous system must invert a generative model of how causes produce sensations. The Helmholtz machine of Dayan, Hinton, and colleagues mechanized this inversion with separate recognition and generative networks, bound together by minimizing a variational free energy [1]. Friston's radical move was to claim that this free-energy minimization is not merely a useful algorithmic trick for perceptual learning, but the **unifying objective** of action, perception, and learning across the entire nervous system — and indeed, of any self-organizing system that endures.

Section 2 establishes the background (variational inference, Markov blankets, predictive coding [2]); Section 3 the methodology (the free-energy functional and the Laplace approximation [4]); Section 4 the four pillars — Markov blankets, the accuracy–complexity decomposition, hierarchical message passing [3][7], and active inference [6]; Sections 5–7 the evidence, the critics, and the conclusions.

> Theorem: *Any ergodic random dynamical system that possesses a Markov blanket — i.e., a set of states rendering its internal and external states conditionally independent — will appear to engage in active inference, minimizing a variational free energy bound on the surprise of its blanket states.*

This is the principle's boldest assertion: that Bayesian mechanics is not an optional modeling choice but a description of *what it is* to be a persisting thing [1][5].

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The Bayesian brain hypothesis

The Bayesian brain hypothesis holds that the nervous system maintains *beliefs* — probability distributions — over the hidden causes of its sensations, and updates them in approximately Bayes-optimal fashion. Perception is then not a passive registration of stimuli but an **inferential** process: the brain explains away sensory input by finding the most probable configuration of hidden states under its generative model. What distinguishes the FEP from generic Bayesian-brain storytelling is its insistence on a single, tractable objective function that governs inference, learning, and action jointly.

### 2.2 Surprise, entropy, and self-organization

Let the internal states of an organism be denoted $\mu$ and its sensory (blanket) states $s$. *Surprise* is defined as $-\ln p(s \mid m)$, the negative log-evidence of the sensory data under the organism's generative model $m$. The time-average of surprise is the **entropy** of the sensory states. A system that persists — a flame, a cell, a brain — visits only a tiny subset of its possible state space; it therefore has low sensory entropy, and must somehow suppress surprise. Because surprise itself cannot be evaluated directly (it requires integrating over all hidden causes), the organism instead minimizes a computable upper bound: variational free energy $F$ [1][4].

### 2.3 Historical roots

Three traditions converge in the FEP:

- **Helmholtzian unconscious inference**: perception as inverse modeling of the causes of sensation.
- **Predictive coding** (Rao & Ballard, 1999) [2]: cortical hierarchies in which feedback connections carry predictions of lower-level activity and feedforward connections carry the residual prediction errors — a scheme that accounted for extra-classical receptive-field phenomena such as endstopping in early visual cortex.
- **Variational Bayes and the Helmholtz machine**: approximating intractable posteriors with a recognition density $q$ and minimizing the KL divergence between $q$ and the true posterior, which is exactly minimizing free energy [1][4].

### 2.4 Markov blankets

A *Markov blanket* is a set of variables that renders two other sets conditionally independent: given the blanket, external states $\eta$ and internal states $\mu$ are independent, $p(\eta, \mu \mid b) = p(\eta \mid b)\, p(\mu \mid b)$. In the FEP, the blanket partitions into *sensory states* (which influence but are not influenced by internal states) and *active states* (which influence but are not influenced by external states). The blanket is what makes an organism *an organism*: it defines the boundary across which inference and action are coupled [1][5].

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 The generative model and the variational density

The FEP methodology begins by writing down a **generative model** $p(s, \vartheta \mid m)$ of how sensory states $s$ are generated by hidden causes $\vartheta$ (states, parameters, and model structure). Perception corresponds to inverting this model to obtain the posterior $p(\vartheta \mid s, m)$. Because exact inversion is generally intractable, we introduce a **variational density** $q(\vartheta; \mu)$ parameterized by the system's internal states $\mu$, and optimize $\mu$ so that $q$ approximates the true posterior. Under the Laplace approximation, $q$ is Gaussian, with its mean and covariance encoded by neural activity and synaptic efficacy [4].

### 3.2 The free-energy functional

Variational free energy is defined as the expected log-ratio of the variational density to the joint density:

$$F(s, \mu) = E_{q}[\ln q(\vartheta; \mu) - \ln p(s, \vartheta \mid m)] \geq -\ln p(s \mid m).$$

$F$ is an upper bound on surprise. Minimizing $F$ with respect to $\mu$ therefore minimizes surprise indirectly, and simultaneously drives $q$ toward the true posterior. The methodology of the FEP is, at its core, the methodology of **variational Bayesian inference implemented as a dynamical system**: internal states evolve along the negative gradient of $F$ [4][6].

The following Python sketch computes free energy for a simple Gaussian generative model, illustrating the two quantities that must be tracked — the prediction error and the complexity penalty:

```python
import numpy as np

def variational_free_energy(s, mu, g, dg, Pi_s, Pi_mu, eta):
    """Free energy for s ~ N(g(mu), Pi_s^-1), mu ~ N(eta, Pi_mu^-1).
    s: sensory input, mu: posterior mean, g: prediction function."""
    eps_s = s - g(mu)                      # sensory prediction error
    eps_mu = mu - eta                      # prior prediction error
    accuracy = 0.5 * eps_s @ Pi_s @ eps_s  # -E_q[ln p(s|mu)]
    complexity = 0.5 * eps_mu @ Pi_mu @ eps_mu  # KL[q || p(mu)]
    return accuracy + complexity

# gradient descent on F == precision-weighted prediction-error minimization
def infer(s, mu0, lr=0.01, steps=500):
    mu = mu0.copy()
    for _ in range(steps):
        grad = (dg(mu).T @ Pi_s @ (g(mu) - s)) + Pi_mu @ (mu - eta)
        mu -= lr * grad
    return mu
```

### 3.3 Precision as gain control

A crucial methodological innovation is the treatment of **precision** — inverse variance — as a first-class computational quantity. Prediction errors are weighted by their precision: reliable sensory channels drive belief updating strongly, while noisy ones are discounted. Neurally, precision is hypothesized to correspond to synaptic *gain* under neuromodulatory control [3][6]; attention is then the optimization of precision — turning up the volume on the prediction errors that matter.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Markov blankets: the boundary that makes inference possible

The Markov blanket is the formal device that converts a metaphysical intuition — *things exist* — into a probabilistic one. If internal states $\mu$ are conditionally independent of external states $\eta$ given blanket states $b = (s, a)$, then internal dynamics can only *track* external dynamics through the blanket. This has a profound consequence: for every blanket state there exists a conditional distribution over internal states, and — under ergodicity assumptions — a mapping from internal states to posterior beliefs about external states. The internal states *parameterize beliefs*; the system cannot help but look like it is performing inference [1][5].

The blanket also partitions action from perception: sensory states are children of external states, active states children of internal states, closing the perception–action loop. Critics dispute how much work the blanket does — whether the partition is discovered or imposed by the analyst [8] (see Section 6).

### 4.2 Free energy decomposed: accuracy minus complexity

The free-energy functional admits a canonical decomposition that reveals its dual nature as an inference *and* learning objective [4]:

> Theorem: *Variational free energy decomposes as*
> $$F = \underbrace{D_{KL}[q(\vartheta) \,\|\, p(\vartheta \mid m)]}_{\text{complexity}} - \underbrace{E_{q}[\ln p(s \mid \vartheta, m)]}_{\text{accuracy}}.$$ 
> *Minimizing $F$ therefore trades off fitting the data (accuracy) against staying close to prior beliefs (complexity) — exactly the bias–variance trade-off of Bayesian model selection.*

This decomposition explains why free-energy minimization performs **model selection** as a side effect: because complexity penalizes models whose posteriors diverge from their priors, minimizing $F$ maximizes (a lower bound on) Bayesian model evidence. Learning — updating parameters and model structure — is gradient descent on the same functional, operating on slower timescales than perceptual inference. Perception, learning, and model selection are thus three faces of one optimization [1][4][5].

| Component | Mathematical form | Neural interpretation | Timescale |
|---|---|---|---|
| Accuracy | $E_q[\ln p(s\mid\vartheta)]$ | Precision-weighted prediction-error minimization | Fast (perception) |
| Complexity | $D_{KL}[q(\vartheta)\|p(\vartheta)]$ | Regularization toward priors; synaptic homeostasis | Slow (learning) |
| Expected free energy | $E_q[\ln q(\vartheta\mid\pi) - \ln p(s,\vartheta\mid\pi)]$ | Policy evaluation; epistemic + pragmatic value | Planning |
| Precision | Inverse variance of prediction errors | Neuromodulatory gain control | Attentional |

### 4.3 Predictive coding as hierarchical message passing

Under Gaussian assumptions and the Laplace approximation, gradient descent on $F$ reduces to a remarkably simple and biologically suggestive scheme: **predictive coding** [2][3][4]. Each cortical level predicts the activity below; only *prediction errors* ascend via forward connections while predictions descend via backward connections, and the hierarchy relaxes toward the posterior mode by local message passing.

Bastos and colleagues [3] mapped this architecture onto the *canonical cortical microcircuit*: superficial pyramidal cells as prediction-error units, deep pyramidal cells as prediction units, and interneuron populations as precision-weighting machinery — with gamma-band activity ascending with errors and beta-band activity descending with predictions [3].

The message-passing perspective was generalized by Friston, Parr, and de Vries [7], who formulated neuronal processing as **belief propagation on factor graphs**. Using Forney-style factor graphs, they showed that discrete and continuous generative models mandate interoperable message-passing schedules on the same architecture, with thalamocortical loops implementing the model averaging that links them — the cortex as a *graphical brain* for variational message passing at multiple scales [7].

The following Haskell sketch expresses the variational fixed-point iteration as a pure function over belief states, emphasizing the message-passing character:

```haskell
-- Belief = (posterior mean, posterior precision)
type Belief = (Double, Double)

-- One predictive-coding update: prediction error, precision-weighted correction
pcUpdate :: (Double -> Double) -> Double -> Double -> Double -> Belief -> Belief
pcUpdate g s precSens precPrior (mu, _) =
    let epsS  = s - g mu                    -- sensory prediction error
        epsMu = mu - 0.0                    -- prior prediction error (eta = 0)
        dF    = -precSens * epsS + precPrior * epsMu  -- gradient of F
        mu'   = mu - 0.01 * dF              -- gradient descent on free energy
    in (mu', precSens + precPrior)

-- Iterating pcUpdate to convergence == variational message passing
inferBelief :: (Double -> Double) -> Double -> Double -> Double -> Belief -> Belief
inferBelief g s ps pp b0 = iterate (pcUpdate g s ps pp) b0 !! 500
```

### 4.4 Active inference: action as inference under expected free energy

The FEP's most distinctive move is its treatment of **action**. Rather than bolting a controller onto a perceptual inference engine, active inference treats policies (sequences of actions) as random variables to be inferred alongside hidden states. Action selection becomes posterior inference over policies, where policies are evaluated by their **expected free energy** $G(\pi)$ — the free energy expected under the outcomes a policy would produce [6].

Expected free energy decomposes into two interpretable terms:

1. **Epistemic value** (information gain): expected uncertainty reduction — *curiosity* and exploration with no ad hoc bonus.
2. **Pragmatic value** (expected utility): expected log-probability of preferred outcomes — goals as prior beliefs about the sensory states the organism *expects* to inhabit.

> Theorem: *Under active inference, the posterior over policies satisfies $q(\pi) \propto \exp(-G(\pi))$. Action selection is therefore softmax over negative expected free energy, unifying exploration and exploitation in a single Bayes-optimal rule* [6].

Motor control is thereby reinterpreted: descending signals are not *commands* but *proprioceptive predictions*, fulfilled by spinal reflexes minimizing proprioceptive prediction error [6]. The dark-room problem — why not seek the dark, silent room? — is answered by prior preferences: agents expect to occupy characteristic allostatic states (fed, hydrated, warm), and expected free energy drives them to realize those states. Whether this fully satisfies remains debated (Section 6).

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 The mathematical core

Three results anchor the framework's mathematical credibility [4][5]:

1. **The bound**: $F(s, \mu) \geq -\ln p(s \mid m)$, with equality when the variational density equals the true posterior. Free-energy minimization is therefore approximate Bayesian inference, with the approximation gap exactly the KL divergence between $q$ and the true posterior.
2. **The decomposition**: $F = \text{complexity} - \text{accuracy}$, which turns inference into simultaneous model fitting and model selection.
3. **The Lyapunov property**: Friston and colleagues [6] showed that variational free energy is a Lyapunov function for the neuronal dynamics derived from gradient descent on $F$ — the dynamics provably converge, and conform to Hamilton's principle of least action.

Buckley et al. [5] provide the most careful mathematical review of the principle's foundations, making explicit the measure-theoretic and ergodicity assumptions on which the "Bayesian mechanics" of self-organizing systems rest, and clarifying exactly which claims are theorems and which are modeling choices.

### 5.2 Neural process theories and their predictions

The process theory of active inference [6] derives neuronal dynamics as gradient descent on $F$ under a Markov decision process generative model and reproduces, in simulation, a remarkable catalog of electrophysiological phenomena: repetition suppression, mismatch negativity, violation responses, place-cell activity, phase precession, theta sequences, theta–gamma coupling, evidence accumulation, and dopamine responses as precision signals. Each of these is a *qualitative* prediction of the message-passing architecture: e.g., mismatch negativity is simply the electrophysiological signature of a precision-weighted prediction error evoked by a violated prior.

The microcircuit mapping [3] predicts laminar-specific roles for prediction and error units, probed with laminar recordings and dynamic causal modeling; the belief-propagation formulation [7] predicts state-dependent effective connectivity — a connectome that reorganizes with inference itself, seen in attentional modulation of connectivity.

### 5.3 Computational psychiatry

A productive application is **computational psychiatry**: disorders as aberrant precision control. Schizophrenia becomes a failure of sensory-precision attenuation — hallucinations as overly precise sensory errors, delusions as compensatory high-level updates [1][6]; autism an imbalance toward sensory over prior precision. These generate differential predictions about dopaminergic and cholinergic interventions and hierarchical inference tasks in patients.

## 6 Limitations

No framework this ambitious escapes serious criticism, and the FEP has attracted some of the sharpest.

**Falsifiability.** The most persistent charge is that the principle is *unfalsifiable*: because any persisting system can be described as minimizing free energy under *some* generative model, the claim risks being true by definition. Defenders reply that falsifiability attaches not to the principle — a mathematical tautology about ergodic systems with Markov blankets, akin to least action — but to specific *process theories* making concrete predictions [5][6]. Critics counter that this two-level defense lets grand claims borrow prestige from modest ones.

**The dark-room problem.** If organisms minimize surprise, why don't they seek dark, silent rooms — or, in the limit, death, the state of minimal sensory surprise? The active-inference reply invokes prior preferences: agents expect to occupy characteristic, allostatic states, and expected free energy [6] drives them toward those states, with epistemic value mandating exploration. Skeptics note this shifts the burden onto the *content* of the priors, which the principle does not constrain.

**The Markov blanket as analyst's imposition.** Recent critique argues the FEP conflates logical categories — treating mathematical constructs (the variational density, the blanket) as physical mechanisms, smuggling the analyst's partition into the explanation itself [8]. If the blanket is imposed rather than discovered, the inferred inference may be the modeler's own.

**Biological plausibility gaps.** The neurocentric critique observes that much of the free-energy literature is divorced from biophysical reality: real neurons spike, adapt, and fatigue in ways the rate-coded, Gaussian, Laplace-approximated process theories abstract away [3][6]. The microcircuit mapping [3] narrows this gap, but laminar-resolved human confirmation remains limited, and non-predictive accounts of mismatch negativity (adaptation, deviance detection) are not ruled out.

**Scope of the Gaussian assumptions.** Almost all neural process theories derived from the FEP rely on the Laplace (Gaussian) approximation and conjugate, linearizable dynamics [4]. Brains are nonlinear, non-Gaussian, and nonstationary; the approximation error in realistic regimes is poorly characterized, and it is an open question how much of the framework's elegance survives contact with genuinely non-Gaussian inference.

## 7 Conclusion

The free energy principle offers something rare in neuroscience: a single mathematical object — variational free energy — from which perception as predictive coding [2][3], action as active inference [6], learning as model selection, and attention as precision optimization all follow as gradient flows. Its process theories have generated genuine, testable predictions about cortical microcircuitry, electrophysiological signatures of inference, and the computational anatomy of psychiatric disorders, and its mathematical foundations have been clarified to the point where the theorems and the modeling choices can be told apart [4][5][7].

Whether the principle is a deep law of self-organization or an elegant redescription remains, appropriately, an empirical question — one the framework equips us to ask with unusual precision. The Markov blanket tells us where the organism ends and the world begins; free energy tells us what the organism must do to remain itself; and predictive coding and active inference tell us, mechanistically, how a brain might do it. Even if the grandest metaphysical claims do not survive, the FEP will have earned its place as the most systematic attempt yet to write the Bayesian mechanics of the mind.
