# The Heckscher–Ohlin Model and the Factor-Proportions Theory of International Trade: Stolper–Samuelson, Rybczynski, and Factor-Price Equalization

## Abstract

The Heckscher–Ohlin model remains the canonical general-equilibrium account of *why* nations trade: not because of intrinsic technological gaps, as in Ricardo, but because countries differ in the relative abundance of the factors of production with which they are endowed. This thesis reconstructs the factor-proportions research program from first principles — Eli Heckscher's 1919 insight and Bertil Ohlin's 1933 formalization — and derives its three great comparative-statics theorems in a unified *hat-calculus* framework: the Stolper–Samuelson theorem linking goods prices to factor rewards with a striking magnification effect [1]; the Rybczynski theorem linking endowment shocks to biased output responses [2]; and Samuelson's factor-price equalization theorem, which shows that free commodity trade can substitute for factor mobility [3]. Each result is proved, illustrated geometrically, and verified computationally. The empirical record — from Leontief's 1953 paradox through Trefler's productivity-augmented rehabilitation [4] and Romalis's quasi–Heckscher–Ohlin tests [5] — is surveyed to delineate where the theory succeeds, where it fails, and why it endures as the organizing framework of trade theory.

## 1 Introduction

International economics asks two deceptively simple questions: *what* determines the pattern of trade between nations, and *who* gains or loses when borders open? David Ricardo answered the first with technology: countries export goods in which their labor is relatively most productive. The answer is elegant, empirically relevant, and incomplete — it says nothing about the second question, because in the Ricardian model there is only one factor of production and hence no distributional conflict.

The factor-proportions theory, originating with Eli Heckscher (1919) and developed by Bertil Ohlin (1933), replaces technological difference with *endowment* difference as the engine of comparative advantage: a capital-abundant country finds capital cheap, produces capital-intensive goods cheaply, and exports them. Trade is thus an *indirect exchange of factor services* — exporting aircraft and importing apparel implicitly exports abundant-factor services and imports scarce-factor services.

What elevates the model from intuition to science is the trio of theorems proved in its wake, each a masterpiece of comparative statics:

1. **Stolper–Samuelson (1941):** a rise in the relative price of a good raises the real return to the factor used intensively in that good *more than proportionally*, while lowering the other's real return [1].
2. **Rybczynski (1955):** at constant commodity prices, growth in one factor's endowment expands output of the good intensive in that factor *more than proportionally* and contracts the other sector absolutely [2].
3. **Factor-price equalization (Samuelson, 1948, 1949):** under stated conditions, free trade in goods alone equalizes factor prices across countries, even with complete factor immobility [3].

This thesis derives all three from a single mathematical apparatus, proves them, visualizes them, tests them numerically, and confronts them with seven decades of empirical evidence.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 From Ricardo to factor proportions

Ricardo's model explains trade by *technology*, but cannot explain distributional conflict — why unions and capitalists disagree about trade liberalization — because a single factor cannot be paid two different wages. Heckscher's 1919 paper supplied the missing machinery: with two factors, trade alters *relative* factor prices, creating winners and losers within each nation. Ohlin's 1933 *Interregional and International Trade* generalized this into a complete theory of production location.

### 2.2 The canonical 2x2x2 model

The workhorse is the **two-country, two-good, two-factor** model. Home and Foreign produce goods *X* and *Y* using labor (*L*) and capital (*K*) under constant returns to scale. The assumptions are collected in Table 1.

| # | Assumption | Role |
|---|-----------|------|
| A1 | Two goods, two factors, two countries; CRS technologies | Tractability; clean duality |
| A2 | Identical technologies across countries | Isolates endowments as the *sole* source of comparative advantage |
| A3 | No factor-intensity reversals (FIR) | Unambiguous ranking of goods by factor intensity at all factor prices |
| A4 | Perfect competition; full employment | Zero-profit pricing; factor-market clearing |
| A5 | Factors mobile domestically, immobile internationally | Domestic factor-price uniformity; cross-country differences persist |
| A6 | Identical homothetic preferences | Demand does not reverse the supply-driven pattern of trade |

*Table 1: The canonical assumptions of the 2×2×2 Heckscher–Ohlin model.*

Two definitions carry the entire theory:

> **Definition (Factor abundance):** Home is *labor-abundant* relative to Foreign if its endowment ratio exceeds Foreign's, that is, when the ratio of labor to capital at Home is larger than the corresponding ratio abroad. Abundance is strictly *relative* — a country can be small in absolute terms yet abundant in one factor.

> **Definition (Factor intensity):** Good *X* is *labor-intensive* relative to good *Y* if, at any common factor-price ratio, *X* production employs a higher labor–capital ratio than *Y* production. By assumption A3 this ranking never flips.

> **Theorem (Heckscher–Ohlin):** Each country exports the good that uses its relatively abundant factor intensively. The labor-abundant country exports the labor-intensive good; the capital-abundant country exports the capital-intensive good.

The proof (Section 5) runs through autarky prices: relative abundance depresses the pre-trade wage–rental ratio and hence the relative price of the labor-intensive good, which becomes the export good once trade opens [6].

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 The dual approach: unit costs and zero profits

The modern treatment, following Jones and Scheinkman [6], works with the *dual* of the production problem. Let *w* be the wage, *r* the rental rate of capital, and let *a_Lj(w, r)* and *a_Kj(w, r)* be the cost-minimizing unit input requirements for good *j*. With perfect competition, price equals minimum unit cost:

$$p_X = a_{LX}(w,r)\,w + a_{KX}(w,r)\,r, \qquad p_Y = a_{LY}(w,r)\,w + a_{KY}(w,r)\,r$$

These are the **zero-profit conditions**. Full employment of fixed endowments gives the **factor-market clearing conditions**:

$$a_{LX} X + a_{LY} Y = L, \qquad a_{KX} X + a_{KY} Y = K$$

### 3.2 Jones algebra: the hat calculus

All three theorems are comparative-statics results proved transparently in *hat calculus* ($\hat{z} = dz/z$). With cost shares $\theta_{Lj} = w a_{Lj}/p_j$ and employment shares $\lambda_{Lj} = a_{Lj}X_j/L$, differentiating the zero-profit conditions and invoking the envelope theorem yields the **price–cost system**:

$$\theta_{LX}\,\hat{w} + \theta_{KX}\,\hat{r} = \hat{p}_X, \qquad \theta_{LY}\,\hat{w} + \theta_{KY}\,\hat{r} = \hat{p}_Y \tag{1}$$

Differentiating the market-clearing conditions yields the **quantity–endowment system**:

$$\lambda_{LX}\,\hat{X} + \lambda_{LY}\,\hat{Y} = \hat{L}, \qquad \lambda_{KX}\,\hat{X} + \lambda_{KY}\,\hat{Y} = \hat{K} \tag{2}$$

Systems (1) and (2) are *duals*: each theorem inverts one share matrix. The determinants $|\theta|$ and $|\lambda|$ share the sign of the factor-intensity ranking, and every magnification effect is division by one of them — prices map to rewards exactly as endowments map to outputs [6].

### 3.3 Computational verification

Section 5 verifies the theorems numerically by solving systems (1) and (2) for calibrated share matrices, and four purpose-built diagrams supply the geometry.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Heckscher–Ohlin core: abundance, intensity, and the pattern of trade

Begin with autarky: in labor-abundant Home, relative labor plenty depresses the equilibrium *w/r* below Foreign's, which depresses the relative cost and autarky price of labor-intensive *X*. Once trade opens at an intermediate world price, Home expands *X* and contracts *Y* — the mechanism underlying every theorem that follows:

- **Endowment difference** → relative factor-price difference in autarky.
- **Factor-price difference** → relative goods-price difference in autarky.
- **Goods-price difference** → trade pattern once arbitrage is possible.

Trade in goods thus *substitutes* for trade in factors — the **factor-content** view formalized by Vanek into the Heckscher–Ohlin–Vanek equations: with *A* the input matrix, *T* net exports, *V* endowments, and *s* the spending share, $A\,T = V - s\,V^{world}$, so each country is a net exporter of its abundant factors' services [4].

*Figure 1 (Edgeworth box):* the fixed endowment is allocated between the two industries in an Edgeworth box of dimensions *L* by *K*; the contract curve traces efficient allocations and the labor-abundant country's endowment point pins down its autarky factor-price ratio.

### 4.2 The Stolper–Samuelson theorem: commodity prices and the magnification of factor rewards

In 1941, Wolfgang Stolper and Paul Samuelson asked a question with immediate policy bite: *who gains, in real terms, from a tariff?* Their answer overturned the presumption that protection helps everyone in the protected economy, or even everyone in the protected industry [1].

> **Theorem (Stolper–Samuelson):** Let good *X* be labor-intensive. Then a rise in the relative price *p_X/p_Y*, holding factor endowments fixed, raises the real wage *w* and lowers the real rental *r* — where "real" means measured in terms of *either* good. Moreover, the effect is **magnified**: the proportional wage change exceeds the proportional price change that caused it.

*Proof via Jones algebra.* Subtract the two equations of system (1):

$$(\theta_{LX} - \theta_{LY})\,\hat{w} + (\theta_{KX} - \theta_{KY})\,\hat{r} = \hat{p}_X - \hat{p}_Y$$

Since shares sum to one, $\theta_{KX} - \theta_{KY} = -(\theta_{LX} - \theta_{LY})$, and with $|\theta| = \theta_{LX} - \theta_{LY} > 0$:

$$\hat{w} - \hat{r} = \frac{\hat{p}_X - \hat{p}_Y}{|\theta|} \tag{3}$$

Because $|\theta| < 1$, the wage–rental ratio moves *more than proportionally* to the commodity-price ratio — the **magnification effect**. To see that the wage rises in terms of both goods, note from (1) that $\hat{p}_X$ is a weighted average of $\hat{w}$ and $\hat{r}$; since $\hat{w} > \hat{r}$, we must have $\hat{w} > \hat{p}_X > \hat{p}_Y > \hat{r}$. The real wage *w/p_X* rises and *w/p_Y* rises, while *r/p_X* and *r/p_Y* both fall. ∎

The economics: a tariff on the labor-intensive import raises its domestic price; the expanding sector bids workers away from the other; both sectors substitute toward capital as labor grows dearer; and the wage is bid up by more than the initiating price rise. Protection is thus *factional* — it enriches the scarce factor at the abundant factor's expense, in real terms.

*Figure 2 (Lerner diagram):* unit-value isoquants with two unit-isocost lines; a rise in *p_X* shifts the *X* isoquant inward, pivoting the common tangent steeper — the wage–rental ratio rises by more than the price ratio.

### 4.3 The Rybczynski theorem: endowments, outputs, and biased growth

Tadeusz Rybczynski's 1955 *Economica* paper is the exact dual of Stolper–Samuelson: where they shocked prices and read off factor rewards, he shocked endowments and read off outputs [2].

> **Theorem (Rybczynski):** Let good *X* be labor-intensive and hold commodity prices (hence factor prices) fixed. Then an increase in the labor endowment raises output of *X* *more than proportionally* and *reduces* output of *Y* in absolute terms.

*Proof.* From system (2), by the same algebra:

$$\hat{X} - \hat{Y} = \frac{\hat{L} - \hat{K}}{|\lambda|}, \qquad |\lambda| = \lambda_{LX} - \lambda_{KX} > 0 \tag{4}$$

With $\hat{K} = 0$ and $\hat{L} > 0$, we get $\hat{X} - \hat{Y} > \hat{L}$, and since $\hat{L}$ is a weighted average of $\hat{X}$ and $\hat{Y}$, it follows that $\hat{X} > \hat{L} > 0 > \hat{Y}$. ∎

The intuition: at *unchanged* factor prices (hence unchanged techniques), the extra labor can only be absorbed by expanding the labor-intensive sector — which also needs some capital, obtainable only by *shrinking* the capital-intensive sector. Growth is therefore **biased**. The theorem underpins the *Dutch disease*, immigration economics, and development strategy alike [5].

*Figure 3 (Rybczynski expansion):* the kinked PPF with linear labor and capital constraints; an outward shift of the labor constraint moves production from point *A* to *B*, expanding the labor-intensive good while contracting the other.

### 4.4 Factor-price equalization: the Lerner diagram and the diversification cone

Samuelson's 1948 and 1949 papers delivered the program's most startling claim: under free trade, *w* and *r* can be equalized across countries even though labor and capital never cross a border [3].

> **Theorem (Factor-price equalization):** If both countries share the same technology, produce both goods (no complete specialization), and there are no factor-intensity reversals, then free trade in goods equalizes factor prices internationally.

*Proof sketch.* Under trade both countries face the same $(p_X, p_Y)$, and system (1) maps any goods-price vector to a *unique* $(w, r)$ — two equations, two unknowns, nonzero determinant by A3. Same system, same right-hand side, same factor prices. ∎

Geometrically, each good's unit-value isoquant is tangent to a common unit-isocost line in the **Lerner diagram**. As long as both endowment rays lie inside the **diversification cone** — endowment ratios consistent with producing both goods — the common tangent coincides and factor prices equalize; endowments outside the cone force specialization and break equalization. Trade and factor mobility are thus *substitutes* (Mundell, 1957).

*Figure 4 (equalization):* two endowment points inside a shared diversification cone, tangent to the same unit-isocost line — one factor-price vector, two output bundles.

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 Analytical proof of the Heckscher–Ohlin pattern

The H-O pattern follows from autarky prices: the labor-abundant country's lower *w/r* lowers the relative unit cost of the labor-intensive good — $\partial \ln(c_X/c_Y)/\partial \ln(w/r) = \theta_{LX}-\theta_{LY} > 0$ — so its autarky relative price of *X* is lower, and trade at an intermediate world price makes Home a net exporter of *X*.

### 5.2 Computational verification of the magnification effects

The two magnification theorems are verified numerically for calibrated share matrices. Good *X* is labor-intensive ($\theta_{LX} = 0.7$); the simulation shocks the price of *X* by 10 percent (Stolper–Samuelson) and the labor endowment by 10 percent (Rybczynski), then checks the orderings.

```python
import numpy as np

# Cost shares: rows = goods (X labor-intensive, Y capital-intensive), cols = (L, K)
theta = np.array([[0.7, 0.3],
                  [0.3, 0.7]])
# Employment shares: rows = factors (L, K), cols = goods (X, Y)
lam = np.array([[0.6, 0.4],
                [0.3, 0.7]])

def stolper_samuelson(p_hat):
    """Solve theta @ [w_hat, r_hat] = p_hat. Returns proportional factor-price changes."""
    w_hat, r_hat = np.linalg.solve(theta, p_hat)
    return w_hat, r_hat

def rybczynski(v_hat):
    """Solve lam @ [X_hat, Y_hat] = v_hat. Returns proportional output changes."""
    X_hat, Y_hat = np.linalg.solve(lam, v_hat)
    return X_hat, Y_hat

# Experiment 1: price of labor-intensive good X rises 10%, price of Y flat
w_hat, r_hat = stolper_samuelson(np.array([0.10, 0.00]))
print(f"Stolper-Samuelson: w_hat={w_hat:.4f}, r_hat={r_hat:.4f}")
assert w_hat > 0.10 > 0.00 > r_hat, "magnification ordering violated"

# Experiment 2: labor endowment rises 10%, capital fixed, prices constant
X_hat, Y_hat = rybczynski(np.array([0.10, 0.00]))
print(f"Rybczynski:       X_hat={X_hat:.4f}, Y_hat={Y_hat:.4f}")
assert X_hat > 0.10 > 0.00 > Y_hat, "magnification ordering violated"

print("Both magnification orderings hold: w_hat > pX_hat > pY_hat > r_hat, "
      "X_hat > L_hat > K_hat > Y_hat.")
```

Output: `w_hat=0.1750, r_hat=-0.0750` and `X_hat=0.2333, Y_hat=-0.1000`. Both orderings,

$$\hat{w} > \hat{p}_X > \hat{p}_Y > \hat{r}, \qquad \hat{X} > \hat{L} > \hat{K} > \hat{Y},$$

hold exactly, with magnification factors $1/|\theta| = 2.5$ and $1/|\lambda| \approx 3.33$ quantifying the amplification.

The same duality can be expressed functionally in Haskell, emphasizing that each theorem is matrix inversion against a share matrix:

```haskell
-- | Magnification factor: inverse determinant of a 2x2 share matrix.
magnification :: Double -> Double -> Double -> Double -> Double
magnification a b c d = 1.0 / (a * d - b * c)

-- | Stolper-Samuelson: factor-price response to a goods-price shock.
--   thetaLX, thetaKX, thetaLY, thetaKY are cost shares; pX, pY are price shocks.
stolperSamuelson :: (Double, Double, Double, Double) -> (Double, Double) -> (Double, Double)
stolperSamuelson (tLX, tKX, tLY, tKY) (pX, pY) =
  let m = magnification tLX tKX tLY tKY
      w = m * (tKY * pX - tKX * pY)
      r = m * (tLX * pY - tLY * pX)
  in (w, r)

main :: IO ()
main = do
  let (w, r) = stolperSamuelson (0.7, 0.3, 0.3, 0.7) (0.10, 0.00)
  putStrLn ("w_hat = " ++ show w ++ ", r_hat = " ++ show r)
```

### 5.3 The empirical record

The theory's predictions are sharp — and the data have been correspondingly unforgiving.

1. **The Leontief paradox (1953).** Leontief's input–output analysis of 1947 U.S. trade found exports *less* capital-intensive than import-competing production. Resolutions: human vs. physical capital (exports were skill-intensive), natural-resource bias in imports, and industry-level factor-intensity reversals.
2. **Heckscher–Ohlin–Vanek tests.** Bowen, Leamer, and Sveikauskas (1987) tested $A\,T = V - sV^{world}$ across countries and factors and rejected it decisively: measured factor contents correlated weakly with predictions.
3. **Trefler's rehabilitation (1993, 1995).** Allowing *factor-augmenting international productivity differences* — endowments in efficiency units — rescues HOV: the modified model explains much of the factor content of trade and cross-country factor-price variation, vindicating Leontief's claim that the 1947 United States was labor-abundant in productivity-equivalent workers [4]. His 1995 "missing trade" paper showed measured factor trade an order of magnitude below prediction, attributing the gap to home bias, technology gaps, and Armington preferences.
4. **Quasi–Heckscher–Ohlin evidence.** Romalis (2004) integrated a many-country H-O model with monopolistic competition and transport costs: countries capture larger world shares of goods intensive in their abundant factors, and rapid factor accumulation shifts export structure toward intensive industries — with direct evidence of quasi–Stolper–Samuelson and quasi–Rybczynski effects [5].

| Episode | Verdict on factor-proportions theory |
|---------|--------------------------------------|
| Leontief (1953), U.S. 1947 | Paradox: raw H-O prediction rejected |
| Human-capital reinterpretation | Paradox substantially resolved |
| HOV sign tests (Bowen–Leamer–Sveikauskas 1987) | Strict HOV rejected |
| Trefler (1993) productivity adjustment | HOV rehabilitated in efficiency units |
| Trefler (1995) missing trade | Factor trade far smaller than predicted; bias identified |
| Romalis (2004) quasi-HO / quasi-Rybczynski | Core comparative statics strongly supported |

*Table 2: Six decades of empirical verdicts on the factor-proportions program.*

The pattern is characteristic: the *strict* 2×2×2 predictions fail, while the *mechanisms* — magnification, biased growth, factor-content logic — survive every serious test and reappear in modern quantitative trade models.

## 6 Limitations

Intellectual honesty requires stating where the edifice cracks:

- **Factor-intensity reversals.** If *X* is labor-intensive at low wages but capital-intensive at high wages, the goods-price/factor-price mapping breaks, the H-O pattern can reverse, and multiple equilibria emerge (Minhas, 1962).
- **Identical technology.** Assumption A2 is the theory's most heroic. Trefler's work shows productivity differences are first-order; once admitted, "abundance" must be measured in efficiency units, blurring the clean endowment story.
- **More goods than factors.** With many goods and two factors, the theory predicts *correlation* patterns (Deardorff's comvariance results) rather than sharp good-by-good predictions.
- **Zero trade costs and nontraded goods.** Transport costs, tariffs, and nontraded sectors shrink the diversification cone and weaken equalization — Davis and Weinstein (2001) show the HOV model performs markedly better once these are modeled.
- **Within-product specialization.** Schott (2003, 2004) documents that rich and poor countries export very different *varieties* within the same product codes, so industry-level factor intensities mismeasure true specialization.
- **Distributional bluntness.** Stolper–Samuelson assumes frictionless domestic factor mobility; with sector-specific human capital, trade's losers are concentrated workers, not "labor" as an aggregate.

None of these limitations overturns the core logic; they circumscribe its domain. The theorems remain the benchmark against which every richer model is measured.

## 7 Conclusion

The factor-proportions program is one of economics' great unifications: from two share matrices and the envelope theorem flow the *pattern* of trade (Heckscher–Ohlin), the *distributional* consequences of price shocks (Stolper–Samuelson), the *structural* consequences of growth (Rybczynski), and the *substitutability* of trade and factor mobility (factor-price equalization) — four faces of one mathematical structure, in which prices map to rewards exactly as endowments map to outputs.

Empirically the theory survived by evolving: from the stark 2×2×2 model Leontief embarrassed, through Trefler's productivity-augmented HOV framework, to Romalis's quantitative quasi–Heckscher–Ohlin models. The enduring policy lessons: protection is redistribution disguised as national interest, growth is never sector-neutral, and open goods markets can do the work of open borders. A century after Heckscher, factor proportions remain the grammar of trade's political economy.
