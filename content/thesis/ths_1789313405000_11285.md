# Succinct Verifiable Computation at Scale: A Comparative Treatise on zk-SNARKs, STARKs, Plonkish Arithmetization, and Recursive Proof Composition

## Abstract

Zero-knowledge succinct arguments have matured from asymptotic curiosities into the load-bearing machinery of verifiable computation, underpinning rollups, private identity, and auditable machine learning. This treatise surveys the three dominant proof families — Groth16 zk-SNARKs, the universal PLONK proof system instantiated with Kate-Zaverucha-Goldberg polynomial commitments, and hash-based STARKs built on the FRI low-degree test — together with Plonkish arithmetization, recursive proof composition, and the engineering economics of trusted setup ceremonies. We trace the shared algebraic core of the pairing-based constructions, contrast their per-circuit, universal, and fully transparent trust models, and quantify the classical trade triangle among proof size, prover time, and verifier cost using published benchmarks. We analyze recursive composition as the mechanism converting linear proving work into logarithmic verification chains, examine multi-party ceremonies that distribute trust over toxic waste, and assess deployment hazards including Fiat-Shamir fragility and assumption mismatch. The conclusion distills practical decision rules for selecting among these systems in production.

## 1 Introduction

The ambition of *verifiable computation* is disarmingly simple: a computationally weak verifier should be convinced of the correctness of a long computation without re-executing it. Zero-knowledge proofs add the further demand that the verifier learn *nothing* beyond the truth of the statement. From the interactive protocols of Goldwasser, Micali, and Rackoff, through Kilian's and Micali's succinct constructions, to the pairing-based systems of Gennaro, Gentry, Parno, and Raykova [1], the field advanced in two alternating waves: first making proofs *succinct*, then making them *practical*.

The inflection point arrived with deployable zk-SNARKs. Zcash's Sprout protocol put a pairing-based SNARK into a production cryptocurrency, forcing the community to confront questions that theory had deferred: how is the *common reference string* generated, who holds the trapdoor, and what happens when the circuit changes? The answers bifurcated the field. One branch minimized proof size and verification cost at the price of a per-circuit trusted setup, culminating in Groth's 2016 construction with proofs of just three group elements [1]. A second branch traded a small constant factor in proof size for a *universal* setup reusable across circuits, crystallized in PLONK and its KZG commitment discipline [2]. A third branch abandoned pairings entirely, achieving transparency and plausible post-quantum security from collision-resistant hashing alone, at the cost of proofs measured in tens to hundreds of kilobytes: the STARK family [3].

This treatise is organized as a comparative engineering study. **Section 2** fixes definitions and the arithmetization ladder. **Section 3** describes our methodology. **Section 4** dissects each family in depth. **Section 5** presents empirical comparisons and proof sketches. **Section 6** catalogs limitations, and **Section 7** gives deployment guidance.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Proof systems and their desiderata

A *non-interactive argument of knowledge* for an NP relation R is a triple of algorithms (Setup, Prove, Verify) with the following properties:

- **Completeness.** If (x, w) is in R, an honest prover convinces an honest verifier with probability one.
- **Knowledge soundness.** From any convincing prover one can *extract* a valid witness, except with negligible probability. This is strictly stronger than mere soundness and is the property applications such as rollups actually require.
- **Zero-knowledge.** The verifier's view is simulatable from the statement alone; no information about the witness leaks.
- **Succinctness.** Proof size and verification time grow at most polylogarithmically in the size of the computation.

> **Theorem:** *(Knowledge soundness via extraction.)* A non-interactive argument is an argument of *knowledge* if there exists a polynomial-time extractor that, given black-box access to any prover producing an accepting proof with non-negligible probability, outputs a valid witness with non-negligible probability. All three families studied here target this notion, though their extraction arguments live in different idealized models — the *generic group model* for Groth16 [1], the *algebraic group model* for PLONK [2], and the *random oracle model* for STARKs [3].

The *setup* axis distinguishes the families most sharply:

1. **Per-circuit trusted setup** (Groth16): the structured reference string (SRS) depends on the circuit. Changing the circuit requires a fresh ceremony.
2. **Universal updatable setup** (PLONK): one SRS supports all circuits up to a bounded size, and participants can sequentially contribute randomness such that security holds if *any one* contributor was honest.
3. **Transparent setup** (STARKs): public randomness only; no secrets exist to protect.

### 2.2 Arithmetization: from programs to polynomials

Every practical system compiles computation into algebra over a finite field F. The classical ladder is:

- **R1CS (Rank-1 Constraint Systems).** Each gate becomes a constraint of the form (A · w) ∘ (B · w) = (C · w), where w is the witness vector and ∘ denotes the Hadamard product.
- **QAP (Quadratic Arithmetic Programs).** R1CS matrices are interpolated into polynomials A_i(x), B_i(x), C_i(x) such that satisfiability is equivalent to the divisibility relation A(x)·B(x) − C(x) = H(x)·t(x), where t(x) is the vanishing polynomial of the constraint domain [1].
- **AIR (Algebraic Intermediate Representation).** Used by STARKs: the computation is an *execution trace*, a table whose rows are consecutive machine states, with polynomial constraints enforcing each transition [3].
- **Plonkish.** A generalization of PLONK's constraint format: a fixed-width table of advice columns with selector polynomials enabling *custom gates* and lookup arguments, now the lingua franca of zkEVM and zkVM design.

The prover's central task in the pairing-based systems is to commit to the witness polynomials and convince the verifier of the divisibility or permutation identities; in STARKs, it is to prove that the trace satisfies the transition constraints and that the relevant polynomials are of low degree — the latter via the FRI protocol [4].

### 2.3 Polynomial commitments and Fiat-Shamir

A *polynomial commitment scheme* lets a prover commit to a polynomial f with a short digest and later prove evaluations f(z) = y. The Kate-Zaverucha-Goldberg (KZG) scheme gives constant-size commitments and evaluation proofs verified by a handful of pairing equations, but requires a trusted SRS containing powers of a secret [5].

Interactive protocols are compiled to non-interactive ones with the *Fiat-Shamir heuristic*, replacing verifier challenges with hashes of the transcript.

## 3 Methodology

This treatise follows a comparative-analytic methodology rather than presenting a novel construction:

1. **Primary-source grounding.** Every construction is traced to its canonical paper: Groth's EUROCRYPT 2016 SNARK [1], the PLONK ePrint [2], the STARK ePrint [3], the FRI protocol [4], KZG-style commitment enhancements [5], and the Fiat-Shamir attack analysis [6].
2. **Benchmark triangulation.** Asymptotic claims are cross-checked against published measurement tables comparing proof size and verifier time across families [7][8].
3. **Ceremony analysis.** Trusted-setup practice is evaluated through documented multi-party ceremonies and the Powers-of-Tau framework underlying per-circuit and universal SRS generation [8].
4. **Deployment lens.** Each family is scored on five engineering axes: proof size, prover time and memory, verifier time, setup trust, and assumption strength (including post-quantum status).

We deliberately exclude interactive systems, linear PCPs without implementations, and fully-homomorphic approaches, keeping the comparison within deployed or deployment-ready succinct arguments.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Groth16: the minimal pairing-based argument

Jens Groth's 2016 construction remains the efficiency frontier for pairing-based succinctness: a proof consists of exactly **three group elements** (two in G_1, one in G_2), roughly 200 bytes on a 254-bit curve, and verification requires only three pairing checks, independent of circuit size [1]. The protocol flow is:

1. Compile the computation to R1CS, then to a QAP via Lagrange interpolation over the constraint domain.
2. A per-circuit trusted setup samples toxic waste (τ, α, β, γ, δ) and publishes the proving and verification keys: encodings of QAP polynomials evaluated at τ, with α, β, γ, δ blinding the components that enforce zero-knowledge.
3. The prover computes the witness polynomials, forms the quotient H(x) = (A(x)B(x) − C(x)) / t(x), and outputs randomized group elements (π_A, π_B, π_C).
4. The verifier checks a single pairing equation binding π_A, π_B, π_C against the verification key and the public inputs.

In Rust-like pseudocode, verification is strikingly small:

```rust
// Groth16 verification: three pairings, constant time
fn groth16_verify(
    vk: &VerifyingKey, proof: &Proof, public_inputs: &[Fr],
) -> bool {
    let mut acc = vk.alpha_g1 * vk.beta_g2; // e(α, β)
    // accumulate public inputs against gamma-encoded CRS
    let gamma_abc = vk.gamma_abc.iter()
        .zip(public_inputs)
        .map(|(g, x)| g * x)
        .sum::<G1>();
    acc += pairing(gamma_abc, vk.gamma_g2);
    acc += pairing(proof.c, vk.delta_g2);
    pairing(proof.a, proof.b) == acc
}
```

The price of this minimality is the *per-circuit* SRS: any circuit change invalidates the keys and demands a new ceremony. Subsequent work strengthened the security notion to *simulation extractability* — non-malleability under chosen-proof attack — which the original knowledge-soundness proof does not guarantee, at a cost of a couple of extra pairings and one group element [9].

### 4.2 PLONK and the KZG commitment discipline

PLONK (Permutations over Lagrange-bases for Oecumenical Noninteractive arguments of Knowledge) restructured the SNARK around a *universal* SRS [2]. Instead of compiling to R1CS and a circuit-specific QAP, PLONK expresses the circuit as polynomial identities over a multiplicative subgroup H of the field:

- Wire polynomials a(X), b(X), c(X) interpolate the left, right, and output wire values across gates.
- Selector polynomials q_M, q_L, q_R, q_O, q_C encode each gate's type; the gate identity q_M·a·b + q_L·a + q_R·b + q_O·c + q_C = 0 must vanish on H.
- A *permutation argument* enforces copy constraints (wiring) via a grand-product polynomial z(X) and the permutation σ over wire positions.

> **Theorem:** *(KZG evaluation binding.)* Under the q-strong Diffie-Hellman assumption, a KZG commitment c = [f(τ)]_1 binds the prover to f: no efficient adversary can produce an accepting evaluation proof for (z, y′) with y′ ≠ f(z). PLONK inherits knowledge soundness in the algebraic group model from this binding property composed with the permutation argument [2][5].

The five-round protocol commits to a, b, c, z, and the quotient polynomial t(X) obtained by dividing the combined constraint identity by the vanishing polynomial Z_H(X).

PLONK's decisive engineering advantage is the *updatable* universal SRS: contributors sequentially add randomness, and the SRS is secure if at least one contributor destroyed their secret. The same Powers-of-Tau transcript can serve every circuit up to the SRS degree bound, which is why PLONK underlies most zkEVM and general-purpose zkVM efforts.

### 4.3 STARKs and FRI: transparency from hash functions

STARKs (Scalable Transparent ARguments of Knowledge) take the opposite bargain: no trusted setup, no pairings, no elliptic curves at all — only a collision-resistant hash function [3]. The computation becomes an *execution trace*, and the prover commits to the trace's Reed-Solomon encoding with Merkle trees. Two sub-protocols do the heavy lifting:

1. **Constraint checking.** Transition constraints are combined into a composition polynomial via random linear combination (the DEEP-ALI methodology); the verifier checks the composition at a single out-of-domain point.
2. **Low-degree testing.** The FRI protocol (Fast Reed-Solomon Interactive Oracle Proofs of Proximity) convinces the verifier that the committed function is close to a low-degree polynomial, through logarithmically many rounds of *folding*: each round halves the degree by combining even and odd coefficients with verifier randomness [4].

```python
# FRI folding round: degree halving with verifier randomness beta
def fri_fold(evaluations: list[Field], beta: Field) -> list[Field]:
    """One FRI commit round over a coset domain of even size."""
    half = len(evaluations) // 2
    folded = []
    for i in range(half):
        even, odd = evaluations[i], evaluations[i + half]
        # f(x^2) = (f(x) + f(-x)) / 2, x*f(x^2) = (f(x) - f(-x)) / 2
        folded.append((even + odd) / 2 + beta * ((even - odd) / 2))
    return folded  # Merkle-commit; recurse until constant
```

> **Theorem:** *(FRI soundness.)* If the committed function is δ-far from every Reed-Solomon codeword of the claimed rate, then each FRI round preserves distance with high probability over the verifier's randomness, and the verifier rejects except with error exponentially small in the number of query repetitions [4].

The asymptotic profile is distinctive: prover time quasilinear in trace length, proof size polylogarithmic (practically 40–150 KB), verifier time polylogarithmic — concretely around 10 ms [7][8].

### 4.4 Plonkish arithmetization and custom gates

*Plonkish* is PLONK's constraint format generalized into a full arithmetization language: a fixed number of columns (advice, instance, fixed) and rows, with each row constrained by a low-degree polynomial identity whose terms are toggled by *selector* columns. Its expressive power comes from two extensions:

- **Custom gates.** Frequently used sub-circuits (a Poseidon round, an ECDSA scalar multiplication step, a range check) are baked into dedicated high-degree gate identities, amortizing constraint count far below what generic multiplication gates would need.
- **Lookup arguments.** Plookup-style protocols prove that a tuple of wire values appears in a precomputed table, making range checks, bitwise operations, and S-boxes nearly free [2].

The practical consequence is that prover time in modern PLONK deployments is dominated by multi-scalar multiplications linear in the row count, and the original paper reports prover group operations roughly an order of magnitude below the fully succinct mode of its predecessor Sonic [2].

### 4.5 Recursive proof composition and aggregation

*Recursion* verifies a proof inside another proof: the outer circuit implements the inner verifier, so one proof attests to the validity of many. *Incrementally verifiable computation* (IVC) chains this step indefinitely, and *aggregation* merges many independent proofs into one. Three consequences make recursion the central scaling primitive of the field:

1. **Bounded on-chain work.** A rollup can prove thousands of transactions and post a single constant-size proof; verification cost no longer grows with batch size.
2. **Heterogeneous composition.** Proofs from different systems (say, a STARK for bulk execution and a Groth16 wrapper for cheap on-chain verification) can be nested, exploiting each family's strengths.
3. **Proof-carrying data.** Long-running or distributed computations carry their own correctness evidence, checkable at any hop.

Recursion imposes a hard constraint on the arithmetization: the outer circuit must efficiently express the inner verifier's algebra. Pairing-based verifiers need non-native field arithmetic (verifying a BN254 proof inside a BN254 circuit is awkward), which motivated *cycles of elliptic curves* — pairs of curves whose base fields match each other's scalar fields — and, more recently, recursion-friendly hash-based designs where the verifier is mostly hashes and field operations. The idea of composing succinct proofs has deep roots: early scalable-PCP implementations already used proof composition to bootstrap efficiency [10], and modern folding schemes continue that lineage by replacing full recursion with cheaper accumulation.

```haskell
-- Recursion as a type: a proof whose statement includes
-- verification of another proof of the same shape
data RecursiveProof c = RecursiveProof
  { innerProof  :: Proof c          -- proof being wrapped
  , verifierCkt :: Circuit c        -- arithmetized verifier
  , outerProof  :: Proof c         -- attests: verifierCkt accepts innerProof
  }
```

The engineering trade is prover overhead: recursion multiplies proving cost by a super-constant factor and demands careful circuit design to keep the verifier small.

## 5 Empirical Results and Proof Sketches

### 5.1 Quantitative comparison

The following table triangulates published measurements; absolute numbers move with curves and implementations, but the *ordering* is stable across sources [7][8]:

| System | Proof size | Verifier time | Setup | Post-quantum |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Groth16 | ~200 bytes, O(1) | ~1.5 ms, O(1) | Trusted, per circuit | No |
| PLONK (KZG) | ~400 bytes, O(1) | ~3 ms, O(1) | Universal, updatable | No |
| Bulletproofs | ~1.5 KB, O(log n) | ~3 s, O(n) | Transparent | No |
| zk-STARK | ~100 KB, O(log² n) | ~10 ms, O(log² n) | Transparent | Yes |

Groth16 minimizes proof size and verifier work absolutely; PLONK pays roughly a factor of two in both for universality; STARKs pay two to three orders of magnitude in proof size for transparency and quantum resistance.

### 5.2 The ceremony economy

Trusted setups are multi-party computations in which each participant contributes randomness to the SRS; security reduces to a *1-of-N honesty* assumption — all participants would have to collude (or all leak) to recover the trapdoor [8].

### 5.3 Security proof sketches

- **Groth16.** Knowledge soundness holds in the generic group model under linear knowledge assumptions: the extractor rewinds the algebraic adversary's representation of group elements as known linear combinations of CRS terms, recovering the QAP witness [1]. Simulation extractability requires the stronger construction of [9].
- **PLONK.** Soundness reduces to KZG evaluation binding plus the Schwartz-Zippel analysis of the permutation grand product; the algebraic group model with random oracles yields knowledge soundness [2][5]. The Fiat-Shamir instantiation must hash all prover messages that precede each challenge [6].
- **STARKs.** Soundness composes the FRI low-degree guarantee with DEEP sampling: if the trace violates a constraint, the composition polynomial is far from low-degree with overwhelming probability, and FRI catches it [3][4]. The random oracle (hash function) is the only idealization, giving the post-quantum claim.

## 6 Limitations

No family dominates on all axes, and each carries failure modes that practitioners must price explicitly:

- **Groth16: ceremony rigidity.** Every circuit revision demands a new trusted setup — an operational treadmill for fast-iterating products — and a compromised ceremony forges unbounded proofs silently.
- **PLONK: assumption stacking and verifier fragility.** Security rests on pairing-friendly curves *and* correct Fiat-Shamir transcript hashing; the last-challenge attack shows that a single omitted commitment in the hash input voids soundness in a real deployment [6]. Updatability also means the SRS is a shared point of failure across all dependent circuits.
- **STARKs: proof bulk and prover memory.** Hundred-kilobyte proofs are prohibitive for bandwidth-constrained verifiers, and proving large traces demands gigabytes of RAM for FFT domains — a centralization pressure on provers.
- **Recursion: verifier arithmetization cost.** Non-native field arithmetic inflates recursive verifier circuits; curve cycles and folding schemes mitigate but do not eliminate the overhead.
- **Quantum horizon.** All pairing- and discrete-log-based systems fall to Shor's algorithm; only hash-based STARKs survive, and "plausibly post-quantum" still assumes the hash resists future cryptanalytic and quantum advances [3].
- **Zero-knowledge vs. succinctness conflation.** Several deployed "zk" rollups use these systems for *validity* over public data; the zero-knowledge property is unused yet paid for in prover overhead — match the primitive to the actual privacy requirement.

## 7 Conclusion

The field has converged on a stable division of labor. **Groth16** remains the right choice when on-chain verification cost is the binding constraint and the circuit is stable enough to amortize a ceremony — the minimal proof is still unmatched [1]. **PLONK with KZG commitments** is the default for general-purpose verifiable computation: the universal updatable SRS removes the ceremony treadmill, Plonkish arithmetization with custom gates and lookups delivers prover efficiency, and the proof stays under a kilobyte [2][5]. **STARKs** win wherever transparency is mandated, quantum resistance is priced in, or computations are large enough that quasilinear proving and polylogarithmic verification dominate proof-size concerns [3][4]. **Recursion and aggregation** sit above all three as the scaling layer, converting linear proving effort into constant settlement cost — with the caveat that the recursive verifier's arithmetization must be designed, not assumed.

Three rules of thumb for deployment follow. *First*, choose the trust model before the proof system: if no ceremony is politically or operationally acceptable, the decision is already made. *Second*, audit the Fiat-Shamir transcript as carefully as the circuit: recent forgeries exploited the verifier, not the mathematics [6]. *Third*, benchmark the prover, not just the verifier: memory and proving time, not proof bytes, are what centralize real networks. The next frontier — folding schemes, lattice-based succinct arguments, and hardware-accelerated proving — will shift these trade-offs, but the underlying discipline this treatise documents, reducing trust to explicit, auditable assumptions, will remain the measure of the field.
