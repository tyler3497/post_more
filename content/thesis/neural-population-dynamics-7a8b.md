# Neural Population Dynamics in Motor Cortex: Latent Variable Models, Gaussian-Process Factor Analysis, and the Manifold Hypothesis for Movement Generation

## Abstract

Motor cortex has long been studied one neuron at a time, yet movement emerges from coordinated population activity. This thesis develops the latent-variable framework of modern neural population dynamics: simultaneous spike-train recordings from motor cortical neurons are best understood as noisy observations of a low-dimensional latent state. We present Gaussian-process factor analysis (GPFA) in full mathematical detail, deriving its EM updates and the squared-exponential kernel that enforces temporal smoothness. We then turn to the dynamical-systems view of Churchland and colleagues, where a skew-symmetric linear system (jPCA) exposes rotational structure in reaching trajectories, and to the manifold hypothesis of Gallego, Perich, Miller and Solla, in which movement is generated by time-varying activation of neural modes spanning a low-dimensional surface. Empirical results from delayed-reach experiments in macaque motor cortex, including single-trial inference with sequential autoencoders, establish that 8-12 latent dimensions capture the bulk of explainable variance and decode behavior with high fidelity, while we critically evaluate statistical pitfalls that can make rotations appear artifactually.

## 1 Introduction

The classical doctrine of motor neurophysiology held that individual neurons in primary motor cortex (M1) *represent* movement parameters: direction, velocity, force, muscle activation. This representational paradigm was inherited, by analogy, from sensory systems — where, for example, neurons in primary visual cortex transparently encode oriented luminance edges [2]. Yet decades of single-unit recordings in motor cortex have produced a paradox: individual responses are spectacularly complex, heterogeneous, and resistant to clean parameterization. Tuning curves shift with context, responses multiplex preparatory and movement-period signals, and the mapping from firing rate to kinematics is noisy and nonlinear [2][3].

The population-dynamics perspective resolves this tension by abandoning the neuron as the unit of explanation:

> **Thesis Statement.** *Motor cortical activity is generated by a low-dimensional latent dynamical system. What a single neuron "represents" is incidental; what matters is the trajectory of the population state through neural state space, whose evolution law encodes the computation that produces movement.*

This thesis develops that claim in four movements. We begin with the measurement problem: spike trains are stochastic point processes, and smooth latent trajectories must be inferred from them. We then build three pillars of increasing structural commitment:

1. **Gaussian-process factor analysis (GPFA)** — a nonparametric latent-variable model extracting smooth single-trial neural trajectories from binned spike counts [1].
2. **Dynamical-systems analysis via jPCA** — the discovery that motor cortical trajectories during reaching contain strong rotational structure, captured by a linear system with imaginary eigenvalues [2].
3. **The neural manifold hypothesis** — the claim that task-relevant activity is confined to a low-dimensional, approximately invariant surface in neural state space, spanned by *neural modes* re-activated across behaviors [3].

Each pillar is accompanied by the probabilistic machinery that makes it concrete, the empirical evidence that supports it, and the statistical caveats that constrain interpretation.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Neural recordings as high-dimensional time series

Consider a macaque monkey performing a delayed center-out reaching task while a multi-electrode array records $n \approx 60$–$200$ neurons. Each trial yields $n$ spike trains, binned at $\Delta = 20$ ms into a count matrix $\mathbf{Y} \in \mathbb{N}^{n \times T}$. Three statistical facts dominate any analysis:

- **Discreteness and noise.** Spike counts are non-negative integers with approximately Poisson variability: $\mathrm{Var}(y_{it}) \approx \mathbb{E}[y_{it}]$ [1].
- **Dimensionality exceeds sample size.** The ambient space $\mathbb{R}^n$ is vastly under-sampled by a few hundred trials; naive trajectory estimation is noise-dominated.
- **Correlated variability.** The covariance $\mathrm{Cov}(\mathbf{y}_t)$ is structured, not diagonal. This structure is the signal.

### 2.2 From tuning curves to population states

The classical approach fits a per-neuron encoding model, $y_{it} \sim \mathrm{Poisson}(f_i(\boldsymbol{\theta}_t))$, where $\boldsymbol{\theta}_t$ is a kinematic covariate and $f_i$ a tuning curve [2]. The population approach inverts this logic. Define the **population state** at time $t$ as the vector of instantaneous firing rates $\mathbf{r}_t \in \mathbb{R}^n$. The scientific object is no longer a set of $n$ univariate functions but the *geometry and dynamics of the trajectory* $\{\mathbf{r}_t\}_{t=1}^{T}$ in $\mathbb{R}^n$: its dimensionality, curvature, fixed points, and relation to behavior [3].

### 2.3 Dimensionality reduction as scientific instrument

Dimensionality reduction is not visualization convenience here; it is the central scientific instrument [1]. The methods form a natural hierarchy of increasing structural commitment:

| Method | Latent model | Noise model | Temporal structure |
|---|---|---|---|
| PCA | linear subspace | isotropic Gaussian | none |
| FA | linear subspace | diagonal Gaussian | none |
| GPFA | linear subspace | diagonal Gaussian | GP prior |
| LDS | linear subspace + dynamics | diagonal Gaussian | linear ODE |
| LFADS | RNN generator | Poisson | nonlinear dynamics |

We climb this ladder rung by rung in Section 4, showing at each step what new scientific conclusion the additional structure enables.

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 The GPFA generative model

GPFA, introduced by Yu, Afshar, and Sahani [1], models square-rooted spike counts as noisy linear observations of a latent trajectory with a Gaussian-process prior. Let $\mathbf{y}_t \in \mathbb{R}^n$ be the transformed counts and $\mathbf{x}_t \in \mathbb{R}^p$ the latent state ($p \ll n$, typically $8$–$15$):

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbf{y}_t \mid \mathbf{x}_t &\sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{C}\mathbf{x}_t + \mathbf{d},\ \mathbf{R}), \quad t = 1, \dots, T, \\
\mathbf{x}_{1:T}^{(j)} &\sim \mathcal{GP}(0, k_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_j}), \quad j = 1, \dots, p,
\end{aligned}
$$

where $\mathbf{C} \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times p}$ is the loading matrix, $\mathbf{d}$ absorbs per-neuron means, $\mathbf{R} = \mathrm{diag}(r_1, \dots, r_n)$ allows each neuron its own noise variance (the factor-analysis innovation over PCA), and each latent dimension evolves as an independent GP with squared-exponential kernel

$$
k_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_j}(t, t') = \sigma_{f,j}^2 \exp\!\left(-\frac{(t - t')^2}{2\tau_j^2}\right) + \sigma_{n,j}^2 \delta_{tt'}.
$$

Why the square-root transform? It is variance-stabilizing for Poisson counts, so $\sqrt{y}$ is approximately Gaussian with near-constant variance [1]. This renders the Gaussian likelihood defensible while preserving tractability — no point-process likelihood is required.

### 3.2 EM inference

GPFA is fit by expectation–maximization. In the **E-step**, the posterior $p(\mathbf{x}_{1:T} \mid \mathbf{y}_{1:T})$ is Gaussian (the model is linear-Gaussian given the kernel hyperparameters) and computed exactly. In the **M-step**, closed-form updates obtain for $\mathbf{C}$, $\mathbf{d}$, and $\mathbf{R}$ (weighted least squares against posterior moments), while kernel hyperparameters are optimized by gradient ascent on the expected complete-data log-likelihood. The **posterior mean** $\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{x}_{1:T} \mid \mathbf{y}_{1:T}]$ is the *neural trajectory*: a smooth, single-trial estimate of the population state.

> **Theorem 1 (GPFA trajectory optimality).** *Under the GPFA generative model, the posterior mean $\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{1:T} = \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{x}_{1:T} \mid \mathbf{y}_{1:T}]$ minimizes the expected squared error among all estimators. Orthonormalizing $\mathbf{C}$ orders latent dimensions by explained variance, giving a principled trajectory dimensionality.*

The latent dimensionality $p$ is selected by **leave-one-neuron-out prediction**: each neuron's activity is predicted from the trajectory inferred from the others, and the $p$ minimizing prediction error is chosen [1]. This cross-validation guards against fitting noise dimensions — the central pathology of high-dimensional neural data.

### 3.3 jPCA: finding rotational dynamics

Where GPFA is a *descriptive* smoother, jPCA (Churchland et al. [2]) asks a *dynamical* question: what linear differential equation best explains condition-averaged trajectories $\bar{\mathbf{x}}(t) \in \mathbb{R}^p$? The ansatz is $\dot{\bar{\mathbf{x}}}(t) = \mathbf{M}\,\bar{\mathbf{x}}(t)$, with $\mathbf{M}$ constrained to be **skew-symmetric** ($\mathbf{M}^\top = -\mathbf{M}$). Skew-symmetry is not arbitrary: a real skew-symmetric matrix has purely imaginary eigenvalues, so its flow is pure rotation — the natural linear-algebraic model of oscillation [2].

The procedure: (1) reduce trial-averaged data to $p = 10$ PCA dimensions; (2) numerically differentiate to obtain $\dot{\bar{\mathbf{x}}}(t)$; (3) solve $\min_{\mathbf{M} = -\mathbf{M}^\top} \sum_t \|\dot{\bar{\mathbf{x}}}(t) - \mathbf{M}\bar{\mathbf{x}}(t)\|^2$, whose closed form symmetrizes the least-squares fit, $\mathbf{M}^\star = \tfrac{1}{2}(\mathbf{A} - \mathbf{A}^\top)$; (4) diagonalize $\mathbf{M}^\star$ to obtain the **jPC planes**, ordered by rotation frequency. Fit quality is the variance in $\dot{\bar{\mathbf{x}}}$ captured by $\mathbf{M}^\star\bar{\mathbf{x}}$ [2].

### 3.4 Manifold estimation

Formally, a **neural manifold** $\mathcal{M} \subset \mathbb{R}^n$ is a low-dimensional surface ($d \ll n$) such that task-relevant activity satisfies $\mathbf{r}_t \in \mathcal{M}$ (up to noise) across conditions and times. Its tangent space is spanned by the **neural modes**: the principal directions of covariation, estimated by the leading eigenvectors of the condition-averaged covariance [3]. The hypothesis makes a strong, testable prediction — **invariance**: the same neural modes should be re-used across behaviors, with behavior-specific trajectories unfolding within one shared surface [3].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The noise model matters more than the smoother

The observation model in GPFA deserves scrutiny, because everything downstream — trajectory smoothness, dimensionality estimates, decoding — depends on it. Yu et al. [1] compared three nested models on the same 61-neuron premotor dataset:

- **PPCA** (isotropic noise): dominated by high-firing neurons, whose Poisson noise masquerades as signal variance.
- **FA** (diagonal noise): correctly attributes Poisson variability to private noise, revealing the shared subspace.
- **GPFA** (diagonal noise + GP smoothing): additionally exploits temporal continuity, improving single-trial estimates.

Leave-one-neuron-out error decreased monotonically across this hierarchy, establishing that *both* the noise model and the temporal prior matter empirically, not merely aesthetically [1]. A corollary: PCA on raw spike counts, still common in practice, systematically overestimates dimensionality because it mistakes Poisson noise for shared structure.

### 4.2 Rotational dynamics: mechanism or epiphenomenon?

Churchland et al.'s [2] central finding is startling: during a *non-rhythmic* reach, motor cortical population activity contains a brief, strong *oscillatory* component. Projected onto the top jPC plane, trajectories for different reach directions trace rotations of consistent handedness, with amplitude and phase predicted by the preparatory state. The oscillation ($\approx 2$–$3$ Hz) is far slower than the movement, and the reach is not periodic. Why would the brain generate rotations to produce non-rotational behavior?

The dynamical-systems answer [2] is that motor cortex is not representing movement parameters at all; it is a *pattern generator*. Preparatory activity sets the initial condition $\mathbf{x}(0)$; rotational dynamics unfold deterministically; downstream circuits read out the appropriate linear combination. The rotation is the engine's cycle, not the car's trajectory.

Skepticism is warranted, and three concerns structure the debate:

1. **Temporal-ordering artifacts.** If neurons fire in a consistent temporal sequence, projecting into a low-dimensional space can produce circular-looking trajectories even with purely feedforward, non-recurrent dynamics. Churchland et al.'s shuffle controls — destroying condition structure while preserving temporal order — address this, and rotations survive them [2].
2. **Smoothing-induced rotations.** Low-pass filtering of sequentially activated units induces apparent curvature. GPFA's timescale $\tau$ must be chosen by cross-validated prediction, not aesthetics [1].
3. **Linearity.** A skew-symmetric $\mathbf{M}$ is a strong assumption; a nonlinear flow (e.g., a limit cycle with radial contraction) would be mischaracterized. This motivated nonlinear extensions (Section 4.4).

### 4.3 The manifold hypothesis and neural modes

Gallego, Perich, Miller, and Solla [3] synthesized a decade of dimensionality-reduction findings into one framework:

- **Neural modes**: population-wide activity patterns arising from network connectivity.
- **Neural manifold**: the low-dimensional surface spanned by a small set of neural modes.
- **The control model**: movement is generated by *time-dependent activation* of neural modes; the trajectory $\mathbf{x}(t)$ on the manifold drives muscles through a readout, while the manifold itself reflects recurrent connectivity.

The framework's power lies in predictions about *learning*. If the manifold reflects connectivity, learning a new behavior should be easier when expressible within the existing manifold (re-aiming trajectories) than when it requires new modes — a prediction confirmed in brain–computer interface experiments where monkeys learned "within-manifold" perturbations far faster than "outside-manifold" ones [3]. The manifold is thus a *causal* object: it constrains what the animal can learn.

### 4.4 Nonlinear latent dynamics: from GPFA to LFADS

GPFA assumes linear observations of GP latents — smoothness without dynamics. The LDS adds $\mathbf{x}_{t+1} = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}_t + \mathbf{w}_t$; jPCA is essentially a constrained LDS fit to trial-averaged data. But cortical dynamics are nonlinear, and single-trial spike trains are far noisier than trial averages.

**LFADS** (Latent Factor Analysis via Dynamical Systems), introduced by Pandarinath et al. [4], addresses both with a sequential variational autoencoder: an encoder RNN infers a distribution over the initial condition $\mathbf{g}_0$ of a generator RNN, which unrolls deterministic nonlinear dynamics $\mathbf{g}_{t+1} = f_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{g}_t)$ read out to firing rates $\mathbf{r}_t$, with spikes modeled as $\mathbf{y}_t \sim \mathrm{Poisson}(\mathbf{r}_t)$ — no variance-stabilizing transform needed. Training maximizes a variational lower bound (ELBO).

On synthetic Lorenz-attractor data, LFADS recovered the true 3-D latents with $R^2 \in \{0.85, 0.92, 0.87\}$, substantially beating GPFA ($R^2 \in \{0.71, 0.73, 0.33\}$) [4]. Applied to 202-channel M1/PMd recordings during a 108-condition maze task (2,296 trials), LFADS recovered the jPCA rotational structure on *single trials* — structure invisible in raw single-trial projections. This is a landmark: the rotational dynamics are a property of the underlying computation, not an artifact of trial averaging [4]. A further capability, **dynamic neural stitching**, combined separately recorded, non-overlapping populations into one consistent dynamical model [4].

### 4.5 Null space, potent space, and what dynamics are *for*

A dynamical view raises a puzzle: if preparatory activity is a strong initial condition for movement-generating dynamics, why does preparation not cause movement? Kaufman, Churchland, Ryu, and Shenoy resolved this with the **null-space / potent-space** decomposition. Let $\mathbf{W}$ be the cortical-to-muscle readout. Then

$$
\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{x}_{\text{potent}} + \mathbf{x}_{\text{null}}, \quad \mathbf{W}\mathbf{x}_{\text{null}} = 0,\ \ \mathbf{x}_{\text{potent}} \in \mathrm{row}(\mathbf{W}).
$$

Preparatory activity lives predominantly in the **null space** — large without driving muscles — while movement-period activity rotates into the **potent space**. The jPCA rotation is, in this telling, literally the population state swinging from null space into potent space. Preparation and movement are different phases of one trajectory, not different codes [2].

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 GPFA on macaque premotor cortex

Applied to 61 simultaneously recorded premotor neurons during a delayed-reach task with 14 targets [1]:

- **Dimensionality.** Cross-validation selected $p \in [8, 12]$ latent dimensions; residual error plateaued sharply — extra dimensions captured only private noise.
- **Prediction.** GPFA achieved the lowest held-out error among two-stage smooth-then-reduce methods, FA, and PCA.
- **Single-trial structure.** For each target, trajectories *converged during the delay period* — a signature of preparatory computation previously only inferred indirectly [1]. Trials with outlying reaction times showed systematically different trajectories, linking latent state to behavior on individual trials.

### 5.2 The jPCA evidence

In Churchland et al. [2], 218 M1/PMd neurons were recorded during reaches to 28 targets at varying distances and speeds. The best skew-symmetric fit captured the majority of $\dot{\bar{\mathbf{x}}}$ variance within a single 2-D jPC plane; rotation amplitude and phase were lawful functions of reach direction and preparatory state. The same motif appeared in *human* motor cortical data, where LFADS recovered jPCA-like rotations from single trials of attempted movement — a cross-species replication [4].

### 5.3 Manifold invariance across behaviors

Gallego, Perich, and colleagues recorded M1 during reaching, grasping, and isometric wrist tasks [3]. The leading neural modes were highly aligned across tasks — one shared low-dimensional surface hosted all three behaviors. Long-term recordings showed the manifold stable over days to weeks even as individual neurons' tuning drifted: the population-level object is more stable than its single-neuron constituents [3].

### 5.4 A worked example: GPFA in Python

A pedagogical sketch of the EM loop (fixed kernel hyperparameters, square-rooted counts). The reference MATLAB toolbox is maintained by the authors [6][7].

```python
import numpy as np

def gpfa_em(Y, p, n_iter=50, tau=40.0, dt=20.0):
    # Y: (n, T) square-rooted binned spike counts; p: latent dims
    n, T = Y.shape
    tt = np.arange(T) * dt
    K = np.exp(-0.5 * ((tt[:, None] - tt[None, :]) / tau) ** 2)
    K += 1e-6 * np.eye(T)
    Kinv = np.linalg.inv(K)
    Yc = Y - Y.mean(axis=1, keepdims=True)
    U, S, _ = np.linalg.svd(Yc, full_matrices=False)
    C = U[:, :p] * S[:p]            # loadings (n, p)
    R = np.var(Yc, axis=1)          # private noise (n,)
    for it in range(n_iter):
        # E-step: exact Gaussian posterior per latent dim
        Ex = np.zeros((p, T))
        for j in range(p):
            lam = float(C[:, j] @ (C[:, j] / R))
            post_cov = np.linalg.inv(Kinv + lam * np.eye(T))
            Ex[j] = post_cov @ ((Yc / R[:, None]).T @ C[:, j])
        # M-step: C, R via weighted least squares on posterior moments
        Sxx = (Ex @ Ex.T) / T
        C = (Yc @ Ex.T) / T @ np.linalg.inv(Sxx)
        R = np.maximum(np.mean(Yc ** 2, axis=1)
                       - np.einsum('ij,jk,ik->i', C, Sxx, C), 1e-6)
    return C, R, Ex  # Ex: posterior-mean neural trajectory
```

The didactic points: the E-step is exact because the model is linear-Gaussian; the M-step for $\mathbf{C}$ is weighted least squares against posterior moments; the kernel timescale $\tau$ governs the smoothness–fidelity tradeoff and must be chosen by cross-validated prediction.

### 5.5 Decoding: dimensionality vs. accuracy

A pragmatic test of any latent-variable model is *decoding*: how well behavior is predicted from the latent state. The consistent finding across GPFA, LDS, and LFADS studies is a saturating curve — decoding accuracy ($R^2$ for hand velocity, classification accuracy for reach target) rises steeply over the first $\approx 5$ dimensions, continues to $\approx 10$–$12$, then plateaus [1][4]. Dimensions beyond the plateau add no behavioral information; they model private noise. This saturation is the operational definition of manifold dimensionality, and its agreement across methods, tasks, and animals is among the strongest evidence that the manifold is real rather than method-induced.

## 6 Limitations

1. **Linearity of the observation model.** GPFA assumes $\mathbf{y}_t = \mathbf{C}\mathbf{x}_t + \mathbf{d} + \boldsymbol{\epsilon}_t$, but real manifolds are likely *curved*. Linear methods then overestimate dimensionality, approximating curvature with extra dimensions. Nonlinear extensions (LFADS [4]) address this at the cost of identifiability.
2. **Identifiability.** The latent state is defined only up to invertible transformation: $(\mathbf{C}, \mathbf{x}) \mapsto (\mathbf{C}\mathbf{A}^{-1}, \mathbf{A}\mathbf{x})$ leaves the likelihood invariant. Scientific claims must be invariant under such transformations; orthonormalization conventions are arbitrary choices, not discoveries.
3. **The rotation debate is not settled.** Despite shuffle controls, some reported rotational structure could reflect sequential activation filtered through dimensionality reduction rather than genuine recurrent dynamics [2]. LFADS single-trial recovery [4] weakens — but does not eliminate — this concern, since the generator RNN has its own inductive bias toward smooth dynamics.
4. **Causality.** All evidence here is observational: trajectories correlate with behavior. Whether latent dynamics *cause* movement or merely *reflect* it (efference copy, proprioceptive feedback) requires population-scale perturbation experiments — e.g., optogenetic clamping of the population state — that remain technically demanding.
5. **Task generality.** Nearly all quantitative results come from constrained laboratory tasks. Whether naturalistic, unconstrained movement lives on the same manifolds — or far higher-dimensional ones — is unknown; Gallego et al. [3] explicitly warn that complex natural behaviors may reveal richer structure.

## 7 Conclusion

The arc of the argument:

- **Measurement** (GPFA [1]): spike trains are noisy observations of smooth low-dimensional trajectories; with the right noise model and GP prior, single-trial trajectories are recoverable, and 8–12 dimensions suffice.
- **Dynamics** (jPCA [2]): those trajectories are the flow of a dynamical system — during reaching, a strongly rotational one, with initial conditions set during preparation in the readout's null space.
- **Geometry** (manifolds [3]): trajectories live on a low-dimensional surface spanned by neural modes; the manifold is stable across days, re-used across behaviors, and constrains learning.
- **Nonlinear synthesis** (LFADS [4]): sequential autoencoders with Poisson observations recover the same dynamical motifs on single trials, at higher fidelity, and generalize across sessions.

> **Open Problem.** *Formulate a statistical test that distinguishes, with calibrated false-positive control, genuine recurrent rotational dynamics from sequential-activation artifacts in single-trial data — without assuming the linear observation model.*

The deepest open questions are mathematical as much as biological: identifiability of nonlinear latent dynamics, causal validation of the readout, and the geometry of manifolds under natural behavior. What is no longer in serious doubt is the framework's central premise — that to understand motor cortex, one must study the population trajectory, not the neuron. The single-neuron doctrine had a productive half-century; the population-dynamics era, armed with latent-variable models of increasing sophistication, is now writing its own.
