# Iris and Concurrent Separation Logic: Ghost State, Invariants, and Machine-Checked Verification of Fine-Grained Concurrent Data Structures

## Abstract

Verifying fine-grained concurrent data structures — lock-free queues, safe-memory-reclamation schemes, and reference-counted cells — remains one of the hardest problems in program verification, because linearization points, helping, and reclamation interact with thread interleavings in ways that defeat syntactic checks or testing. **Iris**, a higher-order concurrent separation logic mechanized in Coq, addresses this by reducing the landscape of concurrent separation logics to two orthogonal mechanisms: *user-defined ghost state* (formalized through resource algebras, or "cameras") and *impredicative invariants* [1]. This thesis develops, from first principles, how these two mechanisms are combined to produce machine-checked proofs of linearizability and memory safety for fine-grained algorithms: the Michael–Scott queue [7], hazard-pointer and RCU-based reclamation [3], and arc-style reference counting [6]. We show how linearization points are encoded with prophecy variables, how fractional permissions model shared ownership, and how the adequacy theorem turns logical derivations into guarantees about real executions. The treatment is evidence-driven: every technique is tied to a mechanized case study, and §5 tabulates the resulting proof artifacts, their scale, and what they teach about the cost of rigor at the concurrency frontier.

---

## 1. Introduction

Concurrent data structures are the gears of modern systems software. The Michael–Scott queue powers message passing in runtimes; hazard pointers and read-copy-update (RCU) let lock-free structures reclaim memory without stopping the world; atomically reference-counted cells (`Arc`) underpin safe sharing in Rust. Yet their correctness arguments are famously delicate: the *linearization point* of an operation may lie in another thread's code ("helping"), a pointer may be logically deleted but physically reclaimed only epochs later, and an innocent-looking compare-and-swap (CAS) may hide an ABA anomaly.

Testing cannot establish the absence of such bugs; it can only fail to find them. Formal verification, and in particular **concurrent separation logic (CSL)**, offers a principled alternative: thread-modular, resource-based reasoning in which each thread's proof obligation is discharged against a shared invariant, and *linearizability* [10] is recovered as a theorem rather than assumed. The difficulty is historical fragmentation: as Matthew Parkinson observed, "each new library or concurrency primitive requires a new separation logic." Iris resolves this fracture. Its key insight — documented across the foundational papers [1, 2] and summarized in the Iris project literature — is that *ghost state* and *invariants* suffice to derive the most powerful reasoning principles of earlier CSLs within a single unified logic [3].

This thesis is a deep, technically precise treatment of that claim. We:

1. Reconstruct the logical core of Iris: the weakest-precondition judgment, the update modality, cameras for ghost state, and the invariant access rule.
2. Show, in detail, how a canonical fine-grained algorithm — the Michael–Scott lock-free queue — is verified, including linearization via prophecy variables.
3. Present the modern treatment of safe memory reclamation (hazard pointers, RCU) as modular separation-logic specifications [3].
4. Evaluate the approach empirically through mechanized case studies: what was proven, at what proof-engineering cost, and where the method reaches its limits.

All claims about Iris's proof rules are grounded in the mechanized metatheory [1]; all case studies cited are Coq developments whose adequacy theorems tie proofs to operational semantics.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 From Hoare logic to the frame rule

Classical Hoare logic reasons about sequential programs with triples $\{P\}\,e\,\{v.\,Q\}$: if $P$ holds before $e$ runs, then $e$ terminates with value $v$ satisfying $Q$. Separation logic, introduced by Reynolds and O'Hearn, adds the *separating conjunction* $P * Q$ — "the heap splits into two disjoint parts satisfying $P$ and $Q$" — and with it the **frame rule**:

> **Theorem (Frame):** $\dfrac{\{P\}\,e\,\{v.\,Q\}}{\{P * R\}\,e\,\{v.\,Q * R\}}$

The frame rule is the engine of *local reasoning*: a proof of $e$ need not mention resources $R$ that $e$ does not touch. Concurrent separation logic lifts this to threads: if thread proofs only require disjoint resources, their parallel composition is sound. The points-to assertion $\ell \mapsto v$ — exclusive ownership of location $\ell$ holding value $v$ — is the canonical example.

### 2.2 Linearizability and the verification target

*Linearizability* [10] is the gold-standard correctness criterion for concurrent objects: every operation appears to take effect instantaneously at some point (its *linearization point*) between invocation and response, and the resulting sequential history respects the object's specification. Verifying linearizability of a lock-free queue means proving that every interleaved execution is equivalent to *some* sequential execution of an abstract queue.



### 2.3 Ghost state: fictional resources for real protocols

*Ghost state* is logical state that does not exist at runtime but exists in the proof: counters, tokens, permissions, and protocol phases. In Iris, ghost state is governed by **cameras** — unital resource algebras $(M, \cdot, \varepsilon, \mathcal{V})$ where $\cdot$ is a partial composition and $\mathcal{V}$ a validity predicate [1]. The two canonical constructions are:

- **Authoritative cameras** $\text{Auth}(M)$: one party holds the authoritative element $\bullet a$ (the "global truth"), others hold fragments $\circ b$ with the coherence law $b \preccurlyeq a$. This models knowledge shared among threads: fragments cannot disagree with the authority.
- **Fractional permissions** $[\ell \mapsto v]^q$ with $q \in (0,1]$: $q = 1$ grants write access, $q < 1$ grants read access, and fractions compose: $[\ell \mapsto v]^{q_1} * [\ell \mapsto v]^{q_2} \dashv\vdash [\ell \mapsto v]^{q_1+q_2}$.



### 2.4 Invariants: sharing under atomicity



> **Theorem (Invariant access):** $\dfrac{e\ \text{atomic} \quad \{\triangleright I * P\}\,e\,\{\triangleright I * Q\}}{\{\boxed{I}^N * P\}\,e\,\{Q\}}$ (mask bookkeeping elided) [1, 9]

The later modality $\triangleright$ ("later") guards the invariant's contents to keep the logic's impredicative fixed points sound — invariants may mention other invariants and even Hoare triples themselves [2]. The price of sharing is *atomicity*: after the atomic step, the invariant must be re-established, which is exactly the proof obligation that the shared state was left consistent.

## 3. Methodology



1. **Abstract specification.** Each data structure is given an abstract predicate (e.g., `is_queue q vs`: "$q$ is a queue whose abstract contents are the sequence $vs$") and logically atomic specifications in the HOCAP/TAU style: each operation's pre/postcondition describes a single atomic effect on the abstract state, regardless of how many physical steps the implementation takes [3].

2. **Representation invariant + ghost protocol.** The physical heap layout is tied to an invariant $\boxed{I}^N$. A ghost protocol — typically an authoritative camera tracking the abstract sequence and per-thread contributions — records *which thread is entitled to linearize when*. Fractional permissions distribute read access to shared nodes.

3. **Linearization via prophecy.** For operations whose linearization point is determined only in the future (e.g., a dequeue whose CAS may fail and retry), *prophecy variables* [1, 6] let the proof "peek" at the value a future atomic read will return, resolving the linearization decision at the moment the prophecy is created rather than when it is fulfilled.

4. **Mechanization in Coq.** Proofs are written in Iris Proof Mode, an interactive tactic language embedded in Coq [1]. Every lemma is machine-checked; the adequacy theorem is not re-proven per project but inherited from the Iris metatheory.

5. **Case-study selection.** We concentrate on algorithms where the *concurrency is the point*: the Michael–Scott queue (helping, lagging tail), hazard pointers and RCU (reclamation races), Harris's list and the Chase–Lev deque (SMR clients) [3], and reference counting with weak pointers (token protocols) [6].

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 The two pillars: cameras and invariants, in full detail



- $\text{Auth}(\text{List}(Val))$ tracking the abstract queue contents authoritatively;
- an agreement/exclusive camera for node identities;
- fractional points-to for shared node payloads.



```haskell
-- The update modality, in the Iris logic (schematic)
-- (ε₁ ⇒ ε₂) P  :  "P can be established by opening invariants in mask ε₁,
--                  ending with mask ε₂, via frame-preserving ghost updates"
spec_update :: InvMask -> InvMask -> iProp -> iProp
spec_update e1 e2 p = updateModality e1 e2 p
```

Invariants, meanwhile, are *persistent* (duplicable) but their contents are *ephemeral*: each atomic step borrows the invariant's resources and must return them. The later modality $\triangleright$ appears because invariants are defined by guarded recursion — an invariant may refer to assertions about invariants, and $\triangleright$ stratifies the definition to keep it well-founded [2]. In practice, the Iris Proof Mode tactic `iInv` opens an invariant around an atomic expression and `iModIntro` discharges the later; the proof engineer rarely thinks about step-indices directly.

The deep point — the one that unifies the "700 separation logics" — is that *every* advanced reasoning principle (linearizability tokens, helping protocols, lifetime logic, prophecy) is *derived* from these two mechanisms rather than built into the logic's core [3]. When a new concurrency primitive appears, the response is a new camera and a new invariant pattern, not a new logic.

### 4.2 Anatomy of a proof: the Michael–Scott queue

The Michael–Scott (MS) queue [7] is the canonical fine-grained benchmark: a linked list with sentinel dummy node, `Head` and `Tail` pointers, where `enqueue` links a new node via CAS on `tail.next` and `dequeue` swings `Head` via CAS. The tail may *lag* — point to a node whose successor is non-null — and any thread may *help* advance it. This breaks naive linearization arguments: an `enqueue`'s effect (the node becoming visible) and its "official" linearization step are entangled with helpers.

The Iris proof (in the style of the mechanized developments surveyed in [1]) proceeds as follows:

1. **Abstract state.** `is_queue q vs` with ghost authority $\bullet vs$ over the abstract sequence.
2. **Invariant.** $\boxed{\exists\, h\,t\,xs.\; \text{Head} \mapsto h * \text{Tail} \mapsto t * \text{linked}(h, xs) * \bullet xs \ldots}^N$: the physical list always matches the authoritative sequence, and the tail points somewhere *at or behind* the true end — the lag is explicitly permitted by the invariant.
3. **Enqueue.** The thread allocates a node, then CASes `tail.next` from `null` to the new node. The *successful* CAS is the linearization point: at that instant, the thread performs a ghost update appending to $\bullet vs$ and transfers node ownership into the invariant. If the CAS fails because another thread helped, the operation retries — the prophecy variable predicts whether *this* iteration's CAS will succeed, so the ghost update is attached to the correct physical step even though success is decided by contention.
4. **Dequeue.** Reading `Head`, then `head.next`; if null, the queue is empty (linearizes at the read); else CAS `Head` to `head.next`. The old dummy node's payload is returned; its memory is retired to the SMR scheme rather than freed immediately — which is where §4.3 takes over.

```rust
// Michael–Scott enqueue, annotated with linearization reasoning
fn enqueue(&self, val: T) {
    let node = Box::new(Node { val, next: AtomicPtr::null() });
    loop {
        let tail = self.tail.load(Acquire);          // open invariant: read tail
        let next = tail.next.load(Acquire);
        if next.is_null() {
            // *** linearization point: successful CAS publishes the node ***
            if tail.next.compare_exchange(null, node, Release, Relaxed).is_ok() {
                let _ = self.tail.compare_exchange(tail, node, Release, Relaxed); // help
                return; // ghost update: vs := vs ++ [val]
            }
        } else {
            let _ = self.tail.compare_exchange(tail, next, Release, Relaxed);    // help lagging tail
        }
    }
}
```

The TLA+-level intuition behind the invariant — that every reachable state satisfies "tail is behind the end" — can be stated as a state predicate checked against the abstract model:

```tla
------------------------------ MODULE MSQueue ------------------------------
VARIABLES head, tail, nodes, abstractSeq
TypeOK == /\ head \in DOMAIN nodes
          /\ tail \in DOMAIN nodes
          /\ abstractSeq \in Seq(Values)
\* The representation invariant: the physical list from head
\* always realizes abstractSeq, and tail lags at most arbitrarily.
Inv == /\ ListFrom(head, nodes) = abstractSeq
       /\ IsReachable(tail, head, nodes)   \* tail never ahead of the end
=============================================================================
```

The proof's real content is showing that each CAS *preserves* `Inv` while moving the ghost authority in lockstep — the frame rule then guarantees that other threads' reasoning is unaffected.

### 4.3 Safe memory reclamation: hazard pointers and RCU as modular specifications

Lock-free structures cannot simply `free` a dequeued node: a concurrent thread may still hold a private pointer to it. Two industrial schemes solve this:

- **Hazard pointers** [8]: each thread publishes the pointers it is using in a shared array; a thread retiring a node scans the array and frees the node only if no hazard pointer protects it.
- **RCU** (read-copy-update) [11]: readers execute in *read-side critical sections* without synchronization; a reclaimer waits for a *grace period* (all pre-existing readers finished) before freeing.

Jung et al. [3] give the first *modular* CSL specifications of both, mechanized in Iris. The key abstraction is a **protection predicate**: `protected(p)` means "pointer $p$ is safe to dereference." For hazard pointers, the spec centers on *validating* protection — after publishing a hazard pointer and re-reading the source, the thread obtains a persistent `protected` token; the reclamation proof shows that a node is freed only when no such token exists. For RCU, the spec supports *optimistic traversal* to possibly-retired nodes, with the grace-period protocol encoded as ghost state: an authoritative "current generation" camera, fragments witnessing that a reader's critical section began before the generation advanced.

In Iris terms, the SMR library exports abstract predicates and logically atomic triples; clients (Harris's list, the Harris–Michael list, the Chase–Lev deque, RDCSS) are verified *against the spec*, never against the reclamation implementation [3]. This is compositionality at the specification level — the exact property Parkinson's "700 logics" complaint demanded.

```rust
// Hazard-pointer protection pattern (C++26 interface [P3427R1]-style)
hazard_pointer hp = make_hazard_pointer();
Node* p = hp.protect(src);   // publish + validate: afterwards, `protected(p)`
int v = p->value;            // safe: dereference under protection
hp.reset_protection(p);      // release: protection token consumed
```

The subtlety the proof captures: between publishing the hazard pointer and re-reading `src`, the node could have been retired and freed — the validation read *re-checks* that `src` still holds `p`, and only then is `protected(p)` granted. The invariant ties the retired-set ghost state to the hazard-pointer array contents, so the scan's "no hazard pointer protects $p$" conclusion is a *logical* fact, not a race-prone observation.

### 4.4 Prophecy, helping, and linearization points of the future

Two mechanisms handle the hardest linearization arguments:

- **Prophecy variables** [1, 6] resolve *future-dependent* linearization points. A dequeue's CAS may fail; whether *this* attempt linearizes depends on a future event. The proof creates a prophecy for the CAS result at the attempt's start, and case-splits immediately: if the prophecy says "success," the ghost update is performed *now*, justified retroactively when the CAS indeed succeeds. Soundness holds because prophecies can only be resolved by the actual future value.
- **Helping protocols** are encoded as ghost-state obligations: when thread A begins an operation, it deposits a "receipt" token in shared ghost state; if thread B's atomic step completes A's logical effect (as in the MS queue's tail-advance or a flat-combining-style protocol), B consumes the receipt and performs A's ghost update. A's proof then linearizes at B's step — the token transfer makes this precise rather than metaphorical.

> **Theorem (Logical atomicity):** An operation with a logically atomic specification $\langle P\rangle\,e\,\langle v.\,Q\rangle$ linearizes at exactly one physical step, even if that step executes in a helping thread; the receipt-token protocol transfers the linearization obligation soundly.

Together, these give Iris its characteristic strength: *no syntactic restriction* on where linearization points may fall. The proof engineer pays in ghost-state design — the protocol must be invented per algorithm — but the logic never needs extension.

Several observations emerge:

1. **Proofs compose.** The SMR work [3] is the clearest demonstration: the hazard-pointer and RCU *implementations* are verified once against abstract specs, and four sophisticated clients are verified against the specs alone. A bug fix in the reclamation implementation requires re-verifying only the implementation proof.
2. **Ghost-state design dominates cost.** In every case study, the intellectual effort lies in *inventing* the protocol (which tokens, which invariant, where linearization happens); the Coq mechanization is then largely systematic, aided by Iris Proof Mode tactics (`iInv`, `iMod`, `iApply`) [1].
3. **Adequacy is inherited.** None of these projects re-proves soundness of the logic. The Iris adequacy theorem [1] turns each $\text{WP}$ derivation into a guarantee about the operational semantics of the language (HeapLang/λRust), so the trusted base is Coq + the Iris model + the language semantics — not the thousands of lines of proof script.
4. **Automation is partial but real.** Diaframe-style proof search [6] can discharge parts of these proofs automatically, and the type-based approach of [4] verifies many SMR clients *fully automatically* (up to two orders of magnitude faster than competitors) by compiling the invariant check to off-the-shelf verifiers — at the cost of expressiveness relative to full Iris.

## 6. Limitations

**Proof-engineering cost.** The numbers in §5 are honest: thousands of lines of Coq per data structure, and tens of thousands for RustBelt [5]. Iris does not make verification cheap; it makes it *possible* and *compositional*. The ghost protocol must still be invented by a human expert.

**Invariant discipline.** Invariants can only be opened around *atomic* physical steps. Algorithms whose correctness depends on multi-step atomicity (e.g., reasoning about a sequence of instructions as one logical step without hardware support) need additional machinery (e.g., logically atomic triples with more elaborate protocols), and reentrancy through invariants is restricted by masks to prevent logical inconsistency.

**Weak memory.** The core Iris program logic targets sequentially consistent (or language-level) semantics; extending these proofs to hardware weak-memory models (x86-TSO, Arm, RC11) is active research with its own logics (e.g., GPS, and Iris-based weak-memory extensions [9]) rather than a solved problem.

**Liveness and progress.** Iris, like most CSLs, proves *safety* (nothing bad happens: memory safety, linearizability). Lock-freedom, wait-freedom, and starvation-freedom are liveness properties requiring different techniques (e.g., Iris-based logics with obligations, or refinement approaches); a linearizable queue that never returns is still "verified" under safety alone.

**Trusted base.** Adequacy reduces trust to Coq's kernel, the Iris model, and the language semantics — but the *specification* itself (e.g., "the abstract queue behaves like a FIFO") is a human artifact. A verified implementation of the wrong spec is still wrong.

## 7. Conclusion

Iris's central thesis — that *user-defined ghost state* and *invariants* are a complete basis for modern concurrent separation logic [1, 2, 3] — has moved from slogan to engineering practice. We have shown how the two mechanisms compose to verify the algorithms that define the fine-grained concurrency frontier: the Michael–Scott queue with its helping and lagging tail [7], hazard-pointer and RCU reclamation through modular protection-predicate specifications [3], and token-based reference counting [6]. Prophecy variables and receipt-token helping protocols handle linearization points that lie in the future or in another thread's code, with no extension to the logic's core. The adequacy theorem converts every such derivation into a guarantee about real executions.

The honest accounting of §5–§6 stands: verification remains expensive, safety is not liveness, and weak memory is still frontier territory. But the trajectory is clear — from hand proofs of single algorithms, to mechanized proofs of algorithm *families* sharing one reclamation spec, to type-based automation [4] discharging whole classes without manual proof. Iris did not end the need for human ingenuity in concurrency verification; it gave that ingenuity a stable, checkable foundation, and a machine that checks the work.
