# Gradual Typing and the Dynamic Gradual Guarantee: Contract Semantics, the Blame Theorem, and Space-Efficient Casts

## Abstract

Gradual typing lets programmers mix static and dynamic typing in a single program by introducing a dynamic type `?` alongside a precision ordering on types. Siek and Taha's semantics [1] gives this idea a rigorous foundation through cast insertion: every boundary between a more and a less precise type is elaborated into an explicit runtime cast. When casts fail they produce *blame*, and the blame theorem of Wadler and Findler [3] pins that failure on exactly the program component that violated the contract — provably exonerating fully typed code. This article develops the theory end to end: the precision and consistency relations of the Gradually Typed Lambda Calculus (GTLC), higher-order casts as polarity-carrying contracts in the Findler–Felleisen tradition [2], coercion-based compilation that bounds space (Herman, Tomb, and Flanagan [4]), and the static and dynamic gradual guarantees that make type migration behavior-preserving [5]. It closes with the empirical record — Reticulated Python [6], Gradualtalk [7], Typed Racket, and the soundness–performance spectrum [8] — and with open limitations.

---

## 1. Introduction

Statically typed languages catch errors early but punish exploratory programming; dynamically typed languages move fast but defer every invariant check to runtime. **Gradual typing** is the thesis that these are endpoints of one continuum rather than rival camps. A gradually typed language adds a distinguished *dynamic type*, written `?` (or `⋆`, `Dyn`, `Any` depending on the tradition), to an otherwise static type system. Programmers annotate the parts they understand; the rest stays dynamic; and the type system guarantees that typed and untyped regions interact safely [1].

Safety at the boundary is enforced *dynamically*, by casts. Wherever a value flows from a less precise type to a more precise one, the compiler inserts a cast that is checked at runtime. A function of type `? → Int` applied to an argument of type `?` is fine, but the moment a value of type `?` is demanded at type `Int`, a cast `⟨? ⇒ᵖ Int⟩` inspects the value and either succeeds or raises **blame** `p` — a runtime error carrying the identity of the cast that failed.

Blame is not mere error reporting. In higher-order settings a cast on a function type cannot be decided immediately: a function `f : ?` cast to `Int → Int` might return a non-integer only on some future call. The cast therefore installs a *contract* — a wrapper that monitors every interaction — and blame must correctly identify which party, the wrapped function or its calling context, broke the agreement. Wadler and Findler's **blame theorem** [3] proves that this accounting is exact: a fully typed component can never be blamed. That result is the moral center of the theory: gradual typing does not merely *tolerate* typed–untyped mixing; it *vindicates* the typed parts.

This article surveys the resulting edifice: Siek and Taha's semantics [1], the Findler–Felleisen contract machinery it builds on [2], the blame calculus and its theorem [3], space-efficient coercions [4], the gradual guarantees that legitimize incremental migration [5], and the systems that carried the theory into practice [6][7][8].

## 2. Background

### 2.1 The precision ordering and consistency

The core calculus is the **Gradually Typed Lambda Calculus (GTLC)** [1][5]. Types are given by

```
T ::= ? | B | T → T          (B ranges over base types)
```

and the *precision* relation `T₁ ⊑ T₂` — read "`T₁` is less precise than `T₂`" — is generated by:

- `? ⊑ T` for every `T` (the dynamic type is the least precise type);
- `B ⊑ B`;
- `T₁ → T₂ ⊑ T₁′ → T₂′` iff `T₁′ ⊑ T₁` and `T₂ ⊑ T₂′` (contravariant in the domain).

Precision extends pointwise to terms: `e ⊑ e′` when `e′` is `e` with some `?` annotations replaced by more precise types. This ordering is what makes "gradual migration" a formal notion rather than a slogan: moving along `⊑` *is* adding annotations.

Because `?` is compatible with everything, the static checker cannot use subtyping. It uses **consistency**, `T₁ ~ T₂`, defined by `? ~ T` and `T ~ ?` for all `T`, `B ~ B`, and `(T₁ → T₂) ~ (T₁′ → T₂′)` iff `T₁ ~ T₁′` and `T₂ ~ T₂′`. Consistency is reflexive and symmetric but *not transitive* — `Int ~ ?` and `? ~ Bool` hold while `Int ~ Bool` does not — and that non-transitivity is precisely why boundaries need runtime casts.

### 2.2 Casts and the cast calculus

Type checking elaborates the surface program into a **cast calculus** by inserting a cast `⟨T ⇒ᵖ S⟩ e` wherever consistency, rather than subtyping, justifies a use. Each cast carries a blame label `p` identifying the source boundary. First-order casts reduce directly:

```
⟨B ⇒ᵖ B⟩ v      → v
⟨? ⇒ᵖ B⟩ v      → v        if v has tag B
⟨? ⇒ᵖ B⟩ v      → blame p  if v has tag B′ ≠ B
```

Higher-order casts decompose into **wrappers**. Casting a function from `T₁ → T₂` to `S₁ → S₂` produces a new function that casts its argument *contravariantly* and its result *covariantly* [3]:

```
⟨T₁→T₂ ⇒ᵖ S₁→S₂⟩ v  →  λx:S₁. ⟨T₂ ⇒ᵖ S₂⟩ (v (⟨S₁ ⇒ᵖ̄ T₁⟩ x))
```

The bar over `p` is **polarity**: the argument cast blames the *context* (negative polarity, `p̄`) while the result cast blames the *term* (positive polarity, `p`). This polarity discipline is inherited directly from higher-order contracts [2], where a function contract `C₁ → C₂` blames the function's producer for violating `C₂` and the consumer for violating `C₁`.

### 2.3 Contracts as the semantic ancestor

Gradual typing's runtime semantics is an application of Findler and Felleisen's theory of contracts for higher-order functions [2]. In that theory, a contract is not a predicate but a *protocol* between two parties: the *positive* party (the term being contracted) and the *negative* party (its context). A first-order contract is checked immediately; a higher-order contract is decomposed into a wrapper that swaps blame polarity at each contravariant position. Wadler and Findler [3] observed that a gradual cast is exactly such a contract — the cast `⟨T ⇒ᵖ S⟩` is a contract between the value (positive) and the context demanding type `S` (negative) — and rebuilt the blame assignment of the entire gradual language on this foundation.

## 3. Methodology

The results surveyed here were established with three complementary methods.

**Operational metatheory.** The GTLC and its cast calculus are given as small-step operational semantics, and the central theorems — type safety of the cast calculus, the blame theorem, the gradual guarantees — are proved by syntactic methods: progress and preservation lemmas, simulation arguments between source and elaborated terms, and logical-relations-style reasoning about precision [1][3][5].

**Cast and coercion calculi as compilation targets.** Rather than reasoning about the surface language directly, the methodology elaborates it into an explicit intermediate language (the *blame calculus* [3]) or a *coercion calculus* [4] where every runtime check is a first-class term. Space bounds, blame correctness, and error behavior are then proved about the intermediate language and lifted back. This elaboration strategy — *cast insertion* — is itself a design object: where casts are inserted (eagerly at boundaries, lazily at elimination forms) determines both semantics and performance [7].

**Empirical implementation.** The theory was validated by building real systems — Typed Racket, Reticulated Python [6], Gradualtalk [7] — and measuring migration cost on benchmarks [8]. These implementations double as experiments: Reticulated's three cast semantics (guarded, transient, monotonic) are three points in the design space, and their measured overheads test the theory's predictions about where the cost of soundness lives.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 The blame calculus and the Blame Theorem

Wadler and Findler's blame calculus [3] makes casts first-class: the term `⟨T ⇒ᵖ S⟩ e` casts `e` from `T` to `S`, with `p` a blame label and `p̄` its negation. Evaluation of a failed cast yields `blame p` or `blame p̄`. The crucial definitions:

- A cast is a **narrowing** when `S ⊑ T` (the target is more precise — information is demanded).
- A cast is a **widening** when `T ⊑ S` (the target is less precise — information is forgotten).

> **Theorem (Blame [3]).** *Narrowing casts can only produce positive blame; widening casts can only produce negative blame.*

In the slide-talk example: casting `⌈λy. y+1⌉ : ?` to `Int → Int` (narrowing) and applying it can yield `blame p` if the body misbehaves — the *term* is at fault — but never `blame p̄`. Conversely, widening `λy:Int. y+1` to `?` and misusing it in a dynamic context can only blame the *context*.

The corollary is the paper's title:

> **Corollary (Well-typed programs can't be blamed).** *If a term elaborates from a fully statically typed source program, evaluation never produces blame on a label introduced by the elaboration.*

Every failure is attributable to a cast the programmer effectively wrote — a boundary between typed and untyped code. Static code is provably exonerated. This is the sense in which blame is a *theorem* rather than a debugging aid: it is a guarantee about the language, proved once, that every user inherits.

### 4.2 Higher-order casts, threesomes, and contract decomposition

A naive implementation of `⟨T ⇒ S⟩` for function types checks the cast by comparing `T` and `S` structurally at runtime, which is expensive and, worse, can stack unboundedly many wrappers. Siek and Wadler's *threesomes* [POPL 2010] factor every cast through the dynamic type:

```
⟨T ⇒ᵖ S⟩  =  ⟨? ⇒ᵖ² S⟩ ∘ ⟨T ⇒ᵖ¹ ?⟩
```

The first cast (*tag check*) verifies that the value's runtime tag matches the source type's top-level constructor; the second (*re-tag*) installs the target contract. Because the middle type is always `?`, casts compose: adjacent casts `⟨T ⇒ ?⟩` followed by `⟨? ⇒ S⟩` can be *fused* into a single cast whose blame behavior is the composition of the two. This factorization is the bridge to the coercion calculi of §4.3 — it shows that arbitrary casts are expressible with a small, closed set of primitive coercions (identity, injection, projection, failure, function coercions), and that a normal form exists for their composition.

The practical payoff is that higher-order cast decomposition becomes *lazy and compositional*: a function proxy carries a single composed coercion rather than a tower of wrappers, and each β-reduction applies the coercion exactly once.

### 4.3 Space-efficient coercions (Herman–Siek–Taha)

The naive proxy semantics has a space leak with teeth: each cast around a function allocates a wrapper, and in a loop the wrappers accumulate without bound — destroying tail-call optimization and making memory usage proportional to the number of boundary crossings rather than the program's types. Herman, Tomb, and Flanagan [4] replace proxies with **coercions**, a first-class representation of casts with an explicit *composition operator*:

```
c ::= id_T | ⊥ᵖ (failure with blame p) | G! (injection into ?) | G?ᵖ (projection)
    | c₁ → c₂ (function coercion) | c₁ ; c₂ (sequential composition)
```

Evaluation applies coercions to values, and — critically — *composes adjacent coercions eagerly* according to equations such as

```
(G! ; G?ᵖ)       →  id          (injection then projection cancels)
(c₁ ; c₂) ; c₃   →  c₁ ; (c₂ ; c₃)
((c₁→c₂) ; (c₃→c₄)) → (c₃;c₁) → (c₂;c₄)
```

Because composition is associative and admits these cancellation laws, every coercion has a normal form, and Herman et al. prove a **space bound**: the size of any runtime coercion is bounded by a function of the largest type appearing in the program — *not* by the number of casts performed. Tail calls stay tail calls. The result preserves the blame behavior of the proxy semantics while making the asymptotic space cost honest. Their calculus also *detects certain errors earlier* than the proxy semantics, since composing a projection with an incompatible injection reduces to `⊥ᵖ` immediately rather than waiting for the wrapped value to be used.

### 4.4 The static and dynamic gradual guarantees

Siek, Vitousek, Cimini, and Boyland [5] asked what "gradual" should *mean* as a formal property of a language, and answered with two criteria — the *refined criteria for gradual typing*:

> **Static gradual guarantee.** *If `⊢ e : T` and `e ⊑ e′` (e′ has more precise annotations), then `⊢ e′ : T′` for some `T′ ⊑ T`.*

Removing annotations never breaks typeability; adding them never breaks it either. Migration is statically safe in both directions.

> **Dynamic gradual guarantee.** *If `⊢ e : T`, `⊢ e′ : T′` with `e ⊑ e′` and `T ⊑ T′`, then the observable behavior of `e′` is at least as defined as that of `e`: evaluation outcomes are related by precision, where `blame p` is the least outcome.*

Concretely: making annotations more precise can turn a value into *blame* (a new check can fail), but it can never turn a value into a *different* value, and removing annotations can turn blame into a value but never change which value a program computes. Migration is dynamically safe: you cannot "break" a working program by adding types — the only new behavior is a contract violation that pinpoints a genuine inconsistency between the annotation and the code.

Together with the blame theorem, the guarantees complete the story: the gradual guarantees say migration preserves meaning, and the blame theorem says that when meaning is *not* preserved (a cast fails), the fault lies with the less-typed side. Subsequent work (e.g., Garcia et al.'s *Abstracting Gradual Typing*) showed these criteria are not ad hoc: they fall out of a general construction that derives gradual type systems from static ones via Galois connections on precision.

## 5. Empirical Results and Proofs

**Metatheory.** The headline proof results are: (i) type safety of the blame calculus — well-typed cast-calculus terms never get stuck, only blame [3]; (ii) the blame theorem and its corollary above [3]; (iii) soundness and the space bound for the coercion calculus — coercion size is `O` of the maximum type size in the program [4]; (iv) the static and dynamic gradual guarantees for the GTLC [5]. These are paper proofs, but the calculi are small enough that mechanization is feasible, and later work has machine-checked versions of the blame theorem.

**Reticulated Python.** Vitousek, Kent, Siek, and Baker [6] implemented gradual typing for Python with three cast semantics selectable per-module: *guarded* (proxy-based, full blame tracking), *transient* (shallow tag checks at use sites, no proxies, no blame), and *monotonic* (references gain precision monotonically, enabling unboxed representations). On case-study programs, the guarded semantics imposed severe overhead on boundary-heavy code, while the transient semantics stayed within small constant factors of untyped Python — at the price of abandoning the blame theorem's strong guarantees. This is the empirical face of a theoretical trade-off: full blame tracking costs; shallow checking is cheap but blames imprecisely.

**Gradualtalk.** Allende et al. [7] brought gradual typing to Smalltalk and, crucially, studied *cast insertion strategies* for objects: where to place casts around message sends, instance-variable access, and first-class classes. Their DLS 2013 study showed that eager boundary casts versus lazy use-site casts differ markedly in both error-reporting quality and runtime cost, and that a practical object system needs both. Gradualtalk demonstrated that the theory survives contact with a real image-based, reflective object model.

**Typed Racket and the soundness–performance spectrum.** Tobin-Hochstadt and Felleisen's migratory typing for Racket (DLS 2006; POPL 2008) was the first large-scale deployment of blame-tracked contracts at module boundaries. Greenman and Felleisen [8] later put three enforcement strategies — *deep* (contract-based, full soundness à la [3]), *shallow* (transient tag checks à la [6]), and *erasure* (no enforcement) — into a single semantic framework and measured them on identical benchmarks. The results were stark: deep enforcement can slow programs by *orders of magnitude* on contract-heavy benchmarks, while shallow enforcement typically costs well under 2×. The paper's message is methodological as much as empirical: claims about "the cost of gradual typing" are meaningless without saying *which* soundness theorem you are paying for.

| System | Enforcement | Blame tracking | Typical overhead |
|---|---|---|---|
| Typed Racket (deep) | Higher-order contracts | Full (polarity-exact) | 1×–100×+ on boundary-heavy code [8] |
| Reticulated (guarded) | Proxies | Full | High on fine-grained boundaries [6] |
| Reticulated (transient) | Shallow tag checks | None (first failure only) | Near-untyped [6] |
| Gradualtalk | Boundary + use-site casts | Partial | Moderate; strategy-dependent [7] |
| Coercion calculus [4] | Composed coercions | Full | Bounded space; constant-factor time |

## 6. Limitations

The theory, for all its elegance, has sharp edges.

**Blame precision vs. performance.** The blame theorem's exact accounting requires tracking every cast's polarity through every higher-order flow — precisely the bookkeeping that makes deep enforcement expensive [8]. Production systems routinely weaken blame (Reticulated's transient mode, TypeScript's erasure) because exact blame does not fit performance budgets. "Well-typed programs can't be blamed" holds only in the calculus with full tracking; in a shallow system, a typed component *can* observe a failure it did not cause.

**The dynamic type is viral in practice.** The gradual guarantees promise smooth migration, but user studies and experience reports find that programmers often annotate "all or nothing": partial annotation yields partial checking, and the value of a half-annotated codebase is unclear. The guarantees are about *possibility*, not *ergonomics*.

**Objects and state strain the model.** The GTLC is a pure functional core. Mutable references need monotonic or guarded-reference disciplines; objects need per-message-send cast strategies [7]; first-class classes, mixins, and reflection resist clean precision orderings. Each extension re-proves the metatheory from scratch — there is no fully general recipe, though abstracting gradual typing (Garcia et al.) goes furthest toward one.

**Blame is not debugging.** A blame label identifies *which cast* failed and its polarity, but in a large program the label points at a boundary, not a root cause. Collaborative-blame work (Vitousek, Swords, and Siek, POPL 2017) improves this by accumulating provenance across casts, but blame remains an error *attribution* mechanism, not a program slicer.

**Space bounds are about coercions, not programs.** Herman et al.'s bound [4] controls coercion size, but a gradually typed program can still allocate heavily at boundaries (boxing values into `?`, building closures for wrappers). Space-efficiency of casts is necessary but not sufficient for space-efficiency of programs.

---

## 7. Conclusion

Gradual typing's twenty-year arc — from Siek and Taha's 2006 semantics [1] through the blame theorem [3], space-efficient coercions [4], and the refined criteria [5], to the empirical spectrum of enforcement strategies [8] — is one of the cleanest theory-to-practice pipelines in programming-languages research. Its central intellectual achievement is to have made *blame* a mathematical object: not a stack trace, but a polarity-carrying label governed by a theorem that exonerates typed code. Its central practical lesson is humbler: soundness has a price, and the bill comes due at every typed–untyped boundary.

The open frontier is reconciling the two — blame that is both exact and cheap, migration that is both guaranteed and ergonomic, and a metatheory that covers the objects, effects, and reflection of real languages without being rebuilt for each one. The gradual guarantees give us the specification of what "gradual" must mean; the blame theorem gives us the specification of what failure must mean. Whatever comes next will be judged against both.
