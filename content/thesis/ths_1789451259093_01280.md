# Cavity Quantum Electrodynamics: Jaynes–Cummings Dynamics, Strong Coupling, and Quantum Nondemolition Readout of Superconducting Qubits

## Abstract

Cavity quantum electrodynamics (QED) describes the coherent interaction of a single two-level emitter with a single quantized mode of the electromagnetic field, the simplest nontrivial setting in which light and matter exchange energy reversibly. This thesis develops the Jaynes–Cummings model from its 1963 formulation through the strong-coupling experiments that resolved its dressed-state ladder, culminating in the dispersive regime that underpins quantum nondemolition (QND) readout of superconducting qubits. We derive the dressed-state spectrum, the anharmonic ladder responsible for vacuum Rabi splitting and photon blockade, and the Schrieffer–Wolff transformation that yields the dispersive Hamiltonian with shift χ and critical photon number ncrit = (Δ/2g)². A quantitative comparison of weak, strong, and ultrastrong coupling frames optical cavity QED — Rydberg atoms, trapped atoms — against circuit QED with transmon qubits, where cooperativities exceeding ten thousand are routine. We analyze measurement-induced dephasing, the quantum efficiency limits of dispersive readout, and the breakdown of the rotating-wave approximation, closing with limitations and open research directions.

## 1 Introduction

Few models in quantum physics have repaid six decades of attention as handsomely as the **Jaynes–Cummings model** of a two-level atom coupled to a single cavity mode. Introduced to settle a controversy over whether the maser required a quantized field, it became the canonical laboratory for *dressed states*, *collapse and revival* of Rabi oscillations, and the *photon blockade* — and, in its far-detuned limit, the theoretical backbone of **circuit quantum electrodynamics (circuit QED)**, the architecture behind virtually all present-day superconducting quantum processors [1].

The central physical idea is deceptively simple. Confine one mode of the electromagnetic field in a high-finesse resonator, place a single two-level emitter at an antinode of that mode, and the emitter can exchange a photon with the field faster than either subsystem loses coherence. When the coherent coupling rate *g* exceeds both the cavity photon decay rate κ and the atomic decay rate γ, the atom and field cease to be separate entities: the eigenstates are *polaritonic* superpositions, the transmission spectrum splits into a doublet, and the ladder of excited states becomes anharmonic. This *strong-coupling regime* was first conquered with Rydberg atoms traversing superconducting microwave cavities and later with single trapped atoms in optical Fabry–Pérot resonators [8], and its clearest spectroscopic fingerprint — the **vacuum Rabi splitting** — was eventually observed for a *single* trapped atom [11].

The second half of this thesis concerns what happens when the qubit and resonator are *deliberately detuned*. Then the interaction no longer exchanges excitations; instead the resonator frequency acquires a qubit-state-dependent shift ±χ. Probing the resonator's transmitted phase thereby measures the qubit **without destroying it** — a *quantum nondemolition* measurement. This dispersive readout, proposed and analyzed for superconducting qubits in 2004 [1] and demonstrated the same year [2], remains the industry standard for qubit measurement, and its theory — photon-dependent dephasing [5], amplifier quantum efficiency [4], and the dynamics of the transmitted field [6] — is among the most complete measurement theories in physics.

We proceed as follows: § 2 traces the historical arc from Rydberg-atom microwave cavities to circuit QED and the transmon; § 3 develops the Hamiltonian, dispersive, and open-system machinery; § 4 dives into the ladder, coupling regimes, dispersive measurement, blockade, and backaction; § 5 confronts theory with the landmark experiments; § 6 states the limitations; § 7 concludes.

## 2 Background

### From masers to microwave cavities

The Jaynes–Cummings Hamiltonian was written down in 1963 to describe a two-level atom interacting with a quantized cavity field in the context of the beam maser, originally to settle whether the maser required field quantization at all.

The experimental story begins with **Rydberg atoms**. Because a Rydberg atom's electric dipole moment scales as the square of the principal quantum number, circular Rydberg states couple enormously to microwave photons, and superconducting niobium cavities at millikelvin temperatures can store a microwave photon for a tenth of a second. The Haroche group in Paris sent individual Rydberg atoms through such cavities and observed vacuum Rabi oscillations, Schrödinger-cat states of the field, and — ultimately — *quantum nondemolition counting of single photons* by Ramsey interferometry on atoms that never absorbed the photon, work recognized with the 2012 Nobel Prize in Physics. A concise pedagogical account of the strong-coupling phenomenology in these systems is given in [8].

### Optical cavity QED: single atoms, single photons

Parallel progress in the optical domain, led by the Kimble group at Caltech, pushed Fabry–Pérot resonators to finesses above one million and trapped single cesium atoms at cavity antinodes. Milestones include:

1. Observation of the **vacuum Rabi spectrum for one trapped atom**, a clean normal-mode doublet with no ensemble averaging [11].
2. **Photon blockade**: driving the atom–cavity system at one dressed-state frequency blocks the transmission of a second photon, producing strongly sub-Poissonian light with g^(2)(0) well below unity [9].
3. **Nonlinear spectroscopy of the second doublet**: driving a two-photon transition revealed the |2,±⟩ dressed states, whose splitting 2√2 g differs from the first doublet's 2g — direct spectroscopic proof of the ladder's anharmonicity [10].

These experiments established cavity QED as the definitive platform for *deterministic* light–matter interaction: one atom, one photon, with unit efficiency in principle.

### Circuit QED and the transmon era

In 2004 the Yale–Sherbrooke collaboration transplanted cavity QED onto a chip. A Cooper-pair box (an artificial atom formed by a Josephson junction) was coupled capacitively to a coplanar-waveguide transmission-line resonator, and the transmission spectrum displayed the vacuum Rabi splitting — *strong coupling in a solid-state device* [2]. The accompanying theory [1] showed that in the far-detuned *dispersive* limit the same device performs QND qubit readout, and derived the measurement-induced dephasing that accompanies it.

The Cooper-pair box was soon superseded by the **transmon** (2007), which shunts the junction with a large capacitance so that the Josephson energy dominates the charging energy (E_J/E_C ≫ 1), exponentially suppressing charge noise while retaining enough anharmonicity for qubit operation [3]. The transmon's weak anharmonicity (typically −200 to −350 MHz against a 4–6 GHz transition) forces a multi-level treatment of the dispersive shift, worked out in detail for Cooper-pair-box and transmon devices in [1] and generalized to fluxonium and other multi-level qudits [12].

## 3 Methodology

Our analysis rests on three theoretical pillars: the Jaynes–Cummings Hamiltonian with its exact dressed-state solution, the Schrieffer–Wolff transformation that block-diagonalizes it in the dispersive limit, and the Lindblad master equation that adds the dissipative channels κ (cavity) and γ (qubit). Numerical illustrations use exact diagonalization in truncated Fock bases.

### 3.1 The Jaynes–Cummings Hamiltonian

Consider a two-level emitter with transition frequency ω_q coupled to a cavity mode of frequency ω_r. The full dipole coupling is the **quantum Rabi Hamiltonian**

H_R = ħω_r a†a + (ħω_q/2) σ_z + ħg (a + a†)(σ_+ + σ_-),

where a annihilates a cavity photon, σ_± flip the qubit, and g is the vacuum coupling rate. The terms aσ_+ and a†σ_- — which create an excitation in both subsystems simultaneously — oscillate at ω_r + ω_q in the interaction picture. When g ≪ ω_r, ω_q and the detuning Δ = ω_q − ω_r satisfies |Δ| ≪ ω_r + ω_q, these *counter-rotating* terms average to zero and may be dropped: the **rotating-wave approximation (RWA)**. What remains is the Jaynes–Cummings Hamiltonian [8]:

H_JC = ħω_r a†a + (ħω_q/2) σ_z + ħg (aσ_+ + a†σ_-).

Crucially, H_JC conserves the total excitation number N = a†a + σ_+σ_-, so it decomposes into 2×2 blocks spanned by {|g, n+1⟩, |e, n⟩}. Diagonalization is elementary and yields the **dressed states**

|n, ±⟩ = cos(θ_n/2) |e, n⟩ ± sin(θ_n/2) |g, n+1⟩,

with mixing angle tan θ_n = 2g√(n+1)/Δ and eigenenergies

E_{n,±} = ħω_r (n + 1) ± (ħ/2) √(Δ² + 4g²(n+1)).

On resonance (Δ = 0) each rung splits by 2g√(n+1) — the **√(n+1) anharmonic ladder** at the heart of cavity QED.

> **Theorem (Vacuum Rabi splitting):** *For a resonantly coupled, lossless Jaynes–Cummings system, an initial state |e, 0⟩ undergoes sinusoidal population exchange with |g, 1⟩ at the vacuum Rabi frequency 2g. With cavity decay κ and qubit decay γ, the splitting is spectroscopically resolvable iff g > κ, γ, up to factors of order unity.*

### 3.2 The dispersive transformation

In the *dispersive regime* |Δ| ≫ g, excitations are never really exchanged; the coupling acts only through virtual photons. A Schrieffer–Wolff transformation U = exp(S) with anti-Hermitian generator S = (g/Δ)(a†σ_- − aσ_+) eliminates the interaction to first order in g/Δ, yielding [1]:

H_disp ≈ ħ(ω_r + χσ_z) a†a + (ħ/2)(ω_q + χ) σ_z,

with the **dispersive shift** χ = g²/Δ for a strict two-level system.

> **Theorem (Dispersive QND condition):** *To second order in g/Δ, the effective Hamiltonian commutes with the measured observable σ_z. The readout is QND up to corrections of order (g/Δ)², which permit residual Purcell decay at rate γ_P = κ(g/Δ)² and set the critical photon number n_crit = (Δ/2g)² beyond which the dispersive expansion breaks down.*

For the transmon — a weakly anharmonic multi-level system — virtual transitions through higher levels renormalize χ substantially [3].

### 3.3 Open-system dynamics

Dissipation is modeled by the Lindblad master equation ρ̇ = −(i/ħ)[H, ρ] + κD[a]ρ + γD[σ_-]ρ + (γ_φ/2)D[σ_z]ρ, with D[L]ρ = LρL† − ½{L†L, ρ}.

### 3.4 Numerical illustration: collapse and revival

The following compact simulation exhibits collapse and revival of atomic inversion for an atom initially excited in a coherent field with mean photon number n̄, the textbook dynamical fingerprint of field quantization [8]:

```python
import numpy as np

def collapse_revival(nbar=25, g=1.0, tmax=30.0, nmax=80, nt=1200):
    """Atomic inversion W(t) for |e> x |alpha> under H_JC on resonance."""
    n = np.arange(nmax)
    # Poisson weights of the coherent state (avoid bare index syntax in prose)
    poisson = np.exp(-nbar) * np.power(nbar, n)
    poisson = poisson / poisson.sum()
    t = np.linspace(0.0, tmax, nt)
    # Rabi frequency of each Fock component: Omega_n = 2 g sqrt(n+1)
    rabi = 2.0 * g * np.sqrt(n + 1.0)
    # W(t) = sum_n P(n) cos(Omega_n t)
    wt = np.sum(poisson[:, None] * np.cos(np.outer(rabi, t)), axis=0)
    t_collapse = np.sqrt(2.0) / g
    t_revival = 2.0 * np.pi * np.sqrt(nbar) / g
    return t, wt, t_collapse, t_revival
```

The dephasing of incommensurate Rabi frequencies produces collapse at t_c ∼ 1/g; their rephasing produces revivals at multiples of t_r = 2π√n̄/g.

A minimal Haskell encoding of the dressed-state structure makes the excitation-number conservation manifest at the type level:

```haskell
-- Excitation-number sectors of the Jaynes-Cummings model
data Qubit = G | E deriving (Show, Eq)

type PhotonNumber = Int

-- A basis state carries its total excitation number as a phantom tag
data Sector (total :: Nat) where
  Ground :: PhotonNumber -> Sector total   -- |g, n+1> with n+1 excitations
  Excited :: PhotonNumber -> Sector total  -- |e, n> with n excitations

-- Dressed doublet: mixing angle from detuning and coupling
dressedPair :: Double -> Double -> PhotonNumber -> ((Double, Double), Double)
dressedPair g delta n =
  let omega = sqrt (delta ** 2 + 4 * g ** 2 * fromIntegral (n + 1))
      theta = atan2 (2 * g * sqrt (fromIntegral (n + 1))) delta
  in (((cos (theta / 2), sin (theta / 2))), omega)
```

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The dressed-state ladder and vacuum Rabi physics

The spectrum E_{n,±} is a ladder of doublets whose splittings grow as √(n+1).

### 4.2 A taxonomy of coupling regimes

Whether the dressed states are observable depends on the hierarchy of three rates: the coupling *g*, the cavity decay κ, and the emitter decay γ.

| Regime | Condition | JC valid? | Signature |
|---|---|---|---|
| Weak coupling | g < κ, γ | yes | Purcell-enhanced exponential decay; no splitting |
| Strong coupling | g > κ, γ | yes | Vacuum Rabi splitting 2g; resolved dressed states |
| Ultrastrong coupling (USC) | g/ω_r ≳ 0.1 | **no** (RWA fails) | Bloch–Siegert shifts; ground-state virtual photons |
| Deep-strong coupling | g/ω_r ≳ 1 | no | Doubly-dressed spectrum; photon parity chains |

The **cooperativity** C = 4g²/(κγ) marks the onset of strong coupling at C > 1 [7].

- *Optical Fabry–Pérot, single Cs atom:* g/2π ∼ 10–30 MHz against κ/2π ∼ 1–5 MHz and γ/2π ∼ 3 MHz → C ∼ 10–100, yet normalized coupling g/ω_r ∼ 10⁻⁷ — firmly strong-coupled but utterly perturbative in g/ω_r [7].
- *Circuit QED transmon:* g/2π ∼ 50–200 MHz, κ/2π ∼ 1–10 MHz, γ/2π ∼ 0.01–0.1 MHz → g/κ ∼ 10–100 and C routinely above 10⁴, with g/ω_r ∼ 0.01 — deep in strong coupling while remaining safely within the RWA [1], [2], [3].

Reaching USC with a *single* atom is fundamentally hard: for hydrogenic matter the normalized coupling obeys Ω_R/ω_c ≈ α_fs^{3/2}/(ℓπ√V) with α_fs ≈ 1/137 the fine-structure constant, so sub-wavelength confinement (and its attendant losses) is mandatory [7].

### 4.3 Dispersive readout as QND measurement

Dispersive readout converts the qubit-state-dependent cavity pull ±χ into a measurable microwave phase shift.

Three quantities govern the measurement:

- **Measurement rate** Γ_m = κ|α_e − α_g|²/2 — the rate at which information leaks into the transmission line, which also equals the measurement-induced dephasing rate in the ideal case [4].
- **Signal-to-noise ratio**, set by the separation of the pointer states against the noise of the amplification chain; near-quantum-limited Josephson parametric amplifiers brought single-shot fidelities above 99 percent.
- **Quantum efficiency** 0 ≤ η ≤ 1 — the fraction of the leaking information actually recorded. A general three-step protocol (active photon depletion, optimal quadrature weighting) extracts η robustly in the linear regime, enabling optimal biasing of the parametric amplifier [4] (see the efficiency-extraction literature building on [4]).

Crucially, the dispersive measurement is only *approximately* QND.

### 4.4 Photon blockade and the anharmonic ladder as a resource

When the first doublet is driven resonantly, the transition to the second doublet is detuned by g(2 − √2) ≈ 0.59g (for the |1,+⟩ → |2,+⟩ path), so a second photon cannot enter the cavity: **photon blockade**.

### 4.5 Backaction, dephasing, and the quantum limit

Every measurement disturbs.

The dynamics of the transmitted field during weak continuous and pulsed measurement were worked out in full from the dispersive Jaynes–Cummings Hamiltonian with dissipation, yielding Bloch-type equations for the cavity field whose solutions construct the actual measurement operator used in state tomography [6].

## 5 Empirical Results / Proofs

**Vacuum Rabi splitting in circuit QED.** Wallraff et al. coupled a Cooper-pair box to a coplanar-waveguide resonator and observed the transmission spectrum split into two peaks separated by 2g/2π, with g exceeding both κ and the qubit decoherence rate — the first demonstration of strong coupling between a *macroscopic artificial atom* and a single microwave photon [2]. The splitting persisted as the qubit was tuned through the resonator, tracing the avoided crossing predicted by E_{n,±}.

**Photon blockade with one atom.** Birnbaum et al. trapped a single Cs atom in a high-finesse optical cavity and drove the |1,+⟩ dressed resonance. The transmitted light showed strong antibunching, g^(2)(0) < 1, with the blockade lifting as the drive was detuned from the dressed resonance — quantitative agreement with the Jaynes–Cummings master-equation model including birefringence and ac-Stark corrections [9].

**Two-photon spectroscopy of the second doublet.** Schuster et al. drove the atom–cavity system with sufficient intensity to deposit two photons at once, revealing a resonance at the |2,±⟩ frequency, detuned from the vacuum Rabi peaks by the ladder anharmonicity. The quadratic intensity dependence of this transmission proved its two-photon character and constituted direct spectroscopic evidence that the "cavity can accommodate only an integer number of photons" [10].

**Dispersive readout dynamics and fidelity.** Bianchetti et al. measured the full time-dependent transmitted field for weak continuous and pulsed dispersive readout and showed excellent agreement with Bloch-type equations derived from the dissipative dispersive Hamiltonian, validating the measurement-operator formalism used for tomography [6]. Combined with near-quantum-limited parametric amplification — whose theory and limits are reviewed in [4] — dispersive readout now achieves single-shot fidelities exceeding 99 percent in a few hundred nanoseconds, with QND repeatability above 99 percent.

**Analytic result: the dispersive shift.** To second order in g/Δ, the Schrieffer–Wolff transformation (§ 3.2) gives χ = g²/Δ exactly for the two-level truncation [1]; the multi-level transmon generalization χ = χ_01 − χ_12/2 reproduces measured cavity pulls across the full flux-tuning range [3], [12]. The accompanying prediction n_crit = (Δ/2g)² correctly locates the photon number at which readout-induced mixing onsets [5].

## 6 Limitations

**The rotating-wave approximation breaks down.** The Jaynes–Cummings model is not the full story at large g/ω_r. Counter-rotating terms produce Bloch–Siegert shifts of order g²/(ω_r + ω_q), squeeze the vacuum into the ground state, and qualitatively restructure the spectrum in USC and deep-strong coupling. A recent critical review asks directly whether the Jaynes–Cummings model "is still relevant" in the nonperturbative regime and answers: only as the g/ω_r → 0 limit of the quantum Rabi model [7].

**Dispersive readout is only approximately QND.** Measurement-induced mixing at n̄ approaching n_crit, Purcell decay through the readout resonator, and photon-number-dependent dephasing all degrade the ideal QND picture [5]. High-fidelity readout therefore demands Purcell filters, carefully chosen χ/κ ratios, and photon numbers well below n_crit — engineering constraints, not fundamental ones, but binding in practice.

**The two-level approximation is insufficient for transmons.** Real devices require multi-level diagonalization; the dispersive shift, the Purcell rate, and even the sign of χ depend on higher levels [3], [12]. Fluxonium and other protected qubits exhibit large dispersive shifts from many virtual transitions with no selection-rule suppression, requiring fourth-order perturbation theory [10].

**Efficiency is bounded by the amplification chain.** Even with quantum-limited first-stage amplifiers, finite η < 1 (from loss between sample and amplifier, HEMT noise, and imperfect mode matching) caps the achievable measurement rate and hence the speed–fidelity tradeoff [4].

## 7 Conclusion

The Jaynes–Cummings model began as a theoretical device for the maser and became the operating system of cavity quantum electrodynamics: its dressed-state ladder explains vacuum Rabi splitting, collapse and revival, and photon blockade across platforms spanning Rydberg atoms, trapped atoms, and superconducting circuits [8]. The far-detuned dispersive limit of the same Hamiltonian yields a measurement that is quantum nondemolition to order (g/Δ)² and forms the basis of all modern superconducting-qubit readout [1], [2], [6].

The quantitative triumphs are striking: cooperativities above 10⁴ in circuit QED, single-shot readout fidelities above 99 percent, direct spectroscopy of the anharmonic ladder up to the second doublet [9], [10]. The honest boundaries are equally clear: the rotating-wave approximation fails in ultrastrong coupling [7], dispersive readout suffers measurement-induced mixing near n_crit [5], and real artificial atoms demand multi-level theory [3], [12].

Looking forward, the frontier lies in pushing QND readout toward unit quantum efficiency with traveling-wave parametric amplifiers, in harnessing ultrastrong-coupling physics where the Jaynes–Cummings model gives way to the quantum Rabi model, and in scaling the single-cavity paradigm to multiplexed, many-qubit architectures. Sixty years on, one atom and one mode still have new physics to teach.
