# Differentially Private Deep Learning: The DP-SGD Algorithm, Rényi DP Accounting, Per-Example Gradient Clipping, and the Privacy–Utility Frontier

## Abstract

Differentially private stochastic gradient descent (DP-SGD) is the dominant method for training deep neural networks with formal privacy guarantees. This thesis develops the complete theory of DP-SGD: per-example gradient clipping that bounds sensitivity, Gaussian perturbation calibrated to the clipping norm, and tight privacy accounting via the moments accountant and its modern formalization as Rényi differential privacy (RDP). We derive the RDP of the subsampled Gaussian mechanism, its additive composition over thousands of training steps, and the optimal conversion back to standard (ε, δ)-DP. We analyze the bias–variance anatomy of per-example clipping, the distinct roles of the clipping norm C, the noise multiplier σ, the subsampling rate q, and the batch size, and we characterize the privacy–utility frontier they jointly trace. We compare the classical moments accountant against tighter modern accountants based on f-DP, privacy loss distributions, and numerical composition, and we contrast DP-SGD with the alternative PATE paradigm. We close with limitations: hyperparameter-search privacy costs, clipping bias, and the gap between provable ε and empirical attack resistance.

##1 Intro

Deep neural networks memorize. A network trained on sensitive records — medical histories, keystroke logs, face images — will encode individual training examples in its weights to a degree that membership-inference and reconstruction attacks can exploit. Standard stochastic gradient descent offers no formal barrier: a single outlier can dominate a minibatch gradient and imprint itself on the released model. Differential privacy (DP) supplies the missing contract: a randomized training algorithm is differentially private when its output distribution is nearly unchanged by the addition or removal of any single training record [7].

Differentially private stochastic gradient descent, introduced by Abadi et al. [1], adapts the workhorse of deep learning to this contract with three modifications: compute per-example gradients, clip each gradient's L2 norm to a fixed bound C, and add calibrated Gaussian noise before averaging. The innovation that made the method practical was not the noise itself — output perturbation of gradients was already understood — but the *moments accountant*, a mechanism-specific composition analysis that tracks the cumulant generating function of the privacy loss random variable across training steps and yields dramatically tighter ε bounds than generic composition theorems [1].

This thesis is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews differential privacy, sensitivity, the Gaussian mechanism, and composition. Section 3 presents the DP-SGD methodology: the algorithm, the clipping operator, noise calibration, and the accounting procedure. Section 4 develops the deep theory in five parts — the moments accountant as a CGF tracker, Rényi DP as a cleaner algebra, the bias–variance anatomy of clipping, hyperparameter tradeoffs and the privacy–utility frontier, and modern tighter accountants. Section 5 gives proofs of the core guarantees and representative empirical behavior. Section 6 discusses limitations, and Section 7 concludes.

##2 Background

### Differential privacy and sensitivity

> **Definition:** A randomized mechanism M is (ε, δ)-differentially private if, for every pair of neighboring datasets X and X′ differing in a single record and every measurable set S of outputs, Pr[M(X) ∈ S] ≤ e^ε · Pr[M(X′) ∈ S] + δ.

The parameter ε ≥ 0 bounds the worst-case multiplicative change in output likelihood induced by one record; δ ≥ 0 (taken cryptographically small, conventionally much less than 1/n for a dataset of n records) permits a small probability of catastrophic privacy failure [7]. A mechanism is *pure* DP when δ = 0.

The amount of noise a mechanism needs is governed by the *sensitivity* of the underlying deterministic function f: the maximum L2 distance between f(X) and f(X′) over neighboring datasets. The Gaussian mechanism releases f(X) + N(0, σ²I); its privacy guarantee is a clean function of the ratio of sensitivity to σ. In deep learning, raw gradients have unbounded sensitivity — one adversarial example can make a gradient arbitrarily large — so sensitivity must be *enforced* rather than assumed. That enforcement is clipping.

### Composition

Training touches the data thousands of times, and privacy loss accumulates. The basic composition theorem gives (kε, kδ)-DP for k mechanisms; the advanced composition theorem improves this to roughly (ε√(k log(1/δ′)), kδ + δ′)-DP [7]. Both are generic. The moments accountant beats them by exploiting the specific structure of the subsampled Gaussian mechanism, tracking higher moments of the privacy loss rather than only its worst case [1].

### The privacy loss random variable

For neighboring datasets X, X′ and an outcome o drawn from M(X), the *privacy loss* is L(o) = log(Pr[M(X) = o] / Pr[M(X′) = o]). Its distribution under o ∼ M(X) is the privacy loss random variable. The moments accountant tracks the cumulant generating function (CGF) K(λ) = log E[exp(λL)], because CGFs compose additively under independent composition [1, 3].

##3 Methodology

### The DP-SGD algorithm

Each training iteration of DP-SGD proceeds as follows [1]:

1. **Poisson subsampling.** Form a minibatch by including each training example independently with probability q = B/n, where B is the target batch size and n the dataset size. Subsampling *amplifies* privacy: a mechanism that is (ε, δ)-DP becomes roughly (O(qε), qδ)-DP when applied to a q-subsample [1, 4].
2. **Per-example gradients.** For each example xᵢ in the batch, compute the gradient gᵢ = ∇θ ℓ(θ; xᵢ) of the loss at the current parameters.
3. **Clipping.** Replace each gᵢ with ḡᵢ = gᵢ · min(1, C / ‖gᵢ‖₂). Every clipped gradient now has L2 norm at most C, so the batch sum has sensitivity C (under add/remove adjacency) regardless of the data.
4. **Noise addition.** Release (1/B)(Σᵢ ḡᵢ + N(0, σ²C²I)), i.e., add Gaussian noise with standard deviation σC to the summed clipped gradients, then average.
5. **Descent.** Update θ ← θ − η · (noisy averaged gradient) with learning rate η.

```python
import torch

def dpsgd_step(params, batch, loss_fn, C, sigma, lr):
    """One DP-SGD step with Poisson subsampling assumed upstream."""
    per_sample_grads = []  # list of per-parameter gradient lists
    for x, y in batch:  # per-example loop; vectorized in practice (Opacus)
        loss = loss_fn(params, x, y)
        grads = torch.autograd.grad(loss, params)
        # per-example L2 clipping
        total_norm = torch.sqrt(sum((g**2).sum() for g in grads))
        clip_coef = min(1.0, C / (total_norm + 1e-12))
        per_sample_grads.append([g * clip_coef for g in grads])
    # aggregate, add Gaussian noise calibrated to sensitivity C
    noisy_grads = []
    B = len(batch)
    for p_idx in range(len(params)):
        summed = sum(g[p_idx] for g in per_sample_grads)
        noise = torch.randn_like(summed) * (sigma * C)
        noisy_grads.append((summed + noise) / B)
    with torch.no_grad():
        for p, ng in zip(params, noisy_grads):
            p -= lr * ng
    return params
```

### The accounting procedure

After T training steps with subsampling rate q and noise multiplier σ, the privacy cost is computed by [1, 3]:

1. For each integer Rényi order α in a grid (commonly 2 through 64, sometimes extended to 512), compute the RDP of one subsampled Gaussian step, ρ(α; q, σ), using the analytic bound of Zhu and Wang [4] or the tighter accountant of Wang et al. [3].
2. Compose: the T-step mechanism is (α, T·ρ(α))-RDP by additivity.
3. Convert to (ε, δ)-DP by minimizing over α: ε = min_α (T·ρ(α) + log(1/δ)/(α − 1)) [2].

The minimization over α is the crucial step: small α gives loose conversion, large α inflates ρ(α), and the optimum balances them. Numerically this is a cheap one-dimensional scan, which is why RDP accounting displaced the original moments accountant in every major library [9].

| Symbol | Meaning | Typical value |
|--------|---------|---------------|
| C | Per-example clipping norm | 0.1 – 1.0 (tuned) |
| σ | Noise multiplier (noise std = σC) | 0.5 – 4.0 |
| q | Poisson subsampling rate (B/n) | 10⁻⁴ – 10⁻² |
| B | Expected batch size | 512 – 16384 |
| T | Training steps | 10³ – 10⁵ |
| δ | Failure probability | 10⁻⁵ – 10⁻⁶ |
| α | Rényi order grid | 2, 3, …, 64 |

##4 Deep Dive

###4.1 The moments accountant as a cumulant-generating-function tracker

The original moments accountant [1] is best understood as tracking K_M(λ), the CGF of the privacy loss, at integer λ. For the subsampled Gaussian mechanism with sampling probability q and noise multiplier σ, Abadi et al. bound the CGF by a closed form involving binomial sums over the subsampling randomness:

> **Theorem:** Let M be the Gaussian mechanism with noise multiplier σ applied to a Poisson q-subsample. Then for integer λ, the λ-th log-moment satisfies α_M(λ) ≤ q²λ(λ+1)/((1−q)σ²) + O(q³λ³/σ³), provided q is small and σ ≥ 1.

Composition is then trivial: α_{M^T}(λ) = T · α_M(λ), and the tail bound converts this to (ε, δ)-DP via δ = min_λ exp(α(λ) − λε) [1].

###4.2 Rényi DP: a cleaner algebra for composition and subsampling

Mironov's Rényi differential privacy [2] reframes the moments accountant as a privacy *definition* rather than a bookkeeping trick:

> **Definition:** A mechanism M is (α, ε)-RDP for order α > 1 if, for all neighboring X, X′, the Rényi divergence of order α satisfies D_α(M(X) ‖ M(X′)) ≤ ε.

Three properties make RDP the natural language of DP-SGD [2]:

- **Gaussian mechanism in closed form.** If f has L2 sensitivity Δ and M(X) = f(X) + N(0, σ²I), then M is (α, αΔ²/(2σ²))-RDP for every α > 1. No tail-bound slack, no δ: the RDP curve is exactly linear in α.
- **Lossless composition.** If M₁ is (α, ε₁)-RDP and M₂ is (α, ε₂)-RDP, their adaptive composition is (α, ε₁ + ε₂)-RDP. Privacy budgets add; there is nothing to optimize across steps.

Subsampling amplification also takes its sharpest known form in RDP.

###4.3 Per-example clipping: bias–variance anatomy

Clipping is the only step of DP-SGD that touches the *signal* rather than the noise, and it is the least innocent.

- **Bias.** Clipping discards the magnitude information of gradients above C. When gradients are heavy-tailed across examples — common in language modeling and early training — a small C systematically underweights informative outliers and can stall convergence. The bias vanishes only as C → ∞, exactly the regime where noise (σC) explodes.
- **Variance.** The added noise has covariance (σ²C²/B²)I per step. Once gradients saturate at C, increasing C *hurts* the signal-to-noise ratio linearly: noise grows with C while the clipped signal does not.
- **The sweet spot.** The optimal C is data- and architecture-dependent and typically small (order 0.1 to 1.0): large enough that most gradients pass through unclipped, small enough that noise stays manageable. Because C interacts with the learning rate, practitioners tune C and η jointly [1, 9].

A Haskell rendering of the clipping operator emphasizes its pure, per-example character:

```haskell
-- Per-example L2 clipping: a pure function on gradient vectors.
-- No monadic noise here; randomness is added later at aggregation.
clipGrad :: Double        -- clipping norm C
         -> [Double]      -- per-example gradient g
         -> [Double]      -- clipped gradient gbar
clipGrad c g =
  let nrm = sqrt (sum (map (^2) g))
      coef = min 1.0 (c / (nrm + 1e-12))
  in map (* coef) g

-- Sensitivity guarantee: ||clipGrad c g||_2 <= c for every g.
```

Adaptive clipping variants set C from a private quantile of gradient norms (spending a slice of the privacy budget to estimate it), which removes one tuning knob at the cost of a more complex accountant.

###4.4 Hyperparameter tradeoffs and the privacy–utility frontier

Fix a target (ε, δ).

1. **Batch size.** Larger B at fixed epochs *reduces* the number of compositions T while the per-step noise σC/B shrinks — a double win. Modern DP training uses very large batches (thousands to tens of thousands) with scaled learning rates. The cost is compute: per-example gradients are memory-hungry even with vectorized tricks [9].
2. **Noise multiplier σ.** Larger σ buys smaller ε at fixed T (ρ ∝ 1/σ²) but degrades SNR as 1/σ. The frontier is steep at small σ and flattens as σ grows: the first units of privacy are cheap, the last are dear.
3. **Training length T.** More steps improve optimization but compose linearly in RDP (T·ρ). Under a fixed ε budget, T and σ trade through ε ≈ Tq²α/σ²: doubling T requires σ to grow by √2 to hold ε constant.
4. **Subsampling rate q.** Smaller q amplifies privacy quadratically (ρ ∝ q²) but yields noisier gradient estimates per step. At fixed epochs the product q²T is what matters, which is why practitioners reason in (B, T) rather than q.
5. **Clipping norm C.** As analyzed above, C mediates signal vs. noise. Notably, the *privacy* guarantee is nearly independent of C (it enters only through the ratio sensitivity/noise = 1/σ), so C is tuned purely for utility — a rare clean separation in the DP-SGD design space.

> **Theorem (informal frontier):** For DP-SGD with Poisson subsampling at rate q, noise multiplier σ, and T steps, the RDP-optimal ε at failure probability δ satisfies ε ≈ min_α (Tq²α/σ² + log(1/δ)/(α−1)). Utility, to first order, degrades with the per-step noise-to-signal ratio σC/(√B·ḡ_typ), where ḡ_typ is the typical clipped gradient norm. The privacy–utility frontier is the lower envelope over (C, σ, B, T) of achievable (ε, accuracy) pairs.

Empirically, well-tuned DP-SGD reaches high accuracy at single-digit ε on standard benchmarks [1], with the residual gap to non-private training concentrated in the low-ε regime and in data-scarce settings where large batches are infeasible.

###4.5 Tighter accountants: f-DP, privacy loss distributions, and numerical composition

RDP is not the last word in accounting; its conversion to (ε, δ)-DP is lossy.

- **f-DP** reframes privacy as a hypothesis-testing tradeoff and gives a *lossless* composition theory via tradeoff functions; its Gaussian analysis yields strictly tighter (ε, δ) curves than RDP conversion, with a central limit theorem making thousand-step accounting analytic.
- **Privacy loss distributions (PLDs)** discretize the exact per-step privacy loss distribution and convolve them with FFTs, producing numerically tight (ε, δ) values for heterogeneous compositions.
- **Numerical composition via characteristic functions** [6] computes the optimal (ε, δ) from the characteristic function of the privacy loss, unifying and in some regimes beating PLD accountants.

These accountants change *reported* ε, not the trained model: the same DP-SGD run can be certified at a smaller ε simply by analyzing it more cleverly.

For contrast, Private Aggregation of Teacher Ensembles (PATE) [5] sidesteps per-step gradient noise: teachers trained on disjoint private partitions vote with noise on public unlabeled data, and only the student is released.

##5 Empirical Results or Proofs

### Proof sketches of the core guarantees

**RDP of the Gaussian mechanism.** Let f have L2 sensitivity Δ. The Rényi divergence of order α between N(μ, σ²I) and N(μ′, σ²I) equals α‖μ − μ′‖₂²/(2σ²) ≤ αΔ²/(2σ²), by direct computation of the Gaussian moment generating function. Hence the mechanism is (α, αΔ²/(2σ²))-RDP for all α > 1 [2]. With per-example clipping enforcing Δ = C and noise σC, the per-step, pre-subsampling guarantee is (α, α/(2σ²))-RDP — note C cancels, confirming that privacy depends on the *multiplier* σ alone.

**Subsampling amplification (RDP form).** Applying an (α, ρ)-RDP mechanism to a Poisson q-subsample yields (α, ρ_q(α))-RDP with ρ_q given by the Zhu–Wang analytic bound [4]; asymptotically ρ_q(α) = Θ(q²ρ(α)) for small q, since with probability roughly 1 − q the differing record is absent and the step costs nothing.

**Composition and conversion.** By additivity of Rényi divergence under product distributions, T adaptive steps compose to (α, Σ_t ρ_t(α))-RDP [2]. For any δ strictly between 0 and 1, Markov's inequality applied to exp((α−1)L) gives Pr[L > ε] ≤ exp((α−1)(ρ(α) − ε)); setting the right side to δ and solving yields the conversion ε = ρ(α) + log(1/δ)/(α − 1), minimized over α [2].

### Representative accounting behavior

The table below shows RDP-optimal ε for a canonical configuration (σ = 1.0, δ = 10⁻⁵, α scanned over integers from 2 to 64), illustrating how ε scales with sampling rate and steps — the quantitative shape of the frontier's privacy axis [3, 4]:

| q (subsampling rate) | T = 1,000 steps | T = 10,000 steps | T = 100,000 steps |
|----------------------|-----------------|------------------|-------------------|
| 0.01 | ε ≈ 1.2 | ε ≈ 4.1 | ε ≈ 13.5 |
| 0.002 | ε ≈ 0.35 | ε ≈ 1.1 | ε ≈ 3.6 |
| 0.0005 | ε ≈ 0.12 | ε ≈ 0.32 | ε ≈ 0.95 |

Values illustrate the scaling laws (ε growing roughly linearly in T and quadratically in q at fixed σ); exact numbers depend on the accountant and α grid. The message is robust across accountants [3, 6, 8]: privacy cost is dominated by the product Tq²/σ², so large batches and moderate σ are the primary design tools.

On the utility axis, the literature reports that carefully tuned DP-SGD — large batches, tuned C and learning rate — attains accuracy within a few percentage points of non-private baselines at single-digit ε on MNIST and CIFAR-10 class benchmarks [1], with the gap widening as ε is pushed below 1 or as datasets shrink.

##6 Limitations

1. **Hyperparameter search spends privacy.** Every configuration evaluated on private data — every candidate C, σ, or learning rate — consumes budget unless the search is privatized or done on public proxy data. Published ε values often exclude this cost, so the *end-to-end* privacy of a tuned pipeline exceeds the reported per-run ε.
2. **Clipping bias is irreducible by accounting.** No tighter accountant fixes the signal distortion of clipping. On heavy-tailed gradient distributions, the small C needed for reasonable noise levels can systematically bias optimization; the frontier's utility axis is bounded by this bias.
3. **δ semantics and group privacy.** The δ term permits catastrophic failure with small probability, and group privacy degrades multiplicatively: protecting a group of k correlated records costs roughly kε. Datasets with multiple rows per user need explicit group-level accounting that naive per-record DP-SGD does not provide.
4. **Subsampling model mismatch.** Tight analyses assume Poisson subsampling while implementations usually shuffle fixed-size minibatches. The leading-order equivalence [8] justifies the approximation, but replace-one vs. add/remove adjacency choices change the constants — a deployed system should account for the mechanism it actually runs.
5. **Provable ε vs. empirical attack resistance.** A large ε (say, above 10) is a weak formal guarantee, yet such models often resist known membership-inference attacks in practice; a small ε certifies worst-case protection but says nothing about which attacks are feasible. ε values should be communicated with that caveat.
6. **Compute cost.** Per-example gradients inflate memory and compute several-fold over standard training even with Opacus-style vectorization [9], and the large batches DP-SGD favors compound the cost. Privacy here is paid for twice: in utility and in FLOPs.

##7 Conclusion

DP-SGD reduces differentially private deep learning to three ideas that fit in one training loop: clip per-example gradients to bound sensitivity, add Gaussian noise scaled to the clipping norm, and account tightly with Rényi DP. The moments accountant [1] made the privacy cost measurable; RDP [2] made it compositional; analytic subsampling bounds [3, 4] and numerical accountants [6] made it tight. The remaining difficulty is the engineering of the frontier: choosing C, σ, batch size, and training length so the noise a formal guarantee demands does not drown the signal the model needs — while honestly budgeting the privacy cost of the search itself. As tighter accountants keep converting the same training runs into smaller reported ε values, the practical gap between private and non-private deep learning keeps narrowing — but the underlying tension, that learning from data and hiding individual records are opposing objectives, is information-theoretic and here to stay.
