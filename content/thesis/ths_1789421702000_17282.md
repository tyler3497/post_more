# The Keller–Segel Model of Chemotaxis: Finite-Time Blow-Up, Critical Mass, and Self-Similar Collapse

## Abstract

The Keller–Segel system is the canonical partial differential equation model of chemotaxis, describing how cell populations drift up gradients of a chemoattractant they themselves secrete [1]. In two space dimensions its parabolic–elliptic form exhibits a sharp dichotomy governed by a single invariant, the total mass $M$: below the critical mass $8\pi$ solutions exist globally in time, while above it they aggregate into a Dirac singularity in finite time [2]. This survey develops the analysis behind the dichotomy — the free-energy dissipation structure, the logarithmic Hardy–Littlewood–Sobolev inequality, and the virial identity — and proceeds to the fine structure of the singularity: the self-similar collapse profile $U_0(y) = 8/(1+|y|^2)^2$, the logarithmic corrections to the compression scale $\lambda(t)$, and the infinite-time blow-up that occurs at exactly the critical mass [3][4][5]. Higher dimensions, where blow-up occurs for arbitrarily small mass, and nonlinear-diffusion variants, which restore a critical mass in $d \geq 3$, are treated as extensions [3][4]. The result is a unified picture of how diffusion and nonlinear advection compete to produce one of the most studied singularity-formation mechanisms in mathematical biology.

## 1 Introduction

Chemotaxis — directed motion of cells or organisms in response to a chemical gradient — underlies phenomena ranging from the aggregation of the slime mold *Dictyostelium discoideum* into fruiting bodies to bacterial colony patterning, immune-cell homing, and tumor-induced angiogenesis. In 1970, Evelyn Keller and Lee Segel proposed a continuum model in which the cell density $u(x,t)$ and the chemoattractant concentration $v(x,t)$ satisfy a coupled drift–diffusion system, with aggregation interpreted as an instability of the uniform state driven by the chemotactic feedback [1]. The *minimal* or *parabolic–elliptic* Keller–Segel system in $\mathbb{R}^2$ reads

$$
u_t = \Delta u - \nabla \cdot (u \nabla v), \qquad -\Delta v = u,
$$

or equivalently, with the Newtonian potential $E_2(x) = -(2\pi)^{-1}\ln|x|$,

$$
u_t = \nabla \cdot \big(\nabla u - u \, \nabla (E_2 \ast u)\big), \qquad u(\cdot, 0) = u_0 \geq 0.
$$

This single equation encodes a genuinely nonlinear, nonlocal competition whose outcome depends on exactly one number: the total mass $M = \int_{\mathbb{R}^2} u_0\, dx$, conserved by the divergence structure of the flow [3][4]. The central discovery, developed over four decades, is that the value $8\pi$ acts as a *sharp threshold*:

- **Subcritical mass** $M < 8\pi$: diffusion wins; classical solutions exist for all time and diffuse toward self-similar profiles [2].
- **Critical mass** $M = 8\pi$: solutions exist globally, but a distinguished class concentrates an $8\pi$ Dirac mass in *infinite* time, with a collapsing core shrinking only logarithmically slowly [5].
- **Supercritical mass** $M > 8\pi$: attraction wins; the density concentrates at isolated points and the $L^\infty$ norm diverges in *finite* time [2][4].

## 2 Background: The Keller–Segel Hierarchy and Its Scales

### 2.1 From biology to equations

The full Keller–Segel model is parabolic–parabolic:

$$
u_t = \Delta u - \chi \nabla \cdot (u \nabla v), \qquad \tau v_t = \Delta v - v + u,
$$

where $\chi > 0$ is the chemotactic sensitivity and $\tau \geq 0$ measures the ratio of signal to cell diffusion rates [1]. When the signal diffuses far faster than the cells ($\tau \to 0$), the second equation becomes elliptic, $-\Delta v = u$ (up to normalization), yielding the parabolic–elliptic system that is the focus of this work. The 1953 Patlak model of random walks with persistence is an ancestor of the same structure, so the system is often called the *Patlak–Keller–Segel* (PKS) model [3].

Three features shape everything that follows:

1. **Mass conservation.** The flux form $\nabla \cdot (\nabla u - u\nabla v)$ implies $dM/dt = 0$ for decaying solutions; the total population is an exact invariant [3].
2. **Scaling invariance.** The map $u_\lambda(x,t) = \lambda^{-2} u(x/\lambda, t/\lambda^2)$ sends solutions to solutions, and $\|u_\lambda\|_{L^1(\mathbb{R}^d)} = \lambda^{d-2}\|u\|_{L^1(\mathbb{R}^d)}$. Hence $d = 2$ is the *mass-critical* dimension, $d = 1$ is mass-subcritical, and $d \geq 3$ is mass-supercritical [4].
3. **Gradient-flow structure.** The system is steepest descent of a free energy in the Wasserstein-2 geometry of probability measures, a fact that makes variational and optimal-transport tools available [2].

### 2.2 What "blow-up" means

A classical solution on a maximal time interval, with existence time $T$ finite or infinite, *blows up in finite time* if

$$
\limsup_{t \to T} \|u(t)\|_{L^\infty(\mathbb{R}^d)} = +\infty, \qquad T < \infty.
$$

- **Type I blow-up:** $\limsup_{t \to T} (T-t)\, \|u(t)\|_{L^\infty} < \infty$ — the natural parabolic rate.
- **Type II blow-up:** the rate is faster; the singularity forms through a slower, more delicate concentration mechanism.

| Dimension | Scaling class | Role of mass | Typical outcome |
|---|---|---|---|
| $d = 1$ | Mass-subcritical | Irrelevant | Global existence for all data [4] |
| $d = 2$ | Mass-critical | Sharp threshold $8\pi$ | Dichotomy: global existence vs. finite-time blow-up [2] |
| $d \geq 3$ | Mass-supercritical | No threshold | Blow-up possible for arbitrarily small mass [3] |

## 3 Methodology: Energy Methods, the Virial Identity, and Matched Asymptotics

### 3.1 The free-energy functional

The decisive methodological tool is the free energy, introduced into the chemotaxis context by Nagai, Senba, and Yoshida and developed systematically by Blanchet, Dolbeault, and Perthame [2]:

$$
\mathcal{F}[u] = \int_{\mathbb{R}^2} u \ln u\, dx - \frac{1}{2} \int_{\mathbb{R}^2} u v\, dx,
\qquad v = -\frac{1}{2\pi} \ln|x| \ast u.
$$

Along smooth solutions one computes the dissipation identity

$$
\frac{d}{dt} \mathcal{F}[u(t,\cdot)] = -\int_{\mathbb{R}^2} u \, \big|\nabla (\ln u) - \nabla v\big|^2 dx \leq 0,
$$

so the free energy is a Lyapunov functional [2]. The entire dichotomy is the question of which term dominates — and the answer is encoded in the logarithmic Hardy–Littlewood–Sobolev (log-HLS) inequality of Carlen and Loss, whose *optimal constant* is exactly $8\pi$ [2]. Below $8\pi$ the free energy is bounded from below, and this coercivity, combined with hypercontractivity estimates, yields global existence of weak "free energy" solutions; above $8\pi$ the functional is unbounded below, opening the door to collapse [2].

> Theorem: (Blanchet–Dolbeault–Perthame [2]) Let $u_0 \geq 0$ with $u_0 \in L^1(\mathbb{R}^2, (1+|x|^2)dx)$ and $u_0 \ln u_0 \in L^1(\mathbb{R}^2)$. If $M < 8\pi$, the Keller–Segel system admits a global-in-time free-energy solution; if $M > 8\pi$, any such solution blows up in finite time.

### 3.2 The virial identity

The second pillar is the *virial identity* for the second moment $I(t) = \int_{\mathbb{R}^2} |x|^2 u(x,t)\, dx$:

$$
\frac{d}{dt} I(t) = 4M\left(1 - \frac{M}{8\pi}\right).
$$

This exact relation is obtained by testing the equation against $|x|^2$ and using the explicit kernel of $-\Delta$ in the plane [2][5]. Its consequence is immediate and devastating: if $M > 8\pi$ and the initial second moment is finite, then $I(t)$ becomes negative in finite time, contradicting $u \geq 0$. Hence no global classical solution can exist — the *first* proof of finite-time blow-up for this model, due to Jäger and Luckhaus in 1992, exploited exactly this mechanism, and Horstmann and Wang later removed symmetry assumptions [6].

### 3.3 Matched asymptotic expansions

Knowing *that* blow-up occurs is only half the story; the other half is *how*. The refined analysis proceeds by matched asymptotics: one postulates a collapsing ansatz

$$
u(x,t) \approx \frac{1}{\lambda(t)^2} U\!\left(\frac{x}{\lambda(t)}\right), \qquad \lambda(t) \to 0 \;\; \text{as } t \to T,
$$

inserts it into the equation, and solves order by order in an inner region (the collapsing core, $r \ll 1$) and an outer region (the residual mass, $r \gg \lambda$), matching the expansions in an overlap zone [4]. The steady states $U$ of the rescaled equation serve as the leading-order profile, and the slow dynamics of $\lambda(t)$ is governed by a modulation equation whose solution produces the famous logarithmic corrections [4][5]. ### 3.4 Numerical methodology

Computational verification of the blow-up law uses radial finite-volume discretizations with adaptive mesh refinement near the origin. A minimal schematic implementation illustrates the structure:

```python
import numpy as np

def keller_segel_radial(u0, R, Nr, dt, T, chi=1.0):
    """Schematic radial solver: u_t = div(grad u - chi*u*grad v), -Laplacian v = u."""
    r = np.linspace(0, R, Nr)
    dr = r[1] - r[0]
    u = u0(r).copy()
    t, peak = 0.0, []
    for _ in range(int(T / dt)):
        v = solve_poisson_radial(u, r, dr)      # quasi-static chemical field
        flux = chi * u * np.gradient(v, dr)     # chemotactic flux
        lap = np.gradient(np.gradient(u, dr), dr)
        u = np.maximum(u + dt * (lap + np.gradient(flux, dr)), 0.0)
        t += dt
        peak.append(u.max())
        if u.max() > 1e6:                        # singularity detected
            break
    return r, u, np.array(peak)
```

## 4 Deep Dive: The Anatomy of Chemotactic Collapse

### 4.1 The critical mass $8\pi$ and why it is optimal

The number $8\pi$ appears as the optimal constant in the log-HLS inequality

$$
\int_{\mathbb{R}^2} u \ln u\, dx + \frac{2}{M} \iint_{\mathbb{R}^2 \times \mathbb{R}^2} u(x) \ln|x-y|\, u(y)\, dx dy \geq -C(M),
$$

which holds precisely for $M \leq 8\pi$ [2]. Equivalently, $8\pi$ is the mass of the unique (up to scaling and translation) steady state

$$
U_0(y) = \frac{8}{(1+|y|^2)^2}, \qquad -\Delta U_0 = \text{related Poisson coupling},
$$

the *Barenblatt-type* profile that saturates the inequality. Dolbeault and Perthame first identified $8\pi$ as the optimal threshold in $\mathbb{R}^2$ via this inequality [6], and Blanchet, Dolbeault, and Perthame completed the picture by proving global existence of weak solutions for every $M < 8\pi$ and finite-time blow-up for every $M > 8\pi$ in the whole plane [2]. The optimality is two-sided: below $8\pi$ diffusion always wins; above $8\pi$ there are always data that collapse.

In Haskell, the variational structure can be expressed as a pure functional program over discretized states:

```haskell
-- | Chemotaxis state: cell density, chemical field, and conserved total mass.
data ChemoState = ChemoState
  { density   :: [Double]  -- ^ cell density u on a grid
  , chemical  :: [Double]  -- ^ attractant concentration v
  , totalMass :: Double    -- ^ invariant: d/dt ∫u = 0
  }

-- | Free energy F[u] = ∫ u log u dx − 1/2 ∫ u v dx, non-increasing along the flow.
freeEnergy :: ChemoState -> Double
freeEnergy s = entropyTerm (density s) - 0.5 * interactionTerm (density s) (chemical s)
  where
    entropyTerm u     = sum [x * log x | x <- u, x > 0]
    interactionTerm u v = sum (zipWith (*) u v)

-- | Sharp dichotomy: the log-HLS constant is 8π.
isSubcritical :: ChemoState -> Bool
isSubcritical s = totalMass s < 8 * pi
```

### 4.2 Finite-time blow-up above the critical mass

For $M > 8\pi$, the virial identity forces $T_{\max} < \infty$ whenever the initial second moment is finite [2]. Herrero and Velázquez went much further: they constructed radial solutions that concentrate *all* of a threshold $8\pi$ portion of the mass into a Dirac delta at the origin in finite time, with an explicit asymptotic description of the developing singularity [4]. The solution takes the collapsing form

$$
u(x,t) \sim \frac{1}{\lambda(t)^2} U\!\left(\frac{x}{\lambda(t)}\right),
$$

where $U$ is the steady state $U_0$ and the compression scale obeys the refined law

$$
\lambda(t) \sim \sqrt{T-t}\; \exp\!\left(-\sqrt{\frac{|\log(T-t)|}{2}}\right),
$$

so the collapse is *slower* than the naive parabolic scaling $\sqrt{T-t}$ by a logarithmic factor [4]. The extra logarithm reflects a subtle resonance: the linearized operator around $U_0$ has an approximate kernel (generated by scaling), and the dynamics of $\lambda$ is driven by a nonlinear resonance at third order — the same mechanism that appears in critical wave-map and Schrödinger blow-up problems [4].

> Theorem: (Herrero–Velázquez collapse law) There exist radial solutions of the 2D parabolic–elliptic Keller–Segel system with mass above $8\pi$ that blow up at the origin in finite time $T$, with $u(x,t) \approx \lambda(t)^{-2} U_0(x/\lambda(t))$ and $\lambda(t)$ obeying the log-corrected law above. All the mass $8\pi$ concentrates at $x = 0$ [4].

### 4.3 Self-similar collapse and the universal profile

The profile $U_0(y) = 8/(1+|y|^2)^2$ is universal: it is the unique radial steady state of the rescaled equation with mass $8\pi$, and every refined blow-up construction in the plane approaches it [4][5]. In rescaled time $\tau = -\log(T-t)$ and variable $y = x/\lambda(t)$, the equation becomes an autonomous (up to the slow $\lambda$ dynamics) flow for which $U_0$ is a stationary solution with a one-dimensional unstable direction — the scaling mode. Controlling this mode by a careful choice of $\lambda(t)$ (a *modulation* argument) is the heart of the rigorous construction [4][5].

```rust
/// Parameters for a radial Keller–Segel simulation.
pub struct KsParams {
    pub mass: f64,       // total initial mass M (conserved)
    pub chi: f64,        // chemotactic sensitivity
    pub blowup_tol: f64, // L-infinity threshold for singularity detection
}

impl KsParams {
    /// 8π is the sharp critical mass in two dimensions [2].
    pub fn regime(&self) -> &'static str {
        let m_c = 8.0 * std::f64::consts::PI;
        if self.mass < m_c {
            "subcritical: global existence, diffusive decay"
        } else if (self.mass - m_c).abs() < 1e-12 {
            "critical: global existence, infinite-time concentration"
        } else {
            "supercritical: finite-time blow-up, 8π mass quantization"
        }
    }
}
```

### 4.4 The critical mass: infinite-time blow-up

The most delicate case is $M = 8\pi$ exactly. Global existence holds [2], yet the solution need not remain bounded: Blanchet, Carrillo, and Masmoudi showed that infinite-time aggregation occurs, and Dávila, del Pino, Dolbeault, Musso, and Wei constructed it with full asymptotics and proved its stability [5]. Near the critical mass, for data close to a distinguished radial profile $u_0^*$ of mass $8\pi$, the solution is global and satisfies

$$
u(x,t) \approx \frac{1}{\lambda(t)^2} U_0\!\left(\frac{x - \xi(t)}{\lambda(t)}\right),
\qquad \lambda(t) \approx \frac{c}{\sqrt{\log t}}, \quad \xi(t) \to q,
$$

so the core shrinks to a point only as $t \to \infty$, at the excruciatingly slow rate $1/\sqrt{\log t}$ [5]. ```tla+

### 4.5 Higher dimensions and the return of critical mass

In $d \geq 3$ the linear-diffusion model has no mass threshold: scaling makes the $L^1$ norm supercritical, and blow-up occurs for arbitrarily small mass [3]. But the singularity structure becomes far richer. Backward self-similar solutions take the form

$$
u(x,t) = \frac{1}{T-t} U(y), \qquad y = \frac{x}{\sqrt{T-t}},
$$

with the profile satisfying $\Delta U - \tfrac{1}{2} y \cdot \nabla U - U - \nabla \cdot (U \nabla \Phi_U) = 0$ [4]. Nguyen, Wang, and Zhang constructed *infinitely many* such profiles for $3 \leq d \leq 9$ by matched asymptotics and a Banach fixed-point argument: each $U_n$ approximates a rescaled radial steady state near the origin and behaves like $2(d-2)/|y|^2$ at infinity, with $u(x,t) \sim |x|^{-2}$ as $t \to T$ [4]. Even more strikingly, a critical mass can be *restored* in higher dimensions by strengthening diffusion at high densities. For the porous-medium variant

$$
\rho_t = \nabla \cdot \big(\nabla (\rho^m) - \rho \nabla c\big), \qquad c = E_d \ast \rho,
$$

there is exactly one critical exponent, $m_d = 2(d-1)/d$: for $m > m_d$ diffusion always wins, for $m < m_d$ blow-up occurs regardless of mass, and for $m = m_d$ and $d \geq 3$ there is a unique threshold mass $M_c > 0$ with the same qualitative dichotomy as the 2D linear case [3]. Moreover, unlike the planar case, finite-mass self-similar blowing-up solutions *do* exist: for masses $M \in (M_c, M_2]$ there are radial, compactly supported, non-increasing profiles $\Phi_M$ with $\rho(t,x) = s(t)^{-d} \Phi_M(x/s(t))$, $s(t) = (d(T-t))^{1/d}$, and $\|\rho(t)\|_\infty \to \infty$ as $t \to T$ [3]. > Theorem: (Blanchet–Laurençot [3]) For $d \geq 3$ and $m = m_d = 2(d-1)/d$, there exists $M_c > 0$ such that solutions with mass $\leq M_c$ exist globally while for each $M \in (M_c, M_2]$ there is a finite-mass self-similar solution blowing up in finite time.

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

We now collect the rigorous core as a sequence of statements, each with its proof idea.

> Theorem: (Mass conservation) For classical solutions decaying sufficiently fast, $M(t) = \int u(x,t)\, dx$ is constant. *Proof.* Integrate the divergence-form equation and apply the divergence theorem; boundary terms vanish [3].

> Theorem: (Virial identity) In $\mathbb{R}^2$, $dI/dt = 4M(1 - M/8\pi)$ with $I(t) = \int |x|^2 u\, dx$. *Proof.* Test against $|x|^2$; the chemotactic term produces $-(M^2/2\pi)$ via the explicit logarithmic kernel, and the diffusion term produces $4M$ [2][5].

> Theorem: (Finite-time blow-up above $8\pi$) If $M > 8\pi$ and $I(0) < \infty$, then $T_{\max} < I(0)/[4M(M/8\pi - 1)] < \infty$. *Proof.* The virial identity makes $I(t)$ reach zero in finite time, contradicting nonnegativity of $u$ [2].

> Theorem: (Universal lower bound on the blow-up rate) If blow-up occurs at $T < \infty$, then $\|u(t)\|_{L^\infty} \geq (T-t)^{-1}$. *Proof.* Comparison with the ODE $\dot{y} = y^2$ satisfied by spatial maxima via the maximum principle [4].

> Theorem: (Sharpness of $8\pi$) The constant $8\pi$ cannot be improved: for every $M < 8\pi$ there are global solutions, and for every $M > 8\pi$ there are data blowing up in finite time [2].

The logical chain of a blow-up event can be summarized as an ordered mechanism:

1. **Concentration.** The chemotactic flux $-u\nabla v$ transports mass up the self-generated gradient, sharpening any initial inhomogeneity.
2. **Positive feedback.** A sharper density produces, via $-\Delta v = u$, a steeper potential, which strengthens the flux — the instability Keller and Segel identified [1].
3. **Threshold crossing.** Once the local mass budget exceeds $8\pi$, the log-HLS deficit makes the free energy unbounded below and diffusion can no longer arrest the collapse [2].
4. **Core formation.** The inner region locks onto the universal profile $U_0$ while the outer region sheds the excess mass [4].
5. **Logarithmic collapse.** The compression scale $\lambda(t)$ follows the modulated law, reaching zero in finite time (supercritical) or infinite time (critical) [4][5].
6. **Quantization.** An $8\pi$ Dirac mass remains at each blow-up point; the rest of the solution continues smoothly [5].

## 6 Limitations

The theory, for all its depth, has clear boundaries:

- **Radial symmetry assumptions.** The sharpest results — the Herrero–Velázquez profile, the infinite-time construction, the self-similar families — are proved for radial data. Non-radial blow-up without symmetry was pioneered by Horstmann and Wang, but a complete non-radial classification remains open [6].
- **The parabolic–parabolic gap.** Most of the sharp theory concerns the parabolic–elliptic reduction. The fully parabolic system ($\tau > 0$) has a critical mass theory in 2D but the refined blow-up asymptotics are far less complete [6].
- **Bounded domains.** On domains with boundary the thresholds shift ($4\pi$ vs. $8\pi$) and boundary blow-up introduces new phenomena; the whole-space theory does not transfer verbatim [6].
- **Continuation past blow-up.** Measure-valued and "generalized" solutions extend the flow beyond $T$ remain active research areas [6].
- **Biological fidelity.** Real cells have finite size, volume-filling constraints, and signal degradation; the minimal model's Dirac singularities are mathematical idealizations. Volume-filling variants prevent blow-up entirely [6].
- **Stability of exotic profiles.** Infinitely many self-similar profiles exist in $3 \leq d \leq 9$, but only a few are known to be stable; the rest may be non-generic [4].

---

## 7 Conclusion

The Keller–Segel model compresses one of the deepest stories in nonlinear PDEs into a single equation: a sharp, optimal threshold $8\pi$ separating eternal diffusion from finite-time collapse; a free-energy structure governed by the logarithmic Hardy–Littlewood–Sobolev inequality; a virial identity that kills global existence above the threshold in one line; and a refined asymptotic anatomy of the singularity — universal profile $U_0$, logarithmically corrected compression scale, mass quantization, and infinite-time concentration at exactly the critical mass [1][2][3][4][5]. The extensions to higher dimensions and nonlinear diffusion show the same motifs recurring: scaling dictates the criticality class, variational inequalities set the threshold, and matched asymptotics reveal the profile [3][4].

What began in 1970 as a model of slime-mold aggregation has become a paradigm for singularity formation — a reminder that the competition between spreading and attraction, written in the simplest possible nonlinearity, already contains the full subtlety of critical phenomena [1][6].
