# Rowhammer in the DDR5 Era: Target Row Refresh, Refresh Management, Half-Double Patterns, and Probabilistic Defenses Against Disturbance Errors

## Abstract

*Rowhammer, the DRAM read-disturbance phenomenon first rigorously characterized in 2014, remains the most consequential hardware fault-injection primitive available to unprivileged software. This thesis presents a unified analytical treatment of Rowhammer in the DDR5 era. We dissect in-DRAM Target Row Refresh (TRR) and explain why its sampler-based aggressor tracking is structurally vulnerable to many-sided hammering, as demonstrated by the TRRespass black-box fuzzer [1][2]. We formalize the Half-Double effect, in which a mitigation's own refresh operations become the aggressors that disturb distance-two victims [3]. We analyze the DDR5 Refresh Management (RFM) interface, Per-Row Activation Counting (PRAC), and Alert Back-Off (ABO) as JEDEC's standardized countermeasures, quantifying their performance cost and residual timing-channel exposure [3][5][6][8]. Finally, we prove probabilistic security bounds for PARA-style defenses and evaluate modern tracker management policies [4][5]. Our synthesis argues that no single deterministic in-DRAM mitigation survives the steadily falling RowHammer threshold, and that principled probabilistic layering is the only viable path forward.*

---

## 1. Introduction

In 2014, Kim et al. showed that repeatedly *activating* a DRAM row flips bits in physically adjacent rows without accessing them — **RowHammer** — with a minimum activation count to first flip, the *RowHammer threshold* $T_{RH}$, of roughly **139K** on DDR3 [1]. A decade-long arms race followed: each DRAM generation lowered $T_{RH}$, and each mitigation fell to a more sophisticated hammering pattern.

The industry's flagship response was **Target Row Refresh (TRR)**, publicized as the definitive solution: track frequently activated *aggressor* rows and proactively refresh their neighbors (*victims*). In 2020, TRRespass demystified TRR and *debunked its guarantees*: TRR is an umbrella term for a family of counter- and sampler-based trackers, and a black-box fuzzer automatically discovered *many-sided* hammering patterns bypassing in-DRAM TRR on 13 of 42 DDR4 modules across all three major vendors [1][2].

The story did not end with TRR bypasses. Google's Project Zero demonstrated **Half-Double**, in which aggressor rows *two* rows from the victim induce flips because the mitigation's own refresh of the intermediate row becomes the hammering agent [3]. JEDEC's DDR5 response was the **Refresh Management (RFM)** interface — controller-triggered, DRAM-executed mitigation time — later extended with **Per-Row Activation Counting (PRAC)** and **Alert Back-Off (ABO)** [5][6]. Yet even these interfaces carry side effects: RFM enables covert channels and denial-of-service, and PRAC leaks timing channels [3][8].

Our contributions:

1. A precise structural model of in-DRAM TRR and the sampler bottleneck that makes many-sided hammering inevitable.
2. A formal account of the Half-Double effect as *mitigation-induced disturbance*.
3. A quantitative analysis of the DDR5 RFM/PRAC/ABO stack: its security guarantees, its performance cost, and its residual side channels.
4. Probabilistic security bounds for PARA-style defenses and modern probabilistic trackers (PROTEAS, PrIDE, MINT) [4][5], and a synthesis arguing deterministic in-DRAM tracking cannot scale below $T_{RH} \approx 100$.

## 2. Background and Related Work

A DRAM bank is an array of *rows* (typically 8 KiB) sharing sense amplifiers. An **ACT** opens a row into the row buffer; **PRE** closes it. Cells leak, so every row is refreshed once per $t_{REFW}$ (64 ms DDR3/DDR4, 32 ms DDR5), with refreshes every $t_{REFI}$ (7.8 $\mu$s DDR4, 3.9 $\mu$s DDR5). Enough ACT/PRE cycles inside one $t_{REFW}$ flip neighboring cells before refresh restores them [4].

The critical parameter is the **RowHammer threshold** $T_{RH}$: the minimum number of aggressor activations required to induce the first bit flip. Table 1 summarizes its alarming trajectory.

| DRAM generation | $T_{RH}$ range (activations) | Source |
|---|---|---|
| DDR3 | 22.4K – 139K | [4], citing [1] |
| DDR4 | 10K – 17.5K | [4] |
| LPDDR4(X) | 4.8K – 9K | [4] |
| HBM2 | ~20K | [6] |
| Future (projected) | < 500, trending toward 100 | [4][5] |

*Table 1: The RowHammer threshold has fallen by more than two orders of magnitude in a decade, collapsing the defender's margin.*

TRRespass introduced **many-sided** hammering: activations spread across $n > 2$ aggressor rows to overflow tracking structures [1][2].

**Target Row Refresh.** TRR began in memory controllers (Intel's *pseudo-TRR*, ~2014) and migrated *into* DRAM chips as **in-DRAM TRR** on DDR4, LPDDR5, and HBM2 [1][5]. The in-DRAM variants track candidate aggressors in small counter or sampler tables and issue extra *targeted* refreshes during REF commands. Vendors kept every implementation proprietary — *security by obscurity* — forcing researchers to reverse-engineer behavior via timing side channels and FPGA controllers such as SoftMC [1].

TRRespass reverse-engineered these implementations and reported three structural observations that recur across vendors [1]:

- **Observation 1.** The mitigation samples aggressor rows into a *limited* table holding only a handful of entries.
- **Observation 2.** The mitigation can sample *more than one* aggressor per refresh interval, but its capacity is strictly bounded.
- **Observation 3.** A single refresh operation can remediate only a *single* victim row, so hammering more than a small number of rows (empirically, more than four) overwhelms the mechanism.

The conclusion follows directly: an attacker who spreads activations across sufficiently many aggressor rows *thrashes* the sampler, so the true aggressors evade tracking and their victims are never refreshed [1]. The follow-up **Blacksmith** work refined this picture, showing that TRR samples aggressor rows only at *specific time instances* within each $t_{REFI}$, enabling frequency-domain patterns that evade even the sampling step [6].

**Half-Double.** In 2021, Google's Project Zero disclosed **Half-Double**, a qualitatively new effect: hammering a *far* aggressor row at distance two from the victim induces flips even though the attacker never hammers any adjacent row. The mechanism is perverse — the in-DRAM mitigation observes the far aggressor, issues a *mitigative refresh* to the intermediate (distance-one) row, and that refresh itself acts as the hammering agent against the victim [3]. In short, **the cure hammers**: every corrective activation is itself a disturbance source, and defenses must account for the *blast radius* of their own actions.

**Probabilistic and software defenses.** The original 2014 study already proposed **PARA** (*Probabilistic Adjacent Row Activation*): on every row activation, with small probability $p$, refresh the two adjacent rows [1]. PARA's beauty is its statelessness — no tracker to thrash — at the cost of extra refresh energy; later variants include PRA, MRLOC, and ProHIT [3]. Deterministic counters (CRA, TWiCe, Graphene, Hydra, Panopticon) hit a storage wall tracking every row [3]. **BlockHammer** throttles activation rates below $T_{RH}$, **RRS/AQUA/SRS/SHADOW** *migrate* hot aggressors, and **SoftTRR** showed a software-only TRR can still protect page tables where hardware TRR is bypassable [3][7].

**Why ECC is not the answer.** Commodity ECC DIMMs correct only single-bit errors per word, while RowHammer routinely flips multiple bits per row — TRRespass observed *tens of thousands* of flips per module [1]. ECC was designed for random soft errors, not adversarial disturbance; attackers can target multi-bit patterns exceeding the code's capacity while staying invisible to the OS. ECC raises the bar; it does not close the vulnerability.

## 3. Methodology

We combine an analytical security model with provable bounds, instantiated with parameters from published measurement studies [1][4][5][6].

**Notation and adversary model.** $T_{RH}$: RowHammer threshold. $BR \in \{1,2\}$: *blast radius* of victim rows refreshed per tracked aggressor [5]. $t_{REFI}$, $t_{REFW}$: refresh interval and window. $A_{max}$: max attacker activations per $t_{REFW}$ (~550K for DDR5 32 Gb [8]). $p$: PARA probability; $k$: tracker entries; $n$: aggressor rows in a many-sided pattern.

Our threat model follows recent defense papers: an attacker with **native code execution** issuing requests to arbitrary physical addresses, who knows the defense algorithm (Kerckhoffs's principle) but has no physical access and cannot read in-DRAM secrets such as RNG seeds [4]. The attacker wins the *disturbance game* if any victim row accumulates $T_{RH}$ unmitigated aggressor-equivalent activations within one $t_{REFW}$.

**Analytical framework.** We model each defense as a stochastic process over activation counts: sampler survival for TRR, closed-form flip bounds for PARA (Section 5), and counter-plus-service-rate modeling with the RFM bandwidth tax for DDR5 designs.

**Simulation harness.** We implement a cycle-level Python simulator of one DDR5 bank (128K rows, $t_{REFI} = 3.9\,\mu$s, per-bank counters, $k$-entry sampler, configurable $T_{RH}$) replaying TRRespass-style $n$-sided and Half-Double patterns, recording maximum *unmitigated disturbance* per victim row. The core PARA loop is:

```python
import random

def para_bank_step(row, p, t_rh, disturb):
    """One ACT to `row` under PARA with probability p."""
    disturb[row] += 1
    # Probabilistic adjacent refresh: stateless, unthrashable
    if random.random() < p:
        for v in (row - 1, row + 1):
            if 0 <= v < len(disturb):
                disturb[v] = 0  # mitigative refresh resets accumulation
    # Adversary wins if any victim reached the threshold
    return any(d >= t_rh for d in disturb)
```

The simulator sweeps $p$, $k$, $n$, $T_{RH}$ to validate the Section 5 bounds and reproduce the TRRespass collapse-versus-graceful-degradation result.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 In-DRAM TRR Anatomy: The Sampler Bottleneck

In-DRAM TRR fits its state into kilobytes of die area: a tiny table — often under 32 entries per bank — of candidate aggressors with saturating counters [4][5]. Each refresh selects the hottest entries and refreshes their neighbors. The design has an intrinsic *capacity* limit: it remembers only a bounded number of hot rows per $t_{REFI}$.

> **Theorem 1 (Sampler thrash).** *Consider a TRR sampler with $k$ entries that admits at most $s$ new aggressor samples per refresh interval. An adversary hammering $n > s$ distinct aggressor rows uniformly can ensure that any fixed aggressor row is absent from the sampler at the refresh boundary with probability at least $1 - s/n$ per interval, independent of the replacement policy, provided $n$ exceeds the table's effective tracking capacity.*
>
> *Proof sketch.* In each interval the sampler can retain information about at most $s$ of the $n$ hot rows. By symmetry over the adversary's uniform schedule, each aggressor is retained with probability at most $s/n$. Refresh decisions are made from retained state, so the victim of an unretained aggressor receives no targeted refresh. ∎

The practical consequence is what TRRespass measured: with ~45K activations per aggressor row, 19-sided patterns induced flips where double-sided hammering was fully suppressed [1]. ### 4.2 Many-Sided Hammering and the TRRespass Breakthrough

TRRespass reframed RowHammer as a **fuzzing problem**: treat DRAM plus opaque mitigation as a black box, mutate the pattern (aggressor count, phase alignment, address mapping), and observe flips [1][2]. The fuzzer rediscovered the sampler bottleneck automatically, converging on many-aggressor patterns no human had tried — over 16K flips on average across one vendor's vulnerable modules [1].

A canonical many-sided hammer loop (simplified) looks like this in C:

```c
/* n-sided hammer: cycle through n aggressor rows to thrash the sampler */
void many_sided_hammer(volatile char **aggr, int n, long iters) {
    for (long i = 0; i < iters; i++) {
        for (int r = 0; r < n; r++) {
            /* ACT + PRE on each access; clflush evicts from caches */
            *(aggr[r]);
            __builtin_ia32_clflush(aggr[r]);
        }
        __builtin_ia32_mfence();
    }
}
```

With $n$ above the sampler's tracking capacity (empirically $n > 4$ suffices on many modules [1]), victims accumulate disturbance while the mitigation refreshes the wrong rows.

### 4.3 Half-Double: When the Mitigation Hammers

With victim $V$, *near* row $N = V \pm 1$, and *far* row $F = V \pm 2$, the attacker hammers only $F$. In-DRAM TRR observes $F$ as an aggressor and issues mitigative refreshes to $F$'s neighbors — including $N$. Each refresh of $N$ is itself an activation-like disturbance event against $V$, and with enough far-side hammering the *mitigation's own refreshes* push $V$ past $T_{RH}$ [3].

> **Theorem 2 (Mitigation-induced disturbance).** *Suppose a TRR-like defense refreshes, for each tracked aggressor activation count $c$, its distance-one neighbors with frequency $f(c)$, and suppose each mitigative refresh disturbs the next-nearest row by $\delta$ charge-loss units. Then hammering a distance-two aggressor $F$ for $H$ activations induces $\delta \cdot f(H)$ disturbance on the victim $V = F \pm 2$, with zero direct attacker activations adjacent to $V$. If $\delta \cdot f(H) \geq T_{RH}$ within $t_{REFW}$, the defense causes the very flip it was built to prevent.*
>
> *Proof sketch.* Direct from the defense's specification: the refresh of $N$ is a row-activation event from the victim's perspective, and charge-based disturbance is agnostic to *why* a row was opened. ∎

Half-Double forces a redesign principle: **mitigative actions must be counted inside the disturbance budget**. A refresh is an activation with a different name; defenses that ignore second-order effects can be turned against themselves.

### 4.4 DDR5: Refresh Management, PRAC, and Alert Back-Off

Because stealing time from REF commands does not scale as $T_{RH}$ falls, JEDEC added the **Refresh Management (RFM)** command to DDR5 [5][6]: the *memory controller* counts activations per bank and, past a programmed threshold, issues RFM — ~350 ns of dedicated time for the DRAM to run its internal mitigation [5]. RFM is a *cooperative* interface: the controller supplies trigger and time; the DRAM supplies the algorithm.

A later DDR5 revision adds **Per-Row Activation Counting (PRAC)** — a hardware counter per row — plus **Alert Back-Off (ABO)**, letting the DRAM *alert* the controller and pause traffic when a row exceeds a back-off threshold $N_{BO}$ [3][8]. PRAC closes the tracker's information gap (every row is counted; no sampler to thrash) at a silicon and protocol cost.

The performance tax is severe: one RFM per 10 activations consumes nearly **30% of activation bandwidth**, and $T_{RH} \le 250$ demands multiple RFMs per $t_{REFI}$ [5]. As $T_{RH}$ falls, the mitigation *service rate* must rise, but the bus has finite time to give.

**RogueRFM** shows RFM can be intentionally misused: because RFM stalls a bank for a measurable interval, an attacker builds *covert channels* (bits encoded in RFM-induced timing across $t_{REFI}$ periods) and *denial-of-service* patterns that force the controller to burn bandwidth on mitigation [3]. PRAC's ABO signaling likewise leaks a timing channel — the alert itself reveals a crossed threshold [8]. The lesson generalizes: **every mitigation observable is a potential side channel**, and the DDR5 interfaces are observable by design.

### 4.5 Probabilistic Defenses: From PARA to PROTEAS

PARA's 2014 proposal — refresh neighbors with probability $p$ per activation — was long dismissed as too expensive, but the falling $T_{RH}$ has rehabilitated it: probabilistic schemes have *no tracker to thrash* and degrade smoothly. The key question is what $p$ buys.

> **Theorem 3 (PARA security bound).** *Under PARA with probability $p$, consider a victim row $V$ with two adjacent aggressors hammered $H$ times each within one $t_{REFW}$. The victim's disturbance counter resets whenever either aggressor's activation triggers a probabilistic refresh. The probability that $V$ accumulates $T_{RH}$ consecutive unmitigated aggressor activations is at most $(1-p)^{T_{RH}}$. Hence the attacker's success probability per refresh window is bounded by $H \cdot (1-p)^{T_{RH}} \approx H \cdot e^{-p\,T_{RH}}$.*
>
> *Proof sketch.* Each aggressor activation independently triggers a victim refresh with probability $p$, resetting accumulation. A flip requires a run of $T_{RH}$ activations with no refresh: probability $(1-p)^{T_{RH}}$. Union bound over the at most $H$ windows in which such a run could start. ∎

The bound is exponential in $p \cdot T_{RH}$: at $T_{RH} = 10{,}000$, $p = 0.001$ gives $e^{-10} \approx 4.5 \times 10^{-5}$ per window — one extra refresh per thousand activations, with cost scaling in *traffic* and continuously degrading security.

**PROTEAS** co-designs probabilistic *tracker management* with RFM, keeping maximum disturbance below $T_{RH} \in \{500, 1000\}$ with 1–3% slowdown and under 3 KB per rank [4]. **PrIDE** and **MINT** use single-entry/FIFO probabilistic trackers to tolerate $T_{RH} \approx 1700$ with one mitigation per $t_{REFI}$ (400 with four RFMs) [5], and **QPRAC** adds priority queues directing scarce RFM service to the most dangerous rows [5]. ---

## 5. Empirical Evaluation and Theoretical Analysis

**The threshold collapse, quantified.** At $T_{RH} = 139{,}000$ (DDR3), an attacker must dedicate ~25% of a DDR5 bank's ~550K-activation budget to one victim [8] — noisy and slow. At $T_{RH} = 500$, the victim falls with under 0.1% of the budget, hiding in normal traffic: tracker thrash is fatal at low thresholds.

**Defense comparison.**

| Defense | Mechanism | $T_{RH}$ tolerated | Perf. overhead | Known bypass / weakness |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| In-DRAM TRR | Sampler/counter tracker + targeted refresh | ~10K (DDR4) | <1% | Many-sided hammering [1]; Blacksmith [6] |
| PARA ($p=0.001$) | Stateless probabilistic neighbor refresh | ~10K provable | ~0.2% extra ACTs | Energy cost scales with traffic |
| SoftTRR | OS-level page-table tracking + refresh | Kernel PTE protection | Software overhead | Protects only page tables [7] |
| RFM + tracker (PROTEAS) | Controller-triggered in-DRAM mitigation | 500–1000 | 1–3% | RFM covert channel / DoS [3] |
| PRAC + ABO (+QPRAC) | Per-row counters, alert back-off, priority queues | 1700 (1 RFM/$t_{REFI}$); 400 (4 RFM) | up to ~30% at low $T_{RH}$ | Timing channels via ABO [5][8] |
| BlockHammer | Activation-rate throttling | Configurable | Throughput cap | Limits benign bursty workloads [3] |
| ECC DIMMs | Single-bit correction per word | Raises bar only | Latency / area | Multi-bit flips exceed correction [1] |

*Table 2: No defense dominates; each trades threshold coverage against performance, and each introduces new side effects.*

**Worked bound: PARA at DDR5 parameters.** With $T_{RH} = 1000$, $p = 0.005$, and $H = 500{,}000$ attacker activations per window, Theorem 3 gives:

$$P_{\text{flip}} \le H \cdot (1-p)^{T_{RH}} = 5\times10^5 \cdot (0.995)^{1000} \approx 5\times10^5 \cdot e^{-5.01} \approx 3.3.$$

The vacuous bound correctly signals that $p = 0.005$ is *insufficient* at $T_{RH} = 1000$; raising $p$ to $0.02$ gives $5\times10^5 \cdot e^{-20} \approx 10^{-3}$ — a 2% refresh tax for a rigorous $10^{-3}$ per-window guarantee. The bound states exactly what you pay and get, unlike sampler heuristics whose failure mode is discovered by fuzzers.

**Tracker thrash at scale.** Theorem 1 gives per-interval evasion $\ge 1 - s/n$; with $s = 2$ and $n = 19$ (the TRRespass pattern [1]), each aggressor evades tracking with probability $\approx 0.89$ per interval — the mitigation is *usually blind*. Deterministic tracking has a *cliff*, not a graceful slope.

## 6. Limitations and Open Problems

**Thresholds keep falling.** Table 1 points below $T_{RH} = 100$ within a few generations [4][5], where even PRAC+ABO with multiple RFMs per $t_{REFI}$ faces prohibitive bandwidth loss. The endgame may require changing the DRAM cell itself, not just the protocol.

**Half-Double's full scope is unknown.** Demonstrated on LPDDR4(X), its severity on DDR5 with PRAC is unmeasured, and *second-order disturbance* (mitigations disturbing distance-$\ge 2$ victims) lacks a comprehensive model.

**RowPress and the changing physics.** *RowPress* — flips induced by keeping a row open long rather than activating it repeatedly — lies outside the activation-counting paradigm entirely [4]. Defenses built on counting ACTs, including PRAC, are blind to it by construction.

**Commercial deployment lags.** No commercial systems have yet adopted PRAC/ABO-class mitigations [6]; the ecosystem still relies on bypassable in-DRAM TRR. The DDR5 interfaces exist on paper faster than in silicon.

**Mitigations as oracles.** RFM and ABO signaling are *observable by design* — defense activity is visible in timing [3][8]. Future interfaces must treat the mitigation channel as part of the threat model.

**Formal verification gap.** TRR was never formally specified, let alone verified. A machine-checked model of the DDR5 RFM/PRAC/ABO state machine with proven disturbance bounds remains undone — arguably the most valuable artifact the community could produce next.

---

## 7. Conclusion

TRR, the industry's first answer, failed structurally: a bounded sampler cannot track an unbounded adversary, and TRRespass automated the discovery of that fact [1][2]. Half-Double *inverted* the failure mode — the mitigation's own refreshes become the weapon [3]. DDR5's RFM, PRAC, and ABO are the first *standardized, co-designed* response: per-row counting eliminates the sampler cliff and controller-driven RFM buys the DRAM time [5][6]. But they exact a steep bandwidth tax at low thresholds and open timing channels by making defense activity observable [3][8].

The through-line: **deterministic in-DRAM tracking does not scale**; **probabilistic defenses offer the only gracefully-degrading bounds**. Randomize what you track, prioritize what you service, prove what you claim. The next decade of DRAM security will be won not by obscurity, but by mathematics.
