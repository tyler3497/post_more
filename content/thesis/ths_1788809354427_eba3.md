# End-to-End Verifiable Electronic Voting with Homomorphic Tallying: Cryptographic Foundations from Helios to STAR-Vote

## Abstract

The deployment of electronic voting at national scale has repeatedly collided with one unforgiving requirement: voters must not be asked to trust the computers that count their votes. End-to-end verifiable (E2E-V) systems answer this by making correctness checkable by voters and observers, without privileged access. This thesis develops the full technical stack of modern E2E-V systems: exponential ElGamal encryption and the homomorphic aggregation at the heart of Helios; re-encryption mixnets with the Neff and Bayer-Groth zero-knowledge arguments for correctness of a shuffle; the Benaloh challenge-or-cast protocol for cast-as-intended verification; the STAR-Vote architecture, pairing a voter-verifiable paper trail with end-to-end cryptography on commodity hardware; and risk-limiting audits, culminating in Stark's SHANGRLA framework, which reduces auditing most social choice functions to testing whether the means of finite lists exceed one half. We state the core security theorems, quantify implementation costs from published experiments, and identify the residual assumptions — discrete-log tally extraction, setup ceremonies, and coercion exposure — that bound what cryptography alone can guarantee.

---

## 1. Introduction

Paperless direct-recording electronic (DRE) voting machines promised accessibility and rapid tallies. In practice, they concentrated trust in opaque software: a compromised machine could alter votes while displaying the voter's intended choices, leaving no evidence. The cryptographic response, developed from Chaum's early proposals to deployed systems such as Helios and the STAR-Vote design, is *end-to-end verifiability*: correctness of the outcome is verifiable from public evidence by voters and observers, without trusting the devices, servers, or officials that ran the election [1][4].

End-to-end verifiability decomposes into three checkable properties:

1. **Cast as intended** — the encrypted ballot matches the voter's selections.
2. **Recorded as cast** — the stored ballot is exactly the one the voter authorized.
3. **Tallied as recorded** — the published outcome is the correct function of the recorded ballots.

Each property is enforced by a distinct mechanism. Cast-as-intended is the domain of voter-verifiable challenge protocols, above all Benaloh's *challenge-or-cast*. Recorded-as-cast is enforced by an append-only public *bulletin board* cross-checked against voter receipts. Tallied-as-recorded is enforced either by homomorphic aggregation — adding votes *under* encryption so only the total is decrypted, as in Helios — or by verifiable shuffling: encrypted ballots permuted and re-encrypted by mix servers, each publishing a zero-knowledge proof of faithful shuffling. Above the cryptography, *risk-limiting audits* bound the probability of certifying a wrong outcome by statistically examining a random sample of the paper record.

Section 2 fixes the cryptographic background. Section 3 describes our analytic methodology. Section 4 is the deep dive: homomorphic tallying, mixnets with proofs of correct shuffle, Benaloh verification, the STAR-Vote architecture, and risk-limiting audits through SHANGRLA. Section 5 states the central theorems and surveys empirical costs. Section 6 confronts limitations. Section 7 concludes.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 Groups, discrete logarithms, and the DDH assumption

Let `G` be a cyclic group of large prime order `q` with generator `g`. The *decisional Diffie-Hellman (DDH) assumption* asserts that no efficient adversary can distinguish `(g^a, g^b, g^{ab})` from `(g^a, g^b, g^c)` for uniform random `a, b, c`. DDH underwrites the semantic security of ElGamal and of every homomorphic tallying scheme built on it [3][6]. Deployments instantiate `G` as a subgroup of a finite field (2048-bit safe primes) or an elliptic-curve group, where the mathematics is identical.

### 2.2 ElGamal encryption and its exponential variant

Key generation picks secret `x ∈ Z_q`, public `h = g^x`. Encrypting `m ∈ G` with randomness `r` gives `Enc(m; r) = (g^r, m · h^r)`; decryption: `m = c_2 / c_1^x`. ElGamal is *multiplicatively* homomorphic. Voting needs *additive* homomorphism, obtained by the exponential variant — encode the integer vote `t` as `g^t`:

```
Enc(t; r) = (g^r, g^t · h^r)
```

> **Theorem: (Additive homomorphism).** `Enc(t_1; r_1) ⊙ Enc(t_2; r_2) = Enc(t_1 + t_2; r_1 + r_2)` component-wise. Decryption yields `g^{t_1+t_2}`; the sum is recovered by a small discrete logarithm (table lookup or baby-step giant-step), feasible exactly when the tally space is small.

This is the crux of homomorphic tallying: anyone can multiply all encrypted ballots into a single ciphertext encrypting the total, and the trustees decrypt *only the total*, so no individual ballot is ever exposed [3].

### 2.3 Zero-knowledge proofs of ballot validity

Homomorphic aggregation is worthless if a voter encrypts `t = 100` instead of `t ∈ {0, 1}`. Every ballot must carry a *zero-knowledge proof of well-formedness*: the Chaum-Pedersen proof of equal discrete logarithms, made non-interactive via Fiat-Shamir, in disjunctive composition — the prover shows a ciphertext encrypts `g^0` *or* `g^1` without revealing which. Helios attaches such a proof to each per-candidate ciphertext plus an overall proof on the number of selections. Because proofs are publicly verifiable, *any* observer discards malformed ballots before aggregation [4].

### 2.4 Threshold decryption and distributed trust

No single official should hold the election secret. With Pedersen distributed key generation, `n` trustees jointly produce a public key `h = g^x` whose secret is shared so any quorum of `t + 1` can decrypt while `t` or fewer learn nothing. Each trustee publishes a *partial decryption* with a Chaum-Pedersen correctness proof, making decryption itself universally verifiable [4][7].

### 2.5 The bulletin board

All of the above presupposes an *append-only public bulletin board*: a broadcast channel for encrypted ballots, proofs, trustee actions, and the final tally, integrity-protected in practice by a hash-chained log mirrored by independent observers. The bulletin board converts individual verifiability into universal verifiability — it is the single public dataset against which every claim is checked.

## 3. Methodology

Our analysis proceeds along three complementary axes.

* **Formal modeling.** We adopt game-based definitions of ballot privacy and verifiability: ballot privacy as indistinguishability of bulletin boards, and verifiability as *tally uniqueness* plus correctness of the accepted tally [4]. For audits we use the risk-limit formulation: an audit is *risk-limiting* at level `α` if, whenever the reported outcome is wrong, it escalates to a full hand count with probability at least `1 − α` [2].
* **Construction survey.** We trace each mechanism to its realization: exponential ElGamal aggregation in Helios [4][7], re-encryption mixnets with Bayer-Groth proofs [6], Benaloh challenge-or-cast as implemented in Helios ballot auditing, and the STAR-Vote specification [1].
* **Empirical costing.** We report measured performance from the literature — shuffle proof times and deployment-scale ballot volumes — and implement a toy homomorphic tally in Python to exhibit the homomorphism concretely.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Homomorphic aggregation with exponential ElGamal

In Helios, a ballot for a `k`-candidate contest is a vector of `k` exponential-ElGamal ciphertexts encrypting `0`/`1`, with disjunctive validity proofs and a proof on the number of selections. Tallying is pure public computation:

```
for each candidate j:
    C_j = ⊙_{i=1..n} Enc(v_{ij}; r_{ij})     # homomorphic sum over n ballots
trustees jointly decrypt each C_j -> g^{T_j} -> T_j
```

No mixnet is needed, no ballot is individually decrypted, and any observer can re-perform the aggregation. Discrete-log extraction is trivial since each `T_j` is bounded by the electorate size. The price is the *encoding constraint*: ranked-choice and STV do not linearize, and encoding them either explodes the plaintext space or leaks more than the outcome. Threshold *fully* homomorphic encryption extends the paradigm to alternative vote, at much heavier cost [3].

The following toy implementation exhibits the homomorphism end to end (toy group; production uses 2048-bit groups or elliptic curves):

```python
import random

p = 23            # toy prime
g = 5
x = 6             # trustee secret
h = pow(g, x, p)  # election public key

def enc(t, r):
    """Encrypt integer vote t (exponential ElGamal)."""
    return (pow(g, r, p), pow(g, t, p) * pow(h, r, p) % p)

def dec_sum(A, B):
    """Decrypt aggregate; returns g^T."""
    return B * pow(pow(A, x, p), -1, p) % p

votes = [1, 0, 1, 1, 0]
cts = [enc(v, random.randrange(1, p)) for v in votes]

A = B = 1
for a, b in cts:                       # aggregate: multiply ciphertexts
    A, B = (A * a) % p, (B * b) % p

gt = dec_sum(A, B)                     # g^3
tally = next(t for t in range(6) if pow(g, t, p) == gt)
assert tally == sum(votes) == 3
```

Security rests on DDH, but Helios's history shows the *surrounding* protocol matters as much as the primitive. Cortier and Smyth demonstrated ballot-malleability attacks on early Helios: an adversary observing another voter's ballot could post a meaningfully *related* ballot, breaking ballot independence and secrecy; their fixes — binding ballots to the election context — were adopted in later versions [5]. Their formalization proved that, with those fixes, Helios achieves ballot privacy and strong verifiability under DDH in the random oracle model, under weakened server-trust assumptions [4].

### 4.2 Mixnets and zero-knowledge proofs of correct shuffle

Where homomorphic tallying cannot reach — complex ballots, or designs decrypting anonymized ballots for flexible counting — the tool is the *re-encryption mixnet*. A cascade of servers each applies a secret random permutation and re-encrypts every ciphertext, so if *at least one* server is honest, outputs cannot be linked to inputs. But a malicious server could substitute ciphertexts, so each must prove its shuffle *correct* — same plaintext multiset, permuted — without revealing the permutation. Early proofs (Sako-Kilian, Furukawa-Sako) communicated linearly in the ballot count; Neff's breakthrough used polynomial-root invariance, and Groth pushed toward sublinearity. The practical culmination is the Bayer-Groth argument [6]:

![Mixnet shuffle with zero-knowledge proof chain](public/thesis/ths_1788809354427_eba3-1.webp)

* **Communication** sublinear in `N`: an `m × n` arrangement yields `O(m + n)` group elements — `O(√N)` with `m = n = √N`.
* **Prover** cost `O(N log m)` exponentiations — decisive over Groth-Ishai's `O(N·m)`, via multiexponentiation arguments.
* **Verifier** cost minimal among known schemes; 100,000 ElGamal ciphertexts proved and verified in around two minutes [6].

Fiat-Shamir makes the argument non-interactive, postable on the bulletin board at each mix stage. The result: privacy holds if one mix server is honest and encryption is semantically secure; integrity holds if the shuffle arguments verify, however many servers are corrupt.

| Primitive | Prover cost | Proof size | Trust for privacy |
|---|---|---|---|
| Homomorphic aggregation (Helios) | `O(n·k)` encryptions | `O(n·k)` ciphertexts + ZKPs | threshold trustees |
| Sako-Kilian / Furukawa-Sako shuffle | `O(N)` exps | `O(N)` | 1-of-`m` mix servers |
| Neff shuffle | `O(N)` exps | `O(N)` | 1-of-`m` mix servers |
| Bayer-Groth shuffle | `O(N log m)` exps | `O(√N)` | 1-of-`m` mix servers |

### 4.3 Cast-as-intended: the Benaloh challenge-or-cast protocol

The most insidious failure mode of electronic voting is a device that *displays* the voter's intent while *encrypting* something else. Benaloh's challenge-or-cast protocol resolves this without asking the voter to understand cryptography. The device first *commits* to an encrypted ballot and shows the voter a commitment hash. The voter then chooses, unpredictably to the device, to either **challenge** or **cast**:

* **Challenge:** the device reveals the encryption randomness; the voter (or a helper app) re-encrypts the claimed selections and checks equality with the committed ciphertext. The challenged ballot is *spoiled* — never cast — so no counted vote leaks.
* **Cast:** the voter authorizes the committed ciphertext, which is signed and posted to the bulletin board.

A device cheating on fraction `f` of ballots is caught with probability `1 − (1 − f)^c` after `c` challenges, so even a handful of challengers gives strong statistical protection electorate-wide. Helios implements this as its *ballot auditing* step: voters may audit any number of encrypted ballots before sealing the final one [7].

![Benaloh challenge-or-cast verification flow](public/thesis/ths_1788809354427_eba3-2.webp)

The limitation is behavioral: inattentive voters do not challenge. STAR-Vote therefore institutionalizes challenge at the *ballot-marking device* level, with poll workers and observers performing routine live audits of machines on election day, backed by a paper record voters are prompted to inspect.

### 4.4 STAR-Vote: belt and suspenders

STAR-Vote, architected in 2012 by Benaloh, Byrne, Kortum, McBurnett, Pereira, Stark, and Wallach with Travis County, Texas, is a deliberate synthesis: a DRE-style interface combined with *both* a voter-verifiable paper trail *and* end-to-end cryptography on commercial off-the-shelf hardware [1]. Its guiding metaphor is *belt and suspenders*: no single mechanism is load-bearing.

The architecture separates concerns: **voting terminals** capture selections and produce encrypted exponential-ElGamal ballots with validity proofs, supporting Benaloh-style challenge; **paper printouts** give a human-readable summary the voter reviews before depositing in the ballot box, serving as the authoritative record for audits; **take-home receipts** carry a ballot hash letting each voter confirm recording on the public bulletin board without revealing content; and a **hash-chained controller log**, mirrored publicly, enables observer verification of the homomorphic tally and threshold decryption.

STAR-Vote was designed *from the start* for ballot-level risk-limiting audits: every electronic record matches a paper ballot with a unique identifier, so an RLA samples ballots and compares paper to cast-vote records efficiently. The design confronts ballot-marking-device integrity — voters rarely verify printed output — through procedural live auditing of BMDs and usability research on the review step [1].

### 4.5 Risk-limiting audits and SHANGRLA

Cryptography proves the *electronic* computation correct; it cannot prove the paper matches the electronics. Risk-limiting audits close that gap statistically: an RLA with risk limit `α` guarantees that if the reported outcome is wrong, the audit escalates to a full hand count with probability at least `1 − α`. The classical ballot-polling method, BRAVO, applies Wald's sequential probability ratio test to a random sample; comparison audits, checking sampled paper against electronic cast-vote records, are far more efficient but were historically bespoke per voting method.

Stark's SHANGRLA framework [2] unifies this with one reduction: for most social choice functions in practical use — plurality, multi-winner plurality, supermajority, approval, Borda, STAR, instant-runoff — correctness of the reported outcome is equivalent to a set of *assertions*, each of canonical form: *the mean of a finite list of nonnegative numbers exceeds `1/2`.*

Each assertion is witnessed by an *assorter* mapping each ballot to `[0, 1]`, correct iff every assorter's mean exceeds `1/2`. For two-candidate plurality: `1` for the reported winner, `0` for the loser, `1/2` otherwise; the assorter *margin* `v = 2(Ā − 1/2)` is the familiar vote margin. For comparison audits, each assertion becomes an equivalent claim about the mean of re-scaled *overstatement* errors, testable with identical machinery.

The tests are sequential martingale tests (Kaplan-Markov, ALPHA) valid with or without replacement, handling stratified sampling, missing ballots (*phantoms to evil zombies*), and ballot-style manifests. Crucially, *no multiplicity adjustment is needed*: the audit stops short of a full count only if *every* null is rejected [2].

```haskell
-- SHANGRLA assorter, two-candidate plurality:
-- winner correct iff mean assorter value exceeds 1/2.
data Candidate = Winner | Loser | Other deriving Eq

assorter :: Ballot -> Double
assorter b | vote b == Winner = 1.0
           | vote b == Loser  = 0.0
           | otherwise        = 0.5

assertMajority :: [Ballot] -> Bool
assertMajority bs = sum (map assorter bs) / fromIntegral (length bs) > 0.5
```

| BRAVO (ballot polling) | random ballots | moderate | plurality-family |
| SHANGRLA polling | random ballots | like BRAVO, wider scope | plurality, IRV, Borda, approval, STAR |
| SHANGRLA comparison | ballots vs. CVRs | far smaller samples | same, needs CVR linkage |
| Full hand count | all ballots | maximal cost, zero risk | any |

## 5. Empirical Results and Formal Analysis

### 5.1 Security theorems

> **Theorem: (Ballot privacy).** Under DDH in the random oracle model, with an honest threshold quorum and election-bound ballot encoding, Helios reveals nothing about individual votes beyond the published tally [4][5].

> **Theorem: (Tally uniqueness).** No efficient adversary can produce a verifying bulletin board and transcript for two distinct tallies; verification accepts only tallies consistent with the recorded ballots [4].

> **Theorem: (Shuffle soundness).** The Bayer-Groth argument is complete, sound, and honest-verifier zero-knowledge for correctness of a shuffle, with negligible soundness error and `O(√N)` communication [6].

> **Theorem: (SHANGRLA validity).** Testing each half-average null `Ā_j ≤ 1/2` with any sequentially valid level-`α` test, escalating to a full count unless all nulls reject, yields a risk-limiting audit with risk limit `α` [2].

### 5.2 Measured costs

* **Shuffle proving:** 100,000 ElGamal ciphertexts proved and verified in ~2 minutes, proof sizes an order of magnitude below linear predecessors [6].
* **Deployment scale:** UCL ran Helios for a presidential election with ~4,000 ballots per round — decided by a 2-vote margin — demonstrating E2E-V cryptography at real-election scale [7].
* **Audit workload:** SHANGRLA comparison audits typically examine tens to hundreds of ballots at comfortable margins versus thousands for ballot polling; workload scales inversely with the assorter margin `v` [2].
* **Tally extraction:** per-candidate tallies need only a table lookup bounded by the electorate — negligible. Packed base-`B` encodings cost `O(√N)` with `N = O(v^n)`, motivating FHE extensions for complex methods [3].

### 5.3 Composition argument

The layers compose into one end-to-end claim. Suppose (i) devices are challenged with independent probability, bounding undetected device fraud; (ii) the bulletin board is append-only and widely mirrored, so recorded-as-cast reduces to receipt checks; (iii) the homomorphic aggregation or shuffle arguments verify, so tallied-as-recorded holds computationally; and (iv) a SHANGRLA RLA at risk limit `α` confirms the paper record. Then the probability a wrong outcome stands is bounded by negligible cryptographic errors plus `α` (commonly 5%) — the precise sense in which E2E-V systems deliver *evidence-based elections* [1][2].

## 6. Limitations and Open Problems

**Coercion and receipt-freeness.** Nothing above prevents a voter from *proving* how they voted — challenge protocols and receipts create exactly the evidence a coercer wants. Helios explicitly does not target coercion resistance [5]; coercion-resistant systems (Civitas, JCJ) pay quadratic tallying cost that has defeated practical deployment. Challenge-or-cast must therefore be confined procedurally (e.g. challenges only inside the polling place, content-free receipts).

**Setup ceremonies and trust roots.** Threshold key generation, device provisioning, and bulletin-board initialization are trusted setup phases; their compromise defeats everything downstream. STAR-Vote mitigates this with COTS hardware, public ceremonies, and hash-chained logs, but the problem is reduced, not eliminated [1].

**The discrete-logarithm tally bottleneck.** Exponential ElGamal confines homomorphic tallying to small tally spaces and simple contests. Packed encodings extend the reach slightly; FHE-based approaches [3] extend it greatly but are far from Helios's operational simplicity. Complex methods at national scale still favor mixnet-then-decrypt, which reintroduces per-ballot decryption and its privacy fragility.

**Usability and verification behavior.** The theorems assume voters actually verify: challenge devices, check receipts, inspect paper summaries. Field evidence shows low spontaneous verification rates. The frontier here is behavioral and procedural — directed audits by observers, mandatory BMD live-testing, interfaces that make verification the path of least resistance.

**Post-quantum exposure.** Every construction surveyed relies on discrete-logarithm hardness. Lattice-based homomorphic schemes exist [3], but the surrounding zero-knowledge ecosystem (disjunctive validity proofs, sublinear shuffle arguments) is immature, and migration would require re-proving the entire stack.

**Long-term ballot secrecy.** Bulletin-board ciphertexts are *permanently* published. An adversary who records them today and breaks the discrete logarithm in twenty years learns every vote. Everlasting privacy against future cryptanalysis requires additional machinery current deployments do not use.

---

## 7. Conclusion

End-to-end verifiable voting is no longer a theoretical curiosity. Its components are theorems with implementations: exponential ElGamal gives additive homomorphism under DDH, making Helios-style tallying publicly recomputable; disjunctive Chaum-Pedersen proofs make every ballot self-certifying; Neff's polynomial-invariance idea, refined by Bayer and Groth into a sublinear-communication shuffle argument, makes mixnet integrity checkable at the scale of hundreds of thousands of ballots; Benaloh's challenge-or-cast gives voters a statistical grip on device honesty; STAR-Vote shows how to fuse these with a paper trail on commodity hardware; and SHANGRLA reduces auditing nearly every voting method in practical use to one canonical sequential test. The honest accounting is that cryptography has solved the *counting* problem and the *evidence* problem, while the *human* problems — coercion, setup trust, and whether voters verify — remain. The belt-and-suspenders lesson of STAR-Vote generalizes: verifiability must be layered, because no single mechanism, however elegant its proof, survives contact with a real election alone.
