# The Weil Conjectures and the Riemann Hypothesis for Curves over Finite Fields: Étale Cohomology and Deligne's Proof

## Abstract

The Weil conjectures, formulated in 1949, predicted that the zeta function of a smooth projective variety over a finite field behaves like the Riemann zeta function: rationality, a functional equation, an analogue of the Riemann hypothesis, and a topological interpretation of its zeros via Betti numbers. This thesis develops the theory from the zeta function of curves—where Schmidt, Hasse, and Weil proved the Riemann hypothesis by elementary and Jacobian methods—through Grothendieck's construction of étale and l-adic cohomology, which supplied the missing Weil cohomology theory, to Deligne's 1974 proof of the Riemann hypothesis for varieties over finite fields using the Lefschetz trace formula, monodromy, and Rankin's method. We explain the purity theorem, the decomposition theorem's origins, and applications to exponential sums, coding theory, and the Langlands program. Limitations include the absence of a characteristic-zero analogue and the open standard conjectures.

## 1 Introduction

In 1949, André Weil published a short paper proposing that the number of solutions to polynomial equations over finite fields is governed by a zeta function with properties mirroring the Riemann zeta function [1]. For a smooth projective variety $X$ over the finite field $\mathbb{F}_q$, define

$$
Z(X, t) = \exp\left(\sum_{n \ge 1} \frac{\#X(\mathbb{F}_{q^n})}{n} t^n\right).
$$

Weil conjectured four statements:

1. **Rationality**: $Z(X,t)$ is a rational function of $t$.
2. **Functional equation**: $Z(X, 1/q^d t) = \pm q^{d\chi/2} t^\chi Z(X, t)$ for $X$ of dimension $d$.
3. **Riemann hypothesis**: writing $Z(X,t) = \prod_i P_i(t)^{(-1)^{i+1}}$ with $P_i$ polynomials, the reciprocal roots of $P_i$ have absolute value $q^{i/2}$.
4. **Betti numbers**: $\deg P_i$ equals the $i$-th Betti number of the corresponding complex variety.

> Theorem: (Deligne, 1974) The Riemann hypothesis holds for smooth projective varieties over finite fields: every eigenvalue $\alpha$ of geometric Frobenius acting on $H^i_{\text{ét}}(\bar X, \mathbb{Q}_\ell)$ satisfies $|\alpha| = q^{i/2}$ [2].

The conjectures drove the creation of modern algebraic geometry: Grothendieck built étale cohomology to prove (1), (2), and (4), and Deligne proved (3)—the deepest—by a brilliant detour through monodromy and Rankin's analytic method. This thesis traces that arc: §2 covers zeta functions of curves; §3 constructs étale cohomology and the Lefschetz trace formula; §4 presents Deligne's proof; §5 gives applications; §6 discusses limitations.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Zeta functions of curves

For a smooth projective curve $C$ of genus $g$ over $\mathbb{F}_q$, let $N_n = \#C(\mathbb{F}_{q^n})$. The Hasse–Weil bound asserts

$$
|N_1 - (q+1)| \le 2g\sqrt{q},
$$

proved by Hasse for $g = 1$ (1936) and Weil for general $g$ (1948) using the Jacobian variety and the positivity of the Rosati involution on endomorphisms [3]. In zeta-function language, $Z(C, t) = P(t)/(1-t)(1-qt)$ where $P$ is a degree-$2g$ polynomial whose reciprocal roots $\alpha_i$ satisfy $|\alpha_i| = \sqrt{q}$—the Riemann hypothesis for curves.

Weil's proof for curves already contained the germ of the general strategy: interpret point counts as traces of Frobenius on a cohomology theory. For a curve, the Jacobian $J(C)$ provides this: $N_n = q^n + 1 - \sum_i \alpha_i^n$ where the $\alpha_i$ are eigenvalues of Frobenius on the Tate module $T_\ell(J)$, a $2g$-dimensional $\ell$-adic representation.

### 2.2 What is a Weil cohomology?

Weil axiomatized the needed properties: a contravariant functor $H^*$ from smooth projective varieties over an algebraically closed field to graded vector spaces over a characteristic-zero field, equipped with Poincaré duality, Künneth formula, cycle class maps, and a Lefschetz trace formula

$$
\#X(\mathbb{F}_{q^n}) = \sum_i (-1)^i \operatorname{Tr}(\mathrm{Frob}^n \mid H^i(X)).
$$

Singular cohomology works over $\mathbb{C}$ but not in characteristic $p$. Grothendieck's insight: replace the Zariski topology by the **étale topology**, whose "open sets" are étale morphisms $U \to X$; this topology is fine enough to admit locally constant sheaves $\mathbb{Z}/\ell^n$ with $\ell \ne p$, and the inverse limit yields **$\ell$-adic cohomology** $H^i_{\text{ét}}(\bar X, \mathbb{Q}_\ell)$ [4].

The construction required rethinking foundations: sites, topoi, derived functors of the global-section functor on the étale site, proper and smooth base change, and Poincaré duality via the trace map $H^{2d}_{\text{ét}}(\bar X, \mathbb{Q}_\ell(d)) \to \mathbb{Q}_\ell$. Grothendieck, Artin, Verdier, and Deligne built this machinery in SGA 4, 4½, and 5; with it, rationality, the functional equation, and the Betti-number statement fell [4][5].

| Conjecture | Status | Prover |
|---|---|---|
| Rationality | Proved 1960 | Dwork (p-adic), Grothendieck (étale) |
| Functional equation | Proved 1960s | Grothendieck (Poincaré duality) |
| Betti numbers | Proved 1960s | Grothendieck (comparison) |
| Riemann hypothesis | Proved 1974 | Deligne |

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 The Lefschetz trace formula in étale cohomology

The bridge between point counts and cohomology is the Grothendieck–Lefschetz trace formula. For the geometric Frobenius $F: \bar X \to \bar X$, the fixed points of $F^n$ are exactly $X(\mathbb{F}_{q^n})$, and

$$
\#X(\mathbb{F}_{q^n}) = \sum_{i=0}^{2d} (-1)^i \operatorname{Tr}(F^{*n} \mid H^i_{\text{ét}}(\bar X, \mathbb{Q}_\ell)).
$$

Exponentiating the logarithmic derivative gives the cohomological expression

$$
Z(X, t) = \prod_{i=0}^{2d} \det(1 - F^* t \mid H^i_{\text{ét}}(\bar X, \mathbb{Q}_\ell))^{(-1)^{i+1}}.
$$

Rationality is now obvious; the functional equation follows from Poincaré duality pairing $H^i$ with $H^{2d-i}$; the Betti-number statement follows from comparison theorems identifying $\ell$-adic Betti numbers with topological ones [5]. Everything reduces to bounding the eigenvalues of $F^*$—the Riemann hypothesis.

### 3.2 Deligne's strategy: reduction to curves via Lefschetz pencils

Deligne's masterstroke was to reduce the general case to *curves*, where Weil had already won. The ingredients:

1. **Lefschetz pencils.** A smooth projective $X$ of dimension $d$ admits a pencil of hyperplane sections: a family $X_t$ of $(d-1)$-folds degenerating at finitely many singular fibers. The vanishing cycles generate the "new" cohomology in each induction step.
2. **Monodromy.** The fundamental group of the punctured parameter line acts on the cohomology of a smooth fiber; Deligne proved the monodromy representation has large image (Zariski-dense in the symplectic group), so the invariant part is small.
3. **Rankin's method.** For a lisse $\ell$-adic sheaf $\mathcal{F}$ on a curve, Deligne adapted Rankin's analytic argument: the L-function $L(\mathcal{F}, s)$ and $L(\mathcal{F} \otimes \mathcal{F}^\vee, s)$ constrain the Frobenius eigenvalues to have absolute value $q^{w/2}$ via positivity of Dirichlet coefficients—a purely formal argument given the right estimates.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Purity and weights

Deligne's proof yielded more than the Riemann hypothesis: the **purity theorem**. For a proper smooth morphism, $R^i f_* \mathbb{Q}_\ell$ is *pure of weight $i$*: every Frobenius eigenvalue at a closed point has absolute value $q^{i/2}$. Purity propagates through the six operations, and mixed sheaves acquire **weight filtrations**—the $\ell$-adic analogue of Hodge theory. This became the foundation of the theory of perverse sheaves and the decomposition theorem [7].

The weight formalism explains *why* the Riemann hypothesis is true: eigenvalues carry intrinsic weights, and geometric operations (proper pushforward, tensor product) shift weights predictably. The Riemann hypothesis is the assertion that cohomology of a smooth projective variety is pure—the $\ell$-adic shadow of the Hodge decomposition.

### 4.2 Exponential sums and the Weil bounds

A spectacular application: estimates for exponential sums. For a polynomial $f$ over $\mathbb{F}_q$, the sum $S = \sum_{x \in \mathbb{F}_q} \psi(\operatorname{Tr} f(x))$ (with $\psi$ a nontrivial additive character) is the trace of Frobenius on the cohomology of an Artin–Schreier sheaf; purity gives $|S| \le (d-1)\sqrt{q}$ where $d = \deg f$—the Weil bound, vastly generalizing classical estimates of Gauss and Weil [6].

Kloosterman sums $K(a) = \sum_{x} \psi(x + a/x)$ satisfy $|K(a)| \le 2\sqrt{q}$; Deligne interpreted them via the **Kloosterman sheaf**, a rank-2 lisse sheaf on $\mathbb{G}_m$ pure of weight 1, whose symmetric powers govern moments of Kloosterman sums. This sheaf-theoretic viewpoint—pioneered by Deligne and systematized by Katz—turned analytic number theory over finite fields into representation theory of monodromy groups [8].

### 4.3 From Weil to the Langlands program

The Weil conjectures are the geometric case of the Langlands correspondence: the eigenvalues of Frobenius on $H^i_{\text{ét}}$ are the local parameters of automorphic representations. Deligne's purity theorem became the **Ramanujan conjecture** for $GL_n$ over function fields (proved by Drinfeld for $n=2$ and Lafforgue for general $n$): cuspidal automorphic representations are tempered, i.e., their Satake parameters have absolute value 1 [9].

Grothendieck's dream of **motives**—a universal cohomology theory factoring all Weil cohomologies—remains unrealized, blocked by the standard conjectures. But Deligne's theory of absolute Hodge cycles and André's motivated cycles provide working substitutes, and the Weil conjectures stand as the strongest evidence that motives exist.

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

We sketch the logical spine of Deligne's proof of the Riemann hypothesis (Weil II) [2].

**Step 1 (Reduction to the middle dimension).** By Poincaré duality and the hard Lefschetz theorem (known for $\ell$-adic cohomology), it suffices to bound eigenvalues on $H^d_{\text{ét}}$ for $X$ of even dimension $d$, and the problem is self-dual.

**Step 2 (Lefschetz pencil).** Choose a Lefschetz pencil $f: \tilde X \to \mathbb{P}^1$ (after blowing up the base locus). The Leray spectral sequence relates $H^*(\tilde X)$ to $R^i f_* \mathbb{Q}_\ell$ on $\mathbb{P}^1$. By induction on dimension, all $R^i f_*$ for $i < d-1$ are known pure; the new content is $\mathcal{F} = R^{d-1} f_* \mathbb{Q}_\ell$ modulo constants.

**Step 3 (Monodromy).** The sheaf $\mathcal{F}$ is lisse on $U = \mathbb{P}^1 \setminus S$ (punctured at singular fibers). Deligne proves the geometric monodromy group is the full symplectic group $Sp$ (or orthogonal) via the Picard–Lefschetz formula: vanishing cycles are conjugate under monodromy and span irreducibly. Hence $H^0$ and $H^2_c$ of $\mathcal{F}$ are understood, and the L-function $L(U, \mathcal{F}, t)$ is essentially a polynomial.

**Step 4 (Rankin's trick).** Consider $L(\mathcal{F} \otimes \mathcal{F}^\vee, t)$: its logarithmic coefficients are $\sum_j |\alpha_j|^{2n} \ge 0$ (sums of nonnegative terms), where $\alpha_j$ are the Frobenius eigenvalues. Deligne shows this L-function is rational with controlled poles (using the Fourier transform and the already-known curve case), forcing $|\alpha_j| = q^{(d-1)/2}$ for all $j$. This is Rankin's method geometrized: positivity plus rationality pins down the absolute values exactly [2][6].

**Step 5 (Conclusion).** Purity of $\mathcal{F}$ implies purity of $H^d(X)$, completing the induction. The Riemann hypothesis for $X$ follows.

## 6 Limitations

1. **No characteristic-zero analogue.** There is no known cohomology theory over $\mathbb{C}$ (or $\mathbb{Q}$) playing the role $\ell$-adic cohomology plays over $\mathbb{F}_q$; the classical Riemann hypothesis remains open, and the function-field proof offers no direct transfer.
2. **The standard conjectures.** Grothendieck's conjectures of Lefschetz type and Hodge type—asserting algebraicity of the Künneth projectors and positivity of intersection forms on algebraic cycles—remain open; they would imply the existence of motives and independence of $\ell$.
3. **Singular varieties.** The Weil conjectures as stated require smoothness; for singular $X$, one needs intersection cohomology (proved pure by Gabber, and by Beilinson–Bernstein–Deligne–Gabber in general), a deeper theory.
4. **Effectivity.** The bounds are non-effective in parameters: Deligne's proof gives $|\alpha| = q^{i/2}$ but no algorithm to compute the $\alpha$'s; explicit point-counting (Schoof–Elkies–Atkin) is a separate computational industry.
5. **$p$-adic side.** The $\ell$-adic proof says nothing about $p$-adic valuations of eigenvalues (slopes); the Newton-over-Hodge polygon inequalities (Mazur, Ogus) belong to crystalline cohomology, a parallel universe.

---

## 7 Conclusion

The Weil conjectures transformed algebraic geometry twice: first by demanding a cohomology theory that did not exist, thereby forcing Grothendieck to invent étale cohomology and the modern apparatus of schemes, sites, and topoi; and second by Deligne's proof, which revealed that the deepest analytic estimates in number theory are consequences of geometric monodromy and positivity. The $\ell$-adic formalism—weights, purity, perverse sheaves—has become the language in which arithmetic geometry thinks.

The conjectures' legacy extends far beyond their statements: the Langlands program over function fields, the theory of motives, mirror symmetry's point-counting predictions, and the error-correcting codes of Goppa all trace their lineage to Weil's 1949 insight that finite fields have a topology—one visible only through the lens of étale cohomology. The Riemann hypothesis over $\mathbb{Q}$ still stands; but over $\mathbb{F}_q$, Weil's vision is theorem, and its proof remains the single most consequential achievement of twentieth-century arithmetic geometry.
