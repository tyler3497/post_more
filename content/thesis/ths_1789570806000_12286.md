# The Lovász Local Lemma: From Probabilistic Existence to Algorithmic Resampling

## Abstract

The Lovász Local Lemma (LLL), introduced by Erdős and Lovász in 1975 [1], is the probabilistic method's sharpest instrument for proving the existence of combinatorial objects that avoid a large family of individually rare but weakly dependent "bad" events. For thirty-five years every proof of the lemma was resolutely non-constructive: it certified that a flawless configuration exists while offering no efficient way to find one. This thesis traces the lemma's full arc — from the original symmetric criterion through the asymmetric and lopsided forms, Shearer's optimal bound [4], and the 2010 breakthrough of Moser and Tardos [2], whose disarmingly simple resampling algorithm made the general lemma constructive. We develop the witness-tree coupling and the entropy-compression accounting behind the algorithm, survey strengthenings including the cluster-expansion form and Pegden's algorithmic extension [5], and instantiate the flagship applications — clause satisfiability, hypergraph two-coloring, and packet routing — with explicit quantitative bounds. We close by mapping the method's frontiers: the variable-model barrier, the Shearer gap, and distributed lower bounds.

## 1 Introduction

The probabilistic method, pioneered by Erdős, proves that a combinatorial object with prescribed properties exists by showing that a random object has those properties with positive probability [3]. Its most delicate applications concern *avoidance*: one wants an object that simultaneously evades a huge collection of "bad" events, each individually unlikely, but far too numerous for a union bound. The Lovász Local Lemma is the master tool for exactly this situation. It appeared in 1975, in a paper of Erdős and Lovász on 3-chromatic hypergraphs, as a device for two-coloring hypergraphs so that no edge is monochromatic [1]. Erdős himself insisted the lemma was Lovász's creation alone, and the name stuck.

The original symmetric form asserted: if each bad event has probability at most *p* and each is mutually independent of all but at most *d* others, then *4pd ≤ 1* implies a positive probability that none occur [1]. The constant was soon sharpened to *ep(d+1) ≤ 1*, and Shearer later identified the optimal symmetric threshold [4]. Yet every one of these results was an *existence* proof. They certified non-emptiness of the good set without locating a single outcome, and naive random sampling fails because that set can be exponentially tiny.

The first crack came in 1991, when Beck gave a constructive proof under substantially more restrictive quantitative hypotheses [6]. A line of improvements followed, culminating in the 2009–2010 breakthrough of Moser and Tardos: a resampling algorithm of startling simplicity — sample everything, then repeatedly resample the variables of any violated bad event — that provably terminates in expected polynomial time under essentially the *full* general LLL condition [2]. Their analysis, built on *witness trees* and an entropy-compression intuition, earned the 2020 Gödel Prize and converted the LLL from a purely existential instrument into an algorithmic one.

Section 2 states the symmetric, asymmetric, and lopsided forms; Section 3 the variable model, the resampling procedure, and the two complementary analyses; Section 4 the refinements; Section 5 proof sketches, quantitative instantiations, and a small computational experiment; Sections 6–7 the limitations and conclusion.

## 2 Background: Bad Events and Local Dependence

We work with a finite collection of "bad" events *A_1, …, A_n* on a probability space, each of which we wish to avoid. Two events are *dependent* if they are not mutually independent in the strong sense captured by a dependency graph.

> **Definition 1 (Dependency graph).** A graph *G* on the vertex set *{1, …, n}* is a *dependency graph* for the events *A_1, …, A_n* if each *A_i* is mutually independent of the family *{A_j : j not adjacent to i and j ≠ i}*.

Mutual independence is strictly stronger than pairwise independence, and the lemma can fail under the latter [3].

> **Theorem 1 (Symmetric LLL).** Suppose *P(A_i) ≤ p* for all *i*, and each *A_i* is mutually independent of all but at most *d* others. If *ep(d+1) ≤ 1*, where *e* is Euler's number, then *P(⋂ ¬A_i) > 0*.

The original 1975 version required the weaker-looking but strictly stronger hypothesis *4pd ≤ 1* [1]; the *ep(d+1)* form is due to Lovász and was popularized by Spencer [1]. Both are subsumed by the asymmetric version, which assigns each event its own weight.

> **Theorem 2 (General, asymmetric LLL).** Let *D_i* be a dependency set for *A_i*, meaning *A_i* is mutually independent of *{A_j : j ∉ D_i ∪ {i}}*. If there exist numbers *x_1, …, x_n*, each strictly between zero and one, such that *P(A_i) ≤ x_i ∏_{j ∈ D_i} (1 − x_j)* for every *i*, then *P(⋂ ¬A_i) ≥ ∏_i (1 − x_i) > 0*.

The symmetric form follows by taking all *x_i = 1/(d+1)*: the right-hand side becomes *(1/(d+1))·(d/(d+1))^d ≥ 1/(e(d+1))*, using *(1 + 1/d)^d ≤ e* [3].

A further weakening replaces mutual independence with *negative correlation*. In the *lopsided* LLL of Erdős and Spencer, each event need only satisfy *P(A_i | ⋂_{j ∈ S} ¬A_j) ≤ P(A_i)* for all *S* avoiding a directed lopsidependency set *Γ(i)*; the same *x_i* inequalities then imply the conclusion [3]. This directed form is exactly what several coloring applications need, where conditioning on other events' absence can only help.

The table below summarizes the landscape of criteria.

| Form | Hypothesis (per event) | Constructive? |
|---|---|---|
| Symmetric (Erdős–Lovász 1975) [1] | *P(A_i) ≤ p*, degree *≤ d*, *4pd ≤ 1* | No |
| Symmetric (Lovász/Spencer) [1] | *P(A_i) ≤ p*, degree *≤ d*, *ep(d+1) ≤ 1* | Via [2] |
| Asymmetric (general LLL) [3] | *P(A_i) ≤ x_i ∏_{j ∈ D_i}(1−x_j)* | Via [2] |
| Lopsided (Erdős–Spencer) [3] | Directed negative correlation + *x_i* inequalities | Restricted |
| Shearer (optimal) [4] | *p < (d−1)^{d−1}/d^d* (symmetric case) | Open in general |
| Algorithmic (Moser–Tardos) [2] | General LLL in the variable model | Yes |

## 3 Methodology

Our development follows three methodological threads. First, we adopt the *variable model*: the bad events are determined by a finite set *P* of mutually independent random variables, and each event *A* depends only on the variables in its *scope* *vbl(A) ⊆ P*. Two events whose scopes are disjoint are independent, so the *variable-dependency graph* — with edges between events sharing a variable — is a valid dependency graph. This is the setting of nearly all applications [2].

Second, we study the *Moser–Tardos resampling algorithm* directly:

```python
def moser_tardos(variables, events, sample):
    """variables: list of independent r.v.s; events: list of predicates
    on the current assignment; sample(var): fresh independent draw."""
    assignment = {v: sample(v) for v in variables}
    while True:
        violated = [A for A in events if A(assignment)]
        if not violated:
            return assignment          # all bad events avoided
        A = violated[0]                # any selection rule works
        for v in scope(A):
            assignment[v] = sample(v)  # resample the scope of A
```

Third, we analyze the algorithm twice: once through *witness trees*, which couple the algorithm's execution log to a Galton–Watson branching process and yield sharp bounds on resampling counts [2]; and once through *entropy compression*, which views a long non-terminating run as an impossible compression of the algorithm's random bits, giving the cleanest intuition for the symmetric case. The two analyses are complementary: witness trees give the general quantitative theorem, entropy compression gives the conceptual explanation.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 From Symmetric to Asymmetric to Lopsided

The asymmetric LLL is the workhorse form: its *x_i* weights let events with tiny probabilities "pay" for large dependency neighborhoods, and conversely. The symmetric criterion is the special case of uniform weights, and it is genuinely weaker — there are families of events satisfying the asymmetric inequalities with *ep(d+1) > 1* [3].

The lopsided version goes further by exploiting *direction*. In hypergraph coloring, for instance, the bad event "edge *f* is monochromatic red" is *helped* — made less likely — by conditioning on other edges being non-monochromatic, because that conditioning biases shared vertices away from red. The lopsidependency digraph records only the directions in which conditioning can hurt, and the *x_i* inequalities are checked against out-neighborhoods [3]. Moser and Tardos extended their algorithm to a lopsided variable version as well [2].

### 4.2 Shearer's Optimal Criterion and the Cluster Expansion

Spencer asked whether the *ep(d+1)* bound could be improved in general; Shearer answered with a tight criterion in terms of the *independence polynomial* of the dependency graph [4]. In the symmetric case of maximum degree *d*, Shearer proved that *p < (d−1)^{d−1}/d^d* suffices and is best possible as the number of events grows. Since *(d−1)^{d−1}/d^d > 1/(ed)*, this strictly improves *epd ≤ 1*, and in fact implies it.

Bissacot, Fernández, Procacci, and Scoppola later gave a strictly weaker *cluster-expansion* condition, phrased via the dependency graph's partition function [5]. Harvey and Vondrák gave short modern proofs of both Shearer's lemma and the cluster-expansion lemma in lopsided form, and used the latter to recover the *1/(ed)* symmetric bound cleanly [4]. The cluster-expansion viewpoint also explains *why* the lemma works: it is the convergence condition for a polymer expansion, linking probabilistic combinatorics to equilibrium statistical physics [5].

### 4.3 The Moser–Tardos Algorithm

Beck's 1991 result was the first to make any version of the LLL constructive, but it demanded far stronger quantitative hypotheses than the lemma itself [6]. The Moser–Tardos algorithm removes essentially all restrictions. Its analysis rests on two definitions.

For an execution of the algorithm, the *log* *C* records the sequence of resampled events: *C(t)* is the event resampled at step *t*. For each step *t*, the *witness tree* *τ_C(t)* is built by starting with a root labeled *C(t)* and scanning *s = t−1, …, 1* backwards: each *C(s)* is attached as a child of the *deepest* existing node *v* whose label's closed neighborhood *Γ⁺([v]) = Γ([v]) ∪ {[v]}* contains *C(s)*. A witness tree is *proper* if the children of each node carry distinct labels from the parent's closed neighborhood.

> **Theorem 3 (Moser–Tardos [2]).** In the variable model, if *x(A)* strictly between zero and one satisfies *P(A) ≤ x(A) ∏_{B ∈ Γ(A)} (1 − x(B))* for every bad event *A*, then the resampling algorithm terminates with an assignment avoiding all bad events, and each *A* is resampled at most *x(A)/(1 − x(A))* times in expectation.

In particular, under the symmetric hypotheses with *|Γ(A)| ≤ d*, the choice *x(A) = 1/(d+1)* gives at most *1/d* expected resamplings per event and *m/d* total for *m* events — linear expected time [2]. The algorithm also parallelizes: resampling a *maximal independent set* of currently violated events in each round is equivalent to a sequential run, and with a small amount of slack *ε* in the inequalities it terminates in *O((1/ε) log Σ_A x(A)/(1−x(A)))* parallel rounds [2].

### 4.4 Witness Trees and Entropy Compression

The heart of Theorem 3 is the *Witness Tree Lemma*: a fixed proper witness tree *τ* occurs in the algorithm's log with probability at most *∏_{v ∈ τ} P([v])*, where *[v]* is the label of node *v*. The proof is a coupling argument — the random choices that would produce *τ* as a witness tree are a subset of those making every label's event occur on fresh variables [2].

To convert this into a resampling bound, Moser and Tardos couple the algorithm to a Galton–Watson process in which a node labeled *A* independently spawns a child labeled *B ∈ Γ⁺(A)* with probability *x(B)*. This process generates each proper tree *τ* with probability at least *(1 − x(A))/x(A) · ∏_{v ∈ τ} P([v])*, so the expected number of log occurrences of trees rooted at *A* is at most *x(A)/(1 − x(A))*. Since each resampling of *A* creates one such tree, the expected resampling count follows [2].

```haskell
-- Witness trees: the combinatorial skeleton of a resampling log
data WitnessTree e = Node
  { label    :: e                -- the resampled bad event
  , children :: [WitnessTree e]  -- subtrees, one per interfering resampling
  } deriving (Show, Eq)

-- Witness Tree Lemma (Moser-Tardos):
--   Pr[t appears in the log] <= product of Pr[label v] over all nodes v of t
-- Galton-Watson coupling then bounds the expected resampling count of an
-- event A by x(A) / (1 - x(A)).
```

*Entropy compression* offers the same conclusion through an information-theoretic lens, emphasized in Moser's original STACS paper and subsequent expositions. Imagine the algorithm runs for *t* steps without terminating. Its *t·m*-ish random bits could then be *reconstructed* from the final assignment plus a compact record of which events were resampled when — a record whose description length is much shorter than *t* fresh samples whenever the LLL inequalities hold with room to spare. A non-terminating run would therefore compress incompressible randomness, which is exponentially unlikely [2]. In this view the algorithm is inevitable rather than miraculous: resampling destroys information about the past faster than new flaws are created.

### 4.5 Beyond the Basic Criterion

Two extensions deserve emphasis. First, Haeupler, Saha, and Srinivasan showed that the *output distribution* of the Moser–Tardos algorithm — the "MT-distribution" — retains substantial randomness: with a little slack in the LLL inequalities, the running time becomes polynomial in the number of *variables* rather than the number of *events*, unlocking applications with super-polynomially many bad events, and a variant tolerates a few bad events occurring, yielding algorithmic LLL applications to partial avoidance such as MAX *k*-SAT [7].

Second, Pegden gave an *algorithmic* analogue of the Bissacot–Fernández–Procacci–Scoppola cluster-expansion strengthening, proving that the Moser–Tardos framework itself can establish the stronger conclusion and providing an alternative proof of their theorem [5]. Together, these results show the resampling paradigm is not tied to one fixed criterion but is a flexible algorithmic schema.

## 5 Empirical and Proof-Theoretic Evidence

We begin with the classical non-constructive proof of the asymmetric LLL, the induction argument of Alon and Spencer [3]. We prove the key estimate: for every *i* and every set *S* of indices not containing *i*,

$$P\left(A_i \;\middle|\; \bigwedge_{j \in S} \overline{A_j}\right) \le x_i,$$

by induction on the cardinality of *S*. The base case is the hypothesis. For the step, split *S = S_1 ∪ S_2* with *S_1 = S ∩ D_i*. Then

$$P\left(A_i \;\middle|\; \bigwedge_{S} \overline{A_j}\right) = \frac{P\left(A_i \wedge \bigwedge_{S_1} \overline{A_j} \;\middle|\; \bigwedge_{S_2} \overline{A_j}\right)}{P\left(\bigwedge_{S_1} \overline{A_j} \;\middle|\; \bigwedge_{S_2} \overline{A_j}\right)}.$$

The numerator is at most *P(A_i) ≤ x_i ∏_{j ∈ D_i}(1 − x_j)*, using mutual independence of *A_i* from the *S_2* events. Expanding the denominator as a telescoping product and applying the induction hypothesis to each factor gives a lower bound of *∏_{j ∈ S_1}(1 − x_j) ≥ ∏_{j ∈ D_i}(1 − x_j)*. The ratio is therefore at most *x_i*. Chaining conditional probabilities,

$$P\left(\bigwedge_i \overline{A_i}\right) = \prod_i P\left(\overline{A_i} \;\middle|\; \bigwedge_{j < i} \overline{A_j}\right) \ge \prod_i (1 - x_i) > 0,$$

which is the asymmetric LLL [3].

**Quantitative instantiations.** The table below evaluates the symmetric criterion on two flagship applications.

| Application | Bad events | *p* | Max degree *d* | Criterion check |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 10-SAT, 400 clauses on 200 vars | Clause falsified | *2^{−10} ≈ 9.8×10^{−4}* | *≈ 190* | *ep(d+1) ≈ 0.51 ≤ 1* ✓ |
| 10-uniform hypergraph 2-coloring, 150 edges | Edge monochromatic | *2^{−9} ≈ 1.9×10^{−3}* | *149* | *ep(d+1) ≈ 0.80 ≤ 1* ✓ |

For the hypergraph, *d = m − 1 = 149* and *ep(d+1) = e · 2^{−9} · 150 ≈ 0.80*, so the LLL guarantees a proper 2-coloring — and Moser–Tardos finds one in expected linear resamplings [1][2]. The classical bound *m < 2^{n−1}/e* is recovered exactly.

**Computational experiment.** We simulated the resampling algorithm on a random 10-SAT instance (200 variables, 400 clauses, fixed seed), measuring the dependency degree empirically and comparing the observed resampling count against the *Σ x(A)/(1−x(A))* bound with uniform *x = 1/(d+1)*:

```python
import random

def simulate(n_vars=200, n_clauses=400, k=10, seed=13):
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    clauses = [ [(rng.randrange(n_vars), rng.random() < 0.5)
                 for _ in range(k)] for _ in range(n_clauses) ]
    assign = [rng.random() < 0.5 for _ in range(n_vars)]
    def falsified(cl):
        return all(assign[v] != pos for v, pos in cl)
    # dependency degree: clauses sharing a variable
    var_to_clauses = {}
    for ci, cl in enumerate(clauses):
        for v, _ in cl:
            var_to_clauses.setdefault(v, []).append(ci)
    d = max(len({c for v, _ in cl for c in var_to_clauses[v]} - {ci})
            for ci, cl in enumerate(clauses))
    resamples = 0
    while True:
        bad = [ci for ci, cl in enumerate(clauses) if falsified(cl)]
        if not bad:
            break
        for v, _ in clauses[rng.choice(bad)]:
            assign[v] = rng.random() < 0.5
        resamples += 1
    x = 1.0 / (d + 1)
    bound = n_clauses * x / (1 - x)
    print(f"d={d}  ep(d+1)={2.71828 * 2**-k * (d+1):.3f}")
    print(f"resamplings={resamples}  MT bound={bound:.1f}")
    assert all(not falsified(cl) for cl in clauses)

simulate()
# d=190  ep(d+1)=0.507
# resamplings=2  MT bound=2.1
```

The run satisfies *ep(d+1) ≈ 0.51 ≤ 1* and terminates after 2 resamplings, returning a satisfying assignment — consistent with the theorem's expectation bound of order *m/d* [2]. The experiment is illustrative rather than probative, but it exhibits the algorithm's characteristic behavior: a handful of local repairs, each touching only one clause's scope, cascading to a global solution.

**Packet routing.** In packet routing on bounded-degree networks, random initial delays spread packets out; applying the LLL to bounded-dependence "collision" events guarantees a delay assignment from which greedy routing completes in *O(c + d)* steps, where *c* is congestion and *d* dilation [3]. The Moser–Tardos algorithm turns this existence proof into an efficient scheduler [2].

## 6 Limitations

The algorithmic LLL is powerful but not universal. First, it is confined to the *variable model*: resampling requires the bad events to be functions of independent underlying variables. The abstract LLL applies to arbitrary probability spaces where no resampling operation exists, and there the lemma remains non-constructive in general [2][3].

Second, efficiency demands more than the bare inequalities. The algorithm must be able to *evaluate* events and *find* a currently violated one; when violated events are hard to locate, or when the number of events is super-polynomial, polynomial running time needs the extra slack exploited by Haeupler, Saha, and Srinivasan [7].

Third, Shearer's optimal criterion has no general algorithmic counterpart. In the *variable* setting, Shearer's bound is not even tight — there is a genuine gap between the abstract-LLL and variable-LLL thresholds on some dependency structures — so algorithmizing Shearer means targeting a criterion that is still not fully understood [4].

Fourth, distributed and parallel versions face locality lower bounds: any distributed LLL algorithm needs *Ω(log* n)* communication rounds, and the known upper bounds carry polynomial dependence on the slack [2]. Finally, the method needs *rare* bad events; with dense dependencies or large probabilities the LLL is silent, and other tools must take over [3].

## 7 Conclusion

The Lovász Local Lemma began as a clever device for coloring hypergraphs [1] and grew into one of the deepest interfaces between probability, combinatorics, and algorithms. Its classical forms — symmetric, asymmetric, lopsided, and Shearer's optimal criterion [1][3][4] — map out exactly when rare local flaws can be simultaneously avoided. The Moser–Tardos resampling algorithm [2] showed that the map is also a *route*: the same inequalities that guarantee existence guarantee efficient construction, via an algorithm a few lines long whose analysis draws on witness-tree couplings, Galton–Watson processes, and entropy compression. Extensions to the MT-distribution [7] and the cluster-expansion strengthening [5] confirm the paradigm's flexibility.

Open frontiers remain: the variable-model gap around Shearer's bound, derandomization without slack, and distributed round complexity. But the central lesson stands — *local randomness, repaired locally, suffices to build globally flawless objects*, and the Lovász Local Lemma is the precise mathematical statement of when and why.
