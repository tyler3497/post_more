# Compute-in-Memory Accelerators: Memristive Crossbars, Charge-Domain MAC Arrays, and the Limits of Non–Von Neumann Inference

## Abstract

The von Neumann memory wall — the energy cost of shuttling weights and activations between DRAM and logic — now dominates deep learning inference, where moving a word across the package costs two orders of magnitude more than the multiply-accumulate itself. Compute-in-memory (CIM) dissolves this dichotomy by evaluating matrix-vector products in the physics of the memory medium: currents summing through a memristive crossbar under Kirchhoff's and Ohm's laws complete a vector-matrix multiplication in O(1) time. This thesis develops the theory of analog CIM inference accelerators: memristive crossbars (ReRAM, PCM, FeFET) and charge-domain SRAM macros, the mapping of quantized networks onto conductance matrices, and the device non-idealities — variability, write noise, PCM drift, IR drop, stuck cells — separating nominal from realized accuracy. We survey the mitigation families: iterative write-verify, hardware-aware noise-injection training, and mixed-precision hybrids pairing an analog engine with a digital refiner. We derive scaling bounds on signal-to-noise ratio per unit energy and argue that CIM will absorb the dense low-precision kernels of inference while digital logic retains control, calibration, and high-precision duties.

## 1 Introduction

The trajectory of modern computing has been defined by a single architectural constant: the separation of storage from computation. Since the early formulations of the stored-program architecture, processors have fetched operands from memory, transformed them, and written them back, a cycle whose physical cost is set not by the arithmetic but by the interconnect. For deep neural networks, in which inference over a single image may demand billions of multiply-accumulate (MAC) operations and terabytes of weight traffic, the memory wall is no longer a metaphor but the dominant term in the energy ledger: a 64-bit DRAM access costs on the order of 1 nJ, while a floating-point MAC in a modern node costs tens of femtojoules [1]. The arithmetic has become nearly free; the data movement has not.

Compute-in-memory — also called processing-in-memory or analog in-memory computing — proposes to invert the relationship by embedding the operation *in* the storage medium. A crossbar of programmable conductance devices performs a full vector-matrix multiplication (VMM) in a single read cycle: input voltages applied to wordlines induce currents through each device proportional to the product of voltage and conductance, and Kirchhoff's current law sums these along each bitline [2]. The entire O(n²) MAC field executes in O(1) analog time, with energy amortized over the array rather than over the interconnect. Demonstrated prototypes now report hundreds of TOPS/W on quantized networks [6], and phase-change memory systems have run end-to-end object detection networks with thousand-fold energy advantages over GPUs [6].

The promise is tempered by physics. Analog devices are stochastic, nonlinear, drifting, and spatially variant: the same conductance programmed twice is rarely the same twice, and PCM devices relax structurally over time with a power-law decay of conductance [8]. The thesis that follows formalizes this tension. Section 2 establishes the device physics and array primitives; Section 3 details the mapping of deep networks onto analog arrays; Section 4 examines crossbar, charge-domain, and mixed-precision architectures in depth; Section 5 presents empirical evidence and formal SNR arguments; and Section 6 confronts the limitations that bound the domain of applicability.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Device Physics

*Memristive devices* modulate their conductance in response to applied stimuli, retaining state without power. Three families dominate CIM research:

- **Resistive RAM (ReRAM/RRAM):** filamentary oxide devices (HfO₂, TaOₓ) switched by ion migration; sub-10 ns switching, high endurance, but significant cycle-to-cycle and device-to-device variability, plus nonlinear and asymmetric switching that complicates precise multi-level programming [4].
- **Phase-change memory (PCM):** Ge₂Sb₂Te₅ or Ge-rich GST alloys switched between amorphous (high resistance) and crystalline (low resistance) states by Joule heating. PCM offers large ON/OFF ratios and mature integration (128 Mb demonstrated in CIM contexts) but suffers *conductance drift*: structural relaxation of the amorphous phase produces a time-dependent resistance R(t) = R₀(t/t₀)^ν with drift exponent ν ≈ 0.05–0.1, degrading programmed levels over months unless compensated [6][8].
- **Ferroelectric devices (FeFET, FeRAM, FTJ):** polarization-based switching, symmetric and low-energy, with growing interest for both inference and training accelerators.

Multi-level cells store several bits per device by partial programming; iterative program-and-verify sequences tighten distributions at the cost of programming latency that scales with the number of levels.

### 2.2 The Crossbar Primitive

A crossbar with wordline voltages **v** ∈ ℝᵐ and conductance matrix **G** ∈ ℝᵐˣⁿ produces column currents **i** = **G**ᵀ**v** by Ohm's law (each cell current g_ij·v_i) and Kirchhoff's current law (summation along the column). Because weights may be negative, *differential encoding* is standard: w_ij = g⁺_ij − g⁻_ij across two devices or two columns, doubling area but centering the signal [2].

> **Theorem:** Under ideal devices, a crossbar computes an exact vector-matrix product in constant analog time, with energy per MAC decreasing as the array grows, because peripheral overhead (DAC/ADC) is amortized over the array area rather than per operation.

The corollary is sobering: once peripheral circuits dominate (as they do in small arrays or high-precision sensing), the advantage erodes — motivating the architectural innovations of Section 4.

### 2.3 SRAM Charge-Domain Macros

Not all CIM is memristive. *Charge-domain* SRAM CIM performs MAC by charge sharing or redistribution on bitline capacitors rather than by current summation. Because capacitors are deterministic, linear, and CMOS-native, charge-domain macros sidestep conductance variability and drift entirely, at the cost of density (SRAM cells are far larger than 1T1R crossbar cells) and volatility (weights must be reloaded). The analog-CIM versus digital-CIM distinction is thus really a *current-domain versus charge-domain versus digital* trilemma, and modern frameworks benchmark all three [7].

## 3 Methodology

We adopt a three-layer methodology: (i) device-level characterization of non-idealities, abstracted into stochastic conductance models; (ii) array-level simulation with calibrated circuit models (NeuroSim-class frameworks validated to <1% error against post-layout SPICE of taped-out macros) [7]; and (iii) algorithm-level co-design, where networks are trained or fine-tuned with hardware noise models in the loop. Weights are quantized to 2–8 bits, activations to 4–8 bits, and mapped onto arrays of 32×32 to 256×256 cells — the practical ceiling set by sensing margin and IR drop [7]. Non-idealities are injected as Gaussian or measured-distributional perturbations at programming time (write noise), read time (RTN/thermal noise), and retention time (drift), and accuracy is evaluated end-to-end on vision benchmarks.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Memristive Crossbars for Deep Inference

Large-scale demonstrations anchor the field. Yao et al. pioneered fully hardware-implemented memristor CNNs; subsequent systems mapped complete YOLOv2 object-detection pipelines onto parallel 128 Mb C-doped GST PCM chips, reporting 4,180× energy efficiency and 228× throughput gains over a high-end GPU, with a hardware drift-calibration unit restoring accuracy to within 4.7% of the software baseline after four months of conductance drift [6]. Memristive crossbars have also been extended beyond CNNs to memory-augmented networks with hashing and similarity search [2], and to transformer self-attention by storing Kᵀ and V in crossbars and exploiting the single-read-cycle VMM [1].

The mapping recipe is by now canonical:

1. **Quantize** weights and activations to the device's effective precision (typically 4–6 bits equivalent after non-idealities).
2. **Tile** large layers across multiple arrays with differential encoding; partial sums are digitized and accumulated.
3. **Encode inputs** via multi-bit DACs, pulse-width modulation, or bit-serial streaming — trading latency for DAC area [7].
4. **Calibrate** periodically against drift and temperature.

### 4.2 Charge-Domain vs. Current-Domain Sensing

Current-domain sensing (the classic crossbar read) is fast but exposes the analog signal to IR drop along wordlines/bitlines, device nonlinearity, and summed leakage — errors that grow with array size and limit practical arrays to ~256×256 [7]. Charge-domain sensing instead accumulates MAC results as charge on capacitors and reads them by redistribution, which is intrinsically linear and largely immune to device variability. The price is area (capacitors and SRAM cells are bulky) and the volatility of SRAM. Recent design-space studies therefore treat the choice as workload-dependent: current-domain memristive arrays maximize density and TOPS/W for stationary-weight inference; charge-domain SRAM macros maximize accuracy-per-joule where reprogrammability or determinism matters. Hybrid ACIM/DCIM architectures are now supported in benchmarking frameworks for exactly this reason [7].

### 4.3 The Non-Ideality Zoo and How Each Is Tamed

| Non-ideality | Physical origin | Effect on inference | Standard mitigation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Write (programming) noise | Stochastic filament/phase formation | Weight error ~1–5% of range | Iterative write-verify; N devices per synapse [5] |
| Conductance drift (PCM) | Structural relaxation, R ∝ t^ν | Accuracy decay over weeks/months | Drift calibration units; periodic refresh [6][8] |
| Device-to-device variation | Process variation | Systematic weight offsets | Hardware-aware training; per-column scaling |
| IR drop | Wire resistance in large arrays | Position-dependent attenuation | Array tiling ≤256×256; conductance-range optimization |
| Stuck-at faults | Failed cells | Outlier weights | Fault-aware mapping; redundant columns |
| Read noise / RTN | Trapping-detrapping, thermal | SNR floor on sensing | Averaging over devices; moderate precision targets |
| Nonlinear I–V | Field-dependent conduction | Distorted MAC | Low read voltages; nonlinearity-aware mapping |

The *multi-memristive synapse* — using N devices per weight and reading all while programming one via arbitration — demonstrated software-equivalent accuracy where N = 1 failed catastrophically, needing N = 7 to close the gap [5]. At the circuit level, non-linear current-scaling strategies have compensated ePCM drift for projected two-year retention in spiking networks without retraining [8].

### 4.4 Training With and On Analog Hardware

Training on AIMC hardware is strictly harder than inference: backpropagation demands high-precision, symmetric weight updates that filamentary devices cannot natively provide [4]. Three approaches have emerged. **(a) Hardware-aware training in software:** noise models of the target chip are injected during forward passes so the network learns flat minima robust to device statistics [3]. **(b) Chip-in-the-loop fine-tuning:** a network pretrained in FP32 is fine-tuned with the physical chip in the loop, adapting to the specific device's quirks. **(c) Dedicated AIMC training algorithms:** e.g., in-memory SGD variants with chopper-like asymmetry compensation that have demonstrated near-FP accuracy on prototype arrays [4]. A related paradigm, *learning-to-learn*, meta-trains networks so that few-shot adaptation succeeds on low-precision neuromorphic hardware despite its constraints [3].

### 4.5 Mixed-Precision: The Pragmatic Compromise

The mixed-precision architecture of Le Gallo et al. resolves the accuracy dilemma by refusing to choose: a von Neumann machine and a memristive computational-memory unit cooperate, the analog unit performing the bulk low-precision work while the digital machine iteratively refines the solution (e.g., iterative refinement around GMRES). The team solved a 10,000-equation linear system with 959,376 PCM devices at high final accuracy, and applied the same hybrid to gene-interaction networks from RNA data [5]. The lesson generalizes: *analog for throughput, digital for precision* — a division of labor that also appears in drift-calibration units and digital residual paths in modern CIM macros.

## 5 Empirical Results and Formal Arguments

**Prototype evidence.** The quantitative record is strong for inference: the PCM YOLOv2 system above (4,180× energy, 228× throughput vs. GPU) [6]; memristive MANNs matching software accuracy on few-shot Omniglot with all dot products in physical crossbars, including measured device fluctuations and wire-resistance drops [2]; and variation-aware ViT accelerators evaluated on ImageNet-1K across Gaussian analog error, conductance variation, and quantization granularity, showing that full-analog designs (analog normalization, residual paths) remove the AD/DA conversion bottleneck [1].

**Calibration.** Benchmark claims rest on validated models: DNN+NeuroSim was calibrated against a 40-nm RRAM CIM macro's post-layout simulations, reaching <1% chip-level error in area, latency, and energy after calibration, and its system-level conclusions (which device/architecture wins per workload) survived calibration [7].

**Formal SNR bound.** Consider an m×n array with per-device conductance noise variance σ²_g and signal power P. The column SNR scales as:

```python
import numpy as np

def column_snr(m, g_rms, sigma_g, v_rms):
    # uncorrelated device noise: signal ~ (m * g_rms * v_rms)^2
    # noise power ~ m * sigma_g^2 * v_rms^2  (variances add)
    signal = (m * g_rms * v_rms) ** 2
    noise = m * (sigma_g ** 2) * (v_rms ** 2)
    return 10 * np.log10(signal / noise)

for m in (64, 128, 256, 512):
    print(m, round(column_snr(m, 10e-6, 0.3e-6, 0.2), 1), "dB")
```

SNR grows as √m — favorable — but ADC energy grows exponentially with the bits needed to digitize the larger dynamic range, and IR drop eventually caps usable m near 256 [7]. Hence the optimum is a *tiled ensemble of medium arrays*, not one giant crossbar. A parallel argument bounds training: with asymmetric switching, the effective update SNR per pulse train is limited by the write-noise floor, which is why hardware-aware algorithms outperform naive SGD-by-pulses by orders of magnitude in convergence [4].

## 6 Limitations

CIM is not a general-purpose replacement for digital logic, and the boundaries are now well mapped. **Precision:** analog MAC realistically delivers 4–8 equivalent bits; workloads needing FP32 (scientific computing, training gradients) require mixed-precision hybrids [5]. **Reprogrammability:** memristive weights are slow and energy-costly to rewrite (row-by-row programming; attention's dynamic K, V matrices stress this particularly) [1], favoring stationary-weight inference. **Retention:** PCM drift demands calibration or refresh on month timescales [6][8]; ReRAM retention is better but variability worse. **Array size:** IR drop and sensing margin cap arrays at ~256×256, forcing tiled architectures with digital accumulation overhead [7]. **Toolchain maturity:** mapping, tiling, and calibration still require expert co-design; frameworks like NeuroSim, AIHWKit, and XBTorch are closing the gap but remain research-grade. **Benchmark hygiene:** TOPS/W figures often exclude DAC/ADC and digital periphery or assume ideal devices; calibrated end-to-end numbers are typically 3–10× less flattering.

## 7 Conclusion

Analog and in-memory computing has crossed from device curiosity to systems reality: memristive crossbars now execute complete vision pipelines and transformer attention in constant-time analog physics, charge-domain SRAM macros offer a deterministic alternative where precision matters, and mixed-precision hybrids deliver high final accuracy by pairing analog throughput with digital refinement [1][5][6]. The non-idealities — write noise, drift, variation, IR drop — are real but increasingly *engineered around* rather than wished away, through write-verify, multi-device synapses, hardware-aware training, drift calibration, and tiled medium-size arrays validated by calibrated benchmarks [3][4][7][8]. The emerging consensus is architectural, not ideological: CIM absorbs the dense, low-precision, stationary-weight kernels that dominate inference energy, while digital logic retains control flow, calibration, and residual precision. The memory wall will not be demolished in a single stroke; it is being dissolved kernel by kernel, in the physics of the devices themselves.
