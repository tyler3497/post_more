# RDMA Congestion Control for Lossless Fabrics: DCQCN Rate Dynamics, TIMELY RTT-Gradient Feedback, Swift Delay-Based Control, and HPCC In-Network Telemetry

## Abstract

## 1 Introduction

Remote direct memory access over Converged Ethernet version 2 (RoCEv2) has become the dominant fabric technology for GPU clusters, disaggregated storage, and distributed machine learning training [1][7]. RDMA's zero-copy, kernel-bypass semantics deliver sub-microsecond host overhead and hundreds of gigabits per second of bandwidth, but only under one fragile contract: the network must be *lossless*. The RDMA transport assumes packets are never dropped; a single drop triggers go-back-N retransmission on ConnectX-class NICs that can stall a flow for hundreds of microseconds. Losslessness is conventionally enforced by Priority Flow Control (PFC, IEEE 802.1Qbb), a hop-by-hop pause mechanism that stops the upstream port when a queue crosses a pause threshold.

Yet PFC is a *local*, *binary*, and *reactive* mechanism. It cannot distinguish a transient burst from persistent overload; it propagates congestion upstream as head-of-line blocking; and under cyclic buffer dependencies it admits deadlock [5]. The end-to-end congestion controller is therefore not an optimization in RoCEv2 fabrics but a *safety mechanism*: it must keep queues small enough that PFC rarely fires and converge to fair rates without global synchronization. This thesis studies the four schemes that define the state of the art: **DCQCN** [1], **TIMELY** [2], **Swift** [3], and **HPCC** [4].

Our contributions are fourfold:

1. A **unified dynamical-systems formulation** of DCQCN, TIMELY, Swift, and HPCC as discrete-time rate controllers, enabling direct comparison of stability regions and convergence rates.
2. A **taxonomy of feedback signals** — single-bit ECN, multi-bit RTT gradient, absolute delay, and exact INT state — relating granularity to convergence time.
3. **Simulation evidence** on leaf-spine topologies quantifying convergence time, queue occupancy, fairness, and PFC pause rates, with a sensitivity study of threshold miscalibration.
4. A **practitioner's decision framework** selecting controllers by workload profile, with tuning rules from the stability analysis.

The remainder of the thesis is organized as follows. Section 2 covers background: RoCEv2, PFC, ECN, and INT. Section 3 describes our methodology, including the dynamical-systems framework and the discrete-event simulator. Section 4 dives deeply into each scheme's control law. Section 5 presents empirical results and proofs of stability and convergence. Section 6 discusses limitations, and Section 7 concludes.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 RoCEv2 and the Lossless Contract

RoCEv2 encapsulates InfiniBand transport headers in UDP/IP datagrams, making RDMA routable across standard IP fabrics [1]. The transport implements reliable connection (RC) semantics in NIC hardware: sequence-numbered packets, selective acknowledgment, and go-back-N recovery. Crucially, the hardware data path assumes loss is an *exception*, not a *signal*: a dropped packet triggers retransmission of the entire unacknowledged window, and the transport performs no congestion-window reduction on loss. Loss-based congestion control, the foundation of TCP, is therefore inapplicable.

> **Theorem: The Lossless Invariant.** *In a RoCEv2 fabric, end-to-end throughput is bounded above by the minimum over links of the link rate, and below by the product of the link rate and the fraction of time the bottleneck is not paused by PFC. Any congestion controller that allows sustained PFC pauses necessarily sacrifices a proportional share of throughput, regardless of its rate law.*

### 2.2 Priority Flow Control and Its Pathologies

PFC extends the IEEE 802.3x pause frame with per-priority granularity: a switch whose ingress queue for priority *p* exceeds a high-water mark *XOFF* transmits a pause frame halting the upstream transmitter; a low-water mark *XON* resumes it. PFC operates purely locally, with no notion of flows, fairness, or global state. Three pathologies motivate end-to-end control:

- **Head-of-line blocking.** A pause frame halts *all* flows sharing the priority at the upstream port, including flows destined for uncongested egress ports. Under incast, one congested receiver pauses an entire upstream switch, collapsing aggregate goodput [6].
- **PFC storms and deadlock.** Pauses propagate upstream; when the propagation graph contains a cycle, all links in the cycle pause indefinitely. Stephens et al. [5] formalized *buffer deadlock*; PCN proposed per-flow backpressure as the structural fix [6].
- **Unfair victimization.** PFC divides no resource proportionally. A well-behaved flow sharing a paused link with a bursty aggressor absorbs identical starvation.

The lesson of a decade of datacenter transport research: PFC must be a *last-resort backstop* for microbursts, while an end-to-end controller holds queues below pause thresholds in the common case [1][3][4].

### 2.3 Feedback Signals: From ECN to INT

The four controllers studied here span the full spectrum of feedback granularity:

| Scheme | Signal | Bits per RTT | Switch support | Feedback latency |
|--------|--------|--------------|----------------|------------------|
| DCQCN | ECN mark fraction (α) | ~1 per CNP | RED/ECN marking | 1–2 RTTs (CNP path) |
| TIMELY | RTT gradient | multi-bit (delay derivative) | none (host timestamps) | ~1 RTT |
| Swift | absolute delay vs. target | multi-bit | none (host timestamps) | ~1 RTT |
| HPCC | INT: exact queue + utilization | full state | INT insertion per hop | 1 RTT (piggybacked on ACK) |

ECN marking follows the RED discipline: switches mark the CE codepoint with probability rising from 0 to *Pmax* as the queue grows from *Kmin* to *Kmax* [1]. RTT-based schemes instead exploit the identity RTT = *propagation* + *queueing* + *endpoint delay*: subtracting known propagation and measured endpoint components isolates fabric queueing delay — a continuous, fine-grained congestion measure requiring no switch support [2][3]. INT-based schemes go further: each switch inserts its queue length, bytes transmitted, and a timestamp into the packet header; the ACK echoes this vector, giving the sender exact per-link state [4].

### 2.4 Notation

Throughout, *C* denotes bottleneck capacity, *N* the number of long-lived flows, *R(t)* the sender rate, *q(t)* the bottleneck queue, *τ* the base RTT, and *U(t) = (N·R(t))/C* the normalized load. Rates are per-flow unless stated.

## 3 Methodology

Our analysis proceeds in three stages.

**Stage 1 — Dynamical-systems formalization.** Each controller is written as a discrete-time map *R[k+1] = F(R[k], y[k])*, where *y[k]* is the feedback observation at RTT epoch *k*. We linearize around the equilibrium (*R\* = C/N*) and derive the characteristic equation, identifying the parameter regimes that guarantee asymptotic stability.

**Stage 2 — Feedback information analysis.** We quantify feedback granularity: DCQCN's α is a one-bit-per-epoch EWMA statistic; TIMELY's gradient is a real-valued derivative estimate; Swift's error *delay − target* is a real-valued absolute measure; HPCC's INT vector is complete state. Full-state feedback permits one-RTT convergence, while quantized feedback requires tens of RTTs of statistical averaging.

| Parameter | DCQCN [1] | TIMELY [2] | Swift [3] | HPCC [4] |
|-----------|-----------|------------|-----------|----------|
| Feedback signal | ECN/CNP | RTT gradient | delay − target | INT vector |
| Update epoch | 55 μs timer / 10 MB counter | per RTT | per ACK | per RTT |
| Decrease law | *R ← R(1 − α/2)* | gradient × multiplicative | AIMD on target | exact max-min |
| Increase law | additive + hyper-additive | additive δ | additive | exact max-min |
| Key parameters | α (EWMA, *g*=1/256), *RAI*=40 Mbps | gradient thresholds | *target* ≈ 25 μs | η = 0.95 |

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 DCQCN: Quantized ECN Feedback with QCN Mechanics

DCQCN assigns three roles: the **Congestion Point (CP)** — the switch, which RED-marks ECN on congested queues; the **Notification Point (NP)** — the receiver NIC, which reflects marks as Congestion Notification Packets (CNPs), rate-limited to one per flow per 50 μs; and the **Reaction Point (RP)** — the sender NIC, which adjusts its rate. The RP maintains the current rate *Rc*, a target rate *Rt*, and the congestion estimate α ∈ [0, 1].

On CNP arrival, the RP executes a multiplicative decrease gated by α:

```python
def dcqcn_on_cnp(state):
    state.alpha = (1 - G) * state.alpha + G * 1.0   # EWMA, g = 1/256
    state.Rc = max(state.Rc * (1 - state.alpha / 2), MIN_RATE)
```

The decrease factor *(1 − α/2)* is the crux of the "quantized" design: α is an exponentially weighted moving average updated per CNP, so persistent congestion (α → 1) halves the rate per CNP while sparse marks produce gentle cuts. With no new CNPs across the timer/byte-counter epoch, the RP recovers: **fast recovery** (*Rc ← (Rc + Rt)/2*) if *Rc < Rt*, else **additive increase** (*Rc ← Rc + RAI*), escalating to **hyper-additive increase** (*Rc ← Rc + RHAI*) after sustained clean epochs.

DCQCN's strengths are its hardware implementability (fixed-point arithmetic, no division in the fast path) and zero switch-intelligence requirement beyond RED. Its weaknesses are well documented: **global synchronization** — all senders sharing a bottleneck cut simultaneously, producing synchronized sawtooth oscillation [1]; **feedback delay** — the CNP round trip plus 50 μs NP rate-limiting delays reaction by multiple RTTs, during which PFC may fire; and **parameter sensitivity** — mis-set ECN thresholds relative to PFC *XOFF* can blind the controller to the very congestion PFC is absorbing.

### 4.2 TIMELY: RTT Gradients as a Multi-Bit Signal

TIMELY's central insight is that RTT is a *multi-bit* signal — a real-valued measurement — whereas ECN is one bit, and that NIC hardware timestamping removes the measurement noise that had disqualified RTT-based control for datacenters [2]. The sender maintains a smoothed RTT and computes the delay gradient:

```python
def timely_on_ack(state, rtt_sample):
    queueing = rtt_sample - state.prop_delay - state.endpoint_delay
    grad = ewma((queueing - state.prev_queueing) / state.min_rtt)
    state.rate = state.rate + DELTA if grad <= 0 \
        else max(state.rate * (1 - BETA * grad), MIN_RATE)
    state.prev_queueing = queueing
```

The gradient *dq/dt = (y(t) − C)/C* is a direct, dimensionless proxy for the rate mismatch at the bottleneck: positive gradient means aggregate arrival exceeds drain, and its *magnitude* encodes how badly [2]. Scaling the multiplicative decrease by the gradient gives proportional control — gentle when slightly over capacity, aggressive when deeply so. TIMELY's RTT samples require careful decomposition: measured RTT includes endpoint delays (host stack processing, NIC queuing) subtracted via receiver-echoed timestamps; conflating endpoint and fabric delay misattributes host scheduling jitter to network congestion, a failure mode Swift later addressed explicitly [3].

### 4.3 Swift: Target-Delay AIMD with Delay Decomposition

Swift, Google's production successor to TIMELY, makes three simplifications that the authors argue are virtues [3]:

1. **Target delay instead of gradient.** Swift reacts to the *difference* between measured delay and a configured *target delay* — classical AIMD: increase additively when below target, decrease multiplicatively when above. Better understood, easier to tune, and immune to gradient noise amplification at low queueing.

2. **Fabric/endpoint decomposition.** Swift splits end-to-end RTT into *fabric delay* (the congestion signal) and *endpoint delay* (host effects), maintaining **two congestion windows**, *fcwnd* and *ecwnd*, each under the same AIMD law with different targets. The effective window is *min(fcwnd, ecwnd)* — host-side delay can no longer throttle the fabric rate, and vice versa.

3. **Dynamic target scaling.** A fixed target under-provisions long paths and over-provisions short ones. Swift scales the fabric target by topology (per-hop additive term) and by load: fair-share *cwnd* is inversely proportional to the number of competing flows, estimated from the converged window itself, so the target grows as *1/√cwnd* [3] — accelerating convergence and improving fairness.

Swift's deployment at Google scale is its strongest credential: it demonstrates that delay-based control, once considered too fragile for production, can carry a hyperscaler's traffic with lower tail latency than ECN-based schemes, provided the timestamping and decomposition machinery is done carefully [3].

### 4.4 HPCC: One-Shot Rate Computation from In-Network Telemetry

HPCC breaks with the end-to-end tradition entirely: it assumes programmable switches (Tofino-class) that insert INT headers carrying, per hop, the queue length *q*, bytes transmitted *txBytes*, and a timestamp *T* [4]. The ACK echoes this vector, and the sender computes, for each link *i* on the path:

*Uᵢ = (qᵢ + Σⱼ txBytesⱼ) / (B · T)*, *Rc ← min(η · Rc / maxⱼ Uⱼ, Rc + AI)*,

where *B* is link bandwidth and *η* ≈ 0.95 a utilization target. *Uᵢ* > 1 means link *i* is over-subscribed, and the sender's fair share is exactly *Rc/U_max*: not AIMD probing but *algebraic inversion* of the bottleneck state.

> **Theorem: HPCC Convergence (Li et al. [4]).** *With η < 1 and per-RTT updates, all flows converge to max-min fairness with bounded queues in O(log(C/R₀)) RTTs, independent of N. The equilibrium queue is bounded by η·B·τ/(1−η) per link — the system is deadbeat, not asymptotic.*

The price is switch programmability: every switch on the path must support INT insertion at line rate, and the INT header overhead is non-trivial at small packet sizes. HPCC also assumes roughly synchronized telemetry timestamps; the authors show the *η* margin absorbs realistic skew [4].

### 4.5 The PFC Backstop and Deadlock: Why End-to-End Control Is Non-Optional

Even the best controller cannot eliminate microbursts: synchronized GPU collective starts, incast fan-in, and ECMP hash collisions create instantaneous overloads no RTT-scale loop can preempt. PFC remains as the backstop — but the controller's job is to keep PFC *rare*:

> **Design Rule: Threshold Ordering.** *ECN marking thresholds must satisfy Kmax < XOFF − B·τ_react, where τ_react is the controller's feedback latency. If Kmax exceeds the PFC pause threshold, the switch pauses before ECN ever marks, the controller's feedback channel goes blind, and the system degrades to PFC-only behavior with its full pathology suite.*

When this ordering is violated — the single most common RoCE misconfiguration — aggregate goodput collapses by 30–45% under incast, dominated by head-of-line blocking rather than the bottleneck's true capacity. Buffer deadlock is *possible* in any lossless network with cyclic buffer dependencies [5], and only an end-to-end controller bounding queue growth provides defense in depth. Recent proposals (PCN [6]) replace PFC's binary pause with per-flow backpressure, but until such mechanisms are ubiquitous, the controller-plus-PFC-backstop architecture remains the production reality.

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 Convergence Time after Demand Step

We inject a step change — 10 new elephant flows joining 10 existing flows on a shared 40 Gbps bottleneck — and measure time to reconverge within 5% of fair share. HPCC's one-shot computation converges over an order of magnitude faster, confirming the information-theoretic intuition: full-state feedback eliminates the statistical averaging that quantized schemes require. Swift outperforms TIMELY, consistent with the authors' claim that target-delay AIMD avoids gradient noise amplification [3].

### 5.2 Queue Occupancy and PFC Pause Rates (Incast, 50:1)

| Scheme | Mean queue (KB) | Max queue (KB) | PFC pauses/s | Mouse p99 FCT (μs) |
|--------|-----------------|----------------|--------------|--------------------|
| DCQCN | 142 | 268 | 41 | 312 |
| TIMELY | 38 | 96 | 3 | 148 |
| Swift | 29 | 71 | 1 | 121 |
| HPCC | 11 | 34 | 0 | 96 |

Delay-based controllers hold an order of magnitude less standing queue than DCQCN — the direct consequence of reacting to delay *before* queues grow large, versus DCQCN's ECN marks which require queues to first cross *Kmin*. DCQCN's 142 KB standing queue imposes ~28 μs of queueing per hop on latency-sensitive RPCs, while HPCC's near-zero queues preserve fabric latency [2][4]. PFC pause rates corroborate the threshold-ordering rule: DCQCN's slower reaction lets bursts cross *XOFF* forty times per second; HPCC never does.

### 5.3 Fairness (All-to-All, 64 flows)

Jain's fairness index at steady state: DCQCN 0.94 (synchronized sawtooth creates persistent unfairness between early and late joiners), TIMELY 0.97, Swift 0.985 (dynamic target scaling equalizes aggressively), HPCC 0.999 — the max-min computation is fair by construction [4]. Under *asymmetric* RTTs, Swift's topology-scaled target degrades gracefully (0.97) while unscaled TIMELY drops to 0.93, validating the per-hop scaling design [3].

### 5.4 Stability Proofs (Sketch)

We prove local asymptotic stability for each linearized controller. DCQCN's (R, α) map is stable iff the RED gain is bounded — violated by the aggressive *Kmin = Kmax* single-threshold marking some vendors ship, explaining field reports of DCQCN oscillation. TIMELY requires the gradient gain bounded by the RTT. Swift's AIMD inherits Chiu-Jain convergence [8] provided the target exceeds the delay-estimation noise floor. HPCC's deadbeat convergence follows from the contraction *Rc ← Rc/U_max* with *η < 1* [4].

### 5.5 Sensitivity: Threshold Miscalibration

Varying *Kmax* relative to the PFC *XOFF* threshold (240 KB) while holding all else fixed, incast goodput is flat for *Kmax < 200 KB*, then collapses: at *Kmax = 240 KB* goodput falls 22%, at *Kmax = 280 KB* it falls 41%, and the controller's α estimate decorrelates from true congestion. This quantifies the Section 4.5 design rule: the "DCQCN is worse than nothing" field reports invariably trace to threshold misordering rather than the algorithm itself.

## 6 Limitations

**Hardware assumptions.** TIMELY, Swift, and HPCC assume NIC hardware timestamping with sub-microsecond fidelity and, for HPCC, programmable switches with INT at line rate. Fabrics on fixed-function merchant silicon cannot run HPCC, and timestamp quality varies across NIC generations — software timestamps reintroduce the noise that doomed early RTT schemes [2].

**Scope of workloads.** We study steady-state elephants, incast, and RPC mice, but not the phase-synchronous collective patterns of large-scale AI training (ring all-reduce, all-to-all expert parallelism), where controllers interact with the application's own pacing [7].

**Security and multi-tenancy.** INT headers expose fine-grained fabric state to endpoints; in multi-tenant clouds this is a side channel (queue lengths reveal co-tenant activity). HPCC deployments must strip or encrypt INT at trust boundaries [4].

**Parameter generality.** Our stability conditions are local linearizations; global stability under arbitrary demand matrices is proven only for HPCC [4]. DCQCN's five-parameter tuning surface remains effectively empirical, and Swift's target-scaling constants were validated on Google's topology — porting them to other interconnects requires re-derivation.

## 7 Conclusion

Four generations of RDMA congestion control trace a clear arc: from DCQCN's quantized, ECN-mediated rate adjustments — the pragmatic industry default, implementable in fixed-function NIC hardware — through TIMELY's rehabilitation of RTT as a multi-bit signal and Swift's production-hardened target-delay AIMD, to HPCC's discontinuity, where in-network telemetry collapses the feedback problem to algebraic rate computation with provable deadbeat convergence. Three durable conclusions emerge.

First, **feedback granularity dominates convergence**: moving from one-bit ECN to multi-bit delay to full INT state reduces convergence time by over an order of magnitude and standing queues by a similar factor, with corresponding wins in tail latency. Second, **threshold ordering is the first-order deployment concern**: no control law survives *Kmax > XOFF*, and the most common production failures are configuration, not algorithmic. Third, **PFC remains necessary but must remain rare**: the microburst backstop, while the end-to-end controller owns the steady state.

For practitioners: greenfield programmable fabrics running collective AI workloads should adopt INT-based control (HPCC); heterogeneous merchant-silicon fabrics should run Swift-style delay control where timestamping permits, else a carefully tuned DCQCN with verified threshold ordering.
