# Differential Dataflow: Timely Dataflow, Iterative Computation, and the Engineering of Incremental View Maintenance

## Abstract

Incremental computation promises a simple bargain: when inputs change by a small delta, outputs should be recomputed with cost proportional to that delta rather than to the entire dataset. Classical techniques honor this bargain only for acyclic pipelines; they break down in the presence of iteration — the fixed-point loops underlying graph analytics, recursive queries, and iterative machine learning. This thesis presents differential dataflow, a model that unifies incremental and iterative processing by generalizing logical time from a total to a partial order. We develop its formal core — collections as integer-weighted multisets, changes as signed differences, accumulation semantics that make iteration and incrementality composable — and show how timely dataflow's progress-tracking protocol makes the model executable at scale. We survey its engineering descendants — Naiad, the Rust differential-dataflow crate, DDlog, Materialize, DBSP, and Feldera — and analyze asymptotic maintenance costs of relational operators, the consolidation bottleneck, and memory/state tradeoffs bounding practical deployments. Literature evidence shows interactive latencies on continuously changing graphs and SQL views. We conclude with open problems in non-monotone recursion and adaptive consolidation.

## 1 Introduction

Modern data systems face a structural tension. Batch processors deliver high throughput but stale answers; stream processors deliver fresh answers but historically only for non-iterative computations; and view maintenance engines handle incremental input yet cannot express iteration [1]. Applications needing all three — batch throughput, streaming latency, and fixed-point iteration — have been forced onto multiple platforms, paying in duplicated infrastructure [2].

Differential dataflow resolves this with a single move: it replaces the *total order* on logical time with a *partial order*. A streaming system distinguishes times only as "before" and "after"; differential dataflow timestamps each update with a partially ordered coordinate — e.g. *(epoch, iteration)* — so two updates can be *incomparable*: neither precedes the other. Incomparable updates are independent and may be processed concurrently; comparable updates compose exactly as in classical incremental computation. Iteration becomes nothing more exotic than advancing along one timestamp coordinate while holding the other fixed [1].

This thesis develops the model from first principles, explains the timely dataflow execution substrate that makes it practical, traces its lineage through DBSP's algebraic formalization, and examines the systems carrying it into production: a unified formal account, a proof sketch of correctness, an asymptotic cost analysis of incremental relational operators, and an honest assessment of where the model strains — consolidation costs, non-monotone recursion, and state growth.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Incremental view maintenance before differential computation

Incremental view maintenance (IVM) is among the oldest problems in database theory: given a query *Q* over a database *D* and a small change *ΔD*, compute *Q(D + ΔD)* without recomputing from scratch [4]. Classical results characterize *self-maintainable* queries and derive delta rules for the relational algebra, e.g.:

$$\Delta(R \bowtie S) = (\Delta R \bowtie S) \cup (R \bowtie \Delta S) \cup (\Delta R \bowtie \Delta S)$$

That total order is the root of the iteration problem: a fixed-point computation needs many "times" *within* a single version — the successive iterates of the loop — and classical IVM has no coordinate in which to place them. The standard escapes are unsatisfying: re-run the whole loop per version, restrict to stratified programs with bespoke incremental algorithms, or forbid iteration entirely [1].

### 2.2 Naiad and timely dataflow

Naiad, built at Microsoft Research, introduced the execution model differential dataflow rests on: **timely dataflow** [2][3]. A computation is a directed (possibly cyclic) graph of operators; every message carries a timestamp from a partially ordered set, and operators are notified — via a lightweight protocol tracking *pointstamps* (location, timestamp) — when no further messages at or below a given timestamp can arrive. This notification is the *frontier*: the minimal outstanding timestamps, advancing monotonically as work completes [8].

The crucial property, formalized and mechanically checked in later work:

> **Theorem (Progress safety).** Once a pointstamp *(e, t)* leaves the system's outstanding set, no message on edge *e* will ever again bear a timestamp *t′ ≤ t*. [8]

Timely dataflow therefore gives programmers cyclic graphs with a sound, decentralized notion of "logical time has advanced past *t*," without global barriers. Differential dataflow is the programming model layered on top: incremental, iterative computations as ordinary dataflow operators over partially ordered timestamps [1].

### 2.3 The intellectual lineage

The ideas did not stop at the 2013 papers. Frank McSherry reimplemented the model as the open-source Rust crates `timely-dataflow` and `differential-dataflow`, which became the substrate for **DDlog**, a bottom-up incremental Datalog engine compiling declarative rules into differential dataflow programs [6]; for **Materialize**, a PostgreSQL-compatible streaming database recasting SQL views as incrementally maintained dataflows [7]; and, in refined algebraic form, for **DBSP**, which recasts the theory in the language of digital signal processing — streams, differentiation, and integration over abelian groups called *Z-sets* — underpinning the Feldera streaming SQL engine [4][5]. DBSP's authors describe their work as beginning "as an attempt to provide a simpler formalization of DD" [4].

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 The differential representation

Classical incremental computation maintains a collection *Cₜ* at version *t* and a delta *δCₜ = Cₜ ∖ Cₜ₋₁*. Differential dataflow generalizes the delta into a *signed* difference over a *partially ordered* time domain, modeling a collection as a function from records to integer multiplicities:

```haskell
-- A collection: each record maps to a signed multiplicity.
-- Positive = insertions, negative = retractions.
type Collection r = r -> Integer
```

For each time *t* in a partially ordered set *(T, ≤)*, the input provides a difference *δCₜ : D → ℤ*, and the collection *at* time *t* is the accumulation over its down-set:

$$C_t \;=\; \sum_{s \le t} \delta C_s$$

where the sum is pointwise over records. Records with accumulated multiplicity zero are logically absent. This single definition is the whole trick: iteration and incrementality become the same mechanism — an *epoch* (new input data) and an *iteration* (one more loop pass) are both just coordinates of *t* [1].

Time is typically a *product order*: for one epoch and one nested loop, *t = (e, i)* with *(e, i) ≤ (e′, i′)* iff *e ≤ e′* and *i ≤ i′*. The pairs *(1, 5)* and *(2, 0)* are *incomparable* — logically independent, computable concurrently. Comparability is exactly the dependency relation, so the partial order exposes all available parallelism while preserving determinism [1][2].

### 3.2 Differential operators

Operators in differential dataflow consume and produce *differences*, never whole collections. Because accumulation is linear, any linear operator *F* satisfies the defining property of incremental computation for free:

$$F\!\left(\sum_{s \le t} \delta C_s\right) \;=\; \sum_{s \le t} F(\delta C_s)$$

Map, filter, flat-map, union, and projection are linear: applying them to the difference *is* the incremental rule, with cost proportional to the change [1]. Non-linear operators — join, aggregation, iteration itself — need accumulated state, maintained as indexed arrangements of consolidated differences. For differences *δRₜ, δSₜ*, the output difference must pair new records against the accumulated history of the other side:

$$\delta(R \bowtie S)_t \;=\; (\delta R_t \bowtie \delta S_t) \;\cup\; (\delta R_t \bowtie S_{<t}) \;\cup\; (R_{<t} \bowtie \delta S_t)$$

where *R_{<t} = Σ_{s<t} δRₛ* — the classical delta rule, now indexed by a partial order, so iteration *within* an epoch and new *epochs* are maintained by identical machinery [1].

Iteration is expressed with an `iterate` operator that feeds an operator's output back to its input while advancing the iteration coordinate. If epoch *e+1* changes a few graph edges, only the differences propagate through the fixed-point iteration, and unchanged regions contribute nothing [1]. The original CIDR paper demonstrates this on incrementally maintained strongly connected components over changing graphs — a computation intractable for prior incremental models [1].

### 3.3 Consolidation and state

Signed multiplicities create a bookkeeping obligation: an inserted-then-retracted record yields *+1* and *−1* differences that must cancel. *Consolidation* merges the difference stream, summing multiplicities for equal records and discarding zeros, so state stays proportional to the *logical* collection. Consolidation is the dominant systems cost — essentially a parallel sort-and-reduce over the difference log — and its scheduling determines memory footprint and tail latency in every implementation [1][7].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Partially ordered time and progress tracking

Timely dataflow's contribution is making partially ordered time *executable*. Each operator instance tracks, for each input edge, the frontier of timestamps it may still receive — the minimal elements of the outstanding pointstamp set. Operators exchange frontiers through a distributed protocol; the global frontier advances only when every participant agrees no earlier work remains. Because the frontier is an *antichain* of mutually incomparable times, workers advance along incomparable coordinates independently — which is precisely what lets iteration-5-of-epoch-1 proceed concurrently with iteration-0-of-epoch-2 [2][8].

A minimal TLA⁺ sketch of the safety invariant, in the style of the mechanically checked formalization [8]:

```tla
------------------------------ MODULE Progress ------------------------------
EXTENDS Naturals, FiniteSets
CONSTANTS Edge, Time
VARIABLES outstanding          \* (edge, time) pointstamps with pending work

\* Safety: (e,t) retired => no later message on e with time <= t.
Safety == \A e \in Edge, t \in Time :
            (<<e,t>> \notin outstanding)
                => \A m \in Messages(e) : ~(time(m) \leq t)
===============================================================================
```

The invariant holds because operators only emit messages at or beyond observed frontiers, and retire a pointstamp only when their inputs' frontiers have passed it [8].

### 4.2 The differential operators: consolidation, join, and iteration

1. **Consolidate.** Merges *(record, time, multiplicity)* triples, summing multiplicities of identical pairs and dropping zeros. Semantically a no-op; operationally, the garbage collector of the difference stream. Implementations share consolidated, pre-indexed *arrangements* across operators [1][7].

2. **Join.** Maintains the delta rule of §3.2 against arrangements: the *δR ⋈ S_{<t}* terms probe indexed history rather than rescanning it, so cost is proportional to change-times-state. Multi-way joins chain binary joins under the same rule [7].

3. **Iterate.** Scopes a sub-dataflow whose timestamps gain an extra coordinate. Differences circulate until a round produces none — the fixed point, reached *for every epoch simultaneously* [1].

The following Rust sketch (simplified from the canonical examples in the `differential-dataflow` crate) computes, per graph node, the minimum reachable label — label propagation to a fixed point, expressed as differences:

```rust
use differential_dataflow::input::Input;
use differential_dataflow::operators::{Iterate, Join, Map};

timely::execute_from_args(std::env::args(), |worker| {
    worker.dataflow(|scope| {
        let (mut input, edges) = scope.new_collection::<(u32, u32), isize>();

        // iterate to a fixed point: join current labels against
        // edges each round, keeping the per-node minimum.
        let labels = edges.iterate(|inner| {
            let edges = edges.enter(&inner.scope());
            inner
                .join_map(&edges, |_node, label, dst| (*dst, *label))
                .concat(&inner)
                .reduce(|_, vals, out| {
                    let min = vals.iter().map(|(l, _)| *l).min().unwrap();
                    out.push((min, 1)); // consolidated minimum, weight +1
                })
        });

        labels.inspect(|x| println!("label update: {:?}", x));
        input
    })
});
```

Feeding edge insertions and retractions into `input` advances only affected labels through the loop; the frontier protocol retires each epoch's iteration coordinate when its differences are exhausted [1].

### 4.3 Z-sets and the DBSP formalization

DBSP re-derives differential dataflow from the mathematics of digital signal processing, simplifying its foundations. The central object is the **Z-set**: a function from tuples to integers, forming an abelian group under pointwise addition. Sets, multisets, and deltas are all Z-sets; insertion and deletion are the same operation with opposite signs [4][5].

A **stream** is an infinite sequence of Z-sets indexed by discrete time, each element the delta at that step. Four operators suffice:

| Operator | Action on stream *s* | Role |
|---|---|---|
| Lift *↑Q* | apply *Q* pointwise | embed queries |
| Delay *z⁻¹* | shift stream by one step | reference past state |
| Differentiation *𝒟* | *s[t] − s[t−1]* | extract changes |
| Integration *ℐ* | cumulative sum | rebuild state |

*𝒟* and *ℐ* are inverses. The incremental version of *any* query *Q* is a single formula:

$$Q^{\Delta} \;=\; \mathcal{D} \;\circ\; \uparrow\!Q \;\circ\; \mathcal{I}$$

— integrate deltas into state, apply the query, differentiate. Incrementalization *composes*: *(Q₁ ∘ Q₂)^Δ = Q₁^Δ ∘ Q₂^Δ* [4]. Linear operators satisfy *Q^Δ = ↑Q* — the delta passes through untouched. The bilinear join expands to the familiar three-term rule:

$$\Delta(R \bowtie S) \;=\; \Delta R \bowtie \Delta S \;\oplus\; z^{-1}(\mathcal{I}(R)) \bowtie \Delta S \;\oplus\; \Delta R \bowtie z^{-1}(\mathcal{I}(S))$$

Non-linear operators such as `DISTINCT` require the integrated state and are the most expensive — a precise algebraic explanation of an observed cost [4]. Recursion is modeled with nested streams indexed by a second time dimension: DBSP's answer to differential dataflow's product-order timestamps [4][5].

### 4.4 From dataflow to SQL: Materialize, DDlog, and Feldera

The model's most demanding application is SQL view maintenance. **Materialize** compiles SQL — joins, subqueries, aggregations — into differential dataflow plans executed by the Timely/Differential crates. Unlike classical optimizers, its planner must choose plans efficient *both* to compute initially and to maintain incrementally, since dataflows cannot re-plan dynamically [7]. The payoff is a streaming database answering queries over materialized views at millisecond latency while doing work proportional to change volume rather than data size [7].

**DDlog** attacks from the logic-programming side: a typed Datalog dialect whose compiler synthesizes differential dataflow programs, so the programmer writes declarative rules and receives incremental maintenance — insertions *and* retractions — without hand-writing delta logic, built on the Rust differential-dataflow library [6]. Studies of differential Datalog engines confirm the central promise: input changes trigger only minimal recomputation, and uniform signed-multiplicity treatment of additions and deletions yields temporal consistency ad-hoc schemes struggle to match [9].

**Feldera** (and its open DBSP core) completes the arc: the DBSP algebra of §4.3 executing as a streaming SQL engine, where every operator's incremental form derives from the *Q^Δ* formula rather than bespoke delta rules [4]. One mechanism — signed differences over partially ordered time — scales from research prototype (Naiad) to production streaming SQL.

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 Correctness: the accumulation theorem

The model's correctness rests on one theorem: accumulating differences over the down-set of *t* always yields the value the computation *would* have produced had it been run from scratch at *t*.

> **Theorem (Differential correctness).** Let *P* be a dataflow program whose operators are linear or equipped with the differential delta rules of §3.2, over a partially ordered time domain *(T, ≤)*. For every time *t*, the accumulated output *Oₜ = Σ_{s≤t} δOₛ* equals the result of evaluating *P* on the accumulated inputs *Iₜ = Σ_{s≤t} δIₛ*. [1]

> **Proof sketch.** By induction over the partial order. *Base:* at minimal times, differences coincide with the collections, so the claim is direct evaluation. *Inductive step:* assume the claim for all *s < t*. Linear operators commute with accumulation by linearity. For join, summing the three-term expansion over all *s ≤ t* telescopes — each record pair contributes exactly when both records' times precede *t*, i.e. precisely the pairs in *Rₜ ⋈ Sₜ*. For `iterate`, differences at iteration *i* depend only on coordinates *< i*, so the hypothesis applies round by round; the loop stabilizes exactly at the fixed point. Consolidation only cancels opposite multiplicities and is semantically invisible. ∎

A companion systems guarantee comes from timely dataflow's progress protocol: an operator learns a frontier has passed *t* only when no message with timestamp *≤ t* can still arrive — established by pointstamp accounting and mechanically verified [8]. Together: differential programs are *correct* (accumulation reconstructs the right answer) and *live* (frontiers advance, loops terminate).

### 5.2 Asymptotic maintenance costs

The following table summarizes incremental maintenance cost per output delta under an input change |Δ| against state |S|, assuming indexed arrangements [1][4][7].

| Operator | Incremental cost | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Filter, project, map | *O(\|Δ\|)* | Linear; delta passes through untouched |
| Union | *O(\|Δ\|)* | Pointwise addition of Z-sets |
| Equi-join | *O(\|ΔR\|·\|S\| + \|R\|·\|ΔS\|)* | Three-term rule against indexed history |
| Aggregation (SUM, COUNT) | *O(\|Δ\|)* | Linear in weights |
| GROUP BY | *O(\|Δ\|)* | Indexed Z-sets redistribute by key [4] |
| DISTINCT | *O(\|Δ\| + consolidation)* | Requires integrated state; most expensive relational op |
| MIN / MAX | *O(\|state of group\|)* worst case | Non-monotone; retraction may force rescan |
| Recursive (monotone) | *O(changed derivations)* | Differences propagate only where they change the fixpoint [1] |

The table explains the model's practical profile: common operators are linear and essentially free to maintain; joins cost change-times-state; the expensive cases (DISTINCT, MIN/MAX, non-monotone recursion) are exactly those whose delta rules need accumulated history. The original paper demonstrated the payoff on incrementally maintained strongly connected components over changing inputs — computations with no efficient prior incremental formulation [1]. Naiad's evaluation showed the timely substrate outperforming specialized systems in their own domains [2]. In production, Materialize's differential engine sustains millisecond-level view freshness on complex joins while doing work proportional to change volume [7], and differential Datalog studies confirm updates trigger only minimal recomputation [6][9].

```python
from collections import Counter

def delta_join(delta_r, hist_r, delta_s, hist_s):
    """One maintenance step of R JOIN S on key. Signed weights."""
    out = Counter()
    for (k, r), wr in delta_r.items():          # new-new + new-old
        for (k2, s), ws in (list(delta_s.items()) + list(hist_s.items())):
            if k == k2:
                out[(k, r, s)] += wr * ws
    for (k, r), wr in hist_r.items():           # old-new
        for (k2, s), ws in delta_s.items():
            if k == k2:
                out[(k, r, s)] += wr * ws
    return +out  # drop zero-weight entries (consolidation)
```

### 5.3 What the evidence establishes — and what it does not

Three claims are well supported: (i) partially ordered time makes iteration and incrementality composable without bespoke delta logic [1][2]; (ii) the asymptotic maintenance costs above are realized by implementations [1][4][7]; (iii) production systems achieve interactive latencies on maintained SQL views [7]. What the literature does *not* establish is a universal constant-factor advantage: consolidation scheduling, arrangement design, and timestamp-domain choice dominate real performance. The model guarantees *asymptotic* proportionality to change; engineering determines the constants.

## 6 Limitations

**Consolidation is the tax on the model.** Every difference must eventually be consolidated — a global sort-and-reduce over the difference log. Eager consolidation burns CPU on churn; lazy consolidation bloats state and poisons join probes. All production implementations invest heavily in scheduling heuristics here, and it remains the least principled part of the stack [1][7].

**Non-monotone recursion is only partially tamed.** MIN/MAX under deletion and negation in recursive Datalog can force work proportional to state rather than change — the *O(|state|)* table row is fundamental, not incidental [4][6].

**Timestamps are a programming burden.** Product orders like *(epoch, iteration, sub-iteration)* are powerful but unintuitive, and mis-designed timestamp domains cause lost updates or runaway recomputation. DBSP's stream algebra is in part a response to this ergonomic problem [4][5].

**State grows with history, not with the present.** Arrangements retain the history needed by join delta rules; without explicit compaction, high-churn deployments grow without bound [7].

**Plans are static.** Dataflow plans cannot re-plan dynamically; the optimizer must choose, ahead of time, a plan efficient both initially and incrementally — strictly harder than classical query planning [7].

## 7 Conclusion

Differential dataflow's enduring idea is disarmingly small: let logical time be partially ordered, represent change as signed differences, and define collections as the accumulation of their differences. From that definition flow the composition of iteration and incrementality, the relational delta rules, the DBSP formula *Q^Δ = 𝒟 ∘ ↑Q ∘ ℐ*, and a family of systems — Naiad, the Rust crates, DDlog, Materialize, Feldera — carrying the idea from prototype to production SQL [1][2][4][6][7].

The open problems are engineering problems with deep theoretical edges. *Adaptive consolidation* — scheduling compaction from observed workload statistics rather than heuristics — would remove the model's largest unprincipled cost. *Finer timestamp disciplines* preserving the product order's expressiveness while approaching DBSP's algebraic simplicity would lower the barrier to correct differential programs. *Principled state expiration* — deciding, with guarantees, which history a long-lived dataflow may forget — remains the central capacity question for deployments that never stop. The bargain — work proportional to change — is asymptotically kept; making the constants small is the work ahead.
