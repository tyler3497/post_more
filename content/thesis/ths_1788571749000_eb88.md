# Neuromorphic Computing with Spiking Neural Networks: LIF Dynamics, Surrogate Gradients, and the Loihi 2 Event-Driven Architecture

## Abstract

Neuromorphic computing replaces dense, synchronous matrix arithmetic with sparse, asynchronous, event-driven computation inspired by the brain. Spiking neural networks (SNNs) communicate through discrete spikes, letting hardware such as Intel's Loihi 2 and IBM's TrueNorth spend energy proportional to activity rather than model size. This thesis develops the full neuromorphic stack: the leaky integrate-and-fire (LIF) neuron and its discrete-time silicon formulation, spike-timing-dependent plasticity (STDP) as the canonical local learning rule, and surrogate gradient methods that bring gradient-based optimization to SNNs. We compare temporal and rate coding, conversion of trained artificial neural networks to SNNs, and the architectural trade-offs of Loihi 2's programmable neurocores versus TrueNorth's tiled neurosynaptic cores. Published benchmarks show recurrent SNN workloads achieving up to 1000–10000× lower energy on Loihi than on conventional processors. Event-driven neuromorphic hardware is a viable complement to von Neumann accelerators for low-power real-time inference, while training scalability and software maturity remain the field's principal open problems.

## 1. Introduction

The von Neumann bottleneck has become the defining constraint of modern machine learning hardware. In a conventional accelerator, every layer of a neural network forces weights and activations to shuttle between memory and compute units, consuming the dominant share of system energy. The human brain, by contrast, performs roughly $10^{15}$ synaptic operations per second on a power budget of about 20 watts, precisely because memory and computation are co-located and communication is sparse and event-driven [5].

Neuromorphic engineering, a term introduced by Carver Mead in the 1980s, seeks to instantiate these principles in silicon. Its central computational primitive is the **spiking neural network**, in which neurons communicate through stereotyped electrical pulses—spikes—rather than continuous-valued activations [3]. Computation occurs only when and where spikes arrive, so that energy consumption scales with neural activity rather than with the nominal size of the network.

Two decades of neuromorphic hardware research have produced two landmark digital processors that anchor this thesis. IBM's **TrueNorth** demonstrated a non-von Neumann architecture with one million programmable spiking neurons and 256 million synapses operating at 63 mW on real-time video classification [5]. Intel's **Loihi** line advanced the field by adding on-chip learning to the neuromorphic substrate, and **Loihi 2** (fabricated on the Intel 4 process) introduced graded spike messages and a fully programmable neurocore supporting an expanded class of neuron models [1][2][7].

Progress in algorithms has paralleled progress in silicon. **Spike-timing-dependent plasticity** provided the first biologically grounded local learning rule; **surrogate gradient** methods subsequently showed how the non-differentiable spike mechanism could be circumvented to apply backpropagation-style optimization directly to SNNs [3][4]. Together with **ANN-to-SNN conversion**, which ports trained conventional networks into the spiking domain, these methods have closed much of the accuracy gap on benchmark tasks.

This thesis is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews the historical and biological background. Section 3 formalizes the discrete-time LIF model, STDP, surrogate gradients, and conversion. Section 4 deep-dives into neuron dynamics, coding schemes, training methods, and the Loihi 2 and TrueNorth architectures. Section 5 presents empirical evidence and a proof sketch of ANN-to-SNN convergence. Section 6 enumerates limitations, and Section 7 concludes.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 From McCulloch–Pitts to Spikes

Artificial neurons of the McCulloch–Pitts (1943) and perceptron (1958) type emit graded real-valued outputs on every evaluation. Biologically, however, neurons communicate through **action potentials**—brief, all-or-nothing voltage excursions of fixed waveform. The only information a spike carries is its *time of arrival* (and, in generalized hardware such as Loihi 2, an optional graded magnitude [1]). This observation motivated Maass (1997) to formalize SNNs as the **third generation** of neural network models, after McCulloch–Pitts/threshold gates and rate-coded sigmoidal networks, and to prove that spiking networks can approximate any continuous function with fewer neurons than sigmoidal counterparts.

### 2.2 The Energy Argument

In a dense ANN layer, computing $y = f(Wx)$ requires touching every weight of $W$ per inference pass. In an SNN, synapse $w_{ij}$ contributes a postsynaptic potential only when presynaptic neuron $j$ spikes. With mean firing rates of a few hertz and millisecond-scale simulation, typical SNNs exhibit **activity sparsity** of 1–10%, so most synaptic operations are *skipped entirely* on event-driven hardware. TrueNorth's published 63 mW consumption on 400×240 video at 30 fps—reported as roughly 150,000× less energy than a general-purpose microprocessor on the same workload—demonstrates the magnitude of the prize [6].

### 2.3 Neuromorphic Hardware Generations

The field distinguishes several design philosophies:

- **Digital synchronous** (TrueNorth, Loihi, Loihi 2): deterministic, reproducible, design-flow friendly.
- **Mixed-signal analog/digital** (BrainScaleS, DYNAP): continuous-time dynamics, compact, but sensitive to mismatch.
- **Massively parallel many-core** (SpiNNaker): programmable general-purpose cores simulating neurons in software.

This thesis concentrates on the digital synchronous line, because it is the branch most tightly coupled to today's deep learning practice and the one for which training algorithms are most mature [8].

### 2.4 The Learning Problem in SNNs

Three challenges have historically blocked SNN training [3]:

1. **Non-differentiability**: the spike generation mechanism is a Heaviside step function whose derivative is zero almost everywhere.
2. **Credit assignment through time**: SNNs are recurrent dynamical systems; gradients must flow across simulation timesteps (backpropagation through time, BPTT).
3. **The dead-neuron problem**: neurons that never spike receive no gradient under naive schemes.

The algorithms reviewed in Sections 3–4 address each of these in turn.

## 3. Methodology

### 3.1 Discrete-Time LIF Neuron Model

The leaky integrate-and-fire neuron is the workhorse of neuromorphic hardware because its dynamics map cleanly onto digital counters and accumulators. Loihi implements the discrete LIF update [7]:

$$
a_i[t] = \sum_j w_{ij}\, s_j[t-1]
$$

$$
u_i[t] = \lambda_u\, u_i[t-1] + a_i[t]
$$

$$
v_i[t] = \lambda_v\, v_i[t-1] + u_i[t]
$$

where $a_i[t]$ is the accumulated synaptic activation, $u_i$ and $v_i$ are the neuron's current and membrane voltage, and $\lambda_u, \lambda_v \in (0,1)$ are decay factors. Whenever $v_i[t]$ exceeds threshold $\vartheta$, a spike is emitted ($s_i[t]=1$) and the voltage is reset to zero; otherwise $s_i[t]=0$. All states and parameters use fixed precision [7]. The continuous-time analogue is the familiar differential equation

$$
\tau_m \frac{dv}{dt} = -(v - v_{\text{rest}}) + R\,I(t), \qquad s(t) = \Theta(v(t) - \vartheta),
$$

with $\Theta$ the Heaviside step function and an absolute refractory period following each spike.

A minimal simulation captures the essential dynamics:

```python
import numpy as np

def simulate_lif(I, tau_m=20.0, dt=1.0, v_rest=0.0, v_reset=0.0, theta=1.0):
    """Leaky integrate-and-fire with Euler integration and reset-by-subtraction."""
    n = len(I)
    v = np.zeros(n)
    spikes = np.zeros(n, dtype=bool)
    decay = np.exp(-dt / tau_m)
    for t in range(1, n):
        dv = (decay * (v[t-1] - v_rest) + (1 - decay) * I[t-1])
        v[t] = v_rest + dv
        if v[t] >= theta:
            spikes[t] = True
            v[t] = v_reset  # hard reset
    return v, spikes

# Step current -> regular spiking with adaptation-free interspike intervals
I = np.zeros(200); I[20:] = 0.12
v, s = simulate_lif(I)
print("spike count:", s.sum(), "| rate: %.1f Hz (1 ms steps)" % (s.sum() / 0.2))
```

### 3.2 Spike-Timing-Dependent Plasticity

STDP is a *local* learning rule: the change in synaptic weight depends only on the relative timing of pre- and postsynaptic spikes. For a pre-post pair with time difference $\Delta t = t_{\text{post}} - t_{\text{pre}}$:

$$
\Delta w = \begin{cases}
A_+ \, e^{-\Delta t/\tau_+} & \Delta t > 0 \quad \text{(potentiation)}\\[4pt]
-A_- \, e^{\Delta t/\tau_-} & \Delta t < 0 \quad \text{(depression)}
\end{cases}
$$

Pre-before-post ordering strengthens the synapse (causal, Hebbian); post-before-pre weakens it. Triplet-based variants and reward-modulated STDP extend the rule to capture higher-order statistics and reinforcement signals [3].

> **Theorem (Competitive Hebbian learning via STDP):** Under Poisson input statistics and the standard asymmetric STDP window, a neuron with multiple plastic synapses converges to a state in which its weight vector aligns with the principal component of the correlated input subspace, implementing unsupervised feature extraction without global supervision.

### 3.3 Surrogate Gradients

The spike function $s = \Theta(v - \vartheta)$ has derivative $\delta(v-\vartheta)$, zero almost everywhere, which blocks backpropagation. The **surrogate gradient** trick keeps $\Theta$ in the *forward* pass but replaces it with a smooth proxy $\sigma$ in the *backward* pass [3][4]:

$$
\frac{\partial s}{\partial v} \approx \sigma'(v - \vartheta), \qquad
\sigma'(x) = \frac{1}{(1 + \beta |x|)^2} \quad \text{(fast sigmoid)},
$$

or alternatively a boxcar, triangular, or exponential surrogate. Training then proceeds by BPTT through the unrolled network, treating the SNN as a special case of a recurrent neural network [4]. This single idea underpins essentially all modern supervised SNN training, achieving near-ANN accuracy on MNIST, CIFAR-10, and event-camera benchmarks.

### 3.4 ANN-to-SNN Conversion

An alternative methodology trains a conventional ANN and then *converts* it: ReLU activations $a = \max(0, Wx)$ are interpreted as firing *rates* of integrate-and-fire neurons. Conversion requires weight normalization (to bound firing rates below saturation), bias handling, and simulation over $T$ timesteps; as $T \to \infty$ the time-averaged spike rate converges to the ANN activation [3]. Conversion yields high accuracy but inherits the latency of rate coding—typically hundreds of timesteps—whereas surrogate-gradient training produces networks that operate in tens of timesteps.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Membrane Dynamics: Subthreshold Integration, Threshold, and Reset

The LIF model strips the Hodgkin–Huxley formalism to its computational essentials. Subthreshold, the membrane potential performs a leaky integration of synaptic current: recent inputs matter more than distant ones, with exponential forgetting governed by $\tau_m$. This leak is not a bug but a feature—it gives the neuron a finite *memory horizon* and makes the network's response invariant to the DC offset of the input.

At threshold, two reset disciplines dominate hardware implementations:

| Reset scheme | Update rule | Hardware cost | Effect on coding |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hard reset | $v \leftarrow v_{\text{reset}}$ | Minimal (clear register) | Discards suprathreshold charge; simple |
| Reset-by-subtraction | $v \leftarrow v - \vartheta$ | One subtract | Preserves residual; exact rate coding |
| Adaptive threshold | $\vartheta \leftarrow \vartheta + \Delta$ | Extra state per neuron | Implements refractoriness, gain control |

Loihi 2's microcode-programmable neurocore can express all three, plus resonate-and-fire and Hopf-resonator variants—programmability that Davies' team added precisely because the single fixed LIF model of Loihi 1 proved insufficient for emerging workloads [1][7].

The refractory period, implemented as a transient threshold elevation or a forced hold-off on spiking, bounds the maximum firing rate and prevents pathological bursting. In discrete-time hardware it is typically a countdown counter per neuron, set on spike and decremented each timestep.

### 4.2 Temporal Coding versus Rate Coding

How information is encoded in spike trains is the central design decision of any SNN system.

**Rate coding** represents a scalar by the *frequency* of spikes over a window. It is robust to noise and jitter, maps directly onto ANN activations (hence conversion), but is energetically expensive: transmitting one value may require dozens of spikes, and latency grows with the required precision.

**Temporal coding** represents information in *precise spike times*: time-to-first-spike, rank-order coding, or latency codes. A single spike can carry $\log_2 T$ bits of information about a value quantized into $T$ bins. This is dramatically more efficient—one spike per neuron per inference—but demands precise timing infrastructure and learning rules sensitive to spike order (STDP is naturally a temporal-coding rule).

*Population coding* and *phase coding* occupy intermediate positions. Loihi 2's graded spike messages—spikes carrying both timing and magnitude—can be understood as a hardware-level compromise: magnitude restores some of the precision that pure binary spikes sacrifice, at small energy cost [1].

> **Theorem (Rate-coding fidelity bound):** For an integrate-and-fire neuron with reset-by-subtraction driven by constant current $I$, the time-averaged firing rate satisfies $\bar{r} = I / \vartheta$ exactly in the absence of leak, so that ANN-to-SNN conversion with $T$ timesteps approximates each activation with quantization error bounded by $\vartheta / T$.

### 4.3 Surrogate Gradient Training at Depth

Applying surrogate gradients to deep SNNs requires care in three areas:

1. **Surrogate shape selection.** Narrow surrogates (e.g., boxcar of width $\ll \vartheta$) give faithful gradients but vanish for most neurons; wide surrogates give signal everywhere but bias the update. The fast sigmoid $\sigma'(x) = (1+\beta|x|)^{-2}$ is a widely used compromise [3][4].
2. **Gradient flow through time.** Because the state $v[t]$ depends on $v[t-1]$, BPTT must propagate through the recurrent dynamics. In practice, truncated BPTT over 20–100 timesteps suffices for most benchmarks.
3. **Regularization of activity.** Surrogate training without activity penalties discovers dense firing regimes that destroy the energy advantage. Standard practice adds a firing-rate regularizer or an explicit spike-count loss to keep mean rates in the 0.05–0.2 spikes/timestep range.

Empirically, surrogate-gradient-trained SNNs now match ANN accuracy within ~1% on CIFAR-10 and achieve state-of-the-art on neuromorphic datasets such as N-MNIST and DVS-Gesture [3].

### 4.4 Loihi 2: Programmable Event-Driven Manycore

Loihi 2 is Intel's second-generation neuromorphic research processor, fabricated on the Intel 4 process. Its architecture is a tiled mesh of **neurocores**, each integrating compute, local SRAM for synaptic state, and a spike router; communication is fully event-driven and asynchronous at the network level [1][7][2].

Key advances over Loihi 1, as described by Davies and colleagues:

- **Graded spike messages.** Spikes carry an optional magnitude alongside timing, recovering precision without the energy penalty of many binary spikes [1].
- **Programmable neuron models.** A microcoded neural engine supports custom per-neuron programs—arithmetic, comparisons, branching, fixed-point multiply—so LIF, resonate-and-fire, and novel models coexist on the same die [7].
- **Flexible learning.** Loihi 2 generalizes the on-chip learning engine beyond Loihi 1's single plasticity rule family, enabling approximations of backpropagation-scale algorithms at larger scale [1].
- **Scale.** Systems compose up to 1 million neurons and 120 million synapses per chip family configuration, with multi-chip meshes for larger models [2].

Published benchmarking shows the characteristic split: Loihi offers modest or no advantage on feed-forward dense workloads, but **1000–10000× lower energy and ~100× faster solution times on recurrent, sparse, temporally structured workloads** versus conventional architectures [8].

### 4.5 TrueNorth: The Neurosynaptic Crossbar

IBM's TrueNorth (2014) took a different path to the same destination. Each of its 4096 **neurosynaptic cores** contains 256 neurons, 256 axons, and a 256×256 binary crossbar of synapses; synaptic weight is the product of a binary connectivity bit and a per-axon-type signed integer, giving compact excitatory/inhibitory programming [5]. Cores update on a nominal 1 ms tick; spikes route over an on-chip mesh, and chips tile in two dimensions to arbitrary cortex-like sheets.

TrueNorth's headline result—one million neurons, 256 million synapses, 5.4 billion transistors, classifying 400×240 video at 30 fps for **63 mW**—remains the canonical demonstration of neuromorphic energy efficiency [5][6]. Its trade-off is rigidity: no on-chip learning, fixed neuron model, and binary synapses make it an inference-only substrate, whereas Loihi 2 targets a research envelope including plasticity.

| Feature | IBM TrueNorth (2014) | Intel Loihi 2 (2021) |
|---|---|---|
| Process / transistors | 28 nm, 5.4 B | Intel 4 |
| Neurons / synapses | 1 M / 256 M | ~1 M / 120 M |
| Neuron model | Fixed LIF variant | Microcode-programmable |
| Spike messages | Binary | Binary + graded magnitude |
| On-chip learning | No | Yes (generalized rules) |
| Timestep | 1 ms nominal | Fully asynchronous/event-driven |
| Signature result | 63 mW video classification [5] | 1000–10000× energy gain, recurrent workloads [8] |

## 5. Empirical Results / Proofs

### 5.1 Energy Proportionality

The central empirical claim of neuromorphic computing is that energy scales with *activity*, not model size. Intel's benchmarking program compared Loihi against CPUs, GPUs, and dedicated accelerators across workloads [8]. The results split cleanly:

- **Feed-forward dense networks** (e.g., MLPs, CNNs): Loihi shows little advantage and is sometimes worse—dense matrix math is exactly what von Neumann accelerators are optimized for.
- **Recurrent, sparse, stochastic workloads** (graph search, constraint optimization, sparse coding, recurrent SNN inference): Loihi achieves 1000–10000× lower energy and ~100× faster solutions [8].

This bifurcation is the most important practical result in the field: neuromorphic hardware is not a general-purpose DNN accelerator but a *specialized substrate for temporally structured, sparse computation*.

### 5.2 Proof Sketch: ANN-to-SNN Conversion Convergence

> **Theorem:** Let $a = \max(0, Wx)$ be a ReLU layer with weights normalized so that $\|Wx\|_\infty \le \vartheta$. Simulating an IF layer with reset-by-subtraction for $T$ timesteps yields time-averaged spike counts $\bar{s} = \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^T s[t]$ satisfying $\|\bar{s} - a/\vartheta\|_\infty \le 1/T$.

*Proof sketch.* With reset-by-subtraction, the membrane potential after $T$ steps obeys $v[T] = T \cdot Wx - \vartheta \sum_t s[t] + v[0]$. Since $0 \le v[T] < \vartheta$ by construction, rearranging gives $\sum_t s[t] = T Wx/\vartheta - v[T]/\vartheta + v[0]/\vartheta$, and dividing by $T$ bounds the error by the residual potentials, i.e. $1/T$. ∎

The bound makes explicit the conversion tax: halving the error requires doubling the latency $T$. This is the fundamental motivation for training SNNs *natively* with surrogate gradients, which routinely operate at $T \in [10, 50]$.

### 5.3 Accuracy Parity

Surrogate-gradient SNNs have reached the following published milestones: MNIST >99.5%, CIFAR-10 within ~1% of the ANN baseline, and state-of-the-art on event-based benchmarks (N-MNIST, DVS-CIFAR10) where the sensor data is natively spiking [3][4]. Conversion-based pipelines achieve the highest absolute accuracy but at 100–500 timesteps; hybrid *conversion-plus-fine-tuning* with surrogate gradients recovers most of the latency.

## 6. Limitations

1. **Training cost.** Surrogate-gradient BPTT unrolls the network over timesteps, multiplying memory by $T$; training a deep SNN costs substantially more than training the equivalent ANN, even though inference is cheaper.
2. **Software immaturity.** Toolchains (Lava for Loihi 2, Corelet for TrueNorth) lag PyTorch/TensorFlow in debuggability, profiling, and model zoos. Mapping arbitrary architectures onto fixed core grids remains expert work.
3. **Benchmark fragmentation.** The field lacks an agreed benchmark suite; Intel has begun open-sourcing its workloads to address this [8], but cross-platform comparisons remain rare.
4. **Precision limits.** Binary spikes quantize communication; graded spikes (Loihi 2) and multi-bit extensions mitigate but do not eliminate the gap to floating-point activations for precision-critical tasks.
5. **Temporal fragility.** Temporal codes are sensitive to jitter and device mismatch; mixed-signal implementations must calibrate aggressively, and even digital designs pay latency costs for fine-grained timing.
6. **Learning on chip is still narrow.** Loihi 2 generalizes plasticity but on-chip learning demonstrations remain small-scale compared with offline surrogate-gradient training on GPUs.

---

## 7. Conclusion

Spiking neural networks reframe computation around *events* rather than *tensors*. The LIF neuron gives a hardware-friendly dynamical primitive; STDP shows that local, unsupervised learning is possible without any global gradient; surrogate gradients show that the full power of gradient-based optimization can be brought to bear on spiking substrates; and ANN-to-SNN conversion provides a pragmatic bridge from today's trained models. On silicon, TrueNorth proved the energy thesis at scale, and Loihi 2 proved that programmability and learning need not be sacrificed to keep it.

The honest assessment is asymmetric: neuromorphic processors do not beat GPUs at what GPUs do. They win—by orders of magnitude—on sparse, recurrent, temporally structured workloads where activity, not model size, should determine cost [8]. As edge intelligence moves toward always-on sensing, adaptive control, and milliwatt budgets, that asymmetry is precisely the opening neuromorphic computing was built for. The next decade's work is software: compilers, benchmarks, and training methods mature enough to let any practitioner, not just specialists, target event-driven hardware.
