# Verifiable Delay Functions: Sequentiality, Succinct Proofs of Exponentiation, and the Path to Trustless Randomness

## Abstract

A *verifiable delay function* (VDF) is a function whose evaluation provably consumes a prescribed number of sequential steps, while producing a compact output that anyone can verify in time exponentially smaller than evaluation. This article develops the theory and practice of modern VDFs. We begin with the syntax and security notions introduced by Boneh, Bonneau, Bünz, and Fisch — sequentiality, uniqueness, and succinct public verifiability — and trace the lineage to the Rivest–Shamir–Wagner time-lock puzzle. The core is a detailed treatment of the two dominant constructions: Wesolowski's single-element proof of exponentiation, and Pietrzak's recursive halving protocol with statistical soundness under the bare repeated-squaring assumption. We then examine MinRoot, an algebraic candidate over prime fields proposed for Ethereum's randomness infrastructure, and its recent cryptanalysis. Finally, we survey deployed systems — drand-style beacons, Chia's proof-of-space-and-time, leader election, and proofs of replication — and close with open problems: trusted-setup-free groups, tight sequentiality bounds, and post-quantum assumptions.

## 1 Introduction

Few primitives combine such elegant mathematics with such immediate demand as the verifiable delay function. Informally, a VDF is a function that **requires a specified amount of *sequential* time to evaluate** — no parallelism can shorten the delay — yet whose output can be **verified quickly** by any third party [1]. This asymmetry is what decentralized systems need: a way to *prove that time has passed* without a trusted clock, a trusted party, or energy-wasting proof-of-work.

Consider the public randomness beacon: an ideal service publishing random values no party can predict or bias [1]. The naïve construction — hash public entropy like stock prices — is vulnerable to an adversary who influences the entropy before the beacon fires. A VDF resolves this by *delaying* the output: entropy is committed first, and the beacon value emerges only after the prescribed sequential steps elapse. Biasing the outcome requires computing the VDF faster than everyone else — which sequentiality forbids, since unbounded parallel hardware buys nothing.

The conceptual ancestor is the *time-lock puzzle* of Rivest, Shamir, and Wagner: encrypt a secret so decryption needs a prescribed number of *sequential* squarings in an RSA group, where knowledge of the group order gives a trapdoor shortcut [9]. The evaluator must compute $y = x^{2^T} \bmod N$ one squaring at a time; verification, however, needs the trapdoor — a *secret* — and is not public. Boneh et al. formalized making such puzzles *publicly* verifiable, giving the first candidates with an *exponential* gap between evaluation $T$ and verification $\mathrm{polylog}(T)$ [1].

The subsequent history is one of efficiency gains. Wesolowski's proof is a **single group element** with verification costing two small exponentiations [2]; Pietrzak's has $O(\log T)$ group elements but rests on *no additional assumptions* beyond repeated squaring [3]. Both remain the state of the art for transparent, trapdoor-free VDFs [7].

More recently, the community has explored *algebraic* VDFs over groups of **known** order — notably MinRoot, proposed for Ethereum's randomness infrastructure [5] — and subjected them to serious cryptanalysis [6]. Deployed beacons, blockchains, and standards work have meanwhile turned VDFs from theory into infrastructure.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Syntax and Security

A VDF is a triple $(\mathsf{Setup}, \mathsf{Eval}, \mathsf{Verify})$ [1]:

> **Definition:** A *verifiable delay function* with delay $T$ and security parameter $\lambda$ is $(\mathsf{Setup}, \mathsf{Eval}, \mathsf{Verify})$ where $\mathsf{pp} \leftarrow \mathsf{Setup}(1^\lambda, T)$; $(y, \pi) \leftarrow \mathsf{Eval}(\mathsf{pp}, x)$ deterministically computes $y = f(x)$ and proof $\pi$; and $\mathsf{Verify}(\mathsf{pp}, x, y, \pi) \in \{0, 1\}$ decides validity.

Security comprises three properties:

1. **Correctness.** Honest evaluation always verifies.
2. **Soundness.** No efficient adversary produces verifying $(y', \pi')$ with $y' \neq f(x)$, except negligibly.
3. **Sequentiality.** Any parallel algorithm with parallel time $< T$ and $\mathrm{poly}(T, \lambda)$ work computes $f(x)$ only negligibly.

A fourth desideratum, **uniqueness**, requires that for each $x$ there is exactly one $y$ that any verifying proof could attest to; it follows from soundness plus a natural functional property of the construction [1].

> **Lemma:** Sequentiality is a *fine-grained* hardness notion: it bounds *parallel depth*, not total work. An adversary with $2^{100}$ processors has no advantage over one with a single processor, provided the function's dependency graph has depth $T$.

### 2.2 The Repeated-Squaring Assumption

The workhorse of practical transparent VDFs is *iterated squaring in a group of unknown order* [1][2][3]: the RSA group $(\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z})^\times$ for secret $N = pq$, or the class group $\mathrm{Cl}(\Delta)$ of an imaginary quadratic order, which needs *no trusted setup* [2]. Define $f(x) = x^{2^T}$ via $T$ sequential squarings. The **repeated-squaring assumption** states that no parallel-depth-$o(T)$ algorithm with $\mathrm{poly}(T,\lambda)$ processors computes $x^{2^T}$ with non-negligible probability. Without $|\mathbb{G}|$ the exponent $2^T$ cannot be reduced; with the trapdoor (the factorization), one reduces $2^T \bmod \varphi(N)$ and evaluates in $O(\log T)$ steps — the *trapdoor VDF*, from which the transparent VDF is derived by ensuring nobody knows the trapdoor [2].

Key subtlety: the group must have **no small-order elements** the adversary could exploit; in RSA groups one works in the subgroup of quadratic residues to avoid Jacobi-symbol attacks. Pietrzak's analysis formalizes this via a *low-order assumption*: finding $\mu \neq 1$ with $\mu^d = 1$ for small $d$ is hard [3].

### 2.3 Interactive Proofs of Exponentiation and Fiat–Shamir

Both flagship constructions first build an *interactive* protocol for $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{EXP}} = \{(\mathbb{G}, g, h, T) : h = g^{2^T}\}$, then compile it to non-interactivity via the **Fiat–Shamir heuristic**, replacing verifier challenges with hashes of the transcript [2][3]. Security is argued in the *random-oracle model*.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Wesolowski's Protocol: A One-Element Proof

Wesolowski's insight is that a *single* random challenge suffices if the prover answers with a quotient in the exponent [2]. Let $g \in \mathbb{G}$ and $h = g^{2^T}$:

1. The **verifier** samples a random prime $\ell$ from the first $2^\lambda$ primes.
2. The **prover** computes $\pi = g^{\lfloor 2^T / \ell \rfloor}$ and sends $\pi$.
3. The **verifier** computes $r = 2^T \bmod \ell$ and accepts iff $\pi^\ell \cdot g^{\,r} = h$.

Correctness is the division identity $2^T = \ell \cdot \lfloor 2^T/\ell \rfloor + r$ [2]. The proof is **one group element** ($\approx$ 256 bytes for RSA-2048); verification is two exponentiations with small exponents — $O(\lambda)$ multiplications, exponentially faster than $T$ squarings [2].

> **Theorem:** (Wesolowski [2]) The protocol is sound under the *adaptive-root assumption*: given random $w \in \mathbb{G}$ and a random prime $\ell$, no efficient adversary outputs $u$ with $u^\ell = w$. The assumption holds in the generic group model and is implied by the low-order assumption plus a standard RSA-type assumption.

Intuition: a cheating prover must answer a challenge $\ell$ it cannot predict; the check forces extraction of an $\ell$-th root, which the assumption forbids. Computing the honest proof $\pi = g^{\lfloor 2^T/\ell \rfloor}$ dominates the extra prover cost, though Wesolowski's optimization brings it to about the cost of evaluation itself [2]; in practice the proof is generated *alongside* evaluation with modest overhead [7]. The challenge must be sampled *after* $h$ is fixed; in the Fiat–Shamir variant, $\ell = H(\mathbb{G}, g, h, T)$ mapped to a prime binds the challenge to the claimed output [2].

### 4.2 Pietrzak's Protocol: Recursive Halving

Pietrzak takes a different route: recursively *halve* the exponent, committing to the midpoint at each level [3]. Assume $T = 2^t$. To prove $h = g^{2^T}$:

1. **Prover** computes the midpoint $v = g^{2^{T/2}}$ and sends $v$.
2. **Verifier** sends a random $r \in \{1, \dots, 2^\lambda\}$.
3. Both compute $g_1 = g^r v$ and $h_1 = v^r h$; recurse on $h_1 = g_1^{2^{T/2}}$.

1. **Prover** computes the midpoint $v = g^{2^{T/2}}$ and sends $v$.
2. **Verifier** sends a random $r \in \{1, \dots, 2^\lambda\}$.
3. Both compute $g_1 = g^r v$ and $h_1 = v^r h$; recurse on $h_1 = g_1^{2^{T/2}}$.
### 4.3 The MinRoot Construction: Algebraic VDFs over Prime Fields

A parallel line of work — motivated by Ethereum's desire for a VDF *without* RSA trusted setup — builds VDFs over groups of **known** order, iterating an algebraic map $F: \mathbb{F}_p \to \mathbb{F}_p$ conjectured sequential, with correctness proven via a SNARK [5].

The leading candidate is **MinRoot**. Fix a prime field $\mathbb{F}_p$ and define the round function

$$x_{i+1} = (x_i + \delta)^e \bmod p \quad \text{or, in root form,} \quad x_{i+1} = x_i^{1/e} + \delta,$$

where $e$ is chosen so that exponentiation by $e$ is a *sequential* bottleneck: square-and-multiply needs $\Theta(\log e)$ *dependent* multiplications, and the designers chose parameters so no parallel circuit meaningfully reduces one round's *latency* [5]. The VDF output after $T$ rounds is $x_T$, proven correct with a SNARK (3 constraints per round) [5].

MinRoot's designers claimed 128 bits of *multitarget* VDF security over a 256-bit field against classical and quantum adversaries, benchmarked a CPU round at roughly $10\,\mu\mathrm{s}$, and projected $\sim$300 ns per round on optimized ASICs ($\approx$ 3.3M iterations/s) [5].

> **Definition:** (Multitarget VDF security [5]) An adversary given many challenge inputs cannot compute *any* $f(x^{(j)})$ in parallel time less than $T$ — strictly stronger than single-target sequentiality, and the right notion when one VDF secures many downstream applications.

The algebraic approach is seductive — no trusted setup, hardware-friendly rounds, SNARK-compressed proofs — but the history of algebraic VDFs is a history of broken assumptions, as Section 6 discusses [6].

### 4.4 Randomness Beacons, Leader Election, and Proof of Replication

VDFs earn their keep in deployed systems. The canonical application is the **randomness beacon**: chain VDF evaluations so each round's output is unpredictable before its delay elapses but publicly verifiable afterward [1].

- **drand** (League of Entropy) runs a distributed beacon where a threshold of nodes jointly produce BLS signatures over chained round data on a short fixed cadence. Its *current* design uses threshold cryptography rather than a VDF, but the VDF-based beacon is the trust-minimized ideal it approximates: replacing the committee with a VDF removes the honest-majority assumption — the substitution Ethereum's randomness roadmap has long considered [5].
- **Chia** uses a VDF as *proof of space and time*: a network of *timelords* evaluates a Wesolowski-style VDF chain in class groups that timestamps blocks, replacing proof-of-work's energy with a few sequential squarers [2][3]. Sequentiality is load-bearing: a parallelizable VDF would let an attacker forge the timeline.
- **Leader election** in proof-of-stake protocols uses VDF-derived randomness to select block proposers unpredictably yet verifiably [1].
- **Proofs of replication** (e.g., Filecoin) use VDF-like sequential encoding to force providers to dedicate *physical* storage that cannot be recomputed on the fly [1].

In each case the security reduction has the same shape: *bias the beacon* $\Rightarrow$ *break sequentiality* $\Rightarrow$ *contradict repeated squaring*.
## 5 Empirical Results / Formal Proofs

### 5.1 The Core Security Theorems

> **Theorem:** (Wesolowski soundness [2]) Under the adaptive-root assumption, the non-interactive protocol is a VDF: correct, sound, and $\Delta$-sequential under repeated squaring, with verification in $O(\lambda)$ group operations.

*Proof sketch.* Correctness is the division identity. A verifying forgery $(h', \pi')$ with $h' \neq g^{2^T}$ yields, by division against the honest relation, an $\ell$-th root contradicting the adaptive-root assumption. Sequentiality is exactly repeated squaring. ∎

> **Theorem:** (Pietrzak soundness [3]) The recursive protocol is statistically sound: any prover convinces the verifier of a false claim with probability at most $(t+1)2^{-\lambda+1}$ for $T = 2^t$. Non-interactive via Fiat–Shamir, it is a VDF under only the repeated-squaring assumption.

*Proof sketch.* The halving lemma shows each recursion level preserves falsity except with probability $\le 2^{-\lambda+1}$: if $h \neq g^{2^T}$ then $h_1 = g_1^{2^{T/2}}$ can hold only if $r$ satisfies a nontrivial low-degree equation in the exponent, which a random $r$ avoids. Union-bounding over $t = \log T$ levels gives the bound. ∎

### 5.2 Measured Performance

Implementation studies ground the asymptotics [7][8]:

- For $T = 2^{40}$ and a 2048-bit RSA modulus, Pietrzak proofs measure **under 8 KB** in optimized implementations, with per-round verification costs modeled for smart-contract deployment [8].
- Wesolowski proofs are a **single group element** ($\approx$ 256 bytes for RSA-2048); verification is two $\lambda$-bit exponentiations — milliseconds on commodity hardware [2][7].
- Prover overhead is reported at roughly $8000\times$ faster than puzzle solving in Pietrzak's parameterization even *without* parallelism, when checkpoints are reused [3]; independent benchmarks confirm only a small constant factor over raw evaluation [7].
- For MinRoot over a 256-bit field, a naive CPU round costs $\approx 10{,}000$ ns; ASIC projections put a round at $\approx$ 300 ns, with one GPU generating SNARK proofs for about two ASIC evaluators in real time [5].

These numbers explain the deployment pattern: Wesolowski where bandwidth is precious and the low-order assumption is acceptable; Pietrzak where assumption-minimality matters; MinRoot-style designs where trusted-setup-free fields and SNARK verification are required [5][6][7].

### 5.3 Tightness

A subtle efficiency notion is *tightness*: an *Eval* running in $T + o(T)$ rather than $cT$. Döttling, Garg, Malavolta, and Vasudevan showed both a limitation and a fix [4]:

> **Theorem:** (Döttling et al. [4]) No black-box construction from random oracles yields a VDF evaluable in $T + O(T^\delta)$ for any constant $\delta < 1$. Conversely, any VDF with prover time $cT$ and a natural *self-composability* property compiles into one with prover time $T + O(1)$, at logarithmic cost in proof size and prover parallelism.

The compiler chunks the evaluation into segments, proves each, and composes the proofs — yielding a tight VDF from any succinct argument plus repeated hashing [4]. Wesolowski/Pietrzak already have small constant-factor overhead, hence are "morally tight".
## 6 Limitations

**The trusted-setup problem.** RSA-group VDFs need a modulus $N = pq$ whose factorization is unknown to *everyone*. Multiparty ceremonies can generate such moduli, but they are operationally heavy. Class groups of imaginary quadratic fields avoid trusted setup — the order is unknown *because* computing it is hard — but their arithmetic is slower and the low-order assumption is less battle-tested there [2][3].

**Assumption fragility.** Wesolowski's soundness needs the adaptive-root assumption *on top of* repeated squaring; Pietrzak's needs only repeated squaring but pays in proof size. Neither reduces to factoring in the standard model, and both are *fine-grained* sequential lower bounds — notoriously hard to prove, easy to get subtly wrong.

**Algebraic VDFs keep breaking.** The MinRoot/Sloth++/VeeDo line conjectures that $x \mapsto x^e$ in $\mathbb{F}_p$ needs $\log_2 e$ *sequential* multiplications. Biryukov et al. showed exponentiation latency can be reduced with parallel computation, and gave smoothness-based attacks exploiting MinRoot's special prime shape for precomputation tables [6]. Lesson: *algebraic simplicity is not sequentiality* — every algebraic candidate needs adversarial cryptanalysis, not just benchmarks [5][6].

**Quantum considerations.** Shor's algorithm breaks RSA groups outright, so Wesolowski/Pietrzak over $(\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z})^\times$ are *not* post-quantum; class groups fall to quantum hidden-subgroup algorithms too. MinRoot's designers claimed quantum resistance for the *sequentiality* assumption (no hidden-subgroup structure to exploit) [5], but the accompanying SNARK must itself be post-quantum (e.g., STARK-based) — and the cryptanalysis of [6] already applies classically. A genuinely post-quantum, assumption-lean VDF remains open; lattice candidates exist but are far from practical [4].

**Verification asymmetry in practice.** Pietrzak verification at $O(\lambda \log T)$ is cheap but not free on-chain, which is why gas-cost modeling studies exist [8]. Wesolowski verification is cheaper, but its Fiat–Shamir challenge derivation must be implemented carefully — weak domain separation silently destroys soundness [2][7].

**Sequentiality vs. wall-clock.** $T$ counts *group operations*, not seconds: a VDF calibrated for "one hour" on 2024 hardware may take minutes on 2030 ASICs. Deployments must over-provision $T$, accept drift, or re-parameterize — itself a consensus-sensitive event for a live beacon.

## 7 Conclusion

Verifiable delay functions transform *time* into a cryptographic resource: a publicly checkable certificate that a prescribed amount of sequential computation has elapsed. Verifiable delay functions transform *time* into a cryptographic resource: a publicly checkable certificate that a prescribed amount of sequential computation has elapsed. The two pillars — **Wesolowski's one-element proof** [2] and **Pietrzak's recursive halving** [3] — both reduce trust to repeated squaring in groups of unknown order, differing in the trade-off between proof size and assumption strength. Implementations are benchmarked to the kilobyte and millisecond [7][8], and deployments — Chia's timelords, beacon roadmaps, leader election, proofs of replication — show sequentiality is load-bearing infrastructure, not a curiosity [1].

The frontier has three fronts: *trusted-setup-free groups* (class groups; cryptanalysis of algebraic candidates [5][6]); *tighter theory* (exact sequentiality bounds, standard-model proofs [4]); and *post-quantum sequentiality* (assumptions surviving Shor, with proofs surviving with them).

The deepest lesson is methodological. Sequentiality is a *negative* claim — "no parallel machine can do better" — and such claims fail in instructive ways, from smoothness attacks on algebraic designs [6] to the delicate soundness conditions of succinct proofs [2][3]. Progress will come from the patient, adversarial cryptanalysis the field has already begun to institutionalize. Time is hard to prove — which is exactly why proving it is valuable.
