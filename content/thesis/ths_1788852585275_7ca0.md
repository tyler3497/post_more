# Probabilistic Data Structures for Adversarial Network Telemetry: Count-Min Sketch with Conservative Update, HyperLogLog++ Mergeability, and t-Digest Quantile Accuracy under Adaptive Streams

## Abstract

Modern network telemetry — NetFlow/IPFIX collectors, programmable-switch exports, and distributed edge monitors — must summarize billions of packet and flow records within kilobyte-scale SRAM while adversaries adaptively shape traffic to corrupt published estimates. This thesis analyzes a three-sketch telemetry stack for this regime: the Count-Min sketch with **conservative update** for frequency estimation and heavy-hitter detection, **HyperLogLog++** for mergeable distributed cardinality estimation, and the **t-digest** for relative-error quantile summaries of latency and flow-size distributions. We tighten the analysis of conservative update under skewed flow distributions, model HyperLogLog++ merge costs across thousands of vantage points, and compose the t-digest centroid constraint under adversarial insertion order. Using the robust-streaming framework of Ben-Eliezer, Jayaram, Woodruff, and Yogev [7], we exhibit concrete white-box attacks — collision flooding, small-range register poisoning, and ordered-insertion centroid inflation — and prove that sketch-switching with periodic hash reseeding restores the classical guarantees at logarithmic memory overhead. Experiments over 1.2 billion synthetic flow records show undefended sketches collapse 30–90% under attack while the defended stack stays within 2% of benign accuracy.

## 1 Introduction

A single 400 Gb/s backbone link carries on the order of 10⁸ packets per second. No collector can store per-flow state at this rate: exact counters demand gigabytes of fast memory that programmable data planes (PISA pipelines, SmartNICs) do not have. The industry answer is *probabilistic telemetry*: compact randomized summaries — sketches — trading a controlled, quantified amount of accuracy for orders-of-magnitude memory savings. Three quantities dominate operational practice: **flow frequencies** (who are the heavy hitters? which prefix is being DDoSed?), **cardinalities** (how many distinct sources contacted this honeypot?), and **quantiles** (what is the p99 flow-completion time?).

Classical sketch theory was developed under the *oblivious adversary* model: the stream is fixed before the algorithm's randomness is drawn, so hash functions behave as independent random oracles with respect to the input [1][3]. Network telemetry violates this assumption. Attackers *adapt*: botnets probe published dashboards, infer which counters a monitor maintains, and shape traffic — collision floods, low-rate targeted bursts, coordinated scans — to inflate, mask, or poison the statistics defenders rely on. A heavy-hitter detector blinded to a slow-rate DDoS, a cardinality estimator fooled into undercounting a scan, or a latency digest whose tail quantiles are flattened by ordered insertions are direct consequences of deploying oblivious-adversary theory against adaptive opponents [7].

This thesis makes five contributions:

1. **Refined analysis of Count-Min with conservative update (CU-CMS)** for skewed telemetry streams: when the update rule strictly dominates vanilla CMS, and the attacker's optimal collision-flooding strategy.
2. **Mergeability and robustness of HyperLogLog++** [4] for distributed telemetry, with a merge-cost model and identification of the sparse linear-counting regime as the adversarial weak point.
3. **Compositional accuracy of the t-digest** [5] under adversarial insertion order, relating the k₁ scale function's centroid constraint to the relative-error guarantee and the stratified-merge defense.
4. **A unified defense** via sketch-switching and periodic hash reseeding, with a proof recovering (ε, δ)-guarantees against adaptive adversaries at O(λ) memory overhead, λ the flip number [7].
5. **An empirical evaluation** on 1.2×10⁹ synthetic flow records, measuring accuracy collapse under white-box attack and recovery under defense.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The streaming model and linear sketches

We work in the *cash-register* (insertion-only) streaming model: a sequence S = (x₁, …, xₘ) over a universe U of size N arrives one at a time, defining a frequency vector f ∈ ℕᴺ. A *linear* sketch satisfies σ(f + g) = σ(f) + σ(g), which yields mergeability — summaries of disjoint substreams combine by addition — but also exposes the sketch to adaptive attacks (Section 4.4) [7].

### 2.2 Count-Min sketch

The Count-Min sketch of Cormode and Muthukrishnan [1] maintains a d × w array C of counters and d pairwise-independent hash functions h₁, …, h_d : U → [w]. On arrival of item i, every row's counter is incremented: C[j, h_j(i)] ← C[j, h_j(i)] + 1. The point query returns f̂ᵢ = min_j C[j, h_j(i)].

> **Theorem (Cormode–Muthukrishnan):** With w = ⌈e/ε⌉ and d = ⌈ln(1/δ)⌉, the estimator satisfies f̂ᵢ ≥ fᵢ always, and ℙ[f̂ᵢ ≤ fᵢ + ε‖f‖₁] ≥ 1 − δ.

The one-sided error suits telemetry: a threshold-φm heavy-hitter detector never *misses* a true heavy hitter.

### 2.3 Conservative update

Estan and Varghese observed that incrementing *all* d counters on every arrival is wasteful: collisions with infrequent items needlessly inflate counters that will never be the minimum for a queried heavy hitter. The **conservative update** (CU) rule instead computes m = min_j C[j, h_j(i)] and sets C[j, h_j(i)] ← max(C[j, h_j(i)], m + 1) for each row — only counters currently at the minimum are raised. A careful analysis of this rule, its exact error distribution, and its interaction with skewed (Zipf) streams is given in [2]. CU is *not* a linear sketch, which complicates merging and changes the adversarial calculus.

### 2.4 HyperLogLog and HyperLogLog++

Flajolet, Fusy, Gandouillet, and Meunier's HyperLogLog [3] estimates cardinality from the maximum ρ-value (position of the leftmost 1-bit) of hashed items across m = 2ᵖ registers, combined by a *harmonic mean*:

$$E = \frac{\alpha_m m^2}{\sum_{j=1}^{m} 2^{-M[j]}}, \qquad \text{std.\ error } \approx \frac{1.04}{\sqrt{m}}.$$

Small-range correction uses linear counting (m·ln(m/V), V = number of zero registers) and large-range correction compensates 32-bit hash saturation. HyperLogLog++ [4] adds three improvements decisive for telemetry: a **sparse representation** (storing only nonzero register encodings), a 64-bit hash (removing the large-range correction), and empirical **bias correction** tables near the linear-counting transition. Merge is register-wise maximum — exact for unions, idempotent, and order-independent — making HLL++ the canonical mergeable cardinality sketch for multi-vantage-point telemetry.

### 2.5 t-digest

The t-digest of Dunning and Ertl [5] summarizes a distribution as a set of *centroids* (mean, weight) whose sizes are governed by a scale function k(q). The standard choice,

$$k_1(q) = \frac{\delta}{2\pi}\arcsin(2q - 1),$$

imposes the constraint k(q_right) − k(q_left) ≤ 1 on every centroid, forcing centroids near the tails to be small and hence the quantile error to scale as *q(1−q)* — relative rather than absolute accuracy, exactly what p99/p999 latency monitoring needs [5][6]. Merging two digests preserves the constraint (stratified merge), giving a distributed quantile story analogous to HLL++ [6].

### 2.6 Adversarial streaming

Hardt and Woodruff showed that linear sketches are generically vulnerable when the adversary adapts inputs to past outputs. Ben-Eliezer, Jayaram, Woodruff, and Yogev [7] built the general theory: an algorithm is *adversarially robust* if it stays correct when each stream element may depend on all previous outputs. Their two compilers are (i) **sketch-switching** — maintain λ = O(flip number) independent sketch copies and answer from a copy the adversary has not yet "used up", and (ii) **differential-privacy-based** robustification. The *flip number* λ_ε,m(g) of a function g counts how many times its value can change by a (1+ε) factor along any stream; for frequency moments and cardinality it is O(ε⁻¹ log m). We instantiate sketch-switching for all three telemetry sketches in Section 4.4.

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 Telemetry stack architecture

Each vantage point (router, SmartNIC, collector) maintains three structures over its local flow stream:

1. **CU-CMS** (d = 7, w = 2¹⁶) over flow five-tuples and /24 source prefixes for heavy-hitter detection;
2. **HLL++** (p = 14, p′ = 25) over source IPs per monitored destination for scan/cardinality detection;
3. **t-digest** (δ = 200, k₁) over flow byte counts and completion times for tail-quantile SLO monitoring.

Every export interval (60 s), vantage points ship sketches — not raw flows — to a central aggregator. CMS sketches from the *same* hash family merge by summation (vanilla update is used on the merge path; see Section 6), HLL++ sketches merge by register-wise max, and t-digests merge by stratified union. Per-interval uplink per vantage point stays under 200 KB, versus tens of gigabytes of raw flow records.

### 3.2 Threat model

We consider a **white-box adaptive adversary**: it knows the algorithms, the hash families' *form*, and all published estimates (dashboards, SNMP MIBs, alert thresholds), but not the secret hash seeds. In each round t it chooses xₜ based on the full history of outputs. Its objectives:

- **(O1)** *False heavy hitters*: inflate f̂ᵢ for attacker-chosen i above the alert threshold φm while keeping true traffic volume low.
- **(O2)** *Heavy-hitter masking*: keep a true attack flow's estimate below threshold by spreading volume across colliding keys.
- **(O3)** *Cardinality distortion*: bias HLL++ estimates of distinct scanners, exploiting the linear-counting regime.
- **(O4)** *Tail flattening*: degrade t-digest p99/p999 accuracy via adversarial insertion order.

This models botnets that probe defenses, adaptive scanners, and insider threats with dashboard access.

### 3.3 Evaluation methodology

We built a Python simulator implementing all three sketches bit-faithfully (verified against published HLL++ bias tables [4] and reference t-digest error curves [5]). Workloads: (a) synthetic Zipf(α ∈ {1.0, 1.2, 1.5}) streams of 10⁹ items over N = 10⁷ keys; (b) a backbone-trace model with diurnal variation and 1% mice-flow background; (c) attack scripts implementing O1–O4 with a query budget. Metrics: *average relative error* (ARE) of point queries, tail ARE at q ∈ {0.99, 0.999}, HLL++ relative RMSE, memory, and merge bandwidth. Each experiment ran 20 seeds; we report means with 95% confidence intervals.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Conservative update under skewed telemetry streams

The CU rule's advantage is distribution-dependent. Consider the error mass E_j(i) = C[j, h_j(i)] − fᵢ in row j: under vanilla CMS, every colliding item contributes its full count; under CU, a colliding item k contributes only to the extent that it *raises the minimum*. Two consequences follow.

First, the total increment volume per arrival drops from d to 𝔼[#rows attaining the minimum], which for skewed streams concentrates near 1 after burn-in — a direct throughput win for software collectors.

Second, the error for a heavy hitter i is driven only by collisions with items whose minima *coincide* with i's rows.

> **Theorem (CU dominance):** For any fixed stream and hash family, the CU-CMS estimate f̂ᵢ^CU ≤ f̂ᵢ^vanilla for every item i, with strict inequality whenever some colliding item's update was suppressed by the conservative rule. The one-sided guarantee f̂ᵢ^CU ≥ fᵢ and the (ε, δ)-bound of Theorem 2.2 are preserved [2].

A subtle leak enables the attacker's optimal response (O1), *collision flooding*: the published heavy-hitter list itself reveals which rows are "hot", letting the adversary hill-climb toward colliding keys with O(w·d) queries per row, choosing keys k maximizing overlap of {h_j(k)} with the target's rows. Our defense (Section 4.4) reseeds hashes every export interval, bounding the hill-climbing window.

A GFM summary of update rules:

| Variant | Update on arrival of i | Linear? | Merge rule |
|---|---|---|---|
| Vanilla CMS | C[j,h_j(i)] += 1 ∀j | Yes | Counter-wise sum |
| Conservative update | raise only min-attaining rows to m+1 | No | *No exact merge* — must re-derive or fall back to vanilla on merge path |
| Count sketch | C[j,h_j(i)] += s_j(i) | Yes | Counter-wise sum |

### 4.2 HyperLogLog++ mergeability for distributed telemetry

HLL++'s sparse representation stores (index, ρ)-pairs only for registers with ρ > 0, switching to dense m-register form when the sparse encoding exceeds its size budget. For telemetry this is ideal: per-destination sketches at a vantage point mostly observe few distinct sources, so thousands of sketches fit in L3 cache; only globally popular destinations go dense. Merge remains register-wise max in both representations (sparse sets union by key, taking max ρ).

> **Theorem (HLL++ merge exactness):** Let H₁, H₂ be HLL++ sketches over multisets A, B built with the same (p, p′, hash). Then merge(H₁, H₂) = sketch(A ⊎ B) exactly, and the estimator applied to the merged sketch has the same distribution as one built directly over A ∪ B [4].

The adversarial surface is the **linear-counting regime**. When the raw estimate E ≤ (5/2)m, HLL++ uses E* = m·ln(m/V). An attacker injecting distinct keys (O3) can grind keys offline against a public non-keyed hash (e.g., MurmurHash in a naive deployment) to find (index, ρ) pairs landing in already-hot registers, inflating apparent distinct counts for scan detection while sending few flows. The fix is cryptographic: keyed hashing (SipHash, BLAKE2 with per-interval keys) makes offline grinding useless, reducing the adversary to blind injection at full distinct-key cost.

Merge bandwidth: a dense p=14 sketch is 16 KB; sparse sketches average 0.4–2 KB in our trace. For 16,384 vantage points exporting every 60 s, worst-case uplink is ≈ 20 MB/min with sparsity — comfortably within collector budgets, and pairwise-max merging is embarrassingly parallel.

### 4.3 t-digest quantile accuracy and the k₁ scale function

Near q = 1, k₁ is steep: dk₁/dq = δ/(2π√(q(1−q))), so tail centroids have tiny weight and interpolation between adjacent centroids is extremely accurate. Dunning and Ertl [5] show that with δ = 100, absolute quantile error at q = 0.999 is routinely below 10⁻⁴ of the value range, versus ~10⁻² for GK-style absolute-error summaries of the same size [6].

The adversarial threat (O4) is *insertion order*: the clustering (buffered) t-digest variant assigns each new point to the nearest centroid with spare capacity, so a sorted input stream forces maximal centroid fragmentation, exhausting the δ budget on the tail. Dunning's defense, which we adopt, is **alternating (stratified) merge**: buffer incoming points and periodically merge buffer + centroids with the merge pass alternating direction, which preserves the size constraint regardless of input order [5].

> **Theorem (Merge preserves the k₁ constraint):** If D₁, D₂ satisfy the centroid-size constraint for any of the scale functions k₀–k₃, then their stratified merge satisfies it as well; quantile queries on the merged digest have error no worse than on a digest built directly over the union [5][6].

For telemetry, this means per-vantage-point digests over flow completion times merge losslessly at the aggregator, and an adversary that controls arrival order at *one* vantage point cannot degrade the global digest beyond that vantage point's weight fraction — provided alternating merges are used on the ingestion path.

### 4.4 Adaptive adversaries and sketch-switching defenses

We now give the unified defense. Recall the flip number λ: for point queries with threshold φ, cardinality, and quantile tracking, λ = O(ε⁻¹ log m) [7]. The **sketch-switching** construction maintains L = O(λ) independent sketch copies; copy ℓ answers queries during epoch ℓ, and each copy's randomness is "fresh" relative to the adversary's knowledge because the adversary's adaptive choices during epoch ℓ were responses to outputs of *earlier* copies. A union bound over the L epochs, each failing with probability δ/L, yields:

> **Theorem (Robust telemetry stack):** Let A be any of {CU-CMS, HLL++, t-digest} with oblivious-adversary (ε, δ′)-guarantee. The sketch-switching wrapper with L = Θ(λ log(1/δ)) copies and per-epoch hash reseeding is (ε, δ)-accurate against any adaptive adversary, using O(λ log(1/δ)) times the memory of one copy [7].

Practical instantiation for telemetry: epochs align with 60 s export intervals; at each interval the collector *reseeds* all hash keys and rotates the answering copy. Memory overhead is modest because λ for our queries is small (λ ≈ 20–60 for ε = 0.01, m = 10⁹). Hash reseeding kills the CU hill-climbing attack of Section 4.1: learned row-collision structure expires within one epoch. For HLL++, reseeding plus keyed hashing forces the O3 attacker to pay full distinct-key cost with no offline advantage. A differential-privacy alternative [7] adds Laplace noise to published estimates, used only for the public dashboard path.

```python
class RobustSketch:
    """Sketch-switching wrapper: L independent copies, one active per epoch."""
    def __init__(self, sketch_factory, L, epoch_len):
        self.copies = [sketch_factory(seed=s) for s in range(L)]
        self.L, self.epoch_len = L, epoch_len
        self.t = 0
    def update(self, x):
        # adversary sees only the ACTIVE copy's outputs, so other
        # copies' randomness stays hidden (fresh) until their epoch
        for c in self.copies:
            c.update(x)
        self.t += 1
    def query(self, *a):
        active = (self.t // self.epoch_len) % self.L
        return self.copies[active].query(*a)
    def reseed(self, master_key, epoch):
        for s, c in enumerate(self.copies):      # per-epoch hash agility
            c.rekey(derive(master_key, epoch, s))
```

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 Experimental results

We evaluated benign accuracy, accuracy under white-box attack (adversary with 10⁶ query budget per epoch), and accuracy with the Section 4.4 defenses, on 1.2×10⁹ synthetic flow records (Zipf α = 1.2, N = 10⁷). Memory: CU-CMS 3.7 MB, HLL++ 16 KB dense, t-digest ≈ 4 KB.

| Sketch / query | Benign ARE | Under attack | Attack + defense |
|---|---|---|---|
| CU-CMS point query (top-10⁴ flows) | 0.8% | 34.2% (collision flood, O1) | 1.1% |
| CU-CMS heavy hitters (φ = 10⁻⁴) | F₁ = 0.99 | F₁ = 0.41 | F₁ = 0.98 |
| HLL++ cardinality (n = 10⁶) | 0.32% RMSE | 8.7% (register targeting, O3) | 0.35% |
| t-digest q = 0.99 latency | 1.1×10⁻⁴ rel. | 2.3×10⁻² (ordered insert, O4) | 1.4×10⁻⁴ rel. |
| t-digest q = 0.999 latency | 3.0×10⁻⁴ rel. | 9.1×10⁻² (ordered insert, O4) | 3.8×10⁻⁴ rel. |

Key observations: (i) undefended CU-CMS collapses under collision flooding — 34% ARE from only 2×10⁵ adversarial flows against 10⁹ benign ones; (ii) ordered-insertion attacks inflate t-digest tail error by two orders of magnitude, as the centroid-fragmentation argument predicts; (iii) with sketch-switching (L = 32) and per-epoch reseeding, *all* attacked metrics return to within 2% relative of benign — the adversary's budget is spent learning randomness that expires each epoch.

Merge scalability: aggregating 16,384 HLL++ sketches took 41 ms on a single core; 16,384 t-digest stratified merges took 380 ms. CU-CMS is not merged (per-vantage-point detection, Section 6).

### 5.2 Proof sketch: defended CU-CMS

*Claim.* The robust wrapper of Section 4.4 around CU-CMS with parameters (w, d) answers every point query with f̂ᵢ ≥ fᵢ and f̂ᵢ ≤ fᵢ + ε‖f‖₁ except with probability δ, against any adaptive adversary.

*Proof sketch.* Fix epoch ℓ with active copy c_ℓ. The adversary's stream segment in epoch ℓ is a function of outputs from epochs < ℓ only, hence *independent* of c_ℓ's hash seeds — the oblivious-adversary setting holds within the epoch for the active copy [7]. Apply the CU guarantee [2] with failure δ/L per epoch; union-bound over L epochs. ∎ The same template covers HLL++ (concentration of the harmonic estimator [3][4]) and t-digest (merge-constraint theorem [5] plus stratified-merge order-independence).

## 6 Limitations

1. **CU-CMS is not mergeable.** Conservative update's state-dependence breaks linearity: the sum of CU sketches is not the CU sketch of the union. Our architecture therefore runs heavy-hitter detection *per vantage point*, shipping only candidate keys (with vanilla-CMS cross-checks) to the aggregator. Turnstile streams with deletions are likewise unsupported by CU [1].
2. **Sketch-switching multiplies memory by the flip number.** For rapidly oscillating quantities (λ large) the O(λ) overhead can exceed the sketch itself; our telemetry queries have small λ, but this is not universal.
3. **t-digest without stratified merge stays fragile under adversarial order**: the naive clustering variant degrades badly (Table 5.1). The defense is algorithmic hygiene, not a theorem about the naive variant.
4. **Side channels.** Update timing (CU's variable work per arrival) and export sizes (HLL++ sparse vs. dense) leak information about traffic mix; constant-time variants are open engineering work.
5. **Keyed hashing costs** ~2–5 ns/hash extra versus non-cryptographic hashes; at 10⁸ packets/s per core this measured under 4% throughput loss in our benchmarks.
6. **The DP dashboard path adds noise** calibrated to sensitivity/ε_DP; operators must budget this into alert thresholds.

## 7 Conclusion

This thesis showed that the three workhorse structures of probabilistic telemetry — Count-Min with conservative update, HyperLogLog++, and the t-digest — each carry a distinct adversarial weak point (row-collision hill-climbing, linear-counting register targeting, ordered-insertion centroid fragmentation), and that a single defensive principle, *sketch-switching with per-epoch hash reseeding*, neutralizes all three at logarithmic memory cost while preserving each structure's signature strength: one-sided frequency bounds, exact mergeability, and relative-error tail quantiles. Our 1.2-billion-record evaluation confirms the theory: undefended accuracy collapses 30–90% under white-box attack; defended accuracy stays within 2% of benign. For operators, the summary is short: key your hashes, reseed every export interval, rotate sketch copies, and merge with stratified discipline.
