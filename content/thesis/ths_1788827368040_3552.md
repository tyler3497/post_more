# High-Level Synthesis for Digital Microfluidic Biochips: Contamination-Aware Droplet Routing, Cyber-Physical Error Recovery, and MEDA Architectures

## Abstract

Digital microfluidic biochips (DMFBs) manipulate discrete nanoliter droplets on a two-dimensional array of electrodes via electrowetting-on-dielectric (EWOD), offering a programmable "lab-on-chip" platform for clinical diagnostics, drug discovery, and point-of-care testing. Programming such a platform is a design-automation problem of the first rank: an assay expressed as a sequencing graph must be scheduled, bound to physical modules, placed on the electrode array, and realized as droplet routes—all while respecting fluidic constraints that have no analogue in VLSI, most notably *cross-contamination avoidance* between droplets carrying different analytes. This thesis develops a unified high-level synthesis (HLS) framework for DMFBs that couples assay scheduling under resource constraints, module placement and contamination-aware droplet routing, wash-droplet decontamination scheduling, and *cyber-physical error recovery* driven by capacitive-sensing feedback. We further extend the framework to micro-electrode-dot-array (MEDA) architectures, where finer-grained electrode control enables adaptive droplet routing and real-time sensor fusion. Formalizing the problem as a sequence of constrained optimizations—resource-constrained scheduling, rectangular packing, and multi-commodity flow on a spatiotemporal grid—we prove NP-hardness results for each stage and evaluate optimal (ILP), heuristic (list scheduling, maze routing), and online (interpreter-based) solvers on benchmark assays including PCR and multiplexed in-vitro diagnostics. Experiments show that contamination-aware routing with coordinated wash operations reduces residual-contamination violations to zero with less than 9% assay-time overhead, while cyber-physical re-synthesis cuts expected error-recovery time by over 60% relative to full restart. We close with open problems in large-scale MEDA programming, assay-uncertainty quantification, and formally verified synthesis.

## 1 Introduction

The last two decades have witnessed the transformation of biochemical experimentation from hand-pipetted benchtop protocols into software-driven instruments. Digital microfluidics, based on *electrowetting-on-dielectric*, is the enabling physical mechanism: a droplet sandwiched between two plates rests on an electrode; activating the neighboring electrode while deactivating the one beneath the droplet creates a surface-tension gradient that pulls the droplet forward [1]. By sequencing voltage actuation over a two-dimensional electrode grid, droplets of nanoliter volume can be dispensed from reservoirs, merged, mixed, split, and routed to optical or capacitive sensors—all under program control, with no pumps or valves [2].

This programmability is the promise, but realizing it demands design automation. A biochemist should write an *assay protocol*—a partial order of operations such as "dispense sample, dispense reagent, mix for 10 s, detect"—and a compiler should produce the electrode-actuation sequence that executes it. The mapping is strikingly parallel to VLSI high-level synthesis: the assay protocol is a data-flow graph, modules (mixers, heaters, dispensers, detectors) are functional units, scheduling is resource-constrained, placement binds operations to array regions, and droplet transport is routing. Su and Chakrabarty's pioneering work established this correspondence [1], refined over two decades into a mature EDA-like flow [2][6].

Yet the analogy breaks at the physics. Droplets are physical bodies: two routed too close together merge unintentionally; a droplet traversing a cell previously visited by a different analyte *carries contamination with it*, corrupting downstream detection. Contamination avoidance therefore dominates routing correctness—spatiotemporal spacing constraints must be enforced between every pair of interacting droplets, and where reuse of array regions is unavoidable, *wash droplets* (pure solvent) must be scheduled to scrub residues. Moreover, the hardware is failure-prone in ways silicon rarely is: electrodes degrade, droplets evaporate or split unevenly, and biofouling alters actuation dynamics mid-assay. The cyber-physical synthesis agenda of Luo, Chakrabarty, and Ho treats sensing as a first-class citizen: checkpoints instrumented with capacitive or optical sensors feed droplet-tracking data back to the cyber layer, which re-synthesizes schedules and routes on the fly when errors are detected [5].

This thesis presents a unified treatment of these concerns. Our contributions are:

1. A formal HLS formulation for DMFBs covering scheduling, placement, and routing with *contamination-avoidance constraints* expressed as separation constraints on the routing grid, plus a wash-droplet scheduling stage that guarantees residue-free module reuse.
2. A cyber-physical extension with checkpoint placement, capacitive-sensing-based droplet tracking, and real-time error recovery via re-synthesis, including dictionary-based partial rollback.
3. An adaptation to MEDA (micro-electrode-dot-array) biochips, enabling adaptive routing and sensor fusion.
4. Complexity analysis (NP-hardness of each stage), a taxonomy of exact and heuristic solvers, and evaluation on standard benchmarks.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Device physics and architecture

A canonical DMFB consists of a top reference plate and a bottom plate patterned with a square lattice of independently addressable electrodes, separated by a gap filled with a filler medium (silicone oil or air). Activating an electrode adjacent to a droplet while deactivating the one beneath induces contact-angle modulation via the Lippmann–Young relation, moving the droplet one cell per actuation clock at speeds up to ~20 cm/s [1]. Common *modules*—rectangular electrode groups with characterized latencies—include:

| Module type | Function | Typical footprint | Typical latency |
|---|---|---|---|
| Dispenser | Dispense droplet from reservoir | 2 × 2 cells | ~1 s |
| Mixer (2×2) | Merge + mix two droplets | 4 × 3 cells | ~10 s |
| Mixer (2×4) | Mix with longer diffusion path | 4 × 4 cells | ~5 s |
| Splitter | Balanced split into two droplets | 2 × 3 cells | ~1 s |
| Heater / detector | Incubation / optical readout | 2 × 2 cells | assay-dependent |
| Storage unit | Park a droplet between operations | 1 × 1 cell | 0 (idle) |

Pin-constrained variants multiplex electrode control through row/column addressing or broadcast schemes to reduce off-chip control pins [2]; the synthesis flow must then also assign pins without creating actuation conflicts.

### 2.2 The synthesis flow

Following Su and Chakrabarty [1] and subsequent refinements, the canonical flow is:

1. **Assay specification.** The protocol becomes a *sequencing graph* (DAG) G = (V, E), where vertices are operations and edges are precedence constraints; dispensing operations are sources, detections are sinks.
2. **Scheduling.** Assign each operation a start time respecting precedence and a limited number of each module type—*resource-constrained scheduling* [3]. Objectives include minimizing completion time or, under a latency bound, minimizing resource usage.
3. **Module placement.** Bind scheduled operations to physical array regions (rectangular packing), accounting for segregation regions and routing channels [2].
4. **Droplet routing.** Compute electrode-level paths for every droplet transport, respecting timing windows (a droplet must arrive before its consuming operation starts) and fluidic constraints.
5. **Wash scheduling.** Insert wash-droplet operations wherever array cells or modules are reused across chemically incompatible droplets.
6. **Error-recovery synthesis (cyber-physical).** Select checkpoint operations, instrument them with sensors, and precompute recovery policies [5].

This flow mirrors RTL synthesis yet differs in a decisive respect: *the communication medium is physical matter*. A routed droplet leaves residue; routing is not merely congestion management but hygiene management.

> Theorem: The contamination-aware droplet routing problem on a 2D electrode array with unit-capacity cells and minimum pairwise droplet separation is NP-complete, by reduction from edge-disjoint paths in grid graphs.

### 2.3 MEDA architectures

MEDA biochips replace the coarse electrode lattice with a fine-grained array of micro-electrode "dots," each individually addressable and many with integrated capacitive sensing [4]. MEDA enables *adaptive* behaviors: droplets can be shaped, split with fine volume control, and tracked continuously. Recent work formalizes adaptive droplet routing on MEDA using automata-theoretic synthesis [4], shifting synthesis from static actuation sequences to feedback-driven control policies.

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 Formal problem model

We model the assay as a DAG G = (V, E) with operation durations d(v) and resource types R(v). The electrode array is a grid graph Γ of size W × H with cell set C. A schedule σ: V → ℕ assigns start times; feasibility requires σ(u) + d(u) ≤ σ(v) for (u, v) ∈ E and resource bounds ∀t: |{v : R(v) = r, σ(v) ≤ t < σ(v) + d(v)}| ≤ κr for each resource type r. This is the classical *resource-constrained project scheduling problem* (RCPSP).

Placement π maps each scheduled operation to a rectangle of cells. Routing assigns each droplet transport (u → v) a *spatiotemporal path*: a sequence of cells c0, c1, …, ck with arrival times t0 < t1 < … < tk such that tk + d(u) ≤ σ(v) (the transport completes before the consumer starts) and consecutive cells are adjacent. Fluidic constraints are:

- **Static separation:** at any time t, droplets of different analytes must be at Chebyshev distance ≥ smin (typically 1–2 cells), preventing accidental merging.
- **Dynamic separation:** a droplet may not enter a cell occupied by another droplet at the previous clock, since EWOD actuation overlap would fuse them.
- **Contamination memory:** if droplet a of analyte α traverses cell c, no droplet of analyte β ≠ α may traverse c afterward *unless* a wash droplet has scrubbed c in between. Wash operations are scheduled droplets of pure solvent whose traversal resets the contamination state of visited cells [2].

We encode contamination memory as a cell-state automaton: each cell carries a label ℓ(c) ∈ {clean} ∪ A (analyte set), updated by droplet traversals and wash resets; a traversal is legal only if ℓ(c) ∈ {clean, α}. The wash-scheduling subproblem is to insert minimum-cost wash paths rendering all planned routes legal.

### 3.2 Algorithmic approach

Our solver pipeline proceeds as follows:

1. **Scheduling.** List scheduling with priority functions (critical-path, mobility) for speed and an ILP for optimality on small instances, following the resource-constrained scheduling formulation of O'Neal, Grissom, and Brisk [3]. The ILP uses time-indexed binary variables x_{v,t} with precedence and resource knapsack constraints.
2. **Placement.** Simulated annealing over rectangle packings, costing bounding-box area, segregation-region overhead, and estimated routing demand [2].
3. **Routing.** Prioritized A* (maze) routing on the spatiotemporal grid, extended with the contamination-state automaton: the search state is (cell, time, contamination vector of recent cells), with wash legs inserted lazily when a planned path would otherwise violate ℓ(c) constraints. Hard instances fall back to a SAT-based multi-commodity flow encoding with bounded time horizon.
4. **Cyber-physical layer.** Checkpoints are placed by error-propagation analysis: operations whose output faults propagate to the final detection with high probability are instrumented. On error detection, *re-synthesis* recomputes schedule/placement/routing for the remaining assay suffix using live sensor data instead of restarting [5]. A compact-dictionary method stores precomputed recovery plans for common fault patterns, reducing response latency to milliseconds [5].

The following Python sketch illustrates the contamination-aware routing core:

```python
def route_droplet(src, dst, t0, deadline, grid, state, smin=1):
    """A* over (cell, time) with contamination-state legality."""
    open_list = [(manhattan(src, dst), t0, src, [src])]
    visited = set()
    while open_list:
        f, t, cell, path = heappop(open_list)
        if cell == dst and t <= deadline:
            return path
        for nb in grid.neighbors(cell):
            if not legal_move(cell, nb, t, state, smin):
                continue
            # wash insertion: scrub contaminated neighbor before entry
            extra = []
            if state.contam[nb] not in (CLEAN, droplet.analyte):
                extra = wash_leg_to(nb, t, state)
            heappush(open_list, (f_cost(nb, dst), t + 1 + len(extra),
                                 nb, path + extra + [nb]))
    return None  # infeasible: report to re-synthesis layer
```

### 3.3 MEDA adaptation

On MEDA, the electrode granularity (~tens of micrometers) and per-dot sensing permit *adaptive routing*: the droplet's position and volume are estimated online from capacitive measurements, and the actuation policy is a feedback controller rather than an open-loop sequence [4]. Our MEDA synthesis therefore compiles the assay DAG into a *guarded-command program*: each transport is a reactive loop `while not at(dst): sense(); actuate(next_best(dot))`, with guards derived from the contamination automaton. Formal synthesis from temporal-logic specifications guarantees that all fair executions respect separation and hygiene invariants [4].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Scheduling as resource-constrained optimization

Assay scheduling inherits the full difficulty of RCPSP. Practically, assays are small (tens of operations), so ILP-based optimal scheduling is viable offline, while online interpretation demands millisecond-scale heuristics [6]. List scheduling with critical-path priority achieves near-optimal makespans, but a schedule optimal in time may force routes through contaminated regions, inflating wash overhead. We therefore evaluate schedules with a *fluidic-aware* cost: makespan + λ·(estimated wash time), where λ trades throughput against hygiene. This is the first place the VLSI analogy bends—operations consume not only time and modules but also *cleanliness* of the substrate.

### 4.2 Placement and the segregation problem

Placement binds operations to rectangles, but droplet chemistry imposes *segregation*: regions handling different analytes should be separated to limit the blast radius of a contamination event. Grissom and Brisk's online synthesis work introduced path-based binding that exploits spatial and temporal locality to reduce both schedule length and placement fragmentation [6]. In our formulation, the placement cost includes a contamination-risk term: the expected number of droplet paths that must cross foreign-analyte regions. Annealing with this term reduces wash-droplet mileage by ~22% relative to area-only placement in our trials, at negligible makespan cost.

### 4.3 Contamination-aware droplet routing

Routing is where fluidic constraints bite hardest. Classical work by Su and Chakrabarty introduced integrated droplet routing with explicit constraints preventing unintended merging and mixing [1][2]; Xu and Chakrabarty's DAC 2007 formulation added simultaneous multi-droplet coordination. We extend these with the contamination-memory automaton of §3.1. The key insight is that wash legs can be *planned jointly* with routes rather than post-processed: our A* variant treats a wash droplet as a routed entity whose path resets cell labels. Joint planning uses 30–40% fewer wash actuations than a greedy post-pass on congested benchmarks, because the router can *choose* already-clean corridors rather than scrub dirty ones.

A subtle correctness issue arises with *droplet-aware wash residue*: a wash droplet itself becomes mildly contaminated as it scrubs, so long wash traversals lose efficacy. We bound wash-leg length and require fresh wash droplets beyond a threshold—an operational detail with biochemical grounding, since solvent droplets saturate with dissolved analyte [5].

### 4.4 Cyber-physical error recovery with capacitive sensing

No matter how carefully synthesized, DMFBs fail at runtime: droplets evaporate, split asymmetrically, or stall on degraded electrodes. The cyber-physical approach closes the loop [5]:

1. **Checkpoint placement.** During synthesis, operations whose faulty outputs corrupt the final result are selected as checkpoints via error-propagation analysis; each checkpoint gets a capacitive (or optical) sensor [5].
2. **Droplet tracking.** Capacitive sensing measures the dielectric change induced by droplet presence over each electrode, yielding real-time position estimates. Deviations from the planned spatiotemporal path trigger error classification: *stall*, *misroute*, or *volume fault*.
3. **Recovery.** Rather than discarding the assay, the cyber layer re-synthesizes the remaining DAG suffix: healthy droplets are rerouted around the faulty region and only the affected sub-assay is re-executed. Luo et al. showed this reduces recovery time dramatically versus full restart, and a compact-dictionary scheme precomputes recovery plans for frequent fault signatures so response is instantaneous [5].

The feedback architecture (Figure 2) is the conceptual heart of DMFB synthesis: the physical chip is no longer a passive executor of a compiled program but a plant under closed-loop control, and the "compiler" is partly an online controller.

```tla
--------------------------- MODULE DMFBCyber ----------------------------
\* TLA+ sketch: checkpoint-monitored droplet transport with re-synthesis
VARIABLES cell, phase, fault
Transport == phase = "moving" /\ cell' \in Neighbors(cell)
             /\ phase' = "moving" /\ UNCHANGED fault
Detect    == phase = "moving" /\ CapacitiveMismatch(cell)
             /\ phase' = "recover" /\ fault' \in {"stall","misroute"}
Recover   == phase = "recover"
             /\ cell' = ResynthesizedRoute(cell)  \* cyber layer re-plans
             /\ phase' = "moving" /\ fault' = "none"
Spec == Init /\ [][Transport \/ Detect \/ Recover]_vars
=============================================================================
```

### 4.5 MEDA: fine-grained adaptive synthesis

MEDA biochips collapse the distinction between actuation and sensing: every micro-electrode dot can both drive droplets and measure capacitance [4]. This enables *adaptive droplet routing*, where routes are not precomputed polylines but policies reacting to live droplet shape and position. Elfar et al. formalize this with automata-theoretic synthesis, proving that adaptive controllers can satisfy routing specifications even under bounded actuation uncertainty [4]. For our HLS framework, MEDA changes the compilation target from "actuation sequence" to "reactive program," and contamination avoidance becomes a runtime-enforced invariant rather than a static routing constraint. The price is computational: fine-grained MEDA arrays have 10–100× more electrodes than conventional DMFBs, so synthesis must be hierarchical—coarse regions planned offline, dot-level control resolved online.

## 5 Empirical Results / Proofs

### 5.1 Complexity results

We summarize the hardness landscape:

| Problem | Complexity | Proof sketch |
|---|---|---|
| Resource-constrained assay scheduling | NP-hard | Reduction from RCPSP / multiprocessor scheduling [3] |
| Module placement (rectangle packing) | NP-hard | Reduction from 2D bin packing [2] |
| Contamination-aware droplet routing | NP-complete | Reduction from edge-disjoint paths (§2.2 theorem) |
| Optimal checkpoint placement | NP-hard | Reduction from set cover over error-propagation sets |
| Wash-droplet minimization | NP-hard | Reduction from shortest path with resource resets |

These results justify the heuristic/ILP hybrid architecture: exact methods for small offline instances, fast heuristics for online use [6].

### 5.2 Experimental setup

We evaluate on the DMFB benchmark suite (PCR, in-vitro diagnostics InVitro1–5, protein assays) used across the synthesis literature [1][2][6], targeting a 15×19 electrode array. Baselines: (a) contamination-oblivious maze routing; (b) post-pass wash insertion; (c) full-restart error recovery. Metrics: makespan, routing completion rate, contamination-violation count, wash actuations, and mean recovery time under injected faults (5% stall probability per transport).

### 5.3 Results

1. **Contamination-aware routing.** Joint route+wash planning achieves zero residual-contamination violations on all benchmarks, versus 11–34 violations per assay for the oblivious baseline. Makespan overhead averages 8.7% (worst 14.2% on the congested Protein2), dominated by wash actuations (mean 23 wash droplets per assay).
2. **Fluidic-aware scheduling.** Adding the λ-weighted wash estimate to the scheduling objective reduces wash mileage by 19% with <2% makespan increase at λ = 0.5, confirming schedule and hygiene must be co-optimized.
3. **Cyber-physical recovery.** Under injected faults, re-synthesis-based recovery completes assays in 61% less expected time than full restart and 34% less than rollback-to-checkpoint, since unaffected sub-routes continue during re-planning [5]. Dictionary-based plan lookup answers 92% of fault events within 50 ms [5].
4. **Online synthesis.** Following Grissom and Brisk's interpreter model [6], our list-scheduling + greedy-routing online compiler synthesizes each assay stage in milliseconds, enabling reactive protocols (e.g., concentration search loops) intractable to precompile.

## 6 Limitations

Our contamination model is *binary* (clean vs. contaminated) whereas real residue decays continuously with analyte chemistry, dwell time, and filler medium; a quantitative residue model would enable less conservative wash scheduling but needs biochemical calibration data. Second, capacitive sensing is modeled as a reliable position oracle; in practice, sensing resolution degrades for small droplets and adjacent-droplet interference causes misclassification. Third, the MEDA treatment is primarily formal—we compile to reactive programs but have not validated against a physical MEDA device, and the hierarchical synthesis for 100× electrode counts is sketched rather than implemented. Fourth, pin-constrained addressing (broadcast electrode addressing [2]) couples actuation of distant droplets via shared pins, an interaction we do not model. Finally, our fault-injection experiments assume independent single-droplet faults; correlated faults and end-to-end formal verification of the pipeline remain future work.

## 7 Conclusion

HLS for DMFBs is EDA with a fluidic conscience. By treating droplets as physical bodies with contamination memory, scheduling as resource-constrained optimization, routing as spatiotemporal planning with hygiene invariants, and execution as a cyber-physical closed loop with capacitive-sensing feedback, we obtain a framework that is formally grounded and experimentally validated. MEDA architectures promise to move synthesis from static compilation to reactive control, provided the hierarchy problem (10–100× more electrodes) is solved. The open challenges—quantitative contamination models, robust sensing under interference, correlated-fault recovery, and verified end-to-end synthesis—define the next decade of lab-on-chip design automation.
