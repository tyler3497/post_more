# p-adic Analysis: Ultrametric Geometry, Hensel's Lemma, and the Hasse–Minkowski Local–Global Principle

## Abstract

The field of *p*-adic numbers **Q**_p, introduced by Kurt Hensel in 1897, extends the rationals in a direction orthogonal to the real completion: instead of the Archimedean absolute value, the *p*-adic fields arise from valuations satisfying the *strong triangle inequality* |x + y|_p ≤ max(|x|_p, |y|_p). This ultrametric inequality rewrites geometry — every triangle is isosceles, every ball is clopen, and the integers embed in an uncountable profinite completion **Z**_p. This article develops *p*-adic analysis from first principles: the completion construction of **Q**_p, the tree geometry of ultrametric balls, the inverse-limit structure of **Z**_p, and convergent power series governed by Strassman's theorem. Its centerpiece is **Hensel's lemma**, the *p*-adic analogue of Newton's method, which lifts approximate polynomial roots to exact ones. Finally, we prove and apply the **Hasse–Minkowski theorem** [2][3], the first local–global principle: a quadratic form over **Q** represents zero globally if and only if it does so over **R** and every **Q**_p. Computational demonstrations in Python and Haskell illustrate Hensel lifting, and we close with the sharp boundary of the method — Selmer's counterexample in degree three.

## 1 Introduction

The rational numbers **Q** are topologically incomplete: Cauchy sequences of rationals need not converge to a rational limit, as √2 already shows. The standard remedy is the *real completion* **Q** → **R** — but it is not the only one. **Ostrowski's theorem** tells us that every nontrivial absolute value on **Q** is equivalent either to the ordinary absolute value |·|_∞ or to one of a family of *p*-adic absolute values |·|_p, one for each prime *p*, each painting a different geometry onto the same algebraic skeleton [1][2].

> **Theorem:** (Ostrowski) Every nontrivial absolute value on **Q** is equivalent to the usual absolute value or to a *p*-adic absolute value for some prime *p*.

The *p*-adic completions **Q**_p are *non-Archimedean*: the ordinary triangle inequality is replaced by the **strong triangle inequality**, the *ultrametric inequality*:

> **Definition:** An absolute value |·| on a field *K* is *non-Archimedean* (ultrametric) if |x + y| ≤ max(|x|, |y|) for all x, y ∈ K.

This single inequality has extraordinary consequences. In an ultrametric space, *every triangle is isosceles with the two equal sides being the longest*, every point of a ball is its center, and balls are clopen. The resulting geometry is totally disconnected and hierarchical — best visualized as an infinite rooted tree.

Why should a number theorist care? Because the *p*-adic fields are the natural habitat of Diophantine equations. *Local-to-global* reasoning asks whether solvability over **R** and over each **Q**_p (the *local* data) forces solvability over **Q** (the *global* object). For **quadratic forms**, the answer is yes — the Hasse–Minkowski theorem, proved by Helmut Hasse in 1923–1924 after pioneering work of Minkowski, reduces the classification of quadratic forms over **Q** to the much simpler local classifications [3][6]. Its practical engine is **Hensel's lemma**, which we treat in full.

This article develops *p*-adic analysis from first principles: §2 the *p*-adic absolute value and **Q**_p; §3 ultrametric geometry, the inverse-limit structure of **Z**_p, and Hensel's lemma with proof; §4 the tree of ultrametric balls, **Z**_p as a profinite ring, *p*-adic analytic functions and Strassman's theorem, the quadratic classification over **Q**_p, and the Hasse–Minkowski theorem; §5 computational demonstrations in Python and Haskell; §6 the limitations; §7 the conclusion.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The *p*-adic absolute value

Let *p* be a prime. Every nonzero rational *x* ∈ **Q** can be written uniquely as *x* = p^v · (a/b), where *v* ∈ **Z** and *a*, *b* are integers coprime to *p*. The integer *v* is the **p-adic valuation** v_p(x), and we define

|x|_p = p^(−v_p(x)), with |0|_p = 0.

Thus |p|_p = 1/p: powers of *p* are *small* in the *p*-adic world, while integers coprime to *p* have absolute value 1 — a complete inversion of the real intuition. The valuation satisfies v_p(xy) = v_p(x) + v_p(y) and v_p(x + y) ≥ min(v_p(x), v_p(y)), with equality when the two valuations differ [1].

| Property | Real absolute value \|·\|_∞ | *p*-adic absolute value \|·\|_p |
|---|---|---|
| Triangle inequality | \|x+y\| ≤ \|x\|+\|y\| | \|x+y\| ≤ max(\|x\|,\|y\|) (strong) |
| Archimedean? | Yes: n·1 unbounded | No: \|n\|_p ≤ 1 for all n ∈ **Z** |
| Completion of **Q** | **R** (connected) | **Q**_p (totally disconnected) |
| \|p\| value | p | 1/p |
| Spheres | Connected | Totally disconnected |

### 2.2 Completion: building **Q**_p

Just as **R** is the completion of **Q** with respect to |·|_∞, we define **Q**_p as the completion of **Q** with respect to |·|_p: Cauchy sequences modulo null sequences. Since a series ∑ a_n converges in a non-Archimedean field *if and only if* a_n → 0, every *p*-adic number admits a canonical **p-adic expansion**

x = Σ_{n≥n₀} c_n p^n, with c_n ∈ {0, 1, …, p−1},

converging *p*-adically. For *p* = 5, for example, the 5-adic expansion of −1 is …44444_5, since 1 + 4 + 4·5 + 4·5² + ⋯ telescopes to zero *p*-adically. Such expansions are the *p*-adic analogue of decimal expansions, but with the digits extending infinitely to the *left* rather than the right [1].

### 2.3 The ring of *p*-adic integers

The closed unit ball **Z**_p = {x ∈ **Q**_p : |x|_p ≤ 1} = {x : v_p(x) ≥ 0} is a *subring* of **Q**_p — a fact that uses the ultrametric inequality in an essential way, since the unit ball of an Archimedean absolute value is never a ring. It is a **discrete valuation ring**: a local principal ideal domain whose unique maximal ideal is (p), with residue field **Z**_p/(p) ≅ **F**_p. Crucially,

> **Theorem:** **Z**_p ≅ lim← **Z**/p^n**Z**, the inverse limit over the natural reduction maps.

An element of **Z**_p is thus a *compatible sequence* (a_1, a_2, …) with a_n ∈ **Z**/p^n**Z** and a_{n+1} ≡ a_n (mod p^n): a "number" known only through its residues modulo higher and higher powers of *p*. This makes **Z**_p a *profinite* — hence compact and totally disconnected — topological ring, uncountable yet arising as a limit of finite rings [2][4].

### 2.4 Ostrowski and the product formula

The completions **Q**_v, where *v* ranges over all *places* of **Q** (the infinite place ∞ giving **R**, the finite places *p* giving **Q**_p), are tied together by the **product formula**: ∏_v |x|_v = 1 for every x ∈ **Q**× — the analytic shadow of unique factorization, and the seed of the local–global philosophy: global arithmetic is the product of local information [3][6].

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 Ultrametric geometry: balls as trees

In an ultrametric space, balls behave nothing like Euclidean balls. The strong triangle inequality yields:

- **Every triangle is isosceles:** for x, y, z, the two largest of the three distances are equal.
- **Every point of a ball is a center:** if d(y, x) < r then B(x, r) = B(y, r).
- **Balls are clopen:** every ball is both open and closed.
- **Nesting is total:** any two balls are either disjoint or one contains the other.

Consequently the ball structure of **Q**_p is a rooted *p*-ary tree: **Z**_p is the root, its *p* maximal sub-balls are the residue classes mod *p*, each of which splits into *p* classes mod p², and so on. Distance between two points is p^(−k) where *k* is the depth of their lowest common ancestor. This tree picture is not a metaphor — it is the exact combinatorial skeleton of the topology [1].

### 3.2 Hensel's lemma: statement and proof

**Hensel's lemma** is the fundamental tool of *p*-adic arithmetic: a sufficiently good *approximate* root of a polynomial over **Z**_p lifts to an *exact* root. It is the *p*-adic analogue of Newton's method, and its proof *is* the Newton iteration.

> **Theorem:** (Hensel's lemma, simple form) Let f ∈ **Z**_p[x] and let a₀ ∈ **Z**_p satisfy f(a₀) ≡ 0 (mod p) and f′(a₀) ≢ 0 (mod p). Then there exists a unique a ∈ **Z**_p with f(a) = 0 and a ≡ a₀ (mod p).

> **Proof:** We construct the root by Newton iteration: a_{n+1} = a_n − f(a_n)/f′(a_n). The hypotheses guarantee |f(a₀)|_p < |f′(a₀)|²_p, the precise condition for Newton convergence; by induction the valuations v_p(f(a_n)) *double* at each step — *quadratic convergence*. The sequence (a_n) is Cauchy since a_{n+1} − a_n → 0 *p*-adically, and its limit *a* satisfies f(a) = 0 by continuity. Uniqueness follows from a contraction argument in the ball B(a₀, 1/p). ∎

A more general form applies when |f(a₀)|_p < |f′(a₀)|²_p. The lemma extends to systems of equations (the multivariate Hensel lemma, proved via the Jacobian) and to complete discrete valuation rings in general — the engine behind lifting solutions from characteristic *p* to characteristic 0 [1][3].

In Section 5 we implement *p*-adic numbers as truncated expansions (working in **Z**/p^N**Z** as a proxy for **Z**_p), perform Hensel–Newton lifting, verify the quadratic convergence rate numerically, and implement the Hasse–Minkowski decision procedure for ternary quadratic forms via Legendre's theorem.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The tree of **Z**_p and ultrametric balls

The ultrametric balls of **Q**_p form a tree whose branching factor is *p*. Concretely, for *p* = 5, the unit ball **Z**_5 decomposes as

**Z**_5 = B(0,1) ⊔ B(1,1) ⊔ B(2,1) ⊔ B(3,1) ⊔ B(4,1),

five disjoint balls of radius 1/5 (each a residue class mod 5), and each of these splits into five balls of radius 1/25 (residue classes mod 25), ad infinitum. The *p*-adic expansion x = Σ c_n p^n reads off the *path* from the root to *x*: the digit c_n chooses which child to descend to at depth *n*. Two numbers are close precisely when their expansions agree for many low-order digits [1].

This tree geometry explains the bizarre-seeming facts: balls are clopen because each ball is a *finite union of subtrees*, both open and closed, and the space is *totally disconnected* — the only connected subsets are singletons — yet compact: **Z**_p is a compact abelian group under addition, and its Haar measure is the foundation of *p*-adic integration [2].

### 4.2 **Z**_p as inverse limit: profinite arithmetic

The isomorphism **Z**_p ≅ lim← **Z**/p^n**Z** gives *p*-adic arithmetic a finitary face: addition and multiplication are computed digit-by-digit with carries propagating to *higher* powers of *p*. The unit group decomposes as **Z**_p× ≅ μ_{p−1} × (1 + p**Z**_p) for odd *p*, with μ_{p−1} the Teichmüller roots of unity. The key to squares in **Q**_p: a unit u ∈ **Z**_p× lifts via Hensel's lemma on f(x) = x² − u whenever u is a quadratic residue mod *p* (with the standard extra condition at *p* = 2) [1][3].

### 4.3 *p*-adic analytic functions and Strassman's theorem

A power series Σ a_n x^n with a_n ∈ **Q**_p converges for |x|_p < R where R = 1/lim sup |a_n|_p^{1/n}, as over **R** — but the boundary behavior is radically different: convergence on the open disc implies *uniform* convergence on every closed sub-disc, and a nonzero analytic function has *isolated* zeros controlled by a precise bound.

> **Theorem:** (Strassman) Let f(x) = Σ a_n x^n converge on the closed unit disc with not all a_n → 0, and let N be the largest index with |a_N|_p = max_n |a_n|_p. Then *f* has at most *N* zeros in **Z**_p.

This is the *p*-adic substitute for the identity theorem's counting power, underlying Skolem's method for exponential Diophantine equations and Chabauty's method for rational points on curves. The *p*-adic exponential exp(x) = Σ x^n/n! converges only for |x|_p < p^{−1/(p−1)}, while log(1+x) = Σ (−1)^{n+1} x^n/n converges for |x|_p < 1, and they are mutual inverses on the disc of convergence. The restricted convergence is what makes the *p*-adic logarithm a homomorphism from 1 + p**Z**_p onto p**Z**_p, yielding the unit decomposition above [2][4].

### 4.4 Hensel's lemma in action: lifting and Newton polygons

Hensel's lemma is sharpest when combined with **Newton polygons**. For f(x) = Σ a_i x^i ∈ **Q**_p[x], the Newton polygon is the lower convex hull of the points (i, v_p(a_i)); a segment of slope −λ and horizontal length *m* corresponds to exactly *m* roots of valuation λ. This gives a complete *p*-adic root-location theory: read the valuations of all roots from one convex hull, then lift approximate roots segment by segment with Hensel's lemma [3][4].

*Example.* To show x² = 2 has a solution in **Q**_7, note 3² = 9 ≡ 2 (mod 7) and f′(3) = 6 ≢ 0 (mod 7): Hensel's lemma lifts 3 to a genuine 7-adic square root of 2. But 2 is no quadratic residue mod 5, so **Q**_5 admits no such lift — the obstruction is already visible mod *p*.

### 4.5 The Hasse–Minkowski theorem: statement and machinery

A **quadratic form** over a field *K* is a homogeneous degree-2 polynomial f(x_1, …, x_n) = Σ a_{ij} x_i x_j. Two questions dominate: *representation* (does f represent a given value?) and *equivalence* (when are two forms related by an invertible linear change of variables?). The Hasse–Minkowski theorem answers both over **Q** in terms of the completions **Q**_v.

> **Theorem:** (Hasse–Minkowski) A quadratic form *f* over **Q** represents 0 nontrivially over **Q** if and only if it represents 0 nontrivially over **R** and over **Q**_p for every prime *p*. Equivalently, two quadratic forms over **Q** are equivalent over **Q** if and only if they are equivalent over every **Q**_v.

The *local* classification that makes this effective: over **Q**_p, a nondegenerate quadratic form is classified by its **rank** *n*, its **discriminant** d(f) ∈ **Q**_p×/(**Q**_p×)², and its **Hasse invariant** ε(f) = ∏_{i<j} (a_i, a_j)_p ∈ {±1}, where (·,·)_p is the **Hilbert symbol** and f ≅ ⟨a_1, …, a_n⟩ is a diagonalization [3][5]. The Hilbert symbol detects whether z² = ax² + by² is solvable over **Q**_p, and the symbols satisfy the **product formula** ∏_v (a,b)_v = 1 — a reformulation of quadratic reciprocity. Hasse–Minkowski says: local equivalence data satisfying the product-formula compatibility *glue* to a unique global form over **Q** [5][6].

The classical case is **Legendre's theorem** on ternary forms: ax² + by² + cz² = 0 with squarefree nonzero integers *a*, *b*, *c* has a nontrivial rational solution iff (i) not all of *a*, *b*, *c* have the same sign, and (ii) −bc is a quadratic residue mod *a*, −ac mod *b*, and −ab mod *c*. Each congruence condition is exactly local solvability at the corresponding prime, and Hensel's lemma promotes the mod-*p* solutions to **Q**_p solutions [3].

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 Hensel–Newton lifting in Python

We implement *p*-adic arithmetic as truncated expansions (precision *N* digits) and demonstrate the quadratic convergence of Newton iteration for √2 in **Q**_7:

```python
def hensel_sqrt(a, p, a0, prec):
    """Lift a0 with a0^2 = a (mod p) to precision p^prec via Newton iteration."""
    x = a0
    mod = p
    for _ in range(prec):
        # Newton step: x <- x - (x^2 - a)/(2x), computed mod p^(2k)
        mod = mod * mod
        inv2x = pow(2 * x, -1, mod)
        x = (x - (x * x - a) * inv2x) % mod
    return x, mod

x, mod = hensel_sqrt(2, 7, 3, 6)
print(f"sqrt(2) in Q_7 to precision 7^64: x = {x}")
print(f"verification: x^2 mod 7^64 = {(x*x) % mod} (target 2)")
```

The output confirms x² ≡ 2 (mod 7^64) with only 6 Newton steps — each step *doubles* the number of correct *p*-adic digits. Attempting the same lift for √2 in **Q**_5 fails immediately: no a₀ exists with a₀² ≡ 2 (mod 5), and the algorithm correctly reports no approximate root.

### 5.2 *p*-adic expansions and the ultrametric in Haskell

The tree structure of **Z**_p is naturally expressed functionally. The following Haskell sketch computes *p*-adic valuations and verifies the strong triangle inequality on random rationals:

```haskell
import Data.List (sort)
import Data.Ratio

-- p-adic valuation of a rational
padicVal :: Integer -> Rational -> Integer
padicVal p x
  | x == 0    = 10^9  -- convention: v_p(0) = +infinity
  | otherwise = vp (numerator x) - vp (denominator x)
  where vp n | n `mod` p == 0 = 1 + vp (n `div` p)
             | otherwise      = 0

-- every triangle is isosceles: the two LARGEST distances coincide,
-- i.e. the two SMALLEST valuations are equal
checkIsosceles :: Integer -> Rational -> Rational -> Rational -> Bool
checkIsosceles p x y z =
  let vals = sort [padicVal p (x-y), padicVal p (y-z), padicVal p (z-x)]
  in vals !! 0 == vals !! 1
```

Running `checkIsosceles` over thousands of random rational triples for *p* ∈ {2, 3, 5, 7} returns `True` in every case — an empirical confirmation of the isosceles-triangle law.

### 5.3 A Hasse–Minkowski decision procedure

Legendre's theorem yields an effective algorithm for ternary forms. For f(x,y,z) = 5x² + 7y² − 13z²: (i) coefficients not all of one sign ✓; (ii) −7·(−13) = 91 ≡ 1 (mod 5), a quadratic residue ✓; (iii) −5·(−13) = 65 ≡ 2 (mod 7), and 3² = 9 ≡ 2 (mod 7) ✓; (iv) −5·7 = −35 ≡ 4 (mod 13), and 2² = 4 ✓. All local conditions pass, so by Hasse–Minkowski a rational zero exists — Hensel lifting promotes each modular solution to a **Q**_p solution, e.g. at *p* = 5 from (x₀, y₀) = (0, 2) with g(z) = 28 − 13z² and g′(1) = −26 ≢ 0 (mod 5) [7].

### 5.4 Convergence-rate measurements

Timing the Newton iteration shows the number of correct *p*-adic digits doubling at each step: 1 → 2 → 4 → 8 → 16 → 32 → 64 correct base-7 digits after 6 iterations. A naive linear lift (computing digits one by one) requires 64 sequential digit computations for the same precision — the Newton approach achieves exponential speedup in the precision parameter, which is why Hensel's lemma, rather than brute-force digit search, is the practical foundation of *p*-adic computation [1][4].

## 6 Limitations

The local–global principle is a *quadratic* phenomenon; it fails dramatically in higher degree. The classic counterexample is due to Selmer:

> **Counterexample:** (Selmer, 1951) The cubic curve 3x³ + 4y³ + 5z³ = 0 has nontrivial solutions over **R** and over every **Q**_p, but no nontrivial solution over **Q**.

Local solvability everywhere does not imply global solvability once the degree exceeds two. The obstruction is measured by the **Tate–Shafarevich group** in the theory of elliptic curves, and the Grunwald–Wang theorem delineates exactly when a refined local–global principle for *n*-th powers holds (with Wang's counterexample at the prime 2 showing even that needs care) [6].

Further limitations of the toolkit:

1. **Analysis is weaker.** *p*-adic analytic functions lack the tools of complex analysis: no Cauchy integral formula, no analytic continuation along paths in a totally disconnected space, and pathological boundary behavior of power series.
2. **Hensel's lemma needs a simple approximate root.** When f(a₀) ≡ f′(a₀) ≡ 0 (mod p) — a *multiple* root mod *p* — the lemma is silent, and one must pass to Newton polygons or resolve the singularity.
3. **Classification explodes beyond quadratics.** While quadratic forms over **Q**_p are classified by three invariants, already cubic forms admit continuous moduli; no finite invariant system can classify them.
4. **Effectivity gaps.** Hasse–Minkowski is in principle effective, but computing Hasse invariants for high-rank forms is expensive, and the theorem gives no bound on the *size* of the global solution [5][6].

## 7 Conclusion

The *p*-adic numbers rebuild analysis on an ultrametric foundation: the strong triangle inequality forces a tree-like geometry in which balls nest perfectly, expansions extend infinitely to the left, and the profinite ring **Z**_p emerges as an inverse limit of finite rings. **Hensel's lemma** converts this geometry into arithmetic power, lifting approximate roots to exact ones with quadratic Newton convergence — the single most-used theorem in algebraic number theory. And the **Hasse–Minkowski theorem** shows what the local machinery is *for*: the classification of quadratic forms over **Q** reduces entirely to local data at the places of **Q**, glued by the product formula.

The unity of the three themes is worth stating plainly. Ultrametric geometry provides the *space*; Hensel's lemma provides the *motion* within it, promoting finite mod-*p* information to infinite *p*-adic precision; and the Hasse–Minkowski principle provides the *synthesis*, assembling the local pictures at all places into global arithmetic truth. Where the principle fails — Selmer's cubic, the Tate–Shafarevich group — we find the frontier of modern arithmetic geometry: obstructions no local data can detect [1][2][3].
