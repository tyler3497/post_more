# Greedy Routing on Multi-Scale Proximity Graphs: Hierarchical Navigable Small Worlds for Approximate Nearest-Neighbor Retrieval, from Construction Heuristics to Filtered Search

## Abstract

Approximate nearest-neighbor search over high-dimensional vector collections underpins semantic search, recommendation, retrieval-augmented generation, and biometric identification. Hierarchical Navigable Small World (HNSW) graphs [1] recast ANN search as greedy routing over a multi-layer proximity graph whose layers are nested subsets of the data with link lengths stratified by distance scale. This thesis formalizes the insertion-time neighbor-selection heuristic as diversity-constrained edge pruning, analyzes the logarithmic complexity claim via the skip-list analogy and small-world navigability theory [6], and dissects the recall–latency Pareto frontier parameterized by construction degree \(M\) and beam width \(ef\). It further treats distance-metric geometry, product and scalar quantization [4][8], and filtered attribute-constrained retrieval [7], and proves a convergence lemma for the beam search used at query time.

## 1. Introduction

Given a dataset \(X = \{x_1, \dots, x_n\} \subset \mathbb{R}^d\) and a query vector \(q \in \mathbb{R}^d\), the \(k\)-nearest-neighbor problem asks for the \(k\) points minimizing a distance \(\delta(q, x)\). In modern workloads \(d\) ranges from 128 to 4096+ while \(n\) reaches \(10^8\)–\(10^9\); beyond \(d \gtrsim 30\), exact spatial indexes degenerate to near-linear scan. The field therefore studies *approximate* nearest-neighbor (ANN) search, trading bounded accuracy loss — measured by *recall*, the fraction of true neighbors recovered — for orders-of-magnitude latency reduction.

Three families dominate: tree/partition methods (Annoy, FAISS-IVF), quantization methods (IVF-PQ [4]), and graph methods. Since ~2016, graph-based indexes — HNSW [1], NSG, DiskANN's Vamana graph — have occupied the Pareto-optimal recall–latency frontier on standard benchmarks [3], with the sharpest advantage at high recall (≥0.95) on data with intricate local geometry, where quantization-only methods degrade.

This thesis asks: (1) what graph property makes HNSW searchable in polylogarithmic time, and under what assumptions; (2) how the insertion-time neighbor-selection heuristic shapes the degree distribution and the recall–latency frontier; (3) how distance metric, quantization, and attribute filters compose with the graph layer, and where these compositions break.

Section 2 situates HNSW among small-world networks and prior ANN techniques; Section 3 details construction and search; Section 4 dives into the five pillars (hierarchy, heuristic, routing, quantization, filtering); Section 5 covers empirical methodology, a convergence lemma, and a reported experiment; Section 6 catalogs limitations; Section 7 concludes.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 The small-world phenomenon and navigability

Watts and Strogatz (1998) showed that rewiring a few lattice edges yields short average path length with high clustering — but short *paths* do not imply *findability* by local routing. Kleinberg (2000) gave the first navigability model: on a \(d\)-dimensional grid, augment local edges with one long-range edge per node sampled with probability proportional to \(d(u,v)^{-\alpha}\); greedy routing then succeeds in \(O(\log^2 n)\) hops *if and only if* \(\alpha = d\), the unique exponent balancing edge-length scales [6]. Bringmann et al. later proved \(\Theta(\log \log n)\)-hop greedy routing with constant success probability in geometric inhomogeneous random graphs, tight up to constants [6]. This literature is HNSW's intellectual foundation: a layered graph whose edges are stratified by length scale is *routable* by a local rule.

### 2.2 Before HNSW: NSW and the flat proximity graph

Malkov et al. (2014) introduced Navigable Small World (NSW) graphs: incremental construction where each inserted point connects to its \(M\) approximate nearest neighbors among previously inserted points, found by greedy search from random entry points. Two defects were evident: **random entry points** (early query phases traverse aimlessly) and **no scale separation** (a greedy step cannot distinguish coarse long jumps from fine refinement).

HNSW [1] repairs both with a hierarchy: each element is assigned a maximum layer \(\ell\) from an exponentially decaying distribution and inserted into every layer \(0 \le i \le \ell\). The top layer is sparse with long edges; the bottom layer is dense with short edges. Search starts at a fixed entry point in the top layer, descends greedily, and finishes with a best-first (beam) search at the bottom layer.

### 2.3 Distance metrics in vector retrieval

The three standard choices are:

| Metric | Definition | Index implication |
|---|---|---|
| Euclidean (\(L_2\)) | \(\lVert q - x \rVert_2\) | Symmetric, satisfies the triangle inequality; HNSW directly indexes |
| Inner product (MIPS) | \(\langle q, x \rangle\) (maximize) | Not a metric; for unit-norm data, \(\arg\max \langle q,x \rangle = \arg\min \lVert q-x \rVert_2^2\) |
| Cosine | \(1 - \frac{\langle q,x \rangle}{\lVert q \rVert \lVert x \rVert}\) | Equivalent to Euclidean on normalized data |

A critical caveat: greedy routing assumes "closer to the query" is a monotone potential. For maximum inner-product search (MIPS) on non-normalized data, inner product is not a metric and greedy search can stall; systems normalize embeddings at ingestion or convert MIPS to Euclidean search. All guarantees in this thesis assume a true metric, typically \(L_2\).

### 2.4 Quantization and the memory wall

A billion 768-dimensional float32 vectors consume ~2.9 TB — far beyond single-node RAM. *Product quantization* (PQ) [4][8] splits \(x\) into \(M\) subvectors, runs \(k\)-means (\(K=256\)) per subspace, and stores one byte per subspace; *asymmetric distance computation* (ADC) then evaluates distances via \(M\) table lookups. *Scalar quantization* (SQ) rounds each coordinate to 8 or fewer bits, composing cleanly with SIMD distance kernels [8]. Graph indexes and quantization are increasingly combined: store the graph with PQ-compressed vectors, re-ranking top candidates with full vectors [5].

## 3. Methodology

### 3.1 Construction

HNSW construction maintains per-node adjacency lists per layer, capped at \(M\) (and \(M_{\text{max0}} = 2M\) at layer 0, which carries all fine-grained connectivity). The layer of a new element is drawn as

\[
\ell = \left\lfloor -\ln(\text{uniform}(0,1)) \cdot m_L \right\rfloor,
\]

where \(m_L\) is a normalization constant — in the reference implementation, \(m_L = 1/\ln M\), balancing layer populations so each layer holds roughly a constant fraction of the layer below [1].

Insertion of \(q\) with maximum layer \(\ell\) descends greedily from the top layer to \(\ell{+}1\) (\(ef{=}1\)), then inserts at every layer \(i \le \ell\) using a beam of width \(efConstruction\), selecting \(M\) diverse neighbors per layer and re-pruning overfull nodes with the same heuristic; if \(\ell\) exceeds the current maximum layer, \(q\) becomes the new entry point. `SEARCH_LAYER` is a best-first expansion over a dynamic candidate list of size \(ef\): pop the closest unvisited candidate, expand its neighbors, and stop when the candidate list's farthest element is closer than any unvisited frontier node (\(ef{=}1\) reduces to pure greedy hill-climbing).

> **Theorem:** *Search-layer convergence.* `SEARCH_LAYER` with candidate-list size \(ef\) terminates, and the returned set \(W\) contains the \(ef\) closest *visited* nodes to \(q\). Moreover, every node of the connected component containing the entry point is eventually visited if \(ef\) is unbounded.

*Proof sketch.* Termination follows because the visited set grows monotonically and is bounded. The stopping condition implies no unvisited node can displace any member of \(W\), since any path to an unvisited node passes through a visited frontier node. ∎

### 3.2 The neighbor-selection heuristic

The naive choice — connect to the \(M\) closest candidates — produces edges that cluster in one direction, creating *occlusion*: a node surrounded by nearby neighbors in one sector has no edges pointing elsewhere. The HNSW heuristic, `SELECT_NEIGHBORS`, implements a diversity criterion reminiscent of relative neighborhood graphs: scanning candidates in order of increasing distance to \(q\), accept a candidate only if it is *not* closer to an already-accepted neighbor than to \(q\) itself [1]. This greedy maximal-independent-set construction in the "occlusion graph" guarantees angular dispersion of edges. The shrink step applied to overfull nodes reuses the same heuristic, so the graph maintains diverse connectivity under degree pressure. Ablations in the original paper show this heuristic dominates the naive nearest-\(M\) rule at high recall and on clustered data [1].

![Neighbor selection heuristic](public/thesis/ths_1789007491491_23285-1.webp)

### 3.3 Query

Querying descends greedily from the top layer to layer 1 with \(ef{=}1\), then runs `SEARCH_LAYER` at layer 0 with a wide beam \(ef \ge K\), returning the \(K\) closest of the final candidate list. The parameter \(ef\) (the *beam width*) is the primary recall–latency knob: larger \(ef\) explores more candidates, increasing both recall and distance computations — it controls the probability that the beam contains a node in the basin of attraction of each true neighbor.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 The skip-list analogy and the logarithmic complexity claim

HNSW's layer assignment is structurally identical to a skip list: each element participates in layer \(i\) with probability decaying exponentially in \(i\), the top layers are sparse express lanes, and search descends from the sparsest to the densest layer. Malkov and Yashunin argue by analogy that query complexity scales as \(O(\log n)\) [1].

The analogy is exact for the *layer structure* but not for the *routing*: skip-list search follows deterministic forward pointers, whereas HNSW's greedy descent depends on data geometry. The rigorous navigability results [6] apply to random-graph models with specific degree and edge-length distributions — not to the HNSW heuristic on arbitrary data. In practice logarithmic scaling is well supported empirically from \(10^6\) to \(10^9\) vectors [3][4], but constants depend strongly on intrinsic dimensionality (§6). Proving polylogarithmic-hop greedy routing for HNSW insertion on data from a doubling measure remains open.

### 4.2 Degree distribution, diameter, and the role of \(M\)

The construction parameter \(M\) (typical values 8–64) caps the out-degree per layer; because edges are bidirectional and shrink steps re-prune, *in*-degree can exceed \(M\), producing heavy-tailed hubs that serve as navigational landmarks [6].

| \(M\) | Memory per node (layer 0) | Typical recall@10 regime | Build cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| 8 | ~64 B links | low-latency, recall ≤ 0.9 | fast |
| 16 | ~128 B links | balanced (default) | moderate |
| 32–64 | 256–512 B links | recall ≥ 0.99 | slow |

Increasing \(M\) densifies the graph, shrinking its diameter and hops per query, at the cost of memory and of more distance computations per hop. The product — hops × degree — is the quantity minimized on the Pareto frontier, and its optimum is dataset-dependent [3].

### 4.3 Recall–latency tradeoffs and the \(ef\) parameter

At query time the dominant tradeoff is between \(ef\) (beam width) and latency, and it is characteristically *log-linear*: each additional 0.01 of recall beyond 0.95 costs a multiplicative factor in distance computations. This is visible in every ANN-Benchmarks recall–QPS plot: graph methods trace a frontier that dominates quantization methods at high recall but converges with them near recall ≈ 0.8 [3].

A second tradeoff involves \(efConstruction\): a wider beam at build time shifts the *entire query-time frontier* outward. Recommended practice — set \(efConstruction\) generously (e.g., 200) once at build time, then tune \(ef\) per query-time SLA — exploits the fact that build cost is amortized while query cost is not [1].

### 4.4 Quantization inside the graph: PQ, SQ, and re-ranking

Storing full float32 vectors alongside the graph is often infeasible at billion scale. Three compositions are standard:

1. **Graph over PQ codes.** Beam-search distances use ADC lookups [8]; the graph is built on approximate distances. Memory drops 10–30× at slight neighbor-quality cost. Learned, routing-aware quantizers (e.g., RPQ [5]) close part of the gap by training codebooks with a loss penalizing distortion along graph-routing paths rather than raw reconstruction error.
2. **Graph over full vectors + SQ.** Scalar quantization to 8 bits halves memory with negligible recall loss on normalized embeddings and keeps distance computation a simple SIMD dot product [8].
3. **Two-stage re-ranking.** Traverse with compressed vectors, then re-score the top-\(ef\) candidates with exact vectors fetched from SSD — the architecture popularized by DiskANN [7].


### 4.5 Filtered search: attributes meet the graph

Production vector databases rarely serve pure \(k\)-NN; queries carry predicates — *"nearest product images, but only in category 'footwear' and price < $100"* [7]. Filtered ANN (FANNS) returns the \(k\) nearest among the subset satisfying the filter. **Pre-filtering** evaluates the predicate first (optimal at low selectivity, degenerates to brute force on large subsets); **post-filtering** runs ANN for \(k' \gg k\) candidates then filters (optimal at high selectivity, wasteful and incorrect without over-fetching at low selectivity); **inline/hybrid filtering** integrates the predicate into traversal, e.g., FAISS-IVF skipping non-matching entries or Weaviate/Milvus per-label approved lists [7].

Graph-native designs go further. *Filtered-DiskANN* (Gollapudi et al., 2023) builds per-label Vamana subgraphs and stitches them into a single navigable index, so traversal restricted to matching vertices stays connected and routable [7]. *ACORN* constructs a predicate-agnostic densified HNSW whose induced subgraph on any filter's matching set is itself navigable, giving selectivity-robust performance without per-label indexes [7]. The key invariant — *the induced subgraph of matching vertices must preserve greedy navigability* — is the filtered analogue of HNSW's core design principle; violating it, as naive post-filtering does at low selectivity, collapses recall.

## 5. Empirical Evaluation and Proofs

### 5.1 Benchmarking protocol

We follow the ANN-Benchmarks methodology [3]: fix a dataset and query set with precomputed ground truth; for each configuration, measure recall@\(k\) and single-thread queries-per-second (QPS); plot the Pareto frontier. Standard datasets:

| Dataset | \(n\) | \(d\) | Metric | Character |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| SIFT-1M | \(10^6\) | 128 | \(L_2\) | well-clustered, "easy" |
| GloVe-100 | \(1.18 \times 10^6\) | 100 | cosine | angular, "hard" |
| DEEP-1B | \(10^9\) | 96 | \(L_2\) | billion-scale |
| SIFT-1B | \(10^9\) | 128 | \(L_2\) | billion-scale |

A frequently repeated finding: on SIFT-like data, well-tuned HNSW (\(M{=}16\), \(efConstruction{=}200\)) reaches recall@10 ≈ 0.99 at several thousand QPS per core, while on GloVe the same configuration needs substantially larger \(ef\) — evidence that intrinsic dimensionality, not ambient \(d\), governs difficulty [3].

### 5.2 A reported experiment: sensitivity of recall to \(ef\)

Consider this reproducible protocol (`hnswlib` reference implementation, SIFT-1M, \(M{=}16\), \(efConstruction{=}200\), single thread): build the index, then sweep \(ef \in \{10, 20, 50, 100, 200, 400\}\), measuring recall@10 against ground truth at each setting:

```python
import hnswlib, numpy as np
index = hnswlib.Index(space='l2', dim=128)
index.init_index(max_elements=1_000_000, ef_construction=200, M=16)
index.add_items(data)
for ef in [10, 20, 50, 100, 200, 400]:
    index.set_ef(ef)
    labels, _ = index.knn_query(queries, k=10)
    recall = np.mean([len(set(l) & set(gt)) / 10
                      for l, gt in zip(labels, ground_truth)])
```

The expected output shape is concave: recall rises steeply from \(ef{=}10\) to \(ef{=}50\) and then asymptotes, while latency grows roughly linearly in \(ef\) — the quantitative content of the recall–latency tradeoff.

### 5.3 Lemma: beam width and basin capture

Fix a query \(q\) and consider the directed graph \(G_q\) where each node points to its closest neighbor strictly closer to \(q\) (ties broken arbitrarily); sinks of \(G_q\) are local minima of greedy search. Let \(B(t)\) be the nodes from which greedy descent reaches the true nearest neighbor \(t\) — its *basin of attraction*.

> **Theorem:** *Basin capture.* Let the beam search maintain candidate list size \(ef\). If at any point the candidate list intersects \(B(t)\), then \(t\) is eventually returned. Consequently, the failure probability of the query is bounded by the probability that a width-\(ef\) beam, seeded at the entry point, avoids \(B(t)\) entirely.

*Proof.* Once \(v \in B(t)\) enters the candidate list, the beam follows the unique \(G_q\)-path \(v \to \dots \to t\): each successor is strictly closer to \(q\) and is always admitted (it improves the worst element), and the search cannot terminate while an unexpanded node closer than the current worst exists. Thus \(t\) is visited and returned. ∎

This lemma explains two practical phenomena: (i) increasing \(ef\) raises the chance of hitting a basin by enlarging the explored entry-adjacent regions; (ii) the selection heuristic's diversity guarantee (§3.2) enlarges basins by reducing spurious sinks.

### 5.4 Complexity accounting

Per query, descent through \(O(\log n)\) layers costs \(O(1)\) greedy steps per layer, and the ground-layer beam evaluates \(O(ef \cdot M)\) distances: total \(O(\log n + ef \cdot M)\) distance computations at \(O(d)\) each. Memory is \(O(n \cdot M)\) edges plus vectors; build is \(O(n \log n \cdot efConstruction)\) — the dominant cost of graph indexes and the reason billion-scale builds are distributed or GPU-accelerated [4].

```haskell
queryCost  :: Int -> Int -> Int -> Int -> Int
queryCost n ef deg d = d * (log n + ef * deg)

buildCost  :: Int -> Int -> Int -> Int -> Int
buildCost n efC deg d = d * n * log n * efC
```

```rust
fn search_layer(q: &[f32], entry: NodeId, ef: usize, layer: u8) -> Vec<NodeId> {
    let mut visited = HashSet::from([entry]);
    let mut candidates: BinaryHeap<Reverse<(OrderedFloat<f32>, NodeId)>> = ...;
    let mut results: BinaryHeap<(OrderedFloat<f32>, NodeId)> = ...;
    while let Some(Reverse((dc, c))) = candidates.pop() {
        if dc > results.peek().unwrap().0 { break; }
        for n in graph.neighbors(c, layer) {
            if visited.insert(n) {
                let dn = distance(q, &vectors[n]);
                if results.len() < ef || dn < results.peek().unwrap().0 {
                    candidates.push(Reverse((dn, n)));
                    results.push((dn, n));
                    if results.len() > ef { results.pop(); }
                }
            }
        }
    }
    results.into_sorted_vec().into_iter().map(|(_, id)| id).collect()
}
```

## 6. Limitations

1. **No worst-case guarantees.** Unlike LSH with provable sublinear query time, HNSW offers no approximation-ratio bound; adversarial datasets can force the beam into exponentially many basins.
2. **Intrinsic dimensionality.** As intrinsic dimension grows and distances concentrate, greedy routing loses its signal and \(ef\) must approach \(n\) [3].
3. **Dynamic updates.** Construction is insert-only; deletions are tombstoned, degrading graph quality, and true deletion with local repair is theoretically underdeveloped.
4. **Build cost and memory.** \(O(n \log n)\) build with large constants (billion-scale builds take hours on multi-GPU setups [4]); at \(M{=}32\), \(n{=}10^9\), edges alone exceed 100 GB.
5. **Metric restriction.** Greedy descent assumes a true metric; MIPS on unnormalized data, learned asymmetric similarities, and hyperbolic embeddings each require bespoke adaptation.
6. **Filtered-search composition.** Inline filtering on a label-unaware graph can disconnect the traversable subgraph at low selectivity; label-aware construction fixes this but multiplies build complexity [7].
7. **Parameter sensitivity.** \(M\), \(efConstruction\), \(ef\), and \(m_L\) interact non-orthogonally; misconfiguration (e.g., \(efConstruction < M\)) silently produces a poor graph.

## 7. Conclusion

HNSW's success rests on a simple geometric insight: stratify a proximity graph's edges by length scale, and a local greedy rule becomes a global routing algorithm. The skip-list-like hierarchy supplies scale separation; the occlusion-aware heuristic supplies edge diversity; the beam width dials the recall–latency frontier. Around this core, a mature ecosystem has grown — PQ/SQ for the memory wall [4][8], GPU and SSD-backed billion-scale construction [4][7], label-aware graphs for filtered retrieval [7] — each preserving the invariant that greedy local decisions remain globally routable.

Open problems: a rigorous navigability theorem for the HNSW construction heuristic on realistic distributions is the field's most important theoretical gap; routing-aware learned quantization [5] suggests co-designing compression and topology rather than stacking them; and the filtered-search invariant — navigability of the induced subgraph — deserves the foundational attention scale separation received a decade ago.
