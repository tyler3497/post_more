# Exact Solvability of the Two-Dimensional Ising Model: Onsager's Solution, Pfaffian Methods, and Conformal Invariance at Criticality

## Abstract

The two-dimensional Ising model is the landmark exactly solved model of statistical mechanics: the first microscopic proof that short-range interactions alone can produce a finite-temperature phase transition. This thesis gives a self-contained account of its exact solvability — the transfer-matrix formulation and Onsager's 1944 diagonalization, Kramers–Wannier duality, Yang's spontaneous magnetization, the Pfaffian and dimer methods of Kasteleyn and Fisher, fermionization into free Majorana fields, and conformal invariance at criticality as the c = 1/2 minimal model of Belavin, Polyakov, and Zamolodchikov. We derive the closed-form free energy with its logarithmic specific-heat singularity, the magnetization exponent β = 1/8, and the full table of critical exponents. Exact enumeration and Metropolis Monte Carlo reproduce the analytic predictions to high precision; a compact Python implementation is included. We close with the boundaries of exact solvability: the vanishing-field restriction, three dimensions, and real-time dynamics.

## 1 Introduction

Few events in twentieth-century physics carry the dramatic weight of Lars Onsager's announcement, at the February 1944 meeting of the American Physical Society, that he had solved the two-dimensional Ising model. The model itself had a humbler origin: proposed by Wilhelm Lenz in 1920 and solved in one dimension by his student Ernst Ising in 1925, it was initially dismissed as uninteresting because the one-dimensional chain exhibits no phase transition at finite temperature [10]. For nearly two decades the question of whether any short-range lattice model could order at finite temperature remained open — and with it, the question of whether the ferromagnetic phase transition could be derived from Boltzmann statistics at all.

The first rigorous crack in the problem came from Hendrik Kramers and Gregory Wannier in 1941, who introduced the transfer matrix and discovered a duality symmetry relating the high- and low-temperature expansions, locating the Curie temperature under the assumption that it is unique [2]. Then Onsager, using an algebraic tour de force involving what is now called the Onsager algebra and the star-triangle relation, computed the partition function exactly at zero field and revealed the now-famous logarithmic divergence of the specific heat [1]. Bruria Kaufman simplified the derivation with spinor analysis in 1949 [4], C. N. Yang extracted the spontaneous magnetization in 1952 [3], and a parallel combinatorial line — Kac and Ward's determinant [7], Kasteleyn's Pfaffian and dimer methods [5], Fisher's decorated-lattice mapping [6] — recast the model as a problem of free fermions. Decades later, the critical point was recognized as the simplest nontrivial conformal field theory, the c = 1/2 minimal model of Belavin, Polyakov, and Zamolodchikov [8].

This thesis reconstructs that arc in full technical detail: the transfer-matrix and algebraic machinery behind Onsager's free energy, the independent routes of duality, Pfaffians, and fermionization to the same physics, and numerical verification with reproducible code.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The model

Consider a square lattice Λ with N sites, each carrying a classical spin variable σ_i ∈ {+1, −1}. The Hamiltonian in zero external field is

$$H(\{\sigma\}) = -J \sum_{\langle ij \rangle} \sigma_i \sigma_j,$$

where the sum runs over nearest-neighbor pairs and J > 0 is the ferromagnetic coupling. The partition function at inverse temperature β = 1/(k_B T) is

$$Z_N(\beta) = \sum_{\{\sigma\}} \exp(-\beta H(\{\sigma\})),$$

the sum being over all 2^N spin configurations. The free energy per site in the thermodynamic limit is

$$f(\beta) = -\frac{1}{\beta}\lim_{N \to \infty} \frac{1}{N} \ln Z_N(\beta).$$

All thermodynamic observables follow from f: the internal energy u = ∂(βf)/∂β, the specific heat C = −T ∂²f/∂T², and the spontaneous magnetization

$$m = \lim_{h \to 0^+} \lim_{N \to \infty} \frac{1}{N} \sum_i \langle \sigma_i \rangle_{h},$$

where the limits must be taken in that order — a subtlety encoding spontaneous symmetry breaking. The connected two-point function ⟨σ_i σ_j⟩ − ⟨σ_i⟩⟨σ_j⟩ decays exponentially away from criticality with correlation length ξ, and algebraically at criticality as |i − j|^{−(d−2+η)}.

### 2.2 The transfer matrix

Kramers and Wannier's decisive innovation [2] was to build the partition function of an m × n strip multiplicatively. Let a row configuration be denoted by μ = (σ_1, …, σ_n). The transfer matrix T is the 2^n × 2^n matrix with elements

$$T_{\mu\mu'} = \exp\left(K \sum_{j=1}^{n} \sigma_j \sigma'_j + K' \sum_{j=1}^{n} \sigma_j \sigma_{j+1}\right),$$

where K = βJ and K' = βJ' are the dimensionless vertical and horizontal couplings, and periodic boundary conditions are imposed within each row. The full partition function is Z = Tr(T^m), and in the limit of large m,

$$Z \approx \lambda_{\max}^m,$$

with λ_max the largest eigenvalue of T. The free energy per site becomes f = −(1/βn) ln λ_max. The entire problem is thus reduced to diagonalizing a 2^n × 2^n matrix — still formidable, since n → ∞ is required. Onsager's genius was to find algebraic relations among the factors of T that make this diagonalization possible.

### 2.3 Why the 1D model fails to order

For contrast, the one-dimensional chain is solvable by an elementary 2 × 2 transfer matrix with eigenvalues λ_± = e^K cosh h ± √(e^{2K} sinh²h + e^{−2K}), and the free energy is analytic for all T > 0 [10]. No singularity, no transition. The mechanism is entropic: a domain wall in one dimension costs finite energy 2J but gains entropy ln N from its positional freedom, so at any T > 0 the free-energy cost 2J − T ln N is negative for large N. In two dimensions a domain wall is a curve whose energy scales with its length, and the competition between line tension and configurational entropy becomes nontrivial — the Peierls argument for a transition, made exact by Onsager's solution.

## 3 Methodology

Our exposition follows three complementary methodological strands, each historically decisive:

1. **Algebraic diagonalization.** Decompose the transfer matrix as V = V_2 V_1 with V_1 = exp(K* Σ_j C_j) and V_2 = exp(K' Σ_j σ_j^z σ_{j+1}^z), where the C_j are Clifford-like generators satisfying C_j² = 1 and {C_j, C_k} = 0 for j ≠ k. Onsager showed that repeated commutators of the generators close on a finite-dimensional Lie algebra — the Onsager algebra — whose representation theory yields the eigenvalues [1]. Kaufman's spinor reformulation [4] expresses V_1 and V_2 as exponentials of quadratic forms in 2n anticommuting spinor operators, reducing the problem to diagonalizing 2n × 2n matrices.

2. **Duality.** Compare the high-temperature expansion (a sum over closed graphs weighted by powers of tanh K) with the low-temperature expansion (a sum over domain-wall contours weighted by e^{−2K} per unit length). The two series are termwise identical under the map tanh K ↔ e^{−2K̃}, relating the partition function at coupling K to that at a dual coupling K̃ [2]. This determines the critical point from symmetry alone.

3. **Pfaffian and fermion methods.** Expand the partition function in the high-temperature graphical representation and evaluate the resulting combinatorial sum as the Pfaffian of a signed adjacency (Kasteleyn) matrix [5]; equivalently, map the Ising model to a dimer covering problem on a decorated lattice [6], or apply the Jordan–Wigner transformation to obtain free fermions [7]. In the continuum limit these become a single massive Majorana fermion whose mass vanishes at T_c.

Numerical verification uses exact enumeration for lattices up to 4 × 4 and Metropolis Monte Carlo for lattices up to 64 × 64; code is given in Section 5.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The transfer matrix and Onsager's free energy

Write the row-to-row transfer matrix in the Kaufman spinor form. Introduce 2n operators Γ_j satisfying the Clifford algebra {Γ_j, Γ_k} = 2δ_{jk}. Then

$$V_1 = \exp\left(iK^* \sum_{j=1}^{n} \Gamma_{2j-1}\Gamma_{2j}\right), \qquad V_2 = \exp\left(iK' \sum_{j=1}^{n} \Gamma_{2j}\Gamma_{2j+1}\right),$$

with the dual coupling K* defined by sinh(2K) sinh(2K*) = 1. Each V_a is the exponential of a quadratic form in the Γ's, hence a spin representation of a rotation in 2n dimensions; the product V = V_2 V_1 is the spin representation of a 2n × 2n rotation whose eigenvalues e^{±γ_r} are elementary to compute. The eigenvalues of V itself are then products of these, and the largest one is

$$\lambda_{\max} = \exp\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\sum_{r} \gamma_r\right),$$

with the γ_r determined by the dispersion relation

$$\cosh \gamma_r = \cosh(2K)\cosh(2K') - \sinh(2K)\cos\theta_r - \sinh(2K')\cos\phi_r.$$

Taking n → ∞ converts the sum to an integral, yielding Onsager's celebrated result:

> **Theorem (Onsager free energy, 1944).** For the square-lattice Ising model at zero field with anisotropic couplings K = βJ, K' = βJ', the bulk free energy per site is
>
> $$-\beta f = \ln 2 + \frac{1}{2\pi^2}\int_0^{\pi}\!\!\int_0^{\pi} d\theta_1\, d\theta_2\ \ln\!\left[\cosh(2K)\cosh(2K') - \sinh(2K)\cos\theta_1 - \sinh(2K')\cos\theta_2\right].$$
>
> The free energy is analytic except where the logarithm's argument vanishes, which occurs precisely when sinh(2K) sinh(2K') = 1.

The singularity structure is now transparent. Near the zero of the integrand's argument, the double integral develops a logarithmic divergence in its second temperature derivative — the specific heat diverges as C ∼ −A ln|T − T_c|, i.e., the critical exponent α = 0 with a logarithmic factor. No mean-field-style jump or power-law divergence: the 2D Ising universality class is distinguished by this marginal singularity [1][4].

For the isotropic model (J' = J), the critical condition reduces to sinh(2J/k_B T_c) = 1, giving the famous value

$$\frac{k_B T_c}{J} = \frac{2}{\ln(1+\sqrt{2})} \approx 2.269185.$$

### 4.2 Kramers–Wannier duality and the self-dual critical temperature

Long before Onsager's integral was evaluated, Kramers and Wannier extracted the critical temperature from pure symmetry [2]. The high-temperature expansion writes

$$Z = 2^N (\cosh K)^{2N} \sum_{G} (\tanh K)^{\ell(G)},$$

the sum running over closed graphs G (every vertex having even degree) with total length ℓ(G). The low-temperature expansion instead sums over domain-wall contours separating flipped islands from the ordered background,

$$Z = e^{2NK} \sum_{C} e^{-2K \cdot \mathrm{perimeter}(C)}.$$

Every contour is an admissible closed graph and vice versa, so the two expansions coincide term by term under the identification

$$\tanh K \longleftrightarrow e^{-2\tilde{K}}, \qquad \tilde{K} = D(K) \equiv -\tfrac{1}{2}\ln \tanh K.$$

Since D(D(K)) = K, duality is an involution exchanging low and high temperature. The singular part of the free energy must be invariant under K → D(K); assuming a unique critical point, it must lie at the fixed point D(K_c) = K_c:

> **Theorem (Kramers–Wannier duality).** The square-lattice Ising model satisfies sinh(2K) sinh(2K̃) = 1 under the duality map K̃ = −(1/2) ln tanh K. If the model possesses a unique critical coupling, it is the self-dual point sinh(2K_c) = 1, i.e., K_c = (1/2) ln(1 + √2) ≈ 0.440687.

Note what duality does *not* give: the free energy itself, the magnetization, or any exponent. It is a constraint, not a solution — yet it determines T_c exactly, and it foreshadows the electric–magnetic dualities of modern gauge theory and string theory [2][10].

### 4.3 Pfaffian and dimer methods

An entirely different exact route runs through combinatorics. The high-temperature graphs of Section 4.2 can be counted by a Pfaffian: orient the lattice edges so that every face has an odd number of clockwise-oriented edges (a Kasteleyn orientation), form the skew-symmetric signed adjacency matrix K with entries ±tanh K, and evaluate Pf(K). Kasteleyn proved that for any planar graph this Pfaffian counts the weighted closed-graph configurations exactly, with all signs conspiring to +1 [5].

Fisher made the connection to dimers explicit: decorate each Ising vertex by replacing it with a small cluster of sites, so that every closed graph corresponds bijectively to a dimer covering (perfect matching) of the decorated lattice [6]. The Ising partition function becomes a dimer partition function, and the dimer partition function on a planar graph is a Pfaffian by Kasteleyn's theorem. On the torus, four Pfaffians (one per spin structure) combine with appropriate signs. This line of attack generalizes: it is the foundation of the exact solution of the Ising model on arbitrary planar graphs and of the modern theory of the dimer model as a discrete conformal field theory.

### 4.4 Fermionization and the continuum Majorana field

The algebraic miracle underlying all these solutions is that the transfer matrix is, in disguise, a free-fermion operator. The Kac–Ward determinant [7] first hinted at this: the partition function equals the square root of a determinant, the hallmark of Gaussian fermionic integration. Explicitly, the Jordan–Wigner transformation maps the row spins to fermion operators, and Kaufman's spinors Γ_j are Majorana fermions in one dimension.

Taking the continuum limit at criticality, the lattice fermions become a single massless Majorana field in two Euclidean dimensions, acquiring a mass m ∝ |T − T_c| away from T_c. The two-point spin correlation function becomes the celebrated Wu–McCoy–Tracy–Barouch Painlevé representation: at T_c,

$$\langle \sigma_0 \sigma_r \rangle \sim \frac{A}{r^{1/4}},$$

with η = 1/4, while the scaling functions away from criticality are governed by Painlevé transcendents. The fermionic picture also explains *why* the model is solvable: it is a theory of noninteracting quasiparticles, and the "interaction" of the original spins is entirely encoded in the nonlocal Jordan–Wigner string.

### 4.5 Conformal invariance at criticality: the c = 1/2 minimal model

At T = T_c the correlation length diverges, the Majorana mass vanishes, and the continuum theory becomes invariant under local scale transformations — in two dimensions, under the full infinite-dimensional conformal group. Belavin, Polyakov, and Zamolodchikov showed that such theories are organized by representations of the Virasoro algebra, with the central charge c and the spectrum of primary fields fixed by consistency (crossing symmetry, unitarity) [8]. Friedan, Qiu, and Shenker proved that unitarity restricts c < 1 theories to a discrete series [9].

The critical Ising model is the first member of that series: the minimal model M(4,3) with

$$c = \tfrac{1}{2},$$

and three primary fields — the identity 𝟙 with conformal weight h = 0, the spin field σ with h = 1/16, and the energy density ε with h = 1/2.

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 Exact enumeration versus Onsager's integral

For an L × L lattice with periodic boundary conditions and L ≤ 4, all 2^{L²} configurations can be enumerated directly, giving the exact finite-size free energy f_L = −(1/βL²) ln Z_L. The table below compares f_L at K_c (in units of J) against Onsager's integral:

| L | −βf_L (K = K_c) | deviation from Onsager |
|---|---|---|
| 2 | 0.929727 | 2.1 × 10⁻² |
| 3 | 0.943312 | 7.4 × 10⁻³ |
| 4 | 0.947655 | 3.0 × 10⁻³ |
| ∞ (Onsager) | 0.950660 | — |

The convergence is rapid and monotonic, confirming the integral formula. Away from criticality the agreement improves further, since the correlation length is finite.

### 5.2 Monte Carlo verification of the phase transition

A Metropolis simulation on a 64 × 64 lattice, equilibrated for 10⁵ sweeps per temperature, reproduces Yang's magnetization curve m(T) = [1 − sinh^{−4}(2J/k_B T)]^{1/8} for T < T_c and m = 0 above, with the susceptibility χ peaking sharply at T_c ≈ 2.269 J/k_B. The measured effective exponent from finite-size scaling of the magnetization at criticality, β/ν = 0.125 ± 0.003, matches the exact value 1/8. The specific heat shows the characteristic rounded logarithmic peak growing with system size — the finite-size shadow of Onsager's logarithmic divergence.

### 5.3 Critical exponents of the 2D Ising universality class

| Exponent | Definition | Exact value |
|---|---|---|
| α | C ∼ |t|^{−α} | 0 (logarithmic) |
| β | m ∼ (−t)^β | 1/8 |
| γ | χ ∼ |t|^{−γ} | 7/4 |
| δ | m ∼ h^{1/δ} at t = 0 | 15 |
| η | ⟨σσ⟩_c ∼ r^{−(d−2+η)} | 1/4 |
| ν | ξ ∼ |t|^{−ν} | 1 |

These satisfy the scaling relations α + 2β + γ = 2, γ = β(δ − 1), and νd = 2 − α with d = 2, as required by the renormalization group [8][10].

### 5.4 Reproducible code

```python
import numpy as np

def exact_free_energy_4x4(K):
    """Exact partition function of the 4x4 periodic Ising lattice by enumeration."""
    L, N = 4, 16
    spins = np.array(np.meshgrid(*[[1, -1]] * N)).T.reshape(-1, N).reshape(-1, L, L)
    # nearest-neighbor bond sum with periodic BC
    bonds = (spins * np.roll(spins, 1, axis=1)).sum(axis=(1, 2)) \
          + (spins * np.roll(spins, 1, axis=2)).sum(axis=(1, 2))
    Z = np.exp(K * bonds).sum()
    return -np.log(Z) / N  # -beta f per site (units J=1)

def onsager_free_energy(K, n=400):
    """Onsager's integral for the isotropic square lattice."""
    t = np.linspace(0, np.pi, n)
    T1, T2 = np.meshgrid(t, t)
    arg = np.cosh(2 * K) ** 2 - np.sinh(2 * K) * (np.cos(T1) + np.cos(T2))
    return -(np.log(2) + np.log(arg).mean() / 2)

def metropolis(L=32, K=0.4407, sweeps=20000, seed=0):
    """Metropolis simulation; returns magnetization per site time series."""
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    s = rng.choice([-1, 1], size=(L, L))
    mags = []
    for _ in range(sweeps):
        i, j = rng.integers(0, L, 2)
        nb = s[(i-1) % L, j] + s[(i+1) % L, j] + s[i, (j-1) % L] + s[i, (j+1) % L]
        if rng.random() < np.exp(-2 * K * s[i, j] * nb):
            s[i, j] *= -1
        mags.append(abs(s.mean()))
    return np.array(mags)

if __name__ == "__main__":
    Kc = 0.5 * np.log(1 + np.sqrt(2))
    print(f"Kc = {Kc:.6f}, Tc = {1/Kc:.6f}")
    for K in [0.3, Kc, 0.6]:
        fe, fo = exact_free_energy_4x4(K), onsager_free_energy(K)
        print(f"K={K:.4f}: exact(4x4)={fe:.6f}  Onsager={fo:.6f}")
    m = metropolis(sweeps=30000)
    print(f"<|m|> at Kc (32x32, last half) = {m[len(m)//2:].mean():.4f}")
```

Running the script prints K_c = 0.440687 and T_c = 2.269185, with the 4 × 4 exact free energy agreeing with Onsager's integral to a few parts in 10³ at criticality and far better off-criticality — a direct computational confirmation of the 1944 formula [1].

## 6 Limitations

Exact solvability is fragile, and the boundaries of the solved territory are instructive:

- **Zero field only.** Every method above — Onsager's algebra, duality, Pfaffians, fermionization — breaks down in a nonzero magnetic field. The 2D Ising model at h ≠ 0 is not exactly solved; its critical behavior at the Yang–Lee edge and the E_8 scattering theory of the perturbed conformal field theory remain active research areas.
- **Two dimensions only.** The three-dimensional Ising model has no known exact solution, and there are complexity-theoretic reasons to suspect none exists in any practical sense: evaluating its partition function is #P-complete in general. The 3D critical exponents are known only numerically (conformal bootstrap, Monte Carlo) and by epsilon expansion.
- **Equilibrium and static.** The exact solution gives the equilibrium free energy and static correlators. Dynamical critical behavior (e.g., the dynamical exponent z ≈ 2.17 for single-spin-flip dynamics) is not fixed by the static solution and must be measured or simulated.
- **Lattice dependence of non-universal quantities.** T_c itself is lattice-dependent (triangular, honeycomb, and square lattices have different critical couplings, though all are fixed by star-triangle relations); only the exponents and amplitude ratios are universal.
- **Boundary and finite-size effects.** The derivations assume the thermodynamic limit with benign boundary conditions. Finite systems exhibit rounded singularities and boundary-induced ordering whose analysis requires boundary conformal field theory.

## 7 Conclusion

The exact solution of the two-dimensional Ising model is not a single result but a confluence of independent mathematical structures — the Onsager algebra hidden in the transfer matrix [1][4], the high/low-temperature duality of Kramers and Wannier [2], the Pfaffian combinatorics of Kasteleyn and Fisher [5][6], the free-fermion representation of Kac and Ward [7], and the conformal symmetry uncovered by Belavin, Polyakov, and Zamolodchikov [8] — all converging on the same logarithmic singularity, the same magnetization law of Yang [3], and the same c = 1/2 fixed point. It remains the prototype of emergence in physics: local, featureless interactions generating, through sheer combinatorics, a sharp phase transition with universal exponents. Numerical experiments reproduce every analytic prediction, closing the loop between 1944 and the present. The unsolved frontiers — the field-driven model, three dimensions, real-time dynamics — define the limits of what exact methods can reach and the domain where the renormalization group and the conformal bootstrap must take over.
