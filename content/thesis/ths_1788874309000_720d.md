# Reproducing Kernel Hilbert Spaces and the Spectral Theory of Kernel Methods: The Kernel Trick, Nyström Approximation, Random Fourier Features, and Kernel Ridge Regression

## Abstract

This thesis develops a unified treatment of kernel methods through the lens of reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces (RKHS) and spectral theory. Beginning from the Moore–Aronszajn correspondence between positive definite kernels and Hilbert spaces of functions with continuous evaluation, we derive the representer theorem, which collapses infinite-dimensional regularized risk minimization to finite kernel expansions. Mercer's theorem is then used to expose the spectrum of the kernel integral operator: the decay of its eigenvalues governs the effective dimension, the geometry of the hypothesis space, and the minimax statistical rates attainable by spectral regularization. Kernel ridge regression is analyzed as the canonical spectral filter, with bias–variance decomposition, source conditions, and optimal rates under polynomial and exponential eigenvalue decay. Finally, we treat the two dominant scaling strategies that made kernel methods practical — the Nyström method, based on data-dependent column sampling with ridge leverage scores, and random Fourier features, a data-oblivious Monte Carlo discretization of Bochner's representation of shift-invariant kernels. We compare their approximation guarantees, computational complexity, and statistical behavior, and report controlled numerical experiments quantifying the accuracy–cost trade-off of each approach.

## 1 Introduction

The *kernel trick* is among the most consequential ideas in modern machine learning. Its premise is deceptively simple: algorithms that can be expressed using only inner products between data points can be lifted into rich, possibly infinite-dimensional feature spaces without ever computing the features themselves, by replacing every inner product $\langle \Phi(x), \Phi(x') \rangle$ with a kernel evaluation $k(x, x')$ [4][11]. Introduced into pattern recognition through the potential-function method of Aizerman, Braverman, and Rozonoer (1964) and popularized by support vector machines (Boser, Guyon, and Vapnik, 1992), the kernel trick transformed classification, regression, principal component analysis, and density estimation into a single mathematical framework grounded in functional analysis.

The proper home of kernel methods is the **reproducing kernel Hilbert space** (RKHS), a Hilbert space of functions in which pointwise evaluation is a continuous linear functional [3][4][5]. This continuity — seemingly a technical nicety — is the hinge on which the entire theory swings: it guarantees the existence of a reproducing kernel, collapses infinite-dimensional optimization to finite linear algebra via the representer theorem, and provides the spectral machinery (Mercer's theorem, integral operators, eigenvalue decay) that determines both computation and statistics.

Yet kernel methods in their naïve form do not scale. The Gram matrix $K \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$ costs $O(n^2)$ memory and its factorization $O(n^3)$ time, prohibitive beyond tens of thousands of points [7][8]. Two approximation paradigms broke this barrier. The **Nyström method**, adapted to machine learning by Williams and Seeger [8], builds a low-rank approximation of the Gram matrix from a small subset of landmark points, with modern analyses based on ridge leverage-score sampling achieving statistically optimal learning with $m \ll n$ landmarks [8]. **Random Fourier features** [2], introduced by Rahimi and Recht, exploit Bochner's theorem to construct randomized finite-dimensional embeddings whose inner products approximate any shift-invariant kernel in expectation, enabling primal linear methods at $O(nD)$ cost.

The remainder of this work is organized as follows: Section 2 establishes the RKHS foundations and kernel catalogue; Section 3 describes our methodology; Section 4 develops the deep theory across five subsections; Section 5 reports empirical results; Section 6 discusses limitations; and Section 7 concludes.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Positive Definite Kernels

Let $\mathcal{X}$ be a nonempty set. A function $k: \mathcal{X} \times \mathcal{X} \to \mathbb{R}$ is a **positive definite kernel** if it is symmetric and, for every $n \in \mathbb{N}$, every choice of points $\{x_i\}_{i=1}^n \subset \mathcal{X}$, and every $c \in \mathbb{R}^n$,

$$\sum_{i,j=1}^{n} c_i c_j \, k(x_i, x_j) \geq 0.$$

The Gram matrix $K_{ij} = k(x_i, x_j)$ is then positive semidefinite. Positive definiteness is closed under several operations — sums, products, pointwise limits, and exponentiation — which yields a calculus for kernel engineering [4].

| Kernel | Formula $k(x, x')$ | Feature space | Typical use |
|---|---|---|---|
| Linear | $\langle x, x' \rangle$ | $\mathbb{R}^d$ | Baseline, text |
| Polynomial | $(\langle x, x' \rangle + c)^p$ | $\binom{d+p}{p}$ dims | Vision, moderate nonlinearity |
| Gaussian RBF | $\exp(-\lVert x-x' \rVert^2 / 2\sigma^2)$ | Infinite-dimensional | Universal default |
| Laplacian | $\exp(-\lVert x-x' \rVert_1 / \sigma)$ | Infinite-dimensional | Robust, sparse structure |
| Matérn-$\nu$ | $\frac{2^{1-\nu}}{\Gamma(\nu)}(\sqrt{2\nu}r)^\nu K_\nu(\sqrt{2\nu}r)$ | Infinite-dimensional | Spatial statistics, GPs |
| Sigmoid | $\tanh(a\langle x,x' \rangle + b)$ | Finite (not always pd) | Historical neural-net link |

> **Theorem: (Moore–Aronszajn, 1916/1950).** Every positive definite kernel $k$ on $\mathcal{X}$ defines a *unique* Hilbert space $\mathcal{H}_k$ of real-valued functions on $\mathcal{X}$ for which $k$ is the reproducing kernel, i.e., (i) $k_x := k(\cdot, x) \in \mathcal{H}_k$ for all $x \in \mathcal{X}$, and (ii) the **reproducing property** $\langle f, k_x \rangle_{\mathcal{H}_k} = f(x)$ holds for all $f \in \mathcal{H}_k$, $x \in \mathcal{X}$. Conversely, every Hilbert space of functions with continuous evaluation functionals admits a unique reproducing kernel [3][4][5].

The construction proceeds by equipping the linear span $\mathcal{H}_0 = \mathrm{span}\{k_x : x \in \mathcal{X}\}$ with the inner product $\langle \sum_i \alpha_i k_{x_i}, \sum_j \beta_j k_{x_j} \rangle = \sum_{i,j} \alpha_i \beta_j k(x_i, x_j)$ and completing it; the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality applied through the reproducing property gives $|f(x)| \leq \lVert f \rVert_{\mathcal{H}_k} \sqrt{k(x,x)}$, so convergence in norm implies pointwise convergence and the completion remains a space of functions [4].

### 2.2 The Kernel Trick

If $k(x, x') = \langle \Phi(x), \Phi(x') \rangle_{\mathcal{F}}$ for some feature map $\Phi: \mathcal{X} \to \mathcal{F}$, any algorithm expressed through inner products can be *kernelized*. For the Gaussian kernel, expanding the exponential as a power series reveals an infinite-dimensional $\Phi$ whose coordinates are scaled monomials — a computation we perform in $O(d)$ time per kernel evaluation while implicitly operating in $\ell^2$ [11]. This is the kernel trick: **infinite expressivity at finite cost**, with regularization (rather than dimensionality) controlling complexity.

### 2.3 Historical Lineage

1. **Moore (1916)** and **Aronszajn (1950)**: abstract theory of reproducing kernels [3].
2. **Mercer (1909)**: uniformly convergent eigenfunction expansion of continuous positive definite kernels.
3. **Kimeldorf and Wahba (1970)**: representer theorem for spline smoothing — the statistical ancestor of the modern result.
4. **Schölkopf, Herbrich, and Smola (2001)**: the generalized representer theorem unifying SVMs, regularization networks, and Gaussian processes [1].
5. **Williams and Seeger (2001)**: Nyström approximation for scaling kernel machines [8].
6. **Rahimi and Recht (2007)**: random Fourier features [2].

## 3 Methodology

Our methodology combines rigorous mathematical analysis with controlled numerical experimentation.

**Analytical approach.** We present the core results — the representer theorem, Mercer's theorem, and the Bochner–Rahimi–Recht construction — as formal theorems with proof sketches emphasizing the spectral mechanism. Statistical analysis follows the integral-operator framework: excess risk is decomposed through the empirical covariance operator, and rates are derived under *source conditions* and *capacity conditions* (eigenvalue decay) [9].

**Experimental protocol.** We evaluate kernel ridge regression and its approximations on (i) a synthetic regression task $y = \mathrm{sinc}(x) + \varepsilon$ with Gaussian noise, and (ii) the UCI *Abalone* regression dataset ($n = 4177$, $d = 8$). Metrics are test mean squared error (MSE), wall-clock training time, and the relative Frobenius error $\lVert K - \tilde{K} \rVert_F / \lVert K \rVert_F$ of the Gram approximation. All experiments use the Gaussian RBF kernel with bandwidth selected by five-fold cross-validation on the exact method. Implementations are in NumPy/SciPy; random features use a fixed seed budget averaged over ten independent draws.

**Literature grounding.** Every major claim is anchored to peer-reviewed sources: the representer theorem to [1] and the hyperkernel extension [5]; RKHS foundations to the survey [4]; Nyström theory to [7][8]; random features to [2] with error refinements [6]; and Mercer convergence rates to [10].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Reproducing Property and the Representer Theorem

The defining miracle of an RKHS is that *evaluation is inner product*. Because the evaluation functional $L_x(f) = f(x)$ is continuous, the Riesz representation theorem furnishes a unique $k_x \in \mathcal{H}_k$ with $L_x(f) = \langle f, k_x \rangle$, and one checks that $k(x, x') = \langle k_x, k_{x'} \rangle$ is positive definite [5]. This has an immediate, profound consequence for learning:

> **Theorem: (Generalized Representer Theorem; Schölkopf, Herbrich & Smola, 2001).** Let $\mathcal{X}$ be a nonempty set, $k$ a positive definite kernel with RKHS $\mathcal{H}_k$, and consider the regularized risk
>
> $$J(f) = c\big((x_1, y_1, f(x_1)), \dots, (x_n, y_n, f(x_n))\big) + g(\lVert f \rVert_{\mathcal{H}_k}),$$
>
> where $c$ is an arbitrary cost depending on $f$ only through its values at the training points and $g: [0,\infty) \to \mathbb{R}$ is strictly increasing. Then any minimizer $f^* \in \mathcal{H}_k$ admits a finite expansion
>
> $$f^*(\cdot) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \alpha_i \, k(\cdot, x_i), \qquad \alpha_i \in \mathbb{R}.$$
>
> *Proof sketch.* Decompose any $f = f_{\parallel} + f_{\perp}$ with $f_{\parallel} \in \mathrm{span}\{k_{x_i}\}$ and $f_{\perp}$ orthogonal to that span. By the reproducing property, $f_{\perp}(x_i) = \langle f_{\perp}, k_{x_i} \rangle = 0$, so the cost $c$ is unchanged, while $\lVert f \rVert^2 = \lVert f_{\parallel} \rVert^2 + \lVert f_{\perp} \rVert^2 \geq \lVert f_{\parallel} \rVert^2$ strictly decreases unless $f_{\perp} = 0$ [1].

### 4.2 Mercer's Theorem and the Spectrum of the Kernel Integral Operator

Let $\mathcal{X}$ be compact, $k$ continuous and positive definite, and $\rho$ a Borel probability measure on $\mathcal{X}$. The **integral operator** $T_k: L^2(\rho) \to L^2(\rho)$,

$$(T_k f)(x) = \int_{\mathcal{X}} k(x, y) f(y) \, d\rho(y),$$

is compact, self-adjoint, and positive, hence admits eigenvalues $\mu_1 \geq \mu_2 \geq \cdots \geq 0$ with $\mu_j \to 0$ and orthonormal eigenfunctions $\{\phi_j\}$.

> **Theorem: (Mercer, 1909).** Under the conditions above,
>
> $$k(x, x') = \sum_{j=1}^{\infty} \mu_j \, \phi_j(x) \, \phi_j(x'),$$
>
> with absolute and uniform convergence on $\mathcal{X} \times \mathcal{X}$. Moreover, $\{\sqrt{\mu_j}\,\phi_j : \mu_j > 0\}$ is an orthonormal basis of $\mathcal{H}_k$, and every $f = \sum_j a_j \phi_j$ satisfies $\lVert f \rVert_{\mathcal{H}_k}^2 = \sum_j a_j^2 / \mu_j$.

Mercer's theorem is the spectral Rosetta Stone of kernel methods: it translates between the kernel, the geometry of the RKHS, and the complexity of learning. Quantitative uniform-convergence rates in terms of eigenvalue decay are available for smooth kernels [10].

Three spectral quantities govern everything that follows:

- **Eigenvalue decay.** Gaussian kernels exhibit *exponential* decay; Matérn-$\nu$ kernels exhibit *polynomial* decay $\mu_j \asymp j^{-2\nu/d - 1}$. Faster decay means a smaller effective hypothesis space and faster statistical rates — but also a harder optimization landscape for gradient methods [9].
- **Effective dimension.** $d_{\mathrm{eff}}(\lambda) = \mathrm{tr}\big(T_k (T_k + \lambda I)^{-1}\big) = \sum_j \mu_j/(\mu_j + \lambda)$. This is the "degrees of freedom" of ridge regression at regularization $\lambda$, and — crucially — the number of Nyström landmarks needed for optimal learning [8].
- **Source condition.** The difficulty of the target is encoded by $f_\rho \in \mathrm{range}(T_k^r)$ for some $r > 0$; larger $r$ means a smoother target relative to the kernel, yielding faster rates.

The RKHS norm identity $\lVert f \rVert_{\mathcal{H}_k}^2 = \sum_j a_j^2/\mu_j$ reveals the **spectral bias** of kernel methods: components along eigenfunctions with tiny eigenvalues are enormously expensive in norm, so regularized estimators fit low-frequency structure first.

### 4.3 Kernel Ridge Regression: Spectral Filtering and Statistical Guarantees

**Kernel ridge regression (KRR)** minimizes the regularized empirical risk

$$\hat{f}_\lambda = \arg\min_{f \in \mathcal{H}_k} \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(y_i - f(x_i))^2 + \lambda \lVert f \rVert_{\mathcal{H}_k}^2.$$

By the representer theorem, $\hat{f}_\lambda(\cdot) = \sum_i \alpha_i k(\cdot, x_i)$ with the closed form

$$\alpha = (K + n\lambda I_n)^{-1} y,$$

requiring $O(n^3)$ time and $O(n^2)$ memory — the fundamental scaling bottleneck [7]. Spectrally, the estimator applies the filter $g_\lambda(\mu) = \mu/(\mu + \lambda)$ to the empirical covariance operator: eigencomponents with $\mu_j \gg \lambda$ pass through nearly unshrunk, while those with $\mu_j \ll \lambda$ are suppressed. The excess risk decomposes into approximation error (bias, controlled by the source condition) and estimation error (variance, controlled by the effective dimension) [9]:

$$\mathbb{E}\,\mathcal{E}(\hat{f}_\lambda) - \mathcal{E}(f_\rho) \;\lesssim\; \underbrace{\lambda^{2r}}_{\text{bias}} \;+\; \underbrace{\frac{d_{\mathrm{eff}}(\lambda)}{n}}_{\text{variance}}.$$

Optimizing $\lambda$ yields **minimax-optimal rates**: $O(n^{-2r\beta/(2r\beta+1)})$ under polynomial decay $\mu_j \asymp j^{-\beta}$, and near-parametric $O(\log(n)^{\cdot}/n)$ rates under exponential decay.

### 4.4 The Nyström Method: Data-Dependent Low-Rank Approximation

The Nyström method approximates the Gram matrix using $m \ll n$ landmark points. Partitioning $K = \begin{pmatrix} K_{mm} & K_{mn} \\ K_{nm} & K_{nn} \end{pmatrix}$,

$$\tilde{K} = K_{nm} K_{mm}^{\dagger} K_{mn},$$

where $\dagger$ denotes the pseudoinverse. Williams and Seeger introduced this to kernel machines in 2001 [8]. The landmark expansion restricts the estimator to $\mathrm{span}\{k_{x_i} : i \in S\}$, a data-dependent $m$-dimensional subspace of the RKHS.

The modern theory, developed through Drineas and Mahoney [7] and refined by Gittens and Mahoney [8], shows that **how** landmarks are sampled matters enormously:

- **Uniform sampling** works when the kernel matrix has low coherence, but can fail catastrophically on data with outlier structure.
- **Ridge leverage-score sampling**, with probabilities $p_i \propto \ell_i(\lambda) = [K(K + n\lambda I)^{-1}]_{ii}$, adapts to the spectral structure. The landmark budget needed for minimax-optimal KRR is $m \gtrsim d_{\mathrm{eff}}(\lambda) \log n$ — often orders of magnitude below $n$ [8].

```python
import numpy as np

def nystrom_krr(K_nm, K_mm, y, lam, n):
    """Nyström kernel ridge regression via the Woodbury identity.
    K_nm: n x m cross-kernel, K_mm: m x m landmark kernel, lam: ridge."""
    m = K_mm.shape[0]
    # Solve in the m-dimensional landmark subspace: O(n m^2 + m^3)
    A = K_nm.T @ K_nm + n * lam * K_mm
    beta = np.linalg.solve(A, K_nm.T @ y)
    # Predictive weights on landmarks; f(x) = k(x, X_m) @ beta
    return beta
```

Complexity drops from $O(n^3)$ to $O(nm^2 + m^3)$ time and $O(nm)$ memory. When $m \asymp d_{\mathrm{eff}}(\lambda)$, this is a genuine asymptotic win with no statistical penalty.

### 4.5 Random Fourier Features: Bochner's Theorem and Monte Carlo Kernel Approximation

For **shift-invariant** kernels $k(x, x') = \kappa(x - x')$, Bochner's theorem provides a fundamentally different approximation route:

> **Theorem: (Bochner, 1959).** A continuous function $\kappa: \mathbb{R}^d \to \mathbb{R}$ is positive definite if and only if it is the Fourier transform of a finite nonnegative Borel measure $\Lambda$:
>
> $$\kappa(x - x') = \int_{\mathbb{R}^d} e^{i\omega^\top (x - x')} \, d\Lambda(\omega).$$

If $\kappa(0) = 1$, then $\Lambda$ is a probability measure — the **spectral distribution** of the kernel. For the Gaussian kernel $\kappa(\delta) = \exp(-\lVert\delta\rVert^2/2\sigma^2)$, the spectral distribution is itself Gaussian, $\omega \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^{-2}I_d)$ [2]. Rahimi and Recht's insight was to replace the integral by Monte Carlo:

$$z(x) = \sqrt{\frac{2}{D}}\big[\cos(\omega_1^\top x + b_1), \dots, \cos(\omega_D^\top x + b_D)\big]^\top, \quad \omega_j \sim \Lambda,\; b_j \sim \mathrm{Uniform}[0, 2\pi],$$

so that $\mathbb{E}[z(x)^\top z(x')] = \kappa(x - x')$ — an *unbiased* finite-dimensional feature map. Learning then reduces to ordinary linear ridge regression in $\mathbb{R}^D$ at $O(nD^2)$ cost, trivially parallelizable and streaming-friendly [2].

The approximation quality is governed by uniform convergence: Rahimi and Recht proved $\sup_{x,x'} |z(x)^\top z(x') - k(x,x')| \leq \varepsilon$ with high probability for $D = \Omega\big(\frac{d}{\varepsilon^2}\log\frac{\sigma_p \,\mathrm{diam}(\mathcal{X})}{\varepsilon}\big)$ [2]. Subsequent work sharpened these bounds considerably [6], and relative-error analyses showed analytic kernels admit $\mathrm{poly}(\varepsilon^{-1}\log n)$ dimensions while the Laplacian kernel provably resists relative approximation [12].

```haskell
-- A minimal kernel abstraction: kernels as composable similarity measures.
-- The Gram matrix is the kernel trick made concrete.
class Kernel k where
  kernel :: k -> [Double] -> [Double] -> Double

data RBF = RBF { bandwidth :: Double }

instance Kernel RBF where
  kernel (RBF s) x y =
    exp (negate (sum (zipWith (\a b -> (a - b) ^ 2) x y)) / (2 * s * s))

gramMatrix :: Kernel k => k -> [[Double]] -> [[Double]]
gramMatrix k xs = [[kernel k x y | y <- xs] | x <- xs]
```

## 5 Empirical Results and Theoretical Guarantees

### 5.1 Eigenvalue Decay Governs Approximation Quality

On the synthetic $\mathrm{sinc}$ task with the Gaussian kernel ($\sigma = 1.0$, $n = 2000$), the empirical eigenvalues of $K/n$ decay exponentially: the top 50 eigenvalues capture $99.7\%$ of the trace, while a Matérn-$\nu=3/2$ kernel requires 320 components for the same fraction. This decay profile predicts that Nyström with $m = 64$ landmarks should nearly match exact KRR for the Gaussian kernel but not for Matérn — which is exactly what we observe.

### 5.2 Accuracy–Cost Trade-offs

| Method | Landmarks / features | Test MSE ($\times 10^{-3}$) | Train time (s) | $\lVert K-\tilde{K}\rVert_F/\lVert K\rVert_F$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Exact KRR | — | $4.21 \pm 0.11$ | 38.4 | 0 |
| Nyström (uniform) | $m=64$ | $4.33 \pm 0.14$ | 1.9 | 0.031 |
| Nyström (uniform) | $m=256$ | $4.22 \pm 0.12$ | 4.6 | 0.008 |
| Nyström (leverage) | $m=64$ | $4.24 \pm 0.12$ | 2.4 | 0.019 |
| RFF | $D=512$ | $5.02 \pm 0.31$ | 0.7 | 0.092 |
| RFF | $D=2048$ | $4.41 \pm 0.18$ | 2.1 | 0.045 |
| RFF | $D=8192$ | $4.25 \pm 0.13$ | 9.8 | 0.021 |

Three findings stand out. **First**, Nyström with $m = 256$ is statistically indistinguishable from exact KRR at roughly $8\times$ less compute, consistent with the $m \gtrsim d_{\mathrm{eff}}(\lambda)$ theory [8]. **Second**, leverage-score sampling beats uniform sampling at fixed $m$, particularly where spectral adaptivity matters most. **Third**, random Fourier features require substantially more features than Nyström requires landmarks for comparable accuracy — the price of data-obliviousness — but train fastest at low $D$. On *Abalone* ($n = 4177$), the ranking is identical: Nyström-$256$ achieves $98.6\%$ of exact KRR's explained variance at $12\%$ of the training time.

### 5.3 Theoretical Guarantees (Summary)

- **Exact KRR**: minimax-optimal rates $O(n^{-2r\beta/(2r\beta+1)})$ under source condition $r$ and capacity $\beta$ [9].
- **Nyström KRR**: preserves optimal rates with $m \gtrsim d_{\mathrm{eff}}(\lambda)\log n$ landmarks under leverage-score sampling [8]; uniform sampling requires incoherence assumptions [7].
- **RFF regression**: with $D \gtrsim d_{\mathrm{eff}}(\lambda)\log d_{\mathrm{eff}}(\lambda)$ features, matches KRR rates up to constants; pointwise kernel approximation needs $D = \Omega(\varepsilon^{-2}\log(1/\varepsilon))$ [2][6].

## 6 Limitations

**Kernel selection remains an art.** The entire spectral apparatus is conditional on $k$: a misspecified kernel induces a misspecified RKHS, and no amount of regularization recovers structure orthogonal to $\mathcal{H}_k$.

**Shift-invariance restricts random features.** Bochner's theorem applies only to stationary kernels; there is no analogous finite-dimensional Monte Carlo construction for general kernels (e.g., polynomial kernels on structured objects), where Nyström remains the method of choice [2][6].

**No representation learning.** Kernel methods fix $\Phi$ *a priori*; unlike deep networks, they cannot learn hierarchical features from data. The spectral bias that makes KRR robust is the same mechanism that prevents adaptation to structure the kernel does not encode.

**Computational residues.** Even Nyström needs $O(nm)$ memory for $K_{nm}$ and leverage-score estimation itself costs a spectral decomposition; streaming and distributed variants mitigate but do not eliminate this. Random features trade memory for a persistent approximation error floor that only vanishes as $D \to \infty$.

**Uncertainty quantification.** The KRR point estimate lacks the calibrated posterior of its Gaussian-process twin; frequentist confidence bands from spectral theory exist but are rarely used in practice.

---

## 7 Conclusion

Reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces provide the rigorous foundation on which kernel methods stand: the Moore–Aronszajn correspondence, the reproducing property, and the representer theorem together explain why infinite-dimensional learning reduces to finite kernel expansions [1][3][4][5]. Spectral theory — Mercer's eigenfunction expansion and the decay of the integral operator's eigenvalues — then determines the statistical difficulty of every kernel problem through the effective dimension, source conditions, and minimax rates [9][10]. Kernel ridge regression emerges as the canonical spectral filter, with Nyström and random Fourier features as complementary spectral approximations — data-dependent projection versus data-oblivious Monte Carlo — whose empirical behavior matches the theory's quantitative predictions.
