## Abstract

Quantum theory resists two classical pictures of reality at once: the *local* picture, in which distant systems carry preassigned outcomes, and the *noncontextual* picture, in which every observable possesses a value independent of how it is measured. This thesis unifies the two great no-go theorems that expose these failures. We derive the Clauser–Horne–Shimony–Holt inequality from the factorization of local hidden-variable correlations, exhibit the Tsirelson bound of 2√2 as the operator-norm ceiling on quantum violations, and prove the Kochen–Specker theorem via the Mermin–Peres magic square, a state-independent parity contradiction in a 3×3 array of two-qubit Pauli observables. We then recast both theorems inside the sheaf-theoretic framework of Abramsky and Brandenburger, where nonlocality and contextuality appear as obstructions to the existence of global sections over measurement covers. Finally, we survey the loophole-free experiments of 2015 and the device-independent cryptographic protocols they enable, arguing that these no-go theorems have become the positive specification of quantum information advantage.

## 1 Intro

The 1935 paper of Einstein, Podolsky and Rosen [1] argued that quantum mechanics, if complete, must violate either *locality* — the principle that operations on a distant system cannot affect local outcomes — or *realism* — the principle that measurement outcomes are predetermined by the physical state. For three decades the argument remained philosophy. John Bell changed that in 1964 by deriving an inequality that every locally realistic theory must satisfy and that quantum mechanics violates [2]. Bell's theorem turned a metaphysical dispute into an experimental program, and its modern form, the Clauser–Horne–Shimony–Holt (CHSH) inequality [3], is now the workhorse of experimental quantum foundations.

A second, independent no-go theorem constrains hidden variables even without separated parties. The Kochen–Specker theorem [5], proved by Bell himself in 1966 and by Kochen and Specker in 1967, shows that in Hilbert space dimension three or higher, observables cannot be assigned values independently of the measurement context. Where Bell's theorem establishes *nonlocality*, the Kochen–Specker theorem establishes *contextuality*.

These two results were long treated as cousins. The sheaf-theoretic framework of Abramsky and Brandenburger [7] revealed them as instances of a single mathematical phenomenon: the obstruction to gluing locally consistent data into a global assignment. Meanwhile, experiment caught up with theory: in 2015 three independent groups performed the first *loophole-free* Bell tests [9][10][11], and the same inequalities now certify cryptographic keys and random numbers without trusting the devices that produce them [12][13].

This thesis derives the CHSH inequality from first principles, proves the Tsirelson bound on quantum violations, proves contextuality through the Mermin–Peres magic square, unifies both no-go theorems in sheaf-theoretic language, and traces the path from abstract contradiction to loophole-free experiment and device-independent cryptography.


## 2 Background

### The EPR argument and local hidden variables

EPR considered two particles prepared in a perfectly correlated state and then separated [1]. In Bohm's spin version, the singlet state predicts that spin measurements along the same axis always yield opposite results. EPR argued that since the outcome on one side can be predicted with certainty without disturbing the other particle, that outcome must correspond to an *element of reality* fixed before measurement. A *local hidden-variable* model formalizes this: a variable λ, distributed with density ρ(λ), determines outcomes

E(a,b) = ∫ dλ ρ(λ) A(a,λ) B(b,λ)

where A(a,λ), B(b,λ) take values in {+1, −1} and denote Alice's and Bob's outcomes for settings a and b. *Locality* is the factorization itself — Alice's outcome cannot depend on Bob's setting — and *measurement independence* is the assumption that ρ(λ) does not depend on the chosen settings.

### The Kochen–Specker theorem

Drop the separated parties and ask a sharper question: can every observable of a single quantum system be assigned a definite value, independent of which other compatible observables are measured alongside it? A *noncontextual* hidden-variable theory answers yes. The Kochen–Specker theorem answers no, for any Hilbert space of dimension three or greater [5]: there is no assignment v of values 0 or 1 to projection operators such that every orthogonal resolution of the identity contains exactly one projector valued 1. Kochen and Specker's original proof used 117 vectors; the essential content is that the functional relations among commuting observables cannot all be preserved by a context-independent valuation.

Bell's and Kochen–Specker's theorems are complementary attacks on classicality:

|  | Bell nonlocality | Kochen–Specker contextuality |
|---|---|---|
| Assumption refuted | Locality + realism | Noncontextuality |
| Physical setup | Two or more spacelike separated parties | Single system, dimension at least 3 |
| Character of proof | Statistical: inequalities on correlations | Logical: parity contradiction |
| Experimental loopholes | Locality, detection, freedom of choice | Compatibility, no-disturbance |
| Canonical example | CHSH inequality [3] | Mermin–Peres magic square [6] |

## 3 Methodology

This thesis proceeds by *constructive contradiction*: for each no-go result we assume the classical picture, derive a rigid constraint, and exhibit a quantum arrangement violating it. Four proof techniques are used. First, the CHSH inequality is derived from a pointwise algebraic identity over ±1-valued outcome functions, following Bell [2] and Clauser et al. [3]; no quantum formalism enters the bound. Second, the Tsirelson bound is obtained as an operator-norm estimate on the Bell operator [4], which explains why quantum violations stop at 2√2 rather than the algebraic maximum 4. Third, the Kochen–Specker theorem is proved through the Mermin–Peres magic square [6], replacing the original 117-vector geometric argument with a parity count in a 3×3 array of two-qubit Pauli observables — a proof that is state-independent, unlike Bell's. Fourth, both theorems are recast in the sheaf-theoretic language of Abramsky and Brandenburger [7], where empirical data are sections of a presheaf over measurement contexts and the no-go results become theorems about the nonexistence of global sections.

Our standing assumptions are: measurement independence (settings uncorrelated with λ), fair sampling in the experiments of §5 (later discharged by the loophole-free tests), projective measurements on finite-dimensional systems, and the no-signalling condition satisfied by quantum theory.


## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Derivation of the CHSH Inequality

Consider two parties, each choosing between two binary measurements: Alice chooses a or a′, Bob chooses b or b′. Define the CHSH combination

S = E(a,b) + E(a,b′) + E(a′,b) − E(a′,b′).

> **Theorem (CHSH).** In any local hidden-variable model, |S| ≤ 2 [2][3].

> **Proof.** For each λ define s(λ) = A(a,λ)B(b,λ) + A(a,λ)B(b′,λ) + A(a′,λ)B(b,λ) − A(a′,λ)B(b′,λ). Factoring,
>
> s(λ) = A(a,λ)(B(b,λ) + B(b′,λ)) + A(a′,λ)(B(b,λ) − B(b′,λ)).
>
> Since B(b,λ), B(b′,λ) take values in {+1, −1}, either the sum is ±2 and the difference vanishes, or the difference is ±2 and the sum vanishes. In either case |s(λ)| = 2. Averaging over λ with the setting-independent density ρ gives |S| = |∫ dλ ρ(λ) s(λ)| ≤ 2. ∎

The bound uses only the ±1 range of outcomes and the factorization of the local model — it is a theorem of classical probability, not of quantum theory. Quantum mechanics violates it. For the singlet state, spin correlations obey E(a,b) = −a·b; choosing coplanar measurement directions with successive angles of 45° yields S = 2√2 ≈ 2.828 > 2. For photon polarization, where E = −cos(2Δ) with Δ the relative polarizer angle, the optimal settings a = 0°, a′ = 45°, b = 22.5°, b′ = −22.5° achieve the same value. Bell's original 1964 inequality required perfect anticorrelations and was experimentally awkward; CHSH removed that requirement, which is why every modern Bell test is a CHSH test [3][8].

![CHSH experimental schematic with Alice and Bob measurement stations and a comparison of the classical bound S=2 against the quantum Tsirelson bound](public/thesis/ths_1789457338920_03288-0.webp)

Fine later showed that the CHSH inequalities are not merely necessary but *sufficient* for a local hidden-variable model in this scenario: the local polytope for two parties, two settings and two outcomes is exactly cut out by the eight CHSH inequalities [15].

### 4.2 The Tsirelson Bound: The Quantum Ceiling

Quantum mechanics violates the classical bound 2, but it cannot violate it arbitrarily. The algebraic maximum of S is 4 — attainable by hypothetical *Popescu–Rohrlich boxes*, no-signalling correlations with E(a,b) = E(a,b′) = E(a′,b) = +1 and E(a′,b′) = −1 [16]. Quantum correlations stop strictly below that.

> **Theorem (Tsirelson).** For any quantum state and any ±1-valued observables, |S| ≤ 2√2 [4].

> **Proof.** Write the Bell operator B = A₀⊗(B₀+B₁) + A₁⊗(B₀−B₁) with Aᵢ² = Bⱼ² = I and [Aᵢ,Bⱼ] = 0. Squaring,
>
> B² = 4 I⊗I − [A₀,A₁] ⊗ [B₀,B₁].
>
> Each commutator has operator norm at most 2, since ‖[X,Y]‖ ≤ 2‖X‖‖Y‖ for unit-norm Hermitian operators. Hence ‖B²‖ ≤ 8, so ‖B‖ ≤ 2√2, and |⟨B⟩| ≤ 2√2 for every state. ∎

The bound is *tight*: the singlet state with Pauli measurements at 45° relative angles saturates it. The Tsirelson bound therefore marks a genuine frontier — quantum correlations are strictly more nonlocal than classical ones yet strictly less than no-signalling allows. Characterizing the quantum set of correlations device-independently remains an active program; the Navascués–Pironio–Acín hierarchy approximates it from outside by semidefinite programming [17].

### 4.3 The Kochen–Specker Theorem and the Mermin–Peres Magic Square

> **Theorem (Kochen–Specker).** In Hilbert space dimension three or greater, there is no noncontextual assignment of definite values to all observables that preserves the functional relations among commuting observables [5].

> **Proof (Mermin–Peres magic square).** Consider the 3×3 array of two-qubit Pauli observables [6]:

|  |  |  |
|---|---|---|
| σz⊗I | I⊗σz | σz⊗σz |
| I⊗σx | σx⊗I | σx⊗σx |
| σz⊗σx | σx⊗σz | σy⊗σy |

> Each row and each column consists of mutually commuting observables with eigenvalues ±1. Every row multiplies to +I⊗I, as do the first two columns — but the third column multiplies to −I⊗I. A noncontextual assignment v would give each observable a value ±1, independent of its row or column context, with the value of a product equal to the product of values. Then the product of all nine values, grouped by rows, is (+1)(+1)(+1) = +1, while grouped by columns it is (+1)(+1)(−1) = −1 — a contradiction. ∎

This proof is *state-independent*: it constrains value assignments for every quantum state at once, whereas Bell's theorem constrains correlations of a particular entangled state. Cabello, Estebaranz and García-Alcaine later compressed the argument to 18 vectors in four dimensions [18], but the magic square remains the most transparent form.

### 4.4 The Sheaf-Theoretic Framework of Abramsky and Brandenburger

Abramsky and Brandenburger [7] showed that Bell nonlocality and Kochen–Specker contextuality are two faces of one mathematical structure. A *measurement scenario* is a triple (X, M, O): X labels the individual measurements, M is a *cover* of X by *contexts* (maximal sets of jointly measurable observables), and O is the outcome set. The *event sheaf* E assigns to each context U the set O^U of local sections — assignments of outcomes to the measurements in U — with restriction maps given by function restriction. An *empirical model* is a compatible family of probability distributions e_C over the sections of each context C, agreement on overlaps encoding no-signalling (or no-disturbance). A *global section* is a single distribution over O^X restricting to every e_C: precisely the noncontextual hidden-variable explanation.

> **Theorem (Abramsky–Brandenburger).** An empirical model is contextual if and only if it admits no global section; nonlocality is the special case where contexts are distributed across spacelike separated parties [7].

The framework stratifies no-go theorems by strength: *probabilistic* contextuality (Bell's model — no global section, though every individual section is locally possible), *logical* contextuality (Hardy's paradox — the mere support of the model already resists gluing), and *strong* contextuality (GHZ and Kochen–Specker — not even the support admits a global section). A further theorem equates the no-signalling models with exactly those admitting *signed* global sections: nonlocality is the price of insisting on positive probabilities, and negative-probability local models always exist [7]. Quantum theory, finally, satisfies no-signalling not only across tensor factors but for arbitrary families of commuting observables.

## 5 Empirical/Proofs

### Closing the loopholes

A Bell test is only as convincing as the assumptions it does not smuggle in. The three great loopholes are:

| Loophole | Extra assumption | How it is closed |
|---|---|---|
| Locality | Settings and outcomes spacelike separated | Kilometer-scale separation with fast random switching [9]; strict Einstein locality conditions [19] |
| Detection | Detected pairs fairly sample all pairs | High-efficiency detectors, event-ready schemes [9][10][20] |
| Freedom of choice | Settings independent of hidden variables | Cosmic photons and quantum random number generators [9] |

Early experiments paid these assumptions one at a time: Aspect's 1982 experiments used time-varying analyzers to enforce a form of locality [8]; Weihs et al. (1998) imposed strict Einstein locality with fast electro-optic modulators [19]; Rowe et al. (2001) closed the detection loophole with trapped ions [20]. None closed all at once.

In 2015 the dam broke. Hensen et al. at Delft entangled electron spins in diamond nitrogen-vacancy centers 1.3 km apart using an event-ready protocol, achieving efficient spin readout that removed the fair-sampling assumption; over 245 trials they found S = 2.42 ± 0.20, rejecting local realism with p = 0.039 even allowing the devices memory of past trials [9]. Giustina et al. in Vienna performed a significant loophole-free test with entangled photons and superconducting detectors [10], and Shalm et al. at NIST reported a strong loophole-free test of local realism with photons the same year [11]. Together, these experiments rule out the large classes of local realist theories that had survived on auxiliary assumptions for fifty years.

The CHSH logic can be checked numerically. The following script enumerates every deterministic local strategy — proving the classical ceiling — and evaluates the quantum value at the optimal angles:

```python
import itertools
import math

def chsh(Aa, Aap, Bb, Bbp):
    """CHSH combination for deterministic +/-1 outcomes."""
    return Aa*Bb + Aa*Bbp + Aap*Bb - Aap*Bbp

# all 16 deterministic local strategies
classical_max = max(
    abs(chsh(*s)) for s in itertools.product((-1, 1), repeat=4)
)
print(f"maximum |S| over local strategies: {classical_max}")  # 2

# quantum: singlet correlations E = -cos(2*delta), optimal angles
angles = {"a": 0.0, "ap": math.pi/4, "b": math.pi/8, "bp": -math.pi/8}
E = lambda x, y: -math.cos(2 * (angles[x] - angles[y]))
S_qm = E("a", "b") + E("a", "bp") + E("ap", "b") - E("ap", "bp")
print(f"quantum S at optimal angles: {S_qm:.4f}")
print(f"Tsirelson bound 2*sqrt(2):   {2*math.sqrt(2):.4f}")
```

The output confirms the theory: no local strategy exceeds 2, while quantum mechanics reaches −2√2.

### From contradiction to cryptography

Ekert realized in 1991 that Bell violation is not just a refutation but a *resource*: the very correlations that defeat local realism can certify secrecy, because monogamy of entanglement limits what any eavesdropper can know [14]. *Device-independent* cryptography pushes this to its logical extreme — security proved from the observed Bell violation alone, without trusting the internal workings of the devices. Barrett, Hardy and Kent proved security against no-signalling adversaries [21]; Pironio et al. showed that Bell violation certifies genuine randomness, with the output unpredictable to any adversary limited only by no-signalling [13]. Vazirani and Vidick then gave the first fully device-independent proof of quantum key distribution secure against general coherent quantum attacks, achieving a linear key rate while tolerating constant device noise [12]. The no-go theorems of the 1960s have become the security proofs of the 2020s.


## 6 Limitations

No-go theorems are only as strong as their assumptions, and several caveats survive. First, the *freedom-of-choice* loophole cannot be closed by logic alone: a superdeterministic theory in which settings and hidden variables share a common cause reproduces any correlations, at the price of a cosmic conspiracy. Cosmic Bell tests push the conspiracy back billions of years but cannot eliminate it in principle. Second, finite statistics always leave a nonzero p-value; the memory loophole — devices adapting across trials — is handled by martingale analyses such as Hensen's [9], but every experiment is finite. Third, Bell violations refute *local realism*, not every local theory: retrocausal and superdeterministic models remain logically open, and interpretations of quantum mechanics disagree about what exactly is ruled out.

On the contextuality side, experimental tests of the Kochen–Specker theorem require their own auxiliary assumptions — compatibility of the jointly measured observables and no-disturbance between contexts — and the finite-precision debate questions whether ideal projective measurements are ever realized. State-independent tests, such as the trapped-ion experiment of Kirchmair et al. [22], address the objection as far as experiment can. Finally, device-independent cryptography, while conceptually airtight, still yields modest key rates and demands detection efficiencies that remain challenging outside the laboratory; and the Tsirelson bound itself is a theorem *about* quantum theory, with device-independent characterizations of the quantum correlation set still incomplete [17].


## 7 Conclusion

Bell's theorem and the Kochen–Specker theorem began as impossibility results — proofs that the world cannot be both local and real, cannot assign values without contexts. Sixty years on, they read instead as *specifications*. The CHSH inequality [3] tells us exactly how far classical correlations can go; the Tsirelson bound [4] tells us exactly how far quantum correlations can go; the magic square [6] tells us that contextuality is woven into the operator algebra itself, independent of any state; and the sheaf-theoretic framework [7] tells us that all of these are obstructions to gluing local data into a global picture — nonlocality as the spacelike-separated special case of contextuality.

The loophole-free experiments of 2015 [9][10][11] converted these mathematical obstructions into laboratory facts, and device-independent cryptography [12][13] converted the facts into technology. The deepest lesson is methodological: in quantum foundations, a well-crafted *no* — a contradiction derived from clean assumptions — has repeatedly proven more fertile than a thousand speculative *yeses*. Contextuality is no longer a philosophical embarrassment to be explained away; it is the resource that powers quantum advantage, and its theory, from the CHSH inequality to sheaves, is the operating manual.
