# Silicon Photonics and Co-Packaged Optics for Datacenter Interconnects: Microring Modulators, Wavelength-Division Multiplexing, 1.6T Optical Engines, and the Path Beyond 224G Electrical SerDes

## Abstract

The scaling of datacenter switch and accelerator bandwidth is colliding with the fundamental limits of electrical signaling: at 224 Gb/s per lane, copper channel loss on a printed circuit board exceeds what even aggressive digital signal processing can compensate at acceptable power. This thesis develops the case for silicon photonics as the architectural substrate of next-generation datacenter interconnects, tracing the physics of depletion-mode microring modulators, the system implications of dense wavelength-division multiplexing on a single waveguide, and the packaging revolution of co-packaged optics (CPO) that relocates optical engines from the front panel into the switch or accelerator package. We analyze 1.6T optical engine architectures built on 8x200G lanes, compare ring-based and Mach-Zehnder modulation trade-offs, and evaluate the economic and reliability constraints that will decide whether CPO displaces pluggable optics at 3.2T and beyond. Link-budget models, packaging power comparisons, and a formal treatment of microring resonance alignment stability are included, alongside a critical survey of competing material platforms.

---

## 1. Introduction

Datacenter networks have absorbed a thousandfold increase in aggregate bandwidth over two decades while the underlying interconnect physics changed surprisingly little. Through the 400G and 800G eras, the optical-electrical-optical boundary sat at the faceplate: pluggable transceivers converted photons to electrons at the edge of the line card, and everything inside the box remained copper [1]. That arrangement is now failing. Switch ASICs at 51.2T radix and AI accelerators demanding petabit-per-second scale-out fabrics must push electrical lanes at 224 Gb/s, where printed-circuit-board trace losses of 30–35 dB over 40 mm force receiver architectures into power budgets that exceed the compute they serve [2].

Silicon photonics (SiPh) answers by migrating the electro-optic conversion point *inside* the package. By co-packaging photonic engines with switch ASICs and XPUs, the electrical channel shrinks from centimeters of PCB to millimeters of interposer, eliminating the retimer or heavy DSP stages that dominate pluggable power [3]. The enabling device is the silicon microring modulator (MRM): a resonant structure tens of micrometers in diameter that combines modulation and wavelength filtering, permitting dense wavelength-division multiplexing (WDM) on a single bus waveguide with aggregate bandwidths exceeding 1 Tb/s per fiber [4].

This thesis is organized as follows. Section 2 establishes the background physics: the plasma-dispersion effect in silicon, coupled-mode theory of ring resonators, and the link architecture of modern datacenter fabrics. Section 3 describes our methodology: how we model microring link budgets, derive alignment tolerances, and benchmark packaging alternatives. Section 4 presents a deep dive across four axes — microring modulators, WDM scaling, CPO packaging, and the 224G SerDes wall. Section 5 formalizes the key results as theorems and derives empirical consequences. Section 6 confronts the limitations honestly: thermal instability, laser integration, and the economics of yield. Section 7 concludes with a roadmap to 3.2T and the emerging material platforms that may follow silicon.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 Why copper is exhausted

The electrical channel is governed by the **Shannon–Hartley theorem** and the practical realities of skin effect, dielectric loss, and crosstalk. A differential pair on a server motherboard at 112 Gb/s PAM-4 tolerates roughly 25 dB of channel insertion loss with a strong DSP-based receiver; at 224 Gb/s, the Nyquist frequency doubles, skin losses scale as √*f*, and dielectric losses scale linearly with *f*, pushing realistic PCB channels past 35 dB [2]. Two responses exist: (i) burn more power in the receiver — forward-error correction, decision-feedback equalization with dozens of taps, and baud-rate clock recovery — or (ii) shorten the channel. Co-packaged optics choose (ii) and then exploit the physics of light, whose propagation loss in fiber is essentially independent of the modulation rate.

### 2.2 The plasma-dispersion effect in silicon

Silicon has no Pockels effect, so modulation exploits the **free-carrier plasma-dispersion effect** described by Soref and Bennett: injected or depleted carriers change the refractive index Δ*n* and absorption Δ*α* [5]. In depletion-mode MRMs, a reverse-biased *pn* junction straddles the waveguide; depleting carriers shifts the ring resonance wavelength by picometers per volt. The efficiency is captured by the *V*<sub>π</sub>*L* figure of merit, but for rings the more relevant metric is the modulation efficiency normalized to resonance linewidth — a sharp resonance converts small index shifts into large intensity modulation, at the cost of thermal sensitivity.

> **Theorem:** For a microring of radius *R* and group index *n*<sub>g</sub>, the resonance wavelengths satisfy *mλ*<sub>res</sub> = 2π*Rn*<sub>eff</sub>. A fractional effective-index change Δ*n*<sub>eff</sub>/*n*<sub>eff</sub> shifts the resonance by Δ*λ* = λ<sub>res</sub> · (Δ*n*<sub>eff</sub>/*n*<sub>eff</sub>).

### 2.3 Datacenter fabric context

Modern scale-out fabrics (Ethernet for AI, InfiniBand, UALink) and scale-up links (NVLink, UALink, PCIe/CXL) all converge on the same requirement: **bandwidth density** (Tb/s per mm of package edge) and **energy per bit** (pJ/bit). Pluggable optics achieve roughly 800 Gb/s per faceplate module; CPO optical engines target 1.6T and 3.2T per engine with shoreline densities above 1 Tb/s/mm [3][6]. The OIF and COBO/CPO industry bodies have formalized multi-source agreements for 1.6T and 3.2T engines, with 200 Gb/s electrical lanes (CEI-224G) as the immediate SerDes target and 400 Gb/s lanes on the horizon.

## 3. Methodology

Our analysis proceeds on three methodological pillars:

1. **Analytical device modeling.** We derive microring transmission from coupled-mode theory, compute extinction ratio (ER), insertion loss (IL), and optical modulation amplitude (OMA) as functions of coupling coefficient κ, round-trip loss *a*, and resonance detuning. Thermal drift is modeled as a first-order wavelength perturbation with a measured coefficient of ~80 pm/°C for silicon rings.
2. **Link-budget accounting.** End-to-end budgets are constructed for two reference architectures: (a) a pluggable 800G-DR8/1.6T module with retimed DSP, and (b) a CPO engine with 8 × 200 Gb/s PAM-4 lanes, direct-drive or lightly equalized. Power is partitioned into laser, modulator driver, thermal tuning, receiver TIA, and DSP/FEC contributions using published component data [4][7].
3. **Comparative systems benchmarking.** We benchmark ring-based versus Mach–Zehnder transmitter banks on area, power, and WDM channel count, and compare material platforms (Si, thin-film lithium niobate, indium phosphide) on modulation bandwidth, drive voltage, and integration complexity [8].

All quantitative claims are traceable to published measurements; where the literature reports ranges, we adopt conservative (pessimistic-for-optics) values to avoid overstating the case.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Microring Modulators: Physics, Bandwidth, and the 400G-per-λ Frontier

The microring modulator is the workhorse of dense WDM SiPh. A ring of radius 5–10 μm coupled to a bus waveguide exhibits a comb of resonances spaced by the free spectral range (FSR ≈ λ²/*n*<sub>g</sub>·2π*R*). Modulating the carrier density shifts one resonance across the laser wavelength, producing intensity modulation with an extinction ratio governed by the coupling regime:

| Coupling regime | κ² vs. (1−a²) | Extinction behavior | Suitability |
|---|---|---|---|
| Under-coupled | κ² < 1−a² | Shallow notch, low ER | Poor for modulation |
| Critically coupled | κ² ≈ 1−a² | Deep notch, max ER | Optimal for OOK/PAM-4 |
| Over-coupled | κ² > 1−a² | Broad notch, lower Q | Higher bandwidth, lower ER |

State-of-the-art depletion-mode MRMs now demonstrate 400 Gb/s per wavelength using high-baud-rate PAM signaling, with driver swings below 1 V<sub>pp</sub> — a result that would have seemed implausible five years ago [4]. The key enablers are:

- **Photon-lifetime-limited bandwidth engineering.** Over-coupling trades Q for bandwidth; 3-dB electro-optic bandwidths above 60 GHz are reported with acceptable ER penalty.
- **Self-heating compensation.** Integrated heaters lock the resonance to the laser grid; closed-loop thermal control units in the companion electronic IC (EIC) sense photocurrent and servo heater power [7].
- **Driverless and bias-free architectures.** Recent work demonstrates MRM transmitters operating below CMOS core voltage (0.87 V in 12 nm FinFET), eliminating the power-hungry high-swing driver entirely [4].

```python
# Resonance wavelength shift from carrier depletion (Soref–Bennett, first order)
def ring_resonance_shift(lambda_res_nm, dN_cm3, overlap=0.8):
    # Δn ≈ -8.8e-22 * ΔN  (electrons, 1550 nm), Δλ = λ * Δn_eff / n_eff
    dn = -8.8e-22 * dN_cm3 * overlap
    n_eff = 2.44
    return lambda_res_nm * dn / n_eff  # nm

for dN in [1e17, 5e17, 1e18]:
    print(f"ΔN={dN:.0e} cm^-3 -> Δλ={ring_resonance_shift(1310, dN)*1e3:.1f} pm")
```

> **Theorem:** The small-signal electro-optic bandwidth of an MRM is bounded by *f*<sub>3dB</sub> ≤ *c*/(2π*n*<sub>g</sub>*R*·*Q*<sub>loaded</sub>) + *f*<sub>RC</sub>, i.e., the loaded quality factor and the junction RC time constant impose independent, additive-rate limits. Bandwidth can therefore only be raised by lowering Q (over-coupling) or shrinking junction capacitance — never by drive voltage alone.

### 4.2 Wavelength-Division Multiplexing: 8, 16, 32 Lanes on One Fiber

WDM is what converts per-λ heroics into system bandwidth. A single silicon bus waveguide can carry 8, 16, or 32 wavelength channels, each modulated by its own ring and dropped by a matched ring demultiplexer at the receiver [4][7]. The scaling law is straightforward:

- **Aggregate bandwidth** = *N*<sub>λ</sub> × *R*<sub>λ</sub>: 8 λ × 200 Gb/s = 1.6 Tb/s per fiber per direction.
- **Channel spacing** is set by ring FSR, thermal crosstalk between adjacent heaters, and inter-channel crosstalk in the demultiplexer; 100–200 GHz DWDM grids are standard in the O-band (1310 nm) for datacenter reach.
- **Light sources** remain the thorniest integration problem. Options include (i) external multi-wavelength DFB arrays coupled through edge couplers [7], (ii) quantum-dot comb lasers, and (iii) heterogeneous integration of III–V gain sections via wafer bonding or micro-transfer printing [6].

Frequency-comb sources based on silicon-nitride microresonators offer an elegant alternative: a single pump laser generates dozens of phase-coherent lines, collapsing the laser inventory problem [9]. Inverse-designed multiplexers further reduce insertion loss and crosstalk in the (de)multiplexing fabric [9].

### 4.3 Co-Packaged Optics: Moving the O/E/O Boundary Into the Package

Co-packaged optics relocates the optical engine from the front panel to the switch or XPU package substrate. The electrical channel between the SerDes and the photonic IC collapses from ~100 mm of PCB, connectors, and cage to a few millimeters of package routing, cutting channel loss from 20–35 dB to roughly 4 dB [1][10]. Consequences:

1. **DSP elimination.** With 4 dB of loss, a simple analog CDR or even direct-drive architecture suffices; the retimer/DSP ASIC — historically 30–40% of pluggable module power — can be removed [10].
2. **Power.** Published CPO demonstrators report ~5 pJ/bit versus 15–20 pJ/bit for DSP-based pluggables, with Intel's integrated-photonic path targeting ~1.3 pJ/bit in advanced implementations [10].
3. **Bandwidth density.** Optical engines achieve multi-Tb/s per package edge, enabling 51.2T and 102.4T switch packages without faceplate congestion [6].
4. **Reliability.** Fewer connectors and shorter channels reduce link flap; field data cited by CPO proponents shows improved link stability versus pluggable chains [1].

The architecture is not free: fiber must now be attached to the package (fiber pigtailing or detachable connectors), the laser's thermal and reliability domain merges with the ASIC's, and repairability changes from "swap a module" to "service a package." The industry is converging on standards (OIF CPO-112G/224G, COBO) precisely to manage these trade-offs [3].

### 4.4 The 224G SerDes Wall and the 1.6T Optical Engine

The 1.6T optical engine is the industry's consensus next step: 8 lanes × 200 Gb/s electrical (CEI-224G-LR with PAM-4) driving 8 × 200 Gb/s optical lanes [6][8]. The "224G wall" refers to the observation that each doubling of electrical lane rate now costs disproportionately more power per bit than the last, because equalization complexity grows faster than linearly with channel loss. Quantitatively:

- At 112G PAM-4, a typical long-reach receiver consumes ~3–4 pJ/bit in the DSP.
- At 224G PAM-4, published DSP figures approach 6–8 pJ/bit — and the channel must be *shorter*, not longer, to close the link.
- Beyond 224G, coherent-lite or co-packaged optics become not merely attractive but necessary: there is no credible PCB channel at 448G PAM-4.

Linear pluggable optics (LPO) and linear receive optics (LRO) represent the pluggable camp's counter-move: removing the DSP from the module and pushing equalization back into the host ASIC, recovering much of CPO's power advantage while preserving field replaceability [8]. The contest between LPO and CPO will be decided less by physics than by operations — who owns the failure domain, and who pays for the fiber attach.

```rust
// Simplified link-budget check: can a 224G lane close over `loss_db`?
fn link_closes(loss_db: f64, rx_sensitivity_dbm: f64, tx_oma_dbm: f64) -> bool {
    tx_oma_dbm - loss_db > rx_sensitivity_dbm
}

let tx = 3.0;      // dBm OMA, 200G PAM-4 optical lane
let rx = -9.5;     // dBm sensitivity at 1e-4 pre-FEC BER
for loss in [4.0, 12.0, 25.0, 35.0] {
    println!("loss {loss:>5.1} dB -> closes: {}", link_closes(loss, rx, tx));
}
```

## 5. Empirical Results and Proofs

We now state the central quantitative results of this thesis.

> **Theorem (CPO power advantage):** For a 1.6T engine with 8 × 200 Gb/s lanes, relocating the O/E/O boundary from the faceplate (channel loss *L*<sub>p</sub> ≈ 25 dB) into the package (*L*<sub>cpo</sub> ≈ 4 dB) reduces receiver equalization power by at least a factor of two, because required DSP tap count and ADC resolution scale superlinearly with loss. *Proof sketch:* The minimum-mean-square-error of a *K*-tap FFE scales as σ² ∝ 1/SNR<sub>eff</sub>, and SNR<sub>eff</sub> degrades exponentially with channel loss at fixed transmit swing; published 224G DSP power figures (6–8 pJ/bit at 25 dB vs. <3 pJ/bit for short-reach direct-drive) confirm the superlinear trend [2][10].

> **Theorem (WDM shoreline scaling):** An MRM-based WDM transmitter bank of *N* channels on one bus waveguide achieves aggregate bandwidth *B* = *N*·*R*<sub>λ</sub> with area scaling *O*(*N*) and laser-power scaling *O*(*N*) but fiber-count scaling *O*(1). Hence shoreline bandwidth density grows linearly with channel count at fixed faceplate, unlike parallel-fiber (PSM) approaches whose density saturates at connector pitch. *Empirical basis:* 8-λ demonstrations at 32 Gb/s/λ aggregate 256 Gb/s per fiber [7]; 400 Gb/s/λ results extend the same architecture to 3.2 Tb/s per fiber with 8 wavelengths [4].

> **Theorem (Thermal alignment bound):** A silicon microring with thermal coefficient 80 pm/°C and loaded Q of 10,000 (linewidth ≈ 130 pm at 1310 nm) requires resonance locking to within ±65 pm — i.e., ±0.8 °C — to hold ER within 1 dB of optimum. This bound *mandates* per-ring closed-loop thermal control in any production WDM engine, and its power (milliwatts per ring) must be included in every honest pJ/bit accounting [4][7].

**Empirical power comparison** (representative published values, 1.6T class):

| Architecture | DSP/retimer | Laser + drivers | Thermal tuning | Total (pJ/bit) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| DSP pluggable 1.6T | 6–8 | 4–5 | 1–2 | 15–20 |
| Linear pluggable (LPO) | 0 (host-side) | 4–5 | 1–2 | 8–12 |
| CPO, direct-drive | 0–1 | 3–4 | 1–2 | ~5 |
| CPO, advanced (Intel path) | 0 | 1–2 | <1 | ~1.3 |

The table explains the industry's direction of travel: every architectural step that shortens the electrical channel removes a power term that copper physics had made mandatory [1][10].

## 6. Limitations

Intellectual honesty requires confronting where the silicon-photonics story is weakest.

1. **Thermal fragility of rings.** The ±0.8 °C locking requirement derived above is unforgiving. In a switch package dissipating hundreds of watts, thermal crosstalk between the ASIC and the photonic IC is a first-order design constraint; heater power for dozens of rings is not negligible and scales with channel count [7].
2. **Laser integration and reliability.** Silicon cannot emit light efficiently. Every architecture depends on III–V lasers — external, heterogeneously integrated, or quantum-dot — and the laser remains the dominant failure-rate contributor. Moving lasers into the package (as some CPO designs do) merges the laser's wear-out domain with the switch's service model, an operational regression versus faceplate replacement [6][10].
3. **Fiber attach at scale.** A 51.2T CPO switch requires hundreds of fibers attached to the package with sub-micron alignment stability over temperature and mechanical shock. Detachable fiber connectors for CPO remain an active standardization battleground [3].
4. **Material limits of silicon modulation.** The plasma-dispersion effect is weak and lossy; silicon MRMs will struggle past ~400 Gb/s per λ [1]. Thin-film lithium niobate (bandwidths >110 GHz at sub-1 V drive) and indium phosphide offer headroom but at integration and cost penalties [8].
5. **Economic and ecosystem risk.** CPO concentrates value in advanced packaging (TSMC COUPE, Samsung's SiPh PDK) and a handful of photonic foundries; yield learning on photonic-electronic co-integration is years behind CMOS logic. Pluggable optics, by contrast, benefit from a mature multi-vendor ecosystem and will not stand still — Broadcom's latest DSP generations cut pluggable power by over 20% per node [1].
6. **Modulation-format ceiling.** Intensity-modulation direct-detection (IM-DD) with PAM-4 faces an OSNR wall as baud rates climb; the eventual move to coherent-lite for datacenter reach would reintroduce DSP complexity that CPO sought to eliminate.

---

## 7. Conclusion

The evidence assembled in this thesis supports a single conclusion: **the electrical interconnect has reached the end of its economical scaling, and silicon photonics — via microring-based WDM engines in co-packaged form — is the successor technology, not a supplement.** The 224 Gb/s SerDes generation is the last for which copper channels can be closed at acceptable power on practical PCBs; 1.6T optical engines built on 8 × 200 Gb/s lanes are in production now, and the 3.2T generation will be co-packaged or it will not ship at competitive power.

Three predictions follow. *First*, WDM channel counts per fiber will grow (8 → 16 → 32 λ) faster than per-λ rates, because rings make wavelengths cheap and electronics make baud rates expensive. *Second*, the pluggable-vs-CPO contest will bifurcate by application: CPO for switch and scale-out AI fabrics where density rules, linear pluggables where field serviceability rules. *Third*, silicon's modulation weakness will eventually force heterogeneous integration of stronger electro-optic materials — thin-film lithium niobate or electro-optic polymers — into the silicon photonics flow, repeating the industry's earlier absorption of germanium photodetectors.

The photon, having waited a century in the wings of communications, is finally being invited onto the package. The engineering now is execution: thermal control, fiber attach, yield, and standards. The physics is settled.
