## Abstract

Ordinary gradient descent implicitly assumes that the space of model parameters is flat and Euclidean, an assumption that collapses the moment parameters encode a probability distribution. Information geometry, Shun-ichi Amari's program beginning in the 1980s, supplies the missing structure: the Fisher information matrix equips the statistical manifold with a Riemannian metric — the Fisher–Rao metric — under which the steepest-descent direction is not the Euclidean gradient but the *natural gradient* [1][2]. This thesis develops the full geometric machinery: exponential families as dually flat manifolds carrying a pair of torsion-free affine connections (the e-connection and the m-connection), the Legendre duality between canonical and expectation parameters, and the α-divergence family containing Kullback–Leibler divergence as a special case [3]. We trace the natural gradient into modern deep learning — Kronecker-factored approximations (K-FAC) making Fisher preconditioning tractable [4], natural policy gradients and trust-region methods (TRPO/PPO) in reinforcement learning [5][6], and the information-geometric reading of the EM algorithm as alternating e/m-projections [3]. The claim: second-order learning is not merely an acceleration trick but the correct intrinsic calculus of learning on curved statistical manifolds.

## 1 Introduction

A small Euclidean displacement $\delta\theta$ can correspond to an enormous change in the distribution — or to no change at all, when directions in parameter space lie along fibers of an unidentifiable symmetry. Standard gradient descent, $\theta \leftarrow \theta - \eta \nabla L(\theta)$, is blind to this structure: it measures the size of a step with the Euclidean norm $\|\delta\theta\|_2$ rather than with the only norm the problem actually cares about, the information-theoretic distance between the distributions before and after the step [1].

Amari's decisive move was to take the statistical manifold seriously as a geometric object. The Fisher information matrix $F(\theta)$, defined as the covariance of the score,

$$F(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{p(x;\theta)}\big[\nabla_\theta \log p(x;\theta)\,\nabla_\theta \log p(x;\theta)^\top\big],$$

turns out to be a Riemannian metric tensor: it defines an infinitesimal line element $ds^2 = d\theta^\top F(\theta)\, d\theta$ whose integral along a path is the *information length*, and whose associated geodesic distance is — up to a monotone transformation — the unique distance on distributions that is invariant under sufficient statistics and monotone maps. The celebrated Fisher–Rao–Cencov theorem [3] establishes that the Fisher metric is essentially the *only* Riemannian metric on a statistical manifold with this invariance property, which explains why it keeps reappearing uninvited in maximum likelihood theory, Bayesian inference, and deep learning.

The practical consequence of this geometry is the *natural gradient* $\tilde{\nabla} L = F(\theta)^{-1} \nabla L(\theta)$, the direction of steepest descent measured against the Fisher metric rather than the Euclidean one [1][2]. The natural gradient is Fisher-efficient in the online setting, escapes the flat plateaus that plague multilayer perceptrons, and is invariant under smooth reparameterizations — a property no Euclidean gradient can claim [1]. Yet two facts kept this elegant theory at arm's length from practitioners for decades: inverting $F(\theta)$ costs $O(N^3)$ for $N$ parameters, and the full geometric apparatus — connections, divergences, duality — appeared to require a differential geometry PhD to operate.

This thesis argues that the barrier was pedagogical rather than technical. We develop the complete theory from the ground up, prove the core structural theorems, and then show that the modern algorithmic renaissance — K-FAC, TRPO, PPO, diagonal Fisher penalties in continual learning — is best understood, and best debugged, as applied information geometry. The geometry is not decoration; it is the *reason* these algorithms work.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Statistical manifolds and the Fisher metric

A *statistical model* is a family $S = \{p(x;\theta) : \theta \in \Theta \subset \mathbb{R}^n\}$ of probability distributions, where the parameterization $\theta \mapsto p(\cdot;\theta)$ is smooth and injective. The set $S$ is treated as a differentiable manifold, with $\theta = (\theta^1, \dots, \theta^n)$ as local coordinates and the score functions $\partial_i \ell(x;\theta) = \frac{\partial}{\partial\theta^i} \log p(x;\theta)$ spanning the tangent space $T_p S$ at each point [3].

> **Theorem:** Under regularity conditions, the Fisher information matrix $F(\theta)$ with components $g_{ij}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}[\partial_i \ell \cdot \partial_j \ell]$ is a positive semi-definite symmetric tensor. When the model is identifiable, it is positive definite and defines a Riemannian metric on $S$, the Fisher–Rao metric.

The proof rests on the score identity $\mathbb{E}[\partial_i \ell] = 0$, which makes $g_{ij}$ a genuine covariance. Critically, the Fisher metric is invariant under reparameterization: if $\phi = \phi(\theta)$ is a diffeomorphism, the metric transforms covariantly and the Fisher norm of any tangent vector is preserved [3].

Chentsov's theorem sharpens this invariance into uniqueness: any Riemannian metric on the probability simplex (or on general statistical manifolds, in the Amari–Nagaoka formulation) that is *monotone* under Markov morphisms — i.e., that never increases when data are passed through a stochastic channel — must be a scalar multiple of the Fisher metric [3]. Monotonicity under information processing is a non-negotiable requirement for any sane notion of statistical distance, so the Fisher metric is not one choice among many: it is the only one.

### 2.2 Exponential families: the canonical examples

The *exponential family* is the arena where information geometry is fully explicit. A distribution in canonical form reads

$$p(x;\theta) = \exp\big(\theta \cdot T(x) - \psi(\theta)\big)\, h(x),$$

with canonical (natural) parameter $\theta$, sufficient statistic $T(x)$, log-partition function $\psi(\theta)$, and carrier measure $h(x)$. The family includes the Gaussian, Poisson, Bernoulli, exponential, Dirichlet, and Wishart distributions, among many others [3].

The log-partition function is convex, and its gradient yields the *expectation parameter* $\eta = \nabla\psi(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_\theta[T(X)]$. Amari's dually flat geometry arises from the pair of affine connections $\nabla^{(e)}$ and $\nabla^{(m)}$ — the exponential (e) connection, flat in the $\theta$-coordinates, and the mixture (m) connection, flat in the $\eta$-coordinates. The two connections are dual with respect to the Fisher metric, and the convex potential $\psi$ mediates the Legendre duality between them [3]. This dually flat structure will return in Section 4 as the engine of the EM algorithm.

### 2.3 Divergences and the α-family

Information geometry replaces the single Euclidean notion of distance with a *family* of divergences. For $\alpha \in \mathbb{R}$, the α-divergence is

$$D^{(\alpha)}(p \| q) = \frac{4}{1-\alpha^2}\left(1 - \int p^{\frac{1-\alpha}{2}}(x)\, q^{\frac{1+\alpha}{2}}(x)\, dx\right),$$

with the Kullback–Leibler divergence $D_{KL}(p\|q)$ recovered in the limit $\alpha \to 1$, the Hellinger distance at $\alpha = 0$, and the reverse KL divergence at $\alpha \to -1$ [3]. The Fisher metric is the infinitesimal limit of *every* α-divergence: for any fixed α,

$$D^{(\alpha)}(p_\theta \| p_{\theta + d\theta}) = \frac{1}{2}\, d\theta^\top F(\theta)\, d\theta + O(\|d\theta\|^3),$$

so the entire family agrees to second order and induces the same Riemannian geometry, differing only in their higher-order, affine-connection structure. This fact is the geometric explanation for why natural gradient descent is indifferent to which divergence one uses to define the local trust region — provided the trust region is small.

## 3 Methodology

Our methodology is twofold: first, a rigorous reconstruction of the natural gradient from first principles of information geometry, culminating in the dually flat structure of exponential families and the e/m-connection calculus; second, an analytical derivation of the principal algorithmic approximations — diagonal Fisher, block-Kronecker factorization (K-FAC), empirical Fisher, and trust-region policy updates — as principled geometric compromises, each justified by which structural property of the exact Fisher metric it preserves and which it sacrifices.

We proceed axiomatically where possible. The key analytical moves are:

1. **Derive the natural gradient as constrained steepest descent.** Minimize the first-order loss decrement $\nabla L(\theta)^\top \delta\theta$ subject to the Fisher-norm constraint $\delta\theta^\top F(\theta)\, \delta\theta = \epsilon^2$ rather than the Euclidean constraint $\|\delta\theta\|_2^2 = \epsilon^2$. The Lagrangian yields $\delta\theta \propto -F(\theta)^{-1}\nabla L(\theta)$ [1][2].
2. **Establish the dual affine structure.** For exponential families, compute the e- and m-connection coefficients in $\theta$- and $\eta$-coordinates, and show both connections are torsion-free and dually coupled: $X g(Y,Z) = g(\nabla_X Y, Z) + g(Y, \nabla^*_X Z)$ [3].
3. **Factor the Fisher matrix for deep networks.** Under the mean-field random-network analysis of Amari, Karakida, and Oizumi, prove that the Fisher matrix is unit-wise block diagonal up to small off-block terms, justifying quasi-diagonal and Kronecker-factored approximations with explicit inverses [7].
4. **Map RL trust regions onto the manifold.** Show that the natural policy gradient of Kakade [6] is steepest ascent of the expected return under the Fisher metric on the policy manifold, and that TRPO's KL-constrained surrogate objective is a practical realization of a Fisher trust region [5].

The code snippets below encode the core computations in executable form: a Python implementation of the natural gradient update with damping, a Haskell formulation of the Legendre transform underlying dual flatness, a Rust K-FAC layer update, and a TLA+ specification of the EM alternating-projection loop.

```python
import numpy as np

def natural_gradient_update(theta, grad, fisher, eta=1e-2, damping=1e-3):
    """Amari natural gradient step: theta <- theta - eta * F^{-1} grad.

    Damping (F + lam I) stabilizes inversion when F is ill-conditioned,
    a standard compromise preserving approximate Fisher efficiency [1].
    """
    n = fisher.shape[0]
    F_damped = fisher + damping * np.eye(n)
    nat_grad = np.linalg.solve(F_damped, grad)   # F^{-1} grad
    return theta - eta * nat_grad
```

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The natural gradient as covariant steepest descent

Let $L(\theta)$ be a differentiable loss on the parameter manifold and consider the infinitesimal update that minimizes the linearized loss subject to a bounded Fisher displacement:

$$\min_{\delta\theta}\, L(\theta) + \nabla L(\theta)^\top \delta\theta \quad \text{s.t.}\quad \delta\theta^\top F(\theta)\, \delta\theta = \epsilon^2.$$

With Lagrange multiplier $\lambda$, the stationarity condition $\nabla L + 2\lambda F\,\delta\theta = 0$ gives $\delta\theta \propto -F(\theta)^{-1}\nabla L(\theta)$ [1]. Two properties distinguish this update from its Euclidean cousin:

- **Reparameterization invariance.** If $\phi = \phi(\theta)$, then $\tilde{\nabla}_\phi L = J^{-T} \tilde{\nabla}_\theta L$ with $J = \partial\phi/\partial\theta$, meaning the update direction as a *tangent vector* is intrinsic to the manifold: it does not depend on which coordinates we happened to choose [2][3].
- **Fisher efficiency.** In online maximum-likelihood estimation, the natural gradient update achieves the Cramér–Rao bound asymptotically: no other unbiased estimator can converge faster. Amari proved this Fisher efficiency for the natural gradient learning rule in [1], the result that first brought information geometry into machine learning.

The contrast with Newton's method is instructive. Newton's method uses the Hessian $\nabla^2 L$ as its preconditioner; the natural gradient uses the *expected* outer product of scores, which equals the expected negative Hessian of the log-likelihood under the model (Fisher's identity) [3]. The natural gradient is therefore a *model-based* second-order method: it trusts the geometry of the statistical manifold rather than the local curvature of a particular loss function, and it remains sensible even when the loss is not locally quadratic — the regime where Newton fails [2].

### 4.2 Dually flat manifolds, e/m-connections, and the generalized Pythagorean theorem

The deepest structural result of information geometry is the characterization of *dually flat* manifolds: manifolds equipped with a Riemannian metric $g$ and a pair of torsion-free affine connections $\nabla$, $\nabla^*$ such that both are flat and dually coupled via $g$ [3]. Exponential families are the canonical dually flat manifolds, with $\nabla^{(e)}$ flat in canonical coordinates $\theta$ and $\nabla^{(m)}$ flat in expectation coordinates $\eta$.

> **Theorem:** (Generalized Pythagorean theorem [3].) Let $S$ be dually flat with dual affine coordinate systems $\theta$ (e-flat) and $\eta$ (m-flat). For points $p, q, r \in S$, if the e-geodesic from $p$ to $q$ is $g$-orthogonal to the m-geodesic from $q$ to $r$, then $D_\psi(p\|r) = D_\psi(p\|q) + D_\psi(q\|r)$, where $D_\psi$ is the canonical (Bregman) divergence generated by the convex potential $\psi$.

This Pythagorean decomposition is the geometric heart of the EM algorithm (Section 4.4) and of variational inference more broadly: likelihood maximization decomposes into orthogonal projections that can be performed in closed form in dual coordinates.

```rust
// K-FAC layer update: Fisher block F_l ≈ A_l ⊗ G_l, inverted as
// F_l^{-1} = A_l^{-1} ⊗ G_l^{-1}, reducing inversion from O((mn)^3)
// to O(m^3 + n^3) per layer [4].

struct KfacLayer {
    a: DMatrix<f64>, // activation covariance E[a a^T]
    g: DMatrix<f64>, // pre-activation grad covariance E[ds ds^T]
}

impl KfacLayer {
    fn precondition(&self, grad: &DMatrix<f64>) -> DMatrix<f64> {
        // (A ⊗ G)^{-1} vec(dW) = vec(G^{-1} dW A^{-1})
        let a_inv = self.a.clone().try_inverse().unwrap();
        let g_inv = self.g.clone().try_inverse().unwrap();
        g_inv * grad * a_inv
    }
}
```

### 4.3 Making it tractable: diagonal, Kronecker, and empirical Fisher approximations

The exact Fisher matrix is $N \times N$ for $N$ parameters — $10^{14}$ entries for a billion-parameter model. Practical natural gradient descent is therefore an art of *approximation*, and each approximation can be read geometrically as a choice of which structure to keep [7]:

| Approximation | Fisher structure kept | Cost | Geometric sacrifice |
|---|---|---|---|
| Diagonal Fisher | $\mathrm{diag}(F)$ only | $O(N)$ | Loses all curvature coupling; coincides with Adam-style scaling |
| Unit-wise block diagonal | Per-neuron blocks [7] | $O(N \cdot h^2)$ | Ignores inter-unit correlations |
| K-FAC | $F_\ell \approx A_\ell \otimes G_\ell$ per layer [4] | $O(m^3+n^3)$ per layer | Assumes activation/gradient independence |
| Empirical Fisher | $\sum_i \nabla\ell_i \nabla\ell_i^\top$ on data | $O(N)$ per sample | Uses data labels, not model samples; biased for $F$ |
| Diagonal + damping | $\mathrm{diag}(F) + \lambda I$ | $O(N)$ | Shrinks toward Euclidean gradient |

The distinction between the *true Fisher* (expectation over the model's predictive distribution $p_\theta(y|x)$) and the *empirical Fisher* (outer products of gradients on the training data) is frequently blurred in practice but geometrically essential: only the true Fisher is a Riemannian metric on the model manifold, invariant under reparameterization [3][4].

K-FAC (Martens and Grosse [4]) is the most faithful scalable approximation.

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

We now state the key theoretical results that the preceding machinery makes available, in theorem form.

> **Theorem:** (Amari, 1998 [1].) For online maximum-likelihood learning, the natural gradient update $\theta_{t+1} = \theta_t - \eta_t F(\theta_t)^{-1} \nabla \ell(\theta_t; x_t)$ is Fisher-efficient: its asymptotic covariance attains the Cramér–Rao lower bound, and no stochastic gradient rule using only the ordinary gradient can do better.

> **Theorem:** (Chentsov uniqueness [3].) Up to a constant factor, the Fisher information metric is the unique Riemannian metric on a statistical manifold that is monotone (non-increasing) under Markov morphisms. Hence any reparameterization-invariant, information-processing-consistent notion of local distance must be the Fisher metric.



1. **Plateau escape.** Rattray, Saad, and Amari showed via statistical-mechanical analysis that natural gradient learning escapes the symmetric plateaus of multilayer perceptrons exponentially faster than ordinary gradient descent, because the Fisher metric correctly rescales the nearly-flat directions [1].
2. **K-FAC at scale.** Martens and Grosse [4] demonstrated that Kronecker-factored natural gradients train deep autoencoders and classifiers in far fewer iterations than well-tuned SGD or Adam, with per-iteration cost only a small constant factor above first-order methods — the theoretical promise of second-order learning finally realized in wall-clock time.
3. **Natural policy gradients.** Kakade [6] proved that the natural policy gradient — steepest ascent of expected return under the Fisher metric on the policy manifold — converges to a locally optimal policy, and is invariant to the policy parameterization. Schulman et al. [5] turned this into the practical TRPO algorithm by constraining each update to a KL trust region (a Fisher-norm ball to first order), producing the monotone-improvement guarantee $\eta(\pi') \geq L_{\pi}(\pi') - C \cdot \max_s D_{KL}(\pi\|\pi')$ that made deep reinforcement learning stable. 
4. **Variational inference.** Hoffman et al.'s stochastic variational inference is natural gradient descent on the variational parameters of an exponential-family posterior, where the Fisher inverse is available in closed form — the dually flat structure of Section 4.2 doing the heavy lifting silently [3].

The through-line is consistent: whenever an algorithm respects the Fisher geometry — even approximately — it inherits invariance, efficiency, and stability properties that Euclidean methods must recover by luck or by tuning.

## 6 Limitations

The theory, for all its elegance, carries genuine limitations that practitioners must weigh honestly.

- **Computational cost is fundamental, not incidental.** The $O(N^3)$ inversion (or $O(N^2)$ storage) of the exact Fisher matrix is not an implementation detail; it reflects the genuine information content of second-order structure. Every scalable approximation — diagonal, K-FAC, empirical — discards some of the geometry, and with it some of the guarantees. 
- **Singularities and model misspecification.** The Fisher metric degenerates at singularities of the parameter manifold (where the model is unidentifiable), and the natural gradient is undefined there without damping or regularization. Amari, Park, and Ozeki showed that learning dynamics near singularities can be pathological even for the natural gradient [3]. Moreover, Fisher efficiency is a statement about the *well-specified* case; under model misspecification, the natural gradient loses its optimality guarantees while retaining its cost.
- **The geometry is local.** The Fisher metric governs infinitesimal displacements; the KL divergence is its second-order shadow. Trust-region methods like TRPO extrapolate this local geometry to finite steps, and the monotone-improvement bound degrades as steps grow [5]. 
- **Divergence choice matters at finite scale.** All α-divergences induce the same Fisher metric infinitesimally, but they define different finite-step geometries and different projections. The choice between KL, reverse KL, and Hellinger objectives in variational inference is therefore a genuinely consequential modeling decision that the local geometry cannot settle [3].
- **Dually flat structure is special, not generic.** The beautiful e/m-projection calculus of Section 4.4 applies exactly to exponential families. For general deep generative models, the manifold is not dually flat, the Pythagorean theorem fails, and EM-style alternating projections become heuristic rather than exact [3].

## 7 Conclusion

Information geometry reframes learning as movement on a curved statistical manifold, and the Fisher information matrix is the unavoidable metric of that movement — unique by Chentsov's theorem, invariant under reparameterization, and the infinitesimal limit of every well-behaved divergence [3]. The natural gradient is not an exotic alternative to gradient descent; it *is* gradient descent, computed correctly, with the geometry the problem actually possesses [1][2]. The dually flat structure of exponential families — the e/m-connection pair, Legendre duality, and the generalized Pythagorean theorem — turns this abstract geometry into concrete algorithms: closed-form natural gradients, alternating-projection EM, and natural variational inference [3][7].

The modern renaissance of second-order learning is, at bottom, the rediscovery of this geometry under engineering constraints. K-FAC preserves the Kronecker structure the mean-field analysis reveals [4][7]; TRPO and PPO implement Fisher trust regions on the policy manifold [5][6]; diagonal Fisher penalties in continual learning approximate the metric's memory of important directions. Each works precisely to the extent that it respects the underlying manifold — and fails in exactly the ways the geometry predicts when it does not. The lesson for the next generation of optimizers is Amari's original one: before asking *how fast* an algorithm descends, ask *in what space* it is descending. Get the geometry right, and the speed follows.
