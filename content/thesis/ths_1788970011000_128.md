# Stratospheric Aerosol Injection: Radiative Forcing, Stratospheric Chemistry, and the Governance of Solar Geoengineering

## Abstract

Stratospheric aerosol injection (SAI) proposes to cool the planet by lofting reflective particles—most studied as sulfur dioxide that oxidizes into sulfate aerosols—into the lower stratosphere, raising planetary albedo and scattering a fraction of incoming solar radiation back to space. This thesis develops the physical, chemical, and institutional analysis of SAI as a climate intervention. It derives the radiative forcing budget from Mie scattering theory, examines aerosol microphysical constraints that impose diminishing returns on injection rate, quantifies the heterogeneous chemistry by which sulfate surfaces activate halogen reservoirs and erode the ozone layer, and surveys delivery engineering and governance architectures. Drawing on volcanic analogues, the Geoengineering Model Intercomparison Project, and National Academies assessments, we show that SAI can in principle reduce global mean temperature with startling leverage—teragrams of sulfur per year against teratonnes of carbon dioxide—but that this leverage concentrates risk: nonlinear microphysics, ozone depletion, regional hydrological disruption, and a termination shock that makes governance failure existentially costly.

---

## 1 Introduction

On 15 June 1991, Mount Pinatubo injected roughly 20 megatonnes of sulfur dioxide into the tropical stratosphere. Within weeks the gas oxidized into a veil of sulfate aerosols that circled the globe, and the planet cooled by approximately half a degree Celsius for more than a year [1]. The eruption was an unplanned experiment in exactly the intervention now called *stratospheric aerosol injection*: deliberate, sustained injection of reflective particles into the stratosphere to offset greenhouse warming by manipulating Earth's radiation budget.

The modern era of serious consideration began when Nobel laureate Paul Crutzen published "Albedo enhancement by stratospheric sulfur injections: a contribution to resolve a policy dilemma?" in 2006, arguing that the prospect deserved rigorous research as a complement—never a substitute—for emissions reductions [1]. The U.S. National Research Council followed with its landmark 2015 assessment *Climate Intervention: Reflecting Sunlight to Cool Earth*, which concluded that deployment at climate-altering scale should not proceed but that a deliberate research program, including governance of that research, was warranted [2]. A 2021 National Academies report sharpened the governance agenda further [3].

This thesis asks three questions in sequence:

1. **Physics:** How much cooling can a given injection of sulfur buy, and what limits the exchange rate?
2. **Chemistry:** What does a permanent stratospheric sulfate layer do to the ozone layer humanity has spent four decades repairing?
3. **Institutions:** Who decides the planetary thermostat setting, and what happens if the decision is ever reversed?

The answers reveal an intervention of extraordinary technical leverage and correspondingly extraordinary governance fragility.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The planetary energy balance

Earth's climate is, to first order, an energy balance. The planet absorbs solar irradiance $S_0 \approx 1361\ \mathrm{W\,m^{-2}}$ over its cross-section and re-radiates over its full surface, so the global mean absorbed shortwave flux is $\tfrac{S_0}{4}(1 - \alpha)$, where $\alpha \approx 0.29$ is the planetary albedo. A *radiative forcing* $\Delta F$—the instantaneous perturbation of net flux at the tropopause—translates into an equilibrium surface temperature change $\Delta T = \lambda \Delta F$, with climate sensitivity $\lambda$ in the range $0.5$–$1.2\ \mathrm{K\,(W\,m^{-2})^{-1}}$.

SAI operates on the *shortwave* side of this ledger: by increasing $\alpha$, it reduces absorbed solar energy. Crutzen estimated that compensating $1.4\ \mathrm{W\,m^{-2}}$ of warming would require a stratospheric sulfate loading of about $1.9\ \mathrm{Tg\,S}$, sustained by continuous injection of roughly $1$–$2\ \mathrm{Tg\,S\,yr^{-1}}$ given one-to-two-year stratospheric residence times [1]. That sulfur flux is only $2$–$4\%$ of the $55\ \mathrm{Tg\,S\,yr^{-1}}$ humanity already emits at the surface—yet because it is placed *in the stratosphere*, above the weather that washes tropospheric aerosols out within days to weeks, its radiative leverage per unit mass is orders of magnitude larger.

### 2.2 Why sulfate, and why the stratosphere

Sulfate aerosols are nearly ideal scatterers: their single-scattering albedo $\omega_0$ is essentially unity across the visible spectrum [8]. They form spontaneously from gas-phase oxidation of $\mathrm{SO_2}$ by hydroxyl radicals, so the injection precursor is a cheap industrial gas rather than an exotic engineered particle. Alternatives have been studied—black carbon, which absorbs and would heat the stratosphere dramatically [6]; titania; calcite, proposed partly because its chemistry may spare ozone [7]—but sulfate remains the reference case because the volcanic record provides a natural experiment for validation.

The stratosphere is essential because of its dynamical isolation. Injected at $18$–$25\ \mathrm{km}$ in the tropics, aerosols are dispersed by the Brewer–Dobson circulation to near-global coverage within months and persist $1$–$2$ years before sedimentation removes them [1]. Tropospheric injection would wash out in days and acidify rainfall near sources; stratospheric injection buys residence time and global reach at the cost of placing the perturbation exactly where the ozone layer lives.

### 2.3 The promise and the price, in brief

The promise is *speed, leverage, and reversibility of effect*: models indicate SAI could reduce global mean temperature within a year or two of deployment, at direct engineering costs far below those of deep decarbonization [1][2]. The price is stratospheric heating, ozone loss, shifts in precipitation patterns, and the *termination problem*—plus a governance problem with no historical precedent: the continuous, century-scale management of a planetary control knob.

## 3 Methodology

This thesis synthesizes four methodological traditions:

1. **Radiative transfer theory.** Mie scattering calculations for lognormal sulfate size distributions yield extinction efficiency, single-scattering albedo, and asymmetry parameter as functions of the size parameter $x = 2\pi r/\lambda$. Optical depth $\tau$ integrates extinction over the stratospheric column, and forcing follows via the Beer–Lambert law, $I = I_0 e^{-\tau}$.

2. **Aerosol microphysics modeling.** Sectional and modal schemes (e.g., CARMA, M7/HAM) resolve nucleation of sulfuric acid vapor, condensation, coagulation, and sedimentation. Coagulation is the crux: the self-coagulation kernel scales with the square of number concentration, so higher injection rates produce *larger* particles that scatter less efficiently per unit mass and sediment faster.

3. **Coordinated model intercomparison.** The Geoengineering Model Intercomparison Project (GeoMIP) prescribes standardized experiments across Earth system models. The G6 experiments are the most policy-relevant: **G6sulfur** injects stratospheric $\mathrm{SO_2}$ to reduce net anthropogenic forcing from SSP5-8.5 down to SSP2-4.5 levels, while **G6solar** achieves the same temperature target by uniformly reducing the solar constant, isolating aerosol-specific effects [4].

4. **Volcanic analogue validation.** Pinatubo (1991), El Chichón (1982), and Agung (1963) provide observed forcing–response pairs. Models that cannot reproduce Pinatubo's $\sim 0.5^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ cooling from $20\ \mathrm{Mt}$ of $\mathrm{SO_2}$ are not trusted to project deliberate injection [1].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Radiative Transfer Physics of Sulfate Aerosols

Cooling efficiency is governed by particle size relative to sunlight's wavelength. Mie theory gives the extinction efficiency $Q_{\mathrm{ext}}(x, m)$ for refractive index $m \approx 1.43 + 0i$; $Q_{\mathrm{scat}}$ peaks near $x \sim 1$, i.e., radii $r \sim 0.1$–$0.2\ \mu\mathrm{m}$—the *optical sweet spot*. Much smaller particles scatter in the Rayleigh regime (negligible per unit mass); much larger ones enter the geometric regime where mass grows as $r^3$ but scattering only as $r^2$, and forward scattering increasingly dominates over the backscattering that cools the planet.

For small optical depths, top-of-atmosphere shortwave forcing scales approximately as

$$\Delta F_{\mathrm{SW}} \approx -\frac{S_0}{4}\, T^2\, (1 - \alpha_c)\, \beta\, \tau,$$

where $T$ is atmospheric transmissivity, $\alpha_c$ cloud albedo, $\beta$ the upscatter fraction, and $\tau$ aerosol optical depth. Two corrections matter. First, sulfate absorbs upwelling longwave radiation, heating the lower tropical stratosphere by $5$–$13\ \mathrm{K}$ in G6sulfur simulations [4] and perturbing stratospheric dynamics and water vapor. Second, forcing saturates: doubling injection does *not* double $\tau$.

> **Theorem:** Under coagulation-limited steady-state microphysics, the marginal shortwave radiative forcing per unit sulfur injection rate decreases monotonically with injection rate; consequently, linear extrapolation from small-injection efficiency to climate-scale deployment systematically overestimates cooling.

### 4.2 Aerosol Microphysics: Nucleation, Coagulation, and Sedimentation

Injected $\mathrm{SO_2}$ oxidizes via $\mathrm{SO_2} + \mathrm{OH} \rightarrow \mathrm{HSO_3}$, ultimately forming $\mathrm{H_2SO_4}$ vapor over weeks. Sulfuric acid vapor either nucleates new particles or condenses onto pre-existing ones; continuous injection into an already-laden stratosphere favors condensation onto existing particles—*growing* them. Brownian coagulation then removes small particles and builds large ones at a rate proportional to $n^2$. At Pinatubo-scale loadings the effective radius grew from $\sim 0.1\ \mu\mathrm{m}$ to $\sim 0.5\ \mu\mathrm{m}$ within months, and Stokes settling velocity ($\propto r^2$) cut residence time accordingly.

The consequence is a *strategy dependence* that dominates deployment design: higher injection altitude lengthens the sedimentation path; distributing injection across latitudes avoids concentrating number density where coagulation is fiercest; injecting pre-formed or solid particles could bypass the growth problem, though each alternative carries its own unknowns [6][7]. The naive picture of "spray and cool" dissolves into an optimization problem over altitude, latitude, season, and particle composition, with large remaining model disagreement.

### 4.3 Heterogeneous Stratospheric Chemistry and Ozone Loss

The most established side effect of SAI is damage to the ozone layer via heterogeneous chemistry on aerosol surfaces:

| Reaction | Role |
|---|---|
| $\mathrm{N_2O_5} + \mathrm{H_2O\,(aer)} \rightarrow 2\,\mathrm{HNO_3}$ | Denoxification: removes $\mathrm{NO_x}$, freeing $\mathrm{ClO}$ from the $\mathrm{ClONO_2}$ reservoir |
| $\mathrm{ClONO_2} + \mathrm{HCl\,(aer)} \rightarrow \mathrm{Cl_2} + \mathrm{HNO_3}$ | Chlorine activation on sulfate surfaces |
| $\mathrm{ClONO_2} + \mathrm{H_2O\,(aer)} \rightarrow \mathrm{HOCl} + \mathrm{HNO_3}$ | Additional activation channel |

Activated chlorine photolyzes readily, feeding catalytic destruction, e.g., the polar-spring dimer cycle: $\mathrm{Cl} + \mathrm{O_3} \rightarrow \mathrm{ClO} + \mathrm{O_2}$; $\mathrm{ClO} + \mathrm{ClO} + \mathrm{M} \rightarrow \mathrm{Cl_2O_2} + \mathrm{M}$; $\mathrm{Cl_2O_2} + h\nu \rightarrow 2\,\mathrm{Cl} + \mathrm{O_2}$; net $2\,\mathrm{O_3} \rightarrow 3\,\mathrm{O_2}$. Each chlorine atom destroys on the order of $10^5$ ozone molecules before sequestration. Sulfate aerosols amplify this both directly, by providing reaction surfaces, and indirectly, by serving as condensation nuclei that increase polar stratospheric cloud occurrence [5].

Tilmes, Müller, and Salawitch (2008) showed geoengineering-scale sulfate loading could *delay Antarctic ozone-hole recovery by 30–70 years*, raising erythemal UV by up to $5\%$ at mid/high latitudes and $10\%$ over Antarctica by mid-century [5]. GeoMIP G6 experiments with interactive chemistry refine this: springtime Antarctic total column ozone declines $\sim 10\ \mathrm{DU}$ at onset (range $0$–$30\ \mathrm{DU}$) and up to $\sim 20\ \mathrm{DU}$ by 2100 (range $10$–$40\ \mathrm{DU}$), with no significant Northern Hemisphere high-latitude change [4]. Declining stratospheric halogens under the Montreal Protocol shrink the penalty over time—but do not erase it.

### 4.4 Delivery Architectures and Engineering Constraints

Moving megatonnes of gas to $20\ \mathrm{km}$ continuously for decades is a nontrivial aerospace program. No existing aircraft fleet can do it: the requirement sits above commercial tanker ceilings, demanding purpose-built high-altitude aircraft. Crutzen's reference arithmetic: $1$–$2\ \mathrm{Tg\,S\,yr^{-1}}$ at $\$25$–$50$ billion per year—"about $\$25$–$50$ per capita in the affluent world"—explicitly set against $\sim \$1000$ billion in annual military spending to argue that *cost is not the binding constraint* [1].

That observation is double-edged. If planetary cooling costs tens of billions per year—affordable to a single wealthy nation or well-funded private actor—the barrier to *unilateral* deployment is political and ethical rather than economic. The technology's cheapness is itself a governance hazard, a point central to the National Academies' governance work [3]. Deployment would additionally require a monitoring and attribution architecture that does not yet exist, plus agreed protocols for adjusting or halting injection; the 2021 report recommends an international research registry, funder codes of conduct, and an ad hoc UN working group [3].

## 5 Empirical Results and Formal Analysis

### 5.1 The volcanic calibration point

Pinatubo is the single most important empirical anchor: $\sim 20\ \mathrm{Mt}$ of $\mathrm{SO_2}$ produced peak aerosol optical depth near $0.15$, peak shortwave forcing of roughly $-3$ to $-4\ \mathrm{W\,m^{-2}}$, and $\sim 0.5^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ of global cooling persisting over a year [1]. The response was non-uniform—stronger in the Northern Hemisphere, over land—and accompanied by several kelvin of tropical stratospheric warming, qualitatively matching G6sulfur projections [4]. Critically, Pinatubo was a *pulse*; SAI would be a *press*—a forcing sustained against sedimentation for as long as the greenhouse forcing it offsets persists, i.e., centuries.

### 5.2 What the multi-model ensemble says

Across GeoMIP G6 experiments [4][6], several findings are robust:

- **It cools.** All models hit the temperature target; the required $\mathrm{SO_2}$ injection varies substantially, reflecting microphysical and transport differences.
- **It heats the stratosphere.** Lower tropical stratospheric warming of $5$–$13\ \mathrm{K}$, with downstream effects on water vapor, cirrus, and wave propagation [4].
- **It depletes Antarctic ozone.** Springtime total column ozone declines $10$–$20\ \mathrm{DU}$ by century's end, with large inter-model spread [4].
- **It weakens the hydrological cycle relative to a same-temperature world.** For a given surface temperature, a solar-dimmed world precipitates less than a greenhouse world, because reduced surface shortwave suppresses evaporation. Regional precipitation shifts—including monsoon risks—are among the least constrained and most consequential outcomes [2][6].

### 5.3 Diminishing returns: a quantitative illustration

The sublinear forcing–injection relationship is captured by a saturating fit of the form used to summarize microphysical model output:

```python
import numpy as np

def forcing_sai(injection_tg_s_yr, a=3.2, b=0.55):
    """
    Toy saturating model of global-mean shortwave forcing (W/m^2)
    vs SO2 injection rate. F(q) = -a*q/(1 + b*q): linear at small q
    (efficiency a), saturating toward -a/b at large q as coagulation
    grows particles. Illustrative only -- real values are
    model- and strategy-dependent.
    """
    q = np.asarray(injection_tg_s_yr, dtype=float)
    return -a * q / (1.0 + b * q)

qs = np.array([1, 2, 5, 10, 20])
for q, f in zip(qs, forcing_sai(qs)):
    print(f"{q:>3.0f} TgS/yr -> {f:5.2f} W/m^2   "
          f"(marginal {abs(f)/q:.2f} W/m^2 per TgS/yr)")

#  1 TgS/yr -> -2.06 W/m^2   (marginal 2.06 W/m^2 per TgS/yr)
#  2 TgS/yr -> -3.05 W/m^2   (marginal 1.52 W/m^2 per TgS/yr)
#  5 TgS/yr -> -4.27 W/m^2   (marginal 0.85 W/m^2 per TgS/yr)
# 10 TgS/yr -> -4.92 W/m^2   (marginal 0.49 W/m^2 per TgS/yr)
# 20 TgS/yr -> -5.33 W/m^2   (marginal 0.36 W/m^2 per TgS/yr)
```

Marginal efficiency collapses by more than a factor of five between $1$ and $20\ \mathrm{Tg\,S\,yr^{-1}}$. "Just inject more" is not a plan: beyond a few teragrams per year, each additional teragram buys rapidly diminishing cooling while adding roughly linearly to side effects.

### 5.4 The termination shock

The gravest modeled risk may be not deployment but *abrupt cessation*. The sulfate veil washes out within a year or two while $\mathrm{CO_2}$ persists for centuries, so stopping SAI suddenly unmasks the full greenhouse forcing almost immediately—warming rates an order of magnitude faster than the gradual warming SAI was masking [2]. SAI is therefore not merely a decision to cool; it is a commitment to *keep cooling, without interruption, for as long as greenhouse concentrations remain elevated*—a commitment measured in centuries, to be honored by institutions that have never survived that long.

## 6 Limitations

**Microphysical uncertainty.** Forcing efficiency varies by factors of two or more across models; the optimal particle size, altitude, and latitude remain contested [4][6].

**Chemical uncertainty.** Interactive-chemistry ESMs disagree on early-decade Antarctic ozone response ($0$–$30\ \mathrm{DU}$ range) [4]. Montreal Protocol halogen decline mitigates but does not eliminate the risk.

**Regional unknowns.** Shifts in the intertropical convergence zone and monsoon systems could redistribute rainfall with devastating regional consequences that global-mean temperature targets do not capture [2].

**Attribution and liability.** In a geoengineered climate, every extreme event becomes potentially attributable to the intervention. No legal framework assigns liability for probabilistic, globally distributed harms, and detecting SAI's fingerprint against internal variability is itself a hard statistical problem.

**Governance vacuum.** No treaty regime authorizes or prohibits SAI deployment; no agreed decision rule sets the global thermostat; no enforcement mechanism constrains unilateral action. The 2021 National Academies recommendations address *research* governance; deployment governance remains uncharted [3].

**Moral hazard.** A seemingly cheap cooling lever may weaken political resolve for emissions reductions—the only intervention addressing ocean acidification and the root cause [2][8].

## 7 Conclusion

Stratospheric aerosol injection is the most potent and most perilous climate intervention under serious study. Its physics is sound in outline—Mie scattering by submicron sulfate demonstrably cools the planet, as volcanoes prove—but the exchange rate between injected sulfur and realized cooling is governed by nonlinear microphysics that punishes scale, and the chemistry of the intervention runs directly against the ozone layer. The GeoMIP ensemble shows deployment calibrated to moderate-emissions temperature trajectories is physically achievable, yet consistently projects stratospheric heating, Antarctic ozone erosion, and hydrological side effects whose regional distribution remains poorly constrained [4][6].

The deeper conclusion is institutional. SAI's direct costs are low enough that deployment is constrained not by economics but by collective choice—planetary-scale, sustained over centuries without interruption, a capability humanity has never demonstrated [1][3]. The termination shock converts any lapse into a catastrophe worse than the warming it was meant to prevent [2]. Responsible engagement therefore requires: a transparent international research program; a governance framework preceding any deployment decision that includes the populations most exposed to side effects; continued aggressive mitigation so the required intervention scale—and the termination liability—shrinks; and honest accounting of the possibility that the wisest use of this knowledge is to understand precisely why we should be reluctant to use it.

*The thermostat exists. Whether anyone should touch it remains the hardest question in climate policy.*
