##1 Introduction

The no-cloning theorem states that an unknown quantum state cannot be copied, a fact that has tantalized cryptographers since Wiesner first proposed *conjugate coding* in the 1970s: a bank could encode a secret serial number in the choice of measurement basis for a sequence of qubits, producing banknotes that are information-theoretically impossible to counterfeit [10]. Wiesner's scheme, however, is a *private-key* construction: verification requires the bank's secret database, so every merchant must query the bank for each transaction. For nearly forty years the central open question was whether the bank could *delegate verification to the public* without enabling forgery — the problem of **public-key quantum money**.

The breakthrough came in 2012 with Aaronson and Christiano's *quantum money from hidden subspaces* [1]: the first public-key scheme equipped with a rigorous security proof, albeit relative to a classical oracle. A banknote is the uniform superposition $\lvert A\rangle$ over a secret random subspace $A$ of the binary vector space of dimension $n$, of dimension $n/2$; the public key consists of membership oracles for $A$ and its orthogonal complement $A^\perp$. Verification applies projective measurements derived from these oracles in the computational and Hadamard bases, accepting genuine notes with overwhelming probability. Security reduces to the hardness of recovering $A$ from oracle access — the *hidden subspace problem* — via a novel variant of Ambainis's quantum adversary method [1].

Five years later, Zhandry introduced **quantum lightning** [2], a dramatic strengthening in which the no-cloning property holds *even against the party that generates the state*. In quantum money, the mint knows the secret key and can trivially re-mint an identical banknote; in quantum lightning, the generation procedure outputs a bolt together with a serial number, and security demands that no efficient adversary — not even the honest generator itself, run twice — can produce two bolts that verify under the *same* serial number. Lightning therefore enables cryptographic primitives that money cannot: publicly verifiable proofs of entropy (a string that is guaranteed fresh), and, in principle, a decentralized cryptocurrency without a blockchain, in which double-spending is forbidden by physics rather than by consensus [2].

This thesis examines the full arc:

- **Foundations (§2):** Wiesner's conjugate coding, the formal syntax of public-key quantum money (mint, verify, and the mini-scheme abstraction), and the lightning security game.
- **Constructions (§3):** the hidden-subspace scheme, the knot-based scheme of Farhi et al. [3], and Zhandry's lightning construction from degree-two polynomial systems, plus its win–win corollaries.
- **Analysis (§4):** five deep-dives covering verification circuits and gentle measurement, the inner-product adversary method, win–win theorems for signatures and hash functions, cryptanalytic defeats of the oracle schemes, and the invariant-money / lattice impossibility program [5].
- **Frontiers (§5):** noise-tolerant verification [8], group-action lightning [7], modular-form money [6], and quantum fire.
- **Limits (§6) and conclusions (§7).**

Throughout, our emphasis is on *which computational assumptions buy which unforgeability guarantees*, and where the boundary between oracle security and standard-model security currently lies.

##2 Background

### Wiesner's conjugate coding and the private-key baseline

Wiesner's idea [10] is disarmingly simple.

### Public-key syntax: mini-schemes and full schemes

Aaronson and Christiano formalized public-key quantum money through a **mini-scheme** abstraction [1].

1. **Mint** — a quantum polynomial-time procedure producing a banknote: a pair $(s, \lvert \$\rangle)$ of a classical serial number and a quantum state.
2. **Verify** — a quantum polynomial-time procedure taking $(s, \rho)$ and outputting accept or reject.

A *full* scheme additionally requires that serial numbers be unguessable and that verification be non-destructive in the honest case.

### Quantum lightning: the security game


- **Gen** — outputs a bolt $\lvert \text{\bolt}\rangle$ and a serial number $s$.
- **Ver** — on input $(s, \rho)$, outputs accept or reject.

The security experiment is stark: the adversary *chooses* its own generation procedure (it may even run the honest Gen with adversarially chosen randomness, or a completely different algorithm) and must output two bolts $\rho_1, \rho_2$ and a single serial number $s$ such that both verify.

### Assumptions in play

The field's assumptions range from falsifiable to tautological.

##3 Methodology


**Reconstruction.** For each construction we extract the honest algorithms (mint/generation, verification) and identify the exact verification circuit: which projective measurements are performed, in which bases, and with what completeness and soundness parameters.

**Reduction analysis.** We examine how a counterfeiting adversary is converted into an algorithm breaking the underlying assumption. The two landmark techniques are Aaronson and Christiano's *inner-product adversary method* [1] — which lower-bounds the queries needed to distinguish subspace membership oracles — and Zhandry's *win–win* paradigm [2], which shows that any attack on the money scheme constitutes a break of a strong security property of the underlying primitive.

**Cryptanalytic review.** We survey the attacks that defeated the oracle instantiations: the quasi-polynomial algorithm of Peña et al. and its polynomial-time refinement by Bilyk, Doliskani, and Gong using Zariski tangent spaces [4]; the lattice impossibility theorem of Liu et al. [5]; and the modular-form cryptanalysis that reduced Kane's scheme to linear algebra [4, 6].

**Frontier assessment.** Finally we evaluate the three live research directions — noise-tolerant verification [8], group-action and duality-based lightning [7], and quantum fire — against the standard-model bar: does any construction offer a reduction to a *falsifiable, standard* assumption?


##4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Hidden-Subspace Construction and Its Verification Circuit

Let $n$ be even and let $A \subseteq \mathbb{F}_2^n$ be a uniformly random subspace of dimension $n/2$.


and the public key is a pair of classical oracles $U_A, U_{A^\perp}$ testing membership in $A$ and its orthogonal complement.

1. **Computational-basis test.** Measure whether the state lies in $A$ using $U_A$; equivalently, apply the projector onto $\mathrm{span}\{\lvert x\rangle : x \in A\}$.
2. **Hadamard-basis test.** Apply the Hadamard transform $H^{\otimes n}$, which maps $\lvert A\rangle$ to $\lvert A^\perp\rangle$, and test membership in $A^\perp$ using $U_{A^\perp}$.

> **Theorem:** (Completeness and soundness [1].) An honest banknote $\lvert A\rangle$ passes both tests with probability $1$. Any state orthogonal to $\lvert A\rangle$ is rejected with constant probability; repetition amplifies soundness exponentially.

The intuition for security is a *complementary-information tradeoff*: $U_A$ reveals information about $A$ only in the computational basis, while $U_{A^\perp}$ reveals information only in the Hadamard basis, and the uncertainty principle prevents an adversary from extracting a full description of $A$ from both.

The construction is exquisite but fragile in exactly the way oracle schemes are: it proves security *relative to oracles that no efficient party can instantiate*.

### 4.2 Quantum Lightning from Degree-Two Polynomials and Win–Win Theorems

Zhandry's construction [2] begins from an unlikely place: a *non-collapsing* hash function $H$, meaning a hash function for which finding a superposition of preimages of the same output is hard even for a quantum adversary given quantum access.


measures the second register to obtain a serial number $y = H(x)$, and retains the collapsed superposition over preimages as the bolt.

Zhandry could not base non-collapsing hashing on standard assumptions directly, so he built it from the conjectured **multi-collision resistance of random systems of degree-two polynomials** over finite fields: given $m$ random quadratic polynomials, it is hard to find $k$ distinct inputs mapping to the same output tuple, for suitable parameters.


> **Theorem:** (Win–win for hash functions [2].) Let $H$ be any hash function. Then *either* $H$ is collapsing in a strong sense (a powerful security guarantee previously only conjectured), *or* $H$ can be used to build quantum lightning.

Analogous win–win results hold for digital signatures and commitment schemes: either these ubiquitous primitives satisfy extremely strong, recently proposed security notions, or they *already imply* quantum money or lightning.

### 4.5 Duality, Group Actions, and Quantum Fire

The newest line of attack on standard-model lightning comes from a *duality principle* generalizing an observation of Aaronson, Atia, and Susskind: the ability to map between quantum states in one basis is computationally equivalent to distinguishing their superpositions in a complementary basis [7].

The same paper formalizes **quantum fire**: states that are efficiently *clonable* but not efficiently *telegraphable* — they can spread like fire provided they are kept alive quantumly and never decohere, yet cannot be transmitted classically.

##5 Empirical Results and Proofs

Because quantum money is a cryptographic primitive rather than an experimental science, "empirical results" here means *theorems with concrete parameters* and the cryptanalytic record.

**Security parameters of the hidden-subspace scheme.** For subspace dimension $n/2$ in $\mathbb{F}_2^n$, the adversary-method lower bound shows that distinguishing the oracles requires $\Omega(2^{n/4})$ queries [1]; with $n = 200$, this is roughly $2^{50}$ queries — a comfortable margin in the oracle world. Verification is a constant-depth circuit plus oracle calls, so honest verification is efficient. The polynomial-time attack of Bilyk et al. [4] runs in time polynomial in $n$ with overwhelming success probability, collapsing the margin to zero for *any* instantiation that exposes the algebraic structure the oracles hide.

**Lightning parameters.** Zhandry's construction requires degree-two systems with $m$ polynomials in $n$ variables where multi-collision resistance holds for the needed collision multiplicity; the bolt is a superposition over preimages whose size is exponential in the security parameter, so generation is efficient (a quantum Fourier-like preparation) while forging requires breaking the assumption [2]. The Bilyk et al. cloning attack [4] succeeds with high probability in polynomial time against the multivariate instantiation, using $O(1)$ verification queries per forged note.

**Win–win corollaries as "empirical" evidence.** One reading of Zhandry's win–win theorems [2] is as a *meta-empirical* result: the fact that no one has built standard-model public-key quantum money from falsifiable assumptions, despite intense effort since 2012, is consistent with the hypothesis that common hash functions already satisfy the strong collapsing-style notions. This is not a proof, but it reframes a decade of failure as Bayesian evidence — a provocative stance the field continues to debate.

**Noise-tolerance bounds.** The noise-tolerant scheme [8] accepts states within a bounded Hamming distance of the ideal subspace state, with the acceptance radius tunable against the soundness error; the analysis shows a non-trivial regime where honest noisy notes pass with overwhelming probability while the adversary's forgery advantage remains negligible in the oracle model.

**Implementation reality check.** No public-key quantum money scheme has been demonstrated on real hardware. Wiesner's private-key scheme has been implemented optically with weak coherent pulses, but public-key verification circuits require fault-tolerant-scale resources: the subspace-state preparation alone needs $O(n^2)$ gates for the basis change, and the oracle queries are placeholders for circuits no one knows how to build. The honest engineering assessment is that *even a provably secure standard-model scheme would await large-scale fault-tolerant quantum computers for deployment* — the cryptography is ahead of the physics by decades.

##6 Limitations


**No standard-model construction from standard assumptions exists.** Every public-key scheme with a proof is either oracle-relative [1, 8], broken [4], or based on a bespoke non-standard assumption [2, 3, 5, 6]. The group-action lightning of [7] comes closest to the standard-model bar, but *pre-action security* of group actions is itself a young, untested assumption family. The central open problem — public-key quantum money from LWE, or from any assumption already trusted for post-quantum encryption — remains wide open, and [5] shows at least one natural lattice route is a dead end.

**Verification is inherently quantum and inherently destructive at the margins.** Public verification requires a quantum computer on the merchant's side; there is no classical-verification analogue (that would imply cloning). Moreover, each verification slightly disturbs the note — the gentle measurement lemma bounds the disturbance, but a banknote verified thousands of times degrades, imposing a finite *transaction lifetime* absent re-minting protocols.

**Noise tolerance is proven only in the oracle model** [8], and the thickened verification oracles may admit forgery strategies that the noiseless analysis excludes. Realistic error models (correlated errors, leakage) are unaddressed.

**Lightning's applications assume what money cannot give.** Blockchain-free currency from lightning [2] requires not just unforgeability but a full transaction protocol handling *change-making* (a bolt has fixed denomination), loss recovery, and offline double-spend detection — none of which follow from the lightning security game alone.

**Cryptanalytic history counsels humility.** Three of the field's flagship oracle schemes fell to polynomial-time attacks [4]; the proofs were correct, but the *instantiation gap* swallowed them. Any future claim of standard-model security must survive the lesson that algebraic structure invisible to black-box lower bounds is routinely fatal in the white-box world.

##7 Conclusion

Quantum money began as Wiesner's conjugate-coding thought experiment [10] and matured, through Aaronson and Christiano's hidden subspaces [1] and Zhandry's lightning [2], into a precise science of *computational unclonability*: the project of making the no-cloning theorem hold against efficient adversaries even when verification is public, and even when the adversary mints the state itself.

The honest summary is twofold. *Intellectually*, the area has been extraordinarily fruitful: the adversary method variants, win–win theorems, and duality principles developed for quantum money are now tools of general quantum cryptography [1, 2, 7, 9]. *Practically*, unforgeable public quantum money remains a distant prospect: no standard-model construction from standard assumptions, no hardware demonstration, and a graveyard of broken instantiations. Whether the next decade closes the instantiation gap — or proves, via some quantumly non-relativizing barrier, that it cannot be closed — is the deepest open question the field owns. Lightning, at least, never strikes the same state twice; the same cannot yet be said of our assumptions.
