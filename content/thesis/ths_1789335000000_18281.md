# The Distributional Transmission of Monetary Policy: Heterogeneous-Agent New Keynesian Macroeconomics, Marginal Propensities to Consume, and Incomplete-Market Channels

## Abstract

The representative-agent New Keynesian (RANK) model attributes monetary transmission to consumption almost entirely to intertemporal substitution. This paper develops the heterogeneous-agent New Keynesian (HANK) paradigm, in which uninsurable idiosyncratic risk, borrowing constraints, and incomplete asset markets generate empirically realistic distributions of wealth and marginal propensities to consume (MPCs). In HANK economies, an interest-rate cut operates predominantly through *indirect* general-equilibrium channels — rising labor demand and redistributive balance-sheet revaluation — rather than direct substitution [1]. We dissect Auclert's decomposition of the redistribution channel into earnings heterogeneity, Fisher, and interest-rate exposure channels [2], the two-asset quantitative HANK framework of Kaplan, Moll, and Violante [3], and tractable-HANK results on determinacy, amplification, and the forward guidance puzzle [4]. With aggregate quarterly MPCs near 0.5, redistribution from low-MPC savers to high-MPC borrowers systematically amplifies transmission. We review sequence-space Jacobian solution methods [5] and evidence on the distributional incidence of policy [6][7], concluding that redistribution is a first-order channel of aggregate demand, not a mere side effect.

## 1 Intro

A prerequisite for the successful conduct of monetary policy is a satisfactory understanding of the monetary transmission mechanism — the ensemble of economic forces that determine how the actions of the monetary authority affect the aggregate performance of the economy. For decades, the workhorse for this analysis has been the representative-agent New Keynesian (RANK) model, in which a single infinitely lived household consumes according to a linearized Euler equation, and changes in the real interest rate propagate to consumption almost exclusively through *intertemporal substitution*: when rates fall, saving becomes less attractive, and the household shifts consumption toward the present. This elegant mechanism has a striking empirical implication — that the aggregate effect of monetary policy can be summarized by a single sufficient statistic, the elasticity of intertemporal substitution (EIS) — and it has anchored the policy models of central banks worldwide.

Yet this consensus rests on an assumption that microeconomic data decisively reject: that all households are alike, perfectly insured, and unconstrained. Empirical studies of household spending responses to transitory income shocks find marginal propensities to consume that are large (aggregate quarterly MPCs on the order of 0.5), highly heterogeneous across households, and declining sharply in wealth and liquidity [3]. The representative agent, by construction, exhibits an MPC that is low, uniform, and counterfactual. Because the representative agent cannot be a net borrower in equilibrium and faces no uninsurable risk, the RANK model is silent about who gains and who loses when interest rates change — and therefore about how the *distribution* of gains and losses shapes the aggregate response.

The heterogeneous-agent New Keynesian (HANK) literature, launched in its modern quantitative form by Kaplan, Moll, and Violante [3] and surveyed comprehensively by Auclert, Rognlie, and Straub [1], takes these micro facts as its starting point. Households face idiosyncratic income risk that cannot be fully insured, borrow subject to constraints, and hold assets with different degrees of liquidity. When embedded in a New Keynesian environment with nominal rigidities, this economy produces a radically different transmission mechanism: indirect effects — operating through a general-equilibrium expansion in labor demand, and through the redistribution of wealth between borrowers and savers with different MPCs — far outweigh the direct substitution effect [3].

This paper provides a PhD-level synthesis of that paradigm. We proceed in four steps. First, we reconstruct the incomplete-markets microfoundation: the buffer-stock savings problem, the role of borrowing constraints and asset illiquidity, and the calibration strategies that match the joint distribution of wealth and MPCs. Second, we develop the direct-versus-indirect decomposition of monetary transmission and Auclert's three-channel taxonomy of redistribution [2]. Third, we present the tractable HANK toolkit — two-agent (TANK) approximations, the "Catch-22" of amplification versus forward-guidance resolution identified by Bilbiie [4], and the sequence-space Jacobian methods that enable fast computation of high-dimensional HANK models [5]. Fourth, we confront the theory with empirical evidence on the distributional incidence of monetary policy [6][7]. Along the way, we formalize key results as theorems and illustrate the computational skeleton in executable code.

> **Theorem (HANK indirect-effects dominance):** In the calibrated two-asset HANK model of Kaplan, Moll, and Violante, an unexpected 25-basis-point cut in the nominal interest rate raises aggregate consumption on impact primarily through indirect general-equilibrium effects on labor income rather than through direct intertemporal substitution; the indirect component accounts for the overwhelming majority of the consumption response, in stark contrast to RANK models where substitution drives virtually all of the transmission [3].

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The RANK transmission benchmark

In the textbook RANK model, the log-linearized consumption Euler equation reads

```python
# Log-linearized RANK Euler equation
# c_t = E_t[c_{t+1}] - sigma * (r_t - rho)
# Consumption responds to the real rate ONLY via intertemporal substitution.
```

A monetary easing lowers the real rate, households substitute toward current consumption, firms meet the higher demand, and output expands. The strength of the response is governed by the EIS parameter. Crucially, the model implies Ricardian equivalence and a uniform MPC approximately equal to the annuity value of a transitory shock — far below empirical estimates.

### 2.2 Incomplete markets and the buffer-stock household

HANK replaces the representative household with a continuum of households that differ in asset holdings and idiosyncratic productivity. Each household solves a dynamic program of the Bewley–Huggett–Aiyagari type:

```haskell
-- Stylized Bellman equation for a HANK household
-- V(a, z) = max_{c, a'} u(c) + beta * E[ V(a', z') | z ]
-- s.t.  c + a' = (1 + r) * a + w * z * l
--       a' >= -b            -- borrowing constraint
-- where z is idiosyncratic productivity, a is asset holdings
```

Because markets are incomplete — there is no state-contingent insurance against the idiosyncratic shock `z` — households engage in precautionary saving, accumulating a buffer stock of wealth. Households near the borrowing limit `a' >= -b` exhibit high MPCs: a transitory dollar of income is spent, not saved, because the constraint binds or is at risk of binding. Kaplan, Moll, and Violante [3] enrich this structure with *two assets* — liquid bonds and illiquid productive capital subject to a transaction cost — and with an income process featuring leptokurtic (fat-tailed) shocks calibrated from administrative earnings data. The two-asset structure generates a class of *wealthy hand-to-mouth* households: households with substantial illiquid wealth but little liquid buffer, whose MPCs are as high as those of the poor. This is essential for matching the empirical fact that high MPCs are found not only at the bottom of the wealth distribution but throughout it.

### 2.3 Empirical MPC facts the model must match

Three micro facts discipline quantitative HANK calibrations [1][3]:

1. **High level.** The aggregate quarterly MPC out of transitory income shocks is on the order of 0.5 — several times the representative-agent prediction.
2. **Heterogeneity.** MPCs decline steeply with liquid wealth; constrained and wealthy hand-to-mouth households have MPCs near unity for small shocks.
3. **Nonlinearity.** MPCs are larger for losses than for gains and decline with shock size, consistent with concave consumption functions under borrowing constraints.

These facts are the raw material of monetary transmission in HANK: when policy redistributes income or wealth toward high-MPC households, aggregate spending rises even if no household substitutes intertemporally.

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 The direct–indirect decomposition

Following Kaplan, Moll, and Violante [3], the consumption response to a monetary shock can be decomposed into *direct* effects — those that operate even holding household disposable labor income fixed, chiefly intertemporal substitution — and *indirect* effects — those operating through general-equilibrium changes in labor demand and hence in disposable income. Formally, letting household consumption be a functional of the path of interest rates and the path of after-tax labor income:

```
dC_t = [direct: partial w.r.t. interest rates, income held fixed]
     + [indirect: partial w.r.t. labor income path, rates held fixed]
```

In RANK, the indirect term is a second-order refinement; in HANK, it dominates, because high-MPC households spend a large fraction of any income gain. The policy implication is immediate: what matters for aggregate demand is not only the path of interest rates but the *incidence* of the income gains the easing produces.

### 3.2 Auclert's redistribution channels

Auclert [2] refines the indirect decomposition into three distinct redistribution channels through which monetary policy affects aggregate consumption whenever winners and losers have different MPCs:

| Channel | Mechanism | Who gains from easing | Sufficient statistic |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Earnings heterogeneity** | Unequal pass-through of the demand expansion to labor income | High-MPC workers whose earnings are cyclically sensitive | Covariance of MPC with earnings cyclicality |
| **Fisher channel** | Unexpected inflation revalues nominal balance sheets | Net nominal debtors (real debt burden falls) | Unhedged nominal positions × MPC |
| **Interest rate exposure** | Real rate changes revalue long-duration assets and liabilities | Holders of short-duration assets; debtors rolling over debt | Unhedged interest rate exposure (URE) × MPC |

The *unhedged interest rate exposure* (URE) of a household is the difference between all maturing assets and liabilities at a point in time — the net cash flow that must be reinvested or refinanced at the new rate. A fall in real rates is a capital gain for holders of long-duration assets but a capital loss for those who must reinvest maturing short assets; aggregating requires weighting each household's exposure by its MPC. Using Italian and U.S. microdata, Auclert [2] finds that all three channels are quantitatively significant and likely to *amplify* monetary transmission relative to the representative-agent benchmark.

### 3.3 Quantitative solution: sequence-space Jacobians

High-dimensional HANK models were long considered computationally intractable for policy analysis because the aggregate state includes the entire cross-sectional distribution of households. Auclert, Rognlie, Souchier, and Straub [5] introduced the *sequence-space Jacobian* method: linearize the household block once, in sequence space, to obtain Jacobians mapping sequences of prices (wages, interest rates) into sequences of aggregate consumption and asset demand; then combine these "building blocks" with the linearized general-equilibrium blocks. The method reduces solution time by orders of magnitude and underlies the modern HANK toolkit (including the public `shadeecon` codebase referenced in [1]). A schematic of the algorithm:

```python
# Sequence-space Jacobian skeleton (Auclert et al.)
# 1. Solve household problem once at steady state -> policy functions
# 2. Differentiate aggregate outcomes w.r.t. input price sequences:
#    J_C_r[t, s] = d C_t / d r_s   (consumption Jacobian)
#    J_A_r[t, s] = d A_t / d r_s   (asset demand Jacobian)
# 3. Stack with linearized GE blocks (Phillips curve, Taylor rule,
#    market clearing) and solve the stacked linear system.
# Result: impulse responses in seconds, not hours.
```

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Why indirect effects dominate: the two-asset mechanism

The dominance of indirect effects in [3] is not a generic property of heterogeneity — it requires the model's two key ingredients. First, multiple assets with different liquidity: because most household wealth is illiquid, the aggregate intertemporal substitution response is muted — wealthy households cannot easily convert capital gains into current spending, and the liquid-asset market that clears the interest rate is small relative to total wealth. Second, leptokurtic income risk: rare large income drops make precautionary motives strong and keep a large mass of households near their liquid borrowing limits, where MPCs are high and spending tracks current income closely.

A quantitative illustration of the MPC distribution in the calibrated model:

```python
import numpy as np
# Stylized MPC schedule matching Kaplan-Moll-Violante calibration facts
liquid_wealth_pct = np.array([10, 25, 50, 75, 90])   # percentiles
mpc_quarterly    = np.array([0.75, 0.55, 0.35, 0.20, 0.10])
# Wealthy hand-to-mouth: ~20% of households hold <2 weeks of income
# in liquid assets despite owning illiquid wealth -> MPC ~ 0.5+
print("Aggregate quarterly MPC ~", mpc_quarterly.mean().round(2))
```

With average MPCs near 0.5 rather than the RANK annuity value near 0.05, any general-equilibrium income gain is spent at a rate an order of magnitude higher — which is precisely why the indirect channel dominates.

### 4.2 Tractable HANK and the determinacy frontier

To obtain analytical insight, Bilbiie [4] develops THANK — a tractable HANK model with two types of agents (constrained hand-to-mouth and unconstrained savers) that captures cyclical inequality, precautionary saving, and realistic MPCs in closed form. The key parameter is *cyclical inequality*: whether the income of constrained households moves more or less than proportionally with aggregate income. Countercyclical inequality (constrained households' income share rises in booms) *amplifies* demand shocks — good news compounds — but it also makes Taylor-rule determinacy more stringent and aggravates the *forward guidance puzzle*, the counterfactual prediction that announcements about far-future rates have enormous current effects. Resolving the puzzle requires procyclical inequality — a Catch-22, since countercyclical inequality is what delivers amplification. Bilbiie shows the dilemma can be resolved by adding a distinct cyclical-risk channel with compensating cyclicality, or by adopting alternative policy rules such as Wicksellian price-level targeting, which ensures determinacy and is puzzle-free regardless of inequality cyclicality [4]. Optimal policy in THANK features a novel *inequality-stabilization motive*: the central bank optimally tolerates higher inflation volatility to compress inequality, and in a liquidity trap does not mechanically extend forward guidance even when its power is amplified, because the welfare cost rises in tandem [4].

### 4.3 The forward guidance puzzle through a HANK lens

The forward guidance puzzle — that standard NK models predict implausibly large responses to announcements of future rate cuts — is naturally dampened in quantitative HANK.

### 4.4 Fiscal footprints and the failure of Ricardian equivalence

A corollary of incomplete markets is the failure of Ricardian equivalence, which makes the *fiscal* response to a monetary shock a first-order determinant of its aggregate effect [1][3].

### 4.5 Distributional incidence: who gains, who loses

The IMF's cross-country review [6] organizes the distributional channels of monetary policy into income composition, earnings heterogeneity, portfolio composition, and savings redistribution channels, and stresses that their net effect on inequality is theoretically ambiguous and empirically contested.

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

We now consolidate the quantitative findings that anchor the HANK paradigm, drawing on the calibrated models and sufficient-statistic evidence in the cited literature.

**Result 1 — Indirect dominance (Kaplan–Moll–Violante).** In the two-asset HANK model calibrated to match the U.S. distributions of wealth and MPCs, the consumption response to an unexpected nominal rate cut is driven overwhelmingly by indirect effects through labor income; direct intertemporal substitution accounts for only a small minority of the impact response [3]. The RANK counterfactual, by contrast, attributes virtually all transmission to substitution.

**Result 2 — Redistribution amplifies (Auclert).** Calibrating the earnings heterogeneity, Fisher, and interest-rate exposure channels to Italian and U.S. household balance-sheet data, all three redistribution channels are positive and quantitatively meaningful, implying that monetary easing is more stimulative — and tightening more contractionary — than the EIS alone would predict [2].

**Result 3 — Amplification–puzzle Catch-22 (Bilbiie).** In THANK, countercyclical inequality amplifies contemporaneous demand shocks but tightens determinacy conditions and worsens the forward guidance puzzle; procyclical inequality resolves the puzzle but sacrifices amplification. The Catch-22 is resolved by a separate procyclical risk channel or by Wicksellian price-level targeting [4].

**Result 4 — Fiscal–monetary interaction (Auclert–Rognlie–Straub).** In HANK, deficit-financed fiscal expansions have larger output multipliers than in RANK because transfers flow disproportionately to high-MPC households, and the size and duration of the government balance sheet shape monetary transmission through the fiscal footprint [1].

**Result 5 — Distributional incidence (IMF; Minneapolis Fed).** Monetary easing compresses income inequality via employment gains for low-income workers but can widen wealth inequality via asset-price appreciation; the net effect depends on the joint distribution of income cyclicality, balance-sheet duration, and MPCs [6][7].

> **Theorem (Redistribution as aggregate channel):** Let household i have MPC m_i out of a transfer, and let a monetary shock redistribute amounts tau_i with aggregate sum zero. Then the first-order aggregate consumption effect equals the covariance between MPCs and transfers, Cov(m_i, tau_i), scaled by the marginal utility normalization. If and only if this covariance is positive does redistribution amplify easing — and the microdata indicate that it is, because debtors and cyclically exposed workers have systematically higher MPCs than the savers and asset holders who lose [2].

A compact formalization of the sufficient-statistic logic in code:

```rust
// Covariance logic of the redistribution channel (Auclert 2019)
fn redistribution_effect(mpcs: &[f64], transfers: &[f64]) -> f64 {
    let n = mpcs.len() as f64;
    let m_bar: f64 = mpcs.iter().sum::<f64>() / n;
    let t_bar: f64 = transfers.iter().sum::<f64>() / n;
    // Aggregate consumption response ~ Cov(MPC, transfer)
    mpcs.iter().zip(transfers.iter())
        .map(|(m, t)| (m - m_bar) * (t - t_bar))
        .sum::<f64>() / n
}
// Positive covariance => easing redistributes toward high-MPC
// households => aggregate demand amplified.
```

## 6 Limitations

The HANK paradigm, for all its empirical discipline, faces significant limitations. *First*, quantitative HANK models remain computationally demanding: although sequence-space methods [5] have reduced solution times dramatically, estimation by likelihood or full Bayesian methods on high-dimensional heterogeneous-agent models is still frontier research, and most policy applications rely on calibration rather than formal estimation. *Second*, the profession has not converged on the income process: the leptokurtic specification in [3] matches earnings dynamics better than AR(1) alternatives, but measurement of higher-order income risk from administrative data remains contested, and model conclusions about precautionary strength hinge on it. *Third*, the supply side of quantitative HANK models is often rudimentary — sticky prices with flexible wages, as in the textbook NK model, produce implausibly countercyclical profits that interact with heterogeneity to drive results [1]; adding sticky wages, search frictions, and firm heterogeneity (as in Bayer–Born–Luetticke) complicates the transmission story. *Fourth*, the Fisher and interest-rate exposure channels depend on the maturity structure of household balance sheets and on expectations of inflation, both of which are measured with error and vary across countries, limiting the portability of sufficient-statistic estimates [2][6]. *Fifth*, HANK models typically abstract from the financial intermediary sector, so the bank-lending and risk-taking channels of monetary policy — central to the 2008 crisis and its aftermath — are absent. *Finally*, the framework's very richness is a vice for policy communication: a central bank that must explain that its policy works through a covariance between MPCs and unhedged interest rate exposures faces a harder task than one invoking intertemporal substitution, and the mapping from model objects to observable statistics is less transparent.

## 7 Conclusion

The HANK revolution has permanently altered the macroeconomics of monetary policy. Where the representative-agent model saw a single channel — intertemporal substitution governed by the EIS — the heterogeneous-agent model sees an ecology of channels: direct substitution muted by illiquidity, indirect income effects amplified by high and heterogeneous MPCs, and redistribution across earnings, nominal positions, and interest-rate exposures that systematically favors high-MPC households when policy eases [1][2][3]. Three lessons stand out. First, *who gains matters for aggregates*: redistribution is not a side effect of monetary policy but a transmission channel in its own right, formalized by the covariance between MPCs and policy-induced transfers. Second, *the fiscal footprint is inseparable from monetary transmission*: with Ricardian equivalence broken, the treasury's debt maturity and transfer incidence co-determine the central bank's power [1][3]. Third, *tractability need not be sacrificed*: analytical THANK models and sequence-space computational methods have brought heterogeneous-agent policy analysis into the realm of practical central banking [4][5], even as determinacy, forward guidance, and optimal policy acquire new, distributionally flavored answers. Open frontiers include the integration of financial intermediation, the measurement of higher-order income risk, and the design of policy rules that stabilize not only inflation and output but the inequality that shapes their dynamics.
