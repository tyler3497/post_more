# Differentiable Radiance Fields and 3D Gaussian Splatting: From Positional Encodings to Real-Time Tile-Based Novel View Synthesis

## Abstract

Photorealistic novel view synthesis from sparse calibrated images is now dominated by two differentiable paradigms: neural radiance fields, which regress continuous density and radiance with multilayer perceptrons under the classical volume-rendering integral [1], and 3D Gaussian splatting, which replaces neural evaluation with explicit anisotropic 3D Gaussians composited by a tile-based differentiable rasterizer [3]. This thesis unifies the trajectory connecting them: positional encodings that cured spectral bias, multiresolution hash grids that cut optimization from hours to seconds [2], integrated positional encodings over conical frustums that tamed aliasing [4], nonlinear scene contraction for unbounded captures [5], and the final migration of all representational work out of the network and into sortable, projectable primitives. We analyze the differential geometry of covariance projection, the statistics of hash collisions, the sampling theory of frustum integration, and the asymptotics that separate ray marching from rasterization. Benchmark results, ablations, and the open problems of dynamics, relighting, and exact sorting are reviewed.

## 1 Intro

Reconstructing a three-dimensional scene from a finite set of two-dimensional photographs, and then rendering it from viewpoints that were never captured, is among the oldest ambitions of computer graphics. For decades, the standard pipeline moved from structure-from-motion calibration to multi-view stereo geometry, then to mesh texturing — a brittle chain in which each stage amplified the errors of the last. The introduction of **neural radiance fields** in 2020 [1] collapsed this pipeline into a single differentiable optimization: a fully connected network directly regresses a continuous volumetric scene function, and images with known camera poses are the only supervision required. Because the classical volume-rendering integral is differentiable, gradients flow from pixel-space photometric error all the way back to the parameters of the field, requiring no geometry priors and no explicit correspondence matching.

Yet the original formulation was slow: training required hours and rendering a single frame required minutes, because every pixel demanded hundreds of network evaluations. Three years of intense research attacked this cost from orthogonal directions. **Instant neural graphics primitives** [2] replaced fixed Fourier encodings with trainable multiresolution hash grids, cutting training to seconds while keeping network capacity tiny. **mip-NeRF** [4] and its successors fixed the aliasing pathology by integrating the encoding over conical frustums rather than sampling points along rays. And finally, **3D Gaussian splatting** [3] discarded the network entirely, representing the scene as a collection of anisotropic 3D Gaussians whose projection and compositing can be accelerated by the same tile-based rasterization hardware philosophy that powered real-time graphics for half a century — achieving state-of-the-art quality at over one hundred frames per second at 1080p.

This thesis argues that these developments are not disconnected engineering tricks but a coherent trajectory: the progressive *devolution of work from the neural network to the representation and the renderer*. Positional encodings moved frequency content out of network weights and into the input embedding; hash grids moved spatial structure out of the network and into trainable memory; Gaussian splatting moved everything out of the network and into an explicit, sortable, projectable primitive. Understanding why each step worked — mathematically, numerically, and empirically — is the subject of what follows.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Classical volume rendering

Long before neural methods, graphics researchers formalized how light accumulates through participating media. For a ray $\mathbf{r}(t) = \mathbf{o} + t\mathbf{d}$, the emitted radiance reaching the camera is governed by the **volume-rendering integral**

$$C(\mathbf{r}) = \int_{t_n}^{t_f} T(t)\,\sigma(\mathbf{r}(t))\,\mathbf{c}(\mathbf{r}(t), \mathbf{d})\,dt,$$

where $\sigma$ is volume density, $\mathbf{c}$ is emitted color, and the **transmittance** $T(t) = \exp(-\int_{t_n}^{t} \sigma(\mathbf{r}(s))\,ds)$ captures accumulated occlusion. Because density and color are unknown functions, the integral has no closed form; the standard discretization evaluates $N$ samples along the ray and applies front-to-back alpha compositing:

$$C(\mathbf{r}) \approx \sum_{i=1}^{N} T_i\,(1 - \exp(-\sigma_i \delta_i))\,\mathbf{c}_i, \quad T_i = \exp\left(-\sum_{j=1}^{i-1} \sigma_j \delta_j\right).$$

This quadrature is piecewise-differentiable in every $\sigma_i$ and $\mathbf{c}_i$, which is the entire mathematical license for the neural era: any differentiable parameterization of the field inherits differentiability of the rendered image [1].

### 2.2 Differentiable rendering and inverse graphics

The broader program of **differentiable rendering** seeks to make the image-formation process itself a layer in a learning system, so that 3D hypotheses can be fit by gradient descent on 2D observations. Neural radiance fields instantiate this for unstructured multi-view capture: the unknowns are the parameters $\theta$ of a field $F_\theta: (\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{d}) \mapsto (\sigma, \mathbf{c})$, and the loss is simply the squared photometric residual between rendered and observed rays. Hierarchical sampling — a coarse pass whose weights guide importance resampling in a fine pass — concentrates computation where the transmittance changes most rapidly [1].

### 2.3 Spectral bias and the need for encodings

A plain multilayer perceptron exhibits **spectral bias**: gradient descent preferentially fits low-frequency functions, so raw coordinates $(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{d})$ yield blurry reconstructions. The remedy, introduced in [1], is a **positional encoding** that lifts coordinates into a Fourier basis:

$$\gamma(p) = \left(\sin(2^0 \pi p), \cos(2^0 \pi p), \ldots, \sin(2^{L-1} \pi p), \cos(2^{L-1} \pi p)\right).$$

Applied to each coordinate of position ($L=10$) and direction ($L=4$), this deterministic mapping gives the network direct access to high-frequency variation, and its optimality is supported by the analysis of random Fourier features in kernel regression. Every subsequent acceleration technique in this thesis can be read as a response to the cost and rigidity of this fixed encoding.

> Theorem: Under stratified sampling along the ray and the piecewise-constant density approximation, the quadrature estimator is consistent for the volume-rendering integral as $N \to \infty$ and $\max_i \delta_i \to 0$, with bias vanishing at the rate of the local variation of $\sigma$ and $\mathbf{c}$.

### 2.4 From implicit to explicit primitives

Between 2021 and 2023, a parallel literature explored **explicit** representations — voxel grids, factorized tensors, and point primitives — that trade the compactness of a neural field for direct memory access and hardware-friendly evaluation. The culmination of this line is 3D Gaussian splatting [3], which keeps the differentiable volume-rendering loss but replaces the MLP with millions of optimizable Gaussians. The intellectual continuity is exact: both frameworks optimize a continuous volumetric function by gradient descent on rendered pixels; they differ only in how the function is parameterized and how the integral is evaluated.

## 3 Methodology

Our analysis considers two canonical optimization setups that share a photometric objective but diverge in representation.

**Neural setup (NeRF family).** The scene is $F_\theta(\gamma(\mathbf{x}), \gamma(\mathbf{d})) = (\sigma, \mathbf{c})$ with $\theta$ the weights of an 8-layer MLP of width 256 in the original design [1], or a far smaller network (2 layers of width 64) sitting atop a trainable multiresolution hash encoding [2]. Rays are sampled with stratified or conical-frustum sampling [4]; color is accumulated by the alpha-compositing quadrature; the loss $\mathcal{L} = \sum_{\mathbf{r}} \lVert \hat{C}(\mathbf{r}) - C(\mathbf{r}) \rVert_2^2$ is minimized with Adam. Poses come from structure-from-motion calibration, and a coarse-to-fine sampling hierarchy focuses samples near surfaces.

**Explicit setup (3D Gaussian splatting).** The scene is a set of $M$ Gaussians, each with position $\boldsymbol{\mu} \in \mathbb{R}^3$, covariance $\boldsymbol{\Sigma} = \mathbf{R}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{S}^{\top}\mathbf{R}^{\top}$ parameterized by a rotation quaternion $\mathbf{q}$ and a scale vector $\mathbf{s}$, opacity $o$, and view-dependent color encoded as spherical-harmonics coefficients [3]. Initialization uses the sparse point cloud from calibration. Rendering proceeds by (i) projecting each 3D Gaussian to a 2D covariance on the image plane via $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}' = \mathbf{J}\mathbf{W}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mathbf{W}^{\top}\mathbf{J}^{\top}$, where $\mathbf{W}$ is the viewing transformation and $\mathbf{J}$ the Jacobian of the projective mapping; (ii) assigning each Gaussian to the screen tiles it overlaps; (iii) depth-sorting Gaussians per tile; and (iv) front-to-back alpha blending per pixel. Optimization alternates gradient steps with **adaptive density control**: Gaussians with large view-space positional gradients are cloned (if small) or split (if large), and low-opacity Gaussians are pruned.

Both setups are evaluated on the same task — novel view synthesis from calibrated multi-view images — with PSNR, SSIM, and LPIPS as metrics and training time and rendering frame rate as efficiency measures.

| Method | Representation | Renderer | Training | 1080p FPS |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| NeRF [1] | MLP + Fourier encoding | Ray-march quadrature | hours | < 0.05 |
| Instant-NGP [2] | Hash grids + tiny MLP | Ray-march quadrature | seconds–minutes | ~10 |
| mip-NeRF [4] | MLP + integrated encoding | Conical-frustum quadrature | hours | < 0.05 |
| mip-NeRF 360 [5] | MLP + contraction | Frustum + proposal MLP | hours | < 0.05 |
| 3D Gaussian Splatting [3] | Anisotropic Gaussians | Tile-based rasterizer | ~50 min | > 100 |

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Positional encoding and the Fourier feature hypothesis

The positional encoding of [1] is best understood not as a heuristic but as a **kernel shaping** device. Mapping $p \mapsto \gamma(p)$ makes the neural tangent kernel of the network stationary with tunable bandwidth: the frequencies $2^0 \pi$ through $2^{L-1} \pi$ determine which spatial scales gradient descent can fit at a practical rate. Choosing $L=10$ for position and $L=4$ for direction reflects an empirical bandwidth budget — more frequencies increase both the fidelity of fine detail and the risk of overfitting to view-dependent noise. The weakness of the scheme is its rigidity: the same frequency set serves smooth walls and sharp textures, and every sample pays the full cost of evaluating the encoding and the deep network. This observation motivates trainable encodings.

```haskell
-- Positional encoding as a pure function over octaves
positionalEncoding :: Int -> Double -> [Double]
positionalEncoding l p =
  concatMap (\k -> let f = 2^k * pi * p in [sin f, cos f]) [0 .. l - 1]

-- Applied per-coordinate; direction uses fewer octaves
encodeInput :: [Double] -> [Double]
encodeInput [x, y, z, dx, dy, dz] =
  concatMap (positionalEncoding 10) [x, y, z]
  ++ concatMap (positionalEncoding 4) [dx, dy, dz]
```

### 4.2 Multiresolution hash grids: moving structure into memory

Instant-NGP [2] replaces the fixed Fourier basis with **trainable feature vectors stored in multiresolution hash tables**. At each of $L$ levels, the space is covered by a grid of resolution growing geometrically; the feature at a query point is the trilinear interpolation of the features stored at the eight surrounding voxel vertices, where the vertex index is hashed via $h(\mathbf{x}) = (\bigoplus_i x_i \pi_i) \bmod T$ into a table of size $T$. Collisions are not resolved — they are *tolerated*, because the multiresolution structure lets the network disambiguate: a collision at a fine level is unlikely to collide again at every other level, and gradient averaging over collisions acts as a regularizer. The concatenated features feed a tiny MLP (2 hidden layers), and the whole pipeline is implemented in fully fused CUDA kernels that keep the working set in on-chip memory. The result is a speedup of several orders of magnitude — NeRF-quality reconstructions in seconds — achieved not by a better network but by **relocating representational capacity from weights to addressed memory** [2].

The trade-off is memory locality: hash tables of size $2^{19}$ entries per level consume hundreds of megabytes, and collisions can produce subtle high-frequency artifacts that the fixed Fourier encoding never exhibited. Subsequent work showed that the same hash encoding, combined with the anti-aliasing machinery of mip-NeRF, yields error rates 8 to 77 percent lower than either technique alone while training 24 times faster than mip-NeRF 360 [6].

### 4.3 Anisotropic Gaussians: covariance, projection, and sorting

3D Gaussian splatting [3] parameterizes the scene as a mixture of anisotropic kernels

$$G(\mathbf{x}) = \exp\left(-\tfrac{1}{2}(\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu})^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}(\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu})\right),$$

with $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}$ factored as $\mathbf{R}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{S}^{\top}\mathbf{R}^{\top}$ so that optimization over rotations and scales preserves positive-definiteness by construction. View-dependent color is carried by spherical-harmonics coefficients up to degree three, evaluated per viewing direction — a compact, interpretable alternative to a directional MLP. Projection to screen space uses the local affine approximation $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}' = \mathbf{J}\mathbf{W}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mathbf{W}^{\top}\mathbf{J}^{\top}$, which is exact in the limit of small Gaussians and is the classical EWA splatting covariance [3].

Rendering then becomes a sorting problem. The screen is divided into $16 \times 16$ tiles; each Gaussian is assigned to every tile its 2D extent overlaps; within each tile, Gaussians are sorted by depth using a fast GPU radix sort on 64-bit keys combining tile ID and depth; and each pixel blends its tile's sorted list front-to-back:

$$C = \sum_{i} \mathbf{c}_i \alpha_i \prod_{j<i} (1 - \alpha_j),$$

where $\alpha_i$ is the opacity-weighted 2D Gaussian response. Because the sort is global per tile and the blend is a scan, the entire pipeline maps to GPU parallelism with no ray marching and no network evaluation — this is why frame rates exceed one hundred at 1080p while quality matches or exceeds the best neural methods [3].

```python
# Covariance factorization: Sigma = R S S^T R^T, always PSD by construction
import numpy as np

def covariance_from_rs(scale, quat):
    S = np.diag(scale)                       # anisotropic scales
    R = quaternion_to_matrix(quat)           # rotation
    return R @ S @ S.T @ R.T

def project_covariance(Sigma, W, J):
    # EWA splatting: view transform then projective Jacobian
    return J @ W @ Sigma @ W.T @ J.T
```

Adaptive density control closes the optimization loop: Gaussians whose view-space positional gradients exceed a threshold are **densified** — cloned when small (replicating detail) or split when large (increasing resolution) — while near-transparent Gaussians are pruned. Starting from the sparse calibration point cloud, the representation grows to millions of primitives where the scene demands detail and stays sparse in empty space, recovering the adaptive-computation property that hierarchical sampling gave NeRF [1][3].

### 4.4 Anti-aliasing: conical frustums and integrated encodings

A single ray per pixel is a point sample of a continuous signal, and point sampling aliases. mip-NeRF [4] replaces rays with **conical frustums** matched to the pixel footprint and integrates the positional encoding over each frustum's Gaussian approximation, yielding the **integrated positional encoding** $\mathbb{E}[\gamma(\mathbf{X})]$ in closed form from the frustum's mean and covariance. This single change reduces error rates by 17 percent on the original benchmark and 60 percent on a multiscale variant, at 7 percent lower cost than NeRF, because one frustum evaluation carries the information of many supersampled rays [4].

For unbounded 360-degree captures, mip-NeRF 360 [5] adds a **nonlinear scene contraction** that maps distant space into a bounded ball, a proposal MLP that distills sampling weights for efficiency, and a distortion regularizer that concentrates density into compact surfaces. Zip-NeRF [6] later ported the anti-aliasing insight to grid-based representations via multisampling and prefiltering, demonstrating that the aliasing problem was never intrinsic to neural fields — it was intrinsic to point sampling, and it afflicts Gaussian splatting too, where recent work derives exact ray-Gaussian integration to remove the affine projection approximation.

> Theorem: The integrated positional encoding equals the expected Fourier feature of a Gaussian-distributed position, and its closed form attenuates exactly those frequencies whose period is smaller than the frustum cross-section — i.e., it implements a scale-aware low-pass filter matched to the pixel footprint [4].

## 5 Theoretical Analysis and Empirical Results

**Complexity.** Ray-marching a neural field costs $O(P \cdot N \cdot C)$ for $P$ pixels, $N$ samples, and $C$ the per-sample network cost; Instant-NGP [2] minimizes $C$ via tiny networks and fused kernels, while mip-NeRF 360 [5] minimizes $N$ via a proposal network. Gaussian splatting costs $O(M \log M)$ for the per-tile sort of $M$ Gaussians plus $O(P \cdot K)$ for blending $K$ overlapping splats per pixel — with no per-sample neural cost at all. This is the fundamental asymptotic reason rasterization wins at inference: the constant factors of neural evaluation dominate any plausible $N$ once $P$ reaches megapixel scale [3].

**Quality.** On the standard real-world benchmarks, 3D Gaussian splatting trained for roughly 51 minutes slightly exceeds mip-NeRF 360 [5] in PSNR while rendering hundreds of times faster; with training times comparable to Instant-NGP it matches Instant-NGP quality, which is the maximum quality that method reaches [3]. mip-NeRF's frustum formulation remains the quality ceiling for bounded scenes at the cost of minutes per frame [4], and Zip-NeRF [6] currently dominates grid-based methods on anti-aliased detail.

**Ablations.** Removing adaptive densification from 3DGS collapses thin structures; removing the SH coefficients degrades view-dependent effects such as specular highlights to diffuse averages; replacing the per-tile radix sort with per-pixel insertion sort destroys real-time performance. In the neural family, ablating the hierarchical sampler or the positional encoding reproduces the original paper's dramatic quality drops [1][2][3].

**Failure modes of the theory.** The affine projection approximation in 3DGS breaks down for very large Gaussians near the camera, producing popping; the per-tile sort is by Gaussian center depth, so interpenetrating Gaussians can blend in the wrong order; and hash-grid collisions in Instant-NGP, while statistically benign, are not provably so — adversarial scenes can concentrate collisions and produce visible artifacts [2][3].

## 6 Limitations

1. **Static, bounded-by-capture scenes.** All methods surveyed assume rigid scenes and known poses; dynamic content, deforming geometry, and pose-free optimization remain active research areas with no consensus solution.
2. **Memory footprint of explicitness.** Millions of Gaussians with SH coefficients consume hundreds of megabytes to gigabytes — far more than a neural field's weights — and unbounded scenes scale poorly without level-of-detail hierarchies [3].
3. **Sorting and projection approximations.** Tile-based sorting by center depth is not a true per-pixel depth order, and the Jacobian projection is only locally affine; both introduce artifacts under extreme close-ups [3].
4. **Aliasing is reduced, not solved.** Conical frustums [4] and multisampled grids [6] tame aliasing for neural fields, but Gaussian splatting's screen-space splats still alias under minification without explicit prefiltering.
5. **Calibration dependence.** Every method inherits the failure modes of structure-from-motion: textureless regions, reflective surfaces, and rolling-shutter distortion corrupt the poses on which all subsequent optimization depends.
6. **No relighting.** Radiance fields bake illumination into emitted color; changing the lighting requires inverse-rendering extensions that factor reflectance from illumination, which the surveyed methods do not address.

## 7 Conclusion

The arc from NeRF [1] to 3D Gaussian splatting [3] is the story of a field learning where computation belongs. The volume-rendering integral made the problem differentiable; positional encodings made it learnable at high frequency; hash grids made it fast by moving structure into memory; conical frustums made it correct across scales; and anisotropic Gaussians with tile-based rasterization made it real-time by moving everything out of the network. Each step preserved the photometric objective while changing the answer to a single question: *what is the cheapest substrate that can carry the scene's information?* The current answer — sorted, projectable, explicitly stored primitives — looks remarkably like classical point-based graphics, except that every parameter is now fit by gradient descent. The remaining frontiers are clear: dynamics, relighting, unbounded scale, and exactness of the rasterization approximations. The differentiable rendering program that NeRF inaugurated is not finished; it has simply outgrown the network that started it.
