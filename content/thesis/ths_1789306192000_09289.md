# Equality Saturation and E-Graphs: Congruence Closure, Rewriting, and Optimal Program Extraction at Scale

## Abstract

Equality saturation is a rewrite-driven optimization methodology that avoids the phase-ordering problem of traditional compilers by simultaneously exploring *all* reachable program variants rather than committing to a single sequence of transformations. At its core lies the e-graph, a data structure originally developed for automated theorem proving that compactly represents a congruence relation over an exponentially large set of terms. This thesis develops the theory and practice of equality saturation: the e-graph's congruence-closure invariants and amortized rebuilding discipline, e-class analyses that lift semantic reasoning into syntactic rewriting, e-matching as conjunctive query evaluation with worst-case-optimal join algorithms, and cost-driven extraction of near-optimal programs from the saturated space. We trace the lineage from the PEG-based saturation engines of Tate, Stepp, Tatlock, and Lerner [1] through the egg library of Willsey et al. [2] to relational e-matching [3] and the unification of Datalog with equality saturation in egglog [4], grounding the technique in its semantic foundations [5]. Empirical evidence spans floating-point rewriters, deep-learning graph superoptimizers [6], and abstraction-learning program synthesizers [7], establishing equality saturation as a general, principled framework for optimization modulo equivalence.

## 1 Introduction

Program optimization has historically been organized as a pipeline: a sequence of passes, each destructively rewriting the intermediate representation before handing control to the next. This architecture suffers from two deep pathologies. First, the *phase-ordering problem*: the quality of the final program depends on the order in which optimizations are applied, yet no universally good order exists, because an early transformation can enable or foreclose opportunities that a later pass would exploit. Second, *local profitability*: heuristics decide whether to fire a single rewrite in isolation, with no global view of how that decision interacts with future transformations [1].

Equality saturation, introduced by Tate et al., proposes a radical restructuring of the optimizer. Optimizations are recast as *equality analyses* that do not replace program fragments but instead record equivalences between them in a shared representation [1]. The optimizer repeatedly applies these analyses until the representation is *saturated* — no new equalities can be added — at which point it encodes, in compressed form, the full set of programs reachable by any ordering of the analyses. A global profitability heuristic then selects the best representative from this space. Rather than choosing a phase order up front, equality saturation *defers every choice until all information is available*.

The data structure that makes this deferred-choice discipline feasible is the **e-graph**. Originally developed in the late 1970s for use in automated theorem provers [2], an e-graph efficiently represents a congruence relation over a huge set of ground terms, sharing structure so aggressively that exponentially many equivalent expressions occupy polynomial space. Equality saturation repurposes this machinery: rewrite rules add equalities to the e-graph, and congruence closure automatically propagates those equalities through all contexts in which the rewritten terms appear [1].

This thesis presents a comprehensive account of equality saturation and e-graphs. We formalize the e-graph and its invariants, develop the saturation algorithm and its modern performance engineering — including amortized *rebuilding* and *e-class analyses* [2] — and explain the recent reconceptualization of e-matching as database query evaluation [3] and of the entire algorithm as a fragment of the database chase [5]. We cover the convergence of e-graphs with Datalog in egglog [4], survey empirical results across domains, and examine the technique's fundamental limitations: e-graph explosion, the intractability of optimal extraction, and the boundaries of termination.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Congruence relations and the word problem

Let Σ be a finite ranked alphabet of function symbols. A *ground term* over Σ is built from function symbols applied to ground terms. A binary relation ∼ on ground terms is a **congruence** if it is an equivalence relation and respects the term constructors: for every n-ary f ∈ Σ, if t_i ∼ s_i for all i, then f(t₁, …, tₙ) ∼ f(s₁, …, sₙ). The *word problem* asks, given a set E of identities between terms, whether E implies t₁ ≈ t₂ for two ground terms — i.e., whether t₁ and t₂ are related by the smallest congruence containing E.

Nelson's e-graph, introduced in the context of his thesis work on program verification and further developed for the Simplify and Z3-era theorem provers, is a data structure for answering exactly this question [2]. Rather than enumerating the (potentially infinite) congruence closure, the e-graph maintains it *symbolically*: each e-node represents a function application whose children are *canonical e-class identifiers*, and the union-find structure over e-classes records discovered equalities. Two invariants define an e-graph: the union-find relation is a congruence, and the hashcons canonicalizes e-nodes so congruent applications are never duplicated [2]. A foundational result, made precise in the semantic account of [5], is that a *finite* e-graph can represent an *infinite* set of ground terms: cycles in the e-graph correspond to infinite unfoldings, just as in regular tree automata.

### 2.2 Term rewriting and its discontents

A rewrite rule ℓ → r asserts an oriented identity; a *term rewriting system* (TRS) is a set of such rules, and rewriting replaces instances of ℓ by corresponding instances of r.

Equality saturation takes the opposite stance. Rather than orienting rules and demanding termination, it treats rules as *bidirectional* axioms, applies them exhaustively inside the e-graph, and lets congruence closure do the heavy lifting [1]. Where Knuth–Bendix completion is brittle, saturation is robust: non-terminating rules are tamed by *bounding* the number of iterations or by *schedulers* that throttle explosive rules, and the e-graph's sharing keeps the representation tractable even when the term space it encodes is enormous.

### 2.3 From E-PEGs to egg

The original equality saturation engine, Peggy, operated on **E-PEGs**: program expression graphs augmented with equality edges [1]. However, E-PEGs were specialized to Java-like imperative programs, and converting back to executable code (reversion) was delicate.

The egg library generalized and industrialized these ideas [2]. egg provides a language-agnostic e-graph in Rust, achieving asymptotic speedups through a new *rebuilding* discipline for invariant restoration and adding *e-class analyses* so that semantic information (constant folding, cost models, interval bounds) can cooperate with syntactic rewrites. Where Peggy was a compiler, egg is a framework: it has since underwritten floating-point accuracy tools, tensor-graph optimizers, hardware-design explorers, and program synthesizers [2].

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 The e-graph, formally

Fix a signature Σ. An **e-node** is a pair (f, c₁, …, cₙ) where f ∈ Σ is n-ary and each cᵢ is an e-class identifier. An **e-class** is a set of e-nodes, intuitively the set of all terms known to be equal. The e-graph maintains:

- a union-find over e-class identifiers, recording which classes have been merged;
- a hashcons mapping each *canonical* e-node (all children canonicalized) to its e-class, enforcing the no-duplicates invariant.

Adding a term t inserts its subterms bottom-up: for f(t₁, …, tₙ), canonicalize each child's class, form the e-node, and look it up in the hashcons; if present, merge the existing class with the target, otherwise create a fresh class. **Merging** two e-classes unions their e-node sets and their union-find entries, which may *break* canonicalization: e-nodes elsewhere that point to the old identifiers must be re-canonicalized, potentially triggering further merges. This is congruence closure, and the e-graph maintains it incrementally.

### 3.2 The saturation loop

Equality saturation is a fixpoint iteration over the e-graph:

1. **E-match**: for each rewrite rule ℓ → r, find all substitutions σ such that the pattern ℓσ occurs in the e-graph (i.e., each subterm of ℓσ is represented in some e-class).
2. **Apply**: for each match, add the term rσ to the e-graph and merge its e-class with the e-class of ℓσ.
3. **Rebuild**: restore the congruence invariant broken by the merges.
4. Repeat until no new e-nodes are added (*saturation*) or a bound (iteration limit, node limit, timeout) is reached. A *scheduler* modulates which rules fire in each iteration; egg's backoff scheduler exponentially delays rules whose matches grow without yielding new information, taming rules like associativity that would otherwise explode the e-graph [2].

### 3.3 Rebuilding: amortized invariant restoration

The naive approach restores congruence after every single merge, re-canonicalizing e-nodes eagerly. In equality saturation workloads — thousands of merges per iteration, most of them redundant — this is disastrous. egg's **rebuilding** instead *defers* invariant restoration to the end of each iteration [2]. A worklist collects e-classes whose canonical representatives may have changed; a single rebuild pass canonicalizes each e-node once, deduplicates via the hashcons, and merges classes that have become congruent, iterating the worklist to a local fixpoint. This amortized discipline batches canonicalization work per iteration instead of per merge [2].

### 3.4 E-class analyses

Purely syntactic rewrites are often insufficient: one wants conditional rules (e.g., x / x → 1 *when x is provably nonzero*), constant folding, and cost models. egg's **e-class analyses** integrate such domain knowledge directly into the e-graph [2]. An analysis attaches a value to each e-class, computed bottom-up from e-node values:

```rust
pub trait Analysis<L: Language> {
    type Data: Clone;
    fn make(egraph: &EGraph<L, Self>, enode: &L) -> Self::Data;
    fn merge(a: &mut Self::Data, b: Self::Data) -> DidMerge;
    fn modify(egraph: &mut EGraph<L, Self>, id: Id) { }
}
```

`make` computes the analysis value of a single e-node from its children's values; `merge` combines values when e-classes union (returning whether anything changed); `modify` optionally *adds* new e-nodes based on analysis results, enabling, for example, constant folding that inserts the folded literal into the class.

### 3.5 Extraction

Once saturated, the e-graph contains many equivalent programs; **extraction** selects one. The standard approach defines a *cost function* over e-nodes — e.g., AST size, estimated runtime, or floating-point error — and computes, for each e-class, the minimum-cost representative by a bottom-up dynamic program over the e-graph's DAG structure. For richer objectives — including cyclic e-graphs or global constraints — extraction can be formulated as an integer linear program (ILP) over e-class choices, trading optimality guarantees against solver cost.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Congruence closure and the rebuilding invariant

The correctness of every e-graph rests on a single invariant.

> **Theorem (Congruence invariant).** At every quiescent point of the e-graph (i.e., after rebuilding completes), two e-nodes are in the same e-class if and only if the congruence closure of all asserted equalities relates them; furthermore, no two distinct e-nodes in the hashcons are congruent.

Rebuilding maintains this invariant by a classic worklist algorithm, but its *amortized* character is what matters for saturation workloads. Because a saturation iteration performs Θ(m) merges for a batch of rule firings and only one rebuild, the total canonicalization work per iteration is proportional to the number of e-nodes actually affected by merges rather than to the number of merges [2]. The deferral is sound precisely because e-matching is *monotone* with respect to congruence inclusion: matches found against a stale (not-yet-rebuilt) e-graph remain valid after rebuilding, since rebuilding only adds equalities. This observation — that the equality-saturation workload tolerates lazy invariant restoration — is the theoretical content behind rebuilding's practical speedups [2].

### 4.2 E-matching as conjunctive query evaluation

E-matching dominates equality-saturation runtime; profiling consistently shows it, not congruence closure, as the bottleneck [3]. The classical algorithm is *backtracking*: it walks the pattern top-down, enumerating candidate e-nodes at each position and checking the equality constraints between repeated variables only at the end. For a pattern like f(α, g(α, β)) against an e-graph with N f-nodes and N g-nodes, backtracking takes Θ(N²) time to produce N matches, because it cannot exploit the constraint that the two α positions must agree until after constructing each candidate [3].

Relational e-matching reframes the problem in database terms [3]. View each function symbol f of arity n as a relation R_f(id, c₁, …, cₙ) containing one tuple per e-node, where id is the e-class and the cᵢ are canonical child classes. A pattern becomes a **conjunctive query**: each pattern position contributes an atom, and each repeated variable becomes an *equality constraint* — a join key. For f(α, g(α, β)):

| Atom | Relation | Constraint |
|------|----------|------------|
| R_f(r, α, x) | f-nodes | parent link |
| R_g(x, α, β) | g-nodes | α shared ⇒ join key |

The query Q(r, α, β) ← R_f(r, α, x), R_g(x, α, β) is evaluated with a **generic join** algorithm, which processes variables in an order chosen by a query optimizer and intersects candidate sets per variable. This yields the first *data complexity* result for e-matching: runtime bounded by the AGM bound in terms of e-graph size and output size, i.e., *worst-case optimal* [3]. Concretely, the join checks the α-equality constraint *first* (it is a join key, not a post-filter), reducing the f(α, g(α, β)) example from Θ(N²) to Θ(N). Zhang et al. report orders-of-magnitude speedups over the backtracking baseline in egg, with a simpler implementation [3].

### 4.3 E-class analyses and conditional rewriting

E-class analyses are the mechanism by which equality saturation escapes pure syntax. Consider constant folding: an analysis maps each e-class to `Option<Constant>`; `make` evaluates an e-node when all children are constant, `merge` keeps agreement, and `modify` inserts the folded literal e-node into the class. The rewrite x + 0 → x then fires on the *folded* forms automatically, and — crucially — the analysis values flow *upward* through congruence: folding a subterm can trigger folding of its parents in the same rebuild pass [2].

Conditional rewrites follow the same pattern. The rule x / x → 1 is unsound when x = 0; with an analysis tracking whether a class is provably nonzero, the applier checks the analysis value before firing [4]. This pattern — analysis-gated rewriting — is pervasive in practical saturation systems: interval analyses justify reassociation of floating-point operations, sign analyses justify square-root simplifications, and cost analyses guide scheduler backoff [2], [4].

### 4.4 Extraction: from greedy heuristics to global optimality

Extraction is where the deferred choices of saturation are finally resolved. The workhorse is the *local-cost* extractor: given a cost function c on e-nodes, define the cost of an e-class as the minimum over its e-nodes of c(node) plus the costs of the node's child classes, computed bottom-up. When costs are additive and the e-graph is acyclic, this dynamic program is exactly optimal, and it runs in linear time [2].

Reality is messier. Cyclic e-graphs (arising from rules like x → x + 0 applied bidirectionally) break the bottom-up order; egg resolves cycles by a fixpoint iteration that is optimal for monotone cost functions on the DAG of *strongly connected components* [2]. Genuinely global objectives become integer linear programs over e-class selections — NP-hard in general, hence used sparingly on small e-graphs [2].

The deeper point is architectural: because saturation *separates* the exploration of equivalences from the selection among them, the extraction objective can be changed without re-running saturation.

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

The empirical record of equality saturation spans compilers, numerical software, machine learning systems, and program synthesis.

**Origins: Peggy.** Tate et al.'s E-PEG saturation engine demonstrated practicality on Java bytecode optimization: 84% of methods could be completely saturated without artificial bounds, the engine discovered intricate optimization opportunities missed by the baseline compiler, and the same machinery performed *translation validation* of an external optimizer, proving equivalence between unoptimized and optimized programs [1]. These results established that representing exponentially many program variants in one structure was not merely a theoretical curiosity.

**egg: performance and generality.** Willsey et al. evaluated egg on three previously published equality-saturation applications, showing that rebuilding and e-class analyses deliver state-of-the-art results across domains with a single unified implementation [2].

**Relational e-matching.** Zhang et al. established the first data-complexity bound for e-matching and showed that the relational implementation is orders of magnitude faster than conventional backtracking within the same egg framework, while being simpler to implement [3].

**egglog: unifying Datalog and EqSat.** Zhang et al.'s egglog merges Datalog's semi-naïve bottom-up evaluation with equality saturation's congruence closure [4]. Microbenchmarks show the non-incremental variant (egglogNI) achieving a 3.34× speedup over egg, and full egglog — with semi-naïve evaluation — reaching 9.27× [4]. The paper's two motivating applications, a unification-based pointer analysis and a floating-point term rewriter, became faster, simpler, and less buggy when reimplemented in egglog [4].

**Semantic foundations.** Wang, Zhang, and Suciu gave the first rigorous semantic account of equality saturation, connecting it to the database *chase*: the Skolem chase terminates if and only if equality saturation terminates, and e-graphs correspond to a class of tree automata closed under the chase steps [5]. This places EqSat on the same formal footing as decades of database theory and opens the transfer of chase termination criteria to saturation.

**Applications at scale.** In deep learning, TASO's superoptimization of tensor computation graphs achieved substantial inference speedups on production architectures [6]. In program synthesis, babble uses e-graphs and anti-unification to learn library abstractions, achieving better compression ratios than DreamCoder on its benchmarks, and doing so much faster [7]. Together, these results show the technique thriving wherever the search space is a large equivalence class of programs.

## 6 Limitations

**E-graph explosion.** Saturation is a fixpoint computation over a space that can grow without bound. Rules like associativity and distributivity generate combinatorially many terms; without schedulers, node limits, or iteration caps, the e-graph exhausts memory. Backoff schedulers mitigate but do not eliminate the problem, and tuning them remains a black art [2].

**Extraction intractability.** Local-cost extraction is linear and optimal only for additive costs on acyclic graphs. Global objectives require ILP solving, which is NP-hard and impractical on large e-graphs; practitioners fall back to greedy extraction and accept suboptimality [2].

**Conditional reasoning is shallow.** E-class analyses are bottom-up and per-class; they cannot express context-sensitive or relational properties across classes. Conditional rewrites gated on analyses are only as strong as the analyses themselves, and strengthening them often reintroduces the ad-hoc extensions analyses were meant to replace [4].

**Termination is undecidable in general.** Like the chase it mirrors, equality saturation need not terminate; the semantic correspondence of [5] transfers both the chase's power and its undecidability. Bounds guarantee termination only by abandoning completeness.

**Control flow and reversion.** The original E-PEG work showed that converting imperative programs with loops and branches into a functional, saturable IR — and converting back — is delicate; reversion from PEGs to CFGs can degrade code quality [1].

**Proof burden for rules.** Every rewrite rule is an axiom the system trusts. An incorrect rule silently poisons the e-graph, and — unlike a miscompiled pass that can be bisected — the damage is a wrong equivalence woven through the congruence closure. Systems like TASO address this with verified substitution generation [6], but verification of rule sets remains the exception rather than the norm.

## 7 Conclusion

Equality saturation restructures optimization around a simple idea with deep consequences: never choose when you can defer. By recording equivalences in an e-graph rather than committing to transformations, it converts the phase-ordering problem from a scheduling dilemma into a search problem over a compactly represented space, resolved at the end by a global extraction heuristic [1]. The engineering advances of the last decade — amortized rebuilding, e-class analyses [2], worst-case-optimal relational e-matching [3], and the fusion with Datalog in egglog [4] — have turned a compelling prototype into a general methodology with verified semantic foundations [5] and demonstrated impact from floating-point arithmetic to deep-learning superoptimization [6] and program synthesis [7].

The open frontiers are clear: taming e-graph explosion with principled scheduling, scaling optimal extraction beyond greedy heuristics, enriching analyses with contextual and relational reasoning, and extending saturation soundly to effectful, concurrent, and higher-order languages. The trajectory from Nelson's congruence-closure data structure to today's fixpoint engines suggests that the deepest progress will continue to come from recognizing equality saturation as what it has always been — a database problem wearing a compiler's clothes.
