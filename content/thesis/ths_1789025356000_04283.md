# Auction Theory and Mechanism Design: VCG Mechanisms, Myerson's Lemma, and Revenue Equivalence

## Abstract

Auctions are among the oldest economic institutions, yet their rigorous analysis is recent. This thesis reconstructs the core of modern auction theory: William Vickrey's 1961 proof that truth-telling is a dominant strategy in second-price auctions and his original revenue equivalence result [1], and Roger Myerson's 1981 characterization of incentive-compatible mechanisms and the optimal auction [2]. We develop the central machinery — the **revelation principle**, **Myerson's lemma** (the payment identity), and the **revenue equivalence theorem** — and show how it unifies disparate auction formats. We examine the Vickrey–Clarke–Groves (VCG) mechanism [1][3], its pivot-payment rule and its celebrated deficiencies; Myerson's virtual-valuation framework, the ironing procedure, and the **Bulow–Roberts marginal-revenue interpretation** [5]; and sponsored search advertising, contrasting the industry-standard Generalized Second-Price auction with VCG [7]. We close with the Bulow–Klemperer competition theorem [6], stressing where the classical assumptions — symmetry, risk neutrality, independent private values — break, and why Myerson–Satterthwaite impossibility [9] bounds the field.

## 1 Introduction

An auction is a *game designed on purpose*: an institution whose rules map the private information of participants — their valuations — into an allocation of goods and a vector of payments. Governments sell radio spectrum, treasury bills, and timber rights by auction; platforms sell billions of dollars of advertising by auction; procurement agencies buy everything from highway construction to hospital services by auction. The choice of rules changes behavior in subtle, sometimes catastrophic ways [8].

The modern mathematical theory of auctions begins with a single remarkable paper. In "Counterspeculation, Auctions, and Competitive Sealed Tenders" (1961), William Vickrey posed what now looks like the canonical mechanism-design question: given that bidders know their own valuations but not each other's, which rules make bidding honestly the optimal strategy, and what revenues do different rules produce [1]? First, in a second-price sealed-bid auction — the highest bidder wins but pays the second-highest bid — *truth-telling is a weakly dominant strategy*. Second, under symmetric, risk-neutral, independent private values, the four canonical formats — English, Dutch, first-price, and second-price sealed-bid — all yield the *same expected revenue*: the original **revenue equivalence theorem**, which launched a research program that dominated information economics for the next half-century.

The subsequent history of the field is a sequence of increasingly powerful answers to one question: *what can any mechanism achieve?* The revelation principle reduces every equilibrium to a truthful direct mechanism. Myerson (1981) then derived a single *payment identity* — Myerson's lemma — pinning expected payments to the allocation rule alone, delivering revenue equivalence as a corollary and turning revenue maximization into pointwise optimization over allocation rules [2]. The optimal auction maximizes expected *virtual surplus* rather than welfare; Bulow and Roberts (1989) showed virtual values are exactly marginal revenues [5].

Yet elegance is not practicality. The Vickrey–Clarke–Groves (VCG) mechanism generalizes the Vickrey auction to arbitrary allocation problems — efficient, dominant-strategy, charging each agent the externality she imposes — but is, in Ausubel and Milgrom's phrase, "lovely but lonely" [8]: vulnerable to collusion, shill bidding, and revenue deficits. Meanwhile, the multi-billion-dollar sponsored search industry converged on the *Generalized Second-Price* (GSP) auction, whose locally envy-free equilibria recover VCG payoffs despite having no dominant strategy [7].

This thesis proceeds as follows. Section 2 fixes the model and the foundational solution concepts. Section 3 develops Myerson's analytical machinery. Section 4 contains the deep dive: VCG, the lemma and revenue equivalence, optimal auctions and ironing, and the sponsored search application. Section 5 states and proves the central theorems and surveys the empirical record, including the Bulow–Klemperer competition result. Section 6 catalogues the limitations, and Section 7 concludes.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The Model

We consider a seller with one indivisible good and *n* risk-neutral bidders. Bidder *i* has a private valuation *vᵢ* drawn independently from a distribution *Fᵢ* with strictly positive density *fᵢ* on support *[v̲ᵢ, v̄ᵢ]*. The profile of values is **v** = (*v₁, …, vₙ*), and values are independent private values (IPV): each bidder's value depends only on her own information. A *mechanism* is a pair of functions (*x*, *p*) where *xᵢ*(**v̂**) ∈ [0,1] is the probability bidder *i* receives the good given reported profile **v̂**, with ∑*ᵢ xᵢ*(**v̂**) ≤ 1, and *pᵢ*(**v̂**) is *i*'s expected payment. Bidder *i* has quasilinear utility *uᵢ* = *vᵢ xᵢ* − *pᵢ*.

Three constraints discipline every mechanism:

- **Incentive compatibility (IC):** truthful reporting is a Bayesian–Nash equilibrium. Bidder *i* with value *vᵢ* cannot gain by reporting *v̂ᵢ ≠ vᵢ*:
  *E*[vᵢ xᵢ(vᵢ, **v**₋ᵢ) − pᵢ(vᵢ, **v**₋ᵢ)] ≥ E[vᵢ xᵢ(v̂ᵢ, **v**₋ᵢ) − pᵢ(v̂ᵢ, **v**₋ᵢ)].
- **Individual rationality (IR):** each bidder's expected utility from participation is nonnegative: *Uᵢ*(v̲ᵢ) ≥ 0.
- **Feasibility:** the allocation probabilities sum to at most one.

> **Theorem: The Revelation Principle.** For any mechanism and any Bayesian–Nash equilibrium of the game it induces, there exists an *equivalent direct mechanism* — one in which bidders report their types to a mediator — with an equilibrium in which all bidders report truthfully and which generates the same allocation and payments. Consequently, restricting attention to incentive-compatible direct revelation mechanisms is without loss of generality.

The revelation principle — developed through Gibbard (1973), Green and Laffont (1977), and Myerson — is what makes mechanism design tractable: we search only over *truthful direct mechanisms* (*x*, *p*) satisfying IC and IR.

### 2.2 The Four Canonical Formats

| Format | Bidding | Winner | Payment | Strategy |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| English (ascending) | Open, ascending | Last remaining | Price where second-last dropped | Dominant: stay in until price exceeds value |
| Dutch (descending) | Price falls | First to accept | Price at acceptance | Shade below value (strategically = first-price) |
| First-price sealed-bid | Sealed bids | Highest bid | Own bid | Shade below value; symmetric BNE bid *b(v)* = E[Y \| Y < v] |
| Second-price sealed-bid (Vickrey) | Sealed bids | Highest bid | Second-highest bid | Dominant: bid true value |

Dutch and first-price auctions are strategically equivalent: both reduce to choosing the price at which to stop. English and second-price auctions share the property that the winner pays (approximately) the second-highest valuation, though this equivalence is weaker.

## 3 Methodology

Our methodology is the classical one of Bayesian mechanism design: characterize the set of incentive-compatible mechanisms, express the designer's objective as a function of the allocation rule alone, and optimize pointwise. The central objects are:

1. **Interim allocation:** *xᵢ*(vᵢ) = E[xᵢ(vᵢ, **V**₋ᵢ)], the probability *i* wins given report *vᵢ*.
2. **Interim payment:** *pᵢ*(vᵢ) = E[pᵢ(vᵢ, **V**₋ᵢ)], the expected payment given report *vᵢ*.
3. **Interim utility:** *Uᵢ*(vᵢ) = vᵢ xᵢ(vᵢ) − pᵢ(vᵢ).

Myerson's key insight [2] is that IC imposes rigid differential structure: if *Uᵢ*(v̂ᵢ; vᵢ) = vᵢxᵢ(v̂ᵢ) − pᵢ(v̂ᵢ) is the utility of reporting *v̂ᵢ* with true value *vᵢ*, truthfulness means *vᵢ* maximizes this expression, and the envelope condition gives *Uᵢ′*(vᵢ) = *xᵢ*(vᵢ). Integrating yields Myerson's lemma:

> **Theorem: Myerson's Lemma (Payment Identity).** In any incentive-compatible mechanism with interim allocation rule *xᵢ*(·), the interim payment rule is pinned down up to the constant *Uᵢ*(v̲ᵢ):
>
> *pᵢ*(vᵢ) = vᵢ xᵢ(vᵢ) − ∫ᵥ̲ᵢᵛⁱ xᵢ(s) ds − *Uᵢ*(v̲ᵢ),
>
> and *xᵢ*(·) must be nondecreasing. Conversely, any nondecreasing *xᵢ* with this payment rule is incentive compatible.

**Expected revenue depends only on the allocation rule** (plus bottom rents):

E[∑ᵢ pᵢ(**V**)] = E[∑ᵢ φᵢ(Vᵢ) xᵢ(**V**)] − ∑ᵢ Uᵢ(v̲ᵢ),

where φᵢ(v) = v − (1 − Fᵢ(v))/fᵢ(v) is the **virtual valuation**. Hence any two mechanisms with the *same* allocation rule and zero bottom rents yield the *same* expected revenue — the **revenue equivalence theorem** [1][2][4]. The four standard auctions are all efficient with zero bottom rents, so their revenues coincide: the designer maximizes *virtual surplus*, not welfare.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Vickrey–Clarke–Groves Mechanism

The Vickrey auction generalizes beyond single-item settings. Consider an arbitrary set of feasible outcomes *X* and quasilinear agents with value functions *vₙ*(*x*). The **VCG mechanism** [1][3]:

1. Chooses the welfare-maximizing outcome *x\** = argmaxₓ∈*X* ∑ₙ vₙ(x).
2. Charges each agent *n* her **pivot payment**: the externality she imposes on the rest of society,
   *pₙ* = maxₓ∈*X* ∑ₘ≠ₙ vₘ(x) − ∑ₘ≠ₙ vₘ(x\*).

That is, agent *n* pays the difference between what others *could* have obtained without her and what they actually obtain with her present.

> **Theorem: VCG Properties.** The VCG mechanism is (a) *dominant-strategy incentive compatible* — truthful reporting is optimal regardless of others' reports; (b) *efficient* — it always selects the welfare-maximizing outcome; and (c) *individually rational*. Moreover, among all efficient, dominant-strategy mechanisms with no payments to losers, VCG is essentially unique [8].

Truthfulness follows because agent *n*'s payoff is ∑ₘ vₘ(x\*) minus a term independent of her report, so truthful reporting aligns the mechanism's maximization with her own.

Yet VCG's practical record is dismal, famously summarized by Ausubel and Milgrom as "lovely but lonely" [8]. Its vulnerabilities include:

- **Collusion:** losing bidders can costlessly inflate bids; coalitions can coordinate to suppress payments, since a bidder's payment depends only on others' reports.
- **Shill bidding:** a bidder can submit fake identities to fragment her own pivotal payment.
- **Revenue non-monotonicity:** adding bidders or raising bids can *decrease* revenue — the mechanism lacks the basic sanity properties of real auctions.
- **Privacy and complexity:** full value revelation is required, and computing the allocation and payments can be intractable (NP-hard in combinatorial settings).

No major real-world auction — spectrum, treasury, advertising — uses pure VCG, despite its theoretical centrality as the efficiency benchmark.

### 4.2 Myerson's Lemma and Revenue Equivalence

We now state the two crown-jewel corollaries of the payment identity more formally. Suppose distributions *Fᵢ* have strictly positive densities and mechanisms satisfy IR with *Uᵢ*(v̲ᵢ) = 0.

> **Theorem: Revenue Equivalence.** Any two mechanisms that (a) induce the same interim allocation probabilities *xᵢ*(vᵢ) for every bidder and type, and (b) give zero expected utility to the lowest type, yield identical expected payments from every type and identical expected revenue. In particular, the four standard auctions (English, Dutch, first-price, second-price) with no reserve are revenue equivalent under symmetric IPV with risk-neutral bidders [1][4].

Revenue is thus not a property of the auction *format* at all — it is a property of *who wins*. The seller's problem reduces entirely to choosing an allocation rule; charges are then forced [2].

The result's boundaries are as instructive as its content. Revenue equivalence fails when bidders are **risk-averse** (first-price then dominates, since shading provides insurance), when values are **asymmetric** (formats rank differently), when values are **affiliated/common** (the linkage principle of Milgrom and Weber favors open formats that reveal information), and when **entry** is endogenous. Each failure mode has become its own literature.

The following Python snippet verifies revenue equivalence numerically for the uniform case, where the symmetric first-price equilibrium bid is *b(v)* = (*n*−1)/*n* · *v* and both formats yield expected revenue (*n*−1)/(*n*+1):

```python
import numpy as np

def expected_revenue(n, trials=2_000_000, seed=7):
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    v = rng.random((trials, n))
    second_price = np.sort(v, axis=1)[:, -2]          # winner pays 2nd-highest value
    first_price  = (n - 1) / n * np.max(v, axis=1)    # symmetric BNE shading
    return second_price.mean(), first_price.mean()

for n in (2, 5, 10):
    sp, fp = expected_revenue(n)
    print(f"n={n}: second-price={sp:.4f} first-price={fp:.4f} theory={(n-1)/(n+1):.4f}")
# n=2: 0.3333 0.3333 theory=0.3333
# n=5: 0.6667 0.6667 theory=0.6667
# n=10: 0.8182 0.8182 theory=0.8182
```

### 4.3 Optimal Auctions, Virtual Values, and Ironing

Revenue equivalence tells the seller that only the allocation rule matters; Myerson's virtual-surplus formula tells her *which* allocation rule to choose. Expected revenue equals expected virtual surplus E[∑ᵢ φᵢ(Vᵢ) xᵢ(**V**)] (ignoring bottom rents), so the revenue-maximizing mechanism simply allocates to maximize virtual surplus pointwise:

> **Theorem: Myerson's Optimal Auction.** The revenue-maximizing IC, IR mechanism allocates the good to the bidder with the highest *nonnegative* virtual valuation φᵢ(vᵢ) = vᵢ − (1−Fᵢ(vᵢ))/fᵢ(vᵢ), breaking ties arbitrarily; if all virtual values are negative, the good is withheld. Payments follow Myerson's lemma.

When bidders are symmetric and the distribution is **regular** — φ(·) nondecreasing — the highest virtual value belongs to the highest value, so the optimal auction is just a standard auction with a **reserve price** *r\** solving φ(r\*) = 0 (equivalently, the monopoly price against *F*). Bulow and Roberts (1989) gave this its most memorable interpretation: φ(v) is exactly the *marginal revenue* of selling to a buyer with value *v*, so Myerson's auction is a monopolist equating marginal revenue across "markets" — the bidders [5]. The reserve price *r\** is where marginal revenue crosses zero.

When distributions are **irregular** (φ decreasing somewhere), pointwise maximization violates the monotonicity IC requires. Myerson's fix is **ironing**: replace φ by its ironed version φ̄, the smallest nondecreasing majorant, pooling ("bunching") types over binding intervals — analogous to bunching in nonlinear pricing. Pooled types are treated identically, restoring monotonicity at the cost of randomizing among them; φ̄ is the concave envelope of the integrated virtual-value function.

The optimal auction openly discriminates among ex-ante asymmetric bidders — handicapping strong bidders to extract more from weak ones — and its reserve price *r\** typically sits far above the seller's own value. Both features are politically fraught, which is why real auctions rarely implement Myerson's optimum directly, and why the Bulow–Klemperer result below matters.

### 4.4 Sponsored Search: GSP versus VCG

The largest real-world laboratory for auction theory is sponsored search: Google, and later its competitors, selling keyword advertising in real time, billions of auctions per day. The industry converged on the **Generalized Second-Price (GSP)** auction: advertisers are ranked by bid (times a quality score), the *k*-th ranked advertiser receives the *k*-th slot, and pays the (*k*+1)-st ranked bid per click. GSP *looks* like VCG — it even reduces to the Vickrey auction with one slot — but as Edelman, Ostrovsky, and Schwarz (2007) showed, its properties are profoundly different [7]:

- GSP has **no dominant strategy**; truth-telling is not an equilibrium.
- GSP has multiple Nash equilibria, including inefficient ones with low revenue.
- Yet the *locally envy-free* equilibria of GSP — the refinement in which no bidder wants to swap positions with the bidder above her — yield **exactly the VCG payoffs**, and this equilibrium is the *worst* locally envy-free equilibrium for the search engine and the best for advertisers [7].

Varian (2007) independently derived the same equilibrium structure, implementable without knowledge of click-through rates by regrouping the VCG payment terms per click [7]. The industry chose GSP — simpler to explain, less informationally demanding, and (in the focal equilibrium) payoff-equivalent to VCG anyway. Later Myersonian analysis showed optimal reserve prices could raise search-engine revenue by double-digit percentages with only small efficiency losses.

## 5 Empirical Results / Proofs

### 5.1 Proof Sketches of the Central Results

**Myerson's lemma.** Fix bidder *i* and define *Uᵢ*(v̂ᵢ; vᵢ) as above. IC requires *vᵢ* ∈ argmax_{v̂ᵢ} *Uᵢ*(v̂ᵢ; vᵢ). Under smoothness, the envelope theorem gives d/dvᵢ *Uᵢ*(vᵢ) = ∂/∂vᵢ *Uᵢ*(v̂ᵢ; vᵢ)|_{v̂ᵢ=vᵢ} = *xᵢ*(vᵢ). Integrating from v̲ᵢ and substituting *Uᵢ*(vᵢ) = vᵢxᵢ(vᵢ) − *pᵢ*(vᵢ) yields the payment identity. Monotonicity of *xᵢ* follows from the second-order condition (the maximizer must be increasing in the parameter). ∎

**Revenue equivalence.** Taking expectations of the payment identity over *Vᵢ* and summing over *i* expresses expected revenue purely through the allocation rules *xᵢ*(·) and the constants *Uᵢ*(v̲ᵢ). Two mechanisms sharing these have identical revenue. ∎

**Optimality of virtual-surplus maximization.** Integration by parts on the payment identity gives E[*pᵢ*(Vᵢ)] = E[φᵢ(Vᵢ)xᵢ(**V**)] − *Uᵢ*(v̲ᵢ). Since *Uᵢ*(v̲ᵢ) ≥ 0 by IR and the designer minimizes it to zero, maximizing revenue is exactly maximizing E[∑ᵢ φᵢ(Vᵢ)xᵢ(**V**)] subject to monotone *xᵢ* and feasibility — a pointwise problem when regularity holds. ∎

### 5.2 The Bulow–Klemperer Competition Theorem

Perhaps the most policy-relevant theorem in the subject needs no mechanism design at all:

> **Theorem (Bulow–Klemperer 1996).** Under standard IPV assumptions, a plain ascending (English) auction with *no reserve price* and *n*+1 bidders raises *more* expected revenue than the *optimal* auction — with its finely tuned reserve price and discrimination — run with *n* bidders [6].

The proof is a one-line application of revenue logic: the optimal *n*-bidder auction earns at most expected *virtual surplus*, bounded by the winner's expected *value*; the (*n*+1)-bidder English auction earns the expected *second-highest value*, and a coupling argument shows the latter dominates. *One extra bidder is worth more than all the designer's cleverness* — so practitioners should invest in **market thickness** (entry) rather than elaborate engineering, a lesson applied in M&A sales and platform design alike [6].

### 5.3 Empirical Record

Field and laboratory evidence broadly confirms that (i) second-price auctions elicit near-truthful bidding while first-price auctions show predicted shading; (ii) revenue equivalence holds approximately in symmetric laboratory IPV settings but breaks in the predicted directions with risk aversion and affiliation; (iii) reserve prices in online advertising raise revenue substantially, consistent with Myersonian optimization; and (iv) VCG's predicted vulnerabilities — particularly collusive bid suppression — have materialized in the laboratory [8]. The FCC spectrum auctions (1994 onward), designed by Milgrom, Wilson, and McAfee with simultaneous ascending formats, generated tens of billions of dollars: the flagship applied success of the theory.

## 6 Limitations

The classical edifice rests on assumptions that real markets routinely violate, and the violations are not technicalities:

1. **Independent private values.** High-stakes auctions (oil leases, spectrum, IPOs) often have *common* or *affiliated* values: winning conveys bad news — the **winner's curse**. Rational bidding must condition on the event of winning, revenue equivalence fails, and the linkage principle favors open formats that release information publicly.
2. **Risk neutrality.** With risk-averse bidders, first-price auctions raise more revenue than second-price ones because shading provides partial insurance.
3. **Exogenous entry.** The theory takes the bidder set as given, but auction format shapes entry: formats that are "fairer" ex post can attract more entrants ex ante, and the Bulow–Klemperer logic suggests entry effects dwarf format effects [6].
4. **The Myerson–Satterthwaite impossibility.** In bilateral trade with two-sided private information, no mechanism can simultaneously be efficient, incentive compatible, individually rational, and budget balanced [9]. This is the fundamental boundary: it proves that the VCG program — efficiency plus dominant strategies — cannot extend to settings where both sides hold private information and the designer cannot subsidize.
5. **Collusion and false-name bidding.** VCG and second-price formats are fragile to coalitions and identity manipulation; first-price and ascending formats are more robust but sacrifice dominant-strategy simplicity [8].
6. **Computational and informational demands.** The optimal auction requires the designer to *know* the distributions *Fᵢ* — a heroic assumption. The modern response is *prior-independent* mechanism design (the Bulow–Klemperer auction itself is a prior-free approximation) and the sample-complexity literature on learning near-optimal auctions from data.
7. **Multi-dimensional and combinatorial settings.** With multiple heterogeneous goods, optimal mechanisms are unknown in general; VCG stays efficient and truthful but computationally intractable and revenue-poor, while practical combinatorial auctions use clock and proxy formats justified more by experiment than theorem.

## 7 Conclusion

From Vickrey's insight to Myerson's complete characterization, auction theory is one of economics' rare closed-form triumphs: a full description of what mechanisms can achieve, a proof that revenue depends only on allocation, and an explicit optimal-mechanism formula. VCG stands as the *efficiency* ideal — dominant strategies, welfare maximization, pivot payments equal to externality — and as a cautionary tale, "lovely but lonely" in practice [8]. Virtual valuations recast the seller as a monopolist pricing information rents, and Bulow and Roberts revealed the apparatus as marginal-revenue economics [2][5]. In sponsored search, the theory explains both why the industry rejected VCG and why its chosen GSP reproduces VCG payoffs in the focal equilibrium [7]. And Bulow and Klemperer remind us that competition substitutes for cleverness: one more bidder beats perfect mechanism design [6].

The frontier has moved from optimal mechanisms under known priors to *learning* them from data, prior-free guarantees, and dynamic and combinatorial settings where the classical theorems are only guideposts. But the core lesson endures: where participants hold private information, the rules are not neutral plumbing — they are the market.
