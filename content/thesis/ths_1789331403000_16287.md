# Moiré Heterostructures: Magic-Angle Twisted Bilayer Graphene, Flat Bands, and Correlated Insulators

## Abstract

Twisted bilayer graphene near the magic angle of approximately 1.1 degrees hosts electronic bands so flat that the kinetic energy of charge carriers is suppressed to a few millielectronvolts, while Coulomb repulsion remains of order tens of millielectronvolts. In this strongly correlated regime, a carbon monolayer system realizes physics previously exclusive to transition-metal oxides: correlated insulators at integer moiré fillings, superconducting domes adjacent to those insulators, and interaction-driven topological phases with quantized anomalous Hall conductance. This thesis develops the moiré heterostructure concept from the moiré interference geometry through the Bistritzer-MacDonald continuum model to the many-body frontier encompassing the moiré Hubbard paradigm, orbital magnetism, and extensions to twisted trilayers and transition-metal dichalcogenides. We review the theoretical machinery, the experimental evidence established by Cao and collaborators in 2018 and refined in the years since, the device fabrication that makes twist control possible, and the open problems that keep this field at the center of condensed-matter physics.

## 1 Introduction

Few episodes in modern condensed matter physics have compressed as much novelty into as small a parameter as the magic angle of twisted bilayer graphene. A relative rotation of barely one degree between two sheets of carbon, each one atom thick, transforms a weakly interacting semimetal into a strongly correlated quantum material whose phase diagram mirrors the copper oxides: insulating states at integer band fillings, superconducting domes upon doping, and a normal state with anomalous, non-Fermi-liquid transport [2][7]. The governing knob is geometry rather than chemistry. Two copies of the graphene honeycomb lattice, overlaid with a relative twist θ, generate a moiré interference pattern whose period,

λ = a / (2 sin(θ/2)),

grows without bound as θ → 0 (with a = 2.46 Å the graphene lattice constant [3]). This long-wavelength modulation folds the Dirac cones of the two layers into a tiny moiré Brillouin zone, hybridizes them through interlayer tunneling, and — at a discrete sequence of *magic angles* — collapses the Fermi velocity of the lowest moiré bands to zero [1]. Correlations, topology, and tunability then combine into what has become the flagship platform of *twistronics*: the engineering of correlated and topological quantum phases through the twist degree of freedom.

This thesis proceeds as follows. Section 2 reconstructs the theoretical and material background: graphene's Dirac spectrum, moiré geometry, and the single-particle theory of twisted bilayers. Section 3 documents the methodology — the continuum and tight-binding models, the ab initio corrections from lattice relaxation, and the experimental techniques (tear-and-stack fabrication, scanning probes, transport, and optics) that made the field possible. Section 4 deep-dives into the core phenomena: the magic-angle sequence, flat-band physics and the energy-scale hierarchy, the moiré Hubbard paradigm, superconducting domes, interaction-driven topology, and the generalization beyond the bilayer. Section 5 collects the empirical record and the strongest theoretical proofs of mechanism. Section 6 confronts the limitations — disorder, twist inhomogeneity, lattice reconstruction, and the still-mysterious pairing mechanism — before Section 7 concludes with the outlook for moiré quantum matter.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Graphene and the Dirac spectrum

Monolayer graphene is a semimetal whose low-energy electrons are massless Dirac fermions: near the two inequivalent corners **K** and **K′** of the hexagonal Brillouin zone, the band structure is a pair of cones with linear dispersion E = ±ℏv_F |**k**|, where the Fermi velocity v_F ≈ 10^6 m/s plays the role of an effective speed of light [5]. The density of states vanishes at the Dirac point, interactions are marginal, and the material is a clean, weakly correlated two-dimensional electron gas with record mobility. Nothing in the chemistry of carbon hints at strong correlations — the surprise of the moiré program is that *geometry alone* can drive the kinetic energy below the interaction scale.

### 2.2 The moiré pattern and its electronic consequences

When two periodic lattices are overlaid with a small relative rotation or lattice mismatch, their beating produces a moiré pattern with period much larger than either atomic spacing. For twisted bilayer graphene (TBG), the moiré wavelength λ = a/(2 sin(θ/2)) ranges from a few nanometers to tens of nanometers for twist angles of a few degrees [1]. Within one moiré unit cell the local stacking interpolates continuously between AA regions (atoms directly atop one another), AB and BA regions (Bernal stacking of opposite polarity), and the saddle-point configurations bridging them. The electronic structure inherits this superlattice: momentum states differing by moiré reciprocal lattice vectors hybridize, the original Dirac cones are folded into a mini Brillouin zone, and a ladder of moiré minibands emerges. Because the moiré period is two orders of magnitude larger than the atomic spacing, the carrier density corresponding to *full filling* of the flat bands is of order 10^12 cm^-2 — small enough to be reached with electrostatic gating, yet large enough per moiré cell (four electrons per spin-valley flavor, eight per cell including degeneracy) to define a clean integer-filling lattice problem [2].

### 2.3 From prediction to experiment

The theoretical foundation was laid by Bistritzer and MacDonald in 2011: their continuum Dirac model predicted that the renormalized Fermi velocity of twisted double-layer graphene crosses zero at a discrete set of magic angles, the first near 1.05°, where the lowest moiré band flattens and the density of states at the Dirac point is strongly enhanced [1].

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 The Bistritzer–MacDonald continuum model

The workhorse of moiré theory is the continuum Hamiltonian introduced by Bistritzer and MacDonald [1]. Two Dirac Hamiltonians, rotated by ±θ/2, are coupled by a spatially periodic interlayer tunneling with three dominant Fourier components corresponding to the three moiré wavevectors:

H = ⎡ h(θ/2)    T(**r**)  ⎤
    ⎣ T†(**r**)  h(−θ/2) ⎦

where h(φ) = −iℏv_F (∇ × σ)·ẑ is the Dirac operator rotated by φ and the tunneling matrix T(**r**) = Σ_j T_j exp(i **q**_j · **r**) encodes the moiré periodicity. Diagonalizing this Hamiltonian in a plane-wave basis yields the moiré minibands; the renormalized velocity v*(θ) oscillates and vanishes at the magic-angle sequence, the first at θ ≈ 1.05° [1][6]. Subsequent work has placed this construction on rigorous footing, deriving the continuum model from the underlying tight-binding Hamiltonian with controlled error estimates [14].

```python
import numpy as np

def moire_wavelength(a: float, theta_deg: float) -> float:
    """Moiré period in the same units as lattice constant a."""
    theta = np.deg2rad(theta_deg)
    return a / (2.0 * np.sin(theta / 2.0))

def superlattice_density_nm2(theta_deg: float) -> float:
    """Carrier density per moiré cell at full filling, per nm^2."""
    lam = moire_wavelength(0.246, theta_deg)          # graphene a = 0.246 nm
    area = np.sqrt(3.0) / 2.0 * lam ** 2             # moiré cell area
    return 4.0 / area                                # 4 electrons per flavor set
```

### 3.2 Lattice relaxation and ab initio corrections

Real moiré lattices relax: AA-stacked regions are energetically costly, so the atoms reconstruct to shrink AA domains and expand the lower-energy AB/BA regions, separated by a triangular network of domain walls [5]. This relaxation renormalizes the interlayer tunneling amplitudes — suppressing the AA tunneling t_AA relative to the AB tunneling t_AB (typical fitted values t_AA ≈ 79.7 meV, t_AB ≈ 97.5 meV [4]) — and shifts the magic angle, narrows or widens the flat bands, and can suppress higher magic angles entirely [5][7].

### 3.3 Experimental probes

*Device fabrication.* The tear-and-stack technique plus hexagonal boron nitride (hBN) encapsulation produces dual-gated devices in which carrier density *n* and perpendicular displacement field *D* are independently controlled; twist-angle homogeneity is characterized by transport and scanning probes [2][4].

*Transport.* Longitudinal and Hall resistivity as functions of *n*, *D*, temperature, and magnetic field reveal the correlated insulators (resistance peaks at integer fillings ν), the superconducting domes (zero resistance, critical currents, Fraunhofer patterns), quantum oscillations mapping small Fermi surfaces, and anomalous linear-in-temperature resistivity suggestive of quantum criticality [2][7].

*Scanning probes.* STM/STS directly images the moiré lattice and the flat-band local density of states; scanning single-electron transistors map the chemical potential and compressibility across the phase diagram [11].

*Optics and symmetry probes.* Optical conductivity tracks the symmetry-breaking reconstruction of the flat bands [3], while anomalous Hall measurements detect orbital ferromagnetism and the quantized anomalous Hall effect at odd-integer fillings in hBN-aligned devices [9][10].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The magic-angle sequence and the vanishing of the Fermi velocity

The central analytical result of Bistritzer and MacDonald is that the Dirac velocity renormalizes as a function of twist angle and crosses zero at a discrete, infinite sequence of magic angles, θ_1 ≈ 1.05°, θ_2 ≈ 0.5°, θ_3 ≈ 0.35°, and so on [1][6]. At each magic angle the two lowest moiré bands become nearly dispersionless, with bandwidths of order 5–15 meV, and the density of states develops a sharp peak at charge neutrality. Near but not exactly at the first magic angle the bands remain extremely narrow over a finite angular window, and the band topology evolves through a series of transitions mediated by the creation and annihilation of Dirac nodes at the Γ, **K**, and **K′** points of the moiré Brillouin zone [6]. Lattice relaxation modifies this picture quantitatively — fitted models place the effective first magic angle near 1.1° and suppress the second magic angle [7] — and devices twisted measurably below the magic angle (e.g. 0.93°) can still host correlated insulators and superconductivity, because the relevant figure of merit is the ratio of interaction to bandwidth rather than the precise angle [4].

### 4.2 Flat bands and the hierarchy of energy scales

The flatness of the magic-angle bands inverts the usual solid-state hierarchy. The bandwidth W is only a few millielectronvolts while the on-site Coulomb energy U = e²/(4πελ) for a moiré period λ ≈ 13 nm is of order 20–30 meV, giving U/W ≫ 1: the system is a *strongly correlated* electron problem built from carbon π orbitals [2][3].

> **Theorem (strong-coupling criterion):** When the moiré bandwidth W is suppressed below the Coulomb scale U by twist tuning, the low-energy physics is controlled by interactions, and mean-field band theory fails qualitatively. *Corollary:* any quantitative theory of MATBG must treat the flat-band manifold as an interacting many-body problem, not a renormalized single-particle one [3][9].

### 4.3 The moiré Hubbard paradigm and correlated insulators

Projecting the Coulomb interaction onto the isolated flat bands yields an effective lattice model on the triangular moiré superlattice with spin and valley degrees of freedom — a multi-flavor Hubbard model [9]. At integer fillings ν (electrons per moiré cell, with ν = ±4 at full filling), experiments find insulating states at nearly every integer: the celebrated correlated insulators at ν = ±2 and ±3 in the original devices [2], later extended to ν = ±1 and charge neutrality in cleaner samples. Hartree–Fock studies show that flavor-polarized states — spin-polarized, valley-polarized, or both — are energetically favored at all integer fillings, with the insulating gaps set by the exchange splitting rather than the bare Hubbard U [9]. Pressure tuning provides a decisive test of the correlation picture: hydrostatic pressure increases interlayer coupling, renormalizes the effective magic angle, and drives a metal–insulator transition consistent with a correlation-driven (Wigner-like) rather than single-particle gap [4].

|---|---|---|
| 0 (charge neutrality) | Dirac semimetal / gapped insulator | Sample-dependent; can be valley-polarized insulator [9] |
| ±1 | Correlated insulator; QAH at ν = 3 (hBN-aligned) | Spin-valley polarized; Chern C = ∓1 [9][10] |
| ±2 | Correlated insulator (half-filling) | Mott-like; parent state of SC domes [2] |
| ±3 | Correlated insulator | Flavor-polarized [2][9] |
| ν ≈ ±2 ± δ | Superconducting domes | Unconventional; Tc up to ~1.7 K [2] |

### 4.4 Superconducting domes and the pairing question

Doping the half-filling correlated insulator produces dome-shaped superconducting regions on both electron and hole sides, with critical temperatures up to 1.7 K in the original devices [2] — and up to 2.1 K in magic-angle twisted *trilayer* graphene at θ ≈ 1.56° [11]. The phenomenology carries the fingerprints of unconventional pairing: the ratio Tc/T_F places MATBG among the strongest-coupled superconductors known, alongside cuprates and iron pnictides [7]; quantum oscillations reveal small Fermi surfaces adjacent to the insulators, echoing underdoped cuprates [2]; and the normal state exhibits giant linear-in-temperature resistivity that has been attributed — controversially — to ordinary acoustic-phonon scattering amplified by the flat-band density of states [7]. Candidate pairing symmetries proliferate: phonon-mediated s-wave, spin-fluctuation d+id, intervalley pairing, and skyrmion-mediated mechanisms have all been proposed, with the d+id channel favored by renormalization-group analyses [7].

### 4.5 Topology: Chern bands, orbital magnetism, and the quantum anomalous Hall effect

The flat bands of MATBG are not merely narrow — they are topological. Each valley's flat bands carry nonzero Chern numbers (±1), with time-reversal symmetry relating the valleys so the total Chern number cancels [9]. Interactions can spontaneously break this balance: when hBN alignment imposes a sublattice potential, or when correlations themselves break C_2z symmetry, a single valley-spin flavor can be selectively occupied, producing a Chern insulator with quantized Hall conductance at zero magnetic field. Serlin and collaborators observed this: an intrinsic quantum anomalous Hall effect with C = 1 at ν = 3 in MATBG aligned to hBN [10]. Whether the QAH effect appears in a given device depends sensitively on the commensurability of the graphene–graphene and graphene–hBN moiré patterns: only near commensurate twist pairs does a percolating topologically nontrivial phase survive the disorder introduced by the substrate [10]. Most recently, generalized anomalous Hall *crystals* — states that spontaneously enlarge the moiré unit cell while carrying Chern number — have been observed in twisted bilayer–trilayer graphene, raising the prospect of fractionalized descendants of these topological electron crystals [12].

### 4.6 Beyond the bilayer: trilayers, double bilayers, and dichalcogenides

The moiré program has expanded far beyond its bilayer origin. *Alternating-twist trilayer graphene* (top and bottom layers twisted by ±θ relative to the middle) hosts coexisting flat bands and Dirac cones at a magic angle of θ ≈ 1.56°, with electric-field-tunable superconductivity reaching Tc = 2.1 K [11]. Incommensurate trilayer pairs form moiré *quasicrystals* that nevertheless support robust flat bands, showing that strict periodicity is not required for correlation physics [11]. *Transition-metal dichalcogenides* offer an even cleaner Hubbard-model realization: in twisted WSe_2, correlated insulators appear over a continuous range of twist angles (4°–5.1°) rather than at isolated magic angles, a Mott-like insulator at half-filling is tuned by displacement field, and superconductivity emerges upon doping with zero resistance near 3 K [8]; very recently, superconductivity was reported in twisted bilayer WSe_2 at 5.0° [13].

## 5 Empirical Evidence and Proofs

The empirical case for moiré-driven strong correlations rests on converging lines of evidence, each independently reproduced:

1. **Correlated insulators at integer fillings.** Resistance peaks at ν = ±2 and ±3 in the original MATBG devices [2], extended to all integers including charge neutrality in subsequent work, with gaps that grow under in-plane magnetic field (consistent with spin polarization) and respond to pressure as expected for interaction-driven states [4][9].
2. **Gate-tunable superconducting domes.** Zero-resistance states with Tc up to 1.7 K flanking the half-filling insulator, tunable critical currents, and a Tc/T_F ratio in the strong-coupling regime [2][7].
3. **Quantum oscillations and Fermi-surface reconstruction.** Small Fermi pockets near the correlated insulators, with degeneracy changing across integer fillings — direct evidence that interactions reconstruct the Fermi surface [2].
4. **Flat-band spectroscopy.** STM/STS maps showing the moiré lattice, AA-localized flat-band peaks, and their evolution with twist angle, matching relaxed continuum-model calculations [3][5][11].
5. **Quantized anomalous Hall effect.** C = 1 quantization at ν = 3 in hBN-aligned MATBG, proving the flat bands carry Chern number and that correlations can produce orbital ferromagnetism [10].
7. **Generalization.** Superconductivity in MATTG with Tc = 2.1 K and Pauli-limit violation [11]; correlated insulators and superconductivity across a continuum of angles in twisted WSe_2 [8]; superconductivity at 5.0° in twisted bilayer WSe_2 [13].

The theoretical proof of *mechanism* is equally developed. The Bistritzer–MacDonald derivation has been placed on rigorous mathematical footing with explicit error bounds [14]; Hartree–Fock and exact-diagonalization studies of the projected Coulomb Hamiltonian reproduce the flavor-polarized insulators and the Chern states [9]; and the Hubbard-model mapping in TMD moirés is quantitative enough to extract U/t from experiment [8]. What remains unproven is the pairing glue of the superconducting domes — the single most important open question in the field.

## 6 Limitations

*Twist-angle disorder.* The magic-angle condition is sharp: variations of 0.1° across a device change the local bandwidth by factors of order unity, producing a patchwork of metallic, insulating, and superconducting regions. Most "phase diagrams" are therefore device-averaged, and quantitative comparison with theory requires local probes [2][4].

*Lattice relaxation and strain.* Relaxation reshapes the bands, suppresses higher magic angles, and introduces heterostrain that breaks the moiré C_3 symmetry; disentangling intrinsic correlation physics from strain-induced band-structure effects remains difficult [5][7].

*The pairing mechanism.* Despite the cuprate-like phase diagram, no consensus exists on what pairs the electrons. Phonon, spin-fluctuation, and topological mechanisms all remain viable, and the trilayer's Pauli-limit violation deepens rather than resolves the mystery [7][11].

*Energy and temperature scales.* Correlated gaps of a few meV and Tc of a few kelvin restrict experiments to dilution-fridge temperatures and make the phases fragile against disorder [2][8].

*Model uncertainties.* The projected interacting models depend on relaxation parameters and screening assumptions; small changes in t_AA/t_AB shift phase boundaries substantially [5][9][14].

---

## 7 Conclusion

Moiré heterostructures have realized, in a tunable carbon platform, the central drama of strongly correlated electron physics: flat bands that quench kinetic energy, a Hubbard-like lattice emerging from geometry, correlated insulators at integer fillings, superconducting domes upon doping, and interaction-driven topology culminating in the quantum anomalous Hall effect [1][2][9][10]. The field has progressed from a 2011 band-structure prediction [1] through the 2018 experimental breakthrough [2] to a mature quantum-simulation program spanning trilayers, double bilayers, and dichalcogenides [8][11][13]. The deepest questions endure: the pairing mechanism of the superconducting domes, the full taxonomy of the topological phases, and whether moiré engineering can push correlated superconductivity toward technologically relevant temperatures. Whatever the answers, the lesson is secure — in two dimensions, a single degree of twist is enough to rewrite the electronic ground state.
