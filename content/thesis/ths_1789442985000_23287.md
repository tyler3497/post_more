# From Counts to Dynamics: Computational Methods for Single-Cell Transcriptomics — RNA Velocity, Pseudotime Trajectories, Clustering, and Spatial Omics

## Abstract

Single-cell RNA sequencing destroys each cell at the moment of measurement, so every dataset is a static snapshot of a dynamical process. This thesis develops the computational path from raw count matrices to predictive dynamical models: negative-binomial count models and graph-based cell-type clustering, RNA velocity estimation from spliced and unspliced mRNA kinetics, pseudotime and trajectory inference on transcriptomic manifolds, and the integration of spatially resolved omics. We derive the steady-state and dynamical velocity models, analyze expectation-maximization estimation of latent time and kinetic rates, and show how velocity fields are converted into Markov transition operators for fate mapping. We compare trajectory inference frameworks — diffusion pseudotime, minimum spanning trees, and optimal transport — and examine spatial deconvolution methods that localize dynamic states in tissue. Benchmarks on developmental atlases, perturbation screens, and cortex datasets illustrate where current methods succeed and where misspecification of kinetic assumptions distorts inferred direction. We conclude with open problems: transcriptional bursting, multi-rate kinetics, and the unification of velocity with spatial and multimodal measurements.

## 1 Introduction

Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) has transformed developmental biology, immunology, and oncology by resolving gene expression at the resolution of individual cells. Yet the technology carries a fundamental epistemic limitation: the measurement is *destructive*. A cell is lysed to capture its mRNA, and the resulting count vector records the transcriptomic state at one instant only. Any developmental process — embryogenesis, tissue regeneration, tumor evolution — unfolds in time, while the data arrive as unordered snapshots. The central computational question of single-cell transcriptomics is therefore a reconstruction problem: **how do we recover directed, time-resolved dynamics from static, destructive measurements?**

The earliest answer was *pseudotime* [3][4]: order cells along a learned trajectory so that neighbors in the ordering approximate neighbors in developmental time. Pseudotime methods impose an undirected manifold geometry on the data and then select a root, producing a scalar coordinate that correlates with, but does not derive from, real time. A more ambitious answer came with **RNA velocity** [1]: by distinguishing *unspliced* (nascent, intron-containing) from *spliced* (mature) mRNA reads in standard protocols, one can estimate the time derivative of each gene's expression state — a high-dimensional vector pointing from a cell's current state toward its future state on a timescale of hours. Velocity generalizes pseudotime from description to prediction, from an ordering to a vector field.

This thesis follows the full computational pipeline from raw counts to dynamical models. Our contributions are expository but rigorous: we formalize the count models and preprocessing that make velocity estimation possible, derive the steady-state and dynamical velocity estimators and their identifiability conditions, analyze trajectory inference as manifold learning with and without directional information, and extend the picture to *spatial* transcriptomics, where tissue coordinates constrain and contextualize the dynamics. Throughout, we distinguish what the mathematics guarantees from what practitioners merely hope — a distinction the literature has not always observed [11].

## 2 Background

### 2.1 From reads to count matrices

A scRNA-seq experiment produces a count matrix **C** with one row per cell (or nucleus, or spatial spot) and one column per gene. Entries are unique molecular identifier (UMI) counts in droplet protocols or read counts in full-length protocols. These counts are sparse, noisy, and heteroscedastic: typical matrices are over 90 percent zeros, and the variance of a gene's counts grows faster than its mean. The standard generative model is the negative binomial (NB), parameterized by mean μ and dispersion θ, with the zero-inflated variant (ZINB) historically used to absorb excess zeros now understood to arise largely from sampling rather than true biological zero-inflation. Normalization corrects for differences in capture efficiency across cells — via size factors, log1p transforms of counts-per-ten-thousand, or regularized NB regression — and highly variable gene (HVG) selection reduces the feature space to the genes carrying biological signal, typically two to five thousand [12].

> Theorem: *Identifiability of the NB mean.* Under the NB parameterization with known dispersion θ, the maximum likelihood estimator of the gene mean μ from n independent cell observations is consistent and asymptotically normal, with variance μ(1 + μ/θ)/n. Overdispersion (small θ) inflates this variance multiplicatively, which is why dispersion shrinkage across genes is a prerequisite for stable downstream geometry.

### 2.2 Dimensionality reduction and clustering

After normalization and HVG selection, principal component analysis compresses the signal into tens of components, and a k-nearest-neighbor (kNN) graph is built in PC space. Nearly all modern analysis operates on this graph: clustering via community detection, trajectory inference via graph distances, and velocity projection via transition probabilities. Nonlinear embeddings such as UMAP [6] render the graph in two dimensions for visualization, though the embedding is a view, not the analysis — quantitative work should always run on the graph or the PCs, never on the UMAP coordinates.

Cell-type clustering partitions the kNN graph into communities of transcriptionally similar cells. The **Leiden algorithm** [13] improved on Louvain by guaranteeing that communities are well-connected, and it remains the default in the Scanpy ecosystem [12], with Seurat's shared-nearest-neighbor modularity optimization playing the analogous role elsewhere [5]. Key practical points:

- Resolution is a free parameter; there is no statistically privileged number of clusters, and marker-gene validation is mandatory.
- Doublets (two cells in one droplet) and ambient RNA contamination create artifactual intermediate clusters that can masquerade as transitional states — a confounder that directly corrupts velocity and trajectory analyses downstream.
- Batch integration [5] aligns datasets across donors, protocols, and time points by finding mutual nearest neighbors or learning batch-corrected embeddings; over-correction can erase the very biological gradients velocity aims to exploit.

### 2.3 The splicing kinetics that make velocity possible

Standard 3-prime droplet protocols capture polyadenylated RNA, which includes both mature spliced transcripts and nascent unspliced pre-mRNA still containing introns. By aligning reads to intronic versus exonic regions, one obtains, for each gene in each cell, a pair (u, s): unspliced and spliced counts. The unspliced fraction is small — typically 10 to 25 percent of reads — but it carries *regulatory* information: a gene whose unspliced counts are high relative to its spliced counts is being actively upregulated, because transcription (which produces unspliced RNA) leads splicing (which converts it) with a temporal delay. This lead-lag relationship is the physical basis of RNA velocity [1].

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 The velocity ordinary differential equations

For a single gene, let u(t) and s(t) denote unspliced and spliced mRNA abundance. The standard kinetic model is the linear system

```haskell
-- Transcriptional kinetics as a coupled linear ODE system
-- du/dt = alpha - beta * u      (transcription minus splicing)
-- ds/dt = beta * u - gamma * s   (splicing minus degradation)
data Kinetics = Kinetics
  { alpha :: Double   -- transcription rate
  , beta  :: Double   -- splicing rate
  , gamma :: Double   -- degradation rate
  }
```

with α the transcription rate, β the splicing rate, and γ the degradation rate. The **RNA velocity** of the gene is v = ds/dt = βu − γs: the instantaneous rate of change of mature mRNA. Given (u, s) observed in a cell and estimates of β and γ, velocity is computable — the entire difficulty lies in estimating the kinetic parameters from snapshot data.

The original *steady-state* model [1] assumes each gene's cells populate the phase portrait densely enough that extreme quantiles approximate the steady states, where du/dt = ds/dt = 0 and hence u = (γ/β)s. A linear fit through the upper and lower quantiles of the (s, u) cloud yields the steady-state slope γ/β; velocity is then the residual v = u − (γ/β)s up to scaling. This is elegant but fragile: it requires the data to actually contain cells at steady state for every gene, an assumption violated in transient developmental systems [2][11].

### 3.2 The scVelo dynamical model

scVelo [2] replaces the steady-state assumption with a full dynamical solution. Each gene is modeled as switching between transcriptional phases — induction (α = α_on) and repression (α = α_off, often near zero) — with per-gene switching times. The ODE system admits a closed-form solution in each phase, and crucially scVelo introduces a **latent time** t_i for each cell i: the position of that cell along the gene's kinetic curve. Parameters (α, β, γ, switching times) and latent times are estimated jointly by expectation-maximization: the E-step assigns each cell a latent time given current kinetics, and the M-step refits kinetics given latent times. Two flavors exist:

1. **Stochastic mode** — moment-based, using first- and second-order moments of the (u, s) distribution; fast and robust, analogous to the original formulation with improved estimation.
2. **Dynamical mode** — the full likelihood-based EM described above; resolves transient kinetics and assigns each gene a time-resolved trajectory, at the cost of stronger parametric assumptions.

Deep generative extensions such as VeloVI [8] place the same kinetic model inside a variational autoencoder, learning a low-dimensional latent space in which the dynamics are better behaved and uncertainty is quantified — a response to the chronic instability of EM on noisy, sparse unspliced counts.

### 3.3 From velocities to trajectories and fates

A velocity field over cells is not yet a trajectory. The standard downstream pipeline projects velocities onto the kNN graph: for each cell i and neighbor j, the transition probability T_ij is proportional to the cosine similarity between the velocity vector v_i and the displacement x_j − x_i (exponentiated and normalized). Powers of **T** then define random walks biased along the flow, from which one extracts:

- **Velocity streamlines** on embeddings, the familiar arrow plots;
- **Fate probabilities** via absorption analysis of the Markov chain — **CellRank** [7] formalizes this by identifying macrostates (terminal states) and computing, for each cell, the probability of ending in each macrostate;
- **Pseudotime with direction**, e.g. diffusion pseudotime [4] rooted at velocity-inferred initial states rather than user-specified roots.

Classical trajectory inference without velocity — Monocle's reversed graph embedding [3], Slingshot's principal curves through clusters [14], Palantir's diffusion-based pseudotime — instead learns an undirected backbone (a tree, a set of curves, a diffusion geometry) and orients it with a prior root. **Waddington-OT** [10] takes yet another route: given time-stamped samples, it computes optimal-transport couplings between consecutive time points, yielding ancestor-descendant distributions without any kinetic model at all. Velocity and optimal transport are complementary: velocity gives direction within a snapshot; transport gives coupling across known time points.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Phase portraits: reading induction and repression from a scatter plot

For one gene, plot every cell as a point (s, u). Under the kinetic model, a gene undergoing a single induction-then-repression cycle traces a characteristic *almond* shape: during induction, u rises before s follows, so points arc above the steady-state diagonal; during repression, u collapses first and points arc below it. The steady-state model fits a line through the diagonal band; cells above the line have positive velocity (upregulation), cells below have negative velocity (downregulation). This geometry is the entire content of velocyto [1] — a fact worth stating plainly, because it clarifies both the power and the brittleness: everything downstream depends on the phase portrait containing a legible almond, and on the extreme quantiles genuinely sitting on the diagonal rather than on a transient.


### 4.2 Latent time and the identifiability of kinetic parameters


> Theorem: *Scale non-identifiability of the kinetic model.* For the linear system du/dt = α − βu, ds/dt = βu − γs observed only through instantaneous (u, s) pairs across cells, the transformation (α, β, γ, t) ↦ (cα, cβ, cγ, t/c) for any c > 0 leaves all observable trajectories invariant. Hence kinetic rates are identifiable only up to a common positive multiplicative factor, and latent time only up to a reciprocal scaling.

## 5 Empirical Results / Proofs

### 5.1 Benchmarks on developmental systems

The canonical demonstrations remain instructive. La Manno et al. [1] validated velocity in the neural crest lineage, recovered the branching lineage tree of the developing mouse hippocampus, and quantified transcription kinetics in human embryonic brain — showing that velocity vectors on a t-SNE/UMAP embedding point from progenitors toward differentiated fates without any temporal labels. Bergen et al. [2] extended the analysis to transient systems where the steady-state assumption fails outright: pancreatic endocrinogenesis, where scVelo's dynamical model recovered the known cascade from ductal progenitors through endocrine progenitors to alpha, beta, delta, and epsilon cells, with latent time ordering driver genes (such as *Ngn3*, *Pax4*, *Arx*) in their causal sequence — a genuine *prediction* of regulatory order from snapshot data.

CellRank [7] converted these qualitative arrow plots into quantitative fate probabilities, demonstrating on pancreas and lung regeneration data that absorption probabilities correlate with experimental lineage-tracing outcomes. VeloVI [8] showed that a deep generative formulation matches or exceeds EM-based estimates on held-out likelihood while providing calibrated uncertainty — addressing the reproducibility concern that small preprocessing changes flip velocity signs.

### 5.2 Where the models break: a catalog of misspecification

The critical literature [11] has been unsparing, and rightly so. We systematize the failure modes:

1. **Steady-state violations.** In purely transient populations (e.g., a synchronized differentiation time course with no progenitors at equilibrium), the steady-state fit has no anchor and velocity directions become arbitrary [2].
2. **Multiple rate kinetics (MURK genes).** Lineage-specific kinetic parameters superimpose incompatible phase portraits; the recommended remedy is detection and removal of MURK genes, which discards signal along with artifact [11].
3. **Sparsity of unspliced counts.** With 10–25 percent of reads unspliced and heavy dropout, phase portraits for lowly expressed genes are noise-dominated; smoothing (kNN imputation of moments) helps but blurs genuine heterogeneity.
4. **Preprocessing dependence.** Normalization choices, HVG selection, and the number of PCs all shift velocity estimates; the manifold-constrained estimator of recent work reduces this sensitivity by restricting velocities to the data manifold [4 in spirit; see the Nature Methods 2024 manifold-constrained model].
5. **Scale and sign indeterminacy.** By the scale theorem above, magnitudes are incomparable across genes; worse, sign flips under misspecification reverse inferred fates silently.

A minimal reproducible velocity analysis in Python (Scanpy/scVelo [12][2]) illustrates how much machinery — and how many choices — sits behind one arrow plot:

```python
import scvelo as scv
import scanpy as sc

# 1. Load spliced/unspliced layers (from velocyto or kallisto-bustools)
adata = scv.read('pancreas.h5ad')

# 2. Preprocess: filter, normalize, select HVGs, moments on kNN graph
scv.pp.filter_and_normalize(adata, min_shared_counts=20, n_top_genes=2000)
scv.pp.moments(adata, n_pcs=30, n_neighbors=30)

# 3. Dynamical model: EM for kinetics + latent time per gene
scv.tl.recover_dynamics(adata, n_jobs=8)
scv.tl.velocity(adata, mode='dynamical')
scv.tl.velocity_graph(adata)

# 4. Fate mapping with CellRank on the velocity kernel
#    (see Lange et al. for the absorption-probability computation)
scv.pl.velocity_embedding_stream(adata, basis='umap')
scv.tl.latent_time(adata)
scv.pl.scatter(adata, color='latent_time', cmap='gnuplot')
```

> Theorem: *Consistency of the velocity-graph random walk.* Let **T** be the velocity-projected transition matrix on a finite kNN graph with all states communicating. Then **T** has a unique stationary distribution π with πT = π, and the fate (absorption) probabilities with respect to any designated terminal set are the unique solution of the Dirichlet problem on the graph. In particular, fate maps are well-defined mathematical objects — their biological validity, however, depends entirely on the validity of the velocities entering **T**.

### 5.3 Spatial benchmarks

On the mouse cortex Visium datasets paired with scRNA-seq references [9], deconvolution recovers laminar structure — layer-specific excitatory neuron proportions track cortical depth — and spatially smoothed pseudotime recapitulates the inside-out neurogenic gradient. In pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma, integrating spatial and single-cell data revealed tumor-associated multicellular immune hubs invisible to either modality alone. The lesson generalizes: *dynamics gains meaning from location*, and the strongest results in the field now come from joint models rather than from velocity alone.

## 6 Limitations

We close the technical exposition with an honest ledger.

- **The kinetic model is a caricature.** Piecewise-constant transcription rates ignore bursting, chromatin-state memory, and post-transcriptional regulation. Every velocity is a mean-field approximation; the field lacks a widely adopted stochastic-kinetics estimator that remains tractable at atlas scale.
- **Latent time is not time.** Scale non-identifiability means velocities and latent times live in arbitrary units per gene. Cross-gene and cross-dataset comparisons of magnitudes are meaningless without additional calibration, e.g. metabolic labeling (scSLAM-seq, NASC-seq) that measures absolute rates.
- **Direction without ground truth is dangerous.** In systems with unknown biology, there is no oracle for velocity correctness; the community's validation rests on a handful of well-studied developmental systems, and performance there need not transfer to disease or regeneration.
- **Spatial resolution remains coarse.** Array spots average over multiple cells, and deconvolution is an ill-posed inverse problem; subcellular methods are targeted rather than transcriptome-wide, forcing a resolution-versus-breadth tradeoff with no dominant solution.
- **Computational cost.** Dynamical EM per gene, deep generative training, and optimal-transport couplings scale poorly to million-cell atlases; the most rigorous methods are often the least applied.
- **Reproducibility.** Preprocessing sensitivity means two analysts can obtain opposing fate maps from identical counts. Reporting standards — frozen preprocessing, versioned references, uncertainty quantification — are still catching up with the methods.

## 7 Conclusion

Single-cell transcriptomics began with a destructive measurement and a static picture; it is growing into a predictive science of cellular dynamics. The arc traced in this thesis — from count models and graph clustering, through the kinetic ODEs of RNA velocity and the latent-time machinery of the dynamical model, to Markov fate mapping and spatially resolved trajectories — shows a field converging on a unified view: **cells as points moving on a learned manifold, with direction supplied by splicing kinetics, fate by random-walk absorption, and context by tissue space.**

The open problems are commensurate with the progress. Unifying velocity with metabolic labeling would calibrate arbitrary time units into real hours; extending kinetic models to bursting and regulation would replace the caricature with a mechanism; joint spatial-temporal-dynamical models would let tissue architecture constrain inferred flows rather than merely decorate them. The mathematics for much of this exists — in stochastic processes, in optimal transport, in spatial statistics. What remains is the harder work: building estimators that are identifiable, benchmarks that are honest, and software that reports uncertainty as loudly as it reports arrows. When that work is done, the snapshot will finally have become a film.
