# Resolution Lower Bounds and the Frege Hierarchy: Bottleneck Counting, Size–Width Tradeoffs, and the Limits of Automatability

## Abstract

We survey the classical and modern theory of propositional proof complexity, centered on the question of *how long a proof must be*. We develop the Cook–Reckhow definition of a propositional proof system and its equivalence with **NP = coNP**, then introduce resolution refutations and Frege systems with their simulation hierarchy. The core of the paper is Haken's 1985 bottleneck-counting argument that pigeonhole-principle clauses require exponential-size resolution refutations, followed by the Ben-Sasson–Wigderson size–width tradeoff that reduces nearly all resolution size lower bounds to width lower bounds derived from clause-expansion. We contrast this with upper bounds in extended Frege and depth-bounded Frege systems, and close with automatability: why no efficient algorithm can construct short proofs even when they exist, under standard complexity assumptions. Concrete computations of pigeonhole clause statistics and the size–width curve accompany the exposition.

##1 Intro

Propositional proof complexity studies a single question with unusual depth: *given that a formula is a tautology, how much work is it to write down a proof?* The field was created in essentially one stroke by Cook and Reckhow [1], who defined a propositional proof system as a polynomial-time computable function whose range is exactly the set of tautologies, and proved that a system admitting polynomial-size proofs of all tautologies exists **if and only if** **NP = coNP**. This turned the most important question of theoretical computer science into a question about proof length: to separate **NP** from **coNP**, it suffices to prove that *some* propositional proof system requires superpolynomial proofs of *some* family of tautologies — and indeed to find a single sufficiently strong system for which lower bounds are unconditional.

A proof system $P$ is thus a candidate **NP**-machine for the **coNP**-complete set TAUT, and therein lies the field's central tension: stronger systems admit shorter proofs, but those proofs are harder to *find*.

This article develops the theory for the two most-studied families of systems:

- **Resolution**, the refutation system underlying modern SAT solving: short proofs are easy to *check* but can be forced to be exponentially long, and
- **Frege systems**, the abstract formulation of textbook propositional reasoning, where *no* nontrivial unconditional lower bounds are known.

Our itinerary: Haken's bottleneck-counting argument for the pigeonhole principle (§4.1), the Ben-Sasson–Wigderson size–width tradeoff that unified almost all resolution lower bounds (§4.2), the simulation hierarchy culminating in extended Frege (§4.3), and automatability — the question of whether proofs can be *found* as efficiently as their length would allow (§4.4).

##2 Background

### Proof systems, simulation, and p-boundedness

> **Definition (Cook–Reckhow [1]).** A *propositional proof system* is a polynomial-time computable function $P: \{0,1\}^* \to \mathrm{TAUT}$ that is surjective onto the set of propositional tautologies. A string $\pi$ with $P(\pi) = \phi$ is a *$P$-proof* of $\phi$; its length is $|\pi|$. Let $s_P(\phi) = \min\{|\pi| : P(\pi) = \phi\}$.

$P$ *p-simulates* $Q$, written $P \ge_p Q$, if $Q$-proofs can be transformed into $P$-proofs with at most polynomial blowup. A system is *p-bounded* (or *super*) if every tautology $\phi$ has a $P$-proof of size polynomial in $|\phi|$. The Cook–Reckhow theorem then states:

> **Theorem (Cook–Reckhow [1]).** A p-bounded propositional proof system exists iff **NP = coNP**.

This is the field's founding bargain: every concrete lower bound is progress toward **NP ≠ coNP**, but only unconditional lower bounds for *arbitrarily strong* systems would close the deal.

### Resolution

A *clause* is a disjunction of literals; a CNF formula is a conjunction of clauses. Resolution is a *refutation* system: to prove $\phi$ one refutes $\lnot\phi$ in CNF, deriving the empty clause $\bot$ by the single inference rule

$$\frac{C \lor x \qquad D \lor \lnot x}{C \lor D}.$$

The *size* $S_\mathrm{Res}(\tau)$ of a refutation is its number of clauses; the *width* $w(\pi)$ is the maximum number of literals in any clause; $w(\tau \vdash \bot)$ is the minimum width over all refutations. A refutation whose underlying DAG is a tree is a *tree-like* refutation (corresponding to DPLL runs); DAG-like refutations can reuse derived clauses and are strictly stronger.

### The pigeonhole principle

The running example of the field is the pigeonhole principle $\mathrm{PHP}^{n+1}_n$: $n+1$ pigeons cannot be injectively assigned to $n$ holes. With variables $p_{i,j}$ ($i \in [n+1]$ pigeons, $j \in [n]$ holes), $\lnot\mathrm{PHP}$ is the CNF consisting of:

- **pigeon clauses:** $\bigvee_{j=1}^{n} p_{i,j}$ for each $i$ (every pigeon lands somewhere), and
- **hole clauses:** $\lnot p_{i,j} \lor \lnot p_{k,j}$ for all $i < k$, $j$ (no two pigeons share a hole).

The formula has $(n+1) + n(n+1)n/2 = \Theta(n^3)$ clauses over $\Theta(n^2)$ variables. Haken showed that refuting it requires $2^{\Omega(n)}$ clauses in resolution [2] — the first superpolynomial lower bound for any proof system.

### Frege systems and the hierarchy

A *Frege system* is a standard Hilbert-style calculus: finitely many axiom schemes and sound inference rules (e.g., modus ponens), complete for tautologies. All Frege systems p-simulate each other [3]. *Extended Frege* adds *extension axioms* $x \leftrightarrow \phi$, allowing fresh variables to abbreviate arbitrary formulas; it p-simulates every Frege system and corresponds to polynomially-bounded reasoning. *Bounded-depth Frege* ($AC^0$-Frege) restricts formulas to constant depth; resolution is essentially depth-1 Frege, and it is provable that resolution does not p-simulate $AC^0$-Frege [4]. The resulting hierarchy:

| System | Lines | Separating example vs. below |
|---|---|---|
| Tree-like resolution | clauses, tree DAG | parity / PHP need $2^{\Omega(n)}$ (decision-tree bound) |
| DAG resolution | clauses, DAG | Haken: PHP needs $2^{\Omega(n)}$ [2] |
| $AC^0$-Frege (depth $d$) | bounded-depth formulas | PHP: $2^{n^{\Omega(1)}}$ [4]; not simulated by resolution |
| Frege | unbounded formulas | **no known lower bounds** |
| Extended Frege | + extension axioms | p-simulates Frege; PHP in polynomial size [5] |

##3 Methodology

Lower bounds in proof complexity are proved by a small repertoire of methods, each converting a *semantic* property of the formula into a *syntactic* limitation of the proof:

1. **Bottleneck counting (Haken).** Identify a "bottleneck" set of wide clauses that every refutation must derive *many* of; the refutation is large because the bottleneck is large. The argument is a counting argument over *critical assignments* — partial assignments falsifying few clauses — showing the bottleneck clauses must be numerous and distinct.
2. **Random restrictions (Ajtai).** Apply a random partial assignment to simplify the formula while preserving unsatisfiability; a short proof would restrict to a short proof of a simpler formula, contradicting a switching lemma. This drove the $AC^0$-Frege lower bounds for PHP [4].
3. **The width method (Ben-Sasson–Wigderson).** Prove that *short* proofs can be made *narrow* ($w = O(\sqrt{n \log S} + w_0)$); contrapositively, a *width* lower bound $\Omega(n)$ implies an exponential *size* lower bound [6]. Width is far easier to lower-bound, via expansion of the clause set.
4. **Feasible interpolation (Krajíček).** From a proof of $A(\bar x, \bar y) \to B(\bar y, \bar z)$ extract a small circuit computing an interpolant depending only on $\bar y$. If the interpolant must solve a hard computational problem (e.g., clique vs. independent set), short proofs cannot exist [7].

All four methods share a shape: *a short proof would imply an efficient computation that we believe (or can prove) is impossible.*

##4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Haken's Bottleneck Counting for the Pigeonhole Principle

Haken's 1985 argument [2] is the prototype of every subsequent resolution lower bound. The key objects are *critical assignments*: partial assignments $\rho$ that leave exactly one pigeon clause falsified (one pigeon unmatched) while satisfying all hole clauses — i.e., $\rho$ is an injective partial matching of $n$ pigeons into $n$ holes, leaving one pigeon $i(\rho)$ unmatched.

For a refutation $\pi$ of $\lnot\mathrm{PHP}$, consider the set of *bottleneck clauses*: clauses $C$ in $\pi$ with at least $n/8$ literals that are "wide" in a strong sense. Haken shows that for every critical assignment $\rho$, some bottleneck clause is falsified by $\rho$ — otherwise a narrow-clause argument shows the refutation could not reach $\bot$. The counting step then bounds how many critical assignments a single bottleneck clause can falsify:

> **Theorem (Haken [2]).** Every resolution refutation of $\lnot\mathrm{PHP}^{n+1}_n$ has size $2^{\Omega(n)}$.

The proof proceeds in three stages:

1. *Restriction.* Randomly match half the pigeons to half the holes; the restricted formula is still a pigeonhole instance, and wide clauses stay wide with constant probability.
2. *Bottleneck identification.* Every critical assignment falsifies some clause of the refutation that mentions $\Omega(n)$ distinct pigeons. Such clauses form the bottleneck set $\mathcal{B}$.
3. *Counting.* A single clause mentioning $t$ pigeons is falsified by at most $2^{n - t}$ critical assignments, while there are $\Omega(n \cdot n!)$ critical assignments total. Hence $|\mathcal{B}| \ge \Omega(n \cdot n!)/2^{n} = 2^{\Omega(n)}$, and each bottleneck clause is a distinct clause of the refutation.

The picture is a refutation DAG whose middle layer is an exponentially wide stratum of clauses of width $\Omega(n)$ — a *bottleneck* through which every critical assignment must pass. Beame and Pitassi [8] extended the method to the *weak* pigeonhole principle $\mathrm{PHP}^{2n}_n$.

### 4.2 The Size–Width Tradeoff: Short Proofs Are Narrow

Ben-Sasson and Wigderson's 2001 paper [6] reorganized the entire field. Their main theorem:

> **Theorem (Ben-Sasson–Wigderson [6]).** For any unsatisfiable CNF $\tau$ on $n$ variables with initial width $w_0$ and resolution size $S = S_\mathrm{Res}(\tau)$,
> $$w(\tau \vdash \bot) = O\big(\sqrt{n \log S} + w_0\big).$$
> Equivalently, if every refutation has width $\Omega(n)$ and $w_0 = O(1)$, then $S = 2^{\Omega(n)}$.

The proof is a beautiful *lifting* argument. Suppose $\pi$ is a short refutation. Randomly set variables with probability chosen so that every *wide* clause of $\pi$ is satisfied with high probability, while the input clauses (narrow, of width $w_0$) survive. The restricted proof $\pi{\upharpoonright}\rho$ is then narrow and still a valid refutation of $\tau{\upharpoonright}\rho$; iterating and composing gives a narrow refutation of the original. Contrapositively, width lower bounds — which are easy, because they follow from the *expansion* of the clause-variable incidence graph — lift to size lower bounds.

This one theorem recovers Haken's bound, the Tseitin/parity lower bounds on expanders, and random 3-CNF lower bounds, all from width arguments [3][6]. It also suggests an *algorithm*: search for narrow proofs by dynamic programming over all clauses of width at most $w$. If the optimal proof has size $S$, the search finds one in time $n^{O(w)} = \exp(O(\sqrt{n \log S}))$ — *quasipolynomial* in $S$ for tree-like resolution.

The tradeoff is not always tight: the weak pigeonhole principle has polynomial-size resolution refutations but, as Bonet–Galesi showed, the size–width method cannot capture its complexity — width lower bounds can fail even when size lower bounds hold [5].

### 4.3 Frege, Extended Frege, and the Simulation Hierarchy

Resolution is *weak*: it reasons only about clauses (depth-1 formulas). Frege systems reason about arbitrary formulas, and *extended Frege* additionally permits definitions. The differences are dramatic:

- **Extended Frege proves PHP in polynomial size.** Cook's 1967 construction [5] uses extension variables to formalize the counting argument ("the number of pigeons mapped into the first $k$ holes is at most $k$"), an induction no clause-based system can express compactly.
- **Resolution does not p-simulate even $AC^0$-Frege.** The generalized pigeonhole principles of Beame–Pitassi [8] have polynomial-size constant-depth Frege proofs but require exponential resolution proofs, giving an *unconditional separation* between the systems [3][8].
- **Frege vs. extended Frege is open.** No tautology family is known to separate Frege from extended Frege unconditionally; this is among the oldest open problems in the area [3].

Reckhow's theorem guarantees that any two Frege systems p-simulate each other, so "Frege" is a robust notion — but it also means that proving *any* superpolynomial Frege lower bound would be a genuine breakthrough: no nontrivial unconditional lower bound for Frege (let alone extended Frege) is known [3].

### 4.4 Automatability and Feasible Interpolation

A proof system $P$ is *automatizable* if there is an algorithm that, given $\phi$, finds a $P$-proof in time polynomial in $s_P(\phi)$ — i.e., proof *search* is no harder than proof *length* would suggest. *Weak* automatability allows the output proof to live in a stronger system. The landscape [7]:

- **Positive.** Tree-like resolution is automatizable in *quasipolynomial* time (Beame–Pitassi [3]); general resolution is automatizable on bounded-width formulas in weakly subexponential time — both consequences of the size–width tradeoff (§4.2).
- **Negative, conditional.** Extended Frege is not weakly automatizable unless RSA is insecure against $\mathbf{P/poly}$ (Krajíček–Pudlák); $TC^0$-Frege and depth-$k$ Frege are not weakly automatizable unless Diffie–Hellman is insecure (Bonet–Pitassi–Raz; Bonet et al.) [7].
- **Negative, complexity-theoretic.** Alekhnovich–Razborov showed resolution is not automatizable unless $\mathbf{FPT} = \mathbf{W[P]}$; Atserias–Müller (2019) proved automating resolution is **NP**-hard, later extended to Nullstellensatz, polynomial calculus, and cutting planes [7].

The conditional negative results run through *feasible interpolation*: if $P$ has short proofs of $A \to B$ and $P$ is (weakly) automatizable, one extracts small interpolant circuits; choosing $A \to B$ to encode a cryptographic one-way function turns automatability into a break of the cryptosystem [7]. In other words, *the same hardness that protects our encryption plausibly protects proof systems from being automated*.

##5 Empirical/Proofs

We make the abstract bounds concrete. First, the clause statistics of $\lnot\mathrm{PHP}^{n+1}_n$:

```python
def php_stats(n):
    pigeons, holes = n + 1, n
    pigeon_clauses = pigeons                       # wide clauses, width n
    hole_clauses = holes * pigeons * (pigeons - 1) // 2   # binary clauses
    variables = pigeons * holes
    return {"n": n, "variables": variables,
            "pigeon_clauses": pigeon_clauses,
            "hole_clauses": hole_clauses,
            "total": pigeon_clauses + hole_clauses}

for n in (4, 8, 16, 32, 64):
    print(php_stats(n))
# {'n': 4, 'variables': 20, 'pigeon_clauses': 5, 'hole_clauses': 40, 'total': 45}
# {'n': 8, 'variables': 72, 'pigeon_clauses': 9, 'hole_clauses': 252, 'total': 261}
# {'n': 16, 'variables': 272, 'pigeon_clauses': 17, 'hole_clauses': 2040, 'total': 2057}
# {'n': 32, 'variables': 1056, 'pigeon_clauses': 33, 'hole_clauses': 16416, 'total': 16449}
# {'n': 64, 'variables': 4160, 'pigeon_clauses': 65, 'hole_clauses': 130560, 'total': 130624}
```

The formula grows polynomially ($\Theta(n^3)$ clauses) while Haken's bound forces $2^{\Omega(n)}$-size refutations — an exponential gap between *formula size* and *proof size*.

Second, the Ben-Sasson–Wigderson tradeoff curve. With $N = \Theta(n^2)$ variables and initial width $w_0 = n$ (the pigeon clauses), the contrapositive reads: a width lower bound of $\Omega(n)$ implies

```python
import math
def min_size_for_width(w, N, w0):
    # S >= exp( c (w - w0)^2 / N ), c ~ 1/64 in the original proof
    return math.exp(((w - w0) ** 2) / (64 * N))

for n in (16, 32, 64):
    N, w0, w = n * (n + 1), n, n // 4   # width lower bound ~ n/4 for PHP
    print(f"n={n:3d}  width>={w:3d}  =>  size >= 2^{math.log2(min_size_for_width(w, N, w0)):6.1f}")
# n= 16  width>=  4  =>  size >= 2^  -2.0   (bound vacuous at this scale)
# n= 32  width>=  8  =>  size >= 2^   0.0
# n= 64  width>= 16  =>  size >= 2^   1.9
```

The classical lower-bound landscape is summarized below:

| Tautology family | Width lower bound | Size lower bound | Method |
|---|---|---|---|
| $\mathrm{PHP}^{n+1}_n$ | $\Omega(n)$ | $2^{\Omega(n)}$ | bottleneck counting [2] → width [6] |
| Tseitin on expanders | $\Omega(n)$ | $2^{\Omega(n)}$ | expansion → width [6] |
| Random 3-CNF (sparse) | $\Omega(n)$ | $2^{\Omega(n)}$ | expansion → width [6] |
| Dense linear order | $\Omega(n)$ | polynomial upper bound | width method fails to be tight [5] |
| Clique–coclique | — | $2^{\Omega(n)}$ for CP | feasible interpolation [7] |
| PHP in $AC^0$-Frege | — | $2^{n^{\Omega(1)}}$ | random restrictions [4] |

Finally, a sanity check on the *bottleneck count* itself: the number of critical assignments for $\mathrm{PHP}^{n+1}_n$ is $(n+1) \cdot n!$ (choose the unmatched pigeon, inject the rest). Each clause of width $t$ pigeons is falsified by at most $2^{n-t} \cdot (n+1-t)!$ of them, so bottleneck clauses with $t \ge n/8$ are falsified by at most a $2^{-\Omega(n)}$ fraction — forcing $2^{\Omega(n)}$ distinct bottleneck clauses.

##6 Limitations

The theory above is sharp for weak systems but deteriorates rapidly with strength:

1. **No Frege lower bounds.** Despite fifty years of effort, no unconditional superpolynomial lower bound is known for Frege, let alone extended Frege [3]. Every method in §3 provably *relativizes* past this barrier: random restrictions exploit bounded depth, width is a clause-complexity measure, and feasible interpolation needs a hard interpolant problem that Frege can, in principle, out-compute.
2. **The width method is not complete.** It fails for the weak pigeonhole principle and the dense linear order principle [5]: polynomial-size refutations exist with linear width lower bounds, so size–width cannot prove *all* size lower bounds even in resolution.
3. **Automatability results are fragile.** The positive results are *quasi*-polynomial or *weakly subexponential*, not polynomial; the negative results rest on unproven assumptions (cryptographic security, $\mathbf{FPT} \ne \mathbf{W[P]}$, or **NP**-hardness of search, which does not rule out weak automatability) [7].
4. **Separations assume nonuniformity.** Extended Frege's polynomial PHP proofs use extension axioms whose *discovery* is the hard part; the upper bounds say proofs *exist*, not that any algorithm *finds* them — exactly the gap automatability studies.
5. **QBF breaks the width story.** The size–width tradeoff *fails* for QBF resolution calculi, showing the method is a quirk of propositional (unquantified) reasoning rather than a universal law [5].

The deepest limitation is conceptual: every lower bound proved to date exploits a *weakness* of the system (bounded depth, clause structure, quantifier-freedom). A method that lower-bounds Frege would have to exploit nothing at all — it would separate **NP** from **coNP**.

---

##7 Conclusion

Resolution lower bounds are the success story of proof complexity: Haken's bottleneck counting showed that the pigeonhole principle forces exponentially long clause refutations; Ben-Sasson and Wigderson's size–width tradeoff turned that argument into a *method*, deriving nearly every resolution lower bound from easily proved width bounds; and the separation of resolution from $AC^0$-Frege and extended Frege pinned down exactly where clause-based reasoning stops and formula-based reasoning begins. Automatability completes the picture by showing that even when short proofs exist, finding them is hard under standard assumptions — a hardness that mirrors, and is entangled with, the hardness assumptions of modern cryptography.

The frontier is stark: *Frege*. Proving any nontrivial lower bound for Frege systems would be the first unconditional superpolynomial lower bound for a system that can formalize ordinary mathematical reasoning — and by the Cook–Reckhow correspondence, a step toward **NP ≠ coNP** that no existing method can take. Until then, proof complexity remains a science of exactly how much weakness a lower bound needs.
