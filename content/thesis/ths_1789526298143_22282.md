# Free Boundary Problems: Optimal Regularity, Monotonicity, and Singularities in Obstacle-Type Problems

## Abstract

Free boundary problems sit at the interface of the calculus of variations and geometric analysis: the unknown is not merely a function but also the region on which its equation holds. This thesis develops the modern theory of the classical obstacle problem and its relatives. We begin with the variational formulation and the Frehse optimal C^{1,1} regularity of solutions, then turn to the free boundary itself: Caffarelli's dichotomy between regular and singular points, the classification of blow-ups, and the Alt–Caffarelli–Friedman monotonicity formula as the engine behind Lipschitz estimates in two-phase problems. We present the Weiss and Monneau energies, the epiperimetric route to uniqueness of blow-ups, and the stratification of the singular set, including recent generic-regularity breakthroughs. The thin obstacle (Signorini) problem is treated through the Almgren frequency formula and the frequency gap. We close with applications: the American option as a parabolic obstacle problem in mathematical finance, and Hele–Shaw flow via the Baiocchi transform.

##1 Introduction

Consider an elastic membrane stretched over a frame and forced to lie above a fixed obstacle. Where the membrane touches the obstacle it conforms to it; where it does not, it is a minimal surface. The *a priori* unknown interface between the contact region and the detached region is a **free boundary**. This deceptively simple physical picture encodes one of the deepest chapters of twentieth-century partial differential equations.

The classical obstacle problem is the variational problem of minimizing the Dirichlet energy among functions constrained to stay above a given obstacle function psi. Its Euler–Lagrange structure is a complementarity system: the solution is harmonic where it strictly exceeds the obstacle and is pressed against the obstacle elsewhere, with the Laplacian jumping discontinuously across the free boundary. Two questions dominate the theory. First, what is the *optimal regularity* of the solution across the free boundary — how smooth can u be, given that its second derivatives must jump? Second, what can be said about the *regularity of the free boundary itself* — is it a smooth hypersurface, or can it develop singularities?

The answers, developed principally by Frehse, Caffarelli, Kinderlehrer, Nirenberg, Alt, and Friedman in the 1970s and 1980s, form a template for an enormous subsequent literature [1][2][3]. The solution is optimally C^{1,1}: bounded second derivatives, and no better in general, since the Laplacian genuinely jumps. The free boundary splits into *regular points*, near which it is an analytic hypersurface, and *singular points*, where blow-up analysis reveals the profile of a homogeneous quadratic polynomial and the singular set admits a fine stratification. The principal analytical tool is *monotonicity*: quantities such as the Alt–Caffarelli–Friedman functional and the Weiss energy that are monotone under the natural quadratic rescaling and therefore rigidify the possible blow-up limits [2][4].

Section 2 sets up the variational formulation and complementarity conditions; Section 3 develops optimal regularity, nondegeneracy, and the blow-up machinery; Section 4 treats the ACF formula, the regular/singular dichotomy, the thin obstacle, and applications; Section 5 states the landmark theorems; Sections 6 and 7 discuss limitations and conclude.

##2 Background

### The variational problem

Let Omega be a bounded domain in Euclidean n-space and let psi be a smooth obstacle function. The admissible class is the convex set K consisting of Sobolev functions that lie above psi and attain prescribed boundary values g (with g at least psi on the boundary). The energy is the Dirichlet integral, possibly with a forcing term:

E(u) = integral over Omega of one half the squared gradient plus f times u.

Because K is closed and convex and E is coercive and weakly lower semicontinuous, a unique minimizer u exists. The first variation against admissible competitors yields the **variational inequality**: the integral of the gradient of u against the gradient of any admissible test direction is bounded above by the integral of minus f times that direction. Choosing test functions localized where u is strictly above psi recovers harmonicity there; globally one obtains the complementarity system

Delta u = f in the non-coincidence set where u exceeds psi,
u = psi and Delta u at most f in the coincidence set,
with the product of the gap and the Laplacian defect vanishing everywhere [1].

It is convenient to normalize: subtract the obstacle and reduce to psi equal to zero, so that u is nonnegative, Delta u equals f where u is positive, and Delta u is at most f everywhere in the distributional sense. The **free boundary** Gamma is the boundary of the positivity set intersected with Omega — the edge of the contact region. Note the structure of the equation: the right-hand side f times the indicator of the positivity set has a jump discontinuity across Gamma. Standard elliptic theory therefore cannot give more than bounded second derivatives, and the central question is whether even that much holds up to the free boundary.

##3 Methodology

### Penalization and optimal regularity

The classical route to optimal regularity, due to Frehse, approximates the constraint by a penalty: minimize the Dirichlet energy plus a large multiple of the integral of the squared negative part of u. The penalized problems are smooth, with Euler–Lagrange equation Delta u_epsilon = f + beta_epsilon(u_epsilon) where the penalty term beta_epsilon is nonpositive, supported where u_epsilon is negative, and uniformly bounded. Differentiating the equation and applying Bernstein-type estimates to the gradient gives uniform Lipschitz bounds on the gradient of the penalized solutions, independent of epsilon. Passing to the limit yields a solution of the obstacle problem with bounded second derivatives: **u belongs to C^{1,1} locally**, with the Hessian bound controlled by the supremum of f [1].

Sharpness is elementary: in one dimension with constant forcing, the solution near a free boundary point is a half-parabola glued to zero, whose second derivative jumps. No C^2 regularity can hold in general. The C^{1,1} estimate is therefore *optimal*, and it is the foundation on which all free boundary analysis rests: it provides the compactness needed for blow-up sequences.

### Nondegeneracy

A second pillar is **nondegeneracy**: near a free boundary point, the solution cannot grow more slowly than quadratically. Precisely, the supremum of u in a ball of radius r centered at a free boundary point is bounded below by a positive constant times r squared. The proof is a barrier argument: if u were too small in the ball, the function w(x) = u(x) minus a small multiple of the squared distance to the center would attain an interior minimum where its Laplacian is negative, contradicting the equation. Nondegeneracy guarantees that blow-up limits are nontrivial and is the key ingredient in showing that regular points are dense in the free boundary in an appropriate measure-theoretic sense [2].

### The blow-up machine

Let x_0 be a free boundary point. The **quadratic rescaling** u_r(x) = r^{-2} u(x_0 + r x) preserves the C^{1,1} bound and the equation structure (with forcing scaled to a constant in the limit). By compactness, along subsequences r_j tending to zero, u_{r_j} converges locally uniformly (in fact in C^{1,alpha} for every alpha below one) to a **blow-up limit** u_0: a global solution in all of space, growing at most quadratically, with zero as a free boundary point. Classifying the possible u_0 is the heart of the theory, because the infinitesimal shape of the solution at x_0 dictates the local geometry of the free boundary. The classification, due to Caffarelli, states that every blow-up is either a *half-space solution* — one half of a parabola in a single direction, vanishing on a half-space — or a *nonnegative homogeneous quadratic polynomial* with Laplacian equal to one [2].

> Theorem: (Caffarelli blow-up classification) Let u_0 be a blow-up limit of a solution to the normalized obstacle problem at a free boundary point. Then either the coincidence set of u_0 has nonempty interior and u_0 is, after rotation, one half of the squared positive part of the first coordinate; or the coincidence set has empty interior and u_0 is a homogeneous quadratic polynomial, nonnegative everywhere, with constant Laplacian one.

The first case defines a **regular point**; the second a **singular point**. At regular points, the free boundary is a C^{1,alpha} — indeed analytic — hypersurface near x_0; the proof flattens the boundary via the implicit function theorem applied to the directional derivative, which is strictly monotone across the free boundary by the Hopf lemma. At singular points the contact set is thin — contained in a lower-dimensional subspace — and the analysis is far subtler [2][5].

```python
# Quadratic blow-up at a free boundary point (1D model solution)
import numpy as np

def blow_up(u, x0, r, xs):
    return u(x0 + r * xs) / r**2   # rescaling u_r(x) = r^-2 u(x0 + r x)

xs = np.linspace(-2.0, 2.0, 401)
u = lambda x: 0.5 * np.maximum(x, 0.0) ** 2
for r in (1.0, 0.5, 0.25, 0.125):
    profile = blow_up(u, 0.0, r, xs)   # all profiles coincide: the half-parabola
```

##4 Deep Dive

###4.1 The Alt–Caffarelli–Friedman monotonicity formula

The ACF formula was introduced to handle *two-phase* free boundary problems, where the solution changes sign and both phases carry energy. Let w_1 and w_2 be nonnegative continuous functions, subharmonic in the unit ball, with disjoint supports and both vanishing at the origin. Define the functional

Phi(r) = r^{-4} times the product of two weighted Dirichlet integrals: the integral over the ball of radius r of the squared gradient of w_i divided by the distance to the origin raised to n minus two, for i equal to one and two.

Then Phi is finite, bounded, and **monotone nondecreasing** in r [4]. The weight is the fundamental solution of the Laplacian, and the proof differentiates under the integral sign and applies the Friedland–Hayman eigenvalue inequality on the sphere to control the boundary terms. The exponent four is dictated by scaling: each factor scales like r squared for linear functions, and the product like r to the fourth.

The original application was the variational problem of Alt, Caffarelli, and Friedman: minimize the Dirichlet energy plus the volume of the positivity set. Minimizers satisfy a Bernoulli-type free boundary condition — the jump of the squared gradient across the free boundary equals one — and the ACF formula yields the crucial **Lipschitz regularity** of minimizers across the free boundary, from which the regularity theory of the one-phase Bernoulli problem unfolds [3][4]. Variants exist for variable-coefficient operators, for the fractional Laplacian, and on Riemannian manifolds [6].

For the one-phase obstacle problem, the companion tools are the **Weiss energy**

W(r) = r^{-n-2} times the integral over the ball of radius r of the squared gradient plus u, minus r^{-n-3} times the integral over the sphere of u squared,

which is monotone and constant exactly on homogeneous blow-ups, and the **Monneau energy**, tailored to singular points, which is monotone when the blow-up is a fixed quadratic polynomial. Together these energies force blow-up limits to be homogeneous and, in the singular case, allow comparison against the polynomial class [5].

###4.2 Regular versus singular points

Caffarelli's dichotomy reduces the free boundary to two species. **Regular points** are those admitting a half-space blow-up; near them the free boundary is a C^{1,alpha} graph, and by a hodograph transform and elliptic regularity it is in fact analytic. The set of regular points is relatively open in the free boundary, and under mild nondegeneracy assumptions it carries full surface measure.

**Singular points** are the complement. Their blow-ups are nonnegative quadratic polynomials p with Laplacian one, and the contact set near a singular point is contained in the zero set of p — a lower-dimensional cone. Caffarelli showed the singular set stratifies by the dimension of the kernel of the blow-up polynomial: points where the kernel has dimension k form a set that is locally contained in a k-dimensional C^1 manifold. In particular the top stratum has dimension at most n minus one [2][5].

Weiss's **epiperimetric inequality** yields *uniqueness of blow-ups* at singular points and sharp regularity of each stratum [5]; later work refined the stratification to countable unions of C^1 manifolds. Figalli and Serra proved **generic regularity**: for a generic smooth obstacle, the singular set is empty in low dimensions — singularities are non-generic [5].

|---|---|---|
| Regular point | Half-parabola in one direction | Analytic hypersurface |
| Singular point, kernel dim k | Nonnegative quadratic polynomial | Contained in a k-dim C^1 manifold |
| Thin-obstacle regular point | Homogeneous of degree three halves | C^{1,alpha} hypersurface in the thin space |
| Thin-obstacle singular point | Homogeneous polynomial, even degree at least two | Stratified, lower dimensional |

###4.3 The thin obstacle (Signorini) problem

In the **thin obstacle problem**, the constraint is imposed only on a lower-dimensional manifold — typically the hyperplane where the last coordinate vanishes. One minimizes the Dirichlet energy among functions staying nonnegative on that hyperplane. The Euler–Lagrange conditions are the **Signorini complementarity conditions**: the solution is harmonic away from the hyperplane, nonnegative on it, with nonpositive normal derivative from above (nonnegative in the symmetric formulation), and the product of the trace and the normal derivative vanishing [7].

The scaling changes everything: the model solution is homogeneous of degree three halves, and the optimal regularity is C^{1,1/2} rather than C^{1,1}, proved by Athanasopoulos and Caffarelli via a boundary Harnack principle. The Almgren **frequency formula**

N(r) = r times the Dirichlet integral over the ball of radius r, divided by the boundary L^2 integral over the sphere,

is monotone for solutions of the Signorini problem, and its limit as r tends to zero — the *frequency* — equals the homogeneity of blow-ups.

###4.4 Applications: American options and Hele–Shaw flow

**American options.** The price of an American put is the value function of an optimal stopping problem: the holder chooses a stopping time to maximize the expected discounted payoff. The dynamic programming principle yields a *parabolic* variational inequality: the minimum of the parabolic operator applied to the price and the difference between the price and the payoff vanishes. The region where the price equals the payoff is the exercise region; its boundary is the **optimal exercise boundary**, a free boundary. The parabolic obstacle theory gives optimal regularity of the price and, via the methods above adapted to the heat operator, regularity of the exercise boundary: it is a smooth graph under standard nondegeneracy conditions, with the smooth-fit principle as the financial manifestation of C^{1} matching across the free boundary [9].

**Hele–Shaw flow.** When a viscous fluid is injected between two closely spaced plates, the fluid domain expands with normal velocity proportional to the pressure gradient — Darcy's law. The Baiocchi transform integrates the pressure in time, converting the moving-boundary problem into an *elliptic* obstacle problem for the time-integrated pressure, with the fluid front becoming the free boundary. Regularity of the front follows from the obstacle theory; conversely, singularities of Hele–Shaw flow (cusps, waiting times) illuminate the sharpness of the elliptic results. The variational-inequality approach of Gustafsson and the existence theory of DiBenedetto and Friedman made this correspondence rigorous [10].

##5 Empirical Results and Proofs

We collect the landmark theorems. Proofs are sketched; full details are in the cited monographs [2][7].

> Theorem: (Frehse optimal regularity) Let u solve the obstacle problem with bounded forcing f. Then u is locally C^{1,1}, with the local Hessian bound depending only on the supremum of f and the dimension. The exponent is sharp.

> Proof: Penalize the constraint, differentiate the penalized equation, and apply a Bernstein estimate to the gradient: the penalty term has the favorable sign where the solution goes negative. Uniform C^{1,1} bounds pass to the limit. Sharpness follows from the one-dimensional half-parabola example. [1]

> Theorem: (Caffarelli free boundary regularity) The free boundary splits into a relatively open set of regular points, near each of which it is an analytic hypersurface, and a closed singular set stratified into countably many C^1 manifolds of dimension at most n minus one.

> Proof: Blow-up classification via the Weiss energy and a compactness argument; at regular points the Hopf lemma applied to a directional derivative gives strict monotonicity across the boundary, and the implicit function theorem yields a C^{1,alpha} graph, bootstrapped to analytic by elliptic theory. At singular points the Monneau energy and the epiperimetric inequality give uniqueness of the polynomial blow-up and tangential regularity of each stratum. [2][5]

> Theorem: (Alt–Caffarelli–Friedman) Minimizers of the two-phase Bernoulli energy are locally Lipschitz, and the free boundary is smooth near flat points; the ACF monotonicity formula is the decisive estimate.

> Proof: The ACF functional applied to the positive and negative parts controls the gradient at two-phase points; a comparison argument then yields linear growth, hence Lipschitz continuity. Flatness implies C^{1,alpha} regularity via an improvement-of-flatness iteration. [3][4]

> Theorem: (Thin obstacle optimal regularity and frequency gap) Solutions of the Signorini problem are C^{1,1/2} up to the thin space, and this is optimal. The Almgren frequency at any free boundary point is either three halves or at least two.

> Proof: The boundary Harnack principle gives the Hölder exponent one half for the normal derivative; the frequency gap follows from a classification of homogeneous global solutions combined with a Monneau-type monotonicity. [7][8]

The constants are explicit, the blow-up profiles classified, and the singular strata have sharp dimension bounds — which is why attention has shifted to generic regularity and to nonlocal and parabolic variants.

##6 Limitations

1. **Singularities persist for fixed obstacles.** The Figalli–Serra generic regularity theorem says singularities are non-generic, but for a *given* obstacle — the one arising in an application — singular points can and do occur, and their fine geometric structure in high dimensions is not fully understood.
2. **Higher-order and fully nonlinear variants.** The obstacle problem for fully nonlinear operators (Bellman, Isaacs) has an optimal-regularity theory, but the free boundary regularity lags behind the linear case; the blow-up classification is less rigid without a monotonicity formula as strong as Weiss's.
3. **Parabolic theory is harder.** Time dependence breaks the clean variational structure; while the parabolic obstacle problem enjoys good solution regularity, free boundary regularity for the Stefan-type and American-option problems requires additional nondegeneracy hypotheses that can fail at maturity or at degenerate points.
4. **Nonlocal extensions.** The fractional obstacle problem is well understood at the level of optimal regularity, but the singular set of the fractional free boundary and the analogue of the epiperimetric inequality in the nonlocal setting remain active research areas [6][8].
5. **Numerics.** Free boundaries are hard to compute with guaranteed accuracy near singular points, where the solution is only as smooth as the theory allows; rigorous adaptive methods for obstacle-type free boundaries are still developing.

---

##7 Conclusion

The obstacle problem teaches a general lesson: a variational problem with a jump discontinuity in its Euler–Lagrange equation can nevertheless enjoy a *complete* regularity theory, provided one finds the right monotone quantities. The C^{1,1} optimal regularity of solutions, Caffarelli's regular–singular dichotomy, the Alt–Caffarelli–Friedman formula, and the Weiss and Almgren energies together form a machine that converts compactness of rescalings into geometric conclusions about an *a priori* unknown interface. The thin obstacle problem shows the same architecture at work with a different homogeneity, and the applications — from the optimal exercise boundary of an American option to the moving front of a Hele–Shaw flow — show that the theory earns its keep outside pure analysis.

The frontier has moved from "is the free boundary smooth?" to "how structured can the singular set be, and how typical are singularities?" Stratification, epiperimetric uniqueness of blow-ups, and generic smoothness suggest the classical theory continues to generate some of the finest analysis of our time.
