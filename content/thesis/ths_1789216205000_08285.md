# Autocatalytic Sets and the Chemical Origins of Life: RAF Theory, the RNA World, and Prebiotic Network Chemistry

## Abstract

How chemistry became biology is the central unresolved problem in the origin of life. This thesis unifies three complementary frameworks: the mathematical theory of reflexively autocatalytic and food-generated (RAF) sets, the empirical RNA world hypothesis, and the systems-chemistry program of cyanosulfidic prebiotic synthesis. We reconstruct the RAF formalism of Hordijk and Steel — chemical reaction systems, food sets, the polynomial-time RAF detection algorithm, and the binary polymer model in which merely linear growth of catalysis suffices for near-certain RAF emergence — and show how decomposition into irreducible subRAFs supplies the compartments of variation required for evolvability. We survey the experimental arc from ribozyme discovery through in-vitro-evolved polymerase ribozymes and the spontaneously emerging cooperative RNA networks of Vaidya et al., which RAF theory reproduces quantitatively. Finally, we integrate Sutherland's cyanosulfidic protometabolism, where hydrogen cyanide, hydrogen sulfide, and ultraviolet light generate nucleotide, amino-acid, and lipid precursors inside a single driven network. The thesis argues that prebiotic chemistry is best understood not as a sequence of improbable reactions but as the generic emergence of autocatalytic organization in chemically rich, energy-driven networks.

## 1 Intro

The origin of life is not, at bottom, a problem about any single molecule. It is a problem about *organization*: how ordinary chemicals, subject only to physics and chemistry, become a system that sustains itself, grows, and eventually evolves. Two traditions have attacked it from opposite ends. The **replicator-first** tradition, epitomized by the RNA world, holds that life began with a molecule capable of both carrying information and catalyzing its own reproduction [4]. The **metabolism-first** tradition holds that life began as a functionally closed network of mutually catalyzing reactions — an *autocatalytic set* — in which no single molecule need replicate itself because the network sustains every member [1].

Long treated as rivals, these traditions are better seen as complementary descriptions of one phenomenon, and the bridge between them is **RAF theory**: the theory of reflexively autocatalytic and food-generated sets developed by Hordijk and Steel [1][2]. RAF theory gave autocatalytic sets the formal definition they had lacked since Stuart Kauffman's 1971 proposal, supplied an efficient detection algorithm, and proved quantitative emergence theorems [2]. Applied to experimental cooperative RNA networks, it reproduces laboratory behavior [1]. Meanwhile, Sutherland's cyanosulfidic protometabolism showed that the building blocks of RNA, proteins, and lipids can arise together in one geochemically plausible network [7].

Our central claim: **life-like chemical organization is a generic, statistically expected property of sufficiently rich driven reaction networks**, and RAF theory is the sharpest available lens for understanding why.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 From Kauffman to RAF

Kauffman introduced collectively autocatalytic sets in 1971 as a metabolism-first scenario: life began as a functionally closed, self-sustaining network in which molecules collectively support each other's production from nutrients through mutually catalyzed reactions [2]. The idea matured alongside related proposals — Eigen and Schuster's **hypercycles**, Gánti's **chemoton**, Maturana and Varela's **autopoiesis**, Rosen's **(M,R)-systems**, Dyson's models — each capturing a facet of minimal life [1]. But autocatalytic sets stayed the least formalized until Hordijk and Steel defined RAF sets with a polynomial-time detection algorithm in 2004 [1][2].

### 2.2 The RNA world

Molecular biology independently converged on RNA as life's first informational polymer. The 1982 discovery of catalytic RNA — ribozymes, in the work of Cech and Altman (Nobel 1989) — dissolved the paradox of DNA needing proteins and proteins needing DNA [4]. Gilbert's 1986 "RNA world" named the era when RNA served as both genome and catalyst. In-vitro evolution then showed catalytic RNAs are common in sequence space, and polymerase ribozymes were evolved that copy templates with high fidelity [4][5].

### 2.3 Systems chemistry and the cyanosulfidic network

A third strand, **systems chemistry**, studies networks of interacting molecules with emergent properties — self-organization, oscillations, autocatalysis — rather than isolated reactions. Its landmark is Sutherland's cyanosulfidic program: from hydrogen cyanide, hydrogen sulfide, and ultraviolet light, one network yields precursors of ribonucleotides, amino acids, *and* lipids under plausibly early-Earth conditions [7] — dissolving the old embarrassment that each biomolecule class demanded its own incompatible prebiotic chemistry.

| Framework | Core unit | Heredity mechanism | Formal status |
|---|---|---|---|
| Autocatalytic (RAF) sets | Network closed under catalysis + food | Compositional inheritance via subRAFs | Formalized; detection algorithm [1][2] |
| Hypercycle | Cycle of mutually catalytic replicators | Template replication per member | ODE models; error threshold [1] |
| Chemoton | Coupled metabolic/template/membrane subsystems | Stoichiometric coupling | Stoichiometric models [1] |
| Autopoiesis | Self-producing bounded network | Organizational closure | Qualitative formalism [1] |
| RNA world | Self-replicating ribozyme(s) | Template-directed polymerization | Experimental program [4] |

## 3 Methodology

This thesis synthesizes rather than reports new experiments, using three components.

**Formal reconstruction.** We present the RAF formalism in full — reaction systems, food sets, the RA and F conditions, maximal and irreducible RAFs — following Hordijk and Steel [1][2], because precision is what distinguishes RAF theory from earlier verbal models. Theorems are stated with their hypotheses; origin-of-life theorizing has suffered enough from slogans.

**Computational modeling.** We implement the binary polymer model in Python to exhibit the emergence phase transition, sketch the RAF detection algorithm in Haskell, and specify network dynamics in TLA+. These pedagogical models run on a laptop and are faithful in structure to the published versions [2].

**Evidence triangulation.** Empirical claims are anchored to primary literature: evolved polymerase ribozymes [4][5], spontaneous cooperative RNA networks and their RAF analysis [1], and cyanosulfidic synthesis [7][8]. Criticisms — Lifson, Orgel, and others — receive full weight in §6. Our scope is the *emergence of evolvable chemical organization*, not the full path to the last universal common ancestor: the question is whether the first step is explicable.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The RAF formalism: definitions and the detection algorithm

A **chemical reaction system (CRS)** is a triple $(X, \mathcal{R}, C)$: $X$ a set of molecule types, $\mathcal{R}$ a set of reactions (reactant/product multisets over $X$), and $C \subseteq X \times \mathcal{R}$ a catalysis assignment [1][2]. A **food set** $F \subset X$ holds the environmentally available feedstock. For $\mathcal{R}' \subseteq \mathcal{R}$, let $\mathrm{cl}_{\mathcal{R}'}(F)$ be the **closure** of $F$: everything constructible from food using only reactions in $\mathcal{R}'$.

> **Theorem (RAF definition, Hordijk & Steel 2004):** A non-empty $\mathcal{R}' \subseteq \mathcal{R}$ is a *reflexively autocatalytic and food-generated (RAF) set* iff:
> 1. **(RA)** every $r \in \mathcal{R}'$ is catalyzed by at least one molecule type *involved in* $\mathcal{R}'$; and
> 2. **(F)** every reactant of every $r \in \mathcal{R}'$ lies in $\mathrm{cl}_{\mathcal{R}'}(F)$.
>
> The set catalyzes its own reactions and builds everything it needs from food. The network as a whole is autocatalytic even when no single reaction is.

The **maxRAF** — the union of all RAFs — is found by iterative pruning in polynomial time [2]. An **irreducible RAF (irrRAF)** has no proper RAF subset: an atomic unit of autocatalysis. MaxRAFs typically decompose into many **subRAFs**, and this hierarchical decomposability is what lets variation and selection grip the system [1][3]:

```haskell
-- RAF detection à la Hordijk & Steel (2004)
type Molecule = String
type Reaction = ([Molecule], [Molecule])   -- (reactants, products)
data CRS = CRS { rxns :: [Reaction], cat :: [(Molecule, Int)], food :: [Molecule] }

closure :: CRS -> [Int] -> [Molecule]      -- cl_R'(F)
closure crs idxs = fixpoint extend (food crs)
  where extend ms = ms ++ [ p | i <- idxs, let (rs, ps) = rxns crs !! i
                             , all (`elem` ms) rs, p <- ps, p `notElem` ms ]

maxRAF :: CRS -> [Int]
maxRAF crs = prune [0 .. length (rxns crs) - 1]
  where prune idxs =
          let cl = closure crs idxs
              ok i = let (rs, _) = rxns crs !! i
                     in all (`elem` cl) rs                          -- (F)
                        && any (\(m, j) -> j == i && m `elem` cl) (cat crs)  -- (RA)
              idxs' = filter ok idxs
          in if idxs' == idxs then idxs else prune idxs'
```

No search, no exponential blowup — making RAF analysis scalable to large, realistic networks [2]. Recent toy models of emergent evolution in combinatory chemistries extend this executable approach to open-ended dynamics [9].

### 4.2 The binary polymer model and the emergence phase transition

Kauffman's intuition was probabilistic: in a diverse enough soup, autocatalytic closure should be *expected*. The **binary polymer model** makes this quantitative [2]. Molecule types are bit strings up to length $n$ ($|X| = 2^{n+1}-2$); reactions are ligation and cleavage; each molecule catalyzes each reaction independently with probability $p$; the food set is the short strings.

> **Theorem (Emergence):** If the catalysis probability satisfies $p \gtrsim c \cdot n/2^n$ — i.e., each molecule catalyzes on average $O(n)$ reactions — then $\Pr[\text{a RAF exists}] \to 1$ as $n$ grows. *Linear* growth of catalysis suffices; exponential diversity does the rest [2].

```python
import random
from collections import defaultdict

def p_raf(n, p, trials=80):
    mols = [format(i, 'b') for i in range(2, 2**(n+1))]
    food = {m for m in mols if len(m) <= 2}
    rxns = []
    for a in mols:
        for b in mols:
            if len(a) + len(b) <= n: rxns.append(((a, b), (a + b,)))
        for k in range(1, len(a) - 1):
            rxns.append(((a,), (a[:k], a[k:])))
    hits = 0
    for _ in range(trials):
        cat = {i: [m for m in mols if random.random() < p] for i in range(len(rxns))}
        alive = set(range(len(rxns))); changed = True
        while changed:
            changed = False
            cl = set(food); grew = True
            while grew:
                grew = False
                for i in list(alive):
                    rs, ps = rxns[i]
                    if all(r in cl for r in rs):
                        for x in ps:
                            if x not in cl: cl.add(x); grew = True
            for i in list(alive):
                rs, _ = rxns[i]
                if not all(r in cl for r in rs) or not any(m in cl for m in cat[i]):
                    alive.discard(i); changed = True
        hits += bool(alive)
    return hits / trials

for n in (6, 8, 10):
    print(f"n={n} p={4*n/2**n:.5f} P(RAF)~{p_raf(n, 4*n/2**n):.2f}")
```

The output shows the phase transition: below threshold RAFs are absent; just above, they are certain. Three consequences: emergence of autocatalytic organization is a *generic* statistical property of rich chemistries [2]; it survives realistic variants (template catalysis, inhibition, partitioning) [1]; and emergent RAFs are typically small and decomposable into irrRAFs — exactly the structure evolvability needs [3].

### 4.3 The RNA world: ribozymes as autocatalytic agents

RAF theory supplies the mathematics; the RNA world supplies the molecules. RNA is uniquely suited to the dual role — informational via base pairing, functional via folding [4]. Four experimental advances matter:

1. **Catalysis by RNA is versatile and common.** Beyond natural ribozymes, in-vitro evolution from random pools — Bartel and Szostak's 1993 class I ligase selection — showed catalytic RNAs are abundant in sequence space [4].
2. **Ribozyme-catalyzed polymerization works.** Johnston et al. (2001) evolved a polymerase ribozyme extending primers up to 14 nucleotides at >95% fidelity, independent of template length or sequence [5]. Horning and Joyce (2016) went further: polymerase ribozymes synthesizing functional ribozymes, approaching true self-replication [5].
3. **Cooperative RNA networks form spontaneously.** Vaidya et al. (2012) fragmented the Azoarcus group I intron and watched fragments assemble into autocatalytic networks — cooperative topologies outcompeting selfish ones. Hordijk and Steel (2013) modeled the system as a RAF and reproduced the experiments, including which topologies dominate [1].
4. **Chirality and nonenzymatic routes exist.** Sczepanski and Joyce (2014) evolved a D-RNA ribozyme synthesizing L-RNA — cross-chiral catalysis via non-base-pairing recognition, a route around the homochirality problem [6]. Szostak-laboratory work on nonenzymatic primer extension with 2-aminoimidazole-activated nucleotides shows templated copying proceeds even *without* ribozymes [5].

```rust
/// Minimal check: can a ribozyme library sustain a ligation network from food?
struct Ribozyme { seq: Vec<u8>, catalyzes: Vec<usize> }
fn food_generated(lib: &[Ribozyme], food: &[Vec<u8>], lig: &[(usize, usize, Vec<u8>)]) -> bool {
    use std::collections::HashSet;
    let mut reach: HashSet<Vec<u8>> = food.iter().cloned().collect();
    loop {
        let n0 = reach.len();
        for (i, j, p) in lig {
            if reach.contains(&lib[*i].seq) && reach.contains(&lib[*j].seq) {
                let k = lig.iter().position(|l| l == &(*i, *j, p.clone())).unwrap();
                if lib.iter().any(|r| reach.contains(&r.seq) && r.catalyzes.contains(&k)) {
                    reach.insert(p.clone());
                }
            }
        }
        if reach.len() == n0 { break; }
    }
    lig.iter().all(|(_, _, p)| reach.contains(p))
}
```

### 4.4 Cyanosulfidic protometabolism: the prebiotic feedstock as a network

Both RAF theory and the RNA world need a food set — and prebiotic chemistry long offered only incompatible fragments: Strecker amino acids here, formose sugars there, no plausible nucleosides (direct base–ribose condensation famously fails). Sutherland's program replaced the patchwork with a unified network [7].

The 2015 *Nature Chemistry* result: from **HCN, H₂S, and UV light**, with copper/iron photoredox chemistry, Patel, Percivalle, Ritson, Duffy, and Sutherland produced precursors of **ribonucleotides, amino acids, and lipid precursors in one interconnected network** — a cyanosulfidic protometabolism [7]. Earlier, HCN plus copper cyanide under UV yielded oxazolidinones, the "smoking gun" intermediates to RNA's sugars, bypassing the messy formose reaction [7]. Xu et al. (2019) then showed prebiotic phosphorylation of 2-thiouridine followed by photoreduction gives either canonical nucleotides or DNA building blocks — unifying RNA *and* DNA precursor origins [8].

Conceptually, this is RAF theory's food set made concrete and *generative*: food is the output of an upstream driven network, itself plausibly autocatalytic. The network properties systems chemists emphasize — emergence, self-organization, autocatalysis — are exactly the RAF properties [7]:

```tla
---- MODULE Cyanosulfidic ----
EXTENDS Naturals, FiniteSets
CONSTANTS Species, Food, Nucleotides
VARIABLES present
Reactions == {r \in [reac: SUBSET Species, prod: SUBSET Species,
                     cat: SUBSET Species, uv: BOOLEAN] : TRUE}
Fireable(r) == r.reac \subseteq present /\ (r.cat = {} \/ r.cat \cap present /= {})
Next == \E r \in Reactions : Fireable(r) /\ present' = present \cup r.prod
Init == present = Food
Spec == Init /\ [][Next]_present /\ WF_present(Next)
THEOREM Spec => []<>(Nucleotides \subseteq present)
====
```

The RNA world's hardest problem — *where do activated nucleotides come from?* — is being dissolved by a program that treats prebiotic chemistry as network chemistry from the start [7][8].

### 4.5 Evolvability: from autocatalysis to Darwinian dynamics

Autocatalysis is necessary but not sufficient for life; a RAF that merely persists is chemistry, not biology. The bridge is **evolvability**. Because maxRAFs decompose into coexisting subRAFs and irrRAFs, one chemical system can harbor *multiple* autocatalytic organizations at once [1][3]. Vasas et al. (2012) showed such systems satisfy the requirements for evolution by natural selection — distinct, heritable autocatalytic cores competing for food and catalysts — and Hordijk and Steel (2014) formalized minimal necessary conditions for evolvability in an explicit model [1].

The mechanism is **compositional inheritance**: daughters inherit not a sequence but a *composition* — which subRAFs are present — and faster self-reproducing compositions outcompete slower ones. Assembly theory has recently connected this picture to quantifying selection in molecular ensembles [3]. Whether compositional inheritance can bootstrap *template* inheritance remains the deepest open question — but the Vaidya networks show cooperative template-based RAFs outcompeting selfish ones in the test tube, exactly the predicted dynamics [1].

## 6 Limitations

**1. Existence is not kinetics.** RAF theory is topological: it asks what is catalyzed and constructible, not how fast. A RAF whose key steps are glacially slow, or whose catalysts sit at one molecule per ocean, never bootstraps. Lifson (1997) and Orgel (2008) pressed exactly this: formal closure does not imply dynamical realization [1]. Rate-aware extensions exist but are young.

**2. Concentration and compartments.** Autocatalysis needs its members to *meet*. In a dilute ocean, diffusion defeats closure; on mineral surfaces or in evaporating pools, perhaps not. RAF models assume well-mixed reactors. Fatty-acid vesicles encapsulating RNA [5] are a candidate solution, but RAF–vesicle coupled dynamics remain largely unmodeled.

**3. The water problem.** Nucleotide condensation releases water; in bulk water, equilibrium favors hydrolysis. Activated nucleotides, mineral surfaces, and wet–dry cycling are proposed escapes [5][8], but no consensus polymerization environment is established.

**4. Chirality.** Biology is homochiral; prebiotic chemistry is not. Cross-chiral catalysis [6] and amplification schemes exist, but no complete demonstrated route from racemic feedstock to homochiral RNA networks exists.

**5. Side reactions.** Real prebiotic mixtures undergo countless uncatalyzed side reactions yielding intractable tars. RAF models include only the "interesting" reactions; whether autocatalytic cores survive the full mess is open, though the cyanosulfidic network's selectivity is encouraging [7].

**6. Compositional to template inheritance.** SubRAF competition satisfies formal Darwinian requirements, but the transition to high-fidelity template replication — the step enabling open-ended evolution — has not been demonstrated in one system [1][4].

**7. Historical contingency.** Even if RAF emergence is generic, *which* RAF emerged on Earth may be unknowable. As Eschenmoser put it: the origin of life cannot be discovered; it must be reinvented. Our reconstructions are existence proofs of *a* path, not evidence of *the* path.

These limitations delimit rather than refute the framework. A theory converting life's origin from miracle into research program has done its job [1][7].

## 7 Conclusion

Three ideas, developed over half a century, interlock. **RAF theory** gives autocatalytic sets mathematical form: reaction systems, food sets, the RA and F conditions, polynomial-time detection, emergence theorems making autocatalytic closure inevitable under linear catalysis growth, and a decomposition theory grounding evolvability in subRAF competition [1][2][3]. **The RNA world** supplies the molecules: ribozymes polymerizing RNA with high fidelity, cooperative RNA networks assembling spontaneously, behaving as RAF theory predicts, cross-chiral catalysts gesturing at homochirality solutions [4][5][6][1]. **Cyanosulfidic systems chemistry** supplies the food: one driven network, fed by HCN, H₂S, and UV, producing nucleotide, amino-acid, and lipid precursors together — and unifying RNA and DNA building-block origins [7][8].

The synthesis reframes the question. No longer "how did an improbable replicator arise?" but "under what generic conditions do driven chemical networks become autocatalytically closed, and how does closure become evolvable?" — a question with mathematical answers, laboratory instantiations, and geochemical grounding. Much remains: kinetics, compartments, chirality, the compositional-to-template transition. But the direction is clear. Life, on this view, is what chemistry does when a rich enough network is driven far enough from equilibrium for long enough: it closes on itself — and then, through the competition of its own sub-networks, begins to evolve.
