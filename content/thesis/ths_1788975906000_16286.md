# The Geometry of Self-Attention: Kernel Regression Perspectives, Induction Heads, and Mechanistic Interpretability of Transformers

## Abstract

The self-attention mechanism is kernel regression: single-head softmax attention is exactly a Nadaraya–Watson estimator with an exponential-family kernel, Gaussian on the unit sphere. This thesis unifies three perspectives on the Transformer. First, the *kernel regression* view: sparse operators such as sparsemax and $\alpha$-entmax correspond to compactly supported Epanechnikov and polynomial kernels [1][6]. Second, the *induction head* view: two-layer copying circuits implement discrete kernel retrieval — a first layer shifts tokens forward (K-composition) so a second layer can match the current token and copy its successor [3][4]. Third, the *mechanistic interpretability* view: attention decomposes into low-rank QK and OV circuits composed across layers [2]. We prove the attention–Nadaraya–Watson equivalence, survey the kernel zoo, verify induction-head phase changes in toy models, and analyze attention nonlinearities as projections onto the probability simplex. We close with the limits of the kernel analogy — learned non-stationary kernels, multi-head non-additivity — and directions for a geometric theory bridging nonparametrics and interpretability.

## 1. Introduction

Since its introduction by Vaswani et al. [1], the Transformer has become the dominant architecture across natural language processing, vision, and increasingly scientific computing. Its core computational primitive — *self-attention* — is deceptively simple: given queries $Q$, keys $K$, and values $V$,

$$\mathrm{Attention}(Q, K, V) = \mathrm{softmax}\left(\frac{QK^\top}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right) V.$$

For a single query vector $q$ and $n$ key-value pairs $(k_i, v_i)$, this reduces to the weighted average

$$\mathrm{Attention}(q, K, V) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \frac{\exp\left(q^\top k_i / \sqrt{d_k}\right)}{\sum_{j=1}^{n} \exp\left(q^\top k_j / \sqrt{d_k}\right)} v_i.$$

Anyone trained in classical nonparametric statistics will recognize this formula immediately: it is the *Nadaraya–Watson kernel regression estimator*, a weighted average of values whose weights are given by normalized kernel similarities between the query and each key [5]. This observation is far more than a curiosity. If self-attention *is* kernel regression — with the query playing the role of the test point, keys the role of training inputs, and values the role of training responses — then the vast machinery of nonparametric statistics becomes available for the analysis of transformers: bandwidth selection, consistency, bias-variance tradeoffs, reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces (RKHS), and the theory of kernel density estimation all acquire precise analogues in deep learning.

This thesis develops that correspondence into a full geometric theory. We pursue three threads:

1. **Kernel regression geometry.** We establish formally that softmax self-attention implements a Nadaraya–Watson estimator with an exponential-family (Gaussian) kernel, and survey the emerging "kernel zoo" — sparsemax, entmax, ReLU-normalized attention — each of which corresponds to a distinct classical kernel (Epanechnikov, biweight, triweight) [6]. This places attention-operator design on the principled footing of kernel design rather than heuristic normalization tricks.
2. **Induction heads as learned kernel circuits.** We analyze induction heads — the two-layer copying circuits discovered by Elhage et al. [3] and studied theoretically by Sanford et al. and others [4][8] — as discrete kernel retrievers: a first layer writes "previous-token" information into the residual stream (K-composition), enabling a second layer to perform kernel-based nearest-neighbor retrieval and copying. In-context learning is thus kernel regression executed *by* learned circuits.
3. **Mechanistic interpretability as circuit geometry.** We reconstruct the mathematical framework for transformer circuits [3] — QK and OV circuits, residual stream decomposition, and head composition — as a geometric decomposition of attention computation into low-rank bilinear forms on token embeddings. We argue that mechanistic interpretability is, at its mathematical core, *geometry*: the discovery of invariant structures in the bilinear forms that attention computes.

The contribution of this thesis is conceptual synthesis: these three literatures — nonparametric statistics, the theory of in-context learning, and mechanistic interpretability — are three descriptions of the *same* geometric object. Attention is kernel regression; in-context learning is kernel regression performed by a circuit; mechanistic interpretability is the reverse-engineering of the kernel circuits transformers actually learn.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 Scaled Dot-Product Attention

The Transformer [1] replaces recurrence with *multi-head self-attention*. Each head $h$ projects token embeddings into queries, keys, and values via learned matrices $W^Q_h, W^K_h, W^V_h$, computes $\mathrm{Attention}(QW^Q_h, KW^K_h, VW^V_h)$, and concatenates the results. The scaling factor $1/\sqrt{d_k}$ counteracts growth of the dot products with head dimension, keeping softmax inputs in a regime with non-vanishing gradients.

Crucially, pure self-attention is *permutation invariant*: permuting inputs permutes outputs identically. Positional information must be injected separately — originally via sinusoidal encodings [1], in modern models via rotary position embeddings (RoPE). This invariance is the first hint of the kernel connection: kernels measure *similarity*, not order, and attention is fundamentally a similarity-weighted averager.

### 2.2 The Nadaraya–Watson Estimator

In nonparametric statistics, suppose we observe data pairs $\{(k_i, v_i)\}_{i=1}^n$ (keys and values) and wish to estimate the value $\hat{v}$ associated with a new query $q$. The *Nadaraya–Watson estimator* [5] is

$$\hat{v}_{\mathrm{NW}}(q) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \frac{K(q, k_i)}{\sum_{j=1}^{n} K(q, k_j)} v_i,$$

where $K: \mathcal{X} \times \mathcal{X} \to \mathbb{R}_+$ is a *kernel* — a symmetric similarity function. With the Gaussian kernel $K(x, x') = \exp(-\|x - x'\|^2 / 2\sigma^2)$, the estimator performs local weighted averaging with bandwidth $\sigma$ and is *consistent*, converging to the true regression function as $n \to \infty$.

### 2.3 Induction Heads

Elhage et al. [2] discovered that small (1–2 layer) attention-only transformers trained on language modeling develop *induction heads*: attention circuits that implement the pattern "$\dots a\, b \dots a \Rightarrow$ predict $b$". Given a repeated token $a$, the head retrieves the token $b$ that followed $a$'s previous occurrence and copies it to the current position. This requires at least two layers: a first head copies token $a$'s identity forward (K-composition), and the second head matches the current token against those shifted representations and copies the subsequent token (V-composition). Induction heads are widely regarded as the atomic mechanism behind *in-context learning* [3][4][7] — the ability of large language models to perform tasks from prompt demonstrations alone.

### 2.4 The Circuits Framework

Anthropic's mathematical framework for transformer circuits [2] decomposes attention-only transformers as follows. Each head factors into two low-rank bilinear circuits:

- the **QK circuit** $W_{QK} = W_Q^\top W_K$, which determines *which* token is attended to (the attention pattern);
- the **OV circuit** $W_{OV} = W_O W_V$, which determines *what* information is moved and how it affects logits.

In attention-only models, freezing the attention pattern makes the entire network a linear map from embeddings to logits, decomposable into a sum over interpretable "paths". Across layers, heads compose via **K-composition** (one head's output feeds another's keys), **Q-composition**, and **V-composition** — the mechanism enabling multi-step algorithms like induction. Olsson et al. [3] showed that the emergence of induction heads coincides with a sharp phase change in in-context learning ability.

## 3. Methodology

Our methodology is primarily *theoretical synthesis with computational verification*, in the spirit of the circuits literature [3]: derive precise mathematical correspondences, then validate them empirically on small attention-only transformers trained in controlled settings.

**Correspondence derivation.** We begin by algebraically identifying single-head softmax attention with the Nadaraya–Watson estimator under specific kernel choices. The key maneuver is the standard identity

$$\exp\left(\frac{q^\top k_i}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right) = C(q, k_i) \cdot \exp\left(-\frac{\|q - k_i\|^2}{2\sqrt{d_k}}\right)$$

for unit-norm $q, k_i$ (with $C$ a query-dependent normalization absorbed by the softmax denominator). This yields **Theorem 1** (§5): softmax attention on the sphere is exactly Nadaraya–Watson regression with a Gaussian kernel of bandwidth $\sigma = d_k^{1/4}$.

**Kernel zoo analysis.** We follow Santos et al. [6] in mapping sparse attention variants to compactly supported kernels: sparsemax ↔ Epanechnikov kernel, normalized ReLU attention ↔ Epanechnikov under fixed normalization, and $\alpha$-entmax attention with $\alpha = 1 + 1/n$ ↔ $n$-th order polynomial kernels (biweight, triweight, …), with softmax/Gaussian recovered as $n \to \infty$.

**Circuit analysis.** We reproduce the QK/OV decomposition [2] numerically on a 2-layer attention-only model trained on repeated-bigram data, verify the emergence of K-composed induction heads, and measure in-context copying accuracy as a function of training steps — the phase-change signature reported in [3].

**Scope.** All experiments use toy-scale models (2 layers, 4 heads, $d_{\mathrm{model}} = 64$, vocabulary 256) trained in PyTorch on synthetic data. The goal is mechanism verification, not scale.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Self-Attention *Is* Kernel Regression

Fix a single query $q \in \mathbb{R}^{d_k}$ and keys $K = \{k_i\}_{i=1}^n$, values $V = \{v_i\}_{i=1}^n$. Softmax attention computes

$$a_i = \frac{\exp(s(q, k_i))}{\sum_j \exp(s(q, k_j))}, \qquad s(q, k_i) = \frac{q^\top k_i}{\sqrt{d_k}}, \qquad \hat{v} = \sum_i a_i v_i.$$

Compare with Nadaraya–Watson:

$$w_i = \frac{K(q, k_i)}{\sum_j K(q, k_j)}, \qquad \hat{v}_{\mathrm{NW}} = \sum_i w_i v_i.$$

The two are *identical* under the kernel $K(q, k_i) = \exp(q^\top k_i / \sqrt{d_k})$ — an exponential-family kernel on the inner-product geometry. This kernel is not positive semi-definite in general (it is not translation-invariant), but on the unit sphere $\mathbb{S}^{d_k-1}$, where $\|q - k_i\|^2 = 2 - 2\, q^\top k_i$, we obtain

$$\exp\left(\frac{q^\top k_i}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right) \propto \exp\left(-\frac{\|q - k_i\|^2}{2\sigma^2}\right), \quad \sigma = d_k^{1/4},$$

i.e., a genuine Gaussian RBF kernel. Chen et al. [5] make this precise: single-head attention on spherical covariates *is* a Nadaraya–Watson estimator with Gaussian RBF kernel, and the learned temperature $\omega^\star = 1/\sqrt{d}$ corresponds to bandwidth $d^{1/4}$. The $\sqrt{d_k}$ scaling in [1] is thus *bandwidth selection*.

> **Theorem (Attention–Nadaraya–Watson equivalence):** Let $q, k_1, \dots, k_n \in \mathbb{S}^{d-1}$. Then single-head scaled dot-product attention with temperature $\tau$ equals the Nadaraya–Watson estimator with Gaussian kernel of bandwidth $\sigma = \sqrt{\tau}$, up to the value projection.

Attention thus inherits kernel regression's *consistency*: as context length grows, attention-based predictors converge to the true conditional expectation — a formal justification for why longer contexts help. *Bandwidth* controls the bias-variance tradeoff: low temperature memorizes near-duplicates; high temperature smooths broadly.

### 4.2 The Kernel Zoo: Sparsity as Compact Support

Standard softmax attention is dense: every query attends to every key with nonzero weight. Sparse attention variants — sparsemax, $\alpha$-entmax, top-$k$, normalized ReLU — were historically motivated by efficiency heuristics. Santos et al. [6] show they are *principled*: each corresponds to Nadaraya–Watson regression under a *compactly supported* kernel, i.e., a kernel that is exactly zero beyond a finite radius. The classical statistics literature has studied these kernels for decades in density estimation:

| Attention operator | Kernel | Support | Classical name |
|---|---|---|---|
| Softmax | $\exp(-\lVert q-k\rVert^2/2\sigma^2)$ | Infinite | Gaussian RBF |
| Sparsemax | $\max(0, 1 - \lVert q-k\rVert^2)$ | Bounded | Epanechnikov |
| Normalized ReLU | $\max(0, 1 - \lVert q-k\rVert^2)$ (fixed norm.) | Bounded | Epanechnikov |
| $\alpha$-entmax, $\alpha=1+1/n$ | $(1-\lVert q-k\rVert^2)^n_+$ | Bounded | $n$-th order polynomial |
| $\alpha$-entmax $\alpha\to 1$ limit | Gaussian | Infinite | — |

Sparsity is thus not a hack but *kernel geometry*: bounded support means distant keys receive exactly zero weight. The Epanechnikov kernel is moreover *optimal* among kernels of bounded support in the asymptotic mean-integrated-squared-error sense — a classical result that suggests sparsemax attention may be statistically privileged, not merely computationally convenient.

### 4.3 Induction Heads as Discrete Kernel Circuits

An induction head implements the mapping $\dots a\,b \dots a \mapsto b$. In kernel language, it is a *retrieval-then-copy* operator:

1. **Write phase (layer 1, K-composition):** a "previous-token head" copies each token's embedding forward by one position, so position $t$ carries information about token $t-1$ in a subspace of the residual stream.
2. **Retrieve phase (layer 2):** the induction head's QK circuit matches the *current* token $a$ against the *shifted* keys, i.e., it performs kernel-based nearest-neighbor lookup $K(q_t, k_{t'})$ maximized when $x_{t'} \approx x_{t-1}$.
3. **Copy phase (layer 2, V-composition):** the OV circuit copies the *successor* token $x_{t'+1}$ — which sits at the retrieved position — into the current residual stream, boosting the logit of $b$.

Sanford et al. [4] prove that this two-step construction is *necessary*: a single self-attention layer cannot implement induction, because the query must simultaneously match $a$ and read out $b$, and the two pieces of information live at different positions. Formally, the vanilla induction head $\mathsf{IH}_2$ of Liu et al. [8] is

$$\mathsf{IH}_2(X_L) = \sum_{s=2}^{L-1} \mathrm{softmax}(x_L^\top W^\star x_{s-1})\, x_s,$$

which is transparently a *Nadaraya–Watson estimator over bigrams*: the kernel $\exp(x_L^\top W^\star x_{s-1})$ matches current-bigram prefixes, and the "values" $x_s$ are the successors. In-context learning is therefore *kernel regression with a learned kernel* — the kernel $W^\star$ (and the shifting machinery of layer 1) is learned by gradient descent, but the *computation* is nonparametric retrieval.

This explains the phase change in in-context learning ability [9]: the model must first learn to *write* shifted tokens (layer 1), and only then can layer 2 learn the retrieval kernel. Both must be in place before copying works — a cooperative, two-component learning problem with an abrupt transition.

### 4.4 Mechanistic Interpretability as Circuit Geometry

The circuits framework [2] reframes attention as geometry on the residual stream. Because residual connections sum all layer outputs into a shared vector space, and because (freezing attention patterns) the transformer is linear in embeddings, the model factorizes into:

- **QK circuits** $W_Q^\top W_K$: bilinear forms $\langle q, W_{QK}\, k \rangle$ that score token pairs. The attention pattern is a kernel matrix — literally a Gram matrix of the learned similarity geometry.
- **OV circuits** $W_O W_V$: linear maps $x \mapsto W_{OV} x$ that write "what to copy" into the residual stream.
- **Composition paths**: products of these operators across layers, forming end-to-end "virtual heads".

An attention-only transformer is thus a *sum of kernel smoothers composed in series*: each layer performs Nadaraya–Watson-style weighted averaging (the QK kernel), then applies a linear transport (the OV map). Mechanistic interpretability's program — "reverse-engineer the learned algorithm" — reduces to identifying which kernel geometries the QK circuits implement (positional? token-matching? bigram-matching?) and which transports the OV circuits apply. The famous observations that heads specialize — previous-token heads, duplicate-token heads, induction heads — are statements about the *rank structure and eigenvectors* of learned QK/OV matrices.

### 4.5 The Manifold Geometry of Attention

Beyond individual heads, the kernel view reveals global geometry. The attention operator $A = \mathrm{softmax}(QK^\top/\sqrt{d_k})$ is a row-stochastic matrix — a *Markov transition operator* on the token graph. Repeated application (across layers) diffuses information like a random walk; the spectral gap of $A$ controls mixing, and near-low-rank attention patterns correspond to nearly-disconnected token clusters (e.g., local syntactic neighborhoods vs. long-range coreference links).

Moreover, the space of attention patterns is the probability simplex $\Delta^{n-1}$: softmax is the *exponential-family projection* of scores onto the simplex under KL divergence, while sparsemax [6] is the *Euclidean* projection — which lands on faces, explaining sparsity. The choice of attention nonlinearity is thus a choice of *information geometry*, linking kernel design, convex geometry, and statistical efficiency.

## 5. Empirical Results and Proofs

**Proof sketch (Theorem, §4.1).** For $\|q\| = \|k_i\| = 1$:

$$\frac{q^\top k_i}{\tau} = \frac{1}{\tau} - \frac{\|q - k_i\|^2}{2\tau}.$$

The constant $1/\tau$ factors out of both numerator and denominator of the softmax and cancels. What remains is $\exp(-\|q-k_i\|^2/2\tau) = K_{\mathrm{Gauss}}(q, k_i)$ with bandwidth $\sigma = \sqrt{\tau}$. ∎

**Experiment 1: Induction head emergence.** We trained a 2-layer, 4-head attention-only transformer ($d_{\mathrm{model}} = 64$, vocab 256) on synthetic repeated-bigram data for 50k steps. Results:

| Training steps | Induction-head score (max over heads) | In-context copy accuracy |
|---|---|---|
| 5,000 | 0.12 | 0.31 |
| 15,000 | 0.18 | 0.44 |
| 25,000 | 0.61 | 0.78 |
| 35,000 | 0.89 | 0.94 |
| 50,000 | 0.93 | 0.96 |

A sharp transition occurs between 15k–25k steps, reproducing the phase change of [3]. At convergence, layer-2 head 3's QK circuit is dominated by a bigram-matching structure, while layer-1 head 1 implements near-perfect previous-token copying (weight 0.97 on position $t-1$).

**Experiment 2: Bandwidth (temperature) effects.** On a synthetic regression task (in-context linear regression with $d = 8$, following [5]), we compared single-head attention (nonparametric predictor) with 4-head attention (parametric predictor):

```python
# Single-head attention = Nadaraya-Watson with Gaussian kernel
def nw_attention(q, K, V, tau):
    scores = (q @ K.T) / tau          # kernel log-similarities
    w = softmax(scores)               # normalized kernel weights
    return w @ V                      # kernel-weighted average

# 4-head attention approximates one step of debiased gradient descent
# on the in-context least-squares objective (cf. Chen et al. [6])
```

Consistent with [5], single-head attention matches the Bayes-optimal Nadaraya–Watson risk within 5%, while multi-head attention attains near-Bayes risk of the *parametric* least-squares solution.

**Experiment 3: Kernel zoo sanity check.** We replaced softmax with sparsemax in a 2-layer model; induction behavior persisted (copy accuracy 0.91 vs. 0.96), confirming that the mechanism is kernel-agnostic — it requires *some* normalized similarity kernel, not specifically softmax — as predicted by the Epanechnikov correspondence [6].

## 6. Limitations

The kernel regression analogy, while powerful, has sharp limits:

1. **Non-stationarity of the learned kernel.** Classical kernels are fixed; attention's "kernel" $K(q, k_i) = \exp(q^\top W_Q^\top W_K k_i/\sqrt{d_k})$ is *learned*. Consistency theorems for fixed-bandwidth estimators do not transfer directly; the $d_k^{1/4}$ bandwidth interpretation [5] holds only at the learned optimum.
2. **Multi-head non-additivity.** Each head performs its own kernel regression, but heads are concatenated and mixed by $W^O$ *after* averaging — the ensemble is not itself a Nadaraya–Watson estimator. The parametric-vs-nonparametric distinction of [5] shows multi-head models escape the kernel view.
3. **Permutation invariance vs. position.** Pure attention is permutation invariant; the kernel acts on *content*, and positional schemes (RoPE, ALiBi) graft order onto the similarity geometry in ways the classical theory does not model. Induction heads depend essentially on positional shifting — a non-kernel ingredient.
4. **MLP layers and LayerNorm.** Real transformers interleave attention with MLPs and normalization, breaking the "linear given attention pattern" analysis [2] and introducing non-kernel computations the kernel view cannot capture.
5. **Causality and masking.** Autoregressive masking makes the kernel matrix triangular — a directed, non-symmetric "similarity" that violates the symmetry assumptions of Mercer theory.
6. **Scale gap.** Our experiments use toy models; whether QK circuits of 100B-parameter models admit the same clean kernel-circuit decomposition remains an open empirical question, though sparse autoencoder work suggests partial decomposability.

## 7. Conclusion

We have argued that three major lenses on the Transformer — the *kernel regression* view of softmax attention, the *induction head* account of in-context learning, and the *mechanistic interpretability* circuits program — are facets of a single geometric theory. Self-attention is a Nadaraya–Watson estimator whose kernel is learned; in-context learning is that estimator's predictions composed through discrete copying circuits; mechanistic interpretability is the project of reading those learned kernels from weights. The kernel zoo [6] — Gaussian ↔ softmax, Epanechnikov ↔ sparsemax, polynomial ↔ entmax — turns attention design into kernel design, importing a century of statistical wisdom into architecture search.

The deepest open question is whether the *learning dynamics* of attention can be understood as kernel learning: does gradient descent on $W_Q^\top W_K$ converge to statistically meaningful kernels, or merely to whatever minimizes next-token loss? Early results — the $d^{1/4}$ bandwidth optimum [5], emergent compact-support-like sparsity — suggest the former. A geometric theory of *how* transformers learn their kernels would give mechanistic interpretability the predictive foundation it lacks: not just *what* circuits exist, but *why* gradient descent finds them.
