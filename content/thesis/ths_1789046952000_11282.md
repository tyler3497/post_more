# Conflict-Driven Clause Learning: 1UIP Learning, VSIDS Branching, Restarts, and Resolution Proof Complexity

## Abstract

Conflict-Driven Clause Learning (CDCL) is the dominant paradigm for propositional satisfiability (SAT) solving, underpinning industrial solvers capable of deciding instances with millions of variables and clauses. This thesis develops a complete treatment of modern CDCL, tracing its lineage from the Davis–Putnam–Logemann–Loveland (DPLL) procedure through the GRASP framework to the engineering breakthroughs of Chaff and MiniSat. We present the formal machinery of unit propagation, implication graphs, and the 1UIP (first unique implication point) clause-learning scheme that guarantees an *asserting* clause after every conflict, together with the VSIDS/EVSIDS family of activity-based branching heuristics and aggressive restart schedules such as Luby's universal sequence. The second half of the thesis situates CDCL within proof complexity: we show how CDCL search traces induce resolution refutations, survey exponential lower bounds for resolution (pigeonhole, Tseitin, clique–coloring), and describe the DRAT clausal proof format that allows solvers to emit machine-checkable certificates of unsatisfiability. We close with a sober assessment of the paradigm's limitations — clause-database blowup, heuristic brittleness on crafted benchmarks, and the gap between regular and general resolution — and identify open directions for theory and practice.

## 1. Introduction

The Boolean satisfiability problem (SAT) occupies a singular position in computer science: the canonical NP-complete problem [Cook, 1971], the lingua franca of bounded model checking, hardware verification, planning, and scheduling, and the testing ground for nearly every advance in automated reasoning [9]. Despite its worst-case intractability, industrial SAT instances of staggering size are decided routinely. The credit belongs overwhelmingly to the *conflict-driven clause learning* architecture.

The story begins with the Davis–Putnam–Logemann–Loveland procedure [1][2], a backtracking search with unit propagation whose modern incarnations are still recognizable. GRASP [3] introduced conflict analysis and non-chronological backtracking, transforming backtracking into *learning*: every conflict teaches the solver a new clause, pruning vast regions of the search space permanently. Chaff [4] then supplied the two engineering ideas — the *two-watched-literal* scheme for O(1)-amortized unit propagation and the *VSIDS* branching heuristic — that turned CDCL into an industrial tool. MiniSat [6] distilled these ideas into a minimal, extensible reference implementation that became the substrate for hundreds of research solvers.

> **Theorem (informal, CDCL completeness):** Let *F* be a CNF formula. A CDCL solver with exhaustive search, sound clause learning, and unbounded restarts terminates and returns SAT iff *F* is satisfiable and UNSAT otherwise.

This thesis makes the following contributions:

1. A self-contained exposition of the CDCL main loop, implication graphs, and the 1UIP learning scheme, with a worked example of conflict analysis as iterated resolution [5].
2. A comparative analysis of VSIDS and EVSIDS branching, with the decay dynamics formalized as an exponential moving average over conflict participation.
3. A treatment of restart policies — heavy-tailed models of runtime distributions and Luby's near-optimal universal sequence — and their interplay with learnt-clause quality metrics such as LBD.
4. An introduction to proof complexity relevant to CDCL: CDCL as resolution search, exponential lower bounds for regular resolution, the pool and tree-like restrictions, and DRAT as the de facto certified proof format [7][8].

## 2. Background

### 2.1 Propositional Satisfiability and CNF

A *literal* is a Boolean variable *x* or its negation ¬*x*. A *clause* is a disjunction of literals; a formula in *conjunctive normal form* (CNF) is a conjunction of clauses. A clause is *unit* under a partial assignment σ if all but one of its literals are falsified by σ, forcing the remaining literal to true. The *unit clause rule* — repeatedly applying this forced inference — is called *Boolean constraint propagation* (BCP) or *unit propagation*.

### 2.2 From DP to DPLL

The Davis–Putnam procedure [1] eliminated variables by *resolution*: for variable *x*, replace clauses containing *x* and clauses containing ¬*x* with all pairwise resolvents. While complete, this *resolution rule* can square the formula. Davis, Logemann, and Loveland [2] replaced variable elimination with *splitting*: pick a variable, assign it, propagate, and recurse. This is the DPLL procedure — depth-first backtracking with unit propagation — and its correctness rests on a simple lemma:

> **Lemma (Soundness of splitting):** *F* is satisfiable iff *F*|ₓ₌₀ is satisfiable or *F*|ₓ₌₁ is satisfiable, where *F*|ₓ₌ᵥ denotes the formula after unit propagation of *x = v*.

DPLL's chronic weakness is *thrashing*: the same conflict is rediscovered exponentially often because backtracking discards all information about *why* the conflict occurred.

### 2.3 GRASP: Conflict Analysis Enters the Picture

Marques-Silva and Sakallah's GRASP [3] diagnosed thrashing and prescribed *conflict diagnosis*: when a conflict is reached, analyze the implication graph to derive a *conflict clause* — a clause falsified by the current trail that is implied by the original formula — and backtrack *non-chronologically* to the earliest decision level responsible. GRASP's learning schemes (rel-sat, UIP-based) are the direct ancestors of the 1UIP scheme that Chaff popularized.

## 3. Methodology

### 3.1 The CDCL Main Loop

Modern CDCL (in the MiniSat presentation [6]) is captured by the following loop:

```python
def cdcl(formula):
    trail = []            # assignment stack: literals in order
    dl    = 0             # decision level
    learnt_db = []
    while True:
        conflict = unit_propagate(formula, trail)
        if conflict is not None:
            if dl == 0:
                return "UNSAT"
            clause, level = analyze_conflict(trail, conflict)  # 1UIP
            backtrack(level)              # non-chronological
            trail.append(asserting_literal(clause))
            restart_policy.maybe_restart()
        elif all_variables_assigned(trail):
            return "SAT", model(trail)
        else:
            v = pick_branching_variable()   # VSIDS / EVSIDS
            dl += 1
            trail.append(decide(v))
```

Each iteration performs one of three actions — *propagate*, *analyze-and-learn*, or *decide* — and the invariant is that the trail is always a consistent partial assignment.

### 3.2 The Implication Graph

During unit propagation, every propagated literal *l* records its *antecedent clause* `ante[l]`: the clause that became unit and forced *l*. The resulting directed acyclic graph — decision literals as sources, propagations as internal nodes, and the conflicting clause κ as sink — is the *implication graph*. Conflict analysis is graph surgery: it selects a *cut* separating the decision literals from the conflict and derives the clause corresponding to the cut's frontier via iterated resolution.

### 3.3 Unit Propagation: The Two-Watched-Literal Scheme

Up to 90% of CDCL runtime is spent in unit propagation [4]. The naive approach visits every clause on every assignment. Chaff's *two-watched-literal* scheme [4] instead maintains, per clause, two *watched* literals with the invariant: as long as both watched literals are unassigned, the clause cannot be unit or conflicting. Assignment of a literal ℓ requires visiting only clauses *watching* ℓ — a small subset — and when a watched literal is falsified, the solver seeks a replacement among the unwatched literals, or derives unit/conflict. Unassignment during backtrack costs *nothing*, since the invariant never breaks on undo. This laziness is what makes modern BCP fast.

```python
def propagate(clause_db, watches, assignment):
    while propagation_queue:
        lit = propagation_queue.pop()
        for clause in watches[neg(lit)]:       # only clauses watching ~lit
            other = clause.other_watched(lit)
            if assignment[other] is True:
                continue                        # clause satisfied
            for cand in clause.literals:
                if cand not in (lit, other) and not assignment.falsified(cand):
                    clause.replace_watched(lit, cand)
                    watches[neg(cand)].append(clause)
                    break
            else:
                if assignment.falsified(other):
                    return clause               # conflict
                assignment[other] = True        # unit propagation
                clause.antecedent = clause
                propagation_queue.append(other)
    return None
```

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 The 1UIP Scheme: Conflict Analysis as Iterated Resolution

A *unique implication point* (UIP) at decision level *d* is a literal *u* at level *d* that *dominates* the conflict node κ: every path from the level-*d* decision literal to κ passes through *u*. The *first* UIP (1UIP) is the one closest to κ. The 1UIP learning scheme [5] resolves the conflict clause backward through antecedents until exactly one level-*d* literal remains:

```python
def analyze_conflict(trail, conflict_clause):
    learned = set(conflict_clause.literals)
    seen = set(); counter = 0
    p = None; dl = current_decision_level()
    # resolve until exactly one literal at level dl remains (the 1UIP)
    while True:
        for lit in learned:
            if lit not in seen and level(lit) == dl:
                seen.add(lit); counter += 1
        p = last_assigned(seen)               # traverse trail backwards
        learned.remove(p)
        learned |= set(antecedent(p).literals) - {neg(p)}
        counter -= 1
        if counter == 0:                      # p is the 1UIP
            break
    backtrack_level = max(level(l) for l in learned)  # asserting level
    return frozenset(learned), backtrack_level
```

The learned clause is *asserting*: after backtracking to the asserting level, the clause is unit and immediately forces ¬*p* (the negation of the 1UIP), driving search away from the explored region. Crucially, the clause is derived by *resolution* from existing clauses, hence logically implied — learning is sound. The empirical triumph of 1UIP over alternatives (decision scheme, all-UIP) is that its clauses are short, asserting, and empirically "high quality" [5].

| Learning scheme | Clause property | Empirical behavior |
|---|---|---|
| Decision | Contains only decision literals | Long clauses, weak pruning |
| All-UIP | Resolves past every UIP | Shortest clauses, expensive analysis |
| **1UIP** | Exactly one literal at current level | Asserting, best cost/benefit [5] |
| Rel-sat [3] | Cut at decision-variable frontier | Precursor; longer clauses |

### 4.2 VSIDS and EVSIDS: Activity-Based Branching

Static variable orders (MOMs, Jeroslow–Wang) were displaced by Chaff's *Variable State Independent Decaying Sum* (VSIDS) [4]: each variable carries an *activity* score, bumped when the variable participates in conflict analysis, with all scores periodically *decayed* (multiplied by 1/*c*, *c > 1*). The solver branches on the unassigned variable of maximal activity. Decay implements a *recency bias*: recent conflicts dominate older ones, focusing search on the current "active" subproblem.

MiniSat's EVSIDS [6] refines this into an additive bump with multiplicative decay on a lazy heap:

```python
class EVSIDS:
    def __init__(self, nvars, decay=0.95):
        self.activity = [0.0] * nvars
        self.bump = 1.0
        self.decay = decay
    def on_conflict(self, learnt_clause, analyzed_vars):
        for v in set(learnt_clause) | analyzed_vars:
            self.activity[v] += self.bump      # additive bump
        self.bump *= 1.0 / self.decay          # multiplicative decay
        if self.bump > 1e100:                  # rescale to avoid overflow
            for v in range(len(self.activity)):
                self.activity[v] *= 1e-100
            self.bump *= 1e-100
```

The decay constant is itself adaptive in modern solvers (e.g., Glucose raises decay on "good" conflicts). VSIDS's surprising effectiveness was only partially understood until Liang et al. showed it approximates a *community-structure* detector on the variable-incidence graph — activity concentrates within tightly coupled communities of the formula.

### 4.3 Restarts: Taming Heavy Tails

CDCL runtime distributions on industrial instances are *heavy-tailed*: most runs finish quickly, but some wander for orders of magnitude longer. Randomized restarts — resetting the trail to level 0 while *keeping* learnt clauses — provably mitigate heavy tails [Gomes et al., 1998]. Chaff and successors restart aggressively. The theoretical gold standard is *Luby's universal sequence* [Luby, Sinclair, Zuckerman, 1993]:

> **Theorem (Luby et al.):** For any Las Vegas algorithm with unknown runtime distribution, restarting at intervals given by the Luby sequence 1, 1, 2, 1, 1, 2, 4, 1, 1, 2, 1, 1, 2, 4, 8, … is optimal up to a logarithmic factor.

Modern solvers (Glucose, CaDiCaL) use *dynamic* restarts driven by search statistics — e.g., restart when the recent average LBD exceeds the global average by a threshold — but Luby remains the canonical static schedule and the theoretical justification for restart-heavy CDCL.

### 4.4 Clause Database Management and LBD

Unbounded learning eventually chokes the solver: each new clause costs propagation work forever. Modern solvers therefore *delete* learnt clauses aggressively, using the *Literal Block Distance* (LBD) metric — the number of distinct decision levels in a clause — as a quality proxy. Clauses with LBD ≤ 2 ("glue clauses") are kept permanently; high-LBD clauses are the first deleted. This single heuristic, introduced in Glucose, is arguably the most important CDCL advance of the 2010s: it bounds memory while preserving the clauses that glue the search together.

## 5. Empirical Results / Proofs

### 5.1 CDCL Simulates Resolution

The learned clauses of a CDCL run form a *resolution refutation* of an unsatisfiable formula: each conflict analysis is a resolution derivation, and the empty clause is derived at the top-level conflict. Conversely, Pipatsrisawat and Darwiche showed that CDCL with 1UIP, non-chronological backtracking, and restarts can *polynomially simulate general resolution* — the proof system of unrestricted resolution DAGs. This places CDCL among the strongest known proof systems for SAT, and it explains the paradigm's empirical dominance: any short resolution proof is, in principle, discoverable.

### 5.2 Exponential Lower Bounds: What CDCL Cannot Do Fast

Simulation cuts both ways: resolution lower bounds become CDCL lower bounds. The landmark results:

- **Pigeonhole principle** (PHPₙⁿ⁺¹): Haken [1985] proved that every resolution refutation of the pigeonhole formulas has size 2^Ω(n). CDCL without extended reasoning inherits this exponential cost.
- **Tseitin formulas** on expander graphs: Urquhart [1987] showed exponential resolution lower bounds, separating regular resolution from stronger systems.
- **Clique–coloring**: Pudlák [1997] established exponential lower bounds via feasible interpolation.

> **Theorem (Haken, 1985):** Any resolution refutation of PHPₙⁿ⁺¹ requires size at least 2^{n/20}.

The practical consequence is sharp: on *crafted* combinatorial benchmarks (PHP, Tseitin, parity), CDCL solvers hit exponential walls. The paradigm's industrial success is therefore not about proof-system strength in the worst case but about the *structure* of real-world formulas — bounded treewidth, community structure, and abundant unit propagation — that CDCL exploits heuristically.

### 5.3 DRAT: Certifying Unsatisfiability

A solver claiming UNSAT should not be trusted blindly — a bug could declare a satisfiable instance unsatisfiable. The modern answer is *clausal proof logging*: the solver emits every learnt clause (and deletions) in the **DRAT** (Delete Resolution Asymmetric Tautology) format [7], and an independent checker (DRAT-trim) verifies each step in polynomial time. DRAT generalizes RUP (reverse unit propagation): a clause *C* has the RAT property if, for some pivot literal *l ∈ C*, all resolvents of *C* with clauses containing ¬*l* are RUP-tautologies. Since 2014 the SAT competitions have *required* DRAT proofs for UNSAT answers, and LRAT [8] adds dependency hints enabling formally verified checkers. The result is a trustworthy pipeline: an unverified, blazing-fast solver plus a small verified checker [9].

## 6. Limitations

1. **Clause-database pressure.** Even with LBD-based deletion, learnt clauses accumulate; propagation over hundreds of thousands of learnt clauses dominates runtime on hard instances, and deletion policies remain heuristic with known failure modes.
2. **Heuristic brittleness.** VSIDS/EVSIDS excel on industrial formulas with community structure but degenerate on random and crafted instances, where look-ahead or local-search solvers dominate. Phase saving and restarts partially compensate but do not close the gap.
3. **Resolution ceiling.** CDCL polynomially simulates *general* resolution but not stronger systems: extended resolution, cutting planes, and symmetry reasoning remain out of reach, as witnessed by exponential lower bounds on PHP and Tseitin formulas. Symmetry-breaking and Gaussian elimination are bolted on (CryptoMiniSat) rather than native.
4. **Proof-logging overhead.** DRAT emission and checking add real cost — sometimes doubling total wall-clock time — and inprocessing techniques (variable elimination, blocked-clause removal) complicate faithful proof logging, requiring care to preserve the RAT invariant [7].
5. **Theory gap.** The precise conditions under which VSIDS succeeds, the optimal restart schedule for real distributions, and the power of CDCL *without* restarts (known to be strictly weaker: *pool* resolution lower bounds) remain active research questions [9].

---

## 7. Conclusion

CDCL's arc — from DPLL's backtracking [1][2] through GRASP's conflict diagnosis [3], Chaff's watched literals and VSIDS [4], the 1UIP scheme [5], MiniSat's minimalism [6], to certified DRAT proofs [7][8] — is the story of SAT solving becoming an engineering discipline without abandoning its proof-theoretic foundations [9]. The 1UIP clause is a resolution step; the VSIDS activity is a recency-weighted conflict statistic; the restart schedule is a hedge against heavy tails; and the DRAT log is a checkable certificate. The paradigm's limitations are equally principled: wherever resolution is exponentially weak, CDCL is exponentially slow, and no heuristic tuning escapes Haken's bound. The frontier lies in proof systems *beyond* resolution — propagation-redundant (PR) reasoning, symmetry, and verified proof checking — integrated without sacrificing the brutal efficiency that two watched literals bought. CDCL endures not because it is optimal, but because it is the best-known compromise between proof-theoretic power and engineering reality.
