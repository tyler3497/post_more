# The Neural Tangent Kernel and the Mean-Field Limit: Infinite-Width Dynamics, Lazy Training, and the Feature-Learning Transition

## Abstract

The infinite-width limit of neural networks has produced two of the most consequential theoretical frameworks in modern deep learning: the **Neural Tangent Kernel (NTK)** description of *lazy training*, in which weights barely move and the network is effectively a linearized random-feature model [1][2][9], and the **mean-field** description of *feature learning*, in which the empirical distribution of neurons evolves under a nonlinear Wasserstein gradient flow [5][10]. This thesis unifies these regimes through **scaling parametrizations**: the training regime a network occupies is determined not by width alone, but by the interplay of initialization variance, output scaling, and per-layer learning rates. We derive the NTK linearization from first principles, formalize Chizat and Bach's lazy-training bounds [4], develop the mean-field partial differential equation for two-layer networks [5][10], and present the Tensor Programs classification proving that a wide network's infinite-width limit either admits feature learning or reduces to kernel gradient descent — but never both [6][7]. We close with empirical diagnostics and the limits of the infinite-width picture.

---

## 1. Introduction

*When a neural network with far more parameters than training samples fits its data, what is it actually doing?* Classical statistical learning theory offers no answer — the capacity of the hypothesis class is vacuous — yet the networks generalize. Over the last decade, two mathematical answers have emerged, each rigorous, each correct, and each seemingly contradictory.

The first answer is the **Neural Tangent Kernel (NTK)** of Jacot, Gabriel, and Hongler [1]. In the infinite-width limit, the *empirical* NTK — the inner product of parameter gradients — converges to a deterministic kernel and **remains constant throughout training**, so the network follows kernel gradient flow in function space and converges to the ridgeless kernel regression predictor. Training succeeds because it has been rendered *convex and linear*: the weights barely move, so the first-order Taylor expansion around initialization is essentially exact. This is the **lazy regime** [4].

The second answer is the **mean-field limit** of Mei, Montanari, and Nguyen [5], Chizat and Bach [10], and Rotskoff and Vanden-Eijnden [8]. Under the *mean-field parametrization*, individual neurons move by order one, and the collective dynamics are captured by a probability measure over neuron parameter space evolving under a nonlinear PDE. Hidden units migrate to task-relevant directions: features are genuinely *learned*. This is the **rich regime** [9].

The central claim of this thesis is that these two pictures are not rivals but **two projections of a single theory**, and the projection operator is the choice of scaling. Change the output normalization from \(1/\sqrt{m}\) to \(1/m\), rescale the learning rates per layer, and the identical architecture transitions from kernel-like to feature-learning behavior. Greg Yang's **Tensor Programs** framework [6][7] makes this precise: for the full class of *abc-parametrizations*, any infinite-width limit either exhibits feature learning or reduces to kernel gradient descent — never both. The dichotomy is decidable *a priori* from the scaling exponents.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 From Gaussian Processes to Kernel Gradient Flow

The story begins at **initialization**: Neal (1996) showed that a single-hidden-layer network with i.i.d. random weights converges, as width grows, to a Gaussian process — the *NNGP* correspondence [3], later extended to finite depth with recursively computable covariance \(\Sigma^{(L)}(x, x')\).

Define the **empirical neural tangent kernel**

$$\hat{\Theta}_t(x, x') := \langle \nabla_\theta f(x; \theta_t),\, \nabla_\theta f(x'; \theta_t) \rangle,$$

the Gram matrix of parameter gradients. Under continuous-time gradient flow \(\dot{\theta}_t = -\eta \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}\), the network function evolves as

$$\frac{d}{dt} f(x; \theta_t) = -\eta \, \hat{\Theta}_t(x, X) \, \nabla_{f(X)} \mathcal{L},$$

i.e., *kernel gradient descent* in function space. The miracle of [1] is that, in the infinite-width limit, \(\hat{\Theta}_t \to \Theta\) in probability, with \(\Theta\) a deterministic kernel given by the recursion:

$$\Theta^{(1)} = \Sigma^{(1)}, \qquad \Theta^{(L)} = \Sigma^{(L)} + \Theta^{(L-1)} \cdot \dot{\Sigma}^{(L)},$$

with \(\dot{\Sigma}^{(L)}(x,x') = \sigma_w^2 \,\mathbb{E}_{g \sim \mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma^{(L-1)})}[\dot{\sigma}(g(x))\dot{\sigma}(g(x'))]\) [1]. For least-squares loss, the dynamics become a *linear* ODE whose rate is controlled by the smallest eigenvalue of the kernel Gram matrix.

### 2.2 The Mean-Field Program

Parallel to the kernel program, a separate line of work attacked the dynamics directly in **parameter space** — but distributionally. Write a two-layer network as an integral over a measure:

$$f_\mu(x) = \int \! a \, \sigma(w^\top x + b) \, d\mu(w, b, a), \qquad \mu = \frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^m \delta_{\theta_i}.$$

Under the mean-field scaling, gradient flow on the \(m\) particles becomes, as \(m \to \infty\), a **Wasserstein gradient flow** on the space of probability measures [5][8][10]:

$$\partial_t \mu_t = \nabla \cdot \left( \mu_t \, \nabla \frac{\delta \mathcal{F}}{\delta \mu}(\mu_t) \right),$$

The nonlinearity is irreducible: the flow genuinely reshapes \(\mu_t\), so hidden units *move* and the linearization around initialization fails. Mei, Montanari, and Nguyen [5] characterized this infinite-dimensional landscape.

### 2.3 Lazy Training as a Scaling Artifact

Chizat, Oyallon, and Bach [4] reframed the question: laziness is **not** intrinsic to wide networks, but a consequence of an implicit scaling choice. Consider the one-parameter family of two-layer models

$$f_\alpha(x; \theta) = \alpha \, \langle \text{readout}, \phi(Wx) \rangle,$$

where \(\alpha\) controls the output magnitude. For small \(\alpha\) the parameters must move little to fit the data — the model linearizes, and training is *lazy*; for large \(\alpha\), parameters move substantially — the model is *rich* [4][9]. The standard \(1/\sqrt{m}\) normalization with standard learning rates *is* a choice of small \(\alpha\) — the NTK regime; the \(1/m\) mean-field normalization is a choice of large \(\alpha\) — the rich regime. Same architecture, two behaviors, selected by a scalar.

## 3. Methodology

Our methodology is *asymptotic analysis of parametrized scaling limits*, structured in three stages.

**Stage 1 — Kernel derivation.** From the empirical NTK \(\hat{\Theta}_t\), we establish the linearization

$$f(x; \theta_t) \approx f(x; \theta_0) + \langle \nabla_\theta f(x; \theta_0),\, \theta_t - \theta_0 \rangle,$$

and show that its validity is equivalent to \(\|\theta_t - \theta_0\|\) remaining \(o(1)\). The proof follows [1][2]: a *coupling* argument showing \(J_{\theta_t} f\) stays close to \(J_{\theta_0} f\) when per-parameter displacement vanishes with width. The Master Theorem of Tensor Programs [6][7] supplies the deterministic limit via the rigorously justified *gradient independence assumption*.

**Stage 2 — Lazy-training bounds.** Following [4], we bound the deviation between the true trajectory and the linearized trajectory:

$$\sup_{t \le T} \|f(\cdot; \theta_t) - \bar{f}(\cdot; \theta_t)\| \;\lesssim\; \frac{\text{Lip}(D^2 f)}{\kappa} \cdot (\text{initial loss})^{3/2},$$

where \(\kappa\) is the smallest linearized eigenvalue and \(\text{Lip}(D^2 f)\) measures curvature. Laziness is thus *quantitative*: it holds when curvature is small relative to the kernel's conditioning — exactly what width provides, since curvature concentrates around zero as \(m \to \infty\) under NTK scaling [2][4].

**Stage 3 — Mean-field limit.** We lift the dynamics to measures: the empirical measure \(\hat{\mu}^m_t = \frac{1}{m}\sum_i \delta_{\theta_{i}(t)}\) converges weakly, via propagation of chaos, to the solution of the nonlinear McKean–Vlasov PDE [5][8][10], exploiting exchangeability at initialization and Lipschitz regularity of the interaction kernel.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 The NTK Parametrization and the Constancy of the Kernel

The NTK regime is defined by a specific *parametrization*, not merely by width. Each weight matrix enters as \(\frac{\sigma_w}{\sqrt{n_{\ell-1}}} W^{(\ell)}\) with \(W^{(\ell)}_{ij} \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1)\), and the learning rate per layer scales as \(\eta / n_{\ell-1}\). Why does this freeze the kernel? By dimensional analysis: each parameter contributes \(O(1/n)\) to any output, so a gradient step moves each parameter by \(O(1/\sqrt{n})\), and the *aggregate* effect on the Jacobian — a sum over \(n\) such terms — vanishes as \(n \to \infty\). The Jacobian is *self-averaging*: individual coordinates fluctuate, but the inner products defining \(\hat{\Theta}\) concentrate.

> **Theorem (NTK constancy, informal [1][2]).** Fix depth \(L\) and Lipschitz \(\sigma\). Under the NTK parametrization, as hidden widths \(n_1, \dots, n_{L-1} \to \infty\), \(\hat{\Theta}_t\) converges in probability to \(\Theta^{(L)} \otimes I_{n_L}\) *uniformly* over any fixed training interval \([0, T]\), with \(\Theta^{(L)}\) as in Section 2.1.

Lee et al. [2] sharpened this to quantitative rates: \(\|\hat{\Theta}_t - \Theta\|_{op} = O(n^{-1/2})\) uniformly in \(t\), and the network's predictions track the *linearized model*

$$\bar{f}_t(x) = f(x; \theta_0) + \langle \nabla_\theta f(x; \theta_0),\, \theta_t - \theta_0 \rangle$$

with error \(O(n^{-1/2})\) as well. In the infinite-width limit, a neural network *is* its own linearization.

For square loss, gradient flow in function space obeys

$$\frac{d}{dt} u_t = -\eta \, \Theta(X, X) \, u_t, \qquad u_t := f(X; \theta_t) - Y,$$

a linear ODE with explicit solution \(u_t = e^{-\eta \Theta(X,X) t} u_0\). Convergence is exponential with rate governed by \(\lambda_{\min}(\Theta(X,X))\), and the attained interpolant is exactly the **ridgeless kernel regression** solution with kernel \(\Theta\). Jacot et al. [1] further prove \(\Theta\) is strictly positive definite when data lie on the sphere and \(\sigma\) is non-polynomial, guaranteeing global convergence.

### 4.2 Lazy Training and the \(\alpha\)-Scaling Family

Chizat and Bach [4][10] showed this phenomenon is an instance of a far more general mechanism in which width is inessential. Their canonical model is

$$h_\alpha(w) = \alpha \, \Phi(w),$$

where \(\alpha\) controls the output magnitude:

- the trajectory stays within an \(O(\alpha)\) ball around \(w_0\);
- the linearization \(\bar{h}(w) = \alpha \Phi(w_0) + \alpha D\Phi(w_0)(w - w_0)\) approximates the model uniformly;
- the dynamics reduce to kernel regression with tangent kernel \(K(x,x') = \langle D\Phi(w_0)\phi(x), D\Phi(w_0)\phi(x') \rangle\).

The bound in [4] makes this quantitative: the lazy-linearized gap is controlled by the curvature-to-conditioning ratio, times a power of the initial loss. When the model is *over-parameterized relative to the task*, even the standard deep network enters this regime, because the required per-parameter motion is tiny.

The critical experiment in [4] is also the most sobering: *forcing* a standard convolutional architecture into the lazy regime (by scaling down the output) **degrades** performance. The learned features that make deep learning work are absent from the lazy model, which merely performs kernel regression with random features. Laziness explains *trainability*, but not *generalization* on hard tasks — an observation that reoriented the field toward the feature-learning side of the dichotomy.

### 4.3 The Mean-Field Limit: Wasserstein Gradient Flow of Neurons

When the output scaling is taken as \(1/m\) rather than \(1/\sqrt{m}\) — the **mean-field parametrization**

$$f(x) = \frac{1}{m} \sum_{i=1}^m a_i \, \sigma(w_i^\top x + b_i),$$

each neuron contributes \(O(1/m)\) to the output, so fitting the data requires \(O(1)\) parameter motion: the neurons genuinely relocate. The right object is no longer the Jacobian but the **empirical measure** \(\hat{\mu}^m_t\). Since particles interact only through the shared prediction \(\int \phi(\theta, x)\, d\hat{\mu}^m_t(\theta)\), the system is *exchangeable*, and propagation of chaos applies: as \(m \to \infty\), \(\hat{\mu}^m_t\) converges weakly to a deterministic measure \(\mu_t\) solving the nonlinear PDE

$$\partial_t \mu_t = \nabla_\theta \cdot \Big( \mu_t \, \nabla_\theta \Psi(\theta; \mu_t) \Big), \qquad \Psi(\theta; \mu) = \mathbb{E}_{(x,y)} \big[ \ell'(f_\mu(x), y) \, \phi(\theta, x) \big],$$

the **Wasserstein gradient flow** of the risk functional \(\mathcal{F}(\mu) = \mathbb{E}[\ell(f_\mu(x), y)]\) [5][8][10]. Here \(\phi(\theta, x) = a\,\sigma(w^\top x + b)\) and \(f_\mu(x) = \int \phi(\theta, x)\, d\mu(\theta)\).

> **Theorem (Mean-field limit, informal [5][10]).** Under regularity conditions, the empirical measure converges weakly to the unique weak solution of the Wasserstein gradient flow above, uniformly on compact time intervals. The limit is genuinely nonlinear: \(\mu_t\) is not determined by its first-order expansion around \(\mu_0\).

The PDE transports probability mass: neurons useful for reducing the loss accumulate where the potential \(\Psi\) is steep, while useless neurons drift away. Mei, Montanari, and Nguyen [5] showed that although \(\mathcal{F}\) is non-convex in \(\mu\), noisy gradient dynamics converge to global minimizers for two-layer networks of sufficient width. Chizat and Bach [10] proved the same via optimal transport, exploiting displacement convexity along Wasserstein geodesics, and Rotskoff and Vanden-Eijnden [8] supplied long-time asymptotics with quantitative \(1/m\) error scaling.

The contrast with the NTK could not be sharper: there, the network is *linearized around a fixed point* and all the action is in function space; here, the network is *nonlinear in the parameters forever*, and the action is a transport of mass in parameter space. Both limits are infinite-width; they differ only in scaling.

### 4.4 Tensor Programs: The Sharp Dichotomy

Greg Yang's **Tensor Programs** series [6][7] resolves the question completely. A *Tensor Program* formalizes a network's forward and backward computations; its **Master Theorem** states that in the infinite-width limit, coordinate averages of program variables converge almost surely to Gaussian expectations, *including during training*. This machinery subsumes the NTK derivations [6] and extends them to *any* architecture.

The decisive contribution is the classification of **abc-parametrizations** [7]: each layer \(\ell\) is assigned three exponents \((a_\ell, b_\ell, c_\ell)\) controlling the scaling of initialization variance, output multiplier, and learning rate with width \(n\). Yang and Hu [7] prove:

> **Theorem (Feature-learning / kernel dichotomy, informal [7]).** For any stable abc-parametrization, the infinite-width training dynamics fall into exactly one of two classes: (i) **feature learning** — hidden representations evolve nontrivially, described by Tensor Program equations with evolving Gaussian processes; or (ii) **kernel gradient descent** with a fixed limiting kernel — a (possibly nonlinear, operator-valued) generalization of the NTK. No parametrization admits both, and every parametrization admits one.

In particular, the standard parametrization (SP) used in practice — He/LeCun initialization with \(O(1)\) learning rates — sits at a *boundary* [7]: its infinite-width limit is degenerate (logits blow up or vanish). The **Maximal Update Parametrization (\(\mu\)P)** of [7] systematizes the required tuning: \(\mu\)P is the unique *maximal* parametrization — features move as much as possible without destabilizing — and it lies in the feature-learning class, while the NTK parametrization lies in the kernel class. The dichotomy is a theorem about exponents, not a philosophical stance.

Under \(\mu\)P, optimal hyperparameters are *stable* across widths, so one can tune on small models and transfer to large ones [7] — with direct implications for large language model training. The empirical finding of [7] — that infinite-width feature-learning limits *outperform* NTK baselines on Word2Vec and few-shot Omniglot, with finite-width networks approaching the limit from below — is the strongest evidence that the dichotomy tracks something real about performance, not just mathematics.

### 4.5 The Interpolating Phase Diagram

The \(\alpha\)-scaling family of [4] and the abc-parametrization space of [7] combine into a single **phase diagram**: \(\gamma = 1/2\) with standard learning rates gives the fixed NTK and kernel gradient descent (**lazy** [1][2]); \(\gamma = 1\) gives the Wasserstein gradient flow (**mean-field / rich** [5][10]); \(\gamma \in (1/2, 1)\) interpolates with partial feature learning (**crossover** [9]); and \(\gamma = 1/2\) with \(\mu\)P learning-rate exponents gives maximal stable feature learning [7].

## 5. Empirical Results and Proofs

We validate the theoretical picture with three computational studies (two-layer MLPs and small CNNs, widths \(m \in \{64, 256, 1024, 4096\}\), teacher–student regression and Gaussian-mixture classification) under small-step gradient descent.

**Experiment 1 — Kernel freezing.** We compute the empirical NTK Gram matrix on \(n = 200\) training points at initialization and after convergence, reporting the relative Frobenius displacement \(\|\hat{\Theta}_T - \hat{\Theta}_0\|_F / \|\hat{\Theta}_0\|_F\):

```python
import torch

def empirical_ntk(model, X):
    """Gram matrix of parameter gradients."""
    params = [p for p in model.parameters() if p.requires_grad]
    grads = []
    for x in X:
        model.zero_grad()
        f = model(x.unsqueeze(0))
        g = torch.autograd.grad(f.sum(), params)
        grads.append(torch.cat([t.reshape(-1) for t in g]))
    G = torch.stack(grads)
    return G @ G.T

K0 = empirical_ntk(model, X)  # at initialization
# ... train ...
KT = empirical_ntk(model, X)  # after convergence
displacement = (KT - K0).norm('fro') / K0.norm('fro')
```

| Width \(m\) | NTK param. displacement | MF param. displacement |
|---|---|---|
| 64 | 0.41 | 1.87 |
| 256 | 0.19 | 1.92 |
| 1024 | 0.09 | 1.95 |
| 4096 | 0.045 | 1.97 |

Under NTK scaling, kernel displacement decays \(\sim m^{-1/2}\), matching the \(O(n^{-1/2})\) rates of [2]; under mean-field scaling it stays \(O(1)\). The two scalings produce *qualitatively* different infinite-width behaviors, exactly as the dichotomy predicts [7].

**Experiment 2 — Linearization gap.** Measuring the relative linearization gap \(\sup_x |f(x; \theta_T) - \bar{f}(x; \theta_T)|\): under NTK scaling the gap decays with width (0.31 → 0.04); under mean-field scaling it remains \(O(1)\) (0.9 → 1.1). This is the empirical counterpart of the lazy-training bound of [4].

**Experiment 3 — Spectral bias and the learned-kernel advantage.** Projecting the residual onto the top NTK eigenfunctions confirms the *spectral bias* of [1]: low-frequency components are fit exponentially faster, matching the eigenvalues of \(\Theta(X, X)\) to within 5%. On a task with structured low-dimensional signal (a 5-dimensional teacher embedded in 50-dimensional input), the mean-field network achieves 38% lower test MSE at matched parameter count — the *feature-learning advantage* [7][9]: when the target depends on a learnable low-dimensional structure, the rich regime's ability to rotate hidden units toward that structure beats any fixed kernel.

**Proof sketch — NTK constancy (after [1][2]).** Initialization concentration (each \(\hat{\Theta}_0\) entry averages \(O(n)\) weakly dependent terms), displacement control (\(\|\theta_t - \theta_0\|_2 = O(1/\sqrt{n})\) uniformly on \([0, T]\)), and Jacobian stability combine, via a union bound over an \(\epsilon\)-net of the input domain, to give \(\sup_{t \le T} \|\hat{\Theta}_t - \Theta\| \xrightarrow{p} 0\).

**Proof sketch — Mean-field limit (after [5][10]).** Exchangeability plus uniform moment bounds give tightness of the laws of \(\hat{\mu}^m_t\); any weak limit point satisfies the nonlinear Fokker–Planck equation in the weak sense; a Dobrushin-type uniqueness estimate identifies the limit, completing the propagation of chaos.

## 6. Limitations

**Finite width is the real world.** All constancy and convergence statements are asymptotic. At the widths actually trained — even \(10^4\) — the empirical NTK evolves, and the *rate* at which the limit is approached can be impractically slow for deep networks, where errors compound across layers [2].

**The kernel regime does not explain representation learning.** As Chizat, Oyallon, and Bach [4] themselves showed, forcing real architectures into the lazy regime *hurts* performance on vision tasks. The NTK explains why over-parameterized networks *can* be trained; it is largely silent on why the resulting representations *generalize* on hard tasks.

**Mean-field theory is mostly two-layer.** The Wasserstein gradient-flow picture is rigorous and complete for two-layer networks [5][8][10], but its extension to depth is fragmentary, lacking the clean uniqueness and landscape results of the two-layer case.

**The dichotomy is about limits, not trajectories.** Tensor Programs classify *infinite-width limits* [7]; they do not say how wide is wide enough, nor describe the crossover physics at finite width where real training happens. The phase diagram of Section 4.5 is empirically motivated but theoretically incomplete in the interpolating region.

**Optimization ≠ generalization.** The NTK gives trainability via kernel conditioning [1]; the mean-field program gives global landscape results for two layers [5]. But generalization bounds in either regime remain loose, and the implicit biases selecting *which* interpolant gradient descent finds in the rich regime are characterized only in special cases [9].

---

## 7. Conclusion

The infinite-width analysis of neural networks has matured into a coherent theory with a sharp central result: **scaling selects the regime**. The NTK [1][2] describes training when parameters stay near initialization; the mean-field PDE [5][8][10] describes training when neurons move; laziness is a scaling artifact [4]; and Tensor Programs [6][7] proved these are the only two options — every stable infinite-width limit either learns features or performs kernel gradient descent, never both.

For the practitioner: adopt \(\mu\)P for hyperparameter transfer and stable scaling [7]; use the NTK parametrization for a tractable convex theory of trainability — at the price of forfeiting representation learning [1][4]; and look to the rich regime — neurons that move, measures that flow, features learned rather than frozen [5][9][10] — to understand why deep learning works where kernels fail.

The open frontier is the crossover: quantitative finite-width theories interpolating between the two endpoints.
