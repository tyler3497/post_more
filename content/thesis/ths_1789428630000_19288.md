# Implicit Deep Learning: Deep Equilibrium Models, Neural Ordinary Differential Equations, and Implicit Differentiation

## Abstract

Implicit deep learning replaces explicit layer stacks with outputs defined *implicitly*: as fixed points of a single learned operator, as trajectories of a differential equation, or as zeros of a monotone inclusion. This thesis unifies three pillars of the field. **Deep Equilibrium Models (DEQs)** find equilibria via root-finding and differentiate through them with the implicit function theorem, achieving constant memory independent of effective depth [1]. **Neural Ordinary Differential Equations (Neural ODEs)** parameterize continuous-depth dynamics and train by the adjoint sensitivity method, again with constant memory [3]. **Monotone Operator Equilibrium Networks (monDEQs)** recast the equilibrium problem as monotone operator splitting, guaranteeing existence, uniqueness, and stable convergence of the forward solve [2]. We derive the exact implicit gradient, analyze phantom and Jacobian-free surrogate gradients, and examine stability through Jacobian regularization and spectral control [5]. Empirically, DEQs match transformer language-modeling quality with up to 88 percent memory reduction [1], while monDEQs vastly outperform Neural ODE baselines on vision benchmarks [2]. We close by mapping open challenges: forward-solver instability, initialization sensitivity, and scaling implicit models toward foundation-model sizes.

---

## 1 Intro

Conventional deep networks are explicit: the output is the composition of a fixed, finite sequence of transformations, and training stores every intermediate activation for backpropagation. This design couples three quantities that need not be coupled — *representational depth*, *memory footprint*, and *differentiability* — and it creates well-known bottlenecks: vanishing and exploding gradients, memory walls on long sequences, and depth-limited adaptive computation.

Implicit deep learning breaks the coupling. Instead of specifying *how* to compute the hidden state, the modeler specifies a *condition* the hidden state must satisfy — a fixed-point equation, an ordinary differential equation with boundary data, or an operator inclusion — and delegates the actual computation to a numerical solver. Gradients are obtained not by unrolling the solver but by differentiating the *condition itself*: the implicit function theorem for fixed points, the adjoint method for ODEs, and sensitivity analysis for monotone inclusions. The memory cost collapses from linear in depth to constant, because the solver's internal iterates never enter the autodifferentiation tape.

Three research threads make this vision concrete, and each solves a different piece of the puzzle:

1. **Deep Equilibrium Models** show that weight-tied sequence networks already drift toward fixed points, and that finding the point directly with root-finding yields an "infinitely deep" model at constant memory [1].
2. **Neural ODEs** take the continuous limit, parameterizing the *derivative* of the hidden state and using adaptive black-box solvers whose precision can be traded against speed [3].
3. **Monotone operator equilibrium networks** impose operator-theoretic structure on the fixed-point map so that a unique equilibrium provably exists and the forward solver provably converges [2].

This thesis develops these threads, the shared mathematics of implicit differentiation, the stability theory that makes training practical, and the empirical record to date.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 From Explicit to Implicit

An explicit residual network maps input *x* to output through *L* layers:

$$h_{k+1} = h_k + f_k(h_k; \theta_k), \qquad k = 0, \dots, L-1$$

A weight-tied variant reuses one transformation *f* at every layer. Bai, Kolter, and Koltun observed that the hidden states of such tied networks — including self-attention transformers and trellis networks — often converge toward a limit point as depth grows [1]. The DEQ hypothesis elevates this observation to an architecture: *compute the limit directly*.

Formally, a DEQ defines its representation as the solution *z\** of

$$z^\star = f_\theta(z^\star; x)$$

where *f_\theta* is a single layer (e.g., a transformer block with input injection). Training and inference require only the ability to (a) find *z\** with a root-finder applied to *g(z) = f_\theta(z; x) − z*, and (b) differentiate through the fixed point without storing solver iterates.

### 2.2 Fixed Points and the Implicit Function Theorem

Suppose *z\*(θ)* satisfies *g(z\*(θ), θ) = 0* with *g* continuously differentiable and *∂g/∂z* invertible at the solution. The implicit function theorem gives

$$\frac{\partial z^\star}{\partial \theta} = -\left(\frac{\partial g}{\partial z}\right)^{-1} \frac{\partial g}{\partial \theta}\Big|_{z^\star}.$$

For a loss *L(z\*)*, the chain rule yields the celebrated implicit gradient:

$$\frac{dL}{d\theta} = -\frac{\partial L}{\partial z^\star} \left(I - \frac{\partial f_\theta}{\partial z}\right)^{-1} \frac{\partial f_\theta}{\partial \theta}\Big|_{z^\star}.$$

Critically, this involves only the Jacobian *at the equilibrium* — no intermediate iterates. The vector–Jacobian product *(I − J)^{−T} v* is itself obtained by solving a *linear* fixed-point equation, so the backward pass is another root-finding problem of the same shape as the forward one [1].

### 2.3 Continuous Depth and the Adjoint Method

Neural ODEs take a different implicit route [3]. Rather than iterating a map, they parameterize the time derivative of the hidden state:

$$\frac{dh(t)}{dt} = f(h(t), t, \theta), \qquad h(0) = x,$$

and define the output as *h(T)* computed by a black-box ODE solver. Backpropagation uses the *adjoint* *a(t) = ∂L/∂h(t)*, which satisfies its own ODE run backward in time:

$$\frac{da(t)}{dt} = -\,a(t)\,\frac{\partial f}{\partial h}(h(t), t, \theta).$$

Solving this adjoint system requires only the trajectory *h(t)* — reconstructible by re-solving the forward ODE in reverse — so memory stays constant regardless of solver steps. The adjoint method predates deep learning (it is the workhorse of PDE-constrained optimization), but its application to learned vector fields was transformative [3].

### 2.4 Monotone Operators

A (possibly set-valued) operator *F* is *monotone* when

$$\langle F(u) - F(v),\, u - v \rangle \ge 0 \qquad \forall\, u, v,$$

and *m-strongly monotone* when the inner product is bounded below by *m‖u − v‖²*. Monotone operator theory, developed for convex optimization and variational inequalities, supplies exactly what raw DEQs lack: *guaranteed* existence and uniqueness of solutions and *provably convergent* splitting algorithms such as forward–backward and Peaceman–Rachford splitting. Winston and Kolter's key insight is that an equilibrium network *z\* = σ(Wz\* + Ux + b)* can be rewritten as the zero of a monotone operator, provided the linear part satisfies a monotonicity constraint [2].

## 3 Methodology

Our exposition follows a unified analytical template applied to each model family:

1. **Implicit specification.** Write the model's output as the solution of an equation or inclusion *G(z\*, θ, x) ∋ 0*.
2. **Forward solve.** Select a numerical method (Broyden's quasi-Newton method, adaptive Runge–Kutta, forward–backward splitting) and characterize its convergence.
3. **Implicit differentiation.** Derive the exact gradient via the implicit function theorem or adjoint calculus, and implement it as a custom vector–Jacobian product that re-solves a linear system at the equilibrium.
4. **Stability analysis.** Bound the Jacobian's spectral radius at the fixed point; where bounds fail, introduce regularization (Jacobian penalties [5]) or structural constraints (monotonicity [2]).
5. **Empirical validation.** Report memory, accuracy, and solver-iteration statistics on sequence, vision, and control benchmarks.

The following Python sketch implements the canonical DEQ forward–backward pattern used throughout the literature [1]:

```python
import torch
from torch import autograd

def deq_forward(f, x, solver, z0, f_thres=1e-5, b_thres=1e-5):
    """Forward: find z* with f(z*; x) - z* = 0, no autograd tape."""
    with torch.no_grad():
        z_star = solver(lambda z: f(z, x) - z, z0, threshold=f_thres)["result"]
    # Re-engage autograd only at the equilibrium point
    z_star = f(z_star.requires_grad_(), x)

    def backward_hook(grad):
        # Solve the linear fixed point: u = J^T u + grad
        u = solver(
            lambda y: autograd.grad(z_star, z_star, y,
                                    retain_graph=True)[0] + grad,
            torch.zeros_like(grad), threshold=b_thres)["result"]
        return u

    z_star.register_hook(backward_hook)
    return z_star
```

The forward pass stores nothing; the backward hook solves a linear system whose operator is the transposed Jacobian at *z\**. Memory is constant in effective depth.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Deep Equilibrium Models: Infinite Depth at Constant Memory

The DEQ forward pass replaces *L* stacked layers with a root-finding problem. In practice, naive fixed-point iteration *z_{k+1} = f(z_k)* converges too slowly, so implementations use **Broyden's method**, a quasi-Newton scheme that builds a low-rank approximation to the inverse Jacobian and typically converges superlinearly near the root [1]. Anderson acceleration is a popular alternative with similar cost.

The backward pass solves

$$\left(I - J_f^\top\right) u = \frac{\partial L}{\partial z^\star}, \qquad J_f = \frac{\partial f_\theta}{\partial z}\Big|_{z^\star},$$

again by fixed-point iteration on *u ↦ J_f^T u + ∂L/∂z\** — a linear system, hence globally convergent whenever the spectral radius *ρ(J_f) < 1*. This condition is also what makes the forward iteration stable, tying forward and backward robustness to a single spectral quantity [1].

> **Theorem:** *Let f_θ be continuously differentiable and suppose the fixed-point iteration converges to z\* with ρ(∂f_θ/∂z|_{z\*}) < 1. Then the vector–Jacobian product computed by the linear fixed-point backward iteration equals the exact gradient dL/dθ given by the implicit function theorem, and both forward and backward passes use memory independent of the number of solver iterations.*

On WikiText-103 language modeling, DEQ-Transformer variants match or exceed the perplexity of explicit transformers with the same parameter budget while cutting memory by up to 88 percent — memory, not FLOPs, being the binding constraint for long sequences [1].

### 4.2 Implicit Differentiation: Exact, Phantom, and Jacobian-Free Gradients

The exact implicit gradient requires solving a linear system at *z\**, which costs a second root-finding run. Three cheaper surrogates have emerged:

| Gradient estimator | Formula sketch | Cost | Fidelity |
|---|---|---|---|
| Exact implicit [1] | *−(∂L/∂z\*)(I − J)^{−1} ∂f/∂θ* | One linear solve | Exact |
| Phantom gradient | Backprop through *K* unrolled steps of the forward solver | *O(K)* memory | Biased but convergent as *K* grows |
| Jacobian-free (one-step) | Treat *z\** as constant: *∂L/∂θ ≈ (∂L/∂z\*) ∂f/∂θ* | One VJP | Coarse; works surprisingly often |

**Phantom gradients** backpropagate through a truncated unrolling of the forward iteration, producing a biased but consistent approximation whose bias shrinks as the truncation length grows; they are the implicit-world analogue of truncated BPTT. **Jacobian-free backpropagation** drops the inverse-Jacobian term entirely — equivalent to one step of the Neumann series *(I − J)^{−1} = I + J + J² + …* — and recent imaging-inverse-problem studies find it underperforms exact implicit differentiation on equivariant losses, though it can suffice for coarse objectives [8].

The choice matters most when the forward solver is imperfect: exact gradients assume the root was found accurately, while phantom gradients inherit the solver's trajectory and degrade gracefully. A pragmatic rule emerging from the literature is to train with exact implicit gradients and fall back to phantom truncation when the backward linear solve stalls [1][5].

### 4.3 Neural ODEs: Continuous Depth and Adaptive Computation

Neural ODEs replace the discrete layer index with continuous time [3]. The forward pass calls `ODESolve(h(0), f, 0, T)`, where modern adaptive solvers (e.g., Dormand–Prince) choose step sizes to meet an error tolerance — the model *adapts its evaluation strategy to each input*, spending more function evaluations on difficult trajectories. Depth becomes a real-valued quantity: one can even query *h(t)* at arbitrary intermediate times, enabling irregularly-sampled time-series modeling that discrete RNNs handle awkwardly.

The adjoint backward pass solves an augmented ODE backward from *T* to *0*:

```python
# Augmented state: [h(t), a(t), dL/dtheta]; solved backward in time.
def augmented_dynamics(s, t):
    h, a, _ = s
    with torch.enable_grad():
        hh = h.detach().requires_grad_(True)
        fh = f(hh, t, theta)
    vjp_h, vjp_theta, *_ = autograd.grad(fh, (hh,) + theta, -a,
                                        allow_unused=True)
    return (fh, vjp_h, vjp_theta)

sT = (hT, dL_dhT, torch.zeros_like(theta_flat))
s0 = odesolve(augmented_dynamics, sT, T, 0)   # constant memory
dL_dtheta = s0[2]
```

Because the forward trajectory is reconstructed by re-integration rather than stored, memory is constant in the number of solver steps [3]. The price is numerical: the adjoint trajectory can be unstable when the forward dynamics are stiff, and the "recompute instead of store" trick assumes the reverse solve reproduces the forward states accurately — an assumption that fails for chaotic vector fields, motivating interpolated checkpointing variants.

Continuous normalizing flows extend the idea to generative modeling: the change-of-variables formula becomes an ODE for the log-density, and the trace term is estimated unbiasedly with Hutchinson's estimator, yielding maximum-likelihood training without architectural constraints like coupling layers [3].

### 4.4 Monotone Operator Equilibrium Networks: Guarantees by Construction

Raw DEQs can diverge: nothing in *z\* = f(z\*; x)* guarantees a fixed point exists, is unique, or is reachable by the solver. Monotone operator equilibrium networks (monDEQs) fix this by construction [2]. Write the equilibrium with a proximal (resolvent) form:

$$z^\star = \operatorname{prox}^\alpha_\sigma\big((1-\alpha) z^\star + \alpha (W z^\star + U x + b)\big),$$

which is the fixed point of a forward–backward splitting applied to the monotone inclusion *0 ∈ (I − W)z − (Ux + b) + ∂φ(z)* for a convex *φ* whose proximal operator is the activation. If *I − W* is *m*-strongly monotone — enforced by parameterizing *W = (1−m)I − AᵀA + B − Bᵀ* — then:

> **Theorem:** *Under strong monotonicity of I − W with modulus m > 0, the equilibrium inclusion admits a unique solution z\*, and the forward–backward / Peaceman–Rachford splitting iterates converge to z\* linearly with rate governed by m.*

No spectral-radius prayers: the parameterization *guarantees* the operator properties, so the forward solver cannot diverge and the implicit gradient's linear system is always well-posed [2]. Davydov and coauthors extended the framework beyond Euclidean geometry, developing monotone operator theory for the ℓ₁ and ℓ∞ norms and showing that the resulting non-Euclidean splittings accelerate equilibrium computation in recurrent networks [4]. On CIFAR-10 and ImageNet-scale vision tasks, monDEQs vastly outperform Neural ODE baselines at lower compute cost, closing the performance gap that had made Neural ODEs a theoretical curiosity [2].

A compact Haskell rendering of the resolvent iteration captures the mathematical essence:

```haskell
-- Forward-backward splitting for 0 in F(z) + G(z), F cocoercive, G maximal monotone
forwardBackward :: Double -> (Vec -> Vec) -> (Vec -> Vec) -> Vec -> [Vec]
forwardBackward alpha gradF resolventG z0 = iterate step z0
  where step z = resolventG (z - alpha `scale` gradF z)
-- resolventG = (I + alpha G)^{-1}; linear convergence under strong monotonicity
```

## 5 Empirical/Proofs

The empirical record, summarized below, supports three claims: implicit models match explicit quality, memory collapses to constant, and operator-theoretic structure restores reliability.

| Model family | Benchmark | Key result | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| DEQ-Transformer / DEQ-TrellisNet | WikiText-103 | Matches or beats explicit baselines; up to 88% memory reduction | [1] |
| Neural ODE / Latent ODE | PhysioNet, MNIST | Constant-memory training; adaptive compute per input; continuous normalizing flows | [3] |
| monDEQ (multiscale conv) | CIFAR-10, ImageNet-scale | Vastly outperforms Neural ODE baselines; guaranteed unique equilibrium | [2] |
| IDE (implicit diff. equilibrium) | CIFAR-10 SNN | 91.7–92.0% accuracy at 2.8 GB vs STBP out-of-memory at 100 timesteps | [6] |
| Jacobian regularization | DEQ training | Stabilizes forward/backward solves; spectral-radius control | [5] |

Two theoretical results anchor the practice. First, the implicit-gradient identity of Section 4.1 is exact whenever the forward root is accurate and *I − J* is invertible — no approximation is inherent to the method, only to the solver [1]. Second, the monDEQ convergence theorem converts the method's weakest link (the forward solve) into its strongest: a contractive operator with a certified rate [2].

Stability in the wild is enforced by **Jacobian regularization**: penalizing *‖J_f‖_F* (estimated stochastically with Hutchinson-type trace estimators) keeps the spectral radius below unity, simultaneously stabilizing the forward Broyden iterations and the backward linear solve [5]. This single regularizer explains much of the gap between early unstable DEQ experiments and later robust ones.

Implicit differentiation has also escaped the DEQ/ODE niche: feedback spiking neural networks can be trained by differentiating their *average-firing-rate equilibrium* rather than backpropagating through time, cutting CIFAR-10 training memory from tens of gigabytes to under 3 GB [6] — evidence that "differentiate the condition, not the computation" is a general design pattern.

## 6 Limitations

1. **Forward-solver fragility.** Broyden's method and Anderson acceleration lack global convergence guarantees for non-monotone *f*; poor initialization or ill-conditioned Jacobians cause stalls that no backward-pass trick can fix. monDEQs solve this by construction [2], but at the cost of architectural constraints.
2. **Backward linear solves.** The exact implicit gradient needs *(I − J)^{−T}v*; when *ρ(J)* approaches 1 the system becomes ill-conditioned and the backward iteration crawls. Jacobian regularization mitigates but does not eliminate the issue [5].
3. **Initialization sensitivity.** Both DEQs and Neural ODEs are sensitive to the initial *z₀* or vector-field scale; a bad start wastes solver iterations or lands on an undesirable equilibrium branch when uniqueness is not guaranteed.
4. **Expressivity constraints of guarantees.** The monDEQ parameterization *W = (1−m)I − AᵀA + B − Bᵀ* restricts the linear operator to ensure monotonicity; whether this restriction costs representational power at foundation-model scale is unresolved [2].
5. **ODE-specific pathologies.** Neural ODE trajectories cannot cross, limiting their ability to represent certain functions (e.g., the reflection map); augmented Neural ODEs lift the state space to recover universality but sacrifice some elegance. Stiff dynamics inflate solver cost unpredictably.
6. **Scaling evidence is thin.** The strongest DEQ results remain at moderate scale; no implicit model has yet been trained at frontier-LLM scale, and solver overhead per step may dominate at that regime.

---

## 7 Conclusion

Implicit deep learning reframes the network as a *condition to be solved* rather than a *computation to be unrolled*. Deep Equilibrium Models demonstrated that fixed-point semantics deliver infinite effective depth at constant memory [1]; Neural ODEs showed that continuous depth buys adaptive computation and elegant generative modeling [3]; monotone operator equilibrium networks proved that guarantees — existence, uniqueness, convergence — can be designed into the architecture rather than hoped for [2]. The shared engine is implicit differentiation: gradients of the condition, not of the solver.

The trajectory of the field points toward hybrid designs — monotone-structured operators solved with adaptive methods, Jacobian-regularized training, and phantom-gradient fallbacks — and toward the open question of scale. If implicit models can be stabilized at foundation-model sizes, the payoff is a class of architectures whose memory footprint is decoupled from their effective depth, whose computation adapts to each input, and whose forward passes carry certificates of convergence. That is a sufficiently attractive trade to justify the considerable analytical machinery it demands.
