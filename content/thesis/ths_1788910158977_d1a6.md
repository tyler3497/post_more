# Decidable Protocol Verification with the Ivy Language: EPR Fragments, Inductive Invariants, and Automated Proofs of Raft and Paxos

## Abstract

Verifying the safety of distributed consensus protocols such as Paxos and Raft for an unbounded number of nodes has long demanded either deep interactive-theorem-proving expertise or fragile SMT heuristics operating inside undecidable logics. The Ivy verification language offers a third path: by constraining specifications, transition relations, and invariants to the *effectively propositional* (EPR, or Bernays–Schönfinkel) fragment of first-order logic, every verification condition becomes *decidable*, so checking is fully algorithmic and terminating [2]. This thesis develops that methodology in depth. We characterize the EPR fragment and its small-model property, explain how Ivy's counterexample-guided invariant-generalization workflow turns a failed induction into a graphical, human-readable repair step, and reconstruct the modular proof structure of Paxos Made EPR [1], including the ballot-based voting theory, the quorum intersection invariant, and the stratification tricks that keep the verification conditions inside EPR. We compare the decidable approach against the undecidable but expressive TLA+/TLAPS methodology [3] and survey extensions to liveness via first-order liveness-to-safety reduction [5]. The result is a practical roadmap for automated, trustworthy proofs of industrial consensus protocols.

## 1. Introduction

Distributed consensus protocols sit at the foundation of fault-tolerant computing: Raft drives Kubernetes control planes and etcd, while Paxos variants underpin Chubby, Spanner's coordination layer, and countless replicated state machines [1][3]. Yet these protocols are notoriously subtle. A single missed case in the ballot-disjointness argument of Paxos can silently break the agreement property for executions that test suites, however extensive, will never exercise. Testing covers a tiny fraction of the behavior space of a concurrent, message-passing system; formal verification is the only known way to obtain guarantees for *all* executions and *all* system sizes.

The verification community has responded with two broad traditions. The first, exemplified by TLA+ and its proof assistant TLAPS, embraces an extremely expressive specification logic — untyped set theory with temporal operators — and asks a human expert to decompose the proof into steps small enough for backend provers to discharge [3]. This approach has produced machine-checked proofs of Basic and Multi-Paxos [3], but the required expertise and manual effort are substantial, and the underlying logic is undecidable, so automated provers may diverge or return "unknown" at exactly the moment trust is most needed.

The second tradition, and the subject of this thesis, turns the problem around: **restrict the logic so that decidability is guaranteed**, then exploit the resulting algorithmic certainty. The Ivy language, introduced by Padon, McMillan, Panda, Sagiv, and Shoham [2], compiles a protocol specification and candidate invariant into verification conditions in the *effectively propositional* (EPR) fragment of first-order logic. In EPR, satisfiability is decidable (indeed NEXPTIME-complete in the general case, but practically fast for protocol encodings), so the verifier never hangs: it answers *satisfiable* or *unsatisfiable*, always. When a verification condition fails, Ivy presents the user with a **finite, graphical counterexample to induction**, which the user then generalizes into a stronger invariant candidate. This counterexample-guided loop, made tractable by decidability, is what made possible the first fully automated-solver-backed proofs of Paxos and its many variants [1].

This thesis is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews first-order logic fragments and the small-model property that makes EPR decidable. Section 3 presents the Ivy methodology. Section 4 reconstructs the EPR proof of Paxos and its variants. Section 5 reports the empirical evidence and the comparison with TLAPS, Section 6 examines limitations, and Section 7 concludes.

## 2. Background: Decidable Fragments of First-Order Logic

### 2.1 The verification-condition pipeline

Deductive verification of a transition system reduces correctness to a finite set of *verification conditions* (VCs): logical formulas whose validity implies that an invariant is inductive. Given an initial-state formula *Init*, a transition relation *T*, and a candidate invariant *I*, the proof obligation is

> **Theorem:** *I* is an inductive invariant establishing the safety property *P* if (i) *Init ⇒ I* (initiation), (ii) *I ∧ T ⇒ I′* (consecution, where *I′* is *I* with primed state variables), and (iii) *I ⇒ P* (safety). When all three implications are *valid*, every reachable state satisfies *P*.

The difficulty is that validity in full first-order logic is undecidable. SMT solvers apply heuristics — quantifier instantiation, E-matching — that work often but unpredictably; on protocol VCs they routinely diverge or time out, and the user cannot distinguish "the invariant is wrong" from "the solver gave up." Decidability removes this ambiguity.

### 2.2 The EPR (Bernays–Schönfinkel) fragment

EPR is the fragment of first-order logic consisting of formulas of the form *∃\*∀\*\ φ*, where *φ* is quantifier-free and contains no function symbols of positive arity (relations and constants only). Equivalently, after Skolemization, EPR formulas are purely universal with a finite relational vocabulary.

The key property is the **small-model property**: if an EPR formula is satisfiable, it is satisfiable in a model whose universe size is bounded by the number of constants plus Skolem constants appearing in the formula. Satisfiability therefore reduces to grounding over a finite domain — effectively a SAT problem — and is decidable. This is the celebrated Bernays–Schönfinkel–Ramsey theorem, and it is the engine beneath every Ivy proof.

For protocol verification this is a remarkably good fit: nodes, ballots, quorums, and values are naturally modeled as uninterpreted sorts with relations between them, and protocol invariants are naturally universal statements ("for all ballots *b1*, *b2* and quorums *q1*, *q2*, …"). Ivy enforces, largely through its type system and syntactic restrictions, that every VC it generates stays within EPR, and it rejects or isolates constructs that would escape the fragment.

### 2.3 Stratification and the limits of alternation

One subtlety: the transition relation of a protocol naturally introduces quantifier alternation beyond *∃\*∀\**. When the VC *I ∧ T ⇒ I′* is negated for satisfiability checking, Skolemization can produce *∀\*∃\** patterns that leave EPR. Ivy addresses this with **stratification**: the sorts of the model are assigned to ordered strata, and quantifier dependencies are required to respect the stratification order [1]. Intuitively, an existential quantifier may depend only on universally quantified variables from strictly lower strata, which preserves the small-model property. Stratification is the single most important modeling discipline an Ivy user learns, and Section 4 shows how the Paxos proof is carefully stratified.

### 2.4 TLA+ and TLAPS: the expressive alternative

TLA+ is Lamport's untyped specification language based on ZF set theory with temporal operators; TLAPS is its proof system, which decomposes a hierarchical proof into leaf obligations dispatched to SMT solvers, an Isabelle backend, and decision procedures [3]. The logic is far more expressive than EPR — functions, arithmetic, temporal operators are all available — but validity is undecidable, and the human must supply the entire proof structure. Chand et al. demonstrated a TLAPS proof of Multi-Paxos built by minimizing the difference from the Basic Paxos proof [3], a tour de force that also illustrates the cost: deep protocol and prover expertise, with automation confined to the leaves of a manually built proof tree. A complementary line of work attempts *automatic* invariant inference for Paxos with quantified inductive invariants [4], trading the human's structural insight for search.

## 3. Methodology: The Ivy Language and Its Workflow

### 3.1 The Ivy modeling language

An Ivy program describes a protocol as a set of *isolates* — modules encapsulating state, actions, and invariants. The core constructs are:

- **Types and relations**: uninterpreted sorts (`type node`, `type ballot`) and relations over them (`relation voted(n:node, b:ballot, v:value)`).
- **Actions**: guarded commands with parameters, e.g. `action vote(n:node, b:ballot, v:value)`, whose bodies update relations.
- **Invariants**: first-order formulas asserted to hold in all reachable states.
- **Ghost and derived state**: auxiliary relations (e.g. `chosen`) that record protocol-relevant facts without affecting execution.

The following sketch shows the flavor of an Ivy Paxos model (simplified for exposition):

```ivy
#lang ivy1.7

type node
type ballot
type value
type quorum

relation member(n:node, q:quorum)
relation voted(n:node, b:ballot, v:value)
relation maxb(n:node, b:ballot)
relation isleader(n:node, b:ballot)

action become_leader(n:node, b:ballot, q:quorum) = {
    require forall N:node. member(N,q) -> exists B:ballot. maxb(N,B) & B < b;
    isleader(n,b) := true
}

action cast_vote(n:node, b:ballot, v:value) = {
    require isleader(n,b);
    require forall B:ballot, V:value. voted(n,B,V) -> B < b;
    voted(n,b,v) := true
}

invariant [agreement] forall B1:ballot, B2:ballot, V1:value, V2:value, Q1:quorum, Q2:quorum.
    (exists N:node. member(N,Q1) & voted(N,B1,V1)) &
    (exists N:node. member(N,Q2) & voted(N,B2,V2)) ->
    V1 = V2
```

Note the modeling choices that keep the VCs in EPR: relations rather than functions, universal invariants, and carefully bounded existential quantifiers. Ivy's compiler checks the stratification discipline and rejects violations with precise diagnostics [7].

### 3.2 Counterexample-guided invariant generalization

Ivy's signature contribution [2] is not just decidability but what decidability *enables*: a predictable, interactive invariant-discovery loop.

> **Theorem (Ivy workflow):** Starting from a candidate invariant *I*, Ivy checks initiation and consecution. If consecution fails, decidability guarantees a *finite* counterexample model — a concrete assignment of relations over a small universe — which Ivy renders graphically. The user inspects the counterexample, identifies the missing protocol fact, and generalizes it into a new conjunct of *I*. The loop repeats until *I* is inductive.

Because the logic is decidable, "the solver returned a counterexample" unambiguously means "the invariant is not inductive" — never "the solver got lost." The graphical display shows nodes, ballots, and messages as an explicit finite structure, so the user reasons about a concrete bad scenario rather than an abstract proof failure [2].

### 3.3 Modular proofs and isolates

Ivy supports *modular* verification: an isolate can be verified against the abstract interface of another isolate, and the composition is sound. This matters for Paxos, whose proof decomposes into layers — a ballot-numbering theory, a voting theory, and the agreement argument — each verified separately [1]. Modularity is also what lets the same ballot theory serve Paxos, Multi-Paxos, Fast Paxos, and Flexible Paxos with only the top layer changing.

## 4. Deep Dive: EPR Verification of Paxos

### 4.1 The ballot-number theory in EPR

The heart of the Paxos proof is an abstract theory of ballots. Ballots are totally ordered (typically natural numbers, but Ivy models them as an uninterpreted sort with a strict partial order satisfying the needed axioms — transitivity and totality expressed universally, avoiding arithmetic, which would leave EPR). The critical lemma is **quorum intersection combined with ballot discipline**:

> **Theorem (Ballot safety):** If a value *v* is chosen at ballot *b* (some quorum voted for *v* at *b*), then every vote cast at any ballot *b′ > b* is also for *v*. Consequently, at most one value is ever chosen.

The proof proceeds by induction on ballots. The key auxiliary invariant states that a leader elected at ballot *b* must propose the value voted at the greatest ballot below *b* among its quorum — the classic Paxos Phase-2 rule. In Ivy, this becomes a universal formula over ballots, nodes, and values, stratified so that the "greatest ballot below *b*" reasoning (which superficially involves an existential "there exists a maximal voted ballot") is expressed through relations that respect the stratification order [1].

### 4.2 Quorum intersection as a universal invariant

Quorum intersection — any two quorums share a node — is the combinatorial fact that makes agreement possible. Ivy models quorums as an uninterpreted sort with a membership relation and a single axiom:

```ivy
axiom [quorum_intersection] forall Q1:quorum, Q2:quorum.
    exists N:node. member(N,Q1) & member(N,Q2)
```

This axiom is *not* in EPR as stated (it is *∀\*∃\**), but it is stratified: the existential node depends only on the universally quantified quorums at a lower stratum. After Skolemization, the Skolem function from quorums to their witness node respects the stratification, preserving decidability. This pattern — state the natural axiom, then verify stratification — recurs throughout the Paxos development and is the main technical craft of EPR protocol modeling.

### 4.3 From single-decree to Multi-Paxos, Fast, and Flexible Paxos

Padon et al. verified not one protocol but a family: single-decree Paxos, Multi-Paxos, Vertical Paxos, Fast Paxos, Flexible Paxos, and Stoppable Paxos [1]. The methodology is *layered reuse*: the ballot theory and quorum theory are proved once as isolates; each protocol variant instantiates them with different action definitions. Fast Paxos, which allows clients to send directly to acceptors, requires an additional invariant about fast-ballot collision recovery; Flexible Paxos, which relaxes quorum intersection between Phase 1 and Phase 2 quorums, requires re-proving the intersection lemma under the weaker hypothesis. Because each variant's VCs remain in EPR, every check terminates in seconds (Section 5).

For contrast, the TLAPS proof of Multi-Paxos [3] proceeds by *refinement*: Multi-Paxos is shown to refine Basic Paxos. The proof is manual and hierarchical; Ivy's is modular and solver-checked. Both are trustworthy, but they distribute the effort differently — human structural insight in TLAPS versus human invariant insight plus algorithmic checking in Ivy. Compositional rely/guarantee approaches [6] confirm that modularity is the common theme across all successful Paxos verifications.

### 4.4 Raft and the leader-completeness argument

Raft's safety argument centers on *leader completeness*: any leader elected for term *t* contains all committed entries from earlier terms. In EPR terms, this is again a universal statement over terms (Raft's analog of ballots), log indices, and nodes, with the election restriction ("a candidate wins only with votes from a majority whose logs are at least as up-to-date") encoded as guarded actions. The log-matching property and the "a leader never overwrites its log" discipline become auxiliary invariants discovered through the same counterexample-guided loop. Raft's stronger leader discipline actually simplifies the EPR encoding relative to Paxos — there is no Phase-2 value-picking rule to stratify — which is one reason Raft is often recommended as a first Ivy project.

### 4.5 Reducing liveness to safety in first-order logic

Safety ("nothing bad happens") is only half the story; liveness ("something good eventually happens") — e.g., that a proposed value is eventually chosen — requires temporal reasoning. Padon, Hoenicke, Losa, Podelski, Sagiv, and Shoham showed that liveness verification for infinite-state first-order systems can be *reduced to safety* via fair-cycle detection [5]. The input is a first-order transition system plus an FO-LTL temporal specification; it is transformed into a fair transition system whose fair traces are exactly the violating traces, and then into a new transition system whose safety implies the temporal property. Ivy implements this as its liveness-to-safety (`l2s`) feature, used for example in verified futex-based mutex developments [9].

## 5. Empirical Evidence and Proofs

### 5.1 Solver performance across Paxos variants

The decisive empirical claim of Paxos Made EPR [1] is that decidability delivers *predictable, fast* checking. The paper's evaluation (Figure 8) reports per-check runtimes on a laptop for each protocol variant and each proof layer (EPR checks, auxiliary invariants, refinement steps, and finite-instance FOL checks):

| Protocol | EPR checks (mean s) | Auxiliary invariants (s) | FOL-2 inst (s) | FOL-16 inst (s) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Paxos | 1.0 | 0.7 | 1.2 | 278 |
| Multi-Paxos | 1.2 | 0.8 | 1.2 | 229 |
| Vertical Paxos | 2.2 | — | 27 | 274 |
| Fast Paxos | 4.7 | 0.9 | 3.7 | 300 (timeout) |
| Flexible Paxos | 1.0 | 0.7 | 1.1 | 275 |
| Stoppable Paxos | 3.8 | 1.0 | 186 | 300 (timeout) |

Two observations stand out. First, the *EPR* checks — the decidable core — complete in roughly 1–5 seconds for every variant: verification is interactive-speed. Second, the finite-instance checks (unbounded FOL reduced to fixed-size instances) degrade sharply and hit timeouts, exactly illustrating the cost of leaving the decidable fragment [1]. Decidability is not a theoretical nicety; it is the difference between a usable tool and an unpredictable one.

### 5.2 Comparison with TLAPS-based proofs

| Dimension | Ivy / EPR [1][2] | TLA+ / TLAPS [3] |
|---|---|---|
| Underlying logic | EPR fragment of FOL — decidable | Untyped set theory + temporal logic — undecidable |
| Solver behavior | Always terminates; counterexamples are finite models | May diverge; "unknown" is ambiguous |
| Human effort | Invariant discovery via graphical CTIs | Full hierarchical proof structure by hand |
| Proof reuse | Modular isolates; ballot theory shared across 6 variants | Refinement proofs minimizing diff from Basic Paxos |
| Liveness | Via liveness-to-safety reduction [5] | Native temporal operators |
| Expertise required | Systems intuition + stratification discipline | Deep prover and TLA+ expertise |

Neither dominates the other. TLAPS handles arbitrary mathematics (arithmetic, recursive operators) that EPR cannot express; Ivy gives push-button certainty for the large class of protocols whose arguments are essentially universal. The automatic invariant-inference line [4] attempts to remove Ivy's remaining human step — generalizing counterexamples — using syntax-guided search, at the cost of completeness.

### 5.3 Trustworthiness of the toolchain

A decidable logic does not by itself guarantee a correct tool. Ivy's trusted base includes its VC generator, the stratification checker, and the Z3 backend. The graphical counterexamples let the user *see* what the solver proved, and the modular structure means each isolate's proof can be audited independently. The 2026 SIGPLAN Most Influential Paper citation notes the paradigm went on to enable verified QUIC testing and the first mechanized proof of the Stellar consensus protocol [8].

## 6. Limitations

**Expressiveness ceiling.** EPR cannot express transitive closure, arithmetic, or recursive functions. Protocols whose correctness depends on integer arithmetic (e.g., sequence-number arithmetic with wraparound) or on reachability in an unbounded graph must be abstracted before Ivy can handle them, and the abstraction itself is unverified. TLA+ has no such ceiling [3].

**Stratification burden.** Keeping VCs inside EPR requires the stratification discipline, which is a genuine modeling skill. A natural formalization of a protocol frequently violates stratification, and repairing it — reordering quantifiers, introducing ghost relations, splitting an invariant into stratified conjuncts — demands understanding the small-model argument, not just the protocol. The tool reports violations precisely, but the repair is manual [1][7].

**Invariant discovery is still human.** Ivy automates *checking* completely but only *assists* discovery. The user must generalize each counterexample to induction into a sound invariant conjunct; a wrong generalization leads to more iterations, and there is no completeness guarantee that the loop converges. Automatic techniques [4] help but remain incomplete.

**Liveness is second-class.** The liveness-to-safety reduction [5] is powerful but less mature than the safety workflow: fair-cycle arguments introduce prophecy-like constructions that are harder to stratify and harder for users to debug when they fail.

**Finite counterexamples can mislead.** A graphical CTI shows *a* finite model refuting inductiveness, not necessarily the *intended* one; users occasionally "fix" the wrong thing and iterate. Discipline — always relating the CTI back to a concrete protocol scenario — is required.

## 7. Conclusion

The Ivy project demonstrates a thesis with both theoretical and practical force: **decidability is a design choice, and choosing it transforms protocol verification from an expert craft into an interactive engineering discipline**. By restricting specifications and invariants to the EPR fragment of first-order logic, Ivy guarantees that every verification condition is decided algorithmically; by rendering failures as finite graphical counterexamples, it turns each failure into a concrete, actionable step toward an inductive invariant. Applied to Paxos, this methodology produced solver-checked proofs of six protocol variants in seconds per check [1], and its descendants now verify blockchain consensus, find bugs in QUIC implementations, and influence tools from Verus to mypyvy [8].

The open frontiers are automatic invariant generalization [4], richer decidable fragments admitting limited arithmetic, and smoother liveness reasoning [5]. A decade after PLDI 2016, the research program Ivy launched — *find the decidable fragment that captures your domain, then verify inside it* — remains one of the most productive ideas in formal methods.
