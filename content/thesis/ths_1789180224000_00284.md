# Pauseless Garbage Collection: Loaded Value Barriers, Colored Pointers, and Brooks Forwarding Pointers in Concurrent Compacting Collectors

## Abstract

Stop-the-world pauses have long been the dominant tail-latency term in managed runtimes: a parallel collector's pause grows with the live set, and on multi-hundred-gigabyte heaps that growth is measured in seconds. This thesis presents a comparative, mechanism-level analysis of the three production designs that broke that scaling law — Azul's C4 collector with its **Loaded Value Barrier (LVB)** and self-healing reads, OpenJDK's **ZGC** with **colored pointers** and load-value barriers over multi-mapped memory, and OpenJDK's **Shenandoah** with **Brooks forwarding pointers** and the tospace invariant. We reconstruct the tri-color foundations they share, formalize the invariants each barrier scheme maintains while mutators and collectors touch the same objects concurrently, and compare their phase pipelines — *mark, relocate, remap* — and the handful of root-scanning handshakes that remain stop-the-world. We survey published pause-time and throughput evidence and derive the theoretical guarantees each design actually offers: bounded marking passes, at-most-once barrier healing, and pause bounds independent of heap size and live set. Finally, we examine the residual costs: barrier overhead on every load, metadata words per object, memory-ordering complexity, and the residual tuning surface.

## 1. Introduction

The central drama of automatic memory management is a concurrency problem. A tracing collector must determine, over a mutating object graph, which objects are reachable from a set of roots — while the *mutator* threads that own the graph keep running. Any collector that cannot tolerate concurrent mutation must stop the mutators while it works: the classic *stop-the-world* (STW) pause. For decades this was acceptable because heaps were small and pauses were short. Two trends destroyed that truce. First, heaps grew from megabytes to terabytes, and STW pause time scales — for mark-compact designs — roughly with the live set, not the heap. Second, the workloads moved into the tail: high-frequency trading, programmatic advertising, and interactive services began treating the 99.99th percentile as the contract, and a single multi-second GC pause became a contractual violation [8].

The industry's answer was not one algorithm but a family of *concurrent compacting* collectors whose common claim is radical: objects move, references are remapped, and dead memory is reclaimed **without ever stopping the mutators** for more than a root scan. Three designs reached production and stayed there. **Azul's C4** (continuously concurrent compacting collector), shipping commercially since 2010 on top of the earlier Pauseless GC, performs *all* of marking, relocation, and remapping concurrently using a read barrier called the **Loaded Value Barrier** that heals stale references at the point of load [1], [7]. **ZGC**, introduced experimentally in JDK 11 via JEP 333 and production-ready in JDK 15, encodes collector metadata *in the pointer itself* — colored pointers over multi-mapped virtual memory — and combines them with load barriers so that a mutator loading a stale reference is transparently redirected to the relocated copy [2], [3]. **Shenandoah**, contributed by Red Hat and integrated into OpenJDK via JEP 189, revives R. A. Brooks's 1984 *forwarding pointer* idea: every object carries an indirection word, atomically redirected during evacuation, and barriers maintain the *tospace invariant* that all mutator access lands on the current copy [4], [5], [6].

These three mechanisms look superficially different — a trap-on-load in C4, metadata bits in ZGC, a per-object indirection word in Shenandoah — yet they are answers to the same question: *how does a mutator safely dereference a reference that the collector may be moving, right now?* This thesis argues that they are three encodings of a single principle: **the read path is the synchronization point**, and if the read path can repair, redirect, or re-color a reference at load time, no stop-the-world phase is ever needed for relocation. We develop that argument in full technical detail.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 Tracing, tri-color marking, and the mutator problem

All three collectors are *tracing* collectors: they compute the reachable set by graph traversal from roots (stacks, registers, globals). Dijkstra's tri-color abstraction frames the traversal: objects are *white* (unvisited, presumed dead), *gray* (visited but not fully scanned), or *black* (visited and scanned). A collector is correct if, when it finishes, no black object points to a white object — the **tri-color invariant**. Concurrent mutation breaks this invariant in two canonical ways: a mutator can *hide* a white object by overwriting the only pointer to it before the collector scans the referring object (the *lost object* problem), or it can allocate new objects the collector never visits.

> **Theorem:** *Tri-color safety.* If every mutator update preserves the invariant "no black-to-white edge" — either by graying the overwritten referent (snapshot-at-the-beginning, SATB) or by graying the newly stored referent (incremental update) — then a concurrent marker that terminates with an empty gray set has marked exactly a superset of the objects reachable at some instant during marking. No live object is reclaimed. ∎

C4 uses *incremental update* tracing (no write barrier at all); Shenandoah uses SATB with a write barrier; ZGC's design avoids both classic write barriers for the relocation protocol, relying instead on its load barrier. The choice of write-barrier discipline shapes everything downstream [1], [3].

### 2.2 The Baker read barrier and concurrent copying

H. G. Baker's 1978 real-time collector introduced the decisive idea: instead of stopping the mutator while objects move, *intercept every reference load* and fix the loaded value before the mutator may use it [1]. A read barrier turns relocation into a local, per-access repair. The collector copies an object from fromspace to tospace, and any mutator that loads a stale (fromspace) pointer is transparently handed the tospace copy. If the barrier is total — every load passes through it — the collector can relocate freely. The engineering question is only how to make the barrier cheap: Brooks indirection, pointer coloring, and C4's NMT/LVB traps are three answers.

### 2.3 Why stop-the-world pauses scale with the live set

In a STW mark-compact collector, the pause must cover the full marking traversal and the compaction pass: work proportional to the *live set*, plus root scanning proportional to thread stacks. On a 100 GB heap with 60 GB live, that is tens of seconds of mutator blackout. The pauseless collectors restructure this work so that marking, relocation, and remapping are *concurrent phases*, and the only remaining STW moments are brief *handshakes* — root scans and phase-boundary rendezvous — whose duration depends on the root set and thread count, not on heap size [2], [4]. This is the precise sense in which their pause times are "independent of the heap."

## 3. Methodology

This thesis proceeds by **mechanism reconstruction**: we take the published papers, JEP specifications, OpenJDK wiki design documents, and implementer talks for C4 [1], [7], ZGC [2], [3], and Shenandoah [4], [6], [8], extract the exact barrier protocols and phase machines, and compare them on a common footing — the invariant maintained, the mutator-visible protocol at each load and store, the phase pipeline, and the residual STW work. We then survey the published quantitative evidence and state, for each design, the *theoretical guarantee* its protocol actually provides, distinguishing implementer claims from provable bounds.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Azul C4: The Loaded Value Barrier and Self-Healing Reads

C4 is a *generational*, concurrent, always-compacting collector that runs two instances of a modified Pauseless GC algorithm simultaneously — one per generation — so young-generation collections proceed continuously even during a full-heap cycle [1]. Its entire concurrent protocol rests on a single mechanism: the **Loaded Value Barrier (LVB)**, a read barrier executed on *every reference load*.

The LVB exploits bits stolen from the 64-bit reference. On the custom Azul hardware the Pauseless collector used a **Not-Marked-Through (NMT)** bit: the barrier maintains a notion of the *desired* NMT value for the current GC phase and traps when a loaded reference disagrees [7]. On commodity x86, C4 generalizes this to the LVB, which verifies each loaded reference against *two* phase-dependent invariants — a marking invariant and a relocation invariant — in a single barrier. The barrier's fast path is a few instructions: test the reference's metadata bits against the expected phase value; if they match, the mutator proceeds at full speed.

When they do not match, the LVB takes corrective action in a slow-path trap — and here lies C4's signature property, **self-healing**.

```python
# Conceptual model of C4's Loaded Value Barrier (simplified)
def lvb_load(address, expected_mark, expected_reloc):
    ref = raw_load(address)                      # the raw 64-bit reference
    if ref.nmt_bit != expected_mark:             # marking-phase invariant
        ref = mark_trap(address, ref)            # queue with marker, fix NMT
        raw_cas(address, ref)                    # HEAL: fix the source slot
    if ref.reloc_bit != expected_reloc:          # relocation-phase invariant
        ref = relocate_trap(address, ref)        # resolve to tospace copy
        raw_cas(address, ref)                    # HEAL: fix the source slot
    return ref
```

The consequence is profound: *each reference slot in the heap triggers the LVB slow path at most once* (modulo rare atomicity races in healing). Once healed, the slot holds a reference satisfying the current phase invariants and never traps again during that phase. Since the number of reference slots is finite, the total slow-path work is bounded — marking completes in a single forward pass, and remapping completes in a single forward pass, with no repeated rescanning [1]. The mutators themselves perform the healing as they touch references, which distributes the remap work across the application threads and eliminates the separate remap pass that STW collectors require.

To support this on commodity x86, Azul modified the Linux virtual-memory subsystem: C4 needs extremely high rates of virtual-memory operations (page remapping for relocation) with low overhead, plus mechanisms for the LVB's expected-value switching at phase boundaries [1]. The generational design adds *simultaneous generational concurrency* — the young and old generations are collected by independent, concurrently active C4 instances, a property essentially no other generational collector offers.

### 4.2 ZGC: Colored Pointers and Load-Value Barriers over Multi-Mapped Memory

ZGC's core design choice, per JEP 333, is "the use of load barriers in combination with colored object pointers (i.e., colored oops)" [2]. A ZGC reference is 64 bits wide but carries only a 42-bit object address (in the original non-generational design), leaving the high bits free for **color metadata**. In non-generational ZGC, four metadata bits are defined [2], [3]:

| Bit(s) | Name | Meaning when set |
|---|---|---|
| 42 | *Marked0* | Object marked in mark phase, generation 0 |
| 43 | *Marked1* | Object marked in mark phase, generation 1 |
| 44 | *Remapped* | Reference already remapped to the relocated copy |
| 46 | *Finalizable* | Object needs finalization processing |

(The exact bit positions shifted across JDK releases; the architecture is what matters.) The two mark colors alternate between GC cycles so that marking the *previous* cycle's color never confuses the current one — a classic double-buffering trick that eliminates a global "clear all marks" pass.

The brilliant complement is **multi-mapped memory**: the heap is mapped into *three* virtual address ranges, one per "view" (Marked0, Marked1, Remapped). The same physical page is accessible through all three mappings. This means the load barrier never needs to strip or rewrite bits in software on the hot path: dereferencing a colored pointer through the appropriate mapping is done by the MMU, and the barrier only needs to check whether the color is *current* [3]. If a mutator loads a reference whose color indicates "not yet remapped" while the relocation phase has already moved the object, the load barrier slow path resolves the forwarding information and returns the tospace address — transparently, without stopping any thread.

```
                    64-bit ZGC colored pointer (non-generational layout)
 ┌──────────┬──────────┬────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
 │ metadata │ unused   │              object address (42 bits)          │
 │ 4 bits   │          │                                                │
 └──────────┴──────────┴────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
   M0 M1 R F                                    16 TB addressable heap

Load barrier (conceptual):
  ref = load(field)
  if (color(ref) != current_global_color):
      ref = slow_path_remap(ref)   # resolve forwarding, return tospace ptr
  use(ref)
```

Because the metadata lives *in the pointer*, ZGC can **reclaim and reuse memory during relocation before all pointers into the region have been fixed** — the old mapping simply stops being used once every live reference has been recolored through the barrier [2]. There is no need for a separate mark-compact fallback for full collections: the same mechanism handles everything. The generational ZGC reimplementation (JDK 21, default in later releases) changed the encoding — metadata moved to the *low* bits with the address in the high bits, and stack/register references became *colorless*, translated by the barriers — removing the multi-mapping and its triple-reported heap size while opening the door to larger heaps and more metadata bits [3].

### 4.3 Shenandoah: Brooks Forwarding Pointers and the Tospace Invariant

Shenandoah takes the oldest and most literal approach to concurrent relocation: **Brooks forwarding pointers**, proposed by R. A. Brooks in 1984 for real-time collection on stock hardware [5]. Every object header carries an extra word — the *forwarding pointer* — which at all times points to the "current" copy of the object. Before evacuation it points to the object itself; the evacuating thread copies the object to tospace, initializes the copy's forwarding pointer to itself, and then performs a single **CAS** installing the fromspace object's forwarding pointer to the new copy. Any thread that loses the CAS discovers the winner's copy through the forwarding pointer it just read [6].

The protocol maintains the **tospace invariant**: *all mutator writes go to the tospace copy, and all mutator reads resolve through the forwarding pointer to the tospace copy*. Two barriers enforce it [4], [6], [8]:

```python
# Shenandoah Brooks-pointer read path (conceptual)
def shenandoah_read(obj):
    # LRB / read barrier: resolve to the current (tospace) copy
    return obj.forwarding_pointer   # one extra indirection per read

def shenandoah_write(obj, field, value):
    # SATB write barrier first (for concurrent marking correctness)
    satb_enqueue(old_value_of(field))
    tospace = obj.forwarding_pointer
    tospace.field = value           # all writes land on the tospace copy

def evacuate(obj):                  # GC thread
    copy = allocate_in_tospace(obj.size)
    memcpy(copy, obj); copy.forwarding_pointer = copy
    if cas(obj.forwarding_pointer, obj, copy):
        return copy                 # we won the race
    else:
        discard(copy)               # lost: use obj.forwarding_pointer
        return obj.forwarding_pointer
```

Because every object is reachable through its forwarding pointer regardless of whether evacuation has reached it, the *update-references* phase — rewriting all heap pointers to the new locations — can proceed **concurrently at the collector's own pace**, even while mutators keep accessing both copies [6]. Once all references are updated, the fromspace copies are recycled. Shenandoah's marking is SATB-based (snapshot-at-the-beginning), which requires the write barrier above, and its cycle is *Init Mark → Concurrent Mark → Final Mark → Concurrent Evacuation → Init/Final Update Refs* [8]. Later Shenandoah versions supplemented Brooks pointers with a **Load Reference Barrier (LRB)** design closer in spirit to ZGC, trading the per-object header word for barrier-side resolution — but the tospace invariant remains the correctness core.

### 4.4 Phase Pipelines and the Residual Mutator–Collector Handshakes

All three collectors decompose a cycle into the same logical pipeline — **mark, relocate, remap** — but distribute the work differently across threads and time:

| Phase | C4 (LVB) | ZGC (colored ptrs) | Shenandoah (Brooks) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mark | Concurrent, incremental-update; mutators heal NMT bits on load | Concurrent, colored marking; load barrier keeps colors current | Concurrent SATB marking via write barrier |
| Relocate | Concurrent evacuation; LVB resolves stale refs to tospace | Concurrent relocation; load barrier remaps on access | Concurrent evacuation; forwarding-pointer CAS |
| Remap | **No separate phase** — mutators heal references at load; collector sweeps the rest concurrently | Concurrent remap; old mapping retired when recoloring completes | Concurrent update-references at collector pace |
| STW handshakes | Phase-boundary root handling, safepoint rendezvous | Root scanning at pause points (mark start/end, relocate start) | Init Mark, Final Mark, Init/Final Update Refs |

The crucial observation is what is *absent*: none of these pipelines contains a phase whose duration scales with the live set while mutators are stopped. The STW pauses that remain — JEP 333 limits them to root scanning [2]; Shenandoah's are the init/final marks [8] — scale with the *root set* (thread stacks), which is why measured pauses stay flat as heaps grow from gigabytes to terabytes. C4 pushes furthest: because even remapping is absorbed into mutator loads via self-healing, its STW component is essentially the phase-boundary rendezvous alone, and Azul's published data shows pauses dominated by thread-scheduling effects rather than GC work [1].

## 5. Empirical Results and Theoretical Guarantees

**Published pause behavior.** The three collectors report the same qualitative result through different measurement programs. Azul's C4 paper presents sustained-workload pause-time data alongside throughput comparisons against parallel and CMS collectors, showing worst-case pauses decoupled from heap occupancy [1]. ZGC's design goal, stated normatively in JEP 333, is pauses not exceeding 10 ms *and not increasing with heap or live-set size*, over heaps from 8 MB to 16 TB; subsequent development targeted sub-millisecond pauses, and production reports consistently show pauses well under 1 ms on multi-terabyte heaps [2], [3]. Shenandoah's PPPJ paper and the OpenJDK wiki report pauses independent of heap size, typically in the low single-digit milliseconds, dominated by root scanning [4], [8]. The honest summary: all three hold pauses roughly constant as heaps scale past 100 GB, where a parallel collector's pauses grow into seconds.

**Theoretical guarantees worth stating precisely.**

> **Theorem:** *At-most-once healing (C4).* In any GC phase, each heap reference slot triggers the LVB slow path at most once (excluding atomicity races in the healing CAS), because the handler writes back a reference satisfying the phase invariants. Hence the total barrier slow-path work per phase is O(number of reference slots), and both marking and remapping terminate in a single forward pass. ∎

> **Theorem:** *Tospace coherence (Shenandoah).* If every mutator read resolves through the forwarding pointer and every mutator write lands on the forwarding pointer's target, then at most one copy of each object is ever *observable* by mutators, and evacuation's losing CAS path is linearizable: the discarded copy is never referenced. ∎

> **Theorem:** *Color safety (ZGC).* If the load barrier maps every loaded reference to the current global color before the mutator dereferences it, and the collector reclaims a physical page only after all live references into it carry the Remapped color, then no mutator ever dereferences reclaimed memory. ∎

These are the actual load-bearing claims. Note what they do *not* promise: none bounds mutator *throughput* overhead, none bounds the *number* of concurrent GC threads needed to keep up with allocation, and none eliminates the root-scanning STW pauses — they only bound them by the root set.

## 6. Limitations

**Barrier tax on every load.** All three designs pay on the read path: C4's LVB is a few instructions plus a trap on mismatch; ZGC's load barrier is cheap in the multi-mapped design but still a check per load; Shenandoah's Brooks indirection adds a dependent load to *every* object access, measurably increasing cache pressure. Published throughput deltas versus parallel collectors are typically in the 5–20% range depending on workload and collector [4], [8]. This is the fundamental trade: tail latency is purchased with average throughput.

**Metadata and memory overhead.** Brooks forwarding costs one word per object header — a significant tax on small objects, and one reason later Shenandoah work moved toward LRB. ZGC's multi-mapped design triple-reports heap size to OS tooling and reserves large virtual ranges. C4 requires kernel enhancements for its virtual-memory operation rate and offers no compaction *reserve* shortcuts — it always compacts, always concurrently.

**Correctness complexity.** The protocols are subtle under weak memory models: the healing CAS, the evacuation CAS, and the color-transition fences must all be placed correctly or the tri-color invariant fails silently. Formal verification of such collectors remains an active research area, and production bugs in barrier fast paths have historically been among the hardest JVM bugs to diagnose.

**Residual STW and tuning surface.** Root scanning still stops the world, and the heuristics — cycle triggering, GC thread counts, region sizing, allocation pacing — form a tuning surface that, while smaller than G1's, still demands expertise.

## 7. Conclusion

C4's Loaded Value Barrier, ZGC's colored pointers, and Shenandoah's Brooks forwarding pointers are one idea in three encodings: **make the read path the synchronization point, and relocation never needs to stop the world**. C4 heals the reference *and its source slot* at load time, bounding total barrier work by the number of reference slots. ZGC colors the pointer itself and lets the MMU do the heavy lifting, reclaiming memory before remapping completes. Shenandoah pays a word per object for an indirection that linearizes concurrent evacuation through a single CAS. All three reduce the stop-the-world component to root scanning, making pause times independent of heap size and live set — the defining achievement of the pauseless generation.

The open frontier is cost, not correctness: shrinking the barrier tax, eliminating per-object metadata, and taming the heuristics. Recent work — generational ZGC's low-bit coloring, Shenandoah's LRB evolution, and research collectors building on these ideas — suggests the next decade will keep the pauseless guarantees while clawing back the throughput. The STW pause, as the dominant term in managed-runtime tail latency, is not coming back.
