# Tensor Network Methods for Nonequilibrium Quantum Dynamics: TEBD, the Time-Dependent Variational Principle, and the Entanglement Growth Barrier

## Abstract

This thesis surveys tensor network methods for simulating the real-time dynamics of quantum many-body systems. Beginning from matrix product states and their canonical forms, we develop the density matrix renormalization group as variational ground-state optimization, then pivot to the distinct algorithmic challenges of time evolution: the time-evolving block decimation algorithm with Suzuki–Trotter gates, the time-dependent variational principle on the MPS manifold, and the growth of entanglement entropy after quantum quenches. We connect these methods to rigorous results — Lieb–Robinson bounds, Hastings' one-dimensional area law and its proof via approximate ground-state projectors, and the Calabrese–Cardy conformal field theory of quench dynamics — and explain why dynamics encounters an entanglement barrier that static problems avoid: under unitary evolution, entanglement grows linearly in time inside a ballistic light cone, so fixed bond dimension approximations fail exponentially. Matrix product operators, open-system evolution, and two-dimensional projected entangled pair states and entanglement renormalization round out the picture. Limitations, complexity-theoretic obstructions, and future directions are assessed.

##1 Introduction

The quantum many-body problem is cursed by dimensionality: the Hilbert space of *N* spin-half particles has dimension 2^N, and generic states of such a system require an exponential number of parameters even to write down. Yet the states encountered in physics are not generic. Ground states of locally interacting Hamiltonians possess intricate but highly structured patterns of quantum entanglement, and the entire tensor network research program [7] is built on the insight that *entanglement structure* — not microscopic detail — determines whether a state admits an efficient classical description.

Tensor networks factorize the amplitude of a many-body wavefunction into a network of small tensors whose connectivity mirrors the geometry of the lattice. The workhorse in one dimension is the *matrix product state* (MPS), whose entanglement is quantified by the bond dimension *D*; states obeying an *area law* for entanglement entropy can be approximated by MPS with modest bond dimension [1][2]. For static ground-state problems, this correspondence is now both rigorous and practical: Hastings' proof of the area law for gapped one-dimensional systems [1] was sharpened by Arad, Kitaev, Landau and Vazirani into a sub-exponential simulation algorithm via Chebyshev-based approximate ground-space projectors, and the density matrix renormalization group (DMRG) — understood since Östlund and Rommer as variational optimization over MPS — routinely achieves energies accurate to many significant figures [3].

Dynamics is a different beast. Under a quantum quench, where a parameter of the Hamiltonian is suddenly changed, entanglement does *not* stay at its area-law ground-state value. It grows *linearly* in time inside a ballistic light cone, a prediction of conformal field theory verified in cold-atom experiments and tensor network simulations alike. A fixed bond dimension can then only track the evolution up to times logarithmic in the computational effort: the *entanglement growth barrier*. This thesis develops the tensor network machinery for nonequilibrium dynamics — the time-evolving block decimation (TEBD) algorithm, the time-dependent variational principle (TDVP), matrix product operators, and higher-dimensional extensions — against the backdrop of the rigorous entanglement bounds that delimit what any classical algorithm can hope to achieve.

##2 Background

### Matrix Product States and the Schmidt Decomposition

Consider a chain of *N* sites, each with local Hilbert space of dimension *d*. A *matrix product state* writes the wavefunction amplitudes as a product of site-indexed matrices [3][4]:

> **Theorem:** Every state of the chain admits an MPS representation with bond dimension at most exponential in *N*; moreover, the *minimal* bond dimension across a bipartition equals the *Schmidt rank* of the state with respect to that cut.

Concretely, let the chain be split between sites *ℓ* and *ℓ+1*. The Schmidt decomposition reads

```python
# Schmidt decomposition across a cut: the bond dimension D is the
# number of retained singular values lambda_i
# S(vN) = -sum_i lambda_i^2 log(lambda_i^2)  <- von Neumann entropy
# S(Renyi, alpha) = (1/(1-alpha)) log sum_i lambda_i^(2 alpha)
```

so that an MPS with bond dimension *D* can carry at most *log D* units of entanglement entropy across any cut: *S(ρ_A) ≤ log D* (in natural units). This single inequality is the engine of the whole field [4]. It means that *representational power is entanglement power*: the bond dimension is not a mere truncation parameter but a direct measure of how much entanglement the ansatz can encode.

### Canonical Forms and Gauge Freedom

The matrices defining an MPS are far from unique. Inserting any invertible *D*-by-*D* matrix *X* and its inverse between two sites,

```python
# Gauge freedom: A[s_i] -> A[s_i] X ; A[s_{i+1}] -> X^{-1} A[s_{i+1}]
# leaves the physical state invariant while changing all tensors.
```

leaves the physical state unchanged [3]. This *gauge freedom* is exploited to bring the MPS into *canonical forms*:

- **Left-canonical form:** every tensor satisfies a left-normalization condition, so that the reduced density matrix of the left block is the identity.
- **Right-canonical form:** the mirror condition for the right block.
- **Mixed-canonical form:** the orthogonality center sits at a chosen site, with left-canonical tensors to its left and right-canonical tensors to its right.

In mixed-canonical form the *entanglement spectrum* — the eigenvalues of the reduced density matrix, equivalently the squares of the Schmidt values — appears explicitly at the orthogonality center. The entanglement spectrum encodes far more than the entropy: its level statistics diagnose topological order (the Li–Haldane conjecture relates entanglement spectra to physical edge modes), and its decay determines how accurately an MPS of fixed bond dimension approximates the true state [4]. Truncation of the smallest Schmidt values is *optimal* in the two-norm by the Eckart–Young theorem, which is why every dynamical algorithm below is built on singular value decompositions.

### Entanglement Area Laws

For a region *A* of a lattice, the *area law* states that the ground-state entanglement entropy scales with the boundary of *A* rather than its volume: *S(ρ_A) = O(|∂A|)* [2]. Key facts:

| Setting | Status of the area law | Scaling |
|---|---|---|
| 1D gapped Hamiltonians | **Proven** (Hastings [1]; sharpened bounds [AGSP literature]) | *S = O(1)* |
| 1D critical (CFT) | Logarithmic violation | *S ∼ (c/3) log L* |
| 2D gapped | Conjectured; proven for frustration-free / special classes | *S = O(L)* boundary |
| 2D with Fermi surface | Logarithmic violation | *S ∼ L log L* |
| Excited / thermal states | Volume law (generic) | *S ∼ vol(A)* |

The area law matters because MPS automatically satisfy it: an MPS in one dimension has *S ≤ log D = O(1)* across any cut [4]. The converse — that every state obeying a (Rényi) area law is well approximated by an MPS of polynomial bond dimension — is a theorem of Verstraete–Cirac and refined versions [4], and it is precisely what justifies the DMRG enterprise. In two dimensions, projected entangled pair states (PEPS) satisfy the boundary law by construction, but *contracting* a PEPS is generically #P-complete, a complexity-theoretic wall absent in one dimension [7].

##3 Methodology

### The Density Matrix Renormalization Group as Variational MPS Optimization

DMRG was introduced by White in 1992 as a real-space renormalization procedure that retains, at each step, the eigenvectors of the block *reduced density matrix* with largest eigenvalues. The modern understanding, developed by Östlund, Rommer, Verstraete, Cirac and systematized by Schollwöck [3], is that DMRG is *variational optimization within the MPS manifold*:

1. Write the Hamiltonian itself as a *matrix product operator* (MPO), a chain of rank-four tensors carrying two physical and two virtual indices.
2. Fix all MPS tensors except those on one or two sites; the energy expectation becomes a quadratic form in the free tensors.
3. Minimize via the effective eigenvalue problem *H_eff v = E N_eff v*; in canonical form *N_eff* is the identity.
4. Sweep left-to-right and right-to-left, truncating by SVD at each step, until the energy converges.

> **Theorem:** Two-site DMRG, in the limit of vanishing truncation, converges to the exact ground state within the MPS manifold of the current bond dimension; the discarded weight *ε = Σ_{i>D} λᵢ²* bounds the approximation error in the two-norm.

The practical consequence is a method whose cost scales as *O(N D³)* per sweep and whose energies for gapped one-dimensional chains are often correct to ten or more significant digits [3]. The same variational machinery underlies time evolution: every algorithm in Section 4 is, at heart, a method for keeping a time-evolved state on or near the MPS manifold.

### From Static Area Laws to Dynamical Questions

The area-law technology of [1][2] guarantees efficient *static* representations, but a quench drives the state far from any gapped ground state. The methodological question is therefore sharper for dynamics than for statics: given that entanglement *will* grow, how do we (i) apply the unitary evolution operator to an MPS, (ii) choose the optimal truncation at each step, and (iii) know when the representation has failed? The three workhorse answers are TEBD, TDVP, and the entanglement-barrier analysis, developed in depth in Section 4. The infinite-system variants (iTEBD [6]) additionally allow simulations directly in the thermodynamic limit using translation-invariant MPS, with the same per-step structure.

##4 Tensor-Network Time Evolution: Algorithms, Geometry, and Physical Limits

### Time-Evolving Block Decimation: Trotter Gates and Local Truncation

The time-evolving block decimation algorithm, introduced by Vidal [6][7], evolves an MPS by discretizing the unitary *U(δt) = exp(−iHδt)* into a product of local two-site gates. For a Hamiltonian that is a sum of nearest-neighbor terms, the second-order Suzuki–Trotter decomposition gives

*U(δt) ≈ Π_{odd bonds} exp(−i h_{j,j+1} δt/2) · Π_{even bonds} exp(−i h_{j,j+1} δt) · Π_{odd bonds} exp(−i h_{j,j+1} δt/2)*

with Trotter error *O(δt³)* per step. Each gate application proceeds as follows:

1. Contract the gate with the two neighboring MPS tensors, forming a *d² × D²*-dimensional object.
2. Perform an SVD and truncate to bond dimension *D*, keeping the largest singular values.
3. Update the site tensors with the truncated factors (using canonical form to keep the truncation optimal).

Because the gate only ever touches two sites, the cost per gate is *O(d³ D³)*, and a full time step costs *O(N D³)*. The *infinite* TEBD (iTEBD) variant [6] exploits translational invariance to simulate infinite chains, and was extended by Orús and Vidal to non-unitary evolution — master-equation dynamics, thermal states via imaginary-time evolution, and two-dimensional classical partition functions [6]. TEBD's weakness is its *local* truncation: each gate's SVD is optimal only for that bond at that instant, ignoring correlations with the rest of the chain, which motivates the global variational approach of TDVP.

### The Time-Dependent Variational Principle: Geometry of the MPS Manifold

The time-dependent variational principle (TDVP) replaces local gate-by-gate truncation with a *global* geometric projection. The Dirac–Frenkel variational principle demands that the MPS velocity *|Ψ̇⟩* be the best approximation, in the two-norm, to the exact Schrödinger velocity *−iH|Ψ⟩* within the *tangent space* of the MPS manifold:

> **Theorem:** TDVP integrates the projected equation *i|Ψ̇⟩ = P_T(−iH|Ψ⟩)* where *P_T* projects onto the tangent space of the MPS manifold. The scheme conserves energy and norm exactly, and reduces to single- and two-site updates that never inflate the bond dimension beyond its current value.

In practice TDVP is implemented by a Lie–Trotter splitting over sites (one-site TDVP, cost *O(D³)*) or bonds (two-site TDVP, which permits bond-dimension growth, cost *O(D³)* with larger prefactor). Two facts make TDVP the method of choice for long-time evolution:

- *No Trotterization of the Hamiltonian* is required: *H* enters only through its MPO action, so long-range interactions are handled as easily as nearest-neighbor ones.
- *Global optimality*: the projection is optimal over the whole chain at each instant, unlike TEBD's bond-local truncations.

The price is that the tangent-space projector is singular at points where the MPS loses rank, requiring careful regularization; and that TDVP, like TEBD, is ultimately defeated by the same entanglement growth barrier.

### Entanglement Growth After Quenches: Light Cones and the Entanglement Barrier

Consider a *global quench*: the system is prepared in the ground state of *H₀* and evolved under a different Hamiltonian *H*. Calabrese and Cardy's conformal field theory analysis predicts that the entanglement entropy of a block of length *ℓ* grows as

*S(t) ∝ t* for *t < ℓ/2v*, then saturates at *S ∝ ℓ*,

where *v* is the maximal quasiparticle velocity. Entangled pairs of quasiparticles emitted at the quench propagate ballistically, and entropy grows because an ever-larger set of pairs straddles the bipartition — a *light cone* with slope set by the Lieb–Robinson velocity, the quantum analogue of a speed of light [2].

The computational consequence is brutal. Since an MPS can carry at most *log D* entropy per cut, tracking evolution to time *t* requires *D ∼ exp(const · t)*: the bond dimension must grow *exponentially* with simulation time. This is the **entanglement growth barrier**, and it is *not* an artifact of any particular algorithm — it is information-theoretic. Any tensor network with bounded bond dimension fails after *t = O(log D)*. Empirically, the barrier is clearly visible: in transverse-field Ising quenches, the half-chain entropy rises linearly with a slope matching the Lieb–Robinson velocity, then the fixed-*D* truncation error explodes [4]. Integrable systems offer a partial reprieve (quasiparticle pictures allow tailored ansätze), and *dissipative* or *monitored* dynamics can suppress the growth, but for generic chaotic unitary evolution the barrier is the fundamental limit of classical tensor network simulation.

### Beyond Pure States: Matrix Product Operators, Open Systems, and Two Dimensions

Real experiments are never perfectly isolated, and two natural extensions of the MPS formalism address this:

- **Matrix product operators (MPOs)** represent operators — Hamiltonians, thermal density matrices *exp(−βH)*, and time-evolution superoperators — as tensor networks with doubled physical indices. Imaginary-time evolution *|ψ(τ)⟩ ∝ exp(−Hτ)|ψ₀⟩* applied via TEBD/TEBD-like updates yields ground states (the *finite-temperature* analogue prepares Gibbs states), while real-time evolution of *density matrices* under Lindblad master equations proceeds by vectorizing *ρ* and evolving an MPS in the doubled space [6].
- **Two-dimensional dynamics** uses projected entangled pair states (PEPS): each site tensor carries four virtual bonds to its neighbors, automatically encoding the boundary law *S = O(L)*. Time evolution applies Trotter gates as in TEBD, but truncation requires an *environment* — approximated by the simple update (local), full update, or corner-transfer-matrix renormalization group (CTMRG) — because exact PEPS contraction is #P-complete [7]. The *multiscale entanglement renormalization ansatz* (MERA), developed by Vidal and extended to two dimensions by Evenbly and Vidal [5], adds disentanglers and isometries in a hierarchical network that efficiently encodes the *logarithmic* entanglement of critical systems, where both MPS and PEPS struggle.

| Method | Geometry | Cost per step | Handles | Barrier |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| TEBD | 1D MPS | *O(N D³)* | Nearest-neighbor unitary | Linear *S(t)* growth |
| iTEBD | Infinite 1D | *O(D³)* | Thermodynamic limit, thermal states [6] | Same |
| TDVP | 1D MPS manifold | *O(N D³)* | Long-range *H*, conserved quantities | Same |
| PEPS + updates | 2D | *O(D^10)*–*O(D^12)* | 2D quenches, small systems | Contraction is #P-hard |
| MERA | Hierarchical | *O(χ^16)* (2D) | Critical ground states [5] | Log-scaling only |

##5 Empirical Results and Proofs

The rigorous backbone of tensor network dynamics rests on three pillars:

1. **Lieb–Robinson bounds.** For Hamiltonians with short-range interactions there exists a velocity *v_LR* such that commutators of local operators separated by distance *x* are bounded by *exp(−μ(x − v_LR t))*. This emergent causality underlies the light-cone picture of quench dynamics and the area-law proofs [2]: correlations (and hence entanglement) cannot be created faster than *v_LR*.
2. **Hastings' area law and its strengthenings.** Hastings [1] proved that ground states of gapped one-dimensional Hamiltonians satisfy *S = O(1)*; Arad, Kitaev, Landau and Vazirani gave a new proof via Chebyshev-polynomial approximate ground-space projectors with an exponentially improved bound *S = O(log³ d / ε)* in the gap *ε*, plus a sub-exponential classical algorithm. The key lemma — that powers *H^ℓ* of a one-dimensional Hamiltonian have entanglement rank at most *(ℓd)^{O(√ℓ)}* — directly controls how well MPS approximate ground states.
3. **Calabrese–Cardy quench CFT.** For (1+1)-dimensional critical systems, the post-quench entanglement entropy of an interval grows linearly with slope proportional to the central charge *c*, then saturates: *S(t) ≈ (π c / 3β) t* at early times. Tensor network simulations of Ising and XXZ quenches reproduce both the linear ramp and the saturation quantitatively, validating TEBD/TDVP against exact CFT in the regime before the entanglement barrier bites.

On the practical side, DMRG ground-state energies for the spin-half Heisenberg chain with *D = 200* match Bethe-ansatz results to better than one part in 10^8 [3]; TEBD simulations of the Bose–Hubbard superfluid-to-Mott quench reproduce the experimentally observed light-cone spreading of correlations; and iPEPS with CTMRG environments now reaches bond dimensions *D ≈ 10* for two-dimensional ground states, competitive with quantum Monte Carlo where the sign problem permits comparison [5][7].

> **Theorem (informal):** For any family of one-dimensional states whose Rényi entropies satisfy *S_α(ρ_A) ≤ c log n + C* for all *0 < α < 1*, there exist MPS approximations of bond dimension polynomial in *n* and *1/ε* achieving two-norm error *ε* [4]. This is the precise sense in which area laws *are* efficient classical descriptions.

##6 Limitations

- **The entanglement barrier is fundamental.** Volume-law states — highly excited eigenstates, long-time-evolved states, thermal states at finite energy density — require *D ∼ exp(S)*, i.e., exponential resources. No algorithmic cleverness circumvents an information-theoretic bound; tensor networks are a theory of *low-entanglement* states, full stop.
- **Two-dimensional contraction is #P-complete.** PEPS can *represent* area-law states in 2D, but computing expectation values requires contracting a 2D network, which is #P-complete in the worst case [7]. All practical algorithms (simple/full update, CTMRG, boundary MPS) are uncontrolled approximations whose errors are hard to bound.
- **Critical and gapless systems.** A (1+1)D critical ground state has *S ∼ (c/3) log L*, requiring *D ∼ L^{c/3}*: polynomial but often large. MERA [5] handles this scaling natively, but its optimization is far more demanding than DMRG.
- **Fermions and the sign structure.** Fermionic tensor networks require graded (parity-symmetric) tensors and careful ordering of contraction to respect anticommutation; while well understood, this doubles implementation complexity and can worsen conditioning.
- **TDVP rank deficiency and TEBD Trotter errors.** TDVP's tangent-space projector is singular when Schmidt values vanish, needing regularization that can bias long-time results; TEBD accumulates both Trotter error *O(δt²)* per step and truncation error, and the two cannot be reduced simultaneously without increasing cost.
- **Gauge and conditioning.** The MPS gauge freedom, while useful for canonical forms, means the variational landscape has flat directions; ill-conditioned environments in PEPS updates can cause catastrophic loss of precision.

##7 Conclusion

Tensor networks translate the abstract statement "entanglement is the resource that makes quantum states hard" into concrete, working algorithms. In one dimension the story is essentially complete for statics: area laws are theorems [1], MPS approximate area-law states with polynomial bond dimension [4], and DMRG-as-variational-MPS [3] is the gold standard for ground states. For dynamics, the same machinery — TEBD's Trotterized gates [6], TDVP's geometric projection onto the MPS manifold, MPO representations of operators and open-system evolution — pushes classical simulation to the very edge of what entanglement permits, and the entanglement growth barrier marks that edge precisely: linear entropy growth inside the Lieb–Robinson light cone forces exponential bond dimension in time.

The frontier is therefore not a better truncation scheme but a better understanding of *which* dynamical questions admit low-entanglement answers. Hydrodynamic observables, local thermalization, and short-time response functions are often captured long after the global state has become unrepresentable; dissipative and monitored dynamics can exhibit entanglement *transitions* that tensor networks resolve sharply; and in two dimensions, the interplay of PEPS representability with #P-hard contraction remains the central open problem [5][7]. Tensor networks will not simulate arbitrary quantum dynamics — complexity theory forbids it — but they delineate, with unusual precision, exactly where classical simulability ends and genuine quantum advantage begins.
