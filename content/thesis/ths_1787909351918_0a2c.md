# High-Throughput DAG-Based BFT Ordering: Narwhal Mempool Tiering and Batch Asynchronous Persistent Storage, Bullshark without Timeout Liveness, and Sailfish Quorum-Across-Views Reconfiguration

## Abstract
We analyze high-throughput DAG-based BFT built around Narwhal's separation of dissemination and ordering, persistent batch storage, and quorum-certified availability. Narwhal workers disseminate transaction batches via reliable broadcast, producing certificates of availability that form a causal DAG replicated by validators [1]. Bullshark then orders this DAG without view-change or view-synchronization, using anchor commits and deterministic linearization that exploits non-equivocation [2]. Sailfish extends the model with per-round leaders and quorum-across-views safety to avoid lost messages during reconfiguration [3]. Comparing to HotStuff's three-chain responsiveness [4] and deployed systems like Aptos [5] and Mysticeti [6], we formalize certificate availability, quorum intersection safety, and reconfiguration correctness. We qualify literature throughput of 130k–170k tx/sec for Narwhal-HotStuff and 160k for Tusk on WAN [1], noting liveness benefits under faults.

## 1 Introduction

DAG-based BFT reframes consensus: instead of a leader driving both data dissemination and ordering, Narwhal decouples them [1]. The mempool layer ensures that transaction batches are widely available and stored persistently, while a consensus layer interprets the DAG structure. This thesis examines that stack: Narwhal tiering, Bullshark without timeouts, and Sailfish quorum-across-views reconfiguration.

> **Central thesis:** Certificate-backed availability plus DAG causality enables deterministic ordering without extra communication, preserving throughput under asynchrony and eliminating timeout-driven view changes in the common case.

HotStuff introduced linear communication and responsiveness via threshold signatures and chained phases [4], but still suffers liveness hiccups during view changes when composed naively with a mempool. Narwhal-HotStuff composition maintains high throughput despite failures, albeit with higher latency under view-change [1]. Tusk removes message overhead entirely by embedding asynchronous consensus inside Narwhal [1]. Bullshark generalizes this to partial synchrony, attaining amortized complexity without view-change or view-synchronization [2][7].

This work synthesizes deployment lessons from Aptos [5] and Sui's Mysticeti [6], where uncertified DAGs lower CPU overhead and push WAN commit latency toward 0.5s.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Model and Threat

We assume $n=3f+1$ validators, at most $f$ Byzantine, partially synchronous network with eventual GST, and asynchronous fallback for Tusk. Reliable broadcast (RBC) prevents equivocation: if two honest parties hold a vertex $v$ by party $p$ in round $r$, its causal history is identical [2]. Quorum $q=2f+1$ ensures intersection in at least one honest validator.

### 2.2 Historical Evolution

Early BFT (PBFT, Tendermint) interleaved dissemination and ordering, incurring $O(n^2)$ view-change. HotStuff reduced authenticator complexity to $O(n)$ with threshold signatures and a Pacemaker [4]. Narwhal introduced worker sharding: multiple workers per validator produce batches in parallel, linearly increasing throughput to 600k tx/sec reported with no latency increase [1].

Bullshark optimizes the synchronous case, exploiting DAG structure to avoid complex view synchronization [2]. The SoK survey notes Bullshark attains the most performant and robust behavior among partially synchronous DAG variants [7]. Sailfish further reduces latency by assigning a leader every round and allowing commitment before BRB delivery, via no-vote proofs [3][7]. Mysticeti removes certificates entirely, achieving 3-message lower bound with single signature per block [6].

## 3 Methodology

Our method follows **trace → certificate → DAG → order → reconfig**.

1. **Batch dissemination:** Workers produce batches $B$ of $\approx$500 KB transactions. Primary proposes header referencing batch digests. Validators sign header availability. Certificate $C(B)$ requires $2f+1$ signatures, proving at least $f+1$ honest hold $B$.
2. **Persistent storage:** Narwhal requires causal history storage in RocksDB. A vertex $v$ in round $r$ references $2f+1$ vertices from $r-1$ [1][3]. Completeness property ensures any honest validator holding $v$ eventually reconstructs its full ancestry via deterministic pull from signers in $C$.
3. **DAG construction:** Each validator moves to round $r$ after observing $2f+1$ round $r-1$ certificates. Non-equivocation via RBC ensures at most one vertex per $(p,r)$.
4. **Ordering logic:** Bullshark observes local DAG without extra messages. Even rounds have predefined anchors. A vertex in odd round $r$ votes for anchor in $r-1$ if edge exists. Anchor commits if $f+1$ votes [2]. Then causal history linearized deterministically.
5. **Reconfiguration:** Sailfish requires that a round $r$ leader includes either round $r-1$ leader or proof of $2f$ round $r$ vertices not voting for it, together with itself forming $2f+1$ non-votes [3]. This quorum-across-views prevents committing a skipped leader after reconfiguration.

> **Theorem 3.1 (Certificate Availability).** *If certificate $C(v)$ exists, at least $f+1$ honest validators store $v$ and its causal predecessors are retrievable.* Proof by quorum intersection and reliable storage replication from [1][2].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Architectural Model and Cost Semantics

State $S = (M, D, O, Q)$ where $M$ mempool certificate set, $D$ DAG vertices, $O$ ordered log, $Q$ pending queue. Transition costs: dissemination $C_{diss}=O(|B|/bw)$, certification $C_{cert}=O(n)$ sigs, DAG advance $C_{dag}=O(n^2)$ edges worst but amortized $O(n)$ with threshold aggregation, ordering $C_{ord}=0$ messages [2].

Tiering:

- **Worker tier:** parallel batch creation, erasure-coded if needed, signature collection off critical path.
- **Primary tier:** certificate broadcast, round advancement.
- **Storage tier:** persistent batch availability, pull-based recovery.

Quorum intersection safety: any two quorums $Q1,Q2$ of size $2f+1$ intersect in at least $f+1$ validators, at least one honest. Therefore if anchor $A$ has $f+1$ votes, no conflicting anchor can gather $2f+1$ non-votes. Bullshark leverages this without timeouts [2].

> **Lemma 4.1 (Completeness).** *If honest $p_i,p_j$ hold vertex $v$, their causal histories of $v$ are identical.* Follows from non-equivocation of RBC and quorum-certified parents [2][3].

Backpressure and fairness: Shoal framework interleaves Bullshark instances to reduce latency of non-leader blocks [7]. HammerHead reputation scoring can bias leader schedule toward performant validators without breaking determinism.

### 4.2 Core Algorithmic Innovation

**Narwhal innovation:** High-throughput reliable dissemination with causal histories. Unlike simple mempool, Narwhal stores causal histories, enabling validators to order without additional dissemination. Evaluation shows no foreseeable throughput limit with workers, scaling linearly [1].

**Bullshark innovation:** Eliminate view-change. Traditional BFT needs Pacemaker timeouts to synchronize views after leader failure. In DAG, full information is encoded in DAG structure. Bullshark observes local view and totally orders without extra communication. Commit rule: anchor $A_r$ committed if $\ge f+1$ votes in round $r+1$; skipped otherwise. Indirect rule allows commit via future anchor's causal history [2].

Formally:

- Anchors are solid green in even rounds.
- Votes are edges from odd round vertices.
- Deterministic linearization: sort causal predecessors by round, then by author id.

This yields amortized complexity and addresses fairness via ordered causal histories, not leader-only payloads.

**Sailfish innovation:** Per-round leaders and 1-RBC+ $1\delta$ commit [7]. Restriction 1 ensures leader continuity: new leader must reference previous leader or present no-vote proof of $2f+1$ vertices not referencing it. This prevents lost messages across views: if a leader was committed by some honest validator, at least $f+1$ honest reference it, so no quorum of $2f+1$ non-references can exist.

> **Lemma 4.2 (Sailfish Safety).** *If leader $L_r$ is directly committed, no future leader $L_{r+1}$ can present valid no-vote quorum against $L_r$.* Proof by counting: $f+1$ honest voters for $L_r$ plus $2f+1$ non-voters would exceed $n$.

**Mysticeti contrast:** Removes certification to achieve 3-message lower bound, requiring only one signature per block and minimizing CPU. Adopted by Sui, reducing p50 from 1.9s to 0.4s on 106 validators [6].

```python
# Sailfish no-vote quorum check
def can_propose_leader(dag, r, leader_r_minus_1):
    if leader_r_minus_1 in dag.refs(dag.current_proposal(r)):
        return True
    non_voters = [v for v in dag.vertices(r) if leader_r_minus_1 not in dag.children(v)]
    # need 2f non-voters + self = 2f+1
    return len(non_voters) >= 2*F  # plus leader self

def direct_commit(dag, anchor_round):
    votes = dag.vote_count(anchor_round)
    return votes >= 2*F+1  # Sailfish direct rule via quorum of votes [3]
```

### 4.3 Composition, Pipelining, and Runtime Interaction

Pipeline: $Worker \rightarrow Primary \rightarrow DAG \rightarrow BullsharkInterpreter \rightarrow Execution$.

- **Backpressure:** If $Q_{mempool} > Q_{max}$, workers stall batch creation. This mitigates mempool explosions where adversary inflates uncommitted vertices [7].
- **Batching:** Narwhal batches transactions, amortizing signature verification. Workers shard by transaction hash, avoiding duplication.
- **Credit-based flow:** Primary credits workers to bound memory. On certificate creation, credits replenished.
- **Pipelining:** Narwhal-HotStuff pipelines dissemination and consensus: HotStuff proposes certificates, not transactions, reducing leader load from $O(n \cdot |tx|)$ to $O(n \cdot |cert|)$ [1].

View handling: Bullshark needs neither view-change nor view-synchronization because DAG itself encodes timing. If anchor in round $r$ not committed, protocol skips it deterministically and tries $r+2$. No timeout complaints needed [2]. This contrasts HotStuff where Pacemaker drives view progression with $O(n)$ authenticators [4].

Failure handling:

- **Slow validator:** Its vertices missing from some DAGs, but $2f+1$ quorum ensures progress. Completeness allows pull.
- **Byzantine equivocation:** Prevented by RBC; certificate requires $2f+1$ signatures on same digest.
- **Reconfiguration:** Sailfish ensures that during committee rotation, messages from old view not lost because new leader must either reference or prove non-commit of old leader [3].

Aptos deployment uses Narwhal-like mempool with Bullshark ordering, demonstrating production robustness under WAN and crash faults [5].

### 4.4 Resource Accounting and Quantitative Modeling

Literature-reported (not experimentally re-measured here) performance on WAN with $n=10$–$50$ validators:

- Narwhal-HotStuff: 130k–170k tx/sec at <2.5s latency vs 1.8k for HotStuff alone; linear scaling to 600k with workers [1].
- Tusk: 160k tx/sec at ~3s, 20× over Dumbo [1].
- Mysticeti: 0.5s WAN commit at 200k+ TPS, first Byzantine to achieve 3-message lower bound [6].

Resource model:

- **Network:** Each vertex references $2f+1$ parents → $O(n^2)$ hashes per round worst, but hash size small (32 B). Batch payload dominates.
- **Storage:** Persistent batch storage $O(|tx|)$ per validator, replicated $f+1$ honest. RocksDB sequential writes favored.
- **CPU:** Signature verification $2f+1$ per certificate; Mysticeti reduces to 1 per block [6].
- **Latency breakdown:** Bullshark: 3$\delta$ optimistic (DAG push), 6$\delta$ consensus [7]. Sailfish: 4$\delta$ push, 5$\delta$ consensus, no pull [7]. Beluga synchronizer can reduce push to $\delta$ [7].

Energy/carbon considerations are workload-dependent; we do not claim carbon optimality. Synchronization overhead dominates under faults: Narwhal-HotStuff suffers latency increase due to HotStuff view-change, while Bullshark/Tusk maintain throughput [1].

> **Lemma 4.3 (Quorum Intersection Safety).** *If anchor $A_r$ committed by honest validator, no conflicting anchor $A'_r$ can be committed.* Follows from $f+1$ honest votes intersecting any $2f+1$ quorum.

> **Lemma 4.4 (Reconfiguration Across Views).** *Sailfish leader restriction preserves safety across reconfiguration: old committed leader remains in causal history of any future valid leader.* Proof via no-vote quorum impossibility as above [3].

## 6 Limitations

- **Certification overhead:** Narwhal/Bullshark require $2f+1$ signatures per vertex, CPU heavy at scale. Mysticeti shows uncertified path viable [6].
- **Storage amplification:** Causal histories store hashes of $2f+1$ parents per vertex, $O(n^2)$ metadata worst case.
- **Mempool explosion:** Bounded memory model not guaranteed; Lifefin shows adversary can force fallback to ACS if uncommitted vertices exceed quota [7]. Quota tuning needed.
- **Implementation gap:** Our analysis is specification-level; no Rust production artifact evaluated. Scale verification beyond $n=4$ needs parameterized model checking.
- **Synchrony assumption:** Bullshark partially synchronous version assumes eventual synchrony for liveness; asynchronous version (full Bullshark) needs additional randomization not detailed here [2].
- **Fairness:** Leader-based ordering may censor non-leader payloads if not interleaved; Shoal pipelining addresses but not proven here.
- **Reconfiguration liveness:** Sailfish no-vote mechanism ensures safety but may delay commitment if Byzantine leaders withhold references; HammerHead reputation mitigates partially [7].

Future: integrate Beluga synchronizer for optimal $\delta$ push latency [7], signature-free RBC as in Sailfish++ [7], and Mysticeti fast path for owned objects.

## 7 Conclusion

We reconstructed high-throughput DAG BFT from first principles: Narwhal separates data dissemination via quorum-certified batches and persistent storage [1], Bullshark orders the resulting DAG without timeouts or view synchronization via anchor voting [2], and Sailfish tightens reconfiguration with quorum-across-views no-vote proofs enabling per-round leaders [3]. Compared to HotStuff's three-chain [4], this stack moves cost from consensus to dissemination, achieving reported WAN throughput two orders of magnitude higher while preserving liveness under faults. Deployed systems Aptos [5] and Sui/Mysticeti [6] validate the architecture, with uncertified DAGs pushing latency to 0.5s. The SoK taxonomy clarifies tradeoffs in push vs consensus latency and pull complexity [7]. Remaining challenges are mempool boundedness, certification CPU, and parameterized verification.

Contributions: unified cost model for dissemination vs ordering, formalization of certificate availability and quorum intersection safety, Sailfish reconfiguration safety argument, and literature-grounded performance qualification without claiming new experiments.

*Takeaway:* DAG structure encodes enough information to remove view-change, provided certificates guarantee availability and RBC prevents equivocation; reconfiguration then reduces to enforcing quorum inclusion across views.
