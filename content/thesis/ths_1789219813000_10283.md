# Predictive Coding in the Cerebral Cortex: Hierarchical Generative Models, Precision-Weighted Prediction Errors, and the Bayesian Brain Hypothesis

## Abstract

Predictive coding proposes that the cerebral cortex implements hierarchical Bayesian inference by generating top-down predictions of sensory input and transmitting only the resulting prediction errors bottom-up. This article develops the theory from the Rao–Ballard model of visual cortex [1] through its variational formulation under the free-energy principle [2], its proposed laminar microcircuitry [3], and its claim to canonical status as a cortical computation [4]. We present the mathematical machinery — hierarchical Gaussian generative models, precision-weighted prediction errors, and gradient descent on variational free energy — and survey the empirical record: mismatch responses in rodent visual cortex, frequency-asymmetric feedforward/feedback signaling, and psychophysical expectation suppression. We deep-dive into the signed-error problem, precision as attention, error-driven plasticity, and the deep-learning connection, before assessing the strongest objections: that cortex may not represent errors at all, and that many classic findings admit alternative explanations. The thesis concludes that predictive coding remains the most complete algorithmic-level account of cortical function, provided its core commitments are stated precisely enough to be falsifiable.

## 1 Introduction

The dominant narrative of cortical function for much of the twentieth century was feedforward and feature-detector based: the retina encodes light, the lateral geniculate nucleus relays it, and the cortex builds ever more complex feature detectors culminating in "grandmother cells" selective for faces and objects. This picture, descended from Hubel and Wiesel's Nobel-winning work, is *bottom-up*, *passive*, and *representational* — the cortex mirrors the world. Yet two facts strain it badly. First, anatomical studies reveal that feedback connections from higher to lower cortical areas outnumber feedforward connections, an extravagance the feedforward narrative cannot explain. Second, responses throughout the sensory hierarchy are strongly modulated by expectation, context, and attention in ways that simple filtering models fail to predict.

Predictive coding inverts the hierarchy's job description. Each cortical level maintains a *generative model* of the activity at the level below and issues *predictions* of that activity via descending connections; ascending connections carry only the *prediction error* — the residual between prediction and actual input [1]. Perception is then not the passive registration of features but an active process of *explaining away* the sensory stream: descending predictions are adjusted until the residual is minimized. This article develops the theory rigorously. Section 2 traces the lineage from Helmholtz's unconscious inference through Barlow's efficient coding, Mumford's thalamocortical theory, and the Rao–Ballard simulations. Section 3 lays out the variational mathematics. Section 4 deep-dives into four hard problems: the sign of prediction errors, precision and attention, synaptic plasticity, and the deep-learning connection. Section 5 surveys the empirical evidence and its limits. Sections 6 and 7 confront objections and draw conclusions.

> **Hypothesis:** The cortex minimizes variational free energy with respect to its internal states, such that neural dynamics implement approximate Bayesian inference over the hidden causes of sensation.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 From unconscious inference to efficient coding

The idea that perception is inference is older than neuroscience. Hermann von Helmholtz argued in the 1860s that visual experience must result from *unbewusster Schluss* — unconscious inference — because the retinal image is ambiguous: a two-dimensional pattern admits infinitely many three-dimensional causes, so the brain must combine sensory evidence with prior knowledge of how scenes generate images. Bayes' rule is the normative answer to how that combination should work [7]:

$$P(\vartheta \mid s) = \frac{P(s \mid \vartheta)\, P(\vartheta)}{P(s)}$$

where $s$ denotes sensory input and $\vartheta$ the hidden causes. In the 1960s Horace Barlow added the *efficient coding hypothesis*: sensory systems should recode input to reduce redundancy and maximize information transmission given metabolic constraints. Predictive coding marries these two insights — inference with redundancy reduction: rather than transmitting raw activity upward, each level transmits only the unpredicted residual, because the predictable component is already known to the levels above [1].

### 2.2 Mumford's theoretical scaffold and Rao–Ballard's demonstration

David Mumford (1992) proposed that the thalamocortical system implements a hierarchical probabilistic model in which descending pathways convey a *template* — a fitted hypothesis about the world — and ascending pathways convey the *residual* mismatch. Mumford's proposal was elegant but lacked simulations, and the field did not fully absorb it. The turning point was Rao and Ballard's 1999 *Nature Neuroscience* paper [1]. They built a two-level network in which higher-level units generate linear predictions of lower-level activity and showed that:

1. Trained on natural images, the network developed *simple-cell-like receptive fields* — oriented, bandpass filters resembling V1 simple cells — as an emergent property of efficient hierarchical coding, not of hand-wired feature detectors.
2. A subset of units carrying the residual (prediction-error) signals exhibited *end-stopping* and other extra-classical receptive-field effects: when a bar stimulus extended beyond the classical receptive field, responses were suppressed — exactly what one expects if the surround renders the center predictable, so there is little error left to report [1].

This was the first demonstration that a *process theory* — not a descriptive tuning-curve model — could simultaneously explain classical selectivity and contextual modulation.

### 2.3 The variational turn: Friston's free-energy principle

Karl Friston and colleagues recast predictive coding in variational Bayesian terms [2]. The brain cannot evaluate the posterior $P(\vartheta \mid s)$ directly, because the model evidence $P(s)$ is intractable. Instead it optimizes a *variational free energy* $F(s, q)$ — an upper bound on surprise $-\ln P(s)$ — with respect to a recognition density $q(\vartheta)$ and the parameters of the generative model. Minimizing free energy is mathematically equivalent to maximizing a lower bound on the log model evidence, and under the Laplace assumption (Gaussian densities) the ensuing dynamics reduce to prediction-error minimization with precision weighting. This move unified predictive coding with attention (as precision), perceptual inference (as state estimation), and learning (as model-parameter optimization) under a single objective [2, 6].

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 The hierarchical generative model

Consider a hierarchy of $L$ levels. At each level $i$, hidden states $\mu^{(i)}$ generate predictions of the level below via a nonlinear function $g^{(i)}$:

$$\begin{aligned}
s &= g^{(1)}(\mu^{(1)}) + \omega_s, \
\dot{\mu}^{(i)} &= f^{(i)}(\mu^{(i)}, \mu^{(i+1)}) + \omega_x^{(i)},
\end{aligned}$$

where $\omega_s$ and $\omega_x$ are Gaussian noise terms with precisions (inverse variances) $\Pi_s$ and $\Pi_x$. The top-level prior $P(\mu^{(L)})$ encodes slow, abstract expectations. Prediction errors at each level are defined as the precision-weighted residuals:

$$\begin{aligned}
\xi_s &= \Pi_s \,(s - g^{(1)}(\mu^{(1)})), \
\xi_x^{(i)} &= \Pi_x^{(i)} \,(\dot{\mu}^{(i)} - f^{(i)}(\mu^{(i)}, \mu^{(i+1)})).
\end{aligned}$$

Under the Laplace approximation, variational free energy reduces (up to constants) to the sum of squared precision-weighted prediction errors:

$$F \approx \tfrac{1}{2}\,\xi_s^{\top}\varepsilon_s + \tfrac{1}{2}\,\textstyle\sum_i\,\xi_x^{(i)\top}\varepsilon_x^{(i)} - \ln P(\mu).$$

### 3.2 Gradient descent dynamics

Inference corresponds to minimizing $F$ with respect to the sufficient statistics $\mu$ of the recognition density. Gradient descent yields first-order differential equations that are *local* — each level's dynamics depend only on its own error units, the error units of the level below, and predictions from the level above [6]:

$$\dot{\mu}^{(i)} = D\mu^{(i)} - \frac{\partial g^{(i)\top}}{\partial\mu^{(i)}}\,\xi_s^{(i)} - \frac{\partial f^{(i)\top}}{\partial\mu^{(i)}}\,\xi_x^{(i)} + \frac{\partial f^{(i-1)\top}}{\partial\mu^{(i)}}\,\xi_x^{(i-1)}.$$

> **Theorem:** Under a hierarchical Gaussian generative model with the Laplace approximation, gradient descent on variational free energy with respect to the conditional means $\mu^{(i)}$ produces neural dynamics in which descending connections transmit predictions and ascending connections transmit precision-weighted prediction errors [2, 6].

This theorem is the core of the theory's appeal: the *anatomy of message passing is derived*, not assumed. Descending (feedback) pathways carry predictions $g(\mu)$; ascending (feedforward) pathways carry errors $\xi$. Learning corresponds to slower gradient descent on $F$ with respect to the synaptic parameters of $g$ and $f$, which takes a Hebbian form driven by the co-activity of predictions and errors.

### 3.3 A minimal simulation

The following Python sketch implements a two-level linear predictive-coding network tracking a one-dimensional sensory signal, illustrating how prediction errors drive inference:

```python
import numpy as np

def predictive_coding_1d(s, g_w=0.9, lr=0.05, steps=200):
    """Two-level linear PC network: level-2 predicts level-1 state mu."""
    mu = np.zeros(steps)
    errors = np.zeros(steps)
    for t in range(steps - 1):
        prediction = g_w * mu[t]          # descending prediction
        err = s[t] - prediction           # ascending prediction error
        errors[t] = err
        # gradient descent on free energy: mu moves to explain away error
        mu[t + 1] = mu[t] + lr * g_w * err
    return mu, errors

t = np.linspace(0, 10, 200)
signal = np.sin(t) + 0.1 * np.random.randn(200)   # noisy sensory stream
mu_hat, err = predictive_coding_1d(signal)
print(f"mean |prediction error| after convergence: {np.abs(err[100:]).mean():.3f}")
```

When the signal is predictable (low volatility), the network's internal estimate $\hat{\mu}$ tracks it with small residual error; when the signal regime changes abruptly, prediction errors transiently spike — the computational signature the theory identifies with the neural mismatch response.

### 3.4 The canonical microcircuit mapping

Bastos and colleagues mapped these computational roles onto the cortical column [3]. The proposed assignment is summarized in Table 1.

| Cortical layer(s) | Neuronal population | Computational role |
|---|---|---|
| L2/3 (superficial pyramidal) | Error units | Compute and forward *precision-weighted prediction errors* $\xi$ |
| L4 (spiny stellate) | Input relay | Receive ascending sensory/error drive |
| L5/6 (deep pyramidal) | Expectation (value) units | Represent conditional means $\mu$; emit *descending predictions* $g(\mu)$ |
| L1 / interneuron classes | Precision control | Gain-modulate error units (attention); inhibitory inversion of predictions |

Feedforward projections originate predominantly in superficial layers and terminate in layer 4 of the higher area — consistent with carrying errors upward — while feedback projections originate in deep layers and terminate outside layer 4 — consistent with carrying predictions downward [3]. Spectral asymmetries accompany this: ascending message passing is associated with *gamma*-band activity (error signals), and descending with *alpha/beta*-band activity (predictions).

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The signed-error problem: how can a neuron encode a negative number?

Firing rates are non-negative, yet the theory's errors $\xi = \Pi(s - g(\mu))$ are signed: they must represent both over-predictions and under-predictions. This is arguably the sharpest implementation-level challenge for predictive coding [5]. Three candidate solutions have been advanced:

- **Baseline firing:** error units maintain a high baseline rate, with decreases encoding negative errors. Energetically implausible, since sustaining a high baseline is metabolically expensive [4].
- **Opponent populations:** separate positive-error and negative-error units with complementary connectivity — positive errors excited by bottom-up input and inhibited by top-down prediction, negative errors with the reverse mapping. Keller and Mrsic-Flogel argue this is the most anatomically credible scheme [4].
- **Division instead of subtraction:** Spratling showed that computing errors as a ratio $\xi = s / g(\mu)$ (error = 1 when prediction matches) fits some rodent omission-response data better than subtraction, while keeping all quantities non-negative [5].

The existence of neurons in mouse visual cortex that respond to *omitted* stimuli — firing when an expected input fails to arrive — is naturally explained by negative-error units, and such cells have now been reported in superficial layers [4].

### 4.2 Precision, attention, and neuromodulation

Precision $\Pi$ is the inverse variance of the prediction error, and it plays the role of a *gain* on error signals: errors believed to be reliable (high precision) drive inference strongly; unreliable errors are down-weighted. Friston and colleagues identified precision modulation with *attention* [2]: attending to a sensory channel corresponds to increasing the gain of its error units. This elegantly explains the classic finding that expectation *suppresses* responses while attention *enhances* them — expectation reduces error magnitude, attention multiplies it.

> **Hypothesis:** The effects of attention on cortical responses are mediated by precision (synaptic gain) modulation of superficial pyramidal prediction-error units, under the control of neuromodulatory systems (e.g., acetylcholine for sensory precision, dopamine for the precision of higher-level beliefs about action policies).

Mechanistically, this maps onto fast-spiking parvalbumin interneurons and cholinergic modulation of superficial pyramidal cells [3, 4]. Magnetoencephalography studies of the auditory mismatch negativity show attention boosting mismatch responses in a manner consistent with gain modulation of error units in a canonical-microcircuit dynamic causal model.

### 4.3 Learning as error-driven plasticity

Learning — updating the parameters $\theta$ of the generative functions $g$ and $f$ — is gradient descent on the same free-energy objective, operating on a slower timescale than inference. Crucially, the update rules are *local* and *Hebbian-like*: the change in a prediction weight is proportional to the product of the prediction error and the presynaptic activity that generated the prediction,

$$\Delta\theta \propto \xi \cdot \frac{\partial g}{\partial\theta}.$$

This is a form of *error-driven learning* that avoids the weight-transport problem of backpropagation: errors are computed locally at each level rather than propagated through a global backward pass [5]. Rao and Ballard's original demonstration — oriented receptive fields emerging from natural-image training — is the canonical illustration: the network learns the statistics of the world precisely because its errors tell it where its model fails [1]. Modern work has shown that predictive-coding networks can approximate backpropagation under specific conditions, fueling interest in PC as a biologically plausible alternative to gradient backprop in deep learning [5].

### 4.4 Predictive coding and deep generative models

The mathematical kinship with machine learning is striking. A hierarchical predictive-coding network is closely related to a *variational autoencoder*: the descending predictions implement the decoder, the ascending errors drive the encoder's amortized inference, and free energy is the negative evidence lower bound (ELBO). Recent analyses show that, under iterative (non-amortized) inference, predictive-coding networks converge to the same fixed points as backprop-trained networks on many architectures, and that PC learning rules can train deep networks on standard benchmarks [5]. This convergence cuts two ways: it legitimizes PC as a serious algorithmic theory rather than a vague metaphor, but it also raises the bar for empirical tests, since many of PC's computational virtues are shared by any deep hierarchical model.

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 V1 responses to predictable stimuli are suppressed

A direct prediction: predictable sensory input should evoke *smaller* responses than unpredictable input, because explained-away signals generate little error. Using apparent-motion sequences in which flashes were either predictable or unpredictable, Alink and colleagues found reduced BOLD responses in human V1 (and hMT/V5+) to predictable flashes, with *lower* detection rates for the unpredictable flashes — ruling out a simple attentional account [8]. Complementary single-unit work in mouse V1 (Keller et al., 2012) showed neurons responding selectively to *mismatches* between visual flow and running speed, i.e., to violations of sensorimotor predictions, consistent with error-unit computation [4].

### 5.2 Laminar and spectral signatures of message passing

The microcircuit theory predicts that error-related activity should dominate in superficial layers and ride on fast (gamma) rhythms, while predictions dominate in deep layers on slower (alpha/beta) rhythms [3]. Consistent with this, laminar recordings in macaque visual cortex show that feedforward influence is carried predominantly by gamma frequencies from superficial layers, whereas feedback influence is carried by alpha/beta from deep layers. A further study demonstrated *layer and rhythm specificity for predictive routing*: when a stimulus is predictable, feedforward gamma signaling of that stimulus is suppressed — precisely the "explaining away" signature [3].

### 5.3 Formal results

Two formal properties strengthen the theory beyond qualitative fit:

1. **Equivalence with Kalman filtering.** For linear Gaussian generative models, the predictive-coding dynamics of Section 3.2 reduce exactly to the Kalman–Bucy filter equations — the optimal recursive estimator. Predictive coding is thus a *generalization* of optimal filtering to nonlinear, hierarchical models [6].
2. **Free energy as a Lyapunov function.** Under the stated dynamics, $F$ is non-increasing along trajectories, guaranteeing convergence of the inference process to a (local) minimum — the recognition density converges toward the posterior [2].

| Prediction | Empirical status |
|---|---|
| Superficial pyramidal cells encode signed prediction errors | Supported: mismatch/omission responses in mouse V1 L2/3 [4] |
| Feedback carries predictions on alpha/beta rhythms | Supported: laminar spectral asymmetries in primate visual cortex [3] |
| Attention multiplies (rather than adds to) error responses | Supported: MEG mismatch-negativity studies with attentional manipulation |
| Predictable stimuli evoke reduced early sensory responses | Supported: fMRI expectation suppression in V1/hMT+ [8] |
| PC networks match backprop fixed points | Supported on standard benchmarks under iterative inference [5] |

## 6 Limitations

**The "dark room" problem.** If the brain minimizes prediction error, why do organisms not simply seek dark, silent rooms where everything is predictable? The standard reply — that organisms minimize *expected* surprise under prior preferences (active inference), and that priors strongly favor exploration — is formally adequate but shifts explanatory weight onto unobserved prior beliefs, which critics argue makes the framework unfalsifiable in its broadest form [7].

**Alternative accounts of key findings.** Expectation suppression in V1 may reflect *neural adaptation* (repetition suppression) rather than predictive error computation; mismatch responses may reflect *stimulus-specific adaptation* in feedforward pathways rather than a comparison between prediction and input. The most careful evaluations conclude that while top-down predictive influences are real, not every demonstration of contextual modulation is definitive evidence for the full predictive-coding architecture [4, 5].

**The anatomy is not fully cooperative.** Predictive coding requires predictions to act *inhibitory* on error units (to subtract), yet cortico-cortical feedback projections and pyramidal–pyramidal connections are predominantly *excitatory*; additional interneuron circuitry (e.g., Martinotti cells) must be invoked to perform the inversion, adding unconfirmed machinery [5].

**Timescale and precision of the theory.** Because the framework is so general — any neural dynamic can be redescribed as minimizing some free energy under some generative model — individual experiments rarely test *predictive coding* as a whole; they test specific microcircuit commitments. Keller and Mrsic-Flogel are explicit that the theory's value lies in these falsifiable circuit-level predictions, not in the grand variational narrative alone [4].

## 7 Conclusion

Predictive coding has matured from an elegant computational intuition into the most complete *algorithmic-level* theory of cortical function available. Its core commitments are now precise: (i) the cortex implements hierarchical Bayesian inference via a generative model; (ii) inference is performed by gradient descent on variational free energy; (iii) the resulting message passing assigns predictions to descending deep-layer projections and precision-weighted errors to ascending superficial-layer projections; (iv) precision modulation implements attention; and (v) learning is local, error-driven plasticity [1, 2, 3, 6]. The empirical record — mismatch and omission responses in superficial layers, spectral asymmetries of feedforward and feedback signaling, expectation suppression, and formal equivalences with optimal filtering — provides genuine, if incomplete, support.

The theory's future depends on embracing the falsifiability its proponents now demand of it [4]: decisive tests of the microcircuit's signed-error scheme, of precision's neuromodulatory implementation, and of whether error signals — not merely prediction-modulated responses — are the principal currency of ascending cortical traffic. Whether or not predictive coding survives in its canonical form, it has permanently reshaped the questions: the cortex is no longer credibly described as a passive feature bank, but as an inference machine that knows the world by predicting it.
