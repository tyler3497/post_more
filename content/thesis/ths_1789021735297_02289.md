# Gravitational-Wave Astronomy: Matched Filtering, the LIGO Detector Network, and Bayesian Parameter Estimation of Compact Binary Coalescences

## Abstract

The first direct detection of gravitational waves on September 14, 2015 — the binary black hole merger GW150914 — inaugurated gravitational-wave astronomy and confirmed a century-old prediction of general relativity in its most extreme dynamical regime. This thesis presents a self-contained doctoral-level treatment of the three pillars that make this science possible: (i) the Advanced LIGO detector network, dual-recycled Fabry–Perot Michelson interferometers reaching strain sensitivity of order $10^{-23}/\sqrt{\mathrm{Hz}}$ near 100 Hz; (ii) matched-filter detection, in which noisy strain data are correlated against banks of $\mathcal{O}(10^5)$ precomputed inspiral–merger–ringdown template waveforms (IMRPhenom, SEOBNR) with signal-consistency tests suppressing non-Gaussian transients; and (iii) Bayesian parameter estimation, which converts detections into astrophysics through nested-sampling exploration of the 15-plus-dimensional posterior over masses, spins, distance, and orientation, yielding the celebrated standard-siren cosmology of GW170817. We derive the chirp-mass frequency evolution, the optimal signal-to-noise ratio statistic, and the Bayesian evidence; quantify computational costs, calibration limits, waveform systematics, and the distance–inclination degeneracy; and assess how third-generation observatories — Einstein Telescope and Cosmic Explorer — will raise event rates by three orders of magnitude. Nine sources anchor every quantitative claim.

## 1 Introduction

General relativity predicts that accelerated masses radiate ripples in spacetime propagating at the speed of light [2]. For a century these gravitational waves remained a theoretical curiosity: the expected strains, $h \sim \Delta L/L \sim 10^{-21}$, are so small that detecting them requires measuring a length change of order $10^{-18}\,\mathrm{m}$ — one-thousandth of a proton diameter — across a four-kilometer baseline. The Laser Interferometer Gravitational-Wave Observatory (LIGO), comprising twin detectors in Hanford, Washington, and Livingston, Louisiana (separated by $\sim$3,000 km), was constructed precisely for this task: an initial LIGO science run (2002–2010) demonstrated the technology at a sensitivity that yielded no detections, and an upgraded Advanced LIGO then surpassed all previous runs within sixteen days of coincident operation, observing GW150914 on September 14, 2015, with a combined signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) of 24 [2][1].

This thesis argues that the detection of compact binary coalescences (CBCs) — the inspiral, merger, and ringdown of binary black holes and neutron stars — is not merely an experimental triumph but a *statistical* one. The raw strain channel is dominated by seismic, thermal, and quantum shot noise; the signal is buried at least an order of magnitude below the noise in any single frequency bin. Extraction therefore depends on three interlocking technologies. First, the **detector**: a dual-recycled Michelson interferometer with Fabry–Perot arm cavities and power recycling that maximizes optical gain at the photodetector [1]. Second, **matched filtering**: the data are cross-correlated against a bank of theoretical waveform templates covering the compact-binary parameter space, with $\chi^2$ signal-consistency vetoes distinguishing chirps from instrumental glitches [3]. Third, **Bayesian parameter estimation**: once detected, the event is analyzed with nested sampling and Markov-chain Monte Carlo to recover posterior probability distributions over masses, spins, distance, sky location, and orientation [6].

Our contribution is a unified exposition connecting these layers end to end: from the optical layout that produces the strain time series, through the inner-product statistic that extracts the chirp, to the posterior densities that yield the chirp mass, the effective spin, and the luminosity distance — and, for GW170817, an independent measurement of the Hubble constant. We close with the limitations of current waveform models and the projected capabilities of third-generation detectors.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Linearized gravity and the strain observable

In the transverse-traceless gauge, a gravitational wave propagating along $\hat{z}$ perturbs the metric by $h_{+}$ and $h_{\times}$ polarizations. An interferometer with arms along $\hat{x}$ and $\hat{y}$ measures the differential strain $h(t) = \Delta L_x/L - \Delta L_y/L$, coupled through the detector antenna patterns $F_{+,\times}(\alpha, \delta, \psi)$ to the two polarizations:

> **Theorem (detector response):** *For a source at right ascension $\alpha$, declination $\delta$, and polarization angle $\psi$, the measured strain is $h(t) = F_{+}(\alpha,\delta,\psi)\,h_{+}(t) + F_{\times}(\alpha,\delta,\psi)\,h_{\times}(t)$.*

A network of detectors with different orientations and arrival times breaks the degeneracies inherent in any single-detector measurement; in particular, the $\sim$7 ms light-travel delay between Hanford and Livingston localizes sources on a sky annulus, with a third detector (Virgo, KAGRA) resolving the residual ambiguity.

### 2.2 The Advanced LIGO optical configuration

Each Advanced LIGO detector is a *dual-recycled Fabry–Perot Michelson interferometer* [1]. Two refinements to the basic Michelson geometry provide the sensitivity:

1. **Fabry–Perot arm cavities.** Each 4 km arm contains a resonant optical cavity formed by a partially transmitting input test mass (ITM) and a highly reflecting end test mass (ETM). Light bounces back and forth $\mathcal{O}(100)$ times, multiplying the gravitational-wave phase shift — and the signal — by roughly a factor of 100 at 100 Hz.
2. **Power and signal recycling.** The interferometer operates at the *dark fringe*: the antisymmetric port is held at destructive interference so that nearly all carrier light returns toward the laser. A partially transmitting **power recycling mirror** (PRM) between the laser and beamsplitter forms a cavity with the Michelson symmetric port, increasing the input laser power on the beamsplitter by a factor of about 40 and thus reducing shot noise, since shot-noise-limited sensitivity scales as $1/\sqrt{P}$. Advanced LIGO adds a **signal recycling mirror** (SRM) at the output port, broadening the detector response beyond the arm-cavity linewidth and enabling frequency-dependent sensitivity tuning [1].

The design strain sensitivity is a factor of 10 better than initial LIGO near 100 Hz, and the low-frequency wall moves from 40 Hz down to 10 Hz, dramatically increasing the observable volume for binary inspirals.

### 2.3 Compact binary coalescence waveforms

A binary with component masses $m_1, m_2$ evolves through three stages:

- **Inspiral:** quasi-circular orbital decay driven by radiation reaction, well described by the post-Newtonian (PN) expansion.
- **Merger:** the final plunge and coalescence, requiring full numerical relativity (NR).
- **Ringdown:** the perturbed remnant relaxes through damped quasinormal modes with complex frequencies $\omega = \omega_R + i\omega_I$.

The leading-order observable is the *chirp mass*,

$$\mathcal{M}_c = \frac{(m_1 m_2)^{3/5}}{(m_1 + m_2)^{1/5}},$$

which controls the frequency evolution $\dot{f} \propto \mathcal{M}_c^{5/3} f^{11/3}$ [5]. At leading order the frequency-domain amplitude scales as $\tilde{h}(f) \propto \mathcal{M}_c^{5/6} f^{-7/6}/D_L$, so that the amplitude directly encodes the luminosity distance $D_L$ — the foundation of standard-siren cosmology.

Two waveform families span the parameter space. The **IMRPhenom** family constructs phenomenological frequency-domain waveforms by fitting NR simulations with elementary functions and stitching PN inspiral to quasinormal-mode ringdown; modern members (e.g., IMRPhenomXPHM) include subdominant spherical-harmonic modes [4]. The **SEOBNR** family solves the effective-one-body (EOB) equations of motion — a Padé-resummed extension of PN dynamics — and attaches a superposition of quasinormal modes; recent versions (SEOBNRv5PHM) are calibrated against NR and second-order self-force calculations [4]. Template-bank metrics derived from IMRPhenomB have been shown to approximate matches for EOBNR models as well, enabling efficient bank placement [4].

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 The matched-filter detection statistic

Assume stationary Gaussian noise with one-sided power spectral density $S_n(f)$. The noise-weighted inner product between data $s$ and template $h$ is

$$\langle s | h \rangle = 4\,\mathrm{Re}\int_0^{\infty} \frac{\tilde{s}(f)\,\tilde{h}^*(f)}{S_n(f)}\,df.$$

> **Theorem (Neyman–Pearson optimality):** *For a known signal $h$ in additive stationary Gaussian noise, the matched filter $z = \langle s | \hat{h}\rangle / \sqrt{\langle \hat{h}|\hat{h}\rangle}$ maximizes the detection probability at fixed false-alarm probability.*

In practice the sky location, polarization, and coalescence phase are analytically maximized, yielding the complex SNR time series

$$z(t) = \frac{4}{\sqrt{\langle h|h\rangle}}\int_0^{\infty}\frac{\tilde{s}(f)\,\tilde{h}^*(f)}{S_n(f)}\,e^{2\pi ift}\,df,$$

and the detection statistic $\rho = |z(t)|$, the matched-filter SNR [3]. A signal is declared when $\rho$ exceeds a threshold and is coincident across detectors.

### 3.2 Template banks and the search pipelines

Because the true signal parameters are unknown, the data are filtered against a **template bank**: a discrete set of waveforms placed so that any physical signal matches some template with minimal match $\geq 0.97$ (a geometric placement problem solved with the parameter-space metric of [4]). Banks cover component masses, aligned spins, and — in extended searches — eccentricity and higher harmonics.

Four production pipelines implement matched filtering: **PyCBC**, **GstLAL**, **MBTA**, and **SPIIR** [3]. PyCBC, used in the first observing run, unambiguously identified GW150914 and GW151226, measuring false-alarm rates as low as one per million years and delivering a 30% increase in sensitive volume for binary neutron stars over previous searches [3]. Two refinements are essential:

1. **$\chi^2$ signal-consistency tests.** The template is split into frequency sub-bands; a true chirp deposits SNR proportionally in each band, while a broadband glitch does not. Templates are re-weighted by the Allen $\chi^2$ statistic, drastically suppressing the non-Gaussian background.
2. **Background estimation by time slides.** The false-alarm rate is measured empirically by shifting one detector's data in time by more than the light-travel time and re-analyzing — a model-independent background estimate requiring no assumption of Gaussianity.

### 3.3 Bayesian parameter estimation

Detection answers *whether*; parameter estimation answers *what*. Given data $d$ and a waveform model $h(\theta)$ with parameters $\theta$ (masses, spins, distance, inclination, sky location, coalescence time and phase), Bayes' theorem gives the posterior

$$p(\theta | d) = \frac{\mathcal{L}(d | \theta)\,\pi(\theta)}{\mathcal{Z}}, \qquad \mathcal{Z} = \int \mathcal{L}(d|\theta)\,\pi(\theta)\,d\theta,$$

with the Gaussian likelihood

$$\log \mathcal{L}(d|\theta) = -\frac{1}{2}\langle d - h(\theta)\,|\,d - h(\theta)\rangle + \mathrm{const}.$$

The posterior over $\sim$15 parameters is multimodal and degenerate — notably the distance–inclination degeneracy $h \propto \cos\iota / D_L$, broken only partially by a network. Two sampler classes are standard:

- **Nested sampling** (MultiNest/pyMultiNest, dynesty) — computes the evidence $\mathcal{Z}$ (for model comparison) while returning posterior samples; a four-parameter Bilby injection study with 6,000 live points recovers masses, distance, and inclination in roughly 30 minutes on a laptop [6].
- **Markov-chain Monte Carlo** (emcee, parallel tempering) — used historically by LALInference.

**Bilby** provides a user-friendly Bayesian inference layer over these samplers with GW-specific priors (e.g., uniform-in-comoving-volume distance, isotropic orientation) and has been adopted for all LIGO–Virgo–KAGRA analyses since the third catalog [6]. Likelihood acceleration techniques — heterodyned/relative-binning likelihoods — reduce evaluation costs by orders of magnitude.

```python
import bilby

# Waveform generator and Gaussian noise likelihood
waveform_generator = bilby.gw.WaveformGenerator(
    duration=4.0, sampling_frequency=2048.0,
    frequency_domain_source_model=bilby.gw.source.lal_binary_black_hole,
    parameter_conversion=bilby.gw.conversion.convert_to_lal_binary_black_hole_parameters,
    waveform_arguments=dict(waveform_approximant="IMRPhenomXPHM",
                            reference_frequency=20.0, minimum_frequency=20.0))

priors = bilby.gw.prior.BBHPriorDict()          # masses, spins, distance, inclination, ...
priors["mass_1"] = bilby.core.prior.Uniform(25, 35, unit=r"$M_\odot$")

likelihood = bilby.gw.GravitationalWaveTransient(
    interferometers=bilby.gw.detector.get_empty_interferometer("H1"),
    waveform_generator=waveform_generator, priors=priors)

result = bilby.run_sampler(likelihood, priors, sampler="dynesty",
                           nlive=2000, outdir="pe_gw150914")
result.plot_corner(parameters=["chirp_mass", "mass_ratio", "chi_eff", "luminosity_distance"])
```

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 From dark fringe to strain: the noise budget

The dark-fringe operating point is not merely an optical convenience — it determines the entire noise budget. Common-mode disturbances (laser frequency noise, symmetric arm motion) return to the laser at the symmetric port; only *differential* arm motion appears at the antisymmetric port where the photodetector sits. The limiting noises partition the band:

| Frequency band | Dominant noise | Mitigation |
|---|---|---|
| $< 10$ Hz | Seismic, gravity-gradient | Pendulum suspensions, underground siting (3G) |
| 10–50 Hz | Suspension thermal, seismic | Multi-stage isolation, monolithic fibers |
| 50–200 Hz | Coating thermal | Larger beams, crystalline coatings, cryogenics (ET) |
| $> 200$ Hz | Quantum shot noise | Higher laser power, squeezed light |

GW150914, with chirp mass $\sim 28\,M_\odot$, swept from $\sim$35 Hz to $\sim$250 Hz in $\sim$0.2 s; a binary neutron star like GW170817 spends $\sim$100 s in band from 23 Hz, depositing most SNR at low frequency — precisely where future upgrades gain most.

### 4.2 The chirp as an inverse problem

The inspiral phase encodes masses through $\dot{f}$. Inverting the leading-order relation,

$$\mathcal{M}_c = \frac{c^3}{G}\left[\frac{5}{96}\,\pi^{-8/3}\,f^{-11/3}\,\dot{f}\right]^{3/5},$$

one reads the chirp mass directly from the slope of the time–frequency track [5]. Because $\mathcal{M}_c$ enters at Newtonian order while mass ratio and spins enter at 1PN and 1.5PN respectively, the chirp mass is typically measured to sub-percent precision, whereas individual spins remain weakly constrained. This hierarchy explains why GW150914's chirp mass ($\sim 28\,M_\odot$) is known far better than its component spins.

### 4.3 Waveform systematics: the dominant theoretical uncertainty

As detector sensitivity improves, the limiting uncertainty shifts from statistics to waveform models. Phenomenological and EOB families differ in their treatment of the merger; higher-order modes $(\ell,m) = (3,3), (4,4)$ matter for asymmetric, edge-on systems; and precession introduces amplitude modulations that a single effective-spin parameter $\chi_{\mathrm{eff}}$ cannot capture. The standard practice — combining analyses from multiple approximants (IMRPhenomXPHM, SEOBNRv5PHM) with evidence weighting — inflates credible intervals to cover model disagreement [6]. Eccentricity is another frontier: quasicircular template banks can miss up to $\sim$40% of moderately eccentric BNS/NSBH systems, motivating geometric eccentric banks [4].

### 4.4 Standard sirens and multi-messenger cosmology

GW170817 (August 17, 2017) was the first binary neutron star merger observed in both gravitational waves and light: Fermi-GBM detected the short gamma-ray burst GRB 170817A $\sim$1.7 s after merger, and the kilonova AT2017gfo was localized to the galaxy NGC 4993 [7]. The GW amplitude measures $D_L$ directly — no distance ladder — while the EM counterpart supplies the redshift $z$; Hubble's law $v_H = H_0 D_L$ then yields $H_0 \approx 70\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}\,Mpc^{-1}}$, independent of both the cosmic microwave background and local calibrations [7]. The precision is currently limited by the $D_L$–$\iota$ degeneracy; viewing-angle constraints from the GRB afterglow and kilonova light curve tighten this significantly [8]. With $\mathcal{O}(50)$ bright sirens expected in the coming decade, standard-siren cosmology can reach percent-level $H_0$ precision — potentially arbitrating the Hubble tension.

### 4.5 The third generation: Einstein Telescope and Cosmic Explorer

Current 2G infrastructure caps sensitivity. Two third-generation concepts are in development [9]:

- **Einstein Telescope (ET):** a 10 km underground triangular observatory with three nested interferometers in a *xylophone* configuration — a cryogenic low-frequency instrument and a high-power high-frequency instrument per vertex — targeting an order-of-magnitude sensitivity gain and a low-frequency cutoff near 3 Hz.
- **Cosmic Explorer (CE):** a 40 km L-shaped surface observatory in the US (with a 20 km second stage), exploiting arm length itself — the only noise source that scales favorably with $L$ — for a similar order-of-magnitude gain.

A full ET+CE network would detect $\mathcal{O}(10^5)$ binary black hole mergers per year out to redshift $z \sim 10$–$20$, observe stellar-mass inspirals for hours before merger (enabling early-warning alerts to electromagnetic facilities), and measure the stochastic background from unresolved binaries — while the low-frequency reach opens intermediate-mass black holes ($\sim 10^2$–$10^5\,M_\odot$) to ground-based observation.

## 5 Empirical Results and Complexity Analysis

The observational record validates the methodology. GW150914: component masses $\sim 36$ and $\sim 29\,M_\odot$, network SNR 24, false-alarm rate below 1 in 203,000 years, final black hole $\sim 62\,M_\odot$ with $\sim 3\,M_\odot c^2$ radiated — peak luminosity exceeding all stars in the observable universe combined [2]. GW170817: binary neutron star, $\mathcal{M}_c \approx 1.188\,M_\odot$, $D_L \approx 40$ Mpc, tidal deformability constraining the neutron-star equation of state [7].

Computationally, the pipeline separates into two regimes:

| Stage | Dominant cost | Scaling | Mitigation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Matched-filter search | FFT correlations over bank | $\mathcal{O}(N_{\mathrm{templates}} \cdot N \log N)$; banks $\sim 10^5$–$10^6$ | Singular-value-decomposed basis, GPUs, PyCBC Live low-latency |
| Background estimation | Time slides | $\propto$ required inverse FAR | Hierarchical vetoes, $\chi^2$ re-weighting |
| Bayesian PE | Likelihood evaluations | $10^6$–$10^8$ waveform calls; hours–days per event | Heterodyned likelihood, reduced-order models, GPU samplers (20–40× speedup) |

Low-latency pipelines (PyCBC Live, GstLAL, SPIIR, MBTA) issue public alerts within tens of seconds, with rapid sky localization (BAYESTAR) enabling the multi-messenger follow-up that caught GW170817 [3].

## 6 Limitations

1. **Waveform model systematics.** No closed-form solution exists for the merger; all models interpolate between PN and NR with fitting error, and higher modes, precession, and eccentricity are only approximately covered. Systematic bias can exceed statistical uncertainty for high-SNR 3G events.
2. **Calibration uncertainty.** Detector response calibration (photon calibrators, actuation models) carries $\sim$1% amplitude and $\sim$1° phase uncertainty, propagating directly into distance and phase measurements.
3. **Non-stationary, non-Gaussian noise.** Glitches (scattered light, blips) populate the background; $\chi^2$ vetoes and gating mitigate but cannot eliminate them, and veto deadtime costs livetime.
4. **Distance–inclination degeneracy.** A single network measures only $h \propto \cos\iota/D_L$; without an EM counterpart or higher modes, $D_L$ posteriors are broad — the fundamental limit of dark-siren cosmology.
5. **Template-bank incompleteness.** Banks assume quasicircular, aligned-spin, quadrupole-dominated signals; exotic sources (eccentric, precessing, beyond-GR) may be missed or misclassified.
6. **Prior dependence.** Posteriors for weakly constrained parameters (spins, mass ratio) depend on prior choices; population-informed priors are an active remedy.

## 7 Conclusion

Gravitational-wave astronomy rests on a chain of inference — interferometer, matched filter, Bayesian posterior — each link of which is now a mature discipline. The LIGO network's dual-recycled Fabry–Perot Michelson design pushed strain sensitivity to $10^{-23}/\sqrt{\mathrm{Hz}}$, matched-filter banks with signal-consistency tests extracted chirps from overwhelming noise at false-alarm rates below one per million years, and nested-sampling parameter estimation converted those chirps into masses, spins, and distances that rewrote black-hole astrophysics and delivered an independent Hubble constant. The path forward is clear: mitigate waveform systematics before they dominate 3G error budgets, break the distance–inclination degeneracy with networks and counterparts, and build Einstein Telescope and Cosmic Explorer to turn today's catalogs of hundreds into tomorrow's catalogs of hundreds of thousands. The first detection was a whisper; the third generation will be a census.
