# Synthetic Aperture Radar: The Range–Doppler Algorithm, Interferometric Phase, and Persistent Scatterer Analysis

![SAR stripmap imaging geometry](ths_1789414204000_14284-0.webp)
*Figure 1 — Stripmap SAR imaging geometry: platform trajectory, antenna footprint, and synthetic aperture.*

## Abstract

Synthetic aperture radar converts the relative motion between radar platform and ground into a two-dimensional imaging problem, yielding metre-scale resolution independent of weather or illumination. This thesis develops three pillars of spaceborne SAR geodesy. First, the **Range–Doppler algorithm**: range compression by matched filtering, range-cell migration correction by interpolation in the range–Doppler domain, and azimuth compression with the estimated azimuth FM rate [1]. Second, **interferometric phase**: how the phase difference of two repeat-pass images encodes topography and line-of-sight displacement, and how coherence quantifies usable signal against decorrelation mechanisms including the critical baseline [4][5]. Third, **persistent scatterer analysis**, which identifies temporally stable point scatterers across long image stacks and solves jointly for linear deformation velocity, residual topography, and the atmospheric phase screen, reaching millimetre-per-year precision [2][3]. Empirical results, error bounds, and fundamental limitations complete the treatment.

## 1 Introduction

The 1978 launch of *Seasat* proved that metre-class resolution could be synthesised from orbit by coherently integrating radar echoes over a long aperture [1]. But SAR's deepest scientific impact comes from *interferometry*: measuring the phase of the returned wave so stably that millimetre changes in radar-to-ground range are detectable across scenes hundreds of kilometres wide [4].

Three developments define the modern discipline. The **Range–Doppler algorithm**, refined for spaceborne processors and canonically documented by Cumming and Wong, turns raw phase-history data into focused complex images [1]. **Differential interferometry**, pioneered by Gabriel, Goldstein, and Zebker over California's Imperial Valley, showed that interferometric phase is a direct observable of surface displacement [7]. **Persistent scatterer interferometry**, introduced by Ferretti, Prati, and Rocca, defeated temporal decorrelation by exploiting only scatterers whose electromagnetic signature remains stable for years, yielding deformation time series with claimed precisions near half a millimetre per year [2][3].

## 2 Background

### The SAR signal geometry

A SAR platform flies with effective velocity *V* along the azimuth direction, transmitting chirped pulses at repetition frequency *f_p* while the beam sweeps a swath in range. For a point target at closest-approach slant range *R_0* and zero-Doppler time *η_c*, the two-way range history is well approximated by the hyperbolic form

    R(η) ≈ R_0 + (V²(η − η_c)²) / (2 R_0)

with *η* slow (azimuth) time. The linear-FM pulse of duration *T_p* and chirp rate *K_r*,

    s_tx(τ) = rect(τ / T_p) · exp(j π K_r τ²),

returns delayed by *2R(η)/c* and phase-shifted by *−4πR(η)/λ*, where *τ* is fast (range) time. Superposition over all scatterers forms the raw data matrix *s_raw(τ, η)* [1]. Two resolution results govern design:

- **Range resolution**: *δ_r = c / (2B)*, set by chirp bandwidth *B = K_r T_p*, independent of altitude.
- **Azimuth resolution**: *δ_a = L / 2*, set by half the physical antenna length *L* — longer antennas yield *coarser* SAR resolution, because the narrower beam shortens dwell time [1][5].

> **Theorem:** *The Cramér–Rao bound for a point scatterer.* Under additive white Gaussian noise, the variance of any unbiased slant-range-delay estimator is bounded below by *c² / (8 π² B² · SNR)*. Pulse compression attains this bound asymptotically, which is why the chirp waveform is information-theoretically optimal for range estimation.

Because each target's energy is smeared across thousands of range bins and the full synthetic aperture in azimuth, the raw data are uninterpretable until focused: the pulse must be compressed in range and the aperture coherently summed in azimuth. That is the processor's entire job.

## 3 Methodology

The Range–Doppler algorithm performs focusing in three stages, interleaved with a Fourier transform into the range–Doppler domain where migration correction is exact per Doppler frequency [1].

![Range–Doppler algorithm block diagram](ths_1789414204000_14284-1.webp)
*Figure 2 — Range–Doppler processor: range compression, RCMC, and azimuth compression.*

### Stage 1 — Range compression

A range FFT converts each pulse to the range-frequency domain; multiplication by the matched filter *H_r(f_τ) = conj(S_tx(f_τ))* and inverse FFT collapse each chirp to width *1/B*, realising the *T_p·B* processing gain. The chirp rate's slight azimuth-frequency dependence — *secondary range compression* — is negligible at moderate squint but mandatory for high-squint or ultra-wide-swath geometries [1][6].

### Stage 2 — Range cell migration correction

In the range–Doppler domain (range time, azimuth frequency *f_η*), the target's range history maps to the deterministic curve

    ΔR(f_η) = (λ² R_0 f_η²) / (8 V²),

quadratic in Doppler frequency. RCMC shifts each azimuth-frequency column by this amount via sinc interpolation (typically 8–16 taps), aligning a target's energy into a single range bin [1]. This is the algorithmic heart of RDA: in this domain migration depends only on *f_η*, so a one-dimensional correction is exact, delivering near-ideal impulse responses at a fraction of time-domain backprojection's cost.

### Stage 3 — Azimuth compression

Each range bin's azimuth signal is a linear-FM chirp with FM rate *K_a ≈ −2V² / (λ R_0)* (broadside). Multiplication by *H_a(f_η) = exp(−j π f_η² / K_a)* and azimuth IFFT yield the single-look complex image. The reference must carry the estimated Doppler centroid *f_dc = −2V sin θ_s / λ* for squint angle *θ_s* and the range-varying rate *K_a(R)*, refined from data by clutterlock, cross-correlation, or autofocus estimators [1].

> **Theorem:** *Optimality of sequential matched filtering.* If range and azimuth modulations are separable — exact for the hyperbolic range history at zero squint — then cascaded one-dimensional matched filters achieve the two-dimensional matched-filter SNR bound, with complexity *O(N² log N)* versus *O(N³)* for unfactored two-dimensional correlation.

Alternative processors trade generality for speed: the **chirp scaling algorithm** equalises migration curves by a phase-multiply identity, eliminating interpolation [6]; the **ω–k** algorithm uses two-dimensional Stolt interpolation and is exact for wide apertures; **SPECAN** targets ScanSAR bursts. A comparison appears in Section 5.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Two-Dimensional Point Target Response

Cascading the signal model through the three stages yields the focused response — a product of sinc functions,

    h(τ, η) ≈ σ · sinc(B (τ − 2R_0/c)) · sinc(B_a (η − η_c)) · exp(−j 4π R_0 / λ),

with complex reflectivity *σ*. The peak phase, *−4πR_0/λ*, is the datum of all interferometry: absolute range modulo wavelength. Two artefacts perturb it. **Azimuth ambiguities** arise when the Doppler spectrum aliases because *f_p < B_a*, folding ghost targets in at azimuth offsets of *V/f_p* multiples; **range ambiguities** fold preceding and succeeding pulses. Both are suppressed by antenna weighting and PRF selection, but they bound the achievable phase fidelity and hence the interferometric noise floor [1].

The azimuth FM rate model is the second subtlety: the true rate varies with range and, under squint, with azimuth position. A constant-*K_a* processor defocuses off-reference targets through quadratic phase error, so operational processors update the reference per range bin and refine it with map-drift or contrast-optimisation autofocus [1].

### 4.2 Interferometric Phase: Formation, Fringes, and Coherence

Form two focused images *s_1*, *s_2* from slightly different vantage points and compute *s_1 · conj(s_2)*. Its phase is

    Δφ = (4π / λ) (R_2 − R_1)   (modulo 2π).

With perpendicular baseline *B_⊥* and look angle *θ*, a height *h* above the reference surface contributes the **topographic phase** *Δφ_topo = (4π B_⊥ h) / (λ R sin θ)*, while a line-of-sight displacement *d_LOS* contributes *Δφ_disp = (4π / λ) d_LOS*. One fringe — a full *2π* cycle — equals *λ/2* of LOS motion (about 2.8 cm for C-band Sentinel-1); the **height of ambiguity** *h_a = λ R sin θ / (2 B_⊥)* is the topographic increment per fringe [4][5]. The ERS-1 interferogram of the 1992 Landers earthquake rendered the coseismic field as dense fringes wrapping the rupture [4].

![Interferometric baseline geometry and wrapped phase fringes](ths_1789414204000_14284-2.webp)
*Figure 3 — Across-track baseline geometry (left), wrapped phase fringes centred on a deformation signal, and a coherence map (right).*

Phase is useful only where the echoes stay correlated. The **complex coherence**

    γ = E[s_1 conj(s_2)] / sqrt(E[|s_1|²] E[|s_2|²])

factorises into thermal, spatial (baseline), temporal, and volumetric terms [5]. The spatial term vanishes at the **critical baseline** *B_⊥,crit = λ R tan θ / (2 δ_r)* — about 1.1 km for ERS — where the two acquisitions' spectral bands no longer overlap. Band-pass filtering both images to their common spectrum (the **wavenumber shift** correction) recovers coherence at the price of resolution [5]. Temporal decorrelation from wind, moisture, and vegetation dominates in non-urban terrain and motivates the persistent scatterer approach of Section 4.4.

> **Theorem:** *Phase variance and coherence.* For a distributed target and *L* looks, the Cramér–Rao bound on interferometric phase standard deviation is *σ_φ = sqrt((1 − γ²) / (2 L γ²))*. Coherence is therefore not a cosmetic quality metric; it is the fundamental parameter controlling deformation precision.

### 4.3 Differential Interferometry: From Fringes to Millimetre Geodesy

**Differential InSAR** removes the topographic term with an external DEM, leaving

    φ_DInSAR = φ_disp + φ_atmo + φ_orb + φ_DEM-err + φ_noise,

whose terms separate by their distinct spatio-temporal signatures [4]. Gabriel, Goldstein, and Zebker's 1989 three-pass Seasat experiment over the Imperial Valley mapped irrigation-induced subsidence at the centimetre scale — the first proof that radar phase functions as a geodetic observable [7]. Massonnet and colleagues then mapped the Landers coseismic field and a decade of volcanic and glacial signals with ERS-1, establishing the operational vocabulary: flat-earth removal, topographic synthesis, Goldstein–Werner adaptive filtering, and minimum-cost-flow unwrapping such as SNAPHU [4].

Two practical nuances dominate. Because the observable is modulo *2π*, displacement gradients exceeding *λ/4* per pixel alias — unwrapping is NP-hard in general and succeeds only where coherence is high and the true phase smooth. And **atmospheric delay** confounds: tropospheric stratification correlates with topography and mimics uplift, while turbulence adds centimetre-scale spatially correlated noise [4]. Long time series exist precisely to defeat these confounders.

### 4.4 Persistent Scatterer Analysis: Millimetre Time Series from Long Stacks

Temporal decorrelation defeats conventional DInSAR where resolution cells hold many sub-scatterers whose relative phases randomise between passes. Ferretti, Prati, and Rocca's insight: *some* pixels are dominated by one stable scatterer — a building corner, a rock outcrop — whose amplitude persists across the whole stack [2]. They are found without any interferogram, from the **amplitude dispersion index**

    D_A = σ_A / μ_A,

the ratio of temporal standard deviation to mean amplitude. For high signal-to-clutter pixels *D_A* approximates the phase standard deviation, and a threshold near *0.25* selects scatterers with sub-pixel phase stability [2].

The PS chain then runs [2][3]:

1. **Master selection and coregistration.** One image minimises total stack decorrelation (a weighted function of baseline and Doppler spread); all slaves resample to sub-pixel accuracy.
2. **Differential stack.** Topography is removed with a DEM; each PS pixel carries *N* wrapped differential phases.
3. **Arc-wise estimation.** Candidates link via Delaunay triangulation. On each arc the neighbour phase *difference* is modelled as
    Δφ_i = (4π/λ) T_i Δv + (4π B_⊥,i / (λ R sinθ)) Δε + w_i,
    with differential LOS velocity *Δv*, DEM error *Δε*, temporal baseline *T_i*, and normal baseline *B_⊥,i*. Differencing cancels the spatially smooth atmospheric term, and a maximum-likelihood search over *(Δv, Δε)* maximises **temporal coherence** *(1/N)|Σ exp(j(Δφ_i − model_i))|* [2].
4. **Network integration.** Arc estimates integrate by graph least squares to absolute velocities and DEM errors.
5. **Atmospheric screen estimation.** Residuals after the linear model are low-pass filtered in space and high-pass in time to isolate the APS, which is subtracted from every interferogram [2].
6. **Nonlinear motion.** Remaining residuals reveal seasonal, episodic, or accelerating deformation, estimated as an additional unknown in the refined estimator [3].

![Persistent scatterer InSAR processing pipeline](ths_1789414204000_14284-3.webp)

On ERS stacks over Pomona, California, the technique resolved subsidence and uplift at claimed accuracies near half a millimetre per year [3]. Complementary developments include **SBAS**, which exploits distributed scatterers through short-baseline interferogram networks [8], and **SqueeSAR**, which extends the PS concept to statistically homogeneous distributed scatterers.

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 Correctness argument for the RDA chain

*Claim.* Under separability of the hyperbolic range history, the three-stage RDA output equals the two-dimensional matched-filter output up to RCMC interpolation error.

*Proof sketch.* The raw signal factorises as *s_raw(τ, η) = a(τ − 2R(η)/c) · b(η)*. Stage 1 applies the range matched filter, which maximises range SNR and compresses *a* to *sinc(Bτ)*. After the azimuth FFT, residual migration *ΔR(f_η)* depends only on *f_η*; shifting each column by exactly this amount aligns all target energy into one range bin, with truncation error below −40 dB for an 8-tap sinc kernel. Stage 3 applies the azimuth matched filter, maximising azimuth SNR. Each stage being individually optimal and the stages commuting under separability, the cascade is optimal. ∎

### 5.2 A runnable Range–Doppler core

The following Python implements the three stages on simulated data, demonstrating processing gain and the focused sinc response:

```python
import numpy as np

def rda_focus(raw, Kr, Ka):
    """Minimal Range-Doppler processor.
    raw : 2D complex array [range, azimuth]
    Kr  : range chirp rate (Hz/s), Ka : azimuth FM rate (Hz/s)
    """
    nr, na = raw.shape
    # --- Stage 1: range compression ---
    tau = np.fft.fftfreq(nr)
    H_range = np.exp(1j * np.pi * tau**2 / Kr)   # chirp matched filter
    R = np.fft.fft(raw, axis=0)
    rc = np.fft.ifft(R * H_range[:, None], axis=0)
    # --- Azimuth FFT -> range-Doppler domain ---
    RD = np.fft.fft(rc, axis=1)
    # --- Stage 2: RCMC (nearest-bin shift; sinc kernel in production) ---
    f_eta = np.fft.fftfreq(na)
    dR = (f_eta**2) / Ka                        # migration curve
    rcmc = np.zeros_like(RD)
    for j in range(na):
        shift = int(round(dR[j] * nr))
        rcmc[:, j] = np.roll(RD[:, j], -shift)
    # --- Stage 3: azimuth compression ---
    H_az = np.exp(1j * np.pi * f_eta**2 / Ka)
    return np.fft.ifft(rcmc * H_az[None, :], axis=1)

# Sanity: a chirp in range compresses to a sharp peak
n = 4096
t = np.arange(n) / n
chirp = np.exp(1j * np.pi * 2e4 * t**2) * (np.abs(t - 0.5) < 0.25)
peak = np.abs(np.fft.ifft(np.fft.fft(chirp) *
              np.exp(-1j * np.pi * 2e4 * t**2)))
print("range compression gain (dB):",
      10*np.log10(np.max(peak)**2 / np.mean(peak**2)))
```

### 5.3 PS arc estimation

The arc-wise double-difference estimator of Ferretti, Prati, and Rocca [2], stated exactly:

```python
import numpy as np

def ps_arc_estimate(dphi, T, Bperp, lam=0.0556, R=850e3,
                    theta=np.deg2rad(23)):
    """Estimate (dv, depsilon) on one PS arc by temporal-coherence
    maximisation. dphi : wrapped differential phases [N],
    T : temporal baselines [N] (yr), Bperp : normal baselines [N] (m).
    """
    K_v = 4*np.pi/lam
    K_e = 4*np.pi/(lam*R*np.sin(theta))
    best = (0.0, 0.0, -1.0)
    for dv in np.linspace(-0.05, 0.05, 401):      # m/yr
        for de in np.linspace(-20, 20, 401):      # m
            model = K_v*T*dv + K_e*Bperp*de
            gamma = np.abs(np.mean(np.exp(1j*(dphi - model))))
            if gamma > best[2]:
                best = (dv, de, gamma)
    return {"dv_m_per_yr": best[0], "depsilon_m": best[1],
            "temporal_coherence": best[2]}
```

### 5.5 Reported empirical performance

- **DInSAR (ERS-1, Landers 1992):** coseismic displacement mapped at centimetre precision over a 100 km swath, validated against GPS and field surveys [4].
- **PS-InSAR (ERS, Pomona CA):** subsidence and uplift rates with reported precision near 0.5 mm/yr; seasonal and anthropogenic components separated from the atmospheric screen [2][3].
- **Sentinel-1 TOPS (C-band, 5.405 GHz):** continental stacks at 6–12 day revisits; SBAS networks routinely reach 1–2 mm/yr velocity precision on distributed scatterers [8].
- **Doppler centroid estimation:** clutterlock and cross-correlation recover *f_dc* to a fraction of the PRF [1].

## 6 Limitations

- **Phase ambiguity.** The observable is modulo *2π*; unwrapping fails where coherence is low or gradients exceed *λ/4* per pixel, and no estimator recovers the integer cycle count without external control.
- **Atmosphere.** Tropospheric stratification correlates with topography and mimics uplift; turbulence adds centimetre-scale correlated noise. PS-InSAR separates the APS only with stacks long enough — typically twenty or more acquisitions — for deformation and atmosphere to be statistically distinguishable [2].
- **Temporal decorrelation.** Vegetation, agriculture, and snow decorrelate within days at C-band. PS needs stable scatterer density; SBAS needs short-baseline pairs [8]. Dense forest and open water are simply blind.
- **Geometric distortion.** Layover folds elevated terrain over nearer ground; shadow leaves steep backslopes dark. Both are intrinsic to side-looking geometry.
- **Line-of-sight sensitivity.** Only the range-parallel displacement component is measured; full 3-D vectors need ascending plus descending geometries and an azimuth-offset observable.
- **Ionosphere.** At L-band and below, electron-content gradients impose centimetre-scale phase ramps needing split-spectrum correction, outside this C-band treatment.
- **Computation.** PS arc estimation scales with the square of PS density times the search grid; continental Sentinel-1 stacks demand distributed implementations and graph partitioning.
- **Scatterer stability.** Thermal dilation of buildings and soil-moisture penetration-depth shifts move the phase centre at the millimetre level, appearing as spurious nonlinear motion [3].

## 7 Conclusion

The path from raw phase history to millimetre deformation time series is one coherent argument. The Range–Doppler algorithm shows that two-dimensional focusing factorises into optimal one-dimensional matched filters once migration is corrected in the range–Doppler domain [1]. Interferometry shows that the focused phase, differenced between passes, directly observes range change, with coherence — governed by baseline, spectral overlap, and surface stability — setting the fundamental precision limit [4][5]. Persistent scatterer analysis shows that by keeping only the electromagnetically stable few pixels, and exploiting the distinct spatio-temporal signatures of deformation, topography error, and atmosphere, archived radar becomes a retrospective geodetic network of extraordinary density [2][3].

Open frontiers remain: joint multi-sensor C/L/X-band time series, fusion of ascending and descending stacks into three-dimensional velocity fields, and extension of the PS paradigm to distributed scatterers. The mathematical core — matched filtering, spectral estimation, and graph-structured estimation on wrapped phases — is unlikely to change. Radar interferometry has earned its place as the space-geodetic technique with the highest spatial sampling density ever deployed [4].
