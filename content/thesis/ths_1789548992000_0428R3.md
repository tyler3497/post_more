# Spectre and Meltdown: Speculative Execution, Microarchitectural Side Channels, and Hardware Defenses

## Abstract

In January 2018, the coordinated disclosure of the Meltdown and Spectre vulnerabilities shattered a foundational assumption of computer security: that speculative execution, the performance technique at the heart of every modern processor, is invisible to software. We show how transient instructions — executed speculatively and then discarded — leave measurable microarchitectural side effects, principally in the cache hierarchy, that attackers convert into arbitrary memory disclosure. This survey develops the full attack anatomy: branch predictor mistraining, out-of-order and speculative memory access, cache covert channels (Flush+Reload, Prime+Probe, Evict+Reload), and the taxonomy of Spectre variants (bounds-check bypass, branch target injection, rogue system register read) alongside Meltdown's deferred permission checking. We analyze the disclosure timeline, quantify real-world exploitability, and survey the defense landscape: software barriers (retpoline, speculation barriers, KPTI), hardware mitigations (speculative load hardening in silicon, invisible speculation proposals), and the performance costs each incurs. We close with the open problem of formally verified speculation safety and the architectural reckoning it demands.

## 1 Introduction

For three decades, processor architects pursued performance through a bargain with software: the microarchitecture could do *anything* — reorder instructions, predict branches, execute speculatively — provided the *architectural* state visible to programs behaved as if instructions executed in order. Security boundaries were drawn at the architectural level: page tables enforced privilege separation, and speculative paths were rolled back before commitment. The bargain held until researchers demonstrated that rollback is incomplete — speculative execution leaves footprints in shared microarchitectural state, and those footprints can be read [1][2].

**Meltdown** (CVE-2017-5754) showed that on affected Intel processors, an unprivileged instruction could speculatively read kernel memory before the permission check completed, exfiltrating data through the cache [2]. **Spectre** (CVE-2017-5753, CVE-2017-5715) showed the more general and more alarming result: by mistraining branch predictors, an attacker can steer a victim's *own* speculative execution into leaking its secrets, defeating process isolation, sandboxing, and even the mitigations built for Meltdown [1].

> **Core insight:** Speculative execution violates the abstraction boundary between performance optimization and security policy. Any microarchitectural state shared across a security domain and observable through timing is a potential covert channel, and speculation writes to such state before authorization is verified.

The disclosure — coordinated across Google Project Zero, Graz University of Technology, Cyberus Technology, and others, with embargoed briefings to vendors months in advance — triggered the largest synchronized patching effort in computing history and a fundamental re-examination of hardware/software contracts [1]. This survey is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews the microarchitectural background: pipelining, branch prediction, out-of-order execution, and cache timing channels. Section 3 develops the methodology of transient-execution analysis. Section 4 presents the deep results: the Spectre variant taxonomy, Meltdown's mechanism, follow-on attacks (Foreshadow, ZombieLoad, RIDL), and the systematic evaluation framework. Section 5 sketches attack primitives and surveys empirical measurements of exploitability and mitigation cost. Section 6 discusses limitations of current defenses. Section 7 concludes.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Speculative and out-of-order execution

Modern superscalar processors sustain throughput via three coupled mechanisms. **Pipelining** overlaps instruction stages; **out-of-order execution** (Tomasulo's algorithm, reorder buffers) executes ready instructions ahead of stalled ones; **speculative execution** guesses the outcome of branches and continues executing along the predicted path, discarding results on misprediction. Branch predictors — pattern history tables, branch target buffers (BTB), return stack buffers (RSB) — achieve prediction accuracies above 95 percent on typical code, making speculation enormously profitable [1].

The security model assumed that discarded speculative state was unobservable. This was wrong in a specific, exploitable way: while *architectural* state (registers, memory) is rolled back, *microarchitectural* state — which cache lines are resident, BTB entries, port contention — is not.

### 2.2 Cache timing side channels

Caches are the canonical shared, timing-observable state. A cache hit resolves in a few cycles; a miss costs hundreds. **Flush+Reload** exploits shared memory (e.g., shared libraries): the attacker flushes a line with `clflush`, waits, then reloads it, learning from the reload latency whether the victim accessed it [2]. **Prime+Probe** needs no shared memory: the attacker fills cache sets, waits, then probes which sets the victim evicted. **Evict+Reload** combines eviction with shared-memory reload. These primitives, developed over a decade of side-channel research, became the *exfiltration* half of transient execution attacks; speculation became the *access* half.

### 2.3 The disclosure timeline

Jann Horn (Google Project Zero), Paul Kocher, and teams at Graz University of Technology and Cyberus Technology independently discovered the flaws in 2017. Vendors were briefed under embargo; mitigations were developed in secret across the Linux kernel, Windows, hypervisors, and compilers. Public disclosure on 3 January 2018 — forced early by press leaks — revealed the coordinated effort and the severity: billions of devices affected, with no complete software fix for Spectre [1].

## 3 Methodology

We analyze transient execution attacks through the framework systematized by Canella et al. [3], decomposing every attack into four phases:

1. **Setup:** prepare microarchitectural state — mistrain predictors (Spectre), flush caches, arrange address space.
2. **Transient execution:** trigger speculative or out-of-order execution of a *gadget* — a short instruction sequence that accesses the secret and encodes it into microarchitectural state (typically: secret byte used as index into a probe array, loading a cache line at a secret-dependent address).
3. **Covert channel:** recover the encoded secret via cache timing (Flush+Reload or variants).
4. **Repeat:** iterate over addresses to dump arbitrary memory.

We classify attacks along three axes: the *speculation primitive* (which predictor or deferred check is abused), the *security boundary crossed* (user–kernel, process–process, VM–hypervisor, enclave), and the *mitigation class* defeated. Empirical evaluation uses both proof-of-concept exploit code and performance measurements of deployed mitigations (SPEC CPU benchmarks, microbenchmarks of context-switch cost).

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Spectre variant 1: bounds-check bypass

The simplest Spectre variant exploits conditional branch misprediction:

```c
if (x < array1_size) {          // mistrained to predict true
    y = array2[array1[x] * 4096]; // transient: out-of-bounds read, cache encode
}
```

By first supplying in-bounds `x`, the attacker trains the pattern history table to predict the branch taken. Then, with an out-of-bounds `x`, the processor speculatively executes the body before the bounds check resolves, reading `array1[x]` — arbitrary memory relative to `array1` — and touching `array2[secret * 4096]`. After rollback, the attacker probes `array2` to recover the secret byte [1]. The 4096 multiplier defeats the hardware prefetcher, which might otherwise load adjacent lines and add noise.

This variant is devastating because the vulnerable pattern — bounds check before array access — is ubiquitous, including in JIT-compiled JavaScript, enabling browser-based attacks. Mitigation requires *speculation barriers* (`lfence` on x86) after every bounds check in security-critical code — or, better, index masking and compiler-inserted hardening [1].

### 4.2 Spectre variant 2: branch target injection

Variant 2 poisons the **branch target buffer**: the attacker executes indirect branches at attacker-chosen virtual addresses that alias the victim's BTB entries, training the predictor to redirect the victim's indirect branch to a gadget of the attacker's choosing [1]. Unlike variant 1, which bypasses a check in the victim's own code path, variant 2 hijacks control flow speculatively — the transient equivalent of return-oriented programming.

Defenses operate at multiple levels: **retpoline** replaces indirect branches with returns (exploiting the RSB's separate prediction path), microcode updates introduce indirect branch predictor barrier (IBPB) and single-thread indirect branch predictors (STIBP), and newer hardware implements enhanced IBRS [1]. Each carries performance cost; retpoline alone cost roughly 5–15 percent on indirect-branch-heavy workloads in early measurements.

### 4.3 Meltdown: deferred permission checks

Meltdown exploits a different flaw: on vulnerable Intel microarchitectures, the permission check for a kernel-memory load is *deferred* until instruction retirement, while the loaded value is forwarded speculatively to dependent instructions [2]. The attack is almost embarrassingly direct:

```c
// running in unprivileged mode
secret = *kernel_address;          // faults, but value forwarded transiently
probe[secret * 4096];              // transient: encode into cache
```

The faulting load still delivers its data to the transient domain. Unlike Spectre, Meltdown needs no victim gadget and no predictor training — any unprivileged process can dump kernel memory at rates exceeding 500 KB/s in optimized implementations [2]. The fix, **KPTI** (Kernel Page-Table Isolation, formerly KAISER), unmaps kernel pages from user page tables so the transient load finds nothing to read — at the cost of TLB flushes on every syscall, measured at 5–30 percent overhead on syscall-heavy workloads.

AMD processors were not affected by Meltdown (their permission checks are not deferred), and ARM's exposure varied by core — a reminder that these are *implementation* flaws, not ISA flaws [2].

### 4.4 The Cambrian explosion: Foreshadow, ZombieLoad, and systematic evaluation

Meltdown and Spectre were the first of a family. **Foreshadow** (L1TF) showed that speculative execution could extract data from Intel SGX enclaves and across hypervisor boundaries by abusing terminal-fault speculation on L1 cache contents [3]. **ZombieLoad**, **RIDL**, and **Fallout** (MDS attacks) demonstrated that *stale data in microarchitectural buffers* — line fill buffers, store buffers — could be sampled transiently, leaking data across threads sharing a core [3]. **SwapGS**, **Load Value Injection** (LVI), and **CrossTalk** extended the pattern to system registers and cross-core shared buffers.

Canella et al.'s systematic evaluation [3] organized this chaos into a taxonomy of *transient execution attacks* with three root causes: Spectre-type (mispredicted control/data flow), Meltdown-type (deferred fault handling), and MDS-type (buffer sampling). Their framework showed that mitigations targeting one root cause leave others exposed — explaining why the years after 2018 brought a steady drip of new CVEs despite massive patching.

## 5 Empirical Results and Proof Sketches

### 5.1 Attack primitive: Flush+Reload receiver

The exfiltration half of every cache-based transient attack is a timing receiver. The following sketches its logic:

```python
CACHE_HIT_THRESHOLD = 80  # cycles; calibrate per machine

def flush_reload(probe_array, n_slots=256, stride=4096):
    # 1. flush all probe lines
    for i in range(n_slots):
        clflush(probe_array + i * stride)
    # 2. victim transiently touches probe_array[secret * stride]
    trigger_transient_execution()
    # 3. reload and time each line; the fast one reveals the secret
    for i in range(n_slots):
        t0 = rdtsc()
        _ = probe_array[i * stride]   # volatile read
        if rdtsc() - t0 < CACHE_HIT_THRESHOLD:
            return i
    return None
```

Real exploits calibrate the threshold, repeat measurements to defeat noise, and use performance counters or TSX to suppress faults [2].

### 5.2 Measured exploitability and mitigation costs

| Attack | Boundary crossed | Bandwidth (reported) | Defeated by |
|---|---|---|---|
| Spectre V1 | Process / sandbox | ~10 KB/s (JS) | `lfence`, index masking, site isolation [1] |
| Spectre V2 | Process / VM | ~10 KB/s | Retpoline, eIBRS, STIBP [1] |
| Meltdown | User → kernel | ~500 KB/s | KPTI [2] |
| Foreshadow | Enclave / VM → host | ~7 B/s – KB/s | L1D flush, SGX attestation updates [3] |
| ZombieLoad (MDS) | Sibling hyperthread | ~KB/s | Buffer overwrite, HT disable [3] |

Mitigation overheads (early 2018–2019 measurements): KPTI 5–30 percent on syscall-intensive benchmarks, retpoline 5–15 percent on indirect-branch-heavy code, L1D flush on VM exit measurable in virtualization benchmarks, and disabling hyperthreading up to 30–40 percent throughput loss on SMT-friendly workloads. The industry converged on *selective* mitigation: apply the expensive defenses only where the threat model demands [3].

## 6 Limitations

**Incomplete software mitigation for Spectre.** Variant 1 has no general software fix — every bounds check in an address space is a potential gadget, and compilers cannot prove the absence of gadgets. The long-term answer is hardware: speculation barriers in silicon, or *invisible speculation* designs (e.g., Delay-on-Miss, InvisiSpec) that prevent transient loads from altering observable cache state [3]. These remain research proposals with area and complexity costs vendors have been slow to pay.

**Performance–security tension.** Every mitigation taxes performance; vendors and cloud providers therefore ship mitigations *disabled by default* in some configurations, or offer toggles — fragmenting the security posture of the ecosystem. The economic incentives favor performance, which is measurable, over speculative-execution safety, which is probabilistic.

**Verification gap.** Proving a processor immune to transient execution attacks requires reasoning about the *entire* microarchitectural state space under speculation — a formal verification challenge at the edge of current capability. Recent work on contracts (e.g., hardware–software leakage contracts) and model-checked speculation semantics is promising but far from covering commercial designs [3].

**New variants keep emerging.** From Spectre to Retbleed (2022, exploiting retpoline itself) to Downfall and Inception (2023, abusing gather instructions and phantom speculation), the attack surface has proven deeper than anticipated. Each new variant reopens the question of whether patch-and-mitigate can converge.

## 7 Conclusion

Meltdown and Spectre ended the era in which performance microarchitecture could be treated as security-invisible. The transient execution attack class — now dozens of variants deep — demonstrated that every speculative optimization is a potential information leak, and that the hardware/software contract must be renegotiated to include speculation semantics explicitly [1][2][3].

The path forward has three prongs: hardware that makes speculation *observably* safe (invisible speculation, taint tracking, speculation-aware memory models); software toolchains that can reason about transient information flow (speculative load hardening, verified barriers); and formal contracts that let each side verify its obligations. Until then, the lesson of 2018 stands: *anything the microarchitecture does can be observed, and anything observable can be exploited.*
