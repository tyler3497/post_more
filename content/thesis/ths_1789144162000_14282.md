# Reverse-Mode Automatic Differentiation from Adjoints to Compilers: Operator Overloading versus Source Transformation, Optimal Checkpointing, Higher-Order Derivatives, and Differentiable Programming Infrastructure

## Abstract

Reverse-mode automatic differentiation (AD) is the algorithmic substrate of differentiable programming: it evaluates the gradient of a scalar-valued function of *n* inputs at a cost bounded by a small constant multiple of the function evaluation itself, *independent of n*. This thesis unifies reverse-mode AD from the continuous **adjoint method** of optimal control theory through the two implementation paradigms — **operator overloading** with a runtime tape and **source transformation** with compile-time adjoint generation — to the **checkpointing frontier** trading memory for time along Griewank's optimal binomial *revolve* schedule, **higher-order derivatives** via principled forward/reverse mode composition, and the modern **differentiable compilers** JAX, Enzyme, and DiffTaichi. We formalize adjoint accumulation on the Wengert list, prove the cheap-gradient complexity theorem, analyze where each implementation strategy wins, derive the optimal checkpointing schedule, show how Pearlmutter's Hessian-vector product arises from forward-over-reverse composition, and assess how SSA-form and LLVM-IR differentiation preserve the asymptotic guarantees under control flow, mutation, and parallelism. We close with the limits of reverse mode: nondifferentiable primitives, chaotic dynamics, and adjoint numerical fragility.

##1 Introduction

Consider a differentiable program *F : ℝⁿ → ℝᵐ* implemented as a sequence of elementary operations

```
v_{-n+i} = x_i            for i = 1..n        (independent variables)
v_k      = φ_k(v_{j : j≺k})  for k = 1..ℓ      (intermediates)
y_i      = v_{ℓ−m+i}       for i = 1..m        (dependents)
```

called the **Wengert list** [1, §3]. Differentiating *F* exactly at machine precision — not approximately as in finite differences, and without the *expression swell* of symbolic differentiation — is the defining problem of automatic (or algorithmic) differentiation [1, 2]. Forward-mode AD propagates directional derivatives *ẋ ↦ JF(x)·ẋ* alongside the primal computation; reverse-mode AD instead propagates **adjoints**

```
v̄_i = ∂y/∂v_i  (for scalar output m = 1)
```

backward through the program, accumulating

```
v̄_j += v̄_i · ∂φ_i/∂v_j    for all j ≺ i, processing i = ℓ down to 1
```

so that a single reverse sweep yields the full gradient ∇*F(x)* ∈ ℝⁿ. This is *backpropagation* generalized from neural networks to arbitrary programs — the adjoint accumulation of Linnainmaa (1970) and Speelpenning (1980), described in essentially modern form in their theses [2].

The reason reverse mode dominates machine learning is purely dimensional: when *m ≪ n* — one scalar loss, millions of parameters — the complexity analysis of Griewank and Walther [1] guarantees a gradient at *O(cost(F))* time, whereas forward mode would need *n* sweeps. Yet reverse mode's memory cost is *also* O(cost(*F*)), because intermediate values must be available during the backward sweep. The engineering history of reverse-mode AD — tapes, checkpointing schedules, adjoint compilers, GPU caches — is a struggle against that linear-memory requirement.

This thesis is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews the mathematical background: the adjoint method in its continuous form from optimal control, dual numbers and the Jacobian-vector-product (JVP) / vector-Jacobian-product (VJP) abstraction, and the fundamental complexity theorems. Section 3 contrasts the two implementation families, operator overloading and source transformation, with precise statements about their overhead models and where each is asymptotically and practically superior. Section 4 develops the deep technical core: binomial checkpointing (*revolve*), activity analysis and recomputation in IR-level tools, higher-order derivatives via mode mixing, and the differentiable compilers JAX, Enzyme, and DiffTaichi. Section 5 presents the formal results — the cheap-gradient theorem and its higher-order variants — with a quantitative accounting. Section 6 catalogues limitations: nondifferentiability, chaotic and stochastic dynamics, and memory scaling on accelerators. Section 7 concludes with the trajectory toward adjoint differentiation of full programs, including parallel and distributed code.

##2 Background

### 2.1 The Adjoint Method in Optimal Control

Long before neural networks, the adjoint method was the standard tool of *optimal control*. Given *ẋ = f(x, u, θ)* and a scalar objective *L(x(T))*, the calculus of variations yields the **adjoint equation**

```
dλ/dt = −λᵀ ∂f/∂x,     λ(T) = ∂L/∂x(T)
dL/dθ = ∫₀ᵀ λᵀ ∂f/∂θ dt
```

computing the gradient with respect to *all* parameters *θ* from *one* backward integration of the adjoint *λ(t)* [6]. This is reverse-mode AD in the continuum limit: *λ(t) = ∂L/∂x(t)* is the continuous analogue of the discrete adjoints *v̄ᵢ*. Chen et al. [6] exploited this to train **neural ordinary differential equations** with *O(1)* memory — solving the adjoint ODE backward in time instead of backpropagating through the solver's internal steps. The same identity underlies reverse-mode differentiation of iterative solvers, implicit layers, and fixed-point iterations, where implicit differentiation via the adjoint avoids unrolling entirely [1, §15].### 2.2 Forward Mode, Dual Numbers, and the JVP/VJP Abstraction

Forward-mode AD replaces each real value *x* by a dual number *x + εẋ* with *ε² = 0*; evaluating the program on dual numbers yields *F(x) + ε·JF(x)ẋ*, one Jacobian-vector product (JVP) per pass [2]. Reverse mode computes the transpose operation, the **vector-Jacobian product** *v̄ ↦ v̄ᵀJF(x)*. Modern frameworks factor all of AD into these two linear primitives: a *JVP rule* and a *VJP rule* per primitive operation — the basis of the higher-order treatment in Section 4.3. For *F : ℝⁿ → ℝ* the full gradient costs one VJP at seed *v̄ = 1*; full Hessians are generally prohibitive — which is why Hessian-free optimization works with Hessian-vector products instead [2].

### 2.3 Fundamental Complexity Bounds

Griewank and Walther [1, §3–4] establish the two bedrock facts of AD complexity, often called the **cheap gradient principle**:

> **Theorem:** Let *F : ℝⁿ → ℝᵐ* be evaluated at cost *c(F)* time and *s(F)* memory. Then for any *ẋ ∈ ℝⁿ*, the forward-mode JVP *JF·ẋ* costs *O(c(F))* time and *O(s(F))* memory; and for any *ȳ ∈ ℝ¹ˣᵐ*, the reverse-mode VJP *ȳ·JF* costs *O(c(F))* time but *O(c(F))* memory in the worst case. Consequently a full gradient (*m = 1*) costs *O(c(F))* time, independent of *n*.

The time bound for reverse mode is achieved by the adjoint accumulation above: each elementary operation *φᵢ* contributes a bounded number of multiply-adds per adjoint update, so the reverse sweep is a constant multiple (in practice roughly 2–5×) of the forward sweep. The memory bound is the price: intermediate values *vᵢ* must persist until their adjoints are accumulated. A naive implementation stores the entire primal trace — the **tape** or Wengert list — consuming *O(c(F))* memory; all of Section 4.2 is devoted to shrinking this term without destroying the time bound.

### 2.4 Source Transformation versus Operator Overloading

The two classical implementation families differ in *when* the adjoint program is constructed [1, §6; 2]:

| Aspect | Operator overloading | Source transformation |
|---|---|---|
| Mechanism | Primal types replaced by AD types; operations record onto a runtime tape | Compiler analyzes primal source/IR and emits adjoint source/IR |
| Tape | Dynamic Wengert list, recorded at runtime | No runtime tape; adjoint code generated statically |
| Control flow | Handled naturally (the tape records the executed path) | Requires reversal of branches, loops, and calls |
| Language coverage | Easy: just overload operators | Hard: must parse/analyze the full language |
| Performance ceiling | Tape recording + interpretation overhead | Near hand-written adjoint performance |
| Examples | Adept, ADOL-C, PyTorch autograd | Tapenade, Zygote, DiffTaichi, Enzyme |

This dichotomy was long considered exhaustive, and the survey literature [2] treats it as the fundamental design axis. Section 3 revisits it with a sharper cost model, and Section 4 shows how recent systems — notably Enzyme, which differentiates **LLVM IR** rather than source [3], and JAX, which traces into a functional *jaxpr* IR [7] — blur the boundary.

##3 Methodology

Our analysis proceeds on three levels. **(i) Formal:** adjoint accumulation on the Wengert list and the cheap-gradient complexity bounds [1], including higher-order variants by mode composition. **(ii) Comparative:** an explicit cost model for operator overloading versus source transformation, parametrized by tape-recording overhead *τ*, activity ratio *α*, and optimization quality *q*, instantiated against published data from the Enzyme [3] and DiffTaichi [4] studies. **(iii) Systems:** three differentiable compilers — JAX's trace-into-jaxpr pipeline [7], Enzyme's LLVM-IR adjoint synthesis [3], and DiffTaichi's megakernel source transformation [4] — evaluated on how each recovers the reverse-mode time bound under control flow, mutation, parallelism, and GPU execution.

The running example throughout is the scalar program

```python
def f(x):
    v1 = x * x        # phi_1
    v2 = sin(v1)      # phi_2
    v3 = v2 + v1      # phi_3
    return v3
```

whose Wengert list has *n = 1, ℓ = 3, m = 1*. Its adjoint sweep is

```python
v3_bar = 1.0                     # seed: dL/dy
v2_bar = v3_bar * 1.0            # dphi_3/dv2 = 1
v1_bar = v3_bar * 1.0 + v2_bar * cos(v1)   # fan-out accumulation
x_bar  = v1_bar * (2.0 * x)      # dphi_1/dx
```

illustrating the two operations that define all of reverse mode: *local partials* (each *∂φᵢ/∂vⱼ* evaluated at recorded primal values) and *fan-out accumulation* (adjoints summed over all uses of a variable). Everything that follows is a study of how to perform these two operations at scale.

##4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Checkpointing and the Memory–Recompute Frontier

The *O(c(F))* memory of naive reverse mode is fatal for long programs: training a transformer stores every activation; backpropagating through a thousand-step simulation stores every state. **Checkpointing** trades time for memory by storing only a subset of intermediate states (checkpoints) and *recomputing* the segments between them during the reverse sweep [1, §12].

For a sequential program of *ℓ* steps with *c* checkpoints (snapshots), the classical analysis gives total work roughly *ℓ + ℓ²/c*: the forward pass plus, for each segment, recomputation from the nearest checkpoint. Griewank's **revolve** schedule [1, §12.3] is the optimal answer to *"given c checkpoints, what is the minimal recomputation?"*: it places checkpoints by binomial coefficients, achieving a logarithmic penalty. Concretely, with *c* checkpoints the binomial schedule evaluates an *ℓ*-step program with

```
work(ℓ, c) = ℓ · t(ℓ, c),   where t(ℓ, c) ≈ minimal r with C(r+c, c) ≥ ℓ
```

so *t* grows like *O(log ℓ)* for fixed *c*. Revolve was published as ACM Algorithm 799 [1] and remains the reference optimal schedule for the *store-all intermediate states* model; its optimality holds under the assumption that each step costs the same and that checkpoints are restored by exact recomputation.

Modern practice refines the picture. **PyTorch** checkpoints at module granularity (`torch.utils.checkpoint`), recomputing a layer's activations during backward — coarse and user-directed, sacrificing revolve-optimality for simplicity. **JAX** exposes `jax.checkpoint` (remat), which rematerializes a function's intermediates under `grad` and composes correctly with `jit`, `vmap`, and `pmap` [7]. **DiffTaichi** [4] takes the opposite route for simulation: its lightweight tape records only the *program structure* (kernel launches), and gradient kernels are replayed in reverse order with fields recomputed or selectively cached — memory stays flat while the schedule is dictated by the kernel call graph rather than revolve's uniform-step model.

> **Theorem:** For a linear chain of ℓ elementary steps, no checkpointing schedule with c stored states achieves total work less than ℓ·t(ℓ,c) with t defined by the binomial schedule above; revolve attains the bound. [1, §12]

The practical upshot is a *frontier*, not a point: practitioners choose where on the memory–time curve to sit, and systems increasingly automate the choice (JAX's remat policies, Enzyme's activity-driven caching, DiffTaichi's structural tape).

### 4.2 Differentiable Compilers: JAX, Enzyme, and DiffTaichi

**JAX** [7] differentiates Python by *tracing*: executing the function on abstract tracers records a functional, first-order intermediate representation — the **jaxpr** — a straight-line program of primitives with static data dependencies. `jax.grad` applies each primitive's VJP rule to produce a transposed jaxpr, which XLA compiles. Because the IR is functional, reversing it is straightforward; control flow uses restricted functional forms (`lax.scan`, `lax.cond`, `lax.while_loop`) with known transpose rules. Composability falls out: `grad(jit(grad(f)))` works because each transformation is an endomorphism on jaxprs. The price is the *tracing discipline*: Python side effects and data-dependent control flow are frozen at trace time [7].

**Enzyme** [3] performs AD on **optimized LLVM IR**: the call `__enzyme_autodiff(f, ...)` is replaced by a compiler pass synthesizing the gradient of the IR *after* optimization. This yields two decisive advantages: (i) AD applies to any language with an LLVM frontend (C, C++, Fortran, Julia, Rust, Swift) without per-language VJP rules; (ii) differentiating *after* optimization gives a geometric-mean **4.2× speedup over AD before optimization** on the ADBench suite, because dead code elimination, LICM, and control-flow simplification have already removed work the adjoint generator would otherwise reverse. Enzyme is thus *source transformation on the compiler IR* — the performance ceiling of source transformation without its language-coverage problem. Its memory handling is **activity analysis**: only values with possibly-nonzero adjoints are cached. On GPUs, Enzyme pioneered reverse-mode AD of CUDA kernels with AD-specific caching optimizations improving runtime by orders of magnitude over naive caching [3].

**DiffTaichi** [4] differentiates an imperative, parallel language for physical simulation. Its design is shaped by the *megakernel* structure of simulators: each time step is a large parallel kernel with high arithmetic intensity, and differentiating operator-by-operator (as TensorFlow/PyTorch would) destroys that intensity. DiffTaichi applies **source code transformations that preserve the megakernel structure**: each primal kernel becomes a gradient kernel of the same parallel shape, and a lightweight tape records kernel launches to replay gradient kernels in reverse order. The results [4] are striking: a differentiable elastic simulator 4.2× shorter than hand-written CUDA at equal speed, and **188× faster than the TensorFlow implementation**. DiffTaichi's lesson is that the *granularity* of adjoint generation should match the granularity of the primal's performance structure — which also explains why Enzyme differentiates optimized IR rather than source.

| System | AD level | Reversal unit | Memory strategy | Control flow |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| JAX | jaxpr (traced) | primitive transpose | remat / checkpoint | functional control ops |
| Enzyme | LLVM IR | IR adjoint synthesis | activity analysis + caching | full CFG reversal |
| DiffTaichi | source kernels | kernel-to-kernel transform | structural tape + selective recompute | kernel-level replay |

### 4.3 Higher-Order Derivatives by Mode Composition

Differentiating an already-differentiated program yields higher-order derivatives, and the JVP/VJP factorization makes the design space explicit [2]. For the Hessian *H = ∇²F* of a scalar *F*:

- **Forward-over-reverse**: apply forward mode to the reverse-mode gradient program. Pearlmutter's **R-operator** [2] computes the Hessian-vector product *Hv* at *O(c(F))* via *R_v{∇F(x)} = ∂/∂r ∇F(x + rv)|_{r=0}* — one forward sweep over the adjoint program, never forming *H*. This is the workhorse of Hessian-free optimization.
- **Reverse-over-forward / reverse-over-reverse**: also yield *Hv* at *O(c(F))*, but reverse-over-reverse requires differentiating the adjoint accumulation itself, demanding care with mutated adjoint variables.

JAX exposes this composition directly: `jax.grad(jax.grad(f))` is reverse-over-reverse on jaxprs, correct because each `grad` is a closed transformation [7]. Zygote [5] achieves higher-order AD the same way at the Julia SSA level: since the adjoint transform maps SSA programs to SSA programs, it can be applied repeatedly. A subtlety: reverse-over-reverse on a program that *mutates* adjoints requires differentiating the mutation itself, which is why Zygote's SSA-based approach — where mutation is explicit in the IR — is cleaner than differentiating an operator-overloading tape, in which the tape is an imperative side effect outside the differentiated language.

> **Theorem:** For F : ℝⁿ → ℝ evaluated at cost c(F), the Hessian-vector product Hv can be computed at O(c(F)) time by forward-over-reverse composition (Pearlmutter's R-technique), and full higher-order tensors of order k at O(n^{k−1}·c(F)) via repeated reverse mode. [1, §13; 2]

### 4.4 Activity, Sparsity, and the Cost of Being General

A large fraction of reverse-mode work is wasted on **inactive** variables — intermediates that do not influence the output. **Activity analysis** [1, §6] statically determines which variables carry nonzero adjoints and prunes the rest; Enzyme's implementation is credited with a substantial share of its advantage over tape-based tools [3]. Beyond activity, **sparsity** matters: compression techniques (CPR seeding, graph coloring [1, §7–8]) exploit structured Jacobians to recover many columns per sweep. Real performance comes from static analyses — activity, alias, sparsity — that specialize the generic adjoint sweep. This is precisely where compiler-based AD (Enzyme on LLVM IR, Zygote on Julia SSA [5]) outperforms runtime tapes: the analyses run on the same IR the optimizer uses.

##5 Empirical Results / Formal Proofs

We consolidate the formal claims and the quantitative evidence reported in the literature.

**Proof sketch of the cheap-gradient theorem [1, §3.4].** Each *φᵢ* depends on a bounded number of arguments. The forward evaluation performs *O(ℓ)* elementary operations; the adjoint sweep processes each *φᵢ* once, performing per argument one multiply-add *v̄ⱼ += v̄ᵢ·∂φᵢ/∂vⱼ* plus the local partial at recorded primal values — constant work per argument. Hence the reverse sweep costs *O(ℓ) = O(c(F))* with empirical overhead *ω* ≈ 2–5, at *O(ℓ)* memory reducible by checkpointing. ∎

**Quantitative results from the literature:**

1. **Enzyme vs. prior art** [3]: on an ML-focused benchmark suite including ADBench, differentiating optimized LLVM IR achieves a geometric-mean **4.2× speedup** over differentiating unoptimized IR, and state-of-the-art performance against Tapenade, Adept, and PyTorch, while requiring *no code rewriting* for C/C++/Fortran/Julia/Rust/Swift programs.
2. **DiffTaichi vs. TensorFlow** [4]: a differentiable elastic-body simulator **188× faster** than the TensorFlow implementation and 4.2× more concise than hand-engineered CUDA at equal runtime.
3. **Neural ODEs** [6]: the adjoint sensitivity method trains continuous-depth models with **O(1) memory** in the number of solver steps, versus *O(steps)* for backpropagation through the solver.
4. **Checkpointing theory** [1]: for an *ℓ*-step chain with *c* checkpoints, revolve achieves *ℓ·t(ℓ,c)* work with *t* growing logarithmically — e.g., *ℓ = 10⁶, c = 100* needs only a small single-digit recomputation factor.
5. **Higher-order scaling** [1, §13]: the full Hessian costs *O(n·c(F))* via *n* reverse sweeps; Pearlmutter's forward-over-reverse *Hv* costs *O(c(F))*, which is why large-scale second-order methods use *Hv* products rather than *H*.

##6 Limitations

1. **Nondifferentiable and discontinuous primitives.** Reverse mode requires each *φᵢ* to be differentiable at the evaluation point. Branching on floating-point comparisons, integer arithmetic, sorting, or table lookups yields adjoints that are zero almost everywhere or undefined — formally correct but *useless* gradients, the "differentiating a simulator does not always yield useful gradients" lesson of DiffTaichi [4].
2. **Chaotic and stiff dynamics.** The adjoint ODE of a chaotic system grows exponentially backward in time; gradients of long chaotic trajectories are numerically meaningless (shadowing theory aside). Neural ODE training [6] is known to suffer when the learned vector field becomes stiff, and the adjoint recomputation then also becomes expensive as the solver takes tiny steps.
3. **Memory on accelerators.** The *O(c(F))* memory bound bites hardest on GPUs, where memory is scarce and bandwidth-dominated. Enzyme's GPU work [3] shows that naive caching of forward values for the reverse pass can slow programs by orders of magnitude; AD-specific cache optimizations are required, and checkpointing interacts poorly with kernel-fusion heuristics.
4. **Higher-order and mutation.** Reverse-over-reverse through mutable adjoint state is semantically delicate; operator-overloading tapes handle it by re-recording, which is correct but slow, while SSA-based systems [5] must differentiate mutation explicitly. True higher-order derivatives of programs with I/O, randomness, or concurrency remain outside the theory.

##7 Conclusion

Reverse-mode AD is best understood not as "backpropagation" but as the **adjoint method applied to programs**: a single backward sweep propagating sensitivity of a scalar output through every intermediate value at *O(1)* overhead, independent of input dimension [1]. The field's history is the progressive removal of obstacles between that asymptotic promise and practice: operator overloading made AD usable on legacy code at the price of tape overhead; source transformation recovered hand-written-adjoint performance at the price of language coverage; checkpointing and revolve tamed the memory blowup along an optimal curve; mode composition delivered higher-order derivatives at one extra sweep per order; and the current generation of differentiable compilers — JAX's traced jaxprs [7], Enzyme's LLVM-IR synthesis [3], DiffTaichi's megakernel transforms [4], Zygote's SSA adjoints [5] — moves AD into the compiler, where activity analysis, optimization-before-differentiation, and structure-preserving transforms recover the theoretical bounds on real hardware. The remaining frontier is semantic rather than asymptotic: differentiating programs with discontinuities, chaos, and effects, where the adjoint exists formally but means nothing numerically.
