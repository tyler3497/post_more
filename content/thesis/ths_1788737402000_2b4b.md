# Software Transactional Memory: Opacity, TL2, NOrec, and the Privatization-Safety Problem

## Abstract

Software transactional memory (STM) offers atomic blocks with the composability that locks lack, but its correctness theory is subtler than the database serializability it borrows. This thesis unifies three pillars of modern STM: the *opacity* criterion of Guerraoui and Kapałka, which requires even aborted transactions to observe only consistent states; the TL2 algorithm of Dice, Shalev, and Shavit, which pairs commit-time locking with a global version clock for opacity at near-lock performance; and NOrec, the ownership-record-free design of Dalessandro, Spear, and Scott, which replaces per-location metadata with a single sequence lock and value-based validation. We dissect the *privatization-safety* problem — the hazardous interplay of transactional and nontransactional access — comparing transactional fences, epoch-based quiescence, and the *fundamental theorem of transactional memory* that restores strong atomicity for data-race-free programs. We survey empirical evidence on STM performance versus fine-grained locking and close with inherent costs and open problems.

## 1. Introduction

The multicore revolution exposed a crisis in concurrent programming: locks do not *compose*. A thread-safe queue and a thread-safe map cannot be fused into an atomic "move" without exposing locking protocols and inviting deadlock. Herlihy and Moss proposed *transactional memory* in 1993 as hardware support for lock-free structures [1]; the software turn — Shavit and Touitou's static STM [2], then Herlihy, Luchangco, Moir, and Scherer's dynamic DSTM [3] — recast transactions as a general programming construct.

STM's bet is simple: mark code `atomic`, and the runtime makes it *appear* atomic. But what does that mean for a transaction that *aborts*? Its reads may return values no committed transaction ever wrote, driving it into an infinite loop or a division by zero before the runtime notices. Classical *serializability* constrains only committed work, so it is silent about this hazard — unsound as an engineering contract for code that executes on real memory.

*Opacity* [4] closes the gap: every transaction, committed or not, must observe a consistent state drawn from one sequential total order. Enforcing opacity efficiently is the hard problem. Naive validation is ruinous, and per-location metadata inflates fast-path latency. Two landmark algorithms attacked it from opposite sides. TL2 [5] kept per-location metadata but reduced validation to a single global version clock. NOrec [6] abolished per-location metadata entirely, validating against values in a redo log guarded by one sequence lock.

A third thread completes the picture: *privatization*. Real programs mix transactional and nontransactional access — a thread may atomically unlink a structure, then manipulate it with raw pointers. A concurrently reading transaction may then be *doomed*, racing with uninstrumented writes and observing impossible states [7]. Solutions range from *transactional fences* to timestamp schemes to NOrec's inherent safety, and Khyzha et al. later proved a *fundamental theorem*: strongly opaque TMs give strongly atomic semantics to data-race-free programs [8].

> **Definition (Opacity).** A finite history $H$ is *opaque* if there exists a sequential history $S$ equivalent to some completion of $H$ such that (i) $S$ preserves the real-time order of $H$, (ii) every transaction in $S$ is *legal* — each read returns the latest preceding write in $S$ — and (iii) every transaction in $H$, committed or not, appears in $S$ [4].

Section 2 surveys the design space; Section 3 describes our methodology; Section 4 develops opacity, TL2, NOrec, and privatization in depth; Section 5 covers empirical results and proofs; Sections 6–7 discuss limitations and conclude.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 The anatomy of an STM

Every STM answers four design questions [9]:

| Design axis | Options | Representative systems |
|---|---|---|
| Version management | Eager (undo log) vs. lazy (redo log) | McRT-STM (eager); TL2, NOrec (lazy) |
| Conflict detection | Eager (encounter-time) vs. lazy (commit-time) | TinySTM (eager); TL2, NOrec (lazy) |
| Read visibility | Visible vs. invisible reads | DSTM2 (visible); TL2, NOrec (invisible) |
| Progress guarantee | Blocking vs. obstruction-free / lock-free | TL2, NOrec (blocking); DSTM (obstruction-free) |

*Lazy* systems buffer writes and publish them only at commit, making abort trivial. *Eager* systems write in place with an undo log — cheaper commits, but uncommitted values become visible to readers. *Invisible reads* leave no metadata trace, so readers never contend, at the cost of validation-based conflict discovery. TL2 and NOrec both choose lazy versioning, lazy detection, and invisible reads: the combination that minimizes the fast path.

### 2.2 Progress conditions

Blocking STMs use internal locks; a halted lock-holder can stall others. Nonblocking designs avoid this at a cost: DSTM guaranteed *obstruction-freedom* — a transaction running alone eventually commits — though concurrent transactions may livelock [3]. Stronger guarantees are provably expensive; Attiya, Hillel, and Milani showed weak disjoint-access-parallel, strictly serializable TMs with invisible reads cannot be obstruction-free under plausible liveness assumptions [10]. Most deployed STMs are therefore blocking, NOrec settling for *livelock freedom*: no execution in which transactions abort forever without any commit [6].

### 2.3 A brief genealogy

From Herlihy and Moss's hardware proposal [1] through Shavit and Touitou [2] and DSTM [3], to Harris et al.'s *composable memory transactions* in Concurrent Haskell — which added `retry` and `orElse`, subsuming condition synchronization [11] — the field then entered its lock-based wave: TL, TL2 [5], RSTM, McRT-STM, TinySTM, and the time-based SwissTM [12]. NOrec [6] demonstrated the metadata itself was optional. Hybrid designs (HyTM) [13] later explored hardware/software cooperation, but the software core remains TL2/NOrec-shaped.

## 3. Methodology

This thesis is an analytical synthesis with three components. First, *algorithmic reconstruction*: we re-derive TL2 and NOrec from their invariants, identifying which steps enforce opacity and which enforce progress. Second, *criterion comparison*: we place opacity, strict serializability, and virtual-world consistency (VWC) [14] on common formal footing and exhibit separating executions. Third, *evidence triangulation*: we corroborate performance and safety claims against published suites (STAMP, STMBench7) and graph-theoretic and machine-checked opacity proofs [4][15]. Throughout we separate *safety* (admissible histories) from *liveness* (progress) and from *performance* (cost) — conflating them has produced both unsound algorithms and unfair benchmarks.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Correctness criteria: from serializability to opacity

Classical *serializability* constrains only the *committed* projection of a history. Consider the canonical anomaly:

```
Initially x = 0, y = 0
T1:  write(x, 0); write(y, 1); commit
T2:  r1 = read(x); r2 = read(y); z = r2 / r1; ...
```

If $T_2$ reads $x = 0$ (before $T_1$) and $y = 1$ (after $T_1$), it divides by zero — yet the committed history $\{T_1\}$ is trivially serializable. The process crashes while the criterion is satisfied. Optimistic STMs with invisible reads routinely expose such *inconsistent snapshots* between validation points; the anomaly is not hypothetical.

Guerraoui and Kapałka's *opacity* [4] requires the sequential witness $S$ to contain **every** transaction in $H$, including aborted and live ones, preserving real-time order. The $T_2$ above cannot exist under opacity: no sequential $S$ containing it admits $r_1 = 0 \land r_2 = 1$. Opacity strictly strengthens serializability and captures what TM designers had informally assumed all along.

A weaker alternative is *virtual-world consistency* [14]: each transaction sees a *causally consistent* past — its reads are mutually consistent but need not fit one global total order. VWC permits the anomaly above and is cheaper to enforce, but cannot prevent the division-by-zero class of failures. The hierarchy is strict:

$$\text{opacity} \;\Rightarrow\; \text{strict serializability} \;\Rightarrow\; \text{VWC} \;\Rightarrow\; \text{serializability}.$$

> **Theorem (informal).** No STM with invisible reads can keep transactions from observing inconsistent states without either validating each read against a global serialization point or sandboxing the transaction's effects (e.g., managed-code fault containment) [4][7].

### 4.2 TL2: transactional locking with a global version clock

TL2 [5] is a blocking, lazy, invisible-read STM whose insight is that a **single global version clock** orders all transactions cheaply. Memory is partitioned into *stripes*, each with a versioned write-lock:

```c
typedef struct { atomic_uint ver; } stripe_t; /* lock bit + version */
atomic_uint GVC = 0;

void tx_begin(tx *T) { T->rv = atomic_load(&GVC); /* snapshot time */ }

int tx_read(tx *T, addr a) {
    s = stripe(a); v = atomic_load(&s.ver);
    if (is_locked(v) || version(v) > T->rv) tx_abort(T);
    val = *a;                                            /* speculative */
    if (atomic_load(&s.ver) != v) tx_abort(T);            /* torn check */
    T->readset ∪= {(a, v)}; return val;
}
void tx_write(tx *T, addr a, val w) { T->writeset[a] = w; /* buffered */ }

void tx_commit(tx *T) {
    for (a in T->writeset) lock(stripe(a));        /* commit-time, sorted */
    T->wv = atomic_fetch_add(&GVC, 1) + 1;        /* linearization point */
    for ((a, v) in T->readset)                     /* validate snapshot */
        if (is_locked(stripe(a)) || version(stripe(a)) > T->rv)
            { unlock_all(); tx_abort(T); }
    for ((a, w) in T->writeset) *a = w;             /* publish */
    for (a in T->writeset) { set_version(stripe(a), T->wv); unlock(stripe(a)); }
}
```

The read version $rv$ sampled at begin defines a snapshot: any stripe versioned above $rv$ was written by a transaction serializing after $T$ began, so reading it aborts. The commit-time `fetch_add` gives each writer a unique $wv > rv$, totally ordering writers; locking the write set before validation keeps the read-set check stable. Opacity follows because every read is validated against versions $\le rv < wv$: $T$ observes exactly the memory state at logical time $rv$ [5].

TL2's engineering mattered as much as its algorithm. Metadata lives in a stripe table indexed by address hash rather than object headers, so TL2 "fits seamlessly with any systems memory life-cycle, including those using `malloc`/`free`" [5] — freed memory needs no metadata cleanup. And because the version check aborts *before the value is used*, TL2 "efficiently avoids periods of unsafe execution": user code sees only consistent states. Benchmarks showed TL2 competitive with the best hand-crafted fine-grained structures — roughly $10\times$ a single global lock [5]. The cost is the GVC itself: one contended cache line per committing writer, a bottleneck on large multi-socket machines.

### 4.3 NOrec: abolishing ownership records

NOrec [6] deletes the stripe table. Its shared metadata is a single global *sequence lock*; consistency comes from *value-based validation* against a per-transaction redo log:

```c
atomic_uint seqlock = 0; /* even = free, odd = writer committing */

int tx_read(tx *T, addr a) {
    while (1) {
        s0 = atomic_load(&seqlock);
        if (s0 & 1) continue;                 /* writer committing: spin */
        val = *a;                             /* plain, uninstrumented read */
        if (atomic_load(&seqlock) != s0) continue;
        if (s0 != T->snapshot) { validate(T); T->snapshot = s0; }
        T->readset ∪= {(a, val)}; return val;
    }
}
void tx_commit(tx *T) {
    if (T->writeset == ∅) return;
    while (!cas(&seqlock, T->snapshot, T->snapshot + 1)) {
        validate(T); T->snapshot = atomic_load(&seqlock);
    }
    for ((a, w) in T->writeset) *a = w;       /* publish under the lock */
    atomic_fetch_add(&seqlock, 1);            /* release */
}
void validate(tx *T) {
    for ((a, v) in T->readset) if (*a != v) tx_abort(T);
}
```

Reads cost two seqlock loads around a plain load — no per-location metadata. A committing writer serializes through the seqlock and revalidates before publishing; the successful CAS is the linearization point. Because the writer holds the seqlock across write-back, no partial commit is observable; because validation compares *values*, version-wraparound anomalies vanish. NOrec is privatization-safe *by construction* (Section 4.4) and *livelock-free*: the CAS loop lets some committer through, though individuals may starve — a documented tradeoff [6].

The price is scalability: the seqlock serializes all committing writers, so write-heavy many-core workloads degrade toward single-lock throughput. NOrec "is unlikely to scale to hundreds of active threads" but offers "very low fast-path latency — as low as any system we know of that admits concurrent updates" [6]. It dominates the low-contention regime; HTM-assisted hybrids like RHNOrec [13] cover the rest.

| | TL2 | NOrec |
|---|---|---|
| Shared metadata | Stripe table (versioned locks) | Single sequence lock |
| Read-set validation | Version $\le rv$ | Value equality |
| Writer serialization | Per-stripe locks | Global seqlock |
| Privatization safety | Requires fence/epochs | By construction |
| Scalability limit | GVC contention | Seqlock serialization |
| Progress | Blocking | Livelock-free |

### 4.4 Privatization safety and the doomed-transaction problem

*Privatization* removes shared data from the transactional domain: a thread atomically unlinks a structure, then manipulates it with raw, uninstrumented accesses — for speed, I/O, or legacy code. The failure mode is the *doomed transaction*. Suppose $T_P$ privatizes list $L$ and walks it with raw pointers while $T_D$, which earlier read `priv == false`, traverses $L$ transactionally. $T_P$'s raw writes are invisible to STM metadata, so $T_D$'s next instrumented read returns a value *no transaction ever wrote*. $T_D$ will abort at its next validation — but first it may dereference a corrupted pointer or divide by zero [7]. TL2's stripe versions stay consistent; the *values* do not.

Three solution families exist. **Transactional fences** (quiescence): after a privatizing commit, wait until all concurrent transactions finish before touching data nontransactionally. Airtight, but $O(P)$ per privatization, and every transaction needs fencing if privatizers cannot be identified statically. **Timestamp/epoch schemes**: the GVC bounds the danger window — a privatizer waits only for transactions with $rv$ older than its commit point [12]. **Inherent safety**: NOrec needs no fence. Its commit protocol linearizes the privatizer before any conflicting reader can commit, and any live reader that sampled the pre-privatization state fails *value* validation — the privatizer's raw writes changed the values — aborting before acting on them [6].

The deepest treatment is the *fundamental theorem of transactional memory* [8]: a *strongly opaque* TM — opaque even amid nontransactional accesses that are race-free under strong atomicity — gives strongly atomic semantics to every program that is data-race-free *assuming* strong atomicity. The programmer's contract is familiar ("avoid data races, as under one global lock"); privatization and publication idioms satisfy it when a fence separates the privatizing transaction from the raw access. What was a bag of tricks becomes a proper contract between language, programmer, and runtime. Attiya and Hillel showed the contract has inherent cost: privatization-safe STMs need $\Omega(P)$ synchronization in the worst case [10].

## 5. Empirical Results and Proofs

**Benchmarks.** TL2 was evaluated on red-black trees, skip lists, and STAMP workloads, matching the best hand-crafted fine-grained structures at roughly $10\times$ a single coarse lock [5]. NOrec was measured against TL2, TinySTM, and RSTM on STAMP: it wins decisively at low thread counts and read-dominated workloads — its read barrier is nearly free — and degrades gracefully, not catastrophically, under writer contention [6]. SwissTM later showed that mixing eager and lazy strategies with two-phase contention management beats both TL2 and TinySTM on several STAMP workloads, at considerable implementation complexity [12]. The stable lesson: lazy/invisible designs minimize the uncontended fast path; eager/visible designs win under high contention by resolving conflicts early. No STM dominates everywhere.

**Proofs of opacity.** Guerraoui and Kapałka's graph characterization reduces opacity to cycle-freedom of the *opacity graph* — vertices for transactions, edges for real-time order, read-from, and version order [4]; TL2-style protocols have since been machine-checked against it. NOrec's opacity rests on a simpler invariant: the seqlock totally orders committing writers, and each transaction's read set is validated against one seqlock value, so every transaction observes the post-state of some committed prefix [6]. *Last-use opacity* [15] refines the criterion for STMs with early release, showing where it can be relaxed without admitting the division-by-zero class of anomalies.

**Privatization in practice.** Spear et al. measured fence-based, epoch-based, and NOrec-style privatization across STMs [7]: privatization support costs little when privatization is rare, but dominates when transactions privatize frequently — the empirical face of the $\Omega(P)$ lower bound. NOrec's zero-marginal-cost safety is most valuable in privatization-heavy workloads such as worklist parallelism.

## 6. Limitations

Four limitations bound these claims. **Progress:** TL2 and NOrec are blocking or livelock-free; neither guarantees an individual transaction commits. Obstruction-free designs pay $2$–$3\times$ overhead [3], and stronger progress is provably expensive [10]. **The single-writer bottleneck:** TL2's GVC and NOrec's seqlock both serialize committing writers; write-heavy workloads remain STM's Achilles' heel, motivating HyTM offload [13]. **Opacity's scope:** it governs safety but not *which* nontransactional accesses are legal — the DRF contract of [8] must be enforced by language or programmer — and it composes poorly with irreversible operations, which is why Haskell's STM isolates effects in the type system [11]. **Empirical scope:** evaluations lean on STAMP microbenchmarks with small transactions; large-transaction, allocation-heavy, and NUMA-sensitive workloads, and STM/GC/JIT interactions, remain sparsely measured.

---

## 7. Conclusion

STM matured when its correctness criterion caught up with its mechanisms. Opacity gave precise meaning to the demand that even doomed transactions never observe impossible states; TL2 showed a global version clock could enforce it at near-lock speed while accommodating `malloc`/`free`; NOrec showed the metadata itself was dispensable, buying privatization safety and minimal fast-path overhead at the cost of writer scalability. Privatization, once ad-hoc fences, is now a contract — strong opacity plus data-race freedom — with provable inherent costs. The frontier has moved to hybrid hardware/software designs and persistent-memory transactions, but the core questions endure: *what may a transaction observe, what must it cost, and what happens when the transactional world meets the nontransactional one.* Every future system will answer them in the vocabulary that opacity, TL2, NOrec, and privatization safety established.
