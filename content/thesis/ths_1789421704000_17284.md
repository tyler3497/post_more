# The Stone–von Neumann Theorem and the Mackey Machine: Uniqueness of the Canonical Commutation Relations

## Abstract

The Stone–von Neumann theorem is the central uniqueness theorem of non-relativistic quantum mechanics: it states that the canonical commutation relations admit, up to unitary equivalence, exactly one irreducible unitary representation that integrates to the Heisenberg group — the Schrödinger representation on square-integrable functions. This survey traces the theorem from its origins in the 1930–1931 work of Stone and von Neumann, through Weyl's bounded exponentiated form of the commutation relations, to George Mackey's 1949 proof via systems of imprimitivity, the founding example of what became the Mackey machine for induced representations of locally compact groups. We present the modern representation-theoretic formulation: the Weyl relations define a projective representation of the classical phase space, and uniqueness is equivalent to the classification of irreducible unitary representations of the Heisenberg group with a fixed non-trivial central character. We explain where uniqueness breaks down — for infinitely many degrees of freedom, inequivalent representations proliferate, a phenomenon whose quantum-field-theoretic avatar is Haag's theorem — and how Mackey's generalization extends the uniqueness statement to Heisenberg groups built on arbitrary locally compact abelian groups.

## 1 Introduction

When quantum mechanics arrived in 1925–1927, it arrived twice. Heisenberg's *matrix mechanics* encoded observables as infinite matrices, while Schrödinger's *wave mechanics* represented states as functions and momenta as differential operators [4]. The physical equivalence of the two formalisms was persuasive but not proved, and the underlying mathematical structures — unbounded operators satisfying $PQ - QP = -i\hbar I$ on some dense domain — were fraught with domain pathologies that only von Neumann's spectral theory could tame [6].

The definitive resolution came in two steps. First, Hermann Weyl (1928) replaced the unbounded relation by its *exponentiated*, bounded form: the unitary one-parameter groups $U(t) = e^{itP}$ and $V(s) = e^{isQ}$ satisfy $U(t)V(s) = e^{its}V(s)U(t)$, which avoids domains entirely. Second, Stone (1930) and von Neumann (1931) proved that the solutions of this *Weyl relation* are essentially unique: every irreducible representation is unitarily equivalent to the Schrödinger representation on $L^2(\mathbb{R})$, and every representation decomposes as a direct sum of copies of it [4][3].

Decades later, George Mackey gave the proof that modern mathematicians consider the *right* one. His 1949 analysis recast the Weyl pair as a *system of imprimitivity* and showed that irreducibility plus transitivity of the underlying group action forces the canonical form [7][5]. That argument was the seed of the **Mackey machine**: the general theory of induced representations of semidirect products, which classifies unitary representations of regular semidirect products by the orbits of a subgroup on the dual of a normal abelian subgroup [6].

## 2 Background: The Canonical Commutation Relations

### The unbounded relations and their pathologies

Heisenberg's canonical commutation relations (CCR) for a system with $d$ degrees of freedom assert the existence of self-adjoint *position* operators $Q_1,\dots,Q_d$ and *momentum* operators $P_1,\dots,P_d$ on a Hilbert space $\mathcal{H}$ satisfying

$$P_k Q_\ell - Q_\ell P_k = \frac{h}{2\pi i}\,\delta_{k\ell}\,I \tag{CCR}$$

on a dense invariant domain [1]. Already in the 1930s it was known that this relation *cannot* hold for bounded operators everywhere defined, and worse, that there exist pathological "representations" of the unbounded relation — for instance, ones in which the exponentiated groups fail to satisfy the Weyl relation at all [1][6].

> **Theorem (Wielandt–Wintner):** There are no bounded operators $A, B$ on any Banach space satisfying $AB - BA = I$. Hence any genuine representation of the CCR must involve unbounded operators, and the unbounded relation alone does not determine the representation up to unitary equivalence.

### The Weyl relations and the Schrödinger representation

Weyl's insight was to exponentiate. Given self-adjoint $P, Q$ satisfying the CCR, define the strongly continuous unitary one-parameter groups

$$U(t) = e^{itP}, \qquad V(s) = e^{isQ}.$$

The Baker–Campbell–Hausdorff formula applied on the common domain yields the **Weyl relations**

$$U(t)\,V(s) = e^{its}\,V(s)\,U(t), \qquad t, s \in \mathbb{R}, \tag{W}$$

which make sense for *all* unitary representations, with no domain subtleties [1][4].

The canonical solution is the **Schrödinger representation** $\pi_S$ on $\mathcal{H} = L^2(\mathbb{R})$:

$$(U(t)\psi)(x) = \psi(x + t), \qquad (V(s)\psi)(x) = e^{isx}\,\psi(x).$$

Here $U$ is translation and $V$ is multiplication by the character $e^{isx}$; a direct computation verifies (W). Its generators are $P = -i\,d/dx$ and $Q = x$, recovering the CCR on Schwartz functions. Mackey's generalization replaces $\mathbb{R}$ by any locally compact abelian group $L$: translations act by $(T_x f)(u) = f(u - x)$ and modulations by $(M_\chi f)(u) = e^{2\pi i\,\chi(u)} f(u)$ for characters $\chi$ in the dual group $\widehat{L}$ [2][4].

### The Heisenberg group

The operators $e^{it}U(a)V(b)$ close under multiplication to form the **Heisenberg group** $H_{2n+1}$, the group of $(n+2)\times(n+2)$ upper-triangular matrices

$$M(a, b, c) = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & a & c \\ 0 & I_n & b \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix},$$

with group law $M(a,b,c)\,M(a',b',c') = M(a+a',\,b+b',\,c+c'+a\cdot b')$. The center consists of the matrices $M(0,0,c)$, and the Lie algebra generators satisfy $[P, Q] = z$ with $z$ central — the integrated form of the CCR [4][3].

## 3 Methodology

The proof strategy we present is Mackey's: reduce the intertwining problem to a statement about systems of imprimitivity, then exploit transitivity. The argument proceeds in five stages.

> **Theorem (Stone–von Neumann):** Let $(U, V)$ be a strongly continuous pair of unitary representations of $\mathbb{R}^d$ on a Hilbert space $\mathcal{H}$ satisfying the Weyl relation $U(a)V(b) = e^{ia\cdot b}V(b)U(a)$ for all $a, b \in \mathbb{R}^d$. Then $\mathcal{H}$ decomposes as an orthogonal direct sum $\mathcal{H} = \bigoplus_\alpha \mathcal{H}_\alpha$ of closed subspaces, each reducing for $(U,V)$, such that every $(U,V)|_{\mathcal{H}_\alpha}$ is unitarily equivalent to the Schrödinger representation $\pi_S$. In particular, there is exactly one irreducible solution up to unitary equivalence. [2][3][4]

The methodology is the following:

1. **Spectral resolution.** Since $V$ is a unitary representation of the abelian group $\mathbb{R}^d$, the spectral theorem for commuting unitary operators yields a *projection-valued measure* (PVM) $P$ on the Borel sets of $\mathbb{R}^d$ with $V(b) = \int e^{ib\cdot \xi}\,dP(\xi)$.
2. **Covariance.** Conjugating the spectral resolution by $U(a)$ and using (W) gives the imprimitivity relation $U(a)\,P(E)\,U(a)^{-1} = P(E + a)$: the pair $(U, P)$ is a *system of imprimitivity* for the translation action of $\mathbb{R}^d$ on itself [5][7].
3. **Transitivity forces multiplicity.** The action of translations on $\mathbb{R}^d$ is transitive. A theorem of Mackey on systems of imprimitivity over transitive $G$-spaces implies that the representation is induced from the stabilizer — here trivial — so $(U, P)$ is equivalent to the *regular* system of imprimitivity: $U$ as translation on $L^2$ of the orbit space, $P$ as multiplication by indicator functions [7][2].
4. **Reconstruction of $V$.** Once $(U,P)$ is identified with the regular system, the operator $V(b)$, which commutes with $P$ in the right way and satisfies the covariance law, is forced to be exactly the modulation operator $M_b$. Hence $(U, V) \cong \pi_S$ [7].
5. **Multiplicity.** If the system is reducible, the same argument applied to each primary component gives a direct sum of Schrödinger copies — the spectral multiplicity may be infinite, but each summand is canonical.

The crucial conceptual move — step 2 — is Mackey's: the unbounded commutator problem is replaced by the covariance of a projection-valued measure, a statement purely about the *bounded* operator algebra generated by the Weyl pair.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Weyl Relations as a Projective Representation

Let $Z = \mathbb{R}^{2n}$ be classical phase space with the standard symplectic form $\omega(z, w) = x\cdot y' - y\cdot x'$ for $z = (x,y)$, $w = (x',y')$. Define the **Weyl operators**

$$W(z) = e^{\frac{i}{2}x\cdot y}\,U(x)\,V(y).$$

A short computation from (W) gives the projective composition law

$$W(z)\,W(w) = e^{\frac{i}{2}\omega(z,w)}\,W(z+w).$$

Thus a Weyl pair is a *projective* unitary representation of the symplectic vector space $Z$ with multiplier $\sigma(z,w) = e^{\frac{i}{2}\omega(z,w)}$, or equivalently an ordinary unitary representation of the central extension of $Z$ by the circle group — which is precisely the Heisenberg group [3][1].

### 4.2 Systems of Imprimitivity

Mackey's fundamental definition abstracts the pair $(U, P)$ of §3. Let $G$ be a locally compact second countable group acting continuously on a standard Borel space $X$.

> **Definition:** A **system of imprimitivity** based on $(G, X)$ is a pair $(U, \pi)$ where $U: G \to \mathcal{U}(\mathcal{H})$ is a strongly continuous unitary representation on a separable Hilbert space $\mathcal{H}$ and $\pi$ is a projection-valued measure on the Borel sets of $X$, satisfying the covariance relation $U_g\,\pi(A)\,U_g^{-1} = \pi(g\cdot A)$ for all $g \in G$ and Borel $A \subseteq X$. [5]

The paradigmatic example is the *Koopman system*: for an invariant measure $\mu$ on $X$, take $\pi(A)$ as multiplication by the indicator of $A$ on $L^2_\mu(X)$ and $U_g$ as the left-regular action. Frobenius's classical induction theory is the special case where $X = G/H$ is a homogeneous space: systems of imprimitivity over $G/H$ are exactly representations *induced* from $H$ [7][6].

### 4.3 Mackey's Proof via Imprimitivity

Mackey's 1949 proof — reconstructed from the exposition in [7] — is the heart of the matter. Start with an arbitrary Weyl pair $(U, V)$ on $\mathcal{H}$.

The spectral theorem for the commuting family $\{V(b)\}$ yields the PVM $P$ on the dual group $\widehat{\mathbb{R}^d} \cong \mathbb{R}^d$. The Weyl relation implies

$$U(a)\,V(b)\,U(a)^{-1} = e^{ia\cdot b}\,V(b),$$

and conjugating the spectral integral gives $U(a)\,P(E)\,U(a)^{-1} = P(E + a)$: the pair $(U, P)$ is a system of imprimitivity for translations acting on the dual.

Now comes Mackey's imprimitivity theorem for the *transitive* case: a system of imprimitivity over a transitive $G$-space $X \cong G/H$ is unitarily equivalent to a representation induced from $H$. Here $H$ is the trivial subgroup, so the induced representation is the left-regular representation twisted by the (trivial) representation of $H$ — that is, $(U, P)$ is a multiple of the regular system $(\lambda, \pi_{\mathrm{reg}})$ where $\lambda$ is translation on $L^2(\mathbb{R}^d)$ and $\pi_{\mathrm{reg}}$ is multiplication by indicators.

Finally, the operator $V(b)$ is characterized within this picture: it commutes with $\pi_{\mathrm{reg}}$, hence is a multiplication operator, and the Weyl relation with $U$ pins it down as multiplication by the character $b \mapsto e^{ib\cdot x}$ — the modulation operator $M_b$ of §2. Therefore $(U, V)$ is a direct sum of copies of $\pi_S$, and if irreducible, exactly one copy [7][2].

### 4.4 The Stone–von Neumann–Mackey Generalization

Mackey observed in 1949 that nothing in the argument used special properties of $\mathbb{R}$ beyond local compactness and abelianity. For any locally compact abelian group $L$, the **Heisenberg group** $H(L) = \{\,e^{2\pi i t}\,T_x M_\chi\,\}$ of translation and modulation operators on $L^2(L)$ satisfies the same theorem: $L^2(L)$ is irreducible under $H(L)$, and every representation with the identity central character decomposes into copies of the canonical one [2][3].

The resulting statement is sometimes called the **Stone–von Neumann–Mackey theorem** [3]. Prasad gave an elementary proof using the Peter–Weyl theorem and Fourier analysis on finite-dimensional real vector spaces [2], while Bekka extended the framework to *rings*: for a locally compact ring $R$ and a faithful unitary character $\lambda$ of its additive group, pairs $(U, V)$ satisfying $U(a)V(b) = \lambda(a\cdot b)\,V(b)U(a)$ are approximately equivalent after infinite inflation [1].

### 4.5 From Uniqueness to the Mackey Machine

The imprimitivity argument of §4.3 is the *prototype* of the Mackey machine. The machine addresses a vastly more general problem: given an extension $1 \to N \to G \to K \to 1$ with $N$ abelian (or type I), describe the unitary dual $\widehat{G}$ in terms of $\widehat{N}$ and the stabilizers of the $K$-action on it [6]. The procedure is:

- **Orbits:** decompose $\widehat{N}$ into $K$-orbits; for each orbit choose a representative $\pi$ with stabilizer $G_\pi$.
- **Projective extension:** extend $\pi$ to a projective representation of $G_\pi$ with multiplier $\omega_\pi$.
- **Induction:** representations of $G$ over that orbit are exactly $\operatorname{Ind}_{G_\pi}^{G}(\rho \otimes U)$, where $\rho$ runs over irreducible $\bar{\omega}_\pi$-projective representations of $G_\pi/N$ and $U$ is the extension [6].

The Stone–von Neumann theorem is the degenerate, luminous case: $G = H_{2n+1}$ itself, the orbit structure on the center's dual trivial, and the machine returns a single irreducible representation per central character. The same pattern — *spectral measure, covariance, transitivity, induction* — classifies the representations of semidirect products, underlies Wigner's description of elementary particles as irreducible representations of the Poincaré group, and specializes to the Kirillov orbit method for nilpotent Lie groups [6][7].

## 5 Empirical Results: The Irreducibility Calculation

The computational core of the theorem is the irreducibility of the Schrödinger representation. The classical proof uses the *double commutant*: we show that the commutant of the Weyl operators is trivial, so the von Neumann algebra they generate is all of $\mathcal{B}(L^2(\mathbb{R}))$.

> **Proposition:** The Schrödinger representation $\pi_S$ is irreducible. Any bounded operator $T$ commuting with all $U(t)$ and $V(s)$ is a scalar multiple of the identity.

*Proof sketch.* If $T$ commutes with every translation $U(t)$, then $T$ commutes with the convolution algebra $L^1(\mathbb{R})$ acting by translation; by standard harmonic analysis $T$ is a *multiplication operator*, $T\psi(x) = m(x)\psi(x)$ for some $m \in L^\infty(\mathbb{R})$. The Fourier transform exchanges translations and modulations, so $FTF^{-1}$ commutes with all multiplications and is itself a multiplication operator; a function that is a multiplier in both the position and momentum pictures is constant almost everywhere. Hence $T = cI$. ∎

The following Python code verifies the finite-dimensional shadow of this structure: the commutator of Heisenberg matrices always lands in the center, mirroring $[P, Q] = i\hbar I$.

```python
import numpy as np

def M(a, b, c):
    """Element of the 3-dim Heisenberg group as upper-triangular matrix."""
    return np.array([[1.0, a, c],
                     [0.0, 1.0, b],
                     [0.0, 0.0, 1.0]])

def group_commutator(g, h):
    """g h g^{-1} h^{-1}: always central for the Heisenberg group."""
    return g @ h @ np.linalg.inv(g) @ np.linalg.inv(h)

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
for _ in range(5):
    g, h = M(*rng.normal(size=3)), M(*rng.normal(size=3))
    k = group_commutator(g, h)
    # off-diagonal entries vanish: k = M(0, 0, c) in the center
    assert np.allclose(k[0, 1], 0) and np.allclose(k[1, 2], 0)
    print(f"commutator central entry c = {k[0, 2]:+.4f}")
```

The five stages of the full uniqueness argument, with their inputs and outputs, are:

| Stage | Input | Output |
|---|---|---|
| Spectral theorem | Unitary group $V$ | PVM $P$ on the dual group |
| Covariance computation | Weyl relation (W) | Imprimitivity $(U, P)$ |
| Mackey transitive case | Transitive action on dual | $(U,P) \cong$ multiple of regular system |
| Commutant analysis | $V$ commuting with $P$ | $V(b) = M_b$ modulation |
| Multiplicity count | Irreducibility hypothesis | Single Schrödinger copy |

A Haskell encoding captures the algebraic content — the Weyl pair as an interface, with the Schrödinger representation as its canonical instance:

```haskell
-- A Weyl pair: two unitary one-parameter groups obeying the Weyl relation
class WeylPair u v where
  weylRelation :: Double -> Double -> (u, v) -> Bool
  weylRelation t s (u, v) = compose u v == phase (t * s) <> compose v u
    where phase theta = exp (0 :+ theta)  -- the scalar e^{its}

-- The Schrödinger representation is the distinguished instance:
-- translations for U, modulations for V, acting on L^2 functions
data Schrodinger = Schrodinger

instance WeylPair Translation Modulation where
  -- uniqueness: every other instance is unitarily equivalent to this one
  weylRelation t s _ = True  -- Stone-von Neumann guarantees the iso
```

Finally, a Rust-flavored statement of the double-commutant irreducibility step treats operators as a trait with a commutant computation:

```rust
/// Bounded operators with a commutant; Schur's lemma in trait form.
trait OperatorAlgebra {
    fn commutes_with(&self, other: &Self) -> bool;
}

/// The von Neumann algebra generated by a Weyl pair is B(H):
/// its commutant is the scalars, hence the representation is irreducible.
fn schur_for_weyl_pair<A: OperatorAlgebra>(pair: &(A, A)) -> bool {
    let (u, v) = pair;
    // every operator commuting with both u and v is scalar
    u.commutes_with(v) && is_scalar_commutant(u, v)
}
```

## 6 Limitations

Uniqueness is fragile; it depends on hypotheses that fail in the settings physicists care about most.

**Infinitely many degrees of freedom.** The theorem concerns finitely many generators. For infinitely many CCR pairs — the situation of quantum field theory — uniqueness fails catastrophically: there exist uncountably many unitarily inequivalent irreducible representations of the infinite Weyl algebra. This is the representation-theoretic root of **Haag's theorem**, which states that the interaction picture of QFT does not exist: the free and interacting representations of the field algebra are inequivalent, so no unitary operator can intertwine them [3][6]. Quantization of fields is therefore *not* canonical in the way quantization of particles is.

**Integrability.** The theorem concerns the *Weyl* (exponentiated) relations, not the raw unbounded CCR. There exist representations of the unbounded relation whose exponentiated unitaries do not satisfy (W) — non-integrable representations — and these are wild and unclassifiable [1]. The Weyl form is doing essential work.

**Separability and standard Borel structure.** Mackey's imprimitivity argument leans on separability of the Hilbert space and standard Borel structures on the group and the space. Without these, the disintegration of measures and the measurable selection of intertwining operators break down, and non-transitive actions produce *ergodic* phenomena: systems of imprimitivity over non-transitive $G$-spaces are classified not by induction but by ergodic actions and their cocycles, a theory Mackey developed into the study of virtual groups [7].

**Beyond type I.** The Mackey machine classifies representations cleanly only for type I groups, where irreducible representations are well-behaved points of a standard Borel dual. For non-type-I groups, the dual is pathological and uniqueness statements of Stone–von Neumann type cannot even be formulated [6].

**Geometric quantization.** The theorem says the symplectic vector space $\mathbb{R}^{2n}$ has a unique quantization — but there is no functorial quantization of general symplectic manifolds. Geometric quantization must choose a *polarization*, and different polarizations give different Hilbert spaces; the Stone–von Neumann theorem is the flat case where the choice is essentially canonical [8].

## 7 Conclusion

The Stone–von Neumann theorem stands at the junction of physics and representation theory. As physics, it guarantees that canonical quantization is *canonical*: Schrödinger's wave mechanics is not one choice among many but, up to unitary equivalence, the only irreducible realization of the commutation relations. As mathematics, it classifies the irreducible unitary representations of the Heisenberg group with fixed central character — and, through Mackey's eyes, is the founding example of systems of imprimitivity and induced representations.

Mackey's machine turned that example into an industry: the same pattern — *spectral measure, covariance, transitivity, induction* — classifies the representations of semidirect products, underlies Wigner's description of elementary particles as irreducible representations of the Poincaré group, and specializes to the Kirillov orbit method for nilpotent Lie groups. Where the machine stalls — infinitely many degrees of freedom, non-type-I groups, ergodic actions — it marks precisely the frontiers where quantization ceases to be unique and the real difficulties of quantum field theory begin. A century after Heisenberg's matrices, the question "in how many ways can the commutation relations be represented?" remains one of the sharpest probes of the boundary between the tame and the wild in mathematical physics.
