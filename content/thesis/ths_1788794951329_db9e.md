# Flink Stateful Stream Processing: Exactly-Once Semantics, Chandy–Lamport Snapshots, and Watermark Mechanics

## Abstract

Apache Flink is the reference architecture for stateful stream processing at scale, underpinning fraud detection, session analytics, and real-time machine learning. This thesis dissects the three pillars of its correctness model: (i) the streaming execution model that unifies batch and stream under one dataflow runtime; (ii) *Asynchronous Barrier Snapshotting*, the Chandy–Lamport-derived distributed snapshot protocol that captures consistent global state without halting computation; and (iii) the watermark mechanism that brings order and completeness reasoning to unbounded, out-of-order event streams. We compare state backends — heap versus the RocksDB LSM-tree backend — and analyze the two-phase-commit sink protocol that extends exactly-once state semantics to end-to-end exactly-once delivery. Formal treatments of barrier alignment, watermark propagation, and recovery are developed alongside TLA+ and Rust models, empirical evidence from the foundational literature, and an honest discussion of limitations: alignment-induced latency, watermark stalls, and the impossibility of true exactly-once output without transactional sinks.

## 1 Introduction

Modern analytics increasingly operates on *unbounded* data: clickstreams, telemetry, market ticks, and sensor feeds that never terminate and frequently arrive out of order. Batch engines demand the entire dataset before producing an answer; streaming engines must produce useful results *while* data is still arriving, and do so correctly even when machines fail. This — continuous, stateful computation with strong processing guarantees — is the central engineering problem of the field [2][4].

*Apache Flink* emerged from the Stratosphere research project as a distributed dataflow engine that treats batch as a special case of streaming [3]. A job is a directed graph of operators; records flow along the edges; each operator maintains partitioned, fault-tolerant state; and global state is periodically captured by a lightweight distributed snapshot protocol. From these primitives flow Flink's headline guarantees — *exactly-once state semantics*, high-throughput *event-time* processing, and fast recovery.

Yet each guarantee rests on nontrivial distributed-systems machinery whose assumptions are poorly understood by many practitioners. Exactly-once does *not* mean each record is physically processed once; it means the *observable effect* is equivalent to single processing [1]. Watermarks do not guarantee that all events below a timestamp have arrived; they assert a *progress contract* that downstream operators rely upon to emit results [4][5]. Checkpoint barriers do not pause the world; they superimpose a consistent cut on a computation that keeps running, exactly as Chandy and Lamport prescribed in 1985 [1][2].

> **Thesis statement:** Flink's correctness is a *layered contract*: Chandy–Lamport barrier snapshots make operator state exactly-once recoverable; watermark protocols make event-time progress decidable on disordered streams; and two-phase-commit sinks extend the guarantee across system boundaries — each layer precise in what it promises, and each with sharp, documented limits.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The Chandy–Lamport distributed snapshot

In 1985, Chandy and Lamport asked how one can record a *consistent* global state of a distributed system while it keeps running [1]. Their answer introduced the marker-sending rule: the snapshot initiator records its local state and sends a *marker* along every outgoing channel. Upon first receiving a marker, a process records its own state, marks that channel as empty of in-transit messages, and records messages on all *other* incoming channels until a marker arrives on each. FIFO channel order guarantees the recorded channel state contains exactly the messages sent before the sender's snapshot and received after the receiver's — a *consistent cut*.

> **Theorem: (Consistency of Chandy–Lamport cuts).** Under FIFO channels with the marker rule, the recorded global state is a consistent cut: for every message recorded as received by process *q*, it is recorded as sent by its sender *p*. Recovery to it introduces neither lost nor phantom messages [1].

Flink's checkpointing is a direct specialization of this algorithm to dataflow graphs, where markers are called *barriers* and flow *with* the data stream rather than out-of-band [2]. The key adaptation: on acyclic graphs, *barrier alignment* eliminates channel-state recording entirely — in-flight records are re-processed from replayable sources after recovery. This is the core optimization of the Asynchronous Barrier Snapshotting (ABS) algorithm [2].

### 2.2 The Dataflow model: time domains and watermarks

Akidau et al.'s Dataflow model [4] provides the semantic vocabulary for Flink's temporal machinery. Three time domains are distinguished:

| Time domain | Definition | Properties |
|---|---|---|
| *Event time* | The instant an event occurred at its source | Immutable; may arrive out of order |
| *Processing time* | The instant the event is observed by the system | Always available; nondeterministic across runs |
| *Ingestion time* | The instant the event enters the system boundary | Deterministic assignment; inherits arrival disorder |

For *unbounded* collections, the central difficulty is *completeness*: when has an operator seen "enough"? *Watermarks* answer: *W(t)* declares event time has reached *t* — no future event should bear *t′ ≤ t* [4]. Begoli et al. give the definitive comparative semantics of watermarks in Flink and Cloud Dataflow, analyzing generation, propagation, and consumption as the three pillars of a correct implementation [5].

### 2.3 State, backends, and the LSM substrate

Stateful operators maintain per-key aggregates, window buffers, and join state that can reach terabytes. Flink abstracts physical storage behind the *state backend* interface. Two production backends dominate:

- **HashMapStateBackend**: state as deserialized Java objects on the JVM heap. O(1) access with minimal serialization overhead, but bounded by heap memory, and full snapshots serialize the entire heap.
- **EmbeddedRocksDBStateBackend**: state in embedded RocksDB on local disk. RocksDB is a log-structured merge-tree (LSM-tree) engine: writes land in an in-memory memtable, flush to immutable sorted string tables (SSTables), and are periodically *compacted* across levels [7]. Out-of-place writes give superior write throughput and unbounded state size at the cost of read amplification and per-access serialization.

The LSM-tree's append-only nature enables *incremental* checkpoints: only SSTables created since the last checkpoint are uploaded to durable storage [2][7].

### 2.4 Lineage of streaming fault tolerance

Flink's design responds to its predecessors' limitations. MillWheel [6] achieved exactly-once through per-record acknowledgments against a durable log — precise but write-amplified. Spark Streaming's discretized streams [9] checkpointed *all* state between micro-batches, stalling the pipeline and eagerly persisting in-flight data — the two drawbacks Carbone et al. explicitly target [2].

## 3 Methodology

Our analysis proceeds in four stages.

**Stage 1 — Formal modeling.** We model the execution graph as a DAG of *tasks* connected by *channels*. Each task *v* maintains local state *σᵥ* with a deterministic transition *δ* mapping (state, input record) to (new state, outputs). A *checkpoint* is a consistent cut: a vector of per-task states such that no record processed *after* the cut on any task was processed *before* the cut downstream — the Chandy–Lamport condition in dataflow language [1].

**Stage 2 — Protocol reconstruction.** From the ABS paper [2] and Flink's documentation [8], we reconstruct the barrier protocol as a per-task state machine and verify its invariants in TLA+.

**Stage 3 — Mechanism comparison.** We compare state backends by write/read amplification under the LSM cost model [7], and sink protocols (idempotent upsert versus two-phase commit) by failure atomicity.

**Stage 4 — Empirical grounding.** We reproduce the headline claims of the ABS paper [2] and the watermark latency/completeness tradeoffs of [5].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Barrier alignment: Chandy–Lamport adapted to streaming DAGs

The checkpoint *coordinator* periodically injects a *checkpoint barrier* *bₙ* into every source task. Sources snapshot their offsets and broadcast the barrier downstream. The critical question is what a *multi-input* operator does when *bₙ* arrives on one input but not yet on another. Flink supports two designs via its checkpointing mode:

- **Aligned checkpoints (EXACTLY_ONCE).** The task *blocks* the channel on which the barrier arrived, buffering its records, until *bₙ* arrives on *all* inputs. Only then does it snapshot and forward the barrier. This is the Chandy–Lamport marker rule with empty channel state: records arriving on blocked channels after the barrier are *post-snapshot* on the sender, so no in-flight records enter the snapshot [2].
- **Unaligned checkpoints (AT_LEAST_ONCE).** The task snapshots on the *first* barrier arrival, recording buffered in-flight records of not-yet-aligned channels as part of the checkpoint. Alignment delay disappears at the cost of larger snapshots and weaker guarantees.

> **Theorem: (Alignment yields consistent cuts on DAGs).** On an acyclic execution graph with aligned barriers, the per-task snapshots triggered by barrier *bₙ* form a consistent cut. *Proof sketch.* Any record *r* sent from *u* to *v* after *u*'s snapshot follows barrier *bₙ* on channel *u→v* (FIFO order). Task *v* snapshots only after *bₙ* arrives on *every* input — hence after *r*'s stream position — so *r* is processed by *v* strictly after *v*'s snapshot. No post-cut send is pre-cut received. ∎ [1][2]

For *cyclic* graphs (iterations), ABS additionally logs records on *back-edges* crossing the cut as channel state, recovering the full Chandy–Lamport construction [2]. The alignment state machine:

```tla
------------------------------ MODULE BarrierAlignment ------------------------------
EXTENDS Naturals, FiniteSets
CONSTANTS Tasks, Inputs   \* Inputs[t] = input channels of task t
VARIABLES seen, snapshotted

Init == /\ seen = [t \in Tasks |-> {}]
        /\ snapshotted = [t \in Tasks |-> FALSE]

BarrierArrives(t, ch) ==
    /\ ~snapshotted[t]
    /\ seen' = [seen EXCEPT ![t] = @ \cup {ch}]
    /\ IF seen'[t] = Inputs[t]
       THEN snapshotted' = [snapshotted EXCEPT ![t] = TRUE]
       ELSE UNCHANGED snapshotted

\* Safety: no task snapshots before the barrier arrived on every input
AlignmentInvariant == \A t \in Tasks : snapshotted[t] => (seen[t] = Inputs[t])
=============================================================================
```

### 4.2 State backends: heap versus RocksDB, and the cost of durability

Keyed state is partitioned into *key groups* — disjoint subsets of the key space, at most `maxParallelism` (default 128) — the unit of redistribution on rescale [8]. The backend determines how a subtask's key-group state is stored and snapshotted:

| Dimension | HashMapStateBackend | EmbeddedRocksDBStateBackend |
|---|---|---|
| Storage | JVM heap (objects) | Local disk, RocksDB (LSM-tree) |
| Max state | Bounded by heap (GBs) | Bounded by disk (TBs) |
| Read path | Pointer dereference | JNI call + deserialization |
| Checkpoint | Full snapshot (serialize all) | Full or *incremental* (new SSTables only) |
| Latency | Lowest per-record | Higher per-record, smoother tail at scale |

The RocksDB backend's power comes from the LSM-tree's *out-of-place* discipline [7]: writes are sequential (WAL append, memtable insert); reads may consult the memtable, several SSTable levels, and Bloom filters (*read amplification*); compactions merge levels in the background (*write amplification*). Streaming state is overwhelmingly *write-heavy*, so the LSM tradeoff is nearly ideal. Incremental checkpoints exploit SSTable immutability: checkpoint *bₙ* uploads only SSTables created since *bₙ₋₁*, with the JobManager retaining a reference-counted manifest [2][8].

```rust
// Incremental checkpoints: only SSTables created after the previous
// barrier are uploaded. A flushed SSTable never changes, so its id
// is a pure, time-stable name.
fn incremental_files(all: &[Sstable], prev_max_id: u64) -> Vec<&Sstable> {
    all.iter()
        .filter(|s| s.id > prev_max_id && s.immutable)
        .collect()
}
```

### 4.3 End-to-end exactly-once: from state semantics to sink transactions

Checkpointing gives *exactly-once state*: after recovery, each operator's state reflects each record's effect exactly once. But outputs already emitted before the failure cannot be un-sent. End-to-end exactly-once requires the sink's cooperation [2][8]:

1. **Idempotent sinks.** If writing the same record twice equals writing it once (keyed upserts, deterministic overwrite naming), at-least-once delivery degrades gracefully to *effectively-once* observable state.
2. **Two-phase-commit (2PC) sinks.** The sink joins the checkpoint protocol as a transactional resource: `beginTransaction()` opens a transaction (e.g., a Kafka producer transaction with a fencing `transactional.id`); records between barriers are written *into the open transaction*, invisible to consumers; on barrier arrival, `preCommit()` flushes without making it visible; when the JobManager reports the checkpoint *completed globally*, `commit()` makes it visible atomically. On failure, uncommitted transactions abort during recovery, and re-processed records open a *new* transaction with a fenced producer epoch, so the zombie transaction can never commit.

> **Theorem: (2PC sink correctness).** If every sink transaction commits iff its checkpoint completes globally, the visible outputs equal the outputs of exactly one successful execution of each record. *Proof sketch.* A committed transaction's records were processed pre-barrier in a completed cut; recovery replays from the cut, so nothing committed is lost. An aborted transaction belongs to an incomplete checkpoint; its records are replayed into a new fenced transaction, and fencing guarantees the old one never becomes visible. Each record's effect is visible exactly once. ∎ [2]

The Kafka sink implements this via the transactional producer: `transactional.id` fencing plus epoch bumps invalidate any in-flight transaction of a failed predecessor. This is the only configuration where `DeliveryGuarantee.EXACTLY_ONCE` is honest [8].

### 4.4 Watermarks: reasoning about completeness on disordered streams

Watermarks solve *completeness*: on an infinite stream, when is it safe to close a window or garbage-collect state? A watermark *W(t)* asserts the event-time clock has reached *t* — no future element should bear *t′ ≤ t* [4]. Three sub-mechanisms compose the implementation [5]:

- **Generation.** Sources or `WatermarkStrategy` emit watermarks, typically *periodic* and computed as `maxObservedEventTime − boundedOutOfOrderness` — a tunable tradeoff of latency against disorder tolerance.
- **Propagation.** Watermarks flow as stream elements. A multi-input task advances its event-time clock to the *minimum* of its inputs' watermarks and emits that minimum downstream:

```haskell
-- The event-time clock is the pointwise minimum of input watermarks.
propagate :: [Timestamp] -> Timestamp
propagate = minimum
-- Consequence ("slowest input wins"): one stalled partition pins the
-- watermark of the entire downstream subgraph. Idleness detection
-- exists precisely to relax this rule for quiescent sources.
```

- **Consumption.** Event-time windows fire when the watermark passes the window end; sub-watermark state may be garbage-collected; *allowed lateness* keeps windows alive past their trigger, routing tardy elements to *side outputs* [4][5].

Flink's answer is *idleness detection* (`withIdleness`): a source seeing no records for a configured timeout is excluded from the minimum until it resumes [5][8]. The timeout is itself a tradeoff: too short misclassifies slow partitions; too long stalls the pipeline.

*Processing-time* semantics sidestep watermarks — windows fire on wall-clock — gaining minimal latency at the cost of *nondeterminism*: replays produce different results [4].

```python
# Periodic watermark generation with bounded out-of-orderness.
class BoundedOutOfOrdernessGenerator:
    def __init__(self, max_out_of_orderness_ms: int):
        self.max_ooo = max_out_of_orderness_ms
        self.max_ts = float("-inf")

    def on_event(self, event_ts: int) -> None:
        self.max_ts = max(self.max_ts, event_ts)

    def on_periodic_emit(self) -> int:
        # W(t): assert no future event bears timestamp <= t
        return self.max_ts - self.max_ooo - 1
```

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 What the ABS paper demonstrates

Carbone et al. evaluate ABS on up to 40 nodes [2]. Three results matter:

1. **Snapshot overhead is small and flat.** With 500 ms checkpoints, throughput degradation versus no checkpointing is in the low single-digit percentages and does not grow with cluster size — *linear scalability*, because barriers flow with the data and each task snapshots independently.
2. **Space efficiency.** On acyclic topologies ABS persists *only* operator state, yielding snapshots an order of magnitude smaller than approaches that eagerly log the stream.
3. **Frequent snapshots are affordable.** Flat overhead means checkpointing every few seconds, bounding post-failure reprocessing to seconds of stream time — the quantitative basis of Flink's fast recovery.

### 5.2 Proof: recovery yields exactly-once state

> **Theorem: (Exactly-once state recovery).** Assume (a) sources are *replayable* from any checkpointed offset, (b) tasks snapshot on aligned barriers forming consistent cuts, (c) tasks are deterministic functions of (state, input sequence). Then after any failures, the recovered execution is *observably equivalent* to a failure-free run processing each record exactly once. *Proof.* By §4.1, each completed checkpoint *cₙ* is a consistent cut. On failure, every task restores *σ(cₙ)* and sources rewind to checkpointed offsets. By (c), re-processing the suffix after *cₙ* reproduces exactly the pre-failure transitions on that suffix — no pre-cut record is re-processed (offsets exclude it), none post-cut is skipped (replay covers it). Induction over failures gives equivalence to a failure-free run. ∎ [1][2]

The load-bearing assumptions: replayable sources (Kafka qualifies; a socket stream does not), determinism (processing-time windows void the warranty), and *completed* checkpoints (in-flight checkpoints at failure time are discarded).

### 5.3 Watermark completeness: what is actually guaranteed

Begoli et al. [5] formalize the watermark contract as a *liveness* property: *W(t)* does not *prove* no straggler with *t′ ≤ t* exists — it *declares* the system's commitment to act as if none will arrive, relegating violators to the late-data path. The empirical upshot: *watermark lag* — max observed event time minus current watermark — is the best health metric for an event-time pipeline, and its production distribution is heavy-tailed, dominated by the slowest partition [5].

### 5.4 A checkpoint cost model

Let *S* be total state, *B* upload bandwidth, *δ* the incremental fraction of new SSTables, and *τ* the checkpoint interval. Per-checkpoint upload cost ≈ *δS/B* (incremental) versus *S/B* (full). ABS makes *τ* small without throughput collapse; RocksDB makes *δ ≪ 1* for update-heavy workloads [2][7].

## 6 Limitations

**1. Alignment-induced latency and stragglers.** Aligned checkpoints couple the job's checkpoint duration to its slowest channel. Under skew — a hot key, a slow disk — barrier alignment serializes on the straggler and checkpoint durations balloon from seconds to minutes. Unaligned checkpoints [8] remove the coupling but reintroduce in-flight logging and forfeit exactly-once: the guarantee has a *price* measured in tail latency.

**2. Watermark stalls and the idleness heuristic.** Event-time progress is *weakest-link*: idleness detection uses a timeout that cannot distinguish dead partitions from slow ones — misclassification stalls the pipeline or silently misroutes late data [5].

**3. Exactly-once is not end-to-end by default.** Enabling checkpointing does *not* give exactly-once outputs. Without a transactional or idempotent sink, duplicates are emitted on every recovery — exactly-once *state* with at-least-once *effects* [8]. Worse, the 2PC protocol assumes the external system supports fencing and atomic commit; many do not, and the failure mode is silent duplication.

**4. Fragile determinism.** The recovery proof requires deterministic operators. Processing-time windows, wall-clock reads in user functions, and nondeterministic iteration orders void the warranty with no warning. Flink cannot statically verify determinism; it is a *discipline* the author must maintain.

**5. Workload-dependent backend tradeoffs.** RocksDB's read amplification punishes read-heavy state with JNI and deserialization overhead; the heap backend punishes large state with GC pauses and full-snapshot serialization storms. No backend dominates.

**6. Operations beyond the theory.** Checkpoints are for machines; *savepoints* are for humans — manual snapshots for upgrades and rescaling [8]. Rescaling redistributes key groups and schema evolution demands serializer discipline; the theory of consistent cuts says nothing about evolving a stateful job over years.

## 7 Conclusion

Flink's correctness architecture is a study in *layered contracts*. Chandy–Lamport barrier snapshots [1][2] solve *state* fault tolerance: aligned barriers superimpose consistent cuts on a running DAG without pausing it, and replayable sources plus deterministic operators turn those cuts into exactly-once recovery. Watermarks [4][5] solve *temporal* completeness: the *W(t)* contract lets operators decide, on disordered infinite streams, when a result is final. Two-phase-commit sinks solve the *boundary* problem: binding external visibility to checkpoint completion with producer fencing extends exactly-once from state to effects [2][8]. The LSM-tree substrate [7] makes it *affordable* at terabyte scale.

Open problems abound: eliminating the alignment tax without forfeiting exactly-once; replacing watermark idleness heuristics with principled partition-aware progress estimation; pushing recovery point objectives toward milliseconds via changelog techniques; and building static analyses that *verify* the determinism discipline Flink's recovery proof assumes.

For the practitioner: checkpoint every few seconds with RocksDB incremental snapshots; monitor watermark lag as the primary event-time health signal; never claim exactly-once outputs without a transactional or idempotent sink; and treat nondeterministic user functions as loaded guns aimed at your recovery guarantees.
