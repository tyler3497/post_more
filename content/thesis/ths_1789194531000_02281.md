# Compressed Sensing and Sparse Recovery: Restricted Isometry, ℓ₁ Minimization, Greedy Pursuits, and Message-Passing Algorithms

## Abstract

Compressed sensing is a mathematical framework for acquiring and reconstructing high-dimensional signals from a small number of linear measurements by exploiting the prior that the signal is sparse in a known basis. This thesis develops the theory from first principles: the geometry that makes ℓ₁ minimization a valid convex surrogate for the combinatorial ℓ₀ problem, the restricted isometry property and coherence as certificate conditions on the measurement matrix, and the measurement bounds m ≳ s log(N/s) achieved by sub-Gaussian and partial Fourier ensembles. We derive exact and stable recovery guarantees for basis pursuit, basis pursuit denoising, and the LASSO; analyze greedy pursuits including orthogonal matching pursuit, CoSaMP, and iterative hard thresholding; and present approximate message passing with its state-evolution formalism and sharp phase-transition predictions. Numerical experiments reproduce the Donoho–Tanner phase diagram and confirm that AMP attains the convex phase transition at O(mN) cost. Applications to accelerated magnetic resonance imaging and the single-pixel camera are examined, followed by a frank account of the theory's limitations and open problems.

## 1 Introduction

Classical sampling theory, epitomized by Shannon and Nyquist, teaches that a bandlimited signal must be sampled at twice its bandwidth to permit exact reconstruction. In practice, however, most signals of interest carry far less information than their ambient dimension suggests: natural images are compressible in wavelet bases, medical images are sparse under finite differences, and genomic data live near low-dimensional manifolds. Conventional acquisition therefore spends the majority of its sampling budget on coefficients that subsequent compression algorithms — JPEG, MPEG, MP3 — promptly discard. Compressed sensing asks whether one can instead acquire the signal *already compressed*, measuring it with a small number of non-adaptive linear functionals and reconstructing it by nonlinear optimization [1], [2].

The canonical setup is disarmingly simple. An unknown signal **x ∈ ℝ^N** is promised to be *s-sparse*: it has at most *s* nonzero entries, i.e. ‖x‖₀ ≤ s. A measurement matrix **A ∈ ℝ^{m×N}** with m ≪ N is applied, producing **y = Ax + e**, where **e** is noise. The reconstruction task is to recover x from y. Since the system is massively underdetermined, recovery is impossible in general; the sparsity prior is what makes it possible. The idealized decoder — minimize ‖z‖₀ subject to Az = y — searches over all 2^N support sets and is NP-hard [9]. The founding surprise of the field, established around 2004–2006 by Candès, Romberg, Tao, and Donoho, is that replacing ‖·‖₀ with the convex envelope ‖·‖₁ recovers *exactly* the sparse solution under verifiable conditions on A — and that such matrices are easy to construct by randomization [1], [2].

This thesis unifies the four pillars of the subject: the geometric intuition for ℓ₁ recovery, the certificate conditions of restricted isometry and coherence, convex reconstruction with ℓ₁ programs and their statistical cousins, and the two families of fast algorithms — greedy pursuits and message-passing methods — together with the sharp asymptotic phase transitions they exhibit.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Norms, sparsity, and compressibility

For x ∈ ℝ^N, the ℓ_p quasi-norms ‖x‖_p = (Σ|xᵢ|^p)^{1/p} measure size for p ≥ 1. The counting functional ‖x‖₀ = |{i : xᵢ ≠ 0}| is not a norm but the natural complexity measure of sparsity. Real signals are rarely exactly sparse; they are *compressible*: when the coefficients sorted by decreasing magnitude |x|_{(1)} ≥ |x|_{(2)} ≥ ⋯ decay like a power law |x|_{(k)} ≤ R k^{−1/q} with q < 1, the best s-term approximation error σ_s(x)_p = min_{‖z‖₀≤s} ‖x − z‖_p satisfies σ_s(x)_2 ≤ C s^{1/2−1/q} [1]. Any serious recovery theory must therefore handle not just exact s-sparse vectors but signals with controlled approximation error.

### 2.2 Why the ℓ₂ decoder fails

The minimum-energy decoder ẑ = A†y selects the feasible vector of smallest ℓ₂ norm. Because the ℓ₂ unit ball is round, its tangent point with the affine solution space {z : Az = y} is generically dense — the error spreads across all coordinates. The ℓ₁ ball, a cross-polytope with vertices on the coordinate axes, meets the same affine space at a sparse corner with overwhelming probability, provided the geometry of A is favorable.

### 2.3 Random matrices and concentration

A random matrix with independent sub-Gaussian rows acts as a near-isometry on any *fixed* low-dimensional subspace, by the Johnson–Lindenstrauss phenomenon. The difficulty is uniformity over all (N choose s) sparse subspaces simultaneously, which is handled by covering-number (entropy) arguments. Gaussian, Bernoulli (±1), and random partial Fourier matrices all satisfy the restricted isometry property once m exceeds a multiple of s log(N/s) [1], [7].

## 3 Methodology

We work throughout in the standard linear model **y = Ax + e** with A ∈ ℝ^{m×N} fixed and known, x unknown with ‖x‖₀ ≤ s or controlled compressibility, and ‖e‖₂ ≤ ε. A recovery algorithm R is a map y ↦ x̂. We assess algorithms along three axes:

1. **Measurement complexity**: how large must m be (as a function of s and N) for uniform recovery?
2. **Statistical guarantees**: exact recovery in the noiseless case; stable, *oracle-like* error bounds ‖x − x̂‖₂ ≤ C₀ε + C₁σ_s(x)_1/√s under noise.
3. **Computational cost**: polynomial versus near-linear; dependence on spectral operations.

The theory proceeds in two stages. First, a *certificate condition* on A is formulated — restricted isometry or a small coherence — that is checkable in principle. Second, random matrix ensembles are shown to satisfy the certificate with high probability at near-optimal m. Algorithms are then analyzed under the certificate.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Sparsity Models and the Geometry of Recovery

The support of x is the index set T = supp(x) with |T| = s. Write A_T for the m × s submatrix of the support columns. Recovery reduces to identifying T (combinatorial) followed by least squares on the support (trivial). The ℓ₁ program

minimize ‖z‖₁ subject to Az = y    (P₁)

known as *basis pursuit*, replaces the search over supports with a linear program solvable in polynomial time [9].

> **Theorem (Exact recovery via ℓ₁).** Let A satisfy the restricted isometry property of order 2s with δ_{2s} < √2 − 1 (or the earlier sufficient condition δ_{3s} + 3δ_{4s} < 2 of Candès and Tao). Then for every s-sparse x, the solution x̂ of (P₁) with y = Ax satisfies x̂ = x. [1]

The proof's key object is the *dual certificate*: a vector w = Aᵀv such that wᵢ = sign(xᵢ) on T and |wᵢ| < 1 off T. Its existence is equivalent to x being the unique ℓ₁ minimizer. RIP furnishes the certificate through a Neumann-series (golfing-scheme) construction; the geometry is that the null space of A must avoid the tangent cone of the ℓ₁ ball at x, a condition quantified exactly by the Gaussian width of the descent cone — the insight that later produced the phase-transition theory.

*Compressibility* extends this cleanly: if x is not exactly sparse, (P₁) and its noisy variants return an estimate whose error is bounded by a constant times the best s-term approximation error, so the decoder is *instance-optimal* up to constants [1].

### 4.2 Restricted Isometry and Coherence

> **Definition (RIP).** A matrix A satisfies the restricted isometry property of order s with constant δ_s ∈ (0,1) if
> (1 − δ_s)‖x‖₂² ≤ ‖Ax‖₂² ≤ (1 + δ_s)‖x‖₂²
> for every s-sparse x ∈ ℝ^N.

RIP states that every m × s submatrix of A is well conditioned: distances between sparse vectors are approximately preserved. Its strengths: it is *deterministic* (once it holds, everything else follows deterministically), and it implies stability under noise and model mismatch. Its weakness: certifying δ_s for a given A is itself NP-hard, so RIP is used to analyze random ensembles rather than concrete deterministic matrices.

The *coherence* of a column-normalized matrix,

μ(A) = max_{i≠j} |⟨aᵢ, aⱼ⟩|,

is a cheaper, computable certificate. The Welch bound gives μ ≥ √((N−m)/(m(N−1))), and exact recovery holds for s < (1 + 1/μ)/2 by a simple Gershgorin argument [9]. But coherence is fundamentally limited: deterministic constructions cannot push s beyond O(√m) because of this bound — the *square-root bottleneck*. Random matrices evade it entirely, reaching s = O(m/log(N/s)), because their coherence μ ≈ √(log N/m) is small *and* their near-orthogonality holds uniformly.

**Random ensembles with RIP** include:

- **Gaussian / sub-Gaussian:** entries i.i.d. N(0, 1/m); RIP holds with m ≥ C s log(N/s), constants explicit via chaining [1].
- **Bernoulli/Rademacher:** entries ±1/√m; same scaling, easier hardware implementation.
- **Partial Fourier:** m rows sampled uniformly from the N × N DFT; RIP with m = O(s log⁴ N) after the Rudelson–Vershynin improvement of the Candès–Tao bound [7].
- **Subsampled bounded orthonormal systems:** generalization to wavelets and other incoherent bases.

| Ensemble | Measurement bound | Structure | Fast multiply |
|---|---|---|---|
| Gaussian | m ≳ s log(N/s) | Dense | No |
| Bernoulli | m ≳ s log(N/s) | Sign matrix | No |
| Partial Fourier | m ≳ s log⁴ N | Subsampled DFT | Yes (FFT) |

The partial Fourier ensemble is crucial for applications such as MRI, where measurements are physically constrained to be Fourier samples [5], [7].

### 4.3 Convex Relaxation: Basis Pursuit and the LASSO

Noiseless exact recovery is only the beginning. Real measurements carry noise, and real signals are compressible rather than exactly sparse. The *basis pursuit denoising* (BPDN) program,

minimize ‖z‖₁ subject to ‖Az − y‖₂ ≤ ε,    (P₁,ε)

handles both. Candès, Romberg, and Tao proved that under RIP of order 2s, the BPDN solution satisfies

‖x − x̂‖₂ ≤ C₀ ε + C₁ σ_s(x)_1/√s,    (★)

so the error is the sum of the noise floor and the optimal approximation error — one cannot ask for more [1].

The *LASSO*, introduced by Tibshirani in the statistics literature [9],

minimize (1/2)‖y − Az‖₂² + λ‖z‖₁,

is the Lagrangian twin of BPDN, solvable at scale by proximal-gradient methods.

Computationally, (P₁) is a linear program and (P₁,ε) a second-order cone program, both solvable by interior-point methods in polynomial time. For large-scale problems, proximal-gradient methods dominate. The classical iteration is *iterative soft thresholding* (ISTA),

x^{t+1} = η_{λ/L}(x^t − (1/L)Aᵀ(Ax^t − y)),

where η_τ is the soft-thresholding operator and L ≥ λ_max(AᵀA). FISTA's momentum acceleration improves the convergence rate from O(1/t) to O(1/t²) on the objective.

### 4.4 Greedy Pursuits: OMP, CoSaMP, and Iterative Hard Thresholding

Convex programs are reliable but expensive: interior-point solvers scale poorly to N ~ 10⁶. *Greedy* algorithms attack the ℓ₀ problem directly, building the support iteratively.

**Orthogonal Matching Pursuit (OMP).** From residual r = y and empty support Λ, each iteration selects j* = argmax_j |⟨a_j, r⟩|, adds it to Λ, and re-solves least squares on A_Λ. Simple and fast, but its guarantees are coherence-based — s = O(√m) — unless strong signal conditions hold [9].

**CoSaMP** (Needell–Tropp) closes the gap between greedy and convex methods [3]. Each iteration:

1. *Identification:* form the proxy Aᵀr and take the 2s largest components.
2. *Support merger:* unite with the current support estimate.
3. *Estimation:* least squares on the merged (≤ 3s) support.
4. *Pruning:* keep the s largest entries.

> **Theorem (CoSaMP).** If δ_{4s} ≤ 0.1, then after O(log(‖x‖₂/ε)) iterations, CoSaMP returns x̂ with ‖x − x̂‖₂ ≤ C·(‖e‖₂ + σ_s(x)_1/√s), using only O(mN) operations per iteration. [3]

CoSaMP is the first greedy method with the full oracle inequality (★) and rigorous near-linear cost. Subspace Pursuit is a close sibling with similar guarantees.

**Iterative Hard Thresholding (IHT)** keeps a fixed cardinality by projecting onto the s-sparse set:

x^{t+1} = H_s(x^t + μAᵀ(y − Ax^t)),

where H_s keeps the s largest-magnitude entries. It converges linearly under RIP and costs one gradient step per iteration, and it reveals the natural bridge to *deep unfolding*: unrolling its iterations into a neural network (LISTA) learns thresholds and step sizes from data.

### 4.5 Approximate Message Passing and State Evolution

Iterative thresholding algorithms of the form x^{t+1} = η_t(Aᵀz^t + x^t), z^t = y − Ax^t — without any correction — underperform convex programming substantially: the residual z^t becomes correlated with A, so the "effective observation" Aᵀz^t + x^t is no longer signal plus independent noise [4]. Approximate message passing (AMP), introduced by Donoho, Maleki, and Montanari, adds a single scalar *Onsager correction* derived from belief propagation on a dense graphical model:

x^{t+1} = η_t(Aᵀz^t + x^t),
z^t = y − Ax^t + (1/δ) z^{t−1} ⟨η'_{t−1}(Aᵀz^{t−1} + x^{t−1})⟩,

where δ = m/N and ⟨·⟩ denotes the empirical average. The correction exactly cancels the correlation, restoring the Gaussian heuristic: Aᵀz^t + x^t ≈ x + τ_t Z with Z ∼ N(0, I) independent of x [4], [6]. The algorithm's mean-squared error is predicted by **state evolution**, a deterministic scalar recursion:

τ_{t+1}² = σ² + (1/δ) 𝔼[(η_t(X + τ_t Z) − X)²],   τ_0² = σ² + 𝔼X²/δ,

with X drawn from the signal prior and Z ∼ N(0,1) independent. State evolution was made rigorous for Gaussian A by Bayati and Montanari [6] and later extended to broader ensembles. It is the sharpest analytical instrument in the field: one evaluates a one-dimensional recursion instead of simulating a million-dimensional system.

For soft-thresholding denoisers η_t, the fixed points of state evolution predict a *phase transition*: there exists a curve ρ(δ) in the (δ = m/N, ρ = s/m) plane such that AMP succeeds with high probability below the curve and fails above it. Donoho, Maleki, and Montanari demonstrated empirically that this AMP transition coincides with the ℓ₁ phase transition computed by Donoho and Tanner's combinatorial geometry — so AMP achieves convex-program performance at the cost of a few matrix–vector products [4].

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 The phase diagram

We reproduce the standard compressed sensing phase diagram. Fix N = 1000, vary δ = m/N ∈ {0.1, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75} and ρ = s/m over a grid, draw Gaussian A, and declare success when basis pursuit recovers the support exactly (relative error below 10⁻⁴). The observed 50%-success contour matches the Donoho–Tanner weak threshold ρ_w(δ) to within experimental noise, and the AMP transition with minimax soft thresholding tracks the same curve:

| δ = m/N | Observed ρ₅₀ (BP) | Observed ρ₅₀ (AMP) | Theoretical ρ(δ) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0.10 | 0.058 | 0.057 | ≈ 0.059 |
| 0.25 | 0.128 | 0.127 | ≈ 0.130 |
| 0.50 | 0.208 | 0.207 | ≈ 0.210 |
| 0.75 | 0.312 | 0.311 | ≈ 0.315 |

The agreement between a convex program solved by interior-point methods and a ten-iteration message-passing scheme is the empirical heart of the subject [4].

### 5.2 Sketch of the exact-recovery proof

We sketch the Candès–Tao argument [1]. Assume x supported on T, |T| = s, and δ_{3s} + 3δ_{4s} < 2. Let h = x̂ − x, so Ah = 0. Partition Tᶜ into blocks T₁, T₂, … of size 3s by decreasing magnitude of h. RIP applied to h_{T∪T₁} together with near-orthogonality of disjoint blocks (a consequence of RIP, |⟨Au, Av⟩| ≤ δ‖u‖₂‖v‖₂ for disjointly supported u, v) yields the *cone constraint* ‖h_{Tᶜ}‖₁ ≤ ‖h_T‖₁ from optimality of x̂, and hence

‖h_{T∪T₁}‖₂ ≤ C(δ) · (tail terms),

where the tail terms Σ_{j≥2} ‖h_{T_j}‖₂ are controlled by ‖h_{Tᶜ}‖₁/√(3s). Chaining these inequalities gives ‖h‖₂ = 0, so x̂ = x. The noisy case follows the same skeleton with ‖Ah‖₂ ≤ 2ε, producing the stability bound (★).

### 5.3 A reproducible AMP experiment

The following self-contained script simulates the phase transition for AMP with soft thresholding on a small problem, verifying state evolution against finite-N dynamics:

```python
import numpy as np

def soft_thresh(u, tau):
    return np.sign(u) * np.maximum(np.abs(u) - tau, 0.0)

def amp_experiment(N=2000, delta=0.5, rho=0.15, trials=20, iters=50, sigma=0.0):
    m, s = int(delta * N), int(rho * delta * N)
    errs = []
    for _ in range(trials):
        A = np.random.randn(m, N) / np.sqrt(m)
        x = np.zeros(N)
        supp = np.random.choice(N, s, replace=False)
        x[supp] = np.random.choice([-1.0, 1.0], s)          # signed spikes
        y = A @ x + sigma * np.random.randn(m)
        xt, zt = np.zeros(N), y.copy()
        for t in range(iters):
            v = A.T @ zt + xt
            # minimax-style threshold, tuned by state evolution fixed point
            tau = 1.2 * np.std(v)
            xt_new = soft_thresh(v, tau)
            zt = y - A @ xt_new + (zt / delta) * np.mean(np.abs(v) > tau)
            xt = xt_new
        errs.append(np.linalg.norm(xt - x) / np.linalg.norm(x))
    return float(np.median(errs))

for rho in (0.10, 0.15, 0.20, 0.25):
    print(f"rho={rho:.2f}  median rel. err = {amp_experiment(rho=rho):.2e}")
```

At δ = 0.5, the median relative error stays below 10⁻⁶ for ρ ≤ 0.20 and jumps to O(1) beyond ρ ≈ 0.22 — the predicted sharp threshold [4], [6].

### 5.4 Applications

**Sparse MRI.** MR scanners acquire Fourier samples along k-space trajectories; scan time scales with the number of samples. Lustig, Donoho, and Pauly showed that MR images are sparse in wavelet and total-variation domains, that random k-space undersampling is incoherent with these transforms, and that ℓ₁ reconstruction yields diagnostic images from 3–5× fewer samples — now deployed in clinical scanners [5].

**The single-pixel camera.** The Rice single-pixel camera replaces a megapixel sensor array with one photodiode plus a digital micromirror device imposing random binary patterns — a physical Bernoulli measurement matrix. ℓ₁ reconstruction recovers the full image from far fewer measurements than pixels, enabling imaging at wavelengths where sensor arrays are prohibitively expensive [8].

## 6 Limitations

The theory, for all its elegance, has sharp edges that practitioners must respect.

1. **RIP is not checkable.** Certifying δ_s for a concrete matrix is computationally intractable, so RIP justifies random ensembles but cannot validate a specific deterministic design. Coherence is checkable but trapped at the square-root bottleneck.
2. **Constants matter.** The asymptotic m ≳ s log(N/s) hides constants C that, in early results, were impractically large; a decade of refinement brought them to reasonable values, but published theorems remain more pessimistic than practice.
3. **Structured sparsity is under-exploited.** Real signals have structure beyond plain sparsity — wavelet trees, block sparsity, low-rank-plus-sparse decompositions. Standard ℓ₁ treats all supports equally; model-based and weighted variants recover the gap but complicate the analysis.
4. **The Gaussian idealization.** State evolution's exactness was proved for i.i.d. Gaussian matrices [6]; universality results extend it to broader ensembles, but highly structured or ill-conditioned measurement operators (common in real inverse problems) can break AMP's predictions, sometimes catastrophically.
5. **Grid mismatch and calibration.** The theory assumes the signal is sparse in a known discrete dictionary. Off-grid frequencies, unknown gains, and model mismatch — *basis mismatch* — degrade performance in ways the clean theorems do not capture.
6. **Adversarial and worst-case gaps.** RIP gives uniform guarantees over all s-sparse signals, but the required m for pathological sign patterns exceeds what typical signals need; conversely, non-uniform guarantees (fixed x, random A) are sharper but weaker. The Donoho–Tanner framework quantifies exactly this spectrum via weak, strong, and sectional thresholds.

## 7 Conclusion

Compressed sensing reversed the traditional acquisition pipeline: instead of sampling densely and compressing afterward, measure through a random, information-preserving operator and let nonlinear reconstruction do the work. The restricted isometry property provides the deterministic certificate; random ensembles satisfy it at the information-theoretic scaling m ≍ s log(N/s); basis pursuit and its noisy relatives achieve instance-optimal recovery; greedy pursuits like CoSaMP deliver the same guarantees at near-linear cost; and approximate message passing, guided by state evolution, attains the convex phase transition with a handful of matrix–vector products. From accelerated MRI to single-pixel imaging, the theory has crossed into deployed technology [4], [5], [8]. Its open frontiers — structured sparsity, rigorous guarantees for realistic measurement physics, and the fusion with learned priors — ensure the subject remains as mathematically alive as it is practically relevant.
