## Abstract

Control barrier functions (CBFs) provide a Lyapunov-like certificate for the forward invariance of safe sets in nonlinear control systems, enabling the synthesis of safety-critical controllers through real-time quadratic programs. This article develops the modern theory of CBFs from first principles: zeroing barrier functions and their invariance guarantees via the comparison lemma, the min-norm quadratic-program safety filter that mediates nominal performance objectives against hard safety constraints, and the unification of control Lyapunov functions with barrier certificates in a single convex optimization. We examine high-relative-degree constraints through exponential and high-order CBF constructions, robust variants that preserve invariance under bounded disturbances via input-to-state stability arguments, and the feasibility question that arises when actuator limits meet safety constraints. Discrete-time CBFs and their role in safety-critical model predictive control are analyzed, together with Lipschitz regularity of the resulting closed-loop controllers. A numerical case study of obstacle avoidance for a planar double integrator demonstrates the theory, and we close with a candid assessment of the open problems in constructive CBF synthesis for general nonlinear systems.

## 1 Introduction

Safety-critical control systems — autonomous vehicles, surgical robots, power grids — must satisfy hard state constraints at all times, even while pursuing performance objectives that may conflict with them. Classical approaches to constrained control, such as model predictive control (MPC), enforce constraints through online optimization over a receding horizon, but provide invariance guarantees only through carefully constructed terminal ingredients and can be computationally demanding for fast nonlinear dynamics. An alternative paradigm treats safety as a *certificate*: a scalar function whose level sets encode the safe region and whose derivative condition, when enforced point-wise in time, guarantees that trajectories never leave the safe set [1].

Control barrier functions (CBFs), introduced in their modern control-theoretic form by Wieland and Allgöwer and developed systematically by Ames, Grizzle, Tabuada and co-workers [1], are precisely such certificates. They generalize the barrier certificates of Prajna and Jadbabaie — originally a verification tool for hybrid systems — to the synthesis setting, in the same way that Sontag's control Lyapunov functions extended Lyapunov's stability theory to controller design. The central appeal of the CBF framework is computational: the safety condition reduces to an *affine inequality in the control input*, so a nominal (possibly unsafe) controller can be filtered through a convex quadratic program (QP) solved at kilohertz rates, producing the minimally invasive safe control action [1].

This article presents a self-contained, PhD-level treatment of the theory. Section 2 reviews barrier certificates, the comparison lemma, and class-$\mathcal{K}$ functions. Section 3 develops zeroing CBFs and the QP safety-filter architecture, including unification with control Lyapunov functions. Section 4 deep-dives into four advanced topics: the forward-invariance theorem, high-order CBFs for high-relative-degree constraints, robustness under disturbances, and feasibility under input constraints. Section 5 gives proof sketches of the main regularity results and a numerical case study. Section 6 discusses limitations, and Section 7 concludes.

> **Theorem (informal preview):** If a continuously differentiable function $h$ satisfies $\sup_{u} \dot{h}(x,u) \geq -\alpha(h(x))$ for some extended class-$\mathcal{K}$ function $\alpha$, then any Lipschitz controller rendering the inequality true keeps the superlevel set of $h$ forward invariant — and, remarkably, asymptotically stable.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Barrier certificates and set invariance

Consider the control-affine system

$$\dot{x} = f(x) + g(x)u, \qquad x \in \mathcal{D} \subset \mathbb{R}^n, \; u \in \mathcal{U} \subset \mathbb{R}^m,$$

with $f, g$ locally Lipschitz. A set $\mathcal{C} \subset \mathcal{D}$ is *forward invariant* under a closed-loop vector field if trajectories starting in $\mathcal{C}$ remain in $\mathcal{C}$ for all forward time. Prajna and Jadbabaie introduced *barrier certificates* as a verification device: a function $B(x)$ that is nonpositive on the initial set, positive on the unsafe set, and nonincreasing along trajectories certifies that no trajectory can reach the unsafe set. This is a safety analogue of Lyapunov's second method, but with a crucial asymmetry: we need only prevent *exit* through a boundary, not attract *toward* a point [2].

Nagumo's theorem gives the geometric content of invariance: a closed set with smooth boundary is forward invariant under a locally Lipschitz vector field if and only if the vector field points into the tangent cone at every boundary point. CBFs convert this geometric condition into an algebraic inequality on the control input, enabling synthesis rather than mere verification.

### 2.2 Class-$\mathcal{K}$ functions and the comparison lemma

A continuous function $\alpha: (0,a) \to (0,\infty)$ is *class-$\mathcal{K}$* if it is strictly increasing and $\alpha(0) = 0$; it is *extended class-$\mathcal{K}$* if additionally defined on all of $\mathbb{R}$ and strictly increasing there. The class-$\mathcal{K}$ function in the CBF condition plays the role of a *relaxation*: rather than demanding $\dot{h} \geq 0$ everywhere (which would freeze the system at the boundary), it permits $\dot{h}$ to be negative in the interior, proportional to how safely positive $h$ is, while demanding $\dot{h} \geq 0$ exactly on the boundary $\partial \mathcal{C}$ where $h = 0$ [1].

The *comparison lemma* is the analytical engine. If $\dot{h}(t) \geq -\alpha(h(t))$ along a trajectory and $\alpha$ is locally Lipschitz, then $h(t)$ is bounded below by the solution of $\dot{z} = -\alpha(z)$ with $z(0) = h(0)$. Since $z \equiv 0$ is an equilibrium of the comparison system, trajectories starting with $h(0) \geq 0$ satisfy $h(t) \geq 0$ for all $t \geq 0$. This single argument yields forward invariance from a point-wise differential inequality — no integration of the true dynamics required [1].

### 2.3 Zeroing versus reciprocal barrier functions

Two flavors of barrier function appear in the literature, and the distinction matters:

- **Reciprocal CBFs** take the form $B(x) = 1/h(x)$, blowing up at the boundary. They are defined only on the interior of $\mathcal{C}$ and produce controllers that can become ill-conditioned near the boundary.
- **Zeroing CBFs** use $h(x)$ directly, with $\mathcal{C} = \{x : h(x) \geq 0\}$. They are well defined on all of $\mathcal{D}$, including outside $\mathcal{C}$, which additionally yields *asymptotic stability* of the safe set: trajectories starting outside are driven back in [1].

The modern literature, and this article, focuses on zeroing CBFs for their superior regularity and the stability bonus.

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 Zeroing control barrier functions

Let $\mathcal{C} = \{x \in \mathcal{D} : h(x) \geq 0\}$ with $h$ continuously differentiable and $\nabla h(x) \neq 0$ on $\partial \mathcal{C}$. Then $h$ is a *zeroing control barrier function* on $\mathcal{D}$ if there exists an extended class-$\mathcal{K}$ function $\alpha$ such that

$$\sup_{u \in \mathcal{U}} \big[ L_f h(x) + L_g h(x)\, u \big] \geq -\alpha(h(x)), \qquad \forall x \in \mathcal{D}, \tag{1}$$

where $L_f h = \nabla h \cdot f$ and $L_g h = \nabla h \cdot g$ are Lie derivatives. Condition (1) is *affine in $u$*: for each fixed $x$, the admissible controls form a half-space (possibly intersected with $\mathcal{U}$),

$$K_{\mathrm{cbf}}(x) = \big\{ u \in \mathcal{U} : L_f h(x) + L_g h(x)\, u + \alpha(h(x)) \geq 0 \big\}.$$

Any Lipschitz selection $u(x) \in K_{\mathrm{cbf}}(x)$ renders $\mathcal{C}$ forward invariant [1].

### 3.2 The min-norm safety filter

In practice one rarely designs a controller from the CBF condition alone. Instead, a nominal controller $u_{\mathrm{nom}}(x)$ — designed for performance, tracking, or optimality, and possibly unsafe — is *filtered* through the QP

$$\begin{aligned}
u^*(x) = \arg\min_{u \in \mathcal{U}} \quad & \|u - u_{\mathrm{nom}}(x)\|^2 \\
\mathrm{s.t.} \quad & L_f h(x) + L_g h(x)\, u \geq -\alpha(h(x)), \tag{2}
\end{aligned}$$

which returns the safe control closest to the nominal one in the Euclidean norm. When $\mathcal{U} = \mathbb{R}^m$ and $L_g h(x) \neq 0$, (2) admits the closed form

$$u^*(x) = u_{\mathrm{nom}}(x) + \max\!\left(0, -\frac{L_f h(x) + L_g h(x)\, u_{\mathrm{nom}}(x) + \alpha(h(x))}{\|L_g h(x)\|^2}\right) L_g h(x)^\top,$$

so the filter intervenes only when the nominal control violates the barrier constraint, and otherwise passes it through untouched. This *minimal invasiveness* is the practical signature of CBF-based safety [1].

### 3.3 Unification with control Lyapunov functions

Performance is encoded dually through a *control Lyapunov function* (CLF) $V$, requiring $\inf_u [L_f V + L_g V\, u] \leq -\gamma(V)$ for stabilization. Ames et al. [1] unify the two certificates in a single QP:

$$\begin{aligned}
\min_{u, \delta} \quad & \|u\|^2 + p\,\delta^2 \\
\mathrm{s.t.} \quad & L_f V(x) + L_g V(x)\, u \leq -\gamma(V(x)) + \delta, \\
& L_f h(x) + L_g h(x)\, u \geq -\alpha(h(x)),
\end{aligned} \tag{3}$$

where the relaxation variable $\delta \geq 0$ (penalized by $p > 0$) softens the stabilization constraint while the safety constraint remains hard. Safety is thereby *prioritized over performance by construction*: the QP is always permitted to sacrifice convergence to preserve invariance, never the reverse. This mediation was demonstrated on adaptive cruise control and lane keeping, where actuator bounds and safety constraints coexist [1].

| Certificate | Encodes | Inequality direction | Relaxation at boundary |
|---|---|---|---|
| Control Lyapunov function $V$ | Stability of an equilibrium/set | $\dot V \leq -\gamma(V)$ | Strict decrease required |
| Zeroing CBF $h$ | Invariance of a safe set | $\dot h \geq -\alpha(h)$ | $\dot h \geq 0$ on $\partial\mathcal{C}$ |
| Reciprocal CBF $1/h$ | Invariance (interior only) | $\dot B \leq \alpha(1/B)$ | Blows up at boundary |

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The forward-invariance theorem and its proof

The foundational result deserves a precise statement [1]:

> **Theorem (CBF invariance):** Let $h$ be a zeroing CBF on $\mathcal{D}$ with associated extended class-$\mathcal{K}$ function $\alpha$, and let $u: \mathcal{D} \to \mathcal{U}$ be locally Lipschitz with $u(x) \in K_{\mathrm{cbf}}(x)$ for all $x$. Then $\mathcal{C}$ is forward invariant for the closed loop. Moreover, $\mathcal{C}$ is asymptotically stable on $\mathcal{D}$: there exists a class-$\mathcal{KL}$ function $\beta$ such that $\|x(t)\|_{\mathcal{C}} \leq \beta(\|x(0)\|_{\mathcal{C}}, t)$.

*Proof sketch.* Along closed-loop trajectories, $\dot h \geq -\alpha(h)$ by construction. The comparison lemma yields $h(t) \geq z(t)$ where $\dot z = -\alpha(z)$, $z(0) = h(0) \geq 0$; since the comparison system leaves the nonnegative orthant invariant, $h(t) \geq 0$ for all $t$, proving invariance. For stability, consider the Lyapunov-like candidate $V_{\mathcal{C}}(x) = \max(0, -h(x))^2$, which vanishes exactly on $\mathcal{C}$ and satisfies $\dot V_{\mathcal{C}} \leq 0$ outside $\mathcal{C}$ with strict decrease away from a neighborhood of $\partial\mathcal{C}$; standard set-stability arguments then give asymptotic stability [1]. ∎

A subtle but essential hypothesis is the *Lipschitz continuity of the QP controller*. The closed form above is Lipschitz when $L_g h(x)$ is bounded away from zero, and Xu et al. [2] prove Lipschitz regularity of (2) under mild constraint qualifications, which is what guarantees existence and uniqueness of closed-loop solutions in the first place.

### 4.2 High-relative-degree constraints: exponential and high-order CBFs

The basic CBF condition requires the input to appear explicitly in $\dot h$, i.e., $L_g h(x) \neq 0$ — the safety constraint must have *relative degree one*. Many physical constraints violate this: for a double integrator with position constraint $h(x) = d - x_1$, we have $L_g h \equiv 0$ since force affects only acceleration. Nguyen and Sreenath addressed this with *exponential CBFs* [4]: for relative degree $r$, define the auxiliary state $\eta = [h, \dot h, \ldots, h^{(r-1)}]^\top$ and impose

$$h^{(r)}(x,u) \geq -K_\alpha\, \eta(x), \tag{4}$$

where $K_\alpha$ is chosen so that the polynomial $s^r + K_{\alpha,r} s^{r-1} + \cdots + K_{\alpha,1}$ is Hurwitz. If additionally the initial auxiliary state lies in the appropriate invariant region, then $h(t) \geq C e^{(F - G K_\alpha)t}\eta(0) \geq 0$, giving invariance through a stable linear comparison system rather than a scalar one [4].

Xiao and Belta generalized this to *high-order CBFs* (HOCBFs) [3], a more flexible recursive construction. Given $h$ of relative degree $m$, define

$$\psi_0(x) = h(x), \qquad \psi_i(x) = \dot\psi_{i-1}(x) + \alpha_i(\psi_{i-1}(x)), \quad i = 1,\ldots,m,$$

with each $\alpha_i$ a differentiable class-$\mathcal{K}$ function. The HOCBF condition constrains only the last function,

$$\sup_{u} \big[ L_f^m h(x) + L_g L_f^{m-1} h(x)\, u + O(h(x)) + \alpha_m(\psi_{m-1}(x)) \big] \geq 0, \tag{5}$$

where $O(h)$ collects lower-degree Lie-derivative terms. The key theorem: if $x(0)$ lies in the intersection $\bigcap_{i=0}^{m-1} \{x : \psi_i(x) \geq 0\}$ and (5) holds, then this intersection — and hence $\{h \geq 0\}$ — is forward invariant [3]. Each $\alpha_i$ can be tuned independently, giving a design freedom that exponential CBFs (with their fixed pole placement) lack; Xiao and Belta show the HOCBF constraint unifies with CLFs in a QP sequence that conservatively reduces a constrained optimal control problem to real-time solvable convex programs, demonstrated on adaptive cruise control with explicit control bounds [3].

### 4.3 Robustness: preserving safety under disturbances

Real systems suffer disturbances. Xu, Tabuada, Grizzle and Ames [2] extended the framework to

$$\dot x = f(x) + g(x)u + p(x)d(t), \qquad \|d(t)\| \leq \bar d,$$

via *input-to-state safety* (ISSf). The robust CBF condition strengthens (1) to

$$\sup_{u} \big[ L_f h + L_g h\, u \big] \geq -\alpha(h) + \iota(\bar d),$$

where $\iota$ is a class-$\mathcal{K}$ function of the disturbance bound. The comparison argument then yields invariance not of $\mathcal{C}$ itself but of an *inflated* set $\mathcal{C}_{\bar d} = \{x : h(x) \geq -\gamma(\bar d)\}$ for a suitable class-$\mathcal{K}$ function $\gamma$ — safety degrades gracefully and quantitatively with disturbance magnitude, vanishing exactly as $\bar d \to 0$ [2]. This ISSf perspective is the safety analogue of Sontag's input-to-state stability, and it composes naturally with robust CLF designs.

### 4.4 Feasibility under input constraints

The Achilles' heel of CBF-QP synthesis is *feasibility*: with bounded inputs $\mathcal{U} = \{u : \|u\| \leq u_{\max}\}$, the half-space $K_{\mathrm{cbf}}(x)$ may be empty — the barrier demands a deceleration the actuators cannot deliver. Three complementary remedies have emerged:

1. **Backup and reachability-based CBFs.** Construct $h$ from a backup controller's forward reachable set, so that the CBF is *valid by construction* under the input limits [7].
2. **Feasibility-aware HOCBF tuning.** The class-$\mathcal{K}$ functions $\alpha_i$ directly shape how aggressively the barrier pushes back; Xiao et al. [8] show that for systems with multiple inputs, integral-control reformulations and penalty-based methods substantially enlarge the feasible region, and that the $\alpha_i$ can be optimized to trade conservatism against feasibility.
3. **Learning feasibility margins.** Recent work learns corrections to candidate CBFs from data so that the QP remains feasible under complex constraints and limited actuation, with self-supervised objectives that require no labeled safe/unsafe data [6].

The interaction of *multiple* CBFs (obstacle avoidance with several obstacles) compounds the issue: the intersection of half-spaces can empty even when each is individually feasible. Nonsmooth composition via $\min$/$\max$ operators and their smooth approximations provide a calculus for Boolean combinations of constraints [4].

### 4.5 Discrete-time CBFs and safety-critical MPC

For sampled-data implementation, the discrete-time CBF condition for $x_{k+1} = f_d(x_k, u_k)$ reads

$$h(f_d(x_k, u_k)) - h(x_k) \geq -\gamma(h(x_k)), \qquad 0 < \gamma \leq 1, \tag{6}$$

with $\gamma$ now a scalar contraction rate. Condition (6) is generally *nonconvex* in $u_k$ for nonlinear $f_d$, but for control-affine discretizations it remains tractable. Safety-critical MPC embeds (6) as a constraint at each prediction step, combining the multi-step foresight of MPC with the invariance certificate of CBFs; on a 2D double-integrator obstacle-avoidance benchmark this MPC-CBF formulation outperforms both plain MPC with distance constraints and CLF-CBF QP designs in feasibility and safety margin [5]. Alternatively, (2) itself can be viewed as a one-step MPC with a trivial prediction horizon — a viewpoint that clarifies why CBF-QPs are computationally cheaper than full MPC while sacrificing multi-step optimality [3].

## 5 Empirical Results and Proof Sketches

### 5.1 Regularity: why the QP controller is well-behaved

Two analytical results underpin practical deployment [2]:

> **Proposition (Lipschitz continuity):** If $u_{\mathrm{nom}}$, $L_f h$, $L_g h$ are locally Lipschitz, $\mathcal{U} = \mathbb{R}^m$, and $L_g h(x) \neq 0$ whenever the constraint is active, then the min-norm solution $u^*(x)$ of (2) is locally Lipschitz.

> **Proposition (ISSf inflation bound):** Under the robust condition of Section 4.3, trajectories satisfy $h(x(t)) \geq -\gamma(\bar d)$ for all $t \geq 0$ whenever $h(x(0)) \geq -\gamma(\bar d)$, with $\gamma$ explicitly computable from $\alpha$, $\iota$, and the Lipschitz constants of the dynamics.

The first guarantees classical (Carathéodory) solutions of the closed loop; the second quantifies the safety-disturbance trade-off. Together with the invariance theorem of Section 4.1, they form the complete analytical backbone: *existence, invariance, stability, robustness* [1][2].

### 5.2 Numerical case study: double-integrator obstacle avoidance

We demonstrate the min-norm safety filter on a planar double integrator avoiding a circular obstacle.

```python

# Planar double integrator: x = [px, py, vx, vy], u = [ax, ay]
A = np.block([[np.zeros((2, 2)), np.eye(2)],
              [np.zeros((2, 4))]])
B = np.block([[np.zeros((2, 2))],
              [np.eye(2)]])

p_obs = np.array([5.0, 0.0])   # obstacle center
r = 1.5                        # obstacle radius
goal = np.array([10.0, 0.0])


def cbf_qp_filter(x, u_nom, u_max=6.0):
    """Min-norm safety filter for relative-degree-2 constraint
    h(x) = ||p - p_obs||^2 - r^2 via exponential CBF condition."""
    p, v = x[:2], x[2:]
    d = p - p_obs
    h = d @ d - r**2
    dh = 2 * d @ v              # first derivative (no input appears)
    # ddh = 2 v@v + 2 d@u ; enforce ddh >= -K1*h - K2*dh
    Lg = 2 * d                  # input coefficient row vector
    rhs = -K1 * h - K2 * dh - 2 * (v @ v)
    lhs_nom = Lg @ u_nom
    if lhs_nom >= rhs:
        u_safe = u_nom
    else:
        # closed-form projection onto the affine half-space
        u_safe = u_nom + (rhs - lhs_nom) / (Lg @ Lg) * Lg
    return np.clip(u_safe, -u_max, u_max)

# Simulate: PD nominal control filtered at 100 Hz
x = np.array([0.0, 0.0, 0.0, 0.0])
dt, h_min = 0.01, np.inf
for _ in range(1200):
    u_nom = 1.5 * (goal - x[:2]) - 1.2 * x[2:]
    u = cbf_qp_filter(x, u_nom)
    x = x + dt * (A @ x + B @ u)
    h_min = min(h_min, (x[:2] - p_obs) @ (x[:2] - p_obs) - r**2)

print(f"minimum barrier value over trajectory: {h_min:.4f} (safe iff >= 0)")
print(f"final distance to goal: {np.linalg.norm(x[:2] - goal):.3f}")
```


### 5.3 What the experiments in the literature show


1. **Automotive (ACC and lane keeping):** the CLF-CBF QP (3) tracks a desired speed while provably maintaining headway, with solve times under a millisecond [1].
2. **High-relative-degree cruise control:** HOCBF-QP sequences respect explicit actuator bounds where a naive relative-degree-one CBF is undefined, at the cost of tuning $m$ class-$\mathcal{K}$ functions [3].
3. **Racing (MPC-CBF):** embedding discrete-time CBF constraints in MPC yields larger safety margins than distance-constrained MPC at comparable solve times [5].
4. **Approximate dynamic programming alternative:** incorporating safety cost into the value function can outperform the greedy QP in convergence speed, suggesting the QP's myopia is a real (if practically acceptable) limitation [6].

## 6 Limitations

The framework's elegance should not obscure its genuine weaknesses.

- **Constructive synthesis is hard.** Verifying a *given* $h$ is easy; *finding* one for a general nonlinear system with input bounds is not. Sums-of-squares programming scales poorly beyond modest dimensions, and hand-designed CBFs rely on structural insight (feedback linearizability, mechanical energy) that may not exist [7].
- **Relative degree and feasibility interact badly.** HOCBFs handle high relative degree, but each added $\alpha_i$ is another tuning knob, and aggressive class-$\mathcal{K}$ functions that improve responsiveness shrink the feasible set under input limits. There is no free lunch between conservatism and feasibility [3][8].
- **Myopia.** The QP (2) is a one-step greedy projection; it guarantees safety but can produce sluggish or oscillatory behavior near the boundary (e.g., chattering when $L_g h$ is small). Multi-step MPC-CBF formulations mitigate this at higher computational cost [5].
- **Model dependence.** The Lie derivatives in (1) require an accurate model; unmodeled dynamics can violate the barrier condition between samples. Robust ISSf variants [2] and model-free CBF extensions trade performance for guaranteed margins, but the gap between theory and hardware remains the field's central engineering challenge.
- **Composition.** Boolean combinations of many constraints via nonsmooth $\min$/$\max$ composition lose differentiability exactly where the QP needs gradients; smooth approximations reintroduce conservatism [4].

## 7 Conclusion

Control barrier functions recast safety as a *certificate* rather than a *prediction*: a single scalar inequality, affine in the control input, whose enforcement at each instant guarantees forward invariance of the safe set for all future time. From the zeroing-CBF invariance theorem [1] through its robust ISSf extension [2], the high-order constructions for high-relative-degree constraints [3], and the discrete-time embedding in safety-critical MPC [5], the theory now spans the full pipeline from certificate to real-time convex synthesis. The min-norm QP safety filter has become a standard architectural pattern — a transparent, minimally invasive layer between performance-oriented autonomy stacks and physical hardware.

The frontier has shifted from *analysis* to *synthesis*: constructive methods that produce valid CBFs under input constraints for systems without special structure [7], data-driven feasibility repair [6], and principled tuning of high-order class-$\mathcal{K}$ functions [8]. Until synthesis is as routine as verification, CBFs will remain a powerful but artisanal tool — one whose mathematical foundations, at least, are now firmly in place.
