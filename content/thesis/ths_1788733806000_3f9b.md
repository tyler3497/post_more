# Continuous-Depth Learning: Neural Ordinary Differential Equations, Adjoint Sensitivity, and Liquid Time-Constant Networks

## Abstract

Discrete-layer neural networks treat depth as an integer hyperparameter, yet many natural processes evolve in continuous time. Continuous-depth learning replaces discrete layer stacks with parameterized dynamical systems: neural ordinary differential equations (NODEs) define the hidden-state derivative with a neural network and evaluate it with a black-box ODE solver, while liquid time-constant networks (LTCs) extend this with biologically grounded, state-dependent time constants. This thesis develops the full theoretical stack: the ResNet-to-ODE limit, the adjoint sensitivity method enabling memory-constant gradients through arbitrary solvers, continuous normalizing flows for unrestricted generative modeling, LTC dynamics with provable stability bounds and trajectory-length expressivity guarantees, and the closed-form approximations (CfCs) that remove the solver bottleneck and accelerate training by orders of magnitude. We evaluate the empirical record — MNIST parity at half the parameters, irregular time-series modeling, sub-100-neuron driving policies — and delineate hard limits: solver stiffness, adjoint instability, CfC non-invertibility, and the gap between approximation theory and trainability.

## 1 Introduction

The modern deep network is a discrete object. A residual network applies

h_{t+1} = h_t + f(h_t, θ_t)

a finite number of times, and "depth" counts how many. This discretization is a convenience, not a necessity: many of the phenomena we ask networks to model — continuous physiological signals, financial tick data, robot kinematics, neural firing rates — live in continuous time and are sampled only irregularly. When the underlying process is continuous but the model is discrete, practitioners resort to binning, imputation, or interpolation hacks that destroy information about *when* observations occurred [1].

Neural ordinary differential equations, introduced by Chen, Duvenaud and colleagues at NeurIPS 2018, remove the discretization entirely: instead of specifying a discrete sequence of hidden layers, they parameterize the *derivative* of the hidden state with a neural network and compute the output with a numerical ODE solver [2]. Depth becomes a real number — the integration interval — and the model adapts its computation to each input, trading numerical precision for speed on demand. Training such models naively would require backpropagating through every internal step of the solver, but the **adjoint sensitivity method** — a continuous-time analogue of backpropagation dating to Pontryagin's optimal control theory — computes all gradients with a second ODE solve of constant memory cost [3].

This thesis treats continuous-depth learning as a unified discipline spanning three generations of models:

1. **Neural ODEs (2018):** the continuous-depth paradigm, adjoint training, and continuous normalizing flows.
2. **Liquid time-constant networks (2020):** a biologically grounded refinement in which every neuron's effective time constant is itself a function of input and hidden state, yielding provable stability and strictly greater expressivity within the neural-ODE family [4].
3. **Closed-form continuous-time networks (2022):** a tightly bounded closed-form approximation of LTC dynamics that eliminates the numerical solver, delivering one to five orders of magnitude speedups [5].

We proceed from mathematical foundations to empirical evidence, and close with the limitations that bound what continuous-depth learning can and cannot do.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 From ResNets to continuous dynamics

Consider a residual block h_{t+1} = h_t + f(h_t, θ_t). As the number of layers grows and each layer's contribution shrinks, this Euler update converges — in the limit — to an ordinary differential equation:

dh(t)/dt = f(h(t), t, θ).

The neural ODE makes this limit the *definition* of the model: a single function f(·, θ), shared across the entire continuous depth interval [t_0, t_1], replaces L distinct layer functions. This parameter tying is what lets an ODE-Net match a six-block ResNet on MNIST while using roughly half the parameters [2].

### 2.2 Continuous-time RNNs and the irregular sampling problem

Standard recurrent networks assume regularly spaced observations. For irregularly sampled series, ODE-RNNs interleave discrete RNN updates at observation times with continuous ODE evolution between them, and latent ODEs replace the recognition network of a variational autoencoder with an ODE-RNN encoder [6]. Both handle arbitrary time gaps natively, without binning. LTCs inherit this strength but restructure the dynamics themselves: rather than adding a continuous evolution to a discrete gating scheme, every LTC neuron *is* a first-order dynamical system whose time constant adapts — "liquidly" — to the input stream [4].

### 2.3 Optimal control and the adjoint heritage

Training a continuous-depth model is an optimal control problem: choose parameters θ to minimize a terminal loss L(z(t_1)) subject to dz/dt = f(z, t, θ). Pontryagin's maximum principle (1962) answers exactly this question via the *adjoint* (costate) variables λ(t), which obey their own differential equation running backward in time. The adjoint sensitivity method used in neural ODEs is this classical machinery restated for deep learning: it computes dL/dθ with a single additional ODE solve rather than storing the entire forward trajectory [2], [3].

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 The neural ODE forward pass

Given an initial state z(t_0) (possibly produced by an encoder), the forward pass is a black-box initial-value problem:

z(t_1) = z(t_0) + ∫_{t_0}^{t_1} f(z(t), t, θ) dt = ODESolve(z(t_0), f, t_0, t_1, θ).

Any solver may be used — fixed-step Euler, classical fourth-order Runge–Kutta, or adaptive methods such as Dormand–Prince (dopri5) that adjust step size to meet error tolerances. Adaptivity is a feature, not an implementation detail: the solver performs more function evaluations precisely where the learned vector field is complicated, so per-example computation scales with problem difficulty. A striking empirical finding is that the number of function evaluations (NFE) grows steadily during training, tracking the model's increasing complexity [2].

### 3.2 The adjoint sensitivity method

Let the scalar loss L depend on the terminal state z(t_1). Define the adjoint a(t) = −∂L/∂z(t). Then:

- The adjoint obeys its own ODE: da(t)/dt = −a(t)^⊤ ∂f(z(t), t, θ)/∂z(t), with terminal condition a(t_1) = ∂L/∂z(t_1).
- Parameter gradients follow from a second integral: dL/dθ = −∫_{t_1}^{t_0} a(t)^⊤ ∂f(z(t), t, θ)/∂θ dt.
- The three quantities — state z(t), adjoint a(t), and the accumulated gradient dL/dθ — can be computed in a **single call** to an ODE solver, integrated backward from t_1 to t_0.

Because the forward trajectory z(t) is reconstructed by re-solving the forward ODE backward in time, no intermediate activations are stored: memory cost is **O(1)** in the number of solver steps, versus O(T) for backpropagation through the solver's unrolled steps. Empirically, the adjoint backward pass can require *fewer* function evaluations than the forward pass, making it computationally as well as memory efficient [2].

### 3.3 Continuous normalizing flows

Normalizing flows transform samples through bijective maps, paying O(D³) for Jacobian determinants. In continuous time, the instantaneous change of variables takes an elegant form: if dz/dt = f(z(t), t), then

d log p(z(t))/dt = −tr(∂f/∂z(t)).

Integrating this trace alongside the state yields exact log-densities with O(D²) cost — and with an unbiased Hutchinson trace estimator, O(D) [3]. Free-form Jacobians (FFJORD: Free-form Jacobian of Reversible Dynamics) remove all architectural restrictions of coupling layers: any neural network can serve as the vector field. The price is a numerical solver in the loop and estimator variance during training, but the result is a maximum-likelihood generative model with unrestricted architecture and no data partitioning.

### 3.4 Liquid time-constant dynamics

LTCs replace the implicit nonlinearity of a neural ODE with an explicit biophysical structure. Each neuron is a leaky integrator with input-dependent conductance-based synapses:

dx_i/dt = −(w_τi + f(x, I, θ)) x_i(t) + A f(x, I, θ),

where I(t) is the exogenous input, w_τ a base time-constant vector, A a bias, and f a neural network. The effective time constant 1/(w_τ + f(·)) is *liquid*: it varies with the input and hidden state, letting the network allocate fast dynamics to rapidly changing signals and slow dynamics to long-horizon memory. Because the structure mirrors synaptic transmission in the nervous systems of small species (notably *C. elegans*), LTCs exhibit stable, bounded behavior by construction and admit explicit bounds on neuronal states [4], [7].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Expressivity: trajectory length and the LTC advantage

Raghu et al. showed that deep ReLU networks amplify the *trajectory length* of input curves exponentially in depth. Hasani et al. extend this measure to continuous-time models and prove that LTCs grow trajectory length strictly faster than vanilla neural ODEs and continuous-time RNNs. Intuitively, the liquid time constant acts as a state-dependent amplifier of gradient signal: the bound on expected trajectory length for LTCs contains an extra multiplicative factor (σ_w + ‖z‖/min(δt, L)) absent from the neural-ODE bound [4]. The consequence is concrete: LTCs achieve comparable approximation quality with far fewer neurons, which is why a 19-neuron LTC policy can steer a real car.

> **Theorem (LTC trajectory-length growth, informal):** Under standard weight-initialization conditions, the expected latent trajectory length of an LTC network grows exponentially in effective depth with a strictly larger base than that of a neural ODE or CT-RNN of the same width. [4]

### 4.2 Stability and boundedness guarantees

A vanilla neural ODE can learn arbitrarily stiff or explosive vector fields; nothing in its formulation prevents divergence. LTC dynamics, by contrast, inherit passivity-like properties from their conductance-based form. Hasani et al. prove explicit, parameter-dependent bounds on the hidden states, and the liquid time-constant formulation guarantees that the effective dynamics remain in a stable regime — a property that matters enormously when the network is deployed in closed-loop control, where an unstable hidden state means a crashed vehicle [4], [8].

### 4.3 The closed-form breakthrough: CfC networks

Numerical ODE solvers are the bottleneck of the entire paradigm: they serialize computation, resist batching, and cap scalability. Hasani et al. (2022) show that a single-cell LTC system admits a tightly bounded closed-form approximation:

x(t) ≈ (x_0 − A) exp(−[w_τ + f(I(t), θ)] t) · f(−I(t), θ) + A.

Time appears *explicitly*, so no solver is needed at all. Inspired by this expression — with the exponential replaced by a smoother inverted-sigmoid gate and additional learned functions g, h for flexibility — the authors design **closed-form continuous-time (CfC)** networks [5]. The empirical payoff is dramatic:

- 100× faster than neural ODEs on complex time-series tasks;
- one to five orders of magnitude faster in training and inference versus differential-equation counterparts;
- state-of-the-art performance on long-horizon sequence prediction, including a lane-keeping task with only ~4,000 RNN parameters.

CfCs preserve the causal, continuous-time, time-explicit nature of LTCs — the gate is literally a function of elapsed time — while being ordinary feedforward-recurrent computations that parallelize on GPUs [5].

### 4.4 The sequence-modeling lineage: ODE-RNN, latent ODE, CfC

A consistent empirical story runs through the lineage. On irregularly-sampled data (PhysioNet ICU records, MuJoCo physics, human activity), ODE-RNNs and latent ODEs outperform their discrete counterparts because they interpolate the hidden state between observations instead of holding it constant [6]. LTCs then outperform ODE-RNNs on the same benchmarks with fewer units [4]. CfCs finally match or exceed LTC accuracy at a fraction of the compute [5]. The table below summarizes the complexity scaling per sequence of length n, hidden size k, and solver steps p:

| Model | Sequence prediction | Per-step prediction |
|---|---|---|
| RNN | O(nk) | O(k) |
| ODE-RNN | O(nkp) | O(kp) |
| Transformer | O(n²k) | O(nk) |
| CfC | O(nk) | O(k) |

CfCs recover RNN-level efficiency while retaining continuous-time semantics [5].

### 4.5 Interpretability: from wiring diagrams to attention maps

Because LTCs are derived from physical neuron models, they can be *compiled* back into wiring diagrams: Algorithm 1 in the CfC paper translates a trained LTC into per-neuron closed-form expressions, letting researchers visualize which synapses drive which behaviors [5]. This stands in sharp contrast to the opaque vector fields of generic neural ODEs. The "liquid" framing also yields an interpretable decomposition of timescales — empirical studies of learned time constants show bimodal fast/slow clusters that map onto distinct phenomena in the data (e.g., spectral-frame versus prosodic-phrase timescales in audio) [8].

## 5 Empirical Evaluation and Proofs

**MNIST parity at half the parameters.** The original neural ODE paper trains an ODE-Net that reaches accuracy comparable to a six-block ResNet while using ~58% fewer parameters — direct evidence that continuous depth can substitute for discrete width/depth [2]. Independent reproductions confirm the parameter-efficiency finding across Euler and RK4 solvers.

**Backward-pass efficiency.** The paper's Figure 3 reports that NFE in the adjoint backward pass is roughly *half* that of the forward pass — the adjoint method is not merely memory-cheap but compute-cheap relative to backpropagating through the integrator [2].

**Irregular time series.** On PhysioNet, MuJoCo, and human-activity benchmarks, latent ODEs and ODE-RNNs consistently beat GRU/LSTM baselines on extrapolation and classification, with the gap largest where sampling is sparsest [6].

**LTC benchmarks.** Across a suite of time-series prediction tasks, LTCs outperform classical and modern RNNs (LSTM, GRU, CT-RNN, neural ODE baselines) with smaller networks, and the trajectory-length analysis provides the theoretical account of why [4]. LTC networks were also shown to be universal approximators: any finite trajectory of an n-dimensional dynamical system can be approximated by an LTC network's hidden and output units [7].

**Autonomous driving.** A 19-neuron LTC-based neural circuit policy steered a real vehicle in lane-keeping tests — a striking demonstration of sample and parameter efficiency in closed-loop control [5]. The closed-form CfC variant reproduced these dynamics with mean-squared error ≈ 0.006 against the ODE solution, validating the tightness theorem in practice [5].

**Speed.** CfCs train and infer 10×–100,000× faster than their ODE-based counterparts, fundamentally changing which problems continuous-time models can address [5].

# Sketch: adjoint-method training loop for a neural ODE (torchdiffeq)
import torch
from torchdiffeq import odeint_adjoint as odeint

class ODEFunc(torch.nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, dim):
        super().__init__()
        self.net = torch.nn.Sequential(
            torch.nn.Linear(dim + 1, 64), torch.nn.Tanh(),
            torch.nn.Linear(64, dim))
    def forward(self, t, z):
        # concatenate time so the vector field can be non-autonomous
        return self.net(torch.cat([z, t.expand(z.shape[0], 1)], dim=1))

odefunc = ODEFunc(dim=16)
z0 = torch.randn(32, 16)                       # batch of initial states
zt = odeint(odefunc, z0, torch.linspace(0, 1, 2), method='dopri5')
# odeint_adjoint: gradients flow via the adjoint ODE -- O(1) memory
loss = zt[-1].pow(2).mean()
loss.backward()                                # constant-memory backprop

## 6 Limitations

1. **Solver stiffness and cost.** Adaptive solvers can take tiny steps on stiff learned dynamics, making training unpredictably slow; the "NFE grows during training" phenomenon means the model gets *more* expensive precisely as it gets better [2].
2. **Adjoint numerical instability.** Reconstructing the forward trajectory by solving backward in time amplifies numerical error; for chaotic or stiff systems the adjoint gradients can be unreliable, and practitioners often fall back to checkpointed backprop-through-solver.
3. **CfCs are not invertible.** Because closed-form networks only *approximate* ODEs, they no longer guarantee bijectivity — continuous normalizing flows remain the domain of true ODE models [5].
4. **Approximation theory ≠ trainability.** Universal approximation results for LTCs [7] say nothing about whether gradient descent finds those approximators; the practical success stories rely on small, carefully structured networks.
5. **Irregular-sampling overhead.** ODE-RNN batching requires the union of time grids across a minibatch, which can dominate runtime on highly irregular data [6].
6. **Benchmark narrowness.** Much of the empirical case rests on time-series and control tasks; on large-scale language or vision workloads, continuous-depth models have not displaced discrete architectures.

## 7 Conclusion

Continuous-depth learning reframes the neural network as a dynamical system rather than a stack of layers. Neural ODEs established the paradigm — parameterize the derivative, integrate with a solver, differentiate with the adjoint method — and continuous normalizing flows showed its generative power. Liquid time-constant networks grounded the dynamics in biophysics, proving stability bounds and strictly greater expressivity, and closed-form networks removed the solver bottleneck that had limited the paradigm's scale. The through-line is a productive tension: the more structure we impose on the continuous dynamics (from free-form vector fields to conductance-based neurons to closed-form gates), the more we gain in stability, interpretability, and speed, at the cost of raw flexibility. Open frontiers include stiff-aware training, hybrid discrete-continuous architectures, and scaling CfC-style models to the sequence lengths where transformers currently dominate.
