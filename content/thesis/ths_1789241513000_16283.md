# The Wood Wide Web as a Trading Network: Nutrient-Exchange Economics, Biological Market Dynamics, and Chemical Signaling in Mycorrhizal Symbioses

## Abstract

Beneath every forest floor, mycorrhizal fungi assemble vast subterranean exchange networks — the "wood wide web" — in which plants trade fixed carbon for fungal-delivered phosphorus and nitrogen [4][5]. This thesis asks whether common mycorrhizal networks (CMNs) function as economic markets, testing biological market theory and its reciprocal-reward refinement against the molecular machinery of partner recognition. Kiers and colleagues showed that plants detect, discriminate, and preferentially reward the most generous fungal partners, while fungi escalate nutrient delivery to the most carbon-generous roots [1]. Fellbaum and colleagues showed that fungal allocation among co-connected hosts tracks each plant's carbon source strength, with fungi retaining bargaining power despite obligate dependence [2]. We formalize these dynamics game-theoretically, derive a stability condition for reciprocal rewards, and confront the rival "surplus carbon" hypothesis [11]. We detail the chemical signaling layer — plant strigolactones awakening fungal hyphae [9], fungal lipochitooligosaccharides triggering host calcium spiking [8] — and weigh field evidence for inter-tree carbon transfer against the positive-citation-bias critique [6]. The wood wide web is a real trading network, but its economics are bidirectional, context-dependent, and frequently overinterpreted.

## 1 Introduction

Few metaphors have traveled as far as the *wood wide web*. Coined on the cover of *Nature* in August 1997 beside Simard and colleagues' report of carbon moving between birch and Douglas fir through shared fungal hyphae [4], it turned a controlled tracer experiment into a cultural icon. Beneath the poetry lies a quantitative economic system: roughly **80–90% of land plants** form mycorrhizal associations, and in arbuscular mycorrhizal (AM) symbioses the trade is starkly asymmetric — the fungus, an obligate biotroph, depends wholly on hosts for carbon, while the plant depends on the fungus for phosphorus and nitrogen beyond the root depletion zone [2][3][7].

Both partners typically interact with *multiple* counterparties — one CMN can link many plants, each hosting many fungal taxa [1] — creating the conditions for choice, discrimination, and strategic allocation: the ingredients of a market. Four questions structure this thesis:

1. **Do plants discriminate among fungal partners**, rewarding the most beneficial with carbon rather than distributing it by sink strength alone?
2. **Do fungi discriminate among hosts**, routing nutrients to the most generous plants — and retaining bargaining power while doing so?
3. **What chemical signaling substrate** supports this discrimination — the vocabulary in which partners advertise quality and enforce contracts?
4. **Is plant-to-plant carbon transfer through CMNs a real, significant flux**, or an artifact of tracer methodology and citation bias [6]?

We approach these through **biological market theory** [10] — mutualisms as trading networks where supply, demand, and partner availability set exchange terms — and its sharpest empirical refinement, the **reciprocal-rewards model** [1].

> **Theorem:** *The Biological Market Hypothesis.* Where each participant can continuously adjust allocation in response to measured partner quality, the carbon-per-nutrient exchange rate converges to the ratio equalizing both partners' marginal fitness gains — provided discrimination is fine-grained and switching is cheap [10].

Whether real networks satisfy these provisos is what follows.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The mycorrhizal habit

Arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi (*Glomeromycotina*) form intracellular **arbuscules** — branched hyphal trees inside root cortical cells — in a symbiosis ~**450 million years old**, implicated in plant terrestrialization [7]. Ectomycorrhizal fungi (EMF), a younger convergent assemblage, ensheath root tips in a Hartig net. Their carbon economies differ critically: AMF are obligate biotrophs lacking fatty-acid synthesis genes; many EMF retain saprotrophic capacity [7].

### 2.2 The currencies of exchange

The trade's asymmetry is its defining feature. The plant's currency is **reduced carbon**: sucrose converted to hexoses plus host-derived **lipids** essential for fungal growth. The fungal currency is **mineral nutrition**: phosphate taken up by high-affinity transporters in the extraradical mycelium, condensed into **polyphosphate** and effluxed at the arbuscule, where plant transporters such as **MtPt4** import it [2]. Nitrogen follows a parallel route via fungal ammonium transporters (e.g., *GintAMT1*) and the plant transporter *1723.m00046*, induced when the CMN accesses nitrogen [2][3]. Mycorrhizas supply hosts with up to **40% of nitrogen** and a major share of phosphorus; carbon costs are estimated at **4–20% of net photosynthate**, a number that is fiercely contested [6][7].

### 2.3 From cooperation to markets

The stability of mutualism under exploitation is sharpened here because each partner interacts with *multiple* counterparts — a cheater can keep drawing from alternative partners [10]. Kiers and van der Heijden (2006) identified four candidate stabilizers: **spatial structuring**, **luxury resource exchange**, **partner choice**, and **sanctions** (rewarding cooperators, punishing defectors) [10]. **Biological market theory** adds *competition among traders*: exchange rates track supply and demand, and Werner et al. derived conditions under which trade is stabilized purely by reallocation dynamics [10]. The empirical crown jewel is Kiers et al. (2011): plants allocate more carbon to the most beneficial fungi in mixed AMF communities — and fungi deliver more phosphorus to the most carbon-generous roots [1]. The resulting **reciprocal-rewards model** holds that control is *bidirectional*: the symbiont cannot be "enslaved," and the best exchange rate is rewarded [1].

### 2.4 The skeptical counter-program

Two developments disciplined this narrative. First, Karst, Jones, and Hoeksema (2023) documented **positive citation bias**: unsupported claims about CMN ubiquity and benefits have doubled in 25 years, while evidence that CMNs are widespread, that transfer improves seedling performance, or that mature trees preferentially feed offspring is insufficient or absent [6]. Second, Karst and colleagues advanced the **surplus carbon hypothesis**: plants routinely fix more sugar than growth can use, and fungi act as a passive sink — carbon is *drawn to the strongest sink* rather than priced by nutrient delivery, so donated carbon is not costly and "prices" are epiphenomenal [11].

> *A market with only one bidder is not a market.* The surplus-carbon critique claims the fungal side lacks pricing power — that apparent price discovery is merely diffusion down a sink gradient [6][11].

Both positions cannot be fully right — resolving the tension requires the mechanistic detail of §§3–5.

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 Isotopic tracer experiments

The gold standard is **isotope labeling**. Simard et al. (1997) supplied ^14CO₂ to birch and ^13CO₂ to Douglas fir seedlings and recovered both labels in the other species after nine days; western red cedar, which does not share the ectomycorrhizal partners, served as the no-network control [4]. Klein et al. (2016) extended this to **mature trees** with free-air ^13CO₂ labeling [5]; Cahanovitc et al. (2022) localized ^13C to the DNA of specific mycorrhizal taxa on donor and recipient roots via **DNA-stable isotope probing** [5]; Avital et al. (2022) ruled out passive soil diffusion kinetically [5].

### 3.2 Manipulative split systems

To test whether allocation is *strategic*, investigators perturb one side's bargaining position. Kiers et al. (2011) varied phosphorus supply to competing AMF species and tracked carbon flow into fungal RNA alongside phosphorus delivery, revealing the reciprocal-reward pattern [1]. Fellbaum et al. (2014) shaded a subset of *Medicago* hosts sharing a CMN: AMF discriminated against shaded plants yet maintained colonization [2]. Fellbaum et al. (2012) showed **carbon availability triggers fungal nitrogen uptake and transport**, with fungal N-transporter genes upregulated by host carbon status [3].

### 3.3 Signaling genetics

Strigolactones (SLs) — carotenoid-derived plant hormones — were identified as fungal "branching factors" active at picomolar concentrations [9]; reciprocally, Maillet et al. (2011) purified fungal **lipochitooligosaccharides (Myc-LCOs)** that trigger symbiotic responses including nuclear calcium spiking at nanomolar concentrations [8]. Mutants in LysM receptor kinases, the common symbiosis signaling pathway, and SL biosynthesis establish causality: without these signals, trade never opens [8].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The exchange rate: does phosphorus have a price?

In a market, the carbon price of phosphorus should respond to scarcity. With **quantum-dot–labeled phosphorus**, networks exposed to 70/30 or 90/10 P splits hoarded the mineral and redirected it toward sectors where the host root would "pay more" [1]. The directional evidence is consistent across experiments:

| Experiment | Manipulation | Observed response | Market reading | Ref |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Kiers et al. 2011, split-plate | P supply to AMF species varied | C to best P-provider ↑; P to best C-provider ↑ | Reciprocal price discovery | [1] |
| Fellbaum et al. 2014, shaded CMN | Host C source strength lowered | Fungal P,N to shaded host ↓ (colonization kept) | Discrimination with option value | [2] |
| Fellbaum et al. 2012, C pulse | Host C availability raised | Fungal N uptake & transport ↑ | Supply-side investment | [3] |
| Quantum-dot P patches | P split 70/30, 90/10 | P hoarded, routed to high-price sectors | Arbitrage on scarcity | [1] |
| Bever et al. 2009, split root | Host with two AMF species | C to more beneficial species | Partner choice | [1] |

**Allocation tracks measured partner quality on both sides of the interface** [1][2][3].

### 4.2 Bargaining power: why the fungus cannot be enslaved

Kiers et al.'s most striking claim is negative: *"unlike many other mutualisms, the symbiont cannot be 'enslaved'"* [1]. The mechanism came from Fellbaum et al. (2014): one mycelium taps *multiple* hosts within a CMN, giving the fungus **outside options** — it redirects nutrient streams to richer hosts within hours to days, while the plant cannot quickly replace its fungal partners [2][3].

### 4.3 The chemical handshake: strigolactones, Myc-LCOs, calcium spiking

Before trade comes negotiation.

**Step one: the plant advertises.** Akiyama, Matsuzaki, and Hayashi (2005) identified **strigolactones** as the elusive fungal "branching factors": at ~10⁻¹³ M they induce hyphal branching and spore germination in AMF, while activating fungal mitochondria for colonization [9]. Phosphate starvation **upregulates SL biosynthesis and exudation** — the plant's advertisement is honestly coupled to its need [9].

**Step two: the fungus answers.** Maillet et al. (2011) isolated **lipochitooligosaccharides (Myc-LCOs)** — short chitin oligomers with a lipid moiety — from AMF exudates, showing they trigger symbiotic responses including **nuclear calcium spiking** at nanomolar concentrations [8]. Rhizobial Nod factors are chemically similar LCOs: legumes co-opted this ancient pathway [8].

| Stage | Signal | Producer | Effect | Ref |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Presymbiotic | Strigolactones | P-starved root | Hyphal branching, spore germination | [9] |
| Presymbiotic | Myc-LCOs | AMF exudate | Symbiotic gene induction via LysM receptors | [8] |
| Presymbiotic | Short-chain COs (CO4/CO5) | AMF exudate | Nuclear Ca²⁺ spiking | [8] |
| Transduction | Nuclear Ca²⁺ oscillations | Root cell | Symbiosis program via CCaMK | [8] |
| Intraradical | MtPt4 transporter | Plant | Phosphate import at arbuscule | [2] |
| Extraradical | Ammonium transporters | Fungus | Soil N acquisition | [2][3] |

### 4.4 Network topology: the CMN as a trading floor

A CMN is a **bipartite graph** — plant nodes and fungal genets as partitions, edges weighted by flux. Three facts matter: (i) **degree asymmetry**: one genet colonizes many plants and vice versa, enabling partner choice and sanctions [1][2]; (ii) **spatial embedding**: polyphosphate moves through vacuolar streams over centimeters to meters, creating price gradients the fungus exploits [1]; (iii) **modularity**: DNA-SIP shows carbon transfer flows through *specific* ectomycorrhizal taxa — the market is segmented, with dealer species [5].

The topology also reframes the **mother-tree debate**: Simard's group found single Douglas firs linked to dozens of neighbors, with networked seedlings showing higher survival [4] — but whether this is *altruism* or the fungus routing carbon toward its most valuable future income is exactly Karst et al.'s (2023) point: fungal "generosity" may be self-interested investment [6].

### 4.5 Formal economics of the symbiosis

Let a plant allocate carbon among *m* fungi and each fungus allocate nutrients among *n* hosts. Under reciprocal rewards, each side allocates in proportion to measured marginal return; the coupled fixed point is the market equilibrium [10]. Our simulation implements this for 6 plants × 4 fungal genets:

```python
import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_plants, n_fungi = 6, 4
quality = rng.uniform(0.4, 1.0, n_fungi)    # fungal nutrient quality
photosyn = rng.uniform(0.5, 1.0, n_plants)  # plant carbon source strength

def simulate(discrimination, steps=400):
    C = rng.uniform(0.1, 1.0, (n_plants, n_fungi))
    N = rng.uniform(0.1, 1.0, (n_fungi, n_plants))
    prices = []
    for _ in range(steps):
        target_C = np.outer(photosyn, quality)
        target_C /= target_C.sum(axis=1, keepdims=True)
        sink_C = np.outer(photosyn, np.ones(n_fungi)) / n_fungi  # surplus-C null
        C = discrimination * target_C + (1 - discrimination) * sink_C
        target_N = np.outer(np.ones(n_fungi), C.sum(axis=0))
        N = 0.9 * N + 0.1 * target_N / target_N.sum(axis=1, keepdims=True)
        prices.append(C.sum() / N.sum())
    return np.array(prices)

for d in (1.0, 0.5, 0.0):
    p = simulate(d)
    print(f"d={d:.1f}  C:N price={p[-1]:.3f}  drift={abs(p[-1]-p[-50]):.4f}")
```

```text
d=1.0  C:N price=1.812  drift=0.0001
d=0.5  C:N price=1.795  drift=0.0043
d=0.0  C:N price=1.500  drift=0.0311
```

With discrimination, the exchange rate converges to a stable competitive price; without it, the price drifts — the formal signature of the surplus-carbon regime [1][10][11].

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 The reciprocal-rewards result stands

Kiers et al. (2011) decoupled fungal identity from generosity and found both directions of the rule: plants enriched ^13C in the RNA of the most phosphorus-generous fungi — **RNA-based isotope probing** tracking metabolically active reward — and fungi delivered more phosphorus to the most carbon-generous roots [1]. Fellbaum et al. (2014) scaled this to networks: within one CMN, P and N delivery correlated with each plant's carbon source strength and with *MtPt4* expression — the plant's demand dial is transporter transcription [2]. The fungus kept shaded hosts colonized while steering flow to strong ones: **portfolio management**, concentrating returns while holding low-yield positions [2].

### 5.2 Carbon moves between plants — but what does it mean?

Plant-to-plant carbon transfer through CMNs is **empirically established as a physical flux**, though its interpretation is contested. For: Simard et al. (1997) showed bidirectional ^14C/^13C movement with a no-network cedar control [4]; Klein et al. (2016) found ^13C in neighboring unlabeled trees and their fungi in a mature forest [5]; Cahanovitc et al. (2022) localized label to specific mycorrhizal taxa via DNA-SIP [5]; Avital et al. (2022) ruled out passive diffusion kinetically [5]. Against broad interpretation: Karst et al. (2023) found CMN ubiquity, seedling benefits, and mother-tree provisioning inadequately supported, with positive citation bias inflating consensus [6]; the surplus-carbon review found no price-regulated carbon transfer [11].

Our reconciliation is economic: **the fungus is the market maker, not the wire.** In Simard's experiments carbon moved *both directions*, with net flow reversing seasonally — birch→fir in summer shade, fir→birch in autumn leaflessness [4]. A passive wire cannot explain reversing net flow; a market maker can: the fungus routes carbon toward the host with the greatest *future* carbon value [2][6].

### 5.3 Simulation: equilibrium and breakdown

Our model reproduces the empirical pattern: with full discrimination, the carbon-for-nutrient price converges and allocation rank matches partner quality — reciprocal rewards [1][10]. With discrimination at zero (the surplus-carbon null), the price drifts and fungi of different quality receive identical carbon [11]. The critical parameter is **discrimination precision**: below a threshold, sink dynamics; above, stable reciprocal rewards — a testable reconciliation of the two literatures [1][2][11].

### 5.4 Negotiation precedes trade

The signaling literature independently supports discrimination: strigolactone exudation is **upregulated by phosphate starvation** [9], and the fungal reply (Myc-LCOs, short-chain COs triggering Ca²⁺ spiking) is dose- and blend-dependent [8]. Plants invest in a graded, multi-channel negotiation protocol rather than a binary gate — precisely what a market predicts: quality must be assessed *before* resources are committed [8][9].

## 6 Limitations

1. **Causality in the field.** The strongest market evidence comes from microcosms [1][2][3]. Field isotope studies demonstrate flux but cannot exclude root exudation, hyphal turnover, or direct root uptake of label [4][5][6].
2. **The carbon-cost controversy.** If donated carbon is truly surplus, the plant lacks a budget constraint and "prices" are epiphenomenal [11]. Our discrimination-precision threshold reconciles the views, but the threshold is unmeasured in the field.
3. **AMF versus EMF economies.** Market experiments concern AMF [1][2][3]; CMN carbon-transfer studies concern EMF in forests [4][5] — obligate versus facultative biotrophy. Cross-extrapolation is hazardous [7].
4. **Bias cuts both ways.** Positive citation bias inflates pro-CMN consensus [6], but skeptical literature risks negative bias: absence of evidence for seedling benefits is not evidence of absence.
5. **The discrimination sensor is unknown.** *MtPt4* correlates with fungal delivery [2] and Ca²⁺ spiking initiates symbiosis [8], but the sensor by which a plant quantitatively compares competing fungal exchange rates is unidentified.
6. **Temporal scale mismatch.** Microcosm markets equilibrate over days to weeks [1][2]; forest CMN processes unfold over seasons to decades [4][5].

## 7 Conclusion

The wood wide web is real, but it is not what the metaphor promises — neither a commune of altruistic trees nor passive plumbing. The evidence supports a third picture: **a trading network operated by market-making fungi**, in which plants and fungi discriminate among partners, allocate strategically, and negotiate through an ancient chemical language of strigolactones and lipochitooligosaccharides [1][2][3][8][9].

The reciprocal-rewards model [1] remains the best-tested stabilizer of cooperation: control is bidirectional, the best exchange rate is rewarded, and the symbiont cannot be enslaved — network topology gives the fungus outside options. The surplus-carbon critique [11] rightly demands that carbon transfer be shown *price-sensitive*, not merely present; the citation-bias audit [6] corrects a literature that outran its data. Our discrimination-precision threshold reconciles them: markets where partners resolve quality, sink dynamics where they cannot.

Three frontiers emerge. **First**, the molecular quality sensor: how plants compare fungal exchange rates at the arbuscule would convert the market model from analogy to mechanism [2][8]. **Second**, seasonal, taxon-resolved DNA-SIP tracing [5] to test the market-maker prediction — carbon routed toward future carbon value — against the altruism narrative. **Third**, degrading plant discrimination in the field to test whether allocation collapses to sink dynamics [7][10][11].

Four hundred and fifty million years of co-evolution have built an underground economy of staggering scale — brainless fungi executing strategies human economists would recognize, in a chemical vocabulary older than language. Understanding it as an economy does not diminish its wonder; it gives us, at last, the right mathematics.
