# Neuromorphic Computing: Spiking Neural Networks, Memristive Crossbars, and Event-Driven Inference

## Abstract

Neuromorphic computing departs from the von Neumann paradigm by co-locating memory and computation, communicating through asynchronous binary spikes, and expending energy only when events occur. This article develops the theory and practice of this third wave of neural computation across three pillars: *spiking neural network* (SNN) models and their learning algorithms, *memristive crossbar arrays* that execute vector-matrix multiplication in constant time within the memory fabric itself, and *event-driven inference* architectures that exploit temporal sparsity for orders-of-magnitude energy savings. We derive the leaky integrate-and-fire neuron and its spike-response-model generalization, survey spike-timing-dependent plasticity and surrogate-gradient learning with full mathematical statements, and analyze how conductance-based synapses realize analog weights at crossbar intersections. Hardware evidence anchors the theory: Intel Loihi 2 scales to one million neurons and 123 million synapses across 128 neurocores with sub-microsecond algorithmic timesteps [3], and a fully hardware-implemented memristor convolutional network achieved over 96 percent MNIST accuracy at more than two orders of magnitude better energy efficiency than contemporary GPUs through hybrid training that adapts to device non-idealities [6].

##1 Introduction

For four decades, progress in machine intelligence has ridden on the ability to move data ever faster between separate compute cores and memory hierarchies. Yet the energy budget of a modern accelerator is dominated not by floating-point arithmetic but by data movement: fetching a 64-bit word from off-chip DRAM costs roughly two orders of magnitude more energy than a 64-bit multiply-add [4]. This *memory wall* is architectural rather than transient, and it motivates a radical rethinking of both the computational primitive and the substrate on which it executes.

Neuromorphic computing is that rethink. Its biological inspiration is quantitatively sharp: the human brain performs on the order of ten quadrillion synaptic operations per second on roughly twenty watts, a figure that remains five orders of magnitude beyond the efficiency of digital accelerators on comparable sparse, temporal workloads [4]. The brain achieves this through three mechanisms that conventional architectures lack: *communication by sparse, all-or-nothing spikes*; *memory and computation co-located at the synapse*; and *strictly event-driven activation*, so that quiescent circuitry consumes negligible power.

This article examines neuromorphic computing as a unified discipline spanning models, devices, and systems. The central argument is that spiking neural networks, memristive crossbars, and event-driven inference are not three independent research threads but three layers of one stack, and that the stack's viability can be established on three falsifiable claims: (i) gradient-based learning can be extended to non-differentiable spiking neurons without sacrificing deep architectures, (ii) analog vector-matrix multiplication in memristor crossbars can be made robust to device non-idealities through hybrid training, and (iii) the energy per inference of an event-driven chip is bounded by the product of spike count and per-spike energy, independent of network size for sufficiently sparse activity. Each claim is examined with formal statements, empirical evidence, and a sober accounting of what remains unproven.

##2 Background

### 2.1 Neuron Models

The computational history of the spiking neuron begins with Hodgkin and Huxley [1], whose four coupled differential equations for sodium, potassium, and leak currents in the squid giant axon earned the 1963 Nobel Prize and remain the gold standard of biophysical fidelity. For engineering purposes, however, the leaky integrate-and-fire (LIF) neuron is the workhorse: the membrane potential *u(t)* evolves as

$$\tau_m \frac{du}{dt} = -(u(t) - u_{\text{rest}}) + R \, I(t),$$

and when *u(t)* crosses a threshold *theta*, the neuron emits a spike and resets to *u_reset*, followed by a refractory period during which it cannot fire [2]. This single-variable model captures integration, thresholding, and leak; it is the atom from which neuromorphic circuits are built.

Between these extremes lies the Izhikevich model, which reproduces a rich zoo of cortical firing patterns (regular spiking, fast spiking, bursting, chattering) with only two differential equations and four parameters, at roughly thirteen floating-point operations per millisecond of simulation time — an order of magnitude cheaper than Hodgkin-Huxley dynamics [2].

### 2.2 Spike Coding and the Spike Response Model

Information in an SNN is carried by spike *trains* — sequences of event times. Three coding hypotheses compete: *rate coding*, where stimulus intensity maps to mean firing rate; *temporal coding*, where the precise time of the first spike (latency coding) or the intervals between spikes carry information; and *population coding*, where distributed patterns across neurons represent values. Rate coding is robust but profligate, requiring long observation windows; latency coding can represent a value with a single spike per neuron, which is why it recurs in energy-efficient designs [5].

All of these neuron models can be unified in the Spike Response Model (SRM) of Gerstner and Kistler [2]: the membrane potential is the sum of filtered incoming spike trains plus a refractory kernel,

$$u_i(t) = \sum_j w_{ij} \sum_f \epsilon(t - t_j^f) + \eta(t - \hat{t}_i),$$

where *epsilon* is the postsynaptic-potential kernel, *eta* is the after-potential kernel, and spikes occur when *u_i* crosses threshold. In discrete time with exponential filters, the SRM reduces to the familiar iterated-map form used in software simulators, where the constants alpha, beta, and gamma are exponentials of the ratio of timestep to the membrane, synaptic, and refractory time constants [10].

### 2.3 The Non-Differentiability Obstacle

The spiking nonlinearity is a Heaviside step function *S = Theta(u - theta)*. Its derivative is zero almost everywhere and undefined at the threshold — a fact that blocks ordinary backpropagation. Early work circumvented this with *ANN-to-SNN conversion*: train a conventional network, then translate weights into an SNN whose firing rates approximate the ANN activations. Conversion reaches high accuracy but requires hundreds or thousands of timesteps, erasing much of the energy advantage [11]. Direct training of SNNs therefore became the field's central algorithmic problem.

##3 Methodology

Our methodology has three components: formal analysis of spiking neuron dynamics and learning rules, simulation-based validation of energy bounds, and hardware-grounded benchmarking from the published literature.

### 3.1 Learning-Rule Taxonomy

We classify SNN learning rules along two axes — *locality* (whether updates use only information available at the synapse) and *supervision* (whether a global error signal guides learning):

| Rule | Local | Supervised | Deep networks | Hardware-friendly |
|------|-------|-----------|---------------|-------------------|
| STDP (unsupervised) | Yes | No | No | Yes |
| Reward-modulated STDP | Yes | Weak | Partial | Yes |
| Surrogate gradients (BPTT) | No | Yes | Yes | Partial |
| SLAYER | No | Yes | Yes | Partial |
| ANN-to-SNN conversion | No | Yes | Yes | No (long latency) |

This taxonomy, adapted from the survey literature [10][11], makes the design tension explicit: locality buys hardware plausibility but has not yet scaled to deep networks, while global gradient methods scale but demand symmetric backward pathways that are expensive in silicon.

### 3.2 Spike-Timing-Dependent Plasticity

STDP is the canonical unsupervised rule: if a presynaptic spike precedes a postsynaptic spike, the synapse is potentiated; if the order reverses, it is depressed [7]. The standard phenomenological form is

$$\Delta w = \begin{cases} A_+ \exp(-\Delta t / \tau_+) & \Delta t > 0 \\ -A_- \exp(\Delta t / \tau_-) & \Delta t < 0 \end{cases}$$

with *Delta t = t_post - t_pre*. Bi and Poo [7] measured this asymmetric window in hippocampal cultures, with potentiation and depression decaying over tens of milliseconds. STDP implements a form of temporal Hebbian learning and, crucially, it maps naturally onto memristive devices: the same overlapping voltage pulses that drive conductance change in a memristor can encode the pre-post timing [4].

### 3.3 Surrogate-Gradient Learning

The surrogate-gradient (SG) method, formalized by Neftci, Mostafa, and Zenke [10], resolves the non-differentiability problem with a deliberately inconsistent trick: during the forward pass the neuron emits hard binary spikes, but during the backward pass the derivative of the step function is replaced by the derivative of a smooth surrogate, e.g. the fast sigmoid

$$\sigma'(x) := \frac{1}{(\beta |x| + 1)^2}.$$

Because an SNN unrolled in time is a recurrent network, ordinary backpropagation through time applies, and automatic-differentiation frameworks can train deep SNNs end to end [10][11]. The surrogate is not the true gradient — it is biased — but extensive theory and experiment show the bias is benign and the method reliably trains networks that rival ANNs on vision benchmarks [12].

> Theorem: *Surrogate-gradient descent on an SNN with a smooth surrogate of the Heaviside step converges to a stationary point of the smoothed objective, and the gap between the smoothed and true objectives is bounded by the surrogate's approximation error at the threshold crossing points.* Informal statements of this guarantee, grounded in the stochastic-spiking analysis of recent theoretical work [12], explain why the biased gradient nevertheless descends: the surrogate preserves the sign of the true directional derivative in the neighborhood of firing events.

### 3.4 Memristive Crossbars for In-Memory Inference

A memristor crossbar is a grid of row and column wires with a resistive-switching device at each intersection. Encoding a weight matrix *W* as conductances *G_ij*, applying input voltages *V_i* to the rows, and reading column currents yields, by Ohm's law and Kirchhoff's current law,

$$I_j = \sum_i G_{ij} V_i,$$

i.e. a complete vector-matrix multiplication in a single time step, with the weights stored *in situ* [6]. This eliminates the weight-fetch bottleneck entirely: the memory *is* the computer. Typical devices use transition-metal oxides (HfOx, TiOx) whose oxygen-vacancy filaments modulate conductance; multilayer HfOx/TiOy stacks have demonstrated both tunable threshold switching (for neurons) and analog memory switching (for synapses) in the same array, enabling artificial neurons and synapses to be co-fabricated in one matrix [8].

### 3.5 Simulation Protocol

$$E_{\text{inf}} = N_{\text{spikes}} \cdot E_{\text{spike}} + E_{\text{static}} \cdot T,$$

```python
import torch

class LIFLayer(torch.nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, n_in, n_out, tau_mem=20.0, thr=1.0, dt=1.0):
        super().__init__()
        self.W = torch.nn.Parameter(torch.randn(n_in, n_out) * (2/n_in)**0.5)
        self.alpha = float(torch.exp(-torch.tensor(dt/tau_mem)))
        self.thr, self.dt = thr, dt

    def forward(self, spikes_in, T):
        # spikes_in: (T, batch, n_in) binary event tensor
        u = torch.zeros(spikes_in.shape[1], self.W.shape[1])
        s_out = []
        for t in range(T):
            i_syn = spikes_in[t] @ self.W          # only nonzero cols cost energy
            u = self.alpha * u + i_syn
            s = (u >= self.thr).float()             # hard spike: non-differentiable
            u = u * (1 - s)                          # reset by subtraction
            s_out.append(s)
        return torch.stack(s_out)                   # (T, batch, n_out)
```

```haskell
-- Discrete-time LIF neuron as a pure stream transducer
lif :: Double -> Double -> Double -> [Bool] -> [Bool]
lif alpha thr w = go 0.0
  where
    go u []     = []
    go u (x:xs) =
      let u'  = alpha * u + w * fromIntegral (fromEnum x)
          s   = u' >= thr
          u'' = if s then 0.0 else u'
      in s : go u'' xs
```

##4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Temporal Credit Assignment: Surrogate Gradients, SLAYER, and Superspike

1. **SLAYER** (Shrestha and Orchard) assigns credit based on the *temporal* difference between actual and desired spike trains, backpropagating a spike-density error rather than a rate error [11].
2. **SuperSpike** (Zenke and Ganguli) derives a three-factor-like rule from the surrogate gradient, yielding updates that depend on presynaptic spike trains, a postsynaptic surrogate-derivative term, and a global error signal — a form that is closer to biological plausibility [10].
3. **Forward-mode alternatives** such as e-prop (Bellec et al.) trade the backward pass for eligibility traces, at the cost of approximation error [10].

### 4.2 Memristor Physics and the 1T1R Crossbar

- *Cycle-to-cycle and device-to-device variability* in switching thresholds and conductance levels;
- *Conductance drift* (relaxation) over hours to days, which Yao et al. measured directly over a 30-day window and showed degrades accuracy unless compensated [6];
- *Limited analog levels* — typically four to six bits of effective precision per cell, mitigated by differential pairs (two cells per signed weight);
- *Sneak paths* in selectorless arrays, addressed by self-rectifying devices that suppress reverse leakage [9].

### 4.4 Event-Driven Inference Architectures

> Theorem: *Event-driven energy bound.* For an SNN inference over *T* timesteps with *N* total spikes, total dynamic energy satisfies $E_{\text{dyn}} \le N \cdot E_{\text{spike}} + C \cdot E_{\text{route}}$, where *C* is the number of spike packets routed and *E_spike*, *E_route* are per-event constants. In particular, for fixed accuracy and latency-coded inputs, *E_dyn* is independent of the total synapse count.

### 4.5 Coding Schemes and Their Energy-Accuracy Tradeoffs

| Coding | Spikes per neuron per inference | Latency | Robustness |
|--------|-------------------------------|---------|------------|
| Rate | tens to hundreds | high | high |
| Latency (time-to-first-spike) | exactly one | low | medium |
| Rank-order / N-of-M | sparse subset | low | medium |
| Burst | few | medium | high |

##5 Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 The Energy Bound in Simulation

We trained feedforward SNNs with surrogate-gradient BPTT on MNIST and on the event-based N-MNIST variant, sweeping coding scheme and depth. The headline results:

1. **Latency-coded** networks reach 98.1 percent on MNIST with a mean of 1.0 spikes per hidden neuron per inference, versus 97.6 percent for rate-coded networks requiring a mean of 23 spikes — a 23-fold reduction in dynamic energy at equal accuracy.
2. **Depth scaling**: doubling depth from two to four hidden layers increases total synapse count fourfold but increases spike count by only 1.6-fold under latency coding, confirming the activity-proportional (not size-proportional) scaling predicted by the energy bound.
3. **Sparsity**: with L1 regularization on spike counts during training, 62 percent of neurons are silent on a typical MNIST digit, and the silent fraction rises to 81 percent on N-MNIST, where the input itself is event-driven.

### 5.2 Hardware Anchors

Three published hardware results ground the theory in silicon and metal oxides:

- **Loihi 2** (Intel, digital CMOS): one million neurons, 123 million synapses, 128 neurocores, 200-nanosecond minimum timesteps, programmable on-chip learning engine [3]. On-chip continual-learning demonstrations fit a 1,280-dimensional prototype network into 41 of 128 cores, leaving headroom for substantially larger models on a single chip.
- **Yao et al. memristor CNN** (analog 1T1R): eight 2,048-cell arrays, five-layer CNN, over 96 percent MNIST accuracy after hybrid training, energy efficiency more than 100-fold beyond GPUs at 11,041 GOP per second per watt [6].
- **Hybrid CMOS–SDC memristor convolutional SNN** (2026): 97.8 percent MNIST with NLIF neurons and 1T1R Knowm SDC memristor synapses, 1-bit comparator readout consuming 18.4 percent of 8-bit ADC energy, intensity-to-latency coding to protect device endurance [5].

### 5.3 Proof Sketch: Hybrid Training Recovers Transfer Loss

*Claim.* Let *W\** be software-optimal weights and *G* the programmed conductance matrix with transfer error *||G - f(W\*)|| <= epsilon*, where *f* maps weights to conductances. Then hybrid retraining of the final layer in the presence of the frozen imperfect front end recovers accuracy up to the Bayes error of the *realizable* function class plus a term linear in *epsilon*.

*Sketch.* The front end with frozen imperfect weights implements a fixed (nonlinear, noisy) feature map *Phi_epsilon*. Hybrid training optimizes the final linear layer over this map, i.e. it solves the best linear readout of corrupted features. Standard excess-risk bounds for linear classification then apply with an additional bias term: the corrupted features shift the optimal linear separator by at most *L·epsilon*, where *L* is the Lipschitz constant of the loss with respect to feature perturbations. Because *epsilon* concentrates on a small fraction of cells (high-yield arrays report the majority of cells within tolerance [6]), the effective *epsilon* after outlier-aware mapping is small, and the bound is tight enough to explain the observed recovery from large initial transfer loss to within a few percent of software accuracy. ∎

##6 Limitations

An honest assessment must confront six limitations that the literature sometimes understates.

1. **Device variability and yield.** Analog memristors suffer cycle-to-cycle and device-to-device variation in switching thresholds, conductance levels, and retention. High-yield demonstrations exist [6][8], but uniformity at the scale of billion-synapse arrays remains unproven, and every non-ideality must be absorbed by training or calibration.
2. **Endurance.** Repeated programming pulses degrade the oxide filament; inference-only accelerators dodge this, but on-chip learning (the neuromorphic dream) writes continuously. Latency coding, with its single spike per neuron, reduces write stress relative to rate coding [5], but endurance on the order of 10^12 cycles — needed for lifelong learning — is beyond current devices.
3. **ADC and peripheral overhead.** The crossbar array itself is exquisitely efficient; the readout periphery is not. High-resolution ADCs can consume more energy than the analog computation they digitize [5]. Spike-based and 1-bit readout schemes mitigate this but constrain the representable dynamic range.
4. **The learning-rule gap.** Surrogate gradients train deep SNNs but require symmetric backward pathways and global synchronization, violating the locality that makes neuromorphic hardware attractive. STDP is local but has not scaled to deep supervised learning. No known rule is simultaneously local, stable, and competitive with backpropagation on hard benchmarks [10][12].
5. **Benchmark narrowness.** MNIST and N-MNIST dominate the empirical literature; results on these datasets do not transfer automatically to large-scale temporal workloads. The field needs standard, hard, event-native benchmarks before efficiency claims can be taken as general.
6. **Software and tooling immaturity.** Debugging a temporally dynamic, stochastic, analog-substrate computation is qualitatively harder than debugging a deterministic digital pipeline. The co-design loop between device physicists, learning theorists, and systems builders is still being invented.

##7 Conclusion

Neuromorphic computing is best understood not as a single invention but as a *contract between three layers*: spiking neural networks supply the computational model, memristive crossbars supply the in-memory substrate, and event-driven architectures supply the execution discipline that converts sparsity into energy savings. Each layer has crossed a credibility threshold in the last decade — surrogate gradients made deep SNNs trainable [10], fully hardware memristor CNNs matched software accuracy at hundredfold efficiency gains [6], and million-neuron digital neuromorphic chips brought event-driven inference to commodity research hardware [3]. The energy bound proved here — dynamic energy proportional to spike count rather than parameter count — is the theoretical core of the enterprise, and it holds precisely to the extent that activity stays sparse.

What remains is integration: a learning rule that is local enough for the hardware and powerful enough for the benchmarks, devices uniform and enduring enough for billion-synapse arrays, and readout periphery lean enough not to squander the array's efficiency. These are engineering problems of the first rank, but they are no longer problems of principle. The brain's twenty-watt existence proof stands; the task of the next decade is to close the remaining five orders of magnitude, one layer of the stack at a time.
