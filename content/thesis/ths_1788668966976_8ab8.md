# Persistent and Confluently Persistent Data Structures: Path Copying, Fat Nodes, Functional Treaps, and Retroactive Updates

## Abstract

Persistence transforms an ephemeral data structure — one in which every update destroys the past — into a structure that retains all historical versions for query. This thesis develops the persistence hierarchy of Driscoll, Sarnak, Sleator, and Tarjan [1]: partial persistence, where only the newest version may be modified; full persistence, where every version may be both read and forked; and confluent persistence [3], which permits *melding* two versions, making the version graph a directed acyclic graph. We analyze the two classical implementation strategies — *path copying* and the *fat-node* method — and the node-splitting transformation that combines them to achieve O(1) amortized time and space per update with O(1) access slowdown for bounded-indegree structures [1][10]. We then show how purely functional programming obtains persistence by construction, with functional treaps [11] and persistent segment trees as case studies, with split/merge implementations. Finally, we examine the Fiat–Kaplan transformation [3][5], confluently persistent tries [4], and *retroactive* data structures [6][7], in which the update timeline itself is editable. Central amortized and expected-case bounds are proved, and open problems are identified.

## 1. Introduction

Ordinary data structures are *ephemeral*: an update overwrites the old state, and the previous version is irrecoverable. A *persistent* data structure preserves every version it has ever held and permits queries against any of them [1][10]. The distinction is not merely a convenience for undo buffers. Persistence underlies version-control systems, multiversion concurrency control in databases, backtracking search, and the dimension-reduction technique in computational geometry whereby a persistent structure turns a dynamic problem into a static one [1][2].

The modern taxonomy of persistence originates with Driscoll, Sarnak, Sleator, and Tarjan [1], who distinguished three degrees:

- **Partial persistence.** All versions may be accessed, but only the newest version may be modified. The version graph is a *path*.
- **Full persistence.** Every version may be both accessed and modified; updating an old version forks a new branch. The version graph is a *tree*.
- **Confluent persistence.** In addition to full persistence, two versions may be *melded* — combined into a single new version. The version graph is a *directed acyclic graph (DAG)* [3][5].

This thesis is organized around the central question: *how much does persistence cost?* We measure cost along three axes — the slowdown imposed on access operations, the amortized time of updates, and the space consumed per version — and we trace how each successive degree of the hierarchy demands new algorithmic ideas. Along the way we encounter a striking orthogonal axis: *retroactivity* [6], in which the timeline of updates itself becomes editable, and queries may be posed about arbitrary moments in the past.

Our contributions are: (i) a self-contained development of path copying, fat nodes, and the DSST node-splitting transformation with its amortized analysis; (ii) a functional treatment of treaps with split/merge as the canonical persistently-updatable balanced tree, with reference implementations and expected-depth proofs; (iii) an analysis of the Fiat–Kaplan transformation for confluent persistence and the effective-depth measure governing its overhead; and (iv) a survey of retroactive data structures, from the Demaine–Iacono–Langerman framework [6] through hierarchical checkpointing [7] to modern lower bounds [8][12].

## 2. Background and Related Work

The study of persistence began in earnest with Driscoll et al. [1], whose 1989 paper gave systematic, efficient transformations turning any linked data structure of bounded indegree into a partially or fully persistent one with only constant-factor overhead. Their work built on earlier special-purpose constructions, notably Sarnak and Tarjan's persistent search trees for planar point location — a landmark application in which persistence over a sweep line converts a dynamic geometric problem into a static one, achieving O(log n) query time with O(n) space and O(n log n) preprocessing [1][10].

Okasaki's *Purely Functional Data Structures* [9] reframed persistence from the functional-programming perspective: in a language without destructive assignment, *every* data structure is automatically fully persistent, because updates create new values rather than mutating old ones. Okasaki's deeper contribution was to show that classical *amortized analysis* must be rebuilt for the persistent setting: the banker's method, which relies on each saved credit being spent at most once, is unsound when a single version can be forked into many. The physicist's method, combined with lazy evaluation and careful debit invariants, survives [9].

Several landmark results refined the picture. Dietz gave a fully persistent array with O(lg lg m) access time, essentially tight in the cell-probe model [5]. For confluence, Kaplan, Okasaki, and Tarjan designed simple confluently persistent catenable lists [5], while Fiat and Kaplan gave the first *general* transformation for confluent persistence [3], later simplified by Collette, Iacono, and Langerman [5]. On the applied side, Demaine, Langerman, and Price built confluently persistent tries supporting version-control meld operations in O(log n) time [4].

Orthogonally, Demaine, Iacono, and Langerman introduced *retroactive* data structures [6]: instead of merely preserving the past, these structures allow *editing* it. A partially retroactive structure supports inserting or deleting update operations at arbitrary points in the timeline while queries remain restricted to the present; a fully retroactive structure additionally supports queries at any point in time. Hierarchical checkpointing converts partial to full retroactivity with polylogarithmic overhead [7], while lower bounds — including separations between the models [12] and OMv-hardness for retroactive graph problems [8] — delineate what is achievable.

## 3. Methodology / Formal Framework

We work in the **linked data structure model** of [1]: memory consists of nodes, each holding a constant amount of data and a constant number of pointers to other nodes. The *indegree* of a node is the number of pointers referencing it; we assume indegree bounded by a constant *d* wherever the DSST transformation is applied. An *ephemeral* update mutates nodes in place; a *persistent* update must leave all previously reachable configurations intact.

> **Definition 1 (Version graph).** Let each version be a node in a directed graph, with an edge from version *u* to version *v* iff *v* was created by an update applied to *u*. For partial persistence the version graph is a path; for full persistence it is a tree; for confluent persistence it is a DAG [3][5].

We measure three costs, where *n* is the structure size, *m* the number of updates, and *U*, *Q* the ephemeral update and query times:

| Dimension | Ephemeral | Partially persistent | Fully persistent | Confluently persistent |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Version graph | single state | path | tree | DAG |
| Versions modifiable | newest only | newest only | all | all, plus meld |
| Access slowdown (target) | 1 | O(1) | O(1) | polylog |
| Update overhead (target) | 1 | O(1) amortized | O(1) amortized | depends on effective depth |

*Amortized analysis under persistence* requires care. We use the potential method on the version *tree*: Φ must be defined per version such that updates to one branch do not invalidate the accounting of another — the key technical hurdle in [1].

For randomized structures we use *expected-case* analysis over the internal coin flips (priorities), independent of the version structure: a functional treap's shape distribution is unchanged by persistence because persistence only *shares* nodes, never altering the random choices [11].

Finally, retroactivity is formalized as an ADT transformation [6]: given an ADT *A*, *Partial-Retro(A)* supports `Insert_u(t, x)` (insert update *u(x)* at time *t* in the timeline) and `Delete(t)`, with queries evaluated at the present; *Full-Retro(A)* additionally supports `Query(t, q)`, evaluating query *q* after the first *t* updates of the (possibly edited) timeline.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Path Copying and the Fat-Node Method

The two naive strategies for persistence expose a fundamental tension between update cost and access cost [10].

**Path copying.** Before modifying a node, copy it, then recursively copy every node pointing to it, cascading up to the root. Versions share unmodified subtrees, and an array of roots indexed by timestamp identifies each version in O(log m). For a balanced BST an update copies O(log n) nodes — but in general the cascade can copy the entire structure, giving O(n) worst-case time and space per update [10]. Path copying yields full persistence for free: updating an old version starts a new cascade from that version's root.

**Fat nodes.** Instead of copying, widen each node to store its entire *modification history*: every field update appends a (timestamp, new value) pair to the node. An update costs O(1) amortized space (one history entry, in a growable array) [10]. The price is paid at access time: traversing a node requires binary-searching its history for the latest entry not exceeding the query timestamp, imposing a multiplicative O(log m) slowdown on every access. For full persistence, each node stores a *version tree* rather than a linear history, and the lookup becomes a tree search — again O(log m) per node visited [10].

> **Theorem (Naive trade-off, folklore [10]).** Path copying gives O(log m) additive access overhead but O(n) worst-case update cost; fat nodes give O(1) amortized update cost but O(log m) multiplicative access slowdown.

Neither dominates. The breakthrough of [1] was to *combine* them so that each pays only its cheap cost.

### 4.2 The Driscoll–Sarnak–Sleator–Tarjan Node-Splitting Transformation

The DSST method [1] equips every node with a single **modification box** — a fat node with capacity exactly one — plus the path-copying cascade as a fallback:

1. To modify a node, if its modification box is empty, store the modification (field, new value, timestamp) in the box: O(1) time and space.
2. If the box is full, *copy* the node using only the latest field values, apply the modification to the fresh copy (whose box starts empty), and cascade the pointer change to the node's parents — recursively, until the root, which is appended to a sorted array of version roots.

The bounded-indegree assumption (*d* incoming pointers per node) is crucial: a copy invalidates at most *d* parent pointers, so each copy triggers at most *d* further box-fill attempts, and boxes are "full" only a constant fraction of the time.

> **Theorem (Driscoll–Sarnak–Sleator–Tarjan [1]).** Any linked data structure with indegree bounded by a constant *d* can be made partially persistent with O(1) amortized time and space per update and O(1) slowdown per access; the fully persistent variant achieves the same amortized update bounds with version-tree indexing of roots.

Concretely, this yields persistent balanced BSTs with O(log n) time per insertion/deletion, O(log n) access, and only O(1) amortized extra space per update [1] — the persistent analogue of the ephemeral bounds, and the foundation of the planar point-location structure mentioned in §2.

### 4.3 Functional Treaps: Split, Merge, and Persistence by Construction

In a purely functional setting [9], persistence requires no transformation at all: updates *construct* new nodes and share the rest. The treap — a binary search tree keyed by *key* and heap-ordered by random *priority* [11] — is the ideal vehicle, because its entire update repertoire reduces to `split` and `merge`, both naturally persistent. A treap node stores `(key, priority, left, right)` with the BST invariant on keys and the min-heap invariant on priorities, giving expected O(log n) depth regardless of key insertion order [11].

```haskell
data Treap k p = Empty | Node k p (Treap k p) (Treap k p)

-- Split into (keys < k) and (keys >= k); persistent: allocates O(h) new nodes.
split :: Ord k => k -> Treap k p -> (Treap k p, Treap k p)
split _ Empty = (Empty, Empty)
split k (Node x p l r)
  | k < x     = let (l1, l2) = split k l in (l1, Node x p l2 r)
  | otherwise = let (r1, r2) = split k r in (Node x p l r1, r2)

-- Merge two treaps with all keys of t1 < all keys of t2.
merge :: Ord p => Treap k p -> Treap k p -> Treap k p
merge Empty t = t
merge t Empty = t
merge t1@(Node k1 p1 l1 r1) t2@(Node k2 p2 l2 r2)
  | p1 < p2   = Node k1 p1 l1 (merge r1 t2)
  | otherwise = Node k2 p2 (merge t1 l2) r2
```

Every recursive call allocates fresh `Node` constructors along the *spine* of the recursion while reusing untouched subtrees by reference — path copying performed implicitly by the language runtime. Insert is `split`-then-`merge`, delete is `split`-twice-then-`merge`, and each version is simply a root pointer; old roots remain fully usable, so the structure is *fully persistent by construction* [9][11]. Because persistence never perturbs priorities, the expected-depth analysis of [11] carries over verbatim (see §5.2).

The same pattern yields the **persistent segment tree**, the workhorse of offline range-query problems:

```python
class PSTNode:
    __slots__ = ("left", "right", "val")
    def __init__(self, left, right, val):
        self.left, self.right, self.val = left, right, val

NULL = PSTNode(None, None, 0)

def pst_update(node, l, r, idx, delta):
    """Return the root of a new version with A[idx] += delta; O(log N) new nodes."""
    if l == r:
        return PSTNode(None, None, node.val + delta)
    mid = (l + r) // 2
    if idx <= mid:
        new_left = pst_update(node.left or NULL, l, mid, idx, delta)
        return PSTNode(new_left, node.right, node.val + delta)
    new_right = pst_update(node.right or NULL, mid + 1, r, idx, delta)
    return PSTNode(node.left, new_right, node.val + delta)

def pst_query(node, l, r, ql, qr):
    if node is None or qr < l or r < ql:
        return 0
    if ql <= l and r <= qr:
        return node.val
    mid = (l + r) // 2
    return pst_query(node.left, l, mid, ql, qr) + pst_query(node.right, mid + 1, r, ql, qr)
```

Each point update allocates O(log N) nodes along the root-to-leaf path; a query on version *v* runs on *v*'s root in O(log N). Storing one root per prefix of an array yields the classic static range-*k*th-order-statistic structure in O(n log n) space — persistence converting a *temporal* dimension (array prefixes) into a *structural* one [2], the discrete analogue of sweep-line persistence in computational geometry [1].

### 4.4 Confluent Persistence and the Fiat–Kaplan Transformation

Confluent persistence adds a **meld** operation: given versions *v₁* and *v₂*, produce a new version *v₃* combining both, with the version graph becoming a DAG [3]. The motivating application is version control — merging branches requires copying subtrees from one version into another — realized efficiently for tries by Demaine, Langerman, and Price [4]. Confluence introduces a new danger: *exponential version blowup*. Concatenating a deque with itself *u* times creates 2^u distinct root-to-version paths in the version DAG, and a naive representation explodes [5].

> **Definition (Effective depth [3][5]).** For a version *v* in the version DAG, *e(v) = 1 + log₂(P(v))*, where *P(v)* is the number of distinct paths from the initial version to *v*.

The transformation unravels the DAG into a tree (duplicating shared versions along distinct paths) and rebalances; the overhead of an operation creating version *v* is then bounded by *O(log U + max_v e(v))*, where *U* is the number of updates [5]. The deterministic variant of [3] can incur linear worst-case slowdown, while the randomized variant achieves polylogarithmic amortized slowdown at the cost of linear expected space per modification — and whether a *general* transformation with polylogarithmic time *and* near-constant space exists remains open [3][5].

For restricted structures the picture is far better. Kaplan, Okasaki, and Tarjan gave simple confluently persistent catenable lists with O(1) amortized concatenation [5], and the confluently persistent trie of [4] supports meld in O(log n) time with O(log n) new nodes, because tries share structure along key prefixes and meld duplicates only the O(log n) nodes on the merge frontier. Collette, Iacono, and Langerman later simplified the general theory substantially [5].

### 4.5 Retroactive Data Structures: Rewriting the Timeline

Persistence preserves the past; **retroactivity** edits it [6]. The model maintains a *timeline* — the sequence of update operations ever performed — and supports:

- `Insert_u(t, x)`: insert update *u(x)* at position *t* in the timeline;
- `Delete(t)`: remove the update at position *t*;
- queries, in the *partially* retroactive model, only at the present; in the *fully* retroactive model, `Query(t, q)` evaluates query *q* against the prefix of the timeline ending at *t* [6].

The models are separated: Demaine et al. exhibit structures with O(1) partially retroactive updates for which fully retroactive queries require Ω(log n) time in the cell-probe model [12] — partial retroactivity is provably easier. The canonical general transformation is **hierarchical checkpointing** [7]: a balanced BST over the timeline ("checkpoint tree") where each node stores a partially retroactive structure over its interval; an update touches O(log m) nodes and a query at time *t* merges O(log m) disjoint intervals. For time-fusible structures this yields O(A(m) log² m) amortized updates and O(T(m) log m + Q(m, k)) queries [7] — turning the original O(√m) overhead of [6] into polylogarithmic cost, while OMv-based lower bounds show fully retroactive graph problems cannot beat O(n^{1−ε}) per operation [8].

## 5. Empirical Analysis and Proofs

### 5.1 Amortized Analysis of Node Splitting

We sketch the potential argument of [1] for partial persistence. Let Φ(*v*) count the *full* modification boxes reachable from version *v*. An update fills at most *h* boxes along its traversal path; when a box is full, the node is copied at cost O(*d*), which *empties* the box and releases its potential. Each copy thus consumes the potential accumulated by the fills that caused it, giving O(1) amortized time and space per update. Access pays O(1): each node visit checks one box and compares one timestamp [1].

### 5.2 Expected Depth of Functional Treaps

> **Theorem (Seidel–Aragon [11]).** In a treap built from *n* keys with i.i.d. continuous random priorities, the expected depth of any node is O(log n), and split/merge run in expected O(log n) time.

*Proof sketch.* Node *x* is an ancestor of node *y* iff *x* has the minimum priority among keys in the interval between *x* and *y* — probability 1/(|rank(x) − rank(y)| + 1). Summing over all *x* gives expected depth Σ_{k=1}^{n} 1/k = H_n = O(log n). Split and merge each traverse the two *spines* whose lengths are bounded by the depths of the boundary keys, hence the same expectation [11]. Crucially, the persistent variants allocate exactly the nodes on these spines — so expected *space* per update is also O(log n), and the analysis is unaffected by versioning since priorities are immutable.

### 5.3 Comparative Complexity Summary

The following table collects the proven bounds (ephemeral baseline *U* update, *Q* query):

| Technique | Persistence | Update (amortized) | Access slowdown | Space / update | Constraint |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Fat node [10] | partial | O(1) | O(log m) mult. | O(1) | none |
| Path copying [10] | full | O(n) worst | O(log m) add. | O(n) worst | none |
| Node splitting [1] | partial/full | O(1) | O(1) | O(1) | indegree ≤ *d* |
| Functional treap [11][9] | full | O(log n) exp. | none | O(log n) exp. | purity |
| Persistent segtree [2] | partial | O(log n) | none | O(log n) | static key range |
| Fiat–Kaplan [3] | confluent | O(log U + e(v)) | polylog | O(e(v)) | pointer machine |
| Confluent trie [4] | confluent | O(log n) | O(1) | O(log n) | trie-structured |
| Checkpointing [7] | full retro. | O(A(m) log² m) | O(T(m) log m) | — | time-fusible |

### 5.4 Confluent Overhead in Practice

The effective-depth measure *e(v)* is the right complexity parameter for confluence [5]: structures whose melds keep the version DAG shallow (tries [4], catenable lists [5]) enjoy near-ephemeral bounds, while adversarial meld patterns (self-concatenation) force the Fiat–Kaplan transformation to pay for exponential path multiplicity. The empirical rule of thumb: *design the version DAG, not just the data structure* — confluence is cheap exactly when version sharing is disciplined.

## 6. Limitations and Open Problems

1. **Bounded indegree.** The DSST transformation [1] requires constant indegree; structures with unbounded sharing (e.g., DAG-shaped functional graphs) need alternative treatments or accept weaker bounds.
2. **Fully persistent arrays.** Dietz's O(lg lg m) bound is essentially optimal (cell-probe lower bound Ω(lg lg n) [5]), so RAM-like random access cannot be made persistent for free — a fundamental separation between pointer-based and array-based persistence.
3. **Confluent optimality.** The Fiat–Kaplan transformation's deterministic linear worst-case slowdown versus its randomized variant's linear space blowup [3] leaves open whether a general transformation achieves polylogarithmic time *and* O(1) amortized space. Collette et al. simplified but did not close this gap [5].
4. **Amortization under persistence.** Okasaki's dictum stands: banker's-method analyses are unsound with persistence, and even physicist's-method bounds require lazy evaluation with memoization to survive forking [9]. Many classical amortized structures (e.g., splay trees) still lack satisfactory fully persistent analyses.
5. **Retroactive hardness.** The OMv-based lower bounds [8] suggest fully retroactive dynamic graph problems are inherently expensive; the boundary between tractable and hard retroactive problems is largely unmapped.
6. **Systems costs.** Persistence multiplies allocation (GC pressure) and fragments locality; production systems (MVCC databases, persistent memory) pay constant factors the theory abstracts away. Bridging the RAM-model bounds with cache-aware persistent design remains engineering-heavy and theory-light.

## 7. Conclusion

From the path-copying/fat-node duality through the DSST node-splitting transformation [1], the functional purity of treaps [9][11], the DAG-shaped version histories of confluent persistence [3][4][5], to the timeline-editing power of retroactive structures [6][7], persistence has grown from a clever implementation trick into a deep theory of *how data structures relate to time*. The unifying lesson is that the version graph — path, tree, or DAG — is the true object of study, and each degree of the hierarchy exacts a precisely characterizable price: O(1) amortized for partial and full persistence under bounded indegree, effective-depth-governed overhead for confluence, and polylogarithmic-to-polynomial costs for retroactivity, with matching lower bounds [8][12] showing these prices are largely unavoidable.
