# Sparse Expert Models at Trillion Scale: Mixture-of-Experts Routing Collapse, Load Balancing via Auxiliary Losses, Expert-Choice vs Token-Choice, and MegaBlocks Sparse Kernels

##Abstract
Mixture-of-Experts promises to decouple parameter count from FLOPs, enabling trillion-parameter models where each token activates only a fraction of the network. In practice, naive scaling fails due to routing collapse: a small early advantage for a few experts compounds into catastrophic under-utilization of the rest. This work provides a comprehensive analysis of sparse expert models at trillion scale. We trace the historical evolution from GShard to Switch Transformer to GLaM and modern trillion-scale deployments like DBRX and Ring-1T, formalize why load imbalance emerges from non-differentiable TopK routing, and compare two families of mitigation—auxiliary balancing losses and auxiliary-loss-free bias adaptation. We contrast Token-Choice and Expert-Choice routing paradigms with respect to load guarantees, token dropping, causal masking, and expert specialization. Finally, we dissect MegaBlocks' block-sparse reformulation that eliminates token dropping while achieving up to 40% speedup over Tutel and 2.4x over Megatron-LM dense baselines. We conclude with empirical tradeoffs, open problems in specialization vs. balance, and systems requirements for next-generation sparse training.

##1 Intro
The dense scaling laws that powered GPT-3 and T5—*more parameters, more data, more compute*—hit a wall of economic infeasibility long before they hit a wall of statistical benefit. Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) breaks this coupling by making the model **sparsely activated**: for each token representation x_t, a router selects k << E experts out of E total, enabling models with >1e12 parameters while keeping FLOPs per token constant [1][2].

Yet this elegance hides a treacherous optimization dynamic. Unlike dense models, sparse models introduce a discrete, competitive assignment problem inside every forward pass. The router and the experts co-adapt in a way that is fundamentally unstable. Without intervention, training converges to a degenerate solution where **90% of tokens are routed to 2-3 experts** out of 128.

This thesis makes four contributions:

- **A taxonomy of collapse** — we distinguish *hard collapse*, *soft collapse*, and *pseudo-balancing* and show how they emerge from early-training variance.
- **A unified analysis of load balancing** — from the N sum f_i P_i Switch loss [2] to the recent auxiliary-loss-free bias update rule [3].
- **A rigorous comparison of Token-Choice vs Expert-Choice** — proving their equivalence to dual formulations of an assignment problem and analyzing implications for autoregressive decoding.
- **A systems dissection of MegaBlocks** — showing how block-sparse SDD/DSD kernels enable dropless MoE training on A100/H100 GPUs [4][5].

Trillion-scale MoE is therefore not just a modeling problem; it is a systems, optimization, and combinatorial challenge simultaneously.

##2 Background
### From Conditional Computation to MoE

The idea of conditional computation dates to Jacobs et al. (1991), but modern MoE resurgence began with Shazeer et al. (2017) and GShard (Lepikhin et al. 2020). A standard Transformer FFN is replaced by:

y_t = sum_i g_i(x_t) * E_i(x_t), where E_i(x_t) = W2_i GeLU(W1_i x_t)

where g_i(x_t) is sparse: only k entries are non-zero. Router logits r_t = W_g x_t, then p_t = Softmax(r_t), and g_t = TopK(p_t).

### Scaling Milestones

- GShard (600B): Introduced expert parallelism and importance balancing loss [6].
- Switch Transformer (1.6T): Simplified to top-1 routing, demonstrated bfloat16 stability at trillion scale, introduced the f_i P_i auxiliary loss [2].
- GLaM (1.2T): Showed 7x computation savings vs GPT-3 while matching quality.
- Mixtral 8x7B, DBRX 132B, DeepSeekMoE 16B, Ring-1T: Modern MoEs rely heavily on fine-grained experts and dropless training [1][7].
- MegaScale-MoE (352B on 1440 Hoppers): 1.88x MFU improvement over Megatron-LM via communication optimizations [7].

At trillion scale, the MoE layer dominates both memory and all-to-all communication. Each layer requires a dispatch (tokens to experts) and combine (experts to tokens), typically implemented as all-to-all collective calls across expert-parallel ranks.

> Theorem: Routing Instability Amplification. Let u_e(t) be the EMA usage of expert e at step t and Delta_t = max_e u_e(t) - min_e u_e(t). Under vanilla TopK without auxiliary terms, there exists c>1 such that E[Delta_{t+1}] >= c * Delta_t in early training, leading to exponential divergence of utilization.

This positive feedback, first documented empirically by Fedus et al. and later formalized by Chi et al. (2022) and Wang et al. (2024), is the central antagonist of sparse scaling.

##3 Methodology
Our analysis synthesizes three methodological lenses:

1.  **Optimization-theoretic**: We view routing as a bi-level optimization problem where the router learns a stochastic policy whose actions change the reward landscape for experts.
2.  **Combinatorial**: Token-Choice and Expert-Choice are duals under capacity constraints as shown in Expert Threshold Routing [8].
3.  **Systems-centric**: We measure performance not just in perplexity but in Model FLOPs Utilization (MFU), memory overhead from padding/dropping, and all-to-all latency under imbalance.

We replicate key experiments conceptually and re-derive loss formulations to understand gradient interference. We also analyze Triton implementations of MegaBlocks kernels to evaluate portability [5].

*Key notation*:

- N: number of tokens per batch
- E: number of experts
- k: activated experts per token (Token-Choice) or tokens per expert (Expert-Choice)
- f_i: fraction of tokens dispatched to expert i
- P_i: mean gating probability for expert i
- C: capacity factor, where per-expert capacity is k * N / E * C

##4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Routing Collapse: Three Phenomenologies

**Hard collapse** occurs when f_i approx 0 for most i. The gradient to router logits for unused experts is near zero because they receive no tokens; they cannot recover.

**Soft collapse** is more insidious: f_i is uniform due to auxiliary loss, but P_i distributions are sharp and tokens are effectively assigned randomly to satisfy balance, destroying specialization. Ziang et al. (2026) call this *pseudo-balancing* [9].

**Load skew collapse** occurs at inference when batch composition (e.g., vision-language mix) shifts P_i even if training balance was perfect, with imbalance varying 5x across resolutions.

Causes:

- *Random initialization bias*: small logit differences early lead to winner-takes-all.
- *Rich-get-richer gradient dynamics*: experts that receive tokens receive gradients and improve.
- *Capacity-induced dropping*: when C<1, overloaded experts drop tokens; dropped tokens produce no expert gradient, reinforcing avoidance.

Mitigations fall into two classes: *loss-based* and *assignment-based*.

### 4.2 Load Balancing via Auxiliary Losses and Beyond

The Switch Transformer auxiliary loss is:

L_aux = alpha * E * sum_i f_i * P_i

with [1][2]:

- f_i = 1/N sum_t 1[argmax(p_t) == i]
- P_i = 1/N sum_t p_{t,i}

Intuition: f_i * P_i penalizes when an expert both receives many tokens (*hard*) and wants many tokens (*soft*). The product is minimized at 1/E^2 when f_i = P_i = 1/E.

Implementation concerns:

- **Coefficient sensitivity**: alpha in [0.001, 0.1] works; alpha >0.5 forces uniformity at cost of language modeling loss.
- **Gradient interference**: Since L_aux is added to the LM loss L_lm, its gradient flows through router weights W_g, potentially opposing LM gradients. Wang et al. [3] measure up to 12% gradient cosine conflict at 3B scale.

Recent **auxiliary-loss-free** approaches therefore eliminate L_aux altogether:

```python
# DeepSeek-V3 Loss-Free Balancing (simplified)
class BiasRouter:
    def __init__(self, num_experts, bias_lr=0.001):
        self.bias = torch.zeros(num_experts)
        self.bias_lr = bias_lr

    def forward(self, logits, tokens):
        # logits: [N, E]
        adjusted = logits + self.bias.unsqueeze(0)
        probs = torch.softmax(adjusted, dim=-1)
        assignment = torch.topk(probs, k=2, dim=-1)
        # update bias: decrease if overloaded, increase if underloaded
        with torch.no_grad():
            load = torch.bincount(assignment.indices.view(-1), minlength=len(self.bias))
            load = load.float() / load.sum()
            mean_load = 1.0 / len(self.bias)
            self.bias -= self.bias_lr * torch.sign(load - mean_load) * 1.0
        return probs, assignment

def diagnose_routing_health(router_probs, top_k=2):
    # Check if router is collapsing
    num_experts = router_probs.shape[-1]
    probs_flat = router_probs.view(-1, num_experts)
    _, top_indices = probs_flat.topk(top_k, dim=-1)
    unique_experts = torch.unique(top_indices).numel()
    usage_counts = torch.bincount(top_indices.flatten(), minlength=num_experts).float()
    usage_dist = usage_counts / usage_counts.sum()
    entropy = -(usage_dist * torch.log(usage_dist + 1e-10)).sum()
    max_entropy = torch.log(torch.tensor(float(num_experts)))
    collapse_ratio = entropy / max_entropy
    return {'unique': unique_experts, 'collapse_ratio': collapse_ratio.item()}
```

This *bias-only* adaptation ensures load balance via the routing decision boundary, not gradient coercion, and was shown to improve both balance (CoV 0.08 vs 0.32) and downstream accuracy (+1.4% avg) on 3B models trained on 200B tokens [3].

Memory-Aware Routing (MAR) [9] further adds per-expert memory buffers to encourage token-expert affinity consistency across steps, improving specialization by 35%.

**Comparison Table:**

| Method | Loss-Free? | Gradient Interference | Guarantees Balance? | Specialization |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| GShard importance CV2 | No | Medium | Approximate | Low |
| Switch f_i P_i | No | Medium-high | Approximate | Medium |
| ST-MoE z-loss + aux | No | High | Stronger | Medium |
| DeepSeek Loss-Free Bias | Yes | None | Strong (EMA) | Higher |
| MAR with buffers | Yes | None | Strong + consistent | 35% up |

### 4.3 Expert-Choice vs Token-Choice: Dual Assignment

**Token-Choice (TC)** is standard. Formally:

max_z sum_{t,i} z_{t,i} r_{t,i} s.t. sum_i z_{t,i}=k for all t, z in {0,1}

Each token chooses k experts independently. Load constraint sum_t z_{t,i} = N/E * k is *soft*, enforced only via auxiliary loss.

**Expert-Choice (EC)** [Zhou et al. 2022] flips constraints:

max_z sum_{t,i} z_{t,i} r_{t,i} s.t. sum_t z_{t,i}=k' for all i

Each expert chooses its top k' tokens. This guarantees **perfect load balance by construction**—no auxiliary loss needed—but violates two desiderata:

1. *Uneven token coverage*: a token may be selected by 0 or multiple experts.
2. *Causality violation*: choice depends on future tokens in batch, problematic for autoregressive decoding.

The unified view [8] frames both as relaxations of a balanced assignment problem requiring Hungarian O(N^3) for exact solution; TC strictly enforces row sparsity, EC strictly enforces column cardinality.

Empirical findings:

- *Why TC dominates LLMs*: GShard, Switch, GLaM, Mixtral, DeepSeek all use TC because autoregressive generation processes tokens sequentially. EC requires entire batch to decide routing, non-trivial to serve.
- *Why EC wins for vision & encoders*: For ViT-MoE and diffusion DiT-MoE, EC improves stability and avoids token dropping [10].
- *Hybrid ETR* (Expert-Token Resonance) [11] adaptively switches TC->EC mid-training, showing 5.4-46.6% training efficiency gains and reducing capacity lower bound by 40%.

> Theorem: EC Load Variance Bound. Under EC routing with capacity k' = N/E * k, the load variance Var(f) = 0 exactly, while token coverage c_t = sum_i z_{t,i} satisfies E[c_t]=k and Var(c_t) <= k(1-k/E).

Thus EC trades zero load imbalance for non-zero coverage variance, while TC does opposite.

### 4.4 MegaBlocks: Dropless MoE via Block-Sparse Kernels

Traditional frameworks (Tutel, DeepSpeed-MoE) handle variable token-per-expert counts by padding to capacity C or dropping overload. Both are wasteful:

- Dropping (C<1) harms quality, especially for rare tokens.
- Padding (C>1) wastes memory and compute up to 35% at E=64.

MegaBlocks [4][5] reformulates MoE as **block-sparse matrix multiplication**. Consider stacked expert weights W1, W2. By permuting tokens grouped by expert assignment into tilde{X}, the forward becomes:

Y = BlockSparse[tilde{X} W1, tilde{X} W2]

Three kernel challenges solved:

1.  **Dynamic sparsity**: token counts per expert change each step; MegaBlocks uses a *blocked-CSR-COO* hybrid where row indices are dense but column blocks are sparse, allowing SDD (Sparse-Dense-Dense) and DSD operations.
2.  **Transposed efficiency**: backward DD^T S requires transposed block iteration. MegaBlocks introduces *transpose indices* precomputed to allow coalesced transposed access without format conversion.
3.  **Block size selection**: 128x128 blocks match A100 tensor core tile efficiency, balancing arithmetic intensity vs load imbalance of sparse ops. Triton rewrite [5] shows portability to different dtypes and GPUs with <5% overhead.

Rust pseudocode for SDD kernel launcher (conceptual):

```rust
/// MegaBlocks SDD - Sparse x Dense -> Dense
/// S is block-sparse (num_blocks), D in [B, K], out in [M_blocks, N]
fn block_sparse_sdd(
    row_ptrs: &[i32],
    col_indices: &[i32],
    block_ids: &[i32],
    dense_a: &CudaBuffer<f16>,
    dense_b: &CudaBuffer<f16>,
    out: &mut CudaBuffer<f16>,
    block_m: usize, block_n: usize
) {
    // one threadblock per sparse block
    for blk in 0..row_ptrs.len()-1 {
        let r = block_ids[blk] as usize / grid_n;
        let c = block_ids[blk] as usize % grid_n;
        // ldg from sparse index, mma.sync on tensor cores
        unsafe {
            cute_gemm(
                &dense_a[r*block_m..],
                &dense_b[c*block_n..],
                &mut out[blk*block_m*block_n..]
            );
        }
    }
}
```

Systems impact at trillion scale:

- Enables **dropless** training without hyperparameter C; simplifies launch configs for 130B+ DBRX.
- 40% end-to-end speedup over Tutel best C (1.0-1.25) on 8xA100, 2.4x over Megatron-LM dense.
- Enables fine-grained experts (DBRX 16 experts, DeepSeek 64 routed + shared) where token distribution is extremely skewed.

Modern stacks (Databricks LLMFoundry, Mistral Mixtral, Nanotron) all import MegaBlocks core for this reason [12].

> Theorem: Dropless Optimality. Let T_e be tokens assigned to expert e. Any padded MoE with capacity factor C uses sum_e max(C*N/E, T_e) compute, whereas MegaBlocks dMoE uses sum_e T_e = N compute. The ratio N / (E*C*N/E) =1 when all experts exceed capacity, but excess idle compute (E*C*N/E - N) is non-negative and zero only when T_e uniform.

##6 Limitations

Despite progress, *fundamental tensions remain*:

- **Specialization vs Balance**: The Switch auxiliary loss enforces *statistical* balance, not *semantic* specialization. Forcing f_i approx 1/E may route a medical token to a code expert just to balance load. This *pseudo-balance* problem [9] is unsolved; MAR memory buffers help but add memory overhead O(E*D).

- **Loss-free bias stability**: Bias updates rely on EMA of load; with small batch sizes or highly skewed modalities (VL), bias can oscillate. Tuning bias learning rate remains heuristic.

- **Expert-Choice at Scale**: EC's non-causal batch dependency makes it incompatible with efficient speculative decoding and paged KV-cache serving. Attempts to make EC causal via prefix-restricted top-k degrade its balance guarantee to approximate.

- **Systems heterogeneity**: MegaBlocks' kernels are optimized for A100 128x128 FP16/BF16. On H100 with FP8, block size and transpose indices need retuning; Triton version closes gap but still 5-10% slower than hand-tuned CUDA for 64-expert 1T models.

- **Communication bottleneck**: Even with dropless kernels, all-to-all communication scales as O(k*N*d) per layer. At 1T scale on 1440 GPUs [7], all-to-all is 34% of step time; reducing k to 1 helps but hurts quality.

- **Evaluation gap**: Most MoE papers report perplexity and few-shot accuracy, not expert interpretability. We lack metrics for *expert diversity*—do 128 experts learn 128 skills or 10 skills repeated?

Open questions:

1. Can we learn k adaptively per token (dynamic compute) without violating dropless guarantees?
2. Does expert granularity (64x 1/4 FFN vs 8x full FFN) change collapse modes? DeepSeekMoE suggests finer experts improve specialization.
3. Can we co-design routing and quantization (QMoE sub-1-bit [13]) to push beyond 1T with limited HBM?

##7 Conclusion

Trillion-scale MoE is a story of taming discrete competition inside continuous optimization. Routing collapse emerges from early variance amplified by self-reinforcing gradients; it can be controlled via auxiliary losses that trade gradient interference for balance, or—more recently—via auxiliary-loss-free bias adaptation that preserves the optimization objective. Token-Choice and Expert-Choice represent dual relaxations of balanced assignment, each ideal for different regimes: TC for causal decoders, EC for encoders and diffusion models where batch-level selection is feasible. MegaBlocks shows that systems co-design—block-sparse reformulations, transpose indices, and CSR-COO hybrids—can eliminate the token dropping/padding dilemma entirely, enabling truly dropless training with up to 40% speedup.

The next frontier is not merely more experts, but *smarter* routing: memory-aware, domain-aware, and predictability-aware, preserving the promise of MoE—**outrageous parameter counts at constant compute**—without sacrificing specialization, inference causality, or hardware efficiency.
