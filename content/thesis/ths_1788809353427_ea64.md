# Tensor Network Contraction for the Classical Simulation of Quantum Circuits: Path Optimization, Bond-Dimension Truncation, and the Limits of Verifiable Advantage

## Abstract

Tensor network (TN) contraction is the dominant classical technique for simulating quantum circuits that exceed state-vector memory limits. This thesis develops the full stack: matrix product state (MPS) and projected entangled pair state (PEPS) ansätze, contraction-path optimization recast as approximate treewidth minimization, hypergraph partitioning via KaHyPar for the explicit construction of near-optimal contraction trees, and dynamic index slicing for memory-bounded, embarrassingly parallel execution on GPU clusters. We formalize bond-dimension truncation error through Schmidt-coefficient analysis and establish how per-step truncation errors compound along a contraction tree. We survey the modern software stack — cotengra, quimb, and NVIDIA cuTensorNet — and explain how distributed GPU contraction reduced the estimated classical cost of sampling Google's Sycamore *m* = 20 random circuits from ~10,000 years to under 20 days [2][3]. Finally, we connect contraction complexity to complexity-theoretic hardness of random circuit sampling (RCS) and to the barren-plateau phenomenon: we argue, following recent perspectives, that circuits engineered to be trainable are frequently simulable, and that verifiable quantum advantage in RCS now resides in a regime defined by *tensor contraction width* rather than raw qubit count.

---

## 1. Introduction

The classical simulation of quantum circuits sits at the intersection of condensed-matter physics, numerical linear algebra, and computational complexity theory. A naive state-vector simulation of an *n*-qubit circuit stores 2<sup>*n*</sup> complex amplitudes; the contemporary distributed-memory frontier lies at roughly 45–46 qubits on top-tier supercomputers, beyond which the memory wall makes amplitude storage infeasible [4][5]. Yet physical and near-term quantum circuits are rarely generic states in Hilbert space: their entanglement structure is constrained by geometry and depth, and this constraint is precisely what tensor networks exploit.

A tensor network generalizes matrix factorizations to arbitrary graph structures. Each tensor is a multidimensional array; edges shared between tensors denote index contraction (summation). A quantum circuit itself *is* a tensor network: every gate is a tensor, every qubit line at a fixed time step is a wire, and the amplitude ⟨*x*|*U*|0<sup>⊗n</sup>⟩ of a bitstring *x* is the full contraction of that network to a scalar. The computational cost of that contraction depends catastrophically on *order*: contracting tensors in a poor sequence can require intermediate tensors of size 2<sup>*n*</sup>, while an optimal sequence is governed by the *treewidth* of the network graph — a quantity that is NP-hard to compute exactly but admitting powerful heuristics.

This thesis traces the arc from the one-dimensional density matrix renormalization group (DMRG) of the 1990s to the distributed hyper-optimized contraction pipelines that undermined the first "10,000 years" supremacy claim. Three questions organize the exposition:

1. *Representation*: when do MPS and PEPS ansätze compress circuit states, and what do their bond dimensions cost in fidelity?
2. *Ordering*: how do hypergraph partitioning (KaHyPar) and stochastic hyper-optimization find contraction paths within small factors of optimal?
3. *Scale*: how does index slicing convert a memory-infeasible contraction into millions of parallel subtasks executable on GPU supercomputers, and where does this strategy hit fundamental limits?

> **Theorem:** (Contraction cost and treewidth.) Let *G* be the line graph of a tensor network. Any pairwise contraction order corresponds to a tree decomposition of *G*, and the maximum intermediate tensor size is 2<sup>*W*+1</sup> where *W* is the width of the decomposition. Conversely, an optimal tree decomposition yields a contraction order achieving that bound. Hence finding the optimal contraction order is NP-hard in general, being equivalent to minimum treewidth.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 Tensor networks in quantum many-body physics

Tensor networks emerged from White's DMRG (1992) and were recast in the modern language by Vidal's time-evolving block decimation (TEBD). The **matrix product state** represents an *n*-site state as a chain of rank-3 tensors:

|ψ⟩ = Σ<sub>{s}</sub> Tr[A<sup>[1]</sup><sub>s<sub>1</sub></sub> ··· A<sup>[n]</sup><sub>s<sub>n</sub></sub>] |s<sub>1</sub>···s<sub>n</sub>⟩

with bond dimension χ bounding the entanglement entropy across any cut: *S* ≤ log χ. Ground states of gapped one-dimensional Hamiltonians obey an *area law* (*S* ~ O(1)), so constant χ suffices — the raison d'être of DMRG. **PEPS** generalizes this to 2D lattices, where area law gives *S* ~ O(L) and χ grows exponentially with boundary length; exact PEPS contraction is #P-complete, so approximation is intrinsic to the method [5].

### 2.2 Circuits as tensor networks

Mapping a circuit to a tensor network is immediate: a single-qubit gate is a rank-2 tensor, a two-qubit gate a rank-4 tensor, and each qubit world-line becomes a sequence of contracted indices. The *open* indices at the circuit output correspond to the measured bitstring. Two simulation modes exist:

- **Full-amplitude simulation**: contract the entire network for one bitstring — cost ~ 2<sup>*W*</sup> FLOPs, memory ~ 2<sup>*W*</sup>.
- **Fidelity-targeted / sliced sampling**: contract with a subset of indices *sliced* (fixed to specific values), producing 2<sup>*k*</sup> independent subtasks each with reduced width — the workhorse of distributed RCS simulation [1][2].

### 2.3 Complexity-theoretic context

Random circuit sampling is hard under plausible complexity conjectures: approximating output probabilities of random circuits to multiplicative error is #P-hard (Bouland et al.), and the linear cross-entropy benchmark (XEB) fidelity framework of Arute et al. ties classical simulation cost to achieving fidelity *f* via *frugal* sampling at cost 1/*f* of a perfect sample [8][9]. The supremacy claim of 2019 asserted ~10,000 years on Summit for *m* = 20 cycles [8]; classical tensor-network methods have since compressed that estimate by five orders of magnitude [2].

## 3. Methodology

Our methodology follows the standard hyper-optimized contraction pipeline, decomposed into four stages:

1. **Network construction.** Translate the circuit (e.g., a Sycamore *m*-cycle instance) into an einsum equation. Absorb single-qubit gates into neighboring two-qubit gates to reduce tensor count, a standard preprocessing step that shrinks the hypergraph before optimization.
2. **Contraction-tree search.** Build an explicit binary *contraction tree* whose leaves are input tensors and whose internal nodes are pairwise contractions. Candidate constructors include:
   - *Greedy* (opt_einsum): contract the cheapest pair first; fast but myopic.
   - *Hypergraph partitioning* (KaHyPar): recursively bipartition the network hypergraph to fix the top layers of the tree, then solve subtrees greedily below a cutoff size (typically 25 tensors) [1][2].
   - *Simulated annealing / subtree reconfiguration*: local moves that rewire the tree, interleaved with slicing.
   - *Hyper-optimization* (cotengra): sample many trees while tuning the meta-parameters (partition count *K*, imbalance ε) via Bayesian optimization or CMA-ES [1][4].
3. **Dynamic slicing.** Select a set of *sliced indices* to fix, trading a multiplicative 2<sup>*k*</sup> subtask count against exponential memory reduction. cotengra's `SliceFinder` interleaves slicing with subtree reconfiguration — *dynamic slicing* — so the path stays near-optimal under the sliced constraints [4].
4. **Distributed execution.** Dispatch sliced subtasks across GPUs/nodes (e.g., via cuTensorNet path inputs), each subtask contracting independently, then combine amplitudes. Because subtasks are independent, weak scaling is nearly ideal.

```python
import cotengra as ctg
import quimb.tensor as qtn

# 1. Build the circuit as a tensor network (quimb)
circ = qtn.Circuit(53)                       # Sycamore-like width
# ... append random single- and two-qubit gates ...
tn = circ.to_dense() if False else None       # placeholder for TN conversion

# 2. Hyper-optimize a contraction tree with KaHyPar partitioning
opt = ctg.HyperOptimizer(
    methods=['kahypar', 'greedy', 'branch'],
    max_repeats=512,
    parallel=True,
)
# 3. Add dynamic slicing targeting 2**28 memory
opt.set_slice_opts({'target_size': 2**28})
tree = opt.search(tn.arrays, tn.eq, tn.shapes)
print(f"FLOPs: {tree.total_flops():.3e}, max intermediate: {tree.max_size():.3e}")
```

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 MPS and PEPS Ansätze for Circuit States

For shallow circuits, the output state admits an efficient MPS representation: each layer of two-qubit gates can at most double the Schmidt rank across a cut, so depth-*d* circuits need χ ≤ 2<sup>*d*</sup> only in the worst case; structured circuits (e.g., QAOA on sparse graphs) need far less. The **zip-up / boundary-MPS** algorithm contracts a 2D PEPS row by row, treating each row as a matrix product operator applied to a boundary MPS, truncating back to bond dimension *m* via SVD at cost O(*dm*²*r*³) + O(*m*³*r*²) per step, where *r* is the PEPS bond dimension [5]. Canonicalization (alternating absorption of singular values) suppresses truncation-error amplification.

PEPS is the natural ansatz for the Sycamore layout itself: the circuit's light-cone structure maps onto a 2D tensor network whose contraction is exactly the amplitude. The key quantitative fact, established in the approximate-contraction literature, is that transforming a depth-20 random-circuit PEPS into an MPS requires bond dimension at least 2<sup>12</sup> = 4096 (by a graph-cut argument), while approximate contraction at χ = 2<sup>10</sup> = 1024 already achieves absolute error ~10<sup>−2</sup> [5][7].

### 4.2 Contraction Ordering as Treewidth Minimization

The cost model is unforgiving. Contracting tensors *A* (indices *I*<sub>A</sub>) and *B* (indices *I*<sub>B</sub>) costs roughly Π<sub>i∈I<sub>A</sub>∪I<sub>B</sub></sub> *d*<sub>i</sub> FLOPs, and the result has size Π<sub>i∈I<sub>A</sub>△I<sub>B</sub></sub> *d*<sub>i</sub>. Two metrics matter:

| Metric | Definition | Governs |
|---|---|---|
| `total_flops` | Σ over contractions of product of all involved index sizes | Wall-clock time |
| `max_size` / width *W* | max over intermediates of log₂(size) | Peak memory |

Gray and Kourtis showed that a stochastic **hyper-optimizer** over partitioner meta-parameters yields contraction trees with FLOP counts orders of magnitude below greedy baselines on irregular networks, and estimated a >10,000× speedup over the original classical cost estimate for Sycamore supremacy circuits — 241 days at 55% FLOP efficiency, before slicing and distribution [1].

### 4.3 Hypergraph Partitioning and Dynamic Slicing

Why hypergraphs rather than ordinary graphs? A tensor index shared by *k* tensors is a *hyperedge* of cardinality *k*; cutting it during partitioning corresponds to placing those tensors in different contraction subtrees. KaHyPar's multilevel scheme — coarsening by heavy-edge matching, initial partitioning via a portfolio of greedy/BFS/label-propagation algorithms, and FM refinement during uncoarsening — minimizes the cut metric that proxies contraction cost [2][6]. The Alibaba ACQDP framework augments this with a two-phase scheme: hypergraph decomposition fixes the top of the contraction tree, then index slicing is interleaved with *local reconfiguration* of the tree, achieving contraction costs up to 10<sup>6</sup>× lower than qFlex and 10<sup>3</sup>× lower than cotengra on Sycamore instances [2].

*Slicing* deserves emphasis because it converts memory infeasibility into parallelism. If the unsliced contraction needs an intermediate of size 2<sup>40</sup> (8 TB), slicing *k* = 12 indices yields 4096 subtasks each needing 2<sup>28</sup> (2 GB) — fitting comfortably on a single GPU. The overhead is the slicing multiplicative factor, and the art is choosing indices whose fixation reduces *W* maximally; cotengra's `SliceFinder` visualizes the saved-memory vs. increased-cost frontier explicitly [4].

### 4.4 Bond-Dimension Truncation Error Analysis

Approximate contraction introduces error at every truncation. For an SVD truncation keeping χ singular values {σ<sub>i</sub>}, the local 2-norm error is ε<sub>loc</sub> = √(Σ<sub>i>χ</sub> σ<sub>i</sub>²), and this is *optimal* among rank-χ approximations (Eckart–Young). Along a contraction tree with *T* truncations, the global error obeys a triangle-inequality bound:

> **Theorem:** (Truncation error accumulation.) Let a contraction tree perform *T* successive bond truncations with local errors ε<sub>1</sub>,…,ε<sub>T</sub> in 2-norm. Then the total error satisfies ε<sub>tot</sub> ≤ Σ<sub>t</sub> ε<sub>t</sub>. In practice, errors add sub-linearly when truncations are performed in canonical form, since the kept singular vectors remain near-orthonormal across steps.

The practical consequence is a **Pareto frontier**: increasing χ reduces ε<sub>tot</sub> roughly as the tail of the entanglement spectrum, while FLOPs grow as χ<sup>3</sup>–χ<sup>4</sup>. For random circuits the spectrum is nearly flat (volume-law entanglement), so truncation buys little — exact contraction with slicing is preferred. For structured circuits (chemistry, condensed matter), the spectrum decays fast and MPS/PEPS truncation is exponentially efficient. This dichotomy is why RCS is the *hardest* target and also the least physically interesting one.

### 4.5 Distributed GPU Contraction: cuTensorNet, quimb, cotengra

The modern stack separates *path finding* from *execution*:

- **cotengra** [4]: path optimization and slicing; emits contraction trees consumable by numpy, opt_einsum, quimb, or cuTensorNet.
- **quimb** [1]: tensor-network data structures, circuit-to-TN conversion, approximate contraction algorithms (DMRG-style, boundary MPS, loop-TNR).
- **cuTensorNet** (NVIDIA cuQuantum SDK): GPU execution of a fixed path, delivering 8–20× speedups over CuPy on A100 for TN workloads, with multi-GPU and multi-node sliced execution [5][9].

The landmark demonstration: cotengra + quimb + cuTensorNet on NVIDIA's Selene supercomputer produced a sample from a depth-20 Sycamore circuit in under 10 minutes [9]. Meanwhile, the GPU-optimized TN simulator of recent work sustains >21 TFLOPS on a single A100 for the 18-cycle Sycamore verification, a 3.96× reduction in verification time and 12.5× over state-of-the-art CPU results [7].

## 5. Empirical Results and Formal Analysis

We consolidate the quantitative record from the literature into a single comparison (costs normalized to the *m* = 20, *n* = 53 Sycamore sampling task at comparable fidelity):

| Method | Estimated cost | Hardware basis | Key technique |
|---|---|---|---|
| Arute et al. (2019) original estimate [8] | ~10,000 years | Summit | State-vector extrapolation |
| Gray & Kourtis hyper-optimization [1] | ~241 days (55% FLOP eff.) | — | Contraction trees + slicing |
| Huang et al. "stem" optimization [3] | < 20 days | Summit-comparable cluster | Stem identification |
| Pan & Zhang big-batch sampling [10] | ~15 hours (1M bitstrings, XEB 0.739) | 60 GPUs | Correlated-batch contraction |
| ACQDP (Pan et al., Nature Comp. Sci.) [2] | < 20 days projected, 10<sup>6</sup>× lower FLOP cost than qFlex | Summit-comparable | Hypergraph decomposition + dynamic slicing |
| cuTensorNet on Selene [9] | < 10 min per sample (depth 20) | Selene supercomputer | GPU path execution |

Three formal observations follow:

1. **Fidelity targeting dominates.** Simulating at XEB fidelity *f* costs ~1/*f* of perfect simulation; Pan and Zhang's 0.739-fidelity million-sample run exceeded Google's hardware fidelity, reframing "supremacy" as a fidelity race rather than a capability cliff [10].
2. **The hardness parameter is contraction width, not qubit count.** Belief-propagation-contracted generalized PEPS has classically reproduced 127-qubit Eagle, 433-qubit Osprey, and 1121-qubit Condor kicked-Ising experiments with effective bond dimensions above 16M [5]. Qubit count alone no longer demarcates the classical frontier.
3. **Slicing overhead is the residual bottleneck.** Dynamic slicing reduces memory exponentially in *k* but multiplies work by 2<sup>*k*</sup>; the hyper-optimizer's central achievement is pushing the memory–FLOPs Pareto frontier outward so that feasible (*k*, path) pairs exist at all [1][4].

## 6. Limitations and Open Problems

- **Approximation guarantees are weak.** Truncation error bounds (Section 4.4) are *a posteriori* and pessimistic; no efficient *a priori* certificate exists for general networks, and PEPS contraction remains #P-complete even approximately in the worst case. Rigorous fidelity accounting for sliced, truncated pipelines is an open problem.
- **Optimizer runtime vs. contraction runtime.** Hyper-optimization can itself take hours; for one-shot contractions the search may dominate. Amortization works when the same network geometry is contracted many times (e.g., batched sampling), but single-amplitude queries remain awkward.
- **The barren-plateau / simulability nexus.** A growing body of work argues that parametrized circuits engineered to avoid barren plateaus — via shallow depth, small dynamical Lie algebras, or restricted subspaces — are frequently classically simulable, sometimes provably so given modest classical data [11]. This suggests a *simulability–trainability tradeoff*: the circuits we can train may be the circuits we can simulate. Whether non-trivial counterexamples (trainable *and* intractable) exist at scale is the central open question for variational quantum advantage.
- **Verification asymmetry.** As classical simulation improves, *verifying* a quantum device's output distribution becomes as hard as simulating it; XEB itself requires classical amplitude computation. The field needs verification protocols whose classical cost scales better than simulation cost.
- **Beyond qubits and unitary circuits.** Noisy circuits, mid-circuit measurement, and fermionic systems each demand extensions (MPO/DM ansätze, trajectory methods, fermionic TN) whose contraction theory is less mature.

---

## 7. Conclusion

Tensor network contraction has matured from a condensed-matter numerical method into the principal instrument for auditing claims of quantum computational advantage. The combination of hypergraph-partitioned contraction trees, dynamic slicing, and distributed GPU execution compressed a 10,000-year classical estimate to minutes-per-sample on real supercomputers — not by refuting complexity theory, but by exploiting the structure that random circuits retain: bounded treewidth growth, sliceable indices, and fidelity-targeted sampling. The frontier has consequently moved: it is now parametrized by contraction width and achievable XEB fidelity rather than by qubit count, as thousand-qubit simulations of structured circuits demonstrate. The deepest lesson is a dual one. First, for the practitioner: *path optimization is the algorithm* — FLOPs follow the tree. Second, for the theorist: the barren-plateau literature's emerging simulability–trainability tradeoff hints that the class of useful, trainable quantum circuits may overlap heavily with the class of classically simulable ones, and tensor networks are the lens through which that overlap is measured. Until a quantum device demonstrates advantage in a task whose tensor contraction width provably defeats hyper-optimized classical pipelines, the classical side of the race remains very much alive.
