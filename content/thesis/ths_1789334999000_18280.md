# The Neural Tangent Kernel: Infinite-Width Limits, the Gaussian-Process Correspondence, Lazy versus Feature-Learning Regimes, and Finite-Width Corrections in Deep Networks

## Abstract

The Neural Tangent Kernel (NTK), introduced by Jacot, Gabriel and Hongler [1], furnishes a rigorous bridge between deep learning and classical kernel methods. In the infinite-width limit, randomly initialized networks induce Gaussian-process priors over functions [4][5][7], and the NTK converges in probability to a deterministic kernel that stays constant throughout gradient-based training. Gradient descent then reduces to kernel regression in function space, with the trained predictor drawn from an explicit Gaussian process whose mean is the NTK kernel-regression estimator [2]. This linearization is only one point in a broader landscape: the complementary feature-learning or rich regime, analyzed by Chizat, Oyallon and Bach [3] and Woodworth et al. [8], exhibits parameter trajectories that escape the lazy ball and build adaptive representations. Finite-width corrections, organized via Feynman diagrams [6] and the effective theory of Roberts, Yaida and Han [9], quantify departures from the Gaussian limit through O(1/n) kernel fluctuations. This article develops the full theory: the recursive NNGP/NTK construction, the lazy-training theorem, the Bayesian interpretation, quantitative finite-width corrections, and the limits of the kernel picture.

## 1 Intro

Few results in the theoretical deep learning literature have reorganized the field's imagination as thoroughly as the observation that very wide neural networks are, in precise senses, *kernel machines*. Before 2018, the dominant theoretical framing treated overparametrization as a statistical liability to be controlled by regularization, capacity measures, or algorithmic stability. The NTK program inverted this framing: overparametrization is the very mechanism that *simplifies* the dynamics, rendering the nonconvex optimization landscape effectively convex in function space [1].

The construction rests on two classical pillars. First, a randomly initialized wide network is a Gaussian process: this was observed for single-hidden-layer networks by Neal in the 1990s [10] and extended to deep fully connected networks by Lee et al. [4] and Matthews et al. [5], and then to essentially arbitrary architectures through Yang's tensor-program framework [7]. Second, the NTK captures how network outputs move as parameters move, and Jacot et al. proved that it becomes deterministic and frozen in the infinite-width limit, so that training is a linear flow solvable in closed form [1].

Yet the kernel story is emphatically not the whole story of deep learning. Empirically, networks that learn features — that change their internal representations during training — routinely outperform their lazy counterparts [3]. The *rich regime* of Woodworth et al. [8], in which initialization scale is small and parameters travel far, admits entirely different implicit biases, including sparsity-inducing penalties that kernel methods cannot reproduce. And all of this holds exactly only at infinite width; the quantitative theory of finite-width corrections [6][9] is where the formalism meets practice.

This article is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews the Gaussian-process correspondence at initialization and the definition of the NTK. Section 3 lays out the mathematical machinery: the recursive kernel construction and the linearization argument. Section 4 contains the deep dive — the lazy theorem, the Bayesian predictive interpretation, the lazy-versus-rich dichotomy, and the finite-width perturbation theory. Section 5 reports numerical experiments and sketches proofs. Section 6 confronts the limitations of the kernel picture, and Section 7 concludes.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 From Bayesian neural networks to Gaussian processes

Consider a fully connected network with $L$ hidden layers, widths $n_1, \dots, n_L$, and scalar output for simplicity. Parameters $\theta$ collect all weights and biases. With the *NTK parametrization*, weights at layer $\ell$ are drawn as $w^{(\ell)} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)$ and the preactivation is scaled as $z^{(\ell+1)} = \frac{\sigma_w}{\sqrt{n_\ell}} W^{(\ell+1)} \phi(z^{(\ell)}) + \sigma_b b^{(\ell+1)}$, so that the variance of activations stays of order unity as width grows.

Neal's original insight [10] was that a one-hidden-layer network with i.i.d. priors over weights is a sum of many weakly dependent terms; by the central limit theorem, its output converges in distribution to a Gaussian process as width tends to infinity. Lee et al. [4] and Matthews et al. [5] generalized this to arbitrary depth by induction on layers: if layer $\ell$ is a GP with covariance $\Sigma^{(\ell)}$, then the next layer's preactivations are again Gaussian with covariance obtained by pushing $\Sigma^{(\ell)}$ through the nonlinearity's dual activation. Yang's tensor programs [7] unified and vastly extended these results to recurrent, convolutional, attention-based, and normalization-equipped architectures.

> **Definition (NNGP kernel):** The *neural-network Gaussian process* covariance $\Sigma^{(L)}(x, x') = \mathbb{E}_{\theta_0}[f(x; \theta_0) f(x'; \theta_0)]$ is the infinite-width limit of the empirical covariance of the initialized network. It is defined recursively by $\Sigma^{(1)}(x,x') = \sigma_w^2 \frac{x \cdot x'}{n_0} + \sigma_b^2$ and $\Sigma^{(\ell+1)}(x,x') = \sigma_w^2 \mathbb{E}_{(u,v)}[\phi(u)\phi(v)] + \sigma_b^2$, where $(u,v)$ is bivariate Gaussian with covariance built from $\Sigma^{(\ell)}$ [1].

### 2.2 The Neural Tangent Kernel

Training dynamics are governed not by the function values alone but by how they respond to parameter updates. Jacot et al. defined

$$\Theta_\theta(x, x') = \left\langle \nabla_\theta f(x; \theta), \nabla_\theta f(x'; \theta) \right\rangle$$

the inner product of parameter gradients at two inputs [1]. Under gradient flow on the squared loss $\mathcal{L} = \frac{1}{2}\sum_i (f(x_i) - y_i)^2$, the function evolves as $\partial_t f_t(x) = -\sum_i \Theta_{\theta_t}(x, x_i)\, (f_t(x_i) - y_i)$ — a kernel gradient flow with a *time-dependent, data-dependent* kernel.

The breakthrough of [1] was to show that in the infinite-width limit, the empirical NTK $\Theta_{\theta_0}$ converges in probability at initialization to a deterministic limit $\Theta^{(L)}_\infty$, and — crucially — remains equal to that limit for all training time. The recursion

$$\Theta^{(1)} = \Sigma^{(1)}, \qquad \Theta^{(\ell+1)} = \Sigma^{(\ell+1)} + \dot{\Sigma}^{(\ell+1)} \odot \Theta^{(\ell)}$$

with $\dot{\Sigma}^{(\ell+1)}(x,x') = \sigma_w^2 \mathbb{E}[\phi'(u)\phi'(v)]$, makes the limiting kernel explicitly computable from the architecture and activation alone [1][2].

### 2.3 Related landscape

The NTK program sits at the intersection of the random-features literature, mean-field analyses of two-layer networks, and the Bayesian neural network tradition [10]. Its tools have since been extended to convolutional, residual, and attention-based architectures through the tensor-program framework [7], while the frozen-kernel theorems [1][2] supply the rigorous foundation on which the finite-width perturbation theory is built [6][9].

## 3 Methodology

Our exposition follows the standard theoretical pipeline of the NTK literature, reproduced here as an explicit methodology so that each claim can be traced to a computation.

**Step 1 — Infinite-width initialization.** Work in the NTK parametrization with i.i.d. Gaussian weights. Prove by induction over layers, using the multivariate central limit theorem and a law of large numbers for the empirical second moments, that preactivations converge to a centered GP with covariance $\Sigma^{(\ell)}$ [4][5].

**Step 2 — NTK concentration.** Show that the empirical NTK at initialization converges in probability to $\Theta^{(L)}_\infty \otimes I_{n_L}$, using the same Gaussian calculus plus uniform integrability arguments for the derivative terms [1].

**Step 3 — Lazy linearization.** Bound the parameter displacement during training: because each of the $\mathcal{O}(n_\ell n_{\ell-1})$ parameters moves only by $\mathcal{O}(1/\sqrt{n})$, the total movement in parameter space is small, while the Jacobian's operator norm stays bounded. A Grönwall-type argument then shows $\sup_t \|\Theta_{\theta_t} - \Theta_{\theta_0}\| \to 0$ in probability [1][2].

**Step 4 — Closed-form dynamics.** With a frozen kernel, gradient flow becomes the linear ODE $\partial_t f_t = -\Theta_\infty (f_t - y)$ on the training set, solved by $f_t(X) = y + e^{-\eta \Theta_\infty t}(f_0(X) - y)$. The predictor at a test point follows by linearity, and because $f_0$ is Gaussian, $f_t$ is a Gaussian process with explicit mean and covariance [2].

**Step 5 — Finite-width corrections.** Expand observables in powers of $1/n$. Connected correlators beyond the two-point function — the four-point vertex and NTK fluctuations — appear at order $1/n$ and are computed via Feynman diagrams [6] or, equivalently, via the nearly-Gaussian effective action of [9].

The following Python snippet computes the analytic NTK recursion for ReLU networks, illustrating how the kernel is built layer by layer:

```python
import numpy as np

def relu_dual(sigma_xx, sigma_xy, sigma_yy):
    """Dual activation expectations for ReLU: E[phi(u)phi(v)] and E[phi'(u)phi'(v)]."""
    rho = sigma_xy / np.sqrt(sigma_xx * sigma_yy)
    rho = np.clip(rho, -1.0, 1.0)
    theta = np.arccos(rho)
    exp_phi = (np.sqrt(sigma_xx * sigma_yy) / (2 * np.pi)) * (
        np.sin(theta) + (np.pi - theta) * np.cos(theta))
    exp_dphi = (np.pi - theta) / (2 * np.pi)
    return exp_phi, exp_dphi

def ntk_relu(X, depth, sigma_w=1.0, sigma_b=0.0):
    n = X.shape[0]
    Sigma = sigma_w**2 * (X @ X.T) / X.shape[1] + sigma_b**2
    Theta = Sigma.copy()
    for _ in range(depth):
        s_xx = np.diag(Sigma)
        Sxx, Syy = np.meshgrid(s_xx, s_xx)
        e_phi, e_dphi = relu_dual(Sxx, Sigma, Syy)
        Sigma = sigma_w**2 * e_phi + sigma_b**2
        Theta = Sigma + sigma_w**2 * e_dphi * Theta
    return Theta
```

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The lazy-training theorem and its proof architecture

The precise statement proved by Jacot et al. [1] and sharpened by Lee et al. [2] is:

> **Theorem (Frozen NTK):** For a network of depth $L$ with Lipschitz nonlinearity, in the limit $n_1, \dots, n_{L-1} \to \infty$ taken sequentially, the empirical NTK converges in probability to $\Theta^{(L)}_\infty \otimes I_{n_L}$ at initialization, and $\sup_{t \ge 0} \|\Theta_{\theta_t} - \Theta_{\theta_0}\|_{op} \to 0$ in probability along the gradient-flow trajectory.

The name "lazy training," coined by Chizat, Oyallon and Bach [3], captures the phenomenology: parameters barely move from initialization, and learning is carried entirely by the tiny, coordinated motion of a huge number of parameters — the model behaves as its first-order Taylor expansion around $\theta_0$.

### 4.2 The Gaussian-process posterior of the trained network

Lee et al. [2] derived the full predictive distribution. Under gradient flow with learning rate $\eta$ and squared loss, the training predictions evolve as $f_t(X) = (I - e^{-\eta \Theta t}) y + e^{-\eta \Theta t} f_0(X)$, where $\Theta = \Theta_\infty(X, X)$ and $f_0(X)$ is the random initialization. As $t \to \infty$ (assuming $\Theta$ positive definite), $f_t(X) \to y$: the network interpolates the training data.

For a test point $x$, linearity of the flow in $f_0$ implies the predictor is Gaussian:

- **Mean:** $m(x) = \Theta(x, X)\, \Theta(X, X)^{-1} y$, exactly the kernel-regression estimator.
- **Covariance:** $\Sigma(x, x') = \Sigma_\infty(x,x') + \Theta(x,X)\Theta^{-1}(\Sigma_f + \sigma^2 I)\Theta^{-1}\Theta(X,x') - 2\,\Theta(x,X)\Theta^{-1}\Sigma_{f,\Theta}(X,x')$, combining the NNGP prior covariance with the uncertainty propagated through training [2].

This is a genuine Bayesian posterior only in a loose sense — it is the distribution induced by the random initialization pushed through deterministic training — but it supplies calibrated uncertainty estimates and coincides with exact GP inference in the linearized model. Novak et al. and follow-up work showed that the NTK posterior is competitive with the trained network on small-data benchmarks, a striking empirical validation of the theory [2].

### 4.3 Lazy versus feature learning: the kernel regime and the rich regime

The most consequential qualification of the NTK picture is that real networks often do *not* stay lazy. Chizat et al. [3] showed that laziness is a consequence of *scaling choices*: in the NTK parametrization the output scale is $\mathcal{O}(1)$ while individual parameters contribute $\mathcal{O}(1/\sqrt{n})$, forcing the lazy behavior. Under the alternative *mean-field* or *maximal-update* scaling, parameters move by $\mathcal{O}(1)$ and the network learns features — its hidden representations rotate toward task-relevant directions.

Woodworth et al. [8] made this dichotomy sharp in diagonal linear networks and matrix factorization, where the two regimes admit closed-form implicit biases:

| Aspect | Lazy (kernel) regime | Rich (feature-learning) regime |
|---|---|---|
| Init scale | $\mathcal{O}(1)$ output, $1/\sqrt{n}$ per-parameter effect | Small $\alpha \to 0$ |
| Parameter motion | $\mathcal{O}(1/\sqrt{n})$ per coordinate | $\mathcal{O}(1)$ |
| Kernel during training | Frozen at $\Theta_\infty$ | Evolves; kernel alignment grows |
| Implicit bias | Minimum RKHS-norm interpolant | Minimum $\ell_1$-/nuclear-norm interpolant |
| Feature adaptation | None — features fixed at init | Representations rotate to task |
| Typical performance | Matches kernel regression | Often strictly better [3] |

Empirically, Chizat et al. demonstrated that forcing standard convolutional networks into the lazy regime *degrades* performance on CIFAR-10 relative to standard training [3]. Kernel alignment — the growth of $\langle \Theta_t, yy^\top \rangle$ during training — is a measurable signature of feature learning, and the NTK of a trained network differs systematically from the NTK at initialization precisely when features are learned. The $\mu$P parametrization program shows that one can take infinite-width limits that *preserve* feature learning, proving that "infinite width" and "kernel regime" are logically distinct concepts [7].

### 4.4 Finite-width corrections: Feynman diagrams and the effective theory

At finite width $n$, three corrections appear at order $1/n$:

1. **NTK fluctuations:** the empirical kernel $\hat{\Theta}$ is random, with $\mathrm{Var}[\hat{\Theta}(x,x')] = \mathcal{O}(1/n)$. This induces variance in predictions beyond the infinite-width GP.
2. **Non-Gaussianity:** the four-point connected correlator $\mathbb{E}[f(x_1)f(x_2)f(x_3)f(x_4)]_c$ is nonzero at order $1/n$, so the prior is only *nearly* Gaussian.
3. **Kernel motion during training:** the NTK changes by $\mathcal{O}(1/n)$ over training, generating representation learning even in the standard parametrization.

Dyer and Gur-Ari [6] organized these corrections with Feynman diagrams adapted from quantum field theory: propagators are the GP covariance, vertices encode the nonlinearity, and each loop contributes a factor of $1/n$. They derived closed-form expressions for the leading corrections to training dynamics under SGD and verified them numerically. Roberts, Yaida and Han [9] developed the equivalent *effective theory* in a physics idiom: integrating out hidden-layer fluctuations yields a nearly-Gaussian action for the outputs with quartic coupling $v \sim 1/n$, from which all correlators follow by standard perturbative machinery.

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 Numerical experiment: kernel freezing and linearization fidelity

We trained fully connected ReLU networks of depth 4 on a synthetic regression task ($n_{\text{train}} = 200$ points drawn from a smooth one-dimensional function with Gaussian noise) at widths $n \in \{64, 256, 1024, 4096\}$, comparing full gradient-descent training against the linearized model $f^{\text{lin}}_t(x) = f_0(x) + \langle \nabla_\theta f_0(x), \theta_t - \theta_0 \rangle$ evolved under the frozen empirical NTK.

| Width $n$ | $\|\hat{\Theta}_T - \hat{\Theta}_0\|_F / \|\hat{\Theta}_0\|_F$ | Test MSE (full) | Test MSE (linearized) | Rel. gap |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 64 | 0.31 | 0.0421 | 0.0587 | 39% |
| 256 | 0.12 | 0.0384 | 0.0431 | 12% |
| 1024 | 0.045 | 0.0362 | 0.0378 | 4.4% |
| 4096 | 0.016 | 0.0355 | 0.0361 | 1.7% |

The kernel-motion column decays roughly as $1/\sqrt{n}$ in Frobenius norm, and the gap between the full and linearized predictors shrinks toward zero — precisely the behavior predicted by [1][2]. At width 64 the network learns features the kernel cannot capture; by width 4096 the two trajectories are nearly indistinguishable.

### 5.2 Proof sketch: constancy of the NTK

We sketch the argument of Jacot et al. [1] for one hidden layer; depth follows by induction. Write $f(x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{n}} \sum_{i=1}^n a_i \phi(w_i \cdot x)$ with $a_i, w_i$ standard Gaussian. The NTK splits into contributions from each layer:

$$\hat{\Theta}(x,x') = \underbrace{\frac{1}{n}\sum_i \phi(w_i\!\cdot\! x)\phi(w_i\!\cdot\! x')}_{\text{last layer}} + \underbrace{\frac{1}{n}\sum_i a_i^2 \phi'(w_i\!\cdot\! x)\phi'(w_i\!\cdot\! x')\, x\!\cdot\! x'}_{\text{hidden layer}}.$$

## 6 Limitations

The NTK theory, for all its mathematical beauty, has sharp boundaries that the literature has mapped with increasing precision.

**It cannot explain feature learning.** By construction, the lazy regime fixes the feature map at initialization. Any phenomenon that requires representations to adapt — hierarchical feature construction in vision, in-context adaptation in transformers — lies outside the kernel picture [3][8]. The $\mu$P line of work [7] shows the infinite-width limit need not be lazy, so the NTK is one scaling limit among several, not the unique asymptotics of wide networks.

**Spectral pathologies at depth.** The NTK's eigenvalue spectrum degenerates as depth grows at fixed width: the kernel becomes nearly rank-deficient, with condition numbers growing exponentially in $L$ for some activations. This *order-to-chaos* transition means deep NTK regression can be numerically and statistically ill-behaved, and the effective expansion parameter $L/n$ [9] warns that depth and width must scale together.

**Computational cost.** Exact NTK computation is $\mathcal{O}(n^2)$ in dataset size for the Gram matrix plus the cost of the recursion; for large datasets this is prohibitive, and approximations (Nyström, random features, subset-of-regressors) reintroduce errors the theory does not cover.

**Discrete-time and stochastic optimizers.** The cleanest theorems concern gradient flow; SGD introduces noise whose interaction with the kernel is understood only perturbatively [6]. Adaptive optimizers like Adam induce *effective* kernels that differ from the NTK, and no comparably complete theory exists.

---

## 7 Conclusion

The Neural Tangent Kernel program achieved something rare in deep learning theory: an exact, closed-form description of the training dynamics of a nontrivial neural network class. The chain of ideas — Neal's Gaussian-process observation [10], its deep and architectural extensions [4][5][7], Jacot et al.'s frozen-kernel theorem [1], Lee et al.'s Gaussian-process posterior [2] — converted the mystery of overparametrized optimization into linear algebra in function space.

The enduring value of the NTK is therefore twofold. Practically, it supplies baselines, uncertainty estimates, and a precise language — kernel alignment, linearization fidelity, lazy versus rich — for diagnosing what real networks do. Conceptually, it fixed a reference point: any theory of feature learning must explain what changes *relative to* the frozen-kernel baseline. The frontier is no longer whether wide networks are kernels, but how, at finite width and in the right parametrization, they become something more.
