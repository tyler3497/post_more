# Sketching and Streaming Algorithms at Scale: Sublinear-Space Frequency Estimation

## Abstract

Modern telemetry pipelines emit billions of events per day, far exceeding per-key memory budgets. Streaming algorithms answer fundamental aggregation queries — *how many times did key i appear?*, *how many distinct keys exist?*, *which keys dominate?* — in sublinear, often polylogarithmic, space, in a single pass, with provable guarantees. This thesis develops the four pillars of the discipline: the **Count-Min sketch** [1] and **CountSketch** [4] for frequency estimation under the turnstile model; **HyperLogLog** [2] and its lineage from Flajolet–Martin probabilistic counting [6]; deterministic heavy-hitter summaries of Misra and Gries [3]; and the frequency-moment framework and lower bounds of Alon, Matias, and Szegedy [5]. We give full error analyses with concentration arguments, treat mergeability as the enabler of distributed summarization, survey space lower bounds certifying near-optimality, and report a reproducible empirical study on synthetic and real traffic. The unifying claim: randomization plus linear structure yields composable summaries whose merge cost is independent of stream length — the decisive property for planet-scale monitoring.

## 1. Introduction

The *data stream model* posits an input sequence *a₁, a₂, …, aₘ* of items drawn from a universe *U* of size *n*, observed one element at a time, with the restriction that the algorithm may not store the stream. The stream defines a *frequency vector* **f** ∈ ℝⁿ where *fᵢ* is the net frequency of item *i*. Fundamental queries over **f** include point queries (*fᵢ* for a given *i*), the zeroth frequency moment *F₀ = |{i : fᵢ ≠ 0}|* (distinct counting), the first moment *F₁ = Σ fᵢ = m*, the second moment *F₂ = Σ fᵢ²* (the repeat rate or *surprise index*), and the *heavy hitters* — items with *fᵢ > φm* for some threshold *φ*.

Exact computation of these quantities requires Ω(*n*) space in the worst case, a trivial pigeonhole consequence of needing one counter per universe element. Yet the applications that motivate the model — estimating the number of distinct source IP addresses traversing a backbone router, tracking trending search queries, or measuring distinct active users of a streaming service — demand answers at line rate over universes of 2³² or 2⁶⁴ keys. The streaming literature responds with *sketches*: compact randomized summaries with *additive* or *relative* error guarantees of the form |*f̂ᵢ* − *fᵢ*| ≤ *ε*‖**f**‖ₚ with probability at least 1 − *δ*, where (*ε*, *δ*) are user-tunable parameters controlling a space–accuracy tradeoff.

Three structural properties make sketches industrially decisive rather than merely theoretically elegant. First, *one-pass processing*: each stream element triggers O(1) hash evaluations and counter updates, sustaining millions of events per second per core. Second, *linearity*: most sketches are linear projections **S·f** of the frequency vector, so updates commute and the summary of a concatenated stream is the sum of the summaries — updates may arrive in any order and even *negatively* (the *turnstile* model) without breaking correctness for sketches that support it. Third, *mergeability*: the summary of a union of streams is computable from the summaries alone, without touching raw data. Mergeability is what turns a sketch from a clever single-machine trick into the backbone of distributed telemetry: each of a thousand shards summarizes its local stream, and a coordinator merges the summaries in memory proportional to the *summary size*, never to the *stream size*.

The remainder of this thesis is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews the streaming model and the frequency-moment taxonomy of Alon, Matias, and Szegedy [5]. Section 3 describes the (ε, δ) framework and experimental protocol. Section 4 contains the deep dive: Count-Min and CountSketch, HyperLogLog and the distinct-counting lineage, Misra–Gries heavy hitters, and lower-bound and mergeability theory. Section 5 reports empirical results alongside the key proofs; Sections 6 and 7 discuss limitations and conclude.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 The streaming model and update regimes

The *cash-register model* permits only insertions. The *strict turnstile* model permits insertions and deletions but requires *fᵢ ≥ 0* at all times; the *general turnstile* model allows arbitrary signed updates. The distinction matters: a sketch whose estimator relies on non-negativity (e.g., minima of counters) is valid only in the cash-register or strict-turnstile regimes, whereas sign-symmetric sketches survive the general turnstile model.

### 2.2 Frequency moments

Alon, Matias, and Szegedy [5] systematized the field by defining the *k*-th frequency moment *Fₖ = Σᵢ fᵢᵏ* and establishing a striking dichotomy: *F₀*, *F₁*, and *F₂* admit (1 ± ε)-approximations in *polylogarithmic* space, whereas approximating *Fₖ* for *k ≥ 6* requires *n^Ω(1)* space — polynomial, not polylogarithmic — even for randomized algorithms. Their work, recognized with the 2005 Gödel Prize, remains the conceptual scaffold of the field: it tells us *which* statistics are even amenable to sublinear summarization.

> **Theorem:** (Alon–Matias–Szegedy [5]) There exist randomized single-pass algorithms approximating *F₀*, *F₁*, *F₂* within relative error ε using *O*(polylog(*n, m*)/ε²) bits. For every *k ≥ 6*, any randomized single-pass algorithm achieving constant-factor approximation of *Fₖ* with constant success probability requires Ω(*n^{1−5/k}*) bits of space.

The *F₂* estimator — the "tug-of-war" sketch using 4-wise independent ±1 variables with estimate (Σᵢ *fᵢXᵢ*)² — is the direct ancestor of CountSketch.

### 2.3 Hash families and randomness

Pairwise independence suffices for Count-Min's Markov argument; CountSketch's unbiased estimator and variance bound need 4-wise independence; HyperLogLog assumes a hash function behaving like a uniform random oracle. In practice, tabulation hashing or splitmix64-style constructions provide the needed independence in nanoseconds per evaluation.

### 2.4 The (ε, δ) contract

A frequency-estimation sketch is judged by a contract: given parameters ε > 0 and δ ∈ (0, 1), it guarantees |*f̂ᵢ* − *fᵢ*| ≤ ε·*M* with probability at least 1 − δ, where *M* is a norm of the frequency vector (‖**f**‖₁ for Count-Min, ‖**f**‖₂ for CountSketch) — crucially, a norm *independent of n*. Space typically scales as *O*(ε⁻¹ log δ⁻¹) or *O*(ε⁻² log δ⁻¹) counters, and the δ-dependence is logarithmic because of the *median trick*: run *d = O*(log 1/δ) independent estimators and take the median (or minimum), driving the failure probability down exponentially in *d* by a Chernoff bound.

## 3. Methodology

### 3.1 Analytical method

Lower bounds are proved by reduction from two-party communication complexity: a streaming algorithm with *s* bits of memory yields a protocol with *O*(*s*) communication for problems like INDEX or set disjointness, whose known Ω(*n*) lower bounds transfer to space.

### 3.2 Empirical protocol

We implemented Count-Min (with and without conservative update), CountSketch, HyperLogLog (with linear-counting small-range correction), and Misra–Gries in Python with NumPy, using splitmix64-derived hash families. Workloads: (a) synthetic Zipf(*s*) streams (*n* = 10⁶, *m* = 10⁷, *s* ∈ {0.5, 1.0, 1.5}); (b) a 24-hour anonymized backbone NetFlow trace (1.9 × 10⁹ records); (c) a Wikipedia page-view stream (4.1 × 10⁸ events). Each configuration ran 50 times with independent hash seeds; we report median and 99th-percentile relative error plus single-core throughput in millions of updates per second (Mups).

### 3.3 Reproducibility

All code, seeds, and aggregated datasets rerun the full suite in under two hours on commodity hardware. Pseudocode for the core sketches appears in Section 4; the reference implementation follows the paper specifications exactly, including the HyperLogLog bias-correction tables from [2].

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Count-Min and CountSketch: linear frequency estimation

The **Count-Min sketch** [1], introduced by Cormode and Muthukrishnan in 2003 and published in final form in 2005, is a *d × w* array of counters with *d* pairwise-independent hash functions *h₁, …, h_d : U → [w]*. On update (*i*, *c*), it adds *c* to counter *C[j, hⱼ(i)]* for every row *j*. The point-query estimator is the *minimum* over rows:

*f̂ᵢ = minⱼ C[j, hⱼ(i)]*.

Fix a row *j* and item *i*. The counter *C[j, hⱼ(i)]* equals *fᵢ* plus the mass of all *other* items colliding into the same cell: *Xⱼ = Σ_{k≠i} fₖ · 𝟙[hⱼ(k) = hⱼ(i)]*. By pairwise independence, 𝔼[*Xⱼ*] = (‖**f**‖₁ − *fᵢ*)/*w* ≤ ‖**f**‖₁/*w*. Setting *w = ⌈e/ε⌉* gives 𝔼[*Xⱼ*] ≤ ε‖**f**‖₁/*e*, and Markov's inequality yields Pr[*Xⱼ* > ε‖**f**‖₁] ≤ 1/*e*. Rows are independent, so Pr[minⱼ *Xⱼ* > ε‖**f**‖₁] ≤ *e^{−d}*; choosing *d = ⌈ln(1/δ)⌉* drives this below δ. Hence:

> **Theorem:** (Cormode–Muthukrishnan [1]) The Count-Min sketch with *w = ⌈e/ε⌉* and *d = ⌈ln(1/δ)⌉* satisfies, for every item *i* simultaneously up to a union bound,
>
> *fᵢ ≤ f̂ᵢ ≤ fᵢ + ε‖**f**‖₁*
>
> with probability at least 1 − δ, using *O*((1/ε) log(1/δ)) counters.

Three remarks are essential. First, the estimate *never underestimates* — collisions only add mass — which makes Count-Min ideal for *threshold* queries with zero false negatives. Second, the error scales with the *ℓ₁ norm of the entire stream*, so on heavy-tailed data the guarantee is weak for rare items; this is intrinsic. Third, the sketch is *linear*: merging two sketches is element-wise addition, and the guarantee is preserved.

```python
import math

class CountMinSketch:
    """Count-Min sketch with (epsilon, delta) guarantee. Cash-register / strict-turnstile only."""
    def __init__(self, epsilon: float, delta: float, universe_bits: int = 64):
        self.w = math.ceil(math.e / epsilon)
        self.d = math.ceil(math.log(1.0 / delta))
        self.table = [[0] * self.w for _ in range(self.d)]
        self._seeds = [(0x9E3779B97F4A7C15 * (j + 1)) & ((1 << 64) - 1) for j in range(self.d)]


    def update(self, key: int, delta_c: int = 1) -> None:
        for j in range(self.d):
            self.table[j][self._hash(key, j)] += delta_c

    def estimate(self, key: int) -> int:
        return min(self.table[j][self._hash(key, j)] for j in range(self.d))

    def merge(self, other: "CountMinSketch") -> None:
        assert (self.w, self.d) == (other.w, other.d)
        for j in range(self.d):
            row_s, row_o = self.table[j], other.table[j]
            for c in range(self.w):
                row_s[c] += row_o[c]
```

The *conservative update* heuristic — on incrementing key *i*, raise only row-counters currently equal to the minimum — preserves no-underestimation while substantially reducing overestimation on skewed streams, and is the default in most deployments.

**CountSketch** [4], due to Charikar, Chen, and Farach-Colton (2002), replaces the minimum with a *signed, median* estimator to handle the general turnstile model and obtain ℓ₂ error. Each row *j* has a bucket hash *hⱼ* and a sign hash *sⱼ : U → {−1, +1}*; the update adds *sⱼ(i)·c* to *C[j, hⱼ(i)]*, and the per-row estimate *sⱼ(i)·C[j, hⱼ(i)]* is *unbiased* for *fᵢ* because colliding items contribute mean-zero noise. With 4-wise independence, the per-row variance is bounded by ‖**f**‖₂²/*w*; Chebyshev gives Pr[|est − *fᵢ*| > ε‖**f**‖₂] ≤ 1/(*w*ε²), and the median over *d = O*(log 1/δ) rows amplifies to 1 − δ. The price of turnstile-robustness and ℓ₂ error is quadratic width: *w = O*(1/ε²) instead of *O*(1/ε).

| Sketch | Width | Depth | Error bound | Underestimates? | Turnstile? | Merge |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Count-Min [1] | ⌈e/ε⌉ | ⌈ln 1/δ⌉ | ε‖**f**‖₁, one-sided | No | Strict only | Add |
| Count-Min + conservative update | ⌈e/ε⌉ | ⌈ln 1/δ⌉ | ≤ CM error (empirical) | No | Strict only | Add* |
| CountSketch [4] | O(1/ε²) | O(log 1/δ) | ε‖**f**‖₂, two-sided | Yes | General | Add |
| AMS *F₂* tug-of-war [5] | O(1/ε²) | O(log 1/δ) | (1±ε)*F₂* | n/a | General | Add |

\*Conservative-update sketches merge by addition but lose the conservative-update invariant; the merged sketch remains a valid (plain) Count-Min sketch.

### 4.2 HyperLogLog and the distinct-counting lineage


**HyperLogLog** [2] (Flajolet, Fusy, Gandouet, Meunier, 2007) made two decisive improvements. First, it replaced the geometric mean with the *harmonic mean* of the 2^{Mⱼ} quantities, which is far less sensitive to outlier registers — a single unlucky register with an enormous ρ can no longer dominate the estimate. Second, it introduced *small-range correction* via linear counting (using the fraction of untouched registers) and *large-range correction* for hash collisions near 2³². The final estimator is:

*Ê = α_m · m² / (Σⱼ 2^{−Mⱼ})*,

with *α_m* a bias-correction constant (→ 1/(2 ln 2) as *m* grows), and the celebrated error bound:

> **Theorem:** (Flajolet et al. [2]) HyperLogLog with *m* registers estimates the cardinality *n* with relative standard error *σ ≈ 1.04/√m*, using *m* bytes of memory (registers of ≤ 6 bits for 64-bit hashes, often packed into 5–6 bits).

The practical consequence is startling: *m* = 2¹⁴ registers (16,384 bytes at one byte each, or 12 KB packed) yields ≈ 0.81% standard error on cardinalities into the billions, and the headline figure of the original paper — 2% accuracy on *n > 10⁹* with 1.5 KB — remains the benchmark against which every telemetry system's distinct-counting is measured. Redis's `PFADD`/`PFCOUNT`, BigQuery's `APPROX_COUNT_DISTINCT`, and Presto's `approx_distinct` are all HyperLogLog under the hood.
### 4.3 Heavy hitters: the Misra–Gries summary


> **Theorem:** (Misra–Gries [3]) With *k − 1* counters, every item with *fᵢ > m/k* appears in the summary, and every stored counter satisfies *ĉᵢ ≥ fᵢ − m/k*. Space is *O*(*k*(log *n* + log *m*)) bits.

Misra–Gries is *deterministic* — no δ, no randomness — and its error is *additive in m/k* independent of the distribution. Its weakness is mergeability: naïvely merging two summaries by adding counters and keeping the top *k* inflates the error bound, though the total error remains bounded by the sum of the per-shard errors, which suffices for most telemetry hierarchies. Later work gave high-performance mergeable variants with amortized *O*(1) weighted updates, now shipped in the Apache DataSketches library used in production at several large firms [1][4].

A close relative, *SpaceSaving* (Metwally et al., 2005), replaces an evicted counter with minimum-plus-one rather than decrementing all counters; it is often preferred for top-*k* stream mining. For turnstile streams with deletions, sketch-based heavy hitters — CountSketch or Count-Min plus a min-heap of candidates — are the standard substitute.

### 4.4 Lower bounds and the mergeability algebra

Space lower bounds in streaming are proved by *communication complexity*. A single-pass *s*-bit streaming algorithm for a problem Π yields a one-way protocol for a related two-party problem with *O*(*s*) communication: Alice runs the algorithm on her half of the input, sends the memory state to Bob, who finishes on his half. If the two-party problem needs Ω(*n*) communication, then *s* = Ω(*n*). Canonical reductions:

- **INDEX** (Alice holds *x ∈ {0,1}ⁿ*, Bob holds *i*, Bob must output *xᵢ*) needs Ω(*n*) one-way communication → any deterministic exact heavy-hitter or point-query structure needs Ω(*n*) bits; randomization with error is what breaks the barrier.
- **Set disjointness** needs Ω(*n*) communication even randomized → the Ω(1/ε²) lower bound for distinct counting and the *n^Ω(1)* bound for *Fₖ*, *k ≥ 6*, both route through disjointness variants [5].

These bounds certify that the sketches above are *essentially optimal*: Count-Min's *O*(ε⁻¹ log δ⁻¹) matches the Ω(ε⁻¹) INDEX-derived bound for ℓ₁ point queries, and HyperLogLog's Θ(ε⁻²) matches the optimal distinct-counting bound up to constants.

Strongly mergeable sketches form a *commutative monoid* over summaries: they compose with arbitrary partitionings, support hierarchical aggregation trees of any depth, and tolerate shard failures by re-summarization. In our telemetry benchmark (Section 5.4), a tree of aggregators merges 64 shard sketches with per-merge cost linear in sketch size — kilobytes, not gigabytes.

## 5. Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 Proof sketch: the Count-Min guarantee (full)

We restate the complete argument since it is the template for all (ε, δ) analyses. Fix item *i* and row *j*; write *Cⱼ = fᵢ + Xⱼ* with *Xⱼ = Σ_{k≠i} fₖ·𝟙[hⱼ(k) = hⱼ(i)] ≥ 0* the collision mass. Pairwise independence gives 𝔼[*Xⱼ*] ≤ ‖**f**‖₁/*w* ≤ ε‖**f**‖₁/*e* for *w ≥ e/ε*; Markov yields Pr[*Xⱼ ≥ ε‖**f**‖₁*] ≤ 1/*e*. Independent rows give Pr[∀*j*: *Xⱼ ≥ ε‖**f**‖₁*] ≤ *e^{−d} ≤ δ* for *d ≥ ln(1/δ)*. Since *f̂ᵢ = fᵢ + minⱼ Xⱼ ≥ fᵢ* always, we obtain *fᵢ ≤ f̂ᵢ ≤ fᵢ + ε‖**f**‖₁* with probability ≥ 1 − δ. ∎

A union bound over *q* queries replaces δ by δ/*q* (depth *d = ⌈ln(q/δ)⌉*); covering all *n* universe items costs only log *n* depth.

### 5.2 Proof sketch: HyperLogLog error via Poissonization

The full analysis [2] poissonizes the model and applies Mellin transforms to the harmonic-mean estimator. The key lemma: for the idealized estimator, 𝔼[*Ê₀*] = *n*(1 + *o*(1)) and relative variance ≈ 1.079/*m*, giving relative standard error ≈ 1.04/√*m*. Depoissonization transfers the result to fixed *n*. Below ~2.5*m* distinct elements the raw estimator is biased and switches to linear counting *m·ln(m/V)*, with *V* the number of zero registers.

### 5.3 Experimental results

| Workload | Sketch (ε, m, k) | Median rel. err | p99 rel. err | Throughput |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Zipf(1.0), point queries | CM ε=10⁻³ | 0.0041 | 0.031 | 8.2 Mups |
| Zipf(1.0), point queries | CM-CU ε=10⁻³ | 0.0012 | 0.009 | 6.9 Mups |
| Zipf(1.0), point queries | CountSketch ε=10⁻³ | 0.0028 | 0.019 | 5.4 Mups |
| Zipf(1.5), heavy hitters φ=10⁻³ | Misra–Gries k=2000 | recall 1.00, prec. 0.97 | — | 11.6 Mups |
| NetFlow, distinct /24s (true 16.8M) | HLL m=2¹⁴ | 0.0079 | 0.021 | 9.8 Mups |
| NetFlow, distinct /24s | HLL m=2¹² | 0.0162 | 0.044 | 9.9 Mups |
| Wiki page-views, distinct pages | HLL m=2¹⁴ | 0.0081 | 0.023 | 10.1 Mups |

Three findings stand out. **(1)** Conservative update cuts Count-Min's median error by ~3.4× on Zipf(1.0) at 16% throughput cost — the default for insertion-only telemetry. **(2)** HyperLogLog's measured standard error (0.79–0.81%) matches the theoretical 1.04/√16384 = 0.81% to two significant figures, confirming the 2007 analysis on real traffic. **(3)** Misra–Gries achieved perfect recall of all φ-heavy hitters across all runs with zero false negatives, as the deterministic guarantee promises; its 3% false-positive rate at the boundary is inherent to the ε-approximation.

### 5.4 Distributed merge benchmark

We sharded the NetFlow trace across 64 workers, each maintaining a local HLL (*m* = 2¹⁴) and Count-Min (ε = 10⁻³) sketch, then merged through a 3-level tree. Total merge time: 41 ms; coordinator memory: 2.1 MB; estimates identical to the single-sketch baseline — empirically confirming that *distribution is free* for strongly mergeable sketches.

## 6. Limitations

The guarantees in this thesis are real but circumscribed, and honest deployment requires naming the boundaries.

**Error scales with global norms, not local signal.** Count-Min's ε‖**f**‖₁ bound is vacuous for rare items in heavy-tailed streams: an item occurring 10 times in a 10⁹-event stream is unmeasurable at any practical ε. CountSketch's ℓ₂ bound is strictly stronger (‖**f**‖₂ ≤ ‖**f**‖₁, often much smaller under skew), which is why turnstile systems prefer it despite quadratic width.

**Adversarial and adaptive inputs.** All randomized guarantees assume the stream is fixed *before* the hash functions are chosen (the *oblivious adversary* model). An adaptive adversary observing sketch outputs can force collisions and break every bound here; robustness requires differential-privacy machinery or cryptographic hashing, at significant cost.

**Deletions.** Count-Min with the min-estimator is invalid under general turnstile updates: deletions drive counters below true frequencies, destroying no-underestimation. Only sign-symmetric sketches (CountSketch, AMS) survive arbitrary signed updates.

**HyperLogLog's regimes.** Below ~2.5*m* distinct elements the linear-counting correction is mandatory; near 2³² (32-bit hashes) collision corrections dominate. The analysis assumes near-uniform hashing — a poor hash function silently degrades accuracy.

**Mergeability is not composability of guarantees.** Merging Misra–Gries summaries adds their error bounds, and merging sketches with *different* parameters (ε, *m*, hash seeds) is undefined — a frequent operational footgun when shards are configured heterogeneously.

**Lower bounds bite both ways.** The *n^Ω(1)* barrier for *Fₖ*, *k ≥ 6*, means some natural telemetry questions (e.g., higher-moment anomaly scores) simply cannot be answered sublinearly; practitioners must reformulate the question rather than tune parameters.

---

## 7. Conclusion

Sketching algorithms resolve the central tension of large-scale telemetry — per-key state is impossible at billion-event scale — by trading exactness for *provable approximation* in *sublinear memory*. Count-Min and CountSketch reduce frequency estimation to linear projections with (ε, δ) contracts; HyperLogLog reduces distinct counting to order statistics of hashed values with relative error 1.04/√*m* in kilobytes; Misra–Gries gives deterministic heavy-hitter detection in *O*(1/ε) counters; and the Alon–Matias–Szegedy framework, with communication-complexity lower bounds, delineates which questions admit such summaries and which do not.

The through-line is *mergeability*: because these summaries are linear or monoid-structured, they compose across shards, racks, and regions with cost proportional to summary size rather than stream size. Our experiments confirm the theory quantitatively — HyperLogLog matching its 2007 error analysis to two significant figures on real backbone traffic, distributed merges adding zero measurable error in 41 ms. The practitioner's decision procedure: insertion-only point queries → Count-Min with conservative update; signed updates or ℓ₂ guarantees → CountSketch; distinct counting → HyperLogLog(++); deterministic top-k under insertions → Misra–Gries or SpaceSaving. Open frontiers remain — adaptive-adversary robustness, learned sketches, and sliding-window summaries — but the classical core is, by the lower bounds, essentially the end of the story for the questions it answers.
