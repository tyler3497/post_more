# Graph Neural Networks and the Weisfeiler–Leman Hierarchy: Expressivity Limits and Higher-Order Architectures

## Abstract

Graph neural networks inherit their inductive bias from graph topology, yet for years their power to distinguish non-isomorphic structures lacked a precise characterization. This thesis develops a unified account of the expressivity theory of graph neural networks through the classical Weisfeiler–Leman color-refinement hierarchy. We show that message-passing GNNs are bounded above by the one-dimensional test, 1-WL, and identify the exact architectural condition — injective neighborhood aggregation — under which this bound is attained, as realized by the Graph Isomorphism Network [2]. We then survey the three principal escape routes from this ceiling: higher-order tensor architectures whose discriminative power tracks the k-WL tests [1][3]; topological lifts to simplicial and cell complexes that transcend 1-WL while preserving linear-time local message passing [5]; and subgraph-based encodings that inject substructure counts or aggregate over bags of subgraphs [4][6]. Throughout we quantify the computational price of expressivity, report empirical evidence from molecular and synthetic benchmarks in the TUDataset collection [7], and close with open questions on the gap between expressivity and learnability.

##1 Introduction

Learning over graphs requires architectures that respect permutation symmetry: relabeling the vertices of a graph must relabel the learned representations in the same way, and graph-level predictions must be invariant to relabeling altogether. Message-passing neural networks satisfy these constraints by construction, propagating information along edges and pooling with symmetric functions. Empirically, they dominate molecular property prediction, social network analysis, and combinatorial optimization. Yet until 2019, their *theoretical* capabilities were poorly understood. Practitioners could not say, for a given pair of non-isomorphic graphs, whether any message-passing model could in principle separate them — a foundational question, since graph-structured data derives much of its semantics from exactly such distinctions.

The answer arrived by reconnecting deep learning with a 1960s graph-isomorphism heuristic. The Weisfeiler–Leman procedure, originally devised for the graph-isomorphism problem, refines a coloring of vertices by iteratively hashing each vertex's color together with the multiset of its neighbors' colors [1]. Two papers appearing within months of each other — one introducing higher-order graph neural networks [1], the other analyzing the expressive power of message-passing networks and proposing the Graph Isomorphism Network [2] — established that standard GNNs are *exactly* as discriminative as this color-refinement test. The result is both a ceiling and a compass: a ceiling, because 1-WL fails on many elementary structures, including all regular graphs of the same degree; a compass, because every subsequent architecture can be located on the expressivity map the hierarchy provides.

This thesis presents the theory in full. Section 2 defines the WL hierarchy and the message-passing formalism. Section 3 describes the analytic methodology — simulation arguments, universality conditions, and complexity accounting. Section 4 examines the four great families of expressive architectures: k-GNNs, equivariant tensor networks, subgraph encodings, and topological lifts. Section 5 reports empirical evidence and formal theorems, and Sections 6 and 7 discuss limitations and conclude.

##2 Background

A graph is a pair G = (V, E) with vertex set V and edge set E, possibly carrying continuous attributes on vertices and edges. Two graphs G and H are *isomorphic* when a bijection of vertices maps edges to edges. The graph-isomorphism problem admits no known polynomial-time algorithm in the worst case, and the Weisfeiler–Leman tests are a family of combinatorial heuristics that decide isomorphism correctly on large classes of graphs in polynomial time.

The one-dimensional test, 1-WL (also called *color refinement* or *naive vertex classification*), proceeds as follows [1]:

1. Initialize every vertex with the same color.
2. In each iteration, replace the color of a vertex v by the pair consisting of its old color and the *multiset* of old colors of its neighbors.
3. Stop when the partition stabilizes; if two graphs yield different color histograms, they are non-isomorphic.

The multiset is essential: two vertices receive the same refined color precisely when their neighborhoods are color-isomorphic, so the coloring encodes the *unfolding tree* rooted at each vertex. Graphs that 1-WL cannot separate — for example, a 6-cycle versus two disjoint triangles, or any pair of d-regular graphs with equal order — are exactly those whose vertices have identical unfolding trees [2].

Higher-order variants color *k-tuples* of vertices instead of single vertices. In k-WL, the initial color of a tuple records its isomorphism type (which pairs are adjacent, which entries coincide), and refinement aggregates over tuples that differ in one coordinate. The resulting hierarchy is strict for sufficiently large k: (k+1)-WL distinguishes graphs that k-WL conflates, and the Cai–Fürer–Immerman construction provides explicit hard families separating consecutive levels.

Message-passing GNNs, meanwhile, compute node representations by the recursion

h_v^t = COMBINE( h_v^{t-1}, AGG( { h_u^{t-1} : u in N(v) } ) )

where AGG is a permutation-invariant neighborhood aggregator (sum, mean, max, or attention) and COMBINE merges the result with the node's own state [2]. After T layers, a graph representation is obtained by a permutation-invariant READOUT over all node states. The structural parallel with 1-WL — replace colors by vectors, multisets by aggregators, hashing by learned functions — is what makes the expressivity connection possible.

##3 Methodology

Our analysis rests on three methodological pillars.

**Reduction and simulation.** To bound a neural architecture, we simulate it inside the combinatorial test: we show that whenever the architecture assigns different representations to two graphs, the test also separates them. Conversely, to prove a lower bound (the architecture is *at least* as powerful as the test), we construct neural modules that faithfully implement the test's refinement steps — injective multiset functions for aggregation, injective hashing for color updates [2].

**Universality via injective aggregation.** A central lemma, proved in the analysis of the Graph Isomorphism Network, states that a message-passing layer is injective on multisets of neighbor features if and only if its aggregator is an injective multiset function followed by an injective combine [2]. Sums over a learned embedding followed by a multilayer perceptron satisfy this; means and maxima do not, because they collapse distinct multisets. Architectures meeting the condition are *maximally expressive* among message-passing models.

**Complexity accounting.** Expressivity is never free. We track, for each architecture family, the time and memory cost per layer as a function of the number of vertices n, edges m, and the order k: 1-WL-style message passing costs linear time in the size of the (lifted) structure, while k-tuple methods cost polynomial time of degree k or higher. Empirical validation follows the standardized protocols of the TUDataset collection, which aggregates more than 120 graph classification and regression datasets with fixed evaluation procedures [7].

```python
# 1-WL color refinement: the combinatorial yardstick
def wl_refine(adj, max_iter=10):
    n = len(adj)
    colors = [0] * n  # uniform initialization
    for _ in range(max_iter):
        sigs = []
        for v in range(n):
            neigh = tuple(sorted(colors[u] for u in adj[v]))
            sigs.append((colors[v], neigh))
        # injective recoloring: compress signatures to new colors
        mapping = {}
        new_colors = []
        for s in sigs:
            if s not in mapping:
                mapping[s] = len(mapping)
            new_colors.append(mapping[s])
        if new_colors == colors:
            break
        colors = new_colors
    return colors
```

##4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Message Passing Is Bounded by 1-WL

The foundational negative result states that no message-passing GNN, regardless of depth, width, or learned parameters, can distinguish graphs that 1-WL deems identical [1][2]. The proof is an induction over layers: if two vertices share the same 1-WL color at iteration t, an identical induction shows their GNN embeddings coincide at layer t, because both procedures aggregate the same multiset of neighbor states through symmetric functions. Global READOUT, being permutation invariant, preserves the collision.

> Theorem: *For any message-passing GNN and any two graphs G, H that 1-WL cannot distinguish, the GNN produces identical graph-level representations for G and H.*

The bound bites in practice. All d-regular graphs on n vertices are 1-WL-indistinguishable, so a standard GNN cannot tell a molecular ring from a collection of disconnected cycles — a distinction chemists consider elementary [2]. This single theorem explains a long trail of empirical failures on synthetic benchmarks and motivates everything that follows.

### 4.2 Attaining the Bound: Injective Aggregation and GIN

The ceiling is reachable. Xu et al. showed that a GNN matches 1-WL exactly when its neighborhood aggregation is *injective* on multisets [2]. Their Graph Isomorphism Network instantiates this with

h_v^t = MLP^t( (1 + eps^t) * h_v^{t-1} + sum_{u in N(v)} h_u^{t-1} )

where the sum is injective over multisets of countably supported features and the MLP is a universal approximator of the injective color-hashing map. Mean and max aggregators fail the injectivity condition: mean cannot distinguish a neighborhood of two identical vectors from one, and max cannot distinguish multiplicities at all [2]. Empirically, GIN tops the message-passing leaderboard on the TUDataset graph-classification benchmarks, confirming that the theory predicts practice [2][7].

```haskell
-- Types for k-WL tuple coloring: colors refine over k-tuples
data TupleColor k = TupleColor
  { isoType  :: IsoType k        -- adjacency + equality pattern
  , history  :: [Multiset (TupleColor k)]  -- one multiset per coordinate
  }

refine :: Int -> Graph -> [Tuple k] -> Map (Tuple k) Color
refine k g tuples = fixpoint step initial
  where
    step coloring = compress (\s -> (coloring s, neighbors coloring s)) tuples
    neighbors coloring s = [ multiset [ coloring (replace s j w)
                                      | w <- vertices g ]
                           | j <- [1..k] ]
```

### 4.3 Higher-Order Architectures: k-GNNs and Equivariant Tensor Networks

Morris et al. generalized message passing from vertices to *k-sets* of vertices [1]. In a k-GNN, each k-set s carries a feature vector h_s^t, and layers aggregate over the *neighborhood* of s — all k-sets differing from s in exactly one element. Initial features encode the isomorphism type of the induced subgraph on s. The main theorem establishes a precise correspondence: k-GNNs are exactly as discriminative as the set-based variant of k-WL, and the hierarchy of architectures mirrors the strictness of the WL hierarchy [1]. Experiments on molecular regression (QM9) and social-network classification show consistent gains over 1-WL-bounded models, confirming that higher-order structure — triangles, rings, cliques — carries learnable signal [1].

A parallel algebraic line builds *k-order invariant and equivariant networks* operating on tensors indexed by k-tuples, with linear layers characterized via the Bell numbers [3]. Maron et al. proved that these networks match k-WL in distinguishing power, and — crucially — exhibited a *reduced 2-order* model that achieves 3-WL expressivity using only order-2 tensors: the architecture interleaves MLPs applied to the feature dimension with a single quadratic operation, matrix multiplication [3]. Since 1-WL and 2-WL are equivalent while 3-WL is strictly stronger, this yields the first practical model provably beyond message passing, running in cubic time rather than the far steeper cost of naive k-tuple methods.

| Architecture | WL level matched | Per-layer cost | Key mechanism |
|---|---|---|---|
| MPNN / GCN / GraphSAGE | 1-WL | O(m) | neighborhood aggregation |
| GIN | 1-WL (maximal) | O(m) | injective sum + MLP |
| k-GNN | k-WL (set variant) | O(n^k) tuples | message passing on k-sets |
| k-order invariant nets | k-WL | O(n^{2k}) | equivariant tensor layers |
| Reduced 2-order (PPGN) | 3-WL | O(n^3) | MLP + matrix multiplication [3] |
| GSN | beyond 1-WL (not in hierarchy) | O(m), preprocessing extra | substructure count features [4] |
| CW Networks | at least 3-WL | linear in complex size | cellular message passing [5] |
| ESAN (DSS-GNN) | between 1-WL and 3-WL | O(n) subgraphs x O(m) | bag of subgraphs [6] |

```rust
// k-set adjacency in a k-GNN: two k-sets are neighbors
// when their symmetric difference has size 2
fn kset_neighbors(s: &[usize], n: usize) -> Vec<Vec<usize>> {
    let mut out = Vec::new();
    for (j, _) in s.iter().enumerate() {
        for w in 0..n {
            if s.contains(&w) { continue; }
            let mut t = s.to_vec();
            t[j] = w;
            t.sort_unstable();
            out.push(t);
        }
    }
    out
}
```

### 4.4 Escaping the Hierarchy: Subgraph Encodings and Topological Lifts

Two families sidestep the WL hierarchy rather than climbing it, retaining the linear-time locality of message passing.

**Subgraph encodings.** Graph Substructure Networks augment each node's features with counts of *subgraph isomorphisms* of small pattern graphs (triangles, cycles, cliques) in which the node participates [4]. Because 1-WL cannot count substructures, these features provably break the 1-WL barrier; with suitable pattern families the architecture is universal, yet per-layer complexity stays linear since counting is a preprocessing step. The design also admits domain priors — chemists supply rings and functional groups as patterns — which explains GSN's strong molecular results [4].

**Bags of subgraphs.** Equivariant Subgraph Aggregation Networks represent a graph as a *set of subgraphs* generated by a policy such as node deletion or edge deletion, encode each with a shared base GNN, and aggregate with an equivariant set architecture [6]. Bevilacqua et al. develop new 1-WL variants tailored to bags of subgraphs and prove matching lower bounds: ESAN with the DSS (deep set of subgraphs) layer is strictly more expressive than any MPNN and bounded above by 3-WL, with a stochastic sampling scheme controlling the computational overhead [6].

**Topological lifts.** Message passing can be decoupled from the input graph entirely by *lifting* the graph to a higher-dimensional complex — its clique complex (simplicial) or, more flexibly, a regular cell complex whose 2-cells may be arbitrary rings [5]. Cellular WL refines colors of cells of all dimensions using boundary, coboundary, and adjacency relations; the resulting CW Networks are strictly more expressive than 1-WL and at least as expressive as 3-WL, while message passing stays linear in the size of the complex [5]. On molecules, ring-based lifts capture exactly the substructures chemists care about, yielding state-of-the-art property prediction.

##5 Empirical Results and Proofs

**Formal core.** The section's theorems are proved by mutual simulation. The upper bound (MPNN at most 1-WL) follows by induction on layers, as sketched in Section 4.1 [1][2]. The lower bound (GIN attains 1-WL) uses the injectivity lemma: sums are injective multiset aggregators, and MLPs universally approximate the injective hash, so each layer can realize one refinement iteration [2]. For k-GNNs, Morris et al. show that k-set message passing simulates set-based k-WL and vice versa [1]; for tensor networks, Maron et al. reduce k-order equivariant layers to k-WL refinement via multisymmetric power-sum polynomials [3]. Subgraph results rest on the 1-WL inability to count triangles — any two graphs with identical unfolding trees but different triangle counts separate GSN from MPNNs [4] — and cellular results on the observation that ring 2-cells disambiguate graphs whose 1-skeleta are WL-identical [5].

**Empirical evidence.** On the TUDataset benchmarks (MUTAG, PTC, PROTEINS, NCI1, IMDB, COLLAB), GIN consistently matches or exceeds earlier MPNNs, validating the injectivity thesis [2][7]. Higher-order models improve further where higher-order structure matters: k-GNNs gain several accuracy points on social-network datasets rich in triangles [1], the reduced 2-order network of Maron et al. sets records on molecular regression tasks at cubic rather than prohibitive cost [3], GSN achieves state-of-the-art on ZINC and OGB molecular benchmarks by injecting cycle counts [4], CW Networks lead on molecular property prediction with ring lifts [5], and ESAN improves every base MPNN it wraps across synthetic expressivity suites and real datasets [6].

##6 Limitations

The WL-centric view of expressivity, powerful as it is, has sharp boundaries.

1. **Expressivity is not learnability.** All upper bounds are *existential*: they assert that some parameter setting implements the refinement, not that gradient descent finds it. Empirically, highly expressive models can underperform simpler ones when data is scarce, because the same capacity that distinguishes hard graphs also fits noise.
2. **Cost of higher order.** k-tuple methods scale as n^k or worse; even the cubic reduced 2-order model [3] strains on graphs beyond a few thousand nodes. Topological and subgraph methods mitigate this but introduce preprocessing costs (subgraph isomorphism counting is NP-hard in general, tamed only by small pattern sizes [4]) and lifting choices that lack a canonical answer.
3. **Continuous attributes.** The classical theory assumes discrete colors; real graphs carry continuous node and edge features. Injectivity arguments extend via universal approximation, but the clean combinatorial correspondence frays, and little is known about expressivity with noisy continuous attributes.
4. **Oversmoothing and depth.** Expressivity results count *layers* as refinement iterations, yet deep GNNs suffer oversmoothing — node representations collapse toward a common vector — so the depth needed to realize the theory may be unreachable in practice.
5. **Beyond isomorphism.** Many tasks (e.g., counting, optimization, generation) are not isomorphism tests; a model that distinguishes more graphs is not automatically better at them. The WL yardstick measures one capability, not general intelligence on graphs.

##7 Conclusion

The Weisfeiler–Leman hierarchy transformed the study of graph neural networks from an empirical art into a principled science. The twin results of 2019 — message passing bounded by 1-WL [1][2] — gave the field its first impossibility theorem, and the architectures that followed mapped the territory above it: higher-order message passing on k-sets [1], equivariant tensor networks culminating in a practical 3-WL model [3], subgraph encodings that trade hierarchy membership for locality and domain priors [4][6], and topological lifts that make higher-order structure first-class [5]. Each advance was validated on standardized benchmarks [7], and each paid a quantified computational price.

The open frontier is no longer *whether* a network can distinguish two graphs but *whether it should*: aligning provable expressivity with learnability, scalability to web-scale graphs, and robustness to continuous noisy attributes. The hierarchy remains the map; the destination is architectures that are expressive where it matters, cheap where it counts, and trainable in practice.
