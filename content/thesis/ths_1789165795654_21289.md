# Quantum Money and Uncloneable Cryptography: Wiesner's Scheme, Public-Key Quantum Money, and the No-Cloning Foundations of Unforgeable Tokens

## Abstract

Quantum money asks whether quantum mechanics can end counterfeiting: a physical token anyone can verify yet no one can copy. From Wiesner's 1983 conjugate-coding proposal — private-key notes encoded in non-orthogonal bases, secured by the no-cloning theorem — this thesis traces uncloneable cryptography through public-key quantum money. We give a self-contained account of Wiesner's scheme with an explicit exponential forgery bound, formalize the public-key security game, and analyze the principal computational constructions: the hidden-subspace states of Aaronson and Christiano, Zhandry's quantum lightning and its win-win reductions to strong collision resistance, and the knot-invariant money of Farhi, Gosset, Hassidim, Lutomirski, and Shor. We extend the analysis to uncloneable encryption, from Broadbent and Lord's random-oracle construction through Anshu, Boddu, and Touchette's revisited bounds to recent progress toward the unconditional uncloneable bit. A Monte Carlo simulation of Wiesner forgeries grounds the theory numerically; we close with the attacks that broke early schemes and the open problems — noise tolerance, standard-model proofs, practical verification — still separating quantum money from deployable currency.

---

## 1. Introduction

Classical information is *intrinsically clonable*. Every bit string can be copied perfectly, and this fact is the root of a thousand security pathologies: stolen credentials, pirated media, and, most relevantly, counterfeit currency. Physical banknotes survive only because of a computational and engineering asymmetry — printing is cheap for the mint and hard for everyone else. Digital information has no such asymmetry. Wiesner's radical insight, conceived in the late 1960s and finally published in 1983 [1], was that **quantum information possesses a built-in asymmetry**: the no-cloning theorem [2] forbids the creation of an identical copy of an unknown quantum state, while still permitting efficient verification by a party holding the right classical secret. A quantum banknote could therefore be *physically* unforgeable in a sense no classical token can match.

The half-century since has transformed a curiosity into a research program. Three conceptual leaps define the field. First, the transition from **private-key** to **public-key** quantum money: Wiesner's notes must be returned to the bank for verification, but the far more useful primitive lets anyone verify a note while only the bank can mint one — a quest that replaced information-theoretic security with computational assumptions. Second, the recognition that unforgeability is one instance of a general phenomenon of **uncloneability**, which also underwrites uncloneable encryption [6], copy-protection, and single-decryptor encryption. Third, the emergence of **quantum lightning** [4], in which even the party that generates a state cannot produce two copies of it — collapsing the distinction between the honest mint and the adversary.

This thesis is a systematic treatment of that trajectory. Our contributions are expository, analytical, and empirical:

1. A self-contained formalization of Wiesner's scheme with an explicit $(3/4)^n$ forgery bound and proof sketch.
2. The public-key quantum money security game and an analysis of the hidden-subspace construction [3], including why its original instantiation fell to algebraic cryptanalysis and how indistinguishability obfuscation repairs it.
3. An exposition of quantum lightning [4], its win-win reductions, and its applications to decentralized currency and proofs of entropy.
4. A comparative study of knot-theoretic candidates [5] and uncloneable encryption [6][7][8], unified by the monogamy-of-entanglement property.
5. A Monte Carlo simulation of Wiesner forgeries validating the theoretical bound numerically.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 The No-Cloning Theorem

> **Theorem (Wootters–Zurek, 1982):** There exists no unitary operation $U$ on $\mathcal{H} \otimes \mathcal{H}$ such that $U(|\psi\rangle \otimes |0\rangle) = |\psi\rangle \otimes |\psi\rangle$ for all $|\psi\rangle \in \mathcal{H}$.

*Proof sketch.* Suppose such a $U$ existed. Then for two states $|\psi\rangle, |\phi\rangle$,
$\langle\psi|\phi\rangle = \langle\psi|\phi\rangle^2$, forcing $\langle\psi|\phi\rangle \in \{0,1\}$ — a contradiction for non-orthogonal, non-identical states. ∎

The theorem is the physical foundation of everything that follows. Critically, it is a statement about *unknown* states: if you know the classical description of $|\psi\rangle$, you can of course prepare arbitrarily many copies. Quantum money exploits precisely this gap — the bank knows the description, the counterfeiter does not.

### 2.2 Conjugate Coding

Wiesner's scheme encodes each bit of a random string in one of two conjugate bases [1]:

$$\{|0\rangle, |1\rangle\} \quad \text{vs.} \quad \{|+\rangle, |-\rangle\}, \quad |\pm\rangle = \frac{|0\rangle \pm |1\rangle}{\sqrt{2}}.$$

Measuring a qubit prepared in one basis with a measurement in the conjugate basis yields a uniformly random outcome and *destroys* the encoded information.

### 2.3 Private-Key vs. Public-Key Quantum Money

The taxonomy of unforgeable quantum tokens is summarized below.

| Scheme | Verification | Security basis | Quantum memory | Public verifiability |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Wiesner [1] | Secret key at bank | Information-theoretic | Long-lived | No |
| BBBW / qPRF variant | Secret key at bank | Computational (qPRF) | Long-lived | No |
| Aaronson–Christiano [3] | Public oracles for $A, A^\perp$ | iO / hidden subspaces | Long-lived | Yes |
| Farhi et al. knots [5] | Public (Alexander poly.) | Knot equivalence hardness | Long-lived | Yes (broken) |
| Zhandry lightning [4] | Public hash | Multi-collision resistance | Long-lived | Yes |
| Broadbent–Lord UE [6] | Decryption key reveal | Random oracle + MoE | Ciphertext lifetime | n/a (encryption) |

> **Definition (Public-key quantum money).** A scheme is a triple of QPT algorithms $(\mathsf{KeyGen}, \mathsf{Mint}, \mathsf{Verify})$ such that: (i) $\mathsf{Verify}(pk, \mathsf{Mint}(sk))$ accepts with overwhelming probability; (ii) verification is *non-destructive* in the gentle-measurement sense; and (iii) no QPT adversary given $pk$ and $q = \mathrm{poly}(n)$ valid notes can output $q+1$ states all accepted by $\mathsf{Verify}$ except with negligible probability.

A fundamental observation, due to Aaronson, is that public-key quantum money *cannot* be information-theoretically secure: given the public verification procedure and unlimited computation, an adversary could simply search for an accepting state.

### 2.4 Monogamy of Entanglement

The modern unifying tool is the **monogamy-of-entanglement (MoE) game**: Alice prepares a state, splits it between Bob and Charlie, and then Bob and Charlie — who can no longer communicate — must simultaneously guess some classical value.

## 3. Methodology

This thesis proceeds by *formal reconstruction and comparative cryptanalysis*.

1. **Formalization.** We restate each scheme in uniform game-based syntax (key generation, minting, verification, counterfeiting game), making explicit where information-theoretic arguments end and computational assumptions begin.
2. **Proof reconstruction.** For Wiesner's scheme we give a complete elementary proof of the $(3/4)^n$ bound. For the computational schemes we reconstruct the reduction structure of the original arguments and identify the exact hardness assumption each consumes.
3. **Attack archaeology.** We review the published breaks — the Pena et al. algebraic attack on noisy Aaronson–Christiano, the Bilyk–Doliskani–Gong Zariski-tangent-space attack, Lutomirski's break of the knot scheme — and extract the design lesson each teaches.
4. **Numerical validation.** We implement a Monte Carlo simulation of the optimal intercept-resend forgery against Wiesner notes, confirming the exponential decay numerically.

We take as primary sources the original papers [1][3][4][5][6] and the revisiting analyses [7][8], supplemented by Aaronson's survey literature and the cryptanalysis literature.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Wiesner's Private-Key Scheme: Construction and the $(3/4)^n$ Bound

The bank samples a secret key $k$ indexing a pseudorandom function $g_k$.

> **Theorem (Wiesner forgery bound):** Any counterfeiter holding one valid note and attempting to produce two notes that both pass verification succeeds with probability at most $(3/4)^n$, even with arbitrary entangled measurements.

*Proof sketch.* Consider a single qubit prepared as one of $\{|0\rangle, |1\rangle, |+\rangle, |-\rangle\}$ uniformly at random. Any CPTP map producing a bipartite output $\rho_{BC}$ faces the optimal $1 \to 2$ cloning fidelity for the BB84 ensemble, which is $3/4$: the optimal strategy is *intercept-resend* — measure in a random basis, then prepare two copies of the outcome. Formally, for each qubit the maximum probability that *both* output marginals pass the bank's projective test is

$$\max_{\mathcal{E}} \frac{1}{4}\sum_{b \in \{0,1,+,-\}} \mathrm{Tr}\big[(\Pi_b \otimes \Pi_b)\,\mathcal{E}(|b\rangle\langle b|)\big] = \frac{3}{4},$$

where $\Pi_b$ is the bank's verification projector.

The bound is tight: intercept-resend achieves it.

```python
import numpy as np

# BB84 ensemble: |0>, |1>, |+>, |->
STATES = {
    '0': np.array([1, 0], dtype=complex),
    '1': np.array([0, 1], dtype=complex),
    '+': np.array([1, 1], dtype=complex) / np.sqrt(2),
    '-': np.array([1, -1], dtype=complex) / np.sqrt(2),
}
BASES = {'Z': ['0', '1'], 'X': ['+', '-']}

def mint_note(n, rng):
    """Bank mints an n-qubit Wiesner note; returns list of (bit, basis)."""
    return [(rng.integers(2), rng.choice(['Z', 'X'])) for _ in range(n)]

def intercept_resend_forge(note, rng):
    """Optimal forgery: measure each qubit in a random basis, resend outcome twice."""
    forged = []
    for bit, true_basis in note:
        meas_basis = rng.choice(['Z', 'X'])
        # Born-rule outcome of measuring the true state in meas_basis
        if meas_basis == true_basis:
            outcome = bit
        else:
            outcome = rng.integers(2)  # conjugate measurement: uniform
        forged.append((outcome, meas_basis))  # prepare eigenstate of outcome
        forged.append((outcome, meas_basis))  # second "copy"
    return forged

def verify(note, candidate, key_check=True):
    """Bank verifies: candidate is a list of 2n (bit, basis) halves; both must match."""
    halves = [candidate[::2], candidate[1::2]]
    for half in halves:
        for (true_bit, true_basis), (c_bit, c_basis) in zip(note, half):
            if c_basis != true_basis or c_bit != true_bit:
                return False
    return True
```

### 4.2 Public-Key Quantum Money from Hidden Subspaces

Aaronson and Christiano [3] gave the first public-key scheme with a security proof.

$$|A\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{|A|}} \sum_{x \in A} |x\rangle.$$

The public key consists of obfuscated membership programs for $A$ and $A^\perp$.

$$|A\rangle\langle A| \propto \sum_{x \in A}\sum_{y \in A^\perp} (-1)^{x\cdot y} |x\rangle\langle y|,$$

implemented via the membership oracles and the quantum Fourier transform over $\mathbb{F}_2^n$.

The scheme's history is a cautionary tale about instantiation.

```haskell
-- Verification of a hidden-subspace money state (oracle model)
verifyMoney :: Oracle -> Oracle -> QuantumState -> Bool
verifyMoney oracleA oracleAperp psi =
  let psi1 = projectOntoSubspace oracleA psi        -- Π_A
      psi2 = qft psi1                               -- Hadamard on all qubits
      psi3 = projectOntoSubspace oracleAperp psi2   -- Π_{A^⊥} in Fourier domain
      psi4 = qft psi3
  in fidelity psi4 psi > 1 - epsilon
```

Zhandry later showed [4] that the Aaronson–Christiano scheme instantiated with quantum-secure $i\mathcal{O}$ is indeed secure — but $i\mathcal{O}$ remains wildly impractical, and no *efficient, non-obfuscated* instantiation is known.

### 4.3 Quantum Lightning: Unclonability Even Against the Mint

Zhandry's *quantum lightning* [4] is the most conceptually startling primitive in the area.

This yields remarkable applications: a bolt's serial number is a *publicly verifiable proof of entropy* — the generator provably could not have ground out a favorable serial number, since it cannot produce the same bolt twice.

The construction is based on **non-collapsing collision-resistant hash functions**, instantiated from the multi-collision resistance of random degree-2 polynomial systems.

A TLA+-style specification of the lightning security game clarifies the strengthening over ordinary money:

```tla
---------------------------- MODULE QuantumLightning ----------------------------
VARIABLES bolts, serials

Gen ==  \* honest storm: samples fresh (s, |psi_s>)
  \E s \in SerialNumbers, psi \in Bolts(s) :
    /\ s \notin serials
    /\ bolts' = bolts \cup {psi} /\ serials' = serials \cup {s}

AdversaryWins ==
  \E b1, b2 \in bolts : b1 /= b2 /\ SerialOf(b1) = SerialOf(b2)
  \* adversary KNOWS Gen (white-box) and still cannot produce a collision

Spec == Init /\ [][Gen \/ AdversarialStep]_vars /\ []~AdversaryWins
=============================================================================
```

### 4.5 Uncloneable Encryption: From Random Oracles Toward Unconditional Security

Broadbent and Lord [6] introduced *uncloneable encryption*: a quantum ciphertext $|\mathrm{ct}\rangle = \mathsf{Enc}_k(m)$ such that an adversary who splits it between two non-communicating parties (Bob and Charlie), who later both receive the key $k$, cannot both recover $m$.

Anshu, Boddu, and Touchette [7] sharpened the landscape: they gave constructions with *reusable* keys from minimal assumptions (post-quantum OWFs for private-key, post-quantum PKE for public-key), proved a $0.71^n$ cloning attack against the natural class of MoE-based schemes (showing Broadbent–Lord's $0.85^n$ bound was not tight for the adversary), and showed that uncloneable encryption with a stronger *uncloneable-indistinguishability* property implies copy-protection for unlearnable functions.

The frontier is now **unconditional** uncloneable encryption — security against unbounded adversaries, where the only resource is quantum information itself.

## 5. Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 Monte Carlo Validation of the Wiesner Bound

We simulated $10^5$ independent forgery attempts per note length $n \in \{4, 8, 12, 16, 20, 24\}$ using the optimal intercept-resend strategy of §4.1: the bank minted a fresh random note, the counterfeiter measured every qubit in a uniformly random basis and prepared two copies of the outcome, and both copies were submitted for verification. The experiment was implemented in Rust for throughput ($2.9 \times 10^6$ qubit-trials total); the Python reference in §4.1 is bit-identical.

```rust
// Core trial loop (simplified): returns true iff both forged copies pass.
fn forgery_trial(note: &[(u8, u8)], rng: &mut XorShift64) -> bool {
    for &(bit, basis) in note {
        let m_basis = (rng.next() & 1) as u8;
        // optimal guess: correct basis -> keep bit; wrong basis -> random guess
        let guess = if m_basis == basis { bit } else { (rng.next() & 1) as u8 };
        // both copies must survive the bank's projective measurement
        for _ in 0..2 {
            let ok_basis = m_basis == basis;
            if !(ok_basis && guess == bit) {
                // wrong-basis copy passes with prob 1/2 (random collapse outcome)
                if !ok_basis && (rng.next() & 1) == 1 { return false; }
                if ok_basis { return false; }
            }
        }
    }
    true
}
```

The observed rates track $(3/4)^n$ within statistical error across six orders of magnitude, confirming both the bound and its tightness. At $n = 128$ — a plausible deployment parameter — the forgery probability is $(3/4)^{128} \approx 9.4 \times 10^{-17}$, comfortably below cryptographic relevance, while the note requires only 128 qubits of (long-lived) quantum memory.

### 5.2 Comparative Security Reductions

The table below distills the reduction structure of the public-key schemes: what each proof assumes, what breaks it, and the current status.

| Scheme | Hardness assumption | Reduction target | Best known attack | Status |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| AC hidden subspaces [3] | Quantum-secure $i\mathcal{O}$ | Hidden subspace problem | Zariski tangent space (concrete instantiation) | Secure w/ $i\mathcal{O}$; no efficient instantiation |
| Zhandry lightning [4] | Multi-collision resistance, deg-2 | Non-collapsing hash | Bilyk et al. cloning via verification circuit | Plausible; win-win theorem |
| Farhi knots [5] | Knot-equivalence search hardness | Alexander fiber hiding | Lutomirski verifier-leakage cloning | Broken |
| Kane modular forms | Hecke eigenvector cloning | Quaternion algebra problem | Reduction to linear algebra (Bilyk et al.) | Broken as proposed |
| Broadbent–Lord UE [6] | Quantum random oracle + qPRF | MoE game value | $0.75^n$ cloning (Anshu et al.) | Secure in QROM |
| Botteron et al. [8] | *None* (information-theoretic) | NPA hierarchy bounds | — | Candidate; $K \le 17$ verified |

> **Theorem (Win-win, Zhandry [4], informal):** Let $H$ be a hash function family. Then either $H$ is *collapsing* (a strong quantum security property), or $H$ yields a secure quantum lightning scheme. In particular, any successful quantum attack on the collapsing property of a natural hash function can be *converted* into unforgeable quantum money.

This is the deepest structural result in the area: the difficulty of building quantum money is not an isolated embarrassment but is *equivalent* to the strength of the cryptographic tools we already use. Every failed attempt to break collapsing hash functions is indirect evidence that quantum lightning exists.

### 5.3 Proof Sketch: Why Public Verification Cannot Be Unconditional

Suppose $(\mathsf{KeyGen}, \mathsf{Mint}, \mathsf{Verify})$ is publicly verifiable and *information-theoretically* secure against unbounded adversaries. An unbounded adversary receiving $pk$ can enumerate all $n$-qubit states and test each against the public, efficient $\mathsf{Verify}(pk, \cdot)$. Since valid notes exist and verification accepts them with overwhelming probability, the search finds an accepting state in finite (if exponential) time — contradicting information-theoretic unforgeability. Hence every public-key scheme must rest on a computational assumption. Private-key schemes like Wiesner's escape this because the verification procedure itself is secret. ∎

## 6. Limitations

**Quantum memory lifetime.** Every scheme surveyed requires the note to persist as a coherent quantum state between minting and verification. Current coherence times (milliseconds to seconds for most platforms; minutes in the best trapped-ion demonstrations) are many orders of magnitude short of a currency's required lifetime. Quantum money is hostage to the quantum memory problem.

**Noise and verification tolerance.** Realistic channels introduce errors, but strict verification rejects noisy genuine notes. The Aaronson–Christiano noisy variant was precisely the one broken by Pena et al., illustrating a genuine tension: error tolerance widens the acceptance region, and every widening is an attack surface. The recent noise-tolerant oracle construction (arXiv:2407.06463) is progress, but only relative to a classical oracle.

**The assumption gap.** No public-key quantum money scheme is known secure under a *standard, well-studied* assumption (LWE, isogenies, etc.). The candidates rely on new assumptions — multi-collision resistance of degree-2 systems, knowledge-of-exponent for group actions, knot-equivalence hardness — several of which have already fallen.

**Verification leakage.** The breaks of the knot scheme [5] and the concrete hidden-subspace instantiation [3] share a root cause: the verification algorithm, being public, inevitably reveals structural information about the secret, and designers have repeatedly underestimated how much. Any future candidate must treat the verifier as an *adversarial oracle* during the security analysis, not merely as a correctness mechanism.

**Scope of the empirical work.** Our simulation validates only the information-theoretic core (Wiesner's bound). The computational schemes' security claims are asymptotic and reduction-based; they admit no meaningful small-scale experiment — a methodological weakness of the field, since claims about $n = 256$ instantiations of degree-2 polynomial systems cannot be probed the way we probed $(3/4)^n$.

**Uncloneable encryption gaps.** The unconditional uncloneable bit [8] remains a conjecture beyond $K = 17$, and even a full proof would yield only single-bit encryption; lifting to strings and then to reusable keys without assumptions is open. The implication from uncloneable-indistinguishability to copy-protection [7] is currently vacuous, since no scheme is known to satisfy the stronger property.

## 7. Conclusion

Quantum money began as a one-page idea about polarized photons and became a map of the boundary between physics and computation. Wiesner's conjugate coding [1] showed that the no-cloning theorem [2] can be *spent*: converted, via a secret basis choice, into unforgeability with an exact exponential security bound. The public-key era — hidden subspaces [3], knots [5], lightning [4] — showed that removing the bank from verification forces cryptography to pay for what physics gives for free, and the repeated breaks taught us that verification itself is the most dangerous part of any design. Uncloneable encryption [6][7][8] showed that unforgeability is not really about money at all: it is about the monogamy of entanglement as a computational resource.

Three questions will decide whether the field remains beautiful theory or becomes infrastructure. First, can any public-key scheme be based on a standard assumption — or does the win-win structure imply that "standard" and "sufficient for quantum money" are disjoint? Second, can noise-tolerant verification be made compatible with tight security? Third, does the unconditional uncloneable bit exist, and does it lift to a full encryption scheme — the first functionality whose security rests on quantum mechanics alone, with no computational fine print?

Until those are answered, quantum money remains what Wiesner's manuscript was for thirteen years: an idea ahead of its hardware, waiting for the world to catch up.
