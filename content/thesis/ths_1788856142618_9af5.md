# Mechanistic Interpretability of Transformers: Circuits, Induction Heads, Superposition, and Sparse Autoencoders

## Abstract

Mechanistic interpretability treats trained neural networks as reverse-engineering targets: artifacts whose internal computations can be decomposed into human-comprehensible algorithms and the circuits that implement them. This thesis surveys the central program that grew out of Anthropic's Transformer Circuits Thread and its successors — the mathematical framework of transformer circuits, the induction-head account of in-context learning, the superposition hypothesis and its account of polysemanticity, and the sparse-autoencoder (SAE) dictionary-learning program that attempts to resolve superposition at scale. We formalize the QK/OV decomposition and residual-stream communication channel, reconstruct the empirical evidence for induction heads as the mechanistic substrate of in-context learning, prove the geometric capacity argument underlying superposition, derive the SAE objective and its scaling laws, and contrast correlational probing with causal interventions such as activation patching, path patching, and feature steering. We close with a disciplined assessment of the field's evaluation problem: what counts as evidence that an interpretation is correct, and where the current machinery falls short.

## 1. Introduction

Deep neural networks achieve remarkable performance by discovering internal representations and algorithms that are *learned, not designed*. This opacity presents a dual problem: it frustrates safety auditing — we cannot verify properties we cannot observe — and it limits scientific understanding of what gradient descent finds. *Mechanistic interpretability* is the research program that answers by treating the trained network as a compiled program and attempting to *decompile* it: to identify its features (the meaningful variables), its circuits (the connected subgraphs implementing computations), and the algorithms those circuits realize [1].

The modern form of this program crystallized in the Transformer Circuits Thread (Elhage et al., 2021; Olsson et al., 2022) [2][3], which developed a mathematical framework for understanding transformers as information-routing systems built on a shared residual stream. Two empirical discoveries then reoriented the field. First, **induction heads** — attention-head circuits that copy completions of previously observed token patterns — were shown to emerge at a discrete phase change during training, and to account for a substantial fraction of the in-context learning behavior that makes large language models flexible few-shot predictors [3]. Second, the **superposition hypothesis** explained why individual neurons so often respond to semantically unrelated inputs: networks represent *more features than they have dimensions*, encoding them as nearly-orthogonal directions in activation space, so that each neuron becomes a polysemantic mixture [4]. This hypothesis was formalized in toy models demonstrating phase changes between dedicated-feature and superposed regimes, and it motivated the **sparse autoencoder** program: training overcomplete dictionaries on activations to recover the monosemantic features packed into superposition (Bricken et al., 2023; Cunningham et al., 2023) [5][6]. Scaling this machinery to production models — 34 million features extracted from Claude 3 Sonnet's middle-layer residual stream — produced the first plausibly comprehensive decomposition of a frontier model's representations, including safety-relevant features that causally steer behavior (Templeton et al., 2024) [7].

This thesis unifies these threads. It is organized around three questions: *what are the components* (the circuit framework, §2–3), *how do learned structures map to behavior* (induction heads, superposition, SAEs, §4), and *how do we know an interpretation is true* (probing vs. intervention, evaluation, §5–6).

## 2. Background

### 2.1 The residual stream as communication channel

The transformer can be rewritten, without approximation, as a sum of contributions written into and read from a shared residual stream. For a model with layers indexed by $\ell$, attention heads $h$, and MLPs, the residual stream $x_\ell(t)$ at token position $t$ evolves as

$$x_{\ell+1}(t) = x_\ell(t) + \sum_h \text{AttnHead}_{\ell,h}(x_\ell) + \text{MLP}_\ell(x_\ell),$$

where each component *reads* from the stream and *writes back* to it. This "bandwidth-limited communication channel" view (Elhage et al., 2021) [2] turns the network into a collection of *circuits*: sets of components connected by learned weight matrices, whose behavior can be analyzed compositionally.

### 2.2 The QK/OV decomposition

Each attention head decomposes into two independent linear circuits:

- **The QK circuit**, $W_Q W_K^\top$, computes attention scores: which source tokens the head attends to. It determines *where information moves from*.
- **The OV circuit**, $W_O W_V$, transforms the attended token's representation into the output written back to the stream. It determines *what information moves*.

Crucially, because attention is linear in the values, the total effect of a head can be read as "on query match $q \approx W_Q^\top x_q$, copy $W_O W_V x_k$ into the residual stream." This decomposition underlies every later circuit result: induction heads, duplicate-token heads, and name-mover heads are all characterized as particular QK/OV circuit configurations [2][3][8].

### 2.3 Features and the linear representation hypothesis

A *feature* is a human-interpretable property the network tracks — e.g., "this token is a French verb" — represented as a *direction* in activation space: the model's internal state has a large dot product with a fixed vector $f$ exactly when the property holds. This **linear representation hypothesis** is supported by probing (linear classifiers succeed on activations), vector arithmetic (king − man + woman analogies), and steering (adding feature vectors changes behavior predictably). Mechanistic interpretability's fundamental bet is that networks compute *linear operations on features* composed with nonlinear routing — and that these features, not neurons, are the right units of analysis.

## 3. Methodology

This thesis is an analytic survey with formal derivations. Our method:

1. **Literature grounding.** We restrict substantive empirical claims to the peer-reviewed and Transformer Circuits corpus: the Circuits Thread (2021–2022) [2][3][4], the dictionary-learning papers (Bricken et al., 2023; Cunningham et al., 2023; Templeton et al., 2024) [5][6][7], and the circuit-discovery literature on the indirect-object-identification (IOI) task (Wang et al., 2022) [8].
2. **Formal reconstruction.** We re-derive the QK/OV decomposition, the induction-head algorithm, the Johnson–Lindenstrauss capacity bound for superposition, and the SAE training objective, exposing the assumptions each argument depends on.
3. **Epistemic stratification.** We separate, explicitly, four evidence tiers: (i) *correlational* (probes, attention visualization), (ii) *interventional* (ablation, activation patching, path patching), (iii) *constructive* (toy models where ground truth is known), and (iv) *behavioral* (feature steering that changes outputs as predicted). Interpretability claims are evaluated by the highest tier they reach.
4. **Computational illustration.** Pseudocode in Python and Haskell demonstrates the core algorithms (induction-head detection, SAE training loop), not as replication of the original codebases but as executable specifications of the mechanisms.

> **Theorem (Evidence hierarchy):** A claim of the form "component $C$ implements algorithm $A$" is *corroborated* by correlational evidence, *supported* by single-node interventions, and *established* only by interventions on the full causal path $C$'s outputs take to the logits, together with a demonstration that no alternative path explains the behavior. We adopt this standard throughout.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 The circuits framework: composition and virtual weights

The mathematical framework's most consequential insight is *composition*: because the residual stream is additive, heads and MLPs compose by matrix multiplication of their circuits. If head $h_1$ in layer 1 moves information from token $t-k$ to $t$ via its OV circuit, and head $h_2$ in layer 2 attends based on that information via its QK circuit, the composed behavior is governed by the *virtual attention head* $W_Q^{(2)} (W_O^{(1)} W_V^{(1)}) W_K^{(2)\top}$ — an effective QK circuit spanning two layers. Elhage et al. [2] showed that the *induction head* is precisely such a composition: a **previous-token head** (layer 1) whose OV circuit shifts information one position forward, composed with a **late head** (layer 2) whose QK circuit matches the current token against that shifted information.

Formally, let $e_t$ be the embedding of token $t$. The previous-token head's OV circuit implements approximately

$$W_O W_V e_t \;\approx\; \text{"the token at position } t-1\text{" direction},$$

so that at position $t$ the residual stream carries a record of the *preceding* token. The induction head then attends from current token $x_t$ to any past position $s$ where the token *preceding* $s$ matched $x_t$ — i.e., it searches for $[A]$ to find what followed it, and its OV circuit copies $[B]$ forward. The algorithmic signature is:

$$[A][B]\ldots[A] \;\longrightarrow\; \text{predict } [B].$$

This is not a heuristic: in small attention-only models the framework's predictions about which heads implement this composition can be verified weight-by-weight, because there are no MLPs to complicate the information flow [3].

### 4.2 Induction heads and the phase change of in-context learning

Olsson et al. [3] tracked dozens of transformer models through training, measuring two quantities per checkpoint: (a) a behavioral **in-context-learning score** — how much per-token loss decreases between early and late context positions — and (b) a mechanistic **prefix-matching score** for each attention head, quantifying induction-like behavior on synthetic sequences. The central finding is a *phase change*: at a specific point in training, the loss curve's derivative jumps, the in-context-learning score discontinuously improves, and heads with high prefix-matching scores appear — simultaneously. The coincidence of a behavioral discontinuity with a mechanistic emergence is the paper's evidentiary core.

Three lines of follow-up evidence sharpened the causal claim:

1. **Ablation.** In small models, ablating (pattern-preserving zeroing of) induction heads degrades in-context learning far more than ablating matched control heads [3].
2. **Architectural intervention.** A "smeared key" variant that *prevents* the previous-token-head composition from forming delays or eliminates the phase change, showing the circuit is not merely correlated with but *necessary for* the capability jump [3].
3. **Cross-scale continuity.** In models from 2 layers to 40+ layers, heads satisfying the induction-head operationalization appear in the same training regime where in-context learning emerges, with locations shifting (last layer in attention-only models, earlier in large models) but the composition motif preserved [3].

The interpretive upshot is significant: a large fraction of the generic "loss decreases with token index" signal — the empirical signature of in-context learning — is implemented by a *specific, reverse-engineered circuit*, not by a diffuse statistical property of the weights.

```python
# Operationalization: induction-head prefix-matching score (schematic)
def prefix_matching_score(head, seqs):
    """Fraction of the time the head, at token x_t, attends to a token
    whose predecessor equals x_t's predecessor's successor pattern."""
    scores = []
    for tokens in seqs:                      # synthetic repeated-pattern sequences
        attn = head.attention_pattern(tokens)  # [T, T] attention weights
        for t in range(1, len(tokens)):
            # find earlier position s where token s-1 == token t
            matches = [s for s in range(1, t) if tokens[s-1] == tokens[t]]
            if matches:
                scores.append(sum(attn[t, s] for s in matches))
    return sum(scores) / max(len(scores), 1)
```

### 4.3 Superposition: the geometry of polysemanticity

If features are directions, the network's representational capacity is limited by how many *nearly orthogonal* directions can be packed into $d$ dimensions. The **superposition hypothesis** (Elhage et al., 2022) [4] asserts that networks exploit exactly this: they represent $m \gg d$ features as directions $\{f_i\}_{i=1}^m \subset \mathbb{R}^d$ with small pairwise inner products, accepting bounded interference in exchange for representational capacity. A single neuron then responds to many features — *polysemanticity* — because the neuron axis has nonzero dot product with many feature directions.

The capacity argument is a Johnson–Lindenstrauss-style bound. For random unit vectors in $\mathbb{R}^d$, $|\langle f_i, f_j \rangle| \lesssim \sqrt{(2\log m)/d}$ with high probability, so a network can store $m = \exp(O(d\epsilon^2))$ features with mutual interference bounded by $\epsilon$. In toy models with sparse feature activations, gradient descent indeed learns superposed representations — and exhibits a *phase change*: below a critical sparsity the network dedicates one neuron per feature; above it, superposition wins because the interference penalty is outweighed by the capacity gain [4].

```haskell
-- Schematic: superposition capacity bound (executable specification)
-- Given dimension d and interference tolerance eps, the max number of
-- features packable satisfies  m <= exp(d * eps^2 / 2).
capacityBound :: Double -> Double -> Double
capacityBound d eps = exp (d * eps * eps / 2)

-- e.g. d = 512, eps = 0.1  =>  ~13 features at strict tolerance,
--      but random-vector geometry permits exponentially more in practice
```

Two consequences follow. First, *polysemanticity is not a bug but an economic optimum*: the network trades a small, bounded amount of interference for exponentially more features. Second, *neurons are the wrong unit of analysis*: the features are the directions, and the neuron basis is an arbitrary, uninterpretable rotation. This is the theoretical justification for the entire SAE program: we need a method that recovers the *overcomplete feature dictionary*, not the neuron axes.

| Regime | Feature count $m$ vs. dimension $d$ | Neuron semantics | Interpretability strategy |
|---|---|---|---|
| Dedicated | $m \le d$ | Monosemantic | Read neurons directly |
| Superposition | $m \gg d$, sparse activation | Polysemantic | Dictionary learning (SAEs) |
| Dense superposition | $m \gg d$, dense activation | Uninterpretable mixtures | No current decomposition |

### 4.4 Sparse autoencoders: dictionary learning at scale

A sparse autoencoder trained on activations $x \in \mathbb{R}^d$ learns an encoder $f_\theta: \mathbb{R}^d \to \mathbb{R}^k$ and decoder $g_\phi: \mathbb{R}^k \to \mathbb{R}^d$ with $k \gg d$ (overcomplete), minimizing

$$\mathcal{L}(x) \;=\; \|x - g_\phi(f_\theta(x))\|_2^2 \;+\; \lambda \|f_\theta(x)\|_1,$$

where the $\ell_1$ penalty enforces sparsity: each activation is reconstructed from a *small* subset of dictionary atoms. The decoder columns $\{d_i\}_{i=1}^k$ are the recovered feature directions. Bricken et al. (2023) [5] demonstrated this on a one-layer transformer: dictionary learning decomposed a 512-neuron MLP layer into over 4,000 features with substantially higher monosemanticity (measured by automated interpretability scoring) than neurons, PCA directions, or ICA components. Cunningham et al. (2023) [6] extended SAEs to deeper language models and, critically, showed *causal* relevance: on the IOI task, ablating the SAE features implicated by the circuit recovered the known circuit behavior more precisely than neuron-level ablations — features were not just interpretable, they were the right causal variables.

Templeton et al. (2024) [7] then scaled the program to Claude 3 Sonnet: SAEs with up to **34 million features** trained on the middle-layer residual stream. The results: features are multilingual and multimodal (generalizing to images despite text-only training), respond to abstract concepts (sarcasm, code errors, deception, sycophancy, power-seeking), and — decisively — *steering* by clamping a feature's activation changes model behavior in the predicted direction ("Golden Gate Claude," forced to discuss the Golden Gate Bridge, being the famous demonstration). For the first time, an interpretability method produced a *manipulable* decomposition of a production model.

```python
# SAE training loop (schematic, TopK variant)
for x in activation_stream:                      # x: [batch, d]
    z = relu(x @ W_enc + b_enc)                  # [batch, k], k >> d
    z = topk(z, k_active)                        # hard sparsity: keep k_active largest
    x_hat = z @ W_dec + b_dec                    # reconstruct
    loss = ((x - x_hat) ** 2).sum() + aux_loss(z)  # aux loss revives dead features
    loss.backward(); optimizer.step()
    W_dec = W_dec / W_dec.norm(dim=0)            # unit-norm dictionary atoms
```

```rust
// Feature steering at inference time (schematic)
fn steer(residual: &mut Vec<f32>, feature_dir: &[f32], strength: f32) {
    // clamp the projection of the residual stream onto a feature direction
    let proj: f32 = residual.iter().zip(feature_dir).map(|(a, b)| a * b).sum();
    let delta = strength - proj;
    for (a, b) in residual.iter_mut().zip(feature_dir) {
        *a += delta * b;
    }
}
```

## 5. Empirical Evidence and Proofs

We now consolidate what is *established* versus *suggestive*, by evidence tier.

**Established (interventional + constructive):**

1. *Induction heads exist and are composed as the framework predicts.* In attention-only models the QK/OV weight analysis directly identifies previous-token heads feeding induction heads, and ablation removes the in-context-learning phase change [3].
2. *Superposition is learnable and phase-transitions in toy models.* With known ground-truth features, networks trained under sparsity provably pack $m > d$ features into $d$ dimensions with bounded interference, and SAEs recover the ground-truth dictionary up to the expected symmetries [4][5].
3. *A complete task circuit can be reverse-engineered in a real model.* Wang et al. (2022) [8] used activation patching and **path patching** to identify the full IOI circuit in GPT-2 small: duplicate-token heads flag repeated names, S-inhibition heads suppress the subject, and name-mover heads copy the indirect object to the output — a 26-component circuit whose ablation reproduces the behavioral deficit quantitatively.

**Strong but partial (interventional, single-node):**

4. *SAE features are more causally precise than neurons.* On IOI, SAE-feature ablations localize the causally responsible components more finely than neuron ablations [6].
5. *Feature steering changes behavior predictably at production scale.* Clamping identified features in Claude 3 Sonnet alters outputs in the interpreted direction across diverse prompts [7].

**Suggestive (correlational):**

6. *Most of in-context learning is induction-like.* The claim rests on the coincidence of the phase change with induction-head formation plus continuity across scales; in large models with MLPs the evidence is correlational and the decomposition is not yet weight-level precise [3].
7. *SAE feature universality.* Features learned from differently-seeded one-layer models overlap substantially [5], hinting that features — not just circuits — may be universal across training runs. At production scale this remains unproven.

The IOI work deserves emphasis as the field's methodological template. Its **path patching** protocol — patching a sender node's activations from a corrupted run and measuring the effect on a receiver node, then patching the receiver's resulting state forward — isolates *direct causal edges* in the computational graph, distinguishing "head A matters" from "head A matters *via* head B." This edge-level resolution is what separates circuit claims from component-wise ablation stories.

## 6. Limitations

**1. The evaluation gap.** There is no agreed-upon metric for "interpretation quality." Automated interpretability scores (LLM judges rating feature explanations) measure *plausibility*, not *causal fidelity* [7]. A feature can have a compelling dashboard and still misdescribe the computation — the field's central open problem, acknowledged explicitly in the Scaling Monosemanticity paper [7].

**2. SAEs are lossy and incomplete.** Reconstruction is imperfect (nontrivial fraction of variance unexplained), large fractions of dictionary atoms are dead or uninterpretable, and features may split or merge across SAE scales (the "feature splitting" problem). An SAE decomposition is a *useful lens*, not a verified decompilation.

**3. Cross-layer superposition.** SAEs are trained per-layer (or per-location); features that are superposed *across* layers or across token positions are not recovered by single-site dictionaries. The residual stream is a shared channel, so per-layer decompositions may cut circuits at the joints.

**4. Circuits are task- and distribution-bound.** The IOI circuit is complete *for the IOI distribution*; whether the same heads implement the same algorithm on natural text is a generalization claim requiring separate evidence. Induction heads in large models are characterized behaviorally, not weight-mechanistically.

**5. Probing confounds.** Linear probes can detect features the model *represents* without using, or features present in the *input distribution* rather than computed by the model. Probing is a ceiling on "representable," not evidence of "used" — only interventions discriminate.

**6. Scale of human effort.** Full circuit analyses remain artisanal: months of researcher time per circuit. Automated circuit discovery (ACDC, attribution patching) trades completeness for scalability, and the gap between automated subgraphs and verified circuits is still large.

## 7. Conclusion

Mechanistic interpretability has moved, in under five years, from a manifesto (Olah et al., 2020) to a mathematical framework (Elhage et al., 2021), to verified circuits (induction heads, IOI), to a geometric theory of why networks are hard to read (superposition), to a scalable technology for decomposing production models (sparse autoencoders, 34M features, causal steering). The intellectual arc is coherent: each stage answered the obstacle the previous stage exposed — circuits needed features, features needed a theory of polysemanticity, superposition needed a recovery method, and SAEs needed a scale and causality demonstration.

The honest ledger: *components and algorithms* are now reverse-engineerable in small models and on narrow tasks; *representations* are decomposable into manipulable features at production scale; but *verified completeness* — the claim that a decomposition captures everything a model computes — remains out of reach. The field's next phase will be defined not by more features but by better evaluation: rigorous, causal, quantitative standards for when an interpretation is correct. Until then, mechanistic interpretability is best understood as the most promising *research program* for understanding neural computation — with its strongest results being existence proofs that decompilation is possible at all.
