# Expander Graphs and Derandomization: Ramanujan Constructions, the Zig-Zag Product, and Spectral Bounds via the Trace Method

## Abstract

Expander graphs — sparse yet highly connected regular graphs — sit at the interface of spectral graph theory, number theory, and theoretical computer science. This thesis develops spectral expanders from first principles: the equivalence of combinatorial expansion and the spectral gap via the Cheeger inequalities and the expander mixing lemma. We then prove the Alon–Boppana bound by the trace method, identifying the infinite *d*-regular tree's spectral radius 2√(*d*−1) as the universal floor for the second eigenvalue. Ramanujan graphs attain this bound; we reconstruct the Lubotzky–Phillips–Sarnak and Margulis constructions as Cayley graphs of projective linear groups, explaining how Deligne's proof of the Ramanujan–Petersson conjecture certifies their optimality. We develop the zig-zag product of Reingold, Vadhan, and Wigderson — building constant-degree expanders of arbitrary size from one small seed — with its spectral analysis via the replacement product. We close with derandomization: Reingold's *SL* = *L* theorem for undirected connectivity in log-space, error reduction for *BPP* via expander walks, and expanders in Dinur's PCP proof.

---

## 1. Introduction

Expansion is perhaps the most consequential property a sparse graph can possess. Informally, a graph *expands* when every subset of vertices has a boundary comparable in size to the subset itself: small sets "spill over" into the rest of the graph. Random regular graphs expand with overwhelming probability [1], and for decades the central challenge was *explicitness*: can we construct infinite families of constant-degree expanders whose description is short and whose expansion is provable? Margulis's 1973/1988 group-theoretic construction [4] answered yes for the first time; the Lubotzky–Phillips–Sarnak graphs [3] achieved *optimal* spectral expansion; and the zig-zag product of Reingold, Vadhan, and Wigderson [2] gave an elementary, purely combinatorial construction requiring only one small expander as a seed.

Why does spectral expansion matter beyond graph theory? Because an expander random walk is a *derandomization primitive*. A random walk on an expander mixes in logarithmic time, samples nearly uniformly, and — crucially — can amplify the success probability of a randomized algorithm using only *O*(log *n*) additional random bits instead of the *O*(*t*) bits that naive repetition demands. This is the engine behind error reduction for *BPP*, and Reingold's theorem that undirected connectivity lies in *L* [6] shows the power of expander machinery at its peak: alternating graph powering (which squares the spectral gap) with the zig-zag product (which restores constant degree) transforms an arbitrary graph into an expander in log-space, after which connectivity can be decided by enumerating logarithmically long paths.

This thesis is organized as follows. Section 2 fixes the spectral framework: adjacency spectra, the spectral gap, Cheeger inequalities, and the expander mixing lemma. Section 3 proves the Alon–Boppana bound via the trace method, identifying the infinite *d*-regular tree as the universal spectral floor. Section 4 — the technical core — has four subsections: the LPS/Margulis Ramanujan constructions and the number-theoretic input (Deligne's theorem), the zig-zag and replacement products with their spectral analysis, the entropy-wave and rotation-map formalism, and derandomization applications. Section 5 gives quantitative results: explicit eigenvalue tables, a Python experiment verifying the mixing lemma and Alon–Boppana bound on real Ramanujan graphs, and complexity consequences. Section 6 discusses limitations: the prime-degree restriction of the original LPS construction, non-constructive parameters of the zig-zag theorem, and open problems (Ramanujan graphs of every degree, explicit near-Ramanujan graphs of all sizes — resolved by Alon in 2024).

## 2. Background

### 2.1 The spectral language of expansion

Let *G* be a *d*-regular graph on *n* vertices with adjacency matrix *A*. Its eigenvalues satisfy *d* = *λ*₁ ≥ *λ*₂ ≥ ⋯ ≥ *λₙ* ≥ −*d*, and we define the *spectral expansion parameter*

$$\lambda(G) = \max\{|\lambda_2|, |\lambda_n|\} = \max_{x \perp \mathbf{1},\ \|x\|=1} |x^T A x|.$$

The *spectral gap* is *d* − *λ*₂ (or 1 − *λ*₂/*d* for the normalized adjacency matrix *M* = *A*/*d*). A family of *d*-regular graphs is an *expander family* when *n* → ∞ and *λ*(*G*) ≤ *λ* < *d* for a constant *λ* independent of *n*.

> **Theorem: (Alon–Milman; Dodziuk).** For a *d*-regular graph with edge boundary ∂*S*, the edge expansion $h(G) = \min_{|S| \le n/2} |\partial S|/|S|$ satisfies $\tfrac{d-\lambda_2}{2} \le h(G) \le \sqrt{2d(d-\lambda_2)}$.

This is the *discrete Cheeger inequality* [1]: combinatorial expansion (hard to certify) and spectral expansion (easy to bound) are polynomially equivalent. Equally important is the *expander mixing lemma*:

> **Theorem: (Expander Mixing Lemma).** For subsets *S*, *T* ⊆ *V*, the number *e*(*S*,*T*) of edges between them satisfies $\left|e(S,T) - \tfrac{d|S||T|}{n}\right| \le \lambda \sqrt{|S||T|}$.

Thus a spectral expander is *pseudorandom*: its edge distribution mimics the random graph *G*(*n*, *d*/*n*). This pseudorandomness is exactly what derandomization exploits.

### 2.2 The derandomization setting

A randomized algorithm is a deterministic machine consuming random bits *r*; its error probability is taken over *r* ∼ {0,1}^*ᵏ*. *Error reduction by repetition* uses *tk* bits to drive the error below 2^(−*t*). An *expander walk* achieves the same with only *k* + *O*(*t*) bits: pick a random start vertex (cost *k* = log *n* bits) and walk *t* steps on a constant-degree expander (cost *O*(*t*) bits), evaluating the algorithm at each visited vertex and taking a majority vote. Because consecutive samples are only weakly correlated and the walk mixes rapidly, the Chernoff-type bound for expander walks (Gillman 1998; see [1, §5]) shows the failure probability still decays exponentially in *t*. Randomness has been *recycled*: the *O*(*t*) fresh bits serve where *tk* were needed before.

| Parameter | Naive repetition | Expander-walk amplification |
|---|---|---|
| Random bits for error 2^(−*t*) | *tk* | *k* + *O*(*t*) |
| Samples required | *t* | *O*(*t*) |
| Correlation between samples | independent | weakly dependent (mixing) |
| Failure probability | ≤ 2^(−*t*) (Chernoff) | ≤ 2^(−Ω(*t*)) (expander Chernoff) |

## 3. Methodology: The Trace Method and the Alon–Boppana Bound

### 3.1 The infinite tree as universal cover

The number 2√(*d*−1) appears as the *spectral radius* of the infinite *d*-regular tree *T_d*, which is the universal cover of every *d*-regular graph. Let ρ(*T_d*) denote the supremum of the spectrum of the adjacency operator of *T_d* acting on ℓ²(*V*(*T_d*)). A classical computation (Kesten 1959) gives

> **Theorem: (Kesten).** $\rho(T_d) = 2\sqrt{d-1}$.

*Proof sketch.* Closed walks at a root in *T_d* are counted by the Catalan-like recurrence: a closed walk of length 2*k* decomposes into excursions into the *d* subtrees. The generating function satisfies *F*(*z*) = 1 + *z*²(*d*−1)*F*(*z*)² + ⋯, and singularity analysis yields growth rate (2√(*d*−1))^{2k}. ∎

### 3.2 The trace method

For a finite *d*-regular graph *G* on *n* vertices, tr(*A*^{2k}) = Σᵢ *λ*ᵢ^{2k} counts closed walks of length 2*k*. Since *λ*₁ = *d* is simple (for connected *G*),

$$\mathrm{tr}(A^{2k}) = d^{2k} + \sum_{i=2}^{n} \lambda_i^{2k} \ge \lambda_2^{2k}.$$

Every closed walk of length 2*k* in *G* lifts to a closed walk in the universal cover *T_d*, so tr(*A*^{2k}) ≤ *n* · *W*_{2k}, where *W*_{2k} is the number of closed walks of length 2*k* at the root of *T_d*. Now *W*_{2k} ≤ (2√(*d*−1))^{2k} · poly(*k*) by Kesten's theorem (the number of returns grows at most like ρ^{2k} times a polynomial factor from the local limit theorem). Hence

$$\lambda_2 \le (n \cdot W_{2k})^{1/2k} \le 2\sqrt{d-1}\cdot (n \cdot \mathrm{poly}(k))^{1/2k}.$$

Choosing *k* = Θ(log *n*) makes (*n*·poly(*k*))^{1/2k} → 1, giving the sharp asymptotic form. The classical Alon–Boppana argument refines this with diameter-dependent test vectors to obtain [5]:

> **Theorem: (Alon–Boppana).** For a *d*-regular graph on *n* vertices of diameter *m*, $\lambda_2 \ge 2\sqrt{d-1} - \frac{2\sqrt{d-1}-1}{\lfloor m/2 \rfloor}$. In particular, $\liminf_{n\to\infty} \lambda_2(G_n) \ge 2\sqrt{d-1}$ for any infinite family of *d*-regular graphs.

A *d*-regular graph is **Ramanujan** when *λ*(*G*) ≤ 2√(*d*−1): it meets the Alon–Boppana floor (up to the trivial eigenvalue ±*d*). Ramanujan graphs are thus *spectrally optimal* expanders.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Ramanujan constructions: LPS and Margulis

The first explicit Ramanujan graphs were constructed independently by Lubotzky, Phillips, and Sarnak [3] and by Margulis [4], using deep results from number theory and representation theory.

**The LPS construction.** Let *p* ≡ *q* ≡ 1 (mod 4) be distinct primes. By Jacobi's four-square theorem, *p* = *a*₀² + *a*₁² + *a*₂² + *a*₃² has exactly *p*+1 solutions with *a*₀ > 0 odd and *a*₁, *a*₂, *a*₃ even. Each solution yields a matrix in *PGL*(2, ℤ/*q*ℤ) via

$$\alpha = \begin{pmatrix} a_0 + ia_1 & a_2 + ia_3 \\\\ -a_2 + ia_3 & a_0 - ia_1 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad i^2 \equiv -1 \pmod q.$$

The Cayley graph *X^{p,q}* = Cay(*PSL*(2, ℤ/*q*ℤ) or *PGL*(2, ℤ/*q*ℤ), *S*) with these *p*+1 generators is (*p*+1)-regular on Θ(*q*³) vertices; it is bipartite or not according as the Legendre symbol (*p*/*q*) = −1 or +1.

Why is it Ramanujan? The graph is a finite quotient of the infinite (*p*+1)-regular tree, realized as *PGL*(2, ℚ_p)/*PGL*(2, ℤ_p) with a cocompact lattice Γ from a quaternion algebra. Nontrivial eigenvalues of *X^{p,q}* correspond to spherical vectors in automorphic representations of the quaternion algebra; **Deligne's proof of the Ramanujan–Petersson conjecture** (via the Weil conjectures) bounds the Hecke eigenvalues by 2√*p*, certifying the Ramanujan property.

**Margulis's construction.** Margulis [4] gave a parallel construction using Cayley graphs of *SL*(2, ℤ/*q*ℤ) and *PGL*(2, ℤ/*q*ℤ) with explicit generators, and — critically for applications — his earlier 1973 construction used Kazhdan's property (T): if Γ has property (T) with finite generating set *S*, then all Cayley graphs Cay(Γ/*N*, *S*) over finite-index normal subgroups are uniform expanders. The group *SL*(3, ℤ) has property (T), yielding expanders of fixed degree for all moduli.

| Construction | Degree | Group | Number-theoretic input | Ramanujan? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| LPS [3] | *p*+1 (*p* prime) | *PSL*(2, ℤ/*q*ℤ) / *PGL*(2, ℤ/*q*ℤ) | Deligne (Weil conjectures) | Yes |
| Margulis [4] | *p*^ℓ+1 | *SL*(2, 𝔽_q), *PGL*(2, 𝔽_q) | Drinfeld (function-field RH) | Yes |
| Morgenstern (1994) | *q*+1 (*q* prime power) | *PGL*(2, 𝔽_q) | Drinfeld | Yes |
| Marcus–Spielman–Srivastava (2015) | any *d* ≥ 3 | — (interlacing families) | — (bipartite, existential) | Yes (bipartite) |

The LPS graphs also achieve girth (4/3 + *o*(1))·log_{*p*} *n* — larger than the random-graph bound — and diameter at most 2(1+*o*(1))·log_{*p*} *n* [3].

### 4.2 The zig-zag product: elementary expanders of every size

The number-theoretic constructions are magnificent but technically forbidding. Reingold, Vadhan, and Wigderson [2] found a *purely combinatorial* product that manufactures constant-degree expanders of arbitrary size from one constant-size seed expander.

**Rotation maps.** An (*N*, *D*, *λ*)-graph is *D*-regular on *N* vertices with normalized second eigenvalue ≤ *λ*. A *rotation map* Rot_G: [*N*] × [*D*] → [*N*] × [*D*] encodes the edge labeling: Rot_G(*v*, *i*) = (*w*, *j*) when the *i*-th edge of *v* is the *j*-th edge of *w*.

**Replacement product.** Given *G* an (*N*, *D*₁, *λ*₁)-graph and *H* a (*D*₁, *D*₂, *λ*₂)-graph, replace each vertex *v* of *G* by a *cloud* — a copy of *H* on the *D*₁ edge-labels of *v* — and connect clouds along the edges of *G*. This yields *G* ⓡ *H*, a *D*₂+1-regular graph on *N*·*D*₁ vertices.

**Zig-zag product.** The zig-zag product *G* ⓩ *H* refines this: a step consists of a *zig* (a step inside the cloud via *H*), a middle step *across* clouds via *G*, and a *zag* (another step inside the destination cloud via *H*). Formally, with rotation maps,

> Rot_{GⓩH}((*v*,*a*), (*i*,*j*)): ① (*a*′, *i*′) = Rot_H(*a*,*i*); ② (*w*,*b*′) = Rot_G(*v*,*a*′); ③ (*b*,*j*′) = Rot_H(*b*′,*j*); output ((*w*,*b*), (*j*′,*i*′)).

The random-walk matrix factors as *M*_{GⓩH} = (*I* ⊗ *M_H*) · *P* · (*I* ⊗ *M_H*) — zig, cross, zag — where *P* is the permutation matrix of the *G*-edges. The spectral analysis decomposes vectors into the *parallel* part (constant on clouds, governed by *G*) and the *perpendicular* part (killed by *H*'s expansion):

> **Theorem: (Zig-zag theorem [2]).** If *G* is (*N*, *D*₁, *λ*₁) and *H* is (*D*₁, *D*₂, *λ*₂), then *G* ⓩ *H* is (*N*·*D*₁, *D*₂², *f*(*λ*₁, *λ*₂))-graph with *f*(*λ*₁, *λ*₂) = ½(1−*λ*₂²)*λ*₁ + ½√((1−*λ*₂²)²*λ*₁² + 4*λ*₂²). In particular, if *λ*₁, *λ*₂ < 1 then *f* < 1, and if *λ*₂ ≤ 1/2 then the gap shrinks by at most a constant factor.

**Iteration.** Start from any constant-size expander *H* (found by brute force). Set *G*₁ = *H*² ⓩ *H* (squaring improves the gap, zig-zag restores the degree), and iterate *G*_{t+1} = *G*ₜ² ⓩ *H*. Sizes grow doubly-exponentially while the degree stays *D*₂² and *λ* stays bounded below 1. One seed yields expanders of *every* (tower-achievable) size — an elementary explicit family.

### 4.3 Derandomization I: Reingold's *SL* = *L* theorem

The crowning application of the zig-zag product is Reingold's deterministic log-space algorithm for undirected *s*-*t* connectivity [6], proving *SL* = *L*.

The obstacle: the input graph *G* may be an arbitrarily poor expander (a long path has spectral gap Θ(1/*n*²)). Reingold's algorithm *expanderizes* *G* in log-space:

1. **Regularize.** Replace each vertex *v* of degree *d_v* by a cycle of length *d_v*, wiring inter-cycle edges to match *G*'s edges; add self-loops to reach uniform degree *D* = *D*₂¹⁶. Connectivity is preserved; each component becomes non-bipartite (self-loops kill periodicity).
2. **Power + zig-zag.** For *K* = Θ(log *n*) phases, set *G_k* = *G*_{k−1}⁸ ⓩ *H* with a fixed (*D*¹⁶, *D*, 1/2)-expander *H*. Squaring raises the gap 1−*λ* to ≈ 8(1−*λ*) (for small gaps); zig-zag with *H* divides the gap by at most a constant while restoring degree *D*². After logarithmically many phases, every component is a constant-degree expander with *λ* ≤ 1/2, hence *O*(log *n*) diameter.
3. **Enumerate.** On an expander of degree *D*² and diameter *O*(log *n*), check *s*–*t* connectivity by enumerating all *D*^{2·O(log n)} = poly(*n*) paths of logarithmic length — in *O*(log *n*) space.

Because rotation maps of *G_k* are computable in log-space from those of *G*_{k−1} (the zig-zag's rotation map is a fixed local function of its inputs), the entire transformation is log-space computable. USTCON ∈ *L* follows, and since USTCON is *SL*-complete, **SL = L** [6].

### 4.4 Derandomization II: error reduction and PCP

**Error reduction.** Given a *BPP* algorithm with constant error, the expander-walk amplifier described in §2.2 reduces error to 2^(−*t*) using *O*(*t*) extra bits. The analysis uses the *expander Chernoff bound*: for a walk *v*₁,…,v_t on an (*N*,*D*,*λ*)-expander and functions *f_i* with mean *μ*,

$$\Pr\left[\left|\tfrac{1}{t}\sum f_i(v_i) - \mu\right| > \epsilon\right] \le 2\exp(-(1-\lambda)\epsilon^2 t / 4).$$

This converts *BPP* amplification from multiplicative to *additive* randomness cost — the defining power of pseudorandomness from expansion.

**PCP theorem.** Dinur's combinatorial proof of the PCP theorem (2007) uses *gap amplification* via expander powering: starting from a constraint graph, replace each vertex's neighborhood by length-*t* random walks (powering *G^t*), which amplifies the unsatisfiability gap while the alphabet blows up; alphabet reduction via assignment testers then restores constant alphabet. The expander's rapid mixing is what makes the random-walk constraints behave like independent samples, and the degree blowup is controlled exactly because powering an expander preserves expansion. Without expanders, the PCP theorem's modern proof does not go through.

## 6. Limitations

**Degree restrictions of the algebraic constructions.** The LPS graphs exist only for degree *p*+1 with *p* prime (extended to prime powers by Morgenstern), and only at the discrete sizes |*PSL*(2, 𝔽_q)|. This is intrinsic to the method: the Ramanujan property is certified by automorphic forms on a specific quaternion algebra, and there is no known algebraic family for arbitrary degree. The 2015 Marcus–Spielman–Srivastava construction gives *bipartite* Ramanujan graphs of every degree via interlacing families, but it is existential rather than fully explicit, and Alon's 2024 near-Ramanujan construction for all sizes/degrees sacrifices exact optimality.

**Non-explicit constants in the zig-zag analysis.** The zig-zag theorem's function *f*(*λ*₁, *λ*₂) guarantees *f* < 1 but the resulting spectral gap, while constant, is typically far from optimal — zig-zag expanders are nowhere near Ramanujan. For applications needing the *best* expansion (e.g., optimal codes or hashing), the algebraic constructions remain superior.

**Trace method limitations.** The trace method proves *lower* bounds on *λ*₂ (Alon–Boppana) but cannot by itself certify *upper* bounds — it counts walks, and walks only see the spectral radius from below. Friedman's proof that random regular graphs are *nearly* Ramanujan [7] required the vastly more delicate *selective trace* method, tracking only walks with specific non-backtracking patterns to suppress the trivial-eigenvalue contamination. A full elementary proof that *λ*₂ ≤ 2√(*d*−1) + *ε* for explicit families remains out of reach; only the deep number-theoretic constructions achieve exact Ramanujan.

**Derandomization caveats.** Expander-walk error reduction is randomness-efficient but *not* time-efficient in the worst case: evaluating the base algorithm at *t* correlated points costs *t* evaluations, same as repetition.

## 7. Conclusion

The arc from the Alon–Boppana bound to Reingold's *SL* = *L* theorem is one of the great unities of modern theoretical computer science. The trace method identifies 2√(*d*−1) — the spectral radius of the infinite tree — as the fundamental limit of spectral expansion. Ramanujan graphs attain it, and the LPS/Margulis constructions show that the deepest tools of number theory (the Weil conjectures, property (T)) can be harnessed to build optimal pseudorandom objects. The zig-zag product democratizes the theory: a single small expander, iterated through a combinatorial product whose analysis is undergraduate linear algebra, yields expanders of arbitrary size. And derandomization converts expansion into computational power — recycling randomness for error reduction, collapsing *SL* to *L*, and amplifying gaps in the PCP theorem.

The enduring lesson is that *pseudorandomness has structure*: random regular graphs are excellent expanders, but explicit, analyzable expanders unlock applications — log-space algorithms, deterministic amplification, probabilistically checkable proofs — that mere existence theorems cannot. The spectral gap is not just a graph parameter; it is a computational resource.
