# Oblivious RAM: Path ORAM, Circuit ORAM, and the Square-Root Barrier to Constant Client Overhead

## Abstract

Oblivious RAM (ORAM) severs the link between *what* a program computes and *where* it touches memory, hiding the access pattern of a RAM computation from an untrusted storage server. This thesis traces the arc from Goldreich and Ostrovsky's 1996 square-root and hierarchical constructions — which showed that a client with constant private storage could hide its accesses at amortized Θ(√N) and O(log³ N) cost, and proved an Ω(log N) lower bound in the opaque-block model — through the tree-based paradigm of Shi et al. (2011) to Path ORAM (Stefanov et al., 2013), the first construction with O(log N) bandwidth and small client storage, and finally Circuit ORAM (Wang et al., 2015), which proved the Goldreich–Ostrovsky bound asymptotically tight for statistically secure ORAM with large blocks. We formalize the access and eviction invariants of both tree constructions, compare their asymptotic and concrete costs, and explain why the Θ(√N) square-root barrier stood for fifteen years before the tree paradigm dismantled it.

---

## 1 Introduction

Encryption protects the *content* of outsourced data, but not the *pattern* in which it is touched. An adversary observing which encrypted blocks a client reads and writes can infer search keywords, query selectivity, and program control flow [4]. Goldreich and Ostrovsky formalized this in 1996 as *oblivious simulation of RAM programs*: a compiler transforms any RAM program into one whose physical access sequence is independent of the logical sequence, up to its length [1].

The key metric is **bandwidth overhead**: the multiplicative factor by which physical block accesses exceed logical ones. Goldreich and Ostrovsky proved that any ORAM treating blocks as opaque balls, with O(1) client storage and statistical security, needs Ω(log N) amortized overhead [1], and gave two constructions: the **square-root ORAM** (amortized O(√N) overhead, constant client storage) and the **hierarchical ORAM** (amortized O(log³ N), larger client state).

For over a decade, the square-root construction's Θ(√N) amortized cost stood as a *barrier*: the simplest sublinear-overhead scheme with truly constant client storage, seemingly unimprovable without large client memory or heavyweight oblivious sorting. The tree paradigm of Shi et al. (2011) [2], refined into **Path ORAM** (Stefanov et al., 2013) [3], broke through to O(log N) bandwidth. **Circuit ORAM** (Wang et al., 2015) [4] then closed the theoretical loop, proving the Ω(log N) bound *tight* for block size Ω(log² N).

This thesis (i) unifies the square-root, hierarchical, Path, and Circuit ORAM constructions around the invariants that make each secure; (ii) dissects Path ORAM's greedy eviction and Circuit ORAM's metadata-separated eviction down to executable pseudocode; (iii) compares asymptotic and concrete costs with a worked example at N = 2²⁰; and (iv) maps the modern lower-bound landscape and its escape hatches.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The ORAM Security Model

A client with small trusted storage outsources N encrypted blocks to an untrusted server. For a logical access sequence *y* of reads/writes, the client issues a physical access sequence A(ℳ, y). Security demands:

> **Theorem:** *(ORAM security, informal.)* For all equal-length logical sequences y, y′, the physical sequences A(ℳ, y) and A(ℳ, y′) are statistically (or computationally) indistinguishable. The server learns nothing about operation types, addresses, or data — only the total number of accesses.

Three parameters govern every construction: **bandwidth overhead**, **client storage**, and **server storage** (usually O(N) blocks).

### 2.2 Goldreich–Ostrovsky: Square-Root and Hierarchical ORAM

The 1996 paper [1] gave two compilers:

- **Square-root ORAM.** The server stores N blocks permuted by a secret permutation π, plus a *shelter* of √N slots. Each access scans the shelter; on a miss the client fetches π(a) from the permuted array (on a hit, a dummy fetch from a random position). The block is re-encrypted, remapped, and appended to the shelter. Every √N accesses, the memory is obliviously reshuffled under a fresh permutation. Amortized cost: Θ(√N) per access with O(1) client storage — the √N shelter size exactly balances scan cost against reshuffle amortization. This equilibrium is the **square-root barrier**.

- **Hierarchical ORAM.** Data lives in L = O(log N) levels, level i a hash table of 2ⁱ blocks. Each access probes every level; fresh copies go to the smallest level; full levels are obliviously merged upward with refreshed hash keys. Amortized cost O(log³ N); client storage O(log N).

Both treat blocks as *opaque balls* — moved and re-encrypted, never encoded.

### 2.3 The Lower Bound and the Tree-Based Turn

Goldreich and Ostrovsky proved an **Ω(log N) amortized lower bound** for O(1)-storage, opaque-ball, statistically secure ORAM [1]. The gap between this floor and the best upper bounds defined the field's frontier for years. Shi et al. (2011) introduced the **binary-tree ORAM framework** [2]: blocks are assigned to random leaves of a server-side binary tree, each block stored somewhere on the path to its leaf, and remapped to a fresh leaf on every access — achieving O(log³ N) *worst-case* overhead and becoming the substrate for Path ORAM.

## 3 Methodology

This thesis is a *theoretical synthesis with constructive verification*:

1. **Invariant-driven formalization.** For each construction we isolate the probabilistic invariant implying security — e.g., Path ORAM's invariant that a block sits on the path to its assigned leaf or in the stash, with uniform independent leaf assignments — and show the access procedure preserves it.
2. **Unified asymptotic accounting.** All schemes are normalized to one cost model (block size D, O(1)-block client storage unless stated) and cross-checked against the primary sources [1,2,3,4].
3. **Executable specification.** Path ORAM's access procedure is implemented in Python (§5) and Circuit ORAM's eviction in Haskell-flavored pseudocode (§4.3). Concrete figures are computed analytically for N = 2²⁰, B = 4 KiB.

We assume a semi-honest server, semantically secure encryption, and PRF-based leaf assignment.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Path ORAM: Invariants, Read Path, and Greedy Eviction

Path ORAM [3] stores N blocks in a binary tree of height L = ⌈log₂ N⌉ − 1. Each node is a **bucket** of up to Z blocks (Z = 4 in the analysis; Z = 5 is common in deployments), padded with encrypted dummies. The client holds a **position map** (block → random leaf) and a **stash** buffer.

Two invariants hold: *(i)* every block is in the stash or in a bucket on the path to its assigned leaf; *(ii)* leaf assignments are uniform and independent of the access pattern. Each access to block *a*:

1. **Lookup.** x := position[a]; set position[a] ← fresh uniform leaf x′.
2. **Read path.** Read all buckets on P(x) into the stash; extract the block.
3. **Update.** On a write, replace the block's contents in the stash.
4. **Evict.** Greedily push stash blocks back onto P(x): scanning leaf-to-root, place each block in the deepest bucket on P(x) that has free space and lies on the block's own assigned path; re-encrypt and write the path back.

Security follows because every access reads and writes *exactly one full root-to-leaf path* of re-encrypted blocks, and the path sequence P(x₁), P(x₂), … is i.i.d. uniform by invariant (ii) [3].

> **Theorem:** *(Path ORAM stash bound [3].)* For Z = 4 and any R = ω(log N), the stash exceeds R blocks after any polynomial-length access sequence with probability at most N^(−ω(1)). A few hundred stash blocks suffice for failure below 2⁻⁸⁰ at practical N.

### 4.2 The Recursion Trick for the Position Map

The position map costs N·log N bits — O(N) client storage. Path ORAM stores it **recursively** [3]: the map becomes data blocks in a smaller Path ORAM, whose map lives in another ORAM, and so on. Packing X = ⌊B / log N⌋ entries per block of size B, each level shrinks the map by X, giving recursion depth O(log N / log B):

| Block size regime | Recursion depth | Total bandwidth overhead |
|---|---|---|
| B = O(log N) | O(log N / log log N) | O(log² N / log log N) |
| B = Ω(log² N) | O(1) | **O(log N)** |

Path ORAM was thus first to achieve O(log N) bandwidth with small client storage — but only for *large* blocks, a gap Circuit ORAM narrowed.

### 4.3 Circuit ORAM: Metadata-Separate Eviction and the Tightness Result

Path ORAM's eviction is simple but circuit-unfriendly: placing stash blocks needs an oblivious sort over Θ(log N) items, i.e., an Ω(D log² N)-size eviction circuit. Since ORAM's killer application — RAM programs inside garbled circuits — pays *circuit size*, Wang et al. redesigned eviction to be deterministic and local [4]:

1. **Z = 2 buckets**, with *metadata* (leaf labels, occupancy) stored separately from data. A read path fetches only bucket metadata plus **one data block per bucket** — roughly halving read cost.
2. **Deterministic two-pass eviction.** Using only metadata, a *prepare* pass computes each on-path block's deepest legal destination; an *evict* pass moves at most one block per bucket leaf-ward. No oblivious sorting; the whole eviction is a circuit of size O(D log N)·ω(1).

```haskell
-- Circuit ORAM eviction: two deterministic passes over path metadata.
evict :: Path -> [Bucket] -> [Bucket]
evict path buckets =
  let prepared = preparePass path buckets   -- deepest legal target per block
  in  pushDown prepared                     -- move <=1 block per bucket leaf-ward

preparePass :: Path -> [Bucket] -> [(Block, Level)]
preparePass path buckets =
  [ (b, deepestLegal b path) | b <- concatMap slots buckets, occupied b ]
  where deepestLegal b p = maximum [ l | l <- levels p, onPath (leaf b) l ]
```

> **Theorem:** *(Circuit ORAM tightness [4].)* For B = Ω(log² N) and any f(N) = ω(1), there is a statistically secure ORAM with O(1) client storage and O(f(N)·log N) bandwidth. The Goldreich–Ostrovsky Ω(log N) bound is therefore asymptotically tight for large blocks — ruling out any ω(log N) lower bound there, the first such tightness result in the bound's 27-year history.

### 4.4 The Lower Bound and Its Modern Refinements

The original Ω(log N) bound [1] had two caveats, articulated by Boyle and Naor (ITCS 2016): it covered only **opaque-ball** constructions and only **statistical** adversaries — removing the former would imply superlinear sorting-circuit lower bounds. Larsen and Nielsen (2018) removed both caveats for *online* ORAM, proving **Ω(log n) bandwidth for any online ORAM**, even computationally secure with arbitrary encodings [5]; Hubáček et al. (2019) extended this to adversaries that cannot even attribute physical accesses to logical operations [6].

The bound's escape hatches map the research frontier: **server-side computation** (FHE/two-server PIR gives O(1) overhead [8], at the cost of the minimal trust model); **large client storage** (N^ε client blocks make oblivious sorting linear-IO [7]); **small blocks** (below Ω(log² N) the tightness proof fails — the major open gap); and **offline ORAM** (known access sequences admit O(log N) with *perfect* security via oblivious priority queues).

## 5 Empirical Results and Formal Analysis

### 5.1 Asymptotic Comparison

Normalized to N blocks, block size D, O(1)-block client storage (recursive position map where needed):

| Scheme | Year | Bandwidth overhead | Client storage | Server storage | Security |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Trivial (full scan) | — | Θ(N) | O(1) | N | statistical |
| Square-root ORAM [1] | 1996 | Θ(√N) amortized | O(1) | O(N) | statistical |
| Hierarchical ORAM [1] | 1996 | O(log³ N) amortized | O(log N) | O(N log N) | statistical |
| Hierarchical (Kushilevitz et al.) | 2012 | O(log² N / log log N) | O(1) | O(N) | computational |
| Tree ORAM (Shi et al.) [2] | 2011 | O(log³ N) worst-case | O(√N)¹ | O(N) | statistical |
| **Path ORAM** [3] | 2013 | O(log N), B=Ω(log²N) | O(log N)·ω(1) | O(N) | statistical |
| **Circuit ORAM** [4] | 2015 | O(log N)·ω(1), B=Ω(log²N) | O(1) | O(N) | statistical |
| Pyramid ORAM | 2017 | O(log N·(log log N)²) online | O(1) | O(N) | computational |

¹ Non-recursive position map; recursion reduces it at the cost in §4.2.

### 5.2 Concrete Worked Example (N = 2²⁰, B = 4 KiB)

For N = 2²⁰ ≈ 10⁶ blocks of 4 KiB (4 GiB), blocks transferred per logical access:

- **Square-root:** √N = 1024 blocks → **1024×** (4 MiB/access).
- **Hierarchical (GO96):** ≈ log³ N = 8000 probes amortized → **~8000×**.
- **Path ORAM (Z=4, depth 21):** one path read + one write = 2·21·4 = 168 blocks → **168×** (≈672 KiB), recursion depth O(1) since B = Ω(log² N).
- **Circuit ORAM (Z=2, depth 21):** metadata + 21 candidate blocks each way ≈ 2·21·(1+ε) → **~45×** (≈180 KiB).

Circuit ORAM transfers ~3.7× less than Path ORAM and ~23× less than square-root ORAM at this scale — the payoff of metadata-separated access and Z = 2.

### 5.3 Reference Implementation: Path ORAM Access

Python sketch of the §4.1 access procedure (encryption and recursive position map omitted; security-relevant structure preserved) [3]:

```python
import os, math
from dataclasses import dataclass

Z = 4
@dataclass
class Block:
    leaf: int
    addr: int            # -1 == dummy
    data: bytes

class PathORAM:
    def __init__(self, n: int):
        self.L = math.ceil(math.log2(n))
        self.nleaves = 2 ** self.L
        self.tree = {l: [[Block(-1, -1, b'\x00') for _ in range(Z)]
                         for _ in range(2 ** l)]
                     for l in range(self.L + 1)}
        self.position = {a: self._rand_leaf() for a in range(n)}
        self.stash: list[Block] = []

    def _rand_leaf(self) -> int:
        return int.from_bytes(os.urandom(4), 'big') % self.nleaves

    def _path(self, leaf: int):
        return [(l, (leaf >> (self.L - l)) & ((1 << l) - 1) if l else 0)
                for l in range(self.L + 1)]

    def access(self, addr: int, op: str, data: bytes = b'') -> bytes:
        x = self.position[addr]
        self.position[addr] = self._rand_leaf()   # remap before touching tree
        for (l, idx) in self._path(x):            # 1. read path into stash
            self.stash.extend(self.tree[l][idx])
            self.tree[l][idx] = [Block(-1, -1, b'\x00') for _ in range(Z)]
        blk = next(b for b in self.stash if b.addr == addr)
        out = blk.data
        if op == 'write':
            blk.data = data
        blk.leaf = self.position[addr]
        for (l, idx) in reversed(self._path(x)):  # 2. greedy eviction
            bucket = self.tree[l][idx]
            for b in list(self.stash):
                if len(bucket) >= Z:
                    break
                if b.addr != -1 and (l, idx) in self._path(b.leaf):
                    bucket.append(b)
                    self.stash.remove(b)
        return out
```

Every access reads and writes exactly one full path, and every block is remapped to an independent uniform leaf before the path is touched.

## 6 Limitations

1. **Small block sizes remain suboptimal.** Below Ω(log² N), no statistically secure construction matches Ω(log N); recursion inflates Path/Circuit ORAM to ω(log N). The best small-block bounds are computational [7] — a statistical-vs-computational gap.
2. **Concrete constants are heavy.** A 45–168× multiplier (§5.2) is prohibitive for latency-sensitive workloads; deployments reserve ORAM for high-value queries.
3. **The lower bound assumes a passive server.** Server-side computation or non-colluding servers achieve O(1)/sublogarithmic overhead [8], trading away the minimal trust assumption.
4. **Side channels outside the model.** ORAM hides *which* blocks are accessed, not *when* or *how much* flows; timing and volume leakage need complementary defenses.
5. **Stash overflow is probabilistic.** Statistical security means negligible — not zero — failure; Z and stash size must target an explicit bound like 2⁻⁸⁰.

---

## 7 Conclusion

The history of ORAM is a barrier located, formalized, and partially dismantled. Goldreich and Ostrovsky's square-root ORAM set the baseline — Θ(√N) amortized overhead with constant client storage — their hierarchical ORAM traded state for polylogarithmic cost, and their Ω(log N) lower bound drew the floor. The tree paradigm — Shi et al.'s framework, Path ORAM's greedy eviction and stash analysis, Circuit ORAM's deterministic metadata-separated eviction — drove the upper bound down to meet the floor: for B = Ω(log² N), statistically secure ORAM with O(log N)·ω(1) overhead exists, and no ω(log N) lower bound can ever be proved there [4].

What remains is precisely delimited: small block sizes, concrete efficiency, active adversaries, and the power of server-side computation. The square-root barrier fell, but the logarithmic floor — reinforced by Larsen–Nielsen and Hubáček et al. for the online setting [5,6] — looks like bedrock. Future progress will come from changing the model rather than beating Ω(log N) in it: richer servers, relaxed adversaries, and constructions tuned to the workloads — secure processors, oblivious databases, encrypted search — where hiding the access pattern is worth its logarithmic price.
