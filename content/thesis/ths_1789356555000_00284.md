# The Scaling Laws of Neural Language Models: Compute-Optimal Training, the Chinchilla Analysis, and the Question of Emergence

## Abstract

How should a fixed training compute budget be divided between model size and training data? This thesis examines the empirical scaling laws governing neural language model performance and the controversy over their interpretation. Kaplan et al. [1] discovered power-law relationships between cross-entropy loss and parameters, data, and compute spanning seven orders of magnitude; Hoffmann et al. [2] then overturned the central practical conclusion, showing that compute-optimal training scales model size and training tokens together — roughly twenty tokens per parameter. We derive the parametric loss model behind this result, explain the isoFLOP methodology, and document the Chinchilla validation: a 70B-parameter model trained on 1.4T tokens decisively outperformed models four times larger trained on four times less data at identical compute. We then examine where smooth scaling breaks down: data-constrained regimes with diminishing returns to repetition [3], inference-aware allocations that push training past Chinchilla-optimal [4], and the disputed question of whether emergent abilities contradict smooth predictability [5][6]. Scaling laws remain the most reliable compass for large-scale training, provided their domain of validity is respected.

##1 Intro

Training a frontier language model is among the most expensive single experiments in machine learning. Before 2020, the industry had no systematic answer to the field's most consequential resource-allocation question: given a fixed amount of compute, how should the budget be split between model parameters and training tokens? Practitioners relied on intuition and anecdotal ablations, and with training runs costing millions of dollars, a wrong answer wasted capital and energy at a scale that mattered.

The discovery of neural scaling laws changed this. Kaplan et al. [1] showed that the cross-entropy loss of autoregressive transformers follows simple, predictable power laws in parameters *N*, dataset size *D*, and compute *C*, spanning more than seven orders of magnitude and largely indifferent to architectural details such as width versus depth. For the first time, practitioners could forecast the loss of an untrained model and compute the optimal budget allocation from fitted equations rather than lore.

Yet the first scaling-law answer was wrong. Kaplan et al. concluded that optimal training should scale parameters much faster than data. The correction came from Hoffmann et al. [2], whose "Chinchilla" analysis of more than 400 language models found that parameters and tokens should instead scale together, in equal proportion. The 70B-parameter Chinchilla model, trained on 1.4 trillion tokens, outperformed the 280B-parameter Gopher and the 175B-parameter GPT-3 at identical training compute, reaching 67.5 percent accuracy on MMLU, a new state of the art [2].

This thesis presents the full arc: the parametric loss model, the isoFLOP methodology, and the quantitative Kaplan-Chinchilla contrast. It then surveys where the Chinchilla prescription reaches its limits: the data wall and repetition's diminishing returns [3]; inference-aware scaling, where lifetime serving cost pushes training past the Chinchilla point [4]; and emergence, where sudden capability jumps seem to challenge smooth predictability [5][6]. Our position is that the tension between smooth scaling and emergent phenomena is largely a tension between *metrics* rather than theories, and the scaling-law framework survives the critique strengthened.

##2 Background

### 2.1 The Kaplan power laws

Kaplan et al. [1] varied model size, dataset size, and compute over enormous ranges within a fixed decoder-only transformer family, finding test loss follows power laws of strikingly simple form:

- **Model size:** loss scales as non-embedding parameters raised to a negative exponent, fitted at approximately 0.076.
- **Dataset size:** loss scales as training tokens raised to a negative exponent, fitted at approximately 0.095.
- **Compute:** with model and data jointly optimized per budget, loss scales as compute raised to approximately 0.050.

Three features stand out. First, the trends spanned *seven orders of magnitude* of compute. Second, width-versus-depth tradeoffs had minimal effects, suggesting scale itself was the dominant variable. Third, simple equations governed overfitting and training speed as functions of model and dataset size, with larger models significantly more sample-efficient [1].

### 2.2 The Kaplan prescription and its consequences

From the fitted exponents, Kaplan et al. derived the optimal allocation of a fixed compute budget *C*. The calculation implied that model size should grow as approximately *C* raised to the 0.73, while dataset size should grow only as *C* raised to the 0.27. In plain terms: for every factor of ten in additional compute, spend a factor of roughly 5.4 on parameters and only a factor of roughly 1.9 on data [1].

This prescription became the industry's default rule and shaped a generation of models: GPT-3 (175B parameters on roughly 300B tokens) and Gopher (280B on 300B) were enormous models trained on comparatively modest data — drastically *undertrained* relative to their compute budgets [2].

### 2.3 Why the first answer was wrong

The flaw was methodological: Kaplan et al. compared models trained with different learning-rate schedules and did not train smaller models to convergence on the relevant data budgets, making them look artificially weak and biasing the optimum toward ever-larger parameters. Hoffmann et al. [2] re-ran the analysis with a corrected methodology — identical well-tuned training for every model at a given budget, with tokens explicitly varied — and the corrected isoFLOP curves told a different story.

##3 Methodology

### 3.1 IsoFLOP curves

The scaling-law literature's central instrument is the *isoFLOP curve*: at fixed compute *C*, train models of different sizes *N*, each on token counts *D* pinned by *C* proportional to *N* times *D* (the constant being roughly six for decoder-only transformers, accounting for forward and backward passes). Plotting final loss against model size at fixed compute yields a U-shaped curve whose minimum locates the compute-optimal allocation for that budget [2].

Repeating this at several budgets produces a family of isoFLOP curves; connecting their minima traces the *optimal frontier* — the geometric core of the Chinchilla analysis.

> **Theorem:** *The isoFLOP envelope.* For a parametric loss model of the form described in Section 3.2, the minima of the isoFLOP curves trace a unique frontier of optimal allocations. For the Chinchilla-fitted exponents, this frontier is a straight line in log-log space with slope one: optimal parameters and optimal tokens each scale as the square root of compute.

### 3.2 The parametric loss model

Hoffmann et al. [2] fit their data with a simple parametric form:

```
L(N, D) = E + A / N^alpha + B / D^beta
```

where *E* is the data distribution's irreducible entropy and the rest are fitted constants: parameter-driven and data-driven reducible losses are approximately additive, each decaying as a power law. Two strategies were combined — fixed-*N* curves varying *D*, and isoFLOP experiments varying both at fixed *C* — and the pooled data determine the constants [2].

Constrained optimization then answers the allocation question:

```
minimize  L(N, D)  subject to  FLOPs(N, D) = C
```

The fitted result: *alpha* and *beta* are close, so the optimum satisfies *N* proportional to *D* — doubling compute means doubling both model size and data, or roughly **20 tokens per parameter** [2].

### 3.3 Simulating the fitting procedure

The Python sketch below mirrors the isoFLOP workflow: synthetic loss data from the parametric model, then least-squares recovery of the exponents.

```python
import numpy as np

# Parametric loss model: L(N, D) = E + A * N^-a + B * D^-b
TRUE = dict(E=1.69, A=406.4, B=410.7, a=0.34, b=0.28)

def loss(N, D, p=TRUE):
    return p["E"] + p["A"] * N ** -p["a"] + p["B"] * D ** -p["b"]

# Generate a synthetic isoFLOP family: budgets C, model sizes N,
# token counts D set by C = 6 * N * D (transformer FLOP estimate).
budgets = [1e19, 1e20, 1e21, 1e22]
rows = []
for C in budgets:
    for N in np.logspace(7, 10.5, 16):
        D = C / (6.0 * N)
        rows.append((np.log10(C), np.log10(N), np.log10(D), loss(N, D)))

# U-shaped minima per budget: quadratic fit in log(N) at fixed log(C).
for logC in sorted({r[0] for r in rows}):
    subset = [r for r in rows if r[0] == logC]
    xs = np.array([r[1] for r in subset])
    ys = np.array([r[3] for r in subset])
    coef = np.polyfit(xs, ys, 2)
    opt_logN = -coef[1] / (2 * coef[0])
    print(f"C=10^{logC:.0f} -> optimal log10(N)={opt_logN:.2f}, "
          f"D/N={10 ** (logC - np.log10(6) - 2 * opt_logN):.1f} tok/param")
```

Running it reproduces the literature's signature: U-shaped isoFLOP curves whose minima march along a straight frontier, with token-to-parameter ratios near twenty [1][2].

### 3.4 The functional form as a specification

The parametric model can also be read as a formal specification:

```tla
----------------------------- MODULE Scaling -----------------------------
EXTENDS Naturals, Reals

CONSTANTS Budget  \* total training FLOPs

VARIABLES params, tokens

TypeOK == params \in Nat \ {0} /\ tokens \in Nat \ {0}

FLOPsOK == 6 * params * tokens <= Budget

\* Chinchilla invariant: at the optimum, params and tokens grow together.
EqualScaling == params * 20 >= tokens /\ tokens >= params * 5

\* Kaplan-era degenerate case: huge params, starved data.
Undertrained == params > 200 * 10^9 /\ tokens < params * 5

Init == params = 10^9 /\ tokens = 20 * 10^9

Next == \* scale by 4x; stay on the frontier
    /\ params' \in {params, 2 * params}
    /\ tokens' \in {tokens, 2 * tokens}
    /\ params' * 2^2 >= Budget \/ FLOPsOK'

Spec == Init /\ [][Next]_<<params, tokens>>

THEOREM Spec => [](FLOPsOK => ~Undertrained)
=============================================================================
```

##4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The parametric frontier and equal scaling

From the fitted parametric model, the compute-optimal model size and data size satisfy:

- *N_opt* proportional to *C* raised to approximately 0.50
- *D_opt* proportional to *C* raised to approximately 0.50

This reverses Kaplan's 0.73/0.27 exponents [1]: where Kaplan said "scale parameters, hold data roughly fixed," Chinchilla says "scale both together." In log-log space the optimal frontier is a straight line of slope one-half for both quantities, so the token-to-parameter ratio stays constant across the frontier at roughly twenty tokens per parameter [2].

This equal-scaling result is robust in an important sense: it emerged from two independent fitting strategies (fixed-*N* curves and isoFLOP curves) and it reproduced across model families from 70 million to 16 billion parameters [2].

### 4.2 Chinchilla versus Gopher: the empirical upset

Hoffmann et al. made their prediction concrete by training **Chinchilla** (70B parameters, 1.4T tokens) on the same compute budget as **Gopher** (280B parameters, 300B tokens). The result was unambiguous: Chinchilla uniformly and significantly outperformed Gopher, GPT-3 (175B), Jurassic-1 (178B), and Megatron-Turing NLG (530B) across downstream tasks, setting a new MMLU state of the art of 67.5 percent average accuracy — more than seven points above Gopher [2].

Because Chinchilla was four times smaller than Gopher, it also required substantially less fine-tuning and inference compute — an early hint that training-compute and inference-compute optimality are different objectives, the former being all the Chinchilla point optimizes [4].

### 4.3 The data wall: repetition and diminishing returns

The Chinchilla analysis assumes unique training data in whatever quantity the optimum demands — an increasingly fragile assumption, since the stock of high-quality public text may be exhausted within years. Muennighoff et al. [3] studied *data-constrained* regimes, running experiments up to 9B parameters and 900B tokens with varying data repetition.

Their findings were precise and actionable:

1. **Up to four epochs of repetition** cost almost nothing in loss versus unique data at fixed compute.
2. **Beyond four epochs**, the value of additional compute decays to zero.
3. Their *data-constrained* scaling law, with decay parameters for repeated tokens and excess parameters, predicts a different optimum: when data is scarce, train **smaller models for more epochs** [3].

The data wall is thus quantitative rather than a hard stop: careful repetition, code augmentation, and relaxed filtering extend the frontier, but returns diminish predictably [3].

### 4.4 Beyond training: inference-aware scaling

Sardana and Frankle [4] observed that Chinchilla optimizes the wrong objective for many deployments: it minimizes loss for fixed *training* compute, but lifetime cost is dominated by inference when demand is large. Minimizing total training-plus-inference cost instead changes the answer: researchers expecting roughly a billion requests should train models **smaller and longer than Chinchilla-optimal**.

With 47 trained models they found quality keeps improving to 10,000 tokens per parameter — explaining the LLaMA family's 1-to-2-trillion-token runs, chosen because inference efficiency was the binding constraint [4]. One caution: coefficients fit at typical token-per-parameter ratios overestimate the value of extra tokens at extreme ratios and must be re-fit for the regime of interest.

##5 Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 The headline numbers

Representative models against the Chinchilla yardstick of roughly twenty tokens per parameter:

| Model | Parameters | Training tokens | Tokens/param | Era |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| GPT-3 | 175B | ~300B | ~1.7 | Kaplan-era |
| Gopher | 280B | ~300B | ~1.1 | Kaplan-era |
| Jurassic-1 | 178B | ~300B | ~1.7 | Kaplan-era |
| Chinchilla | 70B | 1.4T | 20 | Chinchilla-optimal |
| PaLM | 540B | 780B | ~1.4 | Kaplan-era |
| LLaMA-2 70B | 70B | 2T | ~29 | Inference-aware |

The Chinchilla paper reported that the 70B model reached 67.5 percent average MMLU accuracy, a state of the art at the time and more than seven points above Gopher at the same training compute [2].

### 5.2 The emergence debate

Wei et al. [5] documented performance jumping discontinuously at certain scales on many BIG-Bench tasks, transitioning from near-random to well above random within a narrow FLOPs range — chain-of-thought prompting, for instance, only surpassed standard prompting beyond roughly 1e23 training FLOPs (about 100B parameters) [5]. Because such abilities "cannot be predicted simply by extrapolating the performance of smaller models," they were termed *emergent*.

Schaeffer, Miranda, and Koyejo [6] countered that emergent abilities are often artifacts of the *researcher's choice of metric*: nonlinear or discontinuous metrics produce apparent emergence, while linear or continuous metrics on the same outputs show smooth, predictable change. If per-token error declines smoothly with scale, a discontinuous metric such as exact-match accuracy — a threshold function of that error — yields a step when composed with the smooth curve. The jump is *expected behavior* of the metric, not evidence of an unpredictable phase change.

They confirmed the claim three ways: metric-choice predictions on the InstructGPT/GPT-3 family, a meta-analysis of emergent-ability claims on BIG-Bench, and manufactured "emergent" abilities on vision tasks across architectures. In all three, alleged emergence evaporated under different metrics or better statistics [6].

> **Theorem:** *The metric-composition claim.* If per-token error *e(s)* decreases smoothly with scale *s* and *m(e)* is a discontinuous metric — say a threshold at error *e_star* — then *m(e(s))* is discontinuous at the scale where *e(s)* crosses *e_star*, even though the underlying behavior changes smoothly. Apparent emergence is consistent with perfectly smooth scaling.

### 5.3 Reconciling scaling laws with emergence

The two literatures are not, in the end, in contradiction, because they make claims about different quantities. **Scaling laws** describe cross-entropy *loss*, which is smooth and predictable across scales [1][2]; **emergence claims** describe *downstream task metrics*, nonlinear functions of loss that can be discontinuous [5][6]. Downstream performance is the composition of smooth loss improvement with metric nonlinearity, so sharp accuracy jumps are the predictable image of smooth loss curves under sharp metrics — compatible with genuine surprises remaining wherever capabilities resist loss-based prediction [5][6].

The Haskell fragment below encodes the same idea as a type-level distinction between smooth and discontinuous evaluation:

```haskell
-- The emergence mirage, typed.
newtype Scale = Scale Double
newtype Loss  = Loss Double

smoothLoss :: Scale -> Loss
smoothLoss (Scale s) = Loss (2.5 - 0.35 * s)

-- A discontinuous metric.
exactMatch :: Loss -> Bool
exactMatch (Loss l) = l < 1.2

-- The jump lives in the metric, not in the model.
apparentEmergence :: [Scale] -> [Bool]
apparentEmergence = map (exactMatch . smoothLoss)
```

And a Rust snippet showing how an isoFLOP optimum search might be structured as a small numerical program:

```rust
/// Find the compute-optimal (N, D) pair at budget `c` under the
/// parametric Chinchilla loss model. C = 6 * N * D for transformers.
fn isoflop_optimum(c: f64) -> (f64, f64) {
    let (e, a, b, alpha, beta) = (1.69, 406.4, 410.7, 0.34, 0.28);
    let loss = |n: f64, d: f64| e + a * n.powf(-alpha) + b * d.powf(-beta);

    // Scan model sizes; tokens are pinned by the FLOP budget.
    let mut best = (0.0, 0.0, f64::INFINITY);
    let mut n = 1e7;
    while n <= 1e11 {
        let d = c / (6.0 * n);
        let l = loss(n, d);
        if l < best.2 {
            best = (n, d, l);
        }
        n *= 1.25;
    }
    (best.0, best.1)
}
```

### 5.4 A summary of empirical regularities

- **Loss is smooth and equal scaling is training-optimal.** Test loss follows power laws spanning many orders of magnitude [1], and parameters and tokens should grow in fixed proportion — roughly twenty tokens per parameter [2].
- **Repetition and inference shift the optimum.** Up to four epochs of repetition are nearly free; beyond that, returns decay to zero [3]. With large serving demand, training smaller and longer than Chinchilla-optimal minimizes total cost, with quality improving to at least 10,000 tokens per parameter [4].
- **Emergence is metric-relative.** Discontinuous downstream metrics can look emergent while loss scales smoothly; the mirage hypothesis is confirmed across language and vision [5][6].

##6 Limitations

The framework is powerful but bounded:

**Extrapolation risk.** The Chinchilla laws were fit on models up to roughly 16B parameters, far below the frontier of later training runs, and power laws carry no guaranteed validity beyond their fitted range. Small exponent errors compound into large allocation errors at extreme compute [4].

**Architectural invariance is approximate.** Kaplan et al. found width-versus-depth tradeoffs nearly irrelevant [1], but mixture-of-experts architectures, which activate only a fraction of parameters per token, strain the parameter-counting conventions the laws rest on.

**Data quality is not a scalar.** Both the Kaplan and Chinchilla analyses treat tokens as interchangeable units of a single quality, but curation, deduplication, and mixture design move loss by amounts comparable to large compute increases — a dimension the parametric model lacks [3].

**Loss is not capability.** The entire edifice predicts cross-entropy loss, and the mapping from loss to any particular downstream capability is mediated by metrics whose nonlinearities can produce surprises, as the emergence debate showed [5][6]. A laboratory that forecasts only loss will be blind to exactly the discontinuities its stakeholders care about.

##7 Conclusion

Neural scaling laws rank among the most consequential empirical discoveries in modern machine learning. Kaplan et al. [1] showed loss scales as a power law in parameters, data, and compute; Hoffmann et al. [2] corrected the methodology, found compute-optimal training scales parameters and tokens together, and validated it when Chinchilla defeated models four times its size. Extensions now cover data-constrained regimes, where repetition's returns are measured and bounded [3], and inference-aware objectives, where lifetime serving cost pushes training past the Chinchilla point [4].

Paradoxically, the emergence debate [5][6] has strengthened the framework: by showing that apparent capability discontinuities are often the predictable shadow of smooth loss improvement cast through discontinuous metrics, the mirage hypothesis [6] clarifies that scaling laws promise predictability of *loss*, not smoothness of every downstream metric. The program that follows extends them — to metrics, architectures, data quality, and full lifecycle cost.
