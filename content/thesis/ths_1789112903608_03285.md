# Lazy Functions: The Neural Tangent Kernel and the Geometry of Wide-Network Learning

## Abstract

The neural tangent kernel (NTK) provides one of the few mathematically complete descriptions of deep learning optimization and generalization, recasting gradient descent on an infinitely wide neural network as kernel regression with a deterministic, architecture-defined kernel. We present a self-contained account of the NTK theory: the tangent-feature map and the linearization identity that converts gradient flow on parameters into a linear dynamical system on function values; the infinite-width limit in which the kernel freezes and the network behaves as a fixed-kernel machine (the "lazy training" regime); the lazy-versus-rich dichotomy separating kernel-like learning from genuine representation learning; and what the framework does and does not explain about generalization, including its relationship to double descent and benign overfitting. We derive the analytic NTK for deep fully-connected networks, sketch the convergence proof for gradient flow at infinite width, and survey the empirical evidence that practical networks sit between the lazy and feature-learning extremes. We close with the open questions — adaptive feature learning, finite-width corrections, scaling laws — that define the frontier beyond the lazy regime.

## 1. Introduction

Deep learning poses a paradox that classical statistical learning theory cannot resolve: models with vastly more parameters than training examples, trained to near-zero training error by a local algorithm, routinely generalize. Among the frameworks proposed to explain this, the **neural tangent kernel** (NTK) stands out for mathematical precision. Introduced by Jacot, Gabriel, and Hongler [1], it establishes an exact correspondence between gradient-based training of sufficiently wide networks and kernel regression, replacing the mysteries of nonconvex optimization with the well-understood machinery of reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces.

The core insight is a change of perspective: track the *function*, not the parameters. For a network $f(\theta, x)$ with parameters $\theta \in \mathbb{R}^p$, gradient flow $\dot{\theta}_t = -\nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}(\theta_t)$ induces dynamics on the predictions $u_t = f(\theta_t, X)$ governed by the Gram matrix of parameter gradients:

$$
\Theta_t(x, x') = \left\langle \nabla_\theta f(\theta_t, x), \nabla_\theta f(\theta_t, x') \right\rangle,
$$

the tangent kernel at time $t$ [1]. The prediction dynamics $\dot{u}_t = -\Theta_t (u_t - y)$ is linear *conditional on the kernel*; the nonlinearity of deep learning is entirely contained in the evolution of $\Theta_t$ itself.

The celebrated result: under the NTK parameterization, as hidden-layer widths tend to infinity, $\Theta_t$ converges to a deterministic, time-independent limit $\Theta_\infty$, and training reduces exactly to kernel gradient descent with that fixed kernel [1][2]. Parameters change by only $O(1/\sqrt{m})$ in width $m$, yet the function traverses macroscopic distances. Chizat and Bach named this **lazy training** [3] — and showed it is generic in overparameterized settings, raising the uncomfortable question of whether the NTK explains deep learning at all, given that the lazy regime provably excludes feature learning.

This article develops the NTK program in full: the linearization identity, the infinite-width limit, the precise meaning of lazy versus rich training, the empirical record, and the connections to the generalization puzzle — interpolation, double descent, and the inductive bias of gradient descent in function space.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 Kernels and the representer view

A positive definite kernel $k(x, x')$ defines a reproducing kernel Hilbert space (RKHS) $\mathcal{H}$ with $h(x) = \langle h, k(x, \cdot) \rangle_{\mathcal{H}}$. The **representer theorem** states that minimizers of $\sum_i \ell(y_i, h(x_i)) + \lambda \|h\|_{\mathcal{H}}^2$ lie in the span of $\{k(x_i, \cdot)\}$ — the learned function is a linear combination of kernel evaluations at the training points.

Neural networks seemed to break with this picture because their features are *learned*. The NTK restores the kernel view subtly: the relevant features are not hidden activations but the *tangent features* $\nabla_\theta f(\theta_0, x)$, the directions in which the output can move at initialization. In the lazy regime these are frozen, and the network is exactly a kernel machine with kernel $\Theta_\infty$ [1][3].

### 2.2 The Gaussian-process connection

At initialization, infinite-width networks with i.i.d. Gaussian weights converge to a Gaussian process — the **neural network Gaussian process** (NNGP) — with covariance computed recursively by layer [2]. The NTK is related but distinct: where the NNGP covariance captures $\mathbb{E}[f(\theta_0, x) f(\theta_0, x')]$, the NTK captures $\mathbb{E}[\langle \nabla_\theta f, \nabla_\theta f \rangle]$, the geometry of the parameter-to-function map. The NNGP governs the *prior* over functions; the NTK governs the *learning dynamics*. Both are deterministic at infinite width and computable in closed form [4].

### 2.3 The optimization miracle that demanded explanation

By 2018, several results showed gradient descent finds global minima of the training loss for sufficiently overparameterized networks [5]. These were pure optimization results — they guaranteed convergence but said little about *which* function was found. The NTK supplied the missing characterization: the found function is the kernel-regression solution of $\Theta_\infty$ [1][2]. Understanding that solution's inductive bias became the route to understanding generalization.

### 2.4 Regimes of width: lazy versus rich

Two distinct infinite-width limits exist, distinguished by parameterization [3][6]:

| Regime | Last-layer scaling | Weight movement | Kernel evolution | Feature learning |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Lazy (NTK)** | $1/\sqrt{m}$ | $O(1/\sqrt{m})$ per weight | Frozen at $\Theta_\infty$ | No |
| **Rich (mean-field)** | $1/m$ | $O(1)$ | Evolves with training | Yes |

In the lazy regime the tangent kernel stays fixed and training is a first-order Taylor expansion about initialization; in the rich (mean-field) regime the distribution of hidden units evolves and the network learns adaptive features [6]. Which regime describes practice is an empirical question with profound consequences.

## 3. Methodology

### 3.1 The linearization identity

The NTK program rests on a single differential identity. For mean-squared error $\mathcal{L} = \tfrac{1}{2}\|f(\theta_t, X) - y\|^2$ under gradient flow:

$$
\frac{d\theta_t}{dt} = -\nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}, \qquad
\frac{du_t}{dt} = \nabla_\theta f(\theta_t, X)^\top \dot{\theta}_t = -\Theta_t(X,X)\,(u_t - y).
$$

> **Theorem:** [1] Under gradient flow, network predictions evolve by the linear ODE $\dot{u}_t = -\Theta_t(u_t - y)$ driven by the tangent kernel $\Theta_t$. If $\Theta_t$ were constant and positive definite, predictions would converge exponentially to the interpolant $y$.

This identity is exact at any width. Approximation enters only in asserting $\Theta_t \approx \Theta_0$ (freezing) or $\Theta_0 \approx \Theta_\infty$ (determinism).

### 3.2 Taking the infinite-width limit

With NTK parameterization (each weight matrix scaled by $1/\sqrt{m}$, standard Gaussian initialization), two concentration phenomena occur as $m \to \infty$ [1][2]:

1. **At initialization**, $\Theta_0(x, x') \to \Theta_\infty(x, x')$ in probability, with $\Theta_\infty$ deterministic and computable by recursion.
2. **During training**, $\sup_t \|\Theta_t - \Theta_0\| \to 0$ in probability — the kernel freezes.

The proof couples the nonlinear network to its linearization $\tilde{f}(\theta, x) = f(\theta_0, x) + \langle \theta - \theta_0, \nabla_\theta f(\theta_0, x) \rangle$ and shows the coupling error vanishes with width, since each parameter contributes $O(1/\sqrt{m})$ to the output while gradient noise averages out [2][5].

### 3.3 The analytic NTK for deep ReLU networks

For a depth-$L$ fully-connected ReLU network, $\Theta_\infty$ admits a closed-form recursion. With $\Sigma^{(h)}$ the NNGP covariance at layer $h$ and $\dot{\Sigma}^{(h)}$ the covariance of activation derivatives [1][4]:

$$
\Theta^{(h+1)}(x,x') = \Sigma^{(h+1)}(x,x') + \Theta^{(h)}(x,x') \cdot \dot{\Sigma}^{(h+1)}(x,x'), \quad \Theta^{(1)} = \Sigma^{(1)}.
$$

The first term accumulates the current layer's contribution; the second propagates tangent geometry from earlier layers. For ReLU, both reduce to elementary functions of the angle between inputs (the arccosine kernel family). The `neural-tangents` library computes these kernels exactly for large-scale kernel regression [7].

### 3.4 Closed-form predictions

In the lazy limit, gradient flow with fixed kernel $\Theta_\infty$ converges to the **ridgeless kernel-regression** interpolant [1][2]:

$$
f_\infty(x) = \Theta_\infty(x, X)\,\Theta_\infty(X,X)^{-1} y.
$$

The snippet below demonstrates the empirical tangent kernel at finite width:

```python
import torch

def empirical_ntk(model, x1, x2):
    """Finite-width tangent kernel via Jacobian Gram matrix."""
    params = [p for p in model.parameters() if p.requires_grad]
    def jacobian(x):
        model.zero_grad()
        out = model(x)
        grads = torch.autograd.grad(out.sum(), params, retain_graph=True)
        return torch.cat([g.reshape(-1) for g in grads])
    J1 = torch.stack([jacobian(x.unsqueeze(0)) for x in x1])
    J2 = torch.stack([jacobian(x.unsqueeze(0)) for x in x2])
    return J1 @ J2.T
# In the lazy regime this Gram matrix is ~constant across training
# and concentrates around Theta_infty as width grows.
```

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Why the kernel freezes: a scaling argument

Freezing follows from a clean scaling argument [2][3]. Each weight enters the output through a $1/\sqrt{m}$ factor, so perturbing one weight by $\delta$ moves the output by $O(\delta/\sqrt{m})$. Gradient flow must move weights by $O(\sqrt{m})$ in aggregate Euclidean norm to change predictions by $O(1)$ — but spread across $O(m)$ weights, *individual* weights move by only $O(1/\sqrt{m})$. Tangent features depend on weights through activations that shift by $O(1/\sqrt{m})$; hence the kernel changes by a vanishing amount. Formally, $\|\theta_t - \theta_0\|_2 = O(1)$ while $\|\theta_t - \theta_0\|_\infty = O(1/\sqrt{m})$ [2].

This is the *laziness*: parameters barely move individually, yet collectively steer the function to an interpolant. The network behaves as its own first-order Taylor expansion:

$$
f(\theta_t, x) \approx f(\theta_0, x) + \langle \theta_t - \theta_0, \nabla_\theta f(\theta_0, x) \rangle.
$$

### 4.2 Lazy versus rich: when features matter

Chizat and Bach [3] made the lazy/rich distinction sharp and raised the central criticism: in the lazy regime the hypothesis class is fixed at initialization, so the network *cannot* perform representation learning. For problems where relevant features are absent from the tangent features — e.g., a single-index model $y = g(w^\star \cdot x)$ with unknown $w^\star$ — the NTK predictor suffers poor sample complexity while a network adapting its hidden units learns efficiently [6][8].

Geiger et al. [6] disentangled the regimes with an interpolation trick: scaling the output by $\alpha$ and the learning rate by $1/\alpha^2$ sends $\alpha \to \infty$ to the lazy limit while $\alpha = O(1)$ retains feature learning. They found that (i) both limits reach zero training error, (ii) the lazy limit typically generalizes worse on structured tasks, and (iii) the gap shrinks on tasks aligned with the NTK's inductive bias. Real networks live on a spectrum; the NTK describes one endpoint [6].

> **Theorem:** [3] In the lazy regime, $\|f(\theta_t, \cdot) - \tilde{f}_t(\cdot)\| = O(1/\sqrt{m})$ uniformly, where $\tilde{f}_t$ is the linearization about initialization. All learning is linear in the initial tangent features $\nabla_\theta f(\theta_0, \cdot)$.

### 4.3 The inductive bias of the NTK: spectral analysis

On the sphere, the ReLU NTK is a *dot-product kernel*, diagonalizable in spherical harmonics with eigenvalues decaying polynomially in frequency [4][8]. Kernel gradient descent fits eigencomponents at rates proportional to eigenvalues — low frequencies first. This **spectral bias** makes the NTK interpolant the minimum-RKHS-norm interpolant, biased toward smooth functions.

The bias cuts both ways. It explains generalization on natural image data (labels smooth in the kernel's eigenbasis) and predicts failures: tasks needing high-frequency structure, or alignment with directions the kernel underweights, are learned slowly or not at all [8]. The convolutional NTK inherits translation-aware structure, which is why CNTK regression reaches ~90% on CIFAR-10 [4] — competitive with early deep networks, but at enormous cost and still short of trained CNNs.

### 4.4 Convergence guarantees via overparameterization

The NTK converts nonconvex optimization into a tractable object. The standard argument [5][2]:

1. **Positive definiteness:** for distinct training points, $\Theta_\infty(X,X)$ has $\lambda_{\min} > 0$.
2. **Coupling:** with width $m \gtrsim \text{poly}(n, L, 1/\lambda_{\min})$, the empirical kernel stays within $\lambda_{\min}/2$ of $\Theta_\infty$ throughout training.
3. **Global convergence:** $\|u_t - y\|^2 \le e^{-\lambda_{\min} t}\|u_0 - y\|^2$ — exponential decay to a global minimum.

Du et al. [5] established this for deep ReLU networks; later work sharpened width requirements and extended it to residual and convolutional architectures. The price is width: polynomial bounds far beyond practical sizes, and they do not explain why much narrower networks still optimize reliably — a gap pointing to loose worst-case coupling rather than a broken picture.

### 4.5 Beyond the kernel: finite-width corrections

The NTK is the *first-order* term of a systematic expansion. **Taylorized training** [9] considers higher-order Taylor expansions about initialization, whose terms capture progressively more feature learning; the **neural tangent hierarchy** [9] tracks the coupled dynamics of the kernel and its derivatives. Low-order Taylorized models empirically recover much of the gap between NTK regression and trained networks.

A complementary phenomenon is **kernel alignment**: finite networks near the lazy regime often undergo a rapid early change — the empirical NTK's top eigenvectors rotating toward the label vector — before settling into approximately linear dynamics [6][10]. Practical training appears *kernel-like but not kernel-fixed*: features move once, early, then dynamics are well approximated by a data-dependent frozen kernel.

## 5. Empirical Results / Proofs

### 5.1 NTK regression matches wide networks

Lee et al. [2] provided the landmark validation: for wide fully-connected and convolutional networks on MNIST and CIFAR-10, trained-network predictions track the analytic NTK predictor closely, with agreement improving in width — a direct test of the linearization identity.

### 5.2 Exact computation at scale

Arora et al. [4] computed the CNTK exactly for realistic architectures and used it for kernel regression on CIFAR-10, reaching 90% accuracy — far better than prior kernel methods, yet trailing trained CNNs by several points and costing $O(n^2)$ kernel construction ($\sim$1000 GPU-hours in the compositional-kernel study [4]). The NTK is a genuinely competitive kernel, and simultaneously a demonstration of its limits [7].

### 5.3 The lazy-rich gap on structured tasks

The evidence *against* the NTK as a complete theory [3][6][8]:

- On teacher-student tasks with planted low-dimensional structure, feature-learning networks achieve information-theoretic sample complexity while NTK regression needs polynomially more samples [8].
- On CIFAR-10, standard training beats CNTK regression; the gap is attributed to learned representations a frozen kernel cannot express [4][6].
- Kernel alignment measurements show the empirical NTK's eigenvectors rotating toward labels early in training — direct evidence of non-lazy dynamics at practical scale [6][10].

### 5.4 Generalization: interpolation, double descent, benign overfitting

The NTK illuminates the **generalization puzzle** through interpolating kernel machines. Belkin et al. [11] documented the **double descent** curve — test error falling, rising near the interpolation threshold, then falling again — and showed it arises naturally in kernel and random-feature models.

The modern synthesis: near the interpolation threshold the model is fragile; deep in overparameterization, the minimum-norm kernel interpolant is smooth and benign. The NTK makes this precise: the risk of the ridgeless interpolant decomposes spectrally, showing exactly which eigencomponents contribute variance and which are safely fit [10][11]. As width grows, the interpolant's RKHS norm concentrates and its effective complexity *decreases*.

```haskell
-- Spectral picture of kernel interpolation (conceptual)
data SpectralComponent = SC
  { eigenvalue :: Double   -- lambda_i: fit speed under gradient flow
  , alignment  :: Double   -- label energy on eigenvector v_i
  , noiseShare :: Double   -- fraction of label noise on v_i
  }
-- Double descent: near the interpolation threshold, tiny eigenvalues
-- amplify noiseShare; deep in overparameterization the interpolant's
-- norm concentrates and effective complexity shrinks.
```

### 5.5 Where the theory stands

The ledger is balanced. The NTK correctly predicts optimization behavior (exponential convergence, width improving conditioning), matches trained networks in the genuinely wide regime, and gives the sharpest account of interpolation and double descent [1][2][4][10][11]. It cannot capture representation learning, underestimates practical networks on structured tasks, and its width requirements are vacuous at realistic scales [3][6][8]. The fair summary: the NTK is the *zeroth-order theory of deep learning* — exactly right about the lazy endpoint, and the baseline against which feature learning must be measured.

## 6. Limitations

1. **No feature learning by construction.** The frozen-kernel assumption is the theory's power and ceiling: grokking, in-context learning, hierarchical feature composition all lie outside the lazy regime [3][8].
2. **Vacuous width requirements.** Proofs demand width polynomial in $n$ with large exponents; practitioners train far narrower networks. The gap between sufficient and necessary width remains open [5].
3. **Intractable kernel computation.** Exact NTK regression costs $O(n^2)$ memory and time — infeasible at the data scales where deep learning shines [4][7].
4. **Data-independent inductive bias.** The NTK is fixed by architecture and initialization; tasks misaligned with its eigenstructure are provably hard for the lazy learner but easy for feature learners [8].
5. **Optimizer and loss gaps.** The cleanest results cover gradient flow with MSE. SGD noise, adaptive optimizers, cross-entropy, and finite learning rates add corrections (implicit regularization, edge-of-stability) the basic picture misses [10].
6. **Parameterization fragility.** Everything hinges on the $1/\sqrt{m}$ scaling; the mean-field $1/m$ scaling yields a qualitatively different limit. Learning-rate choices and normalization can push effective dynamics toward the rich regime [3][6].

---

## 7. Conclusion

The neural tangent kernel transformed deep learning theory by exhibiting a nontrivial limit in which gradient-based training is *exactly* kernel regression [1][2]. From this correspondence flow rigorous convergence guarantees [5], closed-form predictors [4], a spectral account of inductive bias [8], and the clearest explanation of interpolation and double descent [10][11] — the most successful mathematical theory of neural network training yet produced.

Its limits are equally instructive. The lazy regime that makes the theory tractable is precisely the regime without representation learning [3][6], and practical networks exploit non-lazy dynamics — kernel alignment, silent feature adaptation, structured sample efficiency — that the frozen-kernel picture cannot express [6][8][10]. The frontier is what lies beyond: finite-width corrections, the tangent hierarchy [9], mean-field dynamics, and a quantitative theory of *when* features are learned and *how much* they matter. The NTK endures as the indispensable baseline — the zeroth-order theory against which every richer account of deep learning must be judged.
