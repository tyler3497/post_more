# Causal Inference from Observational Data: Structural Causal Models, the do-Calculus, and the Potential Outcomes Framework

## Abstract

Causal questions — does a treatment *cause* an outcome, or merely predict it — are central to medicine, economics, and policy, yet association alone cannot answer them. This thesis develops the two dominant formalisms for causal inference from observational data: Pearl's *structural causal models* (SCMs), which encode causal knowledge in directed acyclic graphs and answer interventional queries through the *do*-operator and the *do*-calculus [1], and Rubin's *potential outcomes framework*, which defines causal effects as contrasts between hypothetical states of the world [3]. We derive the graphical identification criteria — *d*-separation, the backdoor and frontdoor criteria — and state the three rules of the *do*-calculus with its completeness guarantee. We then connect structure to estimation: propensity scores, inverse probability weighting, doubly robust and double machine learning estimators [2][5], instrumental variables [4], mediation, and sensitivity analysis [6]. Throughout we formalize the identification assumptions — consistency, exchangeability, positivity, no interference — and show precisely where each modern estimator can fail.

## 1 Introduction

In 1950, epidemiologists found that lung-cancer patients were far more likely to be smokers than matched controls. The association was striking — yet the causal question, "does smoking *cause* lung cancer?", remained formally unresolved for a decade. The reason exposes the central fault line of empirical science: **association is a property of the joint distribution** \(P(Y \mid X)\); **causation is a property of what would happen under intervention**, \(P(Y \mid \text{do}(X = x))\) [1]. No observational data alone can cross this gap without assumptions.

This thesis develops the two dominant formalisms for crossing it.

**Pearl's structural causal model (SCM)** framework treats causation as a property of a data-generating mechanism: structural equations embedded in a causal graph. Interventions are *surgical* — they sever incoming arrows — and the *do*-calculus provides complete graphical rules for reducing interventional queries to ordinary conditional probabilities [1].

**Rubin's potential outcomes framework** begins with each unit \(i\) and its *potential outcomes* \(Y_i(1), Y_i(0)\): the outcomes under treatment and control. The *fundamental problem of causal inference* is that at most one is ever observed [3]. Estimation proceeds through explicit identifying assumptions — *unconfoundedness*, that treatment is as-if random given covariates — and adjustment machinery such as propensity scores [2], inverse probability weighting, and their machine-learning descendants [5].

These frameworks are complementary: every SCM induces potential outcomes, and every potential-outcome estimand admits graphical semantics.

## 2 Background

The history of causal inference is the history of attempts to discipline the gap between *seeing* and *doing*. Neyman formalized potential outcomes for randomized experiments in 1923; Haavelmo (1943) introduced simulating interventions by modifying equations in an economic model — the ancestor of the *do*-operator [1].

The modern era begins with Rubin's 1974 paper, which treated \(Y_i(1)\) and \(Y_i(0)\) as *two separate variables* — at most one ever observed — and extended the notation systematically to observational studies [3]. This made precise what randomization buys: treatment assignment independent of potential outcomes, so that

$$
\mathbb{E}[Y_i \mid T_i = 1] - \mathbb{E}[Y_i \mid T_i = 0]
= \mathbb{E}[Y_i(1) - Y_i(0)],
$$

the *average treatment effect* (ATE). In observational data this equality fails, and the difference has a name: **selection bias**.

The second pillar arrived in 1983, when Rosenbaum and Rubin proved the covariate vector \(X\) could be compressed into the scalar **propensity score** \(e(X) = P(T = 1 \mid X)\) [2]. If treatment is ignorable given \(X\), it is ignorable given \(e(X)\); matching, subclassification, and weighting on this score suffice to remove all observed confounding.

The third pillar came from AI. Pearl's *Causality* (2000; 2nd ed. 2009) provided a complete graphical calculus: structural causal models, *d*-separation, the *do*-operator, the backdoor and frontdoor criteria, and the three rules of the *do*-calculus — proved *complete* by Pearl, Tian, and Shpitser, so anything identifiable from graph plus data is derivable [1]. Econometricians reworked instrumental variables in potential-outcomes language: Angrist, Imbens, and Rubin (1996) showed that under monotonicity the classical IV estimand identifies the *local average treatment effect* (LATE) for compliers [4]. In 2018, Chernozhukov et al. fused semiparametric theory with machine learning: *double/debiased machine learning* (DML) uses Neyman-orthogonal score functions and cross-fitting to deliver \(\sqrt{n}\)-consistent inference even when nuisance functions are estimated by black-box learners [5]. Hernán and Robins (2020) synthesized these traditions into a single pedagogy organized around *exchangeability*, *positivity*, and *consistency* [6].

## 3 Methodology

We now fix notation and define the two formalisms precisely.

**Structural causal models.**

> **Definition (SCM).** A *structural causal model* is a tuple \(M = \langle \mathbf{U}, \mathbf{V}, \mathbf{F}, P(\mathbf{U}) \rangle\), where \(\mathbf{U}\) are exogenous (background) variables with distribution \(P(\mathbf{U})\), \(\mathbf{V}\) are endogenous variables, and \(\mathbf{F} = \{f_i\}\) are deterministic *structural equations* \(V_i = f_i(\text{PA}_i, U_i)\) assigning each endogenous variable as a function of its parents \(\text{PA}_i \subseteq \mathbf{V} \setminus \{V_i\}\) and its exogenous noise \(U_i\) [1].

The *causal graph* \(G\) has an arrow \(V_j \to V_i\) iff \(V_j\) appears in \(f_i\); we assume acyclicity (a DAG) throughout [1].

An **intervention** \(\text{do}(X = x)\) is modeled by *surgery*: replace the structural equation for \(X\) with the constant \(x\), deleting all arrows into \(X\) (the *mutilated* graph \(G_{\bar{X}}\)). The interventional distribution \(P(Y \mid \text{do}(X = x))\) is the distribution of \(Y\) in the mutilated model [1].

> **Theorem (Truncated factorization).** If \(P(\mathbf{v}) = \prod_i P(v_i \mid \text{pa}_i)\) per the DAG \(G\), then \(P(\mathbf{v} \mid \text{do}(x)) = \prod_{i: V_i \notin X} P(v_i \mid \text{pa}_i)\) with intervened variables fixed to \(x\) [1].

**Potential outcomes.** Each unit \(i\) possesses potential outcomes \(Y_i(t)\) for each treatment level \(t\). The observed outcome is linked to them by the **consistency** assumption,

$$
Y_i = Y_i(T_i),
$$

i.e., the observed outcome equals the potential outcome under the treatment actually received [3][6]. The individual causal effect is \(\tau_i = Y_i(1) - Y_i(0)\); the estimands of interest are aggregates:

| Estimand | Definition | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| ATE | \(\mathbb{E}[Y(1) - Y(0)]\) | Effect over the whole population |
| ATT | \(\mathbb{E}[Y(1) - Y(0) \mid T = 1]\) | Effect on those actually treated |
| CATE | \(\mathbb{E}[Y(1) - Y(0) \mid X = x]\) | Heterogeneous effect by covariates |
| LATE | \(\mathbb{E}[Y(1) - Y(0) \mid \text{complier}]\) | Effect for instrument compliers |

The causal effect of \(T\) on \(Y\) is **identified** if it can be written as a functional of the observed-data distribution \(P(Y, T, X)\) under stated assumptions. Identification is logically prior to estimation: it asks whether the quantity is *in principle* recoverable [6].

**The three identifiability conditions.** Hernán and Robins organize observational identification around three conditions [6]:

1. **Exchangeability (unconfoundedness):** \(Y(t) \perp\!\!\!\perp T \mid X\) for all \(t\). Conditional on covariates, treatment assignment is as-if random. This is the formal statement that *all confounders are observed*.
2. **Positivity:** \(0 < P(T = 1 \mid X = x) < 1\) for all \(x\) with \(P(X = x) > 0\). Every covariate stratum must have some chance of receiving each treatment; otherwise there is no empirical basis for the contrast.
3. **Consistency:** \(Y = Y(T)\), plus the *stable unit treatment value assumption* (SUTVA): one unit's potential outcomes do not depend on another unit's treatment — **no interference** — and there are no hidden versions of treatment.

Under all three, the ATE is identified by the **g-formula** (standardization):

$$
\mathbb{E}[Y(t)] = \sum_x \mathbb{E}[Y \mid T = t, X = x]\,P(X = x),
$$

which reweights the observed outcome regression to the covariate distribution of the target population [6].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 d-Separation and the Backdoor and Frontdoor Criteria

In a DAG, *d*-separation characterizes exactly which conditional independencies the graph implies. A path between \(X\) and \(Y\) is **blocked** by a set \(Z\) if it contains (i) a chain \(A \to B \to C\) or fork \(A \leftarrow B \to C\) with \(B \in Z\), or (ii) a **collider** \(A \to B \leftarrow C\) with \(B \notin Z\) and no descendant of \(B\) in \(Z\) [1]. Colliders are treacherous: conditioning on a collider (or its descendant) *opens* a path and induces spurious association — the source of **selection bias**.

> **Theorem (Backdoor criterion).** A set \(Z\) of covariates is sufficient for adjustment if (i) no element of \(Z\) is a descendant of \(X\), and (ii) \(Z\) blocks every path between \(X\) and \(Y\) that contains an arrow into \(X\) (every *backdoor* path). Then
>
> $$
> P(Y \mid \text{do}(X = x)) = \sum_z P(Y \mid X = x, Z = z)\,P(Z = z)
> $$
>
> — the *backdoor adjustment* formula [1].

The criterion explains why adjusting for confounders is correct but adjusting for colliders or mediators is not: the former blocks backdoor paths, the latter either opens new ones or destroys the effect of interest.

When no backdoor-satisfying set is observed — say an unmeasured confounder \(U\) links \(X\) and \(Y\) — the **frontdoor criterion** can still identify the effect through a mediator \(M\) transmitting all causal influence, provided \(M\)'s own backdoor paths are blockable [1]:

$$
P(Y \mid \text{do}(X = x)) = \sum_m P(M = m \mid X = x) \sum_{x'} P(Y \mid X = x', M = m)\,P(X = x').
$$

The frontdoor formula identifies a causal effect under *unmeasured confounding* using only observational data, by chaining two backdoor adjustments.

### 4.2 The Three Rules of the do-Calculus

The *do*-calculus transforms expressions containing the \(\text{do}\)-operator into ordinary conditional probabilities, using only graphical tests. Let \(G_{\bar{X}}\) denote \(G\) with all arrows into \(X\) deleted, \(G_{\underline{Z}}\) the graph with all arrows out of \(Z\) deleted, and \(\perp\!\!\!\perp_G\) denote *d*-separation in \(G\) [1]:

1. **Rule 1 (insertion/deletion of observations):** \(P(y \mid \text{do}(x), z, w) = P(y \mid \text{do}(x), w)\) if \((Y \perp\!\!\!\perp Z \mid X, W)_{G_{\bar{X}}}\).
2. **Rule 2 (action/observation exchange):** \(P(y \mid \text{do}(x), \text{do}(z), w) = P(y \mid \text{do}(x), z, w)\) if \((Y \perp\!\!\!\perp Z \mid X, W)_{G_{\bar{X}\underline{Z}}}\).
3. **Rule 3 (insertion/deletion of actions):** \(P(y \mid \text{do}(x), \text{do}(z), w) = P(y \mid \text{do}(x), w)\) if \((Y \perp\!\!\!\perp Z \mid X, W)_{G_{\bar{X}\overline{Z(W)}}}\), where \(Z(W)\) are the \(Z\)-nodes that are not ancestors of any \(W\)-node in \(G_{\bar{X}}\).

The backdoor and frontdoor formulae are both derivable from these rules. Crucially, Shpitser and Pearl proved the calculus **complete**: if an effect is identifiable from the DAG and the observational distribution, the rules find the identifying functional, and the ID algorithm decides identifiability in polynomial time [1]. Causal identification becomes a decidable algorithmic problem rather than an art of clever conditioning.

### 4.3 Propensity Scores, Inverse Probability Weighting, and Doubly Robust Estimation

Assume unconfoundedness \(Y(t) \perp\!\!\!\perp T \mid X\). Rosenbaum and Rubin showed the scalar propensity score \(e(X) = P(T = 1 \mid X)\) is a **balancing score**: \(T \perp\!\!\!\perp X \mid e(X)\), and unconfoundedness given \(X\) implies unconfoundedness given \(e(X)\) [2]. Matching or subclassifying on \(e(X)\) therefore removes all observed confounding.

The **inverse probability weighting** (IPW) estimator reweights by the inverse probability of received treatment:

$$
\hat{\tau}_{\text{IPW}} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \left(\frac{T_i Y_i}{\hat{e}(X_i)} - \frac{(1 - T_i)Y_i}{1 - \hat{e}(X_i)}\right).
$$

Under unconfoundedness and positivity, \(\hat{\tau}_{\text{IPW}} \xrightarrow{p} \text{ATE}\): IPW creates a *pseudo-population* in which treatment is unconfounded. Its weakness is variance — propensity scores near 0 or 1 produce enormous weights — and misspecification of \(\hat{e}\) biases the result [2][6].

The **doubly robust** (augmented IPW) estimator hedges by combining an outcome model \(\hat{\mu}_t(X) = \hat{\mathbb{E}}[Y \mid T = t, X]\) with the propensity model:

$$
\hat{\tau}_{\text{DR}} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \left[ \hat{\mu}_1(X_i) - \hat{\mu}_0(X_i) + \frac{T_i(Y_i - \hat{\mu}_1(X_i))}{\hat{e}(X_i)} - \frac{(1-T_i)(Y_i - \hat{\mu}_0(X_i))}{1 - \hat{e}(X_i)} \right].
$$

It is consistent if *either* the outcome model *or* the propensity model is correct — two chances to get it right — and attains the semiparametric efficiency bound when both are correct [5].

### 4.4 Double/Debiased Machine Learning and Neyman Orthogonality

Classical estimators assume low-dimensional parametric models for \(\mu_t\) and \(e\). With rich covariates, machine learning is tempting — but naive plug-in ML introduces **regularization bias**: shrinkage leaks into the causal parameter at first order, destroying \(\sqrt{n}\)-inference [5].

Double/debiased machine learning solves this with two ingredients. First, a **Neyman-orthogonal score** \(\psi(W; \theta, \eta)\) whose Gateaux derivative with respect to the nuisance functions \(\eta = (\mu, e)\) vanishes at the truth:

$$
\partial_{\eta}\,\mathbb{E}[\psi(W; \theta_0, \eta)]\big|_{\eta = \eta_0} = 0.
$$

Orthogonality makes the estimator *first-order insensitive* to nuisance error, so \(\eta\) may converge at the slow \(o(n^{-1/4})\) rates typical of ML while \(\theta\) still attains \(\sqrt{n}\)-consistency and asymptotic normality [5]. The doubly robust score of §4.3 is exactly such an orthogonal score for the ATE. Second, **cross-fitting**: split the sample into \(K\) folds, estimate nuisances on \(K-1\) folds, evaluate the score on the held-out fold, and average. This removes overfitting bias and eliminates the classical Donsker entropy conditions that ML function classes violate [5].

In practice: fit \(\hat{\mu}_t\) and \(\hat{e}\) with any ML toolbox, cross-fit, plug into the orthogonal score, and report Wald intervals. Chernozhukov et al. prove uniform \(\sqrt{n}\)-inference under mild rate conditions, with extensions to partially linear models, IV settings, and heterogeneous effects [5].

```python
# Cross-fitted orthogonal (AIPW) score for the ATE
import numpy as np
from sklearn.ensemble import RandomForestRegressor, RandomForestClassifier
from sklearn.model_selection import KFold

def dml_ate(X, T, Y, K=5, seed=0):
    n = len(Y)
    psi = np.zeros(n)
    for train, test in KFold(K, shuffle=True, random_state=seed).split(X):
        mu1 = RandomForestRegressor(400).fit(X[train][T[train]==1], Y[train][T[train]==1])
        mu0 = RandomForestRegressor(400).fit(X[train][T[train]==0], Y[train][T[train]==0])
        e   = RandomForestClassifier(400).fit(X[train], T[train]).predict_proba(X[test])[:,1]
        e   = np.clip(e, 1e-3, 1-1e-3)
        m1, m0 = mu1.predict(X[test]), mu0.predict(X[test])
        t, y = T[test], Y[test]
        psi[test] = (m1 - m0
                     + t*(y - m1)/e
                     - (1-t)*(y - m0)/(1-e))
    theta = psi.mean()
    se = psi.std(ddof=1) / np.sqrt(n)
    return theta, theta - 1.96*se, theta + 1.96*se
```

### 4.5 Instrumental Variables, Mediation, and Sensitivity Analysis

When unconfoundedness fails — unmeasured confounders exist — identification requires different structure. An **instrumental variable** \(Z\) satisfies (i) *relevance*: \(Z\) affects \(T\); (ii) *exclusion*: \(Z\) affects the outcome only through \(T\); (iii) *independence*: \(Z\) is as-if randomly assigned; and (iv) *monotonicity*: no defiers [4]. Angrist, Imbens, and Rubin showed the classical Wald/IV estimand then identifies the **LATE** — the average effect for *compliers*, units whose treatment status follows the instrument:

$$
\tau_{\text{LATE}} = \frac{\mathbb{E}[Y \mid Z=1] - \mathbb{E}[Y \mid Z=0]}{\mathbb{E}[T \mid Z=1] - \mathbb{E}[T \mid Z=0]}.
$$

LATE is a genuine average of unit-level causal effects, but only for an unidentifiable subpopulation — a limitation, not a flaw: it is what the instrument can teach us, and nothing more [4]. Weak instruments (small denominator) inflate variance and amplify any exclusion-violation bias, so first-stage F-statistics above conventional thresholds are standard diagnostics.

**Mediation** decomposes a total effect into paths. The *controlled direct effect* \(\mathbb{E}[Y(t, m) - Y(t', m)]\) fixes the mediator; *natural* direct and indirect effects let the mediator respond naturally to treatment, requiring cross-world counterfactuals \(Y(t, M(t'))\) and stronger assumptions (no treatment-induced mediator–outcome confounding). Pearl's analysis shows that naively conditioning on a mediator induces collider bias, while proper mediation formulae follow from the *do*-calculus on the expanded graph [1].

Because unconfoundedness is **untestable**, **sensitivity analysis** quantifies robustness: Rosenbaum's sensitivity model bounds how strongly an unmeasured confounder must distort treatment assignment (the parameter \(\Gamma\)) to overturn a finding; modern extensions (E-values, omitted-variable-bias frameworks) translate \(\Gamma\) into confounding strength on both treatment and outcome sides [6]. A result surviving large \(\Gamma\) is credible; one collapsing at \(\Gamma \approx 1.1\) is fragile.

```haskell
-- Potential outcomes as a total function: only one branch
-- is ever observed per unit (the fundamental problem).
data Treatment = Treated | Control deriving (Eq, Show)

newtype PotentialOutcome = PO { runPO :: Treatment -> Double }

observed :: PotentialOutcome -> Treatment -> Double
observed po t = runPO po t          -- consistency: Y = Y(T)

individualEffect :: PotentialOutcome -> Double
individualEffect po = runPO po Treated - runPO po Control

ate :: [PotentialOutcome] -> Double
ate pos = sum (map individualEffect pos) / fromIntegral (length pos)
```

## 5 Empirical Results and Formal Arguments

**Identification.** Under a known DAG \(G\), the *do*-calculus is sound and complete for identifying \(P(Y \mid \text{do}(X))\) from \(P(\mathbf{V})\) [1]. With unconfoundedness, positivity, and consistency, the g-formula, IPW, and doubly robust functionals all identify the ATE; with a valid instrument, the Wald ratio identifies LATE [4][6].

**Estimation rates.** When nuisances are estimated parametrically at \(\sqrt{n}\) rates, IPW and doubly robust estimators are \(\sqrt{n}\)-consistent and asymptotically normal. The DML contribution is that nuisance rates as slow as \(o(n^{-1/4})\) — attainable by many ML methods — still yield \(\sqrt{n}\)-inference for the target parameter [5]. Empirical studies confirm the theory: in the well-known 401(k) eligibility application, DML with flexible learners produces stable ATE estimates with honest confidence intervals where naive ML plug-in estimates are severely biased [5].

**Simulation evidence.** In a canonical design — \(X \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I_{20})\), propensity \(\text{logit}^{-1}(X_1 + X_2^2 - X_3 X_4)\), outcome \(Y = \tau T + X_1^2 + \sin(X_5) + \varepsilon\), true \(\tau = 1\) — DML over 1,000 Monte Carlo replications at \(n = 2{,}000\) is unbiased (mean bias \(< 0.01\)) with empirical coverage of the nominal 95% interval near 94–96%. A naive plug-in \(\hat{\mu}_1 - \hat{\mu}_0\) inflates bias several-fold and collapses coverage: the ML step alone is insufficient [5].

**Comparative performance.** The trade-offs across estimators are consistent [2][5][6]:

| Estimator | Needs | Robust to | Fails when |
|---|---|---|---|
| g-formula / outcome regression | Correct \(\mu_t(X)\) | Propensity misspecification | Outcome model wrong |
| IPW | Correct \(e(X)\) + positivity | Outcome misspecification | Extreme weights, \(e\) wrong |
| Doubly robust / DML | Either model right | Single-model misspecification | Both wrong, positivity fails |
| IV (2SLS) | Valid instrument | Unmeasured confounding | Weak/exclusion-violating IV |
| Frontdoor | Mediator structure | Unmeasured \(X\!\to\!Y\) confounding | Mediator assumptions fail |

No estimator dominates; each trades one untestable assumption for another.

## 6 Limitations

The edifice rests on strong, partly untestable assumptions, frequently violated in practice.

- **Unconfoundedness is untestable.** By definition, no observed-data test can detect an unmeasured confounder; sensitivity analysis bounds the damage but cannot remove it [6]. Every observational causal claim is therefore conditional on a substantive scientific judgment.
- **The DAG may be wrong.** The *do*-calculus is complete *given the graph*, but the graph encodes causal knowledge data alone cannot verify. Discovery algorithms (PC, FCI, GES) recover only Markov equivalence classes [1].
- **Positivity and overlap.** In high dimensions, some covariate regions inevitably lack both treated and control units; trimming restores positivity only by changing the estimand to a subpopulation [2][6].
- **Interference (SUTVA violations).** In networks and marketplaces, one unit's treatment affects another's outcome; standard estimators are then biased. Interference-aware methods (cluster randomization, exposure mappings) are an active frontier [3][6].
- **Weak and invalid instruments.** A weak first stage magnifies bias from even tiny exclusion violations; monotonicity can fail with heterogeneous behavioral responses [4].
- **Computational costs.** DML requires careful cross-fitting and honest ML tuning, and flexible nuisance estimation demands large samples [5].
- **Transportability.** A perfectly identified ATE in one population need not transport to another with different covariate distributions — external validity is a separate causal problem [1][6].

## 7 Conclusion

Causal inference from observational data is possible, but only through the disciplined combination of *structure* and *statistics*. Pearl's structural framework supplies the structure: causal graphs make assumptions transparent, the *do*-operator formalizes intervention, and the *do*-calculus decides identification algorithmically [1]. Rubin's potential outcomes framework supplies the statistical discipline: explicit counterfactual estimands, the unconfoundedness–positivity–consistency trinity, and a hierarchy of estimators from propensity scores [2][3] to doubly robust and double machine learning methods [5], with instrumental variables [4] and sensitivity analysis [6] for cases where unconfoundedness cannot be defended.

The unity runs deeper than a shared toolbox: every backdoor adjustment is a g-formula, every IPW estimator a reweighting toward the interventional distribution, every instrument an exploitation of graph structure. The practitioner's duty is therefore twofold: *state the causal assumptions in the open* — as a DAG, as potential-outcome independencies, or both — and *choose estimators whose robustness matches the assumptions you can defend*. Association will always be cheap; causation, done honestly, remains expensive. That price — explicit assumptions, sensitivity analysis, and the humility to report what the data cannot identify — is what separates causal inference from curve fitting.
