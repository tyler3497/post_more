# Differential Privacy: The Laplace Mechanism, Composition Theorems, and Renyi Privacy Accounting

## Abstract

Differential privacy has become the gold standard for quantifying information leakage in data analysis, replacing heuristic anonymization with a mathematically rigorous, adversary-agnostic guarantee. This post develops the theory from first principles: the definition of pure and approximate differential privacy over neighboring databases, the sensitivity calculus that underlies the Laplace mechanism, and the exact noise calibration that converts any real-valued query into a private release. We then study how privacy degrades under repeated analysis, progressing from basic composition through the advanced composition theorem to the optimal composition characterization and its computational intractability. The final arc introduces Rényi differential privacy as an analytical divergence-based relaxation, derives the tight Rényi bound for the Gaussian mechanism, and explains how the moments accountant tracks higher moments of the privacy loss to enable private deep learning at practical utility. Worked proofs, mechanism comparisons, and calibration recipes accompany each stage.

## 1 Introduction

Before 2006, statistical disclosure limitation was an *art*: suppression, swapping, aggregation, and k-anonymity, each defined by what it obscured rather than what it guaranteed. The trouble, documented by Dinur and Nissim and others, was that syntactic masking collapses under auxiliary information — an adversary who knows all but one record can often invert aggregates exactly [1].

Differential privacy inverts the framing: instead of masking what data *looks like*, it bounds what an adversary can *infer*, regardless of background knowledge or auxiliary datasets [2]. The mechanism is a randomized algorithm M; the guarantee is a statement about the *distributions* M(D) and M(D') induced on any pair of neighboring databases D and D', databases differing in a single individual's record. When those two distributions are nearly indistinguishable, the output reveals essentially nothing about any one participant, because the analysis would have produced nearly the same output whether that participant's data had been included or not.

> **Definition:** A randomized mechanism M satisfies (ε, δ)-differential privacy if, for every pair of neighboring databases D, D' and every measurable event S in the output space, Pr[M(D) ∈ S] ≤ exp(ε) · Pr[M(D') ∈ S] + δ. The case δ = 0 is called *pure* or ε-differential privacy.

**Figure 1.** Two neighboring databases differing in one record are fed through the randomized mechanism M; differential privacy requires the two output distributions to be nearly indistinguishable, with likelihood ratios bounded by exp(ε).

This post follows that arc in order: definition and mechanism, then composition, then Rényi accounting — each with proofs and calibration recipes.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Neighboring databases and the privacy loss random variable

Two adjacency conventions dominate:

- **Bounded (replace-one) adjacency:** D' is obtained from D by replacing one record. Database size is fixed.
- **Unbounded (add/remove-one) adjacency:** D' is obtained from D by adding or removing one record. Database size varies.

Most accounting results hold under either convention with at most a factor-of-two adjustment in sensitivity; we work with bounded adjacency unless stated otherwise.

For a fixed neighboring pair, the **privacy loss** at output o is L(o) = ln(Pr[M(D) = o] / Pr[M(D') = o]). Pure ε-DP bounds |L| by ε always; approximate (ε, δ)-DP allows the bound to fail with probability δ [2]. The privacy loss random variable is the central object of modern accounting: composition adds independent privacy losses, and Rényi accounting bounds their moment-generating behavior.

### 2.2 Sensitivity: how much one record can matter

> **Definition:** The ℓ1-sensitivity of a function f mapping databases to real vectors is Δf = max over neighboring D, D' of ‖f(D) − f(D')‖₁.

For a counting query, Δf = 1; for the sample mean of n bounded values, Δf = 1/n. Sensitivity is *data-independent* worst-case, computed over all neighboring pairs rather than the realized database.

The ℓ2-sensitivity, defined with the Euclidean norm, plays the analogous role for the Gaussian mechanism. Note that ℓ1-sensitivity of a scalar query equals its absolute difference — a fact that makes the Laplace calibration exact rather than conservative [1].

### 2.3 Post-processing and the privacy budget

Two structural properties make differential privacy usable as an engineering discipline [2]:

1. **Post-processing immunity.** If M is (ε, δ)-DP, then g ∘ M is (ε, δ)-DP for *any* randomized function g that does not touch the private data. Clipping, rounding, thresholding, and training downstream models on private outputs all come free.
2. **Composition.** Releasing M₁(D), M₂(D), …, Mₖ(D) degrades privacy in a quantifiable way. The total cost is tracked against a **privacy budget** (ε_total, δ_total): an organization fixes an acceptable total and spends it across analyses.

These two properties together explain why the field converged on DP rather than alternatives: the definition is closed under the two operations every real pipeline needs.

## 3 Methodology

Our method is the standard theoretical one: *define, calibrate, compose, convert*. Each subsection below follows that arc for one pillar of the theory.

### 3.1 Calibrating the Laplace mechanism

Given a function f with ℓ1-sensitivity Δf, the Laplace mechanism releases

M(D) = f(D) + (Y₁, …, Y_d),   each Yᵢ drawn independently from Lap(Δf / ε)

where Lap(b) denotes the Laplace distribution with density (1/2b)·exp(−|x|/b). The proof that this satisfies ε-DP is a short density-ratio computation (see §5), and the mechanism is tight: no pure-DP mechanism can add less noise in general [1].

```python
import numpy as np

def laplace_mechanism(true_answer: float, sensitivity: float, epsilon: float) -> float:
    """Release a real-valued query answer under pure epsilon-DP."""
    scale = sensitivity / epsilon
    noise = np.random.laplace(loc=0.0, scale=scale)
    return true_answer + noise

def calibrate_for_accuracy(sensitivity: float, epsilon: float, beta: float) -> float:
    """Largest error magnitude alpha such that |noise| <= alpha with prob >= 1 - beta."""
    b = sensitivity / epsilon
    return b * np.log(1.0 / beta)
```

Each coordinate's noise has standard deviation √2·Δf/ε, and the tail bound Pr[|Y| > t] = exp(−tε/Δf) turns an accuracy target directly into an ε choice — the Laplace mechanism's edge over Gaussian alternatives in the pure-DP regime.

**Figure 2.** The Laplace density (sharp peak, exponential tails) versus the Gaussian density (rounded peak, faster-decaying tails). The Laplace mechanism calibrates its scale to ℓ₁ sensitivity for pure ε-DP; the Gaussian mechanism trades the hard ε cap for (ε, δ)-DP with noise calibrated to ℓ₂ sensitivity.

### 3.2 The Gaussian mechanism and why it needs δ

Adding Gaussian noise N(0, σ²) to a query with ℓ₂-sensitivity Δ₂ yields (ε, δ)-DP whenever σ ≥ Δ₂·√(2 ln(1.25/δ))/ε [2] — the δ is unavoidable, since Gaussian tails are too heavy for a hard ε cap. The Rényi analysis of §4.4 improves this calibration substantially.

| Mechanism | Noise distribution | Calibration | Guarantee | Sensitivity norm |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Laplace | Lap(Δ₁/ε) | scale = Δ₁/ε | pure ε-DP | ℓ₁ |
| Gaussian | N(0, σ²), σ ≥ Δ₂√(2 ln(1.25/δ))/ε | classical | (ε, δ)-DP | ℓ₂ |
| Gaussian (Rényi) | N(0, σ²) | Rényi curve α/(2σ²) | (ε, δ)-DP via conversion | ℓ₂ |
| Exponential | ∝ exp(ε·u(D,r)/2Δu) | score sensitivity Δu | pure ε-DP | utility-based |
| Randomized response | flip with prob. 1/(1+e^ε) | truth prob. e^ε/(1+e^ε) | pure ε-DP | n/a (local) |

### 3.3 Tracking composition: the accountant's view

An **accountant** is a stateful object that accumulates privacy cost across a sequence of mechanism invocations and reports the total (ε, δ) spent. The simplest accountant sums epsilons (basic composition); the moments accountant instead accumulates the log-moment-generating function of each step's privacy loss and optimizes over Rényi orders at the end — a strictly tighter accounting that was decisive for private deep learning [3].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Laplace Mechanism and the Sensitivity Calculus

The proof is a density-ratio computation via the triangle inequality (full derivation in §5). Three refinements matter in practice.

*Vector queries.* Adding independent Lap(Δ₁/ε) noise per coordinate spends ε once, not per coordinate — the ℓ₁ sensitivity already accounts for the whole vector. A histogram with k bins therefore costs the same ε as a single count, making Laplace the default for private contingency tables.

*The staircase refinement.* For integer-valued queries, a two-sided geometric (discrete Laplace) distribution achieves the same ε with slightly less variance and returns integers directly.

### 4.2 Basic and Advanced Composition

Suppose M₁, …, Mₖ are each (ε, δ)-DP, possibly chosen *adaptively* (each mechanism may depend on earlier outputs). Then:

- **Basic composition:** the tuple (M₁(D), …, Mₖ(D)) is (kε, kδ)-DP [2].
- **Advanced composition:** for any δ' > 0, the tuple is (ε', kδ + δ')-DP with ε' = √(2k ln(1/δ'))·ε + kε(e^ε − 1) [2].

The second term in the advanced bound is negligible for small ε, so the headline is the square-root scaling: k mechanisms cost roughly √k·ε rather than k·ε. The price is a larger δ and the requirement that all εᵢ be equal (heterogeneous extensions exist but are messier).

> **Theorem (Advanced composition, Dwork–Rothblum–Vadhan):** The k-fold adaptive composition of (ε, δ)-DP mechanisms is (ε√(2k ln(1/δ')) + kε(e^ε − 1), kδ + δ')-DP for any δ' > 0.

The proof's key move is to bound the *expected* privacy loss rather than the worst case: each mechanism's loss has mean at most roughly ε(e^ε − 1)/2, and a martingale concentration argument (Azuma's inequality over the adaptive transcript) lifts the expectation to a high-probability bound. This was the first hint that the privacy loss random variable, rather than worst-case likelihood ratios, was the right object of study — the conceptual seed of concentrated and Rényi DP [2][8].

### 4.3 Optimal Composition and Its Computational Frontier

Advanced composition is not the end of the story. Kairouz, Oh, and Viswanath characterized the *exact* optimal composition bound for k-fold (ε, δ)-DP composition: a closed-form expression, computable in polynomial time, that is tight — no better universal bound exists, and a family of mechanisms attains it [4].

The exact formula is a binomial-tail sum over the hypothesis-testing tradeoff curve — the worst-case privacy loss distribution over all (ε, δ)-DP mechanisms. For homogeneous composition it strictly improves on advanced composition.

Then Murtagh and Vadhan showed the catch: for *heterogeneous* composition — mechanisms with different (εᵢ, δᵢ) — computing the optimal bound exactly is **#P-complete** [5]. The optimal accountant for arbitrary mechanism sequences is therefore computationally intractable in the worst case, though an FPTAS-style approximation reaches arbitrary accuracy in polynomial time via a knapsack-like dynamic program. Production systems overwhelmingly use Rényi/moments accounting — additive, fast, and nearly tight where it matters — rather than the provably optimal but heavyweight formulas.

| Composition rule | Total ε (k mechanisms) | Requirements | Tightness |
|---|---|---|---|
| Basic | kε | none | loose |
| Advanced | ≈ ε√(2k ln(1/δ')) | homogeneous ε | loose by constants |
| Optimal (homogeneous) | exact binomial-tail formula | homogeneous (ε, δ) | tight |
| Optimal (heterogeneous) | #P-complete to compute exactly | arbitrary (εᵢ, δᵢ) | tight but intractable |
| RDP / moments | additive in Rényi ε(α), then convert | Rényi orders | near-tight for Gaussian |

The lesson for practitioners: exactness and tractability trade off. Production systems (differential-privacy libraries, private ML pipelines) overwhelmingly use Rényi/moments accounting — additive, fast, and nearly tight where it matters — rather than the provably optimal but heavyweight composition formulas.

### 4.4 Rényi Divergence as a Privacy Meter

Rényi differential privacy replaces the worst-case likelihood-ratio bound with a bound on the **Rényi divergence** of order α between the output distributions [6]:

> **Definition:** M satisfies (α, ε)-RDP for α > 1 if, for all neighboring D, D', D_α(M(D) ‖ M(D')) ≤ ε, where D_α(P‖Q) = (1/(α−1)) · ln E_{x∼Q}[(P(x)/Q(x))^α].

Three properties make RDP the right relaxation:

1. **Adaptive composition is additive.** If M₁ is (α, ε₁)-RDP and M₂ is (α, ε₂)-RDP (adaptively chosen), the pair is (α, ε₁ + ε₂)-RDP — the clean additivity of pure DP, recovered for a relaxation.
2. **Post-processing holds**, exactly as for standard DP.
3. **Conversion to (ε, δ)-DP is one line:** (α, ε)-RDP implies (ε + ln(1/δ)/(α−1), δ)-DP for any δ in the open unit interval, with the analyst optimizing over α *after* accounting.

The killer application is the Gaussian mechanism. Its Rényi curve has the closed form ε(α) = α/(2σ²) for sensitivity-1 queries [6][7], and converting at the optimal α yields a strictly tighter (ε, δ) guarantee than the classical calibration at typical parameters.

```haskell
-- Renyi orders compose additively: the accountant is a monoid
type RenyiOrder = Double
type RenyiEps  = Double

-- (alpha, eps) pairs: combining two steps adds epsilons at the same order
composeRDP :: (RenyiOrder, RenyiEps) -> (RenyiOrder, RenyiEps) -> (RenyiOrder, RenyiEps)
composeRDP (a, e1) (_, e2) = (a, e1 + e2)

-- convert (alpha, eps)-RDP to (eps', delta)-DP
toApproxDP :: (RenyiOrder, RenyiEps) -> Double -> (Double, Double)
toApproxDP (alpha, eps) delta = (eps + log (1 / delta) / (alpha - 1), delta)
```

### 4.5 The Moments Accountant and Private Deep Learning

Abadi et al. turned Rényi-style accounting into the engine of **DP-SGD**, the first practical algorithm for training deep networks with formal privacy [3]. The recipe has three ingredients:

1. **Per-example gradient clipping.** Each example's gradient is clipped to ℓ₂ norm at most C, bounding the batch gradient sum's sensitivity at C.
2. **Noise addition.** Gaussian noise with standard deviation σC is added to the summed clipped gradients before the parameter update.
3. **Moments accounting.** Each step's Rényi curve is accumulated additively, and the final (ε, δ) comes from optimizing the conversion over α.

The subtlety is **subsampling amplification**: each step touches only a random minibatch (sampling ratio q), and the privacy cost of a subsampled mechanism scales roughly as q² rather than q in the Rényi analysis [6][7]. This quadratic amplification is what makes thousands of DP-SGD steps affordable: without it, the budget would be exhausted long before convergence. Subsequent work tightened the subsampled Rényi bounds further with analytical (closed-form) accountants that remove numerical integration from the loop [6].

## 5 Proofs

We sketch the four load-bearing proofs of the post. Each is complete in its essential steps; routine measure-theoretic details are omitted.

**Proof 1 — The Laplace mechanism is ε-DP [1].** Fix neighboring D, D' and consider a single coordinate; the vector case follows by multiplying density ratios and applying the ℓ₁ triangle inequality. Let b = Δf/ε. The density ratio at output z is

p(z | D) / p(z | D') = exp(−|f(D) − z|/b) / exp(−|f(D') − z|/b) = exp((|f(D') − z| − |f(D) − z|)/b).

By the reverse triangle inequality, the exponent's absolute value is at most |f(D) − f(D')|/b ≤ Δf/b = ε, so the ratio is at most exp(ε). Integrating over any event S gives Pr[M(D) ∈ S] ≤ e^ε Pr[M(D') ∈ S]. ∎

**Proof 2 — Basic composition [2].** Let M₁ be (ε₁, δ₁)-DP and M₂, possibly chosen after seeing M₁'s output, be (ε₂, δ₂)-DP. For any event S over output pairs, the law of total probability gives Pr[(M₁, M₂) ∈ S] = E_y[Pr[M₂(y) ∈ S_y]]; applying M₂'s guarantee inside the expectation and M₁'s outside yields (ε₁ + ε₂, δ₁ + δ₂)-DP. Induction extends to k mechanisms, and adaptivity comes free because conditioning is already built in. ∎

**Proof 3 — Gaussian mechanism Rényi bound [6].** For sensitivity-1 queries with noise N(0, σ²), the order-α Rényi divergence between N(μ, σ²) and N(μ', σ²) with |μ − μ'| ≤ 1 is α|μ−μ'|²/(2σ²) ≤ α/(2σ²) — a standard Gaussian moment-generating computation. One step is thus (α, α/(2σ²))-RDP, and k adaptive steps compose to (α, kα/(2σ²))-RDP by additivity. ∎

**Proof 4 — RDP-to-DP conversion [6].** Suppose D_α(P‖Q) ≤ ε. For any event S, Hölder's inequality (or a Chernoff bound on the privacy loss) gives P(S) ≤ exp(ε + ln(1/δ)/(α−1))·Q(S) + δ. Concretely: the Rényi bound controls the moment-generating function of the privacy loss L, and Markov's inequality applied to exp((α−1)L) bounds the tail Pr[L > ε'] ≤ exp((α−1)(ε − ε')). Setting the right side to δ and solving yields ε' = ε + ln(1/δ)/(α−1). ∎

Together, Proofs 3 and 4 give the modern Gaussian calibration: accumulate (α, kα/(2σ²)) over k steps, then minimize ε(α) = kα/(2σ²) + ln(1/δ)/(α−1) over α > 1 — a one-line derivation of the square-root scaling of advanced composition, with better constants.

## 6 Limitations

Differential privacy's rigor is also its straitjacket. Five limitations deserve emphasis.

1. **The δ loophole is real.** Approximate DP permits total privacy failure with probability δ — a mechanism publishing the whole database with probability δ is (0, δ)-DP. Keeping δ cryptographically small (well below one over the record count) is convention, not theorem, and deployed systems have not always honored it.

2. **Sensitivity can be brutal.** For high-global-sensitivity queries like the median or max, worst-case noise swamps the signal; smooth-sensitivity and propose-test-release techniques help but add complexity and failure modes [2].

3. **Composition is still lossy in practice.** Even Rényi accounting is an upper bound, and the gap between the accountant's ε and the *actual* privacy loss can be large. Empirical auditing via membership-inference attacks routinely finds realized leakage far below the proven ε — the guarantee is safe but potentially vacuous as a predictor of real risk.

4. **The threat model is narrow.** DP bounds inference about single records, not population facts participants might prefer hidden ("smoking causes cancer" is learnable by design). It also trusts the curator — the local model pays steep utility to remove that assumption.

5. **Budgets don't compose across analysts.** The composition theorems assume a single accountant sees every query. In practice, multiple teams query overlapping data through different interfaces with no global budget. Privacy odometers and filters address adaptive budget *management* [8], but cross-organizational accounting remains an open deployment problem.

## 7 Conclusion

Three ideas carry the theory. The **Laplace mechanism** converts worst-case sensitivity into a pure, tight guarantee via calibrated noise [1]. **Composition theorems** make privacy a fungible budget: basic sums, advanced takes square roots, optimal is exact but #P-hard when heterogeneous — all driven by the privacy loss random variable [2][4][5]. **Rényi accounting** recovers additive composition with tight Gaussian analysis and a one-line conversion to (ε, δ)-DP — the analytical core of the moments accountant behind private deep learning [3][6][7][8].

The research frontier has moved outward: tighter *numerical* accountants that compute the exact privacy loss distribution of subsampled mechanisms, *auditing* methods that empirically lower-bound leakage, and *privacy filters and odometers* that manage budgets adaptively at runtime. But the conceptual core is stable, and it is worth stating plainly: differential privacy succeeded because its definition is closed under post-processing and composition — the two operations every real data pipeline performs. Any future privacy definition that hopes to displace it will need to earn those two closures first.
