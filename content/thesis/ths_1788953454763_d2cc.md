# Seismic Full-Waveform Inversion and Adjoint Tomography of Earth's Interior: From Wave Equations to Mantle Structure

## Abstract

Seismic full-waveform inversion (FWI) and adjoint tomography constitute the most complete framework for imaging Earth's interior, fitting entire seismograms rather than isolated travel times or dispersion curves. This thesis develops the mathematical and computational foundations of the method: the elastic and acoustic wave equations governing seismic propagation; the nonlinear least-squares misfit functional at the heart of FWI; the adjoint-state method that delivers exact Fréchet gradients at the cost of a single extra simulation; finite-frequency sensitivity kernels with their characteristic banana–doughnut geometry; and the multiscale frequency-continuation strategy that defeats cycle-skipping, the principal pathology of waveform fitting. We analyze regularization, Hessian preconditioning, and uncertainty quantification; benchmark empirical convergence on synthetic and real datasets; and review landmark applications: mantle plume imaging beneath hotspots, slab morphology and mineralogy at convergent margins, and cylindrical anisotropy of the solid inner core. We conclude with limitations — null spaces, trade-offs between structure and attenuation, and computational cost — and identify open frontiers including Bayesian uncertainty, machine-learned surrogates, and planetary seismology [1][2][3][4][5].

## 1. Introduction

For over a century, seismology has inverted earthquake observations for Earth's interior structure. Classical travel-time tomography [6] uses only ray-theoretic arrival times, discarding the rich information encoded in waveform amplitudes, scattered arrivals, dispersion, and coda. **Full-waveform inversion (FWI)**, introduced by Tarantola [7], and elevated to global scales through *adjoint tomography* by Tromp, Tape, and Liu [1], exploits the **entire seismogram**.

The central difficulty is scale. A modern adjoint-tomography iteration on a 3-D Earth model requires solving the elastic wave equation on an unstructured mesh with hundreds of millions of degrees of freedom — *twice* per earthquake: once forward, once adjoint. That the method works at all is a triumph of three ideas:

1. **The adjoint-state method** computes the exact gradient of a scalar misfit with respect to millions of model parameters at the cost of one additional wave simulation, independent of the number of parameters [1][2].
2. **Finite-frequency sensitivity kernels** replace infinitesimal rays with volumetric "banana–doughnut" sensitivity regions derived from the interaction of forward and adjoint wavefields, correctly capturing wavefront healing and diffraction [1][4][8].
3. **Multiscale frequency continuation** (Bunks et al. [9]) begins the inversion at long periods where cycle-skipping is avoided, and progressively admits higher frequencies, thereby convexifying a violently nonconvex objective.

> **Theorem: (Adjoint gradient identity).** Let **u(m)** solve the wave equation **L(m)u = f** and let the misfit χ(**m**) depend on **u** only through receiver functionals. Then the Fréchet gradient ∇χ exists and equals the time-convolution of the forward field **u** with the adjoint field **u**†, where **u**† solves **L**(m)†**u**† = −∂χ/∂**u** driven by the data residuals as virtual sources. *One adjoint solve suffices for arbitrarily many parameters.*

This thesis is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews the wave physics and classical tomography. Section 3 derives the misfit functional, the adjoint gradient, and kernel formalism. Section 4 deep-dives into banana–doughnut kernels, cycle-skipping and multiscale strategies, regularization and Hessian structure, and source–structure trade-offs. Section 5 examines empirical convergence and theoretical guarantees. Section 6 states limitations honestly; Section 7 concludes.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 The elastic and acoustic wave equations

Seismic wave propagation in a heterogeneous, anisotropic, attenuating Earth is governed by the **elastic wave equation**. In the frequency domain and the absence of body forces:

```
ρ(x) ω² s(x,ω) + ∇·[ c(x) : ∇s(x,ω) ] = 0
```

where **s** is the displacement vector, ρ the density, and **c** the fourth-order elastic stiffness tensor with up to 21 independent components in fully anisotropic media [1]. In the time domain with a moment-tensor source:

```
ρ ∂²s/∂t² − ∇·σ = f,    σ = c : ε,    ε = ½(∇s + (∇s)ᵀ)
```

For many regional and exploration applications the **acoustic (scalar) approximation** suffices:

```
(1/κ) ∂²p/∂t² − ∇·( (1/ρ) ∇p ) = f
```

with pressure *p* and bulk modulus κ. Tarantola's original FWI formulation [7] was developed precisely in this acoustic setting.

Modern global simulations typically employ the **spectral-element method (SEM)** on cubed-sphere meshes, combining the geometric flexibility of finite elements with spectral convergence [2][3]. A single 3-D simulation at periods above ~10 s can involve 10⁸–10⁹ degrees of freedom and thousands of time steps — motivating the adjoint formulation that keeps gradient computation tractable.

### 2.2 Classical travel-time tomography and its limits

Ray-theoretic tomography parametrizes slowness along infinitesimal geometric ray paths Γ, via δT = ∫_Γ δs(x) dl with δs = δ(1/v). Its limitations are severe [4][6]:

- **Ray theory breaks down** when velocity anomalies are comparable to the Fresnel-zone width; finite-frequency effects (diffraction, wavefront healing) are unaccounted for.
- **Amplitudes, attenuation, anisotropy, and scattering** are invisible to first-arrival picks.
- **The Fresnel-volume paradox**: measured travel-time delays are insensitive to structure exactly on the geometric ray — the sensitivity vanishes on the ray itself, the origin of the "doughnut hole" [8].

Finite-frequency tomography (Dahlen, Hung & Nolet [8]) replaced rays with volumetric kernels but still linearizes around a 1-D reference model. **FWI removes the linearization entirely**, iterating toward models that explain complete waveforms [1][5].

### 2.3 The misfit functional

Let **m(x)** denote the model (e.g., shear velocity *v_s*, compressional velocity *v_p*, density ρ, attenuation *Q*⁻¹) and **d**(obs) the observed seismograms. The synthetic seismograms **s(m)** result from the forward operator. The canonical FWI objective is the least-squares waveform misfit [7][9]:

```
χ(m) = ½ Σ_{r=1}^{Nr} Σ_{s=1}^{Ns} ∫_0^T ‖ d_{rs}(t) − s_{rs}(t; m) ‖² dt
```

Variants include cross-correlation travel-time and amplitude misfits [1], envelope misfits, and optimal-transport (Wasserstein) misfits that convexify the objective [10]. The inverse problem is:

> Find **m*** minimizing χ(**m**) subject to **L(m)u = f** for every source — the PDE constraint the adjoint method handles elegantly [1][2].

## 3. Methodology

### 3.1 The adjoint-state method

The naïve gradient of χ with respect to *M* model parameters costs *M* forward simulations — impossible when *M* ~ 10⁶. The adjoint-state method (Lions; formalized for seismology by Tromp, Tape & Liu [1] and Liu & Tromp [2]) reduces this to **one forward plus one adjoint simulation per source**.

**Derivation sketch.** Form the Lagrangian with multiplier **λ**:

```
ℒ(m, u, λ) = χ(m) + ⟨ λ, L(m)u − f ⟩
```

Taking variations and requiring stationarity in **u** yields the *adjoint equation*:

```
L(m)† λ = −∂χ/∂u
```

where the adjoint source is the data residual injected at the receivers, back-propagated in time [1][2]. The gradient then follows from the partial derivative:

```
δχ = ⟨ λ, (∂L/∂m) u ⟩ δm
```

In the acoustic case this produces the celebrated cross-correlation imaging condition:

```
K(x) = −∫_0^T u(x,t) · λ(x,T−t) dt
```

— the Fréchet kernel at **x** is the zero-lag cross-correlation of the forward and adjoint fields. The gradient is thus an *image* formed by the interaction of two wavefields [1].

> **Theorem: (Adjoint-state gradient).** If **L** is the (self-adjoint, in the elastic case with appropriate boundary terms) wave operator, the misfit gradient is obtained from exactly two wave simulations per source — one forward, one adjoint — regardless of parameter count. *Proof sketch:* stationarity of ℒ in **u** forces **λ** to satisfy the adjoint equation with residual sources; substitution into δℒ eliminates δ**u** entirely, leaving δχ expressed through **u** and **λ** alone.

For a model discretized into 10⁶ voxels, finite differences would need 10⁸ simulations; the adjoint method needs ~200 — a ratio that makes adjoint tomography feasible [1][5].

### 3.2 Fréchet kernels and sensitivity

The gradient decomposes into **event kernels** *K*α summed over sources, each a volumetric function giving the sensitivity of the misfit to each model parameter at every point in space [1][2]:

```
δχ = Σ_α ∫_Ω [ K_{κ,α} δlnκ + K_{μ,α} δlnμ + K_{ρ,α} δlnρ ] d³x
```

Parameter cross-talk is the bane of multiparameter FWI: *v_p*, *v_s*, ρ, and *Q* produce overlapping kernel patterns, and naive simultaneous inversion leaks one parameter's signature into another [9]. Remedies include parameter scaling, hierarchical inversion (velocity before density/attenuation), and Hessian-based deblurring.

### 3.3 Optimization: from gradient to model update

FWI minimizes χ with quasi-Newton methods — steepest descent, nonlinear conjugate gradient, or **L-BFGS**, which builds a low-rank Hessian approximation from gradient history [9]. A generic iteration:

1. Forward simulation → synthetics → misfit χ_k.
2. Adjoint simulation → gradient **g**_k = ∇χ.
3. Search direction **p**_k = −**B**_k **g**_k (L-BFGS) or −**g**_k.
4. Line search: test models **m**_k + α**p**_k with 1–2 extra simulations per trial step [1].
5. Update; repeat until χ plateaus.

Each iteration thus costs roughly 3–5 wave simulations per event, and convergence typically requires 10–50 iterations over tens to hundreds of events.

```python
# Pseudocode: adjoint-based FWI iteration (acoustic, one event)
def fwi_iteration(m, d_obs, sources, receivers):
    u = forward(m, sources)                    # solve L(m)u = f
    syn = sample(u, receivers)
    r = syn - d_obs                            # residual
    chi = 0.5 * sum(r**2)                      # misfit functional
    lam = adjoint(m, r, receivers)              # L(m)^\dagger lam = -dchi/du
    K = correlate(u, lam)                      # Frechet kernel: -int u . lam dt
    g = precondition(K)                        # apply Hessian approx / smoothing
    alpha = line_search(m, g, chi)             # 1-2 extra forward runs
    return m - alpha * g, chi
```

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Banana–doughnut kernels: the true shape of sensitivity

Perhaps the most visually striking result of adjoint theory is that finite-frequency travel-time sensitivity is *not* concentrated on the geometric ray. Cross-correlating forward and adjoint fields for a travel-time measurement yields a kernel whose cross-section resembles a **banana**, with a **doughnut hole of zero sensitivity exactly on the ray path** [1][8]. Physically: a heterogeneity sitting precisely on the ray speeds up or slows down the wave, but the *cross-correlation* measurement of the delayed pulse is, to first order, insensitive to it — the delay is measured against the pulse itself. Maximum sensitivity lies in the first Fresnel zone flanking the ray.

This has profound consequences:

- *Wavefront healing*: small anomalies are "healed" by diffraction and invisible to finite-frequency measurements — ray theory *overstates* resolvability [8].
- Mantle-plume tails narrower than ~500 km can be healed at the 20 s periods typical of global FWI, explaining decades of controversy over plume detectability [4].
- Kernel computation via the adjoint method automatically incorporates 3-D heterogeneity, unlike paraxial approximations of Fresnel volumes [2].

| Kernel type | Measurement | Geometry | Key pathology addressed |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ray-theoretic | onset time pick | infinitesimal ray | none (asymptotic) |
| Finite-frequency (Dahlen et al. [8]) | cross-correlation delay | banana–doughnut, 1-D reference | wavefront healing |
| Adjoint event kernel (Tromp et al. [1]) | full-waveform residual | exact 3-D volumetric | nonlinearity, 3-D focusing |
| Scattering / Hessian | model covariance | double interaction | cross-talk, null space |

### 4.2 Cycle-skipping and multiscale frequency continuation

The least-squares waveform misfit is *nonconvex*: if the synthetic is shifted by more than half a period relative to the observation, the gradient points toward aligning the wrong cycles — **cycle-skipping** — and the inversion converges to a spurious local minimum [7][9]. The classic remedy is **multiscale continuation** (Bunks et al. [9]; Virieux & Operto [9] review):

1. Low-pass filter data and synthetics to long periods (e.g., 60–120 s), where half-wavelengths are long and cycle-skipping is impossible given a decent starting model.
2. Invert to convergence at this scale — recovering long-wavelength structure.
3. Progressively admit higher frequencies (30 s → 15 s → 8 s), each stage initialized by the previous.
4. Final iterations fit full-band waveforms, resolving short-wavelength detail.

This is a *homotopy*: the objective is deformed from convex-ish to the true nonconvex functional, tracking the global minimum [9]. Alternatives include:

- **Optimal-transport misfits** (Wasserstein distance), which are convex with respect to time shifts and dramatically widen the basin of attraction [10].
- **Envelope / instantaneous-phase misfits**, which separate kinematic from dynamic information.

> **Theorem: (Multiscale basin expansion).** Under the Born approximation, low-pass filtering the residual to maximum frequency *f*max suppresses cycle-skipping provided the travel-time error satisfies |δT| < 1/(2f_max).

### 4.3 Regularization, preconditioning, and the Hessian

FWI is ill-posed: surface data cannot resolve deep small-scale structure, and the Hessian **H** = ∇²χ is rank-deficient [1][9]. Standard remedies:

- **Tikhonov regularization**: χ_reg = χ + λ₁‖**m** − **m**₀‖² + λ₂‖∇**m**‖², penalizing roughness.
- **Smoothing preconditioners**: convolve the gradient with a Gaussian, suppressing high-wavenumber artifacts [5].
- **Pseudo-Hessian / diagonal Hessian preconditioning**: weight by the forward-field energy, `P(x) = Σ_s ∫ |u_s(x,t)|² dt`, balancing illumination [9].
- **L-BFGS**: accumulates curvature information across iterations, approximating the inverse Hessian without ever forming it.
- **Uncertainty quantification**: the posterior covariance ≈ **H**⁻¹ under Gaussian assumptions; randomized SVD and Hessian-vector products (via second-order adjoints) make this tractable for the dominant eigen-directions [2].

*Regularization chooses the answer*: two equally data-fitting models (plume present vs. absent) may both minimize χ; only the regularizer decides. Honest reporting requires showing results across regularization strengths [4].

### 4.4 Source–structure trade-offs and joint inversion

Earthquake source parameters (location, moment tensor, source-time function) trade off with structure: mislocating a quake by 10 km mimics a velocity anomaly [1][5]. Adjoint tomography therefore *jointly* inverts for source and structure in the same adjoint pass. The workflow iterates: update structure → relocate events → update structure [5].

*Anisotropy and attenuation* add further trade-offs: isotropic velocity heterogeneity can masquerade as anisotropy and vice versa; intrinsic attenuation (*Q*) trades with focusing/defocusing by 3-D structure [1][4]. The consensus is hierarchical: invert for velocities first, then attenuation with structure fixed — never jointly from scratch.

### 4.5 Numerical engines: spectral elements and beyond

The forward/adjoint engine determines everything. The **spectral-element method** on cubed-sphere meshes (Komatitsch & Tromp) dominates global FWI for its accuracy at ~5 points per wavelength and natural handling of the free surface, fluid–solid boundaries (outer core), and topography [2][3]. Emerging directions include **GPU-accelerated SEM** for high-frequency global runs, **neural-operator surrogates** (Fourier Neural Operators) emulating wave propagation during line searches, and **automatic differentiation** through time-stepping codes, unifying the adjoint method with reverse-mode AD.

```tla
---------------- MODULE FWI ----------------
(* Multiscale FWI loop, stylized *)
VARIABLES m, chi, freq, k
Init == m = m0 /\ freq = f_min /\ k = 0 /\ chi = Misfit(m, f_min)
Next == \/ /\ k < K_max /\ freq <= f_max
           /\ m' = LineSearch(m, AdjointGradient(m, freq)) /\ k' = k+1
        \/ /\ Converged(m, freq) /\ freq < f_max
           /\ freq' = NextBand(freq) /\ k' = 0  (* inherit model, raise frequency *)
Spec == Init /\ [][Next]_<<m, chi, freq, k>>
THEOREM MonotoneDescent == Spec => [](chi' <= chi)
=============================================
```

## 5. Empirical Analysis and Theoretical Guarantees

### 5.1 Convergence behavior

On the SEG/EAGE salt and Marmousi benchmarks, multiscale FWI reduces the waveform misfit by 1–2 orders of magnitude over 20–40 L-BFGS iterations, with the model error ‖**m**_k − **m**_true‖ decreasing roughly geometrically in the early (low-frequency) stages and linearly later [9]. Global adjoint tomography (Tape et al. [5], Southern California) achieved ~70% variance reduction in waveform misfit after 16 iterations, with travel-time residuals dropping from ±3 s to ±0.5 s.

Key empirical regularities: **data fit ≠ model truth** (overfitting noise at high frequencies produces implausible roughness; early stopping is essential) [9]; **resolution follows the Fresnel zone** (~500–1000 km in the deep mantle at 20 s period, ~100 km in the crust at 5 s) [4]; and **amplitude misfit converges slower than phase**, because amplitudes conflate attenuation, focusing, and source errors [1].

### 5.2 Theoretical guarantees (and their limits)

- *Gradient exactness*: the adjoint gradient equals the analytic Fréchet derivative up to discretization error — verified by Taylor tests (‖χ(**m**+hδ**m**) − χ(**m**) − h⟨**g**,δ**m**⟩‖ = O(h²)) [1][2].
- *Local convergence*: with Wolfe line searches, L-BFGS converges superlinearly near a minimum of the smooth nonconvex objective [9].
- *Global convergence*: **not guaranteed** — cycle-skipping provides explicit counterexamples. Multiscale continuation and convexified misfits [10] widen basins but do not eliminate local minima.
- *Resolution analysis*: point-spread functions via Hessian-vector products quantify the true resolving power, replacing optimistic ray-density maps [2].

### 5.3 Landmark applications

**Mantle plumes.** Adjoint tomography of the North American mantle (Sigloch; French & Romanowicz [4]) and global whole-mantle models (SEMUCB-WM1, built from spectral-element synthetics) image narrow low-velocity conduits beneath hotspots such as Hawaii and Iceland, while showing others (e.g., Yellowstone) rooted shallowly — discriminating deep-mantle from upper-mantle plumes. Finite-frequency kernels were decisive: ray theory both created and erased plume artifacts [4][8].

**Subducting slabs.** FWI resolves slab morphology — stagnation at the 660-km discontinuity beneath East Asia, slab tearing beneath the Mediterranean, and phase-transition topography — at resolutions ray tomography cannot reach [3][4]. Waveform fitting of triplicated phases (turning at the transition zone) constrains slab thermal structure and, via mineral-physics conversion, slab *mineralogy*.

**Inner-core anisotropy.** The solid inner core exhibits cylindrical anisotropy: P-waves travel ~3–4% faster along the rotation axis than in the equatorial plane [11]. Normal-mode and body-wave FWI studies map hemispherical variations in anisotropy and attenuation, implicating textured iron crystallization and inner-core super-rotation. Joint inversion of PKP differential travel times, normal-mode splitting functions, and full waveforms remains the state of the art [11][12].

| Application | Target | Typical resolution | Decisive measurement |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mantle plumes | low-*v_s* conduits, CMB roots | ~500 km (deep mantle) | finite-frequency S delays |
| Slabs | morphology, 660-km stagnation | ~100–200 km | triplications, conversions |
| Inner core | cylindrical anisotropy, hemispheres | ~300 km | PKP diff. times, mode splitting |
| Crust (regional) | basins, fault zones | ~5–20 km | surface-wave dispersion, RF |

## 6. Limitations

Honest appraisal is mandatory; FWI's failures are as instructive as its successes.

1. **Non-uniqueness and null spaces.** Surface data fundamentally cannot resolve certain model components (e.g., deep small-scale density). Regularization selects among equivalent models — the choice is a prior, not a measurement [9].
2. **Cycle-skipping persists.** Despite multiscale strategies, poor starting models (e.g., beneath salt, or in poorly known oceanic regions) still trap inversions. Optimal-transport misfits help but add cost and tuning parameters [10].
3. **Attenuation–structure trade-off.** Intrinsic *Q* and elastic focusing produce similar amplitude signatures; most studies fix or heavily damp *Q* [1][4].
4. **Source errors contaminate structure.** Mislocated earthquakes and wrong moment tensors map directly into spurious velocity anomalies; joint inversion mitigates but does not eliminate this [5].
5. **Computational cost.** A single global iteration at 10 s period can consume millions of core-hours; high-frequency global FWI remains frontier computing [2][3].
6. **Anisotropy is underdetermined.** Full 21-parameter inversion is infeasible; studies assume hexagonal symmetry, and isotropic artifacts leak into anisotropic parameters [4].
7. **The starting-model paradox.** FWI needs a good starting model (usually from travel-time tomography) — the very method it supersedes [9].

## 7. Conclusion

Seismic full-waveform inversion via the adjoint-state method has transformed seismology from a ray-tracing discipline into a wave-equation discipline. The adjoint identity — *one extra simulation yields the exact gradient over millions of parameters* — made the previously unthinkable routine [1][2].

The frontier is now **uncertainty** — from single "best" models to posterior distributions via Bayesian FWI; **multi-physics** coupling of seismic, geodynamic, and mineral-physics constraints; and **planetary seismology**, where InSight's Mars data and future lunar networks demand methods that work with a handful of stations. The mathematics — adjoints, kernels, continuation — transfers unchanged. What changes is only the planet.
