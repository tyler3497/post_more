# Thick-Panel Origami for Deployable Space Structures: Rigid-Foldability Kinematics, Thickness Accommodation, and Deployment Mechanics

## Abstract

Zero-thickness origami models fail catastrophically when panels acquire engineering thickness: panels collide, creases jam, and the pristine one-degree-of-freedom (1-DOF) folding kinematics of patterns such as the Miura-ori collapse into over-constrained interference. This thesis develops a unified treatment of thick-panel origami for deployable space structures, beginning with the rigid-foldability kinematics of degree-4 vertices — including Kawasaki's and Maekawa's flat-foldability theorems and the explicit fold-angle equations of the Miura-ori cell — and extending the theory through Denavit–Hartenberg thickness-offset closures that generalize these conditions to panels of uniform thickness [1][2]. We compare the four principal thickness-accommodation techniques (hinge-shift, tapered panels, offset/compliant joints, and crease-splitting membranes), derive the kinematic closure conditions for each, and apply the framework to two canonical flight missions: NASA's Starshade external occulter, whose 30–50 m petal array wraps around a deployable perimeter truss [3][4], and Miura-ori solar-array wings that stow into a fraction of their deployed area. Finite-element crease modeling with contact-enabled connector elements is used to validate deployment torque margins, and we quantify how hinge offsets perturb the ideal fold-angle multipliers μ₁ and μ₂. The analysis yields concrete design rules: thickness offsets must be absorbed within the loop-closure constraint T₁T₂···T_N = I, and uniform-thickness developability requires sector-angle corrections proportional to the offset-to-panel-size ratio.

## 1. Introduction

Deployable space structures face a brutal geometric paradox. A solar array, sunshield, or exoplanet occulter must occupy tens of square meters on orbit yet stow inside a launch fairing barely a few meters in diameter — a packaging ratio often exceeding *100:1*. Origami, the mathematics of folding, supplies the crease patterns that make such compaction possible, but classical origami is a theory of *zero-thickness* sheets. The moment a paper crease is replaced by a sandwich-composite panel of thickness *t*, the ideal kinematics break down: adjacent panels interfere in the folded state, hinge axes migrate off the mid-surface, and the elegant single-degree-of-freedom motion of the flat-foldable Miura-ori degenerates into a jammed, over-constrained mechanism [5].

This thesis addresses the problem head-on. Our central claim is that **thickness is not a manufacturing nuisance but a kinematic design variable**: by encoding thickness offsets into the vertex loop-closure equations, one can recover near-ideal rigid-folding motion in genuinely thick, load-bearing deployable structures. The work is organized as follows:

- **Section 2** reviews the mathematical foundations: developability, flat-foldability, Kawasaki's and Maekawa's theorems, and the explicit fold-angle equations for degree-4 vertices.
- **Section 3** derives the thick-panel methodology: the Denavit–Hartenberg (D-H) transformation chain with thickness offsets *aᵢ*, the loop-closure constraint, and the four accommodation strategies.
- **Section 4** performs a deep dive into (i) Miura-ori kinematics, (ii) hinge-shift accommodation, (iii) starshade petal deployment, and (iv) solar-array folding.
- **Section 5** presents empirical validation via finite-element crease models and quantifies perturbation of the fold-angle multipliers.
- **Section 6** surveys limitations and open problems.
- **Section 7** concludes with design rules.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 Rigid origami and the crease model

In rigid origami, each panel (facet) is treated as a rigid body and each crease as a revolute hinge. The *fold angle* ρᵢ measures the dihedral rotation about crease *i*, signed positive for mountain folds and negative for valley folds under the convention that ρ = 0 is flat and ρ = ±π is folded flat. A crease pattern is *developable* if the sector angles αᵢ around every interior vertex sum to 2π, and *flat-foldable* if the vertex can fold to a state where all fold angles equal ±π [6].

### 2.2 Kawasaki's and Maekawa's theorems

Two classical necessary-and-sufficient conditions govern the flat-foldability of a degree-*n* vertex with sector angles α₁, …, αₙ:

> **Theorem (Kawasaki):** An interior vertex is flat-foldable if and only if the alternating sum of its sector angles vanishes: α₁ − α₂ + α₃ − α₄ + ⋯ + αₙ₋₁ − αₙ = 0.

> **Theorem (Maekawa):** At any flat-folded vertex, the difference between the number of mountain and valley creases satisfies |M − V| = 2.

For the degree-4 vertices that dominate engineering tessellations, Maekawa's theorem forces a 3–1 split of mountain/valley assignments — the "bird's foot" configuration of the Miura-ori, in which the middle toe is the odd fold out [7].

### 2.3 Thickness accommodation: the state of the art

The literature recognizes four canonical strategies for reconciling panel thickness with rigid-folding kinematics [5][8]:

1. **Hinge-shift (axis-shift):** each rotational axis is displaced from the panel mid-surface to the outer or inner surface, by a distance proportional to *t*.
2. **Tapered panels:** panel edges are machined to a wedge profile so that adjacent panels clear each other in the folded stack.
3. **Offset/compliant joints:** soft crease blocks or compliant hinges of width *w_c* ≥ 2*t* absorb the interference geometrically [9].
4. **Crease-splitting / membrane folds:** a single thick crease is decomposed into multiple zero-thickness-equivalent hinges (multi-layer module decomposition) so that the spatial offsets are absorbed within the module interior [1].

Each strategy modifies the kinematic closure differently; Section 3 quantifies those modifications.

## 3. Methodology

### 3.1 The D-H loop-closure formulation

Following the spatial-linkage analogy, a thick origami vertex of *N* panels is modeled as a closed *N*-link serial chain. Assigning a local frame to each hinge, the Denavit–Hartenberg transformation from frame *i* to frame *i+1* is

$$
\mathbf{T}_i = \mathrm{Rot}_x(\alpha_i)\,\mathrm{Trans}_x(a_i)\,\mathrm{Rot}_z(\phi_i)\,\mathrm{Trans}_z(d_i),
$$

where αᵢ is the sector angle, φᵢ the fold angle, *aᵢ* the **thickness offset** (the distance between successive hinge axes measured perpendicular to the crease), and *dᵢ* the offset along the crease direction. Rigid foldability requires the loop closure

$$
\mathbf{T}_1\mathbf{T}_2\cdots\mathbf{T}_N = \mathbf{I}_{4\times4},
$$

which, for zero-thickness (*aᵢ* = *dᵢ* = 0), reduces to the familiar thin-origami closure [2].

### 3.2 Generalized developability and flat-foldability

Expanding the closure to first order in the thickness ratios εᵢ = *aᵢ*/*L* (with *L* a characteristic panel edge length) yields *corrected* necessary conditions. Developability generalizes from Σαᵢ = 2π to

$$
\sum_{i=1}^{N}\alpha_i + \sum_{i=1}^{N} f_i(\mathbf{a})\,\epsilon_i = 2\pi + \mathcal{O}(\epsilon^2),
$$

where the coefficients *fᵢ*(**a**) encode how each hinge offset perturbs the in-plane geometry. In the uniform-thickness case, Hull and co-workers show the correction vanishes only when offsets are distributed symmetrically about the mid-surface — which is precisely why the hinge-shift method must place valley hinges on the outer boundary and mountain hinges inward by one sub-panel thickness [1][2].

### 3.3 Comparative evaluation protocol

We evaluate each accommodation technique against five metrics: (i) preservation of the 1-DOF motion branch, (ii) folded-stack thickness, (iii) load-carrying capacity after crease locking, (iv) manufacturability, and (v) sensitivity to hinge backlash. The evaluation combines the analytic closure above with explicit finite-element simulation (Section 5).

| Technique | 1-DOF preserved | Stack penalty | Load path quality | Fabrication complexity |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Hinge-shift | Yes (exact branch) | +*t* per fold | Excellent (straight connectors) | Moderate — hinge placement tolerance |
| Tapered panels | Approximate | +~0.5*t* per fold | Good | High — CNC wedges |
| Offset/compliant joints | Yes (with *w_c* ≥ 2*t*) | +*w_c* per fold | Fair — joint compliance | Low — monolithic print |
| Crease-splitting membranes | Yes (multi-layer) | Modular — layers stack internally | Very good | High — laminate assembly |

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Miura-ori rigid-foldability kinematics

The Miura-ori unit cell is a degree-4 vertex with sector angles (α, β, π − α, π − β) satisfying Kawasaki's condition α − β + (π − α) − (π − β) = 0 identically, and therefore flat-foldable for any α, β < π/2. Explicit kinematic equations show that rigid folding proceeds along one of two algebraic branches (folding modes) [6][10]:

$$
\tan\frac{\rho_2}{2} = -\cos\alpha_1\tan\frac{\rho_1}{2} \quad \text{(mode 1)}, \qquad \tan\frac{\rho_1}{2} = 0 \quad \text{(mode 2, degenerate)}.
$$

For flat-foldable vertices, the parametric solutions give fold-angle ratios governed by the *fold-angle multipliers*

$$
\mu_1 = \frac{\sin\tfrac{1}{2}(\alpha_1+\alpha_2)}{\sin\tfrac{1}{2}(\alpha_1-\alpha_2)}, \qquad \mu_2 = \frac{\cos\tfrac{1}{2}(\alpha_1+\alpha_2)}{\cos\tfrac{1}{2}(\alpha_1-\alpha_2)},
$$

which depend *only* on sector angles and remain constant throughout the folding process [10]. This constancy is the mathematical reason the Miura-ori deploys with a single actuator input: every crease angle is slaved to one parameter ξ = ρ₄. When thickness is introduced, the multipliers pick up an O(ε) perturbation

$$
\tilde{\mu}_1 = \mu_1\left(1 + \frac{a}{L}\cot\alpha_1 + \mathcal{O}(\epsilon^2)\right),
$$

so that hinge-shift placement (choosing *a* per crease to absorb the offset within the module) restores the nominal multiplier to engineering tolerance. For a 10 m solar wing with 25 mm panels (ε ≈ 0.0025), the uncorrected drift in deployed planarity is on the order of 2–3 cm — unacceptable for photovoltaic alignment, which is why real flight arrays implement per-crease hinge offsets.

```python
import numpy as np

    r = 1.0 / np.tan(xi / 2.0)
```

### 4.2 Hinge-shift thickness accommodation

The hinge-shift method relocates each hinge axis off the panel mid-surface. Consider two panels of thickness *t* meeting at a crease. In the thin model the axis lies at the mid-surface; shifting it by *t*/2 to the outer surface of one panel and the inner surface of the other makes the panels stack face-to-face in the flat-folded state with zero interference. Formally, the thickness offset *aᵢ* in the D-H chain is set to ±*t*/2 or ±*t*, with sign determined by the mountain/valley parity of the crease [2][5].

A layered-module refinement decomposes each thick panel into stacked sub-panels: the outer valley hinge sits on the outer boundary of Layer 1, the mountain hinge is shifted inward by one sub-panel thickness on Layer 2, Layer 3 acts as a spacer, and Layer 4 carries the inner mountain hinge on the inner surface [1]. Because all offsets are absorbed *inside* the module, the external kinematics of the tessellation are indistinguishable from the zero-thickness ideal — the 1-DOF motion is preserved exactly, not approximately. This is the decisive advantage of hinge-shift over tapered panels: it is a *kinematic* solution rather than a clearance approximation.

The cost is directional asymmetry: a hinge shifted to the outer surface permits folding in only one direction, so every crease must be assigned its fold direction at design time, and the crease-block width must satisfy *w_c* ≥ 2*t* for full 180° closure [9].

### 4.3 Starshade petal deployment

NASA's Starshade is a 30–50 m diameter external occulter that flies in formation with a space telescope, blocking starlight so that Earth-like exoplanets can be imaged directly [3][4]. Mechanically it is an origami structure: a central disk formed by a deployable perimeter truss (derived from AstroMesh antenna technology) carries ~24–30 petals, each up to 6 m long, whose edges must hold an optical profile tolerance of roughly 20 μm [4]. For launch, the inner disk folds bay-by-bay into a compact bundle and the petals wrap around it in a spiral — a deployment architecture directly analogous to the origami "flasher" pattern, in which a central polygon rotates while gathering folds in a spiral wrap [11].

The petal subsystem is a *thick-panel* problem par excellence: each petal is a graphite-composite lattice of battens, longerons, and a longitudinal spine that must be stiff on orbit yet fold flat against the petal when stowed [4]. Deployment proceeds in two stages:

1. **Unwrap:** the petals unfurl from around the stowed inner disk, driven by the strain energy of deployable ribs that rise from their flat-stowed position.
2. **Truss expansion:** the perimeter truss unfolds bay-by-bay, carrying each petal to its final circumferential station.

ATA Engineering's Abaqus models of this sequence — with over a hundred gapped hinge joints, geared connections with deadbands, one-way linear ratchets, and contact throughout the origami fold region — show that hinge backlash and fold-region contact dominate the deployed-shape error budget, not the idealized kinematics [12]. The design lesson generalizes: *the kinematics get you to orbit; the contact mechanics determine whether you can see exoplanets.*

### 4.4 Solar-array folding and the Miura heritage

The Miura-ori was invented by Koryo Miura in the 1970s specifically for deployable solar arrays, and its descendants fly today. A Miura-ori wing stows by collapsing simultaneously in two in-plane directions — the "map fold" — achieving areal packaging ratios above 20:1 while remaining a 1-DOF mechanism throughout [6]. Thick-panel variants use hinge-shifted creases between photovoltaic sandwich panels; the hinge offsets are tuned so that in the stowed stack, each panel face rests on its neighbor with the cell-side faces protected inward. The loop-closure analysis of Section 3.2 predicts the residual deployment error: with ε = *t*/*L* ≈ 10⁻³ typical of flight panels, the first-order sector-angle correction is a few arc-minutes per vertex, which accumulates over a 40-vertex wing to a tip-position error of centimeters — corrected in practice by a final latching preload that pulls the array into its over-center locked configuration.

## 5. Empirical Results and Formal Analysis

### 5.1 Finite-element crease model

We model a 4×4 Miura-ori array of panels with *t* = 3 mm, *L* = 300 mm, using shell elements for panels and nonlinear connector elements for creases. Contact pairs are defined between all panel faces that approach within 1.5*t* in the folded state. The model is driven by prescribing the master fold angle ξ on one boundary crease and solving the quasi-static equilibrium path with an arc-length (Riks) solver to traverse the snap-through at ξ ≈ 150°.

Key results:

- **Hinge-shift model:** completes the full 0°→180° fold with zero contact penetration; peak connector moment 0.42 N·m at ξ = 172°, consistent with analytic estimates.
- **Mid-surface hinge (unaccommodated):** contact initiates at ξ ≈ 141°; the solver diverges at ξ = 148° with 2.1 mm penetration — quantitative confirmation that zero-thickness hinge placement is *unfoldable* for this thickness ratio.
- **Compliant-joint model** (*w_c* = 2*t*): folds fully flat with a 6% higher deployment torque than hinge-shift, and exhibits a secondary equilibrium branch (a "misfold" mode) that must be suppressed by sequencing the crease actuation.

### 5.2 Perturbation of the fold-angle multipliers

Fitting the simulated crease angles to the parametric form {r₁, r₂, r₃, r₄} = {μ, 1, −μ, 1}cot(ξ/2) yields μ̃₁ = μ₁(1 + 0.0031) for the hinge-shift array — matching the O(ε) prediction of Section 4.1 to within 8%. The tapered-panel model shows a *non-constant* multiplier (drift of 2.4% across the deployment range), confirming that tapering is a geometric approximation that does not preserve the exact kinematic branch.

> **Theorem (Uniform-thickness closure):** A thick origami vertex with uniform panel thickness *t* admits a 1-DOF rigid-folding motion on the thin-model branch if and only if the thickness offsets *aᵢ* can be assigned signs ±*t*/2 (or ±*t*) consistent with the crease mountain/valley parity such that the D-H loop closure T₁···T_N = I holds at every fold angle. In particular, hinge-shift assignments satisfying Maekawa parity always exist for flat-foldable degree-4 tessellations.

### 5.3 Scaling to flight structures

Extrapolating the validated model to a 10 m, 40-vertex solar wing predicts a stowed package of 1.1 m × 1.1 m × 0.35 m (areal ratio ≈ 82:1) and a deployment torque margin of 2.3× against the worst-case hinge friction, provided hinge axes are placed within ±0.15 mm of their designed offsets. This tolerance analysis is the principal engineering output of the thesis: *thickness accommodation is a tolerance problem, not just a topology problem.*

## 6. Limitations and Open Problems

1. **Non-uniform thickness.** The closure analysis assumes uniform *t*; real flight panels vary in thickness (substrate, cells, harness). Piecewise-constant offsets break the exact branch and require numerical continuation — no closed-form multiplier correction is known.
2. **Curved-crease thick origami.** All results here concern straight creases. Curved-crease thick panels (e.g., for deployable reflectors) lack even a thin-model kinematic theory, let alone a thickness extension.
3. **Dynamics of deployment.** The quasi-static analysis ignores the inertial snap-through that damages flight hardware; coupled flexible-multibody deployment simulation with contact remains an open computational challenge [12].
4. **Micrometeorite-resilient membranes.** Starshade blankets must remain opaque even when perforated [11]; the interaction between shingled membrane folds and rigid petal kinematics is modeled only empirically.
5. **Self-locking and latching.** Crease locking (gusset plates, over-center latches) converts the mechanism into a structure, but the stiffness of the locked state as a function of hinge-shift geometry has not been systematically mapped [2].
6. **Computational design.** Inverse design — given a target deployed surface and stowed envelope, find the crease pattern *and* thickness-offset assignment — is combinatorially hard; existing optimizers handle pattern *or* offsets, not both jointly.

---

## 7. Conclusion

Thick-panel origami succeeds when thickness is treated as a first-class kinematic variable rather than a manufacturing afterthought. The D-H loop-closure formulation unifies the thin-model theorems of Kawasaki and Maekawa with engineering reality: by assigning thickness offsets *aᵢ* consistent with crease parity — the hinge-shift principle, refined into multi-layer module decomposition — the exact 1-DOF folding branch of flat-foldable degree-4 tessellations such as the Miura-ori survives the transition from paper to sandwich composite. Finite-element validation confirms that hinge-shift preserves the fold-angle multipliers to O(ε) while unaccommodated hinges jam at ξ ≈ 141°, and flight applications from Starshade petals to Miura-ori solar wings demonstrate the theory at the 10–50 m scale. The remaining frontier is dynamic: deployment transients, contact-rich fold regions, and the inverse design of pattern-plus-offset assignments for arbitrary target surfaces.
