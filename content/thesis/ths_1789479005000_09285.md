# Renyi Differential Privacy: Composition, Amplification by Subsampling, and the Moments Accountant

## Abstract

Rényi differential privacy (RDP), introduced by Mironov in 2017, reframes privacy accounting in terms of the Rényi divergence between output distributions on neighboring datasets. Where classical (ε, δ)-differential privacy tracks only a worst-case multiplicative bound plus an additive tail allowance, RDP tracks the *entire tail* of the privacy-loss random variable through a family of moment bounds indexed by the order α. This article develops the full theory: the definition and its core properties, the sharp two-way conversion between RDP and (ε, δ)-differential privacy, the additive composition rule that makes RDP the natural currency of privacy budgets, and the two pillars that made it the de facto standard for private machine learning — amplification by subsampling and the moments accountant. We derive the Gaussian mechanism's exact RDP curve, prove the composition and conversion theorems, present the tight subsampled-RDP bound of Wang, Balle, and Kasiviswanathan, and show how these ingredients combine in differentially private stochastic gradient descent to deliver guarantees an order of magnitude tighter than advanced composition. A reference Python implementation and computed comparisons close the loop from theory to practice.

##1 Intro

Differential privacy, introduced by Dwork, McSherry, Nissim, and Smith [5], gives a mathematical promise: the output distribution of a randomized algorithm should barely change when a single individual's record is added to or removed from the input. That promise is parameterized, most commonly as (ε, δ)-differential privacy: for all neighboring datasets *D* and *D′* and all measurable output sets *S*,

> **Definition.** A mechanism *M* is (ε, δ)-differentially private if Pr[*M*(*D*) ∈ *S*] ≤ *e*^ε · Pr[*M*(*D′*) ∈ *S*] + δ.

The parameter ε bounds the worst-case multiplicative shift; δ is an additive allowance that tolerates the long tails where a pure multiplicative guarantee would fail — the tails that inevitably appear when one adds Gaussian noise.

This definition conquered the field, but it has an accounting problem. Modern private machine learning runs *thousands* of noisy iterations over the same data [2]. Each iteration consumes a slice of a total privacy budget, and the analyst must compose thousands of (ε, δ) guarantees into one. Basic composition adds the ε values, which is far too pessimistic; advanced composition [7] improves the rate to roughly √(*k* log(1/δ′))·ε, but the analysis becomes combinatorial, the bounds degrade for heterogeneous mechanisms, and the whole edifice must be rebuilt every time the noise distribution or the sampling scheme changes. Practitioners needed a *calculus* of privacy loss — a quantity that composes by simple addition, converts back to (ε, δ) on demand, and rewards the two great tricks of the trade: **subsampling** and **Gaussian noise**.

The moments accountant of Abadi et al. [2] was the first such calculus in practice, and Rényi differential privacy [1] is its clean theoretical form. The key insight is disarmingly simple: instead of bounding the worst case of the privacy-loss random variable, bound its *moment-generating structure* — equivalently, bound the Rényi divergence of order α between the two output distributions, for a whole range of orders α. Composition then becomes addition. Subsampling amplification becomes a one-line bound. And the final (ε, δ) guarantee is recovered by optimizing over α, exactly as the moments accountant optimizes over its moment order λ.

This article is a self-contained treatment of that theory. Section 2 recalls the classical background; Section 3 builds the RDP machinery — definition, properties, the Gaussian mechanism, and conversions; Section 4 goes deep on the order parameter, additive composition, subsampling amplification, the moments accountant as an RDP instance, and numerical practice; Section 5 proves the central theorems, derives the Gaussian RDP curve, and presents a working implementation with computed comparisons; Section 6 is honest about the limitations; Section 7 concludes.

##2 Background

### 2.1 The classical definitions

Pure ε-differential privacy (δ = 0) is the gold standard: no tails are tolerated at all. The Laplace mechanism achieves it tightly — adding Laplace noise scaled to the ℓ₁-sensitivity gives exactly ε-DP [7]. But pure DP is brittle. The Gaussian mechanism, the workhorse of private optimization, cannot satisfy pure DP for any finite noise scale: Gaussian tails decay too slowly, so some δ > 0 is unavoidable. Approximate (ε, δ)-DP was invented precisely to accommodate this [5].

The δ parameter is usually chosen cryptographically small, δ ≪ 1/*n* for a dataset of *n* records, so that the "failure" event it permits is rarer than sampling a single individual. But δ is a blunt instrument: it says nothing about *how* the tails behave, only that their total mass is bounded.

### 2.2 Composition: the budget problem

A privacy *budget* is the total (ε, δ) an analyst is willing to spend, and composition theorems are the accountants. Three landmarks:

- **Basic composition.** The composition of *k* mechanisms with parameters (εᵢ, δᵢ) is (Σᵢ εᵢ, Σᵢ δᵢ)-DP. Simple, and usually far too loose.
- **Advanced composition** [7]. The composition of *k* mechanisms each (ε, δ)-DP is (ε√(2*k* log(1/δ′)) + *k*ε(*e*^ε − 1), *k*δ + δ′)-DP. The √*k* rate is the right one, but the theorem demands small per-step ε, identical parameters, and a non-adaptive sequence fixed in advance.
- **Concentrated differential privacy** (CDP) of Dwork and Rothblum, refined as *zero*-concentrated DP (zCDP) by Bun and Steinke [4]: require that the privacy-loss random variable be subgaussian, i.e., *D*α ≤ ρ·α for all orders α. zCDP composes by adding ρ values and converts cleanly to (ε, δ)-DP. It was the first sign that *a family of divergence bounds, linear in the order*, is the right object to track.

### 2.3 The moments accountant and private deep learning

Abadi et al. [2] attacked the concrete problem of training neural networks with DP-SGD: per-example gradients are clipped to norm *C*, averaged over a random minibatch, perturbed with Gaussian noise of scale σ*C*, and the model is updated — repeated for thousands of steps over subsampled batches. Their *moments accountant* tracks, for each step, the quantity α(λ) = max over neighbors of log E[(*p*/*q*)^λ], the log of the λ-th moment of the likelihood ratio. These log-moments add across steps, and at the end the accountant converts to (ε, δ) via a Chernoff-style tail bound: δ = min_λ exp(α(λ) − λ·ε). The result was a dramatic improvement — single-digit ε on MNIST and CIFAR-10 where advanced composition gave vacuous bounds — and it became the standard accounting method in TensorFlow Privacy and Opacus. The earlier NoisySGD line of Song, Chaudhuri, and Sarwate [9] had shown the algorithm; the moments accountant made its privacy analysis practical.

Mironov's observation [1] was that the moments accountant *is* Rényi differential privacy at integer orders, and that the theory is cleaner, more general, and compositional when stated in RDP language from the start.


##3 Methodology

### 3.1 Rényi divergence and the definition of RDP

For distributions *P*, *Q* and order α > 1, the **Rényi divergence** is

> **Definition.** *D*α(*P* ‖ *Q*) = (1/(α − 1)) · log E_{*x*∼*Q*}[ (*P*(*x*)/*Q*(*x*))^α ].

As α → 1 it tends to the KL divergence; as α → ∞ it tends to the max-divergence sup log(*P*/*Q*), i.e., pure ε-DP. The order α is a dial: larger α magnifies the contribution of rare, high-loss events — the tails.

> **Definition (Mironov [1]).** A randomized mechanism *M* is (α, ε)-RDP if, for all neighboring datasets *D*, *D′*, *D*α(*M*(*D*) ‖ *M*(*D′*)) ≤ ε.

In practice one tracks a *curve* ε(α) over a range of orders rather than a single pair. Two facts make the definition usable: it is closed under post-processing (any function of an RDP output is RDP with the same parameters), and it admits a graceful group-privacy degradation with group size [1].

### 3.2 The Gaussian mechanism, exactly

Let *f* have ℓ₂-sensitivity Δ, i.e., ‖*f*(*D*) − *f*(*D′*)‖₂ ≤ Δ for all neighbors, and let *M*(*D*) = *f*(*D*) + *N*(0, σ²*I*). The Rényi divergence between two Gaussians differing in mean by at most Δ has a closed form (derived in Section 5):

> **Theorem (Mironov [1]).** The Gaussian mechanism is (α, α·Δ²/(2σ²))-RDP for every α > 1.

The RDP curve is *linear* in α — the signature of a subgaussian privacy loss, and exactly the zCDP condition [4] with ρ = Δ²/(2σ²). This is why zCDP is the special case of RDP with a linear curve, and why RDP strictly generalizes it: real mechanisms (especially subsampled ones) have curves that bend, and tracking the bend buys tightness.

### 3.3 Composition: addition, nothing more

> **Theorem (RDP composition [1]).** If *M*₁ is (α, ε₁)-RDP and *M*₂ is (α, ε₂)-RDP (possibly chosen adaptively), the composed mechanism (*M*₂ ∘ *M*₁) is (α, ε₁ + ε₂)-RDP.

The proof is a one-paragraph application of the chain rule for Rényi divergence (Section 5). Contrast this with advanced composition: no √*k* factors, no identical-parameter requirement, no *e*^ε − 1 correction terms, and adaptivity comes free. The price is that the *same* order α must be used for every step — which is why one tracks the whole curve ε(α) and converts only at the very end.

### 3.4 Converting back to (ε, δ)-DP

RDP is an accounting currency; the bill is still paid in (ε, δ). The standard conversion is:

> **Lemma (Mironov [1]).** If *M* is (α, ε)-RDP, then for every δ in the open unit interval it is (ε + log(1/δ)/(α − 1), δ)-DP.

The conversion is proved by a Markov/Chernoff argument on the privacy-loss variable (Section 5). In practice one minimizes the right-hand side over the tracked orders α — large α shrinks the log(1/δ)/(α−1) penalty but usually inflates ε(α), so the optimum balances the two. Canonne, Kamath, and Steinke [6] give a refined conversion that is strictly tighter, shaving the penalty further for small δ.

The reverse direction is also useful: pure DP implies RDP, since (ε, 0)-DP yields (α, α·ε²/2)-RDP [4]. This lets heterogeneous pipelines — some steps Laplace, some Gaussian — be accounted in one common RDP currency.

### 3.5 Why RDP won

Three properties jointly explain RDP's dominance in private ML: **(i)** composition is addition, so thousand-step analyses are trivial; **(ii)** the Gaussian mechanism's curve is known exactly, so no analytic slack is introduced at the mechanism level; **(iii)** subsampling amplification — the subject of the next section — has a clean, tight RDP form that applies to *any* RDP mechanism, not just Gaussians.


##4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The order α as a microscope on the privacy-loss tail

Fix neighboring datasets and consider the *privacy-loss random variable* *L* = log(*p*(*Y*)/*q*(*Y*)) with *Y* ∼ *M*(*D*). Pure DP bounds the *essential supremum* of *L*; (ε, δ)-DP bounds sup *L* outside an event of probability δ; RDP bounds the *exponential moments* E[exp((α−1)*L*)] ≤ exp((α−1)ε). Small α (near 1) sees the typical loss — the KL divergence; large α interrogates the far tail. The conversion penalty log(1/δ)/(α−1) is then exactly the Chernoff price of turning a moment bound into a tail bound at failure probability δ.

This explains a practical rule of thumb: the smaller your target δ, the larger the orders α you must track. For δ = 10⁻⁵, orders up to 32 or 64 suffice; for cryptographically small δ, one wants α in the hundreds. It also explains why the Gaussian's linear curve is the best case — its tails are as thin as the accounting currency can express.

### 4.2 Additive composition and the privacy budget, revisited

Under RDP, a privacy budget is literally a number line. Ten thousand DP-SGD steps, each (α, ε_step(α))-RDP, compose to (α, 10000·ε_step(α))-RDP — no square roots, no δ′ splitting, no advanced-composition preconditions. The final (ε, δ) is min_α [10000·ε_step(α) + log(1/δ)/(α−1)].

The subtlety: composition requires a *common* α, but different steps may have different curves. The resolution is pointwise: for each α in the grid, sum that α's values across steps, convert, and take the minimum. Heterogeneous mechanisms — a Laplace step here, a subsampled Gaussian there, an exponential-mechanism selection elsewhere — all reduce to adding numbers on the same curve. This is what advanced composition could never do gracefully.


### 4.3 Amplification by subsampling

Subsampling amplifies privacy: using each record with small probability *q* shrinks the effective RDP roughly like *q*².

> **Theorem (Subsampled RDP [3]).** Let *M* be (α, ε(α))-RDP and let *S*^q denote Poisson subsampling, which includes each record independently with probability *q*. Then *M* ∘ *S*^q is (α, ε′(α))-RDP with
>
> ε′(α) ≤ (1/(α−1)) · log( 1 + *q*²·C(α,2)·min{4(*e*^{ε(2)}−1), 2*e*^{ε(2)}} + Σ_{*j*=3}^{α} *q*^*j*·C(α,*j*)·*e*^{(*j*−1)ε(*j*)}·min{2, (*e*^{ε(∞)}−1)^*j*} ).


### 4.4 The moments accountant, revisited as an RDP instance

DP-SGD [2][9] executes this loop:

1. **Sample** a minibatch by including each record independently with probability *q* (Poisson sampling).
2. **Clip** each per-example gradient to ℓ₂-norm *C*, bounding the sensitivity of the batch gradient sum by *C*.
3. **Perturb** the averaged gradient with *N*(0, σ²*C*²*I*) noise.
4. **Account**: each step is (α, ε_step(α))-RDP by the subsampled-Gaussian analysis; *T* steps compose by addition.
5. **Convert**: report min_α [*T*·ε_step(α) + log(1/δ)/(α−1)].

##5 Empirical/Proofs

### 5.1 The composition theorem, proved

> **Theorem.** If *M*₁ is (α, ε₁)-RDP and *M*₂(·, *y*₁) is (α, ε₂)-RDP for every fixed first-stage output *y*₁, then the adaptive composition *M* = (*M*₁, *M*₂) is (α, ε₁ + ε₂)-RDP.

> **Proof.** Write *P*₁, *Q*₁ for the first-stage output distributions on neighbors *D*, *D′*, and *P*₂^{y₁}, *Q*₂^{y₁} for the second stage conditioned on first-stage output *y*₁. The joint likelihood ratio factorizes, and Hölder's inequality applied to the product of the two stages' ratio moments gives E_Q[(*P*/*Q*)^α] ≤ E_{Q₁}[(*P*₁/*Q*₁)^α] · sup_{y₁} E_{Q₂}[(*P*₂^{y₁}/*Q*₂^{y₁})^α] ≤ exp((α−1)(ε₁+ε₂)). Taking (1/(α−1)) log of both sides yields *D*α(*P*‖*Q*) ≤ ε₁ + ε₂. ∎

The proof's brevity is the point: Rényi divergence tensorizes over product distributions up to exactly the Hölder step, so composition *is* addition at the level of log-moments.

### 5.2 The conversion lemma, proved

> **Lemma.** (α, ε)-RDP implies (ε + log(1/δ)/(α−1), δ)-DP for every δ in the open unit interval.

> **Proof.** Let *L* be the privacy-loss variable. RDP gives E[exp((α−1)*L*)] ≤ exp((α−1)ε). For any threshold *t*, Markov's inequality yields Pr[*L* > *t*] ≤ exp((α−1)(ε − *t*)). Choose *t* = ε + log(1/δ)/(α−1) so the right side equals δ. On the complement event *L* ≤ *t*, the likelihood ratio is bounded by *e*^*t*, giving the (ε + log(1/δ)/(α−1), δ)-DP guarantee. ∎

### 5.3 The Gaussian RDP curve, derived

For *P* = *N*(μ₀, σ²*I*), *Q* = *N*(μ₁, σ²*I*) with ‖μ₀ − μ₁‖₂ ≤ Δ, the likelihood ratio is log-normal in the sufficient statistic, and E_Q[(*P*/*Q*)^α] = exp(α(α−1)Δ²/(2σ²)) exactly. Dividing the log by (α−1) gives *D*α = αΔ²/(2σ²). Hence the Gaussian mechanism is (α, αΔ²/(2σ²))-RDP for all α > 1 [1] — a straight line through the origin with slope Δ²/(2σ²).

### 5.4 Reference implementation

The following self-contained Python implements the full pipeline — Gaussian RDP, WBK subsampling amplification [3], additive composition, and optimal-α conversion — with no dependencies beyond the standard library:

```python
import math
from math import comb, log, exp

def rdp_gaussian(alpha, sigma, sensitivity=1.0):
    """Exact RDP of the Gaussian mechanism at order alpha (Mironov 2017)."""
    return alpha * sensitivity ** 2 / (2.0 * sigma ** 2)

def rdp_subsampled(alpha, eps_fn, q):
    """Poisson-subsampled RDP via Wang-Balle-Kasiviswanathan (2018).

    eps_fn(j) is the base mechanism's RDP at integer order j.
    """
    e2 = eps_fn(2)
    total = q ** 2 * comb(alpha, 2) * min(4.0 * (exp(e2) - 1.0),
                                          2.0 * exp(e2))
    for j in range(3, alpha + 1):
        total += q ** j * comb(alpha, j) * exp((j - 1) * eps_fn(j)) * 2.0
    return log(1.0 + total) / (alpha - 1)

def dp_sgd_epsilon(steps, q, sigma, delta, clip=1.0):
    """Moments-accountant epsilon for DP-SGD: compose, then convert."""
    best, best_alpha = float("inf"), None
    for alpha in range(2, 65):
        step = rdp_subsampled(alpha,
                              lambda a: rdp_gaussian(a, sigma, clip), q)
        eps = steps * step + log(1.0 / delta) / (alpha - 1)
        if eps < best:
            best, best_alpha = eps, alpha
    return best, best_alpha

eps, a = dp_sgd_epsilon(steps=10000, q=0.01, sigma=4.0, delta=1e-5)
print(f"epsilon = {eps:.2f} at alpha = {a}")
```

### 5.5 Computed comparison: DP-SGD accounting regimes

We fix a realistic DP-SGD workload — *T* = 10,000 steps, Poisson sampling rate *q* = 0.01, noise multiplier σ = 4.0, target δ = 10⁻⁵ — and compute the total ε under five accounting regimes. The per-step (ε₀, δ₀)-DP parameters for the classical regimes use the analytic Gaussian mechanism with δ₀ = δ/(2*T*):

| Accounting regime | Total ε (δ = 10⁻⁵) | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Naive composition | ≈ 15,547 | Σ ε₀; vacuous |
| Advanced composition [7] | ≈ 58,791 | *T*ε₀(*e*^{ε₀}−1) term explodes: per-step ε₀ ≈ 1.55 > 1 |
| zCDP [4] | ≈ 432.5 | ρ = *T*/(2σ²), converted via ρ + 2√(ρ log(1/δ)) |
| RDP, no subsampling [1] | ≈ 636.5 | min_α *T*α/(2σ²) + log(1/δ)/(α−1) |
| RDP + subsampling (WBK [3]) | ≈ 2.86 | optimal α = 9; the moments-accountant regime |

Three lessons. **First**, subsampling is doing the heavy lifting: the *q*² amplification turns ε ≈ 636 into ε ≈ 2.9, a 220× improvement from sampling alone. **Second**, advanced composition can be *worse* than naive composition when per-step ε₀ exceeds 1 — the *e*^{ε₀} − 1 correction detonates, a failure mode the RDP analysis never exhibits. **Third**, the RDP+subsampling number (≈ 2.86) is in the same ballpark as the values reported by production accountants for this workload [2][8]; the remaining gap to the tightest known bounds is closed by the direct sampled-Gaussian analysis of Mironov, Talwar, and Zhang [8].

##6 Limitations
RDP is an accounting framework, not a panacea, and its limits are worth stating plainly.

- **The order grid is fixed ex ante.** Composition adds ε(α) values pointwise, so every order you might convert at must be tracked from the start. Data-dependent choice of α after training requires privacy filters or odometers; the naive "pick the best α on the realized losses" is not valid without paying for the adaptivity.
- **Conversion is lossy.** The log(1/δ)/(α−1) penalty is the Chernoff price of a tail bound, and for very small δ it dominates unless large α is tracked. Refined conversions [6] help but do not eliminate the gap between the RDP curve and the true (ε, δ) frontier.
- **Poisson ≠ minibatch.** The sharpest theorems assume Poisson sampling; production training uses fixed-size batches or shuffling. Dedicated analyses exist, but every practitioner silently absorbs a small modeling gap here.
- **Group privacy is expensive.** RDP's group-privacy degradation grows faster with group size *k* than pure DP's linear scaling [1] — RDP is tuned for single-record neighbors, and correlated-group guarantees need care.
- **Worst-case over neighbors.** RDP bounds the supremum over all neighboring dataset pairs. Data-dependent or per-instance improvements (smooth sensitivity, propose-test-release style analyses) live outside the RDP calculus.
- **Integer orders only, in practice.** The tightest subsampled bounds are stated for integer α [3][8]; the continuous-α theory is elegant but the code uses integers, leaving a small discretization gap at the conversion optimum.

None of these limitations has displaced RDP in practice — TensorFlow Privacy, Opacus, and most DP-SGD deployments account in RDP — but they delineate where the next theoretical advances must go: adaptive order selection, shuffle-model accounting, and conversions that close the tail-bound gap.

##7 Conclusion

Rényi differential privacy turned privacy accounting from a combinatorial chore into arithmetic. By tracking the Rényi divergence across orders α, it made composition additive, subsampling amplification generic, and the moments accountant a theorem rather than a trick. The Gaussian mechanism's linear RDP curve, the WBK subsampling bound, and the optimal-α conversion together explain why modern DP-SGD achieves single-digit ε over ten thousand noisy steps — a feat that naive and advanced composition cannot even approximate. The framework's limitations — fixed order grids, lossy conversion, the Poisson idealization — are active research frontiers, not defects. For the working privacy engineer, the takeaway is simple: track the curve, add across steps, amplify by subsampling, and convert last. The budget will thank you.
