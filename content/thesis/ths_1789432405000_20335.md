# Inference as Computation: Bayesian Quadrature, Probabilistic Linear Algebra, and Filtering-Based ODE Solvers

## Abstract

Probabilistic numerics recasts numerical computation as statistical inference: a solver is not an oracle returning a point estimate, but an agent that quantifies its own epistemic uncertainty after finitely many function evaluations. This treatise develops the three canonical pillars of the field — Bayesian quadrature, probabilistic linear algebra, and filtering-based ordinary differential equation solvers — from first principles. We derive the closed-form Gaussian process posterior over integrals through kernel mean embeddings, show that its posterior variance coincides exactly with the worst-case quadrature error in the kernel's reproducing kernel Hilbert space, and prove convergence rates of order n raised to minus s over d in Sobolev spaces of smoothness s, matching minimax optimal deterministic rates. We then show that Bayesian conjugate gradients recover classical conjugate gradients as a posterior mean while appending a calibrated covariance, and that Gaussian ODE filters reframe Runge–Kutta integration as Kalman filtering with local order h to the q plus 1 and global order h to the q. ## 1 Introduction

Every numerical computation is an act of reasoning under resource constraints. Classical numerical analysis designs algorithms that return a single approximation, together with an asymptotic error bound — *the error behaves like* h to the p, *for sufficiently small* h. This is a statement about the algorithm's worst-case behavior over a function class, not a statement about the uncertainty remaining in *this particular* computation after *this particular* expenditure of flops. The distinction matters. When a quadrature routine, a linear solver, or an ODE integrator sits inside a larger statistical pipeline — a Bayesian inverse problem, a Markov chain Monte Carlo sampler, a model-evidence computation — the downstream inference cannot tell whether the upstream numerical error is negligible, dominant, or catastrophically biased. A point estimate carries no memory of its own fallibility.

Probabilistic numerics (PN) is the proposal that numerical methods should be designed as *inference procedures* [1]. One places a probability distribution over the unknown quantity of interest — the integrand, the solution vector of a linear system, the trajectory of a differential equation — interprets each evaluation of the mathematical object as *data*, and conditions the prior into a posterior. The posterior mean is a numerical estimate, but the posterior covariance is the prize: it is a principled, computable quantification of the uncertainty induced by finite computation. The field has three mature pillars, each mapping a classical workhorse onto its Bayesian counterpart:

1. **Bayesian quadrature (BQ)**, which puts a Gaussian process prior on the integrand and returns a posterior over the integral [3].
2. **Probabilistic linear solvers**, which put a prior on the solution of Ax equals b and recover conjugate gradients as a posterior mean [4][8].
3. **Filtering-based ODE solvers**, which model the solution and its derivatives as a Gauss–Markov process and integrate it by Kalman filtering [6][7].

What follows is a rigorous development of all three, with sharp convergence theory, calibration analysis, and applications.

## 2 Background

The intellectual ancestors of probabilistic numerics predate the name by half a century. Larkin's 1972 "Gaussian measure in Hilbert space and applications in numerical analysis" already computed quadrature weights as posterior means. Diaconis (1988) and O'Hagan (1991) independently revived Bayesian quadrature. What changed in the 2010s was *scale and intent*: machine learning made probabilistic thinking the lingua franca of data science, and Hennig, Osborne, and collaborators showed that classical algorithms — conjugate gradients, Runge–Kutta, quasi-Newton — are special cases of Bayesian inference under specific priors, collected in the monograph *Probabilistic Numerics: Computation as Machine Learning* [1].

The formal pattern shared by all PN methods is a four-tuple: a **prior** over the latent quantity, a **data model** describing what information a numerical evaluation reveals, a **likelihood** (usually exact, noise-free observation of a linear functional), and an **inference rule** (usually exact Gaussian conditioning). Let us fix notation used throughout. For quadrature, the target is Π(f), the integral of f against a probability measure Π on domain X. For linear systems, the target is x-star solving A x-star equals b with A symmetric positive definite. For ODEs, the target is the solution trajectory x(t) of the initial value problem x-dot equals f(x, t), x(0) equals x-zero.

A crucial design decision in every pillar is the prior's *mean function*, which encodes the solver's default belief, and its *covariance structure*, which encodes beliefs about smoothness. The convergence rate of the posterior mean is governed almost entirely by how well the prior's reproducing kernel Hilbert space (RKHS) matches the true regularity of the object being computed — a theme we return to in the misspecification analysis of Section 4.1.

## 3 Methodology

The methodological core of probabilistic numerics is *conjugate Gaussian inference on linear functionals of a latent function*. Suppose f is assigned a Gaussian process prior GP(m, k) and we observe the vector y of f evaluated at design points X-n equals the set of x-1 through x-n. The posterior is again a Gaussian process with mean and covariance

> **Theorem (GP conditioning):** m-n(x) equals m(x) plus k(x, X-n) K inverse (y minus m(X-n)), and k-n(x, x-prime) equals k(x, x-prime) minus k(x, X-n) K inverse k(X-n, x-prime), where K is the n by n Gram matrix with entries k(x-i, x-j).

Because integration is a linear functional, the posterior over the scalar Π(f) is a univariate Gaussian. Because solving a linear system is linear in the solution, a Gaussian prior on x-star yields Gaussian posteriors under linear observations. Because the ODE filtering prior is Gauss–Markov, conditioning on derivative information reduces to the Kalman recursions. In all three cases, exact Bayesian inference is *algebraically closed*, which is why the field could inherit the computational machinery of classical methods while attaching uncertainty estimates to their outputs.

A Python reference implementation of the Bayesian quadrature weight computation illustrates the pattern — inference reduces to one linear solve against the kernel Gram matrix:

```python
import numpy as np

def bayesian_quadrature_weights(X, k, kernel_mean, jitter=1e-10):
    """Posterior weights w = K^{-1} z for Bayesian quadrature.

    X            : (n, d) design points
    k            : kernel function k(x, x')
    kernel_mean  : z_i = int k(x_i, x) dPi(x), the kernel mean embedding
    returns      : weights w such that Pi[f] approx w^T f(X)
    """
    K = k(X, X) + jitter * np.eye(len(X))
    z = kernel_mean(X)                       # embedding of Pi at the nodes
    w = np.linalg.solve(K, z)                # posterior mean weights
    pi_pi_k = kernel_mean_of_kernel_mean(k)  # Pi[Pi[k(.,.)]]
    var = pi_pi_k - z @ w                    # worst-case error in H(k)
    return w, var
```

The returned variance is not an approximation: for the GP prior, it equals the *squared worst-case integration error* over the unit ball of the RKHS, a duality we prove in Section 4.1.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Bayesian Quadrature and Kernel Mean Embeddings

Bayesian quadrature starts from f distributed as GP(0, k) — the zero-mean convention is without loss of generality after centering — and observes y equals f(X-n). The posterior over the integral Z equals Π(f) is

> **Theorem (BQ posterior):** Z given D-n is Gaussian with mean μ-n equals z-n transpose K-n inverse y and variance V-n equals ΠΠ(k) minus z-n transpose K-n inverse z-n, where z-n is the vector of kernel mean embeddings z-i equals ∫ k(x-i, x) dΠ(x) and ΠΠ(k) is the double integral of k against Π in both arguments [3].

The object z-n deserves its name: the map x ↦ ∫ k(x, ·) dΠ is the *kernel mean embedding* of Π into the RKHS, and BQ weights are its orthogonal projection onto the span of the kernel sections at the design points. This yields the celebrated *worst-case error identity*:

- The posterior standard deviation √V-n equals the worst-case error of the quadrature rule with weights w-n over the unit ball of H(k), the RKHS of k.
- Equivalently, V-n equals the squared maximum mean discrepancy between Π and the weighted empirical measure Σ w-i δ-x-i, with kernel k.

Convergence theory for BQ is now sharp.

Sequential Bayesian quadrature (SBQ) closes the loop to *active learning*: choosing each new node x-n-plus-1 to minimize the expected posterior variance turns integration into a decision problem.

### 4.2 Probabilistic Linear Solvers and BayesCG

The conjugate gradient (CG) method solves A x-star equals b by building A-orthogonal search directions and minimizing the A-norm of the error over expanding Krylov subspaces.

- **Prior:** x-star distributed as Gaussian with mean x-zero and covariance Σ-zero.
- **Data:** linear observations y-j equals s-j transpose b equals s-j transpose A x-star along search directions s-j.
- **Posterior:** Gaussian, with mean x-m equal to the m-th CG iterate for a specific *Krylov prior* choice of Σ-zero, and covariance Σ-m encoding the residual uncertainty.

The algorithmic miracle is that the posterior remains computable in O(m) matrix-vector products: constructing search directions that are orthonormal in the A Σ-zero A-transpose inner product diagonalizes the observation operator, so conditioning reduces to scalar updates [8].

A Rust-flavored sketch of the BayesCG iteration exposes how little changes relative to classical CG — the mean update is identical; the covariance update is the addition:

```rust
// BayesCG: posterior mean update coincides with CG; covariance tracks uncertainty
struct BayesCGState {
    x: Vec<f64>,        // posterior mean (== CG iterate under Krylov prior)
    sigma: Matrix,      // posterior covariance factor
    s: Vec<Vec<f64>>,   // A Sigma0 A^T-orthonormal search directions
}

fn bayes_cg_step(state: &mut BayesCGState, a: &Matrix, b: &Vec<f64>) {
    let r = b.sub(&a.mul_vec(&state.x));          // residual
    let s_tilde = r.sub(&state.s.last().unwrap()
        .scale(inner(&r, state.s.last().unwrap(), a, &state.sigma)));
    let s = s_tilde.normalize_a_sigma(a, &state.sigma);
    let y = s.dot(&r);                            // observation s^T b
    let v = a.mul_vec(&s);                        // one mat-vec, as in CG
    let alpha = y / s.dot(&v);
    state.x = state.x.add(&s.scale(alpha));       // mean: identical to CG
    state.sigma = state.sigma.rank_one_downdate(&s, alpha); // covariance shrinks
    state.s.push(s);
}
```

Convergence matches the classical theory: under the Krylov prior the posterior mean contracts geometrically in the A-norm at the CG rate, and the posterior covariance contracts in the Löwner order — uncertainty can only decrease as data arrive [4].

### 4.3 Filtering-Based ODE Solvers and the Kalman Formulation

Filtering-based ODE solvers attack the initial value problem x-dot equals f(x, t) by placing a Gauss–Markov prior on the stacked state X(t) equals (x(t), x-dot(t), …, x to the q-th derivative (t)) — typically a q-times integrated Wiener process — and then *conditioning on the information that the true solution satisfies the ODE* [6].

1. **Predicts** X(t-n) from the previous posterior via the linear SDE transition of the prior (the Kalman prediction step).
2. **Evaluates** the vector field at the predicted mean, forming the "measurement" that the residual I(Y)(t) equals x-dot(t) minus f(x(t), t) should be zero.
3. **Updates** the state with the Kalman gain, since the measurement is (after linearization) linear in X.

The linearization of f determines the flavor: **EK0** uses a zeroth-order approximation (Jacobian replaced by zero), **EK1** uses the full Jacobian — a first-order Taylor approximation amounting to the extended Kalman filter — and the **diagonal EK1** uses only the Jacobian's diagonal, preserving EK0's efficiency while gaining stability [7].

The convergence theory, due to Kersting, Sullivan, and Hennig, is the strongest calibration result in the field [6]:

> **Theorem (Gaussian ODE filter rates):** For a q-times integrated Wiener process prior, the filtering mean converges locally at order h to the q plus 1 and globally at order h to the q (proven for q equals 1, with numerical evidence for higher q). Moreover, in the globally convergent case the posterior credible intervals are *well calibrated*: they contract globally at exactly the same rate as the truncation error.

The last clause is the crown jewel — it is a theorem, not an empirical observation, that the solver's self-reported uncertainty shrinks at the true error's rate.

### 4.4 Calibration of Numerical Uncertainty

A PN method is *calibrated* when its posterior uncertainty is statistically consistent with its actual error.

1. **Contraction at the right rate** — the posterior standard deviation shrinks like the true error as computation increases. Achieved by BQ in Sobolev settings [2] and by Gaussian ODE filters [6].
2. **Frequentist coverage** — credible intervals contain the truth at their nominal rate under repeated sampling of the prior. BayesCG satisfies only weakened versions of this, testable via the Z- and S-statistics [5].
3. **Strong calibration** — the posterior is correct in the Bayesian sense when the prior is correct. This holds by construction but is vacuous if the prior is misspecified, which motivates the misspecification theory of Section 4.1.

Calibration is tuned in practice through the prior's *output scale* γ, a scalar multiplying the diffusion of the Gauss–Markov prior (or the kernel amplitude in BQ).

| Method | Posterior mean rate | Uncertainty rate | Calibration status |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bayesian quadrature (Sobolev s, dim d) | n^(−s/d), minimax optimal | contracts at n^(−s/d) | strong under correct prior; adaptive to misspecification [2] |
| BayesCG (Krylov prior) | geometric, matches CG | Löwner contraction | slightly optimistic; Z/S-statistic validated [5] |
| Gaussian ODE filter (order q) | local h^(q+1), global h^q | contracts at h^q | provably well calibrated [6] |
| Multilevel BQ | beats MLMC at fixed budget | level-wise posteriors | empirical [9] |

### 4.5 Inverse Problems and Active Learning

The payoff of uncertainty-aware numerics appears when the solver is embedded in a larger inference.

In **active learning of model evidence**, Osborne and colleagues used Bayesian quadrature to compute marginal likelihoods, selecting evaluation points to maximally reduce posterior variance — the same acquisition principle as SBQ, now spending computation where the *evidence integral* is most uncertain rather than where the integrand is largest [3].

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

The theoretical rates above are not merely asymptotic folklore; they are confirmed by the standard benchmark suites of the field.

**Bayesian quadrature.** On the Genz test-function family — the classical torture suite for cubature — BQ with Matérn kernels exhibits the predicted n to the minus s over d decay in log-log error plots, with the posterior standard deviation tracking the empirical error across two orders of magnitude of n [3]. The multilevel variant MLBQ on a Poisson-equation benchmark achieves, at a fixed wall-clock budget, errors an order of magnitude below multilevel Monte Carlo, because the fast BQ rate makes a handful of high-fidelity evaluations disproportionately valuable [9]. Misspecification experiments confirm adaptivity: a kernel smoother than the integrand still recovers the integrand's own optimal rate once the design is quasi-uniform [2].

**Probabilistic linear solvers.** On the medical-imaging deconvolution problem studied by Cockayne et al., BayesCG's posterior mean matches CG iterate-for-iterate while the posterior covariance provides error bars that, under the Z-statistic, are only mildly optimistic [4][5]. The unifying view of Wenger et al. further shows that classical preconditioners slot into the framework as prior or observation transformations, so existing HPC practice is preserved rather than replaced [8].

**ODE filters.** Kersting, Sullivan, and Hennig verified the h to the q plus 1 local and h to the q global rates on stiff and non-stiff test problems, and — critically — verified that the posterior standard deviation decays at the same rate as the measured global error, confirming the calibration theorem empirically [6]. Bosch et al. demonstrated the diagonal EK1 on a discretized PDE with millions of dimensions, where the probabilistic solver runs at the speed of its classical counterpart while returning calibrated uncertainty [7].

*Proof sketch of the worst-case error identity (BQ).* For f in H(k) with unit norm, the quadrature error Π(f) minus w transpose f(X-n) is the inner product of f with the *representer of the error functional*, namely the function e(x) equals ∫ k(x, ·) dΠ minus Σ w-i k(x-i, ·). By Cauchy–Schwarz the worst-case error over the unit ball equals the RKHS norm of e, which expands to ΠΠ(k) minus 2 z transpose w plus w transpose K w. Minimizing over w gives w equals K inverse z and the minimum value ΠΠ(k) minus z transpose K inverse z — exactly the BQ posterior variance. ∎

## 6 Limitations

Probabilistic numerics is not a free lunch, and its limitations are by now well mapped.

- **Cubic algebra.** Exact GP inference costs O(n cubed) in the number of evaluations, which caps BQ at a few thousand nodes without inducing-point or conjugate-gradient approximations — ironic, since the latter is itself a PN method. High-dimensional integration (d beyond roughly 10) defeats kernel methods through the usual curse: the rate n to the minus s over d degrades, and the posterior variance becomes uninformative [9].
- **Prior sensitivity.** All guarantees are conditional on the prior. A badly chosen kernel amplitude or diffusion scale yields miscalibrated uncertainty, and while quasi-MLE tuning of the output scale helps, it is itself an estimation problem with finite data [7]. Strong calibration is a theorem about the model, not about the world.
- **Nonlinearity and non-Gaussianity.** The exactness of the framework rests on linear observations of Gaussian priors. Nonlinear PDEs, inequality constraints, and multimodal posteriors force linearizations (EK1) or approximations (Laplace, variational) whose errors the posterior does not account for — the uncertainty is then about the *linearized* problem.
- **Software and adoption overhead.** Classical solvers are entrenched in optimized libraries; probabilistic counterparts (e.g., ProbNum) are younger, and the per-iteration overhead of covariance bookkeeping, while asymptotically negligible, is real at small scale.
- **Calibration gaps.** BayesCG's slight optimism under the S-statistic [5] and the underconfidence induced by unbounded kernels in discrete-space BQ [9] show that honest uncertainty is not automatic — it must be tested, not assumed.

## 7 Conclusion

Probabilistic numerics completes a conceptual circle: the algorithms of classical numerical analysis — quadrature rules, conjugate gradients, Runge–Kutta integrators — are revealed as *maximum a posteriori estimates* under specific priors, and the Bayesian view supplies the missing object, a posterior covariance that quantifies the error of finite computation. The theory surveyed here shows this is not a vague philosophical rebranding but a source of sharp results: minimax-optimal and smoothness-adaptive quadrature rates [2], geometric contraction matching CG with testable calibration [4][5], and ODE filters whose credible intervals provably contract at the truncation-error rate [6], scaling to millions of dimensions [7].

The research frontier is clear. First, *nonlinear and PDE-constrained* probabilistic solvers that retain honest uncertainty through linearization. Second, *scalable* inference — inducing points, Krylov-accelerated GP algebra — that pushes BQ past its cubic bottleneck. Third, *decision-theoretic* numerics: solvers that do not merely report uncertainty but actively spend computation to minimize downstream risk, unifying numerical analysis with Bayesian experimental design. When computation itself is the experiment, inference is the only honest accounting.
