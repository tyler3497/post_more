# Privileged Learning and Online Adaptation for Agile Quadruped Locomotion: A Unified Treatment of Teacher–Student Distillation, Massively Parallel Deep Reinforcement Learning, and Sim-to-Real Transfer

## Abstract

Agile legged locomotion over unstructured terrain has transitioned from a showcase of hand-engineered control to a problem routinely solved by learned policies. This thesis develops a unified mathematical and engineering account of the modern paradigm: *privileged teacher training*, *online adaptation*, *massively parallel simulation*, and *sim-to-real transfer via domain randomization*. We formalize the locomotion task as a partially observable Markov decision process with latent environment factors, derive the teacher–student distillation objective used by contemporary blind and perceptive controllers, and analyze Rapid Motor Adaptation (RMA) as supervised system identification of an extrinsics embedding. We then examine how GPU-resident simulation — thousands of robots stepping in lockstep on a single workstation — compressed wall-clock training from days to minutes, and how game-inspired curricula and bioenergetics-style reward shaping induce emergent gaits without trajectory references. We survey empirical evidence from the ANYmal lineage through Unitree platforms, including rocky, deformable, and perceptive locomotion results, and close with a treatment of safety-constrained deployment, the irreducible sim-to-real residual, and open directions in whole-body agility. The synthesis argues that adaptation, not precision modeling, is the decisive quantity for real-world deployment.

## 1. Introduction

The quadruped locomotion problem is deceptively simple to state and brutally difficult to solve. A legged robot must keep a twelve- or sixteen-joint floating base upright while rejecting contact discontinuities, terrain variation, actuator saturation, and communication latency — all at control frequencies of 50–200 Hz. Classical pipelines addressed this through a separation of concerns: reduced-order models such as the *linear inverted pendulum* or the *spring-loaded inverted pendulum* for center-of-mass planning, quadratic-programming whole-body controllers for torque distribution, and state estimators fusing IMU and leg odometry [4]. These systems achieved remarkable feats, but each new terrain class demanded fresh engineering.

Deep reinforcement learning (RL) reframed the problem. Instead of decomposing locomotion into planner, tracker, and estimator, a single neural network maps sensor observations directly to joint commands, optimized end-to-end against a reward signal. The key enabler was not a new algorithm — the community largely converged on Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) and its trust-region cousins — but a new *training regime*: teachers with privileged information, students constrained to deployable observations, and simulators fast enough to collect billions of transitions before lunch [1][3].

This thesis argues that the modern locomotion stack is best understood as the composition of four ideas:

1. **Privileged teacher–student distillation.** A teacher policy trained with access to ground-truth environment parameters (friction, payload, terrain heightfield) defines near-optimal behavior; a student, restricted to proprioception or exteroception available on hardware, is distilled to match it [2].
2. **Online adaptation as inference.** Rapid Motor Adaptation (RMA) factorizes the student into a base policy and an adaptation module that regresses an *extrinsics vector* from short proprioceptive histories, achieving sub-second adaptation to unseen conditions without fine-tuning [2][6].
3. **Massively parallel GPU simulation.** Isaac Gym and its descendants run physics, observations, rewards, and the learner on the same GPU, eliminating CPU bottlenecks and training thousands of robots concurrently; flat-ground walking policies emerged in under four minutes of wall-clock time [1][3].
4. **Domain randomization and sim-to-real discipline.** Rather than closing the reality gap with better models, policies are trained over *distributions* of models, producing controllers robust to the residual mismatch at deployment [4].

The remainder of this work formalizes each component, connects them through the language of partially observable Markov decision processes (POMDPs), and evaluates the empirical record.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 Legged locomotion as a POMDP

We model locomotion as a discrete-time POMDP $\mathcal{M} = \langle \mathcal{S}, \mathcal{A}, \mathcal{O}, \mathcal{T}, \mathcal{R}, \gamma \rangle$ where the hidden state $s_t$ contains the robot's true configuration, velocities, contact states, *and* latent environment factors $e_t$ (terrain geometry, friction coefficients, actuator degradation, payload). The observation $o_t$ available at deployment is strictly proprioceptive for blind locomotion — joint positions and velocities, base orientation and angular velocity, and recent action history — or augmented with exteroceptive depth and LiDAR for perceptive locomotion [5].

The control objective is the discounted return:

$$J(\pi) = \mathbb{E}_{\pi}\left[\sum_{t=0}^{\infty} \gamma^t r(s_t, a_t)\right], \quad 0 < \gamma < 1.$$

The central difficulty is that $e_t$ is *not* observable at deployment, yet strongly influences the dynamics $\mathcal{T}(s_{t+1} \mid s_t, a_t; e_t)$. Any policy that ignores $e_t$ must be conservative; any policy that conditions on it must *infer* it.

> **Theorem (Privileged Value Bound):** Let $\pi^\star_e$ be the optimal policy with access to environment factors $e$, and $\hat{\pi}$ any policy restricted to observations $o$. Then $J(\pi^\star_e) - J(\hat{\pi}) \ge 0$, with equality iff $o$ is a sufficient statistic for $e$ under the task distribution. *Proof sketch:* conditioning reduces entropy; the optimal deployable policy can do no better than the Bayes-optimal estimator composed with the privileged optimum, and the estimation gap is exactly the expected value of lost information.

This inequality motivates the entire teacher–student program: minimize the estimation gap by training a student to *approximate* privileged behavior from observable histories.

### 2.2 Policy gradients in the massively parallel regime

PPO remains the workhorse. With $N$ parallel environments each collecting $T$ steps per iteration, the surrogate objective is

$$L^{\mathrm{CLIP}}(\theta) = \hat{\mathbb{E}}_t\left[\min\left(r_t(\theta)\hat{A}_t,\ \mathrm{clip}(r_t(\theta), 1-\epsilon, 1+\epsilon)\hat{A}_t\right)\right],$$

where $r_t(\theta) = \pi_\theta(a_t \mid o_t)/\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}(a_t \mid o_t)$. In the massively parallel regime ($N$ in the thousands), batch sizes reach hundreds of thousands of transitions per update, which changes the effective geometry of optimization: value-function learning stabilizes, advantage estimates decorrelate across environments, and the trust region constraint becomes the binding factor rather than sample noise [1].

### 2.3 A brief lineage of learned legged locomotion

| Year | System | Key contribution | Platform |
|------|--------|------------------|----------|
| 2020 | Blind teacher–student locomotion | Proprioceptive student distilled from privileged teacher; rough-terrain blind walking | ANYmal [4] |

This lineage is cumulative rather than competitive: each row inherits the training machinery of the last.

## 3. Methodology

### 3.1 Privileged teacher training

The teacher $\pi_T(a_t \mid o_t, e_t)$ is trained with full access to the environment factor vector $e_t \in \mathbb{R}^m$, which may include per-foot friction, restitution, terrain height samples under each foot, payload mass, center-of-mass offset, motor strength multipliers, and latency parameters. Because $e_t$ disambiguates the dynamics, the teacher's learning problem is nearly fully observed, and PPO converges rapidly to high-quality behavior.

### 3.2 Student distillation

The student $\pi_S(a_t \mid o_t)$ (or $\pi_S(a_t \mid h_t)$ for history $h_t = o_{t-k:t}$) is trained by supervised regression against the teacher's actions on trajectories collected by the student itself — a dataset-aggregation (DAgger) style procedure:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{student}} = \mathbb{E}_{(o,e)\sim \mathcal{D}}\left[\left\|\pi_S(o) - \pi_T(o, e)\right\|_2^2\right].$$

Because the student rolls out its own trajectories, the training distribution matches the deployment distribution up to the sim-to-real gap, mitigating compounding errors that plague pure behavioral cloning.

### 3.3 Rapid Motor Adaptation

RMA introduces an elegant factorization [2]. Instead of distilling the teacher into a monolithic student, it trains:

- a **base policy** $\pi_B(a_t \mid o_t, z_t)$ conditioned on a latent *extrinsics vector* $z_t = \mu(e_t)$ produced by an environment encoder $\mu$;
- an **adaptation module** $\phi(h_t)$ that regresses $z_t$ from a short history $h_t$ of proprioceptive observations and actions.

Phase 1 trains $\pi_B$ and $\mu$ jointly by RL with privileged $e_t$. Phase 2 trains $\phi$ by supervised learning:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{adapt}} = \mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\phi(h_t) - \mu(e_t)\right\|_2^2\right].$$

At deployment, $\pi_B(\cdot \mid o_t, \phi(h_t))$ adapts within fractions of a second to novel terrain, payload, or degradation, because $\phi$ performs *online system identification* in the compressed extrinsics space rather than in raw parameter space.

### 3.4 Domain randomization

Sim-to-real transfer is framed as robustness over a distribution of environments:

$$J_{\mathrm{DR}}(\pi) = \mathbb{E}_{e \sim P(\mathcal{E})}\left[J_e(\pi)\right],$$

where $P(\mathcal{E})$ randomizes masses, inertias, friction, restitution, joint damping and friction, PD gains, sensor noise, latency, and terrain geometry each episode. The policy maximizes *expected* return across the distribution, so the deployed robot — one more draw from a nearby distribution — inherits the guarantee in expectation [4].

### 3.5 Reward design for gait emergence

Rather than prescribing foot trajectories, modern rewards combine task tracking with *bioenergetics-inspired* regularization [2]:

```python
def locomotion_reward(state, cmd, dt):
    r = 0.0
    # task: track commanded linear/angular velocity
    r += 1.5 * exp(-((cmd.vx - state.vx)**2 + (cmd.vy - state.vy)**2) / 0.25)
    r += 0.5 * exp(-((cmd.yaw - state.yaw_rate)**2) / 0.25)
    # efficiency: penalize torque and joint velocity
    r -= 0.01 * sum(tau**2 for tau in state.torques)
    r -= 0.001 * sum(dq**2 for dq in state.joint_vel)
    # gait quality: reward foot air time, penalize slip
    r += 0.5 * state.mean_foot_air_time
    r -= 0.3 * state.foot_slip_velocity
    # stability: penalize base tilt and vertical oscillation
    r -= 0.5 * (state.roll**2 + state.pitch**2)
    r -= 0.2 * abs(state.vz)
    # survival bonus
    r += 0.1
    return r * dt
```

Gaits — walk, trot, bound, gallop — *emerge* as energy-optimal solutions under these penalties, with gait transitions appearing as commanded speed increases. No reference motion is required.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 The extrinsics vector as a learned sufficient statistic

The environment encoder $\mu: \mathcal{E} \to \mathbb{R}^d$ (typically $d \approx 8$) compresses dozens of physical parameters into a compact latent. Empirically, the coordinates of $z$ correlate with interpretable quantities — one axis tracks effective ground friction, another tracks payload — but the network is free to discover whatever factorization the control task demands. This is *system identification by gradient descent*: the latent is optimized so that conditioning on it maximizes return, which is precisely the definition of a sufficient statistic for the control-relevant aspects of $e$.

The adaptation module $\phi$ then solves an inverse problem: given the recent sensorimotor stream, recover $z$. Its 1–2 second receptive field suffices because contact-rich locomotion is highly informative — foot slip, unexpected sinkage, and torque transients all betray the environment within a few strides [2][6].

### 4.2 Why massive parallelism changed the science

Before GPU-resident simulation, a single locomotion experiment consumed days of CPU-cluster time, which meant hyperparameters were inherited, curricula were timid, and ablations were unaffordable. Isaac Gym removed the CPU from the loop: physics buffers are exposed directly as PyTorch tensors, so observations, rewards, and gradients never leave the device [3]. Rudin et al. demonstrated 4096 ANYmal instances training concurrently, with a game-inspired curriculum that promotes robots to harder terrain when their traversal reward crosses a threshold and demotes them on failure [1].

The scientific consequence was a shift from *algorithm* research to *regime* research. In the massively parallel setting, the authors found that:

- **Minibatch size dominates.** Larger batches stabilize the value function and permit higher learning rates; small batches in the parallel regime underperform.
- **Curriculum structure matters more than reward tuning.** The game-inspired promotion/demotion scheme outperformed hand-designed difficulty schedules.
- **Asymmetric actor–critic helps.** Giving the critic privileged information ($e_t$) while the actor sees only $o_t$ accelerates teacher training without changing deployment requirements.

Training a flat-ground policy in under four minutes and a rough-terrain policy in twenty minutes on a single workstation GPU compressed the experimental loop by orders of magnitude and effectively democratized locomotion research [1].

### 4.3 Blind versus perceptive locomotion

*Blind* locomotion uses proprioception only. The student learns to probe the terrain with its feet — stepping, slipping slightly, adjusting — in a closed sensorimotor loop reminiscent of human walking in the dark. Blind policies traverse stairs, rubble, and deformable surfaces surprisingly well, because the adaptation module continuously re-estimates $z_t$ [2][4].

*Perceptive* locomotion adds exteroception: height maps in simulation, depth images or LiDAR on hardware. The teacher sees the terrain ahead; the student must infer a perception latent from noisy onboard sensing, typically via CNN encoders and recurrent fusion, distilled with DAgger and truncated backpropagation through time [5]. Perception enables *anticipatory* control — placing feet before contact, jumping gaps — at the cost of a harder distillation problem and sensitivity to the sim-to-real gap in *rendering* as well as physics.

### 4.4 Safety-constrained deployment

Learned policies are deployed with layered safeguards:

1. **Action filtering.** Joint commands pass through low-pass filters and rate limiters; torque and position limits are enforced in firmware.
2. **Fallback controllers.** A model-based standing or damping controller takes over when anomaly detectors (e.g., base orientation beyond a threshold) trigger.
3. **Runtime teacher–student safety.** Recent frameworks pair a learned student with a verifiable model-based teacher that intervenes near safety boundaries and provides corrective demonstrations, blending performance with guarantees.
4. **Constrained RL.** Safety can be encoded directly as constraints $\mathbb{E}[\sum_t c_i(s_t,a_t)] \le d_i$ solved via Lagrangian relaxation, though tuning the dual variables at scale remains delicate.

These layers acknowledge a core truth: a policy is only as trustworthy as its *out-of-distribution* behavior, and no amount of randomization covers the true tail [4].

## 5. Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 Quantitative record

The empirical claims of the paradigm rest on a consistent set of results:

- **Training speed.** Rudin et al. report flat-terrain ANYmal policies in under 4 minutes and rough-terrain policies in 20 minutes on a single workstation GPU with 4096 parallel environments, a multi-order-of-magnitude speedup over CPU-based RL [1].
- **Zero-shot transfer.** RMA, trained entirely in simulation on a varied terrain generator, deployed on a Unitree A1 across sand, mud, tall grass, pebbles, stairs, and slippery surfaces with no real-world fine-tuning, succeeding in 100% of trials on most terrains and 70–80% on stairs and rubble piles [2][6].
- **Adaptation latency.** The RMA adaptation module converges to accurate extrinsics estimates within approximately 2 seconds of deployment on novel terrain, fast enough to catch a stumble mid-stride [2].
- **High-speed agility.** Perceptive multi-skill systems now exceed 4 m/s outdoors with fused depth and LiDAR, planning gaits at 2 Hz with lookahead beyond 4 m [5].
- **Compute scaling.** Isaac Gym reports 2–3 orders of magnitude end-to-end training speedup over CPU-simulator-plus-GPU-learner pipelines on a single GPU [3].

### 5.2 What the ablations prove

Three ablations recur across papers and jointly constitute the paradigm's evidence base:

1. **Removing privileged information** (training the student directly by RL without a teacher) degrades sample efficiency and final performance on rough terrain, confirming the value of the distillation factorization [4].
2. **Removing the adaptation module** (deploying the base policy with a fixed or zero extrinsics vector) collapses performance on out-of-distribution terrain, confirming that online inference — not just a robust policy — carries the sim-to-real load [2].
3. **Removing domain randomization** yields policies that walk beautifully in simulation and fall immediately on hardware, confirming that the reality gap is a *distributional* problem requiring distributional training [4].

> **Theorem (Randomization Coverage):** Suppose the real environment $e_{\mathrm{real}}$ lies in the convex hull of the support of $P(\mathcal{E})$, and the policy class is rich enough to achieve near-optimal return uniformly over $\mathrm{supp}(P)$. Then the deployed suboptimality is bounded by the uniform approximation error plus the distance from $e_{\mathrm{real}}$ to the hull. *Implication:* widening the randomization distribution is principled only insofar as it covers reality without diluting learnability — the central tension of domain randomization.

## 6. Limitations

The paradigm's successes should not obscure its boundaries.

- **The extrinsics bottleneck.** RMA-style adaptation assumes the environment factors relevant at deployment were *spanned* during training. A qualitatively novel disturbance — a torn actuator cable, a foot caught in wire — produces an extrinsics vector outside the encoder's manifold, and the base policy's response is undefined.
- **Reward hacking at scale.** With billions of transitions, policies reliably discover reward misspecifications: vibrating in place to accumulate air-time bonuses, exploiting contact solver tolerances, or leaning on invisible simulator walls. Reward design remains an art, and each new behavior requires re-tuning.
- **Perception sim-to-real.** Rendering gaps (depth noise, LiDAR ray patterns, lighting) are harder to randomize faithfully than physics parameters, which is why blind locomotion remains the more reliable deployment choice in unstructured settings [5].
- **No formal safety.** Distilled policies carry no certificates; the safety layers of Section 4.4 are engineering practice, not proof. Constrained RL and runtime shielding are active research precisely because this gap is unresolved.
- **Energy and hardware wear.** Learned gaits optimized for simulated energy proxies may still be harsh on real actuators; long-duration deployment studies remain rare.

## 7. Conclusion

Learned quadruped locomotion converged on a coherent engineering doctrine: train a privileged teacher, distill it into a deployable student, factor the student through an online adaptation module, and do all of it inside a GPU-resident simulator with thousands of parallel robots under aggressive domain randomization. Each element addresses a distinct facet of the same underlying problem — the unobservability of environment factors at deployment — and their composition has carried robots from laboratory floors to hiking trails, construction rubble, and deformable natural terrain without a single line of terrain-specific code.

The deeper lesson is methodological. The field's progress over 2020–2026 tracks *training regimes* more closely than it tracks algorithms: PPO was a constant while parallelism, curricula, privileged learning, and adaptation machinery did the moving. Future advances — whole-body loco-manipulation, humanoid agility, certifiable safety — will likely follow the same pattern: not a better optimizer, but a better factorization of what is known in simulation, what must be inferred online, and what can only be survived.
