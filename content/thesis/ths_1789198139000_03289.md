# Boson Sampling and the Photonic Road to Quantum Computational Advantage: From Aaronson–Arkhipov Hardness to Gaussian Boson Sampling, Jiuzhang, and the Classical Spoofing Wars

## Abstract

Boson sampling, proposed by Aaronson and Arkhipov in 2011, offered one of the first concrete, complexity-theoretic arguments that a non-universal, passive linear-optical device could outperform classical computation at a well-defined sampling task [1]. The hardness rests on the link between output probabilities of interfering indistinguishable photons and the matrix permanent, a \#P-complete function, with approximate hardness supported by the permanent-of-Gaussians and permanent anti-concentration conjectures. Gaussian boson sampling (GBS) replaced fragile single-photon sources with squeezed states, shifting the relevant matrix function to the hafnian while preserving conjectured hardness and experimental feasibility [4]. The USTC Jiuzhang experiments (2020–2026) scaled detected photons from 76 to beyond 3000 coincidence events, claiming speedups of $10^{14}$ to $10^{54}$ over classical simulation [3][8]. Parallel classical progress—exact samplers in $O(n2^n)$ time [2], Metropolis-independence sampling [5], tensor-network and MPS contractions, and physically motivated "squashed state" spoofing models [7]—repeatedly reset the threshold for genuine advantage. This thesis reconstructs the theory, surveys the experimental milestones (Jiuzhang 1.0–4.0, Xanadu's Borealis), dissects the spoofing attacks, and analyzes the noise models and verification protocols delimiting what these photonic machines actually prove.

## 1 Intro

In 2011, Scott Aaronson and Alex Arkhipov proposed a computational model so stripped down it barely deserves the name "computer": take $n$ identical photons, send them through a fixed $m$-mode linear-optical network, and count how many arrive at each output mode [1]. No adaptive measurement, no feedback, no error correction, no universality—just beamsplitters, phase shifters, and detectors. Their claim was that *even this* could not be efficiently simulated by any classical computer, unless the polynomial hierarchy collapses to the third level. The result electrified both communities it touched. For physicists, it meant a near-term optical experiment might falsify the extended Church–Turing thesis without building a universal quantum computer. For complexity theorists, it meant quantum advantage could be argued without assuming a full fault-tolerant architecture.

The crucial move was to tie the output statistics of a linear-optical network to a famously hard object in classical computer science: the **matrix permanent**. While the determinant is computable in polynomial time by Gaussian elimination, the permanent—a sum over all permutations with all-positive signs—is \#P-complete even to approximate. Aaronson and Arkhipov showed that the probability of observing a particular photon-occupation pattern at the output is proportional to the squared modulus of the permanent of an $n \times n$ submatrix of the network's unitary transfer matrix [1].

> **Theorem:** *If there exists a polynomial-time classical algorithm that samples from the output distribution of an $n$-photon, $m$-mode linear-optical network with total-variation error bounded away from 1, then, assuming the Permanent-of-Gaussians and Permanent Anti-Concentration conjectures, the polynomial hierarchy collapses to the third level [1].*

Fock-state boson sampling proved experimentally brutal: generating $n$ simultaneous indistinguishable single photons with high fidelity remains hard even today. The community therefore pivoted to **Gaussian boson sampling (GBS)**, introduced by Hamilton et al. [4], which replaces single-photon guns with squeezed-light sources—bright and far more tractable. The cost is a new matrix function, the **hafnian**, also \#P-complete, governing the output probabilities of squeezed inputs. GBS became the platform for the first credible claims of quantum computational advantage: USTC's Jiuzhang series (2020–2026) and Xanadu's Borealis (2022), with photon numbers climbing from dozens to thousands and claimed speedups from $10^{14}$ to $10^{54}$ [3][6][8].

But no advantage claim survives unchallenged. Classical algorithms evolved in lockstep: Clifford and Clifford's $O(n2^n)$ exact sampler [2], Neville et al.'s Metropolis-independence sampler [5], tensor-network and matrix-product-state (MPS) contractions exploiting photon loss, and the *squashed states* spoofing model, which argues Jiuzhang 1.0's samples are better explained by a classical mixture of coherent states than by the targeted squeezed-light model [7]. Each attack forced sharper thinking about *verification*: how does one certify that a photonic device samples the distribution it claims, when verifying even a single probability requires computing a \#P-hard function?

This thesis proceeds as follows. Section 2 reviews the Aaronson–Arkhipov model and its complexity-theoretic foundations. Section 3 lays out the methodological spine: permanents, hafnians, Ryser's algorithm, and the classical sampling machinery used both to simulate and to attack. Section 4 dives into GBS theory, the Jiuzhang experimental lineage, verification protocols, and the spoofing debates. Section 5 assembles the quantitative milestones and the simulation thresholds into a single empirical narrative. Section 6 confronts limitations—noise, verifiability, and the non-falsifiable core of approximate sampling. Section 7 concludes.

## 2 Background

### The Aaronson–Arkhipov model

The model begins with a multi-mode Fock state:

$$|\psi_{\text{in}}\rangle = |1_1, \dots, 1_n, 0_{n+1}, \dots, 0_m\rangle,$$

i.e., one photon in each of the first $n$ of $m$ modes. A passive linear-optical network implements an $m \times m$ unitary $U$ acting on the creation operators, $a_i^\dagger \mapsto \sum_j U_{ij}\, a_j^\dagger$. Photon-number measurements at the output yield an occupation vector $S = (s_1, \dots, s_m)$ with $\sum_i s_i = n$. The probability of outcome $S$ is

$$\Pr[S] = \frac{|\operatorname{Per}(U_S)|^2}{s_1! \cdots s_m!},$$

where $U_S$ is the $n \times n$ submatrix of $U$ formed by taking the first $n$ columns and repeating row $i$ exactly $s_i$ times [1].

For the **collision-free** regime ($m = \Omega(n^2)$), the formula simplifies to $\Pr[S] = |\operatorname{Per}(U_S)|^2$. Quadratic mode scaling is essential: it makes random submatrices of $U$ behave like matrices of i.i.d. complex Gaussians, connecting physical instances to the hardness conjectures.

### Complexity foundations

The hardness argument is a two-layer construction:

1. **Exact sampling is hard.** Suppose a classical randomized algorithm samples *exactly* from the boson sampler's distribution. Then, using Stockmeyer's approximate counting theorem (which allows a $\text{BPP}^{\text{NP}}$ machine to approximate \#P functions), one can estimate $|\operatorname{Per}(X)|^2$ for Gaussian $X$ in $\text{BPP}^{\text{NP}}$. Since approximating such permanents is \#P-hard (under the Permanent-of-Gaussians Conjecture), this yields $\text{P}^{\#\text{P}} = \text{BPP}^{\text{NP}}$ and the polynomial hierarchy collapses to the third level [1].

2. **Approximate sampling is hard (conjecturally).** The exact result assumes an impossibly accurate simulation. To argue that even noisy, approximate sampling is classically hard, Aaronson and Arkhipov introduce two conjectures: the **Permanent-of-Gaussians Conjecture** (\#P-hardness of approximating the permanent of a Gaussian matrix) and the **Permanent Anti-Concentration Conjecture** ($|\operatorname{Per}(X)| \ge \sqrt{n!}/\mathrm{poly}(n)$ with high probability, so that a sampling oracle's outputs carry information about large permanents). Both remain open [1].

The permanent's hardness is best understood through Valiant's 1979 theorem: computing $\operatorname{Per}(A)$ for 0–1 matrices is \#P-complete, and the same holds for matrices with Gaussian entries. The fastest known exact algorithms—Ryser's inclusion–exclusion method—run in $O(n^2 2^n)$ time, exponential in $n$.

### Gaussian boson sampling

Fock-state boson sampling requires $n$ simultaneous indistinguishable photons. Real single-photon sources (quantum dots, heralded SPDC) suffer from brightness–fidelity trade-offs that make scaling painful. Hamilton et al. [4] observed that squeezed states of light—Gaussian states routinely produced at tens of decibels of squeezing—can serve as inputs, at the price of a new matrix function.

For $K$ single-mode squeezed states entering an $m$-mode interferometer, the probability of a photon-occupation pattern $\mathbf{n}$ is

$$\Pr(\mathbf{n}) = \frac{|\operatorname{Haf}(A_{\mathbf{n}})|^2}{\mathbf{n}! \sqrt{|\sigma_Q|}},$$

where $A$ is a symmetric $2m \times 2m$ matrix derived from the Gaussian covariance matrix, and $\operatorname{Haf}$ is the **hafnian**—a sum over all perfect matchings of the complete graph on $2n$ vertices [4].

## 3 Methodology

### Computing permanents and hafnians: Ryser's algorithm

All classical simulation and verification of boson sampling reduces, ultimately, to computing permanents or hafnians.

$$\operatorname{Per}(A) = (-1)^n \sum_{S \subseteq [n]} (-1)^{|S|} \prod_{i=1}^n \sum_{j \in S} a_{ij},$$

which evaluates the permanent in $O(n 2^n)$ time using Gray-code ordering of the subsets [2].

### Exact and approximate classical samplers

Three classical algorithmic ideas dominate the literature:

- **Clifford–Clifford sampler.** An exact sampler producing one boson-sampling event in $O(n2^n + \mathrm{poly}(m,n))$ time and $O(m)$ space [2]—exponentially faster than naive sampling and matching the cost of a single permanent computation. This algorithm is the standard baseline against which advantage claims are scored: if a device samples $n$ photons faster than $\sim 2^n$ Ryser evaluations on a supercomputer, it may hold advantage.

- **Metropolis-independence sampling (MIS).** Neville et al. [5] showed that a Markov-chain Monte Carlo approach could generate samples for up to $n = 30$ photons with only $\sim 200$ permanent evaluations per sample, outperforming then-current experiments by orders of magnitude. The crucial observation: one need not compute the full distribution; a Markov chain with the target distribution as its stationary state produces *samples*, not probabilities.

- **Tensor-network / MPS contraction.** For GBS with photon loss, the output state has limited entanglement, and matrix-product-state representations contract efficiently. Loss—photons escaping before detection—is the dominant noise, and it *reduces* classical simulation cost. Modern GBS simulation thresholds are therefore stated in terms of effective (post-loss) photon numbers, not raw source counts.

A minimal Python illustration of Ryser's algorithm, the primitive at the heart of every classical baseline:

```python
import itertools

def ryser_permanent(A):
    """Compute Per(A) in O(n 2^n) via Ryser's inclusion-exclusion."""
    n = len(A)
    total = 0
    # Gray-code ordering: flip one column at a time (shown here naively)
    for mask in range(1, 1 << n):
        sign = (-1) ** (n - bin(mask).count("1"))
        prod = 1
        for i in range(n):
            rowsum = sum(A[i][j] for j in range(n) if mask & (1 << j))
            prod *= rowsum
            if prod == 0:
                break
        total += sign * prod
    return total

# Example: 3x3 permanent = 6 for the all-ones matrix
print(ryser_permanent([[1]*3 for _ in range(3)]))  # 6
```

### Verification protocols

Since computing any single output probability is \#P-hard, full verification is impossible at scale.

- **Bayesian model comparison**: compare samples' likelihood under the ideal quantum model versus alternatives (distinguishable photons, thermal states, uniform distribution) [3].
- **Heavy Output Generation (HOG)**: check the device preferentially generates outcomes assigned high probability by the ideal model [7].
- **Cumulant / correlation tests**: compare higher-order mode correlations against theory [6].
- **Graph-theoretic validation**: test GBS samples against graph perfect-matching structure [9].

The squashed-states critique [7] showed these tests can be weaker than assumed: a classical distribution built from mixtures of coherent states reproduces up to fourth-order cumulants of Jiuzhang 1.0's output and, under Bayesian comparison, *outperforms* the claimed ground truth—meaning the experiment's validation did not rule out a classical explanation lacking any quantum features.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Aaronson–Arkhipov hardness theorem, dissected

The genius of [1] is a reduction turning a *sampler* into a *permanent estimator*: a classical sampling oracle, combined with Stockmeyer's approximate counting theorem, yields a $\text{BPP}^{\text{NP}}$ algorithm for estimating $|\operatorname{Per}(X)|^2$ on Gaussian $X$—a \#P-hard task under the Permanent-of-Gaussians Conjecture—collapsing the polynomial hierarchy to the third level.

Two features deserve emphasis:

1. **Randomness is load-bearing.** The argument needs *typical* instances to be hard—hence Haar-random unitaries, and the $m = \Omega(n^2)$ no-collision regime that makes submatrices of $U$ statistically Gaussian. A boson sampler with a fixed, structured interferometer proves nothing about typical-case hardness.
2. **Approximate sampling needs anti-concentration.** Without it, the distribution could concentrate on a few outcomes a classical algorithm could find and reproduce. Anti-concentration spreads mass over $\exp(n)$ outcomes so samples genuinely encode permanent information. The conjecture remains unproven—arguably the weakest link in the edifice [1].

### 4.2 Gaussian boson sampling: squeezed light and the hafnian

GBS trades the single-photon sources of the original proposal for squeezed vacuum states, which are far brighter and easier to produce deterministically [4].

$$\operatorname{Haf}(A) = \sum_{\rho \in \mathcal{P}_{2n}} \prod_{\{i,j\} \in \rho} A_{ij},$$

a sum over all $(2n-1)!!$ perfect matchings.

GBS also unlocked applications beyond supremacy demonstrations.

### 4.3 The Jiuzhang lineage: 76, 113, 255, and 3050 photons

The experimental centerpiece of the field is USTC's Jiuzhang program:

| Experiment | Year | Photons (detected) | Modes | Claimed speedup | Source |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Jiuzhang 1.0 | 2020 | up to 76 | 100 | $\sim 10^{14}$ over TaihuLight | [3] |
| Jiuzhang 2.0 | 2021 | up to 113 | 144 | $\sim 10^{24}$, phase-programmable | [10] |
| Jiuzhang 3.0 | 2023 | 255 | pseudo-PNR | $\sim 10^6$ over Jiuzhang 2.0 | [8] |
| Jiuzhang 4.0 | 2025 | up to 3050 coincidence events | 8176 | $> 10^{54}$ over El Capitan | [8] |

Jiuzhang 1.0 [3] sent 50 single-mode squeezed states into a 100-mode ultralow-loss interferometer with full connectivity, detecting up to 76 photon clicks; the $10^{30}$-dimensional output space and $10^{14}$ speedup factor over classical simulation made it the first photonic claim of quantum computational advantage, published in *Science*.

Jiuzhang 2.0 [10] introduced stimulated emission of squeezed light (detected photons to 113), phase programmability of input states, and a 144-mode circuit, with Hilbert space dimension $\sim 10^{43}$.

Jiuzhang 3.0 used fiber-loop time-bin demultiplexing for pseudo photon-number resolution, reaching 255 detected photons.

Jiuzhang 4.0 (arXiv 2025) [8] is a qualitative leap: up to 1024 input squeezed states, 8176 spatiotemporal output modes (hybrid spatial–temporal encoding with delay loops giving cubic connectivity growth at linear physical cost), 51% overall system efficiency, and 3050-photon coincidence events.

Xanadu's Borealis (2022) [6] deserves equal billing: a *programmable*, time-multiplexed photonic processor with 216 modes and photon-number-resolving detectors, validated through cumulant comparisons and, later, graph-theoretic perfect-matching tests [9].

### 4.4 The spoofing wars: classical attacks and their defeats

The classical side of the arms race has four landmark salvos:

1. **Metropolis-independence sampling (Neville et al., 2017).** Using MIS with $\sim 200$ permanents per sample, a laptop-class machine beat the projected rates of near-term Fock-state experiments, showing that pre-2020 hardware was nowhere near advantage [5]. The lesson: *sampling* is far cheaper than *computing the distribution*.

2. **Clifford–Clifford exact sampler.** The $O(n2^n)$ sampler [2] reset the baseline for all subsequent claims and remains the reference classical algorithm in Jiuzhang's speedup accounting.

3. **Squashed states (2022–2023).** The most philosophically damaging attack: a classical hypothesis—mixtures of coherent states through a lossy interferometer, possessing *no quantum correlations*—reproduces the low-order statistics of Jiuzhang 1.0 and, under Bayesian model comparison, explains the data *better* than the experiment's own ground-truth model [7]. For Jiuzhang 2.0 the ground truth survived Bayesian comparison, but the attack exposed that the validation toolkit (cumulants, HOG, Bayesian tests) could not reliably distinguish quantum from classical light in the high photon-density regime.

4. **Tensor-network / MPS simulation.** Lossy GBS states have bounded entanglement, and MPS contraction cost scales with the *effective* squeezed photon number after loss. Jiuzhang 4.0's answer was to attack loss itself: 51% efficiency and thousands of photons push the required MPS bond dimension beyond any feasible budget, with the authors arguing this "rules out all classical spoofing models" [8]. The debate has moved from *whether* spoofing models exist to *whether* any can survive at 51% efficiency and 3000+ photons.

## 5 Empirical/Proofs

The quantitative story is a moving threshold. Define $T_q(n)$ as the device's time to produce one $n$-photon sample and $T_c(n)$ as the best known classical simulation time. Advantage requires $T_q \ll T_c$ with a margin surviving future classical improvements.

**Theoretical anchors.** A permanent costs $\Theta(n^2 2^n)$ via Ryser; exact boson sampling costs $O(n2^n)$ per sample [2]; the output space of $n$ photons in $m$ modes has dimension $\binom{m+n-1}{n}$—$\sim 10^{30}$ for Jiuzhang 1.0 ($n=76, m=100$) and $\sim 10^{2461}$ for Jiuzhang 4.0 ($n \approx 3000$, $m = 8176$) [3][8].

**Experimental anchors.** Measured speedups: $10^{14}$ (Jiuzhang 1.0 vs. TaihuLight) [3], $10^{24}$ (Jiuzhang 2.0) [10], $10^6$ over Jiuzhang 2.0 (Jiuzhang 3.0), $10^{54}$ (Jiuzhang 4.0 vs. El Capitan) [8]; Borealis validated programmability at 216 modes [6].

**Classical anchors.** MIS cut per-sample cost to hundreds of permanents [5]; Clifford–Clifford set the $O(n2^n)$ bar [2]; MPS tied simulation cost to post-loss photon number; and the squashed-states analysis showed at least one headline experiment's data preferred a classical hypothesis [7].

A concise statement of the current frontier:

> *As of 2026, the strongest published photonic advantage claim is Jiuzhang 4.0's $10^{54}$ ratio at 3050-photon coincidences and 51% system efficiency, benchmarked against MPS simulation with unlimited classical memory [8]. The strongest published classical counter-claim is that Jiuzhang 1.0's samples are better explained by a quantum-correlation-free squashed-states model [7]. Both statements can be true simultaneously: advantage is a function of device quality, and the threshold has simply moved from tens to thousands of photons.*

## 6 Limitations

**Unproven conjectures.** The entire approximate-hardness argument for the original model rests on the Permanent-of-Gaussians and Permanent Anti-Concentration conjectures [1]; for GBS, even the *statement* of the analogous hafnian conjectures is less developed. A collapse of either conjecture would not falsify the experiments but would dissolve their complexity-theoretic justification.

**Verification is inherently partial.** Every scalable verification test (Bayesian comparison, HOG, cumulants, graph statistics) is a *necessary but not sufficient* condition for correct sampling [6][7][9]. The squashed-states episode proved that passing all standard tests does not imply quantumness [7]. There is no known efficiently checkable *certificate* of correct GBS sampling—a fundamental asymmetry, since the task is defined by a \#P-hard function.

**Noise is the adversary's ally.** Photon loss, partial distinguishability, and mode mismatch all degrade the ideal distribution toward classically simulable ones. Under realistic independent error models, the asymptotic hardness of noisy boson sampling has been questioned outright: some analyses argue that with constant per-photon loss, the problem becomes classically tractable at scale, and advantage survives only if loss *decreases* as systems grow [7][8]. Jiuzhang 4.0's 51% efficiency is a direct engineering answer to this theoretical objection [8].

**Non-universality and utility.** Boson samplers solve one task: sampling. They are not programmable in the circuit-model sense (Jiuzhang 1.0–3.0 used fixed or phase-only-tunable interferometers), and proposed applications (graph problems, vibronic spectra) remain proofs of concept [4][9]. Photonic advantage is real but narrow.

**Classical algorithms keep improving.** Every speedup ratio quoted above is a snapshot against *today's* classical methods. The history of this field—MIS [5], Clifford–Clifford [2], MPS [8]—is a history of thresholds being reset. Advantage claims must therefore be read as *lower bounds on the classical cost*, never as final.

## 7 Conclusion

Boson sampling began as a complexity-theoretic provocation: a device too simple to compute anything, yet too hard to simulate [1]. Gaussian boson sampling made it buildable [4]; Jiuzhang made it headline news [3]; Borealis made it programmable [6]; and the spoofing wars made it honest [5][7][8].

Three conclusions survive. **First**, the Aaronson–Arkhipov reduction remains the most elegant argument for quantum advantage without universality, and its core—the permanent as the meeting point of quantum interference and classical intractability—is undiminished. **Second**, experimental advantage is now measured in thousands of photons and $10^{54}$ speedup ratios, but only because the classical bar (exact samplers, MPS, squashed-state null models) forced the hardware to get genuinely better; noise, especially loss, is the parameter that matters. **Third**, verification is the field's open wound: we can build devices whose outputs no classical machine can reproduce, yet cannot efficiently prove they are correct. Until that gap closes—through better certificates, self-testing protocols, or application-level validation like the graph-theoretic tests [9]—photonic quantum advantage will remain a claim about *complexity*, argued in conjectures, rather than a fact about *computation*, verified in proof.
