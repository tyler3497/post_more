# Transient Execution Attacks and the Collapse of the Hardware Security Model

## Abstract

On 3 January 2018, coordinated disclosure of *Spectre* and *Meltdown* shattered a foundational assumption of computer security: that out-of-order and speculative execution, though invisible at the architectural level, could be treated as invisible at every level. Both attacks demonstrate that transient instructions — micro-operations issued down a mispredicted path or past a deferred fault — leave measurable residues in microarchitectural state, above all the cache hierarchy, that an adversary can decode through timing side channels such as **Flush+Reload**. Spectre tricks a victim into speculatively disclosing its own secrets; Meltdown lets unprivileged code read kernel memory directly, defeating address-space isolation and KASLR outright. This article presents a unified treatment: the speculative-execution fundamentals that made these attacks possible; the mechanics of Spectre v1, Spectre v2, and Meltdown; the cache side-channel primitives that convert transient traces into bytes; the subsequent variants including Foreshadow/L1TF and the MDS family; and the defensive response spanning KPTI, retpoline, SSBD, and microcode barriers, together with measured performance costs. [1][2][5]

## 1 Introduction

For half a century, processor architects and security practitioners operated under a tacit division of labor. Architects pursued *performance* through aggressive microarchitectural speculation — branch prediction, speculative execution, memory disambiguation — confident that transiently executed instructions would be squashed before altering architectural state. Security practitioners reasoned about programs as defined by the instruction set architecture, treating the microarchitecture as an implementation detail that could not leak information because its effects were ephemeral. The disclosure of Spectre [1] and Meltdown [2] in January 2018 showed this division was a category error: speculative execution is not invisible at every level. Transient instructions leave residues — cache lines fetched, TLB entries populated, execution-port contention — that persist after the instructions producing them are discarded, and an adversary can measure those residues with sub-nanosecond precision.

The shock was conceptual. Earlier microarchitectural attacks exploited *implementation* weaknesses: a particular cache's inclusion policy, a particular predictor's indexing. Transient execution attacks are *paradigm* weaknesses. They exploit the very mechanism by which modern processors extract instruction-level parallelism. Any core that executes speculatively while sharing microarchitectural resources across security domains is, in principle, vulnerable. The following years produced Spectre v1, v2, v4, Meltdown variants, Foreshadow/L1TF [7], and Microarchitectural Data Sampling — each refining our model of what "execution" means, and each demanding a defensive answer [5].

> **Theorem (informal, collapse of the contract):** Let $P$ be a memory-safe program under the ISA contract, and $M$ a speculative out-of-order implementation of that ISA. There exist programs for which $M$ observably discloses secret data to an unprivileged observer during transient execution. The ISA contract is *insufficient* as a security contract on speculative hardware.

The remainder of this article proves that theorem by construction — and catalogs the mitigations attempting to repair the contract.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Speculative execution as performance technology

When the frontend encounters a conditional branch, it does not stall: a *branch predictor* — tournament predictors, loop predictors, indirect-target predictors — guesses the outcome, and the pipeline fetches, decodes, and executes along the predicted path. Independent instructions issue out of order; memory operations may issue past unresolved stores under *memory disambiguation* heuristics. Only at *retirement* does the processor verify each speculation; mispredicted-path instructions are flushed and execution resumes correctly. Kocher et al. [1] note this design wins enormously on performance — but creates a vast transient attack surface.

The critical observation of [1] is that *squashing is not erasing*. A transient load brings its line into the cache; a transient division occupies the divider for dozens of cycles. These side effects are not rolled back, because rollback would cost performance and architects never imagined it necessary. The security boundary was drawn at architectural state; microarchitectural state was treated as a shared, unprivileged commons.

### 2.2 Side channels: making the invisible measurable

Yarom and Falkner's Flush+Reload [3] provides the canonical high-resolution cache channel: on processors with an inclusive last-level cache shared across cores, an attacker flushes a shared line with `clflush`, waits, then reloads it and times the access. A fast reload means the victim touched the line; a slow one means it did not. The channel is byte-granular, kilohertz-bandwidth, and nearly noiseless because it exploits shared memory such as common libraries.

Canella et al. [5] situate Flush+Reload within a broader covert-channel taxonomy — cache-based (Flush+Reload, Prime+Probe, Evict+Reload), TLB-based, port-contention-based, AVX-power-based — all sharing one shape: *encode* a secret into microarchitectural state during the transient window, *decode* it via timing. Transient-execution attacks differ mainly in how they arrange the encoding.

### 2.3 A taxonomy of transient execution

Following [5], we classify attacks along three axes: the **speculation primitive** (what is mispredicted or deferred — conditional branches for Spectre v1, indirect targets for v2, store bypass for v4, deferred faults for Meltdown); the **disclosure gadget** (how the secret reaches a measurable channel — a victim code snippet for Spectre, attacker code for Meltdown); and the **covert channel** (how it is read — overwhelmingly cache timing, with Flush+Reload [3] dominant for its resolution).

| Attack | Speculation primitive | Disclosure locus | Reference |
|---|---|---|---|
| Spectre v1 | Conditional branch mispredict | Victim gadget | [1] |
| Spectre v2 | Indirect branch mispredict | Victim gadget | [1] |
| Meltdown | Deferred page fault | Attacker code | [2] |
| Foreshadow/L1TF | Deferred terminal fault (L1) | Attacker code | [7] |
| SSB (v4) | Store-bypass speculation | Victim gadget | [5] |

This taxonomy explains both the family's breadth and the difficulty of comprehensive defense: each axis admits many instantiations, and mitigations must address all three.

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 Threat model

We adopt the standard model of [1][2][5]. The adversary is an unprivileged process — or sandboxed JavaScript in the browser variant — co-located on the same core or last-level cache as the victim, able to execute arbitrary user-mode code including `clflush` and high-resolution timers (`rdtsc`, or amplified `performance.now()`), and able to train shared branch predictors with attacker-controlled history. In the Meltdown model [2], the adversary additionally attempts loads from kernel addresses, relying on deferred fault handling to permit transient dependent execution. The victim is any code containing a reachable disclosure gadget (Spectre) or sharing microarchitectural resources with attacker code (Meltdown), under standard OS isolation: per-process page tables, supervisor-only kernel mappings, KASLR.

### 3.2 Analytical approach

For each speculation primitive we identify the *transient window* — the interval between speculative issue and squash, bounded by prediction-resolution latency — then construct the disclosure gadget and encode step, and finally quantify the end-to-end channel: exfiltration bandwidth, error rate under noise, and mitigation performance costs as reported in [5] and [6]. Where the literature provides measurements we report them; otherwise we reason from window sizes (tens to hundreds of cycles) and channel capacities.

### 3.3 Scope

We focus on x86-64, where the disclosure literature concentrates, though the principles generalize to speculative ARM and POWER designs [1][5]. We exclude physical attacks and attacks requiring privileged code — the point of this class is that pure unprivileged code suffices.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Spectre v1: bounds-check bypass

The canonical v1 gadget from [1] is disarmingly small:

```c
if (x < array1_size) {             // (A) bounds check — trained "taken"
    y = array2[array1[x] * 4096];  // (B) secret-dependent load, transient
}
```

**Phase 1 — mistraining.** The attacker repeatedly invokes the victim with in-bounds `x`, training the pattern-history table to predict branch (A) as taken; predictor state indexed by branch address is shared across contexts, so the training transfers. **Phase 2 — transient disclosure.** The attacker supplies an out-of-bounds `x` aimed at a secret byte. The predictor guesses "taken"; `array1[x]` issues speculatively and returns secret byte `s`; the dependent load `array2[s * 4096]` issues transiently, caching one of 256 probe lines. When the bounds check resolves — delayed because the attacker first evicted `array1_size` from cache — the pipeline flushes, but the cache line persists. **Phase 3 — decoding.** Flush+Reload [3] over the 256 lines reveals `s` via the single cache hit. The 4096 multiplier spaces probes across cache lines and pages, defeating the prefetcher.

Kocher et al. [1] demonstrate tens of kilobytes per second exfiltration natively, and — critically — a working JavaScript variant, where the gadget is the browser's own JIT-compiled code and timers are amplified to compensate for reduced resolution.

> **Theorem (Spectre v1 universality):** Any conditional branch guarding a secret-dependent access, whose condition can be delayed and whose predictor can be mistrained, is a disclosure gadget. Bounds checks are ubiquitous; therefore gadgets are ubiquitous.

Unlike a buffer overflow, there is no single bug to patch. Defenses must either serialize speculation at sensitive points (`lfence`, requiring gadget identification at high cost) or restrict what transient execution can observe (hardware mitigations, Section 5).

### 4.2 Spectre v2: branch-target injection and the retpoline

Spectre v2 targets *indirect* branches — virtual calls, switch dispatches — predicted by the Branch Target Buffer. The attacker poisons the BTB by executing indirect branches at an aliased address (BTB entries are indexed by low address bits, so attacker and victim branches collide), training the predictor to redirect the victim's indirect branch to an attacker-chosen gadget. The victim speculatively jumps to the gadget, encodes the secret, and only then does the true target resolve [1]. Spectre v2 grants the attacker gadget *choice* rather than gadget *discovery*, at the cost of requiring BTB aliasing and victim address knowledge.

Because indirect branches are frequent in kernels and hypervisors and the BTB is shared across privilege boundaries, v2 demanded an immediate deployable mitigation. The answer was **retpoline** [4]: replace each indirect call/jump with a construct trapping speculation in a loop:

```c
/* conceptual retpoline for: call *%rax */
call retpoline_target
capture:  pause
          lfence        /* speculation trap: predicted path spins here */
          jmp capture
retpoline_target:
          mov %rax, (%rsp)  /* overwrite return address with real target */
          ret               /* RSB predicts 'capture'; CPU goes to *%rax */
```

The `call` pushes `capture` onto the Return Stack Buffer, so the `ret`'s predictor guesses the trap while the architectural path overwrites the stack slot with the true target. Turner [4] shows this converts indirect branches into returns, defeating BTB poisoning at modest cost. Retpoline shipped across Linux, Windows, and hypervisors within weeks — the canonical *software construct* that reshapes speculation rather than merely serializing it.

### 4.3 Meltdown: the deferred fault

Meltdown [2] needs no victim gadget. On affected Intel processors, a load from a supervisor-only page does not fault *immediately* — the permission check is deferred until retirement, and dependent micro-operations execute transiently with the loaded value:

```c
secret = *kernel_address;     /* faults — but only at retirement */
y = probe[secret * 4096];     /* transient: encodes secret into cache */
/* fault suppressed via signal handler; Flush+Reload decodes probe */
```

Since the kernel is mapped into every process's address space, the "secret" can be any kernel memory — credentials, keys, KASLR offsets. Lipp et al. [2] dump kernel memory at hundreds of kilobytes per second, defeating KASLR and address-space isolation outright. Meltdown is arguably a *bug* rather than a paradigm flaw: the ISA contract says the load must fault. The fix could therefore be architectural: **KPTI** unmaps the kernel from user page tables, so the transient load finds no mapping — at 5–30% cost on syscall-heavy workloads [6], the first great performance bill of the transient-execution era.

The lineage continued with **Foreshadow (L1TF)** [7]: even with KPTI, data resident in L1 could be extracted via terminal-fault speculation, breaching SGX enclaves and virtual machines. Van Bulck et al. [7] extracted SGX attestation keys — the "collapse of the hardware security model" in our title is not hyperbole.

### 4.4 Flush+Reload: the decoding lens

Every transient attack needs a decoder, and Flush+Reload [3] is the instrument of choice, exploiting *shared* memory: flush the probe line with `clflush`, let the transient window execute, reload and time it — a load under ~80 cycles is a hit (secret-dependent touch occurred), over ~200 cycles a miss. Yarom and Falkner [3] report sub-1% error rates at tens of kilobits per second on the raw channel; transient attacks amplify this with repetition and error correction. Where shared memory is unavailable (cross-VM without deduplication), Prime+Probe substitutes at higher noise [5]. The deeper lesson of [3]: microarchitectural state is *legible*. Caches were designed as transparent accelerators, but transparency is bidirectional — speculation made the cache legible at the wrong times.

## 5 Empirical Evaluation

### 5.1 Exfiltration performance

Canella et al. [5] give the most comprehensive comparative measurements across Intel and AMD microarchitectures, with and without mitigations (order-of-magnitude, Skylake-class):

| Attack | Bandwidth | Error rate | Mitigation | Source |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Spectre v1 (native) | ~10 KB/s | < 5% | lfence / SSBD | [1][5] |
| Spectre v1 (browser) | ~1 KB/s | < 10% | Site isolation, timer fuzzing | [1] |
| Meltdown | ~500 KB/s | < 1% | KPTI | [2] |
| Foreshadow (SGX) | ~3 KB/s | < 5% | L1D flush, microcode | [7] |
| Flush+Reload (raw) | ~50 KB/s | < 1% | — (channel) | [3] |

Meltdown-class attacks (fault-driven, attacker-local) run *faster* than Spectre-class (branch-driven, gadget-dependent), because the attacker controls the whole encode sequence and can optimize the transient window. And every mitigation exacts a tax that composes: a fully mitigated Skylake system loses 10–20% on mixed workloads [5].

### 5.2 The cost of defense

Intel's guidance [6] catalogs the mitigation portfolio candidly: **KPTI**, 5–30% on syscall/I/O-heavy workloads; **retpoline** [4], 1–5% on indirect-branch-heavy code; **SSBD**, 2–8%, often opt-in since Spectre v4 gadgets are rarer; **L1D flush on enclave/VM exit** (Foreshadow [7]), single-digit percent on SGX workloads; **microcode updates** (IBRS, STIBP), variable — IBRS was initially expensive enough to motivate the retpoline-first strategy. The systematic evaluation [5] adds that mitigations *interact*: KPTI plus retpoline plus SSBD plus L1D flushes compound on workloads stressing all affected paths, e.g. virtualized I/O. Its headline conclusion: transient-execution defense is not a patch but a *permanent performance regime* — the pre-2018 baseline assumed free speculation, and that assumption is now priced in.

### 5.3 Coverage analysis

No single mitigation suffices: KPTI stops Meltdown but not Spectre; retpoline stops v2 but not v1; SSBD stops v4 only [5]. Intel [6] accordingly presents mitigations as a defense-in-depth stack, with in-silicon fixes (Cascade Lake onward; AMD's design-level Meltdown resistance) as the only path to recovering performance.

## 6 Limitations

Our figures come from the cited papers' measurement environments [1][2][3][5][6][7]; treat them as orders of magnitude, varying with microarchitecture stepping, firmware, and workload. We focus on x86-64; ARM and POWER9 show analogous vulnerabilities with different gadget details [1][5]. The landscape keeps evolving: Microarchitectural Data Sampling (Fallout, RIDL, ZombieLoad) extends the taxonomy with *buffer*-based disclosure, covered systematically only in [5]'s later revisions. Our threat model excludes physical side channels and assumes correct microcode deployment — non-trivial given the patching history in [6]. Finally, the cost figures reflect software mitigations; hardware-redesign defenses (speculative taint tracking, invisible speculation) remain beyond our scope.

---

## 7 Conclusion

Spectre [1] and Meltdown [2] invalidated a foundational abstraction: the ISA contract proved insufficient for security on speculative hardware, because transient instructions — architecturally null — are microarchitecturally *legible* through channels like Flush+Reload [3]. The variants that followed — branch-target injection answered by retpoline [4], systematic analysis [5], Intel's mitigation portfolio [6], Foreshadow's breach of SGX [7] — trace one arc: performance and confidentiality were purchased with the same currency, speculation, and the bill has come due.

The response has been pragmatic: KPTI, retpoline, SSBD, microcode barriers — defense in depth restoring safety at 10–20% cost while hardware is redesigned. The principled response — provably invisible speculation via taint tracking or state partitioning — remains an active frontier. Until it arrives, every bounds check is a potential gadget, every shared cache a potential lens, every speculation a potential disclosure. The hardware security model did not bend; it collapsed, and we are still rebuilding it.
