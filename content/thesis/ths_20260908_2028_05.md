# Optimal Transport Theory and Sinkhorn Algorithms: Entropic Regularization, Wasserstein Barycenters, and Domain Adaptation

## Abstract

*This thesis develops the modern computational theory of optimal transport (OT), centered on entropic regularization and the Sinkhorn–Knopp matrix scaling algorithm. We trace the arc from the Kantorovich linear program over coupling measures to the entropy-regularized surrogate whose optimal plans factor as diagonal scalings of a Gibbs kernel, yielding iterations that are exactly alternating Bregman projections onto marginal constraints. We prove and interpret the linear convergence of Sinkhorn iterations in Hilbert's projective metric, analyze log-domain stabilization for vanishing regularization, and quantify the near-linear-time approximation guarantees for the original unregularized problem. We then develop the geometry built on this foundation: Wasserstein barycenters as Fréchet means in the space of measures, the Gromov–Wasserstein distance for incomparable metric spaces, and debiased Sinkhorn divergences. Applications surveyed include unsupervised domain adaptation via regularized transport plans, generative modeling through Kantorovich–Rubinstein duality, and single-cell trajectory inference. The thesis closes with limitations — entropic bias, sample complexity curses, and hardness barriers — and the open problems they motivate.*

---

## 1 Introduction

Few mathematical ideas have migrated as decisively from pure analysis to the computational core of machine learning as *optimal transport* (OT). The classical question — how to move one distribution of mass onto another at minimum cost — originates with Monge's 1781 memoir on excavation and embankments, and was recast by Kantorovich in 1942 as a linear program over *couplings*: joint probability measures whose marginals are the prescribed source and target distributions. This relaxation, conceived for Soviet railway logistics, is today the engine behind image morphing, domain adaptation, generative modeling, and single-cell genomics.

Three obstacles long confined OT to the periphery of data science. First, the discrete OT problem is a linear program with quadratic complexity in its support size, solvable by network-flow methods in roughly *O(n³ log n)* — prohibitive for modern sample sizes [2]. Second, the Wasserstein distance is *non-smooth* in its inputs, a poor loss function for gradient-based learning. Third, the empirical Wasserstein distance suffers a curse of dimensionality, converging at the rate *n^(−1/d)* in dimension *d*.

The decisive breakthrough came in 2013, when Cuturi proposed *entropic regularization* of the transport problem [1]. Adding a negative-entropy penalty to the Kantorovich objective smooths the problem, makes the optimal coupling *unique*, and — crucially — forces it into a factored form: a Gibbs kernel *K = exp(−C/ε)* flanked by two diagonal scaling matrices. Computing these scalings is exactly the Sinkhorn–Knopp matrix balancing algorithm of 1964, repurposed for machine learning. Each iteration is a pair of *O(n²)* matrix–vector products, trivially parallelizable, and differentiable end-to-end. What was a cubic-cost linear program became a sequence of matrix multiplications.

This thesis is a self-contained treatment of that revolution and its consequences. We develop the theory in layers: the Kantorovich formulation and its dual (§2), entropic regularization and the Sinkhorn algorithm with its convergence theory (§3), and the deep structure built on top — convergence geometry, debiased divergences, Wasserstein barycenters, and Gromov–Wasserstein distances (§4). We then examine theoretical guarantees and empirical behavior (§5), confront limitations (§6), and conclude with the research frontier (§7).

## 2 Background and Related Work

### 2.1 The Kantorovich Formulation

Let *a ∈ Δₙ* and *b ∈ Δₘ* be probability vectors supported on points {*xᵢ*} and {*yⱼ*}, with *C ∈ ℝⁿˣᵐ₊* a cost matrix, typically *Cᵢⱼ = ‖xᵢ − yⱼ‖ᵖ*. The **transport polytope** is

$$\mathcal{U}(a,b) := \{P \in \mathbb{R}_+^{n \times m} : P\mathbf{1}_m = a,\; P^\top \mathbf{1}_n = b\},$$

the set of *coupling matrices* with the prescribed marginals. The discrete Kantorovich problem is the linear program [2]

$$L_C(a,b) := \min_{P \in \mathcal{U}(a,b)} \langle P, C \rangle = \min_{P \in \mathcal{U}(a,b)} \sum_{i,j} P_{ij} C_{ij}. \tag{1}$$

The optimizer *P⋆* is the *optimal transport plan*. When *C* derives from a ground metric *d* via *Cᵢⱼ = d(xᵢ, yⱼ)ᵖ*, the quantity *Wₚ(a,b) := L_C(a,b)^{1/p}* is the *p-Wasserstein distance*, a genuine metric on probability measures. For *p = 1* it is also known as the Earth Mover's Distance.

Key structural facts about (1):

- **Sparsity.** An extreme point of *U(a,b)* has at most *n + m − 1* nonzero entries [2]; the optimizer is a *sparse* matching concentrated on few pairs.
- **Non-smoothness.** The value *L_C* is piecewise linear in *(a, b)*; gradients jump when the optimal basis changes.
- **Duality.** The LP dual reads sup{⟨*f*, *a*⟩ + ⟨*g*, *b*⟩ : *fᵢ + gⱼ ≤ Cᵢⱼ*}, where *(f, g)* are *Kantorovich potentials*. For *p = 1*, Kantorovich–Rubinstein duality collapses this to a supremum over 1-Lipschitz functions — the form that underlies Wasserstein GANs [2].

### 2.2 Why Regularize?

The LP (1) is classically solved by the network simplex or specialized transportation simplex in roughly *O(n³ log n)* [2]. Beyond cost, practitioners faced two deeper problems:

1. *Statistical.* The empirical plug-in estimator *Wₚ(âₙ, b̂ₙ)* converges to the population distance at rate *O(n^{−1/d})*, exponentially slow in ambient dimension [2].
2. *Optimization.* Non-smoothness breaks first-order learning pipelines that need gradients through the distance.

Entropic regularization, championed for machine learning by Cuturi [1], attacks all three problems at once: it yields *O(n²)*-per-iteration algorithms, a *smooth* value function differentiable in the inputs, and substantially improved sample complexity. The remainder of this thesis is devoted to this idea and its ramifications.

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 Entropic Regularization and the Gibbs Kernel

Fix *ε > 0* and consider the regularized problem [1]:

$$L_C^\varepsilon(a,b) := \min_{P \in \mathcal{U}(a,b)} \langle P, C \rangle - \varepsilon H(P), \tag{2}$$

where *H(P) = −Σᵢⱼ Pᵢⱼ log Pᵢⱼ* is the Shannon entropy. Because *−εH* is strictly convex, the minimizer *P_ε* of (2) is **unique**.

Writing the Lagrangian of (2) with dual variables *(f, g)* for the marginal constraints and differentiating gives the first-order condition

$$\log P_{ij} = -\frac{C_{ij}}{\varepsilon} + \frac{f_i}{\varepsilon} + \frac{g_j}{\varepsilon} - 1,$$

whence the celebrated factored form [1]:

> **Proposition 1 (Gibbs structure).** *The unique optimizer of (2) has the form* $$P_\varepsilon = \operatorname{diag}(u)\, K \,\operatorname{diag}(v), \qquad K_{ij} := e^{-C_{ij}/\varepsilon}, \tag{3}$$ *for positive scaling vectors u ∈ ℝⁿ₊, v ∈ ℝᵐ₊ determined by the marginal constraints diag(u)Kdiag(v)1ₘ = a and diag(v)Kᵀdiag(u)1ₙ = b.*

The kernel *K* is dense: every entry is strictly positive. Entropic regularization therefore *smears* mass across all pairs — the antithesis of the sparse LP vertex solution. The regularization strength *ε* interpolates between the two regimes: as *ε → 0*, *P_ε* converges to the maximum-entropy optimal plan of (1); as *ε → ∞*, *P_ε → a ⊗ b*, the independence coupling [2].

### 3.2 Sinkhorn–Knopp Iteration as Bregman Projections

Imposing the marginal constraints on (3) yields the fixed-point equations *u ⊙ (Kv) = a* and *v ⊙ (Kᵀu) = b*, where ⊙ is elementwise multiplication. Alternating between them gives the **Sinkhorn iterations** [1]:

$$u^{(\ell+1)} = \frac{a}{K v^{(\ell)}}, \qquad v^{(\ell+1)} = \frac{b}{K^\top u^{(\ell+1)}}, \tag{4}$$

with division elementwise and any positive initialization (e.g., *v⁽⁰⁾ = 1ₘ*). Each half-step is a matrix–vector product: *O(nm)* time, or *O(n²)* for square problems, and each iteration is trivially parallelizable on GPUs.

There is a deeper geometric reading. Define the row- and column-marginal affine sets *𝒞₁ = {P : P1 = a}* and *𝒞₂ = {P : Pᵀ1 = b}*. Then (4) is exactly **alternating KL projection** (Bregman projections for the entropy Bregman divergence) of *K* onto *𝒞₁* and *𝒞₂* [2]. Each update solves *min_{P ∈ 𝒞₁} KL(P ‖ P⁽ℓ⁾)* in closed form, which is precisely row rescaling.

```python
import numpy as np

def sinkhorn(a, b, C, eps, n_iters=1000, tol=1e-9):
    """Entropic OT via Sinkhorn-Knopp. Returns plan P and dual potentials."""
    K = np.exp(-C / eps)          # Gibbs kernel
    u = np.ones_like(a)
    v = np.ones_like(b)
    for _ in range(n_iters):
        u_prev = u
        u = a / (K @ v + 1e-300)  # row projection
        v = b / (K.T @ u + 1e-300)  # column projection
        if np.max(np.abs(u - u_prev)) < tol:
            break
    P = np.diag(u) @ K @ np.diag(v)
    f, g = eps * np.log(u + 1e-300), eps * np.log(v + 1e-300)
    return P, f, g
```

### 3.3 Log-Domain Stabilization

For small *ε* relative to the cost scale, *K = exp(−C/ε)* underflows to zero in floating point, and the iterates (4) collapse. The remedy is to work in the *log domain* [2]: maintain *f = ε log u* and *g = ε log v* and replace matrix products by *soft-minimum* operations,

$$f_i \leftarrow -\varepsilon \log \sum_j \exp\!\left(\tfrac{g_j - C_{ij}}{\varepsilon}\right) + \varepsilon \log a_i,$$

computed with the log-sum-exp trick for numerical stability. This is mathematically identical to (4) but stable down to *ε* values orders of magnitude smaller. Log-domain Sinkhorn is the default in modern libraries and is essential for the small-*ε* regime where the regularized problem approximates the true LP.

A final practical step is **rounding**: the iterate *diag(u)Kdiag(v)* satisfies the marginals only in the limit. Altschuler, Weed, and Rigollet [4] give a simple *O(n²)* rounding scheme producing a feasible *P̂ ∈ U(a,b)* whose cost exceeds the iterate's by a controlled amount — the ingredient that converts Sinkhorn into a *provable approximation algorithm* for the original LP.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Linear Convergence in Hilbert's Projective Metric

Sinkhorn's convergence is best understood through the **Hilbert projective metric** on positive vectors,

$$d_H(u, u') := \log \max_{i,j} \frac{u_i u'_j}{u_j u'_i},$$

which measures distance up to a positive scale factor. Birkhoff's theorem states that a positive linear map *K* is a *strict contraction* in this metric, with contraction ratio

$$\lambda(K) = \frac{\sqrt{\eta(K)} - 1}{\sqrt{\eta(K)} + 1} < 1, \quad \eta(K) := \max_{i,j,k,\ell} \frac{K_{ik} K_{j\ell}}{K_{jk} K_{i\ell}}. \tag{5}$$

> **Theorem 1 (Franklin–Lorenz; Birkhoff–Bushell).** *The Sinkhorn iterates (4) converge linearly: d_H(u⁽ℓ⁾, u⋆) ≤ λ(K)^{2ℓ} d_H(u⁽⁰⁾, u⋆), with λ(K) given by (5). The fixed point (u⋆, v⋆) is unique up to the scaling (cu⋆, v⋆/c).*

Two remarks are critical. First, *η(K)* — and hence the contraction rate — depends on the *spread* of the kernel entries, which grows as *ε → 0*: for *K = exp(−C/ε)*, one has *η(K) ≈ exp(2‖C‖∞/ε)*, so the number of iterations scales like *O(‖C‖∞/ε)*, a linear dependence on the inverse regularization [4]. Small *ε* means a better approximation of the LP but slower convergence — the fundamental **accuracy–speed trade-off** of entropic OT.

Second, the Altschuler–Weed–Rigollet analysis [4] refined this picture into the first *near-linear-time* guarantee for OT: with the greedy **Greenkhorn** variant (updating only the row or column with largest marginal violation, selected in *O(n)* via a heap) plus the rounding scheme, one obtains an *ε̄*-approximate transport plan for the *unregularized* LP in time *Õ(n²/ε̄³)*, where the tilde hides logarithmic factors. Their analysis shows the iteration count is *independent of the dimension n* of the matrix being balanced — a striking and non-obvious fact, since naive bounds degrade with *n*.

| Method | Time to *ε̄*-approx. OT | Key idea |
|---|---|---|
| Network simplex / transportation LP | *O(n³ log n)* | Combinatorial extreme-point search |
| Sinkhorn + rounding [4] | *Õ(n²/ε̄³)* | Matrix scaling; *n*-independent iterations |
| Greenkhorn (greedy) [4] | *Õ(n²/ε̄²)* | Greedy coordinate updates |
| APDAGD (accelerated primal–dual) | *Õ(n^{2.5}/ε̄)* | Nesterov acceleration on the dual |

### 4.2 Debiased Sinkhorn Divergences

The regularized value *L_C^ε* is *biased*: *L_C^ε(a, a) > 0* in general, since the entropy term rewards spreading even when no transport is needed. This disqualifies it as a divergence. The fix, introduced in the literature on Sinkhorn divergences, is the *polarization* correction [2]:

$$S_C^\varepsilon(a, b) := L_C^\varepsilon(a, b) - \tfrac{1}{2} L_C^\varepsilon(a, a) - \tfrac{1}{2} L_C^\varepsilon(b, b). \tag{6}$$

> **Proposition 2 (Debiasing).** *S_C^ε(a, b) ≥ 0 with equality iff a = b. As ε → 0, S_C^ε → L_C; as ε → ∞, it converges to a kernel Maximum Mean Discrepancy. For suitable costs it metrizes weak convergence of measures.*

Thus (6) interpolates between *exact OT* and *MMD* as *ε* varies — a single parametric family spanning the two great traditions of comparing probability distributions [2]. The autocorrelation terms *L_C^ε(a,a)* are computed by the same Sinkhorn routine, and gradients of *S_C^ε* flow through all three solves, making it a drop-in, unbiased-ish loss for learning.

### 4.3 Wasserstein Barycenters

Given measures *μ₁, …, μₛ* and weights *λ ∈ Δₛ*, Agueh and Carlier [3] defined the **Wasserstein barycenter** as the Fréchet mean in the Wasserstein space:

$$\bar{\mu} := \arg\min_{\nu} \sum_{s=1}^{S} \lambda_s \, W_2^2(\mu_s, \nu). \tag{7}$$

This generalizes McCann's displacement interpolation (the *S = 2* case, a constant-speed *W₂*-geodesic) to multiple measures. Their main results: existence always holds; uniqueness holds if at least one *μₛ* is absolutely continuous; the barycenter is characterized via a *multimarginal* optimal transport problem over the product space; and in the Gaussian case the barycenter is Gaussian with an explicitly computable covariance [3].

Computationally, (7) is far harder than pairwise OT. The multimarginal reformulation has *O(n^S)* variables, and recent hardness results show the discrete barycenter problem is NP-hard even for *d = 2, S = 3*, with no polynomial-time randomized additive approximation unless *NP ⊆ BPP*. The practical remedy is again entropic: replacing each *W₂²* by its regularized version yields a problem solvable by **iterative Bregman projections**, alternating KL projections onto the *S* marginal constraints — a direct multi-marginal generalization of Sinkhorn [2].

> **Non-associativity.** *Unlike Euclidean means, Wasserstein barycenters are not associative: the barycenter of barycenters need not equal the joint barycenter for d ≥ 2. Sequential aggregation schemes must therefore be designed with care [3].*

### 4.4 Gromov–Wasserstein Geometry

Classical OT requires both measures to live in the *same* ground space with a *cross-domain* cost *Cᵢⱼ = d(xᵢ, yⱼ)*. The **Gromov–Wasserstein** (GW) distance drops this requirement: given two *metric measure spaces* *(X, d_X, μ)* and *(Y, d_Y, ν)*, it seeks the coupling minimizing *distortion of pairwise distances*,

$$GW^2(\mu, \nu) := \min_{\pi \in \Pi(\mu,\nu)} \sum_{i,j,k,\ell} \big|d_X(x_i, x_k) - d_Y(y_j, y_\ell)\big|^2 \, \pi_{ij} \pi_{k\ell}. \tag{8}$$

This is a *quadratic assignment problem* — NP-complete in general — but invariant to isometries of either space, making it the right tool for *incomparable* domains: aligning single-cell multi-omics measurements, matching graphs, or comparing word embeddings across languages [6].

Peyré, Cuturi, and Solomon [6] introduced the *entropic* GW problem, adding *ε·KL(π ‖ μ ⊗ ν)* to (8). The resulting nonconvex problem is solved by **projected gradient / mirror descent** where each step linearizes the quadratic objective and solves an *entropic linear OT* subproblem — i.e., an inner Sinkhorn loop. For the squared-loss distortion, the objective decomposes so that each gradient evaluation costs *O(n²m + nm²)* rather than *O(n²m²)*. Their block-coordinate scheme alternates between updating the coupling and (for the *averaging* problem) updating a Fréchet-mean distance matrix, yielding GW barycenters of graphs and shapes [6].

## 5 Empirical Evaluation and Theoretical Results

### 5.1 Convergence Behavior in Practice

The theory of §4.1 predicts iteration counts scaling as *‖C‖∞/ε*. This is borne out empirically: on random cost matrices with *n = 4096*, log-domain Sinkhorn reaches a marginal violation of *10⁻⁶* in tens of iterations for *ε = 1.0*, hundreds for *ε = 0.1*, and thousands for *ε = 0.01* — while Greenkhorn's greedy updates empirically dominate the vanilla iteration count by an order of magnitude in the small-*ε* regime [4].

A canonical numerical illustration is the displacement interpolation between two one-dimensional mixtures. With *ε = 10⁻³*, the regularized McCann interpolant *μ_t = Σᵢⱼ P_{ε,ij} δ_{(1−t)xᵢ + tyⱼ}* is visually indistinguishable from the exact geodesic; with *ε = 1*, the interpolant is visibly blurred — mass diffuses rather than translating rigidly. This is the entropic bias made visible, and it is precisely what the debiased divergence (6) corrects.

### 5.2 Statistical Gains

Entropic regularization provably improves sample complexity: the plug-in estimator of the *regularized* OT cost converges at the parametric rate *O(n^{−1/2})*, independent of dimension, versus *O(n^{−1/d})* for the unregularized distance [2].

### 5.3 Domain Adaptation

Courty, Flamary, Tuia, and Rakotomamonjy [5] built one of the most influential OT applications: *unsupervised domain adaptation*. Given labeled source samples {*xᵢˢ*} and unlabeled target samples {*xⱼᵗ*}, they compute a regularized coupling *γ* between the empirical measures and transport each source point via the **barycentric mapping**

$$\hat{x}_i^s = \arg\min_x \sum_j \gamma_{ij} \, c(x, x_j^t), \tag{9}$$

which for the squared Euclidean cost is the closed form *x̂ᵢˢ = Σⱼ γᵢⱼ xⱼᵗ / Σⱼ γᵢⱼ*. A classifier trained on the transported source then applies to the target. Crucially, they add a *label regularization*: a group-sparse penalty encouraging target points to receive mass only from source points of a single class, plus Laplacian terms preserving local geometry [5]. On visual adaptation benchmarks (e.g., Office/Caltech object recognition across feature domains), this consistently outperformed contemporary adaptation methods, and the framework extends naturally to the semi-supervised setting with a few labeled target points.

### 5.4 Generative Modeling and Beyond

The Kantorovich–Rubinstein dual for *W₁* — a supremum over 1-Lipschitz functions — is the mathematical core of Wasserstein GANs, which replaced the Jensen–Shannon objective of classical GANs with an integral probability metric enjoying meaningful gradients even for disjoint supports [2]. Beyond GANs, entropic OT now appears in:

- **Single-cell genomics**, where regularized OT reconstructs developmental trajectories (Waddington landscapes) from snapshot measurements of cell populations;
- **Color transfer and style**, via OT between color histograms, with the entropy term preventing the posterization artifacts of sparse matchings;
- **Neuroscience and brain imaging**, where Wasserstein barycenters average MEG/EEG source estimates across subjects [2];
- **Shape and graph matching**, via the entropic Gromov–Wasserstein machinery of §4.4 [6];
- **Geometric domains**, where convolutional approximations of the Wasserstein distance on meshes achieve near-linear scaling [7].

## 6 Limitations and Open Problems

**The ε frontier.** Small *ε* recovers the true transport problem but demands log-domain arithmetic and many iterations; large *ε* is fast and statistically tame but blurs the geometry. Principled, automatic *ε* selection remains an open problem.

**Memory scaling.** Sinkhorn needs the full *n × m* kernel: *O(n²)* memory. For *n ~ 10⁶* this is infeasible, motivating stochastic, Nyström, and low-rank kernel approximations plus multiscale solvers [2].

**Sample complexity of maps.** While regularized *costs* enjoy parametric rates, estimating the transport *map* or *plan* itself remains cursed in high dimension.

**Nonconvexity of GW.** Entropic Gromov–Wasserstein inherits the nonconvexity of quadratic assignment; current solvers find stationary points with no global optimality certificates [6].

**Hardness of barycenters.** Exact discrete Wasserstein barycenters are NP-hard with no polynomial-time additive approximation barring *NP ⊆ BPP*, so practitioners rely on the entropically smoothed surrogate without guarantees relative to the true barycenter.

**Unbalanced and structured variants.** Real data have unequal total mass, outliers, and invariances; unbalanced, sliced, and subspace-robust variants and their Sinkhorn-compatible formulations are rapidly evolving but not yet consolidated [2].

## 7 Conclusion

Entropic regularization transformed optimal transport from a beautiful but intractable theory into a computational workhorse. The chain of ideas is remarkably tight: penalize the Kantorovich program by entropy; observe the optimizer factors through a Gibbs kernel; compute the factors by Sinkhorn's alternating Bregman projections; understand convergence through Hilbert's projective metric; and recover near-linear-time approximations of the original problem. On this foundation rest Wasserstein barycenters, Gromov–Wasserstein geometry, debiased divergences, and a suite of applications from domain adaptation to generative modeling.

The field's trajectory suggests the next decade belongs to *scale* and *structure*: stochastic and low-rank Sinkhorn solvers pushing to billions of points, nonconvex GW theory catching up with practice, and principled *ε* selection replacing folklore. What began as Monge's problem of moving dirt has become one of the central algorithmic lenses through which machine learning views probability distributions.
