# Contract Theory: Principal-Agent Models, the Holmström–Milgrom Framework, and Incomplete Contracts

## Abstract

Contract theory studies how economic relationships are structured when information is asymmetric and contingencies cannot be fully specified in advance. This essay develops the principal-agent paradigm from its static moral-hazard foundations through the Holmström–Milgrom framework of dynamic incentive provision, and finally to the theory of incomplete contracts and property rights. We derive the informativeness principle, characterize the first-order approach to incentive compatibility, and show how the continuous-time Holmström–Milgrom model with exponential utility and Brownian uncertainty delivers robustly optimal linear contracts, with the optimal incentive intensity balancing marginal productivity against risk and the cost of effort. We then extend the analysis to multitask agency, robustness under Knightian uncertainty, and the computational complexity of contract design. The final section turns to incomplete contracting: when actions and investments are noncontractible, ownership — the allocation of residual control rights — becomes the central instrument, and the Grossman–Hart–Moore framework predicts that assets and the parties with the most important relationship-specific investments should be held together. We close with the principal methodological limitations of each approach.

## 1 The Principal-Agent Problem and Moral Hazard

Consider a *principal* who hires an *agent* to take a costly action on her behalf. The action, call it effort, is chosen privately by the agent and cannot be observed, let alone verified by a court, while the resulting output is observable and contractible. This information asymmetry is the defining feature of **moral hazard**: because the principal must reward the agent on the basis of a noisy signal of effort rather than effort itself, the provision of incentives necessarily exposes the risk-averse agent to risk, creating a fundamental tension between *risk sharing* and *incentive provision* [1].

The canonical timeline has four stages. First, the principal offers a compensation schedule, a function mapping observable output to payments. Second, the agent accepts or rejects the offer; acceptance requires that the contract deliver at least the agent's reservation utility, the **participation constraint** (IR). Third, the agent chooses effort privately, and the contract must make the desired effort level privately optimal for the agent, the **incentive compatibility constraint** (IC). Fourth, output realizes and payments are made. The principal's problem is therefore a constrained optimization: choose the payment function to maximize expected net output subject to IR and IC.

![Principal-agent timeline and information structure](thesis/ths_1789486330878_11283-0.webp)

When effort were observable, the principal would implement the *first-best*: full insurance for the risk-averse agent (a flat wage) together with the efficient level of effort, enforced by a forcing contract that punishes any deviation from the prescribed action. Moral hazard precludes this. Any contract that rewards high output to elicit effort simultaneously imposes output risk on the agent, and the resulting allocation is only *second-best*. The welfare loss relative to the first-best is the **agency cost** of the relationship.

Two mathematical features of the static problem deserve emphasis. First, incentive compatibility is a global constraint: the recommended effort must dominate *every* alternative effort level, which makes the principal's program non-convex in general. The **first-order approach** replaces the global IC constraint with the agent's first-order condition, treating the problem as a calculus-of-variations exercise; its validity requires distributional assumptions — the monotone likelihood ratio property and convexity of the distribution function condition — associated with Rogerson and Jewitt. Recent work argues the first-order approach is valid far more broadly than these classical conditions suggest [7]. Second, with a risk-neutral principal and a risk-averse agent, the optimal contract solves a trade-off point by point: at each output level, the likelihood ratio of the effort-perturbed density dictates how strongly payment should respond, so the optimal contract is typically *nonlinear* and even non-monotone in pathological cases.

## 2 The Informativeness Principle and Optimal Static Contracts

Holmström's 1979 analysis of moral hazard and observability delivers the single most operational result in static contract theory [1]. Suppose that in addition to output, the principal observes some other signal correlated with the agent's action — a supervisor's report, an industry benchmark, a peer's performance. When should such a signal enter the contract?

> **Theorem (Informativeness principle).** *If a signal is informative about the agent's action, in the sense that its distribution depends on effort conditional on output, then any optimal contract must condition on it. Conversely, if the signal carries no information about effort beyond what output reveals, conditioning on it cannot improve the contract.*

Formally, informativeness is evaluated through the likelihood ratio: the signal matters exactly when the likelihood ratio of the joint density is not measurable with respect to output alone. The practical content is that the contract should be written on a **sufficient statistic** for effort. This result rationalizes relative performance evaluation — benchmarking a manager against peers filters common shocks out of the performance measure — and it disciplines debates about which metrics belong in a compensation scheme: *any* incremental signal about effort, however noisy, strictly improves the second-best allocation.

The informativeness principle also clarifies why the optimal static contract is generally complicated. Because every informative signal enters nonlinearly through the likelihood ratio, real-world contracts that are simple — flat salaries, straight commissions, step bonuses — appear anomalous from the static theory's point of view. Explaining the observed *simplicity* of contracts is the central achievement of the dynamic framework developed in the next section.

## 3 The Holmström–Milgrom Framework and Optimal Linearity

The puzzle Holmström and Milgrom (1987) set out to solve is empirical: if optimal contracts are intricate nonlinear functions of every informative signal, why do real compensation schemes so often take the form of a simple linear rule, a base salary plus a constant share of measured performance [2]? Their answer moves the analysis to continuous time and shows that linearity emerges endogenously as the robustly optimal response to an agent who can react continuously to unfolding information.

The model places the agent in control of the drift of a Brownian motion: over a time interval, the agent chooses effort continuously, and the accounting aggregate evolves as the integral of effort plus noise. The agent has **constant absolute risk aversion** (CARA) exponential utility, and only the terminal aggregate is contractible — the principal observes total output, not its path. Under these assumptions, Holmström and Milgrom prove that the optimal compensation scheme is **linear in the aggregate**: payments take the form *w* equals a constant intercept plus a constant slope times total output, and any nonlinear scheme can be strictly improved upon.

![Optimal linear contract: incentive compatibility, participation, and the informativeness principle](thesis/ths_1789486330878_11283-1.webp)

The intuition is a robustness argument of striking elegance. With continuous control, the agent observes the entire history of past performance before choosing subsequent effort. A nonlinear scheme creates perverse dynamic incentives: after a run of good luck the agent slacks off, after bad luck the agent works frantically to climb back up a concave schedule, and the marginal incentive at any instant depends on the accumulated past — which the principal cannot observe. Because the agent's strategy space is rich enough to *undermine and exploit* any nonlinearity, the only schemes immune to such gaming are linear in the aggregate, under which the marginal return to effort is constant and history-independent. The agent then chooses the same effort at every instant, and the dynamic problem collapses to a static one.

The linear-exponential-normal (LEN) specification makes the solution fully explicit. Let output be effort plus normal noise with variance *σ²*, let the agent's cost of effort be quadratic with curvature *k*, and let the agent's CARA coefficient be *r*. Restricting to linear contracts *w(y) = α + βy*, the agent's certainty equivalent is *α + βe − (k/2)e² − (r/2)β²σ²*, so the agent chooses *e = β/k*. The principal maximizes total certainty equivalent, and the intercept *α* merely distributes surplus subject to IR. The optimal incentive intensity is:

> **Proposition (Optimal linear slope).** *In the LEN model, the second-best incentive intensity is β* = 1 / (1 + r k σ²).*

The formula encodes the entire risk–incentive trade-off in four parameters: incentives are stronger when effort is cheap (small *k*), weaker when the agent is more risk-averse (large *r*) or output is noisier (large *σ²*), and the first-best slope of one is recovered only in the limit of risk neutrality or noiseless measurement. The comparative statics are immediate and testable, which explains the LEN model's dominance in applied work. A compact implementation illustrates the computation:

```python
def optimal_beta(risk_aversion: float, cost_curvature: float, noise_var: float) -> float:
    """Second-best incentive intensity in the LEN model: beta* = 1 / (1 + r k s^2)."""
    return 1.0 / (1.0 + risk_aversion * cost_curvature * noise_var)

def certainty_equivalent(alpha: float, beta: float, effort: float,
                         r: float, k: float, s2: float) -> float:
    expected_pay = alpha + beta * effort
    effort_cost = 0.5 * k * effort ** 2
    risk_premium = 0.5 * r * beta ** 2 * s2
    return expected_pay - effort_cost - risk_premium

# Example: moderate risk aversion, unit cost curvature, unit noise
beta_star = optimal_beta(r := 2.0, k := 1.0, s2 := 1.0)
print(f"beta* = {beta_star:.3f}")          # 0.333
print(f"induced effort e = beta/k = {beta_star:.3f}")
```

The Holmström–Milgrom framework has since been extended in several directions. Retaining linearity but relaxing the distributional straitjacket, the continuous-time toolkit now handles volatility ambiguity: when the principal faces *Knightian uncertainty* about the noise process, optimal contracts depend on output and its quadratic variation, and the principal's value is characterized as the viscosity solution of a Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman–Isaacs equation [8]. The lineage from 1987 to these second-order backward stochastic differential equation formulations shows how durable the original insight is: the richness of the agent's dynamic strategy space remains the disciplining force that selects simple contracts.

## 4 Extensions and Frontiers

### 4.1 Multitask Agency and the Equal-Compensation Principle

The 1991 sequel by Holmström and Milgrom extends the framework to an agent who allocates effort across multiple tasks measured with differing precision [3]. The central result is the **equal-compensation principle**: the marginal return to effort must be equalized across all tasks the principal wants performed, including tasks whose output cannot be measured at all. If measurable tasks carry strong incentives while unmeasurable but valuable tasks carry none, the agent diverts all effort to what is measured — the formal version of "you get what you pay for, and only what you pay for."

The striking implication is that *weak* incentives can be optimal even when strong incentives are feasible. When some dimension of the job — teaching quality, long-run research, maintenance — is poorly measured, the principal optimally dulls incentives on the well-measured dimensions to protect the unmeasured ones. This explains flat salaries in bureaucracies, the prevalence of low-powered incentives inside firms, and a theory of job design: tasks should be grouped into jobs so that measurement characteristics are homogeneous, and asset ownership should follow incentive intensity, with strongly incentivized agents owning the assets they work with.

### 4.2 Validity of the First-Order Approach

The first-order approach — replacing global incentive compatibility with the agent's first-order condition — is the workhorse solution method of applied contract theory, yet the classical sufficient conditions justifying it are restrictive: they tie the validity of the approach to concavity properties of the payment schedule itself, which fails for common instruments like stock options. A recent line of research establishes **broad validity** of the first-order approach under substantially weaker conditions, showing that local deviations are the binding ones across a much wider class of technologies and contracts than previously recognized [7]. This matters because it licenses the tractable pointwise characterization of optimal contracts — payment responding to the likelihood ratio — in precisely the applied settings where the classical conditions were most suspect.

### 4.3 Robust Contracting under Knightian Uncertainty

A distinct frontier abandons the assumption that the principal knows the output distribution. Under **Knightian uncertainty**, the principal evaluates contracts against a worst case over a set of plausible models. The robust-contracting program shows that simple contracts — in particular linear ones — retain their appeal precisely because their performance degrades gracefully under misspecification, whereas finely tuned nonlinear schemes that exploit exact distributional knowledge collapse when that knowledge is wrong [8]. This provides a decision-theoretic foundation for contractual simplicity that complements the dynamic-gaming argument of the original 1987 paper: simplicity is what survives both strategic exploitation by the agent and model uncertainty on the part of the principal.

### 4.4 The Computational Lens on Contract Design

Computer science has recently taken up the principal-agent problem as an algorithmic question. When the agent has private *type* information in addition to hidden actions — combining moral hazard with adverse selection — computing the optimal single contract or menu of contracts is **APX-hard**, meaning no efficient algorithm can even approximate it arbitrarily well [6]. Against this hardness, linear contracts perform remarkably well as approximations: with *n* actions and *T* possible types, the best linear contract guarantees an *O(n log T)* approximation to the optimal profit. The complexity perspective thus supplies an independent justification for linearity: when exact optimization is intractable, the simple contract class is not merely convenient but provably near-optimal.

| Approach | Key assumption | Contract form | Main insight |
|---|---|---|---|
| Static moral hazard [1] | Hidden action, known distribution | Nonlinear in likelihood ratio | Informativeness principle; sufficient statistics |
| Holmström–Milgrom (1987) [2] | Continuous control, CARA, Brownian noise | Linear in aggregate | Gaming-proofness selects linearity; β* = 1/(1 + rkσ²) |

## 5 Incomplete Contracts and Property Rights

The entire analysis so far assumes *complete* contracting: every relevant contingency can be described ex ante and verified ex post. The theory of **incomplete contracts** starts from the opposite premise — that parties are boundedly rational, that many future states and actions cannot be foreseen or described in advance, and that relationship-specific investments are observable to the parties but not verifiable by a court [9]. When contracts are necessarily incomplete, the question is no longer what payment rule to write but *who gets to decide* when the contract is silent.

Grossman and Hart (1986) formalize ownership as the possession of **residual control rights**: the right to decide all uses of an asset not explicitly contracted upon [4]. Their framework has a canonical three-stage timing. Ex ante, parties make noncontractible, relationship-specific investments. Ex post, uncertainty resolves and the parties bargain over the gains from trade — renegotiation is assumed efficient, so ex post inefficiency is ruled out by construction and all distortions operate through ex ante investment incentives. Ownership matters because it determines the *disagreement point* of ex post bargaining: the owner can walk away with the asset and is therefore guaranteed at least the value of the asset in its next-best use, which shifts the division of surplus in the owner's favor and thereby strengthens the owner's incentive to invest.

![Property rights and incomplete contracting: the Grossman–Hart–Moore decision tree](thesis/ths_1789486330878_11283-2.webp)

The central normative result allocates each asset to the party whose investment is most important. More precisely, in the two-party, two-asset version: if one party's investment is indispensable, that party should own both assets; if investments are complementary at the margin, joint ownership is never optimal and assets should be held together by one party; and integration — one firm acquiring another — is efficient exactly when the acquirer's control raises the productivity of its management by more than the loss of control depresses the target's. The slogan version: **complementary assets should be commonly owned, and ownership should track the marginal importance of noncontractible investment**.

Hart and Moore (1990) extend the logic to many assets and many agents, delivering a full theory of the firm as a collection of commonly owned nonhuman assets, and the framework has been applied to the boundaries of the firm, internal organization, financial structure, privatization, and the organization of international trade [5]. The incomplete-contracts lens also reverses a naive intuition about contractual sophistication: because ex post renegotiation undoes any attempt to commit to inefficient outcomes, the parties cannot contract their way out of the hold-up problem, and the *governance structure* — who owns what — does the allocative work that prices do in complete-contract models.

## 6 Limitations

Each pillar of contract theory rests on assumptions that limit its reach, and the limitations are as instructive as the results.

*The complete-contracting program.* The static model assumes the principal knows the full mapping from effort to output distributions — an epistemic demand that the robust-contracting literature shows is both unrealistic and consequential, since optimal nonlinear schemes are fragile to exactly this knowledge [8]. The first-order approach, while now on firmer footing [7], still requires enough smoothness that discontinuous real-world instruments like termination threats and bonus caps sit uneasily in the framework. And the LEN model's tractability is purchased with CARA utility and normal noise, functional forms chosen for convenience rather than realism.

*The Holmström–Milgrom linearity result.* Linearity is optimal only because the agent can continuously recalibrate effort against a nonlinear schedule; in genuinely discrete or lumpy tasks, or where the principal can commit to monitoring technologies that restrict the agent's strategy space — random spot checks, for instance — nonlinear schemes can strictly dominate. The model also assumes away career concerns, intrinsic motivation, and multi-agent strategic interactions, all of which reshape optimal incentives in practice.

*The incomplete-contracts program.* The Grossman–Hart–Moore framework assumes costless, efficient ex post renegotiation, which rules out the very haggling and delay costs that transaction-cost economics emphasizes; critics note that it ignores bureaucratic failures of integration, denies any adaptability advantage of fiat within firms, and assumes courts enforce the contractible parts perfectly [5]. The Maskin–Tirole irrelevance critique further argues that with sufficiently clever message games, parties can replicate complete-contract outcomes, making incompleteness a matter of modeling choice rather than a deep friction. Finally, the framework's exclusive focus on ex ante investment incentives leaves no room for *ex post* inefficiency, which behavioral extensions — reference-dependent preferences, shading in the sense of Hart and Moore's later work — have begun to supply.

## 7 Conclusion

Contract theory's arc runs from the precise but fragile to the simple but robust. The static principal-agent model [1] delivers the informativeness principle and the likelihood-ratio characterization of optimal contracts, but its optimal schemes are too complex to resemble practice. The Holmström–Milgrom framework [2] resolves the anomaly: once the agent moves in continuous time against unfolding information, only linear contracts in the aggregate survive strategic gaming, and the LEN specification reduces the entire design problem to choosing a single slope that trades off incentives against risk. Multitask extensions [3] explain why organizations deliberately blunt incentives, robustness analysis [8] explains why simplicity survives model uncertainty, and complexity theory [6] explains why simplicity is near-optimal when exact design is intractable.

Where complete contracting reaches its limits, the incomplete-contracts tradition takes over: ownership as residual control [4] determines investment incentives through ex post bargaining power, and the optimal governance structure assigns assets to those whose noncontractible investments matter most. Together, the two traditions offer a unified account of economic organization — *prices and payment rules* where contingencies can be specified, *ownership and authority* where they cannot. The open frontiers are clear: contracts under ambiguity and behavioral frictions, the interplay of formal incentives with relational and reputational enforcement, and the algorithmic design of mechanisms for environments too complex for closed-form theory. What endures across all of it is the methodological core: write down who knows what, who moves when, and what can be verified — and the shape of the optimal institution follows.
