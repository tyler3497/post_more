# Bootstrapping Fully Homomorphic Encryption: The CKKS EvalMod Pipeline, FHEW Blind Rotation, and Programmable Bootstrapping for Arbitrary-Depth Computation

## Abstract

Fully homomorphic encryption (FHE) promises computation on encrypted data without trusted hardware, but every practical scheme pays for homomorphism with noise. In leveled schemes based on ring learning-with-errors (RLWE), each multiplication injects noise that eventually corrupts decryption. Bootstrapping is the *noise refresh*: it homomorphically evaluates the scheme's own decryption circuit, producing a ciphertext with bounded, input-independent noise and restoring full multiplicative budget. This thesis unifies the three mature bootstrapping paradigms. First, the Cheon–Kim–Kim–Song (CKKS) pipeline for approximate arithmetic — ModRaise, CoeffToSlot, EvalMod, SlotToCoeff — where modular reduction is approximated by a scaled sine function and its Chebyshev expansion. Second, the exact bit-level refresh of FHEW [2] and TFHE [3][4], centered on the blind-rotation accumulator and the GSW–LWE external product. Third, *programmable bootstrapping*, which folds arbitrary function evaluation into the refresh for free. We derive the noise recurrences governing correctness, compare measured performance, and show how parameters are chosen under the LWE estimator [7]: bootstrapping is no longer Gentry's prohibitive bottleneck, but its cost still dictates which applications are practical.

---

## 1. Introduction

Gentry's 2009 blueprint for fully homomorphic encryption introduced a deceptively simple idea: if a scheme can homomorphically evaluate its own decryption circuit *plus one more operation*, it can refresh any ciphertext and evaluate circuits of unbounded depth [1][2]. The refresh operation, **bootstrapping**, squashes accumulated noise back to a fixed level, making the scheme *fully* rather than merely *leveled* homomorphic. For over a decade, bootstrapping remained the dominant cost of FHE: early implementations in HElib required roughly six minutes per refresh, effectively pricing bootstrapping out of every application except offline batch computation.

The landscape has bifurcated since 2015 into two families that solve the noise problem in incompatible ways:

- **Exact schemes** (FHEW [2], TFHE [3][4]) work over bits or small integers on the torus, bootstrap *every gate*, and treat the refresh as the unit of computation. Blind rotation makes each NAND gate cost on the order of milliseconds.
- **Approximate schemes** (CKKS [1][6]) work over real or complex numbers with floating-point-like semantics, bootstrap infrequently at enormous batch width (thousands of slots), and accept an *approximation error* from evaluating a polynomial stand-in for modular reduction.

This thesis argues that the real object of study is not "the" bootstrapping algorithm but a design space parameterized by (i) what the plaintext ring is, (ii) how the decryption circuit is homomorphically evaluated, and (iii) how much error the application tolerates. The remainder of the thesis develops this space rigorously: Section 2 fixes the leveled setting and the noise recurrence; Section 3 explains the bootstrapping paradigm and the methodology used to compare constructions; Section 4 gives the deep technical treatment of CKKS EvalMod, FHEW/TFHE blind rotation, programmable bootstrapping, and parameter selection; Section 5 states theorems and measured results; Section 6 discusses limitations; Section 7 concludes.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 The noise problem in leveled FHE

Modern FHE schemes are built on the *ring learning-with-errors* (RLWE) problem over a cyclotomic ring $R = \mathbb{Z}[X]/(X^N+1)$, with $N$ a power of two. A secret key is a small polynomial $s \in R$, and a ciphertext encrypting a message $m$ under modulus $q$ satisfies a decryption invariant of the form

> **Decryption invariant.** For a ciphertext $\mathbf{ct} = (c_0, c_1)$, decryption computes $\langle \mathbf{ct}, s \rangle = c_0 + c_1 s = m + e \pmod q$, where $e$ is the *noise*. Decryption is correct iff $\|e\|_\infty < q / (2t)$ for a plaintext modulus $t$ (exact schemes) or $\|e\|_\infty \ll q$ for a scale $\Delta$ (approximate schemes).

Homomorphic addition adds noise linearly; homomorphic multiplication is brutal: multiplying two ciphertexts with noise $e_1, e_2$ produces a term whose noise scales as roughly $\|e_1\|\cdot\|m_2\| + \|e_2\|\cdot\|m_1\| + \|e_1\|\cdot\|e_2\|$, and it inflates the ciphertext dimension (tensoring), requiring **relinearization** with an evaluation key to bring it back down. To control the multiplicative blowup, schemes use **modulus switching**: after each multiplication, the modulus is divided by a factor, which shrinks the noise at the cost of one *level*. A ciphertext at level $\ell$ supports only $\ell$ further multiplications; when $\ell = 0$, computation must stop — this is *leveled* FHE, sufficient only for circuits whose depth is known in advance [6].

### 2.2 Two plaintext philosophies

- **BGV/BFV-style exact schemes** encode integers modulo $t$. Noise must stay below a hard threshold; crossing it is a catastrophic decryption failure. Bootstrapping must therefore be *exact*: the refreshed ciphertext must decrypt to precisely the right message.
- **CKKS-style approximate schemes** [1][5] encode scaled real numbers: the message lives in the high-order bits of a fixed-point representation and noise is simply *rounding error* in the low-order bits. A small approximation error is acceptable, and bootstrapping may itself introduce error, provided it stays below the application's precision budget. This relaxation is what makes the polynomial approximation of modular reduction possible.

## 3. Methodology

This thesis compares bootstrapping constructions along four axes:

1. **Correctness model** — exact versus approximate refresh, and the noise recurrence each construction guarantees after the bootstrap.
2. **Homomorphic decoding strategy** — how the construction evaluates the decryption circuit: via polynomial approximation of modular reduction (CKKS), or via blind rotation of a test polynomial (FHEW/TFHE).
3. **Cost model** — measured wall-clock time, bootstrapping-key size, and multiplicative depth consumed by the bootstrap itself.
4. **Security grounding** — how parameters $(N, q, \sigma)$ are validated against the LWE estimator [7] for a target bit-security level (128 bits).

Sources are primary: the original scheme papers [1][2][3][4], the functional-bootstrapping literature [5], the Eurocrypt 2021 refinement of CKKS bootstrapping with non-sparse keys [6], the LWE estimator [7], and the OpenFHE library documentation and paper [8].

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 The CKKS bootstrapping pipeline: ModRaise, CoeffToSlot, EvalMod, SlotToCoeff

CKKS ciphertexts at level $0$ live modulo a small modulus $q_0$. Cheon et al. [1] observed that the decryption relation $\langle \mathbf{ct}, s \rangle = m + q_0 I \pmod{q_0}$ for an integer polynomial $I$ can be lifted and then reduced *homomorphically* in four steps:

1. **ModRaise.** Treat the level-$0$ ciphertext as a ciphertext modulo a much larger modulus $Q \gg q_0$. Algebraically nothing changes, but now $\langle \mathbf{ct}, s \rangle = m + q_0 I$ holds *over the integers* (not just mod $q_0$), where $\|I\|_\infty \le O(\sqrt{h})$ with high probability for a secret key of Hamming weight $h$.
2. **CoeffToSlot (homomorphic encoding).** Apply the inverse discrete Fourier transform homomorphically to move the *coefficients* of the polynomial $m + q_0 I$ into the *slots* of the ciphertext, enabling slot-wise parallel evaluation. This is a linear map implemented by multiplying with plaintext DFT matrices, costing one or two levels.
3. **EvalMod (homomorphic modular reduction).** The heart of the construction. The map $x \mapsto x \bmod q_0$ is not a polynomial, so it cannot be evaluated homomorphically — but it can be *approximated*. Using the identity $x \bmod q_0 = x - q_0 \lfloor x/q_0 \rceil$ and approximating the sawtooth rounding function by a scaled sine,
$$ f(x) = \frac{q_0}{2\pi}\sin\left(\frac{2\pi x}{q_0}\right), $$
one obtains a smooth function that agrees with modular reduction on the intervals around multiples of $q_0$. The sine is then approximated by a low-degree Chebyshev polynomial (or a composition of sine-squaring iterations), which *is* homomorphically evaluable. Bossuat et al. [6] removed the sparse-secret restriction and showed how to pick the approximation domain from the bound on $\|I\|_\infty$; Lee et al. [9] gave a *direct* polynomial approximation of modular reduction with a lazy baby-step giant-step evaluator, reaching 93-bit precision at depth 11.
4. **SlotToCoeff (homomorphic decoding).** Apply the forward DFT homomorphically to move the reduced slots back into coefficients, yielding a ciphertext encrypting $m$ at a fresh level $L - k$, where $k$ is the depth consumed by the bootstrap.

The pipeline consumes roughly 13–15 multiplicative levels and introduces an approximation error that must be budgeted against the application's precision requirement — bootstrapping in CKKS is inherently a *precision refresh* as well as a noise refresh.

> **Theorem (CKKS bootstrap correctness, informal [1][6]).** Let the secret key have Hamming weight $h$ and let the EvalMod polynomial approximate modular reduction with error $\varepsilon$ on $[-Kq_0, Kq_0]$ with $K \ge \|I\|_\infty$. Then the bootstrapped ciphertext encrypts $m$ with error $O(\varepsilon + 2^{-p})$, where $p$ is the working precision, and supports $L-k$ further levels.

```python
# Pseudocode: the CKKS bootstrap as a four-stage pipeline
def ckks_bootstrap(ct_level0, bsk, evalmod_poly, dft_matrices):
    ct = mod_raise(ct_level0, Q_big)            # m + q0*I over the integers
    ct = homomorphic_idft(ct, dft_matrices)     # CoeffToSlot: coefficients -> slots
    ct = evaluate_chebyshev(ct, evalmod_poly)   # EvalMod: kill the q0*I term
    ct = homomorphic_dft(ct, dft_matrices)      # SlotToCoeff: slots -> coefficients
    return ct  # encrypts m at level L - k
```

### 4.2 FHEW: blind rotation and single-gate bootstrapping

Ducas and Micciancio [2] took the opposite approach: instead of approximating decryption, evaluate it *exactly* over bits using a technique now called **blind rotation**. The setting is LWE over the torus $\mathbb{T} = \mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}$. An LWE ciphertext $(\mathbf{a}, b)$ encrypts $\Delta \cdot m + e$ (message bit $m$ scaled by $\Delta$, plus noise $e$).

Blind rotation initializes an *accumulator* — an RLWE encryption of a **test polynomial** $v(X)$ — and then, for each coefficient $a_i$ of the LWE mask, multiplies the accumulator by $X^{-a_i s_i}$ homomorphically, using as key material GGSW encryptions of the secret-key bits $s_i$ (the bootstrapping key). Because the rotation amount $a_i s_i$ is applied *blindly* (multiplied under encryption), the accumulator ends up encrypting

$$ \mathrm{ACC} \cdot X^{-(\langle \mathbf{a}, s \rangle)} = \mathrm{ACC} \cdot X^{-(b - \Delta m - e)} \cdot X^{b} \approx v \cdot X^{\Delta m + e}. $$

A final **sample extraction** reads off the constant coefficient, yielding a fresh LWE encryption of the message bit with *fixed, small* noise. Crucially, the test polynomial $v$ can encode an arbitrary function of the phase: by choosing $v$ as a step function, the bootstrap *simultaneously evaluates a gate* (e.g., NAND) while refreshing — this is **gate bootstrapping**. FHEW achieved a full gate-plus-refresh in about 0.5–0.7 seconds with a bootstrapping key of roughly 1 GB [2].

### 4.3 TFHE: the external product and sub-0.1-second bootstrapping

Chillotti, Gama, Georgieva, and Izabachène [3][4] revisited FHEW through the lens of GSW. Their key observation: the internal GSW product can be replaced by a simpler **external product** between a GSW (or TGSW/TRGSW) ciphertext and an LWE (or TLWE/TRLWE) ciphertext. Blind rotation is then expressed entirely as a sequence of external products — CMUX-like updates of the accumulator — which:

- cut gate-bootstrapping time from under 1 second to under 0.1 seconds (about 13 ms per binary gate, 26 ms for MUX, on a single core [4]);
- shrank the bootstrapping key from 1 GB to about 24 MB at the same security level;
- reduced noise growth by replacing exact gadget decomposition with *approximate* decomposition;
- explained the characteristic noise asymmetry of GSW-style multiplication.

TFHE also generalized the message space to the full torus $\mathbb{T}$, so that plaintexts are elements of $\mathbb{T}$ rather than bits, and introduced efficient **circuit bootstrapping** (LWE-to-GSW conversion) for composing leveled and bootstrapped computation [4]. The external product is the workhorse:

$$\mathbf{C} \boxdot \mathbf{c} = \mathrm{Dec}(\mathbf{c}) \cdot \mathbf{C},$$

where a TRGSW ciphertext $\mathbf{C}$ multiplies a TRLWE ciphertext $\mathbf{c}$ by scaling with the gadget decomposition of $\mathbf{c}$ — the noise grows only with the decomposition error, not with the message.

```haskell
-- Sketch of blind rotation in the external-product formulation
blindRotate :: ACC -> BSK -> LWE -> ACC
blindRotate acc bsk (a, b) =
    foldl' step (rotatePoly acc (-b)) (zip [0..] a)
  where
    -- each step: acc <- CMUX(BSK_i, acc * X^{-a_i}, acc)
    step acc' (i, ai) = cmux (bsk ! i) (rotatePoly acc' (-ai)) acc'
-- sample extraction then reads the constant term of the accumulator
```

### 4.4 Programmable bootstrapping: arbitrary functions for free

In FHEW-like systems the bootstrap is, mathematically, just a *rounding* evaluated through a lookup table encoded in the test polynomial. The programmable (or *functional*) bootstrapping insight [4][5] is that the rounding LUT can be replaced by the LUT of an **arbitrary function** $f$ at no extra cost: the refresh that was already going to happen now also computes $f(m)$. A sign function, a ReLU, a sigmoid approximation, or any univariate map over the discretized torus is evaluated in the same ~13 ms as a plain gate bootstrap.

Carpov, Izabachène, and Mollimard extended this to **multi-value bootstrapping**, packing several LUTs into one bootstrap call, at the cost of larger parameters (a 6-bit-to-6-bit LUT costs on the order of 1.5 s [5]). Kwon et al. [5] showed how to compose multiple functional bootstraps to reach high precision without inflating parameters. The design pattern that results is powerful: *every nonlinear operation in an encrypted neural network or decision tree is folded into a bootstrap that had to happen anyway*, which is why TFHE-family libraries (TFHE-rs, Concrete) dominate encrypted-inference benchmarks.

### 4.5 Parameter selection and security: the LWE estimator

All of the above is meaningless without concrete parameters. The community standard is the **LWE estimator** of Albrecht, Player, and Scott [7] (now the lattice estimator at `github.com/malb/lattice-estimator`), which evaluates the cost of the best-known attacks — primal uSVP, dual, BDD with hints — against parameters $(n, q, \sigma)$ using BKZ cost models. The workflow is:

1. Fix a target security level (128 bits is the modern default; TFHE's library sets claim at least 110 bits under ideal-lattice assumptions [4]).
2. Choose the noise rate and key distribution (binary/ternary secrets have less entropy, which the estimator models explicitly).
3. Increase the ring dimension $N$ until the cheapest attack costs $\ge 2^{128}$ operations, accounting for the *largest modulus ever used* — including the bootstrapping key's modulus and, for CKKS, the full modulus chain $Q$.
4. Validate noise bounds: the estimator checks security; correctness (decryption failure probability $\le 2^{-40}$ or better) is checked by separate noise analysis, e.g., the mean-and-variance tracking in OpenFHE [8].

The tension is fundamental: larger $Q$ gives more levels and cheaper amortized bootstrapping but demands larger $N$ for the same security, which slows every operation. CKKS bootstrapping parameters therefore sit at $N = 2^{16}$ with $\log Q$ in the high hundreds, while TFHE gate bootstrapping uses $N = 1024$–$2048$ with a single modest modulus — fast per gate, but each gate pays a full bootstrap.

## 5. Empirical Results / Proofs

The table below summarizes representative measured performance. Figures are from the cited papers and library documentation; absolute numbers vary with hardware, but the *ratios* are stable across a decade of reproductions.

| Construction | Refresh granularity | Measured bootstrap time | Bootstrapping key | Security basis |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| FHEW [2] | 1 bit (gate) | ~0.5–0.7 s | ~1 GB | LWE, 128-bit est. |
| TFHE gate bootstrap [3][4] | 1 bit (gate) | ~13 ms (MUX ~26 ms) | ~24 MB | LWE/RLWE, ≥110-bit |
| TFHE programmable bootstrap [4][5] | arbitrary $f$, low precision | ~13 ms – 1.5 s (6-bit LUT) | ~24 MB – larger | as above |
| CKKS bootstrap [1] | $2^{15}$–$2^{16}$ slots | seconds – minutes | 100s of MB | RLWE, 128-bit est. |
| CKKS bootstrap, refined [6][9] | same, higher precision | comparable, better precision | comparable | non-sparse keys |

> **Theorem (blind-rotation noise bound, informal [3]).** Let the input LWE ciphertext have noise variance $\sigma_{in}^2$ and let the bootstrapping key have gadget decomposition with $\ell$ levels and base $B_g$. After blind rotation and sample extraction, the output noise variance is $\sigma_{out}^2 = O(n \ell \sigma_{bsk}^2 + N \sigma_{acc}^2)$, independent of $\sigma_{in}^2$, provided the phase $\Delta m + e$ stays within the correct half of the torus.

The independence from $\sigma_{in}^2$ is the whole point: the output noise depends only on *fixed* key material, so it can be made smaller than the input noise of any correctly-decrypting ciphertext — the defining property of a refresh.

A second, practical result: amortized cost. A CKKS bootstrap refreshes up to $2^{16}$ slots at once; even at 60 s per bootstrap, the amortized cost is under 1 ms per slot — competitive with TFHE's 13 ms per gate for wide SIMD workloads, while TFHE wins for narrow, deeply sequential boolean circuits. This is why no single scheme dominates: *the application geometry chooses the scheme*.

## 6. Limitations

1. **CKKS precision loss is one-directional.** Every bootstrap injects approximation error that can never be removed; long chains of bootstraps (hundreds) accumulate error that eventually swamps the signal. High-precision variants [6][9] push this boundary but cannot eliminate it — CKKS is unsuitable for computations requiring exact integer arithmetic.
2. **TFHE bootstrapping is per-gate.** Refreshing every gate means the 13 ms cost is paid $G$ times for a $G$-gate circuit. Throughput for large circuits is bounded by bootstrap latency, and only limited SIMD packing (via multi-value or WoP-PBS techniques [5]) is available, far less flexible than CKKS's native $2^{16}$-wide SIMD.
3. **Key sizes remain heavy.** Bootstrapping keys of tens of megabytes to gigabytes must be transmitted and stored; in client-aided models this is often the true bottleneck, dwarfing ciphertext expansion.
4. **Security estimates are heuristic.** The LWE estimator [7] models *known* attacks with *extrapolated* BKZ cost models; it cannot rule out algorithmic breakthroughs, and the gap between estimated and claimed security (designers routinely add margin) reflects genuine uncertainty.
5. **Side channels and malicious security.** The results above assume semi-honest evaluation; verifiable or maliciously-secure bootstrapping, and constant-time implementations resistant to timing attacks on blind rotation, remain active research areas.

---

## 7. Conclusion

Bootstrapping has traveled from Gentry's theoretical *squashing* — minutes per refresh, a proof-of-concept tax on every computation — to a menu of engineered trade-offs. The CKKS pipeline [1][6][9] turns modular reduction into a polynomial approximation problem and refreshes tens of thousands of real numbers at once, at the price of precision that can never be recovered. FHEW [2] and TFHE [3][4] turned the refresh into the unit of computation itself: blind rotation plus the external product made gate bootstrapping a 13-millisecond operation, and programmable bootstrapping [5] folded arbitrary nonlinear functions into the refresh for free. Underpinning all of it, the LWE estimator [7] converted parameter selection from folklore into a reproducible science, and libraries like OpenFHE [8] made these constructions usable by non-cryptographers.

The open frontier is convergence: schemes that combine CKKS-wide SIMD with TFHE-style functional bootstrapping, bootstrapping-friendly parameter regimes found by automated search, and hardware acceleration of the NTT and blind rotation. The noise problem is managed, not solved — but it is now managed well enough that encrypted computation has moved from the pages of theory into deployed systems.
