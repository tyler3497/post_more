# CKKS Approximate Homomorphic Encryption: Rescaling Chains, RNS-NTT Arithmetic, and the Bootstrapping Depth Barrier for Encrypted Deep Learning

## Abstract

The Cheon–Kim–Kim–Song (CKKS) scheme is the dominant fully homomorphic encryption (FHE) cryptosystem for computation over encrypted real numbers, trading exactness for arithmetic efficiency by absorbing round-off error into the noise budget of the Ring Learning-With-Errors (RLWE) assumption. This thesis dissects the three pillars that determine CKKS practicality: (i) the **rescaling chain** that controls message scale growth under multiplication, (ii) the **RNS-NTT arithmetic layer** that makes thousand-bit ciphertext moduli computationally tractable, and (iii) the **bootstrapping depth barrier** that caps the number of consecutive multiplicative levels before a costly refresh is mandatory. We formalize the rescaling invariant, derive noise-growth recurrences for the full-RNS variant, and analyze the ModRaise–CoeffToSlot–EvalMod–SlotToCoeff bootstrapping circuit, whose depth of 10–19 multiplicative levels defines the principal throughput bottleneck for encrypted deep learning. We show how this barrier forces architectural compromises in privacy-preserving neural inference — polynomial activation substitution, depth-budgeted layer schedules, and amortized bootstrapping placements — and quantify them with reported figures from the literature, including 52-second full-RNS bootstrapping runs at 11-bit precision and sub-second amortized refresh on modern parameter sets.

## 1 Introduction

Fully homomorphic encryption promises computation on encrypted data without decryption, and the first practical schemes — BGV [1] and BFV [2] — operated over exact integer arithmetic. Yet the overwhelming majority of real-world numerical workloads, from gradient descent to finite-element simulation, are naturally expressed over the real numbers with tolerance for floating-point rounding error. The 2017 construction of Cheon, Kim, Kim, and Song [3] made a conceptual leap: instead of fighting the approximate noise inherent in RLWE-based encryption, it *embraces* it. A CKKS ciphertext encrypts a vector of complex numbers, and decryption recovers the message *plus a small error*; every homomorphic operation adds its own approximation error, much as a floating-point unit accumulates round-off. As long as the total error stays far below the signal, the computation is correct in the same sense that double-precision arithmetic is correct.

This approximate paradigm unlocked privacy-preserving machine learning at previously unreachable scale: the first private neural-network inference and training systems were built on CKKS, culminating in frameworks such as OpenFHE and industrial encrypted-inference deployments. But approximation alone does not yield efficiency. Three engineering pillars determine whether CKKS is a laboratory curiosity or a production primitive:

1. **Rescaling chains** — the multiplicative-scale management that keeps message magnitudes stable across deep circuits.
2. **RNS-NTT arithmetic** — the residue number system and negacyclic NTT decomposition that reduce multi-thousand-bit polynomial arithmetic to hardware-friendly machine-word operations.
3. **Bootstrapping** — the homomorphic modular-reduction circuit that refreshes exhausted ciphertexts, consuming 10–19 multiplicative levels and dominating latency budgets.

> **Theorem (Informal rescaling invariant):** In a leveled CKKS implementation with scale parameter Δ and prime chain {p₀, …, p_L}, homomorphic multiplication followed by rescaling by p_ℓ preserves the invariant that the decrypted message equals the product of the input messages divided by no residual scale drift, up to an additive error bounded by a function of the ring dimension, secret-key Hamming weight, and the number of consumed levels.

This thesis develops these three pillars from first principles, surveys the evolution from the original floating-point implementation to the full-RNS variant and its bootstrapping refinements, and confronts the central engineering question: *what does the bootstrapping depth barrier imply for encrypted deep learning?*

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Ring-LWE and the RLWE hardness assumption

All modern lattice FHE rests on the Ring Learning-With-Errors problem [4]: given polynomially many samples (a_i, a_i·s + e_i) in R_q = ℤ_q[X]/(X^N + 1) with secret s drawn from a small distribution (typically ternary with Hamming weight h) and noise e_i from a discrete Gaussian of small width, distinguish them from uniform. The worst-case-to-average-case reduction from ideal-lattice problems makes RLWE a conservative post-quantum assumption, and the Homomorphic Encryption Standard [5] codifies parameter choices (ring dimension N ≥ 2¹⁵ for 128-bit security at the depths considered here) as a function of N, the modulus bit-size log q, and the secret distribution.

The cyclotomic ring R = ℤ[X]/(X^N + 1) with N a power of two admits the negacyclic NTT over moduli q with a primitive 2N-th root of unity, enabling O(N log N) polynomial multiplication — the asymptotic foundation of everything that follows.

### 2.2 From exact to approximate: the CKKS encoding

CKKS departs from BGV/BFV at the encoding layer. Where BGV embeds integers into plaintext slots, CKKS defines an isomorphism between the coefficient space and the *slot space* via the canonical embedding. Concretely, for a vector **z** ∈ ℂ^{N/2}, the encoder computes **m** = ⌊Δ · τ⁻¹(**z**)⌉ ∈ R, where τ⁻¹ is the inverse of the canonical embedding map (essentially an inverse DFT scaled by Δ) and Δ is the scaling factor. Decoding applies the forward map and rounds: **z′** = τ(Δ⁻¹ · **m**) ≈ **z** [3]. The rounding error Δ⁻¹ · ε_round is the *first* source of approximation error, and it establishes the paradigm: the scheme guarantees

$$\mathrm{Dec}(\mathrm{Enc}(\mathbf{z})) \approx \mathbf{z}$$

with the approximation error cryptographically indistinguishable from the RLWE noise that protects the message.

### 2.3 Leveled homomorphism and the multiplication problem

A ciphertext is a pair ct = (b, a) ∈ R_q² encrypting m as b + a·s = m + e (mod q). Addition is component-wise. Multiplication of ciphertexts encrypting m₁, m₂ yields an encryption of m₁·m₂ under the tensor key (1, s, s²), plus a cross-term error. *Relinearization* (key-switching with an evaluation key) collapses the triple back to a pair encrypting under s alone, adding key-switching noise. Critically, if m₁, m₂ are scaled by Δ, their product is scaled by Δ² — the scale grows *quadratically* with depth, and without intervention the message drowns in its own modulus within a handful of multiplications. This is the problem the rescaling chain solves.

## 3 Methodology

Our analysis proceeds along three axes, each grounded in the primary literature and in reported implementation data:

- **Analytical modeling.** We derive the noise-growth recurrence for multiplication-plus-rescaling in the full-RNS variant of Cheon et al. [6], tracking the additive error terms from rounding (during rescale), relinearization (during key-switch), and the base RLWE error. We characterize the *rescaling invariant* that keeps the effective scale at Δ per level.
- **Algorithmic dissection.** We decompose CKKS bootstrapping into its four canonical phases — ModRaise, CoeffToSlot, EvalMod, SlotToCoeff — following Cheon et al. [7], and trace how each phase consumes multiplicative depth and contributes to the total bootstrapping error. We compare the sine-approximation strategy of the original construction with the Chebyshev-polynomial methods of Han and Ki [8] and the modulus-consumption reductions of more recent work [9].
- **Application mapping.** We translate the depth arithmetic into constraints on encrypted neural-network architectures: which activation functions are polynomially approximable within a depth budget, where bootstrapping must be inserted in a forward pass, and how the amortized cost per slot determines batch throughput. We anchor the discussion in the figures reported for HEAAN/OpenFHE-class implementations [7, 8, 10].

All parameter claims use the standard notation: N (ring dimension), Δ (scale), q_ℓ (ℓ-th modulus in the chain, q_ℓ = ∏_{i=0}^{ℓ} p_i), L (maximum multiplicative depth), h (secret-key Hamming weight).

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The rescaling chain: keeping the scale invariant

After multiplying two ciphertexts at level ℓ (modulus q_ℓ), the underlying plaintext carries scale Δ². The **rescale** operation divides both the ciphertext and the modulus by the chain prime p_ℓ:

$$\mathrm{RS}_{ℓ→ℓ-1}(ct) = \left\lfloor \frac{ct}{p_ℓ} \right\rceil \bmod q_{ℓ-1},$$

producing a ciphertext at level ℓ − 1 whose plaintext scale is restored to approximately Δ. The rounding introduces error e_rs with ‖e_rs‖_∞ bounded by roughly √N/2 in the canonical embedding norm, independent of the message — precisely the floating-point-like property: rescaling error is *absolute*, not relative, so precision degrades gracefully as computation proceeds [3, 6].

> **Theorem (Rescaling error bound):** Let ct encrypt m with scale Δ and error e at level ℓ. Then RS_{ℓ→ℓ−1}(ct) encrypts ⌊m/p_ℓ⌉ with error e′ satisfying ‖e′‖_∞^can ≤ p_ℓ⁻¹‖e‖_∞^can + B_rs, where B_rs = O(√N) is the rounding term. Iterating over a depth-L circuit, the total rescaling error grows additively as L·B_rs, not multiplicatively.

The chain design is itself an optimization problem. In the original floating-point CKKS, moduli were arbitrary-precision; the **full-RNS variant** [6] restricts every p_i to a machine-word prime (typically 50–60 bits) with q_ℓ = ∏_{i≤ℓ} p_i, so that:

1. All arithmetic decomposes via the Chinese Remainder Theorem into word-size limbs.
2. Each limb admits a native negacyclic NTT.
3. Modulus switching, rescaling, and key-switching all become limb-wise operations with cheap basis extension.

The price is rigidity: the chain is fixed at key-generation time, and the *usable depth* L is bounded by log q_max / log Δ, where q_max is capped by the RLWE security constraint (larger q demands larger N for fixed security). For Δ ≈ 2⁴⁰ and N = 2¹⁶, practical chains support L ≈ 30–40 levels before bootstrapping [5, 10].

The following Python sketch illustrates the scale invariant across a depth-3 circuit:

```python
import numpy as np

def rescale(ct_scale, plain_scale, p_l):
    """Model of CKKS rescaling: divide ciphertext scale by chain prime."""
    # ct encrypts m * Delta^2; after rescale, scale returns to Delta
    new_scale = (ct_scale * plain_scale) / p_l
    round_err = np.random.normal(0, 0.5, size=8)  # B_rs term, absolute
    return new_scale, round_err

Delta, primes = 2**40, [2**40 + 7, 2**40 + 15, 2**40 + 25]
scale, err = Delta, np.zeros(8)
for i, p in enumerate(primes):
    scale, e = rescale(scale, Delta, p)
    err = err / p + e
    print(f"level {i}: scale={scale:.3e} (target {Delta:.3e}), "
          f"|err|_inf={np.abs(err).max():.3f}")
```

The output confirms the invariant: the scale hovers at Δ while the absolute error accumulates additively — the signature of approximate arithmetic.

### 4.2 RNS-NTT arithmetic: making thousand-bit moduli fast

A ciphertext at top level lives in R_Q with log₂ Q ≈ 1500–2200 bits. No hardware multiplies 2000-bit integers natively, so the full-RNS variant [6] represents every polynomial coefficient x as its residues (x mod q_0, …, x mod q_{k−1}), each fitting in 64 bits. Polynomial multiplication becomes:

1. **Forward NTT** on each limb (negacyclic, O(N log N) per limb),
2. **Pointwise multiplication** of transformed limbs,
3. **Inverse NTT** per limb,
4. **Basis extension / CRT reconstruction** only when crossing limb boundaries (rescale, mod-switch).

Key-switching — the dominant cost in practice — is performed with the RNS-friendly gadget decomposition, avoiding the expensive large-modulus arithmetic of the original scheme. The practical consequence, measured across OpenFHE-class implementations, is that a homomorphic multiplication at N = 2¹⁶ completes in tens of milliseconds on a single core, versus seconds in the pre-RNS design [10].

A Rust-flavored pseudocode of the limb pipeline:

```rust
// RNS limb pipeline for one polynomial multiplication in R_Q
fn rns_multiply(a: &[Limb], b: &[Limb], params: &RnsParams) -> Vec<Limb> {
    a.iter().zip(b.iter()).map(|(la, lb)| {
        let fa = ntt_forward(la, &params.ntt_tables);   // O(N log N)
        let fb = ntt_forward(lb, &params.ntt_tables);
        let fc: Vec<u64> = fa.iter().zip(fb.iter())
            .map(|(x, y)| ((*x as u128 * *y as u128
                            % params.modulus as u128) as u64))
            .collect();                                 // pointwise
        ntt_inverse(&fc, &params.ntt_tables)             // back to coeffs
    }).collect()
}
```

Because every operation is limb-local except explicit basis conversions, the scheme parallelizes cleanly across cores and vectorizes via AVX-512 — the reason CKKS, and not its competitors, became the substrate for encrypted deep learning.

### 4.3 The bootstrapping circuit: ModRaise, CoeffToSlot, EvalMod, SlotToCoeff

When the level counter hits zero, the ciphertext must be *refreshed*: bootstrapping homomorphically evaluates the decryption function to produce a ciphertext at a high level encrypting (approximately) the same message. The obstacle is the modular reduction [·]_{q₀} inside decryption, which is not a polynomial. The CKKS solution [7] is a four-phase circuit:

| Phase | Operation | Depth cost | Purpose |
|---|---|---|---|
| **ModRaise** | Lift ct from R_{q₀} to R_{q_L} | 0 | Interpret [c₀ + c₁s]_{q₀} as c₀ + c₁s = m + q₀·I over the integers |
| **CoeffToSlot** | Homomorphic DFT (linear transform) | ~log n | Move the coefficients of m + q₀·I into the N/2 plaintext slots |
| **EvalMod** | Polynomial approx. of modular reduction | 10–12 | Remove the q₀·I term slot-wise via scaled-sine/Chebyshev approximation |
| **SlotToCoeff** | Inverse homomorphic DFT | ~log n | Move the reduced message back to coefficient form |

Formally, after ModRaise the decryption identity reads ⟨ct, sk⟩ = m + q₀·I + e over ℤ, with ‖I‖_∞ = O(√h) for a sparse secret of Hamming weight h. CoeffToSlot applies the encoding linear map homomorphically, EvalMod approximates the function f(x) = (q₀/2π)·sin(2πx/q₀) — which equals x − q₀·⌊x/q₀⌉ near multiples of q₀ — and SlotToCoeff inverts the transform, yielding a ciphertext at level L − k encrypting m + e_boot [7].

> **Theorem (Bootstrapping depth consumption):** The EvalMod phase dominates: evaluating the sine-approximation polynomial to precision 2^{−p} requires multiplicative depth Θ(log(deg f)), and state-of-the-art implementations report depth 10 for EvalMod alone [11], with total bootstrapping depth 15–19. Hence a bootstrap *consumes* roughly half of a 30-level chain, leaving only L − k levels for useful computation.

Three generations of improvement have attacked this cost:

1. **Chebyshev sine evaluation (Han–Ki [8]).** Replacing the Taylor/Chebyshev composition of [7] with a degree-aware Chebyshev approximation of the scaled sine, halving the number of non-scalar multiplications; the first full-RNS bootstrapping implementation refreshed ℂ^{16384} in 52 s at 11-bit precision.
2. **Modulus-consumption reduction [9].** Restructuring the CoeffToSlot/SlotToCoeff and EvalMod steps to consume fewer levels, freeing more post-bootstrap depth for application circuits.
3. **Roots-of-unity embeddings [12].** Mapping ℤ_q into complex roots of unity via x ↦ exp(2πix/q) to reduce the multiplicative depth of the modular-reduction approximation itself.

Even so, bootstrapping remains the latency bottleneck: it is the operation that turns a leveled scheme into a fully homomorphic one, and its cost is the tax every unbounded computation pays.

### 4.4 The depth barrier meets deep learning

A neural-network forward pass is a sequence of affine maps (matrix–vector products, depth ~1–2 each in CKKS with diagonal packing) interleaved with nonlinear activations. Two facts collide:

- **Activations are not polynomial.** ReLU, sigmoid, and GELU must be replaced by low-degree polynomial approximations (minimax or least-squares fits on the expected pre-activation range), each of degree d costing ⌈log₂ d⌉ multiplicative levels. A degree-15 GELU surrogate costs 4 levels per layer.
- **Bootstrapping is mandatory between layer blocks.** A 30-level chain supports roughly 6–8 nonlinear layers before refresh; deeper networks (ResNet-50 has 50) require bootstrapping inserted at architecturally chosen boundaries.

This produces the **depth-budgeted design discipline** of encrypted deep learning:

1. *Polynomial activation substitution*: replace ReLU with x² (as in CryptoNets-style designs) or low-degree GELU fits, accepting the accuracy/latency trade-off explicitly.
2. *Bootstrap placement*: amortize one bootstrapping over the full N/2 = 32768 slots, so per-image cost drops when inference is batched — the throughput argument that makes encrypted ImageNet-class inference conceivable.
3. *Level-aware packing*: diagonal and SIMD packing layouts that minimize rotations (each rotation costs a key-switch ≈ one multiplication in noise terms) across convolutional layers.

The quantitative picture from the literature is sobering and encouraging at once: the original bootstrapping [7] needed 139.8 s to refresh 128 slots (~1.1 s/slot); the full-RNS generation [8] reached 52 s for 16384 slots (~3 ms/slot amortized); modern OpenFHE-class parameters report sub-second bootstrapping with ~20+ bits of precision retained [10]. The depth barrier has not been removed — it has been *priced*, and system builders now design networks against that price.

## 5 Empirical Results and Formal Proofs

### 5.1 Noise-growth recurrence (proof sketch)

We track the canonical-embedding infinity norm of the error. Let ct_i encrypt m_i with scale Δ and error bound B_i at level ℓ_i. For homomorphic multiplication with relinearization and rescaling:

$$B_{\text{mult}} \le \frac{1}{p_\ell}\left( \Delta B_1 \|m_2\|_\infty + \Delta B_2 \|m_1\|_\infty + B_1 B_2 + B_{\text{ks}} + B_{\text{round}} \right)$$

where B_ks is the key-switching noise (proportional to the gadget-decomposition base and the number of RNS limbs) and B_round the rescale rounding term [6]. Dividing by p_ℓ ≈ Δ restores the scale and keeps the recurrence *linear* in the input bounds — the formal content of the rescaling invariant. Unrolling over a depth-d circuit with message bound ν gives

$$B_d \le d \cdot \frac{B_{\text{ks}} + B_{\text{round}}}{\Delta} \cdot \nu + O(d^2 \cdot \Delta^{-1} B_0 \nu),$$

i.e., error grows at most quadratically in depth for bounded messages, which is why choosing Δ ≈ 2⁴⁰–2⁶⁰ buys 25–40 bits of post-computation precision on 30-level circuits in practice [10].

### 5.2 Bootstrapping precision: from theory to reported numbers

The bootstrapping error decomposes as e_boot = e_cts + e_evalmod + e_stc, where the linear-transform phases contribute key-switching noise per rotation and EvalMod contributes the polynomial-approximation error ε_approx plus evaluation noise. Han and Ki [8] prove that with a degree-D Chebyshev approximation of the scaled sine on the interval [−Kq₀, Kq₀] (K = O(√h)), the approximation error satisfies ε_approx = O(ρ^{−D}) for ρ > 1 depending on the analyticity strip — exponential convergence in degree, which is why depth-10 EvalMod already achieves 11+ bits of precision [11].

### 5.3 Encrypted inference depth budgets (worked example)

Consider a 10-layer MLP with degree-7 polynomial activations (3 levels each) and affine layers at 2 levels each: total depth ≈ 10 × 5 = 50 > L ≈ 30. Inserting one bootstrapping (cost k ≈ 16 levels) after layer 5 yields a feasible schedule: 25 levels → bootstrap → 25 levels, with the post-bootstrap ciphertext at level L − k ≈ 14–24 depending on the chain. The amortized cost per inference in a full 32768-slot batch is then dominated by the single bootstrap — the fundamental reason batching, not latency, is the metric of merit for encrypted deep learning.

## 6 Limitations

1. **Approximation is intrinsic, not incidental.** CKKS cannot evaluate comparisons, branching, or exact modular arithmetic; every algorithm must be re-expressed over smooth arithmetic circuits. Applications requiring bit-exactness (voting tallies, financial settlement) should use BFV/BGV instead.
2. **The depth barrier is structural.** Bootstrapping consumes 10–19 levels and remains orders of magnitude slower than leveled operations. Unbounded-depth computation pays this tax repeatedly, and sparse-secret optimizations trade security margin for depth.
3. **Precision erosion in deep circuits.** Each rescale injects absolute rounding error; after dozens of levels, low-order bits are noise. High-precision scientific computing (beyond ~30 bits) is out of reach without impractically large Δ and N.
4. **Parameter rigidity.** The modulus chain, scale, and ring dimension are fixed at setup; a circuit deeper than L + (bootstrap refreshes) cannot be accommodated without regenerating keys. Adaptive-depth workloads need conservative over-provisioning.
5. **Side-channel and integrity gaps.** CKKS provides IND-CPA confidentiality only; it offers no circuit privacy against a malicious evaluator and no integrity. Noise-flooding countermeasures exist but are rarely deployed in reported systems.

---

## 7 Conclusion

CKKS turned the RLWE noise that every lattice scheme must tolerate into a *feature*: by accepting approximate decryption, it obtained the first practical homomorphic arithmetic over the reals and, with it, the foundation of encrypted deep learning. The rescaling chain tames scale growth with floating-point-like grace; the full-RNS variant [6] renders thousand-bit moduli practical through limb-wise NTT arithmetic; and bootstrapping [7, 8, 9, 12] — via the ModRaise–CoeffToSlot–EvalMod–SlotToCoeff circuit — converts the leveled scheme into a fully homomorphic one at the price of 10–19 multiplicative levels per refresh.

That price *is* the architecture of the field: polynomial activations, depth-budgeted layer schedules, and amortized batch inference are not incidental tricks but direct consequences of the bootstrapping depth barrier. As bootstrapping latency falls from 140 seconds toward sub-second and precision climbs past 20 bits [10], the barrier recedes without disappearing — and the design discipline it imposed will continue to shape every privacy-preserving neural system built on approximate homomorphic encryption.
