# Quantum Error Mitigation for NISQ Devices: Zero-Noise Extrapolation, Probabilistic Error Cancellation, Learning-Based and Distillation Methods, and the Fundamental Sampling-Overhead Walls

## Abstract


Quantum error **mitigation** (QEM) and quantum error *correction* (QEC) are distinct responses to noise: correction encodes logical information redundantly at large qubit cost, whereas mitigation accepts imperfect hardware and uses classical post-processing of many noisy circuit executions to recover unbiased or nearly unbiased estimates of ideal expectation values. This thesis treats the principal mitigation techniques for noisy intermediate-scale quantum (NISQ) devices: **zero-noise extrapolation (ZNE)** with unitary-folding noise scaling and Richardson extrapolation; **probabilistic error cancellation (PEC)** via quasiprobability decomposition and its $\gamma$-factor sampling overhead; **Clifford data regression (CDR)**; and **virtual distillation (VD)** by derangement-based symmetry filtering. We formalize the bias–variance–overhead trade-offs of each method and present the information-theoretic lower bounds of Takagi et al. [4] and Quek et al. [5], showing the sampling overhead of *any* general mitigation protocol scales exponentially with circuit depth under local depolarizing noise. We close by sharply distinguishing the mitigation regime from the fault-tolerance threshold regime.

## 1. Introduction

Contemporary quantum processors — superconducting transmon arrays, trapped ions, neutral-atom systems — operate in the NISQ regime: tens to a few thousand physical qubits, two-qubit gate infidelities of order $10^{-3}$ to $10^{-2}$, and no fault-tolerant error correction [1]. On such devices, a circuit of depth $d$ acting on $n$ qubits accumulates an expected error count $\lambda \sim \epsilon n d$, where $\epsilon$ is a per-gate error rate. For variational quantum algorithms (VQAs) and other near-term workloads, $\lambda$ of order unity or more rapidly washes out the signal, driving output distributions toward the maximally mixed state.

Two philosophies address this problem. **Quantum error correction** stores logical qubits in entangled code spaces, detects syndromes, and corrects errors in real time; it converges to arbitrarily accurate computation only once physical error rates fall below a *threshold* and enough redundant qubits are available. **Quantum error mitigation** instead treats the noisy device as a black box and invests *classical sampling effort* — more circuit executions, clever post-processing — to extract ideal expectation values $\langle O \rangle_{\text{ideal}} = \operatorname{Tr}[O\,\rho_{\text{ideal}}]$ from noisy measurements. Mitigation needs no extra qubits and no real-time feedback, but its sampling cost grows exponentially with the accumulated error rate [4].

> **Theorem:** (informal sampling-overhead law) For layered circuits subject to local depolarizing noise with expected error count $\lambda = \epsilon n d$, any mitigation protocol attaining a target accuracy $\delta$ with bounded variance requires a sampling overhead $C_{\text{em}}$ that is exponential in $\lambda$ under mild generality assumptions. — Takagi et al. [4]

This thesis organizes the field around four pillars and the bounds that constrain them. Section 2 establishes the noise models and the formal mitigation problem. Section 3 describes the unified methodology: every protocol is a procedure that takes $N$ noisy samples and returns an estimator $\hat{O}_{\text{em}}$, characterized by its **bias** $\mathbb{E}[\hat{O}_{\text{em}}] - \langle O \rangle_{\text{ideal}}$, its **variance** $\operatorname{Var}[\hat{O}_{\text{em}}]$, and its **sampling overhead** $C_{\text{em}}$ relative to the unmitigated measurement cost. Section 4 dives deeply into ZNE, PEC, CDR, and VD, plus the lower-bound machinery. Section 5 combines empirical evidence with proof sketches. Section 6 and 7 close with limitations, open problems, and conclusions.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 Noise models for NISQ devices

The most analytically tractable noise models are local and Markovian. The *depolarizing channel* on a single qubit,

$$\mathcal{D}_p(\rho) = (1-p)\rho + \frac{p}{3}(X\rho X + Y\rho Y + Z\rho Z),$$

replaces the state with the maximally mixed state $I/2$ with probability $p$. The *dephasing* (phase-damping) channel $\mathcal{Z}_p(\rho) = (1-p)\rho + p Z\rho Z$ is diagonal in the Pauli basis and appears prominently in PEC optimality analyses [4]. Real devices are better described by **sparse Pauli–Lindblad** models, in which the noise of each gate layer is a product of Pauli channels generated by geometrically local Pauli strings; these models are learnable from experiments and invertible for PEC [3].

A layered circuit $\mathcal{C} = \mathcal{L}_d \circ \cdots \circ \mathcal{L}_1$ of $d$ noisy layers with per-layer error rate $p$ has total *circuit fault rate* $\lambda \approx p\, n\, d$. A phenomenological but powerful approximation is that the noisy expectation value decays as

$$E(\lambda) = \operatorname{Tr}[O\,\rho_\lambda] \approx \langle O \rangle_{\text{ideal}} e^{-\lambda} + \text{(noise-floor terms)},$$

which motivates exponential ansätze in ZNE and explains why $\lambda \gtrsim 1$ marks the onset of severe signal loss.

### 2.2 The mitigation problem, formalized

Given a device implementing $\mathcal{U}_{\text{noisy}}$ and an observable $O$ (with $\|O\| \le 1$), a mitigation protocol $\mathcal{P}$ consumes $N$ executions and outputs $\hat{O}_{\text{em}}$. Three figures of merit govern its quality:

1. **Bias**: $b = |\mathbb{E}[\hat{O}_{\text{em}}] - \langle O \rangle_{\text{ideal}}|$. Zero bias (as in ideal PEC) is the gold standard; ZNE and CDR are generically biased by model-misspecification terms.
2. **Variance**: $\sigma^2 = \operatorname{Var}[\hat{O}_{\text{em}}]$. Because estimators are linear combinations with large coefficients or signed weights, $\sigma^2$ is inflated relative to raw sampling; reducing it to shot-noise level costs more shots.
3. **Sampling overhead**: $C_{\text{em}} = N_{\text{mitigated}} / N_{\text{unmitigated}}$, the multiplicative factor in circuit executions needed to match the variance of the unmitigated estimator.

| Method | Extra qubits | Bias | Variance/overhead scaling | Noise knowledge required |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| ZNE (Richardson) | 0 | $\mathcal{O}(\lambda^M)$ for $M$ noise points | $C_{\text{em}} \sim \gamma_{\text{Rich}}^2$, super-exponential in $M$ | None (black-box noise scaling) |
| PEC | 0 | Zero (with exact noise model) | $\gamma^{2nd}$, $\gamma$ from quasiprobability | Full gate-noise model, learned |
| CDR | 0 | Residual fit error | Small (regression) | None; learned from Clifford data |
| Virtual distillation ($M$ copies) | $(M-1)n$ | Floor set by dominant eigenvector | $\sim 1/\operatorname{Tr}[\rho^M]^2$ | None |

The table already hints at a unifying theme: **accuracy is purchased with samples**, and the exchange rate is exponential in the noise volume [1,4].

### 2.3 Mitigation vs. correction

It is worth stressing the distinction, since the two are often conflated. *Error correction* reduces the *logical* error rate per gate; below the fault-tolerance threshold, increasing code distance *reduces* logical error, and the sampling cost does not blow up. *Error mitigation* does not touch the physical noise at all — it improves the *classical estimate* of an expectation value. Its cost is sampling overhead, and there is **no threshold phenomenon**: no amount of mitigation turns a depth-$10^6$ noisy circuit into a reliable one, because the overhead grows as $\exp(\Omega(\lambda))$ [5]. Mitigation extends the *useful horizon* of NISQ hardware; correction is the path to *scalable* quantum computation. Recent work even combines them, applying ZNE on top of logical qubits [8].

## 3. Methodology

Every protocol in this thesis follows one of three classical-inference patterns over noisy quantum data:

- **(a) Noise-scaling and extrapolation** (ZNE): deliberately amplify the noise by known factors $\lambda_k = c_k \lambda$, measure $E(\lambda_k)$, and infer $E(0)$ by regression.
- **(b) Noise inversion by signed sampling** (PEC): characterize the noise channels, decompose their inverses into quasiprobability distributions over implementable noisy operations, and sample accordingly.
- **(c) Learning and purification** (CDR, VD): learn an empirical noise map from classically tractable surrogate circuits (CDR), or exploit multi-copy symmetry to project onto the dominant eigenvector (VD).

The methodology of this thesis is to (i) derive the estimator and its bias for each method, (ii) compute its sampling overhead in terms of device parameters, (iii) survey the empirical record on superconducting and trapped-ion hardware, and (iv) place each method against the universal lower bounds [4,5]. We draw on the comprehensive review of Cai et al. [1] as a taxonomic backbone.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Zero-Noise Extrapolation: noise scaling and Richardson extrapolation

ZNE, introduced by Li and Benjamin [1] and by Temme, Bravyi, and Gambetta [2], proceeds in two steps. First, **noise scaling**: transform the circuit $\mathcal{C}$ into $\mathcal{C}_{\lambda_k}$ with amplified noise level $\lambda_k \ge \lambda$. The two standard implementations are:

- **Unitary folding**: replace each gate $U$ by $U (U^\dagger U)^i$, i.e.,
  $$U \longrightarrow U\,(U^\dagger U)^i,$$
  which is logically the identity transformation but triples (for $i=1$) the noise of that gate under gate-independent noise [8]. Partial and random folding allow fine-grained scale factors $c \in [1,3]$.
- **Pulse stretching** (for pulse-level control): stretch gate durations by factor $c$ while rescaling amplitudes, amplifying decoherence-dominated noise.

Second, **extrapolation**: fit a model $f(\lambda; \theta)$ to the measured points $\{(\lambda_k, E(\lambda_k))\}_{k=1}^M$ and evaluate $f(0; \theta^*)$. The classical options form a bias–variance ladder:

- *Linear*: $f = \theta_0 + \theta_1 \lambda$ — low variance, $\mathcal{O}(\lambda^2)$ bias.
- *Exponential*: $f = \theta_0 + \theta_1 e^{-\theta_2 \lambda}$ — matches the phenomenological decay, good at moderate $\lambda$.
- *Richardson*: interpolate with the unique degree-$(M-1)$ polynomial through all $M$ points and read off the intercept. The mitigated estimator is the Lagrange combination [8]
  $$\mathbb{E}[\hat{O}_{\text{em}}] = \sum_{m=1}^{M} \operatorname{Tr}[O\rho_{\lambda_m}] \prod_{k \ne m} \frac{\lambda_k}{\lambda_k - \lambda_m},$$
  with bias $\mathcal{O}(\lambda^M)$ — the smallest bias of any polynomial method — but variance inflated by the squared $\ell_1$ norm of the Lagrange weights:
  $$C_{\text{em}} \sim \left( \sum_{m=1}^{M} \left| \prod_{k \ne m} \frac{\lambda_k}{\lambda_k - \lambda_m} \right| \right)^2.$$
  ```python
import numpy as np
def zne_richardson(lambdas, E_vals):
    """Richardson-extrapolated zero-noise estimate (Lagrange form)."""
    lambdas = np.asarray(lambdas, dtype=float)
    M = len(lambdas)
    coeffs = np.array([
        np.prod([lambdas[k]/(lambdas[k]-lambdas[m])
                 for k in range(M) if k != m])
        for m in range(M)])
    est = float(coeffs @ np.asarray(E_vals))
    overhead = float(np.sum(np.abs(coeffs))**2)
    return est, overhead
# Example: exponential decay E(l) = exp(-l), true value 1.0
ls = [1.0, 2.0, 3.0]
Es = [np.exp(-l) for l in ls]
print(zne_richardson(ls, Es))  # -> (~1.0, overhead ~ 35.4)
```

> **Theorem:** (ZNE bias–variance trade-off) Increasing the polynomial order $M$ in Richardson extrapolation reduces the bias from $\mathcal{O}(\lambda^2)$ toward $\mathcal{O}(\lambda^M)$ but increases the sampling overhead super-exponentially in $M$ for fixed noise-level spacing; there is no free choice of $M$ that is simultaneously low-bias and low-variance at $\lambda \gtrsim 1$. — cf. Giurgica-Tiron et al. analysis in [1], direct error-bound analysis in arXiv:2502.20673.

### 4.2 Probabilistic Error Cancellation: quasiprobability and the $\gamma$ factor

PEC, introduced by Temme et al. [2], is the only mainstream method that can be *exactly unbiased*. Suppose each ideal gate $\mathcal{U}$ is implemented as a noisy operation $\tilde{\mathcal{U}} = \mathcal{E} \circ \mathcal{U}$ with known noise channel $\mathcal{E}$. If we can decompose the *inverse* noise channel as a **quasiprobability** distribution over implementable noisy operations,

$$\mathcal{E}^{-1} = \sum_i c_i \,\mathcal{B}_i, \qquad \sum_i c_i = 1,\; c_i \in \mathbb{R},$$

where some $c_i$ are negative, then sampling operation $\mathcal{B}_i$ with probability $p_i = |c_i|/\gamma$ and weighting the measurement outcome by $\gamma \operatorname{sgn}(c_i)$, with $\gamma = \sum_i |c_i|$, yields an *unbiased* estimator of the ideal expectation value. The price is the variance inflation:

> **Theorem:** (PEC sampling overhead) For a circuit with $N_g$ noisy gates, each with quasiprobability 1-norm $\gamma_k$, the sampling overhead of PEC is
> $$C_{\text{em}} = \prod_{k=1}^{N_g} \gamma_k^2 \approx \gamma^{2 n d},$$
> for a depth-$d$, $n$-qubit circuit with per-gate factor $\gamma$. The estimator is unbiased iff the noise model is exact. — Temme et al. [2]; van den Berg et al. [3]

The quantity $\gamma$ (often written $\bar{\gamma}$ per layer) is a genuine hardware quality metric: better coherence and lower crosstalk reduce the negativity of the quasiprobability representation, and $\gamma \to 1$ as noise vanishes. The landmark scalable implementation is van den Berg et al. [3], who learn **sparse Pauli–Lindblad** noise models on IBM processors and demonstrated PEC on 127-qubit circuits with $\bar{\gamma}$ close to unity per layer, enabling the "utility before fault tolerance" experiments. Note the deep connection to the lower bounds: Takagi et al. [4] prove that PEC is **optimal** among a wide class of mitigation strategies for local *dephasing* noise — no protocol can achieve the same bias with asymptotically smaller overhead. For depolarizing noise, PEC saturates the exponential depth scaling but with a suboptimal base, leaving room (in principle) for better protocols.

### 4.3 Clifford Data Regression: learning the noise map from simulable circuits

CDR (Czarnik et al., reviewed in [1]) sidesteps explicit noise characterization. The idea: generate an ensemble of *near-Clifford* circuits $\{C_i\}$ — circuits close to the target circuit of interest but efficiently simulable classically. For each $C_i$, compute the exact ideal expectation $x_i = \langle O \rangle_{\text{ideal}}$ classically and measure the noisy value $y_i = \langle O \rangle_{\text{noisy}}$ on hardware. Fit a regression model, typically affine,

$$y \approx a\, x + b,$$

then invert it to correct the target circuit's noisy measurement: $\hat{O}_{\text{em}} = (y_{\text{target}} - b)/a$.

CDR's strengths are practical: no noise model, no extra qubits, modest sampling overhead (the cost is dominated by running the training ensemble). Its weakness is **extrapolation risk**: the linear ansatz is exact only for global depolarizing noise; for structured or non-unital noise the learned map may not transfer from Clifford circuits to the (non-Clifford) target, producing uncontrolled bias. CDR is best viewed as a *data-driven* complement to the model-driven PEC: cheap and easy, but without PEC's unbiasedness guarantees.

### 4.4 Virtual Distillation and Error Suppression by Derangement

Virtual distillation (VD), introduced by Huggins et al. [6], and the closely related error suppression by derangement (ESD, Koczor) take a different tack: instead of inverting noise, they *purify in post-processing*. Given $M$ copies of the noisy state $\rho = \sum_i p_i |i\rangle\langle i|$, a collective measurement (via a controlled derangement operator $D_M$, a generalization of SWAP that permutes the $M$ registers) estimates

$$\langle O \rangle_{\text{VD}} = \frac{\operatorname{Tr}[\rho^M O]}{\operatorname{Tr}[\rho^M]} = \frac{\sum_i p_i^M \langle i|O|i\rangle}{\sum_i p_i^M}.$$

Because $p_1 > p_2 \ge \cdots$, the weights $p_i^M / \sum_j p_j^M$ concentrate on the **dominant eigenvector** $|1\rangle$ *exponentially fast* in $M$. If the dominant eigenvector is close to the ideal state (true when the expected number of errors $\lambda$ is modest), VD suppresses errors by orders of magnitude [6].

> **Theorem:** (VD exponential suppression) For non-entangling random circuits under depolarizing noise, the trace distance of the virtually distilled state to the ideal state decreases exponentially in the number of copies $M$, until hitting a noise floor set by the distance between the dominant eigenvector and the ideal state. — Huggins et al. [6]

The floor is the crucial caveat: VD converges to $\langle 1|O|1\rangle$, not to $\langle O \rangle_{\text{ideal}}$, and no $M$ removes the coherent mismatch between the dominant eigenvector and the true target state. The sampling overhead scales as $1/\operatorname{Tr}[\rho^M]^2$ (the denominator must be estimated), which grows exponentially in $M \lambda$, and the method costs $(M-1)n$ extra qubits plus the derangement circuit depth. VD shines for VQE-style energy estimation where the dominant eigenvector of a slightly noisy state remains a good approximation to the ground state.

### 4.5 The exponential walls: fundamental lower bounds

The capstone of mitigation theory is the pair of results proving that the exponential overheads above are not artifacts of clumsy protocols — they are *laws of nature*.

**Takagi et al. [4]** model any mitigation protocol as a quantum channel acting on $N$ noisy copies plus classical post-processing, and apply data-processing inequalities for distinguishability measures. Their main bound: the sampling overhead required to reduce the worst-case error below $\delta$ is lower-bounded by a ratio of distinguishabilities between ideal states and their noisy counterparts. Consequences: (i) for local depolarizing noise in layered circuits, the overhead scales *exponentially* in circuit depth for general protocols; (ii) PEC is *optimal* for local dephasing noise — its $\gamma^{2nd}$ scaling cannot be beaten within the protocol class.

**Quek et al. [5]** strengthen this to *universal* sampling lower bounds covering protocols with nonlinear post-processing and multi-copy coherent strategies: to achieve accuracy $\epsilon$ with high probability, the number of noisy-circuit runs must grow exponentially in depth for a broad class of noise models. A Nature Physics refinement [7] shows the effect is even more severe than previously known: the noisy output state becomes indistinguishable from the maximally mixed state at $\operatorname{poly}\log\log n$ depth (rather than $\log n$), so the exponential wall arrives *earlier* than early estimates suggested.

Together, [4,5,7] establish that **mitigation cannot be scaled like correction**: there is no threshold below which deeper circuits become *cheaper* to mitigate. The practical upshot is a *mitigation horizon* — a maximum $(n, d, \epsilon)$ envelope within which mitigation is affordable — and hardware progress (lower $\epsilon$, hence lower $\gamma$ and $\lambda$) pushes this horizon outward linearly in the exponent's base, not by changing the exponential form.

## 5. Empirical Results and Proofs

ZNE was demonstrated on superconducting hardware from its inception [2] and is now routine in Qiskit and Mitiq. PEC at scale is the van den Berg et al. result [3]: sparse Pauli–Lindblad learning plus quasiprobability sampling on up to 127 qubits, with measured $\bar{\gamma}$ per layer near 1, underpinning IBM's 2023 "quantum utility" experiments. VD was demonstrated numerically and on hardware for random circuits and VQE instances, showing multi-order-of-magnitude error suppression at $M=2,3$ before hitting the dominant-eigenvector floor [6]. CDR has been benchmarked on trapped-ion and superconducting devices, typically halving energy-estimation errors in VQE at modest shot cost [1].

*Proof sketch of the Takagi bound [4].* Consider two ideal pure states $|\psi\rangle, |\phi\rangle$ and their noisy versions $\tilde{\psi}, \tilde{\phi}$ after the device channel. Any $(Q,K)$ mitigation protocol — $Q$ coherent copies per round, $K$ rounds — is a CPTP map followed by classical estimation, so by the data-processing inequality the local distinguishability $D_{\mathrm{LM}}$ between protocol outputs cannot exceed that between the $QK$ noisy inputs. But successful mitigation *increases* distinguishability of the estimated expectation values toward the ideal values. Chaining these gives
$$\Delta e_{\max} \;\ge\; \max_{\psi,\phi} \frac{D_{\mathrm{tr}}(\psi,\phi) - 2b_{\max}}{D_{\mathrm{tr}}(\tilde{\psi}_Q^{(K)}, \tilde{\phi}_Q^{(K)})},$$
so the required number of samples $N$ (the sampling overhead) is lower-bounded by the inverse square of the noisy-state distinguishability — which decays exponentially in depth for local depolarizing noise. PEC's saturation of the bound for dephasing noise [4] certifies its optimality there.

## 6. Limitations and Open Problems

1. **The exponential wall is fundamental, not technical.** [4,5,7] rule out any clever protocol that mitigates deep circuits cheaply. Research effort is therefore best spent on *reducing the base* of the exponential (better $\gamma$, lower $\epsilon$) and on identifying problem classes whose effective $\lambda$ stays small (shallow, local observables).
2. **Noise-model mismatch.** PEC's unbiasedness evaporates under model error; drift, crosstalk, and non-Markovian effects are imperfectly captured by Pauli–Lindblad models [3]. Online, adaptive noise learning is an open practical problem.
3. **ZNE's scale-factor problem.** Folding does not amplify all error sources uniformly (readout, leakage, crosstalk), so the $x$-axis of the extrapolation is itself uncertain; noise-aware folding [arXiv:2401.12495] is a first step, but a principled calibration of effective scale factors is missing.
4. **CDR transferability.** Guarantees for the Clifford-to-target transfer of the learned noise map exist only for restricted noise models; characterizing when the affine ansatz fails — and detecting failure *in situ* — remains open.
5. **VD's dominant-eigenvector floor.** Coherent errors rotate the dominant eigenvector away from the ideal state, and no number of copies fixes this. Hybrid VD+PEC or VD+symmetry-verification schemes are promising but unproven at scale.
6. **Mitigation-aware algorithm design.** 7. **Composition and error bars.** Rigorous, composable uncertainty quantification for chained mitigation (e.g., readout mitigation + ZNE + VD) with honest confidence intervals is still maturing; most reported error bars ignore model-misspecification bias.

## 7. Conclusion

Quantum error mitigation — and emphatically *mitigation*, not correction — is the principled framework for extracting value from NISQ devices before fault tolerance. ZNE trades bias against variance along a polynomial ladder with unitary folding as its noise amplifier; PEC achieves unbiasedness through quasiprobability inversion at the cost of a $\gamma^{2nd}$ sampling overhead and a learned noise model; CDR learns an empirical correction from classically simulable Clifford data; and virtual distillation suppresses errors exponentially in the number of copies down to a dominant-eigenvector floor. The crowning theoretical results [4,5,7] prove these exponential costs are unavoidable in general: mitigation extends the NISQ horizon but cannot replace the threshold theorem of error correction. The field's future lies in lowering the exponential's base through better hardware and noise learning, in hybridizing the four pillars, and in co-designing algorithms with their mitigation from the start.
