# Sum-of-Squares Hierarchies: The Lasserre Relaxation, PSD Rank, and Lower Bounds in Combinatorial Optimization

## Abstract

The sum-of-squares (SOS) hierarchy, introduced by Lasserre in 2001 as a ladder of semidefinite relaxations for polynomial optimization, is simultaneously the most powerful known systematic family of convex relaxations and a proof system for certifying polynomial unsatisfiability. This thesis develops its two dual faces — the primal moment-matrix relaxation and the dual sum-of-squares certificate — together with the convergence theory under the Archimedean hypothesis, generic finite convergence, and the phenomenon of duality gaps. We survey the algorithmic frontier: the Goemans–Williamson analysis of Max-Cut at degree two, the Barak–Kelner–Steurer rounding framework, and applications to planted sparse vectors, tensor decomposition, and small set expansion. We then turn to the limits: Grigoriev's degree Omega(n) lower bounds for 3XOR and knapsack, Tulsiani's pairwise-independence characterization, candidate integrality gaps for unique games, and the bridge to PSD rank and extension complexity via Yannakakis's factorization theorem. The central claim: SOS is the strongest known relaxation hierarchy and the proof system whose degree lower bounds supply the field's sharpest unconditional hardness results.

## 1 Introduction

Polynomial optimization — minimizing a polynomial over a set described by polynomial inequalities — is NP-hard even for quartic polynomials, so convex relaxations are the only viable systematic strategy. In 2001, Lasserre proposed a hierarchy of semidefinite programming (SDP) relaxations whose optimal values converge, monotonically and under mild algebraic hypotheses, to the true global optimum [1]. In parallel, Parrilo independently developed sum-of-squares methods for certifying polynomial positivity. What emerged is the **SOS hierarchy** (also called the Lasserre hierarchy): for each degree parameter d, a convex relaxation of size n raised to O(d) whose dual is a *proof* that the relaxed bound is valid.

Two properties make this object exceptional. First, it **dominates** its predecessors: SOS subsumes the Sherali–Adams and Lovász–Schrijver hierarchies, since every nonnegative junta is a sum of squares [4]. Second, it has a **dual life** as a refutation system, placing it at the center of proof complexity and its links to algorithm design [7].

This thesis is organized as follows. Section 2 recalls the real-algebraic foundations — moment sequences, Putinar's Positivstellensatz, and the Archimedean condition. Section 3 describes the methodology: how the hierarchy is solved, how its solutions are rounded, and how its failures are proved. Section 4 develops the technical core: the moment primal and SOS dual, the comparison with Sherali–Adams, the application landscape (Max-Cut, planted clique, unique games), the lower-bound machinery (Grigoriev, Schoenebeck, Tulsiani), and the connection to PSD rank and extension complexity. Section 5 states the sharpest concrete results with proof sketches; Section 6 discusses limitations; Section 7 concludes.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The moment problem and Putinar's theorem

Consider the polynomial optimization problem

> **minimize** f(x) **subject to** g_i(x) >= 0 for i = 1..m, with x in R^n,

where f and each g_i are real polynomials. Lasserre's key move is to linearize the problem in the space of *moments*. Any feasible point x defines the moment sequence y_alpha = x^alpha for multi-indices alpha; conversely, a sequence y is a valid moment sequence of some probability measure supported on the feasible set K exactly when the associated **moment matrix** M_k(y), whose rows and columns are indexed by monomials of degree at most k, is positive semidefinite, and each **localizing matrix** M_{k-d_i}(g_i y) — encoding the constraint g_i >= 0 — is positive semidefinite [5].

The dual statement is the certificate view. Putinar's Positivstellensatz says that under an **Archimedean** hypothesis — the description of K certifies its own boundedness via sums of squares — every polynomial strictly positive on K lies in the *quadratic module* generated by the constraints: it can be written as sigma_0 + sum_i sigma_i g_i with each sigma_i a sum of squares [5]. The level-t relaxation is then exactly the SDP that optimizes over the largest lambda such that f - lambda admits such a representation with degree bounds 2t [1].

### 2.2 Convergence

Under the Archimedean assumption, the optimal values rho_t of the level-t relaxations converge to the global minimum as t grows [5]. Moreover, Nie and coauthors showed that convergence is **generically finite**: for almost every instance, some fixed finite level of the hierarchy already attains the optimum [5]. Convexity helps further: for convex polynomial problems with positive-definite Hessians at minimizers, finite convergence is guaranteed [6].

The cost is steep. The t-th relaxation has O(n raised to 2t) variables and semidefinite matrices of order O(n raised to t) [6], so only low constant levels are tractable. And convergence is not always clean: Schweighofer gave a two-dimensional example where the Archimedean condition holds yet a **duality gap** appears already at the first level [5] — a warning that numerical SDP solvers can misbehave on these relaxations.

### 2.3 SOS as a proof system

For unsatisfiable systems, the SOS hierarchy is a *refutation system*: an SOS refutation of a CNF formula phi = C_1 and ... and C_m is a family of sum-of-squares polynomials q_0..q_m with

> sum_i q_i (C_i - 1) + q_0 = -1,

working in multilinear arithmetic [4]. This places SOS among **semi-algebraic proof systems**, alongside Sherali–Adams (SA) and Lovász–Schrijver (LS). A degree-d SA refutation can be found in time n raised to O(d); a degree-d SOS refutation also reduces to an SDP of size n raised to O(d) [4]. This *degree automatizability* is what makes lower bounds on refutation degree unconditional lower bounds on the running time of a natural class of algorithms.

## 3 Methodology

Three methodological pillars support the theory of SOS.

**Relax and round.** To approximate a combinatorial problem, one writes its natural polynomial formulation, relaxes to level d of the hierarchy, solves the SDP, and *rounds* the fractional solution. The Barak–Kelner–Steurer framework systematizes rounding through the **combining-to-rounding paradigm**: a *combining algorithm* maps a distribution over integral solutions to a weaker integral solution, and the SOS proof system lifts it mechanically into a *rounding algorithm* mapping relaxation solutions to integral ones [2]. This produced improved guarantees for maximizing low-degree polynomials over the sphere, for L_4-versus-L_2 sparsity in subspaces, and for recovering planted sparse vectors [2].

**Prove gaps by pseudoexpectations.** To show the hierarchy *fails*, one constructs a **degree-d pseudoexpectation** tilde-E: a linear functional on polynomials of degree at most 2d that behaves like a true expectation on squares (tilde-E of any squared polynomial is nonnegative) and satisfies all constraints, yet assigns a value far from any integral solution — thereby certifying a large integrality gap or a high refutation degree. Grigoriev's technique reduces this to **resolution width** lower bounds [3]; this is the engine behind nearly every SOS lower bound below.

**Connect to extension complexity.** The PSD rank of a slack matrix characterizes the smallest SDP extended formulation of a polytope, exactly as nonnegative rank characterizes LP extended formulations (Yannakakis's factorization theorem) [9]. SOS degree lower bounds therefore translate into unconditional lower bounds on the size of *any* SDP lift of the relevant polytope, giving the hierarchy a geometric interpretation beyond algorithms.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Moment Primal and the SOS Dual

At level t, the primal SDP searches for a truncated moment sequence y:

- **Variables:** y_alpha for all multi-indices alpha with total degree at most 2t.
- **Objective:** minimize L_y(f), the linear functional induced by y on the objective polynomial.
- **Constraints:** M_t(y) positive semidefinite; M_{t - d_i}(g_i y) positive semidefinite for each constraint; y_0 = 1.

The dual SDP searches for the best provable lower bound lambda:

> **Theorem (Lasserre duality):** The dual of the level-t moment relaxation is the problem of maximizing lambda such that f - lambda = sigma_0 + sum_i sigma_i g_i, where each sigma_i is a sum of squares of degree at most 2t - deg(g_i) [5].

When strong duality holds — which is *not* automatic, as Schweighofer's gap example shows [5] — the optimal lambda is exactly the best degree-2t SOS proof that f >= lambda on K. This primal-dual correspondence is the hierarchy's defining feature: every relaxation value comes with a *certificate* in a fixed proof system, and every certificate yields an upper bound on the relaxation value [1].

### 4.2 SOS versus Sherali–Adams versus Lovász–Schrijver

The three classical lift-and-project hierarchies differ in the proof system they mechanize:

| Hierarchy | Relaxation type | Proof system | Degree-d size | Subsumed by SOS? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Lovász–Schrijver (LS) | LP/SDP lifts | LS derivations | n raised to O(d) | Yes |
| Sherali–Adams (SA) | LP lifts | Nonnegative juntas | n raised to O(d) | Yes |
| Lasserre / SOS | SDP lifts | SOS derivations | n raised to O(d) | — |

The subsumption is strict and meaningful: SA is simulated by SOS because every nonnegative junta is a sum of squares [4], and the simulation preserves degree up to constants. The separation shows up in approximation: Max-Cut is *approximation resistant* for Omega(n) levels of the LS hierarchy — and this was strengthened to the SA hierarchy — yet the Goemans–Williamson SDP, which is essentially degree-2 SOS, achieves the celebrated 0.878 approximation [3]. In proof-complexity language, SOS degree-2 already defeats what defeats linear programming at linear degree.

Over Boolean variables, SOS simulates polynomial calculus (PC) refutations with only a quadratic degree blowup [4]. But outside the Boolean setting the simulation breaks: Grigoriev and Vorobjov's telescopic system has a degree-n PC refutation yet requires *exponential* SOS refutation degree [4].

### 4.3 Integrality Gaps: Max-Cut, Planted Clique, Unique Games

**Max-Cut.** The Goemans–Williamson rounding of the degree-2 SOS relaxation achieves approximation ratio 0.878, and the matching integrality gap — the Feige–Schechtman construction — shows degree-2 SOS cannot do better [3]. Max-Cut thus sits at a fixed point of the hierarchy: already tight at constant degree, resistant to LP hierarchies at linear degree.

**Planted clique.** The planted clique problem — distinguishing a random graph from one with a planted k-clique — is the canonical average-case hard problem, and SOS lower bounds are the main evidence for its hardness: surveys of the area record SOS lower bounds for planted clique due to Meka, Potechin, and Wigderson [3], ruling out subexponential SOS-based refutation algorithms below the sqrt(n) threshold. This is the flagship "SOS predicts computational hardness" story.

**Unique games.** Khot and Moshkovitz proposed a *candidate* Lasserre integrality gap construction for Unique Games, based on systems of linear equations over the reals [7]. A genuine UG gap for SOS would be momentous: it would separate the SOS hierarchy from the Unique Games Conjecture's algorithmic frontier. The construction remains a candidate — but its existence as a plausible attack vector shows how deeply the hierarchy is entangled with the central open problems of approximation.

**Machine learning spin-offs.** The BKS rounding framework unexpectedly powered algorithms far from combinatorial optimization: recovering a planted mu-sparse vector in a random d-dimensional subspace whenever mu = O(min(1, n/d squared)) [2], and dictionary learning across the full sparsity range [10]. Follow-up work distilled these SOS analyses into nearly-linear-time algorithms and gave subquadratic tensor decomposition up to rank tilde-Omega(n raised to 4/3), beating the previous quasipolynomial barrier [8]. SOS here functions as an *algorithm design language*: write the SOS proof first, then mechanize it.

### 4.4 Lower Bounds: Grigoriev, Schoenebeck, Tulsiani

The lower-bound story begins before the hierarchy had a name. In 1999, Grigoriev proved that 3XOR is **approximation resistant** for degree Omega(n) SOS — using the language of Positivstellensatz calculus, since Parrilo's and Lasserre's SDP formulations came later [3]. His technique: build a degree-Omega(n) pseudoexpectation on a random 3XOR instance that satisfies every constraint while the instance is far from satisfiable, by exploiting the fact that small sub-instances are locally consistent (the reduction to resolution width).

Schoenebeck rediscovered this in 2008 and extended it to 3SAT and other CSPs [3]. Tulsiani and Chan then gave the clean characterization: **every predicate containing a pairwise independent subgroup is approximation resistant for Omega(n)-degree SOS** [3]. Grigoriev also proved SOS degree lower bounds for the knapsack problem [3]. Together these form a wall: for a large class of CSPs, no SOS relaxation running in subexponential time can beat the trivial random-assignment guarantee.

Recent work sharpened the frontier on both sides. Dinur, Filmus, Harsha, and Tulsiani constructed *explicit* 3XOR instances — constructible in deterministic polynomial time — that are hard for Omega(sqrt(log n)) SOS levels [7], removing the randomness from earlier constructions. And the program connecting semi-algebraic proofs to algorithm design was systematized by Fleming, Kothari, and Pitassi [7].

> **Theorem (Grigoriev, 1999):** There exist unsatisfiable 3XOR instances on n variables for which every SOS refutation requires degree Omega(n) [3].
>
> *Proof sketch.* Take a random 3XOR instance at clause density below the satisfiability threshold but above the resolution-width threshold. Define tilde-E on monomials of degree at most Omega(n) by local consistency: on any set of at most Omega(n) variables, the marginal matches a satisfying assignment of the induced sub-instance (which exists by expansion). Pairwise independence of the predicate guarantees these local views glue into a valid pseudoexpectation that satisfies every constraint, while the global instance is unsatisfiable. Any degree-o(n) SOS refutation would contradict the existence of tilde-E. QED

### 4.5 PSD Rank, Extension Complexity, and the Limits of Lifts

Yannakakis's factorization theorem gives the geometric meaning of lifts:

> **Theorem (Yannakakis, 1991):** For a polytope P with slack matrix S, the smallest size of an LP extended formulation of P equals the nonnegative rank of S [9].

The theorem generalizes to SDP cones: the smallest SDP extended formulation has size equal to the **PSD rank** of the slack matrix [9]. This is where SOS lower bounds acquire their second life. A degree-d SOS lower bound for a polytope (say, the cut polytope or the TSP polytope) implies a lower bound on the PSD rank of its slack matrix, and therefore on the size of *every* SDP lift — not just the Lasserre hierarchy. In other words, SOS degree is a certificate of *intrinsic* SDP-hardness of the polytope, independent of any particular relaxation scheme.

The result is a two-way dictionary: SOS integrality gaps yield PSD-rank lower bounds, which rule out *any* small SDP formulation — the modern language for the limits of semidefinite programming [9].

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

The sharpest concrete statements about what SOS can and cannot do:

| Problem | Result | Degree | Reference |
|---|---|---|---|
| Max-Cut (GW) | 0.878-approximation; tight | 2 | [3] |
| 3XOR | Approximation resistant | Omega(n) | [3] |
| 3SAT, CSPs w/ pairwise independence | Approximation resistant | Omega(n) | [3] |
| Knapsack | Degree lower bound | Omega(n) | [3] |
| Explicit 3XOR | Hard instances, deterministic | Omega(sqrt(log n)) | [7] |
| Planted clique | SOS lower bound below sqrt(n) | super-constant | [3] |
| Unique Games | Candidate Lasserre gap | — | [7] |
| Planted sparse vector | Recovery up to mu = O(min(1, n/d^2)) | O(1) | [2] |
| Tensor decomposition | Recovery up to rank tilde-Omega(n^{4/3}), subquadratic time | O(1) | [8] |
| PC simulation (Boolean) | SOS simulates PC | d -> 2d | [4] |
| Telescopic system (non-Boolean) | SOS refutation degree exponential | exp(n) | [4] |

Two proof sketches illustrate the methodology.

> **Theorem (Putinar's Positivstellensatz):** Let K be basic closed semialgebraic and Archimedean. If p > 0 on K, then p = sigma_0 + sum_i sigma_i g_i for sums of squares sigma_i [5].
>
> *Proof sketch.* Suppose not. By a Hahn–Banach separation in the space of linear functionals, there exists a linear functional L nonnegative on the quadratic module but with L(p) < 0. The Archimedean property makes L's representing measure have compact support, and a Riesz-representation argument yields a probability measure mu supported on K with integral of p negative — contradicting p > 0 on K. The degree-truncated version gives exactly the dual of the level-t relaxation. QED

> **Theorem (SOS subsumes Sherali–Adams):** Any degree-d SA refutation yields a degree-O(d) SOS refutation [4].
>
> *Proof sketch.* An SA refutation is a nonnegative combination of juntas D = product over i in S of x_i times product over j in T of (1 - x_j). Each junta is a square: D = (product over i in S of x_i times product over j in T of (1 - x_j)) squared, after multilinearization x_i squared = x_i. Hence every SA derivation line is already an SOS derivation line of the same degree, and the refutation -1 = sum q_i(C_i - 1) + q_0 transfers verbatim. QED

A concrete computational illustration — the degree-1 moment matrix for a two-variable Max-Cut-style SDP — can be written directly:

```python
import numpy as np

def moment_matrix_2var(y00, y10, y01, y11):
    """Degree-1 moment matrix M_1(y) for variables (x1, x2) in {+-1}^2.
    Indexed by monomials [1, x1, x2]; PSD iff y is a valid pseudo-moment."""
    M = np.array([[y00, y10, y01],
                  [y10, y00, y11],
                  [y01, y11, y00]], dtype=float)
    assert np.all(np.linalg.eigvalsh(M) > -1e-9), "not a valid pseudo-moment"
    return M

# A degree-1 pseudoexpectation can correlate x1, x2 arbitrarily:
M = moment_matrix_2var(1.0, 0.0, 0.0, -1.0)  # "x1 = -x2" with prob. 1
print("eigs:", np.linalg.eigvalsh(M))  # PSD: a valid level-1 relaxation point
```

And the hierarchy's monotone refinement is naturally a state machine:

```tla+
------------------------------ MODULE SosHierarchy ------------------------------
EXTENDS Naturals, Reals
VARIABLES level, bound
Init == level = 1 /\ bound = -Infinity
Next == \/ /\ level' = level + 1       \* ascend: larger SDP, tighter bound
         /\ bound' >= bound            \* monotone nondecreasing lower bounds
         /\ bound' <= Optimum
       \/ UNCHANGED <<level, bound>>    \* stutter: finite convergence reached
Spec == Init /\ [][Next]_<<level, bound>>
Converges == <>(bound = Optimum)        \* Archimedean => eventually exact
=============================================================================
```

## 6 Limitations

The hierarchy's power comes with hard limits, and intellectual honesty requires stating them.

1. **Exponential scaling.** Level t costs n raised to O(t) time and memory [6]; in practice only levels 1–4 are solvable. The Omega(n)-degree lower bounds [3] thus say something devastating: for those problems, *no* tractable level helps at all.

2. **Duality gaps and numerics.** Strong duality is not guaranteed; Schweighofer's example has a gap at level one despite the Archimedean condition [5]. Interior-point SDP solvers assume strict feasibility, so relaxations can be numerically treacherous.

3. **Degree versus size automatizability.** Finding a degree-d SOS refutation takes n raised to O(d) time via SDP, but finding a *small* (size-s) refutation has worst-case bounds around 2 raised to sqrt(n log s) [4]. Short SOS proofs may exist that no efficient algorithm can find.

4. **Planted clique remains a conjecture.** SOS lower bounds are evidence, not proof, that no polynomial-time algorithm beats sqrt(n). A breakthrough algorithm need not be an SOS rounding.

5. **Non-Boolean fragility.** Outside the Boolean cube, SOS can be exponentially weaker than polynomial calculus [4]. The hierarchy's elegance is partly an artifact of the Boolean axioms doing heavy lifting.

---

## 7 Conclusion

Its dual identity — relaxation and refutation system, primal moment matrix and dual certificate — is not a curiosity but the source of its power: every upper bound is a proof, and every lower bound is a proof that no proof exists.

The open frontiers are clear. A genuine SOS integrality gap for Unique Games [7] would reshape approximability theory; explicit deterministic hard instances at higher degree [7] would derandomize the hardness program; and the PSD-rank dictionary [9] converts every SOS lower bound into a geometric statement about polytopes. Whatever the outcomes, the hierarchy is the central object of study: the strongest systematic relaxations we can write down, and the precise boundary of what semidefinite programming can ever achieve.
