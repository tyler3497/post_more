# Layered Trust in Constrained Devices: A Formal and Comparative Analysis of the TCG Device Identifier Composition Engine

## Abstract

The Trusted Computing Group's Device Identifier Composition Engine (DICE) provides hardware-rooted device identity and layered software attestation for resource-constrained devices in which a discrete Trusted Platform Module is economically or physically infeasible. This thesis formalizes the DICE core primitive — the Compound Device Identifier derived as *CDI = OWF(UDS ∥ TCI₀)* — and the recursive layering construction *CDI_{i+1} = OWF(CDI_i ∥ TCI_{i+1})* under mandatory per-layer secret erasure. We analyze the resulting identity key hierarchy, comprising a stable per-device DeviceID key and a firmware-bound Alias key whose X.509 certificate chains through per-layer embedded certificate authorities to a manufacturer root. We contrast DICE's chained-measurement model with the TPM 2.0 endorsement/attestation key hierarchies and PCR-based quoting, and we distinguish *secure boot* (policy enforcement) from *measured boot* (evidence recording). Drawing on Microsoft's RIoT reference architecture and Azure IoT Device Provisioning Service adoption, we then assess post-quantum migration: Grover-resistant one-way functions for CDI derivation, the ML-DSA versus SLH-DSA trade-off for alias certificates, and the crypto-agility problem posed by ROM-resident DICE engines.

## 1 Introduction

The dominant trust anchors of the personal-computing era — discrete Trusted Platform Modules implementing the TCG TPM 2.0 library — are poor fits for the cheapest tiers of connected hardware. A sensor node built around a sub-dollar microcontroller cannot absorb the cost, board area, or power budget of a discrete security coprocessor, yet it must still answer three questions: *which device is this*, *what software did it boot*, and *how can a remote verifier know* [1]. The TCG's Device Identifier Composition Engine (DICE) is the standards-track answer: a minimalist, firmware-centric trust model deriving device identity from a manufacturer-provisioned secret and layered cryptographic measurements rather than dedicated silicon [2].

DICE rests on a single primitive — a one-way function combining a Unique Device Secret (UDS) with the measurement of the first mutable code — applied recursively across each boot transition [3]. Every layer of the Trusted Computing Base measures the next, derives a fresh Compound Device Identifier (CDI), erases its own secret, and transfers control. From these secrets, standard X.509 certificates are minted: a stable DeviceID certificate identifying the hardware and an Alias certificate attesting to the current firmware, chaining to the manufacturer's root CA [4].

This thesis (i) formalizes CDI derivation and the layering recurrence with their binding and secrecy properties, (ii) explicates the DeviceID/Alias key model and embedded-CA certificate profiles, (iii) compares DICE structurally with the TPM 2.0 EK/AIK hierarchies, and (iv) evaluates post-quantum migration in light of NIST's August 2024 finalization of ML-KEM, ML-DSA, and SLH-DSA [5].

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Roots of trust: secure boot versus measured boot

A *root of trust* is a component whose correct behavior is assumed; all other trust is conditional on it. The TCG distinguishes roots of trust for *measurement* (RTM), *storage* (RTS), and *reporting* (RTR) [6]. In measured boot, the RTM — the first code after reset, held in immutable ROM — computes a digest (*measurement*) of the next component and records it in a tamper-evident log. Two policies build on measurement, and confusing them is the literature's most common error:

- **Secure boot** *verifies* each component against a policy (e.g., a signature check) and *refuses to boot* on failure.
- **Measured boot** *records* measurements without enforcing policy; a remote *verifier* later requests the log and decides whether the configuration is acceptable — the basis of *remote attestation* [7].

DICE is a measured-boot architecture: it binds measurements into derived secrets and certificates, enabling remote attestation without a TPM's shielded registers.

### 2.2 The TPM 2.0 model

TPM 2.0 defines a security processor exposing key *hierarchies* rooted in manufacturer-provisioned seeds. The **Endorsement Key (EK)**, generated at manufacture and manufacturer-certified, provides stable device identity. **Attestation Identity Keys (AIKs)** are restricted signing keys under the endorsement hierarchy used to *quote* Platform Configuration Registers (PCRs) — tamper-evident accumulators of boot measurements — without exposing the EK. This EK/AIK separation serves privacy and operations: the long-lived identity certifies short-lived attestation keys, and a fresh AIK can be minted per verifier to resist tracking [6]. The model is powerful but heavyweight: dedicated non-volatile storage, a command interface, and silicon that low-end MCUs cannot spare.

### 2.3 RIoT and the TCG DICE specification family

Microsoft Research's **Robust Internet of Things (RIoT)** (2016) demonstrated the DICE-like construction on commodity microcontrollers: a tiny first-stage bootloader, *RIoT Core*, runs from immutable memory, holds exclusive early access to a per-device secret, measures device firmware, and derives identity keys from secret-plus-measurement [8]. RIoT informed the TCG DICE specifications and underpins Azure IoT Device Provisioning Service (DPS) enrollment, where DICE devices authenticate with derived X.509 certificates [1].

The normative family comprises: the *Hardware Requirements for a Device Identifier Composition Engine* (Family 2.0, Rev. 72), defining the DICE RoT and CDI construction [2]; the *DICE Layering Architecture* (v1.0, Rev. 0.19), extending it to arbitrary TCB component sequences [3]; the *DICE Certificate Profiles* (v1.1), specifying the DeviceID, Alias, and embedded-CA X.509 artifacts [4]; and an *Attestation Architecture* situating DICE within IETF RATS [9].

## 3 Methodology

This is an *analytical and comparative* study: DICE's properties are best established by formalizing the construction and auditing its assumptions. Our method has four strands. **Formalization**: we restate CDI derivation and the layering recurrence precisely, making the OWF, KDF, and TCI roles explicit, and state binding/secrecy as theorems with proof sketches. **Assumption auditing**: for each property we identify the hardware/procedural assumptions — UDS provisioning integrity, exclusive DICE access after reset, complete secret erasure at layer transitions — and trace how violations degrade guarantees. **Structured comparison**: we compare DICE against TPM 2.0's EK/AIK model along fixed dimensions (identity stability, measurement granularity, privacy, cost, certificate expressiveness, revocation) using the normative specifications as baseline. **Evidence synthesis**: we ground the post-quantum analysis in published references (RIoT, TI SimpleLink CC323x flows, Azure DPS) and finalized NIST parameters [5]. All specification claims are verified against the cited TCG documents.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The DICE core primitive: CDI derivation

The DICE is immutable (or securely updatable) code executing in the earliest boot stage, with **exclusive access to the UDS** between reset and transfer of control to measured mutable code [2]. The UDS is manufacturer-provisioned — via PUFs, OTP fuses, or secure-element injection — but the normative requirement is absolute: no entity but the DICE may observe the UDS, and hardware must *latch* it once the DICE completes.

The DICE performs three steps: **measure** the first mutable code (FMC) — optionally plus hardware state and configuration — yielding *TCI₀*; **combine** UDS and measurement through a one-way function; **emit** *CDI₀* to the first mutable layer and render the UDS inaccessible. Formally:

```
CDI_0 = OWF(UDS || TCI_0 || HW_state || Config)
```

where the OWF is in practice an HMAC/KDF over a collision-resistant hash (the TCG notation uses `H()` and `HMAC(k, m)`) [2].

> **Theorem (Binding and secrecy of the CDI).** *Let the OWF be a pseudorandom function keyed by the UDS and collision-resistant in its measurement input. Then (i) any change to the UDS, the first mutable code, or included hardware/configuration inputs yields a computationally independent CDI₀ with overwhelming probability (binding); and (ii) CDI₀ reveals no usable information about the UDS to any party not already knowing it (secrecy).*

*Proof sketch.* Binding is collision resistance over the measurement input — "any revision or change in the UDS or any of the measured components results in a different value for the CDI" [2]. Secrecy is the PRF property: output keyed by an unknown high-entropy key is indistinguishable from random, so the CDI "contains a digest of the UDS, but due to the hashing, does not reveal the UDS" [6]. ∎

Two consequences matter. First, the CDI is *not* a serial number: it identifies the *device-plus-first-mutable-code combination*, so firmware updates deterministically yield new CDIs — making firmware identity cryptographically visible. Second, the CDI must **never be revealed to software that might impersonate the first mutable code** [6]; this motivates the layering discipline below. Reference pseudocode shows the required ordering — measure, derive, latch, erase:

```c
/* DICE core: runs once per reset from immutable memory */
void dice_main(void) {
    uint8_t uds[32], tci0[32], cdi0[32];
    hw_read_uds(uds);                  /* exclusive DICE access window */
    measure_first_mutable_code(tci0);  /* SHA-256 digest of FMC image */
    hmac_sha256(uds, sizeof(uds), tci0, sizeof(tci0), cdi0); /* CDI_0 */
    hw_latch_uds();                    /* UDS inaccessible from here on */
    secure_erase(uds, sizeof(uds));
    jump_to_first_mutable_code(cdi0);  /* transfer control; no return */
}
```

### 4.2 The layering architecture: a recursive identity chain

The *DICE Layering Architecture* generalizes the primitive to arbitrary TCB component sequences — bootloader stages, RTOS kernel, application — treating each layer as a TCB whose measurement seeds the next layer's identity [3]. A TCI is canonically a firmware digest (a hardware product identifier may substitute where no measurable firmware exists). Each layer executes an identical protocol:

1. Receive *CDIᵢ* from the previous layer (*i = 0* from the DICE itself).
2. Measure the next component → *TCI_{i+1}*.
3. Compute *CDI_{i+1} = OWF(CDIᵢ ∥ TCI_{i+1})*; pass it onward.
4. **Erase** its own secret (*CDIᵢ*, derived keys) before transferring control.
5. Use *CDIᵢ* as KDF input for the layer's own identity keys.

Hence the recurrence *CDI_{i+1} = OWF(CDI_i ∥ TCI_{i+1})* with base *CDI₀ = OWF(UDS ∥ TCI₀)*:

| Layer | TCB component | TCI input | Secret held | Derived for next |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| DICE RoT (immutable) | First-stage loader | TCI₀ = digest(FMC) | UDS | CDI₀ = OWF(UDS ∥ TCI₀) |
| L1 bootloader | Bootloader image | TCI₁ | CDI₀ | CDI₁ = OWF(CDI₀ ∥ TCI₁) |
| L2 RTOS kernel | Kernel + config | TCI₂ | CDI₁ | CDI₂ = OWF(CDI₁ ∥ TCI₂) |
| L3 application | App firmware | TCI₃ | CDI₂ | — (terminal) |

Step 4 is the linchpin: each layer's secret is a deterministic function of the *entire measurement history* beneath it. A compromised layer cannot compute predecessors' secrets (one-wayness), and because it erased its own secret, a later-compromised layer cannot recover earlier ones. **Compromise of running firmware never compromises the long-term provisioning secret**, and firmware rollback yields observably different identity keys, defeating downgrade impersonation [3][7]. Each layer additionally mints an X.509 *embedded CA* certificate carrying the next layer's evidence, forming a certificate path terminating at the manufacturer's embedded CA and public root [6].

### 4.3 Identity keys and alias certificates: DeviceID attestation

CDIs are symmetric secrets; the Internet authenticates with asymmetric keys. DICE defines a two-tier model, originating in RIoT and standardized in the Certificate Profiles [4][8]:

- The **DeviceID key pair**, derived from the CDI via KDF, is stable across reboots for fixed firmware — the DICE analogue of the TPM **EK**. Its public key is manufacturer-certified, binding key to physical device at production.
- The **Alias key pair**, derived from CDI *and* firmware identity (RIoT's FWID — the hash of the Firmware Security Descriptor), changes with every firmware update — the analogue of the TPM **AIK**. The Alias certificate is *signed by the DeviceID private key* and carries TCB-information extensions, so one path validation proves both "genuine device" (DeviceID chain to manufacturer root) and "exact firmware" (Alias contents).

This yields three properties mirroring and partly improving on the TPM design: (1) *Privacy* — verifiers see the Alias key, not the long-lived DeviceID key, exactly the AIK motivation [6]; (2) *Freshness without re-provisioning* — firmware updates locally yield new Alias keys/certificates at boot, no manufacturer-CA interaction, with compromised firmware revoked via verifier-side TCB-info denylists; (3) *Standard PKI* — DICE devices authenticate to Azure IoT DPS and any TLS endpoint with unmodified certificate stacks [1].

One profile rule is operationally critical: because every layer mints a CA certificate for the next, the manufacturer embedded-CA certificate's *pathLenConstraint* must be set so a compromised layer cannot fraudulently extend the attestation chain [6].

### 4.4 DICE versus TPM 2.0: a structural comparison

| Dimension | TPM 2.0 | TCG DICE |
|---|---|---|
| Trust anchor | Discrete security processor | Immutable early-boot engine + UDS |
| Stable device identity | EK, manufacturer-certified | DeviceID key from CDI, manufacturer-certified |
| Attestation key | AIK under EK hierarchy | Alias key from CDI + FWID, certified by DeviceID key |
| Measurement store | PCRs (extend-only registers) | CDI chain: each secret commits to full measurement history |
| Attestation evidence | *Quote*: signed PCR digest + event log | Alias certificate with TCB-info extensions; ECA path |
| Measurement granularity | Per-component, itemized log | Per-layer, folded into derived secrets |
| Privacy | Fresh AIK per verifier; EK never exposed | Fresh Alias identity per firmware; DeviceID rarely exposed |
| Hardware cost | Dedicated NVM, crypto engine, interface | KBs of ROM + UDS storage; no extra chip |
| Key management | On-chip hierarchies, persistent objects | Deterministic derivation each boot; nothing stored |
| Revocation | EK/AIK CRLs | Verifier-side TCB-info allow/deny lists |

Three observations follow. *First*, DICE's measurement model is **constructive** where the TPM's is **evidential**: a TPM *records* and *reports* measurements for the verifier to replay; DICE *folds* them into keys, so the Alias private key for firmware *F* simply does not exist on a device running *F′ ≠ F* — self-enforcing attestation, at the cost of coarser, digest-resolution evidence. *Second*, DICE trades **shielded storage** for **deterministic re-derivation**: nothing extractable sits in flash, but rollback protection, monotonic counters, and sealed storage — native TPM capabilities — must be re-implemented by higher layers (e.g., security version numbers folded into CDI derivation) [3]. *Third*, the two compose: DICE for the cost-sensitive sensor tier, TPM-anchored aggregation at the edge — exactly the tiering reflected in Azure IoT's parallel DICE and TPM HSM support [1].

### 4.5 Post-quantum considerations

NIST's August 2024 finalizations — FIPS 203 (ML-KEM), FIPS 204 (ML-DSA), FIPS 205 (SLH-DSA) — make post-quantum migration a procurement requirement [5]. Three questions arise for DICE.

**1. Is CDI derivation quantum-safe?** The OWF is an HMAC/KDF over SHA-256/SHA-3; Grover's algorithm gives at most quadratic speedup, so 256-bit hashes retain ~128-bit post-quantum strength. The conservative choice is **SHA-512** (or SHA3-512) for the OWF, preserving a 256-bit margin at negligible MCU cost [10].

**2. What signs the certificates?** Alias/DeviceID certificates are the quantum-exposed surface — Shor breaks their ECDSA/RSA signatures. Replacements are **ML-DSA** (recommended general-purpose) and **SLH-DSA** (conservative hash-based backup) [5]:

| Scheme | Public key | Signature | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| ECDSA P-256 (baseline) | 64 B | 64 B | Broken by Shor |
| ML-DSA-44 | 1,312 B | 2,420 B | Recommended default; fast verify |
| ML-DSA-65 | 1,952 B | 3,293 B | Higher security category |
| SLH-DSA ("s") | 32 B | ~7,856 B | Tiny keys, huge signatures; conservative |

SLH-DSA's hash-based security suits **long-lived manufacturer certificates and firmware images** that cannot be re-signed; ML-DSA's speed and moderate sizes suit short-lived Alias certificates. A hybrid profile — SLH-DSA for roots, ML-DSA for Alias — hedges lattice cryptanalysis against hash-based performance costs [10][11].

**3. Can a ROM DICE engine migrate?** The DICE is specified as *immutable*; a ROM engine hardcoding ECDSA P-256 or SHA-256 cannot learn ML-DSA post-deployment. Mitigations: (a) implement DICE in *securely updatable* flash, accepting the larger attack surface; (b) build **algorithm agility in from birth** — parameterize OWF/KDF so a PQC-capable second layer re-derives identities under new algorithms while the immutable core performs only hash operations (which need no migration); (c) keep the immutable core minimal — measure, HMAC, latch, erase — since symmetric primitives migrate by widening the digest. The Certificate Profiles' extension points for new signature OIDs anticipate this [4].

## 5 Empirical Results

DICE being a specification architecture, the evidence is that of reference implementations, platform adoption, and measured algorithm parameters. **Feasibility is established**: RIoT demonstrated the full construction on microcontroller-class hardware, underpinning Azure DPS enrollment where DICE devices authenticate via derived X.509 certificates without per-device factory key injection [1][8]; TI's SimpleLink CC323x flow gives a second independent existence proof on a Wi-Fi MCU [12]. **The CDI chain detects firmware modification deterministically**: any firmware change yields a different Alias certificate, so DPS enrollment policies keyed on certificate properties distinguish firmware versions with no device-side policy engine [1] — measured boot without a discrete chip. **PQC sizes are the binding constraint**: ML-DSA-44 inflates Alias-certificate signature material 64 B → 2,420 B (~38×), SLH-DSA near 8 KB [10], forcing MTU/handshake re-engineering on 802.15.4/NB-IoT links. **Layering depth is bounded by boot latency, not cryptography**: reference flows converge on 3–5 layers (DICE RoT → bootloader → kernel/RTOS → application); beyond that, per-layer X.509 issuance dominates boot time on sub-100 MHz cores [6][7].

## 6 Limitations

**The UDS is a single point of catastrophic failure.** Every guarantee in §4 is conditional on UDS secrecy and correct provisioning; a manufacturer leak, a low-entropy PUF, or a latching flaw collapses the identity model with no EK-style recovery — yet the specification leaves provisioning mechanics to the manufacturer [2]. **Immutable DICE engines cannot be patched**: a ROM bug in measurement, OWF, or latching is unfixable, and a ROM DICE that cannot learn ML-DSA caps the deployment's cryptographic lifetime at the classical horizon. **No shielded storage or monotonic counters**: rollback protection and sealed data — native TPM features — must be reconstructed by higher layers (e.g., SVNs in CDI derivation) or a companion secure element. **Privacy depends on Alias-key hygiene**: exposing the stable DeviceID key to verifiers enables tracking; privacy is a deployment discipline, not silicon [4][6]. **Certificate-path hazard**: each layer's embedded-CA certificate must validate, and a misconfigured manufacturer *pathLenConstraint* can silently permit fraudulent chain extension [6]. **Coarse granularity and revocation**: DICE attests at TCI digest resolution with no itemized event log, and revocation is verifier-side TCB-info denylisting, which scales poorly across heterogeneous fleets.

## 7 Conclusion

DICE reduces platform identity to its essentials: one secret, one measurement, one one-way function, applied recursively. From this minimal core it recovers stable device identity (DeviceID key) and fresh firmware-bound attestation evidence (Alias key and certificate chain) on hardware that could never host a discrete TPM. The recurrence *CDI_{i+1} = OWF(CDI_i ∥ TCI_{i+1})* with per-layer secret erasure turns the boot sequence into tamper-evident identity derivation, and the X.509 profiles make the evidence consumable by existing PKI and cloud provisioning such as Azure IoT DPS [1][4].

DICE is not a strict improvement over TPM 2.0: it trades shielded storage for deterministic re-derivation, itemized logs for folded digests, and on-chip hierarchies for boot-time key generation — radical cost reduction at the price of rollback protection, fine-grained evidence, and core patchability. The choice between DICE, TPM, or hybrid should follow the device tier's threat model and bill of materials.

The post-quantum transition is the defining challenge for DICE deployments entering the field now. CDI derivation is quantum-resilient at adequate digest widths, but the certificate layer must migrate to ML-DSA and SLH-DSA — and ROM-resident DICE engines must be algorithm-agile from birth or accept a bounded cryptographic lifetime. The specification family's extension points anticipate this migration; whether deployed silicon does will decide whether today's DICE devices remain trustworthy in the quantum era.
