# Polar Codes and Channel Polarization: Arıkan's Construction, Successive-Cancellation Decoding, and the Finite-Length Regime

## Abstract

Polar codes, introduced by Erdal Arıkan in 2009, are the first explicit codes proven to achieve the symmetric capacity of arbitrary binary-input discrete memoryless channels with $O(N \log N)$ encoding and decoding complexity. The construction rests on *channel polarization*: a recursive $2\times 2$ combining kernel transforms $N$ channel copies into $N$ synthetic bit-channels that become either perfectly noiseless or perfectly noisy, in proportions matching capacity. Information rides the reliable subset; the rest are frozen. This thesis develops the full stack: the polarization martingale and Bhattacharyya-parameter evolution, exact recursions for the binary erasure channel, Arıkan's rate-of-polarization theorem bounding block error by $O(2^{-N^\beta})$ for $\beta < 1/2$, the successive-cancellation (SC) and successive-cancellation list (SCL) decoders of Tal and Vardy, CRC-aided polar codes as adopted for the 5G NR control channel, construction methods including Gaussian approximation and the universal reliability sequence, and the finite-length scaling law of Hassani, Alishahi, and Urbanke governing the gap to capacity. We close with the principal limitations and open problems for the 6G era.

## 1. Introduction

The problem of constructing *explicit* capacity-achieving codes with *low* complexity is among the oldest in coding theory. Shannon's 1948 random coding argument established that capacity is attainable but said nothing about how to build codes; the ensuing decades produced turbo and LDPC codes, which approach capacity empirically but resisted rigorous proof of capacity-achieving behavior under practical decoders. Arıkan's polar codes broke this deadlock: they are constructive, provably capacity-achieving for every binary-input discrete memoryless channel (B-DMC), and decoded by a recursive *successive-cancellation* (SC) algorithm with complexity $O(N \log N)$ [1].

> **Theorem:** (Channel Polarization, Arıkan 2009). Let $W$ be a symmetric B-DMC with symmetric capacity $I(W)$. For $N = 2^n$, let $W_N^{(i)}$ denote the $i$-th synthetic bit-channel induced by the polar transform $G_N$. Then, for any $\delta > 0$,
>
> $$\lim_{N \to \infty} \frac{|\{i : I(W_N^{(i)}) \in (1-\delta, 1]\}|}{N} = I(W), \quad \lim_{N \to \infty} \frac{|\{i : I(W_N^{(i)}) \in [0, \delta)\}|}{N} = 1 - I(W),$$
>
> and the fraction of unpolarized channels in $[\delta, 1-\delta]$ vanishes.

This single result is remarkable twice over: it gives a *code* achieving capacity (place information bits on the good channels, freeze the rest) and an *algorithm* (SC decoding) with FFT-like recursive structure. The price is the *rate* at which polarization occurs, which turns out to be comparatively slow — the block length needed to close the gap $\epsilon$ to capacity scales like $\epsilon^{-\mu}$ with scaling exponent $\mu \approx 3.55$–$3.63$ [2], far worse than the $\mu = 2$ of optimal random coding. That quantitative shortcoming is precisely what makes the *finite-length regime* the decisive engineering question, and it is why polar codes appear in the 5G standard only for short control-channel payloads with the stronger SCL decoder [4, 5].

This thesis proceeds as follows. Section 2 establishes the background: symmetric B-DMCs, the Bhattacharyya parameter, and the polar transform. Section 3 details the methodology of construction and decoding. Section 4 dives into four deep topics: the polarization martingale and rate of polarization, the SC and SCL decoders, 5G NR polar code design, and construction methods. Section 5 treats the finite-length scaling law with empirical confirmation. Section 6 confronts the limitations, and Section 7 concludes.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 Symmetric Binary-Input Discrete Memoryless Channels

A **binary-input discrete memoryless channel (B-DMC)** $W : \mathcal{X} \to \mathcal{Y}$ is specified by a binary input alphabet $\mathcal{X} = \{0, 1\}$, an output alphabet $\mathcal{Y}$ (discrete for our purposes), and transition probabilities $W(y|x)$. The channel is *symmetric* if there exists a permutation $\pi$ of $\mathcal{Y}$ with $\pi^2 = \mathrm{id}$ such that $W(y|1) = W(\pi(y)|0)$ for all $y$. Symmetry implies that uniform inputs achieve capacity, so the *symmetric capacity*

$$I(W) \triangleq \sum_{y \in \mathcal{Y}} \sum_{x \in \{0,1\}} \frac{1}{2} W(y|x) \log_2 \frac{W(y|x)}{\frac{1}{2} W(y|0) + \frac{1}{2} W(y|1)}$$

coincides with Shannon capacity $C(W)$.

Two canonical examples will recur throughout:

1. The **binary erasure channel** $\mathrm{BEC}(\epsilon)$: outputs $0$ with probability $1-\epsilon$ and an erasure symbol $e$ with probability $\epsilon$. Capacity $I(W) = 1 - \epsilon$.
2. **Binary-input AWGN channel**: $Y = (-1)^X \sqrt{E_s} + Z$ with $Z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, N_0/2)$, the workhorse model for 5G link-level evaluations [4].

### 2.2 The Bhattacharyya Parameter

Arıkan's analysis centers on a fidelity measure: the Bhattacharyya parameter $Z(W)$. Its extremes are meaningful: $Z(W) = 0$ implies $I(W) = 1$ (a noiseless channel), while $Z(W) = 1$ implies $I(W) = 0$ (pure noise) [1]. Under the polar transform, the symmetric capacity is *conserved* while the Bhattacharyya parameter is driven to its extremes — the analytic engine of polarization.

### 2.3 The Polar Transform

For $N = 2^n$, Arıkan defines the generator matrix $G_N = B_N F^{\otimes n}$, where

$$F = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$$

is the $2 \times 2$ polarizing kernel, $\otimes$ denotes the Kronecker product, and $B_N$ is the bit-reversal permutation matrix. Encoding maps the source vector $u_1^N$ to the codeword $x_1^N = u_1^N G_N$. The single-step channel combining and splitting is the conceptual core: given two channels $W', W''$, one forms the combined vector channel $W(y_1, y_2 | x_1, x_2) = W'(y_1 | x_1 \oplus x_2)\, W''(y_2 | x_2)$ and splits it, with a prescribed channel ordering, into two synthetic channels [6]:

- the **minus channel** $W^-(y_1, y_2 | x_1) = \sum_{x_2} \frac{1}{2} W'(y_1 | x_1 \oplus x_2)\, W''(y_2 | x_2)$,
- the **plus channel** $W^+(y_1, y_2, x_1 | x_2) = \frac{1}{2} W'(y_1 | x_1 \oplus x_2)\, W''(y_2 | x_2)$.

Capacity is conserved exactly: $I(W^-) + I(W^+) = I(W') + I(W'')$. For the Bhattacharyya parameter, polarization moves reliability *uphill*: $Z(W^+) = Z(W') Z(W'')$ exactly for symmetric channels, while $Z(W^-) \le Z(W') + Z(W'') - Z(W') Z(W'')$, with equality on the BEC where the recursion is exact:

$$Z(W^-) = 2Z - Z^2, \qquad Z(W^+) = Z^2 \quad \text{(BEC)}.$$

Iterating this splitting $n$ times over $N = 2^n$ channels yields the synthetic bit-channels $W_N^{(i)}$, $i = 1, \dots, N$, whose $Z$ values polarize to $0$ or $1$ as $n \to \infty$.

## 3. Methodology

Our methodological approach is threefold: (i) a rigorous walk through the polarization martingale establishing the asymptotic theorem; (ii) an algorithmic treatment of the SC and SCL decoders with exact complexity accounting; and (iii) a finite-length analysis anchored in the scaling-exponent framework of Hassani, Alishahi, and Urbanke [2], cross-checked against 5G NR link-level results [4, 5]. Where closed-form results exist — the BEC recursion, the min-sum SC update rules, the 3GPP reliability sequence — we derive them explicitly; where only bounds exist, we state the sharpest known.

### 3.1 Polar Code Construction

A polar code $\mathcal{P}(N, K, \mathcal{A})$ is parameterized by block length $N = 2^n$, dimension $K$, and an information set $\mathcal{A} \subset \{1, \dots, N\}$ with $|\mathcal{A}| = K$. The complement $\mathcal{A}^c$ is the *frozen set*; the frozen bits $u_{\mathcal{A}^c}$ are fixed to values known to the decoder (typically $0$). The information set is chosen as the $K$ indices with the smallest Bhattacharyya parameters $Z(W_N^{(i)})$ — the "good" bit-channels. Encoding is the linear map $x_1^N = u_1^N G_N$, implementable by the factor graph of $F^{\otimes n}$ in $O(N \log N)$ operations via the butterfly recursion:

```python
def polar_encode(u: list[int]) -> list[int]:
    """Non-systematic polar encoding via the F^{\otimes n} butterfly."""
    x = u[:]
    n = len(x).bit_length() - 1  # N = 2^n
    step = 1
    for _ in range(n):
        for i in range(0, len(x), 2 * step):
            for j in range(i, i + step):
                x[j] ^= x[j + step]   # the (u1 xor u2, u2) kernel
        step *= 2
    return x
```

### 3.2 Successive-Cancellation Decoding

The SC decoder processes bits sequentially from $i = 1$ to $N$. For each $i$, it computes the likelihood of $u_i$ given the channel outputs and all previously decided bits $\hat{u}_1^{i-1}$, then decides

$$\hat{u}_i = \begin{cases} u_i, & i \in \mathcal{A}^c \ \text{(frozen)}, \\ h_i(y_1^N, \hat{u}_1^{i-1}), & i \in \mathcal{A}, \end{cases}$$

where $h_i$ thresholds the synthetic channel likelihood. Because the likelihoods factor through the polar graph, the decoder reuses the recursive structure, giving $O(N \log N)$ complexity — the first *provable* low-complexity capacity-achieving scheme.

### 3.3 List Decoding and CRC Concatenation

SC suffers from *error propagation*: a single wrong hard decision poisons all subsequent LLRs. Tal and Vardy [3] generalized SC to **successive-cancellation list (SCL)** decoding, which maintains $L$ candidate paths, branching at each information bit and pruning to the $L$ best path metrics. With $L \ge 1$ the decoder explores multiple hypotheses, and selecting the final path by a cyclic redundancy check (CRC) — the CRC-aided SCL (CA-SCL) architecture — yields near-ML performance for short blocks. This is the configuration standardized in 5G NR, with list size $L = 8$ as the 3GPP baseline [5].

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Polarization Martingale and the Rate of Polarization

The cleanest proof of polarization uses a stochastic process. Define the tree process $W_0 = W$, $W_{n+1} = W_n^-$ or $W_n^+$ with probability $1/2$ each, following the random path down the polarization tree. The induced processes $I_n = I(W_n)$ and $Z_n = Z(W_n)$ satisfy:

- $\{I_n\}$ is a bounded martingale (by capacity conservation), hence converges almost surely and in $L^1$ to $I_\infty \in \{0, 1\}$, with $\Pr(I_\infty = 1) = I(W)$;
- $\{Z_n\}$ is a supermartingale converging a.s. to $Z_\infty \in \{0, 1\}$, and for symmetric $W$, $Z_\infty = 1 - I_\infty$ a.s.

This establishes the *qualitative* theorem. The *quantitative* question — how fast does $Z_n$ vanish? — was answered by Arıkan and Telatar [1]: for any $\beta < 1/2$ and sufficiently large $n$,

$$\Pr\left(Z_n < 2^{-2^{n\beta}}\right) \ge I(W) - 2^{-n(1/2 - \beta) + o(n)},$$

so that a polar code of rate $R < I(W)$ under SC decoding achieves block error probability at most $O(2^{-N^\beta})$ for any $\beta < 1/2$. Guruswami and Xia later established the *speed of polarization* bounds proving the exponent $1/2$ is essentially tight [7]: for $\beta > 1/2$, $\Pr(Z_n < 2^{-2^{n\beta}}) \to 0$. The exponent $1/2$ is thus the intrinsic rate-of-polarization constant of the $2 \times 2$ kernel.

### 4.2 SC and SCL Decoders in Detail

The SC decoder operates on the factor graph of $G_N$. At each node, it propagates log-likelihood ratios (LLRs) with the two fundamental update rules (in the min-sum approximation used by hardware decoders):

$$\alpha_j^l = \operatorname{sgn}(\alpha_j)\,\operatorname{sgn}(\alpha_{j+2^{t-1}})\,\min(|\alpha_j|, |\alpha_{j+2^{t-1}}|), \qquad \alpha_j^r = \alpha_{j+2^{t-1}} + (1 - 2\beta_j^l)\,\alpha_j.$$

The partial sums $\beta$ are computed as $\beta_j = \beta_j^l \oplus \beta_j^r$ for $j \le 2^{t-1}$ and $\beta_j = \beta_{j-2^{t-1}}^r$ otherwise. At stage $0$, the bit decision is $\hat{u}_i = 0$ if $i \in \mathcal{F}$ or $\alpha_0 \ge 0$, else $1$ [5].

SCL decoding generalizes this single greedy path into a beam search of width $L$:

1. At each information bit, every surviving path forks into $u_i = 0$ and $u_i = 1$ branches with updated path metrics.
2. Frozen bits produce no branching.
3. If the path count exceeds $L$, only the $L$ paths with the best (smallest penalty) metrics survive.
4. At the end, the path passing the CRC check (if any) is selected; otherwise the best-metric path.

The complexity is $O(L \cdot N \log N)$ with an efficient implementation exploiting the regular graph structure [3]. For the BEC, Hashemi et al. proved SCL coincides with ML decoding for list size exceeding $2^\gamma$, where $\gamma$ is a *mixing factor* counting information bits interleaved among frozen bits — and Fazeli, Vardy, and Yao extended this from the BEC to arbitrary binary-input memoryless symmetric channels. Empirically, on the AWGN channel, $L = 32$ at $N = 1024$ essentially matches ML performance [5].

```rust
/// Path-metric update for one SCL fork (min-sum LLR domain).
/// penalty: 0 if bit agrees with LLR sign, |llr| otherwise.
fn fork_metric(metric: f32, llr: f32, bit: u8) -> f32 {
    let hard: u8 = if llr >= 0.0 { 0 } else { 1 };
    if bit == hard { metric } else { metric + llr.abs() }
}
```

### 4.3 Polar Codes in 5G NR: The Control-Channel Standard

At 3GPP RAN1 #87 (Reno, November 2016), polar codes were selected as the channel code for the enhanced mobile broadband (eMBB) *control* channels — the physical downlink control channel (PDCCH) and physical uplink control channel (PUCCH) — while LDPC codes took the data channels [4]. This was polar codes' first appearance in a commercial standard, and the design choices in 3GPP TS 38.212 are instructive:

- **CRC concatenation**: 24 CRC bits for PDCCH (enabling early termination and false-alarm mitigation) and 11 for PUCCH, always feeding the CA-SCL decoder.
- **Mother code lengths**: up to $N = 512$ for PDCCH and $N = 1024$ for PUCCH, with *rate matching* (puncturing, shortening, repetition) to hit arbitrary payload sizes $A$ and coded lengths $G$.
- **Universal reliability sequence**: a fixed sequence $Q_0^{N_{\max}-1}$ (derived from polarization-weight principles) replaces channel-dependent construction, making the code *universal* across SNRs.
- **Parity-check (PC) bits**: for $A \le 19$ on PUCCH, dynamically frozen PC bits act as additional assistant bits for path pruning.

Figure 13 of the 5G design survey [4] is the canonical finite-length datapoint: with BPSK over AWGN and SCL $L = 8$, the 5G polar code at BLER $10^{-3}$ tracks the Polyanskiy–Poor–Verdù (PPV) meta-converse bound within a fraction of a dB for short payloads — the regime where polar codes beat LDPC and turbo codes and justified their standardization.

| Decoder | Complexity | Short-block performance |
|---|---|---|
| SC | $O(N \log N)$ | Poor; error propagation dominates |
| SCL, $L = 8$ | $O(8\,N \log N)$ | Near-optimal at $N \le 1024$; 3GPP baseline [5] |
| CA-SCL, $L = 32$ | $O(32\,N \log N)$ | Matches ML on AWGN at moderate $N$ [3] |
| SC-flip / SC-stack | $\approx$ SC average | Recovers most of SCL gain at lower average cost |

### 4.4 Construction Methods: From BEC Recursion to Universal Sequences

Polar *construction* — choosing the information set $\mathcal{A}$ — is a reliability-ordering problem. Four generations of methods exist [6]:

1. **Bhattacharyya recursion (Arıkan)**: exact for the BEC via $Z^- = 2Z - Z^2$, $Z^+ = Z^2$; for general B-DMCs the alphabet size of $W_N^{(i)}$ grows doubly exponentially, making exact computation infeasible.
2. **Density evolution (Mori–Tanaka)**: tracks the full LLR density through the graph; theoretically exact but memory-heavy.
3. **Tal–Vardy quantized DE**: quantizes the output alphabet to obtain provable upper and lower bounds on bit-channel error probability — the practical gold standard for off-line construction.
4. **Gaussian approximation (Trifonov)**: assumes LLRs are Gaussian, collapsing density evolution to a scalar mean recursion; $O(N)$ and remarkably accurate on the AWGN channel.

The 5G standard sidesteps all of the above with the **polarization-weight (PW)** universal sequence: the reliability of bit-channel $i$ is approximated by $W(i) = \sum_{j=0}^{n-1} b_j \beta^j$ with $\beta = 2^{1/4}$, where $b_j$ are the bits of the binary expansion of $i$. This channel-independent ordering is within a hair of the channel-specific optimum and enables a single hardwired sequence for all SNRs and rates [4].

## 5. Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 The Finite-Length Scaling Law

The scaling framework asks: at *fixed* block error probability $P_e$, how does the required block length $N$ grow as the rate $R$ approaches capacity $I(W)$? Hassani, Alishahi, and Urbanke [2] proved universal bounds for all binary-input memoryless symmetric channels under SC decoding:

$$N \ge \frac{\alpha}{(I(W) - R)^{\underline{\mu}}}, \qquad N \le \frac{\beta}{(I(W) - R)^{\bar{\mu}}},$$

where the gap $\epsilon = I(W) - R$, $\alpha, \beta$ are constants depending on $P_e$ and $I(W)$, and the scaling exponents satisfy $\underline{\mu} = 3.55$ (valid universally) and $\bar{\mu} = 7$; they conjecture the universal lower exponent can be improved to $\underline{\mu} = 3.627$, the exact value for the BEC. Contrast this with random coding, where the optimal scaling exponent is $\mu = 2$. The gap $3.55$ vs. $2$ quantifies polar codes' finite-length handicap: to halve the gap to capacity at fixed error probability, a polar code needs roughly $2^{3.55} \approx 11.7\times$ the block length, versus $4\times$ for an optimal code.

For the BEC the scaling exponent is known exactly ($\mu = 3.627\ldots$, later refined by Fazeli, Hassani, Mondelli, and Vardy to approximately $3.639$), and the dispersion-style analysis connects the scaling exponent to the fraction of *unpolarized* channels — those with $Z_n \in (a, b)$ for fixed $0 < a < b < 1$ — whose count decays polynomially in $N$ [2]. Numerically, for $\mathrm{BEC}(0.5)$ at $P_e = 10^{-3}$, the required block length at rate $0.4$ is on the order of $10^4$–$10^5$, consistent with $\epsilon^{-\mu} = 0.1^{-3.63} \approx 4.3 \times 10^3$ up to constants.

### 5.2 Proof Sketch of the Scaling Lower Bound

The Hassani–Alishahi–Urbanke argument [2] proceeds in three steps:

1. **Unpolarized-channel counting.** Show that the number of bit-channels with $Z(W_N^{(i)}) \in (a, b)$ is at least $c(a,b)\, N^{1 - 1/\underline{\mu}}$ up to polylogarithmic factors, for any fixed interval $(a,b) \subset (0,1)$. This uses refined bounds on the polarization process $\{Z_n\}$, controlling the probability that the process lingers in the middle region.
2. **Error lower bound.** Each unpolarized channel in the information set contributes at least a constant to the SC block error probability (its first-error-event probability is bounded away from zero), so achieving $P_e$ forces the information set to *exclude* nearly all unpolarized channels.
3. **Rate penalty.** Excluding those channels costs rate: the achievable rate is $R \le I(W) - c'\,\epsilon$-style, and inverting the unpolarized-count bound yields $N \gtrsim \epsilon^{-\underline{\mu}}$ with $\underline{\mu} = 3.55$.

### 5.3 Simulation Confirmation

Link-level simulations corroborate the theory across the three canonical channels:

- On the **BEC**, the measured gap to capacity at $P_e = 10^{-4}$ follows the predicted $\epsilon \sim N^{-1/3.63}$ slope over $N \in [2^{10}, 2^{20}]$ [2].
- On **BI-AWGN** with GA construction and SC decoding, the block error curves at fixed $N$ shift with rate consistent with the universal lower bound exponent $3.55$.
- On the **BSC**, quantized-DE construction confirms the same scaling, with the constants $\alpha, \beta$ absorbing the channel-specific dispersion.

The 5G NR results [4] provide the applied counterpart: CA-SCL with $L = 8$ at $N \le 1024$ achieves BLER $10^{-3}$ within $\sim$0.5 dB of the PPV finite-blocklength bound, demonstrating that while the *asymptotic* scaling exponent is suboptimal, the *absolute* short-block performance with list decoding is state of the art — which is precisely why the standard restricts polar codes to short control payloads.

## 6. Limitations

No honest treatment of polar codes can omit their structural weaknesses:

1. **Error propagation under SC.** The greedy sequential decoder cannot revise early mistakes; at short-to-moderate block lengths the SC block error is dominated by the first information bit-channel error, and plain SC is uncompetitive. SCL repairs this but multiplies complexity by $L$ and — more subtly — the optimal information set for SCL differs from the SC-optimal set, so 5G codes are *mismatched* to their decoder [6].

2. **Suboptimal scaling exponent.** The scaling exponent $\mu \approx 3.55$–$3.63$ versus the information-theoretic optimum $\mu = 2$ is a permanent tax of the $2 \times 2$ kernel. Larger kernels ($l \times l$ with $l > 2$) can improve the exponent — Korada, Şaşoğlu, and Urbanke showed binary kernels exist with exponent arbitrarily close to the optimum — but at the cost of decoding complexity exponential in the kernel size [7].

3. **Poor minimum distance.** Plain polar codes have small minimum distance (the first information rows of $G_N$ have low weight), which caps the ML performance that even infinite-list SCL can reach. CRC concatenation and *pre-transformed* polar codes (PAC codes, polarization-adjusted convolutional codes of Arıkan) address this by coupling information bits through an outer convolution, dramatically improving the distance spectrum while preserving the polarization structure.

4. **Non-universality of construction.** Exact reliability ordering depends on the channel; the 5G universal sequence is a pragmatic compromise, and at extreme rates or exotic channels (fading, MIMO) the mismatch costs tenths of a dB. Compound and non-stationary extensions exist [6] but complicate the elegant theory.

5. **Latency of sequential decoding.** SC/SCL are inherently serial in $N$ steps, unlike the parallel message-passing of LDPC decoders. Fast-SSC prunes the decoding tree (Rate-0, Rate-1, repetition, and SPC nodes) to cut latency, but the fundamental sequentiality remains a drawback for terabit-throughput data-plane use — a key reason polar codes lost the 5G *data* channel to LDPC.

---

## 7. Conclusion

Channel polarization is one of the deepest ideas in modern coding theory: a deterministic, low-complexity transform that *sorts* the randomness of a channel into perfect and useless extremes, thereby converting Shannon's existential capacity theorem into an explicit engineering artifact. From the polarization martingale and the $O(2^{-N^\beta})$ rate-of-polarization bound [1, 7], through the Tal–Vardy list decoder [3] and the finite-length scaling law [2], to the 3GPP control-channel standard [4, 5], polar codes trace a rare arc from pure information theory to deployed infrastructure within a single decade.

The frontier has shifted accordingly: **pre-transformed polar codes** (PAC codes) have nearly closed the short-block gap to the PPV bound, **larger kernels** attack the scaling exponent, and the 6G debate will decide whether polar codes expand beyond control channels into the data plane. The finite-length regime — where asymptotic promises meet silicon's latency budgets — remains the arena in which these battles are won or lost.
