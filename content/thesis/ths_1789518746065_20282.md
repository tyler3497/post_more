# Spectral Expanders and Ramanujan Graphs: the Alon–Boppana Bound, Interlacing Polynomials, and the Zig-Zag Product

## Abstract

Spectral expanders are sparse graphs whose connectivity rivals that of dense complete graphs, and they sit at the crossroads of combinatorics, number theory, pseudorandomness, and theoretical computer science. The Alon–Boppana bound \u2013 that any infinite family of d-regular graphs has second eigenvalue at least 2\u221a(d\u22121)\u2212o(1) \u2013 sets the absolute limit of spectral purity, and Ramanujan graphs are those rare objects that attain it. This thesis surveys the three great pillars of the subject: the lower bound itself and its tree-universal-cover proof; the explicit constructions, from the number-theoretic Lubotzky–Phillips–Sarnak Cayley graphs through the fully combinatorial zig-zag product of Reingold, Vadhan, and Wigderson to the interlacing-polynomial method of Marcus, Spielman, and Srivastava, which yields bipartite Ramanujan graphs of every degree via 2-lifts; and the spectral dictionary linking eigenvalues to edge expansion, random-walk mixing times, and derandomization.

---

##1 Introduction

An **expander graph** is a sparse graph that is nonetheless highly connected: every small set of vertices has a large boundary. Formally, a d-regular graph G on n vertices is an *edge expander* with expansion h if every subset S of vertices with |S| at most n/2 satisfies |E(S, S̄)| \u2265 h\u00b7|S|. Such graphs are the workhorses of derandomization, coding theory, and network design, yet the combinatorial definition is notoriously difficult to certify. The spectral approach replaces the combinatorial condition with a linear-algebraic one that is checkable in polynomial time: the second-largest eigenvalue \u03bb₂ of the adjacency matrix must be bounded away from d. Graphs with \u03bb₂ \u2264 (1\u2212\u03b5)d are expanders, by the discrete Cheeger inequality, and the larger the spectral gap d\u2212\u03bb₂, the better the expansion, the faster random walks mix, and the stronger the pseudorandom properties [1].

How good can a spectral expander be? This question has a sharp and beautiful answer. In 1986, Alon and Boppana proved that for any fixed degree d, any infinite family of d-regular graphs must satisfy liminf \u03bb₂ \u2265 2\u221a(d\u22121) [1]. The number 2\u221a(d\u22121) is not arbitrary: it is the spectral radius of the infinite d-regular tree, the universal cover of every d-regular graph, and it is the edge of the Kesten–McKay bulk of the tree's spectrum. A d-regular graph whose every nontrivial eigenvalue has absolute value at most 2\u221a(d\u22121) is called a **Ramanujan graph**, a term coined by Lubotzky, Phillips, and Sarnak, who in 1988 gave the first explicit infinite families, for degrees of the form p+1 with p prime [2]. Their construction is a cathedral of modern mathematics: Cayley graphs of PGL(2, Z/qZ) whose Ramanujan property follows from Deligne's proof of the Ramanujan–Petersson conjecture for GL(2).

For decades the landscape was dominated by these deep number-theoretic constructions and by probabilistic ones (Friedman's theorem that random d-regular graphs are *almost* Ramanujan). Two revolutions changed everything. First, Reingold, Vadhan, and Wigderson (2002) gave a **fully combinatorial** iterative construction of constant-degree expanders of arbitrary size using a new graph product, the *zig-zag product*, and their entropy-wave intuition reduced the eigenvalue analysis to a clean three-step decomposition [3]. Second, Marcus, Spielman, and Srivastava (2015) introduced the **method of interlacing polynomials**, proving that every graph has a 2-lift whose new eigenvalues are bounded by the roots of the graph's matching polynomial, and thereby establishing infinite families of *bipartite* Ramanujan graphs of *every* degree d \u2265 3, a problem that had resisted number-theoretic methods [4]. Their work also resolved the Bilu–Linial conjecture on graph signings and, in its second part, contributed the decisive ideas behind the resolution of the Kadison–Singer problem [5].

This thesis presents a unified account: the Alon–Boppana bound, the three construction paradigms, and the spectral dictionary that makes them useful.

##2 Background

### 2.1 Spectral definitions

Let G = (V, E) be a d-regular graph on n vertices with adjacency matrix A. Its eigenvalues are real and ordered

d = \u03bb₁ \u2265 \u03bb₂ \u2265 \u22ef \u2265 \u03bbₙ \u2265 \u2212d.

The **spectral gap** is d \u2212 \u03bb₂. Define \u03bb(G) = max(|\u03bb₂|, |\u03bbₙ|), the largest nontrivial eigenvalue in absolute value. G is Ramanujan if \u03bb(G) \u2264 2\u221a(d\u22121). For bipartite graphs the spectrum is symmetric, so it suffices to bound \u03bb₂.

The *mixing lemma* and the discrete Cheeger inequality tie \u03bb₂ to expansion. If \u03bb₂ is small, then for any two sets S, T,

|E(S, T) \u2212 (d/n)|S||T|| \u2264 \u03bb₂\u221a(|S||T|),

so the graph behaves like a random graph for edge densities: this is the precise sense in which spectral expanders are *pseudorandom* [1].

### 2.2 The infinite regular tree and Kesten's bound

The infinite d-regular tree T_d is the universal cover of every d-regular graph: locally, every d-regular graph looks like T_d up to the girth. Kesten proved that the spectral radius of the adjacency operator of T_d is \u03c1(T_d) = 2\u221a(d\u22121). Equivalently, the number of closed walks of length 2k at a vertex of T_d grows like (2\u221a(d\u22121))^{2k} up to polynomial factors \u2013 the Catalan-like combinatorics of nonbacktracking walks. Any finite d-regular graph contains large tree-like neighborhoods (provided its girth is large, or by a trace-method averaging argument regardless), so some eigenvalue must be at least as large as the tree's spectral radius. This is the heart of the Alon–Boppana bound, and Nilli's refinement shows the bound is effective: diameter at least 2k forces \u03bb₂ \u2265 2\u221a(d\u22121)\u2212o(1) with explicit dependence on k [1].

### 2.3 Lifts and signings

A **2-lift** of a graph G is obtained by replacing each vertex v by two copies (v, 0) and (v, 1), and for each edge {u, v} of G choosing either the *parallel* matching {(u,0),(v,0)}, {(u,1),(v,1)} or the *crossing* matching {(u,0),(v,1)}, {(u,1),(v,0)}. Encoding the choice by a signing s: E \u2192 {+1, \u22121}, the adjacency matrix of the lift is

A_s = \u23a1 A_+ + A_s   A_+ \u2212 A_s \u23a4 / symmetric block form

in the obvious basis, and a standard calculation shows the spectrum of the lift is the union of the spectrum of G and the spectrum of the **signed adjacency matrix** A_s, where (A_s)_{uv} = s(e) for each edge e = {u,v}. Bilu and Linial conjectured that every d-regular graph has a signing with \u03c1(A_s) \u2264 2\u221a(d\u22121) \u2013 i.e., every d-regular graph has a Ramanujan 2-lift [4]. This was the conjecture that interlacing families proved.

##3 Methodology

Our exposition follows three methodological threads, each corresponding to one of the deep results surveyed.

1. **Trace and test-vector methods** for lower bounds: we derive the Alon–Boppana bound via the trace method on nonbacktracking walks, making explicit the role of the tree spectral radius and the girth/diameter tradeoff.
2. **Product constructions** for explicit expanders: we define the zig-zag product G ⓩ H of an (n, m, \u03b1)-graph G and an (m, d, \u03b2)-graph H, prove the eigenvalue bound \u03c6(\u03b1, \u03b2) \u2264 \u03b1 + \u03b2, and iterate with graph squaring to obtain infinite families of constant-degree expanders [3]. The **entropy-wave** viewpoint \u2013 expanders as operators that dissipate concentrated probability mass \u2013 guides the decomposition.
3. **Interlacing families** for existence proofs: we define interlacing families of real-rooted polynomials, show that the expected characteristic polynomial of a random signing equals the *matching polynomial* \u03bc_G(x), and apply the Heilmann–Lieb root bound \u03c1(\u03bc_G) \u2264 2\u221a(d\u22121) to guarantee a signing, hence a 2-lift, with spectral radius at most the Ramanujan bound [4][5].

Throughout we use the (n, d, \u03bb)-graph notation: n vertices, d-regular, \u03bb(G) \u2264 \u03bbd with \u03bb < 1.

##4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Alon–Boppana bound via the trace method

> **Theorem:** (Alon–Boppana). Fix d \u2265 3. For every \u03b5 > 0, every sufficiently large d-regular graph G has \u03bb₂(G) \u2265 2\u221a(d\u22121) \u2212 \u03b5 [1].

*Proof sketch.* Let A be the adjacency matrix and consider Tr(A^{2k}) = \u03a3ᵢ \u03bbᵢ^{2k} = d^{2k} + \u03a3_{i\u22652} \u03bbᵢ^{2k}. Each closed walk of length 2k in G projects to a closed walk in the universal cover T_d, and at least a constant fraction of vertices see a full tree-like 2k-neighborhood (or, in Nilli's argument, one restricts to vertices far from short cycles). Counting nonbacktracking closed walks on T_d gives

Tr(A^{2k}) \u2265 n\u00b7c_d\u00b7(2\u221a(d\u22121))^{2k}/k^{3/2}

for an explicit constant c_d. Hence \u03a3_{i\u22652} \u03bbᵢ^{2k} \u2265 n\u00b7c'_d\u00b7(2\u221a(d\u22121))^{2k}, and since there are n\u22121 terms, the largest satisfies |\u03bb₂|^{2k} \u2265 c'_d(2\u221a(d\u22121))^{2k}, i.e. \u03bb₂ \u2265 (c'_d)^{1/2k}\u00b72\u221a(d\u22121) \u2192 2\u221a(d\u22121) as k \u2192 \u221e, with k growing slowly in n. ∎

The bound is **asymptotically optimal**: random d-regular graphs satisfy \u03bb(G) \u2264 2\u221a(d\u22121) + o(1) with high probability (Friedman), so the interval below 2\u221a(d\u22121) cannot be shrunk for infinite families. Srivastava's 2025 survey emphasizes this phase transition: until very recently the only known proof that the bound is *attained* by infinite families was the LPS/Margulis number-theoretic construction [8].

### 4.2 The Lubotzky–Phillips–Sarnak construction

The LPS graphs X^{p,q} are Cayley graphs of PGL(2, Z/qZ) (or PSL(2, Z/qZ)) with a generating set S of size p+1, where p, q are distinct primes congruent to 1 mod 4 with Legendre symbol (p/q) = \u22121. The generators are explicit integral quaternions of norm p. The Ramanujan property \u2013 all nontrivial eigenvalues bounded by 2\u221ap \u2013 is equivalent to the **Ramanujan–Petersson conjecture** for the corresponding automorphic forms on GL(2), proved by Deligne as part of the Weil conjectures [2]. In brief: eigenvalues of the Hecke operator T_p acting on the relevant space of cusp forms are bounded by 2\u221ap, and these Hecke eigenvalues *are* the nontrivial graph eigenvalues. The construction yields (p+1)-regular Ramanujan graphs with n \u2248 q³ vertices and girth at least (4/3)\u00b7log_{p}(n) \u2212 o(1), asymptotically optimal girth. Morgenstern extended the construction to prime powers q [2].

The limitation is philosophical as much as technical: the proof that these graphs are Ramanujan uses some of the deepest mathematics of the 20th century, and it produces Ramanujan graphs only for degrees one more than a prime (or prime power). The 2024 breakthroughs reported by Srivastava \u2013 that random regular graphs are Ramanujan with positive probability \u2013 suggest the phenomenon is robust rather than miraculous [8].

### 4.3 The zig-zag product and entropy waves

The zig-zag product is the first **purely combinatorial** construction of infinite families of constant-degree expanders. Let G be an (n, m, \u03b1)-graph and H an (m, d, \u03b2)-graph, where we identify V(H) with the d-regular neighborhood indices at each vertex of G. The vertex set of G ⓩ H is V(G) \u00d7 V(H): each vertex of G is replaced by a *cloud* copy of H. Edges are three-step walks: a **zig** step inside a cloud along H, a **replacement** step between clouds along an edge of G (using the rotation map to match indices), and a **zag** step inside the destination cloud along H. The product is d²-regular on nm vertices [3].

> **Theorem:** (Reingold–Vadhan–Wigderson). If G is an (n, m, \u03b1)-graph and H is an (m, d, \u03b2)-graph, then G ⓩ H is an (nm, d², \u03c6(\u03b1, \u03b2))-graph with \u03c6(\u03b1, \u03b2) \u2264 \u03b1 + \u03b2, and also \u03c6(\u03b1, \u03b2) \u2264 1 \u2212 (1\u2212\u03b2²)(1\u2212\u03b1)/2 [3].

The **entropy-wave** intuition makes the proof transparent. View the random walk on G ⓩ H as acting on probability distributions. Decompose any distribution into a component constant on clouds (the *parallel* part) and a component summing to zero on each cloud (the *perpendicular* part). A zig or zag step contracts the perpendicular part by factor \u03b2 (since H is an expander); the replacement step acts on the parallel part like a step of G, contracting by \u03b1, while mapping perpendicular to parallel harmlessly. The three steps conspire so the product inherits expansion from *both* factors while its degree depends only on the small graph H.

Iterating with graph squaring gives the explicit family: start with a constant-size (d⁴, d, 1/4)-graph H (found by brute force), set G₁ = H², and define G_{k+1} = (G_k)² ⓩ H. Then G_k is a (d^{4k}, d², 1/2)-graph for all k: an infinite family of constant-degree expanders built by pure combinatorics [3]. A later generalization of the zig-zag product achieves spectral gap 1 \u2212 D^{\u22121/2+o(1)}, nearly the Ramanujan-quality tradeoff, still fully explicitly [7].

The zig-zag product also powers Reingold's celebrated theorem that undirected connectivity is in deterministic logspace (SL = L), via the derandomized square [3].

```python
# Spectral-gap bookkeeping for the zig-zag iteration (Hoory-Linial-Wigderson notation)
def zigzag_bound(alpha, beta):
    """RVW bound: phi(alpha, beta) <= alpha + beta."""
    return alpha + beta

def iterate(k, d=8, alpha0=0.25, beta=0.25):
    # G1 = H^2 has lambda <= alpha0^2; each iteration squares then zig-zags
    lam = alpha0 ** 2
    for _ in range(1, k):
        lam = zigzag_bound(lam ** 2, beta)   # square: lambda -> lambda^2
        lam = min(lam, 0.5)                  # stabilized by the RVW fixed point
    return lam

print([round(iterate(k), 4) for k in range(1, 6)])
# -> expansion parameter stays bounded away from 1 for all k
```

### 4.4 Interlacing polynomials and 2-lifts

The most surprising recent chapter begins with a change of perspective: instead of constructing Ramanujan graphs directly, show that *every* graph has a 2-lift that is "spectrally good," then iterate lifts starting from the complete graph K_{d+1}.

A family of polynomials {f_{s₁,…,s_m}} indexed by signings is an **interlacing family** if every convex combination \u03a3 \u03bc p has only real roots and the roots of siblings interlace appropriately; the key lemma is that some leaf polynomial has its largest root at most the largest root of the *expected* polynomial \u03a3_s p_s [4]. For signings of a graph G, the relevant polynomials are the characteristic polynomials of the signed adjacency matrices, and Godsil–Gutman proved the remarkable identity

E_s[det(xI \u2212 A_s)] = \u03bc_G(x),

the **matching polynomial** of G, where \u03bc_G(x) = \u03a3_{k} (\u22121)^k m_k x^{n\u22122k} with m_k the number of k-matchings. Heilmann and Lieb (1972) proved that matching polynomials of graphs of maximum degree d are real-rooted with all roots bounded by 2\u221a(d\u22121) in absolute value. Chaining these facts:

> **Theorem:** (Marcus–Spielman–Srivastava). Every d-regular graph has a 2-lift in which all *new* eigenvalues (those of A_s) are at most 2\u221a(d\u22121) in absolute value. Consequently there exist infinite families of d-regular *bipartite* Ramanujan graphs for every d \u2265 3 [4].

Starting from K_{d+1} and repeatedly taking Ramanujan 2-lifts (lifts of bipartite graphs stay bipartite) produces the infinite family. The same machinery yields (c,d)-biregular bipartite Ramanujan graphs with bound \u221a(c\u22121)+\u221a(d\u22121), the Feng–Li generalization of Alon–Boppana [4]. The second paper in the series extends interlacing families to *mixed* characteristic polynomials, giving the barrier-method proof underlying the Kadison–Singer resolution [5].

```haskell
-- Matching polynomial: mu_G(x) = sum_k (-1)^k m_k x^(n-2k)
-- Heilmann-Lieb: max root <= 2*sqrt(d-1) for max degree d.
matchingPoly :: Int -> [Integer] -> [Double]  -- n vertices, m_k list
matchingPoly n ms = foldr addPoly [] terms
  where
    terms = [ scale ((-1)^k * fromIntegral mk)
                    (monomial (n - 2*k)) | (k, mk) <- zip [0..] ms ]
    addPoly = zipWith (+); scale c = map (c*); monomial e = replicate e 0 ++ [1]
```

##5 Empirical Evidence and Proofs

The theory is anchored by three kinds of evidence: extremal examples, probabilistic theorems, and computational verification.

**Probabilistic optimality.** Friedman's theorem states that a random d-regular graph satisfies \u03bb(G) \u2264 2\u221a(d\u22121) + \u03b5 with high probability for every fixed \u03b5 > 0. Recent breakthroughs (late 2024) strengthen this dramatically: random regular graphs are *exactly* Ramanujan with probability bounded away from zero (about 69% in the models analyzed), showing the Alon–Boppana bound is not merely a limit but a typical value [8]. This reframes Ramanujan graphs from "miracles" to "robust phenomena."

**Computational certification.** Because the Ramanujan property is a statement about eigenvalues, it is *verifiable* in polynomial time: given a graph, compute \u03bb(G) numerically. The MSS 2-lift proof is currently nonconstructive at each step (it guarantees a good signing exists), but Cohen (2016) and Gribinski–Marcus (2021) made the construction algorithmic, achieving polynomial-time bipartite Ramanujan graphs of all degrees [6]. A natural computational experiment \u2013 implemented below in outline \u2013 samples random signings of a small d-regular base graph and checks the Heilmann–Lieb bound empirically.

```tla+
---- MODULE ZigZagCheck ----
EXTENDS Naturals, FiniteSets
CONSTANTS G, H  (* (n,m,alpha)-graph and (m,d,beta)-graph *)
VARIABLES dist
ZigZagStep(p) ==
  (* one zig-zag-zig triple: H-step, G-step (rotation map), H-step *)
  LET zig1 == HNeighbor(p) IN
  LET mid  == GNeighbor(p, zig1) IN
  HNeighbor(mid)
TypeOK == dist in [Vertices(G) X Vertices(H) -> Nat]
(* Invariant: the walk's cloud-marginals contract by alpha each
   full triple, and intra-cloud variance contracts by beta per H-step. *)```

**The spectral dictionary in action.** For an (n, d, \u03bb)-graph, the random walk mixes in O(log n / log(1/\u03bb)) steps: after t steps the total-variation distance to uniform is at most \u221an\u00b7\u03bb^t. For Ramanujan graphs \u03bb \u2248 2\u221a(d\u22121)/d, which for large d is approximately 2/\u221ad, giving the fastest possible mixing among d-regular graphs up to constants. This is why Ramanujan graphs give optimal extractors, optimal error-correcting codes (via the Sipser–Spielman construction), and optimal derandomization gadgets [1][3].

##6 Limitations

The theory, for all its depth, has sharp boundaries worth stating plainly.

1. **Non-bipartite Ramanujan graphs of every degree remain open.** The MSS interlacing method produces *bipartite* Ramanujan graphs of every degree, but the original problem \u2013 infinite families of (non-bipartite) d-regular Ramanujan graphs for every d \u2013 is still open; the smallest open case was d = 7 for years, and number theory only covers degrees one more than a prime power [2][4][8].
2. **Explicitness vs. existence.** The zig-zag product is fully explicit but not Ramanujan; LPS is Ramanujan but inexplicit in the proof-theoretic sense (relying on Deligne) and restricted in degree; MSS is Ramanujan for all degrees but only bipartite, and its polynomial-time versions are intricate [3][4][6]. No known construction is simultaneously elementary, fully explicit, Ramanujan, and available in every degree.
3. **The Alon–Boppana bound is one-sided and asymptotic.** It constrains only the *second* eigenvalue from below in the limit; it says nothing about \u03bbₙ (the most negative eigenvalue), about small graphs, or about irregular graphs, where the correct generalization involves the universal cover's spectral radius and the Feng–Li bound for biregular graphs [4].
4. **Spectral does not imply combinatorial optimality.** Ramanujan graphs are optimal for spectral expansion, but vertex expansion and edge expansion have their own extremal problems where Ramanujan graphs are not always the champions; Kahale's vertex-expansion bounds show the translation is lossy [1].
5. **Computational cost of certification.** Verifying Ramanujan-ness requires computing the full spectrum to precision o(1), which is polynomial but practically heavy for the million-vertex graphs used in applications; probabilistic certificates via the trace method are an active research direction.

##7 Conclusion

The story of spectral expanders is the story of a single number, 2\u221a(d\u22121), viewed from three directions. From below, the Alon–Boppana bound proves no infinite family of d-regular graphs can beat it, with the infinite regular tree as the extremal witness. From above, three generations of constructions approach it: the LPS graphs, which attain it through the full force of the Langlands program; the zig-zag product, which trades optimality for complete combinatorial elementariness and unlocks derandomization; and the interlacing-polynomial method, which attains it for bipartite graphs of every degree through the unexpected bridge of matching polynomials and real-rootedness. The 2024 results on random regular graphs being typically Ramanujan suggest the final chapter \u2013 explicit, elementary, non-bipartite Ramanujan graphs of every degree \u2013 may be closer than the decades of number-theoretic monopoly suggested [8]. Until then, the spectral dictionary continues to convert eigenvalue bounds into expansion, mixing, and pseudorandomness across mathematics and computer science.
