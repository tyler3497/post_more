# Probabilistic Programming: Sequential Monte Carlo Engines, Variational Inference Compilation, and Programmatic Model Composition

## Abstract

Probabilistic programming languages (PPLs) reconceive Bayesian inference as *program execution*: a generative model is an ordinary program whose random choices denote an unnormalized posterior measure, and an inference engine is an interpreter computing expectations under that measure [6]. This thesis unifies the three architectural pillars of the modern PPL stack. First, **sequential Monte Carlo (SMC) engines** read `observe` checkpoints as the intermediate targets of a particle filter, now on firm footing via the correctness proof for expressive untyped calculi [1], alongside data-driven proposals that slash particle budgets [2]. Second, **variational inference compilation** lowers a model program into a differentiable *guide* program and compiles the evidence lower bound into a stochastic optimization loop driven by reparameterization gradients and automatic differentiation [3,4]. Third, **programmatic model composition** uses algebraic effect handlers and monadic inference combinators to assemble models and inference strategies from reusable, inspectable parts [4,5]. We formalize each pillar, state the key theorems, survey empirical behavior, and chart the hybrid frontier of variational proposals inside sequential Monte Carlo engines.

## 1. Introduction

The central idea of probabilistic programming is disarmingly simple: a Bayesian model is a program, and Bayesian inference is what happens when that program is *executed* by a special-purpose interpreter [6]. Where an ordinary program maps inputs to outputs deterministically, a probabilistic program interleaves deterministic computation with two stochastic primitives — `sample`, which draws a value from a distribution, and `observe` (or `condition`), which constrains a drawn value to observed data and multiplies the execution's weight by the corresponding likelihood. The set of all possible executions, weighted by their likelihoods, *is* the posterior distribution. Inference is therefore not a separate mathematical activity bolted onto modeling; it is a semantics, an evaluation strategy, a way of running code.

This separation of *modeling* from *inference* is the field's founding bargain: the modeler writes a generative story — coin flips, topic mixtures, state-space dynamics — while the inference engineer supplies the engine. The same program can run under rejection sampling, sequential Monte Carlo, or stochastic variational inference without changing a line of model code, a vision Gordon and colleagues articulated as a software-engineering discipline in its own right [6].

History unfolded along two traditions: the *restricted* one (BUGS, Stan) limits models to fixed-dimensional differentiable densities and repays it with superbly tuned inference such as Hamiltonian Monte Carlo; the *universal* one (Church, Anglican, WebPPL, Birch) embraces Turing completeness — recursion, stochastic branching, unbounded random variables — where sampling-based engines dominate. A third wave, the *differentiable* PPLs led by Pyro [4], fuses the two: universal modeling embedded in PyTorch, with inference compiled into stochastic optimization over guide programs.

This thesis argues that three mechanisms now carry essentially the entire engineering load of universal probabilistic programming, and that understanding their interplay is understanding the field:

1. **Sequential Monte Carlo engines**, which turn the program's `observe` statements into the intermediate distributions of a particle filter, giving asymptotically exact, parallelizable inference with strong theoretical guarantees [1,2,5].
2. **Variational inference compilation**, which transforms the inference problem into stochastic optimization by synthesizing a differentiable guide program and differentiating through the evidence lower bound [3,4].
3. **Programmatic model composition**, which treats `sample` and `observe` as algebraic effects intercepted by composable handlers, so that models, conditioning, tracing, and inference strategies are all first-class program transformations [4,5].

Section 2 fixes notation and semantics. Section 3 describes our three-lens methodology. Section 4 develops each pillar in depth. Section 5 states the key theorems and surveys empirical evidence. Section 6 is candid about limitations, and Section 7 concludes with the open frontier.

## 2. Background

### Programs as measures

Formally, the meaning of a probabilistic program is a *measure* over its execution traces. A trace $x = (x_1, \dots, x_n)$ records the values drawn at each `sample` site; its unnormalized density is

$$\gamma(x) = \prod_{i=1}^{n} p_i(x_i \mid x_{<i}) \prod_{j=1}^{m} \ell_j(y_j \mid x),$$

where $p_i$ are the prior densities at sample sites and $\ell_j$ the likelihoods accumulated at the $m$ `observe` statements for fixed data $y = (y_1,\dots,y_m)$. The normalizing constant $Z = \int \gamma(x)\,dx$ is the *model evidence* (marginal likelihood), and the normalized measure $\pi(x) = \gamma(x)/Z$ is the *posterior*. Inference means computing expectations $\mathbb{E}_\pi[f]$ and, often, $Z$ itself. Everything in this thesis is a technique for approximating these integrals given only the ability to *run* the program forward [6].

Two structural facts shape all that follows. First, traces vary in length: stochastic branching makes the dimension of $x$ itself random, defeating fixed-parameter inference. Second, `observe` statements arrive in program order, inducing intermediate targets $\gamma_t$ that each extend the last — the foothold exploited by SMC [5].

### Two approximation paradigms

*Sampling* methods approximate $\pi$ by weighted particles: importance sampling draws $x^i \sim q$ and weights $w^i = \gamma(x^i)/q(x^i)$, so that $\sum_i w^i f(x^i)/\sum_i w^i \to \mathbb{E}_\pi[f]$. SMC iterates this idea along the intermediate targets $\gamma_t$. *Variational* methods instead posit a tractable family $q_\phi$ and minimize the Kullback–Leibler divergence $\mathrm{KL}(q_\phi \,\|\, \pi)$, equivalently maximizing the evidence lower bound

$$\mathcal{L}(\phi) = \mathbb{E}_{q_\phi}\!\left[\log \gamma(X) - \log q_\phi(X)\right] \le \log Z,$$

with equality iff $q_\phi = \pi$. Sampling is asymptotically exact but slow; variational inference is fast and GPU-friendly but biased by the guide family. The modern stack uses both, often in combination.

| Paradigm | Example systems | Model class | Characteristic inference |
|---|---|---|---|
| Restricted / differentiable | Stan, BUGS | Fixed-dimension densities | HMC, ADVI |
| Universal / sampling | Anglican, WebPPL, Birch | Turing-complete programs | SMC, particle MCMC |
| Universal / differentiable | Pyro [4], NumPyro, Gen | Programs + neural components | SVI [3], compiled guides |
| Trace-based SMC | Anglican [2], Birch [1] | Programs with `observe` checkpoints | Bootstrap / guided particle filters |

## 3. Methodology

We study the three pillars through three lenses, each matched to its native mathematics.

**The operational lens (SMC).** We view inference as small-step execution. A particle is a program continuation; `sample` forks continuations stochastically, `observe` multiplies weights, and an explicit `resample` term synchronizes the particle population. Correctness then means: the weighted particle system converges to the denotational posterior *regardless of where resampling is placed* — the theorem proved by Lundén, Borgström, and Broman [1]. We reconstruct their argument and its corollaries.

**The compilation lens (variational inference).** We view inference as program transformation: the model program $P$ is paired with a *guide* program $Q_\phi$ denoting $q_\phi$, and the ELBO becomes a stochastic objective compiled to a differentiable computation graph. Gradients flow via the reparameterization trick or score-function estimators, optimized by stochastic approximation [3]. We analyze which program constructs admit which gradient estimators, following Pyro's design [4].

**The algebraic lens (composition).** We view `sample` and `observe` as *algebraic effects* and inference strategies as *handlers* that intercept them. This makes composition literal function composition: conditioning, tracing, replaying, and blocking are handlers stacked in a programmable pipeline [4], and model fragments compose like ordinary functions [5].

Concretely, a hidden Markov model in a universal PPL reads:

```clojure
(defquery hmm [observations]
  (let [states
        (reduce (fn [states obs]
                  (let [s (sample (transition (peek states)))]
                    (observe (emission s) obs)
                    (conj states s)))
                [(sample (initial-dist))]
                observations)]
    states))
```

Each loop iteration is one SMC time step. A variational treatment instead compiles a guide proposing states from an inference network; a compositional treatment wraps the query in handlers — `condition`, `trace` — without touching model code. Our evaluation combines theorem reconstruction with published empirical results.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Sequential Monte Carlo Inference Engines

SMC attacks the sequence of intermediate targets $\pi_t \propto \gamma_t$ with a population of $N$ particles. The *bootstrap particle filter* recurses on weighted particles $\{(x_{t-1}^i, w_{t-1}^i)\}_{i=1}^N$ approximating $\pi_{t-1}$:

1. **Resample** ancestor indices $a_t^i \sim \mathrm{Categorical}(w_{t-1}^{1:N})$, resetting weights to $1/N$;
2. **Mutate** $x_t^i \sim p_t(\cdot \mid x_{t-1}^{a_t^i})$ by running the program forward through the next `sample` sites;
3. **Weight** $w_t^i \propto \ell_t(y_t \mid x_t^i)$ at the next `observe`.

The weighted system $\sum_i w_t^i \delta_{x_t^i} / \sum_i w_t^i$ approximates $\pi_t$. Resampling focuses computation on promising lineages but introduces *path degeneracy*: after many steps, all particles may share a single ancestor, collapsing diversity in early coordinates. The standard diagnostic is the effective sample size $\mathrm{ESS}_t = 1/\sum_i (\bar w_t^i)^2 \in [1, N]$, with adaptive resampling triggered when ESS falls below $N/2$.

A beautiful fact elevates SMC from heuristic to principled estimator: the product of average unnormalized weights,

$$\hat Z = \prod_{t=1}^{T} \left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} w_t^i\right),$$

is an *unbiased* estimator of the model evidence $Z$, for any $N \ge 1$. Model comparison via Bayes factors therefore comes almost for free from a filtering run.

| Resampling scheme | Variance introduced | Complexity |
|---|---|---|
| Multinomial | Highest | $O(N)$ |
| Stratified | Lower | $O(N)$ |
| Systematic | Lowest (typical) | $O(N)$ |
| Adaptive (ESS-triggered) | Data-dependent | $O(N)$ amortized |

The foundational question — does SMC stay correct when the *programmer* decides where `observe` checkpoints fall? — was settled by Lundén, Borgström, and Broman [1]: in an expressive untyped operational calculus (representative of WebPPL, Anglican, Birch) with explicit `resample` terms, *any placement* of resampling is correct under mild restrictions, yielding the law of large numbers and unbiased $\hat Z$ as corollaries. Implementers may thus place resampling by heuristic, annotation, or adaptive ESS triggers without endangering soundness.

Orthogonal to placement is the *proposal*. The bootstrap filter proposes from the prior, ignoring the upcoming observation — disastrous under informative likelihoods. Perov, Le, and Wood trained neural networks on previous inference runs to approximate the *optimal* proposal, showing in Anglican that data-driven proposals reach accurate posteriors with dramatically fewer particles [2]. Wood, van de Meent, and Mansinghka showed earlier that any valid reordering of `observe` statements induces a valid SMC target sequence, and pushing likelihoods forward improves statistical efficiency [5]: compiling the observation schedule is itself an inference optimization.

A Haskell sketch of the filter's essence:

```haskell
-- One bootstrap filter step: resample, mutate, weight
smcStep :: (s -> Dist s) -> (s -> Double) -> [Particle s] -> IO [Particle s]
smcStep trans likelihood ps = do
  anc <- resample (map weight ps)          -- ancestor indices
  xs  <- mapM (sample . trans . state)     -- mutate along program
          [ ps !! a | a <- anc ]
  let ws = map likelihood xs               -- weight at `observe`
  return (zipWith Particle xs ws)
```

### 4.2 Variational Inference Compilation

Where SMC *executes* the model with many particles, variational inference *compiles* it into an optimization problem. Given guide program $Q_\phi$ denoting $q_\phi$, the ELBO $\mathcal{L}(\phi) = \mathbb{E}_{q_\phi}[\log \gamma(X) - \log q_\phi(X)]$ is estimated by Monte Carlo and maximized by stochastic gradient ascent. The crux is the gradient estimator. Two dominate:

| Estimator | Requirements on $Q_\phi$ | Variance | Handles discrete latents |
|---|---|---|---|
| Score function (REINFORCE) | Score $\nabla_\phi \log q_\phi$ exists | High | Yes |
| Reparameterization | $X = g_\phi(\epsilon)$, $\epsilon \sim p_0$ fixed | Low | No |

The reparameterization trick rewrites sampling as a deterministic transform of fixed noise — e.g., $z = \mu_\phi + \sigma_\phi \odot \epsilon$, $\epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0,I)$ — so gradients flow through the sample path via automatic differentiation. The guide program is thus lowered to a differentiable graph: the ELBO gradient is backpropagation through sampled execution.

Hoffman, Blei, Wang, and Paisley scaled this to massive data with **stochastic variational inference (SVI)** [3]: partitioning variables into global $\beta$ and local $z_n$, they derived *natural* gradients — for exponential-family $q$, simply expected sufficient statistics minus current parameters — and optimized over data subsamples, analyzing 1.8M *New York Times* articles with LDA far beyond batch VI's reach [3]. Automatic differentiation variational inference (ADVI) went further, automatically mapping constrained variables to $\mathbb{R}^d$ and positing a Gaussian guide — inference with no hand-written guide at all.

Pyro [4] is the fullest realization of VI-as-compilation: models and guides are Python coroutines on PyTorch; `pyro.sample` statements in the guide declare the variational family *programmatically*, including amortized guides where an encoder network maps observations to local parameters. `Trace_ELBO` traces both executions, aligns sample sites by name, and assembles the ELBO term by term:

```python
import pyro, torch
import pyro.distributions as dist

def model(data):
    loc   = pyro.sample("loc", dist.Normal(0., 10.))
    scale = pyro.sample("scale", dist.LogNormal(0., 1.))
    with pyro.plate("obs", len(data)):
        pyro.sample("x", dist.Normal(loc, scale), obs=data)

def guide(data):
    q_loc   = pyro.param("q_loc", torch.tensor(0.))
    q_scale = pyro.param("q_scale", torch.tensor(1.),
                         constraint=dist.constraints.positive)
    pyro.sample("loc", dist.Normal(q_loc, torch.tensor(1.)))
    pyro.sample("scale", dist.LogNormal(torch.tensor(0.), q_scale))

svi = pyro.infer.SVI(model, guide,
                     pyro.optim.Adam({"lr": 1e-2}),
                     loss=pyro.infer.Trace_ELBO())
for _ in range(2000):
    svi.step(data)   # stochastic ELBO gradient step
```

The price of compilation is the *amortization gap* and family bias: no optimizer can find a posterior outside the guide family's reach, and a poorly chosen guide silently under-reports uncertainty — the failure mode variational practitioners must audit for.

### 4.3 Programmatic Model Composition

The third pillar observes that `sample` and `observe` are *effects*, and everything else — conditioning, tracing, inference — is a *handler*. In algebraic-effects terminology, a probabilistic program is a computation that *performs* operations `sample(dist)` and `observe(dist, value)`; a handler intercepts each operation and defines its meaning. Change the handler, change the inference, without changing the model.

Pyro's **Poutine** library [4] is the landmark implementation: a suite of composable effect handlers for probabilistic programs. The core handlers include:

- `trace` — records each site's distribution, value, and log-density;
- `replay` — re-executes reusing values from a previous trace (the workhorse of MCMC);
- `condition` — clamps sample sites to observed data, turning generation into conditioning;
- `block` / `unblock` — hides or exposes sites, enabling modular inference over submodels;
- `scale` — multiplies log-densities by a constant, implementing data subsampling and tempering;
- `enum` — enumerates discrete sites for exact marginalization inside variational objectives.

Because handlers compose by ordinary function composition, an inference algorithm becomes a *pipeline of program transformations*: `block(model)` |> `condition(data)` |> `trace` |> `Trace_ELBO`. New strategies are new stacks, prototyped in user code rather than in the PPL's runtime. This is composition at the *inference* level.

At the *model* level, composition is equally programmatic: hierarchies are function calls, mixtures are higher-order combinators, time series are folds over transition kernels. Wood and colleagues' `observe`-reordering result [5] is itself a *program transformation* — an optimizing compiler pass over model code. Together, handlers and combinators deliver the field's promise: models from tested parts, inference from reusable strategies, each reasoned about independently [6].

### 4.4 The Hybrid Frontier: Inference Combinators

The pillars are stages of one pipeline. Current work *compiles* variational proposals into SMC kernels: a particle filter proposing from an SVI-trained guide $q_\phi$ optimizes the importance-weighted bound $\mathcal{L}_K = \mathbb{E}[\log \frac{1}{K}\sum_k w^k]$, tightened monotonically in $K$; data-driven proposals [2] are an early instance. Conversely, SMC's unbiased $\hat Z$ supplies gradients that train deep generative guides. The future PPL engine is a *compiler* taking a model program, a guide sketch, and a handler stack, and emitting a hybrid inference executable — sampling where exactness matters, optimization where scale demands it.

## 5. Empirical Results and Proofs

We now state the two load-bearing theorems precisely.

> **Theorem 1 (SMC law of large numbers for PPLs; after Lundén, Borgström, and Broman [1]).** In the untyped PPL calculus with explicit `resample` terms, let $\hat\pi_t^N = \sum_{i=1}^N \bar w_t^i \delta_{x_t^i}$ be the weighted particle approximation after $t$ checkpoints with $N$ particles. Under mild integrability restrictions, for *any* placement of `resample` terms and any bounded measurable test function $f$,
>
> $$\hat\pi_t^N(f) \xrightarrow[N\to\infty]{a.s.} \pi_t(f).$$
>
> *Proof sketch.* By induction over the operational semantics: `sample` extends traces with the correct kernel, `observe` multiplies by the correct potential, and `resample` preserves the expected empirical measure. The classical SMC law of large numbers applies to the induced Feynman–Kac flow; the novelty is showing resampling *commutes* with the remaining continuation, so placement cannot bias the limit. ∎

> **Theorem 2 (Unbiased model evidence).** The SMC estimator $\hat Z = \prod_{t=1}^T \big(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N w_t^i\big)$ satisfies $\mathbb{E}[\hat Z] = Z$ for every $N \ge 1$, in the PPL calculus of [1].
>
> *Proof sketch.* Each factor $\frac{1}{N}\sum_i w_t^i$ is an unbiased estimator of the ratio $Z_t/Z_{t-1}$ of successive normalizing constants, conditional on the previous generation; telescoping the product and taking expectations yields $Z_T/Z_0 = Z$. ∎

Empirically, the pillars deliver at scale. Hoffman's SVI [3] on 1.8M *New York Times* articles reached higher held-out likelihood than batch VI in a fraction of the wall-clock time, with the nonparametric variant discovering topic cardinality from data. Data-driven proposals in Anglican [2] cut particle counts for accurate nonparametric posteriors by *orders of magnitude* — overnight runs become interactive ones. Pyro's compiled ELBO [4] routinely optimizes guides with millions of parameters (deep Markov models, VAEs) on GPUs, a regime particle methods cannot reach.

| Engine | Cost per effective sample | Scales to | Guarantee |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bootstrap SMC | $O(NT)$ | Long sequences, moderate dimension | Asymptotically exact [1] |
| Guided / data-driven SMC | $O(NT)$ + proposal training | Informative likelihoods | Asymptotically exact [2] |
| SVI (reparameterized) | $O(\text{batch})$ per step | Millions of parameters, huge data | Biased by guide family [3] |
| Compiled ELBO (Pyro) | $O(\text{batch})$ per step, GPU | Deep generative models | Biased; tightenable via $K$ [4] |

## 6. Limitations

**SMC** suffers *weight degeneracy* in high dimensions: weight variance grows exponentially in the number of coupled latents, collapsing naive filters; path degeneracy erodes smoothing even when filtering succeeds. And while resample placement is immaterial to *correctness* [1], it is decisive for *efficiency* — a bad schedule wastes the whole particle budget.

**Variational compilation** is only as good as its guide: mean-field guides miss correlations, amortized guides suffer an amortization gap, and discrete latents defeat reparameterization, forcing high-variance score-function gradients. Silent underestimation of uncertainty remains the characteristic failure — dangerous because the ELBO keeps improving while the approximation stays wrong.

**Composition** via handlers is *unsound by default*: stacking `replay` inside `condition` inside `enum` can silently target a different model, and host type systems do not track active handlers. The hybrid frontier inherits all three failure modes at once; principled diagnostics for composed hybrid engines remain an open problem.

## 7. Conclusion

Probabilistic programming has matured from a clever trick — *run the model, weight the runs* — into a layered compilation stack. SMC engines give asymptotically exact inference over universal programs, now on firm footing with placement-independent correctness [1] and learned proposals taming particle budgets [2]. Variational compilation gives scale, lowering models to differentiable guides optimized with deep learning's full machinery [3,4]. Composition via handlers gives modularity, turning models and strategies into composable program transformations [4,5,6].

The frontier is their synthesis: compilers accepting a model program and emitting hybrid inference executables with end-to-end guarantees. The theorems of Section 5 are the specification; the limitations of Section 6 are the test suite. When that compiler exists, the founding bargain will be kept in full: the modeler writes the story, and the machine does the mathematics.
