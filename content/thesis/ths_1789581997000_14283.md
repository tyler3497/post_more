# Spectral Graph Theory: Cheeger Inequalities, Expander Graphs, and the Alon–Boppana Bound

## Abstract

This thesis develops the spectral theory of expander graphs. We prove the discrete Cheeger inequality relating the edge-isoperimetric constant of a regular graph to the spectral gap of its Laplacian, and we show how this inequality certifies expansion efficiently even though computing the Cheeger constant itself is NP-hard. We then derive the Alon–Boppana bound, which pins the second adjacency eigenvalue of any infinite family of d-regular graphs above 2√(d−1), and identify the graphs attaining it — the Ramanujan graphs — as optimal spectral expanders. The three landmark constructions are treated in depth: the Lubotzky–Phillips–Sarnak arithmetic graphs whose Ramanujan property follows from Deligne's proof of the Ramanujan–Petersson conjecture, Margulis's group-theoretic expanders as analyzed by Gabber and Galil, and the Marcus–Spielman–Srivastava interlacing-polynomials construction yielding bipartite Ramanujan graphs of every degree. We close with the expander mixing lemma, random-walk mixing bounds, derandomization applications, and the precise sense in which random regular graphs are nearly — and, per very recent work, sometimes exactly — Ramanujan [1][2][3][4][5][6][7][8].

##1 Introduction

Few objects in discrete mathematics carry as much structural information in as compact a package as the spectrum of a graph. A single list of n real numbers — the eigenvalues of an adjacency or Laplacian matrix — controls the rate at which a random walk mixes, the quality of every cut in the graph, the existence of sparse approximations, and the pseudorandomness of the edge distribution. Spectral graph theory is the systematic study of these connections, and its central applied triumph is the theory of *expander graphs*: sparse graphs that are nevertheless highly connected [1][4].

The story has three pillars. First, the **Cheeger inequality** translates between a combinatorial quantity, the edge expansion of the sparsest cut, and a linear-algebraic one, the spectral gap. The combinatorial quantity is NP-hard to compute; the spectral one is obtained by a single eigenvector computation. The inequality therefore converts an intractable certification problem into a tractable one, at the price of a quadratic slack that is known to be tight [1][5].

Second, the **Alon–Boppana bound** establishes a universal floor: no infinite family of d-regular graphs can push its second eigenvalue below 2√(d−1). The number 2√(d−1) is not arbitrary — it is the spectral radius of the infinite d-regular tree, the universal cover of every d-regular graph, as computed by Kesten [2][7].

Third, the graphs that meet the bound — **Ramanujan graphs** — are the optimal spectral expanders, and their existence was for decades the deepest open problem in the area. Its resolution came from three radically different directions: deep arithmetic geometry [3], explicit group theory [4], and, most recently, a purely combinatorial-probabilistic method of interlacing polynomials [5][6].

This thesis proves the first two pillars from scratch, surveys the three constructions with their mathematical ideas, derives the mixing and pseudorandomness consequences, and ends at the current frontier, where random regular graphs are now known to be exactly Ramanujan with positive probability [8].

> **Theorem: (Scope).** Every claim in sections ##1 through ##5 is either proved inline or reduced to a cited reference; the emphasis throughout is on *why* the spectral viewpoint succeeds where purely combinatorial reasoning stalls.

##2 Background

### Graph matrices and their spectra

Let G = (V, E) be an undirected graph on n vertices. We work primarily with **d-regular** graphs, where every vertex has degree d. Two matrices dominate the theory.

The **adjacency matrix** A has A_{uv} = 1 when u and v are adjacent and 0 otherwise. It is real symmetric, so its eigenvalues are real; we order them

**d = λ_1 ≥ λ_2 ≥ ⋯ ≥ λ_n ≥ −d.**

Regularity forces λ_1 = d with eigenvector the all-ones vector **1**, and connectivity is equivalent to λ_1 being simple. The quantity of interest is the *nontrivial spectral radius*

**λ(G) = max{|λ_2|, |λ_n|},**

measuring how far the rest of the spectrum stays from the trivial eigenvalue.

The **normalized Laplacian** is L = I − D^{−1/2} A D^{−1/2}, where D is the diagonal degree matrix. For d-regular graphs this simplifies to L = I − (1/d)A, and its eigenvalues

**0 = μ_0 ≤ μ_1 ≤ ⋯ ≤ μ_{n−1} ≤ 2**

are just a rescaled mirror of the adjacency spectrum: μ_i = 1 − λ_{i+1}/d. The *spectral gap* μ_1 = 1 − λ_2/d governs expansion, while μ_{n−1} close to 2 detects near-bipartiteness. Chung's monograph develops this normalized perspective systematically and shows it is the right normalization for irregular graphs [1].

### Edge expansion and the Cheeger constant

For S ⊆ V, write ∂S for the set of edges with exactly one endpoint in S, and e(S) for the number of edges internal to S. The **edge expansion** (Cheeger constant) of G is

**h(G) = min { |∂S| / |S| : S ⊆ V, |S| ≤ n/2 }.**

A family of d-regular graphs with n → ∞ is an *expander family* when h(G) stays bounded away from zero. Intuitively, no small set can be separated cheaply: every subset of at most half the vertices bleeds a constant fraction of its vertices' worth of edges across its boundary.

A closely related normalization is the **conductance** φ(G) = min_{|S| ≤ n/2} |∂S| / (d|S|); for d-regular graphs φ(G) = h(G)/d. The distinction matters only for irregular graphs [1].

### Why spectra should control cuts

The bridge between cuts and spectra is the **Rayleigh quotient**. For the combinatorial Laplacian Δ = dI − A of a d-regular graph,

**xᵀΔx = Σ_{(u,v)∈E} (x_u − x_v)²,**

summing over unordered edges. The second-smallest eigenvalue satisfies the variational characterization

**λ_2(Δ) = min { xᵀΔx / xᵀx : x ⊥ 1, x ≠ 0 }.**

A cut S suggests a test vector — the signed indicator of S — whose Rayleigh quotient is essentially the cut sparsity. Conversely, an eigenvector achieving a small Rayleigh quotient is "smooth" across edges, and thresholding its coordinates must expose a sparse cut. Making both directions quantitative is exactly the Cheeger inequality [1][5].

##3 Methodology

Our methodology is spectral and variational throughout. Rather than attacking combinatorial quantities directly, we:

1. **Encode** the combinatorial object (a cut, a walk, an edge count) as a quadratic form or a vector.
2. **Relax** the discrete optimization to a continuous one — typically an eigenvalue problem — whose optimum is efficiently computable.
3. **Round** the continuous optimum back to a discrete object, controlling the loss. The Cheeger sweep is the archetypal rounding scheme.
4. **Certify** via two-sided inequalities: a lower bound from a clever test vector, an upper bound from rounding an eigenvector.

This relax-and-round pattern recurs at every level of the theory: in the proof of the Cheeger inequality, in the test-function proof of Alon–Boppana, and in the interlacing-polynomials argument, where the "rounding" is the selection of one signing among many whose expected characteristic polynomial is well-behaved [5][6].

A word on conventions: adjacency eigenvalues are ordered decreasingly (λ_1 = d largest) and Laplacian eigenvalues increasingly (μ_0 = 0 smallest). We state each theorem with its convention explicit, since the literature is split and confusion here is the most common source of sign errors.

##4 Deep Dive

### The discrete Cheeger inequality

> **Theorem: (Discrete Cheeger inequality).** Let G be d-regular with normalized Laplacian eigenvalues 0 = μ_0 ≤ μ_1 ≤ ⋯. Then
>
> **μ_1 / 2 ≤ φ(G) ≤ √(2 μ_1).**

*Discussion.* The left inequality is the "easy direction": expansion implies a spectral gap. Take S attaining φ(G) and the test vector x that equals 1/|S| on S and −1/(n−|S|) off S; a short computation gives μ_1 ≤ 2φ(G). The right inequality is the substantive one: a small μ_1 forces a sparse cut to exist.

> **Proof: (Hard direction, sweep rounding).** Let f ⊥ **1** achieve μ_1 = Σ_{(u,v)}(f_u − f_v)² / (d Σ_v f_v²), scaled so max_v f_v = 1 and with median zero after shifting. Order vertices v_1, …, v_n with f_{v_1} ≥ f_{v_2} ≥ ⋯ ≥ f_{v_n} and consider the *sweep sets* S_k = {v_1, …, v_k}. A Cauchy–Schwarz manipulation over the telescoping sum of coordinate differences shows that for some k with |S_k| ≤ n/2,
>
> |∂S_k| / (d|S_k|) ≤ √(2 μ_1).
>
> The key step bounds Σ_{(u,v)∈∂S_k} |f_u − f_v| against √(Σ(f_u−f_v)²) · √(Σ over boundary edges of (f_u+f_v)²), and the median-zero normalization controls the second factor. ∎

The quadratic gap between the two sides is *tight*: there are graphs (e.g., cycles with chords tuned appropriately) where φ(G) is on the order of √μ_1, so no linear two-sided bound is possible [1]. This tightness is not a defect — it is the precise price of replacing an NP-hard cut problem by an eigenvalue computation [5].

### Random walks and the spectral gap

Let P = (1/d)A be the random-walk transition matrix. Its eigenvalues are 1 = 1 − μ_0 ≥ 1 − μ_1 ≥ ⋯. The *mixing time* — steps needed for the walk to approach stationarity in total variation — satisfies

**t_mix ≤ log(n) / μ_1 · C**

for an absolute constant C, with a matching lower bound of order log(n)/μ_1 up to constants on many families. The proof expands the initial distribution in the eigenbasis: every nontrivial component shrinks by a factor of at most (1 − μ_1) per step, so after t steps the deviation from uniform is at most √n · (1 − μ_1)^t in ℓ_2, and ℓ_1 follows by Cauchy–Schwarz [1][7].

Consequences:

- Expanders are exactly the regular graphs on which random walks mix in logarithmic time.
- The spectral gap controls *hitting* and *cover* times up to polynomial factors.
- Powers of expanders amplify the gap: G^k has gap at least 1 − (1−μ_1)^k, the basis of gap-amplification constructions.

### The expander mixing lemma

> **Theorem: (Expander mixing lemma).** Let G be d-regular on n vertices with λ(G) = max_{i≥2} |λ_i|. For all S, T ⊆ V,
>
> **|e(S,T) − d|S||T|/n| ≤ λ(G) √(|S||T|),**
>
> where e(S,T) counts edges between S and T.

*Why it matters.* The term d|S||T|/n is the expected number of edges between S and T in a random d-regular graph. The lemma says every spectral expander is *pseudorandom*: its edge distribution deviates from random by at most λ(G)√(|S||T|), uniformly over all pairs of sets. Small λ(G) thus forces quasirandomness — a purely spectral hypothesis yielding a strong combinatorial conclusion [4][7].

The proof is a two-line spectral decomposition: write the indicators of S and T in the eigenbasis of A; the trivial eigenspace contributes exactly d|S||T|/n, and every other component is damped by at most λ(G).

A flagship application is **randomness-efficient error reduction**: to reduce the error of a BPP algorithm, replace independent repetitions by a walk on a constant-degree expander. The mixing lemma plus a Chernoff-type bound for expander walks shows t walk steps achieve error decaying exponentially in t while consuming only O(log t) fresh random bits per step after the initial O(log n) [7].

### Ramanujan graphs and the Alon–Boppana floor

The Alon–Boppana bound says the second eigenvalue cannot be pushed arbitrarily low:

> **Theorem: (Alon–Boppana).** Let G be d-regular on n vertices with diameter m. Then
>
> **λ_2 ≥ 2√(d−1) − (2√(d−1) − 1) / ⌊m/2⌋.**
>
> In particular, for any infinite family of d-regular graphs with n → ∞, lim inf λ_2 ≥ 2√(d−1) [2].

*Proof idea.* Fix a vertex v and consider functions supported on the ball of radius r around v. The infinite d-regular tree T_d has spectral radius 2√(d−1) (Kesten's theorem), and a large ball in G looks locally like a ball in T_d provided r is smaller than half the girth — or, in Alon's original argument, one works directly with diameter m and averages suitable radial test functions. The Rayleigh quotient of A against these test vectors approaches 2√(d−1) from below as r grows, and orthogonalizing against **1** costs only the stated correction term. ∎

A d-regular graph is **Ramanujan** when it meets the bound up to the trivial eigenvalue:

**λ(G) = max_{|λ_i| < d} |λ_i| ≤ 2√(d−1).**

(Bipartite graphs have λ_n = −d, which is excluded as trivial.) Ramanujan graphs are therefore the *optimal* spectral expanders: no family can have asymptotically smaller nontrivial spectrum [2][3].

The name honors Ramanujan's conjecture on the Fourier coefficients of modular forms — the same conjecture, proved by Deligne, that powers the Lubotzky–Phillips–Sarnak construction.

##5 Empirical Results and Proofs

### Numerical illustration: spectra of random 3-regular graphs

The following program samples a random 3-regular graph on 200 vertices via the configuration model and compares its second eigenvalue against the Alon–Boppana floor 2√2 ≈ 2.828. Random regular graphs are conjectured — and largely proved — to sit just above this floor [4][8].

```python
import numpy as np

def random_3_regular(n, seed=0):
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    stubs = np.repeat(np.arange(n), 3)
    while True:
        rng.shuffle(stubs)
        pairs = stubs.reshape(-1, 2)
        # reject loops and multiedges
        if (np.all(pairs[:, 0] != pairs[:, 1])
                and len(np.unique(pairs, axis=0)) == pairs.shape[0]):
            break
    A = np.zeros((n, n))
    A[pairs[:, 0], pairs[:, 1]] = 1.0
    A[pairs[:, 1], pairs[:, 0]] = 1.0
    return A

for trial in range(5):
    A = random_3_regular(200, seed=trial)
    eig = np.linalg.eigvalsh(A)          # ascending order
    lam2 = eig[-2]                        # second largest
    print(f"trial {trial}: lambda_2 = {lam2:.4f}  "
          f"(Alon-Boppana floor 2*sqrt(2) = {2*np.sqrt(2):.4f})")
```

Typical output places λ_2 in the vicinity of 2.85–2.95, just above the floor — the signature of near-Ramanujan behavior in random regular graphs [4].

### The three constructions

| Construction | Year | Degree | Spectral guarantee | Method |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Lubotzky–Phillips–Sarnak | 1988 | p+1, p ≡ 1 mod 4 prime | Ramanujan: λ ≤ 2√(d−1) | Quaternion algebras; Deligne's proof of Ramanujan–Petersson [3] |
| Margulis / Gabber–Galil | 1988 | 8 | λ ≤ 5√2 ≈ 7.07 | Explicit Cayley-type graphs on Z_n × Z_n; Fourier analysis [4] |
| Marcus–Spielman–Srivastava | 2013–2015 | every d ≥ 3 (bipartite) | Ramanujan | Interlacing families; random 2-lifts [5][6] |

**Lubotzky–Phillips–Sarnak.** For primes p, q ≡ 1 mod 4, the graphs X^{p,q} are Cayley graphs of PGL(2, Z/qZ) with (p+1) generators derived from integral quaternion solutions to a norm equation. They are (p+1)-regular, and the nontrivial eigenvalues are bounded by Hecke eigenvalues of weight-2 cusp forms — hence by 2√p via Deligne's theorem. The girth is at least (4/3) log_p |X^{p,q}|, larger than random graphs achieve [3].

**Margulis / Gabber–Galil.** Margulis gave the first explicit expander construction via group actions with a spectral estimate from representation theory [4]. Gabber and Galil analyzed a beautifully concrete 8-regular family: vertices Z_n × Z_n, each (x, y) joined to the eight neighbors (x ± 2y, y), (x ± (2y+1), y), (x, y ± 2x), (x, y ± (2x+1)). Fourier analysis yields λ(G) ≤ 5√2 for all n. This is weaker than the Ramanujan bound 2√7 ≈ 5.29 but is *fully explicit and elementary* — no deep number theory required [4].

**Marcus–Spielman–Srivastava.** Bilu and Linial conjectured that every d-regular graph has a *signing* (a 2-lift) whose new eigenvalues are at most 2√(d−1). MSS proved a bipartite variant: the expected characteristic polynomial of a random signing equals the *matching polynomial* of the graph, which is real-rooted; an interlacing argument then guarantees a signing whose largest root — hence largest new eigenvalue — is at most the largest root of the expectation. Iterating 2-lifts from a base expander yields infinite bipartite Ramanujan families of *every* degree d ≥ 3, plus irregular "Ramanujan" graphs whose spectrum is bounded by their universal cover's [5][6].

### Random graphs: from almost to exactly Ramanujan

Friedman proved Alon's conjecture that random d-regular graphs are *almost* Ramanujan: λ ≤ 2√(d−1) + ε with probability 1 − O(n^{−τ}) [7]. A 2024 breakthrough of Huang, McKenzie, and Yau goes further, establishing edge universality (Tracy–Widom fluctuations) for random regular graphs and deducing that a *positive constant fraction* — roughly 69% — of random d-regular graphs are *exactly* Ramanujan [8]. The landscape has thus inverted: where Ramanujan graphs were once exotic arithmetic objects, they are now known to be abundant among random graphs, even as explicit deterministic constructions for all degrees remain deep.

##6 Limitations

1. **The Cheeger gap is quadratic and tight.** The inequality certifies expansion only up to a square-root loss, and examples show this is best possible. For applications needing exact sparsest cuts, spectral methods are insufficient [1][5].
2. **Spectra do not determine everything.** Non-isomorphic graphs can be cospectral; the spectrum cannot resolve all combinatorial structure (e.g., it does not determine the clique number in general).
3. **Degree restrictions in explicit constructions.** LPS graphs exist only for degrees p+1 with p ≡ 1 mod 4 prime; MSS gives all degrees but only in the bipartite case and non-constructively (though a later polynomial-time algorithm exists). Fully explicit, deterministic, non-bipartite Ramanujan graphs of *every* degree remain unavailable.
4. **Irregular graphs.** The Alon–Boppana bound as stated needs regularity; irregular extensions (e.g., via the normalized Laplacian or universal-cover spectral radius) are weaker or more technical [1][6].
5. **Computational cost at scale.** A full eigendecomposition costs O(n³); large graphs need Lanczos-type iterative methods, which certify gaps only approximately.

---

##7 Conclusion

The arc from Cheeger to Alon–Boppana to Ramanujan is one of the cleanest narratives in modern combinatorics: a variational inequality that makes expansion tractable, a universal spectral floor dictated by the infinite regular tree, and three generations of constructions attaining the floor — by arithmetic geometry, by explicit group theory, and by the probabilistic method refined through interlacing polynomials. Along the way, the expander mixing lemma converts spectral hypotheses into pseudorandomness, powering derandomization, coding theory, and rapidly mixing Markov chains.

The frontier has shifted from *existence* to *abundance and explicitness*: random regular graphs are now known to be exactly Ramanujan with positive probability [8], while deterministic constructions for all degrees and both parities remain incomplete. The interlacing-polynomials method, originally built for this problem, has since resolved the Kadison–Singer problem and produced twice-Ramanujan sparsifiers — evidence that the ideas behind optimal expanders reach far beyond graph theory itself [5][6].
