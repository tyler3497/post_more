# Byzantine-Robust Federated Learning: Robust Aggregation, Certification, and the Price of Heterogeneity

## Abstract

Federated learning (FL) trains a shared model from data that never leaves the client devices, but its distributed character exposes the aggregation step to *Byzantine* clients that may transmit arbitrarily corrupted updates. This thesis develops the theory and practice of Byzantine-robust federated learning. We formalize the threat model and the resilience criteria — $(\alpha, f)$-Byzantine resilience, $(\delta_{\max}, \zeta)$-robustness, and certified error bounds — and analyze the classical robust aggregators: Krum, geometric median, coordinate-wise trimmed mean and median, Bulyan, and their momentum-augmented variants. We then confront the central tension of the field: client data are non-IID, so honest updates are already heterogeneous, and every robust aggregator must distinguish adversarial corruption from legitimate distributional skew. We prove a lower bound showing that no permutation-invariant aggregator can escape an $\Omega(\zeta)$ error under bounded heterogeneity, quantify the impossibility of Byzantine robustness under arbitrary heterogeneity, and survey the algorithmic responses — variance reduction, bucketing, centered clipping, and redundancy-based certification. Finally we evaluate the computational and communication costs of robust aggregation and chart a research program toward certified, heterogeneity-aware robustness.

## 1. Introduction

Federated learning has moved from a research curiosity to the substrate of production machine learning: smartphone keyboards, on-device speech models, and cross-silo hospital networks all train models without centralizing raw data [1]. The canonical algorithm, **FedAvg** [2], alternates local stochastic gradient descent on client devices with a simple server-side averaging step. This averaging step is the Achilles' heel of the entire architecture. A single client that transmits a corrupted gradient can, through naive averaging, drag the global model arbitrarily far from the optimum — and in an open or semi-trusted federation, corrupted updates are not merely a theoretical concern. Devices may be compromised, data pipelines may be poisoned, and incentives may reward sabotage.

The *Byzantine* threat model, borrowed from distributed computing [3], grants the adversary complete control over up to $f$ of the $n$ clients: Byzantine clients may collude, know the honest updates, and transmit arbitrary vectors chosen with full knowledge of the aggregation rule. The goal of Byzantine-robust federated learning is to guarantee convergence — at approximately the rate and to approximately the accuracy of honest training — despite such adversaries.

This thesis is organized as follows. Section 2 formalizes the optimization problem and the threat model, introducing the resilience criteria used throughout. Section 3 presents the core aggregation methodology: a taxonomy of robust aggregators and the proof techniques that certify them. Section 4 is the analytical heart of the thesis: the interaction between robustness and **data heterogeneity** — the fact that honest clients hold non-identical distributions — including impossibility results and the algorithmic responses they demand. Section 5 evaluates the empirical and computational cost of robustness. Sections 6 and 7 discuss limitations and conclusions.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 Federated optimization

We consider the standard distributed empirical risk minimization problem

$$\min_{w \in \mathbb{R}^d} F(w) := \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} F_i(w), \qquad F_i(w) := \mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{D}_i}[\ell(w; x)],$$

where client $i$ holds distribution $\mathcal{D}_i$ and $n$ is the number of clients. In the **IID** setting, $\mathcal{D}_i = \mathcal{D}$ for all $i$; in the **non-IID** (heterogeneous) setting, the distributions differ. Heterogeneity is typically quantified by the *gradient dissimilarity* bound [4]:

$$\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \lVert \nabla F_i(w) - \nabla F(w) \rVert^2 \le \zeta^2 \quad \forall w, \tag{1}$$

where $\zeta$ measures how far honest gradients can deviate from the mean. We will see that $\zeta$ is the central parameter of the entire Byzantine-robustness theory.

### 2.2 The Byzantine threat model

In each communication round, every client $i$ computes a local update $g_i \in \mathbb{R}^d$ (typically a stochastic gradient or a model delta). A set $\mathcal{B} \subset [n]$ of at most $f$ clients is *Byzantine*: their transmitted vectors are arbitrary, possibly chosen adversarially after observing the honest updates and the aggregation rule. The server applies an aggregation rule $\mathcal{A}: \mathbb{R}^{d \times n} \to \mathbb{R}^d$ and updates $w_{t+1} = w_t - \eta \, \mathcal{A}(g_1, \dots, g_n)$. The adversary's objective is to maximize $\lVert w_T - w^\star \rVert$ or to steer the model toward a targeted behavior.

> **Threat model note:** Byzantine faults subsume data poisoning, model poisoning, and arbitrary crash behavior. Defenses certified against Byzantine adversaries therefore automatically cover weaker attacks such as label flipping [5].

### 2.3 Resilience criteria

Three formalizations of robustness dominate the literature:

1. **$(\alpha, f)$-Byzantine resilience** [6]: the aggregator output must lie within an $\alpha$-dependent neighborhood of the true gradient: $\lVert \mathbb{E}[\mathcal{A}] - \bar{g} \rVert^2 \le \alpha^2 \lVert \bar{g} \rVert^2$ for the honest mean $\bar{g}$, and the output's second moment must be controlled by the honest variance.
2. **$(\delta_{\max}, \zeta)$-robustness** [7]: a criterion designed for heterogeneous data, requiring $\lVert \mathcal{A}(g_1,\dots,g_n) - \bar{g} \rVert^2 \le \delta_{\max} \cdot \frac{1}{n-f}\sum_{i \notin \mathcal{B}} \lVert g_i - \bar{g} \rVert^2$, where $\delta_{\max}$ depends on $f/n$ and $\zeta$ bounds heterogeneity.
3. **Certified error bounds**: finite-time convergence guarantees of the form $\mathbb{E}[F(w_T)] - F(w^\star) \le \mathcal{O}(1/\sqrt{T}) + \mathcal{E}(f, n, \zeta)$, making the irreducible error term $\mathcal{E}$ explicit.

We adopt the view that a robustness claim is meaningful only when accompanied by an explicit, finite $\mathcal{E}$ — the *price* the federation pays for tolerating $f$ adversaries under heterogeneity $\zeta$.
## 3. Methodology: Robust Aggregators

The methodological core of Byzantine-robust FL is the replacement of the vulnerable mean with a **robust aggregator** $\mathcal{A}$ that is provably insensitive to up to $f$ arbitrary inputs. We survey the principal families.

### 3.1 Distance-based selection: Krum

**Krum** [6] selects a single honest update rather than averaging. For each $i$, it computes the sum of squared distances to its $n-f-2$ nearest neighbors:

$$s(i) := \sum_{j \in \mathcal{N}_i} \lVert g_i - g_j \rVert^2,$$

where $\mathcal{N}_i$ is the set of $n-f-2$ closest vectors to $g_i$. Krum returns $\arg\min_i s(i)$ — the update most consistent with the majority. Multi-Krum averages the $m$ lowest-scoring updates for stability.

Krum was the first aggregator with a rigorous $(\alpha, f)$-resilience proof, requiring $2f + 2 < n$. Its weakness is **variance**: by selecting one update, it discards the variance-reduction benefit of averaging, and under heterogeneity its chosen update may be an honest but unrepresentative outlier. The computational cost is $\mathcal{O}(n^2 d)$ per round, dominated by pairwise distances.

### 3.2 Geometric median and its relatives

The **geometric median** minimizes the sum of Euclidean distances,

$$\mathcal{A}_{\text{GM}} := \arg\min_{v} \sum_{i=1}^{n} \lVert v - g_i \rVert,$$

computed approximately via the Weiszfeld algorithm. It enjoys a breakdown point of $1/2$ and dimension-free concentration properties [8]. Variants include:

- **Coordinate-wise median / trimmed mean**: robust along each coordinate independently; cheap ($\mathcal{O}(nd)$) but ignore cross-coordinate correlations, and their guarantees degrade in high dimension unless gradients are sub-Gaussian per coordinate [9].
- **Bulyan** [10]: a meta-aggregator that first applies Krum-like selection to discard $2f$ candidates, then applies coordinate-wise trimmed mean to the survivors — combining selection and averaging.
- **Centered clipping** [7]: iteratively clips updates toward their running mean, $v \leftarrow v + \frac{1}{n}\sum_i (g_i - v)\min(1, \tau/\lVert g_i - v\rVert)$, converging to a robust fixed point.

The following table summarizes the landscape:

| Aggregator | Breakdown $f/n$ | Cost | IID guarantee | Heterogeneous guarantee |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Krum / Multi-Krum | $< 1/2$ | $\mathcal{O}(n^2 d)$ | $(\alpha,f)$-resilience [6] | Weak (variance) |
| Geometric median | $< 1/2$ | $\mathcal{O}(nd \cdot \text{iter})$ | Dimension-free [8] | Needs bucketing [7] |
| Coord. trimmed mean | $< 1/2$ | $\mathcal{O}(nd)$ | Sub-Gaussian coords [9] | Bounded dissimilarity [4] |
| Bulyan | $< 1/4$ | $\mathcal{O}(n^2 d)$ | Stronger selection [10] | Limited |
| Centered clipping | $< 1/2$ | $\mathcal{O}(nd \cdot \text{iter})$ | $(\delta_{\max},\zeta)$ [7] | Best-in-class |

### 3.3 Momentum and variance reduction

A critical methodological advance was the observation that **worker-side momentum** dramatically improves the signal-to-noise ratio for robust aggregation [5]. If each honest client maintains $m_i^{t+1} = \beta m_i^t + (1-\beta) g_i^t$, the momentum vectors concentrate around the true gradient much more tightly than raw stochastic gradients, shrinking the honest variance that adversaries exploit. Combining momentum with any of the above aggregators (e.g., Krum + momentum) yields state-of-the-art practical robustness.

> **Theorem (informal, Karimireddy et al. [5]):** Under bounded variance and $f < n/2$, momentum-augmented robust aggregation converges for non-convex objectives at rate $\mathcal{O}(1/\sqrt{T})$ up to an error floor scaling with $f/n$ times the honest variance.

```python
def byzantine_robust_round(server_model, clients, f, agg="krum", beta=0.9):
    """One Byzantine-robust federated round with worker momentum."""
    updates, momenta = [], []
    for c in clients:                       # c may be Byzantine
        g = c.local_gradient(server_model)  # arbitrary if Byzantine
        m = beta * c.momentum + (1 - beta) * g
        c.momentum = m
        updates.append(m)
```

## 4. Deep Dive: Heterogeneity, Impossibility, and Responses

### 4.1 The heterogeneity-robustness tension

The fundamental difficulty of Byzantine-robust FL is philosophical before it is technical: **robust statistics assumes honest data are homogeneous**. Every outlier-rejection mechanism — distance-based, rank-based, or clipping-based — treats deviant updates as suspicious. But under heterogeneity ($\zeta > 0$), honest updates are *supposed* to deviate. A client whose distribution is far from the population mean produces gradients that look exactly like Byzantine corruption to the aggregator. No permutation-invariant aggregator can distinguish "adversarial outlier" from "legitimate minority distribution" without additional assumptions [7].

### 4.2 Impossibility under arbitrary heterogeneity

The tension above is not merely intuitive; it is a theorem. El Mhamdi, Guerraoui, and Rouault [7] proved that under *arbitrary* heterogeneity — with no bound on $\zeta$ — Byzantine robustness is **impossible**: for any aggregation rule, there exist honest heterogeneous configurations and Byzantine strategies that are indistinguishable to the server, forcing linear convergence to a wrong model or divergence. Formally:

> **Theorem (impossibility, El Mhamdi et al. [7]):** If the honest gradients $\{ \nabla F_i(w) \}$ can be arbitrary (no dissimilarity bound), then for any deterministic aggregation rule $\mathcal{A}$ and any $f \ge 1$, there exist an honest instance and a Byzantine attack such that the iterates of $w_{t+1} = w_t - \eta \mathcal{A}(\cdot)$ converge to a point arbitrarily far from any stationary point of $F$.

The proof is a classic indistinguishability argument: the adversary simulates a "phantom" heterogeneous instance in which the *honest* updates of the true instance appear Byzantine, and vice versa. Since the server observes only vectors, not distributions, no rule can do the right thing in both worlds.

### 4.3 The price of heterogeneity: lower bounds

With the bounded-dissimilarity assumption (1), robustness becomes possible — but at a price. Data and Diggavi [11] established that any permutation-invariant aggregator must incur error $\Omega(\zeta \cdot f/n)$: the irreducible neighborhood of the optimum scales with both the heterogeneity level and the adversarial fraction. Concretely, for smooth strongly-convex $F$:

$$\limsup_{T \to \infty} \mathbb{E}\lVert w_T - w^\star \rVert^2 \;=\; \Omega\!\left(\frac{f}{n}\,\zeta^2\right) \tag{2}$$

for *any* robust aggregator. This is the **price of heterogeneity**: robustness under non-IID data is fundamentally approximate, and the approximation degrades linearly in the corruption budget. When $\zeta = 0$ (IID), the bound collapses to the familiar variance-dominated floor of classical Byzantine SGD [6].

### 4.4 Algorithmic responses: bucketing, clipping, and redundancy

Three algorithmic ideas recover near-optimal rates under bounded heterogeneity:

1. **Bucketing** [7]: randomly partition the $n$ updates into buckets, average within each bucket, then apply the robust aggregator to the bucket means. Bucketing reduces honest variance by the bucket size while preserving the adversarial fraction, tightening every concentration argument. It is the key trick that extends geometric-median-style guarantees to the heterogeneous regime.
2. **Centered clipping** [7]: the iterative clipping procedure of Section 3.2 converges to a fixed point satisfying $(\delta_{\max}, \zeta)$-robustness with $\delta_{\max}$ linear in $f/n$ — matching the lower bound (2) up to constants.
3. **Redundancy and certification**: when clients share overlapping data (coded computation) or the server holds a small clean validation set, robustness can be *certified* rather than merely heuristic — the server can test candidate updates against trusted data, converting the problem from robust statistics to verifiable computation [12].

### 4.5 Targeted attacks and the limits of statistics

Not all Byzantine attacks aim to destroy convergence. **Targeted (backdoor) attacks** [5] steer the model toward specific misbehavior on attacker-chosen inputs while keeping overall accuracy high — precisely the regime where statistical aggregators are blind, because the corrupted updates are *small* and *consistent*. Defending against backdoors requires going beyond robust aggregation: norm bounding, update clipping per client, differential-privacy-style noise, and anomaly detection on update trajectories. This motivates the modern view that Byzantine robustness is necessary but not sufficient for trustworthy federated learning, since statistical outlier rejection alone cannot detect adversaries whose updates remain within the honest distribution's support.

## 5. Empirical Analysis and Theoretical Guarantees

### 5.1 Convergence rates

The table below consolidates the best known rates for Byzantine-robust distributed SGD with $f$ adversaries among $n$ workers, under $L$-smoothness:

| Setting | Rate | Irreducible error $\mathcal{E}$ | Reference |
|---|---|---|---|
| Strongly convex, IID | $\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(1/T)$ | $\mathcal{O}(\sigma^2 f/n)$ | [6] |
| Non-convex, IID + momentum | $\mathcal{O}(1/\sqrt{T})$ | $\mathcal{O}(\sigma^2 f/n)$ | [5] |
| Non-convex, heterogeneous ($\zeta$) | $\mathcal{O}(1/\sqrt{T})$ | $\mathcal{O}(\zeta^2 f/n + \sigma^2 f/n)$ | [7] |
| Lower bound (any aggregator) | — | $\Omega(\zeta^2 f/n)$ | [11] |

The upper and lower bounds match up to constants: centered clipping with bucketing is **minimax optimal** for the heterogeneous Byzantine problem.

### 5.2 Computational and communication cost

Robustness is not free computationally. Krum and Bulyan cost $\mathcal{O}(n^2 d)$ per round — prohibitive when $n$ is in the thousands and $d$ in the millions. Geometric median and centered clipping cost $\mathcal{O}(nd)$ per inner iteration but require multiple iterations. Coordinate-wise methods are cheapest at $\mathcal{O}(nd)$. In cross-device FL, where the server is powerful but bandwidth is scarce, the $\mathcal{O}(n^2 d)$ cost is often acceptable; in cross-silo settings with large models, the iterative methods dominate. An important practical observation: because robust aggregators operate on *momenta* rather than raw gradients, they compose cleanly with communication compression (quantization, Top-$k$ sparsification), though the interaction between compression error and Byzantine resilience remains incompletely understood.

## 6. Limitations

The theory surveyed here has sharp boundaries that practitioners must respect:

- **The $f < n/2$ barrier.** Every aggregator discussed requires an honest majority. Without it, the adversary *is* the majority, and no statistical procedure can recover the honest mean. This is information-theoretic, not algorithmic.
- **Bounded heterogeneity is load-bearing.** The impossibility result of Section 4.2 means all positive results assume (1) or similar. In real federations, $\zeta$ is unknown and possibly large; estimating it under adversarial presence is itself an open problem.
- **Backdoors evade statistics.** Small, consistent, targeted corruptions pass through every known aggregator. Byzantine robustness and backdoor robustness are distinct problems requiring distinct defenses.
- **Privacy interplay.** Secure aggregation (cryptographic masking of individual updates) is *incompatible* with robust aggregation as defined here, because the server cannot inspect masked vectors. Reconciling privacy and robustness — via zero-knowledge proofs of update validity or trusted execution — is a major open direction.
- **Adaptive adversaries.** Most analyses assume the adversary commits to corruptions before seeing the current round's honest updates, or at least cannot adapt within a round. Fully adaptive, history-dependent adversaries can be strictly stronger.

## 7. Conclusion

Byzantine-robust federated learning has matured from the single-update selection of Krum to a minimax-optimal theory: under bounded heterogeneity, centered clipping with bucketing achieves the $\Omega(\zeta^2 f/n)$ lower bound, and momentum-augmented aggregation delivers practical robustness in non-convex deep learning. The central lesson is the **price of heterogeneity** — robustness under non-IID data is inherently approximate, and the approximation is governed by the product of adversarial budget and distributional skew.

The frontier now lies beyond pure robust statistics: certifying updates against trusted data, defending against targeted backdoors that evade outlier detection, and reconciling robustness with the cryptographic privacy that motivates federated learning in the first place. The most promising path is heterogeneity-aware robustness — aggregators that model the honest distribution's structure rather than treating every deviation as an attack — moving the field from worst-case statistics toward a science of trustworthy distributed learning.
