# Solid-State Batteries: Interfacial Electrochemistry, Dendrite Nucleation, and Operando Characterization

## Abstract

All-solid-state lithium batteries promise the energy density of the lithium-metal anode — roughly 454 Wh kg⁻¹ at the cell level against approximately 314 Wh kg⁻¹ for graphite-based cells — combined with the intrinsic safety of a non-flammable electrolyte. Their commercialization is nevertheless blocked not by bulk ionic conductivity, which now rivals liquid electrolytes in sulfides such as Li₁₀GeP₂S₁₂ at 12 mS cm⁻¹ [1], but by the electrochemistry of buried interfaces: the reductive instability of electrolytes against lithium metal, the nucleation of dendritic filaments along grain boundaries and pre-existing pores, and the mechanical coupling between plating kinetics and fracture. This thesis develops a unified framework spanning (i) the thermodynamic stability window and interphase evolution at the Li|solid-electrolyte interface, (ii) a quantitative nucleation-and-growth theory of dendrites incorporating electronic conductivity, local overpotential, and Griffith-type fracture mechanics, and (iii) the operando characterization toolkit — X-ray computed tomography, cryo-electron microscopy, Kelvin probe force microscopy, and X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy under bias — that has finally rendered these buried processes visible. We show that dendrite formation is a cascade: void formation during stripping concentrates current density, grain-boundary electronic conduction permits internal lithium plating, and stress accumulation at filament tips drives crack propagation that the filaments then follow. The synthesis yields quantitative design rules for critical current density, stack pressure, and interlayer engineering, and identifies the open questions that must be closed before solid-state cells reach the 4–10 mA cm⁻² rate capability of their liquid counterparts.

## 1 Introduction

The lithium-ion battery, the enabling technology of electrified transport and grid storage, is approaching its physicochemical limits. Conventional cells pair a graphite anode with a flammable organic carbonate electrolyte; the cathode is the remaining degree of freedom, and energy density gains are asymptotically flattening. The lithium-metal anode, with a specific capacity of 3,860 mAh g⁻¹ and the most negative standard potential of any electrode material, is the thermodynamically sanctioned route to a step change in energy density. Liquid electrolytes cannot host it safely: uneven plating nucleates dendrites that pierce the separator, and the ever-present risk of thermal runaway follows.

Solid electrolytes promised a clean escape. A dense ceramic or glassy separator should, by the conventional Monroe–Newman reasoning, mechanically suppress dendrites provided its shear modulus exceeds roughly twice that of lithium metal. Sulfide glasses and garnets easily satisfy this criterion, and the 2011 discovery of Li₁₀GeP₂S₁² (LGPS) with room-temperature conductivity of 12 mS cm⁻¹ — exceeding liquid electrolytes — removed the last bulk-transport objection [1]. Yet two decades of experiment have revealed that lithium filaments grow *through* dense ceramics at current densities below 1 mA cm⁻², an observation Peter Bruce characterized as the field's exam question: how does a material "more like cold butter than steel" penetrate a hard ceramic [2]?

> **Theorem (Central Claim):** Dendrite failure in solid-state batteries is not a single mechanical event but a cascade of interfacial electrochemical processes — reductive decomposition at the anode, void accumulation during stripping, grain-boundary-assisted internal plating, and stress-driven crack propagation — each of which is quantitatively addressable, and whose conjunction determines the critical current density.

This thesis is organized as follows. Section 2 surveys the electrolyte families and their interfacial chemistry. Section 3 develops the methodological core: thermodynamic stability analysis, Butler–Volmer kinetics at solid–solid contacts, phase-field and molecular-dynamics models of filament growth, and the operando methods that constrain them. Section 4 is the deep dive: interphase evolution, nucleation theory, mechanical coupling, and cathode-side phenomena. Section 5 confronts theory with quantitative experimental results. Section 6 states the limitations honestly, and Section 7 concludes with design rules and open problems.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Electrolyte Families

Three families dominate solid-electrolyte research, each with a distinct compromise:

| Family | Representative | σ_ion (25 °C) | Strengths | Weaknesses |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Sulfides | Li₁₀GeP₂S₁₂, Li₆PS₅Cl | 1–25 mS cm⁻¹ | Highest conductivity; soft, cold-pressable | Narrow stability window; H₂S on moisture exposure; reducible by Li |
| Oxides (garnet) | Li₇La₃Zr₂O₁₂ (LLZO) | 0.1–1 mS cm⁻¹ | Wide stability window; stable vs. Li | Brittle; high grain-boundary resistance; needs sintering |
| Polymers | PEO–LiTFSI | 10⁻⁵–10⁻³ S cm⁻¹ | Flexible; processable; good contact | Low conductivity; oxidatively limited; creep |

Sulfide argyrodites such as Li₆PS₅Cl combine conductivities above 1 mS cm⁻¹ with mechanical softness that permits densification by cold pressing, making them the leading near-term candidate despite their chemical reactivity [2]. Garnet LLZO offers superior chemical stability against lithium metal but suffers from grain-boundary resistances that can depress total conductivity by orders of magnitude relative to the bulk [3]. Polymer electrolytes trade conductivity for interfacial compliance and processability [4].

### 2.2 The Electrochemical Stability Window Problem

A persistent misconception holds that a wide *computed* band gap implies a wide electrochemical stability window. In practice, sulfide electrolytes are thermodynamically unstable against lithium metal: LGPS reduces below approximately 1.7 V vs. Li/Li⁺, decomposing into Li₂S, Li₃P, and Li–Ge alloys. The decomposition products are, however, partially passivating — a mixed ionic-electronic interphase whose growth is self-limiting if its electronic conductivity is low. The operative question is therefore not "is the electrolyte stable?" but "is the interphase kinetically self-limiting?" This distinction organizes the entire anode-interface literature.

### 2.3 Dendrites: From Liquid to Solid

In liquid electrolytes, dendrite nucleation is understood through diffusion-limited aggregation and the Sand's-time criterion: above a critical current, ion depletion at the interface triggers ramified growth. In solids, no analogous depletion exists — the puzzle is inverted. The emerging consensus, reviewed comprehensively in 2026 [3][4], identifies four coupled drivers:

1. **Void formation during stripping.** Lithium self-diffusion in the metal is slow (D ≈ 10⁻¹¹ cm² s⁻¹), so at practical stripping rates the interface cannot be replenished; voids nucleate, contact area shrinks, and local current density during subsequent plating spikes [4].
2. **Grain-boundary electronic conduction.** DFT calculations show that cubic LLZO grain boundaries trap excess surface electrons; phase-field simulations demonstrate that filaments nucleate preferentially at grain boundaries near the anode and propagate along them, consistent with operando Kelvin probe measurements [5].
3. **Internal plating via electronic leakage.** Even bulk electronic conductivities as low as 10⁻⁹ S cm⁻¹ provide pathways for Li⁺ reduction *inside* the electrolyte, generating mechanical stress sufficient to fracture ceramics whose fracture toughness is on the order of 1 MPa m⁰·⁵ [4].
4. **Pore-initiated cracking.** Operando X-ray tomography on argyrodite cells shows fracture in two stages: cracks initiate at pores, then propagate by being pulled apart at the base rather than sliced at the tip — a signature of plating-induced wedging [2].

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 Thermodynamic and Kinetic Formalism

We model the Li|solid-electrolyte interface with grand-potential phase diagrams computed from density functional theory, following the standard formalism in which the electrochemical stability window is the range of lithium chemical potential over which the electrolyte is the ground-state phase. Interfacial kinetics follow a modified Butler–Volmer relation accounting for the true contact area fraction *f* < 1:

```python
import numpy as np

def local_current_density(i_applied: float, contact_fraction: float) -> float:
    """True local current density given geometric current and contact fraction."""
    if contact_fraction <= 0:
        raise ValueError("contact lost: cell has delaminated")
    return i_applied / contact_fraction

def butler_volmer(eta: float, i0: float, alpha: float = 0.5, T: float = 298.0) -> float:
    """Symmetric Butler-Volmer kinetics at a solid-solid interface."""
    F, R = 96485.0, 8.314
    arg = alpha * F * eta / (R * T)
    return i0 * (np.exp(arg) - np.exp(-arg))

# void growth shrinks contact -> local current spikes -> dendrite overpotential
for f in [1.0, 0.5, 0.2, 0.05]:
    print(f"f={f:.2f}  i_local={local_current_density(1.0, f):.1f} mA/cm^2")
```

The loop above encodes the central positive-feedback mechanism of Section 4: any process that reduces *f* — voiding, contact loss, cracking — amplifies the local driving force for filament nucleation.

### 3.2 Phase-Field and Atomistic Simulation

Phase-field models couple the Cahn–Hilliard diffusion equation for Li⁺ with Allen–Cahn order-parameter dynamics for the metal phase and linear elasticity for stress, with DFT-derived inputs for grain-boundary electron trapping energies [5]. Complementarily, molecular dynamics with reactive force fields reproduces dendrite nucleation and penetration at atomic resolution, revealing that dynamically generated lithium deposition accumulates internal stress until Griffith-type fracture occurs at filament tips, and that intra-grain nuclei deflect toward grain boundaries with mixed Mode I/Mode II fracture patterns [6].

In the functional-programming spirit, the nucleation criterion can be stated declaratively:

```haskell
-- Dendrite nucleation predicate over an interface site
data Site = Site { overpotential :: Double   -- V
                 , eTrapping     :: Double   -- eV, grain-boundary electron affinity
                 , flawSize      :: Double   -- m, pre-existing pore radius
                 , toughness     :: Double   -- MPa m^0.5
                 }

nucleates :: Site -> Double -> Bool
nucleates s iCrit =
    let etaNuc = overpotential s - nucleationBarrier (flawSize s)
        electronDriven = eTrapping s > 0.3        -- DFT threshold for LLZO GBs
        stressDriven   = platingStress (overpotential s) > griffithStress (flawSize s) (toughness s)
    in etaNuc > 0 && (electronDriven || stressDriven) && overpotential s > iCrit * chargeTransferResistance s
```

### 3.3 Operando Characterization

Because the critical interfaces are buried, ex situ analysis is systematically misleading — disassembly relaxes stresses and exposes air-sensitive phases. The modern toolkit includes:

- **Operando X-ray computed tomography (XCT):** tracks crack initiation and propagation during cycling in argyrodite cells [2].
- **Operando optical/Kelvin probe microscopy:** maps potential and work-function evolution at LLZO grain boundaries during plating [5].
- **Cryo-STEM/EELS:** preserves and images the nanoscale interphase (Li₂O, Li₂CO₃, LiF-rich layers) without beam damage.
- **Operando XPS under bias:** measures band bending and decomposition-product evolution at the buried interface.
- **Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy with distribution of relaxation times (DRT):** deconvolves bulk, grain-boundary, and interfacial resistances during cycling.

A TLA+ sketch of the experimental protocol's safety invariant — never expose a cycled sulfide cell to ambient moisture before tomography — illustrates the discipline these air-sensitive measurements demand:


And the impedance-analysis pipeline in Rust-flavored pseudocode emphasizes the zero-copy, real-time constraint of operando DRT:

```rust
}
```

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Interphase Evolution at the Lithium-Metal Interface

Contact between lithium metal and a sulfide electrolyte initiates reductive decomposition within seconds. High-resolution cryo-STEM reveals a stratified interphase: an inner Li₂S/Li₃P matrix from electrolyte reduction, decorated with Li₂O and Li₂CO₃ from trace contamination, typically 5–50 nm thick depending on stack pressure and rest time. The interphase is a mixed conductor, and its electronic leakage current sets the long-term degradation rate: if electrons percolate, decomposition continues indefinitely and the interphase thickens parabolically; if the products are electronically insulating, growth arrests and the interface stabilizes.

> **Theorem (Self-Limiting Interphase):** An interphase of thickness *L(t)* is kinetically stable iff its electronic conductivity σₑ satisfies σₑ · (Δμ_Li / L) < j_pass, where Δμ_Li is the lithium chemical-potential drop and j_pass is the passivation current below which decomposition products remain electronically blocking. Sulfide interphases hover near this boundary, which is why stack pressure and interlayers matter so disproportionately.

Garnet LLZO, by contrast, forms essentially no interphase — its stability window genuinely includes 0 V vs. Li/Li⁺ — but pays with lithiophobicity: poor wetting leaves point contacts, and the true contact fraction *f* can be below 0.1 without surface treatment. Acid etching, Al₂O₃ atomic-layer deposition, or alloy-forming interlayers (Sn, Si, Ag) convert point contacts into area contacts, raising critical current density by an order of magnitude.

### 4.2 Nucleation Theory: Where Filaments Are Born

Classical nucleation theory, adapted to electrodeposition, gives the nucleation barrier ΔG* ∝ γ³ / (zFη)², where γ is the effective Li|electrolyte interfacial energy and η the local overpotential. Three refinements are essential in solids:

1. **Heterogeneous sites dominate.** The barrier at a pore or grain-boundary triple junction is reduced by a geometric factor Φ(θ) < 1, so nucleation is effectively *only* heterogeneous. Operando microscopy confirms filaments emerging at LLZO grain boundaries near the anode [5].
2. **Electronic conduction short-circuits the barrier.** Where grain boundaries trap electrons, Li⁺ reduces without requiring electron transport from the anode — the electrochemical step is decoupled from the external circuit, and the nucleation overpotential collapses [5][6].
3. **Stress modifies the barrier.** The chemical potential of lithium in a stressed filament includes a −σ_h Ω term (σ_h hydrostatic stress, Ω molar volume); compressive stress at a constrained tip *lowers* the deposition potential, creating an autocatalytic coupling between mechanics and electrochemistry that MD simulations capture quantitatively [6].

The practical consequence is a *distribution* of nucleation overpotentials across the interface, not a single threshold — which is why critical current density is statistical and cell-to-cell variability is large.

### 4.3 Mechanical Coupling: Fracture Follows Filaments, Then Guides Them

Once nucleated, a filament is a wedge. Lithium plating at the filament tip generates hydrostatic stresses estimated at hundreds of MPa — far above the fracture strength of sulfides (tens of MPa) and sufficient to crack even garnets. MD simulations show the classical Griffith criterion remains predictive provided the fracture toughness is corrected for the local lithium-ion concentration, which plasticizes the crack-tip region electrochemically [6]. Filaments within grains deflect toward grain boundaries, and the resulting fractures show mixed Mode I/Mode II character depending on the filament–boundary angle [6].

Operando XCT adds the crucial temporal order: cracks initiate at pores *before* filaments arrive, then propagate by base-wedging as plated lithium pries them open [2]. The filament does not so much pierce intact ceramic as *follow and widen* a damage network — a distinction with design implications, since it elevates pore elimination (via hot pressing or isostatic pressing) to equal importance with chemical stabilization.

### 4.4 The Cathode Side: Contact Loss and Chemo-Mechanics

The cathode composite — active particles, electrolyte, carbon, binder — breathes. NMC811 contracts by several percent on delithiation; repeated cycling ratchets particles away from the electrolyte matrix, and impedance spectroscopy shows the cathode interfacial resistance growing monotonically with cycle number. State-of-charge mapping by operando micro-diffraction reveals the resulting heterogeneity: particles near current-collector contacts cycle deeply while isolated particles lag, broadening the voltage profile and stranding capacity [3]. Mitigation strategies include zero-strain cathodes, compliant sulfide matrices that accommodate volume change, and single-crystal particle morphologies that eliminate intergranular fracture.

### 4.5 Stack Pressure: A Double-Edged Variable

External stack pressure (typically 1–10 MPa in laboratory cells) suppresses void formation during stripping by promoting lithium creep — lithium's homologous temperature at 25 °C is 0.66, so dislocation creep is active and pressure effectively pumps lithium back into incipient voids [4]. But pressure is not free: excessive pressure drives lithium creep *into* the electrolyte through pores, accelerates shorting, and is impractical for large-format cells. The optimum is therefore a narrow window, and much current engineering focuses on pressure-free operation via 3D-structured anodes or alloy anodes (Li–In, Li–Mg) with higher effective diffusivity.

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

We now confront the framework with quantitative results from the recent literature.

**Result 1 — Grain-boundary nucleation in LLZO (operando).** Combined operando optical microscopy, AFM/KPFM, and phase-field modeling on Li₆.₂₅Al₀.₂₅La₃Zr₂O₁₂ show filaments nucleating at grain boundaries near the lithium anode, with growth accelerating along boundaries due to elevated electron concentration promoting Li⁺ reduction. The measurements identify the anode-adjacent grain-boundary network — not the bulk — as the failure locus, and demonstrate that electron-blocking interlayers suppress the failure mode [5].

**Result 2 — Two-stage fracture in argyrodites (operando XCT).** In Li₆PS₅Cl cells, in-operando X-ray tomography with correlative modeling shows cracks initiating at pores, then propagating by base-wedging rather than tip-slicing. The Monroe–Bruce analysis resolves the "cold butter" paradox: the ceramic is not pierced by a soft filament but fractured by plating-generated stress concentrated at flaws, with the filament following the crack [2].

**Result 3 — Atomic-scale penetration mechanism (MD).** Reactive-force-field molecular dynamics reproduces nucleation and penetration, confirming Griffith-type fracture at filament tips with electrochemically corrected toughness, intra-grain nucleation deflecting to grain boundaries, and mixed Mode I/Mode II fracture patterns — all consistent with experimental fracture-surface analysis [6].

**Result 4 — Diffusion/failure cascade (review synthesis).** The 2026 eScience review quantifies the cascade: grain-boundary ionic conductivity up to three orders of magnitude below bulk, lithium self-diffusion of order 10⁻¹¹ cm² s⁻¹ driving void formation at low stack pressure, and internal plating via defect-mediated electronic conduction generating stresses that fracture ceramics of MPa m⁰·⁵-scale toughness. Inorganic electrolytes typically fail below 1 mA cm⁻² versus 4–10 mA cm⁻² for liquids [4].

**Result 5 — Conductivity benchmark.** LGPS at 12 mS cm⁻¹ room-temperature conductivity remains the reference point proving bulk transport is solved; subsequent germanium-free analogues (Li₁₀SnP₂S₁₂, Li₁₁Si₂PS₁₂) trade small conductivity penalties for elemental abundance [1]. The field's bottleneck is therefore *demonstrably* interfacial, not bulk.

Taken together, these results constitute a proof-by-convergence: independent techniques (microscopy, tomography, atomistic simulation, electrochemistry) agree on the cascade model of Section 4, with quantitative agreement on length scales (nanometer interphases, micrometer filaments), current-density thresholds (sub-1 mA cm⁻² for unprotected inorganic interfaces), and the governing dimensionless groups (contact fraction, electronic leakage, flaw-size-to-toughness ratio).

## 6 Limitations

1. **Observability gap.** Even the best operando methods sample micrometer fields of view; a 10 cm × 10 cm pouch cell contains ~10⁸ such fields. Rare-event nucleation — the single filament that shorts a cell — is statistically invisible to direct imaging, forcing reliance on accelerated or small-geometry tests whose scaling to large format is unproven.
2. **Simulation scale mismatch.** DFT captures picoseconds and nanometers; phase-field reaches micrometers and seconds; neither reaches the hours and centimeters of real cycling. The Griffith-plus-electrochemistry correction [6] is validated at the nanoscale only.
3. **Interphase chemistry under load.** Cryo-STEM interphase structures are measured at open circuit or after disassembly; the interphase *under sustained plating current and stack pressure* — the state that matters — remains largely inferred.
4. **Cathode-side quantification lags.** Anode filaments dominate the literature because they cause catastrophic failure, but gradual cathode contact loss may dominate capacity fade in cells where dendrites are suppressed; its kinetics are poorly parameterized.
5. **Pressure-free operation unsolved.** All high-critical-current-density demonstrations rely on stack pressures impractical for automotive packs; pressure-free cells with alloy or structured anodes remain below 2 mA cm⁻² in most reports.
6. **Electronic conductivity metrology.** Bulk electronic conductivities of 10⁻⁹–10⁻¹² S cm⁻¹ sit at the edge of measurability; reported values vary by orders of magnitude between techniques, yet the models of Section 4.2 are exponentially sensitive to them.

## 7 Conclusion

The solid-state battery field has undergone a conceptual inversion. The founding premise — a hard ceramic separator mechanically blocks dendrites — has been replaced by an electrochemical-mechanical cascade model in which interfaces, not bulk properties, determine fate. The quantitative picture is now clear: bulk ionic conductivity is solved (12 mS cm⁻¹ in LGPS [1]); the interphase at the lithium anode must be electronically self-limiting; grain boundaries must be electronically passivated to prevent internal plating [5]; pores must be eliminated to deny cracks their initiation sites [2]; and the filament–fracture coupling follows Griffith mechanics with electrochemical corrections [6]. The engineering corollaries are equally clear: maximize true contact fraction, block grain-boundary electron pathways with functional interlayers, densify to remove pores, and manage stack pressure within the creep-without-intrusion window [4].

> **Closing proposition:** The first commercially dominant solid-state cell will not be the one with the highest bulk conductivity, but the one whose interfaces are the most boring — chemically passivated, mechanically dense, and electronically quiet. Boredom, at the buried interface, is the engineering target.

Open problems remain formidable: rare-event statistics of nucleation at pouch scale, interphase structure under operating load, pressure-free rate capability, and cathode contact-loss kinetics. But the field finally possesses what it lacked for a decade — a validated, quantitative, multi-scale picture of *why* solid-state cells fail, which is the necessary precursor to making them not fail.
