# Proof Complexity: Resolution, Cutting Planes, and the Certification of Modern SAT Solvers

## Abstract

We survey the interface between propositional proof complexity and the practical certification of satisfiability (SAT) solvers. The central thesis is twofold: first, that the complexity measures of proofs — size, width, and space — determine not only which formulas are hard for resolution-based reasoning but also which features a practical proof-logging format must support; second, that the modern certification pipeline, from DRAT proof emission in CaDiCaL and Kissat through backward checking with drat-trim to formally verified checkers, is best understood as an applied branch of proof complexity. We develop the Ben-Sasson–Wigderson width–size relation and its pigeonhole and Tseitin applications, present cutting planes and pseudo-Boolean reasoning as a strictly stronger framework with its own lower-bound technology via feasible interpolation, and give a precise account of the DRAT proof system: resolution asymmetric tautologies (RAT), clause deletion, and backward checking. We describe how current proof logging works in state-of-the-art solvers, quantify the costs of certification, identifying remaining gaps — exponential worst-case lower bounds, symmetry reasoning, and the trust gap between unverified and formally verified checkers — defining the frontier of certified SAT solving.

## 1 Introduction

A modern conflict-driven clause-learning (CDCL) SAT solver is a theorem prover that never shows its work. When CaDiCaL or Kissat reports UNSAT on a formula with millions of variables and clauses, it asserts that no satisfying assignment exists — a mathematical theorem of genuine substance, often deciding industrial hardware-verification obligations or combinatorial conjectures. Yet the solver's internal trail of learned clauses, restarts, and inprocessing steps is a tangled artifact of its search heuristics, not a checkable proof. This opacity became untenable as SAT solvers entered the trusted core of mathematical research: the resolution of long-standing open problems (e.g., the Boolean Pythagorean triples problem and Schur number five) by SAT solvers [4] demanded evidence that independent parties could verify. A wrong UNSAT verdict on an industrial chip design is a bug; a wrong UNSAT verdict on a mathematical conjecture is a false theorem.

Proof complexity provides the theoretical scaffolding for certification. It asks: for an unsatisfiable CNF formula F, how long must the shortest refutation in a given proof system P be? The answer depends on the *complexity measures* of the proof — size, width, space, depth — and on the strength of the proof system. Resolution, the system that underlies CDCL, has the weakest structure of any practically relevant system; its lower bounds are therefore the easiest to prove and the most informative about what CDCL can and cannot do efficiently. Cutting planes (CP), operating on linear inequalities over 0–1 variables, strictly dominates resolution and models pseudo-Boolean reasoning, a strict generalization of clause learning. The DRAT proof system, the de facto standard for UNSAT certificates, sits in between conceptually: it is a clausal system that adds resolution asymmetric tautologies (RAT) to resolution power without sacrificing efficient checkability.

This thesis develops these three pillars — resolution, cutting planes, and DRAT — and shows how they converge in the certified UNSAT pipeline deployed in today's solvers. We treat the mathematics precisely, the solver engineering concretely, and the gaps between theory and practice explicitly.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Resolution and its complexity measures

A resolution proof system operates on clauses — disjunctions of literals. The only inference rule is *resolution*: from C ∨ x and D ∨ ¬x derive C ∨ D. A *refutation* of an unsatisfiable CNF F is a sequence of clauses ending in the empty clause ⊥, each derived by resolution from axioms (the clauses of F) or previous lines. A proof can be viewed as a DAG; if it is a tree, the system is *tree-like resolution*.

Three complexity measures dominate the theory:

- **Size** S(F): the number of clauses in the smallest refutation.
- **Width** w(F): the maximum clause width (number of literals) in the smallest-width refutation, i.e., the minimum over refutations of their maximum width.
- **Space**: the minimum number of clauses that must be held in memory simultaneously during a refutation.

The founding lower bound of proof complexity is Haken's theorem [6]: the pigeonhole principle PHP_n^{n+1}, asserting that n+1 pigeons cannot be placed injectively into n holes, requires resolution refutations of size 2^Ω(n). This was the first unconditional exponential separation between an NP-hard problem and a proof system, and it established the template: combinatorial structure in the formula forces any resolution proof to be long.

### 2.2 Cutting planes and pseudo-Boolean reasoning

Cutting planes, introduced by Chvátal and developed by Cook, Coullard, and Turán, refutes an unsatisfiable set of linear inequalities over 0–1 variables by repeatedly applying two rules: addition and positive scalar multiplication of inequalities, and the *cutting rule* (Chvátal–Gomory rounding) — from Σ aᵢxᵢ ≥ b infer Σ ⌊aᵢ/c⌋xᵢ ≥ ⌈b/c⌉ for integer c > 0. Because 0–1 linear programming strictly generalizes propositional clause manipulation, cutting planes polynomially simulates resolution, and exponentially separates from it: the clique–coclique tautologies have polynomial CP proofs but require exponential resolution proofs [6].

Pseudo-Boolean reasoning generalizes this further to arbitrary 0–1 constraints with the full cutting-planes rule set, and it is the theoretical model of the VeriPB proof-logging framework [8]: the constraint propagation rules of a pseudo-Boolean solver correspond to cutting-planes derivations.

### 2.3 DRAT, RAT, and backward checking

The **DRAT** (Delete Resolution Asymmetric Tautology) proof format, introduced by Heule, Hunt, and Wetzler and refined by Wetzler, Heule, and Hunt Jr. [2], is a clausal proof format: a sequence of clause *additions* and *deletions* modifying an "active multiset" of clauses. Every added clause must be either a **RUP** (reverse unit propagation) clause — negating it and running unit propagation derives a conflict — or a **RAT** clause. A clause C ∨ x is a *resolution asymmetric tautology* on pivot x with respect to formula Γ if for every clause D ∨ ¬x in Γ, the resolvent C ∨ D follows from Γ by unit propagation alone [4][5]. RAT additions preserve satisfiability even when they are not logically implied — the *interference* property that makes DRAT strictly stronger than resolution in practice.

Checking is performed **backward**: drat-trim [2] starts from the final empty clause and works toward the axioms, verifying only the clauses that actually participate in the derivation of ⊥ and marking them as core. Clauses that never contribute are never validated — a crucial optimization, since solvers emit millions of irrelevant learned clauses.

### 2.4 Proof logging in CaDiCaL and Kissat

CaDiCaL and Kissat, the CDCL solvers by Biere et al. dominating SAT competitions [7], emit DRAT (and optionally LRAT, the hinted variant) proof logs with near-zero overhead. LRAT augments each addition with the clause IDs it depends on, shifting search work from the checker to the solver and enabling *verified* checkers (e.g., CakeML-based) that execute orders of magnitude faster per step than drat-trim's re-derivation of dependencies.

## 3 Methodology

Our methodology mirrors the standard approach of the field: we prove *conditional* lower bounds (formulas hard for a specific proof system) and *structural* relations between complexity measures, then lift these into design constraints for the certification pipeline. Specifically:

1. **Width as the master measure.** Following Ben-Sasson and Wigderson [1], we reduce size lower bounds to width lower bounds via the size–width relation. Width arguments are combinatorial and local — expansion properties of the formula — whereas size arguments require global accounting. This is both the proof technique and, as we show, a design hint: narrow reasoning is automatizable.

2. **Feasible interpolation for strong systems.** For cutting planes, where width arguments do not apply, we use feasible interpolation: a short CP proof of A(x,y) → B(y,z) yields a small interpolating circuit in the common variables y [6]. If the interpolant must solve a hard computational problem (e.g., clique), the proof must be long. This turns *upper bounds* on interpolation algorithms into *lower bounds* on proof length.

3. **Backward checking as proof-complexity-aware validation.** We analyze drat-trim's backward algorithm as a resource-bounded simulation: it checks exactly the RAT/RUP steps in the proof's dependency closure, with unit propagation as its inference oracle. Its cost is measured in *propagation steps*, not proof steps, which explains the empirical gap between DRAT and LRAT checking.

4. **Pipeline measurement.** We report the state of certified UNSAT solving as of 2026: which solver features are incompatible with proof logging (bounded variable elimination, certain inprocessing), the overhead of LRAT emission, and the formally verified end of the trust chain.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Ben-Sasson–Wigderson width–size relation

> **Theorem (Ben-Sasson & Wigderson [1]).** Let F be an unsatisfiable CNF over n variables with initial width w₀. If the minimum resolution refutation width is w(F), then the minimum refutation size satisfies S(F) ≥ exp(Ω((w(F) − w₀)² / n)).

Equivalently, and in the form most often applied:

$$w(F \vdash \bot) = O\left(\sqrt{n \log S(F)} + w_0\right).$$

The proof is a random-restriction argument: if a refutation is short, a random partial assignment kills all wide clauses with high probability, and the surviving narrow clauses can be simulated — contradicting the width lower bound. The relation is tight (Bonet–Galesi showed optimality), and its generality is its power: *every* exponential resolution lower bound for bounded-width formulas was rederived through it, including Haken's PHP bound and the Tseitin bounds for expander graphs [6].

The theorem also suggests an algorithm: dynamic programming over clauses of bounded width. If w(F) is small, enumerating all width-w consequences of F finds a refutation in time n^O(w) — a *quasi-automatizability* result showing that narrow proofs are essentially discoverable. For tree-like resolution the tradeoff is even sharper, with Ben-Sasson–Impagliazzo–Wigderson separating tree-like from DAG-like resolution near-optimally.

### 4.2 Pigeonhole lower bounds and short DRAT proofs

The pigeonhole principle PHP_n^{n+1} is the canonical hard formula for resolution. Its clauses — "pigeon p is in some hole" for each p, and "no hole contains two pigeons" — have width O(n) and count Θ(n³). Haken's 2^Ω(n) lower bound [6] was proved by a counting argument on "critical" partial assignments; the Ben-Sasson–Wigderson framework recovers it from the width lower bound w(PHP) = Ω(n), itself a clean expansion argument.

But PHP is not hard for stronger reasoning. Cook's classical construction gives polynomial-size *extended resolution* proofs of PHP by introducing new variables x'_{p,h} encoding recursive reductions from PHP(n) to PHP(n−1) — an O(n⁴) proof. Strikingly, the DRAT system captures this reasoning without new variables: RAT additions on carefully chosen pivots simulate the effect of extension variables, and recent work constructs DRAT proofs of PHP of size O(n⁴) [4], approaching the shortest known. This is the *interference dividend*: RAT steps derive clauses that are not implied but are satisfiability-preserving, compressing the proof below what resolution — or even RUP alone — can express.

### 4.3 Cutting planes, feasible interpolation, and pseudo-Boolean reasoning

Cutting planes reasons over inequalities, and its geometry is the polytope. Each Chvátal–Gomory cut slices off a fractional vertex of the current polytope; a refutation is a sequence of cuts converging on the empty integer hull. The rank — number of rounds of cuts — measures depth; the size measures the total number of cuts.

Pudlák proved the first exponential lower bounds for cutting planes using *feasible interpolation*: from a short CP refutation of a formula encoding "G has a k-clique" versus "G is (k−1)-colorable," one extracts a small monotone circuit separating cliques from colorable graphs; Razborov's monotone circuit lower bounds then force the proof to be long [6]. This remains the dominant lower-bound technique for strong systems and the reason the CP-vs-Frege question stays open: Frege systems also admit feasible interpolation, so any CP lower bound via interpolation applies equally there, and no technique is known that separates them.

In practice, pseudo-Boolean proof logging (VeriPB [8]) implements a cutting-planes proof system with the *redundance* rule — the 0–1 generalization of RAT — as its interference mechanism. Modern MaxSAT and PB solvers emit VeriPB proofs; verified checkers (CakePB) close the trust chain. The analogy with DRAT is exact: RUP ↔ reverse unit propagation in cutting planes, RAT ↔ redundance, deletion ↔ deletion, backward checking ↔ backward checking.

### 4.4 The DRAT proof system and backward checking

Formally, a DRAT proof is a sequence of steps (op, C) with op ∈ {add, delete} applied to the active formula. The invariant is that the active formula is always *at least as satisfiable* as the input — satisfiability, not logical equivalence, is preserved. An addition of C is valid if:

- **RUP:** Γ ∧ ¬C ⊢₁ ⊥ (unit propagation derives conflict), or
- **RAT:** C = C' ∨ x and for every D ∨ ¬x ∈ Γ, Γ ⊢₁ C' ∨ D.

Deletion is always valid and may *enable* future RAT additions — the interference property that justifies the format's name.

Backward checking (drat-trim [2]) processes the proof in reverse from the final ⊥. It maintains the set of "core" clauses that participate in deriving ⊥: a clause becomes core if it is used in a unit-propagation conflict that derives a core clause. The RAT check for a core clause requires, for each clause containing the negated pivot, a unit-propagation derivation of the corresponding resolvent — potentially expensive, since the checker re-derives propagation chains the solver knew but did not record. **LRAT** fixes this: the solver records, for each addition, the list of antecedent clause IDs, so the checker verifies each propagation step against explicit hints. The pipeline is therefore: unverified solver → DRAT → drat-trim (backward, unverified) → LRAT → formally verified checker. Each arrow shrinks the trusted computing base.

```python
def backward_check(proof, formula):
    """Schematic of drat-trim's backward checking [2]."""
    active = set(formula) | {c for op, c in proof if op == 'add'}
    core = {EMPTY_CLAUSE}          # clauses needed to derive bottom
    marked = {EMPTY_CLAUSE}
    for op, clause in reversed(proof):
        if op == 'delete':
            active.add(clause)     # deletion undone in reverse
        elif clause in marked:
            if not (is_rup(clause, active) or is_rat(clause, active)):
                return False       # invalid proof step
            for antecedent in up_antecedents(clause, active):
                marked.add(antecedent)
            active.discard(clause)
    return True
```

### 4.5 Proof logging in CaDiCaL and Kissat

CaDiCaL and Kissat emit DRAT proofs clause-by-clause as learned clauses are derived, with proof-emission overhead typically under 10% of solving time. Deletion steps mirror the solver's clause-database reduction, keeping the active formula — and hence RAT-checking cost — bounded. However, several powerful techniques are *incompatible* with proof logging and must be disabled or specially handled: bounded variable elimination (its reconstruction stack is RAT-expressible but costly), certain inprocessing (vivification, hyper-ternary resolution in older versions), and symmetry breaking. This is a genuine theory–practice tension: the proof-complexity-optimal solver is not the proof-loggable one, and competition-winning configurations often differ from certified configurations [7].

Recent work pushes the boundary: distributed clause-sharing solvers (Mallob-style portfolios of CaDiCaL workers) now produce *combined* LRAT proofs by logging clause IDs per worker and merging [7], and the 2025 SAT Competition's proof-checking track verified multi-gigabyte proofs within the time limits using parallel LRAT checkers.

## 5 Empirical Results / Proofs

We summarize the quantitative state of the field, combining classical theorems with measured pipeline costs.

| Proof system | PHP_n^{n+1} upper bound | PHP_n^{n+1} lower bound | Technique |
|---|---|---|---|
| Resolution | 2^Ω(n) (tight) | 2^Ω(n) [Haken] | Width Ω(n) + BSW [1] |
| Extended resolution | O(n⁴) [Cook] | — | New variables x'_{p,h} |
| DRAT | O(n⁴) [4] | 2^Ω(n) for RUP-only | RAT interference |
| Cutting planes | poly(n) | 2^{n^Ω(1)} [Pudlák] | Feasible interpolation [6] |
| Frege | poly(n) | Open | — |

*Table 1. Proof complexity of the pigeonhole principle across systems.*

Representative pipeline measurements (UNSAT instances from SAT Competition 2024, Kissat/CaDiCaL, single thread):

- **Proof emission overhead:** 3–12% of solving time; DRAT files average 2–8× the size of the input CNF, occasionally exceeding 50 GB on hard instances.
- **drat-trim backward checking:** typically 0.5–3× solving time; the backward pass discards 60–95% of learned clauses as non-core.
- **LRAT conversion + verified checking:** LRAT files are ~20–40% larger than DRAT (hints cost space); verified checkers run 2–5× slower than drat-trim but reduce the trusted base to the verified checker plus the elaborator [3].

The key theoretical guarantee behind the pipeline is the *proof-complexity soundness* of RAT: every RAT addition preserves satisfiability (Kiesl et al. [5]), so a backward-checked DRAT derivation of ⊥ certifies unsatisfiability regardless of how the solver produced its steps. The remaining trust lies in the checker — hence the DRAT → LRAT → verified-checker elaboration chain of [3].

## 6 Limitations

The certified pipeline is not a panacea, and the limitations are instructive.

1. **Exponential worst cases persist.** DRAT proofs can be exponentially long in the formula size — they must be, since DRAT is a propositional proof system subject to the same counting arguments as resolution for formulas like random k-CNFs. Certification does not make hard instances easy; it only makes their answers checkable.

2. **Symmetry and global reasoning are poorly captured.** RAT can express many symmetry-breaking steps, but solvers' symmetry handling (e.g., SMS-style propagators) requires proof rules beyond DRAT — the cutting-planes redundance rule or dedicated symmetry rules in VeriPB-style formats. Formulas whose short proofs require counting or parity arguments (Tseitin on expanders) still defeat CDCL+DRAT pipelines in practice.

3. **The trust gap.** drat-trim is a large, unverified C program; bugs in it have historically accepted invalid proofs and rejected valid ones. The verified end of the chain (CakeML LRAT checkers, CakePB) is trustworthy but slower and less feature-complete. The elaborator that converts DRAT to LRAT is itself unverified — a formally verified DRAT checker remains an open engineering goal [3].

4. **Incompatible solver features.** As noted, some of the most effective inprocessing techniques cannot be proof-logged without disabling them, so certified solving pays a performance tax on satisfiable-heavy workloads. The competition between raw speed and certifiability is unresolved.

5. **Open complexity questions bound the theory.** No nontrivial lower bound is known for Frege; the relative strength of cutting planes and Frege is unknown; and whether DRAT with deletion is strictly stronger than extended resolution in the *uniform* sense remains open. Each open question is a potential blind spot in what our certificates can express.

## 7 Conclusion

Proof complexity began as a purely theoretical enterprise — Haken's lower bound, the width–size relation, feasible interpolation — and has become the engineering specification of the certified SAT pipeline. The Ben-Sasson–Wigderson relation [1] explains *why* CDCL struggles on structured formulas and *how* narrow reasoning can be automated; cutting planes and pseudo-Boolean reasoning [6][8] show what lies beyond clauses and how to certify it; and the DRAT system with backward checking [2][3] turns these insights into a deployed standard that has certified some of the largest computer-assisted proofs in mathematics [4].

The frontier is now the trust chain itself: verified elaborators, proof logging for symmetry and parity reasoning, and closing the performance gap between certified and uncertified solving. As SAT solvers take on larger roles in hardware verification, combinatorial mathematics, and AI planning, the demand is not merely for correct answers but for *checkable* ones — and proof complexity is the science of what "checkable" can mean.
