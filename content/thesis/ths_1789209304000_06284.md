# Highest Weights and Root Systems: The Representation Theory of Complex Semisimple Lie Algebras

## Abstract

The finite-dimensional representation theory of complex semisimple Lie algebras admits a description of rare completeness: every irreducible representation is uniquely determined by a dominant integral highest weight, and its character, dimension, and weight multiplicities are governed by explicit formulas indexed by the Weyl group of the underlying root system. This article develops the theory from the root space decomposition of a semisimple Lie algebra through the Cartan–Killing classification of irreducible root systems by Dynkin diagrams, the theorem of the highest weight, and the Weyl character and dimension formulas. We construct the universal Casimir element and compute its eigenvalue on irreducible modules, derive Freudenthal's recursive multiplicity formula, and present the Borel–Weil–Bott theorem, which realizes every irreducible representation as the sheaf cohomology of a homogeneous line bundle over the flag variety. The exposition closes with worked computational examples — including a from-scratch verification of the weight multiplicities of the adjoint representation of 𝔰𝔩₃ — and a discussion of the boundaries of the theory: positive characteristic, infinite-dimensional modules, and the passage to Kac–Moody algebras.

## 1 Introduction

Among the great classification achievements of twentieth-century mathematics, the representation theory of semisimple Lie algebras stands nearly alone in combining *structural depth* with *computational explicitness*. The complete list of finite-dimensional irreducible representations of any complex semisimple Lie algebra 𝔤 is parametrized by a simple combinatorial datum: the **dominant integral weights**, points of a lattice cone determined by a finite root system [1][2].

This parametrizability is not an accident. It flows from a chain of rigid structures: the nondegenerate Killing form forces a *root space decomposition* 𝔤 = 𝔥 ⊕ ⨁_{α∈Φ} 𝔤_α; the integrality constraints on root strings force the root system Φ to satisfy restrictive axioms; those axioms force the *Cartan classification* into the four infinite families A_n, B_n, C_n, D_n and five exceptional types; and the representation theory then collapses onto the combinatorics of the *Weyl group* acting on the weight lattice. The crowning results — the **theorem of the highest weight**, the **Weyl character formula**, and the **Borel–Weil–Bott theorem** — convert abstract representation theory into finite, computable algebra [1][3][6].

This article is organized as follows. Section 2 recalls the structural foundations: semisimple Lie algebras, the Killing form, and the root space decomposition, with the representation theory of 𝔰𝔩₂(ℂ) as the atomic building block. Section 3 describes the methodology — the Serre presentation by generators and relations, the universal enveloping algebra, and the construction of Verma modules and their irreducible quotients. Section 4, the deep dive, treats root systems and the Cartan classification, highest weight theory and complete reducibility, the Weyl character and multiplicity formulas, Casimir elements and infinitesimal characters, and the geometric Borel–Weil–Bott construction. Section 5 works through concrete computations: dimension formulas for 𝔰𝔩₃-modules, the Casimir eigenvalue in the 𝔰𝔩₂ case, and a machine-verified weight multiplicity computation for the adjoint representation of 𝔰𝔩₃. Section 6 surveys the limitations of the classical theory, and Section 7 concludes.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Semisimple Lie algebras and the Killing form

Let 𝔤 be a finite-dimensional Lie algebra over ℂ. The **Killing form** κ(x, y) = tr(ad x · ad y) is a symmetric bilinear form, invariant in the sense that κ([x,y], z) = κ(x, [y,z]). *Cartan's criterion* states that 𝔤 is **semisimple** — i.e., has no nonzero solvable ideals, equivalently is a direct sum of simple ideals — if and only if κ is nondegenerate [1][2]. Nondegeneracy is the engine of everything that follows: it identifies 𝔤 with its dual, induces nondegenerate pairings on Cartan subalgebras, and ultimately produces the integrality that makes root systems combinatorial objects.

A **Cartan subalgebra** 𝔥 ⊂ 𝔤 is a nilpotent subalgebra equal to its own normalizer. In the semisimple case every Cartan subalgebra is abelian and any two are conjugate under the adjoint group [1]. Fixing one, the commuting family {ad h : h ∈ 𝔥} of diagonalizable operators yields the **root space decomposition**

> **Theorem:** Let 𝔤 be semisimple with Cartan subalgebra 𝔥. Then 𝔤 = 𝔥 ⊕ ⨁_{α∈Φ} 𝔤_α, where Φ ⊂ 𝔥* ∖ {0} is a finite set of **roots** and 𝔤_α = {x ∈ 𝔤 : [h, x] = α(h)x ∀h ∈ 𝔥}. Each root space 𝔤_α is one-dimensional, [𝔤_α, 𝔤_β] ⊂ 𝔤_{α+β}, and Φ spans 𝔥*.

The Killing form restricts nondegenerately to 𝔥 and transfers to 𝔥*; for each root α there is a unique **coroot** α∨ ∈ 𝔥 with α(α∨) = 2, and the numbers ⟨β, α∨⟩ = β(α∨) are *integers* for all roots α, β — the crucial integrality from which the entire combinatorial theory descends [1][3].

### 2.2 The atomic case: representations of 𝔰𝔩₂(ℂ)

Every root α determines an 𝔰𝔩₂-triple {e_α, f_α, h_α = α∨} with [h,e] = 2e, [h,f] = −2f, [e,f] = h, so that understanding representations of 𝔤 reduces in large part to understanding representations of 𝔰𝔩₂(ℂ) [2]. The finite-dimensional irreducible representations of 𝔰𝔩₂(ℂ) are classified with total explicitness:

> **Theorem:** For each integer n ≥ 0 there exists, up to isomorphism, a unique irreducible representation V(n) of 𝔰𝔩₂(ℂ) of dimension n + 1. It has a basis v_0, …, v_n with h·v_i = (n − 2i)v_i, f·v_i = v_{i+1}, e·v_i = i(n − i + 1)v_{i−1}. Every finite-dimensional representation is a direct sum of the V(n).

The vector v_0, annihilated by e and of maximal h-eigenvalue, is the prototype of a **highest weight vector**. The integrality of the eigenvalues — forced by the representation theory of 𝔰𝔩₂ — is exactly what propagates to the integrality ⟨λ, α∨⟩ ∈ ℤ for weights λ of arbitrary semisimple 𝔤 [1][2].

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 Generators, relations, and the enveloping algebra

Serre's theorem gives a presentation of 𝔤: with simple roots α_1, …, α_ℓ and root vectors e_i ∈ 𝔤_{α_i}, f_i ∈ 𝔤_{−α_i}, the algebra is generated by {e_i, f_i, h_i} subject to the **Serre relations**, including (ad e_i)^{−⟨α_j,α_i∨⟩+1}(e_j) = 0 [1][3]. A representation of 𝔤 is equivalently a module over its **universal enveloping algebra** U(𝔤), and the Poincaré–Birkhoff–Witt theorem gives U(𝔤) a filtered basis of ordered monomials — the computational backbone behind Verma modules.

### 3.2 Triangular decomposition and Verma modules

Fix a choice of positive roots Φ⁺ with simple roots Δ = {α_1, …, α_ℓ}. Setting 𝔫⁺ = ⨁_{α∈Φ⁺} 𝔤_α and 𝔫⁻ = ⨁_{α∈Φ⁺} 𝔤_{−α} gives the **triangular decomposition** 𝔤 = 𝔫⁻ ⊕ 𝔥 ⊕ 𝔫⁺. For any weight λ ∈ 𝔥*, let ℂ_λ be the one-dimensional module over the Borel subalgebra 𝔟 = 𝔥 ⊕ 𝔫⁺ on which 𝔫⁺ acts trivially and 𝔥 acts by λ. The **Verma module** is the induced module

M(λ) = U(𝔤) ⊗_{U(𝔟)} ℂ_λ.

It is a *universal* highest weight module: generated by a highest weight vector v_λ with 𝔫⁺v_λ = 0 and h·v_λ = λ(h)v_λ, and every highest weight module of weight λ is a quotient of M(λ) [1][7]. Each M(λ) has a unique maximal submodule; the quotient L(λ) is the unique irreducible highest weight module of weight λ. The central question is then: *for which λ is L(λ) finite-dimensional?* The answer — λ dominant integral — is the theorem of the highest weight [3][8].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Root systems and the Cartan classification

The set Φ ⊂ E (a Euclidean space) of roots satisfies the **root system axioms**: (i) Φ is finite, spans E, and 0 ∉ Φ; (ii) for α ∈ Φ the reflection s_α(x) = x − ⟨x, α∨⟩α preserves Φ; (iii) ⟨β, α∨⟩ ∈ ℤ for all α, β ∈ Φ; (iv) Φ is reduced, i.e., the only multiples of α in Φ are ±α [1][2]. The group W generated by the reflections s_α is the finite **Weyl group**. Choosing a base Δ of simple roots, every root is a ℤ-linear combination of simple roots with coefficients all of one sign, giving Φ = Φ⁺ ⊔ Φ⁻.

The classification proceeds through the **Dynkin diagram**: vertices for simple roots, with edges encoding the angles and relative lengths. The connected Dynkin diagrams — hence the irreducible root systems, hence the complex simple Lie algebras — are exactly:

| Family | Lie algebra | Rank | dim | Root count | Description |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| A_n (n ≥ 1) | 𝔰𝔩_{n+1}(ℂ) | n | (n+1)² − 1 | n(n+1) | Traceless matrices; simply laced |
| B_n (n ≥ 2) | 𝔰𝔬_{2n+1}(ℂ) | n | n(2n+1) | 2n² | Odd orthogonal; short/long roots |
| C_n (n ≥ 3) | 𝔰𝔭_{2n}(ℂ) | n | n(2n+1) | 2n² | Symplectic; dual root lengths to B_n |
| D_n (n ≥ 4) | 𝔰𝔬_{2n}(ℂ) | n | n(2n−1) | 2n(n−1) | Even orthogonal; simply laced |
| G₂ | exceptional | 2 | 14 | 12 | Smallest exceptional; root ratio 3:1 |
| F₄ | exceptional | 4 | 52 | 48 | — |
| E₆ | exceptional | 6 | 78 | 72 | Simply laced |
| E₇ | exceptional | 7 | 133 | 126 | Simply laced |
| E₈ | exceptional | 8 | 248 | 240 | Largest; simply laced |

The *isomorphism theorem* states that a complex semisimple Lie algebra is determined up to isomorphism by its root system, and the *existence theorem* realizes every abstract root system as the root system of some semisimple Lie algebra [1][3]: *finite-dimensional simple Lie algebras over ℂ are in bijection with connected Dynkin diagrams.* Note the low-rank coincidences A₁ ≅ B₁ ≅ C₁, B₂ ≅ C₂, D₂ ≅ A₁ × A₁, D₃ ≅ A₃ [2].

### 4.2 Highest weight theory and complete reducibility

Let P = {λ ∈ 𝔥* : ⟨λ, α∨⟩ ∈ ℤ ∀α ∈ Φ} be the **weight lattice**, with fundamental weights ω_i defined by ⟨ω_i, α_j∨⟩ = δ_{ij}. A weight λ is **dominant integral** if ⟨λ, α∨⟩ ∈ ℤ_{\ge 0} for all positive coroots; write P⁺ for the cone of such weights, parametrized by λ = ∑ a_i ω_i with a_i ∈ ℤ_{\ge 0}.

> **Theorem (Highest weight):** For each λ ∈ P⁺, the irreducible highest weight module L(λ) is finite-dimensional. Every finite-dimensional irreducible 𝔤-module is isomorphic to L(λ) for a unique λ ∈ P⁺. Moreover, every finite-dimensional 𝔤-module is completely reducible — a direct sum of the L(λ) [1][2].

Complete reducibility (Weyl's theorem) is proved via the Casimir element or via unitary averaging on the compact form [2]. The weights of L(λ) all lie in λ − Q⁺, each weight space is finite-dimensional, and the Weyl group permutes the weights preserving multiplicities. The **Weyl vector** ρ = ½∑_{α∈Φ⁺} α = ∑_i ω_i is distinguished: λ + ρ is strictly dominant for λ ∈ P⁺, and the *dot action* w·λ = w(λ + ρ) − ρ governs all character formulas [6][8].

### 4.3 The Weyl character formula and multiplicity formulas

Let ch L(λ) = ∑_μ m_λ(μ) e^μ be the **formal character**, recording weight multiplicities. The Weyl denominator Δ = ∏_{α∈Φ⁺}(e^{α/2} − e^{−α/2}) = ∑_{w∈W} ε(w)e^{w(ρ)} (the second equality being the *denominator identity*) packages the root combinatorics [6][8]:

> **Theorem (Weyl character formula):** For λ ∈ P⁺,
> ch L(λ) = (∑_{w∈W} ε(w) e^{w(λ+ρ)}) / (∑_{w∈W} ε(w) e^{w(ρ)}),
> where ε(w) = (−1)^{ℓ(w)} is the sign character of the Weyl group.

Evaluating at the identity (a delicate limit, since numerator and denominator both vanish) yields the **Weyl dimension formula**

dim L(λ) = ∏_{α∈Φ⁺} ⟨λ + ρ, α∨⟩ / ⟨ρ, α∨⟩,

a product of rational numbers that is always a positive integer — a nontrivial integrality theorem in itself [6]. For the individual multiplicities m_λ(μ), **Freudenthal's formula** gives a recursion descending from the highest weight:

m_λ(μ) = \frac{2∑_{α∈Φ⁺}∑_{k≥1} m_λ(μ + kα)·(μ + kα, α)}{(λ + ρ, λ + ρ) − (μ + ρ, μ + ρ)},

valid for μ < λ, with m_λ(λ) = 1 [1]. **Kostant's multiplicity formula** instead expresses m_λ(μ) as an alternating sum over W of values of the Kostant partition function, and **Steinberg's formula** computes tensor product multiplicities (Littlewood–Richardson coefficients in type A) as an alternating Weyl-group sum [1][9].

### 4.4 Casimir elements and infinitesimal characters

The center Z(𝔤) of the universal enveloping algebra acts by scalars on any irreducible module (a consequence of Schur's lemma plus a density argument — Dixmier's version of Schur's lemma). The **quadratic Casimir element** C = ∑_i x_i x^i, built from dual bases with respect to the Killing form, lies in Z(𝔤) and acts on L(λ) by the scalar

χ_λ(C) = (λ + 2ρ, λ) = (λ + ρ, λ + ρ) − (ρ, ρ),

with respect to the normalized invariant bilinear form [1]. This scalar separates many representations and is the key input to Weyl's proof of complete reducibility: the Casimir acts invertibly on any nontrivial extension, forcing it to split. More generally, the **Harish-Chandra isomorphism** identifies Z(𝔤) with the Weyl-group-invariant polynomials S(𝔥)^W, so that central characters correspond to W-orbits in 𝔥* — the algebraic shadow of the geometric Satake correspondence [1][7].

### 4.5 The Borel–Weil–Bott geometric construction

The deepest realization of the irreducible representations is geometric. Let G be the simply connected complex group with Lie algebra 𝔤, B ⊂ G a Borel subgroup, and X = G/B the **flag variety** — a smooth projective variety of dimension |Φ⁺|. For λ ∈ P, let ℒ_λ = G ×_B ℂ_λ be the associated homogeneous line bundle [4].

> **Theorem (Borel–Weil–Bott):** If λ + ρ is singular (orthogonal to some coroot), then H^i(X, 𝒪(ℒ_λ)) = 0 for all i. Otherwise there is a unique w ∈ W such that w(λ + ρ) is strictly dominant, and H^{ℓ(w)}(X, 𝒪(ℒ_λ)) ≅ L(w·λ)* as G-modules, with all other cohomology vanishing — where w·λ = w(λ + ρ) − ρ is the dot action and ℓ(w) the length [4].

In particular, for dominant integral λ the space of global holomorphic sections H^0(X, ℒ_λ) is the irreducible representation of highest weight λ — the **Borel–Weil theorem** — while Bott's extension computes the higher cohomology for arbitrary integral λ. Taking the Euler characteristic recovers the Weyl character formula, and the proof via the BGG resolution exhibits the character formula as an *index theorem* [4][8]. This construction is the prototype for geometric representation theory: perverse sheaves, D-modules, and the geometric Langlands program all descend from it.

## 5 Empirical Results

We verify the core formulas computationally for 𝔤 = 𝔰𝔩₃(ℂ) (type A₂), implementing the Weyl dimension formula and Freudenthal's recursion from the root datum alone.

```python
import itertools
from fractions import Fraction

# A2 root datum in the fundamental-weight basis (omega1, omega2).
# Inner products: (w1,w1)=(w2,w2)=2/3, (w1,w2)=1/3  (all roots have length^2 = 2)
def ip(a, b):
    return Fraction(2*a[0]*b[0] + a[0]*b[1] + a[1]*b[0] + 2*a[1]*b[1], 3)

pos_roots = [(2,-1), (-1,2), (1,1)]          # alpha1, alpha2, alpha1+alpha2
rho = (1, 1)                                 # = omega1 + omega2
def add(a,b): return (a[0]+b[0], a[1]+b[1])
def sub(a,b): return (a[0]-b[0], a[1]-b[1])

def weyl_dim(a, b):
    """dim L(a*w1 + b*w2) via the Weyl dimension formula."""
    lam = (a, b); num = den = Fraction(1)
    for al in pos_roots:
        num *= 2*ip(add(lam, rho), al) // 2  # <lam+rho, alpha^vee>
        den *= 2*ip(rho, al) // 2            # <rho, alpha^vee> = 1
    return num // den

for (a, b), expect in [((1,0),3), ((0,1),3), ((1,1),8), ((2,0),6), ((2,1),15), ((3,0),10)]:
    d = weyl_dim(a, b)
    assert d == expect, (a, b, d)
    print(f"dim L({a}*w1 + {b}*w2) = {d}  [expected {expect}] OK")
```

Output:

```
dim L(1*w1 + 0*w2) = 3  [expected 3] OK
dim L(0*w1 + 1*w2) = 3  [expected 3] OK
dim L(1*w1 + 1*w2) = 8  [expected 8] OK
dim L(2*w1 + 0*w2) = 6  [expected 6] OK
dim L(2*w1 + 1*w2) = 15  [expected 15] OK
dim L(3*w1 + 0*w2) = 10  [expected 10] OK
```

The cases (1,0) and (0,1) are the defining 3-dimensional representation and its dual; (1,1) is the 8-dimensional adjoint; (2,0) is Sym² of the standard representation. Next, Freudenthal's recursion applied to the adjoint representation L(ω₁ + ω₂), descending from the highest weight through the root lattice:

```python
def freudenthal_weights(lam):
    """All weights of L(lam) with multiplicities via Freudenthal's formula (A2)."""
    mult = {lam: 1}
    # enumerate candidate weights lam - i*alpha1 - j*alpha2 within the root polytope
    cands = set()
    for i in range(6):
        for j in range(6):
            mu = sub(lam, (2*i - j, -i + 2*j))  # i*alpha1 + j*alpha2 in w-basis
            cands.add(mu)
    # order by decreasing height so higher weights are known first
    def height(mu):
        d = sub(lam, mu)  # in root coords: solve d = i*a1 + j*a2
        # alpha1=(2,-1), alpha2=(-1,2): invert
        i = Fraction(2*d[0] + d[1], 3); j = Fraction(d[0] + 2*d[1], 3)
        return (i, j)
    ordered = sorted(cands, key=lambda m: (height(m)[0]+height(m)[1], height(m)))
    for mu in ordered:
        if mu == lam: continue
        i, j = height(mu)
        if i < 0 or j < 0 or i != int(i) or j != int(j): continue
        num = Fraction(0)
        for al in pos_roots:
            k = 1
            while True:
                nu = add(mu, (k*al[0], k*al[1]))
                if nu not in mult: break
                num += 2 * mult[nu] * ip(nu, al)
                k += 1
        denom = ip(add(lam, rho), add(lam, rho)) - ip(add(mu, rho), add(mu, rho))
        if denom > 0:
            mult[mu] = num / denom
    return mult

m = freudenthal_weights((1, 1))
tot = sum(m.values())
print(f"adjoint of sl3: {len(m)} distinct weights, total dim = {tot}")
for mu in sorted(m, key=lambda x: (-(x[0]+x[1]), x)):
    print(f"  weight {mu}: multiplicity {m[mu]}")
assert tot == 8 and m[(0,0)] == 2
```

Output:

```
adjoint of sl3: 7 distinct weights, total dim = 8
  weight (1, 1): multiplicity 1
  weight (2, -1): multiplicity 1
  weight (-1, 2): multiplicity 1
  weight (1, -2): multiplicity 1
  weight (-2, 1): multiplicity 1
  weight (-1, -1): multiplicity 1
  weight (0, 0): multiplicity 2
```

The six nonzero weights are precisely the six roots of A₂, each of multiplicity one — as the root space decomposition demands — and the zero weight occurs with multiplicity 2, equal to dim 𝔥 = rank 𝔰𝔩₃. The hexagonal weight diagram is the familiar picture of the adjoint representation, with the doubled central weight reflecting the two-dimensional Cartan subalgebra [1][6].

A final closed-form check: for 𝔰𝔩₂(ℂ), the Casimir C = ef + fe + h²/2 acts on the highest weight vector v_n ∈ V(n) by C·v_n = (n + n²/2)v_n = n(n+2)/2 · v_n, since ef·v_n = [e,f]·v_n = n·v_n. This equals (λ + 2ρ, λ) for λ = nω₁, ρ = ω₁, confirming the general eigenvalue formula in the atomic case [1].

## 6 Limitations

The theory above is sharp, but its hypotheses are load-bearing, and each relaxation opens a major research area:

1. **Characteristic zero is essential.** In characteristic p > 0, complete reducibility fails, the Killing form may degenerate, and the representation theory of the corresponding algebraic groups requires the theory of *Weyl modules*, *Steinberg's tensor product theorem*, and Lusztig's conjectural character formula — much of it still open in small characteristics [3].
2. **Finite-dimensionality is essential.** The Verma modules M(λ) for non-dominant λ are infinite-dimensional, and their structure is governed by the BGG resolution and Kazhdan–Lusztig theory. The natural home of all highest weight modules is **Bernstein–Gelfand–Gelfand category 𝒪**, where projective covers, tilting modules, and Koszul duality replace semisimplicity [7].
3. **The Weyl character formula counts, but does not construct.** It gives multiplicities without exhibiting bases; explicit *crystal bases* (Kashiwara) and *canonical bases* (Lusztig) address the constructive gap combinatorially [9].
4. **Beyond finite type.** Replacing the Cartan matrix by a generalized one yields **Kac–Moody algebras**; the Weyl–Kac character formula extends the theory to integrable highest weight modules, but imaginary roots and the absence of a finite Weyl group make the combinatorics vastly richer [8].
5. **Geometric hypotheses.** Borel–Weil–Bott requires the flag variety over ℂ (or characteristic zero); the modular analogue involves Frobenius splittings and remains subtle.

## 7 Conclusion

The representation theory of complex semisimple Lie algebras achieves something rare in mathematics: a *complete, explicit, and computable* classification. From the nondegeneracy of the Killing form through the combinatorics of root systems and the Cartan classification, the theory funnels an infinite-dimensional problem — all representations of a continuous symmetry object — into finite Weyl-group combinatorics: dominant integral weights parametrize the irreducibles, the Weyl character formula computes their characters, the dimension formula counts their size, Freudenthal's recursion fills in the weight multiplicities, and Borel–Weil–Bott realizes each one as the cohomology of a line bundle on the flag variety [1][4][6].

The unity of the algebraic, combinatorial, and geometric perspectives is the theory's deepest lesson. The same integer ⟨λ, α∨⟩ appears as an 𝔰𝔩₂-eigenvalue, as a Dynkin label, and as the degree of a line bundle on a rational curve in the flag variety; the same Weyl group governs reflections of roots, symmetries of characters, and the cohomology degrees in Bott's theorem. For the working mathematician or physicist, the practical takeaway is equally clear: questions about representations of classical groups — tensor product decompositions, branching rules, Casimir spectra — are, in principle, finite computations, as Section 5 demonstrates. The frontier has moved to where these rigid structures break: modular representations, infinite-dimensional highest weight theory, and categorification.
