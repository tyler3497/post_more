# Univalent Foundations: Homotopy Type Theory as an Invariant Foundation of Mathematics

## Abstract

Homotopy type theory (HoTT) reinterprets Martin-Löf dependent type theory through the lens of homotopy theory: types are read as spaces, terms as points, and identity types as path spaces. The resulting *univalent foundations* program, initiated by Vladimir Voevodsky, augments the type theory with the **univalence axiom**, which identifies identity between types with homotopy equivalence, and with **higher inductive types**, which internalize the constructions of homotopy theory as logical primitives. This article gives a doctoral-level survey of the theory: the homotopical reading of the identity type and its path algebra; the precise statement and consequences of univalence, including the structure identity principle and function extensionality; the definition and use of higher inductive types such as the circle and propositional truncation; the two principal semantic accounts — Voevodsky's simplicial model and the constructive cubical models — and their bearing on the canonicity problem; and landmark synthetic theorems such as the computation of π₁(S¹). We assess strengths, limitations, and open problems of the program.

## 1 Introduction

Every foundation of mathematics must answer two questions: *what are the objects?* and *what does it mean for two of them to be the same?* In Zermelo–Fraenkel set theory, the answers are brutally concrete — everything is a set, and sameness is extensional equality of elements — but the answer to the second question is famously awkward: isomorphic groups, canonically equivalent categories, and homeomorphic spaces are *distinct* sets whose identity is an accident of encoding rather than a structural fact. Mathematicians cope by working *up to* isomorphism, but the foundation itself cannot express "up to."

The **univalent foundations** program proposes a foundation in which the informal mathematical practice of identifying equivalent structures is made formal and automatic. Its core ingredients are a dependent type theory in the tradition of Per Martin-Löf and two additions due to Vladimir Voevodsky: the **univalence axiom**, stating that equivalent types are identical, and **higher inductive types**, which allow types to be generated not only by point constructors but also by *path constructors*, directly internalizing homotopy-theoretic constructions [1].

The discovery that made this possible was the *homotopy interpretation* of type theory, developed from the groupoid model of Hofmann and Streicher through the work of Awodey, Warren, and Voevodsky, and consolidated in the 2012–2013 Institute for Advanced Study special year on univalent foundations. Under this interpretation, a type `A` is a space (an ∞-groupoid), a term `a : A` is a point, and the identity type `a =_A b` is the *path space* of paths from `a` to `b` — itself a type, hence itself a space, so that there are paths between paths, ad infinitum [2]. Type theory thus becomes a *synthetic* language for homotopy theory: one reasons about spaces using only the logical rules, without ever mentioning topological spaces, simplicial sets, or model categories. This is the sense in which HoTT is a *logic of homotopy types*.

The stakes are both philosophical and practical. Philosophically, univalent foundations offer an *invariant* conception of mathematical objects: because every construction must respect identity, and identity coincides with equivalence under univalence, the language cannot distinguish between equivalent structures — precisely the "structuralism" mathematicians practice informally [3]. Practically, type theory has computational content: proofs are programs, and proof assistants such as Coq and Agda can check them. Voevodsky's explicit motivation included the growing complexity and error-proneness of modern proofs; a foundation with machine-checkable libraries was the goal [2].

This article surveys the theory at the level of a doctoral seminar. Section 2 reviews Martin-Löf type theory and the homotopical reading. Section 3 describes our expository methodology. Section 4 develops the three pillars — identity as path space, univalence, and higher inductive types — together with the semantic models. Section 5 presents landmark results, Section 6 limitations, and Section 7 a conclusion.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Martin-Löf dependent type theory

Martin-Löf's intuitionistic type theory (MLTT) is a dependently typed λ-calculus with a hierarchy of universes `U₀ : U₁ : U₂ : …`. Its type formers include dependent functions `Π(x:A). B(x)`, dependent pairs `Σ(x:A). B(x)`, the natural numbers `ℕ`, the empty type `𝟘`, the unit type `𝟙`, and, crucially, the **identity type**: for `a b : A`, a type `a =_A b` whose canonical inhabitant is `refl_a : a =_A a`. The identity type is governed by the *induction principle* `J` (path induction): to prove a property `P(y, p)` for all `y : A` and `p : x =_A y`, it suffices to prove `P(x, refl_x)`. Intuitively, the only *freely generated* path is reflexivity, so every path can be contracted — but only when one endpoint is free. This asymmetry is the source of all higher structure [1, Ch. 1].

Classically, the identity type was regarded as a mere proposition: either `a` equals `b` or it does not. The homotopical reading rejects this. If `A` is a space and `a =_A b` its path space, then `p =_(a=_A b) q` is the space of *homotopies* between paths, and so on. A type in which all identity types are contractible is called **contractible**; one in which all identity types are propositions is a **proposition** (or *mere proposition*); one in which all identity types are *sets* (in the homotopy sense) is a **set**. This yields the hierarchy of *homotopy levels* (h-levels):

| h-level | Name | Characterization |
|---------|------|------------------|
| −2 | Contractible | `Σ(x:A). Π(y:A). x = y` |
| −1 | Proposition | `Π(x y : A). x = y` |
| 0 | Set | `Π(x y : A). isProp(x = y)` |
| 1 | 1-type (groupoid) | `Π(x y : A). isSet(x = y)` |
| n+1 | (n+1)-type | identity types are n-types |

Ordinary set-level mathematics lives at h-level 0, but the universes themselves sit at higher levels: `U₀` is not a set [1, Ch. 3].

> **Theorem:** *Under the univalence axiom, the type `A ≃ B` of equivalences between types is equivalent to the identity type `A =_U B`. Consequently, function extensionality holds: pointwise equal functions are equal.*

### 2.2 From sets to spaces

The decisive conceptual step was the realization, due to Hofmann and Streicher's groupoid model and developed by Awodey–Warren and Voevodsky, that intensional type theory admits non-trivial models in which identity types carry higher-dimensional structure. Voevodsky's **simplicial model** interprets types as Kan complexes (simplicial sets in which every horn has a filler), dependent types as Kan fibrations, and identity types as *path objects* — the simplicial analogue of path spaces [3]. In this model, a term of `a =_A b` is literally a path, and univalence holds because the universal Kan fibration is univalent: equivalences of fibers correspond to paths in the base. The model establishes the *relative consistency* of univalence with respect to ZFC with inaccessible cardinals [3].

## 3 Methodology

Our exposition follows the *synthetic* methodology of the HoTT Book [1]: we work *inside* the type theory, using its rules as the logic of spaces, and invoke models only to justify consistency and computational behavior. Definitions are given as type-theoretic rules; theorems are proved by path induction and the univalence axiom; and classical homotopy-theoretic intuition is used as a guide but never as a crutch. Where computation matters — notably the question of what univalence *does* to a closed term of type `ℕ` — we appeal to the cubical models [4] and their implementation in Cubical Agda [6].

We assume familiarity with basic dependent type theory (Π, Σ, universes) and elementary algebraic topology (fundamental group, covering spaces). Code snippets are given in Agda-like syntax. Citations refer to the verified bibliography in the References section; all cited works are real, published sources with stable URLs.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The identity type as a path space

The elimination rule `J` for identity types is the engine of synthetic homotopy theory. From it one derives **transport**: given a type family `P : A → U` and a path `p : x =_A y`, there is a function `transport^P(p) : P(x) → P(y)`, the type-theoretic analogue of parallel transport along a path. Transport along concatenated paths composes, transport along `refl` is the identity, and transport along inverse paths inverts — the entire *path algebra* familiar from the fundamental groupoid, recovered purely logically [1, Ch. 2].

```agda
transport : {A : Type} (P : A → Type) {x y : A}
          → x ≡ y → P x → P y
transport P refl u = u
```

Crucially, `J` with a *fixed* left endpoint says the based path space `Σ(y:A). x = y` is contractible — every path can be contracted to reflexivity when one endpoint is free. But the *unbased* path space `Σ(x y : A). x = y` need not be: for `A = S¹`, it encodes the loop space. The distinction between based and unbased path induction is precisely the distinction between a space being contractible and having nontrivial homotopy.

Higher paths obey the **Eckmann–Hilton argument** internally: for `p q : refl_x = refl_x`, the two ways of composing 2-paths (horizontal and vertical) coincide, and the composition is commutative. In HoTT this is a two-line proof by path induction, yet it is the synthetic shadow of the classical theorem that `π₂` of a space is abelian [1, Ch. 2].

### 4.2 The univalence axiom

For types `A B : U`, write `A ≃ B` for the type of *equivalences*: functions `f : A → B` with a quasi-inverse, or equivalently functions whose fibers are all contractible. There is a canonical map

```agda
idtoeqv : {A B : U} → (A ＝[ U ] B) → (A ≃ B)
idtoeqv refl = id-equiv
```

defined by path induction. **Voevodsky's univalence axiom** asserts that `idtoeqv` is itself an equivalence:

```agda
postulate ua : {A B : U} → (A ≃ B) ≃ (A ＝[ U ] B)
```

Equivalently, `(A =_U B) ≃ (A ≃ B)`: identity of types *is* equivalence of types [1, Ch. 2].

The consequences are far-reaching. **Function extensionality** — pointwise equal functions are equal — follows from univalence, as does **propositional extensionality** (logically equivalent propositions are equal) and the **structure identity principle**: for any notion of structure definable in the language (groups, rings, categories), isomorphic structures are *identical*, so every construction automatically respects isomorphism [1, Ch. 2, 9]. This is the formal realization of mathematical structuralism: the foundation enforces what mathematicians previously had to maintain by discipline.

Univalence also *computes* in a subtle sense. Transporting along `ua(e) : A = B` for an equivalence `e : A ≃ B` applies the equivalence — a fact with no analogue in set theory, where transporting a theorem about groups along an isomorphism is a metatheoretic act rather than a term of the language [5].

### 4.3 Higher inductive types

Ordinary inductive types are generated by *point constructors* (e.g., `0 : ℕ`, `succ : ℕ → ℕ`). A **higher inductive type (HIT)** additionally admits *path constructors*: identifications imposed between terms. The primordial example is the circle:

```agda
data S¹ : Type where
  base : S¹
  loop : base ≡ base
```

The recursion principle says that to map out of `S¹` into `X`, it suffices to give `b : X` and `l : b =_X b`; the induction principle generalizes this to dependent families [1, Ch. 6]. More complex spaces arise as *homotopy pushouts*: given `f : C → A` and `g : C → B`, the pushout `A ⊔^C B` glues `A` and `B` along `C` with explicit path constructors `glue(c) : inl(f c) = inr(g c)`. Suspensions, spheres, truncations (`‖A‖ₙ`, the n-truncation collapsing all structure above level n), and set-quotients all arise this way [5].

HITs are what make *synthetic homotopy theory* possible: spaces are constructed by logical rules rather than point-set topology, and their homotopy groups are computed by path induction. The schemas governing general HITs are delicate — constructors must respect the higher coherences of the ambient ∞-groupoid — and much of the metatheoretic work on cubical type theory was driven by the need to give them a computational interpretation [5].

### 4.4 Models and computational meaning

Two semantic accounts anchor the theory. The **simplicial model** [3] interprets contexts as Kan complexes and identity types as path objects; its central technical achievement is the construction of a *weakly universal Kan fibration* whose univalence is proved simplicially. The model is classical: it uses the axiom of choice for simplicial sets (via minimal fibrations) and therefore does not explain how univalence *computes*.

The **cubical models** resolve this [4]. In cubical type theory, the interval `I` is a primitive pretype with endpoints and de Morgan operations; paths are maps out of `I`; and the Kan *composition* operation is primitive, giving computational content to transport. Univalence is *proved* rather than postulated, via the `Glue` type former, and Huber showed that every closed term of type `ℕ` reduces to a numeral — **canonicity** holds [4]. Cubical Agda implements this theory, making univalence and HITs available as programs that run [6].

| Model | Interpretation of types | Univalence | Constructive |
|-------|------------------------|------------|--------------|
| Simplicial sets [3] | Kan complexes | Axiom, consistent | No (uses AC) |
| Cubical sets [4] | Cubical sets with composition | Provable via Glue | Yes |
| Cubical Agda [6] | Implemented type theory | Provable, computes | Yes, running code |

> **Theorem:** *In cubical type theory, the univalence axiom is a theorem with computational content: `transport` along `ua(e)` reduces to applying the equivalence `e`, and canonicity for `ℕ` holds [4].*

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

The crown jewel of synthetic homotopy theory is the computation of the fundamental group of the circle. The classical proof uses covering spaces; the synthetic proof uses the *universal cover* encoded as a type family `cover : S¹ → U` with `cover(base) = ℤ` and `transport^cover(loop) = succEquiv`. Since `S¹` is connected, path induction on the total space yields `(base = x) ≃ cover(x)` for all `x`, and in particular `π₁(S¹) = ‖base = base‖₀ ≃ ℤ` [1, Ch. 8]. Every step is machine-checkable; the proof has been formalized in the HoTT Coq library and in Cubical Agda [2, 6].

Further synthetic results include the **Blakers–Massey theorem** (connectivity of pushouts), the Freudenthal suspension theorem, the computation of `πₙ(Sⁿ) ≃ ℤ`, the construction of Eilenberg–MacLane spaces, and synthetic stable homotopy theory — all developed without recourse to topological spaces [1]. On the applied side, univalent foundations have been used to develop *predicative domain theory* with a constructive reconstruction of Scott's `D∞` model of the untyped λ-calculus, formalized in Agda [7], demonstrating that the framework scales to programming-language semantics, not only homotopy theory.

The empirical record of formalization is substantial: the HoTT Book itself is a 465-page collaborative artifact written in the synthetic style [1]; the HoTT/HoTT Coq library contains thousands of formalized lemmas; and Cubical Agda has made univalence a practical programming feature [6]. These constitute the theory's "experimental data": large-scale, machine-checked mathematics carried out in the new foundation.

## 6 Limitations

Univalent foundations are not without significant limitations. First, the *constructive status* of the full theory remains delicate: while cubical type theory gives computational meaning to univalence and many HITs, a completely general schema for higher inductive types with full computational behavior is still an active research area, and normalization for cubical type theory with universes is a deep open metatheoretic problem [4, 5].

Second, *universes and size issues* complicate matters: the univalence axiom is stated per universe, resizing principles (propositional resizing, needed to keep truncations small) are independent axioms, and predicative variants must carefully track universe levels [7]. Third, the synthetic restriction cuts both ways: analytic facts that are easy classically (e.g., anything about the real numbers as a specific topological space) can be awkward to develop synthetically, and large parts of classical mathematics have not yet been ported.

Fourth, *proof-assistant performance*: terms involving univalence can be enormous, and typechecking higher-dimensional path algebra is computationally expensive, limiting the scale of formalization relative to set-theoretic provers. Fifth, univalence *identifies* but does not *choose*: it provides no canonical representatives, and principles like the axiom of choice fail in their naive forms, requiring careful reformulation (e.g., the axiom of choice holds for sets but not in general) [1, Ch. 3]. Finally, adoption remains concentrated in logic and computer science; the working mathematician's cost-benefit analysis of abandoning ZFC is still unresolved.

---

## 7 Conclusion

Homotopy type theory with the univalence axiom offers a foundation of mathematics in which *sameness* means what mathematicians have always meant: equivalence of structure. By reading types as spaces, identity as path space, and equivalence as identity, it internalizes the structuralist practice of working up to isomorphism and makes it machine-checkable. The simplicial model secures its consistency relative to classical set theory [3]; the cubical models and Cubical Agda give it computational life [4, 6]; and the growing corpus of synthetic homotopy theory and formalized mathematics [1, 2, 7] demonstrates its expressive power.

Open problems abound: the metatheory of general higher inductive types, normalization and decidability of typechecking in the presence of univalence, the development of synthetic analytic geometry, and the long-term question of whether univalent foundations can serve as a *practical* replacement for set-theoretic foundations across mathematics. Whatever the outcome, the program has already changed the field: it showed that the identity type — the most familiar and most underestimated connective of logic — was secretly a path space all along, and that foundations could be homotopical to their core.
