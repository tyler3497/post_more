# Graph and Partition Indexes for Billion-Scale Approximate Nearest Neighbor Search: HNSW, IVF-PQ, and DiskANN

## Abstract

Approximate nearest neighbor (ANN) search is the engine beneath vector databases, retrieval-augmented generation, and billion-scale image retrieval. Exact search collapses under the curse of dimensionality, so modern systems trade a small loss of recall for orders-of-magnitude gains in latency and memory. This thesis surveys the three index families that dominate billion-scale practice: **partition-and-quantization indexes** (IVF-PQ/IVFADC, optimized product quantization), **hierarchical navigable small-world graphs** (HNSW), and **SSD-resident graph indexes** (DiskANN and its Vamana construction). We develop the mathematical foundations — quantization distortion and asymmetric distance computation, navigable small-world theory, and graph pruning heuristics — and explain the search procedures behind millisecond-latency retrieval: multi-probe inverted lists, layered greedy routing with beam width *ef*, and SSD-backed beam search. We report benchmark behavior on SIFT1M, DEEP1B, and the BigANN billion-point datasets, including the NeurIPS 2021 billion-scale ANN challenge, and map the memory–latency–recall tradeoff surface. We close by analyzing limitations: index build cost, the graph memory wall, out-of-distribution queries, and the lack of worst-case guarantees for greedy graph routing.

## 1 Introduction

Given a corpus $X = \{x_1, \dots, x_n\} \subset \mathbb{R}^d$ of $n$ vectors — *embeddings* produced by neural networks, image descriptors, or text representations — and a query $q \in \mathbb{R}^d$, the **$k$-nearest neighbor ($k$-NN) problem** asks for the $k$ points of $X$ minimizing a distance $\rho(q, x)$, typically Euclidean distance or negative inner product. When $n$ reaches $10^9$ and $d$ ranges from 96 to 4096, exact search by linear scan costs $O(nd)$ distance computations per query: on SIFT1B, roughly a trillion floating-point operations, far beyond any interactive latency budget. Tree-based exact indexes (kd-trees, ball trees) degenerate to near-linear scans in high dimensions — the *curse of dimensionality* — because partition boundaries become meaningless when distances concentrate.

The field therefore embraced **approximation**. An algorithm returns $k$ candidates $S$; its quality is measured by **recall@$k$**,

$$\text{recall}@k(q) = \frac{|S \cap T_k(q)|}{k},$$

where $T_k(q)$ is the true $k$-neighborhood. Recall@$k$ is averaged over a query workload. Practical systems target recall@$k \in [0.9, 1.0]$ while maximizing **queries per second (QPS)** and minimizing **mean latency** and **index memory footprint**. This thesis studies the three architectures that define the modern Pareto frontier [1][3][5]:

1. **IVF-PQ / IVFADC** — inverted-file partitioning with product-quantized residuals [2][6], the memory-frugal workhorse of FAISS [4].
2. **HNSW** — hierarchical navigable small-world graphs [1], the recall–latency champion of in-memory search.
3. **DiskANN (Vamana)** — SSD-resident graphs with beam search [3], the density champion that broke the in-memory barrier.

These families are not mutually exclusive: DiskANN stores PQ-compressed vectors in DRAM and the full graph on SSD, and FAISS ships both IVF-PQ and HNSW indexes. Understanding each in depth is the prerequisite for choosing among them.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 From LSH to the modern frontier

Early ANN relied on **locality-sensitive hashing** (LSH) and randomized kd-trees (FLANN). LSH offers rigorous probabilistic guarantees, but its space partitioning is data-oblivious, and achieving high recall requires probing many hash tables — expensive in memory. Two data-*dependent* ideas changed the field. First, **clustering-based partitioning** (inverted files): learn a coarse quantizer (e.g., $k$-means with $K$ = 4096–1M centroids), assign each database vector to its nearest centroid, and at query time scan only the $P$ nearest cells (*nprobe*). Second, **vector quantization for compression**: represent each vector by a short code so that a billion vectors fit in tens of gigabytes of RAM, with distance estimated directly in the compressed domain.

> **Theorem (Johnson–Lindenstrauss, informal):** Any set of $n$ points in high dimension can be embedded into $O(\log n)$ dimensions with $(1 \pm \epsilon)$ distortion of pairwise distances. ANN does not require preserving the ambient geometry exactly — only *relative neighborhood structure* — which is why compressed and graph-approximated indexes can reach 0.99+ recall.

### 2.2 Product quantization

Product quantization (PQ), introduced by Jégou, Douze, and Schmid [2], decomposes $\mathbb{R}^d$ into a Cartesian product of $M$ subspaces, $\mathbb{R}^d = \mathbb{R}^{d_1} \times \cdots \times \mathbb{R}^{d_M}$ (usually $d_m = d/M$), and quantizes each subspace independently with its own codebook $C_m = \{c_{m,1}, \dots, c_{m,K}\}$, $K = 256$. A vector $x$ is encoded as the $M$ indices

$$\text{code}(x) = (i_1, \dots, i_M), \quad i_m = \arg\min_j \|x^{(m)} - c_{m,j}\|^2,$$

consuming $M \log_2 K$ bits — e.g., 8 bytes for $M = 8$, $K = 256$ on 128-d SIFT. The codebook $C = C_1 \times \cdots \times C_M$ implicitly contains $K^M$ centroids ($256^8 \approx 1.8 \times 10^{19}$) while training only $M \cdot K$ sub-codewords: the Cartesian structure is what makes PQ exponentially expressive.

Distance estimation avoids decompression. In **asymmetric distance computation (ADC)**, the query $q$ stays uncompressed: for each subspace $m$, precompute a lookup table $T_m[j] = \|q^{(m)} - c_{m,j}\|^2$, then

$$\hat{d}^2(q, x) \approx \sum_{m=1}^{M} T_m[i_m].$$

ADC costs $O(M)$ table lookups per candidate — dramatically cheaper than $O(d)$ float operations — and is more accurate than symmetric (code-to-code) comparison because the query quantization error is eliminated [2].

### 2.3 Quantization distortion and optimized PQ

PQ assumes subspaces are statistically independent — rarely true in practice. The distortion $\mathbb{E}\|x - q(x)\|^2$ is minimized when subspaces are balanced and decorrelated. **Optimized PQ (OPQ)** [6] learns an orthonormal rotation $R \in O(d)$ applied before quantization, minimizing

$$\min_{R \in O(d),\, C} \; \mathbb{E}\big\|Rx - q_C(Rx)\big\|^2,$$

via alternating optimization (a nonparametric variant and a Gaussian-parametric variant with a closed-form solution). OPQ is equivalent to **Cartesian $k$-means** (Norouzi & Fleet, 2013) and typically cuts distortion by 20–40% on SIFT/GIST, translating directly into recall gains at fixed code length.

### 2.4 Inverted files: IVFADC

**IVFADC** [2] combines the two ideas: a coarse quantizer partitions $X$ into `nlist` Voronoi cells; within each cell, the *residual* $r = x - c_{\text{cell}(x)}$ is encoded with PQ (residuals are smaller-norm and more concentrated, hence easier to quantize). Search visits the `nprobe` nearest cells and scans their PQ codes with ADC. The `nprobe`/`nlist` ratio is the recall–speed dial: `nprobe = nlist` degenerates to a full scan, while `nprobe = 1` visits one cell. FAISS's `IndexIVFPQ` remains the default billion-scale CPU index [4], with IVFADC+G+P variants (grouping plus polysemous codes) still competitive on memory-constrained tracks.

### 2.5 Small-world graphs and navigability

A parallel lineage builds **proximity graphs**: vertices are database vectors, edges connect near neighbors, and search is greedy graph traversal — from an entry point, repeatedly move to the unvisited neighbor closest to the query. Kleinberg showed that on a $d$-dimensional lattice augmented with long-range edges drawn with probability $\propto r^{-\alpha}$, greedy routing finds targets in $O(\log^2 n)$ hops when $\alpha = d$ — the *navigable small world*. Malkov et al. observed that incrementally built **navigable small-world (NSW)** graphs, where each inserted point connects to its approximate nearest neighbors, approximate the Delaunay graph and are empirically navigable. HNSW [1] fixed NSW's fatal flaw — unbounded degree growth that made hubs expensive — by imposing a layered hierarchy.

## 3 Methodology

We compare the three index families along a unified evaluation methodology drawn from the ANN-Benchmarks protocol [7] and the NeurIPS 2021 billion-scale challenge [5]:

- **Metrics.** Recall@$k$ (usually $k \in \{1, 10, 100\}$) versus QPS and mean/p99 latency; memory footprint in bytes per vector; index build time. The BigANN tracks additionally measure **Joules per query** and the dollar cost of replicating a 100k-QPS fleet.
- **Datasets.** *SIFT1M* (10⁶ 128-d SIFT descriptors; the classic sanity check), *SIFT1B/BIGANN* (10⁹ SIFT vectors, uint8), *DEEP1B* (10⁹ 96-d GoogleNet descriptors [2][5]), and the six NeurIPS'21 tracks (BIGANN, SimSearchNet++, Turing ANNS, SpaceV, Text-to-Image, DEEP), which vary modality, dimensionality, distance function (L2 vs. inner product), and query distribution — including out-of-distribution cross-modal queries [5].
- **Parameters swept.** For IVF-PQ: `nlist`, `nprobe`, $M$, $K$. For HNSW: $M$ (max degree), `efConstruction`, `ef` (search candidate-list size). For DiskANN: $R$ (max degree), $L$ (build beam width), $\alpha$ (pruning threshold), $w$ (search beam width).
- **Analytical tools.** Quantization distortion bounds for PQ/OPQ [2][6]; degree and hop-count analysis for layered graphs [1]; I/O-complexity analysis (SSD reads per query) for DiskANN [3].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Product quantization and asymmetric distance computation

PQ's power lies in the lookup-table trick. With $M = 8$ subquantizers and $K = 256$ centroids each, per-query table construction costs $8 \times 256 = 2048$ distance computations — amortized over millions of candidates. Each ADC evaluation is 8 table lookups and 7 additions. On SIFT1M with 64-bit codes, PQ+ADC reaches ~0.9 recall@100 at millions of candidates scanned per second per core [2].

The encoding itself is a nearest-centroid search per subspace ($M$ small $k$-means assignments), and codebook training is $M$ independent $k$-means runs — embarrassingly parallel and linear in $n$. The distortion decomposes additively:

$$\mathbb{E}\|x - q(x)\|^2 = \sum_{m=1}^{M} \mathbb{E}\|x^{(m)} - q_m(x^{(m)})\|^2,$$

so each subspace can be optimized independently — but this is also PQ's weakness: cross-subspace correlation inflates the sum, motivating OPQ's global rotation [6].

```python
import numpy as np

def adc_distances(query, codes, codebooks):
    """Asymmetric distance computation.
    query: (d,) float32; codes: (n, M) uint8; codebooks: (M, K, d_sub)
    returns approximate squared distances, shape (n,)
    """
    M, K, d_sub = codebooks.shape
    q_sub = query.reshape(M, d_sub)                      # (M, d_sub)
    tables = ((q_sub[:, None, :] - codebooks) ** 2).sum(-1)  # (M, K) lookup tables
    return tables[np.arange(M)[:, None], codes.T].sum(0)    # gather + sum
```

### 4.2 IVF-PQ, IVFADC, and OPQ in practice

In IVFADC, the coarse quantizer (typically $2^{14}$–$2^{20}$ centroids trained by $k$-means on a sample) partitions the space; residuals are PQ-encoded with OPQ rotation learned per index. FAISS trains the coarse quantizer, then the OPQ matrix, then the PQ codebooks on residuals [4]. Query flow:

1. Find the `nprobe` nearest coarse centroids (a small exact search over centroids).
2. For each probed cell, rotate the query residual and build ADC lookup tables once per cell.
3. Scan the cell's PQ codes with table lookups; keep a $k$-sized heap.

Refinements include **IVFADC+R** (store a few bytes of residual refinement), **polysemous codes** (binary codes derived from PQ for Hamming-space pre-filtering), and the **inverted multi-index** (product of two coarse codebooks giving $K^2$ cells). Memory per vector is dominated by the PQ code (8–64 bytes) plus ~4 bytes of ID — a billion 128-d float vectors (512 GB raw) compress to ~12–70 GB, fitting a single server's DRAM [2][4].

> **Theorem (ADC error bound, informal):** If each subquantizer has distortion $D_m$, then $|\hat{d}^2(q,x) - d^2(q,x)| \le 2\|q\|\sqrt{\sum_m D_m} + \sum_m D_m$. ADC error is controlled by quantization distortion — which is exactly what OPQ minimizes [6].

### 4.3 HNSW: layered navigable small-world graphs

HNSW [1] builds a hierarchy of proximity graphs. Each element $x$ is assigned a maximum layer

$$\ell(x) = \big\lfloor -\ln(U) \cdot m_L \big\rfloor, \qquad U \sim \text{Uniform}(0,1),$$

with $m_L = 1/\ln M$ ($M$ the target degree). The assignment is exponentially decaying: most points live only on layer 0, a few reach high layers — precisely the skip-list distribution, but over a graph. Layer $\ell$ contains all points with $\ell(x) \ge \ell$; higher layers are sparser and their edges span longer characteristic distances, giving the scale separation that NSW lacked.

**Insertion** proceeds top-down: greedy search descends from the entry point through upper layers (1-NN search per layer, `ef = 1`), then on each layer $\le \ell(x)$ collects `efConstruction` candidates and selects neighbors with the **diversity heuristic** (`SELECT-NEIGHBORS-HEURISTIC`): candidates are added in order of increasing distance, but a candidate $e$ is skipped if it is closer to an already-selected neighbor than to $x$ (scaled by $\alpha$). This *relative neighborhood graph* pruning keeps edges that cover different directions — long-range shortcuts survive alongside short-range links — which is what makes the graph navigable at high recall [1].

**Search** mirrors insertion: descend upper layers greedily, then run a best-first beam search on layer 0 with candidate list size `ef` (the `efSearch` parameter), maintaining the $k$ best. Raising `ef` monotonically trades latency for recall; `efConstruction` (typically 100–500) trades build time for graph quality. Degree is capped at $M$ per layer ($2M$ at layer 0), so each node stores at most a few dozen 4-byte neighbor IDs — HNSW's memory is dominated by the raw vectors plus ~$M \times 4$ bytes of edges per node.

| Parameter | Typical value | Effect of increasing |
|---|---|---|
| `M` (max degree) | 16–64 | ↑ recall, ↑ memory, ↑ build time |
| `efConstruction` | 100–500 | ↑ graph quality, ↑ build time |
| `ef` (search) | 10–500 | ↑ recall, ↓ QPS (smooth knob) |

HNSW's empirical claim to fame is its recall–latency curve: on SIFT1M it sustains >10k QPS at 0.99 recall@10 on a single thread, and it generalized to arbitrary metric spaces (cosine, inner product) because it never assumes vector structure [1]. The reference implementation, `hnswlib`, and FAISS's `IndexHNSW` made it the default in-memory index across the industry.

### 4.4 DiskANN and Vamana: graphs that live on SSD

In-memory graphs hit a wall: HNSW on a billion 128-d vectors needs ~0.5 TB of DRAM for vectors plus edges — a multi-thousand-dollar machine. **DiskANN** [3] asked whether an SSD-resident index could match in-memory latency. The answer required co-designing the graph, the search, and the storage layout:

- **Vamana graph.** Construction starts from a random graph and iteratively refines it with a **robust prune** heuristic: for node $p$ with candidate set $C$, keep neighbor $v \in C$ only if no already-kept neighbor $p'$ satisfies $\alpha \cdot d(p', v) < d(p, v)$ (with $\alpha \ge 1$; $\alpha = 1$ recovers relative-neighborhood pruning). The $\alpha$ parameter controls the *density–diversity* tradeoff: larger $\alpha$ keeps more long-range edges, improving navigability at the cost of degree. Max degree $R$ (typically 64–128) bounds the on-disk footprint per node.
- **Beam search.** Search uses a beam of width $w$: expand the $w$ closest unvisited candidates, following edges, in a best-first manner reminiscent of HNSW's layer-0 search but with a wider frontier to tolerate SSD-latency-optimized batching.
- **Two-tier storage.** PQ-compressed vectors (a few bytes each) live in DRAM and are used for *approximate* distance during traversal; full-precision vectors and adjacency lists live on SSD in a blocked layout so that each node's data is fetched in a single random 4 KB read. A final **re-rank** step fetches full-precision vectors for the top candidates and recomputes exact distances. Beam search issues SSD reads in parallel batches, hiding latency behind queue depth.

The headline result [3]: on SIFT1B, DiskANN serves **>5,000 QPS at <3 ms mean latency with 95%+ recall@1 on a 16-core machine with 64 GB RAM and a commodity SSD** — where FAISS and IVFADC+G+P with comparable memory plateaued near 50% recall@1. In the high-recall regime, DiskANN indexes 5–10× more points per node than HNSW or NSG. Vamana also proved competitive as a pure in-memory graph, and its ideas (notably $\alpha$-pruning) were absorbed into later graph indexes.

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 Benchmark landscape

| Dataset | Size | Dim | Type | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| SIFT1M | 10⁶ | 128 | uint8 | Classic small-scale sanity check |
| SIFT1B / BIGANN | 10⁹ | 128 | uint8 | Billion-scale SIFT descriptors [5] |
| DEEP1B | 10⁹ | 96 | float32 | GoogleNet descriptors, highly clustered |
| Turing ANNS | 10⁹ | 100 | float32 | Web query similarity [5] |
| Text-to-Image | 10⁹ | 200 | float32 | Cross-modal, out-of-distribution queries [5] |

The **NeurIPS 2021 billion-scale ANN challenge** [5] standardized this evaluation across three tracks: T1 (limited DRAM, FAISS baseline), T2 (limited DRAM + SSD, DiskANN baseline), and T3 (unrestricted hardware). The winning T2 entry, Intel's **OptaNNE GraphNN** (a DiskANN adaptation on Optane persistent memory), reached **0.99978 recall@10 on BIGANN** and 0.99568 on Turing ANNS at the 2,000-QPS minimum-throughput threshold, with 335,991 QPS at 90% recall on BIGANN — roughly 100× the FAISS baseline throughput. On T1, NVIDIA's GPU entries pushed multi-million QPS using IVF-PQ variants [4][5], confirming that partition-and-quantize indexes dominate when raw throughput-per-dollar is the objective, while graph indexes dominate the high-recall, low-latency corner.

### 5.2 The tradeoff surface

Three empirical regularities emerge across benchmarks [1][3][5][7]:

1. **Graphs beat partitions at high recall.** Above ~0.95 recall@10, HNSW and DiskANN/Vamana achieve 2–10× the QPS of IVF-PQ at equal memory, because graph traversal follows the data manifold instead of probing geometrically arbitrary Voronoi cells.
2. **Quantization beats graphs on memory.** IVF-PQ stores 8–16 bytes per vector; HNSW stores the full vector plus edges (often 500+ bytes per vector at 128-d float32). DiskANN's hybrid — PQ in DRAM, graph on SSD — is the Pareto compromise that made billion-scale graphs economical.
3. **Recall is a smooth knob everywhere.** `nprobe`, `ef`, and beam width $w$ all trade latency for recall monotonically, so systems are tuned by sweeping one parameter along the recall–QPS curve rather than by redesigning the index.

### 5.3 Theoretical grounding

The navigability of these graphs rests on two pillars. First, the **NSW construction approximates the Delaunay graph**: connecting each inserted point to its current nearest neighbors incrementally builds a graph in which greedy routing rarely gets trapped, because Delaunay graphs are exactly the graphs on which greedy routing to a query succeeds [1]. Second, **HNSW's layering gives logarithmic scaling**: the expected number of hops per layer is constant (upper layers are exponentially sparser), and the number of layers is $O(\log n)$, so search costs $O(\log n)$ distance computations in expectation — confirmed empirically up to $10^9$ points [1][3]. For PQ, rate–distortion theory bounds the achievable recall: with $b$ bits per vector, the distortion floor is $\Theta(2^{-2b/d})$ per dimension under Gaussian assumptions, which OPQ approaches by aligning subspaces with the data's principal variance directions [6].

## 6 Limitations

**Build cost.** HNSW insertion is $O(\log n)$ per point but with a large constant (`efConstruction` distance computations); building a billion-point HNSW can take days on a single machine, and the construction is hard to parallelize without degrading graph quality. Vamana's iterative refinement is similarly expensive [1][3].

**The memory wall for graphs.** Even DiskANN keeps PQ codes plus a navigation structure in DRAM, and pure HNSW remains DRAM-bound — a fundamental limitation for trillion-scale corpora, where even 8 bytes per vector exceeds single-node memory.

**No worst-case guarantees.** Greedy graph routing has excellent *average-case* behavior but no worst-case bound: adversarial or highly clustered data can create local minima that trap the search, and recall guarantees are empirical, not theoretical — unlike LSH's probabilistic contracts.

**Out-of-distribution queries.** The Text-to-Image track of the BigANN challenge [5] showed sharp recall degradation when query and database distributions differ (text vs. image embeddings). Both partition boundaries and graph neighborhoods are learned from database statistics; distribution shift breaks both.

**Dynamic workloads.** Insertions are supported (HNSW inserts incrementally; DiskANN supports streaming inserts), but deletions and updates degrade graph quality over time, and rebalancing a partitioned index requires costly re-clustering. Fully dynamic billion-scale ANN remains open.

**Parameter sensitivity.** `M`, `efConstruction`, `ef`, `R`, $L$, $\alpha$, `nprobe` interact nonlinearly; mistuning by 2× can cost an order of magnitude in QPS. Auto-tuning frameworks exist but are themselves expensive.

## 7 Conclusion

Billion-scale approximate nearest neighbor search is a solved problem in the engineering sense and an open problem in the theoretical sense. The three index families surveyed here — **IVF-PQ** with its Cartesian codebooks and asymmetric distance computation [2][6], **HNSW** with its skip-list-inspired layered navigability and diversity-pruned neighborhoods [1], and **DiskANN/Vamana** with its $\alpha$-pruned SSD-resident graphs and batched beam search [3] — collectively span the memory–latency–recall tradeoff surface, as validated by the FAISS ecosystem [4] and the NeurIPS 2021 billion-scale challenge [5] against community benchmarks [7]. The practical synthesis is now standard: partition and quantize for memory, traverse graphs for latency, spill to SSD for scale. The remaining frontiers — worst-case navigability guarantees, distribution-robust indexes, and truly dynamic trillion-scale search — will likely require the same kind of co-design, across algorithms, storage hierarchies, and hardware, that made the current generation possible.
