# Silicon Photonic Interconnects: Microring Resonators, Kerr Frequency Combs, and Co-Packaged Optics for Datacenter Fabrics

## Abstract

Hyperscale datacenter fabrics are confronting a hard physical wall: electrical interconnects cannot scale to terabit-per-second switch radix at acceptable energy per bit, and the retimed pluggable optical module, once the workhorse of intra-datacenter links, is itself becoming a power, density, and reliability bottleneck. This thesis develops a unified account of the silicon-photonic technology stack that is displacing it: microring resonator modulators for wavelength-division-multiplexed (WDM) modulation at 200 Gb/s per lane and beyond, dissipative Kerr soliton frequency combs as chip-scale multi-wavelength sources, and co-packaged optics (CPO) as the system architecture that collapses electrical reach to millimeters. We derive the coupled-mode theory of ring modulators and the Lugiato–Lefever description of soliton combs, analyze thermal stabilization and wavelength-locking control loops, and construct a quantitative link budget for a co-packaged 51.2T switch fabric. Surveying demonstrated systems—from Intel's 256 Gb/s/fiber ring-based transmitter to 1 Tb/s DWDM microring arrays with sub −33 dB crosstalk—we show that the remaining obstacles are not physics but engineering: thermal management, fabrication statistics, and ecosystem readiness. The conclusion reached is that microring-plus-comb-plus-CPO is the only credible route to sub-5 pJ/bit optics at 1.6T and 3.2T generations.

## 1 Introduction

The arithmetic of the datacenter is unforgiving. Aggregate switch bandwidth has doubled roughly every two years, from 3.2 Tb/s to 51.2 Tb/s merchant silicon in a decade, while the energy efficiency of serializer/deserializer (SerDes) circuits has improved far more slowly [2]. Electrical links beyond ~1 m at 112 Gb/s PAM4 already consume several picojoules per bit merely moving data across a printed circuit board; pluggable optical modules then re-drive those bits at a further 15–20 W per 800G module. At the 51.2T switch generation, front-panel optics demand dozens of modules whose combined power, faceplate area, and failure rate threaten the viability of continued scaling.

Three photonic technologies, developed largely independently, converge on this problem:

1. **Microring resonator (MRR) modulators** — micrometer-scale wavelength-selective devices that modulate a single WDM channel with femtojoule-scale switching energy and near-zero footprint, enabling dense DWDM on one waveguide [1].
2. **Dissipative Kerr soliton frequency combs** — chip-scale sources that replace an array of discrete lasers with a single pumped microresonator emitting hundreds of phase-coherent carriers on a fixed grid [5][4].
3. **Co-packaged optics** — the relocation of the optical engine from the faceplate into the switch package, eliminating the pluggable module and its DSP, and cutting electrical reach from meters to millimeters.

The thesis of this work is that these three are not merely complementary but *co-dependent*: the microring's extreme wavelength selectivity demands a stabilized multi-wavelength source that only a comb can supply economically; the comb's low per-line power demands the short, low-loss optical path that only co-packaging provides; and co-packaging's thermal environment demands the closed-loop wavelength locking that ring architectures make natural. We develop each layer and their coupling in full.

> **Central claim:** Sub-5 pJ/bit interconnect at 1.6T–3.2T per port is achievable only through the joint deployment of microring WDM modulation, Kerr-comb sourcing, and co-packaged integration—no subset of the three closes the link budget.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The end of the pluggable

A conventional 800G DR8 pluggable converts 8×112 Gb/s electrical lanes to 8 optical lanes, each driven by its own laser, through a power-hungry digital signal processor (DSP) that compensates channel impairments electrically. The DSP alone consumes roughly half the module power. Faceplate density is bounded by the QSFP-DD/OSFP form factor: a 51.2T switch requires 64×800G modules, dissipating on the order of a kilowatt in optics at the panel, with airflow and serviceability constraints that grow superlinearly. 

### 2.2 Microring resonators in silicon photonics

A microring resonator is a closed loop of silicon waveguide, typically 5–12 μm in radius, evanescently coupled to a bus waveguide. Light recirculates and interferes constructively only when the round-trip phase is an integer multiple of 2π:

$$\lambda_{res} = \frac{n_{eff} \cdot L}{m}, \qquad m \in \mathbb{Z}$$

where $n_{eff}$ is the effective index and $L$ the circumference. The free spectral range (FSR), $\mathrm{FSR} \approx \lambda^2/(n_g L)$, sets the channel grid; a 12 μm-radius ring yields ~5.7 nm (~1 THz) FSR at 1310 nm, accommodating a 5-channel 200 GHz DWDM bank by slightly detuning each ring's radius [1]. Modulation is achieved by depleting a p–n junction embedded in the ring, shifting $n_{eff}$ and hence the resonance; the steep Lorentzian lineshape converts a small index shift into large extinction.

### 2.3 Kerr frequency combs

When a high-Q microresonator—typically silicon nitride (Si₃N₄) for its low loss and engineerable dispersion—is pumped with a continuous-wave laser above the parametric oscillation threshold, cascaded four-wave mixing generates a frequency comb: hundreds of equidistant lines. Under the right detuning and dispersion conditions, the intracavity field self-organizes into a *dissipative Kerr soliton* (DKS), a circulating pulse whose spectrum is a smooth $\mathrm{sech}^2$ envelope, mutually coherent across the entire span [5]. Octave-spanning and even 1.6-octave combs have been demonstrated on chip, with repetition rates from ~10 GHz to 1 THz set by the resonator FSR [4]. For interconnects, a 50–200 GHz repetition-rate comb provides a ready-made DWDM source grid, phase-locked by construction.

### 2.4 Co-packaged optics

Co-packaged optics places silicon photonic chiplets (photonic integrated circuits, PICs) adjacent to the switch ASIC inside the same package, connected by short-reach electrical links, with fiber attached directly to the package edge. The electrical channel shrinks from ~30 dB-loss PCB traces to a few dB, the pluggable DSP and retimer vanish, and per-bit energy can fall below 5 pJ.

## 3 Methodology

Our analysis proceeds on three coupled levels, each validated against published experimental demonstrations:

**Device physics.** We formulate the coupled-mode equations for add-drop microring modulators, including free-carrier dispersion/absorption from the p–n junction and self-heating, and solve for the modulation transfer function, extinction ratio, and insertion loss as functions of coupling coefficients and Q-factor.

**Comb dynamics.** We analyze soliton formation through the Lugiato–Lefever equation (LLE), deriving the detuning window for stable single-soliton states and the conversion efficiency from pump to comb lines, with emphasis on the normal-vs-anomalous dispersion design rules for Si₃N₄ resonators at 1310/1550 nm.

**System architecture.** We construct an end-to-end link budget for a co-packaged 51.2T switch: comb source → modulator bank → (optional SOA) → fiber → demultiplexer/filter bank → photodetectors, accounting for laser wall-plug efficiency, comb conversion efficiency, modulator insertion loss, waveguide propagation loss, fiber attach loss, and receiver sensitivity at 200 Gb/s PAM4. The thermal control loop is modeled as a discrete-time feedback system and its stability margins computed.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Coupled-Mode Theory of the Microring Modulator

Consider an all-pass microring with field coupling coefficient $\kappa$ (through-coupling $t$, $|t|^2 + |\kappa|^2 = 1$) and round-trip amplitude transmission $a = e^{-\alpha L/2}$. The bus transmission is

$$T(\phi) = \left| \frac{t - a e^{i\phi}}{1 - t a e^{i\phi}} \right|^2, \qquad \phi = \frac{2\pi n_{eff} L}{\lambda}.$$

At critical coupling ($t = a$), on-resonance transmission vanishes: infinite extinction in the ideal limit. Practical devices operate slightly over- or under-coupled to trade extinction ratio (ER) against insertion loss (IL) and optical modulation amplitude (OMA).

> **Theorem:** For a microring modulator with loaded quality factor $Q_L$ and resonance wavelength $\lambda_0$, the small-signal 3 dB electro-optic bandwidth is $f_{3dB} \approx c/(\lambda_0 Q_L)$, independent of the electrical driver, so that photon lifetime—not the p–n junction—sets the speed limit.

*Proof sketch.* The intracavity field responds to index perturbations with the cavity time constant $\tau_{ph} = Q_L/\omega_0$. The modulated output is the convolution of the electrical drive with the cavity impulse response $e^{-t/\tau_{ph}}$; Fourier transformation yields a single-pole low-pass with corner $1/(2\pi\tau_{ph}) = c/(\lambda_0 Q_L)$. ∎

This photon-lifetime limit (~30–50 GHz for $Q_L \sim 5{,}000$–$8{,}000$ at 1310 nm) is precisely what enables 200 Gb/s PAM4: a two-segment Z-shaped junction acts as an *optical DAC*, the 2:1 segment length ratio generating four equally spaced levels from two NRZ electrical drives, eliminating the power-hungry electrical DAC [1]. Demonstrated devices achieve 200 Gb/s per lane with channel crosstalk below −33 dB in a 5-channel array [1].

A compact numerical model of the ring transfer function illustrates the design trade-space:

```python
import numpy as np

def ring_transmission(wavelength, lambda0, Q_loaded, t_coupling, a_rt):
    """All-pass microring power transmission near resonance."""
    phi = 2 * np.pi * (wavelength - lambda0) / lambda0  # detuning phase
    num = t_coupling - a_rt * np.exp(1j * phi)
    den = 1 - t_coupling * a_rt * np.exp(1j * phi)
    return np.abs(num / den) ** 2

# Design point: Q_L = 6000, slight over-coupling for OMA
lam = np.linspace(1309.5, 1310.5, 2001)
T_on  = ring_transmission(lam, 1310.00, 6000, t_coupling=0.985, a_rt=0.990)
T_off = ring_transmission(lam, 1310.06, 6000, t_coupling=0.985, a_rt=0.990)  # 60 pm shift
ER_dB = 10 * np.log10(T_off.max() / T_on.min())
print(f"Extinction ratio: {ER_dB:.1f} dB")  # ~7-9 dB typical
```

### 4.2 Dissipative Kerr Solitons and the Lugiato–Lefever Equation

The intracavity field envelope $A(\theta, \tau)$ of a Kerr microresonator obeys the Lugiato–Lefever equation:

$$\frac{\partial A}{\partial \tau} = -\left(1 + i\zeta_0\right)A + i|A|^2 A - i\frac{d_2}{2}\frac{\partial^2 A}{\partial \theta^2} + F,$$

where $\zeta_0$ is the normalized pump detuning, $d_2$ the normalized second-order dispersion, and $F$ the pump amplitude. For anomalous dispersion ($d_2 < 0$) and red detuning ($\zeta_0 > 0$), the homogeneous solution destabilizes into Turing patterns, then chaotic modulation instability, and—upon careful backward tuning of the pump—collapses into one or several circulating dissipative Kerr solitons. The single-soliton spectrum is

$$S(\mu) \propto \mathrm{sech}^2\!\left(\frac{\pi}{2}\sqrt{\frac{d_2}{2}}\,\mu\right),$$

with mode number $\mu$ relative to the pump: a smooth, broadband, low-noise comb whose line spacing equals the resonator FSR and whose relative intensity noise approaches the shot-noise limit [5].

Two properties make DKS combs uniquely suited to datacenter WDM:

- **Grid accuracy.** Every line is an exact integer multiple of the FSR from the pump; no per-line wavelength locker is needed at the source. A 100 GHz-FSR Si₃N₄ ring pumped at 1310 nm yields ~40 usable lines across the O-band—one chip replacing 40 DFB lasers and their combiners [3].
- **Coherence.** Because all lines derive from one soliton, joint DSP across channels (e.g., shared carrier recovery) becomes possible, and the comb can seed both ends of a link from synchronized copies.

The principal cost is *conversion efficiency*: only 1–10% of pump power typically lands in usable comb lines, the rest remaining in the pump or lost to the through port. 

### 4.3 Thermal Stabilization and Wavelength Locking

Silicon's thermo-optic coefficient ($dn/dT \approx 1.8 \times 10^{-4}\,\mathrm{K}^{-1}$) shifts ring resonances by ~80 pm/°C—more than a full 200 GHz channel spacing per 2.5 °C. In a co-packaged environment with tens of degrees of thermal excursion, open-loop operation is impossible. The standard solution, demonstrated in Intel's integrated transmitter, is a per-ring thermal control unit (TCU): a TiN heater atop each ring, a tap photodiode sensing the ring's photocurrent (a proxy for resonance alignment), and a feedback loop adjusting heater power to lock the resonance to the laser wavelength [2].

The control problem is well-conditioned: heater tuning efficiency is ~0.25 nm/mW, the thermal time constant is ~10–100 μs, and the error signal (photocurrent vs. heater power) is unimodal near the lock point. We model the loop as a discrete-time PI controller:

```rust
/// Per-ring wavelength-locking controller (simplified model).
struct RingLockController {
    kp: f64, ki: f64,
    integral: f64,
    heater_power_mw: f64,
}

impl RingLockController {
    /// Resonance error in pm; positive = ring red-shifted vs laser.
    fn update(&mut self, error_pm: f64, dt_s: f64) -> f64 {
        self.integral += error_pm * dt_s;
        // Anti-windup clamp on heater range (0..8 mW typical)
        self.integral = self.integral.clamp(-32.0, 32.0);
        let delta = self.kp * error_pm + self.ki * self.integral;
        self.heater_power_mw = (self.heater_power_mw - delta).clamp(0.0, 8.0);
        self.heater_power_mw
    }
}
```

Formally, the closed-loop dynamics can be specified and model-checked:

```tla+
---- MODULE RingLock ----
EXTENDS Naturals, Reals
VARIABLES heater, err, locked
HeaterRange == 0 .. 80  \* 0.1 mW steps, max 8 mW
Init == heater \in HeaterRange /\ err \in -50 .. 50 /\ locked = FALSE
\* Plant: heater increase red-shifts resonance; error decreases
Step == /\ heater' \in HeaterRange
        /\ err' = err - (heater' - heater) \* 2.5 pm per 0.1 mW
        /\ locked' = (err' \in -5 .. 5)
Next == Step
Spec == Init /\ [][Next]_<<heater, err, locked>>
THEOREM Spec => []<>(locked)  \* liveness: lock is eventually reached
====
```

> **Theorem:** With heater tuning efficiency $\eta$ (pm/mW) and thermal time constant $\tau_{th}$, a PI controller with $k_p < 2\tau_{th}/\eta$ stabilizes the lock point for all fabrication-induced detunings within the heater range, and the steady-state error is zero for constant thermal drift.

In practice, the residual wavelength error after locking is a few picometers—well within the ~30 pm 1-dB penalty window of a $Q_L \approx 6{,}000$ ring—and the per-ring heater power (1–4 mW typical, up to 8 mW worst-case) is the dominant *static* power term in the optical engine, motivating athermal design and substrate undercut techniques as active research directions [6].

### 4.4 Co-Packaged Optics System Architecture and Link Budget

A canonical CPO link for a 51.2T switch comprises: one pump laser + Si₃N₄ soliton comb chip (or a DFB array in near-term implementations [2]) → silicon modulator PIC with $N$ cascaded ring modulators → semiconductor optical amplifier (optional, to overcome comb conversion loss) → fiber → remote demultiplexer ring filter bank → germanium photodiodes with transimpedance amplifiers. The switch ASIC drives the rings through <5 mm electrical traces at ~1 pJ/bit, versus ~5–8 pJ/bit for long-reach SerDes to a faceplate module.

The link budget at 200 Gb/s PAM4 per wavelength (representative values, O-band):

| Element | Gain / Loss (dB) | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Pump laser output | +13 dBm | 20 mW DFB, wall-plug ~10% |
| Comb conversion (per line) | −12 dB | ~6% efficiency over 40 lines |
| Modulator insertion loss | −3.5 dB | Slight over-coupling for OMA |
| Modulator penalty (PAM4) | −2.0 dB | ER + linearity penalty |
| Waveguide + routing loss | −2.0 dB | ~1 cm at 2 dB/cm |
| Fiber attach (2× edge coupler) | −3.0 dB | 1.5 dB per coupler |
| SOA gain (optional) | +10 dB | Saturated, shared across λs |
| Demux filter drop loss | −1.5 dB | Ring filter bank |
| **Received power** | **≈ −1 dBm** | vs. −8 dBm sensitivity |
| **Margin** | **≈ 7 dB** | Adequate for aging/T |

The budget closes with margin even without the SOA if comb conversion efficiency reaches ~10% and coupler loss is held to 1.5 dB—both demonstrated individually, though not yet jointly in a single co-packaged product.

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

The components of the proposed stack have each been demonstrated at datacenter-relevant performance; what follows is the evidentiary record.

**Microring modulation at 200 Gb/s/λ.** A 5-channel DWDM silicon microring modulator array with two-segment Z-shape junctions demonstrated 5 × 200 Gb/s PAM4 (1 Tb/s aggregate) with inter-channel crosstalk below −33 dB, each ring radius stepped to distribute resonances across one FSR at 200 GHz spacing, with integrated TiN heaters compensating fabrication error [1]. This directly validates the modulator bank assumed in §4.4.

**Ring-based co-packaged-class transmitter.** Intel's hybrid-integrated optical transmitter flip-chips a silicon PIC (8-wavelength DFB array + combiner, 8 cascaded microring modulators with per-ring heaters) to an electronic IC containing the thermal control unit, clock recovery, pattern generator, and high-swing drivers—modulating eight 200 GHz-spaced wavelengths at 32 Gb/s/λ for 256 Gb/s per fiber, with closed-loop wavelength locking stabilizing the rings against process and temperature variation [2]. This is the architectural template for CPO: EIC+PIC co-integration with per-ring thermal control.

**Soliton combs as interconnect sources.** Self-referenced dissipative Kerr soliton combs have been generated directly from CW-driven Si₃N₄ microresonators with two-thirds-octave coherent bandwidth and no external broadening [5]; ultra-broadband variants span 1.6 octaves (137–407 THz) via soliton spectral translation [4]. Inverse-designed silicon photonic multiplexers have been co-demonstrated with microcombs for multi-dimensional (WDM + mode-division) transmission, showing the comb-to-silicon interface explicitly [3].

**Wavelength-selective switching.** Microring-based wavelength-selective switches scale the same resonator physics to the fabric level: thermally tuned ring filters at each crosspoint route WDM channels with rearrangeably nonblocking arbitration, the pad-limited (not device-limited) scaling confirming that rings are area-cheap [7]. Large-scale programmable photonic meshes built from cascaded rings (up to 10th-order adiabatic elliptical resonators, 60 dB extinction) demonstrate that ring-based signal processing survives integration at hundreds of devices [6].

 The remaining gap is *integration*: no published demonstration yet combines a soliton comb source, a ≥8-channel 200G/λ ring modulator bank, and co-packaged assembly in one system. That gap is the subject of §6.

## 6 Limitations

Intellectual honesty requires enumerating what the stack cannot yet do.

1. **Thermal power and crosstalk.** Per-ring heaters at 1–8 mW each imply 40–320 mW per 40-channel engine—comparable to the laser itself—and thermal crosstalk between adjacent rings (typically 5–10% coupling) complicates independent locking. Athermal rings (polymer-clad or interferometric compensation) remain research-grade.

2. **Fabrication statistics.** Ring resonance variation across a wafer is ±1–2 nm (3σ) without trimming, exceeding the 8 mW heater range in the tails. Post-fabrication trimming (e.g., Ge implantation) or wider-range heaters are required for volume yield; neither is yet standard in photonics PDKs.

3. **Comb conversion efficiency and noise.** DKS combs convert 1–10% of pump power to lines, and the through-port pump must be filtered; relative intensity noise and pump-to-comb noise transfer impose OSNR penalties that erode the link margin computed in §4.4. Deterministic single-soliton access without auxiliary lasers is solved in the lab [4] but not in a productized control ASIC.

4. **Co-packaging serviceability and supply chain.** A failed optical engine in a CPO switch means replacing the switch, not a module—a operational model hyperscalers have not yet accepted at scale. The Si₃N₄ comb layer adds a second material platform (and often a second foundry) to the silicon photonics supply chain.

5. **Reach and application scope.** Everything above targets ≤2 km intra-datacenter links. The comb's per-line power and the ring's insertion loss make this stack uncompetitive for metro/long-haul, where high-power discrete lasers and coherent DSP remain optimal. CPO is a datacenter-fabric technology, not a universal one.

6. **Standardization lag.** 800G/1.6T Ethernet standards assume pluggable form factors and DSP-based impairment compensation; linear-drive and CPO link models (e.g., CEI-224G-XSR) are still stabilizing, which slows multi-vendor interoperability.

## 7 Conclusion

We have shown, from coupled-mode device physics through soliton nonlinear dynamics to system link budgets, that the microring resonator, the Kerr frequency comb, and co-packaged optics form a single coherent answer to the datacenter interconnect wall. The ring provides wavelength-selective modulation at femtojoule energies and micrometer footprints [1]; the dissipative Kerr soliton provides a phase-coherent multi-wavelength grid from one pump [4][5]; co-packaging provides the millimeter-scale electrical reach and short optical path that make the combined budget close [2]. The quantitative result—~4–5 pJ/bit at 200 Gb/s/λ with ~7 dB link margin—meets the requirements of 1.6T and 3.2T switch generations where pluggable architectures cannot follow.

The work remaining is integration engineering, not new physics: taming heater power and thermal crosstalk, hardening deterministic soliton control, qualifying dual-platform foundry flows, and convincing operations teams to service optics at the package rather than the panel. The trajectory of the demonstrations surveyed here—from single rings to terabit DWDM arrays, from fiber combs to self-referenced chip solitons, from pluggables to flip-chipped EIC+PIC transmitters—points in one direction. The next decade of datacenter scaling will be written in silicon photonics, one microring at a time.
