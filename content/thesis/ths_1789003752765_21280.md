# Spectral Learning of Latent-Variable Models: Method of Moments, Tensor Decompositions, and Identifiability in Mixture Estimation

## Abstract

The method of moments, originating with Pearson's 1894 treatment of mixture distributions, has undergone a profound renaissance in modern machine learning through its fusion with multilinear algebra. This article develops the spectral theory of latent-variable estimation: we show how the low-order observable moments of mixture models, hidden Markov models, and topic models admit exact *tensor factorizations* whose components are precisely the hidden parameters of interest, and how these factorizations can be extracted efficiently via whitened tensor power iteration. We present the robust tensor power method of Anandkumar et al. with its Wedin-style perturbation guarantee, analyze sample complexity under spectral separation conditions, and treat the complementary question of *identifiability* through Kruskal's theorem on the uniqueness of three-way tensor decompositions. The result is a unified account of why spectral estimators achieve polynomial-time global recovery in settings where likelihood-based methods such as EM are trapped by local optima, together with a candid assessment of the separation, rank, and noise assumptions on which the theory rests.

## 1. Introduction

Learning a latent-variable model means recovering structure that is, by definition, never observed. In a *finite mixture model*, each observation is drawn from one of several component distributions, but the component label is hidden; in a *hidden Markov model* (HMM), a discrete latent chain evolves through time while only emissions are visible; in *latent Dirichlet allocation* (LDA), documents are mixtures of topics whose proportions are unobserved. The dominant practical approach — maximum likelihood via the expectation-maximization (EM) algorithm [6] — is non-convex with no guarantee of finding the global optimum. Practitioners restart EM from many initializations, yet theory offers no certificate of correctness.

The *spectral* or *method-of-moments* program takes a radically different route. Rather than optimizing a likelihood surface, it exploits algebraic identities: the low-order moments of the observable distribution — the mean, covariance, and third-order moment — factorize as *sums of rank-one tensors* whose factors are the latent parameters themselves [1]. Parameter estimation is thereby reduced to *tensor decomposition*, a problem that is NP-hard in general but provably tractable for the special symmetric orthogonally-decomposable tensors that arise from moments. The payoff is a family of estimators with *polynomial* sample and computational complexity and *global* recovery guarantees, under explicit and checkable separation conditions [1,2].

This article develops the full theory:

1. **Tensor structure of moments**: why second- and third-order moments of canonical latent-variable models are symmetric tensors of rank equal to the number of latent components.
2. **Whitening**: how the covariance matrix converts a general moment tensor into an *orthogonally decomposable* tensor, generalizing the whitening step of independent component analysis.
3. **The robust tensor power method**: the workhorse algorithm, its convergence, and its perturbation theory — an analogue of Wedin's theorem for matrices.
4. **Identifiability**: when parameters can in principle be recovered at all, via Kruskal's theorem on tensor uniqueness [3] and the algebraic statistics program of Allman, Matias, and Rhodes.
5. **Limits**: sample complexity, the curse of non-orthogonality, overcomplete models, and where spectral methods still fail.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 The method of moments: from Pearson to the present

The method of moments is among the oldest ideas in statistics. Karl Pearson [7] estimated the parameters of a mixture of two univariate normals by equating the first five empirical moments to their theoretical expressions and solving the resulting polynomial system — a computation he performed by hand for measurements of *Naples crabs*. The method was later eclipsed by Fisher's maximum likelihood, which is asymptotically efficient, but it returned to the center of machine learning when researchers realized that for latent-variable models, moment equations are *algebraic* rather than merely numerical, and that modern multilinear algebra could solve them at scale [1].

The philosophical advantage of moments is decoupling: likelihood-based estimation couples all parameters in a non-convex landscape, whereas the third-order moment of a mixture is a sum of rank-one terms, one per component — recovering the components is a *decomposition* problem with a unique answer under mild conditions. EM climbs a landscape; spectral methods *diagonalize* it.

### 2.2 Tensors: notation and basic operations

A *tensor* is a multidimensional array. An order-3 tensor $T \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d \times d}$ has entries $T_{ijk}$. The *outer product* of vectors $a, b, c$ is the rank-one tensor $(a \otimes b \otimes c)_{ijk} = a_i b_j c_k$. A *symmetric* tensor satisfies $T_{ijk} = T_{\pi(i)\pi(j)\pi(k)}$ for all permutations $\pi$. The *symmetric rank* of $T$ is the smallest $k$ such that $T = \sum_{i=1}^{k} \lambda_i \, v_i^{\otimes 3}$ for vectors $v_i$ and scalars $\lambda_i$.

The *multilinear form* $T(u,v,w) = \sum_{ijk} T_{ijk} u_i v_j w_k$ and the *tensor-vector product* $T(I, u, u)$ (components $\sum_{jk} T_{ijk} u_j u_k$) generalize matrix-vector multiplication and are the primitives of all tensor algorithms; the standard reference for this toolkit is the survey of Kolda and Bader [4].

### 2.3 The failure mode that motivates spectral methods

Consider the log-likelihood of a mixture model with $k$ components. It is non-convex for every $k \geq 2$, even for mixtures of spherical Gaussians. EM performs coordinate ascent on a lower bound of this landscape and converges only to a *stationary point*. Spectral estimators sidestep this entirely: they make *one pass* over the data to form moment estimates, then run a deterministic decomposition.

| Property | EM / Maximum Likelihood | Spectral (Method of Moments) |
|---|---|---|
| Optimization landscape | Non-convex, local optima | None — deterministic decomposition |
| Global recovery guarantee | No | Yes, under separation [1,2] |
| Sample complexity | Statistically optimal (efficient) | Polynomial but larger constants |
| Requires good initialization | Yes | No |
| Handles model misspecification | Gracefully (KL projection) | Poorly — moments must match exactly |
| Typical runtime | Many iterations over data | One pass + eigendecomposition-like step |

## 3. Methodology

### 3.1 The canonical reduction: moments to tensors

We present the methodology for the *spherical Gaussian mixture*; Section 4 extends it to HMMs and LDA. Suppose observations $x \in \mathbb{R}^d$ come from a mixture of $k$ spherical Gaussians with means $\mu_1, \dots, \mu_k$, common variance $\sigma^2$, and weights $w_i > 0$ summing to one. The observable moments are:

$$M_1 = \mathbb{E}[x] = \sum_{i=1}^{k} w_i \mu_i, \qquad M_2 = \mathbb{E}[x \otimes x] - \sigma^2 I = \sum_{i=1}^{k} w_i \, \mu_i \otimes \mu_i,$$

$$M_3 = \mathbb{E}[x \otimes x \otimes x] - \sigma^2 \sum_{j=1}^{d} \left(M_1 \otimes e_j \otimes e_j + e_j \otimes M_1 \otimes e_j + e_j \otimes e_j \otimes M_1\right) = \sum_{i=1}^{k} w_i \, \mu_i^{\otimes 3}.$$

The crucial fact is that $M_2$ and the corrected $M_3$ are *exactly* sums of rank-one symmetric tensors whose factors are the unknown means $\mu_i$ [1]. The estimation problem is now purely algebraic: given noisy estimates $\hat{M}_2, \hat{M}_3$ of these tensors, recover the pairs $(w_i, \mu_i)$.

### 3.2 Whitening: reduction to orthogonal decomposition

The tensor $M_3$ is not directly decomposable by simple methods because its factors $\{\mu_i\}$ are not orthogonal. *Whitening* repairs this. Let $M_2 = U D U^\top$ be the eigendecomposition of the (rank-$k$) second moment, with $U \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times k}$ and $D \in \mathbb{R}^{k \times k}$ diagonal. Define the whitening matrix $W = U D^{-1/2} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times k}$. Then the transformed vectors $\tilde{\mu}_i = \sqrt{w_i} \, W^\top \mu_i$ are *orthonormal*: indeed,

$$\sum_{i=1}^{k} \tilde{\mu}_i \tilde{\mu}_i^\top = W^\top \left(\sum_i w_i \mu_i \mu_i^\top\right) W = W^\top M_2 W = I_k.$$

Applying the same linear map to all three modes of $M_3$ yields the *whitened tensor*

$$\tilde{M}_3 = M_3(W, W, W) = \sum_{i=1}^{k} \frac{1}{\sqrt{w_i}} \, \tilde{\mu}_i^{\otimes 3},$$

which is an *orthogonally decomposable* (odeco) symmetric tensor: a weighted sum of rank-one tensors with orthonormal factors [1]. Tensor decomposition is NP-hard in general but tractable for odeco tensors — the linchpin of the theory.

### 3.3 The robust tensor power method

For matrices, the power method recovers eigenvectors by iterating $u \leftarrow Au / \|Au\|$. For an odeco tensor $T = \sum_i \lambda_i v_i^{\otimes 3}$ with orthonormal $\{v_i\}$, the analogue iterates

$$\theta \leftarrow \frac{T(I, \theta, \theta)}{\|T(I, \theta, \theta)\|}.$$

Each $v_i$ is a fixed point ($T(I, v_i, v_i) = \lambda_i v_i$), attracting for generic initializations with quadratic local convergence. Running from $L = O(k \log k)$ random starts recovers all components; deflation removes recovered ones.

The *robust* tensor power method [1] analyzes this iteration when the input is $\hat{T} = T + E$, a perturbed tensor with $\|E\| \leq \epsilon$. Its main theorem is a tensor analogue of Wedin's perturbation theorem for matrix singular vectors:

> **Theorem (Robust tensor power method, Anandkumar et al. [1]):** Let $T = \sum_{i=1}^{k} \lambda_i v_i^{\otimes 3}$ be odeco with orthonormal $\{v_i\}$ and $\lambda_{\min} = \min_i \lambda_i > 0$. Given $\hat{T} = T + E$ with operator norm $\|E\| \leq \epsilon \leq c \, \lambda_{\min} / k$ for a universal constant $c$, the robust tensor power method with $L$ random initializations returns estimates $\{(\hat{\lambda}_i, \hat{v}_i)\}$ such that, after permutation, $\|\hat{v}_i - v_i\| \leq O(\epsilon / \lambda_{\min})$ and $|\hat{\lambda}_i - \lambda_i| \leq O(\epsilon)$, with probability at least $1 - \eta$, provided $L \geq \mathrm{poly}(k) \log(1/\eta)$.

Unwhitening then recovers the original parameters: $\mu_i = (W^\top)^{-1} (\tilde{\mu}_i / \sqrt{w_i})$ and $w_i = 1/\hat{\lambda}_i^2$. The complete pipeline — estimate moments, whiten, decompose, unwhiten — is summarized below.

```python
import numpy as np

def tensor_power_iteration(T, theta, n_iter=100):
    for _ in range(n_iter):
        v = np.einsum('ijk,j,k->i', T, theta, theta)  # T(I, theta, theta)
        n = np.linalg.norm(v)
        if n < 1e-12:
            break
        theta = v / n
    lam = np.einsum('ijk,i,j,k->', T, theta, theta, theta)
    return lam, theta

def spectral_mixture_estimate(X, k, sigma2, n_restarts=50):
    n, d = X.shape
    M1 = X.mean(axis=0)
    M2 = (X[:, :, None] * X[:, None, :]).mean(axis=0) - sigma2 * np.eye(d)
    # Whiten using top-k eigendecomposition of M2
    vals, vecs = np.linalg.eigh(M2)
    U, D = vecs[:, -k:], vals[-k:]
    W = U @ np.diag(1.0 / np.sqrt(D))
    M3 = np.einsum('ni,nj,nk->ijk', X, X, X) / n
    corr = sum(np.einsum('i,j,k->ijk', M1, e, e) +
               np.einsum('i,j,k->ijk', e, M1, e) +
               np.einsum('i,j,k->ijk', e, e, M1)
               for e in np.eye(d))
    M3 = M3 - sigma2 * corr
    T = np.einsum('ia,jb,kc,abc->ijk', W, W, W, M3)
    thetas = [t / np.linalg.norm(t) for t in
              np.random.randn(n_restarts, k)]
    found = []
    for t0 in thetas:
        lam, v = tensor_power_iteration(T, t0)
        if all(abs(v @ u) < 0.9 for _, u in found):
            found.append((lam, v))
    Winv = np.linalg.pinv(W)
    params = [((1.0 / lam**2), Winv.T @ v * abs(lam)) for lam, v in found]
    return params
```

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Tensor structure across latent-variable models

The same tensor structure appears across latent-variable models [1]:

- **Hidden Markov models.** For an HMM with $k$ hidden states, the pairwise and triplewise observable moments factorize through the hidden-state parameters. Hsu, Kakade, and Zhang [2] gave the first provably correct spectral algorithm for HMMs — using only SVD and matrix multiplication — under a separation condition on the smallest singular value $\sigma_k$ of the HMM parameters.
- **Latent Dirichlet allocation.** The word co-occurrence moments of LDA admit a factorization $M_2 = \sum_i \alpha_i \beta_i \otimes \beta_i$ and $M_3 = \sum_i \gamma_i \beta_i^{\otimes 3}$, where $\beta_i$ are topic-word distributions; whitening plus decomposition recovers topics without the local-optima pathology of variational inference.
- **Mixtures of linear regressions and subspace clustering.** Higher-order moments of the response factorize over the hidden regression vectors, enabling provable recovery.

In each case, the *rank* of the moment tensor equals the number of latent components and the *factors* are the parameters: the method of moments is an exact algebraic reduction, not a heuristic.

### 4.2 Whitening geometry and the role of the second moment

Whitening is where statistical estimation meets geometry: the matrix $W$ *spheres* the mixture, mapping the data into a space where the component means are orthonormal, so that every component direction becomes an isolated, attracting fixed point of the power iteration rather than a skewed saddle.

This step is also the method's Achilles' heel: it requires $M_2$ to have rank exactly $k$ with a clean spectral gap, and $W = UD^{-1/2}$ *amplifies* estimation noise by $1/\sqrt{\lambda_{\min}(M_2)}$ along small eigendirections. Every sample-complexity bound for spectral estimators contains a factor of $1/\sigma_{\min}$ for this reason — the geometry of whitening converts near-collinearity into noise amplification.

### 4.3 Convergence dynamics of the tensor power method

Unlike the matrix power method, the tensor iteration can converge to *spurious* fixed points when $T$ is only approximately odeco. The robust analysis [1] handles this with many random starts — with high probability at least one falls in each true component's basin of attraction — plus a *clustering* step that merges duplicates and rejects spurious fixed points with inconsistent tensor eigenvalues $\lambda = T(v,v,v)$.

Convergence is *quadratically* fast near a true component, so few iterations suffice; the cost is dominated by $O(d^3)$ tensor contractions, reducible to $O(d^2 k)$ with sketching.

### 4.4 Kruskal rank and the identifiability frontier

Decomposition algorithms answer *"how to recover"*; identifiability answers *"whether recovery is possible in principle."* The foundational result is Kruskal's theorem on the uniqueness of three-way decompositions. For matrices $A, B, C$ with $k$ columns, the *Kruskal rank* $\mathrm{krank}(A)$ is the largest $r$ such that every set of $r$ columns is linearly independent.

> **Theorem (Kruskal, 1977):** Let $T = \sum_{i=1}^{k} a_i \otimes b_i \otimes c_i$ with factor matrices $A, B, C$. If $\mathrm{krank}(A) + \mathrm{krank}(B) + \mathrm{krank}(C) \geq 2k + 2$, then the rank-$k$ decomposition of $T$ is *essentially unique*: any other decomposition differs only by permutation and rescaling of the rank-one terms.

Allman, Matias, and Rhodes [3] built an identifiability program on this theorem: for latent-class models with at least three conditionally independent observed variables, the joint distribution *is* a three-way tensor, and Kruskal's condition yields *generic identifiability* — unique recoverability except on a set of measure zero. This resolved decades-old puzzles such as the identifiability of finite mixtures of Bernoulli products, and established the now-standard distinction between *strict* identifiability (every parameter identifiable) and *generic* identifiability (all but a null set).

Kruskal's condition is thus the logical prerequisite of estimation: without uniqueness, even a perfect decomposition algorithm could return a valid-but-wrong factorization.

### 4.5 Beyond orthogonality: non-orthogonal and overcomplete models

The odeco assumption — that whitened factors are orthonormal — requires the number of components $k$ to not exceed the ambient dimension $d$. In *overcomplete* models ($k > d$), whitening is impossible, and the tensor power method provably fails. Two lines of work address this:

1. **Alternating rank-1 updates.** Anandkumar, Ge, and Janzamin developed a *non-orthogonal* tensor decomposition via alternating minimization with provable guarantees under incoherence conditions, extending spectral learning to mildly overcomplete settings.
2. **Higher-order moments and structured sparsity.** For overcomplete topic models, Anandkumar et al. [5] showed that fourth-order moments combined with structured sparsity (anchor-word–like conditions) restore identifiability even when $k$ exceeds $d$, characterizing precisely when overcomplete representations can be learned.

The gap between what is identifiable and what is efficiently learnable in the overcomplete regime remains one of the field's central open problems.

```haskell
-- Kruskal-rank uniqueness condition for a 3-way decomposition
kruskalUnique :: Int -> Int -> Int -> Int -> Bool
kruskalUnique krankA krankB krankC k =
    krankA + krankB + krankC >= 2 * k + 2

example :: Bool
example = kruskalUnique 4 4 4 4   -- True: generically identifiable
```

```rust
/// Second-order moment M2 = E[x x^T] - sigma^2 I from samples.
fn second_moment(xs: &[Vec<f64>], sigma2: f64) -> Vec<Vec<f64>> {
    let (n, d) = (xs.len() as f64, xs[0].len());
    let mut m = vec![vec![0.0; d]; d];
    for x in xs {
        for i in 0..d { for j in 0..d { m[i][j] += x[i] * x[j] / n; } }
    }
    for i in 0..d { m[i][i] -= sigma2; }
    m
}
```

## 5. Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 What the theorems guarantee

The end-to-end guarantee for the spherical Gaussian mixture [1] is representative: if the means $\{\mu_i\}$ are linearly independent, $w_{\min} = \min_i w_i > 0$, and $\sigma_k(M_2)$ is the $k$-th singular value of the second moment, then with $n$ samples the spectral estimator achieves

$$\max_i \|\hat{\mu}_i - \mu_{\pi(i)}\| \;\leq\; \epsilon, \qquad \max_i |\hat{w}_i - w_{\pi(i)}| \;\leq\; \epsilon$$

for some permutation $\pi$, provided

$$n \;\geq\; \mathrm{poly}\!\left(d,\; k,\; \frac{1}{w_{\min}},\; \frac{1}{\sigma_k(M_2)},\; \frac{1}{\epsilon},\; \log \frac{1}{\delta}\right)$$

with probability at least $1 - \delta$. The proof chains concentration of the empirical moments (matrix and tensor Bernstein inequalities), perturbation of the whitening matrix (Davis–Kahan), and the robust tensor power guarantee of Section 3.3. Hsu, Kakade, and Zhang [2] give an analogous HMM bound whose sample complexity does *not* explicitly depend on the observation alphabet size.

### 5.2 Empirical behavior

Simulation studies in [1] confirm the theory's qualitative predictions on synthetic mixtures: recovery error drops sharply once component separation exceeds the whitening noise-amplification threshold (consistent with the $1/\sigma_k(M_2)$ dependence); runtime grows as $O(d^3)$ for explicit tensors, near-linearly with sketching; and unlike EM, the estimator is deterministic given the moments — restarts affect only which fixed points are found first, not the final answer. Under mild misspecification, error degrades smoothly. For HMMs, the spectral algorithm of [2] was shown to be *orders of magnitude faster* than EM on real sequence data while achieving comparable or better predictive likelihood.

### 5.3 Proof sketch: why the power method works on odeco tensors

The heart of the analysis is a coefficient-dynamics lemma. Writing the iterate in the orthonormal component basis as $\theta = \sum_i c_i v_i$ with $\sum_i c_i^2 = 1$ gives $T(I, \theta, \theta) = \sum_i \lambda_i c_i^2 v_i$, so one iteration maps $c_i \mapsto \lambda_i c_i^2 / \sqrt{\sum_j \lambda_j^2 c_j^4}$: the ratio of the largest to second-largest coefficient *squares* at each step, yielding doubly-exponential amplification of whichever component initially dominates. The robust version [1] shows this dynamics survives perturbations of size $O(\lambda_{\min}/k)$ via an invariant argument on the coefficient ratios.

## 6. Limitations

Spectral learning is not a panacea, and an honest account must stress its boundaries.

1. **Separation dependence.** Every guarantee scales polynomially in $1/\sigma_{\min}$, the smallest singular value of the moment matrices. When components overlap heavily, the required sample size explodes — precisely the regime where practitioners most need help. EM, for all its flaws, is often more sample-efficient when well-specified and well-separated, because maximum likelihood is asymptotically efficient while moment estimators are not.
2. **Model misspecification.** The moment identities are *exact* algebraic consequences of the model. If the truth deviates — non-spherical covariances, Markov order mismatch — the estimated tensor need not be close to odeco, and the decomposition can be meaningless. Likelihood methods degrade to the nearest KL projection; spectral methods have no such graceful semantics.
3. **The whitening bottleneck.** Whitening requires knowing the model order $k$ and inverts $M_2$, amplifying noise along small eigendirections. In high dimensions with limited data, this step dominates the error.
4. **Overcompleteness.** The basic theory requires $k \leq d$. Extensions [5] exist but demand strong incoherence or structured-sparsity conditions and considerably heavier algorithms.
5. **Constants and practicality.** Polynomial sample complexity with unspecified constants can mean $n = 10^6$ where EM succeeds with $n = 10^4$. Spectral estimators are best viewed as *initializers* for EM — a theoretically grounded replacement for random restarts — and the hybrid "spectral initialization + EM refinement" is the configuration most often deployed.
6. **Discrete vs. continuous.** The cleanest results are for discrete observations or spherical Gaussians; general covariance mixtures require fourth-order moments and much heavier machinery.

## 7. Conclusion

The spectral approach to latent-variable learning — estimating low-order moments, whitening, and decomposing the resulting tensor — transforms a non-convex optimization problem into an algebraic one with a unique answer and a provable algorithm. Its lineage runs from Pearson's hand-computed moments [7] through Kruskal's uniqueness theorem [3] to the robust tensor power method [1] and the spectral HMM algorithm [2]. Along the way it settled questions that likelihood theory could not: *which* latent-variable models are identifiable at all, and *how* to estimate them without fear of local optima.

The deepest lesson is a duality. *Identifiability* (Kruskal) tells us the parameters are determined by the data; *computation* (tensor power iteration) tells us they can be found efficiently; *statistics* (perturbation theory) tells us finite samples suffice. Spectral learning is one of the few areas of machine learning where all three halves of this triad are theorems rather than aspirations. Its limitations — separation dependence, misspecification fragility, the overcomplete frontier — delineate exactly where the next generation of theory must go: toward estimators that keep the global guarantees of moments while approaching the statistical efficiency and robustness of likelihood.
