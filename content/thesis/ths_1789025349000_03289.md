# Neural Decoding for Brain-Computer Interfaces: Transformer-Based Spike Train Decoding, Utah Array Signal Processing, and Closed-Loop Adaptive Calibration for Motor Prosthetics

## Abstract

Intracortical brain–computer interfaces (BCIs) translate the spiking activity of motor cortical neurons into control signals for prosthetic effectors, offering restored communication and mobility to people with tetraplegia. This thesis develops a unified treatment of the neural decoding pipeline: from Utah-array signal acquisition and spike sorting, through classical Bayesian decoders (velocity Kalman filters and their recalibrated feedback-intention-trained extensions), to modern sequence models — latent dynamical systems (LFADS) and the Neural Data Transformer (NDT) — that treat spike trains as temporal token streams. We analyze closed-loop decoder adaptation (CLDA) as a problem of intention estimation under distribution shift, proving conditions under which intention-estimated retraining reduces closed-loop cursor-trajectory bias. We further evaluate the pipeline empirically against published benchmarks: the BrainGate clinical trials, the ReFIT-KF factor-of-two acquisition-time reduction, and the 90-characters-per-minute handwriting decoder. Finally, we confront the field's central limitations — chronic recording instability, inter-day nonstationarity, the labeling problem for supervised retraining, and the translation gap between monkey center-out tasks and dexterous human use — and chart a research program toward self-calibrating, lifelong prosthetic decoders.

---

## 1 Introduction

A person with high-cervical spinal cord injury retains an intact motor cortex whose neurons continue to modulate with attempted movement, years after the descending pathways were severed. An intracortical BCI listens to this activity — typically via a 4×4 mm Utah microelectrode array with 96–100 recording electrodes [1] — and maps it to kinematics in real time. The intellectual core of the system is the *decoder*: a statistical map from high-dimensional, noisy spike trains to low-dimensional user intent.

The decoder problem is unusually demanding. Neural observations are point processes with heavy-tailed noise; the mapping drifts on timescales from minutes (electrode micromotion) to months (gliosis, neuronal loss) [2]; the only ground truth about "intention" is behavior itself, which the paralyzed user cannot produce; and the loop is closed — the user's neural strategy adapts to the decoder just as the decoder adapts to the user. Early clinical systems therefore relied on linear, interpretable estimators. The velocity Kalman filter (KF) became the workhorse of human intracortical BCI: a 2011 BrainGate study showed a participant achieving 94% cursor-control success nearly 1000 days after array implantation, with only ~20–30 isolated neurons contributing control signals [2]. Yet linear decoders leave substantial information on the table, motivating nonlinear, adaptive, and deep-sequence architectures.

This thesis is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews the physiological and signal-processing foundations. Section 3 derives the decoding pipeline end to end. Section 4 — the deep dive — treats (i) Utah-array spike processing, (ii) Kalman filtering and the ReFIT-KF intention-estimation innovation, (iii) CLDA as closed-loop system identification, and (iv) transformer-based spike-train decoding. Section 5 evaluates performance empirically and states a formal result on intention-estimated retraining. Section 6 enumerates limitations honestly, and Section 7 concludes with a research agenda.

> **Thesis claim:** *The dominant performance gains in intracortical BCI over the past fifteen years have come not from larger models but from better handling of the closed loop — intention estimation, adaptive recalibration, and training on the statistics of closed-loop control itself. Transformer decoders inherit this lesson: their advantage lies in modeling closed-loop neural dynamics, not merely in parameter count.*

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Motor cortical encoding

Primary motor cortex (M1) neurons exhibit *directional tuning*: firing rates vary approximately cosinusoidally with movement direction,

$$r_i(\theta) = b_i + m_i \cos(\theta - \theta_i^{PD})$$

where $\theta_i^{PD}$ is the neuron's preferred direction — the classical cosine-tuning model of Georgopoulos and colleagues. The *population vector* hypothesis sums preferred-direction vectors weighted by firing rates to estimate movement direction. Although single-neuron tuning is noisy and nonstationary, populations of tens to hundreds of neurons carry enough information for 2-D cursor control and even 7+-DOF robotic arm control, as demonstrated in the 2012 *Nature* BrainGate study in which two participants with brainstem stroke controlled robotic arms to reach and grasp [4].

### 2.2 The Utah array and the recording chain

The Utah (Blackrock) array: 10×10 grid, 1.5 mm shanks, 400 µm pitch, 96 wired electrodes. Raw broadband (30 kHz) is band-passed (250 Hz–7.5 kHz), thresholded at ~−3.5 to −4.5× RMS, and sorted into single units (template matching, Kilosort-style) or collapsed into *threshold crossings* — multi-unit activity that, remarkably, decodes nearly as well as sorted spikes while being far more robust to drift [5].

### 2.3 From spikes to kinematics: the decoding zoo

| Decoder family | Model class | Key strength | Key weakness |
|---|---|---|---|
| Population vector | Linear, instantaneous | Interpretable | Ignores dynamics |
| Wiener filter | Linear, FIR history | Simple, stable | No state uncertainty |
| Velocity Kalman filter | Linear-Gaussian SSM | Optimal under LG assumptions; uncertainty | Linear observation model |
| ReFIT-KF | KF + intention-estimated retraining | ~2× faster target acquisition [6] | Still linear |
| Particle filter / point-process filter | Nonlinear Bayesian | Handles Poisson statistics | Compute cost |
| RNN / LSTM decoders | Deep sequence | Captures nonlinear dynamics | Data-hungry; brittle across days |
| LFADS | Generative latent dynamics | Denoises via dynamics prior | Offline-biased; latent ≠ kinematics |
| Neural Data Transformer | Self-attention over spike bins | Long-range temporal context; parallel | Needs large pretraining corpora |

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 Problem formulation

Let $\mathbf{y}_{1:T}$ be binned spike counts ($C$ channels × $T$ bins, bin width $\Delta \in [10, 50]$ ms) and $\mathbf{x}_{1:T}$ the kinematic state (position, velocity). The decoder estimates $p(\mathbf{x}_t \mid \mathbf{y}_{1:t})$ online under a hard latency budget ($\lesssim 100$ ms end-to-end, since control delays degrade performance measurably [7]). Training data come from two regimes:

1. **Open-loop calibration:** the user watches or imagines cued movements; kinematics are *assumed* (e.g., straight-line reaches to targets).
2. **Closed-loop control:** the user drives the cursor with a seed decoder; kinematics are the *actual decoded trajectory* — biased by the seed decoder's errors.

The mismatch between these regimes is the central pathology of BCI calibration.

### 3.2 Kalman filtering baseline

The velocity KF assumes linear-Gaussian dynamics and observations:

$$\mathbf{x}_t = A \mathbf{x}_{t-1} + \mathbf{w}_t, \qquad \mathbf{w}_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0, W)$$
$$\mathbf{y}_t = H \mathbf{x}_t + \mathbf{q}_t, \qquad \mathbf{q}_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0, Q)$$

with the standard predict–update recursion. $H$ is fit by least squares on calibration data. The KF is the minimum-mean-square-error estimator *under its assumptions*; its failures in practice are failures of the linear-Gaussian assumption, not of the recursion.

### 3.3 Intention estimation and the ReFIT innovation

Gilja *et al.* observed that closed-loop training data are contaminated: when the seed decoder veers off course, the user's corrective neural activity is paired with the *wrong* kinematics. ReFIT-KF applies two corrections [6]:

1. **Intention estimation:** during retraining, rotate each decoded velocity vector to point at the known target (zero velocity inside the target). The assumption: *the user always intends to move toward the target*, regardless of where the seed decoder went.
2. **Zero prior position uncertainty:** the user is assumed to perfectly internalize cursor position (they see it), so the KF gain is computed with $P_{t|t-1}$ projected to zero position uncertainty — sharpening the filter's trust in observations.

In rhesus center-out experiments, ReFIT-KF halved target acquisition time versus the velocity KF and approached native-arm performance [6].

### 3.4 Closed-loop decoder adaptation (CLDA)

CLDA generalizes intention estimation: interleave short control blocks with decoder re-estimation, using inferred intentions as pseudo-labels. Formally it is *online system identification with a human in the loop* — the plant (user's neural strategy) co-adapts, so convergence must be analyzed as a coupled dynamical system, not as supervised learning [8].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Utah-Array Signal Processing: From Broadband to Spike Features

The recording chain determines the information ceiling of every downstream decoder. A typical BrainGate pipeline:

1. **Analog front end:** 30 kS/s, 16-bit, per-channel programmable gain; wireless variants (e.g., the Brown wireless transmitter demonstrated in 2021) preserve single-neuron fidelity at full broadband bandwidth [9].
2. **Common-average referencing and band-passing** (250 Hz high-pass) to reject LFP bleed-through and motion artifact.
3. **Threshold crossing detection** at $k \cdot \mathrm{RMS}$, $k \approx 3.5$–$4.5$. Sorting (optional) via PCA + clustering or Kilosort; many clinical decoders skip sorting and use threshold crossings directly, trading a small offline accuracy loss for large robustness gains across days [5].
4. **Binning and smoothing:** counts in 20–50 ms bins, optionally convolved with a causal Gaussian ($\sigma \approx 30$–$50$ ms) to produce pseudo-firing-rates.
5. **Dimensionality handling:** $C \in [96, 192]$ channels; z-scoring per channel; optional factor-analysis denoising.

> **Theorem (informal, sufficiency of threshold crossings):** *For Poisson-like units with stationary waveform shapes, threshold-crossing rates are a sufficient statistic for the linear decoder's information about kinematics up to waveform-shape nuisance parameters; sorting recovers at most the shape-conditioned residual, which vanishes as waveform SNR grows.*

The practical upshot: chronic BCIs should be engineered around *unsorted* features, because sorting breaks exactly when the decoder is most needed — after drift.

### 4.2 Kalman Filtering, ReFIT-KF, and the Geometry of Intention Estimation

The velocity KF's observation matrix $H$ maps kinematics to expected spike counts. Fitting $H$ on open-loop data assumes the user moves as instructed; fitting on closed-loop data assumes the decoded kinematics equal intention. Both are wrong, in opposite directions.

Intention estimation replaces the second assumption with a structural prior, as analyzed in the intention-estimation literature [3]. Given target $\mathbf{g}_t$ and decoded position $\mathbf{p}_t$:

$$\tilde{\mathbf{v}}_t = v_{\max} \frac{\mathbf{g}_t - \mathbf{p}_t}{\|\mathbf{g}_t - \mathbf{p}_t\|} \cdot \mathbb{I}(\|\mathbf{g}_t - \mathbf{p}_t\| > \rho)$$

Retraining $H$ against $\tilde{\mathbf{v}}_t$ (rather than the seed decoder's $\hat{\mathbf{v}}_t$) removes the systematic bias where corrective neural bursts were paired with off-target velocities. Empirically, intention estimation alone — independent of the retraining step — accounts for the majority of ReFIT-KF's gain [10]. This is a profound lesson: **the labels were the problem, not the model.**

A second, subtler innovation: during online ReFIT-KF operation, the position rows/columns of the posterior covariance are zeroed each step, encoding the assumption that visual feedback makes cursor position known. This is a *feedback-control* design choice, not a statistical one — it treats the BCI as a servo loop with a human observer, and it is why ReFIT-KF orbits targets less and stops faster [6].

### 4.3 Closed-Loop Decoder Adaptation as Coupled System Identification

CLDA iterates: (i) user controls with decoder $\mathcal{D}_k$; (ii) infer intentions $\tilde{\mathbf{x}}$ from task structure; (iii) refit $\mathcal{D}_{k+1}$ on $(\mathbf{y}, \tilde{\mathbf{x}})$. The coupled dynamics are:

$$\begin{aligned}
\mathbf{u}_{k+1} &= f(\mathbf{u}_k, \mathcal{D}_k) && \text{(user adapts neural strategy)} \\
\mathcal{D}_{k+1} &= \arg\min_{\mathcal{D}} \mathcal{L}(\mathcal{D}; \mathbf{y}^{(k)}, \tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{(k)}) && \text{(decoder adapts)}
\end{aligned}$$

Three regimes are documented in the literature [8]:

- **Decoder-dominant adaptation:** fast CLDA with strong intention priors; the decoder chases a moving user. Risk: positive feedback — the decoder learns the user's *corrections to its own errors*, amplifying bias.
- **User-dominant adaptation:** fixed decoder; the user forms a stable "neural output map" over days. Slower but can reach higher asymptotes for fixed mappings.
- **Co-adaptation:** both adapt on matched timescales; requires the intention estimator to be *conservative* (high precision, lower recall) to avoid reinforcing errors.

> **Theorem (CLDA contraction, sketch):** *Suppose the user's intended kinematics $\mathbf{x}^*_t$ are Lipschitz in the decoder parameters with constant $L_u$, the intention estimator has bias bounded by $\epsilon$, and the refit operator is a contraction with factor $\kappa < 1$ on the decoder-parameter manifold. Then the coupled iteration converges to an $\epsilon/(1-\kappa L_u)$-neighborhood of a fixed point provided $\kappa L_u < 1$. Intuition: the decoder must learn faster than the user drifts, and the intention prior must be nearly unbiased.*

The condition $\kappa L_u < 1$ explains a practical rule of thumb: update the decoder in *batch* between blocks (small $\kappa$), not continuously sample-by-sample, so the user experiences a stable plant within each block.

### 4.4 Transformer-Based Spike-Train Decoding: The Neural Data Transformer

Classical decoders impose strong inductive biases (linearity, Markovianity). LFADS relaxed the observation model by learning a nonlinear dynamical system whose latent factors generate spikes via a Poisson process, achieving state-of-the-art denoising of single-trial population activity [11]. The Neural Data Transformer (NDT) goes further, discarding explicit dynamics for self-attention over binned spike trains:

- **Tokenization:** each time bin's $C$-dimensional spike-count vector is linearly embedded (optionally with channel identity embeddings); masked bins are reconstructed during pretraining, BERT-style.
- **Attention:** multi-head self-attention across time captures long-range dependencies — preparatory activity hundreds of milliseconds before movement, which Markovian filters truncate.
- **Readout:** a linear head maps the contextualized representation at each bin to kinematics or discrete targets.

Why this matters for BCI: closed-loop neural activity is *non-Markovian in the observed variables* — the user's corrections depend on the history of decoder errors, which the spikes encode. Attention models this history dependence natively, whereas a KF must expand the state or accept bias. Early NDT results show large offline gains over LFADS and KFs on primate reaching datasets, particularly under *zero-shot* cross-day transfer — exactly the regime where clinical BCIs live or die [11].

A minimal PyTorch sketch of the decoding head:

```python
import torch
import torch.nn as nn

class SpikeTransformerDecoder(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, n_channels, d_model=128, n_heads=4, n_layers=4,
                 bin_ms=20, max_seq=100):
        super().__init__()
        self.embed = nn.Linear(n_channels, d_model)
        self.pos = nn.Parameter(torch.randn(1, max_seq, d_model) * 0.02)
        layer = nn.TransformerEncoderLayer(d_model, n_heads,
                                           dim_feedforward=4*d_model,
                                           batch_first=True)
        self.encoder = nn.TransformerEncoder(layer, n_layers)
        self.readout = nn.Linear(d_model, 2)  # (vx, vy)

    def forward(self, spikes):
        # spikes: (B, T, C) binned counts, z-scored
        h = self.embed(spikes) + self.pos[:, :spikes.size(1)]
        h = self.encoder(h)          # full temporal context
        return self.readout(h)       # per-bin velocity estimate
```

Causal masking (no future bins) is required for online use; the latency cost is one forward pass per bin (~2–5 ms on embedded GPUs), well within the control budget.

## 5 Empirical Evaluation / Proofs

### 5.1 Benchmark results from the literature

| Study | Paradigm | Key result |
|---|---|---|
| Simeral *et al.* 2011 [2] | Human cursor control, ~1000 days post-implant | 94% success with ~20–30 neurons |
| Hochberg *et al.* 2012 [4] | Human robotic reach-and-grasp | First independent drinking via BCI |
| Gilja *et al.* 2012 [6] | Monkey center-out, ReFIT-KF | 2× faster acquisition vs. velocity KF |
| Willett *et al.* 2021 [12] | Human handwriting RNN decoder | 90 chars/min, 94.1% raw accuracy |
| Pandarinath *et al.* 2018 [11] | LFADS on primate M1/PMd | SOTA single-trial dynamics recovery |
| BrainGate wireless 2021 [9] | Wireless broadband BCI | Parity with wired fidelity |

The handwriting result [12] deserves emphasis: by decoding *attempted handwriting* — a temporally rich, high-DOF behavior — with an RNN plus language model, throughput more than doubled the prior BCI typing record, approaching smartphone typing speeds for the participant's age group. Dexterous, overlearned motor programs are *easier* to decode than point-to-point reaches, because their neural trajectories are more stereotyped and higher-dimensional — a direct argument for decoders with long temporal receptive fields, i.e., transformers.

### 5.2 Formal result: intention-estimated retraining reduces bias

> **Theorem:** *Let the seed decoder produce estimates $\hat{\mathbf{x}}_t = \mathbf{x}^*_t + \mathbf{b} + \boldsymbol{\eta}_t$, where $\mathbf{x}^*_t$ is true intention, $\mathbf{b}$ is systematic bias, and $\boldsymbol{\eta}_t$ zero-mean noise. Let the intention estimator output $\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_t = \mathbf{x}^*_t + \boldsymbol{\epsilon}_t$ with $\|\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_t]\| \le \delta \ll \|\mathbf{b}\|$. Then the least-squares observation matrix $\hat{H}_{IE}$ fit on $(\mathbf{y}_t, \tilde{\mathbf{x}}_t)$ satisfies $\|\mathbb{E}[\hat{H}_{IE}] - H^*\|_F \le \|\mathbb{E}[\hat{H}_{naive}] - H^*\|_F - c\|\mathbf{b}\|$ for some $c > 0$ depending on the excitation of the training trajectories, where $H^*$ is the oracle fit on true intentions.*
>
> *Proof sketch.* Naive refitting regresses $\mathbf{y}_t$ on $\hat{\mathbf{x}}_t$; because the user's corrective activity is generated by $\mathbf{x}^*_t$ but labeled with $\hat{\mathbf{x}}_t = \mathbf{x}^*_t + \mathbf{b}$, the regressor is corrupted by *label noise correlated with the residual*, the classic errors-in-variables pathology, which biases $\hat{H}$ toward the seed decoder's own errors. Intention estimation replaces the labels with $\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_t$, whose bias $\delta$ is bounded by the validity of the target-directed prior. Standard errors-in-variables bounds then give $\|\mathrm{bias}(\hat{H}_{IE})\| \le \kappa \delta < \kappa(\|\mathbf{b}\| - \delta') \le \|\mathrm{bias}(\hat{H}_{naive})\|$ under persistent excitation. ∎

### 5.3 Computational budget analysis

For $C=192$ channels, $\Delta=20$ ms, $d=128$, $L=4$ layers, $T=100$ context: NDT forward pass ≈ $O(L T^2 d) \approx 5 \times 10^6$ FLOPs — trivial for modern embedded accelerators at 50 Hz. The KF remains ~100× cheaper, which still matters for fully implanted, power-constrained systems (thermal budget < ~40 mW/cm² at the cortical surface). The pragmatic frontier: *distill* the transformer into a linear dynamical surrogate for the implant, retraining the teacher in the cloud.

## 6 Limitations

1. **Chronic instability.** Electrode yield decays; single units are lost and waveforms drift. Decoders must be *day-agnostic* (aligned latent spaces, domain adaptation) rather than refit from scratch — daily 10-minute calibration is a tax users will not pay forever.
2. **The labeling problem.** Intention estimation works for target-directed tasks; it fails for free behavior, speech, and any task where the prior over intention is weak. Unsupervised CLDA remains unsolved in general.
3. **Co-adaptation hazards.** Aggressive CLDA can trap the user–decoder pair in suboptimal fixed points (the $\kappa L_u < 1$ condition is rarely verified in practice). Maladaptive plasticity is a genuine clinical risk.
4. **Translation gap.** Monkey center-out performance does not predict human activities of daily living. The field's benchmarks (acquisition time, bitrate) underweight robustness, all-day use, and user fatigue.
5. **Transformer data hunger.** NDT-class models need large, diverse pretraining corpora; neural data are scarce, private, and nonstationary. Cross-subject transfer is nascent.
6. **Safety and longevity.** The BrainGate safety cohort (14 participants, 12,203 device-days) found 68 device-related adverse events, mostly skin irritation at the percutaneous pedestal [13]; fully implanted wireless systems (Neuralink N1: 1024 electrodes on 64 threads, robotic insertion [14]) trade the pedestal for new surgical risks. Regulatory pathways for *adaptive* algorithms — whose behavior changes post-implant — are unresolved.

---

## 7 Conclusion

Neural decoding for intracortical BCI has progressed through three eras: *linear estimation* (population vectors, Kalman filters), *closed-loop-aware calibration* (ReFIT-KF, CLDA, intention estimation), and now *sequence modeling of neural dynamics* (LFADS, neural data transformers). The through-line is not model capacity but respect for the closed loop: the training distribution is whatever the current decoder and the adapting user jointly produce, and every successful innovation has been, at bottom, a better way to label or model that distribution.

The research program this thesis recommends: (i) self-supervised pretraining on multi-subject, multi-day spike corpora with masked-bin objectives; (ii) causal, low-latency transformer decoders with certified latency bounds; (iii) conservative, contraction-guaranteed CLDA with explicit user-model co-adaptation analysis; (iv) benchmarks that measure *weeks* of autonomous home use, not minutes of lab control. The goal is not a better decoder for a session, but a prosthetic sense of agency that persists for a lifetime.
