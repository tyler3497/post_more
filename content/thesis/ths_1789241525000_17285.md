# The Turbulence Problem: Kolmogorov's 1941 Theory, Intermittency and the Multifractal Formalism, Onsager's Conjecture, and Convex Integration Constructions

## Abstract

Fully developed turbulence at high Reynolds number remains the last great unsolved problem of classical physics. This thesis reconstructs the century of theory that constrains it: Andrei Kolmogorov's 1941 universality hypothesis, which derives the −5/3 energy spectrum and the four-fifths law from statistical symmetries of the Navier–Stokes equations [1]; the experimental discovery of intermittency, the spatial clumpiness of dissipation that breaks K41's monofractal scaling and is captured by the multifractal formalism of Parisi and Frisch [2,3]; and Lars Onsager's 1949 conjecture that weak solutions of the incompressible Euler equations conserve kinetic energy above 1/3-Hölder regularity and may dissipate it below [4]. We survey the two-sided proof: Constantin, E and Titi's commutator argument establishing the rigid direction [5], and Philip Isett's 2018 convex-integration construction of dissipative 1/3−-Hölder Euler flows resolving the flexible direction [6,7]. We connect convex integration to phenomenological turbulence theory, assess the Navier–Stokes non-uniqueness program of Buckmaster and Vicol, and isolate the open questions that still separate mathematical wildness from physical turbulence: the vanishing-viscosity limit, the local energy inequality, and the physical selection principle among infinitely many weak solutions.

## 1 Introduction

When Richard Feynman reportedly called turbulence "the most important unsolved problem of classical physics," he was diagnosing a failure of reduction. The incompressible Navier–Stokes equations,

$$
\partial_t u + (u\cdot\nabla) u = -\nabla p + \nu \Delta u + f, \qquad \nabla\cdot u = 0,
$$

are among the most thoroughly studied PDEs in mathematics, yet their statistical behavior at high Reynolds number — the regime of rivers, storms, stars, and pipe flow — resists every systematic derivation. The core difficulty is *scale separation*: energy is injected at integral scales $L$, cascaded through an inertial range of eddies, and finally dissipated by viscosity at the Kolmogorov scale $\eta = (\nu^3/\varepsilon)^{1/4}$, with $\eta/L \sim Re^{-3/4}$ so that for $Re \sim 10^6$ the system spans four to five decades of dynamically coupled scales. Direct analytical closure is hopeless; turbulence theory has therefore proceeded as a sequence of *phenomenological principles* — scaling hypotheses that are stated axiomatically and tested experimentally.

> **Thesis statement.** The modern mathematical theory of turbulence is organized around three pillars — (i) Kolmogorov's 1941 dimensional and symmetry analysis yielding universal inertial-range laws, (ii) the multifractal correction demanded by intermittency of the dissipation field, and (iii) Onsager's identification of $1/3$-Hölder regularity as the sharp threshold for anomalous energy dissipation in ideal flow — and its most dramatic recent success is the proof of Onsager's conjecture by convex integration, which converts a physical intuition about dissipation into a theorem about *non-uniqueness* of weak Euler solutions. The unresolved question is no longer whether mathematics can produce wild dissipation, but whether the wildness it produces is the same wildness that nature exhibits.

The remainder of this thesis develops each pillar in turn, culminating in the convex-integration program and its uneasy relationship with physical turbulence.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 From Richardson to Kolmogorov

The qualitative picture of the energy cascade is due to Lewis Fry Richardson (1922), immortalized in the parody-verse *"Big whirls have little whirls that feed on their velocity, and little whirls have lesser whirls, and so on to viscosity."* Energy enters at scale $L$, is transferred essentially losslessly down the inertial range, and is dissipated at scale $\eta$ where the local Reynolds number becomes order one. Kolmogorov (1941) converted this picture into quantitative theory with two hypotheses [1]:

1. **First similarity hypothesis.** At sufficiently high Reynolds number, the statistics of the small-scale motions (scales $\ell \ll L$) are universal and determined solely by the kinematic viscosity $\nu$ and the mean energy dissipation rate $\varepsilon = 2\nu \overline{S_{ij}S_{ij}}$.
2. **Second similarity hypothesis.** For scales $\eta \ll \ell \ll L$ (the *inertial range*), the statistics are universal and determined *solely by $\varepsilon$*, independent of $\nu$.

Dimensional analysis then fixes the form of the energy spectrum: since $[E(k)] = L^3 T^{-2}$ and $[\varepsilon] = L^2 T^{-3}$, the only combination with the right units is

$$
E(k) = C_K \, \varepsilon^{2/3} k^{-5/3},
$$

the celebrated Kolmogorov −5/3 law, with a universal constant $C_K \approx 1.5$ extracted from experiments. Equally central are the *structure functions* of longitudinal velocity increments $\delta_\ell u = [u(x+\ell\hat{r}) - u(x)]\cdot\hat{r}$:

$$
S_p(\ell) = \overline{|\delta_\ell u|^p} = C_p \, \varepsilon^{p/3} \, \ell^{p/3},
$$

where the overbar denotes statistical averaging. The case $p = 2$ reproduces the spectrum; the case $p = 3$ is special, because the Navier–Stokes equations themselves constrain it.

### 2.2 The Four-Fifths Law

Kolmogorov derived in 1941 an *exact* relation, valid in the limit $\nu \to 0$ for the inertial range, from the Kármán–Howarth–Monin equation:

> **Theorem (Kolmogorov's four-fifths law, 1941).** For homogeneous isotropic turbulence in the limit $Re \to \infty$,
>
> $$S_3(\ell) = \overline{(\delta_\ell u)^3} = -\frac{4}{5}\,\varepsilon\,\ell, \qquad \eta \ll \ell \ll L.$$
>
> The relation holds with no undetermined constant and no adjustable parameters; the minus sign reflects the *downscale* direction of the cascade.

This is the single exact, parameter-free prediction of classical turbulence theory, and it has been confirmed to remarkable precision in grid turbulence and direct numerical simulation [2]. It also encodes the *dissipation anomaly*: since $\varepsilon$ stays finite as $\nu \to 0$, the Euler equations ($\nu = 0$) must somehow *dissipate* energy — a paradox for a formally conservative system that Onsager would weaponize eight years later.

### 2.3 The Breakdown: Intermittency

K41's boldest assumption is statistical homogeneity of the increments — that every point of the fluid sees the same scaling. By the 1960s, experiments on the *dissipation field* $\varepsilon(x,t)$ itself revealed violent spatial and temporal fluctuations: dissipation is concentrated in thin filaments and sheets occupying a small fraction of the volume. This is *intermittency*. Measured structure-function exponents,

$$
S_p(\ell) \sim \ell^{\zeta_p},
$$

systematically deviate from the K41 line $\zeta_p = p/3$, with $\zeta_p < p/3$ for $p > 3$ and $\zeta_p > p/3$ for $p < 3$ — the anomalous scaling signature.

## 3 Methodology

This thesis synthesizes three methodological traditions that rarely appear in a single document:

- **Phenomenological analysis.** We derive K41 laws from dimensional and symmetry arguments, following the modern presentation in which symmetries of the Navier–Stokes equations (scaling, Galilean, rotation) are identified, shown to be broken by forcing and boundaries, and *statistically restored* by the chaotic cascade [2]. This "symmetry broken, then restored" narrative is the conceptual engine of Frisch's 1995 monograph *Turbulence: The Legacy of A. N. Kolmogorov*.
- **PDE and convex-integration methods.** We present the two-sided proof of Onsager's conjecture: the *rigid* direction via mollifier-commutator estimates in Besov spaces [5], and the *flexible* direction via convex integration — an iterative scheme descending from Nash's $C^1$ isometric embeddings through De Lellis and Székelyhidi's adaptation to the Euler equations to Isett's resolution [6,7].


```python
import numpy as np

def structure_function_exponents(velocity_series, scales, orders):
    """Estimate zeta_p from S_p(l) ~ l^zeta_p by log-log regression."""
    exponents = {}
    for p in orders:
        log_S, log_l = [], []
        for l in scales:
            increments = np.abs(np.diff(velocity_series, n=l)) if l > 1 else np.diff(velocity_series))
            log_S.append(np.log(np.mean(increments ** p) + 1e-30))
            log_l.append(np.log(l))
        zeta_p, _ = np.polyfit(log_l, log_S, 1)  # slope = zeta_p
        exponents[p] = zeta_p
    return exponents

# K41 prediction: zeta_p = p/3. Intermittent (She-Leveque): zeta_p = p/9 + 2*(1-(2/3)**(p/3))
for p in [2, 4, 6, 8]:
    sl = p/9 + 2*(1-(2/3)**(p/3))
    print(f"p={p}: K41 zeta={p/3:.3f}, She-Leveque zeta={sl:.3f}")
```

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Four-Fifths Law as a Non-Perturbative Anchor

Most of turbulence theory is uncontrolled modeling; the four-fifths law stands apart as a *theorem about the Navier–Stokes equations* (in the $\nu \to 0$ limit, under homogeneity and isotropy). Its derivation repays attention because every later development — multifractal constraints, Onsager's exponent, convex-integration targets — is calibrated against it.

Starting from the Kármán–Howarth–Monin relation for the second-order correlation tensor, one takes the divergence and passes to the limit of vanishing viscosity. The viscous term drops out of the inertial range by order-of-magnitude estimates, and what remains is a balance between the *flux* term $\nabla\cdot\overline{(\delta_\ell u)^2 \delta_\ell u}$ and the mean dissipation $\varepsilon$. In isotropic form this integrates to $S_3(\ell) = -4\varepsilon\ell/5$. Three consequences matter for this thesis:

1. **It fixes $\zeta_3 = 1$ exactly.** Any intermittency model must satisfy the constraint $\zeta_3 = 1$, which in the multifractal formalism becomes the inequality $D(h) \ge 1 - 3h$ with equality attained somewhere [3].
3. **It sets Onsager's exponent.** Since $\zeta_3 = 1$ means $\overline{|\delta_\ell u|^3} \sim \ell$, typical increments scale as $|\delta_\ell u| \sim \ell^{1/3}$: the Euler velocity field of turbulence is naturally $1/3$-Hölder, no more, no less. Onsager's genius was to promote this scaling observation into a *conjecture about weak solutions*.

### 4.2 Intermittency and the Multifractal Formalism

The experimental record is unambiguous. Anselmet, Gagne, Hopfinger and Antonia (1984) measured structure functions up to order $p = 18$ in a turbulent jet and found systematic departures from $p/3$; wavelet analyses of the Modane wind tunnel (Taylor-microscale Reynolds number $R_\lambda \sim 2700$, inertial range spanning four decades) later gave the first *visual* evidence of the Richardson cascade's fractal character [3].
Parisi and Frisch (1985) supplied it. Their *multifractal model* postulates that at scale $\ell$, the velocity increment has local Hölder exponent $h$ with probability

$$
P_\ell(h) \sim \ell^{\,3 - D(h)},
$$

where the set of points with exponent $h$ has Hausdorff dimension $D(h)$. The structure functions then follow from a Laplace-type (large-deviation) integral,

$$
S_p(\ell) \sim \int \ell^{ph}\, \ell^{3-D(h)} \,dh \sim \ell^{\zeta_p}, \qquad \zeta_p = \inf_h \big[\,ph + 3 - D(h)\,\big],
$$

so $\zeta_p$ is the Legendre transform of $D(h)$. The four-fifths constraint $\zeta_3 = 1$ becomes $D(h) \ge 1 - 3h$ with equality for at least one $h$ — the codimension function $C(h) = 3 - D(h)$ satisfies $C(h) \ge 1 - 3h$ [3]. K41 is recovered as the *monofractal* special case $D(1/3) = 3$, $D(h) = -\infty$ otherwise.

The most successful parameterization of $D(h)$ remains She and Lévêque (1994), derived from a hierarchical vortex-filament picture:

| Order $p$ | K41 $\zeta_p = p/3$ | She–Lévêque $\zeta_p$ | Experiment (Modane) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2 | 0.667 | 0.696 | 0.70 ± 0.01 |
| 4 | 1.333 | 1.280 | 1.28 ± 0.02 |
| 6 | 2.000 | 1.778 | 1.78 ± 0.03 |
| 8 | 2.667 | 2.211 | 2.20 ± 0.05 |
| 10 | 3.333 | 2.593 | 2.60 ± 0.08 |

The agreement is striking, yet the model is *kinematic*: it describes the statistics of singularities without deriving them from Navier–Stokes.
### 4.3 Onsager's 1949 Conjecture: The 1/3 Threshold

In a brief 1949 note on statistical hydrodynamics, Lars Onsager — already famous for the exact solution of the 2D Ising model — made an observation that would take seventy years to prove. For smooth solutions of the incompressible Euler equations, kinetic energy $E(t) = \tfrac{1}{2}\int |u|^2 dx$ is exactly conserved. But turbulent flows in the $\nu \to 0$ limit exhibit the dissipation anomaly: $\varepsilon > 0$ persists. Onsager conjectured that this anomaly is carried by the *roughness* of the limiting velocity field [4]:

> **Conjecture (Onsager, 1949).**
> (a) *Rigidity:* Every weak solution $u$ of the 3D incompressible Euler equations with spatial Hölder regularity $C^{0,\alpha}$, $\alpha > 1/3$, conserves kinetic energy.
> (b) *Flexibility:* For every $\alpha < 1/3$, there exist weak solutions in $C^{0,\alpha}$ that *dissipate* kinetic energy — anomalous dissipation without viscosity.

The exponent $1/3$ is no accident: it is the K41 exponent from §4.1. Onsager had identified the precise regularity at which the nonlinear term $u\cdot\nabla u$ can sustain a nonzero energy flux. The conjecture lay dormant for decades — the first weak solutions with compact support in time were constructed by Scheffer (1993) and Shnirelman (1997), but with no Hölder control — until two breakthroughs settled the two directions separately.

**The rigid direction: Constantin, E, Titi (1994).** Eyink (1994) first proved conservation for $\alpha > 1/2$ using Besov-space techniques; Constantin, E and Titi then proved the sharp $\alpha > 1/3$ result [5]. The method is a mollifier-commutator estimate: mollify the weak Euler equations at scale $\ell$,

$$
\partial_t u_\ell + \nabla\cdot (u_\ell \otimes u_\ell) + \nabla p_\ell = \nabla\cdot R_\ell, \qquad R_\ell = u_\ell \otimes u_\ell - (u\otimes u)_\ell,
$$

and show the commutator stress satisfies $\|R_\ell\|_{L^{3/2}} \lesssim \ell^{3\alpha-1}\|u\|_{C^\alpha}^3$, which vanishes as $\ell \to 0$ iff $\alpha > 1/3$. The energy flux $\int R_\ell : \nabla u_\ell$ then tends to zero, and energy is conserved. The argument is robust: it extends to Besov spaces $B^{1/3+}_{3,\infty}$ and to bounded domains with suitable boundary conditions.

**The flexible direction: Isett (2018).** The converse required constructing *wild* solutions, and the only known tool is convex integration. After partial results — De Lellis–Székelyhidi continuous dissipative solutions (2009–2013), Isett's $C^{1/5-}$ solutions, Buckmaster–De Lellis–Székelyhidi–Vicol's $L^1_t C^{1/3-}_x$ solutions — Philip Isett proved the full flexible side [6,7]:

> **Theorem (Isett, 2018).** For every $\alpha < 1/3$ there exist weak solutions $u \in C_t C^{0,\alpha}_x$ of the 3D incompressible Euler equations on $\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{T}^3$, with nonempty compact support in time, that do not conserve kinetic energy. Combined with Constantin–E–Titi, this proves Onsager's conjecture.

The construction iterates: at each stage $q$, a subsolution $(u_q, R_q)$ of the Euler–Reynolds system $\partial_t u_q + \nabla\cdot(u_q\otimes u_q) + \nabla p_q = \nabla\cdot R_q$ is perturbed by highly oscillatory *Mikado flows* — pipe-like Beltrami building blocks introduced by Daneri and Székelyhidi — chosen so the new Reynolds stress $R_{q+1}$ is strictly smaller while the Hölder norm grows in a controlled way. A novel *gluing approximation* repairs the loss of temporal localization. The endpoint is a genuine weak solution whose energy profile can even be prescribed [6].

### 4.4 Convex Integration: From Nash to Turbulence

Convex integration entered fluid dynamics from a seemingly unrelated source: John Nash's 1954 construction of $C^1$ isometric embeddings of Riemannian manifolds, later systematized by Gromov as the $h$-principle — the philosophy that underdetermined PDEs admit *flexible*, wildly non-unique low-regularity solutions. De Lellis and Székelyhidi (2009) recognized the Euler equations as a target: writing them as a differential inclusion with a pointwise constraint set, they adapted Nash's iteration to produce Hölder-continuous Euler flows violating energy conservation [7].

The analogy with turbulence phenomenology runs deeper than technique. Buckmaster and Vicol's 2019 review argues for "fundamental analogies" between convex integration and phenomenological turbulence theories [7]:

1. **Intermittency informs construction.** The newest convex-integration schemes (Buckmaster–Vicol, 2019; subsequent intermittent jets) deliberately *concentrate* the building blocks in space-time, mimicking the spatiotemporal intermittency of real dissipation — and the intermittent constructions reach higher regularity precisely because concentration reduces the $L^2$-cost of oscillation.
2. **Non-uniqueness as a feature.** Convex integration proves that below the Onsager threshold, the Cauchy problem for Euler is ill-posed: infinitely many weak solutions share the same initial data. Turbulence phenomenology has long suspected that the inviscid limit selects *some* non-unique weak solution; convex integration shows the solution set is vast, sharpening the question of *which* selection principle — vanishing viscosity, entropy rate, maximal dissipation — nature uses.
3. **The critical space.** Both theories single out $1/3$: phenomenology via $\zeta_3 = 1$, PDE theory via the sharp Onsager threshold. This coincidence of a physical scaling exponent with a PDE regularity threshold is among the deepest facts in the subject.

### 4.5 The Navier–Stokes Frontier and the Physicality Gap

The Euler story is now essentially complete, but physics cares about Navier–Stokes. Buckmaster and Vicol (2019) extended convex integration to the *viscous* equations, constructing infinitely many weak solutions in $C_t^0 L^{2+}_x \cap C_t^0 W^{1,1+}_x$ — non-uniqueness for Navier–Stokes itself, albeit far below Leray–Hopf regularity [7]. Subsequent work has produced intermittent Onsager-critical constructions and even $H^{1/2-}$ dissipative Euler flows.

Yet a chasm remains between these theorems and physical turbulence:

- **Vanishing-viscosity limits.** Convex-integration solutions are not known to arise as $\nu \to 0$ limits of Navier–Stokes solutions. Isett observed that a strong $L^3$ vanishing-viscosity limit would satisfy the *local* energy inequality, which the known wild solutions violate — so the physical selection, if it exists, must live in a restricted subclass [6].
- **Statistical content.** The theorems produce *individual* wild solutions, while turbulence is a *statistical* theory. Bridging "there exists a dissipative solution" to "typical solutions dissipate with spectrum $k^{-5/3}$" requires a probabilistic convex integration that does not yet exist.

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

We summarize the evidential status of each pillar, distinguishing theorem, measurement, and model:

1. **Four-fifths law.** *Theorem* (in the $\nu\to 0$ inertial-range limit under isotropy) and *measurement*: confirmed in grid turbulence, jets, and DNS to within experimental uncertainty; the prefactor $-4/5$ is exact [1,2].
2. **$-5/3$ spectrum.** *Measurement* with overwhelming universality — atmospheric boundary layers, ocean microstructure, wind tunnels, and DNS all reproduce $k^{-5/3}$ over the inertial range — but *not* a theorem: no derivation from Navier–Stokes without statistical hypotheses exists.
3. **Anomalous exponents $\zeta_p \neq p/3$.** *Measurement*: the departure is small but statistically ironclad (Anselmet et al.; Modane $R_\lambda \approx 2700$ data spanning four decades) [3].
4. **Multifractal formalism.** *Model*: the Legendre pair $(\zeta_p, D(h))$ organizes all data, and the She–Lévêque formula predicts exponents to within error bars (table, §4.2), but $D(h)$ is fitted, not derived.
5. **Onsager rigidity ($\alpha > 1/3$).** *Theorem*: Constantin–E–Titi (1994), sharp in Hölder and Besov scales [5].
6. **Onsager flexibility ($\alpha < 1/3$).** *Theorem*: Isett (2018), Annals of Mathematics, via convex integration with Mikado flows and gluing [6].
7. **Navier–Stokes non-uniqueness.** *Theorem* (Buckmaster–Vicol 2019) at very low regularity [7]; whether non-uniqueness reaches Leray–Hopf solutions is open and is arguably the most important open problem in mathematical fluid dynamics.


## 6 Limitations

Intellectual honesty requires stating what this theoretical edifice does *not* achieve:

- **Multifractality is descriptive.** The Parisi–Frisch formalism is a *kinematic* large-deviation ansatz; it predicts relations among exponents but does not produce $D(h)$ from the equations of motion. Competing intermittency models (log-normal, log-Poisson, She–Lévêque) fit the same data with different physical stories, and experiment cannot yet discriminate their deep claims.
- **Convex integration is non-physical by construction.** The wild solutions are engineered to hit a regularity target, not to resemble turbulence. They lack a cascade, a dissipation range, and any demonstrated connection to vanishing viscosity. Celebrating Isett's theorem as "solving turbulence" confuses a sharp regularity threshold with a physical mechanism.
- **The selection problem is untouched.** Even granting that the inviscid limit of Navier–Stokes is a non-unique weak Euler solution below $1/3$, mathematics currently offers no canonical selection criterion — maximal dissipation rate, entropy conditions, and stochastic selection are all conjectural.
- **Reynolds-number dependence.** Strictly, all "universal" statements are asymptotic in $Re \to \infty$. Finite-$Re$ corrections, bottleneck effects near the dissipation range, and the approach to asymptotics remain active experimental concerns.

## 7 Conclusion

The turbulence problem has been transformed, over eighty years, from a mystery about spectra into a precise dialogue between phenomenology and PDE theory. Kolmogorov's 1941 hypotheses gave the field its universal language — the −5/3 spectrum, the structure functions, the exact four-fifths law. Intermittency broke the monofractal symmetry and forced the multifractal refinement of Parisi and Frisch, which restores universality at the level of the singularity spectrum $D(h)$. Onsager's 1949 conjecture identified $1/3$-Hölder regularity as the exact threshold where ideal flow may dissipate energy, and its two-sided proof — Constantin, E and Titi's commutator estimates on the rigid side, Isett's convex-integration construction on the flexible side — is the century's deepest mathematical result about turbulence.
