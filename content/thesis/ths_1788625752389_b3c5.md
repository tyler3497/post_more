# Kademlia and the XOR Metric: Unidirectional Routing, K-Bucket Maintenance, Parallel Iterative Lookup, and Security Hardening in Distributed Hash Tables

## 1 Introduction

Napster kept a centralized index; Gnutella removed it at the cost of flooding whose traffic grew superlinearly with network size. The research community's answer was the *distributed hash table* (DHT): a decentralized key–value store in which the mapping from keys to responsible nodes is computed deterministically, so that any participant can route a query to the correct server using only partial knowledge of the network. Between 2001 and 2002, four landmark designs — Chord [2], CAN, Pastry, and Tapestry — established the basic vocabulary of consistent hashing, identifier-space partitioning, and logarithmic-hop routing.

Kademlia, presented by Petar Maymounkov and David Mazières at IPTPS 2002 [1], distinguished itself by *subtracting* machinery: distance between identifiers is the bitwise **XOR** operation, and from this single metric flow a symmetric, unidirectional topology; k-bucket routing tables refreshed as a side effect of ordinary lookups; parallel asynchronous queries that never block on a failed node; and formal proofs of consistency under weak assumptions about node uptime.

> **Thesis statement.** The XOR metric is not merely a distance function but a complete design discipline: it simultaneously defines the routing topology, the eviction policy, the lookup algorithm's termination argument, and the security properties of the resulting network. Understanding Kademlia means understanding what XOR buys — and what it costs.

This thesis reconstructs the protocol from first principles, verifies its parameters against production deployments (BitTorrent, Kad, IPFS/libp2p), derives its performance bounds, and analyzes Sybil and eclipse attacks together with the S/Kademlia hardening proposal [2].

---

## 2 Background

A DHT solves the *decentralized lookup problem*: given a key, locate the responsible node(s) using per-node state and hop counts that grow at most logarithmically with network size. The standard technique is **consistent hashing** — node IDs and keys share one space, and each key is assigned to the node(s) closest to it under some metric, so membership changes disturb only neighboring keys.

The contemporary designs differed principally in their choice of metric and the routing tables that choice implied:

| System | Identifier space | Distance metric | Routing state | Hops |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Chord | 160-bit ring | clockwise arc length | $O(\log n)$ fingers | $O(\log n)$ |
| CAN | $d$-dimensional torus | Euclidean | $O(d)$ neighbors | $O(d \cdot n^{1/d})$ |
| Pastry | 128-bit ring | numeric difference | $O(\log n)$ rows | $O(\log n)$ |
| Tapestry | 160-bit, base $\beta$ | prefix/suffix match | $O(\log_\beta n)$ levels | $O(\log_\beta n)$ |
| **Kademlia** [1] | 160-bit | **XOR** | $O(\log n)$ k-buckets | $O(\log n)$ |

Kademlia's innovation was to observe that the metric itself could do more work. Chord's clockwise metric is *asymmetric*: the distance from $x$ to $y$ differs from the distance from $y$ to $x$. This asymmetry has a subtle but corrosive consequence — a node receiving a lookup query learns about the *sender*, but the sender's distribution over the identifier space differs from the distribution of nodes the receiver would like to store, so the learned contact is frequently useless for the receiver's own routing table. Kademlia's XOR metric is *symmetric*, and, as we shall see, this single property means that **every message a node receives teaches it something useful about the region of the identifier space it is responsible for**. Configuration traffic is thereby folded into lookup traffic, and the protocol's maintenance overhead approaches zero in the steady state [1].

### 2.1 Design goals

The paper's stated goals — provable consistency and performance under churn, minimal configuration messaging, flexible low-latency routing through redundant path choice, parallel asynchronous queries that mask node failures, and a contact-recording discipline resistant to basic denial-of-service attacks [1] — shaped every subsequent decision and remain the benchmark for DHT designs.

---

## 3 Methodology

This thesis proceeds in four stages. **First**, we reconstruct the core protocol — XOR metric, k-buckets, RPC layer, lookup algorithm — from the primary source [1], with the key theorems. **Second**, we verify parameters and engineering adaptations against three production deployments: the BitTorrent Mainline DHT (BEP-5 [3]), the IPFS/libp2p Kademlia DHT [4], and the eMule Kad network [5]. **Third**, we derive the asymptotic bounds and report published probabilistic analyses [6] sharpening $O(\log n)$ to an explicit constant. **Fourth**, we analyze Sybil and eclipse attacks and evaluate the S/Kademlia countermeasures — cryptographic identifiers, crypto puzzles, disjoint-path lookups, sibling broadcast [2].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The XOR Metric: Definition and Properties

Every Kademlia participant holds a 160-bit **node ID**, typically the SHA-1 hash of some identifying data or a uniformly random value; every stored item is addressed by a 160-bit **key** in the same space [1]. Distance is defined as the bitwise exclusive-or of the two identifiers, interpreted as an unsigned integer:

$$d(x, y) = x \oplus y$$

For intuition: $x = 1010_2$, $y = 0110_2$ gives $d = 1100_2 = 12$; the highest differing bit dominates the magnitude.

> **Theorem (XOR is a metric).** $d(x,y) = x \oplus y$ satisfies: *(i)* $d(x,x) = 0$; *(ii)* $d(x,y) > 0$ for $x \neq y$; *(iii)* $d(x,y) = d(y,x)$; *(iv)* $d(x,z) \le d(x,y) + d(y,z)$.
>
> *Proof sketch.* (i)–(iii) are immediate from bitwise XOR. For (iv), at every bit position where $x \oplus z$ has a 1, at least one of $x \oplus y$ or $y \oplus z$ also has a 1; the inequality follows bitwise. ∎

> **Theorem (Unidirectionality).** For any point $x$ and any distance $\Delta > 0$, there exists *exactly one* point $y$ with $d(x, y) = \Delta$ — namely $y = x \oplus \Delta$ (XOR is self-inverse). ∎

Unidirectionality is what makes caching effective: every lookup for the same key converges along the *same* path regardless of origin, so key–value pairs cached along lookup paths reliably intercept future queries for popular keys, alleviating hot spots [1].

> **Theorem (Symmetry dividend).** Because $d(x,y) = d(y,x)$, a node's incoming queries arrive from the same distribution of identifier regions its own routing table covers — so every received RPC teaches it something useful. Queries *are* maintenance traffic.

This eliminates Kademlia's need for a separate stabilization protocol. In Chord, by contrast, queries arrive from nodes distributed asymmetrically relative to the receiver's finger table, so learned contacts are often useless [1].

The XOR metric also admits a clean **binary-trie interpretation**. View all 160-bit IDs as leaves of a binary tree branching on each successive bit: the magnitude of $d(x,y)$ is the height of the smallest subtree containing both leaves, and the closest leaf to a target $x$ is the one sharing the longest common prefix with $x$ [1]. Routing reduces to *descending the trie* — each hop should lengthen the shared prefix with the target — so a lookup that gains at least one prefix bit per hop terminates in at most 160 hops, and far fewer in practice (Section 5).

```python
def xor_distance(a: bytes, b: bytes) -> int:
    return int.from_bytes(a, "big") ^ int.from_bytes(b, "big")

def bucket_index(own_id: bytes, peer_id: bytes) -> int:
    return xor_distance(own_id, peer_id).bit_length() - 1  # bucket i: d in [2^i, 2^(i+1))
```

### 4.2 K-Buckets: The Routing Table

For each $0 \le i < 160$, every node maintains a **k-bucket**: up to $k$ contacts — $\langle\text{IP address}, \text{UDP port}, \text{Node ID}\rangle$ triples — at XOR distance $[2^i, 2^{i+1})$ [1]. Nearby buckets (small $i$) span enormous identifier regions and are typically sparse; far buckets are dense and capped at $k$.

The paper sets $k = 20$ so that any $k$ nodes are *very unlikely to fail within an hour of each other* [1]. BitTorrent's Mainline DHT, operating at larger scale with higher churn, uses $k = 8$ [3]; IPFS/libp2p retains $k = 20$ over a 256-bit SHA-256 space [4].

Each k-bucket is kept sorted by **time last seen** — the least-recently seen node at the head, the most-recently seen at the tail — and the eviction policy exploits this ordering for both churn resilience and security:

1. When a node learns of a new contact for a non-full bucket, the contact is appended at the tail.
2. When the bucket is *full*, the node **pings the least-recently seen contact** (the head). Only if that contact *fails to respond* is it evicted and the new contact appended; if it responds, it is moved to the tail and the newcomer is discarded [1].

> **Lemma (Old-node preference).** Under ping-before-evict, a k-bucket's membership converges to the longest-lived nodes in its range.

The bias is deliberate. *Empirically*, the paper validates against P2P measurements that long-lived nodes tend to stay online. *Adversarially*, it resists flooding: an attacker minting fresh Sybil identities cannot displace honest contacts without those contacts actually failing, since eviction requires unresponsiveness [1]. The bucket is a low-pass filter on churn, admitting newcomers only into vacant or dead slots.

Buckets are also **refreshed proactively**: any k-bucket untouched by lookup traffic for an hour is refreshed via a node lookup for a random key in its range [1], bounding routing-state staleness. Per-node state is $O(k \log n)$ contacts — at most 160 buckets of $k$ entries, though only about $\log_2 n$ are non-empty in a network of $n$ nodes.

### 4.3 Parallel Iterative Lookup and the RPC Layer

The wire protocol is minimal: exactly four RPCs over UDP [1]:

| RPC | Arguments | Returns |
|---|---|---|
| `PING` | — | proof of liveness |
| `STORE` | key, value | acknowledgment |
| `FIND_NODE` | 160-bit target ID | $k$ contacts closest to target known to recipient |
| `FIND_VALUE` | 160-bit key | value if stored, else $k$ closest contacts |

Every RPC carries a 160-bit random **RPC ID** echoed by the recipient, giving basic resistance to address forgery; pings may be piggybacked on replies for sender-address assurance [1]. `FIND_VALUE` short-circuits retrieval when a node holds the key, and the *caching* rule then stores the pair at the closest node on the search path that lacked it — unidirectionality places replicas on future lookup paths.

The **node lookup** — locating the $k$ closest nodes to a target ID — is the primitive from which store, retrieve, join, and refresh are all built:

```
LOOKUP(target):
    shortlist ← α closest known nodes; queried ← ∅
    loop:
        candidates ← α closest in shortlist not in queried
        if empty: candidates ← k closest not in queried; if empty: break
        send FIND_NODE(target) to candidates in parallel (async)
        merge responses into shortlist; drop unresponsive nodes
        queried ← queried ∪ candidates
    return k closest in shortlist
```

Concretely: the initiator picks $\alpha$ nodes from its closest non-empty k-bucket (or the $\alpha$ closest known nodes), sending parallel asynchronous `FIND_NODE` RPCs — $\alpha$ is the system-wide **concurrency parameter**, suggested as $\alpha = 3$ [1]. As responses arrive, newly learned contacts are folded into the shortlist and queries re-issued to the $\alpha$ closest unqueried nodes *without waiting for outstanding RPCs*. If a full round surfaces no node closer than the best seen, the initiator escalates to *all* $k$ closest unqueried nodes; the lookup terminates once the $k$ closest seen nodes have been queried and answered [1].

Three decisions deserve emphasis. **Iterative, not recursive:** control never leaves the initiator — in *recursive* routing each hop forwards the query and the originator waits on a chain of delegations, so one slow or malicious intermediary stalls the lookup. Kademlia keeps all state at the originator, which applies per-RPC timeouts and cross-checks $\alpha$ parallel responses [1]. **Parallelism masks failure:** $\alpha$ concurrent queries mean the algorithm never blocks on any single response, so failed nodes impose no timeout delay; with $\alpha = 1$ the message cost resembles Chord's, but any of $k$ candidates may serve as next hop [1]. **Termination is guaranteed by the metric:** each successful round strictly lengthens the shared prefix with the target, so the shortlist converges monotonically, and the widening rule ensures progress even against stale contacts.

To *store* a $\langle\text{key}, \text{value}\rangle$ pair, a participant looks up the key and sends `STORE` to the $k$ closest nodes found. Persistence is maintained by **republication**: nodes republish held pairs as necessary, the original publisher republishes every 24 hours, and unrepublished pairs expire — bounding stale index entries under churn [1].

### 4.4 Join, Bootstrap, and Maintenance

A new node $u$ joins in three phases [1]:

1. **Bootstrap contact.** $u$ inserts a known participant $w$ (obtained out-of-band, e.g. a hard-coded bootstrap list) into its routing table.
2. **Self-lookup.** $u$ runs `FIND_NODE` for *its own node ID*. The $k$ closest nodes to $u$ are exactly those whose buckets should contain $u$; the lookup populates $u$'s tables while announcing $u$ to its neighborhood (every query teaches recipients about the sender).
3. **Distant refresh.** $u$ refreshes all k-buckets *farther* than its closest neighbor via lookups for random keys in each bucket range, acquiring a well-distributed sample of the identifier space.

IPFS operationalizes this: a joining peer needs only one of eight pre-configured bootstrap nodes run by Protocol Labs; querying it for the peer's own ID cascades into full table population [4]. BitTorrent clients bootstrap similarly from `router.bittorrent.com` or `dht.transmissionbt.com` [3].

Steady-state maintenance — hourly refresh, republication, 24-hour expiry [1] — then bounds stale-state half-life with no dedicated stabilization protocol.

### 4.5 Security: Sybil Attacks, Eclipse Attacks, and S/Kademlia

Kademlia's openness is its vulnerability: node IDs are self-selected, identities are free, and routing depends on strangers' honesty. Two attack classes dominate.

**Sybil attacks.** With no admission control and no cost to minting identities, one adversary can inject arbitrarily many nodes [5]. The Kad network (eMule's 128-bit Kademlia variant) is the cautionary tale: persistent but unverifiable IDs with no host binding made Sybil injection straightforward, though measurements showed Sybil alone to be surprisingly ineffective without accompanying routing manipulation [5]. The deeper danger is *targeted* placement: grinding identifiers to surround a specific key with malicious replica roots and capture its traffic.

**Eclipse attacks.** The adversary populates a victim's k-buckets and shortlists with attacker-controlled nodes, isolating it from the honest network so lookups are observed, dropped, or manipulated [7]. Because nodes prefer long-lived contacts, a *patient* attacker keeping Sybils online can gradually infiltrate buckets; because lookups trust the $k$ closest returned contacts, a *concentrated* attacker near a target key can dominate its replica set. A large-scale evaluation against IPFS's libp2p Kademlia demonstrated total eclipse of peer discovery with modest resources, exploiting the unbounded connection "swarm" surrounding the bounded DHT table [4].

**Intrinsic defenses** are real but partial: ping-before-evict blunts flooding [1], the echoed RPC ID complicates forgery [1], and $\alpha$-parallel lookups stop any single liar from derailing a search. None raises the *cost* of identity creation — the root enabler.

**S/Kademlia** (Baumgart and Mies, 2007) is the most complete hardening proposal, adding four interlocking countermeasures while preserving XOR routing [2]:

1. **Crypto puzzles for node-ID generation.** A node must expend computational work to generate its identifier (e.g., a proof-of-work condition on trailing bits), making mass Sybil creation expensive. The work is *incorporated into the node ID itself*, so verification is trivial and XOR routing is unaffected [2][8].
2. **Signed messages and certified identifiers.** Identifiers are bound to public keys, so claimed identities are cryptographically verifiable — defeating the cheap impersonation Kad permitted [2][5].
3. **Disjoint-path parallel lookups.** Lookups proceed over $d \ge 2$ *disjoint paths* through the identifier space; an attacker must compromise *every* path to subvert a lookup, multiplying the required Sybil investment [2]. Storj's V3 network adopted this prescription — public-key-derived IDs, signatures, vetting, disjoint lookups — in consultation with co-author Petar Maymounkov [8].
4. **Sibling lists and reliable broadcast.** Each node tracks its *sibling list* (nodes closest to its own ID); reliable broadcast among siblings closes the inconsistency windows eclipse attacks exploit [2].

Simulations show these measures jointly raise attack cost by orders of magnitude at modest lookup overhead [2]. The general lesson: in an open DHT, *identity must be expensive and verifiable*, and *routing redundancy must be structural* (disjoint paths), not merely statistical ($\alpha$ parallel queries that may share fate).

## 5 Empirical Evaluation and Proofs

### 5.1 The $O(\log n)$ bound

The bound follows from the trie interpretation. Each lookup hop moves to a node sharing a strictly longer prefix with the target with high probability: a node's k-bucket at the relevant level holds up to $k$ independent samples of the identifier subspace one bit closer, and with uniform random IDs the probability that *all* $k$ miss the correct half-space is $2^{-k}$ — negligible for $k = 20$ [1]. A refined probabilistic analysis proves routing time concentrates around $c \log n$ for an explicitly characterized constant $c$, both in the idealized trie model and under churn [6]. The bound is on *hops*; wall-clock latency is further cut by $\alpha$-parallelism, which converts sequential timeout risk into concurrent redundancy.

### 5.2 Deployments at scale

| Deployment | ID space | $k$ | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Kademlia paper [1] | 160-bit (SHA-1) | 20 | $\alpha = 3$; hourly refresh; 24 h republish |
| BitTorrent Mainline DHT [3] | 160-bit infohash space | 8 | BEP-5; `get_peers`/`announce_peer`; millions of nodes |
| Kad (eMule) [5] | 128-bit | 10–20 | search tolerance $\delta$; persistent IDs |
| IPFS / libp2p [4] | 256-bit (SHA-256) | 20 | buckets merged/split on demand; $RT = \text{bits} \times k$ |

The BitTorrent DHT, routinely numbering in the *millions* of concurrent nodes, adapts the RPC set via BEP-5 to `ping`, `find_node`, `get_peers`, and `announce_peer` — peer lists for infohashes instead of generic values [3]. IPFS's libp2p implementation modernizes the cryptography (SHA-256, 256-bit IDs) and merges/splits routing-trie branches on demand instead of fixing 160 buckets, while preserving the XOR metric, LRU eviction, and $\alpha$-parallel lookup [4]. Kad's documented attack history supplies the empirical security baseline against which S/Kademlia is measured [5].

### 5.3 Churn resilience

The paper's claims rest on *weak* uptime assumptions — essentially the heavy-tailed node lifetimes measured in contemporary file-sharing networks — and prove consistency (lookups return the true $k$ closest nodes) with high probability under them [1]. The intuition is redundancy at every layer: $k$ replicas per key, $k$ contacts per bucket, $\alpha$ parallel queries, hourly refresh, 24-hour republication. No single layer is reliable; their composition is.

## 6 Limitations

Kademlia's elegance does not make it flawless. **First**, 160-bit SHA-1 identifiers inherit SHA-1's collision weaknesses; libp2p migrated to SHA-256 — the identifier hash is load-bearing cryptography [4]. **Second**, next-hop selection is *latency-agnostic*: the $\alpha$ nodes are chosen by XOR closeness, not round-trip time, so lookups may traverse slow links when faster alternatives sit in the same bucket (the paper notes RTT estimates could augment bucket entries, but leaves this unimplemented) [1]. **Third**, periodic republication imposes steady-state write amplification, borne even by unpopular keys. **Fourth**, targeted attacks remain feasible for resourced adversaries: S/Kademlia raises Sybil and eclipse costs but does not eliminate them, and the IPFS eclipse study shows the connection *swarm* around the DHT can be softer than the DHT itself [4]. **Fifth**, Kademlia supplies no NAT traversal, no incentive compatibility, and no privacy — lookups leak the initiator's interests to every queried node. These bounds delineate its scope: Kademlia is a *routing substrate*, and privacy, incentives, and NAT traversal belong to layers above it.

---

## 7 Conclusion

Two decades after IPTPS 2002, Kademlia remains the dominant DHT design because its core abstraction proved unusually fertile. The XOR metric unified distance, topology, maintenance, and security policy: symmetry made every message useful; unidirectionality made caching effective and lookups convergent; the binary-trie structure made routing provably logarithmic; the k-bucket's least-recently-seen discipline turned churn itself into a defense. Its limitations — latency-agnostic selection, SHA-1-era cryptography, resourced Sybil and eclipse attacks — have been addressed incrementally by descendants: SHA-256 identifiers and adaptive buckets in libp2p, disjoint-path lookups and crypto puzzles in S/Kademlia and Storj, $k = 8$ tuning and peer-list RPCs in BitTorrent's planetary-scale DHT. The enduring lesson is architectural: *choose the metric first, and let the protocol follow*. When the distance function aligns with the information each message naturally carries, the network maintains itself.
