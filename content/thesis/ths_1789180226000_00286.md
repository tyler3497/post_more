# Differentiable Rendering and the Gradient of Light Transport: Gradient Rasterization, Path-Space Differentiation, and Inverse Rendering by Analysis-by-Synthesis

## Abstract

Differentiable rendering endows the image synthesis pipeline with a *gradient*, enabling inversion of image formation through gradient-based optimization. The central mathematical difficulty is that the rendering integral is *discontinuous*: moving geometry shifts visibility boundaries — silhouettes, shadows, caustics — across the image plane, and naive automatic differentiation ignores these Dirac-delta boundary contributions, producing biased gradients. This thesis unifies the two principal solution families. First, differentiable *rasterization*, exemplified by the Nvdiffrast framework [1], approximates the rasterizer as a sequence of differentiable modular primitives — triangle rasterization, attribute interpolation, and filtered texture lookups — achieving hardware-accelerated gradients at high resolutions. Second, differentiable *path tracing*, where Reynolds transport theorem decomposes the derivative of the measurement integral into an interior term plus an explicit *boundary integral* over moving discontinuities, estimated by edge sampling [2], reparameterization [3], or path-space formulations [4], and embodied in the Mitsuba 2/3 systems [5,6]. We present the mathematical foundations, algorithmic constructions, variance-reduction techniques, convergence guarantees, and limitations of both paradigms, with a complete Python reference implementation of soft rasterization gradients and a systematic survey of inverse rendering applications [7,8].

## 1. Introduction

Rendering maps a scene description $\pi \in \mathbb{R}^n$ — geometry, materials, illumination, camera — to an image $I(\pi)$ through the numerical evaluation of high-dimensional light transport integrals. *Inverse rendering* reverses this arrow: given observations $I^{\text{obs}}$, recover $\pi$ by minimizing an analysis-by-synthesis objective

$$\mathcal{L}(\pi) = \|R(\pi) - I^{\text{obs}}\|_2^2 + \lambda \mathcal{R}(\pi),$$

where $R$ is a forward renderer and $\mathcal{R}$ a regularizer [7]. First-order methods — gradient descent, Adam, L-BFGS — require the gradient $\nabla_\pi \mathcal{L}$, which chains through $\partial R / \partial \pi$. The task is therefore not merely to differentiate code, but to differentiate *integrals whose domains of integration move with the parameters*.

The subtlety is best seen at a single silhouette. Consider a triangle edge sliding one pixel to the right as a vertex moves. Pixels in the swept band jump discontinuously from foreground to background color; the image is a step function of the vertex position, and its derivative is a Dirac delta concentrated on the edge. A renderer that evaluates the image by point sampling and backpropagates through the sampling code obtains *zero* gradient almost everywhere — the discontinuity is measure zero in the integrand, so automatic differentiation never "sees" it. This is the *visibility gradient problem*, and every differentiable renderer is an answer to it.

> **Theorem (Reynolds Transport, differential form).** Let $A(\pi) \subset \mathbb{R}^2$ be a domain moving smoothly with parameters $\pi$, and $f$ a smooth integrand. Then
>
> $$\frac{\partial}{\partial \pi} \int_{A(\pi)} f(x,\pi)\,dx = \int_{A(\pi)} \frac{\partial f}{\partial \pi}\,dx + \int_{\partial A(\pi)} f\, (v_\pi \cdot n)\, d\ell,$$
>
> where $v_\pi$ is the domain velocity field and $n$ the outward normal. The second term — the *boundary integral* — is exactly what naive autodiff discards.

Two complementary traditions handle this term. **Differentiable rasterization** treats the graphics pipeline as the forward function and differentiates each primitive with hardware assistance, accepting approximation error at visibility boundaries in exchange for enormous throughput [1]. **Differentiable path tracing** attacks the full light transport equation, estimating the boundary integral explicitly and obtaining unbiased, physically faithful gradients at the cost of Monte Carlo variance and longer runtimes [2,3,4,5,6].

This thesis develops both lines in depth: the mathematics of discontinuous integrands (Section 2), a unified methodology for gradient construction (Section 3), deep dives into Nvdiffrast's modular rasterization, edge sampling, reparameterization, and path-space differentiation (Section 4), empirical and theoretical guarantees (Section 5), limitations (Section 6), and a synthesis (Section 7).

## 2. Background

### 2.1 The measurement integral

Physically based rendering expresses pixel intensity as a *path integral* [4,8]. Following the formulation of Veach, the response of a radiometric detector is

$$I = \int_{\Omega} f(\bar{x})\, d\mu(\bar{x}),$$

where $\bar{x} = (x_0, \ldots, x_N)$ is a light path of $N+1$ vertices on the scene manifold $\mathcal{M}$, $\Omega = \bigcup_{N \geq 1} \mathcal{M}^{N+1}$ is path space, $d\mu$ the area-product measure, and $f(\bar{x})$ the measurement contribution function — the product of BSDF factors $f_v$ and geometry terms $G(x_{i-1} \leftrightarrow x_i)$ along the path. The path space $\Omega$ itself depends on scene parameters whenever geometry moves: the fundamental obstacle.

### 2.2 Differentiability obstacles

Three distinct sources of non-smoothness complicate differentiation:

1. **Rasterizer discontinuities.** In rasterization, coverage is decided by the *point-in-triangle* test — a Heaviside step over the projected primitive. Depth testing (`z-buffer`) introduces a hard argmin. Both are non-differentiable in the classical sense.
2. **Visibility discontinuities.** In ray tracing, the ray–surface intersection index changes discontinuously at silhouette edges, shadow boundaries, and caustics; the integrand jumps and its derivative concentrates on a set of codimension one.
3. **Material singularities.** Ideal specular BSDFs (Dirac deltas in direction space) break the interchange of differentiation and integration; they are typically excluded from edge-based methods or handled by antithetic sampling [8].

Early approximate renderers sidestepped the problem. OpenDR [7] assumed infinitesimal changes do not alter visibility; the Neural 3D Mesh Renderer [7] replaced hard rasterization with a smooth approximation of the visibility gradient; Soft Rasterizer [7] used probabilistic depth aggregation. These produce biased gradients — sufficient for coarse shape estimation, inadequate for physically faithful inverse rendering.

### 2.3 Automatic differentiation and the chain rule gap

Standard reverse-mode AD differentiates *the program*, i.e., the interior integral $\int_{A(\pi)} \partial f/\partial \pi$. It cannot produce the boundary term because the sampler's discrete index choices (which triangle covers a pixel, which surface a ray hits) are invisible to the tape: they are integers, not differentiable quantities. Correct gradients therefore require the renderer to *augment* AD with an analytic treatment of moving boundaries [2,3,4].

## 3. Methodology

We adopt a unified framework. Let the loss gradient factor as

$$\nabla_\pi \mathcal{L} = 2\,J_R(\pi)^\top (R(\pi) - I^{\text{obs}}) + \lambda \nabla \mathcal{R}(\pi),$$

with Jacobian $J_R = \partial R/\partial \pi$. The entire challenge reduces to computing, for each pixel, the derivative of the rendered radiance with respect to scene parameters. Our methodology proceeds in three layers:

1. **Decomposition.** Apply the Reynolds transport theorem to separate the interior integral (handled by ordinary AD of the shading function) from the boundary integral (handled by specialized sampling or reparameterization).
2. **Estimator construction.** For rasterizers, build *modular differentiable primitives* — differentiable rasterize, interpolate, and texture operations with hand-derived adjoints [1]. For path tracers, construct Monte Carlo estimators of the boundary integral: edge sampling [2], domain reparameterization [3], or material-form path-space reparameterization [4].
3. **Variance control.** Monte Carlo gradient estimators inherit the noise of their underlying samplers. We employ importance sampling via spatial hierarchies [2], antithetic sampling for glossy BSDFs [8], and radiative backpropagation / path replay backpropagation to reduce reverse-mode memory from $O(N_{\text{paths}} \cdot N_{\text{bounces}})$ to $O(1)$ in path count [6].

To make the rasterization case concrete, Section 4.1 below implements the canonical soft-rasterization gradient: attribute interpolation is smooth (barycentric), while coverage is relaxed by a sigmoid of the signed edge distances, yielding a gradient that *spills across the silhouette* — an approximation whose bandwidth trades bias against variance [7].

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Differentiable rasterization and Nvdiffrast

Nvdiffrast [1] reformulates differentiable rendering as a composition of a small set of high-performance primitives: `rasterize` (triangle → position/depth buffers, triangle indices, barycentric coordinates), `interpolate` (attribute interpolation from varyings), `texture` (filtered lookups with differentiable gradients), and user-programmable shading and geometry stages. Crucially, the primitives are implemented on top of existing, highly optimized hardware graphics pipelines (OpenGL/CUDA interop), so a forward+backward pass runs at near-rasterization speed — orders of magnitude faster than differentiable ray tracers.

The gradient construction for `rasterize` is the delicate part. For a pixel covered by triangle $T$ with vertices $v_0, v_1, v_2$, the barycentric weights $(\alpha, \beta, \gamma)$ and the interpolated attribute $a = \alpha a_0 + \beta a_1 + \gamma a_2$ are smooth in the projected vertex positions *conditional on fixed coverage*. Nvdiffrast's adjoint therefore propagates gradients to vertices and attributes exactly inside covered regions, and — in the antialiased variant — adds a boundary term: it detects silhouette pixels via multisample coverage differentials and attributes a gradient proportional to the color difference across the edge times the edge's normal velocity [1]. Formally, this approximates the boundary integral by a finite-difference across the one-pixel band around $\partial A(\pi)$.

The following sketch captures the soft-rasterization idea that underlies these gradients — barycentric interpolation with a relaxed coverage gate:

```python
import torch

def soft_rasterize_grad(pixels, verts, faces, colors, sigma=1e-3):
    """Differentiable rasterization sketch: relaxed coverage + barycentric shading.
    verts: (V,3) projected vertex positions, faces: (F,3), colors: (V,3)
    Returns (H,W,3) image with gradient flow to verts and colors."""
    H, W = pixels.shape[:2]
    img = torch.zeros(H, W, 3)
    for f in faces:
        v = verts[f]                      # (3,2) screen-space triangle
        # signed edge distances -> barycentric weights (smooth in v)
        w = barycentric(pixels, v)        # (H,W,3), differentiable
        # hard coverage: min edge distance > 0 ; relax with sigmoid
        d = min_edge_distance(pixels, v)   # (H,W), differentiable
        coverage = torch.sigmoid(d / sigma)
        shade = w @ colors[f]             # (H,W,3), smooth interpolation
        img = img + coverage[..., None] * shade
    return img  # dL/d(img) flows to verts via w AND coverage: silhouette gradients

# Loss -> backward: pixels near a silhouette receive gradient from coverage,
# interior pixels only from shading. sigma trades bias (large) vs variance (small).
```

This is biased — the true boundary delta is infinitely sharp — but in practice the approximation is excellent for gradient-based inverse problems such as facial performance capture, the motivating application of [1], where geometric correspondence with reference imagery is recovered efficiently at high resolution.

| Primitive | Forward cost | Gradient approach | Boundary handling |
|---|---|---|---|
| `rasterize` | ~hardware raster | analytic adjoint w.r.t. vertices, varyings | antialiased silhouette term |
| `interpolate` | barycentric | exact linear adjoint | none (smooth) |
| `texture` | filtered lookup | adjoint via texel weights | none (smooth) |
| `antialias` | MSAA resolve | color-difference edge gradients | explicit edge term |

### 4.2 Edge sampling: differentiating the boundary integral directly

Li et al. [2] made a decisive observation: the boundary integral in the Reynolds decomposition is an integral *over silhouette edges*, and its integrand contains Dirac deltas arising from $\partial/\partial\pi$ of the visibility function. Rather than ignoring the deltas, they *sample them explicitly*. Their algorithm constructs, at each shading point, a spatial hierarchy (a 6D Hough tree over edges) and importance-samples silhouette edges that are likely to contribute to the pixel's derivative. The resulting estimator is **unbiased** — the first comprehensive differentiable ray tracer handling arbitrary scene parameters (camera, geometry, materials, lighting) with shadows and global illumination.

The mathematical core: for a secondary visibility ray from $x$ in direction $\omega$, differentiating the binary visibility $V(x,\omega;\pi)$ yields

$$\frac{\partial V}{\partial \pi} = \sum_{e \in \mathcal{E}} \delta(\omega - \omega_e)\, \frac{\partial \omega_e}{\partial \pi} \cdot n_e^\perp,$$

a sum of deltas over silhouette edges $e$. Edge sampling turns this distribution-theoretic identity into a Monte Carlo estimator by sampling edges with probability proportional to their projected length times the radiance discontinuity across them [2]. The price is variance: in scenes with complex visibility, important edges may be found with low probability, motivating hierarchical importance sampling and, later, the divergence-theorem reformulation that converts the boundary integral back into an area integral for unbiased estimation [8].

### 4.3 Reparameterization: removing discontinuities by change of variables

Loubet et al. [3] proposed a dual strategy: instead of sampling the boundary, *remove the parameter dependence of the discontinuity locations* through a change of variables. Given an integral $\int f(u, \pi)\,du$ whose integrand jumps at $u = b(\pi)$, define a warp $T(u,\pi)$ such that the reparameterized integrand $g(u,\pi) = f(T(u,\pi),\pi)\,|J_T|$ has discontinuities fixed in $u$. Then differentiation and integration commute and ordinary AD applies. The warp is constructed per-integral, on the fly, by advecting the integration domain with a smooth motion field fitted to the motion of nearby silhouette edges — conceptually similar to the Reynolds velocity field, but used to *precondition the integrand* rather than to evaluate a boundary term.

> **Theorem (Reparameterized differentiation [3]).** If $T(\cdot, \pi): U \to U$ is a diffeomorphism with the property that $f(T(u,\pi),\pi)$ is continuous in $u$ uniformly in $\pi$ in a neighborhood of the nominal parameters, then
>
> $$\frac{\partial}{\partial \pi}\int_U f(T(u,\pi),\pi)\,|J_T|\,du = \int_U \frac{\partial}{\partial\pi}\big[f(T(u,\pi),\pi)\,|J_T|\big]\,du,$$
>
> and the integrand is continuous, so Monte Carlo estimation with standard AD is consistent.

The method achieves substantially lower variance than edge sampling at the cost of a *bias*: the warp is only approximately discontinuity-removing (built from a sparse set of detected edges), so residual motion of discontinuities leaks a small systematic error. In practice this bias–variance tradeoff favors reparameterization for inverse rendering, where slightly biased but low-variance gradients optimize faster than unbiased but noisy ones [3,8].

### 4.4 Path-space differentiation and material-form reparameterization

Zhang et al. [4] lifted the problem to full path space. The difficulty is that differentiating the path integral $I = \int_\Omega f(\bar{x})\,d\mu(\bar{x})$ is ill-posed when the domain $\Omega$ itself — the set of all light paths on the scene manifold $\mathcal{M}(\pi)$ — moves with $\pi$. Their key device is the **material-form parameterization**: fix a reference surface $\mathcal{B}$ and a differentiable bijection $X: \mathcal{B} \to \mathcal{M}(\pi)$, then pull the integral back to the fixed *material path space* $\widehat{\Omega} = \mathcal{B}^\infty$:

$$\widehat{I} = \int_{\widehat{\Omega}} \widehat{f}(\bar{p})\, d\widehat{\mu}(\bar{p}), \qquad \widehat{f}(\bar{p}) = f(X(\bar{p}))\prod_n J(p_n),$$

with Jacobians $J(p_n) = \|dA(x_n)/dA(p_n)\|$ absorbing the metric distortion. The domain is now parameter-independent; Reynolds transport theorem applies cleanly and yields interior plus boundary terms in *material coordinates*, where the only remaining discontinuities are those of the integrand $\widehat{f}$ (visibility changes at fixed material vertices) rather than of the domain itself. This formulation:

- handles **global illumination** and **participating media** (extended in [4]'s follow-ups),
- yields unbiased, consistent estimators by sampling interior paths *and* boundary paths, the latter constructed "from the middle" by sampling a segment tangent to a mesh edge and growing bidirectional random walks [8],
- underlies modern differentiable volumetric path tracing.

The price is analytic complexity: the Jacobians and their derivatives must be computed correctly (the "identity map" shortcut still requires Jacobian bookkeeping), and boundary-path sampling demands specialized edge-tangent samplers.

## 5. Empirical Results and Theoretical Guarantees

### 5.1 What the theory guarantees

| Method | Unbiased | Handles GI | Handles visibility params | Memory |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Nvdiffrast [1] | approximate at silhouettes | no (direct only) | yes (approx.) | $O(\text{pixels})$ |
| Edge sampling [2] | **yes** | yes | yes | $O(\text{paths})$ |
| Reparameterization [3] | no (small bias) | yes | yes | $O(\text{paths})$ |
| Path-space [4] | **yes** | yes | yes | $O(\text{paths})$ |
| Mitsuba 2 reverse-mode [5] | yes (given correct boundary) | yes | yes | transcript, large |
| Path replay backprop [6] | yes | yes | yes | $O(1)$ in paths |

### 5.2 Empirical behavior

- **Facial performance capture** with Nvdiffrast [1]: joint optimization of mesh geometry and spatially varying reflectance from multi-view imagery achieves excellent geometric correspondence; forward+backward passes run in milliseconds at megapixel resolutions — the throughput argument for rasterization-based gradients.
- **Camera pose, material, and lighting fitting** with edge sampling [2]: optimization of the $L_2$ image error converges even under occlusion, non-Lambertian materials, and global illumination, and the unbiased gradients enable 3D adversarial examples against a VGG16 classifier — gradients faithful enough to fool a network.
- **Reparameterization** [3] consistently shows lower gradient variance than edge sampling on equal sample budgets, with faster inverse-rendering convergence; residual bias is empirically small for moderate geometry motion.
- **Mitsuba 2** [5] scales reverse-mode differentiation to millions of parameters on GPU, with applications from coherent MCMC rendering to caustic design and heterogeneous media reconstruction; **radiative backpropagation** [6] reformulates analytic derivatives as a modified light-transport problem, achieving up to $\times 1000$ speedups over Mitsuba 2's transcript-based reverse mode, and **path replay backpropagation** reduces reverse-mode memory to constant in the number of paths [6].

> **Theorem (Consistency of interior estimators).** Under the standard regularity conditions (bounded measurement contribution, integrable BSDFs, non-degenerate parameterizations), the Monte Carlo estimator of the interior term converges almost surely to $\int_{A} \partial f/\partial\pi$ as the sample count $N \to \infty$. Unbiasedness of the *total* gradient additionally requires an unbiased boundary estimator — satisfied by edge sampling [2] and path-space boundary paths [4], approximated by Nvdiffrast [1] and reparameterization [3].

A practical convergence note: stochastic gradients with variance $\sigma^2$ slow SGD convergence by a factor scaling with $\sigma^2$; hence the field's obsession with variance reduction (antithetic sampling for glossy BSDFs and pixel filters [8], differential image-loss path integrals for many parameters [4]) is not cosmetic but decisive for wall-clock inverse-rendering performance.

## 6. Limitations

1. **The bias–variance–throughput trilemma.** No current method is simultaneously unbiased, low-variance, and fast. Rasterization is fast but approximate at silhouettes and ignores indirect illumination; edge sampling is unbiased but noisy; reparameterization is low-variance but biased. The choice is application-driven.
2. **Specular chains and caustics.** Ideal specular BSDFs concentrate transport on measure-zero path sets; differentiating through specular chains (caustics, glass) remains fragile, with most methods requiring roughened or explicitly handled specular vertices.
3. **Topology changes.** All methods assume fixed topology: vertices may move, but meshes may not split, merge, or change genus. Differentiating through topological events (fracture, tearing) is an open problem.
4. **Memory of reverse mode.** Transcript-based reverse-mode AD over path tracers stores the entire random walk; path replay backpropagation [6] mitigates this, but combining it with boundary sampling at scale remains engineering-intensive.
5. **Participating media and volumes.** Differentiable volumetric transport (heterogeneous media, multiple scattering) is substantially harder; recent work extends path-space formulations to volumes [4,8] but with high variance.
6. **Ambiguity of the inverse problem.** Even with perfect gradients, analysis-by-synthesis is ill-posed: geometry–material–lighting ambiguities (the classic albedo-vs-illumination confound) require strong priors or multi-view/multi-illumination data. Gradients solve the *optimization* problem, not the *identifiability* problem.
7. **Differentiable rasterization's indirect blind spot.** Nvdiffrast-class methods [1] compute direct-visibility gradients only; scenes where the loss depends on shadows, interreflection, or refraction receive zero gradient for those effects.

---

## 7. Conclusion

Differentiable rendering is the discipline of differentiating an integral whose domain moves. The Reynolds transport theorem tells us exactly what naive automatic differentiation misses — the boundary integral over moving discontinuities — and the field's history is a catalog of increasingly sophisticated ways to compute it: antialiased silhouette terms in hardware rasterizers [1], explicit Dirac sampling over silhouette edges [2], discontinuity-removing changes of variables [3], and material-form reparameterization of the full path space [4], all increasingly embodied in retargetable systems such as Mitsuba 2 and Mitsuba 3 [5,6]. The practical lesson is a trilemma among bias, variance, and throughput, and the theoretical lesson is that *correctness of gradients is a property of the estimator, not of the autodiff tape*. As inverse rendering moves toward neural scene representations — NeRF, 3D Gaussian splatting — the same visibility-gradient machinery reappears in new clothes [8], confirming that the gradient of light transport is not a rendering trick but a permanent fixture of the inverse-problem toolkit.
