# Good Quantum LDPC Codes: Expander-Graph Constructions, Product-Expansion, and the Pursuit of Constant-Overhead Fault Tolerance

## Abstract

Classical low-density parity-check (LDPC) codes are *asymptotically good*: they simultaneously achieve constant rate and linear distance with constant-weight checks, the triumph of expander-graph constructions in the Sipser–Spielman tradition. For nearly three decades, the quantum analogue remained conjectural — every known family of quantum LDPC codes with bounded stabilizer weight stalled at distance $\Theta(\sqrt{n})$, a square-root barrier that seemed intrinsic to sparse stabilizer measurement. This thesis reconstructs the 2013–2024 revolution that broke the barrier: Bravyi and Hastings's homological products, Panteleev and Kalachev's lifted products over expanding graphs, and Leverrier and Zémor's quantum Tanner codes on the left-right Cayley complex — families of $[[n,\Theta(n),\Theta(n)]]$ quantum codes with *constant* stabilizer weight, provably decodable in linear or logarithmic parallel time, and supporting single-shot error correction under measurement noise. We develop the homological algebra of product constructions, the notion of product-expansion that powers the distance proofs, the efficient decoders that make these codes operationally credible, and the remaining obstacles — nonlocal connectivity, threshold constants, and non-Clifford logic — on the road to constant-overhead fault tolerance.

## 1 Introduction

The central economic fact of fault-tolerant quantum computation is *overhead*. The surface code, the workhorse of the field, protects a single logical qubit with $\Theta(d^2)$ physical qubits at distance $d$; a useful computation demanding $10^3$–$10^4$ logical qubits at distance $d \approx 25$–$50$ therefore requires millions of physical qubits, most of them spent on redundancy rather than computation. This cost is not merely a technological constant to be engineered away: two-dimensional local codes satisfy the Bravyi–Poulin–Terhal bound $kd^2 = O(n)$, so *any* planar architecture must pay superlinear qubit costs as the logical footprint grows [7].

Quantum LDPC codes promise to escape this trap. A code is LDPC if every stabilizer generator has bounded weight and every qubit participates in a bounded number of generators; the syndrome-extraction circuit is then shallow, local in the *Tanner graph* sense, and reusable. Classical coding theory settled the analogous question in the affirmative long ago: expander graphs yield asymptotically good classical LDPC families with constant rate and linear distance. The **qLDPC conjecture** — that *quantum* codes can do the same, with $k = \Theta(n)$, $d = \Theta(n)$, and $O(1)$-weight stabilizers — stood open from the 1990s until 2022, when Panteleev and Kalachev proved it via lifted products over non-abelian groups [3], in parallel with the quantum Tanner codes of Leverrier and Zémor [4].

This thesis is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews the stabilizer/CSS formalism, Tanner graphs, and the classical expander-code heritage, and explains why the hypergraph-product construction of Tillich and Zémor — constant rate, distance $\Theta(\sqrt{n})$ — defined the frontier for over a decade. Section 3 develops the methodology: the chain-complex language of homological products, the lifted-product twist that generalizes them, and the left-right Cayley complex behind quantum Tanner codes. Section 4 dives deep into the four pillars of the breakthrough: homological products, lifted products, asymptotically good Tanner codes, and efficient plus single-shot decoding. Section 5 states the resulting theorems and asymptotic parameter table. Section 7 concludes.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Stabilizer codes, CSS codes, and Tanner graphs

A stabilizer code on $n$ qubits is the common $+1$ eigenspace of an abelian subgroup $\mathcal{S}$ of the $n$-qubit Pauli group, with $-\mathbf{1} \notin \mathcal{S}$. A **CSS code** (Calderbank–Shor–Steane) splits the generators into $X$-type and $Z$-type checks, described by binary parity-check matrices $H_X, H_Z$ satisfying the orthogonality condition

$$H_X H_Z^{\!\top} = 0,$$

which is the *commutativity* requirement in linear-algebraic form. The code parameters are written $[[n,k,d]]$: $n$ physical qubits, $k = n - \mathrm{rank}(H_X) - \mathrm{rank}(H_Z)$ logical qubits, and distance $d$, the minimum weight of a Pauli operator that commutes with all stabilizers but lies outside $\mathcal{S}$.

The **Tanner graph** of a code is the bipartite incidence graph between $n$ *bit nodes* (qubits) and $m$ *check nodes* (stabilizers). The code is *LDPC* when this graph has bounded degree on both sides: every check touches $O(1)$ qubits, and every qubit touches $O(1)$ checks. Bounded degree is what makes syndrome extraction cheap and decoding fast — and it is exactly what the constructions of this thesis preserve while pushing $k$ and $d$ to their information-theoretic scaling limits.

### 2.2 The classical blueprint: expander codes

The classical story is the template everything below imitates. A **Tanner code** $T(G, C_0)$ is built from a $\Delta$-regular expander graph $G = (V,E)$ and a short *local* code $C_0 \subseteq \mathbb{F}_2^\Delta$: bits sit on edges, and at each vertex the incident $\Delta$ bits must form a codeword of $C_0$. Sipser and Spielman showed that when $G$ is a sufficiently strong spectral expander and $C_0$ has good distance, the resulting code is asymptotically good — constant rate, linear distance — and, crucially, *decodable in linear time* by a simple greedy flipping algorithm: expansion guarantees that any small error set has a vertex where flipping a bit strictly reduces the number of unsatisfied checks [7].

Two ingredients therefore recur throughout this thesis:

1. **Local codes** with good parameters, viewed as generalized parity checks.
2. **Global expansion** of the underlying graph or complex, which converts local constraints into global distance and enables greedy decoding.

The quantum difficulty is that the $X$-checks and $Z$-checks must be mutually orthogonal ($H_X H_Z^{\!\top} = 0$), a global algebraic constraint with no classical analogue. Satisfying it while preserving sparsity *and* distance is what made the qLDPC conjecture hard.

### 2.3 The square-root frontier: hypergraph products

The first systematic quantum LDPC family with constant rate came from the **hypergraph product** of Tillich and Zémor: given two classical codes with parity-check matrices $H_1, H_2$, the CSS code defined by

$$H_X = \begin{pmatrix} H_1 \otimes \mathbf{1} & \mathbf{1} \otimes H_2^{\!\top} \end{pmatrix}, \qquad H_Z = \begin{pmatrix} \mathbf{1} \otimes H_2 & H_1^{\!\top} \otimes \mathbf{1} \end{pmatrix}$$

automatically satisfies $H_X H_Z^{\!\top} = 0$ and yields parameters $[[n, \Theta(n), \Theta(\sqrt{n})]]$ with constant-weight checks [7]. For over a decade this was the state of the art: constant rate and constant-weight stabilizers, but distance scaling only as $\sqrt{n}$ — meaning a distance-$d$ code still needs $\Theta(d^2)$ qubits, *no better than the surface code* in scaling. Every attempt to do better — fiber-bundle codes, balanced products, high-dimensional expanders — improved the exponent modestly but never reached linear distance with constant-weight checks [1][7].

> **Lemma (informal).** In all product-type quantum LDPC constructions known before 2020, the distance was bounded by $\tilde{O}(\sqrt{n})$ whenever stabilizer weights were bounded by a constant.

The lemma was never a theorem — which is precisely why the breakthroughs below were possible.

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 The chain-complex viewpoint

Every CSS code is a three-term chain complex over $\mathbb{F}_2$:

$$C_2 \xrightarrow{\;\partial_2\;} C_1 \xrightarrow{\;\partial_1\;} C_0, \qquad \partial_1 \partial_2 = 0.$$

Here $C_1$ is the space of qubits, $\partial_2^{\!\top}$ encodes the $X$-checks, $\partial_1$ encodes the $Z$-checks, and the **homology** $H_1 = \ker \partial_1 / \mathrm{im}\,\partial_2$ is the space of logical $X$ operators (with cohomology giving logical $Z$). The condition $\partial_1\partial_2 = 0$ *is* the CSS commutativity condition. Code parameters become homological invariants:

- $k = \dim H_1$ (first Betti number),
- $d_X = $ minimum weight of a cycle not a boundary (systole),
- $d_Z = $ minimum weight of a cocycle not a coboundary (cosystole).

This dictionary is the methodological key: constructing good quantum codes becomes constructing chain complexes with large homology and large (co)systoles, and *products of chain complexes* give a functorial way to build big complexes from small ones.

### 3.2 Homological and lifted products

The **homological product** (tensor product) of two complexes $A_\bullet, B_\bullet$ is the total complex of the double complex $A_i \otimes B_j$, with differential $\partial = \partial^A \otimes \mathbf{1} + \mathbf{1} \otimes \partial^B$. The Künneth formula computes its homology from the factors, and the product structure yields a CSS code whose checks are sparse whenever the factors' checks are sparse [1][8].

The **lifted product**, introduced by Panteleev and Kalachev [2], generalizes this by replacing the tensor product over $\mathbb{F}_2$ with a product twisted by a group action: one takes a *covering* of an expander graph (the "lift") and lets the local codes vary over the base according to automorphisms — a discrete analogue of a fiber bundle, visually like a Möbius strip in which the fiber code twists as one moves around the base. The twist is what defeats the $\sqrt{n}$ barrier: it correlates the $X$- and $Z$-sectors just enough to boost the distance while keeping every check low-weight [2].

### 3.3 The left-right Cayley complex and product-expansion

Leverrier and Zémor's quantum Tanner codes [4] give the most transparent realization. Start with a group $G$ and two symmetric generating sets $A, B$. The **left-right Cayley complex** has vertices $V = G \times \{0,1\}^2$ (four copies of $G$) and *squares* $Q = \{(g, a, b) : g \in G, a \in A, b \in B\}$; each square touches two $A$-edges and two $B$-edges. Qubits sit on squares. Fix two classical codes $C_A \subseteq \mathbb{F}_2^A$, $C_B \subseteq \mathbb{F}_2^B$. The $X$-checks enforce, at each vertex, that the incident $A$-neighborhood lies in $C_A$ and the $B$-neighborhood in $C_B$ (a *dual tensor code*); the $Z$-checks do the analogous job on the complementary graph. Orthogonality holds by construction, and the code is the common refinement of two classical Tanner codes on graphs sharing the same edge set [4].

The distance proof rests on **product-expansion** (also called *robust testability*): the dual tensor code $C_A^\perp \otimes \mathbb{F}_2^B + \mathbb{F}_2^A \otimes C_B^\perp$ must be such that any low-weight word far from the code violates a constant fraction of local constraints. Random local codes satisfy this with high probability, and it is exactly the property that lets expansion convert local testability into *linear* global distance [4][9].

### 3.4 What "efficient decoding" must mean

A code with linear distance is useless for fault tolerance if decoding takes superlinear time: errors accumulate while the classical decoder deliberates. The three gold standards are **linear-time sequential decoding** ($O(n)$ work, à la Sipser–Spielman flipping), **logarithmic-time parallel decoding** ($O(\log n)$ depth, keeping pace with the quantum clock), and **single-shot decoding** — one round of *noisy* syndrome measurement suffices, with measurement errors corrected from the checks' own redundancy [6].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Homological products and the first good quantum codes

Bravyi and Hastings [1] built the first family of *good* quantum codes with low-weight stabilizers by taking the homological product of a random good CSS code with a classical code. The Künneth formula gives constant rate and linear distance; the stabilizer weight is bounded by $O(\sqrt{n})$ rather than $O(1)$, but this already refuted the belief that good quantum codes require linear-weight checks.

> **Theorem (Bravyi–Hastings [1], informal).** There exist quantum code families with constant encoding rate, linear distance, and stabilizer generators acting on at most $\sqrt{n}$ qubits.

*Proof sketch.* Take a random CSS code $Q$ of length $n_1$ with linear distance and a classical code $C$ of length $n_2$ with linear distance. The homological product $Q \boxtimes C$ has length $n = n_1 n_2$; by the Künneth formula, $\dim H_1(Q \boxtimes C) = \Theta(n)$, so the rate is constant. Any homologically nontrivial cycle has weight $\Omega(n)$: a cycle thin in one factor must be thick in the other, and the random choice of $Q$ rules out low-weight words conspiring across the product. Balancing $n_1, n_2$ gives stabilizer weight $O(\sqrt{n})$. ∎

The authors conjectured the method could push stabilizer weight to $n^a$ for any $a > 0$ — but constant weight required genuinely new ideas [1].

### 4.2 The lifted product: twists that beat $\sqrt{n}$

Panteleev and Kalachev's lifted product [2] starts from a Sipser–Spielman expander code on a regular graph and takes an $\ell$-lift (a covering graph of degree $\ell$), with the local code twisted by automorphisms along the lift. The twist destroys the product structure that capped earlier distances at $\sqrt{n}$: errors can no longer hide in "rectangular" configurations that are simultaneously low-weight in both factors.

> **Theorem (Panteleev–Kalachev [2], informal).** There exist quantum LDPC codes of dimension $\Theta(\log N)$ and distance $\Theta(N/\log N)$ with constant-weight stabilizers; more generally, for $0 \le \alpha < 1$, families with dimension $\Omega(N^\alpha \log N)$ and distance $\Omega(N^{1-\alpha/2}/\log N)$.

The $\alpha \to 1$ limit gives *almost* linear distance — the first LDPC family to break the square-root barrier with truly constant-weight checks. The codes are quasi-cyclic, a practical bonus: the lift of a base graph by cyclic shifts yields parity-check matrices composed of circulant blocks, compatible with structured hardware layouts [2].

### 4.3 Asymptotically good codes and quantum Tanner codes

The qLDPC conjecture fell in 2021–2022 from two directions at once. Panteleev and Kalachev [3] extended the lifted product to *non-abelian* group actions, obtaining fully asymptotically good families — $[[N, \Theta(N), \Theta(N)]]$ with constant-weight stabilizers — and, as a classical byproduct, asymptotically good *locally testable* codes with constant query complexity. Independently, Leverrier and Zémor [4] gave the quantum Tanner code construction on the left-right Cayley complex: arguably the cleanest proof, since it reduces the whole problem to (i) expansion of the two Cayley graphs and (ii) product-expansion (robust testability) of the dual tensor code of the local codes.

> **Theorem (Leverrier–Zémor [4]; Panteleev–Kalachev [3], informal).** There exist quantum LDPC code families with $k = \Theta(n)$, $d = \Theta(n)$, and stabilizer weight $O(1)$. In particular, the qLDPC conjecture is true.

*Why the proof works.* Consider an $X$-error $e$ of weight below a constant fraction of $n$. Its syndrome is supported on vertices incident to $e$. Product-expansion implies that the *local views* of $e$ at those vertices are far from the dual tensor code unless $e$ is locally close to a codeword of the local Tanner code; spectral expansion of the Cayley graphs then propagates this local closeness into a global decomposition $e = s + c$ with $s$ a stabilizer and $c$ small. Choosing the radius below the distance of the local codes forces $c = 0$ for errors under the decoding radius — hence linear distance [4].

Recent work continues to simplify the picture: homological products of codes with product-expansion recover asymptotically good parameters with small polynomial stabilizer weight [9], and subsystem variants of iterated homological products reach close-to-linear distance $[[N, N^{1-\epsilon}, N^{1-\epsilon}]]$ with *constant* stabilizer weight, circumventing the obstructions that had limited many product constructions to $\tilde{O}(\sqrt{N})$ [9].

### 4.4 Efficient decoding and single-shot correction

Distance without decoding is a museum piece. Two results make good qLDPC codes operational:

**Linear-time parallel decoding.** Leverrier and Zémor [5] gave a decoder for asymptotically good quantum codes that corrects any error of weight up to a constant fraction of the block length in *linear* sequential time, with a parallel implementation running in $O(\log n)$ depth. The algorithm is the quantum heir of Sipser–Spielman flipping: while unsatisfied checks remain, find a vertex whose local view admits a flip strictly decreasing a potential function; expansion guarantees such a vertex exists until the error is gone, and product-expansion guarantees the potential cannot get stuck in local minima [5].

**Single-shot decoding.** In real hardware, the syndrome itself is noisy. A code is *single-shot* if one round of noisy stabilizer measurement suffices for reliable correction — the checks' redundancy identifies measurement faults without $O(d)$ repetitions. Gu, Tang, Caha, Choe, He, and Kubica [6] proved that quantum Tanner codes are single-shot against *adversarial* noise, for both the sequential and the parallel decoders: the decoder receives $\tilde{\sigma} = H_Z e + D$ (data error $e$ plus measurement error $D$) and still returns a correction whose residual error is bounded, and running the parallel decoder for merely *constant* time per round suppresses errors over arbitrarily many rounds [6].

> **Theorem (Gu et al. [6], informal).** Quantum Tanner codes admit single-shot quantum error correction: one round of constant-weight parity checks, measured noisily, suffices to keep the logical error bounded; per-round decoding in constant parallel time stabilizes a quantum memory indefinitely.

*Proof idea.* The noisy syndrome decomposes into local syndromes at each vertex. Each vertex computes a minimum-weight local correction from its *noisy* local syndrome; disagreements between the two vertices sharing a square are resolved by a mismatch graph whose expansion — inherited from the complex — guarantees that measurement errors cannot create large connected clusters of disagreement without triggering many violated metachecks. The residual error after correction is proportional to the measurement noise, which the next round absorbs [6].

```python
# Illustrative: syndrome of a CSS code from sparse check matrices.
# (Toy sizes only — real qLDPC instances use expander-derived matrices.)
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
n, m = 24, 12  # qubits, checks per sector

def sparse_check(m, n, w, rng):
    H = np.zeros((m, n), dtype=np.uint8)
    for i in range(m):                       # constant row weight w
        H[i, rng.choice(n, w, replace=False)] = 1
    return H

Hx = sparse_check(m, n, 4, rng)
Hz = sparse_check(m, n, 4, rng)
# enforce CSS orthogonality on a common subspace (illustrative projection)
Hz = Hz @ (np.eye(n, dtype=np.uint8) - Hx.T @ np.linalg.pinv(Hx).astype(np.uint8) % 2) % 2

e = (rng.random(n) < 0.05).astype(np.uint8)   # low-weight X error
syndrome = (Hz @ e) % 2
print("error weight:", e.sum(), "| syndrome weight:", syndrome.sum(),
      "| checks:", m)
```

## 5 Empirical Results / Proofs

Because good qLDPC codes are asymptotic statements, the "empirical" record is a mixture of proven theorems and finite-size computational studies. The theoretical ledger is summarized below.

| Code family | Parameters $[[n,k,d]]$ | Check weight | Decoding | Source |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2D surface code | $[[n, 1, \Theta(\sqrt{n})]]$ | $O(1)$ | MWPM, $O(n^3)$-ish | topological baseline |
| Hypergraph product (Tillich–Zémor) | $[[n, \Theta(n), \Theta(\sqrt{n})]]$ | $O(1)$ | BP / small-set flip | [7] |
| Homological product (Bravyi–Hastings) | $[[n, \Theta(n), \Theta(n)]]$ | $O(\sqrt{n})$ | — | [1] |
| Lifted product, almost-linear (Panteleev–Kalachev) | $[[N, \Theta(\log N), \Theta(N/\log N)]]$ | $O(1)$ | expander-based | [2] |
| Lifted product, good (Panteleev–Kalachev) | $[[N, \Theta(N), \Theta(N)]]$ | $O(1)$ | — | [3] |
| Quantum Tanner (Leverrier–Zémor) | $[[N, \Theta(N), \Theta(N)]]$ | $O(1)$ | linear-time; $O(\log n)$ parallel | [4][5] |
| Quantum Tanner, noisy syndromes | single-shot, adversarial noise | $O(1)$ | constant parallel time/round | [6] |

Three consequences deserve emphasis:

1. **Overhead scaling.** At fixed distance $d$, a good qLDPC code needs $\Theta(d)$ physical qubits per logical qubit — versus $\Theta(d^2)$ for the surface code and hypergraph products. The asymptotic qubit saving is *quadratic*, the difference between a million-qubit and a ten-thousand-qubit machine at the same logical footprint.
2. **Time overhead.** Single-shot correction [6] removes the $O(d)$ measurement-repetition factor that fault-tolerance schemes traditionally pay per logical gate, and logarithmic-depth parallel decoding [5] keeps classical processing off the critical path.
3. **Finite-size progress.** Asymptotic theorems do not ship hardware. Complementary finite-length work — lifted and bicycle families optimized by computational search — is beginning to fill the gap: recent divisor-driven searches for quantum bicycle codes report weight-six check families with substantially improved $kd^2/n$, including the $[[144,12,12]]$ regime that cuts overhead by roughly an order of magnitude relative to surface codes of comparable performance [10].

## 6 Limitations

The theorems are real; the road to hardware is not yet paved. Six limitations structure the current research frontier:

1. **Nonlocal connectivity.** None of the good qLDPC families are geometrically local: their Tanner graphs are expanders, which cannot embed in low-dimensional lattices. Realizing them demands architectures with long-range gates — ion traps, photonic interconnects, or Rydberg arrays [5] — and the routing cost of arbitrary expander connectivity at scale is poorly understood.
2. **Constants, not just asymptotics.** The proofs establish *existence* of constants $\epsilon > 0$ with $k, d \ge \epsilon n$, but the explicit $\epsilon$ values are tiny, and the required block lengths for the asymptotics to "kick in" are enormous. Explicit small codes with competitive thresholds remain scarce; the finite-length optimization program [10] is in its infancy.
3. **Thresholds under circuit noise.** Single-shot theorems [6] assume adversarial but bounded noise; full *circuit-level* thresholds — including faulty syndrome-extraction circuits with hook errors — have been computed only for specific small instances, and it is not yet known whether good qLDPC families enjoy thresholds competitive with the surface code's $\sim 1\%$.
4. **Non-Clifford logic.** The Bravyi–König bound restricts transversal gates in topological codes to the Clifford group, and good qLDPC codes face the same universality problem: a quantum memory is not a quantum computer. Progress exists — Zhu's topological theory of qLDPC exhibits constant-depth logical CCZ gates and a "magic state fountain" injecting $\Theta(\sqrt{N})$ magic states in parallel on homological-product codes [8] — but a complete low-overhead universal gate set is still missing.
5. **Decoding latency constants.** Logarithmic parallel depth is asymptotically ideal, but the hidden constants and the processor count ($O(n)$ parallel units) pose a serious classical co-processing challenge at the million-qubit scale.
6. **Syndrome-extraction depth.** Constant-weight checks still require constant-depth extraction circuits whose depth constant multiplies every logical operation; measurement and reset times dominate in current hardware, eroding the theoretical time-overhead advantage.

> **Caveat.** Nothing here claims good qLDPC codes outperform the surface code *today*. The surface code has two decades of threshold simulations, decoding infrastructure, and hardware demonstrations; good qLDPC codes have theorems and small simulations. The honest statement is that the *asymptotic* scaling laws now favor qLDPC, and the engineering race has begun.

## 7 Conclusion

In under a decade, quantum LDPC theory traveled from the $\Theta(\sqrt{n})$ doldrums to asymptotically good families with constant-weight stabilizers, linear-time and logarithmic-parallel decoders, and single-shot correction of adversarial noise [1][2][3][4][5][6]. The conceptual engine was the fusion of three classical ideas — Tanner's generalized parity checks, Sipser–Spielman expansion, and homological algebra — with the quantum commutativity constraint handled functorially through chain complexes and their twisted products. Product-expansion emerged as the single technical property that converts local testability into global distance, and it now organizes the entire field [4][9].

What remains is an engineering and mathematical program, not a search for a missing theorem: explicit small codes with large constants, circuit-level thresholds, low-overhead universal gate sets [8], and architectures whose connectivity matches expander graphs. If that program succeeds, the payoff is the original promise of the qLDPC conjecture — fault-tolerant quantum computation with *constant* space overhead, collapsing the million-qubit surface-code machine into something buildable. The square-root barrier was a property of our constructions. The next barrier is ours to build through.
