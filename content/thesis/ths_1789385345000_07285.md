# Defect Tolerance, Ion Migration, and Compositional Engineering in Halide Perovskite Photovoltaics: From Shallow Traps to Terawatt-Scale Tandems

## Abstract

Halide perovskites have compressed into fifteen years a photovoltaic learning curve that required five decades for crystalline silicon, rising from 3.8% to laboratory tandem efficiencies above 35%. This thesis unifies the four strands that explain both the ascent and the remaining obstacles: the unusual electronic structure that renders intrinsic point defects electronically benign as shallow traps; the vacancy-mediated ion migration that makes every perovskite cell simultaneously a solid electrolyte and the root cause of current–voltage hysteresis; the compositional engineering of mixed cations and mixed halides that stabilizes the photoactive phase and tunes bandgaps for tandem integration; and the quantitative route from laboratory records to terawatt deployment. We derive defect formation energetics and Shockley–Read–Hall recombination statistics, review activation barriers for halide, A-site, and lead migration, and survey certified efficiency milestones including the 35.5% perovskite–silicon tandem. We close with a candid assessment of lead toxicity, operational stability, and the materials-throughput arithmetic that governs whether perovskites can carry a meaningful share of a multi-terawatt solar future.

## 1 Intro

In 2009, Kojima and colleagues reported that organolead halide perovskite nanocrystals could sensitize mesoporous titania in a photoelectrochemical cell, achieving 3.8% power conversion efficiency with spectral sensitivity extending to 800 nm [1]. Yet within a decade the certified single-junction efficiency had crossed 25%, and by mid-2026 a two-terminal perovskite–silicon tandem was certified at 35.5%, surpassing the single-junction Shockley–Queisser limit of 33.7% [6][7]. No photovoltaic technology in history has climbed so fast.

The central scientific puzzle of this ascent is why solution-processed polycrystalline films, deposited from commercial-grade precursors at temperatures below 150 °C and riddled with point defects at densities of 10^16 to 10^17 cm^-3, can rival the optoelectronic quality of single-crystal gallium arsenide [4]. The answer lies in *defect tolerance*: the property that the most readily formed intrinsic defects introduce only shallow electronic states near the band edges, where trapped carriers are thermally re-emitted rather than lost to non-radiative recombination. But defect tolerance is not a blanket immunity. Extrinsic impurities such as bismuth create deep midgap states that collapse the open-circuit voltage even at parts-per-million concentrations [3], and the same soft ionic lattice that enables tolerance also permits mobile ions to redistribute under bias and illumination, producing the notorious current–voltage hysteresis of early devices [2].

This thesis develops a unified treatment. Section 2 surveys the crystallographic and electronic foundations of the ABX3 perovskite family. Section 3 lays out the theoretical machinery: defect formation energies from density functional theory, Shockley–Read–Hall statistics, nudged-elastic-band calculations of migration barriers, and the Goldschmidt tolerance factor that guides compositional engineering. Section 4 contains the deep technical core, organized around four pillars — defect tolerance, ion migration, compositional engineering, and terawatt-scale deployment. Section 5 collects the empirical evidence, from defect spectroscopy to certified efficiency tables, and Section 6 confronts the limitations honestly: lead, stability, halide segregation, and the scaling gap between laboratory records and gigawatt factories.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The perovskite crystal and its electronic personality

The halide perovskite adopts the ABX3 structure: a three-dimensional network of corner-sharing BX6 octahedra, with the B-site occupied by Pb^2+ (or Sn^2+ in lead-free variants), the X-site by halide anions (I^-, Br^-, Cl^-), and the A-site cavity filled by a monovalent cation — methylammonium (MA^+), formamidinium (FA^+), cesium (Cs^+), or mixtures thereof. The structure is *soft*: the bulk modulus of MAPbI3 is roughly an order of magnitude below that of silicon, and the lattice readily distorts, tilts, and accommodates vacancies and interstitials at modest energetic cost.

The electronic structure is where perovskites depart from convention. In most tetrahedral semiconductors the valence band maximum is bonding in character, so a broken bond inserts a deep state near midgap. In lead halide perovskites, by contrast, the valence band maximum is formed from the *antibonding* combination of Pb 6s and I 5p orbitals. Consequently, removing an atom — creating a vacancy — tends to push the associated defect level toward or into the bands rather than into the gap. The defects that form most easily are thus electronically shallow [4].

> **Theorem (Defect-Tolerance Heuristic):** In a semiconductor whose valence band maximum is antibonding in character, the intrinsic point defects with the lowest formation energies generate electronic states resonant with or shallow relative to the band edges; deep midgap states require either extrinsic impurities with mismatched orbital energies or extreme lattice distortions.

This heuristic explains the empirical miracle: trap densities that would cripple a silicon device leave perovskite photoluminescence quantum yields and carrier diffusion lengths (routinely exceeding one micrometer) largely intact [6].

### 2.2 Device architectures

Modern perovskite solar cells are thin-film stacks a few hundred nanometers thick: a transparent conductive oxide, an electron-transport layer (TiO2, SnO2, or C60), the perovskite absorber, a hole-transport layer (spiro-OMeTAD, PTAA, or self-assembled monolayers), and a metal or transparent back contact.

## 3 Methodology

This thesis is a synthesis of first-principles theory, defect spectroscopy, and device metrology. The theoretical backbone consists of four formalisms.

**Defect formation energetics.** For a defect D in charge state q, the formation energy is

$$\Delta H_f(D^q) = E_{\mathrm{tot}}(D^q) - E_{\mathrm{tot}}(\mathrm{bulk}) - \sum_i n_i \mu_i + q(E_F + E_{\mathrm{VBM}}) + E_{\mathrm{corr}},$$

where the chemical potentials mu_i encode growth conditions (halide-rich versus lead-rich), E_F is the Fermi level, and E_corr collects finite-size electrostatic corrections. The *charge transition level* epsilon(q/q') — the Fermi energy at which two charge states have equal formation energy — determines whether a defect is shallow (level within a few kT of a band edge) or deep (near midgap).

**Shockley–Read–Hall recombination.** The non-radiative recombination rate through a trap of density N_t is governed by the SRH lifetime

$$\tau_{\mathrm{SRH}} = \frac{1}{\sigma v_{\mathrm{th}} N_t},$$

modulated by the trap's energy depth: a shallow trap with emission rate e_n >> capture rate c_n n cycles carriers back into the band, contributing negligibly to recombination, while a deep trap with comparable capture rates for electrons and holes is an efficient recombination center.

**Migration barriers.** Ionic hopping rates follow an Arrhenius law, D = D_0 exp(-E_a / kT), with barriers computed by the nudged elastic band method. Comparing E_a across species identifies the dominant mobile ion [2].

**The Goldschmidt tolerance factor.** For ionic radii r_A, r_B, r_X,

$$t = \frac{r_A + r_X}{\sqrt{2}\,(r_B + r_X)},$$

with photoactive cubic or tetragonal phases favored when t lies roughly between 0.8 and 1.0.

A compact implementation of the tolerance factor and SRH lifetime illustrates the quantitative workflow used throughout this thesis:

```python
import numpy as np

def goldschmidt_tolerance(rA, rB, rX):
    """Goldschmidt tolerance factor for an ABX3 perovskite."""
    return (rA + rX) / (np.sqrt(2.0) * (rB + rX))

# Shannon ionic radii in angstrom (12-coordinate A, 6-coordinate B/X)
radii = {"MA": 2.70, "FA": 2.79, "Cs": 1.81, "Pb": 1.19, "I": 2.20, "Br": 1.96}

for A in ["MA", "FA", "Cs"]:
    t = goldschmidt_tolerance(radii[A], radii["Pb"], radii["I"])
    print(f"{A}PbI3: t = {t:.3f}")

def srh_lifetime(N_t, sigma=1e-15, v_th=1e7):
    """SRH lifetime (s) for trap density N_t (cm^-3)."""
    return 1.0 / (sigma * v_th * N_t)

for N in [1e15, 1e16, 1e17]:
    print(f"N_t = {N:.0e} cm^-3 -> tau_SRH = {srh_lifetime(N)*1e9:.1f} ns")
```

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The electronic origins of defect tolerance

First-principles surveys of MAPbI3 consistently identify the same cast of low-formation-energy intrinsic defects: the iodide vacancy V_I, the methylammonium vacancy V_MA, the lead interstitial Pb_i, and the methylammonium interstitial MA_i [4]. Under typical growth conditions these defects form with energies below 1 eV, implying equilibrium concentrations in the 10^16 to 10^17 cm^-3 range — orders of magnitude above the electrically active impurity levels tolerated in silicon.

Why, then, do devices work? The answer has three layers.

*First*, the antibonding valence band maximum described above places most vacancy- and interstitial-derived levels within a few hundred meV of a band edge. A shallow electron trap 0.1 eV below the conduction band at room temperature has a thermal emission rate exceeding 10^9 s^-1; the trapped electron is re-emitted long before it can encounter a hole, so the trap acts as a transient reservoir rather than a recombination center.

*Second*, dynamic disorder helps. Molecular dynamics simulations show that reorientation of the dipolar MA cation can *heal* deep trap configurations: a defect geometry that is trap-active in a frozen lattice relaxes into a shallow configuration as surrounding cations rotate [8]. Incorporating dipolar MA into otherwise Cs/FA-based wide-bandgap compositions measurably suppresses trap-assisted non-radiative recombination, directly implicating cation dynamics in defect tolerance [8].

> **Theorem (Shallow-Trap Transparency):** A trap of depth E_t below the conduction band with thermal emission rate e_n = sigma_n v_th N_c exp(-E_t / kT) contributes negligibly to steady-state SRH recombination whenever e_n dominates the hole capture rate, i.e., for E_t less than a few kT at operating temperature. Defect tolerance is therefore a statement about the *distribution* of trap depths, not the *density* of traps.

The limits of tolerance are equally instructive. Bismuth intentionally incorporated as an extrinsic impurity substitutes Pb^2+ as Bi^3+ and introduces delocalized states near 0.16 eV and localized deep states near 0.51 eV; the open-circuit voltage falls systematically with Bi concentration even in the parts-per-million regime, while the fill factor degrades through the shallower component [3]. Defect tolerance, in short, extends to the intrinsic defect population of the pristine lattice — it does not survive arbitrary chemistry.

### 4.2 Ion migration, hysteresis, and the solid-electrolyte character of perovskites

The same soft lattice that forgives vacancies also lets ions move. Eames and co-workers combined impedance spectroscopy with density functional theory to show that MAPbI3 is simultaneously a photovoltaic absorber and a solid electrolyte: iodide vacancies migrate with an activation energy of approximately 0.58 eV (measured 0.60 to 0.68 eV from the temperature dependence of hysteresis), while MA^+ vacancy migration requires about 0.84 eV and Pb^2+ vacancy migration about 2.31 eV — effectively frozen at room temperature [2]. Three independent 2015 calculations converged on vacancy-mediated iodide hopping as the lowest-barrier channel, establishing it as the default transport mechanism [2].

The device consequences are profound. Under bias or illumination, mobile iodide vacancies drift and accumulate at interfaces, screening the built-in field over Debye-like lengths of order 10 nm and modulating charge extraction on timescales of seconds. The result is *J–V hysteresis*: the measured efficiency depends on scan direction and scan rate, an artifact that plagued early efficiency claims and forced the community to adopt stabilized maximum-power-point tracking as the honest metric [2][6].

Migration barriers are not immutable. Halide substitution experiments show that alloying bromide into MAPbI3 raises the iodide migration barrier from roughly 0.34 eV to 0.46 eV, suppressing hysteresis concurrently with efficiency gains — direct evidence that the mobile species and the performance artifact share a mechanism [2].

| Migrating species | Computed barrier (eV) | Mobile at 300 K? |
|---|---|---|
| I^- vacancy | 0.34 – 0.68 | Yes — dominant channel |
| MA^+ vacancy | 0.84 | Marginal |
| Pb^2+ vacancy | 2.31 | No — frozen |
| I^- interstitial | 0.1 – 0.3 (est.) | Yes — fast but low concentration |

### 4.3 Compositional engineering: mixed cations, mixed halides, and phase stability

If defect tolerance explains why perovskites work at all, *compositional engineering* explains why they keep getting better. Three compositional axes matter: the A-site cation blend, the halide ratio, and the B-site metal.

**Mixed cations.** Pure FAPbI3 offers the ideal single-junction bandgap near 1.48 eV but suffers a thermodynamic preference for a photoinactive hexagonal "yellow" delta phase at room temperature; pure MAPbI3 is phase-stable but volatile and capped near 1.55 eV; CsPbI3 has a too-wide gap and its own phase instability. Saliba and colleagues demonstrated that a triple-cation blend Cs/MA/FA suppresses yellow-phase impurities, seeds uniform crystallization with grains spanning the film thickness, and delivers stabilized 21.1% efficiency with 18% output retained after 250 hours of maximum-power-point tracking — a reproducibility breakthrough at the time [5].

**Mixed halides.** Substituting bromide for iodide widens the bandgap from about 1.55 eV toward 1.7 eV and beyond, which is exactly the range needed for the top cell of a silicon tandem. The trade is *halide segregation*: under illumination, mixed I/Br films demix into iodide-rich and bromide-rich domains, pinning the photoluminescence to the low-gap phase and eroding the voltage.

**Design rules.** The community has converged on a practical recipe for high-performance, stable compositions:

1. Use FA as the majority A-site cation for its near-ideal bandgap, with 5–15% Cs to entropically stabilize the black phase and a minority of MA (or MA-free formulations for thermal stability) [5][8].
2. Tune the I/Br ratio to the target bandgap: roughly 1.55 eV for single junctions, 1.68–1.75 eV for silicon tandems — accepting that wider gaps demand more aggressive segregation control.

A Rust sketch of the compositional search space illustrates how the tolerance factor, bandgap estimate, and defect-risk flags combine in high-throughput screening:

```rust
/// Candidate ABX3 composition for screening.
pub struct Composition {
    pub a_site: Vec<(String, f64)>, // (cation, fraction)
    pub x_site: Vec<(String, f64)>, // (halide, fraction)
}

impl Composition {
    /// Vegard-averaged Goldschmidt tolerance factor.
    pub fn tolerance_factor(&self) -> f64 {
        let r_a: f64 = self.a_site.iter()
            .map(|(c, f)| f * ionic_radius(c)).sum();
        let r_x: f64 = self.x_site.iter()
            .map(|(h, f)| f * ionic_radius(h)).sum();
        (r_a + r_x) / (2f64.sqrt() * (ionic_radius("Pb") + r_x))
    }

    /// Flags compositions at risk of photoinduced halide segregation.
    pub fn segregation_risk(&self) -> bool {
        let br: f64 = self.x_site.iter()
            .filter(|(h, _)| h == "Br").map(|(_, f)| f).sum();
        br > 0.15 && br < 0.85 // mixed-halide window where demixing is observed
    }
}

fn ionic_radius(species: &str) -> f64 {
    match species {
        "Cs" => 1.81, "MA" => 2.70, "FA" => 2.79,
        "Pb" => 1.19, "I" => 2.20, "Br" => 1.96,
        _ => panic!("unknown species"),
    }
}
```

### 4.4 The path to terawatt scale and tandem dominance

Laboratory records are necessary but not sufficient. A terawatt of photovoltaics at 25% module efficiency and 200 W/m^2 peak irradiance requires roughly 20,000 km^2 of modules — and the materials arithmetic must close. Here perovskites hold a structural advantage: the absorber is less than one micrometer thick, deposited from solution at low temperature, so the energy payback time can fall below six months and the grams-per-watt of semiconductor are two orders of magnitude below silicon wafers.

The more decisive argument for perovskites is the *tandem*. Single-junction silicon is asymptotically approaching its practical limit near 27%; the only credible route past 30% at low cost is a perovskite top cell harvesting blue-green photons stacked on a silicon bottom cell harvesting the red and infrared. The milestones trace a steep curve: 29.1% tandems noted in the 2019 decade review [6], LONGi's 33.9% in late 2023 — the first two-junction result to exceed the 33.7% single-junction Shockley–Queisser limit — then 34.6% in 2024, 34.85% in early 2025, and 35.5% certified by the European Solar Test Installation in July 2026 [7]. The theoretical ceiling for the perovskite–silicon pair sits near 43%, leaving enormous headroom [7].

## 5 Empirical/Proofs

The claims above rest on interlocking experimental evidence:

- **Efficiency trajectory.** Certified records rose from 3.8% in 2009 [1] through 22.1% (2016), 25%+ single junctions, and 29.1% tandems by 2019 [6], to the 35.5% two-terminal perovskite–silicon record of July 2026 [7]. Each step was certified by accredited laboratories (NREL, ESTI, JSIV) and entered in the Solar Cell Efficiency Tables.
- **Defect spectroscopy versus device voltage.** Intentional Bi doping from 10 ppm to 2% produces a monotonic decline in open-circuit voltage and electroluminescence yield, with DFT assigning a shallow delocalized component (~0.16 eV, hurting fill factor) and a deep localized component (~0.51 eV, hurting voltage) [3]. This is the cleanest experimental proof that trap *depth*, not density alone, governs performance.
- **Migration barriers versus hysteresis.** The measured 0.60–0.68 eV activation energy of hysteresis matches the computed 0.58 eV iodide-vacancy migration barrier, while MA^+ (0.84 eV) and Pb^2+ (2.31 eV) are excluded as room-temperature migrants [2]. Bromide alloying raises the iodide barrier and simultaneously suppresses hysteresis — a causal, not correlational, link.
- **Compositional stabilization.** Triple-cation Cs/MA/FA films show stabilized 21.1% PCE with 18% retained after 250 h MPPT, versus rapid degradation and poor reproducibility of single-cation controls [5]. Dipolar-cation incorporation in wide-bandgap CsFA compositions reduces trap-assisted recombination and lifts tandem-relevant efficiencies [8].

## 6 Limitations

Candor requires enumerating what remains unsolved.

1. **Lead toxicity and regulation.** Although the lead inventory per module is small, water-soluble PbI2 and PbBr2 degradation products are bioavailable, and modules in hail-prone or fire scenarios raise legitimate leaching concerns. Lead-sequestration encapsulants mitigate but do not eliminate the issue; tin-based alternatives remain far behind on efficiency and stability.
2. **Operational stability.** Intrinsic degradation pathways — ion migration under bias, halide segregation under light, organic-cation volatilization under heat — are suppressed but not solved. The gap between champion-cell stability claims and module-scale, warranty-grade reliability is the field's central credibility problem.
3. **Hysteresis-free metrology.** Despite progress, slow ionic transients still complicate efficiency certification; stabilized MPPT remains mandatory, and inter-laboratory reproducibility of stability data lags far behind efficiency data.
4. **Scalability of deposition.** Spin coating does not scale; slot-die coating, blade coating, and vapor deposition each trade uniformity against throughput and capital cost. Large-area tandems (e.g., 33% at 260.9 cm^2 [7]) trail small-area records significantly.

## 7 Conclusion

Halide perovskite photovoltaics succeeded because of a rare confluence: an antibonding valence band that renders the dominant intrinsic defects electronically shallow, a soft ionic lattice that enables low-temperature solution processing, and a compositional phase space rich enough to engineer bandgaps, stabilize photoactive phases, and stack tandems past the single-junction limit. The 3.8% dye-sensitized curiosity of 2009 [1] has become a 35.5% tandem technology knocking on commercialization's door [7].

Yet the same softness that grants defect tolerance also grants ion mobility, and the same solution processing that grants low cost also grants variability. The field's next decade will be judged not by another efficiency record but by whether modules survive twenty-five years in the field, whether factories coat square kilometers uniformly, and whether the lead question is answered to regulators' satisfaction. If those tests are passed, perovskites will not merely join silicon — they will ride on top of it, literally and economically, toward the multi-terawatt solar deployment the climate demands.
