# Graph-Based and Quantization-Based Indexing for Approximate Nearest Neighbor Search in Vector Databases: HNSW, DiskANN, and IVF-PQ at Billion Scale

## Abstract

Approximate nearest neighbor (ANN) search is the algorithmic backbone of modern vector databases, retrieval-augmented generation, and large-scale recommender systems, where corpora of a billion or more high-dimensional vectors must be searched in milliseconds. This thesis examines three dominant indexing paradigms — *Hierarchical Navigable Small World* (HNSW) graphs, the *DiskANN Vamana graph* over SSD storage, and the *inverted file with product quantization* (IVF-PQ) — through a unified recall-throughput engineering lens. We formalize the ANN problem, derive the asymptotic and constant-factor costs of each index, analyze the graph-theoretic mechanisms (layered scale separation, RNG-pruned degree bounds, beam search with page-aligned I/O, residual quantization and asymmetric distance computation) that place each method on a distinct segment of the recall-throughput Pareto frontier, and survey billion-scale evidence from SIFT1B, DEEP1B, and the NeurIPS'21 BigANN competition. We further address filtered ANN, hardware acceleration, and the fundamental limitations of all three approaches.

---

## 1 Introduction

The *approximate nearest neighbor* (ANN) problem asks, given a finite set $X \subset \mathbb{R}^d$ of size $n$ and a query $q \in \mathbb{R}^d$, to return a set $R \subset X$, $|R| = k$, whose distances to $q$ approximate those of the true $k$ nearest neighbors. The approximation quality is quantified by *recall@$k$*:

$$
\mathrm{recall}@k = \frac{|R \cap R^\ast|}{k}
$$

where $R^\ast$ is the exact answer set. In practice, the problem is really bi-criterial: maximize recall while maximizing *queries per second* (QPS), under constraints on index memory, build time, and update freshness.

This tension has shaped vector database engineering. Modern embedding workloads — dense retrieval over billions of documents, code, and image embeddings — cannot afford the $O(nd)$ cost of exhaustive scan at $n = 10^9$. Three index families dominate production systems:

1. **HNSW** [1]: a fully graph-based, multi-layer navigable small world index that achieves the best in-memory recall-latency trade-off and is the default index in nearly every vector database (pgvector, Qdrant, Weaviate, Milvus, Vespa).
2. **DiskANN** [2]: a graph-based index (the *Vamana* graph) whose node layout is page-aligned to SSD blocks, enabling billion-scale search on a single machine with tens of gigabytes of RAM; extended by FreshDiskANN [3] for streaming updates and Filtered-DiskANN for attribute predicates [4].
3. **IVF-PQ** [5,6]: the combination of an inverted file (coarse k-means clustering) with *product quantization* compression, which encodes each vector in as little as $m$ bytes; the memory-efficient workhorse of Faiss for billion-scale and GPU-resident search.

> **Theorem (Informal):** *No index can simultaneously achieve optimal recall, optimal QPS, minimal memory, and zero build cost. Each of HNSW, DiskANN, and IVF-PQ occupies a distinct non-dominated point on the four-dimensional Pareto surface defined by recall, throughput, memory, and index mutability.*

Section 2 formalizes ANN and reviews navigable small world theory, quantization, and benchmarking methodology. Section 3 presents each index's construction and search algorithms with cost analysis. Section 4 dissects four core mechanisms in depth. Section 5 reports empirical evidence from standardized benchmarks. Section 6 catalogues limitations, and Section 7 concludes with a practitioner's decision guide.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The ANN problem and navigability

Early graph-based ANN methods built a *navigable small world* (NSW) graph: each inserted point connects to its current approximate neighbors, and search proceeds greedily from an entry point, moving to the neighbor closest to the query [1]. The NSW graph approximates the *Delaunay graph* of the point set, which is known to be navigable — greedy routing on the Delaunay graph converges to the nearest neighbor — but computing Delaunay graphs explicitly is infeasible in high dimensions [7]. Empirically, NSW graphs exhibit *polylogarithmic* search path lengths, $O(A \log^\nu n)$, but they suffer from excessive node degree and connectivity problems in clustered data [1].

### 2.2 Hierarchical decomposition

Malkov and Yashunin [1] resolved NSW's weaknesses by introducing a controllable hierarchy. Each element is assigned a maximum layer $\ell$ drawn from an exponentially decaying distribution, $P(\ell) = \exp(-\ell/m_L) \cdot (1 - \exp(-1/m_L))$ for a level multiplier $m_L$. Layer 0 contains all points; each higher layer is a nested subset whose expected size shrinks by a factor of $M$ (in later descriptions) or exponentially in $m_L$. Edges are then *separated by distance scale*: long-range links live in upper layers, short-range links in lower layers. Search starts at the top layer with a greedy step, then descends, carrying the best candidate as the entry point for the next layer [1]. The result is provably *logarithmic* expected complexity, $O(\log n)$ distance evaluations per layer descent, analogous to skip lists.

### 2.3 Product quantization

Product quantization (PQ), introduced by Jégou, Douze, and Schmid [5], decomposes $\mathbb{R}^d$ into a Cartesian product of $m$ subspaces, $\mathbb{R}^d = \mathbb{R}^{d_1} \times \cdots \times \mathbb{R}^{d_m}$, and trains an independent k-means quantizer with $k^\ast$ centroids in each. A vector $x$ is encoded as the $m$-tuple of centroid indices $(q_1(x_1), \ldots, q_m(x_m))$, requiring $m \log_2 k^\ast$ bits — typically $m = 8$, $k^\ast = 256$, i.e., **8 bytes per vector**. The key innovation is *asymmetric distance computation* (ADC): the query remains uncompressed, and per-subspace lookup tables $T_j[i] = \|q_j - c_{j,i}\|^2$ are precomputed, so the approximate squared distance is a sum of $m$ table lookups [5]:

$$
\hat{d}^2(q, x) = \sum_{j=1}^{m} T_j[\,q_j(x_j)\,]
$$

Combined with an *inverted file* (IVF) coarse quantizer — the database is partitioned into $K$ Voronoi cells, each with an inverted list, and only the $n_{probe}$ cells nearest the query are scanned — the resulting IVFADC index [6] compresses billion-scale datasets into tens of gigabytes and scans only a fraction of the corpus.

### 2.4 Benchmark methodology

Comparing ANN indices requires care. The ann-benchmarks framework of Aumüller, Bernhardsson, and Faith [8] standardized the recall-QPS Pareto plot: each index is evaluated over a sweep of its hyperparameters, and only the *non-dominated* points (the Pareto frontier) are compared. The billion-scale successor, the NeurIPS'21 *BigANN* competition [9], added realistic constraints — hardware cost, power (joules per query), and horizontal replication cost to serve 100K QPS — across six datasets (DEEP1B, BIGANN, MS Turing, MS SpaceV, text-to-image, SSN++), establishing that recall-throughput curves alone understate the engineering trade-offs.

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 HNSW: construction and search

**Construction.** Points are inserted sequentially. For a new point $p$ with max layer $\ell \sim$ exponential, the algorithm runs the search procedure on layers $\ell+1, \ldots, L_{max}$ to find an entry point, then connects $p$ at each layer $l \le \ell$ to up to $M$ neighbors selected by the *heuristic neighbor selection* rule: from the candidate list, repeatedly pick the closest candidate $e$, and add it only if it is closer to $p$ than to any already-selected neighbor — an RNG-style *relative neighborhood graph* pruning that favors directional diversity over pure proximity [1]. If the candidate list exceeds $M$, it is shrunk with the same heuristic; bidirectional links are maintained.

**Search.** Given query $q$, start at the top-layer entry point; at each layer perform greedy 1-NN descent; at layer 0 run a best-first beam search with dynamic candidate list of size $ef_{search}$ [1]. The parameter $ef_{search}$ is the primary recall-latency knob: larger values explore more of the graph.

| Parameter | Role | Typical value |
|---|---|---|
| $M$ | Max out-degree per node per layer | 16–64 |
| $ef_{construction}$ | Candidate list size at build time | 100–800 |
| $ef_{search}$ | Candidate list size at query time | 10–1000 |
| $m_L$ | Level multiplier ($1/\ln M$) | $\approx 0.36$ for $M=16$ |

**Costs.** Memory is $O(n M)$ edges plus the raw vectors; a 128-dimensional float dataset with $M=32$ needs roughly $n \times (512 + 128)$ bytes plus overhead — ~1 GB per ~1.4M vectors, so *billion-scale HNSW requires hundreds of gigabytes of RAM*. Build time is $O(n \log n \cdot ef_{construction})$. Query cost is $O(\log n \cdot ef_{search})$ distance computations with excellent constants, which is why HNSW dominates the high-recall end of in-memory benchmarks [1,8].

### 3.2 DiskANN: the Vamana graph on SSD

DiskANN [2] observes that the barrier to billion-scale search is not computation but *memory capacity*: graph indices must traditionally reside in RAM. Its design co-optimizes the graph and the storage layout:

1. **Vamana graph.** A generalization of the RNG-pruning used in HNSW/NSG with two tunable parameters: $R$ (max degree) and $\alpha \ge 1$ (the *edge occlusion* threshold). During construction, when considering candidate $p$ against accepted neighbor $p'$, $p$ is rejected if $\alpha \cdot d(p^\ast, p') \le d(p^\ast, p)$ — with $\alpha > 1$ retaining longer-range edges that improve navigability at modest degree cost. Vamana is built with multiple randomized insertion orders and refinement passes, making it competitive even as an in-memory index [2,7].
2. **Page-aligned layout.** Each node is stored as a *chunk* containing its full-precision vector and its neighbor list, padded to SSD page boundaries (typically 4 KB). A beam search (beam width $L$, analogous to $ef_{search}$) then issues *one random SSD read per visited node*, and because chunks are page-aligned, each read fetches exactly the needed data with no read amplification [2]. A small in-RAM *product-quantized* copy of the vectors filters candidates before the full-precision re-ranking pass.
3. **Beam search.** Query processing maintains a beam of the $L$ best candidates; each iteration expands the unvisited beam members, issuing parallel SSD I/Os. The parameter $L$ trades I/O volume against recall [2].

On SIFT1B, DiskANN serves **>5,000 QPS at <3 ms mean latency with 95%+ 1-recall@1 on a 16-core machine with 64 GB RAM and a commodity SSD**, where Faiss IVFADC with the same memory budget plateaus near 50% recall@1; equivalently, it serves 5–10× more points per node than HNSW or NSG at equal recall [2].

### 3.3 IVF-PQ: the Faiss formulation

In Faiss [6], the canonical billion-scale index is `IndexIVFPQ`: a coarse quantizer (flat, or HNSW for faster assignment, e.g. `IVF10000_HNSW32,PQ16`) partitions the space into `nlist` cells; each database vector is stored as the PQ code of its *residual* $r(x) = x - q_c(x)$ relative to its cell centroid, exploiting the fact that residuals have lower variance and quantize more accurately [5,6]. At query time, the $n_{probe}$ nearest cells are selected, lookup tables are built per cell (or shared), and inverted lists are scanned with ADC. A refinement stage (`IndexIVFPQR`) can re-rank top candidates with a second, finer quantizer or exact vectors.

```python
import faiss
import numpy as np

d = 128
nlist, m, nbits = 10000, 16, 8
coarse = faiss.IndexHNSWFlat(d, 32)          # fast coarse assignment
index = faiss.IndexIVFPQ(coarse, d, nlist, m, nbits)
index.train(x_train)                          # k-means for coarse + PQ codebooks
index.add(x_base)
index.nprobe = 64                             # recall-latency knob
D, I = index.search(x_query, k=10)
```

Memory is dominated by the codes: $n \times m$ bytes (16 GB for $10^9$ vectors at $m=16$) plus inverted-list overhead and the coarse centroids — small enough to hold billion-scale indices in tens of gigabytes of RAM, or entirely on GPU [6,9].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Layered scale separation in HNSW

The central theoretical contribution of HNSW is *edge scale separation* [1]. In a flat NSW graph, a greedy search must traverse $O(n^\rho)$ hops in the worst case because long-range and short-range edges compete in the same candidate list. By stratifying edges across layers with exponentially decaying layer membership, each layer's greedy phase operates on a graph whose characteristic edge length matches the current distance to the query. The expected number of hops per layer is bounded by a constant (the small-world property), and the number of layers is $O(\log n)$ [1]. The heuristic neighbor selection further guarantees that each node's neighborhood *covers* its surroundings directionally — a discrete analogue of Delaunay navigability — which is why HNSW degrades gracefully on highly clustered data where pure k-NN graphs disconnect [1,7].

### 4.2 Vamana's $\alpha$-pruning and SSD-conscious beam search

Vamana's construction differs from HNSW in two decisive ways. First, the $\alpha$-parameterized occlusion rule ($\alpha > 1$) deliberately keeps some edges that a strict RNG would discard, producing a graph with better *navigability per degree* — shorter greedy paths at the same memory budget [2]. Second, construction is *iterative*: after an initial random graph, multiple passes of greedy search from random starts collect candidate neighbors, prune with the $\alpha$ rule, and rewire, converging to a graph whose quality is robust to insertion order [2]. The SSD layout then converts the graph's I/O pattern from random 4 KB reads into *useful* 4 KB reads: because each node's vector and adjacency list share one page, the beam search's per-node I/O has unit read amplification, and the in-RAM PQ copy means only ~100 full-precision pages are fetched per query at high recall [2].

### 4.3 Residual product quantization and ADC arithmetic

Why quantize *residuals* rather than raw vectors? The coarse centroid $q_c(x)$ absorbs the large-scale structure of the data; the residual $r(x) = x - q_c(x)$ is concentrated near the origin with much smaller norm variance, so a fixed bit budget ($m$ bytes) achieves lower expected squared distortion [5,6]. ADC then evaluates distances with $m$ table lookups and additions per candidate — roughly $m$ CPU cycles per distance versus $d$ multiply-adds for exact computation, a ~8× arithmetic reduction for $d=128, m=16$, plus the elimination of memory bandwidth for raw vectors. Optimized PQ (OPQ) applies a learned rotation to balance variance across subspaces, further reducing distortion at fixed $m$ [6].

### 4.4 The recall-throughput Pareto frontier

The three indices occupy different segments of the frontier because they bottleneck on different resources:

| Index | Bottleneck resource | Best regime | Representative result |
|---|---|---|---|
| HNSW | RAM capacity, distance-compute | In-memory, highest recall@10 at ms latency | Dominates ann-benchmarks high-recall region [8] |
| DiskANN | SSD random-read IOPS | Billion-scale, constrained RAM | 5K+ QPS, 95% recall@1, SIFT1B, 64 GB RAM [2] |
| IVF-PQ | Memory bandwidth for list scans | Memory-frugal, GPU-resident | 8 bytes/vector; CUANNS-IVFPQ 80K QPS at 90% recall on BIGANN [9] |

HNSW's curve is the upper envelope in RAM but collapses past the memory boundary [2]. DiskANN's curve extends the frontier along the *capacity* axis: it concedes a small constant-factor latency penalty per query (SSD round-trips) to shift the feasible region from $10^8$ to $10^9+$ vectors per node. IVF-PQ's curve dominates the *memory* axis: at equal RAM, it indexes 10–30× more vectors than HNSW (16 bytes/vector vs. hundreds), but its recall ceiling at fixed QPS is lower because quantization distortion is irreducible and scanning $n_{probe}$ lists touches far more candidates than a graph walk [6,9].

### 4.5 Filtered ANN: predicates meet geometry

Production vector databases must combine vector similarity with attribute filters ("nearest product images under $50"). Naive *post-filtering* (search, then filter) fails at low selectivity; *pre-filtering* (filter, then search the subset) fails when the subset is tiny. Filtered-DiskANN [4] builds the Vamana graph with *label-aware* edge construction so that the subgraph induced by any frequent label combination remains navigable, enabling single-pass filtered beam search. For HNSW, ACORN scales the degree budget by $1/s$ for selectivity $s$ [4]. Arbitrary-filter ANN remains an active research area with fundamental tension: a graph optimized for the full distribution cannot be simultaneously optimal for all $2^L$ label-induced subgraphs.

## 5 Empirical Results / Proofs

### 5.1 Complexity summary

> **Theorem (HNSW search cost [1]):** *With level multiplier $m_L$ and degree bound $M$, the expected number of distance computations in HNSW search is $O(\log n \cdot M)$, independent of the data distribution's intrinsic dimension up to constants in the NSW navigability assumption.*

> **Theorem (PQ distortion [5]):** *For a $d$-dimensional vector split into $m$ subspaces with $k^\ast$ centroids each, the expected squared quantization error of product quantization satisfies $\mathbb{E}\|x - q(x)\|^2 = \sum_j \mathbb{E}\|x_j - q_j(x_j)\|^2$, i.e., distortion decomposes additively and ADC estimates distances with error bounded by the sum of per-subspace errors.*

### 5.2 Billion-scale benchmark evidence

- **SIFT1B (128-d, $10^9$ vectors)** [2]: DiskANN achieves 95%+ 1-recall@1 at >5,000 QPS, <3 ms mean latency, 64 GB RAM; Faiss IVFOADC+G+P at the same footprint reaches ~50% recall@1. HNSW at this scale needs ~300+ GB RAM, i.e., is infeasible on the same node.
- **NeurIPS'21 BigANN challenge** [9]: At a 2,000 QPS floor, the DiskANN-derived OptaNNE GraphNN reached 0.99978 recall on DEEP1B; GPU IVFPQ (CUANNS) reached 80–110K QPS at 90% recall — hardware choice moves the frontier more than algorithm choice.
- **ann-benchmarks** [8]: HNSW forms the upper envelope of recall vs. QPS at million scale; FAISS-IVF trails at high recall but wins 5–10× on index size.
- **Graph survey** [7]: Across eight datasets, Vamana and HNSW consistently occupy the top-right of QPS-vs-recall@10 plots among graph methods; RNG-pruned graphs (HNSW, NSG, Vamana) strictly dominate k-NN graphs.

### 5.3 Worked engineering calculation

Consider serving $10^9$ 128-d float vectors at 95% recall@10, 10K QPS target:

- **HNSW** ($M=32$): index ≈ $10^9 \times (512\,\text{B vectors} + 32 \times 2 \times 4\,\text{B edges}) \approx 768$ GB RAM — requires a multi-hundred-GB server; per-node cost dominates.
- **DiskANN** ($R=64$, PQ-compressed RAM copy): SSD footprint ≈ 600 GB; RAM ≈ 64 GB; achievable on one workstation [2].
- **IVF-PQ** ($m=16$): RAM ≈ 16 GB codes + lists; fits on a laptop-class machine; recall@10 ≈ 0.90–0.95 achievable at high $n_{probe}$, but QPS per core is lower due to list scanning [6,9].

The calculation locates each index's operating point: HNSW is optimal under large RAM budgets, DiskANN under single-node capacity budgets, IVF-PQ under memory-compression budgets.

## 6 Limitations

1. **HNSW memory wall.** Memory grows linearly in $n$ with a large constant (vectors + $2M$ edges/node/layer). Beyond RAM, performance collapses; distributed HNSW (sharding) multiplies the recall problem across shards and complicates $ef_{search}$ calibration [1,9].
2. **HNSW build cost and dynamism.** Construction is $O(n \log n)$ with high constants ($ef_{construction} \ge 200$ for quality); deletions are handled by tombstoning, which degrades graph quality over time, and true incremental rebalancing remains unsolved [3].
3. **DiskANN tail latency.** SSD I/O introduces p99 latency variance (GC pauses, queue contention); the PQ-compressed RAM filter can misroute beam search on out-of-distribution queries, and the page-aligned layout wastes space for small vectors [2].
4. **IVF-PQ recall ceiling.** Quantization distortion is *irreducible* at fixed $m$; high-recall regimes (≥0.99) require large $n_{probe}$, eroding the throughput advantage, and coarse-quantizer imbalance on skewed data creates hot inverted lists [5,6].
5. **Filtered search.** All three indices degrade under arbitrary attribute predicates; label-aware constructions [4] handle frequent filters but not ad-hoc conjunctions, and selectivity estimation errors cause cliff-like recall drops.
6. **Benchmark realism.** SIFT/DEEP/GIST descriptors are dated proxies for modern text embeddings (e.g., 768–4096-d transformer vectors with different intrinsic dimensionality); results on BigANN's MS Turing/SpaceV tracks show rankings shift with data distribution [9].
7. **Hardware coupling.** Reported QPS numbers are meaningless without the hardware envelope — the BigANN challenge showed 8-GPU systems beating single-node CPU systems by 100× at equal recall [9], so "which index is fastest" is ill-posed without a cost model.

---

## 7 Conclusion

HNSW, DiskANN, and IVF-PQ represent three coherent answers to one question: *where should the index spend its budget?* HNSW spends it on RAM and graph edges to buy the best recall-latency curve in memory. DiskANN spends it on SSD capacity and careful page layout to buy billion-scale feasibility on a single node. IVF-PQ spends it on quantization distortion to buy two orders of magnitude of memory compression and GPU amenability. The recall-throughput Pareto frontier is therefore not a single curve but a *surface* parameterized by memory budget, hardware, and mutability requirements — and the engineering discipline of vector databases consists of locating the workload's operating point on that surface. Future progress will likely come from hybrids: graph-guided quantization, learned adaptive beam widths, hardware-aware layouts, and predicate-aware graphs that close the filtered-ANN gap.
