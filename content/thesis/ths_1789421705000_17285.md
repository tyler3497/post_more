# Byzantine Fault Tolerance: PBFT View Changes, HotStuff's Linear Communication, and the FLP Impossibility

## Abstract

Byzantine fault tolerant state machine replication occupies a position in distributed systems: the FLP impossibility result [3] rules out deterministic consensus in fully asynchronous systems with even a single crash fault, yet production systems routinely reach agreement despite arbitrary, adversarial misbehavior. This article reconstructs the intellectual arc from impossibility to engineering practice. We present the bivalence argument behind FLP and explain why it forces the partial-synchrony assumption formalized by Dwork, Lynch, and Stockmeyer [4], which every practical BFT protocol adopts. We then dissect Castro and Liskov's PBFT [1] — its three-phase normal-case protocol, the quorum-intersection argument behind the 3f+1 resilience bound, checkpoint-based garbage collection, and its view-change protocol, which replaces a faulty primary without sacrificing safety. Finally, we analyze HotStuff [2], whose chained three-phase design, threshold-signature quorum certificates, and rotating leaders reduce both steady-state and view-change communication to linear while achieving responsiveness. Empirical and analytical evidence shows this trajectory from quadratic and cubic protocols to linear ones is not merely an optimization but a structural consequence of how quorums, certificates, and leaders are composed.

## 1 Introduction

Few problems in computer science combine theoretical depth with industrial urgency the way Byzantine agreement does. On the theoretical side, we have one of the sharpest impossibility results in the field: no deterministic protocol can guarantee consensus in an asynchronous system if even one process may crash [3]. On the practical side, we have replicated databases, permissioned ledgers, and blockchain networks that must keep hundreds of replicas consistent while a fraction of them behave arbitrarily — dropping messages, forging signatures, colluding, or simply lying.

The bridge between these two poles is the *partial synchrony* model [4], which assumes that message delays are unbounded only until some unknown Global Stabilization Time, after which they become bounded. Within this model, the history of Byzantine fault tolerance reads as a long campaign against communication complexity:

1. **Lamport, Shostak, and Pease's 1982 formulation** established the 3f+1 bound for Byzantine agreement and gave exponential protocols that proved feasibility.
2. **PBFT [1]** made BFT practical: a three-phase, leader-driven protocol with quadratic normal-case communication and a notoriously subtle view-change subprotocol for leader replacement.
3. **HotStuff [2]** restructured the protocol around quorum certificates and chained phases, making *both* the steady state and the view change linear in the number of replicas, and achieving *responsiveness* — progress at the speed of the actual network rather than a worst-case delay bound.

This article studies that trajectory as a single coherent story. Section 2 fixes terminology and system models. Section 3 reconstructs the FLP impossibility proof. Section 4 gives a deep treatment of PBFT with special attention to view changes. Section 5 analyzes HotStuff's linear design. Section 6 surveys limitations, tradeoffs, and open problems, and Section 7 concludes.

## 2 Background: Consensus, Byzantine Failures, and Timing Models

A *replicated state machine* keeps n deterministic replicas that must execute the same sequence of client operations. The consensus problem at its core requires three properties:

- **Agreement (safety):** no two correct replicas decide differently.
- **Validity:** the decided value was proposed by some correct process.
- **Termination (liveness):** every correct process eventually decides.

Failures are classified by severity. A *crash* fault stops a process silently. An *omission* fault drops some messages. A *Byzantine* fault is arbitrary: a faulty replica may send contradictory messages to different peers, forge content (modulo cryptography), or collude with other faulty replicas. Byzantine faults model both malicious attackers and the most insidious software bugs, which is why the literature treats them as the gold standard of fault tolerance.

Timing assumptions determine what is achievable:

| Model | Message delay bound | Clock bound | Deterministic consensus |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fully synchronous | Known bound, known a priori | Known drift bound | Possible |
| Partially synchronous [4] | Unknown bound, holds after unknown GST | Unknown drift, holds after GST | Possible |
| Fully asynchronous | No bound | No bound | **Impossible** [3] |

The asynchronous model is brutally honest about networks: there is no way to distinguish a crashed process from a merely slow one, because any finite timeout might expire before a slow-but-correct message arrives. This indistinguishability is the engine of the FLP proof.

> **Theorem (Fischer-Lynch-Paterson [3]):** No deterministic protocol can guarantee that all correct processes reach consensus in an asynchronous message-passing system if even one process may fail by crashing.

The theorem does not say consensus is impossible in practice. It says something more precise: *deterministic* protocols cannot *guarantee* termination under *pure asynchrony*. Each italicized qualifier is an escape hatch, and the history of BFT is the history of choosing which hatch to take. PBFT and HotStuff take the partial-synchrony hatch [4]; randomized protocols such as Ben-Or's take the randomization hatch; failure-detector frameworks take the oracle hatch.

## 3 The FLP Impossibility Result

The FLP proof is a masterpiece of the *bivalence argument*. A configuration is **0-valent** if every admissible execution from it decides 0, **1-valent** if every admissible execution decides 1, and **bivalent** if both decision values remain reachable. The proof shows that from any bivalent configuration, the adversarial scheduler can select a next step that keeps the configuration bivalent — forever.

Three lemmas carry the argument:

1. **Existence of an initial bivalent configuration.** With all processes proposing 0, the protocol must decide 0 (validity); with all proposing 1, it must decide 1. Flipping one process's proposal at a time must cross a 0-valent to 1-valent boundary, and the boundary configurations are bivalent.
2. **Commutativity of disjoint schedules.** If two message-delivery events involve disjoint sets of processes, applying them in either order leads to the same configuration.
3. **The deciding step.** From a bivalent configuration C, suppose every possible next step leads to a univalent configuration. Then there exist two steps e and e' applicable to C that lead to 0-valent and 1-valent configurations respectively. A careful case analysis — whether e and e' involve the same process — shows the scheduler can interleave deliveries to contradict the assumption.

The conclusion is stark: *some* execution of *any* deterministic asynchronous protocol with one crash fault never terminates. The adversary simply refuses to deliver the one message that would force a decision, and no correct process can tell whether the sender crashed or the network is slow.

This is why PBFT and HotStuff assume partial synchrony [4]. After the unknown Global Stabilization Time, message delays are bounded by an unknown constant, and timeouts become meaningful: a primary that produces no progress for long enough after GST can be declared faulty and replaced. FLP is not violated, because the guarantee is termination *after GST*, and GST may lie arbitrarily far in the future.

The following Python sketch demonstrates the combinatorial fact that makes quorum-based protocols robust where naive majorities fail — the property FLP's adversary exploits is indistinguishability, while the property BFT protocols exploit is *intersection*:

```python
from itertools import combinations

def quorum_intersection(n: int, f: int) -> bool:
    """Check that any two quorums of size 2f+1 intersect in at least f+1 replicas."""
    replicas = list(range(n))
    quorums = list(combinations(replicas, 2 * f + 1))
    for q1 in quorums:
        for q2 in quorums:
            if len(set(q1) & set(q2)) < f + 1:
                return False
    return True

for f in (1, 2, 3):
    n = 3 * f + 1
    print(f"f={f}, n={n}: intersection >= f+1 holds: {quorum_intersection(n, f)}")
    # With at most f Byzantine replicas, every quorum intersection
    # contains at least one correct replica -- the carrier of truth
    # across view changes.
```

## 4 PBFT: Practical Byzantine Fault Tolerance

Castro and Liskov's PBFT [1] was the first Byzantine fault tolerant replication protocol fast enough for real systems — their BFT NFS implementation ran only 3 percent slower than unreplicated NFS. It replicates a deterministic service across n = 3f+1 replicas, one designated *primary* and the rest *backups*, and it proceeds in *views*, each with a fixed primary given by p = v mod n.

### 4.1 The Normal-Case Protocol: Pre-Prepare, Prepare, and Commit

A client sends a request to the primary, which assigns it a sequence number s in the current view v and drives three phases:

| Phase | Message | Sender | Recipient | Purpose |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Pre-prepare | PRE-PREPARE(v, s, d) | Primary | All backups | Propose ordering for digest d |
| 2. Prepare | PREPARE(v, s, d, i) | Replica i | All replicas | Echo: "I accept this ordering" |
| 3. Commit | COMMIT(v, s, d, i) | Replica i | All replicas | Confirm: "a quorum accepted it" |

A replica considers a request **prepared** when it holds the pre-prepare plus 2f matching prepares — a quorum certificate of 2f+1 votes binding value to (v, s). It then multicasts COMMIT. The request is **committed-local** at replica i when i collects 2f+1 matching commits; i then executes the operation and replies to the client. The client accepts the result after f+1 matching replies, guaranteeing at least one came from a correct replica.

The three phases are not redundant. Pre-prepare plus prepare establishes that a *quorum* agrees on the ordering despite a Byzantine primary that might send different pre-prepares to different backups — this is exactly the equivocation attack the prepare phase defeats. The commit phase establishes that a quorum *knows* a quorum agreed, which is the knowledge needed to survive a primary replacement: any prepared value is witnessed by enough correct replicas that the next view cannot miss it.

Communication is quadratic: each of the two all-to-all phases sends O(n squared) messages. For the modest replica counts of the late 1990s this was a triumph over the exponential protocols of the 1980s, but the quadratic cost — and the far worse cost of view changes — is precisely what HotStuff later attacked [2].

```rust
/// PBFT message types for a single consensus instance (view v, sequence s).
#[derive(Clone, Debug)]
enum PbftMessage {
    PrePrepare { view: u64, seq: u64, digest: [u8; 32] },
    Prepare { view: u64, seq: u64, digest: [u8; 32], replica: usize },
    Commit { view: u64, seq: u64, digest: [u8; 32], replica: usize },
    ViewChange { new_view: u64, last_checkpoint: u64, prepared: Vec<PreparedCert> },
    NewView { new_view: u64, view_changes: Vec<PbftMessage>, pre_prepares: Vec<PbftMessage> },
}

struct PreparedCert { view: u64, seq: u64, digest: [u8; 32], votes: Vec<usize> }

fn is_prepared(pre_prepare: bool, prepares: usize, f: usize) -> bool {
    // prepared  <=>  1 pre-prepare + 2f matching prepares  ==  quorum of 2f+1
    pre_prepare && prepares >= 2 * f
}

fn is_committed_local(commits: usize, f: usize) -> bool {
    // committed-local  <=>  2f+1 matching commits (including own)
    commits >= 2 * f + 1
}
```

### 4.2 Quorum Intersection and the 3f+1 Resilience Bound

PBFT's safety rests on one combinatorial fact: any two quorums of size 2f+1 drawn from 3f+1 replicas intersect in at least f+1 replicas, and therefore in at least one *correct* replica. This correct replica in the intersection is the bridge across views — it carries the knowledge that some value was prepared into any future quorum.

> **Theorem (PBFT Agreement [1]):** If a correct replica commits a request with sequence number s in view v, then no correct replica commits a different request with sequence number s in any view v' >= v.

*Proof sketch.* Committed-local requires 2f+1 commits, so at least f+1 correct replicas are prepared on the value. During a view change, the new primary collects 2f+1 VIEW-CHANGE messages, which intersect the prepared set in at least one correct replica that reports the prepared certificate. The new primary must re-propose the prepared value with the highest view, so no conflicting value can gather a 2f+1 quorum in the new view. By induction across views, the value sticks forever. ∎

The bound n = 3f+1 is tight for this style of protocol: with only 3f replicas, two quorums of size 2f+1 could intersect in exactly f replicas, all of which might be Byzantine, and the bridge collapses.

### 4.3 Checkpoints, Watermarks, and Log Garbage Collection

Replicas cannot keep every message forever. PBFT defines a *stable checkpoint* as a state snapshot certified by 2f+1 CHECKPOINT messages. Checkpoints are taken every K sequence numbers (K = 128 in the original implementation), and each replica maintains a low watermark h (the last stable checkpoint) and a high watermark H = h + K. Messages with sequence numbers outside the open interval between h and H are discarded, which bounds log size and lets slow replicas catch up by fetching a certified checkpoint rather than replaying ancient history.

Checkpoints interact delicately with view changes: a VIEW-CHANGE message carries the sender's last stable checkpoint plus every prepared request with sequence number above it. This is how the new primary learns both what is durably decided and what is merely in flight.

### 4.4 View Changes: Replacing a Faulty Primary

The view-change protocol is PBFT's most intricate component — and its most expensive. When a backup's timer expires without progress, it multicasts VIEW-CHANGE for view v+1 containing its latest stable checkpoint and all prepared certificates above it. Upon collecting 2f+1 VIEW-CHANGE messages, the new primary p' = (v+1) mod n broadcasts NEW-VIEW carrying the set V of view-change messages and a recomputed set O of PRE-PREPARE messages: for each sequence number between the new low watermark and high watermark, if some prepared certificate exists in V, the request with the highest view is re-proposed; otherwise a null pre-prepare fills the slot so sequence numbers stay dense.

Safety is preserved by the quorum-intersection argument of Section 4.2: any value committed-local in the old view was prepared at f+1 correct replicas, and the 2f+1 view-change quorum intersects that set, forcing the new primary to carry the value forward. Liveness is recovered because timers grow exponentially across views, so after GST some view has both a correct primary and a long enough timeout for progress.

The cost, however, is severe. Each replica multicasts its VIEW-CHANGE (quadratic messages), and the NEW-VIEW message embeds 2f+1 VIEW-CHANGE messages, each carrying prepared certificates of size linear in n. The worst-case authenticator complexity of a PBFT view change is therefore *cubic* in n — a single faulty primary can impose O(n cubed) cryptographic work on the system. This asymmetry, cheap steady state versus catastrophically expensive leader replacement, is the wound HotStuff was designed to close [2].

> **Theorem (PBFT Liveness [1]):** After GST, if at most f replicas are faulty, exponential timer backoff guarantees that some view with a correct primary lasts long enough for the normal-case protocol to make progress.

## 5 HotStuff: Linear Communication via Chained Phases

HotStuff [2] reframes BFT consensus around two ideas: *quorum certificates* built from threshold signatures, and *chaining* that pipelines the protocol's phases. The result is the first partially synchronous BFT protocol that is simultaneously responsive and linear in both the steady state and view changes.

### 5.1 The Three-Chain Commit Rule and Quorum Certificates

HotStuff proceeds in views with rotating leaders. Each view has three phases — prepare, pre-commit, and commit — but communication is always *star-shaped*: the leader broadcasts a proposal, replicas send votes *to the leader*, and the leader aggregates 2f+1 votes into a threshold-signature quorum certificate (QC) that it broadcasts. Every phase costs O(n) messages and O(n) signature verifications rather than PBFT's O(n squared).

```haskell
-- Simplified HotStuff three-chain commit rule.
data Block = Block { blkView :: Int, blkParent :: Maybe Block, blkQC :: Maybe QC }
data QC = QC { qcView :: Int, qcBlock :: Block }  -- 2f+1 threshold-signed votes

-- A direct chain: each block's QC certifies its parent, views strictly increase.
extends :: Block -> Block -> Bool
extends child parent =
  case blkQC child of
    Just qc -> qcBlock qc == parent && qcView qc == blkView parent
               && blkView child > blkView parent
    Nothing -> False

-- Three-chain commit rule: b is committed if it heads a 3-chain.
committed :: Block -> Block -> Block -> Bool
committed b b' b'' = extends b' b && extends b'' b'
```

ReplicaVote(r, leader) ==
  /\ phase[r] = "prepare"
  /\ SendVote(r, leader)  \* linear: vote goes to the leader only
  /\ UNCHANGED <<lockedQC, highQC>>

ThreeChainCommit(r, b1, b2, b3) ==
  /\ Extends(b2, b1) /\ Extends(b3, b2)
  /\ ConsecutiveViews(b1, b2, b3)

## 6 Limitations, Tradeoffs, and Open Problems

No protocol escapes tradeoffs; HotStuff's linearity buys new costs elsewhere:

- **Latency in phases.** Chained HotStuff needs more network round trips per commit than PBFT's three phases, trading latency for throughput and scalability. HotStuff-1 [6] attacks this with speculative early finality confirmations after a single phase, resolving the *prefix speculation dilemma* that arises when speculation meets streamlined leader rotation.
- **Cryptographic assumptions.** Threshold signatures make QCs constant-sized, but they add setup complexity (distributed key generation) and rely on stronger assumptions than PBFT's MAC-and-signature hybrid.
- **Leader rotation overhead.** Rotating the leader every view improves fairness but means every view pays a view-change-like cost; stable-leader variants amortize better under a correct leader.
- **The FLP boundary remains.** All of this machinery buys liveness only *after GST*. Before GST, or under sustained asynchrony, no deterministic protocol in this family can guarantee progress [3] — the impossibility result is a permanent ceiling, not a temporary obstacle.
- **Optimistic fast paths are fragile.** The literature on optimistic BFT (Zyzzyva, FaB) shows how easily two-phase fast paths break safety or liveness under view changes; a careful analysis exhibits safety violations and stuck executions in published protocols with only four replicas and one or two view changes [5]. HotStuff's lesson is that the slow path must be as cheap and simple as the fast path, not bolted on beside it.

## 7 Conclusion

The story from FLP to PBFT to HotStuff is the story of learning exactly which assumptions buy exactly which guarantees. FLP [3] tells us that pure asynchrony plus determinism cannot guarantee consensus — so practical protocols assume partial synchrony [4] and promise liveness only after stabilization. PBFT [1] showed that Byzantine agreement could be practical, paying quadratic steady-state cost and cubic view-change cost for the privilege. HotStuff [2] showed that restructuring around quorum certificates and chained phases makes the entire protocol — steady state *and* leader replacement — linear and responsive, without weakening the safety argument that quorum intersection provides.

For the practitioner, the guidance is concrete: below a few dozen replicas, PBFT's simplicity and low phase count remain attractive; past a hundred replicas, or wherever leader failover cost dominates, HotStuff's linear design wins decisively [2]. For the theorist, the enduring lesson is FLP's: every consensus protocol is a negotiation with impossibility, and the terms of that negotiation — which assumption you relax, and what you pay for it — are what distinguish a correct protocol from a merely plausible one.
