# Tropical Geometry: Min-Plus Algebra, Tropical Curves, and Amoebas

## Abstract

Tropical geometry studies the piecewise-linear shadows cast by algebraic varieties when classical arithmetic is replaced by the min-plus (equivalently max-plus) semiring, where addition becomes taking a maximum and multiplication becomes ordinary addition. This essay develops the subject from its algebraic foundations through its three great pillars: the idempotent algebra of the tropical semifield, the combinatorics of tropical curves as balanced weighted graphs dual to subdivisions of Newton polytopes, and the analytic theory of amoebas whose tentacled shapes degenerate, in the Maslov dequantization limit, onto tropical varieties. We explain valuation-based tropicalization over Puiseux series, the balancing condition as a combinatorial residue of the fundamental theorem, the Ronkin function and the order map on amoeba complements, and Mikhalkin's correspondence theorem equating classical and tropical enumerative counts. Along the way we give concrete computations — tropical matrix multiplication as shortest paths, the tropical line, and the twelve rational cubics — and close with the subject's genuine limitations: realizability, superabundance, and the failure of naive functoriality.

## 1 Introduction

Classical algebraic geometry studies solution sets of polynomial equations over fields, objects of enormous subtlety whose invariants — genus, degree, intersection numbers — have occupied mathematicians for centuries. Tropical geometry proposes a radical simplification: replace the field by an *idempotent semiring* in which addition is idempotent, watch polynomials become piecewise-linear convex functions, and watch varieties become polyhedral complexes. The miracle is that this drastic simplification loses surprisingly little: degrees, genera, and even enumerative invariants survive the passage, now computable by combinatorics rather than by intersection theory on moduli spaces [1][4].

The algebraic heart of the subject is the **min-plus algebra**, the set of real numbers equipped with the operations

- **tropical addition:** $x \oplus y = \min(x, y)$,
- **tropical multiplication:** $x \odot y = x + y$,

with $+\infty$ as the additive identity. An equivalent and often more convenient convention is the **max-plus algebra** on $\mathbb{R} \cup \{-\infty\}$, where $x \oplus y = \max(x, y)$; the map $x \mapsto -x$ is an isomorphism between the two, so nothing mathematical depends on the choice. We will work primarily with the max-plus convention, which arises most naturally from Maslov dequantization, and translate freely between the two.

The name "tropical" is itself a small joke of mathematical history: coined by French researchers in honor of the Brazilian computer scientist Imre Simon, whose pioneering work in the 1970s and 1980s on min-plus algebra in automata theory laid the computational foundations of the field. The geometry came later. In the 1990s, Bergman studied the logarithmic limit sets of amoebas; Gelfand, Kapranov, and Zelevinsky connected discriminants to secondary polytopes; and around the turn of the millennium, Mikhalkin demonstrated that complex enumerative geometry — the counting of algebraic curves — could be performed tropically, inaugurating the modern subject [1][4]. The subsequent decade added the valuative foundations of Speyer and Sturmfels, the correspondence theorems of Nishinou–Siebert and Tyomkin [6], and the floor-diagram calculus of Brugallé and Mikhalkin.

This essay is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews the background: the tropical semifield, its history, and valued fields. Section 3 describes the methodology — dequantization, tropicalization, and Newton polytopes. Section 4 develops the deep theory: the semiring itself, the fundamental theorem, balancing, duality, and amoebas. Section 5 works through concrete examples and proofs. Section 6 is honest about limitations. Section 7 concludes.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The tropical semifield

A **semiring** is a ring-like structure in which additive inverses need not exist. The **tropical semifield** $\mathbb{T} = \mathbb{R} \cup \{-\infty\}$ with $x \oplus y = \max(x,y)$ and $x \odot y = x+y$ satisfies all the ring axioms except the existence of additive inverses, and it satisfies the extra **idempotency** law $x \oplus x = x$ [2][3]. The following table summarizes the dictionary:

| Classical | Tropical (max-plus) | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| $0$ (additive unit) | $-\infty$ | $\max(x, -\infty) = x$ |
| $1$ (multiplicative unit) | $0$ | $x + 0 = x$ |
| $a + b$ | $\max(a, b)$ | select the dominant term |
| $a \cdot b$ | $a + b$ | exponents add |
| $a^n$ | $n \cdot a$ | repeated addition |
| polynomial $\sum c_i x^i$ | $\max_i (c_i + i\,x)$ | piecewise-linear convex function |

### 2.2 A short history

The min-plus algebra first appeared as a tool, not a geometry. In the 1960s and 1970s it surfaced in **operations research**: the Bellman optimality equations of dynamic programming and the Floyd–Warshall all-pairs shortest-path algorithm are tropical linear algebra in disguise — tropical matrix multiplication $C = A \otimes B$ with $C_{ij} = \min_k (A_{ik} + B_{kj})$ computes shortest paths, and tropical powers of the adjacency matrix converge to the distance matrix. Imre Simon and the French school developed its role in automata theory, recognizing rational series over the min-plus semiring as the right language for certain optimization problems.

### 2.3 Valued fields and the tropicalization map

The cleanest foundation for tropicalization uses **non-Archimedean valued fields**. Let $K = \mathbb{C}\{\{t\}\}$ be the field of Puiseux series — formal series $\sum c_q t^q$ with rational exponents bounded below and sharing a denominator — equipped with the valuation $\mathrm{val}(\sum c_q t^q) = \min\{q : c_q \neq 0\}$ and $\mathrm{val}(0) = +\infty$ (with the max-plus convention we use $-\mathrm{val}$). The valuation is a homomorphism from the multiplicative group onto an ordered group: $\mathrm{val}(xy) = \mathrm{val}(x) + \mathrm{val}(y)$, and crucially $\mathrm{val}(x+y) \geq \min(\mathrm{val}(x),\mathrm{val}(y))$, with equality when the minimum is unique [8].

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 Maslov dequantization

The bridge from classical to tropical arithmetic is **Maslov dequantization**, a one-parameter family of semirings interpolating between the nonnegative reals and the tropical semifield. For $h > 0$, define on $\mathbb{R}$ the operations

$$a \oplus_h b = h\log(e^{a/h} + e^{b/h}), \qquad a \odot b = a + b.$$

### 3.2 Tropicalization via valuations

Methodologically, tropicalization is a three-step procedure:

1. **Lift** the problem to a non-Archimedean valued field $K$ (e.g., Puiseux series), replacing coefficients by series whose valuations encode the desired degeneration.
2. **Apply the coordinatewise valuation** $\mathrm{Val}: (K^*)^n \to \mathbb{R}^n$ to the variety, obtaining a polyhedral set.
3. **Read off invariants** — degree, genus, intersection numbers — from the combinatorics of that set, using the balancing condition and duality.

### 3.3 The Newton polytope method

For a polynomial $f = \sum_{\alpha \in A} c_\alpha x^\alpha$ with finite support $A \subset \mathbb{Z}^n$, the **Newton polytope** $\mathrm{New}(f) = \mathrm{conv}(A)$ records which monomials appear. Choose a **height function** $h: A \to \mathbb{R}$ (in the valuative picture, $h(\alpha) = \mathrm{val}(c_\alpha)$), lift each lattice point to $(\alpha, h(\alpha))$ in one dimension higher, take the convex hull, and project the lower faces back down. The result is a **regular (coherent) subdivision** of $\mathrm{New}(f)$. The tropical hypersurface of $\mathrm{trop}(f)$ is *dual* to this subdivision: cells of the subdivision correspond to cells of the tropical hypersurface with dimensions complementary, and the duality reverses inclusions [3][5]. This Newton-polytope method, pioneered by Gelfand–Kapranov–Zelevinsky in their theory of discriminants, is the workhorse that turns algebra into combinatorics.

### 3.4 Weights, directions, and balancing

A tropical curve in $\mathbb{R}^2$ is a finite weighted graph whose edges are line segments or rays with rational slopes, equipped with positive integer **weights** $w_e$ on each edge $e$. At every vertex $v$, let $u_e \in \mathbb{Z}^2$ be the primitive integer vector pointing away from $v$ along each incident edge. The **balancing condition** demands

$$\sum_{e \ni v} w_e\, u_e = 0 \qquad \text{at every vertex } v.$$

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The tropical semiring, formally

Let us be precise. A **semiring** $(S, \oplus, \odot)$ satisfies: $(S,\oplus)$ is a commutative monoid with identity $\mathbb{0}$; $(S,\odot)$ is a monoid with identity $\mathbb{1}$; multiplication distributes over addition; and $\mathbb{0}$ annihilates: $\mathbb{0} \odot x = \mathbb{0}$. The tropical semifield $\mathbb{T} = \mathbb{R} \cup \{-\infty\}$ with $\oplus = \max$ and $\odot = +$ is a semiring with $\mathbb{0} = -\infty$ and $\mathbb{1} = 0$, and every nonzero element has a multiplicative inverse (negation), hence "semifield". It is **idempotent** ($x \oplus x = x$) and **totally ordered**, and the order is compatible with both operations.

### 4.2 Tropical polynomials and the fundamental theorem

A **tropical polynomial** in $n$ variables is an expression $P(x) = \bigoplus_{\alpha \in A} c_\alpha \odot x^{\odot \alpha} = \max_{\alpha \in A}(c_\alpha + \alpha\cdot x)$ with finite support $A \subset \mathbb{N}^n$ and $c_\alpha \in \mathbb{T}$. As a function $\mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}$ it is convex and piecewise-linear. Its **tropical hypersurface** $V_{\mathrm{trop}}(P)$ is the set of points where the maximum is attained by at least two distinct terms — the non-differentiability locus.

> **Theorem:** (Fundamental theorem of tropical geometry). Let $K$ be an algebraically closed field with a nontrivial valuation, and let $I \subset K[x_1^{\pm 1},\dots,x_n^{\pm 1}]$ be an ideal. Then the following three sets coincide: (i) the coordinatewise valuation image $\mathrm{Val}(V(I))$; (ii) the intersection over all $f \in I$ of the tropical hypersurfaces $V_{\mathrm{trop}}(\mathrm{trop}(f))$; (iii) the polyhedral complex obtained from the Gröbner fan of $I$ by intersecting with the appropriate weight cone.

### 4.3 Balancing and the structure of tropical curves

The structure theorem states that the tropicalization of a $d$-dimensional variety is a **pure $d$-dimensional weighted balanced polyhedral complex**: every maximal cell carries a positive integer weight, and at every codimension-one face the weighted primitive normal vectors sum to zero — the higher-dimensional balancing condition [2][4].

### 4.4 Duality: subdivisions and the secondary fan

The duality between a plane tropical curve $C$ and the regular subdivision of its Newton polygon $\Delta$ is the combinatorial core of the subject [3][5]:

- **Vertices** of $C$ correspond to **2-dimensional cells** of the subdivision; the valency and local geometry of the vertex are read off from the polygon.
- **Edges** of $C$ correspond to **interior edges** of the subdivision, and the two are **perpendicular**; the weight of the tropical edge equals the **lattice length** (one plus the number of interior lattice points) of the dual segment.
- **Unbounded rays** of $C$ correspond to **boundary edges** of $\Delta$, perpendicular to them.
- **Connected components of the complement** of $C$ correspond to **lattice points** of $\Delta$ not used as subdivision vertices.

### 4.5 Amoebas, the Ronkin function, and the tropical limit

Return to analysis. For a Laurent polynomial $f$, the **amoeba** $\mathcal{A}(f) = \mathrm{Log}(V(f)) \subset \mathbb{R}^n$ is a closed blob with tentacles reaching to infinity in directions normal to the faces of $\mathrm{New}(f)$ — the **logarithmic limit set** of Bergman [1]. Its complement has finitely many convex connected components, and the theorem of Forsberg–Passare–Tsikh injects these components into the lattice points of the Newton polytope via the **order map**: for $x$ outside the amoeba, the gradient of the **Ronkin function**

$$R_f(x) = \frac{1}{(2\pi i)^n}\int_{\mathrm{Log}^{-1}(x)} \log|f(z)|\,\frac{dz_1}{z_1}\wedge\cdots\wedge\frac{dz_n}{z_n}$$

is an integer vector in $\mathrm{New}(f)$, constant on each component. The Ronkin function is convex, and it is affine precisely off the amoeba — so the amoeba is exactly the non-linearity locus of $R_f$.

## 5 Empirical/Proofs

### 5.1 Worked example: the tropical line

Consider the tropical polynomial $P(x,y) = x \oplus y \oplus 0 = \max(x, y, 0)$. The plane divides into three regions where a single term dominates, and the corner locus — where the maximum is attained at least twice — consists of three rays from the origin:

- the ray $\{(t,t) : t \geq 0\}$ (northeast), where $x = y \geq 0$;
- the ray $\{(-t,0) : t \geq 0\}$ (west), where $x = 0 \geq y$;
- the ray $\{(0,-t) : t \geq 0\}$ (south), where $y = 0 \geq x$.

Each edge has weight $1$, and the primitive direction vectors $(1,1)$, $(-1,0)$, $(0,-1)$ sum to zero: the balancing condition holds. The three unbounded directions each carry total weight $1$, so this is a tropical curve of degree $1$ — the tropicalization of an ordinary line. Its dual subdivision is the standard triangulation of the triangle with vertices $(0,0)$, $(1,0)$, $(0,1)$: three unit triangles meeting at the interior lattice point, dual to the single trivalent vertex [2][3].

### 5.2 The twelve rational cubics

The first nontrivial enumerative triumph is the count of rational plane cubics through $8$ generic points: classically $N_{0,3} = 12$, by Kontsevich's formula. Mikhalkin's correspondence theorem [4] re-derives this tropically, and the computation is small enough to do by hand via **floor diagrams** — decorated graphs encoding tropical curves through vertically stretched point configurations. For degree $3$ there are exactly three unmarked floor diagrams; with markings and multiplicities (each diagram weighted by the product of squares of its edge weights) they contribute $5 + 3 + 4 = 12$. One diagram carries a weight-$2$ edge, contributing multiplicity $4$ — the tropical shadow of a classical curve with a node of higher contact order.

> **Theorem:** (Mikhalkin's correspondence theorem). Let $N_{g,d}^{\mathbb{C}}$ be the number of complex curves of genus $g$ and degree $d$ through $3d-1+g$ generic points in $\mathbb{CP}^2$, and $N_{g,d}^{\mathbb{T}}$ the number of tropical curves of the same discrete data through a generic configuration in $\mathbb{TP}^2$, counted with vertex multiplicities. Then $N_{g,d}^{\mathbb{C}} = N_{g,d}^{\mathbb{T}}$.

The theorem extends to other toric surfaces and, via Tyomkin's stacky tropical limits, to large positive characteristic [6]; refined real counts (Welschinger invariants) admit parallel tropical formulas [7]. The table below shows the classical rational-curve numbers, each now computable by tropical combinatorics:

| Degree $d$ | Points $3d-1$ | $N_{0,d}$ (rational curves) |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 2 | 1 |
| 2 | 5 | 1 |
| 3 | 8 | 12 |
| 4 | 11 | 620 |
| 5 | 14 | 87304 |

### 5.3 Proof sketch: why balancing holds

Let $C$ be a curve over the Puiseux series field $K$, and let $v$ be a vertex of its tropicalization $\Gamma = \mathrm{Val}(C)$. Near $v$, the branches of $C$ approaching $v$ correspond to edges of $\Gamma$; along the $e$-th branch, the valuations of the coordinate functions grow linearly with slope given by the primitive direction $u_e$, and the ramification contributes the weight $w_e$. The sum $\sum_e w_e u_e$ is the vector of total orders of zeros minus poles of the coordinate functions restricted to the part of $C$ tropicalizing near $v$ — and a meromorphic function on a complete curve has total order zero. Hence the sum vanishes: balancing is the residue theorem, tropicalized [2][4].

### 5.4 Computation I: tropical matrices are shortest paths

The following Python program implements min-plus matrix multiplication and shows that tropical powers compute all-pairs shortest paths — the original "killer app" of min-plus algebra:

```python
INF = float("inf")

def tadd(a, b):
    """Tropical addition: min (min-plus convention)."""
    return min(a, b)

def tmul(a, b):
    """Tropical multiplication: ordinary addition."""
    return a + b

def tmatmul(A, B):
    """Min-plus matrix product: C[i][j] = min_k A[i][k] + B[k][j]."""
    n = len(A)
    C = [[INF] * n for _ in range(n)]
    for i in range(n):
        for j in range(n):
            best = INF
            for k in range(n):
                cand = tmul(A[i][k], B[k][j])
                best = tadd(best, cand)
            C[i][j] = best
    return C

def tropical_power(A, m):
    """m-th tropical power: shortest paths using at most m edges."""
    n = len(A)
    # tropical identity: 0 on diagonal, inf elsewhere
    R = [[0 if i == j else INF for j in range(n)] for i in range(n)]
    P = [row[:] for row in A]
    e = m
    while e:  # repeated squaring, tropically
        if e & 1:
            R = tmatmul(R, P)
        P = tmatmul(P, P)
        e >>= 1
    return R

# Graph: 0 -> 1 (weight 3), 1 -> 2 (weight 4), 0 -> 2 (weight 10)
W = [[0, 3, 10],
     [INF, 0, 4],
     [INF, INF, 0]]
D = tropical_power(W, 4)
print("Shortest 0 -> 2:", D[0][2])  # 7 = 3 + 4, the tropical product found it
```

Running it prints `Shortest 0 -> 2: 7`: the tropical square of the weight matrix discovers the two-hop path $0 \to 1 \to 2$ of total weight $7$, beating the direct edge of weight $10$. Every dynamic-programming shortest-path algorithm is a tropical linear-algebra computation.

### 5.5 Computation II: the tropical semiring in Haskell

The idempotent algebra is cleanly expressed functionally. Below, tropical numbers are just doubles, and a tropical polynomial is a list of (coefficient, exponent) terms evaluated by taking the maximum:

```haskell
-- Max-plus tropical arithmetic
newtype Trop = Trop Double deriving (Eq, Show)

tzero :: Trop            -- additive identity
tzero = Trop (-1/0)

tone :: Trop             -- multiplicative identity
tone = Trop 0

tadd :: Trop -> Trop -> Trop
tadd (Trop x) (Trop y) = Trop (max x y)

tmul :: Trop -> Trop -> Trop
tmul (Trop x) (Trop y) = Trop (x + y)

tpow :: Trop -> Int -> Trop
tpow (Trop x) n = Trop (fromIntegral n * x)

-- A tropical polynomial: list of (coefficient, exponent) terms.
evalTrop :: [(Trop, Int)] -> Trop -> Trop
evalTrop terms (Trop x) =
    foldr tadd tzero [ tmul c (tpow (Trop x) e) | (c, e) <- terms ]

-- Example: P(x) = max(2 + 3x, 0 + x, -1). Its corner locus (the "roots")
-- sits where two terms tie for the maximum: at x = -1 and x = 0.5.
example :: Trop -> Trop
example = evalTrop [(Trop 2, 3), (Trop 0, 1), (Trop (-1), 0)]
```

The Frobenius identity $(x \oplus y)^{\odot n} = x^{\odot n} \oplus y^{\odot n}$ is a one-line consequence of `tadd` distributing over `tmul` and idempotency — cross terms are absorbed by the maximum, exactly as in the proof of the binomial theorem's tropical collapse.

## 6 Limitations

Tropical geometry is powerful but not omnipotent, and its boundaries are instructive.

1. **Realizability and superabundance.** Not every balanced weighted graph lifts to an algebraic curve. In genus $1$, Speyer's *well-spacedness* criterion characterizes the realizable tropical elliptic curves; in higher genus, *superabundant* curves — those whose deformation space is larger than expected — may fail to lift, and no complete combinatorial criterion is known. The tropical moduli space and the skeleton of the Berkovich analytification agree only after careful compactification.
2. **Genericity hypotheses.** The correspondence theorem counts curves through *generic* point configurations with toric transversality. Special configurations, non-toric ambient varieties, and positive characteristic all require extra machinery (Tyomkin's results cover large characteristic, but small characteristic remains delicate) [6].
3. **Intersection theory is subtle.** Set-theoretic intersections of tropical varieties can have the wrong dimension; the correct product is the *stable intersection*, defined via perturbations. Naive tropical Bézout arguments fail without it.
4. **Dependence on embedding.** Tropicalization is not intrinsic: it depends on the chosen embedding of the variety into a torus (equivalently, on the choice of coordinates). Different embeddings give different — though related — tropical varieties.
5. **Computational complexity.** Enumerating regular subdivisions means enumerating the cones of the secondary fan, whose size grows explosively; practical Gröbner-fan computations stall on modest examples despite the combinatorial setting.
6. **Amoeba approximation is not uniform.** The convergence of rescaled amoebas to the tropical limit is well behaved for curves but subtler in higher dimensions, where the spine construction of Passare–Rullgård must be invoked with care [1].

These are active research frontiers, not defects: each limitation marks a place where the dictionary between the classical and tropical worlds is still being written.

## 7 Conclusion

Tropical geometry replaces fields with idempotent semirings and thereby trades algebraic subtlety for combinatorial transparency — and the trade is astonishingly favorable. From the min-plus algebra of shortest paths, through Maslov dequantization, to the balancing condition on tropical curves, the duality with Newton subdivisions, and the tentacled amoebas collapsing onto their tropical skeletons, a single principle recurs: *the dominant term wins, and geometry lives where dominance is contested*. Mikhalkin's correspondence theorem shows this is not merely philosophy but a working enumerative machine, turning counts of algebraic curves into weighted counts of graphs [4][6][7].

The subject's deepest lesson may be methodological. Tropicalization is a *degeneration technique*: by pushing a one-parameter family to its limit, intricate global invariants become local and combinatorial. The same spirit animates toric degenerations, Gröbner degenerations, and Berkovich skeletons. Tropical geometry is the most elementary — and therefore the most widely applicable — member of this family, a piecewise-linear lens through which the whole of algebraic geometry can be refracted.
