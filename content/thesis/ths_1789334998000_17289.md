# Abstract Interpretation: Galois Connections, Widening Operators, and the Static Analysis of Programs at Scale

## Abstract

Abstract interpretation provides the mathematical foundation for sound static program analysis by replacing undecidable concrete semantics with computable approximations over ordered structures. This thesis presents the Cousot–Cousot framework: concrete properties modeled as complete lattices, program semantics as monotone fixpoint equations, and abstraction formalized through Galois connections that guarantee each concrete property possesses a best abstract approximation. We develop widening and narrowing operators that force convergence of abstract iterates on infinite-height domains, turning infinitary analyses terminating without sacrificing soundness. A comparative study of numeric domains — intervals, congruences, octagons, and convex polyhedra — quantifies the precision-cost spectrum from linear-time non-relational reasoning to exponential relational inference. We then examine industrial practice through the Astrée analyzer, which proved the absence of runtime errors in large safety-critical embedded software without false alarms within hours of computation, demonstrating that principled abstraction scales to millions of lines of production code. The thesis concludes with open challenges in relationality, concurrency, and completeness.

## 1 Intro

Every serious attempt at program verification collides with a classical impossibility: by Rice's theorem, all nontrivial semantic properties of programs are undecidable. No algorithm can decide, for an arbitrary program, whether it is free of runtime errors, whether it terminates, or whether two variables are ever equal. The engineering response is not surrender but *approximation*: compute, instead of the exact property, a sound over-approximation that is guaranteed to cover every possible execution. If the approximation contains no bad state, the program is safe; if it does, we obtain a possibly spurious alarm rather than a missed bug.

The mathematical theory that made this compromise rigorous was introduced by Patrick and Radhia Cousot in 1977 [1]. Their "unified lattice model" recast program analysis as the construction or approximation of fixpoints over ordered structures, showing that seemingly unrelated techniques — type inference, dataflow analysis, symbolic execution — are instances of one scheme. The framework's two pillars are **Galois connections**, which formalize what it means for one domain of properties to abstract another, and **widening and narrowing operators**, which force convergence of iterative analyses on infinite domains where naive iteration would diverge [3].

This thesis reconstructs the theory from first principles and follows it to industrial scale. Section 2 reviews the lattice-theoretic background and the Galois connection machinery. Section 3 describes the methodology of building an abstract interpreter, including a worked interval analysis. Section 4 develops four deep topics: the calculus of best abstractions, widening and narrowing and their duals, the landscape of numeric domains, and the engineering of multi-domain industrial analyzers. Section 5 gives proof sketches of the central soundness theorems and surveys empirical evidence, culminating in the Astrée analyzer's zero-false-alarm verification of safety-critical embedded software [2]. Section 6 surveys limitations, and Section 7 concludes.

## 2 Background

**Ordered structures.** Program properties are modeled as elements of a *complete lattice*: a partially ordered set in which every subset has a least upper bound (join, ⊔) and a greatest lower bound (meet, ⊓), with least element ⊥ and greatest element ⊤. The canonical example is the *collecting semantics*: the powerset lattice of program states, ordered by inclusion, where a property is a set of states that may arise during execution.

**Semantics as fixpoints.** The semantics of a program can be presented as the least fixpoint of a monotone operator F on such a lattice. For reachability, F(X) adds to X all states reachable in one more step from states in X; the least fixpoint lfp F collects precisely the reachable states. Tarski's theorem guarantees that monotone maps on complete lattices have least and greatest fixpoints, and Kleene's theorem gives a constructive route: the ascending chain ⊥, F(⊥), F(F(⊥)), … converges to lfp F whenever F is continuous.

**Galois connections.** Let (C, ⊆) be a concrete lattice of properties and (A, ⊑) an abstract lattice. A *Galois connection* is a pair of monotone maps α : C → A (abstraction) and γ : A → C (concretization) satisfying the adjoint law:

> Theorem: (Adjoint characterization). For monotone α and γ, the following are equivalent: (i) α(c) ⊑ a if and only if c ⊆ γ(a) for all c and a; (ii) c ⊆ γ(α(c)) and α(γ(a)) ⊑ a for all c and a; (iii) α preserves all existing joins and γ preserves all existing meets.

The left inequality c ⊆ γ(α(c)) says abstraction is *sound*: no concrete property is lost when we abstract and concretize. The composition γ ∘ α is an *upper closure operator* — extensive, monotone, and idempotent — and its fixpoints form a *Moore family*, the precise image of the abstraction [1].

**Abstract transfer functions and soundness.** Each concrete operation f : C → C is replaced by an abstract counterpart f# : A → A. Soundness is the pointwise condition α(f(c)) ⊑ f#(α(c)): the abstract operation must over-approximate the abstraction of the concrete result. When the abstract domain is obtained by a Galois connection, the *best* abstract transformer f# = α ∘ f ∘ γ exists and is optimal [3].

**The convergence problem.** Kleene iteration on the abstract side need not terminate: the interval lattice has infinite ascending chains, since bounds can grow one step at a time forever. This is where widening enters.

## 3 Methodology

Building an abstract interpreter is a disciplined five-step engineering process:

1. **Fix the concrete semantics.** Define the property of interest as a fixpoint over a concrete lattice, usually the collecting semantics.
2. **Choose the abstract domain.** Select (A, ⊑) balancing precision against cost: intervals for cheap range reasoning, octagons for light relationality, polyhedra for strong relationality.
3. **Establish the abstraction.** Ideally a Galois connection (α, γ); where none exists, a concretization-only framework with widening suffices [3].
4. **Design abstract transfer functions.** Over-approximate each concrete operation; prove the soundness inequality.
5. **Compute the abstract fixpoint.** Iterate with widening to guarantee termination, then refine with narrowing.

Consider interval analysis of a simple loop. The classic widening with thresholds jumps an unstable bound to the nearest threshold rather than to infinity, preserving useful precision:

```python
INF = float("inf")

class Interval:
    """A non-relational numeric abstraction: one range per variable."""
    def __init__(self, lo, hi):
        self.lo, self.hi = lo, hi

    def join(self, other):
        return Interval(min(self.lo, other.lo), max(self.hi, other.hi))

    def widen(self, other, t_lo=-1000.0, t_hi=1000.0):
        lo = self.lo if other.lo >= self.lo else t_lo
        hi = self.hi if other.hi <= self.hi else t_hi
        return Interval(lo, hi)

    def narrow(self, other):
        # narrowing refines a widened bound when the iterate shrinks
        lo = other.lo if self.lo == -INF else self.lo
        hi = other.hi if self.hi == INF else self.hi
        return Interval(lo, hi)

def analyze_loop():
    x = Interval(0, 0)          # x = 0 before the loop
    prev = Interval(0, 0)
    for step in range(50):      # widening guarantees early stabilization
        body = Interval(x.lo, x.hi + 1)   # abstract effect of x = x + 1
        nxt = prev.join(body)
        if nxt.lo == prev.lo and nxt.hi == prev.hi:
            return prev.narrow(nxt)
        prev = prev.widen(nxt) if step > 2 else nxt
    return prev
```

The Galois connection laws themselves can be stated as a small executable specification, here in Haskell:

```haskell
-- Adjoint laws of a Galois connection between concrete (c) and abstract (a)
class (Ord c, Ord a) => Galois c a where
  alpha :: c -> a   -- abstraction: forget detail
  gamma :: a -> c   -- concretization: recover meaning

-- The adjoint property: alpha c <= a  iff  c <= gamma a
adjointHolds :: (Galois c a) => c -> a -> Bool
adjointHolds c a = (alpha c <= a) == (c <= gamma a)

-- Soundness of an abstract transformer fsharp for a concrete f:
-- abstracting the concrete result stays below the abstract result.
soundTransformer :: (Galois c a) => (c -> c) -> (a -> a) -> c -> Bool
soundTransformer f fsharp c = alpha (f c) <= fsharp (alpha c)
```

This methodology — semantics, domain, abstraction, transformers, widened fixpoint — is uniform across analyses as different as sign analysis, pointer analysis, and cache-behavior prediction [1].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Galois Connections and the Calculus of Best Abstractions

The Galois connection is the gold standard of abstraction because it confers *optimality*: given a concrete operation f and a Galois connection (α, γ), the abstract transformer α ∘ f ∘ γ is the most precise sound abstraction of f expressible in the abstract domain [3]. No search or tuning is needed; precision is inherited from the mathematics.

Several equivalent presentations exist — closure operators, Moore families, complete join-congruences, families of principal ideals — and each illuminates a different facet. The closure-operator view γ ∘ α shows that an abstraction is characterized by which concrete properties it can express *exactly* (its fixpoints). The Moore-family view shows how abstractions compose: intersecting Moore families yields the *reduced product*, the most precise abstraction combining several domains without redundant representation.

A subtle point is that best abstractions need not exist when no Galois connection is available. Cousot and Cousot's 1992 comparison shows this is no obstacle: any abstraction achievable with Galois connections can be reformulated with widenings alone, and the widening calculus is strictly more general [3]. In practice, modern analyzers freely mix both styles.

### 4.2 Widening and Narrowing: Forcing Convergence

A *widening* ▽ : A × A → A satisfies two laws: (i) it is an upper bound, x ⊑ x ▽ y and y ⊑ x ▽ y (soundness); (ii) for every sequence (x_n), the widened sequence y_0 = x_0, y_{n+1} = y_n ▽ x_{n+1} is ultimately stationary (termination). Soundness and termination are *independent* properties — a frequent source of confusion [3].

The upward iteration with widening is defined by X_0 = ⊥ and X_{n+1} = X_n ▽ F(X_n); its limit is a sound over-approximation of the least fixpoint. Two engineering refinements matter enormously:

- **Widening with thresholds.** Instead of jumping an unstable bound to infinity, jump to the next value in a finite, syntactically chosen set (constants appearing in the program). Precision improves dramatically at negligible cost.
- **Delayed widening.** Apply plain joins for the first k iterations so the analysis discovers the loop's natural rhythm before extrapolating.

A *narrowing* △ then recovers precision: starting from the widened post-fixpoint, decreasing iterates X_{n+1} = X_n △ F(X_n) refine bounds while remaining sound. The dual operators — *dual widening* and *dual narrowing* — serve the symmetric problem of under-approximating greatest fixpoints, which arises in backward analyses and in Craig interpolation, itself a form of dual narrowing that does *not* enforce convergence [3].

> Theorem: (Widened fixpoint soundness). Let F# be a sound abstraction of the monotone concrete operator F, and let ▽ be a widening on the abstract lattice. Then the upward widened iteration stabilizes in finitely many steps at a post-fixpoint X with lfp F ⊆ γ(X).

Notably, on non-Noetherian domains such as polyhedra, no widening is *best*: every terminating widening can be strictly improved infinitely often. Precision is therefore an open-ended engineering frontier, not a closed mathematical question [3].

### 4.3 Numeric Abstract Domains: Intervals, Octagons, Polyhedra

Numeric domains track the values program variables may take. Their design space trades relationality against cost:

| Domain | Invariant shape | Memory | Operator cost | Precision |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Intervals | per-variable ranges | O(n) | O(n) | non-relational |
| Congruences | x ≡ c (mod m) | O(n) | near-linear | orthogonal |
| Octagons | ±x ± y ≤ c | O(n²) | O(n³) | weakly relational |
| Convex polyhedra | arbitrary linear inequalities | exponential (worst case) | exponential | fully relational |

**Intervals** are the workhorse: cheap, and surprisingly effective when combined with widening with thresholds. **Congruences**, introduced by Granger, track arithmetic progressions of values; they discovered alignment properties enabling automatic vectorization and remain essential for reasoning about strides and low-level bit manipulations [6].

**Octagons** (Miné) capture constraints of the form ±x ± y ≤ c — enough to express relations such as x + y bounded above or x − y bounded below — using difference-bound matrices with O(n²) memory and O(n³) closure via Floyd–Warshall. On Miné's random-walk benchmark, octagonal analysis automatically discovered the invariants proving that no out-of-bounds array access occurs, a property invisible to intervals [5].

**Convex polyhedra** (Cousot and Halbwachs) infer arbitrary linear relations among variables — the first implemented static analyzer in the abstract-interpretation tradition, and famously precise because it discovers affine relations that never appear syntactically [4]. The price is the double-description method, exponential in the worst case, which restricts polyhedra to modest variable counts or to *packing* strategies that apply them to small variable clusters.

### 4.4 Combining Domains and Scaling to Industry

No single domain suffices for real software. The *reduced product* combines domains so that each refines the others: intervals bound what octagons relate, congruences capture strides that ranges miss, and specialized domains handle pointers, floating-point rounding, and digital filters. Astrée's breakthrough was to instantiate this philosophy with dozens of numeric and symbolic domains, each tuned to constructs common in synchronous control-command code (rate limiters, filters, boolean control logic), coordinated through a shared, widened fixpoint iteration [2][7].

Floating-point arithmetic deserves special mention: sound analysis must model IEEE rounding, and Astrée's float domains over-approximate rounding error rather than pretending floats are reals. Memory is handled by a *memory domain* that maps the byte-level C memory model onto the numeric domains above it. The result is an analyzer that is *sound* (covers the whole state space, never omits a potential error), *automatic* (no user annotations), and *infinitary* (all abstractions use infinite domains with widening/narrowing) — a combination that distinguishes abstract-interpretation analyzers from bounded model checkers and testing tools [2].

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

**Proof sketch: soundness of the abstract fixpoint.** We prove that the abstract iteration over-approximates the concrete least fixpoint. Assume a Galois connection (α, γ) between the concrete lattice C and abstract lattice A, a monotone concrete operator F, and a sound abstract operator F# with α ∘ F ⊑ F# ∘ α. By induction on Kleene iterates: α(F^n(⊥_C)) ⊑ (F#)^n(⊥_A), using soundness at each step and monotonicity of α. If the abstract Kleene sequence stabilizes at X with F#(X) ⊑ X, then lfp F = ⊔_n F^n(⊥_C) satisfies α(lfp F) ⊑ X, i.e. lfp F ⊆ γ(X) [1]. With widening, the same argument applies to the widened sequence, whose limit is likewise a post-fixpoint above every Kleene iterate [3].

**Completeness is unattainable in general.** Soundness is a one-way guarantee; the converse fails, and false alarms are the price. The theory quantifies exactly when completeness holds for restricted classes, but for Turing-complete languages no sound and complete terminating analysis exists.

**Empirical evidence at scale.** The decisive experiment is Astrée. Applied to large embedded control-command safety-critical real-time software generated from synchronous specifications, it produced a *correctness proof* — the absence of runtime errors — without any false alarm, in a few hours of computation [2]. This was not a toy: the analyzed codebases were industrial flight-control and automotive systems, and the analyzer has since scaled to over ten million lines of C/C++ code [8], a scale at which manual proof is inconceivable. The PLDI 2003 account of the same project documents the design decisions that made a static analyzer for large safety-critical software practical [7]. The enabling factors were exactly the theory of Sections 2–4: Galois-connection-based domain design, widening/narrowing for termination, and the reduced product of many specialized domains.

On the domain level, Miné's octagon experiments showed that weakly relational invariants are inferable in cubic time on realistic programs, closing much of the precision gap to polyhedra at a fraction of the cost [5]. Granger's congruence analysis demonstrated end-to-end value in compilers, where discovered alignment facts enabled vectorization that range analysis alone could not justify [6].

**A unifying empirical-theoretical result.** Cousot and Cousot's comparison established that the widening approach subsumes the Galois-connection approach in expressive power while remaining strictly more flexible — every Galois abstraction can be replayed as a widening calculus, but widenings exist where no Galois connection does [3]. This justifies the modern practice of designing domains by concretization and widening first, and recovering Galois structure only where it is cheap.

## 6 Limitations

- **Incompleteness and false alarms.** Soundness guarantees no missed bugs; it says nothing about spurious alarms. Widening, in particular, sacrifices precision for termination, and on some programs the widened invariant is too coarse to discharge the property. Threshold widening and narrowing mitigate but do not eliminate this.
- **The cost of relationality.** Polyhedral analysis is exponential in the worst case; octagons are cubic; both degrade as variable counts grow. Industrial analyzers therefore use *packing* (relational reasoning on small variable clusters) and trace partitioning rather than global relationality [2][7].
- **Non-numeric and heap reasoning.** The numeric domains above say nothing about pointers, aliasing, or dynamic allocation. Separate shape and points-to domains exist, but their combination with numeric reasoning remains less mature than pure numeric analysis.
- **Concurrency and weak memory.** Sound interleaving semantics explode combinatorially; Astrée handles scheduled real-time concurrency with a dedicated thread-modular abstraction, but racy, weakly-ordered concurrent code remains at the frontier [2].
- **Path sensitivity.** Classical abstract interpretation merges information at control-flow joins, losing correlations between branches. Disjunctive completions and trace partitioning recover some of it at significant cost.
- **Specification burden.** The analyzer proves *absence of runtime errors* — a fixed, universal specification. Functional correctness still requires user-supplied contracts, which abstract interpretation can check but not invent.

## 7 Conclusion

Abstract interpretation turned program analysis from a bag of tricks into a science: fixpoints give the semantics, Galois connections give the notion of a *best* abstraction, and widening/narrowing give terminating algorithms on infinite domains [1][3]. The numeric-domain hierarchy — intervals, congruences, octagons, polyhedra — maps out a precision-cost spectrum that lets engineers buy exactly as much relationality as they can afford [4][5][6]. And Astrée demonstrated that the theory survives contact with industry: sound, automatic, zero-false-alarm verification of safety-critical embedded software in hours [2][7].

The open challenges are now clear. We need relational domains that scale beyond packing heuristics, principled abstractions for weak-memory concurrency, and tighter integration with complementary techniques — model checking, SMT solving, and counterexample-guided refinement — each of which can itself be understood as an abstract interpretation [3]. Half a century after the unified lattice model, the research program it launched is still expanding: wherever a program's behavior must be understood without running it, abstraction, soundly done, remains the way.
