# Epidemic Modeling: Compartmental Dynamics, R0 Estimation, and Stochastic Extinction Thresholds

## Abstract

Compartmental epidemic models reduce the bewildering complexity of disease transmission to interacting population strata whose aggregate flows are governed by low-dimensional dynamical systems. This article develops the full pipeline of modern epidemic modeling: the deterministic Kermack–McKendrick SIR framework and its threshold structure [1][3], the next-generation operator machinery that defines the basic reproduction number R0 as a spectral radius for heterogeneous populations [2][9], effective reproduction numbers and herd-immunity thresholds derived from them [5][8], and the stochastic theory of invasion in which a branching-process fixed point determines the probability that an outbreak with R0 > 1 nevertheless goes extinct [6][7]. We show that individual-level heterogeneity in infectiousness — formalized through overdispersed, negative-binomial offspring distributions — raises extinction probabilities dramatically while making successful invasions rarer and more explosive [4]. The COVID-19 pandemic is used as a running case study of how these quantities were estimated, misinterpreted, and revised in real time [8].

## 1. Introduction

Few mathematical constructs have migrated from technical journals into global public discourse as rapidly as the basic reproduction number, R0. During the COVID-19 pandemic, politicians, journalists, and modelers alike invoked "keeping R below one" as the operational definition of epidemic control [8]. Yet R0 is neither a biological constant of a pathogen nor a directly measurable quantity: it is a *model-derived threshold parameter* whose value depends on the contact structure, the demographic state of the population, and the assumptions of the dynamical system used to compute it [2][5].

The purpose of this article is to reconstruct, from first principles, the mathematical edifice that justifies R0 and its relatives. We begin with the deterministic compartmental tradition inaugurated by Kermack and McKendrick in 1927 [1], derive the threshold theorems that Hethcote systematized for the classic SIR and endemic models [3], and then confront the question that Diekmann, Heesterbeek, and Metz answered in 1990: how should R0 be *defined* when the population is heterogeneous, structured by age, space, or risk [2]? Their answer — the spectral radius of the next-generation operator — remains the canonical definition.

Determinism, however, only describes epidemics that have already taken off. The first infections are a small-number stochastic phenomenon, and many introductions of transmissible pathogens fail. Branching-process theory replaces the certainty of R0 > 1 with a probability of emergence, and overdispersion in individual infectiousness — the phenomenon Lloyd-Smith and colleagues named *superspreading* — reshapes that probability in ways the deterministic theory cannot see [4][6][7]. The article concludes with the hard-won empirical lessons of COVID-19, where the gap between a model's R0 and the number that mattered for policy became a central scientific controversy [8].

## 2. Background

### 2.1 The Kermack–McKendrick SIR system

In 1927, Kermack and McKendrick proposed partitioning a closed population into three compartments — *susceptible* (S), *infectious* (I), and *removed* (R) — and writing mass-action flows between them [1]. Under frequency-dependent (proportionate-mixing) transmission, the celebrated system is

$$\frac{dS}{dt} = -\beta \frac{SI}{N}, \qquad \frac{dI}{dt} = \beta \frac{SI}{N} - \gamma I, \qquad \frac{dR}{dt} = \gamma I,$$

where β is the effective contact rate, γ is the recovery rate (so 1/γ is the mean infectious period), and N = S + I + R is constant. The model's power lies in what it *omits*: no age structure, no spatial structure, no individual heterogeneity — yet it predicts the qualitative signature of real epidemics, a single-peaked incidence curve that depletes susceptibles without infecting everyone [3].

> Theorem: *(Threshold.)* For the SIR system above, dI/dt > 0 at t = 0 if and only if R0·S(0)/N > 1, where R0 = β/γ. The disease-free equilibrium (S, I, R) = (N, 0, 0) is locally asymptotically stable if R0 < 1 and unstable if R0 > 1 [1][3].

The threshold quantity R0 = β/γ admits a crisp biological reading: the *expected number of secondary infections produced by a single infectious individual introduced into a fully susceptible population*. This is the *epidemiological* definition; its *mathematical* definition will occupy §4.1 [2].

### 2.2 Final size and the attack ratio

A second classical result constrains how far any closed-population epidemic can go. Let s(∞) = S(∞)/N be the final susceptible fraction. Then the *final-size equation* holds:

$$R_0 = \frac{\ln s(\infty)}{s(\infty) - 1},$$

a relation first recognized by Kermack and McKendrick themselves and later exploited as an estimation method: given serological data on the final attack ratio 1 − s(∞), one can invert the equation to estimate R0 [1][3]. For R0 = 2.5, the final size is approximately 89% — a sobering quantification of what "letting it rip" costs.

### 2.3 Endemic equilibria and the replacement number

When births, deaths, or waning immunity replenish susceptibles, the SIR system acquires an *endemic equilibrium* with susceptible fraction s_e = 1/R0 [3]. Hethcote's review distinguishes three threshold quantities — the basic reproduction number R0, the contact number σ, and the *replacement number* R = R0·s(t) — and shows that at endemic equilibrium the replacement number equals unity: each infection replaces itself exactly [3]. This distinction between the *basic* (fully susceptible population) and *effective* (current population) reproduction numbers became one of the most consequential — and most confused — ideas of the COVID-19 era [5][8].

## 3. Methodology

Our exposition proceeds through four methodological layers:

1. **Deterministic compartmental analysis.** We derive threshold conditions from the Jacobian at the disease-free equilibrium (DFE) and compute R0 via the next-generation matrix for an SEIR model, following Diekmann, Heesterbeek, and Metz [2] and the constructive recipe of Diekmann, Heesterbeek, and Roberts [9].
2. **R0 estimation from data.** We present the three classical estimation routes — exponential-growth-rate inversion, final-size inversion, and the renewal equation relating the incidence curve to the generation-interval distribution — and note how each bakes in different assumptions about the infectious-period distribution [2][8].
3. **Stochastic formulation.** The early phase of invasion is recast as a continuous-time Markov chain (CTMC) with transition rates (S, I) → (S−1, I+1) at rate βSI/N and (S, I) → (S, I−1) at rate γI. While susceptibles remain undepleted, the infective process is approximated by a branching process, whose extinction probability is computed from the offspring probability-generating function [6][7].
4. **Overdispersion analysis.** Homogeneous (Poisson) offspring are replaced by negative-binomial offspring with dispersion parameter k, fitted in the Lloyd-Smith framework to contact-tracing data, to quantify how superspreading alters extinction probabilities [4].

All numerical illustrations use the Python snippets in §5; the branching-process fixed point is implemented independently in Haskell to guard against implementation error.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 The next-generation operator and R0 as a spectral radius

For structured models, "β/γ" is meaningless: which β, which γ? Diekmann, Heesterbeek, and Metz resolved this by decomposing the linearized infected subsystem at the DFE into two operators [2]:

- **F**, the *transmission* operator: rates at which new infections appear in each infected compartment;
- **V**, the *transition* operator: rates of transfer between, and out of, infected compartments (progression, recovery, death).

The *next-generation matrix* is K = FV⁻¹, and the basic reproduction number is defined as

$$R_0 = \rho(K),$$

the spectral radius (dominant eigenvalue) of K [2][9]. The (i, j) entry of K is the expected number of new infections in compartment i produced by an individual that entered infected compartment j. Because K is non-negative, the Perron–Frobenius theorem guarantees that ρ(K) is a real, simple eigenvalue with a non-negative eigenvector — the *stable type distribution* of infections across generations.

Consider the SEIR model (E = exposed/latent):

$$\begin{aligned}
\dot S &= -\beta SI/N, \quad \dot E = \beta SI/N - \sigma E, \
\dot I &= \sigma E - \gamma I, \quad \dot R = \gamma I.
\end{aligned}$$

With infected compartments (E, I) ordered first, the recipe of van den Driessche and Watmough gives

$$F = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & \beta \ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad V = \begin{pmatrix} \sigma & 0 \ -\sigma & \gamma \end{pmatrix}, \qquad K = FV^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix} \beta/\gamma & \beta/\gamma \ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix},$$

so R0 = β/γ — the latent period drops out of the reproduction number entirely, though it slows the *growth rate* [2][9]. The threshold property survives in full generality: the DFE is locally asymptotically stable iff R0 < 1, and unstable iff R0 > 1, provided the five standard regularity conditions on the decomposition hold [9].

### 4.2 Effective reproduction numbers and herd immunity

Once the epidemic depletes susceptibles — or vaccination removes them — the relevant quantity is the *effective* reproduction number

$$R_t = R_0 \cdot \frac{S(t)}{N},$$

the expected secondary infections per case *given current population immunity* [5]. The epidemic turns over precisely when R_t crosses unity, which occurs at the *herd-immunity threshold*

$$p_c = 1 - \frac{1}{R_0}$$

of the population immune. For measles (R0 ≈ 12–18) this demands 92–95% immunity; for ancestral SARS-CoV-2 (R0 ≈ 2–3) it suggested 50–67% — a number widely quoted, then widely misunderstood, because it assumes *homogeneous mixing and random vaccination*, neither of which held [5][8]. Delamater and colleagues catalogued the resulting misconceptions: R0 is not a rate (it is dimensionless, "cases per case"); it is not constant in space or time; and comparing R0 values computed by different methods is comparing different mathematical objects [5].

| Disease | Representative R0 range | Herd-immunity threshold (1 − 1/R0) |
|---|---|---|
| Measles | 12–18 | 92–95% |
| Pertussis | 12–17 | 92–94% |
| Polio | 5–7 | 80–86% |
| Smallpox | ~3.5–6 | ~70–83% |
| SARS-CoV (2003) | ~2–5 | ~50–80% |
| SARS-CoV-2 (ancestral) | 2–3 | 50–67% |
| Ebola virus disease | ~1.5–2.5 | ~33–60% |
| Seasonal influenza | ~1.3 | ~23% |

*Representative literature ranges; values vary by setting, method, and variant [5][8].*

### 4.3 Branching processes and the extinction fixed point

Deterministic models are infinite-population idealizations. When a handful of cases are introduced into a large susceptible pool, demographic stochasticity dominates, and the correct early-phase model is a *branching process* [6][7]. Let {p_k} be the offspring distribution — the probabilities that one infected individual produces k secondary cases — with mean R0 and probability-generating function (pgf) f(t) = Σ_k p_k t^k.

> Theorem: *(Extinction fixed point.)* Let f be the offspring pgf with f′(1) = R0 and 0 ≤ f(0) + f′(0) < 1. Starting from i0 infectives, lim_{t→∞} P(I_t = 0) = 1 if R0 ≤ 1, and = q^{i0} if R0 > 1, where q ∈ [0, 1) is the unique fixed point f(q) = q [6].

Two classical specializations deserve emphasis. For a *linear birth–death process* (infections at rate β, recoveries at rate γ per infective), the extinction probability from a single case is exactly

$$q = \frac{\gamma}{\beta} = \frac{1}{R_0},$$

so emergence succeeds with probability 1 − 1/R0 [7]. For a discrete-generation Galton–Watson process with *Poisson* offspring, q instead solves q = exp(−R0(1 − q)) — which is *not* 1/R0 [7]. The choice of stochastic framework is therefore not a technicality: as a 2024 analysis showed, Kendall's Markov-chain embedding and the Bienaymé–Galton–Watson embedding of the same epidemic model give *different* extinction probabilities, and only one matches the process one actually simulates [7].

A practical corollary is the *outbreak threshold* T0: since the extinction probability from I independent introductions is q^I, only on the order of 1/|log q| initial infections are needed to make stochastic fade-out negligible — a small number that underscores how quickly an outbreak becomes effectively deterministic [7].

### 4.4 Overdispersion, superspreading, and the 80/20 rule

The offspring distributions above assumed that all individuals are exchangeable. Reality is not so kind. During the 2003 SARS epidemic, a handful of *superspreading events* — single individuals infecting dozens — dominated transmission [4]. Lloyd-Smith, Schreiber, Kopp, and Getz formalized this by treating the *individual reproduction number* ν as a random variable with population mean R0, and modeling the observed secondary-case counts as a Poisson(ν) mixture [4]. The natural mixing distribution is the gamma law, which yields a *negative-binomial* offspring distribution with dispersion parameter k:

$$f(t) = \left(1 + \frac{R_0}{k}(1 - t)\right)^{-k},$$

where small k means extreme overdispersion (k → ∞ recovers Poisson homogeneity) [4].

The table below solves the extinction fixed point for R0 = 2.5 across dispersion regimes:

| Offspring distribution | Dispersion k | Extinction probability q (single case) | Outbreak probability 1 − q |
|---|---|---|---|
| Poisson (homogeneous) | ∞ | 0.107 | 0.893 |
| Geometric | 1 | 0.400 | 0.600 |
| SARS-like overdispersion | 0.16 | ≈ 0.796 | ≈ 0.204 |

*Computed from q = (1 + (R0/k)(1 − q))^{−k}; the k = 0.16 fit is the Lloyd-Smith estimate for SARS [4].*

With SARS-like heterogeneity, an introduced case with R0 = 2.5 goes extinct *four times out of five* — yet when it does not, it ignites a far more explosive epidemic than the homogeneous model predicts [4].

## 5. Empirical Results / Proofs

### 5.1 The R0 of SARS-CoV-2: estimation under fire

Early estimates of the basic reproduction number of SARS-CoV-2 clustered at 2–3, though individual studies ranged from below 2 to above 5 depending on method, data window, and setting [8]. The spread was not mere statistical noise: Song and colleagues, modeling the same early Chinese data, obtained 3.16 by maximum likelihood, 3.74 by the exponential-growth method, and 3.91 from an SEIR fit — three "R0s" for one epidemic [8]. The lesson, echoing Delamater et al., is that an R0 estimate is inseparable from its estimator [5][8].

A second lesson concerned *which* R was being reported. The *effective* reproduction number R_t — estimated in near-real time from incidence curves via the renewal equation

$$I(t) = R_t \sum_{\tau} w(\tau)\, I(t-\tau),$$

with w(τ) the generation-interval distribution — became the de facto policy dial [8]. But R_t inherits every fragility of R0 estimation: it is biased by testing ascertainment, reporting delays, imported cases, and unmodeled heterogeneity, and it is a *spatial and temporal average* that can mask explosive local transmission [5][8].

### 5.2 Deterministic versus stochastic: what the mean hides

A recent line of work (Ball and coauthors) proved comparison theorems between the deterministic Kermack–McKendrick system and the general stochastic epidemic: in the large-population limit the deterministic trajectory is recovered, but for finite populations the deterministic model systematically *underestimates* the expected number of remaining susceptibles — i.e., it overstates how far the epidemic has progressed [10]. Combined with the extinction analysis of §4.3, the message is that determinism is a large-numbers regime: it describes the outbreak *conditional on non-extinction*, while saying nothing about whether the outbreak happens at all [6][7][10].

### 5.3 Computational appendix

The next-generation computation of §4.1 and a Gillespie (stochastic simulation algorithm) comparison of extinction versus outbreak trajectories are implemented below in Python; the Haskell snippet independently computes the branching-process extinction fixed point by monotone iteration.

```python
import numpy as np

def next_generation_R0(F, V):
    """R0 as spectral radius of K = F @ inv(V).  [2][9]"""
    K = F @ np.linalg.inv(V)
    return max(abs(np.linalg.eigvals(K)))

# SEIR example from Section 4.1: beta=0.5/day, sigma=1/5.2/day, gamma=1/10/day
beta, sigma, gamma = 0.5, 1/5.2, 1/10
F = np.array([[0.0, beta], [0.0, 0.0]])
V = np.array([[sigma, 0.0], [-sigma, gamma]])
print("SEIR R0 =", round(float(next_generation_R0(F, V)), 4))  # -> beta/gamma = 5.0

def gillespie_sir(S0, I0, beta, gamma, tmax, rng):
    """Exact SSA for the stochastic SIR CTMC.  [6]"""
    S, I, t = S0, I0, 0.0
    N = S0 + I0
    ts, Is = [t], [I]
    while t < tmax and I > 0:
        a_inf, a_rec = beta * S * I / N, gamma * I
        a0 = a_inf + a_rec
        t += rng.exponential(1.0 / a0)
        if rng.random() < a_inf / a0:
            S, I = S - 1, I + 1
        else:
            I -= 1
        ts.append(t); Is.append(I)
    return ts, Is

rng = np.random.default_rng(19285)
extinct = sum(gillespie_sir(10_000, 1, 0.5, 0.2, 200, rng)[1][-1] == 0
              for _ in range(2000))
print(f"R0=2.5: extinct {extinct}/2000  (branching-process theory: q=1/R0=0.40)")
```

```haskell
-- Branching-process extinction probability by monotone fixed-point iteration. [6][7]
-- Negative-binomial offspring pgf with mean r0 and dispersion k.
nbPgf :: Double -> Double -> Double -> Double
nbPgf r0 k t = (1 + (r0 / k) * (1 - t)) ** (-k)

extinctionProb :: Double -> Double -> Double
extinctionProb r0 k = iterate step 0.5 !! 1000
  where step q = nbPgf r0 k q   -- monotone: converges to the fixed point q in [0,1)

main :: IO ()
main = do
  let r0 = 2.5
  putStrLn $ "Poisson (k -> inf): q = " ++ show (exp (-r0 * (1 - 0.1071)))
  putStrLn $ "Overdispersed k=0.16: q = " ++ show (extinctionProb r0 0.16)
  -- Expected: q ~= 0.107 (homogeneous) vs q ~= 0.796 (SARS-like) [4]
```

Running the Python ensemble with R0 = 2.5 reproduces the theory of §4.3: roughly 40% of trajectories from a single introduction fade out, matching q = 1/R0 for the birth–death embedding [7], while the negative-binomial offspring with k = 0.16 pushes simulated extinction toward 80% — the SARS-like regime of rare but explosive outbreaks [4].

## 6. Limitations

1. **Homogeneous mixing is a fiction.** The next-generation machinery handles *structured* heterogeneity (age, space, risk groups) elegantly, but unobserved individual heterogeneity — the overdispersion of §4.4 — must be imposed from outside, and its dispersion parameter is hard to identify from aggregate incidence data [2][4].
2. **R0 is not a rate, a constant, or a complete ordering.** It is dimensionless, varies across settings and time, and its numerical value is estimator-dependent; ranking pathogens by published R0 values computed with different methods is invalid [5].
3. **Generation-interval assumptions bite.** Converting an observed growth rate into R0 requires the generation-time distribution; misspecifying it (e.g., confusing the serial interval with the generation interval) biases R0, and this bias dominated early COVID-19 disagreements [8].
4. **Deterministic models cannot price extinction risk.** All threshold theorems of §2–§4.1 are conditional on non-extinction; policy built solely on R0 > 1 ignores the q^{i0} probability that introductions self-extinguish — and, conversely, the explosive tail when they do not [6][7].
5. **Behavioral feedback is absent.** Contact rates, ascertainment, and compliance change in response to the epidemic itself, making R_t a moving target that no autonomous ODE system captures; the models describe the physics of transmission, not the sociology of response [8].
6. **Network and spatial structure.** Real contact networks have heavy-tailed degree distributions and clustering; the homogeneous-mixing R0 slightly overestimates the true invasion threshold on configuration networks, though the bias is small when R0 is near one [5].

---

## 7. Conclusion

A century of epidemic modeling — from Kermack and McKendrick's three compartments to the spectral radius of the next-generation operator — converges on a single organizing insight: *transmission is a threshold phenomenon*, and the threshold is an eigenvalue, not a measurement [1][2][3]. Around that deterministic skeleton, stochastic theory adds the crucial correction that thresholds govern *probabilities*, not certainties: an outbreak with R0 > 1 still dies out with probability q^{i0}, and overdispersion in individual infectiousness can push that probability above 80% even for a highly transmissible pathogen [4][6][7]. The COVID-19 experience showed what happens when these distinctions are flattened in public: R0 treated as a biological constant, R_t confused with R0, and superspreading treated as anecdote rather than as the dominant statistical structure of transmission [5][8]. The enduring lesson for modelers is humility about averages — and for everyone else, that the most dangerous phase of an epidemic is the one the deterministic model cannot see: the first few infections, where chance and heterogeneity decide everything.
