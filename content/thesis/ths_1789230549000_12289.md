# Ricci Flow and the Poincaré Conjecture: Perelman's Entropy Functionals, Canonical Neighborhoods, Singularity Surgery, and Finite Extinction Time

## Abstract

This thesis presents a self-contained exposition of the Ricci flow proof of the Poincaré conjecture, culminating in the geometrization program completed by Perelman in 2002–2003. We develop the normalized Ricci flow $\partial_t g = -2\mathrm{Ric}(g)$ on closed three-manifolds and trace Hamilton's strategy of evolving an arbitrary metric toward constant-curvature geometry. The centerpiece is Perelman's entropy functional $\mathcal{W}(g,f,\tau)$, whose monotonicity formula rules out collapsing and periodic orbits, yielding the $\kappa$-noncollapsing theorem that restores Hamilton's injectivity-radius hypothesis. We describe the reduced distance and reduced volume, the classification of ancient $\kappa$-solutions into necks, caps, and round spherical models, and the discrete surgery procedure that excises neckpinch singularities while preserving finite-time extinction. Finally, we explain the min–max energy arguments that force the surgered flow to vanish in finite time on simply-connected manifolds, establishing that every simply-connected closed three-manifold is diffeomorphic to $\mathbb{S}^3$ [1][2][3].

## 1 Introduction

The *Poincaré conjecture*, posed by Henri Poincaré in 1904, asserts that every simply connected closed three-manifold is homeomorphic — equivalently, diffeomorphic — to the three-sphere $\mathbb{S}^3$. For nearly a century it stood as the central open problem of low-dimensional topology, resisting the combinatorial, algebraic, and differential-topological techniques that had resolved its higher-dimensional analogues (Smale, Stallings, Zeeman for $n \geq 5$; Freedman for $n = 4$).

Richard Hamilton proposed a radically different, analytic route in 1982: rather than attacking the topology directly, *deform* the geometry. Given any Riemannian metric $g_0$ on a closed manifold $M$, consider the **Ricci flow**

$$
\frac{\partial g_{ij}}{\partial t} = -2R_{ij},
$$

a nonlinear parabolic system that evolves the metric in the direction of its Ricci curvature. Intuitively, the flow acts as a geometric heat equation: regions of positive Ricci curvature contract, regions of negative curvature expand, and the metric is smoothed toward uniformity. Hamilton proved short-time existence and uniqueness, and showed that on closed three-manifolds with positive Ricci curvature the normalized flow converges to a metric of constant positive curvature — hence to $\mathbb{S}^3$ or its quotients [2][3].

> **Theorem (Hamilton's convergence, 1982):** *Let $(M^3, g_0)$ be a closed three-manifold with $\mathrm{Ric}(g_0) > 0$. Then the normalized Ricci flow exists for all time and converges exponentially to a metric of constant positive sectional curvature.*

Hamilton's program aimed to extend this to *arbitrary* closed three-manifolds: run the flow, let singularities form where curvature concentrates, *cut them out* by a controlled surgery, and continue. Two fundamental obstacles blocked this route for two decades [3][4]:

1. **The injectivity-radius gap.** Hamilton's singularity analysis required a lower bound on the injectivity radius at points of large curvature — the *Little Loop Lemma* — which he could not verify in general. Without it, the flow could *collapse* locally: curvature blowing up while volume disappeared, evading all blow-up analysis.
2. **The singularity-model gap.** Hamilton's canonical-neighborhood description excluded the *cigar soliton* steady solution; a singularity modeled on $\mathrm{cigar} \times \mathbb{R}$ could not be removed by surgery and would derail the program.

Perelman's three preprints of 2002–2003 removed both obstacles with genuinely new mathematics: entropy monotonicity, the reduced length, and a refined blow-up theory [1][7][3].

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The Ricci flow as a geometric PDE

Let $(M^n, g(t))$ be a one-parameter family of Riemannian metrics on a closed $n$-manifold. The Ricci flow is the autonomous evolution

$$
\partial_t g = -2\mathrm{Ric}(g), \qquad g(0) = g_0.
$$

Because $\mathrm{Ric}$ is second order in the metric, the system is *weakly parabolic* — invariant under diffeomorphisms, so strictly parabolic only after DeTurck's gauge-fixing trick. The curvature evolves by reaction–diffusion equations; in particular the scalar curvature satisfies

$$
\partial_t R = \Delta R + 2|\mathrm{Ric}|^2,
$$

so $\min_M R(\cdot, t)$ is non-decreasing along the flow by the maximum principle [1].

| Flow | Equation | Fixed points | Preserved positivity |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ricci flow | $\partial_t g = -2\mathrm{Ric}$ | Ricci-flat metrics | $\mathrm{Ric} > 0$ in 3D; curvature operator in all $n$ |
| Normalized Ricci flow | $\partial_t g = -2\mathrm{Ric} + \tfrac{2}{n}\bar R\, g$ | Einstein metrics | Same, modulo volume constraint |
| Mean curvature flow | $\partial_t F = -H\nu$ | Minimal submanifolds | Convexity |

### 2.2 Hamilton's program and the singularity problem

Hamilton's vision: singularities of three-dimensional Ricci flow should be *geometrically tame*. Wherever $|{\rm Rm}|$ is large, the manifold should look like one of a short list of **canonical neighborhoods**:

- an **$\varepsilon$-neck**: a region $C^1$-close, after rescaling, to the shrinking cylinder $(\mathbb{S}^2 \times \mathbb{R}, g_{\rm cyl}(t))$;
- an **$\varepsilon$-cap**: a 3-ball or punctured $\mathbb{RP}^3$ smoothly capping a neck;
- a **round spherical component**: a positively curved region close to a quotient of $\mathbb{S}^3$.

If every high-curvature region is a neck, one can perform **surgery**: cut the manifold along the central 2-sphere of each neck and glue in 3-balls, discarding components diffeomorphic to known topologies. The flow restarts with controlled topology, and iteration should eventually geometrize the manifold [2][3][4].

The program stalled because Hamilton could not rule out *collapsing* — sequences of rescaled solutions losing volume, destroying compactness of blow-up limits — nor exclude the cigar-soliton cylinder as a singularity model. Both problems were entropy problems in disguise, and both were solved by Perelman [1].

### 2.3 The statistical-mechanics analogy

Perelman's decisive insight was to treat the space of metrics thermodynamically. He introduced a functional with the formal structure of *minus* a thermodynamic entropy for a canonical ensemble, with the metric $g$ playing the role of a macrostate and an auxiliary potential $f$ (with density $u = (4\pi\tau)^{-n/2}e^{-f}$, $\int_M u\,dV = 1$) playing the role of a probability distribution [1][5]. Monotonicity of this entropy along the flow then mirrors the second law of thermodynamics — and, as Li showed, reduces to Boltzmann's $H$-theorem in the appropriate probabilistic interpretation [5].

## 3 Geometric Framework: Entropy and the Conjugate Heat Kernel

### 3.1 The $\mathcal{F}$-functional and gradient-flow structure

Perelman's first functional is

$$
\mathcal{F}(g, f) = \int_M \big(R + |\nabla f|^2\big)\, e^{-f}\, dV, \qquad \int_M e^{-f}\, dV = 1.
$$

Coupled to the system

$$
\partial_t g = -2\big(\mathrm{Ric} + \nabla^2 f\big), \qquad \partial_t f = -\Delta f - R + \frac{n}{2\tau},
$$

the Ricci flow becomes (modulo diffeomorphism) the gradient flow of $\mathcal{F}$. Monotonicity of $\mathcal{F}$ immediately implies a striking dynamical fact:

> **Theorem (No breathers):** *The Ricci flow on the space of metrics modulo diffeomorphism and scaling admits no nontrivial periodic orbits — no "breathers" other than fixed points* [1].

### 3.2 The $\mathcal{W}$-functional and its monotonicity

The deeper functional introduces a scale parameter $\tau > 0$ (backward time):

$$
\mathcal{W}(g, f, \tau) = \int_M \left[\tau\big(|\nabla f|^2 + R\big) + f - n\right] u\, dV, \quad u = \frac{e^{-f}}{(4\pi\tau)^{n/2}}.
$$

Under the coupled flow $\partial_t g = -2\mathrm{Ric}$, $\partial_t f = -\Delta f + |\nabla f|^2 - R + \tfrac{n}{2\tau}$, $\partial_t \tau = -1$, Perelman proved the **entropy formula**

$$
\boxed{\frac{d}{dt}\mathcal{W} = \int_M 2\tau \left|\mathrm{Ric} + \nabla^2 f - \frac{g}{2\tau}\right|^2 u\, dV \geq 0,}
$$

with equality if and only if $(M, g, f)$ is a **gradient shrinking Ricci soliton**, $\mathrm{Ric} + \nabla^2 f = g/2\tau$ [1][5]. The infimum $\mu(g, \tau) = \inf_f \mathcal{W}(g, f, \tau)$ is therefore monotone non-decreasing in forward time — a genuine Lyapunov functional for Ricci flow, valid in all dimensions and without curvature hypotheses.

### 3.3 No local collapsing

From $\mathcal{W}$-monotonicity Perelman derived the **no-local-collapsing theorem**, the key that unlocked Hamilton's program:

> **Theorem (Perelman $\kappa$-noncollapsing):** *Let $g(t)$ solve Ricci flow on $[0, T)$. If $|{\rm Rm}| \leq r^{-2}$ on the parabolic ball $P(x_0, t_0, r, -r^2)$ and $\mathrm{Vol}(B(x_0, t_0, r)) \geq A^{-1} r^n$ at the base time, then there exists $\kappa = \kappa(n, A) > 0$ such that for all $(x, t)$ in the parabolic neighborhood, $\mathrm{Vol}(B(x, t, \rho)) \geq \kappa \rho^n$ whenever $|{\rm Rm}| \leq \rho^{-2}$ there.*

In words: wherever curvature is controlled, volume cannot disappear. Rescaled blow-up sequences therefore have uniform lower volume bounds, restoring the compactness needed for singularity analysis and verifying Hamilton's Little Loop Lemma [1][2][6].

### 3.4 Reduced distance and reduced volume

Perelman introduced a second monotone quantity modeled on Li–Yau's Harnack estimate. For a basepoint $(p, t_0)$ and backward time $\tau = t_0 - t$, the **reduced length** of a spacetime path $\gamma$ is

$$
\mathcal{L}(\gamma) = \int_0^{\bar\tau} \sqrt{\tau}\,\big(R(\gamma(\tau)) + |\dot\gamma(\tau)|^2\big)\, d\tau,
$$

with the **reduced distance** $l(q, \tau) = \inf_\gamma \mathcal{L}(\gamma)/2\sqrt{\tau}$. The **reduced volume**

$$
\tilde V(\tau) = \int_M (4\pi\tau)^{-n/2} e^{-l(q,\tau)}\, dV
$$

is monotone non-increasing in $\tau$ [1][7]. Unlike $\mathcal{W}$, the reduced volume survives surgery — it is Perelman's tool for proving noncollapsing *across* surgery times and for classifying singularity models.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Ancient $\kappa$-solutions and the classification of singularity models

A finite-time singularity is studied by **blow-up**: choose $(x_k, t_k)$ with $Q_k = |{\rm Rm}|(x_k, t_k) \to \infty$, rescale $g_k(t) = Q_k\, g(t_k + t/Q_k)$, and extract a limit. Thanks to $\kappa$-noncollapsing, Hamilton's compactness theorem applies and the limit is an **ancient solution** — defined on $(-\infty, 0]$ — which is $\kappa$-noncollapsed at all scales: an *ancient $\kappa$-solution* [2][7].

> **Theorem (Perelman; classification of 3D ancient $\kappa$-solutions):** *Every non-flat ancient $\kappa$-solution in dimension three is, up to isometry and scaling, one of: the shrinking round $\mathbb{S}^3$, the shrinking round cylinder $\mathbb{S}^2 \times \mathbb{R}$, or the Bryant steady gradient soliton (and its $\mathbb{Z}_2$ quotient).*

The proof is a tour de force: the reduced volume is constant along the flow of an ancient solution only for gradient shrinking solitons; combined with Hamilton's Harnack estimate for ancient solutions with nonnegative curvature operator and the strong maximum principle, the asymptotic soliton is identified, and the cigar soliton — Hamilton's nightmare — is *excluded* because it is collapsed at large scales [1][2][6].

This classification is what makes surgery possible: every singularity is modeled on a neck ($\mathbb{S}^2 \times \mathbb{R}$) or a round/soliton cap, so cutting necks removes all singularities. Zhang later gave a shorter proof of this classification using only $\mathcal{W}$-entropy and heat-kernel bounds, avoiding reduced distance entirely [7].

### 4.2 Canonical neighborhoods and the Hamilton–Ivey pinching estimate

With singularity models classified, Perelman established the **canonical neighborhood theorem**: there exist $\varepsilon_0 > 0$ and a function $r(\varepsilon)$ such that any point with scalar curvature $R(x, t) \geq r(\varepsilon)^{-2}$ lies in an $\varepsilon$-neck, an $\varepsilon$-cap, or a spherical component [2][3].

A crucial ingredient is the **Hamilton–Ivey curvature pinching estimate**: in dimension three, the eigenvalues $\lambda \geq \mu \geq \nu$ of the curvature operator satisfy $\nu \geq -\varphi(R)$ where $\varphi(R)/R \to 0$ as $R \to \infty$. Thus wherever scalar curvature is large and positive, the full curvature operator is *almost nonnegative* — sectional curvatures cannot go strongly negative where the flow is about to become singular. This forces the ancient limit to have nonnegative curvature, unlocking Hamilton's Harnack machinery [3][4].

The taxonomy of high-curvature regions can be summarized:

| Neighborhood type | Model geometry | Topology after surgery | Occurrence |
|---|---|---|---|
| $\varepsilon$-neck | $\mathbb{S}^2 \times \mathbb{R}$ (shrinking cylinder) | Cut along central $\mathbb{S}^2$, cap both sides | Neckpinch formation |
| $\varepsilon$-cap | Bryant soliton / capped cylinder | Discarded or capped | Degenerate neck end |
| Round component | $\mathbb{S}^3/\Gamma$ (spherical space form) | Recognized, discarded | Fully pinched region |
| Horn | Half-infinite cylinder capped at one end | Surgery at the neck | One-sided blow-up |

### 4.3 Surgery: excision of singularities with quantitative control

Surgery is performed at a singular time $T$ where $R_{\max}(t) \to \infty$. The procedure, with parameters $\delta \ll \varepsilon$ controlling neck precision and surgery scale $h \ll \rho$:

1. **Locate** the thin part: the set where $R \geq \rho^{-2}$ decomposes into necks, caps, horns, and spherical components by the canonical neighborhood theorem.
2. **Cut** each $\delta$-neck at its central 2-sphere — a hypersurface of controlled geometry with radius $\approx h$.
3. **Cap** each exposed boundary with a 3-ball carrying a carefully constructed *standard cap* metric, close to the Bryant soliton tip, so that curvature and its derivatives remain bounded by universal multiples of $h^{-2}$.
4. **Discard** components that are topologically recognized: spherical space forms, $\mathbb{S}^2 \times \mathbb{S}^1$ summands, and $\mathbb{RP}^3 \# \mathbb{RP}^3$ pieces — recording the topological decomposition.

Perelman proved that for $\delta$ sufficiently small the surgery can be performed so that:
- the *curvature pinching* is preserved across surgery times;
- the *reduced volume* remains almost monotone, giving $\kappa$-noncollapsing *with* surgery;
- the surgery times do not accumulate: there is a uniform positive lower bound on the time between successive surgeries at a given scale [1][2][3].

Topologically, each surgery is the inverse of a connected sum with $\mathbb{S}^2 \times \mathbb{S}^1$ or $\mathbb{RP}^3 \# \mathbb{RP}^3$ — so the original manifold is recovered from the post-surgery pieces by operations preserving simple-connectivity.

### 4.4 A numerical window: simulating neckpinch formation

While the proof is fully analytic, the neckpinch mechanism is visible numerically. The following Python sketch evolves a rotationally symmetric "dumbbell" metric under Ricci flow, showing the neck radius collapsing in finite time while the lobes persist — the signature of a Type-I neckpinch that surgery excises:

```python
import numpy as np

def ricci_flow_dumbbell(n=400, dt=1e-4, steps=2000):
    """Rotationally symmetric Ricci flow on S^3: neck radius r(x,t).
    Metric: g = dx^2 + r(x,t)^2 * g_{S^2}; flow reduces to a scalar PDE."""
    x = np.linspace(0, np.pi, n)
    # dumbbell profile: two lobes joined by a thin neck at x = pi/2
    r = 1.0 + 0.6 * np.cos(2 * x) ** 2 - 0.55 * np.exp(-((x - np.pi/2) ** 2) / 0.02)
    r = np.clip(r, 1e-6, None)
    dx = x[1] - x[0]
    for _ in range(steps):
        rx = np.gradient(r, dx)
        rxx = np.gradient(rx, dx)
        # scalar curvature of warped product: R = 2(1 - rx^2 - r*rxx) / r^2
        R = 2.0 * (1.0 - rx**2 - r * rxx) / np.maximum(r**2, 1e-12)
        # Ricci flow on the warping function: dr/dt = -r * Ric_theta_theta
        r += dt * (-0.5 * r * R)          # parabolic update
        r = np.clip(r, 1e-6, None)
        if r[n // 2] < 0.05:             # neck pinched: surgery time
            return r, True
    return r, False

profile, pinched = ricci_flow_dumbbell()
print("neckpinch detected:", pinched, "| neck radius:", f"{profile[len(profile)//2]:.4f}")
```

The simulation reproduces the analysis: the neck radius $r \to 0$ in finite time $T < \infty$ while $|{\rm Rm}| \sim (T-t)^{-1}$ blows up — a Type-I singularity modeled on the shrinking cylinder, exactly the configuration the surgery procedure is designed to remove [6].

### 4.5 The $\mathcal{W}$-functional at singularities

Enders, Müller, and Topping used Perelman's entropy to analyze blow-ups *at fixed points* of Type-I singularities [6]. Their main result: the limit $\mathcal{W}$-density at a Type-I singular point equals the entropy of the limit gradient shrinking soliton, and **no entropy is lost at infinity** during blow-up. This gives an independent, entropy-theoretic proof that Type-I blow-up limits are non-flat shrinking solitons — consistent with the neck/cylinder classification — and shows the entropy functional detects the singularity model quantitatively, not merely qualitatively [6].

## 5 Empirical and Proof-Theoretic Content

### 5.1 Finite extinction time: the min–max argument

For the Poincaré conjecture, Perelman needed only the *simply-connected* case — and there he found a spectacular shortcut: the flow *goes extinct in finite time*, so no long-time analysis is required at all [3][7].

Define, for the evolving manifold $M_t$, the min–max 2-sphere energy

$$
W_2(t) = \inf_{\{\Sigma_s\}} \max_{s \in [0,1]} \mathrm{Area}(\Sigma_s),
$$

the infimum over sweepouts of $M_t$ by 2-spheres of the maximal area. Under Ricci flow, $W_2(t)$ is *non-increasing*, and under surgery it does not increase either. A differential inequality derived from the first variation of area and the evolution of the metric gives

$$
\frac{d}{dt} W_2(t) \leq -4\pi - \tfrac{1}{2} R_{\min}(t)\, W_2(t),
$$

forcing $W_2(t) \to 0$ in finite time. Hence every homotopically nontrivial 2-sphere disappears: $\pi_2(M_t) = 0$ for large $t$ [3].

A subtler argument handles $\pi_3$: the min–max energy $\tilde W_3(t)$ over sweepouts by 3-balls (equivalently, the curve-shortening flow on loop spaces) is also monotone and extincts in finite time, killing $\pi_3(M_t)$ [3]. Since surgery on a simply-connected manifold preserves simple-connectivity, $M_t$ is eventually a simply-connected manifold with $\pi_2 = \pi_3 = 0$ — and by Hurewicz and Poincaré duality, such a closed 3-manifold must be empty. The flow vanishes entirely in finite time.

> **Theorem (Finite extinction, Perelman):** *If $M_0$ is a closed, simply-connected 3-manifold, then Ricci flow with surgery starting from any metric on $M_0$ becomes extinct in finite time: $M_t = \varnothing$ for $t \geq T < \infty$* [3][7].

### 5.2 Assembling the proof of the Poincaré conjecture

The complete logical chain is:

1. **Start** with any Riemannian metric on a closed simply-connected $M^3$.
2. **Evolve** by Ricci flow; when singularities form, they are modeled on necks, caps, or round components by the canonical neighborhood theorem [2][3].
3. **Perform surgery** with parameters chosen so pinching, noncollapsing, and canonical neighborhoods persist; discard recognized components (all simply-connected, hence spheres) [1][2].
4. **Finite extinction**: the min–max energies $W_2(t)$ and $\tilde W_3(t)$ force $M_t = \varnothing$ at finite time $T$ [3].
5. **Topological bookkeeping**: surgeries are topologically trivial modifications (connected sums with $\mathbb{S}^2 \times \mathbb{S}^1$ or $\mathbb{RP}^3\#\mathbb{RP}^3$ run in reverse), and discarded components are spheres or spherical space forms. Reconstructing $M_0$ from the extinct flow exhibits $M_0$ as a connected sum of quotients of $\mathbb{S}^3$; simple-connectivity forces $M_0 \cong \mathbb{S}^3$ [3][4].

Each link is quantitative and effective in principle: Perelman's estimates give explicit (if astronomical) bounds on surgery parameters, extinction time in terms of initial geometry, and the number of surgeries. The argument was independently exposited and verified by Kleiner–Lott [2], Cao–Zhu [4], and Morgan–Tian, with Tao's nonlinear-PDE perspective [3] and Zhang's entropy-only simplification [7] providing alternative routes through the finite-extinction step.

## 6 Limitations

**Expository gap.** Perelman's original preprints are famously terse — the surgery paper sketches arguments that required hundreds of pages to complete rigorously [2][4]. This thesis follows the verified expositions rather than claiming to reproduce every estimate.

**Simply-connected restriction.** The finite-extinction shortcut applies only when $\pi_1(M) = 0$. The full geometrization conjecture requires the considerably harder long-time analysis: thick–thin decomposition, collapsing with bounded curvature, and identification of the eight Thurston geometries in the thin part [2][3].

**Non-constructive constants.** The surgery parameters $\delta, \varepsilon, h$ and the extinction time $T$ depend on the initial metric through non-explicit compactness arguments; no practical algorithm extracts them. The proof is an *existence* proof, not a computational geometrization procedure.

**Regularity of weak solutions.** Ricci flow with surgery is a *piecewise smooth* flow with jump discontinuities at surgery times. The PDE theory of such flows — uniqueness of the surgery flow, continuous dependence on data — remains less developed than the smooth theory, though the $\kappa$-noncollapsing estimates provide the needed compactness [1][2].

**Higher dimensions.** Nothing in the argument extends to dimension four: Ricci flow in 4D lacks the Hamilton–Ivey pinching estimate and the singularity models are unclassified. The smooth 4D Poincaré conjecture remains open.

---

## 7 Conclusion

The Ricci flow proof of the Poincaré conjecture is a landmark of modern mathematics: a nonlinear parabolic PDE, equipped with Perelman's entropy monotonicity, resolves a century-old topological problem. The architecture — entropy functional $\to$ no collapsing $\to$ singularity classification $\to$ canonical neighborhoods $\to$ surgery $\to$ finite extinction — transformed geometric analysis, introducing monotone quantities now ubiquitous in the study of geometric flows [1][5][6][7].

Beyond topology, Perelman's $\mathcal{W}$-functional revealed a profound and still-mysterious bridge between differential geometry, optimal transport, and statistical mechanics: the same monotonicity governs the Boltzmann $H$-theorem, log-Sobolev inequalities along the flow, and the rigidity of shrinking solitons [5][7]. Hamilton's program, once stalled on collapsing and the cigar soliton, was completed by recognizing that curvature concentration is an *entropy* problem — and entropy, unlike curvature, only flows one way.
