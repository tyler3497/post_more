# The Lattice Boltzmann Method for Computational Fluid Dynamics: Multiple-Relaxation-Time Collision Operators, Chapman–Enskog Multiscale Analysis, and Boundary Treatments for Complex Flows

## Abstract

The lattice Boltzmann method (LBM) recovers the incompressible Navier–Stokes equations from a minimal kinetic description in which particle populations stream along a discrete velocity lattice and relax toward a local equilibrium. This thesis develops the theory from the Bhatnagar–Gross–Krook single-relaxation-time skeleton through d'Humières' multiple-relaxation-time (MRT) collision operator, carries out the Chapman–Enskog multiscale expansion that formally links the lattice dynamics to continuum hydrodynamics, derives the Guo forcing scheme that correctly embeds body forces at the discrete level, and compares wall treatments from half-way bounce-back to the Zou–He velocity/pressure closures and curved-boundary interpolations. A central theme is that MRT relaxes distinct hydrodynamic and kinetic moments at independent rates, decoupling shear viscosity from bulk and ghost-mode damping and thereby restoring stability in low-viscosity and high-Reynolds-number regimes where BGK becomes fragile. Benchmark evidence — lid-driven cavity, Taylor–Green vortex decay, flow past a cylinder — is reviewed to ground the formal analysis in measured convergence rates and stability margins. The exposition closes by delineating hard limitations: compressibility errors, Galilean-invariance defects of standard lattices, Mach-number ceilings, and the treatment of high-Reynolds turbulence.

## 1 Introduction

Computational fluid dynamics (CFD) has long been dominated by discretizations of the Navier–Stokes equations obtained from continuum mechanics. The lattice Boltzmann method proposes the inverse route: begin with a kinetic equation, discretize it on a lattice with a handful of particle velocities, and recover hydrodynamics as an *emergent* property through a multiscale expansion [1]. The resulting algorithm is strikingly simple — local collision followed by exact streaming along lattice links — yet it reproduces, to second order in the lattice Mach number, the incompressible Navier–Stokes equations with a viscosity controlled by a single relaxation time [1][7].

The simplicity is deceptive. Early lattice-gas and BGK-based lattice Boltzmann models exhibited well-documented pathologies: numerical instability at modest Reynolds numbers, unphysical slip and wall penetration artifacts, velocity-dependent viscosity through Galilean-invariance violations, and forcing implementations that corrupted the recovered macroscopic equations. The program this thesis traces is the *repair* of these pathologies by three interlocking advances:

1. **The multiple-relaxation-time collision operator** of d'Humières [2], generalized to three dimensions by d'Humières *et al.* [3] and analyzed for stability by Lallemand and Luo [4], which relaxes moments in a basis where hydrodynamic modes (density, momentum) are exactly conserved and kinetic modes are damped independently.
2. **The Chapman–Enskog multiscale expansion** adapted to the discrete-velocity lattice [1][7], which makes the recovery of the Navier–Stokes equations *auditable*: every transport coefficient is derived, not postulated.
3. **Principled forcing and boundary treatments** — the Guo forcing scheme that removes discrete-lattice artifacts from body-force terms [5], and the Zou–He velocity/pressure boundary closures [6] alongside bounce-back and interpolated curved-wall schemes.

The thesis is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews the lattice-gas origins and the BGK discretization that made the method practical. Section 3 defines the D$d$Q$q$ lattice formalism, the MRT collision step, and the stream-collide cycle. Section 4 deep-dives into (i) the LBGK skeleton, (ii) the MRT moment-space construction, (iii) the Chapman–Enskog expansion, (iv) forcing and boundary treatments, and (v) stability and Galilean invariance. Section 5 presents benchmark evidence and formal guarantees. Section 6 states limitations, and Section 7 concludes.

## 2 Background

### From lattice gases to lattice Boltzmann

The method's ancestry runs from the Frisch–Hasslacher–Pomeau lattice gas cellular automaton (1986), through McNamara and Zanetti's 1988 lattice Boltzmann formulation that replaced Boolean occupation numbers with real-valued distribution functions, to Higuera, Succi, and Benzi's 1989 enhanced-collision linearized model, and the landmark Qian–d'Humières–Lallemand 1992 paper [7] that introduced the lattice BGK model on D2Q9 and D3Q15 lattices with an equilibrium derived from a low-Mach-number expansion of the Maxwellian. Chen and Doolen's 1998 review [1] consolidated this decade of development and remains the standard entry point.

The decisive simplification of the LBGK model is the collision operator

$$\Omega_i = -\frac{1}{\tau}\,(f_i - f_i^{\mathrm{eq}}),$$

a single relaxation time $\tau$ applied to all distribution functions $f_i$, with equilibrium

$$f_i^{\mathrm{eq}} = w_i \rho \left[ 1 + \frac{\mathbf{c}_i\cdot\mathbf{u}}{c_s^2} + \frac{(\mathbf{c}_i\cdot\mathbf{u})^2}{2c_s^4} - \frac{\mathbf{u}^2}{2c_s^2} \right],$$

where the weights $w_i$, discrete velocities $\mathbf{c}_i$, and lattice sound speed $c_s$ are fixed by isotropy constraints on the velocity set [1][7]. Through the Chapman–Enskog analysis, the kinematic viscosity emerges as $\nu = c_s^2(\tau - 1/2)\Delta t$ — viscosity is a *parameter of the collision model*, not a property of a differencing stencil. This is conceptually elegant but numerically brittle: lowering viscosity pushes $\tau \to 1/2$, where the BGK dynamics lose stability and develop catastrophic checkerboard instabilities [3][4].

### The MRT response

d'Humières' 1992 proposal [2] replaced the single relaxation rate with a collision performed in **moment space**. If $\mathbf{f} = (f_0, \dots, f_{q-1})^T$ is the population vector, an invertible matrix $\mathbf{M}$ maps it to moments $\mathbf{m} = \mathbf{M}\mathbf{f}$: density, momentum, viscous stress, energy, heat flux, and higher "ghost" moments. Collision then reads

$$\mathbf{m}^\star = \mathbf{m} - \mathbf{S}\,(\mathbf{m} - \mathbf{m}^{\mathrm{eq}}),$$

with a diagonal relaxation matrix $\mathbf{S} = \mathrm{diag}(s_0, s_1, \dots, s_{q-1})$, and populations are recovered by $\mathbf{f}^\star = \mathbf{M}^{-1}\mathbf{m}^\star$ before the streaming step. Conserved moments carry $s = 0$ (no relaxation); shear viscosity is set by the stress relaxation rates; remaining rates are free parameters that can be tuned — most famously $s \approx 1$ or $s = 8(2-s_\nu)/(8-s_\nu)$ (Lallemand–Luo "two-relaxation-time" optimality) — to maximize linear stability [3][4]. The MRT framework reduces to BGK when all non-conserved rates coincide [4].

### Forcing and boundaries: the other two pillars

Body forces — gravity, Lorentz, interfacial tension — enter real flows and must be embedded without corrupting the hydrodynamic limit. Naive additions of a force term to the LBE generate discrete-lattice artifacts; Guo, Zheng, and Shi [5] derived the now-standard representation

$$F_i = w_i\left(1 - \frac{1}{2\tau}\right)\left[ \frac{\mathbf{c}_i - \mathbf{u}}{c_s^2} + \frac{(\mathbf{c}_i\cdot\mathbf{u})\mathbf{c}_i}{c_s^4} \right]\cdot \mathbf{F},$$

together with the velocity redefinition $\rho\mathbf{u} = \sum_i \mathbf{c}_i f_i + \tfrac{1}{2}\mathbf{F}\Delta t$, which makes the scheme second-order accurate and recovers the correct Navier–Stokes forcing term under Chapman–Enskog [5].

At walls, the simplest treatment — bounce-back of populations, $f_{\bar{\imath}}(\mathbf{x}_f, t+\Delta t) = f_i^\star(\mathbf{x}_f, t)$ with $\mathbf{c}_{\bar{\imath}} = -\mathbf{c}_i$ — imposes no-slip to first order, but the *half-way* variant, in which the wall sits midway between fluid and solid nodes, yields second-order accuracy and is the workhorse of complex-geometry LBM [8]. Zou and He [6] supplied the complementary closure for prescribed velocity or pressure at open boundaries: using bounce-back of the non-equilibrium part, the unknown incoming populations are solved exactly from mass/momentum conservation plus transverse momentum corrections. For curved boundaries, interpolation schemes (Bouzidi *et al.*; Filippova–Hänel; Mei–Luo–Shyy) place the wall at a fractional distance $q \in (0,1]$ along the link and blend post-collision populations accordingly [8].

## 3 Methodology

### The D$d$Q$q$ lattice formalism

A model is specified by its dimension $d$ and velocity count $q$: **D2Q9** (2D, nine velocities: rest, four axial, four diagonal), **D3Q15**, **D3Q19**, and **D3Q27** (3D). The discrete velocities $\mathbf{c}_i$ and weights $w_i$ satisfy the isotropy conditions

$$\sum_i w_i = 1, \qquad \sum_i w_i c_{i\alpha} c_{i\beta} = c_s^2 \delta_{\alpha\beta}, \qquad \sum_i w_i c_{i\alpha} c_{i\beta} c_{i\gamma} c_{i\delta} = c_s^4(\delta_{\alpha\beta}\delta_{\gamma\delta} + \delta_{\alpha\gamma}\delta_{\beta\delta} + \delta_{\alpha\delta}\delta_{\beta\gamma}),$$

with $c_s^2 = 1/3$ for the standard D$d$Q$q$ families (in lattice units $\Delta x = \Delta t = 1$) [1][7]. The evolution equation is

$$f_i(\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{c}_i \Delta t, t + \Delta t) - f_i(\mathbf{x}, t) = \Omega_i(\mathbf{x}, t) + \Delta t\,F_i(\mathbf{x}, t),$$

split in practice into two half-steps:

- **Collision (local, nonlinear):** $f_i^\star(\mathbf{x}, t) = f_i(\mathbf{x}, t) + \Omega_i + \Delta t\,F_i$,
- **Streaming (exact, linear):** $f_i(\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{c}_i \Delta t, t+\Delta t) = f_i^\star(\mathbf{x}, t)$.

Macroscopic fields are moments: $\rho = \sum_i f_i$, $\rho\mathbf{u} = \sum_i \mathbf{c}_i f_i$ (with the Guo half-force correction when forcing is present [5]), and pressure $p = c_s^2 \rho$.

### The MRT collision step in detail

For D2Q9, Lallemand and Luo [4] choose the orthogonal moment basis

$$\mathbf{m} = (\rho, e, \epsilon, j_x, q_x, j_y, q_y, p_{xx}, p_{xy})^T,$$

with $\mathbf{M}$ constructed from monomials of the velocity components. Equilibrium moments are functions of $\rho$ and $\mathbf{j} = \rho\mathbf{u}$, e.g. $e^{\mathrm{eq}} = -2\rho + 3(j_x^2 + j_y^2)/\rho$ and $p_{xx}^{\mathrm{eq}} = (j_x^2 - j_y^2)/\rho$, $p_{xy}^{\mathrm{eq}} = j_x j_y/\rho$ [4]. The relaxation matrix is

$$\mathbf{S} = \mathrm{diag}(0, s_e, s_\epsilon, 0, s_q, 0, s_q, s_\nu, s_\nu),$$

zeros on conserved moments (density, momentum), $s_\nu$ fixing shear viscosity via $\nu = c_s^2(1/s_\nu - 1/2)\Delta t$, $s_e$ fixing bulk viscosity $\zeta = c_s^2(1/s_e - 1/2)\Delta t$, and $s_q, s_\epsilon$ free for stability tuning [4]. The matrix $\mathbf{M}^{-1}$ can be precomputed and the entire collision implemented as a small dense product; modern implementations use the *raw-moment* or *central-moment* variants for improved Galilean invariance, and the *cascaded* (central-moment MRT) scheme of Geier *et al.* as the limiting refinement [8].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The LBGK skeleton: stream, collide, and why one relaxation time is not enough

The lattice BGK equation is the minimal viable LBM. Its virtues are real: exact advection (no numerical diffusion in the streaming step), unconditional positivity for $\tau > 1/2$, and trivial parallelization since collision is pointwise [1][7]. Its defect is equally real and quantifiable. Linear stability analysis of LBGK shows that the spectral radius of the evolution operator approaches unity as $\tau \downarrow 1/2$, and nonlinear simulations develop grid-scale oscillations — "checkerboard" modes — that grow in shear layers at Reynolds numbers where continuum physics should be perfectly smooth [3][4].

> **Theorem (LBGK viscosity–stability tradeoff).** For the D2Q9 LBGK model with viscosity $\nu = c_s^2(\tau - 1/2)\Delta t$, reducing $\nu$ at fixed resolution forces $\tau \to 1/2$, in which limit the collision operator approaches an involution and non-hydrodynamic moments are undamped; consequently attainable Reynolds numbers scale unfavorably with grid resolution.

The practical consequence, documented in lid-driven cavity benchmarks, is that BGK requires roughly twice the resolution of MRT to hold a stable Re $= 10^4$ cavity, or fails outright in three-dimensional turbulence [3][4]. The defect is *structural*: a single parameter cannot simultaneously encode transport physics (shear viscosity) and numerical hygiene (damping of kinetic modes).

```python
# Minimal LBGK stream-collide cycle (D2Q9), lattice units
import numpy as np
def collide_stream(f, tau, w, cx, cy):
    rho = f.sum(axis=0)
    ux = (f * cx[:, None, None]).sum(axis=0) / rho
    uy = (f * cy[:, None, None]).sum(axis=0) / rho
    usqr = ux**2 + uy**2
    cu = cx[:, None, None]*ux + cy[:, None, None]*uy
    feq = w[:, None, None] * rho * (1 + 3*cu + 4.5*cu**2 - 1.5*usqr)
    f = f - (f - feq) / tau                      # collide (single rate!)
    for i in range(9):                           # stream (exact shift)
        f[i] = np.roll(f[i], shift=(int(cx[i]), int(cy[i])), axis=(0, 1))
    return f
```

The loop above is the entire solver; its failure mode at $\tau \to 1/2$ is the entire motivation for MRT [4].

### 4.2 The MRT operator: moment space as a control room

The MRT construction converts the collision from a scalar rescaling into a *diagonal operator in a physically chosen basis*. Let $\mathbf{M}$ be the $q \times q$ moment matrix, $\mathbf{S} = \mathrm{diag}(s_k)$ the relaxation-rate matrix. The collision is

$$\mathbf{f}^\star = \mathbf{M}^{-1}\Big[\mathbf{m} - \mathbf{S}(\mathbf{m} - \mathbf{m}^{\mathrm{eq}})\Big], \qquad \mathbf{m} = \mathbf{M}\mathbf{f}.$$

Because $\mathbf{M}$ is built from polynomials in $\mathbf{c}_i$ ordered by tensor rank, the moments carry direct physical meaning: scalar $\rho$ and vector $\mathbf{j}$ (conserved, $s = 0$); energy $e$ and energy-squared $\epsilon$ (bulk-viscosity carriers); heat-flux vectors $\mathbf{q}$; the deviatoric stress tensor $(p_{xx}, p_{xy})$ whose relaxation rate $s_\nu$ alone sets shear viscosity; and remaining "ghost" moments that exist only to complete the basis [2][4].

The stability dividend follows from a simple observation: *the rates that control physics need not be the rates that control numerics*. Lallemand and Luo [4] performed von Neumann analysis of the linearized MRT evolution operator and showed that setting the non-hydrodynamic rates $s_q, s_\epsilon$ to values near $1$ (or to the magic relation $s_q = 8(2-s_\nu)/(8-s_\nu)$) dramatically expands the stability envelope, while $s_e$ can be raised to suppress acoustic noise via bulk viscosity without touching shear viscosity. In the diagonally driven 3D cavity at Re $= 2000$, the MRT D3Q19 model remained stable where BGK diverged — the canonical demonstration of [3].

Key structural facts worth committing to memory:

- **BGK is a special case:** MRT with $s_k \equiv 1/\tau$ for all non-conserved $k$ is exactly LBGK [4].
- **Conservation is exact:** rows of $\mathbf{M}$ for $\rho, j_x, j_y$ are left eigenvectors of the collision with eigenvalue $1$, so mass and momentum are conserved to machine precision, independent of all rate choices [2].
- **Bulk viscosity is tunable:** BGK fixes $\zeta$ in terms of $\nu$ (for D2Q9, $\zeta = \nu$ up to lattice corrections); MRT decouples them, allowing acoustic damping without altering the shear Reynolds number [4].
- **Equilibrium moments are polynomial:** $\mathbf{m}^{\mathrm{eq}}$ is at most quadratic in velocity, so the collision remains cheap — a $9 \times 9$ dense product per node in D2Q9 [4].

### Benchmark suite

The following canonical tests are the community's acceptance criteria; results summarized here are drawn from the cited primary sources.

| Benchmark | What it measures | Key result |
|---|---|---|
| Taylor–Green vortex decay | Viscous dissipation rate vs. theory | MRT matches analytic decay to 2nd order; BGK develops spurious modes at low $\nu$ [4] |

| Poiseuille flow | Parabolic profile, $\tau$-independent wall position | TRT magic parameter removes viscosity-dependent slip; 2nd-order convergence [8] |
| Decaying homogeneous turbulence | Energy spectrum, dissipation | MRT maintains Kolmogorov $-5/3$ range without subgrid model at moderate Re [8] |

Measured convergence is uniformly second-order in space for the velocity field with half-way bounce-back or Zou–He boundaries [6][8], and the Chapman–Enskog-derived viscosity is recovered to within discretization error in the Taylor–Green test — the sense in which the formal guarantees are *empirically* verified.

> **Theorem (Discrete conservation).** For any choice of the relaxation rates $s_k \in (0,2)$, the MRT collision conserves total mass $\sum_{\mathbf{x}} \rho$ and total momentum $\sum_{\mathbf{x}} \rho\mathbf{u}$ exactly (to roundoff), because conserved-moment rows of $\mathbf{M}$ are collision invariants. This holds independently of forcing only if the Guo half-force momentum correction is applied [2][5].

```rust
// MRT collision kernel sketch (D2Q9): moments -> relax -> back to populations
fn mrt_collide(f: &[f64; 9], m_eq: &[f64; 9], s: &[f64; 9],
               m: &[[f64; 9]; 9], m_inv: &[[f64; 9]; 9]) -> [f64; 9] {
    let mut mom = [0.0; 9];
    for k in 0..9 { mom[k] = m[k].iter().zip(f.iter()).map(|(a, b)| a * b).sum(); }
    for k in 0..9 { mom[k] -= s[k] * (mom[k] - m_eq[k]); } // s=0: conserved
    let mut fs = [0.0; 9];
    for i in 0..9 { fs[i] = m_inv[i].iter().zip(mom.iter()).map(|(a, b)| a * b).sum(); }
    fs
}
```

Performance characteristics are a further empirical guarantee: the algorithm is *local* except for the nearest-neighbor streaming shift, giving near-ideal weak scaling on distributed memory and GPUs; memory traffic dominates, so the MRT $9\times9$ products are essentially free relative to the bandwidth cost of the population arrays [8].

## 6 Limitations

Honesty requires stating what the method cannot do, or does only with difficulty.

1. **Compressibility ceiling.** LBM recovers the *incompressible* Navier–Stokes equations only in the limit $\mathrm{Ma} \to 0$; at finite $\mathrm{Ma}$ there are $O(\mathrm{Ma}^2)$ errors in the momentum equation and density variations masquerade as pressure via $p = c_s^2\rho$ [1][8]. High-speed compressible flow is outside the standard method's remit.
2. **Galilean-invariance defects.** Standard lattices lack the isotropy to make the $O(\mathbf{u}^3)$ viscous-stress terms frame-invariant; errors scale as $\mathrm{Ma}^3$ and are negligible at low Mach but disqualify the raw scheme for strongly sheared, high-speed flows without correction [8].
3. **Reynolds-number ceiling without modeling.** Even MRT becomes unstable at sufficiently low viscosity; genuine turbulence at high Re requires subgrid models (Smagorinsky LES is the standard LBM pairing) or entropic stabilizers, each reintroducing tuning [8].
4. **Thermal and multiphase complexity.** The isothermal D$d$Q$q$ skeleton has no energy equation; thermal and multiphase extensions (Shan–Chen, free-energy, phase-field couplings) multiply the parameter space and can suffer spurious currents and thermodynamic inconsistency at large density ratios [8].
5. **Boundary-condition sensitivity.** Bounce-back wall position, Zou–He corner closures, and curved-boundary interpolation all carry $O(\Delta x^2)$ errors with constants that matter in confined or porous geometries; staircased walls on coarse grids remain a practical hazard [6][8].
6. **Memory footprint.** Nineteen or twenty-seven populations per node (D3Q19/D3Q27) is memory-heavy relative to finite-volume stencils; the simplicity of the kernel is paid for in bandwidth [8].

None of these limitations is fatal for the method's core domain — low-Mach, complex-geometry, moderate-Re flows — but each marks a boundary beyond which the Chapman–Enskog certificate in Section 4.3 no longer applies without modification.

## 7 Conclusion

The lattice Boltzmann method earns its place in CFD not through its algorithmic simplicity alone but through the *auditability* of its approximations. The Chapman–Enskog expansion derives the Navier–Stokes equations from the lattice dynamics with every transport coefficient explicit; the MRT collision operator gives the practitioner independent control over physics and numerics; the Guo forcing scheme and the Zou–He/interpolated boundary treatments extend the certified core to driven flows and complex geometries. Three decades of benchmarks — from the Taylor–Green vortex to 3D lid-driven cavities at Re $= 2000$ and cylinder wakes — confirm the formal analysis where it applies and delineate precisely where it does not. The open frontier is not whether kinetic discretizations can capture hydrodynamics — that question is settled — but how far the certified envelope extends: entropic and central-moment stabilizers for higher Reynolds numbers, thermodynamically consistent multiphase couplings, and rigorous boundary treatments for moving, deforming interfaces. The MRT philosophy, that collisions are best designed in moment space, will underwrite all of them.
