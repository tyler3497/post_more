# Gradual Typing: The Blame Calculus, the Gradual Guarantee, and Soundness

## Abstract

Gradual typing promises the best of two worlds: the machine-checked discipline of static types and the flexibility of dynamic typing, coexisting in a single language and evolving smoothly between the two. Turning that promise into a science required two metatheoretical advances. The first is the *blame calculus* of Wadler and Findler [2], an intermediate language of explicit casts with blame labels that elaborates surface programs and proves the slogan *"well-typed programs can't be blamed"*: every cast failure is allocated to the less precisely typed side of the cast. The second is the *gradual guarantee* of Siek et al. [3], which formalizes type migration: making annotations more precise can never change a program's behavior except by introducing new cast failures. This thesis develops both ideas from their foundations in the consistency relation and the precision order of Siek and Taha [1], through occurrence typing, space-efficient coercions, threesomes, transient and monotonic semantics, to the empirical performance crisis measured by Takikawa et al. [5] and the compiler techniques of Grift [7] that answer it.

## 1. Introduction

For half a century, programming language design has been organized around a seemingly forced choice. *Static typing* promises that entire classes of errors are ruled out before a program runs, that compilers can exploit type information for optimization, and that annotations serve as machine-checked documentation. *Dynamic typing* promises rapid prototyping, flexible idioms that fit no static discipline, and fast adaptation to changing requirements. The two disciplines appeared irreconcilable: each language picked a side, and programmers paid the cost of the side they did not pick.

The central insight of Siek and Taha [1] is that this dichotomy is false. A single type system can host *both* disciplines simultaneously, provided it introduces a special *unknown* (or *dynamic*) type, written `?` in the theory, governed by compatibility rules rather than equality. A programmer annotates the regions where static discipline pays off and leaves the rest dynamically typed; the regions interoperate seamlessly; and a program can be *migrated* incrementally from untyped to typed as its design stabilizes. This idea, called *gradual typing*, has since been realized in Typed Racket [6], Reticulated Python, and a dozen research languages [10].

But a slogan is not a science. What does it *mean* for a language to be gradually typed, as opposed to merely bolting a dynamic type onto a static checker? Two formal properties now define the answer. The first, *blame safety*, due to Wadler and Findler [2], states that runtime cast failures are never the fault of statically typed code: *"well-typed programs can't be blamed."* The second, the *gradual guarantee* [3], states that the type annotations are *behaviorally transparent*: making them more precise can only introduce new cast errors, never change what a successful program computes. Together with the *consistency relation* and the *precision order* on types, these properties form the metatheoretical core of gradual typing — and the subject of this thesis.

The remainder of this work is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews the foundations: the original gradual lambda calculus, contracts and boundaries, occurrence typing, and hybrid checking. Section 3 describes the field's methodology — cast calculi, elaboration, and the refined criteria used to judge designs. Section 4 develops the deep theory: consistency and precision, the blame calculus, the gradual guarantee, and the space-efficiency problem solved by coercions and threesomes. Section 5 turns to empirical results: the performance methodology of Takikawa et al. [5], the transient-versus-guarded debate, and the Grift compiler [7]. Section 6 discusses limitations, and Section 7 concludes.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 The gradual lambda calculus

The original system of Siek and Taha [1] extends the simply typed lambda calculus with an unknown type `?`:

```haskell
T ::= B | T -> T | ?          -- gradual types: base, function, unknown
e ::= x | c | \x : T. e | e e -- surface terms with annotations
```

The decisive innovation is the *consistency relation* `~`, which replaces type *equality* in the typing rules. Two types are consistent when they are plausibly the same type, where `?` is consistent with everything:

```
–––––––––––          ––––––––––––
  T ~ T                ? ~ T

T1 ~ T3      T2 ~ T4
–––––––––––––––––––––––
 T1 -> T2 ~ T3 -> T4
```

Consistency is *reflexive* and *symmetric* but crucially *not transitive*: `Int ~ ?` and `? ~ Bool`, yet `Int ≁ Bool`. This non-transitivity is what prevents the dynamic type from collapsing all distinctions — the failure mode of earlier attempts that made the dynamic type both the top and the bottom of a subtyping hierarchy [12]. Where the types disagree statically but are consistent, the elaborator inserts a *runtime cast* that checks the value when it flows across the boundary, raising a cast error on mismatch.

### 2.2 Contracts, blame, and multi-language boundaries

The runtime half of gradual typing descends from *contracts*. Findler and Felleisen introduced higher-order contracts with *blame* [2]: when a contract between two parties is violated, blame is allocated to the party at fault — the function for a bad result, the environment for a bad argument. Matthews and Findler [1] gave this a multi-language operational semantics: typed and untyped code meet at explicit *boundaries*, values crossing a boundary are wrapped in contracts, and the resulting blame theorem already allocated failures to the untyped side. Wadler and Findler [2] internalized blame into the cast calculus itself, so the intermediate language — not just the boundary discipline — tracks responsibility.

### 2.3 Typed Racket and occurrence typing

The most influential realization of gradual typing is Typed Racket [6], born as Typed Scheme from Tobin-Hochstadt and Felleisen's *interlanguage migration* program: viewing an untyped program as a gradually typed program in which every type is `?`, then migrating annotations toward precision. Typed Racket works at *module* granularity (a macro-level approach) and adds *occurrence typing*: predicates in conditional position refine the types of variables along control-flow paths, so idiomatic dynamic code type-checks without rewriting:

```racket
(: f : (U Number String) -> Number)
(define (f x)
  (if (number? x)
      x                    ; here x : Number
      (string-length x)))   ; here x : String
```

Occurrence typing demonstrates that gradual typing is not only a theory of `?` but a theory of *how much a type system can learn from dynamic tests* — a theme that resurfaces in the precision order of Section 4.1.

### 2.4 Hybrid typing and the design space

Flanagan's *hybrid typing* and its Sage implementation [2] explored a neighboring point: refinement types discharged statically by a solver when possible and enforced dynamically otherwise. Siek and Taha's 2007 extension to objects [11], space-efficiency work [9], and the abstract-interpretation reconstruction of Garcia, Clark, and Tanter (surveyed in [10]) filled out the design space Siek et al. later disciplined with formal criteria [3].

## 3. Methodology

Research in gradual typing proceeds through a characteristic pipeline, and this thesis follows it.

**Surface, elaboration, target.** A gradually typed language is specified as a *surface language* with `?` and partial annotations, elaborated by a type-directed translation into a *cast calculus* in which every boundary crossing is an explicit cast term. The metatheory is proved about the *target*, then lifted to the surface through the elaboration.

**Small-step operational semantics.** Soundness is proved over small-step, call-by-value, contextual reduction on the cast calculus: progress (a well-typed term is a value, reduces, or raises *blame* attributed to a specific label) plus preservation. Big-step semantics would squash the intermediate states in which blame becomes observable, so the small-step relation is load-bearing [3].

**The refined criteria as checklist.** Siek et al. [3] propose that a gradual type system should (i) conservatively extend a static discipline, (ii) fully embed the unityped (dynamically typed) calculus, (iii) satisfy blame safety, and (iv) satisfy the gradual guarantee, statically and dynamically. We use these criteria to organize the theory and to critique implementation strategies.

**Mechanization and measurement.** The gradual guarantee has been *mechanized* for the Gradually Typed Lambda Calculus [3]; performance claims are evaluated against the configuration-lattice methodology of Takikawa et al. [5] rather than single benchmarks.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 The consistency relation and the precision order

Consistency `~` answers *"could these be the same type?"* The *precision order* `⊑` answers a different question: *"is this annotation more informative?"* Siek et al. [3] define `⊑` as the least relation with `? ⊑ T` for every `T` — the unknown type is the *least* precise — closed under structural congruence:

```
–––––––            T1 ⊑ T3      T2 ⊑ T4
 ? ⊑ T           ––––––––––––––––––––––––
                  T1 -> T2 ⊑ T3 -> T4
```

Thus `? ⊑ Int` and `(? -> ?) ⊑ (Int -> Bool)`: the right-hand side is more precise. Precision lifts from types to *terms*: `e ⊑ e′` when `e′` is `e` with some annotations made more precise — for example, `(λx : ?. x) ⊑ (λx : Int. x)`. *Type migration* is then the search for `e′` with `e ⊑ e′` that the gradual checker accepts [3].

| `T₁` | `T₂` | `T₁ ~ T₂`? | Reading |
|---|---|---|---|
| `Int` | `?` | yes | dynamic is consistent with everything |
| `?` | `Bool` | yes | consistency is symmetric |
| `Int` | `Bool` | **no** | distinct base types stay distinct |
| `Int -> ?` | `? -> Bool` | yes | structural consistency |
| `Int -> Int` | `Bool -> Int` | **no** | inconsistent domains |

The abstract-interpretation view of Garcia et al. [10] reconstructs both relations from a single idea: a gradual type denotes the *set* of static types it could stand for (`?` denotes everything), consistency holds when concretizations *overlap*, and precision is *subset inclusion* of concretizations. Under this lens, `?` is least precise because it denotes the most — which also explains why precision, unlike consistency, *is* transitive and forms a genuine order.

### 4.2 The blame calculus: casts, labels, and polarity

The blame calculus [2] makes the casts of elaboration *first-class syntax with provenance*:

```haskell
e ::= ... | <T <=p T'> e     -- cast from T' to T, blame label p
v ::= ... | <T2 <=p T1> v    -- higher-order casts become proxies
```

A label `p` names the cast; its complement `p̄` names the *context* surrounding it. First-order casts check tags and either succeed or raise `blame p`. Higher-order casts cannot be decided immediately — a function cast `⟨Int -> Int ⇐ᵖ ?⟩` must monitor *future* calls — so they elaborate into *wrappers* that decompose the check contravariantly on the domain and covariantly on the codomain, flipping polarity on the domain:

```
<(T1 -> T2) <=p (S1 -> S2)> v  -->  \x. <T2 <=p S2> (v (<S1 <=-p T1> x))
```

The deep result of [2] is a *polarity analysis* of blame. Decompose subtyping into *positive* and *negative* subtyping: `T <:⁺ S` when `T` can safely flow to `S` in positive (result) positions, `T <:⁻ S` dually for negative (argument) positions. Their intersection is *naive subtyping* — a notion that had previously appeared only in *unsound* systems, here rehabilitated as the key to soundness.

> **Theorem (Blame safety).** *Well-typed programs can't be blamed* [2]: for a cast between a less precisely typed and a more precisely typed side, any blame is allocated to the *less precisely typed* side. If `T <:⁺ S`, the cast `⟨S ⇐ᵖ T⟩` can never raise positive blame `p`; if `T <:⁻ S`, it can never raise negative blame `p̄`.

Concretely: *narrowing* `⌈λy. false⌉ : ? ⇒ᵖ Int -> Int` can raise positive blame `p` — the untyped term is at fault — but never negative blame; *widening* `⌈2⌉ : Int -> Int ⇒ᵖ ?` can raise negative blame `p̄` — the untyped context is at fault — but never positive blame [2]. The slogan follows: the statically typed side of any cast is *never* blamed. Wadler and Findler further prove the *Tangram Lemma*, which instantiates blame safety across the whole family of gradually typed calculi [8].

| Cast | Blame possible | Blame impossible | At fault |
|---|---|---|---|
| `⟨Int->Int ⇐ᵖ ?⟩` (narrowing) | positive `p` | negative `p̄` | the term |
| `⟨? ⇐ᵖ Int->Int⟩` (widening) | negative `p̄` | positive `p` | the context |

### 4.3 The gradual guarantee, statically and dynamically

The gradual guarantee [3] is the formal contract between the programmer and the annotation system. It has two parts, both stated with the precision order.

> **Theorem (Gradual guarantee, static part).** If `e₁ ⊑ e₂` and `⊢ e₁ : T₁`, then `⊢ e₂ : T₂` for some `T₂` with `T₁ ⊑ T₂` [3]: making annotations more precise preserves well-typedness, and the resulting type is itself more precise.

> **Theorem (Gradual guarantee, dynamic part).** If `e₁ ⊑ e₂`, then `e₁` and `e₂` behave identically, *unless* `e₁` evaluates to a value while `e₂` raises a blame error [3]: adding precision can only *introduce* new cast failures; it can never change a successful outcome into a different value, turn termination into divergence, or vice versa.

The practical consequence is the one programmers actually need: *migration is safe*. You may add types to a working untyped program module by module; nothing that worked will silently compute a different answer — the worst that happens is a loud, blamed cast failure at the spot where your new annotation contradicts reality. Siek et al. mechanized both parts for the Gradually Typed Lambda Calculus and used the guarantee to *critique* existing designs, showing that several published systems fail it [3].

### 4.4 Space, coercions, and threesomes

Naive proxies have a notorious defect: they break *tail calls*. Each boundary crossing wraps the value in another proxy layer, so two mutually recursive functions in tail position accumulate wrappers linearly in the call depth — constant space becomes linear [9]. Herman, Tomb, and Flanagan solved this with *coercions* in the style of Henglein: a compact runtime representation of casts in which sequences *compose and normalize*, so a chain of casts collapses to bounded size; their formulation also detects inconsistencies *eagerly* rather than waiting for the wrapped value to be used [9].

Siek and Wadler [4] contributed the complementary algebraic insight, the *threesome*: any cast `⟨T₂ ⇐ T₁⟩` — a *twosome* — factors through the *greatest lower bound* of the two types into a *downcast* followed by an *upcast*:

```
<T2 <=p T1>  =  <T2 <=p (T1 ⊓ T2)> ∘ <(T1 ⊓ T2) <=p T1>
```

and any chain of threesomes collapses to a single threesome by taking greatest lower bounds of the intermediate types. Blame labels ride along, mapping each threesome failure back to the offending twosome of the source program. Finally, Siek, Thiemann, and Wadler [8] unified the two strands — *blame and coercion, together* — proving full abstraction between the coercion-based and contract-based formulations. The engineering moral is sharp: space efficiency, eager errors, and precise blame are *jointly* achievable, not a pick-two tradeoff.

## 5. Empirical Results and Proofs

Theory predicts that casts cost something; measurement says *how much*, and the answer was alarming. Takikawa et al. [5] proposed the first serious *methodology* for the question: because cost depends on *where* annotations sit, one must measure the *lattice of configurations* — for `m` modules, all `2ᵐ` typed/untyped combinations — and report the distribution, not a point. Applied to Typed Racket, partially typed configurations suffered *up to two orders of magnitude* slowdown versus untyped Racket [7], prompting the famous question: *is sound gradual typing dead?* A formative study of Typed Racket agreed from the other direction: boundary crossings dominate, and programmers need profiling tools that point at the expensive boundaries first [5].

Three implementation strategies have emerged in response:

| Strategy | Runtime mechanism | Blame story | Performance character |
|---|---|---|---|
| Guarded (Siek–Taha) | proxies at boundaries | full blame calculus | sound but wrapper-heavy |
| Transient (Reticulated) | shallow checks at use sites | weakened | cheap, no proxies |
| Coercions + monotonic refs (Grift) | normalized coercions, precise-only heap writes | blame retained | near-static speed |

Reticulated Python's *transient* semantics [10] checks only first-order properties where values are *used* — function entry, attribute reads — never installing proxies. It is dramatically cheaper, but it weakens the soundness story and has been critiqued against the refined criteria [3]. The *Grift* compiler [7] takes the opposite bet: ahead-of-time compilation to native code with space-efficient coercions [8] and *monotonic references* — heap cells that may only become *more precise* over time, so typed code pays no check overhead at all. The results vindicate the approach: statically typed Grift programs run on par with OCaml; untyped programs sit between Gambit and Racket; partially typed programs land at a geometric mean of `0.42×`–`2.36×` untyped Racket, with *no catastrophic configurations* — coercions range from 15% slower to nearly `2×` faster than naive casts, and monotonic references beat proxied references by up to `1.48×` [7]. The crisis of [5] was a crisis of *implementation strategy*, not of the theory.

## 6. Limitations

The theory is not complete, and the honest limitations are where the research frontier lies.

*Blame is coarse.* The blame calculus allocates failure to a *side* of a cast, but in deep call stacks the label identifies the boundary, not the human-scale cause; debugging a `blame p` through layers of wrappers remains painful, and several designs satisfying blame safety still fail the gradual guarantee's dynamic part [3].

*State is hard.* Mutable references cross the typed/untyped boundary badly: proxied references break identity and aliasing reasoning, while monotonic references [7] recover performance only by restricting the heap discipline. Occurrence typing [6] interacts unsoundly with mutation in corner cases that Typed Racket handles with deliberate, documented restrictions.

*Polymorphism needs sealing.* Extending blame to parametric polymorphism requires *dynamic sealing* — fresh runtime seals for abstract types — as shown by Ahmed, Findler, Siek, and Wadler's "Blame for All" [1]; the seals are correct but add their own runtime tax, and inference of polymorphic gradual types remains largely open.

*The guarantee has exceptions.* Transient semantics [10] deliberately departs from the refined criteria for speed; nominal approaches such as Nom sidestep blame entirely by grounding gradual types in nominal rather than structural identity [10]; and decidability questions were still being mapped as recently as 2020 [11].

*Migration tooling lags theory.* The gradual guarantee promises safe migration, but programmers still lack refactoring tools that *propose* the `e′` with `e ⊑ e′`, and error messages at blame boundaries remain the most common complaint in user studies of Typed Racket [5].

## 7. Conclusion

Gradual typing began as a pragmatic compromise — one language, two disciplines, a `?` type to mediate between them [1]. It became a science when its obligations were written down as theorems. The *blame calculus* [2] made responsibility precise: casts carry labels, polarity analysis tells us which side can be at fault, and *"well-typed programs can't be blamed"* is a proved property rather than a hope. The *gradual guarantee* [3] made migration precise: precision is an order, annotations move monotonically along it, and behavior is invariant under that motion except for the introduction of loud, blamed failures. Around these two pillars, the field built the engineering that makes them affordable — coercions and threesomes for space [4, 8, 9], monotonic references and ahead-of-time compilation for speed [7] — and the methodology that keeps the engineering honest [5].

The remaining work is as much *human* as mathematical: better blame explanations, migration assistants, and profilers that turn the configuration lattice from a measurement device into a development tool. The theorems tell us what a gradually typed language *must* do; the next decade will decide how pleasantly it does it.
