# Certified Capability Machines: Formalizing CHERI Memory Safety with Sail ISA Semantics, Morello Compartmentalization, and Verified Capability Monotonicity in Isabelle/HOL

## Abstract

Memory safety remains the dominant root cause of exploitable software defects, and mainstream architectures address it only with coarse virtual-memory mechanisms and prose specifications. This thesis examines *certified capability machines*: architectures in which pointers are unforgeable, bounded capabilities enforced by hardware, and in which the central security property — *capability monotonicity* — is stated over a machine-readable ISA semantics and proved by machine-checked proof. We study CHERI's protection model, the Arm Morello AArch64 prototype, and the Sail ISA description language that unifies documentation, emulation, and verification. We reconstruct the verification methodology that translated Arm's ASL specification into Sail, generated Isabelle/HOL definitions, and proved *reachable capability monotonicity* — that arbitrary code cannot manufacture authority beyond its reachable capabilities — across the full Morello ISA. We analyze the per-instruction proof decomposition, sealed capabilities and domain transitions as controlled exceptions to monotonicity, the empirical cost of the proof, and open problems: scalable capability safety, temporal safety, and side-channel-aware contracts.

---

## 1. Introduction

Two facts jointly explain the persistence of memory-safety vulnerabilities in systems software. First, conventional instruction-set architectures treat pointers as plain integers: an address is a number, and nothing in the hardware distinguishes a legitimate pointer from a corrupted one. Second, the engineering of those architectures rests on informal, prose specifications — documents that cannot be executed, tested, or proved against. The result is what the CHERI ISA report calls, following Szekeres, Payer, Wei, and Song, the "eternal war in memory": every generation of mitigations (stack canaries, ASLR, DEP/NX, control-flow integrity) is defeated by the next generation of exploits, because the underlying abstract machine is unchanged [1].

CHERI — *Capability Hardware Enhanced RISC Instructions* — attacks the first problem directly. Developed through a collaboration between the University of Cambridge, SRI International, and Arm Research [2], CHERI extends conventional ISAs with a new architectural data type, the *capability*: an unforgeable, bounded token of authority that replaces integer pointers. Hardware enforces, on every memory access, that the dereference lies within the capability's bounds, that the requested operation is covered by its permissions, and that the capability itself is valid (tracked by an out-of-band *tag bit*). The second problem is addressed by the CHERI design process itself: the architecture is specified in **Sail**, a formal ISA-description language from which the architecture manual text, executable emulators, randomized test generators, and theorem-prover definitions are all automatically derived [3]. When the specification changes, every artifact changes with it.

The central contribution of this thesis is an exposition and critical analysis of the *verification* of these guarantees. Capability monotonicity — the property that newly constructed capabilities can never exceed the authority of the capabilities from which they were derived — is the linchpin of the CHERI security argument: it is what makes software compartmentalization *architecturally* enforceable rather than merely a programming convention. We reconstruct how this property was formalized for the **Arm Morello** prototype — a full-scale industry architecture implementing CHERI on AArch64 — and proved in **Isabelle/HOL** over the Sail-generated model [4,5]. We analyze the property decomposition that made a whole-ISA proof tractable, the controlled exceptions (sealing, sentry capabilities, exception handling) through which monotonicity is deliberately relaxed, and what the proof does and does not guarantee. We then place this work in the broader landscape: per-instruction monotonicity proofs for CHERI-MIPS in HOL4 [6], universal-contract formulations that aim to be robust against ISA evolution [7], and the emerging temporal-safety extensions [8].

The remainder of this thesis is organized as follows. Section 2 surveys background and related work: the CHERI capability model, Sail semantics, the Morello platform, and the verification landscape. Section 3 describes our methodology: how we reconstruct and analyze the proof architecture, the formal statements, and the trust base. Section 4 presents the deep dive: capability representation, monotonicity, sealing and compartmentalization, and the proof decomposition. Section 5 evaluates the empirical evidence: proof scale, compute cost, and testing. Section 6 discusses limitations and open problems. Section 7 concludes.

## 2. Background and Related Work

### 2.1 The CHERI capability model

A CHERI capability is a hardware data type of *twice the natural pointer width plus one tag bit*: 129 bits on a 64-bit architecture (128 bits of addressable content plus one out-of-band tag) and 65 bits on 32-bit architectures [1]. It carries:

- an **address** (the current cursor),
- **bounds** (base and top, or equivalently base and length),
- a **permissions** bitmask (load, store, execute, capability-load/store, sealing rights, etc.),
- an **object type** (*otype*) used for sealing,
- a **tag bit** recording validity, stored out-of-band so that bytewise memory operations cannot forge it.

The tag is cleared by any architectural operation that is not a legal capability manipulation — for example, writing a capability's memory with ordinary integer stores clears its tag, making the capability permanently invalid. This is the *integrity* half of unforgeability. The *provenance* half is **monotonicity**: capabilities are never created from thin air; every capability in the machine is derived from a more-authoritative ancestor by narrowing bounds, clearing permissions, or adjusting the address within bounds [2].

*CHERI Concentrate* (Woodruff et al., IEEE Transactions on Computers, 2019) compresses the bounds into a compact floating-point-inspired encoding so that a 128-bit capability plus tag fits in the register file and cache hierarchy with acceptable overhead, while *CheriABI* (Davis et al., ASPLOS 2019) ports the entire FreeBSD user space to capability pointers, demonstrating that memory safety can be retrofitted onto existing C software with minimal source changes.

### 2.2 Sail: one specification, many artifacts

Sail is an ISA-description language designed so that a single specification serves as *documentation, simulator, and verification target* [6]. From a Sail model, the toolchain generates:

1. architecture-manual text (instruction descriptions embedded directly in documents),
2. executable emulators in OCaml or C (fast enough to boot CheriBSD),
3. randomized and SMT-guided ISA test generators, and
4. definitions in the languages of the HOL4, Isabelle, and Coq theorem provers.

Crucially, the CHERI ISA manual itself (version 8, UCAM-CL-TR-951) is generated from the Sail model [2]: the pseudocode in the manual *is* the model. This eliminates the drift between specification and implementation that plagues prose ISAs, and it means a proof about the Sail model is a proof about the documented architecture — the two are the same artifact.

### 2.3 Arm Morello

Morello is Arm's experimental CHERI platform: an AArch64 application-class processor implementing the CHERI protection model, fabricated as a development board and used for research operating systems (CheriBSD) and software stacks [4]. For formal verification, Morello posed a qualitatively harder problem than earlier CHERI-MIPS research prototypes: a full-scale *industry* architecture with the complete AArch64 instruction set plus CHERI extensions. Arm's authoritative specification is written in ASL (Arm Specification Language); the verification effort translated ASL to Sail with the `asl_to_sail` tool, yielding the `sail-morello` model, from which Isabelle definitions were generated [4,5].

### 2.4 Related verification efforts

**Nienhuis et al. (IEEE S&P 2020)** [6] proved machine-checked security properties of the *entire CHERI-MIPS ISA* in HOL4, including per-instruction monotonicity and a compartment-monotonicity theorem: arbitrary code sequences confined to a compartment cannot amplify their rights beyond what is reachable via the register file or loadable memory. This was the first whole-ISA capability proof and established the methodology.

**Bauereiss et al. (ESOP 2022)** [5] scaled the approach to Morello, proving *reachable capability monotonicity* in Isabelle/HOL. The key innovation was decomposing the property into four per-instruction obligations, trading the stronger "capability safety" property (which they report does not yet scale to industry architectures) for a property that is both meaningful and automatable.

**Dang, Mulder, Sammler, and Krebbers (2023)** [7] proposed *universal contracts*: a more abstract statement of ISA security guarantees that is robust against ISA evolution (e.g., merged vs. split capability register files), verified semi-automatically with their Katamaran tool over Sail-generated separation-logic semantics.

**FRESCO (2025)** [8] addresses the complementary *temporal* dimension — use-after-free — with complete and scalable temporal safety for CHERI application processors, evaluated on SPEC CPU, SQLite, PostgreSQL, and gRPC workloads.

## 3. Methodology

Our method is *proof reconstruction and critical analysis* rather than new mechanization:

1. **Source consolidation.** We assemble the primary artifacts: the CHERI ISA v8 report [2], the `sail-morello` model and Isabelle proof corpus [4,5], the ESOP 2022 proof paper [5], the Nienhuis et al. methodology [6], universal contracts [7], and the temporal-safety literature [8].
2. **Formal-statement extraction.** We isolate the precise theorem statements — reachable capability monotonicity, its per-instruction decomposition, the abstraction relation, and the enumerated exceptions — in a uniform notation.
3. **Trust-base analysis.** We enumerate what the proofs assume: the ASL→Sail translation, Sail's Isabelle backend, the Isabelle kernel and AFP libraries, and the gap between ISA model and silicon.
4. **Empirical reconstruction.** We collect the reported quantitative evidence: proof size, compute cost, and the testing infrastructure validating the model against hardware.

We do not re-run the Isabelle proof (reported at ~7.5 CPU-hours and 18 GB peak memory on an i7-10510U [4]); instead, we analyze its *structure* to extract reusable verification principles.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Capability representation: the 129-bit token and CHERI Concentrate

On Morello, every general-purpose register holds a 129-bit value: 128 bits of capability content plus one tag bit [4]. The content encodes the address cursor together with compressed bounds and permissions. The compression scheme, CHERI Concentrate, represents a capability's *base* and *top* using a floating-point-like format with an exponent, trading exact representability of arbitrary ranges for a compact encoding that is exact for all "normal" allocations (power-of-two-aligned objects up to large sizes) and conservatively rounded otherwise. The security-relevant invariant is that decoding is *monotone*: the representable bounds always *contain* the requested bounds, never shrink them, so imprecision can only grant *no more* authority than requested — and any attempt to widen bounds clears the tag.

The tag bit is the linchpin of unforgeability. Consider the tag-propagation rules as a small transition system:

| Operation | Tag outcome |
|---|---|
| Capability-aware instruction (e.g., `CSetBounds`) on tagged capability | tag preserved |
| Integer store overwriting capability memory | tag cleared |
| Bytewise `memcpy` of capability | tag cleared (copied as data) |
| Capability load/store (`LDP`/`STP` with capability semantics) | tag preserved iff source tagged and access authorized |
| Capability written outside its bounds | exception; no write occurs |

This table captures *intentionality* at the hardware level: authority flows only through instructions that explicitly name capabilities, and ordinary data manipulation can never mint authority [2].

### 4.2 Monotonicity: the one property that rules them all

> **Theorem (Reachable capability monotonicity, informal).** For any execution of arbitrary code on the Morello ISA, the set of capabilities *reachable* by that code — held in registers or loadable from reachable memory — can never grow in authority: no new capability appears whose bounds are wider or whose permissions are greater than those of the capabilities from which it was derived, except through the well-defined exception mechanisms (sealing, exception entry/return).

Formally, the proof works over an abstraction of the concrete capability format — a "t-cheri" model [4] — and defines a *derivation relation* `c' ⊑ c` ("`c'` is monotonically derivable from `c`"):

- `bounds(c') ⊆ bounds(c)` and `perms(c') ⊆ perms(c)`;
- `otype(c')` is either unsealed or a validly sealed transition;
- `tag(c') = 1 ⟹ tag(c) = 1` along the derivation chain (validity is inherited, never created).

*Reachability* closes this relation over the machine state: a capability is reachable if it is in a register, or stored in memory that is itself reachable through a chain of authorized loads. The theorem then states that a step of execution cannot enlarge the reachable set's authority. This is the property that makes **compartmentalization** enforceable: a compartment given only narrow capabilities *cannot*, by any sequence of instructions, escape them [5,6].

Three aspects deserve emphasis:

1. **It is a property of arbitrary code.** Unlike per-instruction lemmas, the top-level theorem quantifies over *any* instruction sequence — including attacker-controlled code. This is what elevates it from a functional-correctness result to a *security* guarantee.
2. **It is reachability-indexed, not state-indexed.** The guarantee is relative to what the compartment can already touch, which is exactly the granularity at which software compartmentalization policies are expressed.
3. **It admits exceptions.** Sealed capabilities, sentry invocations, and exception handling deliberately break pure monotonicity; the proof characterizes these as a closed, enumerated set of *domain transitions* rather than as violations.

### 4.3 Sealing, sentries, and compartmentalization

Pure monotonicity would make an OS impossible: a kernel must *amplify* authority when servicing a system call, and compartments must be able to invoke each other across trust boundaries. CHERI provides this through **sealed capabilities** and **sentry capabilities**:

- *Sealing* binds a capability to an *object type* (`otype`), rendering it opaque: it cannot be dereferenced or modified until unsealed by a holder of the matching unseal capability. A sealed code/data pair constitutes a *compartment entry point*.
- A *sentry* capability is a sealed executable capability that, when jumped to, performs an atomic domain transition: the caller's registers are saved, the callee's sealed data capability is installed, and execution continues under the callee's authority — which was *delegated* to it at setup time, not manufactured at call time.

In the Morello proof, these mechanisms appear as the "well-defined exceptions" in the monotonicity statement: the four per-instruction properties explicitly account for the instructions that seal, unseal, and invoke sentries, showing that each such transition moves authority only along pre-authorized delegation edges [5]. The compartment-monotonicity corollary then reads: *code executing within a compartment, bounded by its reachable capabilities, can obtain new authority only by invoking a sealed entry point whose target was granted that authority by a more privileged party.*

### 4.4 The proof decomposition: four properties per instruction

Proving a whole-ISA property directly is intractable: Morello's Sail model spans the full AArch64 ISA plus CHERI extensions. The Bauereiss et al. methodology decomposes reachable capability monotonicity into **four properties of arbitrary single-instruction execution** [5]:

1. **Capability monotonicity of results.** Every capability produced by an instruction (in registers or memory) is monotonically derivable from the capabilities available before the instruction, modulo the enumerated exceptions.
2. **Memory-access authorization.** Every memory access performed by the instruction is authorized by a capability held beforehand with the appropriate permissions and covering bounds — formalized in `CHERI_Mem_Properties.thy`.
3. **Fetch authorization.** Instruction fetch checks the *Execute* permission (not merely *Load*) on the program-counter capability — `CHERI_Fetch_Properties.thy`.
4. **Invariant preservation.** Helper functions and instructions preserve the invariants (e.g., on the tag directory, sealed-capability well-formedness) on which the global proof depends — `CHERI_Invariant.thy`, with per-function lemmas in `CHERI_Cap_Properties.thy`.

These per-instruction lemmas are *generated* from the Sail model and then checked by Isabelle, so the lemma-generation tool itself is untrusted [4]. Key properties of individual capability-manipulating functions (e.g., monotonicity of `CapSetBounds`) were additionally discharged with Sail's SMT tooling, which uncovered specification bugs before the Isabelle proof was developed [4]. The global theorem follows by induction over the execution trace: since each step preserves the invariant, no finite trace can manufacture authority.

### 4.5 The ASL→Sail→Isabelle pipeline

The Morello verification rests on a translation pipeline whose every stage is engineered for auditability [4]:

1. **Arm ASL → Sail** via `asl_to_sail`: Arm's authoritative specification is machine-translated into the Sail model (`sail-morello`).
2. **Sail → Isabelle/HOL**: Sail's backend generates Isabelle definitions of the ISA semantics.
3. **Lemma generation**: per-instruction proof obligations are generated as `.thy` files.
4. **Isabelle checking**: the full proof is machine-checked (Isabelle 2020 + AFP `Word_Lib`).

The pipeline also *tested* the model: a test suite was automatically generated from weekly ASL snapshots *during* Morello's architectural development, so the formal model co-evolved with the architecture rather than being fitted after the fact [4] — the "rigorous engineering" methodology of Nienhuis et al. [6] scaled to an industry ISA.

## 5. Empirical Evaluation / Formal Proofs

### 5.1 Proof scale and cost

The `sail-morello-proofs` corpus [4] is organized into theory files mirroring the decomposition: `CHERI_Cap_Properties.thy`, `CHERI_Mem_Properties.thy`, `CHERI_Fetch_Properties.thy`, and `CHERI_Invariant.thy`. The reported cost of checking the complete proof is approximately **7.5 CPU-hours (3.5 hours wall-clock) with a peak memory footprint of 18 GB** on an i7-10510U [4] — substantial but routine for whole-ISA verification, and notably achieved with a high degree of automation rather than manual proof-script heroics.

| Effort | Scale | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| ISA modeled | Full AArch64 + CHERI (Morello) | Industry architecture, not a research subset |
| Property proved | Reachable capability monotonicity | Up to enumerated domain transitions |
| Proof assistant | Isabelle/HOL 2020 + AFP | `Word_Lib` for bitvector reasoning |
| Check time | ~7.5 CPU-h / 3.5 h wall | i7-10510U, 18 GB peak |
| Pre-proof bug finding | Sail SMT checks on `CapSetBounds` etc. | Spec bugs caught before proof |

### 5.2 Testing as empirical grounding

Formal proof establishes that the *model* has the property; testing establishes that the model matches *reality*. The Morello effort generated ISA test suites automatically from the Sail model derived from weekly ASL snapshots, running them against implementations during architectural development [4]. This closed the loop in the direction proof alone cannot: model↔silicon conformance. The CHERI-MIPS effort similarly used generated emulators as golden models for hardware prototypes, booting CheriBSD above them [2,6].

### 5.3 What the theorem buys — and what it does not

**It buys:** a machine-checked guarantee that the ISA's capability mechanisms cannot be subverted by *any* instruction sequence to forge or amplify authority — the precise property on which all CHERI software compartmentalization (library sandboxing, process isolation, CheriABI) depends. Combined with the tag-integrity rules, it yields *spatial* memory safety for correctly compiled C/C++ [2].

**It does not buy:**

- *Temporal safety.* Use-after-free is outside the proved property; pointer-revocation mechanisms and systems like FRESCO [8] address it separately.
- *Side-channel freedom.* The proof is over the sequential ISA semantics; speculative-execution and timing channels require separate contracts (cf. the "safe speculation" extensions surveyed in [8]).
- *Silicon correctness.* The proof is about the Sail model generated from ASL; microarchitectural bugs in the Morello implementation are a distinct verification target (the ESOP 2022 paper addresses the modeling-to-implementation relationship [5]).
- *Full capability safety.* The authors explicitly note that proving the stronger property — that compartments can *only* affect state through their capabilities — does not yet scale to industry architectures with current separation-logic automation [5,7].

## 6. Limitations and Open Problems

1. **Scaling to capability safety.** Reachable monotonicity says authority cannot be *manufactured*; full capability safety would additionally say authority cannot be *misused* — e.g., that a compartment's observable effects are confined to its reachable state. Bauereiss et al. report this stronger property is beyond current automation at Morello's scale [5]; universal contracts [7] and the Katamaran verifier are the active research direction.
2. **Temporal safety and revocation.** Capabilities as specified are *spatial*: they say nothing about lifetime. CHERIvoke characterized pointer revocation [6]; FRESCO proposes complete temporal safety [8]. A unified, proved spatio-temporal contract remains open.
3. **Composition with software verification.** The ISA guarantee is necessary but not sufficient: the compiler (CHERI LLVM), the OS (CheriBSD), and the compartmentalization framework must each preserve the property. Verified compilation for capability machines and OS-level proofs that compose with the ISA contract are largely future work.
4. **Speculative and concurrent semantics.** The proved property is sequential and non-speculative. Extending universal contracts to cover transient execution, and reconciling capability monotonicity with relaxed-memory concurrency (Morello is a multicore AArch64 machine), are open problems.
5. **Proof maintenance.** Architectures evolve; the proof must evolve with them. The universal-contracts agenda [7] — stating properties abstractly enough to survive ISA refactoring — is as much an engineering discipline as a logical one, and its long-term viability at industry scale is unproven.
6. **The translation trust base.** The ASL→Sail translation and Sail's Isabelle backend are trusted but unverified. A bug in `asl_to_sail` could in principle prove a property of a model that diverges from Arm's intent; differential testing mitigates but does not eliminate this risk.

---

## 7. Conclusion

The verification of capability monotonicity for Arm Morello is a landmark in hardware security: the first machine-checked proof of the central security property of a *full-scale industry capability architecture*, carried out over a formal ISA semantics from which documentation, emulators, tests, and proof definitions are all generated. Its methodology — decompose a global security property into per-instruction obligations, generate the obligations from the Sail model, check them in Isabelle/HOL, and validate the model against silicon by generated testing — offers a reusable template for certifying ISA security guarantees.

Yet the work is also candid about its boundaries: reachable monotonicity, not full capability safety; spatial, not temporal; sequential, not speculative; model, not silicon. The deeper lesson is methodological: *security properties worth proving are worth specifying formally first*, and the Sail-based "one specification, many artifacts" discipline is what made a whole-industry-ISA proof possible at all. Certified capability machines are no longer a research aspiration; they are a proved, if partial, reality — and the partiality is precisely mapped, which is what makes the next steps tractable.
