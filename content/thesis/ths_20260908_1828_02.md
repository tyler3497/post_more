# RDMA Congestion Control: DCQCN, TIMELY, HPCC, and Swift for Lossless Data Center Fabrics

## Abstract

Remote Direct Memory Access over Converged Ethernet v2 (RoCEv2) delivers kernel-bypass messaging at line rate, yet it rests on a precarious foundation: lossless Ethernet provided by Priority Flow Control (PFC), a hop-by-hop brake that reacts to buffer exhaustion rather than to congestion. When hundreds of flows incast onto a single port, PFC pause frames cascade into storms and even routing deadlock, while end-to-end TCP-style control is unavailable inside NIC firmware. This thesis surveys the four landmark congestion controllers that made large-scale RDMA deployment possible — DCQCN, TIMELY, HPCC, and Swift — through a unified lens of signal richness, feedback delay, and hardware cost. We reconstruct DCQCN's quantized ECN/CNP loop, TIMELY's RTT-gradient controller, HPCC's in-network-telemetry rate equation, and Swift's decomposed-delay AIMD; evaluate them against incast bursts, fairness, queue oscillation, and PFC pressure; and derive when each scheme dominates, where each fails, and what a next-generation programmable fabric must provide.

---

## 1. Introduction

The modern datacenter runs on Remote Direct Memory Access. Distributed storage, GPU collectives, and in-memory analytics demand microsecond messaging with zero CPU on the data path, and only RDMA semantics — single-sided reads and writes executed entirely by the NIC — deliver it [1]. The dominant vehicle is RoCEv2, which encapsulates the InfiniBand transport in UDP/IP datagrams so RDMA can traverse ordinary IP-routed fabrics.

Yet RoCEv2 inherits a fundamental tension from its InfiniBand ancestry: the transport assumes a *lossless* network. InfiniBand achieves losslessness with hop-by-hop, credit-based flow control in purpose-built hardware. Ethernet has no native equivalent; the industry grafted Priority Flow Control (PFC, IEEE 802.1Qbb) onto switches instead, letting a receiver emit PAUSE frames that halt a priority class upstream until buffers drain. PFC is a blunt instrument: it prevents drops but does nothing to *resolve* congestion, and when pause frames propagate hop by hop the pause graph can close into a cycle — PFC deadlock, a partial or total fabric freeze [5].

End-to-end congestion control is therefore the load-bearing wall of every RoCEv2 fabric. Kernel bypass forces the controller into NIC hardware or firmware, reacting within one or two round trips (single-digit microseconds) and surviving the defining datacenter workload: *incast*, the synchronized many-to-one burst in which hundreds of senders simultaneously target one receiver. This thesis examines the four algorithms that defined the field:

1. **DCQCN** (Zhu et al., SIGCOMM 2015) — quantized ECN feedback, three-point NIC architecture, Microsoft's production workhorse [1];
2. **TIMELY** (Mittal et al., SIGCOMM 2015) — RTT-gradient control from NIC timestamps, no switch support needed [2];
3. **HPCC** (Li et al., SIGCOMM 2019) — per-hop in-network telemetry driving a direct rate equation [3];
4. **Swift** (Kumar et al., SIGCOMM 2020) — delay-based control at Google, decomposing host and fabric queueing [4].

We compare them on common ground: signal, feedback delay, hardware cost, and behavior under incast, fairness, and PFC pressure.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 RoCEv2 and the lossless imperative

RoCEv2 maps the InfiniBand queue-pair abstraction onto UDP/IP (destination port 4791). The NIC segments, reassembles, acknowledges, and retransmits entirely in hardware; the host CPU registers memory and posts work requests, then steps aside. Because go-back-N retransmission over RoCE is punishing — one dropped packet stalls a queue pair — the fabric must not drop packets. Switches therefore run PFC on the RoCE priority, buying losslessness at the cost of pause propagation [5].

### 2.2 ECN, RED, and why TCP signals do not transfer

Explicit Congestion Notification (ECN, RFC 3168) [6] lets a switch mark a packet's IP header instead of dropping it when its queue exceeds a RED threshold. TCP receivers echo the mark via ECE flags and the sender cuts its window. RoCEv2 breaks this loop twice: UDP has no acknowledgment stream for an echo, and the controller lives in NIC firmware rather than the kernel. RoCEv2 controllers must therefore invent their own back-channel — DCQCN's Congestion Notification Packet (CNP) — or abandon switch feedback entirely, as TIMELY and Swift do with RTT.

### 2.3 Incast: the defining workload

*Incast* describes synchronized many-to-one traffic: a storage client fanning reads across hundreds of servers, a shuffle phase gathering partitions, or a GPU all-reduce. If *N* senders each burst *B* bytes at a receiver whose port buffer holds *Q* bytes, losslessness demands *Q ≥ N·B* — an inequality no commodity switch buffer can satisfy at datacenter fan-in. The result is synchronized queue growth, PFC pauses fired within microseconds, and synchronized rate cuts followed by synchronized recoveries: *global synchronization*, a persistent oscillation the DCQCN authors explicitly characterize [1].

### 2.4 PFC storms, deadlock, and deadlock-free routing

A PFC pause halts an entire priority class on a link, not a single flow. When pauses propagate hop by hop, the fabric can enter a *PFC storm*, and if the pause-dependency graph contains a cycle, *deadlock*: switches waiting on each other forever. Deadlock-free routing (up/down routing, virtual-channel separation, escape paths) addresses the topology but not the queue buildup that fires pauses [5]. The congestion controller's job: keep queues shallow enough that the emergency brake is never pulled.

### 2.5 QCN: the direct ancestor

DCQCN descends directly from Quantized Congestion Notification (QCN, IEEE 802.1Qau), the Layer-2 datacenter bridging controller. QCN's three-point architecture — Congestion Point, Notification Point, Reaction Point — and its feedback-proportional rate decrease survive almost intact in DCQCN, transplanted from Layer 2 to Layer 3 for IP-routed fabrics [1].

## 3. Methodology

1. **Reconstruct the control law** of each scheme from its primary publication in common notation;
2. **Classify the congestion signal** along a richness ladder — binary ECN mark, RTT gradient, decomposed delay, full per-hop telemetry — and derive the consequences for convergence speed and oscillation;
3. **Evaluate against a common workload battery** drawn from the papers' own experiments: incast fan-in, fairness, queue-length time series, PFC pause volume, and short-flow completion time;
4. **Verify empirical claims** against reported numbers and independent reproductions;
5. **Stress-test the boundary conditions**: parameter sensitivity, hardware availability (notably P4 ASICs for INT), PFC-deadlock interaction, and sub-RTT bursts no end-to-end loop can catch.

The analysis is control-theoretic throughout: every scheme is a feedback loop with a sensor (the signal), a controller (the rate law), an actuator (the NIC pacer), and a delay (the feedback RTT). Performance differences reduce, in the end, to these four components.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 DCQCN: quantized ECN feedback and the three-point architecture

DCQCN is the end-to-end controller Microsoft co-designed with Mellanox and deployed across its datacenters; it remains the default on NVIDIA ConnectX NICs [1]. Three roles:

- **Congestion Point (CP)** — the switch, running RED per egress queue: no marking below *K_min*, probabilistic marking up to *P_max* between *K_min* and *K_max*, mark-everything above *K_max* (production values at 40 Gbps: 5 KB, 200 KB, 1%).
- **Notification Point (NP)** — the receiver NIC, emitting a Congestion Notification Packet (CNP) per ECN-marked packet, rate-limited to one per flow per 50 µs.
- **Reaction Point (RP)** — the sender NIC, holding current rate *R_C*, target rate *R_T*, and congestion estimate *α ∈ [0, 1]*, an EWMA of the marked-bytes fraction:

```python
# DCQCN reaction point
g = 1/256            # alpha decay factor
R_AI = 40e6          # additive increase step (bps)
TIMER = 55e-6        # rate-increase timer
BYTE_COUNTER = 10e6  # bytes between increase phases

def on_cnp(alpha, R_C, F):
    alpha = (1 - g) * alpha + g * F          # F: marked-byte fraction
    R_C = R_C * (1 - alpha / 2)              # multiplicative cut, scaled by alpha
    R_T = R_C
    return alpha, R_C, R_T

def on_timer(alpha, R_C, R_T, phase):
    if phase == 'fast_recovery':             # first 5 expiries: binary search
        R_C = (R_C + R_T) / 2
    elif phase == 'active_increase':
        R_T = R_T + R_AI
    return R_C, R_T
```

The elegance of DCQCN lies in *α*: severe congestion drives *α → 1* and the cut approaches halving; light marking keeps *α* small and the cut gentle. Fast recovery binary-searches back toward the pre-congestion rate, active increase probes upward additively, and hyperactive increase (timer and byte counter both expiring without further CNPs) doubles the target after idle periods. The paper shows fast convergence to fairness, high utilization, and shallow queues on a large Clos testbed, with far fewer PFC pauses than uncontrolled traffic [1].

Yet the binary signal carries one bit per marked packet, and feedback traverses receiver, switch, and sender: roughly two RTTs from queue buildup to rate cut. Under heavy incast, every sender receives CNPs simultaneously, cuts simultaneously, and recovers simultaneously — the global synchronization of §2.3. Tuning *g*, the timer, and the RED thresholds trades stability against utilization, and the right values drift with link speed, topology, and workload.

### 4.2 TIMELY: RTT-gradient control on commodity NICs

TIMELY (Mittal et al., Google, SIGCOMM 2015) [2] asked a heretical question: what if the sender needs no switch feedback at all? The classical objection was measurement noise — microsecond queueing signals drowned in software timestamp jitter. The paper's first contribution is empirical: modern NICs with hardware timestamping and OS-bypass completion events measure RTT with microsecond accuracy, and those RTTs track switch queue occupancy almost perfectly.

TIMELY's second contribution is the *gradient*. Rather than comparing absolute RTT against a threshold (as TCP Vegas does), TIMELY reacts to the *rate of change* of RTT — an earlier signal, since rising RTT indicates growing queues before any threshold is crossed:

```python
# TIMELY gradient controller (simplified)
beta, delta = 0.8, 10e6      # decrease factor, additive step (bps)

def timely_update(rate, rtt, rtt_prev, interval):
    gradient = (rtt - rtt_prev) / interval
    if gradient >= 0:
        rate = rate * (1 - beta * min(abs(gradient), 1.0))
    else:
        rate = rate + delta
    return rate
```

The gradient is normalized and low-pass filtered against noise; separate thresholds (*T_low*, *T_high* relative to minimum RTT) catch persistent queueing a pure gradient would miss. Implemented in host software over OS-bypass NICs and evaluated on Clos topologies with hundreds of machines, TIMELY cut 99th-percentile tail latency by roughly **9×** over a PFC-enabled fabric while sustaining near-line-rate throughput [2].

TIMELY's signal is strictly richer than DCQCN's — multi-bit delay versus a binary mark — and it needs no switch features, making it deployable on commodity silicon. Its weaknesses are the mirror image: RTT is a *lumped* signal that cannot localize the congested hop, confounds forward- and reverse-path queueing, and is polluted by path changes and host scheduling noise. Under severe incast, delay-based schemes converge more slowly than ECN-based ones, and they still react no faster than one RTT.

> **Theorem (Delay-signal informativeness):** *For a bottleneck queue with drain rate C and arrival rate λ(t), the RTT gradient d(RTT)/dt is proportional to (λ − C)/C whenever the queue is non-empty, providing a strictly earlier congestion indication than any queue-threshold mark, at the cost of conflating all queues on the path into a single scalar.*

### 4.3 HPCC: in-network telemetry and the precision rate equation

HPCC (Li et al., SIGCOMM 2019) [3] rejects the premise that the sender must *infer* congestion from impoverished signals. Every switch inserts In-band Network Telemetry (INT) into each data packet — queue length, transmitted bytes, timestamp, link bandwidth — and the receiver reflects the per-hop record to the sender. The sender thus learns the *exact* load of every hop, including the bottleneck.

The control law is strikingly direct. For each hop *i*, the sender computes link utilization over window *T*:

$$U_i = \frac{\text{qlen}_i}{B_i \cdot T} + \frac{\text{txRate}_i}{B_i}, \qquad U_{\max} = \max_i U_i$$

and sets its window from a per-RTT reference value *W_ref*:

$$W = \frac{W_{ref}}{U_{\max} / \eta} + W_{AI}, \qquad W_{ref} \leftarrow \frac{W}{U_{\max} / \eta} + W_{AI} \text{ each RTT}$$

where *η* = 0.95 is the target utilization and *W_AI* a small additive term. A bottleneck 50% over target cuts the window to two-thirds in one step — no probing, no binary search. HPCC reacts *per ACK* against the slowly updated *W_ref*, reacting fast without overreacting, and its *inflight-bytes* window directly bounds queue buildup. The paper proves rapid convergence to Pareto-optimal allocation then fairness, prototyped on FPGA NICs with P4 ASICs inserting standard INT records [3][8].

Empirically, HPCC dominates: large-scale PFC pauses appear only under DCQCN and TIMELY; adding a sending window to either reduces pauses to near zero, confirming inflight control as the key stability lever; and HPCC achieves the shallowest queues with the least oscillation. The price is hardware: *every* switch must insert INT (4 hops × ~20 bytes ≈ 80 bytes per packet — negligible for 64 KB messages, a 16% tax on 512-byte RPCs), and INT insertion needs P4-capable ASICs (Tofino-class) or vendor extensions, still absent from much fixed-function merchant silicon. Later work trims this cost with probabilistic telemetry (PINT/HPCC++) and faster notification (FNCC) [5], but the fundamental trade — precision for programmability — remains.

> **Theorem (HPCC convergence, Li et al. [3], paraphrased):** *For arbitrary topologies with multiple bottleneck links, HPCC's per-RTT reference update converges rapidly to a Pareto-optimal rate allocation; subsequent per-ACK adjustments converge the allocation to max-min fairness. The convergence rate is independent of the number of flows sharing a bottleneck.*

### 4.4 Swift: delay-based simplicity at hyperscale

Swift (Kumar et al., Google, SIGCOMM 2020) [4] is the production synthesis of the delay-based line: a single controller for an entire datacenter's traffic, from microsecond RPCs to bulk transfers, on lossy and lossless fabrics. Its central insight is that *delay must be decomposed*. A measured RTT conflates fabric queueing with host-side queueing (a saturated PCIe path, a delayed application), and reacting to host delay by cutting the *network* rate is wrong and destabilizing. Swift uses host-side timestamping to separate the two and applies its AIMD law only to fabric delay: decrease multiplicatively in proportion to the excess over a *target delay*, increase additively otherwise, and handle host-congestion episodes with host-local mechanisms rather than throttling the fabric rate.

Deployed across Google's business-critical workloads, Swift sustains per-server throughput near 100 Gbps at full load with low queueing delays under bursty incast; the paper reports DCTCP loss rates at least 10× higher at moderate-to-high load, and near-baseline latency for short RPCs even under O(10⁴)-flow incast [4]. Where TIMELY showed delay *could* work, Swift proves it *does* work at hyperscale, with the pacing, flow mapping, and multitenant isolation production demands.

## 5. Empirical Results and Proofs

The four schemes were evaluated in different eras and testbeds, so direct numeric comparison requires care; Table 1 aligns them on the dimensions the papers report.

| Dimension | DCQCN [1] | TIMELY [2] | HPCC [3] | Swift [4] |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Year / venue | 2015, SIGCOMM | 2015, SIGCOMM | 2019, SIGCOMM | 2020, SIGCOMM |
| Congestion signal | Binary ECN/CNP | RTT gradient | Per-hop INT telemetry | Decomposed fabric delay |
| Feedback path | Switch → receiver → sender (~2 RTT) | Sender-measured (~1 RTT) | Switch → receiver → sender (~1–2 RTT) | Sender-measured (~1 RTT) |
| Hardware required | RED/ECN switches + capable NICs | NIC hw timestamps | P4 ASICs (INT) + FPGA NICs | Host pacing + timestamps |
| Headline result | Production RoCE at Microsoft | 9× lower p99 latency | Near-zero PFC pauses | ~100 Gbps/server, 10× lower loss than DCTCP |
| Incast behavior | Global synchronization oscillation | Slower convergence | Fast, precise per-hop reaction | Low delay at O(10⁴)-flow incast |

Three cross-cutting findings deserve emphasis. **First**, HPCC's experiments show *controlling inflight bytes* is the decisive stability mechanism: grafting a window onto DCQCN and TIMELY collapses their PFC pause volume to nearly zero — much of the gap between ECN-based and telemetry-based schemes is really a rate-versus-window gap [3]. **Second**, TIMELY's study establishes microsecond-accurate RTT on commodity NICs tracking true queue depth — validated by Swift at planetary scale [2][4]. **Third**, Swift shows a *single* delay-based algorithm can replace a menagerie of specialized controllers, serving microsecond RPCs and bulk flows on lossy and lossless fabrics alike [4].

Theoretically, HPCC's convergence proof is the strongest of the four: Pareto optimality on the fast timescale, fairness on the slow, for arbitrary topologies [3]. DCQCN's analysis establishes fairness under quantized feedback but says less about adversarial incast, where synchronized feedback oscillates [1]. Delay-based stability rests on keeping the delay target small relative to the bandwidth-delay product and on pacing — unpaced delay-based control at 100+ Gbps overshoots on bursts, which is why TIMELY and Swift both pace [2][4].

Follow-on work sharpens the picture: FNCC observes HPCC's notification path costs at least an RTT and proposes switch-to-sender fast notification [5]; TOD (APNet 2023) builds a trend-oriented delay controller converging an order of magnitude faster than DCQCN and TIMELY for sub-120 KB flows [7].

## 6. Limitations

No scheme escapes the constraints of end-to-end control over a lossless fabric.

- **DCQCN** is parameter-sensitive (RED thresholds, *g*, timer, byte counter interact and drift with link speed), its binary signal and two-RTT feedback make it slowest on incast, and global synchronization remains unsolved.
- **TIMELY** inherits lumped-signal weaknesses: no hop localization, confusion of host/reverse-path queueing with fabric congestion, sensitivity to route changes, plus gradient-filter knobs (*β*, *δ*, *T_low*, *T_high*) that misconfigure into sluggishness or oscillation.
- **HPCC** demands the most hardware: INT on every switch (P4 ASICs), per-packet overhead on small RPCs, programmable NICs for the per-ACK loop. Where any are absent it cannot run — hence DCQCN's persistence on fixed-function silicon.
- **Swift** pays in host-stack complexity: two-ended timestamping, pacing, RPC-aware scheduling; its target delay is workload-dependent, and it loses to ECN-based control against buffer-filling loss-based flows.

Above all, *no end-to-end controller prevents PFC deadlock by itself*. Deadlock is a property of the pause-dependency graph, addressed by deadlock-free routing and buffer management; congestion control only reduces how often the emergency brake is pulled [5]. And every scheme reacts on RTT timescales — sub-RTT bursts will always fire some PFC pauses. The controller's job is to make those pauses rare, brief, and non-propagating.

## 7. Conclusion

The decade from DCQCN to Swift traces a clear arc: the congestion signal grows steadily richer — from a single ECN bit, to a multi-bit RTT gradient, to decomposed fabric delay, to full per-hop telemetry — and each enrichment buys faster convergence, shallower queues, and fewer PFC pauses at the cost of greater hardware demands. DCQCN proved large-scale RoCEv2 is deployable with only RED and ECN from switches; TIMELY proved precise delay measurement obviates switch support; HPCC proved per-hop telemetry enables near-optimal control; Swift proved delay-based control scales to a hyperscaler's production traffic.

The practical decision reduces to fabric capability. On commodity fixed-function silicon, DCQCN (increasingly with window-based augmentations) remains the rational default. Where hosts control their stack and NICs timestamp precisely, delay-based control — TIMELY's gradient or Swift's decomposed AIMD — delivers lower tail latency without switch upgrades. Where P4 ASICs and programmable NICs are available, HPCC's telemetry-driven precision is unmatched, and its descendants keep lowering its cost.

Looking forward, three frontiers stand out. *Faster-than-RTT notification* (FNCC-style switch-to-sender signals) attacks the residual feedback delay all four schemes share [5]. *Programmable data planes* (P4) are moving telemetry — and even parts of the control law — into the switch, blurring the end-to-end boundary HPCC already strained [8]. And *receiver-driven* designs (Homa, NDP) question the sender-centric orthodoxy entirely. Whichever prevails, the lesson of this decade is durable: in lossless fabrics, congestion control is not an optimization — it is what stands between line-rate RDMA and a deadlocked network.
