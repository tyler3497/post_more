# Exactly-Once Semantics in Distributed Stream Processing: Asynchronous Barrier Snapshotting in Apache Flink, Transactional Idempotent Producers in Apache Kafka, MillWheel Watermarks and Low-Watermark Progress, and Deterministic Replay for End-to-End Correctness

## Abstract

Guaranteeing that every record in an unbounded stream is processed exactly once — neither lost nor duplicated — remains one of the central problems of distributed data engineering. This thesis gives a rigorous treatment of exactly-once semantics as they are achieved in modern stream processors, decomposing the problem into four orthogonal mechanisms: (i) *consistent global state capture* via asynchronous barrier snapshots, the dataflow incarnation of the Chandy–Lamport distributed snapshot algorithm; (ii) *idempotent, transactional output* via Kafka's producer-identifier/sequence-number protocol and transaction coordinator, which converts the producer's ambiguity under retries into a provably duplicate-free write path; (iii) *event-time progress* via MillWheel's low-watermark protocol and its descendants, which transform an unbounded temporal problem into a sequence of well-defined epoch boundaries; and (iv) *deterministic replay* of replayable logs, which closes the loop by making recovery a re-execution rather than a re-computation. We formalize each mechanism, prove the key composition theorem — that the conjunction of exactly-once state recovery, transactional sinks, and deterministic replay yields *end-to-end effectively-once* observable behavior — and quantify the latency, throughput, and state-size trade-offs that real systems pay. The analysis is grounded in Apache Flink's checkpointing engine, Apache Kafka's transactional messaging (KIP-98), Google's MillWheel, and the Dataflow Model's watermark semantics.

## 1. Introduction

Stream processing systems promise to turn unbounded, continuously arriving data into actionable results with low latency. The promise is conditional on a subtle correctness contract: when a machine fails mid-stream — and at scale, machines fail constantly — the system must recover without losing records and without emitting duplicates. The classical taxonomy distinguishes *at-most-once* (results may be lost), *at-least-once* (results may be duplicated), and *exactly-once* (results coincide with a failure-free execution). The third is the one users want and the one that is genuinely hard.

Exactly-once is hard for a structural reason. A distributed stream processor is a directed dataflow graph whose operators hold *keyed mutable state*, exchange records over asynchronous channels, and emit side effects to external systems. Failure recovery must reconstruct a coherent past for all of this at once. The problem decomposes, usefully, into four sub-problems, each of which has been solved at least once in the literature and in production systems, and which this thesis unifies:

1. **Consistent state capture.** How does one record every operator's state such that the collection forms a *consistent cut*? The answer is the Chandy–Lamport snapshot algorithm [2], adapted to dataflow as *asynchronous barrier snapshotting* [1].
2. **Output atomicity and idempotence.** Recovering state exactly once is insufficient if the sink re-emits already-emitted results. Kafka's idempotent producer and transactional writes (KIP-98) [4] solve the *output* half via producer fencing and two-phase commit.
3. **Event-time progress.** Real data is out of order. Watermarks — introduced by MillWheel [3] and generalized in the Dataflow Model [5] — give a principled notion of temporal completeness for windows, joins, and state retention.
4. **Deterministic replay.** Given a consistent checkpoint, a transactional sink, and epoch boundaries, recovery reduces to rewinding sources to checkpointed offsets and re-executing deterministically. Replayability of the log is the bedrock assumption that makes everything else compose.

The thesis's central claim is compositional: **no single mechanism yields end-to-end exactly-once; the conjunction of all four yields effectively-once observable behavior**, and dropping any one of them degrades the guarantee to at-least-once output or at-most-once state. Section 5 states and proves this as a theorem.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 The delivery-semantics taxonomy

Under failures, three contracts are distinguished. **At-most-once**: each record's effects apply zero or one times (achieved trivially by never retrying). **At-least-once**: effects apply one or more times (retry without deduplication). **Exactly-once**: observable outputs and final state are *identical* to those of some failure-free execution — the only formulation well-defined under non-determinism, and strictly weaker than "every record is physically processed once," a physical impossibility under failure. We therefore speak of *effectively-once* semantics: duplicates may transiently exist inside the system (e.g., reprocessed records after rollback) but are never *observably* emitted [7].

### 2.2 Consistent cuts and the Chandy–Lamport algorithm

Chandy and Lamport's 1985 algorithm captures a consistent global state of a distributed system without halting it [2]. Processes record local state upon first receiving a *marker*; channel state is recorded as the messages received after local recording but before the marker arrives on that channel. The resulting cut satisfies the fundamental invariant:

> **Theorem (Consistent cut):** If a receive event is included in the snapshot, then the corresponding send event is also included — no message crosses the cut from the future side to the past side.

The algorithm assumes FIFO channels and is the direct ancestor of every barrier-based checkpointing scheme in stream processing.

### 2.3 Kafka's delivery model and KIP-98

Kafka, as a distributed commit log, natively offers at-least-once delivery: a producer that times out waiting for an acknowledgment cannot distinguish "request lost" from "response lost," so retrying may append the batch twice. KIP-98 introduced two cooperating mechanisms [4]: the **idempotent producer** (unique producer ID plus per-partition sequence numbers; the broker rejects stale batches, making retries safe) and **transactions** (a transaction coordinator runs two-phase commit across partitions; commit markers delimit transaction boundaries for `read_committed` consumers). Producer *fencing* via epochs ensures a zombie producer can never commit again.

### 2.4 MillWheel and the Dataflow Model

MillWheel (Google, 2013) [3] pioneered the combination of *fine-grained, per-record checkpointing* with a *low-watermark* notion of logical time: each computation tracks the minimum timestamp it has fully processed, and downstream watermarks are the *minimum* of upstream watermarks — a conservative, monotone progress guarantee. The Dataflow Model [5] generalized this into an algebra of *windows, watermarks, and triggers*, separating "when are results complete?" (watermarks), "when are they emitted?" (triggers), and "how are they revised?" (accumulation modes). Begoli et al. [6] give a comparative semantics of watermark implementations in Flink and Dataflow.

## 3. Methodology

Our method is analytic and compositional rather than experimental on a single codebase. We:

1. **Formalize** each of the four mechanisms as a small operational model — barrier alignment as a cut-construction procedure, Kafka transactions as a 2PC protocol with fencing, watermarks as a monotone progress function over event time, and replay as deterministic re-execution from a logged prefix.
2. **Prove** the composition theorem (Section 5) in the style of the Chandy–Lamport correctness argument, reducing end-to-end exactly-once to the conjunction of the four mechanism-level invariants.
3. **Quantify** the costs: barrier alignment latency as a function of channel skew, checkpoint size and frequency versus recovery time, transaction overhead on the produce path, and watermark heuristics under out-of-orderness. Where closed forms exist we derive them; where they do not, we report the known measurements from the cited systems.
4. **Delineate** the boundary: we exhibit, for each mechanism, a concrete failure scenario in which its absence collapses the guarantee (Section 6).

Notation: a dataflow graph *G = (V, E)*; operator *v ∈ V* holds state *S_v*; channel *(u, v) ∈ E* carries a FIFO record sequence; barrier *B_n* is the *n*-th checkpoint marker; *W_v(t)* is the watermark of operator *v* at processing time *t*; a Kafka transaction *T* spans partitions *P(T)*.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Asynchronous barrier snapshotting: Chandy–Lamport on a DAG

Flink's checkpointing [1] injects barriers at the sources and propagates them downstream. The dataflow setting simplifies Chandy–Lamport in one crucial way and complicates it in another. The simplification: in an acyclic dataflow, barriers travel along unidirectional channels, and an operator that has received a barrier on *all* inputs can snapshot immediately — no per-channel message recording is required, because any record arriving after the barrier on an input channel is definitionally post-snapshot. The complication: operators with multiple inputs must *align* barriers, buffering the fast channels while waiting for the slow one, which introduces backpressure proportional to channel skew.

> **Theorem (ABS consistent cut):** Let *B_n* be a barrier wave. The set of operator states recorded upon receipt of *B_n*, together with source offsets, forms a consistent cut: every record processed before the cut on any operator was emitted before the cut by its upstream.

*Proof sketch.* Barriers are FIFO-ordered with data on each channel, so an operator snapshots exactly the state from all pre-barrier records on every input and no post-barrier record. If *v* processed *r* before its snapshot, *r* arrived before *B_n*, hence upstream *u* emitted *r* before forwarding *B_n* and snapshotted *r*'s effects. By induction over the DAG, the cut is consistent. ∎

![Chandy-Lamport barrier snapshot alignment](public/thesis/ths_1788836918000_9f2a-0.webp)

Two refinements matter in practice. *Unaligned checkpoints* trade the alignment wait for logging in-flight records, bounding latency at the cost of larger snapshots — the same consistent cut, reached via channel-state recording à la the original algorithm [2]. *Incremental snapshots* (e.g., RocksDB SST diffing) reduce per-checkpoint write volume from *O(|S|)* to *O(|ΔS|)*, which is what makes frequent checkpointing affordable at terabyte state sizes.

A minimal executable model of the core idea — marker propagation with channel-state recording — is the Chandy–Lamport rule set itself [2]; Flink's barrier alignment is the same logic with "buffer data instead of recording channel state" as the multi-input policy:

```python
class Process:
    def __init__(self, pid):
        self.pid = pid
        self.state = 0
        self.recording = {}          # channel -> list of in-transit msgs
        self.snapshotted = False

    def on_message(self, msg, channel):
        if self.snapshotted and channel in self.recording:
            self.recording[channel].append(msg)   # in-transit channel state
        else:
            self.state += msg                     # ordinary processing

    def on_marker(self, channel, send):
        if not self.snapshotted:
            self.local_snapshot = self.state
            self.snapshotted = True
            for c in self.in_channels:
                if c != channel:
                    self.recording[c] = []        # start recording others
            send(self.pid)                        # propagate marker
        else:
            self.channel_snapshot[channel] = self.recording.pop(channel)
```

This is precisely the Chandy–Lamport rule set [2] in executable form.

### 4.2 Kafka transactions and the idempotent producer: making the output half exact

Barrier snapshots give *exactly-once state*, but users observe *outputs*: after restoring checkpoint *n*, reprocessing records *n+1…m* re-sends outputs already emitted before the crash. The output must therefore be made idempotent or transactional. Kafka's answer has two layers [4]:

1. **Idempotence (single partition).** The broker tracks, per (PID, partition), the greatest committed sequence number; a retried batch with a stale sequence number is acknowledged but *not* appended. Retries thus become fixed points of the log-append function, giving effectively-once appends per partition — provided the producer does not restart with a new PID.
2. **Transactions (multi-partition atomicity).** The producer brackets sends in `beginTransaction … commitTransaction`. The coordinator runs 2PC: `PREPARE_COMMIT` is first durably logged, then commit markers are appended to each involved partition. Consumers reading with `read_committed` observe a transaction's records iff its commit marker is present; aborted transactions are skipped by offset arithmetic. *Fencing* closes the zombie hole: `initTransactions` bumps the producer epoch, and any in-flight request from an older epoch is rejected, so a "dead" producer that is actually alive cannot interleave writes into a new transaction.

![Kafka transactional two-phase commit](public/thesis/ths_1788836918000_9f2a-1.webp)

The stream processor's *two-phase-commit sink* ties the two halves together: checkpoint barrier *B_n* triggers `preCommit` (flush the transaction), and checkpoint *completion* triggers `commitTransaction`, while recovery triggers `abortTransaction` for any transaction newer than the restored checkpoint. Offsets are committed *transactionally* via `sendOffsetsToTransaction`, atomically binding "I consumed up to offset *o*" with "I produced these outputs" — the read-process-write cycle becomes a single atomic unit.

| Mechanism | Scope | Failure mode it closes | Cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| Idempotent producer | single partition | duplicate appends on retry | sequence tracking per (PID, partition); `acks=all` |
| Transaction coordinator | multi-partition | partial multi-partition writes | 2PC latency; commit-marker amplification |
| Producer fencing (epochs) | producer identity | zombie-producer interleaving | epoch bump on `initTransactions` |
| `read_committed` consumers | read path | observing aborted data | log-end-offset bookkeeping per fetch |

### 4.3 Watermarks: turning unbounded time into decidable epochs

Exactly-once *state* is meaningless without a definition of *progress*: when is a window's result final, when may state be garbage-collected, and what does "replay from checkpoint *n*" mean for event-time windows? MillWheel's answer is the *low watermark* [3]: each computation maintains *W*, the greatest timestamp such that all records with timestamp ≤ *W* have been processed, and downstream watermarks are the *minimum* of upstream watermarks — a conservative, monotone progress guarantee.

The Dataflow Model [5] elevates this to a first-class semantic dimension, separating three questions: "when are results complete?" (**watermarks**, a lower bound on future event times; records below the watermark are *late*), "when are they emitted?" (**triggers** — on watermark arrival, processing-time timers, data arrival, or combinations), and "how are they revised?" (**accumulation modes** — discarding, accumulating, or accumulating-and-retracting).

The interaction with exactly-once is subtle and essential. A watermark is *derived state*: a deterministic function of the records seen. Under deterministic replay from a checkpoint, watermarks are recomputed identically, windows fire identically, and triggers emit identically — *provided* the watermark heuristic itself is deterministic (e.g., bounded-out-of-orderness with a fixed bound, or count-based rather than wall-clock-driven emission). A wall-clock-driven watermark generator breaks determinism across replays and can therefore break effectively-once windowed outputs even when state recovery is perfect — the least-appreciated coupling in the whole architecture.

![Watermark propagation in event-time windows](public/thesis/ths_1788836918000_9f2a-2.webp)

Formally, let *W_v* be monotone non-decreasing and let window assignment *ω(r)* and trigger *τ* be pure functions of (record, watermark history). Replaying the same record sequence from the same checkpointed watermark vector then reproduces the same pane sequence — the *watermark determinism lemma* on which Section 5's composition theorem depends.

### 4.4 Deterministic replay: the log as the ground truth of time

The fourth mechanism is the least glamorous and the most load-bearing: *the input log must be replayable*. Kafka's partitioned, offset-indexed log gives this almost for free — a source checkpoint stores (partition → offset), and recovery seeks and re-reads. Three properties make this work: **total order per partition** (offsets are dense, so "everything after *o*" is well-defined); **retention exceeding recovery distance** (otherwise recovery is impossible and the system must fall back to a savepoint); and **deterministic operators** (same input sequence plus same starting state yields same outputs — wall-clock reads, unseeded RNGs, and hash-map iteration order must be eliminated or logged into the checkpoint).

MillWheel's design is instructive here [3]: rather than periodic global snapshots, it checkpoints *per-record* — each record's processing is atomically committed with its state update — achieving effectively-once through fine-grained journaling plus deterministic re-execution. Flink's approach is coarser (periodic barriers) but the principle is identical: recovery = restore state + replay log suffix deterministically.

The end-to-end data path, composed: sources checkpoint offsets at *B_n*; operators snapshot state at *B_n*; the sink pre-commits *T_n* at the barrier and commits on checkpoint completion; on failure, restore to *n*, abort *T_{>n}*, seek sources to checkpointed offsets, and replay deterministically. Reprocessed records either overwrite already-restored state (never merged) or land in aborted transactions invisible to `read_committed` consumers — so observable behavior is indistinguishable from a failure-free run.

## 5. Empirical Evaluation / Proofs

### 5.1 The composition theorem

> **Theorem (End-to-end effectively-once):** Consider a dataflow *G* with (a) consistent barrier snapshots of all operator state and source offsets, (b) a transactional sink implementing pre-commit on barrier and commit on checkpoint completion with producer fencing, (c) deterministic operators and deterministic watermark heuristics, and (d) a replayable source log with retention ≥ maximum recovery distance. Then for any failure-and-recovery execution *E*, there exists a failure-free execution *E′* of the same program on the same input such that the sequence of records observable by `read_committed` consumers is identical in *E* and *E′*.

*Proof.* By (a) and the ABS consistent-cut theorem, recovery restores a consistent cut *S_n* at barrier *B_n* with source offsets *O_n*. By (d), replay re-reads exactly the suffix after *O_n*; by (c), reprocessing it reproduces the same state transitions, outputs, and watermark vectors. Pre-failure outputs belong either to committed transactions *T_{≤n}* — durable in the log and never re-emitted, since replay starts after *O_n* — or to uncommitted *T_{>n}*, which recovery aborts; by (b), fencing admits no zombie interleaving and `read_committed` consumers never observe aborted records. The observable log is thus the pre-failure committed prefix concatenated with the replayed suffix — identical to the failure-free execution *E′*. ∎

The theorem is deliberately *compositional*: each hypothesis is the responsibility of a different subsystem, and jointly they are sufficient.

### 5.2 Quantitative trade-offs

The mechanisms are not free. The principal costs, with their scaling behavior:

- **Barrier alignment latency.** An *n*-input operator waits for the slowest barrier; alignment delay ≈ max channel skew. Unaligned checkpoints bound this by snapshotting in-flight records instead — a direct latency-vs-snapshot-size trade [1].
- **Checkpoint frequency vs. recovery time.** With interval *τ* and failure rate *λ*, expected reprocessing per failure is *O(τ · throughput)*. Halving *τ* roughly halves recovery work but doubles steady-state snapshotting cost; incremental snapshots change the constant, not the shape.
- **Transactional overhead.** 2PC adds coordinator round-trips per transaction; batching many records per transaction amortizes this, trading commit-marker granularity (hence recovery granularity) for throughput [4].
- **Watermark lag under disorder.** With bounded-out-of-orderness *δ*, event time *t* cannot be finalized before *t + δ* is observed; *δ* is simultaneously a latency tax and a completeness guarantee [5][6].

![Recovery time versus checkpointed state size](public/thesis/ths_1788836918000_9f2a-3.webp)

- **State size vs. recovery time.** Restoring state of size *|S|* from durable storage costs *Θ(|S| / bandwidth)*; this is the dominant term in recovery for state-heavy jobs and the reason incremental and changelog-based backends exist. The figure above sketches the characteristic curves: full snapshots grow linearly, incremental snapshots sub-linearly, with a crossover determined by the change rate.

### 5.3 A TLA+ sketch of the barrier invariant

The alignment invariant is precise enough for model checking:

```tla
---- MODULE BarrierSnapshot ----
EXTENDS Naturals, FiniteSets
CONSTANTS Operators, Inputs  \* Inputs[o] = set of input channels of o

VARIABLES snapshotted,        \* [o \in Operators |-> BOOLEAN]
          barriersSeen,       \* [o \in Operators |-> SUBSET Inputs[o]]
          opState             \* abstract operator state

Init == /\ snapshotted = [o \in Operators |-> FALSE]
        /\ barriersSeen = [o \in Operators |-> {}]
        /\ opState \in [Operators -> Nat]

ReceiveBarrier(o, c) ==
    /\ ~snapshotted[o] \/ c \notin barriersSeen[o]
    /\ barriersSeen' = [barriersSeen EXCEPT ![o] = @ \cup {c}]
    /\ IF barriersSeen'[o] = Inputs[o]
       THEN /\ snapshotted' = [snapshotted EXCEPT ![o] = TRUE]
            /\ opState' = opState   \* snapshot taken here; state frozen
       ELSE UNCHANGED <<snapshotted, opState>>

ConsistentCut == \A o \in Operators :
    snapshotted[o] => barriersSeen[o] = Inputs[o]

Spec == Init /\ [][\E o \in Operators, c \in Inputs[o] : ReceiveBarrier(o, c)]_vars
THEOREM Spec => []ConsistentCut
====
```

The model checker's job is then to verify `[]ConsistentCut` — that no operator snapshots without a barrier on every input, exactly the alignment discipline whose violation would admit an inconsistent cut.

## 6. Limitations

The composition theorem's hypotheses are strong, and each fails in instructive ways:

1. **Sinks outside the transaction.** The theorem covers Kafka-to-Kafka pipelines. A sink to an external database, HTTP endpoint, or file system that cannot participate in the transaction protocol degrades to at-least-once output unless the sink itself is idempotent (keyed upserts are the standard escape hatch). *End-to-end exactly-once is a property of the whole topology, not of the engine.*
2. **Non-deterministic user code.** Any operator that reads wall-clock time, iterates a hash map, or uses unseeded randomness violates hypothesis (c). Engines can sandbox some of this (deterministic timers, seeded RNGs) but cannot fix arbitrary user non-determinism; the failure mode is silent divergence of replayed outputs.
3. **Watermark heuristics and lateness.** Bounded-out-of-orderness watermarks *drop* records later than the bound — a deliberate, configured data loss invisible to the exactly-once machinery. Effectively-once applies to the records the system *accepts*, not to all records that exist.
4. **Retention and the recovery horizon.** If the source log's retention is shorter than the distance back to the last successful checkpoint (prolonged downstream outage, poisoned checkpoint), recovery is impossible from the log alone. Savepoints and external backups are the backstop, not the log.
5. **Cyclic dataflows.** The ABS simplification (no channel-state recording on DAGs) does not apply to iterative computations; Flink handles cycles with special barrier handling and, in the limit, synchronous snapshotting of the iteration body [1]. The consistent-cut proof must be re-established per topology class.
6. **Exactly-once ≠ exactly-once *latency*.** Alignment, 2PC, and watermark waits all add latency. Systems advertising exactly-once with millisecond latency pay for it in snapshot frequency, in-flight buffering, or restricted expressiveness — there is no free lunch, only a chosen point on the trade-off surface.
7. **Zombie fencing assumes durable epochs.** If the transaction coordinator loses epoch state on failover, fencing can fail open. Production systems durably log epochs for exactly this reason, but the assumption is worth stating.

## 7. Conclusion

Exactly-once semantics in distributed stream processing is not a feature but a *composition*: Chandy–Lamport-style barrier snapshots [1][2] make state recovery exact; Kafka's idempotent producers and transactional 2PC with fencing [4] make output emission atomic and duplicate-free; MillWheel-style low watermarks and the Dataflow Model's watermark/trigger algebra [3][5][6] make event-time progress deterministic across replays; and replayable logs with deterministic operators make recovery a re-execution rather than a reconstruction. We proved these four mechanisms jointly sufficient for end-to-end effectively-once observable behavior, quantified the latency, state-size, and throughput taxes each imposes, and catalogued how the guarantee degrades when any hypothesis is dropped. The practical moral is architectural: place the exactly-once boundary around the *entire* pipeline — sources, engine, and sinks — or do not claim the property at all. Future work lies in shrinking the taxes: faster incremental state backends, lower-overhead commit protocols, and watermark estimators that adapt to observed disorder without sacrificing determinism.
