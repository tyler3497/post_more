# Planning with Learned Models: Dyna Architectures, MuZero's Value-Equivalent Dynamics, and Regret Bounds for Model-Based Reinforcement Learning

## Abstract

Model-based reinforcement learning occupies the tension between the sample efficiency of planning against an internal simulator and the brittleness of compounding model error. This thesis traces three intellectual threads that jointly define the modern discipline. First, Sutton's Dyna architectures, which fuse trial-and-error learning, model learning, and execution-time planning into a single approximate-dynamic-programming loop [1]. Second, MuZero, which abandons reconstructing the environment entirely in favor of value-equivalent latent models paired with Monte Carlo tree search, mastering Go, chess, shogi, and the Atari suite without knowledge of the rules [2]. Third, the theory of optimistic exploration culminating in UCRL2, whose regret bound is near-optimal in the tabular setting [3]. These classical results are connected to the deep model-based renaissance — probabilistic ensembles with trajectory sampling (PETS) [4] and model-based policy optimization (MBPO) [5] — and to the Bayesian Gaussian-process lineage of PILCO [6]. The thesis presents the core algorithmic constructions, a structured regret proof for optimistic model-based control, comparative empirical evidence, and an honest account of remaining failure modes: model bias, computational burden, and the theory's dependence on strong structural assumptions.

## 1 Intro

The central promise of model-based reinforcement learning is disarmingly simple: if an agent can learn how its world works, it can plan. A learned model converts the hard problem of sequential decision-making under uncertainty into the comparatively well-understood problem of *search*, and it does so at the cost of simulated rather than real experience. In domains where real experience is expensive — robotics, clinical trials, autonomous driving — this conversion is not a luxury but a necessity. The central peril is equally simple: a model is a fiction, and planning against a fiction compounds its errors exponentially in the planning horizon.

This thesis argues that the history of model-based reinforcement learning is best understood as a sequence of increasingly sophisticated answers to one question: *what should the model be a model of?* The earliest answers, crystallized in Sutton's Dyna architectures [1], treated the model as a faithful simulator of transitions and rewards, learned alongside the policy and queried for cheap hallucinated experience. A decade of deep learning then exposed the fragility of reconstruction: predicting every pixel of the next frame is both unnecessary and hopelessly difficult. MuZero [2] supplied the decisive reframing — the model need only be *value-equivalent*, predicting the quantities that planning actually consumes: rewards, policies, and values. In parallel, a theoretical literature asked what guarantees are even possible, producing optimistic algorithms whose cumulative regret grows sublinearly with near-optimal constants [3]. Finally, the deep model-based renaissance — PILCO [6], PETS [4], MBPO [5] — translated these ideas into continuous control, replacing maximum-likelihood point models with uncertainty-aware ensembles and replacing full-horizon rollouts with short branched ones.

## 2 Background

A finite Markov decision process is a tuple *(S, A, P, R)* with finite state and action spaces, transition kernel *P(s′|s,a)*, reward function *R(s,a)* bounded in the unit interval, and discount factor *γ*. The agent seeks a policy *π* maximizing expected return. The optimal action-value function satisfies the Bellman optimality equation

*Q*(s,a) = R(s,a) + γ Σ P(s′|s,a) max Q*(s′,a′).*

In the *learning* setting the transition kernel and reward function are unknown, and performance is measured by cumulative *regret*. For the undiscounted average-reward criterion with optimal gain *ρ**, the regret after *T* steps is

*Regret(T) = Σ (ρ* − r_t),*

the cumulative shortfall of the agent's rewards against the optimal stationary policy. An algorithm is *no-regret* when *Regret(T)/T → 0*, equivalently when regret grows sublinearly in *T*. The difficulty of exploration is captured by the *diameter* *D* of the MDP: the maximum, over state pairs, of the minimum expected hitting time under the best policy [3]. Large diameter means some states are hard to reach, and exploration is correspondingly expensive.

Two classical design principles organize the field. *Certainty equivalence* learns a point estimate of the model and plans as if it were true — simple, but prone to catastrophic overconfidence in poorly explored regions. *Optimism in the face of uncertainty* instead maintains a *set* of plausible models consistent with observations and acts optimally with respect to the most favorable among them, thereby guaranteeing that exploration is directed precisely where uncertainty is greatest. UCRL2 [3] is the canonical realization of optimism; R-MAX is its conceptual predecessor, treating unknown state–action pairs as maximally rewarding until visited sufficiently often.

A further taxonomy concerns the *target* of the model. *Reconstructive* models predict the next observation in full — the natural supervised objective, and the hardest. *Generative* models predict the next state under a distributional assumption. *Value-equivalent* models [2] predict only the quantities needed for planning: the immediate reward, the policy, and the value. ## 3 Methodology

## 3 Methodology

### The Dyna construction

Dyna-Q interleaves four operations per real time step [1]: (a) act greedily with respect to current *Q*; (b) apply a one-step Q-learning update to the experienced transition; (c) update a tabular model with the observed *(s, a, r, s′)*; (d) perform *n* additional Q-learning updates on *hallucinated* transitions sampled from the model. The integer *n* is the planning budget, continuously trading wall-clock computation against sample efficiency. Dyna-PI replaces Q-learning updates with policy-evaluation/policy-improvement steps, and prioritized sweeping focuses hallucinated updates on state–action pairs whose values changed most, propagating information backward along the model's reverse dynamics.

```python
def dyna_q(env, n_planning=50, alpha=0.1, gamma=0.95, eps=0.1):
    Q = defaultdict(float)   # action-value table
    model = {}               # (s, a) -> (r, s_next)
    s = env.reset()
    while True:
        a = eps_greedy(Q, s, eps)          # (a) acting
        s2, r, done = env.step(a)
        # (b) direct reinforcement learning
        td = r + gamma * max_a(Q, s2) - Q[(s, a)]
        Q[(s, a)] += alpha * td
        # (c) model learning
        model[(s, a)] = (r, s2)
        # (d) planning on hallucinated experience
        for _ in range(n_planning):
            ss, aa = random.choice(list(model))
            rr, ss2 = model[(ss, aa)]
            td = rr + gamma * max_a(Q, ss2) - Q[(ss, aa)]
            Q[(ss, aa)] += alpha * td
        s = env.reset() if done else s2
```

### The MuZero construction

MuZero learns three coupled functions over a latent hidden state *s* [2]: a *representation* function *h* mapping observations to hidden states, a *dynamics* function *g* mapping *(hidden state, action)* to *(reward, next hidden state)*, and a *prediction* function *f* mapping hidden states to *(policy logits, value)*. The hidden state carries no obligation to reconstruct the observation; it is trained end-to-end so that *k*-step unrolled predictions match observed rewards, MCTS-improved policies, and bootstrapped values. At decision time, Monte Carlo tree search with a pUCT selection rule plans over the learned latent dynamics, and the search-improved policy becomes both the behavior and the training target — a closed loop of planning and learning. A periodic *reanalyze* step refreshes old trajectories' targets with the current network, dramatically improving sample efficiency.

```haskell
-- MuZero's three learned functions over a latent state
type Hidden     = Vector Double
type Observation = Vector Double

representation :: Observation -> Hidden
-- h_theta: observation -> hidden state
dynamics       :: Hidden -> Action -> (Reward, Hidden)
-- g_theta: (hidden state, action) -> (reward, next hidden state)
prediction     :: Hidden -> (Policy, Value)
-- f_theta: hidden state -> (policy logits, value estimate)

-- K-step unrolled trajectory used for training targets
unroll :: Hidden -> [Action] -> [(Reward, Policy, Value)]
unroll s0 actions = zip3 rewards policies values
  where
    (rewards, states) = mapAccumL step s0 actions
    (policies, values) = unzip (map prediction (s0 : states))
    step s a = let (r, s') = dynamics s a in (s', (r, s'))
```

### Optimistic model-based control

UCRL2 proceeds in *episodes* [3]. Within an episode it maintains empirical transition counts and rewards, constructs *confidence sets* of plausible MDPs via concentration inequalities, and computes an optimistic policy by *extended value iteration* over the union of plausible models. An episode ends when any state–action count doubles, which bounds the number of episodes and hence the number of policy switches. The algorithm never commits to a single estimated model; it plans against the most favorable model consistent with data, and the regret analysis shows this suffices.

### The probabilistic-ensemble methodology

PILCO models continuous dynamics with Gaussian processes, propagates uncertainty analytically through moment matching, and optimizes parametric policies by analytic gradients of the expected long-term cost [6]. PETS replaces GPs with an ensemble of probabilistic neural networks, propagates uncertainty by *trajectory sampling* — each particle follows a randomly drawn ensemble member — and selects actions by the cross-entropy method [4]. MBPO combines probabilistic ensembles with an off-policy actor–critic, generating *short branched rollouts* from real states rather than long rollouts from initial states, and provides a monotonic-improvement analysis relating rollout length to model error [5].

| Family | Model | Planner | Exploration driver | Representative |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Dyna | tabular counts | hallucinated TD updates | ε-greedy | Sutton [1] |
| Value-equivalent | latent dynamics | MCTS over latent states | pUCT + noise | MuZero [2] |
| Optimistic | confidence-set MDP | extended value iteration | optimism | UCRL2 [3] |
| Probabilistic ensemble | PNN ensemble | CEM / short branched rollouts | ensemble disagreement | PETS [4], MBPO [5] |
| Bayesian GP | Gaussian process | analytic policy gradient | uncertainty propagation | PILCO [6] |

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Dyna and the planning–learning spectrum

Dyna's enduring insight is architectural rather than algorithmic: *planning is just learning from a model instead of the world*. The same TD update applies to real and hallucinated transitions, so the planning budget *n* interpolates smoothly between pure model-free learning (*n = 0*) and pure planning. 

Prioritized sweeping sharpens this picture: rather than sampling hallucinated transitions uniformly, it prioritizes updates by Bellman error and propagates value information backward through the model's predecessors — an idea that survives in prioritized experience replay and in MuZero's *reanalyze* mechanism [2]. The failure mode Dyna exposes is also instructive: with a *wrong* model, planning does not merely waste computation — it actively teaches the policy falsehoods. Every subsequent method in this thesis can be read as a response to that danger.

### 4.2 Value-equivalent models and the MuZero program

The value-equivalence principle states that two models are equivalent for planning purposes when they induce the same Bellman updates, regardless of whether they agree on observation prediction. MuZero operationalizes this by training the latent dynamics *only* on reward, policy, and value targets [2]. The hidden state is therefore free to discard every aspect of the observation irrelevant to control — the texture of the bricks in Breakout, the exact pixel noise — and to represent only decision-relevant structure. This is why MuZero succeeds where reconstructive world models struggled on the Atari suite: it refuses to solve a harder problem than planning requires.

The search component is not ornamental. MCTS over the learned latent model serves as a *policy-improvement operator*: the visit-count distribution at the root is a strictly better policy than the network prior under mild conditions, and distilling it back into the prediction function implements a form of approximate policy iteration in which both the evaluation (value targets) and improvement (search) steps use the learned model. The representation, dynamics, and prediction networks are thus co-adapted: the dynamics is trained to support the search, and the search supplies the targets that train the dynamics.

> **Key distinction:** reconstructive models minimize observation-prediction error, a sufficient but unnecessary condition for good planning; value-equivalent models minimize planning-relevant prediction error, which is both necessary and, empirically, far easier to satisfy at scale [2].

### 4.3 Optimism, confidence sets, and regret bounds

UCRL2's analysis is the cleanest available statement of what model-based RL can guarantee. The algorithm maintains, for each state–action pair, an empirical transition distribution and reward mean, surrounded by confidence intervals shrinking as one over the square root of visit counts [3]. Extended value iteration then computes the optimal policy *over the optimistic MDP* — the plausible model with highest optimal gain. The regret proof decomposes per-episode regret into three terms: the deviation of empirical rewards from true rewards, the deviation of empirical transitions from true transitions (controlled via a martingale concentration argument over the value function's span), and the number of episodes (bounded by the doubling criterion). Summing yields the headline result.

> **Theorem:** For any communicating MDP with *S* states, *A* actions, and diameter *D*, UCRL2 attains cumulative regret *Õ(DS√AT)* after *T* steps with high probability, against a lower bound of *Ω(√DSAT)* for any algorithm [3].

The bound is near-optimal: the upper and lower bounds match up to the diameter factor *D* and logarithmic terms. The proof's structure — episodes with doubling, optimism over confidence sets, regret decomposition via the Bellman error — became the template for a generation of follow-up work, including posterior-sampling (PSRL) and variance-aware (EULER-style) variants that tighten constants. Yet the theorem's assumptions are strong: finite tabular MDPs, known reward scale, and a diameter that is finite but unknown and appears multiplicatively. Nothing in the analysis survives the passage to deep function approximation, which is precisely the regime where MuZero [2] and PETS [4] operate — a theory–practice gap that remains the field's central embarrassment.

### 4.4 Probabilistic ensembles and the short-rollout regime

PETS and MBPO confront Dyna's failure mode — planning against a wrong model — with two complementary instruments: *uncertainty quantification* and *horizon control* [4][5]. The probabilistic ensemble captures *epistemic* uncertainty (disagreement among bootstrapped models about the dynamics) and *aleatoric* uncertainty (each network's predicted output variance). Trajectory sampling propagates both forward: each imagined particle is simulated by a randomly selected ensemble member, so the planner sees the full distribution of plausible futures rather than a single hallucinated one. Actions are then chosen by the cross-entropy method to maximize expected return under this distribution, which naturally avoids regions where the ensemble disagrees — the operational meaning of uncertainty-aware planning.

MBPO adds the theoretical observation that model error compounds *linearly* in the rollout length *k* but the benefit of model data saturates, yielding an optimal finite *k* [5]. Branching short rollouts from *real* states (rather than chaining long rollouts from initial states) keeps the imagined distribution close to the data distribution, controlling distribution shift.

```rust
// MBPO-style branched rollouts: short imagined trajectories from real states
fn branched_rollouts(
    real_states: &[State],
    model: &Ensemble,
    policy: &Policy,
    k: usize, // short rollout horizon
) -> Vec<Transition> {
    let mut data = Vec::new();
    for s0 in real_states {
        let mut s = s0.clone();
        for _ in 0..k {
            let a = policy.sample(&s);
            // bootstrap a random ensemble member: epistemic uncertainty
            let (r, s_next) = model.sample_member(&s, &a);
            data.push(Transition { s, a, r, s_next });
            s = s_next;
        }
    }
    data
}
```

## 5 Empirical/Proofs

### Regret proof sketch for UCRL2

We sketch the argument of Jaksch, Ortner, and Auer [3]. Time is partitioned into episodes *k = 1, 2, …*, where episode *k* ends when the visit count of some state–action pair doubles its count at the episode's start; this guarantees at most *O(SA log T)* episodes. Within episode *k*, let *M̃_k* be the optimistic MDP and *π̃_k* its optimal policy. The per-episode regret splits as

*Δ_k ≤ Σ ν_k(s,a)(r̃ − r) + Σ ν_k(s,a) Σ (p̃ − p)·v + span-penalty,*

where *ν_k(s,a)* are visit counts, *r̃, p̃* the optimistic rewards and transitions, and *v* the optimistic value function. The first two terms are controlled by Hoeffding and Weissman-type concentration: with high probability the true MDP lies in the confidence set, so optimism implies *ρ̃_k ≥ ρ**. The martingale term involving *(p̃ − p)·v* is bounded by the span of *v*, itself bounded by *D*. Summing over episodes and applying Cauchy–Schwarz gives *Õ(DS√AT)*. The matching *Ω(√DSAT)* lower bound follows from a two-armed-bandit-style construction embedded in an MDP, showing no algorithm can do fundamentally better [3].

> **Theorem (MBPO monotonic improvement):** Let *ε_m* bound the model generalization error and *ε_π* the policy divergence. Then the return under *k*-step branched rollouts satisfies *η(π) ≥ η_branch(π) − C(ε_m, ε_π, k)*, where the penalty grows with *k* — formalizing why short rollouts are provably safer than long ones [5].

## 6 Limitations

**Model bias is not solved, only managed.** Ensembles quantify uncertainty but cannot eliminate it; in regions far from the data, all members may agree and all be wrong. Short rollouts bound the damage but also bound the planning depth, and the optimal rollout length is a hyperparameter, not a derived quantity [5].

**Computation is the hidden currency.** MuZero trains for a million mini-batches with 800 MCTS simulations per move in board games, using 16–20 residual blocks per evaluation [2]. PETS runs the cross-entropy method at every environment step. The sample-efficiency gains are real, but the wall-clock and energy costs are formidable, and they scale poorly to real-time robotics.

**The theory covers a different world than the practice.** UCRL2's near-optimal regret bound [3] requires finite tabular MDPs and pays the diameter *D* multiplicatively; MuZero, PETS, and MBPO enjoy no regret guarantees whatsoever. The eluder-dimension and linear-MDP programs extend optimism to function approximation, but under structural assumptions — linearity, Bellman completeness — that deep networks do not satisfy. Closing this gap, particularly a regret analysis for *value-equivalent* models, is the outstanding open problem.

**Latent models drift.** Because MuZero's hidden states are unconstrained by reconstruction [2], the latent geometry can shift during training, invalidating previously computed search trees and value targets; *reanalyze* mitigates this at further computational cost. Gaussian-process methods like PILCO [6] avoid drift through calibrated uncertainty but scale cubically in data, confining them to low-dimensional problems.

## 7 Conclusion

Three ideas, spanning three decades, jointly explain modern model-based reinforcement learning. Dyna [1] established the architecture: learning, model-learning, and planning as one loop, with planning compiling itself into reactive skill. MuZero [2] established the modeling target: not the world, but the quantities planning needs — rewards, policies, values — in a latent space optimized end to end. UCRL2 [3] established the theoretical standard: optimism over confidence sets attains near-optimal regret, and the proof's anatomy — episodes, concentration, Bellman-error decomposition — remains the template for rigorous analysis. The deep methods — PILCO's Bayesian caution [6], PETS's ensembles [4], MBPO's branched short rollouts [5] — are best read as engineering answers to Dyna's original warning that planning against a wrong model teaches falsehoods.

The synthesis points forward. The most pressing open problem is a *theory of value-equivalent models*: regret or sample-complexity guarantees for agents that plan in learned latent spaces without reconstructive grounding. Next is *compute-aware* model-based RL — methods whose planning budgets adapt to uncertainty rather than fixed simulation counts. The arc from Dyna's hallucinated TD updates to MuZero's latent search suggests the field's next decade will be won not by bigger models of the world, but by more precise models of what planning requires.
