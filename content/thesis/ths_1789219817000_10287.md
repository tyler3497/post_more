# Marine Ice-Sheet Instability and the Grounding-Line Dynamics of Antarctica: Boundary-Layer Theory, Buttressing Loss, and the Sea-Level Commitment of the Amundsen Sea Embayment

## Abstract

Antarctica's marine ice sheets — grounded on bedrock far below sea level that deepens toward the interior — obey a dynamics distinct from terrestrial ice. This thesis develops the theory of marine ice-sheet instability (MISI): grounding lines on *retrograde* bed slopes are dynamically unstable, so a small forced retreat can trigger self-sustaining collapse with no guaranteed restoring force [1][2]. We derive Schoof's (2007) boundary-layer theory of grounding-line flux, show how the power-law dependence of discharge on grounding-line thickness generates hysteresis under sea-level, accumulation, and basal-slipperiness forcing, and extend the analysis to buttressed three-dimensional configurations where ice shelves modulate the instability [1][3]. We review the observational ledger — multi-kilometer grounding-line retreat across the Amundsen Sea Embayment since 1992 and warm Circumpolar Deep Water intrusions — consistent with engaged irreversible retreat [4][5], and assess the contested marine ice-cliff instability hypothesis against viscoelastic mitigation arguments and coupled simulations projecting Antarctic sea-level contributions from centimeters to meters by 2100 [6][7]. We close with the uncertainty hierarchy — basal friction, grounding-zone melt, fracture physics — defining the field's frontier.

**Keywords:** marine ice-sheet instability, grounding line, boundary-layer theory, Schoof flux law, ice-shelf buttressing, Thwaites Glacier, sea-level commitment, hysteresis

---

## 1 Introduction

Of the approximately 65 m of sea-level equivalent (SLE) locked in terrestrial ice, Antarctica holds roughly 58 m — by far the largest reservoir of potential sea-level rise on Earth [6]. Unlike the Greenland Ice Sheet, whose margins predominantly terminate on land above sea level, the West Antarctic Ice Sheet (WAIS) and substantial subglacial basins of East Antarctica rest on bedrock depressed well below sea level by isostatic loading, with bed topography that in many sectors *deepens inland* [4]. This geometric fact, first appreciated by Mercer (1978) and Weertman (1974), endows marine ice sheets with a peculiar and potentially catastrophic dynamical property: under certain conditions, their grounding lines — the loci where ice detaches from the bed and begins to float — are *intrinsically unstable* [1].

The physical intuition is disarmingly simple. Consider a flow-line ice sheet whose grounding-line ice thickness is $h_g$. In steady state, the ice flux discharged across the grounding line, $q_g$, must balance upstream accumulation. Boundary-layer analysis reveals that $q_g$ increases *sharply* with $h_g$ — approximately as a high power of thickness [1]. Now suppose the bed slopes downward in the direction of flow (*retrograde* slope, $db/dx > 0$ where $x$ points downstream). A small retreat of the grounding line moves it into deeper water; by the flotation condition, the new grounding-line thickness is larger; the flux law then demands *greater* discharge; and the ice sheet thins further, driving additional retreat. The feedback is positive and self-amplifying: retreat begets thicker grounding-line ice, which begets more retreat [2].

This positive feedback loop is the *marine ice-sheet instability* (MISI). Satellite altimetry, interferometry, and airborne radar have documented sustained grounding-line retreat across the Amundsen Sea Embayment (ASE) since at least 1992 — Pine Island retreating 31 km, Smith/Kohler 35 km, Thwaites 14 km, Haynes 10 km — with no topographic obstacles that might arrest the retreat [4]. Numerical models suggest Thwaites Glacier, which alone holds ~59 cm of SLE and dams the WAIS's additional 3+ m, may already be committed to collapse on timescales of centuries [5].

Yet the story is far richer than the elementary feedback suggests. Real grounding lines are three-dimensional: lateral confinement of ice shelves provides *buttressing* back-stress that can stabilize otherwise unstable configurations [3]. Warm water intrusion into grounding zones may exhibit its own tipping-point behavior distinct from MISI [8]. And the more speculative *marine ice-cliff instability* (MICI) — runaway calving of tall ice cliffs exposed by ice-shelf disintegration — has been both invoked to explain paleo sea-level highstands and challenged on mechanical grounds [6][7].

This thesis synthesizes the mathematical theory, the numerical evidence, and the observational record into a coherent account of where Antarctic ice-sheet stability stands — and where it does not.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Flotation, grounding, and the marine ice-sheet configuration

A marine ice sheet is grounded where the ice thickness $h$ exceeds the *flotation thickness* $h_f$ determined by Archimedes' principle:

$$h_f = -\frac{\rho_w}{\rho_w - \rho_i}\,b,$$

where $b(x) < 0$ is bedrock elevation relative to sea level, $\rho_i \approx 917\ \mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$ the ice density, and $\rho_w \approx 1028\ \mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$ the seawater density.

Two geometric regimes matter:

- **Prograde beds** ($db/dx < 0$ in the downstream direction, bed rising toward the interior): retreat onto shallower bed thins the grounding line, reducing flux — a *negative* feedback, stable.
- **Retrograde beds** ($db/dx > 0$ downstream, bed deepening inland): retreat thickens the grounding line, increasing flux — *positive* feedback, the MISI regime [1][2].

West Antarctica's bed, mapped by Operation IceBridge airborne radar and compiled in Bedmap/BedMachine, is retrograde across much of the ASE and in deep East Antarctic troughs (Recovery, Aurora, Wilkes basins), where ice above flotation in reverse-sloping troughs up to 1,500 m deep holds ~20 m of SLE [6].

> **Definition:** A *marine ice sheet* is an ice sheet whose bed lies below sea level. *Marine ice-sheet instability* (MISI) denotes the self-sustaining grounding-line retreat that theory predicts on retrograde bed slopes, driven by the thickness-dependence of grounding-line flux.

### 2.2 Historical development of the instability hypothesis

Weertman (1974) first derived a stability condition for a marine ice sheet frozen to its bed, and Mercer (1978) famously warned that a CO₂-induced warming could disintegrate WAIS.

The decisive advance came from Schoof (2007), who attacked the opposite limit — an ice sheet undergoing *very rapid basal sliding*, so shear stresses in the ice are negligible relative to bed stresses and basal normal stresses are cryostatic [1].

### 2.3 Buttressing and the three-dimensional reality

An ice shelf confined within an embayment or pinned on seafloor rises exerts a back-stress — *buttressing* — on the grounded ice upstream, reducing the extensional stress at the grounding line and hence the discharge flux [3].

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 The flow-line model and its scaling

We consider the two-dimensional (flow-line) marine ice sheet of Schoof (2007a): grounded ice for $x_d \le x < x_g$ (divide at $x_d$, grounding line at $x_g$), transitioning to a floating shelf for $x > x_g$.

$$\dot{\epsilon}_{ij} = A\,\tau_e^{\,n-1}\,\tau_{ij},$$

with rate factor $A(T)$ strongly temperature-dependent.

1. **Outer (lubrication) flow.** Away from the grounding line, the shallow-ice (lubrication) approximation holds: longitudinal stresses are negligible, and the momentum balance reduces to a balance among driving stress, basal drag, and (in the fast-sliding limit) negligible internal shear [1].
2. **Boundary-layer analysis.** In a narrow transition zone of width $O(\epsilon)$ near $x_g$, extensional (membrane) stresses become leading-order.

> **Proposition:** In the rapid-sliding limit, the ice flux through the grounding line is a function *only* of the bed depth (hence grounding-line thickness) at $x_g$, together with material and friction parameters — independent of the upstream ice-sheet geometry.

### 3.2 Numerical implementation strategy

Verifying the boundary-layer theory requires numerical solutions that *resolve the transition zone*.

The following Python prototype illustrates the hysteresis sweep methodology: for a sequence of sea-level (or accumulation) forcings, the steady grounding-line position is found by root-finding on the mass-balance residual, and stability is diagnosed from the sign of $dq_g/dx_g$:

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.optimize import brentq

# Schoof (2007) grounding-line flux law (unbuttressed, rapid sliding)
# q_g = C * h_g^{n+1} / (sliding law exponent terms), here simplified
def grounding_flux(h_g, A=1e-16, C_drag=7.6e6, n=3.0, m=1.0/3.0):
    rho_i, rho_w, g = 917.0, 1028.0, 9.81
    pref = (A * (rho_i * g) ** (n + 1) * (1 - rho_i / rho_w) ** n
            / (4 ** n * C_drag)) ** (1.0 / (m + 1))
    return pref * h_g ** ((n + m + 3) / (m + 1))

def flotation_thickness(bed_depth):
    rho_i, rho_w = 917.0, 1028.0
    return (rho_w / (rho_w - rho_i)) * bed_depth

def steady_residual(x_g, bed_profile, accumulation, width=1.0):
    b = bed_profile(x_g)          # bed elevation (negative below sea level)
    h_g = flotation_thickness(-b)
    q_g = grounding_flux(h_g)
    q_in = accumulation * (x_g - 0.0) * width  # integrated accumulation
    return q_in - q_g             # zero at steady state

# Sweep sea level upward then downward to reveal hysteresis
bed = lambda x: -600.0 - 4.0 * x + 0.02 * x ** 2  # overdeepened (retrograde then prograde)
xg_forward, xg_backward = [], []
for sl in np.linspace(0, 60, 61):
    bshift = lambda x, s=sl: bed(x) - s
    try:
        xg_forward.append(brentq(steady_residual, 1.0, 400.0,
                                args=(bshift, 0.3)))
    except ValueError:
        xg_forward.append(np.nan)
```

The sweep generically reveals the *fold bifurcations* and the S-shaped steady-state curve: for an overdeepened bed, a given forcing admits up to three steady grounding-line positions, of which the intermediate branch is unstable [1].

### 3.3 From flow-line to continental scale

Continental-scale projections (e.g., PISM, the Penn State ice-sheet model) embed Schoof-type flux conditions or their buttressed generalizations (e.g., Tsai–Stewart–Thompson; Haseloff–Sergienko–Wingham) as subgrid grounding-line parameterizations within shallow-shelf / higher-order stress-balance solvers, coupled to ocean melt parameterizations and, in the most ambitious experiments, to full climate models [5][6].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Schoof flux law and its derivation

The crown jewel of the theory is the grounding-line flux formula. For the unbuttressed, rapidly sliding sheet with a Weertman-type sliding law $\tau_b = C|u|^{m-1}u$ and Glen exponent $n$, matched asymptotics give

$$q_g = \left[\frac{A(\rho_i g)^{n+1}(1-\rho_i/\rho_w)^n}{4^n C}\right]^{1/(m+1)} h_g^{\frac{n+m+3}{m+1}},$$

where $h_g$ is the grounding-line thickness.

### 4.2 Hysteresis, fold bifurcations, and the loss of neutral equilibrium

Three principal results follow [1]:

1. **No neutral equilibrium.** Marine ice sheets do not possess the continuum of neutrally stable states once conjectured; they have well-defined, *discrete* equilibrium profiles.
2. **No stable steady grounding lines on reverse slopes.** Any steady grounding line on a retrograde bed is unstable to infinitesimal perturbations.
3. **Hysteresis under parameter variation.** For overdeepened beds, varying sea level, accumulation rate, basal slipperiness, or ice viscosity traces a hysteresis loop: the advance path and retreat path differ, and reversing the forcing does *not* restore the original configuration [1].

The hysteresis has a profound corollary for climate policy: even if the forcing that triggered retreat is reversed (e.g., ocean cooling), the ice sheet does not regrow along the retreat path. Continental-scale modeling confirms this at the Antarctic scale: the West Antarctic Ice Sheet does not regrow to its modern extent until temperatures fall at least ~1 °C *below* pre-industrial levels, and at ~2 °C of warming West Antarctica is committed to long-term partial collapse via MISI [9].

### 4.3 Buttressing: the three-dimensional correction

Real ice shelves are laterally confined. Gudmundsson and others showed that buttressing can be quantified through the ratio of the actual grounding-line flux to the unbuttressed Schoof flux — a *buttressing number* — and that loss of buttressing (by shelf thinning or calving-front retreat past pinning points) increases discharge even without any change at the grounding line itself [3]. The Thwaites Eastern Ice Shelf (TEIS) exemplifies this: two decades of observation show its flow dynamics were long controlled by the neighboring Thwaites Western Ice Tongue; after the tongue's disintegration (~2008), new rifting and strain concentration developed, and basal-melt-driven thinning near the grounding line suggests the TEIS may fully destabilize within decades — *now through dynamic feedbacks independent of basal melt* [10].

A subtle and important recent result: the Schoof flux formula, derived for *unbuttressed* grounding lines, produces inaccurate and sometimes physically implausible results when applied to buttressed Antarctic shelves [3].

### 4.4 Marine ice-cliff instability: hypothesis and pushback

DeConto and Pollard (2016) coupled ice-sheet and climate dynamics with two previously underappreciated processes: hydrofracturing of buttressing ice shelves by surface meltwater, and structural collapse of marine-terminating ice cliffs taller than ~100 m [6]. Calibrated against Pliocene and Last Interglacial sea-level estimates, their model projected Antarctic contributions exceeding 1 m by 2100 and 15 m by 2500 under unabated emissions — with atmospheric warming eventually dominating ocean forcing, and recovery delayed for millennia [6]. The 2021 update found that overshooting Paris Agreement targets risks rapid, *unstoppable* Antarctic sea-level rise, with carbon-dioxide removal unable to halt the loss once begun in earnest [7].

The MICI component has proven controversial. Bassis and Walker (2012) showed cliffs taller than ~1 km should fail by shear, but the *rate* of failure matters enormously. Clerc, Minchew, and Behn (2019) incorporated Maxwell viscoelasticity and found that cliffs up to 500 m tall collapse in brittle chunks *only if* the buttressing shelf is removed within hours; on realistic timescales of days to weeks (Larsen B disintegrated over ~two weeks), tall cliffs slough viscously like cold honey rather than failing catastrophically [11].

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 The observational ledger of the Amundsen Sea Embayment

The satellite record since 1992 is unambiguous in direction, if debated in implication [4]:

| Glacier | Grounding-line retreat (1992–2011) | Bed character | Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pine Island | ~31 km | Deep retrograde trough | Accelerating; multiple tipping points possible |
| Thwaites | ~14 km | Retrograde; ridge at ~600 m depth | Early-stage collapse; 200–1000 yr to full loss |
| Smith / Kohler | ~35 km | Retrograde | Rapid retreat |
| Haynes | ~10 km | Retrograde | Retreating |

*Table 1: Observed grounding-line retreat in the Amundsen Sea Embayment [4]. Retreat is driven from below: warm modified Circumpolar Deep Water (mCDW) intrudes through submarine troughs, melting ice shelves near their grounding zones [5][10].*

Rignot's team found *no* bathymetric highs that could halt the ASE retreat — the bed deepens monotonically inland, the precise geometry that MISI theory identifies as unstable [4].

Recent work sharpens and complicates the picture. Goldberg et al. (2026) show that reproducing observed inland thinning requires weak beds within the Thwaites trough, implying loss rates up to 200 Gt yr⁻¹ before 2100 are consistent with current observations — comparable to the *entire* present Antarctic mass imbalance [12].

### 5.2 Paleo-calibration: the Last Interglacial and Pliocene tests

Any instability theory must survive the paleo record. During the Last Interglacial (~130–115 ka), polar temperatures were only slightly warmer than today, yet sea level stood 6–9 m higher; during the Pliocene (~3 Ma), with CO₂ near today's ~400 ppm, seas may have stood 10–30 m higher [6]. The Antarctic ice sheet is implicated as the primary contributor in both cases — and conventional models *without* hydrofracturing and cliff physics cannot reproduce these highstands [6]. The DeConto–Pollard model's ability to match them was, in the authors' words, the "epiphany" motivating the instability parameterizations [6]. Independent evidence continues to accumulate: sediment cores from the deep Southern Ocean identify eight post-Last-Glacial-Maximum phases in which the Antarctic Ice Sheet destabilized *within a decade* and contributed to sea-level rise for centuries to millennia, with equally rapid re-stabilization [13].

### 5.3 The commitment accounting

Garbe, Albrecht, and Levermann's comprehensive stability analysis (PISM, 2020) quantified the commitment as a function of warming [9]:

| Warming above pre-industrial | Committed Antarctic response | Dominant mechanism |
|---|---|---|
| ~2 °C | WAIS partial collapse | Marine ice-sheet instability |
| 6–9 °C | >70% of ice volume lost | Surface-elevation feedback |
| >10 °C | Virtually ice-free Antarctica | Runaway surface melt |

*Table 2: Temperature thresholds for irreversible Antarctic ice loss [9]. Temperature sensitivity: 1.3 m SLE/°C below 2 °C, 2.4 m/°C between 2–6 °C, ~10 m/°C between 6–9 °C.*

Every threshold exhibits hysteresis: the modern configuration is not regained upon cooling to present temperatures [9].

A Julia snippet illustrates how the commitment calculation is structured in practice — equilibrium ice volume as a function of sustained warming, the core loop of Garbe et al.-style analysis:

```julia
# Equilibrium Antarctic ice volume vs. sustained warming (schematic)
function equilibrium_volume(dT; V0=58.0)
    # Piecewise-linear fit to PISM hysteresis experiments (m SLE retained)
    if dT < 2.0
        return V0 - 1.3 * dT          # MISI-dominated regime
    elseif dT < 6.0
        return V0 - 2.6 - 2.4 * (dT - 2.0)
    elseif dT < 9.0
        return V0 - 12.2 - 10.0 * (dT - 6.0)
    else
        return max(0.0, V0 - 42.2 - 5.0 * (dT - 9.0))
    end
end
```

## 6 Limitations

**The flux law's domain of validity.** The Schoof flux formula assumes rapid sliding, negligible vertical shear, and — critically — an *unbuttressed* grounding line [1]. Its misapplication to buttressed shelves is a documented source of error [3]. Generalized theories exist but are harder to implement as subgrid parameterizations, and intercomparison shows model spread in grounding-line migration remains large.

**Basal friction is the dominant uncertainty.** Inversions of basal slipperiness from surface velocities are ill-posed; Goldberg et al. (2026) show that matching observed Thwaites thinning *requires* weak beds whose extent is poorly constrained, and the choice of friction law (Weertman vs. Coulomb vs. regularized Coulomb) qualitatively changes retreat rates [12]. We cannot measure the bed directly beneath kilometers of fast-flowing ice.

**Grounding-zone melt parameterizations.** Warm-water intrusion into grounding zones exhibits tipping-point-like behavior — small ocean-temperature changes can flip intrusion length from bounded to unbounded — with timescales far shorter than grounded-ice response [8]. Most continental models parameterize melt as a simple function of thermal forcing and cavity geometry, omitting the intrusion physics.

**Fracture and calving.** MICI remains the largest single source of projection spread [6][7][11]. Damage-mechanics models are improving, but the viscoelastic mitigation result [11] and the paleo highstand constraint [6] have not been reconciled in a single framework.

**Observational sparsity.** Bed topography, ocean temperature in sub-shelf cavities, and basal conditions remain sampled at orders of magnitude coarser resolution than the models that consume them.

## 7 Conclusion

The theory of marine ice-sheet instability has matured from Weertman's ill-posed construction, through Schoof's boundary-layer resolution, into a quantitative — if still contested — account of Antarctica's future [1]. Its core predictions are stark: steady grounding lines cannot persist on retrograde slopes; overdeepened beds generate hysteresis so that retreat, once triggered, is not undone by reversing the forcing; and the Amundsen Sea Embayment, retreating for decades into deepening basins with no topographic brakes, exhibits every geometric symptom of the instability [1][4][5].

Three frontiers will decide how much of the theoretical worst case materializes. First, *buttressing*: the generalized flux laws that properly account for confined ice shelves must replace the unbuttressed Schoof formula in continental models [3]. Second, *fracture*: the MICI debate must be settled by models that simultaneously honor viscoelastic reality and paleo sea-level constraints [6][11]. Third, *the ocean*: grounding-zone intrusion tipping points and the delivery of warm Circumpolar Deep Water are now observable processes demanding process-resolving treatment [8][10].

What is no longer in serious doubt is the *commitment* framing. Because of hysteresis, the relevant question is not only how fast Antarctica contributes to sea level this century, but how much future rise is already locked in — and the evidence increasingly suggests that at current warming, substantial irreversible loss is underway or imminent [7][9][12]. The grounding line, a curve of zero width on a map, may be the most consequential line in climate science.
