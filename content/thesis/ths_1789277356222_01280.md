# Visual-Inertial SLAM: Factor Graphs, Loop Closure, and the Geometry of Probabilistic Mapping

## Abstract

Simultaneous localization and mapping (SLAM) is the canonical problem of building a map of an unknown environment while estimating the trajectory of the sensor that observes it. Modern visual-inertial systems treat this as *maximum a posteriori* (MAP) inference over a factor graph: a bipartite graphical model whose factors encode probabilistic constraints from odometry, inertial preintegration, and camera observations, all composed on the non-Euclidean manifold of rigid-body poses. This article develops the three pillars of that formulation. First, we derive the factor-graph MAP objective from Bayes' rule, showing how Gaussian noise reduces inference to nonlinear least squares on SO(3) and SE(3), solved by Gauss–Newton iterations with retraction-based updates. Second, we present on-manifold IMU preintegration, which collapses hundreds of high-rate inertial measurements into one relative-motion factor with analytic bias Jacobians. Third, we treat loop closure as place recognition — from bag-of-binary-words vocabularies to geometric verification and Sim(3) correction — and explain why it restores global consistency and annihilates accumulated drift. We conclude with incremental solvers (iSAM2's Bayes tree), robust cost functions for outlier rejection, consistency arguments, and empirical validation on standard benchmarks.

## 1 Intro

Few estimation problems expose the structure of probabilistic inference as completely as simultaneous localization and mapping. A robot moves through an unknown environment, integrating noisy proprioceptive measurements that drift, while exteroceptive observations — camera frames, LiDAR scans — constrain its motion only *relative to a world it is simultaneously reconstructing*. The resulting estimation problem is high-dimensional, non-convex, non-Euclidean, and real-time. Early solutions were filters: the extended Kalman filter over robot pose plus landmarks, which *marginalizes out the past* at every step and in doing so both densifies the information matrix and quietly destroys the sparsity that would have made the problem easy [2]. The modern consensus, crystallized over two decades, is that smoothing — optimizing over the full trajectory with all measurements retained — is both more accurate and, counterintuitively, more efficient, because the *naturally sparse* information matrix of the full problem factorizes cheaply [2,3].

Three mathematical technologies converged to make this possible:

1. **Factor graphs** — the bipartite graphical language of Kschischang, Frey, and Loeliger [1], which factorizes the joint posterior into local factors and exposes conditional independence as graph sparsity.
2. **Lie-group optimization** — poses live on the manifolds SO(3) and SE(3), where ordinary addition is undefined and updates must be composed through the exponential map.
3. **Incremental sparse solvers** — iSAM and its successor iSAM2 [2,3], which maintain a QR factorization of the square-root information matrix (or equivalently, a Bayes tree) and update only the variables affected by each new measurement.

This article unifies these threads for visual-inertial SLAM, adding the two measurement frontiers that define practical systems: *on-manifold IMU preintegration*, which tames the 100–200 Hz inertial stream into one factor per keyframe pair [4], and *loop closure*, which detects revisits via appearance-based place recognition and injects the long-range constraints that collapse accumulated drift [5,6].

> **Theorem (MAP ↔ Nonlinear Least Squares):** Let the joint posterior over variables $\Theta$ factorize as $p(\Theta \mid \mathcal{Z}) \propto \prod_i f_i(\Theta_i)$ with Gaussian factors $f_i(\Theta_i) \propto \exp(-\tfrac{1}{2}\|h_i(\Theta_i) - z_i\|^2_{\Sigma_i})$. Then the MAP estimate $\Theta^* = \arg\max_\Theta p(\Theta \mid \mathcal{Z})$ coincides with the minimizer of $\sum_i \|h_i(\Theta_i) - z_i\|^2_{\Sigma_i}$, i.e., MAP inference under Gaussian noise is exactly nonlinear least squares.

The remainder of this article is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews factor graphs and the Lie-group machinery of poses. Section 3 derives the visual-inertial factor-graph objective and its incremental solution. Section 4 dives into four topics: manifold optimization, IMU preintegration, loop-closure machinery, and robustness. Section 5 sketches consistency and empirical results; Section 6 discusses limitations; Section 7 concludes.

## 2 Background

### Factor graphs and the anatomy of SLAM

A factor graph is a bipartite graph $G = (\mathcal{F}, \Theta, \mathcal{E})$ in which variable nodes $\Theta = \{\theta_1, \dots, \theta_n\}$ are connected only to factor nodes $\mathcal{F} = \{f_1, \dots, f_m\}$, each factor a function of its adjacent variables $\Theta_i \subseteq \Theta$. The global function is the product $\prod_i f_i(\Theta_i)$ [1]. In SLAM, variables are *poses* $\mathbf{x}_k \in \mathrm{SE}(3)$ (or $\mathrm{SE}(2)$ for planar robots), *landmark positions* $\ell_j \in \mathbb{R}^3$, and possibly *calibration parameters*; factors encode priors, odometry or inertial motion models, and sensor observations. The sum–product algorithm unifies Kalman filtering, forward–backward, and belief propagation as message passing on such graphs [1]; but for continuous, nonlinear, high-dimensional SLAM, we use the max-product limit — MAP inference via numerical optimization.

The SLAM posterior over trajectory $X = \{\mathbf{x}_0, \dots, \mathbf{x}_N\}$ and map $M = \{\ell_j\}$ given all measurements $Z$ decomposes as:

$$p(X, M \mid Z) \propto \underbrace{p(\mathbf{x}_0)}_{\text{prior}} \prod_k \underbrace{p(\mathbf{x}_k \mid \mathbf{x}_{k-1}, \mathbf{u}_k)}_{\text{motion}} \prod_{k,j} \underbrace{p(\mathbf{z}_{kj} \mid \mathbf{x}_k, \ell_j)}_{\text{observation}}$$

Each factor is typically Gaussian in its *residual*: $p(\mathbf{x}_k \mid \mathbf{x}_{k-1}, \mathbf{u}_k) \propto \exp(-\tfrac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{x}_k \ominus f(\mathbf{x}_{k-1}, \mathbf{u}_k)\|^2_{\Sigma})$, where $\ominus$ denotes the manifold difference operator. Maximizing this posterior is the smoothing problem. The crucial structural fact is that each observation involves only a handful of variables — one pose and one landmark, or two poses — so the *information matrix* $\Lambda = J^\top \Sigma^{-1} J$ (with $J$ the measurement Jacobian) is sparse, with a characteristic block pattern: a block-tridiagonal backbone from odometry plus sparse off-diagonal blocks from landmark co-visibility and loop closures [2]. Filters, by marginalizing past poses, *fill in* this matrix, destroying sparsity and with it any hope of efficient exact inference — the fundamental argument for smoothing.

### The geometry of poses: SO(3) and SE(3)

Rotations are not vectors. The set of $3 \times 3$ orthogonal matrices with determinant $+1$,

$$\mathrm{SO}(3) = \{R \in \mathbb{R}^{3\times3} \mid R^\top R = I,\ \det R = 1\},$$

is a compact Lie group of dimension 3; rigid-body poses $\mathrm{SE}(3) = \mathrm{SO}(3) \ltimes \mathbb{R}^3$ form a 6-dimensional group. Perturbations live in the tangent space at the identity — the Lie algebra $\mathfrak{so}(3)$ of skew-symmetric matrices, isomorphic to $\mathbb{R}^3$ via the hat operator $\hat{\boldsymbol{\omega}}\mathbf{v} = \boldsymbol{\omega} \times \mathbf{v}$. The exponential map $\mathrm{Exp}: \mathfrak{so}(3) \to \mathrm{SO}(3)$ is given by Rodrigues' formula:

$$\mathrm{Exp}(\boldsymbol{\phi}) = I + \frac{\sin\|\boldsymbol{\phi}\|}{\|\boldsymbol{\phi}\|}\hat{\boldsymbol{\phi}} + \frac{1 - \cos\|\boldsymbol{\phi}\|}{\|\boldsymbol{\phi}\|^2}\hat{\boldsymbol{\phi}}^2,$$

and optimization on the manifold proceeds by *retraction*: at iterate $R^{(t)}$, solve the linearized problem for a tangent increment $\delta\boldsymbol{\phi} \in \mathbb{R}^3$ and update $R^{(t+1)} = R^{(t)} \circ \mathrm{Exp}(\delta\boldsymbol{\phi})$. In code this is written $R \boxplus \delta\boldsymbol{\phi}$, with $\boxplus$ the generic retraction and $\boxminus$ its inverse difference. Covariances of pose estimates are then defined in the tangent space — a Gaussian on SO(3) is a Gaussian on the Lie algebra pushed through $\mathrm{Exp}$ — the only consistent way to attach uncertainty to a rotation. Getting this geometry wrong (e.g., treating Euler angles as Euclidean) produces singularities and inconsistent linearizations; getting it right yields Jacobians that respect the group structure and near-quadratic convergence.

| Paradigm | Variables kept | Information matrix | Relinearization | Consistency |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| EKF SLAM | current pose + map | dense (fill-in from marginalization) | none (one-shot) | prone to inconsistency |
| Fixed-lag smoothing | sliding window | sparse, bounded | windowed | consistent within window |
| Full smoothing (iSAM/iSAM2) | all poses + landmarks | sparse, unbounded | fluid, data-driven | asymptotically consistent |

## 3 Methodology

The visual-inertial SLAM pipeline is a factor graph whose construction, optimization, and maintenance we now describe as a coherent methodology.

### Graph construction

Time is discretized at *keyframes*: camera frames selected when parallax or feature-track loss exceeds a threshold. Between consecutive keyframes $i$ and $j$, all IMU measurements (typically 100–200 Hz) are collapsed by preintegration into a single *IMU factor* $\phi_{ij}$ over $(\mathbf{R}_i, \mathbf{p}_i, \mathbf{v}_i, \mathbf{R}_j, \mathbf{p}_j, \mathbf{v}_j, \mathbf{b})$ where $\mathbf{b}$ are the accelerometer and gyroscope biases [4]. Each visual landmark observation contributes a *reprojection factor* with residual $\mathbf{r} = \pi(\mathbf{T}_{cw}\ell_j) - \mathbf{z}$, where $\pi$ is the camera projection model and $\mathbf{T}_{cw} \in \mathrm{SE}(3)$ the camera pose. Loop closures contribute *relative-pose factors* between temporally distant keyframes [5,6]. The full objective:

$$\Theta^* = \arg\min_\Theta \sum_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{I}} \|\mathbf{r}_{\text{imu}}(\Theta_i, \Theta_j)\|^2_{\Sigma_{ij}} + \sum_{(k,j)\in\mathcal{V}} \rho\!\left(\|\mathbf{r}_{\text{vis}}(\mathbf{x}_k, \ell_j)\|^2_{\Sigma}\right) + \sum_{(a,b)\in\mathcal{L}} \|\mathbf{r}_{\text{loop}}(\mathbf{x}_a, \mathbf{x}_b)\|^2_{\Sigma_{ab}}$$

where $\rho$ is a robust kernel (Section 4.4) applied to visual residuals to blunt the influence of mismatched features.

### Incremental optimization: from iSAM to iSAM2

Batch optimization would re-factorize the entire system at every keyframe — prohibitively expensive as the trajectory grows. iSAM [2] observed that adding new factors to a QR factorization $A = QR$ of the square-root information matrix can be done *incrementally*: only the rows and columns touched by the new measurements change, and periodic variable reordering controls fill-in. iSAM2 [3] generalizes this by representing the factorized posterior as a *Bayes tree*: variable elimination converts the factor graph into a chordal Bayes net, and cliques of that net form a tree whose root-to-leaf conditionals $p(F_k \mid S_k)$ encode the entire solution. When a new factor arrives, only the cliques on the paths from the factor's variables to the root need re-elimination; the rest of the tree is untouched. Two further ideas make iSAM2 practical:

- **Fluid relinearization:** rather than relinearizing everything at fixed intervals, variables are relinearized only when their estimate has moved beyond a threshold $\beta$ since the last linearization — computation is spent exactly where the nonlinearity demands it.
- **Incremental variable reordering:** the elimination ordering is updated locally (constrained COLAMD) to preserve sparsity without the global reorderings that made iSAM's periodic step expensive.

Together these give near-constant-time updates for exploration and bounded cost at loop closures, recovering the *exact* batch solution in the Gaussian case while running in real time on a single CPU thread [3].

### The algorithm in miniature

The following Python sketch (in the spirit of GTSAM's factor-graph API [3]) shows the incremental update loop. It is pseudocode for exposition, not a library call:

```python
import numpy as np

def slam_step(isam2, keyframe, imu_buffer, dbow2, robust_kernel):
    """One incremental update of a visual-inertial SLAM backend."""
    # 1. Preintegrate IMU between previous and current keyframe
    pim = preintegrate_imu(imu_buffer, bias=isam2.bias_estimate())
    graph.add(CombinedImuFactor(pim, keyframe.prev, keyframe.curr))

    # 2. Visual factors with robust kernel against bad matches
    for landmark, obs in keyframe.observations():
        f = ProjectionFactor(landmark, camera_model, obs.pixel)
        f.set_robust_kernel(robust_kernel)   # e.g. Huber
        graph.add(f)

    # 3. Loop closure: place recognition -> geometric verification
    candidates = dbow2.query(keyframe.descriptors, min_score=0.35)
    for cand in candidates:
        T_rel = geometric_verify(keyframe, cand)   # RANSAC PnP
        if T_rel is not None:
            graph.add(BetweenFactor(keyframe.pose_key, cand.pose_key,
                                   T_rel, noise_model))

    # 4. Incremental update: only affected Bayes-tree cliques re-solved
    isam2.update(graph, initial_estimates)
    return isam2.calculate_estimate()
```

The loop's elegance is that steps 1–3 merely *append factors*; all the computational intelligence — what to relinearize, what to reorder, what to refactor — lives inside `isam2.update` [3].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Optimization on manifolds: retractions, adjoints, and the right Jacobian

Linearizing a residual $\mathbf{r}(\Theta)$ at $\bar{\Theta}$ on a manifold requires the directional derivative along the retraction: $J = \left.\frac{\partial\, \mathbf{r}(\bar{\Theta} \boxplus \boldsymbol{\xi})}{\partial \boldsymbol{\xi}}\right|_{\boldsymbol{\xi}=0}$. For SO(3) with the right-trivialized retraction $R \boxplus \boldsymbol{\phi} = R\,\mathrm{Exp}(\boldsymbol{\phi})$, the Baker–Campbell–Hausdorff formula governs composition of increments, and the *right Jacobian* $J_r(\boldsymbol{\phi})$ relates additive tangent vectors to group multiplication: $\mathrm{Exp}(\boldsymbol{\phi} + \delta\boldsymbol{\phi}) \approx \mathrm{Exp}(\boldsymbol{\phi})\,\mathrm{Exp}(J_r(\boldsymbol{\phi})\,\delta\boldsymbol{\phi})$. Forster et al. derive these Jacobians in closed form for the IMU residuals [4], and modern libraries (GTSAM, g2o [7], Ceres) implement them once per manifold type so that user-defined factors differentiate automatically. The practical consequence is Gauss–Newton on manifolds:

1. Linearize all factors at the current estimate $\bar{\Theta}$, producing $A\,\delta\boldsymbol{\xi} \approx \mathbf{b}$ with $\delta\boldsymbol{\xi}$ in the *tangent bundle*.
2. Solve the sparse normal equations (or the square-root form) for $\delta\boldsymbol{\xi}$.
3. Retract: $\bar{\Theta} \leftarrow \bar{\Theta} \boxplus \delta\boldsymbol{\xi}$.

Because the linearization lives in a vector space while the iterate lives on the group, the optimizer never leaves the manifold — no projection, no constraint drift, no gimbal singularities. *This is the sense in which SLAM is differential geometry before it is statistics.*

### 4.2 On-manifold IMU preintegration

An IMU delivers angular velocity $\tilde{\boldsymbol{\omega}}$ and specific force $\tilde{\mathbf{a}}$ at hundreds of hertz, corrupted by slowly varying biases $\mathbf{b}^g, \mathbf{b}^a$ and white noise. Naively adding one factor per IMU sample would explode the graph; worse, the motion model between keyframes depends on the *linearization point* of the endpoint states, so every relinearization would force re-integration of the raw samples. Preintegration [4] resolves this by integrating the IMU kinematics *in the local frame of the starting keyframe*:

$$\begin{aligned}
\Delta\mathbf{R}_{ij} &= \prod_{k=i}^{j-1} \mathrm{Exp}\big((\tilde{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_k - \mathbf{b}^g_i)\Delta t\big), \\
\Delta\mathbf{v}_{ij} &= \sum_{k=i}^{j-1} \Delta\mathbf{R}_{ik}\,(\tilde{\mathbf{a}}_k - \mathbf{b}^a_i)\Delta t, \\
\Delta\mathbf{p}_{ij} &= \sum_{k=i}^{j-1} \left(\Delta\mathbf{v}_{ik}\Delta t + \tfrac{1}{2}\Delta\mathbf{R}_{ik}\,(\tilde{\mathbf{a}}_k - \mathbf{b}^a_i)\Delta t^2\right),
\end{aligned}$$

so that the residuals decouple the preintegrated measurements from the absolute states:

$$\begin{aligned}
\mathbf{r}_{\Delta R} &= \mathrm{Log}\!\left(\Delta\tilde{\mathbf{R}}_{ij}(\bar{\mathbf{b}}^g)^\top \mathbf{R}_i^\top \mathbf{R}_j\right), \\
\mathbf{r}_{\Delta v} &= \mathbf{R}_i^\top(\mathbf{v}_j - \mathbf{v}_i - \mathbf{g}\Delta t_{ij}) - \Delta\tilde{\mathbf{v}}_{ij}(\bar{\mathbf{b}}), \\
\mathbf{r}_{\Delta p} &= \mathbf{R}_i^\top(\mathbf{p}_j - \mathbf{p}_i - \mathbf{v}_i\Delta t_{ij} - \tfrac{1}{2}\mathbf{g}\Delta t_{ij}^2) - \Delta\tilde{\mathbf{p}}_{ij}(\bar{\mathbf{b}}).
\end{aligned}$$

Three subtleties make this rigorous rather than heuristic. *First*, rotation noise is modeled on SO(3): Forster et al. show the preintegrated rotation noise remains concentrated Gaussian in the tangent space, covariance propagated through the right Jacobians [4]. *Second*, when the bias estimate changes, preintegrated measurements are corrected *analytically* to first order via precomputed Jacobians $\partial\Delta_{ij}/\partial\mathbf{b}$ — no re-integration, since biases drift slowly and the linearization holds across the small bias updates of a converged estimator. *Third*, residuals are expressed in the body frame at time $i$, making the factor's noise model independent of the global pose — a factorization that preserves the graph's sparsity. Preintegration thus converts the IMU's curse (data rate) into its blessing (a strong, high-bandwidth motion prior that resolves visual scale and renders the system observable even during aggressive motion).

### 4.3 Loop closure: from appearance to geometry to global consistency

Odometry — visual or inertial — integrates *relative* motion, so its error is a random walk: pose uncertainty grows without bound, typically superlinearly in distance traveled. Loop closure is the mechanism that bounds it. When the sensor revisits a previously mapped place, recognizing the revisit injects a *long-range factor* between temporally distant poses; optimizing the graph then distributes the accumulated error around the loop, and posterior uncertainty collapses globally. The pipeline has three stages, each a research field unto itself:

- **Place recognition.** The keyframe's local descriptors (e.g., ORB) are quantized through a pre-trained visual vocabulary into a *bag of binary words* vector; an inverted index retrieves past keyframes with similar word distributions in sublinear time [5]. DBoW2's hierarchical vocabulary and direct index make this fast enough for real-time use, and its TF-IDF weighted scoring is robust to partial occlusion and viewpoint change.
- **Geometric verification.** Appearance similarity is necessary but not sufficient — perceptual aliasing (two identical corridors) generates false positives that would catastrophically corrupt the map. Candidate matches are therefore verified by estimating a geometric transform (fundamental matrix, or PnP given 3D landmarks) inside RANSAC; only candidates with enough inliers become loop factors [6]. *A single false loop closure can destroy a map; a missed true one merely delays convergence.* This asymmetry drives the entire design toward precision over recall.
- **Map correction.** Once verified, the loop factor enters the graph and the optimizer corrects the trajectory. For monocular systems, ORB-SLAM2 goes further: it optimizes a *similarity* (Sim(3)) constraint that also absorbs the scale drift endemic to monocular vision, then fuses duplicated map points and runs a final global bundle adjustment [6].

> **Theorem (Loop closure and information gain):** In a Gaussian factor graph, adding a loop-closure factor between poses $\mathbf{x}_a$ and $\mathbf{x}_b$ strictly increases the information matrix $\Lambda \mapsto \Lambda + J_{ab}^\top \Sigma_{ab}^{-1} J_{ab}$ in the positive-semidefinite order. Hence every marginal covariance of the posterior is non-increasing, and the trace of the pose covariance — the system's global uncertainty — drops monotonically with each verified loop closure.

### 4.4 Robustness: when factors lie

Real data association is imperfect: mismatched features, moving objects, and the occasional false loop closure inject *outlier factors* whose residuals are not Gaussian at all. Least squares is catastrophically non-robust — a single large residual dominates the quadratic cost and drags the solution toward the outlier. Three defenses are standard:

- **Robust kernels** replace $\|\mathbf{r}\|^2$ with $\rho(\|\mathbf{r}\|)$, e.g., the Huber kernel $\rho(s) = s$ for $s \le \delta^2$, $2\delta\sqrt{s} - \delta^2$ beyond — quadratic near zero, linear in the tails. Implemented as iteratively reweighted least squares, this preserves the sparse normal equations while down-weighting outliers [7].
- **Switchable constraints** attach a latent switch variable $s_{ab} \in [0,1]$ to each loop-closure factor, scaling its information by $s_{ab}^2$ and penalizing $1 - s_{ab}$; the optimizer then *turns off* inconsistent loops automatically.
- **Graduated non-convexity** solves a sequence of problems with the kernel's convexity annealed from convex to the true robust shape, widening the basin of convergence.

The deep lesson is that *the factor graph is only as honest as its front end*: backend robustness exists precisely because perception cannot be trusted, and the boundary between the two is where most real systems fail.

## 5 Empirical/Proofs

The theoretical claims above are validated on standardized benchmarks with ground-truth trajectories (motion-capture rooms for EuRoC MAV, GPS/INS for KITTI). The evaluation protocol is uniform: run the estimator, align the estimated trajectory to ground truth with a Sim(3) transform (Umeyama), and report the root-mean-square absolute trajectory error (ATE). Forster et al. evaluate their preintegration-based monocular VIO on EuRoC and on long real-world trajectories, demonstrating real-time operation with accuracy exceeding the filtering baselines of the era — the structureless vision model (which marginalizes landmarks analytically rather than optimizing them) further reduces the state dimension without sacrificing accuracy [4]. ORB-SLAM2 reports trajectory errors on KITTI, EuRoC, and TUM RGB-D that were state-of-the-art for CPU-only systems at publication, with the loop-closing and relocalization threads shown to be decisive: disabling loop closure measurably degrades ATE on sequences with revisits, while the Sim(3) pose-graph correction restores metric consistency after large loops [6].

On the theoretical side, two results deserve emphasis. *First*, the smoothing-vs-filtering argument is not merely empirical: the EKF's marginalization induces exactly the fill-in that densifies its information matrix, and its one-shot linearization is known to produce *inconsistent* (overconfident) covariance estimates in SLAM — a defect that smoothing with relinearization avoids by construction [2,3]. *Second*, iSAM2's Bayes-tree update is provably equivalent to batch factorization: re-eliminating only the affected cliques yields the identical $R$ factor and solution that full re-factorization would produce, so "incremental" here means *algorithmically* faster, not *approximately* correct [3].

## 6 Limitations

The factor-graph formulation, for all its power, rests on assumptions that real deployments routinely violate:

- **Static-world assumption.** Landmarks are treated as fixed; dynamic objects violate the observation model and must be detected and excluded by the front end, an unsolved problem in the general case.
- **Gaussianity.** Real sensor noise is heavy-tailed and data association is discrete; robust kernels mitigate but do not eliminate the mismatch, and the MAP point estimate discards the multimodal ambiguity (e.g., "which corridor am I in?") that a full posterior would retain.
- **Local convergence.** The objective is non-convex; without a good initialization from odometry or a relocalization module, Gauss–Newton settles into local minima. Global optimality certificates via semidefinite relaxation exist only for the pose-graph subproblem and remain too expensive for online use.
- **Observability and degeneracy.** Pure rotation with a monocular camera, textureless scenes, and IMU saturation each render parts of the state unobservable; the graph returns an answer with spuriously small covariance unless degeneracy is explicitly detected.
- **Compute and memory.** Full smoothing retains all keyframes; lifelong operation demands sparsification, marginalization, or submapping, each reintroducing the approximations that smoothing was meant to escape.

## 7 Conclusion

Visual-inertial SLAM is MAP inference on a factor graph, optimized on Lie groups, updated incrementally, and closed by loops. Each clause corresponds to a hard-won insight: factor graphs expose the sparsity that makes the problem tractable [1]; smoothing preserves it where filtering destroys it [2,3]; the manifold formulation makes "optimizing a rotation" mathematically coherent; IMU preintegration compresses the inertial firehose into analytic, bias-correctable factors [4]; and loop closure — appearance-based recognition, geometrically verified, fused as long-range constraints — converts unbounded odometric drift into a globally consistent map [5,6]. The open frontiers are robustness to dynamics, certifiable global optimality, and lifelong scalability. But the conceptual core is settled: *to localize is to infer, and inference over trajectories is inference over graphs.*
