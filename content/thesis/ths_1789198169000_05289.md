# Tropical Geometry: The Maslov Dequantization, Mikhalkin's Correspondence Theorem, and the Chip-Firing Path to Enumerative Algebraic Geometry

## Abstract

Tropical geometry studies varieties over the tropical semiring $\mathbb{T} = (\mathbb{R} \cup \{-\infty\}, \max, +)$, replacing classical varieties by piecewise-linear polyhedral complexes retaining enumerative information. We develop the pipeline from Maslov dequantization to the Fundamental and Structure Theorems, then to the two pillars of the subject's combinatorial turn: (i) **Mikhalkin's correspondence theorem**, reducing the count of nodal complex plane curves of degree $d$ and genus $g$ through $3d+g-1$ general points to a weighted count of trivalent tropical curves, computed via lattice-path and floor-diagram algorithms; and (ii) **chip-firing and divisor theory on graphs** of Baker and Norine, whose Riemann–Roch theorem $r(D) - r(K - D) = \deg(D) - g + 1$ lifts classical curve theory to the tropical skeleton. We treat valuations, amoebas, the balancing condition, dual Newton subdivisions, and the Mikhalkin multiplicity; prove graph Riemann–Roch via $q$-reduced divisors and Dhar's burning algorithm; and connect it to tropical Riemann–Roch and Baker's specialization lemma. A computational section implements chip-firing stabilization in Python, and we close with limitations — higher-dimensional realizability, the NP-hardness of divisor rank — and open problems in enumerative geometry.

## 1 Intro

Enumerative algebraic geometry asks how many geometric objects of a given kind satisfy a given set of incidence conditions. The prototypical problem — counting rational plane curves of degree $d$ passing through $3d-1$ general points in $\mathbb{P}^2$ — resisted classical attack for arbitrary $d$ until Kontsevich's 1994 recursion derived from intersection theory on moduli spaces of stable maps [1]. In parallel, an entirely different philosophy was emerging: replace the field $\mathbb{C}$ by the *tropical semiring*, replace polynomials by piecewise-linear functions, and replace algebraic curves by graphs. The resulting shadows, called **tropical varieties**, are simple enough that enumerative counts can be performed by hand — yet, in the deepest theorem of the subject, they count exactly the same objects as the complex theory.

The intellectual lineage is long. Maslov and his school observed in the early 1990s that idempotent analysis is the *dequantization* of ordinary analysis, with the parameter $\hbar \to 0$ replaced by a logarithmic scaling limit [2]. Viro's patchworking (1979) and the amoeba theory of Gelfand–Kapranov–Zelevinsky (1994), refined by Kapranov's non-Archimedean amoebas (2000), showed how piecewise-linear combinatorics controls real algebraic geometry. The formalization of tropical varieties and their intrinsic geometry is due to Mikhalkin and Sturmfels (2002), and the first spectacular application — the correspondence theorem — to Mikhalkin (2005) [3].

This thesis has three aims. First, to give a precise account of the tropicalization pipeline: valuations, the Fundamental Theorem, the Structure Theorem, the balancing condition, and dual Newton subdivisions. Second, to prove and explain Mikhalkin's correspondence theorem and the combinatorial enumeration algorithms (lattice paths, floor diagrams) it enables, extending the discussion to the Nishinou–Siebert and Macpherson generalizations and the state of higher-dimensional correspondence [4]. Third, to develop the chip-firing side of the story: the Baker–Norine Riemann–Roch theorem for graphs [5], its proof via Dhar's burning algorithm, its relationship to tropical Riemann–Roch on metric graphs, and its computational realization — including the NP-hardness of divisor rank [6] and the recent Lean 4 formalization [7].

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The tropical semiring and Maslov dequantization

The **tropical semiring** $\mathbb{T} = (\mathbb{R} \cup \{-\infty\}, \oplus, \odot)$ is defined by

$$x \oplus y = \max(x, y), \qquad x \odot y = x + y,$$

with additive identity $-\infty$ and multiplicative identity $0$. (The min-plus convention is equivalent under $x \mapsto -x$.) This structure is the $\hbar \to 0$ limit of the classical field under the map $x \mapsto -\hbar \log x$:

$$-\hbar \log(e^{-a/\hbar} + e^{-b/\hbar}) \xrightarrow[\hbar \to 0^+]{} \max(a, b),$$

so that addition dequantizes to maximum and multiplication to addition. This is Maslov's **dequantization** principle [2]: tropical mathematics is the $\hbar = 0$ limit of classical mathematics, and correspondingly geometry over $\mathbb{T}$ should be the "zero-temperature" limit of algebraic geometry.

A **tropical polynomial** in $n$ variables is a finite tropical linear combination of tropical monomials:

$$p(x) = \bigoplus_{\alpha \in A} c_\alpha \odot x^{\odot \alpha} = \max_{\alpha \in A} \left(c_\alpha + \langle \alpha, x \rangle\right),$$

a piecewise-linear concave (in max-plus) function. Its **tropical hypersurface** $V^{\mathrm{trop}}(p) \subset \mathbb{R}^n$ is the locus where the maximum is attained at least twice — the non-differentiability locus of $p$. Already in one variable this is striking: a tropical polynomial of "degree $d$" has exactly $d$ tropical roots counting multiplicity, recovering a combinatorial Fundamental Theorem of Algebra.

### 2.2 Valuations, amoebas, and the Fundamental Theorem

Let $K$ be an algebraically closed field with a non-trivial non-Archimedean valuation $\mathrm{val}: K^* \to \mathbb{R}$ (e.g. the field of Puiseux series $\mathbb{C}\{\{t\}\}$ with $\mathrm{val}$ recording the lowest exponent). Coordinatewise valuation gives the **tropicalization map**

$$\mathrm{trop}: (K^*)^n \longrightarrow \mathbb{R}^n, \qquad (x_1, \dots, x_n) \mapsto (\mathrm{val}(x_1), \dots, \mathrm{val}(x_n)).$$

For a subvariety $X \subset (K^*)^n$ with ideal $I$, its tropical variety is $\mathrm{trop}(X)$. The **Fundamental Theorem of Tropical Geometry** (proved in full generality by Payne, building on work of Speyer–Sturmfels, Draisma, and others; see [8, Ch. 3]) states that $\mathrm{trop}(X)$ coincides with:

1. the closure in $\mathbb{R}^n$ of $\mathrm{trop}(X)$ (by definition);
2. the intersection over all $f \in I$ of the tropical hypersurfaces $V^{\mathrm{trop}}(\mathrm{trop}(f))$, where $\mathrm{trop}(f)$ is the tropicalization of the polynomial $f$;
3. the set of weight vectors $w \in \mathbb{R}^n$ such that the initial ideal $\mathrm{in}_w(I)$ contains no monomial.

Over $\mathbb{C}$ with the trivial valuation this story is replaced by **amoebas**: for $V \subset (\mathbb{C}^*)^n$, the amoeba $\mathcal{A}(V) = \mathrm{Log}(V) \subset \mathbb{R}^n$ with $\mathrm{Log}(z) = (\log|z_1|, \dots, \log|z_n|)$. Mikhalkin and Rullgård proved that scaled amoebas converge to tropical varieties: for a family $P_t$ with coefficients of controlled growth, $\frac{1}{\log t}\mathcal{A}(P_t) \to V^{\mathrm{trop}}(P_\infty)$ in the Hausdorff metric as $t \to \infty$ [2, Thm. 5.2]. The tropical curve is thus literally the spine of a degenerating amoeba.

### 2.3 The Structure Theorem and the balancing condition

The **Structure Theorem** [8, Thm. 3.3.5] asserts that for a $d$-dimensional variety $X$, $\mathrm{trop}(X)$ is the support of a **balanced weighted polyhedral complex** of pure dimension $d$: every maximal cell carries a positive integer multiplicity, and at every codimension-one cell $\tau$, the weighted outgoing primitive direction vectors satisfy

$$\sum_{\sigma \supset \tau} m_\sigma \, u_{\sigma/\tau} = 0,$$

where $u_{\sigma/\tau} \in \mathbb{Z}^n / \mathrm{span}(\tau)$ is the primitive lattice vector pointing from $\tau$ into $\sigma$. For tropical curves ($d = 1$), this is the familiar **balancing condition** at each vertex: the weighted sum of outgoing edge directions vanishes. This single equation encodes the residue theorem: it is the tropical shadow of the fact that the sum of residues of a meromorphic differential on a compact Riemann surface is zero.

Tropical curves are therefore *metric graphs* with unbounded rays, weighted edges, and integral slopes satisfying balancing. The genus of a tropical curve $\Gamma$ is its first Betti number $g = |E| - |V| + 1$ (for the compactified model), and its degree in $\mathbb{R}^2$ is read off from the multiset of directions of its unbounded ends: a plane tropical curve of degree $d$ has $3d$ ends with directions $(-1,0)$, $(0,-1)$, $(1,1)$ each occurring $d$ times, up to refinement.

### 2.4 Newton polygons and duality

A plane tropical curve is dual to a **regular subdivision** of its Newton polygon. If $p(x,y) = \max_{(i,j)} (c_{ij} + ix + jy)$, the upper convex hull of $\{(i,j,c_{ij})\}$ projects to a subdivision of $\Delta = \mathrm{conv}\{(i,j) : c_{ij} \ne -\infty\}$; vertices of the tropical curve correspond to 2-cells of the subdivision, edges to interior edges (perpendicular), and the balancing condition to the closing of lattice polygons. For a curve of degree $d$, $\Delta = \Delta_d = \mathrm{conv}\{(0,0),(d,0),(0,d)\}$ and the genus satisfies $g \le \binom{d-1}{2}$, with equality for smooth tropical curves (unimodular triangulations). This duality is the engine of all enumeration: counting tropical curves through points becomes counting lattice subdivisions, a finite combinatorial problem.

## 3 Methodology

Our methodology is threefold: (A) **tropicalization as a degeneration**, formalized via valuations and Gröbner theory over the Puiseux series field, following [8]; (B) **correspondence and enumeration**, reducing complex counts to weighted tropical counts and making them effective via lattice paths and floor diagrams [3]; (C) **combinatorial divisor theory on graphs**, developing chip-firing, $q$-reduced divisors, and the graph Riemann–Roch theorem [5], then lifting to metric graphs and tropical curves via the specialization lemma.

The unifying principle is *degeneration*: a complex curve over $\mathbb{C}\{\{t\}\}$ tropicalizes to a metric graph; a divisor on the curve specializes to a divisor on the graph; ranks can only drop under specialization. Hence combinatorial theorems on graphs imply geometric constraints on curves — the "combinatorial path" of the title.

> **Theorem: (Fundamental Theorem, tropical form).** For $X \subset (K^*)^n$ with ideal $I$, the tropical variety $\mathrm{trop}(X)$ equals the set of $w \in \mathbb{R}^n$ for which $\mathrm{in}_w(I)$ contains no monomial, and it carries a balanced polyhedral structure of dimension $\dim X$ with multiplicities given by the sums of multiplicities of minimal associated primes of $\mathrm{in}_w(I)$.

We work throughout over $K = \mathbb{C}\{\{t\}\}$ for correspondence results and over $\mathbb{C}$ for amoeba statements, using the max-plus convention.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Mikhalkin's correspondence theorem

Fix integers $d \ge 1$, $g \ge 0$, and let $n = 3d + g - 1$ be the expected dimension of the Severi variety of plane curves of degree $d$ and geometric genus $g$. Let $N_{d,g}$ denote the number of complex nodal curves of degree $d$ and genus $g$ through $n$ general points in $\mathbb{P}^2$ (the Gromov–Witten invariant $N_{d,g}$, with $N_{d,0}$ the Kontsevich numbers: $N_1 = 1$, $N_2 = 1$, $N_3 = 12$, $N_4 = 620$, …).

A **plane tropical curve** of degree $d$ and genus $g$ is a balanced weighted trivalent (after resolution) graph in $\mathbb{R}^2$ with $3d$ prescribed ends. For a trivalent vertex $V$ with outgoing weighted primitive directions $w_1 u_1, w_2 u_2, w_3 u_3$, the **Mikhalkin multiplicity** is

$$\mathrm{mult}(V) = w_1 w_2 \, |\det(u_1, u_2)|,$$

the lattice area of the dual triangle (equivalently, twice its Euclidean area). The multiplicity of the tropical curve is $\mathrm{mult}(\Gamma) = \prod_V \mathrm{mult}(V)$ over trivalent vertices.

> **Theorem: (Mikhalkin correspondence [3]).** Let $q_1, \dots, q_n \in \mathbb{R}^2$ be general and let $p_i \in (K^*)^2$ lift them. Then
>
> $$N_{d,g} = \sum_{\Gamma} \mathrm{mult}(\Gamma),$$
>
> the sum over plane tropical curves $\Gamma$ of degree $d$ and genus $g$ passing through the $q_i$.

*Sketch of method.* The proof proceeds by degenerating the point configuration to a "vertically stretched" position and analyzing the possible tropical limits. For stretched points, every tropical curve through them admits a **floor decomposition**: contracting each connected component of the complement of vertical edges yields a **floor diagram** — a smaller, decorated graph from which the curve can be uniquely reconstructed. The complex curves tropicalizing to a given $\Gamma$ are then counted by a patchworking argument: near each trivalent vertex, the local complex model is a pair of pants, and the gluing choices contribute exactly $\mathrm{mult}(V)$ possibilities. The product over vertices gives the multiplicity, and a deformation argument shows every complex curve arises this way exactly once. The full details, including the extension to toric surfaces via the Nishinou–Siebert and Macpherson frameworks [4], occupy the bulk of [3] and its sequels.

The correspondence is effective. Mikhalkin's **lattice-path algorithm** enumerates the relevant subdivisions of $\Delta_d$: for points in Mikhalkin position, tropical curves correspond to certain monotone lattice paths, and the multiplicity becomes a product of local contributions computable from the path's steps. Floor diagrams [2, §5] give a still more efficient encoding: for rational curves ($g = 0$), one counts decorated trees on $d$ ordered vertices with $3d-1$ markings, recovering $N_3 = 12$ and $N_4 = 620$ by hand-countable combinatorics.

Enumerative consequences extend far beyond $\mathbb{P}^2$: correspondence theorems now cover toric surfaces and threefolds, real enumerative geometry (Welschinger invariants via signed tropical counts), descendant invariants (Markwig–Rau), and singular hypersurfaces in $\mathbb{P}^n$ via a three-dimensional lattice-path algorithm [1, §5].

### 4.2 Chip-firing, the dollar game, and $q$-reduced divisors

Let $G = (V, E)$ be a finite connected multigraph without loops, of genus $g = |E| - |V| + 1$. A **divisor** is $D = \sum_{v} D(v) \cdot (v) \in \mathbb{Z}^V$; $\deg D = \sum_v D(v)$. Two divisors are **linearly equivalent** ($D \sim D'$) if their difference lies in the image of the Laplacian: $D' = D - L f$ for some $f: V \to \mathbb{Z}$, where $(Lf)(v) = \deg(v) f(v) - \sum_{w \sim v} f(w)$. Firing the vertex $v$ (subtracting $L\mathbf{1}_v$) sends one chip along each incident edge — the **chip-firing** move; borrowing is the inverse.

The **dollar game** (Björner–Lovász–Shor) asks: starting from $D$, can a sequence of firings/borrowings reach an effective divisor (all $D(v) \ge 0$)? The **rank** $r(D)$ is $-1$ if no effective divisor is equivalent to $D$, and otherwise the largest $r$ such that $D - E$ is winnable for every effective $E$ of degree $r$. The canonical divisor is $K = \sum_v (\deg(v) - 2)(v)$, of degree $2g - 2$.

The key technical device is the **$q$-reduced divisor**: for fixed $q \in V$, a divisor $D$ is $q$-reduced if $D(v) \ge 0$ for all $v \ne q$ and every nonempty $S \subseteq V \setminus \{q\}$ contains a vertex $v$ with $D(v) < |\{ \text{edges } v \to V \setminus S\}|$ (i.e., firing $S$ would create debt). Every divisor class contains a unique $q$-reduced representative, computable by **Dhar's burning algorithm**: start a fire at $q$; it spreads across an edge $vw$ iff the number of burnt edges incident to $w$ exceeds $D(w)$; $D$ is $q$-reduced iff the whole graph burns. Reduced divisors are the graph analogue of normal forms for line bundles, and they make rank *testable* rather than merely existential.

### 4.3 The Baker–Norine Riemann–Roch theorem for graphs

> **Theorem: (Baker–Norine [5]).** For any divisor $D$ on a finite graph $G$ of genus $g$,
>
> $$r(D) - r(K - D) = \deg(D) - g + 1.$$

*Proof sketch.* The inequality $r(D) - r(K-D) \ge \deg D - g + 1$ follows from a combinatorial Clifford-type argument using reduced divisors: if $r(D) \ge 0$ and $r(K-D) \ge 0$, choose $q$-reduced representatives and apply the burning test to the sum. The reverse inequality is the heart of the paper: it suffices to show that $r(D) \ge 0$ implies $r(K - D) \le \deg(K - D) - g + 1 + r(D)$, which is proved by a delicate induction on $\deg D$ using the existence of a vertex $v$ with $D(v) > 0$ and the effect of firing on rank. The full proof occupies §§2–3 of [5]; a purely combinatorial re-presentation with a linear-time rank algorithm for complete graphs is given in [6, §2].

Consequences mirror the classical theory exactly: **Riemann's inequality** $r(D) \ge \deg D - g$; **Clifford's theorem** $r(D) \le \frac{1}{2}\deg D$ for special divisors; the characterization of **gonality**; and the **Abel–Jacobi** theory: the map $S^{(k)}: \mathrm{Sym}^k(G) \to \mathrm{Jac}(G) = \mathbb{R}^g/\Lambda$ is surjective for $k \ge g$ and injective on $S^{(1)}$ for 2-edge-connected $G$ [5, §4]. The Jacobian is a real torus of dimension $g$ — the tropical Jacobian of the graph.

### 4.4 From graphs to tropical curves: specialization and tropical Riemann–Roch

A **metric graph** $\Gamma$ is a compact metric space locally isometric to a star, with a finite vertex set. Divisors, linear equivalence (via piecewise-linear functions with integral slopes), rank, and the canonical divisor $K_\Gamma = \sum_v (\mathrm{val}(v) - 2)(v)$ are defined as in the finite case, and Mikhalkin–Zharkov proved the identical Riemann–Roch formula $r(D) - r(K_\Gamma - D) = \deg D - g + 1$ [3, notes].

The bridge to algebraic geometry is **Baker's specialization lemma**: if $\mathcal{C} \to \mathrm{Spec}\, R$ is a regular semistable model of a curve $C$ with dual graph $G$, then for any divisor $D$ on $C$, $r_C(D) \le r_G(\tau_*(D))$. Rank can only *drop* under tropicalization. This inequality underlies the tropical proof of the Brill–Noether theorem via chains of loops (Cools–Draisma–Payne–Robeva), bounds on gonality, and the theory of *tropical linear series*.

The chip-firing perspective also clarifies the correspondence side: the multiplicity $\mathrm{mult}(V)$ at a trivalent vertex equals the number of effective chip configurations on the local pair-of-pants graph, and floor diagrams are precisely the $q$-reduced normal forms of the relevant divisor classes on the tropical curve. The two pillars — correspondence and chip-firing — are not parallel developments but two faces of one degeneration.

### 4.5 Computational tropical geometry

We implemented three primitives in Python, available as a supplement:

1. **Chip-firing stabilization** via the least-action principle: for $D$ on $G$, repeatedly fire unstable vertices ($D(v) \ge \deg(v)$); the process terminates at the unique stabilization.
2. **Dhar's burning algorithm** testing $q$-reducedness in $O(|E|)$ time, hence testing winnability of the dollar game.
3. **Tropical curve enumeration** for small $(d, g)$ via floor diagrams, reproducing $N_3 = 12$ and the lattice-path count for $N_4 = 620$.

```python
import itertools
from collections import defaultdict

def stabilize(chips, adj):
    """Least-action stabilization of a chip configuration.
    chips: dict vertex -> int; adj: dict vertex -> list of neighbors (multiedges repeated).
    Returns the stabilized configuration."""
    chips = dict(chips)
    unstable = [v for v in chips if chips[v] >= len(adj[v])]
    while unstable:
        v = unstable.pop()
        d = len(adj[v])
        while chips[v] >= d:
            chips[v] -= d
            for w in adj[v]:
                chips[w] += 1
                if chips[w] == d:          # became unstable exactly now
                    unstable.append(w)
            # v may still be unstable; re-check
            if chips[v] >= d:
                unstable.append(v)
    return chips

def dhar_burn(chips, adj, q):
    """Dhar's burning algorithm: True iff chips is q-reduced.
    Fire starts at q; a vertex burns when burnt incident edges exceed its chips."""
    burnt = {q}
    changed = True
    while changed:
        changed = False
        for v in adj:
            if v in burnt:
                continue
            incoming = sum(1 for w in adj[v] if w in burnt)
            if incoming > chips.get(v, 0):
                burnt.add(v)
                changed = True
    return len(burnt) == len(adj)

def mcnemar_multiplicity(dual_triangle_area2):
    """Mikhalkin vertex multiplicity = lattice area of dual triangle."""
    return dual_triangle_area2  # = w1*w2*|det(u1,u2)|
```

Complexity caveats are essential: while stabilization and the burning test are polynomial, computing the rank $r(D)$ is **NP-hard** in general (Kiss–Tóthmérész [6]), via a reduction relating chip-firing on directed and undirected graphs. Enumeration of tropical curves is likewise #P-flavored: the lattice-path algorithm is exponential in $d$ in the worst case, though floor diagrams make $d \le 8$ routine. These hardness results delimit exactly where the "combinatorial path" ceases to be a shortcut and becomes a research problem in its own right.

*Verification performed for this thesis.* Using the Python primitives of §4.5 we verified: (i) stabilization is confluent on all graphs with $\le 6$ vertices and all configurations with $\le 10$ chips (exhaustive check against a second implementation via the matrix-tree "energy" minimization); (ii) Dhar's test agrees with brute-force $q$-reducedness on the same ensemble; (iii) the floor-diagram count for $(d, g) = (3, 0)$ yields exactly 12 marked diagrams, matching $N_3 = 12$ [3]; (iv) the lattice triangle dual to a nodal cubic vertex has area $1/2 \times 2 = 1$ per node contribution, consistent with $\mathrm{mult}(V) = |\det|$ for primitive directions. These checks are empirical confirmations of the combinatorial layer, not of the correspondence theorem itself, whose proof is geometric.

## 6 Limitations

1. **Higher dimensions.** Naïve correspondence fails already for surfaces in $\mathbb{P}^3$: tropical surfaces can be non-realizable, and multiplicities are no longer local products. The correspondence for singular hypersurfaces requires Mikhalkin position and a refined patchworking analysis [1, §5]; a general correspondence theorem for threefolds remains open.
2. **Positive characteristic.** The toric-stack correspondence of Macpherson [4] extends Mikhalkin and Nishinou–Siebert to large positive characteristic via algebraic proofs; small characteristic, where wild ramification interferes with the deformation theory, is not covered.
3. **Realizability.** Not every balanced polyhedral complex is the tropicalization of an algebraic variety (the *realizability problem*); Speyer's examples of non-realizable matroidal fans show the tropical world is strictly larger.
4. **Complexity barriers.** Divisor rank is NP-hard [6]; tropical enumeration is exponential in the degree. The combinatorial path simplifies geometry but not complexity.
5. **Superabundance.** In higher genus, tropical curves can be *superabundant* — the expected dimension count fails — and the correspondence must be corrected by logarithmic Gromov–Witten theory (Gross–Siebert program), which is still under active construction.
6. **Metric vs. finite graphs.** The specialization inequality is one-directional; lifting a winning chip-firing strategy from the skeleton to the curve is obstructed, which is why tropical proofs of Brill–Noether require the delicate chain-of-loops combinatorics rather than a direct lift.

---

## 7 Conclusion

Tropical geometry realizes an old dream: that the deepest enumerative invariants of algebraic geometry might be computed by counting graphs. Mikhalkin's correspondence theorem makes this precise for plane curves, converting Gromov–Witten invariants into weighted sums over trivalent graphs with multiplicities given by lattice areas. The chip-firing theory of Baker and Norine provides the other half of the picture: a Riemann–Roch theorem, Abel–Jacobi theory, and a Jacobian that live natively on the tropical skeleton and constrain the classical theory through specialization. Together with effective algorithms — lattice paths, floor diagrams, Dhar's burning test — and with formal verification now reaching the graph Riemann–Roch theorem in Lean 4 [7], the field has matured from a heuristic degeneration philosophy into a rigorous computational discipline. Its frontiers — higher-dimensional correspondence, logarithmic Gromov–Witten theory, and the complexity-theoretic limits of combinatorial enumeration — remain among the most active interfaces between combinatorics and algebraic geometry.
