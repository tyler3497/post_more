# Fully Homomorphic Encryption: From Gentry's Bootstrapping to TFHE Programmable Bootstrapping and the Fourth Generation

## Abstract

Fully homomorphic encryption (FHE) enables arbitrary computation on encrypted data without decryption — a possibility raised by Rivest, Adleman, and Dertouzos in 1978 but realized only with Craig Gentry's 2009 ideal-lattice construction [1]. This thesis traces the field across four generations. We formalize Gentry's bootstrapping theorem — that a *somewhat* homomorphic scheme able to homomorphically evaluate its own decryption circuit can be *recursively self-embedded* into a fully homomorphic one — together with its costs: decryption-circuit squashing and the circular-security assumption [1]. We analyze the second generation of Brakerski–Gentry–Vaikuntanathan (BGV) and Brakerski/Fan–Vercauteren (BFV) leveled schemes, which rebased security on (Ring-)LWE and managed noise via modulus switching and relinearization [2]; the third generation of Gentry–Sahai–Waters (GSW), Ducas–Micciancio (FHEW), and Chillotti et al. (TFHE) fast gate-bootstrapping constructions, cutting bootstrap latency from minutes to milliseconds [4][5]; and the fourth generation, where Cheon–Kim–Kim–Song (CKKS) approximate arithmetic and TFHE *programmable bootstrapping* recast bootstrapping as a function-evaluation primitive, culminating in the OpenFHE production library [3][6][7]. A complexity analysis compares the generations; we close with open problems in circular security, scheme switching, and parameter standardization.

---

## 1 Introduction

For three decades after Rivest, Adleman, and Dertouzos first asked whether encryption could support *homomorphisms* — structure-preserving maps under which `Dec(Eval(f, Enc(m))) = f(m)` — cryptography possessed only partial answers. RSA and ElGamal supported unbounded multiplication; Paillier supported unbounded addition; the Boneh–Goh–Nissim cryptosystem supported unlimited additions and exactly one multiplication [7].

Craig Gentry's 2009 STOC paper shattered that ceiling with the first *fully* homomorphic encryption scheme, based on ideal lattices [1]. Its centerpiece was *bootstrapping*: homomorphically evaluating the scheme's own decryption circuit on a noisy ciphertext — using an encrypted copy of the secret key published in the evaluation key — yields a fresh ciphertext encrypting the same plaintext with *reset* noise. If a scheme can bootstrap its own decryption circuit *plus one additional gate*, it can bootstrap indefinitely. This recursive self-embedding is Gentry's **bootstrapping theorem**, and every subsequent FHE scheme follows his blueprint: first construct a somewhat homomorphic scheme, then apply the bootstrapping transformation [1][7].

> **Theorem (Gentry's bootstrapping theorem, informal).** Let `E` be a somewhat homomorphic encryption scheme whose decryption circuit `D` (augmented by one homomorphic operation) lies within the class of circuits `E` can evaluate. Then there exists a fully homomorphic scheme `E*` derived from `E`, under the assumption that `E` remains secure when its secret key is encrypted under its own public key (*circular security*) [1].

This thesis examines how the field refined, accelerated, and generalized that theorem across four generations. The narrative has three intertwined threads:

1. **Noise management.** Every lattice-based ciphertext carries noise that grows under homomorphic operations; correctness demands it stay below a decryption threshold. Generations differ in *how* they keep it bounded: Gentry refreshed it by bootstrapping; BGV *switched moduli*; CKKS *rescaled* it and accepted approximation [2][3].
2. **Security foundations.** The field migrated from ideal-lattice and sparse-subset-sum assumptions to the (Ring-)LWE problem, bringing worst-case-to-average-case reductions and post-quantum resistance [2][7].
3. **Bootstrapping as computation.** TFHE's *programmable bootstrapping* (PBS) turns the refresh operation into a function evaluator: instead of merely resetting noise, the bootstrap simultaneously applies an arbitrary univariate function via a lookup table in a *test polynomial* [5].

The remainder: Section 2 covers background (lattices, LWE/RLWE, the noise model), Section 3 our methodology, Section 4 the deep dive, Section 5 complexity analysis, Section 6 limitations, and Section 7 the conclusion.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The LWE and RLWE problems

The *Learning With Errors* (LWE) problem asks an adversary to distinguish samples `(a, <a, s> + e)` — `a` uniform, `s` secret, `e` a small discrete-Gaussian error — from uniform random pairs. Solving LWE is as hard as approximating worst-case lattice problems (GapSVP, SIVP) to polynomial factors [7]. *Ring*-LWE works over polynomial rings `R_q = Z_q[X]/(X^N + 1)`, gaining efficiency from the NTT-friendly ring structure. Nearly all modern FHE is built on LWE/RLWE [2][7].

### 2.2 The noise model

In a typical LWE-based scheme, a ciphertext encrypting message `m` has the form `c = (a, b)` with `b = <a, s> + Δ·m + e`, where `Δ` separates the message from the noise `e`. Decryption computes `b − <a, s> = Δ·m + e` and rounds to recover `m` — which succeeds *only if* `|e| < Δ/2`. This inequality is the field's central constraint:

- **Homomorphic addition** adds noises: `e₁ + e₂`. Growth is *additive*.
- **Homomorphic multiplication** roughly multiplies them: `Δ·(m₁e₂ + m₂e₁) + e₁e₂`, plus key-switching noise. Growth is *multiplicative* and dominates depth limits.

Once `|e|` approaches `Δ/2`, the ciphertext is *indecipherable*. Everything in FHE is a strategy for keeping noise below this threshold across arbitrarily deep circuits [7].

### 2.3 Generational taxonomy

The community's standard periodization, articulated in the ACM Queue survey [7], is:

| Generation | Representative schemes | Defining innovation |
|---|---|---|
| First | Gentry (2009), DGHV | Ideal lattices; bootstrapping theorem |
| Second | BGV, BFV | (R)LWE security; leveling; modulus switching; relinearization |
| Third | GSW, FHEW (DM), TFHE (CGGI) | Approximate eigenvector method; fast gate bootstrapping via blind rotation |
| Fourth | CKKS, TFHE with PBS, OpenFHE | Approximate arithmetic; programmable bootstrapping; production libraries |

## 3 Methodology

This thesis is a *comparative analytical survey* grounded in the primary literature:

1. **Primary-source reconstruction.** For each generation we reconstruct the noise-growth equations and bootstrapping procedure from the original papers: Gentry's STOC 2009 construction and its Gentry–Halevi implementation (~30 minutes per bit operation, the performance baseline) [1]; the BGV leveled construction [2]; the FHEW gate-bootstrapping scheme [4]; the TFHE/CGGI external-product formulation [5]; the CKKS approximate-arithmetic construction [3]; and the OpenFHE library design paper [6].
2. **Complexity-theoretic comparison.** We extract asymptotic noise-growth bounds, per-gate costs, and bootstrapping latencies, tabulated against a common baseline — 128-bit security on comparable hardware — using published benchmarks summarized in the Duality Technologies bootstrapping survey [8].
3. **Architectural analysis.** We analyze how the *role* of bootstrapping changed — from periodic maintenance (generations 1–3) to a first-class compute operation (generation 4, PBS) — and the engineering consequences: key-size reductions (FHEW's 1 GB → TFHE's ~24 MB bootstrapping key [5]), hardware acceleration in OpenFHE [6], and ISO standardization.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Gentry's Bootstrapping: Decrypting Homomorphically

Gentry's construction begins with a *somewhat* homomorphic encryption (SWHE) scheme: one supporting, say, many additions but only a few multiplications before noise breaches the threshold. The refresh operation [1]:

1. Publish, as part of the evaluation key, an encryption of the secret key bits: `Enc_pk(sk)`.
2. Express the *decryption* function `D_sk(c)` as a Boolean circuit.
3. Evaluate that circuit **homomorphically** on the doubly-encrypted inputs: `Eval(D, Enc_pk(sk), c)`.

The output is a new ciphertext `c'` encrypting the same `m`, whose noise depends only on *one* homomorphic evaluation of the decryption circuit — a fixed, small quantity — rather than on all prior accumulated noise. Gentry called this **bootstrapping** [1].

Two obstacles had to be overcome. First, the SWHE decryption circuit had to be shallow enough for the SWHE scheme to evaluate it — Gentry achieved this via **squashing**, preprocessing the secret key into a sparse subset-sum representation that keeps the decryption polynomial low-degree, at the cost of assuming hardness of the *sparse subset sum* problem [1]. Second, **circular security**: publishing `Enc_pk(sk)` must not compromise the scheme, an assumption that remains unproven and is still open today.

> **Theorem (Bootstrappability criterion).** A SWHE scheme is *bootstrappable* if its decryption circuit's depth, plus the depth of one additional NAND gate, is within the scheme's evaluable depth. Any bootstrappable SWHE scheme yields a fully homomorphic scheme via recursive self-embedding [1].

The first implementation, by Gentry and Halevi, confirmed both the theory and the cost: roughly 30 minutes per basic bit operation [1]. FHE was *possible* but not *practical* — a gap the next generations would close by four to five orders of magnitude [7].

### 4.2 The Second Generation: Leveled Schemes, LWE, and Modulus Switching

The second generation, led by Brakerski, Gentry, and Vaikuntanathan (BGV) and by Brakerski and Fan–Vercauteren (BFV), made two decisive moves [2]:

**Move 1: Rebase security on (R)LWE.** The ideal-lattice and sparse-subset-sum assumptions were replaced by LWE/RLWE with worst-case-to-average-case reductions. Security became *standard*, and quantum resistance followed from the underlying lattice problems [2][7].

**Move 2: Leveling — postpone bootstrapping.** Given an a-priori depth bound `L`, BGV generates a chain of moduli `q₀ > q₁ > … > q_L` and, after each multiplication level, applies **modulus switching** — dividing the ciphertext by the noise factor to produce an equivalent ciphertext under a smaller modulus with proportionally smaller noise. Noise is *managed geometrically*, and bootstrapping becomes an optional optimization rather than a necessity [2].

Two companion techniques complete the picture:

- **Relinearization (key switching).** Homomorphic multiplication naturally produces a ciphertext decryptable under `s ⊗ s` — its dimension *squares* per multiplication. Relinearization collapses it back to the dimension of `s` using an evaluation key, at the cost of additional noise [7].
- **Batching / SIMD packing.** Via the Chinese Remainder Theorem over plaintext slots, one RLWE ciphertext encrypts a *vector* of messages; one homomorphic operation acts on all slots in parallel [2].

BGV and BFV differ in noise placement: BGV puts the message in the *most* significant bits and uses modulus switching; BFV puts it in the *least* significant bits with scale-invariant noise management. Both remain the workhorses for exact integer arithmetic, implemented in HElib, SEAL, PALISADE, and OpenFHE [6].

### 4.3 The Third Generation: GSW, FHEW, and Fast Gate Bootstrapping

The third generation attacked bootstrapping *latency*. The Gentry–Sahai–Waters (GSW) scheme introduced the *approximate eigenvector* method: ciphertexts are matrices `C` with `C · v ≈ m · v` for a secret eigenvector `v`, and homomorphic multiplication becomes *matrix multiplication* with only *additive* noise growth — eliminating relinearization [5][7].

Building on this, Ducas and Micciancio's **FHEW** ("Fastest Homomorphic Encryption in the West," Eurocrypt 2015) introduced *gate bootstrapping*: refreshing after *every single NAND gate* via a homomorphic accumulator and **blind rotation** [4]. Each gate's output is freshly bootstrapped, so noise never accumulates — roughly *half a second per gate*, versus six minutes per bootstrap in HElib [4].

Chillotti, Gama, Georgieva, and Izabachène (**TFHE/CGGI**, Asiacrypt 2016) reformulated FHEW's accumulator via a simpler *external product* between GSW and LWE ciphertexts, cutting gate bootstrapping to *under 0.1 seconds* (~13 ms per binary gate on one core) and shrinking the bootstrapping key from 1 GB to 24 MB at the same security level [5].

The mathematical core is **blind rotation**. Given an LWE ciphertext `(a, b)` with phase `φ = b − ⟨a, s⟩ ≈ Δ·m + e`, blind rotation homomorphically computes the monomial `X^φ` — exploiting the negacyclicity of `Z[X]/(X^N + 1)` with `q = 2N` so that arithmetic modulo `q` happens *in the exponent* at no extra cost — and uses it to rotate a *test polynomial* encoding the gate's truth table [5]. **Sample extraction** then pulls the constant term out as a fresh LWE ciphertext. In pseudocode:

```python
def blind_rotate(acc, a, b, bsk):
    # acc: RLWE encryption of test polynomial v(X)
    acc = acc * X^{-b}            # initial rotation by b
    for i, a_i in enumerate(a):
        # CMUX selects acc or acc * X^{a_i} using GGSW encryptions of s_i
        acc = cmux(bsk[i], acc, acc * X^{a_i})
    return acc                    # RLWE encryption of v(X) * X^{phi}
```



### 4.4 TFHE Programmable Bootstrapping: Computing While Refreshing

The pivotal fourth-generation insight is that blind rotation need not merely *refresh* — it can *compute*. In **programmable bootstrapping (PBS)**, the test polynomial `v(X)` encodes an arbitrary *lookup table*: coefficient `i` holds `f(m)` for the message value whose phase maps to position `i`. After blind rotation and sample extraction, the output LWE ciphertext encrypts `f(m)` with *fresh noise* [5]. One bootstrap thus evaluates *any* univariate function — ReLU, sigmoid approximations, comparisons — on small integers while resetting the noise budget.

Two properties make PBS transformative:

1. **Negacyclicity as a feature.** Since `X^N = −1`, the second half of the torus encodes the negated function; only *negacyclic* (antiperiodic) functions are directly representable, and arbitrary functions are handled by padding or domain doubling [5].
2. **Amortized non-linearity.** In leveled schemes, non-polynomial functions demand deep polynomial approximations or digit-extraction bootstraps. With PBS, each non-linearity costs *one* fast bootstrap (~tens of milliseconds in TFHE-rs), making encrypted neural-network inference and encrypted control flow practical [5][6].

The engineering lineage is direct: TFHE-rs (Zama's Rust implementation) provides the low-level PBS backend; Concrete and ConcreteML build lookup-table-based compilers and ML frameworks atop it; OpenFHE extends FHEW/TFHE plaintext spaces from bits to larger moduli with arbitrary function evaluation via PBS [6].

```rust
// Conceptual TFHE-rs programmable bootstrap (simplified)
let mut acc = trivial_rlwe(&test_poly_lut);   // LUT encoded as polynomial
blind_rotate_assign(&mut acc, &lwe_input, &bootstrapping_key);
let refreshed: LweCiphertext = sample_extract(&acc); // encrypts f(m), fresh noise
```

### 4.5 The Fourth Generation: CKKS Approximate Arithmetic and the Production Stack

Running parallel to PBS, Cheon, Kim, Kim, and Song's **CKKS** scheme (Asiacrypt 2017) rethought *plaintext semantics*. Observing that real-world data — sensor readings, ML weights — is already approximate, CKKS encrypts vectors of *complex numbers* and treats homomorphic noise as *just another rounding error*, akin to floating-point arithmetic [3]. The key operation is **rescaling**: after each multiplication the ciphertext modulus is divided by the scaling factor `Δ`, keeping the message scale fixed while precision loss stays bounded by circuit depth — exceeding unencrypted floating-point error by at most about one bit [3].

CKKS's native **SIMD batching** (tens of thousands of slots per ciphertext) gives it the highest bootstrapping *throughput* of any scheme for large real-valued workloads, even though a single CKKS bootstrap is slower than a TFHE gate bootstrap [8]. Its bootstrap is approximate: it refreshes the modulus chain while introducing a small, well-understood error [3][8].

The generation's capstone is **OpenFHE** (2022), the community-consolidated library merging PALISADE, HElib, HEAAN, and FHEW lineages. OpenFHE ships BGV, BFV, CKKS, FHEW, and TFHE under one API, adds CKKS bootstrapping and extended-plaintext-space FHEW/TFHE with PBS, introduces a **Hardware Acceleration Layer (HAL)** for FPGA/ASIC backends, and serves as the FHE backend for Google's Transpiler project [6]. The ISO effort is now considering BGV, BFV, CKKS, and TFHE as the four canonical schemes [7].

## 5 Empirical Results and Complexity Analysis

### 5.1 Bootstrapping latency across generations

The most dramatic trend in FHE history is the collapse of bootstrapping latency:

| Scheme (generation) | Bootstrap latency | Key material | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Gentry–Halevi (1st) | ~30 min / bit op | — | Proof of concept [1] |
| HElib BGV (2nd) | ~6 min / bootstrap | hundreds of MB | Batch-amortized [4] |
| FHEW (3rd) | ~0.5 s / gate | ~1 GB | Gate bootstrapping [4] |
| TFHE/CGGI (3rd) | ~13 ms / gate | ~24 MB | External product [5] |
| TFHE PBS (4th) | tens of ms / LUT eval | tens of MB | Computes *while* refreshing [5] |
| CKKS (4th) | seconds / bootstrap | hundreds of MB | Highest throughput for real vectors [8] |

From 30 minutes to 13 milliseconds is a factor of roughly **140,000×** — achieved not by hardware alone but by algorithmic restructuring: the external product, approximate gadget decomposition, and blind rotation [5][7].

### 5.2 Noise-growth regimes

The generations differ in noise asymptotics under a depth-`L` circuit:

- **First generation:** noise refreshed by bootstrapping; per-bootstrap cost dominates.
- **BGV/BFV (leveled):** per-gate computation `Õ(λ·L³)` without bootstrapping (RLWE, exponential-in-`L` approximation factor), or `Õ(λ²)` independent of `L` with bootstrapping as an optimization [2].
- **GSW/FHEW/TFHE:** per-gate bootstrapping with noise *independent* of circuit depth — each gate output has fixed, small noise [4][5].
- **CKKS:** noise grows linearly in depth; rescaling bounds the relative error, and bootstrapping refreshes the modulus chain approximately [3].

### 5.3 Throughput and scheme selection

Published benchmarks at the 128-bit security level show a clear division of labor [8]:

- **CGGI/TFHE** wins *latency* for Boolean circuits and small-integer logic: sub-0.1 s bootstraps with native LUT evaluation — the fit for comparisons, branching, and encrypted control flow.
- **CKKS** wins *throughput* for large real-valued workloads: one bootstrap refreshes tens of thousands of SIMD slots simultaneously, dominating privacy-preserving ML.
- **BGV/BFV** win *exactness*: integer arithmetic with zero approximation error, at throughput between the two, with digit-extraction bootstrapping when unbounded depth is needed.

> **Theorem (Bootstrapping divergence).** No single rounding strategy is optimal across plaintext types: exact integer schemes require digit extraction, approximate real schemes require modulus-chain refresh with homomorphic linear transforms, and torus-based schemes require blind rotation. The optimal scheme is determined by the computation's algebraic structure, not by raw gate speed [8].

### 5.4 Correctness argument sketch

For PBS, correctness reduces to two claims: (i) blind rotation computes `X^φ·v(X)` with error bounded by the gadget-decomposition approximation plus bootstrapping-key noise, and (ii) the phase `φ = Δ·m + e` lands in the coefficient window encoding `f(m)` whenever `|e|` is below the decoding radius. The TFHE analysis bounds the rotation error by `n·(k+1)·ℓ·N·σ_BK + n·(1+kN)·2^{−2(t+1)}` in the worst case — sample extraction adds *no* noise [5].

## 6 Limitations

Fundamental limitations remain — several inherited directly from Gentry's original construction:

1. **Circular security.** Every bootstrapping scheme publishes an encryption of (part of) the secret key. No general proof shows this is safe; it remains an *assumption*, and recent work formalizes bootstrapping as the transform from circular-secure HE to composable FHE [1].
2. **Performance vs. plaintext.** FHE remains 4–6 orders of magnitude slower than plaintext computation for general programs. PBS and CKKS batching narrow the gap for *structured* workloads (LUT-heavy logic, SIMD linear algebra) but unstructured branching still pays per-gate bootstrapping costs [7][8].
3. **PBS domain restrictions.** Programmable bootstrapping natively supports only negacyclic functions on small domains (typically ≤ 8–16 bits per bootstrap); larger functions require decomposition into multiple bootstraps or scheme switching [5][6].
4. **CKKS approximation.** CKKS bootstrapping is approximate: each bootstrap injects a small error, and errors *compound* across many bootstraps. Exact applications must still use BGV/BFV [3][8].
5. **Parameter fragility and standardization.** Security estimates for LWE parameters continue to be revised. The ISO effort toward standardizing BGV, BFV, CKKS, and TFHE parameter sets is ongoing but incomplete [6][7].
6. **Side channels and circuit privacy.** Homomorphic evaluation can leak the *circuit* being evaluated through timing and noise patterns; circuit-private constructions exist but add cost, and most libraries do not enable them by default.

## 7 Conclusion

The seventeen years from Gentry's 2009 breakthrough to the present trace a coherent arc. **Generation 1** proved *possibility*: bootstrapping converts any bootstrappable SWHE scheme into a fully homomorphic one [1]. **Generation 2** proved *leveled practicality*: BGV and BFV rebased security on (R)LWE and showed that leveling, modulus switching, and relinearization could defer bootstrapping across bounded-depth circuits [2]. **Generation 3** proved *speed*: FHEW's gate bootstrapping and TFHE's external-product blind rotation cut refresh latency from minutes to milliseconds and shrank keys from gigabytes to megabytes [4][5]. **Generation 4** proved *utility*: CKKS made approximate real arithmetic first-class, programmable bootstrapping turned the refresh operation into a function evaluator, and OpenFHE consolidated the schemes into a production stack with hardware acceleration [3][5][6].

The deepest lesson is that bootstrapping *changed meaning*. In 2009 it was maintenance — the price of unbounded depth. In TFHE's programmable bootstrapping it is the *compute primitive itself*: every non-linearity in an encrypted neural network, every comparison in an encrypted database query, is a bootstrap that simultaneously refreshes noise and evaluates a function. The overhead became the operation.

Open problems remain: circular security, unifying exact and approximate paradigms via scheme switching, parameter standardization, and the performance gap to plaintext. From 30 minutes per bit to 13 milliseconds per gate, FHE has traveled from theoretical curiosity to deployable infrastructure — and the fourth generation's synthesis of speed, expressiveness, and engineering maturity suggests the next decade will be defined not by *whether* we can compute on encrypted data, but by *what* we choose to compute.
