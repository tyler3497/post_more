# Optimal Control Theory: Pontryagin's Maximum Principle, the Linear-Quadratic Regulator, and Model Predictive Control

## Abstract

Optimal control theory formalizes the problem of steering a dynamical system along a trajectory that minimizes a prescribed cost functional while respecting dynamics, boundary conditions, and constraints. This article develops the three pillars of the field: the Pontryagin maximum principle (PMP), which furnishes first-order necessary conditions via the Hamiltonian, costate dynamics, and transversality conditions; the linear-quadratic regulator (LQR), which delivers an explicit optimal feedback law through the matrix differential and algebraic Riccati equations; and model predictive control (MPC), which converts finite-horizon open-loop optimization into a stabilizing feedback policy through the receding-horizon strategy. We present the Hamiltonian dynamical system and two-point boundary-value structure, derive the Riccati synthesis with convergence criteria, contrast dynamic programming and the Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman equation with the maximum principle, and characterize the stability theory of constrained MPC via terminal cost, terminal sets, and feasibility-implies-stability arguments. Applications in robotics and aerospace are discussed with quantitative performance evidence. Limitations — the curse of dimensionality, nonconvexity, and computational load of online optimization — delimit the frontier where learning-based approximations now operate.

## 1 Introduction

The problem of choosing control inputs to make a dynamical system behave *optimally* arises in every engineering discipline in which trajectories matter: launch vehicles must reach orbit with minimum propellant, robotic manipulators must track paths with bounded torque, and process plants must operate near economic optima without violating safety limits. Optimal control theory is the mathematical machinery that turns these desires into computable policies. Its intellectual lineage runs from the calculus of variations of Euler and Lagrange, through Bellman's dynamic programming, to Pontryagin's maximum principle and the receding-horizon methods that dominate industrial practice today.

The central object of study is a controlled dynamical system

$$\dot{x}(t) = f(x(t), u(t), t), \qquad x(t_0) = x_0,$$

with state $x \in \mathbb{R}^n$, control $u(t) \in \mathcal{U} \subset \mathbb{R}^m$, and a cost functional

$$J(u) = \Phi(x(t_f), t_f) + \int_{t_0}^{t_f} L(x(t), u(t), t)\, dt,$$

where $\Phi$ is the terminal (Mayer) cost and $L$ the running (Lagrangian) cost [1]. The task is to find $u^\star$ minimizing $J$ subject to the dynamics and any state or control constraints. Three complementary solution paradigms have emerged. First, the *indirect* approach of Pontryagin's maximum principle transforms the optimization problem into a Hamiltonian two-point boundary-value problem: necessary conditions on the optimal state, costate, and control. Second, the *direct* specialization to linear dynamics and quadratic cost — the LQR problem — admits a closed-form feedback solution via Riccati equations, and became the workhorse of aerospace and process control after Kalman [5]. Third, *model predictive control* sidesteps the global two-point boundary-value problem entirely: at each sampling instant it solves a finite-horizon optimal control problem from the measured state and applies only the first control, achieving feedback and constraint handling simultaneously, with stability certified by the landmark theory of Mayne, Rawlings, Rao, and Scokaert [7].

This article is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews the calculus-of-variations background, the Hamiltonian formalism, controllability, and observability. Section 3 derives the methodology: the maximum principle, the Riccati synthesis, the HJB equation, and the receding-horizon construction. Section 4 develops four deep dives into PMP structure, LQR theory, dynamic programming, and MPC. Section 5 presents proof sketches and quantitative evidence; Sections 6 and 7 catalogue limitations and conclude.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Calculus of variations and the Hamiltonian

Classical calculus of variations seeks extremals of functionals $J(x) = \int L(x, \dot{x}, t)\, dt$; the Euler–Lagrange equation $\frac{d}{dt}L_{\dot{x}} - L_x = 0$ is the ancestor of every necessary condition in optimal control. Pontryagin's group introduced the *control* Hamiltonian

$$\mathcal{H}(x, u, \lambda, t) = L(x, u, t) + \lambda^\top f(x, u, t),$$

where the costate (adjoint) vector $\lambda(t) \in \mathbb{R}^n$ plays the role of a Lagrange multiplier enforcing the dynamics. As derived from first variations of the augmented functional, the necessary conditions at an optimal triple $(x^\star, u^\star, \lambda^\star)$ are the state equation $\dot{x}^\star = \partial \mathcal{H}/\partial \lambda$, the costate equation $\dot{\lambda}^\star = -\partial \mathcal{H}/\partial x$, and the Hamiltonian maximization condition $\mathcal{H}(x^\star, u^\star, \lambda^\star, t) \geq \mathcal{H}(x^\star, u, \lambda^\star, t)$ for all admissible $u$ [1]. Recent work has shown these conditions can be embedded directly into physics-informed neural architectures, using the PMP residual as a training loss so that learned trajectories satisfy the maximum principle by construction [1].

### 2.2 Controllability and observability

Before optimizing, one must ask whether the system can be *steered* at all. For $\dot{x} = Ax + Bu$, the pair $(A, B)$ is controllable if the Kalman controllability matrix $\mathcal{C} = \begin{bmatrix} B & AB & \cdots & A^{n-1}B \end{bmatrix}$ has full row rank $n$; the dual notion of observability is governed by the observability matrix built from $(C, A)$. These rank conditions are not decorative: convergence of the Riccati equation to a stabilizing solution requires controllability of $(A, B)$ (or stabilizability) together with detectability of the cost-weighted pair [5].

### 2.3 Direct versus indirect methods

Numerical optimal control divides into *indirect* methods, which discretize the PMP boundary-value problem (shooting, collocation of the Hamiltonian system), and *direct* methods, which discretize the control and state first and solve a nonlinear program (multiple shooting, pseudospectral collocation). Indirect methods are exquisitely accurate when they converge but suffer from small convergence basins — the costate initial guess is notoriously hard to obtain — whereas direct methods are robust but only approximate the necessary conditions. The fuel-optimal aerospace literature illustrates the trade: indirect PMP solvers, sometimes accelerated by neural-network warm starts, achieve bang-bang thrust profiles with a precision direct methods struggle to match [3].

## 3 Methodology

Our methodological spine is the Bolza problem with fixed final time $t_f$ and free final state. The derivation proceeds in four steps.

1. **Augment the cost.** Introduce the costate $\lambda(t)$ and the augmented functional $\bar{J} = J + \int \lambda^\top (f - \dot{x})\, dt$. First-order variations $\delta x$, $\delta u$, $\delta \lambda$ yield the state, costate, and stationarity conditions.
2. **Maximize the Hamiltonian.** Pontryagin's needle variations strengthen stationarity $\partial \mathcal{H}/\partial u = 0$ into pointwise maximization over the admissible set $\mathcal{U}$, which correctly handles control saturation and bang-bang arcs where the classical stationarity condition fails [2].
3. **Specialize to LQ structure.** For $\dot{x} = Ax + Bu$ and quadratic cost, the value function ansatz $V(x,t) = x^\top P(t) x$ converts the HJB partial differential equation into the matrix differential Riccati equation, an ordinary differential equation solvable backward in time [5].
4. **Recede the horizon.** Instead of solving the infinite-horizon problem, solve the finite-horizon problem repeatedly from the current state; stability is recovered by design of the terminal ingredients [7].

> **Theorem (Pontryagin's maximum principle, Bolza form):** Let $(x^\star, u^\star)$ be optimal for the Bolza problem with fixed $t_0, t_f$ and free $x(t_f)$. Then there exists an absolutely continuous costate $\lambda^\star : [t_0, t_f] \to \mathbb{R}^n$ such that, almost everywhere, $\dot{x}^\star = \nabla_\lambda \mathcal{H}$, $\dot{\lambda}^\star = -\nabla_x \mathcal{H}$, $u^\star(t) \in \arg\max_{u \in \mathcal{U}} \mathcal{H}(x^\star(t), u, \lambda^\star(t), t)$, with transversality $\lambda^\star(t_f) = \nabla_x \Phi(x^\star(t_f))$ [1][2].

The maximum principle has been extended far beyond its classical continuous-time ODE setting — to sampled-data and time-scale formulations, where the Hamiltonian maximization condition must be weakened to a nonpositive-gradient condition in discrete time [2], to state-constrained problems, and even to mean-field control in Wasserstein spaces, where the costate becomes a measure-valued adjoint [4].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Maximum Principle: Hamiltonian Dynamics, Costates, and Transversality

The PMP converts an infinite-dimensional optimization problem into a finite-dimensional dynamical system of doubled dimension.

Several structural insights deserve emphasis:

- **Costate as shadow price.** The costate $\lambda_i(t)$ measures the marginal sensitivity of the optimal cost to perturbations of the state: $\lambda^\star(t) = \nabla_x V(x^\star(t), t)$ whenever the value function is differentiable, linking the maximum principle to dynamic programming.
- **Transversality conditions.** When the final state is free, $\lambda(t_f) = \nabla \Phi$; when it is fixed, $\lambda(t_f)$ is free instead. For free final time, the additional condition $\mathcal{H}(t_f) = -\partial \Phi / \partial t_f$ determines $t_f$. For infinite-horizon problems, the boundary condition is replaced by the asymptotic requirement $\lim_{t_f \to \infty} \lambda(t_f) = 0$.
- **Bang-bang and singular arcs.** When $\mathcal{H}$ is affine in $u$ and $\mathcal{U}$ is a polytope, the maximizing control lies at a vertex: the optimal control switches discontinuously (bang-bang). When $\partial \mathcal{H}/\partial u \equiv 0$ along an interval, the arc is *singular* and higher-order conditions (the generalized Legendre–Clebsch condition) are required. Fuel-optimal spacecraft trajectories are canonical: thrust is either at maximum or zero, with switching structure determined by the costate [3].
- **Sufficiency.** The Mangasarian conditions state that the PMP necessary conditions are also sufficient when $L$ and $f$ are jointly concave in $(x, u)$ and $\lambda(t) \geq 0$; Arrow's generalization relaxes this to concavity of the maximized Hamiltonian in $x$.

> **Theorem (Hamiltonian constancy):** For autonomous problems ($f, L$ independent of $t$) with free final time, the Hamiltonian is constant along optimal trajectories, and $\mathcal{H}(x^\star(t), u^\star(t), \lambda^\star(t)) \equiv 0$ when $t_f$ is free. This first integral is the optimal-control analogue of energy conservation in mechanics [2].

### 4.2 The Linear-Quadratic Regulator and the Riccati Equation

Consider the finite-horizon LQR problem

$$\min_u \int_0^T \left( x^\top Q x + u^\top R u \right) dt + x(T)^\top X x(T), \quad \dot{x} = Ax + Bu,$$

with $Q \succeq 0$, $X \succeq 0$, $R \succ 0$.

$$-\dot{P} = A^\top P + PA - PBR^{-1}B^\top P + Q, \qquad P(T) = X,$$

integrated backward from $T$ to $0$, with optimal feedback $u^\star(t) = -R^{-1}B^\top P(t) x(t)$ [5].

$$A^\top P + PA - PBR^{-1}B^\top P + Q = 0,$$

and the constant gain $K = R^{-1}B^\top P_\infty$ places all eigenvalues of $A - BK$ in the open left half-plane — the closed loop is asymptotically stable by construction [5].

The following Python routine integrates the DRE backward and verifies closed-loop stability:

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.linalg import solve_continuous_are, eigvals

# Double integrator: canonical benchmark for LQR tuning
A = np.array([[0., 1.], [0., 0.]])
B = np.array([[0.], [1.]])
Q = np.diag([10., 1.])   # penalize position error 10x velocity error
R = np.array([[0.1]])    # cheap control -> aggressive gains

P = solve_continuous_are(A, B, Q, R)   # algebraic Riccati solution
K = np.linalg.inv(R) @ B.T @ P         # optimal state-feedback gain
cl_poles = eigvals(A - B @ K)

print("P =\n", P)
print("K =", K.ravel())
print("closed-loop poles:", cl_poles)
assert np.all(np.real(cl_poles) < 0), "closed loop must be Hurwitz"

# Backward DRE integration (finite horizon T=5) converges to P
T, dt = 5.0, 1e-3
Pd = Q.copy()
for _ in range(int(T / dt)):
    dP = -(A.T @ Pd + Pd @ A - Pd @ B @ np.linalg.inv(R) @ B.T @ Pd + Q)
    Pd = Pd - dt * dP          # backward Euler on -dP/dt
print("DRE terminal error:", np.linalg.norm(Pd - P))
```

Weight selection encodes engineering intent: increasing $Q$ relative to $R$ produces faster, more aggressive regulation at the expense of control effort, while diagonal structure keeps tuning interpretable.

| Tuning change | Closed-loop bandwidth | Control effort | Robustness margins |
|---|---|---|---|
| Increase $Q$ | Higher | Larger | Phase margin shrinks |
| Increase $R$ | Lower | Smaller | Gain margin grows |
| Add terminal weight $X$ | Faster terminal convergence | Larger near $T$ | Improves finite-horizon stability |
| Diagonal $Q$ on velocities | Damping increases | Moderate | Better high-frequency roll-off |

The Haskell fragment below expresses the Riccati fixed-point iteration as a pure function, illustrating how the ARE solution can be phrased as the limit of a monotone operator on the positive-semidefinite cone:

```haskell
-- Discrete algebraic Riccati iteration: P_{k+1} = dare P_k
dare :: Matrix -> Matrix -> Matrix -> Matrix -> Matrix -> Matrix
dare a b q r p =
    let s  = r + tr b <> p <> b
        k  = inv s <> tr b <> p <> a
    in  q + tr a <> p <> a - tr a <> p <> b <> k

-- iterate to the stabilizing fixed point from P0 = Q
solveDARE a b q r = head $ dropWhile notConverged $ iterate (dare a b q r) q
  where notConverged (p1:p2:_) = norm (p1 - p2) > 1e-10
```

### 4.3 Dynamic Programming and the Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman Equation

Bellman's principle of optimality yields a functional equation for the value function $V(x, t) = \min_u J$.

$$-\frac{\partial V}{\partial t} = \min_{u \in \mathcal{U}} \left\{ L(x, u, t) + \nabla_x V \cdot f(x, u, t) \right\}, \qquad V(x, t_f) = \Phi(x),$$

whose characteristic curves are precisely the PMP Hamiltonian trajectories, with $\lambda = \nabla_x V$.

The catch is regularity.

| Approach | Object computed | Handles constraints | Dimensionality | Optimality |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| PMP (indirect) | Single extremal trajectory | Control bounds naturally | High ($n \sim 10^2$) | Local (necessary conditions) |
| HJB / dynamic programming | Global value function | Via state constraints in PDE | Low ($n \lesssim 5$) | Global (viscosity solution) |
| LQR | Feedback gain matrix | Unconstrained only | High (linear systems) | Global for LQ class |
| MPC | Receding-horizon policy | Explicit state/input constraints | Medium–high | Suboptimal, stabilizing |

### 4.4 Model Predictive Control and the Receding Horizon

Model predictive control resolves the tension between optimality and implementability.

$$\min_{\mathbf{u}} \sum_{i=0}^{N-1} \ell(x_{k+i|k}, u_{k+i|k}) + V_f(x_{k+N|k})$$

subject to $x_{k+i+1|k} = f_d(x_{k+i|k}, u_{k+i|k})$, $x_{k+i|k} \in \mathcal{X}$, $u_{k+i|k} \in \mathcal{U}$, and terminal constraint $x_{k+N|k} \in \mathcal{X}_f$, then applies only $u_{k|k}^\star$ to the plant and repeats at $t_{k+1}$ — the *receding-horizon* principle [7].

Stability of the resulting closed loop is not automatic — a naive finite-horizon controller can destabilize — and its certification is the central theoretical achievement of the field.

1. **Terminal cost** $V_f$ as a local control Lyapunov function inside a terminal set $\mathcal{X}_f$.
2. **Terminal constraint** $x_{k+N|k} \in \mathcal{X}_f$ with $\mathcal{X}_f$ positively invariant under a local stabilizing law.
3. **Feasibility implies stability:** the optimal value function $V_N^\star(x)$ serves as a Lyapunov function because the shifted tail of the previous optimal sequence is feasible at the next step, yielding the descent $V_N^\star(x_{k+1}) - V_N^\star(x_k) \leq -\ell(x_k, u_k)$ [7].

> **Theorem (MPC nominal stability):** Under the terminal-cost/terminal-set conditions above and continuity of the model, the origin of the MPC closed loop is asymptotically stable with region of attraction equal to the set of states feasible for the finite-horizon problem [7].

A receding-horizon loop in Rust-flavored pseudocode makes the online/offline decomposition explicit:

```rust
// Receding-horizon MPC: solve FHOCP, apply first move, shift horizon.
fn mpc_step(x0: &State, horizon: usize) -> Control {
    // 1. Solve the finite-horizon optimal control problem (QP/NLP).
    let seq: Vec<Control> = solve_fhocp(x0, horizon); // u*_{0..N-1}
    // 2. Receding horizon: apply ONLY the first element.
    let u_apply = seq[0].clone();
    // 3. The tail seq[1..] warm-starts the next solve (feasibility
    //    propagation underlies the stability proof [7]).
    warm_start(seq.into_iter().skip(1));
    u_apply
}
```

For discrete-event or logical specifications, the same receding-horizon idea can be expressed as a temporal-logic refinement loop:

```tla+
---- MODULE RecedingHorizon ----
EXTENDS Naturals, Sequences
VARIABLES x, plan, k
Init == x \in X0 /\ plan = <<>> /\ k = 0
Next ==
    \/ \E u \in Admissible(x) :      \* re-optimize from measured state
         plan' = Tail(Optimize(x))   \* optimal N-step sequence
       /\ x' = Dynamics(x, Head(plan'))
       /\ k' = k + 1
Spec == Init /\ [][Next]_<<x, plan, k>>
====
```

Modern extensions include *explicit MPC*, which precomputes the piecewise-affine control law offline for fast systems; *tube-based robust MPC*, which tightens constraints to guarantee satisfaction under bounded disturbances; and *economic MPC*, which optimizes a general economic stage cost, with stability recovered through dissipativity arguments.

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

**Proof sketch: convergence of the DRE.** Assume $(A, B)$ controllable and $Q \succ 0$. The DRE with $P(T) = X \succeq 0$ admits a unique positive-semidefinite solution. Monotonicity in the terminal data implies that extending the horizon can only increase $P(0)$; boundedness follows from the existence of *some* stabilizing linear feedback (guaranteed by controllability), whose finite cost upper-bounds $x_0^\top P(0) x_0$ for every $x_0$. The monotone bounded limit satisfies the ARE, and $A - BR^{-1}B^\top P_\infty$ is Hurwitz by a standard Lyapunov-contradiction argument [5].

**Proof sketch: feasibility implies stability for MPC.** Let $V_N^\star(x_k)$ be the optimal finite-horizon cost at $x_k$ with optimizer $\mathbf{u}^\star_k$. At $t_{k+1}$, the shifted sequence $\tilde{\mathbf{u}} = (u^\star_{1|k}, \dots, u^\star_{N-1|k}, \kappa_f(x^\star_{N|k}))$ — with $\kappa_f$ the local law rendering $\mathcal{X}_f$ invariant — is feasible by construction, and its cost satisfies $\tilde{V} = V_N^\star(x_k) - \ell(x_k, u^\star_{0|k}) + \Delta_f$ with $\Delta_f \leq 0$ by the terminal-cost Lyapunov property. Since $V_N^\star(x_{k+1}) \leq \tilde{V}$, we get $V_N^\star(x_{k+1}) - V_N^\star(x_k) \leq -\ell(x_k, u_k)$: $V_N^\star$ is a strict Lyapunov function [7].

**Quantitative evidence.** The practical impact of these theories is measurable:

- **Aerospace trajectory optimization.** Indirect PMP methods with neural-network warm starts have produced fuel-optimal low-thrust transfers matching high-fidelity collocation solutions to within fractions of a percent while cutting solve time by an order of magnitude, enabling onboard re-targeting [3].
- **Robotics.** LQR and its time-varying extension (iterative LQR / differential dynamic programming) are the inner loop of modern legged locomotion: whole-body MPC running at 50–100 Hz on quadrupeds solves a constrained LQ approximation each cycle, achieving dynamic gaits unattainable otherwise.
- **Process industry.** MPC governs thousands of refinery and chemical-plant loops worldwide; surveys report typical energy-efficiency improvements of 3–5% and substantial reductions in constraint violations relative to decentralized PID architectures [7].
- **Estimation duality.** The LQR–Kalman duality has been exploited to transfer Riccati solver technology between control and filtering, with the deterministic minimum-energy filter derived as the exact dual of the LQ tracking problem [5].

Partially observed settings extend the picture: a recent maximum principle on the belief space shows that the forward Fokker–Planck equation and backward Kolmogorov equation are generated by continuous and discrete Hamiltonians exactly as in the classical PMP, unifying filtering and control under one variational umbrella [6].

## 6 Limitations

No theory is without boundaries, and intellectual honesty requires stating them plainly.

- **The curse of dimensionality.** Global methods (HJB, dynamic programming) scale exponentially in state dimension; even approximate dynamic programming and reinforcement learning struggle beyond dozens of dimensions without strong structure.
- **Nonconvexity and local minima.** For nonlinear dynamics or nonconvex costs, PMP conditions are merely necessary: numerical solvers converge to local extremals, and certifying global optimality is generally intractable. Multiple shooting mitigates but does not eliminate the dependence on initialization.
- **Computational burden of MPC.** Solving a nonlinear program at every sampling instant demands powerful hardware and careful solver engineering; worst-case solve times are hard to bound, which complicates certification for safety-critical aerospace systems. Explicit MPC shifts the burden offline but its complexity grows exponentially with horizon and constraint count.
- **Model fidelity.** All three pillars assume a known model $f$. Plant–model mismatch degrades LQR margins and can destroy MPC stability guarantees; robust and adaptive variants exist but introduce conservatism or lose guarantees [7].
- **Nonsmoothness and hybrid phenomena.** Contact dynamics in robotics, switching in power electronics, and state-triggered constraints violate the smoothness hypotheses of the classical PMP; extensions exist — including time-scale and measure-valued formulations [2][4] — but the theory and numerics are less mature.
- **Stochasticity.** The results above are deterministic. Stochastic optimal control requires the second-order HJB equation or stochastic PMP with backward SDEs, a substantially harder analytical and computational regime.

## 7 Conclusion

Optimal control theory offers a rare combination: deep mathematical structure and immediate engineering utility. Pontryagin's maximum principle distills optimality into a Hamiltonian boundary-value problem, revealing the costate as the fundamental sensitivity object [1][2]. The linear-quadratic regulator shows that when structure is present — linear dynamics, quadratic cost — the abstract theory collapses into a matrix equation whose solution is a stabilizing feedback law, with a duality to optimal estimation that unifies control and filtering [5]. Model predictive control turns the finite-horizon approximation into a practical feedback technology, with constraint handling and stability certificates that have reshaped process industries, robotics, and aerospace [7]. The current frontier — learning dynamics and value functions from data, warm-starting indirect solvers with neural networks [3], and extending the maximum principle to belief spaces and mean-field limits [4][6] — suggests the classical pillars will endure as the scaffolding on which data-driven control is built.
