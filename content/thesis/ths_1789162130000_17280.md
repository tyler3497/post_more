# Kolmogorov–Arnold Networks: Spline-Parameterized Function Approximation, Representation Theory, and the Interpretability–Scaling Tradeoff

## Abstract

Kolmogorov–Arnold Networks (KANs) replace the fixed node activations and scalar weights of multilayer perceptrons with *learnable univariate functions on edges*, each parameterized as a B-spline plus a residual base [2]. Motivated by the 1957 Kolmogorov–Arnold representation theorem [4] — every continuous $f:[0,1]^n\to\mathbb{R}$ is a finite superposition of continuous univariate functions and addition — KANs reframe approximation around compositional structure rather than affine maps. This thesis reconstructs the theorem and its constructive proofs [5], derives the B-spline parameterization via the Cox–de Boor recurrence [6], and analyzes the KAN pipeline: grid extension, sparsification, pruning, and symbolification. We survey the basis-function zoo — Gaussian RBF (FastKAN) [3], Chebyshev [7], Fourier, wavelet variants — and the criticisms: cubic B-spline evaluation is far costlier than fused multiply-add, the theorem's inner functions are fractal and unlearnable, and on non-compositional data the MLP advantage evaporates. We close asking whether KANs can scale to the frontier problems defining contemporary deep learning.

## 1 Introduction

The multilayer perceptron is, mathematically, a monument to the universal approximation theorem: a composition of affine maps $x \mapsto Wx + b$ with fixed pointwise nonlinearities $\sigma$ can approximate any continuous function on a compact set to arbitrary precision. Architecturally, this means every expressive degree of freedom sits in the *weight matrices*, while the nonlinearities — ReLU, GELU, SiLU — are frozen, shared, and uninformative. Interpretability research has spent a decade retrofitting meaning onto these affine sandwiches: probing classifiers, sparse autoencoders, circuit tracing. The Kolmogorov–Arnold Network inverts this design. Motivated by the Kolmogorov–Arnold representation theorem [2,4], KANs eliminate linear weights entirely. Every edge of the computation graph carries a *learnable univariate function*, parameterized in the original formulation as a B-spline plus a residual SiLU base [2]:

$$\phi(x) = w_b\, b(x) + w_s \cdot \mathrm{spline}(x), \qquad b(x) = \frac{x}{1+e^{-x}}.$$

Nodes perform no nonlinearity at all; they simply sum incoming signals. The network is a composition of *function matrices* $\Phi_\ell$, where $\mathrm{KAN}(x) = (\Phi_{L-1} \circ \cdots \circ \Phi_0)(x)$ and each $\Phi_\ell$ is a matrix whose entries are univariate functions rather than scalars.

The shift is philosophically loaded. MLPs learn *where* to place a fixed nonlinearity by adjusting affine arguments; KANs learn the *shape* of the nonlinearity itself, edge by edge. This buys two things. First, parameter efficiency on compositional targets: when the ground truth is itself a composition of univariate functions — $f(x,y) = \exp(\sin(\pi x) + y^2)$ — a small KAN recovers the structure while an MLP must tile the domain with hidden units [2]. Second, interpretability: each edge function is a one-dimensional curve that can be plotted, inspected, pruned, and *symbolified* — fitted to closed-form expressions like $\sin$, $\exp$, $x^2$ via a symbolic library, enabling a human to read the discovered law off the network [2].

But the design is also a provocation to the efficiency orthodoxy of deep learning. A B-spline edge with grid size $G$ and order $k$ carries $G + k$ coefficients where an MLP edge carries one scalar. Evaluation requires the Cox–de Boor recurrence — memory-unfriendly, branch-heavy, hostile to the fused multiply-add pipelines that make MLPs fast on GPUs. The community response has been a proliferation of basis-function variants — Gaussian RBFs (FastKAN) [3], Chebyshev polynomials [7], Fourier modes, wavelets — each trading approximation locality, smoothness, and speed against one another. And critics have asked the uncomfortable questions: is the Kolmogorov–Arnold theorem even the right justification, given that its inner functions are typically fractal, non-smooth, and unlearnable? Do the reported scaling laws survive on real, high-dimensional, non-compositional data? This thesis takes these questions seriously, reconstructing the theory, the method, and the debate from the primary literature.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The Kolmogorov–Arnold representation theorem

In 1900, Hilbert's 13th problem asked whether the roots of a general seventh-degree equation — continuous functions of three variables — could be expressed as superpositions of continuous functions of *two* variables [1]. Hilbert conjectured not. In 1957, Kolmogorov proved something far stronger: every continuous function of $n$ variables is a superposition of continuous functions of *one* variable and addition [4].

> **Theorem (Kolmogorov–Arnold, 1957).** For every $n \geq 2$ there exist continuous univariate functions $\phi_{q,p} : [0,1] \to \mathbb{R}$ ($q = 1,\dots,2n+1$; $p = 1,\dots,n$) such that every continuous $f : [0,1]^n \to \mathbb{R}$ admits the representation
> $$f(x_1,\dots,x_n) = \sum_{q=1}^{2n+1} \Phi_q\left(\sum_{p=1}^n \phi_{q,p}(x_p)\right)$$
> for suitable continuous univariate *outer* functions $\Phi_q : \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$.

Two features matter for machine learning. First, the inner functions $\phi_{q,p}$ are *universal*: they depend only on $n$, not on $f$; all dependence on $f$ is absorbed into the $2n+1$ outer functions $\Phi_q$. Second, in Sprecher's refined formulation, a *single* universal inner function $\phi$ (up to shifts and scalings) suffices [5]. Structured modern proofs [5] make the construction explicit: the inner functions are monotone, built by a careful combinatorial interleaving of the cube's cells, and they are spectacularly non-smooth — typically Hölder continuous with small exponent, fractal in character.

This is the first fracture line between the theorem and the network. KANs do not learn the theorem's inner functions; they learn *smooth* surrogates (splines) organized in a *deeper, wider, but trainable* architecture that only rhymes with the theorem's two-layer skeleton. The theorem supplies inspiration and a compositional prior, not a training target.

### 2.2 MLPs and the universal approximation theorem

The foil is the classical result: an MLP with a single hidden layer, $f(x) \approx \sum_{i=1}^{N} a_i \sigma(w_i \cdot x + b_i)$, approximates any continuous $f$ on a compact set [2]. The nonlinearity $\sigma$ is fixed; expressivity comes from the number of units $N(\epsilon)$, which grows unfavorably with dimension and smoothness — the curse of dimensionality. KANs attack a different axis: instead of tiling the input space with more units, they enrich each edge with a learnable function, so that *structure* in the target (compositionality, smoothness) is absorbed by the edge parameterizations rather than by width.

### 2.3 Splines and the Cox–de Boor recurrence

The workhorse of the original KAN is the B-spline. Given a nondecreasing knot vector $t_0 \leq t_1 \leq \cdots \leq t_{m}$, the B-spline basis functions of degree $k$ are defined recursively [6]:

$$B_{i,0}(x) = \begin{cases} 1 & t_i \leq x < t_{i+1} \\ 0 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}, \qquad B_{i,k}(x) = \frac{x - t_i}{t_{i+k} - t_i} B_{i,k-1}(x) + \frac{t_{i+k+1} - x}{t_{i+k+1} - t_{i+1}} B_{i+1,k-1}(x).$$

A spline is then $s(x) = \sum_i c_i B_{i,k}(x)$ with learnable coefficients $c_i$. The classical approximation theory [6] gives the key quantitative fact: for a function $f \in C^{k+1}$, B-spline approximation with grid spacing $h$ achieves $\|f - s\| = \mathcal{O}(h^{k+1})$. This is the mathematical engine behind KAN's claimed *scaling laws*: refining the grid (decreasing $h$) improves approximation at a predictable polynomial rate in the number of parameters — a cleaner lever than adding MLP width, at least on smooth compositional targets [2].

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 The KAN layer

A KAN layer is a *function matrix* $\Phi = \{\phi_{q,p}\}$ mapping $\mathbb{R}^{n_{\text{in}}} \to \mathbb{R}^{n_{\text{out}}}$ via $x_{\ell+1,q} = \sum_{p} \phi_{\ell,q,p}(x_{\ell,p})$. Each univariate function is parameterized [2] as

$$\phi(x) = w_b\, b(x) + w_s \sum_{i=0}^{G+k-1} c_i B_{i,k}(x),$$

where $b(x) = \mathrm{SiLU}(x)$ is a fixed residual base (ensuring nonzero gradients and a good initialization near the identity-like regime), $\{c_i\}$ are the learnable spline coefficients on a grid of size $G$ with spline order $k$ (default $k=3$, cubic), and $w_b, w_s$ are learnable scalars (initialized $w_s = 1$, spline $\approx 0$). The residual form mirrors ResNet thinking: the network starts near a well-behaved baseline and deviates via the spline.

```python
import torch, torch.nn as nn

class KANEdge(nn.Module):
    """One learnable univariate edge function: SiLU residual + B-spline."""
    def __init__(self, grid_size=5, spline_order=3,
                 grid_range=(-1.0, 1.0)):
        super().__init__()
        self.k, self.G = spline_order, grid_size
        h = (grid_range[1] - grid_range[0]) / grid_size
        # extended knot vector with k repeated boundary knots
        grid = torch.arange(-spline_order, grid_size + spline_order + 1)
        self.register_buffer("grid", grid * h + grid_range[0])
        n_basis = grid_size + spline_order
        self.coef = nn.Parameter(torch.zeros(n_basis))   # c_i
        self.w_b = nn.Parameter(torch.ones(1))           # residual scale
        self.w_s = nn.Parameter(torch.ones(1))           # spline scale

    def bspline_basis(self, x):
        # Cox-de Boor recursion, vectorized over basis index
        B = ((x.unsqueeze(-1) >= self.grid[:-1]) &
             (x.unsqueeze(-1) <  self.grid[1:])).float()
        for d in range(1, self.k + 1):
            left  = (x.unsqueeze(-1) - self.grid[:-d-1]) / \
                    (self.grid[d:-1] - self.grid[:-d-1]).clamp_min(1e-8)
            right = (self.grid[d+1:] - x.unsqueeze(-1)) / \
                    (self.grid[d+1:] - self.grid[1:-d]).clamp_min(1e-8)
            B = left * B[..., :-1] + right * B[..., 1:]
        return B

    def forward(self, x):
        spline = (self.bspline_basis(x) * self.coef).sum(-1)
        return self.w_b * torch.nn.functional.silu(x) + self.w_s * spline
```

A `KANLayer` stacks $n_{\text{in}} \times n_{\text{out}}$ such edges; forward evaluation sums over inputs. The reference implementation's naive form expands the input to shape `(batch, out, in)` to apply distinct edge functions [2]; EfficientKAN's reformulation instead evaluates the shared B-spline basis once per input and reduces edge application to a matrix multiplication, dramatically cutting memory.

### 3.2 Grid extension: coarse-to-fine training

A distinctive KAN technique is *grid extension*: train on a coarse grid $G_1$, then refit the learned spline onto a finer grid $G_2 > G_1$ by least-squares projection of the old spline onto the new basis, and continue training [2]. Because splines are linear in their coefficients, the projection is exact in function space — the network's function is preserved while its resolution increases. This yields a multigrid-like training schedule and underwrites the empirical scaling curves: error versus parameter count follows the $\mathcal{O}(G^{-(k+1)})$ spline approximation rate on smooth targets.

### 3.3 Sparsification, pruning, and symbolification

Interpretability is engineered, not emergent, via a three-stage pipeline [2]:

1. **Sparsification.** An $L_1$-style penalty on the *mean absolute output* of each edge, $\lvert\phi_{q,p}\rvert_1 = \frac{1}{N}\sum_s \lvert\phi_{q,p}(x^{(s)})\rvert$, plus an entropy regularizer over the normalized edge magnitudes, drives irrelevant edges toward zero functions.
2. **Pruning.** Edges (and nodes) whose incoming/outgoing magnitudes fall below a threshold are removed; the shrunken graph is retrained briefly. A KAN that began $[4,4,1]$ may collapse to $[2,1,1]$ — literally readable.
3. **Symbolification.** Surviving edge functions are curve-fitted against a library of symbolic candidates ($\sin, \exp, x^2, \log, \dots$) by $R^2$; the best match replaces the spline, exposing an affine-symbolic skeleton. The network then reports a *closed-form hypothesis* for the data.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The representation theorem and its two-layer skeleton

The classical theorem describes a *shallow* architecture: one inner summation layer, one outer summation layer [4,5]. KANs generalize this to arbitrary depth — the theorem becomes the $L=2$ case of a broader family, just as the universal approximation theorem is the $L=2$ case of deep MLPs. Depth matters because the theorem's exact inner functions are unusable: they are universal but fractal, and gradient descent on smooth spline surrogates cannot find them. Depth compensates by letting the network *rediscover* compositional structure hierarchically — each layer learns smooth univariate pieces whose composition approximates the target. The honest reading is that KANs inherit the theorem's *compositional inductive bias* (sums of univariate functions of sums) while abandoning its exact constructions [5].

The same compositional prior can be stated as a typed program, which clarifies what the architecture assumes about the world:

```haskell
-- The Kolmogorov-Arnold skeleton as a typed combinator.
-- A KAN layer is a matrix of univariate functions; composition is layer stacking.
type Univariate = Double -> Double

kaLayer :: [[Univariate]] -> [Double] -> [Double]
kaLayer phi xs = [ sum [ f x | (f, x) <- zip row xs ] | row <- phi ]

kaNet :: [[[Univariate]]] -> [Double] -> [Double]
kaNet layers = foldl (flip (.)) id (map kaLayer layers)
-- Theorem: for n >= 2, some 2-layer net of this shape represents any
-- continuous f : [0,1]^n -> R. Practice: we learn smooth splines instead.
```

### 4.2 B-spline parameterization and the Cox–de Boor recurrence

Why B-splines rather than raw polynomials or Fourier modes? Three properties [6]: **local support** (each $B_{i,k}$ is nonzero on only $k+1$ knot intervals, so coefficient updates are local and gradients sparse), **partition of unity** ($\sum_i B_{i,k}(x) = 1$, giving numerical stability), and **optimal approximation order** ($\mathcal{O}(h^{k+1})$ for $C^{k+1}$ targets). The price is the recurrence itself: evaluating all basis functions costs $\mathcal{O}(k^2)$ per point with irregular memory access, versus $\mathcal{O}(1)$ fused multiply-adds per MLP weight. On GPUs this is a severe handicap — the original implementation is reported at orders of magnitude slower per parameter than an MLP, which is precisely what motivated the efficient variants.

### 4.3 The basis-function zoo: RBF, Chebyshev, Fourier, wavelets

The community rapidly generalized "learnable univariate function" beyond B-splines, producing a taxonomy of KAN variants [3,7]:

| Variant | Basis on edges | Key property | Tradeoff |
|---|---|---|---|
| Original KAN [2] | B-spline, order $k$, grid $G$ | Local support, $\mathcal{O}(h^{k+1})$ approx | Slow Cox–de Boor evaluation |
| EfficientKAN | B-spline, reformulated matmul | Same theory, far less memory | Sparsification regularizer altered |
| FastKAN [3] | Gaussian RBFs $\exp(-\lVert x-c\rVert^2/2h^2)$ | RBFs approximate cubic B-splines well; fast | Global support; needs LayerNorm to keep inputs in domain |
| ChebyKAN [7] | Chebyshev $T_n$, recurrence $T_n = 2xT_{n-1} - T_{n-2}$ | Spectral accuracy on $[-1,1]$; fast $\mathcal{O}(d)$ | Requires input normalization; Runge-type edge effects |
| FourierKAN | $\sin/\cos$ modes | Natural for periodic structure | Poor localization; Gibbs phenomena |
| WaveKAN | Wavelet bases | Multiresolution locality | More complex parameterization |

FastKAN's derivation is instructive [3]: a cubic B-spline is bell-shaped and well approximated by a Gaussian RBF, so replacing the recurrence with $\phi(r) = \exp(-r^2/2)$, $r = (x - c)/h$, on fixed centers yields an RBF network that is *functionally* a KAN but evaluates in a single vectorized pass. Chebyshev variants exploit the three-term recurrence for $\mathcal{O}(d)$ evaluation with near-minimax approximation on bounded intervals [7].

### 4.4 Interpretability machinery: sparsification, pruning, symbolification

The interpretability claim rests on the sparsification pipeline of §3.3, and its flagship demonstrations are genuinely striking: KANs *rediscovering* knot-theoretic invariants and the Anderson localization length formula from data [2]. The mechanism deserves scrutiny. Sparsification works because the $L_1$-on-output-magnitudes penalty is well-matched to the edge-function parameterization — an edge can be killed by flattening its curve, a continuous relaxation of subset selection. Symbolification then projects onto human-legible functions. The limitation is that symbolification succeeds exactly when the truth is in (or near) the symbolic library; on messy real data it returns the nearest library member, which may be a mirage. Interpretability here is *conditional on compositional, near-symbolic ground truth* — powerful for scientific discovery, less meaningful for natural data.

A TLA+-style sketch of the sparsification contract makes the intended invariant precise:

```tla+
------------------------------ MODULE KANSparsify ------------------------------
EXTENDS Reals, FiniteSets
CONSTANTS Edges, Threshold
VARIABLES mag  \* mag[e] = mean |phi_e(x)| over the batch

TypeOK == mag \in [Edges -> Real]

Prune ==
    /\ TypeOK
    /\ mag' = [e \in Edges |->
                 IF mag[e] < Threshold THEN 0 ELSE mag[e]]
    \* pruned edges are exactly the sub-threshold ones; survivors unchanged

Liveness == <>[][Prune]_mag
=============================================================================
```

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

The empirical record, as reported in the primary paper and follow-ups [2,3,7], clusters into four claims:

1. **Function fitting.** On synthetic compositional targets (e.g., $f(x_1,\dots,x_4) = \exp(\sin(x_1^2+x_2^2) + \sin(x_3^2+x_4^2))$), small KANs achieve RMSE orders of magnitude below MLPs with matched parameter counts, and the error scales as $\approx G^{-4}$ for cubic splines — the spline-theoretic rate — versus the slower, less predictable MLP scaling [2].
2. **PDE solving.** KAN-based physics-informed losses outperform MLP PINNs on several low-dimensional PDEs (Poisson, Burgers), attributed to the absence of spectral bias: splines fit high-frequency components that MLPs learn slowly [2].
3. **Symbolic discovery.** The sparsify–prune–symbolify pipeline recovers exact formulas on synthetic symbolic tasks and plausible laws on real scientific datasets [2].
4. **Efficiency variants.** FastKAN matches B-spline KAN accuracy at a fraction of the wall-clock cost [3]; ChebyKAN reports superior parameter efficiency on digit classification and synthetic approximation [7]; EfficientKAN's matmul reformulation removes the memory blowup of the naive implementation.

The *theoretical* scaling argument is the cleanest part: because each edge is a spline approximator with known rate $\mathcal{O}(h^{k+1})$, a KAN's error as a function of total parameters follows a predictable power law on smooth compositional targets — a "neural scaling law" with a derivation, not just a fit [2]. The caveat, developed in §6, is the restriction to *smooth compositional* targets. The theorem behind the rate is approximation theory [6]; the theorem behind the architecture [4,5] guarantees existence but says nothing about learnability — and its constructions are, provably, not smooth.

A minimal benchmark harness in Rust illustrates how one would fairly compare the scaling curves (parameter-matched, multiple seeds):

```rust
// Sketch: parameter-matched scaling comparison KAN vs MLP.
struct ScalingPoint { params: usize, rmse_kan: f64, rmse_mlp: f64 }

fn scaling_curve(target: fn(f64, f64) -> f64) -> Vec<ScalingPoint> {
    let mut pts = Vec::new();
    for grid in [3, 5, 9, 17] {
        // KAN params ~ edges * (grid + k); MLP params matched by width solve.
        let kan = train_kan(target, grid);      // cubic splines, k = 3
        let mlp = train_mlp(target, kan.n_params()); // width chosen to match
        pts.push(ScalingPoint {
            params: kan.n_params(),
            rmse_kan: kan.test_rmse(),
            rmse_mlp: mlp.test_rmse(),
        });
    }
    pts
}
```

## 6 Limitations

A serious assessment must confront the criticisms that have accumulated since 2024:

- **The inner functions are not learnable.** The classical theorem's universal inner functions are fractal and non-smooth [5]; no gradient-based spline learner approximates them. KANs therefore do not *implement* the theorem — they implement a smooth, deep generalization of its skeleton. The theorem is marketing as much as mathematics.
- **Wall-clock efficiency.** Cubic B-spline evaluation via Cox–de Boor is dramatically more expensive per parameter than MLP multiply-adds. KANs win *parameter* efficiency on compositional tasks but often lose *FLOP* and *latency* efficiency; the variant zoo [3,7] exists precisely to repair this.
- **The compositional prior is narrow.** On high-dimensional, non-compositional data — natural images, language — there is no evidence KANs beat MLPs, and the spline grid suffers its own curse of dimensionality if the target lacks additive structure. The impressive results are concentrated where the prior is true.
- **Symbolification is library-bound.** Discovering "laws" requires the truth to be near the symbolic library; otherwise the pipeline reports confident false positives.
- **Training pathologies.** Spline grids must cover the activation distribution; inputs drifting outside the grid (common early in training) silently degrade to the residual base, which is why grid updates and LayerNorm-style stabilization [3] are load-bearing, not optional.
- **Scale unproven.** No KAN has been trained at anything approaching frontier scale; optimizers, initialization, and normalization for deep spline networks remain under-explored relative to the transformer/MLP stack.

## 7 Conclusion

Kolmogorov–Arnold Networks are a genuine architectural idea: moving learnable nonlinearity from nodes to edges, parameterized by splines, reorganizes the approximation problem around compositional structure and yields a principled scaling theory inherited from spline approximation [2,6]. The representation theorem [4,5] provides the aesthetic and the inductive bias, if not a literal blueprint. The practical contribution is arguably the *pipeline* — grid extension, sparsification, pruning, symbolification — which turns a fitted network into a readable, sometimes symbolic, hypothesis [2]. The variant explosion [3,7] shows a community converging on the real tradeoff: expressivity per parameter versus evaluability per FLOP.

The open questions are empirical and large. Can the basis-function zoo produce a KAN layer that is FLOP-competitive with an MLP inside a transformer? Does the interpretability apparatus survive contact with non-synthetic data? And most fundamentally: is the compositional prior — sums of learned univariate functions — the right prior for the data regimes where deep learning actually wins? Until KANs are tested at scale on vision, language, or diffusion, they remain what the theorem itself is: a beautiful existence proof awaiting its domain of dominance.
