# Causal Discovery from Interventional Data: Constraint-Based and Score-Based Methods, the PC Algorithm, and the Frontier of Identifiability

## Abstract

We survey the theory and practice of causal discovery from observational and interventional data, with emphasis on the two dominant algorithmic paradigms — constraint-based search exemplified by the PC algorithm and score-based search exemplified by greedy equivalence search — and on the identifiability gains afforded by interventions. Beginning with structural causal models and the Verma–Pearl characterization of Markov equivalence, we develop the causal Markov condition, d-separation, and faithfulness as the axioms linking probability distributions to graphs. We give a precise account of the PC algorithm's skeleton-learning and v-structure orientation phases, its high-dimensional consistency properties, and its score-based counterpart GES/GIES together with the Meek conjecture proof. We then analyze how perfect and imperfect interventions refine Markov equivalence into interventional Markov equivalence classes, characterize identifiability as a function of intervention targets, and discuss differentiable relaxations of the combinatorial search. The treatment closes with empirical benchmarks, statistical and computational limitations, and open problems at the frontier of identifiability.

## 1. Introduction

Causal discovery — the automated inference of causal structure from data — sits at the intersection of statistics, machine learning, and the philosophy of science. Its central promise is audacious: given only samples from a joint distribution, or better yet from a *collection* of observational and interventional distributions, recover the directed graph that encodes the data-generating mechanism. This graph, typically assumed to be a *directed acyclic graph* (DAG), licenses predictions under intervention — the celebrated $do$-operator of Pearl — and therefore answers questions of the form "what would happen if we changed $X$?" that no purely associational model can answer.

Two intellectual traditions structure the field. The *constraint-based* tradition, originating with the PC algorithm of Spirtes, Glymour, and Scheines [1], treats conditional independence relations estimated from data as logical constraints on the space of admissible graphs, and reconstructs the skeleton and as many orientations as those constraints license. The *score-based* tradition treats graph learning as combinatorial model selection: assign every candidate DAG a penalized-likelihood score and search the space — or better, the space of equivalence classes — greedily [2]. For decades these two families competed on observational data, where both confront a hard epistemic ceiling: from passive observation alone, a DAG is identifiable only *up to Markov equivalence* [5].

Interventions break this ceiling. A perfect intervention $do(X_i = x)$ severs $X_i$ from its causal parents, replacing its mechanism with a point mass. As Hauser and Bühlmann [3] showed, a family of interventions induces a strictly finer partition of DAG space — the *interventional Markov equivalence class* (I-MEC) — which can shrink to a singleton as intervention targets accumulate. More recently, differentiable formulations [4] have recast the combinatorial search as continuous optimization over weighted adjacency matrices, trading theoretical guarantees for scalability.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 Structural causal models

A *structural causal model* (SCM) over endogenous variables $V = (V_1, \dots, V_p)$ consists of exogenous noise variables $U = (U_1, \dots, U_p)$ with joint distribution $P_U$, together with structural equations

$$V_i = f_i(\mathrm{PA}_i, U_i), \qquad i = 1, \dots, p,$$

where $\mathrm{PA}_i \subseteq V \setminus \{V_i\}$ denotes the *parents* of $V_i$. The parent relation defines a directed graph $\mathcal{G}$ over $V$; we assume it is acyclic. The noise variables are taken to be jointly independent, which enforces the *causal Markov condition*: each variable is independent of its non-descendants given its parents,

$$V_i \perp\!\!\!\perp \mathrm{ND}_i \mid \mathrm{PA}_i.$$

The observational distribution $P(V)$ then factorizes as $P(V) = \prod_{i=1}^{p} P(V_i \mid \mathrm{PA}_i)$, the factorization that score-based methods exploit through decomposable criteria [2].

### 2.2 d-separation and the Markov property

The graphical criterion that reads independencies off a DAG is *d-separation* (directional separation). A path between $X$ and $Y$ is *blocked* by $Z$ if it contains a non-collider in $Z$, or a collider whose descendants avoid $Z$. If every path is blocked, $X \perp_{\mathcal{G}} Y \mid Z$.

> **Theorem:** (Global Markov property.) If $P$ factorizes over the DAG $\mathcal{G}$, then $X \perp_{\mathcal{G}} Y \mid Z$ implies $X \perp\!\!\!\perp Y \mid Z$ in $P$.

The converse — *faithfulness* — asserts that every conditional independence in $P$ is entailed by d-separation in the true graph $\mathcal{G}$. Faithfulness is a strong and empirically fragile assumption (see §6), but without it, constraint-based inference collapses, because a single undetected dependence can derail the entire skeleton-learning phase [1].

### 2.3 Markov equivalence and CPDAGs

Two DAGs are *Markov equivalent* when they entail exactly the same d-separation relations, hence are indistinguishable from observational data under faithfulness. Verma and Pearl [5] gave the definitive graphical characterization:

> **Theorem:** (Verma–Pearl.) Two DAGs are Markov equivalent if and only if they share the same skeleton (undirected adjacency structure) and the same *v-structures* — induced subgraphs of the form $X \to Z \leftarrow Y$ with $X$ and $Y$ non-adjacent.

A Markov equivalence class is uniquely represented by a *completed partially directed acyclic graph* (CPDAG): edges directed in every class member are directed, the rest undirected — the maximally informative object identifiable from observation alone [1][5].

## 3. Methodology

### 3.1 Constraint-based learning: the PC algorithm

The PC algorithm (named for **P**eter Spirtes and **C**lark Glymour) proceeds in two phases [1]. Phase I learns the *skeleton*: from the complete undirected graph, delete edge $X$–$Y$ whenever some $Z \subseteq \mathrm{adj}(X) \setminus \{Y\}$ separates them, recording $Z$ as a *separation set*. Phase II orients edges: each unshielded triple $X$–$Z$–$Y$ becomes the v-structure $X \to Z \leftarrow Y$ iff $Z$ is absent from the recorded separation set; Meek's rules then propagate orientations to closure, yielding the CPDAG.

```python
def pc_skeleton(variables, ci_test, alpha=0.05):
    # Phase I of the PC algorithm: learn the skeleton.
    graph = complete_undirected_graph(variables)
    sepsets = {}
    order = 0
    while True:
        removed = False
        for (x, y) in ordered_adjacent_pairs(graph):
            candidates = [s for s in subsets(adj(graph, x) - {y}, order)]
            for z in candidates:
                if ci_test(x, y, z, alpha):      # e.g. Fisher's z on partial correlation
                    graph.remove_edge(x, y)
                    sepsets[(x, y)] = sepsets[(y, x)] = z
                    removed = True
                    break
        if not removed:
            if order >= max_degree(graph):
                break
            order += 1
    return graph, sepsets
```

The Gaussian conditional independence test is typically Fisher's $z$-transform of the partial correlation $\hat{\rho}_{XY \mid Z}$, rejecting $X \perp\!\!\!\perp Y \mid Z$ when $\sqrt{n - |Z| - 3}\,|z| > \Phi^{-1}(1 - \alpha/2)$; for discrete data, $G^2$ or $\chi^2$ tests on contingency tables are standard [1].

### 3.2 Score-based learning: GES and its interventional extension

Greedy Equivalence Search (GES) [2] traverses *equivalence classes* rather than DAGs: a forward phase greedily adds the single edge whose inclusion most improves the score, a backward phase greedily deletes edges. Chickering's landmark result was to prove the *Meek conjecture* — that whenever $\mathcal{H}$ is an independence map of $\mathcal{G}$, a sequence of edge additions and covered reversals transforms $\mathcal{G}$ into $\mathcal{H}$ — which implies GES is asymptotically consistent, recovering the true MEC with probability tending to one [2].

The following table summarizes the methodological landscape:

| Property | Constraint-based (PC) | Score-based (GES) | Interventional (GIES) | Differentiable (DCDI) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Search space | CI constraints | Equivalence classes | Interventional classes | Continuous matrices |
| Tuning | CI significance $\alpha$ | Penalty (BIC) | Intervention targets | Sparsity $\lambda$ |
| Consistency | Pointwise, under faithfulness | Uniform, under faithfulness | Under $\mathcal{I}$-faithfulness | Partial guarantees |
| Complexity | Exponential worst case; fast if sparse | Exponential worst case | Higher (multiple regimes) | Polynomial in $p$ (heuristic) |
| Identifiability | CPDAG (MEC) | CPDAG (MEC) | I-CPDAG (I-MEC) | Up to thresholding |

### 3.3 The d-separation oracle as a formal object

Formalizing d-separation as a pure graph predicate separates correctness from any statistical test. The following TLA+ sketch captures the blocking rule for a single path:

```tla+
------------------------------ MODULE DSeparation ------------------------------
EXTENDS Naturals, FiniteSets

VARIABLES path,   \* sequence of vertices v_1 ... v_k
          cond    \* conditioning set Z

IsCollider(i) == /\ i > 1 /\ i < Len(path)
                 /\ Edge(path[i-1], path[i]) = "in"
                 /\ Edge(path[i], path[i+1]) = "in"

IsBlocked(i) == \/ /\ ~IsCollider(i) /\ path[i] \in cond
               \/ /\ IsCollider(i)
                  /\ (Descendants(path[i]) \cup {path[i]}) \cap cond = {}

DSeparated == \E i \in 2..(Len(path)-1) : IsBlocked(i)
=============================================================================
```

And a Haskell rendering of the core PC skeleton loop, emphasizing its purely functional character:

```haskell
-- | PC skeleton learning over an abstract CI oracle.
pcSkeleton :: [Var] -> (Var -> Var -> Set Var -> Bool) -> Graph
pcSkeleton vars ci = go (completeGraph vars) 0
  where
    go g l
      | l > maxAdj g = g
      | otherwise    = go (prune g l) (l + 1)
    prune g l = foldr (tryRemove l g) g (adjacentPairs g)
    tryRemove l g (x, y) =
      case find (ci x y) (subsets l (neighbors g x \\ [y])) of
        Just s  -> deleteEdge x y g   -- record separation set s
        Nothing -> g
```

### 3.4 From observation to intervention

A *perfect* (or "hard") intervention $do(X_i = x)$ replaces the structural equation for $X_i$ by the constant $x$, deleting all incoming edges to $X_i$ in the *mutilated graph* $\mathcal{G}_{\overline{X_i}}$. Under $do(X_i = x)$, the distribution factorizes as $P(V \mid do(X_i = x)) = \prod_{j \neq i} P(V_j \mid \mathrm{PA}_j)\,\mathbf{1}\{X_i = x\}$. An *imperfect* ("soft") intervention instead perturbs the mechanism $f_i$ without severing parental influence — a better model of gene knockdowns and policy nudges [4].

Given a family $\mathcal{I}$ of intervention targets (including the empty target $\emptyset$ for the observational regime), two DAGs are *$\mathcal{I}$-Markov equivalent* if they entail the same d-separation relations in every mutilated graph $\mathcal{G}_{\overline{I}}$, $I \in \mathcal{I}$ [3]. The resulting I-MEC is represented by an *interventional essential graph* (I-CPDAG) and refines the observational MEC monotonically as targets are added; under the *conservative-target* condition [3] — each variable un-intervened in some regime — it is a well-defined object whose size is non-increasing in the number of distinct targets.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 The logic of v-structures: why colliders identify direction

The v-structure $X \to Z \leftarrow Y$ is the only local pattern observation alone can orient. With skeleton $X$–$Z$–$Y$ and $X$, $Y$ non-adjacent, faithfulness gives a separating set $S$ not containing $Z$. Under a chain or fork orientation, $Z$ itself would separate $X$ and $Y$ — a contradiction. The collider is thus the unique orientation under which $X$ and $Y$ are marginally independent yet become *dependent* given $Z$: Berkson's paradox as an identification strategy [1][5].

This logic generalizes to Meek's orientation rules. Rule R1: if $X \to Y$ and $Y$–$Z$ with $X$, $Z$ non-adjacent, orient $Y \to Z$ (else a new v-structure appears). Rule R2: if $X \to Z \to Y$ and $X$–$Y$, orient $X \to Y$ (else a cycle). Rules R3–R4 close the system: Meek showed the four rules are *complete* — they orient exactly the edges common to all DAGs in the class, producing the CPDAG [2].

### 4.2 The PC algorithm in detail: correctness and high-dimensional consistency

Under a perfect conditional-independence *oracle*, PC is sound and complete: Phase I returns the true skeleton (an edge survives iff no adjacency subset separates its endpoints), and Phase II orients exactly the v-structures plus Meek closure [1].

The finite-sample story was substantially advanced by Kalisch and Bühlmann [6], who proved *uniform consistency* of the PC algorithm for Gaussian DAGs in the regime $p = O(n^a)$ for any fixed $a > 0$ — the number of nodes may grow polynomially in sample size — provided maximal neighborhoods grow slower than $n$ and partial correlations are bounded away from zero. The proof controls family-wise error over the data-dependent collection of CI tests by a union bound exploiting the algorithm's adaptivity: conditioning sets are drawn only from *current* adjacencies, so in sparse graphs the algorithm never performs the full battery of tests [6].

A practical subtlety is *order dependence*: the skeleton can depend on the order in which variable pairs are tested, because an early erroneous deletion shrinks the adjacency sets used later. The PC-stable variant (Colombo–Maathuis) removes this dependence by freezing adjacency snapshots at each conditioning-set size $\ell$.

```rust
/// Fisher's z-test for conditional independence in the Gaussian case.
fn ci_test_fisher_z(x: usize, y: usize, z: &[usize],
                    corr: &Matrix, n: usize, alpha: f64) -> bool {
    let rho = partial_correlation(x, y, z, corr).clamp(-0.9999, 0.9999);
    let stat = 0.5 * ((1.0 + rho) / (1.0 - rho)).ln()
             * ((n as f64) - (z.len() as f64) - 3.0).sqrt();
    let cutoff = inverse_normal_cdf(1.0 - alpha / 2.0);
    stat.abs() <= cutoff   // true  => independent (remove edge)
}
```

### 4.3 Score-based learning and the Meek conjecture

GES operates on CPDAGs with two greedy phases. The *forward equivalence search* repeatedly applies the highest-scoring *valid* single-edge addition (an addition is valid if the resulting PDAG extends to a consistent DAG); the *backward phase* symmetrically deletes edges. Chickering [2] proved that if the scoring criterion is *locally consistent* (BIC and the Bayesian Dirichlet equivalent uniform score both qualify) and the generative distribution is faithful to some DAG, then in the large-sample limit the forward phase ends at an independence map containing the truth, and the backward phase then prunes to the true MEC.

The engine of the proof is the resolution of Meek's conjecture: if $\mathcal{H}$ is an independence map of $\mathcal{G}$, there exists a sequence of edge additions and *covered* edge reversals carrying $\mathcal{G}$ to $\mathcal{H}$ such that every intermediate graph remains an independence map of $\mathcal{H}$. Each step is score-improving under local consistency, so the greedy walk cannot get stuck before reaching the truth [2].

### 4.4 Interventional Markov equivalence and GIES

Hauser and Bühlmann [3] lifted the apparatus to the interventional setting. Their *Greedy Interventional Equivalence Search* (GIES) generalizes GES to multi-regime data: the score sums local log-likelihoods over regimes evaluated on the *mutilated* graphs. Search moves — insertions, deletions, and a *turning* phase flipping edge directions — act on interventional essential graphs, and GIES is consistent for the I-MEC under $\mathcal{I}$-faithfulness.

Identifiability gains are quantifiable. With a single perfect intervention on $X_i$, every edge incident to $X_i$ becomes oriented in the I-CPDAG: in regime $do(X_i)$, the mutilated graph has no parents of $X_i$, so any adjacency $X_i$–$Y$ must be $X_i \to Y$. Eberhardt-style bounds show that in the worst case $\lfloor \log_2 p \rfloor + 1$ experiments suffice to identify a DAG on $p$ variables fully, while adaptively chosen interventions can do exponentially better than random ones.

### 4.5 Differentiable relaxations

The combinatorial bottleneck motivated continuous-optimization reformulations. NOTEARS-style methods [4] encode the graph in a weighted adjacency matrix $W$ with the smooth acyclicity constraint $h(W) = \mathrm{tr}(e^{W \circ W}) - p = 0$. Brouillard et al. [4] extended this to interventional data: the objective sums negative log-likelihoods of normalizing-flow density estimators across regimes under one shared acyclicity penalty. Perfect interventions mask the intervened conditional, imperfect ones use regime-specific mechanisms, and unknown targets can be *learned* as latent masks.

The price of differentiability is theory. The acyclicity constraint is non-convex, the augmented-Lagrangian optimization lands at stationary points with no global guarantees, and the final graph is obtained by thresholding $\hat{W}$ — a step with no finite-sample justification. Nevertheless, [4] reports competitive structural Hamming distances against GIES on synthetic nonlinear benchmarks, particularly under *unknown* intervention targets where classical methods require the target list as input.

## 5. Empirical Results

We summarize representative benchmark findings from the literature; all numbers below are reported values from the cited studies, obtained on synthetic DAGs with known ground truth.

**PC vs. GES on sparse high-dimensional graphs.** Kalisch and Bühlmann [6] simulated DAGs with $p \in \{10, 30\}$ nodes, $n = 50$ samples, and expected neighborhood size $s \in \{0.1, 0.4\}$. With $p = 10$, $n = 50$, $s = 0.1$ over 50 replicates, PC ($\alpha = 0.01$) achieved average true positive rate (TPR) $0.57$, false positive rate (FPR) $0.02$, and true discovery rate (TDR) $0.91$; GES achieved TPR $0.85$ but FPR $0.13$ and TDR only $0.71$. The lesson is characteristic: *constraint-based methods are conservative* — they find fewer edges, but the edges they find are far more likely to be correct [6].

| Setting ($p=30$) | Method | TPR | FPR | TDR |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| $n=20$, $s=0.1$ | PC ($\alpha=0.01$) | 0.089 | 0.0082 | 0.78 |
| $n=20$, $s=0.1$ | GES (BIC) | 0.31 | 0.041 | 0.62 |
| $n=100$, $s=0.4$ | PC ($\alpha=0.01$) | 0.42 | 0.011 | 0.83 |
| $n=100$, $s=0.4$ | GES (BIC) | 0.68 | 0.052 | 0.66 |

*Representative values after Kalisch & Bühlmann [6]; TDR = fraction of discovered edges that are correct.*

**GIES with interventions.** Hauser and Bühlmann [3] simulated $p = 10$–$40$ node DAGs with one observational and several single-target interventional regimes. Structural Hamming distance (SHD) to the true I-CPDAG dropped monotonically with the number of distinct intervention targets, and GIES dominated both GES-on-pooled-data (inconsistent under interventions) and PC restricted to the observational regime. With targets covering all variables, SHD approached zero — full identification realized in practice [3].

**Differentiable methods.** On nonlinear synthetic benchmarks with neural-network mechanisms, Brouillard et al. [4] report that their flow-based interventional method attains lower SHD than GIES and IGSP, particularly under *unknown* intervention targets. The advantage narrows in the linear-Gaussian regime, where the likelihood is correctly specified for the classical scores [4].

The SHD metric used above, in compact Python:

```python
def structural_hamming_distance(est_cpdag, true_cpdag):
    # SHD between two CPDAG adjacency encodings (0/1/-1).
    shd = 0
    p = est_cpdag.shape[0]
    for i in range(p):
        for j in range(i + 1, p):
            a, b = est_cpdag[i, j], true_cpdag[i, j]
            if a == b:
                continue
            # edge present in one but not the other, or orientation mismatch
            shd += 1 if (a == 0) != (b == 0) or abs(a) != abs(b) else 2
    return shd
```

## 6. Limitations

**Faithfulness is fragile.** Near-cancellation of causal paths — e.g., a direct effect exactly offsetting an indirect one — produces independencies no d-separation entails. Such *unfaithful* distributions are Lebesgue-null under standard parametrizations, but *near*-violations, where a partial correlation is nonzero but tiny, are dense in parameter space and cause systematic skeleton errors. One failed CI test cascades through constraint-based search; score-based methods are somewhat cushioned by the penalty term but still inconsistent under gross unfaithfulness [1][2].

**Causal sufficiency fails in practice.** The PC algorithm assumes no unobserved confounders; a latent common cause induces a dependence no conditioning set removes, so PC inserts a spurious edge. The FCI algorithm relaxes sufficiency, outputting a *partial ancestral graph* with circle marks ($\circ$) where orientation is undetermined, at the cost of many more CI tests.

**Interventions are rarely perfect.** Real experiments — gene knockouts with off-target effects, encouragement designs — are imperfect or of unknown target. $\mathcal{I}$-faithfulness can fail when an intervention's mechanism interacts with the system, and unknown-target methods [4] trade identifiability for flexibility. Moreover, the $\lfloor \log_2 p \rfloor + 1$ worst-case bound counts *experiments*, not samples; each regime needs enough data for its own tests or likelihoods.

**Computational walls.** PC is exponential in the worst case (dense graphs), GES/GIES explore super-exponential DAG spaces heuristically, and differentiable methods [4] merely relocate the non-convexity. High-dimensional consistency [6] demands sparsity; without it, no known method scales.

## 7. Conclusion

Causal discovery has matured from a philosophical proposal into an engineering discipline with two rigorous pillars. Constraint-based methods, led by the PC algorithm [1][6], convert statistical independencies into logical constraints with admirable data efficiency on sparse problems; score-based methods, led by GES [2], convert the same problem into penalized model selection with strong large-sample guarantees rooted in the Meek conjecture. Interventions refine both: the I-MEC framework [3] quantifies exactly how much identifiability each experiment buys, and in the limit of comprehensive interventions the full DAG becomes identifiable.

The frontier is defined by the assumptions we cannot yet drop: faithfulness under near-violations, causal sufficiency under pervasive confounding, and the gap between the interventions our theorems assume and the messy perturbations our instruments deliver. Progress will come not from faster search alone, but from methods that *quantify their own identifiability* — reporting, alongside every edge, the precise assumptions on which its orientation depends.
