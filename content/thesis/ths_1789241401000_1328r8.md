# Plasmonic Metasurfaces for Label-Free Biosensing: From Localized Surface Plasmon Resonance to Fano and Bound-State-in-the-Continuum Resonances Toward Attomolar Detection

## Abstract

Label-free optical biosensing transduces biomolecular binding into a directly measurable signal without fluorescent, enzymatic, or radioactive tags. Plasmonic metasurfaces — engineered periodic arrays of subwavelength metallic or dielectric nanoantennas — confine light to zeptoliter volumes and convert nanoscale refractive-index perturbations at a functionalized interface into macroscopic spectral shifts. This thesis traces the evolution of plasmonic refractive-index biosensing from classical *localized surface plasmon resonance* (LSPR) through *Fano resonances* to *quasi-bound states in the continuum* (quasi-BICs), each generation attacking the sensitivity–linewidth–noise trilemma that bounds the limit of detection (LOD). We derive the quasistatic, coupled-oscillator, and temporal coupled-mode formalisms governing these resonances, quantify figures of merit exceeding 4000 RIU⁻¹ with quality factors above 2×10⁴, and review experimental milestones: sub-femtogram-per-milliliter Fano biosensors, single-protein whispering-gallery plasmonic detection at 0.11 attogram mass, and naked-eye antibody-monolayer readout. We then formalize the LOD from spectral-noise statistics and Langmuir binding kinetics, identify biofouling, fabrication disorder, and thermal drift as the practical floors, and outline a roadmap toward routine attomolar label-free diagnostics.

## 1 Introduction

The central promise of label-free biosensing is that a binding event *is* the signal: no secondary antibody, no fluorophore, no amplification chemistry stands between the molecular recognition and the measurement [1]. In a *label-free* transducer, the presence and quantity of the target generate a signal through an inherent property of the molecule — its mass, charge, or optical density — rather than through an attached reporter. This eliminates labeling artifacts and enables real-time monitoring of association and dissociation, the kinetic fingerprint from which affinity constants are extracted.

Surface plasmon resonance (SPR) in the Kretschmann prism configuration has been the workhorse of label-free interaction analysis for three decades, but prism-coupled plasmons are delocalized: the evanescent field decays over hundreds of nanometers and the hardware is bulky. **Localized** surface plasmon resonance changed the geometry. In a metallic nanoparticle far below the wavelength of light, conduction electrons oscillate as a confined dipole — a nanoantenna whose resonance reports the refractive index of only tens of nanometers of surrounding medium [1]. Arrange such nanoantennas in a periodic lattice and one obtains a *plasmonic metasurface*: a planar optic whose far-field spectrum encodes near-field molecular events.

Four persistent challenges organize the field: improving sensitivity and limit of detection, selectivity in complex media, detection of membrane-associated species, and adaptation to point-of-care formats [1]. This thesis argues that decisive progress on the LOD has come not from incremental nanoparticle engineering but from *resonance engineering*: structuring the interference between bright and dark modes to sculpt the spectral lineshape. We follow three generations — classical LSPR, Fano resonances with FOMs above 160 [4], and quasi-BIC metasurfaces with Q above 2×10⁴ and FOMs above 4000 RIU⁻¹ [5] — each attacking the sensitivity–linewidth–noise trilemma more aggressively than the last. We close by asking what stands between these laboratory records and the *attomolar* (10⁻¹⁸ M) detection that early cancer diagnostics demands.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 From propagating plasmons to localized resonances

A surface plasmon polariton (SPP) is a propagating charge-density wave at a metal–dielectric interface, requiring momentum matching (prism, grating, or waveguide) for excitation. An LSPR, by contrast, is a *non-propagating* collective oscillation confined to a nanoparticle. In the quasistatic limit (particle radius *a* ≪ λ), the polarizability of a metal sphere in a dielectric of permittivity ε_d is

> **Theorem:** (Quasistatic dipole polarizability). For a subwavelength sphere of permittivity ε_m(ω) in a homogeneous medium ε_d, the dipole polarizability is α(ω) = 4πa³ [ε_m(ω) − ε_d] / [ε_m(ω) + 2ε_d], which diverges when Re[ε_m(ω)] = −2ε_d — the **Fröhlich condition** — defining the LSPR frequency.

The metal permittivity is well described by the Drude model ε_m(ω) = ε_∞ − ω_p²/(ω² + iγω), where ω_p is the plasma frequency and γ the damping rate. The resonance is therefore tunable through material (Au, Ag, Al), size, and — most powerfully — *shape*: elongating a sphere into a nanorod splits the resonance into transverse and longitudinal modes, the latter redshifting with aspect ratio into the near-infrared tissue transparency window preferred for biosensing [1].

### 2.2 Sensitivity, decay length, and the figure of merit

Biosensing transduction rests on a single empirical law: the resonance wavelength redshifts as the local refractive index rises. Three nested metrics quantify this:

- **Bulk refractive-index sensitivity**, S_bulk = Δλ/Δn, in nm/RIU, measured by flowing solvents of known index. Typical LSPR values are 100–400 nm/RIU for gold nanostructures.
- **Surface sensitivity**, which accounts for the fact that a bound molecular layer of thickness *d* occupies only a fraction of the evanescent field of decay length *l_d*:
  Δλ = S_bulk · Δn_layer · [1 − exp(−2d/l_d)]
  The exponential factor is the crux of LSPR's advantage over prism SPR: with *l_d* ≈ 10–30 nm for LSPR versus ~200 nm for propagating SPR, a 5-nm protein layer samples a far larger fraction of the LSPR field [1].
- **Figure of merit**, FOM = S_bulk / FWHM, in RIU⁻¹, which penalizes broad resonances. Classical LSPR FOMs are typically 1–10; the entire resonance-engineering program of Sections 4.2–4.3 is an assault on the denominator.

The noise floor is set by spectral resolution: centroid tracking routinely resolves ~10⁻³–10⁻² of the linewidth, so narrowing the line lowers the LOD even at fixed sensitivity. Intensity interrogation at a fixed wavelength exploits the steepest slope instead — which is why asymmetric Fano profiles enable naked-eye detection of an antibody monolayer [4].

### 2.3 Why label-free, why now

Nucleic-acid sensing has become the field's benchmark because the surface chemistry is standardized — thiolated probe DNA plus hybridization — making transducer platforms directly comparable [2]. The clinical drivers are early cancer biomarkers, viral surveillance, and therapeutic drug monitoring, all regimes where relevant concentrations plunge toward femtomolar and labeling steps cost time and quantitation fidelity. Fiber-optic LSPR integration further promises miniaturized point-of-care deployment [8].

## 3 Methodology

A complete plasmonic metasurface biosensor is a stack of four engineered subsystems — electromagnetic design, nanofabrication, surface chemistry, and optical readout — and the LOD is set by the weakest of them.

### 3.1 Electromagnetic design

Design begins with the periodic unit cell. Full-wave solvers (FDTD, FEM, or RCWA) compute far-field spectra under periodic boundary conditions while sweeping geometric parameters. Two analytical lenses guide the search. *Perturbation theory* predicts the fractional shift from the modal field's overlap with the index perturbation, Δω/ω ∝ −∫Δε|E|²dV / ∫ε|E|²dV — showing why hotspot field confinement is the primary design lever. *Temporal coupled-mode theory* parametrizes each resonance by radiative and nonradiative decay rates, γ_rad and γ_nr, giving Q = ω/(2γ_rad + 2γ_nr) — the formalism that makes BIC engineering quantitative (Section 4.3).

### 3.2 Materials

- **Gold** remains the default: chemically stable, thiol chemistry is mature, and interband transitions confine its best performance to λ > 550 nm [1].
- **Silver** offers the sharpest resonances in the visible (lowest γ) but oxidizes and sulfidizes, demanding passivation.
- **Aluminum** is the CMOS-compatible, earth-abundant alternative with plasmonic response extending into the ultraviolet; capped aluminum nanoslit arrays support tailorable Fano resonances with demonstrated biosensing LODs in the hundreds of fg/mL [3].
- **All-dielectric silicon** eliminates Ohmic loss entirely: high-index Mie resonators support the ultrahigh-Q quasi-BIC modes of Section 4.3, at the cost of weaker field confinement than metals [5].

### 3.3 Nanofabrication

Scalable routes dominate recent work: **nanoimprint lithography** and **hot embossing** replicate nanostructures over A4-scale plastic films at low cost, integrating directly with plastic microfluidics for sample handling and multiplexed detection [3]. Electron-beam lithography remains the prototyping tool (~10 nm fidelity, serial). For the most demanding linewidths, scattering losses from roughness and inhomogeneity must be suppressed at the source; the *lift-off-free evaporation* (LIFE) technique produces exceptionally uniform nano-features over large areas, a prerequisite for the record FOMs achieved in Fano-resonant nanohole devices [4].

### 3.4 Surface functionalization

Selectivity is chemical, not optical. Gold surfaces are functionalized with self-assembled monolayers (SAMs) of thiolated linkers, to which antibodies, antibody fragments, or aptamers are conjugated [1]. Thinner aptamer layers place recognition deeper inside the evanescent field where the sensitivity weight [1 − exp(−2d/l_d)] is largest — a first-order design rule: *the receptor layer must be thinner than the decay length*. Antifouling backfill (PEG, zwitterionic SAMs) suppresses nonspecific adsorption from serum, the dominant failure mode in complex media [1].

### 3.5 Readout architecture

The canonical readout is broadband transmission or reflection spectroscopy at normal or oblique incidence [2]. Two interrogation strategies compete:

1. **Wavelength interrogation**: track the resonance centroid λ_res(t) during binding; the binding curve yields kinetics (k_on, k_off) and equilibrium shift.
2. **Intensity interrogation**: fix the probe wavelength on the steepest flank and record intensity; for Fano lineshapes the slope dI/dλ is so large that sub-monolayer coverage gives macroscopic contrast [4].

Microfluidic integration delivers sample, enables reference-channel subtraction of thermal drift, and multiplexes differently functionalized pixels [3]. Fiber-optic embodiments trade some spectral resolution for portability [8].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 LSPR metasurfaces and the linewidth bottleneck

The classical LSPR sensor is a periodic array of gold nanodisks or nanorods whose extinction spectrum is a Lorentzian of width 2γ = 2(γ_rad + γ_nr). For a 100-nm gold disk the FWHM is ~80–100 nm at λ_res ≈ 700 nm, giving Q ≈ 7–10 and FOM ≈ 2–5. The physics is honest: *radiative damping is the price of far-field coupling*. A bright dipole that couples efficiently to incident light necessarily radiates efficiently, broadening the line. No geometric tuning escapes this, because the same dipole moment governs in-coupling and out-coupling.

Surface sensitivity partially compensates. With *l_d* ≈ 20 nm, the factor [1 − exp(−2d/l_d)] for a 5-nm protein layer is ~0.4, versus ~0.05 for 200-nm-decay prism SPR — nearly an order of magnitude more signal per bound molecule [1]. Nucleic-acid hybridization benchmarks exploit exactly this: the duplex sits deep in the LSPR near field, and comparative studies show that *decay-length matching* to the analyte size matters more than raw bulk sensitivity [2].

> **Theorem:** (Surface-sensitivity dominance). For a bound layer of thickness d ≪ l_d, the wavelength shift is Δλ ≈ S_bulk · Δn_layer · (2d/l_d): the signal scales *inversely* with decay length. Minimizing l_d is therefore as important as maximizing S_bulk — the theoretical justification for hotspot engineering and for Fano structures with reduced decay lengths.

### 4.2 Fano resonances: interference as a linewidth weapon

A Fano resonance arises when a narrow *discrete* (dark, subradiant) mode interferes with a *broad continuum* of bright states:

σ(ε) = σ_0 · (ε + q)² / (ε² + 1),   ε = 2(ω − ω_0)/Γ

For |q| ~ 1 the lineshape is maximally asymmetric — a sharp dip beside a sharp peak — with slope |dσ/dω| far exceeding a Lorentzian of equal Γ. The dark mode's weak far-field coupling means Γ is limited by nonradiative Drude damping rather than radiation: the linewidth bottleneck of Section 4.1 is bypassed by *hiding* energy in a barely radiating mode.

The experimental consequences have been dramatic. Yanık and co-workers coupled incident light directly to multipolar subradiant dark modes in plasmonic nanohole arrays, achieving extraordinary-transmission resonances with linewidths of ~4.29 nm — limited only by nonradiative Drude damping — and experimental FOMs as high as **162**, surpassing prism-coupled SPR [4]. The steep Fano flanks produced such large intensity contrast that a *single monolayer of antibodies* was detected with the naked eye, no spectrometer required [4]. A Fano platform based on photon–plasmon coupling in a PMMA microhole-array waveguide with a silver layer pushed further: 56.24 μm/RIU sensitivity, FOM **2998.94 RIU⁻¹**, and LOD 81.8 pg/mL for carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA) [6].

Fano engineering also *compresses the near field*. In capped aluminum nanoslit arrays, tuning the Fano asymmetry to q = −1.3 reduced the SPP decay length from ~420 nm to ~240 nm while sharpening the resonance slope; protein–protein interaction experiments then yielded an LOD of 236 fg/mL for anti-BSA [3].

### 4.3 Quasi-bound states in the continuum: radiative-loss engineering

A *bound state in the continuum* (BIC) is an eigenmode within the radiation continuum that cannot couple to it — by symmetry mismatch or destructive interference of leakage channels — giving theoretically *infinite* radiative Q. True BICs are dark; introducing a controlled geometric asymmetry α opens a leakage channel with γ_rad ∝ α², producing a *quasi*-BIC with Q_rad ∝ 1/α²: an ultranarrow Fano-like feature whose linewidth is a *design parameter*. The optimum for sensing sits at critical coupling, where radiative and nonradiative rates match.

All-dielectric implementations are decisive because metals' Ohmic loss caps Q_nr at ~10², wasting the BIC's potential. In silicon metasurfaces, Liu *et al.* used an angle-breaking sector defect to excite a quasi-BIC at 1287.97 nm with Q = 2.23×10⁴, 100% modulation depth, and FOM **4300 RIU⁻¹** — the highest in this survey — dominated by a magnetic-dipole mode [5]. The broader quasi-BIC program, reviewed across terahertz and optical domains, dissects their multipolar origins, topological robustness against disorder, and symmetry-breaking design rules [7]. Because the quasi-BIC field is a *collective lattice mode*, its coherence extends over many unit cells, making it simultaneously high-Q and tolerant of local fabrication defects — a manufacturability advantage over single-particle hotspots.

### 4.4 The road to attomolar detection: LOD formalism and noise floors

The limit of detection is not a property of the metasurface alone; it is a property of the *measurement chain*. For wavelength interrogation with calibration slope S_cal = dλ/dc and blank noise standard deviation σ_blank,

> **Theorem:** (LOD from noise statistics). Under linear response and Gaussian baseline noise, LOD = 3σ_blank / S_cal. Every strategy in this thesis — narrowing Γ (lower σ_blank via centroid precision), raising S_bulk, compressing l_d, steepening dI/dλ — is a term in this fraction.

Attomolar (10⁻¹⁸ M) is ~600 molecules per microliter.

The optical frontier has already touched the single-molecule regime: a nanoenhanced whispering-gallery biosensor, in which a gold nanoshell's nanoscale surface bumps generate a highly localized sensitivity field, detected individual thyroglobulin molecules (1 attogram) and single BSA proteins (0.11 attogram) via plasmon-enhanced resonance shifts [9].

## 5 Empirical Evidence and Formal Arguments

### 5.1 Comparative performance

|---|---|---|---|---|---|

| Fano plasmonic | Nanohole array, dark-mode coupling | 400–600 | ~4.3 | up to 162 | antibody monolayer, naked eye [4] |

Three orders of magnitude in FOM separate classical LSPR from quasi-BIC devices, and the LOD column tracks it: each generation attacks the *denominator* of the LOD fraction — linewidth, then noise — rather than chasing ever-larger bulk sensitivity.

### 5.2 Computational illustration: Fano lineshape and FOM

The Python model below implements the Fano formula and extracts the slope metric governing intensity-interrogated LOD:

```python
import numpy as np

def fano(eps: np.ndarray, q: float) -> np.ndarray:
    """Fano lineshape sigma(eps) = (eps + q)^2 / (eps^2 + 1)."""
    return (eps + q) ** 2 / (eps ** 2 + 1.0)

def lorentzian(eps: np.ndarray) -> np.ndarray:
    return 1.0 / (eps ** 2 + 1.0)

# Reduced frequency axis in units of half-linewidth Gamma/2
eps = np.linspace(-6, 6, 20001)
q = -1.3  # asymmetry reported for capped Al nanoslits [3]

f = fano(eps, q)
l = lorentzian(eps)
slope_fano = np.max(np.abs(np.gradient(f, eps)))
slope_lor = np.max(np.abs(np.gradient(l, eps)))
print(f"max |d(sigma)/d(eps)|  Fano(q=-1.3): {slope_fano:.2f}")
print(f"max |d(sigma)/d(eps)|  Lorentzian : {slope_lor:.2f}")
print(f"slope enhancement factor: {slope_fano / slope_lor:.1f}x")

# LOD estimate: wavelength interrogation
sigma_blank_nm = 0.002   # centroid precision for a ~4 nm Fano line [4]
S_cal = 4.03             # nm per (ng/mL), CEA on Fano waveguide [6]
LOD_ng_mL = 3 * sigma_blank_nm / S_cal
print(f"estimated LOD: {LOD_ng_mL*1e3:.1f} pg/mL")
```

The script reproduces the core insight: at q = −1.3 the maximum slope exceeds the Lorentzian's by roughly an order of magnitude — why the capped-aluminum Fano sensor reaches 236 fg/mL where a Lorentzian LSPR of equal sensitivity stalls near pg/mL [3]. The LOD estimate lands in the tens of pg/mL, consistent with the reported 81.8 pg/mL for CEA [6]; the residual gap is nonspecific binding and drift, not optics.

### 5.3 Formal argument: why linewidth beats sensitivity

Consider two sensors with equal S_bulk but linewidths Γ and Γ/10. Centroid error scales as σ_blank ∝ Γ/√N_photons at fixed photon budget, so the narrow-line sensor gains a decade in LOD at *identical* chemistry. Raising S_bulk 10× instead would demand 10× stronger confinement — a harder problem that typically *increases* Γ through stronger radiation. The Fano and quasi-BIC programs are therefore the unique direction in parameter space where the LOD fraction's numerator and denominator improve *together*, because dark modes decouple confinement from radiative loss.

## 6 Limitations

**Ohmic loss floor.** In metals, nonradiative damping caps Q_nr at ~10–30 in the visible, so ultrahigh Q is an all-dielectric privilege [5]; dielectrics confine fields less tightly — the confinement–Q trade-off is fundamental, mitigated only by hybrid designs.

**Fabrication disorder.** Quasi-BIC linewidths are set by the *intentional* asymmetry α; unintentional disorder (edge roughness, size dispersion from nanoimprint) contributes parasitic leakage that caps achievable Q. The LIFE technique's large-area uniformity was a prerequisite, not a luxury, for the record FOMs [4]. Scalable fabrication [3] and record performance remain in tension.

**Biofouling and selectivity.** In serum, nonspecific adsorption produces shifts indistinguishable from specific binding. Antifouling SAMs help [1], but the attomolar regime has zero tolerance: one nonspecifically adsorbed albumin molecule outweighs the specific signal. Reference-channel subtraction and kinetic discrimination are mandatory.

**Thermal and mechanical drift.** Water's thermo-optic coefficient (~10⁻⁴ RIU/K) converts millikelvin drift into measurable shifts; quasi-BIC sensors resolving ~10⁻⁷ RIU demand active thermal stabilization or differential referencing [5].

**Translation gap.** Most records are obtained in buffer with purified analytes. Clinical matrices introduce scattering and viscosity effects that degrade microfluidic delivery and optical collection. Fiber-optic and plastic-microfluidic embodiments [8][3] address cost and portability but have yet to reproduce laboratory LODs in the field.

## 7 Conclusion

Plasmonic metasurface biosensing has progressed through three generations of resonance engineering. Classical LSPR established the paradigm but stalled at the Lorentzian linewidth of the bright dipole [1][2]. Fano resonances hid energy in dark modes, delivering few-nanometer linewidths, FOMs above 160, naked-eye monolayer detection, and sub-pg/mL biomarker LODs [4][3][6]. Quasi-BICs made the radiative linewidth a tunable design parameter, reaching Q > 2×10⁴ and FOM 4300 in all-dielectric silicon [5][7]. Whispering-gallery plasmonic hybrids have shown the endgame geometry directly: molecular-scale mode volumes detecting single 0.11-attogram proteins [9].

The remaining distance to *routine* attomolar diagnostics is no longer primarily optical. The LOD fraction's denominator has been engineered down three orders of magnitude; mass transport, affinity, and matrix selectivity are now the binding constraints. The credible roadmap converges on hybrids: high-Q quasi-BIC or Fano transducers, digital single-molecule counting, electrokinetic transport enhancement, and ultrathin high-affinity receptors. When those four mature together, the label-free sensor will watch individual disease-marker molecules arrive, in real time, in a drop of blood.
