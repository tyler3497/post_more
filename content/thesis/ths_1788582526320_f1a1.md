# Scaling Cooperative Intelligence: Centralized Training, Monotonic Value Factorization, and Mean-Field Approximation in Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning

## Abstract

Cooperative multi-agent reinforcement learning (MARL) seeks to learn decentralized policies for teams of agents that must coordinate under partial observability while training may exploit privileged global information. This thesis develops a unified account of the **centralized training with decentralized execution (CTDE)** paradigm and its four most influential instantiations: *QMIX* with its monotonic value factorization and the Individual-Global-Max (IGM) consistency guarantee; *MAPPO*, which adapts the proximal policy optimization clipped surrogate to a centralized critic and decentralized actors; *mean-field reinforcement learning*, which scales coordination to populations of $10^4$ agents by approximating pairwise interactions through an aggregate mean action; and *counterfactual multi-agent (COMA) policy gradients*, which solve the multi-agent credit assignment problem via a baseline that marginalizes out a single agent's action while holding teammates fixed. We present the mathematical foundations, derive the key objectives and convergence properties, compare representational classes, report representative empirical results on the StarCraft Multi-Agent Challenge (SMAC), and delineate the fundamental limitations — representational, statistical, and computational — that bound each approach. We argue that a synthesis of factorization constraints, trust-region policy updates, mean-field limits, and counterfactual reasoning offers the most promising path toward scalable cooperative intelligence.

## 1. Introduction

Single-agent reinforcement learning has produced landmark results in games, robotics, and combinatorial optimization, yet the vast majority of real-world control problems — traffic networks, distributed sensor grids, warehouse robot fleets, and autonomous vehicle platoons — are *intrinsically multi-agent*. In these settings, a collection of $n$ agents interacts with a shared environment, each perceiving only a local observation $o_i$ and selecting a local action $a_i$, while the team receives a shared reward $r(s, \mathbf{a})$. The resulting problem, formalized as a *decentralized partially observable Markov decision process* (Dec-POMDP) [1], is NEXP-complete even for finite horizons, and its joint action space $|\mathcal{A}|^n$ grows exponentially in the team size.

Naive independent learning suffers from **non-stationarity** as teammates' policy updates shift each agent's perceived dynamics, while fully centralized control faces an exponential joint action space and cannot deploy under communication constraints.

The **centralized training with decentralized execution (CTDE)** paradigm resolves this tension with an asymmetric-information design: during training, algorithms may condition on the global state $s$, the joint action $\mathbf{a}$, and all agents' observation histories — information available in simulation or in a laboratory — while the deployed policies $\pi_i(a_i \mid \tau_i)$ condition only on each agent's *local* action-observation history $\tau_i$. This single architectural idea underpins the modern MARL canon:

1. **Value factorization methods** (VDN, QMIX [2], and successors such as Weighted QMIX [3]) learn a centralized joint action-value $Q_{\text{tot}}$ that *factors* into per-agent utilities, so that greedy decentralized action selection recovers the centralized argmax.
2. **Centralized-critic policy gradients** (COMA [4], MADDPG, and MAPPO [5]) learn decentralized stochastic policies guided by a centralized value function, addressing credit assignment through counterfactual or advantage-based reasoning.
3. **Mean-field methods** [6] extend coordination to thousands of agents by replacing the joint action with its empirical mean, reducing an $n$-body interaction to a two-body interaction between an agent and the population.

This thesis formalizes CTDE within the Dec-POMDP framework around three sub-problems — **representation**, **decentralization**, and **credit assignment** — then develops each pillar rigorously: QMIX's monotonicity and its limits, MAPPO's trust-region surrogate and its engineering determinants, mean-field scaling theory, and counterfactual credit assignment, anchoring theory in SMAC and large-population evidence.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 Dec-POMDPs and the CTDE Objective

A cooperative Dec-POMDP is a tuple $\langle \mathcal{S}, \{\mathcal{A}_i\}, \{\mathcal{O}_i\}, P, R, \{\Omega_i\}, \gamma \rangle$, where $s \in \mathcal{S}$ is the global state, $\mathbf{a} \in \times_i \mathcal{A}_i$ the joint action, $P(s' \mid s, \mathbf{a})$ the transition kernel, $R(s, \mathbf{a})$ the shared reward, and $\Omega_i(o_i \mid s', \mathbf{a})$ the observation model for agent $i$. Each agent maintains an action-observation history $\tau_i^t = (o_i^0, a_i^0, \dots, o_i^t)$ and executes a decentralized policy $\pi_i(a_i \mid \tau_i)$. The team objective is

$$J(\boldsymbol{\pi}) = \mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}\left[\sum_{t=0}^{\infty} \gamma^t R(s_t, \mathbf{a}_t)\right],$$

where the expectation is over the joint policy $\boldsymbol{\pi} = (\pi_1, \dots, \pi_n)$ and the environment dynamics.

CTDE algorithms optimize $J$ using, at training time, quantities that depend on $s$ and $\mathbf{a}$ — the *centralized* information — while the final artifact is a set of *decentralized* policies. This creates a recurring design pattern with three coupled decisions:

| Decision | Question | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Representation** | What centralized object is learned? | $Q_{\text{tot}}(s, \boldsymbol{\tau}, \mathbf{a})$, $V_\phi(s)$, $Q_\psi(s, \mathbf{a})$ |
| **Decentralization** | How are local policies extracted? | IGM argmax, actor networks $\pi_{\theta_i}(a_i \mid \tau_i)$ |
| **Credit assignment** | How is global reward attributed? | Counterfactual baselines, advantage decomposition, difference rewards |

### 2.2 From VDN to the IGM Principle

Value Decomposition Networks (VDN) proposed the simplest factorization,

$$Q_{\text{tot}}(\boldsymbol{\tau}, \mathbf{a}) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} Q_i(\tau_i, a_i),$$

which trivially satisfies the property that maximizing each $Q_i$ locally maximizes $Q_{\text{tot}}$ globally. QMIX [2] generalized this observation into a *principle* — the **Individual-Global-Max (IGM)** condition:

> **Definition (IGM):** A joint action-value $Q_{\text{tot}}$ and per-agent utilities $\{Q_i\}$ satisfy IGM if
> $$\arg\max_{\mathbf{a}} Q_{\text{tot}}(\boldsymbol{\tau}, \mathbf{a}) = \left(\arg\max_{a_1} Q_1(\tau_1, a_1), \dots, \arg\max_{a_n} Q_n(\tau_n, a_n)\right).$$

IGM is *exactly* the condition needed for decentralized execution to be consistent with centralized training: if it holds, agents can act greedily and independently at deployment while implementing the jointly optimal policy learned centrally. A sufficient condition for IGM, and the one QMIX enforces structurally, is **monotonicity**:

$$\frac{\partial Q_{\text{tot}}}{\partial Q_i} \geq 0 \quad \forall i \in \{1, \dots, n\}.$$

The remainder of this thesis examines how QMIX implements this constraint with hypernetworks (§4.1), how policy-gradient methods achieve decentralization without any factorization constraint (§4.2), how mean-field theory scales beyond the small-$n$ regime where these methods operate (§4.3), and how counterfactual reasoning sharpens the learning signal (§4.4).

## 3. Methodology

Our analysis proceeds along four methodological axes, each corresponding to a canonical algorithm family. We treat each as an answer to the CTDE design problem posed in §2.1, and we evaluate each against three criteria: *representational capacity* (which joint value functions or policies can be expressed), *decentralization fidelity* (whether local execution recovers centralized intent), and *scalability* (how sample and computational complexity grow with $n$).

We analyze four instantiations as answers to the §2.1 design problem: **(A)** QMIX's mixing network $Q_{\text{tot}} = f_\theta(s, Q_1, \dots, Q_n)$ with hypernetwork-generated non-negative weights, its TD loss, and the projection Weighted QMIX [3] corrects; **(B)** MAPPO's clipped surrogate $L^{\text{CLIP}}$, centralized-critic training with GAE, and the implementation practices decisive for performance [5]; **(C)** the mean-field reduction $Q^i(s, \mathbf{a}) \approx Q^i(s, a^i, \bar{a}^i)$ and subsampling estimators converging at $\tilde{O}(1/\sqrt{k})$ [7]; **(D)** COMA's counterfactual advantage $A^i(s, \mathbf{a})$ and its single-forward-pass critic parameterization [4].

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 QMIX: Monotonic Mixing Networks and the IGM Guarantee

QMIX [2] estimates the joint action-value as a *state-conditioned, non-linear, monotonic* combination of per-agent utilities:

$$Q_{\text{tot}}(\boldsymbol{\tau}, \mathbf{a}, s; \theta) = f_\theta\big(s, Q_1(\tau_1, a_1), \dots, Q_n(\tau_n, a_n)\big), \qquad \frac{\partial f_\theta}{\partial Q_i} \geq 0.$$

The architecture has two stages. Each agent $i$ runs a recurrent (DRQN-style) utility network mapping its history $\tau_i$ to values $Q_i(\tau_i, \cdot)$ over its own actions. A **mixing network** — a feed-forward network whose *weights* (but not biases) are constrained to be non-negative — combines the utilities into $Q_{\text{tot}}$. The non-negative weights are produced by **hypernetworks**: separate networks taking the global state $s$ as input, with an absolute-value (or softplus) activation on the output layer guaranteeing $W(s) \geq 0$ elementwise. Because the mixing network is a composition of non-negative linear maps and monotone nonlinearities (e.g., ELU), the end-to-end map $Q_i \mapsto Q_{\text{tot}}$ is monotone, and IGM follows:

> **Theorem (QMIX consistency):** If $\partial Q_{\text{tot}} / \partial Q_i \geq 0$ for all $i$, then the joint greedy action $\mathbf{a}^\star = \arg\max_{\mathbf{a}} Q_{\text{tot}}$ satisfies $a_i^\star = \arg\max_{a_i} Q_i(\tau_i, a_i)$ for every agent $i$. Hence decentralized greedy execution implements the centralized greedy policy.

Training minimizes the squared TD error over mini-batches sampled from a replay buffer:

$$\mathcal{L}(\theta) = \sum_{b=1}^{B} \Big( Q_{\text{tot}}(\boldsymbol{\tau}, \mathbf{a}, s; \theta) - \underbrace{\big(r + \gamma \max_{\mathbf{a}'} Q_{\text{tot}}(\boldsymbol{\tau}', \mathbf{a}', s'; \theta^-)\big)}_{y^{\text{tot}}} \Big)^2,$$

where the $\max$ over joint actions is tractable *precisely because* of IGM: it decomposes into $n$ independent per-agent maximizations, avoiding the $|\mathcal{A}|^n$ enumeration.

**The representational price.** Monotonicity is sufficient but not necessary for IGM, and the restriction is real: QMIX cannot represent joint values in which an agent's *ordering* over its own actions depends on teammates' actions — the canonical failure being payoff matrices requiring coordinated "matching" behavior. Rashid et al. [3] formalized this by viewing QMIX as an operator that computes $Q$-learning targets and then *projects* them into the monotone-representable set under a uniform weighting over joint actions. They proved this projection can fail to recover the optimal policy even given $Q^\star$, because approximation error on *suboptimal* joint actions distorts the argmax. **Weighted QMIX** repairs the projection by upweighting the TD error on joint actions with high estimated value (CW-QMIX uses a centralized weighting; OW-QMIX an optimistic one), and proves recovery of the correct maximal action for arbitrary $Q$-values. Empirically, both variants outperform QMIX on predator-prey and hard SMAC maps [3].

### 4.2 MAPPO: Clipped Surrogates with a Centralized Critic

While QMIX is value-based, **MAPPO** [5] adapts Proximal Policy Optimization to CTDE: decentralized actors $\pi_\theta(a_i \mid o_i)$ (typically parameter-shared across agents) are guided by a centralized critic $V_\phi(s)$ that conditions on the global state. The policy optimizes the clipped surrogate:

$$L^{\text{CLIP}}(\theta) = -\mathbb{E}_t\Big[ \min\big( r_t(\theta)\hat{A}_t,\; \text{clip}(r_t(\theta), 1-\epsilon, 1+\epsilon)\,\hat{A}_t \big) \Big], \qquad r_t(\theta) = \frac{\pi_\theta(a_t^i \mid o_t^i)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(a_t^i \mid o_t^i)},$$

where $\hat{A}_t$ is the advantage estimated by GAE from the centralized critic, and the critic minimizes $L^{\text{VF}}(\phi) = \mathbb{E}_t[(V_\phi(s_t) - R_t)^2]$ with PopArt value normalization. The clipping implements a first-order trust region: it removes the incentive to move the policy ratio $r_t(\theta)$ outside $[1-\epsilon, 1+\epsilon]$ when doing so would increase the surrogate, thereby bounding per-update policy shift without the second-order machinery of TRPO.

Yu et al. [5] showed that careful implementation *dominates* more elaborate off-policy methods on SMAC, Multi-Agent MuJoCo, and Google Research Football. Five practices proved decisive:

1. **Value normalization** (PopArt): regress the critic to normalized targets; denormalize for GAE. Never hurts, often decisive.
2. **Agent-specific global state**: concatenate agent-specific features (e.g., agent ID, available actions) to the global state for the critic.
3. **Training data usage**: tune the number of PPO epochs and mini-batches per rollout; MAPPO is sensitive to data reuse.
4. **Action masking**: mask invalid actions in SMAC rather than penalizing them.
5. **Death masking**: zero out the value targets and advantages appropriately for dead agents.

MAPPO's simultaneous updates lack single-agent PPO's monotonic-improvement guarantee, since each agent's update shifts the others' environment. **HAPPO** restores it via the *multi-agent advantage decomposition theorem*,

$$A_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}(\mathbf{o}, \mathbf{a}) = \sum_{m=1}^{n} A_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}^{i_m}\!\big(\mathbf{o}, \mathbf{a}^{i_{1:m-1}}, a^{i_m}\big),$$

which permits *sequential* trust-region updates with a provable monotonic improvement bound [8] — theoretically clarifying why trust regions need sequentialization in MARL, though tuned MAPPO remains the stronger empirical baseline.

### 4.3 Mean-Field Approximation: From $n$ Bodies to Two

QMIX and MAPPO both degrade as $n$ grows: the mixing network's input dimension and the critic's joint-action conditioning scale with the team size, and exploration in $|\mathcal{A}|^n$ becomes hopeless. **Mean-field reinforcement learning** [6] attacks the $n \to \infty$ regime by exploiting a structural assumption: each agent's $Q$-function decomposes into *pairwise* interactions with its neighbors,

$$Q^i(s, \mathbf{a}) = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{N}(i)|} \sum_{k \in \mathcal{N}(i)} Q^i(s, a^i, a^k),$$

where $\mathcal{N}(i)$ is agent $i$'s neighborhood. Expanding each pairwise term around the neighborhood **mean action** $\bar{a}^i = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{N}(i)|}\sum_{k} a^k$ (one-hot encoded for discrete actions) and applying Taylor's theorem,

$$Q^i(s, a^i, a^k) = Q^i(s, a^i, \bar{a}^i) + \nabla_{\bar{a}} Q^i \cdot \underbrace{(a^k - \bar{a}^i)}_{\delta a^{i,k}} + o(\|\delta a^{i,k}\|),$$

the first-order fluctuation terms average to zero over the neighborhood, yielding the mean-field approximation

$$Q^i(s, \mathbf{a}) \;\approx\; Q^i(s, a^i, \bar{a}^i).$$

Each agent now plays a *two-player game* against the aggregate "mean agent" rather than tracking $n-1$ individuals. Yang et al. [6] prove, via contraction-mapping arguments, that mean-field $Q$-learning (MF-Q) and mean-field actor-critic (MF-AC) converge to Nash $Q$-values under Lipschitz and homogeneity assumptions, and demonstrate the framework on Gaussian Squeeze, the Ising model, and a large-scale Battle game with hundreds of agents.

SUBSAMPLE-MFQ [7] learns on a random $k$-agent subsystem and deploys to all $n$ agents, converging at $\tilde{O}(1/\sqrt{k})$ *independent of $n$*, which makes $n = 10^4$ practical; constrained mean-field control attains $O((\sqrt{|\mathcal{X}|} + \sqrt{|\mathcal{U}|})/\sqrt{N})$ error [9]. The price is homogeneity: mean-field methods cannot represent specialized heterogeneous roles or sparse, high-stakes pairwise coordination.

### 4.4 Counterfactual Baselines: Solving Credit Assignment

Even with centralized training, a shared team reward leaves each agent's gradient noisy: an agent cannot distinguish *its* contribution from teammates' exploration. **COMA** [4] addresses this with a *counterfactual baseline* that marginalizes out a single agent's action while holding all others fixed:

$$A^i(s, \mathbf{a}) = Q(s, \mathbf{a}) - \underbrace{\sum_{a'} \pi^i(a' \mid \tau^i)\, Q\big(s, (\mathbf{a}^{-i}, a')\big)}_{b^i(s, \mathbf{a}^{-i})}.$$

The baseline $b^i$ answers the counterfactual question: *"holding my teammates' actions fixed, how would the team have done on average if I had acted according to my current policy?"* The advantage is positive exactly when the agent's *actual* action outperformed its policy-average alternative — a per-agent credit signal with the teammates' behavior differenced out. The baseline introduces no bias (it is independent of $a^i$) while sharply reducing variance through its correlation with $Q(s, \mathbf{a})$.

The naive cost of the baseline is $|\mathcal{A}_i|$ critic evaluations per agent per step. COMA's key engineering insight is a critic parameterization that computes all counterfactual values in a **single forward pass**: the critic takes $(s, \mathbf{a}^{-i})$ as input and outputs a vector of $Q$-values, one per possible action of agent $i$. The resulting gradient,

$$g = \mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}\left[ \sum_i \nabla_{\theta} \log \pi^i(a^i \mid \tau^i)\; A^i(s, \mathbf{a}) \right],$$

trains decentralized actors with a centralized, credit-aware signal. On StarCraft micromanagement, COMA significantly outperformed independent actor-critic baselines, with the best agents competitive against full-state centralized controllers [4].

## 5. Empirical Results and Theoretical Proofs

### 5.1 Representative Benchmark Performance

The StarCraft Multi-Agent Challenge (SMAC) [2] remains the canonical CTDE benchmark. Representative win rates (median over seeds; exact numbers vary with implementation) are:

| Algorithm | `2s3z` (easy) | `3s_vs_5z` (hard) | `6h_vs_8z` (super-hard) | Paradigm |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| IQL (independent) | ~95% | ~0% | ~0% | Independent |
| VDN | ~99% | ~45% | ~5% | Factorization (additive) |
| QMIX [2] | ~100% | ~85% | ~30% | Factorization (monotonic) |
| Weighted QMIX [3] | ~100% | ~92% | ~55% | Factorization (weighted projection) |
| COMA [4] | ~97% | ~60% | ~10% | Centralized critic + counterfactual |
| MAPPO [5] | ~100% | ~95% | ~75% | Centralized critic + clipped surrogate |

Three patterns are robust: factorization dominates coordination-heavy maps, with Weighted QMIX's correction decisive on super-hard maps [3]; tuned MAPPO is the strongest on-policy method overall [5]; and all small-$n$ methods collapse in the many-agent regime, where mean-field approaches alone scale [6][7].

### 5.2 A Minimal Reference Implementation

The following PyTorch-style sketch implements the CTDE pattern — decentralized actors, a centralized critic, and the counterfactual advantage:

```python
import torch
import torch.nn as nn

class DecentralizedActor(nn.Module):
    """pi(a_i | tau_i): executed locally, trained centrally."""
    def __init__(self, obs_dim, act_dim, hid=128):
        super().__init__()
        self.rnn = nn.GRU(obs_dim, hid, batch_first=True)
        self.head = nn.Sequential(nn.Linear(hid, hid), nn.ReLU(),
                                  nn.Linear(hid, act_dim))
    def forward(self, tau):
        h, _ = self.rnn(tau)
        return torch.softmax(self.head(h[:, -1]), dim=-1)

class CentralizedCritic(nn.Module):
    """Q(s, a_{-i}) -> R^{|A_i|}: one forward pass, all counterfactuals."""
    def __init__(self, state_dim, n_agents, act_dim, hid=256):
        super().__init__()
        in_dim = state_dim + (n_agents - 1) * act_dim
        self.net = nn.Sequential(nn.Linear(in_dim, hid), nn.ReLU(),
                                 nn.Linear(hid, hid), nn.ReLU(),
                                 nn.Linear(hid, act_dim))  # one Q per a_i
    def forward(self, s, a_minus_i):
        return self.net(torch.cat([s, a_minus_i.flatten(1)], dim=-1))

def coma_loss(actors, critic, s, taus, actions, n_agents):
    """Counterfactual policy-gradient loss summed over agents."""
    total = 0.0
    for i in range(n_agents):
        pi = actors[i](taus[i])                       # [B, |A|]
        a_oh = torch.nn.functional.one_hot(actions[:, i],
                                           pi.shape[-1]).float()
        others = torch.cat([torch.nn.functional.one_hot(actions[:, j],
                            pi.shape[-1]).float()
                            for j in range(n_agents) if j != i], dim=-1)
        q_all = critic(s, others)                      # [B, |A|], single pass
        q_taken = (q_all * a_oh).sum(-1)               # Q(s, a)
        baseline = (q_all * pi).sum(-1)                # E_{a'~pi}[Q(s,(a_-i,a'))]
        advantage = (q_taken - baseline).detach()      # counterfactual A^i
        logp = torch.log((pi * a_oh).sum(-1) + 1e-8)
        total = total - (logp * advantage).mean()
    return total / n_agents
```

The sketch exhibits the CTDE asymmetry: the critic consumes global state and teammates' actions (training-only), while each actor consumes only its own history $\tau_i$ and deploys without communication.

### 5.3 Theoretical Guarantees at a Glance

> **Theorem (Mean-field contraction, Yang et al. [6]):** Under Lipschitz continuity of the pairwise $Q$-functions and a bounded reward, the mean-field Bellman operator $\mathcal{T}_{\text{MF}}$ is a contraction in the sup norm, and MF-Q iterates converge to the Nash $Q$-value $Q^\star_{\text{MF}}$ with error decaying as the neighborhood size grows.

> **Theorem (Weighted QMIX recovery [3]):** For the CW and OW weighting schemes, the weighted projection of *any* joint action-value $Q$ into the monotone-representable class preserves the maximal joint action; in particular, the projection of $Q^\star$ recovers an optimal policy.

> **Theorem (Multi-agent advantage decomposition [8]):** For any joint policy $\boldsymbol{\pi}$ and any agent ordering $i_{1:n}$, the joint advantage decomposes exactly into sequential per-agent advantages, enabling HAPPO's sequential trust-region updates with a monotonic improvement bound $J(\boldsymbol{\pi}_{k+1}) \geq J(\boldsymbol{\pi}_k) - O(\epsilon)$.

These results delineate a clear division of labor: factorization theory governs *representability*, trust-region theory governs *update safety*, and mean-field theory governs *scalability*.

## 6. Limitations

**Representational.** QMIX's monotonicity excludes non-monotonic coordination; Weighted QMIX repairs the projection but keeps a monotone mixer, so tasks requiring *sacrificial* individual actions (lower $Q_i$, higher $Q_{\text{tot}}$) remain inexpressible. Policy-gradient methods avoid factorization constraints but pay in sample inefficiency and local optima.

**Statistical.** Centralized critics condition on $(s, \mathbf{a})$, whose dimension grows with $n$, rendering MAPPO and COMA impractical beyond tens of agents. Mean-field methods escape only under homogeneity and weak-interaction assumptions that fail for heterogeneous teams with specialized roles.

**Computational.** COMA's single-pass critic scales as $O(|\mathcal{A}_i|)$ outputs per agent — prohibitive for large or continuous action spaces. QMIX's hypernetworks add parameter overhead, and off-policy replay introduces deadly-triad instability, mitigated in practice only by target networks and careful tuning.

**Game-theoretic.** All methods target cooperative optima or team Nash equilibria; none guarantees robustness to adversarial teammates, and a shared reward makes CTDE vulnerable to reward misspecification — one corrupted channel misleads the whole team.

## 7. Conclusion

CTDE is a *design discipline*: exploit privileged information where it is cheap (training) and respect information constraints where they are hard (execution). QMIX showed structural factorization constraints buy exact decentralization consistency; Weighted QMIX showed the constraint must be imposed on the right projection; MAPPO showed a well-engineered centralized critic with clipped trust-region updates is a formidable baseline; mean-field RL showed the $n \to \infty$ limit is tractable under weak homogeneous interactions; and COMA showed credit assignment admits a principled counterfactual answer computable in a single forward pass.

The frontier lies in synthesis: *factored critics with counterfactual baselines* for sample-efficient credit assignment; *trust-region updates over mean-field policies* for safe large-population learning; and *heterogeneous mean-field models* relaxing homogeneity while preserving $O(1)$-in-$n$ scaling. As cooperative AI moves from game benchmarks to traffic, energy, and robotic fleets, these tools — monotonicity, trust regions, mean-field limits, counterfactual reasoning — remain the load-bearing elements of any scalable solution.
