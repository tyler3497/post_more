# Entropic Optimal Transport: Sinkhorn Iterations, Schrödinger Bridges, and Sample Complexity

## Abstract

Optimal transport gives a geometrically faithful distance between probability distributions, but its classical form is computationally intractable, non-smooth, and cursed by dimensionality: empirical Wasserstein distances converge at rates that degrade exponentially in the ambient dimension. Entropic regularization — penalizing the coupling by its relative entropy — resolves all three defects at once. The problem becomes strongly convex with a smooth dual, its optimizer takes a Gibbs form solvable by Sinkhorn's matrix-scaling iterations, and the regularity of the resulting potentials yields dimension-free parametric sample complexity rates. This thesis develops the full arc: the Kantorovich formulation and its duality, the Gibbs structure and Hilbert-metric convergence theory of Sinkhorn iterations, the dynamical Schrödinger bridge interpretation of entropic transport as a most-likely particle evolution, and the statistical theory of empirical entropic optimal transport. We then study debiased Sinkhorn divergences interpolating between Wasserstein geometry and maximum mean discrepancy statistics, survey machine learning applications from Wasserstein GANs to domain adaptation, and extend the picture to unbalanced and Gromov–Wasserstein transport — closing with the statistical–computational tradeoffs that govern the choice of regularization strength.

## 1 Intro

The problem of comparing probability distributions is among the oldest in applied mathematics. When two distributions overlap substantially, information-theoretic divergences such as the Kullback–Leibler divergence behave well. But modern machine learning routinely confronts distributions with *disjoint supports* — the distribution of natural images and the distribution of images produced by a generator early in training share essentially no common support — and in this regime information divergences are either infinite or degenerate, carrying no gradient signal to guide optimization [2].

Optimal transport (OT) was conceived precisely to answer this deficiency. Introduced by Monge in 1781 and formalized as a linear program by Kantorovich in 1942, OT measures the distance between two distributions as the minimum cost of a coupling that redistributes the mass of one into the other. The resulting *Wasserstein distances* metrize weak convergence, lift the geometry of the ground space onto the space of probability measures, and provide meaningful gradients even for mutually singular distributions — the property that made them the loss of choice for generative modeling [7].

Yet classical optimal transport carries three severe handicaps. **Computationally**, exact OT between two distributions supported on *n* points is a linear program whose best-known solvers run in time super-quadratic in *n*, rendering histograms beyond a few hundred bins intractable [1]. **Analytically**, the value of the unregularized problem is a piecewise-linear, non-smooth function of its inputs, and differentiating through the linear program is numerically fragile [2]. **Statistically**, the empirical Wasserstein distance between a distribution and its *n*-sample empirical counterpart converges at rates like *n* raised to the negative reciprocal of the dimension, a curse that makes the metric nearly unusable in high-dimensional estimation [4].

The turning point came in 2013, when Cuturi proposed smoothing the Kantorovich program with an entropic penalty [1]. The regularized problem is *strongly convex*, so its optimizer is unique and varies smoothly with the data. Its optimizer has a *Gibbs form* — a diagonal rescaling of a Gibbs kernel — which reduces computation to Sinkhorn's matrix-scaling algorithm: alternate row and column normalizations of a fixed matrix. And the same Gibbs structure is exactly the solution of Schrödinger's 1931 bridge problem: the most likely stochastic evolution of particles between two observed marginals [3].

Our thesis proceeds as follows. Section 2 recalls the Kantorovich formulation, its dual, and the analytical properties of Wasserstein distances. Section 3 develops the entropic methodology: the regularized primal, its smooth unconstrained dual, the Sinkhorn iteration as block-coordinate ascent, and a numerically stabilized log-domain implementation. Section 4 dives into five advanced topics — the Schrödinger bridge dynamic formulation, Sinkhorn convergence theory, debiased Sinkhorn divergences, machine learning applications, and unbalanced and Gromov–Wasserstein extensions. Section 5 presents the statistical theory of sample complexity with proof sketches. statistical–computational tradeoffs that govern the choice of regularization strength.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The Kantorovich Formulation

Let *X* and *Y* be Polish spaces and let *μ* and *ν* be Borel probability measures on *X* and *Y* respectively. A **coupling** of *μ* and *ν* is a probability measure *π* on the product space *X × Y* whose marginals coincide with *μ* and *ν*; we denote the set of all couplings by *Π(μ, ν)*. Given a lower semicontinuous cost function *c : X × Y →* the nonnegative reals, the **Kantorovich optimal transport problem** is

$$\mathrm{OT}_c(\mu,\nu) = \inf_{\pi \in \Pi(\mu,\nu)} \int_{X \times Y} c(x,y)\, d\pi(x,y).$$

When *X = Y* is a metric space with metric *d* and the cost is *c = d^p*, the *p*-th root of the optimum is the ***p*-Wasserstein distance** *W_p(μ, ν)*, which metrizes weak convergence together with convergence of *p*-th moments [5].

In the discrete case, where *μ = ∑ a_i δ_{x_i}* and *ν = ∑ b_j δ_{y_j}* with probability vectors *a* and *b*, the problem becomes a finite linear program over the **transportation polytope** *U(a, b)* of matrices *P* with prescribed row and column sums:

$$\min_{P \in U(a,b)} \langle P, C \rangle_F = \sum_{i,j} P_{ij} C_{ij}.$$

The polytope has sparse extreme points, but finding them requires combinatorial algorithms that scale poorly.

### 2.2 Duality and Potentials

The Kantorovich dual reframes the problem as a maximization over pairs of *potential functions*:

$$\mathrm{OT}_c(\mu,\nu) = \sup_{(f,g) \in \Phi_c} \int_X f\, d\mu + \int_Y g\, d\nu,$$

where *Φ_c* is the set of pairs satisfying *f(x) + g(y) ≤ c(x,y)* for all *(x, y)*. For the quadratic cost, the optimizers are linked by *c*-concavity and, under regularity, by Brenier's theorem to a convex gradient map *T = ∇φ* pushing *μ* forward to *ν*.

### 2.3 The Statistical Curse

Suppose we observe *n* i.i.d. samples from *μ* and form the empirical measure *μ̂_n*. How fast does *W_p(μ̂_n, μ)* decay? The definitive answer, due to Fournier and Guillin, is that the expected Wasserstein distance converges like *n^{-1/d}* (up to logarithmic factors) in dimension *d ≥ 3*, and this rate is minimax optimal [4]. The intuition is geometric: Wasserstein distance is sensitive to the fine-grained covering of space, and covering the unit cube in *d* dimensions requires exponentially many cells. For the machine learning practitioner, this is devastating — estimating a Wasserstein distance from samples in, say, *d = 64* dimensions is statistically hopeless at any feasible sample size.

This is the backdrop against which entropic regularization must be understood: not merely a computational trick, but a *statistical* intervention trading controlled bias for dramatically improved estimation rates.

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 Entropic Regularization

Fix *ε > 0*. The **entropic optimal transport** problem adds the relative entropy of the coupling with respect to the product measure as a penalty:

$$\mathrm{OT}_{c,\varepsilon}(\mu,\nu) = \inf_{\pi \in \Pi(\mu,\nu)} \int c\, d\pi + \varepsilon\, \mathrm{KL}(\pi \,\|\, \mu \otimes \nu).$$

The KL term makes the objective *ε*-strongly convex, guaranteeing a unique, *diffuse* optimizer rather than a sparse Monge map. First-order optimality yields the celebrated **Gibbs representation** of the optimal plan:

$$\pi_\varepsilon(dx,dy) = e^{\frac{f(x)+g(y)-c(x,y)}{\varepsilon}}\, \mu(dx)\, \nu(dy),$$

i.e., the optimal coupling is a multiplicative rescaling of the Gibbs kernel *K(x,y) = exp(−c(x,y)/ε)* by two potential-driven factors. The potentials *(f, g)* — the **Schrödinger potentials** — maximize a smooth, unconstrained dual, which is what makes the problem amenable to gradient methods and empirical process theory [5].

### 3.2 The Sinkhorn Algorithm

In the discrete setting, the Gibbs representation says the optimizer has the form *P = diag(u) K diag(v)* for the kernel matrix *K_{ij} = exp(−C_{ij}/ε)*. Satisfying the marginal constraints *P 1 = a* and *P^T 1 = b* then reduces to finding positive scaling vectors *u, v*. **Sinkhorn's algorithm** — independently known as iterative proportional fitting — alternates *u ← a ⊘ (Kv)* and *v ← b ⊘ (K^T u)*, where *⊘* denotes elementwise division. Each half-iteration is a single matrix–vector product: quadratic time and parallelizable. Cuturi showed that this elementary procedure computes an *ε*-approximate transport distance orders of magnitude faster than any linear programming solver [1], and the iterations are differentiable, so gradients flow through them by automatic differentiation [2].

> **Theorem:** (Sinkhorn–Knopp, 1967). For any strictly positive matrix *K* and any positive marginal vectors *a, b* with equal total mass, the alternating rescaling iterates converge to the unique pair *(u, v)* — unique up to the reciprocal scaling *(tu, v/t)* — such that *diag(u)K diag(v)* has row sums *a* and column sums *b*.

The theorem guarantees convergence but is silent on speed. The rate theory is a beautiful application of the **Hilbert projective metric**: one Sinkhorn step is a strict contraction in this metric, yielding **linear convergence** of the marginal violation. The contraction factor degrades as *ε → 0*, which is the algorithmic face of the statistical–computational tradeoff of Section 5.

### 3.3 Numerically Stabilized Implementation

A naive implementation overflows, since *exp(−C_{ij}/ε)* underflows for small *ε*. The standard remedy works in **log-domain** with the log-sum-exp trick [5]:

```python
import numpy as np

def sinkhorn_log(a, b, C, eps, max_iter=1000, tol=1e-9):
    """Entropic OT via log-domain stabilized Sinkhorn iterations.

    Returns the transport plan P and the regularized OT cost.
    a, b : marginal histograms (sum to 1); C : cost matrix; eps : regularization.
    """
    n, m = C.shape
    log_a, log_b = np.log(a), np.log(b)
    f = np.zeros(n)          # log-potential for rows
    g = np.zeros(m)          # log-potential for columns
    log_K = -C / eps

    for it in range(max_iter):
        f_prev = f.copy()
        # update f: enforce row marginals in log domain
        f = eps * log_a + _softmin(f, g, log_K, eps, axis=1)
        # update g: enforce column marginals in log domain
        g = eps * log_b + _softmin(f, g, log_K, eps, axis=0)
        if np.max(np.abs(f - f_prev)) < tol:
            break

    log_P = (f[:, None] + g[None, :] - C) / eps
    P = np.exp(log_P)
    cost = np.sum(P * C)   # regularized cost: <P, C> + eps * KL(P || a (x) b)
    return P, cost

def _softmin(f, g, log_K, eps, axis):
    """Batched soft-minimum of (f_i + g_j - C_ij) over the given axis."""
    M = f[:, None] + g[None, :] + log_K * eps   # = f_i + g_j - C_ij
    m0 = M.min(axis=axis, keepdims=True)
    s = -eps * np.log(np.exp(-(M - m0) / eps).sum(axis=axis))
    return (s - np.squeeze(m0, axis=axis))
```

The stabilized iterates never form *K* explicitly; all arithmetic happens in logarithmic space where the dynamic range is manageable.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Schrödinger Bridges: The Dynamic Formulation

In 1931–1932, Erwin Schrödinger posed a question in statistical physics: given a cloud of particles observed with empirical distribution *μ* at time 0 and *ν* at time 1, evolving a priori as independent Brownian motions, what is the *most likely* collective evolution consistent with both observations? Formally, let *R* be the law of the reversible Brownian motion (the reference path measure). The **Schrödinger bridge problem** seeks the path measure *P* minimizing the relative entropy *KL(P ‖ R)* subject to the marginal constraints *P_0 = μ*, *P_1 = ν* [3].

The solution is a *mixture of Brownian bridges* built on the static entropic coupling *π_ε*: draw endpoints from *π_ε*, then follow a Brownian bridge between them. In other words, **entropic optimal transport is the static projection of the Schrödinger bridge**, and the Sinkhorn potentials *(f, g)* are the logarithms of the harmonic functions solving the Schrödinger system — hence the name *Schrödinger potentials*.

This dynamical viewpoint has deep consequences. As *ε → 0*, the bridge converges to the displacement interpolation of unregularized OT — Brownian noise vanishes and particles follow straight-line geodesics [3].

### 4.2 Convergence Rates of Sinkhorn Iterations

Beyond the qualitative Sinkhorn–Knopp theorem, modern analysis gives quantitative rates. In the Hilbert projective metric *d_H*, one step of Sinkhorn is a contraction with factor

$$\kappa = \tanh\left(\tfrac{1}{4}\,\Delta\right) < 1, \qquad \Delta = \max_{i,j,k,\ell} \log \frac{K_{ij}K_{k\ell}}{K_{i\ell}K_{kj}},$$

where *Δ* measures the multiplicative variation of the kernel. Since *K_{ij} = exp(−C_{ij}/ε)*, we have *Δ ≤ 2‖C‖_∞/ε*, so *κ → 1* as *ε → 0*: small regularization means slow convergence, and the iteration count to reach tolerance *δ* scales like *‖C‖_∞/(εδ)* [5].

Finer analyses improve on this worst case: **greedy (Greenkhorn) variants** updating only the most-violated row or column achieve near-linear total complexity, and **mirror-descent** interpretations yield rates in terms of dual suboptimality [5].

| Algorithm | Per-iteration cost | Iterations to tolerance *δ* | Remarks |
|---|---|---|---|
| Exact LP (network simplex) | super-quadratic | — | Non-smooth, no gradients |
| Sinkhorn (matrix scaling) | *O(n²)* | *O(‖C‖_∞ / (εδ))* | GPU-friendly, differentiable |
| Greenkhorn (greedy) | *O(n)* amortized | *O(n ‖C‖_∞ / (εδ))* | Sparse updates |
| Log-domain Sinkhorn | *O(n²)* | same as Sinkhorn | Numerically stable |

### 4.3 Debiased Sinkhorn Divergences: Between Wasserstein and MMD

Raw entropic OT has an embarrassing defect: *OT_{c,ε}(μ, μ) ≠ 0* — the regularized "distance" from a distribution to itself is positive, because the entropy term rewards diffuse self-couplings. Genevay, Peyré, and Cuturi proposed the **debiased Sinkhorn divergence** [2]:

$$S_{c,\varepsilon}(\mu,\nu) = \mathrm{OT}_{c,\varepsilon}(\mu,\nu) - \tfrac{1}{2}\mathrm{OT}_{c,\varepsilon}(\mu,\mu) - \tfrac{1}{2}\mathrm{OT}_{c,\varepsilon}(\nu,\nu).$$

This correction restores the identity of indiscernibles: *S_{c,ε}(μ, ν) = 0* iff *μ = ν*, and for the quadratic cost the divergence is *positive definite* — it metrizes weak convergence [2]. The family *S_{c,ε}* **interpolates between geometry and statistics**: as *ε → 0* it recovers the squared Wasserstein distance, while as *ε → ∞* it converges to an energy-distance / maximum mean discrepancy (MMD). The practitioner can therefore tune *ε* to trade the sharp geometric sensitivity of OT against the dimension-free *n^{-1/2}* sample complexity and unbiased gradient estimates of MMD — the "sweet spot" that made Sinkhorn divergences the loss of choice for large-scale generative modeling [2].

### 4.4 Computational Optimal Transport in Machine Learning

Two applications reshaped machine learning's relationship with OT. First, **Wasserstein GANs**: Arjovsky, Chintala, and Bottou observed that the Wasserstein-1 distance between the data distribution and the generator's distribution provides usable gradients even when supports are disjoint, unlike the Jensen–Shannon divergence of standard GANs [7]; the Kantorovich–Rubinstein dual turns the distance into an adversarial game over 1-Lipschitz critics. Second, **domain adaptation**: Courty and coauthors framed transfer learning as transporting the source distribution onto the target, learning a coupling that aligns labeled source samples with unlabeled target samples [5]. Entropic regularization makes this practical: the Gibbs plan is dense and differentiable, so the alignment can be learned jointly with the classifier by gradient descent.

### 4.5 Beyond Balance: Unbalanced and Gromov–Wasserstein Transport

**Unbalanced OT** relaxes the hard marginal constraints with divergence penalties, allowing mass creation and destruction at a price set by the penalty weights. The resulting problem is still solvable by a generalized Sinkhorn iteration with softened rescaling updates [5].

**Gromov–Wasserstein (GW)** transport drops the shared-space assumption entirely: given two metric measure spaces *(X, d_X, μ)* and *(Y, d_Y, ν)*, GW seeks the coupling minimizing the distortion of pairwise distances. This is a non-convex quadratic program — but entropic regularization again comes to the rescue, and a Sinkhorn-like projected gradient scheme computes entropic GW efficiently, enabling alignment of heterogeneous objects such as word embeddings across languages [5].

## 5 Empirical/Proofs

### 5.1 The Curse, Quantified

We sketch the statistical picture. Let *μ̂_n* be the empirical measure of *n* i.i.d. draws from *μ* supported in the unit cube of dimension *d*. Fournier and Guillin proved [4]

$$\mathbb{E}\, W_1(\hat\mu_n, \mu) \asymp n^{-1/d} \quad (d \geq 3),$$

up to logarithmic factors, and the rate is minimax-optimal: no estimator can do better uniformly.

### 5.2 Smoothness Breaks the Curse

Entropic regularization changes the estimand's regularity. Genevay et al. showed that the *Sinkhorn divergence* between empirical measures converges at the parametric rate [2]:

$$\mathbb{E}\,|S_{c,\varepsilon}(\hat\mu_n,\hat\nu_n) - S_{c,\varepsilon}(\mu,\nu)| = O\!\left(\frac{e^{C/\varepsilon}(1 + \varepsilon^{-\lfloor d/2 \rfloor})}{\sqrt{n}}\right).$$

The *n^{-1/2}* rate is dimension-free in the exponent — the curse is broken — but the constant depends *exponentially* on *1/ε* and polynomially on dimension through the smoothness of the Schrödinger potentials. Mena and Niles-Weed sharpened this for the quadratic cost: exploiting the Gaussian-convolution structure of the potentials, they proved a central limit theorem for entropic OT with far milder *ε*-dependence, extending the theory to unbounded subgaussian measures [6].

The proof strategy runs through the **dual**: because the regularized dual is smooth and unconstrained, the empirical potentials inherit uniform bounds, and empirical process theory over the resulting function class yields parametric rates — potential regularity converts a geometric estimation problem into a smooth functional one.

### 5.3 The Statistical–Computational Tradeoff

We can now state the central tradeoff of entropic OT as a trilemma among **fidelity** (small *ε*: close to true Wasserstein geometry), **statistics** (large *ε*: fast *n^{-1/2}* estimation, stable gradients), and **computation** (large *ε*: fast Sinkhorn convergence, no underflow). No single *ε* optimizes all three. The theoretically grounded prescription is to *anneal*: choose *ε = ε_n* decaying with sample size so that the bias *O(ε log(1/ε))* balances the estimation error. For the debiased divergence, the bias of *S_{c,ε}* relative to *W_2^2* is of order *ε*, so *ε_n ≍ n^{-1/2}* yields an estimator converging at nearly the parametric rate to the *unregularized* quantity — paying only logarithmic factors for the privilege of tractable computation [6]. This annealing principle is the statistical justification for the *ε*-schedules used in every large-scale Sinkhorn implementation.

A complementary computational-statistical phenomenon: **stochastic Sinkhorn** optimizes the smooth dual with stochastic gradients over mini-batches, giving an online algorithm whose per-iteration cost is independent of dataset size [2]. The entropic smoothing is what makes this possible — the unregularized dual's nonsmoothness would defeat stochastic approximation.

## 6 Limitations

Entropic optimal transport is not a panacea, and an honest account must record its boundaries.

1. **The blurring bias.** Entropic plans are diffuse by construction: mass from a single source point spreads across many targets. For applications requiring *sparse, interpretable* matchings — e.g., aligning specific cells across biological samples — the Gibbs plan's blur is a genuine defect, and practitioners resort to annealing *ε* toward zero, recovering the computational pain they sought to escape.
2. **Choice of ε.** The regularization parameter controls geometry, statistics, and computation simultaneously, and there is no universal selector. Cross-validation on downstream task performance is the norm, but this is unsatisfying theoretically and expensive practically.
3. **Quadratic scaling.** Sinkhorn iterations cost *O(n²)* per step with a dense kernel. For *n* in the hundreds of thousands, even quadratic is too slow; remedies include Nyström and random-feature kernel approximations, multiscale coarse-to-fine schemes, and minibatch OT — each introducing its own approximation error that interacts with the entropic bias.
4. **Curse of dimensionality, residual form.** The parametric rates of Section 5.2 carry constants exponential in *1/ε*; pushing *ε* small to reduce bias reintroduces dimensional dependence through the constant. The curse is *mitigated*, not abolished.
5. **Unbalanced and GW gaps.** The statistical theory for unbalanced and Gromov–Wasserstein variants is far less developed than for the balanced case, and entropic GW remains non-convex with only local convergence guarantees.

## 7 Conclusion

Entropic optimal transport represents one of the rare unifications in applied mathematics where a single modification — adding *ε* times relative entropy to the Kantorovich program — simultaneously resolves computational intractability, analytical nonsmoothness, and statistical curse-of-dimensionality. The Sinkhorn algorithm turns the resulting strongly convex problem into matrix rescalings that run at hardware speed [1]; the Schrödinger bridge reveals the regularized plan as the most likely stochastic evolution between marginals, linking 1931 physics to modern generative modeling [3]; and the dual regularity theory delivers parametric sample complexity rates that make OT estimation feasible in high dimensions [2][6].

The debiased Sinkhorn divergences distill these advances into a practical loss family interpolating between Wasserstein geometry and MMD statistics [2][7], and unbalanced and Gromov–Wasserstein extensions carry the paradigm to mass-varying and heterogeneous settings [5].

The field's open frontier is the joint optimization of the statistical–computational tradeoff: adaptive, data-driven *ε* selection and dimension-robust constants in sample complexity. What is no longer in doubt is the paradigm itself — entropic regularization turned optimal transport from a beautiful but intractable theory into the workhorse of scalable distribution comparison.
