# Consensus in Permissionless Networks: Nakamoto Consensus, GHOST, and Proof-of-Stake Finality Gadgets

## Abstract

Permissionless consensus must produce a single, irreversible transaction history among mutually distrusting participants with no admission control and no trusted identities. This thesis traces the arc from probabilistic to accountable finality across three landmark designs: Nakamoto's longest-chain rule [1], whose security the backbone model reduces to chain growth, chain quality, and the common-prefix lemma under honest majority [2]; Sompolinsky and Zohar's GHOST, which replaces longest-chain with heaviest-subtree fork choice to preserve security at high block rates [3]; and Casper FFG, a proof-of-stake finality gadget overlaying accountable Byzantine-fault-tolerant checkpoints on any proposal mechanism [4]. We show how latest-message-driven GHOST adapts heaviest-subtree selection to validator voting, and how Gasper composes it with Casper FFG into an ebb-and-flow protocol that is simultaneously dynamically available and accountably safe [5]. We quantify latency–security trade-offs [6], survey LMD GHOST's attack surface [7,8], and examine the availability–finality dilemma [9]. The central finding: no single fork-choice rule escapes the tension between throughput, confirmation latency, and resilience to adversarial withholding — the modern answer is architectural layering, a fast available fork choice crowned by a slow accountable finality gadget.

##1 Introduction

Classical Byzantine agreement — from Lamport, Shostak, and Pease through PBFT — assumes a *known, fixed* participant set and tolerates at most one third faulty. Permissionless networks violate both premises: anyone may join or leave, identities are cheap pseudonyms, and no registry exists against which to count a quorum. Against this backdrop, Satoshi Nakamoto's 2008 proposal was a double rupture [1]: it replaced identity with *scarce resource expenditure* (proof of work) as the Sybil-resistance mechanism, and deterministic agreement with *probabilistic convergence* — finality that is never absolute but becomes exponentially unlikely to reverse with depth.

Garay, Kiayias, and Leonardos' *backbone protocol* supplied the missing formalism — chain growth, chain quality, common prefix under honest hash-power majority [2]; later work sharpened the latency–security trade-off to within hours of the lower bound [6].

But Nakamoto consensus degrades as block production rate rises relative to network delay: honest miners fork against each other, their work fragments across branches, and a privately mining adversary captures a disproportionate share of *effective* chain weight. Sompolinsky and Zohar's GHOST attacks exactly this pathology by counting *stale* blocks toward subtree weight, keeping honest work productive at high rates [3]. Proof of stake introduces its own pathologies — costless equivocation (*nothing at stake*) and history fabrication from dead keys (*long-range attacks*) — answered not by another fork-choice rule but by a *finality gadget*: Casper FFG checkpoints the chain with Byzantine-fault-tolerant supermajority votes and slashes equivocating validators [4]. Gasper fuses the lineages: LMD GHOST provides a live, dynamically available fork choice while Casper FFG provides accountable finality on top — an *ebb-and-flow* construction [5,9].

This thesis unifies these designs in one framework. Our contributions: (i) the private-mining bound governing Nakamoto security; (ii) longest-chain versus heaviest-subtree fork choice; (iii) Casper FFG's justification/finalization rules, slashing conditions, and accountable-safety theorem; (iv) Gasper as an ebb-and-flow protocol, including the balancing and avalanche attacks [7,8]; and (v) remaining limitations, from bounded-capacity models to validator centralization.

##2 Background

We work in the standard permissionless model: *participants* (miners, validators) communicate over a Δ-*synchronous* gossip network — every message reaches all honest parties within Δ seconds. The adversary controls a β fraction of the scarce resource (hash power or stake), coordinates corrupted parties arbitrarily, and may spawn unlimited identities, so influence is weighted by provable expenditure, never headcount.

**Safety and liveness** [2]: *chain growth* — honest chains grow at a steady minimum rate; *chain quality* — the adversary's block share in any long honest-chain segment is bounded; *common prefix* — two honest chains pruned by k blocks from the tip agree. A *k-deep confirmation rule* settles a transaction k blocks below the tip; security error is the probability a k-deep transaction is later reverted.

**Fork choice.** A *fork-choice rule* maps a local block tree to one canonical head despite divergent views: the *longest-chain* rule (greatest accumulated work) in Nakamoto consensus, the *heaviest observed subtree* rule in GHOST [3].

**Proof-of-stake pathologies.** *Nothing at stake*: signing blocks costs nothing, so rational validators sign every competing fork and fork choice loses selective pressure. *Long-range attacks*: an adversary acquiring long-exited validators' keys fabricates an alternative history newcomers cannot distinguish from the real one. Defenses: *slashing*, *weak subjectivity* (new nodes obtain a recent trusted checkpoint out-of-band), and *finality gadgets* marking checkpoints irreversible [4].

**Finality.** *Probabilistic finality*: reversal probability decays with depth but never reaches zero (Nakamoto). *Accountable finality*: a finalized block is reverted only after a provable, punishable safety violation (Casper) [4]. An *ebb-and-flow* protocol outputs an available ledger growing under dynamic participation plus an accountably safe finalized prefix; the two coincide when conditions are good [9].

---

##3 Methodology

Our analysis proceeds on four complementary tracks, each matched to the character of the object under study.

1. **Formal reduction.** We adopt the backbone decomposition [2] — chain growth, chain quality, common prefix as lemmas; double-spend resistance as corollary — comparing all three designs on identical footing.
2. **Stochastic race analysis.** Competing Poisson processes — honest (1 − β)λ, adversarial βλ; the *private-mining attack* reduces security to their race, and renewal-theoretic bounds [6] yield closed-form confirmation latencies.
3. **Adversarial strategy enumeration.** We reconstruct published attacks — selfish mining, balancing, avalanche — identifying which protocol invariants each violates [7,8].
4. **Mechanized specification.** TLA+ invariants for the finality gadget and executable Haskell/Rust reference models for the fork-choice rules anchor prose claims to checkable artifacts.

Comparison criteria are fixed across all designs: *fault threshold* (maximal adversarial resource fraction tolerated), *finality type* (probabilistic vs. accountable), *confirmation latency* for a canonical error target, *throughput regime* (blocks per unit of network delay), and *accountability* (whether safety violations produce slashable evidence).

##4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Nakamoto Consensus and the Private-Mining Bound

Bitcoin's consensus loop is simple: miners grind a nonce until the block hash falls below a difficulty target, everyone adopts the valid chain with greatest accumulated work, and mines atop it. Block discovery is memoryless, so block production is a Poisson process of rate λ — honest rate (1 − β)λ, adversarial rate βλ [6].

The adversary's canonical strategy is the *private-mining* (double-spend) attack: pay a merchant, wait for k public confirmations, meanwhile secretly mining a double-spend fork. If the secret fork ever outgrows the public chain, the adversary publishes it; the longest-chain rule makes everyone switch, and the payment vanishes. Security reduces to: starting k blocks behind, what is the probability that a Poisson process of rate βλ ever catches one of rate (1 − β)λ? When β < 0.5 the honest process has positive drift and the catch-up probability decays exponentially in k — the content of the common-prefix lemma [2].

> **Theorem (Common Prefix, informal [2]).** In a Δ-synchronous network with honest hash-power majority, the probability that two honest parties disagree on any block more than k deep is negligible in k — exponentially small in the honest advantage (1 − 2β).

The backbone analysis further establishes *chain growth* and *chain quality* — honest chains grow at an explicit minimum rate, and the adversary's block share degrades gracefully with β [2]. Selfish mining refines the picture: above roughly one third of hash power, selective withholding earns disproportionate rewards, though *reversing* confirmed transactions still requires approaching one half.

Quantitatively [6]: with β = 0.10 and ten-second delays, k-confirmation reaches error below 10<sup>−3</sup> after four hours and below 10<sup>−9</sup> after ten hours — within two hours of the private-mining lower bound:

```python
import random

def double_spend_prob(beta, k, trials=200000):
    lam_h, lam_a = 1.0 - beta, beta
    success = 0
    for _ in range(trials):
        deficit = k
        t = 0.0
        while deficit > 0 and t < 200.0:
            t += random.expovariate(lam_h + lam_a)
            if random.random() < lam_a / (lam_h + lam_a):
                deficit -= 1
            else:
                deficit += 1
        success += deficit <= 0
    return success / trials

for k in (1, 6, 12):
    print(k, double_spend_prob(0.30, k))
```

### 4.2 GHOST and Inclusive Fork Choice

Nakamoto consensus assumes blocks are rare relative to propagation delay. As the *fork rate* — expected blocks per Δ — grows, honest miners fork against *each other*, orphaning one another's work while the privately mining adversary wastes nothing. Its *effective* chain-weight share thus exceeds β, eroding the honest-majority threshold from below.

Sompolinsky and Zohar's GHOST eliminates this waste [3]: instead of the longest chain it selects the *heaviest subtree* — from genesis, at each fork descend to the child whose subtree, *including stale off-chain blocks*, contains the most blocks. Honest work is never discarded; only *withheld* adversarial blocks fail to contribute, and withholding is precisely what the rule punishes. A Rust reference implementation:

```rust
use std::collections::HashMap;
type Hash = u64;

fn subtree_weight(children: &HashMap<Hash, Vec<Hash>>, b: Hash) -> usize {
    1 + children.get(&b).map(|cs| cs.iter().map(|c| subtree_weight(children, *c)).sum()).unwrap_or(0)
}

fn ghost_head(genesis: Hash, children: &HashMap<Hash, Vec<Hash>>) -> Hash {
    let mut b = genesis;
    while let Some(cs) = children.get(&b) {
        b = *cs.iter().max_by_key(|c| subtree_weight(children, **c)).unwrap();
    }
    b
}
```

Ethereum's uncle mechanism partially adopted this philosophy: stale blocks earn reduced rewards and contribute chain weight, softening the centralization pressure high fork rates exert on small miners [3]. The DAG generalization — SPECTRE, PHANTOM, and the GHOSTDAG deployed in Kaspa — extends heaviest-subtree reasoning from trees to DAGs, but the insight is unchanged: *security should be a function of total honest work performed, not of work that won a propagation race.*

### 4.3 Casper FFG: Accountable Finality Gadgets

Fork-choice rules deliver only probabilistic finality. Casper the Friendly Finality Gadget instead *overlays* a Byzantine-fault-tolerant checkpointing protocol on the proposal mechanism [4]: the proposal layer keeps the chain live while the gadget periodically declares checkpoints irreversible, destroying violators' stake.

Checkpoints are block–epoch pairs (in practice epoch-boundary blocks); *votes* (s, t) are cast by validators weighted by stake. A *supermajority link* s → t exists when ≥ two thirds of stake votes for the pair. *Justification* is inductive: genesis is justified, and t is justified given a supermajority link s → t from justified s. *Finalization*: t is *finalized* when justified and a supermajority link t → u exists with height(u) = height(t) + 1 [4].

Accountability comes from two *slashing conditions* — violations constitute cryptographic proof of misbehavior, triggering deposit confiscation:

1. **No double vote:** never sign two distinct votes with the same target epoch — no equivocation on the target checkpoint.
2. **No surround vote:** never sign (s₁, t₁) and (s₂, t₂) with s₁ < s₂ < t₂ < t₁ — no vote may "surround" another, which would permit finalizing two conflicting histories.

> **Theorem (Accountable Safety [4]).** Two conflicting checkpoints cannot both finalize unless validators holding ≥ one third of total stake violated a slashing condition — so any safety failure yields publicly verifiable evidence against culprits whose deposits are destroyed.

The proof is a counting argument: conflicting finalized checkpoints imply two supermajority quorums intersecting in ≥ one third of stake, and the surround condition converts that double-voting into slashable evidence. Liveness holds under partial synchrony with two thirds honest. Core types in Haskell:

```haskell
type Epoch = Int
type Stake = Integer

data Checkpoint = Checkpoint { cpBlock :: BlockHash, cpEpoch :: Epoch }
  deriving (Eq, Ord, Show)
data Vote = Vote { vSource :: Checkpoint, vTarget :: Checkpoint, vValidator :: ValidatorId }
  deriving (Eq, Show)

doubleVote :: Vote -> Vote -> Bool  -- slashing condition (i)
doubleVote v1 v2 = vValidator v1 == vValidator v2
                && cpEpoch (vTarget v1) == cpEpoch (vTarget v2)
                && vTarget v1 /= vTarget v2

surroundVote :: Vote -> Vote -> Bool  -- slashing condition (ii)
surroundVote v1 v2 = vValidator v1 == vValidator v2
  && cpEpoch (vSource v1) < cpEpoch (vSource v2)
  && cpEpoch (vTarget v2) < cpEpoch (vTarget v1)
```

Casper deliberately does *not* solve liveness — a halted gadget simply stops finalizing while the underlying chain continues. This separation is the point: the gadget is *agnostic* to the proposal mechanism and can crown a proof-of-work chain, a GHOST chain, or a rotating-leader proof-of-stake chain alike [4]. Its Achilles' heel is the *long-range attack*: finality is rooted in historical stake, so a newcomer syncing from genesis cannot distinguish the true finalized history from a fabrication signed by since-withdrawn keys. The remedy is *weak subjectivity* — clients obtain a recent finalized checkpoint out-of-band, bounding the attack window to the withdrawal delay.

### 4.4 Gasper: Ebb-and-Flow Synthesis in Ethereum

Gasper — the idealized protocol behind Ethereum's proof-of-stake beacon chain — fuses the two lineages: **LMD GHOST** for liveness, **Casper FFG** for accountable safety [5]. Time is divided into twelve-second *slots* grouped into thirty-two-slot *epochs*; each slot has a *proposer* extending the LMD GHOST head and a *committee* attesting to it.

LMD GHOST adapts heaviest-subtree selection to validator voting: only each validator's *latest* attestation counts toward block weight, and descent starts from the *last justified checkpoint*. Attestations double as Casper FFG votes (each names source and target checkpoints), and equivocating validators contribute only their first-seen vote, blunting nothing-at-stake equivocation [7]. Finality cascades across epochs: with two thirds of stake attesting across consecutive epoch-boundary blocks, checkpoints justify then finalize — within two epochs, roughly thirteen minutes, in healthy operation — and reverting a finalized block requires burning at least one third of all staked ether [5].

This composition is an instance of the *ebb-and-flow* paradigm [9]: the available ledger (LMD GHOST head) keeps growing under dynamic participation, while the finalized ledger (Casper prefix) advances whenever two thirds of stake is online. The ledgers coincide in good times and diverge gracefully in bad — finality stalls, but the chain never halts.

The fork-choice layer is brittle. Neu, Tas, and Tse demonstrated two devastating attacks [8]. The *balancing attack* exploits the absence of locking in GHOST-style rules: an adversary with a small validator fraction withholds attestations, then releases them selectively to split honest validators evenly between two chains, so neither side assembles the two-thirds supermajority needed for finality — the *finalized* ledger's liveness is destroyed indefinitely with, in the limit, arbitrarily small adversarial stake. The *avalanche attack* exposes a deeper tension: costless attestations plus latest-message counting admit cascading reorgs displacing honestly proposed blocks, and the LMD discipline itself can be weaponized into a balancing variant surviving naive mitigations [8].

Mitigations are partial: *proposer boost* raises the reorg bar but introduces timing games; vote-expiry and view-merge bound how long withheld votes stay potent. D'Amato and Zanolini's *Goldfish* replaces LMD GHOST with a slot-synchronized propose-and-vote protocol provably reorg-resilient under dynamic participation, composable with finality gadgets [7].

The TLA+ invariant at the heart of the finality layer is simple:

```tla
------------------------------ MODULE CasperFFG ------------------------------
EXTENDS Integers, FiniteSets
CONSTANTS Validators, Stake
VARIABLES justified, finalized, votes

AccountableSafety ==
    \A c1, c2 \in finalized :
        Conflict(c1, c2) => Cardinality(Slashable(c1, c2)) >= TotalStake \div 3
=============================================================================
```

*No two finalized checkpoints conflict without slashable evidence covering at least one third of stake.* Everything else — slots, committees, proposer boost, fork-choice subtleties — is engineering around this guarantee.

##5 Empirical Results and Proofs

**Latency–security numbers** [6] (β = 0.10, ten-second delays):

| Target error | Confirmation latency | Lower bound (private mining) |
|---|---|---|
| 10<sup>−3</sup> | ≈ 4 hours | ≈ 2 hours |
| 10<sup>−6</sup> | ≈ 7 hours | ≈ 5 hours |
| 10<sup>−9</sup> | ≈ 10 hours | ≈ 8 hours |

The gap between achievable latency and the information-theoretic lower bound is roughly two hours across error targets — far tighter than the orders-of-magnitude gaps of earlier analyses [6].

**Fork rate and GHOST's advantage.** Simulations [3] show that at tens-of-seconds block intervals, longest-chain security demands honest supermajorities well above one half of *effective* power, while GHOST holds the one-half threshold because stale honest blocks keep weighting their subtrees. Ethereum's historical proof-of-work uncle rate — several percent — is the empirical trace of the honest-fork waste GHOST reclaims.

**Finality-layer measurements.** On Ethereum proof of stake, justification typically lands within one epoch and finalization within two (≈ 13 minutes) whenever two thirds of stake attests correctly. During the May 2023 finality incidents, missed attestations stalled finalization for several epochs while the LMD GHOST head kept advancing — a live ebb-and-flow demonstration, with the *inactivity leak* (penalizing offline validators until the online supermajority is restored) recovering finality without manual intervention.

**Proof sketches.** The common-prefix proof [2] hinges on *convergence opportunities* — windows with exactly one honest block forcing all honest parties onto one tip — frequent enough (Chernoff) that adversarial lead is a supermartingale. Casper's accountable-safety proof [4] is the pigeonhole argument of §4.3. The ebb-and-flow theorem [9] shows Gasper-style composition is *optimal*: no protocol is both always-available and accountably-safe under dynamic participation, but the two-ledger construction achieves each property on its own ledger.

**Comparative summary.**

| Protocol | Fault threshold | Finality | Confirmation latency | Throughput regime | Accountability |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Nakamoto longest-chain [1,2] | β < 1/2 (hash) | Probabilistic | Hours for 10<sup>−9</sup> | λΔ ≪ 1 required | None |
| GHOST [3] | β < 1/2 at high λΔ | Probabilistic | Minutes–hours | High λΔ tolerated | None |
| Casper FFG [4] | β < 1/3 (stake) | Accountable | Epochs (overlay) | Inherits base layer | Slash ≥ 1/3 |
| Gasper (LMD GHOST + FFG) [5] | β < 1/3 for finality | Ebb-and-flow | ~13 min finalized | 12 s slots | Slash ≥ 1/3 |
| Goldfish [7] | β < 1/2 (validators) | Reorg-resilient | Slot-scale | Synchrony assumed | Gadget-composable |

##6 Limitations

1. **The honest-majority assumption is economic, not cryptographic.** All designs assume the adversary's resource share stays below threshold *forever*; mining-pool concentration and liquid-staking dominance make this an empirical wager, not a theorem.
2. **Bounded-delay models are optimistic.** Real nodes have finite *processing capacity*: block bursts cause congestion that delay-based models ignore, where a *teasing* strategy exploiting congestion strictly dominates private mining, and proof-of-stake variants suffer equivocation-spam amplification. Δ-synchrony bounds are thus upper bounds on real security.
3. **LMD GHOST remains brittle.** The balancing and avalanche attacks [8] admit reorg strategies with small adversarial stake; deployed mitigations (proposer boost, vote expiry) are patches on a disputed foundation, and Goldfish [7] argues the fork-choice rule itself should be replaced.
4. **Finality gadgets trade availability for safety.** When more than one third of stake goes offline or the network partitions, Casper finality *halts by design* — the inactivity leak recovers it only by slowly bleeding offline validators' stake, arguably a liveness failure by another name [4,9].
5. **Weak subjectivity is a trust assumption.** New or long-offline nodes must obtain a recent finalized checkpoint out-of-band; pure genesis sync remains vulnerable to long-range fabrication.
6. **Slashing is not free deterrence.** Accountable safety punishes *after* the fact and assumes the adversary values its stake above the attack's payoff; a sufficiently motivated or hedged attacker may rationally accept slashing.
7. **MEV is out of scope.** The analyses assume participants maximize protocol rewards; in practice, maximal extractable value routinely exceeds block rewards, and behavior under MEV may deviate from the honest strategies the proofs assume.

---

##7 Conclusion

From Nakamoto consensus through GHOST to Casper FFG and Gasper, permissionless consensus grew up: heuristic with informal security [1]; formalized backbone with quantified trade-offs [2,6]; fork choice refusing to waste honest work [3]; finality gadget converting stake into slashable accountability [4]; ebb-and-flow synthesis with availability and finality each optimal on its own ledger [5,9].

No single mechanism escapes the fundamental tension: longest-chain rules are simple but surrender throughput; GHOST reclaims throughput but complicates fork choice and, in latest-message-driven form, opens reorg surface [8]; finality gadgets deliver accountability but halt when participation falters and lean on weak subjectivity. The mature answer is *layered*: a fast, dynamically available fork choice for liveness, crowned by a slow, accountably safe gadget for settlement.

Open problems: single-slot finality; subsampled committees [7]; post-quantum slashing evidence; MEV-aware incentives. The deepest lesson is already secure: in permissionless networks, *finality is not a property of a rule but of an architecture* — and the architectures that endure separate the job of staying live from the job of staying honest.
