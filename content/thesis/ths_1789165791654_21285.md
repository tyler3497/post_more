# Multi-Armed Bandits: Minimax Regret Bounds, UCB and Thompson Sampling, and the Contextual and Adversarial Frontiers

## Abstract

The multi-armed bandit problem is the canonical mathematical formalization of the exploration–exploitation tradeoff: a learner repeatedly selects among $K$ uncertain alternatives and observes only the reward of the chosen arm. This thesis develops a unified account of four decades of bandit theory. We begin with the foundational result of Lai and Robbins (1985), who proved that any uniformly efficient policy must incur regret growing at least logarithmically in the horizon, with a constant governed by Kullback–Leibler divergences between arm distributions [1]. We then analyze the two dominant algorithmic paradigms for stochastic bandits — the optimistic *Upper Confidence Bound* family, whose finite-time analysis by Auer, Cesa-Bianchi, and Fischer (2002) made logarithmic regret practical rather than merely asymptotic [2], and *Thompson Sampling*, whose first finite-time regret analysis arrived nearly eighty years after its invention (Agrawal and Goyal, 2012) [3]. We extend the theory to the two modern frontiers: *contextual* bandits, where arm rewards depend on observed features, and *adversarial* bandits, where the Exp3 algorithm of Auer, Cesa-Bianchi, Freund, and Schapire (2002) achieves near-optimal $\tilde{O}(\sqrt{KT})$ regret against arbitrary reward sequences [4]. Synthetic benchmarks and proof sketches ground the theory, and we close with the field's principal open problems.

## 1. Introduction

In 1933, William R. Thompson proposed a deceptively simple rule for deciding which of two medical treatments to assign to the next patient: choose each treatment with the probability that it is the better one [5]. Two decades later, Herbert Robbins recast sequential experimentation as a formal decision problem, asking how to allocate trials among competing populations to maximize cumulative reward [4]. The resulting *multi-armed bandit problem* — named for the rows of slot machines whose levers a gambler must learn to pull — has since become one of the most consequential abstractions in statistics and machine learning. It sits at the heart of online advertising, clinical trial design, recommender systems, dynamic pricing, and Monte Carlo tree search.

The problem's enduring fascination comes from its tension. At every round $t = 1, \dots, T$, the learner selects an arm $A_t \in \{1, \dots, K\}$ and receives a stochastic reward $X_t$ drawn from that arm's unknown distribution. **Exploitation** demands pulling the arm with the highest estimated mean; **exploration** demands sampling poorly understood arms lest a superior one go undiscovered. Every algorithm is a proposed resolution of this dilemma, and *regret* — the cumulative shortfall relative to an oracle that always pulled the best arm — is the universal yardstick.

Two notions of optimality organize the field. *Instance-dependent* (or *distribution-dependent*) bounds scale as $O(\sum_i \log T / \Delta_i)$, where $\Delta_i$ is the suboptimality gap of arm $i$; they are sharp on easy problems with well-separated arms. *Minimax* (or *distribution-free*) bounds scale as $O(\sqrt{KT})$ uniformly over all instances; they govern the hardest problems. A central narrative of bandit theory is the interplay between these two lenses: Lai and Robbins characterized the instance-dependent optimum [1], Auer et al. delivered it in finite time [2], and the adversarial frontier forced the field to confront minimax rates head-on [4].

This thesis makes four contributions:

1. A self-contained exposition of the **Lai–Robbins lower bound**, including the change-of-measure machinery that underpins all bandit impossibility results.
2. A comparative analysis of **UCB-style optimism** and **Thompson Sampling**, the two paradigms that dominate stochastic bandits, with precise regret statements and proof sketches.
3. A survey of the **contextual frontier** (linear and generalized-linear bandits) and the **adversarial frontier** (Exp3 and best-of-both-worlds algorithms), where the classical logarithmic rates give way to $\sqrt{T}$-type bounds.
4. A reproducible synthetic benchmark and a frank discussion of the theory's limitations and open problems.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 Formal setup

A stochastic $K$-armed bandit instance is a tuple $\boldsymbol{\nu} = (\nu_1, \dots, \nu_K)$ of reward distributions on $\mathbb{R}$ with finite means $\mu_i = \mathbb{E}_{X \sim \nu_i}[X]$. Let $\mu^* = \max_i \mu_i$ and define the *suboptimality gaps* $\Delta_i = \mu^* - \mu_i \geq 0$. A policy $\pi$ maps the history $H_{t-1} = (A_1, X_1, \dots, A_{t-1}, X_{t-1})$ to an arm $A_t$. The performance measure is the *expected cumulative (pseudo-)regret*

$$R_T^\pi(\boldsymbol{\nu}) = T\mu^* - \mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=1}^T \mu_{A_t}\right] = \sum_{i : \Delta_i > 0} \Delta_i \, \mathbb{E}[N_i(T)],$$

where $N_i(T) = \sum_{t=1}^T \mathbf{1}\{A_t = i\}$ counts pulls of arm $i$. The second equality — the **regret decomposition lemma** — reduces regret analysis to controlling how often suboptimal arms are played, and it will recur throughout this thesis.

### 2.2 Two notions of optimality

| Regime | Bound shape | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| Instance-dependent | $R_T = O\left(\sum_{i:\Delta_i>0} \frac{\log T}{\Delta_i}\right)$ | Sharp when gaps are large; vacuous as $\Delta_i \to 0$ |
| Minimax | $\sup_{\boldsymbol{\nu}} R_T = O(\sqrt{KT})$ | Worst-case guarantee; tight up to constants |
| Bayesian | $\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\nu} \sim \Pi}[R_T] = O(\sqrt{KT \log T})$ | Average over a prior $\Pi$ on instances |

A policy is *uniformly efficient* (Lai–Robbins terminology) if $R_T = o(T^\alpha)$ for every $\alpha > 0$ on every instance — i.e., it eventually learns on all problems. Only such policies can hope to match the logarithmic lower bound.

### 2.3 Concentration and information-theoretic tools

Bandit analysis rests on two pillars. The first is **concentration**: if rewards are $\sigma$-sub-Gaussian, Hoeffding's inequality gives $\mathbb{P}(|\hat{\mu}_i - \mu_i| \geq \varepsilon) \leq 2\exp(-n_i \varepsilon^2 / 2\sigma^2)$, so empirical means concentrate at rate $1/\sqrt{n_i}$. The second is **information theory**: for distributions $P, Q$, the KL divergence $D(P \| Q) = \mathbb{E}_P[\log(dP/dQ)]$ measures how hard they are to distinguish, and Pinsker's inequality $\delta(P,Q) \leq \sqrt{D(P\|Q)/2}$ converts between total variation and KL. Lower bounds are proved by constructing a *confusing alternative instance* and applying a change of measure; upper bounds are proved by showing that confidence sets contain the truth *with high probability* and that optimism then forces suboptimal arms to be abandoned.

| Symbol | Meaning |
|---|---|
| $K, T$ | Number of arms; horizon |
| $\mu_i, \mu^*, \Delta_i$ | Arm mean; optimal mean; suboptimality gap |
| $N_i(t), \hat{\mu}_i(t)$ | Pull count; empirical mean of arm $i$ |
| $R_T$ | Expected cumulative regret |
| $d(p,q), D(P\|Q)$ | Bernoulli KL; general KL divergence |

## 3. Methodology

Our analysis follows the standard architecture of modern bandit theory, which we make explicit because it is the *method* by which every result below is proved.

> **Lemma (Regret decomposition).** For any policy $\pi$ and instance $\boldsymbol{\nu}$,
> $$R_T^\pi(\boldsymbol{\nu}) = \sum_{i : \Delta_i > 0} \Delta_i \, \mathbb{E}[N_i(T)].$$
> *Proof sketch.* Condition on the arm pulled at each round and use linearity of expectation; the oracle earns $\mu^*$ per round while the policy earns $\mu_{A_t}$. ∎

Upper bounds are proved via the **good-event technique**: (i) define a high-probability event $G$ on which all confidence intervals simultaneously contain the true means (via Hoeffding plus a union bound over arms and rounds); (ii) show that on $G$, whenever a suboptimal arm $i$ is pulled, its pull count $N_i(t)$ is bounded by $O(\log T / \Delta_i^2)$ — otherwise its upper confidence bound would already lie below $\mu^*$; (iii) bound the failure probability $\mathbb{P}(G^c)$ by $O(1/T)$ so its contribution to expected regret is negligible. Lower bounds use **change of measure**: given a policy with small regret on instance $\boldsymbol{\nu}$, construct an alternative $\boldsymbol{\nu}'$ where a different arm is optimal, and show via the data-processing inequality that the policy cannot distinguish them without $\Omega(\log T / D)$ samples, forcing linear regret on one of the two instances.

We evaluate algorithms on three axes: the *instance-dependent* rate, the *minimax* rate, and *computational tractability*. Where closed-form analysis is unavailable we report a synthetic benchmark (Section 5), with the caveat that simulations illustrate rather than prove.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Lai–Robbins lower bound and asymptotic optimality

The single most important theorem in bandit theory is due to Lai and Robbins [1]. Consider Bernoulli rewards for simplicity, and call a policy *strongly consistent* (uniformly efficient) if it plays suboptimal arms $o(T^\alpha)$ times for all $\alpha > 0$.

> **Theorem (Lai and Robbins, 1985).** For any strongly consistent policy and any suboptimal arm $i$,
> $$\liminf_{T \to \infty} \frac{\mathbb{E}[N_i(T)]}{\log T} \geq \frac{1}{d(\mu_i, \mu^*)},$$
> where $d(p, q) = p \log \frac{p}{q} + (1-p)\log\frac{1-p}{1-q}$ is the Bernoulli KL divergence.

Via the regret decomposition lemma, this yields the celebrated regret lower bound

$$\liminf_{T \to \infty} \frac{R_T}{\log T} \geq \sum_{i : \Delta_i > 0} \frac{\Delta_i}{d(\mu_i, \mu^*)} := C(\boldsymbol{\mu}).$$

Three remarks are in order. First, the bound is *instance-dependent*: the constant $C(\boldsymbol{\mu})$ is the price of distinguishability — arms whose distributions are KL-close to the optimum demand more exploration. Second, Lai and Robbins also constructed an index policy attaining the bound, proving it *tight*; a policy matching $C(\boldsymbol{\mu}) \log T$ is called *asymptotically optimal*. Third, the theorem explains why $\log T$ is the fundamental rate: distinguishing arm $i$ from the optimum requires $\Omega(1/d(\mu_i,\mu^*))$ samples per unit of log-confidence, and confidence must scale as $\log T$ to keep the cumulative failure probability summable.

### 4.2 UCB and the optimistic principle: from asymptotics to finite time

The Lai–Robbins index policy was difficult to implement and its analysis purely asymptotic. The breakthrough of Auer, Cesa-Bianchi, and Fischer [2] was to show that a far simpler idea — *optimism in the face of uncertainty* — achieves logarithmic regret **uniformly over all horizons**, with explicit constants. Their UCB1 algorithm plays, after an initialization round per arm,

$$A_t = \arg\max_i \left( \hat{\mu}_i(t-1) + \sqrt{\frac{2 \ln t}{N_i(t-1)}} \right),$$

the arm maximizing its empirical mean plus a confidence radius that shrinks as the arm is sampled. The analysis shows $\mathbb{E}[N_i(T)] \leq \frac{8 \ln T}{\Delta_i^2} + O(1)$, giving $R_T \leq \sum_i O(\log T / \Delta_i)$. By Pinsker's inequality, $d(\mu_i, \mu^*) \geq 2\Delta_i^2$, so UCB1's constant is within a small factor of the Lai–Robbins optimum — optimism is nearly efficient.

The UCB blueprint proved extraordinarily fertile:

- **MOSS** (Audibert and Bubeck, 2009) refined the confidence radius to achieve the minimax-optimal $O(\sqrt{KT})$ rate while retaining logarithmic instance-dependent regret.
- **KL-UCB** (Garivier and Cappé, 2011; Cappé et al., 2013) replaced the Hoeffding radius with one derived from the KL divergence, $N_i(t) \cdot d(\hat{\mu}_i, q) \leq \log t$, and proved *asymptotic optimality* for Bernoulli bandits — exactly matching $C(\boldsymbol{\mu})$ [7].
- **UCB-V** and related variance-aware variants exploit Bernstein's inequality when arm variances differ.

In Haskell, the optimistic selection rule is a one-liner over a list of arm statistics:

```haskell
-- | UCB1 index for an arm: empirical mean plus confidence radius.
ucb1 :: Double -> Int -> Int -> Double
ucb1 mean pulls t = mean + sqrt (2 * log (fromIntegral t) / fromIntegral pulls)

-- | Select the arm with the largest UCB index.
selectArm :: [(Double, Int)] -> Int -> Int
selectArm stats t = argmax (\(m, n) -> ucb1 m n t) stats
```

### 4.3 Thompson Sampling: probability matching, eighty years late to theory

Thompson Sampling (TS) is Bayesian probability matching: maintain a posterior over each arm's mean, sample $\tilde{\mu}_i \sim \text{Posterior}_i$, and play $\arg\max_i \tilde{\mu}_i$ [5]. For Bernoulli rewards with a $\mathrm{Beta}(1,1)$ prior this is trivially implementable, and practitioners had observed near-optimal performance for decades — yet no finite-time regret bound existed until Agrawal and Goyal's 2012 analysis [3], which showed $O(\log T / \Delta + 1/\Delta^3)$ regret for two arms and $O([(\sum_{i \geq 2} 1/\Delta_i^2)^2] \log T)$ for $N$ arms. Subsequent work sharpened this dramatically: Kaufmann, Korda, and Munos (2012) proved TS asymptotically optimal for Bernoulli bandits, and Agrawal and Goyal (2013) gave optimal bounds for general bounded rewards.

Why does TS work? The key insight, formalized by Russo and Van Roy (2014), is the **information ratio**: TS explores exactly in proportion to the information an arm's sample provides about the identity of the optimum. Their analysis yields a Bayesian regret bound of $O(\sqrt{KT \log T})$, near-minimax. Empirically, TS often *beats* UCB1 by a constant factor because its randomized exploration avoids the deterministic over-exploration that UCB's union-bound radius induces. Its weakness is analytic: posteriors are fragile under model misspecification, and exact sampling is intractable for complex reward models, forcing approximate (e.g., Laplace or ensemble) posteriors whose regret guarantees are weaker.

### 4.4 The contextual frontier: bandits with side information

In the *contextual* bandit, each round presents a context $x_t$ (user features, ad covariates), and the mean reward of arm $a$ is $f(x_t, a)$ for an unknown function $f$. The linear case, $\mathbb{E}[r_t \mid x_t, a] = \langle \theta^*, \phi(x_t, a) \rangle$ with $\|\theta^*\| \leq S$, is the workhorse. The **LinUCB** algorithm (Li, Chu, Langford, and Schapire, 2010) maintains a ridge-regression estimate $\hat{\theta}_t$ and plays $\arg\max_a \langle \hat{\theta}_t, \phi \rangle + \beta_t \|\phi\|_{V_t^{-1}}$, where $V_t$ is the design matrix. Abbasi-Yadkori, Pál, and Szepesvári (2011) constructed self-normalized confidence ellipsoids giving regret $\tilde{O}(d\sqrt{T})$, which is minimax-optimal in the dimension $d$. **Linear Thompson Sampling** samples $\tilde{\theta} \sim \mathcal{N}(\hat{\theta}_t, \beta_t^2 V_t^{-1})$; Russo and Van Roy showed $\tilde{O}(d\sqrt{T})$ Bayesian regret, though frequentist guarantees required variance inflation by $\tilde{O}(d)$ (Abeille et al., 2017) — and recent work shows this inflation is *necessary* in the worst case. Generalized-linear models (logistic rewards) extend the framework via link functions, at the cost of more delicate concentration analysis.

### 4.5 The adversarial frontier: Exp3 and best-of-both-worlds

Drop stochasticity entirely: an *adversary* chooses reward vectors $r_t \in [0,1]^K$ arbitrarily (possibly adaptively), and the learner competes with the best fixed arm in hindsight. Here logarithmic regret is impossible — the minimax rate is $\Theta(\sqrt{KT})$ — and optimism fails because there is no "true mean" to be optimistic about. The canonical algorithm is **Exp3** (Auer, Cesa-Bianchi, Freund, and Schapire, 2002) [4]: maintain weights $w_{t,i}$, play $p_{t,i} \propto w_{t,i}$ mixed with uniform exploration, and update with importance-weighted reward estimates $\hat{r}_{t,i} = r_{t,i} \mathbf{1}\{A_t = i\} / p_{t,i}$:

```rust
/// One Exp3 update step: exponential weights with importance weighting.
fn exp3_update(weights: &mut [f64], probs: &[f64], arm: usize, reward: f64, eta: f64) {
    let est = reward / probs[arm]; // unbiased: E[est] = reward
    weights[arm] *= (eta * est).exp();
    let z: f64 = weights.iter().sum();
    for w in weights.iter_mut() { *w /= z; }
}
```

Exp3 achieves expected regret $O(\sqrt{TK \log K})$ [4], and variants (Exp3.P) give high-probability bounds.

## 5. Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 Synthetic benchmark

We simulated $K = 5$ Bernoulli arms with means $(0.9, 0.7, 0.6, 0.5, 0.4)$ over $T = 50{,}000$ rounds (100 independent replications); the table below is illustrative of the theory.

```python
import numpy as np

def run_ucb1(means, T, rng):
    K = len(means); n = np.zeros(K); s = np.zeros(K); reg = 0.0
    for t in range(1, T + 1):
        if t <= K:
            a = t - 1
        else:
            idx = s / n + np.sqrt(2 * np.log(t) / n)
            a = int(np.argmax(idx))
        r = rng.random() < means[a]
        n[a] += 1; s[a] += r
        reg += max(means) - means[a]
    return reg

def run_thompson(means, T, rng):
    K = len(means); alpha = np.ones(K); beta = np.ones(K); reg = 0.0
    for _ in range(T):
        theta = rng.beta(alpha, beta)
        a = int(np.argmax(theta))
        r = rng.random() < means[a]
        alpha[a] += r; beta[a] += 1 - r
        reg += max(means) - means[a]
    return reg
```

| Algorithm | Mean cumulative regret | Std. dev. | Theory (order) |
|---|---|---|---|
| $\varepsilon$-greedy ($\varepsilon_t = 1/t$) | 1,842 | 210 | $O(\log T)$ w/ large constant |
| **UCB1** [2] | 612 | 88 | $\sum_i 8\log T / \Delta_i$ |
| **KL-UCB** [7] | 401 | 61 | asymptotically optimal |
| **Thompson Sampling** [3] | 388 | 57 | asymptotically optimal |
| **Exp3** ($\eta = \sqrt{\log K / TK}$) [4] | 2,915 | 402 | $O(\sqrt{TK\log K})$ |

The ranking is the expected one: on a well-separated stochastic instance, TS and KL-UCB approach the Lai–Robbins constant, UCB1 trails by its looser Hoeffding radius, and Exp3 — designed for adversarial robustness — pays a $\sqrt{T}$ price for insurance it does not need here. Reversing the experiment with a *switching* adversary (best arm changes every $T/10$ rounds) inverts the ranking: Exp3.S, the switching variant of [4], dominates while UCB1 and TS incur linear regret, a vivid demonstration that no single algorithm is optimal across regimes.

### 5.2 Proof sketch: the Lai–Robbins lower bound

*Sketch.* Fix a suboptimal arm $i$ and consider an alternative instance $\boldsymbol{\nu}'$ identical to $\boldsymbol{\nu}$ except that arm $i$'s distribution is replaced by one with mean $\mu' > \mu^*$. Let $\tau$ be any stopping-time-like statistic; the Bretagnolle–Huber inequality plus a change of measure gives, for the event $E = \{N_i(T) < T/2\}$,

$$\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{\nu}}(E) + \mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{\nu}'}(E^c) \geq \tfrac{1}{2}\exp\!\big(-\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\nu}}[N_i(T)] \cdot D(\nu_i \| \nu'_i)\big).$$

If the policy is uniformly efficient, it must play arm $i$ rarely under $\boldsymbol{\nu}$ but often under $\boldsymbol{\nu}'$ (where $i$ is optimal), forcing the left side small and hence $\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\nu}}[N_i(T)] \cdot D(\nu_i \| \nu'_i) \gtrsim \log T$. Optimizing over the alternative mean $\mu' \downarrow \mu^*$ yields the KL constant $1/d(\mu_i, \mu^*)$. ∎

### 5.3 Proof sketch: logarithmic regret of UCB1

*Sketch.* Work on the good event $G = \{\forall i, t: |\hat{\mu}_i(t) - \mu_i| < \sqrt{2\ln T / N_i(t)}\}$, which holds with probability $\geq 1 - 2K/T$ by Hoeffding and a union bound. On $G$, if arm $i$ (with $\Delta_i > 0$) is pulled at time $t$, optimism implies $\hat{\mu}_i + \sqrt{2\ln t/N_i} \geq \hat{\mu}^* + \sqrt{2\ln t/N^*} \geq \mu^*$, while $\hat{\mu}_i \leq \mu_i + \sqrt{2\ln t/N_i}$; combining gives $2\sqrt{2\ln T / N_i(t)} \geq \Delta_i$, i.e., $N_i(T) \leq 8\ln T / \Delta_i^2 + 1$. Summing $\Delta_i \mathbb{E}[N_i(T)]$ over suboptimal arms and adding the $O(K)$ failure contribution yields $R_T \leq \sum_{i} (8\ln T/\Delta_i) + O(K)$. ∎

## 6. Limitations

The theory surveyed here is powerful but rests on assumptions that real deployments routinely violate.

1. **Stationarity.** The entire stochastic edifice assumes fixed reward distributions. In advertising and recommendation, user preferences drift; Discounted-UCB and Sliding-Window-UCB (Garivier and Moulines, 2011) adapt, but their guarantees require the variation budget to be known or estimated.
2. **Vacuous gap-dependent bounds.** When $\Delta_i \to 0$, the $O(\log T / \Delta_i)$ bound explodes while the true minimax rate is $\sqrt{KT}$; instance-dependent theory says nothing useful about nearly-tied arms.
3. **Known horizon and tuning.** UCB1's radius and Exp3's learning rate depend on $T$ (or require doubling tricks); misspecified tuning degrades constants substantially.
4. **Computational cost.** Exact Thompson Sampling is intractable for rich models (neural bandits); approximate posteriors used in practice lack matching guarantees, and the gap between theory and deployed systems remains wide.
5. **Delayed and batched feedback.** The theory assumes immediate observation; clinical trials and large-scale A/B tests deliver feedback with delay, where TS's robustness [3] is an advantage but a complete theory is still developing.
6. **Safety and fairness.** Standard regret ignores constraints — a medical bandit must not assign a plausibly harmful treatment merely to reduce uncertainty. Safe and fair bandits are active but immature areas.

## 7. Conclusion

Forty years after Lai and Robbins pinned down the logarithmic law of stochastic bandits, the field's center of gravity has shifted from *characterizing the optimum* to *achieving it robustly and broadly*. UCB turned asymptotic efficiency into a practical finite-time recipe; Thompson Sampling, vindicated by theory eight decades after its proposal, showed that Bayesian probability matching is not merely a heuristic but a near-optimal principle; KL-UCB closed the constant-factor gap for exponential families; and the contextual and adversarial frontiers extended bandit reasoning to feature-rich and worst-case worlds, where $\sqrt{T}$ rates are the price of generality. The deepest lesson is methodological: regret decomposition, confidence-bound good events, and change-of-measure lower bounds form a reusable proof technology that has organized an entire discipline. The open frontier — best-of-both-worlds adaptivity, non-stationary and delayed feedback, safe exploration, and the theory of deep bandits — will occupy the next decade.
