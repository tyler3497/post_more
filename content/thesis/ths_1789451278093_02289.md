## Abstract

The tokamak remains the leading magnetic confinement concept for fusion energy, and its performance is fundamentally governed by magnetohydrodynamic (MHD) equilibrium and stability together with the active magnetic control systems that enforce them. This thesis develops a unified treatment of axisymmetric equilibrium via the Grad–Shafranov equation, the energy principle governing ideal and resistive MHD stability, and the feedback architectures that stabilize vertically elongated plasmas and suppress resistive wall modes at reactor-relevant beta. We derive the physical mechanisms behind the vertical instability, the Troyon beta limit, error-field amplification, and neoclassical tearing mode growth, and we analyze how poloidal-field coil systems, internal correction coils, and digital controllers extend the operational space beyond passive wall stabilization. Drawing on experiments from DIII-D, TCV, and JET and on the ITER and STEP design bases, we assess model-based linear controllers, deep reinforcement learning approaches to shape control, and the remaining open problems of disruption avoidance, power-supply saturation, and sensor noise in burning plasmas [1][2][3][4][5][6][7][8].











##1 Intro

Magnetic confinement of a thermonuclear plasma is an exercise in sustaining a configuration that nature abhors: a hundred-million-degree ionized gas held away from material walls by the Lorentz force alone. Among toroidal concepts, the tokamak — a toroidal chamber in which a strong toroidal magnetic field is combined with a poloidal field generated by the plasma current itself — has demonstrated the best-confined, highest-performance fusion plasmas to date, and it underpins the ITER project and the majority of national fusion roadmaps [1]. Yet the tokamak's confinement is never passive. The same current that provides the rotational transform also supplies free energy for kink and tearing instabilities; the elongation that improves confinement simultaneously excites a vertical instability on the microsecond time scale; and the high plasma pressure required for economic fusion power pushes the discharge against ideal MHD beta limits that only a nearby conducting wall — or active magnetic feedback — can relieve [2][8].

This thesis treats equilibrium, stability, and control as a single coupled problem: the Grad–Shafranov equation defines the space of possible equilibria, the ideal MHD energy principle and resistive MHD theory partition it into stable, wall-stabilized, and feedback-requiring regions, and the PF coil system navigates the plasma through this space in real time [3][6]. We develop the equilibrium and stability foundations (§2), the integrated-control methodology (§3), a deep dive into the four principal control problems (§4), and a confrontation with experimental evidence (§5), before discussing limitations (§6) and conclusions (§7).

> **Thesis statement:** The operational space of a high-performance tokamak is the intersection of the MHD-stable equilibrium manifold with the set of configurations that are *actively controllable* given finite actuator authority, sensor fidelity, and wall conductivity; understanding and enlarging that intersection is the core scientific task of tokamak magnetic control.

The remainder of this thesis is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews the equilibrium and stability foundations. Section 3 describes the methodology of integrated plasma control modeling. Section 4 presents a deep dive into the four principal control problems. Section 5 confronts theory with experimental evidence. Section 6 discusses limitations, and Section 7 concludes.











##2 Background

### Axisymmetric equilibrium

In steady state, the MHD force balance reads **J** × **B** = ∇p, where **J** is the current density, **B** the magnetic field, and p the plasma pressure.

```
Δ* ψ = -μ₀ R² dp/dψ - F dF/dψ
```

with the elliptic operator Δ* = R ∂/∂R (R⁻¹ ∂/∂R) + ∂²/∂Z².

Two derived quantities dominate confinement physics.

### Ideal MHD stability and beta limits

The ideal MHD energy principle states that an equilibrium is stable if and only if the potential energy change δW associated with every admissible displacement ξ is non-negative [8].

```python
def troyon_limit(plasma_current_MA, minor_radius_m, toroidal_field_T, li=1.0, C=3.5):
    """Maximum stable beta (percent) from the Troyon scaling: beta_max = C * li * I_p / (a * B_T)."""
    return C * li * plasma_current_MA / (minor_radius_m * toroidal_field_T)

# ITER baseline: I_p = 15 MA, a = 2.0 m, B_T = 5.3 T
print(f"ITER Troyon beta limit ~ {troyon_limit(15.0, 2.0, 5.3):.1f} %")
```

A perfectly conducting wall close to the plasma stabilizes the external kink by flux conservation, raising the limit from the *no-wall* beta limit β_N^no-wall to the *ideal-wall* limit β_N^ideal-wall. In practice the wall is resistive, so flux eventually penetrates and the mode re-emerges as the *resistive wall mode* (RWM), growing on the wall's resistive diffusion time — milliseconds rather than microseconds — and therefore amenable to feedback [2][5].

### The vertical instability

Elongating the plasma cross section (elongation κ = b/a > 1) increases the plasma current at fixed q and improves energy confinement, but the resulting equilibrium sits in a magnetic field whose curvature is unfavorable in the vertical direction: a small displacement δZ experiences a destabilizing force on the microsecond Alfvénic time scale [3]. A nearby conducting wall slows the growth to the wall's L/R time via induced image currents, and active feedback using the PF coils completes the stabilization. Vertical control is therefore *mandatory* for every elongated tokamak, and its failure is the fastest route to a disruption.

### Resistive MHD and tearing modes

Finite resistivity permits magnetic reconnection at rational surfaces (q = m/n), producing *tearing modes* that form magnetic islands. The *neoclassical tearing mode* (NTM) is metastable: a seed island (often from a sawtooth crash) triggers loss of bootstrap current inside the island, driving it to large amplitude and frequently triggering disruptions [2][9]. Stabilization requires localized electron cyclotron current drive (ECCD) deposited precisely on the island's rational surface — a demanding real-time control problem.











##3 Methodology

Our analysis follows the methodology of *integrated plasma control* (IPC) as practiced in the design of ITER and STEP [3][6]. The approach has three stages:

1. **Equilibrium and linear response modeling.** A reference equilibrium is computed with a free-boundary Grad–Shafranov solver. The plasma response to coil currents and to rigid displacements is linearized, and the passive conductors (vessel, blanket modules, cryostat) are represented as a network of toroidal filaments with mutual inductances computed from geometry. The result is a linear time-invariant state-space model

   ```
   dx/dt = A x + B u,    y = C x
   ```

   where x contains the coil, vessel, and plasma current perturbations, u the applied coil voltages, and y the magnetic diagnostic signals. The vertical instability appears as a single unstable eigenvalue of A; all other modes are stable or marginally stable [3].

2. **Controller synthesis.** For axisymmetric control, a fast vertical-stabilization loop (bandwidth of order 100 Hz–1 kHz) is combined with a slower shape and current controller. Classical designs use PID and linear-quadratic-Gaussian (LQG) methods with explicit handling of power-supply voltage saturation, which otherwise destroys the region of attraction of the linear controller [3]. For non-axisymmetric RWM control, the controller identifies the n = 1 mode structure from sensor arrays and drives correction coils with a phase and gain chosen to oppose the mode's growth [2][5].

3. **Validation against experiment and nonlinear simulation.** Linear designs are tested in nonlinear axisymmetric simulators (e.g., the TokSys/MATLAB environment validated against DIII-D, NSTX, and JET discharges) and, where available, against dedicated experiments: TCV for shape control including deep reinforcement learning [4], DIII-D for RWM feedback at low rotation [2][5], and JET for vertical-stability margins at ITER-relevant parameters [1].

##4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The vertical stability problem: from image currents to power-supply saturation

The vertical instability is the purest example of a control problem that is *created* by the pursuit of performance. Consider an elongated plasma as a current-carrying wire of current I_p in an external field whose radial component varies vertically. Displacing the wire upward by δZ changes the Lorentz force by an amount proportional to the field's decay index n = −(R/B_Z)(∂B_Z/∂R); for the field curvature produced by the PF coils that elongate the plasma, n < 0 and the force is destabilizing [3]. The open-loop growth rate without a wall is of order the inverse Alfvén time — several megahertz — utterly beyond any actuator.

Salvation comes from the conducting structures surrounding the plasma. A vertical displacement induces image currents in the vessel wall whose field opposes the motion (Lenz's law), and the coupled plasma–wall system grows only on the wall's L/R time — tens of milliseconds for ITER's double-walled vessel. The control problem is thus transformed from impossible to demanding: the feedback system must detect the displacement from magnetic diagnostics and drive the PF coils to supply a restoring field faster than the wall-time growth rate, with enough voltage authority to handle large disturbances such as edge-localized modes (ELMs) [3].

The ITER design illustrates the engineering reality. The fast vertical-stabilization loop uses dedicated in-vessel coils driven by a high-voltage converter, with the plasma vertical velocity as the primary feedback signal. Linearized models of ITER-FEAT show a single unstable eigenmode; the controller's region of attraction is bounded by power-supply *voltage* saturation, which — unlike current saturation — is produced by the controller itself with no intrinsic delay and instantly removes a degree of freedom from the feedback [3]. Nonlinear anti-windup designs enlarge the region of attraction to the full null-controllable region, but the fundamental lesson stands: for ITER-scale devices, the vertical control margin is a *power-supply* margin, and every large transient (H-mode transitions, ELMs, impurity influxes) spends it. Spherical-tokamak power plants such as STEP amplify the problem further, with elongation near 3, minimal plasma–wall gaps, and position tolerances near 1 mm for the double-null exhaust solution [6].

### 4.2 Resistive wall modes: rotation, error fields, and feedback

Above the no-wall beta limit, the plasma is ideally unstable to the external kink, and only the wall's conductivity holds it. The resulting RWM is a global mode with growth rate set by the wall time and a rotation frequency tied to the plasma rotation at the resonant surface. Two stabilization mechanisms compete [2][5][8]:

- **Rotational stabilization.** If the plasma rotates sufficiently fast relative to the wall, dissipative coupling (through ion Landau damping, continuum resonances, or collisional effects) damps the mode. DIII-D experiments demonstrated sustained operation above the no-wall limit with high rotation, but burning plasmas are expected to rotate slowly — ITER's predicted rotation is well below the critical value — so rotation alone is not a reactor solution.
- **Magnetic feedback.** Non-axisymmetric coils sense the mode's magnetic signature and apply a counteracting field. The MARS-F code predicts, and DIII-D's internal I-coil experiments confirm, that the power-supply response time must be a fraction of the RWM growth time for feedback to succeed [2].

The subtlety that makes RWM control genuinely difficult is *error-field amplification*, predicted by Boozer [8]: near marginal stability, even a tiny residual error field resonantly excites the stable RWM, producing a large plasma response that brakes the rotation and can trigger the mode. The DIII-D program showed that successful high-beta operation requires *simultaneous* slow dynamic error-field correction and fast RWM feedback: the former removes the quasi-static drive, the latter suppresses the dynamic mode, and the two together handled even ELM-induced RWM pile-up at beta values 20 percent above the no-wall limit for over two seconds [2]. This guides the allocation of correction-coil authority in ITER, whose resonant magnetic perturbation (RMP) coils double as the RWM feedback actuators [9].

Recent theoretical work suggests the stakes are higher still: resistive wall *tearing* modes (RWTMs) may underlie many locked-mode disruptions, and simulations indicate that feedback with the RMP coils could prevent these disruptions in ITER [9] — reframing disruption avoidance as, in part, a magnetic-control problem.

### 4.3 Shape, position, and current control: the poloidal-field system as actuator

While vertical stabilization and RWM suppression are the dramatic control problems, the routine work of the PF system — maintaining the plasma's shape, position, and current against the slow evolution of profiles — determines the discharge's confinement quality and divertor power handling. The PF coil set must simultaneously:

1. Provide the equilibrium vertical field for radial force balance (the hoop-force balance against the plasma's outward expansion);
2. Impose the elongation and triangularity that optimize confinement and MHD stability;
3. Position the X-point(s) of the divertor configuration to control power exhaust; and
4. Regulate the plasma current during ramp-up, flat-top, and ramp-down.

These objectives are coupled through the mutual inductances of the plasma–coil–vessel system, and the controller must decouple them — typically via singular-value-decomposition-based control of shape descriptors (plasma–wall gaps, X-point positions) with the most effective coil-current combinations [6].

The actuator constraints are severe. Superconducting PF coils — required in ITER and any reactor — respond on timescales of seconds due to their large inductance and converter voltage limits; fast disturbances must be handled by in-vessel copper coils with limited current capability. The STEP design study makes this hierarchy explicit: superconducting PF coils under voltage control for the slow shape program, supplemented by fast in-vessel coils for vertical stabilization [6].

### 4.4 Learning-based control: deep reinforcement learning on TCV

The classical pipeline — build a linear model, synthesize a controller, validate in simulation — rests on the quality of the model, and tokamak models are perpetually incomplete: profile evolution, ELMs, and MHD events are poorly captured by linear response theory. An alternative paradigm demonstrated on the TCV tokamak trains a deep reinforcement learning (RL) agent in simulation and deploys it directly on the device [4].

The TCV experiments are striking. An RL controller stabilized elongations up to κ = 1.9 with vertical-instability growth rates exceeding 1.4 kHz while acting at only 10 kHz — a regime where the growth time is comparable to the control cycle. It tracked time-varying ITER-relevant H-mode trajectories as β_p rose to 1.12, maintained plasma current and shape to RMSE values of a few kA and about 1.5 cm, and — most remarkably — directly synthesized a *negative-triangularity* diverted configuration (δ = −0.8) with X-points at both corners, a configuration of active research interest that would conventionally require a dedicated controller design [4].

The significance is twofold. Scientifically, it shows that model-free policies can discover robust nonlinear control laws for a system whose disturbances are poorly captured by linear theory. Practically, it suggests a future in which controllers are *trained* rather than *designed* — adapted to each device's as-built geometry and calibrated against its actual diagnostic noise. The open questions concern safety certification (an RL policy carries no classical stability certificate), generalization across the discharge program, and the sim-to-real gap for burning plasmas where training data cannot be gathered by trial and error [4].

| Control problem | Time scale | Actuators | Key physics limit |
|---|---|---|---|
| Vertical stabilization | 1–100 ms (wall time) | In-vessel VS coils, PF coils | Power-supply voltage saturation |
| RWM feedback | 1–10 ms (wall time) | Non-axisymmetric correction coils | Error-field amplification, rotation |
| NTM suppression | 10–100 ms (island growth) | ECCD launcher steering | Seed-island threshold, alignment |
| Shape/position control | 0.1–10 s (profile evolution) | Superconducting PF coils | Coil voltage/current limits |
| Disruption avoidance | 10 ms–1 s (precursor growth) | Massive gas injection, RMP coils | Locked-mode deceleration |

##5 Empirical/Proofs

The theoretical framework above is anchored in five decades of experimental evidence. We summarize the decisive results.

**Beta-limit scalings and wall stabilization.** Multi-machine databases established the Troyon scaling of the maximum achievable β_N and demonstrated the stabilizing effect of close-fitting conducting walls, with the no-wall and ideal-wall limits — computed by codes such as DCON and MARS — becoming the standard language for specifying a device's pressure capability [1][2]. The ITER Physics Basis codified these results, setting β_N = 1.8 for the Q = 10 scenario safely below the no-wall limit while noting that advanced scenarios at β_N ≈ 3 would require active RWM control [1].

**RWM feedback at low rotation.** The DIII-D internal-coil experiments of Okabayashi et al. provide the key proof of principle for reactor-relevant RWM control: with plasma rotation reduced by n = 3 magnetic braking to values far below the critical rotation for passive stabilization, simultaneous RWM and NTM suppression was sustained for over two seconds at β_N twenty percent above the no-wall limit, using modest ECCD and combined slow error-field correction with fast feedback [2]. This is the closest existing demonstration to ITER's expected low-rotation, high-beta regime, and it validates the MARS-F modeling that underlies the ITER RWM coil design.

**Deep RL shape control.** The TCV results of Degrave et al. constitute an existence proof that learned controllers can outperform the classical design cycle on the actual device: the same training pipeline produced controllers for high elongation, H-mode trajectory tracking, and negative triangularity without device-specific redesign, achieving millimeter-to-centimeter shape accuracy [4]. The sim-to-real transfer succeeded because the training simulator, while imperfect, captured the dominant linear dynamics and the actuator constraints faithfully.

**Disruption statistics and the RWTM hypothesis.** Database studies of DIII-D locked-mode disruptions identified two signatures consistent with resistive wall tearing modes: proximity of the q = 2 surface to the resistive wall and a peaked current profile from edge cooling. Simulations show that feedback using the RMP coils can prevent the RWTM growth, suggesting that a substantial fraction of disruptions — the events that most threaten ITER's plasma-facing components — may be addressable by the same coil systems built for RWM control [9]. This reframes disruption avoidance as, in part, a magnetic-control problem rather than purely a plasma-physics one.

> **Theorem (informal, energy principle):** *An axisymmetric toroidal equilibrium with nested flux surfaces is ideally stable if and only if δW(ξ, ξ) ≥ 0 for all admissible displacements ξ, where δW is the Bernstein–Frieman–Kruskal–Kulsrud potential energy functional. Violation of this condition in the presence of a resistive wall defines the RWM-unstable region β_N^no-wall < β_N < β_N^ideal-wall in which active feedback can restore stability* [7][8].











##6 Limitations

The program described here faces limitations that are physical, technological, and methodological.

**Extrapolation to burning plasmas.** Every feedback result cited above was obtained in plasmas heated by external sources, with rotation, collisionality, and fast-particle populations different from those of a burning plasma. In ITER's Q = 10 scenario, alpha-particle heating will dominate, the rotation is predicted to be low (challenging RWM rotational stabilization), and the consequences of control failure — a disruption at 15 MA — are qualitatively more severe than in present devices. The models extrapolate, but they have never been tested in the regime they are meant to serve [1][2].

**Actuator and sensor limits.** Feedback authority is finite and expensive. In-vessel coils must survive neutron and heat fluxes that will destroy conventional copper coils in a reactor; superconducting PF coils are too slow for the fast loops; and magnetic sensors behind a breeding blanket will suffer radiation-induced degradation and drift. Control theory can optimize within these limits but cannot remove them.

**Disruption prediction and mitigation.** Reliable disruption *prediction* with sufficient warning time and low false-positive rates remains unsolved, and mitigation by massive gas injection deposits its own thermal and electromagnetic loads. A reactor cannot afford the disruption rates tolerated in present experiments [1][9].

**Model fidelity and certification.** Linear plasma-response models omit the profile evolution, ELMs, and MHD events that constitute the real disturbance environment; nonlinear MHD codes (JOREK, M3D-C1, NIMROD) capture more physics but are too slow for control design iteration, let alone real-time use. Learned controllers evade the modeling problem but introduce a certification problem: no stability proof accompanies an RL policy, and the regulatory framework for a nuclear-licensed fusion plant will demand guarantees that current learning-based methods cannot provide [4][6].











##7 Conclusion

Tokamak magnetic control has matured from an engineering afterthought into the discipline that defines the accessible operational space of fusion plasmas. The Grad–Shafranov equilibrium sets the stage; the ideal and resistive MHD stability theories, from the energy principle to the Troyon limit to error-field amplification, map the regions of that stage that are safe, wall-stabilized, or feedback-dependent; and the hierarchy of controllers — fast vertical stabilization, RWM feedback, NTM suppression, and shape control — is what allows a discharge to inhabit the high-performance regions at all [1][2][3][7][8].

Three conclusions emerge. First, *the wall and the feedback system are a single coupled stabilizer*: passive wall currents buy the milliseconds that active coils need, and neither alone suffices for reactor-relevant beta at low rotation. Second, *the binding constraints are actuator constraints* — power-supply voltage saturation for vertical control, coil response time for RWM feedback, ECCD alignment for NTM suppression — and progress in tokamak performance is increasingly progress in power electronics, coil technology, and real-time algorithms rather than in plasma physics alone. Third, *learning-based control has earned a place alongside classical synthesis*: the TCV results demonstrate capabilities — rapid reconfiguration to novel shapes, robustness to unmodeled disturbances — that the classical pipeline achieves only with great effort, even as certification and sim-to-real gaps remain open [4][6].

The path to fusion energy therefore runs through control rooms as much as through physics laboratories. ITER will be the first device in which every high-performance discharge is simultaneously a control experiment; STEP and subsequent power plants will demand control systems that are radiation-hardened, fault-tolerant, and certifiable. The theoretical foundations reviewed here — equilibrium, stability, and feedback as one coupled problem — provide the language in which those systems will be designed.
