# Conditional Entropy Modeling and Rate–Distortion Optimization in End-to-End Learned Video Compression: Scale Hyperpriors, Motion-Compensated Contexts, and Autoregressive Priors

## Abstract

Learned video compression has matured into end-to-end differentiable codecs rivaling H.265/HEVC and H.266/VVC. The enabling advances are *conditional entropy models* fusing scale-hyperprior side information, autoregressive spatial context, and learned temporal priors; *motion-compensated warping* in learned feature spaces; and *rate–distortion optimization* of the full pipeline via Lagrangian objectives with differentiable quantization relaxations. This thesis unifies these mechanisms mathematically. We formalize the conditional coding paradigm — coding the frame conditioned on a learned temporal context rather than an explicit pixel-domain residual — and prove it dominates residual coding information-theoretically. We analyze scale-hyperprior entropy models, prior-fusion estimators, the parallelization trade-offs of autoregressive contexts (checkerboard, channel-wise, and quadtree decompositions), and variable-rate mechanisms. We survey empirical BD-rate evidence on UVG, MCL-JCV, and HEVC sequences, and close with limitations: decoder complexity, cross-platform determinism, temporal error propagation, and prior generalization.

## 1. Introduction

For three decades, video compression was the province of hand-engineered standards. From MPEG-2 through H.264/AVC, H.265/HEVC, and H.266/VVC, each generation refined the same architecture: block-based hybrid coding — inter-frame prediction, transform coding of the residual, and context-adaptive arithmetic coding — tuned by experts over years of standardization [1][3]. This architecture is extraordinarily successful, yet carries an inherent limitation: every module is designed and optimized *locally*, and the end-to-end rate–distortion objective is never directly optimized. The motion estimator minimizes a surrogate (sum of absolute differences), the transform is fixed, and the entropy coder adapts to statistics it never influenced.

*Learned video compression* (LVC) rejects this decomposition. Encoder, entropy model, motion compensation, and decoder are *all* neural networks, trained end-to-end on the Lagrangian

$$ \mathcal{L} = \underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x} \sim p_{\mathbf{x}}}\left[-\log_2 p_{\hat{\mathbf{y}}}(\hat{\mathbf{y}})\right]}_{\text{rate } R} + \lambda \underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x} \sim p_{\mathbf{x}}}\left[d(\mathbf{x}, \hat{\mathbf{x}})\right]}_{\text{distortion } D}, \tag{1} $$

where $\hat{\mathbf{y}} = \lfloor f_{\text{enc}}(\mathbf{x}) \rceil$ is the quantized latent representation, $p_{\hat{\mathbf{y}}}$ is a learned entropy model, and $\hat{\mathbf{x}} = f_{\text{dec}}(\hat{\mathbf{y}})$ is the reconstruction [1][3]. Two facts dominate everything that follows: the entropy model upper-bounds the true rate (any misspecification is paid in bits), and a single amortized encoder must serve all inputs (the *amortization gap*). Everything — hyperpriors, autoregressive contexts, motion-compensated warping, conditional coding — is machinery for making the two terms of (1) simultaneously small.

The intellectual history is instructive. The first end-to-end framework, **DVC** (CVPR 2019), mirrored the classical hybrid pipeline with learned modules — optical-flow motion estimation, motion-vector coding, warping-based compensation, residual coding — all jointly optimized [3]. The decisive break came with **DCVC** (NeurIPS 2021), which abandoned residual coding for *conditional coding*: compressing the frame conditioned on a learned temporal context $\bar{\mathbf{x}}_t = f_{\text{context}}(\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{t-1})$ rather than on the difference $\mathbf{x}_t - \tilde{\mathbf{x}}_t$ [4]. This unlocked DCVC-TCM (multi-scale temporal context mining), DCVC-HEM (hybrid spatial-temporal entropy model, multi-granularity quantization), and DCVC-DC (diverse long-term contexts, quadtree entropy partitioning) — codecs that outperform H.266/VVC reference software on standard benchmarks [5][6].

This thesis is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews Shannon rate–distortion theory and variational image compression with the *scale hyperprior* [1][2]. Section 3 presents the end-to-end methodology. Section 4 analyzes conditional entropy models: hyperpriors for video latents, temporal priors with prior fusion, autoregressive parallelization, variable-rate mechanisms, and the conditional-vs-residual comparison. Section 5 presents empirical evidence and guarantees; Section 6, limitations; Section 7 concludes.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 Rate–distortion theory and the Lagrangian formulation

Shannon's rate–distortion theory characterizes the fundamental trade-off between the bitrate $R$ and the expected distortion $D$ of any lossy compression scheme. For a source $X$ with distribution $p_X$ and a distortion measure $d(\cdot, \cdot)$, the *rate–distortion function* is

$$ R(D) = \min_{p_{\hat{X}|X} : \mathbb{E}[d(X,\hat{X})] \le D} I(X; \hat{X}), \tag{2} $$

where $I(X; \hat{X})$ is the mutual information between the source and its reconstruction. The unconstrained Lagrangian form,

$$ \min_{p_{\hat{X}|X}} I(X; \hat{X}) + \lambda \, \mathbb{E}[d(X, \hat{X})], \tag{3} $$

parameterized by $\lambda > 0$, traces the convex hull of the achievable rate–distortion curve. Equation (1) is its neural realization: the transforms implement $p_{\hat{X}|X}$, the entropy model upper-bounds the mutual information via cross-entropy, and SGD searches for a point near the frontier. Two facts dominate everything that follows: the entropy model bounds the true rate (misspecification is paid in bits), and one amortized encoder must serve all inputs (the *amortization gap*).

### 2.2 Variational image compression and the scale hyperprior

All modern LVC is built on the variational image compression framework of Ballé et al. [1]. An analysis transform maps image $\mathbf{x}$ to latents $\mathbf{y} = g_a(\mathbf{x})$; quantization gives $\hat{\mathbf{y}} = \lfloor \mathbf{y} \rceil$; a synthesis transform reconstructs $\hat{\mathbf{x}} = g_s(\hat{\mathbf{y}})$. The **scale hyperprior** adds a second autoencoder on the latents,

$$ \mathbf{z} = h_a(\mathbf{y}), \qquad \hat{\mathbf{z}} = \lfloor \mathbf{z} \rceil, \qquad \boldsymbol{\sigma} = h_s(\hat{\mathbf{z}}), \tag{4} $$

where the hyper-latent $\hat{\mathbf{z}}$ is transmitted as *side information* under a nonparametric fully-factorized density model, and the scale parameters $\boldsymbol{\sigma} = h_s(\hat{\mathbf{z}})$ parameterize a conditional Gaussian model for the primary latents:

$$ p_{\hat{\mathbf{y}} \mid \hat{\mathbf{z}}}(\hat{\mathbf{y}} \mid \hat{\mathbf{z}}) = \prod_i \big( \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma_i^2) * \mathcal{U}(-\tfrac{1}{2}, \tfrac{1}{2}) \big)(\hat{y}_i). \tag{5} $$

The convolution with the unit uniform distribution is a mathematical necessity: it is the exact density of $\hat{y}_i = \lfloor y_i \rceil$ when $y_i \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma_i^2)$. The total rate decomposes cleanly into latent rate plus side-information rate. The hyperprior works because natural images are non-stationary: it adapts the entropy model to local statistics — high-variance textures get large $\sigma_i$, smooth regions small $\sigma_i$ — and the side-information overhead is more than repaid. This pattern, *spending bits on side information to save more bits on the primary signal*, recurs at every level of LVC design.

Minnen et al. [2] extended this framework by combining the hierarchical (hyperprior) model with an **autoregressive context model**. The entropy parameters become

$$ \boldsymbol{\mu}, \boldsymbol{\sigma} = f_{\text{params}}\big(h_s(\hat{\mathbf{z}}),\, C(\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{<i})\big), \tag{7} $$

where $C(\cdot)$ is a masked convolution that sees only *previously decoded* spatial neighbors $\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{<i}$ (in raster-scan order), upgrading the model from a zero-mean scale hyperprior to a *mean-and-scale* conditional Gaussian. This joint autoregressive–hierarchical prior substantially improves rate–distortion performance — at the cost of *serial decoding*, since each latent element must wait for its predecessors. The tension between model expressiveness and decoding parallelism is one of the field's central engineering trade-offs, and Section 4.3 analyzes the resolutions the community has developed, including channel-wise conditioning and checkerboard parallelization.

> **Theorem (Hyperprior as adaptive side information).** *Let $\hat{\mathbf{y}}$ be quantized latents with true marginal $p$. Let $p_{\hat{\mathbf{z}}}$ be any factorized hyperprior and $p_{\hat{\mathbf{y}}\mid\hat{\mathbf{z}}}$ any conditional model of the form (5). Then the achievable rate $R = H(p, p_{\hat{\mathbf{y}}\mid\hat{\mathbf{z}}}) + H(p_{\hat{\mathbf{z}}}^{\text{true}}, p_{\hat{\mathbf{z}}})$ is minimized when the hyper-analysis $h_a$ extracts precisely the sufficient statistics of the local scale of $\mathbf{y}$. In particular, for any fixed-capacity hyper-network, the optimal $h_a$ is an estimator of local variance — which is why trained hyper-encoders empirically converge to local-energy detectors.*

## 3. Methodology

### 3.1 End-to-end formulation and the differentiable rate–distortion objective

A learned video codec processes frames $\mathbf{x}_{1:T}$, partitioned into *intra* (I) frames coded independently and *inter* (P) frames coded against previously decoded references. For a P-frame $\mathbf{x}_t$ with reference $\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{t-1}$, the classical-architecture LVC (DVC [3]) chains four learned modules: an optical-flow network estimates motion $\mathbf{v}_t$; a motion autoencoder compresses it to $\hat{\mathbf{m}}_t$; differentiable bilinear warping produces the prediction $\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_t = \text{warp}(\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{t-1}, \hat{\mathbf{v}}_t)$; and a residual autoencoder compresses $\mathbf{r}_t = \mathbf{x}_t - \tilde{\mathbf{x}}_t$ to $\hat{\mathbf{y}}_t$, yielding $\hat{\mathbf{x}}_t = \tilde{\mathbf{x}}_t + f_{\text{dec}}^r(\hat{\mathbf{y}}_t)$.

The training objective is the multi-term Lagrangian

$$ \mathcal{L} = R_m + R_r + \lambda \, d(\mathbf{x}_t, \hat{\mathbf{x}}_t), \tag{8} $$

where $R_m = \mathbb{E}[-\log_2 p_{\hat{\mathbf{m}}}(\hat{\mathbf{m}}_t)]$ and $R_r = \mathbb{E}[-\log_2 p_{\hat{\mathbf{y}}\mid\hat{\mathbf{z}}}(\hat{\mathbf{y}}_t\mid\hat{\mathbf{z}}_t)]$ are the motion and residual rates. Every operator — including the warping — is differentiable, so gradients flow from the final distortion through the residual decoder, the compensation network, and the flow estimator. The flow network thus learns the field that *minimizes the rate–distortion Lagrangian*, which may differ from photometrically correct motion in occluded or textureless regions, where the cheapest description is not the truest one.

### 3.2 Motion estimation, compression, and motion-compensated warping

Motion is the dominant source of temporal redundancy, and how a codec *represents* it sets the efficiency ceiling. The standard pipeline initializes an optical-flow network (PWC-Net/SPyNet style) and fine-tunes it under the rate–distortion objective; the flow field is itself compressed by a motion autoencoder with a hyperprior, since motion is smooth almost everywhere with discontinuities at object boundaries [3].

Recent architectures generalize warping in two directions. **Feature-space warping** warps *learned features* $\hat{\mathbf{F}}_{t-1}$ rather than pixels, at multiple scales — the *temporal context mining* of DCVC-TCM [5]. **Multi-offset compensation** (DCVC-DC [6]) warps features at several motion-vector offsets (*offset diversity*), handling occlusions and transparencies better than a single deterministic warp.

A subtle but critical point: the motion field is *not* required to be a physically correct optical flow. Under the Lagrangian (8), the optimal $\hat{\mathbf{v}}_t$ is the field that minimizes the *sum* of motion rate and residual rate plus distortion. In smooth regions, the network learns to spend almost no bits on motion (near-zero flow); at occlusion boundaries, it learns flow fields that minimize the *residual's* entropy rather than matching photometric correspondences. This is a feature, not a bug — it is precisely what "end-to-end" means — but it implies that the motion representation is *task-optimal* rather than *interpretable*, which complicates debugging and cross-codec analysis.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Scale-hyperprior entropy models for video latents

When the scale hyperprior is transplanted from images to video, every latent stream — motion latents $\hat{\mathbf{m}}_t$, residual/contextual latents $\hat{\mathbf{y}}_t$ — receives its own hyperprior hierarchy. Two refinements matter. First, **heavy-tailed conditionals**: video residuals are empirically Laplacian, so DCVC adopts $\mathcal{L}(\mu_{t,i}, \sigma_{t,i})$ conditionals (or Gaussian mixtures), matching the model family to the marginal and shrinking the cross-entropy gap [4]. Second, **feature-level residuals** [7]: computing the residual between *learned features* rather than pixels yields smoother, more compressible signals, since the feature extractor has already factored out high-frequency nuisance variation.

### 4.2 Conditional entropy modeling with temporal priors and prior fusion

The centerpiece of modern LVC entropy modeling is the **conditional entropy model** introduced by DCVC [4]. For the contextual latents $\hat{\mathbf{y}}_t$ of frame $t$, the model fuses three sources of prior information:

1. **Hierarchical prior** $f_{\text{hpd}}(\hat{\mathbf{z}}_t)$ — the hyperprior decoder output, capturing side information not predictable from neighbors.
2. **Spatial prior** $f_{\text{ar}}(\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{t,<i})$ — the autoregressive context from already-decoded spatial neighbors.
3. **Temporal prior** $f_{\text{tpe}}(\bar{\mathbf{x}}_t)$ — a *temporal prior encoder* that extracts predictive features from the learned temporal context $\bar{\mathbf{x}}_t$.

A **prior fusion network** $f_{\text{pf}}$ combines them into the parameters of the conditional distribution:

$$ \mu_{t,i},\, \sigma_{t,i} = f_{\text{pf}}\!\Big(f_{\text{hpd}}(\hat{\mathbf{z}}_t),\; f_{\text{ar}}(\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{t,<i}),\; f_{\text{tpe}}(\bar{\mathbf{x}}_t)\Big), \tag{10} $$

$$ p_{\hat{\mathbf{y}}_t}(\hat{\mathbf{y}}_t \mid \bar{\mathbf{x}}_t, \hat{\mathbf{z}}_t) = \prod_i \big( \mathcal{L}(\mu_{t,i}, \sigma_{t,i}) * \mathcal{U}(-\tfrac12, \tfrac12) \big)(\hat{y}_{t,i}). \tag{11} $$

> **Theorem (Conditional coding dominates residual coding).** *Let $\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_t = g(\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{t-1})$ be any pixel-domain predictor and let $\bar{\mathbf{x}}_t = f(\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{t-1})$ be a learned context with $\dim(\bar{\mathbf{x}}_t) \ge \dim(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_t)$. Let $R_{\text{res}}$ be the optimal rate of coding the residual $\mathbf{r}_t = \mathbf{x}_t - \tilde{\mathbf{x}}_t$ and $R_{\text{cond}}$ the optimal rate of coding $\mathbf{x}_t$ conditioned on $\bar{\mathbf{x}}_t$ at equal distortion. Then $R_{\text{cond}} \le R_{\text{res}}$, because residual coding is the special case of conditional coding with $f_{\text{enc}}(\mathbf{x}_t \mid \bar{\mathbf{x}}_t) = f_{\text{enc}}^r(\mathbf{x}_t - \tilde{\mathbf{x}}_t)$ and a context restricted to the prediction. The inequality is strict whenever $\bar{\mathbf{x}}_t$ contains information about $\mathbf{x}_t$ that is destroyed by subtraction — e.g., illumination changes, where the residual has high energy but the context plus a global gain parameter predicts the frame nearly perfectly.*

This theorem is the formal justification for the field's migration from DVC-style residual coding [3] to DCVC-style conditional coding [4]: the conditional formulation *contains* the residual formulation as a special case, so a sufficiently expressive conditional codec can always match and typically beat its residual counterpart.

### 4.3 Autoregressive context models and the parallelization frontier

The spatial prior $f_{\text{ar}}(\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{t,<i})$ in (10) is the most expensive term in the entropy model. A full raster-scan autoregressive model requires $H \times W$ sequential network evaluations per latent tensor — thousands of serial steps that no GPU can parallelize, since each step depends on all previous ones. For video, where latency budgets are measured in tens of milliseconds per frame, this is prohibitive. The community has developed a spectrum of approximations, each trading modeling power for parallelism:

| Context model | Decoding steps | Parallelism | Rate cost vs. full AR |
|---|---|---|---|
| Full raster-scan AR [2] | $H \cdot W$ | None (serial) | Baseline (best) |
| Channel-wise AR (slices) | # slices ($\approx$ 4–10) | Within-slice parallel | Small ($\approx$ 1–3%) |
| Checkerboard (2-step) | 2 | Half the latents per step | Moderate ($\approx$ 2–5%) |
| No spatial prior (hyperprior + temporal only) | 1 | Fully parallel | Larger ($\approx$ 5–10%) |

*Table 1: The parallelization spectrum for autoregressive spatial context (rate costs are illustrative typical values).*

The **checkerboard** decomposition partitions the latent grid into two interleaved sets. Step one codes the "black" latents conditioned only on hyperprior and temporal priors; step two codes the "white" latents conditioned additionally on the decoded black neighbors. Each step is fully parallel, recovering most spatial correlation. DCVC-HEM [5] extends this to a **hybrid spatial-temporal** model: channel splits, two-step checkerboard coding conditioned on multi-scale temporal contexts, the hyperprior, and previously decoded latents $\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{t-1}$. DCVC-DC [6] goes further with a **quadtree** partition — four interleaved groups for finer spatial conditioning at constant step count.

The channel-wise approach splits latents into $S$ slices, conditioning slice $s$ on slices $<s$, capturing *cross-channel* correlations in only $S$ serial steps. In practice, winners combine both: channel slices *within* checkerboard steps — a 2-D factorization of the dependency structure.

The key insight is that *autoregressivity is a modeling choice, not a requirement*. The entropy model needs $p(\hat{y}_i \mid \text{context}_i)$ to be sharp; the art lies in choosing dependency structures that are parallelizable yet capture the strongest correlations first — temporal, then cross-channel, then spatial-neighbor. The trajectory from full AR [2] through checkerboard to quadtree [6] is the story of the field learning which dependencies are worth serial steps for — and the answer, increasingly, is *temporal* dependencies, which are both stronger and parallelizable.

### 4.4 Rate–distortion optimization, quantization relaxations, and variable rates

Training requires differentiating through quantization $\lfloor \cdot \rceil$, which has zero gradient almost everywhere. Two relaxations dominate: **uniform-noise** substitution ($\tilde{\mathbf{y}} = \mathbf{y} + \mathcal{U}(-\frac12,\frac12)$ in training, exact rounding at test) and the **straight-through estimator** (STE), which rounds forward but passes gradients through unchanged. The standard training loop is:

```python
for x_t, x_hat_prev in dataloader:          # frames and decoded references
    y, z = enc(x_t, ctx(x_hat_prev))        # conditional encoder + hyperprior
    y_tilde = y + torch.rand_like(y) - 0.5  # uniform-noise quantization
    mu, sigma = entropy_params(z, temporal_prior(x_hat_prev))
    rate = -log2_laplace_cdf(y_tilde, mu, sigma).sum() / num_pixels
    x_hat = dec(ste_round(y), ctx(x_hat_prev))
    loss = rate + lmbda * mse(x_t, x_hat)   # Lagrangian (1)
    loss.backward(); optimizer.step()
```

A single model must also serve *multiple rate points*. The classical approach trains one network per $\lambda$; modern codecs use **conditional (FiLM-style) modulation** by a $\lambda$ embedding, or **multi-granularity quantization** as in DCVC-HEM [5]: a global step size from user input, a learned channel-wise step, and a content-adaptive spatial-channel-wise step — letting one network span the full rate–distortion curve.

### 4.5 Why conditional coding wins: the information-theoretic view

Residual coding transmits $\mathbf{r}_t = \mathbf{x}_t - \tilde{\mathbf{x}}_t$; its rate is lower-bounded by $H(\mathbf{r}_t)$, and the subtraction is *lossy with respect to information*: once $\mathbf{x}_t - \tilde{\mathbf{x}}_t$ is formed, any structure shared nonlinearly between frame and prediction is destroyed. Conditional coding transmits $\mathbf{x}_t$ given $\bar{\mathbf{x}}_t$ with rate lower-bounded by $H(\mathbf{x}_t \mid \bar{\mathbf{x}}_t) \le H(\mathbf{r}_t)$, and the encoder/decoder networks are free to discover arbitrary (non-subtractive) relationships. The type-level distinction is crisp:

```haskell
-- Residual coding: prediction is subtracted, information destroyed
residualCode :: Frame -> Prediction -> (Bits, Frame)
residualCode x p = let r = x ^-^ p in (entropyCode r, p ^+^ decode r)

-- Conditional coding: prediction is side information, never subtracted
conditionalCode :: Frame -> Context -> (Bits, Frame)
conditionalCode x c = let y = enc x c in (entropyCode y (prior c), dec y c)
```

```rust
// Motion-compensated warping: the differentiable core of temporal prediction
fn warp(ref_frame: &Tensor, flow: &Tensor) -> Tensor {
    // bilinear sampling; gradients flow to both frame and flow
    grid_sample(ref_frame, identity_grid() + flow, Bilinear, BorderPad)
}
```

> **Proposition (Sufficiency of learned context).** *If $\bar{\mathbf{x}}_t$ is a sufficient statistic of $\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{t-1}$ for $\mathbf{x}_t$ — i.e., $I(\mathbf{x}_t; \hat{\mathbf{x}}_{t-1} \mid \bar{\mathbf{x}}_t) = 0$ — then $H(\mathbf{x}_t \mid \bar{\mathbf{x}}_t) = H(\mathbf{x}_t \mid \hat{\mathbf{x}}_{t-1})$, the information-theoretic optimum for single-reference coding. The context network's training objective is therefore, implicitly, to* learn a sufficient statistic *of the reference frame — a far richer target than pixel prediction.*

## 5. Empirical Results and Theoretical Guarantees

The theory above is borne out by measurement. Rate–distortion performance is compared via **BD-rate** (Bjøntegaard delta rate): the average percentage bitrate difference between two codecs' R–D curves, negative meaning savings. Representative results reported in the literature:

| Codec | Anchor | Dataset | BD-rate (PSNR) |
|---|---|---|---|
| DVC [3] | x265 (very slow) | UVG / HEVC-B | ≈ −5 to −9% |
| DCVC [4] | x265 (very slow) | MCL-JCV / UVG / HEVC-B | −23.9% / −25.3% / −26.0% |
| DCVC-TCM [5] | VTM (H.266) | UVG | competitive, ~2× faster decode than DCVC |
| DCVC-HEM [5] | VTM (H.266) | UVG / MCL-JCV | consistent gains via hybrid entropy model |
| DCVC-DC [6] | x265 / VTM-17.0 | UVG / MCL-JCV / HEVC | −38.5% (RGB) / −7.1% vs VTM |

*Table 2: Illustrative BD-rate summary; exact figures vary with test conditions. DCVC's jump over DVC isolates the value of conditional coding; DCVC-DC's lead over VTM marks learned codecs overtaking the latest standard.*

Beyond benchmarks, two formal guarantees matter. First, the **cross-entropy bound**: for any entropy model, the operational rate satisfies $R \le \mathbb{E}[-\log_2 p_{\text{model}}(\hat{\mathbf{y}})]$, so every improvement to the conditional model is a *certified* rate reduction, no empirical luck required. Second, the **dominance theorem** of Section 4.2 is constructive: initializing a conditional codec's encoder as $f_{\text{enc}}(\mathbf{x}_t \mid \bar{\mathbf{x}}_t) = f_{\text{enc}}^r(\mathbf{x}_t - \tilde{\mathbf{x}}_t)$ recovers residual coding exactly, so gradient descent from a residual-coding initialization can only improve the Lagrangian — conditional coding is a strict generalization, never a gamble.

## 6. Limitations

Four limitations temper the optimism. **(1) Decoder complexity.** Even with checkerboard parallelization, learned decoders require hundreds of GMACs per 1080p frame (DCVC: ~2268 GMACs, ~857 ms/frame on a P40 GPU [4]) — orders of magnitude beyond real-time on edge hardware, and the autoregressive components that remain resist acceleration. **(2) Cross-platform determinism.** Entropy coding demands *bit-exact* agreement between encoder and decoder probability estimates; floating-point nondeterminism across GPUs can cause catastrophic error propagation, forcing integerized or quantized inference paths that complicate deployment. **(3) Temporal error propagation.** P-frames condition on *decoded* references, so quantization errors compound along the prediction chain; learned codecs lack the mature reference-picture management (long-term references, adaptive GOP structures) of classical standards, and hierarchical quality patterns (as in DCVC-DC [6]) are only a partial remedy. **(4) Generalization of learned priors.** Entropy models are trained on finite video corpora (e.g., Vimeo-90K); out-of-distribution content — screen content, animation, medical video — can inflate rates unpredictably, whereas classical codecs' hand-designed contexts degrade gracefully. The open-source OpenDCVCs effort [5] is beginning to make these failure modes reproducible and measurable — the precondition for fixing them.

## 7. Conclusion

Learned video compression has converged on a coherent technical doctrine: *model the conditional distribution of quantized latents as accurately as possible, condition on everything the decoder will know, and optimize the entire pipeline against the rate–distortion Lagrangian*. The scale hyperprior [1] taught the field to buy side information; autoregressive priors [2] taught it the price of serialism; DVC [3] proved end-to-end optimization viable; and the DCVC lineage [4][5][6] demonstrated that conditional coding with fused temporal, spatial, and hierarchical priors dominates residual coding both in theory and on the BD-rate ledger — to the point of surpassing H.266/VVC. The remaining challenges are engineering as much as science: real-time decoders, deterministic arithmetic, robust temporal structures, and priors that generalize. If the last decade replaced hand-designed modules with learned ones, the next is about making the learned whole deployable.
