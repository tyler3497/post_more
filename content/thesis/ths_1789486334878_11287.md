# Distributed Graph Algorithms in the LOCAL and CONGEST Models: Lower Bounds, LCL Problems, and Derandomization

## Abstract

We survey the modern theory of distributed graph algorithms in the synchronous message-passing setting, centered on the LOCAL model, where messages are unbounded, and the CONGEST model, where each message is restricted to O(log n) bits. We trace the lineage of lower bounds from Linial's indistinguishability arguments and neighborhood-graph chromatic numbers through round elimination, fooling views, and communication-complexity embeddings, to recent non-signaling and Supported LOCAL strengthenings. We then present the complexity classification of locally checkable labeling (LCL) problems on bounded-degree graphs, including the gap theorems of Chang and Pettie and the central role of the distributed Lovász Local Lemma as a universal sublogarithmic problem. Finally, we describe how derandomization transformed the landscape: from the decades-old 2^{O(sqrt(log n))} network decomposition of Panconesi and Srinivasan to the polylogarithmic decompositions of Rozhoň and Ghaffari, and the near-optimal decompositions and relaxed clusterings of recent years, which pushed deterministic MIS, coloring, and matching to within polylogarithmic factors of their lower bounds. We close with the most important open gaps that remain.

## 1 Models of Distributed Computation

In the synchronous message-passing setting, the communication network is modeled as an n-node graph G = (V, E), where each node is an independent processor with a unique O(log n)-bit identifier, unlimited local computation, and no shared clock beyond synchronous rounds: in each round, every node sends a message to each neighbor, receives the incoming messages, and updates its state. Two models dominate the theory. In the **LOCAL** model, introduced by Linial, messages are of unbounded size [1]. In the **CONGEST** model, each message is restricted to O(log n) bits per edge per round [2]. The LOCAL model is therefore a relaxation of CONGEST: every CONGEST algorithm is a LOCAL algorithm, but not conversely. Both models can be unified as Congest(B) with B bits of bandwidth, where LOCAL corresponds to Congest(infinity) and CONGEST to Congest(log n) [7].

The reason this distinction matters is that LOCAL isolates the *locality* of a problem from its *communication* cost. Any T-round algorithm in either model has the property that the output of a node depends only on its T-neighborhood: the topology, identifiers, and inputs within distance T. Thus LOCAL round complexity measures how far information must travel, whereas CONGEST round complexity measures both distance and the total volume of information that must cross each edge. This separation is sharp: triangle detection can be solved in a single round in LOCAL, since every node can see its full 1-neighborhood, yet it provably requires polynomially many rounds in CONGEST, because the edges of a triangle are witnessed by three different endpoints and the evidence must be assembled across bandwidth-limited edges [7].

Variants of the LOCAL model probe which ingredients of the model are truly essential for lower bounds. In the *weak* LOCAL model, nodes have no identifiers and instead begin with a valid initial coloring [8]. In the *Supported* LOCAL model, the communication graph is known to all nodes in advance, and the input graph on which the problem must be solved is guaranteed to be a subgraph of it; lower bounds in this stronger model are correspondingly more robust [9]. In the *non-signaling* model, one considers output distributions rather than deterministic or randomized algorithms, strictly subsuming LOCAL, quantum-LOCAL, and bounded-dependence settings [10]. A further refinement is the *volume* model, in which complexity is measured by the number of nodes a node must inspect rather than by the radius it must see, yielding a different and sometimes finer complexity theory [10].

## 2 Indistinguishability and the Neighborhood Graph

The foundational lower-bound technique of the field is *indistinguishability*: a node running for T rounds cannot distinguish two global inputs that agree on its T-neighborhood, so any correct algorithm must assign outputs that are simultaneously correct for every input consistent with the view the node sees. Linial's seminal lower bound shows that 3-coloring an n-node ring requires Omega(log^* n) rounds, even deterministically, and the bound is tight: Cole and Vishkin's algorithm achieves O(log^* n) [1]. The proof constructs, from the assumption of a fast algorithm, a coloring of the *neighborhood graph* whose vertices are possible labeled T-neighborhoods and whose edges join neighborhoods that can belong to adjacent nodes in some feasible input. If the algorithm runs in T rounds, its output function is a proper coloring of the neighborhood graph; showing that this graph has chromatic number larger than the palette available then refutes the assumed speed. The same framework, applied to d-regular trees, shows that any algorithm running in at most 2r/3 rounds on trees of radius r needs Omega(sqrt(d)) colors [1].

For symmetry-breaking problems this technique is essentially complete: all classic symmetry-breaking tasks, including Delta-coloring of bounded-degree graphs, have deterministic LOCAL complexity Theta(log^* n) [3]. The deeper lower bounds concern problems whose complexity sits above log^* n. Kuhn, Moscibroda, and Wattenhofer proved that approximating vertex cover, dominating set, matching, and maximal independent set requires Omega(min(log Delta / log log Delta, sqrt(log n / log log n))) rounds in LOCAL, where Delta is the maximum degree [2]. Their construction is a family of clustered graphs in which, after k rounds, two adjacent edges see exactly the same topology: both are equally qualified to join the matching, yet choosing wrong yields a globally poor approximation. These bounds depend on Delta and are weak when Delta is small, but they established the paradigm of *cluster-graph* lower-bound families that much later work refines [2, 9].

> **Theorem (Linial [1]):** Any deterministic distributed algorithm that properly 3-colors every n-node directed path requires at least log^* n / 2 - 2 rounds.

## 3 LCL Problems and the Complexity Landscape

The class of *locally checkable labeling* (LCL) problems, introduced by Naor and Stockmeyer, captures essentially all classic LOCAL graph problems: vertex and edge coloring with a fixed number of colors, maximal independent set, maximal matching, and many others [3, 10]. Formally, an LCL is specified by finite input and output label sets and a radius c such that a global output is valid if and only if it is valid on the c-neighborhood of every node; on bounded-degree graphs this gives every LCL a finite description [10].

In a landmark result, Chang and Pettie mapped the *complexity landscape* of LCLs on bounded-degree graphs, proving that the set of possible deterministic complexities has surprising gaps. There is no LCL with deterministic complexity in the range omega(log^* n) to o(log n), and no LCL with randomized complexity in the range omega(log^* n) to o(log log n). Between Omega(log n) and O(n) the landscape is dense: every complexity in that range is realized by some LCL [3]. On bounded-degree trees the picture is even sparser: the only possible deterministic complexities are Theta(1), Theta(log^* n), Theta(log n), and Theta(n^{1/k}) for integers k >= 1 [3].

Randomness adds its own structure. A central discovery is that the *distributed Lovász Local Lemma* (LLL) acts as a universal problem for the sublogarithmic regime: any LCL admitting an o(log n)-round randomized algorithm can be sped up to T_LLL(n) rounds, where T_LLL(n) is the randomized complexity of a relaxed LLL on bounded-degree graphs [8]. The best known upper bound on T_LLL(n) is 2^{O(sqrt(log log n))}, against a lower bound of Omega(log log n), so every such LCL inherits an automatic speedup to 2^{O(sqrt(log log n))} rounds [8]. There remain exponential separations between randomized and deterministic complexities for specific problems: Delta-coloring degree-Delta trees and sinkless orientation have randomized complexity Theta(log log n) against deterministic complexity Theta(log n) [3]. Whether the deterministic landscape admits further gaps above Omega(log n) is open; for instance, locally optimal cut is an LCL on 3-regular graphs for which no o(n)-round algorithm is known at all [3].

The modern workhorse for proving LCL lower bounds is *round elimination*, which derives from a problem Pi a sequence of relaxed problems Pi_1, Pi_2, ..., each solvable one round faster than the previous, so that a lower bound on the last problem in the sequence propagates back to Pi [9]. Round elimination powered essentially every LOCAL lower bound of the past decade, including the Omega(log n) lower bounds for maximal matching and maximal independent set [5]. A recent framework extends round elimination to the Supported LOCAL model, reducing a lower bound for Pi to the graph-theoretic task of proving that no solution exists for a mechanically derived problem Pi' on a suitable graph; this recovers the major LOCAL lower bounds in the stronger model and yields a purely deterministic form of round elimination [9].

## 4 Lower-Bound Techniques Under Congestion

### 4.1 Fooling views

Fooling views adapt the neighborhood-graph technique to Congest(B) [7]. Instead of neighborhoods as atomic objects, the construction considers *views*: the transcripts of bounded-bandwidth communication a node can observe. A 2-round coloring algorithm is still a function from views to colors, but now two views that could belong to adjacent nodes in *some* input under the bandwidth constraint must receive different colors. The technique recovers the classical locality lower bounds as the special case of unbounded bandwidth, but it cannot separate LOCAL from Congest(B), because it never exploits the limited volume of communication [7].

### 4.2 Communication-complexity embeddings

The most successful method for proving CONGEST lower bounds reduces from two-party communication complexity. One embeds an instance of a hard communication problem, such as set disjointness on K-bit inputs, into a graph G_{x,y} whose edge set encodes the private inputs x and y of Alice and Bob. Alice and Bob then simulate a distributed algorithm on G_{x,y}: all communication across the cut between Alice's side and Bob's side must travel over the cut edges, each carrying only O(log n) bits per round. A T-round CONGEST algorithm therefore yields a two-party protocol with O(T times cut size times log n) bits of communication, so the known Omega(K) lower bound for set disjointness implies a round lower bound of Omega(K / (cut size times log n)). Applying this with K = Theta(n^2 / c^2) and a cut of O(c log n) edges shows that distinguishing graphs of chromatic number at most 3c from those of chromatic number at least 4c requires Omega(n^2 / (c^3 log^2 n)) rounds, and hence that any constant-factor approximation of the chromatic number needs Omega(n^2 / log^2 n) rounds in CONGEST [2]. The same embedding paradigm gives near-linear lower bounds for odd-cycle detection and Omega(sqrt(n) / log n) bounds for even-cycle detection [7].

### 4.3 The Supported LOCAL model

In the Supported LOCAL model the communication graph is known a priori and the input graph is a subgraph of it [9]. Because knowledge of the support removes the need to discover the topology, lower bounds here are stronger and closer to CONGEST: they must come from the problem's inherent locality rather than from the cost of learning the graph. The round-elimination framework of [9] yields tight Supported LOCAL lower bounds for maximal matching, maximal independent set, ruling sets, and arbdefective colorings, mirroring the LOCAL bounds and suggesting that the two models coincide for the classical LCL problems up to the usual slack.

### 4.4 Non-signaling and quantum lower bounds

The non-signaling model considers arbitrary output distributions whose marginals on any subset of nodes depend only on the corresponding inputs, a condition satisfied by every LOCAL, randomized-LOCAL, and quantum-LOCAL algorithm [10]. Lower bounds here automatically transfer to all of these models. Recently, every O(log Delta)-approximate non-signaling distribution for dominating set was shown to require locality Omega(log n / (Delta times polylog log Delta)), and a weaker approximation still forces Omega(log n / log Delta) locality; combined with the Kuhn-Moscibroda-Wattenhofer upper bound, this yields a degree-independent Omega(sqrt(log n / log log n)) quantum-LOCAL lower bound, removing the weak degree-dependent term from the classical result [10]. This is the first setting in which quantum distributed algorithms are provably limited by a bound that does not follow from any classical randomized bound.

### 4.5 The local information cost

A complementary viewpoint quantifies, in bits, how much information nodes must learn. The *local information cost* (LIC) of a problem is a lower bound on the communication complexity of any distributed algorithm: in the CONGEST KT_1 model, where nodes initially know their neighbors' identifiers, solving a problem P in tau rounds with error at most gamma requires Omega(LIC_gamma(P) / (log tau log n)) bits [2]. Applying this to routing-table construction gives an Omega(n^2 / log^2 n)-bit lower bound for all-pairs-shortest-paths routing in polynomial time, and to spanner construction gives a tilde-Omega(n^{1 + 1/(2t)} / t^2)-bit bound for stretch-(2t-1) spanners [2]. These were the first general time-communication trade-offs in CONGEST.

## 5 Derandomization: Network Decomposition and Shattering

For decades, the central open question of the area, posed by Linial, was the deterministic complexity of maximal independent set. The difficulty is symmetry: randomized algorithms break symmetry with coin flips, and derandomizing them seemed to require global coordination. The key tool is the *network decomposition*: a partition of the vertices into clusters of small (polylogarithmic) diameter, colored with few colors so that same-colored clusters are non-adjacent. Given such a decomposition, a deterministic algorithm can process color classes one at a time, gathering each cluster's full topology at a leader, which then simulates the randomized algorithm locally with fresh private coins and broadcasts the result; correctness follows because same-colored clusters are independent [4].

The catch was computing the decomposition itself deterministically. Panconesi and Srinivasan's celebrated 1992 algorithm needed 2^{O(sqrt(log n))} rounds, and this stood unimproved for nearly three decades, becoming the bottleneck for every deterministic algorithm built on top of it [4]. The breakthrough of Rozhoň and Ghaffari gave the first polylogarithmic-time deterministic network decomposition, with O(log^5 n) rounds and O(log n)-bit messages, and with it a general derandomization theorem: combined with the conditional-expectation derandomization of Ghaffari, Kuhn, and Harris, it shows that P-RLOCAL equals P-LOCAL, meaning that for problems checkable in polylogarithmic time, any polylogarithmic-time randomized algorithm can be derandomized with only polylogarithmic overhead [4]. Informally, in the standard interpretation of efficiency as polylogarithmic time, distributed algorithms do not need randomness.

Since then the parameters have been driven down aggressively. The decomposition was improved to O(log^5 n) rounds with small messages and made identifier-independent so that it works inside the shattering framework in CONGEST [6]. Chang and Ghaffari gave the first strong-diameter decomposition with small messages [6]. Most recently, near-optimal decompositions with O(log^2 n) rounds, O(log n) diameter, and O(log n) colors were achieved, which is essentially optimal since even using an ideal decomposition costs the product of diameter and color count [6]. The same work gives an O(log log n) ruling set in O(log n) rounds, nearly matching the Omega(log n) lower bound, and these tools propagate through the *shattering* technique: a fast randomized phase reduces the problem to small connected components of undecided nodes, on which a deterministic algorithm then runs, so improvements in deterministic complexity automatically improve randomized complexity [5].

The flagship application is maximal independent set. Combining a relaxed network decomposition, in which each node is allowed a few neighboring clusters of the same color, with local rounding of fractional solutions, Ghaffari and Grunau obtained a deterministic MIS algorithm in tilde-O(log^2 n) rounds, down from the previous Theta(log^3 n), with matching improvements for maximal matching, Delta+1 vertex coloring, and (2Delta-1) edge coloring, and a new randomized Delta+1 coloring bound of tilde-O(log^2 log n) via shattering [5]. A further advance broke the log-squared barrier entirely, reaching approximately O(log^{5/3} n) rounds for deterministic MIS by methods that go beyond network decomposition [6]. Against the tilde-Omega(log n) lower bound of Balliu et al. [5], deterministic MIS is now within a polylogarithmic factor of optimal, a striking resolution of Linial's question.

```haskell
-- Schematic: derandomization via network decomposition
-- clusters :: [(Color, [Cluster])], each cluster has diameter D
derandomize :: Decomposition -> RandAlgorithm -> DetAlgorithm
derandomize decomp rand = forM_ (colors decomp) $ \c -> do
  -- same-colored clusters are non-adjacent: process in parallel
  forM_ (clustersOfColor decomp c) $ \cluster -> do
    topo <- gather cluster        -- leader learns the cluster topology
    let out = simulate rand topo  -- fresh private coins, no communication
    broadcast cluster out
```

## 6 Limitations

Despite this progress, fundamental gaps persist. The deterministic complexity of MIS sits between tilde-Omega(log n) and O(log^{5/3} n), and it is unknown whether the lower bound or the upper bound is closer to the truth. The randomized complexity of the distributed Lovász Local Lemma lies between Omega(log log n) and 2^{O(sqrt(log log n))}, and closing this gap would automatically speed up every sublogarithmic LCL algorithm [8]. For CONGEST, the landscape is far less complete than for LOCAL: most CONGEST lower bounds apply to specific problems via bespoke communication-complexity embeddings, and there is no general classification of CONGEST complexities analogous to the LCL landscape. The Supported LOCAL model may be the right intermediate setting in which to develop such a theory [9]. Finally, the derandomization theorems apply to problems checkable in polylogarithmic time; whether P-RLOCAL equals P-LOCAL holds under weaker checkability assumptions, and whether randomness helps for problems outside this class, remain open.

## 7 Conclusion

The theory of distributed graph algorithms has matured from isolated lower bounds into a structured complexity theory. The LOCAL model is now well understood for LCL problems: gap theorems delimit the possible complexities, round elimination generates tight lower bounds, and the Lovász Local Lemma anchors the randomized sublogarithmic regime. The CONGEST model retains its mysteries, with communication-complexity embeddings and fooling views as the principal tools and non-signaling arguments as the newest frontier. On the algorithmic side, the derandomization program that began with network decomposition has nearly closed the book on deterministic MIS and its relatives, turning Linial's most outstanding open problem into a question of polylogarithmic factors. The remaining challenges, closing the LLL gap, mapping CONGEST, and pinning down the exact complexity of deterministic symmetry breaking, are among the most attractive open problems in theoretical computer science.
