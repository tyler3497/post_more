# Quantum Low-Density Parity-Check Codes: Hypergraph-Product Constructions, Single-Shot Decoding, and the Overhead of Fault-Tolerant Quantum Computation

## Abstract

The threshold theorem guarantees that arbitrarily long quantum computations can be executed reliably below a physical error threshold, but the standard form of the theorem demands *polylogarithmic* overhead in the number of physical qubits per logical qubit. Quantum low-density parity-check (qLDPC) codes promise to collapse this overhead to a constant. This thesis surveys the decade-long theoretical arc from the first constant-rate qLDPC families — the hypergraph-product (HGP) codes of Tillich and Zémor, achieving rate Θ(1) with distance Θ(√n) — through the 2022 resolution of the qLDPC conjecture by Panteleev and Kalachev (lifted products over non-abelian groups) and by Leverrier and Zémor (quantum Tanner codes on left–right Cayley complexes), yielding asymptotically good families with *linear* rate and *linear* distance. We develop the chain-complex formalism unifying these constructions, analyze decoding through the small-set-flip and single-shot paradigms of Fawzi, Grospellier, and Leverrier, and trace how efficient decoding feeds Gottesman's constant-overhead fault-tolerance framework.

## 1 Introduction

Quantum error correction (QEC) is the indispensable technology separating noisy intermediate-scale quantum devices from fault-tolerant quantum computation. The threshold theorem guarantees that below a physical error rate $p_{\text{th}}$, arbitrarily reliable computation is achievable with overhead growing only *polylogarithmically* in the size of the computation. For two decades, the surface code stood as the de facto implementation standard: its strictly local stabilizers and high threshold (~0.7–1%) made it attractive for superconducting hardware, yet its encoding rate $k/n \to 0$ imposes a staggering physical-qubit tax on large algorithms.

Quantum low-density parity-check codes offer a fundamentally different bargain. Like their classical counterparts — Gallager codes that asymptotically approach the Shannon capacity — qLDPC codes keep stabilizer weights bounded by a constant $w$ independent of block length $n$, while permitting the *rate* $k/n$ and the *relative distance* $d/n$ to remain bounded away from zero. The central question of the field, posed as the *qLDPC conjecture*, asked whether asymptotically good quantum LDPC codes exist at all: families with $k = \Theta(n)$, $d = \Theta(n)$, and bounded check weight. Until 2022, every known construction fell short:

| Family | Rate $k/n$ | Distance $d$ | Reference |
|---|---|---|---|
| Surface / toric codes | $O(1/n)$ | $\Theta(\sqrt{n})$ | Kitaev; Dennis *et al.* |
| Hypergraph-product codes | $\Theta(1)$ | $\Theta(\sqrt{n})$ | Tillich–Zémor [2] |
| Quantum expander codes | $\Theta(1)$ | $\Theta(\sqrt{n})$ | Leverrier–Tillich–Zémor |
| Fiber-bundle codes | $\tilde{\Theta}(n^{-2/5})$ | $\tilde{\Theta}(n^{3/5})$ | Hastings–Haah–O'Donnell |
| Balanced-product codes | $\tilde{\Theta}(n^{-1/5})$ | $\tilde{\Theta}(n^{3/5})$ | Breuckmann–Eberhardt |
| Lifted-product codes (Panteleev–Kalachev) | $\Theta(1)$ | $\Theta(n)$ | Panteleev–Kalachev [3] |
| Quantum Tanner codes (Leverrier–Zémor) | $\Theta(1)$ | $\Theta(n)$ | Leverrier–Zémor [4] |

The 2022 breakthrough resolved the conjecture affirmatively *three times over*, within months. This thesis reconstructs that arc, from the chain-complex algebra of the hypergraph product to the expansion-theoretic machinery of the asymptotically good families, and then examines the two operational questions that determine whether qLDPC codes reduce real overhead: *decoding* and *fault-tolerant embedding*.

> **Theorem (Asymptotic goodness, Panteleev–Kalachev [3]; Leverrier–Zémor [4]):** *There exist infinite families of CSS quantum codes with parity-check rows and columns of uniformly bounded weight such that both the rate $k/n$ and the relative distance $d/n$ are bounded below by a positive constant.*

The practical stakes are captured by a simple accounting. A surface-code logical qubit at distance $d$ costs $\Theta(d^2)$ physical qubits; a qLDPC code at constant rate costs $\Theta(1)$ per logical qubit. For an algorithm requiring $10^6$ logical qubits, this is the difference between a billion-physical-qubit machine and a million-physical-qubit machine — the difference between impossible and merely ambitious.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Stabilizer and CSS formalism

A stabilizer code on $n$ qubits is the common $+1$ eigenspace of an abelian subgroup $\mathcal{S}$ of the Pauli group $\mathcal{P}_n$, with $-\mathbb{I} \notin \mathcal{S}$. A Calderbank–Shor–Steane (CSS) code is specified by two classical binary linear codes $C_X^\perp \subseteq C_Z$ and $C_Z^\perp \subseteq C_X$; the $X$-stabilizers derive from $C_X^\perp$ and the $Z$-stabilizers from $C_Z^\perp$. The code parameters $[\\![n,k,d]\\!]$ satisfy

$$k = \dim C_Z/C_X^\perp, \qquad d = \min(d_X, d_Z),$$

where $d_X$ ($d_Z$) is the minimum weight of a vector in $C_Z \setminus C_X^\perp$ (resp. $C_X \setminus C_Z^\perp$). The LDPC condition demands that the parity-check matrices $H_X, H_Z$ have uniformly bounded row and column weights.

Chain complexes provide the cleanest language for the modern constructions. A CSS code is a length-2 chain complex

$$C_2 \xrightarrow{\partial_2} C_1 \xrightarrow{\partial_1} C_0,$$

over $\mathbb{F}_2$, with qubits on $C_1$, $X$-checks on $C_0$ ($\partial_1 = H_X$), and $Z$-checks on $C_2$ ($\partial_2^T = H_Z$). The CSS commutation constraint is exactly $\partial_1 \circ \partial_2 = 0$. Homology $H_1 = \ker\partial_1 / \operatorname{im}\partial_2$ encodes the logical operators.

### 2.2 The hypergraph product

Tillich and Zémor's 2009 construction [2] takes two classical binary linear codes with parity-check matrices $H_1$ ($m_1 \times n_1$) and $H_2$ ($m_2 \times n_2$) and forms the *hypergraph product* — the tensor product of their length-1 complexes. Qubits live on $(n_1 \times n_2) \cup (m_1 \times m_2)$ positions, and the CSS checks are

$$
H_X = \begin{pmatrix} H_1 \otimes I_{n_2} & I_{m_1} \otimes H_2^T \end{pmatrix}, \qquad
H_Z = \begin{pmatrix} I_{n_1} \otimes H_2 & H_1^T \otimes I_{m_2} \end{pmatrix}.
$$

The resulting parameters are $[\\![n_1n_2 + m_1m_2,; k_1k_2 + k_1^T k_2^T,; \min(d_1, d_2, d_1^T, d_2^T)]\\!]$, where $k_i^T, d_i^T$ denote the dimension and distance of the *transpose* code $\ker H_i^T$. Choosing both classical codes as good LDPC codes of rate $r$ and linear distance gives a quantum code of rate $r^2 + (1-r)^2 - \text{(dual corrections)} = \Theta(1)$ and distance $\Theta(\sqrt{n})$ — the first qLDPC family with *both* constant rate and polynomial distance [2]. The $\sqrt{n}$ barrier is intrinsic: the product structure forces at least one of $d_X, d_Z$ to be inherited from a single classical factor.

### 2.3 The distance barrier and the qLDPC conjecture

For over a decade, the $\Theta(\sqrt{n})$ distance of HGP codes stood as the best known among constant-rate qLDPC families. Progress arrived through geometric constructions (hyperbolic codes, fiber-bundle codes, balanced products), each improving the distance exponent, but none reaching linear distance at constant rate. Meanwhile, decoding theory advanced in parallel: the *small-set-flip* decoder of Leverrier, Tillich, and Zémor gave linear-time adversarial decoding up to $O(\sqrt{n})$ errors for quantum expander codes, and Fawzi, Grospellier, and Leverrier [5] extended this to *random* errors of linear weight via a percolation analysis — precisely the decoder Gottesman's constant-overhead framework required [7].

## 3 Methodology

This thesis is a theoretical synthesis rather than an experimental study. Our methodology comprises:

1. **Algebraic reconstruction.** We re-derive the HGP parameter calculus from the Künneth formula for chain-complex tensor products, making explicit where the $\sqrt{n}$ barrier originates (the homology of the product inherits the minimum of the two factor distances).
2. **Expansion-theoretic analysis.** We examine how Panteleev–Kalachev [3] and Leverrier–Zémor [4] break the barrier by replacing the tensor product with *lifted* and *balanced* products over groups, so that the local structure at each vertex is itself a robustly testable tensor code rather than a single parity check. The key technical instrument is the *product-expansion* (robust testability) of the local code: a pair of classical codes $(C_A, C_B)$ has product-expansion $\rho > 0$ if every word in $C_A \otimes \mathbb{F}_2^{n_B} + \mathbb{F}_2^{n_A} \otimes C_B$ close to a codeword of $C_A \otimes C_B$ admits a decomposition reflecting that closeness.
3. **Decoding analysis.** We compare adversarial (small-set-flip) versus random-error decoding, and the *single-shot* regime in which syndrome measurement noise is corrected by *metachecks* in a single round rather than by $O(d)$ repeated measurements [5, 6].
4. **Overhead accounting.** We quantify the space overhead of fault-tolerant schemes built on these codes, contrasting the surface-code polylogarithmic overhead with the constant overhead enabled by good qLDPC codes under Gottesman's construction [7] and its explicit instantiation by Fawzi, Grospellier, and Leverrier [6].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Künneth formula and the square-root barrier

For chain complexes $A$ and $B$ over $\mathbb{F}_2$, the tensor product complex $A \otimes B$ has homology governed by the Künneth formula:

$$H_k(A \otimes B) \cong \bigoplus_{i+j=k} H_i(A) \otimes H_j(B).$$

In the HGP construction, each classical code is a length-1 complex $C_1 \xrightarrow{H} C_0$; the quantum code's logical operators are $H_1(A \otimes B) \cong (H_1(A) \otimes H_0(B)) \oplus (H_0(A) \otimes H_1(B))$. The distance calculation is the crux: a logical operator supported on $H_1(A) \otimes H_0(B)$ has weight $d(A) \cdot w$ where $w$ is the weight of a *homology class representative* in $H_0(B)$ — but $H_0(B) = C_0/\operatorname{im} H$ contains classes of weight $1$ (single check vertices are not boundaries in general). Hence the distance is bounded by $\min(d_1, d_2)$, the distance of a *single* classical factor, and since $n = n_1n_2 + m_1m_2$ while $d = \Theta(\min(n_1, n_2))$, one obtains $d = \Theta(\sqrt{n})$ at best. This is not a proof artifact — it is structural.

```python
# Parameter calculus for the hypergraph product (illustrative)
def hgp_params(n1, k1, d1, m1, n2, k2, d2, m2):
    # transpose-code parameters: kT = m - rank, approx m - (n - k)
    k1T, d1T = m1 - (n1 - k1), d1   # heuristic; exact needs H1^T analysis
    k2T, d2T = m2 - (n2 - k2), d2
    n = n1 * n2 + m1 * m2
    k = k1 * k2 + k1T * k2T
    d = min(d1, d2, d1T, d2T)       # <- square-root barrier lives here
    return n, k, d
```

### 4.2 Lifted products: Panteleev and Kalachev's breakthrough

Panteleev and Kalachev [3] observed that the HGP's weakness is its *abelian* product structure. Their *lifted product* replaces the tensor product of vector spaces with a *balanced product* over a group algebra: given a group $G$ and $G$-modules (classical codes with a $G$-action), the product is taken over the group ring $\mathbb{F}_2[G]$, identifying $(g \cdot a) \otimes b \sim a \otimes (g \cdot b)$. Lifting an expander graph by a group action multiplies the number of vertices while preserving expansion, and — critically — the lifted complex inherits a *free $G$-action* that symmetrizes the local constraints.

Their 2021 paper first achieved *almost* linear distance $\Omega(n/\log n)$ at constant rate via lifted products over abelian groups [2]. The 2022 sequel [3] went further: lifted products over *non-abelian* groups, combined with a two-sided robustly testable product code as the local ingredient, yield *linear* distance and *constant* rate — asymptotically good qLDPC codes. The same paper simultaneously proved that the classical codes produced are asymptotically good and *locally testable* with constant query complexity, resolving a long-standing conjecture in classical coding theory as a byproduct. The analysis hinges on showing that the lifted complex is a *small-set expander* in the sense of Dinur et al., so that small errors expand to large syndromes — the quantum analogue of the Sipser–Spielman distance proof.

### 4.3 Quantum Tanner codes: the left–right Cayley complex

Leverrier and Zémor [4] gave a second, more transparent route to asymptotic goodness. Classical Tanner codes place bits on the edges of a graph and enforce a short local code at each vertex; Sipser and Spielman showed that with a spectral expander and a good local code, the result is asymptotically good. The quantum generalization requires *two* graphs on the same edge set whose local codes are *dual tensor codes* $C_A \otimes C_B$ and $C_A^\perp \otimes C_B^\perp$.

The geometric substrate is the *left–right Cayley complex*: given a group $G$ with symmetric generating sets $A, B$, vertices are $V = G \times \{0,1\}^2$, and squares are indexed by $(g, a, b)$. Left multiplication by $A$ and right multiplication by $B$ give the two graph structures; their *squares* (faces) carry the qubits. The local codes are tensor products of short random codes, and the distance proof again flows from product-expansion plus the expansion of the two Cayley graphs. The construction is arguably the most promising for explicit finite-length instantiation, since random local codes of modest length can be searched computationally — indeed, recent work has found $[\![144,12,11]\!]$ and $[\![96,2,12]\!]$ instances with check weight below $10$, already surpassing the $\sqrt{n}$ bound at small sizes.

### 4.4 Single-shot decoding and metachecks

Standard fault-tolerant QEC repeats syndrome measurement $O(d)$ times to combat *measurement errors*; this time overhead is often the dominant cost. *Single-shot* decoding, formalized in the qLDPC context by Fawzi, Grospellier, and Leverrier [5], exploits *redundancy among the checks themselves*: a set of *metachecks* $M$ with $M H = 0$ constrains the syndrome, so that a noisy syndrome $s + e_s$ can be cleaned to $s$ (up to stabilizer ambiguity) in a *single* round, provided the residual syndrome noise is below threshold.

> **Theorem (Single-shot decoding [5]):** *For quantum expander codes built from sufficiently expanding graphs, there exists a constant single-shot threshold $p_{\text{ss}} > 0$ such that one round of noisy syndrome measurement followed by metacheck correction and small-set-flip decoding suppresses the logical error rate, with the residual error bounded by $O(p)$ times the physical error.*

Haskell-style pseudocode captures the structure:

```haskell
-- Single-shot decoding pipeline
singleShot :: Code -> NoisySyndrome -> Either LogicalError Correction
singleShot code (s, e_s) = do
  s'  <- metacheckCorrect (metachecks code) (s + e_s)  -- clean syndrome noise
  e_hat <- smallSetFlip code s'                       -- decode data errors
  return e_hat
```

The requirement is nontrivial: the metacheck matrix must itself have good expansion properties, and the *soundness* — the ratio of syndrome weight to error weight — must survive the cleaning step. Quantum Tanner codes and lifted-product codes admit natural metacheck structures from the underlying complex, making single-shot operation a realistic prospect rather than a theoretical curiosity.

### 4.5 Constant-overhead fault tolerance

Gottesman [7] proved that fault-tolerant quantum computation need not incur growing overhead: given a family of qLDPC codes with constant rate, linear distance, and an *efficient decoder for random errors*, an arbitrary quantum circuit of size $S$ can be simulated with $O(S)$ physical qubits — *constant* space overhead — below a threshold error rate. The construction concatenates the qLDPC code with itself in a hierarchical scheme where each level's logical gates are implemented via gate teleportation on encoded ancillae, and the decoder's linearity keeps the classical processing tractable.

The missing piece in 2014 was the decoder: no known code family simultaneously had constant rate, linear distance, *and* an efficient random-error decoder. Fawzi, Grospellier, and Leverrier [5] supplied the decoder for quantum expander codes, and Fawzi, Grospellier, and Leverrier [6] combined it with Gottesman's framework to give the first *fully explicit* constant-overhead fault-tolerance scheme. The 2022 asymptotically good families [3, 4] close the remaining gap — linear distance with efficient (parallel, logarithmic-time) decoders — making constant-overhead fault tolerance a theorem about *explicit* codes rather than a conditional claim.

The overhead comparison is stark:

| Scheme | Physical qubits per logical qubit | Syndrome rounds per cycle | Decoder |
|---|---|---|---|
| Surface code + MWPM | $\Theta(d^2) = \Theta(\log^2(1/\epsilon))$ | $\Theta(d)$ | Minimum-weight matching |
| HGP + BP-OSD | $\Theta(1)$ (rate $\approx 0.1$–$0.3$) | $\Theta(d) = \Theta(\sqrt{n})$ | Belief propagation + OSD |
| Quantum Tanner + single-shot | $\Theta(1)$ (rate $\approx 0.05$–$0.2$) | $O(1)$ | Parallel flip / BP |
| Lifted-product (asymptotic) | $\Theta(1)$ | $O(1)$ (conjectured) | Parallel decoder |

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

While the asymptotically good families are recent, the HGP family has substantial numerical backing. Circuit-level simulations (Tremblay–Delfosse–Beverland, 2022) demonstrated HGP codes *outperforming* surface codes at equal physical-qubit counts under circuit noise, once efficient syndrome-extraction circuits and BP-OSD decoding are employed — the first such demonstration. Panteleev and Kalachev's BP-OSD studies showed degenerate qLDPC codes beating large surface codes under depolarizing noise by several orders of magnitude in logical error rate [2].

On the proof side, the central technical achievements are:

1. **Distance.** The small-set-expansion framework: for the lifted-product and Tanner complexes, any error $e$ with $|e| \le \alpha n$ has syndrome weight $|\sigma(e)| \ge \beta |e|$, implying $d \ge \alpha n$ by a standard counting argument. The proof decomposes the error across the group action (lifted product) or across the two Cayley graphs (Tanner), applying product-expansion of the local tensor code at each step.
2. **Decoding random errors.** The $\alpha$-percolation theorem [5]: for a random error set $W$ at rate $p < p_0$, the connected $\alpha$-dense clusters have size $O(\log n)$ with high probability. Small-set-flip then corrects each cluster locally, giving linear-time decoding of a *linear* number of random errors — the precise property Gottesman's scheme demands.
3. **Threshold with noisy syndromes.** The single-shot analysis [5] shows the metacheck-cleaned syndrome retains enough signal for the flip decoder, establishing a nonzero threshold even when every syndrome bit is independently flipped with constant probability.

A TLA⁺ sketch of the single-shot correctness invariant illustrates the formal-methods angle increasingly applied to these decoders:

```tla
------------------------------- MODULE SingleShot -------------------------------
VARIABLES err, syn, synNoise
Init == err \in SUBSET Qubits /\ syn = H * err /\ synNoise \in SUBSET Checks
Clean == synNoise' = MetacheckCorrect(syn + synNoise)  (* |residual| <= c*|synNoise| *)
Decode == LET ehat == SmallSetFlip(synNoise') IN err' = err + ehat
Spec == Init /\ [][Clean \/ Decode]_vars /\ WF_vars(Decode)
THEOREM Correctness == Spec => [](err + DecodedErr \in Stabilizer + LogicalOK)
=============================================================================
```

## 6 Limitations

Enthusiasm for qLDPC codes must be tempered by five concrete limitations:

1. **Check weights and connectivity.** Asymptotically good families require check weights of order $10$–$20$ and *long-range* connectivity — every qubit participates in checks with distant partners, since expander graphs cannot be embedded locally in two dimensions. Superconducting architectures with planar couplers cannot natively implement these codes; the realistic platforms are trapped ions (QCCD), neutral atoms with reconfigurable tweezers, and photonic interconnects.
2. **Constants, not just asymptotics.** The proven rate and distance constants are small (relative distance on the order of $10^{-3}$–$10^{-2}$ in current proofs). Explicit finite-length codes with competitive *effective* distance remain scarce, though the $[\![144,12,11]\!]$ Tanner instances are encouraging.
3. **Decoder complexity at scale.** BP-OSD, the workhorse finite-length decoder, has a cubic-time OSD post-processing step; the theoretically clean parallel flip decoders have thresholds well below BP-OSD in practice. The gap between *provable* and *practical* decoding persists.
4. **Fault-tolerant gate sets.** High-rate codes lack the transversal gate structure of topological codes. Logical gates require code deformation, gate teleportation, or lattice-surgery analogues — all active research areas with incomplete cost accounting.
5. **Single-shot thresholds are low.** Proven single-shot thresholds are orders of magnitude below surface-code thresholds; whether they can be pushed into the $10^{-3}$ regime numerically is open.

## 7 Conclusion

The arc from Tillich and Zémor's hypergraph product to the asymptotically good codes of 2022 represents one of the most consequential decades in quantum coding theory. The qLDPC conjecture's resolution did not merely answer an existence question — it delivered *explicit, decodable* families whose parameters provably collapse the space overhead of fault tolerance from polylogarithmic to constant. Single-shot decoding further attacks the *time* overhead, reducing $O(d)$ syndrome rounds to $O(1)$.

The field's center of gravity has consequently shifted from *existence* to *engineering*: finding finite-length instances with large effective distance and small check weight, designing syndrome-extraction circuits for long-range architectures, and closing the gap between provable flip decoders and practical BP-OSD. If these engineering challenges are met on neutral-atom or trapped-ion platforms within the decade, the first fault-tolerant quantum computers may owe their feasibility not to the surface code, but to the expander graphs and tensor codes surveyed here. The overhead wall is, in principle, down; what remains is to walk through it.
