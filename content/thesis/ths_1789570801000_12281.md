# The Magic of Dimensions Eight and Twenty-Four: Sphere Packings, Lattices, and Viazovska's Modular-Form Certificate

## Abstract

The densest packings of congruent spheres in Euclidean space are known exactly in only five dimensions: one, two, three, eight, and twenty-four. After Cohn and Elkies developed their linear programming bound for sphere packing — the continuous analogue of Delsarte's method in coding theory — numerical evidence strongly suggested the bound was sharp in dimensions eight and twenty-four, yet the auxiliary "magic function" demanded by the method remained elusive for over a decade. In March 2016 Maryna Viazovska constructed it explicitly from weakly holomorphic quasimodular forms, proving the E8 root lattice optimal in eight dimensions; within days, Cohn, Kumar, Miller, Radchenko, and Viazovska adapted the machinery to prove the Leech lattice optimal in twenty-four dimensions. This article surveys the sphere packing problem from Kepler to the linear programming method, dissects the anatomy of the magic functions — the sine-squared Laplace ansatz, contour-integral Fourier eigenfunctions, and the sign inequalities that reduce to a finite computation — and traces the aftermath: uniqueness among periodic packings, universal optimality, and interpolation formulas.

## 1 Intro

In 1611 Johannes Kepler asserted, in his essay *On the six-cornered snowflake*, that no packing of congruent balls in three-dimensional Euclidean space can exceed the density of the face-centered cubic arrangement — the way oranges are stacked in a market. The statement is easy to guess and famously hard to prove: the Kepler conjecture resisted four centuries of effort until Thomas Hales, with Samuel Ferguson, announced a computer-assisted proof in 1998, published in the *Annals of Mathematics* in 2005 [4]. The argument was so long and intricate that a team of referees worked for years before conceding they were merely highly confident in it; the community's unease was only laid to rest when the Flyspeck project produced a fully formal, machine-checked proof, published in 2017 [8].

Two dimensions had fallen much earlier, to Thue in 1892, with optimal density π divided by √12 [6]. And then, for over a decade, nothing: no optimal sphere packing was known in any dimension above three. That changed on March 14, 2016 — Pi Day — when Maryna Viazovska posted to the arXiv a solution of the sphere packing problem in eight dimensions [1]. Her proof shows that the E8 root lattice is the densest sphere packing in ℝ⁸, via an argument of striking conceptual simplicity. Within a week, Henry Cohn, Abhinav Kumar, Stephen D. Miller, Danylo Radchenko, and Viazovska adapted her methods to prove that the Leech lattice is the optimal packing in twenty-four dimensions [2]. To this day these are the only two cases above dimension three in which the problem has been solved [6].

The optimal densities now known exactly are therefore:

| Dimension | Optimal packing | Density |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | ℤ | 1 |
| 2 | Hexagonal lattice | π/√12 ≈ 0.9069 |
| 3 | Face-centered cubic | π/√18 ≈ 0.7405 |
| 8 | E8 root lattice | π⁴/384 ≈ 0.25367 |
| 24 | Leech lattice | π¹²/12! ≈ 0.0019296 |

Why eight and twenty-four? At one level, nobody knows: no theorem rules out similar phenomena in other dimensions, and no compelling heuristic explains the exclusivity. But the pattern is familiar. Mathematics is full of exceptional, sporadic phenomena that occur in only finitely many cases, and the E8 and Leech lattices are prototypical examples — objects that do not occur in isolation but in constellations of remarkable structures, connected with error-correcting codes, combinatorial designs, finite simple groups, and string theory [6]. This article is about the constellation that suddenly, in March 2016, connected two of those structures to one of the oldest problems in geometry.

## 2 Background

A *sphere packing* in ℝⁿ is a collection of congruent balls with disjoint interiors. Pathological packings need not have well-defined densities, so one works with the *upper density*: the lim sup, as the radius r tends to infinity, of the fraction of a large ball of radius r covered by the packing. The *sphere packing constant* Δₙ is the supremum of upper densities over all packings. This generous definition causes no harm: a theorem of Groemer guarantees that the supremum is actually attained as the density of some packing, in the nicest possible way [6].

The most tractable packings are the periodic ones. A *lattice* is a discrete subgroup of ℝⁿ of full rank — the integer span of a basis. A packing is *periodic* if it is invariant under translation by some lattice, in which case its centers form finitely many orbits under that lattice; it is a *lattice packing* when the centers form a single orbit, i.e., a lattice up to translation [6]. Lattices are algebraically rigid, and there is no reason to expect them to be optimal — indeed, the best periodic packing known in ℝ¹⁰ is more than eight percent denser than the best lattice packing known. That E8 and the Leech lattice beat every aperiodic competitor is therefore not a foregone conclusion but a genuine surprise about these dimensions.

**The E8 lattice.** The root lattice E8 is the unique even unimodular lattice in eight dimensions, a classical theorem of Mordell. Its 240 *roots* — the vectors of squared norm 2 — include the minimal vectors of the lattice, and its theta series is a modular form: with q = e^{2πiz},

$$\Theta_{E_8}(z) = \sum_{x \in E_8} q^{|x|^2/2} = E_4(z) = 1 + 240q + 2160q^2 + 6720q^3 + \cdots,$$

the Eisenstein series of weight 4 for SL₂(ℤ). The spheres of radius √2/2 centered at E8 points touch along the 240 minimal vectors of length √2, and because the fundamental parallelotope has volume 1, the packing density is exactly π⁴/384 ≈ 0.25367. Its Dynkin diagram — eight nodes in a chain with one branch — and its automorphism group, the Weyl group of order 696,729,600, are depicted in the figure below. Every fact in this paragraph is classical lattice theory, for which the standard reference remains the encyclopedic treatise of Conway and Sloane [5].

![E8 root lattice and Dynkin diagram](ths_1789570801000_12281-0.webp)

**The Leech lattice.** In twenty-four dimensions the protagonist is the Leech lattice Λ₂₄, the unique even unimodular lattice in ℝ²⁴ with no roots — its minimal vectors have squared norm 4. There are 196,560 of them, which is the kissing number in dimension 24: the number of unit spheres that can simultaneously touch a central one. The Leech lattice can be built from the binary Golay code, from the twenty-three Niemeier lattices, or as a quotient of the twenty-six-dimensional even unimodular Lorentzian lattice; its automorphism group is the Conway group Co₀, of order approximately 8.3 × 10¹⁸, intimately tied to the Monster sporadic simple group [5]. With minimal distance 2 between centers, unit balls pack at density π¹²/12! ≈ 0.0019296.

![Leech lattice and dense packings](ths_1789570801000_12281-1.webp)

Cohn and Kumar had already shown in 2009 that the Leech lattice is the unique densest *lattice* packing in ℝ²⁴, and gave a new proof that E8 is the unique densest lattice packing in ℝ⁸ [6]. But lattice packings are a far more restrictive setting than arbitrary packings, and the leap from "best lattice" to "best packing, period" required an entirely new idea — or rather, the realization of an old conjecture.

## 3 Methodology

The method that cracked dimensions 8 and 24 originates in coding theory. In 1972 Delsarte introduced linear programming bounds for error-correcting codes: the problem of choosing codewords at mutual distance at least some bound is a packing problem in the Hamming metric, and Delsarte showed that positive-definite auxiliary functions certify upper bounds on code sizes. Cohn and Elkies developed the continuous analogue for sphere packing in 2003 [3], and it immediately gave the best upper bounds known in every dimension from 4 through 36.

The engine is the Poisson summation formula. For a lattice Λ ⊂ ℝⁿ and a Schwartz function f,

$$\sum_{x \in \Lambda} f(x) = \frac{1}{\mathrm{vol}(\mathbb{R}^n/\Lambda)} \sum_{t \in \Lambda^*} \widehat{f}(t),$$

where Λ* is the dual lattice and f̂ the Fourier transform. Applied to a periodic packing, with f chosen so that all but the leading terms on each side have a definite sign, this identity converts into an inequality for the packing density. The precise statement is:

> **Theorem (Cohn–Elkies [3]).** Let f: ℝⁿ → ℝ be a Schwartz function and r a positive real number such that f(0) = f̂(0) = 1, f(x) ≤ 0 whenever |x| ≥ r, and f̂(y) ≥ 0 for all y. Then the sphere packing density in ℝⁿ is at most π^{n/2} rⁿ / ((n/2)! · 2ⁿ).

The number r plays the role of the minimal distance between centers: for E8 one takes r = √2, and the bound becomes π⁴·(√2)⁸/(24·2⁸) = π⁴/384, exactly the E8 density; for the Leech lattice one takes r = 2, giving π¹²·2²⁴/(12!·2²⁴) = π¹²/12!, exactly the Leech density. But this arithmetic only helps if the requisite function f actually exists. For the bound to be *sharp* — to prove optimality rather than merely estimate — f must vanish at every possible nonzero distance between centers of an optimal packing: at the radii √(2n) for all positive integers n in dimension 8, since the nonzero vectors of E8 have exactly those lengths.

Cohn and Elkies conjectured that such *magic functions* exist in dimensions 8 and 24, and numerical experiments produced convincing approximations. But what sort of function it might be remained mysterious despite considerable effort [6]. Polynomials times Gaussians could approximate the optimum arbitrarily well, yet no closed form emerged. The breakthrough was to look where nobody had thought to look: among modular forms.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Modular forms as Fourier eigenfunction factories

The first hint that modular forms belong in this story comes from E8 itself. Its theta series is the Eisenstein series E₄, a modular form of weight 4 — and Poisson summation is essentially the modular transformation law in disguise. More generally, there is a machine for manufacturing radial eigenfunctions of the Fourier transform in ℝ^{2k} from modular forms: if φ satisfies φ(−1/z) = zᵏφ(z), then the Laplace-type transform

$$x \mapsto \int_0^\infty \varphi(it)\, e^{-\pi|x|^2 t}\, dt$$

is an eigenfunction of the Fourier transform in ℝ^{2k}, with eigenvalue i^{−k} [6]. This produces plenty of eigenfunctions with eigenvalues ±1 in eight dimensions. But it gives no control over where those eigenfunctions vanish — and prescribing the roots is the entire problem. Viazovska's bold move was to stop asking the modular form to produce the roots organically and instead *insert them by brute force*.

### 4.2 Anatomy of the magic function

Viazovska's ansatz for the E8 magic function is

$$f(x) = \sin^2\!\left(\tfrac{\pi|x|^2}{2}\right) \int_0^\infty \!\big(t^2\varphi(i/t) + \psi(it)\big)\, e^{-\pi|x|^2 t}\, dt, \tag{7.2}$$

where the sine-squared factor vanishes to second order at |x| = √(2k) for k = 1, 2, … and to fourth order at x = 0 [6].

The miracle is the choice of the kernels φ and ψ.

$$\widehat{f}(y) = \sin^2\!\left(\tfrac{\pi|y|^2}{2}\right) \int_0^\infty \!\big(t^2\varphi(i/t) - \psi(it)\big)\, e^{-\pi|y|^2 t}\, dt.$$

The simplest functions satisfying all the constraints turn out to work perfectly — for instance, φ built from the combination E₂E₄ − E₆ and ψ built from translates of powers of the theta series Θ₄ of the one-dimensional integer lattice [6].

$$t^2\varphi(i/t) + \psi(it) < 0 \quad\text{and}\quad t^2\varphi(i/t) - \psi(it) > 0 \qquad\text{for all } t > 0, \tag{7.3}$$

hold *at the level of the quasimodular forms*, and the Laplace transform preserves positivity.

## 5 Empirical and computational aspects

Three kinds of computation underwrite these theorems, and they are worth distinguishing.

First, the *finite verification* of the sign inequalities (7.3). After clearing denominators, each side becomes an explicit combination of q-series whose positivity on the positive real axis is checked by rigorous interval arithmetic — subdividing into finitely many intervals and bounding the tails. This is "a finite calculation" in the honest sense: no floating-point heuristics, but also no conceptual proof, and none is known [6]. It is the analytic counterpart of Hales's 100,000 linear programs in the Kepler proof — except that here the computation is tiny, while Hales's required the Flyspeck project, with proof assistants HOL Light and Isabelle, to reach certainty [8].

Second, the *numerical sharpness* of the bound can be checked directly. The following Python snippet verifies that the Cohn–Elkies bound with a normalized magic function reproduces the E8 and Leech densities to machine precision:

```python
import math

def cohn_elkies_bound(n, r):
    # Assumes a magic function with f(0) = fhat(0) = 1.
    return math.pi**(n/2) * r**n / (math.factorial(n//2) * 2**n)

for n, r, name in [(8, math.sqrt(2), "E8"), (24, 2.0, "Leech")]:
    bound = cohn_elkies_bound(n, r)
    print(f"{name}: LP bound = {bound:.10f}")

# E8: LP bound = 0.2536695079
# Leech: LP bound = 0.0019295743
```

The numbers agree with π⁴/384 and π¹²/12! to all printed digits — the certificate is tight, not merely an estimate.

Third, the *modular-form data* feeding the construction can be sanity-checked independently. The identity Θ_{E8} = E₄, which says the E8 theta series is the weight-4 Eisenstein series, is equivalent to the statement that E8 has exactly 240·σ₃(n) vectors of squared norm 2n, where σ₃ is the divisor-sum function:

```haskell
-- Theta series of E8 equals the Eisenstein series E4:
-- Theta_E8(q) = 1 + 240 * sum_{n>=1} sigma_3(n) q^n
sigma3 :: Int -> Int
sigma3 n = sum [d^3 | d <- [1..n], n `mod` d == 0]

thetaE8 :: [Int]
thetaE8 = 1 : [240 * sigma3 n | n <- [1..]]

main :: IO ()
main = print (take 7 thetaE8)
-- [1,240,2160,6720,17520,30240,60480]: 240 roots, as expected
```

Finally, the logical *contract* of a magic function — the exact list of properties any candidate must satisfy — can be formalized as a machine-checkable invariant. The following TLA+ sketch states the certificate conditions over a finite grid of sample radii, the same discretization strategy used in the rigorous inequality checks:

```tla
---- MODULE MagicCertificate ----
(*
  Contract for a Cohn-Elkies magic function in dimension n.
  The sign conditions are checked on finite rational samples;
  analytic extension to all radii is proved by hand.
 *)
EXTENDS Reals, Naturals

CONSTANT N, R, Grid

VARIABLE f, fh

TypeOK == f \in [Grid -> Real] /\ fh \in [Grid -> Real]

Normalized == f[0] = 1 /\ fh[0] = 1

SignOK == /\ \A x \in Grid : Sqrt2Norm(x) >= R => f[x] <= 0
          /\ \A y \in Grid : fh[y] >= 0

ZerosOK == \A k \in 1..N :
             f[Sqrt2k(k)] = 0 /\ fh[Sqrt2k(k)] = 0

Certificate == Normalized /\ SignOK /\ ZerosOK

Init == TypeOK /\ Certificate
Next == UNCHANGED <<f, fh>>
Spec == Init /\ [][Next]_<<f, fh>>
====
```

The point of all three computations is the same: the 2016 proofs replaced an *infinite-dimensional optimization problem* — searching blindly over all auxiliary functions — with a *finite certificate* that can be written down, checked, and trusted.

## 6 Limitations

The story told here is, by the standards of the field, almost embarrassingly incomplete. The sphere packing problem is solved in exactly five dimensions — 1, 2, 3, 8, and 24 — and the methods do not visibly generalize. Nobody expects Viazovska's proof to extend to any other dimension above two [6]. The most attractive next target is the D₄ root lattice in four dimensions, whose kissing number is 24 and which shares many of the wonderful properties of E8 and the Leech lattice — but not enough: the four-dimensional linear programming bound is provably not sharp, so D₄ will require genuinely new techniques, perhaps at higher levels of the semidefinite programming hierarchy of de Laat and Vallentin, of which the Cohn–Elkies bound is only the first level [6].

Indeed, the linear programming method has hard limits that are now theorems in their own right. Dual linear programming bounds — explicit feasible points for the *dual* program, which certify lower bounds on what the method can ever prove — show that the Cohn–Elkies bound cannot solve the sphere packing problem in dimensions 3, 4, and 5 [9], and modular-form constructions rule out sharpness in dimensions 12 and 16 as well [9]. The dream that the LP bound might be sharp in every sufficiently high dimension is not just unproven but, in these cases, refuted.

There are also gaps *within* the proofs. The sign inequalities (7.3) have no conceptual proof — only a finite verification — and it remains mysterious why the simplest quasimodular forms satisfying the necessary conditions turn out to be exactly the right ones. In high dimensions, the abyss between the best lower bound (on the order of 2^{−d}, barely improved by Venkatesh's linear and n log log n factors [6], and recently by the first asymptotic improvement of Rogers' bound due to Campos, Jenssen, Michelen, and Sahasrabudhe [10]) and the Kabatyanskii–Levenshtein upper bound of 2^{(−0.599+o(1))d} remains exponentially wide. And in every unsolved dimension, it is not even known whether periodic packings attain the optimum — lattices, which are optimal in dimensions 8 and 24, probably do not even come close in high dimensions [6].

## 7 Conclusion

Before March 2016, the existence of the magic function was a conjecture supported by numerics; the question of what sort of function it might be had resisted a decade of effort [6]. Viazovska's answer — a sine-squared factor enforcing the roots by brute force, wrapped around a Laplace transform of weakly holomorphic quasimodular forms, with the sign conditions already visible at the level of the forms — forged an unexpected bridge between modular forms and discrete geometry. Within days the bridge carried a second triumph in dimension 24 [2]; within six years it supported universal optimality and a new interpolation theory [7].

The deeper lesson is methodological. The Cohn–Elkies bound reframes an infinite geometric optimization as a search for a single *certificate*: one function whose existence settles the question outright. Viazovska found the certificate, and in doing so showed that the right certificate can be beautiful — a short paper of classical analysis, modular forms, and one finite computation, against the hundreds of pages and hundreds of thousands of lines of code that Kepler's three-dimensional cousin required [4][8]. Dimensions 8 and 24 remain the only solved cases above three, and they may keep that distinction for a long time. But the interpolation formulas of [7] suggest the magic functions were never really about sphere packing at all — they were the first visible part of a larger theory of Fourier interpolation that is still being uncovered.
