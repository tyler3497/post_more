# Spatial Transcriptomics: Statistical Models, Graph-Based Inference, and the Mathematics of Tissue Architecture

## Abstract

Spatial transcriptomics has transformed tissue biology from a dissociative science into a cartographic one: genes are no longer measured in suspension but mapped onto the geometry of the tissue that produced them. This thesis develops the statistical foundations of that mapping. We formalize spatially variable gene detection as variance-component inference in Gaussian process and generalized linear spatial models, tracing the lineage from SpatialDE through SPARK and SPARK-X to the nearest-neighbor Gaussian process framework of nnSVG, and prove why gene-specific length scales are non-negotiable for biological fidelity. We then recast tissue architecture as a graph inference problem — spatial adjacency, domain segmentation, and graph neural message passing — and treat cell–cell communication as collective optimal transport over a distance-weighted cost manifold, from SpaOTsc to COMMOT. A unified probabilistic scaffold connects capture noise models, spatial covariance, and signaling transport plans, with theorems on complexity scaling, posterior consistency, and identifiability, empirical calibration results, and an honest accounting of where the mathematics still falls short.

## 1 Intro

The central dogma of single-cell biology held for a decade that a cell's transcriptome is defined by *what it expresses*. Spatial transcriptomics [2][3] amends this: a cell's transcriptome is defined by what it expresses *and where it sits*. The same malignant epithelial cell behaves differently at the tumor core, the invasive front, and the immune-infiltrated margin; the same cortical neuron differs by laminar address. Once spatial coordinates are recovered, the tissue becomes a **stochastic field** — a random function over a two-dimensional domain — and every classical question of genomics is re-posed as a question in spatial statistics.

This thesis is organized around three inference problems that together reconstruct tissue architecture from spatial data:

1. **Detection**: which genes vary with space? (spatially variable genes, SVGs)
2. **Segmentation**: what regions does the tissue decompose into? (spatial domains)
3. **Communication**: who signals to whom? (cell–cell communication, CCC)

Each problem admits a clean mathematical formulation, each has generated a family of methods with genuine statistical content, and each exposes tradeoffs between model fidelity, computational scaling, and identifiability that remain unresolved. We develop all three under one roof, emphasizing the Gaussian process machinery that underpins the first, the graph and optimal-transport machinery that underpins the second and third, and the count-noise realities that condition everything.

The scope deliberately excludes raw experimental protocols beyond what the models require; our object of study is the *mathematics of inference*, not the chemistry of capture. We assume throughout a standard data object: an $n \times p$ count matrix $Y$ and spatial coordinates $S \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times 2}$, with $n$ spots (or cells) and $p$ genes.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Measurement technologies and their statistical footprints

Spatial transcriptomics platforms fall into two broad families, and the family's signature is stamped on the noise model. **Spot-based capture arrays** (Spatial Transcriptomics, 10x Genomics Visium) place a tissue section over barcoded capture probes; each spot integrates transcripts from one to a dozen cells at 55 μm resolution. The resulting counts are spatially coarse but transcriptome-wide, with overdispersion driven by both biological heterogeneity *within* a spot and capture inefficiency. **Single-molecule imaging** methods (MERFISH, seqFISH) localize individual transcripts at subcellular resolution but measure only hundreds to thousands of genes, and their dominant noise is optical crowding and decoding error rather than dropout. **Bead-based** approaches (Slide-seq) push capture arrays toward 10 μm beads, approaching single-cell resolution with higher sparsity.

> **Theorem (Noise model selection):** For spot-based data with per-spot cell mixtures, a negative binomial model with spatial random effects dominates a Poisson model whenever the within-spot biological variance exceeds the mean; for imaging-based data with near-single-molecule resolution, Poisson models with known decoding-error probabilities are sufficient. The choice is dictated by the *scale of aggregation*, not by platform branding.

### 2.2 Classical spatial statistics in miniature

Three classical ideas recur throughout spatial transcriptomics:

- **Spatial autocorrelation** (Moran's *I*, Geary's *C*): scalar summaries of whether nearby locations covary. Fast, model-free, but gene-agnostic in scale — they cannot distinguish a broad laminar gradient from a fine vascular pattern.
- **The variogram** $\gamma(h)$: half the expected squared difference of a field at lag $h$. The empirical variogram is the diagnostic that reveals which length scales are present in a tissue before any model is fit.
- **Kriging / Gaussian processes**: optimal linear prediction under a covariance model. Nearly every SVG method is, at bottom, a likelihood ratio test against a GP null or alternative.

### 2.3 Why naive differential expression fails in space

Applying standard differential expression between annotated regions ignores that annotations are themselves estimates, that boundaries are soft, and — most importantly — that spatial expression patterns need not respect any *a priori* partition. A gene varying smoothly along a dorsoventral gradient, or in radial patterns around vessels, is invisible to region-based testing. SVG detection must therefore be **partition-free**: it asks whether expression depends on location at all, with no reference to labels [1][2].

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 The Gaussian process variance-component model

The workhorse formulation, introduced by SpatialDE and refined by its successors, models the transformed expression $y \in \mathbb{R}^n$ of one gene as

$$y \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu \mathbf{1},\, \sigma_s^2 K(\theta) + \sigma_e^2 I)$$

where $K(\theta)$ is a spatial covariance matrix built from pairwise distances $d(s_i, s_j)$, most commonly the squared-exponential kernel $K_{ij} = \exp(-d_{ij}^2 / 2\ell^2)$ with **length scale** $\ell$. The decomposition is the entire method: $\sigma_s^2$ is the spatial variance, $\sigma_e^2$ the non-spatial noise, and the null hypothesis $\sigma_s^2 = 0$ is tested by a likelihood ratio

$$\Lambda = -2\left(\log L(\hat\sigma_e^2; \sigma_s^2 = 0) - \log L(\hat\sigma_s^2, \hat\sigma_e^2, \hat\ell)\right).$$

Under regularity conditions $\Lambda \xrightarrow{d} \chi^2_1$, giving calibrated *p*-values [1][2]. The estimated $\ell$ is not a nuisance parameter — it is the **biological readout**: large $\ell$ marks broad tissue programs (cortical layers), small $\ell$ marks fine structure (vasculature, immune foci).

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.spatial.distance import cdist

def se_kernel(coords, ell):
    D2 = cdist(coords, coords, "sqeuclidean")
    return np.exp(-D2 / (2 * ell ** 2))

def svg_loglik(y, K, sigma_s2, sigma_e2):
    n = y.shape[0]
    V = sigma_s2 * K + sigma_e2 * np.eye(n)
    L = np.linalg.cholesky(V)
    alpha = np.linalg.solve(L.T, np.linalg.solve(L, y - y.mean()))
    return -0.5 * (y @ alpha + 2 * np.log(np.diag(L)).sum()
                   + n * np.log(2 * np.pi))
```

### 3.2 Count-valued extensions: SPARK and the Cauchy combination

Gaussian models require variance-stabilizing transformations that distort the mean–variance relationship of sparse counts. SPARK instead builds a **generalized linear spatial model** with a Poisson (or overdispersed) link and spatial random effects, testing $\sigma_s^2 = 0$ under each of several candidate kernels and combining the resulting *p*-values with the **Cauchy combination rule** [2]:

$$T = \sum_k w_k \tan\left((0.5 - p_k)\pi\right), \qquad p_{\text{combined}} = \tfrac{1}{2} - \tfrac{\arctan(T)}{\pi}.$$

The Cauchy combination is robust to arbitrary dependence among the *p*-values — a critical property, since tests under different kernels on the same gene are maximally dependent. SPARK-X pushes further, replacing likelihood fits with a covariance-structure test that scales linearly [1].

### 3.3 Nearest-neighbor Gaussian processes: nnSVG

The cubic $O(n^3)$ Cholesky in the GP likelihood is the fundamental bottleneck: Slide-seq and Visium HD datasets carry tens of thousands of locations. nnSVG replaces the full GP with a **nearest-neighbor Gaussian process (NNGP)** [1]: the joint density factorizes over a directed acyclic graph of $m \ll n$ neighbors per location,

$$p(y) = \prod_{i=1}^n p(y_i \mid y_{N(i)}),$$

where $N(i)$ are the $m$ nearest previously-ordered neighbors. Estimation scales as $O(nm^3)$ — linear in $n$ — while retaining **gene-specific length scales**, the feature that fixed-kernel methods like SPARK-X sacrifice. The empirical consequence is striking: nnSVG recovers both broad laminar markers and fine vascular patterns in human dorsolateral prefrontal cortex that single-scale methods miss [1].

> **Theorem (NNGP consistency):** Under increasing-domain asymptotics with fixed $m$, the NNGP likelihood converges to the full GP likelihood at rate governed by the decay of the true covariance beyond the $m$-th neighbor. For squared-exponential kernels with moderate $\ell$ relative to the domain, $m = 10$ neighbors capture effectively all dependence, and the SVG test retains asymptotic validity.

```rust
// NNGP sparse Cholesky sketch: each row touches only m neighbors
fn nngp_row(neighbors: &[usize], k_self: f64, k_cross: &[f64],
            k_nn: &mut [f64]) -> Vec<f64> {
    // solve k_nn * w = k_cross, then conditional variance = k_self - w'k_cross
    let w = cholesky_solve(k_nn, k_cross);
    let cond_var = k_self - w.iter().zip(k_cross).map(|(a, b)| a * b).sum::<f64>();
    w.into_iter().chain(std::iter::once(cond_var.sqrt())).collect()
}
```

### 3.4 The SVG method landscape

| Method | Model | Noise | Complexity | Length scale | Calibration |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| SpatialDE | GP, variance components | Gaussian (transformed) | $O(n^3)$ | gene-specific | LRT $\chi^2$ |
| SPARK | GLMM, spatial random effects | Poisson / NB | $O(n^3)$ | fixed grid + Cauchy | Cauchy combination |
| SPARK-X | Covariance structure test | non-parametric | $O(n)$ | fixed grid | projection test |
| nnSVG | NNGP, variance components | Gaussian (transformed) | $O(nm^3)$ | gene-specific | LRT $\chi^2$ |
| SOMDE | SOM compression + GP | Gaussian | $O(k^3)$, $k \ll n$ | gene-specific | LRT $\chi^2$ |
| Trendsceek | marked point process | permutation | $O(n^2 \cdot B)$ | implicit | resampling |

The table's message is the field's central tension: **flexibility versus scale**. Only nnSVG and SPARK-X scale linearly, and only nnSVG among them keeps the gene-specific length scale that the DLPFC experiments show is biologically load-bearing [1].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Gaussian processes as the lingua franca of spatial variation

Strip away the platform branding and every SVG method answers one question: does a model with spatial covariance explain the data better than one without? The GP formulation makes the assumptions explicit and therefore testable. The kernel choice encodes a belief about *smoothness*: the squared-exponential kernel assumes infinitely differentiable fields, the Matérn family parameterizes roughness via $\nu$, and periodic kernels capture repeating structures such as somites or cortical columns.

The gene-specific length scale $\ell_g$ deserves emphasis because it is the parameter most often sacrificed on the altar of scalability, and the one whose loss hurts most. In the human DLPFC data, laminar markers (e.g., *MOBP*, *SNAP25*) operate at length scales spanning millimeters, while blood-associated transcripts vary over hundreds of microns [1]. A method with a single fixed $\ell$ is a microscope with one magnification: it cannot be optimal for both. nnSVG's central contribution is therefore not only computational — it is the demonstration that linear scaling *and* gene-specific $\ell_g$ can coexist through the NNGP factorization [1].

> **Theorem (Variance decomposition identifiability):** In the model $y \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu\mathbf{1}, \sigma_s^2 K(\ell) + \sigma_e^2 I)$, the triple $(\sigma_s^2, \sigma_e^2, \ell)$ is identifiable if and only if the spatial design $S$ contains at least three distinct inter-point distances and $K(\cdot)$ is strictly positive definite. Degenerate designs (perfect grids at a single spacing) admit a ridge of equivalent parameterizations — a caution for bead arrays with rigid lattice geometry.

```haskell
-- Kernel matrix assembly, functional style
seKernel :: Double -> [(Double,Double)] -> [[Double]]
seKernel ell pts =
  [ [ exp (negate (dist2 p q) / (2 * ell * ell)) | q <- pts ] | p <- pts ]
  where dist2 (x1,y1) (x2,y2) = (x1-x2)^2 + (y1-y2)^2

-- Likelihood ratio against the null (sigma_s^2 = 0)
lrt :: [Double] -> [[Double]] -> Double
lrt y kFull = 2 * (logLikFull - logLikNull)
```

### 4.2 Graph-based inference of tissue architecture

If SVG detection asks *which genes* vary with space, domain segmentation asks *where the tissue breaks*. The natural object is a **spatial graph**: nodes are spots or cells, edges connect spatial neighbors (Delaunay triangulation, $k$-nearest neighbors, or distance thresholds), and edge weights encode proximity. Tissue architecture then becomes a problem in **graph-partitioned latent variable modeling**.

The Bayesian workhorse is a hidden Markov random field: each node carries a latent domain label $z_i \in \lbrace 1, \dots, K \rbrace$, expression $y_i \mid z_i$ follows a domain-specific distribution, and labels are smoothed by a Potts prior

$$p(z) \propto \exp\left(\beta \sum_{(i,j) \in E} \mathbf{1}[z_i = z_j]\right),$$

with $\beta$ controlling spatial coherence.

The graph view also unifies SVG detection: Moran's $I$ is exactly a quadratic form on the spatial adjacency matrix, $I \propto y^\top W y$, and the GP covariance $K$ is the graph's continuous limit.

### 4.3 Cell–cell communication as collective optimal transport

Cell–cell communication inference begins with a ligand–receptor database and asks which cell pairs actually exchange signals.

COMMOT reformulates the problem as **collective optimal transport** [4]: all ligand and receptor species are transported simultaneously over a cost manifold defined by physical distance, with capacity constraints modeling competition — a stronger interaction with one partner reduces capacity for others.

SpaOTsc pioneered the OT route earlier, solving two sequential transport problems: first mapping dissociated scRNA-seq cells onto spatial positions (source to target distributions with expression-dissimilarity cost), then transporting sender distributions to receiver distributions with distance-defined cost, and finally estimating each pathway's spatial signaling range with a random-forest model [6].

```tla
---- MODULE CommOT ----
EXTENDS Reals, FiniteSets
CONSTANTS Spots, Species
VARIABLES plan
\* plan[s][t][k] : mass of species k transported from spot s to t
TypeOK == plan \in [Spots -> [Spots -> [Species -> Real]]]
CostOK(c) == \A s,t \in Spots, k \in Species :
    plan[s][t][k] >= 0
CapacityOK(cap) == \A t \in Spots, k \in Species :
    Sum({plan[s][t][k] : s \in Spots}) <= cap[t][k]
====
```

### 4.4 Alignment, deconvolution, and the multi-section problem

Real atlases stitch many tissue sections.

$$\sum_{i,j,k,l} \big|d_S(i,k) - d_{S'}(j,l)\big|^2 \Pi_{ij}\Pi_{kl} + \lambda \sum_{i,j} c_{ij} \Pi_{ij}$$

simultaneously respects molecular similarity (cost $c$) and geometric distortion (the Gromov term), yielding alignments robust to section rotation, tearing, and partial overlap.

**Deconvolution** addresses the spot-mixture problem: each Visium spot integrates several cells, so spot-level SVG signals conflate composition with regulation. Methods like RCTD and cell2location model each spot as a mixture over reference cell-type signatures, separating *which cells are where* from *what each cell expresses*. The statistical payoff is large: apparent SVGs that merely track cell-type proportions (compositional artifacts) are distinguished from genuine spatially regulated genes.

### 4.5 A unified probabilistic scaffold

The three problems share one scaffold.

1. **Observation**: $Y_{ig} \sim \text{NB}(\mu_{ig}(Z, \Pi), \phi_g)$ — counts with domain- and signaling-dependent means.
2. **Spatial field**: $\log \mu_{\cdot g} = f_g(S) + X\beta_g$, $f_g \sim \mathcal{GP}(0, \sigma_{s,g}^2 K(\ell_g))$ — the SVG layer.
3. **Architecture**: $Z \sim \text{Potts}(\beta)$ on the spatial graph — the domain layer.
4. **Communication**: $\Pi = \arg\min$ collective OT over distance cost with ligand/receptor capacities — the signaling layer.

Fitting all four jointly is the field's open frontier; current practice fits them in a pipeline.

## 5 Empirical/Proofs

### 5.1 Complexity: from cubic to linear, with proofs of concept

The GP Cholesky is $O(n^3)$ time and $O(n^2)$ memory. The NNGP reduces this to $O(nm^3)$ with $m \approx 10$: for $n = 53{,}208$ Slide-seqV2 locations, nnSVG completes transcriptome-wide SVG testing in roughly five hours on a single core, while SpatialDE and SPARK cannot be run at all [1]. The empirical scaling study in the nnSVG paper subsamples spots from 200 to 53,208 and shows a clean linear runtime trend versus the cubic blowup of the predecessors — the rare case where the theory plot and the wall-clock plot agree [1].

### 5.2 Calibration under the null

A method that cries "spatial" at noise is worse than no method. SPARK's Cauchy combination was introduced precisely because naive kernel-averaging inflates type I error; the resulting *p*-values are empirically uniform under permutation nulls [2]. nnSVG's likelihood-ratio *p*-values show the same calibration in null simulations, and the method's SVG rankings are stable under varying stringency of low-expression filtering — the top hits are highly expressed genes whose rankings barely move [1].

### 5.3 Biological ground truth: cortical layers and beyond

The human DLPFC Visium dataset is the field's de facto benchmark: cortical layers L1–L6 plus white matter provide histological ground truth. nnSVG recovers known laminar markers with correct layer specificity and — the discriminating result — simultaneously identifies fine-scale vascular and immune SVGs at small $\ell_g$ that fixed-scale methods miss [1]. In mouse olfactory bulb (Spatial Transcriptomics platform) and mouse embryo (seqFISH), the same pattern holds: gene-specific length scales recover biology at multiple spatial frequencies within one dataset [1].

### 5.4 Communication: PDE-validated transport

Because true ligand–receptor co-localization cannot be measured at scale, COMMOT's authors built PDE models simulating diffusion, binding, and competition in space, then asked whether inference recovers the simulated connections. COMMOT reconstructs the ground-truth signaling network across varying numbers of ligand/receptor species and competition patterns, and is statistically distinct from — and superior to — unbalanced and partial OT variants that lack the collective competition structure [4]. The downstream vector-field interpolation further identifies signaling direction and CCC-affected target genes, closing the loop from transport plan to biological hypothesis [4]. Complementary ligand-diffusion-and-transport models extend this picture by modeling the physical spread of secreted ligands explicitly, capturing spatial decay of signaling strength that pure expression-based scoring misses [7].

## 6 Limitations

1. **The dropout–overdispersion tangle.** Zero inflation in spatial counts conflates technical dropout with genuine absence. GP methods on transformed data and count GLMMs make different, untestable assumptions about the zero-generating process; sensitivity analyses are rare and badly needed.

2. **Stationarity is a fiction.** Squared-exponential and Matérn kernels assume the field's covariance depends only on lag, yet tissues are emphatically non-stationary — the cortex has layers, the tumor has a necrotic core. Non-stationary kernels and deep GP warps exist but are absent from production SVG pipelines.

3. **Anisotropy.** Signaling and structure have directions (cortical columns, axon tracts, invasion fronts). Isotropic kernels smear directional patterns; anisotropic extensions multiply parameters and are rarely fit.

4. **Resolution versus identity.** Spot-based platforms convolve cell-type composition with regulation; deconvolution helps but inherits reference bias. Imaging platforms resolve cells but sample few genes. No current platform gives both, so every analysis chooses its blindness.

5. **Identifiability of communication.** Ligand and receptor mRNA are proxies for protein activity; post-translational regulation, cofactor requirements, and receptor internalization are invisible to transcriptomics. OT plans quantify *potential* signaling, not realized signaling — a distinction the literature sometimes blurs [4][5].

6. **The pipeline problem.** SVG detection, domain segmentation, deconvolution, and CCC are fit sequentially, so errors propagate forward with no joint uncertainty. The unified scaffold of §4.5 is aspirational; variational or MCMC joint inference at atlas scale remains out of reach.

## 7 Conclusion

Spatial transcriptomics has matured from a collection of striking images into a discipline with theorems: likelihood-ratio tests with known null distributions, transport plans with PDE-validated recovery, complexity bounds that separate the scalable from the ornamental. Three mathematical ideas carry most of the weight — the Gaussian process variance decomposition that turns "is this gene spatial?" into a testable hypothesis, the spatial graph that turns tissue architecture into a partitioning problem, and collective optimal transport that turns cell–cell communication into a resource-allocation problem with competition built in.

The through-line of the empirical record is that **scale parameters are biological parameters**: gene-specific length scales, distance-dependent signaling costs, and multi-resolution domain structure are not modeling luxuries but the quantities the tissue is actually written in. Methods that fix them for computational convenience systematically miss the fine structure — the vasculature, the immune infiltrate, the directional signal — where much of the actionable biology lives.

The next decade belongs to joint models that fit the field, the domains, and the transport plans together with honest uncertainty, to non-stationary and anisotropic covariances that respect tissue directionality, and to the integration of protein-level measurements that will finally let us test whether the inferred conversations actually happened. The map is being drawn; the mathematics is learning to read it.
