For seventeen years Linux scheduled CPUs with the Completely Fair Scheduler (CFS), a weighted-fair-queueing design built on a red-black tree keyed by virtual runtime. This thesis traces the two revolutions that followed: EEVDF (Earliest Eligible Virtual Deadline First), which replaced CFS's accumulated latency heuristics with a principled lag-and-virtual-deadline formulation derived from Stoica and Abdel-Wahab's 1995 proportional-share algorithm, becoming the sole fair class in Linux 6.12; and sched_ext, the BPF-based extensible scheduling class that moves scheduling policy out of the kernel into verified, loadable eBPF programs with automatic EEVDF fallback. We derive the governing equations of vruntime accumulation, lag-based eligibility, virtual-deadline selection, and dispatch-queue dispatch; profile the scx scheduler family including throughput-oriented scx_rusty, latency-aware scx_lavd, and desktop-tuned scx_bpfland; and synthesize published benchmark evidence comparing latency, throughput, and robustness across the three eras.

# From Virtual Runtime to Programmable Policy: CFS, EEVDF, and the sched_ext Revolution in Linux CPU Scheduling

## 1 Introduction

Few subsystems in an operating system carry as much responsibility as the CPU scheduler. Every runnable thread in a Linux system — from an audio callback with a 2&nbsp;ms deadline to a compiler saturating thirty-two cores — passes through the scheduling class hierarchy on every context switch. For seventeen years, Linux answered the question "which thread runs next?" with the **Completely Fair Scheduler (CFS)**: a weighted-fair-queueing approximation built on a red-black tree keyed by *virtual runtime* [1][2]. In 2023–2024 the fair class was rewritten around **EEVDF** (*Earliest Eligible Virtual Deadline First*), a proportional-share algorithm dating to Stoica and Abdel-Wahab's 1995 paper [8], replacing accumulated heuristics with a principled notion of *lag* and *virtual deadlines*. And in November 2024, Linux 6.12 merged **sched_ext**, a BPF-based extensible scheduling class that lets operators load entirely new schedulers — `scx_rusty`, `scx_lavd`, `scx_bpfland` — as signed eBPF programs at runtime, without rebuilding the kernel [5][6].

This thesis presents a unified treatment of these three eras. We reconstruct the mathematical machinery of CFS (virtual time scaling, weight tables, `vruntime` accumulation, and the rbtree ordering invariant), derive EEVDF's lag-based eligibility and deadline computation from first principles and show why the Linux implementation removes entire families of CFS heuristics, and dissect sched_ext's `struct_ops` dispatch architecture — the DSQ (dispatch queue) abstraction, the enable/disable lifecycle, and the safety contract that allows a BPF program to fail over to EEVDF without crashing the machine. Along the way we examine representative schedulers in the `sched-ext/scx` tree [6]: the throughput-oriented *scx_rusty*, the latency-aware *scx_lavd*, and the desktop-tuned *scx_bpfland*, and we survey published benchmark evidence comparing them against the default fair class [7].

The remainder of this work is organized as follows. Section&nbsp;2 surveys scheduling theory background. Section&nbsp;3 describes our methodology. Section&nbsp;4 is the deep technical core. Section&nbsp;5 presents empirical evidence. Section&nbsp;6 discusses limitations, and Section&nbsp;7 concludes.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Fairness in single-processor scheduling

The ideal against which Linux's fair class measures itself is **Generalized Processor Sharing (GPS)** [3]: a fluid model in which each backlogged flow *i* with weight *w_i* receives instantaneous service rate *w_i / Σ_j w_j*. Real schedulers approximate GPS with packetized disciplines; the classical result is **Weighted Fair Queueing (WFQ)** and its virtual-time formulations (PGPS) by Parekh and Gallager [3], which bound the deviation between the fluid and packet systems.

> **Theorem: (Parekh–Gallager GPS emulation).** A packetized scheduler that serves packets in order of their GPS virtual finish times satisfies |W_i(τ) − Ŵ_i(τ)| ≤ Lmax / r for every flow *i*, where *W_i* and *Ŵ_i* are the service in the fluid and packet systems, *Lmax* is the maximum packet size, and *r* is the link rate [3].

CPU scheduling differs from packet scheduling in one essential way: there is no natural "packet size." A thread's *time slice* is chosen by the scheduler itself, and preemption can occur at essentially arbitrary points. This freedom — and the cost of context switches — shapes every design decision below.

### 2.2 Nice values, weights, and load

Linux exposes scheduling priority through *nice* values in [−20, 19]. CFS converts each nice value to a weight via a fixed table anchored at `NICE_0_LOAD = 1024`, with each level separated by a factor of roughly 1.25 (weights 88761 for −20 down to 15 for +19). A task's *load* is its weight multiplied by a CPU-capacity factor, and load tracking uses **PELT** (Per-Entity Load Tracking) — a geometric series with ~32&nbsp;ms half-life — smoothing instantaneous utilization into `util_avg`, `load_avg`, and `runnable_avg` signals consumed by the load balancer, the `schedutil` frequency governor, and energy-aware scheduling [9].

### 2.3 The road to sched_ext

CFS's success masked a growing problem: latency-sensitive workloads (gaming, audio, interactive desktop) were repeatedly forced to fight fairness machinery with tunables — `sched_latency`, `sched_min_granularity`, `sched_wakeup_granularity`, sleeper fairness bonuses, autogroup, and others — each heuristic interacting with the rest. Concurrently, the rise of eBPF demonstrated that kernel policy could be safely programmable: verified, JIT-compiled programs with bounded execution. Tejun Heo's **sched_ext** proposal (developed publicly from 2023) argued that *scheduling policy is the wrong thing to hard-code*; the kernel should provide mechanism (dispatch, migration, wakeup, preemption) while policy lives in loadable BPF programs with a safe fallback [5].

## 3 Methodology

This study combines three research methods:

1. **Primary-source analysis.** We read the Linux implementation in `kernel/sched/fair.c` (EEVDF as of v6.12), the sched_ext core in `kernel/sched/ext.c`, and the in-tree documentation `Documentation/scheduler/sched-design-CFS.rst` and `Documentation/scheduler/sched-ext.rst`, together with the original algorithm papers [3][8] and LWN's coverage of the EEVDF merge [4] and the sched_ext LPC 2024 discussions.
2. **Architectural reconstruction.** From these sources we derive the governing equations — vruntime accumulation, lag and virtual-deadline computation, DSQ dispatch — and restate them with consistent notation so the three designs can be compared directly.
3. **Benchmark synthesis.** We aggregate publicly reported measurements from the `sched-ext/scx` project, independent test harnesses [7], and Phoronix reporting on EEVDF refinements in recent kernels [10], and we present a normalized comparison across latency, throughput, and robustness axes.

We deliberately avoid presenting our own kernel benchmarks as reproducible claims; instead §5 explains what the existing evidence shows and where its limits lie.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 CFS: virtual runtime and the red-black runqueue

The central abstraction of CFS is **virtual runtime** (`vruntime`). When a task *i* executes for a physical interval Δ*t*, its virtual clock advances by

```
ΔV_i = Δt · (NICE_0_LOAD / w_i)
```

so that a task with twice the weight accumulates virtual time half as fast. Fairness then reduces to an ordering problem: *keep the vruntime values of all runnable tasks as close together as possible*. The task with the smallest vruntime has received the least weighted service and should run next.

To support "pick the minimum" efficiently under frequent insertions and deletions, each per-CPU `cfs_rq` stores its `sched_entity` nodes in a **red-black tree keyed by vruntime** [1][2]. Insertion and removal are *O(log n)*; picking the next task is *O(1)* via the cached leftmost node. This is the famous data structure choice that made CFS self-explanatory at a glance: *the leftmost node is the most deserving task on the CPU*.

A tick-driven accounting path, `update_curr()`, charges the running entity's `sum_exec_runtime` and advances its vruntime, after which the entity is typically reinserted at its new key. Two refinements mattered enormously in practice:

- **Sleeper fairness.** A task that slept should not be punished for CPU time it could not have consumed; on wakeup, CFS granted it a vruntime bonus (via `place_entity()` with `initial = 1`, capping vruntime at `min_vruntime − thresh`). This was latency-critical — without it, interactive tasks woke into the far right of the tree.
- **Granularity and latency targets.** `sched_latency` (default 6&nbsp;ms, scaled up when runnable tasks exceed `sched_latency / sched_min_granularity`) bounded the scheduling period, while `sched_min_granularity` (default 0.75&nbsp;ms) bounded the minimum slice, trading fairness resolution against context-switch overhead.

> **Theorem: (CFS fairness bound, informal).** With bounded slices and the leftmost-pick discipline, the difference in *weighted* service between any two continuously runnable tasks is bounded by one scheduling period's worth of vruntime spread, i.e., fairness error is *O(sched_latency)* in virtual time [2].

The weakness of CFS was not its fairness but its **latency model**: virtual runtime measures *share received*, not *urgency*. A freshly woken interactive task with a moderate vruntime could still wait behind CPU hogs, and the accumulated heuristics (sleeper credit, wakeup preemption granularity, `sched_migration_cost`) interacted in ways that were hard to reason about — precisely the motivation for EEVDF [4].

### 4.2 EEVDF: lag, eligibility, and virtual deadlines

EEVDF replaces "smallest vruntime runs next" with a cleaner formulation built on **lag**. For each entity, define

```
lag_i(t) = S_i(t) − R_i(t)
```

where *S_i(t)* is the weighted service the task *should* have received by time *t* (its ideal GPS share) and *R_i(t)* is what it actually received. A positive lag means the task is *owed* CPU; a negative lag means it has consumed *more* than its share.

**Eligibility.** An entity is *eligible* iff `lag_i ≥ 0`. Ineligible tasks (negative lag) must wait until their entitlement catches up with their consumption — a future instant called the *eligible time*. This single rule replaces CFS's sleeper-fairness machinery: a woken task naturally has positive lag (it received nothing while entitled), so it is eligible immediately, while a hog that over-consumed is gated out without special cases [4].

**Virtual deadlines.** Each entity carries a *request* *r_i* — the time slice it asks for, derived in Linux from an EWMA of its past run durations (`avg_vruntime`-based request tracking). Its *virtual deadline* is

```
d_i = eligible_time_i + r_i / w_i      (weight-normalized)
```

and among eligible entities the scheduler picks the **earliest virtual deadline** — hence the name. If no entity is eligible, it picks the one with the largest lag (least negative), guaranteeing progress.

**Time slices.** EEVDF computes the slice as `min(sched_slice, 2 · r_i)`-style bounds with a lag-based clamp: a task may run at most until its lag would go negative plus a bounded overrun, which removes the CFS granularity knobs from the latency path. The Linux implementation (Peter Zijlstra's 2023 series, merged for 6.6 as an option and made the sole fair scheduler in 6.12 [10]) expresses lag in virtual-time units inside `update_entity_lag()` and orders the rbtree by *deadline* rather than raw vruntime for the pick path, while retaining vruntime as the underlying clock.

The conceptual payoff is significant: *latency* (how soon a woken task runs) and *fairness* (long-run share) are now controlled by orthogonal quantities — deadlines versus lag — instead of being conflated in one vruntime number plus a stack of heuristics [4].

```c
/* Simplified sketch of the EEVDF pick path (kernel/sched/fair.c) */
static struct sched_entity *pick_eevdf(struct cfs_rq *cfs_rq)
{
	struct rb_node *node = cfs_rq->tasks_timeline.rb_root.rb_node;
	struct sched_entity *curr = cfs_rq->curr, *best = NULL;
	s64 best_lag = 0;

	/* Among eligible entities (lag >= 0), pick earliest virtual deadline */
	while (node) {
		struct sched_entity *se = rb_entry(node, struct sched_entity, run_node);
		if (entity_eligible(cfs_rq, se)) {
			if (!best || entity_before(best, se))
				best = se;
			node = node->rb_left;   /* deadlines ordered in-tree */
		} else {
			if (!best || se->vlag > best_lag) { best = se; best_lag = se->vlag; }
			node = node->rb_right;
		}
	}
	return best ?: curr;
}
```

### 4.3 sched_ext: BPF-programmable scheduling classes

If EEVDF is the triumph of *principled* in-kernel policy, **sched_ext** is the admission that one policy cannot serve all masters. Merged in Linux 6.12 after extensive review, sched_ext adds a new scheduling class (`SCHED_EXT`) implemented through BPF `struct_ops` [5]. The architecture decomposes scheduling into *mechanism* (stays in the kernel: task lifecycle, migration, timers, the fallback EEVDF path) and *policy* (moves into a verified BPF program plus a user-space loader daemon):

- **`struct sched_ext_ops`.** The BPF program implements callbacks: `select_cpu` (placement at wakeup), `enqueue`/`dequeue` (admission to dispatch queues), `dispatch` (assigning queued tasks to CPUs), `runnable`/`stopping`, `cpu_acquire`/`cpu_release`, and `update_idle`. These run in sleepable BPF context with strict verifier constraints — bounded loops, no unbounded allocation — keeping scheduling decisions deterministic and crash-safe.
- **DSQs (dispatch queues).** Tasks live in per-scheduler *dispatch queues*; `scx_bpf_dispatch()` moves them from the global DSQ to per-CPU DSQs, and `scx_bpf_consume()` lets a CPU pull work. This two-level queueing gives BPF schedulers explicit control over locality without reimplementing migration machinery.
- **Lifecycle and fallback.** A scheduler is loaded by a user-space daemon (e.g., `scx_rusty`) that pins the BPF program; `/sys/kernel/sched_ext/state` reports `enabled`/`disabled`. If the daemon exits or the BPF program errors, the kernel atomically reverts every task to the built-in EEVDF class — *scheduling policy becomes a process you can kill* [5]. `switch_all` controls whether all tasks or only opt-in tasks (`sched_setattr`) use the BPF class.

```c
/* Sketch of a minimal sched_ext ops table (BPF, from scx_simple) */
struct sched_ext_ops simple_ops = {
	.select_cpu	= (void *)simple_select_cpu,
	.enqueue	= (void *)simple_enqueue,
	.dispatch	= (void *)simple_dispatch,
	.name		= "simple",
	/* tasks fall back to EEVDF automatically on unload */
};
```

### 4.4 The scx scheduler zoo: rusty, lavd, bpfland

The `sched-ext/scx` repository [6] ships a family of production-oriented schedulers that demonstrate how different policies exploit the same mechanism:

1. **`scx_rusty` — throughput-first.** Written in Rust, topology- and cache-aware: it keeps communicating threads within the same LLC/CCD, uses per-domain DSQs, and optimizes for batch throughput (kernel builds, HPC, compilation). Independent harness testing on CPU-bound Spring Boot workloads reported dramatically higher success rates and lower mean response times versus the default class [7].
2. **`scx_lavd` — latency-aware virtual deadline.** LAVD classifies tasks by observed behavior (latency-critical vs. regular vs. compute-bound) using per-task statistics, then applies virtual-deadline scheduling within classes — effectively *EEVDF with task-type awareness*. It targets gaming and interactive desktop use, where it prioritizes short-burst input/audio threads over bulk work. Meta notably evaluated LAVD-class designs for latency-sensitive fleets.
3. **`scx_bpfland` — desktop responsiveness.** A C BPF scheduler inspired by the out-of-tree PDS/MuQSS lineage: it emphasizes wakeup latency and interactive feel on desktop systems, with simpler per-CPU FIFO-ish dispatch and aggressive preemption of batch tasks by interactive ones.
4. **Supporting cast.** `scx_layered` (configurable multi-layer policies for multi-tenant hosts), `scx_flatcg` (cgroup-weighted fairness for containers), `scx_p2dq` (pick-two load balancing), and `scx_simple` (a didactic baseline). The taxonomy shows sched_ext's real value: *policy diversity as software, not kernel forks*.

| Scheduler | Primary goal | Language | Best workload |
|---|---|---|---|
| EEVDF (built-in) | Weighted fairness + low latency | C (in-kernel) | General purpose default |
| scx_rusty | Throughput, locality | Rust (BPF) | Builds, HPC, batch |
| scx_lavd | Latency for interactive tasks | Rust (BPF) | Gaming, desktop, latency SLOs |
| scx_bpfland | Wakeup responsiveness | C (BPF) | Interactive desktop |
| scx_layered | Multi-tenant isolation | Rust (BPF) | Cloud hosts, containers |

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 Correctness arguments

For EEVDF, Stoica and Abdel-Wahab's original analysis [8] proves that the algorithm bounds *lag* for every backlogged flow: no task's lag grows without bound, and the deviation from GPS service is bounded by a function of the maximum request size — the same *O(Lmax)*-style guarantee as PGPS [3], but achieved with the simpler eligibility test rather than virtual-time sorting of all packets. The Linux implementation preserves the invariant that *an entity's lag is computed against the minimum vruntime of the runqueue*, which keeps lag values comparable across entities with different weights [4].

For sched_ext, the "proof" is architectural rather than analytic: the verifier guarantees BPF program termination and memory safety; the kernel retains ultimate authority over preemption and migration; and the unload path provably restores the EEVDF class. The design thus reduces the safety argument for a new scheduler from "audit 50k lines of C" to "audit the ops callbacks plus the verifier's guarantees" [5].

### 5.2 Measured behavior

Published evidence paints a consistent picture:

- **EEVDF vs. CFS.** The EEVDF merge series included latency benchmarks (e.g., `schbench`, pipe-based wakeup tests) showing reduced tail latency for interactive tasks at equal throughput, which was the series' stated goal [4]. Follow-up cycles exposed regressions on some server workloads after 6.11, largely recovered by subsequent refinements from Zijlstra and others — including cache-aware placement work landing in the 7.x series [10].
- **sched_ext schedulers.** An independent QEMU-based harness [7] running a CPU-intensive Spring Boot banking workload (17,370 requests per configuration) reported: `scx_rusty` achieving ~95% success rate with ~1.15&nbsp;s mean response versus ~78% / ~1.67&nbsp;s for the default class, while `scx_lavd` traded throughput for zero-error stability and `scx_bpfland` underperformed on that batch workload — exactly what their design goals predict. The lesson is not "one scheduler wins" but that *workload-matched policy* wins, which is sched_ext's entire thesis.
- **Adoption signals.** Major distributions now ship sched_ext-enabled kernels with `scx_loader` services, and the `sched-ext/scx` tree continues to add schedulers (e.g., `scx_flash` for real-time characteristics), indicating the ecosystem is past the experimental stage [6].

## 6 Limitations

1. **EEVDF's blind spots.** Lag-based eligibility is still a *share* concept; it does not know that a task is an audio callback versus a video encoder. Task-type awareness requires either heuristics (which EEVDF sought to remove) or explicit classification — which is where `scx_lavd`'s statistics engine comes in, at the cost of reintroducing policy complexity.
2. **BPF verifier constraints.** Scheduler callbacks cannot block, allocate freely, or loop unboundedly. Sophisticated policies (e.g., global optimization over hundreds of CPUs) must be approximated with per-CPU or per-domain structures — DSQs are expressive but not a general programming environment.
3. **Overhead.** Crossing into BPF on every enqueue/dispatch adds instruction overhead versus the in-kernel fast path; on many-core systems the global DSQ can become a contention point, which is why production schedulers shard aggressively.
4. **Debuggability.** A misbehaving BPF scheduler manifests as system-wide latency or throughput anomalies with limited introspection; `scx` provides stats maps, but diagnosing policy bugs remains harder than reading `fair.c`.
5. **Benchmark fragility.** Scheduler benchmarks are notoriously workload- and topology-sensitive (the 7.2 cache-awareness results vary dramatically between AMD EPYC and Intel Xeon [10]); single-number claims should be treated with skepticism.

---

## 7 Conclusion

The trajectory from CFS to EEVDF to sched_ext traces a clear arc: from **fairness as a data structure** (the vruntime-ordered red-black tree), to **fairness as a principle** (lag and virtual deadlines, with heuristics deleted rather than added), to **fairness as a choice** (policy compiled to BPF and loaded like any other program, with EEVDF as the eternal fallback). Each step preserved what worked — virtual time remains the accounting currency throughout — while moving one layer of mechanism up into replaceable policy. For practitioners, the actionable summary is simple: stay on EEVDF for the general case, reach for `scx_rusty` when throughput and locality dominate, `scx_lavd` when tail latency is the SLO, and `scx_bpfland` for interactive desktops — and expect this menu, not the kernel version, to be where scheduling innovation happens next.
