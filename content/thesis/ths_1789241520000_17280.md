# Active Dendrites and the Multilayer Neuron: Dendritic Spikes, Compartmental Models, Cable Theory, and the Single-Neuron Deep Network Hypothesis

## Abstract

The point-neuron abstraction that underwrites most of computational neuroscience and machine learning — a neuron as a linear integrator followed by a threshold — collapses under the weight of dendritic electrophysiology. This thesis develops a rigorous account of the pyramidal neuron as a *multilayer* computational device: dendritic branches act as independent nonlinear subunits whose regenerative events (NMDA spikes, sodium and calcium spikes, and the graded dendritic calcium action potentials recently described in human layer 2/3 cortex) compose hierarchically before a final somatic threshold. We review Rall's cable theory [4] and its modern compartmental descendants, survey the experimental record from Stuart and Sakmann's active dendrites [5] through Schiller's NMDA spikes [8] to Gidon *et al.*'s human dCaAPs that solve linearly nonseparable problems in a single cell [1], and formalize the single-neuron deep network hypothesis — the two-layer abstraction of Poirazi, Brannon, and Mel [2] and its extension to a 5–8-layer temporal-convolutional mapping by Beniaguev, London, and Segev [3] — deriving why NMDA-based nonlinearities supply the bulk of the depth.

## 1 Introduction

For most of its history, theoretical neuroscience has treated the neuron as a glorified perceptron: dendrites passively conduct, the soma sums, and an axonal spike issues when the sum crosses threshold. This caricature was never fully believed — Rall's cable analysis in the 1960s already showed that passive dendrites reshape synaptic potentials in geometry-dependent ways [4] — but it persisted because it was tractable. Over the past three decades, dual somatodendritic recordings, two-photon calcium imaging, and compartmental modeling have dismantled it entirely. Dendrites host regenerative sodium, calcium, and NMDA spikes; thin terminal branches behave as semi-independent computational subunits; and a single human cortical neuron can classify linearly nonseparable inputs that a textbook perceptron cannot [1][2][3].

This thesis argues for the *multilayer neuron* as the correct default abstraction. The argument proceeds in four movements. First, we establish the physical substrate: the cable equation, Rall's equivalent-cylinder reductions, and the equalizing time constants that govern how synaptic charge redistributes across a tree [4]. Second, we catalog the active events — NMDA spikes, fast sodium spikes, plateau and calcium spikes, and the graded dendritic calcium action potentials (dCaAPs) discovered in human cortex — as distinct nonlinear operators with distinct timescales and logic [1][5][8]. Third, we develop the computational reading: the two-layer network of Poirazi *et al.* [2], generalized by Beniaguev *et al.* to a five-to-eight-layer temporally convolutional network whose depth is attributable, provably, to NMDA conductances [3]. Finally, we confront the limits — generalization, metabolic cost, and the distance between a fitted DNN and a mechanistic explanation — and extract design principles for neuromorphic hardware.

> **Theorem (Informal):** A single pyramidal neuron equipped with branch-local regenerative conductances implements a feedforward network of at least two hidden layers, and its effective depth grows with the diversity of its dendritic nonlinearities, reaching 5–8 temporal-convolutional layers for a layer 5 pyramidal cell with intact NMDA receptors [2][3].

## 2 Background

### 2.1 From the perceptron to the distributed tree

The McCulloch–Pitts neuron computes $y = \sigma(\sum_i w_i x_i + b)$: linear summation, then a static nonlinearity. Its biological justification rested on two premises: (i) dendrites are passive cables that deliver a weighted sum to the soma, and (ii) the only relevant nonlinearity is the axonal spike. Premise (i) is the target of cable theory; premise (ii) is the target of every result in Section 4.

A pyramidal neuron's dendritic arbor is not a wire but a tree of coupled cables. Each branch has membrane resistance $R_m$, capacitance $C_m$, and axial resistivity $R_i$; a synapse injects conductance, not current; and the resulting potentials attenuate with distance according to the space constant $\lambda = \sqrt{a R_m / 2 R_i}$ for a cable of radius $a$. Distal inputs arrive at the soma slower, broader, and smaller than proximal ones — a fact Rall derived analytically in 1962 and that remains the null hypothesis against which every claim of "dendritic computation" must be tested [4].

### 2.2 Rall's cable theory and the equivalent cylinder

Rall's achievement was to make branching trees analytically tractable. Under the famous $3/2$ power rule — that at a branch point, $d_{\text{parent}}^{3/2} = \sum d_{\text{daughters}}^{3/2}$ — an entire dendritic tree collapses exactly into a single equivalent cylinder. From the cable equation

$$\tau_m \frac{\partial V}{\partial t} = \lambda^2 \frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial x^2} - V + R_m I_{\text{inj}},$$

with membrane time constant $\tau_m = R_m C_m$, Rall extracted the equalizing time constants

$$\frac{\tau_0}{\tau_n} = 1 + \left(\frac{n\pi}{L}\right)^2, \qquad n = 1, 2, \dots$$

where $\tau_0 = \tau_m$ and $L$ is the electrotonic length of the tree [4]. The ratio $\tau_0/\tau_1$ estimates $L$ directly from somatic transients — the celebrated "peeling" method that turned voltage-clamp records into anatomical inferences.

Key classical results that frame everything below:

- **Distal–proximal asymmetry:** distal EPSPs are slower, broader, and attenuated at the soma, but *not* negligible; cat spinal motoneurons have electrotonic lengths $L \approx 1$–$2$, making distal synapses effective [4].
- **Sealed-end reflections:** terminal branches reflect charge, amplifying local depolarization and setting up the conditions for regenerative spikes.
- **Branch-point impedance mismatch:** thin daughter branches have high input impedance, so small synaptic currents produce large local depolarizations — the enabling physics of NMDA spikes.

### 2.3 The catalog of dendritic nonlinearities

| Event | Primary conductance | Timescale | Signature property |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fast dendritic Na$^+$ spike | Voltage-gated Na$^+$ | ~1–2 ms | All-or-none, attenuates with distance; sharpens feature selectivity [6] |
| NMDA spike / plateau | NMDA receptors (Mg$^{2+}$ unblock) | 20–200 ms | Graded, long plateau; requires clustered glutamate; branch-local [8] |
| Dendritic Ca$^{2+}$ spike | Voltage-gated Ca$^{2+}$ (L/T-type) | 10–50 ms | Apical-tuft initiation, couples to somatic burst [7] |
| dCaAP (human L2/3) | Ca$^{2+}$-mediated | ~50 ms | *Graded*, maximal at threshold stimuli, damped by strong stimuli; enables XOR-like classification [1] |

The NMDA spike deserves emphasis: because Mg$^{2+}$ block of the NMDA receptor is relieved by depolarization, clustered synaptic input to a thin branch produces a regenerative plateau whose amplitude is *supralinear* in the input count [8]. Polsky, Mel, and Schiller showed that basal dendrites of layer 5 pyramidal cells operate as independent computational subunits with sigmoid input–output functions — the experimental realization of Poirazi's hidden layer [2][8].

## 3 Methodology

Our treatment synthesizes three methodological traditions rather than reporting a single new experiment.

**Compartmental modeling.** We adopt the standard Hodgkin–Huxley-type multicompartment formalism: each segment obeys $C_m dV_i/dt = -g_L(V_i - E_L) - \sum_j g_j(V_i - E_j) + \sum_k (V_k - V_i)/R_{ik} + I_{\text{syn},i}(t)$. Morphologies are realistic reconstructions; passive parameters follow Rall's conventions ($R_m \approx 20{,}000\,\Omega\text{cm}^2$, $R_i \approx 100\,\Omega\text{cm}$, $C_m = 1\,\mu\text{F/cm}^2$); active conductances (Na$^+$, K$^+$, Ca$^{2+}$, NMDA) are distributed with the density gradients measured in pyramidal dendrites [5][8].

**Electrophysiological characterization.** Dual soma–dendrite patch recordings and two-photon imaging provide the ground truth for spike initiation zones, propagation velocities, and attenuation profiles [1][5]. We treat these as constraints on, not outputs of, the models.

**DNN-based I/O complexity characterization.** Following Beniaguev *et al.* [3], we define the computational complexity of a neuron operationally: the minimal deep network that replicates its spike-level input–output mapping at millisecond resolution on spatiotemporal synaptic patterns. Ablation (removing NMDA receptors, then all active conductances) isolates each mechanism's contribution to effective depth. This is a *measurement* of complexity, not a claim that the neuron "contains" a backpropagation-trained network — a distinction we return to in Section 6.

```python
# Sketch of the Poirazi two-layer abstraction (Poirazi et al., 2003)
import numpy as np

def sigmoid(z):
    return 1.0 / (1.0 + np.exp(-z))

def pyramidal_two_layer(synaptic_inputs, branch_weights, branch_thresholds, soma_threshold):
    """First layer: independent sigmoidal dendritic subunits (thin branches).
    Second layer: summation at soma/trunk + final threshold."""
    subunit_out = sigmoid(branch_weights @ synaptic_inputs - branch_thresholds)  # hidden layer
    soma_potential = subunit_out.sum()                                            # linear trunk
    return float(soma_potential >= soma_threshold)                               # axonal threshold
```

The Haskell encoding makes the compositional structure explicit:

```haskell
-- A neuron as a fold over dendritic subunits (two-layer reading)
type Synapse = Double
type Subunit = [Synapse] -> Double          -- sigmoid branch nonlinearity
type Neuron  = [Subunit] -> Double -> Double -- sum + threshold

pyramidal :: [Subunit] -> Double -> [[Synapse]] -> Double
pyramidal subunits theta inputs =
  let hidden = zipWith ($) subunits inputs   -- hidden layer: branch sigmoids
      trunk  = sum hidden                    -- linear dendritic trunk
  in if trunk >= theta then 1.0 else 0.0     -- somatic threshold
```

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 NMDA spikes: the branch as a sigmoid subunit

NMDA spikes are the workhorse nonlinearity of thin dendrites. In basal dendrites of cortical pyramidal neurons, Schiller *et al.* demonstrated that focal glutamate uncaging evokes regenerative, long-lasting depolarizations mediated by NMDA receptors [8]. The mechanism is a positive feedback loop: depolarization relieves Mg$^{2+}$ block, admitting more Ca$^{2+}$ and Na$^+$, which depolarizes further. Because the loop gain depends on the *local* voltage, only spatially clustered inputs — those landing on the same thin branch — cooperate; inputs scattered across branches sum linearly.

Three properties make the NMDA spike a hidden-unit nonlinearity:

1. **Thresholded sigmoidality.** Below a critical input number the branch responds linearly; above it, the plateau saturates — a sigmoid in disguise [8].
2. **Branch locality.** High input impedance of thin branches confines the event; neighboring branches are unaffected, yielding dozens of independent subunits per cell [2][8].
3. **Temporal integration.** Plateau durations of tens to hundreds of milliseconds bridge asynchronous inputs into a common output window, implementing a slow AND over time.

Poirazi, Brannon, and Mel formalized this in a compartmental CA1 model: with dozens of high-frequency-activated synapses distributed across apical dendrites, the cell's firing rate was predicted by a two-layer network whose hidden units *are* the thin terminal dendrites [2]. The mapping is not metaphorical — branch identity, sigmoid shape, and summation weights were all fit from the biophysical model.

### 4.2 Apical coupling, calcium spikes, and burst generation

The apical dendrite of layer 5 pyramidal neurons adds a second, spatially distinct nonlinearity. Larkum and colleagues showed that distal tuft input coinciding with somatic spiking within a narrow window triggers a dendritic Ca$^{2+}$ spike that converts the somatic output to a high-frequency burst — coincidence detection implemented by the BAC (backpropagation-activated calcium) mechanism [7][8]. Dendritic Ca$^{2+}$ spikes are also directly detectable as surface-positive potentials in the awake brain, implying they are a routine, not exotic, mode of cortical signaling [7].

Functionally, this is a *multiplicative* interaction between two input streams: feedforward drive to basal/proximal dendrites and feedback/contextual drive to the apical tuft. In network terms, the apical compartment implements a context-gated hidden layer — the physiological substrate of the "apical amplification" hypothesis in which top-down predictions modulate bottom-up evidence.

> **Theorem (BAC coincidence):** Let $V_{\text{tuft}}(t)$ and $V_{\text{soma}}(t)$ denote tuft and somatic potentials. A dendritic Ca$^{2+}$ spike, and consequent somatic burst, is initiated iff $V_{\text{tuft}} > \theta_{\text{Ca}}$ within $\Delta t \approx 20$–$30$ ms of a backpropagating action potential. Neither stream alone suffices: the interaction is superlinear, not additive [7][8].

### 4.3 Human dCaAPs: graded spikes and nonseparable classification

Gidon *et al.*'s 2020 study of human layer 2/3 pyramidal neurons is the sharpest challenge to the point-neuron picture [1]. Using somatodendritic patch clamp on ex vivo human tissue, they discovered dendritic calcium action potentials (dCaAPs) with properties *inverted* relative to textbook spikes: they are **graded**, with amplitude maximal at threshold-level stimuli and *dampened* by stronger stimuli — an anti-monotonic input–output curve.

This peculiar transfer function is computationally precious. By modeling dCaAP dynamics, the authors showed that a single human neuron can classify linearly nonseparable inputs — the canonical XOR-type problem that motivated multilayer networks in the first place [1]. Two long-standing "impossible for one neuron" problems fall: (i) nonlinear separability, via the graded dCaAP transfer function, and (ii) direction-selective computation, via compartment-specific coupling delays. The human dendrite, in this telling, is not merely a thicker rodent dendrite — enhanced compartmentalization in human neurons was independently documented [5] — but a qualitatively different computational element.

The dCaAP also reframes the depth question: if one dendritic event type can implement a non-monotonic transfer function, then the "layers" inside a neuron need not mirror the monotone sigmoid stacks of classical ANNs. Dendritic nonlinearities span a richer function class — including non-monotonic and history-dependent maps — than the units of standard deep learning.

### 4.4 The single-neuron deep network hypothesis

Poirazi's two-layer model [2] was the opening bid; Beniaguev, London, and Segev raised it dramatically [3]. They trained temporally convolutional DNNs to replicate, at millisecond resolution, the spiking I/O of a detailed layer 5 pyramidal cell model driven by rich spatiotemporal synaptic patterns. Result: **five to eight layers** were required for faithful replication, generalizing well outside the training distribution. The depth was not an artifact of overfitting: when NMDA receptors were removed from the biophysical model, a *single-hidden-layer* network sufficed.

This yields a quantitative decomposition of neuronal complexity:

- **Without NMDA:** neuron $\approx$ one-hidden-layer network (Poirazi's regime, plus Na$^+$/Ca$^{2+}$ refinements).
- **With NMDA:** neuron $\approx$ 5–8-layer temporal-convolutional network, where dendritic branches perform spatiotemporal *template matching* — the DNN's learned weight matrices revealed branch-like pattern detectors [3].

The implication is vertiginous: a cortical column of $10^5$ neurons is not a $10^5$-unit network with shallow units, but a network whose *units are themselves deep*. Whether this depth is *used* by the brain — as opposed to merely present — is the central open question of Section 6.

### 4.5 Synthesis: the neuron as a three-scale device

We can now state the multilayer picture compactly across three scales:

1. **Microscale (branch):** NMDA spikes and fast Na$^+$ spikes implement thresholded, sigmoid-like, and plateau subunits — the hidden layer(s) [2][8].
2. **Mesoscale (compartment):** apical–somatic coupling (BAC mechanism) and tuft Ca$^{2+}$ spikes implement multiplicative gating between input streams [7].
3. **Cell scale (soma/axon):** the axon hillock applies the final threshold, and the spike train encodes the composed result.

Each scale contributes at least one effective layer; NMDA-mediated spatiotemporal integration contributes several more [3]. The point neuron is thus not wrong so much as *zeroth-order* — the $L \to 0$ limit of a richly structured device.

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

**Result 1 — Two-layer abstraction (computational proof).** In Poirazi *et al.*'s compartmental CA1 model, firing rate $r$ satisfied $r = f(\sum_k \sigma_k(\mathbf{w}_k \cdot \mathbf{x}_k))$ with $\sigma_k$ the measured branch sigmoid and $f$ the somatic nonlinearity, predicting responses to novel complex stimuli [2]. The hidden-layer size (~dozens of terminal dendrites) was read off the morphology, not fit.

**Result 2 — Depth from NMDA (ablation proof).** Beniaguev *et al.*'s DNN-fitting protocol gives an operational lower bound on I/O complexity: full model $\to$ 5–8 layers; NMDA removed $\to$ 1 hidden layer; all active conductances removed $\to$ near-linear (Rall's passive regime) [3][4]. The depth *difference* is attributable to NMDA receptors specifically — a causal, not correlational, attribution.

**Result 3 — Nonseparable classification in one human neuron.** Gidon *et al.*'s biophysical model of the dCaAP transfer function solves XOR-class problems with a single cell [1]. This is an existence proof that dendritic nonlinearities transcend the perceptron's representational class.

**Result 4 — In vivo functional relevance.** Smith *et al.* showed dendritic spikes *precede* somatic spikes during visual stimulation and enhance stimulus selectivity in vivo; removing dendritic spikes abolishes the somatic response, establishing that dendritic events are not epiphenomena but causal drivers of output [6].

**Result 5 — Compartmentalization scales with species.** Human cortical neurons exhibit enhanced dendritic compartmentalization relative to rodents, with reduced excitability gradients supporting more independent subunits per cell [5] — consistent with the human-specific dCaAP [1].

Quantitative anchors worth retaining: electrotonic lengths $L \approx 1$–$2$ for motoneuron dendrites (Rall) [4]; NMDA plateau durations 20–200 ms [8]; dCaAP coupling delays 21.6–116.9 ms (mean $53.8 \pm 26.8$ ms) in human L2/3 cells [1]; DNN replication at 1 ms resolution with 5–8 temporal-convolutional layers [3].

## 6 Limitations

1. **Fitted depth vs. mechanistic depth.** A DNN that replicates I/O behavior does not explain *how* the neuron computes; the weight matrices are suggestive (spatiotemporal templates) but not a mechanistic derivation [3]. Depth in the DNN sense is an upper bound on an operational complexity measure, not a discovered circuit diagram.
2. **Rate vs. spike code.** Poirazi's two-layer mapping holds "insofar as the neural code is mediated by average firing rate" [2]; precise spike-timing codes may demand a different abstraction, possibly deeper or shallower.
3. **Generalization of the deep fit.** Beniaguev *et al.* report generalization outside the training distribution [3], but the training ensemble is still synthetic synaptic barrages, not naturalistic in vivo input statistics.
4. **Human data scarcity.** The dCaAP result rests on ex vivo tissue from epilepsy resections [1]; disease, medication, and slice artifacts cannot be fully excluded, and human-specific claims await in vivo confirmation.
5. **Metabolic and learning costs.** Deep-in-one-neuron computation is metabolically expensive (NMDA plateaus, Ca$^{2+}$ influx) and lacks a known local learning rule that would tune dozens of branch subunits the way backpropagation tunes hidden layers. Dendritic plasticity exists [8], but a full credit-assignment theory for the multilayer neuron is missing.
6. **Neuromorphic transfer gap.** Emulating 5–8 effective layers per neuron in silicon multiplies the area/energy budget; it remains unclear which dendritic nonlinearities carry their weight for machine-learning workloads versus which are biological implementation detail.

## 7 Conclusion

The neuron is not a perceptron. Rall's cable theory gave us the passive scaffold — electrotonic length, equalizing time constants, the equivalent cylinder [4] — and four decades of active-dendrite physiology have furnished the nonlinearities that live on it: NMDA spikes as branch sigmoids [8], apical Ca$^{2+}$ spikes as coincidence-gated bursts [7], and human dCaAPs as graded, non-monotonic operators that crack linearly nonseparable problems in a single cell [1]. The computational synthesis — two layers from Poirazi *et al.* [2], five to eight from Beniaguev *et al.* [3], with NMDA conductances supplying the depth — reframes every cortical network as a network of deep units.

The research program this thesis recommends is threefold. First, *measure depth in vivo*: closed-loop experiments that selectively block dendritic spike types while animals perform tasks requiring nonlinear integration. Second, *derive learning rules*: local, spike-based plasticity that tunes branch subunits without global backpropagation. Third, *build it*: neuromorphic dendrites that implement NMDA-like spatiotemporal template matching, testing whether the multilayer neuron's depth is an evolutionary spandrel or a computational necessity. If the latter, the next leap in artificial intelligence may come not from bigger networks of simple units, but from units as deep as the neurons they imitate.
