# The Four-Color Theorem and Computer-Assisted Proof: Reducibility, Discharging, and Formal Verification in Coq

## Abstract

The four-color theorem — that every planar map needs at most four colors so that no two adjacent regions share a color — was the first major mathematical theorem proved with essential computer assistance, by Appel and Haken in 1976 [1]. This article surveys the full arc of that proof strategy: the minimal-counterexample framework, Kempe chains and reducibility, Heesch's discharging method, the streamlined 1997 Robertson–Sanders–Seymour–Thomas proof with its 633 configurations [2], and Georges Gonthier's complete formal verification in the Coq proof assistant [4]. We examine how discharging rules turn Euler's formula into a combinatorial accounting ledger, how reducibility reduces infinite case analysis to finite machine-checkable enumeration, and how Coq's trusted kernel collapses the trust base from thousands of lines of C to a few thousand lines of type checker. We close by assessing the epistemic status of machine-checked mathematics and the open challenge of a human-surveyable proof.

---

## 1 Introduction

Few results in mathematics carry the cultural weight of the **four-color theorem**. Stated informally, it asserts that any map drawn in the plane can be colored with only four colors so that *no two regions sharing a nonzero-length border receive the same color*. Posed in 1852 by Francis Guthrie and publicized by Arthur Cayley in 1878, it survived false proofs and mistaken counterexamples for over a century before falling to a computer in 1976 [1][2].

The interest of the theorem is not really about cartography. No mapmaker needs four colors; the statement is a deep combinatorial property of *planar graphs*. Every planar map can be converted into a planar graph by placing a vertex in each region and joining vertices whose regions share a border. Coloring the map is then equivalent to coloring the vertices of this graph so that adjacent vertices differ — the *graph coloring* problem. The theorem's proof is historically significant because it was the first major theorem whose verification was infeasible for a human to check by hand, inaugurating the era of *computer-assisted proof* [2].

What follows is a self-contained survey: the history (§2), the minimal-counterexample methodology (§3), the three technical pillars (§4), the theorem statements and computational evidence (§5), the limitations (§6), and the open frontier (§7).

> **Theorem (Four-Color Theorem):** Every planar graph is 4-colorable; equivalently, the chromatic number of any planar graph is at most 4.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Kempe's 1879 proof and Heawood's flaw

In 1879 Alfred Kempe published a proof of the four-color conjecture that stood unchallenged for eleven years [2]. His argument introduced two ideas that would dominate the subject for a century:

- An **unavoidable set**: a finite collection of local configurations such that every minimal counterexample must contain at least one of them. Kempe's set had six configurations: a vertex of degree 2, 3, 4, or 5 (he showed a minimal counterexample has minimum degree at most 5), plus two configurations around vertices of degree 5.
- **Reducibility**: showing that each configuration in the unavoidable set cannot appear in a minimal counterexample, because any 4-coloring of the smaller graph (with the configuration removed) can be extended to the full graph via color-swapping along **Kempe chains** — connected subgraphs induced by two colors.

In 1890 Percy Heawood exposed a subtle flaw: in the degree-5 case, Kempe's chain-swapping procedure could fail, since the two Kempe chains involved might interfere with each other. Heawood salvaged the *five*-color theorem from the wreckage — a proof that is genuinely elementary — and generalized the problem to surfaces of arbitrary genus [2]. Kempe's error was so structurally interesting that the swapping procedure retains his name to this day: *Kempe chains* remain at the core of every subsequent attack [3].

### 2.2 Birkhoff, Franklin, and the reducibility program

George Birkhoff's 1913 paper on the reducibility of maps launched a systematic program: prove larger and larger configurations reducible, hoping to assemble an unavoidable set of reducible configurations by hand. Philip Franklin and others extended these results through the mid-20th century, but the unavoidable sets kept growing faster than human case analysis could handle. The program was sound in principle; it was simply too large for unaided humans.

### 2.3 Heesch and the discharging method

The decisive conceptual advance came from Heinrich Heesch in the 1960s. Heesch was the first to use **discharging** to prove unavoidability, and — crucially — he developed, with Ken Durre, a *computer test* for reducibility [2]. The strategy was now clear:

1. Find an unavoidable set of configurations using discharging.
2. Verify each configuration is reducible using a computer.

Heesch lacked the supercomputer time to finish. Others took up his methods.

### 2.4 Appel and Haken, 1976

On June 21, 1976, Kenneth Appel and Wolfgang Haken at the University of Illinois announced that they had proved the theorem, assisted in algorithmic work by John Koch [2]. Their proof ran as follows [1][8]:

- Haken developed a new **discharging procedure with 487 rules**, constructing an unavoidable set of **1,936 configurations** (later reduced to 1,482 for the reducibility check).
- Each configuration was then shown reducible by computer — requiring more than **1,000 hours** of computation on an IBM 360, with each configuration involving up to 500,000 logical options for full analysis.
- The complete proof ran several hundred pages in the *Illinois Journal of Mathematics* (1977).

### 2.5 Robertson–Sanders–Seymour–Thomas, 1997

In 1997 Robertson, Sanders, Seymour, and Thomas published a simplified proof with just **633 configurations** and 32 discharging rules [2][6], distributing the C sources so anyone could re-run the check. A 2026 independent re-verification confirmed all 633 configurations reducible and all five discharging presents (hub degrees 7–11) verified [7].

### 2.6 Gonthier's Coq formalization, 2005

Even the 1997 proof leaves a trust gap: the C programs, the compiler, and the human-authored discharging argument must all be trusted. In 2005 Georges Gonthier and Benjamin Werner completed a **fully formal proof of the four-color theorem in the Coq proof assistant**, checked by Coq version 7.3.1 [4][5]. Gonthier's account, "Formal Proof—The Four-Color Theorem," appeared in the *Notices of the American Mathematical Society* in December 2008 [9]. This was the first longstanding mathematical problem resolved by a *formally verified* computer proof — the computation itself is checked by a small, independently auditable kernel.

## 3 Methodology

All known proofs of the four-color theorem share a single architecture: **proof by minimal counterexample**. The methodology has four stages.

**Stage 1 — Assume a minimal counterexample.** Suppose the theorem is false. Then there exists a planar graph *G* with the smallest possible number of vertices that is *not* 4-colorable. Call *G* a *minimal counterexample*. Standard arguments show we may take *G* to be a *triangulation* (every face a triangle) with minimum degree δ(G) = 5: degrees 2, 3, and 4 are easily shown reducible by hand, and non-triangulated faces can be subdivided.

**Stage 2 — Unavoidability.** Exhibit a finite set *U* of configurations (a *configuration* is a near-triangulation with a labeled outer ring) such that *every* triangulation with δ(G) = 5 contains at least one member of *U* as a subgraph. This is proved by **discharging**: an accounting argument driven by Euler's formula (see §4.2).

**Stage 3 — Reducibility.** For each configuration *C* in *U*, prove *C* is **reducible**: no minimal counterexample can contain *C*. The argument: delete the interior of *C*, 4-color the smaller graph by minimality, then show the coloring always extends across *C* — typically by Kempe-chain recoloring arguments, verified by computer for the hundreds of cases.

**Stage 4 — Contradiction.** *G* must contain some *C* ∈ *U* (unavoidability), but cannot contain any *C* ∈ *U* (reducibility). Contradiction. Hence no minimal counterexample exists, and every planar graph is 4-colorable.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Kempe chains and reducibility

A **Kempe chain** is the connected component of the subgraph induced by two colors in a partially colored graph. If colors *a* and *b* are swapped along an entire (a, b)-chain, the coloring remains proper — this is the engine of all reducibility arguments.

Consider a minimal counterexample *G* and a vertex *v* of degree 5 with neighbors colored, in the 4-coloring of *G − v*, with all four colors (the hard case). Kempe's idea: if the (1,3)-chain joining two opposite neighbors is absent, swap colors 1 and 3 on one neighbor's chain to free a color for *v*. Heawood's refutation showed that when *both* the (1,3)-chain and the (2,4)-chain are present, swapping one chain can destroy the other's structure — the two chains can cross in the planar embedding, and Kempe's argument collapses.

Modern reducibility generalizes this idea. A configuration *C* with ring *R* is **D-reducible** if every 4-coloring of *R* extends to the interior; it is **C-reducible** if colorings of *R* can be partitioned into Kempe-equivalence classes from which a consistent choice extends. The computer test enumerates all ring colorings and checks extendability for each.

The following Python sketch captures the *structure* of a reducibility check (simplified; production code uses bitmasks and memoization):

```python
from itertools import product

def kempe_swap(coloring, graph, start, c1, c2):
    """Swap colors c1 <-> c2 along the Kempe chain containing `start`."""
    chain, stack = set(), [start]
    while stack:
        v = stack.pop()
        if v in chain:
            continue
        chain.add(v)
        for w in graph.neighbors(v):
            if coloring[w] in (c1, c2) and w not in chain:
                stack.append(w)
    for v in chain:
        coloring[v] = c2 if coloring[v] == c1 else c1
    return coloring

def ring_colorings_consistent(ring, interior, max_colors=4):
    """Check every coloring of the ring extends to the interior (D-reducibility)."""
    for colors in product(range(max_colors), repeat=len(ring)):
        if any(colors[i] == colors[(i + 1) % len(ring)] for i in range(len(ring))):
            continue  # ring coloring must be proper
        if not extends_to_interior(dict(zip(ring, colors)), interior):
            return False
    return True
```

For the actual proof, this enumeration is performed over hundreds of configurations with rings up to size 14 — far beyond hand computation, but routine for a machine.

### 4.2 The discharging method

Discharging is the most elegant idea in the proof. Begin with Euler's formula for a connected planar graph, *V − E + F = 2*. For a triangulation with minimum degree 5, a counting argument shows the average degree is strictly less than 6, so vertices of degree exactly 5 must exist — but we need much more: a *finite unavoidable set*.

The method assigns an **initial charge** to each vertex and then redistributes it according to local rules:

1. **Initial charge.** Appel and Haken set *c(v) = 60(6 − d(v))* for each vertex *v* of degree *d(v)*, and 0 for edges and faces. (The factor 60 merely clears denominators.) Summing over all vertices and applying Euler's formula gives total charge **720**, a positive constant [8]. Note *c(v) > 0* for 5-vertices, *c(v) = 0* for 6-vertices, and *c(v) < 0* for vertices of degree 7 or more.
2. **Discharging rules.** A set of local rules moves charge from vertices to their neighbors — e.g., a vertex of degree 8 might send fixed amounts of charge to adjacent 5-vertices in specific geometric patterns. Haken's procedure used **487 rules** [8]; RSST streamlined this to **32 rules**.
3. **Final charge analysis.** The rules are designed so that *if* the graph avoids every configuration in the unavoidable set, *every* vertex ends with nonpositive final charge — contradicting the positive total of 720, which discharging preserves. Therefore some configuration from the set *must* occur.

| Proof | Year | Configurations | Discharging rules | Machine time |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Appel–Haken | 1976–77 | 1,936 (→ 1,482 checked) | 487 | >1,000 h (IBM 360) |
| Robertson et al. | 1997 | 633 | 32 (→ 67 outlets) | minutes (modern HW) |
| Gonthier (Coq) | 2005 | 633 (formalized) | 32 (formalized) | kernel-checked |

A TLA+ sketch of the discharging invariant — total charge is conserved while every rule fires — illustrates the accounting discipline:

```tla+
---- MODULE Discharging ----
EXTENDS Naturals, FiniteSets
CONSTANTS V, deg, Rules
VARIABLES charge

TypeOK == charge \in [V -> Int]
TotalCharge == (Sum over v in V of charge[v]) = 720

FireRule(r) ==
  /\ r \in Rules
  /\ charge' = [v \in V |-> charge[v] + transfer(r, v)]
  /\ \A v \in V : transfer(r, v) \in Int

Init == charge = [v \in V |-> 60 * (6 - deg[v])]
Next == \E r \in Rules : FireRule(r)

THEOREM Conservation == Spec => []TotalCharge
====
```

### 4.3 The RSST proof: 633 configurations

The Robertson–Sanders–Seymour–Thomas proof is the version a modern reader should study first [6]. Its improvements over Appel–Haken are threefold:

- **Fewer configurations.** 633 instead of 1,936, found by a more clever discharging procedure with only 32 rules. Each rule expands into *outlets* — concrete local patterns — 67 in total.
- **Simpler reducibility.** A cleaner notion of reducibility and a more efficient reducibility test, implemented in about a thousand lines of C (`reduce.c`).
- **Reproducibility.** The authors distributed the source code, so anyone can recompile and re-run the check. A 2026 independent re-verification compiled the original C with a modern toolchain and confirmed: all **633 configurations reducible**, and all five discharging *presents* — exhaustive enumerations of neighborhoods around a hub vertex of degree 7, 8, 9, 10, and 11 — verified [7].

The *presents* complete the unavoidability argument: around any hub vertex, the neighborhood must match one of finitely many patterns, each forcing one of the 633 configurations to appear.

### 4.4 Formal verification in Coq

Gonthier's formalization eliminates the largest remaining trust gap: the C programs themselves [4][5]. The strategy, in brief:

1. **Formalize the combinatorics.** Planar graphs, triangulations, configurations, and colorings are defined inside Coq's logic (the Calculus of Inductive Constructions). The statement proved is exactly the four-color theorem — no paraphrase.
2. **Formalize the proof structure.** The minimal-counterexample argument, the discharging rules, and the reducibility test are all written as Coq definitions and lemmas.
3. **Reflect computation into logic.** The enumeration is performed by *verified decision procedures* inside Coq — the type checker itself evaluates the reducibility test on each configuration. This technique, *proof by reflection*, turns computation into proof.
4. **Check with a small kernel.** Coq's trusted kernel — the type checker for the Calculus of Inductive Constructions — is only a few thousand lines. Everything else (tactics, automation, libraries) produces *proof terms* that the kernel independently validates.

The de Bruijn criterion is satisfied: correctness depends only on the kernel, not on the elaborate tactic machinery that built the proof. In Haskell-like notation, the kernel's job is type checking in a dependently typed lambda calculus:

```haskell
-- Sketch: kernel checks that a proof term has the claimed proposition as type
data Term = Var Name | Lam Name Term Term | App Term Term | Pi Name Term Term

-- check ctx term prop  ==  "term is a proof of prop" (Curry-Howard)
check :: Context -> Term -> Term -> Bool
check ctx (Lam x a b) (Pi x' a' b') = a == a' && check ((x, a) : ctx) b b'
check ctx (App f x)   b             = case synth ctx f of
    Pi _ a b' -> check ctx x a && b == subst x b'
    _         -> False
```

And the reducibility test itself, as it might appear in a systems language with exhaustive matching:

```rust
/// Returns true iff every proper ring coloring extends across the configuration.
fn is_reducible(config: &Configuration) -> bool {
    for coloring in config.ring.proper_colorings(4) {
        // Try all Kempe-equivalence classes reachable from this coloring.
        let mut seen = std::collections::HashSet::new();
        let mut stack = vec![coloring];
        let mut extends = false;
        while let Some(c) = stack.pop() {
            if !seen.insert(c.signature()) { continue; }
            if c.extends_to_interior(&config.interior) { extends = true; break; }
            stack.extend(c.kempe_neighbors(&config.ring));
        }
        if !extends { return false; }
    }
    true
}
```

The payoff: after Coq accepts the development, the *only* things to trust are the kernel's implementation, the hardware, and the adequacy of the formal statement — a dramatically smaller surface than thousands of lines of unverified C plus a hand-written discharging argument [9].

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

The formal record consists of three layers of evidence:

> **Theorem (Appel–Haken, 1976):** Every planar map is four-colorable. Established via an unavoidable set of 1,936 configurations (487 discharging rules) and machine-verified reducibility of each, using over 1,000 hours of IBM 360 computation [1][8].

> **Theorem (Robertson–Sanders–Seymour–Thomas, 1997):** Every planar graph is 4-colorable. Established via an unavoidable set of 633 configurations (32 discharging rules) with independently re-runnable C programs [2][6].

> **Theorem (Gonthier–Werner, 2005):** The four-color theorem holds in the Calculus of Inductive Constructions. The complete proof was checked by the Coq v7.3.1 kernel — the first formal verification of a major computer-assisted theorem [4][5][9].

Empirical corroboration continues to accumulate:

- **Independent re-verification (2026).** The RSST C sources were recompiled with a modern toolchain and re-executed: 633 out of 633 configurations confirmed reducible, and all five discharging presents (hub degrees 7–11) confirmed, with zero substantive defects found in an independent audit of the C logic [7].
- **Scale of formalization.** Gonthier's development ran to tens of thousands of lines of Coq script, showing that proof assistants had matured enough to absorb a proof whose *statement* is simple but whose *case analysis* is vast [5].
- **A new proof in 2026.** Quanta Magazine reported in September 2026 that the four-color theorem has received a rare new proof — the first fundamentally new approach in decades — underscoring that the theorem remains an active research object, not a closed case [3].

Together these results establish the theorem with a redundancy no single proof could provide: two independent computational proofs plus one fully machine-checked formalization, all agreeing.

## 6 Limitations

Enthusiasm for machine-checked proof should not obscure genuine limitations:

1. **The specification gap.** Coq verifies a *formal statement*; a human must judge that the formal statement captures the intended theorem. Gonthier's formalization proves 4-colorability of a combinatorial encoding of planar graphs — the correspondence between that encoding and geometric planarity is itself a (carefully argued) mathematical claim [4].

2. **Surveyability is lost, not restored.** Gonthier's proof is *more* trustworthy but *less* surveyable than RSST's. No human has read the full formal proof, and none will. If understanding — not just certainty — is the goal of proof, formalization is an incomplete victory.
3. **No human-scale proof exists.** Despite the 2026 development [3], there is still no proof of the four-color theorem that a human can check unaided in a lifetime. Whether such a proof exists is one of combinatorics' most tantalizing open questions.
4. **Fragility of the artifact.** The original Appel–Haken programs were tied to 1970s mainframes; only RSST's portable C and Gonthier's Coq sources remain live artifacts. Formal proofs depend on proof-assistant versions — Coq 7.3.1 is already historical.

These limitations do not undermine the theorem's truth, which is now established beyond reasonable doubt. They do counsel humility about what "proved" means when the proof exceeds human scale.

---

## 7 Conclusion

The four-color theorem's proof history is a parable of 20th-century mathematics. Kempe's elegant failure gave us unavoidable sets and Kempe chains [3]; Heesch gave us discharging and the first reducibility programs [2]; Appel and Haken gave us the first computer-assisted proof at the cost of verifiability [1][8]; Robertson, Sanders, Seymour, and Thomas gave us a proof we can re-run [6][7]; and Gonthier gave us a proof we can *trust* without re-running the argument ourselves, by checking it with a small kernel [4][9].

The trajectory is clear: each generation shrinks the trusted base and widens verification. A human-surveyable proof — or a verified proof in a next-generation assistant with an even smaller kernel — remains the open milestone. Until then, the four-color theorem stands as both a triumph and a warning: some truths are too large for the unaided mind, and mathematics has learned to build machines that can hold them.
