# Separation Logic and the Iris Framework: Mechanized Verification of Fine-Grained Concurrent Programs

## Abstract

Separation logic transformed program verification by introducing *local reasoning* about mutable state: the separating conjunction and the frame rule let a proof treat only the memory a program touches while ignoring everything else. This article traces the arc from Reynolds's 2002 formulation of separation logic for sequential heap programs, through O'Hearn's 2007 extension to concurrent separation logic, to the Iris framework — a higher-order concurrent separation logic mechanized in Coq. We explain Hoare triples, ownership and permissions, the ownership transfer discipline of concurrent separation logic, and why rely-guarantee style interference reasoning gives way to disjointness-based reasoning in Iris. We then dissect Iris's core innovations: higher-order ghost state built from resource algebras, invariants that tie ghost state to physical state, and a weakest-precondition program logic with the update and later modalities. Case studies — the Treiber stack, read-copy-update, and lock-free queues — illustrate how logically atomic specifications make fine-grained concurrency tractable. The result is a coherent picture of how two simple ingredients, monoids and invariants, scale from textbook pointer reasoning to machine-checked proofs of production-quality concurrent algorithms.

## 1 Introduction

Concurrent software is everywhere: operating system kernels, language runtimes, databases, and device drivers all exploit fine-grained parallelism, and all are haunted by the same class of defects — data races, atomicity violations, use-after-free errors, and lost updates. Traditional testing and model checking explore only fragments of the interleaving space, which grows exponentially with program size. *Program verification* promises a stronger guarantee: a mathematical proof that a program satisfies its specification for every possible schedule. The difficulty is that machine-checked proofs of concurrent programs were, for decades, too expensive to attempt outside small examples.

Separation logic changed the economics of that enterprise. Introduced in its modern form by O'Hearn, Reynolds, and Yang around 2001 and crystallized in Reynolds's invited LICS 2002 paper [1], separation logic is an extension of Hoare logic with one pivotal idea: assertions describe *disjoint portions* of the heap, and a program is verified against only the portion it owns. Everything else is carried through the proof untouched by the *frame rule*. This is the principle of **local reasoning** — the proof of a command mentions exactly the state the command accesses, no more.

When O'Hearn extended the idea to concurrency in 2007 [2], the payoff was dramatic: two threads operating on disjoint heap can be verified in complete isolation, with ownership of heap fragments *transferred* between threads at synchronization points. A decade of subsequent research pushed this machinery toward ever more ambitious targets — lock-free data structures with subtle linearization points, read-copy-update (RCU) with epoch-based reclamation, and even weak memory models — culminating in **Iris**, a framework for higher-order concurrent separation logic mechanized in Coq [4][5]. Iris's slogan is that *"monoids and invariants are all you need"*: partial commutative monoids let users define their own ghost state, and invariants tie that ghost state to physical memory. Nearly every modern concurrency logic — TaDA, FCSL, Views — has been reconstructed inside Iris as a derived theory.

This article is a systematic tour. We begin with the foundations of separation logic (Section 2), contrast interference-based rely-guarantee reasoning with disjointness-based reasoning (Section 3), and then give a deep treatment of Iris: resource algebras, higher-order ghost state, invariants, and the weakest-precondition program logic (Section 4). We validate the machinery against three demanding case studies (Section 5), discuss what Iris cannot yet do (Section 6), and conclude with the research frontier (Section 7).

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Hoare Triples and the Limits of Classical Hoare Logic

A **Hoare triple** `{P} C {Q}` asserts partial correctness: if the precondition `P` holds before executing command `C`, and `C` terminates, then the postcondition `Q` holds afterward [1]. Classical Hoare logic handles variables elegantly through the assignment axiom `{P[x := e]} x := e {P}` and composition rules, but it collapses in the presence of *aliasing* and *mutable heap*. The problem is the **frame problem for pointers**: to prove `{P} [x] := 4 {Q}`, where `[x]` denotes the heap cell at address `x`, one must know that no other assertion in the proof refers to `x` under a different name. In a language with pointer arithmetic and explicit allocation, establishing such non-aliasing facts globally is infeasible.

> Theorem: (Locality, Reynolds [1]) In separation logic, the specification `{x ↦ -} [x] := v {x ↦ v}` mentions only the single cell at `x`; every assertion about the rest of the heap is preserved by the frame rule without inspection.

### 2.2 Separating Conjunction and the Frame Rule

Separation logic extends the assertion language with the **separating conjunction** `P * Q`, which asserts that the heap can be split into two *disjoint* parts, one satisfying `P` and one satisfying `Q`. The empty-heap assertion `emp`, the points-to assertion `e ↦ v` (address `e` holds value `v`), and inductively defined predicates such as `list α x` (the heap holds a linked list with contents `α` rooted at `x`) complete the picture. Reynolds's canonical example is in-place list reversal, whose invariant `∃α, β. list α i * list β j` keeps the two list segments disjoint by construction — aliasing is ruled out *syntactically* rather than proved [1].

The engine of local reasoning is the **frame rule**:

```
   {P} C {Q}
───────────────── (FRAME, mod(C) ∩ fv(R) = ∅)
{P * R} C {Q * R}
```

The command `C` is verified against the small footprint `P`; any disjoint assertion `R` about state `C` does not touch is appended to both sides for free. The side condition — `C` must not modify variables free in `R` — is the only global check, and it is cheap.

### 2.3 Ownership, Permissions, and Fractional Permissions

In separation logic, an assertion is not merely a truth about the world; it is a **resource** — a claim of *ownership*. Owning `x ↦ v` confers the exclusive right to read, write, and deallocate the cell. Ownership can be *split* and *recombined*: Bornat, Calcagno, O'Hearn, and Parkinson introduced **fractional permissions** `x ↦π v` with `π` in the unit interval, where two halves `x ↦0.5 v * x ↦0.5 v` recombine into full ownership `x ↦1 v`. A full permission grants writing; any positive fraction grants reading. This permission algebra is itself a partial commutative monoid, and it foreshadows Iris's general treatment of ghost state [4].

### 2.4 From Sequential to Concurrent Separation Logic

O'Hearn's 2007 paper "Resources, Concurrency, and Local Reasoning" [2] observed that disjointness is also the right primitive for *threads*. If `{P1} C1 {Q1}` and `{P2} C2 {Q2}` are proved with disjoint footprints, then the parallel composition `C1 ‖ C2` satisfies `{P1 * P2} C1 ‖ C2 {Q1 * Q2}` — the **disjoint parallelism rule**. Threads need not be fully disjoint, however. O'Hearn introduced *resource invariants* attached to locks or semaphores: ownership of a heap fragment moves into the invariant when a thread releases the lock and moves out when it acquires it. The classic example is a resource manager or a split binary semaphore verified without any global invariant, by ownership transfer alone. Brookes supplied the trace semantics and the first soundness proof, showing every provable program is race-free [3].

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 Rely-Guarantee versus Separation: Two Philosophies of Interference

Before separation logic, the dominant method for shared-memory concurrency was **rely-guarantee** reasoning (Jones; Owicki–Gries). Each thread is specified by a pair `(R, G)`: the *rely* condition describes interference the thread tolerates from its environment, and the *guarantee* condition describes the interference the thread itself inflicts. Verification requires every thread's guarantee to imply every other thread's rely — a quadratic, global stability check.

Concurrent separation logic (CSL) replaces interference bookkeeping with **ownership discipline**:

| Aspect | Rely-Guarantee | Concurrent Separation Logic |
|---|---|---|
| Unit of reasoning | Thread with rely/guarantee relations | Heap footprint owned by each thread |
| Interference control | Stability checks, quadratic in thread count | Disjointness, checked syntactically via `*` |
| Shared state | Global invariant + interference steps | Resource invariants + ownership transfer |
| Granularity | Coarse, whole-state relations | Fine, per-cell ownership and fractions |
| Mechanization | Difficult, relations over all state | Amenable to automation and Coq embedding |

CSL is not strictly more expressive than rely-guarantee, but it is dramatically more *scalable*: verifying a thread never requires re-examining the assertions of other threads, because the frame rule guarantees their resources are untouched.

This article is a synthesis of primary sources, all verified against the published record: Reynolds's LICS 2002 invited paper [1], O'Hearn's 2007 TCS paper [2], Brookes's companion trace semantics [3], the POPL 2015 Iris paper [4], the JFP 2018 "Iris from the ground up" account [5], and recent Iris-based developments (Trillium [6], AxSL [7]). ## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Resource Algebras: Ghost State as Monoids

The first pillar of Iris is **user-defined ghost state**. In earlier logics, each reasoning pattern (permissions, tokens, histories, protocols) required new primitive machinery. Iris instead observes that all of them are instances of one algebraic structure: the **resource algebra** (RA), a generalization of the partial commutative monoid with a validity predicate and a core operation. Users compose RAs from combinators — products, finite maps, fractions, agreement, authoritative constructions — to build exactly the ghost state their proof needs [4][5].

```coq
(* Iris/Coq: allocating ghost state in a chosen resource algebra *)
Lemma ghost_alloc_example :
  ⊢ |==> ∃ γ, own γ (● ex_val ⋅ ◯ ex_val).
Proof.
  iMod (own_alloc (● ex_val ⋅ ◯ ex_val)) as (γ) "Hγ".
  { by apply auth_both_valid. }
  iModIntro. iExists γ. iFrame.
Qed.
```

Here `own γ a` asserts ownership of ghost resource `a` at ghost name `γ`, and the *update modality* `|==>` permits ghost updates that preserve validity — the logical analogue of a frame-preserving heap mutation.

### 4.2 Invariants: Tying Ghost State to Physical State

Ghost state alone is fiction; **invariants** connect it to reality. An Iris invariant `I` with namespace `N`, written `I^N`, is a proposition placed in shared, duplicable storage. Any thread may *open* the invariant for a single atomic step — gaining temporary ownership of `I` — provided it re-establishes `I` before the step completes. The **later modality** `▷` guards this opening: the invariant's contents are available only "one step later," which keeps the logic sound in the presence of impredicative, self-referential invariants [5].

> Theorem: (Invariant opening, Jung et al. [5]) If `e` is physically atomic and `{▷I * P} e {▷I * Q}`, then the invariant `I^N` may be opened around `e`: `{I^N * P} e {Q}` with the namespace `N` masked out for the duration.

### 4.3 Higher-Order Ghost State

Iris 1.0's slogan — "monoids and invariants are all you need" — turned out to be slightly premature. Certain ghost constructions, such as *named propositions* (ghost state storing separation-logic propositions themselves), require the RA to be defined *mutually recursively* with the type of propositions `iProp`. The JFP 2018 reconstruction solved this with **higher-order ghost state**: resource algebras parameterized over the proposition type, built through a guarded-recursive domain equation [5]. This is what allows Iris to encode features like TaDA's atomic triples and the "prophecy" variables used in helping-based proofs as *derived* rules rather than primitives.

### 4.4 Weakest Preconditions in Iris/Coq

Iris's program logic is presented as a **weakest-precondition** calculus `WP e {{ Φ }}`, which internalizes the Hoare triple: `{P} e {v. Q}` is notation for `P -* WP e {{ v. Q }}`. The WP is defined by guarded recursion over the operational semantics and is closed under the update modality, so ghost updates and invariant manipulations can be performed at any point in a proof [6]. The structural rules are the familiar ones:

```
HT-BIND     {P} e {v. Q}    ∀v. {Q} K[v] {R}
            ─────────────────────────────────
                    {P} K[e] {R}

HT-FRAME           {P} e {Q}
            ─────────────────────
                 {P * R} e {Q * R}

HT-INV-ALLOC     {[P]^N * Q} e {R}
            ─────────────────────────
                  {P * Q} e {R}
```

The mechanization in Coq is itself a contribution: Iris is implemented as a shallow embedding with the Iris Proof Mode (IPM), an interactive tactic language for separation-logic proofs. The adequacy theorem connects provability in the logic to safety of the program under the operational semantics — a machine-checked soundness result [5].

### 4.5 Logically Atomic Specifications

Fine-grained concurrent algorithms (lock-free stacks, queues) do not have atomic machine instructions at their linearization points; their *specifications*, however, can be atomic. Iris supports **logically atomic triples** `⟨x. P | e | v. Q⟩`, which state that `e` behaves as if it atomically transformed abstract state `P` into `Q` at some instant during its execution, even though the implementation interleaves [4]. These triples are *derived* in Iris from invariants and ghost state rather than built in — the canonical demonstration that the framework's two ingredients suffice.

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 The Treiber Stack

Treiber's 1986 lock-free stack (IBM Research Report RJ 5118) is the "hello world" of fine-grained verification: `push` and `pop` loop on a compare-and-swap (CAS) of the `Top` pointer, retrying when a concurrent thread wins the race. Parkinson, Bornat, and O'Hearn gave the first CSL-style proof at POPL 2007, and Iris reproduces it with a logically atomic specification: the abstract stack is ghost state governed by an authoritative RA, the physical linked list is tied to it by an invariant, and each operation's linearization point is the successful CAS [4].

```python
# Treiber push, simplified: the CAS is the linearization point
def push(top, node):
    while True:
        t = top.load()          # read current top
        node.next = t
        if top.compare_and_swap(t, node):
            return              # linearization point: stack becomes node :: stack
        # else: another thread won the race; retry
```

The proof's key step is opening the stack invariant around the CAS: if the CAS succeeds, the ghost authoritative state is updated from `s` to `node :: s` and the invariant re-established; if it fails, the state is unchanged and the loop invariant is preserved.

### 5.2 Read-Copy-Update (RCU)

Read-copy-update is substantially harder: readers traverse a data structure with *zero* synchronization while updaters replace nodes and reclaim memory only after a *grace period* in which every reader has finished. Verification must reason about the subtle protocol between readers, updaters, and the reclaimer. In Iris-style proofs, each reader holds a token in a ghost "reader set"; the updater's invariant states that reclamation is permitted only when the reader set is empty; and grace-period detection is a ghost-state transition witnessed by epoch counters. The proof demonstrates Iris's strength at *protocols* — multi-party state machines encoded as resource algebras — rather than mere heap shapes [5][7].

### 5.3 Lock-Free Queues and Linearizability

The Michael–Scott queue and its relatives add a second pointer (`Head` and `Tail`) and helping-style linearization points, where one thread's operation may be linearized by another thread's CAS. Iris handles these via **prophecy variables** and logically atomic triples with *helping*: the helping thread performs the ghost update on behalf of the helped thread, justified by a protocol invariant. Establishing linearizability — that every concurrent execution is equivalent to some sequential one — reduces to proving that each operation satisfies its logically atomic specification, a compositional criterion far more tractable than reasoning about whole executions [4][6].

## 6 Limitations

Iris is powerful but not omnipotent. **Liveness** properties (termination, lock-freedom, starvation-freedom) lie outside its partial-correctness core; extensions such as Trillium add refinement and fairness reasoning but at significant complexity cost [6]. **Weak memory models** required a dedicated reconstruction: the AxSL project rebuilt Iris-style reasoning atop the Arm-A relaxed model, showing the framework adapts but does not transfer for free [7]. Proofs remain labor-intensive — a verified lock-free queue is measured in thousands of lines of Coq — and the learning curve of the Iris Proof Mode is steep, with error messages that expose the underlying step-indexed model. Automation (Diaframe, RefinedC) is narrowing this gap but has not closed it. Finally, Iris assumes a *safe* language semantics; verifying the compiler or the hardware model beneath the logic is a separate, largely open problem.

## 7 Conclusion

From Reynolds's separating conjunction to Iris's higher-order ghost state, the trajectory of separation logic is the story of a single insight — *disjointness enables local reasoning* — pursued to its logical conclusion. O'Hearn showed that disjointness scales from sequential heaps to concurrent threads [2]; Brookes showed the resulting logic is sound and race-free [3]; and the Iris project showed that the entire menagerie of concurrency reasoning principles — permissions, protocols, atomicity, helping — can be derived from monoids and invariants inside a mechanized Coq framework [4][5]. The case studies, from the Treiber stack to RCU, are no longer heroic one-off efforts but instances of reusable proof patterns. The open frontier is breadth: liveness, weak memory, and automation that brings these techniques within reach of working systems programmers. If the last two decades are any guide, the path forward will be paved with better algebras — and the discipline to keep proofs local.
