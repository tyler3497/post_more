# The Cosmic Microwave Background: Acoustic Peaks, Polarization, and Inflationary Constraints

## Abstract

The cosmic microwave background (CMB) is the most precise probe of the early Universe: a 2.725 K blackbody relic released at recombination whose temperature anisotropies of one part in 100,000 encode the primordial fluctuation spectrum, the cosmic composition, and the geometry of spacetime. This thesis develops the physics of CMB anisotropies from the Boltzmann hierarchy through the line-of-sight integral to the measured angular power spectra. We derive the acoustic oscillations of the photon-baryon fluid that generate the harmonic peak series, explain how baryon loading sets the odd-even peak height ratio, and show how Silk damping and the finite thickness of the last-scattering surface shape the damping tail. We develop the spin-2 E/B polarization formalism and demonstrate why a primordial B-mode signal is a unique signature of inflationary gravitational waves. Reviewing the Planck 2018 legacy cosmological parameters and the BICEP/Keck BK18 bound of r < 0.036, we assess the inflationary implications and the open tensions — the Hubble discrepancy, the lensing amplitude anomaly, and the foreground-limited B-mode frontier — that will define the next decade of CMB cosmology.

## 1 Introduction

The cosmic microwave background (CMB) is the oldest observable light in the Universe: thermal radiation released when the primordial plasma recombined at a redshift of approximately 1100, roughly 380,000 years after the Big Bang. Its near-perfect blackbody spectrum at 2.725 K and its temperature anisotropies at the level of one part in 100,000 encode the initial conditions of cosmic structure, the composition of the Universe, and the physics of inflation itself [7]. What began in 1965 as an unexplained excess antenna temperature measured by Penzias and Wilson has matured, through COBE, WMAP, and Planck, into the most precise dataset in observational cosmology: the Planck 2018 legacy release constrains the six parameters of the base ΛCDM model to percent or sub-percent precision [1]. Yet the CMB is far more than a parameter-fitting machine. The harmonic series of acoustic peaks in the temperature power spectrum is a direct snapshot of sound waves propagating in the photon-baryon fluid before recombination [2][3]; the polarization field, decomposed into gradient-like E-modes and curl-like B-modes, carries an independent and in some respects sharper cosmological signal [4][5]; and the hunt for primordial B-modes is the most direct experimental probe of the energy scale of inflation [6]. This thesis develops the physics of CMB anisotropies from first principles, traces the chain from the Boltzmann hierarchy to the measured angular power spectra, reviews the empirical state of the art, and examines where the standard picture strains — the Hubble tension, the lensing amplitude anomaly, and the foreground-limited frontier of B-mode cosmology.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Discovery and the thermal history

The CMB was discovered in 1965 when Penzias and Wilson detected an isotropic excess noise temperature of about 3.5 K that could not be attributed to any terrestrial or instrumental source [7]. Dicke, Peebles, Roll, and Wilkinson immediately interpreted it as the cooled remnant of a hot early Universe. The FIRAS instrument aboard COBE later established that the spectrum is a blackbody to extraordinary precision, with deviations bounded below one part in 10,000, confirming that the early Universe was once in near-perfect thermal equilibrium.

The thermal history that produces the CMB is conceptually simple but quantitatively rich. At temperatures above roughly 3000 K, photons and baryons are tightly coupled by Thomson scattering, and hydrogen is ionized; the Universe is opaque. As expansion cools the plasma below this threshold, protons and electrons combine into neutral hydrogen, the free-electron density plummets, and photons decouple, free-streaming to the present day from a thin spherical shell around each observer: the *surface of last scattering*. The visibility function — the probability density that a CMB photon last scattered at a given epoch — peaks at redshift z ≈ 1100 with a width Δz ≈ 80 [2]. That finite thickness will matter: it erases the smallest-scale fluctuations and generates polarization.

### 2.2 The anisotropy hierarchy

COBE's DMR instrument detected temperature fluctuations ΔT/T ≈ 10⁻⁵ on angular scales larger than about 7 degrees [7]. On such large scales the anisotropy is dominated by the Sachs–Wolfe effect: photons climbing out of gravitational potential wells at last scattering are gravitationally redshifted, so that ΔT/T ≈ Φ/3, where Φ is the Newtonian potential. Because the potentials are nearly frozen on super-horizon scales, the large-angle plateau directly measures the primordial power spectrum of curvature perturbations laid down during inflation.

On degree scales and below, the physics becomes dynamical. Before recombination, the photon-baryon fluid supports acoustic oscillations driven by the competition between radiation pressure and gravity in dark matter potential wells [2]. Each Fourier mode of wavenumber k oscillates as a standing sound wave; modes caught at maximum compression or rarefaction at the moment of decoupling imprint the celebrated acoustic peaks. The first peak at multipole ℓ ≈ 220 corresponds to the mode that completed exactly half an oscillation — maximum compression — by recombination. The baryon loading shifts the zero point of the oscillation, enhancing odd (compression) peaks relative to even (rarefaction) ones, which is why the *ratio* of peak heights measures the baryon density [3].

### 2.3 Numerical machinery

Modern analysis rests on Boltzmann codes — CMBFAST historically, and CAMB and CLASS today — that evolve the coupled photon, baryon, neutrino, and dark matter perturbations through recombination [7]. The line-of-sight integral method rewrites the temperature anisotropy as a time integral of a source function convolved with spherical Bessel functions, reducing a stiff hierarchy of thousands of coupled equations to a tractable integral [5]. Parameter inference then proceeds by Markov Chain Monte Carlo exploration of the likelihood of the measured angular power spectra given a cosmological model [1].

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 From the Boltzmann equation to the power spectrum

The fundamental object is the photon distribution function f(x, p, t), whose fractional perturbation Θ = ΔT/T obeys the Boltzmann equation in a perturbed Friedmann–Lemaître–Robertson–Walker metric. Expanding the angular dependence in Legendre multipoles Θ_ℓ(k) yields an infinite hierarchy coupling each multipole to its neighbors through free streaming, with collisions entering only at ℓ ≤ 2 via the Thomson scattering term. In the tight-coupling limit the photon-baryon plasma behaves as a single fluid with sound speed c_s = 1/√(3(1+R)), where R = 3ρ_b/(4ρ_γ) is the baryon-to-photon momentum density ratio [2]. The oscillator equation for the monopole,

> **Theorem (Driven acoustic oscillator):** In the tight-coupling regime, the photon temperature monopole Θ_0 for a mode of wavenumber k obeys a forced harmonic oscillator equation with natural frequency k c_s, driven by the time-dependent gravitational potentials; baryon loading displaces the oscillation zero point by −RΨ, boosting compression peaks over rarefaction peaks [2].

The line-of-sight solution expresses today's multipoles as [5]

    Δ_T,ℓ(k) = ∫ dη S_T(k, η) j_ℓ(k(η_0 − η))

where S_T is the source function (Sachs–Wolfe, Doppler, and integrated Sachs–Wolfe contributions), η is conformal time, and j_ℓ are spherical Bessel functions projecting spatial wavenumber k onto angular multipole ℓ. Squaring and integrating against the primordial power spectrum P(k) gives the angular power spectrum C_ℓ. Polarization multipoles admit an analogous integral with spin-weighted projection functions [5].

### 3.2 Statistical analysis of the maps

A full-sky temperature map is expanded in spherical harmonics, T(n̂) = Σ a_ℓm Y_ℓm(n̂), and for a statistically isotropic Gaussian field the power spectrum C_ℓ = ⟨|a_ℓm|²⟩ contains all the information. The Planck collaboration measured TT, TE, and EE spectra over multipoles from ℓ = 2 to ℓ ≈ 2500, plus the lensing potential spectrum reconstructed from the four-point function, and combined these in a joint likelihood [1]. Cosmological parameters are extracted by MCMC sampling; the base model has six free parameters: the baryon and cold dark matter densities ω_b and ω_c, the angular acoustic scale θ_*, the optical depth τ, and the amplitude and tilt of the primordial scalar spectrum, A_s and n_s.

The numerical coincidence ℓ ≈ 220 for the first peak is no accident: it is π divided by the angular size of the sound horizon, measured by Planck to 0.03 percent precision [1].

### 3.3 Polarization formalism

Linear polarization is described by the Stokes parameters Q and U, which transform as a spin-2 field on the sphere. Kamionkowski, Kosowsky, and Stebbins showed that the polarization pattern decomposes into two geometrically distinct components — E-modes (gradient-like, even parity) and B-modes (curl-like, odd parity) — and that scalar density perturbations generate only E-modes, while tensor perturbations (gravitational waves) generate both [4]. Zaldarriaga and Seljak developed the complete all-sky formalism: with temperature included, statistical isotropy permits exactly four non-vanishing spectra — TT, EE, BB, and the TE cross-correlation — with TB and EB vanishing by parity [5].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Acoustic oscillations: the photon-baryon symphony

The analytic treatment of Hu and Sugiyama remains the clearest physical account of how the peaks arise [2]. Consider a single Fourier mode entering the horizon before recombination. Initially, the photon-baryon fluid falls into the dark matter potential well, compressing and heating; radiation pressure then drives it back out, and the fluid oscillates. Because all modes of a given k share the same phase — they were all "kicked" coherently by inflation — the oscillation phase at decoupling depends only on k, producing coherent peaks rather than a washed-out continuum.

Three effects shape the peak morphology in ways that make the spectrum an exquisite parameter probe [3]:

- **Peak positions.** The harmonic series ℓ_n ≈ nπD_*/r_s fixes the ratio of the angular diameter distance to last scattering, D_*, to the sound horizon r_s. The first-peak position is the premier geometric probe: it established spatial flatness, Ω_K ≈ 0, a decade before dark energy surveys confirmed it.
- **Peak heights and baryon drag.** Baryons add inertia without pressure, deepening compressions and softening rarefactions. The odd-to-even peak ratio therefore measures ω_b, and the Planck value ω_b h² = 0.0224 ± 0.0001 agrees spectacularly with independent Big Bang nucleosynthesis determinations from primordial deuterium [1].
- **The damping tail.** Photon diffusion (Silk damping) exponentially suppresses power on scales smaller than the diffusion length, while the finite thickness of last scattering further smooths small-scale structure. The damping envelope probes the expansion rate and the ionization history [2][3].

### 4.2 Polarization generation and the E-mode spectrum

Polarization is generated only where Thomson scattering sees a quadrupole in the incident radiation — that is, during the brief breakdown of tight coupling at recombination, and again at reionization [4]. The local quadrupole is sourced by velocity gradients in the photon-baryon fluid, so the E-mode polarization spectrum peaks where the velocity (not the density) is maximal: the E-mode peaks are interleaved halfway between the temperature peaks, a distinctive phase signature confirmed by Planck's TE and EE measurements [1][5].

Reionization leaves its own imprint: rescattering at z ~ 8 generates large-angle polarization, the "reionization bump" in EE at ℓ < 10, whose amplitude measures the optical depth τ. Planck's large-scale HFI polarization data pinned τ = 0.054 ± 0.007, breaking the A_s–τ degeneracy that had limited earlier analyses and sharpening every correlated parameter [1].

### 4.3 B-modes: the tensor window and its contaminants

A primordial B-mode signal would be a smoking gun for inflationary gravitational waves, with amplitude set by the tensor-to-scalar ratio r [4]. But three contaminants stand between theory and detection:

1. **Gravitational lensing.** Large-scale structure lenses E-modes into B-modes, producing a guaranteed BB signal peaking near ℓ ≈ 1000 that now dominates the measured B-mode spectrum. It must be modeled — or removed by "delensing" — to reach r ~ 10⁻³.
2. **Galactic dust.** The BICEP2 episode of 2014 is the cautionary tale: an apparent r ≈ 0.2 signal dissolved once Planck's 353 GHz dust maps showed the signal was polarized Galactic dust [6]. Modern analyses fit multi-frequency spectra against a seven-parameter foreground model.
3. **Instrumental systematics.** Beam asymmetries, gain mismatch, and polarization angle miscalibration can leak T into B or E into B at levels comparable to the target signal [4].

The state of the art is the BICEP/Keck XIII (BK18) analysis of all South Pole data through 2018, combined with Planck and WMAP: r_0.05 < 0.036 at 95% confidence, with σ(r) = 0.009 the tightest constraint on primordial gravitational waves ever achieved [6]. Combined with Planck, the bound tightens to approximately r < 0.032 and rules out broad classes of simple inflationary models, including monomial potentials like φ² and φ⁴.

### 4.4 Inflationary implications of the scalar spectrum

Even without a B-mode detection, the scalar sector constrains inflation sharply. Planck finds n_s = 0.965 ± 0.004 — a 8σ deviation from exact scale invariance (n_s = 1), precisely as generic slow-roll inflation predicts — and no evidence for running of the spectral index or for primordial non-Gaussianity beyond the single-field slow-roll floor [1]. The bound on r translates to an upper limit on the inflationary energy scale, V^(1/4) < 1.4 × 10¹⁶ GeV, and on the Hubble rate during inflation, H_*/M_Pl < 2.0 × 10⁻⁵ [6]. Plateau-like models such as Starobinsky R² inflation and Higgs inflation sit comfortably inside the allowed region; large-field monomial models do not.

## 5 Empirical Results

### 5.1 Planck 2018: the six-parameter triumph

The Planck 2018 legacy cosmological parameters, from the combined TT, TE, EE, and lensing likelihoods, are [1]:

| Parameter | Value (68% CL) | Physical meaning |
|---|---|---|
| ω_b h² | 0.0224 ± 0.0001 | Physical baryon density |
| ω_c h² | 0.120 ± 0.001 | Physical cold dark matter density |
| 100θ_* | 1.0411 ± 0.0003 | Angular acoustic scale (×100) |
| τ | 0.054 ± 0.007 | Reionization optical depth |
| n_s | 0.965 ± 0.004 | Scalar spectral index |
| ln(10¹⁰A_s) | 3.044 ± 0.014 | Scalar amplitude |
| H_0 | 67.4 ± 0.5 km/s/Mpc | Hubble constant (derived) |
| Ω_m | 0.315 ± 0.007 | Matter density (derived) |
| σ_8 | 0.811 ± 0.006 | Matter fluctuation amplitude (derived) |
| Σm_ν | < 0.12 eV (95% CL) | Sum of neutrino masses |
| N_eff | 2.99 ± 0.17 | Effective relativistic species |
| Ω_K | 0.001 ± 0.002 | Spatial curvature |

The agreement of N_eff with the Standard Model prediction of 3.046, the sub-eV neutrino mass bound, and the curvature constraint at the 10⁻³ level together constitute the most stringent confirmation of the hot Big Bang framework ever assembled [1].

### 5.2 Extensions and tensions

The base model fits superbly, yet three tensions deserve honest scrutiny [1]:

- **The Hubble tension.** Planck infers H_0 = 67.4 ± 0.5 km/s/Mpc, while the local distance ladder (SH0ES) finds ~73 km/s/Mpc — a 4–5σ discrepancy. No simple single-parameter extension resolves it without degrading the fit elsewhere.
- **The lensing amplitude.** The Planck TT spectrum prefers A_L > 1 (smoothing beyond ΛCDM prediction) at ~2.8σ, though the lensing reconstruction itself is consistent with A_L = 1 — an internal mild inconsistency, not a detection of new physics [1].
- **The S_8 tension.** Some weak-lensing surveys infer slightly lower structure growth than Planck predicts, at the 2–3σ level.

### 5.3 The B-mode frontier

BK18's r_0.05 < 0.036 (95% CL) stands as the definitive tensor bound [6]. The next generation — the Simons Observatory, CMB-S4 targeting σ(r) ≈ 0.001, and the LiteBIRD satellite — aims to probe the entire parameter space of theoretically favored plateau models. A detection at r ~ 0.003–0.01 would fix the energy scale of inflation; a null result at r < 0.001 would push inflationary model-building into qualitatively new territory.

## 6 Limitations

Several limitations qualify every result above. **Foregrounds** remain the dominant systematic for polarization: dust and synchrotron must be separated with multi-frequency data, and any mis-modeling of their spectral energy distributions or spatial correlations biases r directly — the BK18 seven-parameter foreground marginalization is state of the art, not a solved problem [6]. **Cosmic variance** sets an irreducible floor: with only one observable Universe, the low-ℓ multipoles that carry the reionization and tensor signals can never be measured better than ~(2ℓ+1)^(−1/2) fractional precision. **Secondary anisotropies** — the Sunyaev–Zel'dovich effects, the cosmic infrared background, patchy reionization — contaminate the damping tail and must be marginalized with template spectra [7]. **Theoretical priors** matter: constraints on r assume a power-law tensor spectrum and the single-field consistency relation n_t = −r/8; relaxing these weakens bounds substantially [6]. Finally, the **Hubble tension** reminds us that the CMB measures the early Universe exquisitely but infers late-Universe parameters through the model: if ΛCDM is incomplete, the celebrated parameter table inherits that incompleteness.

---

## 7 Conclusion

Half a century after Penzias and Wilson, the cosmic microwave background has become cosmology's Rosetta Stone. The acoustic peaks — explained analytically as driven oscillations of the photon-baryon fluid [2][3] — fix the geometry, baryon content, and dark matter density of the Universe; the polarization field, understood through the spin-2 E/B formalism [4][5], independently confirms the acoustic physics and measures reionization; and the B-mode search, now bounded by r < 0.036 [6], presses directly on the energy scale of inflation. The Planck 2018 parameters [1] define the standard against which all cosmological models are judged. The open questions — the Hubble tension, the physical origin of the inflaton, the primordial B-mode — are not footnotes but the frontier: the CMB has told us the composition and initial conditions of the Universe with percent precision, and its polarization may yet tell us how the Universe began.
