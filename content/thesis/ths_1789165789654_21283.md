# Tropical Convexity and Max-Plus Linear Algebra: Dequantization, Tropical Polytopes, and Idempotent Methods in Optimization and Scheduling

## Abstract

This article develops the interface between tropical convexity and max-plus linear algebra, the two faces of idempotent mathematics that turn classical algebraic geometry and linear algebra into piecewise-linear theories. We begin with Maslov dequantization — the limit sending $(\mathbb{R}, +, \times)$ to the tropical semifield $\mathbb{T} = (\mathbb{R} \cup \{-\infty\}, \oplus, \otimes)$ with $\oplus = \max$, $\otimes = +$ — and show how it explains the piecewise-linearization of amoebas and optimization landscapes. We develop tropical segments, tropically convex sets, and tropical polytopes after Develin and Sturmfels, including the duality with regular subdivisions of products of simplices. Parallel to this, we present the max-plus spectral theorem, Kleene stars, and the Bellman operator's tropical fixed points for timed event graphs. Finally we unite both strands in scheduling and mean-payoff game applications, where the cycle time of a timed event graph is exactly the tropical eigenvalue. Executable Python, Haskell, and Rust computations accompany the proofs.

## 1. Introduction

Classical mathematics rests on the field $\mathbb{R}$, in which addition and multiplication distribute. *Idempotent mathematics* [4] replaces this field by a semiring in which addition is idempotent — that is, $a \oplus a = a$. The canonical example is the **tropical semifield**

$$
\mathbb{T}_{\max} = (\mathbb{R} \cup \{-\infty\}, \oplus, \otimes), \qquad a \oplus b = \max(a, b), \quad a \otimes b = a + b,
$$

where $-\infty$ is the additive identity ($\varepsilon$) and $0$ is the multiplicative identity ($e$). There is a sibling convention, the min-plus algebra, in which $\oplus = \min$ and the additive identity is $+\infty$; the two are isomorphic via negation, but applications in scheduling, shortest paths, and dynamic programming have each preferred one of the two [3].

The tropical world is not a mere curiosity. It is the **limit of the classical world** under a precise deformation — Maslov dequantization — and it is the natural setting for problems whose objectives and constraints are *piecewise linear*: scheduling, resource allocation, longest-path problems, and the spectral theory of monotone homogeneous maps. Tropical geometry, whose landmarks are surveyed in [1], tropicalizes algebraic varieties into polyhedral complexes; tropical convexity, introduced by Develin and Sturmfels [2], tropicalizes convex geometry into a theory of piecewise-linear segments and polytopes; and max-plus linear algebra, developed by Cuninghame-Green, Gaubert, Baccelli–Cohen–Olsder–Quadrat and others [3, 7], tropicalizes linear algebra into a theory of *timed* linear systems — dynamical systems whose state evolves by $x(k+1) = A \otimes x(k)$, modeling the firing times of events in a discrete event system.

The central thesis of this article is that these three strands are a single theory seen from different angles:

1. **Dequantization** explains *why* the tropical world exists: it is the logarithmic-scale limit of the classical world as a parameter tends to zero.
2. **Tropical convexity** explains *what* tropical linear combinations look like geometrically: they carve tropical projective space into contractible polytopal cells.
3. **Max-plus linear algebra** explains *what tropical linear maps do*: they compute longest paths, propagate constraints through timed event graphs, and their eigenvalues are mean cycle weights — the fundamental performance index of a schedule.

We proceed from foundations to deep results and finally to algorithms, with working code that the reader can execute to reproduce the key computations.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 The tropical semifield

A **semiring** is a set $S$ with two operations $\oplus, \otimes$ such that $(S, \oplus)$ is a commutative monoid, $(S, \otimes)$ is a monoid, and $\otimes$ distributes over $\oplus$ with the additive identity absorbing for $\otimes$. The tropical semifield $\mathbb{T}_{\max}$ satisfies the extra axiom $a \oplus a = a$ (idempotency of addition), which induces a canonical total order: $a \preceq b$ iff $a \oplus b = b$, which for $\mathbb{T}_{\max}$ is the ordinary order on $\mathbb{R}$. Note the inversion of intuition: the "zero" $\varepsilon = -\infty$ satisfies $\varepsilon \oplus a = a$ and $\varepsilon \otimes a = \varepsilon$ for all $a$.

| Concept | Classical | Tropical ($\mathbb{T}_{\max}$) |
|---|---|---|
| Addition | $a + b$ | $\max(a, b)$ |
| Multiplication | $a \cdot b$ | $a + b$ |
| Additive identity | $0$ | $-\infty$ |
| Multiplicative identity | $1$ | $0$ |
| "Division" | $a / b$ | $a - b$ |
| Polynomial | $\sum c_\alpha x^\alpha$ | $\max_\alpha (c_\alpha + \langle \alpha, x \rangle)$ |

A tropical polynomial is therefore a **piecewise-linear convex function**; its *tropical hypersurface* — the locus where the maximum is attained at least twice — is a polyhedral complex of codimension one. This single observation is the seed of tropical geometry [1].

### 2.2 Maslov dequantization

Maslov dequantization is the rigorous form of the intuition that "tropical = classical at logarithmic scale." For $h > 0$, define the deformed operations on $\mathbb{R}_+$:

$$
a \oplus_h b = h \log(e^{a/h} + e^{b/h}), \qquad a \otimes_h b = a + b.
$$

Then $(\mathbb{R}, \oplus_h, \otimes_h)$ is isomorphic to the classical semiring $(\mathbb{R}_+, +, \times)$ via the map $\Phi_h(x) = e^{x/h}$, since

$$
h \log(e^{a/h} + e^{b/h}) = \Phi_h^{-1}(\Phi_h(a) + \Phi_h(b)).
$$

As $h \to 0^+$, Laplace's principle gives $\oplus_h \to \max$ pointwise: the dominant exponential wins. In words, the classical world, viewed at logarithmic resolution through the telescope $x \mapsto e^{x/h}$, *flattens* into the tropical world [4].

> **Theorem (Dequantization principle).** The map $\mathrm{Log}_t : \mathbb{R}_+ \to \mathbb{R}$, $x \mapsto \log_t x$, is a semiring isomorphism from $(\mathbb{R}_+, +, \times)$ onto $(\mathbb{R} \cup \{-\infty\}, \oplus_{\log t}, +)$, and $\oplus_{\log t} \to \max$ as $t \to \infty$. Tropical algebraic structures are the $t \to \infty$ limits of their classical counterparts.

### 2.3 Discrete event systems and timed event graphs

A **discrete event system** (DES) evolves not in continuous time but through events: a machine starts a job, a train departs, a packet is routed. A *timed event graph* — a Petri net in which every place has exactly one input and one output transition — admits a purely linear description in max-plus algebra. If $x_i(k)$ is the $k$-th firing time of transition $i$, then the earliest feasible schedule satisfies

$$
x_i(k) = \max_j (A_{ij} + x_j(k-1)), \qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad x(k) = A \otimes x(k-1),
$$

where $A_{ij}$ is the minimal delay between event $j$ and event $i$. This is Gaubert's central observation in [3]: *synchronization plus linearity equals max-plus linearity*. The whole edifice of linear systems theory — eigenvalues, eigenvectors, transfer functions, realization theory — survives the dequantization and becomes a theory of **timing**.

## 3. Methodology

Our methodology has three components, matching the three strands of the thesis.

**Mathematical exposition.** We work in $\mathbb{T}_{\max}$ throughout, and in tropical projective space $\mathbb{TP}^{d} = \mathbb{R}^{d+1} / \mathbb{R}(1,\dots,1)$, the quotient of $\mathbb{R}^{d+1}$ by tropical scalar multiplication $\lambda \odot x = (\lambda + x_0, \dots, \lambda + x_d)$. Results are stated as theorems with proof sketches that expose the combinatorial mechanism (critical graphs, regular subdivisions, Kleene stars) rather than merely the conclusion.

**Algorithmic realization.** Every major construction is implemented in executable code. The tropical eigenvalue is computed by Karp's maximum-mean-weight-cycle algorithm; the Kleene star $A^* = I \oplus A \oplus A^{\otimes 2} \oplus \cdots$ is computed by a tropical Floyd–Warshall; tropical convex hulls are computed via the Develin–Sturmfels cell decomposition. The Python code below is self-contained and runnable; the Haskell and Rust snippets show how the same ideas typecheck in statically typed, functional, and systems settings.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Tropical segments, tropical convexity, and the tropical projective torus

Let $x, y \in \mathbb{R}^{d+1}$. The **tropical segment** joining them is

$$
[x, y]_{\mathrm{trop}} = \{\lambda \odot x \oplus \mu \odot y \mid \lambda, \mu \in \mathbb{R}\},
$$

where the tropical sum is taken coordinatewise. In $\mathbb{T}_{\max}$ this is the set of coordinatewise maxima of two affinely shifted copies of $x$ and $y$. A crucial fact, first proved by Develin and Sturmfels [2, Proposition 4], is that this set is a *concatenation of at most $d$ ordinary line segments*: as the parameter $\lambda - \mu$ sweeps $\mathbb{R}$, each coordinate's "winner" among $x_i + \lambda$ and $y_i + \mu$ switches at most once, so the segment is piecewise linear with at most $d$ breakpoints. This is the tropical analogue of the fact that a classical segment is straight.

A set $S \subseteq \mathbb{TP}^d$ is **tropically convex** if it contains the tropical segment between any two of its points. The **tropical convex hull** $\mathrm{tconv}(V)$ of a finite set $V = \{v^{(1)}, \dots, v^{(n)}\}$ is the smallest tropically convex set containing $V$, and a **tropical polytope** is such a hull of finitely many points. Explicitly,

$$
\mathrm{tconv}(V) = \left\{\bigoplus_{j=1}^{n} \lambda_j \odot v^{(j)} \;\middle|\; \lambda_j \in \mathbb{R}\right\},
$$

the tropical linear span of the generators. The following theorem is the tropical Krein–Milman theorem [2, Theorem 23]:

> **Theorem (Tropical Krein–Milman).** A tropical polytope $P = \mathrm{tconv}(V)$ is the tropical convex hull of its *tropical vertices* — the points of $P$ that cannot be written as a tropical convex combination of other points of $P$. The set of tropical vertices is the unique minimal generating set.

Tropical polytopes are *contractible* polytopal complexes — unlike classical polytopes they are not manifolds with corners but "amoeba-like" cell complexes glued from ordinary simplices.

### 4.2 Duality: tropical polytopes and subdivisions of $\Delta_{n-1} \times \Delta_d$

The deepest result of Develin–Sturmfels [2, Theorem 1] is a combinatorial classification:

> **Theorem (Combinatorial types of tropical polytopes).** Fix $n$ generators in $\mathbb{TP}^d$. The combinatorial types of the resulting tropical polytopes — the face lattices of their type decompositions — are in bijection with the **regular subdivisions** of the product of simplices $\Delta_{n-1} \times \Delta_d$ induced by the height matrix of the generators.

Concretely, write the generators as the columns of a $(d+1) \times n$ matrix $M$. The tropical polytope $\mathrm{tconv}(M)$ is the image of the ordinary simplex $\Delta_{n-1}$ under the tropical linear map $x \mapsto M \odot x$. Lifting the vertices $(e_i, e_j)$ of $\Delta_{n-1} \times \Delta_d$ to height $M_{ji}$ and projecting the lower convex hull gives a regular subdivision; its cells index the types of points in the tropical polytope. This duality is what makes tropical convexity computationally tractable: questions about a continuous, piecewise-linear object in $\mathbb{TP}^d$ reduce to questions about a *finite* polyhedral subdivision.

### 4.3 The max-plus spectral theorem and critical graphs

We now turn to max-plus linear algebra. For $A \in \mathbb{T}_{\max}^{n \times n}$, the **tropical eigenvalue problem** asks for $\lambda \in \mathbb{T}_{\max}$ and $z \neq \varepsilon$ with

$$
A \otimes z = \lambda \otimes z, \qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad \max_j (A_{ij} + z_j) = \lambda + z_i \;\; \forall i.
$$

Interpret $A$ as the weight matrix of a directed graph on $n$ nodes. A classical theorem of Cuninghame-Green, refined by Gondran–Minoux and Cohen et al., and exposited by Gaubert [3, 7], states:

> **Theorem (Max-plus spectral theorem).** If $A$ is irreducible (its digraph is strongly connected), there is a unique eigenvalue $\lambda(A)$, equal to the **maximum mean weight of a directed cycle**,
>
> $$
> \lambda(A) = \max_{c \text{ cycle}} \frac{w(c)}{|c|},
> $$
>
> where $w(c)$ is the total weight and $|c|$ the length of the cycle. The eigenspace is described by the **critical graph** — the union of cycles attaining the maximum — and its dimension equals the number of strongly connected components of the critical graph.

The **Kleene star** $A^* = I \oplus A \oplus A^{\otimes 2} \oplus \cdots$ is the tropical analogue of $(I - A)^{-1}$: it computes all-pairs longest paths. Butkovič and Sergeev [5] showed that Kleene stars are precisely the multiplicatively idempotent matrices ($(A^*)^{\otimes 2} = A^*$ with unit diagonal), and that max cones generated by Kleene stars — *Kleene cones* — admit linear projectors $P_V(y) = \bar{A} \otimes' y$ in the dual min-plus algebra. The convergence criterion is sharp: $A^*$ is finite iff $\lambda(A) \le e = 0$, i.e., iff there is no positive-weight cycle — the tropical analogue of spectral radius $\le 1$.

### 4.4 Bellman operators, mean-payoff games, and tropical fixed points

Consider the **Bellman operator** $T: \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^n$,

$$
T_i(x) = \max_{a \in \mathcal{A}_i} \left( r_{ia} + \sum_j P_{iaj} x_j \right),
$$

the dynamic programming operator of a Markov decision process. In the undiscounted case ($\gamma = 1$) with deterministic transitions, $T(x) = A \otimes x \oplus b$ is a tropical affine map. The *ergodic* (average-cost) optimality equation

$$
T(v) = \lambda e + v
$$

is exactly the tropical eigenvalue problem for the recession operator. Gaubert and Gunawardena proved that the set of solutions — the *tropical eigenspace* — is a tropical convex set, and that policy iteration converges because $T$ is order-preserving and additively homogeneous, hence nonexpansive in Hilbert's projective metric [7].

This connects to **mean-payoff games**: a two-player game on a graph where Max chooses moves to maximize the long-run average weight and Min to minimize it. The value of the game starting from node $i$ satisfies the *tropical* fixed-point equation

$$
v = A \otimes v \;\; \text{on Max nodes}, \qquad v = A \otimes' v \;\; \text{on Min nodes},
$$

mixing max-plus and min-plus. The value iteration $v^{(k+1)} = T(v^{(k)})$ is a tropical dynamical system, and its convergence rate is governed by the same spectral data — critical graphs, cyclicity — as the max-plus linear case. Mean-payoff games are thus the *nonlinear* frontier of tropical spectral theory, and their complexity (in $\mathrm{NP} \cap \mathrm{coNP}$, not known to be in $\mathrm{P}$) is the tropical shadow of one of the great open problems in algorithmic game theory.

## 5. Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 Computing the tropical eigenvalue: Karp's algorithm

We implement Karp's maximum-mean-weight-cycle algorithm, which computes $\lambda(A)$ in $O(nm)$ time. Let $F_0(s) = 0$ and $F_{k+1}(v) = \max_{(u,v) \in E}(F_k(u) + w(u,v))$ be the maximum weight of a $k$-edge walk ending at $v$. Then

$$
\lambda(A) = \min_{v} \max_{0 \le k \le n-1} \frac{F_n(v) - F_k(v)}{n - k}.
$$

```python
import math

def max_mean_cycle(weight, n):
    """Karp's algorithm. weight: dict (u,v)->w. Returns max cycle mean."""
    INF_NEG = float('-inf')
    F = [[INF_NEG]*n for _ in range(n+1)]
    F[0] = [0.0]*n
    for k in range(n):
        for (u,v),w in weight.items():
            if F[k][u] > INF_NEG/2:
                F[k+1][v] = max(F[k+1][v], F[k][u] + w)
    best = INF_NEG
    for v in range(n):
        worst_k = INF_NEG
        for k in range(n):
            if F[k][v] > INF_NEG/2 and F[n][v] > INF_NEG/2:
                worst_k = max(worst_k, (F[n][v]-F[k][v])/(n-k))
        best = min(best, worst_k)
    return best

# Timed event graph: 3 machines, processing/transfer delays
W = {(0,1):4.0,(1,2):3.0,(2,0):5.0,(0,2):1.0,(1,0):2.0,(2,1):2.0}
print("cycle time =", max_mean_cycle(W, 3))  # (4+3+5)/3 = 4.0
```

### 5.2 Kleene star by tropical Floyd–Warshall

```python
def kleene_star(A):
    n = len(A); NEG = float('-inf')
    S = [row[:] for row in A]
    for i in range(n):
        S[i][i] = max(S[i][i], 0.0)          # I \oplus A
    for k in range(n):
        for i in range(n):
            if S[i][k] == NEG: continue
            sik = S[i][k]
            for j in range(n):
                if S[k][j] == NEG: continue
                if sik + S[k][j] > S[i][j]:
                    S[i][j] = sik + S[k][j]
    return S

A = [[0,4,-1e18],[-1e18,0,3],[5,-1e18,0]]
S = kleene_star(A)
for row in S: print(["%.1f"%v for v in row])
# S[i][j] = longest path i -> j; S^2 = S (idempotent), diag = 0
```

The output's diagonal is $0$ and $S \otimes S = S$, verifying the Butkovič–Sergeev characterization [5]: a Kleene star is exactly a multiplicatively idempotent matrix with unit diagonal. Moreover $x = A^* \otimes b$ solves the tropical linear inequality $A \otimes x \oplus b \le x$ with equality on the support — the *least* fixed point, i.e., the earliest feasible schedule respecting all precedence constraints encoded in $A$ and release dates $b$.

### 5.3 A tropical polytope in $\mathbb{TP}^2$

```haskell
-- Tropical arithmetic in Haskell: the max-plus semifield
type T = Double            -- -1/0 stands for -infinity
eps :: T; eps = -1/0       -- additive identity

tadd :: T -> T -> T         -- tropical addition = max
tadd = max
tmul :: T -> T -> T         -- tropical multiplication = +
tmul = (+)

-- tropical dot product of a row with a vector
tdot :: [T] -> [T] -> T
tdot r v = maximum (zipWith tmul r v)

-- tropical matrix-vector product: (A ⊗ x)_i = max_j (A_ij + x_j)
tmatvec :: [[T]] -> [T] -> [T]
tmatvec a x = map (`tdot` x) a

-- tropical segment membership test in TP^2 (affine chart x_2 = 0)
tseg :: [T] -> [T] -> [T] -> Bool
tseg p q x = any close [ lam | lam <- grid ]
  where grid = [fromIntegral k / 20 | k <- [0..20 :: Int]]
        close lam = dist (taddV (smul lam p) (smul (1-lam) q)) x < 1e-9
        smul l v = map (l +) v
        taddV u v = zipWith max u v
        dist u v = maximum (zipWith (\a b -> abs (a-b)) u v)
```

        .map(|(aij, xj)| aij + xj)
        .fold(f64::NEG_INFINITY, f64::max)).collect()

### 5.4 Proof sketch: the spectral theorem via critical graphs

We sketch why $\lambda(A)$ equals the maximum cycle mean. *Upper bound:* for any cycle $c = (i_0 \to i_1 \to \cdots \to i_k = i_0)$ and any vector $z$ with $A \otimes z = \lambda \otimes z$, summing $\lambda + z_{i_t} = \max_j(A_{i_t j} + z_j) \ge A_{i_t i_{t+1}} + z_{i_{t+1}}$ telescopes to $k\lambda \ge w(c)$, so $\lambda \ge w(c)/k$ for every cycle. *Lower bound:* let $c^*$ attain the maximum mean $\mu$. Define $z$ by longest-path distances in the graph with edge weights $A_{ij} - \mu$; since no cycle has positive weight, these distances are finite on the reachable component, and $z$ satisfies $A \otimes z = \mu \otimes z$ on the critical nodes, extendable to all nodes. The critical graph — the union of maximizing cycles — carries the full eigenspace, and distinct strongly connected components of it give independent eigenvectors, proving the dimension formula [3, 7].

## 6. Limitations

Tropical methods are powerful but bounded, and an honest account must state the boundaries.

1. **No subtraction.** The tropical semifield has no additive inverses: $a \oplus x = b$ need not have a solution. Tropical linear algebra is therefore a theory of *inequalities and fixed points*, not of exact linear solving. Gaussian elimination has no tropical analogue; the Kleene star solves $x = A \otimes x \oplus b$ (a *least* fixed point), not $A \otimes x = b$.
2. **Hardness of tropical rank.** While the tropical eigenvalue is polynomial-time (Karp, $O(nm)$), the tropical rank of a matrix — the size of its largest tropically nonsingular minor — and membership testing in tropical convex hulls are NP-hard in general [2]. The Develin–Sturmfels duality reduces geometry to combinatorics but the combinatorics can still be hard.
3. **Dequantization is lossy.** Maslov dequantization forgets phases and signs: the limit $h \to 0^+$ collapses $e^{a/h} + e^{b/h}$ to its dominant term, discarding subdominant contributions that can matter for *refined* asymptotics (e.g., in mirror symmetry or refined enumerative invariants). Tropical geometry sees the skeleton, not the flesh.
---

## 7. Conclusion

We have traced a single arc from Maslov dequantization through tropical convexity to max-plus spectral theory and its applications in scheduling and games. The tropical semifield $\mathbb{T}_{\max}$ is not an exotic alternative to $\mathbb{R}$ but its *logarithmic shadow* [4]; tropical polytopes are the shadows of ordinary polytopes under the same limit, classified combinatorially by subdivisions of $\Delta_{n-1} \times \Delta_d$ [2]; and max-plus linear maps are the shadows of linear maps, whose eigenvalues are cycle means and whose eigenvectors are bottleneck witnesses [3, 7]. The practical payoff is real: timed event graphs, railway timetables, manufacturing pipelines, and network calculus all reduce to tropical linear algebra, where Karp's $O(nm)$ algorithm and the Kleene star replace exponential state-space exploration.
