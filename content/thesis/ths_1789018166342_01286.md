# Primal–Dual Interior-Point Methods for Semidefinite Programming: Nesterov–Todd Scaling, Self-Concordant Barriers, and Large-Scale Alternatives

## Abstract

Semidefinite programming (SDP) is the crown jewel of tractable convex optimization: minimizing a linear function over the intersection of the positive semidefinite cone with an affine subspace unifies linear programming, quadratically constrained quadratic programming, eigenvalue optimization, and a vast array of combinatorial relaxations, from Goemans–Williamson Max-Cut bounds to sum-of-squares hierarchies [1]. This thesis develops the complete algorithmic and theoretical apparatus of primal–dual interior-point methods (IPMs) for SDP. We begin with the primal–dual formulation and the central path, introduce self-concordant barriers with emphasis on the logarithmic-determinant barrier, and construct the Nesterov–Todd scaling — the unique symmetric primal–dual metric that reduces SDP path-following to its LP-like essentials [2][3]. We analyze the Monteiro–Zhang symmetrization family (HKM, NT, AHO directions), prove polynomial iteration complexity of order $O(\sqrt{n}\log(1/\varepsilon))$, and survey the large-scale challengers that dominate practice today: alternating direction methods of multipliers (ADMM/SDPAD/SDPNAL) [6][7] and the Burer–Monteiro low-rank factorization [4][5]. The tension between polynomial-time guarantees and million-variable scalability is the central thread of this work.

## 1. Introduction

Consider the standard primal–dual pair of semidefinite programs. The primal is

$$\min_{X \in \mathbb{S}^n}\ \langle C, X \rangle \quad \text{s.t.}\ \ \langle A_i, X \rangle = b_i,\ i = 1,\dots,m,\ \ X \succeq 0 \tag{P}$$

and the dual is

$$\max_{y \in \mathbb{R}^m, S \in \mathbb{S}^n}\ b^\top y \quad \text{s.t.}\ \ \sum_{i=1}^m y_i A_i + S = C,\ \ S \succeq 0 \tag{D}$$

where $\langle C, X \rangle = \mathrm{tr}(CX)$ and $\mathbb{S}^n_+$ is the cone of symmetric positive semidefinite matrices [1]. The gap between a primal-feasible $X$ and a dual-feasible $(y, S)$ is $\langle C, X \rangle - b^\top y = \langle X, S \rangle \ge 0$, and optimality is certified when this duality gap vanishes.

Linear programming is the special case in which all matrices are diagonal. Interior-point methods for LP, beginning with Karmarkar's 1984 algorithm and refined through the primal–dual path-following framework of Kojima, Mizuno, and Yoshise, achieve both polynomial complexity and extraordinary practical performance. The central discovery reviewed here is that essentially this entire machinery lifts to SDP with only *one* new ingredient: a symmetric way of measuring how far a primal–dual pair is from the central path. That ingredient is the **Nesterov–Todd scaling** [2][3].

> **Theorem:** *(Polynomial complexity of SDP path-following.)* Under strict feasibility of (P) and (D), there exists a feasible primal–dual path-following algorithm with the Nesterov–Todd direction that reduces the duality gap to $\varepsilon$ in $O(\sqrt{n}\log((\langle X_0, S_0\rangle)/\varepsilon))$ Newton iterations, where $n$ is the matrix order.

This bound mirrors the LP bound $O(\sqrt{n}\log(1/\varepsilon))$ exactly: semidefinite programs are, in a precise iteration-complexity sense, no harder than linear programs [8][9].

## 2. Background

### 2.1 From LP to SDP: the central path

For a barrier parameter $\mu > 0$, consider the penalized problem $\min_{X \succeq 0} \langle C, X \rangle + \mu\, F(X)$ with $F(X) = -\log\det X$, restricted to the affine feasible set. Its unique minimizer $X(\mu)$, together with the associated dual iterate $S(\mu) = \mu X(\mu)^{-1}$, traces the **primal–dual central path**, characterized by the perturbed KKT system:

$$\langle A_i, X \rangle = b_i,\quad \sum_i y_i A_i + S = C,\quad XS = \mu I,\quad X \succ 0,\ S \succ 0 \tag{C_\mu}$$

When $n$ reduces to diagonal matrices, $XS = \mu I$ becomes the familiar LP complementarity $x_i s_i = \mu$. The product $XS$ of two *symmetric* matrices is generally not symmetric, however, and this single asymmetry is the entire technical difficulty of SDP interior-point theory: the Newton linearization of $(C_\mu)$ is underdetermined and non-symmetric, so one must *symmetrize* before Newtonizing [3].

### 2.2 Self-concordant barriers

A $C^3$ convex function $F$ on an open convex domain is *self-concordant* with parameter $\vartheta$ if it satisfies the differential inequality controlling its third derivative by its Hessian,

$$|D^3 F(x)[h,h,h]| \le 2\,\|h\|_x^3,\qquad \langle\nabla F(x), [\nabla^2 F(x)]^{-1}\nabla F(x)\rangle \le \vartheta,$$

where $\|h\|_x^2 = \langle \nabla^2 F(x) h, h \rangle$ is the local norm. The number $\vartheta$ is the *complexity value* of the barrier, and it directly controls the iteration complexity of path-following: every standard short-step method needs $O(\sqrt{\vartheta}\log(1/\varepsilon))$ iterations [8].

For the PSD cone, the *logarithmic-determinant barrier* $F(X) = -\log\det X$ is self-concordant with parameter $\vartheta = n$, attaining the optimal possible value for the $n \times n$ semidefinite cone. Its gradient and Hessian are

$$\nabla F(X) = -X^{-1},\qquad \nabla^2 F(X)[H, H] = \mathrm{tr}(X^{-1} H X^{-1} H) = \|X^{-1/2} H X^{-1/2}\|_F^2,$$

so the local norm at $X$ is simply a congruence-scaled Frobenius norm. Crucially, the Hessian maps the interior of the cone onto the interior of its dual cone — and the PSD cone is self-dual — which places SDP squarely inside the Nesterov–Todd theory of **self-scaled** cones [2][3].

### 2.3 Symmetric cones and the self-scaled property

A cone $K$ is *symmetric* if it is self-dual and homogeneous (its automorphism group acts transitively on its interior). By the Koecher–Vinberg theorem, symmetric cones are exactly the cones of squares of Euclidean Jordan algebras; the PSD cone, the second-order cone, and the nonnegative orthant are the three primitive examples. Nesterov and Todd showed that on symmetric cones there exist *self-scaled barriers*: barriers whose Hessian at any interior point provides a scaling of the cone onto its dual, and for which, given any $x \in \mathrm{int}\,K$ and $s \in \mathrm{int}\,K^*$, there exists a *unique scaling point* $w$ with $\nabla^2 F(w)\, x = s$ [2][3]. This uniqueness is the deep fact that makes primal–dual symmetry possible.

## 3. Methodology

### 3.1 The Newton system and the symmetrization problem

Linearizing the perturbed KKT conditions $(C_\mu)$ at $(X, y, S)$ with target parameter $\sigma\mu$ yields the Newton equations

$$\begin{aligned}
\langle A_i, \Delta X \rangle &= 0, \\
\sum_i \Delta y_i A_i + \Delta S &= 0, \\
X\,\Delta S + \Delta X\,S &= \sigma\mu I - XS,
\end{aligned}\tag{N}$$

where the last equation has $n^2$ scalar entries but $\Delta X, \Delta S$ are symmetric ($n(n+1)/2$ degrees of freedom each). The system is overdetermined. The standard remedy, due to Monteiro and Zhang, is to replace the complementarity equation by a *symmetrized* form $H_P(XS + X\Delta S + \Delta XS - \sigma\mu I) = 0$ for an invertible linear operator $H_P$ on $\mathbb{S}^n$; different choices of $P$ produce different search directions [10].

### 3.2 The Nesterov–Todd direction

The Nesterov–Todd (NT) choice sets $P = W^{-1/2}$, where $W$ is the unique scaling matrix satisfying

$$W S W = X \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad W = X^{1/2}\big(X^{1/2} S X^{1/2}\big)^{-1/2} X^{1/2}, \tag{NT}$$

so that in the scaled space $\tilde{X} = W^{-1/2} X W^{-1/2}$ and $\tilde{S} = W^{1/2} S W^{1/2}$ coincide and commute: $\tilde{X} = \tilde{S} = V$ for a single positive definite $V$. In this frame the symmetrized complementarity equation becomes exactly the LP-like equation

$$\Delta\tilde{X} + \Delta\tilde{S} = \sigma\mu V^{-1} - V,$$

and the entire convergence analysis collapses to the classical LP analysis of Kojima–Mizuno–Yoshise. The NT direction is the default in SeDuMi, SDPT3, and MOSEK precisely because of this property: it is the *only* member of the Monteiro–Zhang family for which primal and dual variables are scaled by a single common metric [3].

### 3.3 A primal–dual path-following algorithm

The algorithm we analyze is the feasible short-step path-following method:

1. **Initialize** at a strictly feasible $(X_0, y_0, S_0)$ with $\langle X_0, S_0 \rangle = n\mu_0$.
2. **Repeat** until $\langle X, S \rangle \le \varepsilon$:
   - Compute the scaling $W$ from $(X, S)$ via (NT).
   - Solve the symmetrized Newton system for $(\Delta X, \Delta y, \Delta S)$ with centering parameter $\sigma = 1 - \delta/\sqrt{n}$.
   - Update $(X, y, S) \leftarrow (X, y, S) + (\Delta X, \Delta y, \Delta S)$ (unit step; proximity to the path guarantees $X, S \succ 0$).

The per-iteration work is dominated by forming and factorizing the *Schur complement* matrix $M \in \mathbb{S}^m$, $M_{ij} = \langle A_i, W A_j W \rangle$, at cost $O(mn^3 + m^2 n^2)$ in the dense case — the fundamental scalability bottleneck discussed in Section 4.4 [1].

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Self-concordance and why $\vartheta = n$ is optimal

The iteration bound $O(\sqrt{\vartheta}\log(1/\varepsilon))$ follows from two facts: (i) the Newton decrement controls the barrier suboptimality, and (ii) the barrier parameter $\mu$ can be multiplied by $1 - \Omega(1/\sqrt{\vartheta})$ at each step while remaining inside the quadratic-convergence neighborhood of the path. For $F(X) = -\log\det X$ one checks directly that $\langle \nabla F(X), [\nabla^2 F(X)]^{-1}\nabla F(X)\rangle = \mathrm{tr}(X X^{-1}) = n$, so $\vartheta = n$. Güler proved that $n$ is the *minimal* possible complexity value of any self-concordant barrier for $\mathbb{S}^n_+$ [11]; the log-det barrier is therefore optimal among all barriers, and the $O(\sqrt{n})$ bound cannot be improved by a better barrier choice.

The self-scaled property strengthens self-concordance in exactly the way path-following needs: for self-scaled barriers, the Hessian approximations remain valid not merely within the unit Dikin ellipsoid but up to a constant fraction of the distance to the boundary in *every* direction. This is what licenses long steps in practice while short steps carry the theory [2].

### 4.2 Geometry of the Nesterov–Todd scaling

Given $X \succ 0$ and $S \succ 0$, the scaling point $w$ with $\nabla^2 F(w)^{-1} \nabla F$ mapping $X$ to $S$ has a closed form for the log-det barrier: since $\nabla^2 F(W)[H] = W^{-1} H W^{-1}$, the condition $\nabla^2 F(W)^{-1}(-X^{-1}) = -S$ is equivalent to $W X^{-1} W = S^{-1}$, i.e. $W S W = X$. The explicit solution (NT) is the *matrix geometric mean* of $X$ and $S^{-1}$,

$$W = X \,\#\, S^{-1},$$

and $W$ simultaneously diagonalizes the congruence: $W^{-1/2} X W^{-1/2}$ and $W^{1/2} S W^{1/2}$ commute and share eigenvalues $\lambda_i = \sqrt{\lambda_i(XS)}$. The duality gap satisfies $\langle X, S \rangle = \|V\|_F^2 = \sum_i \lambda_i^2$ in the scaled frame, and the proximity measure

$$d(X, S) = \|V^2/\mu - I\|_F$$

controls both the step length and the centrality. When $d < 1$, the full Newton step stays strictly feasible — the engine of the short-step analysis [3].

### 4.3 The Monteiro–Zhang family: HKM, dual-HKM, AHO, NT

The general symmetrization $H_P(M) = \tfrac{1}{2}(PMP^{-1} + P^{-\top}M^\top P^\top)$ unifies all practical directions [10]:

| Direction | Choice of $P$ | Properties |
|---|---|---|
| **HKM** (Helmberg–Kojima–Monteiro) | $P = S^{1/2}$ | Primal-oriented; simple Schur complement |
| **Dual HKM** | $P = X^{-1/2}$ | Dual-oriented; mirror of HKM |
| **AHO** (Alizadeh–Haeberly–Overton) | $P = I$ | $X\Delta S + \Delta X S + \text{sym}$; strong local convergence but delicate |
| **NT** (Nesterov–Todd) | $P = W^{-1/2}$ | Symmetric; LP-like analysis; default in MOSEK/SDPT3/SeDuMi |

All four achieve $O(\sqrt{n}\log(1/\varepsilon))$ short-step complexity [10]. Long-step variants follow the same pattern with $O(n\log(1/\varepsilon))$ guarantees, but in practice take much larger steps: predictor–corrector implementations (Mehrotra's heuristic adapted to SDP) routinely solve problems with $n \sim 10^3$ in 20–40 iterations. The AHO direction enjoys quadratic local convergence under nondegeneracy but requires careful handling near the boundary, which is why production codes standardize on NT [3][10].

### 4.4 Large-scale alternatives: ADMM and Burer–Monteiro

The per-iteration cost $O(mn^3 + m^2 n^2)$ of IPMs is fatal when $n$ exceeds a few thousand: forming the Schur complement alone exhausts memory. Two families of methods dominate large-scale SDP in practice.

**ADMM and augmented Lagrangian methods.** The alternating direction method of multipliers splits the SDP into projections onto the PSD cone (an eigenvalue thresholding, $O(n^3)$ per iteration but with tiny constants and no linear system) and projections onto the affine subspace. SDPAD (Wen, Goldfarb, Yin) demonstrated that a well-engineered ADMM solves SDPs with $n$ in the tens of thousands to moderate accuracy ($10^{-4}$–$10^{-6}$) [6]. SDPNAL and its successor SDPNAL+ (Zhao, Sun, Toh; Yang, Sun, Toh) wrap an inexact semismooth Newton–CG solver inside an augmented Lagrangian / majorized ADMM framework, achieving $10^{-6}$ relative KKT residuals on 98.9% of 745 large test instances — the current gold standard for first-order SDP at scale [6][7]. The price is the convergence rate: $O(1/k)$ ergodic rates, so high accuracy is expensive.

**Burer–Monteiro factorization.** Burer and Monteiro proposed replacing $X \succeq 0$ by the explicit factorization $X = YY^\top$ with $Y \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times p}$, converting the SDP into a nonconvex problem in only $np$ variables [4]. The Barvinok–Pataki bound guarantees an optimal solution of rank $r$ with $r(r+1)/2 \le m$, so choosing $p$ with $p(p+1)/2 \ge m$ preserves the global optimum; Burer and Monteiro proved that for such $p$, second-order stationary points of the factorized problem are globally optimal (under a mild constraint qualification). The breakthrough of Boumal, Voroninski, and Bandeira showed that for *smooth* SDPs with $p$ above the Barvinok–Pataki threshold, the factorized landscape has no spurious local minima at all, explaining the empirical miracle that local methods (Riemannian trust-region on the manifold $\{Y : \mathcal{A}(YY^\top) = b\}$) reliably recover global optima [5]. Modern solvers such as SDPLR and ManiSDP exploit this to handle $n \sim 10^5$ with $p \ll n$.

## 5. Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 Complexity proof sketch (NT short-step)

Let $\mu_k = \langle X_k, S_k \rangle / n$ and define the scaled variable $V_k = W_k^{-1/2} X_k W_k^{-1/2} = W_k^{1/2} S_k W_k^{1/2}$. With proximity $\delta(V, \mu) = \|V^2/\mu - I\|_F \le \beta < 1$ and centering $\sigma = 1 - \delta/\sqrt{n}$, the Newton step satisfies:

1. **Feasibility preservation.** If $\delta \le \beta$ with $\beta$ small enough (e.g. $\beta = 1/4$), then $X + \Delta X \succ 0$ and $S + \Delta S \succ 0$; the step cannot cross the cone boundary because the Dikin ellipsoid of radius 1 is contained in the cone [8][3].
2. **Proximity restoration.** The new iterate satisfies $\delta(V_+, \sigma\mu) \le \beta$; i.e., one full Newton step recenters for the reduced parameter. This uses the self-scaled Hessian estimates, which hold far beyond the unit Dikin ball — the decisive advantage over generic self-concordance [2].
3. **Geometric gap reduction.** $\mu_{k+1} = \sigma \mu_k = (1 - \delta/\sqrt{n})\mu_k$.

Iterating, $\mu_k \le \varepsilon$ after $k = O(\sqrt{n}\log(\mu_0/\varepsilon))$ steps. The $\sqrt{n}$ arises as $\sqrt{\vartheta}$ with the optimal barrier value $\vartheta = n$; no barrier can do better [11].

> **Theorem:** *(NT short-step complexity.)* Let $(X_0, y_0, S_0)$ be strictly feasible with proximity $\delta_0 \le \beta$. Then the NT path-following algorithm with $\sigma = 1 - \delta/\sqrt{n}$ produces $\langle X_k, S_k \rangle \le \varepsilon$ within $O\big(\sqrt{n}\,\log(n\mu_0/\varepsilon)\big)$ iterations, each requiring one Cholesky factorization of the $m \times m$ Schur complement.

### 5.2 Numerical behavior

The following table summarizes representative behavior on DIMACS-style benchmark families (values are typical orders reported across solver benchmarks [1][6]):

| Solver family | $n$ range | Iterations (typical) | Accuracy | Cost/iter |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| NT path-following (MOSEK/SDPT3) | $10^2$–$2\times10^3$ | 15–40 | $10^{-8}$ | $O(mn^3 + m^2n^2)$ |
| ADMM (SDPAD) | $10^3$–$5\times10^4$ | $10^3$–$10^4$ | $10^{-4}$–$10^{-6}$ | $O(n^3)$ eigendecomposition |
| SDPNAL+ | $10^3$–$10^4$ | $10^2$–$10^3$ outer | $10^{-6}$ KKT | Newton–CG inner solves |
| Burer–Monteiro (rank $p$) | $10^4$–$10^5$ | $10^2$–$10^3$ | $10^{-6}$ | $O(np^2 + mp^2)$ |

The crossover is sharp: below $n \approx 2000$ IPMs are unchallenged on accuracy-per-second; above $n \approx 10^4$ only factorization or first-order methods fit in memory at all [5][6].

### 5.3 Reference implementation sketch

The core of an NT path-following step in Julia-like pseudocode, illustrating the Schur complement assembly that dominates the cost:

```julia
function nt_step!(X, y, S, A, b, C, sigma, mu)
    # Nesterov--Todd scaling: W = X^{1/2} (X^{1/2} S X^{1/2})^{-1/2} X^{1/2}
    Xs = sqrtm(X); M = Xs * S * Xs
    W = Xs * inv(sqrtm(M)) * Xs
    Winv = inv(W)
    # Schur complement Mmat[i,j] = <A_i, W A_j W>
    m = length(A)
    Mmat = [dot(A[i], W * A[j] * W) for i in 1:m, j in 1:m]
    # rhs from linearized feasibility + symmetrized complementarity
    rhs = b - [dot(A[i], X) for i in 1:m]  # + centering terms in scaled space
    dy = Mmat \ rhs
    dS = -(sum(dy[i] * A[i] for i in 1:m))
    # solve for dX from symmetrized complementarity: H_W(dX) = sigma*mu*I - H_W(XS)
    dX = solve_symmetrized(W, X, S, dS, sigma, mu)
    return dX, dy, dS
end
```

A minimal Python prototype of the log-det barrier oracle (gradient/Hessian) used in every IPM line search:

```python
import numpy as np

def logdet_oracle(X):
    """Barrier F(X) = -log det X with gradient and Hessian action."""
    L = np.linalg.cholesky(X)
    logdet = 2.0 * np.sum(np.log(np.diag(L)))
    Xinv = np.linalg.inv(X)
    grad = -Xinv
    def hess_action(H):
        return Xinv @ H @ Xinv
    return -logdet, grad, hess_action
```

## 6. Limitations

The theory above is sharp but narrow, and honest accounting requires stating where it breaks.

1. **Strict feasibility and the $O(mn^3)$ wall.** The analysis assumes strictly feasible starting points; infeasible-start methods (the practical default, e.g. the homogeneous self-dual embedding) have weaker guarantees. Worse, the Schur complement $M_{ij} = \langle A_i, W A_j W \rangle$ is dense even for sparse data, so memory scales as $O(m^2 + n^2)$ and time as $O(mn^3 + m^2n^2)$ per iteration. For $n = 10^4$, $m = 10^5$ this is simply not computable — the regime where ADMM and Burer–Monteiro take over [5][6].
2. **Degeneracy and the central path's endgame.** When strict complementarity fails (common in combinatorial relaxations), the central path approaches the optimal face tangentially and the Jacobian of the KKT system becomes singular; superlinear convergence is lost and final digits are expensive. SDPNAL+'s majorization machinery was built precisely to survive this degeneracy [7].
3. **Non-symmetric cones are excluded.** The entire Nesterov–Todd edifice rests on symmetric cones (Jordan-algebra structure). Exponential cones, power cones, and other non-symmetric cones admit no self-scaled barrier, and extending NT-like primal–dual symmetry there remains an active research frontier [3].
4. **Burer–Monteiro's rank gamble.** The no-spurious-minima guarantee [5] requires $p$ above the Barvinok–Pataki bound $p(p+1)/2 \ge m$ plus smoothness/compactness qualifications; with $p$ chosen too small (the tempting practical choice), the method can and does converge to spurious stationary points with no certificate of global optimality.

## 7. Conclusion

Primal–dual interior-point methods for semidefinite programming represent one of the cleanest generalizations in optimization: by replacing the LP central path $x_i s_i = \mu$ with $XS = \mu I$ and symmetrizing through the Nesterov–Todd scaling $W = X \# S^{-1}$, the entire Kojima–Mizuno–Yoshise analysis transfers verbatim, yielding $O(\sqrt{n}\log(1/\varepsilon))$ iteration complexity with the optimal log-det barrier [2][3][8][9]. The Monteiro–Zhang family organizes the practical directions (HKM, NT, AHO) under one algebraic roof [10].

Yet the $O(mn^3)$ per-iteration cost imposes a hard ceiling that theory cannot lift, and the field has bifurcated accordingly: high-accuracy medium-scale SDP belongs to NT path-following (MOSEK, SDPT3, SeDuMi), while large-scale SDP belongs to first-order augmented Lagrangian methods (SDPNAL+) [6][7] and nonconvex low-rank factorization (Burer–Monteiro) [4][5] — the latter justified by a striking landscape result that turns nonconvexity from a bug into a feature. The central open challenge is a method with the polynomial guarantees of IPMs and the scalability of factorization: the $O(\sqrt{n})$ iteration bound with $O(np^2)$ per-iteration cost remains, to this day, unwritten.
