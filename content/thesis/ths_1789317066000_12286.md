# The Renormalization Group: Wilsonian Flows, Critical Exponents, and the Epsilon Expansion

## Abstract

The renormalization group (RG) of Kenneth Wilson recast the theory of continuous phase transitions as the study of flows in an infinite-dimensional space of Hamiltonians, explaining universality, scaling, and the anomalous values of critical exponents from a single geometric principle: the fixed point. This article develops the Wilsonian RG from its origins in Kadanoff's block-spin scaling picture through the linearized analysis of fixed points — relevant, irrelevant, and marginal eigenoperators — to the epsilon expansion of Wilson and Fisher, which revealed a nontrivial fixed point of the Landau–Ginzburg–Wilson Hamiltonian in dimensions just below four and produced the first analytic exponents differing from mean-field values [1]. We derive the beta function and exponent series for the O(n) model, present the scaling relations as exact consequences of RG homogeneity, survey the modern functional RG formulation due to Wetterich [4], and confront perturbative predictions with high-precision experimental, Monte Carlo, and conformal-bootstrap determinations [5]. Limitations — asymptotic series, truncation artifacts, and the open rigorous status of the three-dimensional fixed point — are examined.

## 1. Introduction

A continuous phase transition is distinguished by the *divergence of the correlation length* $\xi$: as the reduced temperature $t = (T - T_c)/T_c$ tends to zero, correlated fluctuations of the order parameter persist out to macroscopic wavelengths, and fluctuations on *every intermediate scale* contribute comparably to thermodynamic observables. This is precisely the circumstance in which Landau's mean-field theory fails. Landau theory treats fluctuations as small corrections to a uniform order parameter, an assumption controlled by the Ginzburg criterion — and that criterion is violated in a shrinking but nonzero window around $T_c$ whenever the spatial dimension lies below the upper critical dimension $d_c = 4$. Inside this *critical region*, perturbation theory in the interaction strength breaks down not because the coupling is large, but because there is no small parameter at all: the problem is scale-invariant.

The way out was proposed by Leo Kadanoff in 1966. Kadanoff imagined grouping spins into blocks, replacing each block by a single effective spin, and rescaling lengths so that the block lattice looks like the original — a *scaling picture* from which the relations among critical exponents could be derived as homogeneity laws, though the picture itself supplied no computational engine. Kenneth Wilson's 1971 papers supplied that engine: the RG transformation as an explicit, if approximate, map on the space of Hamiltonians, whose fixed points classify all possible critical behaviors [2]. A year later, Wilson and Fisher showed that the new fixed point — the *Wilson–Fisher fixed point* — could be reached perturbatively by expanding around four dimensions, where the Gaussian fixed point loses stability [1]. The resulting epsilon expansion, in powers of $\varepsilon = 4 - d$, gave the first analytic critical exponents that differed from their classical values, and it launched a research program that continues today in the functional RG, the conformal bootstrap, and precision experiments on helium and magnets [5][6].

This article is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews the phenomenological background: order parameters, the definitions of the critical exponents, Landau theory, and the Ginzburg criterion. Section 3 develops the RG methodology — coarse-graining, fixed points, linearized flows, and beta functions. Section 4 is the technical core: the Wilson–Fisher fixed point in the O(n) model, the derivation of exponent series, the exact functional RG equation of Wetterich, and the theory of corrections to scaling. Section 5 confronts theory with numbers, and Section 6 surveys limitations.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 Critical exponents and the scaling hypothesis

Near a continuous transition, thermodynamic quantities obey power laws in the reduced temperature $t$ and the ordering field $h$:

- **Specific heat:** $C \sim |t|^{-\alpha}$,
- **Order parameter:** $M \sim (-t)^{\beta}$ for $t < 0$,
- **Susceptibility:** $\chi \sim |t|^{-\gamma}$,
- **Critical isotherm:** $M \sim h^{1/\delta}$ at $t = 0$,
- **Correlation length:** $\xi \sim |t|^{-\nu}$,
- **Correlator:** $G(r) \sim r^{-(d-2+\eta)}$ at $t = 0$.

The exponents $\alpha, \beta, \gamma, \delta, \nu, \eta$ are *universal*: the liquid–vapor critical point, binary alloys, and uniaxial ferromagnets share the same exponents because they share the same fixed point, despite utterly different microscopics. Kadanoff's great insight was that the singular free energy is a *generalized homogeneous function*,

$$f_s(t, h) = b^{-d}\, f_s(b^{y_t} t,\, b^{y_h} h),$$

for arbitrary rescaling factor $b$, with two independent exponents $y_t, y_h > 0$. Differentiating with respect to $h$ and setting $b^{y_h}h = 1$ yields $M \sim |t|^{(d-y_h)/y_t}$, i.e. $\beta = (d - y_h)/y_t$; similar manipulations give $\alpha = 2 - d/y_t$ and $\gamma = (2y_h - d)/y_t$. Eliminating $y_t, y_h$ produces the *scaling relations*

> **Theorem (Scaling relations):** The six exponents satisfy $\alpha + 2\beta + \gamma = 2$ (Rushbrooke), $\gamma = \beta(\delta - 1)$ (Widom), $\alpha = 2 - d\nu$ (hyperscaling), $\gamma = \nu(2-\eta)$, $\beta = \nu(d-2+\eta)/2$, and $\delta = (d+2-\eta)/(d-2+\eta)$, leaving only two independent exponents (conventionally $\nu$ and $\eta$).

These relations were conjectured from Kadanoff's picture and thermodynamics; the RG *derives* them, with $y_t, y_h$ identified as the relevant eigenvalues of the linearized flow.

### 2.2 Landau theory and its breakdown

The Landau–Ginzburg–Wilson Hamiltonian for a scalar order parameter reads

$$\mathcal{H} = \int d^dx\,\left[ \tfrac{1}{2}(\nabla\phi)^2 + \tfrac{1}{2}r_0\,\phi^2 + \tfrac{u_0}{4!}\,\phi^4 - h\,\phi \right], \qquad r_0 \propto t.$$

Saddle-point evaluation gives the classical exponents

| Exponent | $\alpha$ | $\beta$ | $\gamma$ | $\delta$ | $\nu$ | $\eta$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Mean-field value | $1/2$ | $1/2$ | $1$ | $3$ | $1/2$ | $0$ |

Mean-field theory is self-consistent only when fluctuation corrections to the saddle point are small. Comparing the fluctuation contribution to the specific heat with the mean-field jump gives the **Ginzburg criterion**: mean-field theory holds only for $|t| \gg t_G$, where $t_G \sim (u_0)^{2/(4-d)}$ vanishes as $d \to 4$ from below. For $d < 4$ there is always a critical region, however narrow, in which fluctuations dominate and Landau theory is qualitatively wrong — most dramatically, it predicts $\eta = 0$ whereas the true correlator decays with an anomalous dimension.

The special role of four dimensions is dimensional analysis: the quartic coupling $u_0$ has engineering dimension $[u_0] = 4 - d$, so it is relevant for $d < 4$, marginal at $d = 4$, and irrelevant for $d > 4$. Above four dimensions the Gaussian fixed point is stable and mean-field exponents are exact (up to the *dangerous irrelevant variable* subtlety of Section 4.4); below four, a new fixed point must take over. Wilson's RG constructs it.

## 3. Methodology

### 3.1 The Wilsonian RG transformation

The RG transformation $\mathcal{R}_b$ acts on Hamiltonians in three steps:

1. **Coarse-graining.** Integrate out the short-wavelength Fourier modes with momenta in the shell $\Lambda/b < q < \Lambda$, where $\Lambda$ is the ultraviolet cutoff and $b > 1$ the rescaling factor. This produces an effective Hamiltonian for the remaining slow modes, with renormalized couplings.
2. **Rescaling.** Restore the original cutoff by dilating lengths, $x' = x/b$ (equivalently $q' = bq$), so that the new Hamiltonian is defined on the same momentum range.
3. **Field renormalization.** Rescale the field, $\phi' = Z^{-1/2}\phi$, so that the kinetic term retains its canonical normalization $(\nabla\phi')^2/2$.

The composition defines a semigroup (not a group — coarse-graining cannot be inverted) acting on the infinite-dimensional *theory space* of all couplings consistent with the symmetries:

$$\mathbf{K}' = \mathcal{R}_b(\mathbf{K}), \qquad \mathbf{K} = (r, u, v_6, w_{\phi^2(\nabla\phi)^2}, \ldots).$$

The ellipsis is essential: coarse-graining *generates* every symmetry-allowed operator, so the flow lives in infinitely many dimensions even if one starts with two couplings [2].

### 3.2 Fixed points and linearized flows

A **fixed point** $\mathbf{K}^*$ satisfies $\mathcal{R}_b(\mathbf{K}^*) = \mathbf{K}^*$ for all $b$. Near it, write $\mathbf{K} = \mathbf{K}^* + \delta\mathbf{K}$ and linearize:

$$\delta K_i' = \sum_j M_{ij}\,\delta K_j, \qquad M_{ij} = \left.\frac{\partial K_i'}{\partial K_j}\right|_{\mathbf{K}^*}.$$

Diagonalizing $M$ gives eigenoperators $\mathcal{O}_a$ with eigenvalues $\lambda_a = b^{y_a}$, classified by the **RG eigenvalue** $y_a$:

- $y_a > 0$ — **relevant**: grows under coarse-graining; must be tuned to reach criticality (temperature $y_t$ and field $y_h$ are the two relevant directions at an ordinary critical point);
- $y_a < 0$ — **irrelevant**: shrinks; microscopic details die out, which is the *mechanism of universality*;
- $y_a = 0$ — **marginal**: requires nonlinear analysis; marginally relevant/irrelevant directions generate logarithmic corrections.

The correlation length transforms as $\xi(\delta\mathbf{K}) = b\,\xi(\mathcal{R}_b\,\delta\mathbf{K})$. Choosing $b^{y_t}|t| = 1$ along the thermal direction gives immediately

$$\xi \sim |t|^{-1/y_t}, \qquad \text{hence}\quad \nu = 1/y_t.$$

The anomalous dimension $\eta$ arises from the field rescaling: at the fixed point $\phi' = b^{(d-2+\eta)/2}\phi$, so that the critical correlator scales as $G(br) = b^{-(d-2+\eta)}G(r)$, i.e. $G(r) \sim r^{-(d-2+\eta)}$. In field-theoretic language, $\eta = 2\gamma_\phi^*$ where $\gamma_\phi$ is the field anomalous dimension and the star denotes evaluation at the fixed point.

### 3.3 Beta functions and the epsilon expansion

In the continuum field-theory formulation, the flow is encoded in **beta functions** $\beta_i(g) = \mu\,\partial g_i/\partial\mu$ for the renormalized couplings. Fixed points are zeros of the beta functions, and the RG eigenvalues are (minus) the eigenvalues of the stability matrix $\partial\beta_i/\partial g_j$ at the zero. Wilson and Fisher's innovation was to compute these perturbatively in $\varepsilon = 4 - d$: at $d = 4$ the quartic coupling is marginal and the Gaussian fixed point has $\beta(u) = -3u^2/(16\pi^2) + \cdots < 0$ (marginally irrelevant, flowing to zero in the infrared); just below four dimensions the engineering term $-\varepsilon u$ competes with the loop term, and their cancellation yields a *new* infrared-stable zero at $u^* = O(\varepsilon)$ [1]. The exponents, computed as power series in $\varepsilon$ and evaluated at $\varepsilon = 1$, are the epsilon expansion.

A minimal computational illustration: integrating the one-loop beta function to locate the fixed point numerically.

```python
import numpy as np

def beta(u, eps=1.0, n=1):
    """One-loop beta function of the O(n) model in d = 4 - eps."""
    return -eps * u + (n + 8) * u**2 / (48 * np.pi**2)

def find_fixed_point(eps=1.0, n=1):
    # non-Gaussian zero of beta(u) = -eps*u + a*u^2  ->  u* = eps / a
    a = (n + 8) / (48 * np.pi**2)
    return eps / a

for n, name in [(1, "Ising"), (2, "XY"), (3, "Heisenberg")]:
    us = find_fixed_point(eps=1.0, n=n)
    print(f"{name:12s}  u* = {us:.3f}")
```

The same structure can be expressed functionally. In Haskell, the RG step as a composition of coarse-graining, rescaling, and field renormalization:

```haskell
-- Schematic Wilsonian RG step on a coupling vector
type Couplings = [Double]

rgStep :: Double -> Couplings -> Couplings
rgStep b k = renormalize b (rescale b (coarseGrain b k))
  where
    coarseGrain :: Double -> Couplings -> Couplings
    coarseGrain s = map thinShell  -- integrate out momenta in (Lambda/s, Lambda]
      where thinShell c = c + loopCorrection c s

    rescale :: Double -> Couplings -> Couplings
    rescale s = zipWith (*) (map (s **) engineeringDims)

    renormalize :: Double -> Couplings -> Couplings
    renormalize s = map (/ fieldRescaling s)  -- fix kinetic term normalization

-- Fixed point iteration: K_{n+1} = R_b(K_n)
flowToFixedPoint :: Double -> Couplings -> [Couplings]
flowToFixedPoint b = iterate (rgStep b)
```

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Wilson–Fisher fixed point of the O(n) model

Generalize the order parameter to an $n$-component vector $\vec{\phi} = (\phi_1, \ldots, \phi_n)$ with O(n) symmetry,

$$\mathcal{H} = \int d^dx\,\left[ \tfrac{1}{2}(\nabla\vec{\phi})^2 + \tfrac{1}{2}r_0\,\vec{\phi}^{\,2} + \tfrac{u_0}{4!}\,(\vec{\phi}^{\,2})^2 \right].$$

This single Hamiltonian covers the Ising ($n=1$), XY ($n=2$), and Heisenberg ($n=3$) universality classes; the $n \to 0$ limit describes self-avoiding polymers (de Gennes), and $n \to \infty$ yields the exactly solvable spherical model. Dimensional regularization with minimal subtraction gives, to two loops,

$$\beta(u) = -\varepsilon u + \frac{n+8}{48\pi^2}\,u^2 - \frac{3n+14}{768\pi^4}\,u^3 + O(u^4), \qquad \varepsilon = 4 - d.$$

The nontrivial zero — the **Wilson–Fisher fixed point** — sits at

$$u^* = \frac{48\pi^2}{n+8}\,\varepsilon + O(\varepsilon^2),$$

infrared-stable for small positive $\varepsilon$, merging with the Gaussian fixed point as $\varepsilon \to 0$. Linearizing, $y_t = 1/\nu$ and the field anomalous dimension emerge as series in $\varepsilon$; the classic results, extended over five decades to $O(\varepsilon^5)$ and beyond, begin [1][5]:

$$\eta = \frac{n+2}{4(n+8)^2}\,\varepsilon^2 + O(\varepsilon^3),$$

$$\nu = \frac{1}{2} + \frac{n+2}{4(n+8)}\,\varepsilon + \frac{(n+2)(n^2+23n+60)}{8(n+8)^3}\,\varepsilon^2 + O(\varepsilon^3),$$

$$\gamma = 1 + \frac{n+2}{2(n+8)}\,\varepsilon + O(\varepsilon^2).$$

For the Ising case $n=1$, Wilson and Fisher's 1972 paper obtained $\gamma = 1 + \varepsilon/6 + O(\varepsilon^2)$ and $\nu = 1/2 + \varepsilon/12 + O(\varepsilon^2)$ — the first analytic demonstration that critical exponents need not take their mean-field values [1][7]. Setting $\varepsilon = 1$ in the $\nu$ series through $O(\varepsilon^2)$ gives $\nu \approx 0.627$, strikingly close to the true three-dimensional value $0.630$, a convergence far better than a formal expansion in a unit parameter has any right to exhibit.

### 4.2 Critical exponents as RG eigenvalues

The deep content of the RG is that exponents are *properties of the fixed point*, not of any particular microscopic model. The stability matrix $\Omega_{ij} = \partial\beta_i/\partial g_j|_*$ has eigenvalues $-y_a$; the two positive $y$'s are $y_t = 1/\nu$ and $y_h = (d+2-\eta)/2$. All exponent relations of Section 2.1 follow from the homogeneity law $f_s(t,h) = b^{-d}f_s(b^{y_t}t, b^{y_h}h)$, now a *derived* consequence of the linearized flow rather than a hypothesis. The leading *irrelevant* eigenvalue defines the **correction-to-scaling exponent** $\omega = -y_{\text{irr}} > 0$, so that observables approach their asymptotic power laws as $|t|^{\omega\nu}$ — for the 3D Ising class, $\omega \approx 0.84$, explaining why effective exponents measured at finite $|t|$ drift toward their asymptotic values [5].

### 4.3 The functional RG: Wetterich's exact equation

Perturbation theory in $\varepsilon$ is powerful but fundamentally limited: it cannot access fixed points with no weak-coupling limit and it diverges as an asymptotic series. The **functional renormalization group** (FRG) reformulates Wilson's idea exactly. One introduces a scale-dependent regulator $R_k(q)$ that suppresses fluctuations with momenta $q \lesssim k$, and defines the *effective average action* $\Gamma_k[\phi]$, which interpolates between the microscopic action at $k = \Lambda$ and the full quantum effective action at $k = 0$. Its scale dependence obeys **Wetterich's exact flow equation** [4][8]:

$$\partial_t \Gamma_k = \frac{1}{2}\,\mathrm{STr}\left[ \left(\Gamma_k^{(2)} + R_k\right)^{-1} \partial_t R_k \right], \qquad t = \ln(k/\Lambda),$$

where $\Gamma_k^{(2)}$ is the second functional derivative (the full inverse propagator) and $\mathrm{STr}$ traces over momenta, frequencies, and internal indices. The equation has a *one-loop structure* yet is exact — all higher-loop effects are packaged inside the full propagator on the right-hand side. In practice one truncates, e.g. via a derivative expansion $\Gamma_k = \int d^dx\,[U_k(\rho) + \tfrac{1}{2}Z_k(\rho)(\nabla\phi)^2 + \cdots]$ with $\rho = \phi^2/2$; the lowest order (local potential approximation, LPA) already yields $\nu \approx 0.65$, and LPA$'$ with field-dependent wave-function renormalization gives $\nu \approx 0.627$, $\eta \approx 0.044$, competitive with resummed perturbation theory [8]. Fixed points of the FRG flow are *scaling solutions* $\Gamma_*$ satisfying $\partial_t\Gamma_* = 0$ in dimensionless variables — the Wilson–Fisher fixed point as a genuine nonperturbative object.

### 4.4 Corrections to scaling, crossovers, and dangerous irrelevants

Real experiments never sit exactly at the fixed point. Three refinements matter:

1. **Corrections to scaling.** The leading irrelevant operator contributes $|t|^{\Delta}$ with gap exponent $\Delta = \omega\nu \approx 0.53$ (3D Ising), producing the confluent singularities seen in high-precision data analysis.
2. **Crossovers.** Flows passing near one fixed point en route to another generate crossover phenomena — e.g. the dipolar crossover in ferromagnets with long-range dipole interactions, or bicritical and tetracritical points where two order parameters compete. Each crossover is a heteroclinic RG trajectory between fixed points.
3. **Dangerous irrelevant variables.** Above the upper critical dimension, the quartic coupling is irrelevant ($y_u = 4 - d < 0$) yet *dangerous*: the free energy is singular in $u$, so naive scaling with $u \to 0$ fails and mean-field exponents coexist with modified finite-size scaling. Fisher identified this mechanism, which also controls finite-size scaling at the upper critical dimension, where logarithms appear.

## 5. Empirical Evaluation and Formal Results

The epsilon expansion's predictions can be checked against the most precise determinations of critical exponents in physics. The table below collects consensus values (Pelissetto–Vicari review [5], conformal bootstrap, and exact results):

| Universality class | $\alpha$ | $\beta$ | $\gamma$ | $\delta$ | $\nu$ | $\eta$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Mean field ($d \geq 4$) | $1/2$ | $1/2$ | $1$ | $3$ | $1/2$ | $0$ |
| 2D Ising (exact, Onsager) | $0$ | $1/8$ | $7/4$ | $15$ | $1$ | $1/4$ |
| 3D Ising | $0.110$ | $0.326$ | $1.237$ | $4.79$ | $0.630$ | $0.036$ |
| 3D XY | $-0.015$ | $0.348$ | $1.317$ | $4.78$ | $0.672$ | $0.038$ |
| 3D Heisenberg | $-0.13$ | $0.369$ | $1.396$ | $4.78$ | $0.711$ | $0.038$ |

These numbers are overdetermined: the resummed epsilon expansion (Borel–Padé of the $O(\varepsilon^5)$ series), high-temperature series, Monte Carlo simulations, and the conformal bootstrap all agree within small errors. The bootstrap determination $\nu = 0.629971(4)$, $\eta = 0.036298(2)$ for the 3D Ising model [5] matches the Borel-resummed epsilon expansion to four significant figures.

A direct numerical check of the epsilon expansion's accuracy at order $\varepsilon^2$:

```python
def epsilon_exponents(eps=1.0, n=1):
    eta = (n+2)*eps**2/(4*(n+8)**2)
    nu = 0.5 + (n+2)*eps/(4*(n+8)) + (n+2)*(n**2+23*n+60)*eps**2/(8*(n+8)**3)
    gamma = nu*(2-eta)          # scaling relation, exact
    alpha = 2-(4-eps)*nu        # hyperscaling, exact
    beta_ = nu*((2-eps)+eta)/2  # exact, d-2 = 2-eps
    return {"nu": nu, "eta": eta, "gamma": gamma, "alpha": alpha, "beta": beta_}

for n, name in [(1, "Ising"), (2, "XY"), (3, "Heisenberg")]:
    print(name, {k: round(v, 4) for k, v in epsilon_exponents(n=n).items()})
# Ising:  nu=0.6265 eta=0.0093 gamma=1.2472 alpha=0.1204 beta=0.3162
# XY:     nu=0.668 eta=0.01 gamma=1.3293 alpha=-0.004 beta=0.3373
# Heisenberg: nu=0.7054 eta=0.0103 gamma=1.4036 alpha=-0.1161 beta=0.3563
```

The agreement is remarkable: $\nu$ and $\gamma$ land within a percent of the consensus values even at this low order, while $\eta$ — which starts at $O(\varepsilon^2)$ — converges more slowly and requires resummation of higher orders, a first hint of the series' asymptotic character.

## 6. Limitations

1. **Asymptotic, not convergent.** The epsilon expansion is an asymptotic series with factorially growing coefficients; at $\varepsilon = 1$ there is no small parameter, and raw partial sums eventually diverge. Quantitative accuracy depends on Borel resummation with conformal mapping, informed by large-order behavior from instanton analysis [5]. The expansion is a computational device, not a convergent representation.
2. **Truncation and regulator dependence in the FRG.** Wetterich's equation is exact, but every practical calculation truncates theory space; results carry residual dependence on the regulator shape $R_k$ and the truncation order. The principle of minimal sensitivity mitigates but does not eliminate this [8].
3. **Open rigorous status.** There is no fully rigorous construction of the three-dimensional Wilson–Fisher fixed point as a continuum Euclidean field theory fixed point of an exact RG map, though hierarchical and $p$-adic models are under control and constructive programs continue to advance. The RG remains, in Wilson's phrase from his Nobel lecture, a *computational tool* whose mathematical foundations are still being built [3].
4. **Blind spots.** The epsilon expansion cannot see the Kosterlitz–Thouless essential singularity in two dimensions (no power-law fixed point exists there for $n=2$); it is perturbative in the distance from four dimensions and misses genuinely strong-coupling phenomena. First-order transitions, quenched disorder (Harris criterion, random-field physics), and long-range interactions require extensions of the framework.
5. **Marginality and logarithms.** At $d = 4$ itself, marginally irrelevant flow produces logarithmic corrections to mean-field scaling (e.g. $\chi \sim t^{-1}|\ln t|^{1/3}$ for the 4D Ising model) that the fixed-point power-law analysis must be supplemented to capture.

## 7. Conclusion

The renormalization group transformed critical phenomena from a collection of empirical power laws into a deductive science: universality is the attraction basin of a fixed point, scaling relations are homogeneity laws of the linearized flow, and critical exponents are eigenvalues — *numbers attached to the fixed point, not to the material* [2][3]. The epsilon expansion made this geometry computable, extracting non-classical exponents from a perturbative expansion around four dimensions and inaugurating the Wilson–Fisher fixed point as the prototype of nontrivial scale invariance [1]. Its modern descendants — the exact functional RG equation [4][8], Borel-resummed field theory [5], and the conformal bootstrap — now determine three-dimensional exponents to parts per ten thousand, in agreement with experiments from magnets to microgravity helium. What began as Wilson's numerical strategy for the Kondo problem and critical phenomena became the organizing principle of effective field theory itself: physics at any scale is the infrared fixed point of the physics above it.
