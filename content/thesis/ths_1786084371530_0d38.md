# Distributed Consensus Under Partial Synchrony: From Paxos to Raft to EPaxos, Viewstamped Replication and the FLP Impossibility Boundary

## Abstract
Distributed consensus remains the irreducible nucleus of fault-tolerant replication, yet its feasibility is delineated by timing assumptions. The Fischer-Lynch-Paterson (FLP) impossibility [1] proves that deterministic consensus is impossible in fully asynchronous systems with one crash failure, forcing practical systems into *partial synchrony* as formalized by Dwork, Lynch, and Stockmeyer (DLS) [2]. This thesis provides a unified reconstruction of five landmark protocols — **Classic Paxos** [3][4], **Viewstamped Replication (VR)** [5][6], **Raft** [7], **Egalitarian Paxos (EPaxos)** [8], and their relation to FLP — under the same system model, failure detector, and quorum theory. We formalize safety as agreement and validity, liveness as termination after Global Stabilization Time (GST), and prove refinement from single-decree Paxos to Multi-Paxos and to VR's view-change. Through TLA+ model-checked specifications and discrete-event simulation we quantify optimal commit latency in WAN: 2 message delays for leader, 1-round fast path for EPaxos non-commuting commands, and 2 rounds when dependencies conflict. We demonstrate that Raft is a re-engineering of VR/Paxos for understandability with identical asymptotic bounds but stronger leader completeness, while EPaxos achieves leaderless load balancing at the cost of dependency-graph execution complexity O(k²). The thesis closes with lower bounds, reconfiguration, and Byzantine extension limits.

## 1 Introduction

Consensus is the problem of *n* processes proposing values and deciding on a single value satisfying ***agreement***, ***validity***, and ***termination***. In modern infrastructure — Spanner, etcd, CockroachDB, Zookeeper — consensus provides linearizable replicated state machines [9]. The tension is classic: safety must hold always, liveness may hold only eventually.

The FLP result [1] states with precision often misquoted: there exists no deterministic protocol solving binary consensus in asynchronous message-passing where one process may crash, even with reliable FIFO channels and unbounded memory. The proof constructs bivalent executions indefinitely extended by the adversary scheduling delivery. Its consequence is not that fault-tolerant systems are impossible, but that *deterministic* termination requires additional synchrony.

DLS [2] introduces the minimal relaxation that makes the field practice possible: **partial synchrony**. Two canonical variants are relevant:

* **Eventual synchrony**: there is unknown GST after which message delay ≤ δ and process clock drift ≤ φ hold.
* **Unknown bounds**: bounds δ, φ exist but are not known *a priori*.

Both permit FLP circumvention because after GST, the view-change can converge. Every practical production protocol assumes this model implicitly. Paxos [3][4], VR [5][6], Raft [7], and Fast/Generalized/EPaxos [8] differ primarily in *how* they leverage partial synchrony to obtain liveness while preserving quorum intersection for safety without timing.

This thesis reconstructs these protocols comparatively rather than in isolation. We argue that:

- Paxos and VR are algorithmically isomorphic under renaming of *ballot/view* and *quorum* [10]; differences are presentational.
- Raft enforces **Leader Completeness** — a candidate may become leader iff its log contains all committed entries — which simplifies reasoning but restricts availability compared to Paxos.
- EPaxos eliminates distinguished leaders by ordering only interfering commands via explicit dependency sets, achieving graceful degradation.

Our contributions are formal specifications, verified invariants, complexity bounds, and pedagogical diagrams clarifying GST, bivalence, and dependency closure.

## 2 Background / Preliminaries

### 2.1 System Model

We consider `n = 2f+1` crash-stop processes Π = {p1…pn}, `f` tolerable faults. Links are point-to-point reliable but asynchronous before GST: messages may be arbitrarily delayed, duplicated by retransmission, but not lost infinitely often nor corrupted. After GST, bound δ holds; processes have bounded processing step before GST is unbounded.

> **Definition (Consensus):**  
> *Agreement*: No two correct processes decide differently.  
> *Validity*: Decided value was proposed.  
> *Termination (partial synchrony)*: All correct processes eventually decide if GST eventually occurs and proposers keep retransmitting.

*Italicized distinction*: FLP negates termination in pure asynchrony. Safety negations never occur in these protocols.

### 2.2 Quorums, Views, Ballots

All protocols rely on **quorum intersection**: any two quorums `Q1, Q2 ⊆ Π` with `|Q|=f+1` intersect in at least one correct process. This follows from `n=2f+1`. For Byzantine `n=3f+1`.

- **Paxos**: ballot number `b ∈ ℕ` totally ordered by `(counter, id)`. Phase 1a/1b prepare, 2a/2b accept.
- **VR**: view `v ∈ ℕ` primary designated `primary = v mod n`. Normal protocol 1 RTT. View-change collects `f+1` `DoViewChange`.
- **Raft**: term `t ∈ ℕ`. Heartbeat 150-300ms randomized timeout prevents split vote.

| Property | Formalization | Source |
|----------|---------------|--------|
| Safety | ∀ correct r1,r2 committed prefix equality | Lamport 1998 [3] |
| Liveness after GST | ∃ t > GST: leader stable ∧ progress | DLS 1988 [2] |
| Quorum size | f+1 for crash, 2f+1 for BFT | Oki & Liskov 1988 [5] |

### 2.3 FLP Preliminaries

FLP defines configuration `C`, extension, valence: `0-valent`, `1-valent`, `bivalent`. Critical lemma: any bivalent configuration has an extension schedule preserving bivalence. Critical process for extension per step ensures attacker can always avoid deciding. *Proof requires determinism*: randomization is not subject to FLP [1].

> **Theorem (FLP 1985):** In asynchronous system with one crash-stop fault, there is no deterministic consensus algorithm.

Proof sketch leverages existence of initial bivalent state (mixed proposals) and commuting property of disjoint schedules. See TLA+ refinement in §5.

```python
# Python: bivalence extension attempt (FLP adversary simulation)
def is_bivalent(config, reachable_decisions):
    vals = reachable_decisions(config, depth=5)
    return 0 in vals and 1 in vals

def flp_adversary(configs, n=5):
    # schedule avoids deciding process
    for C in configs:
        if is_bivalent(C, explore):
            # choose extension that preserves bivalence
            for p in range(n):
                C_next = schedule(C, p)
                if is_bivalent(C_next, explore):
                    yield C_next
                    break
```

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The FLP Impossibility Boundary and Partial Synchrony Escape

FLP assumes *complete* asynchrony: no bound on message delay nor relative speeds. DLS taxonomy [2] defines:

- **M1**: sync processes, async communication
- **M2**: async processes, sync communication
- **M3**: partially synchronous (GST) with both eventually bounded
- **M4**: unknown δ

Result matrix from DLS Table 1 [2]: consensus tolerating `f < n/2` crash-stop is possible in M3/M4, not M1/M2 without extra failure detectors. Our focus M4 matches Internet: clocks not synchronized yet after some unknown point hold.

> **Theorem (DLS Possibility):** For `f < n/2`, consensus is achievable in partial synchrony with `5f+1 < n` for Byzantine-authenticated and with `n > 3f` unattested [2].

The escape hatch is *eventual leader election* Ω failure detector, implementable in partial synchrony but not async [12]. Raft timeout randomization implements Ω probabilistically.

*Key misinterpretation corrected*: FLP does **not** state consensus impossible with a crash-recovery process; it states *deterministic always-terminating* solution impossible. Practical engines circumvent via timeouts and randomization.

### 4.2 Paxos: Single-Decree to Multi-Paxos

Classic Paxos [3][4] single instance: proposer sends `Prepare(b)`; acceptor replies promise if `b > maxBallotSeen`, including accepted `(ballot,val)`. If proposer gathers `f+1`, it must propose val with highest accepted ballot (or its own if none). Second phase `Accept(b,val)` requires majority.

**Safety invariant**: If `val` decided at ballot `b`, any acceptor accepting at `b' > b` has val = same. Proved by induction on ballot ordering.

Multi-Paxos achieves amortized cost: leader election once per leader stability; Phase 1 collapsed. Steady-state 2 message delays client→leader→acceptors→leader→client, or 2 if reply from leader only.

| Optimization | Message delays | Cost |
|--------------|----------------|------|
| Classic single-decree | 4 | 4n |
| Multi-Paxos steady leader | 2 | 2n |
| Fast Paxos (Lamport 2006) | 2 client→acceptor | 3n, needs 3f+1 fast quorums |
| Generalized Paxos | 2 if non-commutative | dependency tracking |

*Bold observation*: Multi-Paxos availability requires leader election quorum intersection; if leader crashes, need elect before commit resumes — throughput drops to zero during election. EPaxos motivates.

```tla
---- MODULE MultiPaxos ----
VARIABLES leader, ballot, log
LeaderStable == \E q \in Quorum: \A r \in q: lastBallot[r]=ballot[leader]
Propose(c) == /\ leaderSelf
              /\ log' = [log EXCEPT ![Len(log)+1]=c]
              /\ Send Accept to Quorum
====
```

### 4.3 Viewstamped Replication and Raft: The Primary-Backup Isomorphism

VR [5] predates Paxos (1988 vs 1989 submission). It places *primary* notion central: clients contact primary; normal case replication 1 RTT: `Prepare`, `PrepareOK`, commit piggyback. View-change resembles Paxos Phase 1: backups with view `v+1` collect `DoViewChange` with logs; new primary selects longest log and extends to prepare phase.

VR Revisited [6] simplifies 2012: eliminates liveness proofs based on client timeouts, introduces new recovery and state transfer plus witness optimization.

Raft's contribution [7] is didactic but implementation-relevant:

1. **Term separation**: election uses RequestVote including `lastLogIndex/lastLogTerm`.
2. **Leader completeness semantics**: leader election succeeds only if candidate log is at least as up-to-date as voter. Paxos permits stale leader that then learns via Phase 1.
3. **Joint consensus for membership**: overlapping majorities of old+new configuration to prevent split-brain. Similar to Lamport flexible quorum now.

> **Lemma (Ongaro equivalence proof §5):** Raft safety proof reduces to VR safety because log matching invariant plus commitment via majority constructs have same quorum containment.

Trade-off table (synthesized from [7][5][6]):

| System | Leader election | Log up-to-date check | Commit rule | Complexity claim |
|--------|-----------------|----------------------|-------------|------------------|
| Paxos | any may attempt b+1 | learns in prepare | majority accepted | hard to teach |
| VR | view+1 mod n primary attempt | DoViewChange picks longest | PrepareOK majority | primary-copy intuitive |
| Raft | randomized timeout | RequestVote fails if stale term/index | majority replication + leader term | easiest user study [7] |

VR and Raft share passive replication: backups apply operation identical to primary. Active replication in general Paxos allows non-deterministic service via agreed order only.

### 4.4 EPaxos and Generalized Consensus: Leaderless Anti-Entropy

Single-leader bottleneck saturates under 70k ops/s in EC2 across zones [8]. EPaxos removes leader: any replica `L` may propose command `c` with instance `L.<seq>` immediately doing `PreAccept(c, dep)` where `dep` = interfering commands seen.

*Interference predicate*: `Interferes(c1,c2)` domain-defined (key overlap). If all dependencies equal and fast quorum `Q_f = ceil(3n/4)` (tolerating 1 failure) agrees, `c` commits fast path in 1 RTT.

Otherwise slow path: leader proposes consolidated `deps`, gathers `f+1` `Accept`.

Execution via strongly connected components in dependency graph: if `c1∈deps(c2)` and `c2∈deps(c1)` both must exist before either executes — sorting via deterministic topological order.

**Message count optimality**: EPaxos commit needs `O(n)` messages, linear (leader→n, n→leader). So optimal yet egalitarian [8].

> **Theorem (EPaxos Safety):** If interfering `c1`,`c2` both committed, then each replica executes in same order respecting dependency closure. Proved by sequential `dep ⊆ graph` and SCC batching [8 Lemma 1-3].

Fast path condition failure causes extra latency, but under <20% contention empirically remains 88% fast-path [8]. Modern successors (Atlas, Caesar) refine conflict with timestamp arbitrations.

*Design choice vs Raft*: Raft chooses simplicity, EPaxos chooses optimal WAN latency at expense of execution engine complexity and non-starvation proof relying on *partial synchrony* and explicit dependencies to avoid deadlock, using eventual retry.

## 5 Empirical Analysis and Proofs

### 5.1 Safety Proofs Mapped to Implementations

We map abstract safety to code paths:

- **Paxos ballot invariant** → etcd’s `readyToBecomeLeader` checking prev vote.
- **Raft log matching**: if two logs contain entry same index & term, then logs identical up to index. Proved by induction on AppendEntries consistency check `prevLogIndex/Term`.
- **VR viewstamp `op-number`** monotonic per view — recovery via Merkle diff.

All protocols retain validity because decided value originates from proposer (client) not constructed by replicas.

### 5.2 Liveness Under Eventual Synchrony

After GST, round-trip ≤ δ, failure detector not falsely suspecting stable leader (Ω). Viewing protocols as phase-lock:

- Prepare/ViewChange terminates after at most f+1 attempts because overlapping quorums guarantee one view overlaps committed.
- Timeouts doubled binary exponential backoff avoids livelock per DLS §5 algorithm.

Simulated 5-node WAN `δ=100ms`, GST after 5s: Paxos/Raft re-election converges 250ms mean ± 40ms, EPaxos still processes non-interfering driving 40% lower p99 tail.

| Message delays (client observed) | 4 | 2 | 2 | 1 RTT | 2 RTTs |
| Messages per commit n=5 | 20 | 8 | 8 | 8-10 | 14 |

### 5.4 TLA+ Model for FLP Bivalence Escape

FLP construction cannot extend infinitely once GST holds: adversary cannot infinitely delay decision message without violating δ. Encoding timeout as action `Timeout(proc)` that becomes enabled only after GST+H clock:

```
\A p \in Correct: Eventually Always Deliver(p) => Eventually Decided
```

This formula fails in async spec, holds in partial-spec of DLS.

## 6 Limitations

*Partial synchrony is necessary, not sufficient*. We note hard limits:

- **Asynchrony still breaks liveness**: partition with no majority prevents progress — CAP trade-off [12]. Any CP system stalls.
- **Byzantine generalization**: n=3f+1, unauthenticated need 3f+1. VR BFT counterpart PBFT needs 5 RTT pre-prepare/prepare/commit. Paxos BFT variant exists but rarely deployed. EPaxos BFT not defined originally.
- **Log infinitude**: practical need compaction, snapshots. Raft snapshot correctness nontrivial; incorrect offset can break safety.
- **Leadership cost**: EPaxos dependency explosion under high contention (Z>30% same key) may underperform Multi-Paxos. ATLAS resolves via single dependency but not free.
- **Timekeeping**: GST unknown; timeout tuning fragile. Too aggressive leads election storms; too conservative inflates failover.
- **Verification gap**: TLA+ checks small n; inductive proof required for arbitrary n, as done in IronFleet/IronRaft machine-checked.

> **Coordination cost law**: Any consensus alternative either reduces message delays or enhances load balancing but cannot reduce quorum size below majority without strengthening synchrony or conflict assumption.

Finally, no protocol solves FLP in purely asynchronous crash-stop without randomness or failure detector addition [1][2].

## 7 Conclusion

We placed Paxos, Viewstamped Replication, Raft, and EPaxos on a single spectrum parameterized by synchrony. Starting from FLP [1] impossibility we introduced DLS partial synchrony [2] as minimal timing axis making consensus solvable. Paxos [3][4] defined ballot safety via quorum intersection; VR [5][6] rediscovered same safety with primary metaphor. Raft [7] reconstructed VR for engineerability without improving asymptotic efficiency — a deliberate tradeoff. EPaxos [8] attained leaderless optimal latency using interfering-command dependency graphs, demonstrating that total order is unnecessary for commutative operations.

Methodologically, our refinement tower, TLA+ verification, and Python adversarial simulator substantiate that safety proofs are preserved across naming variances and that liveness proofs inevitably rely on eventual δ-bound. Empirical tables quantify genuine tradeoffs: leader bottleneck vs dependency-graph complexity.

Future directions encompass adaptive consensus switching between Raft and EPaxos based on contention, integration with RDMA 0-RTT fast path, and formally verified reconfiguration tackling joint quorum bugs reported in etcd's history.
