# Model-Theoretic Forcing: Cohen Reals, the Continuum Hypothesis, and Cardinal Invariants

## Abstract

Paul Cohen's 1963 invention of forcing showed that the consistency of ZFC implies the consistency of ZFC together with the negation of the continuum hypothesis [1][2], completing — alongside Gödel's 1940 constructible universe — the proof that CH is independent of ZFC. This article gives a model-theoretic exposition of the method: from a countable transitive model *M* of ZFC, the Cohen poset Add(ω, κ) of finite partial functions adjoins κ mutually generic reals; the generic extension *M[G]* preserves every cardinal because the forcing satisfies the countable chain condition, yet contains at least κ distinct new reals, so ¬CH holds whenever κ ≥ ω₂. We develop the forcing relation, *P*-names, and the Truth and Definability lemmas; survey preservation and iteration theorems; and trace the method's second life in the theory of cardinal invariants of the continuum — the bounding number **b**, the dominating number **d**, and the ten characteristics of Cichoń's diagram — where distinct forcing notions (Cohen, random, Hechler, Laver, Mathias) act as precision instruments tuning individual invariants, culminating in the consistency of Cichoń's maximum [7][9].

##1 Introduction

In 1878 Georg Cantor conjectured that every infinite set of real numbers is either countable or has the cardinality of the continuum — the *continuum hypothesis* (CH), equivalently 2^ℵ₀ = ℵ₁ under the axiom of choice [5]. Hilbert placed it first on his 1900 list. For sixty years the problem resisted every attack, until Kurt Gödel proved in 1940 that CH cannot be *refuted* from ZFC: his constructible universe *L*, an inner model of any universe of sets, satisfies ZFC + CH, so Con(ZFC) implies Con(ZFC + CH) [3]. The converse direction — a model of ZFC + ¬CH — demanded an entirely new technique for *enlarging* models of set theory rather than shrinking them.

Paul Cohen supplied it in 1963–64 with the method of **forcing**, for which he received the Fields Medal in 1966 [5]. Where Gödel carved an inner model out of the universe, Cohen adjoined new sets to a model from the outside: starting with a (countable, transitive) model *M* of ZFC, one selects a partially ordered set *P* of "approximations" in *M* and a *generic* filter *G ⊆ P*, and forms the extension *M[G]* — the smallest transitive model of ZFC containing both *M* and *G* [3][6]. Truth in *M[G]* is controlled from within *M* by the **forcing relation** *p ⊩ φ*, so that *M* can "decide in advance" which statements the extension will satisfy.

This article is a self-contained, model-theoretic account of that construction in its purest form — Cohen's original application — followed by the two great research programmes it spawned:

1. **Independence via cardinal arithmetic.** Adding ω₂ Cohen reals to a model of CH yields a model of ¬CH while preserving all cardinals, because Cohen forcing has the *countable chain condition* (ccc).
2. **The fine structure of the continuum.** Once ¬CH is consistent, the question becomes *how* the continuum can behave. The cardinal *invariants* **b** and **d** and the ten characteristics of **Cichoń's diagram** measure the combinatorial anatomy of the real line, and forcing iterations are the instruments that realize their consistent configurations — up to the landmark result of Cichoń's maximum [7][9].

> **Theorem (Cohen, 1963–64):** If ZFC is consistent, then so are ZFC + CH and ZFC + ¬CH. Hence CH is independent of ZFC [1][2][3].

##2 Background

### Models of set theory

We work in ZFC. A *model* is a pair *(M, ∈)* satisfying the axioms; we restrict to **transitive** models, where *x ∈ y ∈ M* implies *x ∈ M*, so that ∈ means genuine membership and the ordinals of *M* are initial segments of the true ordinals. By the reflection theorem, the Löwenheim–Skolem theorem, and the Mostowski collapse, the consistency of ZFC yields, for each finite fragment ZFC*, a **countable transitive model** *(M, ∈)* of ZFC* [3]. Countability is no restriction for consistency proofs: it merely lets us enumerate, from outside *M*, all the dense sets we shall need to meet.

### Cardinals and the continuum

Cardinals are initial ordinals; ℵ₀ = |ω|, and ℵ₁ is the least uncountable cardinal. The *continuum* is **c** = 2^ℵ₀ = |P(ω)| = |ℝ|. Cantor's theorem gives 2^ℵ₀ > ℵ₀; CH asserts 2^ℵ₀ = ℵ₁, i.e., no cardinal lies strictly between |ω| and |ℝ| [5]. Easton's theorem later showed that, apart from monotonicity and König's cofinality constraint, the behavior of the continuum function on regular cardinals is essentially arbitrary — but the *first* value, 2^ℵ₀, is the province of forcing.

### Posets, filters, and genericity

A **forcing notion** is a poset *(P, ≤)* with a maximum element 1, where *p ≤ q* reads "*p* is stronger than *q*" (more informative). A **filter** *F ⊆ P* is upward-closed and pairwise compatible: any two of its conditions have a common strengthening. A set *D ⊆ P* is **dense** if every condition has a strengthening in *D*, and **open** if it is upward-closed [6].

> **Definition:** Let *M* be transitive and *P ∈ M*. A filter *G ⊆ P* is ***M*-generic** if it meets every dense open *D ⊆ P* with *D ∈ M*.

Because *M* is countable, only countably many such dense sets exist, and a diagonal construction (the Rasiowa–Sikorski lemma) produces an *M*-generic filter from outside *M*. Crucially, **no** *M*-generic filter can belong to *M* itself: the sets *Dₓ = {p : p ⊩ x̌ ≠ Ġ}*–style diagonal dense sets defeat every candidate inside the model. Genericity is thus an essentially *external* property — the new real is genuinely new.

##3 Methodology

Our methodology is the classical countable-transitive-model presentation of forcing [3][6], organized as a pipeline:

1. **Names.** For a poset *P*, the class of ***P*-names** is defined by recursion: τ is a *P*-name if τ is a set of pairs *(σ, p)* with σ a *P*-name and *p ∈ P*. Every *x ∈ M* has a *canonical name* *x̌ = {(y̌, 1) : y ∈ x}*, and *Ġ = {(p̌, p) : p ∈ P}* names the generic filter itself.
2. **Evaluation.** For a filter *G*, *τ_G = {σ_G : ∃p ∈ G, (σ, p) ∈ τ}*. The extension is *M[G] = {τ_G : τ ∈ M* a *P*-name*}.
3. **The forcing relation.** *p ⊩ φ(τ₁, …, τₙ)* ("*p* forces φ") is defined by recursion on formulas, entirely inside *M*. For atomic formulas it is a combinatorial property of *P*; the logical steps follow the natural clauses, e.g., *p ⊩ ¬φ* iff no *q ≤ p* forces φ.
4. **The Forcing Theorems.** (i) **Truth Lemma:** *M[G] ⊨ φ(τ₁_G, …)* iff *∃p ∈ G, p ⊩ φ(τ₁, …)*. (ii) **Definability Lemma:** *{(p, τ₁, …) : p ⊩ φ}* is definable in *M*. Together they imply *M[G] ⊨ ZFC* whenever *M ⊨ ZFC* and *G* is *M*-generic.
5. **Design the poset.** The art lies in choosing *P* so that *M[G]* has the desired theory: we arrange dense sets in *M* forcing each requirement, then let genericity meet them all at once.

```haskell
-- A toy rendering of the forcing pipeline (names as trees, evaluation by a filter)
data PName = Name [(PName, Cond)]      -- pairs (subname, condition)
type Cond  = [Int]                     -- finite binary approximations
eval :: [Cond] -> PName -> [Int]       -- filter G as compatible chain
eval g (Name pairs) = concatMap pick pairs
  where pick (s, p) | any (extends p) g = [head (eval g s)]
                    | otherwise         = []
extends p q = take (length p) q == p   -- q strengthens p
```

##4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Cohen Poset and the Anatomy of a Cohen Real

**Cohen forcing** for adding one real is the poset

> **Add(ω, 1) = 2^{<ω}**, finite binary sequences ordered by extension,

and for adding κ reals, **Add(ω, κ) = Fn(κ, 2)**: finite partial functions from κ to 2, ordered by reverse inclusion. Let *G* be *M*-generic and put *c = ⋃G : ω → 2*, the **Cohen real** — an infinite binary sequence whose finite initial segments are exactly the conditions of *G*.

Why is *c* new? For each *x ∈ (2^ω)^M*, the set *D_x = {p : p ⊄ x}* (conditions incompatible with *x*) is dense open in *M*: any *p* can be extended at a fresh coordinate to disagree with *x*. Genericity meets *D_x*, so *c ≠ x* for every ground-model real [3]. The same argument, applied coordinatewise, shows that Add(ω, κ) adds κ *distinct* Cohen reals *c_α*, any finitely many of which are mutually generic. ### 4.2 The Countable Chain Condition and Preservation of Cardinals

Adding reals is easy; the miracle is **not collapsing cardinals**. An antichain is a set of pairwise incompatible conditions; *P* has the **countable chain condition (ccc)** if every antichain is countable.

> **Theorem (Δ-system lemma):** Every uncountable family of finite sets contains an uncountable subfamily forming a Δ-system — pairwise intersections constant.

Since conditions in Add(ω, κ) have finite domains, the Δ-system lemma implies any uncountable antichain thins to one whose domains form a Δ-system with root *r*; two conditions agreeing on *r*... a standard pigeonhole argument on the finitely many possibilities for *p ↾ r* yields two compatible conditions — contradiction. Hence **Add(ω, κ) is ccc** for every κ.

The ccc is the workhorse of preservation: if *P* is ccc and *f : α → β* is a function in *M[G]* with α, β ordinals in *M*, then *f* is covered by a function in *M* of controlled size, so cofinalities — and hence cardinals — of *M* are preserved. Moreover, every new subset of ω has a **nice name** mentioning only countably many conditions, so with *M ⊨ GCH*:

| Quantity | Value in *M[G]*, *P* = Add(ω, ω₂) |
|---|---|
| Cardinals ℵ₁, ℵ₂ | Preserved (ccc) |
| Number of nice names for reals | ≤ (ω₂^{ℵ₀})^M = ω₂ |
| Continuum 2^ℵ₀ | **Exactly ℵ₂** (≥ ω₂ reals added, ≤ ω₂ by counting) |
| CH | **False** |

Thus *M[G] ⊨ ZFC + 2^ℵ₀ = ℵ₂*, i.e., ¬CH [1][2][3].

### 4.3 The Forcing Relation: Names, Truth, and Definability

The engine room deserves a closer look. The relation *p ⊩* φ* is defined by recursion; the atomic cases carry the content:

- *p ⊩ σ ∈ τ* iff the set *{q ≤ p : ∃(ρ, r) ∈ τ, q ≤ r ∧ q ⊩ σ = ρ}* is dense below *p*;
- *p ⊩ σ = τ* iff *p ⊩ σ ⊆ τ* and *p ⊩ τ ⊆ σ*, with *p ⊩ σ ⊆ τ* iff *{q ≤ p : ∀(ρ,r) ∈ σ (q ≤ r → q ⊩ ρ ∈ τ)}* is dense below *p*.

> **Theorem (Truth Lemma):** *M[G] ⊨ φ(τ⃗_G)* ⇔ *∃p ∈ G : p ⊩ φ(τ⃗)*.
> **Theorem (Definability Lemma):** The relation *p ⊩ φ* is definable in *M*.

Consequently *M* controls *M[G]* completely: to verify *M[G] ⊨ ¬CH* it suffices to check, inside *M*, that every condition forces the existence of ω₂ distinct reals — which the density arguments of §4.1 provide. ### 4.4 Cardinal Invariants: Bounding, Dominating, and Cichoń's Diagram

Once ¬CH is consistent, the continuum's *fine structure* becomes the object of study. Work in the Baire space ω^ω with **eventual domination**: *f ≤* g* if *f(n) ≤ g(n)* for all but finitely many *n*. Two cardinal invariants dominate the theory:

- **b** (bounding number): the least size of a family *F ⊆ ω^ω* **unbounded** — no single *g* eventually dominates every *f ∈ F*;
- **d** (dominating number): the least size of a **dominating** family — every *h ∈ ω^ω* is eventually dominated by some *f ∈ F*.

ZFC proves *ℵ₁ ≤ **b** ≤ **d** ≤ **c***, that **b** is regular, and that cf(**d**) > ω. Cohen forcing **adds unbounded reals** — the generic real escapes every ground-model bound — while Hechler forcing adds a *dominating* real, and Laver and Mathias forcing collapse **d** to ω₁ [11]. Random forcing, by contrast, is ω^ω-bounding: it adds no unbounded reals. Each classical forcing notion is thus a precision instrument acting on specific invariants.

The full anatomy is **Cichoń's diagram**: ten cardinal characteristics — add, cov, non, cof applied to the null ideal **N** and the meager ideal **M**, plus **b**, **d**, ℵ₁ and **c** — with ZFC-provable inequalities between them (Figure 3):

- add(**N**) ≤ add(**M**) and cof(**M**) ≤ cof(**N**) (Bartoszyński–Raisonnier–Stern);
- add(**M**) = min(**b**, cov(**M**)) and cof(**M**) = max(**d**, non(**M**)) [7][9].

*Figure 3 — `ths_1789489846869_15288-2.webp`: Cichoń's diagram of the ten invariants.*

The diagram is **complete**: no further inequalities are provable in ZFC. The deepest modern result is **Cichoń's maximum** — the consistency of all ten entries being pairwise distinct — proved by Goldstern, Kellner, and Shelah assuming four strongly compact cardinals [7], with the large-cardinal hypothesis subsequently eliminated [9]; Kellner, Shelah, and Tanasie also realized a second, distinct maximal constellation [8]. Forcing iterations with ultrafilter-linked and measure-theoretic control are the machinery behind these separations.

##5 Empirical Results and Proofs

We collect the concrete theorems; a finite computational analogue follows.

> **Theorem 1 (Cohen [1][2]):** Con(ZFC) ⇒ Con(ZFC + ¬CH).
> *Proof sketch.* Let *M* be countable transitive with *M ⊨ ZFC + CH*. Force with *P* = Add(ω, ω₂^M). By §4.1, *M[G]* contains ω₂^M distinct Cohen reals; by §4.2, *P* is ccc, so cardinals are preserved and a nice-name count gives 2^ℵ₀ ≤ ℵ₂ in *M[G]*. Hence *M[G] ⊨ 2^ℵ₀ = ℵ₂*, i.e., ¬CH. ∎

> **Theorem 2 (Hamkins [4]):** Every model *V ⊨ ZFC* has forcing extensions *V[G]*, *V[H]* with the *same* reals, the *same* cardinals, yet *V[G] ⊨ CH* and *V[H] ⊨ ¬CH*.
> *Proof sketch.* Assume *V ⊨ CH*; let *H* add ω₂ Cohen reals, so *V[H] ⊨ ¬CH*. Collapse ω₂^V to ω₁^V by countably closed forcing *g* over *V[H]*; the collapse adds no reals, and transports *H* to a filter *G* generic for Add(ω, ω₁), which is ccc. Then ℝ^{V[G]} = ℝ^{V[H]} with identical cardinals, but *V[G] ⊨ CH* while *V[H] ⊨ ¬CH*. ∎

This is philosophically striking: the continuum hypothesis is not even determined by the reals and the cardinals together.

> **Theorem 3 (Cohen's first model [10]):** There is a symmetric submodel of a Cohen extension containing a Dedekind-finite infinite set of reals.
> *Proof sketch.* Add countably many Cohen reals, then pass to the symmetric extension that "forgets the enumeration but remembers the set" [10]. The resulting set of reals is infinite yet has no countably infinite subset — choice fails dramatically, while ZF holds. ∎

**Computational analogue.** True genericity requires meeting infinitely many dense sets, but the finite fragment is executable. The following script builds the Cohen tree to depth *n*, enumerates toy "dense" sets, and greedily constructs a filter meeting each — the Rasiowa–Sikorski construction in miniature:

```python
from itertools import product

def cohen_tree(depth):
    """Conditions of Add(omega,1) truncated to given depth."""
    return [bits for d in range(depth + 1) for bits in product((0, 1), repeat=d)]

def extends(q, p):
    return len(q) >= len(p) and q[:len(p)] == p

def dense_sets(tree, depth):
    """Toy dense-open sets definable in the finite ground model."""
    D = []
    # D_k: conditions deciding coordinate k to be 1 somewhere (newness vs. zero real)
    for k in range(depth):
        D.append([p for p in tree if len(p) > k and p[k] == 1])
    # E_x: conditions incompatible with a fixed ground-model real x
    for x in product((0, 1), repeat=depth):
        D.append([p for p in tree if not extends(x, p) and not extends(p, x)])
    return [s for s in D if s]

def generic_filter(tree, dense):
    """Greedy Rasiowa-Sikorski: strengthen to meet each dense set in turn."""
    cur = ()
    for D in dense:
        cur = next(p for p in D if extends(p, cur))
    return cur

tree = cohen_tree(6)
G = generic_filter(tree, dense_sets(tree, 6))
print("generic branch:", G)   # meets every toy dense set by construction
```

The script always succeeds — the finite analogue of the fact that countably many dense sets can be met simultaneously. The infinitary version is exactly what yields the Cohen real.

##6 Limitations

Forcing is powerful but bounded, and the boundaries are theorems, not gaps in technique:

1. **Consistency strength ceiling.** Forcing proves only *relative* consistency: Con(ZFC) ⇒ Con(ZFC + φ). By Gödel's second incompleteness theorem, no forcing argument can establish Con(ZFC) itself; every extension *M[G]* inherits the ground model's consistency strength (modulo the large cardinals explicitly assumed, as in the original Cichoń's maximum construction [7]).
2. **Preservation constraints.** ccc forcing preserves cardinals and cofinalities, which is a feature for ¬CH but a hard limit elsewhere: no ccc forcing collapses ω₁, and no set forcing can change the cofinality of a cardinal to ω without adding reals. Controlling invariants like **b** and **d** independently required the invention of *proper* forcing and countable-support iterations.
3. **The continuum function above ω.** Easton's theorem shows ZFC barely constrains 2^κ for regular κ; forcing realizes almost any pattern. The "empirical" content of §5 is therefore *consistency*, not truth: forcing tells us what the axioms leave open, never which alternative holds in the intended universe.
4. **Computational fidelity.** The Python and TLA+ sketches above are finite toys. Genuine genericity is irreducibly infinitary — no program can construct an *M*-generic filter for the true Cohen poset, since that would place the filter inside *M*, contradicting the diagonal argument of §2.

##7 Conclusion

Cohen's forcing turned the continuum hypothesis from a conjecture into a *choice*: relative to ZFC, the continuum may be ℵ₁ or ℵ₂ or vastly larger, and the decision propagates through the fine structure measured by **b**, **d**, and Cichoń's ten characteristics. The method's architecture — names, the forcing relation, the Truth and Definability lemmas, preservation via the ccc and the Δ-system lemma — remains the template for every modern independence proof, from Hamkins's same-reals-different-CH models [4] to the maximal separations of Cichoń's diagram [7][8][9] and the choiceless symmetric extensions of Cohen's first model [10]. What began in 1963 as a single consistency proof became set theory's universal laboratory: a disciplined way to adjoin precisely the sets one wants, meet precisely the dense sets one needs, and thereby map the exact boundary between what the axioms of mathematics determine and what they leave forever open.
