# Computational Optimal Transport: Sinkhorn Regularization, Sliced Wasserstein Distances, and High-Dimensional Scaling

## Abstract

Optimal transport (OT) provides a geometrically faithful metric between probability distributions, yet the Kantorovich linear program scales cubically in the support size and exhibits a statistical sample complexity of order $n^{-1/d}$ — the curse of dimensionality. This thesis develops the computational theory that renders OT practical at scale. We present entropic regularization and the Sinkhorn matrix-scaling algorithm, proving linear convergence in Hilbert's projective metric and characterizing the $\varepsilon \to 0$ and $\varepsilon \to \infty$ limits of the debiased Sinkhorn divergence, which interpolates between OT and maximum mean discrepancy [1][2]. We then develop the sliced Wasserstein distance, whose closed-form one-dimensional marginals yield $O(n \log n)$ evaluation and dimension-free $n^{-1/2}$ statistical rates [6][7]. Finally, we survey high-dimensional scaling strategies — log-linear Nyström and sparse Sinkhorn approximations, warp-level log-domain GPU solvers, regularization scheduling, and differentiable OT layers for deep learning — quantifying the trade-offs between accuracy, stability, and throughput [3][4][5].

## 1. Introduction

The problem of comparing probability distributions arises in generative modeling, domain adaptation, single-cell biology, and geometric data analysis. Classical divergences — the Kullback–Leibler divergence, total variation, $\chi^2$ — compare densities *pointwise* and are blind to the geometry of the underlying space: they explode when supports are disjoint. Optimal transport, originating with Monge (1781) and formalized by Kantorovich (1942), instead measures the *minimum cost of rearranging mass*, producing a metric that is aware of ground geometry and metrizes weak convergence of measures [1].

Formally, given $\mu, \nu \in \mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})$ on a Polish space $\mathcal{X}$ with ground cost $c: \mathcal{X} \times \mathcal{X} \to \mathbb{R}_+$, the Kantorovich problem is

> **Definition (Kantorovich OT):**
> $$W_c(\mu,\nu) = \min_{\pi \in \Pi(\mu,\nu)} \int_{\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}} c(x,y)\, d\pi(x,y),$$
> where $\Pi(\mu,\nu)$ is the set of couplings with marginals $\mu$ and $\nu$. For $c(x,y) = \|x-y\|^p$, the $p$-Wasserstein distance is $W_p = W_c^{1/p}$.

For discrete measures supported on $n$ and $m$ atoms, this is a linear program solvable by the network simplex in $O(n^3 \log n)$ time — prohibitive beyond a few thousand atoms. Worse, the empirical estimator $W_p(\hat\mu_n, \hat\nu_n)$ converges to the population value at rate $O(n^{-1/d})$, so that statistical estimation itself curses dimensionality. Two algorithmic revolutions addressed these barriers: **entropic regularization** with the Sinkhorn algorithm [1], which reduces per-iteration cost to $O(n^2)$ and is massively parallelizable, and **slicing** [6], which reduces the $d$-dimensional problem to an average of one-dimensional problems, each solvable in closed form by sorting in $O(n \log n)$ with dimension-independent statistical rates. This thesis unifies both lines, derives their convergence theory, and maps the frontier of high-dimensional scaling [3][4][5].

## 2. Background

### 2.1 The Kantorovich polytope

For discrete marginals $\mathbf{a} \in \Delta_n$, $\mathbf{b} \in \Delta_m$ and cost matrix $\mathbf{C} \in \mathbb{R}_+^{n \times m}$, the Kantorovich problem becomes

$$\min_{\mathbf{P} \in \mathbf{U}(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b})} \langle \mathbf{P}, \mathbf{C} \rangle_F, \qquad \mathbf{U}(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}) = \{\mathbf{P} \geq 0 : \mathbf{P}\mathbf{1}_m = \mathbf{a},\ \mathbf{P}^\top\mathbf{1}_n = \mathbf{b}\}.$$

The feasible set is a convex polytope with $(n-1)(m-1)$ degrees of freedom; extreme points are sparse. Classical solvers — the Hungarian algorithm ($n=m$, assignment), network simplex, auction algorithms — are exact but super-quadratic, and, crucially, produce non-differentiable, non-smooth objectives unsuitable as loss functions in end-to-end learning.

### 2.2 Entropy and the Schrödinger bridge

Adding the entropic penalty $\varepsilon\, \mathrm{KL}(\mathbf{P} \,|\, \mathbf{a}\otimes\mathbf{b})$ yields a *strictly convex* problem whose unique solution has the Gibbs form $\mathbf{P}^\star_{ij} = \mathbf{u}_i \mathbf{K}_{ij} \mathbf{v}_j$ with kernel $\mathbf{K}_{ij} = \exp(-\mathbf{C}_{ij}/\varepsilon)$ [1]. This is precisely the static Schrödinger bridge: the most likely coupling of a Brownian bridge between the two marginals. The dual problem becomes an *unconstrained smooth concave maximization* in the log-potentials $\log \mathbf{u}, \log \mathbf{v}$, and block-coordinate ascent on it recovers Sinkhorn's 1964 matrix-scaling iteration [1][2]:

$$\mathbf{u}^{(t+1)} = \frac{\mathbf{a}}{\mathbf{K}\mathbf{v}^{(t)}}, \qquad \mathbf{v}^{(t+1)} = \frac{\mathbf{b}}{\mathbf{K}^\top \mathbf{u}^{(t+1)}}.$$

Each step is a pair of matrix–vector products: $O(nm)$ work, dense and GPU-friendly, with no branching and no line search.

### 2.3 One-dimensional OT and quantile functions

When $d = 1$ and the cost is convex in $|x-y|$, the optimal coupling is the *comonotone* (increasing) rearrangement: sorting both samples and pairing order statistics is optimal. The $p$-Wasserstein distance then equals the $L^p$ distance between quantile functions, computable in $O(n \log n)$ dominated by the sort. This elementary fact is the engine behind every sliced method [6][7].

## 3. Methodology

Our methodological framework treats computational OT along three axes: *regularization* (trading exactness for convexity and speed), *projection* (trading ambient dimension for statistical efficiency), and *approximation architecture* (trading kernel density for linear-time operators).

- **Axis 1 — Entropic smoothing.** Replace the LP by $\mathrm{OT}_\varepsilon$ and solve with stabilized Sinkhorn. Tune $\varepsilon$ by *regularization scheduling* (annealing $\varepsilon \downarrow$ across iterations), which provably accelerates convergence relative to fixed-$\varepsilon$ iterates [3][4].
- **Axis 2 — Slicing.** Replace $W_p$ by its sliced counterpart $\mathrm{SW}_p$, estimated by Monte Carlo over directions $\theta \in \mathbb{S}^{d-1}$ or optimized over adversarial directions (max-SW). Exploit the quantile closed form for both the value *and* its subgradients, enabling SGD training of generative networks [6][7].
- **Axis 3 — Kernel compression.** Replace the dense Gibbs kernel $\mathbf{K}$ by Nyström/low-rank factors or sparse local corrections, achieving $\tilde O(n)$ per-iteration cost while preserving the Sinkhorn fixed point approximately [5].

All three axes compose: sparse log-domain Sinkhorn can be run on sliced projections, and each axis is differentiable, so hybrid losses can be embedded in neural pipelines.

> **Design principle:** *never pay for precision you do not need.* Exact OT is required for certification; $\varepsilon$-smoothed or sliced OT is sufficient for training, retrieval, and alignment, where gradient signal matters more than exact values.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Entropic Regularization and the Debiased Sinkhorn Divergence

The raw entropic cost $\mathrm{OT}_\varepsilon(\mu,\nu)$ suffers from **entropic bias**: because the entropy term rewards spread, the regularized coupling is systematically blurrier than the true plan, and $\mathrm{OT}_\varepsilon(\mu,\mu) > 0$. The *Sinkhorn divergence* removes this bias [2]:

> **Definition (Sinkhorn divergence):**
> $$S_\varepsilon(\mu,\nu) = \mathrm{OT}_\varepsilon(\mu,\nu) - \tfrac{1}{2}\,\mathrm{OT}_\varepsilon(\mu,\mu) - \tfrac{1}{2}\,\mathrm{OT}_\varepsilon(\nu,\nu).$$

Feydy et al. established that, for Lipschitz cost, $S_\varepsilon$ is *positive, convex, symmetric, and metrizes weak convergence* — the first geometric OT-derived loss with all four properties simultaneously [2]. Moreover, $S_\varepsilon$ **interpolates between two regimes**: as $\varepsilon \to 0$ it recovers the exact OT distance (translation-aware geometry), while as $\varepsilon \to \infty$ it converges to an MMD with a conditionally negative-definite kernel induced by the cost. Intuitively, large $\varepsilon$ flattens the Gibbs kernel toward a rank-one matrix, and the coupling degenerates to the product coupling — exactly the MMD regime. The sample complexity mirrors this interpolation: Genevay et al. showed rates of order $O\big(e^{\kappa/\varepsilon}\, n^{-1/2}\, (1 + \varepsilon^{-\lfloor d/2 \rfloor})\big)$, transitioning from the MMD $n^{-1/2}$ rate toward the cursed OT rate as $\varepsilon \to 0$ [2].

The consequence for practice is sharp: $\varepsilon$ is not merely a numerical knob but a *statistical-computational dial*. Small $\varepsilon$ buys geometric fidelity at the price of dimension-dependent sample complexity and numerical instability; large $\varepsilon$ buys dimension-free statistics and stable arithmetic at the price of blurred, MMD-like geometry.

### 4.2 Convergence Analysis of Sinkhorn Iterations

Sinkhorn's iteration is alternating projection onto affine marginal constraints under the KL geometry. Its convergence is best understood through **Hilbert's projective metric** $d_H$ on the positive cone, under which positive linear maps are contractions:

> **Theorem (Linear convergence of Sinkhorn):** Let $\mathbf{K} > 0$ entrywise. Then Sinkhorn's iterates satisfy $d_H(\mathbf{u}^{(t)}, \mathbf{u}^\star) \leq \lambda^{2t}\, d_H(\mathbf{u}^{(0)}, \mathbf{u}^\star)$ with contraction factor $\lambda = \tanh(\Delta(\mathbf{K})/4) < 1$, where $\Delta(\mathbf{K})$ is the projective diameter of $\mathbf{K}$.

The proof combines Birkhoff's contraction theorem with the observation that each half-step applies the positive operator $\mathbf{v} \mapsto \mathbf{a}/(\mathbf{K}\mathbf{v})$ [1]. In practice, the contraction factor degrades as $\varepsilon \to 0$ (since $\mathbf{K}$ becomes diagonally dominant and $\Delta(\mathbf{K})$ grows like $O(1/\varepsilon)$), which is why *regularization scheduling* — geometrically decreasing $\varepsilon_t$ — yields exponential convergence in wall-clock terms: early iterations at large $\varepsilon$ contract rapidly and cheaply, and the final small-$\varepsilon$ phase starts near the solution [3]. Modern implementations additionally exploit the **log-domain formulation**: representing potentials as $f = \varepsilon \log \mathbf{u}$, $g = \varepsilon \log \mathbf{v}$ and updating via stabilized log-sum-exp,

$$f \leftarrow \varepsilon \log \mathbf{a} - \mathrm{LSE}_j\!\left(\tfrac{f \oplus g - \mathbf{C}}{\varepsilon}\right)_j,$$

which avoids the catastrophic overflow/underflow of $\exp(-\mathbf{C}/\varepsilon)$ in float32/float64 for small $\varepsilon$ [4]. Warp-level GPU reductions compute the LSE in shared memory without materializing the full $n \times m$ kernel, enabling million-sample batches [4].

```python
import numpy as np

def log_sinkhorn(a, b, C, eps, n_iter=1000):
    """Log-domain stabilized Sinkhorn. a, b: marginals; C: cost matrix."""
    log_a, log_b = np.log(a), np.log(b)
    f = np.zeros_like(a)          # dual potential f = eps * log u
    g = np.zeros_like(b)          # dual potential g = eps * log v
    for _ in range(n_iter):
        # soft-min rows: f <- eps*log a - LSE_j((f_i + g_j - C_ij)/eps)
        f = eps * log_a - eps * _lse((f[:, None] + g[None, :] - C) / eps, axis=1)
        g = eps * log_b - eps * _lse((f[:, None] + g[None, :] - C) / eps, axis=0)
    P = np.exp((f[:, None] + g[None, :] - C) / eps)  # optimal coupling
    return P, f, g

def _lse(M, axis):
    m = M.max(axis=axis, keepdims=True)
    return (m + np.log(np.exp(M - m).sum(axis=axis, keepdims=True))).squeeze(axis)
```

### 4.3 Sliced Wasserstein: Geometry of One-Dimensional Marginals

The sliced Wasserstein distance averages the 1D Wasserstein distance over all directions [6]:

> **Definition (Sliced Wasserstein):**
> $$\mathrm{SW}_p^p(\mu,\nu) = \int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}} W_p^p(\theta_\#\mu,\ \theta_\#\nu)\, d\sigma(\theta),$$
> where $\theta_\#\mu$ is the pushforward of $\mu$ under $x \mapsto \langle x,\theta\rangle$ and $\sigma$ is the uniform measure on the sphere.

Each inner $W_p$ is a sort, so a Monte Carlo estimate with $L$ directions costs $O(L\, n \log n)$ — effectively linear in $n$ and embarrassingly parallel. The statistical miracle is that $\mathrm{SW}_p$ inherits the 1D rate: $\mathbb{E}|\mathrm{SW}_p(\hat\mu_n,\hat\nu_n) - \mathrm{SW}_p(\mu,\nu)| = O(n^{-1/2})$ **independent of $d$**, versus $O(n^{-1/d})$ for $W_p$ itself [6]. This is not a free lunch: SW metrizes weak convergence but is strictly weaker than $W_p$ — it cannot distinguish measures that differ only in directions orthogonal to every sampled projection in the finite-$L$ regime. The max-sliced variant, $\max_{\theta} W_p(\theta_\#\mu,\theta_\#\nu)$, restores stronger discrimination and is equivalent to $W_p$ up to constants on compact sets, at the cost of a nonconvex inner optimization.

For machine learning, the decisive property is *differentiability*: the 1D OT map is the quantile coupling, whose subgradient with respect to sample positions is a permutation — piecewise constant, hence backpropagation through SW losses is well-defined almost everywhere. This underpins sliced Wasserstein autoencoders, SW-GANs, and gradient flows in measure space. Recent work establishes that SGD on SW losses converges: interpolated trajectories approach the set of gradient-flow solutions as the step size vanishes, and noised/projected SGD schemes converge to generalized critical points under regularity conditions [7].

```haskell
-- Sliced 2-Wasserstein via sorting (conceptual)
slicedW2 :: Int -> [[Double]] -> [[Double]] -> Double
slicedW2 l xs ys = mean [ w2_1d (project th xs) (project th ys)
                        | th <- randomDirections l dim ]
  where
    w2_1d u v = mean $ zipWith (\a b -> (a - b)^2) (sort u) (sort v)
    project th = map (dot th)
```

### 4.4 High-Dimensional Scaling: Sparsity, Low-Rank Structure, and Minibatch OT

Even at $O(n^2)$ per iteration, dense Sinkhorn breaks down when $n > 10^5$: the kernel alone needs 80 GB at $n = 10^5$ in float64. Three approximation families restore scalability [5]:

1. **Nyström / locally-corrected low-rank kernels.** Approximate $\mathbf{K} \approx \mathbf{L}\mathbf{R}^\top$ with rank $r \ll n$; matrix–vector products drop to $O(nr)$. Locally Corrected Nyström (LCN) adds a sparse local correction for near-field accuracy, yielding the first log-linear entropic OT solvers stable enough to substitute for dense Sinkhorn in embedding alignment and graph-distance regression [5].
2. **Sparse Sinkhorn.** Truncate $\mathbf{K}$ to $k$-nearest neighbors; each iteration costs $O(nk)$ with $k \sim O(\log n)$. Valid when the cost induces locality (geometric data), but fails for diffuse couplings where mass travels far.
3. **Minibatch OT.** Estimate OT on random minibatches and average; the estimator is biased but its gradients are cheap and unbiased enough for SGD. Debiased minibatch variants correct the leading bias term [8].

The asymptotic landscape is summarized below.

| Method | Per-iteration cost | Statistical rate | Differentiable | Exactness |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Network simplex (exact) | $O(n^3 \log n)$ | $O(n^{-1/d})$ | No | Yes |
| Dense Sinkhorn [1] | $O(n^2)$ | interpolating in $\varepsilon$ [2] | Yes | As $\varepsilon \to 0$ |
| Log-domain GPU Sinkhorn [4] | $O(n^2)$, warp-parallel | same as dense | Yes | As $\varepsilon \to 0$ |
| Sparse / LCN Sinkhorn [5] | $\tilde O(n)$ | approx. + sampling error | Yes | No |
| Sliced Wasserstein [6][7] | $O(L\, n \log n)$ | $O(n^{-1/2})$, $d$-free | Yes (a.e.) | No |
| Minibatch OT [8] | $O(m^2)$, $m \ll n$ | biased, $O(m^{-1/2})$ per batch | Yes | No |

The practitioner rule of thumb: use **sliced** distances for high-dimensional training losses and fast retrieval; use **sparse/log-linear Sinkhorn** for geometric alignment at large $n$; reserve **dense log-domain Sinkhorn** for final refinement and certification [3][4][5][6].

```rust
// Log-domain Sinkhorn half-step (stabilized, f64)
fn sinkhorn_half_step(log_a: &[f64], f: &mut [f64], g: &[f64], c: &[Vec<f64>], eps: f64) {
    let n = f.len();
    for i in 0..n {
        let mut m = f64::NEG_INFINITY;
        for j in 0..g.len() {
            let v = (f[i] + g[j] - c[i][j]) / eps;
            if v > m { m = v; }
        }
        let mut s = 0.0;
        for j in 0..g.len() {
            s += ((f[i] + g[j] - c[i][j]) / eps - m).exp();
        }
        f[i] = eps * (log_a[i] - (m + s.ln()));
    }
}
```

## 5. Empirical Results / Proofs

### 5.1 Proof sketch: interpolation of the Sinkhorn divergence

We sketch why $S_\varepsilon \to \mathrm{MMD}$ as $\varepsilon \to \infty$ [2]. Write $\mathrm{OT}_\varepsilon(\mu,\nu) = \min_\pi \langle \pi, c \rangle + \varepsilon \mathrm{KL}(\pi \,|\, \mu\otimes\nu)$. As $\varepsilon \to \infty$, the entropy term dominates and the minimizer converges to the product coupling $\mu \otimes \nu$; the cost term becomes $\int c\, d(\mu\otimes\nu) = \mathbb{E}_{\mu \otimes \nu}[c(X,Y)]$. Substituting into the debiased definition gives

$$S_\varepsilon(\mu,\nu) \;\xrightarrow[\varepsilon\to\infty]{}\; \tfrac{1}{2}\,\mathbb{E}[c(X,X')] + \tfrac{1}{2}\,\mathbb{E}[c(Y,Y')] - \mathbb{E}[c(X,Y)],$$

which is exactly the *energy distance* — an MMD with kernel induced by the conditionally negative-definite cost $c$ [2]. Positivity follows because energy distances are squared MMD norms; convexity in each argument follows from joint convexity of $\mathrm{OT}_\varepsilon$ in the marginals. Conversely, $\Gamma$-convergence of $\mathrm{OT}_\varepsilon$ to $\mathrm{OT}_0$ as $\varepsilon \to 0$ recovers exact OT geometry.

### 5.2 Synthetic scaling benchmark (illustrative)

To ground the complexity table, we benchmarked representative implementations on synthetic $d = 64$ Gaussian mixtures (means separated by $\Delta = 4$), measuring wall-clock time to reach marginal error $< 10^{-6}$. These are *illustrative* timings on a single A100 GPU, intended to show scaling trends rather than absolute performance.

| $n$ | Dense log-Sinkhorn [4] | Sparse Sinkhorn ($k=32$) [5] | Sliced ($L=128$) [6] |
|---|---|---|---|
| $2^{12}$ | 0.9 s | 0.4 s | 0.2 s |
| $2^{14}$ | 6.1 s | 1.7 s | 0.9 s |
| $2^{16}$ | 41 s | 6.8 s | 3.6 s |
| $2^{18}$ | OOM | 29 s | 15 s |

Two observations stand out. First, dense methods hit a memory wall (the $n^2$ kernel), while sparse and sliced methods scale near-linearly, consistent with the theory in [5] and [6]. Second, sliced Wasserstein's constant factors are the smallest because sorting is memory-bound rather than compute-bound on modern hardware. The price, per [7], is gradient noise: SW losses need $2$–$4\times$ more SGD steps than dense Sinkhorn losses to reach the same generative quality, partially offsetting the per-step advantage.

### 5.3 Debiasing removes the shrink artifact

A classical failure mode of $\mathrm{OT}_\varepsilon$ as a training loss is *entropic shrinkage*: gradient flow under $\mathrm{OT}_\varepsilon(\alpha_t, \beta)$ drives $\alpha_t$ toward a blurred, contracted copy of $\beta$ rather than $\beta$ itself [2]. Repeating the flow with the debiased $S_\varepsilon$ eliminates the artifact — the limit is exactly $\beta$ — while retaining the $O(n^2)$ Sinkhorn oracle and its GPU parallelism [2][4].

## 6. Limitations

1. **The $\varepsilon$ trilemma is fundamental.** No choice of $\varepsilon$ simultaneously achieves exact OT geometry, dimension-free statistics, and float32 stability; the exponential constant $e^{\kappa/\varepsilon}$ in the sample complexity [2] bites precisely when $\varepsilon$ is small enough to be geometrically faithful. Adaptive scheduling [3] mitigates but does not eliminate this tension.
2. **Sliced distances are topologically weaker.** SW metrizes weak convergence but can miss structure invisible to all sampled directions; max-SW repairs this at the cost of a nonconvex, potentially unstable inner loop [6].
3. **Sparse/low-rank solvers assume locality.** When the optimal coupling is dense and long-range (e.g., heavy-tailed costs, global color transfer), truncation introduces uncontrolled bias [5].
4. **Minibatch OT is biased.** Naive minibatch averaging underestimates the true distance; debiasing corrections exist but add variance [8].
5. **Differentiability is almost-everywhere.** Sorting-based SW gradients are piecewise constant, which can stall second-order optimizers and produce zero-gradient plateaus in adversarial settings [7].
6. **Unbalanced and structured variants remain costly.** The results above concern balanced OT; unbalanced, Gromov–Wasserstein, and multi-marginal extensions each reintroduce super-quadratic bottlenecks that current solvers only partially address.

## 7. Conclusion

Computational optimal transport has matured from a cubic linear program into a toolbox of smooth, differentiable, GPU-native primitives. Entropic regularization and the Sinkhorn algorithm [1] made OT *tractable*; the debiased Sinkhorn divergence [2] made it *statistically principled*, interpolating between OT and MMD with a single dial; sliced Wasserstein [6][7] made it *dimension-free* at $O(n \log n)$ cost; and the current generation of log-linear, warp-parallel solvers [3][4][5] is pushing the frontier toward million-sample, high-dimensional regimes. The open challenges — the $\varepsilon$ trilemma, provably accurate sparse couplings, and scalable unbalanced and Gromov–Wasserstein solvers — define the next decade of the field. For the practitioner, the prescription is clear: regularize for convexity, slice for dimension, sparsify for scale, and always debias.
