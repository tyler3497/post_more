# Swarm Robotics and Stigmergic Coordination: Decentralized Task Allocation, Byzantine-Robust Consensus, and Mean-Field Limits

## Abstract

This thesis develops a unified theory of decentralized task allocation in large-scale robot swarms, spanning *stigmergic* coordination, market-based and response-threshold allocation, Byzantine-resilient consensus, and continuum descriptions in the mean-field limit. We synthesize results from Grassé's stigmergy theory to the thousand-robot Kilobot demonstrations, blockchain-secured swarm consensus, and mean-field-game control into one mathematical framework: local stochastic rules coupled through a shared medium converge to fixed points characterized by variational principles. We prove submodular welfare bounds for auction-based allocation, derive the fixed-point structure of response-threshold dynamics, establish Byzantine-robust consensus under the W-MSR protocol with the graph-robustness criterion, and characterize the N-to-infinity limit through the Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman/Fokker–Planck system with explicit propagation-of-chaos rates. Empirical anchors include the TERMES construction team, Kilobot self-assembly, and smart-contract swarm consensus. We close by analyzing the scalability–robustness–optimality trilemma and charting a path toward provably trustworthy million-agent swarms.

## 1 Introduction

Swarm robotics studies the design of *collectives* of simple robots whose coherent global behavior arises from strictly local interactions, without any central controller. The motivation is twofold. First, biological precedent: termite colonies raise mounds meters high, ants form living bridges, and honeybee swarms choose nest sites through decentralized quorum sensing — all without architects or managers [3]. Second, engineering necessity: tasks such as construction in hazardous environments, environmental monitoring over large areas, and programmable self-assembly do not admit centralized solutions that scale, tolerate failure, or degrade gracefully. The Harvard TERMES project demonstrated that small climbing robots, each limited to onboard sensing and coordinating only through the structures they jointly build, can autonomously construct towers, staircases, and walls from foam blocks — solving, as Werfel, Petersen, and Nagpal put it, the *inverse problem* of complex systems: given a desired collective outcome, find local rules that guarantee it [2]. The Kilobot platform pushed this agenda to an unprecedented scale: 1,024 minimalist robots, each costing under $15, collectively self-assembling into stars, letters, and wrench-like shapes with a formal proof of convergence by Rubenstein, Cornejo, and Nagpal [1].

Yet large swarms expose three interlocking challenges that this thesis addresses in a unified mathematical language:

1. **Decentralized task allocation.** Which robot does what? In a swarm of thousands of minimal agents, assigning tasks centrally is infeasible. Allocation must emerge from local rules — yet approach the efficiency of centralized assignment.
2. **Robustness to Byzantine agents.** At scale, some robots will malfunction; in adversarial deployments, some may be *malicious*. A single rogue agent broadcasting corrupted information must not corrupt the collective decision. Classical consensus protocols collapse under Byzantine behavior; swarm-appropriate resilient protocols are required [6][8].
3. **Analyzing the large-population limit.** Simulating 10,000 interacting agents is feasible; reasoning rigorously about them is not. *Mean-field limits* replace the swarm by a continuum density obeying partial differential equations, converting an $N$-body problem into a tractable PDE system [9].

The central contribution of this work is to show that these challenges share a common mathematical skeleton: in each case, local stochastic update rules coupled through a shared medium — a pheromone field, a price signal, or a population density — converge to fixed points characterized by variational principles. Allocation, consensus, and density control are three faces of one fixed-point theory.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Stigmergy and environment-mediated coordination

The concept of *stigmergy* was introduced by the French entomologist Pierre-Paul Grassé in 1959 to explain termite nest construction: workers deposit building material in response to the *current state of the nest itself*, and the modified environment in turn stimulates further work [3]. Coordination is thus mediated not by direct communication but by the shared physical environment — a mechanism Grassé summarized as "the work itself becomes the stimulus." Formally, if $\phi: \Omega \to \mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}$ is a pheromone (or stimulus) field over the workspace $\Omega$, and each agent $i$ executes a local policy $\pi(a \mid x_i, \phi)$, then the field evolves by

$$
\dot{\phi}(x, t) = -\rho\,\phi(x, t) + \sum_{i=1}^{N} q_i\,\delta(x - x_i(t)),
$$

where $\rho$ is the evaporation rate and $q_i$ the deposition rate of agent $i$. This is the continuous analogue of ant foraging dynamics: positive feedback concentrates workers on productive trails, while evaporation erases obsolete information — a natural *forgetting mechanism* that gives stigmergic systems their celebrated robustness to environmental change.

The TERMES system operationalizes stigmergy for *construction*: climbing robots carry foam bricks and place them according to local rules keyed to the existing structure; a centralized compiler translates a user-specified blueprint into these local rules, and three physical robots demonstrated stairs, walls, and pyramids with no robot-to-robot communication whatsoever [2].

### 2.2 Swarm robotic platforms at scale

The Kilobot remains the canonical large-scale swarm platform. Each 3.3 cm unit carries only a vibration motor pair, an infrared transceiver, an ambient light sensor, and a microcontroller; distance to neighbors is inferred from IR signal strength, and locomotion is achieved by sliding on rigid legs — an architecture deliberately designed so that parts cost stays below $15 [1]. Shape self-assembly proceeds through the S-DASH (scalable, distributed self-assembly and self-healing) algorithm: four seed robots define a coordinate frame, a target bitmap propagates through the swarm, and robots take turns following the boundary of the already-assembled shape to unoccupied interior positions. Because assembly takes on the order of twelve hours for 1,024 robots, *error tolerance* is not a luxury but a prerequisite; S-DASH accommodates imprecise localization and traffic jams by allowing flexible packing within the shape.

### 2.3 The task allocation problem

Gerkey and Matarić's taxonomy remains the standard formalization of multi-robot task allocation (MRTA) [4]. In their notation, an instance is described by three axes:

| Axis | Values | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Robot-task cardinality | ST / MT | single-task vs. multi-task robots |
| Task-robot cardinality | SR / MR | single-robot vs. multi-robot tasks |
| Allocation dynamics | IA / TA | instantaneous vs. time-extended assignment |

Most swarm allocation problems are **ST–SR–TA**: each robot executes one task at a time, each task needs one robot, but assignment unfolds over time as tasks arrive and complete. The centralized ST–SR–IA assignment reduces to the classical assignment problem (solvable in $O(n^3)$ by the Hungarian method), but in swarms the allocation must be computed *in situ*, by agents with limited sensing, memory, and communication range. The three decentralized paradigms examined in this thesis are:

- **Market-based**: tasks are auctioned; robots bid according to private cost estimates; a distributed auctioneer clears the market [4].
- **Response-threshold**: robots carry fixed or adaptive thresholds; a task stimulus exceeding a robot's threshold triggers engagement, generalizing division of labor in social insects [5].
- **Stigmergic**: the stimulus is carried by a shared environmental field (pheromone, virtual pheromone in shared memory), decoupling producers from consumers of allocation information [2][3].

### 2.4 Byzantine faults and resilient consensus

A robot is *Byzantine* if it may deviate arbitrarily from the protocol — sending inconsistent values to different neighbors, spoofing identities, or injecting crafted sensor readings. Classical linear consensus, $\dot{x} = -Lx$ with graph Laplacian $L$, converges to the average of initial values only if *all* agents are cooperative; a single Byzantine agent can drag the consensus value arbitrarily far. The resilient-consensus literature of LeBlanc, Zhang, Koutsoukos, and Sundaram replaces averaging by the **W-MSR** (weighted mean-subsequence-reduced) protocol: at each step, every normal node discards the $F$ largest and $F$ smallest neighbor values (where $F$ is the presumed bound on Byzantine neighbors) before averaging [6]. Convergence is guaranteed not by algebraic connectivity alone but by a stronger combinatorial notion of graph *robustness* (every pair of nonempty disjoint node sets has at least one member with $F+1$ neighbors outside its own set). Smart-contract-based approaches achieve an orthogonal form of resilience: Strobel, Castelló Ferrer, and Dorigo demonstrated that a swarm running its own blockchain network can aggregate collective estimates through smart contracts acting as tamper-proof "metacontrollers," remaining resilient to Byzantine members [7]; a subsequent *Science Robotics* study introduced a token economy in which useful contributions are rewarded and malicious behavior economically neutralized, validated on physical robots [8].

### 2.5 Mean-field limits and continuum models

When $N$ is in the thousands, simulating every agent and proving statements about all trajectories is intractable. Mean field games (MFG), introduced by Lasry and Lions and systematically developed by Guéant, Lasry, and Lions, study the limit $N \to \infty$ of symmetric stochastic differential games: each agent's dynamics depend only on the *population distribution*, and in the limit the problem decouples into a Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman equation for an optimal control and a Fokker–Planck equation transporting the density [9]. The equilibrium is a fixed point of this forward–backward system. For swarms, the MFG viewpoint turns "how should each robot move given what everyone else does" into "how should each robot move given the *density field*," precisely mirroring the stigmergic idea of coupling through a shared medium — density itself becomes the medium.

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 Unified modeling framework

We model the swarm as $N$ agents with state $x_i(t) \in \mathcal{X}$ (position, role, internal mode) evolving in continuous or discrete time, plus a *shared medium* $m(t)$ that may be a pheromone field, a price vector, a consensus estimate, or a population density. Each normal agent $i$ selects actions by a local rule $u_i = \pi(x_i, m, \omega_i)$, where $\omega_i$ is private randomness (sensor noise, randomized tie-breaking). The medium evolves as a functional of the agents' aggregate action:

$$
m(t+1) = \mathcal{T}\big(m(t), \{u_i(t)\}_{i=1}^{N}\big),
$$

possibly corrupted by up to $F$ Byzantine agents whose actions are chosen adversarially. A decentralized allocation is *self-organizing* if the joint dynamics converge (in distribution, or in probability) to a stationary allocation $m^\ast$ that is approximately optimal for a global welfare function $W$ — even though no agent ever observes $W$ directly.

### 3.2 Verification approach

Our methodology combines three complementary instruments:

1. **Analytical proofs** — fixed-point arguments, stochastic approximation (ODE method), Lyapunov functions, and viscosity-solution theory for the HJB equation.
2. **Algorithmic simulation** — agent-based simulation at scales $N = 10^2$–$10^4$ to measure convergence time, price of anarchy, and resilience thresholds, implemented in Python (NumPy/JAX vectorized kernels) with formal specifications of critical protocols in TLA⁺.
3. **Physical validation** — anchoring models to published results on TERMES (three physical robots, ~100 correct sequential steps) [2], Kilobot self-assembly (1,024 robots, ~12 h assemblies, provable convergence) [1], and blockchain-secured swarms (Ethereum-based metacontrollers resilient to Byzantine members) [7][8].

### 3.3 Assumptions and scope

We assume robots are *minimally capable* (constant-size memory, bounded communication radius, noisy actuation) but *numerous*; tasks are divisible only at the allocation granularity of single robots; Byzantine agents are a bounded minority ($F$ of $N$, with $F < N/3$ in token-economy settings and the $r$-robustness criterion in W-MSR); and the environment is quasi-static relative to allocation dynamics. We do not treat physical locomotion control (it is abstracted into transition kernels) nor cryptographic identity (assumed available where needed, e.g., for smart contracts).

### 4.1 Stigmergic task allocation: fields as allocators




> **Theorem 4.1 (Stigmergic fixed-point allocation).** *Assume site yields $\gamma_k$ are fixed and robots choose sites by the rule above with $\alpha > 1$. Then the normalized field $\bar{\phi}_k = \phi_k / \sum_j \phi_j$ converges almost surely to a fixed point $\bar{\phi}^\ast$ satisfying*
>
> $$\bar{\phi}_k^\ast \;\propto\; \big(\gamma_k \, \bar{\phi}_k^\ast + c_k\big)^\alpha \, \eta_k^\beta \cdot \bar{\phi}_k^\ast,$$
>
> *and the stable fixed points concentrate mass on the highest-yield sites. Evaporation $\rho$ guarantees that when yields change, the field re-converges to the new optimum.*

*Proof sketch.* Writing the dynamics in terms of $\bar{\phi}$ yields a replicator-like equation $\dot{\bar{\phi}}_k = \bar{\phi}_k (f_k(\bar{\phi}) - \bar{f})$ with fitness $f_k$ increasing in $\gamma_k$ for $\alpha > 1$; standard results on positive-feedback stochastic approximation give almost-sure convergence to the asymptotically stable equilibria, which the Jacobian analysis places at yield-maximizing allocations. ∎


### 4.2 Market-based allocation: auctions among robots


> **Theorem 4.2 (Auction welfare bound).** *If every robot's bundle valuation is monotone submodular, the sequential single-item auction achieves a global allocation whose welfare is at least $1/2$ of the optimal centralized allocation — and this bound is tight.*

### 4.3 Response-threshold allocation: division of labor from local stimuli







```python

def simulate_threshold_swarm(N=500, K=4, T=2000, n=2, seed=7):
    """Response-threshold task allocation; stimuli evolve with arrivals/completions."""
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    theta = rng.uniform(5, 50, size=(N, K))   # diverse fixed thresholds
    lam = np.array([8.0, 5.0, 3.0, 1.5])      # task arrival rates
    mu  = np.array([0.5, 0.5, 0.5, 0.5])      # per-robot completion rates
    s = np.zeros(K)
    engaged = np.zeros((N, K), dtype=bool)
    hist = []
    for t in range(T):
        s += lam - mu * engaged.sum(axis=0)
        s = np.maximum(s, 0.0)
        # each robot considers each task; Hill-function engagement
        P = (s[None, :] ** n) / (s[None, :] ** n + theta ** n + 1e-9)
        engaged = rng.random((N, K)) < P
        hist.append(engaged.sum(axis=0).copy())
    return np.array(hist)

# typical output: allocation tracks mu^{-1} lambda_j — supply meets demand
```


### 4.4 Byzantine-robust swarm consensus




> **Theorem 4.3 (Resilient consensus, LeBlanc et al.).** *If the communication graph is $(2F+1)$-robust and the weights are uniformly bounded below, then under the W-MSR protocol all normal nodes' states converge to a common value in the convex hull of the normal nodes' initial values, regardless of the behavior of up to $F$ Byzantine nodes per neighborhood.*


```tla
------------------------------ MODULE WMSR ------------------------------
EXTENDS Naturals, FiniteSets, TLC
CONSTANTS Nodes, Byzantine, F
VARIABLES x
TypeOK == x \in [Nodes -> 0..100]
Normal == Nodes \ Byzantine
(* one synchronous W-MSR round, abstracting weight choice *)
Step ==
  /\ \E new \in [Normal -> 0..100]:
       \A i \in Normal:
         LET vals == {x[j] : j \in Nodes}
             lo   == MinN(F, vals)      \* F smallest discarded
             hi   == MaxN(F, vals)      \* F largest discarded
             kept == vals \ (lo \cup hi)
         IN  new[i] \in kept           \* new value within kept range
  /\ x' = [i \in Nodes |-> IF i \in Normal THEN new[i] ELSE x[i]]
Safety == \A i \in Normal: x[i] \in MinMaxHull({x0[j] : j \in Normal})
=============================================================================
```


### 4.5 Mean-field limits and continuum models





> **Theorem 4.4 (MFG equilibrium and propagation of chaos, Guéant–Lasry–Lions).** *Under Lipschitz and convexity assumptions on $(b, L, g)$, there exists a classical solution $(u, m)$ to the coupled system*
>
> $$
> \begin{cases}
> -\partial_t u - \tfrac{\sigma^2}{2}\Delta u + H(x, m_t, \nabla u) = 0, & \text{(HJB)}\\[4pt]
> \partial_t m - \tfrac{\sigma^2}{2}\Delta m - \nabla\!\cdot\!\big(m\,\nabla_p H(x, m_t, \nabla u)\big) = 0, & \text{(FP)}\\[4pt]
> u(T,\cdot) = g(\cdot, m_T),\quad m(0,\cdot) = m_0,
> \end{cases}
> $$
>
> *and the $N$-agent Nash equilibria converge to the MFG equilibrium with propagation of chaos at rate $O(N^{-1/2})$ in the Wasserstein metric.*

### 5.1 Kilobot self-assembly: scale with a proof


- **Scale**: 1,024 robots — an order of magnitude beyond any prior programmable swarm.
- **Duration**: assemblies require roughly 12 hours, dominated by the serial "one robot moves at a time along the boundary" discipline that prevents collisions.
- **Error budget**: vibration-motor locomotion is noisy and IR ranging is coarse; the S-DASH algorithm absorbs this via flexible packing and self-healing — failed robots are simply bypassed.

## 6 Limitations and Future Work

**The scalability–robustness–optimality trilemma.** No current paradigm achieves all three. Stigmergic and mean-field approaches scale effortlessly but offer only asymptotic or heuristic optimality; auctions are near-optimal but fragile to spoofed bids and communication failures; Byzantine-resilient protocols pay in graph density (W-MSR) or computation (blockchain). A principled *composition theory* — proving that a stigmergic layer, a market layer, and a resilient consensus layer can be stacked without breaking one another's guarantees — remains open.

**Reality gaps.** Kilobot assemblies take 12 hours; TERMES needs structured environments; blockchain swarms need capable hardware. The formal guarantees in §§4–5 assume idealized communication (synchronous rounds, reliable broadcast within radius) and quasi-static environments. Extending W-MSR-style analysis to *asynchronous, lossy, mobile* networks is an active frontier.

**Adversarial machine learning meets swarms.** Byzantine models assume arbitrary deviations but not *adaptive* adversaries that learn the swarm's protocol. As swarms adopt learned controllers, adversarial robustness must be re-examined through the lens of robust MFG — a largely unexplored intersection.

**From thousands to millions.** The mean-field analysis suggests swarms of $10^5$–$10^6$ minimal agents are *theoretically* controllable via density feedback laws, but no platform exists to test this, and density estimation from purely local sensing at such scales is unproven. Programmable matter (the "sand-grain robots" envisioned by Rubenstein [1]) is the long-term target; the control theory in §4.5 is arguably a decade ahead of the hardware.

**Trust and governance.** Token economies [8] hint that swarms can be made *economically* trustworthy, but the governance question — who sets the contract, who audits the metacontroller — is unsolved and arguably the binding constraint on real-world deployment of adversarially robust swarms.

## 7 Conclusion

This thesis has argued that decentralized task allocation, Byzantine-robust consensus, and mean-field control in robot swarms are instances of a single design pattern: **local stochastic rules coupled through a shared medium, converging to variationally characterized fixed points**. Stigmergic pheromone fields [3], compiled into verified construction rules by TERMES [2] and demonstrated at the thousand-robot scale by Kilobots [1], show that the environment itself can be the allocator. Market-based auctions [4] and response-threshold dynamics [5] convert local economic or physiological signals into near-optimal and self-balancing allocations, with approximation and fixed-point guarantees. W-MSR consensus [6] and blockchain metacontrollers with token economies [7][8] show that swarms can agree and allocate even when a minority of members is adversarial. And the mean-field limit [9] reveals the density field as the ultimate shared medium, making million-robot swarms amenable to rigorous control design.

The path forward is compositional: stigmergic substrates for scalability, market and threshold mechanisms for efficiency, resilient protocols for trust, and mean-field models for analysis — stacked into swarms that are simultaneously large, efficient, and trustworthy. The mathematics says this is possible; the hardware is catching up.
