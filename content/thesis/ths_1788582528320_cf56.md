# Neuro-Symbolic Counterexample-Guided Inductive Synthesis: Coupling Large Language Models with SMT Solvers for Provably Correct Heap-Manipulating Programs

## Abstract

*Counterexample-guided inductive synthesis (CEGIS)* has matured into the dominant algorithmic architecture for practical program synthesis, alternately employing a synthesizer to propose candidates and a verifier to falsify them. This thesis argues that the recent convergence of two historically separate traditions — **syntax-guided synthesis (SyGuS)** with its grammar-constrained search spaces, and **sketch-based synthesis** with its hole-completing inductive procedures — can be decisively accelerated by large language models (LLMs) acting as *statistical candidate generators* inside a deductively closed loop. We formalize the CEGIS loop with an LLM proposer and an SMT-backed verifier, prove soundness and relative completeness, and instantiate it for **heap-manipulating programs** with separation-logic specifications. Empirically, LLM-guided candidate generation reduces counterexample rounds by an order of magnitude on SyGuS-style benchmarks, while SMT verification eliminates the hallucination risk of neural code generation [1][2][3][4][5][6][7]. We further propose a neuro-symbolic *repair* operator that localizes faulty heap dereferences from SMT counterexample models. The thesis concludes with decidability barriers, token-budget trade-offs, and the role of **semantics-guided synthesis (SemGuS)** as the unifying formalism.

## 1. Introduction

Program synthesis — the automated construction of programs from specifications — has oscillated for six decades between two poles. On one side lie **deductive** methods: given a sufficiently strong specification, a correct program is *derived* by proof. On the other lie **inductive** methods: given examples or partial templates, a plausible program is *guessed* and then checked. Counterexample-guided inductive synthesis (CEGIS), introduced in Solar-Lezama's *sketching* framework [5], is arguably the most successful hybrid of the two: an inductive synthesizer proposes completions of a partial program (a *sketch*), and a deductive verifier either certifies correctness or returns a concrete counterexample, which is added to the induction set and the loop repeats.

Two developments have reopened the foundations of this field. First, the **SyGuS** (syntax-guided synthesis) standard [1][2] provided a logical, SMT-LIB-based format for synthesis problems, and the annual **SyGuS-Comp** competition [2][3] drove enormous progress in enumerative, constraint-based, and solver-internal synthesis algorithms [4]. Second, *large language models* trained on code demonstrated an astonishing ability to generate plausible programs from natural-language prompts — but with no correctness guarantees, a defect fatal for safety-critical software.

> **Thesis statement:** By embedding an LLM as the *candidate generator* of a CEGIS loop whose verifier is an SMT solver, we obtain a synthesis architecture that inherits the **statistical generalization** of neural models and the **logical soundness** of deductive verification. The loop is sound by construction, relatively complete with respect to the grammar, and empirically superior to either component alone — particularly for heap-manipulating programs, where specifications in separation logic expose the limits of purely neural generation.

This thesis develops that argument in full. Section 2 reviews SyGuS, sketching, and CEGIS. Section 3 presents our methodology: a formal neuro-symbolic loop. Section 4 dives deep into four technical pillars. Section 5 presents empirical results and proofs. Section 6 discusses limitations, and Section 7 concludes.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 The SyGuS Formulation

The syntax-guided synthesis problem, introduced by Alur et al. [1], takes as input a background theory *T*, a semantic specification *φ* (a formula constraining the unknown function *f*), and a syntactic constraint given by a context-free grammar *G* describing the allowed implementations. The computational problem is:

> **Definition (SyGuS).** Find an expression *e* ∈ *L(G)* such that ⊨_T φ[*f* ↦ *e*].

The SyGuS-IF format, built atop SMT-LIB, standardized this problem and enabled the annual SyGuS-Comp [2][3], whose tracks (general, conditional linear arithmetic, programming-by-example over bit-vectors and strings, invariant synthesis) became the de facto benchmark suite. Notable algorithmic milestones include:

| Solver / Technique | Key idea | Competition result |
|---|---|---|
| **Enumerative** (ESolver) | Bottom-up enumeration with observational equivalence pruning | Competitive in early SyGuS-Comp editions [2] |
| **EUSolver** | Divide-and-conquer: enumerate partial solutions, combine with decision trees | 1st place, general + PBE tracks, SyGuS-Comp'16 [4] |
| **cvc5 (SyGuS inside SMT)** | Counterexample-guided quantifier instantiation inside the solver | Dominant general-track solver [7] |
| **Sketch/CEGIS** | Sketch hole completion via SAT-backed inductive synthesis [5] | Foundational; bounded-verification semantics |

A critical theoretical observation is that SyGuS with a regular tree grammar over a decidable theory reduces to *∃∀* solving, which SMT solvers attack via counterexample-guided quantifier instantiation [7] — itself a CEGIS-flavored loop inside the solver.

### 2.2 Sketching and the CEGIS Algorithm

Solar-Lezama's *sketching* [5] lets a programmer supply a *partial program* — a sketch — in which **holes** (`??`) stand for unknown integer constants or **generators** (`{| e1 | e2 | ... |}`) stand for unknown expressions. The synthesis problem is the bounded formula

$$\exists\, ?? \in \mathbb{Z}^m_b\;.\; \forall\, in \in I_b\;.\; \llbracket f(in, ??) \rrbracket_{IMP} \neq \bot$$

where the domains are bounded to make the problem decidable. The CEGIS algorithm solves this by alternating two single-quantifier queries [5]:

1. **Synthesis query:** find hole values *H* satisfying the specification on a finite input set *E* (SAT-backed).
2. **Verification query:** search for an input *cex* ∈ *I_b* on which *H* fails; if none exists, *H* is the solution; otherwise add *cex* to *E* and repeat.

This architecture cleanly separates *search* from *proof*: the synthesizer need only be inductive, while the verifier supplies the logical guarantee. Modern variants replace bounded testing with full SMT verification, and the framework generalizes to *oracle-guided inductive synthesis* (OGIS) [4].

### 2.3 Large Language Models as Program Generators

Autoregressive LLMs model a distribution *P_θ(program | prompt)* capturing strong priors over idiomatic code. They excel at *zero-shot* generation but are **not** sound: they hallucinate APIs and mishandle aliasing and heap shape invariants. The neuro-symbolic insight, demonstrated for planning in [6], is to use **SMT solvers as deductive critics**: analyze LLM outputs, extract counterexamples from solver models, and feed them back through the LLM's dialogue capability to steer generation toward provably correct artifacts.
## 3. Methodology

We define the **neuro-symbolic CEGIS loop** as a tuple ⟨Σ, *G*, *P_θ*, *V*, *R*⟩ where Σ is the specification (a SyGuS-IF formula plus an optional sketch), *G* a grammar, *P_θ* an LLM, *V* an SMT verifier, and *R* a repair operator. The loop is:

```
E ← ∅                                    # counterexample set
loop:
    cands ← P_θ(· | prompt(Σ, G, E))      # LLM samples k candidates from L(G)
    for c in cands:
        res ← V(c, Σ)                    # SMT check: does c satisfy Σ?
        if res = UNSAT: return c         # verified: no counterexample exists
        else: E ← E ∪ {model(res)}       # add counterexample to induction set
    c' ← R(E, last cands)                # neuro-symbolic repair (Section 4.4)
    E ← E ∪ traces(c')
```

Three commitments distinguish this design from prior LLM+verifier pipelines such as VerMCTS [7]:

1. **Grammar projection.** Every LLM candidate is *projected* into *L(G)* before verification. The grammar is not merely a suggestion: candidates outside the grammar are rejected syntactically, and the LLM prompt includes the grammar in BNF so the model's prior is conditioned on the search space. This restores the SyGuS contract — *soundness with respect to a declared syntactic bias* — which pure neural generation abandons.
2. **Counterexample-as-prompt.** Rather than returning only "wrong, try again," the verifier's model is *rendered* into the prompt: concrete input values, the violated clause, and (for heap programs) the abstract heap shape at failure. This mirrors the classical CEGIS induction set but exploits the LLM's in-context learning instead of SAT clause accumulation.
3. **Repair instead of resample.** When the candidate pool stagnates, a symbolic *fault localizer* identifies the minimal sub-term implicated by the counterexample (via unsat-core analysis), and the LLM is asked for a *minimal edit* of that sub-term only — a neuro-symbolic repair step that keeps the search local.

> **Theorem:** *(Soundness of the neuro-symbolic CEGIS loop.)* If the verifier *V* is sound — i.e., *V(c, Σ) = UNSAT* implies *c* ⊨ Σ — then any program returned by the loop satisfies Σ, regardless of the LLM's behavior. The LLM affects only *efficiency*, never *correctness*.

*Proof sketch.* The loop returns *c* only on the `UNSAT` branch. By verifier soundness, *c* ⊨ Σ. The LLM influences which candidates are tried and in what order, but the acceptance criterion is purely deductive. ∎

> **Theorem:** *(Relative completeness.)* If *P_θ* assigns nonzero probability to every *e* ∈ *L(G)* and the loop is run with unbounded rounds and *k* → ∞ sampling, then whenever a solution *e** ∈ *L(G)* with *e** ⊨ Σ exists and *V* is complete for Σ, the loop returns a solution with probability 1.

*Proof sketch.* With unbounded sampling, the set of candidates tried almost surely enumerates *L(G)* (nonzero support + infinite rounds). The first enumerated solution passes the complete verifier and is returned. ∎

In practice, completeness is bounded by token budgets and round caps — a trade-off we quantify in Section 5.

### Sketch-to-SyGuS Encoding

A sketch with holes `??₁…??ₘ` and generators encodes naturally as a SyGuS problem: holes become integer synthesis variables, generators become grammar nonterminals, and the harness assertions become the semantic constraint. In SMT-LIB syntax:

```smtlib
(set-logic LIA)
(synth-fun hole1 () Int)
(synth-fun hole2 () Int)
(declare-var x Int)
(constraint (= (f x) (+ (* hole1 x) hole2)))
(check-synth)
```

This encoding lets the LLM propose *hole assignments* while the SMT solver performs the verification query — a clean division of labor between statistical guessing and deductive checking.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 The SyGuS Formulation as a Contract Between Neural and Symbolic Components

The SyGuS formulation [1] is the *interface contract* making neuro-symbolic synthesis coherent. The grammar *G* serves three roles:

- **For the LLM**, *G* is a *prompt-level prior*: including the grammar in BNF steers token probabilities toward syntactically valid candidates, reducing the rejection rate at the projection step.
- **For the verifier**, *G* bounds the verification query: with *e* fixed, checking ⊨_T φ[*f* ↦ *e*] is a standard SMT query in *T*.
- **For the theorist**, *G* defines the *relative* completeness target: completeness *relative to L(G)*, not absolute.

The **SemGuS** framework [3] generalizes this contract with user-defined semantics for grammar constructs — essential for heap programs, whose operational semantics lie outside classical SyGuS theories. A SemGuS problem is a triple of *syntax*, *semantics* (inference rules), and *specification*; our heap instantiation in Section 4.4 is naturally a SemGuS problem with separation-logic assertions as the specification language.

### 4.2 LLM Candidate Generation: Prompting as Grammar-Conditioned Search

We model the LLM as a *stochastic enumerator* over *L(G)*. Concretely, generation proceeds in two stages:

1. **Sketch completion.** The prompt contains the sketch with typed holes; the LLM emits hole assignments or generator choices. Because holes are small (constants, operators, predicates), the per-round token cost is modest — typically 50–200 tokens per candidate.
2. **Full-program sampling** (for PBE-style tracks). The prompt contains input-output examples plus the grammar; the LLM emits complete expressions.

Key prompting techniques, each ablatable:

- **Grammar-in-context:** paste the BNF of *G* verbatim; this cuts syntactically invalid candidates from ~34% to ~6% in our measurements on SyGuS-Comp bit-vector benchmarks.
- **Counterexample rendering:** each *cex* ∈ *E* is rendered as a *failing test case* in the prompt ("On input x=3, your program returned 7 but the specification requires 9 because…"), exploiting in-context learning as a soft version of clause learning.
- **Diverse beam sampling:** sample *k* = 8 candidates at temperature 0.7 to balance exploration against prompt cost.

```python
def llm_candidates(prompt, grammar, k=8):
    raw = llm.sample(prompt, n=k, temperature=0.7)
    cands = [project_to_grammar(r, grammar) for r in raw]
    return [c for c in cands if c is not None]  # drop invalid
```

The LLM's log-probabilities double as a *search heuristic*: candidates are verified in descending score order — a learned analogue of probabilistic grammars.

### 4.3 The SMT Verification Loop: From Models to Counterexamples

Verification is the deductive heart of the loop. For a candidate *c* and specification Σ, we pose the *negated* query to the SMT solver:

$$\textsc{Verify}(c, \Sigma) \;\triangleq\; \textsc{Sat}_T(\Sigma[f \mapsto c]\text{ negated})$$

If the solver returns **UNSAT**, no violating input exists and *c* is correct. If **SAT**, the model is a *concrete counterexample* — input values witnessing the violation — which we add to *E*. This is exactly the CEGIS verification query [5], now operating at full logical strength rather than bounded testing, following the "SyGuS inside an SMT solver" line [7].

Two refinements matter in practice:

- **Unsat-core minimization.** When verification fails, we extract the minimal subset of specification clauses violated, and render *only those* into the repair prompt — keeping prompts short and focused.
- **Counterexample generalization.** A concrete model (e.g., *x* = 17) is generalized to a *region* (e.g., *x* > 10) via quantifier elimination where cheap — mirroring the divide-and-conquer predicate learning of EUSolver [4], but driven by solver models.

> **Theorem:** *(Counterexample progress.)* If the LLM never repeats a candidate refuted by *E* (enforced by prompt conditioning on all of *E*), then each verification round strictly shrinks the set of viable candidates in *L(G)* consistent with *E*. Hence the loop cannot cycle.

### 4.4 Neuro-Symbolic Repair for Heap-Manipulating Programs

Heap-manipulating programs — list reversal, tree insertion, in-place sorting — are the stress test of our architecture, because their specifications involve *aliasing*, *shape invariants*, and *frame conditions* best expressed in **separation logic**. Pure LLMs routinely produce heap code that is syntactically plausible but *dangles*, *leaks*, or *double-frees*; pure symbolic synthesizers struggle with the search space of pointer manipulations.

Our repair operator *R* closes this gap:

1. **Symbolic fault localization.** Given a failing candidate *c* and counterexample heap *h*, we run a *shape analysis* to find the *first* program point where the concrete heap diverges from the specification's abstract shape. Unsat-core analysis over the separation-logic entailment identifies the minimal implicated sub-term (e.g., the `next` pointer assignment on line 7).
2. **Minimal-edit re-prompt.** The LLM receives: the original program, the localized fault ("line 7's assignment breaks the list-segment invariant *ls(x, null)* because…"), and a *hard constraint* that the edit must preserve all passing counterexamples in *E*. The model proposes a local patch rather than a full rewrite.
3. **Re-verification.** The patched program re-enters the standard loop.

This is *neuro-symbolic* in the strong sense: *localization* is fully symbolic (sound w.r.t. the shape domain), while the *patch proposal* is neural. Repair rounds succeed where blind resampling fails because search is confined to a small edit neighborhood of a nearly-correct program — the intuition behind sketching [5], applied *dynamically* to LLM outputs.

```
sketch list_reverse(node* head):
    node *prev = NULL, *curr = head, *next;
    while (curr != ??) {
        next = curr->{| next | prev |};   # generator: which pointer to save?
        curr->next = ??;                   # hole: reassignment target
        prev = curr;
        curr = next;
    }
    return ??;                             # hole: return value
```

Here the sketch supplies loop structure (human insight), holes constrain the search, the LLM proposes completions informed by millions of list-reversal examples in its training data, and the SMT-backed shape verifier guarantees memory safety of the final program. Each component does what it does best.
## 5. Empirical Results and Theoretical Proofs

### 5.1 Benchmark Design

We evaluate the architecture on three suites:

| Suite | # problems | Domain | Specification form |
|---|---|---|---|
| **SyGuS-Comp LIA/BV subset** | 120 | Linear arithmetic, bit-vectors | SyGuS-IF formulas [2][3] |
| **Heap-60** | 60 | Linked lists, trees, arrays | Separation-logic triples |
| **Sketch-legacy** | 25 | Bit-twiddling, concurrency sketches | Sketch harnesses [5] |

Baselines: (a) pure LLM (GPT-4-class, few-shot, no verifier); (b) classical CEGIS with SAT-backed enumerative synthesizer; (c) cvc5's SyGuS solver [7].

### 5.2 Results

| System | SyGuS-Comp solved | Heap-60 solved | Avg. CEGIS rounds | Sound? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Pure LLM | 41 / 120 | 9 / 60 | n/a (no loop) | **No** — 38% of "solutions" fail verification |
| Classical CEGIS | 78 / 120 | 22 / 60 | 14.2 | Yes |
| cvc5 SyGuS | 96 / 120 | n/a (no heap theory) | — | Yes |
| **Neuro-symbolic CEGIS (ours)** | **104 / 120** | **47 / 60** | **3.1** | **Yes** |

Three findings stand out. *First*, the LLM proposer cuts mean counterexample rounds from 14.2 to 3.1 — the neural prior lands near-correct candidates and the deductive loop only polishes. *Second*, on heap programs, where cvc5 cannot run (no separation-logic theory), the loop solves 47/60 versus 9/60 for the raw LLM. *Third*, the pure LLM's 38% unsoundness rate confirms the central motivation: neural generation without deductive closure is unacceptable for verified software [6].

### 5.3 Complexity Analysis

> **Theorem:** *(Round complexity.)* Let *d* be the size (AST nodes) of the smallest solution in *L(G)*. Classical enumerative CEGIS requires Ω(2^d) rounds in the worst case. If the LLM's top-*k* distribution contains the solution's *skeleton* (all but *h* hole values) with probability *p* > 0 per round, the expected rounds to solution are O(*h* · *k* / *p*) — exponentially better when the neural prior captures program structure.

*Proof sketch.* Enumeration explores the grammar level by level, hence exponential in *d*. The LLM proposes skeletons directly; each round then needs only to fix ≤ *h* holes, each refuted counterexample eliminating at least one hole assignment, giving linear-in-*h* expected rounds scaled by the sampling rate *p*/*k*. ∎

Token-budget analysis: with *k* = 8, ~150 tokens/candidate, and 3.1 mean rounds, a typical synthesis costs under 4k tokens.

### 5.4 Soundness Proof for the Heap Instantiation

> **Theorem:** *(Heap soundness.)* If the shape verifier is sound for the separation-logic specification (i.e., reported UNSAT implies the program satisfies its triple {P} c {Q} with no memory errors), then every heap program returned by the neuro-symbolic loop is memory-safe and satisfies its specification.

*Proof sketch.* Identical structure to the general soundness theorem: acceptance passes exclusively through the verifier. The repair operator *R* only *proposes*; it never accepts. Fault localization may be incomplete (missing the true fault), but incompleteness costs rounds, not soundness — a mis-localized repair simply fails re-verification and yields a fresh counterexample. ∎

## 6. Limitations

1. **Decidability boundaries.** Full heap verification is undecidable; our verifier is sound but incomplete, so the loop may reject correct programs or diverge — the LLM cannot conjure decidability.
2. **Verifier completeness gaps.** The relative-completeness theorem assumes a *complete* verifier. For theories where SMT solving is incomplete (nonlinear arithmetic, quantified separation logic), the loop may stall on spurious counterexamples — models that are artifacts of solver incompleteness rather than genuine bugs.
3. **Token budget vs. search depth.** Each round costs tokens; deep repairs on large programs can exceed context windows. Synthesizing *novel algorithms* (as opposed to completing sketches) remains beyond practical token budgets.
4. **Specification bottleneck.** CEGIS is only as good as its specification. Writing separation-logic specs is expert labor; the loop cannot verify what is not specified.
5. **Distribution shift.** The LLM prior reflects its training corpus. On niche domains (verified cryptography, novel data structures), the prior is weak and the architecture degrades gracefully to classical CEGIS — no worse, but no better either.
6. **SemGuS tooling immaturity.** SemGuS solvers and benchmarks [3] lag SyGuS by years, limiting rigorous comparison.

## 7. Conclusion

This thesis presented a **neuro-symbolic CEGIS architecture** placing an LLM inside the classical counterexample-guided inductive synthesis loop as a statistical candidate generator, disciplined at every step by an SMT-backed verifier. We proved *soundness by construction* and *relative completeness* w.r.t. the SyGuS grammar, showed how sketches [5] encode as SyGuS problems, how counterexamples become prompts, and how symbolic fault localization plus neural patch proposal yields a *repair* operator for heap-manipulating programs with separation-logic specifications.

Empirically, the architecture solves more SyGuS-Comp problems than classical CEGIS with 4.6× fewer rounds, and converts the LLM's 38%-unsound heap guesses into 47/60 certified programs. The broader lesson: **neural models propose, symbolic engines dispose**. As SemGuS [3] matures, neuro-symbolic loops will become the default backend for verified synthesis. The dream of sketching — programmers supplying insight, machines supplying rigor [5] — is finally executable at scale.
