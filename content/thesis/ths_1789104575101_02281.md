# Algebraic Effects and Effect Handlers: From Plotkin and Pretnar's Free-Model Semantics to the Languages Koka and Eff

## Abstract

Algebraic effects and effect handlers generalize exception handling from a hardcoded control construct to a programmable interpretation mechanism. Beginning with Plotkin and Power's identification of computational effects with the operations of an algebraic theory [5], and Plotkin and Pretnar's construction of handlers as models of that theory applied through the universal property of the free model [1], the framework unifies state, nondeterminism, coroutines, and probabilistic choice under one mechanism. This thesis develops the theory from first principles — the free-model monad, handling as homomorphism, deep versus shallow handlers — and studies two landmark realizations: Eff, the first language built around effects and handlers, with first-class handlers, instances, and regions [2]; and Koka, which embeds handlers in a row-polymorphic type-and-effect system with duplicate labels, Hindley–Milner inference, and type-directed evidence-passing compilation [3,4]. We prove adequacy of handling, survey empirical performance evidence, and identify the open problems — unchecked effect equations, one-shot versus multi-shot continuations, and linear resources — that delimit the current frontier.

## 1. Introduction

For most of the history of functional programming, computational effects — state, exceptions, nondeterminism, input/output — were treated as *intrusions* into an otherwise pure semantics. Moggi's proposal to model effects with monads gave semanticists a uniform vocabulary, but at a structural cost: each effect demanded its own monad, and combining two monads required a bespoke *monad transformer* [1,5]. The theory of algebraic effects inverts the question. Rather than asking *which monad* models an effect, it asks *which operations* produce it.

The move is decisive. An effect is identified with a signature of *operations* — `get` and `put` for state, `raise` for exceptions, `choose` for nondeterminism — together with an *equational theory* constraining them. The free model of this theory automatically induces the computational monad for the effect. Effect *handlers*, introduced by Plotkin and Pretnar [1], then supply the complementary half: a handler is a *model* of the theory, and the handling construct applies the unique *homomorphism* from the free model induced by the universal property. In one stroke, exception handling is generalized from a single hardcoded construct to a programmable mechanism: state, backtracking search, coroutines, and probabilistic choice all become instances of one uniform construction.

This thesis has three aims. *First*, a self-contained account of the algebraic theory: free models, generic effects, and the handling construct. *Second*, a study of two landmark realizations — **Eff**, prioritizing semantic fidelity with first-class handlers, instances, and regions [2], and **Koka**, prioritizing type inference, effect rows with duplicate labels, and compilation efficiency [3,4]. *Third*, an evaluation of the empirical evidence, and a precise statement of where the approach falls short.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 Monads and the composition problem

Moggi showed that a language with effects can be interpreted in a category equipped with a strong monad $T$: values live in $A$, computations in $TA$ [1]. Wadler popularized the idea, and Haskell adopted it wholesale. But each effect demands its own monad, and combining two monads requires a bespoke *monad transformer* — an enterprise notorious for brittleness: the state-transformer over the exception monad and the exception-transformer over the state monad are *different* monads, and some combinations admit no transformer at all [5]. Layering decisions become global and irreversible.

### 2.2 Algebraic theories of effects

Plotkin and Power's insight was that most computational effects are *algebraic* in a precise sense [5]: they are generated by operations satisfying equational laws. State supports $\mathsf{get} : 1 \to S$ and $\mathsf{put} : S \to 1$ with the familiar laws; exceptions support $\mathsf{raise}_e : 0 \to 1$ per $e \in \mathit{exc}$; nondeterminism supports binary $\mathsf{or}$ subject to the semilattice laws. Crucially, all these operations *commute with sequencing* in the same way: for an $n$-ary operation $\mathsf{op}$,

$$\mathsf{do}\; x \leftarrow \mathsf{op}(M_1, \ldots, M_n); N \;\equiv\; \mathsf{op}(\mathsf{do}\; x \leftarrow M_1; N, \ldots, \mathsf{do}\; x \leftarrow M_n; N).$$

Operations satisfying this law are *algebraic operations*; each corresponds bijectively to a *generic effect*, a morphism $1 \to Tn$ in the Kleisli category [5]. The computational monad is then recovered *for free* as the monad induced by the free-model construction.

> **Theorem (Plotkin–Power):** Giving an algebraic operation for a monad $T$ is equivalent to giving a generic effect $1 \to Tn$. The free model of an algebraic theory of effects induces a monad whose Kleisli category models the computational $\lambda$-calculus with those effects [5].

### 2.3 Handlers: the missing half

Plotkin and Pretnar supplied the missing half — introduced at ESOP 2009 [6] and developed in the journal version [1]: *handlers* — interpretations of each operation, i.e. *models* of the theory. The handling construct

$$M \;\mathbf{handled\;with}\; \{\mathsf{op}_i(x; k) \mapsto N_i\}_{i}, \; \mathbf{return}\; x \mapsto N_{\mathrm{ret}}$$

re-interprets a computation $M$ by replacing each operation call with its clause, where the continuation variable $k$ denotes the *rest of the computation* re-wrapped in the same handler. Denotationally, the clauses define a model $\mathcal{M}$, and handling is exactly the unique homomorphism $h : F(A) \to \mathcal{M}$ from the free model $F(A)$ extending the return clause [1].

Exception handling is the degenerate case: the exception monad $TA = A + \mathit{exc}$ is the free model of the theory with nullary operations $\mathsf{raise}_e$, and the construct is the induced homomorphism [1]. But the same machinery yields *collecting all nondeterministic results*, *rollback*, *stream redirection*, *timeout*, and CCS relabelling — "previously unrelated examples from both theory and practice" unified by one construct [1].

## 3. Methodology

We (i) reconstruct the core calculus $\lambda_{\mathit{eff}}$ following the fine-grained call-by-value presentation of Kammar, Lindley, and Oury [7]; (ii) state adequacy of handling with respect to free-model semantics; (iii) contrast Eff's *first-class handlers with instances and regions* [2] against Koka's *row-polymorphic effect types with duplicate labels and type-directed compilation* [3,4]; and (iv) evaluate empirical evidence on handler performance, including evidence-passing compilation [4,8].

### 3.1 The core calculus

We work in fine-grained call-by-value: values $V$ and computations $M$ are syntactically distinguished, and the typing judgement carries an *effect context* $E$, as in $\Gamma \vdash_E M : A$ [7]. A handler value $H$ has type $A^E \Rightarrow C^{E'}$. Operationally, when an operation reaches a handler with a matching clause, the clause fires with the continuation reified:

$$\mathbf{handle}\; \mathcal{E}[\mathsf{op}(V; y.M)] \;\mathbf{with}\; H \;\longrightarrow\; N_i[V/x, \; \lambda y.\, \mathbf{handle}\; \mathcal{E}[M] \;\mathbf{with}\; H / k]$$

where $\mathcal{E}$ is an evaluation context free of intervening handlers for $\mathsf{op}$. The continuation $\lambda y.\, \mathbf{handle}\; \mathcal{E}[M] \;\mathbf{with}\; H$ is the *deep-handler* semantics: the continuation is itself wrapped in the handler, so subsequent operations are handled recursively. A *shallow* handler instead reifies $\lambda y.\, \mathcal{E}[M]$ — the continuation escapes handling, and further operations propagate outward [8].

### 3.2 Deep versus shallow handlers

The deep/shallow distinction is semantically load-bearing. Deep handlers correspond exactly to the homomorphism reading: the handler denotes a model $\mathcal{M}$, and the wrapped continuation is the homomorphism applied recursively. Shallow handlers correspond to a *case analysis* on the first operation only, and can encode deep handlers but not vice versa without general recursion [8]. Koka defaults to deep handlers [3]; Eff's handlers are deep, with effect instances providing a complementary mechanism for delimiting handler scope [2].

> **Theorem (Adequacy of handling):** For the fine-grained calculus with deep handlers, the operational handling reduction is sound and adequate with respect to the denotational semantics in free models: $\mathbf{handle}\;M\;\mathbf{with}\;H$ denotes $h(\llbracket M \rrbracket)$, where $h$ is the unique homomorphism from the free model extending the return clause [1,7].

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Handlers as universal homomorphisms

Fix an algebraic theory $\mathbb{T}$ with operations $\mathsf{op}_i$ of arities $n_i$. A *model* of $\mathbb{T}$ is a set $|\mathcal{M}|$ with, for each $\mathsf{op}_i$, a function $|\mathcal{M}|^{n_i} \to |\mathcal{M}|$ satisfying the equations. The *free model* $F(X)$ on $X$ comes with $\eta_X : X \to F(X)$ such that any $f : X \to |\mathcal{M}|$ extends to a *unique homomorphism* $\bar{f} : F(X) \to \mathcal{M}$.

A handler is, literally, the data of such a model plus such a map: the **return clause** is the map $f : A \to |\mathcal{M}|$; each **operation clause** defines the model's interpretation of that operation, with the continuation parameter $k$ standing for the homomorphism-images of the subcomputations; and the **handling construct** is $\bar{f}$, the unique homomorphism.

This explains handler composition, deep re-wrapping of continuations, and propagation of unhandled operations: a homomorphism out of the free model is determined by its action on generators and operations, and operations not mentioned in the handler remain free, to be handled further out. Programmers manipulate operations; the universal property guarantees coherence.

In Haskell, the free-model monad is directly programmable as the *free monad* over an operation functor, and handlers are *folds* — the fold *is* the unique homomorphism:

```haskell
-- Operations as a functor; the free monad is the free model
data EffF k = Get (Int -> k) | Put Int k

-- A handler is a fold: an algebra over the functor (a model)
handleState :: Free EffF a -> Int -> (a, Int)
handleState (Pure x)       s = (x, s)
handleState (Free (Get k)) s = handleState (k s) s
handleState (Free (Put s' k)) _ = handleState k s'
```

Every handler in every language in this thesis is this fold, dressed in different syntax.

### 4.2 Type-and-effect systems: Eff's dirt versus Koka's rows

If §4.1 is the semantics, the type system is where languages diverge. Both Eff and Koka track effects statically, but their treatments differ in expressiveness, inference, and ergonomics.

**Eff: types with dirt.** Eff computations have types $A \mathbin{!} \Delta$, where the *dirt* $\Delta$ is a set of operations the computation may perform [2]. Handlers are first-class values of type $A \mathbin{!} \Delta \Rightarrow C \mathbin{!} \Delta'$. Because handlers are ordinary values, they can be *parameterized by effect instances*: `new` creates a fresh instance $r$ of an effect, operations are invoked *at* an instance ($\#r.\mathsf{op}$), and a region discipline ensures a handled instance never escapes its handler [2]. The cost is explicitness: dirt is a set, and effect polymorphism must be plumbed through quantified dirt variables.

```ocaml
(* Eff: instances make state first-class and region-safe *)
let r = new ref in          (* fresh instance of the state effect *)
let s =
  handle
    let x = !#r in          (* lookup at instance r *)
    #r := x + 1;            (* update at instance r *)
    x
  with
  | val x -> (fun s -> (x, s))
  | #r lookup () k -> (fun s -> k s s)
  | #r update s' k -> (fun s -> k () s')
  | finally f -> f 0
```

**Koka: row-polymorphic effects with duplicate labels.** Koka annotates arrows with *effect rows*: `() -> <exn, div | μ> int` may raise exceptions and divide by zero, and is polymorphic in the remaining effects `μ` [3]. The breakthrough is permitting *duplicate labels*: $\langle \mathsf{exn}, \mathsf{exn} \rangle \not\equiv \langle \mathsf{exn} \rangle$. With set-based rows, unifying $\langle \mathsf{exn} \mid \mu_1 \rangle \sim \langle \mathsf{exn} \rangle$ admits two solutions ($\mu_1 = \langle\rangle$ or $\langle \mathsf{exn} \rangle$) — Wand's ambiguity, previously addressed with lacks-constraints or presence/absence flags [3]. With duplicate labels, $\mu_1 = \langle\rangle$ is the *unique* solution, and effect elimination gets principal types with plain Hindley–Milner inference:

$$\mathsf{catch} : \forall \alpha \mu.\; (() \to \langle \mathsf{exn} \mid \mu \rangle\, \alpha,\; \mathit{exception} \to \mu\, \alpha) \to \mu\, \alpha.$$

Koka further connects rows to semantics: if an expression types *without* the `exn` effect, it provably never raises an unhandled exception, and state can be encapsulated à la Haskell's `runST`, combining safely with let-polymorphism with no value restriction [3].

| Feature | Eff | Koka |
|---|---|---|
| Effect annotation | Dirt sets $A \mathbin{!} \Delta$ | Rows $\langle l_1, \dots \mid \mu \rangle$ |
| Effect polymorphism | Explicit dirt variables | Row polymorphism, inferred |
| Duplicate labels | N/A (sets) | Yes — principal unification |
| Handlers | First-class values | `handle`/`handler` expressions, deep by default |
| Instances/regions | Yes — fresh instances, region discipline | Named effects; `inject`/`mask` for scoping |
| Evaluation | Direct-style interpreter | Type-directed compilation to C |

The design tension is real: Eff's instances give *dynamic* generativity (fresh effects at runtime), which Koka's static rows cannot express; Koka's rows give *static* precision and inference, which Eff's dirt sets approximate only with annotation burden. Neither subsumes the other — a fact that has driven hybrids such as Frank's abilities [9] and Effekt's capability-passing [10].

### 4.3 Multi-shot continuations, instances, and control

The continuation variable $k$ is the locus of the framework's expressive power — and its implementation difficulty. In the collecting handler for nondeterminism, $k$ is invoked *twice*; in a coroutine scheduler, $k$ is *captured and resumed later*. Continuations invoked at most once are *one-shot*; those invoked zero or multiple times are *multi-shot*.

Multi-shot continuations are semantically unproblematic — the free-model homomorphism applies $h$ to each branch independently — but operationally they demand copying the evaluation context or restricting the language to one-shot resumption. Multicore OCaml's effect handlers deliberately restrict continuations to one-shot, gaining a fiber-switching compilation strategy with no copying, at the cost of rejecting double resumption dynamically [11]. Koka permits multi-shot continuations; its runtime copies stack segments, and recent work on scoped effects refines which continuations may escape [4,8].

Eff's *instances* interact subtly with multi-shot continuations. Because an operation is tied to the instance at which it is invoked, resuming a captured continuation under a different handler scope is well-defined: the operation's identity travels with the instance value, not with the dynamic handler stack. This is what makes the region system sound: a region ties each instance's lifetime to its handler, and the type system rejects programs in which a captured continuation would outlive its instance's region [2].

> **Theorem (Region safety, Bauer–Pretnar):** In Eff's region-annotated calculus, a well-typed program never invokes an operation at an instance whose handler region has been deallocated. Instance identity is preserved across multi-shot resumption [2].

### 4.4 Compilation: from handlers to machine code

In compilation, Koka made the decisive advance. Leijen's *type-directed compilation of row-typed algebraic effects* (POPL 2017) [4] translates handlers using *evidence passing*: the effect row of a function determines a vector of *evidence* — runtime records witnessing which handler interprets each effect label — passed alongside ordinary arguments. Because rows are static, evidence vectors have statically known layout; evidence selection is a constant-time projection. Handlers compile to evidence *construction*; operation invocation compiles to evidence *lookup* plus a call through the handler's operation table. The translation is proved correct, and it eliminates the overhead of naive closure-based implementations.

Eff, by contrast, was conceived as a *semantic laboratory*: its reference implementation is a direct-style interpreter in OCaml, prioritizing fidelity to the theory (first-class handlers, instances, regions) over speed [2]. The ideas have since been carried into production runtimes, notably OCaml 5's `Effect` module [11].

In Rust, the same fold-over-operations pattern is directly programmable:

```rust
// Rust: a handler as a fold over an operation stream
enum Op { Get, Put(i32) }

fn handle_state<F>(prog: Vec<Op>, mut f: F) -> i32
where F: FnMut(i32) -> i32
{
    let mut s = 0;
    for op in prog {
        match op {
            Op::Get    => { /* pass s to continuation */ }
            Op::Put(s2) => { s = s2; }
        }
    }
    f(s)   // return clause
}
```

In TLA+, the handler-as-model reading becomes a state-machine refinement — the handling construct is a *refinement mapping* from the free specification to the interpreted one:

```tla+
---- MODULE HandlerModel ----
EXTENDS Naturals
VARIABLES prog, acc              \* prog: free-model term; acc: handler state
Handle(op) ==                    \* operation clause = model's interpretation
  CASE op = "choose" -> acc' \in {0,1}
    [] op = "tick"   -> acc' = acc + 1
    [] OTHER         -> UNCHANGED acc
Init == prog \in Seq({"choose","tick"}) /\ acc = 0
Next == prog # <<>> /\ Handle(Head(prog)) /\ prog' = Tail(prog)
====
```

## 5. Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 Metatheoretic results

The metatheory of handlers is now mature. Kammar, Lindley, and Oury established type safety for $\lambda_{\mathit{eff}}$ [7]; Bauer and Pretnar proved type safety for Eff with instances and regions, including the region-safety theorem of §4.3 [2]. A *modular* account — handlers for *scoped* operations like `catch` or `local`, which delimit a scope in which sub-computations run — was developed by Piróg, Schrijvers, and others, extending the algebraic framework beyond the algebraicity law of §2.2 [12].

> **Theorem (Type soundness, Kammar–Lindley–Oury):** If $\Gamma \vdash_E M : A$ then $M$ either diverges, reduces to $\mathbf{return}\;V$ with $\Gamma \vdash V : A$, or reduces to $\mathcal{E}[\mathsf{op}(V; y.N)]$ with $\mathsf{op} \in E$. Handled programs with empty effect context never get stuck on unhandled operations [7].

### 5.2 Performance evidence

Handler performance hinges on three findings. *First*, naive implementations — heap-allocated closures with continuation capture via stack copying — incur order-of-magnitude slowdowns on handler-intensive benchmarks (backtracking `n-queens`, coroutine schedulers) [8]. *Second*, type-directed compilation closes most of the gap: Koka's evidence-passing translation compiles the standard benchmarks to C competitive with hand-optimized code, since evidence vectors are statically laid out and dispatch is a direct table call [4]. *Third*, one-shot restrictions buy further speed: Multicore OCaml's fiber-based one-shot continuations cost essentially a function call plus a stack switch — which is why OCaml 5 could adopt effect handlers as a production concurrency primitive [11].

Quantitatively, evidence-passing compilation reduces handler dispatch overhead to small constant factors over direct-style code on tight loops, while multi-shot-capable implementations pay a copying cost linear in the captured stack segment [4,8]. The qualitative lesson is stable: *handlers are as fast as their compilation strategy*, and the strategy is determined by which semantic commitments — multi-shot resumption, dynamic instances, deep wrapping — the language retains.

### 5.3 Adoption as evidence

Effect handlers now underpin OCaml 5's concurrency (via one-shot handlers in the `Effect` module) [11], Koka's standard library (where *all* control effects are user-defined handlers) [3], Effekt's capability-passing handlers [10], and Haskell libraries (`fused-effects`, `polysemy`, `effectful`) that retrofit handlers onto a language previously committed to monad transformers. The migration of a production runtime to handlers is strong evidence that the algebraic account is not merely elegant but *engineering-viable*.

## 6. Limitations

**Effect equations are unchecked.** The algebraic theory includes *equations* (associativity of choice, the state laws), but no mainstream language checks that a handler's clauses *satisfy* them. A handler is a purported model; nothing verifies the purport. A "state" handler whose `put` clause ignores its argument silently violates the theory, and equivalences depending on the equations become unsound. Enforcing equations statically remains open [12].

**One-shot versus multi-shot is a semantic cliff.** Multicore OCaml's one-shot restriction is dynamically enforced: resuming twice raises at runtime [11]. A *semantic* distinction becomes a *runtime failure mode*, and programs that are perfectly meaningful denotationally — the free-model homomorphism handles multi-shot resumption without complaint — are rejected operationally. Koka pays the copying cost instead. The choice between expressiveness and performance leaks into the programming model.

**Effect pollution and handler inference.** Row polymorphism mitigates but does not eliminate *effect pollution*: a higher-order function that merely *passes through* effects must still be polymorphic over them, and inferred rows in large programs become long and unreadable. Koka's `mask`/`inject` combinators help, but the ergonomics of effect variables at scale remain inferior to unchecked exceptions [3,4].

**Interaction with linearity and resources.** Handlers and linear resources (file handles, session types) interact badly: a multi-shot continuation that closes a file twice, or a handler that discards a continuation holding a linear resource, breaks resource discipline. Linear handlers — continuations used *exactly once* by typing — are an active research area, but no production language integrates them [12].

**Scoped operations.** Many practical effects — exception catching with scoped recovery, `local` state, parallel composition — are *scoped*: they delimit a region in which sub-computations execute. Scoped operations violate the algebraicity law of §2.2 and need the generalized framework of [12]: the universal-property story of §4.1 extends, but with considerably more categorical machinery.

## 7. Conclusion

From Plotkin and Power's algebraic operations to Plotkin and Pretnar's handlers-as-homomorphisms, from Eff's first-class handlers with instances and regions to Koka's row-polymorphic effects with duplicate labels and evidence-passing compilation, the trajectory of algebraic effects shows how a clean semantic idea becomes an engineering discipline. The core insight has survived contact with implementation intact: *operations are syntax, handlers are models, handling is the unique homomorphism*. What changed is our understanding of the *price* of that insight — in type-system complexity, compilation strategy, and the one-shot/multi-shot divide — and of the machinery (rows, evidence, regions, instances) needed to pay it.

The open problems are now precisely statable: statically checked effect equations, linear handlers for resource discipline, ergonomic effect polymorphism at scale, and a unified account of scoped operations. Each is a research program in its own right. But the direction of travel is settled. Monads taught us to *name* effects; algebraic effects and handlers teach us to *define* them — and then to *redefine* them, at will, by supplying a new model.
