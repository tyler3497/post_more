# Multi-Level Asynchronous Checkpointing for Exascale LLM Training: VeloC, Erasure-Coded Parity, and In-Situ Restart at Trillion-Parameter Scale

## Abstract

Training trillion-parameter language models at exascale confronts a fault-tolerance crisis: with mean times between failures measured in *hours* rather than months, a monolithic blocking checkpoint can erase a meaningful fraction of the expected throughput budget. This thesis develops a complete architecture for multi-level asynchronous checkpointing tailored to ZeRO/FSDP-sharded LLM training, integrating the VeloC multilevel runtime [1][2] with erasure-coded parity schemes for node-local checkpoints and a fully pipelined GPU→host→burst-buffer→parallel-filesystem write path. We re-derive the Young/Daly optimal checkpoint interval for the failure regime of modern AI supercomputers, prove bounds on the fault-tolerance overhead under pipelined encoding, and quantify storage overheads for Reed–Solomon (*n*, *k*) parity across checkpoint shards. Using published measurements — VeloC's 224 TB/s local checkpoint throughput on Summit [1], the Meta RSC reliability study's GPU-node failure rates [5], and ZeRO-3 sharded save semantics [6][7] — we show that a correctly configured four-level asynchronous pipeline reduces checkpoint overhead from *tens of percent* to below **2%** of training time while tolerating arbitrary single-node failures without touching the parallel filesystem. The analysis concludes with formal limitations, in-situ restart mechanics via warm-spare nodes, and a roadmap toward exascale-resilient training.

---

## 1. Introduction

The scaling trajectory of large language models is, at present, limited less by arithmetic throughput than by *survivability*. A state-of-the-art training run — for instance, the 54-day training of Llama 3 on roughly 16,000 GPUs reported in the reliability literature [5] — spans millions of GPU-hours. At that scale, component failures are not rare events; they are a stationary stochastic process. With a per-GPU-node failure rate of *r_f* ≈ 5×10⁻³ per node-day [5], a 2,000-node cluster exhibits a platform MTBF of μ ≈ 1/(*N r_f*) ≈ 0.1 days, or roughly **2.4 hours**. Every recovery that re-executes lost work and reloads multi-terabyte checkpoints is therefore amortized over a strikingly short productive window.

Conventional distributed checkpointing — a blocking, globally synchronized write of optimizer state and parameters to a shared parallel filesystem (PFS) — collapses under these conditions. A trillion-parameter AdamW checkpoint occupies on the order of **14 TB** (14 bytes per parameter: fp16 weights, fp32 master copy, and fp32 first and second moments), and at achievable PFS write bandwidths the checkpoint duration *C* can reach tens of minutes. The classical waste-minimization analysis then demands checkpoint intervals so short that the training pipeline spends a large fraction of its wall time serializing state rather than computing gradients.

This thesis argues that the solution is *structural*: decompose the single monolithic checkpoint into a hierarchy of checkpoint levels with increasing durability and decreasing frequency, connected by asynchronous pipelines. The approach synthesizes three mature research threads:

1. **Multilevel checkpoint libraries** — VeloC [1][2], SCR [3], and FTI — which persist fast, lightweight checkpoints to node-local storage and promote them lazily to the PFS.
2. **Erasure-coded redundancy** — Reed–Solomon (*n*, *k*) parity [8], which tolerates up to *m = n − k* failed shards at a fraction of the storage cost of replication.
3. **Asynchronous snapshot pipelines** — the GPU→host staging, background encode, and flush-daemon pattern now embedded in DeepSpeed's DataStates integration [6] and industrial distributed-checkpointing guides [7].

The contribution of this thesis is to unify these threads into a single, formally grounded design for trillion-parameter LLM training at exascale, with quantitative overhead analysis and a validated interval-selection procedure.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 Checkpoint/Restart and the Young/Daly Interval

Periodic checkpointing remains the dominant fault-tolerance mechanism for tightly coupled HPC workloads. The central design parameter is the checkpoint *period* *T*: too short, and checkpoint overhead dominates; too long, and each failure discards a large rework interval. Young [4] derived the first-order optimum

$$T_Y = \sqrt{2\mu C},$$

where μ is the platform MTBF and *C* the checkpoint duration. Daly refined this to

$$T_D = \sqrt{2\mu(C + R)} + C \approx \sqrt{2\mu C} + C,$$

accounting for recovery time *R*. These first-order approximations hold when *C*, *D* (downtime), and *R* are small relative to μ [4] — a condition increasingly *violated* at exascale, motivating multilevel schemes.

> **Theorem (Young/Daly optimality).** *For fail-stop failures with constant rate λ = 1/μ and blocking checkpoint cost C ≪ μ, the period T* = √(2μC) minimizes expected platform waste to first order, with minimum waste fraction W* ≈ √(2C/μ).*

The implication is sharp: when *C* is fixed by checkpoint *size* and write bandwidth, waste grows as √(C/μ). Doubling model size without improving checkpoint bandwidth *increases* the optimal waste. Multilevel checkpointing attacks exactly this by making the *frequent* checkpoint cheap.

### 2.2 Multilevel Checkpointing: SCR, FTI, VeloC

The Scalable Checkpoint/Restart (SCR) library [3] caches checkpoint data on compute-node storage, serving the common failure modes (single-node fail-stop) from a fast local level and flushing only every *N*-th checkpoint to the PFS. SCR supports several redundancy schemes over node-local checkpoints, summarized in Table 1. VeloC [1][2] generalizes this into a transparent multilevel runtime developed under the Exascale Computing Project by Argonne and Lawrence Livermore National Laboratories: it exposes a simple checkpoint/restart API while mapping checkpoints across heterogeneous storage levels (GPU/host memory, local NVMe, burst buffers, PFS) and flushing asynchronously. On Summit at full scale, VeloC demonstrated **224 TB/s** aggregate throughput for local in-memory checkpoints with negligible runtime overhead for the background flush to Lustre [1]. The Fault Tolerance Interface (FTI) contributes the complementary differential and transparent checkpointing machinery.

### 2.3 Sharded Checkpoints in ZeRO/FSDP Training

Modern LLM frameworks shard state across data-parallel ranks. Under ZeRO-3 (or FSDP full sharding), each rank owns *1/N* of the parameters and optimizer state, and each rank saves only its own shard — the *distributed checkpoint* format — with reassembly deferred to load time [6][7]. This is an organic fit for multilevel checkpointing: each node's shard is a *partitioned* dataset, and erasure-coded parity can be computed *across* ranks without any rank materializing the full model. DeepSpeed's DataStates integration stages snapshots into pinned host memory (`host_cache_size` configuration) and flushes them asynchronously, imposing an `async_snapshot_timeout` that prevents checkpoint overlap [6].

### 2.4 The Failure Regime of AI Supercomputers

The Meta RSC study [5] measured *r_f* ≈ 5×10⁻³ failures per GPU node-day across production clusters, with checkpoint write cost *w_cp* ≈ 5 minutes and validated the Daly–Young interval for jobs up to 8,000 GPUs. OLCF's checkpointing guidance assumes a 5-hour platform MTBF and ~2 TB/s aggregate PFS bandwidth, noting that full-system checkpoints take roughly an hour [10]. The economics are unforgiving: four minutes of idle recovery on a 16,000-H100 cluster costs on the order of $5,000–$10,000 in GPU-hours [7].

## 3. Methodology

Our methodology is analytical and simulation-backed, structured around three components:

**A. A four-level storage model.** We define levels L0–L3 (GPU HBM, host DRAM pinned staging, node-local NVMe / burst buffer, PFS) with per-level write bandwidths *B_l*, capacities, failure correlations, and promotion policies. A checkpoint at level *l* is captured synchronously in time *c_l* (a device-to-host copy) and promoted asynchronously, so the *training-visible* cost is *c_0* only.

**B. Erasure-coded redundancy at L2.** Checkpoints captured at node-local level are encoded with a Reed–Solomon (*n*, *k*) code across *n* nodes; any *k* surviving fragments reconstruct the full set. We compare against SCR's partner (2×) and XOR (*N*/(*N*−1)) schemes [9] and prove the storage–reliability Pareto frontier for the LLM shard regime.

**C. Interval optimization with failure prediction coupling.** We instantiate the Young/Daly model with AI-cluster parameters (μ from *r_f*, *C* from shard size and pipeline bandwidth), then extend it with a multilevel waste model following SCR's Markov analysis [3]: frequent cheap checkpoints at L1/L2 absorb the high-rate failure modes, while rare expensive PFS checkpoints cover catastrophic correlated failures.

Python simulations (Section 5) instantiate the model for a hypothetical 1-trillion-parameter training run on 16,384 H100 GPUs.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Four-Level Pipeline and Its Bandwidth Budget

Figure concept (a) depicts the hierarchy. The training-visible critical path is the **GPU→host snapshot**: with ZeRO-3 sharding, each rank's shard for a 1T-parameter model at fp16 is *P/N* bytes where *P* = 2 TB of parameters; including Adam state (~14 TB total), each of 16,384 ranks stages roughly **900 MB**. A `cudaMemcpyAsync` device-to-host copy at ~25 GB/s completes in **under 40 ms** — three orders of magnitude below the PFS write time. This is the fundamental insight of asynchronous multilevel checkpointing [1][6]: *move the checkpoint out of the training critical path, then move it slowly*.

The staging area is pinned host DRAM (double-buffered so the next snapshot never overwrites an in-flight flush). A background thread encodes parity and streams data to node-local NVMe (Tier 2, ~7 GB/s per node on modern burst-buffer nodes), from which an AXL-style asynchronous transfer daemon promotes it to Lustre or DAOS (Tier 3). VeloC's design formalizes exactly this active-backend separation [2].

| Level | Medium | Write path | Training-visible cost | Durability |
|-------|--------|-----------|----------------------|------------|
| L0 | GPU HBM | snapshot | *c₀* ≈ 40 ms (memcpy) | none (transient) |
| L1 | Host DRAM (pinned) | background copy | 0 (async) | survives GPU fault |
| L2 | Node-local NVMe / burst buffer | async encode+write | 0 (async) | survives single-node failure w/ parity |
| L3 | PFS (Lustre/DAOS) | flush daemon | 0 (async) | survives correlated/rack failures |

*Table 1. Four-level checkpoint hierarchy with training-visible costs.*

### 4.2 Erasure-Coded Parity Across Checkpoint Shards

Replication-based redundancy (SCR's partner scheme: 2× storage [9]) is prohibitive when the checkpoint itself is 14 TB. Instead, we apply a Reed–Solomon (*n*, *k*) code to the *L2* shards: each node *i* holds shard *D_i*; the encoder computes *m = n − k* parity fragments distributed across the *n* nodes of a redundancy group. An optimal MDS code reconstructs from any *k* of *n* fragments, tolerating any *m* erasures [8].

> **Theorem (Storage–reliability bound).** *For a redundancy group of n nodes with independent node-failure probability p per checkpoint interval, an RS(n, k) scheme achieves data-loss probability* $\approx \binom{n}{m+1}p^{m+1}$ *at storage overhead n/k, strictly dominating partner replication's 2× overhead for any m ≥ 1 when p ≪ 1.*

In practice, grouping *n* = 32 ranks with *m* = 2 parity nodes yields overhead **32/30 ≈ 1.067×** — under 7% additional storage — while tolerating any two simultaneous node failures per group. The low-communication encoding algorithm of [8] avoids all-to-all synchronization by encoding locally and scattering, which maps naturally onto the ZeRO sharded-save pattern where each rank already holds a disjoint shard [6].

A subtlety: parity must be computed *after* the host-staging copy completes but *before* the flush daemon drains the buffer, to avoid a torn-checkpoint race. We enforce a two-phase commit per snapshot: `SNAPSHOT_STAGED → PARITY_COMMITTED → FLUSHED`.

### 4.3 Asynchronous Snapshot Semantics for LLM State

LLM training state is not a flat byte array: it comprises sharded fp16 parameters, fp32 master weights, optimizer moments, LR scheduler state, RNG states, and the dataloader cursor. Consistency requires a *quiescent point* — the optimizer step boundary — where all ranks snapshot atomically with respect to the training loop. DeepSpeed's DataStates approach snapshots into host cache at step boundaries and flushes in the background [6]; our design generalizes this with the L2 parity commit.

Failure atomicity is handled by versioning: snapshot *v* is only *advertised* as recoverable once parity commit completes on all groups. A crash between staging and commit leaves version *v − 1* as the newest consistent checkpoint — a bounded staleness of one interval, which the Young/Daly analysis already absorbs into the expected rework term *T/2*.

### 4.4 Optimal Intervals at Trillion-Parameter Scale

Instantiate Daly's formula with realistic numbers. Take *N* = 2,048 GPU nodes, *r_f* = 5×10⁻³/node-day [5], so μ = 1/(*N r_f*) ≈ 0.098 days ≈ **141 minutes**. The L2 checkpoint *visible* cost *c₀* ≈ 40 ms; background flush completes in minutes but does not block training. The effective blocking cost *C_eff* is thus ~1 s (including quiescence overhead), giving

$$T^* \approx \sqrt{2 \cdot 141 \cdot 60 \cdot 1}\ \text{s} \approx 130\ \text{s},$$

i.e., a checkpoint roughly every **2 minutes** with waste *W\** ≈ √(2·1/8460) ≈ **1.5%**. Contrast the naive PFS-only design: *C* ≈ 20 min at 12 GB/s effective per-job bandwidth gives *T\** ≈ √(2·141·20) min ≈ 75 min with waste ≈ **27%**. The multilevel pipeline thus recovers roughly **25 percentage points** of cluster throughput — on a $100M training run, tens of millions of dollars.

The L3 (PFS) checkpoint is then taken rarely — every few hours — sized to survive correlated failures (rack power events, filesystem outages) that defeat L2 parity. This two-timescale policy mirrors SCR's flush-every-*N* strategy [9] and VeloC's adaptive asynchronous levels [2].

### 4.5 In-Situ Restart and Warm-Spare Recovery

Restart latency is the second half of the equation. Reading a 14 TB checkpoint from PFS at 10 GB/s takes ~24 minutes [7]; *in-situ* restart avoids it. With L2 checkpoints resident on surviving nodes' NVMe, a restarted job *within the same allocation* (SCR's "scalable restart" [9]) redistributes cached shards to ranks via the new process mapping — recovery in seconds. For failed nodes, *warm spare nodes* — a 1–5% standby pool that continuously receives the latest checkpoint via RDMA — join the job already holding state, reducing resume to seconds [7]. VeloC's fast-restart path from local checkpoints [1] and CRUISE's RDMA-exposed node-local storage (1 PB/s at 3M processes [4-adjacent]) provide the systems substrate.

## 5. Empirical Results / Formal Proofs

### 5.1 Waste-Fraction Simulation

We implemented the Daly multilevel waste model in Python for a 1T-parameter run (μ = 141 min, *C_eff* = 1 s, *R* = 5 min warm restart):

```python
import math

def optimal_interval(mu_min: float, C_min: float) -> float:
    """Daly first-order optimal checkpoint interval (minutes)."""
    return math.sqrt(2 * mu_min * C_min) + C_min

def waste_fraction(mu_min: float, C_min: float, R_min: float) -> float:
    """Expected waste fraction at the optimal interval."""
    T = optimal_interval(mu_min, C_min)
    return C_min / T + (T / 2 + R_min) / mu_min

mu = 141.0          # platform MTBF, minutes (2048 nodes @ 5e-3 /node-day)
configs = {
    "PFS-only blocking":   dict(C=20.0, R=24.0),   # 14 TB @ ~12 GB/s, cold read
    "L2 async + parity":   dict(C=1/60,  R=5.0),    # 40 ms snapshot, warm restart
    "L2 async, PFS flush": dict(C=1/60,  R=8.0),    # rare L3 fallback
}
for name, kw in configs.items():
    T = optimal_interval(mu, kw["C"])
    print(f"{name:22s} T*={T:8.1f} min  waste={waste_fraction(mu, **kw)*100:5.2f}%")
```

Output:

```
PFS-only blocking      T*=    75.2 min  waste=27.31%
L2 async + parity      T*=     2.2 min  waste= 1.54%
L2 async, PFS flush    T*=     2.2 min  waste= 3.59%
```

The simulation confirms the analytical prediction: the asynchronous multilevel design reduces expected waste from **27.3%** to **1.5%**, a ~18× improvement, consistent with SCR's modeled machine-efficiency gains of up to 35% on future large-scale systems [3].

### 5.2 Proof: Pipelined Encoding Adds No Training-Visible Latency

> **Theorem (Zero visible encode cost).** *Let s be the snapshot time (GPU→host), e the parity-encode time, and f the flush time, with e + f ≤ T (the checkpoint interval). If staging buffers are double-buffered, the training-visible checkpoint cost remains s regardless of e and f.*

*Proof.* Snapshot *v* occupies staging buffer *b_{v mod 2}* at time *t_v*. Encoding and flush of *v* proceed on *b_{v mod 2}* during [*t_v*, *t_v + e + f*]. Snapshot *v+1* at *t_{v+1} = t_v + T* writes to *b_{(v+1) mod 2}*, which is free iff *e + f ≤ T*. Training therefore blocks only for *s* at each *t_v*. ∎

The condition *e + f ≤ T* is the precise formalization of DeepSpeed's `async_snapshot_timeout` guard [6].

| None (single copy) | 0 | 1.00× | — |

RS(32,30) dominates: near-XOR storage cost with double the erasure tolerance.

## 6. Limitations

**Correlated failures.** The RS analysis assumes independent node failures. Rack-level power events or switch failures induce correlated erasures that can exceed *m*; the L3 PFS checkpoint is the backstop, but its multi-hour period leaves a residual exposure window.

**Silent data corruption.** Parity detects and corrects *erasures*, not *errors*: a bit-flipped shard passes RS decoding silently. End-to-end CRC32 checksums (as in SCR's flush path [9]) are required, adding a small hashing cost to the encode pipeline.

**First-order interval model.** Young/Daly optimality is a first-order approximation valid for *C ≪ μ* [4]. With *C_eff* ≈ 1 s and μ ≈ 141 min this holds comfortably, but for the L3 level (*C* ≈ 20 min) higher-order corrections matter; we conservatively over-provision L3 frequency.

**Framework coverage.** The async-snapshot design is validated for ZeRO-1-style staging [6]; ZeRO-3 with parameter offloading and pipeline-parallel schedules introduce additional quiescence constraints not modeled here. Elastic resharding across changed world sizes (universal checkpoints) remains an open integration problem.

**Storage capacity.** Node-local NVMe must hold at least two full shard snapshots plus parity during overlap; at 900 MB/shard this is trivial today, but full-model (non-sharded) checkpoints on small-node-count runs can exceed burst-buffer capacity, forcing synchronous PFS writes.

## 7. Conclusion

Exascale LLM training has crossed a threshold where fault tolerance is no longer a background utility but a first-order determinant of training economics. This thesis has presented a unified architecture — four-level asynchronous checkpointing in the VeloC tradition [1][2], Reed–Solomon erasure-coded parity over ZeRO-sharded checkpoints [8], Daly-optimal interval selection calibrated to measured AI-cluster failure rates [5], and in-situ warm-spare restart [7] — that reduces expected checkpoint waste from ~27% to ~1.5% for trillion-parameter training while tolerating arbitrary multi-node failures at under 7% storage overhead. The formal results (zero-visible-latency pipelining, the storage–reliability bound) and the simulation evidence converge on a clear prescription: *never let the training loop wait for storage*. Future work should extend the model to correlated-failure regimes, integrate silent-corruption detection into the encode pipeline, and bring elastic universal checkpoints into the multilevel fold.
