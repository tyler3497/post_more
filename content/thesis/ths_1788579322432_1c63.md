# Read-Copy-Update and Lock-Free Memory Reclamation: Grace Periods, Quiescent States, and the Formal Verification of Deferred Destruction

## Abstract
Safe memory reclamation is the central obstruction to practical lock-free programming: a thread that unlinks a node cannot free it while others may still hold transient references. This thesis unifies the principal solutions — Read-Copy-Update (RCU), hazard pointers, epoch-based reclamation (EBR), and quiescent-state-based reclamation (QSBR) — around the operational semantics of *grace periods* and *quiescent states*. We formalize the grace-period safety invariant, derive the memory-ordering obligations the Linux Kernel Memory Model imposes on publish-subscribe primitives, and analyze Tree RCU as a case study in hierarchical grace-period detection. We contrast explicit protection (hazard pointers: bounded memory, per-dereference fences) with implicit schemes (EBR/QSBR: amortized-constant reclamation, global epochs), survey formal verification from Gotsman et al.'s Grace logic to model checking of production Tree RCU, and quantify the many-core gap between RCU read paths and reader-writer locking. Deferred destruction, we argue, is a single abstraction — the grace period — with a family of detection mechanisms trading throughput, latency, memory bound, and provability.

## 1 Introduction

The memory-management problem for lock-free data structures is famously harder than the data structures themselves. In a lock-based program, mutual exclusion serializes access, so an object can be freed the moment the lock protecting it is released for the last time. In a lock-free program, no such serialization exists: a thread traversing a linked list may be descheduled indefinitely at any instant, holding a raw pointer into a node that another thread has concurrently unlinked. Freeing the node then is a use-after-free; pointer reuse can additionally resurrect logically dead nodes into live comparisons, the *ABA-adjacent reclamation hazard* [5].

Three families of solutions dominate both research and production. **Hazard pointers** [5] make protection explicit: each thread publishes the addresses it is about to dereference in globally visible slots, and a retiring thread scans all slots before freeing. **Epoch-based reclamation (EBR)** and its read-heavy specialization **QSBR** make protection implicit: readers are assumed to pass through *quiescent states* — points at which they hold no references — and a global epoch advances only after all threads have done so, at which point objects retired in an older epoch are safe. **Read-Copy-Update (RCU)** [1][6] generalizes the quiescent-state idea into a first-class synchronization mechanism: readers execute in *read-side critical sections* delimited only by markers, writers publish new versions with release-ordered pointer stores, and a *grace period* — an interval during which every thread has passed through a quiescent state — guarantees that no reader can still observe the pre-update version, permitting safe reclamation or mutation of the old copy.

RCU is no laboratory curiosity: it underpins the Linux kernel's networking stack, VFS, and scheduler read paths, serving billions of devices for two decades [6]. Its appeal is radical asymmetry — single-digit-nanosecond, *wait-free* read-side overhead, with all cost pushed to the rare update path. This thesis asks: what exactly is a grace period, how can its detection be made scalable and provably correct, and where does the abstraction break down?

We formalize the grace-period invariant and its LKMM memory-ordering obligations, analyze Tree RCU as a case study in hierarchical detection, contrast RCU with hazard pointers and EBR/QSBR in a unified cost model, and survey formal verification from Grace logic to model checking of production Tree RCU.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The reclamation problem, stated precisely

Consider a concurrent sorted linked list. A deleter unlinks node `n` with one CAS on its predecessor's `next` field, while a searcher may have loaded `&n` into a register just before the unlink and be about to read `n->key`. If the deleter frees `n`, the searcher dereferences freed memory. The difficulty is *temporal*: the deleter cannot know, without coordination, whether any thread's *past* reads will produce *future* dereferences of `n`.

> **Definition (Reclamation safety):** A memory-reclamation scheme is *safe* if no thread ever dereferences an object after its memory has been returned to the allocator. It is *live* if every retired object is eventually reclaimed (bounded memory) and *robust* if a stalled thread cannot indefinitely block reclamation of objects retired by others.

Tracing collectors stop the world or need read barriers — unacceptable in kernels and real-time systems. Reference counting needs atomic increments on the read path, destroying read scalability. The schemes studied here all implement *deferred destruction*: unlinked objects are *retired* into per-thread limbo lists and freed only after a *safety condition* is established.

### 2.2 The grace period abstraction

RCU's central concept is the *grace period*: a time interval during which every thread passes through at least one *quiescent state* — a point at which the thread holds no references to RCU-protected data structures [1]. Typical quiescent states: context switches, user-mode execution, explicit `rcu_quiescent_state()` calls. The fundamental law follows directly:

> **Theorem (Grace-period safety):** Let a grace period begin after object `X` is removed from all globally reachable RCU-protected structures. When the grace period ends, no thread can hold a reference to `X` acquired before its removal, so `X` may be safely freed or reused.

*Proof sketch.* Any reference to `X` was acquired in a read-side section begun before `X`'s removal (after removal `X` is unreachable, so no new references form — the *publication* discipline of Section 4.4). That section ends at the thread's next quiescent state, before the grace period ends. ∎

Readers never write shared memory, never execute atomic read-modify-write operations, and never block: the read-side primitives compile to a compiler barrier or nothing at all. All cost is borne by the updater, which must *detect* the grace period's end.

## 3 Methodology

This thesis synthesizes primary sources — McKenney's perfbook [1], the kernel RCU documentation [6], the hazard-pointer paper [5], the user-level RCU study [4], and the verification literature [2][3] — via three methods:

1. **Operational formalization.** We restate the grace-period invariant as a safety theorem with explicit assumptions, so schemes can be compared by *what safety condition they detect*.
2. **Mechanism analysis.** For each scheme we derive read-side and write-side cost models from the published algorithms, including memory-ordering requirements and their LKMM mapping.
3. **Verification survey.** We classify formal results by memory model assumed, property proved, and gap to deployed code.

Tree RCU is our running case study: the most widely deployed and most aggressively optimized grace-period detector in existence [3][6].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 RCU fundamentals: grace periods and quiescent states

An RCU-protected read path has the following shape (kernel API):

```c
rcu_read_lock();                 /* enter read-side critical section */
p = rcu_dereference(gp);         /* safely load the published pointer */
do_something_with(p);            /* must not block or sleep */
rcu_read_unlock();               /* exit; quiescent state follows */
```

The updater mirrors it:

```c
spin_lock(&updatelock);
q = kmalloc(sizeof(*q), GFP_KERNEL);   /* allocate replacement */
*q = *p;                              /* copy old version */
q->field = new_value;                 /* modify the copy */
rcu_assign_pointer(gp, q);            /* publish: release store */
spin_unlock(&updatelock);
synchronize_rcu();                    /* wait for a grace period */
kfree(p);                             /* reclaim the old version */
```

Four invariants [1][6]:

1. **Publication.** `rcu_assign_pointer` is a release store; observing the new pointer implies observing the fully initialized object. `rcu_dereference` is an acquire load (on strongly ordered architectures an address dependency suffices — Section 4.4).
2. **Subscription.** References to RCU-protected objects may only be acquired inside read-side critical sections, via `rcu_dereference`.
3. **Quiescent-state coverage.** Every thread entering read-side sections passes through quiescent states infinitely often; a kernel context switch is an implicit quiescent state, which is why preemptible RCU treats in-section preemption specially.
4. **Grace-period detection.** `synchronize_rcu()` returns only after every thread has passed through a quiescent state subsequent to the call.

Readers are *wait-free* and *oblivious*. The updater's `synchronize_rcu()` is the single synchronization point, and its latency (microseconds to milliseconds) is the price of the scheme; `synchronize_rcu_expedited()` trades CPU throughput for latency by actively IPI-pinging CPUs [6].

### 4.2 Tree RCU in the Linux kernel

Polling every CPU to detect a grace period on a 4096-CPU machine is itself a scalability bottleneck. Tree RCU solves it with a *combining tree*: a hierarchy of `rcu_node` structures whose leaves are CPUs and whose root aggregates quiescent-state reports [3][6]. Each CPU records quiescent states locally and reports up the tree; a parent advances only after all children report, and the root's advancement ends the grace period, waking `synchronize_rcu()` waiters.

Key design elements, each a response to a concrete scalability or correctness hazard:

- **Preemptible RCU.** In `CONFIG_PREEMPT_RCU`, a task preempted inside `rcu_read_lock()` is *not* in a quiescent state; Tree RCU tracks per-task nesting and removes the task from the blocking set only at the outermost `rcu_read_unlock()`. Blocking inside read-side sections is therefore forbidden — it would stall grace periods indefinitely.
- **RCU callbacks and batching.** `call_rcu()` defers frees to callbacks invoked after the grace period; callbacks are batched per-CPU to amortize detection cost across many retired objects.
- **RCU flavors.** SRCU uses per-domain counters for sleepable readers; Tasks RCU covers code preemptible anywhere (trampolines, ftrace); NMI RCU covers non-maskable-interrupt contexts [6].
- **Stall detection and torture.** A single CPU stuck with interrupts disabled delays *all* `synchronize_rcu()` waiters, so Tree RCU includes stall warnings and the `rcutorture` stress suite, which has caught real bugs [3][6].
- **Expedited and polled grace periods.** `synchronize_rcu_expedited()` IPI-pings CPUs instead of waiting passively; polled variants let updaters batch many updates behind one detection pass.

Liang et al. [3] applied CBMC-based bounded model checking to the actual Tree RCU implementation across kernel versions — a rare mechanical check of a production synchronizer of this complexity, which found real discrepancies in test harnesses.

### 4.3 Hazard pointers, epoch-based reclamation, and their modern refinements

**Hazard pointers** [5] invert RCU's trade: instead of the updater waiting for quiescence, each *reader* explicitly publishes the addresses it is about to touch (load–publish–validate):

```c
node_t *p;
do {
    p = atomic_load(&head);          /* speculative load */
    hp[tid][0] = p;                  /* publish hazard pointer */
    /* fence: make publication visible before validation */
    atomic_thread_fence(memory_order_seq_cst);
} while (p != atomic_load(&head));   /* validate: pointer unchanged */
use(p);
hp[tid][0] = NULL;                   /* release protection */
```

The retiring thread scans all threads' hazard slots; any retired node not found in any slot is safe to free. The scan costs O(H·N) for H slots and N threads, so reclamation is *batched*: each thread retires nodes into a private list and scans only when the list exceeds a threshold, giving amortized O(H) per retire. Memory is strictly bounded — at most O(H·N²) unreclaimed nodes [5]. Crucially, hazard pointers are *wait-free* for readers and *robust*: a stalled thread blocks reclamation only of the nodes it actually protects, never of the whole system. The price is per-dereference overhead (a store plus a fence on every protected load) and a fundamental expressiveness limit: a thread can only protect a *bounded, statically known* number of nodes, so algorithms that traverse through unlinked nodes are incompatible with the scheme [7].

**Epoch-based reclamation** replaces per-pointer publication with a global epoch counter. Each thread announces its epoch on operation entry; the updater advances the epoch only when all threads have caught up, then frees objects retired two epochs back. Readers pay one (amortized ~zero) announcement per operation; updaters pay periodic global scans. The scheme is simple and fast but *not robust*: a stalled thread pins the global epoch and blocks all reclamation system-wide.

**QSBR**, the default in `liburcu` [4], is EBR's read-optimized limit: where applications have natural quiescent points (event loops, request boundaries), threads announce nothing; the updater waits for each thread to pass a quiescent point, as in kernel RCU but in userspace. Desnoyers et al. [4] measured QSBR read-side overhead at a compiler barrier, outperforming reader-writer locks by orders of magnitude under read-mostly load on 64-core POWER5+.

**Modern refinements** push on the robustness/efficiency frontier. *Hyaline* achieves wait-free reclamation by batching retirements into per-epoch lists reclaimed with a single atomic operation, eliminating EBR's global scan stalls; *hazard eras* combine epochs with hazard pointers to bound memory while keeping per-dereference cost low. The comparative study in [7] confirms the qualitative ranking: QSBR/RCU dominate read-heavy workloads, hazard pointers dominate when threads may stall, and EBR sits in the middle — simple and fast, but fragile.

| Scheme | Read-side cost | Reclamation scan | Memory bound | Robust to stalls | Needs per-op annotation |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| RCU / QSBR | ~0 (compiler barrier) | Grace-period detect (hierarchical) | Unbounded under stalled reader | No | Quiescent points only |
| EBR | Epoch announce (amortized ~0) | Global epoch scan | Unbounded under stalled thread | No | Yes (enter/exit) |
| Hazard pointers | Store + fence per deref | O(H·N) scan, batched | O(H·N²) bounded | **Yes** | Yes (per pointer) |
| Hyaline / hazard eras | Near-EBR | Batched, wait-free | Bounded | **Yes** | Yes |

### 4.4 Memory ordering, the LKMM, and address dependencies

RCU's publish-subscribe pattern is a litmus test for memory models. The updater's release store must order *before* the initialization writes it publishes; the reader's pointer load must order *before* dependent field reads. On x86/TSO this is nearly free; on weakly ordered architectures (ARM, POWER, RISC-V) the ordering must be explicit.

The Linux Kernel Memory Model (LKMM) [1, Ch. 16] gives `rcu_assign_pointer` release semantics and `rcu_dereference` acquire semantics *strengthened by address dependencies*: CPUs may not speculate a dependent load before the load it depends on, so `rcu_dereference` compiles to a plain load plus `READ_ONCE` on dependency-honoring architectures. Three consequences follow:

1. **Control dependencies are not enough.** A branch on the loaded pointer does not order subsequent loads on all architectures; only *address* dependencies (using the loaded value to compute the next load's address) carry ordering. RCU's subscription discipline is engineered so that every protected access is address-dependent on the `rcu_dereference`.
2. **Compiler barriers are load-bearing.** `rcu_dereference` includes `READ_ONCE` semantics: the compiler may not invent, duplicate, or tear the load, lest a reader observe a half-published pointer.
3. **Grace periods imply transitivity.** The LKMM's *grace-period* axiom states that a grace period orders all prior reads before all subsequent frees — the formal counterpart of our Theorem in Section 2.2. Tassarotti et al.'s verification of a userspace RCU list was carried out in GPS, a program logic for the C11 release-acquire fragment, showing that RCU's guarantees are provable below sequential consistency — but only just, since the proof depends delicately on release-acquire chains through the pointer publication [2].

RCU is thus a *memory-model-sensitive* abstraction in a way locks are not: porting it requires re-verifying dependency chains, not merely the algorithm.

### 4.5 Formal verification of RCU

Verifying RCU is verifying *two* things at once: the client data structure's linearizability *and* the reclamation scheme's safety, where the latter depends on temporal reasoning about quiescent states that ordinary separation logic cannot express. The breakthrough was Gotsman, Rinetzky, and Yang's **Grace** logic [2] (ESOP 2013): concurrent separation logic extended with *grace*, a resource tracking the obligation to wait for quiescent states. Its soundness theorem is our grace-period safety theorem, mechanized.

Later work climbed the fidelity ladder: Tassarotti, Dreyer, and Vafeiadis verified a realistic userspace RCU list under weak memory via GPS protocol reasoning; Liang et al. model-checked the *actual* Linux Tree RCU with CBMC across kernel versions, finding real `rcutorture` harness bugs [3]; Kokologiannakis et al. applied stateless model checking to Tree RCU's publish-subscribe guarantees under POWER [8].

## 5 Empirical Results and Formal Proofs

### 5.1 Performance: RCU versus locking on many-core

Desnoyers et al.'s user-level study [4] remains the cleanest head-to-head comparison. On a 64-core POWER5+ machine, as read-side critical-section length shrinks toward tens of nanoseconds, RCU/QSBR read throughput stays flat while pthread mutex and reader-writer lock throughput collapses: lock read-side overhead grows roughly 10× from 8 to 64 cores due to cache-line bouncing of the lock word, whereas RCU's read-side cost is core-count independent because readers write nothing shared. For read-mostly workloads RCU scales near-linearly where rwlocks serialize; only above roughly 1–10% update rates does grace-period latency dominate, and batched `call_rcu` amortizes detection across thousands of updates. In the kernel, RCU protects the dentry cache, routing tables, and `task_struct` lookups on thousand-CPU machines, with `synchronize_rcu()` latency of tens of microseconds (expedited) to milliseconds (normal) [6].

### 5.2 What the proofs actually guarantee

**Proved:** grace-period safety for abstract RCU over SC [2]; reclamation safety of a userspace RCU list over release-acquire; bounded-model-checked grace-period detection for Tree RCU snapshots [3]; publish-subscribe ordering under POWER [8]. **Not proved:** full functional correctness of Tree RCU (stall detection, expedited paths, NMI handling); liveness under adversarial scheduling — most results are safety-only.

## 6 Limitations

**RCU's limitations are structural.** First, *write-side latency*: `synchronize_rcu()` waits out the longest pre-existing read-side critical section — the latency spike is global, not per-object. Second, *unbounded memory under stall*: reclamation gated on grace periods means a stalled reader causes unbounded accumulation of retired objects — RCU is not robust in the formal sense. Third, *read-side restrictions*: kernel-RCU readers may not block, sleep, or page-fault inside critical sections, which is why SRCU and hazard-pointer-based schemes exist for sleepable contexts. Fourth, *update-side serialization*: classic RCU serializes updaters with an external lock, penalizing highly concurrent update workloads. Fifth, *memory-model fragility*: RCU's correctness rests on dependency chains and compiler discipline re-audited per architecture and per language — the Alpha `smp_read_barrier_depends` episode and the C++ `memory_order_consume` deprecation are cautionary tales.

**Hazard pointers' limitations** are the mirror image: per-dereference fences impose a small read-side tax, *unbounded lookahead* is inexpressible (algorithms traversing unlinked nodes cannot be protected [7]), and the batched O(H·N) scan is a periodic latency spike.

**EBR/QSBR's limitation** is singular but severe: any thread delayed inside an operation pins the epoch for everyone — the dominant operational failure mode in userspace with thread pools, GC pauses, or page faults.

**Verification limitations:** the strongest results [2][3] verify *safety* rather than *liveness* or full-client linearizability, on snapshots of code that has since evolved. The kernel's RCU remains, strictly speaking, unproved software — tested by `rcutorture` and billions of device-hours, not theorem provers.

---

## 7 Conclusion

Read-Copy-Update, hazard pointers, epoch-based reclamation, and QSBR are four points in a single design space parameterized by *how the safety condition for freeing is detected*: by waiting for quiescent states globally (RCU, QSBR), by tracking epochs approximately (EBR), or by publishing protection explicitly (hazard pointers). The grace period is the unifying abstraction — a temporal fence after which the past can no longer reach into the present — and the engineering of its detection, from Tree RCU's combining hierarchy to hazard pointers' load–publish–validate dance, is where the field's intellectual content resides.

Three lessons stand out. First, **asymmetry wins**: pushing all cost to the rare path beats symmetric schemes whenever workloads are read-mostly. Second, **robustness is the real differentiator**: stall behavior under delay — global versus local failure — determines fitness for userspace deployment, and it is hazard pointers' decisive advantage. Third, **proofs are catching up to practice**: reclamation safety is formalizable even under weak memory, but verified artifacts remain simpler than what ships, and liveness remains largely unproved.

Open problems: a mechanically verified liveness proof for production Tree RCU under the full LKMM; a scheme combining QSBR-class read performance with hazard-pointer-class robustness and bounded memory; and an RCU standardization for C++ and Rust preserving dependency-based ordering without resurrecting `memory_order_consume`. Deferred destruction remains where concurrency theory meets hardware at its most unforgiving — and where getting it right matters most.
