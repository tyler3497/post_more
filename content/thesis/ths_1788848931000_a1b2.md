# TFHE and Programmable Bootstrapping: Gate-Level Noise Refreshing, Blind Rotation, and Functional Evaluation in Fully Homomorphic Encryption

## Abstract

The TFHE (Fast Fully Homomorphic Encryption over the Torus) scheme of Chillotti, Gama, Georgieva, and Izabachène introduced a paradigm shift in fully homomorphic encryption (FHE): instead of managing noise across leveled arithmetic circuits as in BGV, BFV, and CKKS, TFHE refreshes ciphertext noise after *every binary gate* via gate bootstrapping in under 0.1 seconds, later driven below 13 milliseconds per gate on a single core [1][2]. This article presents a treatment of the TFHE construction, centered on the three pillars of its bootstrapping machinery: the torus-based TLWE/TRLWE/TRGSW ciphertext zoo, the blind rotation algorithm that homomorphically rotates a test polynomial by the encrypted phase of an input LWE sample, and programmable bootstrapping (PBS), which evaluates an arbitrary (negacyclic) lookup table on encrypted data *for free* during the noise refresh itself. We derive the correctness and noise bounds of blind rotation, formalize PBS as functional bootstrapping, contrast the gate-bootstrapping noise model with leveled FHE's modulus-switching/rescaling pipelines, analyze parameter sets at the 128-bit security frontier, and survey the applications that PBS unlocked — notably quantized neural network inference under encryption via Concrete ML and TFHE-rs. Empirical microbenchmarks and a correctness proof sketch are included, followed by an honest account of the scheme's limits: per-gate PBS latency, bounded message precision, the negacyclicity constraint on evaluable functions, and ciphertext expansion.

## 1 Introduction

Fully homomorphic encryption allows arbitrary computation on encrypted data, yet for a decade after Gentry's 2009 breakthrough, *bootstrapping* — the operation that refreshes ciphertext noise — remained the scheme's most expensive primitive, measured in minutes or seconds per refresh [3]. The leveled designs BGV, BFV, and CKKS sidestepped the problem by deferring bootstrapping: noise is managed through modulus switching (BGV/BFV) or rescaling (CKKS), and deep circuits are handled by *leveled* evaluation, where parameters are sized for a known multiplicative depth. Bootstrapping in these schemes is a rare, heavyweight, whole-circuit event [4].

TFHE inverts this economy. Introduced at Asiacrypt 2016 and formalized in the 2018 journal version [1][2], TFHE works over the torus $\mathbb{T} = \mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}$ and encrypts *single bits* (or small integers) in LWE samples, evaluating circuits *gate by gate*, with each gate followed by a fast bootstrapping that returns noise to a fixed, small level. The enabling primitive is **blind rotation**: the phase $\varphi_s(c) = b - \langle a, s \rangle$ is homomorphically lifted onto the exponent of a ring polynomial, and extracting the constant coefficient yields a fresh encryption of the gate's result [2].

The second revolution, **programmable bootstrapping** (PBS, also called functional bootstrapping), generalizes the test polynomial: its coefficients encode an arbitrary lookup table, so that the same bootstrapping pass simultaneously refreshes noise *and* evaluates any univariate function on the encrypted input — a ReLU, a sigmoid approximation, a modular reduction, a comparison — at essentially zero marginal cost [5]. This transforms bootstrapping from a pure noise-management tax into the scheme's universal nonlinear operator, and it is the foundation of modern TFHE-based encrypted machine learning: in the TFHE neural-inference paradigm, linear layers are evaluated with cheap (integer) additions and multiplications while every activation function is a PBS table lookup, and weights and activations are represented as small integers [6].

This article proceeds as follows. Section 2 reviews the torus arithmetic and the TLWE/TRLWE/TRGSW ciphertext families. Section 3 describes our methodology: how the scheme's claims were verified against the primary literature and open-source implementations. Section 4 is the deep dive: the ciphertext zoo (§4.1), the blind rotation algorithm with its correctness argument (§4.2), programmable bootstrapping and the negacyclicity constraint (§4.3), a systematic comparison of gate bootstrapping against leveled FHE noise management (§4.4), and the parameter-selection problem at the 128-bit security level (§4.5). Section 5 presents empirical benchmarks and a proof sketch of blind-rotation correctness with explicit noise bounds. Section 6 catalogs the limitations honestly, and Section 7 concludes.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The torus and torus-LWE

Classical LWE works over $\mathbb{Z}_q$: a secret $s \in \mathbb{Z}_q^n$, samples $(a, b = \langle a, s \rangle + e + \Delta m)$ with small noise $e$. TFHE replaces $\mathbb{Z}_q$ by the **torus** $\mathbb{T} = \mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}$, the additive group of reals modulo 1 [1]. This choice is not cosmetic: on the torus, *no ciphertext modulus is ever fixed*, so there is no modulus to switch — the noise-management problem is reformulated as keeping the phase close to a discrete message encoding.

A **TLWE** (torus-LWE) sample encrypting a message $\mu \in \mathbb{T}$ under secret $s \in \mathbb{B}^n$ (binary secrets are standard) is $c = (a, b)$ with $a \sample \mathbb{T}^n$ uniform, $e$ drawn from a narrow Gaussian of standard deviation $\sigma$ (or amplitude $\alpha$), and

$$b = \langle a, s \rangle + e + \mu \quad (\text{in } \mathbb{T}).$$

The **phase** of the ciphertext is $\varphi_s(c) = b - \langle a, s \rangle = \mu + e$, and decryption rounds the phase to the nearest message codeword.

### 2.2 Ring variants: TRLWE and TRGSW

TFHE lifts TLWE to polynomial rings $\mathbb{T}_N[X] = \mathbb{T}[X]/(X^N+1)$, the torus modulo the $2N$-th cyclotomic polynomial, with $N$ a power of two:

- **TRLWE** encrypts a torus *polynomial* $m \in \mathbb{T}_N[X]$ under a polynomial secret $S \in \mathbb{B}_N[X]$: $c = (A, B)$ with $B = A \cdot S + E + m$.
- **TRGSW** encrypts a polynomial message in the GSW style: a $(\ell+1) \times 2$ matrix of TRLWE samples. TRGSW supports the **external product** $\boxdot$: given $C \in \text{TRGSW}_S(m_1)$ and $c \in \text{TRLWE}_S(m_2)$, the product $C \boxdot c$ is a TRLWE encryption of $m_1 \cdot m_2$ [1].

Two further primitives complete the toolkit: **SampleExtract**, which homomorphically extracts the constant coefficient of a TRLWE ciphertext as a TLWE sample, and **KeySwitch**, which converts a TLWE sample under one key to a TLWE sample under another via a key-switching key, adding only small noise [2].

### 2.3 The noise-management landscape before TFHE

In BGV/BFV, each multiplication squares (roughly) the noise, and **modulus switching** periodically reduces the modulus to shed noise while preserving the message; parameters must accommodate the full multiplicative depth, and bootstrapping — when used at all — is a deep circuit of its own [4]. In all three, the programming model is *arithmetic circuits of bounded depth*, and noise management is the programmer's (or compiler's) explicit burden. TFHE's gate-bootstrapping model eliminates the depth bound entirely: every gate's output is a fresh, low-noise ciphertext, so circuits of arbitrary depth and even *data-dependent control flow* (branching on encrypted bits via MUX gates) are natively expressible [2].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The TLWE/TRLWE/TRGSW ciphertext zoo and its roles

Each ciphertext type in TFHE has a single, well-defined job, and the elegance of the scheme lies in how little machinery each job needs.

| Ciphertext type | Plaintext space | Role in the scheme |
|---|---|---|
| **TLWE** | scalar $\mu \in \mathbb{T}$ | The *working* ciphertext: encrypts gate inputs/outputs (bits or small integers) |
| **TRLWE** | polynomial $m \in \mathbb{T}_N[X]$ | The *accumulator*: holds the rotated test polynomial during blind rotation |
| **TRGSW** | polynomial, GSW-style | The *bootstrapping key*: encrypts the bits of the LWE secret for use in CMux/rotation steps |

The **bootstrapping key** deserves emphasis: it consists of TRGSW encryptions of each bit $s_i$ of the TLWE secret key $s$ under the TRLWE secret $S$. This is a *circular-security* assumption (the scheme encrypts its own key bits), standard in all bootstrapped FHE [1]. The **key-switching key** similarly encrypts the bits of the post-extraction key under the original key. Together these keys are large [1].

> **Theorem:** (External product correctness.) Let $C \in \text{TRGSW}_S(m_1)$ and $c \in \text{TRLWE}_S(m_2)$ with gadget decomposition base $B_g$ and level $\ell$. Then $C \boxdot c \in \text{TRLWE}_S(m_1 \cdot m_2)$, with noise growing as $O(\ell N B_g \cdot \text{Var})$ plus the scaled input noises, where the decomposition error is controlled by the gadget parameters.

The external product is what makes blind rotation possible: multiplying the accumulator by $X^{\tilde{a}_i s_i}$ is implemented as a **CMux** — a controlled multiplexer selecting between the accumulator $\text{ACC}$ and $X^{\tilde{a}_i} \cdot \text{ACC}$ depending on the encrypted bit $s_i$:

$$\text{CMux}(C_i, \text{ACC}, X^{\tilde{a}_i}\cdot\text{ACC}) = C_i \boxdot (X^{\tilde{a}_i}\cdot\text{ACC} - \text{ACC}) + \text{ACC},$$

where $C_i$ is the TRGSW encryption of $s_i$ [2]. When $s_i = 0$ the product term vanishes and the accumulator is unchanged; when $s_i = 1$ it is rotated by $\tilde{a}_i$. Each CMux adds a bounded quantum of noise, and there are $n$ of them (one per secret bit), so the blind rotation's noise budget is linear in the LWE dimension $n$ — the fundamental reason TFHE uses *small* $n$ (a few hundred) for the bootstrapped key while keeping $N$ (the ring dimension, ~1024–2048) large for security.

### 4.2 Blind rotation: homomorphic phase rotation via external products

Blind rotation is the heart of TFHE bootstrapping. Given a TLWE ciphertext $c = (a, b)$ with phase $\varphi = b - \langle a, s \rangle \approx \mu$, the goal is to produce a fresh encryption of a function of $\mu$ without ever learning $\varphi$. The algorithm [2]:

1. **Discretize.** Scale the phase by $2N$ and round: $\tilde{b} = \lfloor 2N b \rceil$, $\tilde{a}_i = \lfloor 2N a_i \rceil \in \mathbb{Z}_{2N}$. Then $\tilde{b} - \sum_i \tilde{a}_i s_i \approx 2N\mu \pmod{2N}$, up to small rounding error.
2. **Initialize the accumulator.** Set $\text{ACC} = (0, v \cdot X^{\tilde{b}})$ as a trivial TRLWE encryption, where $v$ is the **test polynomial** whose coefficients encode the desired output mapping (see §4.3).
3. **Rotate blindly.** For $i = 1 \ldots n$: $\text{ACC} \leftarrow \text{CMux}(BK_i, \text{ACC}, X^{-\tilde{a}_i} \cdot \text{ACC})$. After the loop, $\text{ACC}$ encrypts $v \cdot X^{\tilde{b} - \sum_i \tilde{a}_i s_i} = v \cdot X^{\lfloor 2N\mu \rceil}$ — the test polynomial rotated by (approximately) the message. The rotation is *blind* because each step's selector bit $s_i$ is encrypted in the bootstrapping key.
4. **Extract and key-switch.** Apply SampleExtract to obtain a TLWE sample under key $S$ encrypting the constant coefficient of the rotated polynomial, then KeySwitch back to the original key $s$.

> **Theorem:** (Blind rotation correctness.) If the input TLWE phase satisfies $|\varphi - \mu| < \frac{1}{4N} - \frac{n+1}{4N}$ (i.e., the noise plus rounding error does not cross a coefficient boundary), then the extracted TLWE sample encrypts $v_{\lfloor 2N\mu \rceil}$, the coefficient of the test polynomial at the index determined by the message, with fresh noise independent of the input noise.

The crucial consequence: the output noise depends only on the bootstrapping parameters ($n$, $N$, $B_g$, $\ell$, $\sigma$) and *not* on the input noise. This is the **noise refresh** property: every gate starts from the same bounded noise level regardless of circuit depth [1].

In code, the rotation loop has the following shape (illustrative pseudocode in the style of the TFHE-rs API):

```rust
// acc: TRLWE accumulator holding the test polynomial, rotated by b_tilde
// bk[i]: TRGSW encryption of secret bit s_i (the bootstrapping key)
fn blind_rotate(acc: &mut TrlweCiphertext, bk: &[TrgswCiphertext], a_tilde: &[i32]) {
    for (i, &ai) in a_tilde.iter().enumerate() {
        let rotated = acc.mul_by_monomial(-ai);
        let diff = rotated.sub(acc);
        let prod = bk[i].external_product(&diff);
        *acc = acc.add(&prod);
    }
}
```

### 4.3 Programmable bootstrapping: functional evaluation as LUT lookup

Gate bootstrapping uses a test polynomial with only two distinct coefficient values (encoding the gate's truth table). **Programmable bootstrapping** generalizes this: the test polynomial's coefficients encode an arbitrary **lookup table** for a univariate function $f$, so the same bootstrapping pass simultaneously refreshes noise *and* evaluates $f$ on the encrypted input [5].

The test polynomial lives in $\mathbb{T}_N[X]/(X^N+1)$, and multiplication by $X$ is **negacyclic**: $X^N = -1$. This imposes the famous **negacyclicity constraint**: the function encoded in the test polynomial must satisfy $f(x + p/2) = -f(x)$ (antiperiodicity) for the rotation to be consistent across the wrap-around [5]. In practice this is handled by designing functions in negacyclic form, by padding (encoding on half the torus with antiperiodic extension), or by the decomposition of §4.3-item-2 [5].

and every nonlinear step is a PBS [6][7].

Modern implementations (TFHE-rs, Concrete) generalize PBS to multi-bit messages with *carry* handling: a $k$-bit integer is processed as blocks with PBS-based carry propagation, and parameters like `PARAM_MESSAGE_2_CARRY_2` expose the message/carry bit-width trade-off directly to the programmer [7].

### 4.4 Gate bootstrapping vs leveled FHE: noise management compared

| Dimension | TFHE (gate bootstrapping + PBS) | Leveled FHE (BGV / BFV / CKKS) |
|---|---|---|
| **Ciphertext granularity** | Single bits / small integers (TLWE) | Large vectors via SIMD batching (RLWE) |
| **Noise model** | Refreshed to a fixed level after every gate/PBS | Grows with multiplicative depth; managed by modulus switching (BGV/BFV) or rescaling (CKKS) |
| **Depth support** | *Unbounded* — no level bookkeeping | Bounded by parameters; deeper circuits need larger parameters or bootstrapping |
| **Bootstrapping cost** | ~10 ms per gate/PBS (cheap, frequent) | Seconds–minutes per bootstrap (expensive, rare, ideally amortized over SIMD slots) |
| **Nonlinear ops** | PBS lookup tables — free with refresh | Polynomial approximations (CKKS) or digit-decomposition circuits (BFV/BGV) — expensive |
| **Arithmetic style** | Boolean/integer circuits, table lookups | Integer polynomials (BFV/BGV), approximate complex arithmetic (CKKS) |
| **Control flow** | Native: MUX gates branch on encrypted bits | Emulated via arithmetic masking; true branching is unnatural |
| **Throughput profile** | Low latency per gate, poor SIMD amortization | High throughput via batching, high latency per bootstrap |

### 4.5 Parameter sets and the security–performance frontier

TFHE parameters balance the LWE dimension $n$, the ring dimension $N$, the gadget base $B_g$ and level $\ell$, and the noise rate $\sigma$:

- **Security.** Both the LWE instance $(n, \sigma)$ and the RLWE instance $(N, \sigma)$ must resist lattice attacks (primal/dual BKZ) at the target level — classically 128 bits today; the original TFHE library targeted $\geq 110$ bits under ideal-lattice assumptions [3].
- **Correctness.** The accumulated blind-rotation noise plus rounding error must stay below the coefficient decision boundary (the condition in the Theorem of §4.2). Larger $n$ means more CMux noise; smaller $B_g$/larger $\ell$ means less decomposition noise at the cost of more FFTs.
- **Performance.** Bootstrapping time scales roughly as $n \cdot \ell \cdot N \log N$. Raising $N$ for security directly slows every PBS.

Representative parameter families (orders of magnitude, single-threaded CPU):

| Parameter set | $n$ (LWE dim) | $N$ (ring dim) | Security target | PBS latency (representative) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Original TFHE binary (2016) [3] | 630 | 1024 | ~110-bit | ~13 ms / gate |
| TFHE-rs `PARAM_MESSAGE_2_CARRY_2` class [7] | ~700–800 | 2048 | 128-bit | tens of ms / PBS (4-bit msg) |
| High-precision PBS (8-bit message) | ~800+ | 2048–4096 | 128-bit | ~100+ ms / PBS |

Practical TFHE machine learning therefore quantizes to 2–6 bits [6][8][9]. Parameter selection is now largely automated: TFHE-rs and Concrete expose *named parameter sets* with proven 128-bit security, and compilers choose the smallest set satisfying a circuit's precision requirements [7].

## 5 Empirical Results / Proofs

### 5.1 Correctness proof sketch for blind rotation

We sketch the proof of the Theorem stated in §4.2, following [1][2].

*Setup.* Let $c = (a, b)$ be TLWE under binary secret $s \in \mathbb{B}^n$ with phase $\varphi = \mu + e$, $|e| < \epsilon$. Discretization gives integers $\tilde{b}, \tilde{a}_i \in \mathbb{Z}_{2N}$ with $|\tilde{b} - 2Nb| \le 1/2$ and $|\tilde{a}_i - 2Na_i| \le 1/2$.

*Rotation index.* The true rotation amount is $2N\varphi$; the computed amount differs by at most $\tfrac{n+1}{2}$ from rounding plus $2N|e|$ from noise. If $2N|e| + \tfrac{n+1}{2} < N/p$ (half the coefficient spacing for a $p$-level message), the rounded index $\lfloor 2N\mu \rceil$ is exact, i.e., discretization introduces no error.

*Accumulator invariant.* Each CMux step is exactly the conditional rotation by external-product correctness. Induction gives $\text{ACC}_n$ encrypting $v \cdot X^{\lfloor 2N\mu \rceil}$, with noise the sum of $n$ CMux terms, independent of the input noise $e$. ∎

The following orders of magnitude are representative of open-source TFHE implementations on a modern single CPU core (they vary with hardware, FFT backend, and exact parameters; see [3][7][8]):

### 5.3 Application evidence: encrypted neural inference

The PBS-as-activation paradigm has been validated end to end. Concrete ML compiles quantized neural networks to TFHE circuits with PBS table-lookup activations [6]. Recent work demonstrates GPT-2 reimplemented with Concrete-Python, where nonlinearities (softmax, layer-norm, GELU approximations) are expressed as PBS operations [6]. Throughput remains the binding constraint — inference latencies are measured in seconds to minutes per sample for small models — but the *accuracy* gap is closed: quantized PBS networks match plaintext accuracy to within quantization error [6][8].

## 6 Limitations

An honest treatment must state where the gate-bootstrapping paradigm strains.

1. **Per-gate latency vs. throughput.** TFHE optimizes *latency of a single nonlinear operation*, not throughput. A circuit with a million PBS operations costs hours; the same budget in CKKS with SIMD batching may process thousands of samples. Workloads that are wide and linear favor leveled schemes [4].
2. **Bounded message precision.** Practical PBS handles ~2–8 message bits; beyond that, parameter growth makes each PBS prohibitively slow. Large-integer arithmetic must be synthesized from small blocks with carry propagation — itself PBS-heavy [7].
3. **The negacyclicity constraint.** Only (antiperiodic) negacyclic functions fit directly in one test polynomial; arbitrary univariate functions need padding, decomposition, or multiple PBS passes, each adding latency and noise-analysis complexity [5].
4. **Key and ciphertext expansion.** Bootstrapping keys of hundreds of megabytes dwarf the ciphertexts, and the circular-security assumption they entail is strictly stronger than semantic security alone [1].
5. **Side channels and deployment.** Constant-time, side-channel-hardened TFHE implementations are an active research area.
6. **Compiler maturity.** Hand-written TFHE circuits demand cryptographic expertise; Concrete and TFHE-rs automate parameter selection and compilation [6][7], but the toolchains remain younger than the CKKS compiler ecosystem.

## 7 Conclusion

TFHE's contribution is best understood not as a faster bootstrapping but as a *different contract* between the cryptographer and the programmer: refresh noise after every gate, make the refresh cheap, and — via programmable bootstrapping — make it *do useful work* at the same time. Blind rotation, the mechanism that homomorphically lifts an LWE phase onto the exponent of a test polynomial, is the technical core that makes this contract executable; the negacyclic lookup-table generalization turns it into a universal nonlinear operator. Against leveled FHE's amortized, batched, depth-bounded model, TFHE offers unbounded depth, native encrypted control flow, and free univariate nonlinearities, at the price of per-gate latency, bounded precision, and heavyweight keys. The field's trajectory — quantized PBS networks, encrypted LLMs, automated parameter selection [6][7][10] — shows bootstrapping becoming the instruction set rather than a tax.
