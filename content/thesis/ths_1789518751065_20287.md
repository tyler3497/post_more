# The Geometry of Deep Learning: Neural Tangent Kernels, Loss Landscapes, and Double Descent

## Abstract

Three puzzles motivate this thesis. First, gradient descent optimizes violently non-convex deep networks to zero training error, despite classical results suggesting such problems are intractability-prone. Second, the loss surfaces traversed appear geometrically benign: minima are plentiful, connected, and largely equivalent. Third, massively over-parameterized models that interpolate noisy data nevertheless generalize, in apparent violation of the bias-variance trade-off. This work unifies the three pillars that together dissolve these paradoxes. The neural tangent kernel (NTK) of Jacot et al. [1] shows that infinitely wide networks evolve as linear kernel methods under gradient flow, the kernel frozen at initialization. Over-parameterized optimization theory [3][8] exploits this near-linearity to prove global convergence in polynomial time. The loss-landscape program — mode connectivity [4], sharp-versus-flat minima [5], and the mean-field perspective [7] — characterizes the geometry the optimizer traverses. Finally, double descent [2] and benign overfitting [6] reframe capacity as an ally. Together they replace the classical U-curve with a geometry in which width, implicit bias, and kernel structure conspire to make deep learning succeed.

##1 Introduction

Deep learning presents a *triad of paradoxes* that classical statistical learning theory cannot resolve:

- **Optimization:** Training minimizes a non-convex empirical risk over millions of parameters with plain stochastic gradient descent (SGD), routinely reaching zero training loss. Classical complexity theory offers no guarantee that this should work.
- **Geometry:** The loss landscape of a deep network is a high-dimensional hypersurface riddled, in principle, with spurious local minima and saddle points. Empirically, optimizers sail through it, find interchangeable solutions, and rarely get trapped.
- **Generalization:** Models with orders of magnitude more parameters than training samples interpolate noisy labels, yet achieve state-of-the-art test accuracy — a direct affront to the bias–variance trade-off.

This thesis argues that these paradoxes are three views of a single geometric fact: *sufficient width linearizes the problem*. The **neural tangent kernel** (NTK) formalizes this: as layer width tends to infinity, the network function evolves under gradient flow as if it were a linear model in a fixed kernel feature space [1]. Once training is approximately linear, global convergence follows from convex analysis [3][8]; the landscape is connected because near-linearity flattens barriers [4]; and generalization follows from the implicit bias of kernel gradient descent toward smooth, minimum-norm interpolants [2][6].

> Theorem: In the infinite-width limit, gradient flow on the parameters of a neural network is equivalent to kernel gradient flow in function space with a *constant* kernel, the neural tangent kernel. Training becomes convex in function space even though it is non-convex in parameter space [1].

The remainder of this thesis develops the NTK (§2–§4.2), the geometry of real loss landscapes (§4.3), the double-descent reinterpretation of generalization (§4.4), empirical corroboration (§5), and honest limitations (§6).

##2 Background

### 2.1 From Gaussian Processes to Kernels

At initialization, an infinitely wide neural network with i.i.d. random weights is equivalent to a Gaussian process (GP): each output is a centered GP whose covariance is determined recursively by the architecture [1]. This *NNGP* correspondence links networks to kernel methods at initialization. The NTK goes further: it characterizes not just the prior at initialization but the entire *trajectory* of training.

### 2.2 Gradient Flow

Consider a dataset X = {x_1, ..., x_n} with targets y in R^n and squared loss L(θ) = (1/2)||f(θ) − y||², where f(θ) is the network's vector of outputs. Gradient flow dθ/dt = −η∇L(θ) induces function-space dynamics:

```python
# Continuous-time gradient flow: df/dt = -eta * J @ J.T @ (f - y)
# where J is the Jacobian of outputs w.r.t. parameters
```

The chain rule gives df/dt = −η Θ_t(X, X)(f_t(X) − y), where Θ_t(x, x′) = ⟨∇_θ f_t(x), ∇_θ f_t(x′)⟩ is the **empirical NTK** at time t. The functional cost is convex; the parameter cost is not — and the kernel is what transports convexity from one space to the other [1].

### 2.3 Loss Landscape Preliminaries

The empirical risk surface L: R^p → R for p parameters has:

1. **Critical points** where ∇L = 0 — minima, maxima, and saddle points, classified by the Hessian spectrum.
2. **Symmetries** — permuting hidden units leaves the function unchanged, so every minimum belongs to an enormous equivalence class of symmetric copies.
3. **Saddle abundance** — random-matrix arguments suggest critical points of high index (many negative Hessian directions) dominate in high dimensions, and gradient methods escape them efficiently.

Early work on the geometry of deep losses argued that most local minima are nearly as good as the global one, and that the real obstruction is saddles, not minima — a claim the landscape program has since made far more precise [4][5].

##3 Methodology

Our analytic toolkit is built from three interlocking instruments.

**Instrument 1 — Kernelization.** We derive the empirical NTK Θ_t from the network Jacobian and study its infinite-width limit Θ^∞, which is deterministic, recursively computable, and *frozen during training*. The freezing is the crux: it converts deep learning into kernel regression.

**Instrument 2 — Spectral analysis of the Hessian.** The local geometry around a minimizer is read off the Hessian eigenvalue density: sharp directions (large eigenvalues) versus flat ones (near-zero eigenvalues). Flatness correlates with generalization under the minimum-description-length view [5].

**Instrument 3 — Risk-curve analysis.** We track expected test risk as a function of model capacity, sample size, and training time, comparing the classical U-curve against the double-descent curve observed for interpolating models [2][6].

A minimal empirical NTK computation illustrates Instrument 1:

```python
import numpy as np

def empirical_ntk(X, params, forward, eps=1e-5):
    """Finite-difference empirical NTK Gram matrix for a scalar network."""
    n, d = X.shape
    p = params.size
    J = np.zeros((n, p))
    for j in range(p):
        dp = np.zeros_like(params); dp[j] = eps
        J[:, j] = (forward(X, params + dp) - forward(X, params - dp)) / (2 * eps)
    return J @ J.T  # Theta[i,k] = <grad f(xi), grad f(xk)>
```

In the infinite-width limit, this random Gram matrix concentrates on the deterministic analytic NTK, whose recursion for ReLU networks is known in closed form [1].

##4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Neural Tangent Kernel

For a network f(x; θ) with scalar output, define the empirical NTK

> Θ̂_t(x, x′) := ⟨∇_θ f(x; θ_t), ∇_θ f(x′; θ_t)⟩.

Jacot, Gabriel, and Hongler proved that as hidden widths tend to infinity, Θ̂_t converges in probability to a deterministic limit Θ^∞ that is *independent of time* [1]. Consequences follow immediately:

- **Linearization.** The model stays in the tangent space of initialization: f_t(x) ≈ f_0(x) + ⟨∇_θ f_0(x), θ_t − θ_0⟩. Training moves parameters only infinitesimally relative to their scale — the *lazy training* regime.
- **Kernel regression equivalence.** Under squared loss, f_t(X) obeys the linear ODE df/dt = −ηΘ^∞(f − y), whose solution is closed-form: f_t(X) = y + e^{−ηΘ^∞t}(f_0(X) − y).
- **Convergence from positive-definiteness.** If Θ^∞ is positive definite (proved for data on the sphere with non-polynomial activations [1]), the training loss decays exponentially, with rate governed by the kernel's smallest eigenvalue.

For ReLU networks the analytic NTK has an explicit recursion built from arc-cosine kernels:

```haskell
-- Analytic NTK recursion for ReLU nets (Bietti & Mairal form)
-- kappa0, kappa1 are the arc-cosine kernel functions of the cosine similarity t
type Kernel = Double -> Double -> Double

kappa0 :: Double -> Double
kappa0 t = (pi - acos t) / pi

kappa1 :: Double -> Double
kappa1 t = (t * (pi - acos t) + sqrt (1 - t*t)) / pi

ntkLayer :: Kernel -> Kernel -> Kernel
ntkLayer sigma k u v =
  sigma u v + k u v * kappa0 (sigma u v / (norm u * norm v))
```

The recursion makes precise how depth composes kernels — and, crucially, how it can also *degenerate* them: very deep NTKs collapse toward constant or rank-one kernels, a first hint that infinite depth and infinite width interact subtly.

### 4.2 Training Dynamics in Function Space

Because the functional cost is convex, the dynamics reduce to kernel gradient descent. Decompose the residual along the kernel principal components: convergence is fastest along the largest eigendirections of Θ^∞(X, X) [1]. This yields a principled account of **spectral bias** — networks fit low-frequency (large-eigenvalue) components first — and a theoretical motivation for *early stopping*: halting before small-eigenvalue directions overfit noise.

Over-parameterized optimization theory turned this picture into finite-width guarantees. Du et al. showed that gradient descent on sufficiently wide two-layer ReLU networks converges to zero training loss in polynomial time [3]; Allen-Zhu, Li, and Song extended the analysis to deep networks, proving that over-parameterization keeps activations near initialization so the NTK stays nearly constant throughout training [8]. The common mechanism is the same: *width buys near-linearity, and near-linearity buys global convergence*.

| Regime | Kernel behavior | Parameters move | Feature learning |
|---|---|---|---|
| Lazy / NTK | Θ_t ≈ Θ^∞ constant | Infinitesimally (relative) | No |
| Mean-field | Kernel evolves | Order-one | Yes |
| Finite-width practical | Θ_t drifts slowly | Moderately | Partially |

### 4.3 Loss Landscapes: Sharpness, Connectivity, and Symmetry

Real networks are finite, so the NTK only approximates them — and the geometry of their actual loss surfaces has its own rich structure.

**Mode connectivity.** Garipov et al. demonstrated that independently trained minima are connected by simple low-loss curves: there exist paths φ(α), α in (0,1), between two solutions θ_a and θ_b along which the loss stays nearly flat [4]. The loss landscape is thus not a scatter of isolated pits but a connected web — a *single basin with many entrances*. This explains why ensembling along such curves (fast geometric ensembling) works, and why the NTK's convex function-space picture is not wildly off even at finite width.

**Sharp versus flat minima.** Keskar et al. observed that large-batch training finds *sharp* minima (large Hessian eigenvalues) that generalize worse than the *flat* minima found by small-batch SGD [5]. The intuition: flat minima are robust to perturbations of parameters, and via PAC-Bayes / minimum-description-length arguments, robustness implies compressibility, which implies generalization. Sharpness-aware minimization later turned this observation into an algorithm.

**Symmetries and redundancy.** Permuting neurons within a layer, or rescaling across ReLU layers, leaves the function invariant. These symmetries tile parameter space with equivalent copies of every solution, which is one reason barriers between "different" minima are low: many apparent distinct minima are symmetry-related, and connectivity results [4] show even genuinely different ones are linked.

A declarative sketch of the descent loop clarifies what landscape geometry must support:

```tla+
---- MODULE GradientDescent ----
EXTENDS Naturals, Reals
VARIABLES theta, loss
Init == theta \in InitRegion /\ loss = L(theta)
Step == /\ grad = NablaL(theta)
        /\ theta' = theta - eta * grad
        /\ loss' = L(theta')
        /\ loss' <= loss + tolerance   \* landscape must permit descent
Spec == Init /\ [][Step]_<<theta, loss>>
====
```

The invariant `loss' ≤ loss + tolerance` holds along mode-connecting curves by construction [4] — the landscape's connectivity is what lets discrete steps approximate continuous descent without encountering walls.

### 4.4 Double Descent and Benign Overfitting

The classical bias–variance trade-off predicts a U-shaped risk curve: risk falls as capacity grows, then rises as overfitting sets in. Belkin, Hsu, Ma, and Mandal showed the true picture has a second act [2]. As capacity increases:

1. **Classical regime.** Risk follows the U-curve down to the *interpolation threshold* — the smallest capacity that fits training data exactly.
2. **Interpolation peak.** Right at the threshold, risk spikes: the barely-interpolating model is forced into wild oscillations to fit every point including noise.
3. **Modern regime.** Beyond the threshold, risk *descends again*, often below the classical minimum. More capacity yields smoother interpolants.

> Theorem (informal): Among all interpolants, gradient descent converges to the minimum-norm solution in the NTK feature space. As capacity grows, the minimum-norm interpolant becomes smoother, and its risk decreases past the interpolation threshold [1][2].

Bartlett, Long, Lugosi, and Tsigler made this precise for linear regression: *benign overfitting* occurs when the covariance spectrum has many small eigenvalues — the noise is absorbed into low-variance directions while the signal lives in a few large ones [6]. The same mechanism operates in kernel regression with the NTK: label noise is fit by high-frequency kernel components that contribute little to test error, provided the spectrum decays appropriately.

Double descent is not one curve but three, all documented empirically [2]:

- **Model-wise:** risk versus number of parameters (the canonical plot).
- **Sample-wise:** risk versus training set size at fixed over-parameterization.
- **Epoch-wise:** risk versus training time — test error dips, rises near interpolation, then falls again as optimization continues.

The NTK unifies these: epoch-wise double descent is kernel gradient descent traversing eigendirections from large to small [1]; model-wise double descent is the minimum-norm interpolant smoothing as the feature space grows [2][6].

##5 Empirical Evidence and Proofs

The theory above is not merely asymptotic poetry; it predicts measurable phenomena.

**NTK predictions match wide networks.** Lee et al. and subsequent studies showed that the analytic NTK predicts the training dynamics and generalization of wide networks quantitatively — linearized training (training only the last layer, or the full tangent model) closely tracks full training at large width, and the gap shrinks monotonically with width. This is the empirical signature of the lazy regime.

**Convergence guarantees hold at finite width.** The polynomial-width bounds of Du et al. [3] and Allen-Zhu et al. [8] are the finite-width shadows of the NTK limit: with width polynomial in sample size and inverse accuracy, gradient descent provably reaches zero training error. The proofs hinge on the Gram matrix staying positive definite during training — the finite-width version of kernel constancy.

**Connectivity is directly observed.** Garipov et al. constructed explicit low-loss Bézier curves between independently trained ResNets on CIFAR, with loss along the curve barely above the endpoints [4]. Ensembles sampled along these curves match or beat standard ensembles at a fraction of the cost.

**Double descent is ubiquitous.** Belkin et al. exhibited model-wise double descent across architectures — fully connected nets, CNNs, ResNets, random forests, random features — and datasets [2]. The interpolation peak sharpens with label noise and vanishes with proper regularization, exactly as the minimum-norm-interpolant theory predicts [6].

**Spectral bias is visible.** Networks trained on targets with mixed frequencies fit low frequencies first; the NTK spectrum explains why: gradient flow along kernel principal components learns large-eigenvalue (smooth) directions fastest [1].

A compact systems view of the kernel-regression equivalence can be expressed in Rust:

```rust
// Kernel gradient flow: df/dt = -eta * K (f - y), K = NTK Gram matrix
fn kernel_flow(k: &DMatrix<f64>, y: &DVector<f64>, f0: &DVector<f64>, eta: f64, t: f64) -> DVector<f64> {
    // eigendecomposition K = Q Lambda Q^T; closed-form solution of the linear ODE
    let eig = k.symmetric_eigen();
    let q = eig.eigenvectors;
    let lambda = eig.eigenvalues;
    let residual = f0 - y;
    let coeffs = q.transpose() * residual;
    let decayed: DVector<f64> = coeffs.iter().zip(lambda.iter())
        .map(|(c, l)| c * (-eta * l * t).exp())
        .collect();
    y + q * decayed
}
```

##6 Limitations

Intellectual honesty requires cataloguing where the geometric picture frays.

- **The kernel regime is not the whole story.** Real networks *learn features*: their internal representations change substantially during training, which a frozen kernel cannot capture. The NTK describes lazy training well but under-describes practical deep learning, where finite-width networks outperform their NTK linearizations [7].
- **Finite-width departures.** At practical widths the empirical NTK drifts during training; the Gram matrix's smallest eigenvalue — the convergence-rate bottleneck — can collapse, and the polynomial width bounds [3][8], while rigorous, demand widths far beyond practice.
- **Architectural gaps.** Convolutional and attention NTKs exist, but their analytic forms are intricate and their predictions track real CNNs and Transformers less tightly than the fully connected case tracks MLPs.
- **Double descent is conditional.** The second descent requires the right inductive bias — minimum-norm interpolation in a suitable space. With heavy label noise, adversarial corruption, or misspecified kernels, the peak can dominate and the second descent may never materialize [2][6].
- **Computational cost.** The NTK Gram matrix is n×n; exact kernel regression scales cubically in dataset size, so the theory's cleanest object is often computationally inaccessible at scale.
- **Mean-field versus lazy tension.** The mean-field view [7], in which parameters move by order-one amounts and the kernel evolves, is arguably the more faithful description of feature learning — yet its global convergence guarantees are weaker and its geometry less understood than the NTK's. Reconciling the two regimes remains open.

##7 Conclusion

The geometry of deep learning is the story of how *width tames non-convexity*. The neural tangent kernel [1] reveals training as kernel gradient descent in disguise — convex in function space, exponentially convergent, spectrally ordered. Over-parameterized optimization theory [3][8] converts this asymptotic insight into finite-width guarantees. The loss landscape, far from a minefield, is a connected web of flat, interchangeable minima [4][5]. And double descent [2][6] shows that the interpolating regime, far from being a failure mode, is where modern models do their best work — provided the implicit bias selects smooth interpolants.

The frontier is the territory the kernel cannot reach: *feature learning*. The mean-field perspective [7] and finite-width corrections to the NTK are the active battleground, and the prize is a theory that explains not just why deep networks can be trained, but why the features they learn are the right ones. Until then, the NTK remains the sharpest lens we have on the geometry of deep learning — a fixed point of clarity in a landscape that, it turns out, was never as rugged as it looked.
