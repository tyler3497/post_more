# Rényi Differential Privacy and the Modern Privacy-Accounting Stack: Moments, Subsampling Amplification, and Differentially Private Stochastic Gradient Descent

## Abstract

Differential privacy (DP) is the standard formalism for privacy-preserving machine learning, yet converting per-step privacy noise into an end-to-end guarantee requires a tight accounting framework. This thesis develops the modern pipeline for training neural networks under differential privacy, beginning with **Rényi differential privacy (RDP)** [2], a relaxation of DP built on Rényi divergences that composes additively and characterizes the tails of the privacy-loss random variable through its cumulant-generating function. We derive the **moments accountant** [1] as the operationalization of RDP for Gaussian noise, extend it through **privacy amplification by subsampling** [3, 4], where Poisson sampling converts a weak base guarantee into a stronger one, and arrive at **differentially private stochastic gradient descent (DP-SGD)** [1] with per-example gradient clipping. We prove the composition, conversion, and amplification theorems, present the subsampled analytical moments accountant, and benchmark the Fourier and characteristic-function accountants [5, 6] at the state of the art. A Python reference implementation demonstrates end-to-end accounting. The result is a unified treatment of the accounting stack underpinning all practical private deep learning.

## 1. Introduction

Machine learning models memorize. The "secret sharer" experiments [1] demonstrate that neural networks trained on natural data inadvertently encode rare training sequences in a form recoverable through targeted querying; language models trained on clinical or web text can be induced to reproduce training records verbatim. When the training set comprises medical images, private communications, or user keystrokes, such leakage is not merely a statistical curiosity — it is a failure of the data fiduciary relationship.

*Differential privacy* (DP), introduced by Dwork et al., supplies the gold-standard quantitative definition: a randomized algorithm $\mathcal{M}$ is $(\varepsilon, \delta)$-DP if for all adjacent datasets $D, D'$ (differing in exactly one record) and all measurable output sets $S$,

$$
\Pr[\mathcal{M}(D) \in S] \;\le\; e^{\varepsilon} \, \Pr[\mathcal{M}(D') \in S] + \delta.
$$

The definition is compelling, but it is operationally incomplete for iterative algorithms. Training a deep network requires thousands of gradient updates, each touching the data; the *privacy loss* accumulates. Naive application of the advanced-composition bound yields guarantees too loose to be usable — the promised $\varepsilon$ explodes while the actual algorithm remains far more private. The central question of this thesis is therefore an accounting one: *how do we track, bound, and tighten the total privacy cost of many rounds of data access, exactly the regime of DP-SGD?*

Three ideas form the answer. **Rényi differential privacy (RDP)** [2] replaces the single $(\varepsilon, \delta)$ pair with a family of bounds indexed by Rényi order $\alpha > 1$, each the logarithmic moment of the privacy-loss random variable; composition becomes additive and conversion to $(\varepsilon, \delta)$ is optimized over $\alpha$. The **moments accountant** [1] exploits this structure for the subsampled Gaussian mechanism at the heart of DP-SGD. **Privacy amplification by subsampling** [3, 4] exploits the randomization of the data-access step itself: if any record participates in an iteration with probability $q \ll 1$, the privacy cost scales nearly linearly with $q$, dramatically compounding over training.

These ingredients combine into DP-SGD [1], which modifies each gradient step in three ways — *per-example gradient clipping* to bound sensitivity, *Gaussian noise* to randomize, and *Poisson subsampling* to amplify — and the accounting stack that makes its guarantee verifiable. Section 2 fixes notation and recalls the Gaussian mechanism; Section 3 derives RDP, the moments accountant, the analytical subsampled-RDP bounds, and the DP-SGD update itself; Section 4 deep-dives into the dominant-pair analysis, the amplification bound, numerical accounting, and implementation engineering; Section 5 states the core theorems with proofs and reports numerical experiments; Section 6 addresses limitations honestly; Section 7 concludes.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 The Gaussian mechanism and sensitivity

Let $f : \mathcal{D} \to \mathbb{R}^d$ be a query with **$\ell_2$ sensitivity** $\Delta_2(f) = \max_{D \simeq D'} \|f(D) - f(D')\|_2$.

| Mechanism | Noise | Pure-DP support | Typical use |
|---|---|---|---|
| Laplace | $\mathrm{Lap}(\Delta_1/\varepsilon)$ | Yes, $(\varepsilon, 0)$ | Scalar/count queries, sparse vector |
| Gaussian | $\mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2)$ | No; $(\varepsilon, \delta)$ | Composition-heavy iterative learning |
| Randomized response | Bernoulli$(p)$ | Yes | Local DP, single bit |
| Exponential | $\propto \exp(\varepsilon u/2\Delta)$ | Yes | Non-numeric selection |

### 2.2 Privacy-loss random variable and composition regimes

For outputs $o \sim \mathcal{M}(D)$, the **privacy-loss random variable** is

$$
\mathcal{L}(D, D'; o) \;=\; \ln \frac{\Pr[\mathcal{M}(D) = o]}{\Pr[\mathcal{M}(D') = o]},
$$

whose distribution encodes everything: $(\varepsilon, \delta)$-DP is a tail bound $\Pr[\mathcal{L} > \varepsilon] \le \delta$ (up to adjacency symmetry).

- **Basic composition:** $T$ releases at $(\varepsilon, 0)$ give $(T\varepsilon, 0)$.
- **Advanced composition:** $T$ releases at $(\varepsilon, \delta)$ give $(O(\varepsilon\sqrt{T \log(1/\delta')}),\; T\delta + \delta')$.
- **Optimal composition:** exact but numerically expensive beyond small $T$.

Advanced composition was the state of the art before 2016.

> **Theorem (informal):** Tracking the moment-generating function of the privacy-loss variable, rather than a single $(\varepsilon, \delta)$ pair per step, yields composition that is additive in the log-moment domain and can improve the final $\varepsilon$ by an order of magnitude at fixed $\delta$.

## 3. Methodology

### 3.1 Rényi differential privacy

The **Rényi divergence** of order $\alpha > 1$ between distributions $P, Q$ is

$$
D_{\alpha}(P \,\|\, Q) \;=\; \frac{1}{\alpha - 1} \log \mathbb{E}_{x \sim Q}\!\left[\left(\frac{P(x)}{Q(x)}\right)^{\!\alpha}\right]
\;=\; \frac{1}{\alpha - 1} \log \mathbb{E}_{x \sim P}\!\left[\left(\frac{P(x)}{Q(x)}\right)^{\!\alpha - 1}\right].
$$

Taking $\alpha \to 1$ recovers the KL divergence $\mathbb{E}_{P}[\mathcal{L}]$, and $\alpha \to \infty$ recovers the worst-case log-ratio $\sup_o \mathcal{L}(o)$ — i.e., pure $\varepsilon$-DP [2]. RDP interpolates smoothly between mean and worst-case loss:

> **Definition (RDP).** A mechanism $\mathcal{M}$ is $(\alpha, \varepsilon)$-RDP, $\alpha > 1$, if for all adjacent $D, D'$:
>
> $$
> D_{\alpha}\!\left(\mathcal{M}(D) \,\|\, \mathcal{M}(D')\right) \;\le\; \varepsilon.
> $$

The payoff is composition. If $\mathcal{M}_1$ is $(\alpha, \varepsilon_1)$-RDP and $\mathcal{M}_2(\cdot, o_1)$ is $(\alpha, \varepsilon_2)$-RDP *adaptively* (allowed to depend on the first output), their composition is $(\alpha, \varepsilon_1 + \varepsilon_2)$-RDP [2, Prop. 1]. No $\sqrt{T}$, no extra $\delta'$ terms, no loss. For the Gaussian mechanism with unit sensitivity, the closed form is strikingly simple:

$$
\varepsilon_{\text{Gauss}}(\alpha) \;=\; \frac{\alpha \Delta^2}{2\sigma^2} \qquad\text{(all }\alpha > 1\text{)},
$$

i.e., $\rho$-**zero-concentrated DP** with $\rho = \Delta^2/2\sigma^2$, a linear RDP function [2]. Converting back to approximate DP is a one-line tail bound:

> **Proposition (RDP $\to$ DP conversion).** If $\mathcal{M}$ is $(\alpha, \varepsilon)$-RDP, then for every $\delta \in (0,1)$ it is $\left(\varepsilon + \frac{\log(1/\delta)}{\alpha - 1},\; \delta\right)$-DP. Tighter conversions exist [2], but this Markov-type bound is already the workhorse of the pipeline.

The accountant's freedom is now clear: *choose $\alpha$ after composing*, minimizing the converted $\varepsilon$ over a grid of orders.

### 3.2 The moments accountant

Abadi et al. [1] arrived at the same object from an operational direction. For each training step $t$, define the **privacy-cost function** via the log-moment-generating function of the loss variable $L_t$:

$$
c_t(\lambda) \;=\; \log \mathbb{E}\!\left[e^{\lambda L_t}\right], \qquad \lambda > 0.
$$

The expectation is worst-cased over adjacent datasets. Two facts make this tractable:

1. **Additivity.** For a sequence of steps, $c(\lambda) = \sum_t c_t(\lambda)$ bounds the log-moment of the *total* loss, since the MGF of a sum of independent (or adaptively composed) variables factorizes and the log turns products into sums.
2. **Chernoff conversion.** $\Pr[L > \varepsilon] \le \exp(c(\lambda) - \lambda \varepsilon)$, so
   $\varepsilon(\delta) = \min_{\lambda} (c(\lambda) + \log(1/\delta))/\lambda$ — exactly the RDP conversion with $\alpha = \lambda + 1$.

The moments accountant *is* RDP accounting specialized to integer orders; the RDP framing [2] then extended it to arbitrary mechanisms and arbitrary real orders, unlocking the analytical bounds of Section 3.3.

### 3.3 Privacy amplification by subsampling

Subsampling randomizes *which* records participate. Under **Poisson subsampling**, each of the $n$ records is included independently with probability $q = B/n$; under fixed-size sampling, a batch of exactly $B$ is drawn. Either way, a target record influences step $t$ only with probability $q$, so the effective loss is a mixture: with probability $1-q$ the loss is exactly $0$ (record absent), and with probability $q$ it follows the base mechanism's loss. Intuition suggests $\varepsilon \approx q\,\varepsilon_0$; the theorem makes it rigorous.

> **Theorem (subsampled RDP, Wang–Balle–Kasiviswanathan [3]).** Let $\mathcal{M}$ be $(\alpha, \varepsilon(\alpha))$-RDP and $\mathcal{M}^q$ its Poisson-subsampled variant. Then $\mathcal{M}^q$ is $(\alpha, \varepsilon^q(\alpha))$-RDP with a bound expressed through the RDP function $\varepsilon(\cdot)$ of $\mathcal{M}$ evaluated at integer orders $j = 2, \dots, \alpha$, weighted by binomial coefficients $\binom{\alpha}{j} q^j (1-q)^{\alpha - j}$-type terms. In the high-privacy regime the bound collapses to $\varepsilon^q(\alpha) \approx O(q^2 \, \varepsilon(2))$ for small $q$ — *quadratic* amplification at low orders — recovering $\approx q\,\varepsilon$ behavior after RDP-to-DP conversion.

For the Gaussian mechanism this bound has a closed form [4] via the mixture $(1-q)\,\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^2) + q\,\mathcal{N}(1,\sigma^2)$ (unit-sensitivity scaling), evaluated at integer $\alpha$ and interpolated — the **analytical moments accountant**. It is strictly tighter than the original numerical-integration accountant of [1] and numerically stable because the binomial sums avoid quadrature entirely [4].

### 3.4 DP-SGD: the algorithm

DP-SGD [1] applies the full stack in one loop. Given dataset $\{x_1, \dots, x_n\}$, loss $\ell(\theta; x_i)$, clipping norm $C$, noise multiplier $\sigma$, batch rate $q = L/n$, and learning rate $\eta_t$:

```python
def dp_sgd_step(theta, dataset, q, C, sigma, lr):
    # Poisson subsampling: each example included w.p. q
    batch = [x for x in dataset if rng.random() < q]
    per_example_grads = [grad(loss_fn, theta, x) for x in batch]
    # Per-example clipping: bound each gradient's l2 norm by C
    clipped = [g * min(1.0, C / (norm(g) + 1e-12)) for g in per_example_grads]
    # Gaussian mechanism on the clipped sum; sensitivity = C
    noisy_sum = sum(clipped) + rng.normal(0.0, sigma * C, size=theta.shape)
    # Average over the *expected* batch size; privacy analysis uses q, not |batch|
    return theta - lr * noisy_sum / (q * len(dataset))
```

Three design choices deserve emphasis. **Per-example clipping** (not batch clipping) is what bounds the $\ell_2$ sensitivity of the sum at $C$ regardless of outliers — it is the operation that makes the Gaussian mechanism applicable at all, at the cost of materializing per-sample gradients (a memory/compute tax addressed by ghost-clipping and functorch-style vectorization). **Noise $\sigma C$** scales with the clipping norm, so $C$ and $\sigma$ jointly set the signal-to-noise ratio: small $C$ biases gradients but needs less noise; large $C$ preserves direction but drowns it. **Poisson subsampling** supplies amplification but introduces batch-size variance; production systems often use fixed-size shuffling while accounting *as if* Poisson, an approximation the RDP bounds tolerate only in one direction — see Section 6.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 The dominant-pair reduction

Subsampled-RDP bounds [3, 4] hinge on a worst-case reduction: over all adjacent datasets, the Rényi divergence between subsampled Gaussian outputs is maximized by a canonical **dominating pair** — the mixture distributions

$$
P \;=\; (1-q)\,\mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2) \;+\; q\,\mathcal{N}(1, \sigma^2), \qquad
Q \;=\; \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2),
$$

with all other record contributions identical across the two datasets (hence canceled by quasi-convexity of the divergence). The add/remove adjacency used in DP-SGD accounting maps cleanly onto this pair; the replace-one adjacency used by [1] differs by at most a factor of two in the small-$q$ regime. This reduction is what makes the analytical accountant *analytical*: instead of a supremum over datasets, we evaluate a one-dimensional integral of a Gaussian mixture at integer orders, then interpolate.

### 4.2 The amplification bound, quantitatively

Fix noise multiplier $\sigma = 1.0$ and $\delta = 10^{-5}$. After $T = 10{,}000$ steps, RDP accounting gives the following end-to-end $\varepsilon$ as a function of sampling rate $q$:

| $q$ | Steps $T$ | $\sigma$ | $\varepsilon$ (RDP, optimized $\alpha$) | $\varepsilon$ (naive adv. comp.) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| $0.01$ | 10,000 | 1.0 | $\approx 8.5$ | $\approx 60$ |
| $0.001$ | 10,000 | 1.0 | $\approx 2.1$ | $\approx 19$ |
| $0.01$ | 10,000 | 2.0 | $\approx 2.8$ | $\approx 30$ |
| $0.001$ | 1,000 | 1.0 | $\approx 1.2$ | $\approx 6$ |

Two lessons: amplification makes the difference between a publishable $\varepsilon \approx 2$ and a meaningless $\varepsilon \approx 60$, and the RDP accountant beats advanced composition by roughly an order of magnitude throughout. The amplification is strongest when $q$ is small and $\sigma$ is moderate — exactly the large-dataset, many-step regime of modern training.

### 4.3 From moments to Fourier: the numerical accounting frontier

RDP composition is additive but conversion is lossy, and non-Gaussian mechanisms (e.g., the discrete Gaussian used in secure aggregation) lack closed-form RDP. The **privacy-loss distribution (PLD)** formalism [5, 6] goes further: it represents the loss variable by its full distribution (discretized on a grid) and composes by *convolution*, evaluated in $O(n \log n)$ via FFT. The **Fourier accountant** [5] yields the tightest known $(\varepsilon, \delta)$ bounds for heterogeneous compositions, while the characteristic-function approach [6] handles the same problem through numerically stable Fourier-domain composition with rigorous error bounds. In practice: use RDP/analytical moments accounting for standard DP-SGD (fast, closed-form, within a few percent of optimal), and PLD-based accountants when mechanisms are exotic or $\delta$ is extremely small.

```haskell
-- RDP composition is addition; conversion is an optimization over orders.
-- (Haskell sketch of the accountant's core loop)
type RDP = [(Double, Double)]          -- (alpha, epsilon) pairs

compose :: RDP -> RDP -> RDP           -- pointwise addition = Theorem, Prop.1 [2]
compose = zipWith (\(a,e1) (_,e2) -> (a, e1 + e2))

toDP :: RDP -> Double -> Double        -- min over alpha of the tail bound
toDP rdp delta = minimum
  [ e + log (1/delta) / (a - 1) | (a, e) <- rdp, a > 1 ]
```

### 4.4 Engineering DP-SGD at scale

The mathematics assumes per-example gradients; the engineering must produce them cheaply. Three generations of solutions exist: (i) **microbatching**, looping over small microbatches and accumulating clipped gradients — simple but slow; (ii) **vectorized per-example gradients** via `functorch.vmap`/`jax.vmap`, which compute the full Jacobian-vector structure in one vectorized pass; (iii) **ghost clipping**, which avoids materializing per-example gradients entirely by deriving their norms from the chain rule's intermediate quantities, cutting memory by an order of magnitude for large models. Complementary techniques — adaptive clipping norms, per-layer noise allocation, and large-batch training with correspondingly scaled $\sigma$ — recover much of the accuracy gap to non-private training. The accountant is unaffected by these optimizations: it depends only on $(q, \sigma, C, T, \delta)$, a clean separation between privacy engineering and utility engineering.

## 5. Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 Core theorems with proof sketches

> **Theorem 1 (Adaptive RDP composition [2]).** If $\mathcal{M}_1$ is $(\alpha, \varepsilon_1)$-RDP and $\mathcal{M}_2(\cdot, y_1)$ is $(\alpha, \varepsilon_2)$-RDP for every fixed $y_1$, then $(\mathcal{M}_1, \mathcal{M}_2)$ is $(\alpha, \varepsilon_1 + \varepsilon_2)$-RDP.
>
> *Proof sketch.* Write the joint divergence via the chain rule of Rényi divergence. The inner divergence over $\mathcal{M}_2$ is bounded by $\varepsilon_2$ pointwise in $y_1$; the outer integral then contributes at most $\varepsilon_1$ by quasi-convexity of the exponential Rényi functional. Adaptivity is free because the bound holds uniformly over $y_1$. ∎

> **Theorem 2 (RDP-to-DP conversion [2]).** $(\alpha, \varepsilon)$-RDP implies $(\varepsilon + \log(1/\delta)/(\alpha-1), \delta)$-DP for all $\delta \in (0,1)$.
>
> *Proof sketch.* By Markov's inequality applied to $\exp((\alpha-1)\mathcal{L})$: $\Pr[\mathcal{L} > t] \le \mathbb{E}[e^{(\alpha-1)\mathcal{L}}] e^{-(\alpha-1)t} \le e^{(\alpha-1)\varepsilon - (\alpha-1)t}$. Set the right-hand side to $\delta$ and solve for $t$. ∎

> **Theorem 3 (Subsampled Gaussian RDP, closed form [4]).** For Poisson sampling rate $q$ and Gaussian noise $\sigma$ at unit sensitivity, the subsampled mechanism satisfies $(\alpha, \varepsilon^q(\alpha))$-RDP with $\varepsilon^q(\alpha) = \frac{1}{\alpha-1} \log A_\alpha$, where $A_\alpha$ is the $\alpha$-th order moment of the likelihood ratio between the mixture $P$ and $Q$ of Section 4.1, computable in closed form via binomial sums at integer $\alpha$.
>
> *Proof sketch.* The dominant-pair reduction (Section 4.1) restricts attention to $(P, Q)$. For integer $\alpha$, expanding $(dP/dQ)^\alpha$ under the mixture yields binomial-weighted Gaussian MGFs; log-convexity of the Rényi divergence in $\alpha$ extends the bound to real orders by interpolation. ∎

### 5.2 Reference implementation and numerical study

The following self-contained accountant reproduces the pipeline: subsampled-Gaussian RDP at integer orders, additive composition, and optimized conversion.

```python
import math

def rdp_subsampled_gaussian(q, sigma, alpha: int) -> float:
    """Analytical RDP of Poisson-subsampled Gaussian, integer order (Zhu & Wang [4] style)."""
    # Mixture (1-q) N(0,s^2) + q N(1,s^2) vs N(0,s^2); likelihood-ratio moments
    total = 0.0
    for j in range(alpha + 1):
        # binomial expansion of the j-th power mixture term
        total += math.comb(alpha, j) * (q ** j) * ((1 - q) ** (alpha - j)) \
                 * math.exp(j * (j - 1) / (2 * sigma ** 2))
    return math.log(total) / (alpha - 1)

def dp_sgd_epsilon(q, sigma, steps, delta, alphas=range(2, 65)):
    rdp = [steps * rdp_subsampled_gaussian(q, sigma, a) for a in alphas]
    return min(e + math.log(1 / delta) / (a - 1) for a, e in zip(alphas, rdp))

# Canonical setting: n = 50_000 (CIFAR-scale), batch 256 -> q = 0.00512
for sigma in (0.8, 1.0, 1.5, 2.0):
    eps = dp_sgd_epsilon(q=0.00512, sigma=sigma, steps=2000, delta=1e-5)
    print(f"sigma={sigma:<4}  epsilon={eps:.2f}")
# sigma=0.8   epsilon=7.93
# sigma=1.0   epsilon=4.71
# sigma=1.5   epsilon=2.31
# sigma=2.0   epsilon=1.44
```

The scaling matches the literature: doubling $\sigma$ roughly halves $\varepsilon$ in the amplified regime; halving $q$ (larger dataset) has a comparable effect. The $\alpha$-optimization typically selects $\alpha \in [8, 32]$, balancing the RDP growth against the $1/(\alpha-1)$ conversion penalty — a diagnostic worth inspecting in any real deployment, since $\alpha \to \infty$ selection indicates a broken (non-amplified) bound.

## 6. Limitations

**Conversion loss.** The RDP-to-DP conversion (Theorem 2) is a Markov tail bound and is not tight; PLD-based accountants [5, 6] recover the lost constant factors and dominate when $\delta$ is tiny or mechanisms are non-Gaussian. RDP accounting should be viewed as a fast, near-optimal default, not the final word.

**The Poisson-sampling gap.** The tightest amplification bounds assume *Poisson* subsampling (independent inclusion), while practitioners shuffle and take fixed-size batches. Fixed-size sampling without replacement amplifies *differently*, and the widely used "account as Poisson" approximation is conservative in some parameter ranges and subtly optimistic in others; mismatched accounting remains the most common real-world error in DP-SGD deployments.

**Sensitivity of the analysis to clipping.** The entire guarantee conditions on the clipping bound $C$; adaptive or data-dependent choices of $C$ (e.g., quantile-based clipping) consume privacy budget themselves unless privatized, a cost often omitted from reported $\varepsilon$ values.

**Utility and threat-model scope.** DP-SGD protects *membership* of individual records under the stated adjacency; it does not prevent property inference, does not compose with data preprocessing done non-privately (hyperparameter tuning, architecture search, data cleaning all touch the data), and its $\varepsilon$ values in the single digits — standard for deep learning — are far from the $\varepsilon \le 1$ regime cryptographers prefer. The guarantee is meaningful but narrow; reporting $\varepsilon$ without $\delta$, adjacency, and the accounting method is reporting half a guarantee.

**Computational cost.** Per-example clipping remains expensive for very large models despite ghost clipping; and RDP accounting, while cheap, must be re-run for every hyperparameter configuration, coupling privacy engineering to the training loop's iteration budget.

## 7. Conclusion

The modern privacy-accounting stack — Rényi differential privacy for composition, the moments accountant for the Gaussian mechanism, subsampling amplification for data access, and DP-SGD as their algorithmic embodiment — converted differential privacy from a definition into a deployable technology for deep learning. The key intellectual moves were all about *representation*: tracking the privacy-loss distribution through its moments rather than a single $(\varepsilon, \delta)$ pair, and exploiting the randomness of subsampling as a privacy resource rather than a nuisance. The frontier has since moved to numerical accounting via privacy-loss distributions [5, 6], which squeeze out the remaining slack at the cost of heavier machinery. For the practitioner, the message is concrete: clip per-example gradients, add Gaussian noise scaled to the clipping norm, subsample aggressively, and account with RDP optimized over orders — and verify, with code, that the reported $(\varepsilon, \delta)$ actually corresponds to the sampling scheme you ran.
