# The Number Field Sieve: Index Calculus, Polynomial Selection and the Sieving Pipeline, and the Security of Discrete-Logarithm Cryptography

## Abstract

The discrete logarithm problem in finite fields underpins Diffie–Hellman key exchange, DSA, ElGamal encryption, and a generation of pairing-based protocols, yet its classical hardness is governed by index calculus — in its most refined form, the Number Field Sieve (NFS). This article presents a self-contained, research-level treatment of NFS for discrete logarithms: the algebraic number theory lifting finite-field arithmetic into number fields, the four-phase pipeline of polynomial selection, relation collection by sieving, sparse linear algebra, and individual-logarithm descent, and the asymptotic calculus yielding the celebrated heuristic complexity $L_Q(1/3, (64/9)^{1/3}) \approx L_Q(1/3, 1.923)$ in prime fields. We develop the modern polynomial-selection landscape — Joux–Lercier, generalized Joux–Lercier, Conjugation, and the Multiple and Tower variants — as a study in trading norm sizes against degree, and trace the empirical record from the 431-bit computation of 2005 through the 795-bit DLP-240 record of 2019 to the quasi-polynomial breakthroughs in small characteristic. We close with the security analysis: what the NFS complexity curve implies for Diffie–Hellman parameter choices, the Logjam lesson, and the re-dimensioning of pairing-friendly curves.

---

## 1 Introduction

The security of public-key cryptography rests, in its classical formulation, on a small number of ostensibly hard problems: integer factorization, the discrete logarithm problem (DLP), and the presumed hardness of lattice and isogeny problems in the post-quantum era. Of these, the discrete logarithm problem in finite fields occupies a peculiar position. On the one hand, it admits generic algorithms — baby-step giant-step and Pollard's rho — that run in time $O(\sqrt{q})$ in any group of order $q$ and are essentially optimal in the generic group model [1]. On the other hand, the rich algebraic structure of finite fields admits *index calculus* methods that exploit smoothness to achieve subexponential complexity, demolishing the square-root barrier for every concrete instantiation of the DLP in $\mathbb{F}_p^*$ and $\mathbb{F}_{p^n}^*$.

The historical arc is instructive. Kraitchik's early index-calculus ideas were systematized by Adleman into an $L(1/2)$ algorithm for prime fields; Coppersmith then gave a characteristic-two variant of similar shape; and in 1993 Gordon adapted the Number Field Sieve — originally a factoring algorithm due to Lenstra, Lenstra, Manasse, and Pollard — to discrete logarithms, achieving the first $L(1/3)$ algorithm for the DLP in prime fields [6]. Schirokauer subsequently resolved the unit-group obstruction that had limited Gordon's approach, establishing the modern complexity constant $(64/9)^{1/3} \approx 1.923$ that has stood for a quarter century [2].

> **Theorem (Gordon–Schirokauer, heuristic).** *The discrete logarithm problem in the prime field $\mathbb{F}_p$ can be solved in heuristic expected time $L_p(1/3, (64/9)^{1/3} + o(1))$, where $L_Q(\alpha, c) = \exp\big((c + o(1))(\log Q)^\alpha (\log \log Q)^{1-\alpha}\big)$.*

Since then, the field has advanced through *polynomial selection* — Joux–Lercier (2003), the generalized Joux–Lercier and Conjugation methods [3], Multiple NFS [4], and Tower NFS for pairing-relevant fields [5] — while in small characteristic Joux's $L(1/4+o(1))$ algorithm and the quasi-polynomial $n^{O(\log n)}$ descent of Barbulescu–Gaudry–Joux–Thomé [1] effectively retired binary-field DLP cryptography.

This article proceeds as follows. Section 2 recalls the index-calculus skeleton and the algebraic number theory it requires. Section 3 describes the four-phase NFS methodology. Section 4 dives into the three technically deepest components: polynomial selection, the sieving pipeline, and linear algebra with Schirokauer maps, plus individual-logarithm descent. Section 5 gives the asymptotic proof sketch and the empirical record. Section 6 discusses limitations and open problems, and Section 7 draws the cryptographic conclusions.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Index calculus: the common skeleton

Every index-calculus algorithm for the DLP in a finite field $\mathbb{F}_Q$ follows the same four movements [6]:

1. **Factor base.** Choose a set $\mathcal{F}$ of "small" field elements (or prime ideals) whose discrete logarithms will be computed first.
2. **Relation collection.** Find many multiplicative relations $\prod_{\mathfrak{p} \in \mathcal{F}} \mathfrak{p}^{e_{\mathfrak{p}}} = g^{e_0}$ among factor-base elements, each yielding a linear equation $\sum e_{\mathfrak{p}} \log \mathfrak{p} = e_0 \log g$ over $\mathbb{Z}/\ell\mathbb{Z}$, where $\ell$ is the prime order of the subgroup of interest.
3. **Linear algebra.** Solve the resulting sparse linear system to obtain the logarithms of all factor-base elements — the *precomputation*, whose cost is amortized over many target logarithms.
4. **Individual logarithm (descent).** Express an arbitrary target $t$ as a product of factor-base elements (a "smooth descent"), then read off $\log t$.

The efficiency of the entire enterprise is controlled by a single probabilistic phenomenon: *smoothness*. An integer (or ideal norm) is $B$-smooth if all its prime factors are at most $B$. The fundamental heuristic estimate is the Canfield–Erdős–Pomerance theorem:

> **Theorem (Canfield–Erdős–Pomerance).** *Let $\psi(x, y)$ count the $y$-smooth integers up to $x$, and write $u = \log x / \log y$. Then $\psi(x,y)/x = u^{-u(1+o(1))} = \rho(u)(1+o(1))$ as $u \to \infty$, where $\rho$ is Dickman's function.*

In short: NFS is a machine for manufacturing *small* integers — norms of algebraic integers — whose smoothness probability $u^{-u}$ is thereby maximized.

### 2.2 The number-field lifting

Let the target field be $\mathbb{F}_{p^n}$ of cardinality $Q = p^n$. NFS begins by selecting two integer polynomials $f_1, f_2 \in \mathbb{Z}[x]$ and an integer $m$ such that both polynomials vanish at $m$ modulo $p$ (for $n = 1$) — or, more generally, such that they share an irreducible factor $\varphi$ of degree $n$ modulo $p$ [2][3]. Writing $\theta_i$ for a complex root of $f_i$, we obtain number fields $K_i = \mathbb{Q}(\theta_i)$ and the celebrated commutative diagram:

![Number field sieve commutative diagram](/thesis/ths_1789115348847_04281-0.webp)

*Figure 1. The NFS commutative diagram: $\mathbb{Z}[x]$ maps onto two number fields via $x \mapsto \theta_i$, which in turn map onto the target finite field $\mathbb{F}_{p^n}$ via $\theta_i \mapsto m$ (reduction modulo a prime above $p$). Relations are collected upstairs as smooth principal ideals and pushed downstairs into the finite field.*

For coprime $a, b$, the element $a - b\theta_i \in K_i$ has norm $N(a-b\theta_i) = b^{d_i} f_i(a/b) =: F_i(a,b)$, the *norm form*. When $(a-b\theta_i)$ factors over small prime ideals on *both* sides, we obtain a relation in $\mathbb{F}_{p^n}$ — both sides map to the same $a-bm$ — up to units and class-group obstructions, which Schirokauer maps resolve [2].

## 3 Methodology: The Four-Phase Pipeline

We now describe the pipeline in the generality of $\mathbb{F}_{p^n}$, following the modern presentation of Barbulescu et al. [3] and the lecture synthesis [6].

### Phase 1 — Polynomial selection

Choose $f_1, f_2$ and the common root $m \in \mathbb{F}_{p^n}$. The desiderata are contradictory: the polynomials should have *small coefficients* (so norms $F_i(a,b)$ are small and smooth often) but must share the degree-$n$ factor $\varphi$ modulo $p$ (which forces some largeness). The entire modern literature of Section 4.1 is devoted to navigating this trade-off.

### Phase 2 — Relation collection (sieving)

Fix a smoothness bound $B$ and region $\mathcal{A} = \{(a,b): |a|,|b| \le A\}$. For each prime ideal below $B$, mark the sublattice with $\mathfrak{q} \mid (a-b\theta_i)$; survivors have *both* norms $B$-smooth. **Lattice sieving** (Pollard) with the **special-$\mathbf{q}$** technique — sieving one medium prime ideal's sublattice at a time — localizes memory access and parallelizes across cores [6].

### Phase 3 — Linear algebra

The relations form a sparse matrix $M$ over $\mathbb{Z}/\ell\mathbb{Z}$ with roughly $\pi(B)$ rows and columns. Because $\ell$ is large (not small characteristic), structured Gaussian elimination is infeasible; instead one uses **block Wiedemann** (Coppersmith) or **block Lanczos** (Montgomery), which find kernel vectors using only matrix–vector products. The relations are not quite over the field: each relation holds only up to units and class-group elements, and **Schirokauer maps** — computable homomorphisms from the unit group into $\mu_\ell$ — append extra columns that absorb this ambiguity [2].

### Phase 4 — Individual logarithm by descent

Given a target $t$, lift it to the number fields and seek a representation with norms smooth over a generous bound, then recursively "descend" large prime ideals via special-$\mathfrak{q}$ sieving until only factor-base elements remain [1][7]. Descent is asymptotically cheaper than precomputation — the asymmetry exploited by Logjam-style attacks.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Polynomial selection: an optimization geometry

Polynomial selection is the most mathematically distinctive phase of NFS-DLP. The classical **base-$m$ method** (for prime fields) writes $p$ in base $m \approx p^{1/(d+1)}$ and takes $f_1$ to be the base-$m$ expansion polynomial (degree $d$, coefficients $\approx m$) and $f_2(x) = x - m$. The norm sizes are then roughly $A^{d/2} m^{d/2}$ on the algebraic side — unbalanced and asymptotically suboptimal.

**Joux–Lercier (2003).** Choose $f_1$ of degree $d$ with *tiny* coefficients and a root $m$ mod $p$; find $f_2$ of degree $d+1$ with the same root via **LLL** on the lattice of polynomials vanishing at $m$ mod $p$ (determinant $p$, dimension $d+1$), giving $\|f_2\|_\infty \approx p^{1/(d+1)}$ — far better balanced than base-$m$ [3][6].

**Generalized Joux–Lercier (GJL)** and the **Conjugation method** (Barbulescu–Gaudry–Guillevic–Morain, 2015) extend this to extension fields $\mathbb{F}_{p^n}$ [3]. GJL picks $f_1$ of degree $d+1 > n$ with tiny coefficients possessing an irreducible degree-$n$ factor $\varphi$ mod $p$, then uses lattice reduction to find $f_2$ of degree $d$ sharing $\varphi$. Conjugation instead exploits polynomials of the shape $g(x) = \operatorname{Res}_y(f(y), \varphi(x) - y)$-like constructions with an auxiliary quadratic, achieving coefficient sizes $\approx \sqrt{p}$ on one side — optimal in the medium-characteristic boundary regime.

**Multiple NFS (MNFS)** (Pierrot, 2015) builds $V$ number fields sharing $m$ and sieves them simultaneously, giving each $(a,b)$ $V$ chances at double smoothness [4]; with Conjugation it attains the best known medium-characteristic complexity:

$$L_{p^n}\!\left(1/3,\ \left(\tfrac{8(9+4\sqrt{6})}{15}\right)^{\!1/3}\right) \approx L_{p^n}(1/3,\ 2.156),$$

improving on NFS-with-Conjugation at $\approx 2.201$ [4].

**Tower NFS (TNFS)** (Schirokauer's tower, revisited by Kim–Barbulescu for composite extension degree $n$) builds a *tower* of number fields $\mathbb{Q} \subset K_1 \subset K_2$ to represent $\mathbb{F}_{p^n}$ when $n$ is composite, shrinking norms dramatically for pairing-friendly fields [5]. Sarkar and Singh gave a unified polynomial-selection framework subsuming both GJL and Conjugation, with worked-out asymptotics for NFS and MNFS [5].

| Method | Year | Degrees $(d_1, d_2)$ | Coefficient scale | Best regime |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Base-$m$ | 1993 | $(d, 1)$ | $p^{1/(d+1)}$ on one side | Prime fields, baseline |
| Joux–Lercier | 2003 | $(d, d+1)$ | tiny vs. $p^{1/(d+1)}$ | Prime fields |
| GJL | 2015 | $(d+1, d)$ | tiny vs. LLL-reduced | Large characteristic, $n > 1$ |
| Conjugation | 2015 | $(2n, n)$-ish | $\approx \sqrt{p}$ balanced | Medium characteristic boundary |
| MNFS + Conj. | 2015 | many fields | as Conjugation | Medium characteristic, best $c \approx 2.156$ |
| TNFS | 2016 | tower degrees | reduced via compositeness | Composite $n$ (pairings) |

*Table 1. Polynomial-selection methods for NFS-DLP. "Coefficient scale" is heuristic; exact bounds carry LLL approximation factors.*

![Polynomial selection trade-offs](/thesis/ths_1789115348847_04281-2.webp)

*Figure 2. The polynomial-selection trade-off: coefficient sizes of the two number-field polynomials across methods, with the resulting asymptotic constant. Balancing the two sides (Conjugation) beats one tiny / one large side (Joux–Lercier) in medium characteristic.*

Beyond size, selection optimizes **Murphy's $E$-value** — expected relation yield, accounting for *root properties* (average $p$-adic valuations biasing smoothness) — and factor-base size. In CADO-NFS, selection is a short search guided by these estimators.

### 4.2 Relation collection: the sieving pipeline

Sieving dominates the wall-clock cost of every record computation. Given the sieve region $\mathcal{A}$ of size $E$ (typically $E = L_Q(1/3, \varepsilon)$) and factor-base bound $B = L_Q(1/3, \beta)$:

1. **Sieve.** For each prime ideal $\mathfrak{p}$ of norm $q \le B$, subtract $\log q$ along the root progressions of $F_i(x,1) \equiv 0 \pmod q$ — in lattice sieving, restricted to a special-$\mathfrak{q}$ sublattice.
2. **Cofactorization.** Small residuals mark $(a,b)$ with $B$-smooth norms up to a cofactor, finished by trial division/ECM.
3. **Filtering.** Drop duplicates ($(a,b) \sim (ca,cb)$), singletons, and excess relations; merge partial relations via large-prime variations.

![Lattice sieving region](/thesis/ths_1789115348847_04281-1.webp)

*Figure 3. Lattice sieving in the $(a,b)$-plane: factor-base prime ideals mark arithmetic progressions (lines) through the sieve region; the special-$\mathfrak{q}$ rectangle localizes the computation to a sublattice, the workhorse of CADO-NFS record runs.*

The special-$\mathfrak{q}$ trick converts sieving into many independent, cache-friendly sublattice tasks — the key to distributing it across thousands of cores, as in the DLP-240 computation [7].

### 4.3 Linear algebra: Wiedemann, Lanczos, and Schirokauer maps

After filtering we hold a sparse $N \times N$ matrix ($N \approx \pi(B)$) over $\mathbb{Z}/\ell\mathbb{Z}$, $\ell \mid (Q-1)$ the large prime subgroup order, each row a relation $\sum_j e_j v_j \equiv 0$ among virtual logarithms. Two complications arise:

- **The unit obstruction.** Ideal relations $(a-b\theta_1) = \prod \mathfrak{p}_j^{e_j}$ lift to elements only up to units and the class group. **Schirokauer maps** — explicit homomorphisms $\chi: \mathcal{O}_K^* \to \mu_\ell$ computable from the factorization of $\ell$ — adjoin extra matrix columns converting ideal relations into genuine equations over $\mathbb{Z}/\ell\mathbb{Z}$ [2].
- **Scale.** $N$ reaches tens of millions for 800-bit fields. Block Wiedemann (Berlekamp–Massey on $x^T M^{2k} y$) and block Lanczos need only matrix–vector products, but demand enormous memory bandwidth — the 768-bit DLP linear algebra consumed months of cluster time [7].

```rust
/// Sketch: block-Wiedemann sequence generation for the NFS relation matrix.
/// M is sparse (CSR) over Z/lZ; X, Y are random blocking matrices.
fn wiedemann_sequence(
    m: &CsrMatrix,      // N x N relation matrix mod l
    x: &DenseBlock,     // N x b block of vectors
    y: &DenseBlock,     // N x b block of vectors
    steps: usize, l: u64,
) -> Vec<DenseBlock> {
    let mut seq = Vec::with_capacity(steps);
    let mut v = y.clone();                 // v_k = M^{2k} y  (exponentiate by squaring steps)
    for _ in 0..steps {
        seq.push(x.transpose_mul(&v, l));  // s_k = x^T v_k  (b x b scalar sequence)
        v = m.mul(&m.mul(&v, l), l);       // advance two powers per step
    }
    seq // Berlekamp-Massey on each scalar stream -> kernel vector of M
}
```

### 4.4 Individual logarithm: the descent

With factor-base logarithms known, computing $\log t$ proceeds by **smoothing** ($t \cdot g^e$ lifted to an element of $B'$-smooth norm, $B' > B$) and then a **descent tree**: each large prime ideal $\mathfrak{q}$ is rewritten via special-$\mathfrak{q}$ sieving as smaller logarithms, recursing to the factor base [1][7]. Guillevic's refinement shortens the tree in composite extension degrees [7]. Descent runs in $L_Q(1/3, c_{\text{desc}})$ with $c_{\text{desc}}$ below the main-phase constant — the asymmetry behind **precomputation attacks**: one expensive phase per field, then fast per-target logarithms, as Logjam demonstrated against 512-bit DH [7].

## 5 Empirical Results and the Asymptotic Proof Sketch

### 5.1 The $L(1/3)$ calculus

We sketch the standard heuristic analysis for prime fields $Q = p$ (the extension-field case follows the same template with adjusted norm bounds [3]). Put $u = (\log p)^{1/3}(\log\log p)^{2/3}$, degree $d = \delta u$, smoothness bound $B = \exp(\beta u)$, sieve region size $E = \exp(\varepsilon u)$. For $(a,b)$ in the region, the two norms satisfy

$$\log |N_1 N_2| \;\approx\; \left(\tfrac{\delta^2}{6} + \tfrac{1}{3\delta}\right) u^2\log u \quad\text{(up to lower-order terms)},$$

where the first term is the algebraic norm (degree $\delta u$, tiny coefficients via Joux–Lercier) and the second is the rational norm ($m \approx p^{1/(\delta u)}$). By Canfield–Erdős–Pomerance, the joint smoothness probability is $\exp(-U \log U (1+o(1))/\log B)$ with $U$ the log-norm size — i.e., roughly $L_p(1/3, -(\delta^2/6 + 1/(3\delta))/(3\beta))^{-1}$... in $L$-notation, the reciprocal probability is $L_p(1/3, \gamma)$ with $\gamma = (\delta^2/2 + 1/\delta)/(3\beta)$.

Requiring $E \cdot P \approx B$ relations ($\varepsilon - \gamma = \beta$), with sieve cost $E = L_p(1/3,\varepsilon)$ and linear-algebra cost $B^2 = L_p(1/3, 2\beta)$, minimizing $\max(\varepsilon, 2\beta)$ gives $\delta^3 = 8/9$ and $2\beta = (64/9)^{1/3}$:

> **Theorem (heuristic complexity).** *NFS-DLP in $\mathbb{F}_p$ runs in $L_p(1/3, (64/9)^{1/3} + o(1)) \approx L_p(1/3, 1.923)$.*

```python
import math

def L(Q_bits, alpha, c):
    """Subexponential complexity L_Q(alpha, c) in log2 (bits of work)."""
    logQ = Q_bits * math.log(2)
    return (c * logQ**alpha * math.log(logQ)**(1 - alpha)) / math.log(2)

for bits, c, label in [(512, 1.923, "NFS-DLP"), (1024, 1.923, "NFS-DLP"),
                       (1024, 1.526, "SNFS (trapdoored)"), (2048, 1.923, "NFS-DLP")]:
    print(f"{label:22s} {bits:5d}-bit field  ~ 2^{L(bits, 1/3, c):6.1f} operations")
```

The script shows why 512-bit DH fell (~$2^{60}$–$2^{70}$ NFS work) while 2048-bit DH is far out of reach — and why the *special* variant ($c \approx 1.526$) broke a trapdoored 1024-bit prime [7].

### 5.3 Small characteristic: the quasi-polynomial revolution

For fields $\mathbb{F}_{q^n}$ with $q$ small (notably characteristic 2), Joux (2013) gave an $L(1/4+o(1))$ algorithm via a clever polynomial-relation search exploiting the Frobenius, and Barbulescu–Gaudry–Joux–Thomé then achieved *quasi-polynomial* complexity $n^{O(\log n)}$ through a refined Gröbner-style descent [1]:

> **Theorem (Barbulescu–Gaudry–Joux–Thomé, heuristic).** *The DLP in $\mathbb{F}_{q^n}$ with $q$ fixed (small characteristic) admits a heuristic algorithm of complexity $n^{O(\log n)}$, quasi-polynomial in the input size.*

This is smaller than $L(\varepsilon)$ for every $\varepsilon > 0$ — asymptotically devastating, and the reason binary-field DLP cryptography is considered dead.

## 6 Limitations

**Heuristic foundations.** The complexity theorems assume norms behave as random integers (CEP applies rigorously only to random integers), that enough relations exist, and that Schirokauer maps behave as needed. Unconditional proofs remain far out of reach.

**The linear-algebra wall.** Sieving parallelizes almost perfectly; sparse linear algebra does not. Block Wiedemann's communication pattern and memory footprint (tens of terabytes at 800 bits) make it the practical bottleneck — records are gated on cluster access, not just CPU-years.

**Extension-field selection.** Despite GJL, Conjugation, MNFS, and TNFS, selection in $\mathbb{F}_{p^n}$ for small $n$ stays delicate: towers need composite $n$, and constants (2.156 vs 2.201 [4]) remain far above the prime-field 1.923. The "Discrete Logarithm Factory" program [7] — amortizing selection and sieving across many fields — is promising but young.

**Special vs. general.** SNFS ($c \approx 1.526$) applies only to special-form fields. No evidence indicts *random* 1024-bit primes at that level — but the Logjam lesson stands: standardized, widely reused primes invite precomputation.

---

## 7 Conclusion

NFS is the culmination of index calculus: finite-field arithmetic converted into number-field geometry, sieving, sparse linear algebra, and descent. Its consequences are concrete and durable:

- **Finite-field Diffie–Hellman** must use 2048-bit (or larger) primes, and — the Logjam moral — *fresh, verifiably random* group parameters, since precomputation amortizes devastatingly against fixed standardized primes.
- **DSA / ElGamal / Schnorr over $\mathbb{F}_p^*$** inherit the same $L(1/3, 1.923)$ cryptanalysis; subgroup-size arguments (Pollard rho in the subgroup) are necessary but not sufficient.
- **Pairing-based cryptography** was re-dimensioned by TNFS: 128-bit security now demands substantially larger embedding fields than the pre-2016 BN-curve parameters provided [5][7].
- **Small-characteristic DLP** is broken in practice and quasi-polynomial asymptotically [1]; no new deployments should touch it.

Open directions include rigorous analyses of pipeline fragments and the Discrete Logarithm Factory vision of amortized multi-field cryptanalysis [7] — and, looming over all classical hardness, the quantum transition, where Shor's algorithm renders the sieve moot. Until then, parameter choices ignore its complexity curve at their peril.
