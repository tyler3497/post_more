# From Tri-Color Marking to Colored Pointers: Concurrent Low-Pause Garbage Collector Design from Stop-the-World Mark-Compact to Shenandoah, ZGC, and Azul C4

## Abstract

Latency-critical systems demand garbage collectors whose worst-case pause times are independent of heap size and live-set cardinality. This thesis develops the design theory of concurrent low-pause collectors, from Dijkstra's tri-color marking abstraction through the engineering milestones that made concurrent compaction practical: Shenandoah's Brooks-style forwarding pointers with SATB barriers, ZGC's colored pointers with self-healing load barriers, and Azul's C4 collector with its loaded-value barrier and generational concurrency. We formalize the pause-time bound theorem showing that in correctly designed concurrent evacuating collectors, stop-the-world time is bounded by root-set scan time plus a small constant, and is asymptotically independent of heap size. We derive the forwarding-pointer race protocol, the memory-ordering requirements of self-healing barriers, and the region-selection mathematics that bound fragmentation without global compaction. Empirical evidence from SPECjbb2015 studies and OpenJDK project data shows sub-millisecond pauses for ZGC, single-digit millisecond pauses for Shenandoah on 100 GB+ heaps, and C4's fully concurrent old-generation relocation. We conclude with open frontiers: NUMA-aware relocation, generational colored-pointer designs, and hardware transactional memory for barrier elision.

## 1. Introduction

The garbage collector is the silent tax collector of managed runtimes: every allocation eventually pays. For most of computing history, that tax was paid in *stop-the-world* pauses whose duration scaled with the heap. A mark-compact collector tracing a 200 GB heap can stall application threads for seconds — a regime incompatible with service-level agreements that promise 10–500 ms end-to-end response times [1].

Concurrent collection attacks this problem not by making collection faster, but by *decoupling* collection from mutator suspension. The insight, first crystallized in the tri-color abstraction, is that the collector need not see a frozen heap; it needs only a consistent *view* of reachability maintained through carefully designed barriers. The engineering cost is substantial: every pointer load or store may require a barrier, mutator threads may race with the collector moving objects, and the correctness argument spans memory models, atomicity, and phase-transition invariants.

Three families of production collectors define the state of the art: **Shenandoah** (OpenJDK, JEP 189), with a Brooks-style forwarding pointer in every object header enabling concurrent compaction [1][6]; **ZGC** (OpenJDK, JEP 333/377/439), encoding collector metadata in pointer bits — *colored pointers* — with self-healing load barriers and sub-millisecond pauses [2][7][9]; and **Azul C4**, built on the Pauseless algorithm with a loaded-value barrier and fully concurrent generational compaction [3][5].

This thesis develops a unified framework for these designs, proves the central pause-bound theorem, and extracts quantitative design rules for the next generation of low-latency collectors.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 The tri-color abstraction and the mutation problem

Concurrent marking begins with the tri-color abstraction: objects are *white* (unvisited), *gray* (visited but not fully scanned), or *black* (fully scanned). The collector's invariant is the tri-color condition — no black object may reference a white object, or equivalently, gray objects must always "shield" white ones from black mutator view. The mutator destroys this invariant by writing references: storing a white reference into a black object hides it from the collector.

Write barriers restore the invariant. The *snapshot-at-the-beginning* (SATB) barrier logs the overwritten (old) value, preserving the initial snapshot; the *incremental-update* (Yuasa-style) barrier logs the newly stored value. Both are sound, but they differ in floating-garbage characteristics and throughput cost — SATB is conservative and simpler to reason about, incremental update reclaims garbage created mid-cycle but requires a final re-scan.

### 2.2 From mark-sweep to mark-compact

Mark-sweep leaves fragmentation; fragmentation kills locality and, in the worst case, allocation itself. Classical compaction — Cheney-style copying or Lisp-2 sliding — moves objects, which is inherently hostile to concurrency: a mutator holding a pointer to an object that is *mid-move* must be redirected atomically. The classical resolution is the *forwarding pointer*: after copying, a forwarding address is installed in the old object; any thread encountering the old copy follows the indirection. Shenandoah makes this idea *permanent*: every object carries a Brooks pointer, an indirection cell that normally points to the object itself and, during evacuation, points to the to-space copy [5]. ZGC and C4 instead make the *reference* carry metadata and repair it lazily.

### 2.3 The taxonomy of barriers

| Barrier | Intercepts | Collector examples | Primary role |
|---|---|---|---|
| SATB write barrier | Reference stores (old value) | Shenandoah, G1 | Concurrent marking correctness |
| Incremental-update write barrier | Reference stores (new value) | — (Yuasa-style) | Concurrent marking correctness |
| Read barrier (Brooks) | Reference loads | Shenandoah | Forwarding-pointer dereference during evacuation |
| Load barrier (colored) | Reference loads | ZGC | Self-healing stale colored pointers |
| LVB (loaded-value barrier) | Reference loads + NMT check | C4 | Self-healing without stopping relocation |
| Store barrier (generational) | Reference stores (cross-gen) | Generational ZGC | Old-to-young remembered tracking [2] |

The fundamental trade-off is *barrier cost vs. pause cost*: stronger barriers (read/load barriers on every reference load) buy shorter or nonexistent stop-the-world phases at the price of mutator throughput — typically single-digit percentages for well-optimized barriers, growing when allocation or pointer-chasing rates are high.

## 3. Methodology

Our analysis combines three methodological threads:

1. **Formal invariant analysis.** We state each collector's phase-transition invariants (Section 4) and prove the pause-bound theorem (Section 5) by showing that stop-the-world work reduces to root scanning plus constant-size atomic transitions, independent of heap size *H* and live-set size *L*.
2. **Mechanism deconstruction.** For each collector we identify the *relocation protocol*: how an object is copied concurrently, how mutator threads are redirected, and how the "all references updated" fixpoint is established. We pay special attention to the atomicity of forwarding-pointer installation and the memory-ordering semantics of self-healing barriers.
3. **Empirical synthesis.** We assemble published measurements — OpenJDK wiki and JEP documentation for ZGC, the Shenandoah performance studies, and the C4 ISMM 2011 evaluation [3][7][6] — into a common comparison frame (pause time vs. heap size vs. mutator utilization), with explicit attention to what each benchmark actually measures.

Notation: *H* is heap size, *L* the live set, *R* the root set, *T<sub>pause</sub>* the maximum stop-the-world pause, *MU* the minimum mutator utilization. A pointer's *color* is the metadata embedded in its unused address bits; a *view* is a pointer-with-color as observed by some thread at some instant.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Shenandoah: Brooks pointers and the evacuation protocol

Shenandoah's defining decision is to pay a *uniform, predictable* price — one machine word per object for the forwarding (Brooks) pointer — to make concurrent compaction a local operation [1][6]. Every reference load passes through a read barrier that dereferences the Brooks pointer:



The GC cycle is a pipeline of mostly-concurrent phases [1]:

1. **Pause Init Mark** — scan roots (stacks, registers, statics). *T ≈ O(R).*
2. **Concurrent Marking** — SATB-based tri-color trace; mutators run freely.
3. **Pause Final Mark** — drain SATB buffers, complete marking, choose evacuation regions. *T ≈ O(R) + SATB backlog.*
4. **Concurrent Evacuation** — copy live objects from collection-set regions to free regions; the first thread to reach an object installs the forwarding pointer atomically (CAS); losers of the CAS follow the winner's pointer. Mutators and GC threads *collaborate*: whichever reaches an object first does the copy.
5. **Pause Init Update Refs / Concurrent Update References / Pause Final Update Refs** — rewrite all references to point at to-space copies; the final pause establishes the "all references remapped" fixpoint.
6. **Concurrent Cleanup** — reclaim from-space regions.

The race protocol's correctness hinges on a single invariant:

> **Invariant (Forwarding uniqueness).** For every evacuated object *o*, at most one thread installs a forwarding pointer in *o*'s Brooks cell, via compare-and-swap from the self-pointer to the to-space address; all other threads observe either the self-pointer (copy themselves, losing the CAS) or the installed forwarder.

Because the Brooks cell is part of the object header and the CAS is atomic, linearizability of the "move" operation follows. The read barrier then guarantees that no thread ever observes a from-space copy after the final reference-update pause — the classic *to-space invariant*.

Shenandoah's graceful degradation ladder deserves emphasis: concurrent collectors implicitly assume *collector throughput exceeds mutator allocation rate*. When allocation spikes, Shenandoah's pacer stalls allocating threads proportionally to GC progress, bounding tail latency rather than falling off a cliff [5].

### 4.2 ZGC: colored pointers and self-healing load barriers

ZGC inverts Shenandoah's trade: instead of one word per object, it steals the *unused high bits of 64-bit pointers*. In the JDK 11–15 design, bits 42–45 of every heap pointer carry four colors [6]:

| Bit | Name | Meaning when set |
|---|---|---|
| 42 | Finalizable | Object needs finalizer processing |
| 43 | Remapped | Pointer address is current (post-relocation) |
| 44 | Marked1 | Marked in odd GC cycle |
| 45 | Marked0 | Marked in even GC cycle |

(64-bit pointers with only 42 address bits address 4 TB; JDK 13 extended this to 16 TB [6].)

Marking uses alternating Marked0/Marked1 colors so the barrier can distinguish the current cycle's mark from the previous one without a global bitmap sweep.

ZGC's stop-the-world phases reduce to three root-scan pauses per cycle — *Pause Mark Start*, *Pause Mark End*, *Pause Relocate Start* — each *O(R)*. The project goal moved from "pause times do not exceed 10 ms" (JEP 333) to sub-millisecond in current releases, with the explicit aim that pauses not grow with heap, live-set, *or root-set* size — concurrent class unloading and concurrent thread-stack processing attack the root-set term directly [7][2].

### 4.3 Azul C4: the loaded-value barrier and never stopping for relocation

C4, described in the Pauseless GC paper [4] and the C4 ISMM 2011 paper [3], takes concurrent compaction to its logical extreme: *no stop-the-world phase performs relocation at all*. Its mechanisms:

- **Loaded-Value Barrier (LVB).** On every reference load, the barrier checks the reference's *NMT* (not-marked-through) state against the generation's expected value. A stale NMT triggers the slow path: mark through the object and, if it resides in a relocation set, *relocate it immediately* — the loading thread performs the copy (or escalates it), a design C4 calls *collaborative relocation* [3].
- **Generational concurrency.** Young and old generations collect concurrently and independently; each generation maintains its own expected NMT state, and the LVB verifies against the correct generation via reference metadata bits — eliminating the pathology where a full-heap mark blocks young-generation progress [3].
- **Virtual-address-preserving compaction.** C4 relocates pages while keeping virtual addresses intact where possible, and uses *shattered object moves* for large objects, so reclamation is immediate once a page is drained.

> **Theorem:** *(C4 relocation liveness).* Let *S* be the relocation set of a generation. Every object in *S* is relocated within finite mutator steps, and no mutator observes a from-space address after the generation's remap completes.
>
> **Proof:** Each load of a reference into *S* triggers the LVB slow path, which either relocates the target or observes it already relocated (idempotent via forwarding state). Loads are *fair*: any live object in *S* is loaded infinitely often by either mutator progress or the GC's own remap scan, so each object is relocated at least once; double relocation is prevented by the atomic NMT transition. Once relocated, the remap pass rewrites all references; the LVB guarantees post-remap loads never produce from-space addresses because their NMT state cannot regress. ∎

### 4.4 The concurrent copying protocol, unified

All three collectors implement the same abstract protocol, differing only in where the forwarding metadata lives:

1. **Select** collection-set regions (garbage-dense regions first, to bound copying work).
2. **Copy**: for each live object *o* in the set, allocate *o′* in to-space, memcpy the payload, then atomically publish the forwarding edge *o → o′*.
3. **Redirect**: every subsequent access to *o* must reach *o′* — via Brooks cell (Shenandoah), healed colored pointer (ZGC), or LVB remap (C4).
4. **Fixpoint**: establish that *no* reference still points to from-space — a stop-the-world *Final Update Refs* (Shenandoah), the remap phase piggybacked on load barriers (ZGC), or per-generation remap (C4).

The protocol's memory-ordering requirements are non-trivial. The payload copy must be *visible* before the forwarding edge is published (release semantics on the forwarding CAS; acquire on the barrier's read of the forwarder). Self-healing writes race with concurrent readers — benign, because any color that passes the barrier's check is *valid for use*, and stale colors always trap into the slow path. This is the *monotonic color* discipline: colors only transition toward "more current," never backward.

### 4.5 The sub-millisecond endgame: generations, roots, and NUMA

Three frontiers dominate current work:

- **Generational low-pause collection.** Generational ZGC (JEP 439, JDK 21) splits the heap into young and old generations collected independently, preserving sub-millisecond pauses while cutting GC CPU overhead and allocation-stall risk; JDK 23 made it default (JEP 474) and JDK 24 removed the non-generational mode (JEP 490) [2].
- **Root-set pauses.** With heap and live-set terms eliminated, the remaining pause term is *O(R)*. Concurrent thread-stack processing and concurrent class unloading attack this term, moving toward pauses independent of root-set size as well [6][9].
- **NUMA-aware relocation.** ZGC's NUMA-aware page allocation directs pages to local nodes [6]; the open problem is *NUMA-aware collection-set selection* — choosing relocation targets that preserve locality.

## 5. Empirical Results / Proofs

### 5.1 The pause-bound theorem

> **Theorem:** *(Concurrent evacuation pause bound).* In a concurrent compacting collector satisfying (i) the to-space invariant via Brooks pointers or monotonic colored pointers, (ii) SATB or incremental-update marking, and (iii) atomic forwarding-pointer installation, the maximum stop-the-world pause satisfies
>
> *T<sub>pause</sub> ≤ T<sub>scan</sub>(R) + T<sub>flip</sub> + T<sub>drain</sub>(SATB backlog),*
>
> where each term is independent of heap size *H* and live-set size *L*.
>
> **Proof:** Enumerate the stop-the-world phases. *Init Mark* scans roots: *O(R)*. *Final Mark* scans roots plus the SATB backlog — bounded by the number of mutated references during concurrent marking, itself *O(mutator stores)*, not *O(H)*. Phase flips (*Init Update Refs*, *Final Update Refs*) perform constant atomic state transitions: *O(1)*. No phase traverses the heap or the live set; all *O(H)*/*O(L)* work (marking, copying, reference updating) executes concurrently. Hence the bound is independent of *H* and *L*. ∎

The theorem's practical content is a *design rule*: every proposed "optimization" that adds heap-proportional work to a pause — e.g., eager remembered-set rebuilding — violates the bound and must be moved concurrent or eliminated. JEP 376's concurrent stack processing is exactly such an elimination applied to the *R* term.

### 5.2 Reported measurements

| Collector | Reported max pause | Heap scale | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| ZGC (JDK 15+, production, JEP 377) | Consistently microseconds; goal ≤ 1 ms | 8 MB – 16 TB | OpenJDK ZGC wiki [6] |
| Generational ZGC (JDK 21, JEP 439) | Typically < 1 ms, lower allocation-stall risk | Hundreds of MB – multi-TB | JEP 439 [2] |
| Shenandoah | < 10 ms target on 100 GB+ heaps; pauses ∝ root set | Tested to 200 GB | Shenandoah slides, JEP 189 [5][1] |
| C4 (Azul Zing) | Millisecond-scale worst case on SPECjbb; no STW relocation | Multi-GB heaps | ISMM 2011 evaluation [3] |

*Interpretation caveats.* These figures come from different benchmarks and cannot be compared naively. The robust cross-collector claim is *asymptotic*, not numeric: pause times do not grow with heap size — the property the theorem predicts and all three collectors demonstrate [1][2][4][7].

Low pauses are never free. The overhead decomposes into barrier cost on mutator reference operations, concurrent CPU for relocation threads, and space overhead (Shenandoah's Brooks word per object; ZGC's multi-mapped address space):
- **Shenandoah**: SpaceOverhead ≈ 1 word/object (Brooks pointer) + region metadata; BarrierCost = read barrier on loads + SATB write barrier [5].
- **ZGC**: SpaceOverhead ≈ 0 (colors ride in pointers) but address-space consumption (multi-mapping) and load-barrier cost on every reference load; concurrent CPU for concurrent relocate/remap [6].
- **C4**: LVB on loads + generational write barriers; no STW relocation but continuous background relocation threads [3].

## 6. Limitations

1. **The allocation-rate ceiling.** Every concurrent collector assumes it reclaims faster than the mutator allocates. Under extreme allocation pressure, all three degrade: Shenandoah paces (adding *invisible* per-thread latency), ZGC risks allocation stalls, C4's relocation set stops draining. No concurrent collector repeals this law; heuristics only soften the landing [1][6][7].
2. **Barrier ubiquity and JIT complexity.** Load/read barriers must be correct in *every* compiled code path — intrinsics, unsafe access, JNI transitions, and deoptimization are all barrier-escape hazards. Shenandoah's `-XX:+ShenandoahVerify` infrastructure exists precisely because barrier bugs are the dominant source of collector defects [5]. This complexity is a permanent tax on VM maintenance.
3. **Colored-pointer address-space limits.** Stealing address bits caps the heap: 4 TB at 42 bits, 16 TB at 44 bits [6]. Future heaps measured in hundreds of terabytes will need wider pointers (128-bit?) or hybrid schemes — an architectural cliff the industry has not yet confronted.
4. **Generational remembered-set costs.** Generational ZGC's store barriers reintroduce remembered-set maintenance, partially eroding the "no card tables" elegance of the non-generational design [2]. The remembered set is itself a source of pause-time risk if it must be rebuilt or scanned under STW.
5. **Benchmark realism.** SPECjbb2015 and synthetic latency benchmarks understate real-world pathologies: JNI-heavy workloads pin objects and defeat relocation; finalizer-heavy workloads serialize on the Finalizable color; reflection-heavy frameworks inflate root sets. Published numbers are best-case envelopes, not guarantees.

## 7. Conclusion

The arc from stop-the-world mark-compact to modern concurrent collectors is the story of *moving coordination out of pauses and into barriers*. Shenandoah showed that a uniform per-object forwarding pointer plus disciplined STW phase transitions yields heap-size-independent pauses [1][6]. ZGC showed that the pointer itself can carry the coordination state, and that mutators can heal the heap through ordinary loads — pushing pauses into the microsecond regime and, with JEP 439, recovering generational efficiency without sacrificing latency [2][7][9]. C4 showed that with the loaded-value barrier, even relocation need never stop the world [3][5].

The pause-bound theorem (Section 5.1) unifies these designs: once heap-proportional work is expelled from stop-the-world phases, pauses are bounded by root scanning plus constants — a property that holds regardless of whether forwarding metadata lives in the object header, the pointer bits, or the barrier's NMT state. The remaining frontiers — root-set pauses, NUMA-aware relocation, and the address-space limits of colored pointers — are well-posed engineering problems with active JEPs behind them.

For the practitioner, the decision rule is simple: if your p99.9 latency budget is measured in milliseconds and your heap in gigabytes or more, the concurrent compactors are no longer experimental — JEP 377 made ZGC production-ready in JDK 15, and generational ZGC is now the default [2]. The tax is real (barriers, background CPU, address-space games), but it is *predictable* — and predictability is what latency engineering buys.
