# Post-Quantum Cryptography Migration: Standardizing ML-KEM, ML-DSA, and SLH-DSA for a Harvest-Now-Decrypt-Later World

## Abstract

The publication of NIST FIPS 203, 204, and 205 in August 2024 converted post-quantum cryptography from a research competition into a deployment mandate. This article surveys the migration landscape: the mathematics of module-lattice key encapsulation (ML-KEM) and signatures (ML-DSA), the conservative hash-based hedge of SLH-DSA, and the hybrid key-exchange constructions — X25519MLKEM768, SecP256r1MLKEM768, SecP384r1MLKEM1024 — now standardized in RFC 10024 over the RFC 9954 framework for TLS 1.3. We analyze the *harvest-now-decrypt-later* threat model that forces early migration of confidentiality-bearing traffic, quantify the concrete costs (public keys, ciphertexts, handshake bloat, signature sizes), and examine the surviving alternatives: HQC's selection as a code-based backup KEM, Classic McEliece's round-4 candidacy, and the 2022 collapse of SIKE. We conclude with a crypto-agility roadmap for staged migration and the open problems — side-channel-hardened implementations, certificate-chain engineering, and lattice cryptanalysis — that will define the next decade of the transition.

---

## 1 Introduction

Public-key cryptography rests on problems that are hard for classical computers: integer factorization and the discrete logarithm problem. Shor's algorithm [1] collapses both to polynomial time on a sufficiently large fault-tolerant quantum computer. The engineering question is no longer *whether* to migrate but *how fast*, because adversaries are already recording today's encrypted traffic for decryption tomorrow — the **harvest-now-decrypt-later** (HNDL) model.

NIST's post-quantum cryptography (PQC) standardization, launched in 2016, concluded its first phase on August 13, 2024 with three finalized standards [2]:

- **FIPS 203 — ML-KEM**, the Module-Lattice-Based Key-Encapsulation Mechanism (CRYSTALS-Kyber), as the primary standard for general encryption;
- **FIPS 204 — ML-DSA**, the Module-Lattice-Based Digital Signature Algorithm (CRYSTALS-Dilithium), as the primary standard for signatures;
- **FIPS 205 — SLH-DSA**, the Stateless Hash-Based Digital Signature Algorithm (SPHINCS+), as a conservative backup signature scheme built on different mathematics.

Migration is a systems problem as much as a mathematical one. PQC keys and signatures are *larger* — often by one to two orders of magnitude — than their classical counterparts, which stresses TLS handshakes, X.509 certificate chains, DNSSEC responses, and embedded firmware. The industry answer is **hybrid key exchange**: combining a classical algorithm (X25519, P-256) with a post-quantum KEM so that security holds if *either* component remains unbroken. That construction moved from experiment (Google and Cloudflare's CECPQ2/X25519Kyber768Draft00 deployments in 2023–2024) to Internet Standard with RFC 10024 in 2026 [3], built on the RFC 9954 hybrid framework [4].

This article proceeds as follows. Section 2 reviews the quantum threat model and the NIST competition. Section 3 describes our survey methodology. Section 4 presents the deep technical dive: the three standards, hybrid TLS 1.3 key exchange, and the alternative families. Section 5 reports empirical performance and deployment data. Section 6 discusses limitations and risks, and Section 7 concludes with a migration roadmap.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Shor's Algorithm and the Quantum Threat

Shor's 1994 algorithm factors an *n*-bit integer in *O(n³)* time on a quantum computer [1], breaking RSA, Diffie–Hellman, and elliptic-curve cryptography (ECDSA, ECDH) alike. Grover's algorithm only halves symmetric-key security bits, so AES-256 and SHA-384 remain viable with adjusted parameters. The asymmetric suite — the part that negotiates every TLS session and signs every software update — must be replaced wholesale.

> **Theorem (informal):** Under the existence of a cryptographically relevant quantum computer (CRQC), no currently deployed public-key key-exchange or signature scheme retains its security. Migration is therefore a *when*, not an *if*.

Estimates of the CRQC timeline vary widely (2030s being a common planning horizon), but the HNDL model below makes the timeline partially irrelevant for confidentiality.

### 2.2 Harvest Now, Decrypt Later

HNDL adversaries passively record encrypted traffic today and store it until a CRQC exists. Any data whose confidentiality lifetime exceeds the CRQC arrival date is already compromised *in expectation*. For medical records (50+ year sensitivity), state secrets, and long-lived industrial credentials, migration of key exchange cannot wait for the CRQC — it is overdue. This asymmetry drives the urgency: **signatures can be upgraded at verification time, but key exchange must be upgraded before the data crosses the wire.**

### 2.3 The NIST PQC Competition

NIST's 2016 call attracted 82 submissions; after three rounds, four algorithms were selected in July 2022 (Kyber, Dilithium, Falcon, SPHINCS+), with BIKE, Classic McEliece, HQC, and SIKE advancing to a fourth round for KEMs [5]. SIKE — the isogeny candidate — was spectacularly broken by a classical key-recovery attack in August 2022 [6], a cautionary tale about young hardness assumptions. In March 2025, NIST selected **HQC** (Hamming Quasi-Cyclic, code-based) as a backup KEM to ML-KEM, with a draft standard expected in early 2026 and finalization in 2027 [5].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 ML-KEM: Module-Lattice Key Encapsulation (FIPS 203)

ML-KEM is a key-encapsulation mechanism built on the **Module Learning With Errors (MLWE)** problem: distinguishing *(A, As + e)* from uniform, where *A* is a random matrix over a polynomial ring *R_q = Z_q[x]/(x²⁵⁶ + 1)*, *s* a secret vector, and *e* small noise. The scheme is an IND-CCA2-secure KEM via the Fujisaki–Okamoto transform applied to a CPA-secure public-key encryption core.

**Parameter sets** trade security category against size:

| Parameter set | Security category | Public key | Ciphertext | Shared secret |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| ML-KEM-512 | 1 (≈ AES-128) | 800 B | 768 B | 32 B |
| ML-KEM-768 | 3 (≈ AES-192) | 1,184 B | 1,088 B | 32 B |
| ML-KEM-1024 | 5 (≈ AES-256) | 1,568 B | 1,568 B | 32 B |

A notable engineering property is **implicit rejection**: decapsulation never returns an error on malformed ciphertexts; instead it derives a pseudorandom key that will not match the sender's. This design neutralizes a class of chosen-ciphertext oracles that exploit error paths [2]. ML-KEM-768 is the recommended default — category 3 security with a ~1.2 KB public key that fits TLS's 2¹⁶−1-byte `key_exchange` field, unlike Classic McEliece's 261,120-byte public key [4].

```python
# ML-KEM-768 KEM API shape (as exposed by PQClean/liboqs bindings)
pk, sk = mlkem768.keypair()        # pk: 1184 bytes, sk: 2400 bytes
ct, ss_enc = mlkem768.encaps(pk)   # ct: 1088 bytes, ss: 32 bytes
ss_dec = mlkem768.decaps(ct, sk)   # implicit rejection: no error branch
assert ss_enc == ss_dec
```

### 4.2 ML-DSA: Module-Lattice Signatures (FIPS 204)

ML-DSA (CRYSTALS-Dilithium) is a Fiat–Shamir-with-aborts signature scheme over module lattices, relying on the hardness of **MLWE** and **Module Short Integer Solution (MSISIS)**. Signing samples a masking vector, computes a challenge from the message and a commitment, and *rejects* candidate signatures that would leak secret information — repeating until the response is independent of the secret key. Deterministic signing derives randomness from the secret key and message, eliminating catastrophic RNG failures.

| Parameter set | Category | Public key | Signature |
|---|---|---|---|
| ML-DSA-44 | 2 | 1,312 B | 2,420 B |
| ML-DSA-65 | 3 | 1,952 B | 3,309 B |
| ML-DSA-87 | 5 | 2,592 B | 4,627 B |

Compare with 64-byte Ed25519 signatures: ML-DSA-65 signatures are ~52× larger. This is the central cost driver for certificate chains — a three-certificate chain grows from ~2 KB to ~15 KB, fragmenting TLS handshakes across many records. Mitigations include certificate compression (RFC 8879), chain trimming, and signature caching.

### 4.3 SLH-DSA: Stateless Hash-Based Signatures (FIPS 205)

SLH-DSA (SPHINCS+) takes a radically conservative approach: security reduces *only* to the properties of the underlying hash function (SHA-256/SHAKE), with no lattice assumption at all. It builds a hypertree of Merkle trees over few-time signature schemes (WOTS+, FORS), making it **stateless** — unlike XMSS/LMS, signers need not track one-time key state.

The price is size and speed:

| Parameter set | Signature | Public key | Sign ops/sec (ref) |
|---|---|---|---|
| SLH-DSA-SHA2-128s | 7,856 B | 32 B | ~tens |
| SLH-DSA-SHA2-128f | 17,088 B | 32 B | ~hundreds |
| SLH-DSA-SHA2-256f | 49,856 B | 64 B | ~tens |

The `s` (small) variants minimize signature size at the cost of signing time; `f` (fast) variants reverse the tradeoff. NIST positions SLH-DSA explicitly as a hedge: deploy ML-DSA as primary, keep SLH-DSA ready in case module lattices fall [2]. Its 32-byte public keys are attractive for constrained verifiers (firmware roots of trust), where verification cost dominates.

### 4.4 Hybrid Key Exchange in TLS 1.3

The migration strategy with the strongest consensus is **hybrid key exchange**: run a classical algorithm and a PQC KEM in parallel and combine their shared secrets, so the construction is secure if *either* component holds. RFC 9954 [4] standardizes the framework: each hybrid group concatenates the two public keys in the `key_exchange` field and concatenates the two shared secrets into the TLS 1.3 key schedule via HKDF.

RFC 10024 [3] instantiates three hybrid groups for TLS 1.3:

| Hybrid group | Components | Key share size | Shared-secret order |
|---|---|---|---|
| X25519MLKEM768 | X25519 + ML-KEM-768 | 32 + 1,184 B | ML-KEM first |
| SecP256r1MLKEM768 | P-256 + ML-KEM-768 | 65 + 1,184 B | ECDH first |
| SecP384r1MLKEM1024 | P-384 + ML-KEM-1024 | 97 + 1,568 B | ECDH first |

The shared-secret ordering is deliberate: NIST SP 800-56Cr2 approves HKDF over concatenated secrets only when the *first* secret comes from a FIPS-approved scheme, so ML-KEM leads in X25519MLKEM768 while ECDH leads in the NIST-curve groups [3]. X25519MLKEM768 descends directly from the widely deployed X25519Kyber768Draft00 used by Chrome and Cloudflare in 2024, giving operators a tested migration path.

```python
# Hybrid key schedule (RFC 9954, simplified)
def hybrid_shared_secret(ecdh_secret, kem_secret, kem_first=True):
    parts = [kem_secret, ecdh_secret] if kem_first else [ecdh_secret, kem_secret]
    return HKDF_Extract(salt=b"", ikm=b"".join(parts))  # into TLS 1.3 key schedule
```

Practical consequences are real but manageable: ClientHello messages exceed a single TCP segment (increasing round trips on lossy links), and servers must handle ~2.4 KB key shares per connection. Measurements from large deployments show handshake latency increases of single-digit milliseconds on typical networks — acceptable against the HNDL imperative.

### 4.5 Code-Based and Isogeny Alternatives

**HQC** (Hamming Quasi-Cyclic), selected in March 2025 as ML-KEM's backup [5], bases security on the quasi-cyclic syndrome-decoding problem — decades-old coding theory with no lattice structure. Its keys are larger (public key ~2.2 KB for hqc-128) but it diversifies the hardness portfolio. The draft standard is expected in early 2026.

**Classic McEliece**, still in round 4, offers the most conservative security story of any KEM candidate (unbroken since 1978) but its 261 KB public key excludes it from TLS and most interactive protocols; its niche is static, long-lived encryption like file archives.

**SIKE** is the cautionary tale: the isogeny-based candidate was broken in August 2022 by Castryck and Decru's classical attack recovering the secret isogeny in about an hour on a single core [6]. The lesson for migration planners is that *diversity of assumptions* matters more than any single scheme's elegance — hence hybrid deployments and the SLH-DSA hedge.

## 5 Empirical Results

### 5.1 Performance Landscape

Reference measurements (PQClean on x86-64, AVX2 where available) show lattice schemes are *faster* than the classical algorithms they replace for key exchange:

| Operation | X25519 | ML-KEM-768 | ECDSA P-256 sign | ML-DSA-65 sign |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Keygen | ~50 µs | ~40 µs | ~60 µs | ~150 µs |
| Exchange/Sign | ~60 µs | ~60 µs | ~70 µs | ~250 µs |
| Verify/Decaps | ~60 µs | ~55 µs | ~150 µs | ~80 µs |

ML-KEM-768 encapsulation/decapsulation is competitive with X25519 on modern CPUs; ML-DSA signing is slower than ECDSA but verification is faster. SLH-DSA is the outlier — signing can take milliseconds — which is why it is a hedge, not a default.

### 5.2 Deployment Evidence

- **Chrome 124+ (2024)** enabled X25519Kyber768Draft00 hybrid key exchange by default, protecting a substantial fraction of global TLS traffic during the transition window.
- **Cloudflare** deployed hybrid PQC TLS across its edge, reporting negligible handshake-failure increases.
- **Signal's PQXDH** (2023) added Kyber-1024 to its X3DH handshake — an early production hybrid in messaging.
- IETF's `draft-ietf-uta-pqc-app` guidance now mandates that HNDL-vulnerable applications move to TLS 1.3 with hybrid key exchange [3].

## 6 Limitations

**Lattice attack surface.** Module-LWE enjoys worst-case-to-average-case reductions, but concrete security rests on the core-SVP methodology and lattice-estimator heuristics, which have been repeatedly revised. A cryptanalytic breakthrough against structured lattices would compromise both ML-KEM and ML-DSA simultaneously — the scenario SLH-DSA and HQC exist to hedge.

**Side channels.** Early lattice implementations leaked secrets through timing (rejection sampling), power analysis (NTT butterflies), and cache behavior. Constant-time discipline — as enforced by PQClean's test harness [7] — is necessary but not sufficient; masked implementations for high-assurance hardware remain an active research area.

**Ecosystem costs.** X.509 certificate chains balloon; DNSSEC responses exceed UDP limits; embedded devices lack RAM for 2.4 KB private keys. Crypto-agility — the ability to swap algorithms without redesigning protocols — is the meta-requirement: systems should negotiate algorithm identifiers (as TLS 1.3's `supported_groups` already does) rather than hard-coding primitives.

**Migration sequencing risk.** Hybrid modes double the implementation attack surface during the transition. Formal analyses of the RFC 9954 combiner show security reduces to the stronger component, but implementation bugs in the *glue* (concatenation order, transcript binding) have historically been where real vulnerabilities hide.

## 7 Conclusion

The post-quantum migration has crossed the point of no return: FIPS 203/204/205 are final [2], RFC 10024 standardizes hybrid TLS 1.3 key exchange [3], and HQC is queued as the code-based backup [5]. The technically sound migration posture is now clear:

1. **Inventory** all uses of public-key cryptography, prioritizing HNDL-vulnerable confidentiality.
2. **Deploy hybrid key exchange** (X25519MLKEM768) for TLS 1.3 immediately — security holds under either assumption.
3. **Plan signature migration** to ML-DSA-65, keeping SLH-DSA as a hedge for long-lived roots.
4. **Build crypto-agility** into protocols and key management so the *next* algorithm swap is a configuration change, not a rewrite.

The open problems — hardened constant-time implementations, certificate-chain engineering, and continued lattice cryptanalysis — will occupy the community through the 2030s. But the direction is settled: the quantum-safe internet is being built with module lattices today, hedged by hash-based signatures, and delivered over hybrid handshakes.
