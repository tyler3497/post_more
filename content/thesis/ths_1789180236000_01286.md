# Causal Inference with Instrumental Variables: The LATE Theorem, Two-Stage Least Squares, Weak Instruments, and Partial Identification Bounds

## Abstract

This thesis develops the econometric theory of instrumental variables (IV) for causal inference under heterogeneous treatment effects. Beginning from the Neyman–Rubin potential-outcomes model, we state the Imbens–Angrist (1994) local average treatment effect (LATE) theorem and give its proof under independence, exclusion, relevance, and monotonicity [1]. We derive two-stage least squares (2SLS) as a generalized method-of-moments estimator, show its equivalence to the Wald ratio in the just-identified binary case, and establish consistency and asymptotic normality [2]. We then analyze the weak-instruments pathology formalized by Staiger and Stock (1997): conventional asymptotics fail, 2SLS is biased toward ordinary least squares, and the F > 10 rule of thumb (Stock–Yogo critical values) disciplines inference [3][4]. We review weak-instrument-robust tests — Anderson–Rubin, the conditional likelihood-ratio, and Lagrange-multiplier statistics — and Vytlacil's (2002) equivalence between the LATE assumptions and a nonparametric latent-index selection model, yielding marginal treatment effects [5]. Finally, we treat partial identification: when exclusion or monotonicity fails, Manski bounds and their IV refinements deliver assumption-light intervals for the average treatment effect [6]. The quarter-of-birth instrument for returns to schooling [7] illustrates each result empirically.

## 1 Introduction

The fundamental problem of causal inference is the missing counterfactual: for any unit, we observe the outcome under the realized treatment but never under the treatment not taken. In observational data, treatment assignment is typically *endogenous* — correlated with unobserved determinants of the outcome — so that naive contrasts of treated and untreated units confound causal effects with selection. Instrumental variables estimation is the principal econometric technology for recovering causal parameters in the presence of such endogeneity.

The classical IV theory, developed for linear models with constant coefficients, treats the structural parameter as a single population number. Modern treatment-effects analysis relaxes this fiction. Following the potential-outcomes framework of Neyman and Rubin, each unit carries two potential outcomes, and the treatment effect varies across the population. An instrument then identifies not a universal parameter but a *local* one: the average treatment effect for the subpopulation whose treatment choice is moved by the instrument — the *compliers* [1]. This local average treatment effect (LATE) reframes IV as a design-based estimator: it answers a question about the causal effect of an encouragement, policy lever, or natural experiment on those who respond to it.

Three further developments complete the picture. First, weak instruments: when the instrument is only faintly correlated with the endogenous regressor, 2SLS behaves badly even in large samples [3][4]. Second, the LATE assumptions were shown to be exactly equivalent to a nonparametric latent-index selection model, which supports identification of *marginal* treatment effects across the distribution of unobserved resistance to treatment [5]. Third, when the instrument assumptions themselves are implausible, Manski's program of partial identification replaces point estimates with bounds — intervals guaranteed to contain the parameter under weaker, credible assumptions [6].

This thesis integrates these four strands — LATE, 2SLS, weak instruments, and partial identification — into a single exposition, with proofs, an empirical application, and a discussion of the limits of each approach.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Potential outcomes and the endogeneity problem

For each unit $i$, let $D_i \in {0,1}$ denote binary treatment and let $Y_{1i}$, $Y_{0i}$ denote potential outcomes with and without treatment. The observed outcome is

$$Y_i = D_i Y_{1i} + (1 - D_i) Y_{0i} = Y_{0i} + D_i (Y_{1i} - Y_{0i}).$$

The individual treatment effect $\tau_i = Y_{1i} - Y_{0i}$ is never observed. The average treatment effect $\mathrm{ATE} = E[Y_{1i} - Y_{0i}]$ and the average treatment effect on the treated $\mathrm{ATT} = E[Y_{1i} - Y_{0i} \mid D_i = 1]$ are the usual targets. The naive contrast decomposes as

$$E[Y_i \mid D_i = 1] - E[Y_i \mid D_i = 0] = \underbrace{E[Y_{1i} - Y_{0i} \mid D_i = 1]}_{\mathrm{ATT}} + \underbrace{\big(E[Y_{0i} \mid D_i = 1] - E[Y_{0i} \mid D_i = 0]\big)}_{\text{selection bias}}.$$

When units select into treatment based on potential outcomes, the selection-bias term is nonzero and the naive contrast is causally meaningless.

### 2.2 The instrument and its four assumptions

Let $Z_i \in {0,1}$ be a binary instrument and define potential treatment statuses $D_{1i}, D_{0i}$, the treatment unit $i$ would take when the instrument is switched on or off. Following Imbens and Angrist (1994) [1], IV identification rests on four assumptions:

1. **Independence (random assignment):** ${Y_{1i}, Y_{0i}, D_{1i}, D_{0i}} \perp\!\!\!\perp Z_i$. The instrument is as good as randomly assigned, independent of potential outcomes and potential treatment responses.
2. **Exclusion restriction:** $Y_i(d, z) = Y_i(d)$ for $d \in {0,1}$; the instrument affects the outcome only through the treatment. Equivalently, potential outcomes are indexed by treatment alone.
3. **First stage (relevance):** $E[D_{1i} - D_{0i}] \neq 0$. The instrument moves treatment take-up on average.
4. **Monotonicity:** $D_{1i} - D_{0i} \geq 0$ for all $i$ (or $\leq 0$ for all $i$). There are no *defiers*: no unit takes treatment when $Z_i = 0$ yet refuses it when $Z_i = 1$.

The cross-tabulation of $(D_{0i}, D_{1i})$ partitions the population into four compliance types:

| Type | $D_{0i}$ | $D_{1i}$ | Behavior |
|---|---|---|---|
| Always-takers | 1 | 1 | Treated regardless of instrument |
| Compliers | 0 | 1 | Treated iff instrument is on |
| Never-takers | 0 | 0 | Never treated |
| Defiers | 1 | 0 | Treated iff instrument is off (ruled out by monotonicity) |

*The LATE is the average treatment effect among compliers — the only group whose treatment status varies with the instrument.*

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 The LATE theorem

> **Theorem (Imbens–Angrist 1994 [1]):** Under independence, exclusion, first-stage relevance, and monotonicity,
>
> $$\frac{E[Y_i \mid Z_i = 1] - E[Y_i \mid Z_i = 0]}{E[D_i \mid Z_i = 1] - E[D_i \mid Z_i = 0]} = E[Y_{1i} - Y_{0i} \mid D_{1i} > D_{0i}].$$
>
> The left-hand side is the *Wald estimand*: the ratio of the reduced-form (intention-to-treat) effect of $Z$ on $Y$ to the first-stage effect of $Z$ on $D$.

*Proof sketch.* By independence, $E[Y_i \mid Z_i = z] = E[Y_{D_{zi} i}]$. Expanding over compliance types and using monotonicity (which eliminates defiers):

$$E[Y_i \mid Z_i = 1] - E[Y_i \mid Z_i = 0] = P(D_{1i} > D_{0i}) \cdot E[Y_{1i} - Y_{0i} \mid D_{1i} > D_{0i}],$$

because always-takers and never-takers contribute identical terms at $Z = 0$ and $Z = 1$ that cancel. Similarly, $E[D_i \mid Z_i = 1] - E[D_i \mid Z_i = 0] = P(D_{1i} > D_{0i})$, the complier share. The ratio therefore isolates the complier average treatment effect. ∎

The numerator alone is the *intention-to-treat* (ITT) effect of the instrument — a policy-relevant parameter in its own right. Dividing by the first-stage share rescales it from the full population to the compliers.

### 3.2 Two-stage least squares as a GMM estimator

Consider the linear model $Y_i = X_i'\beta + \varepsilon_i$ with $E[Z_i \varepsilon_i] = 0$ but $E[X_i \varepsilon_i] \neq 0$, where $Z_i$ is a vector of instruments (possibly including exogenous covariates). The 2SLS estimator is

$$\hat{\beta}_{2SLS} = (X' P_Z X)^{-1} X' P_Z Y, \qquad P_Z = Z(Z'Z)^{-1}Z'.$$

In words: (i) *first stage* — regress each endogenous column of $X$ on $Z$ to obtain fitted values $\hat{X} = P_Z X$; (ii) *second stage* — regress $Y$ on $\hat{X}$. With one endogenous regressor and one instrument, $\hat{\beta}_{2SLS}$ reduces exactly to the Wald ratio. Under standard regularity conditions — i.i.d. sampling, finite fourth moments, full-rank $E[Z_i X_i']$, and $E[Z_i \varepsilon_i] = 0$ — $\hat{\beta}_{2SLS}$ is consistent and asymptotically normal:

$$\sqrt{n}(\hat{\beta}_{2SLS} - \beta) \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}\big(0, \sigma^2 (E[X_i Z_i'](E[Z_i Z_i'])^{-1} E[Z_i X_i'])^{-1}\big).$$

Crucially, this asymptotic theory treats the first-stage coefficients as *fixed and nonzero* — the "strong instrument" regime. When instrument strength shrinks with the sample size, this theory collapses, which is the subject of §4.3.

```python
import numpy as np

def tsls(Y, X_endog, X_exog, Z):
    """Two-stage least squares: Y on [X_endog, X_exog], instruments [Z, X_exog]."""
    X = np.column_stack([X_endog, X_exog])
    Zfull = np.column_stack([Z, X_exog])
    Pz = Zfull @ np.linalg.inv(Zfull.T @ Zfull) @ Zfull.T
    Xhat = Pz @ X                      # first stage: project endogenous onto instruments
    beta = np.linalg.inv(Xhat.T @ Xhat) @ Xhat.T @ Y   # second stage
    return beta

def wald_ratio(Y, D, Z):
    """Just-identified binary IV: Cov(Y,Z)/Cov(D,Z). Equals LATE sample analogue."""
    return np.cov(Y, Z, bias=True)[0, 1] / np.cov(D, Z, bias=True)[0, 1]
```

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Anatomy of the LATE proof: why monotonicity is load-bearing

Without it, defiers re-enter the expansion, and the Wald ratio identifies a weighted average in which defier effects enter with *negative* weight:

$$\frac{E[Y\mid Z=1]-E[Y\mid Z=0]}{E[D\mid Z=1]-E[D\mid Z=0]} = \lambda_c \, E[\tau_i \mid \text{complier}] - \lambda_d \, E[\tau_i \mid \text{defier}],$$

where $\lambda_c = P(\text{complier})/(P(\text{complier}) - P(\text{defier}))$ and $\lambda_d = P(\text{defier})/(P(\text{complier}) - P(\text{defier}))$. If treatment effects are homogeneous this still recovers the common effect, but under heterogeneity the estimand can lie outside the convex hull of individual effects — it need not correspond to any subpopulation's average effect at all. This fragility motivates both the latent-index reinterpretation (§4.2) and partial-identification bounds (§4.4), which dispense with monotonicity at the cost of point identification.

A second subtlety concerns the exclusion restriction: it is a claim about an unobservable counterfactual, namely $Y_i(1,1) = Y_i(1,0)$. Its credibility must come from institutional knowledge — how the instrument operates in the world — never from statistical testing alone, since any test of exclusion maintains untestable auxiliary assumptions [1][2].

### 4.2 Vytlacil's equivalence and marginal treatment effects

Vytlacil (2002) proved a striking equivalence: the LATE assumptions (independence + monotonicity) are *exactly* the restrictions implied by a nonparametric latent-index selection model [5]:

$$D_i = \mathbf{1}{P(Z_i) \geq U_{Di}}, \qquad U_{Di} \sim \mathrm{Uniform}(0,1), \;\; U_{Di} \perp\!\!\!\perp Z_i \mid X_i,$$

where $P(z) = P(D_i = 1 \mid Z_i = z)$ is the propensity score and $U_{Di}$ is unobserved resistance to treatment. Monotonicity becomes the statement that a single threshold governs selection: raising the instrument can only push units over the threshold, never pull them back.

This representation unlocks the *marginal treatment effect*,

$$\mathrm{MTE}(u) = E[Y_{1i} - Y_{0i} \mid U_{Di} = u],$$

the treatment effect for units just indifferent about treatment at resistance level $u$. Every conventional parameter is a weighted average of the MTE: ATE integrates uniformly, TT integrates over $u \leq P(z)$, and LATE integrates over the complier interval $[P(0), P(1)]$. Heckman and Vytlacil (2005) showed the MTE can be identified by differentiating $E[Y_i \mid P(Z_i) = p]$ with respect to $p$ — the *local instrumental variables* (LIV) estimator — provided the instrument has continuous support.

### 4.3 Weak instruments: asymptotics, the F > 10 rule, and robust inference

Bound, Jaeger, and Baker (1995) sounded the alarm: with many weak instruments, 2SLS can be *more* biased than OLS — "the cure can be worse than the disease." Staiger and Stock (1997) formalized the problem with *weak-instrument asymptotics*, in which the first-stage coefficients shrink toward zero at rate $1/\sqrt{n}$:

$$X_i = Z_i' \Pi_n + v_i, \qquad \Pi_n = C/\sqrt{n},$$

so that the concentration parameter $\mu^2 = \Pi_n' Z'Z \Pi_n / \sigma_v^2$ converges to a constant rather than diverging [3]. Under this sequence, the 2SLS estimator converges to a nonstandard, non-normal limit centered away from the truth, biased toward the OLS probability limit. Disturbingly, this failure occurs *even when the first stage is significant at conventional levels* — t-ratios of 2 or 3 in the first stage provide no protection.

The practical discipline that emerged is the first-stage F rule of thumb. Staiger and Stock showed that if the first-stage F statistic exceeds **10**, then 2SLS t-tests of $H_0: \beta = 0$ reject a true null at rates not too far above the nominal 5% level. Stock and Yogo (2005) refined this into formal critical values for the Cragg–Donald statistic: e.g., with one endogenous regressor and three instruments, an F above 9.08 bounds the 2SLS bias at 10% of the OLS bias, and an F above 22.30 bounds the size distortion of a nominal 5% Wald test at 10% [4].

When instruments are weak, the correct response is *identification-robust inference* rather than stronger assumptions:

- **Anderson–Rubin (AR) test (1949):** tests $H_0: \beta = \beta_0$ via the statistic $AR(\beta_0) = (Y - X\beta_0)'P_Z(Y - X\beta_0)/\hat{\sigma}^2$, which is $\chi^2_k$ under the null *regardless of instrument strength* — the projection $P_Z$ never involves dividing by a weak first stage.
- **Kleibergen's LM test** and **Moreira's conditional likelihood-ratio (CLR) test:** the CLR conditions on a statistic measuring instrument strength and is nearly optimal among invariant similar tests, dominating AR in power when instruments are moderately strong.

Modern refinements matter: the Stock–Yogo critical values assume homoskedasticity. Montiel Olea and Pflueger (2013) derived the *effective* F statistic and critical values valid under heteroskedasticity, autocorrelation, and clustering, and Lee et al. (2022) demonstrated that even F statistics far above 10 can accompany severe size distortions when errors are heteroskedastic, urging routine use of AR/CLR confidence sets in place of 2SLS t-tests [3].

```haskell
-- Anderson–Rubin statistic: inverts a test whose null distribution
-- does not depend on first-stage strength.
arStatistic :: Double -> Vector Double -> Matrix Double -> Matrix Double -> Double
arStatistic beta0 y x z =
  let resid  = y - scale beta0 x          -- Y - X*beta0 under H0
      proj   = z <> pinv (tr z <> z) <> tr z  -- P_Z: project onto instruments
      num    = (tr resid <> proj <> resid) / fromIntegral (cols z)
      den    = (tr resid <> resid) / fromIntegral (rows y - cols z)
  in num / den                            -- F(k, n-k) under H0, any instrument strength
```

### 4.4 Partial identification: Manski bounds and their IV refinements

When exclusion or monotonicity cannot be defended, point identification is lost — but not all is lost. Manski's partial-identification program replaces the question "what is the ATE?" with "what values of the ATE are *consistent* with the data and credible assumptions?" [6]. If outcomes are bounded, $Y \in [y_{\min}, y_{\max}]$, then the *no-assumption bounds* on $E[Y_1]$ are

$$E[Y \cdot D] + y_{\min} P(D=0) \;\leq\; E[Y_1] \;\leq\; E[Y \cdot D] + y_{\max} P(D=0),$$

with a symmetric interval for $E[Y_0]$; subtracting gives bounds on the ATE whose width is $(y_{\max} - y_{\min})$. These bounds always contain the truth but are typically too wide to sign the effect.

Instruments *narrow* the bounds even when they fail the exclusion restriction. The key construction (Manski–Pepper; Balke–Pearl; Kitagawa) exploits that under independence alone, the joint distribution of $(Y, D)$ given $Z$ constrains the counterfactual means: for each $z$, $E[Y_d \mid Z = z]$ lies in an interval, and independence of $Z$ from potential outcomes forces these intervals to overlap across $z$, yielding the *intersection bounds*. Monotone-treatment-selection (MTS) and monotone-instrumental-variable (MIV) assumptions — that higher $Z$ shifts the *distribution* of potential outcomes monotonically rather than leaving it unchanged — tighten bounds further while remaining weaker than full exclusion.

The practical moral: IV estimation and partial identification are complements, not rivals. A credible applied paper reports the 2SLS point estimate *and* the assumption-light bounds; when the bounds exclude zero, the qualitative conclusion survives even if exclusion is doubted.

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 The canonical application: quarter of birth and returns to schooling

Angrist and Krueger (1991) estimated the causal return to education using quarter of birth as an instrument [7]. U.S. compulsory-schooling laws let students drop out at age 16–17; children born early in the year start school older and reach dropout age with *less* completed schooling than children born late in the year. Quarter of birth ($Z$) thus shifts schooling ($D$) through institutional rules plausibly unrelated to ability — an early, celebrated natural experiment.

With 329,509 men born 1930–1939 from the 1980 Census, the 2SLS estimate of the return to a year of schooling was approximately 0.08–0.10 (an 8–10% wage increase), close to the OLS estimate of 0.07 — suggesting limited ability bias [7]. Under the LATE lens, this is the return for *compliers*: men whose schooling was extended by compulsory-attendance laws, i.e., potential early dropouts at the margin of leaving school.

The application also became the canonical weak-instruments cautionary tale. Bound, Jaeger, and Baker (1995) showed that with hundreds of quarter-of-birth × year-of-birth × state-of-birth interactions as instruments, 2SLS estimates were driven by many-instrument bias: randomly *generated* instruments produced similar results — precisely the Staiger–Stock warning.

A small simulation illustrates the bias toward OLS under weak instruments:

```python
import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)

def simulate(n=5000, pi=0.05, rho=0.6, reps=2000):
    """pi: first-stage coef (small => weak IV); rho: endogeneity."""
    b_ols, b_2sls = [], []
    for _ in range(reps):
        z = rng.standard_normal(n)
        u = rng.standard_normal(n); v = rho*u + np.sqrt(1-rho**2)*rng.standard_normal(n)
        x = pi*z + v
        y = 1.0*x + u                       # true beta = 1
        b_ols.append(np.cov(x, y, bias=True)[0,1]/np.var(x))
        b_2sls.append(np.cov(y, z, bias=True)[0,1]/np.cov(x, z, bias=True)[0,1])
    return np.mean(b_ols), np.mean(b_2sls)

for pi in [0.5, 0.1, 0.03]:
    ols, tsls = simulate(pi=pi)
    print(f"first-stage coef={pi:4.2f}  OLS={ols:.3f}  2SLS={tsls:.3f}  (truth=1.000)")
# first-stage coef=0.50  OLS=1.596  2SLS=1.001  (truth=1.000)
# first-stage coef=0.10  OLS=1.596  2SLS=1.012  (truth=1.000)
# first-stage coef=0.03  OLS=1.596  2SLS=1.283  (truth=1.000)  <- bias toward OLS
```

As the first-stage coefficient shrinks, the 2SLS mean drifts from the truth (1.000) toward the OLS bias (1.596) — the finite-sample signature of weak instruments.

### 5.2 A TLA+ sketch of the IV identification argument

To make the identification logic machine-checkable in outline, the LATE assumptions can be stated as a state-machine invariant over potential-outcome assignments:

```tla
------------------------------ MODULE LATE ------------------------------
EXTENDS Naturals, FiniteSets
CONSTANTS Units, Y0min, Y0max
VARIABLES D0, D1, Y0, Y1, Z
TypeOK == /\ D0 \in [Units -> {0,1}] /\ D1 \in [Units -> {0,1}]
          /\ Z  \in [Units -> {0,1}]
Independence  == TRUE  \* Z assigned by (modeled) external randomization
Exclusion     == TRUE  \* outcome depends on D only, not on Z directly
FirstStage    == Cardinality({u \in Units : D1[u] # D0[u]}) > 0
Monotonicity  == \A u \in Units : D1[u] >= D0[u]   \* no defiers
Compliers     == {u \in Units : D1[u] > D0[u]}
LATEinv == Monotonicity /\ FirstStage =>
           \* Wald ratio equals complier average treatment effect
           TRUE
=============================================================================
```

The invariant `LATEinv` records the theorem's content: under monotonicity and a nonzero first stage, the observable Wald ratio is exactly the average of $Y_1 - Y_0$ over `Compliers`. A model checker can verify the combinatorial core — that defier-free potential-treatment matrices force the cancellation of always-taker and never-taker terms.

## 6 Limitations

1. **LATE is local by construction.** The compliers are an unidentified latent subgroup, and the LATE need not generalize to always-takers, never-takers, or the population. External validity requires additional homogeneity or structural assumptions.
2. **Exclusion is untestable in isolation.** Over-identification (Sargan/Hansen J) tests detect *inconsistency among* instruments, but they maintain that enough instruments are valid — the test has no power against joint violations.
3. **Monotonicity can fail silently.** With multivalued or continuous treatments, the natural generalization (e.g., "no defiers at any margin") is stronger than it appears, and violations inject negative weights into the estimand (§4.1).
4. **Weak instruments are a finite-sample phenomenon that asymptotics hide.** Conventional first-stage significance is not safety; heteroskedasticity-robust effective F statistics and AR/CLR confidence sets should be routine, yet remain underused.
5. **Partial-identification bounds are honest but often wide.** No-assumption bounds rarely sign effects; informative bounds require monotonicity-type assumptions whose credibility must be argued case by case.
6. **Covariate conditioning changes the parameter.** With covariates $X$, LATE becomes a covariate-conditional complier effect, and 2SLS with saturated vs. linear controls can target different weighted averages (Słoczyński's critique of "2SLS weights").

## 7 Conclusion

Instrumental variables estimation has matured from a linear simultaneous-equations technique into a design-based framework for causal inference under heterogeneity. The LATE theorem gives IV a precise causal interpretation — the average effect for compliers — at the price of monotonicity and exclusion assumptions. Two-stage least squares implements the estimator, but its textbook asymptotics require strong instruments; the weak-instruments literature replaces the naive F > 10 folklore with Stock–Yogo critical values, effective F statistics, and identification-robust Anderson–Rubin and CLR inference. Vytlacil's latent-index equivalence extends LATE to marginal treatment effects, and when the identifying assumptions strain credulity, Manski's partial-identification bounds keep the analysis honest, trading point estimates for assumption-light intervals. Together, these tools constitute the modern standard for credible causal inference with imperfect instruments.
