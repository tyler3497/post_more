# Verified Compilation: Forward Simulations, the CompCert Pipeline, the CakeML Verified Stack, and the Vellvm Formalization of LLVM IR

## Abstract

This thesis gives a unified technical account of machine-checked compiler correctness, centered on three landmark systems: CompCert, the CakeML verified compiler, and the Vellvm formalization of LLVM IR. We develop the theory of semantic preservation through *forward simulation* relations — lock-step, plus, and star diagrams — and show how CompCert composes such simulations across its pipeline from Clight through Cminor, RTL, LTL, and Mach to assembly, with register allocation, parallel-move serialization, and instruction scheduling handled by verified algorithms and validators. We contrast this with the CakeML stack, a bootstrapped end-to-end verified ML implementation in HOL4 reaching x86-64, ARMv8, and RISC-V, whose divergence-preservation argument rests on a clock-indexed big-step semantics. We then analyze Vellvm's nondeterministic small-step semantics for LLVM IR and its verified SSA optimizations, before comparing verified compilers against translation validation in the style of Pnueli, Siegel, and Singerman and its modern descendant Alive2. We close with empirical evidence on proof scale and bug-finding, and an assessment of the trusted computing base these systems cannot eliminate.

## 1. Introduction

Ken Thompson's 1984 lecture "Reflections on Trusting Trust" asked whether we can trust our compilers: a miscompiling compiler undermines every argument made about the source code. The industrial answer has been testing — randomized differential testing, as in Csmith, has found hundreds of bugs in GCC and LLVM — but testing demonstrates only the presence of miscompilations, never their absence [1]. Verified compilation takes the opposite bet: the compiler, and the correspondence between its input and output, are proved correct *once and for all* inside a proof assistant, so every subsequent compilation carries a machine-checked guarantee.

Three projects define the modern state of the art. **CompCert** (Leroy et al.) is a realistic optimizing C compiler written and proved correct in Coq, used in safety-critical domains [2]. **CakeML** (Kumar, Myreen, Norrish, Owens) is a verified implementation of Standard ML in HOL4 that is *bootstrapped*: the machine-checked correctness theorem applies directly to the running binary [3][4]. **Vellvm** (Zhao, Nagarakatte, Martin, Zdancewic) formalizes the semantics of LLVM's intermediate representation in Coq so that aggressive program transformations can be verified against a dynamic semantics [5][6].

This thesis examines the shared mathematical core of these efforts — forward simulation — the engineering of the CompCert pass pipeline, the bootstrapping architecture of CakeML, the nondeterministic operational semantics of Vellvm, and the trade-offs between fully verified compilers and per-compilation translation validation [7][8].

## 2. Background

### 2.1 A brief history of compiler verification

Compiler correctness is among the oldest problems in formal methods. McCarthy and Painter gave the first mechanized-style correctness proof of a compiler for arithmetic expressions in 1967, and Milner and Weyhrauch explored compiler verification in LCF in the 1970s. The turning point was the arrival of mature proof assistants with extraction — Coq and HOL4 — which made it feasible to *program the compiler in the logic* and extract executable code, so that the artifact and the proof cannot drift apart.

### 2.2 What "correctness" means

Following Leroy [2], correctness is *semantic preservation*: the compiled program exhibits only behaviors the source program could exhibit. Fix for each language a notion of *observable behavior* — **termination** with an observable trace of input/output events (system calls), **divergence** with a possibly infinite trace, and **going wrong** (an operation with no defined semantics, e.g. an out-of-bounds access) after a finite trace. Write *S* ⇓ *B* for "source program *S* executes with behavior *B*". The compiler's guarantee is then:

> **Theorem:** If *S* is a source program and *C* its compilation, then whenever *S* ⇓ *B*, we have *C* ⇓ *B′* for some *B′* that *improves upon* *B*.

Improvement (*B′* ⊑ *B*) is the crucial subtlety [2]: *B′* may be *more defined* than *B*. If *S* goes wrong, *C* may do anything; conversely — the direction that matters for safety — *if S does not go wrong, neither does C*. This yields the usable corollary:

> If *S* ⊨ Spec ⇒ *C* ⊨ Spec for every safety specification Spec proved of the source.

Note the asymmetry: we prove preservation of *defined* behaviors, not preservation of *wrongness*. A compiler that introduced new crashes in well-defined programs would be broken; a compiler that happens to make an already-crashing program crash differently is acceptable, because the source-level verification was vacuous anyway.

### 2.3 Semantic styles and their consequences

The three systems in this thesis make different foundational choices. CompCert gives its intermediate languages *small-step* (transition) semantics, which compose naturally with simulation diagrams over execution steps. CakeML gives its source language a *big-step* semantics indexed by a clock, which makes compiler proofs proceed by structural induction — but requires a separate small-step semantics, proved equivalent, to even *state* divergence. Vellvm gives LLVM IR a small-step semantics with *nondeterministic* choice, because LLVM's specification leaves the value of `undef` and the results of underdetermined operations deliberately unspecified.

## 3. Methodology

### 3.1 Forward simulation

The workhorse of verified compilation is the *forward simulation*: a relation *R* between source and target states such that every source step is matched, possibly with stuttering, by target steps. Leroy's framework distinguishes three diagram shapes [2]: **lock-step** (each source step matched by exactly one target step), **plus** (one or more target steps — used when the target is more fine-grained), and **star** (zero or more target steps, guarded by a well-founded *measure* that decreases on stuttering, ruling out infinite stuttering that would let the target diverge while the source terminates).

A forward simulation lifts to behavior preservation: termination and divergence are preserved forward, and "going wrong" is preserved backward. For *deterministic* target languages, forward simulation is essentially complete — the standard, minimal proof obligation.

> **Theorem:** (Simulation implies semantic preservation.) If every execution step of *S* is matched by a forward simulation into *C*, and *S* ⇓ *B* with *B* not going wrong, then *C* ⇓ *B′* for *B′* improving *B*. [2]

*Backward* simulation (every target step matched by a source step) gives a stronger guarantee but is rarely proved directly; CompCert instead exploits target-language determinism to derive the needed consequences from forward diagrams alone.

### 3.2 Verified compilers vs. verified validators vs. translation validation

There are three distinct architectures for obtaining a correctness guarantee:

| Approach | What is proved | When the proof runs | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Verified compiler** | The compiler *program* preserves semantics for *all* inputs | Once, at compiler-construction time | CompCert, CakeML |
| **Verified validator** | A *checker* accepts only correct translations; the optimizer itself is untrusted | Once for the checker; per compilation for the check | CompCert's instruction scheduling [9] |
| **Translation validation** | For *this particular* (source, target) pair, the target refines the source | Per compilation, by an unverified (or separately trusted) tool | Pnueli et al. [7], Alive2 |

Verified validators are the pragmatic middle ground [9]: a clever but hairy optimization runs as ordinary unverified code, and a simple, proved checker verifies its output — the compiler's trusted proof burden shrinks to the checker. Translation validation goes further and checks each compilation with no prior proof about the compiler at all — flexible, but the validator becomes part of the trusted base of every build, and a validator bug silently voids the guarantee.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 The CompCert pipeline: from Clight to assembly

CompCert compiles a large subset of C through a tower of roughly twenty intermediate languages and passes [2]. The principal IRs are:

- **Clight**: a side-effect-free-expression subset of C with a formal semantics (Blazy and Leroy [10]); the *trusted* start of the verified pipeline (the unverified C parser/elaborator are outside the proof).
- **Cminor**: a minimal, low-level imperative language with explicit stack-allocated locals — the last language that still looks like a source language.
- **CminorSel**: Cminor after instruction selection, with operators rewritten to match target addressing modes.
- **RTL** (register transfer language): an infinite-register, control-flow-graph language in which most optimizations run — constant propagation, CSE, dead-code elimination, inlining, tail calls.
- **LTL** (location transfer language): RTL after *register allocation*, where pseudo-registers are mapped to machine registers or stack slots.
- **Mach**: abstract machine language with explicit activation records, one step above assembly.
- **Asm**: the target assembly (x86-32/64, ARM, AArch64, RISC-V, PowerPC), with a formal semantics of the relevant instruction subset.

Each pass is proved correct by a forward simulation between the small-step semantics of its input and output languages, and the whole-compiler theorem is the *composition* of these simulations — a new optimization is a new IR-to-IR simulation proof, and the end-to-end theorem keeps its shape. Typical passes are lock-step or plus simulations; passes that delete code need star diagrams with a well-founded measure to rule out infinite stuttering.

```coq
(* Schematic shape of a CompCert forward-simulation theorem *)
Theorem transf_program_correct:
  forall p tp, transf_program p = OK tp ->
  forall beh, program_behaves (semantics p) beh ->
  exists beh', program_behaves (semantics tp) beh' /\ behavior_improves beh beh'.
```

This single lemma shape, instantiated per pass, is what makes a 20-pass verified pipeline tractable: the proof assistant checks each local diagram, and transitivity of behavior improvement does the rest.

### 4.2 Verified register allocation, parallel moves, and scheduling

Register allocation best illustrates why CompCert usually verifies the *result* rather than the *algorithm*: proving a graph-coloring allocator with coalescing correct would mean reasoning about interference graphs, heuristics, and spill code in one monolithic argument. Instead, CompCert factors the problem:

1. **Parallel moves** — the heart of calling-convention and allocation code, where simultaneous register-to-register copies must be serialized — are handled by a *verified algorithm*. Rideau, Serpette, and Leroy proved in Coq that every parallel-move problem serializes using at most one temporary register per register class, with an inductive specification and an executable version whose extracted code ships in the compiler [11].
2. **Instruction scheduling** (list scheduling over superblocks) is unverified code paired with a *verified validator* in the style of Tristan and Leroy [9]: the validator replays the schedule against the data-dependence graph and rejects any reordering that violates a dependence.
3. The coloring itself is validated against its key invariants (interfering live ranges receive distinct locations), keeping the trusted reasoning proportional to the *specification* rather than the *heuristic*.

This three-way split — verified algorithm where the algorithm is clean (parallel moves), verified validator where the algorithm is heuristic (scheduling, coloring) — is CompCert's signature methodological contribution.

### 4.3 The CakeML verified stack: bootstrapping ML to machine code

CakeML attacks a strictly harder problem than CompCert: not just compiling, but *self-hosting*. The CakeML compiler is written in the CakeML language itself (a substantial subset of Standard ML), verified in HOL4, and bootstrapped: the verified compiler compiles its own source to produce a verified *binary*, so no unverified compiler is in the loop at all [3].

The architecture descends through a verified tower: source AST through closure conversion and lambda lifting, a data-flow IR, a word-level language, stack and lab languages, down to concrete machine code, with a verified garbage collector, bignum library, and basis runtime generated in the same development [4]. Targets include x86-64, ARMv6, ARMv8, and RISC-V.

Two technical ideas deserve emphasis. First, CakeML's source semantics is a *functional big-step* semantics with an explicit clock: evaluation either returns a value or times out. A program diverges iff it times out for *every* clock value, and compiler correctness is proved by induction on the big-step derivation — far more tractable than coinduction over small steps. Second, because everything through lexing, parsing, type inference, compilation, GC, and printing is verified, the project built a verified *REPL* covered by the same theorem [3] — arguably the deepest end-to-end verification ever completed for a general-purpose language implementation, recognized with the 2024 Most Influential POPL Paper award.

### 4.4 Vellvm: a dynamic semantics for LLVM IR

Vellvm takes the third path: rather than verifying a fixed compiler, it *formalizes the intermediate representation itself* so that transformations over it can be verified. Zhao, Nagarakatte, Martin, and Zdancewic gave LLVM IR a rigorous Coq semantics: static typing rules, a typed memory model with block-based allocation and permissions, and several small-step operational semantics, including a nondeterministic one in which underdetermined operations may take any consistent value [5].

The methodology is validation by *executable semantics*: Coq extraction produces an interpreter for LLVM IR, compared differentially against LLVM's own reference interpreter and compiled code over the LLVM test suite. Agreement on the test suite does not prove the semantics right, but it grounds the formalism in observable reality — a discipline every formal semantics of a real artifact should adopt.

The payoff came in two case studies. First, a verified implementation of **SoftBound**, a memory-safety instrumentation pass, was extracted from the Coq development and plugged directly into LLVM — verified instrumentation for an unverified compiler [5]. Second, Zhao et al. verified a suite of **SSA-based optimizations** — dominator computation, SSA construction, global value numbering, loop-invariant code motion, dead-store elimination — against the Vellvm semantics [6]. The project's later evolution toward interaction-tree-based, modular semantics shows the research direction: compositional, executable semantics that scale to concurrency and richer language features.

```haskell
-- Vellvm's nondeterministic small-step judgment (simplified)
--   Γ ; M ; (l, ρ, δ)  --→  Γ ; M' ; (l', ρ', δ')
-- M: typed memory, ρ: value environment,
-- δ: oracle resolving undef/uninitialized reads.
```

### 4.5 Translation validation vs. verified compilers

Pnueli, Siegel, and Singerman's translation validation [7] observes that proving a compiler correct for all inputs is often infeasible — the compiler is too large, too legacy, or too aggressively optimized — while proving *this particular translation* correct is a bounded, per-run problem, discharged by encoding source and target semantics as verification conditions. Necula's PLDI 2000 work made it practical for real optimizing compilers [8], and CompCert's verified validators [9] can be seen as translation validation with the validator itself machine-checked.

The modern heir is **Alive2**, a bounded translation-validation tool for LLVM's IR: for each peephole optimization, it asks an SMT solver whether the target refines the source under LLVM's formalized semantics, including its subtle treatment of `undef` and `poison` [5][6]. Alive2 has *found real miscompilation bugs* in LLVM's optimizer — the empirical complement to Vellvm's foundational work.

The trade-off is crisp. A verified compiler amortizes one large proof over infinitely many compilations and removes the compiler from the per-build trusted base — but it constrains the compiler to what can be proved, which is why CompCert optimizes less aggressively than LLVM. Translation validation imposes no such constraint and applies to legacy compilers — but every build trusts the validator, and bounded validation can miss deep bugs. In practice the approaches compose: verify the compiler where feasible, validate translations where it is not, and use the formal semantics (à la Vellvm) as the common ground.

## 5. Empirical Results and Proofs

**Proof scale.** The CompCert development is on the order of a hundred thousand lines of Coq, the large majority proof rather than compiler code — roughly an order of magnitude more proof than program, typical for simulation-based verification. CakeML's HOL4 development is of comparable scale, spanning frontend, multi-IR backend, garbage collector, and bignum library [4]; Vellvm's covers the full IR semantics plus verified optimization case studies [5][6]. These are person-decade-scale artifacts, refuting the once-common claim that verified compilation cannot scale beyond toy languages.

**Validation against reality.** Vellvm's extracted interpreter was run against LLVM's test suite and LLVM's own reference interpreter with behavior compared differentially [5]. CompCert is validated continuously against the GCC test suite and extensive randomized testing; notably, years of differential testing have found *no miscompilation bugs* in CompCert's verified passes, while regularly finding bugs in the unverified frontend and in competing compilers [2]. CakeML's bootstrapping is the strongest possible self-check: the verified binary compiles the compiler's own source [3].

**Bugs found by the alternatives.** Alive2's SMT-based refinement checks have uncovered genuine miscompilations in LLVM's peephole optimizer — bugs that survived LLVM's test suite because they lurk in the interaction between `undef`/`poison` semantics and aggressive rewriting.

| System | Assistant | Source → Target | Guarantee | Scale signal |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| CompCert [2] | Coq | C (Clight) → Asm (x86/ARM/RISC-V/…) | Forward simulation per pass; behavior preservation | ~10⁵ lines of Coq; zero miscompilations found in verified passes by differential testing |
| CakeML [3][4] | HOL4 | SML → x86-64/ARMv8/RISC-V machine code | End-to-end, bootstrapped; divergence preserved via clock | Full stack incl. GC, bignums, REPL; Most Influential POPL Paper 2024 |
| Vellvm [5][6] | Coq | LLVM IR → LLVM IR (transformations) | Nondeterministic small-step semantics; verified SSA opts | Extracted interpreter validated vs. LLVM test suite; verified SoftBound pass |
| Alive2 (TV) | SMT (Z3) | LLVM IR → LLVM IR, per optimization | Bounded refinement per translation | Found real LLVM miscompilations |

## 6. Limitations

Verified compilation narrows the trusted computing base; it does not eliminate it. The honest limitations:

1. **The proof assistant and extraction.** CompCert and Vellvm trust the Coq kernel, the OCaml extraction pipeline, and the OCaml toolchain. CakeML trusts the HOL4 kernel. A bug in any of these voids every theorem. (CakeML's bootstrapping removes the *compiler* from the TCB but not the *prover*.)
2. **The unverified edges.** CompCert's C parser, elaboration to Clight, assembler, and linker are unverified; the hardware is unverified. The theorem covers Clight-to-assembly, and everything outside that interval is ordinary test-covered software. CakeML closes more gaps (verified lexer/parser) but still trusts HOL4 and the machine.
3. **Semantic gaps.** Vellvm models a large subset of LLVM IR, but concurrency, vectors, and some intrinsics remain outside the proved envelope, and LLVM's specification evolves faster than any formalization.
4. **Optimization strength.** CompCert performs fewer, simpler optimizations than GCC or LLVM; its code is typically within a modest factor of unverified compilers. For performance-critical code, users still reach for LLVM — which is exactly why Vellvm-style verification of LLVM's own transformations, and Alive2-style validation, matter.
5. **Specification fidelity.** All three systems verify against *their formalization* of the language. Where the formalization disagrees with the de facto standard — C's underspecified evaluation order, LLVM's evolving `poison` semantics — the proof guarantees the formalized behavior, and differential validation (as Vellvm practices) is the only bridge back to reality.
6. **Human cost.** Person-decades of proof engineering per system. Simulation proofs are labor-intensive, brittle under language evolution, and demand rare expertise — verified compilation remains a technique for high-assurance niches rather than the default.

## 7. Conclusion

Verified compilation has moved, in two decades, from a research curiosity to an engineering discipline with a shared mathematical core. The forward simulation — lock-step, plus, star — gives a uniform proof obligation composing across twenty-pass pipelines; verified validators let heuristic algorithms coexist with machine-checked guarantees; bootstrapping, as in CakeML, extends the theorem to the running binary; and formal IR semantics, as in Vellvm, turn the optimizer itself into a verification target. Translation validation remains the indispensable complement for compilers that cannot or will not be verified.

The deepest lesson is architectural: *correctness composes when the proof obligation is local*. Each CompCert pass, each CakeML IR, each Vellvm transformation carries its own simulation proof, and the end-to-end theorem is just transitivity — not heroic monolithic proofs, but small, checkable, composable arguments. Thompson asked whether we can trust our compilers. The answer these systems give is: not by trust at all, but by proof — checked by a machine, one simulation diagram at a time.
