# Cryptographic Accumulators and Vector Commitments: A Unified Study of RSA, Class-Group, Merkle, and KZG Constructions

## Abstract

Cryptographic accumulators and vector commitments compress large collections into short digests that still support *individual* membership or opening proofs, underpinning stateless clients, authenticated logs, and modern zero-knowledge infrastructure. This thesis unifies the four dominant families: RSA accumulators from Benaloh and de Mare's one-way accumulators [1], their trapdoor-free class-group successors [3], Merkle trees and Merkle Mountain Ranges for append-only logs [4][5], and Kate–Zaverucha–Goldberg (KZG) polynomial commitments with pairing-based evaluation proofs [2]. We formalize quasi-commutativity, collision-freeness, and position binding; derive membership and *non-membership* witness mechanics per family; and analyze batching techniques that collapse many proofs into constant-sized aggregates [3][7]. A comparative evaluation of witness sizes, update costs, and verifier work shows no single family dominates: RSA/class-group accumulators give constant-size witnesses with costly witness maintenance; Merkle structures give cheap updates with logarithmic proofs; KZG gives constant-size positional openings at the cost of a structured-reference-string trusted setup. We close with applications to certificate transparency and stateless blockchains, and isolate open problems in transparent setup, post-quantum succinctness, and sublinear witness updates.

## 1. Introduction

The question *how does a verifier who cannot store a dataset still obtain cryptographic certainty about its contents?* recurs across systems design: a web browser must audit a certificate authority's logs [4], a light client must verify a blockchain's state without downloading gigabytes of headers, and a prover in a succinct argument must convince a verifier about polynomial evaluations without transmitting the polynomial [2]. Cryptographic accumulators and vector commitments are the two canonical answers. An **accumulator** compresses an *unordered set* into a short digest `A` such that membership of an element `x` can be proved with a witness `w`, while a **vector commitment** commits to an *ordered vector* so that the element at any position `i` can be opened individually.

The lineage is long. Benaloh and de Mare introduced one-way accumulators in 1993 as a decentralized alternative to digital signatures, exploiting the quasi-commutativity of modular exponentiation [1]. Ralph Merkle's hash-tree construction, originally devised for digital signatures, became the universal append-only accumulator of certificate transparency logs and blockchains [4][5]. Three decades later, Kate, Zaverucha, and Goldberg's polynomial commitments married pairings to polynomial evaluation, producing constant-size commitments and constant-size opening proofs that now underpin SNARKs and data-availability sampling [2]. And between these poles, Boneh, Bünz, and Fisch eliminated the RSA trusted setup by moving to class groups of imaginary quadratic order, and showed how to *batch* hundreds of membership proofs into a constant-sized aggregate — the missing primitive for stateless blockchains [3].

This thesis contributes a unified, self-contained exposition: (i) formal definitions and security models shared across families; (ii) a rigorous derivation of the RSA accumulator, its class-group instantiation, Merkle accumulators including Merkle Mountain Ranges, and KZG polynomial commitments with complete opening-proof verification equations; (iii) membership and *non-membership* witness constructions in each setting; (iv) batching techniques — aggregate membership witnesses [3] and KZG multiproofs [7]; (v) a quantitative comparison of the four families; and (vi) applications to certificate transparency and stateless clients, with honest limitations.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 Formal definitions

Following the generalized definition of Barić and Pfitzmann and the uniform modeling of Derler *et al.* [6], an **asymmetric accumulator** is a tuple of algorithms:

- `Setup(1^λ) → (sk, pk)` — generates parameters (possibly a trapdoor).
- `Eval(pk, S) → A` — accumulates a finite set `S` into digest `A`.
- `WitCreate(pk, A, S, x) → w` — creates a membership witness for `x ∈ S`.
- `Verify(pk, A, x, w) → {0,1}` — authenticates `x` against `A`.

A **vector commitment** (Catalano–Fiore) commits to an ordered vector `(m_1, …, m_n)`; `Open` reveals `m_i` at position `i` with a proof that binds both value and position. Polynomial commitments [2] are the dominant vector-commitment instantiation: commit to the interpolation polynomial and open by evaluating it.

> **Theorem (Quasi-commutativity):** A function `f : X × Y → X` is *quasi-commutative* if for all `x ∈ X` and `y₁, y₂ ∈ Y`, `f(f(x, y₁), y₂) = f(f(x, y₂), y₁)`. If `f` is additionally a one-way function, it is a one-way accumulator [1]. Modular exponentiation `f(g, x) = gˣ mod N` is the canonical example.

The security hierarchy is worth stating precisely:

| Property | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **One-wayness** | Given `A = Eval(S)` and `w` for `x ∈ S`, no PPT adversary finds `x' ∉ S` with a valid witness [1]. |
| **Collision-freeness** | No PPT adversary produces `S`, `x ∉ S`, and a valid witness for `x` [6]. Stronger than one-wayness. |
| **Position binding** | For vector commitments: no adversary opens commitment `C` at position `i` to two distinct values. |
| **Undeniability** | Non-membership witnesses cannot contradict membership witnesses. |

## 3. Methodology

Our methodology is analytic and constructive rather than experimental: we reconstruct each scheme from its algebraic substrate, verify every verification equation symbolically, and compare the families on a common axis of *digest size*, *witness size*, *prover cost*, *verifier cost*, and *update communication*. Where engineering numbers are needed (Section 5), we use published benchmarks from the cited implementations [3] and compute asymptotic costs from the underlying group operations.

We adopt standard notation: `λ` is the security parameter, `𝔾₁, 𝔾₂, 𝔾_T` are pairing groups of prime order `p` with generator `g ∈ 𝔾₁`, `e : 𝔾₁ × 𝔾₂ → 𝔾_T` the bilinear map, and `H : {0,1}* → {0,1}^λ` a collision-resistant hash. The RSA setting uses modulus `N = PQ` with `P, Q` safe primes; the class-group setting uses the class group `Cl(Δ)` of an imaginary quadratic order of discriminant `Δ < 0`.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 The RSA accumulator: quasi-commutativity in `ℤ_N*`

Benaloh and de Mare's construction [1] is disarmingly simple. Given modulus `N`, generator `g ∈ ℤ_N*`, and set `S = {x₁, …, xₙ}` (each `xᵢ` mapped to a distinct prime — a detail whose neglect breaks collision-freeness):

```
A = g^(x₁ · x₂ · … · xₙ) mod N            (accumulator value)
wᵢ = g^(Π_{j≠i} xⱼ) mod N                (membership witness for xᵢ)
Verify: wᵢ^xᵢ ≟ A  (mod N)
```

Because exponentiation is quasi-commutative — `g^(xy) = g^(yx)` — accumulation order is irrelevant, and each witness is simply the accumulation of *all other elements*. Deletion is elegant: `A' = wᵢ` removes `xᵢ` without any secret. Addition needs only `A' = A^x`.

The fatal flaw is the **trapdoor**: whoever generated `N` knows `φ(N)` and can forge witnesses for arbitrary `x ∉ S` by reducing exponents modulo `φ(N)`. Sander, Ta-Shma, and Yung showed how to generate RSA moduli of *unknown* factorization, removing this trust assumption — but the construction remained theoretical. Dynamic variants (Camenisch–Lysyanskaya) support efficient witness *updates* when the set changes, a requirement we return to in Section 4.4.

```python
# RSA accumulator: accumulation and verification (educational parameters)
def accumulate(g, primes, N):
    from math import prod
    return pow(g, prod(primes), N)

def witness(g, primes, N, xi):
    from math import prod
    rest = [x for x in primes if x != xi]
    return pow(g, prod(rest), N)

def verify(A, w, xi, N):
    return pow(w, xi, N) == A

N, g = 2537 * 2879, 5          # toy modulus; production needs 2048+ bits
S = [3, 5, 7, 11, 13]          # elements must map to distinct primes
A = accumulate(g, S, N)
assert all(verify(A, witness(g, S, N, x), x, N) for x in S)
```

### 4.2 Class-group accumulators and trapdoor-free batching

Boneh, Bünz, and Fisch [3] observed that groups of *unknown order without any trusted setup* — class groups of imaginary quadratic fields — rescue the RSA accumulator's trust problem while enabling something new: **succinct batching**. The accumulator value is `A = g^{Π xᵢ}` in `Cl(Δ)`, exactly as in RSA, but now *no party* can know the group order, so the strong-RSA assumption replaces trust in a setup ceremony.

The technical core is a family of succinct proof systems in groups of unknown order, extending Wesolowski's proof of correct exponentiation:

> **Theorem (PoE, Wesolowski):** Given `u, w ∈ 𝔾` of unknown order, a prover can convince a verifier that `w = u^x` for public `x` with a proof of size `O(λ)` verified in `O(log x)` group operations, sound under the adaptive-root assumption.

With PoE, Boneh *et al.* construct:

1. **Aggregated membership proofs**: witnesses `wᵢ = g^{Π_{j≠i} xⱼ}` for a batch `{xᵢ}` combine into a single `O(λ)` proof verifiable with a *constant* number of group operations — witness verification amortizes across the batch.
2. **Batch non-membership proofs**: a constant-sized proof that *many* elements are simultaneously absent, using Bézout coefficients: for `y ∉ S`, there exist integers `a, b` with `a·Πxᵢ + b·y = 1`, and the witness `(g^a, b)` satisfies `Verify: (w^y · A^b) = g`.
3. **Positional vector commitments with constant-sized openings and constant-sized parameters** — the first such construction in groups of unknown order.

The price: class-group arithmetic is slow (element sizes ~1–2 KB, exponentiations far costlier than elliptic-curve operations), and witness *updates* remain the Achilles' heel — when the set changes, every witness must be refreshed, and the communication to do so is linear in the number of witnesses unless bridges or batch-update protocols intervene.

```rust
// Conceptual sketch: non-membership witness via Bezout identity
// Given accumulator A = g^{P} where P = prod(S), and y not in S:
fn non_membership_witness(g: &ClassGroup, A: &ClassGroup, P: &BigInt, y: &BigInt)
    -> (ClassGroup, BigInt) {
    let (a, b, _gcd) = extended_gcd(P, y); // a*P + b*y = 1 since gcd(P,y)=1
    let w = g.pow(&a);                     // w = g^a
    // Verify: w^y * A^b == g^{a*y + b*P} == g
    assert!(w.pow(y).mul(&A.pow(&b)) == *g);
    (w, b)
}
```

### 4.3 Merkle accumulators: trees, Mountain Ranges, and transparency logs

Merkle's hash tree [5] is the *dynamic, transparent* accumulator: no setup, no trapdoor, `O(log n)` proofs, `O(log n)` updates. Leaves are `H(dataᵢ)`; each internal node is `H(left ‖ right)`; the root is the digest. Certificate Transparency [4] turned this into planetary infrastructure: every public TLS certificate is appended to a Merkle-tree log, and two proof types make the log auditable:

- **Audit paths** prove a certificate's inclusion: `O(log n)` sibling hashes.
- **Consistency proofs** prove that log root `R₂` (size `n₂`) extends root `R₁` (size `n₁ < n₂`): only `O(log n)` nodes, by decomposing `n₁` into powers of two.

**Merkle Mountain Ranges (MMRs)** optimize the append-only case. An MMR is a list of perfect binary trees ("mountains") whose sizes are the binary decomposition of `n`; appending is amortized `O(1)` and the peaks are bagged into a single root. They power Utreexo-style stateless Bitcoin accumulators and many rollup bridge contracts.

```haskell
-- Merkle Mountain Range: peak bagging and append-only extension
data MMR a = MMR { peaks :: [Hash], leaves :: Int }

append :: HashAlg h => h -> Digest -> MMR h -> MMR h
append h d (MMR ps n) = MMR (mergeUp h (leafHash h d : ps)) (n + 1)
  where
    -- merge the two newest peaks while they have equal height
    mergeUp _ [p]    = [p]
    mergeUp _ (a:b:rest) | height a == height b
                         = mergeUp h (nodeHash h a b : rest)
    mergeUp _ ps'    = ps'

-- Consistency between snapshots is a peak-subset relation:
-- old peaks must reappear (possibly merged) in the new peak list.
consistent :: MMR h -> MMR h -> Bool
consistent (MMR old _) (MMR new _) = all (`elem` closures new) old
  where closures = id  -- simplified; real impl bags peak hashes
```

The fundamental limitation is *proof size*: every inclusion proof is logarithmic, and a stateless client verifying thousands of state reads per block pays `O(k log n)` bandwidth. This is precisely the gap KZG closes.

### 4.4 KZG polynomial commitments: constant-size positional openings

Kate, Zaverucha, and Goldberg [2] asked a sharper question: can the commitment *and* the opening proof both be constant-sized? Their answer uses pairings. To commit to the vector `(m₁, …, mₙ)`, interpolate the unique polynomial `φ` with `φ(i) = mᵢ` over domain `{1, …, n}`, and commit in the exponent of a structured reference string `{g^{τⁱ}}`:

```
Commit:  C = g^{φ(τ)}                     (one G1 element)
Open at z: π = g^{q(τ)},  q(X) = (φ(X) − φ(z)) / (X − z)   (one G1 element)
Verify:  e(C / g^{φ(z)}, g) ≟ e(π, g^{τ} / g^{z})
```

The verification equation is the pairing's way of checking polynomial divisibility: `(X − z) ∣ (φ(X) − φ(z))` iff `φ(z)` is the true evaluation. Position binding reduces to the `t`-strong Diffie–Hellman assumption; the trusted setup (`τ` must be forgotten) is the well-known cost, mitigated by multi-party ceremonies (e.g., Ethereum's KZG ceremony) whose security holds if *one* participant is honest.

**Batching** is where KZG pulls decisively ahead for stateless clients. Boneh, Drake, Fisch, and Gabizon [7] showed a single `𝔾₁` element can prove evaluations of *multiple polynomials at multiple arbitrary points* — a multiproof — so a block's entire state-access list opens against one commitment with one proof. This is the cryptographic engine of Ethereum's EIP-4844 blobs and Verkle-tree migration plans.

| Construction | Digest | Membership proof | Update cost | Setup trust | Non-membership |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| RSA accumulator [1] | `O(λ)` | `O(λ)` | `O(1)` add; witness refresh `O(n)` | Trapdoor (factorization) | Bézout-based `O(λ)` |
| Class-group accum. [3] | `O(λ)` | `O(λ)`, batchable to `O(λ)` total | `O(1)` add; witness refresh `O(n)` | **None** (transparent) | Constant-size batch |
| Merkle tree / MMR [4][5] | `O(λ)` | `O(λ log n)` | `O(λ log n)` proof update | None | Sorted-tree variants |
| KZG [2][7] | `O(λ)` | `O(λ)` (one group element) | `O(1)` proof update with update keys | SRS ceremony | Via vanishing polynomials |

## 5. Empirical Evaluation and Proofs

We now quantify the comparison. Costs are per operation for a set/vector of size `n = 2²⁰`, `λ = 128` bits of security, using published implementation figures [3] and standard operation counts.

**Proof sizes (bytes).** A Merkle audit path at depth 20 with 32-byte hashes is 640 bytes. An RSA/class-group witness is one group element: ~256 bytes (RSA-2048) or ~1 KB (class group at 128-bit security — class groups pay roughly 4× the element size). A KZG opening is one BLS12-381 `𝔾₁` element: 48 bytes, and a multiproof for `k` openings stays near `O(1)` [7]. For a stateless client verifying `k = 1,000` state accesses per block, Merkle costs ~640 KB, KZG costs ~48 bytes plus `k` field elements — a four-order-of-magnitude bandwidth reduction.

**Prover costs.** Accumulating `n` elements in RSA/class groups costs `n` exponentiations (`O(n)` multiplications with large exponents); Merkle tree construction is `2n` hashes; KZG commitment is an `n`-wide multi-scalar multiplication, `O(n)` with large constants but highly parallelizable and GPU-friendly.

**Verifier costs.** RSA/class-group verification is one exponentiation; Merkle is `log n` hashes (microseconds); KZG is two pairings (milliseconds) — the most expensive per proof, but batched verification [7] amortizes pairings across many openings via random linear combination, a standard trick:

```
Verify Σᵢ rᵢ·(e(Cᵢ/g^{yᵢ}, g) − e(πᵢ, g^{τ}/g^{zᵢ})) = 0
```

for random scalars `rᵢ`, collapsing `2k` pairings into `k + 1`.

> **Theorem (KZG evaluation binding):** Under the `t`-SDH assumption, no PPT adversary can produce a commitment `C`, a point `z`, and two valid opening proofs for distinct values `y ≠ y'`. *Proof sketch.* Two valid proofs give `g^{(φ(τ)−y)/(τ−z)}` and `g^{(φ(τ)−y')/(τ−z)}`; their quotient is `g^{(y'−y)/(τ−z)}`, and raising to `(y'−y)⁻¹` yields `g^{1/(τ−z)}` — a forged `t`-SDH pair [2]. ∎

**Witness-update communication** is the metric that decides real deployments. When the accumulated set changes by `Δ` elements, Merkle witnesses update with `O(Δ log n)` downloaded nodes; KZG proofs update in `O(1)` given per-index update keys; RSA/class-group witnesses require `O(n)` refresh communication in the worst case — the problem Boneh *et al.*'s bridge protocol mitigates but does not eliminate [3]. This is why production stateless designs (Ethereum's Verkle roadmap) choose KZG-family constructions despite the trusted setup.

## 6. Limitations and Open Problems

1. **Trusted setup vs. transparency.** RSA accumulators need a trusted modulus; KZG needs an SRS ceremony; only Merkle and class-group constructions are fully transparent [3][5]. Transparent polynomial commitments (FRI, Dory) exist but with `O(log n)`-sized proofs — the constant-size transparent vector commitment remains open.
2. **Post-quantum security.** RSA, class-group, and pairing-based constructions all fall to Shor's algorithm. Lattice-based vector commitments exist but with impractically large parameters; a post-quantum *constant-sized* opening proof is a major open problem.
3. **Dynamic witness maintenance.** Camacho and Hevia proved impossibility results for batch witness *updates* in certain accumulator models; practical systems still choose between expensive witness refresh (RSA/class-group) and logarithmic proofs (Merkle).
4. **Non-membership in Merkle logs.** Plain Merkle trees prove inclusion only; non-membership needs sorted trees or sparse Merkle trees (as in certificate-revocation designs), doubling implementation complexity.
5. **Aggregation soundness subtleties.** Batching proofs [3][7] rely on random-oracle Fiat–Shamir and delicate assumption management (adaptive-root, strong-RSA); several published aggregation schemes required later patches.

## 7. Conclusion

From Benaloh and de Mare's quasi-commutative exponentiation [1] to Boneh–Bünz–Fisch's trapdoor-free batching [3], from Merkle's hash trees [5] securing the web's certificate logs [4] to KZG's single-element polynomial openings [2] with multiproof aggregation [7], the field has converged on a clear taxonomy: choose Merkle when transparency and simplicity dominate, RSA/class-group accumulators when constant-size witnesses must avoid trusted setup, and KZG when bandwidth per opening is the binding constraint. The unifying insight is that all four are *commitments with local opening* — differing only in their algebraic substrate and the trust they demand. The next decade's work — transparent, post-quantum, constant-sized — will decide which substrate survives contact with quantum adversaries and planetary-scale stateless clients.
