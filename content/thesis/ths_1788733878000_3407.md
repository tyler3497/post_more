# QUIC Transport and BBR Congestion Control: Packet Number Spaces, 0-RTT Handshakes, and Model-Based Rate Control

## Abstract

The Internet's transport layer has been dominated for four decades by TCP's loss-based congestion control, a design whose founding assumption — that packet loss is synonymous with congestion — has been steadily eroded by the evolution of NICs, buffer memory, and wireless media [1][2]. This thesis studies two converging innovations that break with that lineage: the QUIC transport protocol, standardized in RFC 9000 with loss detection and congestion control in RFC 9002, and BBR (Bottleneck Bandwidth and Round-trip propagation time), Google's model-based congestion control algorithm that replaces loss reaction with an explicit model of the network path [1][2][3]. We analyze QUIC's three packet number spaces and their interaction with loss detection, the TLS 1.3-integrated handshake that enables zero-round-trip (0-RTT) resumption, and BBR's control-theoretic foundation in Kleinrock's optimal operating point. Formal results include a convergence theorem for BBR's steady-state window under fixed bottleneck capacity, and empirical evidence from Internet-wide and laboratory deployments showing order-of-magnitude latency reductions and 2798× throughput resilience under random loss [4][5][6]. We close with the limitations of model-based control — fairness, shallow-buffer pathologies, and BBRv2/v3 remediation — and identify open problems at the QUIC–BBR interface.

## 1 Introduction

In 1988, Van Jacobson introduced TCP congestion avoidance in response to Internet congestion collapse, embedding in TCP's soul a simple heuristic: when a packet is lost, the network is congested, and the sender must halve its sending rate [3]. This loss-based paradigm, refined through Reno, NewReno, and CUBIC, achieved remarkable stability for three decades. Yet by the 2010s it had become the principal cause of the Internet's most visible pathologies. On links with bloated buffers — the norm on residential broadband and cellular access — loss-based control keeps those buffers full, inflating round-trip times by factors of twenty or more (the *bufferbloat* phenomenon); on links with small buffers or random non-congestive loss, such as Wi-Fi and mobile paths, it mistakes loss for congestion and collapses throughput even when the bottleneck is nearly idle [3][6].

Two separate engineering programs responded to this impasse from different directions. The IETF's QUIC working group rebuilt the transport protocol itself, moving reliability and congestion control into encrypted user-space UDP packets, thereby making the transport evolvable where the ossified middlebox-ridden TCP stack was not [1][2]. Concurrently, researchers at Google reconceived congestion control as a measurement and estimation problem rather than a reaction to binary events, producing BBR, the first widely deployed model-based congestion controller [3][4]. This thesis develops both ideas in depth, and crucially analyzes their synthesis: how BBR's rate-based, model-driven pacing exploits QUIC's unique machinery — monotonically increasing packet numbers, per-packet-number-space loss detection, and rich, unencrypted-transport-head ACK telemetry — and where the marriage still strains.

Our contributions are structured as follows. Section 2 reviews loss-based control and QUIC's departures. Section 3 describes our methodology. Section 4 gives the deep dive: packet number spaces, the 0-RTT handshake, BBR's four-state machine, and their integration. Section 5 proves a steady-state convergence result and summarizes empirical performance; Section 6 catalogs limitations, and Section 7 concludes.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 From Loss-Based to Model-Based Control

Classical congestion control is *event-driven*: the sender infers the network's state from discrete events — ACKs received, packets lost, ECN marks observed — and adjusts a congestion window (*cwnd*) multiplicatively. CUBIC, the Linux default for two decades, updates its window as a cubic function of time since the last loss, converging slowly toward an unknown capacity and deliberately overshooting it to detect the next boundary [3].

BBR's intellectual ancestor is not Reno but Kleinrock's 1979 analysis of power: a network achieves optimal operation when *inflight* (bytes unacknowledged in the network) equals the bandwidth-delay product (BDP), the volume of the "pipe." Sending less leaves capacity unused; sending more accumulates queue and delay without additional throughput. BBR operationalizes this by continuously estimating two path parameters — bottleneck bandwidth *BtlBw* and round-trip propagation delay *RTprop* — and then setting the pacing rate to `BtlBw` and the congestion window to a small multiple of `BtlBw × RTprop`, parking the flow precisely at Kleinrock's optimal point rather than probing blindly past it [3].

### 2.2 QUIC's Architectural Departures

QUIC (RFC 9000) is a UDP-encapsulated, multiplexed, secure transport that fuses the cryptographic handshake (TLS 1.3, per RFC 9001) with connection establishment, and encrypts nearly all transport headers — including packet numbers — so that middleboxes cannot ossify behavior [1]. From the congestion-control standpoint, three departures matter most:

1. **Monotonically increasing packet numbers.** Unlike TCP's byte-sequence numbers, QUIC packet numbers never repeat within a connection; retransmitted frames ride in *new* packets with *new* numbers [2]. This eliminates the retransmission ambiguity that has plagued TCP RTT estimation since Karn's algorithm.
2. **Independent packet number spaces.** QUIC maintains three separate number spaces — Initial, Handshake, and Application Data — each with its own loss detection state and timers [2].
3. **Rich ACK frames.** QUIC ACKs carry per-packet reception ranges, an ACK delay field, and ECN counters, giving the sender high-fidelity telemetry for RTT estimation and for BBR's bandwidth sampling [1][2].

RFC 9002 specifies loss detection and a NewReno-style baseline congestion controller for QUIC; crucially, it permits any conformant algorithm — including BBR — to be used [2].

## 3 Methodology

This thesis proceeds in two complementary registers: formal modeling and empirical synthesis.

**Formal register.** We formalize BBR as a discrete-time control system with state `x(t) = (inflight(t), BtlBw(t), RTprop(t))`, where `BtlBw(t)` is a windowed maximum of delivery-rate samples (~10·RTprop) and `RTprop(t)` a windowed minimum of RTT samples (10 s window) [5][3]. The control law sets `pacing_rate = gain_p · BtlBw` and `cwnd = gain_c · BtlBw · RTprop`; we analyze convergence of `inflight(t)` to the fixed point `BDP = BtlBw·RTprop` (§5).

**Empirical register.** We synthesize results from three sources: (i) Google's production evaluation of BBR v1 on YouTube and its internal WAN, reporting 2–25× latency improvements and the 2798× resilience figure under 1% random loss on a 10 Gbps/100 ms path [4]; (ii) the TUM reproducible-measurements study, which independently validated BBR's steady-state behavior, ProbeRTT synchronization dynamics, and its pathological interaction with shallow buffers and competing CUBIC flows [6]; and (iii) the RFC corpus itself [1][2] as the normative specification of QUIC's number spaces, loss detection, and PTO machinery, corroborated by an independent arXiv specification study [8].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Packet Number Spaces and Loss Detection

The packet number space is QUIC's most consequential departure from TCP. Because Initial, Handshake, and Application Data packets are protected by different keys and carry independent sequence numbers, loss detection runs *per space*: each space has its own sent-packet tracker, its own loss detection timer, and its own probe timeout (PTO) state [2][8]. The space separation exists so that, for example, the loss of a Handshake packet cannot be confused with the loss of an Application Data packet — and so that amplification-limit and anti-amplification accounting can be enforced per encryption level.

Loss detection in each space is the classic dual-threshold scheme, sharpened by the no-reuse property of packet numbers. A packet is declared lost when either:

- **Packet threshold:** a packet sent *kPacketThreshold* = 3 packets before an acknowledged packet in the same space remains unacknowledged [2], or
- **Time threshold:** the packet has been unacknowledged for longer than `max(smoothed_rtt, latest_rtt) × 9/8` [2].

The time threshold is the workhorse: by using a threshold slightly above the current RTT estimate, it converts the retransmission-ambiguity problem away entirely — since retransmissions carry new packet numbers, an ACK of packet *n+7* can never be mistaken for an ACK of the lost packet *n* [8]. RTT estimation itself is textbook EWMA, but the ACK Delay field — the receiver's reported intentional delay before sending the ACK — is subtracted from samples when possible, so that deliberate receiver pacing does not pollute `min_rtt` and, by extension, BBR's `RTprop` [2].

PTO ("probe timeout") replaces TCP's retransmission timeout: when the PTO timer fires, the sender transmits up to two new probe packets (possibly across spaces) rather than retransmitting old ones; a PTO expiration does *not* by itself declare loss [2][8]. The timer is armed per space, and the PTO formula is:

```
PTO = smoothed_rtt + max(4 × rttvar, kGranularity) + max_ack_delay
```

Congestion control in RFC 9002's baseline mirrors TCP NewReno, but the RFC permits conformant replacements — including BBR — and the telemetry BBR needs is richer in QUIC than in TCP [1][2][3].

### 4.2 The 0-RTT Handshake

In TCP, establishing a secure connection costs three round trips in the worst case: TCP handshake, TLS 1.3 handshake, and (for a repeated client) still one round trip for resumption. QUIC collapses this stack. The handshake integrates TLS 1.3 directly: the client's Initial packet carries the TLS ClientHello, the server's Initial + Handshake packets carry ServerHello through Finished, and after one RTT the connection is established and 1-RTT keys are active [1].

The deeper optimization is **0-RTT resumption**. A client that has previously connected — and whose server issued a resumption ticket with `early_data` permission — may send Application Data *in its very first flight*, encrypted under 0-RTT keys derived from the prior session's resumption secret [1]. The cryptographic machinery is standard TLS 1.3; QUIC's contribution is making it a first-class, per-space affair: 0-RTT packets occupy the Application Data number space and are subject to the same loss detection, congestion control, and amplification limits (a server will not send more than 3× the bytes received before address validation) [1][2].

There is an unavoidable cost. 0-RTT data enjoys no forward secrecy and is vulnerable to *replay*: an attacker that captures the client's first flight can replay it. RFC 9001 therefore recommends restricting 0-RTT to idempotent operations with server-side replay defenses [1].

### 4.3 BBR: The Model and the State Machine

BBR's core loop is deceptively simple. On every ACK, the sender computes the *delivery rate* — bytes newly delivered divided by the interval — and updates its two model parameters [3][5]:

```python
# BBR per-ACK update (simplified from draft-cardwell-iccrg-bbr-congestion-control)
def bbr_on_ack(ack):
    sample = DeliverySample(
        delivered=ack.bytes_newly_delivered,
        interval=now() - ack.delivery_interval_start,
    )
    rate = sample.delivered / sample.interval          # delivery-rate sample
    BtlBw = max(BtlBw, rate)                           # windowed max, ~10 RTprop
    if ack.rtt < RTprop or age(RTprop) > 10*s:
        RTprop = ack.rtt                               # windowed min
    pacing_rate  = pacing_gain  * BtlBw
    cwnd         = cwnd_gain    * BtlBw * RTprop
```

The state machine alternates between probing for bandwidth and probing for propagation delay, using four states with distinct gains [5]:

| State | pacing_gain | cwnd_gain | Purpose |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Startup** | 2/ln(2) ≈ 2.89 | 2/ln(2) ≈ 2.89 | Exponential ramp; exit when delivery-rate growth stalls (pipe judged full) |
| **Drain** | ln(2)/2 ≈ 0.35 | 2/ln(2) ≈ 2.89 | Drain the queue created during Startup |
| **ProbeBW** | cycling {1.25, 0.75, 1, 1, 1, 1, 1, 1} | 2 | Steady state; periodically probe for more bandwidth, then drain the probe's queue |
| **ProbeRTT** | 1 | 4 packets | Every 10 s (if `min_rtt` expired): cut inflight to 4 packets for ~200 ms to re-measure RTprop |

The Startup exit condition is what distinguishes BBR from slow start: rather than waiting for loss, BBR exits when three consecutive rounds fail to raise the `BtlBw` estimate by more than 25% — the pipe is *full* because additional inflight no longer raises the delivery rate [5][3].

> **Theorem (BBR steady-state fixed point):** *Let a bottleneck of fixed capacity C and propagation delay d be shared by a single BBR flow in ProbeBW steady state, with exact estimates BtlBw = C and RTprop = d. Then inflight converges to the interval [BDP, 2·BDP] and the queueing delay converges to zero in the cruise phases. Proof sketch in §5.*

### 4.4 The QUIC–BBR Interface: Why They Fit

BBR was first deployed on TCP, where it had to fight the protocol: retransmission ambiguity polluted `RTprop` through Karn's algorithm, SACK's limited ranges coarsened delivery-rate samples, and header-only encryption constraints were irrelevant but pacing had to be bolted on via `fq` qdiscs [4]. QUIC removes each of these obstacles by design:

1. **Unambiguous RTT.** Packet numbers never repeat, so every RTT sample is valid — no Karn's algorithm, no timestamp options needed. `RTprop` converges faster and is robust under heavy loss [2][8].
2. **Precise delivery-rate estimation.** QUIC ACK frames report arbitrary ranges of received packets plus ACK delay, letting the sender compute delivery-rate samples at packet granularity rather than from coarse SACK blocks [1][2].
3. **Encryption-friendly pacing.** Because BBR controls the *sending rate* directly (pacing) rather than a window of unencrypted sequence numbers, it composes naturally with QUIC's encrypted headers and user-space implementation — pacing is just a timer, and user-space timers are cheap [1][3].
4. **Per-space congestion state isolation.** Handshake retransmissions in the Initial/Handshake spaces do not pollute the Application Data space's model, so a lossy handshake cannot corrupt `BtlBw` before bulk transfer begins [2].

The fit is not accidental: both designs emerged from the same Google networking group, and QUIC's telemetry richness reflects requirements that BBR's designers articulated during standardization [1][3].

## 5 Empirical Results and Formal Proofs

### 5.1 Convergence: Proof Sketch

We prove the fixed-point claim of §4.3 under idealized assumptions: a single BBR flow, bottleneck capacity `C` constant, propagation delay `d` constant, infinite (or sufficiently large) buffers, and exact estimates.

> **Theorem:** *Under the assumptions above, a BBR flow in ProbeBW with the gain cycle {1.25, 0.75, 1×6} has inflight bounded in [C·d, 2·C·d] and average queueing delay → 0 across cruise phases.*

*Proof sketch.* Let `B = C·d` be the BDP. In cruise phases (gain 1), the pacing rate equals `C`, so arrivals at the bottleneck match service exactly; inflight converges to `B` plus at most one round's worth of probe overshoot. In the probe-up phase (gain 1.25), the sender injects at 1.25×C for one RTT, adding `0.25·B` to the queue; because `cwnd_gain = 2` caps inflight at `2·B`, the queue cannot exceed `B`. In the probe-down phase (gain 0.75), injection falls below service and the accumulated queue drains within one RTT. Hence inflight ∈ `[B, 2B]` and the time-averaged queue occupancy — and therefore queueing delay — tends to zero as the fraction of probe phases (2 of 8 RTTs) is fixed and the cruise phases dominate. ∎

The proof's fragility is instructive: it assumes a *single* flow and exact estimates. With `n` competing BBR flows, each flow's `BtlBw` samples include the others' ProbeBW overshoots, and the system converges to a fair-ish but oscillatory equilibrium whose analysis requires game-theoretic tools — one reason BBR's fairness properties remain contested [6].

### 5.2 Measured Performance

Google's production evaluation is the canonical dataset [4]. On an emulated 10 Gbps, 100 ms RTT path with 1% random packet loss — a regime where CUBIC's loss halving collapses goodput to **3.27 Mbps** — BBR sustains **9150 Mbps**, a **2798×** improvement, because its rate decisions ignore the loss entirely and its model says the pipe is still 10 Gbps wide. On a 10 Mbps, 40 ms path with a 1000-packet bloated buffer, both algorithms fill the pipe, but BBR's median RTT is **43 ms versus CUBIC's 1.09 s** — a 25× latency reduction achieved by refusing to fill the buffer in the first place [4].

Independent laboratory reproduction broadly confirms these results while adding nuance. The TUM study verified BBR's steady-state throughput and low queueing on deep buffers, but documented that on *shallow* buffers BBR's ProbeBW overshoot (inflight up to 2·BDP against the `cwnd_gain = 2` cap) causes systematic loss, and that ProbeRTT phases of multiple flows rarely synchronize, so competing BBR flows often measure an inflated `RTprop` [6].

QUIC-specific measurements are younger but consistent: QUIC's richer ACK telemetry yields lower-variance delivery-rate samples than TCP SACK, and BBR-over-QUIC deployments report faster `RTprop` convergence after route changes [8][1].

## 6 Limitations

No honest treatment of BBR can stop at its victories. The limitations cluster into four areas.

**Fairness and coexistence.** BBR is neither TCP-friendly nor, strictly, fair to itself. Against CUBIC on a shared bottleneck, BBR's refusal to back off on loss lets it capture a disproportionate share — a feature for the BBR flow, a bug for the Internet's implicit social contract [6]. Among BBR flows, latecomers systematically overestimate `BtlBw` (the queue built by incumbents inflates their delivery-rate samples) and can starve early flows; the equilibrium is unfair in theory and measurably so in practice [6].

**Shallow buffers and loss intolerance of the model.** BBR's `cwnd_gain = 2` permits inflight up to twice the BDP, which on buffers smaller than ~1.5× BDP produces chronic loss. Because BBR ignores loss as a signal, it does not learn to reduce inflight — it simply retransmits forever, wasting capacity [6]. BBRv2 addresses this by adding explicit loss and ECN responses (a loss/ECN-triggered inflight cap), at the cost of reintroducing some loss-reactivity [5].

**RTprop staleness and ProbeRTT cost.** The 10-second `RTprop` window means a route change that *lengthens* the path is detected only after up to 10 s of inflated estimates; the ProbeRTT phase itself cuts throughput to 4 packets for 200 ms every 10 s — a 2% tax keenly felt by latency-sensitive flows [6].

**0-RTT and model bootstrapping.** A QUIC 0-RTT resumption that reuses stale `BtlBw`/`RTprop` from a prior session can massively overshoot on a changed path (e.g., Wi-Fi → cellular handoff), and RFC 9001's replay concerns constrain what data may ride in the first flight [1]. Conservative re-startup wastes the latency win that motivated 0-RTT.

**Standardization status.** As of this writing, BBR remains an Internet-Draft (draft-cardwell-iccrg-bbr-congestion-control), not an RFC — its specification is the draft plus the Linux kernel implementation, which complicates independent interoperable implementation relative to RFC 9002's precisely specified NewReno baseline [5][2].

## 7 Conclusion

QUIC and BBR represent the two most significant revisions to Internet transport in a generation, and they revise it in complementary directions: QUIC rebuilds the *protocol* so that congestion control can see clearly — unambiguous packet numbers, per-space loss detection, rich ACK telemetry, and 0-RTT establishment — while BBR rebuilds the *controller* so that it reasons about the path as a physical system to be modeled rather than an adversary to be reacted to [1][2][3]. The empirical record is unambiguous that the model-based approach resolves the central pathologies of loss-based control: bufferbloat and random-loss collapse [4][6].

Yet the synthesis is incomplete. BBR's fairness deficits, its shallow-buffer pathology, and the staleness dynamics of its own estimates are consequences of the model's assumptions — single flow, exact estimation, stationary path — and each has motivated BBRv2/v3, which graft loss and ECN signals back onto the model-based core [5].
