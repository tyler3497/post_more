# Simultaneous Localization and Mapping: Factor Graphs, Bundle Adjustment, and Loop Closure

## Abstract

Simultaneous Localization and Mapping (SLAM) asks a deceptively simple question: can a mobile robot determine where it is while building a map of an environment it has never seen? This thesis develops the modern answer. We begin with the probabilistic formulation of full SLAM and trace the field's migration from *filtering* — the Extended Kalman Filter heritage, with quadratic covariance updates and well-documented consistency failures — to *smoothing* over factor graphs, where the joint posterior factorizes into local measurement constraints and the MAP estimate reduces to sparse nonlinear least squares. Bundle adjustment is presented as the canonical visual instantiation of this machinery, with the Schur complement and Levenberg–Marquardt damping exploiting the bipartite camera–landmark sparsity behind city-scale reconstruction. We then examine incremental inference through the Bayes tree, the directed graphical model behind iSAM2 that confines relinearization to genuinely affected subtrees. Finally, we analyze loop closure as the coupling of appearance-based place recognition, geometric verification, and global graph correction — the mechanism by which accumulated drift is recognized and undone — and close with the limitations still separating laboratory demonstrations from planetary-scale autonomy.

## 1 Introduction

A robot dropped into an unknown corridor faces a chicken-and-egg problem of unusual purity: to localize itself it needs a map, and to build a map it needs to know where it is. Early formalizations of this coupling, due to Smith, Self, and Cheesman in the late 1980s, framed SLAM as a single estimation problem over a joint state vector containing both the robot pose and the positions of observed landmarks. The Extended Kalman Filter became the workhorse of this era. It maintained a dense Gaussian belief, linearized motion and observation models at the current estimate, and propagated a full covariance matrix whose off-diagonal blocks encoded the correlations between robot and landmarks that make SLAM fundamentally different from odometry plus mapping. For a decade, EKF-SLAM *was* SLAM.

Yet the filter formulation carries structural burdens: quadratic covariance updates, permanent commitment to a single linearization point with the notorious inconsistency analyzed in the invariant-filter literature [6], and inherent forgetfulness — marginalizing past poses discards the constraints future loop closures would need.

The modern resolution is to stop filtering and start smoothing. Rather than compressing history into a single belief, graph-based SLAM retains the entire trajectory and the complete set of measurements, encoding them as a **factor graph** — a bipartite graphical model whose factors express the likelihood of each measurement given the variables it touches [3]. The maximum a posteriori trajectory is then the minimizer of a sum of squared residuals: a nonlinear least-squares problem whose sparsity mirrors the graph topology and which admits efficient solution through sparse linear algebra [1]. This thesis is organized around three pillars of that unification. First, the factor graph formulation itself and the sparse optimization machinery that solves it. Second, bundle adjustment as the flagship visual back-end, from the Schur complement to robust cost functions. Third, loop closure, the process that converts the recognition "I have been here before" into a global geometric correction, and the Bayes-tree machinery of iSAM2 that makes the whole pipeline run incrementally in real time.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The probabilistic statement of SLAM

Let the robot's pose at discrete time $t$ be $x_t$ in the special Euclidean group $SE(3)$, let the landmark positions be $l_1, \dots, l_m$, let controls be $u_t$, and let observations be $z_t$. The **full SLAM** problem estimates the posterior over the *entire* trajectory and map:

$$p(x_{0:T}, l_{1:m} \mid z_{0:T}, u_{0:T})$$

whereas **online SLAM** marginalizes everything but the current pose. Under the standard Markov assumptions — the motion model $p(x_t \mid x_{t-1}, u_t)$ and the observation model $p(z_t \mid x_t, l)$ — the posterior factorizes into a product of local terms. That factorization is the entire game: every term involves only a handful of variables, so the joint density is a *sparse* graphical model regardless of how long the trajectory grows [3].

### 2.2 EKF-SLAM and its discontents

The EKF maintains a mean $\mu_t$ and covariance $\Sigma_t$ over the stacked vector of pose plus landmarks. Each motion update linearizes the dynamics; each measurement update computes a Kalman gain requiring inversion of a matrix sized by the measurement dimension, and — critically — updates the *dense* covariance at $O(n^2)$ cost per step, where $n$ counts landmarks [7]. Three pathologies follow:

- **Quadratic scaling.** Dense cross-correlations between every landmark pair fill the covariance matrix. A thousand landmarks means a million covariance entries updated at every step.
- **Premature linearization.** Jacobians are evaluated once, at the current estimate, and never revisited. In the nonlinear regime of real sensors this produces biased estimates and, worse, *inconsistent* covariances that shrink unjustifiably — the filter becomes confidently wrong [6].
- **Information loss.** Marginalizing past poses destroys the loop-closure constraints that have not yet been observed. When the robot revisits a location, the filter cannot exploit the full geometry of the loop because most of it has been summarized away.

### 2.3 Graphs, factors, and the MAP estimate

A factor graph is a bipartite graph $G = (F, \Theta, E)$ with factor nodes $F$ and variable nodes $\Theta$. It encodes the factorization

$$p(\Theta \mid Z) \propto \prod_i f_i(\Theta_i)$$

where each factor $f_i$ depends only on a small subset $\Theta_i$ of variables. For Gaussian measurement noise, each factor is proportional to $\exp(-\tfrac{1}{2}\lVert h_i(\Theta_i) - z_i \rVert^2_{\Sigma_i})$, and the MAP estimate becomes the nonlinear least-squares problem

$$\Theta^* = \arg\min_\Theta \sum_i \lVert h_i(\Theta_i) - z_i \rVert^2_{\Sigma_i}.$$

Linearizing each residual about the current estimate yields the normal equations $A^\top A\,\delta = A^\top b$, where the information matrix $\Lambda = A^\top A$ is sparse *because the graph is sparse*: variables that never co-occur in a factor have zero coupling in $\Lambda$ [1].

> **Theorem (Sparsity of the SLAM information matrix).** *In the linearized full-SLAM problem, the information matrix entry $\Lambda_{jk}$ is nonzero only if variables $\theta_j$ and $\theta_k$ are connected in the factor graph by a common measurement factor. In particular, the landmark–landmark block is block-diagonal and the pose–pose block is block-tridiagonal plus loop-closure fill-in.*

*Proof sketch.* $\Lambda = \sum_i J_i^\top \Sigma_i^{-1} J_i$, where $J_i$ is the Jacobian of factor $i$, nonzero only on the columns of $\Theta_i$. The outer product $J_i^\top \Sigma_i^{-1} J_i$ therefore populates only the $\Theta_i \times \Theta_i$ sub-block. Summation over factors unions these supports; two variables couple only through shared factors. For odometry chains the pose block is tridiagonal, and each loop closure adds a single off-tridiagonal coupling. ∎

## 3 Methodology

Our methodology is the standard modern SLAM pipeline: (i) a **front-end** that converts raw sensor streams into factors — visual odometry or feature tracking producing reprojection constraints, inertial preintegration producing motion factors; (ii) a **back-end** that solves the factor graph incrementally; (iii) a **loop-closure module** that detects revisits and injects the long-range constraints that annihilate drift.

### 3.1 Building the factor graph

Construction is mechanical but consequential. Each new keyframe introduces a pose variable $x_t$ connected to $x_{t-1}$ by an odometry or IMU-preintegration factor, and each landmark observation introduces a reprojection factor linking $x_t$ to the landmark $l_j$. A prior factor anchors the first pose, fixing the otherwise unobservable global frame — the **gauge freedom** of the problem, a datum choice with a long photogrammetric pedigree [5]. The following Python sketch, in the style of the GTSAM tutorial [3], captures the pattern:

```python
import gtsam
from gtsam import Pose3, Point3, noiseModel

graph = gtsam.NonlinearFactorGraph()
initial = gtsam.Values()

# Gauge: anchor the first pose with a strong prior
prior_noise = noiseModel.Diagonal.Sigmas([1e-6]*6)
graph.add(gtsam.PriorFactorPose3(X(0), Pose3(), prior_noise))
initial.insert(X(0), Pose3())

for t in range(1, T):
    odom = odometry_between(t-1, t)          # from front-end
    graph.add(gtsam.BetweenFactorPose3(
        X(t-1), X(t), odom, odom_noise))     # motion factor
    initial.insert(X(t), initial_guess(t))
    for j, z in observations(t):             # landmark sightings
        graph.add(gtsam.GenericProjectionFactorCal3_S2(
            z, cal_noise, X(t), L(j), K))    # reprojection factor
```

### 3.2 Solving: linearization, damping, and ordering

The back-end minimizes the nonlinear least-squares objective by successive linearization. At each iteration, residuals and Jacobians are evaluated at the current estimate, forming $A\delta \approx b$, and the update $\delta$ solves a sparse linear system. Two refinements are essential:

1. **Levenberg–Marquardt damping.** Adding $\lambda I$ to the normal equations interpolates toward gradient descent when the quadratic model is untrustworthy, recovering Gauss–Newton speed near convergence [5].
2. **Variable ordering.** Cholesky factorization $A^\top A = R^\top R$ incurs fill-in that depends dramatically on the elimination order; heuristics such as COLAMD keep $R$ sparse, and $R$ is exactly what the Bayes tree maintains incrementally [1].

### 3.3 Robustification

Real measurements contain outliers — mismatched features, moving objects, multipath. Replacing the squared residual with a robust kernel $\rho(\lVert r \rVert)$, such as the Huber or Cauchy loss, down-weights large residuals via iteratively reweighted least squares with negligible changes to the linear solver [5].

### 3.4 Loop closure as graph surgery

Loop closure proceeds in three stages. **Detection** proposes candidate revisits from appearance alone, typically via bag-of-words retrieval over binary descriptors [4]. **Geometric verification** estimates a relative transform — in monocular systems a full similarity transform $Sim(3)$, since scale drifts — under RANSAC, and accepts the loop only with sufficient inlier support. **Correction** inserts the verified constraint as a factor between temporally distant poses and re-optimizes, distributing the accumulated error around the loop. The Bayes tree localizes this re-optimization to the affected cliques, which is what makes online operation possible at all [1].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 From Elimination to the Bayes Tree

Variable elimination is the bridge between linear algebra and probability.

Formally, if $C$ is the set of cliques containing variables of the new factors, the update re-eliminates exactly the union of root-ward paths from $C$, reattaches the orphaned subtrees, and leaves all other cliques bit-identical [1].

```haskell
-- A Bayes tree node: frontal variables, separator, and conditional density
data Clique v = Clique
  { frontal   :: [v]               -- variables eliminated at this node
  , separator :: [v]               -- variables shared with the parent
  , conditional :: Density v       -- p(frontal | separator)
  , children  :: [Clique v]
  }

-- Incremental update: re-eliminate only cliques on paths to the root
update :: Ord v => BayesTree v -> [Factor v] -> BayesTree v
update tree newFactors =
  let affected = cliquesContaining tree (variablesOf newFactors)
      (subgraph, orphans) = detachUpward tree affected
      reordered = reorderWith COLAMD subgraph newFactors
      rebuilt = eliminate reordered
  in reattach rebuilt orphans
```

### 4.2 Bundle Adjustment and the Schur Complement

Bundle adjustment is the special case of graph SLAM in which the variables are camera poses and 3D points and the factors are reprojection errors [5].

$$\begin{bmatrix} U & W \\ W^\top & V \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} \delta_c \\ \delta_p \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} e_c \\ e_p \end{bmatrix}$$

where the point block $V$ is block-diagonal — each 3D point couples only to the cameras that observe it — and the camera block $U$ is block-diagonal up to shared observations.

$$(U - W V^{-1} W^\top)\,\delta_c = e_c - W V^{-1} e_p.$$

Because $V^{-1}$ is block-diagonal, forming the reduced camera system costs linear time in the number of points, and the reduced system involves only cameras — typically orders of magnitude fewer than points [5].

Batch solvers relinearize everything at every iteration; iSAM2 relinearizes *fluidly*, only the variables whose estimates have moved enough to invalidate their Jacobians [1].

> **Theorem (Exactness of incremental inference).** *The Bayes tree maintained by iSAM2 encodes exactly the same square-root information matrix — and hence the same MAP estimate — as batch variable elimination applied to the full factor graph with the same ordering. Incremental updates are algebraically identical to re-factorizing from scratch; they differ only in which entries are recomputed.*

A Rust-flavored sketch of the damped update at the heart of each relinearization illustrates the numerical core shared by iSAM2, g2o, and Ceres-style solvers:

```rust
/// One Levenberg-Marquardt iteration on the linearized system A dx = b.
fn lm_step(a: &SparseMatrix, b: &Vector, lambda: f64) -> Vector {
    // Normal equations with damping: (A^T A + λI) dx = A^T b
    let ata = a.transpose() * a;
    let atb = a.transpose() * b;
    let damped = ata + SparseMatrix::identity(ata.ncols()) * lambda;
    // Sparse Cholesky under the current (incremental) ordering
    let r = sparse_cholesky(damped, Ordering::Colamd);
    let dx = r.solve(&atb);
    dx
}
```

### 4.4 Loop Closure: Place Recognition, Verification, Correction

Loop closure is where geometry meets recognition, and it is the least forgiving part of the pipeline: a single false positive can bend the entire map.

1. **BoW retrieval.** Each keyframe's binary descriptors are quantized through a hierarchical vocabulary tree (DBoW2) into a sparse bag-of-words vector. Querying the inverted index returns candidate keyframes with high appearance similarity in time roughly logarithmic in the database size. Covisibility filtering — discarding candidates connected to the current keyframe in the covisibility graph — and the requirement of several *consecutive consistent* candidates suppress the most common false alarms [4].
2. **Geometric verification.** For each surviving candidate, 3D–3D correspondences between map points seed RANSAC estimation of a similarity transform $Sim(3)$ via Horn's method, correcting the seven drifting degrees of freedom of monocular SLAM (rotation, translation, and scale). The transform is then refined by nonlinear optimization over inliers and subjected to a guided search for additional correspondences [4].
3. **Graph correction.** The accepted constraint fuses the two sides of the loop: the $Sim(3)$ correction propagates through the pose graph, map points are transformed accordingly, and a final global bundle adjustment polishes the solution [4].

The Achilles' heel is **perceptual aliasing** — distinct places that look identical, such as repetitive corridors or parking structures.

## 5 Empirical Evidence and Proofs

### 5.1 Complexity: filtering versus smoothing

EKF-SLAM's covariance update costs $O(n^2)$ per step in the number of landmarks, confining it to sparse, small environments [7]. Synthetic comparisons show the filter's error growing with map size while graph-based batch re-optimization holds trajectory error near the measurement noise floor via a larger but sparse linear system solved in near-linear time [7]. Monte Carlo analysis further shows the standard EKF's inconsistency is structural — a consequence of linearizing about a state-dependent estimate — motivating invariant formulations whose Jacobians respect the geometry of $SE(3)$ [6].

### 5.2 Incremental smoothing at scale

The iSAM2 papers evaluate on a ladder of datasets from the synthetic Manhattan world to the Killian Court, Intel Research Lab, and Victoria Park sequences [1][2]. Per-update time stays essentially flat during exploration — the Bayes tree update touches only a handful of cliques — while batch factorization grows superlinearly with trajectory length. On loop closures the cost spikes with the affected subtree yet stays an order of magnitude below batch relinearization, and the MAP estimates are numerically indistinguishable from batch, confirming the exactness theorem [1].

### 5.3 Visual SLAM in the wild

ORB-SLAM instantiated the full pipeline — ORB features, covisibility-graph keyframe management, local bundle adjustment, BoW loop closing, and essential-graph optimization — and evaluated it on the NewCollege, TUM RGB-D, and KITTI sequences [4]. On KITTI, a driving benchmark with kilometers of trajectory, the system achieved translation errors competitive with the best stereo and visual-odometry methods of its era while producing a globally consistent sparse map; crucially, sequences *with* loop closures showed the characteristic error collapse when the loop constraint was fused, the empirical signature of correct loop handling [4]. The system's real-time operation on a standard CPU — tracking at frame rate while mapping and loop-closing threads ran concurrently — demonstrated that factor-graph SLAM had crossed from offline reconstruction to online autonomy [4].

### 5.4 Multi-sensor factor graphs

The factor-graph abstraction is sensor-agnostic. A representative GPS/IMU/LiDAR architecture runs an iterated error-state Kalman filter for high-rate odometry up front while the back-end maintains a factor graph — optimized incrementally with iSAM2 — fusing LIO, IMU-preintegration, GPS, and loop-closure factors; KITTI evaluations under both uninterrupted and interrupted GPS show the graph back-end bounding drift the filter alone cannot [8].

| System | Paradigm | Per-step cost | Handles loops | Consistency |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| EKF-SLAM | Filtering, dense Gaussian | $O(n^2)$ | Poor — history marginalized | Inconsistent under nonlinearity [6] |
| FastSLAM | Rao-Blackwellized particles | $O(M \log n)$ | Moderate — particle diversity | Degenerates in long loops [7] |
| Batch graph SLAM | Smoothing, sparse NLS | Superlinear in $T$ (amortized) | Excellent — full re-optimization | Consistent at convergence |
| iSAM2 | Incremental smoothing, Bayes tree | Near-constant; $O($affected$)$ | Excellent — localized updates [1] | Exact w.r.t. batch [1] |
| ORB-SLAM | Keyframe BA + BoW loops | Real-time on CPU | Excellent with verification [4] | Robust kernels; verified closures |

### 5.5 A convergence guarantee

> **Theorem (Local quadratic convergence of the SLAM back-end).** *Suppose the factor-graph objective $f(\Theta) = \sum_i \lVert h_i(\Theta_i) - z_i \rVert^2_{\Sigma_i}$ is twice continuously differentiable with Lipschitz Hessian near a strict local minimum $\Theta^*$, and that the Jacobians have full column rank at $\Theta^*$ (the graph is well-constrained modulo gauge). Then Gauss–Newton iterates converge quadratically in a neighborhood of $\Theta^*$, and Levenberg–Marquardt converges globally to a stationary point from arbitrary initialization within the basin structure of $f$.*

The fine print is the research program: "near a strict local minimum" is doing heavy lifting, since SLAM objectives are nonconvex and riddled with the local minima of wrong data association. The theorem explains why back-ends are so effective *given* good initialization and correct loop closures — the front-end's job — and so helpless without them.

A TLA+ sketch of the loop-closure acceptance logic captures the safety properties a correct implementation must maintain:

```tla
---- MODULE LoopClosure ----
EXTENDS Naturals
VARIABLES keyframes, candidates, accepted, graph

Consistent(c) == \E k \in keyframes : Covisible(k, c)

AcceptLoop ==
  /\ \E c \in candidates :
       /\ Consistent(c)
       /\ Cardinality(Inliers(c)) >= MinInliers
       /\ accepted' = accepted \cup {c}
       /\ graph' = FuseLoop(graph, c)
  /\ UNCHANGED keyframes

Safety == \A c \in accepted : GeometricVerified(c)
====
```

## 6 Limitations

**Nonconvexity and initialization.** Gauss–Newton and Levenberg–Marquardt guarantee only local convergence; a bad initial guess can trap the back-end in a geometrically absurd local minimum. Global methods (convex relaxations, graduated non-convexity) remain far from real-time [5].

**Data association fragility.** Every factor asserts a correspondence, and a wrong one injects a confidently wrong constraint that robust kernels can blunt but not cure. Loop closure is the extreme case — one false positive can fold the map [4].

**Dynamic and degenerate environments.** The static-world assumption fails around moving objects, and geometry degenerates in textureless or feature-scarce scenes; degenerate motions leave directions unobservable regardless of solver quality.

**Scale and lifelong operation.** The Bayes tree bounds per-update cost, but the graph grows without bound; long-term autonomy demands sparsification, submapping, or forgetting — each reintroducing the information-loss dilemma smoothing was meant to escape.

**Observability and gauge.** Seven degrees of freedom (six pose plus scale in the monocular case) are unobservable and must be fixed by prior or datum choice; mishandled gauge freedom manifests as slow drift along the null directions that no loop closure fully removes [5].

## 7 Conclusion

Three ideas carry modern SLAM. The **factor graph** turns estimation into sparse nonlinear least squares, unifying sensors and algorithms under one abstraction [1][3]. **Bundle adjustment**, armed with the Schur complement and robust damping, exploits visual geometry so thoroughly that city-scale reconstruction is routine [5]. **Loop closure** — retrieval, verification, correction — supplies the long-range constraints converting odometry's random walk into a globally consistent map [4], while the Bayes tree proves incremental inference need not be approximate [1][2].

What remains — nonconvexity, data association, dynamics, lifelong scale — are not deficiencies of the factor-graph formulation but frontiers beyond it.
