# Neural Ordinary Differential Equations: Continuous-Depth Learning, Adjoint Sensitivity, and Tractable Dynamics

## Abstract

We present a comprehensive treatment of neural ordinary differential equations (Neural ODEs), the continuous-depth reformulation of residual networks in which the discrete sequence of hidden layers is replaced by the integral of a learned vector field $dh(t)/dt = f_\theta(h(t), t)$ computed by a black-box differential equation solver. We develop the adjoint sensitivity method as the gradient calculus of this framework, showing how backpropagation reduces to solving an augmented ODE backward in time with constant memory cost independent of solver depth. We analyze the instantaneous change-of-variables theorem that turns normalizing flows into continuous normalizing flows with an $\mathcal{O}(D)$ trace term instead of an $\mathcal{O}(D^3)$ Jacobian determinant, and the Hutchinson stochastic trace estimator that makes this practical at scale. We address the representational topology constraints of continuous flows, the numerical instabilities of optimize-then-discretize gradient estimation, and the latent-time-series architectures that followed. The result is a self-contained account of how continuous dynamics, optimal control, and deep learning converge into a single tractable formalism.

## 1. Introduction

The residual network redefined the geometry of deep learning when it replaced direct layer mappings with additive corrections: $h_{t+1} = h_t + f_\theta(h_t)$, so that each layer learns a *displacement* rather than a full transformation. This architectural choice unlocked training at depths of hundreds of layers. Yet beneath this engineering success lies a mathematical observation of profound consequence: the residual update is the explicit Euler discretization, with unit step size, of the ordinary differential equation $dh(t)/dt = f_\theta(h(t), t)$. Taking the step size to zero while letting the number of steps tend to infinity converts the *layer index* from a discrete integer into a *continuous time variable*, and the network's forward pass becomes an initial value problem whose solution is computed by a numerical ODE solver rather than by a hand-laid sequence of modules [1].

This single move reconfigures the fundamental abstractions of deep learning:

- **Depth becomes duration.** Model capacity is governed not by a fixed parameter tying depth to layer count, but by the *complexity of the learned vector field* and the adaptive tolerance of the solver. An adaptive solver like Dormand–Prince expends more function evaluations on difficult inputs and fewer on easy ones, yielding *input-dependent adaptive computation* for free.
- **Memory becomes constant.** The adjoint sensitivity method, imported from optimal control theory [2], computes gradients by solving a second ODE backward in time rather than differentiating through the solver's internal steps, giving $\mathcal{O}(1)$ memory cost regardless of the effective depth.
- **Dynamics become objects of study.** Once the hidden state evolves continuously, the entire apparatus of dynamical systems — stability, reversibility, topology of flows, conservation laws — applies to learned representations, and deep learning inherits both the power and the constraints of continuous mathematics.

This article develops the theory in full. We begin with the residual-to-ODE correspondence and the solver black-box abstraction, derive the adjoint method rigorously as an application of Pontryagin's maximum principle, prove the instantaneous change-of-variables formula enabling continuous normalizing flows, and confront the twin limitations that followed: the topological restrictions of diffeomorphic flows [5] and the numerical instability of reversed-time adjoint solves [4]. We close with latent ODE architectures for irregularly sampled time series [6] and the frontier of tractable dynamics.

> **Theorem (Informal):** *Any deep network architecture whose layers are indexed by a continuous parameter can be trained by adjoint sensitivity with memory independent of the number of integration steps, at the cost of solving one additional ODE backward in time.*

## 2. Background

### 2.1 From Residual Layers to Vector Fields

Consider a residual block acting on a hidden state $h_t \in \mathbb{R}^D$:

$$\mathbf{h}_{t+1} = \mathbf{h}_t + f(\mathbf{h}_t, \boldsymbol{\theta}_t)$$

with per-layer parameters $\theta_t$. If we tie the parameters across layers ($\theta_t = \theta$) and introduce a step size $\Delta t$, this becomes

$$\frac{\mathbf{h}_{t+\Delta t} - \mathbf{h}_t}{\Delta t} = f(\mathbf{h}_t, t, \boldsymbol{\theta}),$$

and in the limit $\Delta t \to 0$ we obtain the **neural ordinary differential equation**

$$\frac{d\mathbf{h}(t)}{dt} = f_\theta(\mathbf{h}(t), t), \qquad \mathbf{h}(t_0) = \mathbf{h}_0.$$

The forward pass is the solution operator of this initial value problem:

$$\mathbf{h}(t_1) = \mathbf{h}_0 + \int_{t_0}^{t_1} f_\theta(\mathbf{h}(t), t)\,dt \;\approx\; \text{ODESolve}(\mathbf{h}_0, f_\theta, t_0, t_1).$$

The solver is treated as a black box: it may be a fixed-step Euler or Runge–Kutta scheme, or an adaptive method such as Dormand–Prince (`dopri5`) that controls local error against user-specified relative and absolute tolerances (`rtol`, `atol`). The canonical implementation of these ideas is the `torchdiffeq` library, which provides differentiable ODE solvers with autograd integration [7].

### 2.2 The Gradient Problem

Training requires $\partial L / \partial \theta$ for a scalar loss $L(\mathbf{h}(t_1))$. The naive approach — differentiating through every internal operation of the solver (discretize-then-optimize) — has memory cost proportional to the number of solver steps, exactly the cost that made very deep networks painful. The number of function evaluations (NFE) taken by an adaptive solver varies with input difficulty and tolerance, making the memory footprint unpredictable. The adjoint method resolves this by deriving a *second ODE for the gradient itself*, whose solution requires no storage of the forward trajectory.

### 2.3 Optimal Control Prehistory

The mathematics predates deep learning by half a century. Pontryagin's maximum principle characterizes optimal controls $u(t)$ for dynamical systems $\dot{x} = f(x, u, t)$ via a costate (adjoint) variable $\lambda(t)$ satisfying its own differential equation [2]. Chen et al. recognized that network parameters $\theta$ play the role of controls and hidden states the role of trajectories, so the *training* of a Neural ODE is a special case of an *optimal control problem* — and its gradient calculus is Pontryagin's adjoint equation in disguise [1].

## 3. Methodology

### 3.1 The Adjoint Sensitivity Equations

Let the loss depend only on the terminal state, $L = \Phi(\mathbf{h}(t_1))$. Define the **adjoint** (costate) as

$$\mathbf{a}(t) := \frac{\partial L}{\partial \mathbf{h}(t)},$$

a row vector whose terminal value is $\mathbf{a}(t_1) = \partial \Phi / \partial \mathbf{h}(t_1)$. We want the dynamics of $\mathbf{a}(t)$ as $t$ runs backward.

> **Theorem (Adjoint Sensitivity):** *The adjoint satisfies the linear ODE*
>
> $$\frac{d\mathbf{a}(t)}{dt} = -\mathbf{a}(t)\,\frac{\partial f_\theta(\mathbf{h}(t), t)}{\partial \mathbf{h}(t)},$$
>
> *and the gradient of the loss with respect to the parameters is*
>
> $$\frac{dL}{d\boldsymbol{\theta}} = -\int_{t_1}^{t_0} \mathbf{a}(t)\,\frac{\partial f_\theta(\mathbf{h}(t), t)}{\partial \boldsymbol{\theta}}\,dt.$$

> **Proof:** Consider a perturbation $\delta \mathbf{h}(t)$ induced by a parameter perturbation. Linearizing the dynamics gives $\delta\dot{\mathbf{h}} = f_h\,\delta\mathbf{h} + f_\theta\,\delta\theta$. Form the Lagrangian-style inner product with the adjoint and integrate by parts:
>
> $$\int_{t_0}^{t_1} \mathbf{a}(t)\,\delta\dot{\mathbf{h}}\,dt = \big[\mathbf{a}(t)\,\delta\mathbf{h}(t)\big]_{t_0}^{t_1} - \int_{t_0}^{t_1} \dot{\mathbf{a}}(t)\,\delta\mathbf{h}\,dt.$$
>
> Equating $\delta L = \mathbf{a}(t_1)\,\delta\mathbf{h}(t_1)$ and choosing $\dot{\mathbf{a}} = -\mathbf{a} f_h$ eliminates the $\delta\mathbf{h}$ integral, leaving only the parameter term. The crucial subtlety: the backward pass requires $\mathbf{h}(t)$ at all $t$, which is recovered by *re-solving the forward ODE backward from $\mathbf{h}(t_1)$*, requiring no stored trajectory. ∎

In practice, the three equations — forward state, backward adjoint, and parameter gradient accumulation — are concatenated into a single **augmented ODE** solved once in reverse time:

$$\frac{d}{dt}\begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{h}(t) \\ \mathbf{a}(t) \\ dL/d\boldsymbol{\theta} \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} f_\theta(\mathbf{h}, t) \\ -\mathbf{a}\, f_h \\ -\mathbf{a}\, f_\theta \end{bmatrix},$$

with initial condition $[\mathbf{h}(t_1), \partial\Phi/\partial\mathbf{h}(t_1), \mathbf{0}]$ at $t_1$, integrated to $t_0$. The vector–Jacobian products $\mathbf{a}\,f_h$ and $\mathbf{a}\,f_\theta$ are computed by automatic differentiation without ever materializing full Jacobians.

### 3.2 Complexity Accounting

| Approach | Memory | Forward cost | Backward cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| Discretize-then-optimize (backprop through solver) | $\mathcal{O}(\text{NFE})$ | 1 solve | $\mathcal{O}(\text{NFE})$ vector–Jacobian products |
| Adjoint (optimize-then-discretize) | $\mathcal{O}(1)$ in NFE | 1 solve | 1 augmented solve |
| ANODE checkpointing variant [4] | $\mathcal{O}(L) + \mathcal{O}(N_t)$ | 1 solve | $\approx$ 1 solve + checkpoints |

The adjoint method's memory is constant in the number of function evaluations — the property that made Neural ODEs tractable. The price is exactness: gradients computed through the *continuous* dynamics differ slightly from gradients of the *discretized* computation, a discrepancy that becomes the central stability question of §6 [4].

### 3.3 Continuous Normalizing Flows

A normalizing flow maps samples $\mathbf{z}_0$ from a simple base distribution through an invertible transformation $\mathbf{x} = T(\mathbf{z}_0)$; the change-of-variables formula gives the log-density:

$$\log p(\mathbf{x}) = \log p(\mathbf{z}_0) - \log \left| \det \frac{\partial T}{\partial \mathbf{z}_0} \right|.$$

The $\mathcal{O}(D^3)$ Jacobian determinant forces architectural contortions (dimension partitioning in Real NVP, triangular Jacobians in masked autoregressive flows). If instead the transformation is the *flow of an ODE* over unit time, the determinant collapses into a trace:

> **Theorem (Instantaneous Change of Variables):** *For $\mathbf{z}(t)$ satisfying $d\mathbf{z}/dt = f_\theta(\mathbf{z}, t)$,*
>
> $$\frac{\partial \log p(\mathbf{z}(t))}{\partial t} = -\operatorname{tr}\!\left(\frac{\partial f_\theta}{\partial \mathbf{z}(t)}\right).$$

> **Proof:** Apply the continuous-time Liouville equation to the density transported along the flow: $d\log p/dt = -\nabla \cdot f$, and the divergence of a vector field is exactly the trace of its Jacobian. Integrating from $t_0$ to $t_1$ yields the total log-density change as an integral of a scalar — computable with the same augmented-solver machinery, at cost comparable to a single forward solve. ∎

This is the **continuous normalizing flow (CNF)** of Chen et al. [1], and its scalable successor **FFJORD**, which replaces the exact $\mathcal{O}(D^2)$ trace with Hutchinson's unbiased stochastic estimator $\operatorname{tr}(A) = \mathbb{E}_{\epsilon}[\epsilon^\top A \epsilon]$ for random $\epsilon$ with zero mean and identity covariance, achieving $\mathcal{O}(D)$ cost and unrestricted architectures [3].

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Adaptive Computation as Emergent Depth

A discrete network of $L$ layers applies exactly $L$ transformations to every input. An adaptive ODE solver instead chooses its step count — the effective depth — per input, based on the local difficulty of integrating the learned vector field. On the MNIST benchmark, Neural ODEs achieve test error comparable to a parameter-matched ResNet while the number of function evaluations adapts automatically; the solver takes small steps where the vector field curves sharply and large steps where it is smooth [1]. This is *adaptive computation time* emerging from numerical analysis rather than from an architectural controller, and it provides a principled speed–accuracy tradeoff: at test time, loosening `rtol`/`atol` reduces function evaluations and latency while degrading accuracy gracefully.



### 4.2 The Topology of Learned Flows

For fixed parameters, the ODE solution operator $\mathbf{h}_0 \mapsto \mathbf{h}(t_1)$ is a **diffeomorphism**: trajectories of a Lipschitz vector field cannot cross, and the map is invertible with a differentiable inverse (obtained by integrating backward). This is a strength for generative modeling — invertibility comes free — but a representational *constraint* for classification. Dupont, Doucet, and Teh proved that standard Neural ODEs preserve the topology of the input space: they cannot, for example, implement the map $g(x) = -x$ on $\mathbb{R}^1$ separating interleaved classes, since any continuous flow on a connected domain preserves ordering [5].

The remedy is **augmentation**: evolve the ODE in a higher-dimensional space by concatenating zeros to the state, $\tilde{\mathbf{h}} = [\mathbf{h}; \mathbf{0}] \in \mathbb{R}^{D+p}$, and projecting back afterward. The extra dimensions provide "room" for trajectories to maneuver around one another, much as a knot unties in four dimensions. Augmented Neural ODEs are provably more expressive, empirically more stable, and often cheaper to solve — the simpler flows they learn require fewer solver steps [5].

### 4.3 Instability of the Reversed Adjoint

The adjoint method's elegance conceals a numerical trap. The backward pass re-solves the *forward* ODE in reverse time starting from $\mathbf{h}(t_1)$, assuming the reconstructed $\tilde{\mathbf{h}}(t)$ equals the forward $\mathbf{h}(t)$. But reversing a dissipative or expanding dynamical system is inherently ill-conditioned: contracting forward dynamics become *expanding* backward dynamics, and small numerical errors amplify exponentially. Gholami, Keutzer, and Biros demonstrated that for standard convolutional architectures with ReLU activations, the reversed solve diverges from the true trajectory, producing gradients that are not merely inaccurate but actively harmful — the optimize-then-discretize gap [4].

Their **ANODE** framework restores fidelity by checkpointing the forward trajectory (or selected states) and reconstructing $\mathbf{h}(t)$ exactly during the backward pass, trading a modest $\mathcal{O}(N_t)$ memory overhead for unconditionally accurate gradients. This crystallizes the field's central design tension:

1. **Discretize-then-optimize**: exact gradients of the executed computation, memory linear in steps.
2. **Optimize-then-discretize** (pure adjoint): constant memory, gradients of the idealized continuous system, potentially unstable.
3. **Checkpointed hybrids** (ANODE): interpolate between the two, recovering exactness with bounded memory [4].

### 4.4 Latent Dynamics for Irregular Time Series

Irregularly sampled observations — vital signs in an ICU, astronomical transients, sporadic sensor readings — break the fixed-step assumptions of RNNs. Rubanova, Chen, and Duvenaud generalized the VAE to continuous time: an encoder maps the observation history to a distribution over the *initial latent state* $\mathbf{z}(t_0)$, and a Neural ODE evolves it forward; the decoder queries the trajectory at arbitrary observation times [6]. The model naturally handles missing data and uneven gaps because time is an explicit continuous variable rather than an implicit step counter.

Key architectural elements:

- **ODE-RNN encoders** evolve the hidden state continuously *between* observations and apply discrete RNN updates *at* observations, blending continuous dynamics with event-driven corrections.
- **Poisson-process observation models** allow the network to learn not just values but *when* measurements occur, which is itself informative (sicker patients are measured more often).
- **Extrapolation** beyond the training window is well-defined: simply integrate further, unlike RNNs which have no native notion of "more time."

On ICU and human-activity datasets, latent ODEs substantially outperformed discrete baselines at interpolation and extrapolation under missingness [6].

## 5. Empirical Results / Proofs

### 5.1 Density Estimation at Scale

The decisive empirical validation of the CNF program is FFJORD's density-estimation benchmark. Replacing the exact trace with the Hutchinson estimator and using free-form (unrestricted) architectures, FFJORD achieved state-of-the-art exact-likelihood results among efficient-sampling models on the standard tabular benchmarks [3]:

| Dataset | Real NVP | Glow | FFJORD |
|---|---|---|---|
| POWER | −0.17 | −0.17 | **−0.46** |
| GAS | −8.33 | −8.15 | **−8.59** |
| HEPMASS | 18.71 | 18.92 | **15.26** |
| MINIBOONE | 13.55 | 11.35 | **10.43** |
| BSDS300 | −153.28 | −155.07 | **−157.67** |

*(Test negative log-likelihood in nats; lower is better [3].)* The complexity reduction from $\mathcal{O}((DH + D^3)L)$ in partitioned flows to $\mathcal{O}((DH + D)\hat{L})$ in FFJORD — where $\hat{L}$ is the number of solver evaluations — is what makes unrestricted high-dimensional flows computationally feasible [3].

### 5.2 Classification with Adaptive Depth

On MNIST, a Neural ODE classifier with roughly 220K parameters reached approximately 0.6% test error — competitive with a ResNet of similar size — while the adaptive solver automatically modulated function evaluations per input [1]. The augmented variant improved both accuracy and solver efficiency on CIFAR-10, confirming the topological analysis: simpler flows are easier to integrate [5].

### 5.3 Gradient Fidelity

Gholami et al. quantified the optimize-then-discretize error directly: on CIFAR-10/100 with ResNet and SqueezeNext backbones, the naive reversed adjoint produced divergent training at tight tolerances, while ANODE's checkpointed reconstruction matched discretize-then-optimize gradients to numerical precision and converged reliably [4]. This establishes that *exactness of the gradient estimator*, not merely memory efficiency, determines practical trainability.

> **Theorem (Summary of Trade-offs):** *No gradient estimator for Neural ODEs simultaneously achieves (i) $\mathcal{O}(1)$ memory in solver steps, (ii) exact agreement with the discretized forward computation, and (iii) unconditional numerical stability under reversal. The adjoint method sacrifices (ii)–(iii) for (i); backprop-through-solver sacrifices (i) for (ii)–(iii); checkpointed methods like ANODE trade (i) for a bounded $\mathcal{O}(N_t)$ memory cost recovering (ii)–(iii) [4].*

## 6. Limitations

1. **Solver cost and stiffness.** Every forward pass invokes an iterative numerical solver; stiff learned dynamics force tiny steps and explode NFE. Training can be several times slower than an equivalent ResNet, and wall-clock comparisons often favor discrete architectures despite favorable parameter counts.
2. **Gradient pathologies.** Beyond the reversal instability [4], Neural ODEs can suffer vanishing/exploding gradients through long time horizons exactly as RNNs do — continuity does not cure the underlying dynamical-systems pathology; it merely reparameterizes it.
3. **Topological expressivity ceiling.** Un-augmented flows are diffeomorphisms and cannot represent non-invertible or topology-changing maps; augmentation [5] mitigates but does not eliminate this, and the required augmentation dimension is problem-dependent.
4. **Tolerance tuning as a new hyperparameter.** `rtol`/`atol` control an accuracy–speed tradeoff with no discrete-network analogue; overly loose tolerances silently corrupt both predictions and gradients.
5. **Stochastic extensions are subtle.** Extending to stochastic differential equations (Neural SDEs) requires Itô/Stratonovich calculus, adjoint methods for SDEs, and careful handling of Brownian noise in reverse time — each step materially harder than the deterministic case.
6. **Limited advantage for static i.i.d. tasks.** For standard image classification with abundant compute, well-tuned ResNets and transformers remain more practical; the continuous formulation's advantages concentrate in irregular time series, generative modeling, and physics-informed settings.

## 7. Conclusion

Neural ordinary differential equations reframe deep learning's central object — the layer — as the time step of a dynamical system, and in doing so import a century of mathematical machinery: adjoint sensitivity from optimal control, adaptive integration from numerical analysis, Liouville's theorem from statistical mechanics, and diffeomorphism constraints from topology. The adjoint method's constant-memory gradient, the instantaneous change of variables, and the Hutchinson trace estimator together make continuous-depth learning not merely a conceptual curiosity but a tractable engineering discipline, validated on density estimation, time-series modeling, and adaptive-depth classification [1][3][6].

The subsequent corrections — augmentation for expressivity [5], checkpointing for gradient fidelity [4] — are as instructive as the original breakthrough: they show that continuous depth is not a free lunch but a *different* set of trade-offs, governed by the numerical analysis of the learned dynamics rather than by layer counts. As the field extends toward controlled differential equations, neural SDEs, and physics-constrained flows, the lesson endures: when parameters become controls and layers become time, the mathematics of dynamics becomes the mathematics of learning.
