# Differentiable Rendering for Inverse Graphics: Neural Radiance Fields versus 3D Gaussian Splatting, Volumetric Path-Tracing Gradients, and Differentiable Mesh Rasterization

## Abstract

Differentiable rendering unifies image formation and gradient-based optimization, turning the classical forward problem of graphics into the inverse problem of recovering scene parameters from images. This thesis compares the three dominant differentiable rendering paradigms: neural volumetric rendering inaugurated by Neural Radiance Fields (NeRF) [1], explicit primitive rasterization embodied by 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) [2], and differentiable mesh rasterization with silhouette-aware gradient estimators. We derive the volumetric rendering gradient, contrast the continuous-field MLP formulation with discrete anisotropic Gaussians and their tile-based sorting rasterizer, and analyze how rasterization — discontinuous at triangle boundaries — is made differentiable via barycentric interpolation, antialiased edge sampling, and approximate visibility gradients. We further connect these renderers to Score Distillation Sampling [3], which treats a differentiable renderer as a generator inside a diffusion-model objective for text-to-3D synthesis. Quantitatively, NeRF-class methods maximize fidelity at minutes-per-frame cost, while 3DGS achieves 100+ fps at 1080p with comparable perceptual quality but unbounded memory and weak extrapolation guarantees. We close on open problems: unbiased gradients through discontinuities, relightable disentanglement, and the statistical theory of densification.

## 1 Introduction

The rendering equation describes how light propagates through a scene to form an image; it is the forward map $R: \Theta \to \mathcal{I}$ from scene parameters $\theta \in \Theta$ (geometry, materials, lights) to pixel observations. *Inverse graphics* inverts this map: given images, recover $\theta$. Classical inverse graphics relied on analysis-by-synthesis with hand-derived adjoints or stochastic finite differences — slow, fragile, and limited to toy scenes. The advent of automatic differentiation and GPU computing transformed this landscape: if $R$ is (approximately) differentiable, then any image-space loss $\mathcal{L}(R(\theta))$ yields gradients $\nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}$ via the chain rule, and the entire apparatus of first-order optimization — Adam, L-BFGS, natural gradients — becomes applicable to scene recovery.

Three technical families now dominate. First, **neural volumetric rendering**, launched by NeRF [1], represents the scene as a continuous 5D function $F_\theta: (\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{d}) \mapsto (\mathbf{c}, \sigma)$ mapping spatial position and viewing direction to emitted radiance and volume density, synthesized by quadrature along camera rays. Because every operation — MLP evaluation, quadrature, tone mapping — is smooth, gradients flow unobstructed from pixels to weights. Second, **3D Gaussian Splatting** [2] replaces the implicit MLP with an explicit set of anisotropic 3D Gaussians, projects ("splats") them to 2D, sorts by depth, and alpha-composites them in a custom CUDA rasterizer whose backward pass is hand-derived. It retains differentiability while achieving real-time frame rates. Third, **differentiable mesh rasterization** keeps the classical triangle-mesh representation but softens the hard z-buffer decision: gradients with respect to vertex positions are approximated through barycentric interpolation of attributes and analytic or stochastic treatment of silhouette edges.

This thesis is organized as follows. Section 2 establishes the mathematical background: the rendering equation, volume rendering, and the measure-theoretic view of differentiating integrals with moving discontinuities. Section 3 presents the methodology: the NeRF quadrature gradient, the 3DGS splatting gradient, and the rasterization gradient. Section 4 deep-dives into four topics: volumetric gradient geometry, the representation-theoretic comparison of NeRF and 3DGS, the differentiability of rasterization, and Score Distillation Sampling as a consumer of differentiable renderers. Section 5 gives empirical comparisons and a formal theorem on volumetric gradient unbiasedness. Section 6 discusses limitations, and Section 7 concludes.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The Rendering Equation and Its Differentiability

The steady-state rendering equation [Kajiya, 1986] governs outgoing radiance $L_o$ at surface point $\mathbf{p}$ in direction $\omega_o$:

$$L_o(\mathbf{p}, \omega_o) = L_e(\mathbf{p}, \omega_o) + \int_{\Omega} f_r(\mathbf{p}, \omega_i, \omega_o)\, L_i(\mathbf{p}, \omega_i)\, (\omega_i \cdot \mathbf{n})\, d\omega_i$$

Differentiating an image with respect to scene parameters requires differentiating this integral. When geometry moves, the *domain of integration itself* moves: visibility discontinuities sweep across pixels. Formally, for an integral over a parameter-dependent domain,

$$\frac{d}{d\theta} \int_{\Omega(\theta)} f(\mathbf{x}, \theta)\, d\mathbf{x} = \int_{\Omega(\theta)} \frac{\partial f}{\partial \theta}\, d\mathbf{x} + \int_{\partial\Omega(\theta)} f(\mathbf{x}, \theta)\, (\mathbf{v} \cdot \mathbf{n})\, dS$$

the Reynolds transport theorem, where the boundary term captures moving discontinuities. *This boundary term is the entire difficulty of differentiable rendering.* Volumetric methods sidestep it by replacing hard surfaces with soft density fields — the boundary term vanishes because there is no boundary. Rasterization methods must confront it directly.

### 2.2 Volume Rendering: The Emission–Absorption Model

NeRF builds on the classical emission–absorption volume rendering integral. Along a ray $\mathbf{r}(t) = \mathbf{o} + t\mathbf{d}$,

$$C(\mathbf{r}) = \int_{t_n}^{t_f} T(t)\, \sigma(\mathbf{r}(t))\, \mathbf{c}(\mathbf{r}(t), \mathbf{d})\, dt, \qquad T(t) = \exp\!\left(-\int_{t_n}^{t} \sigma(\mathbf{r}(s))\, ds\right)$$

where $T(t)$ is the transmittance — the probability that a photon travels from $t_n$ to $t$ without absorption. NeRF approximates this with stratified quadrature:

$$\hat{C}(\mathbf{r}) = \sum_{i=1}^{N} T_i\, (1 - \exp(-\sigma_i \delta_i))\, \mathbf{c}_i, \qquad T_i = \exp\!\left(-\sum_{j=1}^{i-1} \sigma_j \delta_j\right)$$

Every term is a smooth function of the network outputs $(\sigma_i, \mathbf{c}_i)$, and therefore of the weights $\theta$. The gradient $\partial \hat{C} / \partial \theta$ is obtained exactly by reverse-mode autodiff — no boundary terms, no approximations. This mathematical convenience is the deep reason NeRF optimizes so reliably, and it is the property 3DGS was engineered to preserve in a rasterization context.

### 2.3 Inverse Graphics as Optimization

Given calibrated views $\{I_k\}$ with camera poses $\{\Pi_k\}$, inverse graphics solves

$$\theta^* = \arg\min_\theta \sum_k \mathcal{L}\big(R(\theta; \Pi_k),\, I_k\big) + \lambda\, \mathcal{R}(\theta)$$

where $R$ is the differentiable renderer, $\mathcal{L}$ a photometric loss (typically $\ell_2$ or a perceptual variant), and $\mathcal{R}$ a regularizer. The three paradigms differ only — but profoundly — in their choice of $R$ and of the parameter space $\Theta$:

| Paradigm | Scene representation $\Theta$ | Forward pass | Gradient mechanism |
|---|---|---|---|
| NeRF [1] | MLP weights ($\sim$ MBs) | Ray marching + quadrature | Exact reverse-mode autodiff |
| 3D Gaussian Splatting [2] | Gaussian means, covariances, SH coefficients, opacities ($\sim$ 100s of MBs) | Project, sort, alpha-composite | Hand-derived CUDA backward |
| Differentiable rasterization | Mesh vertices, textures, BRDF parameters | Z-buffer rasterization | Barycentric VJPs + silhouette approximations |

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 The NeRF Forward–Backward Pair

The NeRF network $F_\theta$ maps a 5D input to density and radiance. Positional encoding $\gamma(p) = (\sin(2^0\pi p), \cos(2^0\pi p), \dots, \sin(2^{L-1}\pi p), \cos(2^{L-1}\pi p))$ lifts low-dimensional coordinates into a high-frequency Fourier basis, counteracting the spectral bias of MLPs toward smooth functions [1]. Training minimizes

$$\mathcal{L} = \sum_{\mathbf{r} \in \mathcal{R}} \big\|\hat{C}_c(\mathbf{r}) - C(\mathbf{r})\big\|_2^2 + \big\|\hat{C}_f(\mathbf{r}) - C(\mathbf{r})\big\|_2^2$$

over coarse and fine networks, where the fine network's samples are importance-distributed according to the coarse network's opacity weights $w_i = T_i(1 - \exp(-\sigma_i\delta_i))$ — a form of *differentiable importance sampling* along each ray.

A minimal PyTorch sketch of the volumetric gradient path is:

```python
def render_ray(sigmas, colors, deltas):
    # sigmas, colors: (N,), (N,3) network outputs; deltas: (N,) segment lengths
    alpha = 1.0 - torch.exp(-sigmas * deltas)          # opacity per segment
    T = torch.cumprod(torch.cat([torch.ones(1), 1.0 - alpha[:-1]]), dim=0)
    weights = T * alpha                                # discrete transmittance weights
    return (weights[:, None] * colors).sum(dim=0)      # pixel color; fully differentiable

loss = ((render_ray(s, c, d) - target) ** 2).mean()
loss.backward()  # exact d(loss)/d(theta) via the quadrature chain
```

Note the structural fact that makes this work: `weights` form a *probability distribution* over depth ($\sum_i w_i \le 1$), so the rendered color is an expectation under a learned depth distribution, and its gradient decomposes into a score-function-like term in $\sigma$ and a pathwise term in $\mathbf{c}$.

### 3.2 The 3D Gaussian Splatting Pipeline

3DGS [2] parameterizes the scene as a set of $M$ anisotropic Gaussians, each with mean $\boldsymbol{\mu} \in \mathbb{R}^3$, covariance $\boldsymbol{\Sigma} = \mathbf{R}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{S}^\top\mathbf{R}^\top$ (rotation $\mathbf{R}$ from a quaternion, scale $\mathbf{S}$ diagonal), opacity $o$, and view-dependent color via spherical harmonics coefficients. Rendering proceeds in four stages:

1. **Projection.** Each 3D Gaussian is projected to screen space, yielding a 2D covariance $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}' = \mathbf{J}\mathbf{W}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mathbf{W}^\top\mathbf{J}^\top$ where $\mathbf{W}$ is the viewing transformation and $\mathbf{J}$ the Jacobian of the projective mapping.
2. **Tiling and sorting.** The screen is divided into $16 \times 16$ tiles; Gaussians overlapping a tile are sorted front-to-back by depth via a GPU radix sort.
3. **Alpha compositing.** Per pixel, sorted splats are composited: $C = \sum_i \mathbf{c}_i \alpha_i \prod_{j<i}(1 - \alpha_j)$, where $\alpha_i$ is the opacity-weighted 2D Gaussian falloff.
4. **Backward pass.** A hand-written CUDA kernel propagates $\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial C$ back through the compositing order, the projection Jacobian, and into $\boldsymbol{\mu}, \mathbf{R}, \mathbf{S}, o$, and SH coefficients.

Optimization interleaves gradient steps with **adaptive density control**: Gaussians with large view-space positional gradients are *cloned* (if small) or *split* (if large), and near-transparent Gaussians are pruned. Training minimizes $\mathcal{L} = (1-\lambda)\mathcal{L}_1 + \lambda\mathcal{L}_{\text{D-SSIM}}$ with $\lambda = 0.2$ [2].

### 3.3 Differentiable Mesh Rasterization

Mesh rasterization computes, per pixel, the visible triangle and its barycentric coordinates $(\alpha, \beta, \gamma)$. Pixel color is $C = \sum_k w_k A_k$ where $w_k$ are barycentric weights and $A_k$ vertex attributes (position, normal, UV). The mapping $(\alpha,\beta,\gamma) \mapsto$ attributes is *linear and smooth* — gradients with respect to vertex attributes and (via the chain rule through barycentrics) vertex positions are well-defined in the interior of triangles. The pathology is at edges: coverage is a step function, so $\partial C / \partial \mathbf{v}$ is zero almost everywhere and undefined at silhouettes.

Practical systems resolve this with three complementary devices:

- **Antialiased edge sampling** (nvdiffrast-style): pixels straddling triangle edges receive blended contributions, giving nonzero, finite-difference-consistent gradients near boundaries.
- **Silhouette gradient approximations**: analytic boundary integrals (the Reynolds term) or soft rasterizers that replace the hard z-test with a smooth probabilistic aggregation over candidate faces.
- **Two-stage optimization**: coarse geometry from silhouettes, then fine detail from shading — since shading gradients are reliable even when silhouette gradients are noisy.

### 3.4 Score Distillation Sampling: The Renderer as Generator

DreamFusion [3] showed that a differentiable renderer can serve as the generator $g(\theta)$ inside a distillation objective from a frozen text-to-image diffusion model. Score Distillation Sampling (SDS) optimizes

$$\nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_{\text{SDS}} = \mathbb{E}_{t,\epsilon}\Big[w(t)\,\big(\hat{\epsilon}_\phi(\mathbf{x}_t; y, t) - \epsilon\big)\, \frac{\partial \mathbf{x}}{\partial \theta}\Big]$$

where $\mathbf{x} = g(\theta)$ is the rendered image, $\mathbf{x}_t$ its noised version, and $\hat{\epsilon}_\phi$ the diffusion model's noise prediction. Crucially, the gradient flows *through the renderer* via $\partial \mathbf{x} / \partial \theta$ but *not* through the diffusion U-Net (the score term is stop-gradiented). SDS therefore converts any differentiable renderer — NeRF, 3DGS, or mesh rasterizer — into a text-to-3D engine, and the quality of the 3D result is bounded above by the fidelity of $\partial \mathbf{x} / \partial \theta$. This is why the choice of differentiable renderer is not merely an engineering detail: it determines which scene hypotheses are reachable by gradient descent under a learned prior.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Geometry of Volumetric Gradients

Consider the gradient of the rendered color with respect to a single density sample $\sigma_k$:

$$\frac{\partial \hat{C}}{\partial \sigma_k} = \delta_k e^{-\sigma_k \delta_k} T_k \mathbf{c}_k \;-\; \delta_k \sum_{i>k} T_i (1 - e^{-\sigma_i\delta_i}) \mathbf{c}_i$$

The first term *increases* the pixel's color by adding emission at $k$; the second *decreases* it by occluding everything behind $k$. This push–pull structure is the volumetric analogue of the Reynolds boundary term: instead of a delta-function at a moving surface, occlusion is distributed smoothly over depth. Two consequences follow. First, the gradient is *dense* — every sample along every ray receives signal — which explains NeRF's remarkable ability to bootstrap geometry from scratch. Second, the gradient magnitude is modulated by $T_k$: samples in already-opaque regions are starved of gradient, an automatic "don't fix what isn't broken" weighting that stabilizes optimization but can also trap it in local minima (the well-known "floater" and "background collapse" artifacts).

> **Theorem:** *Unbiasedness of the quadrature gradient.* Let $\hat{C}_N$ be the $N$-sample stratified quadrature estimator of the volume rendering integral $C$, with samples drawn i.i.d. within strata. Then $\mathbb{E}[\nabla_\theta \hat{C}_N] = \nabla_\theta \mathbb{E}[\hat{C}_N]$, i.e., reverse-mode autodiff through the quadrature yields an unbiased estimator of the gradient of the expected rendering. Moreover, as $N \to \infty$ with stratified sampling, $\nabla_\theta \hat{C}_N \to \nabla_\theta C$ almost surely.
>
> *Proof sketch.* Within each stratum the sample positions are independent of $\theta$ (they depend only on the camera ray), so differentiation commutes with expectation by the dominated convergence theorem, provided $\sigma_\theta$ and $\mathbf{c}_\theta$ are uniformly bounded with bounded derivatives — true for MLPs with Lipschitz activations on a compact domain. Stratification gives the almost-sure convergence via the strong law of large numbers applied stratum-wise. ∎

The practical import: NeRF's gradients are *unbiased Monte Carlo estimators*, whereas rasterization gradients are *biased approximations* whose bias concentrates at discontinuities. This is the fundamental statistical advantage of the volumetric formulation.

### 4.2 NeRF versus 3D Gaussian Splatting: A Representation-Theoretic Comparison

NeRF and 3DGS solve the same inverse problem with dual representations, and their trade-offs can be understood through approximation theory:

- **Capacity vs. parsimony.** A NeRF MLP with $\sim 10^6$ parameters represents arbitrarily complex scenes at fixed memory cost, but each query costs a full network forward pass — rendering a $800 \times 800$ image with 192 samples per ray requires $\sim 10^8$ MLP evaluations. 3DGS stores the scene *explicitly* as $10^5$–$10^6$ Gaussians (hundreds of MB), but each primitive contributes to only the pixels it overlaps, and projection is a closed-form matrix operation. The trade is *compute for memory*: NeRF is compute-bound, 3DGS is memory-bound.
- **Smoothness priors.** The MLP's spectral bias is an *implicit regularizer*: NeRF interpolates smoothly between views and extrapolates plausibly. 3DGS has no such prior — each Gaussian is independent, so quality depends entirely on the adaptive density control heuristic, which is a discrete, non-differentiable structural search grafted onto continuous optimization. This explains 3DGS's occasional "popping" artifacts under large viewpoint changes and its weaker performance on sparse-view reconstruction.
- **Frequency control.** NeRF's positional encoding sets a global frequency budget $L$; 3DGS's per-Gaussian scale $\mathbf{S}$ sets a *local, adaptive* frequency budget. The latter is strictly more expressive — fine detail where needed, coarse blobs elsewhere — and is the deeper reason 3DGS matches or exceeds NeRF's PSNR while rendering orders of magnitude faster.
- **Anti-aliasing.** Classical NeRF aliases under minification because it point-samples the field. Mip-NeRF-style integrated positional encodings and, in the splatting world, Mip-Splatting [4] (which adds a 3D smoothing filter and 2D mip filter to the Gaussian projection) address this by making the representation *scale-aware* — a reminder that differentiability alone does not guarantee correct signal processing.

Empirically, on the synthetic NeRF benchmark, both families report PSNR in the low 30s dB; the decisive difference is *throughput*: NeRF-class methods render at seconds-per-frame, while 3DGS reports $\ge 100$ fps at 1080p [2], with training times competitive with the fastest prior art.

### 4.3 Making Rasterization Differentiable: Silhouettes, Barycentrics, and Soft Aggregation

The hard z-buffer is a piecewise-constant function of vertex positions; its true gradient is zero almost everywhere. Differentiable rasterizers therefore *redefine* the forward pass to admit useful gradients:

1. **Barycentric attribute interpolation** gives exact gradients for everything *except* coverage: $\partial C/\partial A_k = w_k$ and $\partial C/\partial \mathbf{v}$ via the Jacobian of barycentrics w.r.t. screen-space vertex positions.
2. **Edge antialiasing** replaces the binary coverage test with a fractional coverage computed from sub-pixel edge distances, yielding gradients that point vertices toward increasing the covered area of correctly-colored triangles — a finite, well-scaled signal.
3. **Soft rasterization** aggregates the $K$ nearest faces per pixel with softmax weights $w_k \propto \exp(-d_k/\tau)$ based on depth or distance, making occlusion itself differentiable at temperature $\tau > 0$. As $\tau \to 0$ the soft renderer converges to the hard one, but gradients vanish — the classic *bias–variance/temperature trade-off* of relaxed discrete computation.

Compared with volumetric methods, rasterization gradients are *sparse but precise*: only silhouette-adjacent vertices receive geometric signal, but that signal directly encodes the boundary motion that volumetric methods must discover through density sculpting. Hybrid systems increasingly exploit both — e.g., optimizing a mesh with a volumetric warm-start, or distilling a NeRF into a rasterizable mesh for real-time deployment.

### 4.4 Score Distillation as a Lens on Renderer Quality

SDS [3] reframes text-to-3D as: find $\theta$ such that renders $g(\theta)$ lie in high-density regions of a diffusion prior. The SDS gradient has a revealing decomposition:

$$\nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_{\text{SDS}} \;\propto\; \underbrace{\big(\hat{\epsilon}_\phi - \epsilon\big)}_{\text{prior direction in image space}} \;\cdot\; \underbrace{\frac{\partial \mathbf{x}}{\partial \theta}}_{\text{renderer Jacobian}}$$

The prior can only "see" $\theta$ through the renderer's Jacobian. If $\partial \mathbf{x}/\partial \theta$ is rank-deficient along some parameter direction — as happens with NeRF in saturated-density regions (recall the $T_k$ modulation) or with meshes at non-silhouette vertices — the prior cannot correct errors in that subspace, no matter how good the diffusion model is. This yields a concrete design principle: *the ideal differentiable renderer for SDS maximizes the rank and conditioning of its Jacobian over the parameter space of interest*. Volumetric renderers offer dense Jacobians (good for discovery); explicit renderers offer well-conditioned Jacobians over their parameters (good for refinement). State-of-the-art text-to-3D pipelines consequently use a *coarse-to-fine renderer schedule*: NeRF-like volumes for structure discovery, then mesh or Gaussian refinement — precisely mirroring the classical coarse-to-fine wisdom of inverse problems.

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 Benchmark Comparison

The table below consolidates representative figures reported in the literature for novel-view synthesis on standard benchmarks (numbers are as reported by the respective papers and should be read as indicative rather than head-to-head, since evaluation protocols differ):

| Method | Representation | Synthetic PSNR (dB) | Render speed | Training time | Memory |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| NeRF [1] | MLP, 5D field | ~31.0 | ~30 s/frame | 1–2 days | ~5 MB |
| 3D Gaussian Splatting [2] | $10^5$–$10^6$ anisotropic Gaussians | ~31.5–33 | $\ge 100$ fps @1080p | 30–45 min | 100s of MB |
| Mip-Splatting [4] | Filtered Gaussians | ~32–33.5 | real-time | ~40 min | 100s of MB |
| Diff. mesh rasterizer | Vertices + textures | task-dependent | real-time | minutes–hours | ~10s of MB |

The qualitative pattern is robust across scenes: volumetric MLP methods achieve the best *memory-normalized* quality; splatting methods achieve the best *time-normalized* quality; mesh methods dominate when a surface parameterization is required downstream (simulation, editing, fabrication).

### 5.2 Gradient-Flow Experiment (Conceptual)

Consider optimizing a single Gaussian's mean $\boldsymbol{\mu}$ to match a target pixel under the 3DGS compositing model. The loss landscape is *unimodal* in $\boldsymbol{\mu}$ when the Gaussian overlaps the pixel (the 2D falloff is log-concave) but flat when it does not — hence the densification heuristic's reliance on accumulated positional gradients as a *detector* of under-reconstruction. Contrast NeRF: the density field's gradient is nonzero everywhere along the ray, so a single MLP can "grow" geometry into empty space without any discrete structural moves. This is the precise sense in which volumetric gradients are *global* and splatting gradients are *local* — and why 3DGS requires a good SfM initialization (COLMAP point cloud) while NeRF can start from random weights.

### 5.3 On the Necessity of the Boundary Term

A short proof clarifies why rasterization cannot evade silhouette handling. Let pixel intensity be $I(\theta) = \int f(\mathbf{x}) \mathbf{1}_{\{\mathbf{x} \in \Omega(\theta)\}} d\mathbf{x}$ for a triangle $\Omega(\theta)$ moving with vertices $\theta$. Then

$$\nabla_\theta I = \int_{\partial\Omega} f(\mathbf{s})\, \big(\nabla_\theta \mathbf{s} \cdot \mathbf{n}\big)\, dS$$

— the *entire* gradient lives on the boundary. Any rasterizer that reports zero silhouette gradients is not approximating the true gradient; it is optimizing a *different objective* (the interior-only variation). Soft rasterizers and antialiased samplers can be understood as convolving $I$ with a smoothing kernel $k_\tau$ before differentiating: $\nabla_\theta (I * k_\tau) = (\nabla_\theta I) * k_\tau$, which spreads the boundary delta-function into a band of width $\tau$ — nonzero, finite, and converging to the true distributional gradient as $\tau \to 0$. This is the mathematical content of "making rasterization differentiable."

## 6 Limitations

**NeRF-class methods.** Training and rendering remain compute-intensive; the MLP must be evaluated hundreds of times per pixel. The representation entangles geometry, reflectance, and illumination — relighting a NeRF is ill-posed without additional priors. View-dependent effects are modeled but not *disentangled*, and extrapolation beyond the training view distribution degrades rapidly. The unbiased-gradient property (Section 4.1) holds for the quadrature but not for the *hierarchical sampling* distribution, whose dependence on coarse-network weights introduces a subtle bias rarely acknowledged.

**3D Gaussian Splatting.** Memory consumption scales with scene complexity without a principled bound; large outdoor scenes require millions of Gaussians. The densification heuristic is non-differentiable structural search with sensitive hyperparameters (gradient thresholds, opacity reset schedules). There is no smoothness prior, so sparse-view performance lags behind MLP methods, and the representation is not a surface — extracting meshes requires post-hoc distillation. Aliasing under extreme minification motivated Mip-Splatting [4], but a complete sampling theory for splatted primitives remains open.

**Differentiable rasterization.** Silhouette gradients, however carefully approximated, remain biased; thin structures and sub-pixel geometry defeat edge-based estimators. The mesh topology is fixed during optimization — topological changes (hole formation, part separation) are unreachable by gradient descent on vertex positions. Material–lighting ambiguity persists: without strong BRDF priors, recovered "albedo" bakes in illumination.

**Score distillation.** SDS is mode-seeking (it maximizes a lower bound related to the diffusion model's score, not the data likelihood), producing the well-documented oversaturation and oversmoothing artifacts [3]. Its gradients inherit all renderer limitations above, and the method is computationally extravagant — thousands of diffusion evaluations per scene.

## 7 Conclusion

Differentiable rendering has matured from a collection of tricks into a principled discipline with a clear mathematical core: *the gradient of an image with respect to a scene is governed by how discontinuities move*. Volumetric methods — NeRF [1] and its descendants — eliminate discontinuities by construction, buying unbiased, dense gradients at the price of compute. 3D Gaussian Splatting [2] keeps the volumetric spirit (soft, order-independent-ish compositing) while restoring the explicit-primitive efficiency of rasterization, buying real-time performance at the price of memory and structural heuristics. Differentiable mesh rasterization confronts discontinuities head-on with smoothing and boundary integrals, buying compatibility with the entire downstream graphics pipeline at the price of biased, sparse geometric gradients. Score Distillation Sampling [3] reveals that these are not competing answers to different questions but interchangeable *gradient oracles* for a single question — how to move scene parameters so that images improve — to be selected by the conditioning of their Jacobians.

The open frontier is convergence: representations that are simultaneously memory-parsimonious, real-time, topologically flexible, relightable, and equipped with unbiased gradients through true discontinuities. Until then, the practitioner's rule stands: *use volumes to discover, splats to render, and meshes to ship*.
