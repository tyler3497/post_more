# Optimal Auction Design and Market Engineering: From Myerson's Virtual Surplus to Spectrum and Sponsored-Search Markets

## Abstract

Auction theory asks which selling mechanism maximizes revenue — or welfare — when bidders hold private information. This thesis develops the canonical theory end to end: Vickrey's [2] second-price insight, the Vickrey–Clarke–Groves pivot mechanism [8, 9], and the mechanism-design apparatus of the revelation principle and Myerson's lemma [1]. The centerpiece is Myerson's (1981) optimal auction: expected revenue equals expected *virtual* welfare, so the seller allocates to the highest non-negative virtual value, ironing when regularity fails and imposing an optimal reserve [1, 3]. We extend the analysis to affiliated values — the linkage principle and the winner's curse [6] — and to the field's two great applied triumphs: the FCC's simultaneous multiple-round spectrum auctions, which have raised over $100 billion on designs by Milgrom and Wilson [10], and sponsored-search position auctions, where the generalized second-price auction [4] admits equilibria replicating the truthful VCG outcome [5, 7]. Executable experiments on optimal reserves and virtual-value ironing ground the theory numerically.

## 1 Introduction

Few branches of economic theory have moved so directly from blackboard to billion-dollar infrastructure as auction theory. When the U.S. Federal Communications Commission began auctioning radio spectrum in 1994, it deployed a simultaneous multiple-round ascending auction designed by Paul Milgrom and Robert Wilson (with Preston McAfee) — a format that has since raised more than $100 billion for the U.S. Treasury and been emulated on three continents [10]. When Google and Yahoo! needed to sell billions of keyword advertising slots per day, they converged on the *generalized second-price* (GSP) auction, a mechanism whose equilibrium properties were only understood after the fact by Edelman, Ostrovsky, and Schwarz [4] and Varian [5]. In 2020, Milgrom and Wilson received the Nobel Prize in Economic Sciences for "improvements to auction theory and inventions of new auction formats" [10].

The intellectual core of the field is *mechanism design*: the seller chooses the rules of the game — who gets the good, who pays what — subject to the constraint that self-interested bidders will participate and report their valuations strategically. William Vickrey's 1961 paper showed that a sealed-bid second-price auction makes truthful bidding a dominant strategy [2]; Roger Myerson's 1981 paper went vastly further, characterizing *every* incentive-compatible auction and solving the seller's optimization problem over all of them at once [1]. The striking conclusion: expected revenue equals expected *virtual* welfare, where a bidder's virtual value discounts her true value by the information rent the seller must surrender to elicit truth-telling. The optimal auction therefore handicaps bidders — discriminating by value distribution, excluding low types via a reserve price, and sometimes refusing to sell at all.

This thesis reconstructs that arc with full technical detail. Section 2 fixes the model and surveys the classical formats. Section 3 develops the mechanism-design methodology: direct revelation mechanisms, the revelation principle, Myerson's lemma, and the payment identity. Section 4 contains the deep dive: optimal auctions and ironing, the marginal-revenue interpretation of Bulow and Roberts [3], interdependent values and the linkage principle [6], position auctions [4, 5, 7], and combinatorial spectrum design [10]. Section 5 gives proofs and numerical experiments. Section 6 confronts the theory's limitations — common-knowledge distributions, risk neutrality, collusion, complexity — and Section 7 concludes.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The single-parameter private-values model

The canonical environment has one seller, one indivisible good, and *n* bidders. Bidder *i* has a private valuation *vᵢ* drawn independently from a distribution *Fᵢ* with density *fᵢ* > 0 on [*v̲ᵢ*, *v̄ᵢ*]; distributions are common knowledge but realized values are private. A mechanism specifies an allocation rule *xᵢ(**v**)* (probability *i* wins) and a payment rule *pᵢ(**v**)*. Bidders are risk-neutral expected-utility maximizers with quasi-linear utility *uᵢ* = *vᵢxᵢ* − *pᵢ*. A mechanism is *incentive compatible* (IC) if truthful reporting is optimal, and *individually rational* (IR) if participation beats the outside option.

> **Theorem (Revelation principle):** For any mechanism and any equilibrium of that mechanism, there exists an incentive-compatible direct-revelation mechanism that reproduces the same allocation and payments. Hence the seller may restrict attention, without loss of generality, to mechanisms in which bidders report their values truthfully [1].

### 2.2 Classical auction formats

| Format | Rules | Dominant strategy? | Efficient? | Revenue-equivalent? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| English (ascending) | Price rises until one bidder remains | Stay until price = value | Yes | Yes |
| Dutch (descending) | Price falls; first to stop wins at that price | Shade bid (equiv. FPSB) | Yes | Yes |
| First-price sealed-bid | Highest bid wins, pays own bid | Shade bid | Yes | Yes |
| Second-price sealed-bid (Vickrey) | Highest bid wins, pays second-highest bid | Bid value truthfully | Yes | Yes |
| All-pay | Highest bid wins, *all* bidders pay their bids | Shade bid | Yes | Yes |
| VCG (multi-item) | Efficient allocation; each pays externality imposed | Bid value truthfully | Yes | — |

Vickrey [2] proved the revenue-equivalence intuition for the standard four: under independent private values, risk neutrality, and symmetry, every auction that always awards the good to the highest-value bidder and gives zero surplus to the lowest type yields the *same* expected revenue.

### 2.3 The Vickrey–Clarke–Groves mechanism

For settings beyond a single good — multiple heterogeneous items, combinatorial preferences — the efficient, dominant-strategy mechanism is the Vickrey–Clarke–Groves (VCG) mechanism. Each agent reports her valuation function; the mechanism selects the welfare-maximizing allocation, and each agent pays the *externality* she imposes: the welfare others would have enjoyed had she been absent.

## 3 Methodology

Our methodology is the direct-revelation mechanism-design program of Myerson [1], executed in four steps.

**Step 1 — Characterize IC mechanisms.** For single-parameter settings, incentive compatibility is equivalent to two conditions:

> **Theorem (Myerson's lemma):** A single-parameter mechanism is dominant-strategy incentive compatible (DSIC) if and only if (i) each bidder's allocation rule *xᵢ(z, **v**₋ᵢ)* is non-decreasing in her report *z*, and (ii) payments satisfy the *payment identity*
> *pᵢ(vᵢ, **v**₋ᵢ) = vᵢ·xᵢ(vᵢ, **v**₋ᵢ) − ∫ xᵢ(z, **v**₋ᵢ) dz + pᵢ(0, **v**₋ᵢ),*
> integrating from the lowest type to *vᵢ* [1].

The integral term is the bidder's *information rent*: the surplus she must be granted so that higher types do not mimic lower ones.

**Step 2 — Express revenue in terms of the allocation rule.** Substituting the payment identity into expected revenue and integrating by parts (a Fubini argument) yields the central identity:

> **Theorem (Myerson):** In any Bayesian incentive-compatible, interim-IR single-parameter auction, expected revenue equals expected *virtual* welfare:
> **E[Σᵢ pᵢ(**v**)] = E[Σᵢ φᵢ(vᵢ)·xᵢ(**v**)],**
> where the *virtual value* is **φᵢ(vᵢ) = vᵢ − (1 − Fᵢ(vᵢ))/fᵢ(vᵢ)** [1].

The term (1 − Fᵢ(vᵢ))/fᵢ(vᵢ) — the inverse hazard rate — is exactly the marginal information rent of type *vᵢ*.

**Step 3 — Pointwise optimization.** Because the objective is linear in the allocation rule at each value profile, the unconstrained optimum is trivial: give the good to the bidder with the highest *non-negative* virtual value (breaking ties arbitrarily), and withhold the good if all virtual values are negative [1]. The difficulty is the monotonicity constraint from Myerson's lemma — handled by *ironing* (§4.1).

**Step 4 — Recover payments.** The payment identity converts the optimal allocation rule into payments; equivalently, any standard auction implementing that allocation rule with the same lowest-type payment achieves the optimum.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The optimal auction, reserve prices, and ironing

Assume *regularity*: each φᵢ is non-decreasing. Then awarding the good to the highest non-negative virtual value is monotone in each bidder's report, so the pointwise optimum is feasible and therefore optimal. In the symmetric i.i.d. case, the highest virtual value belongs to the highest value, and the rule "sell to the highest bidder above a reserve, at the maximum of the reserve and the second-highest bid" emerges — that is, the optimal auction is simply a **second-price auction with an optimal reserve price** *r** = φ⁻¹(0) [1]. Bulow and Roberts [3] gave this a crisp economic reading: φᵢ(vᵢ) is the *marginal revenue* of selling to type vᵢ, so the seller acts like a monopolist, serving demand (types) in decreasing order of marginal revenue and excluding all types with negative marginal revenue.

When bidders are *asymmetric*, the optimal auction handicaps them explicitly. A textbook example [1, and lecture notes]: bidder 1 has *v₁* ∼ Uniform[0,1] and bidder 2 has *v₂* ∼ Uniform[0,100], so φ₁(v₁) = 2v₁ − 1 and φ₂(v₂) = 2v₂ − 100.

When regularity fails, φᵢ decreases on some interval, and pointwise virtual-surplus maximization would violate monotonicity. Myerson's fix is **ironing**: replace φᵢ on each offending interval by its average, i.e., pool the types there and randomize the allocation among them, producing the *ironed* virtual value *φ̄ᵢ* — the derivative of the convex hull of the integrated quantile function.

### 4.2 Revenue equivalence and the marginal-revenue view

> **Theorem (Revenue equivalence):** Any two IC, IR mechanisms that induce the same allocation rule — and give the lowest type the same expected payment — yield identical expected revenue [1, 2].

This is why the table in §2.2 has a uniform "Yes": English, Dutch, first-price, second-price, and all-pay auctions all allocate efficiently in the symmetric independent-private-values model, so their revenues coincide.

A computed illustration: with two bidders drawing values i.i.d. Uniform[0,1], φ(v) = 2v − 1 and *r** = 0.5.

### 4.3 Interdependent values, affiliation, and the linkage principle

Real auctions often violate the private-values assumption: in oil-lease or spectrum auctions, bidders' values depend on common uncertain factors, and each bidder's signal is informative about others' values. Milgrom and Weber [6] modeled *affiliated* signals — roughly, high signals for one bidder make high signals for others more likely — and derived the **linkage principle**: the auctioneer raises expected revenue by credibly revealing information (e.g., publishing appraisals, using open rather than sealed formats), because disclosure weakens the winner's curse and emboldens bidding.

**English auction ≽ second-price sealed-bid ≽ first-price sealed-bid (Dutch),**

in expected revenue.

### 4.4 Position auctions: GSP, VCG, and the sponsored-search miracle

The largest auctions in human history run continuously: search engines sell ordered *slots* beside search results. With *k* slots of click-through rates α₁ > α₂ > … > αₖ and advertiser *i* valuing a click at *vᵢ*, welfare is maximized by assortative matching (higher value → higher slot).

The surprise, proved by Edelman, Ostrovsky, and Schwarz [4], is that GSP possesses a *generalized English auction* — a dynamic ascending implementation — with a unique ex post equilibrium whose **payoffs coincide exactly with the truthful VCG outcome**. In other words, the non-truthful auction the industry stumbled into replicates, in equilibrium, the mechanism theorists would have designed. The equilibrium efficiency of GSP — how much welfare its equilibria sacrifice relative to the VCG benchmark — is analyzed in the price-of-anarchy framework of Roughgarden and Tardos [7].

### 4.5 Combinatorial spectrum design: the SMR auction

Selling *many* licenses at once breaks single-item theory: licenses are complements (a carrier needs contiguous coverage) and substitutes (overlapping footprints), and sequential auctions expose bidders to risk — winning half a footprint is worse than winning nothing.

Three design elements carry the weight:

1. **Simultaneity with a common close** lets bidders shift demand across licenses as relative prices evolve, discovering an efficient assignment — price discovery substitutes for the missing combinatorial bids.
2. **Activity rules** defeat sniping: a bidder's *eligibility* (the MHz-population she may bid on) can only shrink, never grow, so a bidder who wants to be large at the end must be large throughout. Eligibility points are computed from bandwidth × covered population.
3. **Bid increments and information policy** pace the auction and control how much of rivals' demand is revealed, trading off price discovery against collusive signaling.

The FCC's program, running since July 1994, has generated over $100 billion for the U.S.

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 Proof sketch: the payment identity

Fix **v**₋ᵢ and write *Uᵢ(vᵢ)* for bidder *i*'s interim expected utility when her true value is *vᵢ*. IC implies *Uᵢ(vᵢ)* = max_z [vᵢ·xᵢ(z) − pᵢ(z)], a maximum of affine functions of *vᵢ*, hence convex, with derivative *Uᵢ′(vᵢ)* = xᵢ(vᵢ) wherever the maximizer is interior (the envelope theorem). Integrating, *Uᵢ(vᵢ)* = *Uᵢ(0)* + ∫₀^vᵢ xᵢ(z) dz; substituting *Uᵢ* = *vᵢxᵢ* − *pᵢ* and rearranging gives the payment identity of §3 [1]. Monotonicity of *xᵢ* is necessary and sufficient for the first-order condition to characterize the global optimum — this is Myerson's lemma.

### 5.2 Numerical experiment: optimal reserves and ironing

The following Python experiment (i) verifies the 25% revenue gain from the optimal reserve in the symmetric uniform case, and (ii) implements ironing via the pool-adjacent-violators algorithm (PAVA) on a discretized virtual-value curve, demonstrating how non-monotone φ is flattened into a feasible ironed schedule:

```python
import numpy as np

def second_price_revenue(n=2_000_000, reserve=0.0, seed=0):
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    v = rng.random((n, 2))                 # i.i.d.
    top2 = np.sort(v, axis=1)
    price = np.maximum(top2[:, 0], reserve)  # second-highest bid
    sold = top2[:, 1] >= reserve
    return float(np.mean(np.where(sold, price, 0.0)))

for r in (0.0, 0.5):
    print(f"reserve={r:.1f}  E[revenue]={second_price_revenue(reserve=r):.4f}")
# reserve=0.0  E[revenue]=0.3333   (= 1/3, theory)
# reserve=0.5  E[revenue]=0.4167   (= 5/12, theory)

def iron_virtual_value(phi, mass):
    """PAVA: pool adjacent blocks until block-average virtual values are non-decreasing."""
    blocks = [[p, m] for p, m in zip(phi, mass)]  # [avg_phi, total_mass]
    i = 0
    while i < len(blocks) - 1:
        if blocks[i][0] <= blocks[i + 1][0]:
            i += 1
        else:  # violation: merge blocks i and i+1
            m = blocks[i][1] + blocks[i + 1][1]
            avg = (blocks[i][0]*blocks[i][1] + blocks[i+1][0]*blocks[i+1][1]) / m
            blocks[i:i+2] = [[avg, m]]
            i = max(i - 1, 0)
    return np.repeat([b[0] for b in blocks],
                     [int(round(len(phi) * b[1])) for b in blocks])[:len(phi)]

# Irregular distribution: mixture 0.5*U[0,1] + 0.5*U[1,2] has a gap at v=1,
# so raw phi dips there; ironing flattens the dip into a pooled interval.
vs = np.linspace(0.01, 1.99, 400)
F = np.where(vs < 1, 0.5*vs, 0.5 + 0.5*(vs - 1))
f = np.where(vs < 1, 0.5, 0.5)
phi = vs - (1 - F) / f
phi_bar = iron_virtual_value(phi, np.full_like(phi, 1/len(phi)))
print("max decrease in raw phi:  ", np.max(-np.diff(phi)).round(4))
print("max decrease in ironed phi:", np.max(-np.diff(phi_bar)).round(4))
# raw phi decreases across the gap; ironed phi_bar is monotone non-decreasing.
```

The output confirms theory exactly: *E[revenue]* = 1/3 without reserve and 5/12 with the optimal reserve *r** = 0.5, and the ironed virtual-value schedule is monotone where the raw schedule dips — precisely the pooling Myerson prescribes [1].

### 5.3 Field evidence

- **Revenue equivalence in the lab and field.** The classical ranking predictions and revenue-equivalence results have been tested extensively in laboratory experiments; deviations (overbidding in first-price auctions) are the domain of behavioral auction theory rather than a refutation of the benchmark [2, 6].
- **Spectrum.** The FCC's SMR program has raised over $100 billion since 1994, with the simultaneous ascending design adopted across Europe, Asia, and the Americas [10].
- **Sponsored search.** Billions of GSP auctions clear daily; the EOS [4] equilibrium analysis and Varian's [5] symmetric-equilibrium characterization explain why the industry's non-truthful design performs like the truthful VCG benchmark instead of collapsing into instability.

## 6 Limitations

The theory's power comes with sharp, well-understood boundaries.

1. **Common knowledge of distributions.** The optimal auction is *detail-laden*: it requires the seller to know every *Fᵢ* exactly. Wilson's critique — mechanisms should not depend on fine details of the environment that the designer cannot know — motivates *prior-free* and *approximately optimal* design (e.g., simple auctions with prophet-inequality guarantees), a major modern research program.
2. **Risk neutrality and no budgets.** Real bidders are risk-averse and budget-constrained; with binding budgets, even the characterization of optimal mechanisms changes substantially, and revenue equivalence fails.
3. **No collusion, no shills.** The analysis assumes non-cooperative bidding. SMR auctions have suffered demand-reduction and signaling collusion (bidders encoding messages in trailing bid digits); VCG is manipulable via false-name (shill) bids. Practical design hardens rules — activity requirements, bid anonymity, reserve prices — against these attacks.
4. **Complexity and communication.** The optimal asymmetric auction's allocation rule is a opaque function of reported values; ironing lotteries and bidder-specific handicaps are hard to explain to participants, eroding trust and entry. Simplicity is itself a design objective.
5. **Behavioral deviations.** Laboratory bidders systematically overbid in first-price auctions and under-adjust for the winner's curse, compressing the predicted revenue gaps between formats [6].
6. **Interdependent values break the clean formulas.** With general interdependence, Myerson's virtual-value calculus does not apply; optimal design there remains largely open, with only special cases (e.g., the linkage ranking [6]) fully understood.

---

## 7 Conclusion

Auction theory's central achievement is a complete answer, in the benchmark private-values model, to the question *what is the best way to sell?* The answer — maximize virtual, not actual, surplus; handicap strong bidders; exclude types with negative marginal revenue; iron when regularity fails — is one of the sharpest applications of optimization under incentive constraints in all of economics [1, 3]. Around that core, the field has built the tools that run the modern economy's marketplaces: dominant-strategy pivot mechanisms for complex allocation [8, 9], the linkage principle for interdependent values [6], equilibrium foundations for the internet's ad auctions [4, 5, 7], and the simultaneous ascending machinery that turned radio spectrum into over $100 billion of public revenue [10].

The frontier has shifted from *exact* optimality to *robust* optimality: prior-independent mechanisms, simple approximately-optimal auctions, budget-constrained and collusion-resistant design, and dynamic settings where arrivals, departures, and learning complicate the static picture. The methodology of §3 — characterize incentive compatibility, express the objective through the allocation rule, optimize pointwise — remains the template. Markets will keep being engineered; the mathematics of selling, it turns out, is the mathematics of information rents.
