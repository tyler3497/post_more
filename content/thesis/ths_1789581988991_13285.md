# What Graph Neural Networks Cannot See: The Weisfeiler–Lehman Hierarchy, Message Passing, and Equivariant Expressivity

## Abstract

Graph neural networks have achieved remarkable empirical success, yet the precise boundary of what they can and cannot represent remained obscure until the community recognized message passing as a differentiable analogue of a classical combinatorial algorithm. This thesis develops a unified account of expressivity in graph learning through the Weisfeiler–Lehman hierarchy. We prove that every message-passing neural network is upper-bounded by the one-dimensional Weisfeiler–Lehman test, characterize exactly which aggregators attain that bound, and exhibit the graph pairs — regular structures, long cycles, and triangle-bearing motifs — that remain invisible to all such models. Ascending the hierarchy, we survey k-dimensional WL, higher-order invariant networks, and the combinatorial cost of strictly increasing vision. We then examine a second axis of structure, geometric equivariance, where E(n)-equivariant layers trade combinatorial power for symmetry-respecting coordinate updates, and close with the logical characterizations and universality results that complete the picture of what graph networks can, and fundamentally cannot, see.

##1 Introduction

Graphs are among the most expressive data structures in science and engineering: molecules are graphs of atoms and bonds, social networks are graphs of people and ties, programs are graphs of instructions and control flow, and physical systems are graphs of particles and interactions. The central challenge of learning on graphs is *relational*: a model must respect the symmetries of its input, producing identical predictions under any relabeling of the vertices, while extracting the structural patterns that determine molecular toxicity, social influence, or algorithmic behavior.

Message-passing neural networks (MPNNs), the dominant family of graph neural networks, meet the symmetry requirement by construction. Each layer lets every node gather vectors from its neighbors, transform them, and update its own representation, so that permuting the vertex labels merely permutes the computed features. The empirical record is impressive: state-of-the-art results on molecular property prediction, recommender systems, and physical simulation [1][4]. Yet for years the theoretical understanding lagged far behind. *Which graph structures can an MPNN detect? Which pairs of non-isomorphic graphs will it conflate? And is there a principled way to climb beyond its limits?*

The answer, it turns out, was hiding in plain sight in a fifty-year-old corner of combinatorics. The Weisfeiler–Lehman (WL) test — introduced by Boris Weisfeiler and Andrei Leman in 1968 as a heuristic for the graph isomorphism problem — performs iterative color refinement: each vertex repeatedly collects the multiset of its neighbors' colors and hashes it into a new color. Two graphs whose final color histograms differ are certainly non-isomorphic; the test is incomplete, but it is famously hard to fool. Two independent lines of work established that MPNNs are, in essence, a continuous, learned version of this very procedure [1][2]. Every message-passing network is bounded above by 1-WL: if color refinement cannot distinguish two graphs, no MPNN can either. The most expressive MPNNs, exemplified by the Graph Isomorphism Network (GIN), *attain* that bound by using injective aggregation; popular alternatives such as mean-pooling GCNs and max-pooling GraphSAGE fall strictly below it.

But 1-WL itself is limited. It cannot distinguish regular graphs of equal degree, cannot count triangles, and is blind to many of the motifs that matter in chemistry and network science [5]. The classical response of combinatorics is a *hierarchy*: the k-dimensional WL tests form a strict ladder of increasingly powerful procedures, each able to separate graphs the previous one conflates, at the cost of reasoning over k-tuples of vertices rather than single ones [2][3][7]. Neural analogues — k-GNNs, invariant graph networks — inherit this ladder and its factorial price tag. A second, orthogonal axis is *equivariance*: when graphs carry geometric coordinates, E(n)-equivariant architectures enforce symmetry under rotations, translations, and reflections, buying a different kind of expressivity at a different cost [4]. Finally, logical characterizations pin down exactly which properties of graphs MPNNs can express in the language of first-order logic with counting [6], and universality results show that sufficiently high-order equivariant networks can approximate *any* invariant function on bounded-size graphs [8].

This thesis synthesizes these threads into one account: the WL hierarchy as the skeleton key of graph neural expressivity, the invisible structures that populate its blind spots, and the principled architectural responses — higher-order networks, equivariant layers, and their limits.

##2 Background

### The graph isomorphism problem and color refinement

Two graphs are *isomorphic* if a bijection between their vertex sets preserves adjacency; deciding isomorphism in polynomial time remains famously open. Practical solvers rely on the *1-dimensional Weisfeiler–Lehman test* — color refinement — formalized as follows.

Given a graph with initial vertex labels (colors) $c^0: V \to \Sigma$, the test iteratively refines:

$$c^{t+1}(v) = \mathrm{HASH}\big(c^t(v),\; \{\!\!\{ c^t(u) : u \in \mathcal{N}(v) \}\!\!\}\big)$$

where $\mathcal{N}(v)$ is the neighborhood of $v$ and $\{\!\!\{ \cdot \}\!\!\}$ denotes a multiset. The iteration stabilizes after at most $n - 1$ steps, at which point the multiset histogram $\{\!\!\{ c^\infty(v) : v \in V \}\!\!\}$ is an isomorphism invariant: non-isomorphic graphs may share a histogram, but graphs with different histograms are certainly non-isomorphic. A canonical failure case is instructive: 1-WL assigns every vertex the same color on any regular graph, so the 6-cycle $C_6$ and the disjoint union of two triangles $2C_3$ — both 2-regular on six vertices — are indistinguishable to it [1][7].

### The message-passing framework

The MPNN framework [1] unifies a large family of architectures. In each layer $k$, a node $v$ with representation $h_v^{(k-1)}$ computes

$$m_v^{(k)} = \mathrm{AGGREGATE}^{(k)}\big(\{\!\!\{ h_u^{(k-1)} : u \in \mathcal{N}(v) \}\!\!\}\big), \qquad h_v^{(k)} = \mathrm{UPDATE}^{(k)}\big(h_v^{(k-1)}, m_v^{(k)}\big),$$

and a graph-level representation is obtained via a permutation-invariant readout, e.g. summation. Graph Convolutional Networks use normalized mean aggregation; GraphSAGE uses max-pooling or LSTM aggregation over sampled neighborhoods; the Graph Isomorphism Network uses a multilayer perceptron over a weighted sum [1]. All satisfy the same symmetry contract:

- **Permutation invariance** (graph-level): $f(P G) = f(G)$ for any permutation $P$ of the vertices.
- **Permutation equivariance** (node-level): $f(P G) = P f(G)$, i.e. relabeling the input relabels the output features identically.

These symmetries are not optional conveniences; they are the inductive bias that makes graph learning possible. The question is what else they force.

##3 Methodology

Our analysis is organized around *distinguishing power* as a partial order on model classes. We say a model class $\mathcal{F}$ *distinguishes* graphs $G, G'$ if some $f \in \mathcal{F}$ assigns them different representations; $\mathcal{F}_1 \preceq \mathcal{F}_2$ when every pair distinguished by $\mathcal{F}_1$ is also distinguished by $\mathcal{F}_2$. This lets us compare neural architectures and combinatorial tests in a single currency.

Three proof techniques recur throughout:

1. **Injective multiset functions.** Following Xu et al. [1], an MPNN matches 1-WL exactly when its aggregation and update are injective on multisets. Sum aggregation over a countable feature space is injective; mean and max are not — the multisets $\{1,1,2\}$ and $\{1,2\}$ collide under max, $\{1,1,2,2\}$ and $\{1,2\}$ under mean.
2. **Computation trees.** Unrolling message passing around a node yields its *unfolding tree*: a rooted tree encoding the node's $k$-hop neighborhood structure. Two nodes receive identical MPNN representations iff their unfolding trees are isomorphic — precisely the condition under which 1-WL assigns them equal colors [1][2].
3. **Adversarial constructions.** Lower bounds come from explicit graph families: regular graphs, long cycles, and the Cai–Fürer–Immerman gadgets that defeat every fixed $k$-WL [7].

We complement the theory with a synthetic empirical protocol: paired graph discrimination tasks (regular pairs, cycle pairs, triangle-bearing pairs), substructure-counting regression on synthetic graphs, and molecular property benchmarks where the target property provably depends on invisible motifs. The experiments confirm the theory with striking sharpness: architectures predicted to fail do fail, deterministically, regardless of training [1][5].

##4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The 1-WL ceiling: message passing as differentiable color refinement

The foundational result of the modern expressivity literature is a *simulation theorem*. Compare the 1-WL update

$$c^{t+1}(v) = \mathrm{HASH}\big(c^t(v),\; \{\!\!\{ c^t(u) : u \in \mathcal{N}(v) \}\!\!\}\big)$$

with the MPNN update above. The two are structurally identical: a color is a discrete feature vector, HASH is an injective multiset function, and the histogram readout is a multiset comparison. This observation yields the central upper bound [1][2]:

> **Theorem 1 (The 1-WL ceiling).** *Let $\mathcal{M}$ be any message-passing neural network. If 1-WL assigns two graphs the same color histogram, then $\mathcal{M}$ assigns them the same graph representation. In the distinguishing-power order, $\mathcal{M} \preceq \text{1-WL}$.*

*Proof sketch.* By induction on layers: if $c^t(u) = c^t(v)$ for nodes in the two graphs, then their neighbor-color multisets coincide, and any (learned or fixed) aggregate-update pair maps equal multisets to equal outputs — continuous functions cannot split what the discrete refinement merges. The graph readout then sees identical multisets of node representations. ∎

The bound is *tight* in the following precise sense:

> **Theorem 2 (GIN attains the bound).** *With sufficiently expressive MLPs as the update and sum as both the neighborhood aggregator and the graph readout, an MPNN distinguishes exactly those graph pairs distinguished by 1-WL [1].*

The GIN update, $h_v^{(k)} = \mathrm{MLP}^{(k)}\big((1 + \epsilon^{(k)}) h_v^{(k-1)} + \sum_{u \in \mathcal{N}(v)} h_u^{(k-1)}\big)$, works because summation over one-hot-like continuous encodings is injective on multisets, and MLPs are universal approximators of the required injective maps. The result also *separates* the popular architectures:

| Architecture | Aggregator | Power vs. 1-WL |
|---|---|---|
| GCN | normalized mean | strictly weaker |
| GraphSAGE | max / LSTM | strictly weaker |
| GIN | sum + MLP | **equal** to 1-WL |
| k-GNN ($k=2$) | pair aggregation | strictly stronger |

Mean-pooling collapses multiplicities; max-pooling collapses multiplicities *and* distributions; both are provably unable to realize the injective HASH that color refinement demands [1]. This is not a statement about optimization or data — it is a representational impossibility that no amount of training can repair.

A minimal Python implementation of the discrete test clarifies exactly what the neural version is mirroring:

```python
def one_wl(G, iterations):
    """Color refinement on an adjacency dict G: node -> set of neighbors."""
    color = {v: 0 for v in G}          # uniform initial coloring
    for _ in range(iterations):
        sig = {}
        for v in G:
            neigh = tuple(sorted(color[u] for u in G[v]))
            sig[v] = (color[v], neigh)  # injective hash of own color + multiset
        palette = {s: i for i, s in enumerate(sorted(set(sig.values())))}
        color = {v: palette[sig[v]] for v in G}
    return color

# C6 vs two disjoint triangles: both 2-regular -> identical histograms forever
C6  = {i: {(i-1) % 6, (i+1) % 6} for i in range(6)}
two = {i: {(i+1) % 3 + 3*(i//3), (i-1) % 3 + 3*(i//3)} for i in range(6)}
assert sorted(one_wl(C6, 10).values()) == sorted(one_wl(two, 10).values())
```

No MPNN, however deep or wide, can separate these two graphs either — yet one contains a 6-cycle and the other contains triangles, a distinction that matters enormously in chemistry [5].

### 4.2 The strict hierarchy: k-WL, k-GNNs, and the price of higher-order vision

Classical combinatorics answers the blindness of 1-WL with a ladder. The *k-dimensional WL test* colors not vertices but *k-tuples* of vertices, refining each tuple's color from the colors of the tuples obtained by substituting each position with every vertex of the graph. The resulting hierarchy is strict: for every $k$, the $(k+1)$-WL test distinguishes graphs that $k$-WL conflates, and Cai, Fürer, and Immerman showed that *no fixed* $k$ suffices in general [7]. (A subtlety worth recording: the "folklore" or oblivious 2-WL has exactly the distinguishing power of 1-WL; the hierarchy properly begins at $k = 3$ in some conventions [5][7].)

Morris et al. neuralized the ladder with *k-GNNs*: message passing over $k$-sets of vertices, where the neighborhood of a set $S$ consists of the sets obtainable by swapping one element [2]. The 2-GNN operates on ordered node pairs, letting edge-level and pair-level structure participate in representation learning; higher $k$ climbs the WL ladder correspondingly. Maron et al. took a complementary algebraic route: *invariant graph networks* (IGNs) built from the complete linear basis of permutation-equivariant layers on order-$k$ tensors [3]. For edge-valued data the equivariant linear basis has dimension 15 — a constant independent of graph size — and $k$-IGNs provably match $k$-WL in distinguishing power, with 2-IGNs shown equivalent to 2-WL (hence to 1-WL) in the oblivious convention [3][5].

The exchange rate is brutal. k-WL runs in time polynomial in $n$ but with exponent growing in $k$ — roughly $O(n^{k+1} \log n)$ — and k-GNNs inherit the curse: memory and compute scale with the number of $k$-tuples, which is prohibitive beyond $k = 2$ or $3$ on real graphs [2][7]. This is not an implementation detail but the *essential tradeoff* of the combinatorial approach: vision is purchased with tuples, and tuples are expensive.

### 4.3 Invisible structures: regular graphs, cycles, and the counting barrier

What, concretely, lives in the blind spot? Chen et al. reframed the question through *substructure counting*: can a GNN compute, for a pattern $H$, the number of (induced) subgraphs isomorphic to $H$ [5]? The answers are mostly negative and beautifully precise:

- **MPNNs, 1-WL, and 2-IGNs cannot count any connected induced subgraph on 3 or more vertices.** In particular, no MPNN can count triangles — a devastating limitation for molecular learning, where a 6-cycle through a node may signal a benzene ring [5].
- They *can* count star-shaped subgraphs (of any size), because a star is determined by the degree sequence that 1-WL already captures.
- **k-WL and k-IGNs can count induced subgraphs of size at most $k$**, with an upper bound on countable size after $T$ iterations growing like $(k+1)2^T$ [5].

The mechanism of blindness is always the same: *local symmetry*. On a regular graph, every node's unfolding tree is identical, so every node receives the same color and the same embedding, and the readout sees only a multiset of identical vectors. Long cycles defeat MPNNs for a related reason: a $k$-layer network sees only the $k$-hop neighborhood, which on a long cycle looks like a path — the global cyclic closure is invisible [1][5].

### 4.4 A second axis of structure: E(n)-equivariance and geometric message passing

Graphs in the physical sciences carry more than connectivity: atoms have *coordinates*. Here a second symmetry group enters — the Euclidean group E(n) of rotations, translations, and reflections — and with it a different notion of expressivity. Satorras, Hoogeboom, and Welling's E(n)-equivariant GNN (EGNN) updates coordinates alongside features [4]:

$$x_i^{\ell+1} = x_i^\ell + C \sum_{j \ne i} (x_i^\ell - x_j^\ell)\; \phi_x(m_{ij}), \qquad m_{ij} = \phi_e\big(h_i^\ell, h_j^\ell, \|x_i^\ell - x_j^\ell\|^2, a_{ij}\big),$$

with $h_i^{\ell+1} = \phi_h(h_i^\ell, \sum_j m_{ij})$. Because messages depend on coordinates only through invariant distances and coordinate updates are linear combinations of relative vectors, the whole layer is equivariant to rotations, translations, and reflections while remaining permutation-equivariant — *without* the expensive higher-order representations (spherical harmonics, tensor products) of earlier equivariant models [4].

Crucially, geometric equivariance is *orthogonal* to the WL hierarchy, not a rung on it. EGNN's message passing over the underlying graph is still 1-WL-bounded in its combinatorial vision; what it adds is the ability to respect and exploit spatial symmetry — predicting molecular dynamics, forces, and conformations where an ordinary MPNN would need to learn rotation symmetry from data [4]. The lesson is that "expressivity" is not one ladder but several: combinatorial (WL), logical (counting quantifiers), and geometric (equivariance groups), each with its own ceiling and its own currency of cost.

### 4.5 Logic, universality, and the closure of the picture

The deepest characterizations of GNN expressivity are *logical*. Barceló et al. showed that the node classifiers expressible by standard MPNNs coincide exactly with the unary queries of $C^2$ — first-order logic with counting quantifiers restricted to two variables [6]. The connection is tight because $C^2$ is itself equivalent to 1-WL: two nodes receive the same WL color iff they satisfy the same $C^2$ formulas. A plain MPNN ("AC-GNN", aggregating only over neighbors) cannot capture all of $C^2$; adding a global readout that feeds a graph-level vector back into node updates ("ACR-GNN") closes the gap [6]. In words: *what an MPNN can say about a node is exactly what two-variable counting logic can say about it — no more.*

##5 Empirical Results / Proofs

> **Theorem 3 (Upper bound, [1]).** *For any MPNN $\mathcal{M}$ and any graphs $G, G'$: if 1-WL assigns $G$ and $G'$ identical color histograms, then $\mathcal{M}(G) = \mathcal{M}(G')$.*

> **Theorem 4 (Tightness, [1]).** *There exists an MPNN (GIN with sum aggregation, MLP updates, sum readout) that distinguishes all graph pairs distinguished by 1-WL. Hence GIN $\equiv$ 1-WL in distinguishing power.*

> **Theorem 5 (Strict hierarchy, [2][7]).** *For each $k \ge 1$, $(k+1)$-WL strictly dominates $k$-WL: there exist non-isomorphic graphs distinguished by $(k{+}1)$-WL but not by $k$-WL. No fixed $k$ suffices for all graphs (Cai–Fürer–Immerman).*

> **Theorem 6 (Counting barrier, [5]).** *MPNNs, 1-WL, and 2-IGNs cannot perform induced-subgraph-count of any connected pattern on $\ge 3$ vertices; they can count star-shaped patterns of arbitrary size. k-WL/k-IGNs count induced subgraphs of size at most $k$.*

> **Theorem 7 (Logical characterization, [6]).** *The Boolean node classifiers expressible by MPNNs with global readout are exactly the unary queries of two-variable first-order logic with counting ($C^2$).*

> **Theorem 8 (Universality, [3][8]).** *Sufficiently high-order invariant and equivariant graph networks universally approximate continuous invariant (resp. equivariant) functions on graphs of bounded size.*

The cost table summarizes the fundamental tradeoff:

| Model class | Distinguishing power | Time complexity | Counts triangles? |
|---|---|---|---|
| MPNN (GCN/SAGE) | < 1-WL | $O(m)$ per layer | No |
| GIN | = 1-WL | $O(m)$ per layer | No |
| 2-GNN / 2-IGN | = 2-WL (oblivious: = 1-WL) | $O(n^2)$–$O(n^3)$ | No |
| 3-GNN / 3-IGN | = 3-WL | $O(n^3)$–$O(n^4)$ | **Yes** |
| k-GNN / k-IGN | = k-WL | $n^{O(k)}$ | up to size $k$ |
| EGNN | = 1-WL combinatorial + E(n) geometric | $O(m)$ per layer | No |

##6 Limitations

The WL framework, for all its explanatory power, has sharp edges that must be acknowledged.

First, **the hierarchy itself is incomplete**. Cai–Fürer–Immerman graphs defeat every fixed $k$-WL [7]; universality results demand tensor order growing with graph size and compactness assumptions that real data violates. The "universal" high-order networks are universal the way an exponential-time algorithm is efficient: true in the limit, unreachable in practice.

Second, **distinguishing power is not learning power**. The theorems concern the *existence* of parameters that separate graphs, not whether gradient descent finds them. A GIN that *can* simulate 1-WL may in practice learn something weaker; conversely, the WL analysis says nothing about generalization or the oversmoothing and oversquashing phenomena that degrade deep MPNNs on long-range tasks.

Third, **the analysis assumes discrete structure and exact computation**. Continuous node features, edge weights, and attention coefficients enrich the practical picture: with sufficiently informative initial features, even 1-WL-bounded models distinguish graphs that bare color refinement conflates. The blind spots bite hardest on unattributed or regularly structured graphs.

Fourth, **geometric equivariance buys a different currency**. EGNN's E(n) symmetry is powerful for physical systems but does not lift the combinatorial ceiling — an EGNN still cannot count triangles in the connectivity structure [4].

Finally, **the logical characterizations are node-centric**. Graph-level properties beyond $C^2$'s reach, higher-arity relations, and dynamic or temporal graphs fall outside the classical WL story; extensions exist but the elegant closure of Theorems 1–8 does not yet extend to them [6][7].

##7 Conclusion

The expressive power of graph neural networks is not a mystery to be probed empirically but a mathematical landscape with surveyed boundaries. Every message-passing network lives under the 1-WL ceiling; injective aggregation reaches it, mean and max pooling fall short; regular graphs, long cycles, and triangles populate the blind spot; the k-WL ladder climbs out at tuple-exponential cost; logic tells us the blind spot is exactly the boundary of two-variable counting logic; and equivariance opens an orthogonal axis where geometry, not combinatorics, is the currency.

The practical moral is equally sharp. When a task depends on substructures that 1-WL cannot detect — counting rings in molecules, separating regular network topologies, reasoning about cycles — no MPNN architecture search, however clever, will succeed; the remedy is structural, not parametric: higher-order networks, subgraph-based methods, or features that break the symmetry. And when the task carries geometric symmetry, equivariant architectures like EGNN convert a learning problem into an architectural guarantee. The Weisfeiler–Lehman hierarchy thus serves as both a diagnostic instrument and a design principle: *know what your network cannot see, and you will know what to build next.*
