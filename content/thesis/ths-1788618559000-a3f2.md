# Anatomy of the Real-Time Web: ICE, DTLS-SRTP, Adaptive Congestion Control, and Deployment Topologies in WebRTC Media Systems

## Abstract

WebRTC is the dominant framework for interactive audio, video, and data communication on the open web, yet its simple API conceals a densely layered protocol stack drawn from more than two dozen IETF specifications. This thesis analyzes WebRTC real-time media internals across the full session life cycle: session description exchange on the signaling plane, NAT traversal and path selection via Interactive Connectivity Establishment (ICE), authentication and media keying through DTLS-SRTP, RTP/RTCP transport, and adaptive bandwidth estimation with Google Congestion Control (GCC) and its transport-wide feedback successors. We compare full-mesh, Selective Forwarding Unit (SFU), and Multipoint Control Unit (MCU) topologies on bandwidth scaling, server cost, and latency, and survey reliability machinery — jitter buffers, NACK/RTX, and forward error correction. Grounded in RFC 8445, RFC 8835, RFC 8827, and the RMCAT drafts, we synthesize experimental findings and enumerate open limitations: TURN-TCP head-of-line blocking, delay-based control on wireless paths, and the middlebox consequences of mandatory encryption.

---

## 1 Introduction

WebRTC (Web Real-Time Communication) standardized the browser as a peer in a real-time multimedia network. Before its arrival, browser-based voice and video required proprietary plug-ins whose network protocols were opaque; WebRTC replaced them with an open protocol stack whose wire behavior is governed by IETF RFCs and W3C APIs. The framework deliberately *leaves signaling unspecified*: applications negotiate sessions with SDP (RFC 8866) over any transport — typically WebSocket — while the media plane is rigorously standardized [1].

This separation is the first of several design decisions this thesis examines. The second is the insistence on **secure-by-default media**: all WebRTC media is encrypted with SRTP, with keys derived from a DTLS handshake authenticated by fingerprints exchanged in the (application-secured) signaling channel [2]. The third is **congestion-aware self-regulation**: RFC 8835 mandates congestion control and a circuit breaker for every WebRTC media flow, because an unthrottled UDP flow can starve competing TCP traffic and collapse shared access links [1]. The fourth is **adaptive deployment**: depending on scale and cost, the same media stack operates peer-to-peer (mesh), through a selective forwarder, or through a mixing bridge, each with distinct scaling laws.

The remainder of this thesis is organized as follows. Section 2 surveys the protocol background: the signaling/media plane split, RTP and RTCP, and SRTP. Section 3 describes the methodology of this analysis. Section 4 performs a deep dive into four pillars: ICE path establishment; DTLS-SRTP keying; congestion-control evolution from GCC through transport-wide feedback to BBR adaptations; and the SFU/MCU/mesh topology trichotomy, together with the libwebrtc implementation substrate and the reliability machinery (jitter buffer, NACK/RTX, FEC) that sits above it. Section 5 summarizes empirical results from the literature. Section 6 states the limitations of the current architecture, and Section 7 concludes.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Signaling versus media plane

WebRTC specifies no signaling protocol. A *signaling channel* carries session descriptions (SDP offers/answers), ICE candidates, and auxiliary control; the media plane carries RTP/RTCP packets end-to-end between agents. This split decouples *who decides* (application policy) from *what runs on the wire* (standardized media transport). A consequence is that middleboxes and gateways must terminate two independent contexts: they cannot simply copy SDP addresses to achieve interworking, because the media leg's security and transport state is orthogonal to the signaling leg [1].

> Theorem: *Signaling security and media security are independent concerns in WebRTC.* A compromised or observable signaling channel does not directly yield media plaintext, because media keys are derived inside an authenticated DTLS handshake whose fingerprints are bound to the signaling exchange, not carried by it in decryptable form [2].

### 2.2 RTP and RTCP

Media is carried by the Real-time Transport Protocol (RTP), with the RTP Control Protocol (RTCP) providing reception statistics, sender reports, and feedback messages [1]. WebRTC uses the AVPF profile (RFC 4585) extended for reduced-size RTCP (RFC 5506) and RTP/RTCP multiplexing on a single port (RFC 5761), which is essential once ICE nominates one transport tuple per component. RFC 8834 constrains the RTP profile for WebRTC: it mandates a circuit breaker (RFC 8083) and congestion control [1].

### 2.3 SRTP and keying options

SRTP (RFC 3711) provides confidentiality, message authentication, and replay protection for RTP/RTCP. WebRTC mandates key exchange via **DTLS-SRTP** (RFC 8827): the endpoints perform a DTLS handshake over the ICE-selected path, authenticate each other's self-signed certificates against fingerprints exchanged in SDP (`a=fingerprint`), and export keying material for SRTP [2]. Alternative keying schemes (SDES, MIKEY) are explicitly excluded from the browser profile.

## 3 Methodology

This thesis is a *synthetic analytical study*: we reconstruct the WebRTC media pipeline from primary normative sources — the ICE specification (RFC 8445), the WebRTC transports specification (RFC 8835), the WebRTC security architecture (RFC 8827), and the RMCAT congestion-control drafts (GCC, transport-wide congestion-control extensions) — cross-checked against secondary technical analyses and open-source implementation notes (libwebrtc, and independent RFC-compliance engineering documents) [3][4]. We adopt a consistent notation:

- *t(i)* — arrival time of packet group *i* at the receiver;
- *T(i)* — departure timestamp of packet group *i* at the sender;
- *d(i) = t(i) − t(i−1) − (T(i) − T(i−1))* — inter-group delay variation [3];
- *A(t)* — estimated available bandwidth at time *t*.

Where the literature reports measurements (GCC fairness studies, SFU scaling analyses), we summarize the experimental setups and headline quantitative findings. No new experiments are conducted; instead, the contribution is architectural synthesis: showing how the four pillars interact at runtime and where their assumptions conflict.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 ICE: candidate gathering, connectivity checks, and nomination

Interactive Connectivity Establishment (ICE, RFC 8445) solves the *rendezvous problem*: two endpoints behind NATs and firewalls must discover a mutually reachable transport address. ICE orchestrates STUN (RFC 8489) and TURN (RFC 8656) into a three-phase protocol [5][6].

**Phase 1 — Gathering.** Each agent enumerates *candidates*: `host` candidates (local interfaces), `srflx` (server-reflexive, the public mapping learned from a STUN Binding request), and `relay` (allocated on a TURN server). Candidates carry a priority computed as:

```
priority = (2^24) × type_preference + (2^8) × local_preference + (256 − component_id)
```

with type preferences `host=126`, `prflx=110`, `srflx=100`, `relay=0` per RFC 8445 §5.1.2.1 — encoding the economic preference for direct, cheap paths [6].

**Phase 2 — Connectivity checks.** Agents exchange candidates over the signaling channel (trickle ICE streams them incrementally), form all local×remote *candidate pairs*, and sort a checklist by pair priority. Each check is a STUN Binding request authenticated with the peer's `ice-ufrag`/`ice-pwd`; the response's XOR-MAPPED-ADDRESS yields peer-reflexive candidates. Pairs transition through a state machine: *Frozen → Waiting → In-Progress → Succeeded/Failed*, with *triggered checks* accelerating convergence [5].

**Phase 3 — Nomination.** The controlling agent (by SDP role, or the offerer) nominates the highest-priority valid pair with a USE-CANDIDATE-flagged check; the controlled agent agrees, and the nominated pair becomes the media path. RFC 8445 deprecated aggressive nomination, so WebRTC always uses *regular nomination* [5].

```javascript
// Minimal trickle-ICE sketch (browser side)
const pc = new RTCPeerConnection({ iceServers: [{ urls: 'stun:stun.l.google.com:19302' },
                                                { urls: 'turn:turn.example.com', username: 'u', credential: 'p' }] });
pc.onicecandidate = (e) => { if (e.candidate) signal.send({ candidate: e.candidate }); };
await pc.setRemoteDescription(offer);
const answer = await pc.createAnswer();
await pc.setLocalDescription(answer);
signal.send({ sdp: pc.localDescription });
```

A subtle interaction noted in RFC 8835 §6: DTLS-SRTP and ICE share fate — all candidate pairs of one component belong to the *same* DTLS association, so there is exactly one DTLS handshake even when several pairs are valid, and a later ICE restart re-runs checks without necessarily re-running DTLS [1].

| Candidate type | Discovery mechanism | Type preference | Cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| host | local interface enumeration | 126 | cheapest |
| prflx | connectivity checks | 110 | cheap |
| srflx | STUN Binding to STUN server | 100 | moderate |
| relay | TURN Allocate | 0 | expensive (server bandwidth) |

### 4.2 DTLS handshake and SRTP keying (DTLS-SRTP)

Once ICE nominates a path, media security is bootstrapped by DTLS-SRTP (RFC 5764, WebRTC profile in RFC 8827) [1][2]. The handshake runs *over the ICE transport* (multiplexed with RTP/RTCP per RFC 7983 demultiplexing rules), and proceeds as:

1. **ClientHello / ServerHello** — cipher suite negotiation; the DTLS client role follows the SDP `setup` attribute.
2. **Certificate exchange** — endpoints present self-signed certificates (no PKI required).
3. **Fingerprint verification** — each endpoint compares the received certificate's hash against the `a=fingerprint` value carried in the SDP offer/answer. This binds the signaling channel's integrity to the media channel's authenticity: an attacker who cannot alter signaling cannot substitute certificates [2].
4. **Finished / key export** — the DTLS session completes and both sides invoke the TLS exporter (RFC 5705) with a WebRTC-specific label to derive the SRTP master key and salt; the exported keys never traverse the network [1].

The resulting SRTP context typically uses AES-GCM (AEAD) or AES-CM with HMAC-SHA1, protecting RTP payloads and providing RTCP authentication. Because keying material is *endpoint-derived*, a TURN relay — which forwards encrypted packets — cannot inspect or modify media, a deliberate privacy property [2].

> Theorem: *DTLS-SRTP provides peer authentication without a trusted third party.* Security reduces to the integrity of the signaling channel for fingerprints plus the strength of the DTLS handshake; no certificate authority is in the trust path [2].

### 4.3 Congestion control: GCC, SCReAM, transport-wide feedback, and BBR

WebRTC flows must not collapse the network: RFC 8835 mandates congestion control and the RFC 8083 circuit breaker [1]. The dominant deployed algorithm is **Google Congestion Control (GCC)**, contributed to the IETF RMCAT working group [3].

**Delay-based controller.** The receiver groups arriving packets (5 ms burst windows) and computes the inter-group delay variation:

*d(i) = t(i) − t(i−1) − (T(i) − T(i−1))*

An adaptive Kalman filter estimates the queuing-delay gradient *m(tᵢ)* from noisy delay samples; an over-use detector compares *m(tᵢ)* against an adaptive threshold *γ(tᵢ)*, emitting *under-use*, *normal*, or *over-use* signals. The rate controller then adjusts the target bitrate *A(t)*: additive increase on under-use, multiplicative decrease on over-use — an AIMD-style loop tuned for interactivity rather than bulk throughput [3].

**Loss-based controller.** A second loop reacts to RTCP-reported fraction-lost *fₗ*: if *fₗ > 0.1* the rate is multiplicatively decreased; if *fₗ < 0.02* it is increased; between the thresholds the rate holds. The delay-based estimate *Aᵣ* is conveyed to the sender via **REMB** (Receiver Estimated Maximum Bitrate) RTCP messages, recommended at least once per second or immediately upon congestion detection [3].

**Transport-wide feedback.** REMB's receiver-side estimation keeps the receiver "smart." The *transport-wide congestion control* extension (draft-holmer-rmcat-transport-wide-cc) inverts this: a compact RTP header extension stamps transport-wide sequence numbers on every packet, and the receiver returns per-packet arrival feedback, moving estimation *sender-side* with a dumb receiver. This design — now the default in libwebrtc — reacts faster to queue buildup and unifies control across audio, video, and FEC streams sharing a transport [4].

**SCReAM and BBR.** The RMCAT process evaluated alternatives: **SCReAM** (Self-Clocked Rate Adaptation for Multimedia), a sender-based, LEDBAT-inspired algorithm emphasizing self-clocking and low queue occupancy, and **NADA** (unified delay/loss signals). Separately, Google's **BBR** congestion controller — a model-based algorithm estimating bottleneck bandwidth and RTT rather than reacting to loss — has been experimentally adapted to WebRTC; studies report BBR variants achieving higher link utilization with lower standing queues than GCC on emulated paths, at the cost of more complex probing dynamics and ongoing fairness questions against loss-based TCP flows.

The GCC state machine, in condensed form:

```cpp
// libwebrtc-style sender-side controller (simplified)
enum class BweState { kHold, kIncrease, kDecrease };
void OnTransportFeedback(const Feedback& fb) {
  auto trend = delay_detector_.Update(fb);   // Kalman gradient vs gamma
  switch (trend) {
    case Trend::kOverusing:
      state_ = BweState::kDecrease;
      bitrate_ = std::max(bitrate_ * 0.85, kMinBitrate); break;
    case Trend::kUnderusing:
      state_ = BweState::kIncrease;
      bitrate_ = bitrate_ * 1.05 + kAdditiveStep; break;
    default:
      state_ = BweState::kHold; break;
  }
  encoder_->SetTargetBitrate(bitrate_);
}
```

### 4.4 Topologies: mesh, SFU, MCU — and the libwebrtc substrate

The same media stack can be deployed in three canonical topologies with sharply different scaling laws.

- **Full mesh.** Each of *n* participants sends *(n−1)* streams and receives *(n−1)* streams: uplink and total system bandwidth scale as *O(n²)*. No server cost and minimal latency, but each client encodes multiple streams and uplink saturates quickly; practical only for *n ≤ 4–6*.
- **SFU (Selective Forwarding Unit).** Each client sends one stream uplink; the server *routes* (never transcodes) packets to subscribers. Per-client uplink is *O(1)*, server bandwidth *O(n²)* in the worst case but with cheap packet forwarding rather than decode/encode. Simulcast and SVC layer selection let the SFU adapt downlink per receiver without re-encoding.
- **MCU (Multipoint Control Unit).** The server decodes all inputs, *mixes/composites* them, and re-encodes one stream per participant. Per-client bandwidth is *O(1)* in both directions, but server CPU scales with *n* decoders plus *n* encoders — the dominant cost — and transcoding adds latency (typically one extra frame pipeline plus codec delay).

| Property | Mesh | SFU | MCU |
|---|---|---|---|
| Client uplink | O(n) | O(1) | O(1) |
| Server bandwidth | none | O(n²) worst-case | O(n) |
| Server CPU | none | low (forwarding) | high (transcode) |
| End-to-end latency | minimal | low | higher (decode/mix/encode) |
| Per-receiver adaptation | none | simulcast/SVC layers | trivial (own encode) |

**Jitter buffers, NACK/RTX, FEC.** Above the transport, each receiver maintains an adaptive *jitter buffer* that trades playout delay against late-packet loss, driven by delay statistics. Lost packets trigger *NACK* (RTCP negative acknowledgement); retransmitted packets travel on a separate *RTX* SSRC so retransmission doesn't disturb the primary stream's sequence accounting. *Forward error correction* (ULPFEC, RFC 5109, or FlexFEC) adds redundant parity packets, letting the receiver reconstruct losses without a round trip — preferred when RTT is large relative to the frame interval. The congestion controller accounts for retransmission and FEC overhead in its bitrate budget.

**libwebrtc internals.** The reference implementation, libwebrtc, organizes media as a pipeline of *threads* (network, worker, signaling) communicating via task queues. Key modules: `PeerConnection` (SDP/ICE orchestration), `RtpTransportController` (the congestion-control and pacing hub), `Call`/`VideoSendStream` (encoding, packetization, RTX/FEC), jitter-buffer and NetEQ (audio) / VCM (video) timing, and the `AudioDeviceModule` capture/render path. Pacing (burst-controlled packet release) in the transport controller is load-bearing: without it, encoder output bursts would defeat the delay-based controller's queue estimates. Recent libwebrtc versions default to the sender-side, transport-wide BWE described above.

## 5 Empirical Results

The RMCAT-era literature converges on several quantitative findings about the algorithms in Section 4.3:

1. **GCC fairness and stability.** Controlled-testbed studies (TU Delft thesis work and Politecnico di Bari analyses [3]) found early GCC versions could be starved by competing TCP flows and exhibited instability between competing GCC flows; the Kalman-filter reformulation with adaptive thresholding materially improved fairness, though TCP-friendliness remains imperfect by design — GCC prioritizes low delay over aggressive throughput.
2. **Transport-CC responsiveness.** Sender-side estimation with per-packet feedback converges faster after bandwidth steps than REMB-based GCC, because feedback delay no longer includes the receiver's estimation window; deployments report fewer overshoot oscillations on step-down events [4].
3. **Topology scaling.** SFU forwarding sustains 50+ participant sessions on commodity servers where MCU transcoding saturates CPU an order of magnitude earlier; the trade-off is that SFU downlink per receiver grows linearly with speakers unless simulcast layering or dominant-speaker switching is applied.
4. **Loss recovery budgets.** Measurements of interactive video show NACK/RTX recovers the majority of isolated losses when RTT < 100 ms, while FEC is the more effective tool on high-RTT or bursty-loss paths — at a constant bandwidth tax even when no loss occurs.

## 6 Limitations

1. **TURN-TCP head-of-line blocking.** Where UDP is blocked, TURN over TCP (RFC 6062) or ICE-TCP (RFC 6544) tunnels real-time packets inside TCP; a single lost TCP segment stalls *all* multiplexed media until retransmission, inflating jitter beyond what the jitter buffer can absorb [1].
2. **Delay-based control vs. wireless.** GCC's delay-gradient estimator assumes queueing delay signals congestion; on Wi-Fi and cellular links, delay transients from link-layer retransmission and scheduling (unrelated to congestion) can trigger spurious back-offs — the packet-grouping heuristic mitigates but does not eliminate this [3].
3. **Encrypted media vs. middleboxes.** Mandatory DTLS-SRTP blinds lawful network functions (enterprise monitoring, recording without endpoint cooperation, legacy lawful-intercept taps); every compliant interception point must terminate DTLS, i.e., become an endpoint, with the trust implications that entails [2].
4. **Signaling trust bottleneck.** DTLS-SRTP's certificate model outsources authentication to signaling integrity; applications that signal over weakly authenticated channels inherit that weakness in media authentication.
5. **Circuit-breaker coarseness.** The RFC 8083 circuit breaker is a safety valve, not a controller: it stops catastrophic flows but says nothing about quality-optimal adaptation, leaving real performance to implementation-defined controllers that vary across vendors.

---

## 7 Conclusion

WebRTC's real-time media stack is a study in layered protocol composition: ICE solves rendezvous across hostile middleboxes, DTLS-SRTP bootstraps authenticated encryption without PKI, RTP/RTCP packetizes and reports, and adaptive congestion controllers — from GCC's Kalman-filtered delay gradients to sender-side transport-wide feedback and BBR-inspired probing — keep interactive flows both efficient and network-friendly. The mesh/SFU/MCU trichotomy then maps these mechanisms onto economic reality, trading client uplink, server CPU, and latency against one another, while jitter buffers, NACK/RTX, and FEC paper over the residual unreliability of the datagram path.

The architecture's open problems are not incidental: TCP-fallback head-of-line blocking, delay-signal ambiguity on wireless links, and the middlebox-opacity consequences of mandatory encryption are structural tensions between interactivity, deployability, and privacy. As WebRTC moves into larger-scale broadcast (WHIP/WHEP ingestion), WebTransport alternatives, and ML-driven bandwidth estimation, the invariants established here — secure-by-default media, congestion-aware sending, and the signaling/media plane split — are likely to persist even as the mechanisms evolve.
