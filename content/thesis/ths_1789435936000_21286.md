# Substructural Discipline: Linear Logic, Session Types, and Ownership in Programming Language Design

## Abstract
Substructural type systems restrict the structural rules of classical logic to discipline the use of computational resources. This thesis surveys the foundations and applications of linear logic in programming language design: Girard's connectives and their resource interpretation, cut elimination as computation, session types for binary and multiparty concurrency, ownership and borrowing as realized in Rust, and linear and quantitative typing in Haskell. Central results include the Curry-Howard-Lambek correspondence extended to concurrency through propositions-as-sessions, where deadlock freedom follows from logical consistency. We present the metatheory of progress, preservation, and session fidelity, evaluate empirical adoption across compilers and protocol tooling, and identify fundamental limits including undecidability of full linear logic and the open problem of complete type inference. The thesis argues that substructural discipline is becoming the standard vocabulary for resource-aware, concurrent, and memory-safe programming.

##1 Introduction

Classical and intuitionistic logic permit the structural rules of *weakening* and *contraction*: a hypothesis may be discarded without being used, and a hypothesis may be duplicated and used arbitrarily many times. These conveniences, inherited from a mathematics of timeless truths, are lies about computation. A file handle that is closed twice, a cryptographic nonce that is replayed, or a lock that is never released exposes the gap between logical assumption and physical resource. In 1987, Jean-Yves Girard proposed *linear logic*, a refinement of classical logic in which hypotheses are treated as resources that must be consumed *exactly once* [1]. Contraction and weakening are not abolished but are made explicit through the exponential modalities `!` (of course) and `?` (why not), so that the programmer—or the logic—declares precisely where copying and discarding are legitimate.

The influence of this single idea has been extraordinary. Linear logic reorganized proof theory around *cut elimination as computation*, supplied the type-theoretic vocabulary for *session types* that structure concurrent communication, inspired the *ownership and borrowing* discipline of the Rust programming language, and entered mainstream functional programming through *Linear Haskell* and its generalizations to *quantitative type theory* [2][6][8]. Perhaps most strikingly, Philip Wadler showed that classical linear logic propositions *are* session types, proofs *are* communicating processes, and cut elimination *is* communication—an extension of the Curry-Howard correspondence to concurrency in which *deadlock freedom follows from logical consistency* [2].

This thesis offers a unified treatment of these developments. Section 2 reviews the sequent-calculus foundations of substructural logics. Section 3 describes our methodology: proof-theoretic analysis grounded in operational semantics and mechanized metatheory. Section 4 contains the deep dive, in four parts: the connectives as resource operators, binary and multiparty session typing, ownership and borrowing at industrial scale, and the Curry-Howard-Lambek triangle. Section 5 presents the metatheoretic results—progress, preservation, session fidelity, and deadlock freedom—together with evidence of empirical adoption. Section 6 confronts the limitations: undecidability, inference, and expressiveness gaps. Section 7 concludes with a research agenda.

##2 Background

### Structural rules and the substructural spectrum

Gentzen's sequent calculus LK derives sequents `Γ ⊢ Δ` using logical rules that introduce connectives and *structural rules* that manipulate the context: exchange (permutation), weakening (introduction of unused hypotheses), and contraction (merging duplicated hypotheses). A logic is *substructural* when one or more of these is absent or restricted. The spectrum is instructive:

| Logic | Weakening | Contraction | Exchange | Character |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Classical / intuitionistic | yes | yes | yes | truth is eternal and shareable |
| Linear | no | no | yes | resources consumed exactly once |
| Affine | yes | no | yes | resources consumed at most once |
| Relevant | no | yes | yes | resources consumed at least once |
| Ordered (Lambek) | no | no | no | order of resources matters |

Girard's linear logic [1] splits the familiar connectives along a *multiplicative-additive* axis. The multiplicatives `⊗` (tensor), `⅋` (par), and `⊸` (linear implication) split the context between their premises—each resource goes to exactly one side—while the additives `&` (with) and `⊕` (plus) share the whole context, representing a choice made by the environment (`&`) or by the proof itself (`⊕`). The exponentials `!A` (unboundedly many copies of `A`) and `?A` (unboundedly many uses) reintroduce controlled weakening and contraction, so that ordinary intuitionistic reasoning embeds faithfully into the `!`-fragment.

### Intuitionistic linear logic and term calculi

For programming languages, the *intuitionistic* fragment is primary: linear implication `A ⊸ B` reads as a function that consumes its argument exactly once. Abramsky, and later Wadler in *Linear types can change the world!*, gave linear lambda calculi in which variables are used exactly once, and showed that in-place update, reference counting, and protocol state could be tracked by types rather than by runtime checks. This was the seed of everything that follows: a function type that *means* something about resource flow, not merely about value shape.

### From dyadic interaction to session types

Independently, concurrency theory was converging on the same structure. Honda introduced *dyadic* session types for the pi-calculus, and Honda, Vasconcelos, and Kubo gave the first full language discipline: channels carry structured, heterogeneous, bidirectional conversations, and session types describe the protocol each channel endpoint must follow [3]. Duality of session types—`!T.S` dual to `?T.S`, internal choice dual to external choice—turns out to be *linear negation*, a fact that lay dormant until the propositions-as-sessions programme made it explicit [2].

##3 Methodology

Our investigation proceeds along three complementary axes.

1. **Proof-theoretic analysis.** We present linear logic in the sequent calculus and examine cut elimination as the computational dynamics of proofs. Cut elimination is not merely a consistency argument; under the propositions-as-sessions reading, eliminating a cut *is* a communication step between processes [2].

2. **Operational semantics and metatheory.** For each language fragment—a linear lambda calculus, a binary session calculus, and a multiparty session calculus—we define typing judgments and small-step semantics, then establish the standard safety properties:

   - *Preservation (subject reduction):* well-typed terms stay well-typed under reduction.
   - *Progress:* well-typed closed terms are values or can take a step.
   - *Session fidelity:* communication follows the declared protocol.
   - *Deadlock freedom / global progress:* no configuration of well-typed processes becomes permanently stuck.

3. **Empirical survey.** We trace adoption in compilers and tooling: the Rust borrow checker [8], the linear arrow in GHC [6][7], multiparty session-type toolchains such as Scribble and its code generators, and session-typed libraries. We evaluate where the theory has transferred cleanly and where engineering has outrun—or been blocked by—the formalism.

##4 Deep Dive: From Connectives to Capability Types

### The Connectives as Resource Operators

The heart of linear logic is the *resource interpretation* of its connectives. A formula is not a truth to be asserted but a resource to be consumed, and each connective is an operator for combining, choosing, or replicating resources.

**Multiplicatives.** `A ⊗ B` (tensor) means: I have both `A` and `B`, to be used together; `A ⅋ B` (par) is its De Morgan dual, and `A ⊸ B`, defined as `A⊥ ⅋ B`, is a linear function—one use of `A` yields one `B`, and the argument is consumed. The units `1` and `⊥` are the empty tensor and par. The introduction rule for `⊗` splits the context multiplicatively:

```haskell
-- Intuitionistic reading: splitting the context
--   Γ ⊢ A      Δ ⊢ B
-- ───────────────────  (⊗-right)
--     Γ, Δ ⊢ A ⊗ B
```

No hypothesis may appear on both sides; the context is *partitioned*, and this partitioning is exactly what a borrow checker enforces when it moves a value from one owner to another [8].

**Additives.** `A & B` (with) offers a choice to the *environment*: the proof must be ready for either, so the whole context is shared between the premises. `A ⊕ B` (plus) is a choice made *internally*: the proof commits to one side. Their units are `⊤` and `0`. In session types these become *branching*: `&{l1: S1, l2: S2}` offers the peer a menu of labels, and `⊕{l1: S1, l2: S2}` selects one [4].

**Exponentials.** `!A` packages arbitrarily many copies of `A`; dereliction `!A ⊸ A` extracts one, contraction `!A ⊸ !A ⊗ !A` duplicates, weakening `!A ⊸ 1` discards. Girard's *translation* embeds intuitionistic implication as `!A ⊸ B`: an ordinary function may use its argument any number of times precisely because the argument arrives under the exponential. Affine and relevant variants correspond to permitting weakening or contraction without the full exponential.

> **Theorem:** *Cut elimination.* Every proof in propositional linear logic reduces, via a confluent and strongly normalizing rewrite system on proofs (or proof nets), to a cut-free proof [1].

Under the resource reading, a cut `Γ ⊢ A, A⊥ ⊢ Δ` is a *rendezvous*: a producer of `A` meets a consumer. Eliminating the cut performs the interaction. Girard's proof nets—graphical proof syntax in which the bureaucracy of sequent rules is quotiented away—make this dynamics geometric: cut elimination becomes local graph rewriting, a direct ancestor of interaction nets and of the graphical operational semantics used for session-typed processes [2].

### Binary and Multiparty Session Typing

Binary session types assign each channel endpoint a *session type* describing the exact sequence of communications it will perform. The grammar is small but expressive:

```
S ::= !T.S        -- send a value of type T, continue as S
    | ?T.S        -- receive a value of type T, continue as S
    | ⊕{li: Si}   -- select label li, continue as Si
    | &{li: Si}   -- offer labels li, continue as Si
    | end         -- session complete
    | μX.S | X    -- recursion
```

The discipline has two pillars. First, *duality*: the two endpoints of a channel must carry dual types (`!` dual to `?`, `⊕` dual to `&`), and this duality is linear negation in disguise [2]. Second, *linearity of channels*: a channel endpoint is a resource used exactly once at each step, so aliasing a live channel—or dropping one mid-protocol—is a type error. Honda, Vasconcelos, and Kubo proved that well-typed programs in their session calculus never exhibit communication mismatches: sends always meet receives of the right type, and labels always match an offered branch [3].

The leap to *multiparty* session types [4][5] handles protocols among more than two participants—buyers, sellers, and shippers; clients, authenticators, and services. A *global type* describes the choreography from a neutral viewpoint:

```
G ::= p -> q : { li(Ti). Gi }   -- p sends label li with payload Ti to q
    | μt.G | t | end
```

A *projection* operation derives each participant's local session type from the global type, and a *coherence* condition guarantees that the projections are mutually compatible. Honda, Yoshida, and Carbone proved subject reduction, communication safety, and—crucially for asynchronous queues—*progress*: in a well-typed multiparty session, either the session has terminated or some participant can communicate [4].

The practical consequence is *protocol conformance by construction*: a multiparty protocol written as a global type can be projected to per-role stubs, and any implementation typechecking against its stub cannot deadlock or miscommunicate within the session. Toolchains in this lineage generate such stubs for mainstream languages, turning a theorem about coherence into a build-time guarantee.

### Ownership, Borrowing, and Linear Types at Scale

Rust's ownership system is the most successful industrial deployment of substructural ideas to date [8]. Its three rules are a sloganized affine type system:

1. Each value has exactly one *owner*.
2. Ownership can be *moved* but, for non-`Copy` types, not duplicated—this is affinity: no contraction.
3. When the owner goes out of scope, the value is *dropped*—no weakening is needed because destruction is deterministic and total.

*Borrowing* overlays this with a disciplined, temporary relaxation. At any program point, a value may have either any number of shared borrows (`&T`) or exactly one mutable borrow (`&mut T`), never both—the famous *aliasing XOR mutation* rule. Lifetimes then bound how long borrows may live, ensuring no borrow outlives its referent. Consider the canonical move:

```rust
fn main() {
    let s1 = String::from("hello");
    let s2 = s1;            // ownership MOVES; s1 is no longer usable
    // println!("{}", s1); // rejected: use of moved value (E0382)
    let len = calculate(&s2); // shared borrow: s2 remains the owner
    println!("{} has length {}", s2, len);
}

fn calculate(s: &String) -> usize { s.len() }
```

The borrow checker's rejection of the commented line is *contraction failure made into a compiler error*: the program attempted to duplicate a linear resource.

*Linear Haskell* took the complementary route: rather than a new language, it retrofitted linearity onto Haskell with minimal disruption [6][7]. The key design decision was to attach linearity to the *function arrow* rather than to kinds: `a %1 -> b` is a linear function, and multiplicity polymorphism lets `map :: (a %p -> b) -> [a] %p -> [b]` work uniformly for linear and unrestricted functions. Linearity on the arrow makes datatypes *multiplicity-parametric*—a pair `(a, b)` can hold linear or unrestricted components depending on use—and the system enjoys progress and preservation in its core calculus. GHC 9.0 shipped the linear arrow, giving Haskell programmers linear file handles, in-place array updates with pure interfaces, and protocol-state enforcement, all with backward compatibility: existing programs continue to typecheck unchanged [6].

The natural generalization is *quantitative type theory*, where the binary linear/unrestricted distinction becomes a semiring of usage counts: a function may demand its argument be used exactly once, at most once, at least once, or according to an arbitrary grade.

### The Curry-Howard-Lambek Triangle and Its Consequences

The Curry-Howard correspondence identifies propositions with types, proofs with programs, and normalization with evaluation; Lambek added the third vertex, identifying proofs with arrows in a cartesian closed category.

| Logic | Computation | Session reading |
|---|---|---|
| Proposition `A` | Type `A` | Session type: protocol on a channel |
| Proof of `A` | Program of type `A` | Process offering behavior `A` |
| Cut | Substitution / composition | Parallel composition plus hiding |
| Cut elimination | Evaluation | Communication |
| Linear negation `A⊥` | — | Dual protocol (swap send/receive, select/offer) |

Wadler's calculus CP takes the propositions of *classical* linear logic as session types directly.

> **Theorem:** *(Wadler [2]).* If `P` is well-typed in CP, then `P` is deadlock-free: every maximal reduction sequence ends in a configuration with no pending communications, i.e., all sessions complete.

This is the payoff of the entire programme.

##5 Empirical Results and Proofs

### Metatheoretic results

The central metatheoretic results of the field are now standard, and we sketch their shape.

> **Theorem:** *Preservation for the linear lambda calculus.* If `Γ ⊢ e : A` and `e ⟶ e'`, then `Γ ⊢ e' : A`. The proof is by induction on the reduction derivation; the linear substitution lemma—substituting a value for a variable used exactly once preserves the exact-once invariant—is the crux, and it fails precisely when contraction is available without the exponential [6].

> **Theorem:** *Progress.* If `· ⊢ e : A` then `e` is a value or `e ⟶ e'` for some `e'`. Linearity does not threaten progress; the interesting work is in the session calculi, where progress must account for blocked receives.

> **Theorem:** *Session fidelity and global progress (multiparty).* In the asynchronous multiparty session calculus of [4], well-typed processes preserve the projections of their global type under reduction (subject reduction), never suffer arity or label mismatches (communication safety), and never reach a stuck non-terminal configuration (progress). The proof hinges on *coherence* of the global type: projections of a coherent global type are pairwise dual in the appropriate sense, so every send has a matching receive somewhere in the choreography [5].

These theorems have been *mechanized*: subject reduction for multiparty asynchronous session types has been proved in a proof assistant, closing the gap between paper proof and machine-checked certainty.

### Empirical adoption

Theory transfer can be measured by adoption, and here the record is strong:

- **Rust** is the flagship: an affine type system with borrow checking ships in a production compiler used for operating systems, browsers, and embedded firmware, eliminating data races and use-after-free errors at compile time for safe code [8]. The borrow checker's error messages—*use of moved value*, *cannot borrow as mutable more than once*—are substructural proof obligations surfaced to the programmer.
- **GHC's linear types** demonstrate that linearity can be retrofitted onto a mature, lazy, higher-order language without breaking existing code, with case studies in mutable arrays with pure interfaces and protocol enforcement for I/O [6][7].
- **Multiparty session tooling** (global-type specification languages and stub generators in the Scribble lineage) is used to specify and verify real communication protocols, with coherence checking catching protocol design errors before implementation begins [4][5].
- **Session-typed libraries** in Rust, Haskell, and OCaml embed binary session types using linearity or affine encodings, bringing protocol conformance to everyday concurrent programming.

The pattern is consistent: the most adopted systems trade some expressiveness for ergonomics.

##6 Limitations

**Decidability.** Full propositional linear logic—with the exponentials—is undecidable; even fragments are intractable, so practical systems restrict themselves to decidable, well-behaved subsets (intuitionistic linear lambda calculi, session type systems with syntactic duality). The exponential is where both power and undecidability live, and language designers must decide, connective by connective, what to admit.

**Type inference.** Complete inference for linear and quantitative systems remains open. Linear Haskell's authors note explicitly that a systematic treatment of linearity inference is unfinished work: multiplicity polymorphism recovers much convenience, but principal types in the presence of grades and linearity polymorphism are not yet settled [6]. Rust sidesteps the problem by requiring no linearity annotations at all—the analysis is entirely compiler-inferred over an affine core—but pays with conservative rejections (the *fighting the borrow checker* phenomenon) where the analysis cannot see that a program is safe [8].

**Expressiveness gaps.** Session types excel at *static, structured* protocols and struggle with dynamic topologies: participants joining mid-session, failure and timeout handling, and protocol evolution all strain the global-type model, requiring extensions (dynamic session joining, exception-aware types) that complicate the metatheory [5]. Ownership types handle tree-shaped resource graphs beautifully but fight *cyclic* data structures, which is why Rust programs reach for reference counting (`Rc`, `Arc`)—a disciplined retreat to the exponential—when cycles are essential.

**Semantic subtlety.** The interaction of linearity with laziness (in Haskell), with exceptions, and with interior mutability (in Rust) continues to produce soundness-critical edge cases. Each escape hatch from the discipline—`unsafe`, `RefCell`, linear constraints—must be audited against the metatheory, and the audit is never finished once.

##7 Conclusion

Forty years after Girard made weakening and contraction explicit [1], the substructural viewpoint has moved from proof-theoretic curiosity to engineering discipline. The arc is clean: linear logic identified resources as the missing concept in the propositions-as-types correspondence; session types discovered that concurrent protocols *are* linear propositions [2][3][4]; ownership and borrowing industrialized affinity for memory safety [8]; and linear and quantitative typing brought the whole apparatus into mainstream functional languages [6][7].

The research frontier follows the limitations. Graded and quantitative systems promise a unified account of usage, cost, and dependency. Effectful and dependent session types aim to close the expressiveness gaps around failure and dynamic participation. Mechanized metatheory is raising the standard of evidence from paper proof to machine-checked artifact. And the deepest open question remains philosophical as much as technical: how much logical discipline can working programmers be asked to carry before the cure—explicit resource management in the type system—costs more than the disease? The evidence of Rust suggests the answer is *a great deal*, provided the discipline is inferred rather than annotated, and its violations explained rather than merely reported.

Substructural types began by observing that logic had been lying about resources. The lie is now optional. Whether through an affine borrow checker, a linear arrow, or a global protocol type, the programmer can choose to tell the truth—and have the compiler check it.
