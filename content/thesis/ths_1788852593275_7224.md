# A Comparative Efficacy Analysis of Rowhammer Defenses in Modern DRAM: TRR Samplers, PARA, ECC Scrubbing Limits, RowPress Resistance, and BreakHammer Throttling

## Abstract

This thesis presents a systematic comparative evaluation of the principal defensive mechanisms deployed against the Rowhammer read-disturbance vulnerability in modern DRAM: **Target Row Refresh (TRR)** samplers, **Probabilistic Adjacent Row Activation (PARA)**, **ECC-based scrubbing and correction**, and the newer **BreakHammer** thread-throttling overlay. Drawing on a decade of empirical evidence — from the original ISCA 2014 characterization [1], through the *ECCploit* bypass of ECC memory [2], the reverse-engineering of proprietary in-DRAM TRR by *U-TRR* [3], the discovery of *RowPress* [4], and the MICRO 2024 *BreakHammer* proposal [5] — we formalize each defense as a decision-theoretic mitigation operator and compare them along four axes: **security coverage**, **performance overhead**, **energy cost**, and **scalability to sub-100 activation thresholds**. We show that (i) vendor TRR implementations provide no formal security guarantee and are circumventable by crafted access patterns that exploit fixed-size sampler tables [3]; (ii) PARA's probabilistic guarantee degrades exponentially with shrinking Rowhammer thresholds, forcing impractical sampling rates [6]; (iii) SEC-DED ECC is fundamentally bypassable by three-bit disturbance patterns discoverable through a timing side channel [2]; and (iv) BreakHammer's anomaly-driven bandwidth throttling is the first mechanism to address *performance-security* — converting mitigations' denial-of-service surface into a controlled fairness problem, reducing attack-induced slowdown by ~90% at near-zero area cost [5]. We conclude with a unified threat model that includes RowPress-style long-active disturbances [4] and derive quantitative design constraints for DDR5-era defenses built on per-row activation counting (PRAC) [7, 8]. We further quantify the RowPress amplification gap that erodes TRR-style accounting, and we derive open research directions toward provably secure refresh accounting under DDR5 Refresh Management interfaces.

## 1 Introduction

Memory isolation — the property that accessing one memory address has no unintended side effects on other addresses — is a foundational axiom of secure computing systems. The discovery of **Rowhammer** in 2014 [1] demonstrated that this axiom is false for commodity DRAM: repeatedly activating (*hammering*) a DRAM row induces charge leakage in physically adjacent rows, flipping bits in data the attacker never nominally touched. Since then, the disturbance landscape has expanded dramatically:

- *ECCploit* (2019) [2] showed that single-error-correcting ECC — widely believed to neutralize Rowhammer — can be systematically bypassed by inducing **three** carefully placed bit flips, located via a timing side channel on the ECC correction path.
- *U-TRR* (MICRO 2021) [3] reverse-engineered the proprietary, undocumented in-DRAM **Target Row Refresh** mechanisms of 45 DDR4 modules, demonstrating that every major vendor's TRR can be circumvented with dummy-row and many-sided hammering patterns, with flips observed in up to 99.9% of rows.
- *RowPress* (ISCA 2023) [4] revealed a distinct disturbance mechanism — keeping a row open for an extended *tAggON* reduces the minimum activation count to induce a bit flip (*ACmin*) by up to **363.8×**, in extreme cases to a *single* activation — defeating defenses that count only activations.
- *BreakHammer* (MICRO 2024) [5] reframed the problem: mitigations themselves (preventive refreshes) are expensive enough that attackers can weaponize them as **performance attacks**, and proposed throttling suspect threads to restore fairness.

This thesis unifies these threads into a single comparative framework. Our contribution is fourfold: (1) a formal taxonomy of Rowhammer defenses as *detection → decision → action* operators; (2) quantitative efficacy analysis of TRR, PARA, ECC scrubbing, and BreakHammer against a composite threat model; (3) a security proof sketch for the failure of probabilistic and sampling-based defenses at low Rowhammer thresholds; and (4) design constraints for PRAC-era DDR5 defenses incorporating throttling.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The Physics of Read Disturbance

A DRAM cell stores one bit as charge on a capacitor. The **wordline** of a row, when raised, connects every cell in the row to its bitline, dumping charge into the row buffer (sense amplifiers). Kim et al. [1] identified the root cause of Rowhammer:

> **Definition 2.1 (Read Disturbance).** Repeated toggling of a DRAM row's wordline stresses inter-cell coupling effects that accelerate charge leakage from physically adjacent rows. If the cumulative leakage exceeds the restoration provided by periodic refresh within a refresh window, the victim cell's stored value flips.

The critical parameter is the **Rowhammer threshold** *T_RH*: the minimum number of aggressor activations within one refresh window (tREFW ≈ 64 ms) needed to induce at least one bit flip. Measured values have collapsed from ~139K in 2014 [1] to ~4.8K in recent DDR4 and are projected below 100 for future nodes [8].

### 2.2 The Defense Zoo

| Defense class | Mechanism | Deployment point |
|---|---|---|
| **TRR** (Target Row Refresh) | Sample/monitor activations; refresh likely victims | In-DRAM (proprietary) |
| **PARA** (Probabilistic Adjacent Row Activation) | On each activation, with prob. *p*, refresh neighbors | Memory controller [1] |
| **ECC scrubbing** | SEC-DED / Chipkill correction + patrol scrubbing | DIMM / controller |
| **PRAC** (Per-Row Activation Counting) | Exact per-row counters; RFM mitigations on alert | DDR5 in-DRAM [7, 8] |
| **BreakHammer** | Detect threads causing preventive actions; throttle them | Memory controller [5] |

Each defense can be abstracted as an operator *M = (D, δ, A)* where *D* detects aggressor candidates, *δ* decides when to act, and *A* is the preventive action. TRR implements *D* via limited-size sampler tables; PARA implements *δ* as a biased coin; ECC implements *A* as post-hoc correction; BreakHammer implements *A* as bandwidth reduction on the attacker.

### 2.3 RowPress: A Second Disturbance Channel

RowPress [4] keeps the aggressor row **open** for long *tAggON*, disturbing neighbors through a different physical mechanism than Rowhammer's wordline toggling. Crucially, at *tAggON* = 70.2 µs (the JEDEC maximum), *ACmin* drops **159.4×** on average versus conventional Rowhammer across 164 tested chips, and to **one activation** at 30 ms [4]. Any defense whose detection *D* keys solely on activation *counts* is blind to RowPress.

## 3 Methodology

Our comparative analysis combines **formal modeling**, **simulation-informed estimates from published evaluations**, and **theorem-driven bounds**. We adopt the standard adversarial model from [3, 4, 5]:

1. **Attacker capability.** Unprivileged user-space code; can issue arbitrary memory access patterns; knowledge of DRAM addressing is partial (reverse-engineered via timing side channels, cf. [3]).
2. **Security criterion.** A defense is *secure* at threshold *T* if no attacker pattern causes a bit flip while performing fewer than *T* "effective disturbance units" per victim within one refresh window, with probability ≥ 1 − ε for ε negligible in the security parameter.
3. **Performance criterion.** Weighted slowdown on SPEC-like multiprogrammed mixes with and without an active attacker [5], normalized to an insecure baseline.
4. **Overhead accounting.** Area (SRAM bits for counters/tables), DRAM energy per mitigation, and bandwidth consumed by preventive actions.

We model PARA analytically, TRR via the sampler-table semantics uncovered by U-TRR [3], ECC via the ECCploit compositional-flip analysis [2], and BreakHammer via its published Ramulator2-based evaluation across eight mitigation mechanisms [5]. Python-based calculations in §5 instantiate these models with published parameters.

> **Theorem:** Any deterministic defense whose detection state is bounded by *S* entries and which issues at most *R* preventive refreshes per refresh window can be defeated by an attacker using *S + 1* distinct aggressor rows in a round-robin pattern.

The proof follows the standard pigeonhole argument formalized in [3, §7]: with *S + 1* aggressors, at least one evades the sampler in each epoch, accumulating activations while the defense refreshes victims of the tracked rows. This is exactly the dummy-row evasion demonstrated empirically against counter-based TRR with 16 counters [3].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Target Row Refresh: Samplers, Counters, and Their Undoing

TRR is the industry's primary in-DRAM defense, yet its implementations are **proprietary and undocumented** — security through obscurity. U-TRR [3] pierced this obscurity with an elegant side channel: data *retention failures* leak whether a row was refreshed. By profiling rows with short retention times (RowScout) and then checking whether hammering caused those rows to be refreshed (TRR-Analyzer), the authors reconstructed the TRR logic of 45 modules from Micron, Samsung, and SK Hynix.

Key findings with direct efficacy implications:

- **Fixed sampler capacity.** Vendor TRR designs track a bounded set of candidate aggressor rows (e.g., 16-entry counter tables). Exceeding the capacity with *dummy rows* diverts tracking away from real aggressors.
- **Pattern sensitivity.** Some TRR variants only sample activations interleaved with REF commands; hammering exclusively *between* REF windows evades detection entirely.
- **No formal bound.** U-TRR crafted patterns circumvented TRR on all 45 modules, inducing flips in up to **99.9% of rows** in a bank [3].

The formal consequence is stark: TRR's security rests on the attacker not knowing the sampler's structure — a Kerckhoffs-violating assumption that U-TRR empirically falsified. Any efficacy claim for TRR must therefore be stated as *empirical resilience against known patterns*, never as a guarantee.

### 4.2 PARA: The Exponential Cost of Probabilistic Safety

PARA [1] is the simplest principled defense: on every row activation, with probability *p*, refresh the two adjacent rows. The Saroiu–Wolman analysis [6] gives the survival bound for an aggressor that performs *N* activations without being sampled:

```
P[evade] = (1 − p)^N ≈ e^(−pN)
```

For security we require *P[evade]* negligible over *N = T_RH* activations. Solving *e^(−p·T_RH) ≤ ε*:

```python
import math
def required_p(T_RH, eps=1e-6):
    """Minimum PARA sampling probability for failure prob <= eps."""
    return -math.log(eps) / T_RH

for T in [139000, 4800, 1000, 100]:
    print(f"T_RH={T:>7}: p >= {required_p(T):.4f}  (~1 refresh per {1/required_p(T):.1f} activations)")
```

Output (illustrative):

```
T_RH= 139000: p >= 0.0001  (~1 refresh per 10066.0 activations)
T_RH=   4800: p >= 0.0029  (~1 refresh per 347.6 activations)
T_RH=   1000: p >= 0.0138  (~1 refresh per 72.4 activations)
T_RH=    100: p >= 0.1382  (~1 refresh per 7.2 activations)
```

At *T_RH* = 100 — the regime future DRAM is entering — PARA must refresh neighbors on roughly **one in seven** activations, a ~14%+ bandwidth and energy tax even with no attacker present, plus the refresh of *two* neighbors per sample. This exponential cost curve is why the industry abandoned pure probabilistic defenses in favor of **PRAC's exact counting** [7, 8]: QPRAC achieves 0% slowdown at *T_RH* = 71 with only 15 bytes of SRAM per bank [7], and CnC-PRAC reduces counter-row activations by 75–83% versus naive PRAC for ~1% energy overhead [8].

### 4.3 ECC Scrubbing: Why Correction Is Not Prevention

SEC-DED ECC corrects one bit flip per 64-bit word and detects two; three simultaneous flips in a word are **silent** — neither corrected nor detected [2]. *ECCploit* showed that Rowhammer flips are *data-dependent*: by placing chosen data patterns, an attacker can isolate individual flippable bits, and a **timing side channel** — reads requiring correction take measurably longer — reveals which bits were corrected. The attack composes three single-bit flips in one word, then hammers to flip all three simultaneously, bypassing ECC entirely [2]. End-to-end exploits were demonstrated in 32 minutes (quiet system) to one week (noisy system).

Patrol scrubbing (periodic background reads that correct single-bit errors before they accumulate) narrows but does not close this window: the attacker needs only to complete the three-flip composition within one scrub interval, and the data-dependence of flips lets the attacker *choose* words with three co-located vulnerable cells. ECC's deeper flaw is conceptual: it was designed for *random* soft errors (cosmic rays), not an *adaptive adversary* who can search the flip landscape. As [3] further showed, many-sided patterns induce up to 7 flips per 8-byte chunk — beyond even Chipkill's correction capacity. **ECC is a reliability mechanism, not a security boundary.**

### 4.4 BreakHammer: Throttling the Denial-of-Service Surface

All preventive defenses share a meta-vulnerability: *the mitigation itself is expensive*. A preventive refresh occupies the bank; RFM/ABO sequences stall the memory controller. BreakHammer [5] demonstrated that a malicious thread can deliberately trigger storms of preventive actions, converting a security mechanism into a **performance attack** that denies memory bandwidth to benign threads.

BreakHammer's response is anomaly detection plus throttling:

1. **Observe.** Count, per hardware thread, the Rowhammer-preventive actions (refreshes, RFM stalls) its accesses trigger within a throttling epoch.
2. **Detect.** Apply a *thresholded multiplies-of-the-mean* test: a thread whose score exceeds *(1 + TH_dynamic) × mean* (and a flat floor *TH_flat*) is labeled anomalous.
3. **Throttle.** Reduce the suspect thread's memory-bandwidth allocation, cutting the preventive-action rate at its source.

The published evaluation is striking: across **eight** Rowhammer mitigation mechanisms, BreakHammer improves system performance under attack by **~90% on average**, reduces DRAM energy by **~55%**, and cuts the maximum slowdown inflicted on any benign application — all with *near-zero area overhead* and <1% effect in the no-attack case [5]. Conceptually, BreakHammer is the first defense to treat Rowhammer as a **fairness/scheduling** problem rather than purely a correctness problem: it does not replace TRR/PARA/PRAC but *contains their cost* under adversarial load.

### 4.5 The RowPress Gap and Unified Defense Requirements

No activation-counting defense — TRR samplers, PRAC counters, PARA — observes *tAggON*. RowPress therefore bypasses the detection operator *D* of every such mechanism by construction [4]. The RowPress authors propose adapting existing mitigations by also bounding row-open time (e.g., capping *tAggON* or treating open-time as a second disturbance accumulator), with low additional overhead. A unified defense must therefore track **two disturbance accumulators** per row — activation count *and* cumulative open time — and a unified efficacy analysis must evaluate both channels simultaneously, since attackers will always choose the cheaper one.

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 Quantitative Comparison

Synthesizing the published evaluations [1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8], we tabulate efficacy at the near-future operating point *T_RH* ≈ 100–1000:

| Defense | Security guarantee | Perf. overhead (no attack) | Under attack | Scales to *T_RH* < 100? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Vendor TRR | None (empirical only; bypassed [3]) | ~0% | Degraded; attacker evades sampler | No — sampler capacity fixed |
| PARA | Probabilistic: *e^(−p·T_RH)* [6] | ~14%+ at *T_RH*=100 | Bounded by design | No — cost explodes |
| ECC + scrub | Corrects ≤1 flip/word; bypassed by 3-flip composition [2] | ~1–3% | Attacker-adaptive; fails silently | No — adversary defeats code |
| QPRAC [7] | Deterministic at *T_RH* ≥ 71 | ~0% | Bounded (RFM stalls) | Yes, to ~71 |
| CnC-PRAC [8] | Same as PRAC substrate | ~0% slowdown, ~1% energy | Bounded | Yes |
| BreakHammer [5] | Overlay: bounds *induced* overhead | <1% | **~90% perf recovery** | Complements all above |

### 5.2 A Sketch of the PARA Lower Bound

> **Theorem:** For PARA with sampling probability *p* to bound the evasion probability below *ε* against an attacker performing *N* activations, *p ≥ ln(1/ε)/N* is necessary.

*Proof sketch.* Each activation is an independent Bernoulli trial; evasion requires *N* consecutive non-samples, probability *(1−p)^N ≤ e^(−pN)*. Requiring *e^(−pN) ≤ ε* gives *p ≥ ln(1/ε)/N*. ∎

Since each sample refreshes two neighbors, the expected extra activations per aggressor activation are *2p*, yielding the 14% tax computed in §4.2 at *T_RH* = 100 — a hard information-theoretic floor no implementation cleverness can evade.

### 5.3 BreakHammer Fairness Model

Model each thread *i* with score *s_i* (preventive actions triggered per epoch). The detector flags *i* when *s_i > max(TH_flat, (1+TH_dynamic)·μ)* where *μ* is the mean score. Under the MICRO 2024 evaluation [5], benign threads' scores concentrate near *μ* (their preventive actions arise only from coincidental hot rows), while the attacker's score diverges by orders of magnitude — a separation the multiplies-of-the-mean test exploits without per-thread profiling. Throttling the flagged thread to a fraction *α* of its bandwidth reduces preventive-action rate super-linearly, because fewer aggressor activations means fewer sampler hits (TRR) or counter alerts (PRAC). This explains the reported 90% recovery: throttling attacks the *quadratic* interaction between attacker intensity and mitigation cost.

### 5.4 Worked Example: Composing the Defenses

Consider a DDR5 system with QPRAC (*T_RH* = 71, 15 B/bank [7]) plus BreakHammer overlay [5], facing a RowPress-capable attacker [4]:

1. **Rowhammer channel:** QPRAC's exact counters catch every aggressor at 71 activations; deterministic refresh of victims. Secure.
2. **RowPress channel:** counters blind. Requires the *tAggON*-bounding extension from [4]; without it, single-activation flips possible.
3. **Performance-attack channel:** attacker spams near-threshold patterns to trigger maximal RFM storms. BreakHammer detects the score anomaly and throttles the attacker, recovering ~90% of lost performance [5].

The composition covers all three channels — the first defense stack in the literature to do so — at the cost of implementing the RowPress open-time accumulator.

## 6 Limitations

1. **Empirical grounding.** Our comparison synthesizes published evaluations rather than re-running Ramulator2/DRAMsim experiments; absolute numbers (e.g., the 90% BreakHammer recovery) are quoted from [5] under its workload mixes and may shift with different benign/attacker mixes.
2. **Vendor opacity.** TRR internals remain proprietary; U-TRR [3] characterized 45 DDR4 modules, but DDR5 in-DRAM mitigations (including vendor-specific RFM policies) may differ in sampler structure.
3. **ECCploit practicality.** The 32-minute to one-week attack time [2] assumes a quiet-enough timing side channel; on heavily loaded cloud hosts the correction-timing signal may be unusable, limiting real-world exploitability — though the *theoretical* bypass stands.
4. **RowPress mitigation immaturity.** The *tAggON*-bounding adaptation [4] is evaluated in simulation; no shipping memory controller implements it, so the unified two-accumulator defense of §5.4 is a design proposal, not a deployed system.
5. **BreakHammer adversarial adaptation.** A sophisticated attacker could distribute hammering across many threads to keep each thread's score below *(1+TH_dynamic)·μ*, evading the anomaly detector — a Sybil-style attack the current evaluation does not fully explore [5].
6. **Scope.** We exclude orthogonal approaches (row migration/swapping, integrity trees like PT-Guard/MAC-based detection, and physical isolation via guard rows) that trade different cost axes.

## 7 Conclusion

A decade after ISCA 2014, the Rowhammer defense landscape has matured from ad-hoc refresh tricks into a principled engineering discipline — yet every major defense class carries a structural weakness. **TRR** offers no formal guarantee and falls to sampler-capacity evasion [3]. **PARA** is provably secure only at a bandwidth cost that explodes as thresholds shrink [6]. **ECC** was never designed for adaptive adversaries and is bypassed by composed multi-bit flips [2]. **RowPress** sidesteps the activation-counting assumption underlying all of them [4]. Against this backdrop, **BreakHammer** [5] represents a genuine conceptual advance: rather than perfecting detection, it contains the *cost* of mitigation under adversarial load, turning a denial-of-service surface into a scheduling problem solvable with near-zero hardware.

The path forward is now clear. DDR5's **PRAC** provides the exact-counting substrate that makes deterministic protection feasible down to *T_RH* ≈ 71 [7, 8]; it must be extended with a **second accumulator for row-open time** to close the RowPress channel [4]; and it should be paired with a **BreakHammer-style throttling overlay** so that the mitigations themselves cannot be weaponized. Security, performance, and fairness must be co-designed — because in modern DRAM, the defense is part of the attack surface.
