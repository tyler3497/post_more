# Predictable Topology Routing in Optically Interlinked LEO Mega-Constellations: Contact-Graph Forwarding, Laser Acquisition–Tracking–Pointing Control, and Delay-Tolerant Store-and-Forward Architectures

## Abstract

Low Earth orbit (LEO) mega-constellations with optical inter-satellite links (ISLs) are becoming the dominant substrate for global broadband, yet their topology is governed by fast, strictly predictable orbital motion that invalidates conventional Internet routing assumptions. This thesis develops a unified treatment of the network layer for optically interlinked mega-constellations. We formalize constellation geometry through Walker patterns, derive link-budget and acquisition–tracking–pointing (ATP) requirements for laser ISLs at multi-gigabit rates, and reduce routing under predictable mobility to earliest-arrival path computation over a time-expanded contact graph. We prove the optimality of contact-graph routing (CGR) under perfect contact plans, bound the routing diameter of Walker-star and Walker-delta topologies, and analyze seamless handover as a joint mobility-management and link-reconfiguration problem with seconds-scale optical setup penalties. We extend CGR with capacity- and buffer-feasible route search and integrate delay-tolerant store-and-forward with custody transfer for disrupted segments. A simulation methodology over realistic ephemerides demonstrates that contact-graph forwarding with realistic ATP setup delays achieves near-optimal delivery latency while cutting handover-induced loss by an order of magnitude relative to snapshot-based shortest-path schemes.

## 1 Introduction

The migration from bent-pipe satellite architectures to **regenerative, optically interlinked** LEO mega-constellations constitutes a phase change in space networking. Starlink, OneWeb, Telesat Lightspeed and emerging systems each plan thousands of spacecraft carrying four or more laser inter-satellite link (LISL) terminals, supporting capacities of 10 Gbps and beyond [1]. Unlike terrestrial fiber, whose topology is static, and unlike mobile ad hoc networks, whose topology is *unpredictably* dynamic, the LEO constellation topology is *predictably* dynamic: every link's existence interval is computable in advance from orbital ephemerides. This predictability is the central structural property that distinguishes LEO networking from every other domain [2].

Predictability changes routing from a discovery problem into a **planning** problem. Classical satellite routing, e.g. the datagram algorithm of Ekici, Akyildiz and Bender that exploited the regular intra-/inter-plane ISL structure of LEO networks [3], assumed near-continuous connectivity over a slowly changing topology. Mega-constellations break that assumption: with four laser terminals per satellite, links must be reconfigured as geometry evolves, and each reconfiguration incurs an acquisition–tracking–pointing (ATP) setup penalty of seconds [4], during which the terminal is unproductive. This thesis develops the network layer for this regime in five parts:

1. A formal geometric model of Walker-star and Walker-delta mega-constellations, with closed-form bounds on ISL range, contact duration, and routing diameter.
2. An ATP control architecture for laser ISLs, deriving the point-ahead requirement and the pointing error budget that couples micro-vibration to link availability.
3. A **time-expanded contact-graph** formulation of routing under predictable mobility, with a proof of earliest-arrival optimality and capacity/buffer feasibility extensions [5].
4. A joint model of seamless handover covering both the user-access segment and the ISL plane, quantifying the cost of optical setup delays.
5. Integration of delay-tolerant store-and-forward with custody transfer [6][7][8] as a resilience mechanism for contact-plan failures and ATP outages.

What follows: related work, methodology, the four technical pillars, formal results with simulation, limitations, and conclusions.

## 2 Background and Related Work

**Delay-tolerant networking.** Burleigh et al. introduced delay-tolerant networking (DTN) as an approach to the interplanetary Internet, where propagation delays and scheduled contacts preclude end-to-end paths [6]. Fall generalized this to challenged internets with the bundle layer as a message-oriented overlay, with persistent storage and hop-by-hop custody [7]; the DTN architecture was standardized as RFC 4838 [8]. For LEO, the *scheduled contact* abstraction transfers directly: every optical ISL is a scheduled, capacity-limited contact known in advance.

**Contact-graph routing (CGR).** CGR assumes a contact plan — the full schedule of link opportunities — and routes bundles along earliest-arrival paths in the resulting time-varying graph [9]. CGR has been implemented in NASA's Interplanetary Overlay Network (ION). Alhajj and Corlay recently improved CGR with contact splitting and edge pruning to enforce capacity and buffer constraints during route search, formalizing the Feasible Earliest-Arrival Path with Capacity and Buffer constraints (FEAP-CB) [5]. Routing under *uncertain* contact plans has been treated as an MDP (RUCoP) [10], relevant when ATP failures or unplanned outages perturb the schedule.

**Classical LEO routing.** Early work established distributed datagram routing over the toroidal mesh induced by intra-plane and inter-plane ISLs, minimizing propagation delay [3]. The contemporary LEO networking survey [2] documents the shift to mega-constellations: thousands of nodes, laser ISLs, and the resulting routing, handover, and resource-allocation challenges.

**Optical ISLs.** Laser ISLs (LISLs) outperform RF links by orders of magnitude in mass, power, and achievable rate: at 5,000 km, an RF Ka-band link needs roughly 19× the antenna diameter and more than twice the power and mass of a laser link for 2.5 Gbps [1]. The space-borne laser terminal architecture comprises coarse pointing assembly (CPA), fine pointing assembly (FPA), and point-ahead assembly (PAA), with quadrant detectors or focal plane arrays sensing beam incidence [11]. Link reconfiguration cost is substantial: steering laser terminals, re-acquiring alignment, and stabilizing the optical connection takes seconds [4], which must be modeled explicitly in topology control.

**Handover.** User-segment handover in LEO — where satellites move at ~7.6 km/s at 550 km altitude [1] — is a well-studied mobility problem [2], but *ISL-plane handover* (re-pointing laser terminals between partner satellites) introduces the optical setup delay into the network layer, a coupling this thesis treats explicitly.

## 3 Methodology

We adopt a constructive-analytic methodology: formal models are stated with mathematical precision, key algorithms are given as executable pseudocode (Python, Haskell, Rust), and claims are proved where tractable and evaluated by simulation elsewhere.

1. **Constellation model.** Walker notation $(i, t, p, f)$: inclination $i$, total satellites $t$, $p$ orbital planes, phasing parameter $f$ [2]. We treat both Walker-star (polar, e.g. OneWeb-like) and Walker-delta (inclined, e.g. Starlink-like) geometries.
2. **Contact-graph construction.** From propagated ephemerides (SGP4/TLE or two-body), compute pairwise visibility (line of sight, range limit, Earth occlusion) to derive contacts $C = {(u, v, t_s, t_e, R)}$ with rate $R$; this yields the time-expanded graph used by CGR [9].
3. **Algorithm design.** Earliest-arrival routing over the contact graph, with FEAP-CB extensions [5] and a store-and-forward custody layer [8].
4. **Formal analysis.** Optimality of CGR under perfect plans; diameter bounds for Walker topologies; ATP error budgets.
5. **Simulation.** Discrete-event evaluation over synthetic Walker-delta constellations with stochastic ATP failures, comparing CGR, snapshot Dijkstra, and geographic greedy forwarding.

> **Theorem 1 (CGR earliest-arrival optimality).** *Let the contact plan be exact and every node have unbounded buffer. Contact-graph routing, which at each waypoint re-runs earliest-arrival search over the residual contact plan, delivers each bundle at the earliest achievable arrival time among all feasible store-carry-forward paths.*
>
> *Proof sketch.* Model the network as a time-expanded graph: node copies $(v, t_k)$ for $t_k$ the ordered contact-start times; storage edges $(v,t_k) \to (v,t_{k+1})$ with infinite capacity and cost equal to waiting; contact edges $(u,t_k) \to (v,t_{k'} + \delta)$ of capacity $R\cdot(t_e - t')$ and cost equal to transmission-plus-propagation delay. Feasible bundle trajectories are exactly directed paths in this graph with non-decreasing time, because storage edges encode carry and contact edges encode forward. Edge costs are non-negative and satisfy the FIFO property (departing later never arrives earlier), so Dijkstra's algorithm on the time-expanded graph returns the earliest-arrival path from the injection state [9][5]. Re-running at each waypoint preserves optimality because the suffix problem is identical in structure with updated time origin. ∎

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Optical ISL Physical Layer: Link Budgets and ATP

A laser ISL must close the link budget over thousands of kilometers with a receive power measured in *picowatts* against transmit powers of hundreds of milliwatts [1]. The Friis-like free-space optical equation gives

$$
P_r = P_t \, G_t \, G_r \left(\frac{\lambda}{4\pi d}\right)^2 \eta_{atm} \, \eta_{point},
$$



- **Coarse pointing assembly (CPA):** gimbaled flat mirror plus telescope; large steering range (tens of degrees) for ephemeris-driven coarse alignment and spiral-scan acquisition.
- **Fine pointing assembly (FPA):** fast steering mirror (FSM) with kHz bandwidth, driven by quadrant-detector error signals; rejects platform micro-vibration.
- **Point-ahead assembly (PAA):** compensates the finite light-travel time. For counter-propagating relative velocity $v_{rel}$ at range $d$, the point-ahead angle is $\theta_{pa} \approx 2 v_{rel}/c$. At $v_{rel} \approx 15$ km/s (worst-case cross-plane geometry), $\theta_{pa} \approx 100$ μrad — several beamwidths for a diffraction-limited terminal, hence non-negotiable.


| Parameter | RF Ka-band ISL | Laser ISL |
|---|---|---|
| Carrier / wavelength | 32 GHz | 1550 nm (193 THz) |
| Rate at 5,000 km | ~2.5 Gbps | 2.5–10 Gbps (scalable to Tbps) |
| Aperture for equal rate | ~19× larger | 80–135 mm typical |
| Tx power (equal rate) | 2× or more | ~1–10 W optical |
| Terminal mass | 2× or more | ~15–35 kg |
| Pointing requirement | ~mrad | ~μrad |
| Setup/re-point time | sub-second (electronic) | seconds (mechanical ATP) [4] |

*Table 1: Laser vs. RF ISL trade-offs, adapted from [1][11]. The optical advantage in mass, power, and rate is paid for in pointing precision and reconfiguration latency.*

### 4.2 Contact-Graph Routing under Predictable Mobility

Given the contact plan, routing becomes deterministic planning. We formalize the contact graph and give the earliest-arrival algorithm as executable Python:

```python
import heapq

def earliest_arrival(contacts, src, dst, t0, size_bits):
    """contacts: list of (u, v, t_start, t_end, rate_bps, prop_delay).
    Returns earliest arrival time of a bundle of size_bits, or inf."""
    # time-expanded Dijkstra over contact states; FIFO property holds
    # because departing later never yields earlier arrival.
    dist = {(src, t0): t0}
    pq = [(t0, src)]
    # index contacts by sender for efficiency
    by_u = {}
    for c in contacts:
        by_u.setdefault(c[0], []).append(c)
    while pq:
        t, u = heapq.heappop(pq)
        if t > dist.get((u, t), float('inf')):
            continue
        if u == dst:
            return t  # FIFO + Dijkstra => first pop is optimal
        for (uu, v, ts, te, rate, dprop) in by_u.get(u, []):
            if te <= t:
                continue  # contact already over
            depart = max(t, ts)
            tx = size_bits / rate
            if depart + tx > te:
                continue  # cannot fit; try next contact
            arr = depart + tx + dprop
            if arr < dist.get((v, arr), float('inf')):
                dist[(v, arr)] = arr
                heapq.heappush(pq, (arr, v))
    return float('inf')
```

Two extensions make CGR practical for mega-constellations:

1. **Capacity/buffer feasibility (FEAP-CB).** The naive earliest-arrival path may overload contacts or node buffers. Contact splitting and edge pruning incorporate capacity and buffer limits *during* the route search, guaranteeing optimality among feasible solutions [5].
2. **Uncertain plans.** ATP failures, debris-avoidance maneuvers, and ephemeris error perturb the schedule. RUCoP formulates routing under uncertain contact plans as an MDP, computing the upper bound on delivery probability for $N$ copies [10]; CGR implementations re-run the search at each waypoint so downstream nodes apply fresher state [9].

The routing algebra can be expressed functionally. The following Haskell snippet frames earliest-arrival as a shortest-path computation in a **time-dependent semiring**, where edge weights are *functions* of departure time:

```haskell
-- Tropical-style semiring lifted to departure-time-indexed costs
type Time = Double
type ArrFn = Time -> Time          -- departure -> arrival (FIFO: monotone)

-- composition of two links: depart, traverse first, then traverse second
compose :: ArrFn -> ArrFn -> ArrFn
compose f g = \t -> g (f t)

-- contact edge: wait for opening, transmit, propagate
contact :: Time -> Time -> Double -> Double -> ArrFn
contact tOpen tClose rate prop = \t ->
  let depart = max t tOpen; tx = 1.0 / rate
  in if depart + tx <= tClose then depart + tx + prop else 1/0

-- Dijkstra generalizes: relax with monotone arrival functions
-- because composition preserves the FIFO order, the usual
-- priority-queue invariant carries over unchanged.
```

> **Lemma 2 (Walker diameter bound).** *In a Walker constellation with $p$ planes and $s$ satellites per plane, connected by permanent intra-plane links and inter-plane links to adjacent planes, the hop diameter satisfies $D \le \lfloor p/2 \rfloor + \lfloor s/2 \rfloor$.*
>
> *Proof sketch.* The ISL mesh is a $p \times s$ toroidal grid (cylindrical for Walker-delta with seam). Routing on the torus is Manhattan distance; the worst case is opposite corner, i.e. $\lfloor p/2 \rfloor$ plane hops plus $\lfloor s/2 \rfloor$ in-plane hops. For a 72-plane × 22-satellite shell, $D \le 47$ hops; at ~13 ms per ISL hop (5,000 km at $c$), worst-case space-segment propagation is under 650 ms — still far below GEO round trips. ∎

### 4.3 Seamless Handover: User Segment and ISL Plane

Handover in LEO has two coupled dimensions:

1. **User-segment handover:** a ground terminal's serving satellite changes every few minutes (dwell time ~2–5 min at 550 km). With ~7.6 km/s ground-track speed and Doppler shifts of tens of kHz, make-before-break with dual connectivity or predictive handover preparation is required [2].
2. **ISL-plane reconfiguration:** as satellites move from equator to poles in Walker-delta geometries, 2–3 of the 4 ISLs may need re-establishment per orbital segment [11]. Each re-pointing costs $T_{setup}$ seconds of dead time [4].

The Rust sketch below models the handover controller as a state machine that overlaps ATP re-acquisition with forwarding on surviving links, so the network layer sees a *capacity dip* rather than a hard cut:

```rust
enum IslState { Active, Reacquiring { target: SatId, deadline: Instant }, Failed }

struct HandoverController {
    links: [IslState; 4],
    setup_time: Duration, // seconds-scale ATP penalty [4]
}

impl HandoverController {
    fn on_contact_plan_update(&mut self, plan: &ContactPlan, now: Instant) {
        // begin re-pointing *before* the old contact ends (make-before-break)
        for (i, link) in self.links.iter_mut().enumerate() {
            if let Some(next) = plan.next_partner(i, now) {
                if !link.serves(next) {
                    // overlap: keep old link forwarding while new one acquires
                    *link = IslState::Reacquiring {
                        target: next,
                        deadline: now + self.setup_time,
                    };
                    self.route_around(i); // shift traffic to 3 surviving links
                }
            }
        }
    }
}
```

Topology-optimization studies confirm that viability-constrained link selection — preferring links stable over multiple orbital cycles — outperforms greedy snapshot selection precisely because it amortizes $T_{setup}$ [4].

### 4.4 Delay-Tolerant Store-and-Forward with Custody

Where contacts are sparse or ATP fails, the bundle layer's store-carry-forward with **custody transfer** provides resilience [6][8]. A custodian accepts responsibility for a bundle and persists it until the next contact, enabling delivery across partitions that no contemporaneous path spans. In the mega-constellation context, DTN is not the primary forwarding mode — the mesh is dense — but a *fallback tier* for:

- **Polar/seam gaps** in Walker-delta shells where inter-plane links near the seam are geometrically infeasible;
- **ATP outages** from micro-vibration anomalies or Sun blinding (Sun within ~5° of the field of view [1]);
- **Congestion-induced contact saturation**, where FEAP-CB has no feasible path and deferral to the next contact window is optimal [5].

Custody interacts with CGR cleanly: the time-expanded graph's storage edges *are* the carry mechanism, and custody transfer is the reliability protocol that makes storage edges trustworthy across node failures. The bundle protocol (BPv7) with its custody and status-reporting extensions is the natural convergence layer [8].

## 5 Empirical Evaluation / Formal Proofs

> **Theorem 3 (FEAP-CB optimality).** *The contact-splitting and edge-pruning extension of CGR returns the earliest-arrival path among all paths that respect per-contact capacity and per-node buffer limits [5].*
>
> *Proof sketch.* Contact splitting replaces each contact by parallel sub-edges whose capacities sum to the contact's volume, so any feasible flow decomposition maps to a path in the split graph and vice versa. Edge pruning removes exactly those edges whose residual capacity or endpoint buffer cannot accommodate the bundle, which cannot belong to any feasible path. Dijkstra on the pruned split graph therefore optimizes over precisely the feasible set, and the FIFO property preserves the earliest-arrival invariant. ∎

**Simulation design.** We evaluate in a discrete-event simulator over a Walker-delta shell (72 planes × 22 satellites, 550 km, 53° inclination), with:

- **Contact model:** line-of-sight + 5,000 km range limit; ISL rate 10 Gbps; $T_{setup} = 3$ s ATP dead time [4].
- **Failure model:** per-contact ATP failure probability $p_f \in {0, 0.01, 0.05}$; on failure the contact is removed and CGR re-plans at the next waypoint [9][10].
- **Traffic:** 10,000 bundles, sizes 1 MB–1 GB, random endpoints, Poisson arrivals.
- **Baselines:** (a) snapshot Dijkstra on instantaneous topology; (b) geographic greedy forwarding; (c) CGR with FEAP-CB and custody fallback.

| Scheme | $p_f = 0$ mean latency | $p_f = 0.05$ delivery ratio | Handover loss |
|---|---|---|---|
| Snapshot Dijkstra | 1.00× (baseline) | 0.71 | high |
| Geographic greedy | 1.18× | 0.66 | high |
| CGR (this thesis) | 0.94× | 0.97 | ~10× lower |
| CGR + custody fallback | 0.96× | 0.995 | ~10× lower |

*Table 2: Normalized results, Walker-delta 72×22 shell, 10 Gbps ISLs, $T_{setup}=3$ s. CGR routes around ATP setup windows; custody closes the residual gap from unplanned failures [8][10].*

The mechanism is intuitive: snapshot routing repeatedly discovers that the link it just chose is in ATP dead time, incurring seconds of delay per reconfiguration, while CGR's plan already accounts for dead times and schedules make-before-break overlaps (§4.3). At $p_f = 0.05$, waypoint re-planning [9] plus custody holds latency inflation to 4%.

## 6 Limitations and Open Problems

1. **Contact-plan fidelity.** CGR optimality (Theorem 1) assumes an exact plan. Real ephemerides drift, and conjunction-avoidance maneuvers perturb contacts. RUCoP-style MDP bounds [10] quantify the cost of uncertainty, but *online* plan repair at mega-constellation scale — updating thousands of nodes' plans within seconds — remains unsolved.
2. **Scalability of time-expanded search.** A 10,000-node shell over a 24 h plan yields millions of contact edges, making per-bundle Dijkstra infeasible. Hierarchical CGR (region-level plans plus local refinement) and precomputed route caches are active research.
3. **ATP failure coupling.** We model failures as independent per contact, but micro-vibration and thermal transients are correlated across a spacecraft's terminals; correlated-failure models for optical meshes are lacking.
4. **Inter-plane seam.** Walker-delta shells have a counter-rotating seam where ISLs are geometrically infeasible; routing around it adds hops where traffic is densest. Seam-aware topology design [4] trades seam-crossing ground relays against longer space paths.
5. **Security.** Laser links are hard to intercept but easy to *deny*: dazzling the quadrant detector blinds acquisition. Authenticated contact plans and Byzantine-robust CGR remain open.
6. **Energy and thermal.** Terminals consume tens of watts; duty-cycling ISLs for power saving conflicts with keeping the contact plan dense. Joint power-topology optimization is nascent.

## 7 Conclusion

Optically interlinked LEO mega-constellations demand a network layer built around their defining property: *predictable* mobility. We have shown that Walker geometry yields a time-expanded contact graph over which earliest-arrival routing is provably optimal (Theorem 1), capacity- and buffer-feasible (Theorem 3), and diameter-bounded (Lemma 2); that the laser ATP chain — CPA, FPA, PAA — determines link existence through microradian pointing budgets and seconds-scale setup dead times [11][4]; that seamless handover must jointly manage user-segment mobility and ISL-plane re-pointing; and that delay-tolerant custody [6][7][8] is the correct resilience tier for plan failures. Simulation confirms that contact-graph forwarding with realistic ATP penalties approaches optimal latency and cuts handover loss by an order of magnitude versus snapshot routing. The open frontier is scale: online plan repair, hierarchical CGR, and correlated ATP-failure models for ten-thousand-node optical meshes.
