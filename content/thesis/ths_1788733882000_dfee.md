# 3D Gaussian Splatting: Differentiable Rasterization, Spherical Harmonics, and Real-Time Neural Rendering

## Abstract

The advent of Neural Radiance Fields [2] demonstrated that photorealistic novel-view synthesis could be achieved by optimizing a continuous volumetric function with only posed multi-view images as supervision. Yet the canonical NeRF formulation couples quality to expensive neural-network queries along every camera ray, foreclosing real-time rendering of unbounded scenes at display resolutions. This thesis presents a rigorous exposition of **3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS)** [1], the representation and differentiable rasterization pipeline that broke this trade-off: scenes are modeled as collections of anisotropic 3D Gaussians with learnable position, covariance, opacity, and spherical-harmonic appearance, projected to screen space through the linearized EWA mapping and composited by a tile-based, depth-sorted alpha-blending rasterizer. We derive the parameterization and its gradients, analyze adaptive density control as a structural optimization procedure, examine the spherical-harmonics view-dependence model, and survey the principal successor formalisms — anti-aliased Mip-Splatting [3], structured Scaffold-GS [4], surface-consistent 2D Gaussian Splatting [5], and temporally extended 4D Gaussian Splatting [6] — together with their empirical results, failure modes, and open problems.

---

## 1 Introduction

Novel-view synthesis — rendering unseen viewpoints of a scene from a sparse set of captured photographs — is one of the oldest problems in computer vision and graphics, and one of the most demanding: a convincing solution must jointly infer *geometry*, *reflectance*, and *illumination* from projective observations alone, then re-render them under arbitrary camera motion at interactive rates. For decades, the field oscillated between two poles. Explicit geometric reconstructions (meshes, point clouds, depth maps) render quickly but struggle with view-dependent effects such as specularities and with the topological complexity of real scenes. Implicit volumetric models, culminating in NeRF [2], model the scene as a continuous 5D function — volume density and emitted radiance as a function of position and viewing direction — and achieve striking photorealism, but at the cost of hundreds of neural-network evaluations per pixel and rendering times measured in seconds to minutes per frame.

3D Gaussian Splatting [1], introduced by Kerbl, Kopanas, Leimkühler, and Drettakis at SIGGRAPH 2023, occupies a third position that had been hiding in plain sight: a *semi-explicit, primitive-based volumetric representation* coupled to a *fully differentiable rasterizer*. The scene is a set of 3D Gaussians — soft ellipsoidal primitives that preserve the desirable optimization properties of continuous volumetric fields while contributing nothing in empty space. Because each primitive has compact screen-space support after projection, the renderer can abandon ray marching entirely and instead **splat** primitives onto the image in front-to-back depth order, accumulating color with the standard alpha-compositing equations of volume rendering. The result is state-of-the-art quality on the Mip-NeRF360 benchmark (PSNR 27.21 dB) with training in roughly 51 minutes and rendering at over 100 frames per second at 1080p resolution on commodity GPUs — the first method to deliver high-fidelity radiance-field rendering in genuine real time for unbounded, complete scenes [1].

This thesis develops the full technical account. We begin with the mathematical background (Section 2): the NeRF rendering model it generalizes, and the lineage of point-based volume splatting from which its projection theory descends. Section 3 presents the method proper: the anisotropic Gaussian parameterization, the linearized projective mapping, the tile-based differentiable rasterizer, spherical-harmonics appearance, and adaptive density control. Section 4 deepens each component with derivations, implementation detail, and complexity analysis. Section 5 pairs benchmark evidence with formal statements and proof sketches. Section 6 catalogues the method's limitations honestly, and Section 7 concludes with the research frontier.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Neural Radiance Fields

Mildenhall et al. [2] model a static scene as a function

$$F_\Theta : (\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{d}) \mapsto (\mathbf{c}, \sigma)$$

mapping a 3D position $\mathbf{x}$ and viewing direction $\mathbf{d}$ to emitted radiance $\mathbf{c}$ and volume density $\sigma$, approximated by a multilayer perceptron with sinusoidal positional encoding. A pixel's color is the classical volume-rendering integral along its ray $\mathbf{r}(t) = \mathbf{o} + t\mathbf{d}$:

$$C(\mathbf{r}) = \int_{t_n}^{t_f} T(t)\,\sigma(\mathbf{r}(t))\,\mathbf{c}(\mathbf{r}(t), \mathbf{d})\,dt, \qquad T(t) = \exp\!\left(-\int_{t_n}^{t} \sigma(\mathbf{r}(s))\,ds\right)$$

discretized by quadrature over stratified samples. Because the integral is differentiable in $\Theta$, the network is trainable from photometric loss alone. The cost, however, is severe: *every pixel requires dozens to hundreds of MLP evaluations*, and empty space is evaluated as diligently as occupied space. Follow-ups accelerated training (hash grids, tensor factorizations) but rendering remained far from real time for high-resolution unbounded scenes — the gap 3DGS would close.

### 2.2 The Splatting Lineage

Long before neural fields, point-based graphics confronted the same tension between fidelity and speed. Elliptical Weighted Average (EWA) volume splatting (Zwicker, Pfister, van Baar, and Gross, 2001) showed how a 3D reconstruction kernel could be projected to screen space by linearizing the projective mapping: the projected 2D covariance is $\Sigma' = J W \Sigma W^T J^T$, where $W$ is the viewing transformation and $J$ the Jacobian of the projective transform at the kernel center. This formula — affine approximation of perspective — is the geometric heart of 3DGS. Differentiable rasterizers for meshes and points later showed that gradient-based optimization could flow through visibility and projection; 3DGS extends this to *volumetric alpha compositing* over thousands of overlapping soft primitives, which is what allows photometric gradients to sculpt continuous radiance rather than hard surfaces [1].

### 2.3 Why Gaussians

A 3D Gaussian primitive is simultaneously:

- **Locally supported in practice** — its influence decays exponentially, so screen-space footprints are bounded and tile-cullable;
- **Analytically projectable** — affine maps preserve Gaussianity, so projection reduces to the $J W \Sigma W^T J^T$ update;
- **Anisotropic** — elongated, oriented ellipsoids can model thin structures (wires, leaves, edges) with a single primitive where isotropic blobs would need dozens;
- **Differentiable everywhere** — every parameter admits analytic or automatic gradients through projection and compositing.

These properties make Gaussians the natural volumetric analogue of the triangle: a primitive simple enough to rasterize, expressive enough to approximate arbitrary radiance fields when used in large numbers.

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 Scene Representation

A scene is a set of $N$ Gaussians (typically $10^5$–$10^7$ after optimization), each fully described by:

| Parameter | Symbol | Dimension | Role |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mean (position) | $\boldsymbol{\mu}$ | 3 | Primitive center in world space |
| Scaling | $\mathbf{s}$ | 3 | Axis lengths of the ellipsoid |
| Rotation | $\mathbf{q}$ | 4 | Unit quaternion orienting the ellipsoid |
| Opacity | $o$ | 1 | Scalar multiplier on the kernel response |
| SH coefficients | $\mathbf{k}$ | 48 | Degree-3 spherical harmonics, RGB |

The covariance is reconstructed as $\Sigma = R S S^T R^T$, where $R$ is the rotation matrix from $\mathbf{q}$ and $S = \mathrm{diag}(\mathbf{s})$. This factorization guarantees positive semi-definiteness under unconstrained gradient descent — a decisive advantage over optimizing $\Sigma$ directly, which would require projection onto the PSD cone at every step. The unnormalized Gaussian response at $\mathbf{x}$ is

$$G(\mathbf{x}) = \exp\!\left(-\tfrac{1}{2}(\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu})^T \Sigma^{-1} (\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu})\right).$$

Initialization seeds Gaussians at the sparse Structure-from-Motion point cloud produced during camera calibration (COLMAP), with isotropic covariances derived from nearest-neighbor distances and zero-order SH set from point colors [1]. The method thus begins from genuine geometric evidence rather than random initialization.

### 3.2 Differentiable Projection

For a camera with viewing matrix $W$ (world-to-camera) and projective mapping, each Gaussian is mapped to screen space by the EWA linearization. Let $J$ be the Jacobian of the projective transform evaluated at $W\boldsymbol{\mu}$; then

$$\Sigma' = J\,W\,\Sigma\,W^T J^T$$

is the $2\times 2$ projected covariance, and $\boldsymbol{\mu}'$ the projected center. The affine approximation is exact for orthographic cameras and accurate for perspective cameras whenever the Gaussian is small relative to its distance from the camera — the overwhelmingly common case — with error growing only for very large, near-camera primitives.

### 3.3 Tile-Based Rasterization and Compositing

Rendering proceeds in three GPU stages:

1. **Culling and projection.** Gaussians outside the frustum are discarded; the rest are projected and their 2D extents computed.
2. **Tiling and sorting.** The screen is divided into $16 \times 16$ tiles. Each Gaussian is duplicated into the tiles its footprint touches, keyed by tile ID and view-space depth; a single global radix sort orders all (tile, depth) pairs.
3. **Alpha blending.** Within a tile, pixels accumulate primitives front-to-back:

$$C = \sum_{i=1}^{M} \mathbf{c}_i\,\alpha_i \prod_{j=1}^{i-1}(1-\alpha_j), \qquad \alpha_i = o_i \cdot G'(\mathbf{p})$$

where $G'(\mathbf{p})$ is the projected 2D Gaussian response at pixel $\mathbf{p}$ and $\mathbf{c}_i$ the view-dependent color. This is precisely the discretized volume-rendering equation — the transmittance product $\prod(1-\alpha_j)$ is the discrete analogue of NeRF's $T(t)$ [2] — so 3DGS inherits the volumetric semantics of radiance fields while evaluating them by rasterization rather than ray marching.

Crucially, every stage is differentiable: gradients flow from pixels back through $\alpha_i$ to opacity, covariance, and position, and through $\mathbf{c}_i$ to the SH coefficients. The backward pass re-walks the sorted primitive lists per tile, accumulating gradients in shared memory; the sort itself is treated as constant (a straight-through approximation justified because infinitesimal parameter changes do not reorder primitives almost everywhere).

### 3.4 Spherical-Harmonics Appearance

View-dependent color is modeled with real spherical harmonics to degree $L=3$:

$$\mathbf{c}(\mathbf{d}) = \sum_{l=0}^{3}\sum_{m=-l}^{l} \mathbf{k}_l^m\, Y_l^m(\mathbf{d})$$

with $16$ coefficients per channel ($48$ floats per Gaussian). SH bases are orthonormal on the sphere, low-frequency by construction, and cheap to evaluate — a degree-3 expansion captures diffuse and glossy variation while remaining smooth enough to optimize stably from sparse views. Training begins with only the DC term ($l=0$) active and raises the degree every 1000 iterations, a coarse-to-fine schedule that prevents high-frequency SH components from fitting noise early in optimization [1].

### 3.5 Adaptive Density Control

A fixed primitive budget cannot represent scenes of unknown complexity, so 3DGS interleaves optimization with structural surgery every 100 iterations, driven by the magnitude of view-space positional gradients:

- **Under-reconstruction** (small Gaussians, large gradients): *clone* the Gaussian, displacing the copy along the gradient direction — filling holes.
- **Over-reconstruction** (large Gaussians, large gradients): *split* the Gaussian into two smaller ones sampled from the parent's distribution with scaled covariance — resolving detail.
- **Pruning**: remove Gaussians with opacity below $\epsilon_\alpha$, or with excessive world-space or screen-space extent.
- **Opacity reset**: every 3000 iterations, opacities near 1 are damped toward 0, preventing primitives from saturating and blocking gradient flow to geometry behind them.

This procedure is the method's answer to topology discovery: where NeRF grows detail by allocating network capacity implicitly, 3DGS grows it by explicit primitive birth and death, guided by the same photometric gradients that tune the continuous parameters.

### 3.6 Optimization

The loss combines $\mathcal{L}_1$ photometric error with a D-SSIM term,

$$\mathcal{L} = (1-\lambda)\,\mathcal{L}_1 + \lambda\,\mathcal{L}_{\text{D-SSIM}}, \qquad \lambda = 0.2,$$

optimized with Adam for 30,000 iterations. Densification runs from iteration 500 to 15,000; SH degree ramps to 3 by iteration 3000.

```python
# Simplified per-Gaussian forward pass (conceptual)
def splat(gaussian, W, J, pixel):
    mu_cam = W @ gaussian.mu                      # to camera space
    mu_2d  = project(mu_cam)                      # to screen space
    cov_3d = quat_to_mat(gaussian.q) @ diag(gaussian.s**2) @ quat_to_mat(gaussian.q).T
    cov_2d = J @ W[:3,:3] @ cov_3d @ W[:3,:3].T @ J.T   # EWA projection
    d = pixel - mu_2d
    response = exp(-0.5 * d @ inv(cov_2d) @ d)     # 2D Gaussian falloff
    color = eval_sh(gaussian.sh, view_dir)         # degree-3 SH
    return color, gaussian.opacity * response      # (c_i, alpha_i)
```

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Covariance Parameterization, in Full

The choice $\Sigma = RSS^TR^T$ deserves scrutiny, because it is doing three jobs at once. *First*, it enforces validity: any $(\mathbf{q}, \mathbf{s})$ with $\mathbf{s} > 0$ yields a valid covariance, so gradient descent never leaves the manifold of legal primitives. *Second*, it disentangles orientation from extent, which matters for optimization dynamics — rotating an elongated Gaussian and stretching it are independent operations with independent gradient paths, avoiding the ill-conditioning of direct covariance optimization where the two are entangled. *Third*, it admits a clean gradient: with $R$ from the quaternion via the standard quadratic map, $\partial \Sigma / \partial \mathbf{s}$ and $\partial \Sigma / \partial \mathbf{q}$ are elementary, and autograd handles the rest. Positivity of $\mathbf{s}$ is maintained by optimizing in log-space ($\mathbf{s} = \exp(\tilde{\mathbf{s}})$) or by activation clamping.

> **Theorem:** *(EWA projection preserves Gaussianity.)* Let $G(\mathbf{x}) = \exp(-\tfrac12(\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu})^T\Sigma^{-1}(\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu}))$ and let $\phi$ be an affine map $\phi(\mathbf{x}) = A\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{t}$ with $\det A \neq 0$. Then $G \circ \phi^{-1}$ is Gaussian with mean $A\boldsymbol{\mu}+\mathbf{t}$ and covariance $A\Sigma A^T$.
>
> *Proof sketch.* Substitute $\mathbf{y} = A\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{t}$ into the exponent: $(\phi^{-1}(\mathbf{y})-\boldsymbol{\mu})^T\Sigma^{-1}(\phi^{-1}(\mathbf{y})-\boldsymbol{\mu}) = (\mathbf{y}-A\boldsymbol{\mu}-\mathbf{t})^T(A\Sigma A^T)^{-1}(\mathbf{y}-A\boldsymbol{\mu}-\mathbf{t})$, using $(A\Sigma A^T)^{-1} = A^{-T}\Sigma^{-1}A^{-1}$. The perspective projection is not affine, but its first-order Taylor expansion at $W\boldsymbol{\mu}$ is; applying the theorem to $A = JW$ yields exactly the $\Sigma' = JW\Sigma W^TJ^T$ update, with approximation error of order $O(\|\Sigma\| / z^2)$ for a primitive of extent $\|\Sigma\|^{1/2}$ at depth $z$. ∎

### 4.2 The Tile Rasterizer: Why It Is Fast

The rasterizer's speed rests on three design decisions that jointly convert an $O(N \cdot P)$ problem into something near $O(P \log P + K)$:

1. **Tiles bound the work.** A Gaussian touches only the tiles overlapped by its $3\sigma$ ellipse; empty tiles cost nothing, and per-tile primitive lists are short.
2. **One global sort.** Rather than sorting per pixel, all (tile, depth) keys are sorted once with radix sort; each tile then processes a contiguous, pre-sorted segment.
3. **Early termination.** Front-to-back accumulation stops a pixel's walk once transmittance $\prod(1-\alpha_j)$ falls below $10^{-4}$ — saturated pixels skip the remaining primitives.

The backward pass is the mirror image: per-tile, per-pixel gradient accumulation over the same sorted lists, with atomic-free shared-memory reduction within thread blocks. The authors' CUDA implementation renders 1080p frames at well over 100 FPS on scenes with millions of Gaussians [1].

```cuda
// Conceptual per-pixel front-to-back loop (one thread per pixel)
float T = 1.0f; float3 C = {0,0,0};
for (int k = tile_start; k < tile_end && T > 1e-4f; ++k) {
    Gaussian g = sorted[k];
    float  a = g.opacity * gaussian2D(pixel, g.mu2d, g.cov2d);
    float3 c = evalSH(g.sh, viewDir);
    C += T * a * c;      // accumulate premultiplied contribution
    T *= (1.0f - a);     // update transmittance
}
```

### 4.3 Spherical Harmonics: Capacity and Its Price

Degree-3 SH provides $16$ basis functions per channel — enough to represent smooth view-dependent effects (soft specular lobes, Fresnel-ish brightening) but *provably incapable* of sharp, high-frequency reflectance such as mirror reflections or crisp highlights. This is a deliberate trade: SH evaluation is a handful of fused multiply-adds, while an MLP appearance head (as in some successors) costs orders of magnitude more per primitive. The 48 floats of SH dominate per-Gaussian storage (roughly $48/59 \approx 81\%$ of the parameter count), which is why compression research targets SH coefficients first. The coarse-to-fine degree schedule is not cosmetic: activating high-order bands too early lets them memorize view-specific artifacts that then resist correction, a phenomenon analogous to spectral bias in neural networks.

### 4.4 Adaptive Density Control as Structural Search

Adaptive density control can be read as a greedy, gradient-guided architecture search over primitive count and placement. The positional-gradient criterion is well-motivated: $\|\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\mu}} \mathcal{L}\|$ is large precisely where moving mass would reduce photometric error — i.e., where the current discretization is inadequate. Cloning vs. splitting is decided by the Gaussian's screen-space extent: small primitives tile holes by duplication, large ones refine detail by subdivision. The opacity reset is the subtle masterstroke — without it, early-converged foreground Gaussians saturate ($\alpha \to 1$) and permanently occlude the gradients that would build the geometry behind them, a failure mode the authors term "gradient starvation." Empirically, density control roughly triples the primitive count from initialization to convergence while monotonically improving validation PSNR [1].

### 4.5 Successor Formalisms

The core method's very success made its approximations impossible to ignore, and four successor lines are now canonical:

- **Mip-Splatting [3]** diagnoses *aliasing*: 3DGS has no 3D frequency constraint and applies a 2D dilation filter in screen space, so changing the sampling rate (zooming, focal-length shifts) produces dilation artifacts and high-frequency shimmer. The fix is a 3D smoothing filter that bounds each Gaussian's extent by the maximal sampling frequency induced by the training views, plus a 2D Mip filter approximating a box filter — the splatting analogue of mipmapping. It won Best Student Paper at CVPR 2024 and markedly improves SSIM under multi-scale evaluation.
- **Scaffold-GS [4]** diagnoses *redundancy and structure-neglect*: vanilla 3DGS lets millions of Gaussians drift freely, fitting every training view while ignoring scene geometry, which hurts robustness to large viewpoint changes. Anchors on a sparse voxel grid tether sets of *neural* Gaussians whose attributes are predicted on-the-fly by small MLPs from anchor features and viewing direction/distance, with anchor growing and pruning for coverage. Storage collapses (only anchors and MLPs are stored) while quality matches or exceeds 3DGS at $\sim$100 FPS.
- **2D Gaussian Splatting [5]** diagnoses *geometric inconsistency*: 3D Gaussians are volumetric blobs whose "surface" is view-dependent, so extracted geometry is noisy. Collapsing primitives to 2D oriented planar disks (surfels) with perspective-accurate ray-splat intersection, plus depth-distortion and normal-consistency regularization, yields view-consistent normals and depth — and clean meshes via TSDF fusion — at real-time rates (SIGGRAPH 2024).
- **4D Gaussian Splatting [6]** extends the formalism to *time*: a HexPlane-inspired decomposed 4D neural-voxel encoding feeds a lightweight MLP that predicts per-Gaussian deformations (position, rotation, scale) at novel timestamps, achieving 82 FPS at $800 \times 800$ on an RTX 3090 for dynamic scenes (CVPR 2024).

## 5 Empirical Results and Formal Proofs

### 5.1 Benchmark Evidence

On the Mip-NeRF360 dataset (9 unbounded indoor/outdoor scenes), 3DGS at 30k iterations reports **PSNR 27.21 dB, SSIM 0.815, LPIPS 0.214** with ~51 minutes of training — slightly exceeding Mip-NeRF360's own quality while training in a fraction of the time and rendering in real time, where Mip-NeRF360 requires seconds per frame [1]. On Tanks & Temples and Deep Blending the pattern repeats: quality at or above the neural-field state of the art, with rendering throughput two to three orders of magnitude higher.

| Method | Mip-NeRF360 PSNR ↑ | SSIM ↑ | LPIPS ↓ | Train time | Render FPS (1080p) |

### 5.2 Formal Properties

> **Theorem:** *(Compositing equivalence.)* For sorted primitives with opacities $\alpha_i$ and colors $\mathbf{c}_i$, the front-to-back sum $C = \sum_i \mathbf{c}_i \alpha_i \prod_{j<i}(1-\alpha_j)$ equals the back-to-front over-operator composition $C = \mathbf{c}_1 \,\mathrm{over}\, \mathbf{c}_2 \,\mathrm{over}\, \cdots$, and in the limit of infinitesimal primitives converges to the continuous volume-rendering integral of [2].
>
> *Proof sketch.* The over operator is associative; expanding $\mathbf{c}_1$ over $(\mathbf{c}_2$ over $\cdots)$ yields the front-to-back sum by induction on $M$. For the continuum limit, identify $\alpha_i = 1 - \exp(-\sigma_i \delta_i)$; then $\prod_{j<i}(1-\alpha_j) = \exp(-\sum_{j<i}\sigma_j\delta_j) \to T(t)$ as $\max \delta_i \to 0$, recovering NeRF's transmittance and the integral $C(\mathbf{r})$. ∎

> **Theorem:** *(Gradient flow through sorting.)* Let the rendered pixel be a function of primitive parameters with the sort order held fixed at the order induced by current depths. Then the function is differentiable almost everywhere in parameter space, and its gradient equals the gradient of the true sorted rendering except on the measure-zero set where two primitives are at exactly equal depth.
>
> *Proof sketch.* For fixed order, the rendering is a composition of smooth maps (projection, Gaussian response, affine compositing), hence smooth. The sort order changes only when a pairwise depth comparison flips — a codimension-1 event in parameter space. At such crossings the left and right limits of the rendered value agree (swapping two equal-depth primitives leaves the sum invariant), so the function is continuous, and nondifferentiability is confined to the union of these hypersurfaces, which has Lebesgue measure zero. ∎

These two results are the formal license for the whole enterprise: the rasterizer computes the same integral NeRF computes, and backpropagating through a fixed sort order is valid almost everywhere.

## 6 Limitations

A candid accounting must record where the formalism strains:

1. **Memory footprint.** Millions of Gaussians at 59 floats each consume hundreds of megabytes per scene (often 500 MB–1 GB for Mip-NeRF360 scenes), dominated by SH coefficients. This motivated the compression literature and Scaffold-GS's anchor design [4].
2. **View-dependent limits.** Degree-3 SH cannot represent mirrors, sharp speculars, or complex interreflection; scenes with such materials show characteristic blurring of highlights.
3. **Aliasing.** The original formulation's 2D dilation and absent 3D frequency control produce zoom-dependent artifacts — precisely the gap Mip-Splatting [3] closes.
4. **Geometry extraction.** Volumetric blobs have no well-defined surface; mesh extraction from 3DGS is noisy, which 2DGS [5] addresses by design.
5. **Unbounded and thin structure.** Very large scenes still strain primitive budgets; extremely thin geometry can fall between Gaussians; and the affine projection approximation degrades for huge near-camera primitives.
6. **Dynamic scenes.** The static formulation has no temporal model; extensions like 4D-GS [6] add deformation fields at the cost of extra capacity and training complexity.
7. **Optimization fragility.** Poor SfM initialization, textureless regions, and reflective surfaces can trap adaptive density control in local minima, producing floater artifacts — semi-transparent Gaussians hovering in free space.

## 7 Conclusion

3D Gaussian Splatting [1] reframes neural rendering as *differentiable rasterization of volumetric primitives*, and in doing so dissolves the long-standing opposition between the speed of explicit representations and the quality of implicit ones. Its technical contributions — the $RSS^TR^T$ covariance parameterization, the EWA projective mapping, the tile-based sorted alpha-blending rasterizer with straight-through sort gradients, the coarse-to-fine spherical-harmonics appearance model, and gradient-guided adaptive density control — compose into a system that trains in under an hour and renders at triple-digit frame rates without conceding photorealism. The successor literature has been equally instructive: each major follow-up [3][4][5][6] can be read as a diagnosis of one approximation in the original pipeline (aliasing, redundancy, geometric inconsistency, staticity) and a principled correction of it. The open frontier now lies in unifying these corrections — alias-free, structured, surface-consistent, temporally deformable Gaussian scene representations — within a single real-time differentiable renderer, and in pushing primitive counts and scene scales toward city-scale capture. If NeRF [2] taught the field that a scene could be a function, 3DGS taught it that the function could be rasterized.
