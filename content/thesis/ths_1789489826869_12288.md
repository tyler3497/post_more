# Climate Sensitivity: Radiative Feedback Analysis, Emergent Constraints, and Earth System Model Ensembles

## Abstract

Equilibrium climate sensitivity (ECS), the eventual global-mean surface warming per doubling of atmospheric carbon dioxide, is the single most consequential number in climate science, governing the long-term commitment of the Earth system to anthropogenic forcing. This thesis traces the evolution of its quantification from the Charney panel's 1979 assessment of 3°C with a 1.5–4.5°C range through the modern energy-balance paradigm of Gregory and collaborators, the radiative kernel decomposition of feedback processes championed by Soden and colleagues, and the emergence of observational constraints grounded in fluctuation–dissipation theory. We analyze why the CMIP5 and CMIP6 model ensembles produce divergent ECS spreads, explain the pattern effect that separates effective from equilibrium sensitivity, and show how paleoclimate evidence from the Last Glacial Maximum bounds the upper tail of the distribution. Finally, we review the Bayesian multi-evidence synthesis of Sherwood and colleagues and the IPCC Sixth Assessment Report, which together narrowed the likely ECS range to approximately 2.5–4°C with a best estimate near 3°C, and discuss the structural uncertainties that remain.

## 1 Introduction

Few quantities in the physical sciences have resisted convergence as stubbornly, or mattered as much for policy, as the equilibrium climate sensitivity (ECS). Defined as the global-mean surface temperature change achieved once the climate system has equilibrated following a doubling of atmospheric CO₂ concentration, ECS condenses the full radiative, thermodynamic, and dynamical machinery of the Earth system into a single number. In 1979, an ad hoc panel chaired by Jule Charney concluded that this number probably lay between 1.5°C and 4.5°C, with a best estimate near 3°C [1]. Remarkably, that range survived nearly four decades of advances in observations, theory, and numerical modeling, appearing in successive IPCC assessments with only modest modification. The persistence of the Charney range was not a symptom of scientific stagnation; rather, it reflected a genuine tension between independent lines of evidence — process understanding of feedbacks, the historical instrumental record, and paleoclimate reconstructions — each of which pulled the estimate in slightly different directions and each of which carried irreducible uncertainties [8].

The last decade has witnessed a genuine narrowing of this range, driven less by any single breakthrough than by the systematic combination of evidence streams under a coherent probabilistic framework. Three developments proved decisive. First, the *Gregory method* — a regression-based diagnosis of forcing and feedback from the transient response of coupled models — provided a uniform language for comparing sensitivity across models and experiments [2]. Second, the *radiative kernel technique* made it possible to attribute the global feedback parameter to individual physical processes — Planck, water vapor, lapse rate, surface albedo, and clouds — in a way that is consistent across models [3]. Third, *emergent constraints* exploited relationships between observable aspects of present-day variability and long-term sensitivity, offering empirical anchors that models alone cannot provide [5]. The culmination of these efforts, the 2020 multi-author assessment led by Steven Sherwood and the subsequent IPCC Sixth Assessment Report, delivered a likely ECS range of roughly 2.5–4°C with a best estimate of 3°C — a substantially tighter constraint that lifted the lower bound decisively [8].

This thesis develops the full analytical story. Section 2 establishes the historical and theoretical background: the Charney report's reasoning and the zero-dimensional energy-balance framework. Section 3 describes the methodology: the Gregory regression, radiative kernels, emergent-constraint analysis, and Bayesian evidence combination. Section 4 contains the deep technical core, with subsections on the energy-balance mathematics, kernel-based feedback decomposition, the fluctuation–dissipation basis of emergent constraints, the pattern effect that distinguishes effective from equilibrium sensitivity, and the CMIP ensemble spreads with their paleoclimate bounds. Section 5 presents quantitative evaluation, including worked numerical examples and a synthesis of published estimates. Section 6 discusses limitations honestly, and Section 7 concludes.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The Charney Report and the Birth of the 1.5–4.5°C Range

In the summer of 1979, the U.S. National Research Council convened a panel chaired by the MIT dynamicist Jule Charney to answer a question posed by the Carter administration: what would a doubling of atmospheric CO₂ do to the climate? Working over a few weeks at Woods Hole, the panel reviewed the handful of general circulation models then available, radiative-convective calculations, and the limited paleoclimate record. Their conclusion — that the equilibrium warming for doubled CO₂ would most likely be near 3°C, with a probable range of 1.5–4.5°C — was not a model average but a reasoned synthesis: they assessed which physical feedbacks the models captured, which they omitted, and how much error each omission might introduce [1].

Two features of the Charney report deserve emphasis. First, the panel's central value survived for more than forty years essentially unchanged — a testament less to the precision of 1979 modeling than to the robustness of the underlying physics. Second, the panel identified the principal sources of uncertainty that still dominate discussion today: the radiative properties of clouds and the rate at which the ocean takes up heat. The panel noted explicitly that the ocean's thermal inertia would delay the realization of warming by decades, a prediction borne out by the subsequent warming trajectory [1].

### 2.2 The Global Energy-Balance Framework

The conceptual skeleton beneath all modern sensitivity analysis is the zero-dimensional energy-balance model. Let Δ*T* denote the global-mean surface temperature anomaly, Δ*F* the effective radiative forcing (the perturbation to the top-of-atmosphere, TOA, energy budget before any surface warming occurs), and Δ*N* the net downward TOA radiative imbalance. Conservation of energy requires

Δ*N* = Δ*F* + Δ*R*,

where Δ*R* is the radiative response of the system. Observations and model experiments show that, to first order, the radiative response scales linearly with surface warming: Δ*R* = *λ*Δ*T*, where *λ* is the *climate feedback parameter* (negative for a stable climate). The governing equation is therefore

**Δ*N* = Δ*F* + *λ*Δ*T*.**

At equilibrium, the system no longer accumulates heat, so Δ*N* = 0 and the equilibrium warming is Δ*T*ₑq = −Δ*F* / *λ*. For doubled CO₂, Δ*T*ₑq is by definition the ECS. This deceptively simple relation organizes everything that follows: *forcing* tells us how hard the system is pushed, *feedback* tells us how strongly it resists, and their ratio tells us where the system settles. The difficulty lies entirely in measuring Δ*F* and *λ* for the real Earth — or even agreeing on their definitions in models [2].

> **Theorem:** In the linear energy-balance approximation, the equilibrium response to any forcing Δ*F* is Δ*T*ₑq = −Δ*F*/*λ*, where *λ* < 0 is the total climate feedback parameter. Any uncertainty in *λ* is amplified into uncertainty in ECS: differentiating gives *d*(ECS)/ECS = −*dλ*/*λ*, so that a fixed absolute error in the feedback parameter produces fractional errors in ECS that grow as the system approaches the stability boundary *λ* → 0.

This amplification is the mathematical reason for the notoriously skewed, fat-tailed probability distributions of climate sensitivity discussed by Roe and Baker: because ECS depends on the *reciprocal* of the feedback parameter, symmetric uncertainty in *λ* maps to an asymmetric distribution in ECS with a long upper tail [4].

## 3 Methodology

This thesis combines four complementary methodologies, each addressing a different facet of the sensitivity problem.

1. **Gregory regression analysis.** Following Gregory et al. [2], we regress the TOA radiative imbalance Δ*N* against the surface temperature anomaly Δ*T* in a model experiment forced by an abrupt, constant radiative perturbation (typically an instantaneous CO₂ quadrupling). The intercept at Δ*T* = 0 diagnoses the effective radiative forcing; the slope diagnoses the feedback parameter; extrapolation to Δ*N* = 0 diagnoses the sensitivity. Section 5 demonstrates this procedure numerically.
2. **Radiative kernel decomposition.** We attribute *λ* to physical processes using pre-computed radiative kernels — the partial derivatives of TOA flux with respect to temperature, humidity, albedo, and cloud fields, evaluated with an offline radiative transfer code [3]. Multiplying each kernel by the corresponding model-simulated climate change field and summing globally yields the feedback associated with that process. Because all models are analyzed with the *same* kernels, differences in feedbacks reflect genuine differences in simulated climate change rather than differences in radiative transfer.
3. **Emergent-constraint analysis.** We identify observable quantities *x* (e.g., a metric of interannual temperature variability, or a property of present-day clouds) that correlate across a model ensemble with ECS, fit the emergent relationship ECS = *a* + *b x*, and substitute the observed value of *x* to constrain ECS [5]. Statistical rigor demands out-of-sample validation and physical justification of the relationship, since ensemble correlations can arise spuriously. The cautionary case of the Cox constraint's degraded significance in CMIP6, traced to a violated auxiliary assumption about internal variability, shows why such validation is indispensable [6].
4. **Bayesian multi-evidence synthesis.** Following Sherwood et al. [8], we combine independent lines of evidence — process-based feedback assessments, the historical warming record, and paleoclimate reconstructions — into a posterior probability density for ECS using explicit likelihoods and robustness checks under alternative priors.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Gregory Energy-Balance Method in Detail

Before 2004, diagnosing forcing and sensitivity from a coupled atmosphere–ocean GCM required dedicated experiments: slab-ocean runs to equilibrium, or double radiation calls to isolate stratospheric adjustment. Gregory and colleagues proposed a disarmingly simple alternative applicable to *any* experiment with constant forcing [2]: regress the global-mean TOA net radiative flux anomaly Δ*N* against the global-mean surface air temperature anomaly Δ*T*. From the energy-balance equation, Δ*N* = Δ*F* + *λ*Δ*T*, so:

- the **intercept** (Δ*T* = 0) estimates the effective radiative forcing Δ*F*, *including* rapid adjustments of the troposphere and stratosphere that occur before the surface warms appreciably;
- the **slope** estimates the feedback parameter *λ*;
- the **x-intercept** (Δ*N* = 0) estimates the equilibrium warming, i.e., the *effective climate sensitivity* (EffCS). The word *effective* is load-bearing. The method extrapolates from the transient, non-equilibrium regime of the first decades to centuries of a simulation. If feedbacks strengthen or weaken as warming proceeds — and they do, owing to evolving spatial patterns of surface warming — the extrapolated value differs from the true equilibrium sensitivity. Gregory et al. validated the method against slab-ocean equilibrium runs and found good agreement for CO₂ forcing, but later work showed systematic underestimation in many models, a phenomenon now understood as the **pattern effect** (Section 4.4) [7]. The method's practical importance cannot be overstated: it became the standard diagnostic for the CMIP5 and CMIP6 ensembles, underpinning the sensitivity values reported in the IPCC Fifth Assessment Report and providing the uniform yardstick by which model sensitivities are compared today [7]. A minimal numerical illustration of the regression, using synthetic data mimicking a 150-year abrupt-quadrupling experiment, is given in Section 5. ### 4.2 Radiative Feedback Decomposition with Kernels

The total feedback parameter *λ* is a sum of physically distinct contributions:

*λ* = *λ*ₚₗₐₙcₖ + *λ*ᵂⱽ + *λ*ₗᵣ + *λ*ₐₗbₑdₒ + *λ*cₗₒᵤd + (nonlinear residuals). The radiative kernel method, introduced in its modern form by Soden and colleagues, computes each term as follows. A kernel *K*ₓ(**r**, *p*) is the sensitivity of TOA radiative flux to a perturbation in climate variable *x* (temperature, specific humidity, surface albedo, cloud properties) at location **r** and pressure level *p*, evaluated in a base climate with an offline radiation code. The feedback is then the inner product of the kernel with the simulated change in *x* per unit surface warming, integrated over the globe and the atmospheric column [3]. Typical values across the CMIP ensembles (in W/m²/K) are instructive:

| Feedback | Approximate value | Inter-model spread | Physical basis |
|---|---|---|---|
| Planck (*λ*ₚₗₐₙcₖ) | −3.2 | very small | Stefan–Boltzmann response of a blackbody Earth |
| Water vapor (*λ*ᵂⱽ) | +1.8 | small | Clausius–Clapeyron increase in absolute humidity at roughly fixed relative humidity [3] |
| Lapse rate (*λ*ₗᵣ) | −0.6 | moderate | Differential upper-tropospheric warming, strongest in the tropics |
| Surface albedo (*λ*ₐₗbₑdₒ) | +0.3 | small | Retreat of snow and sea ice |
| Clouds (*λ*cₗₒᵤd) | +0.4 | **large** | Competing LW/SW effects of cloud amount, altitude, and optical depth |
| **Total *λ*** | **−1.3** | moderate | ECS ≈ 3.7 / 1.3 ≈ 2.8°C for doubled CO₂ |

Several points demand attention.

An *emergent constraint* is an empirically discovered relationship, across a model ensemble, between an observable property of the present climate and a quantity of interest about the future — here, ECS [5].

Perhaps the most important conceptual advance of the last decade is the recognition that *effective* climate sensitivity — the value extrapolated from the transient Gregory regression — is systematically *lower* than the true equilibrium sensitivity in most models.

The Coupled Model Intercomparison Project ensembles provide the raw material for most sensitivity estimates — and a puzzle.

## 5 Empirical Evaluation and Proofs

### 5.1 Worked Example: The Gregory Regression

To make the methodology concrete, we perform the Gregory regression on synthetic data representative of a 150-year abrupt CO₂-quadrupling experiment. The code below generates annual (Δ*T*, Δ*N*) pairs with a known forcing of 7.4 W/m² (quadrupling) and feedback parameter −1.2 W/m²/K, adds realistic interannual noise, and recovers the parameters by ordinary least squares:
```python
import numpy as np

# Synthetic abrupt-4xCO2 experiment: true F = 7.4 W/m^2, lambda = -1.2 W/m^2/K
rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
years = np.arange(1, 151)
# Surface warming follows a two-timescale response (fast mixed layer + slow deep ocean)
dT = 6.2 * (0.55 * (1 - np.exp(-years / 4.0)) + 0.45 * (1 - np.exp(-years / 180.0)))
dT += rng.normal(0, 0.08, size=years.shape)          # interannual noise
dN = 7.4 + (-1.2) * dT + rng.normal(0, 0.25, size=years.shape)

A = np.vstack([dT, np.ones_like(dT)]).T
slope, intercept = np.linalg.lstsq(A, dN, rcond=None)[0]  # slope=lambda, intercept=F
effcs_4x = -intercept / slope        # equilibrium warming for 4xCO2
ecs_2x = effcs_4x / 2.0             # scale to doubling (approx. logarithmic forcing)

print(f"Recovered forcing F_4x  = {intercept:.2f} W/m^2")
print(f"Recovered feedback lam = {slope:.2f} W/m^2/K")
print(f"Effective climate sensitivity (2xCO2) = {ecs_2x:.2f} K")
# Output: F_4x = 7.41 W/m^2, lam = -1.21 W/m^2/K, EffCS = 3.06 K
```

The regression recovers the known parameters to within the noise — but note that it recovers the *effective* sensitivity of the transient regime. The following Haskell sketch captures the mathematical structure of applying a radiative kernel to a climate-change field:

```haskell
-- Radiative kernel application: feedback = sum over grid of K(x) * dx/dTs
type Kernel = [(Cell, Double)]        -- dR/dx per grid cell
type ChangeField = [(Cell, Double)]   -- dx/dTs per grid cell

kernelFeedback :: Kernel -> ChangeField -> Double
kernelFeedback k dx = sum [ kval * xval
                          | (c, kval) <- k
                          , let xval = lookupField c dx ]

-- Total feedback is the sum of process contributions plus residual
totalFeedback :: [Double] -> Double -> Double
totalFeedback components residual = sum components + residual
-- e.g. totalFeedback [planck, wv, lr, albedo, cloud] residual
```

## 6 Limitations

An honest assessment must state what remains unresolved. - **Cloud feedback uncertainty persists.** Although assessed as likely positive, the cloud feedback retains the largest inter-model spread of any feedback term, and the CMIP6 high-sensitivity models demonstrate that plausible cloud physics can push ECS above 5°C [7]. - **The pattern effect is model-dependent.** The magnitude of the transient-to-equilibrium feedback change varies widely across models and depends on uncertain ocean circulation responses and SST-pattern-dependent cloud physics [7]. - **Aerosol forcing uncertainty propagates into historical constraints.** The historical energy budget infers sensitivity from observed warming minus ocean heat uptake, divided by the net forcing — and the aerosol (negative) forcing component remains uncertain to roughly a factor of two, widening the historical likelihood [8]. - **State dependence of feedbacks.** There is growing evidence that the feedback parameter itself depends on the background climate state: sensitivity may be higher in warmer climates than in colder ones, complicating the use of LGM constraints for future warming and the use of a single ECS number for very different forcing levels [8]. - **Emergent constraints remain fragile.** The Cox et al. - **Deep-ocean equilibration is unobserved.** True equilibrium requires millennial-scale ocean equilibration that no observation constrains directly; the long tail of the response (the "recalcitrant" component of warming) is inferred from models with sparse validation [7]. ## 7 Conclusion

The quantification of equilibrium climate sensitivity has matured from the Charney panel's reasoned judgment of 3°C within 1.5–4.5°C [1] into a quantitative, multi-evidence probabilistic assessment centered near 3°C within a likely range of 2.5–4°C [8]. The practical implications are stark. ---

## 7 Conclusion

This thesis has traced the quantification of equilibrium climate sensitivity from the Charney panel's 1979 assessment of 3°C within a 1.5–4.5°C range to the modern synthesis in which energy-balance inference, radiative kernel decomposition, and emergent constraints converge on a assessed best estimate near 3°C with a very likely range of 2–5°C [1][8]. The Gregory regression framework made transient sensitivity estimable from forced model runs and observations [2]; the kernel method attributed the feedback to physical mechanisms, with water vapor and cloud processes dominating the uncertainty budget [3][7]; and emergent constraints tightened the distribution by exploiting cross-model relationships, though their statistical fragility demands out-of-sample testing [5][6]. Roe and Baker's analysis explains why the long upper tail of the ECS distribution is a structural feature of the inverse feedback relationship rather than an artifact of any single method [4].

For policy, the conclusion is twofold. First, the central estimate has proven remarkably stable across four decades of methodological advance — a genuine triumph of convergent evidence. Second, the irreducible spread above ~4°C, driven by cloud feedback uncertainty, means high-impact, low-probability warming cannot be excluded by the current evidence [7][8]. The research frontier lies in pattern-effect-aware energy budgets, tighter aerosol forcing constraints, and physically grounded cloud parameterizations that can finally narrow the distribution that Charney's committee first sketched in 1979 [1].
