# Gasper Finality: LMD-GHOST Fork Choice, Casper FFG Justification, Proposer–Builder Separation, and MEV Economics in Proof-of-Stake Ethereum

## Abstract

Ethereum's proof-of-stake consensus rests on Gasper, the protocol fusing the LMD-GHOST fork-choice rule with the Casper FFG finality gadget [1]. This thesis unifies Gasper's finality guarantees with the economic layer now surrounding block production. We reconstruct latest-message-driven greedy heaviest-observed-sub-tree selection, derive the two-epoch justification/finalization pipeline and its slashing conditions, and characterize Gasper as an ebb-and-flow protocol separating dynamic availability from accountable finality [3]. We analyze proposer–builder separation — from the relay-based MEV-Boost first-price auction to enshrined PBS under EIP-7732 [6] — as an auction game with commit–reveal semantics, showing how maximal extractable value concentrates block production among a handful of builders and relays [7,10]. Finally, we survey single-slot finality research, including RLMD-GHOST, orbit-style stake-weighted aggregation, and the confirm–finalize paradigm [9,12]. Our claim: the unsolved problem in Ethereum consensus is no longer proving finality, but constraining the adversarial optionality that auction-structured block production grants to specialized actors.

## 1 Introduction

In September 2022, Ethereum abandoned Nakamoto consensus for a Byzantine-fault-tolerant proof-of-stake protocol known as **Gasper** — a portmanteau of GHOST and Casper, and the idealized abstraction of the beacon chain defined in the paper *Combining GHOST and Casper* [1]. Gasper is best understood as two protocols sharing one validator set and one message stream. The first is a **dynamically available** chain: every twelve-second slot, a committee of validators attests to its view of the canonical head, and a greedy fork-choice walk selects the heaviest observed subtree, so the chain always grows even under asynchrony or mass validator absence. The second is a **finality gadget**: at epoch boundaries, those same attestations double as Casper FFG votes that justify and then finalize checkpoints, giving reversals a cryptographic price tag of at least one-third of all staked ether [1,3].

This dual architecture — formalized as an *ebb-and-flow* protocol by Neu, Tas, and Tse [3] — was the field's celebrated resolution of the **availability–finality dilemma**: classical BFT protocols finalize but halt when too many participants sleep, while longest-chain protocols progress but never quite finalize. Gasper does both, on different timescales, in one construction.

Yet the protocol that ships is not the protocol that was proven. Block production has migrated out of the consensus layer almost entirely. Under proposer–builder separation (PBS), as deployed through MEV-Boost, randomly elected proposers no longer build blocks at all: specialized builders compete in first-price sealed-bid auctions, commit to headers through trusted relays, and reveal payloads only after the proposer has signed [7,8]. Maximal extractable value — the profit available from ordering, inserting, or censoring transactions — now flows through this auction, and its industrialization has re-concentrated power that proof-of-stake was meant to distribute [10]. The current response is to **enshrine** PBS in the protocol itself via EIP-7732, replacing relay trust with in-protocol commit–reveal, builder staking, and payload timeliness committees [6].

Simultaneously, consensus research pushes toward **single-slot finality (SSF)**, which would collapse Gasper's two timescales into one: every block finalized within its own slot by a full-validator-set vote, with the fork-choice rule demoted to a fallback [9,12]. SSF promises 5–20 second economic finality but demands every staker publish attestations every slot — a message-complexity and aggregation problem that remains unsolved at Ethereum's scale.

This thesis contributes: *(i)* a rigorous, unified exposition of LMD-GHOST fork choice and Casper FFG justification/finalization; *(ii)* an auction-theoretic account of PBS and MEV economics, from MEV-Boost to enshrined PBS; and *(iii)* a critical survey of SSF research directions and their trade-offs.

## 2 Background

**Byzantine agreement and the dilemma.** A consensus protocol must satisfy *safety* (no two honest parties disagree) and *liveness* (the system keeps making progress). Classical BFT protocols like PBFT finalize each decision but stall when more than a third of participants are unreachable. Longest-chain protocols are *dynamically available* — they progress under fluctuating participation — but offer only probabilistic, ever-deepening finality. Neu, Tas, and Tse formalized this as the **availability–finality dilemma** and showed that Gasper resolves it by construction: one protocol, two guarantees, layered [3].

**The Ethereum validator model.** Validators lock stake (32 ETH per validator at genesis; up to 2048 ETH since EIP-7251's MaxEB) and are randomly assigned to per-slot committees within each 32-slot epoch [1]. Each slot has one designated proposer and a committee of attesters. Attestations are BLS signatures aggregated first within subnets, then globally. Time is divided into 12-second slots; 32 slots form an epoch of roughly 6.4 minutes.

**From GHOST to LMD-GHOST.** Sompolinsky and Zohar's GHOST rule selects, at each fork, the child whose subtree carries the most accumulated proof-of-work. Gasper's adaptation replaces work with **attestations**: each validator's vote counts as weight behind the block it targets and all its ancestors. The *Latest Message Driven* (LMD) refinement counts only each validator's most recent attestation, preventing stale or equivocating votes from accumulating ghost weight [1].

**Casper FFG in brief.** Casper the Friendly Finality Gadget is a finality overlay: validators cast votes as ordered pairs of checkpoints, *(source, target)*; a checkpoint is *justified* when votes carrying it as target accumulate two-thirds of stake; it is *finalized* when a justified checkpoint is followed by another justified checkpoint in the next epoch. Two slashing conditions make conflicting finalizations cryptographically attributable [1].

**Maximal extractable value.** MEV is the profit a block producer can extract by choosing transaction order, inclusion, or exclusion — arbitrage across DEXes, liquidations, sandwich attacks, and subtler variants. What began as a curiosity became the dominant economic force in block production [10].

## 3 Methodology

Our analysis proceeds on three tracks. **First**, we reconstruct Gasper formally from the primary sources: the Gasper paper's idealized protocol [1], the consensus-layer specification, and the ebb-and-flow formalization [3]. We model validators as state machines exchanging attestations in the *partially synchronous sleepy model* — message delays adversarial before a global stabilization time, arbitrary sleep schedules before a global awake time — the standard setting for analyzing protocols that must be both dynamically available and finalizing [3].

**Second**, we treat the block-supply chain game-theoretically. PBS auctions are modeled as first-price sealed-bid auctions with incomplete information, where builders hold private valuations (their extracted MEV) and proposers select the winning bid; we incorporate latency heterogeneity and the commitment structure of the relay protocol [7].

**Third**, we ground the theory empirically: relay and builder market-share measurements, censorship studies of sanctioned-transaction inclusion [10], auction bid-value distributions, and the May 2023 non-finalization incidents as natural experiments in liveness recovery.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The LMD-GHOST Fork-Choice Rule

LMD-GHOST answers one question: *given my local view, which block is the head of the canonical chain?* The algorithm is a greedy descent. Starting from the latest justified checkpoint, it repeatedly moves to the child block whose subtree carries the greatest attestation weight, counting only each validator's **latest** attestation [1]:

```text
function LMD_GHOST(G, start):
    B := start
    loop:
        children := children of B in G
        if children is empty: return B
        B := argmax_{C in children} w(C)     # ties broken deterministically
```

where `w(C)` sums the effective balances of validators whose latest attestation targets `C` or any descendant of `C`. Because attestations are LMD-filtered, an adversary cannot double-count stale votes across forks — but, as we discuss in §6, the filter is also what balancing attacks exploit [4].

Two engineering additions distinguish deployed LMD-GHOST from the paper's HLMD-GHOST: **proposer boost**, which temporarily adds weight to a timely proposal to blunt balancing attacks, and **view-merge**, which synchronizes honest validators' views at slot boundaries so their attestations concentrate rather than scatter [5]. The confirmation-rule literature [2] shows that under synchrony, these mechanisms let honest proposals accumulate decisive weight within a single slot — a property single-slot finality designs lean on heavily.

### 4.2 Casper FFG: Justification and Finalization

While LMD-GHOST keeps the chain growing, Casper FFG decides which history is **irreversible**. Validators cast FFG votes as checkpoint pairs: a vote `(A, j) → (B, k)` links a justified source to a target checkpoint. A checkpoint is *justified* when votes with it as target total at least two-thirds of stake; it becomes *finalized* when it is the source of a supermajority link to a justified checkpoint in the immediately following epoch [1].

> **Theorem (Accountable safety).** If two conflicting checkpoints are ever finalized, then validators controlling at least one-third of total stake violated a slashing condition, and their identities are cryptographically attributable from the conflicting vote sets.

> **Theorem (Plausible liveness).** Regardless of the current state, there always exists a sequence of honest votes that finalizes a new checkpoint — finality can never deadlock permanently.

The two **slashing conditions** that power accountable safety are: *(C1)* no validator may cast two distinct votes with the same target epoch; *(C2)* no validator may cast a vote whose source–target span strictly surrounds another of its votes [1]. Any safety violation implies at least `n/3` slashable offenses by a standard quorum-intersection argument.

In practice, finalization lags justification by one epoch, so economic finality arrives roughly 12.8 minutes after block proposal. When more than a third of validators go offline — as in the May 2023 incidents — the **inactivity leak** drains the balances of non-attesting validators until the active set again holds two-thirds of remaining stake, restoring liveness at the cost of the offline minority [9].

### 4.3 Proposer–Builder Separation: From MEV-Boost to Enshrinement

PBS begins from an observation: building the highest-value block and proposing the canonical block are different jobs requiring different capabilities. In the **MEV-Boost** deployment, the split is mediated by *relays* [8]:

1. Builders construct full execution payloads and submit `(header, bid)` pairs to relays.
2. Relays simulate payloads, track the highest bid, and expose only the winning header to the proposer.
3. The proposer signs the header blindly — the commit — and the relay releases the full payload — the reveal.
4. The winning bid is paid to the proposer (today, via the block's fee recipient).

This commit–reveal structure is a pragmatic first-price sealed-bid auction, but its trust assumptions are severe: relays can censor bids, withhold payloads, or leak order flow, and none of this is verifiable at the protocol level [8]. Empirically, censorship followed the money: the dominant builders and relays excluded sanctioned transactions, forcing proposers to choose between profit and neutrality [10].

**Enshrined PBS (EIP-7732)** moves the exchange into consensus [6]. The beacon block no longer carries an `ExecutionPayload`; it carries a `SignedExecutionPayloadBid` — a builder's signed commitment. The builder later reveals the payload through a `SignedExecutionPayloadEnvelope`, and a 512-member **Payload Timeliness Committee (PTC)** attests to whether payload and blob data arrived on time. Builders become staked protocol actors (minimum 1 ETH), payments are enforced by slashing builder stake and crediting proposers, and execution validation is deferred to the next block — expanding the propagation window from roughly 2 seconds to about 9 seconds [6]. The dual-deadline design lets validators attest to the consensus block and to payload timeliness separately, decoupling block validation from the execution hot path.

### 4.4 The Economics of MEV

With PBS, MEV extraction industrialized into a supply chain: *searchers* find opportunities (arbitrage, sandwiching, liquidations, JIT liquidity), *builders* assemble them into payloads, *relays* auction them, and *proposers* sell the winning signature. The auction's microstructure determines who captures what.

- **First-price dynamics.** Builders bid their private valuation minus a shading margin; the proposer captures the winning bid. Winning-bid distributions are heavy-tailed: most slots clear for basis points of ETH, while volatile slots clear for tens of ETH [10].
- **Order-flow economies of scale.** Private order flow — exclusive transaction streams from wallets, Telegram bots, and searchers — is the decisive input. Builders with superior flow win more auctions, earn more, and buy more flow: a feedback loop that has concentrated production among a handful of builders [10].
- **Latency heterogeneity.** Auctions effectively close in the final milliseconds of the slot. Fast builders observe rivals' disclosed information and rebid; slow builders cannot. Recent equilibrium analysis shows this asymmetry alone generates persistent profit dispersion [7].
- **The ePBS ratchet effect.** Under enshrinement, the proposer can defer the proposal decision and condition on early bid information. Builders anticipate ex-post extraction and shade or pool early bids, producing allocation inefficiency and "revenue-efficiency valleys" — the auction raises less, and allocates worse, than the textbook benchmark [7].
- **Timing games and censorship.** Proposers delay block release to capture late bids, stressing attestation deadlines; and because the most profitable builders and relays censor, neutrality has a measurable price [10].

The net effect is paradoxical: PBS lowered the *validator* hardware bar while raising the *builder* sophistication bar, relocating centralization rather than removing it.

### 4.5 Single-Slot Finality Research

SSF asks why finality should take two epochs when the votes could be counted in one slot. The core design: **every validator attests in every slot** (committees abolished), and a block receiving two-thirds of stake is finalized within its own slot; if the threshold is missed, the fork-choice rule falls back to selecting a head, exactly as today [9]. The finality gadget and fork choice are never simultaneously active on the same block — the structural simplification Vitalik Buterin has argued would eliminate whole classes of interaction bugs [11].

The enabling construction is the **confirm–finalize paradigm**: only blocks already *confirmed* in the available chain are input to the finality gadget, so justification can never reorg honest confirmations — the mechanism behind Gasper's bouncing attack [3]. RLMD-GHOST, with expiring votes and view-merge, supplies the fast confirmations: honest proposals are confirmed within their slot under synchrony with two-thirds honest participation [5].

Three obstacles dominate current research:

1. **Message complexity.** Every staker publishing two messages per 12 seconds multiplies p2p load by orders of magnitude. Mitigations include multi-level BLS aggregation trees and specialized aggregators — at the cost of engineering complexity and latency [9].
2. **Validator-set economics.** With no committees, the 32 ETH effective-balance cap becomes pure overhead. The *Orbit* proposal samples attestations with frequency proportional to stake weight, incentivizing consolidation; the alternative is capping the validator set outright and rationing entry — each with centralization implications [12].
3. **Roadmap compatibility.** SSF must coexist with ePBS timeliness deadlines, shorter slot proposals (4-second slots would halve the tolerable network delay bound, privileging well-connected geographies [11]), and the inactivity leak, which SSF retains as its recovery mechanism.

## 5 Empirical Evaluation and Proofs

**Accountable safety, proof sketch.** Suppose finalized checkpoints `(A, j)` and `(B, k)` conflict. Finalization implies supermajority links `(A,j) → (C,j+1)` and `(B,k) → (D,k+1)`. Any two two-thirds quorums intersect in at least one-third of stake; the intersecting validators cast FFG votes for both branches, violating (C1) or (C2) by the standard Casper argument [1]. The violation is attributable because votes are signed.

**Ebb-and-flow, proof sketch.** LMD-GHOST is safe and live under synchrony with any honest participation (dynamic availability); Casper FFG is safe always and live after `max(GST, GAT)` with two-thirds honest stake. Because the fork choice starts from the latest justified checkpoint, finality can only *constrain* availability, never contradict it [3].

**Fast confirmation.** The confirmation rule of [2] gives validators a local predicate for treating a block as confirmed well before finalization, with a tunable trade-off between confirmation speed and safety margin — the formal basis for pre-confirmation services and, prospectively, SSF's confirm step.

**Measured reality.** Relay-mediated PBS now carries the large majority of blocks; the censorship study era measured the Flashbots relay alone forwarding roughly four-fifths of relayed blocks, with sanctioned-transaction exclusion concentrated among the dominant builders and relays [10]. Auction micro-data show first-price shading consistent with theory, and the May 2023 non-finalization episodes demonstrated the inactivity leak restoring finality without manual intervention — liveness recovery working as specified.

## 6 Limitations

- **Idealized models.** The Gasper proofs assume static validator sets and clean round structure; production adds dynamic balances, late attestations, proposer boost, and timing games that the theorems do not cover [1,4].
- **Fork-choice fragility.** LMD-GHOST has been shown vulnerable to balancing and avalanche-style attacks in which adversaries withhold attestations to keep subtrees in equilibrium; each mitigation (proposer boost, view-merge, vote expiry) adds complexity faster than it adds proof [4,5].
- **Auction-theoretic stylization.** The ratchet effect and latency-heterogeneity results [7] rest on equilibrium concepts and information structures that real builders — with private order-flow deals and vertical integration — may not satisfy.
- **Partial MEV observability.** Private order flow is invisible by design; all concentration and censorship measurements are lower bounds [10].
- **SSF unproven at scale.** No SSF construction has been validated with a million-validator set; aggregation scaling and the orbit-vs-cap dilemma remain open [9,12].
- **Economic finality is not absolute.** Accountable safety prices reversals at one-third of stake — catastrophic, attributable, but not impossible; the social layer remains the ultimate backstop.

## 7 Conclusion

Gasper's enduring achievement is architectural: it decomposed consensus into a dynamically available fork-choice layer and an accountable finality gadget, resolving the availability–finality dilemma by refusing to choose [1,3]. But the protocol's security theorems speak about validators, while its economic reality is governed by builders, relays, searchers, and auction microstructure — actors the original proofs never modeled.

Two research frontiers will decide what finality means in practice. **Enshrined proposer–builder separation** must convert relay trust into protocol guarantees without the ratchet effect quietly taxing every auction [6,7]. **Single-slot finality** must collapse the two-epoch pipeline into one slot without collapsing validator-set decentralization under message complexity [9,12]. Both are, at bottom, the same problem: constraining the optionality of whoever gets to decide what goes into a block, and when. Finality was never the hard part to prove. It is the hard part to keep honest.
