# Superfluid Helium-3: p-Wave Pairing, the Ginzburg–Landau Order Parameter, and Topological Defects in the A and B Phases

## Abstract

Superfluid helium-3 is the most symmetrically elaborate ordered state known in condensed matter: a neutral Fermi liquid whose atoms pair with one unit of relative orbital angular momentum, producing a spin-triplet, p-wave condensate described by a three-by-three complex order-parameter matrix with eighteen real components. This thesis develops the theory from microscopic pairing through the Ginzburg–Landau functional to the homotopy classification of topological defects. We derive the five quartic bulk invariants and three gradient invariants dictated by the symmetry group SO(3) of spin rotations times SO(3) of orbital rotations times U(1) of particle number, identify the Anderson–Brinkman–Morel and Balian–Werthamer states as the minima underlying the A and B phases, and explain why weak-coupling theory alone cannot stabilize the A phase. The A phase emerges as a chiral Weyl superfluid with point nodes, while the B phase is a fully gapped, time-reversal-invariant topological superfluid. Their defect spectra — half-quantum vortices, continuous textures, monopoles, domain walls, and boojums — are classified by homotopy theory and connected to laboratory analogues of cosmic strings and the chiral anomaly.

## 1 Introduction

The discovery of superfluidity in liquid helium-3, reported by Osheroff, Richardson, and Lee in 1972 and honored with the 1996 Nobel Prize in Physics, confronted theorists with a condensate unlike any previously known [1]. Unlike helium-4, whose bosonic atoms condense directly, helium-3 atoms are fermions of spin one-half and must first bind into Cooper pairs before macroscopic phase coherence can develop. The surprise lay in *how* they pair: the strong short-range repulsion between atoms suppresses conventional s-wave pairing, and the nearly ferromagnetic character of the Fermi liquid favors pairs with parallel spins. Fermi statistics then demands an antisymmetric orbital wavefunction, forcing the pairs into a state of relative orbital angular momentum L equal to one — **p-wave, spin-triplet pairing** [2][3].

The consequences of this pairing symmetry are profound. The order parameter is no longer a single complex scalar but a 3×3 complex matrix A with spin index μ and orbital index j, encoding the relative orientation of the pair's spin and orbital angular momenta at every point in the fluid. With eighteen real degrees of freedom, it is arguably the richest order parameter in all of condensed matter physics, supporting multiple distinct superfluid phases, an extraordinary spectrum of topological defects, and low-energy quasiparticles that mimic relativistic Weyl fermions. The A phase breaks time-reversal symmetry and hosts point nodes in its energy gap; the B phase is fully gapped and preserves time reversal while breaking the relative rotation symmetry between spin and orbital spaces.

This thesis presents a unified account: the microscopic origin of p-wave pairing, the symmetry-dictated Ginzburg–Landau theory, the identification of the A and B phases as minima of the free energy, the homotopy classification of their defects, and the experimental record — from NMR spectroscopy to the direct observation of half-quantum vortices and the laboratory verification of the chiral anomaly [4][5][6].

## 2 Background

### The Fermi liquid and the pairing instability

Normal liquid helium-3 is the archetypal Landau Fermi liquid: at temperatures far below its Fermi temperature (of order one kelvin), the strongly interacting atoms behave as weakly interacting quasiparticles with an effective mass several times the bare atomic mass and a sharp Fermi surface [3]. The superfluid transition occurs at Tc between roughly one and two and a half millikelvin, depending on pressure — a ratio Tc/TF of order ten to the minus three that places the system in the extreme weak-coupling limit of BCS theory, where the coherence length (tens of nanometers, pressure-dependent) vastly exceeds interatomic spacing.

The pairing interaction itself is the subtle part. Bare s-wave attraction is overwhelmed by the hard-core repulsion of the atoms, but exchange of spin fluctuations — paramagnons in the nearly ferromagnetic liquid — generates an effective attraction in the spin-triplet channel. Because the total pair wavefunction must be antisymmetric under particle exchange, symmetric spin (S = 1) forces antisymmetric orbital parity: L odd, and in practice L = 1. This mechanism was anticipated theoretically well before the experimental discovery: Anderson and Morel proposed an anisotropic p-wave state in 1961, Balian and Werthamer analyzed the isotropic triplet state in 1963, and Anderson and Brinkman explained the spin dynamics of the anisotropic phase in 1973 [3]. Leggett's landmark 1975 review systematized the spin dynamics and NMR signatures that would identify the phases [2].

### The phase diagram

In zero magnetic field, bulk helium-3 exhibits two stable superfluid phases. The **B phase** occupies most of the pressure–temperature plane and persists to the lowest accessible temperatures; the **A phase** appears only above a polycritical pressure near 21.2 bar, in a wedge between the normal-to-superfluid line and a first-order A–B transition line, with all three lines meeting at the polycritical point [3]. A magnetic field stabilizes the A phase down to zero pressure and opens a narrow **A1 phase** — a non-unitary state in which only one spin projection pairs — between the normal liquid and the A and B phases. Confinement in anisotropic aerogel ("nafen") stabilizes yet another state, the **polar phase**, with a line node in its gap [7].

| Phase | Order parameter | Gap structure | Time reversal |
|---|---|---|---|
| A (ABM) | d̂(m̂ + i n̂) | Point nodes at ±l̂ | Broken (chiral) |
| B (BW) | Rotation matrix R(n̂,θ) | Fully gapped, isotropic | Preserved |
| A1 | Non-unitary, one spin species | Point nodes | Broken |
| Polar | d̂ m̂ | Line node (equator) | Preserved |

## 3 Methodology

Our approach follows the standard two-tier methodology of unconventional superfluidity: **symmetry-constrained phenomenology** near the transition, anchored by **microscopic weak-coupling theory** and corrected by experiment.

1. **Order-parameter construction.** The pair amplitude Δαβ(k̂) for quasiparticles of momenta k̂ and −k̂ must satisfy the antisymmetry condition Δαβ(k̂) = −Δβα(−k̂). For spin-triplet pairing it is parametrized by a vector d(k̂) in spin space, Δ(k̂) = i (d(k̂)·σ) σy, and expansion of the p-wave (L = 1) dependence in the momentum direction yields dμ(k̂) = Aμj k̂j. The object Aμj is the Ginzburg–Landau order parameter: a 3×3 complex matrix transforming as a vector under spin rotations (index μ) and independently as a vector under orbital rotations (index j) [2][4].
2. **Ginzburg–Landau functional.** Near Tc the free-energy density is expanded in powers of A and its gradients, retaining all terms invariant under G = SO(3)S × SO(3)L × U(1)N (spin rotations, orbital rotations, and gauge transformations), plus the discrete symmetries. This yields a bulk part with five independent fourth-order invariants and a gradient part with three invariants [4].
3. **Weak-coupling evaluation.** The coefficients α, β1…β5, K1…K3 are computed from BCS theory in the weak-coupling limit (Gor'kov derivation), giving definite ratios; deviations — essential for stabilizing the A phase — are treated as strong-coupling corrections from spin-fluctuation feedback [3].
4. **Homotopy classification.** Each phase's vacuum manifold R = G/H (symmetry group modulo residual subgroup) determines its topologically stable defects via the homotopy groups πn(R): line defects from π1, point defects from π2, domain walls from π0 [5].
5. **Experimental probes.** Transverse and longitudinal NMR (frequency shifts and the Leggett spin dynamics), torsional-oscillator superfluid density, ultrasound attenuation, and specific heat identify the phases; rotating cryostats with NMR imaging map vortex structures; neutron irradiation quenches test defect-formation kinetics [6][8].

```python
# Illustrative minimization of the GL bulk energy over candidate states.
# Coefficients use the weak-coupling ratios (-1, 2, 2, 2, -2); strong
# coupling is modeled by shifting beta1 (spin-fluctuation feedback).
import numpy as np

def bulk_energy(A, alpha, betas):
    b1, b2, b3, b4, b5 = betas
    AAT = A @ A.T
    AAd = A @ A.conj().T
    t1 = abs(np.trace(AAT)) ** 2
    t2 = np.trace(AAd) ** 2
    t3 = np.trace(AAT @ AAT.conj())
    t4 = np.trace(AAd @ AAd)
    t5 = np.trace(AAd @ AAd.conj())
    return alpha * np.trace(AAd).real + b1*t1 + b2*t2 + b3*t3 + b4*t4 + b5*t5

def bw_state(delta):
    return delta * np.eye(3) / np.sqrt(3)          # Balian-Werthamer (B phase)

def abm_state(delta):
    A = np.zeros((3, 3), complex)
    A[2, 0] = delta / np.sqrt(2)                    # d along z,
    A[2, 1] = 1j * delta / np.sqrt(2)               # m + i n in the xy-plane
    return A                                       # Anderson-Brinkman-Morel (A)

alpha, scale = -1.0, 1.0
weak = scale * np.array([-1.0, 2.0, 2.0, 2.0, -2.0])
strong = scale * np.array([-0.4, 2.0, 2.0, 2.0, -2.0])  # feedback shifts b1
for name, betas in [("weak-coupling", weak), ("strong-coupling", strong)]:
    eB = bulk_energy(bw_state(1.0), alpha, betas)
    eA = bulk_energy(abm_state(1.0), alpha, betas)
    print(name, "B:", round(eB, 3), "A:", round(eA, 3),
          "->", "A wins" if eA < eB else "B wins")
```

> **Theorem (GL universality):** *Any* free-energy density analytic in A and ∇A and invariant under SO(3)S × SO(3)L × U(1)N reduces, through fourth order, to the five bulk invariants and three gradient invariants; no additional symmetry-allowed term exists at this order [4].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The p-wave spin-triplet order parameter

The gap matrix Δ(k̂) = i (d(k̂)·σ) σy with dμ(k̂) = Aμj k̂j encodes everything. The quasiparticle spectrum follows from diagonalizing the Bogoliubov–de Gennes Hamiltonian: E(k̂) = sqrt(ξ² + Δ²(k̂)), where the direction-dependent gap derives from the eigenvalues of ΔΔ†. States with d × d* = 0 are called **unitary** (both spin projections pair identically); states with d × d* ≠ 0 are **non-unitary** and carry net pair spin polarization — the A1 phase is the prime example [2][3].

Two maximal-symmetry states dominate:

- **Balian–Werthamer (BW):** Aμj = Δ(T) Rμj(n̂, θ) e^{iφ}, with R a proper rotation matrix. The gap is isotropic, |Δ(k̂)| = Δ0, and the state preserves time reversal. The residual symmetry is the group of *combined* spin–orbit rotations SO(3)L+S: rotating spins and orbitals together leaves the state invariant, while relative rotations cost energy. The rotation angle θ and axis n̂ are fixed only by the tiny nuclear dipole interaction, which selects the Leggett angle θ0 = arccos(−1/4) ≈ 104° [2].
- **Anderson–Brinkman–Morel (ABM):** Aμj = Δ0 d̂μ (m̂j + i n̂j) e^{iφ}, with (m̂, n̂, l̂ = m̂ × n̂) an orthonormal triad. The gap |ΔABM(k̂)|² = Δ0² (1 − (k̂·l̂)²) vanishes at two **point nodes** on the Fermi surface along ±l̂. The state is chiral — Cooper pairs carry orbital angular momentum ħ along l̂ — and breaks time-reversal symmetry [2][3].

The symmetry breaking pattern is G → H with H_A ≠ H_B, which is why the A–B transition is first order: no continuous path connects the two minima without crossing an energy barrier.

### 4.2 The Ginzburg–Landau functional and the A/B minima

The bulk free-energy density through fourth order reads

f_bulk = α Tr(AA†) + β1 |Tr(AAᵀ)|² + β2 (Tr AA†)² + β3 Tr(AAᵀ(AAᵀ)*) + β4 Tr((AA†)²) + β5 Tr(AA†(AA†)*),

with α changing sign at Tc, plus gradient terms f_grad = K1 (∇jAμk)(∇jAμk)* + K2 (∇jAμj)(∇kAμk)* + K3 (∇jAμk)(∇kAμj)*. Weak-coupling BCS theory fixes β1:β2:β3:β4:β5 = −1:2:2:2:−2 and K1 = K2 = K3 [3][4]. Minimization in strict weak coupling yields the BW state as the *global* minimum at all pressures — the A phase would not exist. Its experimental reality is therefore a **strong-coupling effect**: feedback of spin fluctuations on the pairing interaction, strongest at high pressure, shifts the β ratios enough to make the ABM state the minimum in a pocket of the phase diagram [3]. The phase boundary where the two minima exchange global stability is the first-order A–B line, terminating at the polycritical point.

> **Theorem (Weak-coupling no-go):** *In strict weak-coupling BCS theory the BW state is the unique global minimum of the GL functional; a stable A phase requires strong-coupling corrections to the fourth-order coefficients.*

Dipole energy, though roughly ten-million times smaller than condensation energy, selects orientations: d̂ ∥ l̂ in the A phase and the Leggett angle in the B phase, and governs the NMR signatures [2].

### 4.3 Vacuum manifolds and homotopy classification

For each phase, the manifold of degenerate ground states is R = G/H. The A-phase manifold is (S² × SO(3))/Z2 — the unit vector d̂ plus the orbital triad, with the identification (d̂, m̂, n̂) ~ (−d̂, −m̂, −n̂) since it leaves A invariant. Its fundamental group is **π1 = Z4**, admitting vortices of circulation κ = n1 πħ/m3 with n1 integer *or half-integer*; the n1 = 1/2 case is the **half-quantum vortex**, equivalently an Alice string, around which the d̂ vector rotates by π while the phase winds by π [5]. The B-phase manifold gives **π1 = Z × Z2**: ordinary integer vortices plus Z2 spin vortices (disclinations of the n̂ field that can terminate on domain walls). Second homotopy π2 yields **monopoles** — hedgehogs in the l̂ field of the A phase — and π0 counts disconnected components, i.e., **domain walls**, of which the A–B interface itself is the most prominent example [5].

```haskell
-- Homotopy classification of defects, as a type-level sketch.
-- Line defects come from pi_1 of the vacuum manifold; point defects from pi_2.
data Phase = APhase | BPhase

type family Vacuum (p :: Phase) :: *
type instance Vacuum APhase = Quotient (S2, SO3) Z2   -- (d-hat, triad) / identification
type instance Vacuum BPhase = Quotient (SO3, S1) Z2   -- (rotation, phase) / identification

type family LineDefects (p :: Phase) :: *
type instance LineDefects APhase = Z4        -- circulation n1 * pi hbar / m, n1 in Z or Z+1/2
type instance LineDefects BPhase = (Z, Z2)    -- integer vortices x spin disclinations

type family PointDefects (p :: Phase) :: *
type instance PointDefects APhase = Z         -- l-hat hedgehog monopoles
type instance PointDefects BPhase = Trivial  -- no stable point defects
```

### 4.4 Vortices, textures, and composite defects

The defect zoo of helium-3 is unmatched in condensed matter [5][6]:

- **A phase:** singular vortices with integer or half-integer circulation; *continuous* (coreless) vortices — the Mermin–Ho and Anderson–Toulouse textures — in which l̂ smoothly escapes into the third dimension so the order parameter never vanishes; and the **half-quantum vortex**, whose π rotation of d̂ compensates a π phase winding, predicted by Mineev and Volovik in the 1970s and observed in 2016 in the polar phase confined in nafen aerogel [7].
- **B phase:** vortices with spontaneously broken axial symmetry in the core (the v-vortex and w-vortex), the **double-core vortex**, and spin-mass vortices carrying both circulation and disclination charge.
- **Composite defects:** in the polar-distorted A phase, half-quantum vortices terminate a **Kibble–Lazarides–Shafi wall** — a domain wall bounded by strings, directly analogous to cosmological defects [7]. At the A–B interface, vortices cannot cross continuously and terminate in **boojums**, surface point defects whose dynamics were probed via the **Kelvin–Helmholtz instability** of the interface under shear flow [6].

### 4.5 The A phase as a Weyl superfluid

The point nodes of the ABM gap are not accidents: each node at ±l̂ is a **Weyl point** of the Bogoliubov spectrum, carrying opposite topological (Chern) charge, with linear dispersion E ≈ sqrt(vF² q⊥² + c∥² q∥²) for small deviations q from the node. Low-energy quasiparticles are thus chiral relativistic fermions, and the l̂ texture acts as an effective gauge field. A time-dependent texture generates an effective electric field E ∝ ∂t l̂ alongside the effective magnetic field B ∝ ∇ × l̂, and the **chiral anomaly equation** predicts anomalous production of quasiparticle momentum — *momentogenesis* — from the superfluid vacuum. Integrated over a moving vortex texture, this yields the **Kopnin spectral-flow force**, which was measured in rotating 3He-A at Manchester and found to nearly cancel the Magnus force, in quantitative agreement with the anomaly prediction [9]. The correspondence extends further: the A-phase order parameter, its defects, and its fermionic spectrum mirror structures in the electroweak sector of the Standard Model, making helium-3 a laboratory for early-universe physics [9][10].

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

1. **Discovery and identification.** The 1972 Cornell pressure anomalies, first misread as magnetic ordering in solid helium-3, were reinterpreted as liquid superfluidity; the 1996 Nobel Prize recognized the discovery [1]. Transverse NMR frequency shifts — large in the A phase, texture-dependent in the B phase — together with Leggett's spin-dynamics theory identified the A phase as the ABM state and the B phase as the BW state [2].
2. **Thermodynamics of the A–B line.** The A–B transition shows latent heat and a discontinuity in the order parameter: it is first order, as required by the distinct symmetry-breaking patterns, while the normal-to-superfluid lines are second order [3].
3. **Half-quantum vortices observed.** Rotating the polar phase in nafen aerogel and imaging with NMR revealed vortices with half the usual circulation, terminating domain walls — the condensed-matter realization of Alice strings and walls bounded by strings [7].
4. **Kibble–Zurek mechanism tested.** Neutron absorption events (n + 3He → p + 3H) create rapidly cooling normal-fluid bubbles; the random vortex networks left behind, counted by rotation-assisted NMR, quantitatively confirm cosmological defect-formation theory in the laboratory [8].
5. **Chiral anomaly measured.** Vortex dynamics in 3He-A exhibit the spectral-flow force predicted by the axial anomaly, verifying momentogenesis — the condensed-matter analogue of baryogenesis by cosmic strings [9].
6. **Interface hydrodynamics.** The A–B interface, stabilized by a magnetic-field gradient, undergoes a superfluid Kelvin–Helmholtz instability under shear flow, with vortex penetration dynamics mapped in detail [6].

## 6 Limitations

The Ginzburg–Landau description is asymptotically exact only in the narrow critical region near Tc; at low temperatures the full quasiclassical (Eilenberger) theory is required, and GL extrapolations of gap magnitudes and vortex core structures are qualitative at best. Strong-coupling corrections, though understood in outline through spin-fluctuation feedback models, remain partly phenomenological: no fully microscopic calculation reproduces the entire phase diagram *ab initio*. Boundary conditions complicate comparison with experiment — surfaces strongly pair-break the p-wave state, so confined geometries (slabs, aerogels, nafen) host distorted phases whose order parameters differ from bulk A and B [7][10]. The dipole interaction, essential for NMR and textures, is treated only to leading order. Finally, quantitative puzzles persist: the precise pressure dependence of the strong-coupling β shifts, the stability of the double-core vortex, and the detailed structure of the A1 phase in high magnetic fields remain active research topics [5][6].

## 7 Conclusion

Superfluid helium-3 realizes, in a millikelvin cryostat, the richest symmetry-breaking structure known in a quantum fluid: spin-triplet p-wave pairing encoded in a 3×3 complex order parameter, a Ginzburg–Landau functional whose minima are the chiral, nodal A phase and the isotropic, time-reversal-invariant B phase, and a homotopy-classified menagerie of defects — half-quantum vortices, continuous textures, monopoles, domain walls, and boojums — with direct analogues in cosmology and high-energy physics. From Leggett's spin dynamics to the observation of Alice strings and the measurement of the chiral anomaly, experiment has confirmed the theory's most exotic predictions. Open challenges — a microscopic derivation of strong-coupling stabilization, the full low-temperature defect phase diagram, and engineered topological phases in confinement — ensure that, five decades after its discovery, helium-3 remains a frontier where condensed matter, topology, and the physics of the early universe meet.
