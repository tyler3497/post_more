# Physics-Informed Neural Networks: Embedding PDE Constraints into Deep Learning, Spectral Bias, and Operator Learning

## Abstract

Physics-informed neural networks (PINNs) solve forward and inverse problems for nonlinear partial differential equations by embedding governing physics directly into the training objective via automatic differentiation. This thesis presents a unified treatment of the PINN paradigm: the original formulation of Raissi, Perdikaris, and Karniadakis with continuous- and discrete-time models, the pathologies of competing loss terms and spectral bias that limit resolution of high-frequency solutions, and the operator-learning revolution — DeepONet and the Fourier Neural Operator — that learns entire solution operators rather than single PDE instances. We derive the residual-minimization framework rigorously, analyze the Neural Tangent Kernel eigenvalue decay underlying the frequency principle, compare Fourier feature embeddings and domain decomposition (XPINNs) as remedies, and present complexity analysis contrasting neural approaches with classical finite-element solvers.

## 1 Introduction

The numerical solution of partial differential equations (PDEs) underpins science and engineering. Classical methods — finite differences, finite elements, spectral schemes — discretize the domain and solve algebraic systems, offering rigorous convergence theory at the cost of mesh generation, poor high-dimensional scaling, and a fresh computation for every new parameter instance or boundary condition. A fundamentally different paradigm has emerged: **physics-informed neural networks** (PINNs), which represent the solution as a neural network and penalize violation of the governing PDE through a composite loss evaluated by *automatic differentiation* [1][2][3].

The central idea is simple to state. Given a PDE $\partial_t u + \mathcal{N}[u] = f$ with boundary operator $\mathcal{B}$, a network $u_\theta(\mathbf{x}, t)$ is trained to minimize

> **Definition (PINN loss):** $\mathcal{L}(\theta) = \lambda_r \|\partial_t u_\theta + \mathcal{N}[u_\theta] - f\|^2 + \lambda_b \|\mathcal{B}[u_\theta] - g\|^2 + \lambda_i \|u_\theta(\cdot, 0) - u_0\|^2$, where all derivatives are computed exactly by reverse-mode automatic differentiation.

No mesh, no finite-difference stencil, no discretization error in the differentiation itself. The same formulation handles **forward problems** (predict the solution) and **inverse problems** (identify unknown coefficients from sparse data) with identical code [2].

Yet the simplicity is deceptive. PINNs suffer from documented pathologies: gradient imbalance between residual and boundary terms, a **spectral bias** that cripples resolution of high-frequency and multiscale solutions [4][5], and optimization failures for stiff or long-horizon problems. The remedies — loss reweighting, Fourier feature embeddings [6], domain decomposition (XPINNs) [9] — led ultimately to a paradigm shift: **operator learning**, where DeepONet [7] and the Fourier Neural Operator [8] learn the *mapping from PDE parameters to solutions* as a function-space operator, enabling zero-shot evaluation at new parameters and resolutions.

This thesis develops the complete arc: (1) the PINN formulation [1][2][3]; (2) the spectral bias / frequency principle [4][5] analyzed through the Neural Tangent Kernel; (3) remedies — Fourier features [6], adaptive reweighting, XPINNs [9]; (4) operator learning — DeepONet [7] and FNO [8]; (5) empirical benchmarks and complexity analysis.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Neural Networks as Differentiable Function Approximators

A feedforward network $u_\theta: \mathbb{R}^d \to \mathbb{R}$ with activation $\sigma$ (typically $\tanh$ for PINNs — smooth and bounded) is a universal approximator of continuous functions on compact sets. Crucially, reverse-mode automatic differentiation computes *exact* derivatives $\partial_{x_i} u_\theta$, $\partial_{x_i x_j} u_\theta$ through the computation graph. The PDE residual thus becomes a differentiable function of $\theta$, optimizable by standard gradient methods. This is the enabling technology of the entire field.

### 2.2 The Original PINN Framework

Raissi, Perdikaris, and Karniadakis introduced PINNs in a two-part treatise [2][3], merged into the Journal of Computational Physics paper [1]. Two model classes were proposed:

| Model class | Mapping | Key property |
|---|---|---|
| **Continuous-time** | $(t, \mathbf{x}) \mapsto u$; residual at collocation points | Mesh-free; one network for all spacetime |
| **Discrete-time** | Implicit Runge–Kutta stages as network outputs at $t^n$ | Unlimited RK stages; large time steps |

The continuous-time loss is a weighted sum of the PDE residual at $N_f$ collocation points, the initial condition, and boundary data [1]. Demonstrations covered Burgers', Schrödinger, Allen–Cahn, and Navier–Stokes equations, with data-efficient solutions from remarkably small networks.

### 2.3 Forward vs. Inverse Problems

A distinctive strength of PINNs is the unified treatment of *inverse* problems [3]. Unknown PDE parameters $\boldsymbol{\lambda}$ (viscosity, reaction rates) join the optimization variables:

$$\mathcal{L}(\theta, \boldsymbol{\lambda}) = \mathcal{L}_{\text{data}}(\theta) + \mathcal{L}_{\text{physics}}(\theta, \boldsymbol{\lambda})$$

Gradients flow through the residual into $\boldsymbol{\lambda}$ automatically — no bespoke adjoint derivation. This makes PINNs natural tools for data assimilation and parameter discovery.

### 2.4 The Optimization Landscape

PINN training minimizes a *non-convex, multi-objective* loss with two dominant pathologies:

- **Gradient imbalance:** residual and boundary gradients can differ by orders of magnitude or point in conflicting directions, so the optimizer satisfies one term while ignoring the other.
- **Stiffness:** for convection-dominated or multiscale PDEs, the residual landscape is ill-conditioned, and Adam/L-BFGS converge slowly or to poor minima.

These motivate the remediation literature of Section 4.

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 The Residual-Minimization Framework

Training is a **weighted residual method in parameter space**: with neural manifold $\mathcal{M}_\Theta = \{u_\theta : \theta \in \Theta\}$,

$$\theta^* = \arg\min_{\theta} \; \mathcal{L}_r(\theta) + \sum_k \lambda_k \mathcal{L}_k(\theta)$$

The collocation points act as stochastic quadrature of the $L^2$ norm — Monte Carlo integration of the variational form. Unlike Galerkin methods, the optimizer's dynamics implicitly choose which residual components are reduced first.

> **Theorem (Consistency):** If the PDE admits a unique classical solution $u^*$ in the closure of $\mathcal{M}_\Theta$, and a sequence $\{\theta_n\}$ drives $\mathcal{L}(\theta_n) \to 0$ with exact boundary/initial enforcement, then $u_{\theta_n} \to u^*$ uniformly on compact subsets, under standard PDE stability estimates.

This result is reassuring but *non-constructive*: it says nothing about whether gradient descent finds such a sequence — the central difficulty of PINNs.

### 3.2 Spectral Bias: The Frequency Principle

The deepest limitation of PINNs is **spectral bias** (the *frequency principle*): gradient-trained networks learn low-frequency components far faster than high-frequency ones [4][5]. Rahaman et al. [4] showed via Fourier analysis that deep ReLU networks are biased toward low-frequency functions — frequency-$k$ components decay at least as $\mathcal{O}(k^{-2})$, so high-frequency content demands finely tuned parameters in a small volume of parameter space. Independently, Xu et al. [5] observed that a network fitting $\sin(x) + \sin(5x)$ matches $\sin(x)$ first and acquires $\sin(5x)$ only later.

The mechanism is the **Neural Tangent Kernel (NTK)**: in the infinite-width limit, training equals kernel regression with the NTK, whose eigenvalues decay monotonically with frequency (spherical harmonics for uniform data on a hypersphere), so high-frequency modes converge far slower [5]. Spectral bias is thus intrinsic to the kernel regime, not an architectural bug.

**Consequences for PINNs** are severe:

1. Sharp gradients, boundary layers, and turbulence spectra need high-frequency representation — acquired last and least reliably.
2. The residual involves *derivatives*, which amplify high-frequency errors: a mode of amplitude $\epsilon$ at frequency $k$ produces residual error $\mathcal{O}(k^p \epsilon)$ for a $p$-th order PDE.
3. Long-time integration accumulates phase errors in oscillatory components.

### 3.3 Fourier Feature Embeddings

Tancik et al. [6] proposed a simple remedy: map inputs through **Fourier features** before the MLP,

$$\gamma(\mathbf{x}) = [\cos(2\pi B\mathbf{x});\; \sin(2\pi B\mathbf{x})], \qquad B_{ij} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2),$$

proving via NTK analysis that the mapped network's effective kernel becomes *stationary with tunable bandwidth* — the hyperparameter $\sigma$ directly controls which frequency band is learned efficiently [6]. For PINNs this is a drop-in change: choose $\sigma$ to cover the solution's expected spectrum, dramatically accelerating oscillatory and multiscale problems.

### 3.4 Loss Balancing and Adaptive Reweighting

The gradient pathology of Section 2.4 is addressed by adaptive weights $\lambda_k$ that equalize the *gradient magnitudes* of competing terms:

1. **Learning-rate annealing** — rescale $\lambda_k$ so $\|\lambda_k \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_k\|$ are balanced across terms.
2. **NTK-based weighting** — weight each term inversely to its NTK-block trace, equalizing convergence rates.
3. **Causal training** — weight early-time residuals before later ones, respecting the PDE's forward information flow.

Without such balancing, PINNs routinely satisfy one loss term while ignoring another, producing smooth but physically wrong solutions.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Continuous-Time vs. Discrete-Time Models

The two classes of [1][2] embody a trade-off between *flexibility* and *temporal structure*.

**Continuous-time models** treat time as another input coordinate: fully mesh-free, trivial adaptive resampling, one smooth spacetime function. Their weakness is *causality violation* — nothing stops the optimizer from fitting $t = 0.9$ before resolving $t = 0.1$, converging to locally consistent but temporally wrong solutions for stiff or chaotic systems.

**Discrete-time models** embed a $q$-stage implicit Runge–Kutta scheme: outputs are stage values $u^{n+c_i}$, and RK equations become loss terms. A-stability permits *very large time steps* — the original paper solved Allen–Cahn accurately with a handful of steps where explicit schemes need thousands [1] — at the cost of parameter growth with $q$ and a coarse temporal mesh.

> **Theorem (Discrete-time error):** For a linear PDE with a $q$-stage implicit RK method of order $p$ embedded in the PINN loss, driving stage residuals to $\epsilon$ yields global error $\|u_\theta(T) - u(T)\| \leq C(T)(\Delta t^p + \epsilon)$: neural error adds linearly to classical RK truncation error.

Modern practice favors continuous-time models with *causal loss weighting* restoring temporal order.

### 4.2 XPINNs: Domain Decomposition

Jagtap and Karniadakis generalized PINNs to **XPINNs** [9]: decompose $\Omega = \bigcup_q \Omega_q$, deploy a separate network per subdomain with its own hyperparameters, and enforce interface continuity via

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{interface}} = \tfrac{1}{N_I}\sum \|u_{\theta_q} - u_{\theta_{q'}}\|^2 + \tfrac{1}{N_I}\sum \|\mathcal{R}_{\theta_q} - \mathcal{R}_{\theta_{q'}}\|^2$$

averaging solution *and* residual jumps. XPINNs generalize both plain PINNs and conservative PINNs (restricted to conservation laws), offering three benefits [9]:

- **Parallelism** in space *and* time;
- **Adaptivity** — deep networks where the solution is complex (shocks, layers), shallow ones where it is smooth;
- **Generalization** — provably improved when subdomain target functions are much simpler than the global one, trading off against reduced per-subdomain data.

On complex geometries where meshing is painful, XPINNs match monolithic PINN accuracy at lower wall-clock cost [9].

### 4.3 DeepONet: Learning Operators, Not Solutions

**Operator learning** learns the solution operator $\mathcal{G}: \mathcal{A} \to \mathcal{U}$ mapping input functions (initial conditions, coefficients, forcings) to solutions — an entire PDE family at once [7]. DeepONet (Lu, Jin, Karniadakis) realizes the Chen–Chen universal approximation theorem for operators via:

- a **branch net** encoding the input function at $m$ sensors: $\mathbf{b}(a) = (b_1, \dots, b_p)$,
- a **trunk net** encoding the query location $y$: $\mathbf{t}(y) = (t_1, \dots, t_p)$,

with output as an inner product:

$$\mathcal{G}_\theta(a)(y) = \sum_{k=1}^{p} b_k(a(\mathbf{x}_1), \dots, a(\mathbf{x}_m)) \cdot t_k(y)$$

This is a *learned basis expansion*: the trunk learns basis functions adapted to the solution manifold; the branch learns the coefficients [7]. DeepONet showed polynomial-to-*exponential* test-error convergence in dataset size on ODEs, PDEs, and fractional operators, with far lower generalization error than fully-connected baselines. After training, a new input function needs only a forward pass — enabling real-time control and uncertainty quantification. Physics-informed DeepONet variants add PDE-residual losses, merging PINN data efficiency with operator amortization.

### 4.4 Fourier Neural Operator: Spectral Convolutions

The **Fourier Neural Operator** (FNO; Li, Kovachki, Azizzadenesheli et al.) [8] parameterizes the integral kernel *directly in Fourier space*. One layer updates $v_t$ via

$$v_{t+1}(x) = \sigma\!\left( W v_t(x) + \mathcal{F}^{-1}\!\left( R_\phi \cdot \mathcal{F}(v_t) \right)(x) \right)$$

with learned complex multiplier $R_\phi$ truncated to $k_{\max}$ modes. The design delivers:

1. **Global convolution** via FFT in $\mathcal{O}(n \log n)$ — long-range interactions local CNNs miss;
2. **Resolution invariance** — evaluation on finer grids than trained on: **zero-shot super-resolution**, the first ML method to model turbulent flows this way [8];
3. **Natural spectral truncation** aligned with PDE solution smoothness.

On Burgers', Darcy flow, and Navier–Stokes (including turbulence), FNO achieved state-of-the-art accuracy at up to **1000× speedup** over traditional solvers [8]. Periodic problems are its home turf; learned deformations extend it to general geometries.

### 4.5 Synthesis: PINNs vs. Operators

| Aspect | PINNs | Neural operators (DeepONet / FNO) |
|---|---|---|
| **Learned object** | One solution instance | Entire solution operator |
| **Training data** | Collocation points + physics (little labeled data) | Many input–output function pairs (costly offline) |
| **New parameter query** | Retrain from scratch | Single forward pass (milliseconds) |
| **Inverse problems** | Natural (joint $\theta, \lambda$ optimization) | Needs extra inversion machinery |
| **Spectral bias exposure** | Severe (pointwise residual) | Mitigated (Fourier parameterization in FNO) |
| **Best use case** | Data-sparse discovery, inverse problems | Many-query forward problems, design, control |

The trajectory is toward **hybrids**: physics-informed operator learning — operator architectures trained with PDE-residual losses — combining PINN data efficiency with operator amortization.

## 5 Empirical Results and Complexity Analysis

### 5.1 Canonical Benchmarks

The original PINN papers [1][2] established enduring benchmarks:

- **Burgers' equation** ($u_t + uu_x = \nu u_{xx}$, $\nu = 0.01/\pi$): an 8×20 continuous-time PINN reached relative $L_2$ error $\sim 10^{-3}$ versus the Cole–Hopf exact solution — from initial/boundary data alone, no interior labels.
- **Schrödinger equation:** the complex field $(u, v)$ learned with periodicity enforced *exactly* via a custom output layer; errors below $10^{-3}$.
- **Allen–Cahn:** the discrete-time model with 100 RK stages took a *single* $\Delta t = 0.1$ step accurately — impossible for explicit schemes.
- **Navier–Stokes (cylinder flow):** velocity and pressure recovered from sparse data on a genuinely nonlinear coupled system [1].

DeepONet [7] learned explicit operators (integrals, fractional Laplacians) and implicit PDE operators with generalization error decaying polynomially to exponentially in dataset size — systematically beating fully-connected baselines. FNO [8] on 2D Navier–Stokes at turbulent Reynolds numbers remains the flagship proof that neural operators can replace spectral solvers with 1000× speedups.

### 5.2 Complexity Scaling

With $N$ degrees of freedom and dimension $d$:

| Method | Training cost | Inference cost | Scaling in $d$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| Finite elements | — | $\mathcal{O}(N^{3/2})$–$\mathcal{O}(N^2)$ per solve | Exponential (meshing) |
| PINN (per instance) | $\mathcal{O}(E \cdot N \cdot P)$ ($E$ epochs, $P$ params) | $\mathcal{O}(P)$ per point | Mild (mesh-free sampling) |
| DeepONet | $\mathcal{O}(E \cdot N_{\text{pairs}} \cdot P)$ (one-time) | $\mathcal{O}(P)$ per query | Mild |
| FNO | $\mathcal{O}(E \cdot N_{\text{pairs}} \cdot n \log n)$ (one-time) | $\mathcal{O}(n \log n)$ per query | Mild (tensorized grids) |

The economic argument is decisive: classical solvers pay full price *per query*; neural operators amortize one expensive training phase over arbitrarily many cheap queries. PINNs sit between — cheaper than FEM per instance in high dimensions or complex geometries, but still retrained per instance.

### 5.3 Reference Implementation

A minimal PyTorch continuous-time PINN for 1D Burgers', showing how automatic differentiation replaces all discretization machinery:

```python
import torch
import torch.nn as nn

class PINN(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, layers=(2, 20, 20, 20, 20, 1)):
        super().__init__()
        mods, d = [], layers[0]
        for w in layers[1:-1]:
            mods += [nn.Linear(d, w), nn.Tanh()]
            d = w
        mods.append(nn.Linear(d, layers[-1]))
        self.net = nn.Sequential(*mods)

    def forward(self, x, t):
        return self.net(torch.cat([x, t], dim=1))

def residual(pinn, x, t, nu=0.01/3.14159):
    x.requires_grad_(True); t.requires_grad_(True)
    u = pinn(x, t)
    u_t = torch.autograd.grad(u, t, torch.ones_like(u),
                              create_graph=True)[0]
    u_x = torch.autograd.grad(u, x, torch.ones_like(u),
                              create_graph=True)[0]
    u_xx = torch.autograd.grad(u_x, x, torch.ones_like(u_x),
                               create_graph=True)[0]
    return u_t + u * u_x - nu * u_xx  # exact AD, no stencil

def pinn_loss(pinn, xf, tf, xb, tb, ub, x0, u0):
    loss_r = (residual(pinn, xf, tf) ** 2).mean()       # physics
    loss_b = ((pinn(xb, tb) - ub) ** 2).mean()          # boundary
    loss_i = ((pinn(x0, torch.zeros_like(x0)) - u0) ** 2).mean()
    return loss_r + loss_b + loss_i  # naive: reweight in practice
```

The final comment is the point: the naive unweighted sum exhibits the gradient pathologies of Section 3.4 — production codes use adaptive weights.

## 6 Limitations

1. **No rigorous a priori bounds for realistic training.** Consistency theorems assume the residual reaches zero; they say nothing about whether gradient descent gets there. The *approximation vs. optimization* gap is the field's central open problem.
2. **Spectral bias is fundamental.** Fourier features [6] shift the problem to choosing $\sigma$ — the solution's spectrum is unknown a priori — and NTK theory shows the bias is intrinsic to gradient training [4][5].
3. **Long-time integration and chaos.** Errors compound; the optimizer exploits non-causal shortcuts. Chaotic systems (e.g., Kuramoto–Sivashinsky at large domains) remain largely out of reach.
4. **High dimensions.** Mesh-free sampling avoids grid costs, but the *statistical* curse persists: worst-case collocation counts still grow exponentially in $d$.
5. **Sparse noisy inverse problems.** Joint $(\theta, \lambda)$ optimization is non-convex with identifiability failures; the physics term can overpower weak data.
6. **Operator data cost.** DeepONet/FNO need *many* solved PDE instances for training — each from a classical solver. If one solve is expensive, amortization collapses.
7. **Boundary enforcement.** Soft penalties are approximate; hard enforcement ($u_\theta = g + d(\mathbf{x}) N_\theta$) needs analytic distance functions unavailable for complex geometries.
8. **Reproducibility.** Inconsistent baselines, cherry-picked hyperparameters, and weak comparisons against tuned classical solvers — a methodological debt only beginning to be repaid.

## 7 Conclusion

PINNs reframed PDE solving as *differentiable residual minimization*, turning automatic differentiation into a mesh-free discretization engine [1][2][3]. The elegance — one loss unifying forward simulation, inverse discovery, and data assimilation — is genuine, and the successes on canonical nonlinear PDEs are real. But the pathologies are equally real: competing loss terms demanding careful reweighting, and a spectral bias [4][5] rooted in NTK eigenvalue decay that makes high-frequency physics the last thing learned and the first thing the residual amplifies.

The field responded twofold. *Architecturally*, Fourier features [6] retune the effective NTK to the problem's spectrum, while XPINNs [9] decompose domains for parallelism and local adaptivity. *Paradigmatically*, operator learning — DeepONet's branch–trunk factorization [7] and FNO's spectral convolutions [8] — abandons per-instance solving, learning the solution map as a function-space operator with zero-shot super-resolution and thousand-fold speedups.

The emerging consensus favors their *fusion*: operator architectures trained with physics-informed residual losses, combining PINN data efficiency with operator amortization. As hybrid methods mature — and rigorous error analysis catches up with empirical success — physics-constrained deep learning is poised to join the standard computational toolkit, alongside rather than replacing the classical methods whose theory it still envies.
