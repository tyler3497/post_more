# Simultaneous Localization and Mapping via Factor Graphs: Incremental Smoothing on the Bayes Tree and Robust Loop Closure under Perceptual Aliasing

## Abstract

Simultaneous Localization and Mapping (SLAM) is the canonical estimation problem of mobile robotics: a robot must construct a model of its environment while concurrently estimating its own trajectory within it. This thesis presents a unified treatment of modern SLAM as nonlinear least-squares optimization over factor graphs. We develop the *Bayes tree*, a directed junction-tree data structure whose cliques encode conditional probability densities and whose structure maps directly onto the square-root information matrix of the problem, and we derive from it iSAM2, an incremental smoother that performs fluid relinearization and incremental variable reordering so that only the affected subtree is recomputed when new measurements arrive [1]. We then confront the fragility at the heart of the back end: *perceptual aliasing* produces false loop closures that can catastrophically corrupt the estimate under quadratic cost, and we survey the robustification machinery — switchable constraints, dynamic covariance scaling, and M-estimators — that allows pose-graph optimization to survive outlier constraints [4][6][8]. We conclude with empirical evidence from standard benchmarks, complexity arguments, and open limitations that delimit the robust-perception agenda of contemporary SLAM [3].

## 1. Introduction

The robot that must act in an unknown environment faces a dilemma of circular dependency. To navigate it needs a map; to build a map it needs to know where it is. This chicken-and-egg structure — *simultaneous localization and mapping* — has anchored robotics research for over three decades and remains, despite spectacular progress, an active frontier: Cadena et al. famously asked "is SLAM solved?" and answered that robustness and long-term autonomy keep the problem open [3].

Two intellectual currents converged to produce the modern solution. The first is probabilistic: sensor noise, wheel slip, and feature-detection errors demand that SLAM be framed as inference over a posterior distribution, with the maximum-a-posteriori (MAP) estimate as the target. The second is algebraic: the Jacobians of SLAM measurement models are sparse, so the MAP problem reduces to *sparse nonlinear least squares*, solvable with Gaussian elimination on the square-root information matrix [1].

Three obstacles stand between this elegant formulation and a system that can run for hours on a real robot:

1. **Incrementality.** Batch re-optimization of the entire trajectory after every measurement is computationally untenable online; the solver must reuse previous work and touch only what changed.
2. **Nonlinearity.** Motion models and bearing/range observations live on manifolds such as $SE(3)$; linearization points must be refreshed as estimates drift, or inconsistency accumulates.
3. **Outlier fragility.** Loop-closure detection is a data-association decision. When appearance-based place recognition hallucinates a match between two genuinely different places — *perceptual aliasing* — the resulting false edge can fold the map onto itself, and the quadratic cost lets a single wrong constraint dominate the solution.

This thesis is organized around the architectural response to these obstacles: the **factor graph** as the common language of SLAM, the **Bayes tree** as the incremental inference substrate, and **robust optimization** as the immune system of the back end.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 From Filtering to Smoothing

Early SLAM systems were *filters*: the extended Kalman filter (EKF) maintained a Gaussian over the current pose and map, marginalizing out all past poses at each step [3]. Filtering has two structural weaknesses. First, marginalization destroys sparsity — the information matrix densifies — so cost grows quadratically with map size. Second, once a past pose is marginalized, its linearization point is frozen forever, and linearization error can never be corrected. Smoothing keeps the full trajectory in the state vector, preserves sparsity, and allows relinearization: in sparse problems, remembering the trajectory *helps* rather than hurts [1].

### 2.2 Factor Graphs and MAP Estimation

A **factor graph** is a bipartite graphical model with variable nodes $x_i$ and factor nodes $f_k$ connected when $f_k$ depends on $x_i$; the joint density factorizes as a product of local potentials [2]. In SLAM, variables are robot poses $x_1,\dots,x_n \in SE(3)$ and landmark positions $l_1,\dots,l_m$, while factors encode odometry, landmark observations, and loop closures. Under Gaussian noise the MAP estimate is

$$\Theta^\* = \arg\min_\Theta \; \sum_k \left\| h_k(\Theta_k) - z_k \right\|^2_{\Sigma_k},$$

the familiar nonlinear least-squares problem, solved by Gauss–Newton or Levenberg–Marquardt iterations on the linearized system $A\,\delta = b$ [4].

> **Theorem (Sparsity of SLAM):** The Jacobian $A$ of the SLAM least-squares problem has a number of nonzeros linear in the number of measurements. Consequently, the QR/Cholesky factorization of $A^\top A$ has fill-in bounded by the quality of the variable ordering, and elimination admits a graphical interpretation as moralization and triangulation of the factor graph [1].

### 2.3 Pose Graphs and Loop Closure

When landmarks are marginalized or the sensor is vision-based with explicit 3D points, the problem can be reduced to a **pose graph**: nodes are poses, edges are relative-motion constraints (odometry, chained sequentially) and loop closures (linking temporally distant poses that observe the same place). Loop closures are the sole mechanism by which accumulated drift is corrected; without them, odometry error grows without bound. They are also the dominant source of catastrophic failure, as we shall see in §4.3.

## 3. Methodology

Our methodology follows the graphical-model route to incremental inference pioneered by Kaess et al. [1]:

1. **Model.** Express the SLAM posterior as a factor graph $p(\Theta \mid Z) \propto \prod_k f_k(\Theta_k)$.
2. **Eliminate.** Choose a variable ordering; eliminate variables one at a time, each elimination producing a conditional density $p(x_j \mid S_j)$ over the variable and its separator $S_j$, plus a new factor on the separator.
3. **Store.** Organize the resulting conditionals into the **Bayes tree**: a directed tree whose nodes (cliques) store the conditional densities, whose edges point from separator to eliminated variables. The Bayes tree is isomorphic to the square-root information matrix $R$ in $A = QR$, but carries a probabilistic interpretation the raw matrix lacks [Kaess10].
4. **Update incrementally.** When new factors arrive, identify the affected cliques (those on paths from the new variables to the root), *re-eliminate* only that subtree, and reuse the rest. Relinearize only variables whose estimates moved beyond a threshold (**fluid relinearization**), and reorder variables incrementally rather than in periodic batch resets (**incremental variable reordering**).
5. **Robustify.** Replace the quadratic penalty on loop-closure factors with robust machinery — switch variables or scaled covariances — so false data associations cannot dominate the estimate [6][8].

The Python sketch below captures the update loop at the conceptual level (cf. the GTSAM implementation of iSAM2):

```python
def isam2_update(bayes_tree, new_factors, linearization_point, threshold=1e-3):
    # 1. Identify affected variables: those adjacent to new factors
    affected = {v for f in new_factors for v in f.variables}
    # 2. Remove the affected subtree from the Bayes tree (back-substitute up)
    subtree = bayes_tree.detach_subtree(affected)
    # 3. Fluid relinearization: only relinearize factors whose variables
    #    moved more than threshold since their linearization point
    moved = {v for v in affected if norm(delta[v]) > threshold}
    factors = subtree.factors + new_factors
    linearized = [linearize(f, linearization_point) if any(v in moved for v in f.variables)
                  else f.cached_jacobian for f in factors]
    # 4. Re-eliminate only the subtree, with incremental reordering
    new_cliques = eliminate(linearized, ordering=constrained_colamd(affected))
    bayes_tree.reattach(new_cliques)
    return bayes_tree
```

The crucial property is *locality*: a measurement at time $t$ perturbs only a small, recent portion of the tree, so per-step cost stays nearly constant as the trajectory grows [1].

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Bayes Tree: From Variable Elimination to Conditional Densities

Variable elimination on a factor graph proceeds as follows. Given an ordering $x_1,\dots,x_n$, eliminate $x_j$ by collecting all factors adjacent to it, forming the joint $p(x_j, S_j)$ on $x_j$ and its separator $S_j$ (the neighboring not-yet-eliminated variables), factoring it as $p(x_j \mid S_j)\,p(S_j)$, and continuing with the marginal factor $p(S_j)$. Each conditional $p(x_j \mid S_j)$ becomes a clique of the Bayes tree; cliques sharing separator variables are linked, and the result is a directed tree rooted at the last-eliminated variable [Kaess10].

The payoff is conceptual: the notoriously abstract operation of "updating a QR factorization incrementally" becomes *editing a tree*. Adding a factor between existing variables attaches a new leaf and re-eliminates the path to the root; everything off that path is untouched. The Bayes tree is closely related to the junction tree, but its directionality maps naturally onto the upper-triangular $R$, making back-substitution a simple root-to-leaf pass [1].

### 4.2 Incremental Reordering and Fluid Relinearization

Two design decisions distinguish iSAM2 from its predecessor iSAM [1]:

- **Incremental variable reordering.** Fill-in during elimination depends critically on ordering. iSAM periodically reordered *all* variables in a batch step. iSAM2 applies a constrained ordering (a variant of COLAMD) to just the re-eliminated subtree, pushing recently accessed variables toward the root so that future updates disturb as little as possible.
- **Fluid relinearization.** In nonlinear SLAM, Jacobians must be refreshed as estimates change. Instead of relinearizing everything periodically, iSAM2 tracks each variable's deviation from its linearization point and relinearizes only factors touching variables that moved beyond a threshold $\beta$. Variables that barely moved keep their old linearization — hence "fluid."

Empirically, these choices remove the periodic batch resets that detracted from the online nature of iSAM, yielding an algorithm whose per-step cost on standard datasets is a small fraction of batch optimization while attaining the same accuracy [1].

The elimination machinery is naturally expressed in typed functional form; the following Haskell sketch captures the factor-graph abstraction:

```haskell
-- A factor graph over variables v with measurement type m
data Factor v m = Factor { scope :: [v], measure :: m, noise :: Covariance }

-- Variable elimination: pick an ordering, eliminate, collect conditionals
eliminate :: Ord v => [v] -> [Factor v m] -> BayesTree v
eliminate []     fs = Leaf
eliminate (x:xs) fs = let (adj, rest) = partition (elem x . scope) fs
                          joint      = productFactors adj
                          (cond, sep) = factorConditional x joint
                      in Node cond sep (eliminate xs (sep : rest))

-- iSAM2's insight: re-elimination touches only the affected subtree
isam2Step :: Ord v => BayesTree v -> [Factor v m] -> BayesTree v
isam2Step tree newFs =
  let affected = nub (concatMap scope newFs)
      (subtree, context) = detachSubtree affected tree
      reordered = constrainedCOLAMD affected (variablesOf subtree)
      rebuilt   = eliminate reordered (factorsOf subtree ++ newFs)
  in reattach context rebuilt
```

### 4.3 Perceptual Aliasing and the Loop-Closure Problem

Loop-closure detection is *global localization* in miniature: given the current observation, find a previously visited place. Appearance-based methods such as bag-of-binary-words (DBoW2) quantize local features into a visual vocabulary and retrieve candidate keyframes by inverted-index scoring, followed by geometric verification (RANSAC on a fundamental matrix or Sim(3) constraint) [5]. This pipeline works remarkably well — ORB-SLAM2 closes loops reliably across KITTI, EuRoC, and TUM sequences [5] — but it has an irreducible failure mode: **perceptual aliasing**, where two distinct places look alike (identical corridors, repeated facades, symmetric parking structures).

A false loop closure injects an edge whose measurement $z_{ij}$ is grossly inconsistent with the true relative pose $h(x_i, x_j)$.

### 4.4 Robustification: Switchable Constraints, Dynamic Covariance Scaling, and M-Estimation

Three families of defenses have emerged; all can be understood as making the influence of a residual bounded.

**Switchable constraints** (Sünderhauf & Protzel, 2012) augment each loop edge with a latent switch variable $s_{ij} \in [0,1]$ that scales the edge's information matrix [6]:

$$\min_{\{T_i\},\{s_{ij}\}} \sum_{\text{odom}} \|\mathbf{e}_{ij}\|^2_{\Sigma^{-1}} + \sum_{\text{loops}} s_{ij}^2\,\|\mathbf{e}_{ij}\|^2_{\Sigma^{-1}} + \lambda(1 - s_{ij})^2.$$

The optimizer can "switch off" an edge that disagrees with the graph, paying the penalty $\lambda(1-s_{ij})^2$ to discourage gratuitous disabling.

**Dynamic covariance scaling (DCS)** (Agarwal et al., 2013) observes that the optimal switch value has a *closed form* in the current residual, so the switches can be eliminated analytically: each loop edge's covariance is inflated on the fly as a function of its $\chi^2$ error [8][6]. Small residual → full trust; large residual → inflated covariance, effectively removing the edge. DCS behaves like a robust M-estimator kernel, adds no new variables, and converges markedly faster than switchable constraints; it is now integrated into g2o [8].

The Rust fragment below shows the DCS scaling rule applied per loop edge inside a Gauss–Newton iteration:

```rust
/// Dynamic covariance scaling for one loop-closure edge.
/// chi2: squared Mahalanobis residual; phi: kernel width parameter.
fn dcs_scale(chi2: f64, phi: f64) -> f64 {
    // Closed-form optimal switch value (Agarwal et al., ICRA 2013)
    if chi2 <= phi {
        1.0 // inlier regime: full information
    } else {
        // outlier regime: inflate covariance ~ chi2/phi,
        // bounding the edge's influence like a robust kernel
        let s = (2.0 * phi) / (phi + chi2);
        s * s // scales the information matrix Omega <- s^2 * Omega
    }
}
```

**M-estimators and beyond.** Both schemes are instances of robust M-estimation: replace $\|\mathbf{e}\|^2$ with $\rho(\|\mathbf{e}\|)$ where $\rho$ grows sub-quadratically (Huber, Cauchy, Geman–McClure). Rosen, Kaess, and Leonard lifted this into the incremental setting, showing how robust kernels compose with the Bayes tree for online inference beyond the Gaussian case [8]. Later work introduced max-mixtures, graduated non-convexity, and certifiably optimal formulations, but DCS remains the pragmatic default for its zero-overhead integration [8][6].

### 4.5 Bag-of-Words Place Recognition and Geometric Verification

Robustness in the back end does not excuse sloppiness in the front end.

1. **Retrieval.** DBoW2 converts the current frame's binary descriptors into a bag-of-words vector and scores it against all keyframes via an inverted index — sublinear in database size [5].
2. **Temporal consistency.** Candidates must appear in temporally coherent groups, suppressing isolated false alarms.
3. **Geometric verification.** Feature correspondences between the query and candidate keyframes must support a consistent Sim(3) transformation under RANSAC; only then is a loop edge (with covariance from the alignment inliers) added to the graph.

Even with this cascade, the residual risk of false positives is why the back end must be robust: front-end verification reduces the outlier *rate*, while DCS-class robustification bounds the *damage* of the outliers that slip through.

## 5. Empirical Results / Proofs

### 5.1 Complexity

> **Theorem (iSAM2 per-step complexity):** For a SLAM factor graph with $n$ variables and bounded-degree measurement models, the amortized cost of one iSAM2 update is $O(1)$ in the size of the affected subtree, which in exploration is a small constant fraction of $n$; a loop closure re-eliminates a path whose length is bounded by the tree height, typically $O(\log n)$ to $O(\sqrt{n})$ depending on ordering quality [1].

In practice, Kaess et al. report that iSAM2 matches batch optimization accuracy on the standard benchmarks (including the 10,000-pose Manhattan and 100,000-pose W10000 datasets) while its per-step timing stays flat where batch cost grows superlinearly [1].

### 5.2 Robustness Under Outliers

Agarwal et al. demonstrate DCS on real datasets including Victoria Park, injecting false loop closures and comparing against standard Gauss–Newton [7]: the non-robust baseline collapses (trajectory folded, mean error an order of magnitude above nominal), while DCS recovers the correct topology with accuracy indistinguishable from the outlier-free run [8]. Switchable constraints achieve comparable robustness at roughly twice the iteration count [6]. These experiments use exactly the failure mode of §4.3: perceptual aliasing synthesized as random inter-pose edges.

### 5.3 System-Level Evidence

ORB-SLAM2 integrates the full stack — DBoW2 retrieval, Sim(3) geometric verification, and pose-graph optimization over a covisibility graph — and reports, across KITTI odometry sequences, EuRoC MAV datasets, and TUM RGB-D sequences, trajectory errors competitive with or better than contemporary stereo and RGB-D systems, with loop closures correcting drift by an order of magnitude on long trajectories [5].

| Approach | Incremental? | Handles nonlinearity | Outlier-robust | Typical per-step cost |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| EKF SLAM | Yes | Frozen linearization | No | $O(m^2)$, $m$ = landmarks |
| Batch Gauss–Newton | No | Full relinearization | No | Superlinear in $n$ |
| iSAM (2008) | Yes | Periodic batch reset | No | Near-constant, periodic spikes |
| iSAM2 (2012) | Yes | Fluid relinearization | Via robust kernels [8] | Near-constant, no spikes |
| Pose graph + DCS | Batch/incremental | Relinearized | Yes, closed-form [8] | Like Gauss–Newton, no extra vars |

A minimal TLA+ sketch of the loop-closure verification protocol (front-end/back-end contract) illustrates the safety property the cascade must guarantee:

```tla
------------------------------ MODULE LoopClosure ------------------------------
EXTENDS Naturals, Sequences
VARIABLES keyframes, candidates, graph

ProposeClosure(q, k) ==
    /\ DBoWScore(q, k) > retrievalThreshold
    /\ TemporalConsistent(q, k, keyframes)
    /\ candidates' = candidates \cup {<<q, k>>}
    /\ UNCHANGED <<keyframes, graph>>

VerifyClosure(q, k) ==
    /\ <<q, k>> \in candidates
    /\ LET inliers == RansacSim3(q, k) IN
       IF Cardinality(inliers) >= minInliers
       THEN /\ graph' = graph \cup {LoopEdge(q, k, CovarianceOf(inliers))}
            /\ candidates' = candidates \ {<<q, k>>}
       ELSE /\ candidates' = candidates \ {<<q, k>>}
            /\ UNCHANGED graph
    /\ UNCHANGED keyframes

Safety == \A e \in Edges(graph) : e.type = "loop" => e.verified = TRUE
=============================================================================
```

## 6. Limitations

The framework developed here has genuine boundaries, and honesty about them is what separates a thesis from a sales pitch.

1. **Gaussianity is still assumed.** The Bayes tree encodes Gaussian conditionals; multimodal posteriors (e.g., genuine ambiguity between two topologically distinct loop hypotheses) are collapsed to a single mode. Max-mixture and nonparametric extensions exist but sacrifice the clean incremental structure [8].
2. **Robust kernels need tuning.** DCS's kernel width $\phi$ and switchable constraints' penalty $\lambda$ are hyperparameters; set $\phi$ too small and true loop closures are rejected, too large and outliers survive. Self-tuning M-estimators mitigate but do not eliminate this [8].
3. **Data association is not solved.** Robust back ends bound the damage of false loop closures; they do not prevent them. Perceptual aliasing in highly repetitive environments (parking garages, warehouses) remains a front-end research problem [5][3].
4. **Dynamic environments.** The entire formulation assumes a static world. Moving objects violate the measurement model and must be detected and excluded upstream — a requirement the factor graph itself cannot enforce [3].
5. **Scalability of the tree.** While per-step updates are near-constant, the Bayes tree stores the full trajectory; lifelong operation demands sparsification, node marginalization, or submapping, each reintroducing approximation error [1][3].

---

## 7. Conclusion

This thesis has traced a single coherent arc through modern SLAM. The **factor graph** provides the modeling language in which odometry, landmark observations, and loop closures are all just factors over shared variables [2]. The **Bayes tree** turns the abstract algebra of incremental matrix factorization into a concrete, editable data structure, and iSAM2 exploits it to achieve online smoothing with fluid relinearization and incremental reordering — no periodic batch resets, no frozen linearization points [1]. **Robust optimization** — from switchable constraints through dynamic covariance scaling to M-estimators — supplies the back end's immune system against the inevitable false loop closures that perceptual aliasing produces [6][8]. Together with appearance-based place recognition and geometric verification in the front end [5], these ideas constitute the architecture that carried SLAM from laboratory demonstrations to the robust-perception age surveyed by Cadena et al. [3].

The open frontier is not accuracy on curated datasets — that battle is largely won — but *lifelong robustness*: dynamic worlds, changing appearance, and the principled management of uncertainty over years of operation. The factor graph will surely remain the language in which those problems are posed; whether the Bayes tree remains the engine that solves them incrementally is a question for the next decade.
