# Equality Saturation over E-Graphs: Congruence Closure, Rewrite Scheduling and Saturation Strategies, Extraction with Cost Functions, and the egg Framework for Program Optimization

## Abstract

Equality saturation is a rewrite-driven optimization paradigm that replaces destructive, order-dependent rewriting with non-destructive exploration of all reachable terms inside an e-graph — a compact data structure representing a congruence relation over exponentially many equivalent programs. This article develops the full theory and practice: congruence-closure foundations and e-graph invariants; the saturation loop with read/write phase separation and rewrite-order invariance; the rewrite scheduling problem, including match explosion and egg's exponential-backoff scheduler; e-matching recast as worst-case-optimal relational joins; deferred congruence maintenance via rebuilding, which yields asymptotic speedups; extraction as minimum-cost selection from an e-class DAG, NP-hard in general but tractable by dynamic programming for additive costs and parameterized by treewidth via a circuit correspondence; e-class analyses and the egglog unification of Datalog with equality saturation; and empirical results from floating-point accuracy, CAD synthesis, and industrial deployments, closing with limitations and open frontiers.

---

## 1. Introduction

Program optimization by term rewriting is as old as compilation itself, yet the classical formulation — apply rewrite rules to an abstract syntax tree until no rule matches — suffers from a fundamental pathology: *committed choice*. Each rewrite is destructive; once a rule fires, the un-rewritten alternative is discarded, and the quality of the final program depends critically on the order in which rules are tried [5]. A rule applied too eagerly can foreclose a later, far better derivation; a rule applied too late may never match at all. Compilers compensate with carefully tuned heuristics, ordered rewrite lists, and ad hoc phase ordering, but these are fragile: they encode, rather than solve, the ordering problem.

Equality saturation [5] rejects committed choice: instead of replacing a subterm, a *non-destructive* engine records the equivalence in the **e-graph** and continues exploring. The e-graph grows monotonically, so rewrites may be applied simultaneously, in any order, with no risk of a "wrong turn" [1]. Only at the end — at fixpoint or timeout — does **extraction** select the best representative per e-class under a user-defined cost function.

The past decade has transformed equality saturation from a research curiosity into a production technique. The **egg** library (Willsey, Nandi, Wang, Flatt, Tatlock, and Panchekha) supplied the engineering substrate — deferred congruence maintenance via *rebuilding*, e-class *analyses*, and a principled iteration scheme — and achieved order-of-magnitude speedups over prior e-graph implementations [1]. Relational e-matching recast pattern matching as a worst-case-optimal database join [2]. **egglog** unified Datalog with equality saturation in a single fixpoint engine [3]. Meanwhile, the extraction problem itself has become a research field: the connection between e-graphs and monotone Boolean circuits yields optimal extraction parameterized by treewidth [4], and exact ILP-based extraction powers industrial deployments [6].

This article presents a self-contained, graduate-level treatment: foundations (Section 2), the saturation loop and scheduling (Section 3), e-matching, rebuilding, analyses, and extraction (Section 4), empirical and complexity results (Section 5), limitations (Section 6), and open directions (Section 7).

## 2. Background

### 2.1 Congruence closure and the e-graph

An **e-graph** is a data structure for representing a congruence relation over a set of terms. It was developed in the late 1970s for automated theorem proving, where congruence-closure procedures decide the validity of quantifier-free formulas over uninterpreted function symbols combined with equality [7]. Formally, let Σ be a signature and *T*(Σ) the set of ground terms. An e-graph maintains:

- **E-nodes**: function applications *f*(*c*₁, …, *c*ₙ), where each *c*ᵢ is an *e-class identifier* rather than a term, enabling maximal sharing (hash-consing) of equivalent subterms.
- **E-classes**: sets of e-nodes closed under the union-find equivalence, each with a canonical representative maintained by a union-find structure.
- **Parent lists**: for each e-class, the set of e-nodes that reference it as a child, enabling efficient upward propagation of merges.

Two invariants define a well-formed e-graph [1]:

> **Definition (E-graph invariants).** An e-graph is *valid* when (i) the union-find equivalence is a *congruence*: if *a*ᵢ ≅ *b*ᵢ for all *i*, then *f*(*a*₁,…, *a*ₙ) ≅ *f*(*b*₁,…, *b*ₙ); and (ii) equivalent terms are stored *without duplication*: no two distinct e-nodes in the same e-class are syntactically identical up to canonical e-class ids.

### 2.2 Term rewriting and the phase-ordering problem

A rule ℓ → *r* is applied **destructively**: find σ with ℓσ in the term, replace by *r*σ, repeat. The outcome depends on:

1. **Rule order** — which rule fires when several match.
2. **Match order** — which occurrence is rewritten when a rule matches multiply.
3. **Termination strategy** — when to stop iterating.

This is the *phase-ordering problem*; equality saturation sidesteps it by refusing to choose.

### 2.3 The saturation loop

The canonical equality saturation algorithm [5] is:

```python
def equality_saturation(expr, rewrites):
    egraph = initial_egraph(expr)
    while not egraph.is_saturated_or_timeout():
        matches = []
        # read-only phase: invariants preserved
        for rw in rewrites:
            for (subst, eclass) in egraph.ematch(rw.lhs):
                matches.append((rw, subst, eclass))
        # write-only phase: invariants temporarily broken
        for (rw, subst, eclass) in matches:
            eclass2 = egraph.add(rw.rhs.subst(subst))
            egraph.merge(eclass, eclass2)
        # restore invariants once per iteration
        egraph.rebuild()
    return egraph.extract_best()
```

Three properties distinguish this from naive rewriting [1]: the read phase e-matches patterns against *all terms represented by the e-graph* without mutating it; the write phase tolerates a temporarily broken congruence invariant; and `rebuild` restores congruence once per iteration — an amortization yielding a 21× geometric-mean speedup [1].

> **Theorem (Rewrite-order invariance [1]).** In egg's split-phase saturation loop, if the e-graph reaches a fixed point (true saturation), the result is independent of the order of the rewrite list. Moreover, even when saturation is not reached, no rewrite is starved by another rewrite's output within a single iteration.

The theorem matters because real workloads rarely saturate: the iteration budget is typically exhausted by a timeout, and any dependence on rule ordering would make results irreproducible across runs.

## 3. Methodology

Our treatment follows a layered methodology. We first formalize the e-graph as an abstract data type with explicit invariants, then develop the saturation loop as a monotone fixpoint computation over a lattice of equivalence relations, then analyze scheduling and extraction as separate optimization problems that admit independent algorithmic treatment. Each layer is grounded in implemented systems — principally egg [1], its relational e-matching extension [2], and egglog [3] — so that every theorem stated here corresponds to running code rather than a purely paper construction.

### 3.1 The e-graph as a monotone fixpoint

Saturation is a **least-fixpoint computation**: the iteration function *F*(≅) = ≅ ∪ {equalities from one rewriting round plus congruence closure} is monotone over the lattice of equivalence relations, and the loop computes its least fixpoint above the initial relation..

### 3.2 Pattern language and substitutions

Rewrite patterns in egg are *s-expressions* with variables (`?x`), supporting non-linear patterns (repeated variables enforce equality of the matched e-classes) and multi-patterns. E-matching a pattern against an e-graph returns substitutions mapping each variable to an *e-class* (not a term): the match `(+ ?x ?x)` succeeds on the e-class containing both `(+ a b)` and `(* a a)` whenever `a ≅ b`. This is strictly more powerful than syntactic matching — it is matching *modulo the current theory*, which grows as saturation proceeds.

### 3.3 Cost functions and extraction as optimization

Extraction selects, for a distinguished root e-class, the minimum-cost term among all terms the e-graph represents. A **cost function** maps e-nodes to costs given the costs of their children; the canonical example is AST size, `cost(f(c₁…cₙ)) = 1 + Σ cost(cᵢ)`, but realistic deployments use weighted models (e.g., floating-point operation latencies, or Herbie's accuracy-aware cost [8])..

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 E-matching: from backtracking to relational joins

E-matching is the dominant cost of most saturation workloads, and its algorithmic history is a microcosm of the field. Classical implementations use **top-down backtracking search**: recursively match the pattern's root against e-nodes, then match children against the corresponding e-classes, backtracking on failure. The worst case is exponential in pattern size, and — critically — the search order is fixed by the pattern's tree structure, which may be far from optimal.

Zhang et al. [2] recast e-matching as a **relational query**: e-classes become relations, patterns become conjunctive queries, and matching becomes a *join* executed by a **worst-case-optimal join algorithm** (generic join) with a planner-chosen variable order. The paper also establishes the first *data complexity* result for e-matching.

```rust
// egg's relational e-matching, conceptually: patterns compile to joins
// over the e-graph "database". A pattern like (* (+ ?x ?y) ?x) becomes:
//   JOIN enodes AS n1 ON n1.op = '*'
//   JOIN enodes AS n2 ON n2.op = '+' AND n2.class IN children(n1)
//   WHERE child(n1, 0) = class(n2) AND child(n1, 1) = var(?x)
//        AND child(n2, 0) = var(?x) AND child(n2, 1) = var(?y)
fn ematch_join(pattern: &Pattern, db: &EGraphDB) -> Vec<Subst> {
    let plan = optimize_join_order(pattern); // query planner
    generic_join(&plan, db)                  // worst-case optimal
}
```

Relational e-matching is orders of magnitude faster than backtracking on complex patterns [2]; egglog later removed the translation layer entirely, gaining a further 3.34× over egg via semi-naïve evaluation [3].

### 4.2 Rebuilding: deferred congruence maintenance

The naive congruence-closure repair — re-canonicalizing parent e-nodes and re-merging after *every* union — dominates runtime in saturation workloads, because a single iteration may perform thousands of merges, each triggering overlapping cascades of repair work. Egg's **rebuilding** defers all repair to a single pass per iteration [1]:

1. During the write phase, merges are recorded in a *worklist* of e-classes whose parents may now violate congruence, but no repair is performed.
2. `rebuild()` processes the worklist: for each e-class, it re-canonicalizes the parent e-nodes (looking up their new canonical ids), deduplicates them in the hash-cons table, and merges any duplicates — appending newly affected e-classes to the worklist until it empties.
3. Deduplication of the worklist itself is the key amortization: many merges affect the same parents, and a single repair pass handles them together.

> **Theorem (Rebuild correctness [1]).** When `rebuild()` terminates with an empty worklist, the e-graph satisfies both invariants: the union-find relation is a congruence, and no two e-nodes in the same e-class are syntactically duplicate. The final relation equals the congruence closure of all merges performed since the last rebuild.

The quantitative impact is dramatic: deferred rebuilding makes congruence maintenance 88× faster (geometric mean) and end-to-end equality saturation 21× faster across the egg benchmark suite, with the speedup *growing* with problem size — an asymptotic, not constant-factor, improvement [1].

### 4.3 Rewrite scheduling: the backoff scheduler and saturation strategies

In practice, saturation runs under a time budget, so *which* rewrites fire *when* — the **rewrite scheduling problem** — determines result quality. The pathology is *match explosion*: associativity, `(+ (+ ?a ?b) ?c) → (+ ?a (+ ?b ?c))`, matches combinatorially many times, growing the e-graph exponentially for little new knowledge.

Egg's answer is the **Backoff scheduler** [1], inspired by exponential backoff in networking:

| Scheduler | Policy | Strength | Weakness |
|---|---|---|---|
| `SimpleScheduler` | Apply every match, every iteration | Predictable | Match explosion |
| `BackoffScheduler` (default) | Ban a rule for 2ᵏ iterations after it matches > *limit* times *k* times in a row | Tames explosive rules | May delay useful rules |
| `ReportScheduler` | Wrapper recording per-rule statistics | Observability | Overhead only |
| Custom (`Scheduler` trait) | User-defined `can_apply` predicate | Domain control | Implementation burden |

The backoff scheduler maintains, per rewrite rule, a counter of consecutive over-limit iterations and a ban duration that doubles each time. A rule that matches explosively is silenced for exponentially growing windows, letting the e-graph digest cheaper rules first; if the rule later matches modestly, the ban resets. This is a *saturation strategy* in the technical sense: it does not change the fixpoint (banned rules are eventually reconsidered), but it dramatically changes the trajectory toward it under a budget.

### 4.4 Extraction: cost functions, dynamic programming, and the hardness frontier

Extraction is where optimization objectives enter. Given a cost function *C* mapping e-nodes to reals (typically additive: *C*(*f*(*c*₁…*c*ₙ)) = *w*(f) + Σ *C*(*c*ᵢ)), extraction computes, bottom-up, the minimum-cost e-node in each e-class and then reads out the optimal term from the root. For additive costs over an *acyclic* e-class dependency graph, a simple dynamic program is optimal:

```python
def extract(root, egraph, cost):
    best = {}  # eclass -> (cost, enode)
    # process e-classes in topological order (children before parents)
    for c in egraph.topological_order():
        best[c] = min(
            (cost(n, [best[ch][0] for ch in n.children]), n)
            for n in c.nodes
        )
    return build_term(root, best)
```

> **Theorem (Optimal extraction for additive costs on DAGs).** If the e-class dependency graph is acyclic and the cost function is additive and non-negative, the bottom-up dynamic program returns the minimum-cost term represented by the root e-class. *Proof sketch.* By induction on topological order: the optimal term for class *c* consists of an optimal choice of e-node *n* ∈ *c* and, recursively, optimal terms for each child class. Additive costs make the subproblems independent. ∎

Two complications arise: e-graphs can contain *cycles* (e.g., `?x ≅ (+ ?x 0)`), defeating naive bottom-up DP, and — fundamentally — **optimal extraction is NP-hard in general**, because DAG sharing turns selection into a facility-location-like combinatorial problem [4, 6].

Sun, Zhang, and Ni [4] characterize *when* extraction is tractable via an equivalence between e-graphs and (cyclic) **monotone Boolean circuits** — e-nodes as AND gates, e-classes as OR gates — importing weighted-circuit-satisfiability results to obtain a 2^O(w²)·poly(w, n) algorithm in the e-graph's **treewidth** *w*. Circuit-view simplifications cut real e-graph size and treewidth by 40–80% [4]..

### 4.5 E-class analyses and egglog: extensibility beyond syntactic rewriting

Pure syntactic rewriting cannot express conditional rules like `?x / ?x → 1 if ?x ≠ 0`, because the side condition requires *semantic* knowledge. Egg's **e-class analyses** solve this by attaching a lattice-valued analysis to each e-class, maintained automatically during rebuilding [1]:


Analyses enable conditional rewriting, demand-driven abstraction, and the encoding of abstract interpretations directly in the saturation loop — all with the framework maintaining analysis invariants through merges automatically.

**egglog** [3] unifies Datalog and equality saturation in one language: relations, rules, and rewrites coexist under semi-naïve evaluation, supporting cooperating analyses and lattice reasoning that egg's single-analysis design cannot express. Reimplemented pointer analyses and floating-point rewriters came out *faster, simpler, and less buggy* than the originals [3].

## 5. Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 The egg evaluation

The egg paper [1] evaluates on 32 tests drawn from three previously published equality-saturation applications: the Herbie floating-point accuracy improver [8], the Szalinski CAD decompiler, and a library-learning synthesizer. Key quantitative findings:

- **Deferred rebuilding**: congruence maintenance 88× faster (geometric mean); end-to-end saturation 21× faster; speedups grow with problem size (Fig. 6–7 of [1]).
- **Case studies**: egg reproduced and *extended* prior results — Herbie with simpler rule sets, Szalinski with new inverse transformations [1].
- **Distinguished Paper** at POPL 2021, signaling community consensus on the techniques' importance.

### 5.2 Relational e-matching and egglog benchmarks

Relational e-matching [2] is orders of magnitude faster than backtracking on complex patterns. The egglog evaluation [3] isolates incrementality via egglogNI (no semi-naïve evaluation): on 100 identical math-rewriting iterations, egglogNI achieves **3.34×** over egg and full egglog **9.27×** [3].

### 5.3 The extraction complexity landscape

The theoretical results form a coherent picture:

> **Theorem (Extraction hardness).** Optimal e-graph extraction under additive cost functions with sharing is NP-hard in general (reduction from facility-location / set-cover variants; see [4, 6]).

> **Theorem (Treewidth-parameterized tractability [4]).** There exists an algorithm for optimal extraction running in 2^O(w²)·poly(w, n) time, where *w* is the treewidth of the e-graph viewed as a monotone Boolean circuit.

> **Theorem (E-matching data complexity [2]).** Relational e-matching runs in time bounded by the AGM bound as a function of e-graph size and output size — i.e., worst-case optimal in the database-theoretic sense.

Together these delineate the tractability frontier: the *search* phase is worst-case optimal given the pattern set, while the *selection* phase is tractable exactly when the e-graph's sharing structure is tree-like — which real-world e-graphs often are after simplification [4].

### 5.4 Industrial deployments

Equality saturation has crossed into production. Herbie [8] uses e-graph-based rewriting to improve floating-point accuracy automatically; the egg rewrite enabled more aggressive rule sets. The egglog work reports a **pointer analysis for real codebases** reimplemented with better performance and fewer bugs [3]. The awesome-egraphs survey [9] documents deployments in EVM bytecode simplification (Certora), WebAssembly JIT optimization (Fastly), and datapath optimization (Intel) — evidence that the egg ecosystem's engineering investments transferred across domains.

## 6. Limitations

**Non-termination and saturation failure.** Saturation is a *semi-decision* procedure: with rules like `?x → (+ ?x 0)` the e-graph grows forever. Timeouts are necessities, and budget-limited results are *approximations* whose quality depends on scheduling — reintroducing, through the back door, the order-dependence saturation was designed to eliminate.

**The extraction gap.** The saturation loop explores equivalences without regard to cost; extraction then picks the best. But the *optimal program* may require equivalences the budget never discovered. There is no general theorem bounding how far budget-limited extraction is from the true optimum, and cost functions themselves are heuristic models — Herbie's accuracy cost, for instance, is a proxy for true floating-point error [8].

**E-matching worst cases persist.** Worst-case-optimal joins bound runtime by input and output size, but the *output size* — the number of matches — can itself be exponential (again, associativity). The backoff scheduler mitigates this empirically [1], but no scheduler offers theoretical guarantees under a budget.

**Cost-function design is manual.** Extraction is only as good as its cost model, and cost models are hand-tuned per domain. Learned cost functions and differentiable extraction (e.g., SmoothE [9]) are promising but immature.

**Semantic gap.** E-graphs reason about *syntactic* equivalence under the rewrite theory; they cannot discover equivalences outside the rule set. Rule discovery (as in the Ruler system [1]) and verified lifting remain active research.

## 7. Conclusion

Equality saturation reframes program optimization as *monotone knowledge accumulation* rather than destructive transformation. The e-graph — born in 1970s theorem provers as a congruence-closure device [7] — turned out to be the right substrate for representing the space of equivalent programs compactly; the saturation loop turned out to be the right dynamics for exploring it without committed choice [5]; and the egg framework [1] supplied the engineering — deferred rebuilding, e-class analyses, backoff scheduling — that made the theory fast. Relational e-matching [2] and egglog [3] then showed that the remaining bottlenecks yield to database theory, while the circuit connection [4] mapped the exact boundary of tractable extraction.

The open frontiers are clear: *certified* saturation, where every merge carries a checkable proof certificate (early work exists [9]); *learned* scheduling that replaces exponential backoff with data-driven policies; *compositional* cost models that close the extraction gap; and the continued push of e-graph infrastructure into compilers, solvers, and synthesizers. If the last decade established equality saturation as a practical optimization paradigm, the next will determine how far its guarantees can be pushed — from best-effort improvement toward verified, optimal compilation.
