# Approximate Homomorphic Encryption with CKKS: Residue Number Systems, Bootstrapping without Decryption, and Encrypted Machine Learning

## Abstract

The Cheon–Kim–Kim–Song (CKKS) scheme reframes homomorphic encryption as a *numerical* computing primitive: instead of exact arithmetic over integers or bits, it encrypts vectors of complex numbers and evaluates arithmetic with a controlled approximation error that behaves like floating-point roundoff [1]. This single design decision — accepting an infinitesimal, well-characterized error — unlocks polynomial-depth computation over real data and makes privacy-preserving machine learning practical. This thesis examines the three engineering pillars that carried CKKS from theory to production: the full residue number system (RNS) variant that replaced multi-precision arithmetic with word-sized operations and NTT-friendly moduli [2]; the bootstrapping procedure that refreshes exhausted ciphertexts *without ever decrypting them*, built on homomorphic modular reduction via optimal polynomial approximation [3]; and the encrypted machine learning stack — polynomial activations, packed convolutions, and functional bootstrapping — that now evaluates ResNet-scale networks on ciphertexts [6][7]. We analyze precision semantics rigorously, derive the rescaling error bounds, and survey the empirical state of the art.

## 1. Introduction

Fully homomorphic encryption (FHE) has long promised computation on encrypted data, but its first practical instantiations — BGV, BFV, and the CGGI/TFHE torus framework — were designed for *exact* arithmetic: integers modulo a plaintext modulus, or single bits. Machine learning, however, lives in the real numbers. Weights are floating-point, activations are smooth nonlinearities, and the entire numerical-analysis tradition accepts that a result correct to 15 decimal digits is correct enough. Forcing real-valued pipelines through exact-integer circuits meant either catastrophic quantization error or circuit depths that no implementation could survive.

The breakthrough of Cheon, Kim, Kim, and Song (ASIACRYPT 2017) was to stop fighting this reality [1]: CKKS treats RLWE decryption noise as *part of the message semantics*. A CKKS ciphertext encrypts $m$ but decrypts to $m + e$, where $e$ is small relative to the scaling factor $\Delta$; the scheme guarantees only that the *significant* digits of the message survive. In exchange, CKKS natively supports addition and multiplication of complex vectors, a rescaling operation that mimics floating-point rounding, and SIMD packing of $N/2$ values per ciphertext — precisely the operations that numerical linear algebra and neural networks need.

Three developments turned this elegant idea into infrastructure. First, the **full RNS variant** restructured the ciphertext modulus as a product of word-sized primes, enabling RNS decomposition and the Number Theoretic Transform on every component, so that all arithmetic fits in 64-bit machine words [2][4]. Second, **bootstrapping** — historically the slowest and least practical part of any FHE scheme — was re-engineered for the approximate setting: instead of decrypting homomorphically, CKKS bootstrapping homomorphically evaluates *modular reduction* using polynomial approximations of trigonometric functions, refreshing the modulus budget without ever exposing the plaintext [3]. Third, the **encrypted ML stack** grew up around these primitives: polynomial activations replace ReLUs, multiplexed convolutions pack entire feature maps into slots, and bootstrapping is interleaved with inference to evaluate networks as deep as ResNet-110 on encrypted images [6].

> **Thesis claim.** Approximate homomorphic encryption is not a weakened form of FHE but a distinct computational model — *homomorphic floating point* — whose precision semantics, modulus management, and refresh procedures deserve the same rigorous treatment as classical numerical analysis. This thesis provides that treatment.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 Ring Learning With Errors

CKKS security rests on the *Ring Learning With Errors* (RLWE) problem over the $2N$-th cyclotomic ring $R = \mathbb{Z}[X]/(X^N + 1)$ with $N$ a power of two. A secret $s \in R$ with small (typically ternary) coefficients is sampled; the adversary must distinguish polynomially many samples $(a_i, a_i s + e_i)$ from uniform, where $a_i$ is uniform in $R_q$ and $e_i$ is drawn from a narrow discrete Gaussian. Parameters follow the HomomorphicEncryption.org standard, targeting 128-bit security against lattice-reduction (BKZ/sieving) attacks.

A ciphertext $\mathbf{ct} = (c_0, c_1) \in R_q^2$ satisfies

$$\langle \mathbf{ct}, \mathbf{sk} \rangle = c_0 + c_1 \cdot s = m + e \pmod{q},$$

where $m$ is the *scaled* plaintext polynomial and $e$ is small. Unlike BGV/BFV, **the error $e$ is a permanent, bounded contaminant of the low-order bits** — like rounding error in IEEE floating point.

### 2.2 The Canonical Embedding and Slots

The ring $R$ admits the *canonical embedding* $\sigma: R \to \mathbb{C}^{N/2}$,

$$\sigma(m) = \big(m(\zeta), m(\zeta^3), \dots, m(\zeta^{2(N/2)-1})\big),$$

where $\zeta = e^{2\pi i / 2N}$ is a primitive $2N$-th root of unity. CKKS packs **$N/2$ complex numbers into a single ciphertext** — the SIMD *slots*.

Encoding a message vector $\mathbf{z} \in \mathbb{C}^{N/2}$ with scaling factor $\Delta$ is

$$\mathrm{Ecd}(\mathbf{z}; \Delta) = \big\lfloor \Delta \cdot \sigma^{-1}(\mathbf{z}) \big\rceil \in R,$$

and decoding inverts it: $\mathrm{Dcd}(m; \Delta) = \Delta^{-1} \sigma(m)$. The Galois automorphisms $X \mapsto X^{2k+1}$ permute the slots — the algebraic origin of CKKS's *rotation* operation [1].

### 2.3 Rescaling: Homomorphic Floating Point

Multiplying ciphertexts at scale $\Delta$ yields scale $\Delta^2$ — as with un-normalized fixed-point integers. CKKS's signature operation, **rescaling**, divides out one factor:

$$\mathrm{RS}_{q \to q'}(\mathbf{ct}) = \big\lfloor (q'/q) \cdot \mathbf{ct} \big\rceil \pmod{q'},$$

returning a ciphertext encrypting $\approx m_1 m_2$ at scale $\Delta$. The rounding introduces an error of magnitude $\approx 1/\Delta$ relative to the message — the homomorphic analogue of round-to-nearest. The original paper proves that the total precision loss relative to unencrypted floating-point arithmetic is bounded by roughly **one bit per multiplication**, independent of circuit depth [1]:

> **Theorem (Rescaling precision, Cheon et al.).** Let $\beta_0$ be the relative error of fresh encodings and $\beta_*$ the relative key-switching error. After evaluating a depth-$d$ arithmetic circuit, the relative error of a CKKS ciphertext is bounded by $d \cdot (\beta_0 + \beta_*)$ plus rounding terms of order $2^{-\log \Delta}$. In particular, choosing $\Delta \approx 2^{40}$–$2^{60}$ keeps the scheme within one bit of IEEE double precision for practical depths.

CKKS is thus *homomorphic floating point*: the user picks a working precision $\Delta$ and a depth budget, as with `float32` vs `float64`.

## 3. Methodology

### 3.1 The RNS-CKKS Construction

The original CKKS implementation required multi-precision integer arithmetic on a monolithic modulus $q$ of several hundred to over a thousand bits — a poor fit for modern CPUs. The full RNS variant of Cheon, Han, Kim, Kim, and Song restructures the modulus as a **chain of word-sized primes** [2]:

$$q_\ell = \prod_{i=0}^{\ell} p_i, \qquad \ell = 0, \dots, L,$$

where each $p_i$ is a $\approx 60$-bit prime with $p_i \equiv 1 \pmod{2N}$ (NTT-friendly). A ciphertext at *level* $\ell$ lives in $R_{q_\ell}^2$, and every polynomial is stored in its RNS/CRT representation $(m \bmod p_0, \dots, m \bmod p_\ell)$. Two consequences follow:

1. **All arithmetic is 64-bit** — no big-integer library in the hot loop [2].
2. **Rescaling becomes modulus dropping**: divide the CRT representation by $p_\ell$ and drop that residue — $O(\ell)$ with precomputed inverses, no RNS composition needed [2][4].

Key switching — relinearizing the degree-2 term $c_2 s^2$ after each multiplication — uses hybrid RNS decomposition with a special extension modulus $P$ [1][2].

| Parameter set | $N$ | $L$ (mult. depth) | Slots | $\log q_L$ | Security |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| CKKS-128 small | $2^{13}$ | 4 | 4,096 | $\approx 218$ | 128-bit |
| CKKS-128 medium | $2^{15}$ | 12 | 16,384 | $\approx 615$ | 128-bit |
| CKKS-128 bootstrap | $2^{16}$ | 24–30 | 32,768 | $\approx 1{,}550$ | 128-bit |
| CKKS-128 large | $2^{17}$ | 40+ | 65,536 | $\approx 2{,}300$ | 128-bit |

*Table 1. Representative RNS-CKKS parameter sets. Depth counts multiplications between bootstraps; the bootstrapping row reserves roughly half its levels for the refresh circuit itself [2][3][8].*

### 3.2 Simulating the Precision Model

The following Python model captures the fixed-point abstraction CKKS implements homomorphically:

```python
import numpy as np

class ApproxFloat:
    """Toy model of CKKS fixed-point semantics: value * Delta, rounded."""
    def __init__(self, z, Delta=2**40):
        self.z = np.asarray(z, dtype=complex)
        self.Delta = Delta
        self.m = np.round(self.z.real * Delta).astype(np.int64)  # Ecd

    def __mul__(self, other):
        # ct_mult: scales multiply -> Delta^2; then rescale divides by Delta
        raw = self.m.astype(np.float64) * other.m.astype(np.float64)
        m_new = np.round(raw / self.Delta).astype(np.int64)      # RS
        out = ApproxFloat.__new__(ApproxFloat)
        out.z, out.Delta, out.m = self.z * other.z, self.Delta, m_new
        return out

    def decode(self):
        return self.m.astype(np.float64) / self.Delta

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
x = ApproxFloat(rng.normal(size=8)); y = ApproxFloat(rng.normal(size=8))
for _ in range(10):                      # depth-10 product chain
    x = x * y
err = np.abs(x.decode() - (x.z)) / np.abs(x.z)
print(f"depth-10 relative error: {err.max():.3e}  (~{-np.log2(err.max()):.1f} bits)")
# depth-10 relative error: ~1e-11  (~36 bits) — within ~1 bit of float64
```

Roughly 36 bits survive ten chained multiplications — mirroring the theorem; the real scheme adds RLWE and key-switching noise on top of this shape.

### 3.3 The Bootstrapping Pipeline

A ciphertext at level $0$ (modulus $q_0$) can no longer be multiplied — its modulus budget is spent. **Bootstrapping** homomorphically re-encrypts it at a high level without decryption, in four stages [3][8]:

1. **ModRaise.** Lift $\mathbf{ct} \in R_{q_0}^2$ to modulus $Q = q_0 \cdot q_L$: coefficients are interpreted as integers in $[0, Q)$ rather than $[0, q_0)$. Decryption now yields $t = m + e + q_0 \cdot I$ for some *unknown multiple* $I$ with $\|I\|_\infty < K$ (small, since $m+e$ was small relative to $q_0$).
2. **CoeffToSlot.** Homomorphically evaluate the inverse DFT, moving the *coefficients* of $t$ (including the polluting term $q_0 I$) into the *slots*.
3. **EvalMod.** Homomorphically evaluate the modular-reduction function $t \mapsto t - q_0 \cdot \mathrm{round}(t/q_0) \approx m + e$, using a polynomial approximation. This is the mathematical heart of the procedure.
4. **SlotToCoeff.** Homomorphically evaluate the forward DFT, returning the cleaned message to coefficient form at high modulus.

The pipeline consumes roughly $L_{\mathrm{boot}} \approx 15$–$20$ levels — the deepest circuit most applications evaluate, dominating both latency and precision budgets.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 The SIMD Slot Structure and Its Algebraic Origin

The $N/2$ slots are not a mere packing trick — they are the *Galois orbits* of the cyclotomic field. Concretely, for $N = 2^n$, the $N/2$ distinct embeddings $m \mapsto m(\zeta^{2k+1})$ are permuted by the automorphisms $\kappa_j: X \mapsto X^j$ for odd $j$. Applying $\kappa_j$ to a ciphertext permutes its slots by the index map $k \mapsto jk \bmod 2N$, followed by key switching from $\kappa_j(s)$ back to $s$. Choosing $j = 5$ (resp. $j = 2N-1$) implements rotation by one (resp. complex conjugation) on the slot vector [1].

Rotations enable the *diagonal method* for matrix–vector products: $A\mathbf{x} = \sum_k \mathrm{diag}_k(A) \odot \mathrm{Rot}(\mathbf{x}, k)$ costs $N/2$ rotations but only one multiplicative level [6] — the reason encrypted dense layers are practical.

### 4.2 RNS Decomposition and the Rescaling Engine

The RNS variant's rescaling deserves a closer look, because it is where theory meets silicon. Given $\mathbf{ct}$ at level $\ell$ with residues $(c^{(0)}, \dots, c^{(\ell)})$ modulo $(p_0, \dots, p_\ell)$, the approximate rescale to level $\ell-1$ computes, per residue $i < \ell$:

$$c'^{(i)} = \big(p_\ell^{-1} \cdot (c^{(i)} - c^{(\ell)}) \big) \bmod p_i,$$

which equals $\lfloor c / p_\ell \rceil$ in the CRT reconstruction up to an error bounded by $\ell/2$ in the *integer* representatives — negligible once divided by the message scale $\Delta$ [2]. The improved variant of Kim, Papadimitriou, and Polyakov goes further: it eliminates the *approximate scaling error* entirely by tracking level-specific scaling factors $\Delta_\ell = q_\ell / q_{\ell-1}$ exactly, removing the LWE-noise-induced approximation error from encryption and key switching as well [4]. Their implementation demonstrates measurably lower error than both the original multiprecision CKKS and the first RNS port — a pure software-engineering win with cryptographic consequences, since lower noise means more usable levels for the same modulus budget.

### 4.3 Bootstrapping without Decryption: EvalMod and the Sine Trick

The soul of CKKS bootstrapping is **EvalMod**: given slots holding $t = m + e + q_0 I$ with $\|I\|_\infty \le K$, homomorphically compute $m + e$. The map $t \mapsto t \bmod q_0$ is a discontinuous sawtooth. The classical workaround uses the scaled sine

$$S(t) = \frac{q_0}{2\pi} \sin\!\left(\frac{2\pi t}{q_0}\right)$$

coincides with the modular-reduction map on the *union of intervals* $\bigcup_{i=-K}^{K} [iq_0 - \varepsilon, iq_0 + \varepsilon]$, up to the arcsine correction. EvalMod evaluates $P \approx S$ (Taylor, then Remez-refined) and *iterates* a scaled arcsine to sharpen the sawtooth edges — the composition of [3], refined by multi-interval Remez minimax polynomials over the interval union [5].

The depth–precision tradeoff is the central engineering curve of CKKS bootstrapping:

| Method | EvalMod depth | Post-bootstrap precision | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sine + arcsine composition [3] | 11–12 | $\approx$ 20 bits | Original practical baseline |
| Direct variance-optimal approx. | 10 | state-of-the-art | Lazy baby-step giant-step evaluation halves complexity [3] |
| Multi-interval Remez minimax | 10–12 | +5.4–10.2 bits vs baseline | Optimal $L_\infty$ error over interval union [5] |
| Odd sine series + iterative squaring | 10–12 | −25% error vs EUROCRYPT'22 | Up to $2^{12}$ slots at fixed error [5] |

*Table 2. Evolution of EvalMod precision. "Depth" counts multiplicative levels consumed by modular reduction alone.*

Refinements continue: multi-iteration bootstrapping chains cheap low-precision refreshes to beat one expensive bootstrap, and OverModRaise restructures CoeffToSlot/SlotToCoeff for a measured 41% throughput gain [8]. A stubborn precision ceiling remains (Section 6).

> **Theorem (Bootstrapping correctness sketch).** Let $\mathbf{ct}$ encrypt $m$ with $\|m\|_\infty < q_0/(4K)$. After ModRaise, CoeffToSlot, EvalMod with approximation error $\eta$, and SlotToCoeff, the output encrypts $m + e'$ where $\|e'\|_\infty \le \|e\|_\infty + \eta + O(N \cdot 2^{-\log \Delta})$ with overwhelming probability over the RLWE noise. The procedure reveals nothing about $m$: all steps are public homomorphic operations under the same key.

Note what this theorem does *not* say: the approximation error $\eta$ is deterministic given the parameters, and it is the dominant term — typically $2^{-15}$ to $2^{-25}$ relative. Every bootstrap permanently injects this error. Deep pipelines that bootstrap dozens of times must budget for it exactly as numerical analysts budget for accumulated roundoff.

### 4.4 Encrypted Machine Learning: Polynomials, Packing, and Depth

Neural networks pose three challenges for CKKS: (i) nonlinear activations are not polynomials; (ii) the multiplicative depth of a network far exceeds any leveled budget; (iii) latency must be tolerable. The community's answers, refined over a decade, are now systematic.

**Polynomial activations.** ReLU and sigmoid are replaced by least-squares or minimax polynomial approximations on the empirical activation range; composed minimax sign approximations reach comparison failure rates below $2^{-15}$ on RNS-CKKS, enabling max-pooling [4].

**Packing and multiplexed convolutions.** *Multiplexed parallel convolutions* pack sparse filter outputs densely, slashing the rotation count — the dominant cost. With *imaginary-removing bootstrapping* stabilizing approximate ReLUs, ResNet-20 runs at 128-bit security with **4.67× lower latency and 134× lower amortized time** than prior work, plus the first-ever RNS-CKKS ResNet-110 [6].

**Functional bootstrapping.** This fuses bootstrapping with function evaluation, applying a Hermite-interpolated activation *during* the refresh at negligible marginal depth. On MNIST (100 hidden neurons) it reaches **97.33% accuracy with 0% relative loss** versus plaintext at 128-bit security — at 1.66 s amortized per image and ~201 GB RAM [7].

| Workload | Scheme | Accuracy | Latency (amortized) | Security |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| MNIST, FC-100 | CKKS + functional bootstrap [7] | 97.33% | 1.66 s/image | 128-bit |
| CIFAR-10, ResNet-20 | RNS-CKKS + multiplexed conv [6] | $\approx$ plaintext − small | 134× better than prior SOTA | 128-bit |
| CIFAR-10, ResNet-110 | RNS-CKKS [6] | high (first HE result) | minutes/image | 128-bit |

*Table 3. Representative encrypted-inference results. "Amortized" divides total batch latency by batch size; single-image latency is far higher.*

## 5. Empirical Evaluation and Proofs

### 5.1 The Precision Ledger

We consolidate the error sources of a full RNS-CKKS pipeline into a single ledger — the document an engineer consults when choosing $\Delta$ and $L$:

1. **Encoding rounding:** $\|e_{\mathrm{enc}}\|_\infty \le \sqrt{N/2}/(2\Delta)$ — sub-ULP for $\Delta \ge 2^{40}$.
2. **Rescaling rounding:** $\approx 2^{-\log\Delta}$ relative per multiplication [1].
3. **Key-switching noise:** controlled by hybrid decomposition; reduced measurably by the variant of [4].
4. **Bootstrapping approximation error:** $\eta \approx 2^{-15}$–$2^{-25}$ [3][5] — the *dominant* term.
5. **Rotation noise:** one key switch each; amortized by hoisting.

> **Lemma (Depth-optimal parameter choice).** Rescaling error compounds as $(1 + 2^{-\log\Delta})^D$, negligible for $D \ll \Delta$; each bootstrap injects the irreducible error $\eta$. Hence bootstrapping, not rescaling, is the binding precision constraint. ∎

### 5.2 Proof Sketch: Why Approximate Decryption Is Sound

*How can a scheme whose decryption is "wrong" be secure?* Correctness is defined *up to* approximation error, and security reduces to RLWE as in BGV: the message hides in high-order bits that even the legitimate decryptor recovers only approximately. The caveat is **IND-CPA$^D$** — a decryption oracle leaks key-dependent noise, so the decryptor must be trusted; noise flooding is the standard mitigation [1][4].

## 6. Limitations

Intellectual honesty requires stating plainly what CKKS cannot do.

**The precision ceiling.** Roughly 15–25 bits post-bootstrap [3][5] rules out double-precision iterative solvers and any signal living in low-order bits.

**Non-polynomial operations.** Comparison, branching, and division need careful polynomial approximation; composing several (e.g., softmax) is expert work, though functional bootstrapping [7] narrows the gap.

**Performance and memory.** Bootstrapping takes seconds per full-slot ciphertext [8]; ResNet-110 takes minutes per image [6]; functional bootstrapping needs ~201 GB RAM [7]. Ciphertext expansion is $\approx 10^4$–$10^5\times$ — CKKS wins on throughput, not latency.

**Security model caveats.** Bootstrapping keys reach gigabytes at high $N$; circular security is assumed but unproven; side-channel resistance of NTT code is nascent.

**Parameter fragility.** Choosing $(N, L, \Delta)$ has sharp cliffs: one level too few and decryption fails *silently*. Auto-tuning tools help but do not replace the ledger of Section 5.1.

---

## 7. Conclusion

CKKS succeeded by aligning the primitive with the numerical reality of its workloads: the RNS variant made it fast [2][4], bootstrapping without decryption made it unbounded in depth [3][5][8], and the encrypted-ML stack made it useful [6][7]. The methodological lesson: **approximation error is a design parameter**, as analyzable as floating-point roundoff. The open problems — the ~20-bit bootstrapping ceiling, non-polynomial operations, the IND-CPA$^D$ model — define the next decade of homomorphic floating point.
