# Differentially Private Machine Learning: DP-SGD, the Moments Accountant, and Privacy-Utility Trade-offs

## Abstract

Differentially private stochastic gradient descent (DP-SGD) is the dominant algorithmic paradigm for training deep neural networks with formal privacy guarantees. Introduced by Abadi et al. [2], DP-SGD augments classical mini-batch optimization with *per-example gradient clipping* and *calibrated Gaussian noise*, bounding the sensitivity of each update to any single training record. Its practical viability rests on a second innovation of the same paper: the *moments accountant*, a composition technique that tracks the moment-generating function of the per-step privacy loss random variable and converts the resulting bound to standard $(\epsilon, \delta)$-differential privacy, yielding orders-of-magnitude tighter cumulative guarantees than naive composition [1]. This article reconstructs the theory end to end: the differential-privacy foundations of Dwork et al. [1], Rényi differential privacy and subsampled Rényi accounting [3][4], the moments-accountant composition analysis, the PATE alternative based on teacher ensembles [5][6], and the privacy-utility frontier as revealed by empirical evaluation and membership-inference auditing [7][8].

## 1 Intro

Modern machine learning systems memorize. Large neural networks trained on sensitive corpora — clinical records, keystrokes, photographs — have been shown to regurgitate exact training examples under adversarial prompting, and even ordinary classification models leak membership information about their training sets through their prediction vectors [7]. The classical toolbox of privacy engineering — data anonymization, $k$-anonymity, access control — offers no composable, quantitative bound on this leakage. Differential privacy (DP), introduced by Dwork, McSherry, Nissim, and Smith [1], supplies exactly such a bound: a randomized mechanism $\mathcal{M}$ is $(\epsilon, \delta)$-differentially private if, for every pair of *neighboring* datasets $D \sim D'$ differing in a single record and every measurable output event $S$,

$$\Pr[\mathcal{M}(D) \in S] \le e^{\epsilon}\Pr[\mathcal{M}(D') \in S] + \delta.$$

The parameter $\epsilon$ caps the worst-case multiplicative change in output likelihood induced by one individual's data; $\delta$ permits the guarantee to fail with small probability, which is essential for Gaussian-noise mechanisms.

For more than a decade after 2006, applying this definition to *deep learning* looked infeasible. Gradient computations couple every parameter to every example, sensitivities are data-dependent and unbounded, and the thousands of sequential adaptive queries issued during training would, under the basic composition theorem, accumulate privacy cost linearly — exhausting any plausible budget before the first epoch finished. Early attempts consequently produced privacy parameters so large ($\epsilon \gg 100$) that the guarantees were vacuous, and some were later shown ineffective against concrete attacks [7].

The landscape changed with Abadi et al. [2], who made two coupled contributions. First, **DP-SGD**: clip each per-example gradient to a fixed $\ell_2$ norm $C$, so that a single record can shift the summed gradient by at most $C$, then add Gaussian noise $\mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2 C^2 \mathbf{I})$ to the aggregated update. Clipping converts an unbounded sensitivity into a bounded one; noise calibrates indistinguishability to that bound. Second, the **moments accountant**: instead of composing $(\epsilon_i, \delta_i)$-DP guarantees step by step, track $\alpha(\lambda) = \log \mathbb{E}[\exp(\lambda L)]$ — the log moment-generating function of the *privacy loss random variable* $L = \log \frac{\Pr[\mathcal{M}(D)=o]}{\Pr[\mathcal{M}(D')=o]}$ — across all $T$ steps, and convert the accumulated log-MGF to a final $(\epsilon, \delta)$ pair via a Chernoff tail bound. The moments accountant exploits the *privacy amplification of Poisson subsampling*: because each step sees only a random $q$-fraction of the data, most steps contribute essentially nothing to the privacy loss of any fixed record. The result was deep networks trained to competitive accuracy with single-digit $\epsilon$ — the first credible demonstration that DP and deep learning are compatible [2].

This article examines the full stack: foundations (§2), the DP-SGD algorithm and its accounting machinery (§3), a deep dive into the moments accountant, Rényi-DP accounting, the PATE alternative, hyperparameter interactions, and auditing (§4), empirical results and proof sketches (§5), limitations (§6), and a concluding assessment (§7).

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The differential privacy contract

The neighboring relation is the atomic unit of the definition. Two conventions dominate: *add/remove* ( $D' = D \setminus \{x\}$ ) and *replace* ( $D, D'$ differ in the value of one record). DP-SGD analyses typically adopt the add/remove convention, under which "the privacy of one individual" means "the privacy of one training example" [2]. Two properties make DP uniquely suited to machine learning:

- **Post-processing immunity.** If $\mathcal{M}$ is $(\epsilon,\delta)$-DP, then $f \circ \mathcal{M}$ is $(\epsilon,\delta)$-DP for any (possibly randomized) $f$. Training downstream artifacts — checkpoints, predictions, distilled models — on the DP-SGD output therefore costs nothing further [1].
- **Composition.** Releasing the outputs of $k$ mechanisms with parameters $(\epsilon_i, \delta_i)$ yields $(\sum_i \epsilon_i, \sum_i \delta_i)$-DP under basic composition, and substantially less under advanced composition or the accountant techniques of §3–§4 [1].

### 2.2 Sensitivity and the Gaussian mechanism

For a vector-valued query $f$, the $\ell_2$-*sensitivity* is $\Delta_2 f = \max_{D \sim D'} \|f(D) - f(D')\|_2$. The Gaussian mechanism releases $f(D) + \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2 \mathbf{I})$; for $\epsilon \in (0,1)$ it satisfies $(\epsilon,\delta)$-DP when $\sigma \ge \sqrt{2 \ln(1.25/\delta)} \, \Delta_2 f / \epsilon$ [1]. The crucial observation for machine learning is that *clipping creates sensitivity where none existed*: an unclipped gradient sum has unbounded $\Delta_2$, but after projecting each per-example gradient $\mathbf{g}_i$ to the $\ell_2$-ball of radius $C$,

$$\bar{\mathbf{g}}_i = \mathbf{g}_i / \max\!\left(1, \tfrac{\|\mathbf{g}_i\|_2}{C}\right),$$

### 2.3 Composition: from linear to sublinear

A training run issues $T$ adaptive mechanisms, one per gradient step. Basic composition charges $\epsilon_{\text{total}} = T \, \epsilon_{\text{step}}$, which is catastrophic for $T \sim 10^4$–$10^6$. The *advanced composition* theorem improves this to roughly $O(\sqrt{T \log(1/\delta')} \, \epsilon)$ for $(\epsilon,\delta)$-DP steps, but still treats each step as a generic $(\epsilon,\delta)$-DP black box and ignores the structure that makes DP-SGD cheap: Poisson subsampling and Gaussian noise. The moments accountant [2], and later the Rényi-DP framework [3] with subsampled RDP bounds [4], exploit precisely this structure, and are the reason DP-SGD's reported $\epsilon$ values are *tens* rather than *thousands*.

| Composition method | Total $\epsilon$ scaling (T steps) | Exploits subsampling | Standard in libraries |
|---|---|---|---|
| Basic composition | $O(T\epsilon_0)$ | No | No |
| Advanced composition | $O(\sqrt{T\log(1/\delta)}\,\epsilon_0)$ | No | Rarely |
| Moments accountant [2] | $O(q\sqrt{T}/\sigma)$ (Gaussian, subsampled) | Yes | TensorFlow Privacy (legacy) |
| RDP / analytical moments accountant [3][4] | Same order, tighter constants | Yes | Opacus, TF-Privacy, JAX |

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 The DP-SGD algorithm

One training step of DP-SGD [2] proceeds as follows. Given parameters $\theta_t$, a dataset of size $n$, a sampling probability $q = L/n$ (lot size $L$), a clipping norm $C$, and a noise multiplier $\sigma$:

1. Sample a *lot* $L_t$ by including each example independently with probability $q$ (Poisson subsampling).
2. For each $x_i \in L_t$, compute the per-example gradient $\mathbf{g}_t(x_i) = \nabla_{\theta_t} \mathcal{L}(\theta_t, x_i)$.
3. Clip: $\bar{\mathbf{g}}_t(x_i) = \mathbf{g}_t(x_i) / \max(1, \|\mathbf{g}_t(x_i)\|_2 / C)$.
4. Add noise: $\tilde{\mathbf{g}}_t = \frac{1}{L}\left(\sum_{x_i \in L_t} \bar{\mathbf{g}}_t(x_i) + \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2 C^2 \mathbf{I})\right)$.
5. Descend: $\theta_{t+1} = \theta_t - \eta_t \tilde{\mathbf{g}}_t$.

```python
# Core DP-SGD step (Opacus-style, simplified)
def dp_sgd_step(params, batch, C, sigma, lr):
    per_sample_grads = [torch.autograd.grad(loss(params, x), params)
                        for x in batch]          # 1. per-example gradients
    clipped = [g * min(1.0, C / g.norm(2))       # 2. clip to norm C
               for g in per_sample_grads]
    agg = sum(clipped) / len(batch)
    noise = torch.normal(0, sigma * C, agg.shape) # 3. Gaussian noise
    return params - lr * (agg + noise / len(batch))
```

The implementation above omits the Poisson subsampling wrapper and the accountant bookkeeping, but exhibits the three operations that distinguish DP-SGD from ordinary SGD [2].

### 3.2 The moments accountant

Define, for neighboring $D \sim D'$ and mechanism $\mathcal{M}$, the privacy loss random variable $L(o) = \log \frac{\Pr[\mathcal{M}(D) = o]}{\Pr[\mathcal{M}(D') = o]}$, where $o \sim \mathcal{M}(D)$. Its log moment-generating function at order $\lambda$ is $\alpha_{\mathcal{M}}(\lambda) = \log \mathbb{E}_{o \sim \mathcal{M}(D)}[e^{\lambda L(o)}]$. Two facts make $\alpha$ composable where $(\epsilon,\delta)$ is not:

- **Additivity.** For an adaptive sequence $\mathcal{M}_{1:T}$, $\alpha_{1:T}(\lambda) \le \sum_{t=1}^T \alpha_{\mathcal{M}_t}(\lambda)$, because the expectation factorizes across the conditionally independent steps [2, Theorem 2].
- **Tail conversion.** By Markov's inequality on $e^{\lambda L}$, $(\epsilon,\delta)$-DP holds with $\delta = \min_\lambda \exp(\alpha_{1:T}(\lambda) - \lambda \epsilon)$, and conversely $\epsilon = \min_\lambda \frac{\alpha_{1:T}(\lambda) + \log(1/\delta)}{\lambda}$ for a target $\delta$ [2, Theorem 1].

> **Theorem:** (Moments accountant, Abadi et al. [2].) Let $\mathcal{M}$ be the subsampled Gaussian mechanism with sampling probability $q$ and noise multiplier $\sigma$. Then $\alpha_{\mathcal{M}}(\lambda) \le \frac{q^2 \lambda(\lambda+1)}{(1-q)\sigma^2} + O(q^3 \lambda^3 / \sigma^3)$. Composing $T$ steps, the mechanism is $(\epsilon, \delta)$-DP for $\delta > 0$ and $\epsilon = O\!\left(q\sqrt{T \log(1/\delta)} / \sigma\right)$.

### 3.3 Rényi differential privacy accounting

Mironov [3] reframed the moments accountant's analytic core as a privacy definition in its own right. A mechanism is $(\alpha, \epsilon)$-*Rényi* DP if $D_\alpha(\mathcal{M}(D) \| \mathcal{M}(D')) \le \epsilon$ for all neighbors, where $D_\alpha$ is the Rényi divergence of order $\alpha > 1$. RDP composes by *addition* ($\epsilon$ values simply sum), is immune to post-processing, and converts to $(\epsilon,\delta)$-DP via $\epsilon_{\text{DP}} = \epsilon_{\text{RDP}} + \frac{\log(1/\delta)}{\alpha - 1}$, optimized over $\alpha$ [3]. The connection to the moments accountant is exact: $\alpha_{\mathcal{M}}(\lambda) = (\lambda+1) \, D_{\lambda+1}$, i.e., the log-MGF at order $\lambda$ is the Rényi divergence at order $\lambda+1$ scaled appropriately [4]. Wang, Balle, and Kasiviswanathan [4] extended this to *subsampled* RDP mechanisms and derived the *analytical moments accountant* — a closed-form RDP bound for the subsampled Gaussian mechanism that avoids numerical integration of the MGF and is now the standard accountant in Opacus, TensorFlow Privacy, and JAX-based DP libraries. A Haskell-flavored statement of the composition law:

```haskell
-- RDP composition: epsilons add, orders must match
composeRDP :: [(Order, Epsilon)] -> (Order, Epsilon)
composeRDP mechs
  | allEqual (map fst mechs) = (head (map fst mechs), sum (map snd mechs))
  | otherwise                = error "RDP orders must coincide for addition"
```

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Why the moments accountant beats black-box composition

Advanced composition treats each step as an arbitrary $(\epsilon_0, \delta_0)$-DP mechanism and applies a generic bound, necessarily paying for the worst case over all such mechanisms. The moments accountant instead *opens the box*: it computes the actual log-MGF of the subsampled Gaussian step, which for small $q$ is dominated by the $(1-q)$ mass where the privacy loss is exactly zero. Geometrically, the privacy loss distribution is a spike at $L=0$ with a thin Gaussian-shifted tail; the MGF bound tracks the tail's moments rather than a uniform $\epsilon_0$ envelope. The numerical gap is dramatic: for typical DP-SGD hyperparameters ($q = 0.01$, $\sigma = 4$, $T = 10^4$), naive composition reports $\epsilon$ in the thousands, advanced composition in the hundreds, while the moments accountant reports single digits [2].

### 4.2 Subsampling: Poisson lots versus fixed batches

The amplification theorem requires each step's data selection to be a *random* subset with inclusion probability $q$ per record, independent across steps. Poisson subsampling satisfies this; a fixed-size random batch (sampling without replacement) does not *exactly*, though RDP analyses extend with small corrections [4]. In practice, libraries approximate Poisson sampling by shuffling and slicing, which is a fixed-size batch — a known, usually unquantified, gap between theory and implementation. Formal treatments of DP-SGD with *shuffling* rather than Poisson sampling obtain slightly weaker bounds; practitioners accept the mismatch because the empirical difference in reported $\epsilon$ is modest, but it remains one of the field's open formal loose ends.

### 4.3 PATE: sidestepping noisy gradients entirely

Private Aggregation of Teacher Ensembles (PATE) [5] takes an orthogonal route. Partition the private data into $k$ disjoint shards; train one *teacher* model per shard with no privacy constraints; then train a *student* model on public unlabeled data whose labels come from a *noisy plurality vote* of the teachers. Only the aggregation step touches private information (through the teachers' votes), so only it consumes privacy budget — the teachers themselves are never released. Papernot et al. [6] refined this with data-dependent analysis: when teachers agree strongly, the noisy argmax is overwhelmingly likely to return the consensus label, so the *realized* privacy cost is far below the worst-case bound; smooth-sensitivity techniques sanitize the data-dependent accounting itself. On MNIST, PATE reaches approximately 98% accuracy at $\epsilon \approx 2$, and the scalable variant [6] pushes below $\epsilon = 1$ on larger tasks — a privacy-utility point DP-SGD struggles to reach, at the cost of requiring public unlabeled data and $k$ redundant training runs.

### 4.4 The hyperparameter triangle: clipping norm, noise multiplier, batch size

DP-SGD's utility is governed by three interacting knobs whose effects are easily misread:

- **Clipping norm $C$.** Too small, and gradients are systematically *biased* downward, distorting the optimization trajectory; too large, and the noise scale $\sigma C$ drowns the signal. The optimal $C$ is typically far below the median gradient norm — a counterintuitive finding, since it means most updates are *dominated by clipping bias* rather than noise [2].
- **Noise multiplier $\sigma$.** Directly trades $\epsilon$ against utility; the accountant's $1/\sigma$ scaling makes this the cleanest knob, but $\sigma$ interacts with the learning rate because noise inflates gradient variance.
- **Lot size $L$ (sampling ratio $q = L/n$).** Larger lots reduce the noise-to-signal ratio (noise is added once per lot, then divided by $L$) at the cost of a larger $q$, which weakens subsampling amplification. The net effect usually favors *large* batches in DP-SGD — the opposite of the small-batch generalization wisdom of non-private deep learning.

### 4.5 Auditing: membership inference as the empirical dual

Theory gives *upper* bounds on privacy loss; *auditing* seeks *lower* bounds by mounting actual attacks. Shokri et al. [7] introduced membership inference against ML models: train *shadow models* mimicking the target, then train an attack classifier to distinguish the target's behavior on members versus non-members. On models trained without DP, such attacks achieve high precision, and Yeom et al. [8] formalized the link to overfitting — the generalization gap bounds the adversary's advantage. For DP-SGD, the $(\epsilon,\delta)$ guarantee implies an upper bound on *any* membership-inference advantage (roughly $e^\epsilon - 1$), so auditing a DP-SGD run should find attacks *no stronger* than the theory permits. When audits find attacks *stronger* than the claimed $\epsilon$ allows, the implementation — not the theorem — is at fault; this methodology has caught real bugs in DP libraries (incorrect noise scaling, forgotten subsampling). Tight, single-run auditing without shadow models remains an active research frontier, because shadow-model auditing multiplies training cost by an order of magnitude.

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 Proof sketch: per-step RDP of the subsampled Gaussian

We sketch the bound underlying Theorem [2] in RDP language [4]. Consider add/remove adjacency and a single record $x$. With probability $1-q$, $x \notin L_t$ and the mechanism's output distribution is identical on $D, D'$ (privacy loss $0$). With probability $q$, $x$ is included; conditioned on inclusion, the clipped sum differs by at most $C$ in $\ell_2$ norm between $D$ and $D'$, so the two output distributions are $\mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2C^2\mathbf{I})$ and $\mathcal{N}(\mu', \sigma^2C^2\mathbf{I})$ with $\|\mu - \mu'\|_2 \le C$. The Rényi divergence of order $\alpha$ between these Gaussians is $\frac{\alpha \|\mu-\mu'\|_2^2}{2\sigma^2C^2} \le \frac{\alpha}{2\sigma^2}$. Mixing over the inclusion event and applying the quasi-convexity of Rényi divergence yields $D_\alpha \le \frac{1}{\alpha-1}\log(1 + q^2 \binom{\alpha}{2} \min(4(e^{1/\sigma^2}-1), 2e^{1/\sigma^2}) + O(q^3))$ [4]. For $\sigma \ge 1$ and small $q$ this is $\approx \frac{q^2 \alpha}{\sigma^2}$: the advertised $q^2$ amplification. Summing over $T$ steps (RDP composes additively [3]) and converting via $\epsilon = \min_\alpha (T \cdot \epsilon_{\text{RDP}}(\alpha) + \log(1/\delta)/(\alpha-1))$ completes the argument.

### 5.3 The $\epsilon$ interpretation problem

A persistent difficulty is that $\epsilon$ is a *worst-case* bound over all neighboring dataset pairs and all adversaries with arbitrary side information — a far stronger adversary than any membership-inference audit instantiates. Consequently, models with $\epsilon = 8$ routinely resist all known attacks far better than the bound suggests, while $\epsilon = 1$ remains the informal "strong privacy" bar in the literature [6]. The hypothesis-testing view of DP makes this precise: $(\epsilon,\delta)$-DP bounds the tradeoff between an adversary's true-positive and false-positive rates, and auditing measures the *achieved* point on that tradeoff curve [7][8]. The gap between the theoretical curve and the empirical point is simultaneously DP-SGD's best defense (attacks underperform the bound) and its sharpest criticism (the bound may be too conservative to guide deployment decisions).

## 6 Limitations

DP-SGD's limitations are substantive and structure the current research agenda:

1. **Utility gap on complex tasks.** The clipping bias plus noise variance compound in high-dimensional, long-tailed optimization landscapes. Closing the gap on ImageNet-scale or LLM-scale tasks without inflating $\epsilon$ remains unsolved; reported successes lean heavily on public pre-training followed by private fine-tuning, which shifts rather than eliminates the data-dependence question.
2. **Hyperparameter sensitivity and tuning cost.** The clipping norm, noise multiplier, lot size, and learning rate interact non-trivially (§4.4), and *tuning itself consumes privacy budget* if done on the private data — a cost rarely included in reported $\epsilon$ values. Private hyperparameter selection (e.g., via private selection mechanisms) is theoretically understood but seldom practiced.
3. **The shuffling gap.** Production implementations use shuffled fixed-size batches while the accountant assumes Poisson subsampling (§4.2). The quantitative impact is believed small but is not rigorously bounded in most deployed systems.
4. **Record-level versus user-level privacy.** DP-SGD protects *examples*, not *users*. A user contributing many correlated records (photos, messages) enjoys a guarantee weakened by group privacy: $k$ records cost roughly $k\epsilon$. User-level DP-SGD exists but is far more expensive.
5. **Interpretability of $\epsilon$.** Decision-makers routinely misread $\epsilon = 8$ as "eight times worse than $\epsilon = 1$" or as a breach probability. The hypothesis-testing semantics are precise but unintuitive, and the conservatism of the worst-case bound makes $\epsilon$ a poor comparator across mechanisms with different $\delta$ or accounting methods [3].
6. **Computational overhead.** Per-example gradients defeat the batch-level parallelism of backpropagation; efficient implementations (Opacus's vectorized per-sample grads, JAX's `vmap`) narrow but do not eliminate the gap, and memory consumption grows with lot size — precisely the regime §4.4 recommends.

## 7 Conclusion

DP-SGD, together with the moments accountant and its Rényi-DP successors, converted differential privacy for deep learning from a theoretical curiosity into an engineering discipline [1][2][3][4]. The recipe — *clip* to bound sensitivity, *noise* to enforce indistinguishability, *subsample* to amplify, and *account* via the privacy-loss MGF rather than black-box composition — is now standard infrastructure, embedded in Opacus, TensorFlow Privacy, and JAX tooling. PATE [5][6] demonstrates that a fundamentally different architecture, noisy teacher aggregation, can reach stronger privacy at comparable utility when public data and ensemble redundancy are affordable. Membership-inference auditing [7][8] provides the empirical check that keeps the theory honest, and has already caught implementation bugs that theorems alone could not.

Yet the central tension is unresolved: the privacy-utility frontier for large-scale models still demands either large $\epsilon$, public pre-training, or both, and the semantic gap between a worst-case $\epsilon$ and the adversary one actually faces leaves practitioners navigating by folklore as much as by theorem. The next decade of the field will likely be defined less by new definitions than by *closing the constants* — tighter accountants, bias-aware clipping, private tuning, and audits that certify, rather than merely probe, the deployed guarantee.
