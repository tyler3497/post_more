# Predictive Coding and the Free Energy Principle: Variational Inference and Hierarchical Message Passing in the Brain

## Abstract

The free energy principle proposes that self-organising biological systems — the brain chief among them — resist the natural dispersion of their states by minimizing a variational upper bound on sensory surprise. This essay develops a rigorous treatment of how *predictive coding*, the hierarchical message passing of top-down predictions and bottom-up precision-weighted prediction errors, emerges as the natural algorithmic implementation of variational inference under Gaussian (Laplace) assumptions. We derive variational free energy as the difference between complexity and accuracy, show that gradient descent on this functional yields precisely the canonical predictive-coding update equations, and formalize precision as the gain control gating the influence of ascending error signals. We examine the equivalence between predictive coding and variational message passing on factor graphs, the generalization to active inference through Markov blankets, and the empirical record spanning extra-classical receptive fields, mismatch negativity, and computational demonstrations of backpropagation approximation. We close by appraising open problems: falsifiability, the plausibility of Gaussian assumptions, and the limits of the principle as a normative account of mind.

##1 Introduction

In 1867, Hermann von Helmholtz proposed that perception is *unconscious inference*: the brain, faced with an ill-posed inverse problem, must infer the hidden causes of its sensory impressions from ambiguous and noise-corrupted data [3]. This idea lay largely dormant as a mathematical program until the Bayesian brain hypothesis recast it in the language of probability: perception is the computation of a posterior distribution over world states given sensory evidence. Karl Friston's free energy principle (FEP) pushes the programme to its logical extreme, asserting that *all* self-organising systems that persist over time must, by mathematical necessity, minimize a quantity called variational free energy — an upper bound on the surprise, or negative log-evidence, of their sensory exchanges with the world [1][2].

A crucial distinction, emphasized by Friston himself, separates the principle from the *process theories* that realize it [5]:

> Theorem: (State vs. process). The free energy principle is a *normative principle* about what self-organising systems must do, akin to Hamilton's principle of stationary action. Predictive coding and the Bayesian brain hypothesis are *process theories* about how the brain does it. The former cannot be falsified by observation; the latter can and must be.

This essay takes that distinction seriously. Our central claim is constructive: **predictive coding — hierarchical message passing of predictions and precision-weighted prediction errors — is the canonical algorithmic realization of variational inference under the Laplace (Gaussian) assumption**, and this identification yields concrete, testable predictions about cortical microcircuits, neuromodulation, and behaviour.

##2 Background

### 2.1 Surprise, entropy, and the imperative to self-organise

Let $y$ denote sensory observations and $x$ the hidden states of the world that cause them. A biological agent is characterized by a *Markov blanket* — a statistical partition separating internal states $\mu$ from external states $\eta$ via sensory states $s$ and active states $a$. The agent's continued existence means its states occupy a characteristic, low-entropy distribution: it does not diffuse into thermodynamic equilibrium with its surroundings.

The *surprise* (or surprisal) of a sensory sample is $-\ln p(y)$: the negative log marginal likelihood, also called the *model evidence*. Because surprise is bounded below by the entropy of sensory states, minimizing the long-term average surprise is equivalent to resisting entropy — the defining imperative of life. Yet surprise cannot be evaluated directly: computing $p(y) = \int p(y, x)\,dx$ requires integrating over all possible hidden causes, an intractable marginalization [4].

### 2.2 Variational free energy

The brain's solution, on this account, is to introduce an approximate posterior $q(x)$ — the brain's beliefs about hidden causes — and to minimize instead the **variational free energy**:

$$F = \mathbb{E}_q[\ln q(x) - \ln p(y, x)]$$

This quantity admits two illuminating decompositions. First:

$$F = \underbrace{D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\left[q(x) \,||\, p(x \mid y)\right]}_{\text{divergence}} \;-\; \underbrace{\ln p(y)}_{\text{log evidence}}$$

so that $F$ is an *upper bound* on surprise, tight exactly when $q(x)$ equals the true posterior. Minimizing $F$ therefore minimizes surprise approximately. Second:

$$F = \underbrace{D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\left[q(x) \,||\, p(x)\right]}_{\text{complexity}} \;-\; \underbrace{\mathbb{E}_q[\ln p(y \mid x)]}_{\text{accuracy}}$$

revealing free energy as a trade-off: beliefs should be as *accurate* as possible while departing as little as possible from prior expectations — an information-theoretic formalization of Occam's razor [2][8].

### 2.3 The Laplace assumption and Gaussian beliefs

Exact variational inference remains intractable in general. The crucial simplifying move — the *Laplace assumption* — is to restrict $q(x)$ to a Gaussian whose sufficient statistics are its mean $\mu$ and covariance $\Sigma$. Under this assumption only the means need be optimized, since the covariance follows analytically as the inverse curvature of $F$ at the mode. This transforms inference into a tractable optimization problem: gradient descent on $F$ with respect to the conditional expectations $\mu$ [1][7].

##3 Methodology

### 3.1 The hierarchical generative model

We consider a hierarchical Gaussian generative model in which each level $i$ generates the activity at the level below through a (possibly nonlinear) function $g^{(i)}$:

$$v^{(i)} = g^{(i)}\!\left(v^{(i+1)}\right) + z^{(i)}, \qquad z^{(i)} \sim \mathcal{N}\!\left(0, \Sigma^{(i)}\right)$$

Here $v^{(i)}$ denotes hidden causes at level $i$ (with $v^{(0)} = y$ the sensory data), and $z^{(i)}$ is Gaussian noise with covariance $\Sigma^{(i)}$. The brain's beliefs are the conditional expectations $\mu^{(i)}$. The *prediction error* at each level is the difference between the observed (or expected) activity and the top-down prediction:

$$\epsilon^{(i)} = \mu^{(i)} - g^{(i)}\!\left(\mu^{(i+1)}\right)$$

and the *precision-weighted* prediction error is $\xi^{(i)} = \Pi^{(i)} \epsilon^{(i)}$, where $\Pi^{(i)} = (\Sigma^{(i)})^{-1}$ is the precision, i.e., the inverse variance or *confidence* ascribed to that error signal [7].

### 3.2 From free energy gradients to message passing

Under the Laplace assumption, variational free energy reduces (up to constants) to a sum of squared precision-weighted prediction errors plus complexity terms. Gradient descent on $F$ with respect to the expectations then yields the canonical update equations [1][4]:

$$\dot{\mu}^{(i)} = D\mu^{(i)} + \kappa \left( \frac{\partial g^{(i-1)}}{\partial \mu^{(i)}}^{\!\top} \xi^{(i-1)} \;-\; \xi^{(i)} \right)$$

where $D$ is a drift operator for generalized coordinates of motion and $\kappa$ is a learning rate. The interpretation is immediate and profound: each level's beliefs are driven *upward* by precision-weighted prediction errors from the level below and *pulled* by errors against its own top-down predictions. This is predictive coding, derived — not postulated — from variational principles.

In discrete time, a minimal implementation is strikingly compact:

```python
import numpy as np

def predictive_coding_step(mu, g, dg, y, Pi, kappa=0.1):
    """One variational update of beliefs mu given sensory data y.
    mu    : list of conditional expectations, mu[0] is clamped to y
    g     : list of top-down prediction functions g[i](mu[i+1])
    dg    : list of Jacobians of g
    Pi    : list of precision (inverse covariance) matrices
    """
    L = len(mu)
    eps = [mu[i] - g[i](mu[i + 1]) for i in range(L - 1)]
    xi  = [Pi[i] @ eps[i] for i in range(L - 1)]          # precision weighting
    for i in range(1, L):
        dmu = dg[i - 1](mu[i]).T @ xi[i - 1] - xi[i] if i < L - 1 else dg[i-1](mu[i]).T @ xi[i-1]
        mu[i] = mu[i] + kappa * dmu                        # gradient descent on F
    return mu, eps
```

The same equations emerge, in functional form, as **variational message passing** (VMP) on the factor graph of the generative model: each factor transmits messages that are the sufficient statistics of local beliefs, and predictive coding's $\mu$ and $\xi$ are exactly those messages [8]. In Haskell-flavoured pseudocode, the type signature of the scheme is:

```haskell
-- A factor node maps incoming belief messages to outgoing messages
type Belief   = (Mean, Precision)          -- sufficient statistics under Laplace
type Message  = (PredictionError, Weight)  -- (epsilon, Pi)

vmpStep :: Factor -> [Belief] -> ([Message], Belief)
vmpStep factor beliefs =
  let preds = topDown factor beliefs        -- g(mu^{i+1})
      errs  = zipWith (-) (map fst beliefs) preds
      wtd   = zipWith weight (map snd beliefs) errs  -- Pi * epsilon
  in (wtd, updateBeliefs factor wtd)
```

### 3.3 Precision as attentional gain

Precision $\Pi$ plays a dual role. Statistically, it encodes the estimated reliability of each prediction error. Mechanistically, it implements *gain control*: multiplying error-unit activity before it ascends the hierarchy. This identifies **attention with precision optimization** — attending to a stimulus is increasing the precision of the prediction errors it generates, amplifying their influence on belief updating [1]. Neuromodulators such as dopamine and acetylcholine are the leading biological candidates for broadcasting precision signals, a hypothesis with substantial (if still contested) empirical support.

##4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Predictive coding is variational message passing

The identification of predictive coding with VMP is more than an analogy; it is a structural equivalence [8]. Consider the factor graph of the hierarchical generative model. Under the mean-field and Laplace approximations, the variational free energy factorizes across nodes, and the fixed-point equations for each node's beliefs depend only on messages from its Markov blanket in the graph. The resulting updates are:

1. **Top-down messages**: predictions $g^{(i)}(\mu^{(i+1)})$ flowing from deep to superficial layers — anatomically, backward connections.
2. **Bottom-up messages**: precision-weighted errors $\xi^{(i)}$ flowing from superficial to deep layers — anatomically, forward connections.

This reproduces the canonical cortical microcircuit: superficial pyramidal cells encoding error, deep pyramidal cells encoding expectations, with distinct laminar targets for forward and backward connections [7]. The graph-theoretic view also clarifies *convergence*: because each update is a descent step on a bounded-below functional, the dynamics admit a Lyapunov function, and fixed points are local minima of $F$ under standard contraction conditions.

### 4.2 The anatomy of precision

Precision must itself be inferred — the brain must estimate the reliability of its own error signals. This *second-order* inference is formalized by placing priors on precision (typically Gamma distributions, conjugate to the Gaussian), whose expectations $\mu_\gamma$ scale the error units [7]. The functional consequences are far-reaching:

- **Attention**: endogenous precision increases for task-relevant sensory channels.
- **Sensory attenuation**: precision is *decreased* for the sensory consequences of self-generated movement, explaining why we cannot tickle ourselves — a failure of attenuation produces the misattribution characteristic of certain psychoses [2].
- **Neuromodulation**: distinct transmitter systems may encode precision in different domains — dopamine for the precision of policies and reward prediction errors, acetylcholine for sensory precision, noradrenaline for volatility.

| Phenomenon | Precision mechanism | Empirical signature |
|---|---|---|
| Selective attention | Upregulated sensory precision | Enhanced gain of early evoked responses |
| Sensory attenuation | Downregulated precision during action | Reduced somatosensory evoked potentials |
| Mismatch negativity | Precision-weighted prediction error | Larger MMN for precise, surprising deviants |
| Psychosis (hypothesis) | Aberrant precision assignment | False inference, hallucinations |

### 4.3 Active inference and the Markov blanket

Perception minimizes free energy by changing *beliefs* to fit sensations. **Active inference** adds the complementary strategy: change *sensations* to fit beliefs, by acting on the world [2]. Formally, policies $\pi$ (sequences of actions) are selected by minimizing *expected* free energy:

$$G(\pi) = \underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{q}[\,D_{\mathrm{KL}}[q(x\mid\pi)\,\|\,p(x)]\,]}_{\text{epistemic value (information gain)}} \;-\; \underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{q}[\ln p(y\mid C)]}_{\text{pragmatic value (preferred outcomes)}}$$

where $C$ encodes prior preferences over outcomes. Expected free energy therefore decomposes into an *epistemic* drive (resolve uncertainty — curiosity, exploration) and a *pragmatic* drive (realize preferred states — exploitation), dissolving the exploration–exploitation dilemma into a single objective [2]. The Markov blanket formalism guarantees that internal states can only ever influence external states *through* active states, and be influenced only *through* sensory states — action and perception are the two directions of blanket-mediated exchange.

### 4.4 Predictive coding approximates backpropagation

A striking recent result closes the loop between neuroscience and machine learning: predictive coding networks, run to their inference equilibrium, compute weight updates that *approximate backpropagation* along arbitrary computation graphs [6]. Whittington and Bogacz showed that a predictive coding network with local Hebbian plasticity reproduces the error-backpropagation algorithm [6], and subsequent work demonstrated that the approximation becomes exact in the infinitesimal-inference limit, unifying predictive coding with equilibrium propagation and contrastive Hebbian learning. This reframes a long-standing objection — that the brain cannot implement backprop because it requires non-local weight transport — into an empirical question about whether cortical dynamics approximate these equilibrium conditions.

### 4.5 Generalised coordinates and dynamical generative models

Static hierarchies suffice for images but not for a world in motion. The framework therefore embeds beliefs in **generalised coordinates of motion** $\tilde{\mu} = (\mu, \mu', \mu'', \dots)$ — position, velocity, acceleration, and higher temporal derivatives — allowing the generative model to predict *trajectories* rather than snapshots [7]. Free energy then includes prediction errors on the motion of hidden states, and the resulting dynamics implement a form of Bayesian filtering (a nonlinear generalization of the Kalman–Bucy filter). This extension is essential for modelling smooth pursuit eye movements, motor control, and speech, where the timing of sensory flow carries the information.

##5 Empirical Evidence and Formal Results

### 5.1 What counts as evidence

Because the FEP itself is a mathematical principle, it is not directly falsifiable — attempting to falsify it empirically is, in Friston's phrase, a category mistake akin to falsifying calculus by observation [5]. What *is* falsifiable is the process theory: the claim that cortex implements hierarchical predictive coding with precision-weighted message passing. The evidence is evaluated at three levels:

1. **Computational**: Do predictive coding networks reproduce known cortical response properties?
2. **Electrophysiological**: Do neural responses exhibit the predicted laminar and temporal signatures?
3. **Behavioural/psychophysical**: Do perceptual phenomena follow from precision-weighted inference?

### 5.2 Computational demonstrations

Rao and Ballard's foundational 1999 model showed that a two-level predictive coding network trained on natural images develops oriented, localized receptive fields resembling V1 simple cells, and — critically — reproduces *extra-classical* effects such as end-stopping and surround suppression as consequences of top-down prediction, not lateral wiring [3]. Later work extended this to motion, stereo, and object recognition. More recently, predictive coding networks have been trained on MNIST and ImageNet-scale tasks with performance competitive with backprop-trained networks, while using only local plasticity rules [6].

### 5.3 Neurophysiological signatures

Several canonical findings align with predictive-coding predictions:

- **Mismatch negativity (MMN)**: the enhanced EEG/MEG response to deviant stimuli in a repetitive sequence is naturally interpreted as a precision-weighted prediction error, scaling with both surprise and the precision of the sensory stream [2].
- **Repetition suppression**: reduced responses to repeated stimuli follow from successful prediction — errors shrink as the model learns.
- **Laminar specificity**: forward connections originate predominantly in superficial layers and target layer 4, while backward connections originate in deep layers and target superficial and deep layers outside layer 4 — matching the predicted segregation of error and expectation units [7].
- **Gamma and beta rhythms**: a prominent hypothesis assigns prediction errors to gamma-band activity and predictions to beta-band activity, with cross-frequency coupling mediating their interaction — an active area of empirical testing.

### 5.4 A formal result: the bound

> Theorem: (Variational bound). For any approximate posterior $q(x)$ and any generative model $p(y, x)$, the variational free energy $F[q] = \mathbb{E}_q[\ln q(x) - \ln p(y,x)]$ satisfies $F[q] \geq -\ln p(y)$, with equality if and only if $q(x) = p(x \mid y)$ almost everywhere.

*Proof sketch.* Subtracting the log-evidence from $F$ yields exactly the Kullback–Leibler divergence $D_{\mathrm{KL}}[q(x)\,\|\,p(x\mid y)]$, which is non-negative by Jensen's inequality and zero only at the true posterior. ∎

This elementary result is the load-bearing theorem of the entire framework: everything else — predictive coding updates, precision weighting, active inference — is a corollary of choosing particular approximating families and descent schemes for this single functional [4][8].

##6 Limitations

**Falsifiability and explanatory scope.** The principle's unfalsifiability is a double-edged sword: it guarantees the mathematics but risks vacuity. Critics argue that any behaviour can be redescribed post hoc as free-energy minimization, and that the framework's predictions often lack the specificity to discriminate it from simpler alternatives [5]. The appropriate response is to demand *process-level* predictions — laminar, pharmacological, computational — and to test those, not the principle.

**The Gaussian straitjacket.** Nearly all concrete derivations depend on the Laplace assumption. Real posteriors over natural sensory causes are multimodal and heavy-tailed; whether cortical inference approximates them well enough for the Gaussian theory to be explanatory, or whether particle-based or sampling-based schemes are needed, remains open [6].

**Precision is under-constrained.** Mapping precision onto specific neuromodulators is suggestive but far from settled. Competing accounts assign dopamine to reward prediction errors rather than precision, and the empirical pharmacology is complex and sometimes contradictory.

**The dark-room problem.** If agents minimize surprise, why not seek a dark, silent room and minimize sensory input altogether? The active-inference resolution — that agents minimize *expected* free energy including epistemic value, and that prior preferences $C$ encode the need to forage and explore — is formally adequate but has been criticized as moving the explanatory burden into the priors [2].

**Biological plausibility gaps.** Exact predictive coding requires symmetric forward/backward weights (the weight-transport problem) and precise one-to-one connectivity between expectation and error units. Recent work shows these constraints can be relaxed with learned backward weights and only modest performance cost [6], but the relaxed schemes still await direct neurobiological confirmation.

---

##7 Conclusion

The free energy principle offers something rare in the sciences of mind: a *first-principles* derivation of perception, learning, attention, and action from a single imperative — minimize surprise, approximately, by minimizing variational free energy. Its great technical achievement is showing that this imperative, under Gaussian assumptions, *is* predictive coding: hierarchical message passing of predictions and precision-weighted errors, implementable in known cortical microcircuits and equivalent to variational message passing on factor graphs [1][4][7][8].

Whether this elegant unification survives contact with the full complexity of neural data is the defining empirical question of the research programme. The theory's process-level commitments — laminar segregation of error and expectation units, precision as neuromodulatory gain, expected free energy as the objective of policy selection — are specific enough to be wrong, and that is precisely their virtue. If future experiments confirm them, the Bayesian brain will have graduated from metaphor to mechanism; if they fail, the failure will be informative, constraining whatever process theory replaces predictive coding. Either way, the variational formulation has permanently raised the standard of mathematical explicitness required of theories of cortical function.
