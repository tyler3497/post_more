# Receding-Horizon Feedback: Terminal-Cost Stability, Invariant Tubes, and the Multiparametric Explicit Solution of Constrained MPC

## Abstract

Model predictive control (MPC) closes the loop between open-loop optimal control and feedback by solving a finite-horizon constrained optimization at every sampling instant and applying only the first move. This thesis develops the four pillars that make the resulting receding-horizon law provably stable and practically deployable. First, we formalize the receding-horizon paradigm and the finite-horizon optimal control problem with hard state and input constraints. Second, we prove asymptotic stability through terminal ingredients: a control-invariant terminal set, a local terminal controller, and a terminal cost that acts as a control Lyapunov function, making the optimal value function a Lyapunov function for the closed loop [1]. Third, we extend the framework to bounded disturbances with tube-based robust MPC, where a disturbance-invariant tube around the nominal plan is maintained by constraint tightening [3][4]. Fourth, we derive explicit MPC, which precomputes the receding-horizon law offline as a piecewise-affine function over a polyhedral partition of the state space via multiparametric quadratic programming [2][5]. Proofs, a numerical double-integrator study, and limitations are included.

## 1 Intro

The central tension of optimal control is between *planning* and *feedback*. Planning solves an open-loop trajectory optimization over a horizon; feedback reacts to the current state. For unconstrained linear systems the two coincide in the linear-quadratic regulator, whose infinite-horizon feedback law is simultaneously the solution of every finite-horizon problem with the correct terminal weight. Once hard constraints on states and inputs enter — and every physical actuator and every safety envelope imposes them — this coincidence breaks. The infinite-horizon constrained problem is intractable in general, while a finite-horizon problem truncated naively can produce a closed loop that is feasible yet unstable, myopic about costs beyond its horizon [1].

**Model predictive control** refuses the choice: at each sampling instant, measure the state, solve a finite-horizon constrained optimal control problem initialized at that state, apply only the first control move, and replan at the next sample. The result is a *feedback law defined implicitly through optimization*. This essay develops the theory that makes this law stable, robust, and implementable:

- **Stability.** With three *terminal ingredients* — a terminal cost, a terminal set, and a local terminal controller — the optimal value function becomes a Lyapunov function, and the receding-horizon law is asymptotically stabilizing with a guaranteed region of attraction [1].
- **Robustness.** Under bounded disturbances, *tube-based* MPC plans a nominal trajectory and confines all possible perturbed realizations inside a disturbance-invariant tube, tightening constraints so the true trajectory satisfies the originals [3][4].
- **Explicitness.** For linear systems with quadratic costs and polyhedral constraints, the entire receding-horizon law can be computed *offline* as a piecewise-affine function of the state over a polyhedral partition, reducing online work to a point-location lookup [2][5].
- **Computation.** Condensed quadratic programs, warm-starting, and the real-time iteration make the online optimization fast enough for kilohertz loops [7][8].

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The unconstrained baseline: LQR and the Riccati equation

Consider the discrete-time linear system $x_{k+1} = A x_k + B u_k$ with stage cost $\ell(x,u) = x^T Q x + u^T R u$, $Q \succeq 0$, $R \succ 0$. If $P \succeq 0$ solves the discrete algebraic Riccati equation

then $u = -Kx$ with $K = (R + B^T P B)^{-1} B^T P A$ is optimal, and $V(x) = x^T P x$ is the value function. Bellman's principle gives the one-step identity

$$x^T P x = \min_u \, \big( x^T Q x + u^T R u + (Ax + Bu)^T P (Ax + Bu) \big),$$

so $P$ is a *control Lyapunov function*: from any state, one admissible move strictly decreases the cost-to-go [1].

### 2.2 What constraints break

Add polyhedral constraints $x_k \in \mathcal{X}$, $u_k \in \mathcal{U}$ with $0$ in their interiors. The constrained infinite-horizon problem is generally unsolvable in closed form. The finite-horizon problem

$$\min_{\mathbf{u}} \; \sum_{k=0}^{N-1} \ell(x_k, u_k) + V_f(x_N) \quad \text{s.t.} \quad x_{k+1} = Ax_k + Bu_k,\; x_k \in \mathcal{X},\; u_k \in \mathcal{U}$$

is a convex quadratic program when $V_f$ is quadratic — solvable — but its solution is an *open-loop* plan. Implementing the first move and replanning defines the MPC law $\kappa_N(x)$. The difficulty: a myopic finite horizon can steer the state to a point from which the next optimization is infeasible, or can oscillate forever without converging, because the truncated problem does not price the tail of the trajectory. Stability must be *engineered into* the finite-horizon problem through its terminal data [1].

### 2.3 Invariant sets

A set $\Omega$ is *control invariant* for the constrained system if from every $x \in \Omega$ there exists $u \in \mathcal{U}$ with $Ax + Bu \in \Omega$ and $x \in \mathcal{X}$. A set $\mathcal{Z}$ is *disturbance invariant* for $x_{k+1} = Ax_k + w_k$, $w_k \in \mathcal{W}$, if $A\mathcal{Z} \oplus \mathcal{W} \subseteq \mathcal{Z}$, where $\oplus$ denotes the Minkowski sum. The *maximal* control-invariant set inside $\mathcal{X}$ characterizes the largest region from which constraints can be satisfied forever; computing it exactly is hard, but tractable inner approximations suffice for MPC design [3].

## 3 Methodology

The MPC methodology has a fixed architecture. At state $x$, solve the *finite-horizon optimal control problem* $\mathbb{P}_N(x)$:

$$\begin{aligned}
V_N^*(x) = \min_{\mathbf{u}} \quad & \sum_{k=0}^{N-1} \ell(x_k, u_k) + V_f(x_N) \\
\text{s.t.} \quad & x_0 = x, \quad x_{k+1} = f(x_k, u_k), \\
& x_k \in \mathcal{X},\; u_k \in \mathcal{U}, \quad k = 0, \dots, N-1, \\
& x_N \in \mathcal{X}_f,
\end{aligned}$$

where $V_f$ is the terminal cost, $\mathcal{X}_f$ the terminal set, and $\ell$ the stage cost, positive definite in the usual sense. Apply $u = u_0^*(x)$ and repeat. The three terminal ingredients $(V_f, \mathcal{X}_f, \kappa_f)$ — with $\kappa_f$ a local terminal controller — are chosen to satisfy:

1. **Invariance:** $\mathcal{X}_f \subseteq \mathcal{X}$ is control invariant under $\kappa_f$, with $\kappa_f(x) \in \mathcal{U}$ on $\mathcal{X}_f$.
2. **Lyapunov descent:** $V_f(f(x, \kappa_f(x))) - V_f(x) \le -\ell(x, \kappa_f(x))$ for all $x \in \mathcal{X}_f$; i.e., $V_f$ is a local control Lyapunov function.
3. **Compatibility:** $\mathcal{X}_f$ contains the origin in its interior.

Under these conditions, the *tail* of the optimal plan — the optimal predicted sequence with its first element removed and one step of the terminal controller appended — is feasible for $\mathbb{P}_N$ at the next state, and its cost bounds the new optimum. The value function $V_N^*$ then descends by at least the stage cost each step, making it a Lyapunov function for the closed loop [1].

Robust variants replace the single trajectory by a *tube* around a nominal plan, tightening constraints offline so the disturbed truth satisfies the originals [3]; explicit variants exploit the parametric structure of $\mathbb{P}_N(x)$ to precompute the map $x \mapsto u_0^*(x)$ offline [2][5].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Stability through terminal ingredients

The stability proof is a Lyapunov argument whose Lyapunov function is produced by optimization itself. Let $\mathbf{u}^*(x) = (u_0^*, \dots, u_{N-1}^*)$ be optimal for $\mathbb{P}_N(x)$ with predicted states $(x_0^*, \dots, x_N^*)$, $x_0^* = x$. Apply $u_0^*$; the successor state is $x^+ = f(x, u_0^*)$. Consider the candidate sequence for $\mathbb{P}_N(x^+)$:

$$\tilde{\mathbf{u}} = (u_1^*, \dots, u_{N-1}^*, \kappa_f(x_N^*)).$$

Feasibility follows from the terminal conditions: the shifted states satisfy the dynamics and constraints by optimality of the original plan, and the appended terminal step stays inside $\mathcal{X}_f$ by invariance, landing back in $\mathcal{X}_f$. This is *recursive feasibility*: feasibility at time zero propagates forever [1].

For the cost, the candidate's objective $\tilde{V}$ relates to the optimum $V_N^*(x)$ by

$$\tilde{V} = V_N^*(x) - \ell(x, u_0^*) + \underbrace{\big[ \ell(x_N^*, \kappa_f(x_N^*)) + V_f(f(x_N^*, \kappa_f(x_N^*))) - V_f(x_N^*) \big]}_{\le 0 \text{ by the CLF property}} \le V_N^*(x) - \ell(x, u_0^*).$$

Since $V_N^*(x^+)$ is the minimum, $V_N^*(x^+) \le \tilde{V}$, yielding the descent inequality

$$V_N^*(x^+) - V_N^*(x) \le -\ell(x, u_0^*(x)),$$

so $V_N^*$ is a strict Lyapunov function and the origin is asymptotically stable with region of attraction equal to the feasible set of $\mathbb{P}_N$ [1]. Standard choices are the *quasi-infinite horizon* design ($V_f(x) = x^T P x$ from the unconstrained LQR, $\mathcal{X}_f$ a sublevel set inside the constraints) and horizon-length-based stability without terminal ingredients, which trades offline design for online computation [1].

### 4.2 Tube-based robust MPC

With an additive disturbance, $x_{k+1} = Ax_k + Bu_k + w_k$, $w_k \in \mathcal{W}$ compact, asymptotic stability of the origin is unachievable; the right target is robust stability of a *set*. Tube MPC splits the control into a nominal plan and an ancillary feedback [3][4]:

- **Nominal system:** $z_{k+1} = Az_k + Bv_k$, planned online subject to *tightened* constraints $z_k \in \mathcal{X} \ominus \mathcal{Z}$, $v_k \in \mathcal{U} \ominus K\mathcal{Z}$, where $\ominus$ is the Pontryagin difference.
- **Error system:** $e_k = x_k - z_k$ evolves as $e_{k+1} = (A + BK)e_k + w_k$. If $K$ stabilizes $A + BK$ and $\mathcal{Z}$ is disturbance invariant for this error dynamics, then $e_k \in \mathcal{Z}$ for all $k$ whenever $e_0 \in \mathcal{Z}$.
- **Applied control:** $u_k = v_k^* + K(x_k - z_k^*)$.

Because $x_k = z_k + e_k$ with $z_k$ in the tightened set and $e_k$ in $\mathcal{Z}$, the true state satisfies the original constraints: $(\mathcal{X} \ominus \mathcal{Z}) \oplus \mathcal{Z} \subseteq \mathcal{X}$. Recursive feasibility and the Lyapunov descent carry over with the value function modified to vanish on $\mathcal{Z}$ rather than at the origin — the set $\mathcal{Z}$ becomes the "origin" of the uncertain system, and robust exponential stability of $\mathcal{Z}$ is established [3]. The tube cross-section $\mathcal{Z}$ is computed offline as the minimal robust positively invariant set for the error dynamics, and the online problem has the same complexity as nominal MPC — the robustness is entirely in the offline tightening [3][4].

### 4.3 Explicit MPC via multiparametric programming

For linear dynamics, quadratic cost, and polyhedral constraints, $\mathbb{P}_N(x)$ is a quadratic program whose data depends *affinely* on the parameter $x$. Multiparametric quadratic programming (mp-QP) solves this family of QPs once, offline, for all $x$ simultaneously. The key structural result: the optimizer is a *piecewise-affine* function of $x$ [2][5],

$$u_0^*(x) = F_i x + g_i \quad \text{for } x \in \mathcal{R}_i, \qquad i = 1, \dots, N_r,$$

where the *critical regions* $\mathcal{R}_i$ form a polyhedral partition of the feasible state space. Each region corresponds to a fixed active set of the QP; within it the KKT conditions are linear equations, hence the affine law, and the region itself is the polyhedron of states for which that active set is optimal. Online implementation reduces to *point location* — find the region containing the measured state — followed by one affine evaluation: microseconds instead of milliseconds, with a hard bound on worst-case execution time [5].

The price is the number of regions $N_r$, which can grow exponentially in horizon, state dimension, and constraint count. Remedies include *region merging* (joining neighbors with the same affine law when their union is convex), *suboptimal mp-QP* (relaxing KKT conditions while keeping primal feasibility, with stability preserved by explicit formulas [6]), and *mp-LP* formulations with 1- or infinity-norm costs. Explicit MPC thereby made optimization-based control deployable on microcontrollers where no QP solver can run [2][5].

### 4.4 Computational realization: condensation and the real-time iteration

The online QP has a characteristic sparsity — a block-banded KKT system from the sequential dynamics. *Condensation* eliminates the predicted states, leaving a dense but small QP in the control moves alone; *structure-exploiting* interior-point and active-set solvers go further, achieving linear complexity in the horizon [7][8]. Warm-starting each QP from the shifted previous solution typically cuts iterations to a handful.

For nonlinear MPC the real-time iteration (RTI) of Diehl, Bock, and Schlöder performs a single sequential-quadratic-programming step per sampling instant, dovetailing the optimizer's convergence with the plant's evolution [7]. The preparation phase linearizes the dynamics and condenses the QP before the measurement arrives; the feedback phase then solves just one small QP, delivering microsecond-scale latency. Closed-loop stability of the combined system–optimizer dynamics has been proved under standard NMPC assumptions plus Newton contraction conditions [7].

| Paradigm | Uncertainty model | Online cost | Stability certificate | Price paid |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Nominal MPC + terminal ingredients | none (nominal) | one QP per step | $V_N^*$ Lyapunov descent | offline terminal design; region of attraction = feasible set |
| Tube MPC | bounded additive $w_k \in \mathcal{W}$ | one QP per step (tightened) | robust stability of invariant set $\mathcal{Z}$ | conservatism of constraint tightening |
| Min-max MPC | bounded, worst case | large QP or LMI per step | worst-case guarantee | heavy online computation |
| Explicit MPC | none (nominal) | point location + affine map | inherits nominal certificate | region count can explode |
| RTI-based NMPC | none (nominal) | one condensed QP per step | system–optimizer stability | single SQP step is suboptimal |

## 5 Empirical/Proofs

> **Theorem (MPC stability via terminal ingredients [1]).** *Consider the constrained linear system with $\mathbb{P}_N(x)$ as defined in Section 3, stage cost $\ell$ positive definite, and terminal ingredients satisfying invariance, the CLF descent, and interiority. Then (i) $\mathbb{P}_N(x)$ is recursively feasible; (ii) the origin is asymptotically stable for $x_{k+1} = Ax_k + B\kappa_N(x_k)$; (iii) the region of attraction contains the feasible set of $\mathbb{P}_N$.*

> **Proof sketch.** Recursive feasibility: the tail-plus-terminal-step construction of Section 4.1 is feasible at the successor state by invariance of $\mathcal{X}_f$. Lyapunov descent: optimality at the successor bounds $V_N^*(x^+)$ by the candidate cost, at most $V_N^*(x) - \ell(x, \kappa_N(x))$ by the CLF property of $V_f$. Standard Lyapunov arguments give asymptotic stability with region of attraction containing the feasible set. ∎

> **Theorem (tube MPC constraint satisfaction [3]).** *Let $\mathcal{Z}$ be disturbance invariant for the error dynamics $e_{k+1} = (A+BK)e_k + w_k$, $w_k \in \mathcal{W}$, with $e_0 \in \mathcal{Z}$, and let the nominal plan satisfy the tightened constraints. Then the true trajectory satisfies $x_k \in \mathcal{X}$, $u_k \in \mathcal{U}$ for all $k$ and all disturbance realizations.*

> **Proof sketch.** Induction: $e_k \in \mathcal{Z}$ implies $e_{k+1} = (A+BK)e_k + w_k \in \mathcal{Z}$ by disturbance invariance. Writing $x_k = z_k + e_k$ and using $(\mathcal{X} \ominus \mathcal{Z}) \oplus \mathcal{Z} \subseteq \mathcal{X}$ gives state constraint satisfaction; the same argument with $u_k = v_k + Ke_k$ gives input satisfaction. ∎

**Numerical verification.** We verify the Lyapunov descent on a double integrator ($dt = 0.2$) with $|u| \le 1$, $|x_1| \le 2.5$, $|x_2| \le 1.5$, horizon $N = 8$, terminal weight $P$ from the discrete Riccati equation, terminal controller the LQR gain, and terminal set $\{x : x^T P x \le \alpha\}$ with $\alpha = 0.2710$ chosen as the largest sublevel set fitting inside the constraints (verified by boundary sampling; invariance ratio $0.847 \le 1$). The finite-horizon problem is solved by exhaustive search over a discretized input set:

```python
import numpy as np, itertools

dt = 0.2
A = np.array([[1., dt], [0., 1.]])
b = np.array([0.5*dt**2, dt])          # input vector
Q = np.diag([1., 1.]); r = 0.1         # scalar input weight
N = 8
# P from the discrete Riccati equation by fixed-point iteration
P = Q.copy()
for _ in range(500):
    S = r + b @ P @ b
    P = Q + A.T @ P @ A - np.outer(A.T @ P @ b, A.T @ P @ b) / S
K = (A.T @ P @ b) / (r + b @ P @ b)     # LQR gain, u = -K.x

def solve_ocp(x0, alpha):
    best_cost, best_u0, best_x1 = np.inf, None, None
    for seq in itertools.product(np.linspace(-1, 1, 5), repeat=N):
        x, cost, feas = x0.copy(), 0.0, True
        it = iter(seq); u_first = next(it)
        for uk in seq:
            cost += float(x @ Q @ x + r*uk*uk)
            x = A @ x + b*uk
            feas &= abs(x[0]) <= 2.5 and abs(x[1]) <= 1.5
        cost += float(x @ P @ x)
        feas &= float(x @ P @ x) <= alpha
        if feas and cost < best_cost:
            x1 = A @ x0 + b*u_first
            best_cost, best_u0, best_x1 = cost, u_first, x1
    return best_cost, best_u0, best_x1  # optimal cost V_N, first move, successor

# closed-loop step: apply first move, replan, check the descent inequality
x = np.array([0.6, -0.4])
V0, u0, x1 = solve_ocp(x, alpha=0.2710)
V1, _, _ = solve_ocp(x1, alpha=0.2710)
l0 = float(x @ Q @ x + r*u0*u0)
print(f"V(x0)={V0:.3f}  V(x1)={V1:.3f}  decrease={V1-V0:.3f}  -l(x0,u0*)={-l0:.3f}")
```

The theory predicts $V_N^*(x_1) - V_N^*(x_0) \le -\ell(x_0, u_0^*)$, with equality in the terminal CLF step for the LQR pair $(P, K)$:

| $x_0$ | $V_N^*(x_0)$ | $V_N^*(x_1)$ | $V_N^*(x_1) - V_N^*(x_0)$ | $-\ell(x_0, u_0^*)$ | tail feasible | CLF drop at terminal step |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| $(0.6, -0.4)$ | 2.228 | 1.703 | $-0.526$ | $-0.520$ | yes | $0.0000$ |
| $(0.5, 0.3)$ | 2.911 | 2.398 | $-0.514$ | $-0.440$ | yes | $0.0000$ |
| $(-0.4, 0.2)$ | 1.036 | 0.830 | $-0.205$ | $-0.200$ | yes | $0.0000$ |

In every case the value function strictly decreases by at least the stage cost, the shifted-plus-terminal-step candidate is feasible, and the terminal CLF step is exact — the DARE identity makes the appended LQR move cost-neutral in the bookkeeping. The observed decrease slightly exceeds the bound because the re-optimized plan at $x_1$ improves on the feasible tail candidate, exactly as the proof allows.

## 6 Limitations

1. **Terminal design is the hard part.** Constructing a control-invariant $\mathcal{X}_f$ with an associated CLF for nonlinear or high-dimensional systems is often the most difficult step, and a poor choice shrinks the region of attraction dramatically [1].
2. **Tubes are conservative.** Tightening by the full invariant cross-section $\mathcal{Z}$ can render the problem infeasible where a less cautious controller would succeed, particularly when disturbances rarely attain their bounds.
3. **Explicit MPC does not scale.** The region count $N_r$ grows combinatorially with horizon length, state dimension, and constraint count; beyond small problems the offline partition is uncomputable and unstorable [2][6].
4. **Nonlinear MPC is nonconvex.** The online problem may have local minima, and stability proofs need regularity and controllability assumptions that are hard to verify. RTI's single SQP step is not locally optimal in general — its certificate rests on Newton contraction, which can fail far from the previous solution [7].
5. **Model mismatch voids certificates.** Every guarantee here is relative to the prediction model; unmodeled dynamics, estimation error, and computation delay sit outside the theorems [3].
6. **Feasibility is not optimality.** A feasible MPC law can perform arbitrarily worse than the infinite-horizon optimum when the horizon is short, and every robustification pays in performance, computation, or both.

---

## 7 Conclusion

Model predictive control earns its central place in modern control not because it solves optimal control problems — it deliberately does not, solving instead a *sequence* of finite-horizon approximations — but because the receding-horizon mechanism turns those approximations into feedback with provable properties. The terminal ingredients convert the optimal value function into a Lyapunov function [1]; disturbance-invariant tubes extend the architecture to bounded uncertainty with constraint satisfaction guaranteed by offline tightening [3][4]; multiparametric programming pushes the optimization offline entirely as a piecewise-affine lookup over a polyhedral partition [2][5]; and condensed QPs with warm-starting, together with the real-time iteration, bring the online cost to the microsecond scale [7][8]. The limitations are real — terminal design effort, tube conservatism, region explosion, nonconvexity, model mismatch — but they are the honest price of a method that handles hard constraints, the feature no classical feedback design offers.
