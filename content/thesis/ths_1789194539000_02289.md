# The Riemann Zeta Function and the Distribution of Primes: Euler Products, Zero-Free Regions, and Explicit Formulae

## Abstract

The Riemann zeta function ζ(s) is the central analytic object governing the distribution of prime numbers. Beginning from Euler's product formula, valid for Re(s) > 1, we trace the development of the theory through Riemann's 1859 memoir: the analytic continuation of ζ to the punctured plane, the functional equation relating ζ(s) to ζ(1 − s), and the resulting architecture of the critical strip. The thesis derives the Riemann–von Mangoldt explicit formula, which expresses the Chebyshev function ψ(x) as a main term x plus a sum over the nontrivial zeros ρ of ζ, making precise the sense in which the zeros encode the fluctuations of the primes. We explain how zero-free regions for ζ translate into error terms in the prime number theorem, from the classical de la Vallée Poussin region to the Vinogradov–Korobov region, and survey the computational verification of the Riemann hypothesis, now rigorous to height 3 × 10^12. The exposition closes with the theory of Dirichlet L-functions, Siegel zeros, and the principal open problems.

## 1 Introduction

The prime numbers are the multiplicative atoms of the integers, and the question of how they are distributed among the integers is among the oldest in mathematics. Euclid proved their infinitude around 300 BCE, yet the *rate* at which they thin out resisted quantification for more than two thousand years. From extensive hand computations, Gauss conjectured as a teenager that the counting function

$$\pi(x) = \#\{p \le x : p \text{ prime}\}$$

satisfies the asymptotic law

$$\pi(x) \sim \frac{x}{\log x} \qquad (x \to \infty),$$

now called the *prime number theorem* (PNT). Legendre offered a competing empirical formula, and Chebyshev proved in 1850 that π(x) has the correct order of magnitude — establishing constants 0 < c₁ < c₂ with c₁x/log x < π(x) < c₂x/log x for all large x — and in the same stroke proved Bertrand's postulate [5].

The decisive breakthrough arrived in 1859, when Bernhard Riemann published his memoir *Ueber die Anzahl der Primzahlen unter einer gegebenen Grösse* [1]. Riemann's radical move was to regard

$$\zeta(s) = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{1}{n^s}, \qquad \Re(s) > 1,$$

a function Euler had studied for real s, as a function of a *complex* variable. By analytically continuing ζ past its original domain, Riemann tied the fluctuations of π(x) directly to the zeros of ζ(s) — and conjectured, almost in passing, that every nontrivial zero lies on the line Re(s) = 1/2.

This thesis develops Riemann's program in full: the Euler product, analytic continuation, and functional equation (§4.1–4.2); the explicit formula exhibiting the zeros as the "spectrum" of the primes (§4.3); the zero-free regions that convert analytically into error terms in the prime number theorem (§4.4); the extension to Dirichlet L-functions (§4.5); and the empirical state of the art, from numerical tables to trillion-zero verifications of the Riemann hypothesis (§5).

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The zeta function and the Euler product

For Re(s) > 1 the defining series converges absolutely. Euler discovered in 1737 that it factors over the primes [2]:

> **Theorem (Euler product).** For Re(s) > 1,
> $$\zeta(s) = \prod_{p} \frac{1}{1 - p^{-s}},$$
> the product over all primes p.

*Proof.* Expand each factor as a geometric series, (1 − p^{−s})^{−1} = Σ_{k≥0} p^{−ks}, valid since |p^{−s}| < 1. Multiplying the factors for p ≤ y and invoking unique factorization reproduces Σ n^{−s} over integers all of whose prime factors are ≤ y; letting y → ∞ and using absolute convergence to justify rearrangement gives the result. ∎

The Euler product is unique factorization rendered as analysis. Taking logarithmic derivatives,

$$-\frac{\zeta'(s)}{\zeta(s)} = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{\Lambda(n)}{n^s}, \qquad \Re(s) > 1,$$

where Λ is the *von Mangoldt function*: Λ(n) = log p if n = p^k for a prime p, and 0 otherwise. This Dirichlet series is the bridge from ζ to the primes: its coefficients are supported on prime powers.

### 2.2 Chebyshev's functions

It is convenient to weight each prime by log p. Define

$$\psi(x) = \sum_{n \le x} \Lambda(n), \qquad \theta(x) = \sum_{p \le x} \log p.$$

Partial summation shows the equivalences π(x) ~ x/log x ⟺ ψ(x) ~ x ⟺ θ(x) ~ x [4]; the three forms of the prime number theorem are interchangeable. The function ψ(x), a step function jumping by log p at each prime power, is the object the explicit formula describes most directly.

### 2.3 Mellin inversion: Perron's formula

The Dirichlet series −ζ′/ζ and its summatory function ψ are a Mellin pair. *Perron's formula* gives, for c > 1 and x not a prime power,

$$\psi(x) = \frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{c-i\infty}^{c+i\infty} \left(-\frac{\zeta'}{\zeta}(s)\right)\frac{x^s}{s}\,ds.$$

This contour integral is the engine of the theory. Shifting the line of integration leftward, we collect residues — the pole at s = 1 yields the main term x, and each zero ρ of ζ contributes a term −x^ρ/ρ — while the cost of the shift is controlled by growth estimates for ζ and by how far left we may go: the *zero-free region*.

## 3 Methodology

Our methodology is the classical contour-integration pipeline of analytic number theory, applied uniformly throughout:

1. **Integral representation.** Begin with Perron's formula for ψ(x), the contour placed at Re(s) = c > 1 where −ζ′/ζ is given by its absolutely convergent Dirichlet series.
2. **Contour shift.** Deform the contour leftward past Re(s) = 1 to the boundary of the known zero-free region (or, conditionally, to Re(s) = 1/2 + ε), collecting residues:
   - the simple pole of −ζ′/ζ at s = 1 contributes the main term x;
   - each nontrivial zero ρ contributes −x^ρ/ρ;
   - the trivial zeros contribute an absolutely convergent series, negligible for large x.
3. **Estimation.** Bound the remaining contour integrals using growth estimates for ζ(s) in the critical strip and the width of the zero-free region.
4. **Translation.** Convert estimates for ψ(x) into estimates for π(x) by partial summation, e.g. π(x) = ψ(x)/log x + ∫₂^x ψ(t)/(t log²t) dt + O(√x).

This single framework produces every theorem below: the prime number theorem itself follows from the mere nonvanishing of ζ on Re(s) = 1; the de la Vallée Poussin and Vinogradov–Korobov error terms follow from wider zero-free regions; and the Riemann hypothesis would push the contour to Re(s) = 1/2, yielding ψ(x) = x + O(x^{1/2+ε}). The same pipeline, with ζ replaced by Dirichlet L-functions, governs primes in arithmetic progressions.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Euler Product and Analytic Continuation

The Euler product defines ζ only for Re(s) > 1, but the function extends much further. Summation by parts gives a continuation to Re(s) > 0:

$$\zeta(s) = \frac{s}{s-1} - s\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{\{x\}}{x^{s+1}}\,dx, \qquad \Re(s) > 0,\ s \ne 1,$$

where {x} denotes the fractional part. Riemann's own continuation, via the Hankel contour C encircling the positive real axis, reaches all of ℂ minus the pole:

$$\zeta(s) = \frac{\Gamma(1-s)}{2\pi i}\int_{C}\frac{(-z)^{s-1}}{e^{z}-1}\,dz.$$

The integral is entire in s; the factor Γ(1 − s) contributes exactly one pole, at s = 1, with residue 1. Hence:

> **Theorem.** ζ extends to a meromorphic function on ℂ whose only singularity is a simple pole at s = 1 with residue 1.

The same contour machinery evaluates ζ at even positive integers, recovering Euler's formula ζ(2k) = (−1)^{k+1}B_{2k}(2π)^{2k}/(2(2k)!), whence ζ(2) = π²/6 [1][2].

### 4.2 The Functional Equation and the Critical Strip

The deepest global property of ζ is its symmetry about Re(s) = 1/2. From the theta function Θ(x) = Σ_{n∈ℤ} e^{−πn²x} and Poisson summation, Θ(x) = x^{−1/2}Θ(1/x), Riemann derived the functional equation. With the *completed* zeta function

$$\xi(s) = \tfrac{1}{2}s(s-1)\,\pi^{-s/2}\,\Gamma(s/2)\,\zeta(s),$$

it takes the elegant form:

> **Theorem (Functional equation).** ξ is an entire function of order 1, and ξ(s) = ξ(1−s) for all s ∈ ℂ.

Equivalently, in asymmetric form,

$$\zeta(s) = 2^{s}\pi^{s-1}\sin(\tfrac{\pi s}{2})\,\Gamma(1-s)\,\zeta(1-s).$$

Because the Euler product never vanishes for Re(s) > 1 and Γ has no zeros, the sine factor forces ζ(−2k) = 0 for k = 1, 2, … — the *trivial zeros* — and these are the only zeros with Re(s) ≤ 0. Every other zero, the *nontrivial* zeros, lies in the *critical strip* 0 < Re(s) < 1. From ξ(s) = ξ(1−s) = \overline{ξ(\bar s)} they occur in symmetric quadruples {ρ, \barρ, 1−ρ, 1−\barρ}.

Riemann's famous conjecture is the ultimate form of this symmetry:

> **Conjecture (Riemann hypothesis, 1859).** Every nontrivial zero ρ of ζ satisfies Re(ρ) = 1/2.

The line Re(s) = 1/2 is the *critical line*. The zeros grow steadily denser with height; their counting function obeys the Riemann–von Mangoldt formula

$$N(T) = \#\{\rho : 0 < \Im\rho \le T\} = \frac{T}{2\pi}\log\frac{T}{2\pi} - \frac{T}{2\pi} + O(\log T),$$

so that near height T there are roughly (1/2π)log(T/2π) zeros per unit interval [2].

### 4.3 Nontrivial Zeros, the Explicit Formula, and the Riemann Hypothesis

Riemann's masterstroke was an *explicit formula* that inverts the Mellin transform exactly. For x > 1 not a prime power [3]:

> **Theorem (Riemann–von Mangoldt explicit formula).**
> $$\psi(x) = x - \sum_{\rho}\frac{x^{\rho}}{\rho} - \log(2\pi) - \tfrac{1}{2}\log(1-x^{-2}),$$
> the sum over all nontrivial zeros ρ taken as lim_{T→∞} Σ_{|Imρ|≤T}.

Each zero contributes a wave x^ρ = x^β e^{iγ log x} of "frequency" γ = Im(ρ) and amplitude x^β/|ρ|. The fluctuations of the primes about the main term x are therefore a superposition of harmonics indexed by the zeros — a Fourier analysis of the primes in which *the zeros are the spectrum*. The final two terms are the contributions of the trivial zeros and the archimedean constant, respectively.

The formula makes the role of the Riemann hypothesis transparent: since |x^ρ| = x^β, zeros with β near 1 drive large, weakly damped oscillations, whereas RH (β = 1/2 for all ρ) damps every harmonic to order √x:

> **Theorem (von Koch, 1901).** The Riemann hypothesis implies ψ(x) = x + O(x^{1/2}\log^2 x), and the exponent 1/2 is best possible.

Schoenfeld (1976) made this fully explicit: under RH, |π(x) − Li(x)| < √x log x/(8π) for all x ≥ 2657 [4]. The lowest zeros sit at 1/2 ± 14.13472514…i, and every zero yet computed — trillions of them — lies on the critical line.

### 4.4 Zero-Free Regions: From de la Vallée Poussin to Vinogradov–Korobov

Unconditionally we cannot reach Re(s) = 1/2, but every zero-free region left of Re(s) = 1 converts, through the contour-shift methodology, into an error term in the prime number theorem.

**Nonvanishing on Re(s) = 1.** Hadamard and de la Vallée Poussin independently proved in 1896 that ζ(1+it) ≠ 0 for all real t, completing the first proof of the PNT. The elegant core is the trigonometric inequality

$$3 + 4\cos\theta + \cos 2\theta = 2(1+\cos\theta)^2 \ge 0,$$

applied to −Re(ζ′/ζ)(σ+it) = Σ_p Σ_k cos(kt log p)/(k p^{kσ}), which yields

$$\zeta(\sigma)^3\,\zeta(\sigma+it)^4\,\zeta(\sigma+2it)^2 \ge 1 \qquad (\sigma > 1).$$

Were ζ(1+it₀) = 0, the left side would vanish to at least fourth order as σ → 1⁺ while ζ(σ)³ blows up only to third order — a contradiction [4][5].

**The classical region.** Refining the argument, de la Vallée Poussin established the zero-free region

$$\zeta(\sigma+it) \ne 0 \quad \text{for} \quad \sigma > 1 - \frac{c}{\log(|t|+2)},$$

which yields the error term

$$\psi(x) = x + O\!\left(x\,\exp(-c_1\sqrt{\log x})\right).$$

**Vinogradov–Korobov.** Bounding ζ(σ+it) in the critical strip by deep exponential-sum estimates (Weyl differencing, van der Corput's method), Vinogradov and Korobov (1958) widened the region to

$$\sigma > 1 - \frac{c}{(\log|t|)^{2/3}\,(\log\log|t|)^{1/3}} \qquad (|t| \ge 3),$$

giving the strongest known unconditional error term [6]:

$$\psi(x) = x + O\!\left(x\,\exp\!\left(-c\,\frac{(\log x)^{3/5}}{(\log\log x)^{1/5}}\right)\right).$$

Ford (2002) supplied the sharpest explicit constants for this region [6]. Its qualitative shape has resisted improvement for over six decades; the associated zero-density estimates hit a "one-half barrier" tied to the large sieve, and pushing past it is a central open problem.

### 4.5 Dirichlet L-Functions and Siegel Zeros

The theory extends to primes in arithmetic progressions via Dirichlet L-functions. For a Dirichlet character χ modulo q,

$$L(s,\chi) = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\chi(n)}{n^s} = \prod_{p}\frac{1}{1-\chi(p)p^{-s}}, \qquad \Re(s) > 1,$$

with analytic continuation and functional equation paralleling ζ. The *generalized Riemann hypothesis* (GRH) places every nontrivial zero of every L(s,χ) on Re(s) = 1/2. A hazard with no analogue for ζ is the possible *Siegel zero*: a real zero β of L(s,χ), for real χ, extremely close to 1. Landau proved at most one such zero can occur in each range, and Siegel's theorem gives the bound β < 1 − c(ε)q^{−ε} — but *ineffectively*, meaning the constant c(ε) cannot be computed, so the estimate is unusable in effective results [3]. Eliminating Siegel zeros effectively would sharpen uniform distribution theorems for primes in progressions, including Linnik's theorem on the least prime in an arithmetic progression.

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 From nonvanishing to the prime number theorem

We sketch the deduction of the PNT from ζ(1+it) ≠ 0, following Newman's short proof. Nonvanishing implies −ζ′/ζ extends holomorphically to Re(s) ≥ 1 apart from its pole at s = 1. A Tauberian theorem (Wiener–Ikehara, or Newman's analytic theorem) applied to the Laplace transform ∫₁^∞ (ψ(x)−x)x^{−s−1}dx then converts this analytic input into ψ(x) ~ x, whence π(x) ~ x/log x [4].

### 5.2 Numerical agreement: π(x) against Li(x)

The logarithmic integral Li(x) = ∫₂^x dt/log t tracks π(x) with remarkable fidelity:

| x | π(x) | Li(x) | π(x) − Li(x) |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| 10⁶ | 78,498 | 78,627.5 | −129.5 |
| 10⁹ | 50,847,534 | 50,849,234 | −1,700 |
| 10¹² | 37,607,912,018 | 37,607,950,281 | −38,263 |

Littlewood proved in 1914 that π(x) − Li(x) changes sign infinitely often [2], yet the first crossing is enormously far out: Bays and Hudson (2000) showed the first x with π(x) > Li(x) lies below ≈ 1.4 × 10³¹⁶ [10], far beyond the reach of direct computation.

The snippet below sieves π(x) and compares it with a numerical Li(x), reproducing the agreement:

```python
import numpy as np

def pi_sieve(n: int) -> int:
    """Count primes <= n with an odd-only Sieve of Eratosthenes."""
    sieve = bytearray(b"\x01") * ((n + 1) // 2)
    sieve[0] = 0  # 1 is not prime
    for i in range(3, int(n ** 0.5) + 1, 2):
        if sieve[i // 2]:
            start = (i * i) // 2
            sieve[start::i] = b"\x00" * ((len(sieve) - 1 - start) // i + 1)
    return int(sum(sieve)) + 1  # +1 accounts for the prime 2

def li_numeric(x: float, n: int = 200_000) -> float:
    """Trapezoidal rule for Li(x) = integral_2^x dt / ln t."""
    ts = np.linspace(2.0, x, n)
    return float(np.trapezoid(1.0 / np.log(ts), ts))

for x in (10**4, 10**5, 10**6):
    print(x, pi_sieve(x), round(li_numeric(float(x)), 1))
```

### 5.3 Computational verification of the Riemann hypothesis

Although RH is unproved, large-scale computation tightly constrains any counterexample. Two technologies combine:

- **Zero location:** the Odlyzko–Schönhage algorithm evaluates ζ(1/2+it) at dense grids of points in nearly linear time, and *Turing's method* certifies completeness — that no zeros were missed between computed ones — via rigorous bounds on the argument function S(t).
- **Rigorous certification:** interval arithmetic (Arb/MPFI) turns numerics into proofs.

The landmarks are:

- Gourdon (2004) computed the first 10¹³ nontrivial zeros and verified that all lie on the critical line [7];
- Platt and Trudgian (2021) gave a fully rigorous interval-arithmetic proof that RH holds up to height 3 × 10¹² [8];
- Odlyzko's computations of zeros at heights near 10²⁰ and beyond, tabulated publicly [9], confirmed Montgomery's pair-correlation (GUE) predictions for the spacing statistics of zeros.

These computations cannot prove RH, but they exclude "small" counterexamples and supply overwhelming empirical evidence.

## 6 Limitations

- **The Riemann hypothesis is unproved.** Every conditional estimate — the O(x^{1/2}log²x) error term, Schoenfeld's explicit bound, the sharpest forms of the explicit formula — depends on it. A single off-line zero would falsify RH, though it would barely disturb the unconditional theory.
- **The zero-free-region barrier.** The Vinogradov–Korobov region has stood qualitatively unimproved since 1958. Zero-density estimates of the form N(σ,T) ≪ T^{c(1−σ)}log^C T encounter a one-half barrier connected with the large sieve; the Bombieri–Vinogradov theorem distributes primes in arithmetic progressions only up to modulus x^{1/2}, and the Elliott–Halberstam conjecture beyond that remains open.
- **Siegel zeros and ineffectivity.** Siegel's theorem controls a possible real zero near s = 1 only ineffectively, blocking effective improvements to Linnik-type bounds on the least prime in an arithmetic progression.
- **Conditional convergence.** The explicit formula's sum Σ_ρ x^ρ/ρ converges only conditionally (zeros paired as ρ ↔ \barρ, truncated by height); naive numerical evaluation demands careful truncation analysis, and the main term x emerges only through delicate cancellation.
- **Computational limits.** Verifying RH to height T costs superlinear time; heights like 10²⁴ are out of reach, and no finite computation can constitute a proof.

## 7 Conclusion

The Riemann zeta function is the Rosetta stone of prime distribution. From Euler's product — unique factorization rendered as analysis — through Riemann's analytic continuation and functional equation, to the explicit formula that writes the primes' fluctuations as a superposition of waves indexed by the zeros, a single object governs the entire landscape. The prime number theorem itself is the analytic statement that ζ does not vanish on Re(s) = 1; every widening of our knowledge of where ζ *may* vanish translates, through the contour-shift methodology, into a sharper accounting of the primes.

The frontier is sharply defined: prove the Riemann hypothesis, or at least widen the zero-free region; rule out Siegel zeros effectively; extend the Elliott–Halberstam range past one-half. Until then, the zeta function keeps its secrets — but, as Riemann saw in 1859, it also furnishes the exact language in which those secrets are written.
