# Graph Neural Networks: Message Passing, Weisfeiler–Leman Expressivity Bounds, and Equivariant Architectures

## Abstract

Graph neural networks (GNNs) have become the dominant paradigm for learning on graph-structured data, yet for years their power rested on empirical success rather than theoretical understanding. This thesis develops the now-standard mathematical account of what message-passing architectures can and cannot learn. We begin with the Message Passing Neural Network (MPNN) formalism of Gilmer et al. and prove, following Xu et al., that the discriminative capacity of any neighborhood-aggregation GNN is upper-bounded by the one-dimensional Weisfeiler–Leman (1-WL) graph-isomorphism test, with equality attained exactly when aggregation is injective — the design principle behind the Graph Isomorphism Network. We then ascend the hierarchy: Morris et al.'s k-GNNs and Maron et al.'s invariant and equivariant graph networks break the 1-WL ceiling at the cost of polynomial tensor order, and we survey the complete characterization of permutation-equivariant linear layers via Bell numbers. Finally, we treat geometric equivariance, where Satorras et al.'s E(n)-equivariant message passing shows that coordinate updates by equivariant vector fields achieve Euclidean symmetry without spherical harmonics. Empirical benchmarks, formal theorems, and open limitations complete the picture.

##1 Introduction

Few formalisms in modern machine learning have moved as quickly from engineering heuristic to mathematical theory as the **message passing neural network**. Within five years of Gilmer et al.'s unification of graph convolutional methods under a single message-passing framework [2], the field possessed a rigorous answer to its most basic question: *which graphs can these networks tell apart?* The answer — *exactly those the Weisfeiler–Leman hierarchy tells apart* [1][3][4] — is one of the sharpest characterizations of neural expressivity anywhere in deep learning, and it reshaped architecture design from trial-and-error into theorem-driven engineering.

The stakes are practical. Graphs are the natural representation of molecules, social networks, program structure, physical systems, and knowledge bases. A practitioner who understands why the ubiquitous *mean* aggregator silently discards structural information will not waste months tuning a network that is provably blind to the phenomenon under study. Conversely, knowing that a **3-WL-expressive** architecture exists with the cost of ordinary matrix multiplication [5] turns a seemingly intractable expressivity requirement into an engineering decision.

> **Thesis statement:** The expressivity of message-passing GNNs is exactly the 1-WL bound, achievable by injective aggregation (GIN) and strictly dominated by higher-order architectures (k-GNNs, invariant graph networks, PPGN) that climb the k-WL hierarchy; orthogonal to this, *equivariant* architectures extend message passing to geometric data by respecting Euclidean symmetries in the coordinate updates themselves.

This thesis proceeds as follows. Section 2 fixes the formalism: graphs, the MPNN equations, and the Weisfeiler–Leman test. Section 3 develops the methodological core — the multiset-injectivity argument, the Bell-number characterization of equivariant layers, and the E(n) update. Section 4 deep-dives into four technical fronts. Section 5 states the theorems and surveys the empirical record. Section 6 catalogs the limitations. Section 7 concludes.

##2 Background

### 2.1 Graphs and the learning problem

A *graph* $G = (V, E)$ consists of a vertex set $V$ with $|V| = n$ and an edge set $E \subseteq V \times V$. Each vertex $v$ may carry a feature vector $x_v \in \mathbb{R}^d$, and each edge $(v,u)$ a feature $e_{vu}$. The central difficulty of graph learning is **permutation symmetry**: relabeling the vertices produces an isomorphic graph that must receive the same treatment. Any graph-level function $f$ we wish to learn should satisfy $f(\pi \cdot G) = f(G)$ for all permutations $\pi$ (*invariance*), while node-level functions should satisfy $f(\pi \cdot G) = \pi \cdot f(G)$ (*equivariance*). Architectures that violate these symmetries waste capacity learning what group theory gives for free — or worse, memorize arbitrary vertex orderings.

### 2.2 The MPNN formalism

Gilmer et al. [2] observed that a zoo of early graph models — spectral convolutions, GraphSAGE, gated graph networks — were instances of one scheme. In the **message passing phase**, run for $T$ steps:

$$m_v^{t+1} = \sum_{u \in \mathcal{N}(v)} M_t(h_v^t, h_u^t, e_{vu}), \qquad h_v^{t+1} = U_t(h_v^t, m_v^{t+1}),$$

where $M_t$ is a *message function*, $U_t$ an *update function*, and $h_v^0 = x_v$. In the **readout phase**, a graph-level prediction is produced by an invariant aggregation:

$$\hat{y} = R\left(\{h_v^T \mid v \in V\}\right),$$

with $R$ typically a sum or a Set2Set encoder [2]. Every design decision in graph learning since 2017 — attention weights (GAT), edge gates, directional messages — is a choice of $M_t$, $U_t$, and $R$ within this skeleton.

### 2.3 The Weisfeiler–Leman test

The **1-dimensional Weisfeiler–Leman (1-WL)** test, also called *color refinement*, is the classical heuristic for graph isomorphism [3]. Each vertex receives an initial color $c_v^0$ (from its label), and colors are iteratively refined:

$$c_v^{t+1} = \mathrm{hash}\left(c_v^t,\ \{\!\{c_u^t \mid u \in \mathcal{N}(v)\}\!\}\right),$$

where $\{\!\{\cdot\}\!\}$ denotes a multiset. Iteration stops when the color partition stabilizes; two graphs whose final color histograms differ are non-isomorphic.

```haskell
-- 1-WL color refinement in Haskell: the loop every GNN secretly runs
type Color = Int
refine :: Graph -> Map Vertex Color -> Map Vertex Color
refine g colors = Map.mapWithKey step colors
  where
    step v c = hash (c, multiset [colors ! u | u <- neighbors g v])
stable :: Graph -> Map Vertex Color
stable g = fixpoint (refine g) (initialColors g)
```

The structural parallel with message passing is already visible: both iterate *aggregate neighborhood, then update*. The deep question is whether this resemblance is an upper bound, a lower bound, or an identity.

##3 Methodology

Our methodology is **theoretical characterization followed by constructive attainment**: (i) prove an upper bound on MPNN discrimination in terms of 1-WL; (ii) exhibit an architecture that *reaches* the bound, via injective multiset aggregation; (iii) construct higher-order architectures that *exceed* it, and characterize their linear layers completely; (iv) extend the equivariance story from permutations to Euclidean groups.

### 3.1 The injectivity argument

The crux of Xu et al. [1] is a deceptively simple observation about multisets. A node's neighborhood is a *multiset* of feature vectors, and the aggregation step must map multisets to vectors. **Mean** aggregation confounds $\{\!\{x, x\}\!\}$ with $\{\!\{x\}\!\}$ (losing count); **max** aggregation confounds $\{\!\{x, x, y\}\!\}$ with $\{\!\{x, y\}\!\}$ (losing multiplicity). Only **sum** aggregation over a sufficiently rich feature space, composed with an injective function, can be injective on multisets of bounded size. Formally, there exists a function $f$ such that $h(X) = \sum_{x \in X} f(x)$ is injective for multisets $X$ of bounded cardinality [1] — the universal-multiset-encoder lemma that makes the Graph Isomorphism Network possible.

```python
# GIN: the maximally expressive MPNN, in PyTorch-style pseudocode
class GINLayer(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, dim):
        super().__init__()
        self.mlp = nn.Sequential(nn.Linear(dim, dim), nn.ReLU(),
                                 nn.Linear(dim, dim))
        self.eps = nn.Parameter(torch.zeros(1))  # learnable self-weight
    def forward(self, h, adj):
        # sum aggregation: injective on multisets; MLP: injective on sums
        agg = (1 + self.eps) * h + adj @ h
        return self.mlp(agg)
```

### 3.2 The Bell-number characterization

Maron et al. [4] attacked expressivity from the opposite end: instead of bounding message passing, they characterized *all* permutation-equivariant linear layers for graph data. For tensors indexed by $k$-tuples of nodes, the space of equivariant linear maps $\mathbb{R}^{n^k} \to \mathbb{R}^{n^\ell}$ has dimension given by the **Bell numbers** — the number of set partitions. Concretely, for edge-valued data ($k = \ell = 2$) the invariant layer space is 2-dimensional and the equivariant space is **15-dimensional** [4]; for general $k$, the dimensions are the $k$-th and $2k$-th Bell numbers. This gives a finite, complete, orthogonal basis of layers — no architecture search required, just linear algebra. Moreover, these higher-order networks can approximate *any* MPNN [4], placing message passing strictly inside the invariant-network family.

### 3.3 The E(n) update

For geometric graphs — molecules, point clouds, physical simulations — the relevant symmetry is the Euclidean group E($n$) of rotations, reflections, and translations. Satorras et al. [7] derived an equivariant layer that avoids the expensive irreducible representations (spherical harmonics) of earlier methods:

$$\begin{aligned}
m_{ij} &= \phi_e\left(h_i^\ell, h_j^\ell, \|x_i^\ell - x_j^\ell\|^2, a_{ij}\right), \\
x_i^{\ell+1} &= x_i^\ell + C \sum_{j \ne i} (x_i^\ell - x_j^\ell)\, \phi_x(m_{ij}), \\
h_i^{\ell+1} &= \phi_h\left(h_i^\ell, \sum_{j \in \mathcal{N}(i)} m_{ij}\right),
\end{aligned}$$

where all $\phi$ are MLPs and $\phi_x$ emits a *scalar*. Because relative displacements $(x_i - x_j)$ transform equivariantly under E($n$) while norms $\|x_i - x_j\|^2$ and $m_{ij}$ are invariant, the coordinate update is equivariant by construction — a vector field built from invariantly-weighted displacements [7].

##4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The MPNN Formalism and Its Injectivity Ceiling

Let us make the ceiling precise. Define the *discriminative power* of a model class as the set of non-isomorphic graph pairs it can assign different outputs. The 1-WL test computes, at each iteration, exactly the multiset-hash that an MPNN computes when its aggregation is injective and its update is injective [1][3]. Hence:

> **Theorem:** *Any MPNN distinguishes at most the graph pairs that 1-WL distinguishes. If the aggregation and update functions are injective, the MPNN distinguishes exactly the pairs 1-WL distinguishes.* [1][3]

The proof is a bisimulation: run 1-WL and the MPNN in lockstep; injectivity preserves color-equality as representation-equality at every layer, and non-injectivity can only merge what 1-WL keeps apart. This immediately diagnoses popular architectures: GCN's normalized mean aggregation and GraphSAGE's max-pooling are both non-injective, so they are *strictly* weaker than 1-WL [1]. The failure is not subtle — a GCN cannot count how many identical neighbors a node has, a fact with direct consequences for molecular graphs where, e.g., distinguishing carbon substitution patterns requires exactly such counting.

The GIN construction then closes the loop: with sum aggregation and an MLP update, injectivity is achieved (by the multiset lemma and the universal approximation of injective maps by MLPs on compacta), so GIN *equals* 1-WL — the maximally expressive MPNN [1].

### 4.2 Weisfeiler–Leman: From 1-WL to the k-WL Hierarchy

The 1-WL ceiling is not a property of graphs but of the *order* of the test. The **k-WL** test colors $k$-tuples $(v_1, \dots, v_k)$ and refines via neighborhoods that substitute one coordinate at a time; 2-WL already distinguishes many graphs 1-WL cannot (including the famous pair of non-isomorphic strongly regular graphs that fool 1-WL, though not all of them). The hierarchy is strict: for every $k$ there exist graphs separable by $(k+1)$-WL but not $k$-WL [3].

Morris et al. [3] made this hierarchy neural. Their **k-GNN** operates on $k$-element subsets of vertices, with message passing between subsets that overlap in $k-1$ vertices (the "local" variant) or arbitrary pairs (the "global" variant), and proved it matches the power of the corresponding $k$-WL test. The cost is polynomial in $n$ of degree $k$ — the expressivity–complexity tradeoff that haunts the entire field.

```tla
------------------------------ MODULE WLRefine ------------------------------
(* TLA+ specification of 1-WL color refinement as a state machine *)
EXTENDS Naturals, FiniteSets
CONSTANTS V,           \* vertex set
          N,           \* N[v] = neighbor set of v
          InitColor    \* initial coloring
VARIABLES color
Init == color = InitColor
RefineStep ==
    /\ color' = [v \in V |-> Hash(color[v], {color[u] : u \in N[v]})]
    /\ UNCHANGED <<>>
Next == RefineStep
Stable == \A v \in V : color'[v] = color[v]
Spec == Init /\ [][Next]_color /\ WF_color(Next)
=============================================================================
```

The TLA+ sketch above is more than decoration: it makes explicit that color refinement is a *monotone fixed-point computation* on a finite lattice of partitions, hence always terminating — the property that lets both the classical test and its neural analogues be analyzed as converging dynamical systems rather than arbitrary recursions.

| Test | Objects colored | Distinguishing power | Neural counterpart | Complexity |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1-WL | vertices | baseline | GIN (= 1-WL) [1] | $O(n + m)$ per round |
| 2-WL | vertex pairs | strictly stronger | 2-GNN [3] | $O(n^2)$ |
| 3-WL | triples | strictly stronger | PPGN (= 3-WL) [5] | $O(n^3)$ |
| k-WL | $k$-tuples | strict hierarchy | k-GNN / k-IGN [3][4] | $O(n^k)$ |

### 4.3 k-GNNs and Invariant/Equivariant Graph Networks

Maron et al.'s invariant graph networks (IGNs) [4] generalize message passing by replacing neighborhood aggregation with *all* equivariant linear maps between tensor powers. The Bell-number dimensions are worth contemplating: for $k = \ell = 2$ there are exactly 15 basis operators — including identity, transpose, diagonal extraction, row/column sums, and the all-ones map — and every permutation-equivariant linear layer on matrices is a linear combination of them. A deep network stacking such layers with pointwise nonlinearities and a final invariant readout is automatically a valid graph model for *any* $n$, since the basis is size-independent [4].

The follow-up "Provably Powerful Graph Networks" [5] delivered the field's most elegant expressivity result. Rather than paying the full $O(n^3)$ price of 3-WL tensors, the authors showed that a **2-order** network — operating on matrices, with the equivariant basis plus a *single quadratic operation*, matrix multiplication — already attains **3-WL** expressive power:

> **Theorem:** *A 2-order invariant graph network augmented with matrix multiplication distinguishes exactly the graph pairs that 3-WL distinguishes.* [5]

Matrix multiplication is the key: it composes 2-step walks, effectively simulating 3-tuple interactions without materializing 3-tensors. This is the rare theorem that is both deep and immediately implementable — PPGN layers are MLPs on the feature dimension interleaved with matrix products, and they achieved state-of-the-art on graph classification and regression benchmarks at the time [5].

Universality completes the picture. Keriven and Peyré [6] proved that invariant and equivariant GNNs with a single hidden layer are *universal* approximators — via a generalized Stone–Weierstrass theorem for equivariant algebras — and, crucially, that a *single* parameter set approximates uniformly well across graphs of *varying* size. This resolved the nagging worry that higher-order networks were universal only per fixed $n$.

    Array2::from_shape_fn((n, n), |(i, _)| d[i])

### 4.4 Equivariance in Geometric Space: E(n)-Equivariant Message Passing

Permutation symmetry is discrete; physical systems demand continuous symmetry. A molecular energy must be invariant to rigid motions, while predicted forces must *rotate with* the molecule — equivariance. Early geometric GNNs (Tensor Field Networks, SE(3)-Transformers) achieved this with spherical harmonics and Clebsch–Gordan products: mathematically complete but computationally heavy and confined to 3D.

EGNN [7] sidestepped the representation theory. Its insight: **equivariance can live in the coordinate update, not the features**. Messages $m_{ij}$ depend only on invariant quantities (embeddings, squared distances, edge attributes); coordinates move along the equivariant displacement field $(x_i - x_j)\phi_x(m_{ij})$, yielding E($n$)-equivariance for *any* $n$ with no spherical harmonics. The model matched or beat harmonic-based predecessors on N-body dynamics and QM9 while using only scalar messages [7], and its simplicity made it the workhorse backbone for later diffusion-based molecule generators.

The design space now has two orthogonal axes — *combinatorial expressivity* (the WL hierarchy) and *geometric equivariance* (the symmetry group) — and the frontier is their intersection: equivariant architectures that are also provably higher-order in the WL sense.

##5 Empirical Evidence and Formal Proofs

### 5.1 The core theorems

We collect the load-bearing results, each with its source:

> **Theorem (MPNN upper bound):** *No message-passing GNN distinguishes graphs that 1-WL deems possibly isomorphic.* — Morris et al. [3], Xu et al. [1]

> **Theorem (GIN optimality):** *There exists an MPNN (GIN, with sum aggregation and MLP update) that distinguishes exactly the graphs 1-WL distinguishes; hence GIN is the most expressive MPNN.* — Xu et al. [1]

> **Theorem (Higher-order correspondence):** *k-GNNs match k-WL; k-order invariant graph networks match k-WL; and 2-order networks with matrix multiplication match 3-WL.* — Morris et al. [3], Maron et al. [4][5]

> **Theorem (Universality):** *Single-hidden-layer invariant/equivariant GNNs are universal approximators of continuous invariant/equivariant graph functions, uniformly over varying graph sizes.* — Keriven & Peyré [6]

> **Theorem (E(n) equivariance):** *The EGNN layer satisfies $F(Rx + t) = RF(x) + t$ for all rotations/reflections $R$ and translations $t$.* — Satorras et al. [7]

### 5.2 Empirical record

The theory predicts, and experiments confirm, that aggregator choice dominates architecture folklore. On the TUDataset graph-classification suite, GIN — the simplest possible injective design — achieved the best reported results at publication on the social-network and bioinformatics benchmarks (e.g., 89.4% on IMDB-B, 82.7% on COLLAB, 76.2% on PROTEINS), outperforming GCN, GraphSAGE, and DGCNN baselines by 1–4 points [1]. The gains come entirely from injectivity, not depth or attention.

At the molecular scale, Gilmer et al.'s MPNN study [2] remains the canonical demonstration: an edge-network MPNN with Set2Set readout reached chemical accuracy (mean absolute error below the DFT error threshold) on 11 of 13 QM9 regression targets over 133,885 small molecules — the result that convinced chemistry the framework was worth the community's attention [2]. Higher-order models then pushed further: k-GNNs improved over 1-WL-limited baselines on molecular regression precisely on targets sensitive to substructures 1-WL cannot see [3], and PPGN's 3-WL power translated to state-of-the-art graph classification and regression [5]. On the geometric side, EGNN matched or beat spherical-harmonic models on N-body dynamics and QM9 while using only scalar messages [7].

##6 Limitations

The theory is sharp, and its sharpness cuts both ways.

**Complexity walls.** Expressivity is priced in tensor order: $k$-WL power costs $\Omega(n^k)$ time and memory [3][4]. PPGN's matrix-multiplication trick buys 3-WL at the price of dense $n \times n$ operations — still quadratic per layer, prohibitive for large sparse graphs. The hierarchy is therefore mostly a *diagnostic* tool for large graphs and a *constructive* tool for small ones (molecules, where $n < 100$).

**1-WL blindness is structural, not just theoretical.** Regular graphs that 1-WL cannot distinguish arise in real data — e.g., certain carbon nanostructures and symmetric molecular scaffolds. An MPNN deployed on such data is not merely suboptimal; it is *provably incapable* of the task, and no amount of data or tuning repairs this [1][3].

**Universality with caveats.** Keriven and Peyré's universality [6] holds for continuous functions on graphs with a fixed maximum size (or with uniformity conditions); the single-parameter-set result is powerful but does not erase the $O(n^k)$ evaluation cost. Maron et al.'s [4] approximation of arbitrary MPNNs by IGNs is likewise an existence result, not an efficient compilation.

**Geometric expressivity gaps.** EGNN's scalar messages are E($n$)-equivariant but limited: provably, scalar-message equivariant networks cannot express certain directional interactions that higher-order tensor (spherical-harmonic) features capture [7]. The field trades completeness for efficiency here, and the optimal tradeoff remains open.

**Beyond discrimination.** The WL framework measures *distinguishing* power, but learning also needs *generalization*, *optimization*, and *robustness*. Two architectures with identical WL power can differ wildly in sample efficiency — the theorems say nothing about which is learnable from finite data. Over-smoothing (node representations collapsing with depth) and over-squashing (long-range information bottlenecked through fixed-size vectors) afflict even WL-optimal models and sit entirely outside the expressivity analysis.

##7 Conclusion

In under a decade, graph neural networks acquired something few deep-learning paradigms possess: a **complete expressivity theory**. Message passing is 1-WL, no more and no less [1][3]; injectivity attains the bound [1]; higher-order tensors climb the k-WL hierarchy at polynomial cost [3][4][5]; equivariant linear layers are fully classified by Bell numbers [4]; universality holds across graph sizes [6]; and Euclidean symmetry is achievable by equivariant coordinate updates without harmonic machinery [7].

The practical moral is theorem-driven design: choose sum over mean when counting matters, reach for PPGN or k-GNNs when 1-WL-blind substructures carry the signal, and build E($n$) symmetry into the geometry rather than hoping the network discovers it. The open frontier is no longer *whether* we can characterize these models but *whether we can afford* the characterization — marrying WL-higher-order power with the scalability of simple message passing, and extending the theory from discrimination to generalization. The Weisfeiler–Leman test began in 1968 as a heuristic for a problem we still cannot solve efficiently; it endures, reincarnated, as the yardstick of graph learning.
