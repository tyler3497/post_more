# Congestion Control for RDMA at Datacenter Scale: From DCQCN to HPCC, Swift, and the Post-PFC Era of Resilient Fabrics

## Abstract

Remote Direct Memory Access over Converged Ethernet (RoCEv2) has become the dominant datacenter fabric for distributed machine learning, disaggregated storage, and high-performance computing, yet its reliance on Priority Flow Control (PFC) for losslessness has exposed the network to head-of-line blocking, congestion spreading, and deadlock. This thesis presents a systematic study of RDMA congestion control across five landmark mechanisms — DCQCN, TIMELY, HPCC, Swift, and IRN — tracing the evolution from ECN-based rate control through RTT-gradient schemes to in-network telemetry (INT) driven precision control and receiver-driven, PFC-free transports. We develop a unified analytic model of the feedback-control loops, derive convergence and stability conditions for each scheme, and compare them on a common basis of fairness, flow-completion time under incast, PFC-trigger frequency, and hardware cost. We further analyze PFC deadlock formation as a dependency-cycle problem and examine in-network telemetry as the enabling primitive for single-step rate convergence. Empirical evidence from the original studies shows that HPCC reduces flow-completion time by up to 95% relative to DCQCN and TIMELY in large-scale incast, Swift sustains near-100 Gbps per-server throughput at near-zero loss with accurate RTT measurement, and IRN eliminates PFC entirely while outperforming legacy RoCE by 6-83% with only 3-10% additional NIC resources. We conclude that the field is converging toward telemetry-rich, receiver-assisted, loss-tolerant congestion control, and we identify open problems in multi-tenant isolation, 400/800 GbE scaling, and verifiable switch dataplane guarantees.

## 1. Introduction

Link speeds have grown from 10 Gb/s to 400 Gb/s while per-access latency budgets shrank to microseconds. Kernel network stacks can no longer sustain modern workloads — distributed training, disaggregated NVMe fabrics, partition-aggregate analytics — so Remote Direct Memory Access (RDMA) offloads transport to the NIC, permitting user-space zero-copy access to remote memory.

Two technologies carry RDMA into Ethernet datacenters: **InfiniBand**, a purpose-built lossless fabric, and **RoCEv2** (RDMA over Converged Ethernet v2), which encapsulates InfiniBand transport packets in UDP/IP datagrams. Because RoCEv2 runs on commodity Ethernet, it inherits Ethernet's fundamental assumption of a *lossy* network — one that InfiniBand's go-back-N transport, designed for lossless operation, was never meant to tolerate. The industry's answer has been Priority Flow Control (PFC), a link-level pause mechanism that renders selected traffic classes lossless by forcing upstream ports to halt transmission when downstream buffers fill.

This bargain has proven costly. PFC pauses are coarse — pausing a priority class pauses *all* its flows, including innocent victim flows — producing head-of-line blocking and congestion trees [1]. Worse, cyclic buffer dependencies yield **PFC deadlock**: ports each waiting on buffers held by another, with zero throughput and no automatic recovery. Hyperscalers have suffered network-wide collapses from long-lived PFC storms [3].

The central question this thesis addresses is: *how should congestion be controlled in RDMA datacenter fabrics so that throughput, latency, and stability are jointly achieved, and is the PFC-based lossless fabric even necessary?* We answer it through a comparative, formal treatment of five landmark systems that collectively define the field:

1. **DCQCN** (Datacenter Quantized Congestion Notification) — the ECN-based, NIC-implemented rate controller deployed in Microsoft Azure and the de facto standard for RoCEv2 [1].
2. **TIMELY** — an RTT-gradient scheme from Google that abandons ECN in favor of precise hardware-timestamped round-trip measurements [2].
3. **HPCC** (High Precision Congestion Control) — Alibaba's INT-driven scheme that computes exact flow rates from per-hop queue telemetry in a single step [3].
4. **Swift** — Google's delay-based AIMD controller targeting explicit end-to-end delay with pacing under extreme congestion [6].
5. **IRN** (Improved RoCE NIC / Resilient RDMA) — a NIC redesign demonstrating that PFC is an artifact of legacy NIC design, not a fundamental requirement [7].

## 2. Background

### 2.1 RDMA Transports: InfiniBand and RoCEv2

RDMA verbs — *SEND/RECV*, *WRITE*, *READ*, *ATOMIC* — expose queue pairs (QPs) as the fundamental connection abstraction. Reliable Connection (RC) transport guarantees in-order, at-most-once delivery using per-QP sequence numbers and acknowledgments. The InfiniBand architecture specifies this transport over a credit-based lossless link layer: a receiver never drops due to buffer exhaustion because link-level flow control guarantees buffer availability.

RoCEv2 preserves the InfiniBand transport almost verbatim but replaces the link layer with UDP/IP/Ethernet, enabling routable RDMA across L3 Clos fabrics at the cost of exposing the transport to a lossy substrate.

| Property | InfiniBand | RoCEv2 |
|---|---|---|
| Link layer | Credit-based, lossless | Ethernet (lossy), PFC-simulated losslessness |
| L2/L3 headers | IB LRH | UDP/IP + IB GRH |
| Congestion signaling | FECN/BECN (IB CC) | ECN + CNP (RoCE CC) |
| Loss recovery | Go-back-N (rare losses) | Go-back-N; catastrophic under loss |
| Deployment | Purpose-built clusters | Commodity Ethernet Clos |

RoCEv2 mirrors InfiniBand's control points: a *Congestion Point* (switch) marks packets, a *Notification Point* (receiver) sends CNPs, and a *Reaction Point* (sender) throttles. DCQCN is the canonical RP algorithm.

### 2.2 From QCN to DCQCN

DCQCN fuses two lineages: **QCN** (IEEE 802.1Qau), a rate-based Layer-2 scheme, and **DCTCP**, which uses the *fraction* of ECN-marked packets to gauge congestion extent [1].

### 2.3 Delay as a Signal: the RTT Renaissance

ECN is a *binary, queue-threshold* signal: it reports that a queue exceeded Kmin, not how fast it is growing. With NIC hardware timestamping now providing sub-microsecond RTT accuracy, delay-gradient schemes — pioneered by TIMELY and refined by Swift — recover a *multi-bit, predictive* signal: the derivative of RTT reveals queue buildup *before* thresholds are crossed [2][6].

### 2.4 In-Network Telemetry

INT (and its probabilistic variant PINT) embeds per-hop measurements — queue length, timestamp, link utilization — directly into data or ACK packets [3]. A sender thus observes the *exact* bottleneck state rather than inferring it from marks or delay. HPCC exploits this to compute the fair rate in closed form, often in a single RTT [3].

### 2.5 PFC and Its Pathologies

PFC (IEEE 802.1Qbb) defines per-priority PAUSE frames with a quanta-based resume. Three pathologies dominate [7]:

- **Head-of-line blocking**: a pause halts the entire priority queue, victimizing uncongested flows.
- **Congestion spreading / PFC storms**: pauses propagate upstream into fabric-spanning congestion trees.
- **Deadlock**: cyclic buffer dependency freezes forwarding permanently without external intervention.

> **Theorem:** *In a lossless hop-by-hop flow-controlled network with finite buffers, a cyclic buffer dependency is a necessary and sufficient condition for persistent forwarding deadlock.* Deadlock freedom therefore requires either topological guarantees (e.g., deadlock-free routing), escape buffers, or the elimination of lossless hop-by-hop control altogether — the IRN thesis [7].

## 3. Methodology

Our method is comparative-analytic: we reconstruct each scheme as an instance of a unified discrete-time feedback controller and compare convergence behavior, equilibrium properties, and hardware cost using the primary sources' own reported measurements [1][3][6][7], since the five systems were evaluated on incompatible testbeds. We formalize each sender update law in common notation, analyze equilibrium and max-min fairness under a fluid model, model PFC deadlock as a resource-allocation graph, and specify the DCQCN sender as an executable TLA+ model exposing its synchronized-oscillation pathology [4].

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 DCQCN: The ECN Workhorse

DCQCN's sender tracks the current rate Rc, target rate Rt, and congestion estimate alpha in [0,1]. Each CNP triggers multiplicative decrease Rt ← Rc, Rc ← Rc·(1−alpha/2), with alpha an EWMA (gain g) over CNP arrivals. Recovery has three phases — *fast recovery*, *active increase* (byte-counter paced), *hyper-active increase* — gated by a timer tau ≈ 55 µs [1]. Azure deployment parameters: Kmin = 5 KB, Kmax = 200 KB, Pmax = 1%, g = 1/256, B = 10 MB, R_AI = 40 Mb/s on 40 Gb/s links [1][4].

The design's strength is its NIC-only implementation: no switch changes beyond RED-style ECN marking, and no host CPU involvement. Its weaknesses are well documented: **global synchronization** (identical timers and marking probabilities drive synchronized sawtooth oscillation; retuning g and tau cuts variance 45% at ~3% utilization cost [4]), **slow convergence** (small AIMD steps need many RTTs to reclaim bandwidth after incast, during which PFC absorbs the overshoot), and **parameter sensitivity** (Kmin, Kmax, g, tau, B interact non-linearly; misconfiguration directly causes PFC storms [3]).

We model the DCQCN reaction point in TLA+ to make the synchronization pathology precise:

```tla+
---- MODULE DCQCN_RP ----
EXTENDS Reals, Naturals
CONSTANTS NumSenders, C, G, Tau
VARIABLES rate, alpha, phase

TypeOK ==
  /\ rate \in [1..NumSenders -> Real]
  /\ alpha \in [1..NumSenders -> Real]

OnCNP(s) ==
  /\ alpha' = [alpha EXCEPT ![s] = (1 - G) * alpha[s] + G]
  /\ rate'  = [rate EXCEPT ![s] = rate[s] * (1 - alpha[s]/2)]
  /\ phase' = [phase EXCEPT ![s] = "FastRecovery"]

OnTimer(s) ==
  /\ phase[s] = "ActiveIncrease"
  /\ rate'  = [rate EXCEPT ![s] = rate[s] + R_AI]
  /\ UNCHANGED <<alpha, phase>>

Spec == Init /\ [][\/ \E s \in 1..NumSenders : OnCNP(s)
                    \/ \E s \in 1..NumSenders : OnTimer(s)]_vars
====
```

### 4.2 TIMELY and Swift: Congestion Control from the Delay Gradient

TIMELY replaces ECN with RTT. Each ACK carries a hardware timestamp; the sender computes rtt, maintains rtt_min and a smoothed gradient

gradient = (rtt − prev_rtt) / rtt_min,

and adjusts its rate: negative gradient (queue draining) triggers additive increase, positive gradient triggers multiplicative decrease proportional to the gradient magnitude, with PAT (per-ACK threshold) logic to suppress noise [2]. Queue growth is visible in RTT *before* ECN thresholds trip, so TIMELY reacts earlier than DCQCN.

The price is **measurement fragility**: RTT conflates propagation, serialization, queueing, and host/ACK-path noise, and the scheme demands NIC hardware timestamping [2].

Swift, Google's production delay-based design, keeps the delay signal but targets an explicit **delay target** rather than a gradient [6]. Its per-RTT AIMD loop:

- if rtt < target: cwnd += alpha / cwnd (additive increase);
- else: cwnd *= beta with beta < 1 (multiplicative decrease),

augmented with **pacing under extreme congestion** (RTT far above target triggers paced draining) and careful delayed-ACK handling [6]. Results: near-100 Gb/s per-server goodput at *near-zero loss*, and loss rates at least **10x lower than DCTCP** [6] — evidence that *accurate delay targets beat elaborate multi-phase recovery*.

A minimal Python reference of the Swift-style update clarifies the design's economy:

```python
def swift_update(cwnd, rtt, rtt_min, target, alpha=1.0, beta=0.85):
    """One RTT of Swift-like AIMD delay control."""
    delay = rtt - rtt_min          # queueing delay estimate
    if rtt > 4 * target:           # extreme congestion: pace to drain
        return cwnd * 0.5, True
    if delay < target:
        cwnd += alpha / cwnd        # additive increase
        pacing = False
    else:
        cwnd *= beta                # multiplicative decrease
        pacing = delay > 2 * target
    return max(cwnd, 2.0), pacing
```

### 4.3 HPCC: Precision via In-Network Telemetry

HPCC's insight is that ECN and RTT are both *lossy compressions* of the true bottleneck state, whereas INT delivers the state itself [3]. Each ACK returns per-hop telemetry: queue length q, timestamp t, and transmitted bytes txBytes. From two consecutive ACKs the sender computes, for the bottleneck hop, the utilization

U = (q_cur − q_prev + txBytes) / (B · (t_cur − t_prev)),

where B is link bandwidth, and updates its window once per RTT:

W ← W / max_j U_j + eta,

with eta a small additive term. If U > 1, the link is overdriven and the window shrinks proportionally — in **one step**, not the dozens of AIMD iterations DCQCN needs. If U < 1, spare capacity exists and the window grows to claim it. The algorithm needs only three tuning parameters (for fairness and the additive term), versus DCQCN's half-dozen interacting knobs [3].

Two subtleties matter: **delayed feedback** (INT rides on ACKs that themselves queue, so HPCC caps in-flight bytes to busy links [3]) and **overreaction** (HPCC updates once per RTT on the *maximum* utilization, smoothed with min(q_cur, q_prev) [3]).

Reported gains are the largest in the literature: up to **95% FCT reduction** versus DCQCN and TIMELY under large-scale incast, near-zero standing queues, far fewer PFC pauses [3]. Cost: programmable-switch dataplane support and per-packet telemetry overhead (cut to 1–2 bytes by PINT [4]). A Rust sketch of the core update:

```rust
/// HPCC single-step window update from INT telemetry.
fn hpcc_update(w: f64, u_max: f64, eta: f64, w_min: f64) -> f64 {
    // u_max: maximum link utilization observed across hops this RTT
    let w_new = w / u_max.max(1e-9) + eta;
    w_new.max(w_min)
}
```

The IETF has since standardized HPCC++ (Enhanced HPCC), which generalizes the scheme with congestion-signal (CSIG) carriage and formalizes the u1 = max(qlen/B)/T computation for high-speed networks [8].

### 4.4 IRN: The NIC Redesign That Obviates PFC

IRN ("Revisiting Network Support for RDMA," SIGCOMM 2018) asks the field's most subversive question: *is PFC fundamentally required, or merely an artifact of current NIC design?* [7]. The authors identify three NIC deficiencies that force PFC: (i) **go-back-N loss recovery** — a single lost packet triggers retransmission of the entire window, making any loss catastrophic; (ii) **no BDP-based flow control** — senders blast at line rate regardless of the bandwidth-delay product, guaranteeing buffer overflow under incast; and (iii) **coarse ECN handling**.

IRN's redesign is deliberately modest: selective retransmission instead of go-back-N, a BDP cap on in-flight data per flow, and improved ECN/CNP processing [7]. Results upend a decade of design: **IRN without PFC outperforms RoCE with PFC by 6–83%** at only **3–10% additional NIC resources** [7] — PFC's head-of-line blocking hurts more than occasional loss once the NIC recovers efficiently.

The implication is profound: IRN *jointly* designs loss recovery and congestion control, whereas the DCQCN lineage treats loss as an impossibility PFC must prevent. Receiver-driven schemes extend this philosophy — the receiver, observing incast fan-in directly, assigns rates, a natural fit for RDMA's one-sided WRITE semantics.

### 4.5 Receiver-Driven Control and the PFC Deadlock Problem

Model ports as resources and pause dependencies as wait-for edges: a **cyclic buffer dependency (CBD)** arises when routing or failure-induced detours create a cycle in the channel-dependency graph while PFC holds buffers along it. Dataplane detection schemes [4] are fundamentally *reactive* — the deadlock has already frozen traffic before recovery begins.

> **Theorem:** *No purely end-to-end congestion controller can prevent PFC deadlock, because deadlock is a property of the hop-by-hop buffer dependency graph, invisible to end-to-end signals.* Prevention requires either (a) eliminating hop-by-hop lossless control (IRN), (b) provably acyclic buffer allocation (escape channels, deadlock-free routing), or (c) dataplane deadlock detection with bounded recovery time.

Receiver-driven designs attack the *root cause* — incast overshoot — rather than the symptom. When N senders target one receiver, only the receiver observes N and the true fair share C/N; sender-side schemes must *discover* N through congestion signals, invariably overshooting first. Explicit receiver feedback (credit or rate grants piggybacked on RDMA ACKs/CNPs) converges in roughly one RTT without overshoot, which is why receiver-driven proposals consistently show the lowest PFC-trigger rates in incast benchmarks.

### 5.2 Convergence Analysis

DCQCN's AIMD law converges to fairness slowly because the additive increase step R_AI is deliberately small to avoid oscillation [1]. HPCC's update W ← W/max_j U_j is a contraction when feedback delay is bounded: with U_max > 1 all senders scale down by the *same* factor, preserving shares while eliminating overload in one step [3]. Formally:

> **Theorem:** *Under synchronized per-RTT INT feedback with delay d < RTT and eta sufficiently small, HPCC's window vector converges geometrically to the max-min fair allocation with all bottleneck utilizations ≤ 1.*

Swift's AIMD-on-delay-target inherits classic AIMD fairness (Chiu–Jain) with the delay target playing the role of the capacity signal; pacing under extreme congestion provides a *deterministic* drain mode that AIMD alone lacks, which explains its 10x loss reduction over DCTCP [6]. IRN's contribution is orthogonal: by making loss cheap (selective retransmission) and bounding in-flight data to the BDP, it converts the loss-vs.-PFC tradeoff into a pure congestion-control problem where mild overshoot costs retransmissions rather than deadlock [7].

## 6. Limitations

**DCQCN.** Global synchronization and parameter sensitivity persist; auto-tuning adds its own control loop [4], and RED-marking switches tax brownfield deployment.

**TIMELY / Swift.** Both need *accurate* RTT measurement; virtualization, ACK batching, and noisy hosts corrupt the signal, and Swift's Google success may not transfer to heterogeneous multi-tenant clouds [6]. Delay-based flows also suffer latecomer unfairness against buffer-filling ECN/loss-based flows.

**HPCC.** INT needs dataplane support and per-packet overhead (1–2 bytes with PINT [4]); stale telemetry under delayed ACKs bounds its advantage, and HPCC++ standardization is ongoing [8].

**IRN.** Eliminating PFC means *replacing NICs* — a capital-intensive migration; its evaluation is simulation/testbed-scale rather than hyperscaler-production-scale [7], and interoperation with legacy PFC peers during migration is unsolved.

---

## 7. Conclusion

A decade of RDMA congestion control tells a coherent story. **DCQCN** carried RoCEv2 to hyperscale, but its slow AIMD loop left PFC doing the hard work — badly [1][7]. **TIMELY** and **Swift** showed precise delay measurement beats ECN marks, with Swift proving simplicity plus accurate targets wins [2][6]. **HPCC** collapsed control to a single INT-driven closed-form step, cutting FCTs by up to 95% [3]. **IRN** showed the lossless fabric itself was optional: selective retransmission plus BDP-capped injection runs *better* without PFC [7].

The field is converging on three principles: (i) *telemetry-rich feedback*; (ii) *receiver involvement*; and (iii) *loss tolerance*. Open problems remain: 400/800 GbE scaling, verifiable INT truthfulness, multi-tenant isolation, and the migration path from PFC-based brownfield fabrics to IRN-like loss-tolerant NICs.
