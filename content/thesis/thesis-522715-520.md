# Sublinear Time Spectral Sparsification for Dynamic Graphs via Effective Resistance Sampling, Leverage Scores, and Fully Dynamic Connectivity Oracles

## Abstract
We present a unified framework for sublinear time spectral sparsification in fully dynamic graphs, where edges undergo insertions and deletions under polylogarithmic update time. By connecting effective resistance sampling to statistical leverage scores through the Laplacian pseudoinverse, we show that $O(n \log n / \epsilon^2)$ edges sampled proportional to $w_e R_{\text{eff}}(e)$ suffice to preserve $(1\pm\epsilon)$-spectral approximation with high probability. Our approach integrates dynamic spanning forest maintenance, Johnson-Lindenstrauss sketches for resistance estimation, and offline bucketing by resistance scales. We achieve $\tilde O(n)$ space, $\tilde O(1)$ amortized update time via linear sketches, and $\tilde O(n)$ recovery time using non-backtracking random walks to bound effective resistances. The work bridges Spielman-Srivastava sparsification, Holm-de Lichtenberg-Thorup connectivity, and modern $\ell_2$-heavy hitter recovery.

## 1 Introduction
Spectral sparsification has become a central primitive in *nearly-linear time* graph algorithms, Laplacian solving, and clustering. Given a dense undirected weighted graph $G=(V,E,w)$ with $|V|=n$, $|E|=m$, we seek a sparse $H=(V,\tilde{E},\tilde{w})$ such that for all $x\in\mathbb{R}^V$:

$$(1-\epsilon) x^T L_G x \le x^T L_H x \le (1+\epsilon) x^T L_G x \tag{1}$$

where $L_G = D-W$ is the combinatorial Laplacian. The seminal result of **Spielman and Srivastava [1]** showed that sampling edges with probabilities $p_e \propto w_e R_{\text{eff}}(e)$, where $R_{\text{eff}}(e)$ is effective resistance, yields a $(1\pm\epsilon)$-sparsifier with $O(n\log n/\epsilon^2)$ edges. The quantity $w_e R_{\text{eff}}(e)$ coincides exactly with the *statistical leverage score* of the edge in the vertex-edge incidence matrix.

The *dynamic* setting introduces additional complexity. In the **fully dynamic** model, we support both $\text{Insert}(u,v,w)$ and $\text{Delete}(u,v)$ operations, while in the **dynamic streaming** model we observe a single pass over insertions/deletions and maintain a linear sketch of size $\tilde O(n)$. Recent advances [2][3] have shown that spectral sparsification is possible in $\tilde O(n)$ space and $\tilde O(1)$ update time, but the recovery time was historically $\Omega(n^2)$. The 2019 breakthrough of Kapralov et al. [3] reduced recovery to $\tilde O(n)$ via ball-carving in effective resistance metric.

This thesis synthesizes three threads:

* **Effective resistance sampling** as importance sampling for spectral preservation
* **Leverage scores** as algebraic generalization to arbitrary matrices $A^T A$
* **Fully dynamic connectivity** structures as scaffolding for resistance estimation

> **Theorem 1.1 (Oversampling Theorem):** Let $G=(V,E,w)$ be a graph. If $p'_e \ge w_e R^G(e)$ for all $e$, then $H = \text{SAMPLE}(G,p')$ with $q=C_s t\log t/\epsilon^2$ samples, $t=\sum p'_e$, is a $(1\pm\epsilon)$ spectral sparsifier w.h.p. [1][4].

Our contributions are:

1.  A sublinear-time algorithm for estimating $R_{\text{eff}}$ up to $O(1)$ factor using $O(\log n)$ JL projections of $L^\dagger$ and dynamic low-stretch trees.
2.  A bucketed recovery scheme that partitions vertices by resistance diameter, enabling $\ell_2$-sparse recovery of high-resistance edges.
3.  Integration with Holm-de Lichtenberg-Thorup (HDT) dynamic forest [5] to maintain coarse sparsifiers across updates, yielding fully dynamic $\tilde O(n^{1/2})$-time connectivity queries used for sparsifier validation.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Laplacians, Effective Resistance, and Leverage Scores

For graph $G$, oriented incidence matrix $B\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}$ has $B(e,u)=1$, $B(e,v)=-1$ for $e=(u,v)$. Then $L_G = B^T W B$, $W=\text{diag}(w)$. The effective resistance of $e=(s,t)$ is:

$$R_{\text{eff}}(s,t) = (e_s - e_t)^T L_G^\dagger (e_s - e_t) = b_e^T L_G^\dagger b_e$$

where $L_G^\dagger$ is Moore-Penrose pseudoinverse. Critically,

$$\tau_e = w_e R_{\text{eff}}(e) = w_e b_e^T L_G^\dagger b_e = \|W^{1/2} B L_G^{\dagger/2} e_e\|_2^2$$

which is exactly the leverage score of row $e$ of $\Phi = W^{1/2} B$. Summing over edges, Foster's theorem gives $\sum_e w_e R_{\text{eff}}(e)=n-1$.

*Italicized intuition:* *edges whose removal disconnects electrical flow are precisely those with high leverage; they are structurally non-negotiable.*

### 2.2 Spielman-Srivastava Sampling

The SAMPLE procedure [1]:

* Compute $\tilde{R}_e \approx R_{\text{eff}}(e)$ up to $1\pm 0.1$
* Set $p'_e = w_e \tilde{R}_e$
* Sample $q=O(n\log n/\epsilon^2)$ edges i.i.d. with prob. $p_e = p'_e / t$, reweight to $\tilde{w}=w_e/(q p_e)$

Matrix Chernoff bounds imply $L_H \approx_\epsilon L_G$ [4].

| Property | Cut Sparsifier | Spectral Sparsifier |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Preserves | $x\in\{0,1\}^V$ cuts | $\forall x\in\mathbb{R}^V$ quadratics |
| Sample by | Edge connectivity | $w_e R_{\text{eff}}$ |
| Size | $O(n\log n/\epsilon^2)$ | $O(n\log n/\epsilon^2)$ |
| Dynamic sketch | $\tilde O(n)$ via AGM [6] | $\tilde O(n)$ via $\ell_2$ recovery [2][3] |

### 2.3 Fully Dynamic Connectivity

Holm, de Lichtenberg, and Thorup [5] maintain a spanning forest $F$ of $G$ under insertions/deletions in $O(\log^2 n)$ amortized time. Levels $0..\lfloor\log n\rfloor$ partition non-tree edges; when tree edge $e$ deleted, search level-$i$ edges for replacement. This structure is crucial for maintaining *coarse* sparsifiers that serve as preconditioners for resistance estimation.

| Dynamic Model | Space | Update | Query | Recovery |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Insertion-only | $O(n\log n)$ | $O(\log n)$ | $O(\log n)$ | $O(n\log n)$ |
| Dynamic streaming | $\tilde O(n)$ | $\tilde O(1)$ | N/A | $\tilde O(n)$ [3] |
| Fully dynamic offline | $O(m)$ | $O(\log^2 n)$ [5] | $O(\log n/\log\log n)$ | $O(m\log n)$ |
| Sublinear dynamic (this work) | $\tilde O(n)$ | $\tilde O(1)$ amort. | $O(\log n)$ | $\tilde O(n)$ |

## 3 Methodology

Our pipeline consists of four stages executed each batch of $\Theta(n)$ updates, amortized to $\tilde O(1)$ per update.

**Stage A: Coarse sparsifier maintenance.** Maintain $G_c$ with $O(n\log n)$ edges that is $O(1)$-spectral approximation to $G$. Initially compute via low-stretch tree + off-tree sampling [7]. Under updates, use HDT forest to update $G_c$ locally; re-build every $n$ steps.

**Stage B: JL embedding for resistance.** Sample $k=O(\log n/\epsilon^2)$ Gaussian vectors $z_i\sim N(0,I_n)$. Solve $L_{G_c} y_i = z_i$ using nearly-linear Laplacian solver $\tilde O(m\log n)$ [7], but amortized over batch. Then $\tilde{R}_{\text{eff}}(u,v) \approx \|Y(e_u-e_v)\|_2^2$ where $Y$ has rows $y_i^T$.

**Stage C: Bucketing by effective resistance.** Partition $V$ into clusters $C_j$ with resistance diameter $\le \beta^j$ for geometric scales $\beta=2$. Within each cluster, electrical flows between vertices are localized; high-resistance edges crossing clusters are recovered via $\ell_2$-heavy hitters from linear sketch $S B$, $S$ random sign matrix.

**Stage D: Non-backtracking random walk (NBRW) upper bounds.** For edge $e_{p,q}$ inserted, estimate $R_{p,q}\le \min_{P: p\to q}\sum_{r\in P} 1/w_r$ via $s=O(\log n)$ NBRWs without backtrack, emulating DFS. This yields valid overestimates $p'_e\ge w_e R_e$, satisfying oversampling theorem.

*Procedural summary:*

1.  Maintain sketch $\Pi = S W^{1/2} B \in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}$, $d=\tilde O(n)$
2.  On query, solve coarse Laplacian systems to get $Y$
3.  Carve balls in $Y$-metric to form buckets
4.  For each bucket, decode heavy edges from $\Pi$ restricted to bucket
5.  Sample final sparsifier $H$

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Effective Resistance as Leverage and Its Geometry

Leverage scores originally quantify outlier influence in linear regression: $\tau_i = a_i^T (A^T A)^{-1} a_i$. In graphs, $A=W^{1/2}B$, so $\tau_e$ is exactly $w_e R_{\text{eff}}(e)$. This connection yields two insights.

*First*, $\sum\tau_e = \text{rank}(A)=n-1$, so average leverage is $O(n/m)$, implying most edges in dense graphs are irrelevant spectrally. *Second*, JL sketches preserve $\|L^{\dagger/2} b_e\|$ simultaneously for all $e$, giving fast leverage estimation without $m$ solves.

> **Theorem 4.1 (JL Resistance Approximation):** Let $Q\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times n}$ with $k=O(\log n/\delta^2)$ be a JL matrix. With probability $1-1/n$, for all $u,v$, $(1-\delta)R_{\text{eff}}(u,v)\le\|Q L^\dagger(e_u-e_v)\|^2\le(1+\delta)R_{\text{eff}}(u,v)$ when using a $O(1)$-approximate $L_c^\dagger$ instead of $L^\dagger$ [1][8].

*Proof sketch.* $L_c \approx_2 L$ implies $L_c^\dagger\approx_2 L^\dagger$ on $\text{im}(L)$. JL preserves norms of $k$-dimensional projections of $n$ vectors. Union bound over $n^2$ pairs gives result. $\square$

Practical consequence: we need only $O(\log n)$ solves on coarse graph, not original.

### 4.2 Sublinear-Time Sampling via Ball-Carving and $\ell_2$-Heavy Hitters

The bottleneck is recovering edges sampled by $p_e$. Naive enumeration scans $m=\Omega(n^2)$. Kapralov et al. [3] observe: edges with large $R_{\text{eff}}$ have endpoints close in $Y$-embedding but far electrically? Wait — opposite: large $R_{\text{eff}}$ means endpoints are poorly connected, so they lie in *different* buckets when bucket radius small. Thus algorithm iterates scales $j=0..\log(\Delta)$:

- Carve $V$ into clusters with diameter $O(\beta^j)$ in $Y$-metric using standard ball-growing (Miller et al.).
- For each cluster $C$, consider subgraph $G[C]$ induced. Sketched flow $f_C = \Pi \chi_C$ (characteristic vector) reveals crossing edges with $\|f\|_2$ large.

Formally, define matrix $M^{(j)}$ where $M^{(j)}_{e,C}=1$ if $e$ crosses $C$ at scale $j$ and $R_e\in[\beta^j,\beta^{j+1})$. Then $M^{(j)}$ is $O(1)$-sparse per row; $\ell_2$-heavy hitter recovery finds large entries in $M^{(j)} \Pi^T$ in $\tilde O(n)$ time.

*Complexities:*

- Number of scales: $O(\log n)$
- Buckets per scale: $O(n/\beta^j)$ expected
- Recovery per bucket: $O(|C|\log|C|)$ via sparse-recovery (CountSketch)

Overall $\tilde O(n)$ recovery.

### 4.3 Fully Dynamic Connectivity Oracles and Spectral Maintenance

Fully dynamic connectivity [5] maintains:

- Forest $F$ partitioned into $O(\log n)$ levels
- For each non-tree edge $e$ at level $l(e)$, it covers a cut
- Deletion of tree edge triggers search for replacement at level $\le l(e)$, moving edges down levels if not found

We augment HDT to maintain coarse sparsifier $G_c$:

```python
def update_coarse(Gc, F, e, op):
    if op == 'insert':
        if F.connected(e.u, e.v):
            # off-tree edge, sample with prob ~ stretch
            if random.random() < 1/alpha_stretch(e):
                Gc.add(e)
        else:
            F.link(e)
            Gc.add(e)  # tree edges always kept
    else: # delete
        if e in F:
            repl = F.find_replacement(e)  # level search O(log^2 n)
            if repl:
                F.link(repl)
                Gc.add(repl)
            Gc.remove(e)
        else:
            Gc.remove(e)
    return Gc
```

Amortized $O(\log^2 n)$ per update, but batching $\Theta(n)$ updates amortizes solver calls to $O(1)$.

A second table summarizes tradeoffs of dynamic oracle choices:

| Oracle | Preprocess | Update | Query Conn. | Space | Suitable for |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| HDT [5] | $O(m\log n)$ | $O(\log^2 n)$ amort. | $O(\log n/\log\log n)$ | $O(m)$ | General dynamic |
| ETT + LCT | $O(n)$ | $O(\log n)$ | $O(\log n)$ | $O(n)$ | Forest only |
| Offline dyn. | $O(m\log m)$ | $O(\log n)$ div-conq | $O(\log n)$ | $O(m\log m)$ | Divide-conquer |
| Sketch-based | $\tilde O(n)$ | $\tilde O(1)$ | N/A | $\tilde O(n)$ | Streaming [2][3] |

Integration yields sublinear amortized time because heavy solver work occurs only during rebuilds.

### 4.4 Lower Bounds and Instance Optimality

Any $(1\pm\epsilon)$-spectral sparsifier requires $\Omega(n/\epsilon^2)$ edges (Alon [4] lower bound for expanders). For dynamic setting, $\Omega(n)$ space is necessary: cut sparsifiers encode connectivity, which requires $\Omega(n\log n)$ bits [6]. Sublinear-time *per query* lower bound $\Omega(n)$ follows from needing to output $\Omega(n)$ edges. Our $\tilde O(n)$ recovery is thus optimal up to polylog factors.

## 5 Empirical and Theoretical Validation

### 5.1 Proof of Spectral Guarantee

We prove oversampling suffices.

> **Theorem 5.1 (Spectral Preservation):** With $p'_e\ge w_eR_e$ and $q=C_s t\log t/\epsilon^2$, $t=\sum p'_e$, $H=\text{SAMPLE}(G,p')$ satisfies $(1-\epsilon)L_G\preceq L_H\preceq(1+\epsilon)L_G$ w.p. $1-1/n^2$.

*Proof.* Define $Y_e = \frac{w_e}{p_e} b_e b_e^T$ sampled. Then $\mathbb{E}[\sum_{i=1}^q Y_i]=q L_G$, variance bounded by $\max_e \|L_G^{\dagger/2} Y_e L_G^{\dagger/2}\|\le t$. Matrix Chernoff (Tropp) with intrinsic dimension $n-1$ gives concentration for $q$ as stated. Overestimates only increase $t$ by constant factor, preserving bound [1][4]. $\square$

### 5.2 Complexity Analysis

*Sketch size:* $d=O(\epsilon^{-2} n\log n)$ rows, each $\tilde O(1)$ nonzeros per column via OSNAP, total $\tilde O(n)$ words.

*Update time:* Sketch update $O(\log n)$ per edge insertion/deletion (update $O(\log n)$ sketch entries). Coarse graph maintenance $O(\log^2 n)$ amortized. Solver rebuild $O(n\log n)$ every $n$ updates → $O(\log n)$ amortized.

*Recovery:* $O(\log n)$ scales × $O(n\log n)$ bucketing + $\ell_2$ decoding $\tilde O(n)$.

*Python implementation sketch for leverage scores:*

```haskell
-- Haskell: leverage scores via Laplacian pseudoinverse (conceptual)
type Vertex = Int
type Edge = (Vertex, Vertex, Double)

leverageScores :: Graph -> [Double]
leverageScores g = 
  let l = laplacian g
      ld = pseudoInv l
      score (u,v,w) = w * ((basis u - basis v) `quadForm` ld)
  in map score (edges g)
  where
    quadForm x m = x `dot` (m `mul` x)
    basis i = unitVector n i
```

## 6 Limitations

* **Approximation vs exact:** Our $O(1)$ overestimates inflate $t$ to $O(n\log n)$ worst-case; tight $1+\epsilon$ estimates require more JL dimensions, increasing solver cost.
* **Oblivious adversary:** Linear sketches assume update sequence independent of random coins (oblivious). Adaptive adversary can force $\Omega(n)$ failure by learning sketch kernel [2].
* **Solver precision:** Nearly-linear Laplacian solvers are complex to implement; in practice we use preconditioned conjugate gradient with $10^{-6}$ tolerance, losing theoretical guarantees.
* **Weighted graphs:** Resistance bucketing assumes polynomial weight ratio $w_{\max}/w_{\min}=n^{O(1)}$; exponential ratios require additional scaling.
* **Memory bandwidth:** $\tilde O(n)$ sketch still $>10$GB for $n=10^7$, exceeding GPU HBM; distributed sketch needed.

Future directions:

* Deterministic dynamic sparsifiers via expander decomposition
* Quantum effective resistance estimation via HHL
* Instance-optimal NBRW length tuning via RL

## 7 Conclusion

We have presented a sublinear-time spectral sparsification framework that unifies **effective resistance sampling**, **leverage score** analysis, and **fully dynamic connectivity** oracles. By combining JL-based resistance estimation, ball-carving bucket recovery, and HDT forest maintenance, we achieve $\tilde O(n)$ space, $\tilde O(1)$ update time, and $\tilde O(n)$ recovery time — matching known lower bounds up to polylogs. The core insight, that $w_eR_{\text{eff}}(e)$ simultaneously captures spectral importance, statistical leverage, and cut spanning probability via Kirchhoff's theorem ($P[e\in T]=w_eR_e$ for uniform spanning tree $T$), provides a robust foundation for dynamic graph algorithms.

The implications extend beyond sparsification: our primitives enable *dynamic Laplacian solving*, *approximate all-pairs effective resistance*, and *spectral clustering* under churn. As graphs scale to billions of edges, sublinear spectral primitives will become indispensable.
