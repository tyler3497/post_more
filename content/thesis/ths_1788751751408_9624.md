# Adya Formalism and Serializability Verification for Modern Databases: Elle Anomaly Detection, Cobra Black-Box Checking, and Jepsen Testing of Snapshot Isolation

## Abstract

Modern distributed databases promise transactional semantics at scale, yet the gap between documented isolation guarantees and observed behavior remains stubbornly wide. This thesis develops a unified formal and empirical account of isolation verification, grounded in Atul Adya's dependency-graph formalism from Weak Consistency (MIT, 1999). We reconstruct the Adya formalism — direct serialization graphs, phenomena G0 through G2, and the anti-dependency edge taxonomy — and show how it supplies a common language for two complementary verification paradigms: Elle, the Jepsen project's anomaly detector that checks transactional isolation over client-observable histories [4], and Cobra, a black-box serializability verifier that encodes observed histories into polygraph constraints and discharges them with an SMT solver [5]. We show how Adya's phenomena re-appear in modern harnesses — write skew as G2-item, phantoms as G2-predicate — analyze why snapshot isolation resists black-box characterization, review the NP-hardness barrier, and quantify the trade-offs between Cobra's sound-but-heuristic encoding and Elle's efficient but workload-constrained cycle detection.

## 1 Introduction

When a database vendor claims "serializable" or "snapshot isolation," the claim is a statement about *histories*: the set of interleaved transaction executions the system is willing to produce. In practice, verifying such claims is remarkably difficult. Concurrency control implementations are intricate, distributed systems add clock skew and network partitions to the mix, and isolation levels themselves are notoriously slippery — the ANSI SQL-92 definitions were shown to be ambiguous and incomplete decades ago [3]. Into this landscape, Atul Adya's 1999 doctoral thesis introduced a formalism of unusual clarity: model isolation levels as *prohibited phenomena* in a *direct serialization graph* (DSG), where vertices are transactions and edges are dependencies — write-read (wr), write-write (ww), and the critical read-write anti-dependencies (rw) that capture the subtle anomalies weaker levels permit [1].

This thesis argues that Adya's formalism is not merely historical context but the living theoretical substrate of contemporary isolation verification. Two landmark systems illustrate this. **Elle**, a component of Kyle Kingsbury's Jepsen framework, detects isolation anomalies in client-observable histories using trace reconstruction and cycle detection over Adya-style dependency graphs [4]. **Cobra**, from Tan et al. (OSDI 2020), attacks the strictly harder *black-box* problem — the verifier sees only client-observed reads and writes, with no knowledge of commit ordering — by encoding possible dependency graphs as a *polygraph* and using the Z3 SMT solver to determine whether any acyclic resolution exists [5].

Our contributions are as follows:

1. A self-contained reconstruction of the Adya formalism: the DSG, direct versus indirect dependencies, phenomena G0, G1a–G1c, G2, G-single, and the PL-1/PL-2/PL-3 hierarchy.
2. A formal analysis of how Elle's checker operationalizes Adya's phenomena — in particular, how its append-list workloads make version orders *observable*, collapsing the black-box problem into an efficiently checkable one.
3. A formal analysis of Cobra's polygraph encoding: how constraints encode competing version-order hypotheses, how pruning exploits known-read and read-modify-write structure, and why epochs bound the solver's search.
4. A worked comparison on canonical anomalies (write skew, phantom read, lost update), showing each anomaly's DSG and how Elle and Cobra respectively detect or encode it.
5. An assessment of fundamental limits: NP-hardness of black-box serializability, incompleteness of heuristic pruning, the trust assumptions of Jepsen testing, and the open problem of predicate verification at scale.

The remainder of this thesis is organized as follows. Section 2 surveys background: the ANSI critique, Adya's formalism, snapshot isolation, Jepsen, Elle, and Cobra. Section 3 details our methodology. Section 4 deep-dives into the three pillars — the formalism itself, Elle's checkers, and Cobra's encoding — plus a worked anomaly catalog. Section 5 presents empirical results from published test campaigns and complexity-theoretic proofs. Section 6 discusses limitations, and Section 7 concludes.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The ANSI Critique and the Need for Precision

Berenson, Bernstein, Gray, Melton, O'Neil, and O'Neil demonstrated in their 1995 SIGMOD critique that the ANSI SQL-92 isolation definitions — phrased as prohibitions of "dirty read," "non-repeatable read," and "phantom" — were both ambiguous and insufficient: they permitted executions that were clearly non-serializable while appearing to forbid them, because the definitions were stated in terms of locking behaviors rather than observable phenomena [3]. Adya's thesis answered this critique by defining isolation levels *declaratively*, as sets of prohibited phenomena over histories, in an implementation-independent manner [1].

### 2.2 Adya's Dependency-Graph Formalism

Adya models a *history* H as a partial order of operations grouped into transactions, and constructs a *direct serialization graph* DSG(H) whose vertices are committed transactions and whose edges encode *direct dependencies*:

- **wr (write-read):** T_j reads a version of item x written by T_i (T_i →^wr T_j).
- **ww (write-write):** T_j overwrites a version of x written by T_i (T_i →^ww T_j).
- **rw (read-write, anti-dependency):** T_j overwrites a version of x read by T_i (T_i →^rw T_j).

The **rw** edge is the formalism's masterstroke: it captures conflicts invisible to lock-based intuition, and it is exactly the edge class whose cycles characterize non-serializable executions under snapshot isolation. Adya defines phenomena as patterns in the DSG:

| Phenomenon | Definition | Prohibited at |
|---|---|---|
| **G0** (dirty write) | Cycle of ww edges | PL-1 and above |
| **G1a** (aborted read) | Read of aborted transaction's write | PL-2 and above |
| **G1b** (intermediate read) | Read of non-final write | PL-2 and above |
| **G1c** (circular information flow) | Cycle of wr edges | PL-2 and above |
| **G2-item** | Cycle with ≥1 anti-dependency edge on items | PL-3 |
| **G2-predicate** | Cycle with predicate-based rw edges | PL-3 |
| **G-single** | Read skew / single anti-dependency cycle | Snapshot isolation |

The hierarchy PL-1 ⊆ PL-2 ⊆ PL-3 corresponds, roughly, to read committed ⊆ (snapshot-isolation-adjacent, modulo G-single) ⊆ serializable. Adya's central theorem states that *a history is serializable iff its DSG is acyclic* — reducing isolation checking to cycle detection in a graph, an insight that directly enables both Elle and Cobra [1][2].

### 2.3 Snapshot Isolation

Snapshot isolation (SI), introduced by Berenson et al. and popularized by systems like PostgreSQL and Oracle, gives each transaction a consistent snapshot and applies first-committer-wins on write-write conflicts [3]. Fekete, Liarokapis, O'Neil, O'Neil, and Shasha showed that SI is *not* serializable: the canonical counterexample is **write skew**, where two transactions each read x and y, then write disjoint items based on the reads — a cycle with two rw anti-dependency edges (G2-item) [6]. They further identified precisely which SI workloads are serializable (those avoiding certain "dangerous structures"), a result with direct implications for what Elle and Cobra must detect.

### 2.4 Jepsen, Elle, and Cobra

**Jepsen** (Kingsbury) is a framework for testing distributed systems under faults — network partitions, clock skew, process crashes — by running transactional workloads and checking invariants on the resulting histories [4]. **Elle** is Jepsen's isolation checker: given a history of client operations with *known* values (e.g., append-only lists, counters), it reconstructs version orders and searches for Adya phenomena via cycle detection. **Cobra** (Tan et al., OSDI 2020) removes Elle's key assumption: the verifier observes only reads and writes of opaque values, and must consider *all* possible version orders consistent with observations, encoded as a polygraph and solved with Z3 [5].

## 3 Methodology

Our methodology is comparative-formal: we reconstruct each system's core algorithm from primary sources, re-derive its guarantees in Adya's language, and analyze published empirical results. The procedure has four stages.

1. **Formal reconstruction.** We re-derive Adya's DSG construction, the phenomenon definitions, and the PL hierarchy from the thesis [1] and the accompanying ICDE 2000 paper [2]. We formalize Elle's trace-to-graph mapping (version inference from observed values, anti-dependency inference from read-modify-write patterns) and Cobra's polygraph encoding (vertices = transactions; known edges from observed wr; constraints = disjunctions over competing ww/rw orderings).
2. **Worked anomaly derivations.** For write skew, phantom reads, and lost updates, we construct minimal histories, derive their DSGs by hand, and trace how Elle's checkers and Cobra's encoder respectively handle them. These serve as correctness cross-checks on our reconstruction.
3. **Empirical survey.** We analyze published Jepsen reports (e.g., analyses of distributed databases exhibiting read skew and lost updates under partition) and the Cobra paper's evaluation (verification throughput on histories of thousands of transactions, comparison against a brute-force baseline) [4][5].
4. **Complexity analysis.** We review the NP-hardness argument for black-box serializability checking and examine where Cobra's pruning heuristics recover tractability, and where they sacrifice completeness.

> **Theorem:** (Adya, reconstructed) A history H is serializable (PL-3) if and only if DSG(H) contains no directed cycle. Moreover, H exhibits snapshot isolation anomalies exactly when DSG(H) contains a cycle with at least one rw anti-dependency edge but no ww-only cycle — i.e., phenomenon G2 with G0 absent [1].

This theorem is the pivot on which the entire thesis turns: every checker we study is, at bottom, a cycle-detection procedure over some (possibly hypothesized) DSG.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Direct Serialization Graph, Precisely

A subtlety that rewards attention is Adya's distinction between *direct* and *indirect* dependencies. A direct wr edge T_i →^wr_x T_j holds when T_j reads x_m (the version written by T_i) and *no intervening transaction* wrote x between them in the version order. Indirect dependencies chain direct ones. The DSG contains only direct edges; this minimality is what makes cycle detection correspond exactly to serializability rather than to some coarser property [1][2].

Consider write skew, the signature SI anomaly:

```clojure
;; History H_ws: T1 and T2 each read x=0, y=0, then write disjoint items
;; T1: r1[x=0] r1[y=0] w1[x=10] c1
;; T2: r2[x=0] r2[y=0] w2[y=20] c2
;;
;; DSG(H_ws): T1 ->rw_x T2  (T2 overwrites x=0 which T1 read)
;;            T2 ->rw_y T1  (T1 overwrites y=0 which T2 read)
;; Cycle: T1 -> T2 -> T1 with two anti-dependency edges => G2-item.
```

Neither transaction overwrote the other's write, so first-committer-wins never fires; each transaction's snapshot is individually consistent; yet the combined execution is not equivalent to any serial order. In Adya's terms, SI prohibits G0, G1a–c, and G-single but *permits* G2-item — precisely the gap Fekete et al. characterize [6].

Predicate-based anomalies extend the same machinery. A **phantom** occurs when T1 reads the set of rows matching predicate P, T2 inserts a row matching P, and T1 re-reads: the rw edge is *predicate-based* (T1 →^rw_P T2), giving G2-predicate. Elle's list-append checker detects the item-level analog; full predicate reasoning remains the frontier (see §6).

### 4.2 Elle: Making the Version Order Observable

Elle's key design decision is to *choose workloads that reveal the version order*. The canonical Elle workload is the **append test**: each transaction appends a unique value to a list-valued key and reads the list. Because appends are monotonic and values unique, the observed list order *is* the version order — no guessing required. From this, Elle constructs the DSG directly:

```python
# Simplified Elle-style DSG construction for the append workload
def build_dsg(history):
    dsg = DiGraph()
    for txn in history.committed():
        dsg.add_node(txn.id)
    for key, versions in infer_version_order(history).items():
        # wr edges: reader of version v depends on writer of v
        for (writer, readers) in versions:
            for r in readers:
                dsg.add_edge(writer, r, kind='wr', key=key)
        # ww edges: consecutive writers in version order
        for (w1, w2) in consecutive(versions.writers):
            dsg.add_edge(w1, w2, kind='ww', key=key)
        # rw edges: reader of v_i anti-depends on writer of v_{i+1}
        for i, v in enumerate(versions):
            for r in v.readers:
                dsg.add_edge(r, versions[i+1].writer, kind='rw', key=key)
    return dsg

anomalies = [c for c in simple_cycles(dsg) if has_antidependency(c)]
```

Elle then classifies cycles by Adya phenomenon: cycles of wr edges (G1c), cycles containing rw edges (G2/G-single), and so on. Its **cycle-detection mode** goes further, working over arbitrary key-value histories without append structure, using *trace* information (per-operation invocation/completion intervals) to constrain possible orderings — a middle ground between white-box and black-box [4].

The trade-off is explicit: Elle is *efficient* (polynomial cycle detection) but *workload-constrained* — its strongest guarantees require cooperative data types (lists, counters, sets) whose version orders are observable. On arbitrary workloads it can miss anomalies, i.e., it is sound for detection (reported anomalies are real) but incomplete (absence of reports is not proof of serializability).

### 4.3 Cobra: Black-Box Checking via Polygraphs and SMT

Cobra confronts the problem Elle sidesteps: given a history of opaque reads and writes — where read values reveal only *which* write was read (wr edges are known), but the *version order* among writes is unknown — determine whether *any* acyclic DSG consistent with observations exists. If none exists, the history is definitely non-serializable; if one exists, the database *might* be serializable (the verifier cannot distinguish which version order the database actually used).

The **polygraph** P = (V, E, C) captures this: vertices V are transactions, known edges E are observed wr dependencies, and constraints C are *disjunctions* — for each pair of writes to the same key, either w_i precedes w_j or vice versa, each choice inducing different ww and rw edges. Formally, each constraint is a pair of edge-sets of which exactly one must hold in any compatible DSG. Cobra encodes the polygraph into SMT: boolean variables select constraint branches, and the solver searches for an acyclic selection.

The naive encoding is exponential, so Cobra introduces three pruning techniques [5]:

1. **Known-read pruning.** If T_j reads x from T_i, then chains of observed reads pin down segments of the version order, eliminating constraints outright.
2. **Read-modify-write pruning.** RMW patterns (read x, then write x in the same transaction) force the write to immediately follow the read version, collapsing ambiguity.
3. **Epoch partitioning.** Histories are divided into time epochs; transactions in non-adjacent epochs cannot be concurrent, so constraints between them are dropped. This is sound when epoch boundaries are derived from real-time intervals with sufficient slack.

Even with pruning, Cobra remains a *heuristic*: when the solver times out, the answer is "unknown" rather than "serializable." The evaluation in [5] shows Cobra verifying histories of ~10K transactions against etcd and TiDB, finding real serializability violations that white-box testing missed — while acknowledging exponential worst-case behavior.

### 4.4 Anomaly Catalog: Three Worked Cases

| Anomaly | History sketch | DSG shape | Elle detection | Cobra encoding |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Write skew** (G2-item) | r1[x0]r1[y0]w1[x] ∥ r2[x0]r2[y0]w2[y] | 2-cycle, 2 rw edges | Append/counter checker flags rw-cycle | Constraints on w-order; all resolutions cyclic → violation |
| **Lost update** (G0/G2) | r1[x0]r2[x0]w1[x1]w2[x2] | rw + ww edges | Detected via version-order gap | ww constraint forced; rw edge creates cycle |
| **Phantom** (G2-pred) | r1[P]w2[insert∈P]r1[P] | predicate rw cycle | List-append analog only | Not encodable — predicate reasoning unsupported |

The table's last row is telling: *predicate anomalies are the shared blind spot*. Elle handles them only for specialized data types; Cobra's polygraph is item-based. Full predicate verification — reasoning about arbitrary SQL WHERE clauses — remains open, a point we revisit in §6.

### 4.5 Jepsen in Practice: Faults Meet Formalism

Jepsen's contribution is orthogonal but essential: it generates the *histories* that Elle checks. By injecting network partitions, clock skew, and process pauses during transactional workloads, Jepsen produces precisely the adversarial interleavings where concurrency control bugs surface. Published analyses have documented read skew, lost updates, and fractured reads in production systems — each a concrete Adya phenomenon caught in the wild [4]. The methodology's strength is its *adversarial realism*; its weakness is *coverage*: Jepsen samples histories rather than exhausting them, so a passing run is evidence, not proof. This is why the Elle/Cobra distinction matters: Elle + Jepsen finds bugs efficiently; Cobra-style black-box verification approaches proof, at far greater computational cost.

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 Complexity: Why Black-Box Checking Is Hard

The fundamental result in the literature on black-box serializability is that determining whether an observed history is serializable — with version order unknown — is **NP-hard**, by reduction from problems like feedback arc set over the constraint graph. Intuitively, each pair of un-ordered writes is a binary choice, and choices interact globally through cycle formation. Cobra's SMT encoding is therefore not an implementation convenience but a *necessity*: the problem is inherently combinatorial [5].

Elle avoids the hardness by changing the problem: with cooperative workloads, the version order is *observed*, not hypothesized, reducing checking to polynomial cycle detection. This is the formal content of the completeness-versus-assumption spectrum: *assumptions buy tractability*.

### 5.2 Cobra's Empirical Evaluation (Summary)

Tan et al. evaluate Cobra on histories collected from etcd, TiDB, and other systems, reporting [5]:

- **Throughput:** verification of histories with thousands of transactions in minutes, versus a brute-force baseline that fails to scale past dozens — demonstrating that pruning recovers practical tractability on realistic workloads.
- **Bug-finding:** detection of genuine serializability violations, including anomalies in systems whose developers believed them serializable, validating the black-box approach's real-world value.
- **Solver behavior:** epoch partitioning as the dominant scalability lever; without it, constraint counts explode on long histories.

### 5.3 Jepsen/Elle Empirical Record (Summary)

Kingsbury's published analyses document a long catalog of isolation failures: stale and dirty reads under partition, lost updates under clock skew, and fractured reads in systems claiming snapshot isolation [4]. Elle's contribution is to *classify* these automatically by Adya phenomenon, turning anecdotal bug reports into systematic isolation-level assessments. The pattern across reports is consistent: systems most often fail at exactly the phenomena their claimed level permits vendors to misunderstand — G-single (read skew) under claimed SI, and G2-item under claimed serializability.

### 5.4 Proof Sketch: Cycle Characterization

We sketch Adya's core theorem to make the checkers' soundness explicit.

> **Theorem:** (Adya 1999) For any history H, H is serializable iff DSG(H) is acyclic [1].

*Proof sketch.* (⇒) If H is serializable, some serial history S is equivalent to H. In S, all dependencies point forward in commit order, so any DSG(H) edge is consistent with that order — a cycle would contradict the total order. (⇐) If DSG(H) is acyclic, topologically sort its vertices; the resulting transaction order yields a serial history equivalent to H, since every wr/ww/rw edge is respected by construction. ∎

*Corollary.* Elle's cycle detection is *sound*: any cycle it reports in a correctly constructed DSG witnesses non-serializability. Cobra's verdict of "no acyclic resolution" is likewise sound: it proves *no* consistent DSG is acyclic, hence the observed history cannot be serializable under any version order.

## 6 Limitations

1. **Predicate reasoning.** Neither Elle (in general mode) nor Cobra handles predicate-based phenomena (G2-predicate, phantoms) over arbitrary query predicates. This is arguably the largest gap between theory and practice, since real workloads are predicate-heavy.
2. **Cobra's incompleteness.** Solver timeouts yield "unknown," and epoch pruning rests on real-time assumptions that adversarial clocks could violate. Cobra is therefore a bug-finder with strong guarantees on *positive* verdicts, not a decision procedure.
3. **Elle's workload dependence.** Elle's strongest mode requires cooperative data types; on arbitrary workloads it degrades to heuristic trace analysis. A database could pass Elle's checks on tested workloads while violating serializability on others.
4. **Jepsen's sampling.** Fault-injection testing samples the history space; rare interleavings escape. Formal verification (Cobra) and testing (Jepsen) are complements, not substitutes.
5. **Aborted and intermediate reads.** Real histories include aborts and crashes; mapping G1a/G1b phenomena onto histories with client-observed failures requires careful handling of *pending* transactions, a known source of checker unsoundness if mishandled.
6. **Performance model mismatch.** All three systems verify *safety* (correct histories); none addresses liveness or performance isolation, which matter equally in production.

## 7 Conclusion

This thesis has traced a single formal idea — Adya's direct serialization graph and its phenomenon taxonomy — through three decades of database verification, from a 1999 MIT thesis to the Jepsen project's Elle checker and the SMT-powered Cobra verifier. The unifying lesson is that *isolation verification is cycle detection over hypothesized dependency graphs*, and the differences between systems reduce to *what they assume about the version order*: Elle observes it through cooperative workloads, Cobra hypothesizes it through constraint solving, and Jepsen generates the adversarial histories that make both worthwhile.

The practical upshot is a verification portfolio, not a single tool: run Elle-style checkers continuously for cheap, sound bug detection; deploy Cobra-class verifiers on release candidates for high-assurance black-box auditing; and treat predicate anomalies as the open frontier demanding new formalism. Adya's great insight — that isolation levels are best defined as *prohibited patterns in a graph* — remains the right foundation. The phenomena have not changed; only our instruments for detecting them have improved.
