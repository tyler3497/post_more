# Federated Optimization under Statistical Heterogeneity: FedAvg Divergence, SCAFFOLD Variance Reduction, FedProx, and Communication-Efficient Convergence

## Abstract

Federated learning distributes optimization across a fleet of clients whose local data distributions are typically *non-identically distributed*, a regime known as statistical heterogeneity. The canonical algorithm, Federated Averaging (FedAvg), interleaves multiple local stochastic gradient steps with infrequent server aggregation to amortize communication cost, yet its behavior under heterogeneity is subtle: local objectives pull client iterates toward distinct minimizers, producing a persistent *client drift* that degrades convergence and can even divert FedAvg from the stationary points of the global objective [1, 2]. This thesis surveys the theoretical and algorithmic response to that phenomenon. We derive the mechanics of client drift and FedAvg's tight convergence characterization [2]; analyze SCAFFOLD's control-variate correction as client-variance reduction [2]; present FedProx's proximal regularization and inexact local solves as a remedy for statistical and systems heterogeneity [3]; and examine server-side adaptive optimization and normalized aggregation through the lenses of Reddi et al. [4] and FedNova [8]. We synthesize convergence rates, empirical findings, and communication–computation tradeoffs, and close on the residual gaps — assumption strength, personalization, and beyond-stationary-point analysis.

## 1 Introduction

Federated learning (FL) is a distributed machine-learning paradigm in which many clients — mobile devices, hospitals, or organizations — collaboratively train a shared model under the orchestration of a central server, without ever transmitting their raw data [1, 5]. The optimization problem at its core is deceptively simple:

$$\min_{x \in \mathbb{R}^d} f(x) = \frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m} F_i(x), \qquad F_i(x) = \mathbb{E}_{z \sim \mathcal{D}_i}[f_i(x, z)]$$

where $m$ is the number of clients and $\mathcal{D}_i$ is the local data distribution of client $i$. Two features separate FL from classical distributed optimization and jointly define its difficulty. First, **communication is the binding constraint**: uplink bandwidth is scarce and unreliable, so each bit exchanged must purchase as much optimization progress as possible [6]. Second, the distributions $\mathcal{D}_i$ are typically *heterogeneous* — pathological skews such as "each client holds examples of only two digit classes" are standard benchmarks [1] — meaning local objectives disagree not merely in noise but in their very geometry.

The seminal response to the communication constraint was Federated Averaging (FedAvg), introduced by McMahan et al. [1]. In each round, a subset of clients receives the global model, performs $E$ epochs of local stochastic gradient descent, and returns the updated model; the server averages the returns. This reduces communication rounds by *10–100×* relative to synchronized SGD while preserving accuracy on IID data [1]. Yet the interaction between local steps and heterogeneous objectives introduces a failure mode that the original paper acknowledged only empirically: **client drift**, in which local iterates migrate toward client-specific optima and the averaged update no longer estimates the global gradient [2].

This thesis undertakes a rigorous survey of that phenomenon and the algorithms built to tame it. Our objectives are threefold: (i) to formalize client drift and present the tight convergence characterization of FedAvg under statistical heterogeneity; (ii) to dissect the three principal corrective families — *variance reduction* via SCAFFOLD [2], *proximal stabilization* via FedProx [3], and *server-side adaptivity and normalization* [4, 8]; and (iii) to map the communication-computation frontier they inhabit, from sketched updates [6] to local-SGD theory [9], and to enumerate what the theory still cannot explain.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The federated optimization setting

We distinguish two flavors of FL, following Kairouz et al. [5]. In **cross-device FL**, clients number in the millions, are intermittently available, and only a small fraction participates per round; in **cross-silo FL**, a handful of reliable organizations train jointly. This thesis concerns primarily the cross-device regime, where communication scarcity and heterogeneity are most acute.

Standard assumptions structure the analysis:

- **Smoothness.** Each $F_i$ is $L$-smooth: $\|\nabla F_i(x) - \nabla F_i(y)\| \le L\|x - y\|$.
- **Strong convexity or the Polyak–Łojasiewicz condition**, invoked where noted; non-convex analysis requires only smoothness plus bounded stochastic-gradient variance $\sigma^2$.
- **Dissimilarity.** Heterogeneity is quantified through *bounded gradient dissimilarity*: there exists $B \ge 1$ such that $\frac{1}{m}\sum_i \|\nabla F_i(x)\|^2 \le B^2 \|\nabla f(x)\|^2$ for all $x$ [3]. When $B = 1$ the clients are perfectly homogeneous; larger $B$ marks deeper disagreement. A related quantity, the gradient variance $\zeta^2 = \frac{1}{m}\sum_i\|\nabla F_i(x^\star) - \nabla f(x^\star)\|^2$ at the optimum, recurs in drift analyses.

### 2.2 FedAvg mechanics

FedAvg [1] is parameterized by three quantities: $C$, the fraction of clients sampled per round; $E$, the number of local epochs; and $B$, the local minibatch size. The extreme $E = 1$, $C = 1$ with full-batch gradients reduces to synchronized gradient descent; increasing $E$ amortizes communication over local computation. McMahan et al. demonstrated that $E$ can reach values as large as 100 on IID data without materially harming convergence speed [1], a finding later grounded by Stich's local-SGD theory: communication rounds can be reduced by a factor up to $T^{1/2}$ (with $T$ total steps) while preserving the $O(1/\sqrt{mT})$ rate of minibatch SGD [9].

The averaging step itself is *sample-weighted*: clients with more data receive proportionally larger weight. This choice, innocuous under IID data, becomes load-bearing under heterogeneity — a theme we revisit in Section 4.4.

### 2.3 Early communication-efficiency strategies

Before the drift literature matured, Konečný et al. [6] attacked the problem from the compression side: *structured updates* (learning updates restricted to low-rank or masked subspaces) and *sketched updates* (quantization, random rotations, subsampling applied post hoc), cutting uplink cost by two orders of magnitude on convolutional and recurrent models. These techniques are orthogonal to the number-of-rounds question and compose with the algorithms below.

## 3 Methodology

This thesis is a **theory-driven survey with an empirical anchor**. Our method is to (i) reconstruct the canonical convergence arguments — perturbed-iterate analysis for local SGD [9], control-variate corrections [2], proximal-point envelopes [3], and normalized aggregation [8] — in a unified notation; (ii) extract from each the precise dependence of the rate on heterogeneity parameters ($B$, $\zeta$), local steps ($E$, $\tau_i$), and participation; (iii) tabulate the resulting rates side by side; and (iv) confront the theory with reported empirical behavior, notably the non-IID benchmarks of McMahan et al. [1], the heterogeneous suites of Li et al. [3], and the quadratic case studies of Wang et al. [8].

We deliberately restrict attention to *first-order, synchronous* methods optimizing a single global objective; asynchronous, Byzantine-robust, and personalized variants are acknowledged in Section 6 but lie outside the main arc. All complexity claims are stated in communication rounds unless noted, since rounds — not gradient evaluations — are the scarce resource.

## 4 Deep Dive: From Divergence to Correction

### 4.1 FedAvg under statistical heterogeneity: the anatomy of client drift

Consider two clients with quadratic objectives $F_1(x) = \tfrac{1}{2}\|x - e_1\|^2$ and $F_2(x) = \tfrac{1}{2}\|x - e_2\|^2$, $e_1 \ne e_2$. Each client's local optimum differs; running many local SGD steps from a common initialization drives client 1 toward $e_1$ and client 2 toward $e_2$. Their average drifts away from the global minimizer $\bar{e} = (e_1 + e_2)/2$ whenever the step counts or geometries differ. This is **client drift** in miniature — and Karimireddy et al. [2] prove it is not an artifact of stochasticity or partial participation:

> Theorem: (Client drift is intrinsic to FedAvg.) Even with full-batch gradients and all clients participating in every round, FedAvg suffers a drift term proportional to the heterogeneity: the iterates converge only to a neighborhood of the optimum whose radius scales with $\zeta/\mu$ for $\mu$-strongly convex objectives, and the rate contains an additive penalty that does not vanish as the number of rounds grows unless local steps are annealed.

More sharply, the same authors provide a **tight characterization**: FedAvg attains $O(1/T)$ rates on strongly convex problems, but the constant depends on $B^2$ (the dissimilarity), and a matching lower bound shows this dependence is unavoidable without algorithmic modification [2]. In words: heterogeneity does not merely slow FedAvg — it caps its attainable accuracy.

The statistical story is still bleaker. Su, Xu, and Yang [7] analyze FedAvg in a non-parametric (RKHS) setting and prove that FedAvg and FedProx **fail to reach the stationary points of the global objective even for homogeneous linear regression**, though their limiting points remain unbiased estimators of the underlying truth. Requiring bounded dissimilarity, they argue, is pessimistic: the standard assumption overstates what is needed for estimation consistency, even as it understates the optimization pathology.

Empirically, McMahan et al. already saw the shadow of drift: on pathologically non-IID MNIST partitions, FedAvg needed roughly *an order of magnitude more* communication rounds than on IID partitions to reach the same accuracy, and larger $E$ — helpful on IID data — could hurt [1].

### 4.2 SCAFFOLD: stochastic controlled averaging as client-variance reduction

SCAFFOLD [2] confronts drift directly by estimating it. The algorithm maintains **control variates**: a server variate $c$ approximating the global update direction, and per-client variates $c_i$ approximating each client's direction. During local training, client $i$ corrects its stochastic gradient with $(c - c_i)$:

```python
# SCAFFOLD (Option II) — one communication round
def scaffold_round(x, c, clients):
    updates, c_deltas = [], []
    for i in clients:                       # sampled subset S_t
        y = x
        ci = client_control[i]
        for k in range(K):                   # K local steps
            g = stochastic_gradient(F_i, y)  # local gradient estimate
            y = y - eta_l * (g - ci + c)     # drift-corrected step
        c_new = c - ci + (x - y) / (K * eta_l)  # refresh client variate
        c_deltas.append(c_new - ci)
        client_control[i] = c_new
        updates.append(y)
    x = x + eta_g * mean([u - x for u in updates])  # server step
    c = c + mean(c_deltas)                           # server variate update
    return x, c
```

The correction $(c - c_i)$ is an *estimate of the client drift itself*: it nudges each local step back toward the global direction, analogous to how SVRG/SAGA correct stochastic variance in centralized optimization — hence "client-variance reduction" [2]. The theoretical payoff is decisive:

> Theorem: (SCAFFOLD convergence.) For $\mu$-strongly convex, $L$-smooth objectives, SCAFFOLD with appropriate step sizes converges as $O\!\left(\exp(-\mu R/L) + \sigma^2/(\mu^2 R K S) \right)$ over $R$ communication rounds, where the rate is **independent of the heterogeneity** $\zeta$ and unaffected by client sampling. For quadratics, SCAFFOLD additionally exploits client similarity, requiring fewer rounds when Hessians align — the first result quantifying the usefulness of local steps.

Two properties deserve emphasis. First, the bound contains **no $B^2$ or $\zeta$ penalty**: drift is corrected, not merely tolerated [2]. Second, SCAFFOLD is robust to partial participation because the variates are stateful — a client that misses rounds re-enters with a stale but still informative $c_i$. The price is state: the server stores $c$ and each client stores $c_i$ (doubled communication if variates are transmitted naively, mitigated by transmitting only deltas), and the correction adds one vector subtraction per local step — negligible next to gradient computation.

### 4.3 FedProx: proximal stabilization and inexact local computation

FedProx [3] generalizes FedAvg along two axes simultaneously, addressing *statistical* and *systems* heterogeneity in one framework. Each client approximately minimizes a **proximally regularized** surrogate:

$$\min_w \; h_i(w; w^t) = F_i(w) + \frac{\mu}{2}\|w - w^t\|^2$$

where $w^t$ is the current global model and $\mu \ge 0$ is the proximal coefficient. The proximal term penalizes departure from the global model, directly **bounding client drift by construction**: even under extreme non-IID data, local iterates cannot wander arbitrarily far. When $\mu = 0$, FedProx reduces exactly to FedAvg, making it a strict generalization [3].

The second axis is *inexactness*. Real devices differ in compute power and availability; forcing every client to complete exactly $E$ epochs discards stragglers and wastes work. FedProx instead accepts **$\gamma$-inexact solutions**: client $i$ returns $w_i^{t+1}$ satisfying $\|\nabla h_i(w_i^{t+1}; w^t)\| \le \gamma_i^t \|\nabla h_i(w^t; w^t)\|$, i.e., it reduces the local gradient norm by a client- and round-dependent factor [3]. Stragglers contribute partial progress rather than being dropped — a principled accommodation of systems heterogeneity.

```python
# FedProx — client local solve with proximal term and inexactness
def fedprox_client_update(w_global, F_i, mu, gamma):
    w = w_global.clone()
    grad0 = grad(F_i, w) + mu * (w - w_global)   # grad of h_i at start
    while True:
        g = stochastic_gradient(F_i, w) + mu * (w - w_global)
        w = w - eta * g
        # gamma-inexact stopping: partial progress is acceptable
        if norm(g) <= gamma * norm(grad0):
            break
    return w
```

The theory, stated under bounded dissimilarity $B$, yields $O(1/T)$ convergence for strongly convex objectives with exact solves and $O(1/\sqrt{T})$ for non-convex objectives, with rates that degrade gracefully as inexactness grows [3]. Empirically, the headline result is striking: across a suite of realistic federated datasets with high heterogeneity, FedProx improves absolute test accuracy by **22% on average** over FedAvg, with markedly more stable training curves [3]. The cost is the proximal hyperparameter $\mu$, which must be tuned (the authors suggest sweeping $\{0.001, 0.01, 0.1, 0.5, 1\}$) and interacts with the local learning rate [3].

### 4.4 Server-side adaptivity and the objective-inconsistency correction

Reddi et al. [4] reframe federated optimization as a **two-level procedure**: a *client optimizer* produces local updates, and a *server optimizer* treats the aggregated update as a pseudo-gradient. This decoupling permits federated analogues of adaptive methods — **FedAdam, FedAdagrad, FedYogi** — in which adaptivity lives entirely on the server, avoiding the aggregation of optimizer states across clients (halving per-round communication and client memory relative to client-side adaptivity) [4]. Their analysis establishes convergence for general non-convex objectives under heterogeneity, and experiments show adaptive server optimizers substantially ease the tuning brittleness of FedAvg [4].

A subtler pathology motivates FedNova [8]. When clients perform *different numbers of local steps* $\tau_i$ — inevitable under systems heterogeneity — and the server averages their models with data-proportional weights, the iterates converge to the stationary point of a **surrogate objective** $\tilde{f}(x) = \sum_i w_i(\tau) F_i(x)$ whose weights $w_i(\tau)$ depend on step counts, not data fractions. Wang et al. prove this *objective inconsistency* can place the limit point arbitrarily far from the true optimum, even for quadratics [8]. FedNova's remedy is elegant: clients upload **normalized** updates $(x^t - x_i^{t+1})/\tau_i$, and the server re-aggregates with the correct coefficients, restoring consistency while preserving the fast error convergence of local steps [8]. Notably, FedAvg's sample-weighted averaging combined with heterogeneous $\tau_i$ is precisely the "incorrect" configuration — a finding that overturned prevailing empirical wisdom [8].

### 4.5 The communication–computation frontier

Stepping back, the algorithms above trace a Pareto frontier between **rounds** (communication) and **local work** (computation):

| Approach | Lever | Rounds (strongly convex) | Heterogeneity penalty | Extra cost |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Minibatch SGD | none (baseline) | $O(\kappa \log 1/\epsilon)$ | none | maximal communication |
| Local SGD / FedAvg | many local steps | $O(\kappa \log 1/\epsilon)$ with $T^{1/2}$ fewer rounds [9] | $\Omega(\zeta^2/\mu^2)$ neighborhood [2] | drift |
| SCAFFOLD | control variates | matches FedAvg rounds, better constants | **none** [2] | state + variate communication |
| FedProx | proximal term + inexactness | $O(1/T)$ convex [3] | bounded via $\mu$, $B$-dissimilarity | tuning $\mu$ |
| FedNova | normalized aggregation | as FedAvg, consistent objective [8] | removes $\tau_i$-bias | normalized uploads |
| FedAdam/Yogi | server adaptivity | improved constants, easier tuning [4] | analyzed for non-convex | server state |
| Sketched updates [6] | compression | unchanged | orthogonal | quantization error |

Two orthogonalities matter: **compression** (structured/sketched updates [6]) reduces *bits per round* while local-step methods reduce *round count*, and they compose. **Adaptivity** [4] reduces the *tuning* burden, which in cross-device FL — where re-running sweeps is prohibitively expensive — is arguably as valuable as a better rate.

## 5 Empirical Results and Convergence Proofs

### 5.1 What the experiments report

- **FedAvg baselines.** McMahan et al. [1] established the 10–100× round reduction over FedSGD on IID data across five architectures, but documented the non-IID penalty: pathological label skew multiplied the rounds-to-accuracy by roughly 10×, and increasing $E$ beyond a point degraded non-IID performance.
- **SCAFFOLD.** On the EMNIST and Shakespeare benchmarks with heterogeneous partitions, SCAFFOLD reaches target accuracies in significantly fewer rounds than FedAvg, and — matching theory — its advantage *grows* with heterogeneity; on near-IID partitions the methods are comparable [2]. Client-sampling robustness is confirmed empirically: SCAFFOLD's curves barely move as the participation rate drops.
- **FedProx.** Across federated MNIST, FEMNIST, Shakespeare, and Sent140 variants, FedProx is uniformly at least as good as FedAvg and dramatically better under combined statistical and systems heterogeneity: +22% mean absolute accuracy in the hardest settings, with visibly stabilized training [3].
- **FedNova.** On synthetic quadratics with heterogeneous $\tau_i \in [1, 96]$, FedNova converges to the true optimum while FedAvg and FedProx stagnate at the surrogate's minimizer; with uniform $\tau_i$, FedNova coincides with FedAvg, confirming the normalization is exactly the missing correction [8].
- **Adaptive server optimizers.** FedAdam/FedYogi outperform tuned FedAvg on heterogeneous language-modeling and image tasks, with the gap largest where client learning-rate sensitivity is highest [4].

### 5.2 Rate synthesis

For $\mu$-strongly convex, $L$-smooth objectives ($\kappa = L/\mu$), $R$ rounds, $K$ local steps, and $S$ sampled clients per round, the literature's headline rates are:

> Theorem: (Rate landscape.) Minibatch SGD: $O(\kappa \log(1/\epsilon))$ rounds. Local SGD (Stich [9]): same rate with up to $T^{1/2}$ fewer synchronizations on IID data. FedAvg (Karimireddy et al. [2]): $O(1/(\mu R))$ plus an irreducible $\Omega(\zeta^2/\mu^2)$ drift floor under heterogeneity. SCAFFOLD [2]: $O(\exp(-\mu R/L) + \sigma^2/(\mu^2 RKS))$ — heterogeneity-free. FedProx [3]: $O(1/T)$ convex, $O(1/\sqrt{T})$ non-convex, under $B$-dissimilarity and $\gamma$-inexactness.

The proofs share a skeleton — *perturbed iterate analysis*: track a virtual averaged sequence, bound the deviation of local iterates from it, and show the deviation contracts under the algorithm's correction. SCAFFOLD's innovation is to fold the drift estimate into the perturbation itself; FedProx's is to shrink the perturbation a priori via the proximal penalty; FedNova's is to re-weight the aggregation so the virtual sequence targets the true objective [2, 3, 8].

### 5.3 Beyond stationary points

Su et al. [7] complicate the picture: in the RKHS non-parametric model, FedAvg and FedProx converge to points that are *not* stationary for $f$ — yet these points are unbiased estimators of the ground truth with minimax-optimal estimation error in certain regimes. The lesson is that **optimization inconsistency and statistical efficiency can coexist**: the right metric for FL may be estimation error rather than gradient norm, a reframing the classical analyses miss.

## 6 Limitations

1. **Assumption strength.** Nearly every rate above leans on bounded dissimilarity $B$ or bounded $\zeta$ — quantities that are unverifiable in practice and, per [7], possibly stronger than necessary. Assumption-free or assumption-light analyses remain scarce.
2. **The proximal tax.** FedProx's $\mu$ couples statistical regularization to optimization dynamics; poor choices recover FedAvg's drift or stall progress, and per-dataset tuning undermines FL's "train once" economics [3].
3. **State and memory.** SCAFFOLD's control variates double the communicated state per round in the naive implementation; in cross-device settings with ephemeral clients, variate staleness for rarely-seen clients is poorly understood [2].
4. **Objective inconsistency beyond $\tau_i$.** FedNova fixes step-count heterogeneity, but inconsistent local solvers (mixed SGD, momentum, proximal updates across clients) are only partially covered by its unified framework [8].
5. **Non-convex gaps.** The strongest drift corrections (SCAFFOLD) have their sharpest guarantees in convex settings; non-convex rates exist but are looser, and deep-network practice still relies on heuristics the theory does not fully license [2, 4].
6. **Single global model.** All surveyed methods optimize one $f$; when heterogeneity is extreme, a single model may be the wrong goal entirely — personalization, clustered FL, and multi-task formulations [5] are the natural successors.
7. **Privacy and robustness interplay.** Drift corrections assume honest, non-adversarial updates; their interaction with differential-privacy noise and Byzantine robustness is largely open [5].

## 7 Conclusion

The arc from FedAvg to SCAFFOLD, FedProx, FedNova, and adaptive server optimization is the story of a field learning, theorem by theorem, what *heterogeneity* actually breaks. FedAvg's elegant idea — amortize communication over local computation — survives, but only once its implicit assumption of client interchangeability is dismantled: drift must be **estimated and corrected** (SCAFFOLD [2]), **penalized at the source** (FedProx [3]), or **normalized away at aggregation** (FedNova [8]), while server-side adaptivity [4] tames the tuning fragility heterogeneity induces. The tight lower bounds of Karimireddy et al. [2] and the inconsistency results of Wang et al. [8] and Su et al. [7] are as valuable as the algorithms: they tell us which penalties are intrinsic and which are artifacts of bad aggregation.

What remains is a harder, more interesting problem than the one McMahan et al. posed in 2016 [1]. The next frontier is not merely faster convergence to $x^\star$ under weaker assumptions, but deciding *what to converge to* when one model cannot serve all clients — and doing so under the same communication budgets that made federated optimization necessary in the first place.
