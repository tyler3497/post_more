# The Brunn–Minkowski Inequality: Concavity of Volume, Functional Liftings, and the Geometry of Optimal Transport

## Abstract

The Brunn–Minkowski inequality asserts that n-dimensional volume, raised to the power 1/n, is a concave function on the semigroup of convex bodies under Minkowski addition: for nonempty compact convex sets K and L in R^n and a weight λ strictly between 0 and 1, the Minkowski combination (1−λ)K + λL satisfies V((1−λ)K + λL)^{1/n} ≥ (1−λ)V(K)^{1/n} + λV(L)^{1/n}. This article develops the inequality from its nineteenth-century origins in the work of Brunn and Minkowski through its modern functional liftings: the Prékopa–Leindler inequality for log-concave functions and the Borell–Brascamp–Lieb inequality for power-concave densities, which recovers both predecessors at the endpoints of its parameter range. We present the optimal-transport proof via the Brenier map and McCann's displacement interpolation, characterize equality through homothety, and discuss quantitative stability in the sense of Figalli, Maggi, and Pratelli. Isoperimetric consequences, Riemannian extensions, and a Monte Carlo numerical verification complete the exposition.

## 1 Introduction

Among the miracles of convex geometry, few are as consequential as the discovery that volume is almost concave. The story begins with Hermann Brunn's 1887 Munich dissertation *Über Ovale und Eiflächen*, which established that cross-sectional area varies concavely along the axis of an ovaloid, and was lifted to full generality by Hermann Minkowski in his *Geometrie der Zahlen* of 1896. Their joint legacy is an inequality that Gardner, in his definitive 2002 survey, called the cornerstone of the Brunn–Minkowski theory [1]:

> **Theorem (Brunn–Minkowski).** Let K and L be nonempty compact convex subsets of R^n and let λ be strictly between 0 and 1. Then
>
> |(1 − λ)K + λL|^{1/n} ≥ (1 − λ)|K|^{1/n} + λ|L|^{1/n},
>
> where |·| denotes n-dimensional Lebesgue measure and (1−λ)K + λL = {(1−λ)x + λy : x ∈ K, y ∈ L} is the Minkowski combination of K and L.

Equivalently, in multiplicative form, |K + L|^{1/n} ≥ |K|^{1/n} + |L|^{1/n}. The inequality is *sharp*: equality holds precisely for homothetic bodies, and it is *robust*: it survives the passage from sets to functions, from Euclidean space to Riemannian manifolds, and from geometry to probability. Its functional shadows — the Prékopa–Leindler and Borell–Brascamp–Lieb inequalities — now underpin concentration of measure, information theory, and the synthetic theory of Ricci curvature.

This article is organized as follows. Section 2 fixes notation and recalls the mixed-volume apparatus in which the inequality naturally lives. Section 3 compares the three great proof paradigms: slicing, functional lifting, and optimal transport. Section 4 develops the deep theory: the two functional liftings, the Brenier–McCann transport picture with its Riemannian extension, and the modern stability theory. Section 5 gives a complete transport proof, derives the classical isoperimetric inequality, and verifies the estimate numerically. Section 6 records the limitations of the theory, and Section 7 concludes.

## 2 Background: Convex Bodies, Support Functions, and Mixed Volumes

> **Definition.** A *convex body* is a compact convex subset of R^n with nonempty interior. For sets A and B, the *Minkowski sum* is A + B = {x + y : x ∈ A, y ∈ B}, and tA = {tx : x ∈ A} for scalars t.

Convexity is preserved under Minkowski addition, and the operation is completely captured by the *support function* h_K(u) = max{⟨x, u⟩ : x ∈ K}, since h_{K+L} = h_K + h_L pointwise. The volume functional interacts with Minkowski addition through the Steiner formula: for a convex body K and the Euclidean unit ball B^n,

|K + tB^n| = Σ_{k=0}^{n} binom(n, k) W_k(K) t^k,  t ≥ 0,

whose coefficients W_k(K) are the *quermassintegrals* of K:

| k | Quermassintegral | Geometric meaning |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | W_0(K) = \|K\| | volume |
| 1 | W_1(K) = S(K)/n | surface area divided by n |
| n−1 | W_{n−1}(K) = ω_n · w̄(K) | mean width, up to constants |
| n | W_n(K) = ω_n | volume of the unit ball |

More generally, the volume of a linear combination λ_1 K_1 + … + λ_m K_m is a homogeneous polynomial in the λ_i whose coefficients are the *mixed volumes* V(K_{i_1}, …, K_{i_n}), symmetric and multilinear in their arguments. The Brunn–Minkowski inequality is equivalent to *Minkowski's first inequality*,

V(K, L)^n ≥ |K|^{n−1} |L|,

which in turn follows from the deeper *Alexandrov–Fenchel inequality* for mixed volumes. Equality in Minkowski's first inequality characterizes homothety, a theme to which we return in Section 4.4. The standard reference for this entire apparatus is Schneider's monograph [2].

A first dividend of the theory is the classical *isoperimetric inequality*. Applying Brunn–Minkowski to K and tB^n, dividing by t, and letting t tend to zero from above differentiates volume into surface area:

S(K) ≥ n ω_n^{1/n} |K|^{(n−1)/n},

with equality only for balls. Thus the ball minimizes surface area at fixed volume — a variational principle whose quantitative refinements drive much of modern geometric analysis.

## 3 Methodology: Three Routes to One Inequality

There are three essentially distinct strategies for proving the Brunn–Minkowski inequality, each illuminating a different facet of the result.

1. **Slicing and induction on dimension.** The one-dimensional case is elementary: for measurable sets A, B ⊂ R, the Minkowski combination (1−λ)A + λB has length at least (1−λ)|A| + λ|B|, since intervals are the extremals and length is additive on disjoint unions. Writing a body as a union of parallel sections and applying Fubini's theorem together with the induction hypothesis lifts the estimate one dimension at a time. This Hadwiger–Ohmann argument is the most classical and requires only measure theory.

2. **Functional lifting.** The Prékopa–Leindler inequality (Section 4.1) implies Brunn–Minkowski by specialization to indicator functions: the geometric inequality is the "zero-temperature" limit of a functional one. This route explains *why* the inequality extends to log-concave measures and underlies its probabilistic applications.

3. **Optimal transport.** The Brenier map furnishes a volume-distortion proof (Section 4.3): pushing the uniform measure on K forward to the uniform measure on L, the interpolating maps (1−t)id + tT land inside (1−t)K + tL, and the concavity of log det converts the change-of-variables formula into the desired estimate. This is the proof we develop in full in Section 5, since it generalizes most powerfully — to Riemannian manifolds [5] and to the stability theory [7].

Each route has its virtues: slicing is elementary, functional lifting is flexible, and transport is geometric. Together they suggest that the Brunn–Minkowski inequality is not an isolated curiosity but a manifestation of a single underlying concavity principle.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Prékopa–Leindler Inequality: Brunn–Minkowski for Functions

Independently proved by Prékopa in 1971 and Leindler in 1972, the Prékopa–Leindler inequality is the functional form of Brunn–Minkowski. A nonnegative function f is *log-concave* if f((1−λ)x + λy) ≥ f(x)^{1−λ} f(y)^λ for all x, y and all λ strictly between 0 and 1.

> **Theorem (Prékopa–Leindler).** Let λ be strictly between 0 and 1, and let f, g, h : R^n → [0, ∞) be integrable with h((1−λ)x + λy) ≥ f(x)^{1−λ} g(y)^λ for all x, y ∈ R^n. Then
>
> ∫_{R^n} h ≥ (∫_{R^n} f)^{1−λ} (∫_{R^n} g)^λ.

*Proof sketch.* The one-dimensional case is proved by monotone rearrangement: normalize ∫f = ∫g = 1, let F and G be the distribution functions, and let T = G^{−1} ∘ F push the density f forward to g. The change of variables z = (1−λ)x + λT(x) satisfies dz ≥ T'(x)^λ dx by weighted arithmetic–geometric mean, while the pushforward identity g(T(x))T'(x) = f(x) converts the integrand: h(z)dz ≥ f(x)dx, and integrating gives ∫h ≥ 1. The general case follows by induction on dimension via Fubini's theorem — a tensorization argument. ∎

Two remarks are in order. First, Brunn–Minkowski follows by taking f, g, h to be (suitable regularizations of) the indicators of K, L, and (1−λ)K + λL. Second, the inequality is equivalent to the statement that marginals of log-concave functions are log-concave — the analytic engine behind the geometry of log-concave probability measures.

### 4.2 The Borell–Brascamp–Lieb Inequality: A One-Parameter Family

Borell in 1975 [4] and Brascamp and Lieb in 1976 [3] independently discovered a common roof over Brunn–Minkowski and Prékopa–Leindler. For p ≠ 0 and a weight λ strictly between 0 and 1, the *p-mean* of nonnegative numbers a, b is M_{p,λ}(a, b) = (λa^p + (1−λ)b^p)^{1/p}, with the continuous extensions M_{0,λ}(a,b) = a^{1−λ}b^λ, M_{∞,λ}(a,b) = max(a,b), and M_{−∞,λ}(a,b) = min(a,b).

> **Theorem (Borell–Brascamp–Lieb).** Let λ be strictly between 0 and 1, let p ≥ −1/n, and let f, g, h : R^n → [0, ∞) be integrable with h((1−λ)x + λy) ≥ M_{p,λ}(f(x), g(y)) for all x, y. Then
>
> ∫_{R^n} h ≥ M_{q,λ}(∫_{R^n} f, ∫_{R^n} g),  where q = p/(np + 1),
>
> with the endpoint conventions q = −∞ at p = −1/n and q = 1/n at p = ∞.

The parameter p interpolates between the two earlier inequalities: at *p = 0* the hypothesis and conclusion reduce exactly to Prékopa–Leindler, while at *p = −1/n* the theorem is equivalent to Brunn–Minkowski. To see the latter, let A, B have positive finite measures a = |A| and b = |B|, and apply the inequality to the normalized indicators f = a^{−1}1_A and g = b^{−1}1_B. For x ∈ A and y ∈ B, the hypothesis demands h((1−λ)x + λy) ≥ (λa^{1/n} + (1−λ)b^{1/n})^{−n}, so we may take h equal to that constant times the indicator of (1−λ)A + λB. The conclusion, with q = −∞, reads c·|(1−λ)A + λB| ≥ min(1, 1) = 1, which rearranges to

|(1−λ)A + λB| ≥ (λ|A|^{1/n} + (1−λ)|B|^{1/n})^n,

the multiplicative Brunn–Minkowski inequality — now valid for arbitrary measurable sets, no convexity required. Borell's contribution [4] placed this in the wider context of p-concave measures, while Brascamp and Lieb [3] applied it to the diffusion equation. Equality cases were later characterized by Dubuc.

### 4.3 Optimal Transport, the Brenier Map, and Curved Generalizations

The transport proof rests on Brenier's polar factorization theorem [6]: given two probability measures μ, ν on R^n with μ absolutely continuous, there exists a convex function φ, unique up to additive constants, such that T = ∇φ pushes μ forward to ν, and T is the unique optimal map for the quadratic cost. The Monge–Ampère equation det D²φ = (dμ/dν)∘∇φ governs the volume distortion.

McCann's *displacement interpolation* μ_t = ((1−t)id + tT)_# μ_0 then provides a canonical curve of measures joining μ_0 to μ_1, along which many functionals are convex — the *displacement convexity* that launched the synthetic theory of Ricci curvature of Lott, Villani, and Sturm. The Brunn–Minkowski inequality is the special case obtained from uniform measures on convex bodies, as proved in Section 5.

The transport method escapes Euclidean space. Cordero-Erausquin, McCann, and Schmuckenschläger [5] proved a Riemannian Borell–Brascamp–Lieb inequality: on a complete Riemannian manifold of nonnegative Ricci curvature, with the Minkowski combination replaced by the set A_t of t-midpoints of minimizing geodesics from A_0 to A_1, one has |A_t|^{1/n} ≥ (1−t)|A_0|^{1/n} + t|A_1|^{1/n}, the volume distortion along geodesics being controlled by Jacobi fields and hence by the Ricci lower bound. Their work inaugurated the use of optimal transport in comparison geometry and remains the template for functional inequalities on curved spaces.

### 4.4 Equality Cases and Quantitative Stability

For convex bodies of positive volume, equality in the Brunn–Minkowski inequality holds if and only if K and L are *homothetic* — translates of dilates of one another [1]. The natural follow-up question is one of *stability*: if the Brunn–Minkowski deficit

δ(K, L) = |(1−λ)K + λL|^{1/n} − (1−λ)|K|^{1/n} − λ|L|^{1/n}

is small, must K and L be close to homothetic, and at what rate? Figalli, Maggi, and Pratelli [7] gave the first sharp answer for convex sets, proving a *refined* Brunn–Minkowski inequality: the deficit dominates, up to a dimensional constant, the square of the Fraenkel asymmetry measuring distance to the homothety class. The quadratic exponent is best possible, as shown by nearly spherical ellipsoids. Subsequent work of Eldan and Klartag and others has chased the optimal dependence of the constant on dimension — still polynomial rather than exponential in the best known results — and extended stability to the functional inequalities themselves. Stability transforms Brunn–Minkowski from a rigid algebraic identity into a robust analytic tool: near-equality carries geometric information.

## 5 Proofs and Numerical Verification

We now give the complete optimal-transport proof of the multiplicative Brunn–Minkowski inequality, derive the isoperimetric corollary, and check the estimate computationally.

> **Theorem.** Let K, L ⊂ R^n be convex bodies and t strictly between 0 and 1. Then |(1−t)K + tL| ≥ ((1−t)|K|^{1/n} + t|L|^{1/n})^n.

*Proof.* By homogeneity we may assume |K| = |L| = 1, so the claim becomes |(1−t)K + tL| ≥ 1. Let μ_K = 1_K dx and μ_L = 1_L dy be the uniform probability measures, and let T = ∇φ be the Brenier map [6] pushing μ_K forward to μ_L. Set T_t = (1−t)id + tT = ∇((1−t)|x|²/2 + tφ), which is injective almost everywhere as the gradient of a strictly convex function. By change of variables,

|T_t(K)| = ∫_K det(DT_t(x)) dx ≤ |(1−t)K + tL|,

since T_t(x) ∈ (1−t)K + tL for every x ∈ K. Now DT_t = (1−t)I + tD²φ(x), and log det is concave on positive-definite matrices, so

log det(DT_t(x)) ≥ (1−t) log det I + t log det D²φ(x) = t log det D²φ(x),

i.e. det(DT_t(x)) ≥ (det D²φ(x))^t. Because s ↦ s^t is concave on (0, ∞) and dx is a probability measure on K, Jensen's inequality gives

∫_K (det D²φ)^t dx ≥ (∫_K det D²φ dx)^t.

Finally, change of variables along T yields ∫_K det D²φ dx = |T(K)| = |L| = 1. Chaining the estimates, |(1−t)K + tL| ≥ 1, as required. The general case follows by applying this to the rescaled bodies K/|K|^{1/n} and L/|L|^{1/n}. ∎

*Isoperimetric corollary.* Take L = B^n and divide the inequality by s > 0:

(|K + sB^n|^{1/n} − |K|^{1/n}) / s ≥ |B^n|^{1/n}.

Letting s → 0⁺, the left side converges to (1/n)|K|^{1/n − 1} S(K), since the derivative of volume under outer parallel addition is surface area. Rearranging gives S(K) ≥ n ω_n^{1/n} |K|^{(n−1)/n}, with ω_n = |B^n|.

*Numerical verification.* We test the inequality in the plane with K the unit disk and L the square [−1,1]², estimating |(K+L)/2| by Monte Carlo sampling in the bounding box [−2,2]² (a point z belongs to (K+L)/2 exactly when twice z lies within distance 1 of the square):

```python
import random, math
random.seed(20260916)
N = 2_000_000
cnt = 0
for _ in range(N):
    x = random.uniform(-2, 2); y = random.uniform(-2, 2)
    dx = max(abs(2*x) - 1, 0.0); dy = max(abs(2*y) - 1, 0.0)
    if dx*dx + dy*dy <= 1.0:
        cnt += 1
est = 16.0 * cnt / N          # Monte Carlo estimate of |(K+L)/2|
bound = ((math.sqrt(math.pi) + 2) / 2) ** 2   # Brunn-Minkowski lower bound
print(f"estimate {est:.4f}, bound {bound:.4f}, margin {est-bound:.4f}")
# estimate 3.7911, bound 3.5579, margin 0.2333
```

| Quantity | Value |
|---|---|
| Monte Carlo estimate of \|(K+L)/2\| | 3.7911 |
| Exact value, 3 + π/4 | 3.7854 |
| Brunn–Minkowski lower bound | 3.5579 |
| Observed margin | 0.2333 |

The estimate agrees with the exact value 3 + π/4 to three decimals and clears the theoretical lower bound with room to spare — the inequality is comfortably true, and not sharp for this pair, as expected since a disk and a square are far from homothetic.

The p-mean at the heart of the Borell–Brascamp–Lieb inequality is equally easy to probe computationally. In Haskell:

```haskell
-- Weighted p-mean M_{p,lambda}(a, b), with the p = 0 geometric-mean limit
pMean :: Double -> Double -> Double -> Double -> Double
pMean p lambda a b
  | a <= 0 || b <= 0 = 0
  | p == 0           = a ** (1 - lambda) * b ** lambda
  | otherwise        = (lambda * a ** p + (1 - lambda) * b ** p) ** (1 / p)

-- pMean (-1/2) 0.5 4 9 == 5.76  (harmonic-type mean for n = 2)
-- pMean 0      0.5 4 9 == 6.0   (geometric mean: Prekopa-Leindler case)
```

The three inequalities and their precise relationships are summarized below:

| Inequality | Objects | Controlling mean | Recovers |
|---|---|---|---|
| Brunn–Minkowski | sets K, L | volume^{1/n} concave | isoperimetric inequality |
| Prékopa–Leindler | log-concave functions | geometric mean (p = 0) | Brunn–Minkowski via indicators |
| Borell–Brascamp–Lieb | power-concave functions | p-mean, p ≥ −1/n | both, at p = −1/n and p = 0 |

## 6 Limitations

The theory, for all its power, has sharp boundaries worth stating plainly.

- **Convexity and equality.** The inequality itself needs no convexity — the Borell–Brascamp–Lieb route gives it for arbitrary measurable sets — but the elegant equality characterization (homothety) collapses without convexity. For general measurable sets, equality cases are wild and incompletely understood.
- **Dimension dependence.** Quantitative stability estimates carry constants that grow rapidly with n; the best known bounds are polynomial in dimension, and the sharp dimensional dependence remains open. High-dimensional convex geometry still surprises.
- **Beyond Euclidean volume.** The Gaussian Brunn–Minkowski inequality is *false* in general; the Gardner–Zvavitch conjecture that it holds for symmetric convex sets was a driving problem for over a decade, and the measure-theoretic subtleties persist for other densities.
- **Discrete and L^p frontiers.** The integer lattice admits only partial analogues (Freiman-type inverse theorems rather than direct inequalities), and the L^p Brunn–Minkowski theory for p < 1 replaces concavity with delicate quasi-concavity phenomena.
- **Computability.** Volume computation is #P-hard in general, so numerical verification like that in Section 5 is inherently Monte Carlo; rigorous computer-assisted proofs in this area remain rare.

## 7 Conclusion

The Brunn–Minkowski inequality began as a statement about ovals and has become a principle: *volume to the power 1/n is concave*. Its three proofs — by slicing, by functional lifting, and by optimal transport — each opened a research program. The Prékopa–Leindler and Borell–Brascamp–Lieb inequalities translated geometry into analysis; Brenier's map and McCann's interpolation carried it onto curved spaces; and the Figalli–Maggi–Pratelli stability theory turned a rigid identity into a robust estimate. From the isoperimetric problem of antiquity to synthetic Ricci curvature, the inequality endures because it is not really about convex bodies at all — it is about the concavity hidden inside volume itself, a concavity that reappears wherever mass is moved, mixed, or measured.
