# Linear Logic, Session Types, and the Algebra of Deadlock-Free Concurrent Protocols

## Abstract

Concurrent programming remains one of the least disciplined corners of software engineering: processes exchange messages along channels, and nothing in an ordinary type system constrains the *order* or *direction* of those messages. Session types repair this gap by promoting a communication protocol itself to the status of a type. Introduced by Honda in 1993 for the pi-calculus and matured through subtyping, multiparty generalisations, and a profound Curry-Howard correspondence with linear logic, session types now guarantee, by typing alone, that programs are *communication-safe* and, under mild conditions, *deadlock-free*. This thesis reconstructs that arc. We present binary session types with duality, multiparty session types with endpoint projection, the propositions-as-sessions correspondence, embeddings of session-typed concurrency in functional languages (GV in Haskell, Linear Haskell, and Ferrite in Rust), and the progress and preservation metatheory that underwrites the entire enterprise.

---

## 1 Introduction

Consider an SMTP client: it must send `Sender`, then `Recipient`, then `Body`---all strings. A conventional type system approves any ordering, yet sending the recipient before the sender violates the protocol. The failure is not one of *data* types but of *protocol* types: the sequence of communications along a channel has structure, and structure demands a discipline.

Session types, introduced by Honda [1] and refined by Honda, Vasconcelos and Kubo [2] and Gay and Hole [3], supply that discipline. A session type such as

```
!String.!String.!String.end!
```

describes an endpoint that sends three strings and then terminates. Its *dual*, `?String.?String.?String.end!`, describes the matching endpoint. Duality guarantees that two endpoints always agree about who speaks and who listens, and *linearity* guarantees that every endpoint is used exactly once---no message dropped, no channel reused after termination, no aliasing that could desynchronise a protocol.

Why should this matter beyond elegance? Three reasons:

1. **Safety by construction.** Well-typed session programs never exhibit communication errors: no mismatched sends and receives, no orphan messages.
2. **Deadlock-freedom by typing.** In carefully designed calculi, acyclicity of the communication topology follows from the type rules themselves, so deadlock is statically excluded.
3. **A logical foundation.** Wadler's "propositions as sessions" [4] shows that binary session types *are* classical linear logic propositions, session-typed processes *are* proofs, and communication *is* cut elimination. Concurrency finally gets its Curry-Howard correspondence.

The remainder of this thesis proceeds as follows. Section 2 reviews the background: the pi-calculus, binary session types and duality, multiparty session types, and the two flavours of the linear-logic correspondence. Section 3 states our methodology. Section 4 presents a deep dive across four topics: duality and deadlock-freedom, multiparty projection, the session-typed pi-calculus, and embeddings in Haskell and Rust. Section 5 states the key metatheoretic results---progress, preservation, and deadlock-freedom. Section 6 confronts the limitations. Section 7 concludes.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The pi-calculus

The asynchronous pi-calculus is the canonical model of mobile concurrent computation: processes `P, Q` communicate by sending and receiving names along channels, compose in parallel (`P | Q`), replicate (`!P`), and create fresh channels (`(νx)P`). Honda's insight [1] was that typing each channel uniformly---as carrying values of one fixed type---cannot express *structured dialogues*, where the type of the next message depends on the history of the conversation. Session types relax uniformity while keeping structure: a channel's type evolves as the dialogue proceeds.

### 2.2 Binary session types and duality

Binary session types describe a protocol between *two* participants sharing one private channel. The core grammar is:

| Construct | Meaning |
|---|---|
| `!T.S` | send a value of type `T`, then behave as `S` |
| `?T.S` | receive a value of type `T`, then behave as `S` |
| `⊕{li: Si}` | select label `li` (internal choice), continue as `Si` |
| `&{li: Si}` | offer a branch on labels (external choice), continue as `Si` |
| `end!` / `end?` | terminate (no further communication) |
| `μt.S` | recursive session |

*Duality* `S̅` maps every output to an input and every selection to a branch: `!T.S̅ = ?T.S̅`, `⊕{li:Si}̅ = &{li:S̅i}`, and `end!̅ = end?`. The slogan is:

> **Theorem (Duality is agreement).** Two endpoints of a session channel with types `S` and `S̅` can never miscommunicate: whenever one is ready to send, the other is ready to receive, and whenever one selects a label, the other offers it.

Duality is the typing-level encoding of *complementarity* in communication, and it is what makes type checking decidable and compositional: each endpoint is checked independently against its own type, and dual compatibility is guaranteed structurally.

### 2.3 Multiparty session types

Many protocols involve more than two roles---a buyer, a seller, and a bank. Multiparty session types (MPST), introduced by Honda, Yoshida and Carbone [6][8], describe such interactions via a *global type* `G`, which is then *projected* onto each role to obtain a *local type*:

```
G  =  Buyer → Seller : Title(String).
      Seller → Buyer : Price(Int).
      Buyer → Bank   : Pay(Int).end
```

Projection `G ↾ Buyer` yields `Seller?Title(String).Seller?Price(Int).Bank!Pay(Int).end`: each role sees only its own sends and receives. Well-formedness of the global type (linearity, projectability) transfers to the endpoints, giving *communication safety*, *session fidelity*, and---with suitable conditions---*progress* for the whole choreography.

### 2.4 Linear logic and the two correspondences

Two landmark results connected session types to logic. Caires and Pfenning [5] interpreted intuitionistic linear logic propositions as session types, with cut corresponding to parallel composition plus channel restriction. Wadler [4] gave the classical counterpart: the process calculus CP, in which the typing rules *are* the rules of classical linear logic, and reduction *is* cut elimination. The correspondence table is:

| Linear logic | Session type | Process term |
|---|---|---|
| `A ⊗ B` | send `A`, continue as `B` (`!A.B`) | output |
| `A ⅋ B` | receive `A`, continue as `B` (`?A.B`) | input |
| `1` | close (`end!`) | close |
| `⊥` | wait (`end?`) | wait |
| `A ⊕ B` | internal choice | select |
| `A & B` | external choice | branch |
| cut | `(νx)(P \| Q)` | parallel + restriction |

Under this view, deadlock-freedom is not an accident: it is the *cut-elimination theorem* wearing a concurrency costume.

## 3 Methodology

This thesis is a *synthetic* work: rather than proposing a new calculus, it reconstructs the theoretical development of session types from primary sources and evaluates the strength of each metatheoretic claim. Our method is:

1. **Formal reconstruction.** We re-derive the syntax, duality, subtyping, and projection operations from the literature, checking definitions against the published calculi of Honda [1], Gay and Hole [3], Honda et al. [6], and Wadler [4].
2. **Comparative analysis.** We compare the classical (CP) and intuitionistic (Caires-Pfenning) embeddings, and the binary versus multiparty disciplines, tabulating which guarantees each obtains and at what cost.
3. **Implementation survey.** We examine real embeddings---GV in Haskell [9], its operational semantics [10], and Ferrite in Rust [12]---to determine how faithfully the metatheory survives contact with a production type system.
4. **Boundary mapping.** We catalogue exactly where the guarantees fail: shared channels, exceptions, non-termination, and higher-order delegation, following Fowler et al. [11].

Wherever possible, definitions are given in their standard published form so that the reader can verify each claim against the cited sources.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Duality and deadlock-freedom by typing

Duality alone does not prevent deadlock. Consider two sessions `x : !Int.end!` and `y : ?Bool.end?` held by process `P`, with `Q` holding the duals. If `P` sends on `x` then receives on `y`, while `Q` sends on `y` then receives on `x`, each waits for the other: a classic *circular wait*, perfectly dual and perfectly stuck. Duality guarantees agreement *within* each session; deadlock is a *global* phenomenon.

The remedy, pioneered in GV [9][10] and in the hypersequent calculi HCP/HGV [11], is to constrain the *topology* of process configurations by typing. GV's `fork` primitive has type

```haskell
fork :: (S -> IO ()) -> IO S   -- morally: (S ⊸ end!) ⊸ S
```

It creates a channel, spawns the child with one endpoint, and returns the other. Because a freshly forked process connects to the current one by exactly one edge, process structures are *trees*---and trees are acyclic, so no circular wait can ever form. Linearity preserves the tree shape even when channels are delegated (sent over other channels). Hence:

> **Theorem (Deadlock-freedom, GV).** Every well-typed GV configuration either is a value, can reduce, or is blocked only on an external input. No well-typed configuration is deadlocked.

The proof proceeds by showing that the communication graph of a typed configuration is a forest, and that any leaf process with a pending action has its dual action available at the unique neighbour across the connecting channel. Deadlock-freedom is thus a *geometric* corollary of the type rules, not a property checked by a separate analysis.

### 4.2 Multiparty session types and endpoint projection

In MPST, the programmer writes a single global type and the compiler derives each participant's code obligations. Projection is defined by cases; the interesting one is for an interaction `p → q : {li(Ti).Gi}`:

- the *sender* `p` gets `⊕{li : !Ti.(Gi ↾ p)}` (select and send),
- the *receiver* `q` gets `&{li : ?Ti.(Gi ↾ q)}` (branch and receive),
- a *third party* `r` gets the *merge* of all `Gi ↾ r`, which must coincide for projection to be defined.

*Mergeability* is the subtle condition: a bystander must behave identically in every branch, otherwise it could not know which branch was chosen. Scalas and Yoshida's "less is more" reformulation [7] showed that the classical, heavyweight well-formedness conditions could be simplified without losing the metatheory, and their 2019 POPL paper remains the cleanest presentation of projection soundness.

For *asynchronous* MPST [6], messages travel through FIFO queues per ordered pair of roles, and the metatheory must additionally rule out orphan messages and reception errors. The payoff is a single, sweeping result:

> **Theorem (MPST soundness).** If a global type `G` is well-formed and each role `p` is implemented by a process typed at `G ↾ p`, then the network enjoys communication safety, session fidelity, and progress: every sent message is eventually received, and no participant starves.

### 4.3 The session-typed pi-calculus

Session types were born in the pi-calculus [1][2], and the pi-calculus remains their most flexible host. A representative session pi-calculus has processes

```
x!⟨v⟩.P        -- send value v along x, continue as P
x?(y).P        -- receive along x, bind y, continue as P
x ◃ l.P        -- select label l on x
x ▹ {li: Pi}   -- branch on x
a(x).P          -- accept a session on shared name a
ā(x).P         -- request a session on shared name a
```

Session *initiation* uses shared (unrestricted) names `a` to create fresh linear session channels; thereafter all communication on the session is linear. Subtyping [3] adds flexibility: a process offering *more* branches or selecting *fewer* labels is a safe substitute---the familiar co/contra-variance of choice, dualised across endpoints. Gay and Vasconcelos later gave a complete theory for *asynchronous* session pi-calculus with subtyping, showing that the synchronous subtyping relation remains sound under buffered communication.

The session pi-calculus also hosts the sharpest negative results: with unrestricted delegation (sending channels over channels) and shared-channel initiation, deadlock-freedom is *lost* in general, and recovering it requires either the GV-style tree discipline or priority-based analyses that annotate types with communication ordering constraints.

### 4.4 Embedding in functional languages: GV, Linear Haskell, and Rust

The decisive test of a concurrency theory is whether working programmers can use it. Three embeddings stand out.

**GV in Haskell.** Lindley and Morris [9] embed GV in Haskell using a *parameterised higher-order abstract syntax* (HOAS) encoding of the linear lambda calculus, extended with session primitives, plus indexed type families to express linearity constraints. Because Haskell lacks native linear types (at the time), linearity is enforced by the embedding's type-level machinery rather than the host language. Two interpretations are given: one in terms of GHC's concurrency primitives, one in terms of pure continuations. A follow-up paper [10] supplies a small-step operational semantics and proves deadlock-freedom, determinism, and termination for the core calculus.

```haskell
-- GV-style session in Haskell (Lindley–Morris embedding, simplified)
vending :: Session (Offer (Recv ChocolateBar End) (Recv Lollipop End))
vending = offer
  (recv >>= \bar -> closeWith bar)      -- customer chose chocolate
  (recv >>= \pop -> closeWith pop)      -- customer chose lollipop
```

**Linear Haskell.** GHC's `LinearTypes` extension provides multiplicity-polymorphic arrows `a %1 -> b`, allowing linearity to be enforced by the host type checker directly. Session libraries built on linear types represent a channel endpoint as a linear value; the type system then guarantees each endpoint is consumed exactly once, turning protocol violations into ordinary type errors. This is the most ergonomic route for functional programmers, at the cost of trusting the host's linearity story.

**Ferrite in Rust.** Chen and Balzer's Ferrite [12] is a *judgmental embedding*: a well-typed Ferrite program of type `Session<A>` *is* a typing derivation in SILL_R, the intuitionistic session-typed sequent calculus. Because Rust is affine rather than linear, Ferrite encodes the linear context explicitly as a type-level heterogeneous list, with context *lenses* (type-level de Bruijn indices) restoring exchange:

```rust
// Ferrite: hello-world session program (Chen & Balzer)
let hello_provider: Session<ReceiveValue<String, End>> =
    receive_value(|name| {
        println!("Hello, {}", name);
        terminate()
    });
```

Here `Session<ReceiveValue<String, End>>` is the *proposition*; `receive_value` is the *right rule* of SILL_R; and the Rust compiler, checking the program, is checking the *proof*. Ill-typed protocol code is not merely buggy---it is unrepresentable. The Ferrite book and ECOOP 2022 presentation [12] demonstrate shared session types, recursion, and realistic protocols compiled with zero runtime overhead for the discipline itself.

## 5 Empirical Results / Proofs

Session-type metatheory is proved, not benchmarked; the "empirical" content is the set of theorems that have been machine-checked or stress-tested by implementation. The central results, in increasing strength, are:

1. **Type safety (communication safety).** In every calculus surveyed, well-typed processes never reduce to an *error* process---a configuration with mismatched communication actions on the two endpoints of a session. This is proved by the standard subject-reduction argument: typing is preserved by reduction, and typed normal forms are not errors [2][3].

2. **Session fidelity.** Reduction of a typed process *follows* its session type: if `x : !T.S` then the next action on `x` is a send of a `T`, and the continuation is typed at `S`. Fidelity is the precise sense in which the type is a *specification* of behaviour, and it underwrites separate compilation of endpoints.

3. **Progress.** A closed, well-typed configuration is either finished or can take a step. For binary sessions this rules out stuck states arising from protocol mismatch; for MPST it additionally guarantees that every participant can eventually act [6][7].

4. **Deadlock-freedom.** The strongest and most fragile result. GV [10] and HGV [11] obtain it by the tree-structure discipline; Wadler's CP [4] obtains it as a corollary of cut elimination; asynchronous MPST obtains it modulo the absence of orphan messages. The pattern is uniform: *deadlock-freedom holds exactly when the type system controls the communication topology, not merely the per-channel protocol.*

5. **Determinism and termination.** GV's core calculus is confluent and strongly normalising [10]: session-typed functional programs are not only safe but *predictable*. These properties fail the moment general recursion or shared mutable state is admitted, which is why the embeddings isolate the pure session fragment.

The table below summarises which guarantees each system delivers:

| System | Safety | Fidelity | Progress | Deadlock-free | Deterministic |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Session pi-calculus [2] | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ | ✗ (in general) | ✗ |
| CP (Wadler) [4] | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ |
| GV core [10] | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ |
| HGV [11] | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ (+exceptions) | ✓ |
| MPST async [6] | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ (modulo orphans) | ✗ |
| Ferrite/Rust [12] | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ | as SILL_R | ✗ |

These results have been validated in practice: GV's Haskell embedding runs real protocols with no runtime linearity checks, and Ferrite programs are ordinary Rust binaries whose protocol discipline costs nothing at runtime.

## 6 Limitations

The theory's guarantees are real but *conditional*, and the conditions are exactly where engineering intrudes.

- **Shared channels break linearity.** Session initiation in the pi-calculus uses unrestricted names, and shared (modal) session types reintroduce aliasing. Balzer and Pfenning's shared session types recover safety via acquire/release disciplines, but deadlock-freedom requires additional machinery.
- **Exceptions and failure.** Real protocols fail: connections drop, participants crash. Fowler et al.'s HGV [11] shows that hypersequent structure accommodates exceptions (via Girard's Mix rule) without sacrificing deadlock-freedom, but the resulting type system is considerably more complex, and mainstream embeddings have not adopted it.
- **Asynchrony weakens guarantees.** Buffered communication introduces orphan messages and subtle interactions between subtyping and buffering. The asynchronous subtyping theory is sound but incomplete in known formulations, and several plausible-looking rules are in fact unsound.
- **Ergonomics and adoption.** Writing session-typed code in Rust or Haskell demands fluency in type-level programming; error messages describe failed proof search, not protocol violations. Until diagnostics improve, session types will remain a specialist tool despite their strength.
- **Multiparty projection is fragile.** Mergeability and projectability conditions reject some intuitively reasonable global types, and the asynchronous theory's linearity conditions are notoriously delicate [6].

None of these limitations refutes the programme; they delineate the boundary between the *pure* session fragment, where the metatheory is airtight, and the *impure* extensions, where each added feature must re-earn its guarantees.

---

## 7 Conclusion

Session types have travelled, in three decades, from a pi-calculus curiosity [1] to a *logic of concurrency* with the rare distinction of proving, by typing alone, that programs communicate correctly and do not deadlock. The binary theory gives us duality as the static essence of agreement; the multiparty theory gives us projection as the static essence of choreography; the linear-logic correspondence gives us cut elimination as the static essence of communication itself; and the embeddings in Haskell and Rust give us proof that the theory survives contact with real compilers.

The open frontier is not more expressiveness but *robustness under impurity*: exceptions, failure, distribution, and shared state, all without surrendering deadlock-freedom. Hypersequent formulations such as HGV [11] suggest the logical tools exist. If the lambda calculus furnished the foundations of functional programming in the twentieth century, the session-typed process calculus---as Wadler hoped [4]---may yet furnish the foundations of concurrent programming in the twenty-first.
