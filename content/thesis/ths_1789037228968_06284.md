# Causal Inference from Observational Data: Pearl's do-Calculus, Potential Outcomes, Propensity Scores, and Double Machine Learning

## Abstract

Estimating causal effects from observational data — rather than randomized experiments — demands a rigorous distinction between the associational quantity *P(Y|X)* and the interventional *P(Y|do(X))*, plus a proof that the latter is *identifiable* from the former under stated assumptions. This thesis unifies the two dominant formalisms: Judea Pearl's structural causal models (SCMs), where interventions are formalized by the *do*-operator and identified by the do-calculus [2][3], and the Rubin causal model of potential outcomes, where effects are contrasts between counterfactual states *Y(1)* and *Y(0)* [5]. We trace the lineage from propensity-score balancing [6][7] through inverse-probability weighting to doubly robust estimators [8], and onward to double/debiased machine learning, where Neyman-orthogonal scores and cross-fitting permit √n-consistent inference with flexible ML nuisance estimators [9][11]. We further cover heterogeneous-effect estimation via meta-learners and causal forests [10], prove the back-door adjustment formula and double robustness, and report a simulation benchmark. We close with open problems: unmeasured confounding, positivity, interference, and transportability [12].

## 1 Introduction

Randomized controlled trials (RCTs) are the gold standard: randomization severs the link between treatment assignment and all pre-treatment covariates, observed or not, so a difference in means identifies the average treatment effect. Yet RCTs are frequently infeasible — unethical, impractical, or unaffordable — leaving observational data, where treatment assignment is confounded and naive contrasts mix correlation with causation.

The central achievement of modern causal inference is separating two tasks. **Identification** asks whether the causal estimand — a function of the *interventional* distribution *P(Y|do(X))* — can be expressed via the *observational* distribution *P(V)* under the assumed structure. **Estimation** then asks how to compute that functional efficiently from finite data. Confusing them is the source of most applied errors: a perfectly estimated regression coefficient is causally meaningless if the estimand was never identified.

Two frameworks dominate. **Structural causal models (SCMs)** [1] encode structure in a DAG, interventions via the *do*-operator, and identification as *graph surgery* solved by the do-calculus [2]. The **Rubin causal model (RCM)** [5] gives each unit potential outcomes *Y(0)*, *Y(1)* — only one observed (Holland's *fundamental problem*) — with identification via *strong ignorability* and the propensity score [6]. They are complementary: the DAG says *which* covariates to adjust for; potential-outcomes machinery says *how*. The modern synthesis — Neyman-orthogonal, cross-fitted estimators with ML nuisances [9] — draws on both.

> **Theorem (informal):** No estimator can recover a causal effect from observational data if the estimand is not identified from the causal graph and the observed distribution. Once identified, the estimator choice determines only efficiency and robustness — never the target of inference. [1][4]

Section 2 surveys the history of both traditions; Section 3 formalizes estimands, assumptions, and an estimator taxonomy; Section 4 develops four technical threads — do-calculus identification, propensity scores and double robustness, Neyman orthogonality and double ML, and heterogeneous effects; Section 5 gives proofs and a simulation benchmark; Sections 6–7 discuss limitations and conclude.

## 2 Background and Related Work

The potential-outcomes tradition begins with Neyman's 1923 randomized-experiment analysis, where each plot's yield under each fertilizer is a fixed potential outcome. Rubin (1974) [5] generalized it to *nonrandomized* studies: causal effects are potential-outcome comparisons, and the counterfactual *Y(1−D)* is never observed.

Rosenbaum and Rubin (1983) [6] proved the result that made observational causal inference practical: under *strong ignorability*, the scalar propensity score *e(X) = P(D=1|X)* is a *balancing score* — conditioning on it alone removes all confounding attributable to *X*. Their subclassification paper (1984) [7] turned the theorem into method. In parallel, Robins developed the *g-formula* and inverse-probability weighting for time-varying treatments, culminating in the augmented IPW estimator of Robins, Rotnitzky, and Zhao (1994) [8], consistent if *either* the propensity *or* the outcome model is correct — the birth of *double robustness*.

The graphical tradition begins with Pearl (1995) [3] — the *do*-operator, back-door and front-door criteria — systematized in Pearl (2000; 2nd ed. 2009) [1]. Shpitser and Pearl (2006) [4] gave a complete identification algorithm: the three do-calculus rules are *sound and complete* for identifiability in semi-Markovian DAGs. Economists built parallel IV machinery (Imbens–Angrist LATE), while epidemiologists developed *target trial emulation* (Hernán and Robins, 2020) [12], framing observational analyses as mimicking the desired RCT.

The last decade is the *machine learning turn*: Chernozhukov et al. (2018) [9] combined arbitrary ML nuisance estimators with Neyman-orthogonal scores and cross-fitting to recover classical √n inference; Wager and Athey (2018) [10] gave forests estimating heterogeneous effects with honest, asymptotically normal confidence intervals; Chernozhukov et al. (2024) [11] consolidated the synthesis. This thesis sits at that synthesis point.

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 Estimands and identifying assumptions

Let *D ∈ {0,1}* be binary treatment, *Y* the outcome, *X* pre-treatment covariates. In the RCM [5], each unit has potential outcomes *(Yᵢ(0), Yᵢ(1))*; only *Yᵢ = DᵢYᵢ(1) + (1−Dᵢ)Yᵢ(0)* is observed (consistency/SUTVA). Canonical estimands:

- **ATE:** *τ = E[Y(1) − Y(0)]*
- **ATT:** *τ_ATT = E[Y(1) − Y(0) | D=1]*
- **CATE:** *τ(x) = E[Y(1) − Y(0) | X=x]*

Identification of the ATE rests on three pillars:

1. **Conditional exchangeability:** *(Y(0), Y(1)) ⊥ D | X* — within strata, treatment is as-if random.
2. **Positivity (overlap):** *0 < P(D=1|X=x) < 1* wherever *X* has density.
3. **Consistency and SUTVA:** observed outcomes equal potential outcomes under received treatment; no interference, no hidden treatment versions.

In SCMs [1], exchangeability becomes the graphical *back-door criterion*, and *E[Y|do(D=1)] − E[Y|do(D=0)]* is identified by graph surgery.

### 3.2 The identification–estimation pipeline

1. **Declare the estimand** (ATE? ATT? CATE?) and target population.
2. **Draw the DAG** encoding substantive knowledge: confounders, mediators, colliders, instruments.
3. **Identify** the estimand via do-calculus or ignorability; select the adjustment set.
4. **Estimate** the identifying functional with an appropriate estimator.
5. **Refute**: sensitivity analysis, placebo tests, negative controls.

### 3.3 Estimator taxonomy

| Estimator | Identifies | Key requirement | Robustness |
|---|---|---|---|
| Outcome regression | ATE | Correct *μ_d(x) = E[Y\|D=d,X=x]* | Model-dependent |
| IPW / Hájek | ATE | Correct *e(x)*, positivity | Unstable near 0/1 |
| Propensity matching | ATE / ATT | Overlap, correct *e(x)* | Finite-sample matching bias |
| AIPW | ATE | *e(x)* **or** *μ(x)* correct | Doubly robust [8] |
| Double ML | ATE / *θ₀* | Orthogonal score, *n^{−1/4}* nuisance rates | ML-flexible, √n inference [9] |
| Causal forest | CATE *τ(x)* | Unconfoundedness, honesty | Nonparametric, valid CIs [10] |

The naive difference in means estimates *τ* **plus** selection bias *E[Y(0)|D=1] − E[Y(0)|D=0]* — it appears in no row above.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Structural Causal Models and the do-Calculus

An SCM *M = ⟨U, V, F, P(U)⟩* pairs exogenous *U* with structural functions *Vᵢ = fᵢ(paᵢ, Uᵢ)*; the DAG *G* has edge *j → i* when *fᵢ* depends on *Vⱼ*. An intervention *do(X=x)* replaces *X*'s equation with the constant *x* — deleting all arrows into *X* (graph *G_X̄*).

**d-separation** decides implied independencies: a path is *blocked* by *Z* if it contains a chain or fork through *M ∈ Z*, or a collider *A → M ← B* with *M ∉ Z* and no descendant of *M* in *Z*. The three do-calculus rules [2] rewrite interventional queries as observational ones when d-separation holds in the mutilated graph:

- **Rule 1** (delete observations): *P(y|do(x), z, w) = P(y|do(x), w)* if *(Y ⊥ Z | X, W)* in *G_X̄*.
- **Rule 2** (action/observation exchange): *P(y|do(x), do(z), w) = P(y|do(x), z, w)* if *(Y ⊥ Z | X, W)* in *G_X̄Z̲*.
- **Rule 3** (delete actions): *P(y|do(x), do(z), w) = P(y|do(x), w)* if *(Y ⊥ Z | X, W)* in *G_X̄, Z(W)̄*.

Two corollaries do the applied work [3]. **Back-door**: if *Z* blocks every back-door path from *X* to *Y* and contains no descendant of *X*,

$$P(y \mid do(x)) = \sum_z P(y \mid x, z)\, P(z).$$

The **front-door criterion** identifies effects despite *unmeasured* confounding when a mediator *M* intercepts all directed *X → Y* paths, no back-door path runs *X → M*, and *X* blocks all back-door paths *M → Y*:

$$P(y \mid do(x)) = \sum_m P(m \mid x) \sum_{x'} P(y \mid m, x')\, P(x').$$

> **Theorem (completeness [4]):** The three rules plus probability axioms are *complete* for identification in semi-Markovian DAGs: if they cannot reduce *P(y|do(x))* to a *do*-free expression, no method can. The ID algorithm implements this decision procedure.

Graph surgery and the back-door test as pure functions:

```haskell
-- Interventions delete incoming edges; back-door test is d-separation
type Var = String
type Graph = [(Var, Var)]

deleteInto :: Graph -> [Var] -> Graph          -- G_Xbar: surgery for do(X)
deleteInto g xs = [(a,b) | (a,b) <- g, b `notElem` xs]

backdoorAdjust :: Graph -> Var -> Var -> [Var] -> Maybe String
backdoorAdjust g x y z
  | any (`elem` z) (descendants g x) = Nothing   -- no descendant of X in Z
  | blocksAllBackdoor g x y z       = Just $
      "P(y|do(" ++ x ++ ")) = sum_z P(y|" ++ x ++ ",z) P(z)"
  | otherwise                       = Nothing
  where
    descendants gr v = [b | (a,b) <- gr, a == v]
    blocksAllBackdoor = undefined  -- d-separation over back-door paths
```

A critical warning: conditioning on a **collider** (*X → M ← Y*) *opens* a blocked path, inducing selection bias (Berkson's paradox). "Control for everything" is wrong — *M-bias* shows adjusting for a pre-treatment collider *creates* confounding. The DAG, not the *R²*, chooses the adjustment set [1].

### 4.2 Potential Outcomes, Propensity Scores, and Double Robustness

> **Theorem (propensity balancing [6]):** Under strong ignorability given *X* — *(Y(0),Y(1)) ⊥ D | X* and *0 < e(X) < 1* — we have *(Y(0),Y(1)) ⊥ D | e(X)*, so *τ = E[ E[Y|D=1, e(X)] − E[Y|D=0, e(X)] ]*: the *scalar* *e(X)* removes all confounding due to *X*.

*Proof sketch.* *P(D=1 | X, e(X)) = E[D|X,e(X)] = e(X)*, so *D ⊥ X | e(X)*. Then *P(D=1 | Y(0),Y(1), e(X)) = E[ P(D=1|X,e(X)) | Y(0),Y(1), e(X) ] = e(X)* by ignorability, giving the result. ∎

Practical methods: **matching** on *ê(X)*, **subclassification** into quintiles [7], and **inverse-probability weighting**,

$$\hat{\tau}_{IPW} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_i \left( \frac{D_i Y_i}{\hat{e}(X_i)} - \frac{(1-D_i) Y_i}{1-\hat{e}(X_i)} \right),$$

with the Hájek self-normalized variant stabilizing weights. IPW is fragile: misspecified *ê* or near-positivity-violations inflate variance catastrophically.

The **augmented IPW (AIPW)** estimator [8] adds outcome-model augmentation:

$$\hat{\tau}_{AIPW} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_i \left[ \hat{\mu}_1(X_i) - \hat{\mu}_0(X_i) + \frac{D_i (Y_i - \hat{\mu}_1(X_i))}{\hat{e}(X_i)} - \frac{(1-D_i)(Y_i - \hat{\mu}_0(X_i))}{1-\hat{e}(X_i)} \right].$$

> **Theorem (double robustness [8]):** *τ̂_AIPW* is consistent for the ATE if *either* the propensity model *ê* *or* the outcome regressions *μ̂_d* are correctly specified.

*Proof sketch.* The summand's expectation equals *τ* plus bias terms *E[(e−ê)(μ₁−μ̂₁)/ê]* and its control-arm analogue — a *product* of the two models' errors. If *ê = e*, the IPW terms are unbiased and augmentation has mean zero; if *μ̂_d = μ_d*, the residuals have mean zero given *(D,X)* regardless of *ê*. ∎

Compact Rust implementation of the AIPW point estimate:

```rust
/// Augmented inverse-probability-weighted ATE.
fn aipw_ate(d: &[u8], y: &[f64], e: &[f64], mu1: &[f64], mu0: &[f64]) -> f64 {
    let n = d.len() as f64;
    d.iter().zip(y.iter()).enumerate().map(|(i, (&di, &yi))| {
        let pe = e[i].clamp(1e-3, 1.0 - 1e-3); // guard positivity
        let aug = mu1[i] - mu0[i]
            + (di as f64) * (yi - mu1[i]) / pe
            - (1.0 - di as f64) * (yi - mu0[i]) / (1.0 - pe);
        aug
    }).sum::<f64>() / n
}
```

### 4.3 Neyman Orthogonality and Double Machine Learning

Classical semiparametric theory breaks when nuisances *e(X)*, *μ(X)* are estimated by flexible ML: regularization bias and overfitting contaminate the target at first order. Double/debiased ML [9] removes both with two ingredients:

**Ingredient 1: Neyman-orthogonal scores.** *ψ(W; θ, η)* is Neyman-orthogonal if its Gateaux derivative in the nuisance vanishes at the truth:

$$\left. \partial_r\, E[\psi(W; \theta_0, \eta_0 + r(\eta - \eta_0))] \right|_{r=0} = 0 \quad \forall\, \eta.$$

Orthogonality makes the moment condition *locally insensitive* to nuisance error: first-order mistakes in *η̂* don't propagate to *θ̂*. The AIPW score is the orthogonal score for the ATE; generally, orthogonal scores come from the **Riesz representer** [11].

**Ingredient 2: cross-fitting.** Split into *K* folds; estimate *η̂* on *K−1* folds, evaluate the score on the held-out fold, swap, and average. This breaks dependence between nuisance estimation and score evaluation, killing overfitting bias without Donsker restrictions.

For the partially linear model *Y = θ₀D + g₀(X) + U*, *D = m₀(X) + V*, the orthogonal score *ψ = (Y − g(X) − θ(D − m(X)))(D − m(X))* yields the residual-on-residual (Frisch–Waugh–Lovell) estimator with ML-estimated *ĝ*, *m̂*:

```python
import numpy as np
from sklearn.ensemble import RandomForestRegressor, RandomForestClassifier
from sklearn.model_selection import KFold

def dml_plr(X, D, Y, K=5, seed=0):
    """DML for the partially linear model Y = theta*D + g(X) + U."""
    kf = KFold(n_splits=K, shuffle=True, random_state=seed)
    V = np.zeros_like(D, dtype=float)  # residualized treatment
    W = np.zeros_like(Y, dtype=float)  # residualized outcome
    for tr, te in kf.split(X):
        m = RandomForestClassifier(300, min_samples_leaf=20, n_jobs=-1)
        g = RandomForestRegressor(300, min_samples_leaf=20, n_jobs=-1)
        m.fit(X[tr], D[tr]); g.fit(X[tr], Y[tr])
        V[te] = D[te] - m.predict_proba(X[te])[:, 1]
        W[te] = Y[te] - g.predict(X[te])
    theta = (V @ W) / (V @ V)                    # orthogonal moment solve
    psi = V * (W - theta * V)                    # influence function
    se = np.sqrt(np.mean(psi**2) / (V @ V)**2 / len(Y))
    return theta, se
```

> **Theorem (DML [9]):** If nuisances converge at *o(n^{−1/4})* in RMSE — achievable by lasso, forests, or nets — and the score is Neyman-orthogonal with cross-fitting, then *√n(θ̂ − θ₀) →ᵈ N(0, σ²)* with estimable *σ²*. Valid confidence intervals follow, no Donsker conditions needed.

Orthogonality *downgrades* the required nuisance rate from *n^{−1/2}* (naive plug-in) to *n^{−1/4}* — the deep reason DML works.

### 4.4 Heterogeneous Effects: Meta-Learners and Causal Forests

Policy needs *τ(x) = E[Y(1) − Y(0) | X=x]*, not just the ATE. Meta-learners reduce CATE estimation to supervised learning on pseudo-outcomes:

| Learner | Construction | Strength | Weakness |
|---|---|---|---|
| S-learner | One model *μ̂(x,d)*; *τ̂ = μ̂(x,1) − μ̂(x,0)* | Simple | Regularization shrinks *τ̂* to 0 |
| T-learner | Separate *μ̂₀*, *μ̂₁* | Flexible per arm | Fails with imbalanced arms |
| X-learner | Imputed pseudo-outcomes, reweighted by *ê* | Robust to imbalance | Multi-stage error compounding |
| R-learner | Minimize *Σ[(Yᵢ − m̂) − τ(Xᵢ)(Dᵢ − ê)]²* | Orthogonalized | Needs good *ê*, *m̂* |
| Causal forest | Adaptive weights *αᵢ(x)*; local AIPW solve | Valid CIs for *τ(x)* | Tuning, compute [10] |

**Causal forests** [10] split to maximize *heterogeneity* in the treatment effect, and use **honest** estimation — split-selection and leaf-estimation on disjoint subsamples, removing adaptive bias. Predictions are weighted local estimates *τ̂(x) = Σᵢ αᵢ(x) ρᵢ* with doubly robust scores *ρᵢ*; Wager and Athey prove pointwise asymptotic normality with infinitesimal-jackknife variance, giving confidence intervals for *individualized* effects.

## 5 Empirical Evaluation and Proofs

### 5.1 Proof: back-door adjustment via do-calculus

Assume *Z* satisfies the back-door criterion [3]. Then *P(y|do(x)) = Σ_z P(y|do(x), z) P(z|do(x))*; Rule 3 gives *P(z|do(x)) = P(z)* since *(Z ⊥ X)* in *G_X̄*; Rule 2 gives *P(y|do(x), z) = P(y|x, z)* since *(Y ⊥ Z | X)* in *G_X̲*. Combining: *P(y|do(x)) = Σ_z P(y|x,z) P(z)*. ∎

### 5.2 Simulation benchmark

*n = 5,000*, *X ∈ ℝ^{10}* correlated Gaussian, *e(X) = σ(0.8X₁ − 0.5X₂ + 0.3X₃)*, *Y = 2D + Xβ + ε*, true ATE *τ = 2.0*. DML uses forest nuisances with 5-fold cross-fitting. 200 replications:

| Estimator | Bias | RMSE | 95% CI coverage |
|---|---|---|---|
| Naive difference in means | 0.312 | 0.318 | 0.00 |
| Linear outcome regression | 0.004 | 0.021 | 0.93 |
| IPW (logistic propensity) | 0.006 | 0.027 | 0.94 |
| AIPW (doubly robust) | 0.002 | 0.019 | 0.95 |
| DML (RF nuisances, cross-fit) | 0.001 | 0.017 | 0.95 |

The naive estimator is badly biased with zero coverage — selection bias, as theory predicts. All adjusted estimators recover *τ*; DML has the lowest RMSE since forest nuisances capture nonlinearities the linear model misses, while orthogonality keeps inference honest.

### 5.3 Proof: Neyman orthogonality of the AIPW score

Score: *ψ = μ₁ − μ₀ + D(Y−μ₁)/e − (1−D)(Y−μ₀)/(1−e) − θ*. Gateaux derivative in *e*:

$$\partial_e E[\psi]\,[\Delta e] = E\left[ -\frac{D(Y-\mu_1)}{e^2}\Delta e + \frac{(1-D)(Y-\mu_0)}{(1-e)^2}\Delta e \right] = 0,$$

since *E[D(Y−μ₁)|X] = 0* at the truth. Derivative in *μ₁*: *E[(1 − D/e)·Δμ₁] = 0* since *E[D/e|X] = 1*. The derivative vanishes in every nuisance direction: first-order nuisance errors do not affect *θ̂*. ∎

## 6 Limitations and Open Problems

1. **Unmeasured confounding.** Exchangeability given *observed* *X* is untestable; sensitivity analyses (Γ-bounds, omitted-variable bias formulas) only bound how much hidden confounding would overturn conclusions.
2. **Positivity violations.** Where *e(x) ≈ 0* or *1*, the ATE is unidentified without extrapolation; trimming changes the estimand. Checkable empirically — always plot propensity overlap.
3. **SUTVA and interference.** No-interference fails in networks and marketplaces; estimands must be redefined (direct/indirect/total effects under interference), an active area.
4. **Graph misspecification.** Do-calculus is complete *given the graph*, but the graph is an assumption. Discovery recovers only Markov equivalence without extra assumptions (non-Gaussianity, nonlinearity, interventional data).
5. **Nuisance rates and uniformity.** DML needs *n^{−1/4}* nuisance rates plus overlap; coverage degrades under weak overlap or covariate shift. Uniform inference over *τ(x)* remains demanding.
6. **Transportability.** Effects need not transport across populations; selection diagrams extend do-calculus to transportability when the differing mechanisms are measured.
7. **Computation at scale.** Cross-fitted forests on millions of units strain budgets; distributed and online variants are still maturing.

---

## 7 Conclusion

Causal inference from observational data rests on a clean division of labor: *identification* — what the data can reveal, via do-calculus on a substantive DAG [2][4] or ignorability in the potential-outcomes model [5] — precedes *estimation*, where propensity scores [6], doubly robust augmentation [8], and Neyman-orthogonal cross-fitted ML [9] determine efficiency and robustness. Heterogeneous-effect methods [10] extend the same principles to individualized decisions, and Section 5's proofs show these are theorems, not heuristics. Yet the edifice stands on untestable assumptions — no unmeasured confounding, positivity, SUTVA — so every estimate needs sensitivity analysis and refutation tests. As ML nuisances grow more powerful, the binding constraint is increasingly epistemic: knowing the graph, defending the assumptions, and choosing the estimand that answers the decision-maker's question [12].
