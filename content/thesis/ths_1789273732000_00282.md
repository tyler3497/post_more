# Cryo-Electron Microscopy: Single-Particle Reconstruction, the Resolution Revolution, and In Situ Tomography

## Abstract

Cryo-electron microscopy has transformed structural biology from a discipline constrained by crystallization into one that images macromolecules directly in vitreous ice at near-atomic resolution. This thesis reconstructs the technical stack behind the 2017 Nobel Prize to Dubochet, Frank, and Henderson: plunge-freezing vitrification that preserves native conformation; the weak-phase-object theory of image formation and the contrast transfer function that governs phase contrast; direct electron detectors that count individual electrons and enable movie-mode dose fractionation; anisotropic motion-correction algorithms that undo beam-induced blurring; and the Bayesian maximum-likelihood machinery of RELION that aligns, classifies, and reconstructs millions of noisy projections with honest gold-standard resolution estimates. We extend the framework to cryo-electron tomography and subtomogram averaging, where tilt series and FIB-milled lamellae reveal molecular machines in situ, and survey formal results — the projection-slice theorem, the 0.143 FSC criterion, and information-theoretic bounds on angular accuracy — alongside genuine limits: radiation damage, the air-water interface, and the missing wedge.

##1 Introduction

In October 2017, the Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences awarded the Nobel Prize in Chemistry to Jacques Dubochet, Joachim Frank, and Richard Henderson "for developing cryo-electron microscopy for the high-resolution structure determination of biomolecules in solution" [1]. The citation marked a rare moment in which a decades-old technique was recognized precisely at the point it became transformative: between 2012 and 2015, cryo-electron microscopy (cryo-EM) crossed the resolution threshold from "blobology" — evocative low-resolution envelopes — to near-atomic structures rivaling X-ray crystallography, and it did so without requiring crystals at all. Biochemical maps that had long contained blank spaces, in the Nobel Committee's phrasing, began to fill with atomic detail [1].

The stakes of this revolution were not merely aesthetic. Structure underwrites mechanism in molecular biology: the side chains that line a drug-binding pocket, the coordinated ions in a membrane channel, the hinge motions of a spliceosome. X-ray crystallography had provided such detail, but only for molecules that could be coaxed into ordered crystals — a requirement that excludes most membrane proteins, large dynamic complexes, and heterogeneous assemblies. Cryo-EM promised a different bargain: freeze the molecules in a thin film of vitreous ice, image them in their native, solvated state, and reconstruct the three-dimensional structure computationally from noisy two-dimensional projections [1][5].

This thesis reconstructs the full technical stack of that promise: the physics of vitrification and electron image formation; the detector and motion-correction advances that ignited the *resolution revolution*; the Bayesian statistical machinery — principally RELION — that turned noisy images into atomic maps with honest resolution estimates [2]; and the extension of these ideas into three dimensions of the cell itself via cryo-electron tomography (cryo-ET) and subtomogram averaging [8][9][11].

##2 Background

### 2.1 A brief history of seeing the invisible

### 2.2 The long plateau and the revolution

1. **Detectors were lossy.** Photographic film and scintillator-coupled CCD cameras converted each primary electron into photons, adding Landau noise and readout noise, with detective quantum efficiency (DQE) far below unity at high spatial frequencies [4].
2. **The beam moved the sample.** Beam-induced motion — stage drift plus local doming of the ice layer — blurred every exposure, and the blur could not be corrected after the fact from a single integrated image [3][6].
3. **Classification was fragile.** Distinguishing conformational states in overwhelmingly noisy images demanded statistically principled algorithms, which were computationally prohibitive [2].

The trap snapped open almost simultaneously. Direct electron detectors (notably the Gatan K2) enabled *electron counting* with 400 frames-per-second readout, eliminating Landau and readout noise and permitting dose fractionation into movies [4]. Movie-mode alignment algorithms corrected beam-induced motion anisotropically [3][6][7]. And maximum-likelihood, empirically Bayesian reconstruction software — RELION — replaced heuristic refinement with regularized optimization and honest, gold-standard resolution estimates [2]. The first fruits — TRPV1 at 3.4 Å, the archaeal 20S proteasome at ~3.3 Å, and γ-secretase at 3.4 Å — arrived between 2013 and 2015 and demonstrated crystallographic resolution without crystals [4][5].

##3 Methodology

### 3.1 Specimen preparation: vitrification

Purified macromolecules at ~0.1–5 mg/mL are applied to holey-carbon grids (e.g., Quantifoil), blotted to a thin aqueous film, and **plunge-frozen in liquid ethane** cooled by liquid nitrogen. Cooling rates exceeding 10⁵ K/s outrun ice nucleation, yielding vitreous ice 100–300 nm thick with particles suspended in random orientations [1][8]. The air–water interface is the method's silent adversary: a large fraction of particles adsorb to the interface, where surface forces can denature them or trap them in preferred orientations — a problem that motivates surfactant additives, faster freezing, and support films [5].

### 3.2 Data collection: dose fractionation

A 200–300 kV microscope images the grid at low dose, recording each exposure as a *movie* of 20–70 subframes. A typical single-particle dataset comprises 2,000–10,000 movies with a total dose of ~40–60 e⁻/Å², fractionated so that beam-induced motion can be registered and corrected before the frames are summed [3][4]. Modern facilities collect 5,000–10,000 movies per day, and tilt-series for tomography at ±60° in 2–3° increments [13].

### 3.3 The image formation model

A thin biological specimen is, to first order, a **weak-phase object**: it modulates the electron wave's phase far more than its amplitude. The observed image is then the true projected potential convolved with the microscope's point-spread function, whose Fourier transform is the **contrast transfer function (CTF)**. Because contrast transfer oscillates with spatial frequency — the famous Thon rings — no single defocus transmits all frequencies faithfully; datasets therefore span a defocus range (typically 0.5–3 μm), and CTF parameters are estimated per micrograph and corrected during reconstruction [2][5].

The signal is extraordinarily weak: a single particle image has signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) on the order of 0.05–0.1, far below the Rose criterion for detectability (~3–5). Structure emerges only by **averaging over tens of thousands to millions of particles**, with the resolution scaling roughly as the logarithm of the particle number once the data are CTF-corrected and properly aligned [5].

### 3.4 Motion correction

### 3.5 Bayesian alignment and classification: RELION

RELION (REgularised LIkelihood OptimizatioN) treats reconstruction as **maximum *a posteriori* estimation**: it integrates (marginalizes) over the unknown orientation and class assignment of every particle rather than committing to a single best guess, and regularizes the optimization with a prior that the map has limited power at high frequencies [2]. The gold-standard refinement splits particles into two independent half-sets from the start, preventing noise from masquerading as signal; resolution is then read from the Fourier shell correlation (FSC) between half-maps at the 0.143 threshold [2]. Crucially, the same likelihood framework performs *3D classification*, sorting heterogeneous mixtures into compositional and conformational classes — the mechanism by which a single dataset yields multiple functional states of a molecular machine [2][5].

##4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The contrast transfer function and the weak-phase-object approximation

$$\chi(\mathbf{k}) = \pi\,\Delta z\,\lambda\,|\mathbf{k}|^2 - \frac{\pi}{2}\,\lambda^3 C_s\,|\mathbf{k}|^4 + \cdots$$

where $\Delta z$ is defocus, $\lambda$ the electron wavelength, and $C_s$ spherical aberration. The **CTF** is then $\mathrm{CTF}(\mathbf{k}) = -\sin\chi(\mathbf{k})$ (with amplitude-contrast corrections), oscillating between −1 and +1 — which is why raw micrographs show Thon rings in their power spectra, and why zeros of the CTF permanently destroy information at specific frequencies for a given defocus [2][5].

> **Theorem (CTF envelope and information limit):** The recoverable signal at frequency $\mathbf{k}$ is attenuated by multiplicative envelopes $E(\mathbf{k}) = E_s(\mathbf{k})\,E_c(\mathbf{k})\,E_{\mathrm{motion}}(\mathbf{k})\,E_{\mathrm{dose}}(\mathbf{k})$ — spatial and temporal coherence, motion blur, and radiation-damage decay. The achievable resolution is bounded not by the electron wavelength (~2 pm) but by the product of these envelopes; motion correction and dose weighting are therefore resolution-determining operations, not cosmetic ones [3][4].

Dose weighting exploits the fact that radiation damage is frequency-dependent: high-resolution information fades within the first few e⁻/Å², while low-resolution contrast survives the full exposure. The optimal combination down-weights late frames at high frequency:

```python
def dose_weight(frames, dose_per_frame, B_slope=8.0):
    """Frequency-dependent dose weighting (Grant & Grigorieff style)."""
    import numpy as np
    dose = np.cumsum([dose_per_frame] * len(frames))
    k = frequency_grid(frames[0].shape)          # |k| in 1/Angstrom
    weights = np.exp(-0.5 * B_slope * dose[:, None, None] * k[None]**2)
    return sum(f * w for f, w in zip(frames, weights))
```

### 4.2 Direct electron detectors and the dose-fractionation dividend

The Gatan K2 Summit records electrons at 400 internal frames per second and *counts* individual electron events, centroiding each strike to sub-pixel accuracy (super-resolution mode). Counting rejects both Landau noise (statistical variation in energy deposited per electron) and readout noise, so the DQE approaches the theoretical limit — dramatically better than film or CCD at the high frequencies that encode atomic detail [4].

The second dividend is subtler: because the exposure is a movie, motion can be measured *within* the exposure and undone. Rubinstein and Brubaker's alignparts_lmbfgs showed that individual sub-megadalton particle trajectories could be estimated by maximizing the correlation of shifted frames with their sum, with analytic gradients and spatiotemporal smoothness penalties — without assuming linear trajectories [6]. MotionCor2 generalized this to anisotropic patch-wise correction with dose weighting, and Bayesian polishing closed the loop by fitting particle motions against the 3D reference itself [3][7].

A Rust-flavored sketch of the per-frame accumulation at the heart of motion correction:

```rust
// Register each frame against the running reference, then dose-weight.
fn motion_correct_movie(frames: &[Frame], shifts: &[Vec2]) -> Frame {
    let mut sum = Frame::zeros();
    for (f, s) in frames.iter().zip(shifts) {
        let shifted = f.translate(*s);               // sub-pixel shift
        let w = dose_weight_frame(f.accumulated_dose());
        sum.accumulate(&shifted, w);                 // damage-weighted
    }
    sum
}
```

### 4.3 Bayesian inference and the RELION revolution

RELION's objective is the regularized (marginal) likelihood. Given images $X$ and model parameters $\Theta$ (the 3D map, noise model, CTFs), with unknown per-particle orientations $\phi$:

$$P(\Theta \mid X) \;\propto\; P(X \mid \Theta)\,P(\Theta), \qquad P(X \mid \Theta) = \prod_p \sum_\phi P(X_p \mid \phi, \Theta)\,P(\phi)$$

Marginalization over $\phi$ — rather than hard assignment — is the decisive statistical move: at low SNR, committing to a single orientation per particle amplifies noise into spurious "structure" (*Einstein from noise*), whereas integrating over the posterior weights each possibility by its evidence [2]. The prior $P(\Theta)$ suppresses high-frequency power, acting as an adaptive, data-driven regularizer that requires no user-tuned parameters [2].

The *gold-standard* procedure divides the data into two halves refined independently; because the halves share no information, correlation between them can only arise from true signal. Resolution is declared where the FSC falls below 0.143 — the point corresponding, under the Rosenthal–Henderson analysis, to the interpretability threshold of crystallographic maps [2].

A TLA+ sketch of the refinement loop's invariants:

```tla
---- MODULE RelionRefine ----
EXTENDS Naturals
VARIABLES half1, half2, iteration, converged
Init == half1 \in Maps /\ half2 \in Maps /\ iteration = 0 /\ converged = FALSE
RefineStep ==
    /\ ~converged
    /\ half1' = ExpectationMaximization(half1)   \* no cross-talk
    /\ half2' = ExpectationMaximization(half2)   \* between halves
    /\ iteration' = iteration + 1
    /\ converged' = (FSC(half1', half2') < 0.143 -> resolution)
Spec == Init /\ [][RefineStep]_vars
====
```

The same expectation–maximization machinery performs **3D classification**: with $K$ competing maps, the E-step computes each particle's posterior class membership, and heterogeneous datasets resolve into discrete states — e.g., distinct ribosomal conformations or assembly intermediates — without any prior hypothesis about what the states are [2][5].

### 4.4 Cryo-electron tomography and subtomogram averaging

In tomography the projection-slice theorem is the workhorse: the 2D Fourier transform of a projection at tilt angle $\theta$ is a central slice through the 3D Fourier transform of the object at that angle. A tilt series from −60° to +60° thus samples Fourier space densely except for a **missing wedge** — the unsampled double cone around the tilt axis — which elongates reconstructions along the beam direction and must be compensated during alignment by weighting correlations only over mutually sampled regions [8][9].

The cryo-ET/STA workflow proceeds in three stages [8][13]:

1. **Tilt-series processing** — motion correction and CTF estimation per tilt (with defocus varying across the tilted field), tilt-series alignment via fiducials or patch tracking, and tomogram reconstruction by weighted back-projection.
2. **Subtomogram averaging** — particles are picked (increasingly by template matching or neural networks), extracted as 3D subvolumes, and iteratively aligned; the missing wedge is handled by constrained cross-correlation, and classification separates conformational or compositional states [8].
3. **High-resolution refinement** — in the RELION-4.0 Bayesian formulation, the likelihood is evaluated on the original 2D tilt images (*pseudo-subtomograms*), so tilt-series alignment, per-particle beam-induced motion, per-particle defocus, and even higher-order aberrations are refined against the averaged signal, reaching resolutions sufficient for *de novo* atomic modeling in situ [11].

### 4.5 In situ structural biology: the cell as the specimen

The deepest promise of cryo-ET is contextual: structure *where it works*. Focused-ion-beam (FIB) milling carves 100–300 nm lamellae from vitrified cells, exposing organelles and cytosol to the beam; subtomogram averaging then recovers not only structures but their **spatial statistics** — the clustering of nuclear pores, the polysome arrangements on endoplasmic reticulum, the geometry of viral budding sites [9][10][12]. Reviews of the field emphasize that this spatial meta-information — a protein's location, neighbors, and conformational distribution within its native environment — is inaccessible to purified single-particle work and is where the next decade of discoveries will concentrate [10][12].

### 5.2 Formal results

> **Theorem (Projection-slice theorem):** Let $P_\theta$ be the projection of a 3D density $\rho$ along direction $\theta$. Then $\mathcal{F}_2[P_\theta] = \mathcal{F}_3[\rho]\vert_{\Pi_\theta}$, the 2D Fourier transform of the projection equals the central slice of the 3D transform on the plane $\Pi_\theta$ through the origin perpendicular to $\theta$. *Corollary:* infinitely many projections determine $\rho$ exactly; a missing wedge of half-angle $\alpha$ leaves a double cone of Fourier space unconstrained, bounding the $z$-resolution by $\sim 1/\cos\alpha$ relative to in-plane [8][9].

> **Theorem (Gold-standard FSC, Rosenthal–Henderson):** For two independent half-map reconstructions, $\mathrm{FSC}(k) = \frac{\sum_{\lvert\mathbf{k}\rvert=k} F_1(\mathbf{k})F_2^*(\mathbf{k})}{\sqrt{\sum\lvert F_1\rvert^2 \sum\lvert F_2\rvert^2}}$. The resolution at which $\mathrm{FSC} = 0.143$ corresponds to an effective full-dataset SNR of order unity per Fourier shell at the crystallographic figure-of-merit threshold — the point at which an atomic model becomes interpretable [2].

> **Theorem (Angular accuracy, Cramér–Rao sketch):** The variance of any unbiased estimator of a particle's Euler angles is bounded below by the inverse Fisher information, which scales as $1/(N_{\mathrm{eff}}\cdot\mathrm{SNR})$ over the measured Fourier pixels. RELION's marginalization attains near-optimal weighting: particles contribute to the reconstruction in proportion to the sharpness of their orientation posteriors, so that well-determined views dominate and uncertain views are down-weighted rather than misassigned [2].

A compact Haskell expression of the MAP update at the core of every iteration:

```haskell
-- E-step: posterior over orientations; M-step: regularized map update
refineIteration :: Prior -> [ParticleImage] -> Volume -> Volume
refineIteration prior xs v =
    let posteriors = map (\x -> posterior x v) xs          -- integrate over angles
        wtd        = zipWith weightBySharpness xs posteriors
    in mStep prior wtd                                     -- Wiener-filtered update
```

##6 Limitations

1. **Radiation damage is fundamental.** Inelastic scattering breaks bonds and boils off mass; high-resolution information fades within the first few e⁻/Å², and no detector can recover what the beam destroyed. Cryo-EM is therefore a race between damage and signal, won only by averaging [4][5].
2. **The air–water interface.** Most particles encounter the interface during blotting; denaturation, dissociation, and preferred orientation are endemic, and some specimens simply refuse to behave [5].
3. **Size and symmetry floor.** Below ~100 kDa, alignment becomes unreliable as the per-particle SNR vanishes; small asymmetric proteins remain the hardest targets, and flexible regions blur even in otherwise atomic maps [5].
4. **The missing wedge.** Cryo-ET reconstructions are anisotropic by construction; the unsampled wedge distorts membranes and filaments along the beam axis, and quantitative interpretation demands wedge-aware processing [8][9].
5. **Validation pitfalls.** Model bias, overfitting masked as resolution (the "Einstein from noise" pathology), and over-sharpening can produce convincing-looking artifacts. The field's defenses — gold-standard FSC, half-map cross-validation, map-vs-model FSC, and deposition of raw data — are procedural rather than automatic and must be enforced by reviewers [2].
6. **Scale and throughput.** A high-resolution dataset is terabytes of movies; processing is GPU-intensive and expert-labor-intensive, and while pipelines (RELION, cryoSPARC, emClarity) have matured, structure-guided drug design at industrial throughput remains aspirational [11][13].

##7 Conclusion

Cryo-electron microscopy traveled, in four decades, from 7-Å envelopes of a single favorable protein to atomic structures of megadalton machines captured mid-cycle, and from purified specimens to molecular sociology inside intact cells [1][5][10]. The resolution revolution was not one invention but a stack: vitrification preserved the specimen, direct detectors preserved the signal, motion correction preserved the sharpness, and Bayesian inference preserved the honesty of the result [1][2][3][4].

Two frontiers now dominate. The first is **throughput and democratization**: faster detectors, AI-driven particle picking and on-the-fly processing, and robust pipelines that make atomic structure a routine assay rather than a heroic effort [11][13]. The second is the **cell**: cryo-ET with FIB-milled lamellae, subtomogram averaging, and correlative light microscopy are converging on the long-standing dream of visual proteomics — every complex, in place, at near-atomic resolution [10][12]. If the first revolution made the invisible molecule visible, the second will make the living cell legible.
