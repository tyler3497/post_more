# Threshold Schnorr Signatures: FROST Signing Protocols, Distributed Key Generation, and Proactive Secret Sharing

## Abstract

Threshold Schnorr signatures distribute signing authority across a set of parties so that any quorum of size *t* among *n* signers can jointly produce a signature indistinguishable from one created by a single party, while no coalition smaller than *t* can forge. This thesis analyzes **FROST**, the flexible round-optimized Schnorr threshold scheme of Komlo and Goldberg [1], whose two-round signing protocol — and a preprocessed single-round variant — achieve security under concurrent signing sessions without the multi-round overhead of earlier schemes. We formalize the *binding-factor* defense against the concurrent forgery attacks exposed by Drijvers et al., derive the nonce-commitment and share-aggregation equations, and sketch the reduction of unforgeability to the hardness of the discrete logarithm. We then treat the full lifecycle of a threshold key: **Pedersen-style distributed key generation** with Feldman verifiable secret sharing [6], and **proactive secret sharing** refresh in the style of Herzberg, Jarecki, Krawczyk, and Yung [5], which defeats a mobile adversary that compromises servers over time. We contrast FROST's abort-with-identifiable-blame model with the **ROAST** robustness wrapper [3] and with threshold ECDSA constructions such as GG18 [8] and Lindell–Nof [7], and close with empirical costs, standardization status via RFC 9591 [2], and deployment caveats for custodial wallets and blockchain validator sets.

---

## 1 Introduction

Schnorr signatures possess a property that elliptic-curve DSA conspicuously lacks: *linearity*. A Schnorr signature *(R, s)* over message *m* satisfies the equation

$$s \cdot G = R + e \cdot P, \qquad e = H(R \parallel P \parallel m),$$

where *P = x·G* is the public key and *x* the secret. Because both the nonce contribution and the secret contribution enter *linearly*, signatures generated by distinct parties can be added: if each signer *i* produces a partial signature $s_i = d_i + e \cdot x_i$ over a common challenge *e* and a shared nonce commitment $R = \sum R_i$, the sum $s = \sum s_i$ verifies against the *aggregated* public key $P = \sum P_i$. This is the mathematical engine of every Schnorr-based multisignature and threshold-signature scheme [4].

In a **threshold signature scheme**, *n* participants each hold a *share* $x_i$ of a secret key *x*, and any subset of at least *t* participants can cooperate to produce a valid signature under the single public key *P = x·G*, without ever reconstructing *x* on any one machine. The verifier cannot distinguish the result from a single-party signature — a property called *transparency* that is invaluable in cryptocurrency custody, where on-chain footprint and fee cost are identical to a plain signature.

Early Schnorr threshold schemes (e.g., Stinson and Strobl, 2001) required three or more communication rounds and, critically, were shown by Drijvers et al. (Oakland 2019) to be *insecure* when participants sign several messages concurrently — a devastating observation, because real deployments pipeline signing sessions. **FROST** (Flexible Round-Optimized Schnorr Threshold Signatures), introduced by Komlo and Goldberg [1], repairs exactly this gap: it compresses signing to two rounds (or one round with preprocessing) while remaining secure under *unbounded concurrency*, at the price of abandoning *robustness* — the protocol may abort if a signer misbehaves, though the culprit is identified and expelled [1].

This thesis proceeds as follows. Section 2 reviews the cryptographic substrate: Schnorr signatures, Shamir secret sharing, and verifiable secret sharing. Section 3 develops the FROST signing methodology in full algebraic detail. Section 4 deep-dives into four topics: the binding-factor countermeasure, distributed key generation, proactive share refresh, and the ROAST robustness wrapper. Section 5 sketches the unforgeability proof and reports empirical performance. Section 6 enumerates limitations, and Section 7 concludes.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Schnorr Signatures

A Schnorr signature over a cyclic group $\mathbb{G} = \langle G \rangle$ of prime order *q* is the pair $(R, s)$ where $R = k \cdot G$ for a uniformly random nonce $k \in \mathbb{Z}_q$, $e = H(R \parallel P \parallel m)$ is the Fiat–Shamir challenge, and

$$s = k + e \cdot x \pmod q.$$

Verification checks $s \cdot G \stackrel{?}{=} R + e \cdot P$. Security (existential unforgeability under chosen-message attack, EUF-CMA) reduces in the random-oracle model to the hardness of the discrete logarithm problem via the *forking lemma*. BIP-340 [4] standardizes Schnorr signatures over *secp256k1* for Bitcoin with x-only public keys and tagged hashing, and it is this variant that FROST's *secp256k1* ciphersuite targets in RFC 9591 [2].

> **Theorem (Schnorr EUF-CMA, informal):** Under the discrete-logarithm assumption in $\mathbb{G}$ and modeling *H* as a random oracle, no probabilistic polynomial-time adversary can forge a signature on a message it never queried, except with negligible probability.

### 2.2 Shamir Secret Sharing

A dealer wishing to share a secret $x \in \mathbb{Z}_q$ among *n* parties with threshold *t* samples a random polynomial

$$f(z) = x + a_1 z + a_2 z^2 + \cdots + a_{t-1} z^{t-1}$$

and distributes the share $x_i = f(i)$ to participant *i*. Any *t* shares recover *x* by Lagrange interpolation,

$$x = f(0) = \sum_{i \in S} x_i \cdot \lambda_{i,S}, \qquad \lambda_{i,S} = \prod_{j \in S, j \ne i} \frac{j}{j - i},$$

while any *t − 1* shares reveal nothing about *x* (information-theoretically). Threshold signing applies this to the *signing exponent*: if signer *i* contributes $s_i = d_i + e \cdot \lambda_i x_i$ with group nonce $R = \sum_i d_i G$, then $s = \sum_i s_i$ yields $s \cdot G = R + e \cdot xG$, a valid signature under the joint key [1].

### 2.3 Verifiable Secret Sharing and the DKG Problem

Shamir sharing assumes an *honest dealer*. In a **distributed key generation** (DKG) protocol, no single party ever knows *x*; each participant *P_i* instead shares a random polynomial $f_i$ via *verifiable* secret sharing so that others can check consistency of their received shares against public commitments [6]. Feldman's VSS commits to coefficients as $A_{i,k} = a_{i,k} \cdot G$; Pedersen's variant additionally blinds with a second generator for information-theoretic hiding of the coefficients. Gennaro, Jarecki, Krawczyk, and Rabin gave the first provably secure DKG for discrete-logarithm systems, requiring complaints and reconstruction rounds to eject dealers who distribute inconsistent shares [6]. FROST's key-generation phase builds directly on this Pedersen–Feldman lineage.

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 The FROST Signing Protocol

FROST assumes participants already hold Shamir shares $x_i$ of the joint secret *x* with group public key $P = x \cdot G$ and per-participant verification shares $Y_i = x_i \cdot G$. Let $S$ be the designated signer set, $|S| = t$, with Lagrange coefficients $\lambda_i$ for interpolation at zero. Signing proceeds in two rounds plus aggregation:

**Round 1 — commitment.** Each participant $P_i \in S$ samples *two* random nonces $(d_i, e_i) \xleftarrow{\$} \mathbb{Z}_q^2$ and broadcasts the commitment pair

$$(D_i, E_i) = (d_i \cdot G,\; e_i \cdot G).$$

Using two nonces (rather than one) is essential: the second nonce is scaled by the *binding factor* $\rho_i$, neutralizing the attack of Drijvers et al. described in §4.1.

**Round 2 — signature shares.** Upon collecting all commitments, every participant computes the binding factors and the group commitment. With $L = \langle (i, D_i, E_i) \rangle_{i \in S}$ the ordered commitment list,

$$\rho_i = H_1(i \parallel m \parallel L), \qquad R = \sum_{i \in S} (D_i + \rho_i \cdot E_i),$$

$$c = H_2(R \parallel P \parallel m), \qquad z_i = d_i + (e_i \cdot \rho_i) + \lambda_i \cdot x_i \cdot c.$$

Each participant publishes $z_i$ and erases $(d_i, e_i)$.

**Aggregation and verification.** A coordinator (the *signature aggregator*, SA) verifies each share against the verification share,

$$z_i \cdot G \stackrel{?}{=} D_i + \rho_i \cdot E_i + \lambda_i \cdot c \cdot Y_i,$$

sums the valid shares $z = \sum_{i \in S} z_i$, and outputs $\sigma = (R, z)$, which verifies as an ordinary Schnorr signature: $z \cdot G = R + c \cdot P$ [1][2].

The following Python reference captures the share-computation core:

```python
def frost_sign_share(d_i, e_i, x_i, lam_i, i, msg, comm_list, P, H1, H2, G, q):
    # binding factor ties this share to the full commitment set
    rho_i = H1(i, msg, comm_list) % q
    R = sum(D + rho * E for (_, D, E, rho) in
            [(j, Dj, Ej, H1(j, msg, comm_list) % q) for (j, Dj, Ej) in comm_list])
    c = H2(R, P, msg) % q
    z_i = (d_i + e_i * rho_i + lam_i * x_i * c) % q
    # erase nonces: (d_i, e_i) must never be reused
    return z_i, R
```

### 3.2 The Preprocessed Single-Round Variant

Because Round 1 is message-independent, participants can pre-generate and publish commitment pairs in *batches* ahead of time. When a message arrives, the coordinator selects one unused published pair per signer, and signing collapses to a *single* round of share transmission. FROST proves this preprocessing safe under concurrency — a property earlier schemes lacked — because the binding factor $\rho_i$ still binds each share to the concrete commitment list $L$ actually used [1]. Unused preprocessed nonces must be discarded if their session aborts, since reusing $(d_i, e_i)$ across two challenges leaks the share via elementary algebra ($z_i - z_i' = (e_i - e_i')\rho_i$, exposing no secret directly, but cross-session nonce reuse in Schnorr is catastrophic in general).

### 3.3 Key Generation and Refresh

FROST's DKG follows the Pedersen construction: each participant acts as dealer for a random degree-$(t-1)$ polynomial, distributes Feldman-committed shares, and the joint secret is the *sum* of the individual polynomials' constant terms, never assembled anywhere. Verification shares $Y_i$ and the group key $P$ are derived from the commitments [1][6]. After key generation, proactive refresh (§4.3) periodically re-randomizes shares without changing the secret [5].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Binding Factor: Defeating Concurrent Forgery

Drijvers et al. demonstrated that many two-round Schnorr multisignatures admit a *concurrent forgery*: an adversary interleaving two signing sessions can adaptively choose its nonce commitment in session two *after* seeing the honest parties' commitments in session one, effectively canceling terms and extracting a forgery via the generalized forking lemma. Concretely, with a single nonce per signer, the group nonce $R = \sum D_i$ is malleable by a last-mover who observes others' $D_i$ first.

FROST's countermeasure is the binding factor $\rho_i = H_1(i \parallel m \parallel L)$: because the *effective* nonce contribution $D_i + \rho_i E_i$ of every signer depends on the *entire* ordered commitment list $L$ through the hash, no adversary can re-tune its contribution after seeing others', since any change to $L$ re-randomizes every $\rho_j$ simultaneously. The pair $(d_i, e_i)$ thus implements a *committed-then-adapted* nonce: $d_i$ is fixed at commitment time while $e_i$ is scaled by a value that cannot be predicted before $L$ is finalized [1]. This single design decision is what licenses unbounded concurrency — FROST needs no sequential-session discipline, unlike MuSig's original two-round form.

> **Theorem (FROST unforgeability, informal [1]):** If the discrete logarithm problem is hard in $\mathbb{G}$ and the adversary corrupts fewer than *t* participants, FROST is EUF-CMA secure even when the adversary adaptively interleaves arbitrarily many signing sessions.

### 4.2 Distributed Key Generation in Detail

A trusted dealer is a single point of compromise; production threshold systems therefore generate the key *distributively*. In the Pedersen DKG used by FROST [6]:

1. **Sharing phase.** Each $P_i$ samples $f_i(z) = a_{i,0} + a_{i,1}z + \cdots + a_{i,t-1}z^{t-1}$, broadcasts Feldman commitments $A_{i,k} = a_{i,k} \cdot G$ (optionally Pedersen-blinded), and sends $s_{ij} = f_i(j)$ privately to $P_j$.
2. **Verification and complaints.** $P_j$ checks $s_{ij} \cdot G \stackrel{?}{=} \sum_k A_{i,k} \cdot j^k$; on failure it broadcasts a complaint, and the accused dealer must reveal $s_{ij}$ or be disqualified, with its polynomial reconstructed from *t* honest shares.
3. **Key derivation.** Let $Q$ be the qualified dealer set. Each participant's long-term share is $x_j = \sum_{i \in Q} s_{ij}$; the group key is $P = \sum_{i \in Q} A_{i,0}$, and verification shares are $Y_j = \sum_{i \in Q} \sum_k A_{i,k} j^k$.

The joint secret $x = \sum_{i \in Q} a_{i,0}$ is uniform provided at least one qualified dealer is honest — no trusted setup ceremony is required. Gennaro et al. proved this construction simulatable against static adversaries corrupting $t-1$ parties [6], and RFC 9591 standardizes a concrete instantiation with per-ciphersuite hash functions [2].

| Phase | Rounds | Communication | Adversary tolerance |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pedersen DKG sharing | 2 + complaints | $O(n^2)$ | $t-1$ static corruptions |
| FROST 2-round signing | 2 | $O(t)$ | $t-1$, identifiable abort |
| FROST preprocessed signing | 1 (+ offline batch) | $O(t)$ | $t-1$, identifiable abort |
| ROAST-wrapped signing | $\le n - t + 1$ sessions | $O(n \cdot t)$ | $t-1$, robust termination |

### 4.3 Proactive Secret Sharing: Outrunning the Mobile Adversary

Static-adversary models assume the set of corrupted parties is fixed. Herzberg, Jarecki, Krawczyk, and Yung introduced the **mobile adversary**, who may compromise *different* servers over time, accumulating shares across *epochs* until it holds *t* of them [5]. Without countermeasures, any long-lived threshold key eventually falls.

**Proactive secret sharing (PSS)** divides time into epochs and *refreshes* shares at each epoch boundary while leaving the secret invariant. In the Herzberg et al. construction, each party $P_i$ generates a random polynomial $\delta_i(z)$ with *zero constant term*,

$$\delta_i(z) = \delta_{i,1} z + \delta_{i,2} z^2 + \cdots + \delta_{i,t-1} z^{t-1},$$

sends $u_{ij} = \delta_i(j)$ to $P_j$, and every party updates

$$x_j^{(e+1)} = x_j^{(e)} + \sum_{i=1}^{n} u_{ij}^{(e)}, \qquad \text{then erases } x_j^{(e)}. $$

Because $\sum_i \delta_i(0) = 0$, the shared secret is unchanged; because each $\delta_i$ is random subject to that constraint, the new shares are statistically independent of the old ones. Shares stolen in epoch *e* are *useless* in epoch $e+1$ once old shares are erased — the adversary must compromise *t* parties *within a single epoch* [5]. For FROST deployments, PSS means the signing key gains *forward secrecy of shares*: a breach's blast radius is bounded by the refresh period, at the cost of a periodic $O(n^2)$ all-to-all refresh round and a secure-erasure assumption.

### 4.4 ROAST: From Identifiable Abort to Robustness

FROST deliberately forgoes *robustness*: a single malicious signer can force an abort (though its invalid share $z_i$ identifies it via the verification equation, enabling expulsion) [1]. For automated systems such as the Liquid network's functionaries, aborts are unacceptable. **ROAST** (RObust ASynchronous Threshold signatures) is a *wrapper* around any threshold scheme with identifiable aborts: a semi-trusted coordinator maintains a set of responsive signers, initiates a FROST session whenever *t* are available, and on abort simply starts a *new* session excluding the identified disruptor [3].

> **Theorem (ROAST termination [3]):** With at most $n - t$ disruptors, ROAST terminates after at most $n - t + 1$ signing sessions, with no synchrony assumptions — it is fully asynchronous.

ROAST's coordinator cannot forge or alter signatures; it only schedules sessions. Ruffing et al. demonstrate a 67-of-100 deployment with coordinator and signers on different continents, showing robustness need not sacrifice FROST's round efficiency [3].

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 Proof Sketch: From Forgery to Discrete Logarithm

The FROST security proof [1] proceeds by reduction. Assume a forger $\mathcal{F}$ controlling $t-1$ participants produces a valid signature $(R, z)$ on a fresh message. The simulator embeds a discrete-logarithm challenge $X^\star = x^\star G$ as the honest participant's verification share, and answers $\mathcal{F}$'s signing queries by programming the random oracles $H_1, H_2$. For the honest signer's share, the simulator exploits the *two-nonce* structure: it samples $(d, e)$ and, given the challenge $c = H_2(R \parallel P \parallel m)$, would need $z = d + e\rho + \lambda x c$ — unknowable without *x*. Instead the simulator *programs* the group commitment $R$ and uses the standard Schnorr simulation trick in the exponent, made possible because $\rho$ binds *after* commitments are fixed. Applying the forking lemma to two forgeries with distinct $H_2$ outputs on the same $R$ yields two equations whose difference cancels the nonces, exposing $x^\star$ and solving the discrete logarithm. The binding factor is load-bearing here: without it, the adversary's adaptive choice of commitments across forked executions breaks the extraction [1].

### 5.2 Performance Characteristics

Concrete costs (secp256k1, 128-bit security) per FROST signing operation [1][2]:

| Operation | Per-signer cost | Coordinator cost |
|---|---|---|
| Round 1: sample $(d_i, e_i)$, publish $(D_i, E_i)$ | 2 scalar mults | — |
| Round 2: compute $\rho_i, R, c, z_i$ | $t$ hashes + 3 scalar mults | — |
| Share verification | — | $t$ scalar mults (batchable) |
| Aggregation $z = \sum z_i$ | — | $t$ field additions |

Communication is two broadcasts of 2 group elements plus one scalar per signer — roughly 160 bytes per signer on secp256k1 — versus the multi-round, zero-knowledge-heavy transcripts of threshold ECDSA schemes like GG18 [8], where each signature requires several rounds of Paillier-based multiplication-to-addition subprotocols and range proofs. The tradeoff is explicit: threshold ECDSA retains compatibility with existing ECDSA-only chains, while FROST requires Schnorr verification (natively available in Bitcoin Taproot [4] and most modern chains) and settles for abort-rather-than-robust semantics unless wrapped in ROAST [3].

### 5.3 Standardization and Deployment

RFC 9591 [2] standardizes FROST ciphersuites (ristretto255, Ed25519, P-256, secp256k1, ristretto255 with SHA-512, among others), test vectors, and the DKG interface, giving implementers an interoperable specification. The Zcash Foundation's deployment experience and Blockstream's ROAST-based Liquid federation work [3] constitute the two most mature production case studies: the former emphasizes preprocessing for low-latency signing, the latter robustness across unreliable intercontinental links.

## 6 Limitations

1. **No robustness natively.** FROST aborts under a single Byzantine signer; liveness requires the ROAST wrapper and its extra sessions [1][3].
2. **Secure erasure assumption.** Both preprocessing (aborted sessions' nonces) and proactive refresh (old shares) demand that participants *truly delete* superseded secrets; without reliable erasure, the mobile-adversary protection of §4.3 collapses [5].
3. **Synchrony in DKG.** While signing is non-interactive-friendly, the Pedersen DKG needs complaint and reconstruction rounds with timing assumptions; an asynchronous DKG (e.g., via asynchronous VSS) remains substantially more expensive [6].
4. **Static-corruption proofs.** The core FROST proof assumes a static adversary; adaptive-corruption analyses require stronger assumptions or modified protocols.
5. **Trusted setup of parameters.** Group parameters and hash domain separation must be agreed honestly; a malicious parameter generator can subvert the discrete-logarithm hardness the proofs rely on.
6. **ECDSA incompatibility.** Chains that verify only ECDSA cannot consume FROST signatures, preserving the niche for heavier threshold-ECDSA protocols such as GG18 [8] and Lindell–Nof [7].

---

## 7 Conclusion

FROST reframes the economics of threshold Schnorr signing: by accepting identifiable abort instead of full robustness, it buys two-round — or preprocessed single-round — signing that is provably secure under arbitrary concurrency, a combination earlier schemes could not offer [1]. The binding factor $\rho_i = H_1(i \parallel m \parallel L)$ is the linchpin, converting a malleable multi-session attack surface into a committed protocol transcript. Around this core, a complete key lifecycle is now well understood: Pedersen distributed key generation eliminates the trusted dealer [6], proactive secret sharing neutralizes the mobile adversary across epochs [5], and the ROAST wrapper restores liveness for automated deployments [3]. Standardized as RFC 9591 [2] and battle-tested in custody and sidechain federations, threshold Schnorr via FROST has moved from research artifact to infrastructure. Open frontiers remain — adaptively secure DKG with FROST-grade round efficiency, post-quantum threshold analogues, and formal verification of the RFC's ciphersuite implementations — but the trajectory is clear: the single point of failure that is a private key is, increasingly, an avoidable design choice.
