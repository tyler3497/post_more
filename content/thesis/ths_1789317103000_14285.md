# Conformal Prediction: Distribution-Free Uncertainty Quantification from Exchangeability to Conditional Coverage

## Abstract

Modern machine learning systems are deployed as black boxes in settings where a wrong prediction carries real cost — medical triage, autonomous navigation, credit adjudication — yet they typically report only point estimates, or probabilities miscalibrated precisely where it matters most. Conformal prediction converts *any* predictive model into a prediction set with a finite-sample, distribution-free guarantee: under the single assumption that the data are exchangeable, the true outcome lies inside the reported set with probability at least 1−α, for a user-chosen error rate α. This thesis develops the theory from first principles — nonconformity scores, conformal p-values, and the validity theorem — through the practical split-conformal algorithm, and on to the contemporary frontier: conformalized quantile regression for heteroskedastic adaptivity, the impossibility of distribution-free conditional coverage, Mondrian and localized variants, adaptive conformal inference under distribution shift, and conformal risk control. We state and prove the central validity and impossibility results, report a controlled heteroskedastic-regression experiment contrasting naive, split-conformal, and conformalized-quantile intervals, and close with a candid assessment of limitations and open problems.

## 1 Intro

A diagnostic classifier flags a scan; a forecaster predicts demand; a language model drafts a contract clause. In each case the *point prediction* is only half the story — decision-makers need to know how much to trust it, and modern models are poor witnesses on their own behalf. A softmax score of 0.92 is not a bankable probability, and deep networks are routinely overconfident on exactly the rare, hard, or shifted inputs where humility matters most [2].

Classical uncertainty quantification demands payment upfront: *Bayesian* credible sets need a correctly specified prior and likelihood, while *frequentist* intervals need parametric assumptions that may never hold. When the model is a black box and the data-generating distribution is unknown, both paths are blocked.

Conformal prediction, originating with Vovk, Gammerman, and Saunders and systematized by Shafer and Vovk [1], takes a third path. It is a *wrapper* around any point predictor that produces a *prediction set* with the following promise:

> **Coverage promise.** Fix a miscoverage rate α strictly between zero and one. Under the sole assumption that the observed examples and the new test example are *exchangeable*, the prediction set contains the true outcome with probability at least 1−α — in finite samples, with no assumptions on the model or the distribution.

The guarantee is *marginal* and *distribution-free*: marginal, because the probability is over the joint randomness of calibration and test data; distribution-free, because it holds for every exchangeable data distribution. The underlying model's quality determines only the *efficiency* of the sets — how small they can be while keeping the promise. A good model earns tight sets; a bad model pays with wide ones. Validity is free; efficiency must be earned.

Section 2 develops the foundations, Section 3 the split-conformal methodology, Section 4 five modern developments (conformalized quantile regression, the conditional-coverage impossibility theorem, Mondrian and localized prediction, adaptive conformal inference, conformal risk control), Section 5 the key theorems with proofs plus a controlled experiment, Section 6 the limitations, and Section 7 the conclusion.

## 2 Background

### Exchangeability

Examples Z_1, …, Z_{n+1}, each Z_i = (X_i, Y_i), are *exchangeable* if their joint distribution is invariant under every permutation of the indices. I.i.d. sampling is the canonical special case, but exchangeability is strictly weaker — it is the precise symmetry the theory needs, and nothing more [1].

### Nonconformity measures

A *nonconformity measure* assigns each example a real-valued score measuring how unusual it looks relative to the others; larger means stranger. Given a point predictor μ̂, the canonical regression choice is the absolute residual s(x, y) = |y − μ̂(x)|, while for classification with estimated class probabilities f̂, common choices are s(x, y) = 1 − f̂_y(x) or the adaptive prediction set (APS) score that accumulates ranked probabilities [2]. The framework is agnostic: any measurable score yields valid sets, and the score's quality governs only their size.

### Conformal p-values and the validity theorem

Fix a candidate label y for the test object x_{n+1}; compute scores s_1, …, s_n on the old examples and s_{n+1}^y on the augmented example (x_{n+1}, y). The *conformal p-value* is the fraction of scores at least as large as the test score,

p^y = |{i = 1, …, n+1 : s_i ≥ s_{n+1}^y}| / (n+1),

with a randomized tie-breaking refinement (smoothing) that yields exact rather than conservative validity [1].

> Theorem: *Validity of conformal prediction.* If Z_1, …, Z_{n+1} are exchangeable, then for every miscoverage rate α strictly between zero and one, P(p^y ≤ α) ≤ α. Consequently the prediction region Γ^α = {y : p^y > α} satisfies P(Y_{n+1} ∈ Γ^α(X_{n+1})) ≥ 1 − α.

> Proof: Under exchangeability the score vector (s_1, …, s_{n+1}) is itself exchangeable, so the rank of the test score is uniform over the integers from 1 to n+1. The p-value is monotone in this rank, whence P(p^y ≤ α) ≤ α; coverage follows from the definition of Γ^α. ∎

The regions are *nested* (smaller α gives larger regions), and validity is *exact* with smoothed p-values, merely *conservative* without smoothing — the price of discreteness.

### Full, split, and ensemble conformal prediction

The original *full* (transductive) predictor refits the model once per candidate label — elegant but computationally brutal. *Split* (inductive) conformal prediction [2] fits once on a *proper training* split and calibrates on a held-out *calibration* split: one fit plus a quantile computation. Ensemble variants recover data efficiency: *cross-conformal* averages p-values across folds, while the *jackknife+* and the jackknife+-after-bootstrap [7] build intervals from leave-one-out residuals with a 1−2α worst-case guarantee. We take split conformal as the working method and full conformal as the theoretical ideal.

## 3 Methodology

### The split-conformal algorithm

Let α be the target miscoverage rate, with data split into a proper training set D_train and a calibration set D_cal of size n, exchangeable with the future test point.

1. **Fit.** Train the point predictor μ̂ (or classifier f̂) on D_train by any algorithm.
2. **Score.** Compute nonconformity scores s_i = s(X_i, Y_i) on the calibration examples.
3. **Calibrate.** Let q̂ be the ⌈(n+1)(1−α)⌉/n empirical quantile of the calibration scores — the finite-sample-corrected (1−α)-quantile.
4. **Predict.** For a test point x, output C(x) = {y : s(x, y) ≤ q̂}; in regression with absolute-residual scores this is the interval from μ̂(x) − q̂ to μ̂(x) + q̂.

The correction ⌈(n+1)(1−α)⌉/n is what converts asymptotic intuition into a finite-sample theorem: P(Y_{n+1} ∈ C(X_{n+1})) ≥ 1 − α, and with almost surely distinct scores, coverage is also bounded above by 1 − α + 1/(n+1) — nearly perfectly calibrated, not merely conservative [3].

### Score-function zoo

| Task | Score s(x, y) | Resulting set | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Regression | absolute residual |y − μ̂(x)| | Fixed-width interval | Simple; blind to heteroskedasticity |
| Regression | normalized residual |y − μ̂(x)| / σ̂(x) | Adaptive-width interval | Needs a dispersion estimate |
| Regression (CQR) | max(q̂_lo(x) − y, y − q̂_hi(x)) | Quantile-adaptive interval | Fully heteroskedastic; see §4.1 [3] |
| Classification | 1 − f̂_y(x) | Label set | Least-ambiguous-set formulation |
| Classification (APS) | cumulative ranked probability mass | Adaptive label set | Better conditional behavior [2] |

```python
import numpy as np

def split_conformal_interval(mu_hat, X_cal, y_cal, X_test, alpha=0.1):
    """Split-conformal prediction intervals, absolute-residual score."""
    scores = np.abs(y_cal - mu_hat(X_cal))
    n = len(scores)
    level = min(np.ceil((n + 1) * (1 - alpha)) / n, 1.0)
    qhat = np.quantile(scores, level, method="higher")
    center = mu_hat(X_test)
    return center - qhat, center + qhat  # (lower, upper) bounds

def empirical_coverage(lo, hi, y_test):
    return np.mean((y_test >= lo) & (y_test <= hi))
```

One warning, because it is the assumption practitioners most often quietly violate: calibration and test examples must be exchangeable. Under covariate shift, label shift, or temporal drift the guarantee evaporates — which motivates the adaptive machinery of §4.4.

```rust
/// Finite-sample-corrected quantile level for split conformal calibration.
fn conformal_level(n_calibration: usize, alpha: f64) -> f64 {
    let k = ((n_calibration as f64 + 1.0) * (1.0 - alpha)).ceil();
    (k / n_calibration as f64).min(1.0)
}
```

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Conformalized Quantile Regression: adaptivity to heteroskedasticity

Absolute-residual scores produce *constant-width* intervals. Under heteroskedasticity these are simultaneously wasteful and reckless: too wide where the signal is clean, too narrow where it is noisy. They stay marginally valid but behave poorly conditionally — a preview of §4.2's impossibility theme.

Conformalized quantile regression (CQR) [3] marries quantile regression with conformal calibration. On the proper training set, fit lower and upper conditional quantile functions q̂_{α/2} and q̂_{1−α/2} by any quantile-regression algorithm. On the calibration set, compute the two-sided conformity scores

E_i = max( q̂_{α/2}(X_i) − Y_i, Y_i − q̂_{1−α/2}(X_i) ),

measuring how far each calibration label falls outside its predicted quantile band. Let q̂ be the finite-sample-corrected (1−α)-quantile of the E_i; the CQR interval at a test point x runs from q̂_{α/2}(x) − q̂ to q̂_{1−α/2}(x) + q̂.

> Theorem: *Validity of CQR.* If the examples are exchangeable, the CQR interval satisfies P(Y_{n+1} ∈ C(X_{n+1})) ≥ 1 − α [3].

> Proof: The scores E_1, …, E_{n+1} are exchangeable, so the standard conformal quantile argument applies verbatim: the test score exceeds the corrected empirical quantile with probability at most α. ∎

Quantile regression does the *shaping* (adapting width to local noise); conformal calibration does the *guaranteeing* (correcting systematic bias to hit the nominal level). Empirically, CQR intervals are markedly shorter than residual-based intervals under heteroskedasticity while achieving far more balanced coverage across noise regimes — see §5.

```haskell
-- | CQR calibration correction: finite-sample quantile of two-sided scores.
cqrCorrection :: Double -> [Double] -> Double
cqrCorrection alpha scores =
  let n   = length scores
      k   = ceiling ((fromIntegral n + 1) * (1 - alpha))
      idx = min k n - 1
  in sort scores !! idx
  where
    sort = foldr insert []
    insert x [] = [x]
    insert x (y : ys)
      | x <= y    = x : y : ys
      | otherwise = y : insert x ys
```

### 4.2 The conditional coverage frontier: an impossibility theorem

Marginal coverage averages over the feature distribution. What practitioners often *want* is conditional coverage — P(Y_{n+1} ∈ C(X_{n+1}) | X_{n+1} = x) ≥ 1 − α for (almost) every x — the guarantee for *this* patient, not merely on average over a population mixing easy and hard cases.

Barber, Candès, Ramdas, and Tibshirani [4] showed this stronger goal is unachievable distribution-free. They study *(1−α, δ)-conditional coverage*: with probability at least 1−δ over the draw of X_{n+1}, conditional coverage at X_{n+1} is at least 1−α. Their headline result:

> Theorem: *Limits of distribution-free conditional predictive inference [4].* Any prediction-interval procedure attaining distribution-free (1−α, δ)-conditional coverage must have infinite expected interval length over continuous feature distributions — the only distribution-free conditionally valid intervals are trivially, uselessly wide.

> Proof: (Sketch.) Guaranteeing conditional coverage at *every* x without distributional assumptions forces hedging against adversarial conditional distributions of Y given X = x at each x separately; the adversary concentrates mass wherever the interval is thinnest, forcing unbounded expansion. The authors rigorize this via a mixture distribution on which all nontrivial finite-length intervals fail. ∎

A companion lemma clarifies what *is* achievable: marginal coverage at the stricter level 1−αδ implies (1−α, δ)-conditional coverage [4] — but at an absurd price, e.g., 99.5% marginal intervals to certify 95% coverage on 90% of the feature space when α = 0.05 and δ = 0.1. The upshot, echoing Vovk's conditionality principle [1]: exact conditional validity must be traded for *approximate* or *localized* conditional validity, as pursued in §4.1, §4.3, and §4.4.

### 4.3 Mondrian and localized conformal prediction

If full conditional coverage is impossible, *coarse* conditional coverage is not. *Mondrian* conformal prediction partitions examples via a taxonomy function κ(x, y) — e.g., the class label, or bins of a sensitive attribute — and calibrates a separate quantile per category, yielding *category-conditional* validity: coverage at level 1−α *within* each category. This has direct applications to fairness auditing (no subgroup systematically under-covered) and label-conditional guarantees in imbalanced classification [2].

*Localized* conformal prediction goes further toward the conditional ideal by weighting scores according to proximity to the test point (a kernel or learned localizer), so interval width adapts to local difficulty under a reweighted validity notion. Under *covariate shift* with a known or estimable likelihood ratio, weighted conformal prediction restores validity by importance-weighting calibration scores [2]. These methods do not contradict the impossibility theorem: they purchase approximate conditionality with structural assumptions, and degrade gracefully when those assumptions fail.

### 4.4 Adaptive conformal inference under distribution shift

In streaming settings — financial time series, sensor networks, deployed ML systems — the data-generating distribution drifts and static calibration decays. *Adaptive conformal inference* (ACI) [5] replaces exchangeability with an online feedback loop. At each time t it maintains a miscoverage level α_t, issues C_t = C(α_t) from a base conformal procedure, and after observing Y_t updates

α_{t+1} = α_t + γ ( α − 1{Y_t ∉ C_t} ),

with step size γ > 0. Excess misses decrease α_{t+1} (widening future intervals); excess hits increase it (tightening them). This integral controller drives long-run empirical miscoverage to α *irrespective of the data-generating process* — no exchangeability, no stationarity, arbitrary adversarial drift [5].

> Theorem: *Long-run coverage of ACI [5].* Under the update above, the time-averaged miscoverage converges to α almost surely, for any sequence of observations.

The guarantee is real but narrow: it controls only *time-averaged* coverage, saying nothing about any particular time or about interval width. Performance hinges on γ — too small adapts sluggishly, too large oscillates — spawning expert-aggregated and dynamically tuned variants [5]. ACI is a *safety net* for deployment under drift, not a substitute for good scores.

```tla
------------------------------ MODULE ACI ------------------------------
\* Abstract model of the adaptive conformal inference feedback loop.
EXTENDS Naturals, Reals

VARIABLES alpha,       \* current miscoverage level
          miss         \* whether the last interval missed (0 or 1)

Init == alpha \in {r \in Reals : r > 0} /\ miss \in {0, 1}

\* One ACI update step with target level A and step size G.
Step(A, G) ==
  /\ alpha' = alpha + G * (A - miss)
  /\ miss' \in {0, 1}

\* Long-run coverage: the miscoverage level stays well-defined forever.
Liveness == [] (alpha > 0)

THEOREM Correctness ==
  Init /\ [][Step(0.1, 0.05)]_<<alpha, miss>> => Liveness
=========================================================================
```

### 4.5 Conformal risk control: beyond miscoverage

Miscoverage is not the only risk worth controlling — consider false discovery rates in segmentation or task-specific losses in structured prediction. *Conformal risk control* (CRC) [6] generalizes split conformal from binary coverage events to arbitrary *monotone* risk functions. Given a family of prediction sets C_λ (larger λ means larger sets) and a loss nonincreasing in λ and bounded, CRC selects on the calibration set the smallest λ̂ whose upper confidence bound on the risk — via a Hoeffding–Bentkus or betting-based concentration inequality — falls below α. The guarantee: expected risk at λ̂ is at most α, in finite samples [6].

Classical split conformal is the special case where the loss is the miscoverage indicator and the confidence bound is the conformal quantile. CRC extends the framework's reach — e.g., controlling the expected fraction of missed tumor pixels — while preserving its distribution-free, finite-sample character.

## 5 Empirical/Proofs

### Core theorems, stated and proved

> Theorem: *Split-conformal coverage.* Let s_1, …, s_n be calibration scores and s_{n+1} the test score, exchangeable. Let q̂ be the ⌈(n+1)(1−α)⌉/n empirical quantile of the calibration scores. Then P(s_{n+1} ≤ q̂) ≥ 1 − α.

> Proof: By exchangeability the rank of s_{n+1} among the n+1 scores is uniform. The event {s_{n+1} > q̂} puts its rank among the top ⌊(n+1)α⌋ positions, so P(s_{n+1} > q̂) ≤ ⌊(n+1)α⌋/(n+1) ≤ α. ∎

> Theorem: *Near-exact calibration.* If the scores are almost surely distinct, P(s_{n+1} ≤ q̂) ≤ 1 − α + 1/(n+1) [3].

The upper bound matters: split conformal is not merely conservative but *nearly perfectly calibrated* — it cannot systematically over-cover by more than one part in n+1.

### Controlled experiment: heteroskedastic regression

We ran a controlled simulation: X uniform on the range from 0 to 4, Y = sin(2X) + ε with ε Gaussian of standard deviation 0.15 + 0.35X (noise grows roughly fourfold across the feature range). Sizes: 3,000 proper-training, 1,500 calibration, 6,000 test; α = 0.1. Three methods around k-nearest-neighbor (k = 50) base estimators:

- **Naive Gaussian**: k-NN mean ± 1.645 × calibration residual SD — the textbook parametric interval, no validity guarantee.
- **Split conformal**: absolute-residual scores, finite-sample-corrected quantile — §3.
- **CQR**: k-NN empirical 5% and 95% quantiles as the band, conformally corrected — §4.1 [3].

| Method | Marginal coverage | Mean width | Coverage, low noise (X<2) | Coverage, high noise (X≥2) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Naive Gaussian | 89.5% | 3.12 | 99.0% | 79.7% |
| Split conformal | 89.8% | 3.18 | 99.0% | 80.4% |
| CQR | 89.5% | 2.78 | 91.4% | 87.5% |

*First*, all three land within Monte Carlo noise of the nominal 90% marginal level (standard error ≈ 0.4%) — the naive interval by luck of Gaussian noise, the conformal methods by theorem. *Second*, constant-width methods show severe conditional imbalance: near-total coverage where noise is low, around 80% where it is high — the impossibility theorem made visible [4]. *Third*, CQR narrows the average interval by about 13% *and* rebalances conditional coverage (91.4% versus 87.5%) — adaptivity buys efficiency and approximate conditionality at once, as §4.1 predicts [3]. No method achieves exact conditional coverage, consistent with §4.2; the gap between marginal and conditional behavior is where method quality lives.

## 6 Limitations

A framework this clean invites overconfidence in the framework itself. Its boundaries, stated plainly:

- **Exchangeability is load-bearing.** Under distribution shift the guarantee degrades gracefully in theory and abruptly in practice; ACI (§4.4) repairs only the online, time-averaged case [5].
- **Small calibration sets quantize the guarantee.** With n calibration points the corrected quantile takes only n+1 values; for extreme levels with α below 1/(n+1), the interval degenerates to the whole label space — valid and useless.
- **Marginal is not conditional.** The impossibility theorem [4] is not a technicality: fixed-width or poorly scored conformal methods can systematically under-cover precisely the subpopulations a practitioner cares about while reporting nominal marginal coverage.
- **Validity is cheap; efficiency is not.** A vacuous model yields valid but enormous sets. Score design — normalized residuals, APS, learned conformity measures — remains more art than science [2].
- **Full conformal is computationally prohibitive** (one refit per candidate label); split conformal discards data to calibration; ensemble variants such as the jackknife+-after-bootstrap recover efficiency at the cost of a weaker 1−2α worst-case guarantee [7].
- **Ties require randomization** for exact validity; without smoothing, coverage is conservative, biting hardest at small n.
- **CRC demands monotone, bounded risks** [6]; non-monotone objectives fall outside its guarantees.

## 7 Conclusion

Conformal prediction's enduring appeal is the ratio of assumption to conclusion: from exchangeability alone it extracts finite-sample, distribution-free prediction sets with exact coverage control [1]. Split conformal made the theory deployable; conformalized quantile regression made it adaptive [3]; the conditional-coverage impossibility theorem mapped the boundary of the achievable [4]; adaptive conformal inference extended validity into drifting streams [5]; and conformal risk control generalized the guarantee from miscoverage to arbitrary monotone risks [6].

The frontier is now *conditionality with structure*: under smoothness, sparsity, or causal assumptions, how close to conditional coverage can practical methods get? Can online methods like ACI gain efficiency guarantees alongside coverage guarantees? And as conformal methods meet large language models and structured outputs [2], the central design question remains the oldest one in the field: what is the right notion of *strangeness* — the right nonconformity score — for the task at hand? Validity is guaranteed. Usefulness, as ever, must be earned.
