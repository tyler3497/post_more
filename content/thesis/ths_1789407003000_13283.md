# End-to-End Automatic Speech Recognition: Connectionist Temporal Classification, RNN Transducers, Attention, and Transformer ASR

## Abstract

End-to-end automatic speech recognition collapsed the classical stack of acoustic model, pronunciation lexicon, and language model into a single neural network trained directly from audio to text. This thesis traces that transformation from the hidden Markov model with Gaussian mixture emissions and its brittle conditional-independence assumptions, through connectionist temporal classification [1], which first made alignment-free training of recurrent networks possible with a forward–backward dynamic program over label lattices and an explicit blank symbol, to the recurrent neural network transducer [2], which removed the conditional-independence restriction through a prediction network and a joint network. We then examine attention-based encoder–decoders [4], self-attentive encoders [5], and the convolution-augmented Conformer [6], and show how beam-search decoding with shallow fusion of external language models turned these training objectives into production systems. Benchmarks on LibriSpeech and Switchboard document word error rates falling from double digits to below two percent on clean read speech, and we close by analyzing the remaining limits of streaming, robustness, and data efficiency now being attacked by self-supervised representations [7] and weakly supervised scale [8].

## 1 Introduction

Automatic speech recognition (ASR) asks a deceptively simple question: given acoustic feature vectors x = (x_1, ..., x_T), produce the most likely word sequence y = (y_1, ..., y_U). The difficulty is structural: T is hundreds of frames per second while U is a handful of words, and the alignment between them is unknown and distorted by coarticulation, accent, and noise. For three decades the answer was the hidden Markov model: phones as state chains, state acoustics as Gaussian mixtures over MFCCs, a pronunciation lexicon, an n-gram language model, and a composed weighted transducer searched for the best path [3].

This machinery worked — it powered dictation products and call centers — but it was a cathedral of separately optimized parts. The acoustic model maximized frame likelihood while the language model maximized word likelihood, and neither knew the other's objective. Features were frozen by human design, the lexicon was a hand-maintained dictionary that failed on every out-of-vocabulary word, and pronunciation variants were patched with rules rather than learned.

The end-to-end program, which matured between 2006 and 2020, replaced all of this with a single neural network and a single objective: maximize the probability of the correct transcript given the audio, marginalizing over the unknown alignment. CTC [1] introduced the blank label and a differentiable forward–backward algorithm for unsegmented sequences. RNN-T [2] conditioned each emission on the label history, yielding streaming architectures for on-device assistants. Attention encoder–decoders [4] discarded explicit lattices and learned soft alignments; Transformer [5] and Conformer [6] encoders replaced recurrence with self-attention and convolution. Self-supervised pre-training [7] and massive weak supervision [8] then attacked the appetite for labeled data.

This thesis develops these ideas in full technical depth: Section 2 reviews the classical pipeline and why it broke; Section 3 presents the four canonical end-to-end methodologies; Section 4 goes deep into the trainable mathematics; Section 5 grounds the theory in benchmarks, code, and proofs; Section 6 is honest about what remains unsolved.

## 2 Background

### Acoustic features: from waveform to frame

Speech arrives as a pressure waveform sampled at 16 kHz or higher. The classical front end applies pre-emphasis, chops the signal into 25 ms frames advanced every 10 ms, and computes the short-time Fourier transform. The magnitude spectrum is projected onto 40 to 80 triangular filters spaced on the mel scale; taking the logarithm and applying the discrete cosine transform yields mel-frequency cepstral coefficients (MFCCs). A typical recipe keeps 13 cepstral coefficients plus their first and second temporal derivatives, with cepstral mean normalization per utterance to suppress channel effects.

These choices were motivated by decades of psychoacoustics and by a practical constraint: diagonal-covariance GMMs needed decorrelated, low-dimensional features. But the front end was *frozen*: no gradient ever flowed back into the filterbank. Modern systems increasingly learn from log-mel filterbanks or raw waveforms, letting the network decide which invariances matter [7].

### The HMM–GMM pipeline

The classical recognizer factorizes the problem through Bayes' rule into an acoustic model p(x | y) and a language model p(y), and further factorizes the acoustics into a phone HMM topology, state emission GMMs, and a pronunciation lexicon. Each phone is a three-state left-to-right HMM emitting MFCC vectors from diagonal-Gaussian mixtures. Training uses Baum–Welch (EM) plus discriminative criteria such as maximum mutual information; decoding composes the topology, context dependency, lexicon, and grammar into a single HCLG weighted transducer searched with Viterbi beam pruning. An engineering triumph — and a straitjacket.

### Why the pipeline broke

*Conditional independence.* The HMM assumes frames are independent given the state sequence; real speech violates this, since formant trajectories are smooth. Recurrent networks [3] model these dependencies directly.

*The lexicon bottleneck.* Every word needed a hand-written phonetic spelling. Character- and wordpiece-based end-to-end models [4] need no lexicon at all.

*Objective mismatch.* The acoustic model maximized likelihood of the audio, not word accuracy. End-to-end training optimizes the transcript probability directly.

*Frozen features.* The front end could not learn. Convolutional front ends inside CTC and Conformer models learn data-driven time–frequency filters instead.

Deep recurrent networks with CTC [1, 3] were the first to break all four constraints at once, which is where the end-to-end story properly begins.

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 Connectionist temporal classification

CTC [1] considers a network emitting, at each of T time steps, a softmax over an alphabet plus a special *blank* symbol. A many-to-one map B collapses an alignment by deleting blanks and repeated labels, so "a a – b – –" maps to "ab". The transcript probability sums over all alignments collapsing to it — exponentially many, but computable in quadratic time by forward–backward over the target with blanks interleaved (Section 4.1 proves the gradient takes an elegant form). The model makes one strong assumption: *outputs at different time steps are conditionally independent given the input*. Decoding can be greedy (argmax per frame, then collapse) or use a prefix beam search that merges hypotheses mapping to the same prefix. A bidirectional LSTM trained with CTC on TIMIT reached 17.7% phone error rate with no forced alignment at all [3].

### 3.2 The RNN transducer

Graves' sequence transducer [2] removes CTC's independence assumption. It has three components: an *encoder* mapping acoustic frames to hidden states h_t; a *prediction network* acting as a label-level language model, mapping previous labels to g_u; and a *joint network* combining them, typically z(t, u) = tanh(W h_t + V g_u), projected to a softmax over the alphabet plus blank. Decoding proceeds over a lattice indexed by time t and label position u: each node emits blank (advance t) or the next label (advance u), and the loss is the total probability of all paths from (1, 0) to (T, U) by forward–backward. Because the prediction network conditions on label history, RNN-T models linguistic context internally, and because the lattice is processed left to right in time, it is the natural architecture for *streaming* recognition — the design behind production voice assistants [2].

### 3.3 Attention-based encoder–decoders

Listen, Attend and Spell (LAS) [4] abandoned the alignment lattice entirely. An encoder produces high-level acoustic features; at each output step i, attention computes a distribution α_{i,t} over encoder frames and forms a context vector; a decoder LSTM conditions on the context and the previous character to emit the next one. No blank, no forward–backward, no monotonicity — soft alignments are learned from data. LAS transcribed directly to characters with no pronunciation model or HMM. Its weakness, analyzed in Section 4.3, is that unconstrained attention can skip or repeat input, and that decoding is inherently non-streaming.

### 3.4 Transformer and Conformer encoders

Replacing recurrent encoders with self-attention [5] gave the Transformer ASR family: multi-head attention captures long-range acoustic context in constant sequential depth. The Conformer [6] added *local* modeling — phonetic events span tens of milliseconds — sandwiching a depthwise-separable convolution module between two half-step feed-forward modules around self-attention with relative positional encoding. It is the dominant modern encoder, and the backbone of the best LibriSpeech numbers of 2020 (Section 5).

### 3.5 Training objectives and decoding

Training minimizes the negative log-likelihood of the transcript — CTC, RNN-T, or cross-entropy — often as a multi-task hybrid with an auxiliary CTC loss to enforce monotonicity. Because end-to-end models see only paired audio–text data, practitioners *shallow-fuse* an external language model trained on text alone: at each step the score is log p_model(y | x) + λ log p_LM(y) plus a length bonus, with λ tuned on development data.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 CTC gradient mechanics and the conditional independence critique

Let y_t^k be the network's softmax output for label k at time t, and let y' be the target with blanks interleaved: y' = (blank, y_1, blank, y_2, ..., blank). The forward variable α_t(s) totals the probability of all prefix alignments ending in state s at time t; β_t(s) covers the suffix. Then p(y | x) = Σ_s α_T(s) and the loss is L = −log p(y | x).

> Theorem: (CTC gradient decomposition). With γ_t(s) = α_t(s)β_t(s)/p(y | x) the posterior occupancy of extended state s at time t, the gradient of the CTC loss w.r.t. the pre-softmax logits a_t^k is y_t^k − Σ_{s: y'_s = k} γ_t(s): the network output minus the soft occupancy target. CTC backpropagation is cross-entropy training against forward–backward alignment posteriors.

*Proof sketch.* Differentiating the log-sum over alignments and collecting terms by (t, k) yields the occupancy sum; the softmax Jacobian supplies the y_t^k term [1].

The formula exposes CTC's bargain: training is exact, but the model is *conditionally independent* — p(y | x) factorizes over time steps given x, so label-to-label dependencies must come from the encoder's acoustic context alone. This forces heavy reliance on external language models and produces "peaky" behavior: blanks almost everywhere, spikes on a few frames.

### 4.2 The RNN transducer alignment lattice

RNN-T replaces the one-dimensional CTC time axis with a two-dimensional lattice. The joint network produces a distribution P(k | t, u) over labels plus blank for every node (t, u). A path from (1, 0) to (T, U) that emits blank to advance t and the next label to advance u defines an alignment; the forward variable α(t, u) sums path probabilities via α(t, u) = α(t−1, u) P(blank | t−1, u) + α(t, u−1) P(y_u | t, u−1), with an analogous backward pass.

> Theorem: (RNN-T generalizes CTC). If joint-network outputs are independent of the prediction state — P(k | t, u) = P(k | t) for all u — then the RNN-T forward–backward over the (t, u) lattice collapses to the CTC forward–backward, and the two losses coincide. CTC is the label-history-free special case of the transducer.

*Proof sketch.* With u-independence, the lattice paths factor: summing over all label-emission positions u at each time t reduces the two-dimensional recurrence to the one-dimensional CTC recurrence over the extended label sequence, with blank playing its usual role. The prediction network in a trained RNN-T is precisely what breaks this symmetry, which is why ablating it degrades the model to CTC-like behavior.

The lattice view also explains RNN-T's streaming advantage: decoding emits labels as frames arrive, since every decision at node (t, u) depends only on the past. Streaming systems bound the encoder's right context, trading accuracy for bounded latency [2].

### 4.3 Attention: from LAS to coverage and monotonicity

LAS [4] replaces the lattice with content-based attention. At decoder step i with state s_i, the model scores each encoder frame h_t, normalizes to attention weights α_{i,t} = softmax(e_{i,t}), and reads a context c_i = Σ_t α_{i,t} h_t; the decoder models p(y_i | y_<i, c_i). Training is plain cross-entropy — no dynamic program — which made attention models dramatically easier to extend. The price is structural: nothing constrains α to be monotonic, local, or complete.

> Theorem: (Attention conserves mass over outputs, not inputs). For each decoder step i, Σ_t α_{i,t} = 1 by the softmax. But Σ_i α_{i,t} = 1 is unconstrained per input frame t: the model may re-attend to the same frames or skip some entirely. Attention decoders can thus repeat words, skip words, or stop early on long utterances — impossible in CTC or RNN-T, where every frame is consumed exactly once.

*Proof sketch.* Immediate from the definition: the softmax normalizes over positions for fixed i, so input-side coverage is unconstrained. ∎

The literature responded with *coverage* mechanisms — penalizing deviations of Σ_i α_{i,t} from one — and with *monotonic* attention variants forcing left-to-right alignment. In practice the most successful remedy was multi-task training: an auxiliary CTC loss on the shared encoder pressures it toward monotonic, frame-synchronous representations, the standard recipe for Transformer ASR [5].

### 4.4 The Conformer block and relative positional encoding

A single Conformer block [6] is a "sandwich": feed-forward (half residual), multi-head self-attention with *relative* positional encoding, a convolution module, and a second feed-forward module, each with layer normalization and residuals. The convolution module — pointwise expansion, gated linear unit, depthwise convolution, batch normalization, swish, projection — captures local patterns such as formant transitions, while self-attention captures global dependencies such as cross-syllable coarticulation.

Relative positional encoding reflects speech's translation invariance in time: attention scores incorporate a learned bias depending on i − j, following the Transformer-XL formulation. The encoder front end subsamples 10 ms frames by four with strided convolutions, so a 12-layer Conformer processes one vector per 40 ms — cutting quadratic attention cost sixteen-fold. A Conformer reached 2.1% / 4.3% WER on LibriSpeech test-clean / test-other with no language model, and 1.9% / 3.9% with one [6].

### 4.5 Decoding: beam search and shallow fusion

At inference, all three families search an exponentially large output space. CTC uses *prefix beam search*: hypotheses sharing a collapsed prefix are merged exactly. RNN-T beam search additionally tracks the prediction-network state per hypothesis; attention decoders use autoregressive beam search with length normalization.

Shallow fusion with an external language model remains the cheapest accuracy available: each partial hypothesis scores log p_model + λ log p_LM + β · length, with λ tuned on development data. Theoretically unprincipled — the end-to-end model already contains an implicit language model — it still works because the external LM trains on orders of magnitude more text.

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

### Word error rate milestones on LibriSpeech

LibriSpeech (960 hours of read English audiobooks) is the field's common yardstick. All numbers below are word error rates on the test-clean / test-other splits.

| Year | System | Architecture and loss | test-clean | test-other |
|------|--------|----------------------|------------|------------|
| 2016 | Deep Speech 2 | BiRNN + CTC | 5.33 | 13.25 |
| 2018 | ESPnet Transformer | Transformer, CTC/attention multitask | 2.6 | 5.7 |
| 2020 | Conformer (no LM) | Conformer + CTC/RNN-T [6] | 2.1 | 4.3 |
| 2020 | Conformer + LM | Conformer, shallow fusion [6] | 1.9 | 3.9 |
| 2020 | wav2vec 2.0 Large | Self-supervised + CTC fine-tune [7] | 1.8 | 3.3 |
| 2022 | Whisper large (zero-shot) | Transformer AED, 680k h weak supervision [8] | ~2.7 | ~5.2 |

Two patterns stand out. First, the loss families converged: by 2020, CTC, RNN-T, and attention systems with Conformer encoders all sat within a point of each other — the encoder had become the binding constraint. Second, post-2020 gains came from data: wav2vec 2.0 [7] pre-trained on 53,000 unlabeled hours; Whisper [8] scaled weak supervision to 680,000 hours.

### A working CTC dynamic program

The forward–backward of Section 4.1 in pure Python [1]:

```python
import math

INF_NEG = -math.inf

def logaddexp(*vals):
    m = max(vals)
    if m == INF_NEG:
        return INF_NEG
    return m + math.log(sum(math.exp(v - m) for v in vals))

def ctc_forward_backward(log_probs, target, blank=0):
    """Toy CTC forward-backward [1]. log_probs: T x (K+1) log-softmax outputs.
    Returns (nll, occ): loss and occupancy posteriors (Theorem 1)."""
    T, K1 = len(log_probs), len(log_probs[0])
    ext = [blank]
    for lab in target:
        ext += [lab, blank]          # interleave: b l1 b l2 b ...
    S = len(ext)

    # forward pass
    alpha = [[INF_NEG] * S for _ in range(T)]
    alpha[0][0] = log_probs[0][ext[0]]
    if S > 1:
        alpha[0][1] = log_probs[0][ext[1]]
    for t in range(1, T):
        prev = alpha[t - 1]
        for s in range(S):
            cand = [prev[s]]
            if s - 1 >= 0:
                cand.append(prev[s - 1])
            if s - 2 >= 0 and ext[s] != blank and ext[s] != ext[s - 2]:
                cand.append(prev[s - 2])  # blank skip only across differing labels
            alpha[t][s] = log_probs[t][ext[s]] + logaddexp(*cand)

    # backward pass
    beta = [[INF_NEG] * S for _ in range(T)]    beta[T - 1][S - 1] = 0.0
    if S > 1:
        beta[T - 1][S - 2] = 0.0
    for t in range(T - 2, -1, -1):
        yn = log_probs[t + 1]
        for s in range(S):
            cand = [beta[t + 1][s] + yn[ext[s]]]
            if s + 1 < S:
                cand.append(beta[t + 1][s + 1] + yn[ext[s + 1]])
            if s + 2 < S and ext[s] != blank and ext[s + 2] != ext[s]:
                cand.append(beta[t + 1][s + 2] + yn[ext[s + 2]])
            beta[t][s] = logaddexp(*cand)

    total = logaddexp(alpha[T - 1][S - 1], alpha[T - 1][S - 2]) if S > 1 else alpha[T - 1][0]

    # occupancy posteriors
    occ = [[0.0] * K1 for _ in range(T)]
    for t in range(T):
        for s in range(S):
            occ[t][ext[s]] += math.exp(alpha[t][s] + beta[t][s] - total)
    # Theorem 1: d(nll)/d(logit_t^k) = softmax_t^k - occ[t][k]
    return -total, occ

# demo: T=5 frames, alphabet {blank, a, b}, target "ab"
lp = [[math.log(p) for p in row] for row in
      [[0.6, 0.2, 0.2], [0.3, 0.5, 0.2], [0.3, 0.3, 0.4],
       [0.2, 0.3, 0.5], [0.6, 0.2, 0.2]]]
print(f"NLL: {ctc_forward_backward(lp, [1, 2])[0]:.4f}")
```

Running it prints an NLL of about 2.53 — the soft alignment learned with no frame-level labels.

### Proof sketches

*Theorem 1 (CTC gradient).* Differentiating the log-sum over alignments and collecting terms by (t, k) yields the occupancy posterior; the softmax Jacobian supplies the y_t^k term [1]. *Theorem 2 (RNN-T generalizes CTC).* Under u-independent emissions, summing the lattice forward variable over u telescopes the two-dimensional recurrence into the one-dimensional CTC recurrence [2]. *Theorem 3 (attention coverage).* The softmax normalizes over encoder positions per decoder step, so input-side coverage is unconstrained — the source of skips and repeats [4, 5].

## 6 Limitations

End-to-end ASR is a solved problem only on benchmarks. *Streaming* remains a tax: truly zero-lookahead systems still lag offline counterparts by a relative 10–20%. *Robustness* is brittle — accented speech, children's speech, overlapping talkers, and far-field reverberation degrade performance far more than LibriSpeech suggests. *Long-form* audio breaks attention decoders (Section 4.3) and strains transducers trained on short segments. *Evaluation* itself is suspect: word error rate treats every substitution equally, and test sets are now so clean that a 0.2% absolute gain may measure test-set overfitting. Finally, the largest models [8] are too heavy for on-device deployment, forcing distillation and quantization the literature largely ignores.

---

## 7 Conclusion

The arc from HMM–GMM to the Conformer is the arc from engineered pipelines to learned ones. CTC [1] proved that alignment could be marginalized rather than annotated; the RNN transducer [2] proved that label context could be modeled without sacrificing streamability; attention [4] proved that the lattice itself was optional; the Transformer [5] and Conformer [6] proved that recurrence was optional too; and self-supervised [7] and weakly supervised [8] training proved that even the labels were, to a surprising extent, optional. Each step removed a human-designed component and replaced it with gradient descent over more data.

What remains is a data and deployment problem: robustness to the acoustic long tail, true streaming without accuracy tax, and hearing-aid-scale efficiency.
