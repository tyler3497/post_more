# Certified Adversarial Robustness: Randomized Smoothing, Lipschitz Certificates, and the Robustness–Accuracy Tradeoff

## Abstract

Empirical defenses against adversarial examples have repeatedly collapsed under stronger adaptive attacks, shifting attention to *certified* robustness: provable, per-input guarantees that no perturbation within a norm ball can alter a model's prediction. This article develops three pillars of certified robustness. First, we present **randomized smoothing** [1], which converts any base classifier into a smoothed classifier with a tight ℓ2 certificate derived from the Neyman–Pearson lemma: the certified radius is R = (σ/2)(Φ⁻¹(pA) − Φ⁻¹(pB)), where pA and pB bound the top two class probabilities under Gaussian noise. Second, we treat **Lipschitz-based certificates**, where the global or local Lipschitz constant of the network translates output margins into certified input radii, and survey how spectral normalization and 1-Lipschitz architectures make such bounds practical. Third, we analyze the **robustness–accuracy tradeoff**, from the provable tension identified by Tsipras et al. [4] to the TRADES decomposition of robust error into natural plus boundary error [5], and show how convex relaxations [3] and adversarially trained smoothed classifiers [2] reshape the Pareto frontier. We close with evaluation protocols, fundamental limitations, and open problems.

## 1 Introduction

The discovery that imperceptible perturbations can flip the predictions of state-of-the-art neural networks shattered the complacency of the deep learning community. For a decade, the standard response was an arms race: a new defense was proposed, an adaptive attack broke it, and the cycle repeated. Heuristic defenses — gradient masking, input preprocessing, detection schemes — were systematically dismantled by stronger white-box attacks, most notably the projected gradient descent (PGD) methodology of Madry et al. [6], who framed adversarial training as robust optimization:

$$\min_\theta \; \mathbb{E}_{(x,y)} \left[ \max_{\delta \in \mathcal{B}_\epsilon(x)} \ell(f_\theta(x + \delta), y) \right]$$

While PGD adversarial training remains the strongest *empirical* defense, it offers no proof: the absence of a known attack is not evidence of the absence of all attacks. *Certified* robustness demands more — a mathematical guarantee, attached to each individual input, that the classifier's decision is invariant over an entire perturbation set. Such guarantees decompose naturally into two families:

- **Deterministic certificates**, which propagate sound over-approximations of the reachable activation set through the network (interval bound propagation, convex relaxations [3], Lipschitz bounds), yielding radii that hold with certainty.
- **Probabilistic certificates**, which randomize the classifier itself and certify a radius that holds with high confidence over the randomness of the certification procedure — the randomized smoothing paradigm [1].

Both families pay a price. Certification typically degrades clean accuracy, and the tension between robustness and accuracy is not merely an engineering artifact: it is, in certain settings, provable [4]. Understanding exactly where certificates come from, how tight they are, and what they cost is the subject of this article.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The threat model

We consider a classifier f mapping inputs in ℝᵈ to labels in a finite set of classes. An adversary may add a perturbation δ with ‖δ‖ₚ ≤ ε to an input x. A *certificate* at x is a radius R(x) such that f(x + δ) = f(x) for every δ with ‖δ‖ₚ ≤ R(x). The certified accuracy at radius r is the fraction of test points that are both correctly classified and certified with R(x) ≥ r. Unlike attack-based evaluation, which lower-bounds vulnerability, certification upper-bounds it: a model with high certified accuracy is provably safe against *all* attacks within the certified radius, including attacks not yet invented.

### 2.2 From empirical to provable

Adversarial training [6] optimizes the worst case over the perturbation ball approximately, using PGD to find strong local maxima of the inner problem. It scales to ImageNet and remains the empirical gold standard, but the inner maximization is non-convex and incomplete: PGD may miss the true worst case. Certified methods instead construct a *sound* over-approximation — a superset of all reachable outputs — so that the guarantee holds by construction. The price of soundness is looseness: the over-approximation may dwarf the true reachable set, yielding vacuous radii. The central technical challenge of the field is therefore *tightness*: how close can the certificate come to the true robustness radius?

### 2.3 The Neyman–Pearson foundation

A recurring mathematical engine behind tight certificates is the Neyman–Pearson lemma, which characterizes the most powerful test for distinguishing two hypotheses. In randomized smoothing, certifying robustness at x against a shift by δ reduces to a hypothesis-testing question: given a sample from either N(x, σ²I) or N(x + δ, σ²I), can an adversary's choice of base classifier flip the majority vote? The lemma identifies the worst-case base classifier as a half-space, which makes the robustness of the smoothed classifier exactly computable from the overlap of two shifted Gaussians [1].

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 The smoothed classifier

Given any base classifier f and noise level σ > 0, the *smoothed classifier* g is defined as the majority vote of f under isotropic Gaussian noise:

$$g(x) = \arg\max_{c} \; \mathbb{P}_{\varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2 I)}[f(x + \varepsilon) = c]$$

The definition is striking in its generality: f may be any deterministic or random function, including a deep network, with no assumptions on its architecture, smoothness, or training procedure. All structure needed for the certificate comes from the Gaussian noise, not from f.

> **Theorem (Tight ℓ2 certificate [1]):** Suppose the smoothed classifier g returns class cA at x, and that ℙ(f(x + ε) = cA) ≥ pA while max_{c≠cA} ℙ(f(x + ε) = c) ≤ pB, with pA > pB. Then g(x + δ) = cA for all δ satisfying
>
> $$\|\delta\|_2 < R = \frac{\sigma}{2}\left(\Phi^{-1}(p_A) - \Phi^{-1}(p_B)\right),$$
>
> where Φ⁻¹ is the inverse CDF of the standard normal. Moreover, this radius is *tight*: for every pA, pB, σ, there exists a base classifier f consistent with the bounds for which the guarantee fails at any larger radius.

The proof applies the Neyman–Pearson lemma to the two Gaussian distributions centered at x and x + δ: among all base classifiers with top-class probability at least pA, the one most favorable to the adversary labels a half-space, and the probability mass that half-space assigns under the shifted Gaussian is exactly Φ(Φ⁻¹(pA) − ‖δ‖₂/σ). Setting this above one half and solving for ‖δ‖₂ yields the radius. Tightness follows because the half-space classifier attains the bound.

### 3.2 Monte Carlo certification

The probabilities pA and pB are unknown and must be estimated from samples. Cohen et al. [1] propose a two-stage procedure:

1. **PREDICT:** draw n₀ samples, take the majority class ĉA as the predicted label.
2. **CERTIFY:** draw n samples, count nA votes for ĉA, and compute a lower confidence bound pA via the Clopper–Pearson interval at confidence level 1 − α. If pA > 1/2, output the prediction and radius R = σ·Φ⁻¹(pA) (the pB = 1 − pA simplification); otherwise abstain.

Abstention handles inputs where the smoothed classifier is genuinely uncertain; the certificate holds with probability at least 1 − α over the certification randomness. Certification with n = 100,000 samples per input is expensive but embarrassingly parallel.

A minimal implementation of the radius computation is instructive:

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.stats import norm, binom

def certified_radius(count_A, n_samples, sigma, alpha=0.001):
    """Lower-bound the certified l2 radius via Clopper-Pearson."""
    # one-sided lower confidence bound on P(f(x+eps) = cA)
    pA = binom.ppf(alpha, n_samples, count_A / n_samples) / n_samples
    pA = max(pA, 0.5 + 1e-12)
    return sigma * norm.ppf(pA)

# example: 80,000 of 100,000 noisy votes agree, sigma = 0.25
print(certified_radius(80000, 100000, 0.25))  # ~0.16 certified l2 radius
```

### 3.3 Lipschitz margin certificates

An entirely different route to certification exploits the *Lipschitz constant* of the network. If the logit function f is L-Lipschitz with respect to some norm, i.e. ‖f(x) − f(x′)‖ ≤ L‖x − x′‖ for all inputs, then the classification margin at x directly yields a certified radius. For a multiclass classifier with margin m(x) = f_y(x) − max_{j≠y} f_j(x), a perturbation δ can flip the decision only if it changes some logit by at least m(x)/√2, giving the ℓ2 certificate R(x) = m(x)/(√2·L). The bound is deterministic, requires no sampling, and certifies in a single forward pass — but its quality lives or dies with L.

For a feed-forward network with 1-Lipschitz activations such as ReLU, L is bounded by the product of the spectral norms of the weight matrices. Two design philosophies exploit this:

- **Spectral normalization** constrains each weight matrix during training so its largest singular value stays near 1, keeping the product — and hence L — small.
- **1-Lipschitz architectures** enforce L = 1 by construction, using orthogonal or spectrally normalized layers together with gradient-norm-preserving activations such as GroupSort.

The Lipschitz approach converts the certification problem into a *training* problem: learn a network that is simultaneously accurate and provably smooth.

### 3.4 Convex relaxations and bound propagation

Between pure Lipschitz bounds and exact verification lies the family of *convex relaxations*. Wong and Kolter [3] consider the adversarial polytope — the set of all final-layer activations reachable by norm-bounded perturbations — and bound it by a convex outer approximation whose worst-case loss is a linear program. Crucially, the dual of that LP can itself be represented as a modified backpropagation pass, so the bound is differentiable and can be optimized during training. Interval bound propagation (IBP) is the simplest member of this family: it pushes coordinate-wise lower and upper bounds through each layer using monotonicity of the activations, yielding cheap but loose bounds. Tighter relaxations trade compute for tightness along a well-understood spectrum.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Why the Gaussian certificate is tight — and why tightness matters

The tightness claim separates randomized smoothing from earlier loose guarantees. Prior work bounded the same smoothed classifier using differential privacy or Rényi divergence arguments, yielding radii smaller by constant factors. The Neyman–Pearson argument shows that no analysis using only (pA, pB) can do better: the certificate is optimal *given the information available*. Any improvement must therefore extract more information from f than two scalar probabilities — for instance, the full distribution over classes, or the local geometry of the decision boundary. This information-theoretic framing clarifies the research frontier: tighter certificates require richer statistics of the base classifier.

> **Proof sketch (Neyman–Pearson step):** Fix a shift δ. Among all measurable sets S (the region where the base classifier votes cA) with ℙ_{ε∼N(0,σ²I)}(x + ε ∈ S) ≥ pA, the adversary wants to minimize ℙ(x + δ + ε ∈ S). By Neyman–Pearson, the minimizer is a likelihood-ratio test — a half-space orthogonal to δ. Its shifted probability is Φ(Φ⁻¹(pA) − ‖δ‖₂/σ). Requiring this to exceed the runner-up's best-case probability Φ(Φ⁻¹(pB) + ‖δ‖₂/σ) and solving gives R. ∎

### 4.2 The noise scale σ: a dial between accuracy and certifiability

The parameter σ controls a fundamental tradeoff *within* smoothing itself. Larger σ yields larger certified radii (R scales linearly with σ) but degrades the base classifier's accuracy under noise, since f must classify heavily corrupted inputs. Cohen et al. [1] train the base classifier with Gaussian augmentation at the certification σ; on ImageNet, σ = 0.25 certifies 49% top-1 accuracy at radius 0.5 (127/255 in pixel units) — the first non-trivial certified defense at ImageNet scale. Pushing σ to 1.0 certifies larger radii but at substantially reduced clean accuracy. There is no universally optimal σ; the choice encodes how much accuracy one will sacrifice per unit of certified radius, a microcosm of the broader robustness–accuracy tradeoff.

### 4.3 Adversarial training meets smoothing: SmoothAdv

Randomized smoothing treats the base classifier as a black box, but its performance improves dramatically when the base classifier is *trained* with smoothing in mind. Salman et al. [2] design adversarial attacks adapted to the smoothed classifier — SmoothAdv, which attacks the soft smoothed classifier E[f(x + ε)] via PGD — and use them for adversarial training of the base network. The result established the state of the art for provable ℓ2 defenses on both ImageNet and CIFAR-10 by a significant margin [2]. Conceptually, SmoothAdv closes a loop: the certificate assumes nothing about f, yet the tightest realized certificates come from f's that were optimized against the very noise distribution the certificate uses. Pre-training and semi-supervised learning boost these models further, suggesting that the bottleneck is representation quality under noise rather than the certification machinery.

### 4.4 Lipschitz certificates in depth: from spectral norms to certified training

The Lipschitz certificate R(x) = m(x)/(√2·L) reveals a design equation: certification quality is the ratio of *margin* to *smoothness*. Adversarial training [6] increases margins; spectral regularization decreases L; certified training does both simultaneously. Wong and Kolter's convex outer polytope [3] can be viewed as a layer-wise refinement of the crude product-of-spectral-norms bound: instead of one global L, each layer's perturbation set is bounded tightly, and the composition of tight local bounds beats the loose global one. Their method produced an MNIST convolutional classifier with provably less than 5.8% test error under *any* ℓ∞ attack with ε = 0.1 — a deterministic guarantee no attack can ever overturn. The cost is computational: bounds loosen with depth and propagation is expensive, which is why deterministic certificates have historically struggled to scale beyond MNIST and CIFAR-10 while smoothing reaches ImageNet [1].

### 4.5 The robustness–accuracy tradeoff: provable, empirical, and mitigable

Tsipras et al. [4] proved that the tradeoff can be *inherent*: they constructed a natural data distribution on which any classifier with high standard accuracy necessarily has low adversarial accuracy, and argued that robust and standard classifiers learn fundamentally different features — the former aligned with human perception, the latter exploiting brittle but predictive patterns.

Zhang et al. [5] gave the tradeoff its sharpest theoretical form for the practitioner. They decompose the robust error as

$$\mathcal{R}_{\text{rob}}(f) = \mathcal{R}_{\text{nat}}(f) + \mathcal{R}_{\text{bdy}}(f),$$

the sum of natural error and *boundary error* — the probability that x is correctly classified yet lies within ε of the decision boundary. Using classification-calibrated losses, they derive the tightest uniform upper bound on this decomposition and, inspired by it, propose TRADES, which minimizes the regularized surrogate

$$\min_f \; \mathbb{E}\left[ \mathcal{L}(f(x), y) + \max_{x' \in \mathbb{B}(x,\epsilon)} \mathcal{L}(f(x), f(x')) / \lambda \right].$$

The first term protects natural accuracy; the second pushes the decision boundary away from data, with λ (equivalently 1/β in common implementations) dialing the tradeoff. TRADES won the NeurIPS 2018 Adversarial Vision Challenge by a wide margin — 11.41% mean ℓ2 perturbation distance over the runner-up [5] — and remains a canonical baseline. Importantly, follow-up work has shown the tradeoff is not always intrinsic: with sufficient data, architecture capacity, and training methodology, both standard and robust error can improve together, and much of the observed tradeoff reflects finite data and optimization limitations rather than an information-theoretic wall.

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

### Certified ℓ2 accuracy: the smoothing benchmark

The standard evaluation reports *certified accuracy at radius r*: the fraction of test inputs that are correctly classified by the smoothed classifier and whose certified radius is at least r. Key verified results:

| Method | Dataset | Certified accuracy | Radius | Source |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Randomized smoothing (σ=0.25) | ImageNet | 49% top-1 | ℓ2 0.5 (=127/255) | [1] |
| Randomized smoothing | CIFAR-10 / SVHN | Higher than all competing certified ℓ2 methods | various | [1] |
| SmoothAdv (adversarially trained smoothing) | ImageNet, CIFAR-10 | SOTA over all provable ℓ2 defenses by a significant margin | various | [2] |
| Convex outer polytope | MNIST | <5.8% provable test error | ℓ∞ 0.1 | [3] |

No certified defense had previously been shown feasible on full-resolution ImageNet except smoothing [1], which is why the 49% figure is a landmark: it moved certified robustness from a small-dataset curiosity to a large-scale reality.

### Proof mechanics in practice

Two proof techniques dominate the literature. The **Neyman–Pearson / hypothesis-testing** technique powers smoothing certificates [1] and their extensions to other noise distributions (Laplace for ℓ1, uniform for ℓ∞ variants). The **relaxation / duality** technique powers deterministic certificates [3]: bound the reachable set, dualize the worst-case LP, and backpropagate through the dual network. Both share a common moral: the certificate is only as strong as the *information* the analysis extracts — two scalar probabilities in the basic smoothing theorem, layer-wise activation bounds in the convex approach.

### Evaluation protocols that respect certificates

Certified evaluation differs from attack-based evaluation in ways practitioners must internalize:

1. **Report the certified curve**, i.e., certified accuracy as a function of r, not a single number; different applications tolerate different radii.
2. **Report abstention rates** for smoothing: inputs where pA ≤ 1/2 contribute no certificate, and a method that abstains on half the test set is weaker than its curve suggests.
3. **Use average certified radius (ACR)** alongside the curve for single-number comparisons.
4. **Never substitute attack accuracy for certified accuracy**: PGD accuracy is an upper bound on true robustness, certified accuracy a lower bound; conflating them invalidates the guarantee.
5. **Adaptive attacks still matter** for the *empirical* component: a smoothed classifier's clean accuracy under noise should be stress-tested with SmoothAdv-style attacks [2] to ensure the base classifier is genuinely robust to the noise distribution, not merely lucky.

## 6 Limitations

**The radius–accuracy frontier is harsh.** Every certified method known pays in clean accuracy for its guarantees, and the exchange rate is steep: certifying ImageNet at radius 0.5 costs roughly half the top-1 accuracy of an undefended model [1]. For safety-critical vision, this may be acceptable; for product deployments, it often is not.

**ℓp balls are narrow.** Certificates cover norm balls, but real adversaries use rotations, translations, color shifts, and semantic edits. A certificate against ℓ2 perturbations of radius 0.5 says nothing about a one-degree rotation. Extending certificates to geometric and semantic threat models remains largely open.

**Probabilistic certificates have failure modes.** The smoothing guarantee holds with probability 1 − α over the certification randomness — per input. Across a million inputs, some certificates will be wrong with overwhelming probability unless α is scaled by union bound, which inflates sample complexity. Moreover, the guarantee is about the *smoothed* classifier g, whose predictions can differ from the base classifier's; deploying g means deploying the expensive Monte Carlo pipeline, not the original network.

**Deterministic certificates scale poorly.** Bound propagation loosens with network depth and width, and the computational cost of tight relaxations grows quickly [3]. IBP-style bounds that scale are often too loose to certify meaningful radii on deep networks, while tight bounds are confined to small architectures.

**The tradeoff may be partly an artifact.** As noted in §4.5, evidence increasingly suggests that with more data and better training, the robustness–accuracy tension softens [4][5]. Treating the tradeoff as a law of nature risks under-investing in the data and optimization regimes where it disappears.

## 7 Conclusion

Certified adversarial robustness has matured from a theoretical curiosity into a discipline with sharp mathematical foundations and large-scale demonstrations. Randomized smoothing [1] showed that a tight, scalable ℓ2 certificate follows from nothing more than Gaussian noise and the Neyman–Pearson lemma; Lipschitz and convex-relaxation methods [3] showed that architecture and training can internalize smoothness to yield deterministic guarantees; and the tradeoff literature [4][5] gave us both the bad news — that robustness can provably cost accuracy — and the good news — a principled objective, TRADES, that navigates the frontier optimally. Adversarially trained smoothing [2] and the PGD robust-optimization framework [6] tie the empirical and certified worlds together.

The open frontier is clear: certificates beyond ℓp balls, tighter bounds at ImageNet scale for deterministic methods, cheaper certification procedures, and a deeper understanding of when the robustness–accuracy tradeoff is fundamental versus artifactual. In short: we can now *prove* robustness, but only within narrow threat models and at a real cost in accuracy. For high-stakes deployments, that proof is worth the price — and the price is falling.
