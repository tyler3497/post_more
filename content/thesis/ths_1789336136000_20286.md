# Self-Supervised Representation Learning: Contrastive Objectives, Masked Modeling, and the Scaling Behavior of Foundation Models

## Abstract

Self-supervised representation learning has displaced purely supervised pre-training as the dominant paradigm for building foundation models across vision and language. This thesis unifies the field's three great methodological currents: contrastive objectives that align augmented views while dispersing negatives across a hypersphere; non-contrastive methods that learn without negative samples by architectural asymmetry, redundancy reduction, or predictive bootstrapping; and masked modeling objectives that reconstruct corrupted inputs, from BERT's bidirectional token prediction to masked autoencoders for vision. We formalize the InfoNCE objective and its descendants, analyze the collapse-avoidance mechanisms of BYOL, Barlow Twins, and VICReg, and present the alignment-uniformity and spectral-augmentation-graph theoretical lenses that explain why these objectives succeed. We review scaling laws governing self-supervised pre-training and the emergence of capabilities at scale, then examine empirical benchmarks and the field's persistent limitations: augmentation bias, evaluation protocols, and the gap between discriminative and generative pre-training.

## 1 Intro

The central wager of self-supervised learning is that *structure can be extracted from data without human labels*. Masked language modeling produced BERT and transformed NLP; contrastive methods such as SimCLR, MoCo, and BYOL closed the gap with supervised pre-training on ImageNet; and masked autoencoders showed the reconstruction idea scales to high-capacity vision transformers [1][3][6][7].

Three intellectual threads weave through this history. The first is **contrastive**: learn representations by deciding which pairs of inputs belong together. The second is **predictive without negatives**: learn by bootstrapping a target network or decorrelating features, sidestepping explicit negative samples. The third is **generative-corruptive**: mask part of the input and reconstruct it, forcing the model to build rich contextual representations of what remains. These threads share a deep unity: each defines a *surrogate supervision signal* derived from the data itself, and each can be understood as maximizing some notion of information content under geometric or statistical constraints [4][5].

This thesis surveys all three threads with theoretical precision: background and formal objectives, InfoNCE-style and non-contrastive methods, masked modeling in language and vision, the alignment–uniformity and spectral-augmentation-graph theoretical lenses, scaling laws and emergence, and finally empirical evidence and open limitations.

## 2 Background

Classical *autoencoders* minimized reconstruction error through a bottleneck — a special case of modern masked modeling. *Word2vec*'s skip-gram objective showed that predicting context from words, with negative sampling to keep computation tractable, yields embeddings with striking linear structure. The link between negative sampling and *noise-contrastive estimation* (NCE) seeded modern contrastive learning: turn intractable density estimation into tractable classification between real data and noise [1].

A second seed was the *pretext task* era in computer vision. Researchers trained networks to predict image rotations, solve jigsaw puzzles, or colorize grayscale images, then transferred the features to classification and detection. These tasks worked but were brittle: each encoded the researcher's guess about which invariances mattered. The decisive break came with **instance discrimination**, which treats every training example as its own class and asks the network to recognize augmented versions of the same instance among a sea of others — replacing hand-designed pretext semantics with a generic principle: *representations should be invariant to nuisance transformations and discriminative across instances* [1][2].

The third seed came from *mutual-information maximization*. Contrastive Predictive Coding framed representation learning as estimating a lower bound on the mutual information between an input and its context, using an InfoNCE objective whose optimal critic recovers the density ratio between the joint and the product of marginals. This justified contrastive losses in principled terms — while planting a confusion the field still untangles: whether contrastive learning succeeds *because* it maximizes mutual information, or whether mutual information is merely one convenient bound among several quantities the loss implicitly optimizes [4].

## 3 Methodology

We use unified notation: input space **X** with data distribution P, encoder f: X → R^d, and a *projection head* g applied where the loss is computed (g is discarded after pre-training). A stochastic *augmentation distribution* T generates two views of each input x, a *positive pair*; views of distinct inputs are *negative pairs*.

The canonical **InfoNCE** objective for a positive pair (x, x⁺) and N negatives uses a similarity critic s — cosine similarity of projected embeddings scaled by a temperature τ:

> Theorem: *InfoNCE as mutual-information bound.* With the optimal critic proportional to the pointwise mutual information, the InfoNCE loss satisfies ℒ_InfoNCE ≥ log(N+1) − I(X; X⁺), so minimizing the loss maximizes a lower bound on the mutual information between positive pairs. The bound tightens logarithmically with the number of negatives.

The projection head g is empirically crucial: contrastive learning on g(f(x)) substantially outperforms learning directly on f(x), because g can discard augmentation-sensitive nuisance information that the transferred representation should retain [1].

**Non-contrastive** objectives dispense with negatives. BYOL trains an *online* network to predict the output of a *target* network — an exponential moving average of the online weights — on a differently augmented view; collapse is avoided through predictor asymmetry and the slow-moving target, not repulsion [3]. Barlow Twins drives the cross-correlation matrix of the two views' embeddings toward the identity, balancing invariance against redundancy reduction, while VICReg decomposes the objective into explicit variance, invariance, and covariance terms.

**Masked modeling** corrupts the input by masking tokens or patches and trains an encoder-decoder to reconstruct them: BERT predicts masked tokens with a vocabulary softmax under bidirectional conditioning [6], while MAE encodes only the visible patches of a heavily masked image and reconstructs pixel values with a lightweight decoder [7].

The following Python sketch implements the NT-Xent variant of InfoNCE used by SimCLR, operating on L2-normalized projected embeddings:

```python
import torch
import torch.nn.functional as F

def nt_xent_loss(z, temperature=0.5):
    # z: 2B projected embeddings; rows (2k, 2k+1) are positive pairs
    z = F.normalize(z, dim=1)
    sim = torch.matmul(z, z.t()) / temperature
    batch = z.shape[0]
    # mask out self-similarity on the diagonal
    logits_mask = torch.ones_like(sim) - torch.eye(batch, device=z.device)
    exp_sim = torch.exp(sim) * logits_mask
    log_prob = sim - torch.log(exp_sim.sum(dim=1, keepdim=True))
    pos_idx = torch.arange(batch, device=z.device)
    pos_idx = torch.where(pos_idx % 2 == 0, pos_idx + 1, pos_idx - 1)
    nll = -log_prob[torch.arange(batch), pos_idx]
    return nll.mean()

def train_step(encoder, projector, batch, augment, optimizer, temperature=0.5):
    x1, x2 = augment(batch), augment(batch)
    z = torch.cat([projector(encoder(x1)), projector(encoder(x2))], dim=0)
    loss = nt_xent_loss(z, temperature)
    optimizer.zero_grad()
    loss.backward()
    optimizer.step()
    return loss.item()
```

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Contrastive Objectives: InfoNCE, SimCLR, and MoCo

SimCLR's contribution was as much methodological as algorithmic. By ablating every component of the contrastive pipeline, Chen and colleagues established a recipe now standard: *composition* of augmentations (random crop with color distortion proved essential), a *nonlinear projection head* between representation and loss, large batches supplying hard negatives, and long schedules [1]. With a ResNet-50, SimCLR reached 76.5% top-1 linear accuracy on ImageNet, matching supervised pre-training; fine-tuned on only 1% of labels it reached 85.8% top-5, outperforming a fully supervised AlexNet trained on a hundred times more labels. The linear evaluation protocol — freeze the encoder, train a linear classifier — became the field's common currency.

MoCo reframed contrastive learning as *dictionary look-up*: a query is matched against a queue of keys from recent batches, while the key encoder evolves as a momentum-averaged copy of the query encoder — yielding dictionaries that are *large and consistent* without industrial-scale batches [2]. Its features transferred remarkably, outperforming supervised pre-training on seven detection and segmentation benchmarks across PASCAL VOC and COCO.

Beneath both methods lies the question of what the contrastive loss *computes*. One answer is the **alignment–uniformity decomposition** of Wang and Isola [4]: asymptotically, InfoNCE optimizes two geometric properties of normalized features on the hypersphere — *alignment*, the closeness of positive pairs, and *uniformity*, the evenness of the feature distribution. Both metrics correlate strongly with downstream performance, and directly optimizing them yields representations competitive with full contrastive training. A complementary answer is the **spectral contrastive** framework [5], which models augmentation structure as a graph whose vertices are augmented datapoints and whose edges join augmentations of the same example. The spectral contrastive loss performs an implicit spectral decomposition of the graph's normalized adjacency, and the learned features carry provable linear-probe guarantees whenever ground-truth classes form well-connected subgraphs — the first such analysis for the realistic regime where augmentations of one image are heavily correlated.

### 4.2 Learning Without Negatives: Collapse Avoidance by Design

BYOL's 74.3% linear top-1 on ImageNet with no negative pairs forced the community to reconsider whether repulsion between instances is the engine of contrastive learning or merely one way to prevent a trivial solution [3]. The online network predicts the target network's output across augmented views while the target slowly tracks the online weights via exponential moving average; the predictor head is essential, and the current understanding is that predictor, target, and stop-gradient together implement an *implicit* contrastive dynamic in which the slowly moving target provides a bootstrap signal the online network chases but never collapses onto.

Later methods made collapse-avoidance explicit. **Barlow Twins** drives the cross-correlation matrix of the two views' embeddings toward the identity, enforcing invariance on the diagonal and decorrelation off it. **VICReg** decomposes the objective into variance, invariance, and covariance terms — more predictable under varying augmentation strength, matching contrastive baselines while stable at small batch sizes.

> Theorem: *Variance regularization prevents dimensional collapse.* If every embedding dimension maintains variance above a positive threshold, no non-trivial linear subspace of the embedding space can be constant; combined with an invariance term pulling positive pairs together, the optimum is a full-rank embedding in which augmented views coincide.

Collectively these methods show that the repulsive force of negative sampling can be replaced by *structural* regularization of the embedding space. They are less sensitive to batch size and avoid *false negatives* — pairs drawn as negatives that share a semantic class and whose repulsion damages the representation.

### 4.3 Masked Modeling: From BERT to Masked Autoencoders

Masked language modeling, introduced with BERT, remains the most consequential self-supervised objective ever devised [6]. BERT pre-trains a bidirectional Transformer by masking input tokens and predicting them from the surrounding context, conditioning on both left and right context in every layer — a break from the unidirectional models before it. With one task-specific output layer, BERT reached state of the art on question answering and inference tasks, pushing GLUE to 80.5% and SQuAD v1.1 to 93.2 F1. Its [CLS] pooling convention and fine-tuning recipe became standard infrastructure for a generation of language models.

The masked autoencoder (MAE) translated this idea to vision with two decisive choices [7]: an *asymmetric encoder–decoder* in which the ViT encoder processes only visible patches while a lightweight decoder reconstructs the full image (roughly tripling training speed), and a *very high masking ratio* of 75% — necessary because images are redundant enough that low masking ratios permit cheating by local interpolation. A vanilla ViT-Huge reached 87.8% top-1 on ImageNet with only ImageNet-1K data, transferring better than supervised pre-training. Masked modeling thus unified the modalities under one principle — *predict the missing from the present* — with a generative rather than discriminative objective.

A Haskell sketch of the spectral contrastive objective shows how compactly these ideas can be expressed functionally:

```haskell
-- spectral contrastive loss over one anchor, one positive, and negatives
spectralContrastive :: [Double] -> [Double] -> [[Double]] -> Double
spectralContrastive anchor positive negatives =
  let dot u v = sum (zipWith (*) u v)
      attraction = -2.0 * dot anchor positive
      repulsion = sum (map (\n -> (dot anchor n) ^ 2) negatives)
                  / fromIntegral (length negatives)
  in attraction + repulsion
```

### 4.4 Theoretical Lenses: Geometry and Spectra

Two theoretical frameworks dominate our understanding of contrastive learning, and they are complementary. The **geometric lens** of Wang and Isola treats the normalized embedding space directly [4]. *Alignment* measures how close positive pairs land on the unit hypersphere; *uniformity* measures how evenly the feature distribution spreads, quantified through a pairwise Gaussian kernel minimized when features are maximally dispersed. InfoNCE asymptotically optimizes both, the two scalar metrics predict linear-probe accuracy without labels, and optimizing them directly recovers most of contrastive learning's performance. Much of what contrastive losses accomplish can be redescribed as shaping embedding geometry: pull semantic neighbors together, spread everything else apart.

The **spectral lens** of HaoChen, Wei, Gaidon, and Ma treats the *data* [5]. In the population augmentation graph — vertices are augmented datapoints, edges join augmentations of the same example — the top eigenvectors of the normalized adjacency cluster same-class augmentations whenever classes form well-connected subgraphs. The spectral contrastive loss, attraction minus a squared negative-pair term, is exactly the objective whose minimizers recover these eigenvectors up to rotation, and linear probing succeeds because they are approximately piecewise constant on class subgraphs.

> Theorem: *Spectral recovery under the augmentation graph.* Let the population augmentation graph have normalized adjacency with eigengap γ separating the top-k eigenspace from the remainder. Then minimizers of the spectral contrastive loss recover the top-k eigenspace up to rotation, and linear classification on the learned features errs at most on the order of the graph's cross-class conductance — vanishing when classes are well-connected subgraphs.

Together, the lenses explain complementary facets: geometry describes the *shape* the loss imposes on embeddings, spectra the *structure* of the data that makes that shape useful — and both reveal that augmentations define the invariances a representation learns.

### 4.5 Scaling Behavior and the Road to Foundation Models

Kaplan and colleagues showed that language-model cross-entropy follows smooth power laws in model size, dataset size, and compute, spanning more than seven orders of magnitude, with architectural details contributing only second-order effects [8]. The qualitative picture transfers to self-supervision: masked autoencoders exhibit *promising scaling behavior*, with larger ViT variants gaining more from MAE pre-training and transfer improving monotonically with capacity [7].

Scale does not merely improve metrics; it changes qualitative behavior.

## 5 Empirical/Proofs

The empirical record is unusually clean because the *linear evaluation protocol* — freeze the encoder, train a linear classifier — provides a common yardstick. The table below summarizes landmark ImageNet results, all obtained without labels during pre-training:

| Method | Objective | Negatives | Key mechanism | Linear top-1 (ResNet-50) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| SimCLR [1] | NT-Xent / InfoNCE | In-batch | Projection head, strong augmentation, large batch | 76.5% |
| MoCo v2 [2] | InfoNCE | Queue dictionary | Momentum encoder, consistent dictionary | 71.1% |
| BYOL [3] | Prediction MSE | None | EMA target, predictor, stop-gradient | 74.3% |
| Barlow Twins | Cross-correlation | None | Redundancy reduction toward identity | 73.2% |
| MAE [7] | Pixel reconstruction | None | 75% masking, asymmetric encoder-decoder | 83.6% (ViT-B) |

Several patterns deserve emphasis. First, the gap between contrastive and non-contrastive methods on linear evaluation is small, supporting the view that collapse-avoidance mechanisms are interchangeable means to the same geometric end. Second, transfer to dense tasks tells a different story: MoCo surpassed supervised pre-training on seven detection and segmentation benchmarks [2], and MAE transferred better than supervised pre-training as well [7], suggesting self-supervised features capture spatial structure that label supervision discards. Third, masked modeling scales differently: MAE's gains grow with model size, with ViT-Huge reaching 87.8% — the best among methods using only ImageNet-1K — while contrastive methods plateau earlier, consistent with generative objectives exploiting capacity more effectively at scale.

The strongest *proofs* are the spectral guarantees [5] and the asymptotic alignment–uniformity analysis [4], stated as theorems above. Empirically, Wang and Isola verified that their two metrics track downstream accuracy and that directly optimizing them matches or exceeds contrastive training — prescriptive theory. The remaining frontier is scaling: isoFLOP studies of self-supervised objectives remain sparse, and compute-optimal allocation between model size, data, and objective complexity for vision is an open measurement.

## 6 Limitations

For all its successes, self-supervised learning carries structural limitations the literature has only begun to address.

1. **Augmentation bias.** Contrastive and non-contrastive methods inherit invariances entirely from the augmentation distribution. Features become blind to whatever augmentations destroy — color, orientation, fine-grained texture — desirable for object classification but harmful for tasks depending on those attributes. The augmentation graph is a modeling choice masquerading as a neutral procedure [5].

2. **The false-negative problem.** Contrastive objectives repel all non-paired instances, including those sharing the anchor's semantic class. In datasets with few classes or long-tailed distributions, many negatives are false, and the repulsive term damages class structure. Non-contrastive methods avoid this failure mode entirely [3].

3. **Evaluation fragility.** Linear probing measures only linear separability of frozen features, which need not correlate with fine-tuning, few-shot transfer, or robustness. The field's reliance on ImageNet linear accuracy as a leaderboard metric risks overfitting methods to one protocol.

4. **Generative–discriminative gap.** Masked modeling optimizes reconstruction, not discrimination. MAE features excel at transfer but trail contrastive features on linear probing at matched sizes, and the relationship between reconstruction quality and downstream utility is poorly understood [7].

5. **Compute and data concentration.** Frontier pre-training demands web-scale data and industrial compute. Scaling laws [8] make this concentration predictable and self-reinforcing, directing investment toward scale over algorithmic efficiency.

6. **Security and provenance.** Unlabeled web-scale datasets are vulnerable to poisoning, and self-supervised objectives offer no label-based sanity check. Backdoors embedded during pre-training can survive fine-tuning — a risk that grows with reliance on downloaded checkpoints.

7. **Theoretical incompleteness.** The spectral and geometric analyses assume well-connected subgraphs, infinite negatives, and asymptotic regimes; finite-sample guarantees and a unified account of masked-modeling scaling remain open.

## 7 Conclusion

Self-supervised representation learning has matured from a collection of clever pretext tasks into a principled discipline with three pillars: contrastive objectives that shape embedding geometry through attraction and repulsion [1][2][4][5]; non-contrastive methods achieving the same geometry through architectural asymmetry and statistical regularization [3]; and masked modeling objectives that reconstruct corrupted inputs to build bidirectional contextual representations [6][7]. The lenses of alignment, uniformity, and spectral decomposition explain *why* these objectives work, and scaling laws explain *how far* they can go [8].

The trajectory points toward unification. Contrastive, non-contrastive, and masked objectives are increasingly understood as different estimators of related geometric and spectral quantities, and hybrid objectives combining discriminative and generative signals are an active frontier. The deeper open questions concern *what to be invariant to* — the augmentation design problem — and *evaluation*: whether linear probing measures the capabilities that matter. As self-supervised pre-training becomes the default first step of model development, answering these questions will determine not just benchmark numbers but the structure of the foundation models on which the rest of the field builds.
