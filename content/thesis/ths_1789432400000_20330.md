# Moiré Superlattices in Twisted Bilayer Graphene: Flat Bands, Correlated Insulators, and Superconductivity

## Abstract

Magic-angle twisted bilayer graphene (MATBG) hosts some of the richest correlated-electron physics observed in any solid-state platform: bands flattened to a few meV, interaction-driven insulators at every integer filling of the moiré unit cell, and superconducting domes with critical temperatures far above the BCS expectation for the bare carrier density [2][3]. This thesis develops the Bistritzer–MacDonald continuum theory from the moiré geometry of two rotated honeycomb lattices, derives the chiral-limit proof of an infinite magic-angle sequence [4], and analyzes the fragile topology that obstructs symmetric Wannier localization [5][6]. It reviews the experimental arc from transport signatures of correlated insulation and superconductivity through scanning-tunneling microscopy and quasiparticle interference to orbital Chern magnets [7][9][10], and examines how twist-angle disorder and dielectric screening reshape the phase diagram [8]. The work closes by identifying the outstanding problems — pairing mechanism, reproducibility, and scalable control — that separate MATBG from a true materials-design paradigm.

---

## 1 Introduction

Few predictions in condensed-matter physics have been vindicated as dramatically as the *magic angle* of twisted bilayer graphene (TBG). In 2011, Bistritzer and MacDonald showed that two graphene sheets, rotated by a relative twist of roughly one degree, develop an electronic structure in which the renormalized Dirac velocity vanishes and two nearly flat bands emerge at charge neutrality [1]. Seven years later, the prediction collided with experiment: at a twist angle near 1.1°, transport measurements revealed first a correlated insulator at half-filling of the conduction band [2] and then superconductivity with a critical temperature of 1.7 K in a purely carbon system with carrier densities of order 10^11 cm^-2 [3].

What makes MATBG extraordinary is not any single phase but the *density* of phenomena compressed into a single tunable parameter — the twist angle — and a single control knob — electrostatic gating. Within a few meV of bandwidth, the system exhibits:

- **Mott-like correlated insulators** at every integer filling of the flat bands, breaking spin, valley, or both symmetries [2][10];
- **Superconducting domes** flanking several of these insulators, reminiscent of the cuprate and heavy-fermion phase diagrams [3][8];
- **Orbital ferromagnets and Chern insulators** with quantized anomalous Hall conductance at odd integer fillings [9][10];
- **Cascade transitions** in which flavor symmetry is successively restored as carriers are added, seen directly in local compressibility [10];
- **Fragile topology** in the single-particle band structure, obstructing any description in terms of symmetric localized orbitals [5][6].

This thesis presents a self-contained account of the theory and experiment of MATBG, from the moiré geometry that generates the superlattice, through the continuum model and its exactly solvable chiral limit, to the interacting phases and the probes — transport, scanning tunneling microscopy (STM), and quasiparticle interference (QPI) — that established them.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Graphene and its Dirac quasiparticles

Monolayer graphene is a honeycomb lattice of carbon atoms whose low-energy excitations are massless Dirac fermions with velocity v_F ≈ 10^6 m/s, described near each valley K or K' by the Hamiltonian H = v_F σ · k, where σ is the sublattice Pauli vector. Bilayer graphene in the natural Bernal (AB) stacking is a parabolic-band semimetal. The essential move of twistronics is to abandon crystallographic stacking altogether: rotating one layer by an angle θ generates a long-wavelength interference pattern — the *moiré superlattice* — with period

L_M = a / (2 sin(θ/2)) ≈ a/θ,

where a ≈ 0.246 nm is the graphene lattice constant. At θ ≈ 1.1°, the moiré period is L_M ≈ 13 nm, more than fifty times the atomic spacing, and the moiré unit cell contains on the order of ten thousand atoms [1].

### 2.2 The moiré Brillouin zone

Because the two layers' Dirac cones are displaced in momentum space by the moiré wavevectors q_j (j = 1, 2, 3), interlayer tunneling hybridizes states across a mini-Brillouin zone (mBZ) whose area shrinks as θ^2. The characteristic energy scale of the superlattice is E_M = ℏv_F k_θ with k_θ = 2k_D sin(θ/2), where k_D is the monolayer Dirac momentum. The dimensionless ratio

α = w / (ℏv_F k_θ),

with w the interlayer tunneling scale, controls the entire single-particle problem. As θ decreases, α grows, and perturbation theory in α breaks down — the regime where flat bands appear [1][4].

> **Theorem (Bistritzer–MacDonald, 2011):** There exists a discrete sequence of twist angles — the magic angles — at which the renormalized Dirac velocity v_F^* of the moiré Dirac points vanishes, and the two bands nearest charge neutrality become approximately flat across the entire mini-Brillouin zone [1].

### 2.3 Energy scales: why interactions win

The magic of the magic angle is an inversion of energy scales. The flat-band bandwidth W collapses to 5–10 meV while the Coulomb scale set by the moiré period,

U ≈ e^2 / (4πεε_0 L_M) ≈ 20 meV,

stays large. The ratio U/W ≫ 1 places MATBG firmly in the strong-correlation regime, even though the microscopic interactions are nothing more exotic than the ordinary Coulomb repulsion between electrons in carbon p_z orbitals [2][7]. The kinetic energy is quenched not by chemistry but by *geometry*.

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 Constructing the continuum model

The Bistritzer–MacDonald (BM) model is derived by expanding each layer's tight-binding Hamiltonian around its Dirac points and treating interlayer tunneling as a spatially periodic hopping with the moiré periodicity [1]. In valley K, the 4×4 Hamiltonian reads

H_BM = [ -iℏv_F σ_{-θ/2} · ∇,   T(r) ; T†(r),  -iℏv_F σ_{+θ/2} · ∇ ],

where the interlayer coupling

T(r) = Σ_j T_j exp(-iq_j · r)

sums over three momentum transfers q_j related by threefold rotation, with matrices T_j encoding the sublattice structure of AA- and AB-stacked regions. The model is diagonalized in a plane-wave basis truncated at a momentum cutoff; convergence requires keeping several hundred reciprocal lattice vectors at the first magic angle. The following Python sketch implements the essential geometry and a minimal BM diagonalization:

```python
import numpy as np

# Moiré geometry and the dimensionless coupling alpha
a = 0.246          # graphene lattice constant (nm)
hbar_vF = 0.6582   # eV*nm  (hbar * vF)
w_AB = 0.110        # interlayer tunneling, AB regions (eV)
theta = np.deg2rad(1.08)          # twist angle
k_D = 4*np.pi/(3*a)               # monolayer Dirac momentum (1/nm)
k_theta = 2*k_D*np.sin(theta/2)   # moiré momentum scale
L_M = a/(2*np.sin(theta/2))       # moiré period (nm)
alpha = w_AB/(hbar_vF*k_theta)
print(f"L_M = {L_M:.1f} nm, alpha = {alpha:.3f}")

# Minimal BM Hamiltonian on a truncated plane-wave basis
def bm_block(k, Gvecs, qvecs, Tj):
    """Assemble the valley-K Bistritzer-MacDonald matrix."""
    nG = len(Gvecs)
    H = np.zeros((4*nG, 4*nG), dtype=complex)
    sx = np.array([[0,1],[1,0]]); sy = np.array([[0,-1j],[1j,0]])
    for i, G in enumerate(Gvecs):
        q = k + G
        H[2*i:2*i+2, 2*i:2*i+2] = hbar_vF*(sx*q[0]+sy*q[1])  # top layer
        H[2*nG+2*i:2*nG+2*i+2, 2*nG+2*i:2*nG+2*i+2] = \
            hbar_vF*(sx*q[0]-sy*q[1])                        # bottom layer (rotated)
    for i, G in enumerate(Gvecs):
        for j, (qj, Tm) in enumerate(zip(qvecs, Tj)):
            # interlayer coupling shifts momentum by moire wavevectors
            tgt = np.argmin([np.linalg.norm(G - qj - Gp) for Gp in Gvecs])
            H[2*i:2*i+2, 2*nG+2*tgt:2*nG+2*tgt+2] += Tm
    return H + H.conj().T - np.diag(np.diag(H))  # symmetrize couplings
```

Diagonalizing H_BM along high-symmetry lines of the mBZ yields the celebrated flat bands: two bands per spin per valley pinned near zero energy, separated from remote bands by single-particle gaps of tens of meV.

### 3.2 The chiral limit and exact flat bands

A decisive theoretical advance came from Tarnopolsky, Kruchkov, and Vishwanath, who studied the *chiral limit* w_AA → 0, in which tunneling in AA-stacked regions is switched off while AB/BA tunneling is kept [4].

> **Theorem (Tarnopolsky–Kruchkov–Vishwanath, 2019):** In the chiral limit of the BM model, magic angles form an infinite sequence parameterized by α with asymptotic spacing Δα ≈ 3/2. At each magic α, the entire lowest band is exactly flat, the Dirac velocity vanishes, and the gap to remote bands is simultaneously maximized [4].

The first magic angle, α ≈ 0.586 (θ ≈ 1.09°), is unusually robust: perturbation theory in α up to eighth order reproduces the numerical BM result, and lattice relaxation — which suppresses w_AA relative to w_AB in real samples — pushes the physical system toward the chiral limit, justifying the model's parameters [4].

```haskell
-- Magic-angle sequence in the chiral limit (Tarnopolsky-Kruchkov-Vishwanath)
-- alpha_1 ~= 0.586, with asymptotic spacing Delta alpha ~= 3/2
data MagicAngle = MagicAngle { alphaN :: Double, thetaDeg :: Double }
  deriving Show

magicAngles :: [MagicAngle]
magicAngles = zipWith mk [1..] alphas
  where
    alphas  = 0.586 : map (+ 1.5) alphas   -- asymptotic Delta alpha = 3/2
    mk n a  = MagicAngle a (1.09 * 0.586 / a)  -- theta scales as 1/alpha

firstMagic :: MagicAngle
firstMagic = head magicAngles   -- MagicAngle 0.586, theta ~= 1.09 deg
```

### 3.3 Projected interacting models

With the single-particle basis in hand, the standard methodology projects the dual-gated Coulomb interaction into the flat-band manifold, yielding an extended Hubbard-like model with nontrivial form factors inherited from the Bloch wavefunctions' sublattice and layer polarization.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Renormalized magic angles and lattice relaxation

Real devices deviate from the idealized rigid-lattice BM model in one crucial respect: the lattice *relaxes*.

A complementary microscopic check is the atomistic tight-binding model, whose interlayer hopping decays exponentially with distance:

```rust
/// Slater-Koster interlayer hopping for twisted bilayer graphene.
/// `dr` is the in-plane displacement, `dz` the layer separation (nm).
fn interlayer_hopping(dr: f64, dz: f64) -> f64 {
    let r = (dr * dr + dz * dz).sqrt();
    let (v_pp_pi, v_pp_sigma) = (-2.7, 0.48); // eV
    let decay = 2.218;                        // 1/Angstrom scale
    let cos2 = (dz / r).powi(2);
    (v_pp_pi * (1.0 - cos2) + v_pp_sigma * cos2)
        * (-decay * (r * 10.0 - 3.35)).exp()
}
// Summed over ~10^4 atoms per moire cell at the magic angle,
// this reproduces the BM flat bands and validates the continuum model.
```

### 4.2 Fragile topology and Wilson-loop winding

Perhaps the deepest single-particle insight is that the flat bands are *topological* — but in a sense that had to be invented for the occasion.

The topology is dubbed *fragile* because the obstruction evaporates once additional trivial bands are admixed — unlike stable topological phases, it is not robust to adding bands.

1. **No local Hubbard description exists** with all symmetries manifest; any tight-binding model must either break a symmetry or include extra orbitals [5].
2. **Wilson-loop winding** forces the charge centers to wind across the zone, a gauge-invariant fingerprint visible in the single-particle spectrum.
3. Breaking C_2z with a perpendicular electric field or substrate alignment gaps the Dirac points and converts the fragile obstruction into genuine Chern bands — the microscopic origin of the observed orbital Chern insulators [6][9].

> **Theorem (Po–Zou–Vishwanath–Bernevig, 2018):** The flat bands of magic-angle TBG admit no representation in terms of symmetric localized Wannier orbitals; the obstruction is a fragile topological invariant protected by C_2z T symmetry, diagnosed by nontrivial Wilson-loop winding [5].

### 4.3 Correlated insulators at integer fillings

Each moiré unit cell's flat-band manifold hosts 8 electron states (2 spins × 2 valleys × 2 bands).

| Filling ν | Ground state character | Experimental signature |
|---|---|---|
| ±4 | Single-particle band insulators | Resistive peaks, no symmetry breaking |
| ±3 | Spin- and valley-polarized; orbital ferromagnet | Hysteretic anomalous Hall; Chern insulator with C = ±1 [9][10] |
| ±2 | "Half-filling" Mott-like insulator | Strongest resistive peak; gap ~ meV; resets Hall density [2][10] |
| ±1 | Flavor-polarized insulator | Weaker resistive peak; cascade precursor [10] |
| 0 | Dirac semimetal (single particle) | Minimum conductivity; Landau fans from neutrality |

The ν = ±2 insulator discovered by Cao et al.

At odd fillings, time-reversal symmetry breaks spontaneously through *valley polarization*: electrons preferentially occupy one valley, generating orbital magnetic moments that couple to an out-of-plane field.

### 4.4 Superconducting domes and the pairing puzzle

Superconductivity appears as domes in the temperature–doping plane, typically flanking the ν = ±2 correlated insulators and sometimes the ν = 0 region, with maximum T_c of 1.7 K in the original devices [3] and up to ~3 K in devices with engineered screening [8].

- **Dome shape:** T_c peaks at finite doping away from the insulator and falls toward both the insulator and the overdoped metal [3][10].
- **Pseudogap-like features:** STM tunneling spectra show V-shaped gaps persisting above T_c in some devices [7].
- **Proximity to magnetism:** superconductivity borders flavor-polarized and orbital-magnetic phases [9][10].
- **Anomalous critical fields:** in-plane upper critical fields exceeding the Pauli paramagnetic limit, hinting at spin-triplet or valley-singlet pairing [3].

Yet the pairing mechanism remains fiercely contested.

1. *All-electronic mechanisms* — spin- or valley-fluctuation-mediated pairing near the flavor-polarization quantum critical points;
2. *Electron–phonon coupling* — enhanced by the flat bands' huge density of states, though bare phonon estimates underpredict T_c;
3. *Skyrmion-mediated pairing* — topological textures of the flavor pseudospin acting as the pairing glue, a mechanism with no analog in conventional superconductors.

The fragile topology sharpens the puzzle: any pairing theory must reckon with Bloch states that cannot be localized, which invalidates naive real-space pairing pictures and forces momentum-space formulations that respect the Wilson-loop winding [5][6].

### 4.5 Dielectric screening and twist-angle disorder

Two environmental factors dominate device-to-device variability.

**Twist-angle disorder** is the more pernicious factor. The magic-angle condition is sharp — the flat-band width grows rapidly with detuning — and real devices exhibit twist variations of order 0.1° across microns, comparable to the width of the magic-angle window itself. Nano-SQUID magnetometry maps reveal a patchwork of local twist angles, with superconductivity surviving only in the flattest puddles. This disorder explains the field's reproducibility crisis: two devices with the same *nominal* angle can sit in entirely different regions of the phase diagram.

The cascade dynamics of flavor polarization can be formalized as a discrete transition system over filling and symmetry-breaking order:

```tla+
--------------------------- MODULE FlavorCascade ---------------------------
EXTENDS Integers, FiniteSets
CONSTANTS Fillings  \* {-4, -3, -2, -1, 0, 1, 2, 3, 4}
VARIABLES nu,        \* current integer filling
          polarized  \* set of broken flavor symmetries

TypeOK == /\ nu \in Fillings
          /\ polarized \subseteq {"spin", "valley"}

Init == /\ nu = 0
        /\ polarized = {}

Dope(dn) == /\ nu + dn \in Fillings
            /\ nu' = nu + dn
            /\ polarized' = IF nu' % 2 = 0
                             THEN polarized \cup {"spin", "valley"}
                             ELSE polarized
            \* even fillings reset Hall density: flavors fully polarize

Next == \E dn \in {-1, 1} : Dope(dn)
Spec == Init /\ [][Next]_<<nu, polarized>>
=============================================================================
```

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 Scanning tunneling microscopy and spectroscopy

STM provides the most direct real-space view of the flat bands. Kerelsky et al. mapped the local density of states (LDOS) across the moiré unit cell at the magic angle and found the low-energy spectral weight concentrated on AA-stacked regions, where two van Hove singularities appear split by ~55 meV — a direct image of the flat bands' real-space structure [7]. The extracted on-site Coulomb energy exceeds the bandwidth by a large factor, confirming the U/W ≫ 1 hierarchy inferred from transport. Doping-dependent spectroscopy shows the van Hove peaks splitting and shifting with gate voltage in a way no single-particle model reproduces: the *spectrum itself* is renormalized by interactions, with gaps opening at integer fillings uniformly across the sample [7].

### 5.2 Quasiparticle interference

Fourier-transform STM (quasiparticle interference imaging) maps the momentum-space structure of the flat bands by analyzing standing-wave patterns around defects. QPI measurements confirm the mini-Brillouin-zone folding, locate the residual Dirac points, and — critically — show that the Fermi surface reconstructs at each integer filling, consistent with the cascade picture in which flavors depopulate one by one [7][10]. The QPI-derived dispersion matches BM-model calculations only after including interaction-induced band renormalization, closing the loop between the continuum theory and the microscope.

### 5.4 Orbital Chern magnets

The observation of a quantized anomalous Hall effect at ν = +3 — Hall conductance precisely e^2/h at zero magnetic field — is arguably the sharpest proof that interactions and topology conspire in MATBG [9][10]. The mechanism is now understood: valley polarization breaks time reversal, the fragile topology of the parent bands converts into a nonzero Chern number for the occupied valley-projected bands, and the resulting orbital magnetization is hysteretic. This is *quantum anomalous Hall physics without spin-orbit coupling*, driven entirely by Coulomb interactions in a carbon lattice — a phenomenon with no precedent [6][9].

## 6 Limitations

The field's triumphs should not obscure its fragility:

1. **Reproducibility.** Twist-angle disorder of ~0.1° across a device is comparable to the magic-angle window; nominally identical devices exhibit different phase diagrams. Until twist control improves by an order of magnitude, MATBG remains a physics laboratory, not a materials platform.
2. **Pairing mechanism.** No consensus exists on the superconducting glue. Phononic, fluctuation-mediated, and topological mechanisms all reproduce *some* observables and fail on others; phase-sensitive probes of the order parameter are still missing.
3. **Theory of the cascade.** While strong-coupling expansions capture integer-filling insulators, the metallic cascade regime — with its Dirac revivals and isospin transitions — lacks a controlled analytical theory at finite doping.
4. **Fragile topology vs. interactions.** Most many-body treatments project interactions onto bands while ignoring the Wannier obstruction's constraints on the interaction vertices themselves; a fully consistent interacting theory of fragile topological bands is still being built [5].
5. **Scalability.** Exfoliation-based tear-and-stack fabrication produces micron-scale devices one at a time. Wafer-scale growth of precisely twisted bilayers is an unsolved materials challenge.
6. **Remote-band effects.** The projected flat-band models discard remote bands whose virtual excitations renormalize interactions; quantitative T_c predictions remain unreliable.

## 7 Conclusion

Twisted bilayer graphene at the magic angle condenses the grand themes of modern condensed-matter physics — band topology, strong correlations, unconventional superconductivity, and quantum magnetism — into a single sheet of carbon, tunable in situ by voltage. The Bistritzer–MacDonald continuum model [1] and its exactly solvable chiral limit [4] provide a rare case where the single-particle problem is under essentially complete analytical control, including its fragile topological obstruction [5][6]. On this foundation, experiment has built an edifice of correlated insulators [2], superconducting domes [3][8], and orbital Chern magnets [9][10], imaged down to the atomic scale [7].

The central open question is constructive: can the principles of twistronics — geometric quenching of kinetic energy, topological band engineering, interaction-driven symmetry breaking — be elevated from a delicate laboratory phenomenon to a *design paradigm* for quantum materials? Answering it will require simultaneous advances in twist-angle metrology, scalable fabrication, and the many-body theory of fragile topological bands. If those arrive, the moiré superlattice may be remembered not as a curiosity of carbon, but as the first entry in a catalog of designer quantum matter.
