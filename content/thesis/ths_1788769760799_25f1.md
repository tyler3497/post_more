# Probabilistic Programming: Measure-Theoretic Semantics, Hamiltonian Monte Carlo Inference, and Variational Guides

## Abstract

Probabilistic programming languages promise to decouple *modeling* from *inference*: a program's executions denote a probability distribution, and a generic engine performs Bayesian inference over it. This thesis unifies three strands that make this vision rigorous. First, a *measure-theoretic denotational semantics* for a higher-order calculus with continuous distributions and soft conditioning, grounded in the Giry probability monad, with soundness and adequacy theorems validating compiler transformations and sequential Monte Carlo [2][3]. Second, *Hamiltonian Monte Carlo* as geometry-aware MCMC: the posterior is lifted to phase space, evolved under Hamiltonian flow with a symplectic leapfrog integrator, and sampled via the adaptive No-U-Turn criterion [4][7]. Third, *variational guides* as programs denoting approximating families, optimized through the evidence lower bound with reparameterization and score-function gradients [5][6]. We argue that probabilistic programming is a semantic discipline: one denotation admits multiple provably sound inference strategies, and choosing among them is an engineering decision grounded in the model's geometry.

## 1 Introduction

The central abstraction of probabilistic programming is deceptively simple: a *probabilistic program* is an ordinary program whose evaluation involves random choice, and whose executions therefore induce a probability distribution over return values [8]. Conditioning this distribution on observed data — via `observe` statements or soft `factor` scores — yields the Bayesian posterior, and *inference* is the problem of characterizing that posterior. Two families of inference dominate: asymptotically exact *Markov chain Monte Carlo*, exemplified by Hamiltonian Monte Carlo and its adaptive No-U-Turn variant as deployed in Stan [1][4]; and *variational inference*, which casts posterior approximation as optimization over a parametric family of *guide* distributions, popularized by Pyro [5] and the variational autoencoder [6].

What justifies this enterprise mathematically is not obvious. A program that samples from a continuous Gaussian, scores its trace with an unnormalized density, and returns a higher-order function must still *denote* something coherent. The naive set-theoretic interpretation collapses: the category **Meas** of measurable spaces is not Cartesian closed, so higher-order functions and continuous distributions do not naively coexist [2]. Resolving this tension required genuine mathematics — quasi-Borel spaces, s-finite kernels, and domain-theoretic extensions [3] — which recover a compositional semantics in which conditioning, sampling, and scoring all have precise meaning.

Section 2 reviews the measure-theoretic background and language landscape. Section 3 develops the methodology: a core calculus, its Giry-monad denotation, Hamiltonian dynamics, and the variational objective. Section 4 covers commutativity laws, symplectic integrators, the No-U-Turn criterion, and variational guides. Section 5 gives formal arguments and empirical comparisons; Sections 6 and 7 discuss limitations and conclude.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Measure-theoretic probability

A **measurable space** is a pair $(X, \Sigma_X)$ where $\Sigma_X$ is a $\sigma$-algebra of subsets of $X$. A **probability measure** $\mu$ assigns $\mu(A) \in [0,1]$ to each $A \in \Sigma_X$ with $\mu(X) = 1$ and countable additivity. A **Markov kernel** $k: X \rightsquigarrow Y$ assigns to each $x \in X$ a probability measure $k(x)$ on $Y$ such that $x \mapsto k(x)(B)$ is measurable for each measurable $B$. Kernels compose by integration:

$$ (k_2 \circ k_1)(x)(C) = \int_Y k_2(y)(C)\, k_1(x)(dy), $$

and this composition is associative — the Chapman–Kolmogorov equations. The **Giry monad** $P$ on measurable spaces sends $X$ to the space $PX$ of probability measures on $X$, with unit $\eta(x) = \delta_x$ (Dirac measure) and bind given by kernel composition. Sampling is unit; sequencing random computations is bind; *this is the entire categorical content of a probabilistic program* [2].

Bayes' theorem itself is conditioning of measures. Given a prior $\mu$ on parameters $\Theta$ and a likelihood kernel $k: \Theta \rightsquigarrow \mathcal{X}$, the joint is $\mu \otimes k$ and the posterior given observation $x$ is characterized by the disintegration

$$ p(\theta \mid x) \propto p(x \mid \theta)\, p(\theta), $$

which exists under mild regularity (standard Borel spaces) via the **disintegration theorem** [2]. Probabilistic programs generalize this: the program denotes an *unnormalized* measure $\gamma$ on traces, and inference targets $\pi = \gamma / \gamma(\mathcal{T})$, the normalized posterior.

### 2.2 A brief history of probabilistic languages

Early systems such as BUGS and JAGS restricted users to directed graphical models. *Church* introduced universal probabilistic programming: arbitrary recursion and higher-order functions interpreted via MCMC over program traces. *Stan* took the opposite, pragmatic route: a program imperatively defines a log-density function over continuous parameters, and inference is performed by gradient-based methods — principally the No-U-Turn sampler, an adaptive Hamiltonian Monte Carlo, plus variational and optimization modes [1]. *Anglican* and *Venture* explored particle-based inference with higher-order features, while *Pyro* embedded probabilistic programming in Python on top of PyTorch, making variational guides first-class programs optimized by stochastic gradient descent [5]. The tutorial of van de Meent et al. [8] surveys this landscape and its unifying mathematical themes.

### 2.3 Why semantics matters for inference

Inference algorithms manipulate program executions: sequential Monte Carlo resamples particles mid-execution; trace MCMC proposes local changes to random choices; variational methods differentiate through sampling. Each transformation is *sound* only if it preserves the denoted measure. Ścibior et al. [3] went further, validating sequential Monte Carlo and trace MCMC as *denotationally correct* program transformations. Without this foundation, "inference" is heuristics over syntax; with it, inference is certified computation over measures.

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 A core probabilistic calculus

We work with an idealized higher-order language $\Lambda_p$:

```
t ::= x | λx.t | t s | return t | sample d | score t | ...
```

Here `sample d` draws from a primitive continuous distribution $d$ (e.g., Gaussian, uniform), and `score t` multiplies the current trace weight by $\exp(t)$, implementing *soft conditioning* [2]. Hard conditioning `observe(d, v)` is recovered as `score(density d v)`. The operational semantics is a stochastic transition system over configurations $\langle t, w, s \rangle$: term, accumulated weight, and random seed. The denotational semantics interprets types as quasi-Borel spaces and terms as **s-finite kernels** (countable sums of finite kernels), handling the fact that `score` introduces unnormalized measures [2][3]:

$$ \llbracket \Gamma \vdash t : \tau \rrbracket : \llbracket \Gamma \rrbracket \rightsquigarrow \llbracket \tau \rrbracket $$

The Giry-style monadic structure appears concretely in Haskell:

```haskell
-- The probability monad: distributions as sampling functions
newtype Prob a = Prob { runProb :: StdGen -> (a, StdGen) }

instance Monad Prob where
  return x = Prob $ \g -> (x, g)
  (Prob m) >>= f = Prob $ \g ->
    let (x, g') = m g in runProb (f x) g'

-- Conditioning via scoring: the unnormalized measure
score :: Double -> Prob ()
score w = Prob $ \g -> ((), g)  -- weight tracked in the trace
```

Real systems refine this: Stan compiles a program to a log-density and its gradient via reverse-mode autodiff [1]; Pyro reifies traces manipulated by *effect handlers* (Poutine) [5].

### 3.2 Hamiltonian Monte Carlo as geometric inference

HMC targets a posterior $\pi(\theta) \propto \exp(-U(\theta))$ where $U(\theta) = -\log \pi(\theta)$ is the *potential energy*. It augments the state with momentum $r \sim \mathcal{N}(0, M)$, defining the Hamiltonian $H(\theta, r) = U(\theta) + \tfrac{1}{2} r^\top M^{-1} r$. Hamilton's equations,

$$ \frac{d\theta}{dt} = \nabla_r H = M^{-1} r, \qquad \frac{dr}{dt} = -\nabla_\theta H = -\nabla U(\theta), $$

generate a flow that (i) preserves the canonical distribution $\pi(\theta, r) \propto \exp(-H)$, (ii) preserves volume (Liouville's theorem), and (iii) is reversible [7]. Since the flow cannot be integrated exactly, HMC uses the **leapfrog integrator**, a symplectic (volume-preserving, reversible) discretization:

```python
def leapfrog(theta, r, eps, grad_U, M_inv):
    r = r - 0.5 * eps * grad_U(theta)   # half-step momentum
    theta = theta + eps * (M_inv @ r)    # full-step position
    r = r - 0.5 * eps * grad_U(theta)   # half-step momentum
    return theta, r
```

A proposal $(\theta^*, r^*)$ obtained from $L$ leapfrog steps is accepted with probability $\min(1, \exp(H(\theta, r) - H(\theta^*, r^*)))$, correcting for discretization error. Because Hamiltonian trajectories follow the *geometry* of the posterior rather than a random walk, HMC achieves $\mathcal{O}(d^{5/4})$ scaling versus $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ for random-walk Metropolis in $d$ dimensions [7].

### 3.3 Variational guides and the ELBO

Variational inference posits a tractable family $q_\phi(z)$ — the *guide* — and minimizes $\mathrm{KL}(q_\phi \,\|\, p(z \mid x))$. Since the KL involves the intractable posterior, one maximizes instead the **evidence lower bound (ELBO)**:

$$ \mathcal{L}(\phi) = \mathbb{E}_{q_\phi}[\log p(x, z) - \log q_\phi(z)] \le \log p(x), $$

with equality iff $q_\phi = p(z \mid x)$. In Pyro, the guide is itself a probabilistic program, and stochastic variational inference (SVI) optimizes the ELBO by stochastic gradient ascent [5][6]:

```python
def model(data):
    loc = pyro.sample("loc", dist.Normal(0., 1.))
    scale = pyro.sample("scale", dist.LogNormal(0., 1.))
    with pyro.plate("obs", len(data)):
        pyro.sample("x", dist.Normal(loc, scale), obs=data)

def guide(data):
    loc_q = pyro.param("loc_q", torch.tensor(0.))
    scale_q = pyro.param("scale_q", torch.tensor(1.),
                         constraint=constraints.positive)
    pyro.sample("loc", dist.Normal(loc_q, 1.))
    pyro.sample("scale", dist.LogNormal(0., scale_q))
```

Gradient estimators come in two flavors: the **score-function (REINFORCE)** estimator, applicable to any guide but high-variance, and the **reparameterization** estimator, which writes $z = g_\phi(\epsilon)$, $\epsilon \sim p(\epsilon)$, and differentiates through $g_\phi$, yielding dramatically lower variance for continuous latents [6].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Commutativity, quasi-Borel spaces, and validated inference

The Giry monad on **Meas** fails to be commutative in the presence of higher-order functions: there is no measurable evaluation map $\mathrm{ev}: Y^X \times X \to Y$ making function spaces well-behaved, since **Meas** is not Cartesian closed. Staton et al. [2] resolve this with **quasi-Borel spaces** (QBS): a set $X$ with a distinguished collection $M_X \subseteq X^\mathbb{R}$ of "random elements" closed under precomposition with measurable maps and countable gluing. The category **QBS** *is* Cartesian closed, carries a probability monad, and embeds standard Borel spaces fully and faithfully — so ordinary distributions (Gaussian, Beta, Dirichlet) behave exactly as classically expected, while higher-order programs gain a compositional denotation.

The payoff is the **commutativity law**: for programs $t_1, t_2$ with disjoint random choices,

$$ \llbracket \texttt{do } x \leftarrow t_1; y \leftarrow t_2; \texttt{return } (x,y) \rrbracket = \llbracket \texttt{do } y \leftarrow t_2; x \leftarrow t_1; \texttt{return } (x,y) \rrbracket $$

as measures. This innocent-looking equation is the semantic license for sequential Monte Carlo: SMC resamples and reorders random choices mid-execution, and commutativity guarantees the reordered program denotes the *same* measure. Ścibior et al. [3] exploit exactly this to give a **denotational validation** of SMC and trace MCMC: inference algorithms are presented as source-to-source program transformations, and their correctness reduces to denotational equalities in the model. The slogan: *a correct inference algorithm is a semantics-preserving compiler pass* [2][3].

> **Theorem:** (Soundness and adequacy, Staton et al. [2]). For the metalanguage with higher-order functions, continuous distributions, and soft constraints: (i) *Soundness*: if $t \Downarrow_w v$ operationally with weight $w$, then $\llbracket t \rrbracket$ assigns the corresponding mass to $v$; (ii) *Adequacy*: if $\llbracket t_1 \rrbracket = \llbracket t_2 \rrbracket$ as s-finite kernels, then $t_1$ and $t_2$ are contextually equivalent. In particular, any compiler optimization validated by a denotational equation is observationally invisible.

### 4.2 Symplectic geometry and the leapfrog integrator

Why does HMC work so well? The answer is geometric [7]. The leapfrog integrator is **symplectic**: it exactly preserves a *modified* Hamiltonian $\tilde{H} = H + \mathcal{O}(\epsilon^2)$ (backward error analysis), so energy errors remain bounded and oscillate rather than drift — unlike explicit Euler, whose energy drifts linearly and destroys the acceptance rate. Symplecticity implies volume preservation, which makes the Metropolis–Hastings correction depend only on the energy difference, with no Jacobian determinant to compute. Reversibility (achieved by negating momentum on rejection) guarantees detailed balance with respect to the canonical distribution.

The practical consequence is that HMC proposes *distant* states with high acceptance probability, suppressing random-walk behavior. Betancourt [7] gives the sharp geometric picture: Hamiltonian flow explores the *typical set* — the thin shell where the posterior concentrates in high dimensions — by converting potential energy into kinetic energy and back. The mass matrix $M$ acts as a preconditioner; Stan adapts a diagonal (or dense) $M$ during warmup to match the posterior's curvature [1].

A Stan program makes the log-density view explicit:

```stan
data { int<lower=0> N; vector[N] y; }
parameters { real mu; real<lower=0> sigma; }
model {
  mu ~ normal(0, 10);
  sigma ~ lognormal(0, 1);
  y ~ normal(mu, sigma);
}
```

Each `~` statement *adds* to the log-density accumulator; Stan's reverse-mode autodiff then supplies $\nabla U$ to the leapfrog integrator [1]. The program denotes $\gamma(\mu, \sigma) = p(\mu)p(\sigma)\prod_n p(y_n \mid \mu, \sigma)$; NUTS samples from its normalization.

### 4.3 The No-U-Turn criterion and dual-averaging adaptation

HMC's Achilles' heel is tuning: the step size $\epsilon$ and trajectory length $L$. Too small an $L$ recovers random-walk behavior; too large wastes computation doubling back over already-explored regions. Hoffman and Gelman [4] eliminate $L$ with the **No-U-Turn Sampler (NUTS)**. NUTS builds a binary tree of leapfrog states by doubling forward and backward in time, and stops when the trajectory begins to retrace itself — the *U-turn condition*:

$$ (\theta^+ - \theta^-) \cdot r^- < 0 \quad \text{or} \quad (\theta^+ - \theta^-) \cdot r^+ < 0, $$

where $\theta^-, \theta^+$ are the trajectory endpoints and $r^-, r^+$ the endpoint momenta. Rather than a single accept/reject step, NUTS slice-samples uniformly from the candidate states visited, preserving detailed balance via a careful recursive construction [4]. The step size $\epsilon$ is adapted during warmup by **dual averaging** (a variant of Nesterov's primal-dual scheme) targeting a desired acceptance statistic $\delta \approx 0.8$. The result is a genuinely *turnkey* sampler: Stan's default NUTS requires no hand-tuning and, empirically, matches or exceeds the efficiency (effective sample size per gradient evaluation) of optimally tuned HMC across a range of models [1][4].

| Sampler | Tuning burden | Scaling in $d$ | Handles funnels | Gradient evals / iteration |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Random-walk Metropolis | Proposal covariance | $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ | Poorly | 1 |
| Gibbs | Conditionals required | Model-dependent | Poorly | 0 |
| Static HMC | $\epsilon$, $L$, $M$ | $\mathcal{O}(d^{5/4})$ | Moderately | $L$ |
| NUTS (adaptive HMC) | None (warmup only) | $\mathcal{O}(d^{5/4})$ | Moderately | Adaptive ($\approx 2^j$) |
| Stochastic VI | Guide family, stepsize | $\mathcal{O}(d)$ per step | Often collapses | 1 (+ guide) |

### 4.4 Variational guides: ELBO geometry, reparameterization, and amortization

The ELBO decomposes revealingly:

$$ \mathcal{L}(\phi) = \underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{q_\phi}[\log p(x \mid z)]}_{\text{expected log-likelihood}} - \underbrace{\mathrm{KL}(q_\phi(z) \,\|\, p(z))}_{\text{regularizer toward prior}}. $$

Maximizing $\mathcal{L}$ trades data fit against proximity to the prior — precisely the Bayesian Occam's razor. Two gradient technologies make this practical [6]. The **reparameterization trick** reparameterizes $z = g_\phi(\epsilon)$ with $\epsilon \sim p(\epsilon)$ fixed, so that

$$ \nabla_\phi \mathcal{L} = \mathbb{E}_{p(\epsilon)}\left[ \nabla_\phi \log p(x, g_\phi(\epsilon)) - \nabla_\phi \log q_\phi(g_\phi(\epsilon)) \right], $$

a low-variance pathwise estimator for continuous, reparameterizable guides. The **score-function** estimator $\mathbb{E}_{q_\phi}[(\log p - \log q_\phi)\nabla_\phi \log q_\phi]$ handles discrete latents but suffers high variance, mitigated by baselines and control variates.

Pyro elevates guides to *programs* [5]: a guide mirrors the model's sample sites, each annotated with variational parameters, and Poutine effect handlers (`trace`, `replay`, `block`) separate model definition from inference mechanics. *Amortized* guides — e.g., an encoder network mapping $x \mapsto (\mu_\phi(x), \sigma_\phi(x))$ — share parameters across data points, yielding the variational autoencoder [6].  But the approximation gap is real: mean-field guides underestimate posterior variance and cannot capture multimodality.

## 5 Empirical Results and Formal Arguments

### 5.1 Formal arguments: what the theorems buy

The measure-theoretic semantics yields three concrete guarantees [2][3]:

1. **Compiler optimizations are validated once, in the model.** Common-subexpression elimination of random choices, hoisting of `score` past independent `sample`s, and disintegration-based simplifications are justified by kernel equalities, not by testing.
2. **SMC and trace MCMC are correct by construction.** Ścibior et al. [3] decompose SMC into *representation* changes (sampling-based → distribution-based) and *transformation* steps, each validated denotationally; the resampling step's correctness is exactly the commutativity law of §4.1.
3. **HMC/NUTS target the right distribution.** Detailed balance of the leapfrog–Metropolis scheme follows from symplecticity (volume preservation) plus momentum negation (reversibility) [7]; NUTS's recursive doubling preserves detailed balance by construction, so its stationary distribution is exactly the posterior denoted by the program [4].

> **Theorem:** (Denotational validation of SMC, Ścibior et al. [3]). The sequential Monte Carlo inference transformation, presented as a composition of denotationally validated representation changes and resampling steps, denotes the same posterior measure as the original program. Consequently, SMC estimators are consistent for expectations under the program's denotation.

### 5.2 Empirical comparison

Hoffman and Gelman [4] benchmark NUTS against hand-tuned HMC on four model classes: NUTS matches or exceeds tuned HMC in effective sample size per gradient evaluation, with *no* manual tuning of $L$. Stan's implementation made NUTS the default engine for applied Bayesian statistics, with the funnel pathology diagnosed through *divergent transitions*, a diagnostic that exploits the symplectic integrator's energy-error signal [1][7].

Kingma and Welling [6] show stochastic gradient variational Bayes trains deep generative models where MCMC is infeasible, and Pyro's SVI scales this to universal programs [5]. The trade-off is sharp and well documented:

| Criterion | NUTS / HMC | Stochastic VI with guides |
|---|---|---|
| Asymptotic exactness | Yes (MCMC) | No (approximation gap) |
| Uncertainty calibration | Faithful (given convergence) | Often overconfident |
| Scalability | Limited by gradient cost | Minibatch-friendly, GPU-ready |
| Discrete latents | Requires marginalization | Native (score estimator) |
| Diagnostics | $\hat{R}$, divergences, ESS | ELBO (necessary, not sufficient) |
| User burden | Model only | Model + guide design |

These are not competing philosophies but *different inference transformations over the same denotation*: a program's measure-theoretic meaning [2] is invariant, and HMC, NUTS, SMC, and SVI are alternative strategies for interrogating it.

## 6 Limitations

Several limitations bound the claims above. **Semantically**, quasi-Borel spaces resolve Cartesian closure but the resulting category, while convenient, is less familiar than **Meas**, and some classical results (e.g., certain disintegration theorems) require care in translation [2]. S-finite kernels handle soft conditioning, but *hard* conditioning on measure-zero events (exact observations of continuous variables) remains delicate: `observe(Normal(0,1), 0.5)` denotes conditioning on a null set, handled in practice by densities but requiring the disintegration machinery to formalize [3]. Recursion and non-termination demand domain-theoretic extensions [3].

**Algorithmically**, HMC degrades on posteriors with varying curvature (funnels, heavy tails), where no single step size is stable everywhere. Variational guides suffer an *approximation gap* that the ELBO cannot detect: a higher ELBO need not mean a better posterior approximation across different guide families, and mean-field guides systematically understate uncertainty — dangerous in scientific applications [5][6].

**Practically**, Stan's expressiveness is limited to (mostly) continuous, fixed-dimension models; Pyro's universality comes at the cost of guide engineering and stochastic-optimization fragility [1][5]. Neither system yet delivers push-button exact inference for arbitrary universal programs — the frontier where semantics [2][3] outruns algorithms.

## 7 Conclusion

Probabilistic programming rests on a three-legged stool. *Measure-theoretic semantics* — the Giry monad, quasi-Borel spaces, s-finite kernels, and the commutativity law — gives programs a compositional meaning and certifies inference as semantics-preserving transformation [2][3]. *Hamiltonian Monte Carlo*, through symplectic integration and the No-U-Turn criterion, turns the posterior's differential geometry into an efficient, nearly tuning-free sampler [4][7], industrialized by Stan [1]. *Variational guides* turn inference into optimization, with the ELBO, reparameterization gradients, and amortized encoders scaling Bayesian reasoning to deep models and massive data [5][6]. The unifying insight is that these are views of one object: a program denotes a measure, and inference — whether by Hamiltonian flow, particle resampling, or gradient ascent on a bound — is disciplined computation over that measure. The frontier is closing the gap between what the semantics validates and what the algorithms deliver: automatic, exact, scalable inference for the full higher-order programs the theory already understands.
