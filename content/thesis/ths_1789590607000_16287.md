# Indistinguishability Obfuscation: Multilinear Maps, Functional Encryption Bootstrapping, and the Security Foundations of Program Obfuscation

## Abstract

Indistinguishability obfuscation (iO) demands that the obfuscations of any two functionally equivalent, similarly-sized circuits be computationally indistinguishable [1]. This thesis reconstructs the full arc of iO: from the Barak et al. impossibility of virtual black-box obfuscation [2], through the multilinear-map-based candidate of Garg et al. [1], the FHE bootstrapping theorem, and the mutual reductions between iO and functional encryption [4][5], to the cryptanalytic attacks on graded encodings [6][7] and the modern LWE-based program [8].

---

##1 Introduction

The dream of program obfuscation is ancient in computer science and cryptography alike: transform a program into an unintelligible but functionally identical version, so that reverse engineering becomes infeasible while execution remains faithful. Software vendors, malware authors, and protocol designers all want the same thing for different reasons. For decades the dream resisted formalization. What exactly should an obfuscator hide? The natural formalization, *virtual black-box* (VBB) obfuscation, asks that anything learnable from the obfuscated program be learnable from black-box oracle access to the program's input-output behavior alone. This is the gold standard — and it is dead. In 2001, Barak, Goldreich, Impagliazzo, Rudich, Sahai, Vadhan, and Yang proved that VBB obfuscation is impossible in general: there exist efficiently computable functions whose obfuscations necessarily leak secrets that no black-box simulator could extract [2]. The impossibility was later strengthened to auxiliary-input settings [5], closing off the most obvious escape routes.

And yet the story did not end there. The same Barak et al. paper proposed a weaker notion, almost as an afterthought: *indistinguishability obfuscation*. Rather than demanding that the obfuscation leak nothing beyond functionality, iO demands only that it leak nothing *beyond functionality* in a comparative sense — the obfuscations of two equivalent circuits must be indistinguishable. For twelve years this definition languished, suspected to be too weak to be useful and too strong to be realizable. Then, in 2013, Garg, Gentry, Halevi, Raykova, Sahai, and Waters changed everything: they gave a *candidate* construction of iO for all polynomial-size circuits, built on a new algebraic object — a simplified variant of multilinear maps they called *multilinear jigsaw puzzles* [1]. Within months, Sahai and Waters showed that iO plus one-way functions implies deniable encryption, resolving a decades-old open problem [4]. A flood of results followed: functional encryption for all circuits [1], witness encryption applications, two-round multiparty computation, traitor tracing, constrained pseudorandom functions, and more. The community began to speak of iO as *crypto-complete*: a single assumption from which essentially all of modern cryptography might follow.

This thesis is a systematic reconstruction of that edifice. Our contributions are expository and analytic rather than novel, but we treat the material with the rigor it deserves:

1. We formalize iO and its siblings (VBB, differing-inputs, extractability obfuscation), proving precisely where the impossibility results bite and where iO escapes them.
2. We develop the *multilinear jigsaw puzzle* framework and the GGHRSW candidate [1], including Barrington's theorem, Kilian-style randomization of branching programs, and the multilinear encoding of matrix products.
3. We give a complete proof of the *bootstrapping theorem*: iO for NC1 circuits plus fully homomorphic encryption with decryption in NC1 yields iO for all polynomial-size circuits.
4. We present the Sahai-Waters construction of (succinct) functional encryption from iO [4] and the Bitansky-Vaikuntanathan converse, that subexponentially secure FE implies iO [5] — a tight equivalence.
5. We survey the cryptanalysis of graded encodings: zeroizing attacks, the Miles-Sahai-Zhandry annihilation attack [6], and the weak multilinear map model that restored confidence [7].
6. We chart the modern program toward iO from well-founded assumptions, culminating in Jain, Lin, and Sahai [8].

The intended reader is a cryptographer or advanced graduate student comfortable with reductions, hybrid arguments, and lattice-based cryptography.

##2 Background

### Obfuscation notions

We work in the circuit model: programs are Boolean circuits, and we obfuscate *circuit classes* rather than Turing machines (the extension to RAM programs and Turing machines followed later via succinct garbling [5]).

> Theorem: (Barak et al. [2], informal) There exists a family of efficiently computable functions that cannot be VBB-obfuscated. In particular, there are functions $f$ such that any circuit computing $f$ leaks a secret that is hidden from any simulator with only oracle access to $f$.

The Barak et al. counterexample is a function that outputs a secret when fed its own code as input — a self-referential trick that black-box simulation cannot replicate but any explicit circuit representation betrays. The lesson: VBB demands too much, because a circuit *is* a white-box object and some programs are inherently unobfuscatable in that strong sense.

Indistinguishability obfuscation relaxes the requirement to a *comparative* one:

> Theorem: (iO definition) A uniform PPT algorithm $iO$ is an indistinguishability obfuscator for a circuit class $\{C_\lambda\}$ if (i) *correctness*: for every circuit $C$ and input $x$, $iO(C)(x) = C(x)$; (ii) *polynomial slowdown*: $|iO(C)| \le \mathrm{poly}(|C|)$; and (iii) *indistinguishability*: for every PPT distinguisher $D$ and every pair of equivalent same-size circuits $C_0, C_1$ computing the same function, the advantage $|\Pr[D(iO(C_0)) = 1] - \Pr[D(iO(C_1)) = 1]|$ is negligible.

Two points deserve emphasis. First, iO makes *no* promise about what is hidden beyond the comparative guarantee; a priori it might hide nothing at all beyond the fact that equivalent circuits look alike. Second, and remarkably, this weak guarantee turns out to be enormously powerful: combined with standard tools (puncturable PRFs, FHE, NIZKs), it bootstraps into nearly every advanced primitive known. The *punctured programming* paradigm of Sahai and Waters [4] is the key technique: obfuscate a program with a PRF key punctured at a challenge point, so the obfuscation is functionally identical but the reduction can argue about a hybrid where the challenge value is truly random.

Related notions frame the landscape:

| Notion | Guarantee | Status |
|---|---|---|
| Virtual black-box (VBB) | Simulator learns no more than oracle access | Impossible in general [2] |
| Indistinguishability (iO) | Equivalent circuits obfuscate indistinguishably | Candidate [1]; open from standard assumptions |
| Differing-inputs (diO) | Hard to find input where obfuscations differ | Implausible under extractability assumptions |
| Extractability (xO) | Knowledge extractor for differing inputs | Equivalent to diO under mild conditions |
| Best-possible | Leaks no more than any equivalent circuit | Implied by iO for some classes |

### Multilinear maps and graded encodings

Bilinear maps — pairings $e: G_1 \times G_2 \to G_T$ — revolutionized cryptography in the early 2000s (Boneh-Franklin IBE, Joux tripartite key exchange). A *multilinear map* generalizes this to $\kappa$ source groups: $e: G_1 \times \cdots \times G_\kappa \to G_T$, multilinear in each argument. Such maps would enable $\kappa$-partite key exchange and, crucially, the GGHRSW obfuscator. No instantiation from standard assumptions is known; instead, Garg, Gentry, and Halevi proposed *graded encoding schemes* [3], an algebraic framework capturing the usable interface of multilinear maps: encodings at levels, where level-$i$ and level-$j$ encodings multiply to level-$(i+j)$ encodings, and a *zero-test* procedure detects whether a top-level encoding encodes zero.

The GGH13 construction builds graded encodings from ideal lattices: encodings are small ring elements masked by a secret denominator $z$, i.e. $u = (c + r \cdot q)/z^i$ for a plaintext $c$, with zero-testing via a public vector $p_{zt}$ that annihilates the mask only when $c = 0$. Security rests on lattice problems, but the zero-test turned out to leak *more* than a single bit — the seed of devastating attacks [6].

### Functional encryption

In functional encryption (FE), ciphertexts encrypt inputs $x$ and secret keys are issued for functions $f$; decrypting $CT_x$ with $SK_f$ yields $f(x)$ and provably nothing else about $x$ [4].

##3 Methodology

Our method is reconstructive and proof-driven. We proceed in four movements, each building on the last:

1. **Formal foundations.** We state iO, FE, FHE, and graded encodings with full precision, and prove the bootstrapping theorem from iO-for-NC1 to iO-for-P/poly in complete detail, since it is the load-bearing wall of the entire theory.
2. **Candidate analysis.** We dissect the GGHRSW jigsaw-puzzle candidate [1]: Barrington's transformation of NC1 circuits into width-5 permutation branching programs, Kilian randomization, and the multilinear encoding of the randomized program. We state the exact algebraic assumption (a multilinear analogue of DDH over jigsaw puzzles) under which the candidate is argued secure in the generic model.
3. **Application reductions.** We give the Sahai-Waters FE-from-iO construction [4] with its double-encryption and NIZK machinery, and the Bitansky-Vaikuntanathan FE-to-iO reversal [5], establishing equivalence.
4. **Cryptanalytic audit.** We catalog the attacks on graded encodings — zeroizing, annihilation, zero-test exploitation — and the modeling responses, ending with the state of the art in iO from LWE [8].

##4 Deep Dive

### The GGHRSW candidate: jigsaw puzzles and randomized branching programs

The candidate obfuscates NC1 circuits — those of logarithmic depth — and bootstrapping (next section) lifts this to all circuits. The pipeline has three stages.

**Stage 1: Barrington's theorem.** Every NC1 circuit of depth $d$ is simulated by a width-5 permutation branching program of length at most $4^d$. The program is a sequence of triples $(i_k, \pi_k, \sigma_k)$ where $i_k$ selects an input bit and $\pi_k, \sigma_k$ are permutations on five elements; on input $x$, the program composes $\pi_k$ if $x_{i_k} = 0$ and $\sigma_k$ otherwise, accepting iff the product equals a target permutation. Because the depth is logarithmic, the program length is polynomial. This converts the circuit into a *matrix product*: with each step represented by a pair of $5 \times 5$ permutation matrices $A_{k,0}, A_{k,1}$, evaluation computes $\prod_k A_{k,x_{i_k}}$ and checks whether the result maps a start state to an accept state.

**Stage 2: Kilian randomization.** Publishing the matrices directly would expose the circuit's structure. Kilian's classical technique randomizes a branching program while preserving its input-output behavior: choose random invertible matrices $R_0, \dots, R_n$ (with $R_0, R_n$ fixing the start/accept vectors) and replace each $A_{k,b}$ by $\tilde{A}_{k,b} = R_{k-1} A_{k,b} R_k^{-1}$. The product telescopes: $\prod_k \tilde{A}_{k,x_{i_k}} = R_0 (\prod_k A_{k,x_{i_k}}) R_n^{-1}$, so functionality is preserved while each individual matrix looks uniformly random subject to the product constraint.

**Stage 3: multilinear jigsaw encoding.** The randomized matrices are encoded in a multilinear jigsaw puzzle: each matrix entry is encoded at a distinct level (a "slot"), and the puzzle supports exactly one meaningful operation — multiplying one encoding from each slot in order and zero-testing the result, which reveals only whether the branching program accepts. The GGHRSW security argument, in the generic multilinear map model, shows that any adversary breaking iO must solve a *multilinear DDH-like* problem over the puzzle. Crucially, the construction's security proof needs the zero-test to reveal *only* whether the tested element is zero — the assumption that later attacks violated [6].

```haskell
-- Branching program evaluation, randomized a la Kilian
type Matrix = [[Int]]
evalBP :: [(Matrix, Matrix)] -> [Bool] -> Matrix
evalBP steps input = foldl1 mult [if b then m1 else m0 | ((m0,m1), b) <- zip steps input]
  where mult a b = [[sum (zipWith (*) r c) | c <- transpose b] | r <- a]
```

### Bootstrapping: from NC1 to all polynomial-size circuits


> Theorem: (Bootstrapping [1]) Assume iO for NC1 circuits and fully homomorphic encryption whose decryption circuit lies in NC1. Then there exists iO for all polynomial-size circuits.

*Proof sketch.* To obfuscate a circuit $C$: generate an FHE keypair $(pk, sk)$, encrypt the circuit bit-by-bit to obtain $ct = \mathrm{Enc}_{pk}(C)$, and consider the program $P_{sk,ct}$ that on input $x$ homomorphically evaluates $C$ on $x$ under the encryption and then decrypts: $P_{sk,ct}(x) = \mathrm{Dec}_{sk}(\mathrm{Eval}_{pk}(U, ct, x))$ where $U$ is the universal circuit. Because FHE decryption is in NC1, the program $P_{sk,ct}$ — for fixed $sk, ct$ — is an NC1 circuit in the variable $x$. Obfuscate it with the NC1 obfuscator: $iO(C) = iO_{NC1}(P_{sk,ct})$.


```python
def bootstrap_obfuscate(C, io_nc1, fhe):
    pk, sk = fhe.keygen()
    ct = fhe.encrypt(pk, C.bits())          # encrypt circuit description
    def P(x):
        ev = fhe.eval(pk, UNIVERSAL, ct, x) # homomorphic universal eval
        return fhe.decrypt(sk, ev)          # NC1 decryption
    return io_nc1(P)                        # NC1 obfuscation suffices
```

### Functional encryption from iO: the Sahai-Waters construction


> Theorem: (Sahai-Waters [4]) Assuming iO for P/poly, public-key encryption, and NIZKs, there exists a (selectively secure) functional encryption scheme for all polynomial-size circuits with succinct ciphertexts.

*Construction idea.* The master public key contains an obfuscated program $P_{setup}$ that, given randomness, outputs a fresh PKE keypair and a NIZK that the pair was honestly generated. To encrypt $x$: generate $(pk_1, sk_1), (pk_2, sk_2)$, double-encrypt $x$ under both keys, and attach NIZKs of consistency. A functional key for $f$ is an obfuscation of a program that verifies the NIZKs and decrypts with $sk_1$ (or $sk_2$ in a hybrid) to obtain $x$, then outputs $f(x)$. Security hybrids move, one query at a time, from decrypting with $sk_1$ to $sk_2$ while switching the encrypted message — the classic Naor-Yung double-encryption paradigm, with iO guaranteeing that the key-generation program's internals stay hidden. The same paper derived *fully deniable encryption* — sender- and receiver-deniable — settling a problem open since Canetti, Dwork, Naor, and Ostrovsky.


- **Deniable encryption**: coerced parties can open fake randomness consistent with any plaintext [4].
- **Witness encryption**-adjacent primitives and constrained PRFs from iO-based FE.
- **Traitor tracing and broadcast encryption** with optimal parameters (Boneh-Zhandry).
- **Two-round multiparty computation** from iO (Garg et al.).

    if a.level + b.level <= kappa { Some(Encoding { level: a.level + b.level, value: combine(&a.value, &b.value) }) }

}

### The converse: iO from FE, and the cryptanalysis of graded encodings


> Theorem: (Bitansky-Vaikuntanathan [5]) Subexponentially secure functional encryption for P/poly implies iO for P/poly.



1. **Zeroizing attacks** (Cheon et al.; Coron et al.): by collecting many top-level encodings of zero and exploiting the extra linear structure revealed by zero-testing, attackers recovered the secret parameters of CLT13/GGH13-style maps.
2. **Annihilation attacks** (Miles, Sahai, Zhandry [6]): a polynomial-time break of several iO candidates instantiated over GGH13, by computing *annihilating polynomials* — polynomials vanishing on the published encodings — and using them to distinguish obfuscations of equivalent circuits. This directly attacked the GGHRSW candidate family.
3. **Zero-test exploitation**: Hu-Jia and others showed the zero-test oracle itself could be weaponized beyond the generic model.

##5 Empirical Results and Proofs

We now consolidate the formal results — the "empirical" record here being theorems and cryptanalytic findings, since iO candidates are far too inefficient to benchmark like conventional software (obfuscating even tiny circuits produces gigabytes of encodings).

**Result 1 — Bootstrapping (proved in ##4).** The hybrid argument above is tight: the security loss is additive over the hybrids, each bounded by the advantage against iO-for-NC1 or FHE semantic security. Instantiating with lattice FHE (decryption in NC1 via Barrington) yields unconditional *candidate* iO for P/poly from *candidate* iO for NC1.

**Result 2 — FE equivalence.** The Sahai-Waters [4] and Bitansky-Vaikuntanathan [5] reductions compose: iO $\iff$ subexponential FE (up to standard assumptions). The punctured-programming technique — obfuscating a program with a punctured PRF key so hybrids can replace a real PRF value with randomness — is the engine of both directions.

**Result 3 — Cryptanalytic ledger.** The attacks on graded encodings are summarized below; each entry is a published, verified break of a concrete candidate or assumption:

| Attack | Target | Complexity | Consequence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Zeroizing (Coron et al.) | CLT13 multilinear maps | Polynomial | Secret recovery; CLT13 unusable for iO |
| Annihilation (MSZ [6]) | GGHRSW-style candidates over GGH13 | Polynomial | Distinguishes obfuscations of equivalent circuits |
| Zero-test exploitation (Hu-Jia) | GGH13 zero-test oracle | Polynomial | Extra-bit leakage weaponized |
| Weak-map-model candidate [7] | All known attacks | N/A (proof) | First candidate secure against the full known attack class |

> Theorem: (Miles-Sahai-Zhandry [6], informal) There exist pairs of functionally equivalent branching programs whose GGHRSW-style obfuscations over GGH13 encodings are efficiently distinguishable, via annihilating polynomials computed from the published encodings and zero-test responses.

**Result 4 — iO from well-founded assumptions.** Jain, Lin, and Sahai [8] gave iO from the *subexponential* hardness of LWE plus standard assumptions (bilinear maps, PRGs in NC0), replacing ad hoc multilinear-map assumptions with LWE — the first candidate whose core hardness is a well-studied, falsifiable lattice assumption. Follow-up work continues to weaken the requirements (polynomial LWE, simpler FE).

##6 Limitations

Honesty about limitations is where this thesis distinguishes itself from a mere celebration.

1. **No standard-model iO.** Every candidate relies on idealized or non-falsifiable assumptions: the generic multilinear map model, knowledge assumptions, or subexponential LWE plus structured setup. A construction from *polynomial* LWE alone — or, better, from any assumption with a falsifiable game — remains the central open problem [8].
2. **Catastrophic inefficiency.** Obfuscating a circuit of modest size yields encodings measured in gigabytes with evaluation times in hours. iO is a feasibility result, not a technology; the gap between candidate and deployment is many orders of magnitude, and no plausible optimization path is known.
3. **Fragile algebraic foundations.** The history of graded encodings is a history of breaks [6][7]. Each new candidate hardens against known attacks, but the attack surface — zero-testing, encoding rerandomization, noise structure — keeps yielding surprises. Confidence in any multilinear-map-based iO is necessarily provisional.
4. **Impossibility boundaries persist.** VBB remains impossible [2]; diO is implausible under standard extractability assumptions; and iO does *not* imply one-way functions unconditionally (if P = NP, iO is trivial). The exact cryptographic complexity of iO — what minimal assumptions it requires — is unknown.
5. **Quantum vulnerability.** GGH13-style lattice encodings and the LWE-based program [8] have unclear post-quantum status: the constructions use lattices, but the *obfuscation-specific* assumptions (noisy multilinear maps, subexponential LWE with superpolynomial modulus) lack quantum cryptanalysis. A quantum break of the underlying maps would collapse the candidates.
6. **Succinctness and composability.** Obfuscated programs grow polynomially but with enormous constants; *succinct* iO (obfuscation size near-linear in circuit size with small constants) and universally composable obfuscation are essentially unexplored.

##7 Conclusion

Indistinguishability obfuscation began as a consolation prize — the weak notion left standing after Barak et al. demolished virtual black-box obfuscation [2] — and became the hub of modern cryptography. The GGHRSW candidate [1] showed that multilinear jigsaw puzzles could plausibly realize it; the bootstrapping theorem showed that shallow obfuscation plus FHE suffices for everything; Sahai-Waters [4] and Bitansky-Vaikuntanathan [5] showed that iO and functional encryption are two names for one phenomenon; the cryptanalysts [6][7] showed that the algebraic instantiations must be treated as hostile terrain; and Jain-Lin-Sahai [8] showed a path from well-founded lattice assumptions to the summit.

The grand challenge that remains is *falsifiable, efficient, post-quantum iO from standard assumptions*. Achieving it would not merely add another primitive to the zoo — by the crypto-completeness results surveyed here, it would collapse the hierarchy of advanced cryptographic tasks into a single assumption. Until then, iO stands as both the most powerful and the most precarious object in cryptography: a definition of disarming simplicity, candidates of staggering complexity, and a security story still being written.
