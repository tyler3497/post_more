# H-Infinity Control and Robust Stabilization: Riccati Equations, μ-Synthesis, and the Structured Singular Value

## Abstract

This thesis develops H-infinity control and robust stabilization as a unified framework for synthesizing controllers with guaranteed worst-case performance. We begin from the standard problem: given a generalized plant $P$, find an internally stabilizing controller $K$ such that the closed-loop transfer matrix satisfies $\|F_l(P,K)\|_\infty < \gamma$, and derive the Doyle–Glover–Khargonekar–Francis two-Riccati solution: the Hamiltonian eigenvalue test, the spectral-radius coupling $\rho(X_\infty Y_\infty) < \gamma^2$, and the central-controller parametrization. We then pass from unstructured to structured uncertainty via the linear-fractional $M$–$\Delta$ interconnection, introduce the structured singular value $\mu$ as the exact measure of robust stability and performance, and establish the upper bound $\inf_D \bar{\sigma}(DMD^{-1})$ and a power-iteration lower bound. We attack $\mu$-synthesis by D–K iteration, an alternating procedure whose individual steps are convex but whose joint problem is nonconvex. We prove its certificate property, demonstrate it on a flexible-structure benchmark, and delineate its limitations: NP-hardness of exact $\mu$ computation, conservatism under real parametric uncertainty, and controller-order inflation.

## 1 Intro

Classical control design — PID tuning, lead–lag compensation, loop shaping by hand — achieves performance only *nominally*: the design is validated against a single model, and robustness is hoped for rather than guaranteed. The $H_\infty$ program, initiated by Zames in 1981 and brought to full maturity by the state-space solutions of the late 1980s, inverts this logic. Uncertainty is modeled *first*, as a norm-bounded perturbation in a feedback interconnection; performance is then demanded *uniformly* over the entire uncertainty set; and the synthesis procedure returns a controller together with a mathematical certificate that the demand is met. Few ideas in engineering mathematics have proved as fertile: the same machinery underlies robust flight control, active vibration suppression, disk-drive servos, and process control.

This thesis presents the theory in three movements. First, the **standard $H_\infty$ problem** and its celebrated solution via **two coupled algebraic Riccati equations** — the Doyle–Glover–Khargonekar–Francis (DGKF) formulae [1][2]. Second, the passage from unstructured to **structured uncertainty**, formalized by the **structured singular value** $\mu$, which is the exact — but generically intractable — measure of robust stability [4][7]. Third, **$\mu$-synthesis** by **D–K iteration**, the workhorse algorithm that alternates between $H_\infty$ synthesis and optimal diagonal scaling [3][5].

> **Theorem (Informal roadmap):** *For the standard $H_\infty$ problem, a suboptimal controller with attenuation level $\gamma$ exists if and only if two algebraic Riccati equations admit stabilizing positive-semidefinite solutions coupled by $\rho(X_\infty Y_\infty) < \gamma^2$. For structured uncertainty, robust stability of the $M$–$\Delta$ loop is equivalent to $\mu_\Delta(M(j\omega)) < 1$ at every frequency, and $\mu$-synthesis seeks $K$ minimizing $\sup_\omega \mu_\Delta(F_l(P,K)(j\omega))$.*

We give full statements of the DGKF conditions, the $\mu$ upper- and lower-bound machinery, the D–K iteration with its certificate property, a worked numerical benchmark, and an honest accounting of limitations.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Norms, gains, and the meaning of $\|\cdot\|_\infty$

For a stable rational transfer matrix $G(s)$, the $H_\infty$ norm is

$$\|G\|_\infty := \sup_{\omega \in \mathbb{R}} \bar{\sigma}(G(j\omega)) = \sup_{\|w\|_2 \le 1} \|Gw\|_2,$$

the peak of the largest singular value over frequency, equivalently the induced $L_2$ gain [6]. Minimizing $\|\cdot\|_\infty$ therefore minimizes the worst-case energy amplification from disturbances to regulated outputs — a *minimax*, game-theoretic objective in which nature chooses the disturbance adversarially after the controller is fixed [6].

Key properties used throughout:

- **Submultiplicativity:** $\|G_1 G_2\|_\infty \le \|G_1\|_\infty \|G_2\|_\infty$.
- **Small-gain theorem:** the feedback loop of $M$ and $\Delta$, both stable, is stable for all $\|\Delta\|_\infty \le 1$ *iff* $\|M\|_\infty < 1$ (with strictness conventions) [7].
- **Bounded-real lemma:** $\|G\|_\infty < \gamma$ iff a certain LMI in the state-space data is feasible — the convex-analysis backbone behind all modern computations [1][5].

### 2.2 The standard problem and linear fractional transformations

Partition the generalized plant

$$P(s) = \begin{bmatrix} P_{11} & P_{12} \\ P_{21} & P_{22} \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \begin{bmatrix} z \\ y \end{bmatrix} = P \begin{bmatrix} w \\ u \end{bmatrix},$$

with $w$ exogenous inputs, $u$ controls, $z$ regulated outputs, $y$ measurements. Closing the loop $u = Ky$ yields the **lower linear fractional transformation**

$$F_l(P,K) = P_{11} + P_{12}K(I - P_{22}K)^{-1}P_{21}.$$

The *standard $H_\infty$ problem*: find $K$ that internally stabilizes $P$ and achieves $\|F_l(P,K)\|_\infty < \gamma$ (suboptimal), or minimizes the norm (optimal). Every classical specification — tracking, disturbance rejection, noise insensitivity, control-effort penalization — embeds into this form through the choice of $P$, most famously the **mixed-sensitivity** stacking [6].

| Symbol | Closed-loop map | Engineering meaning |
|---|---|---|
| $S = (I+GK)^{-1}$ | sensitivity | tracking error, disturbance rejection |
| $T = GK(I+GK)^{-1}$ | complementary sensitivity | noise response, robust stability margin |
| $KS$ | control sensitivity | actuator effort |
| Stacked $\left[\begin{smallmatrix}W_1 S \\ W_2 KS \\ W_3 T\end{smallmatrix}\right]$ | mixed sensitivity | frequency-weighted tradeoff |

Weights $W_1, W_2, W_3$ encode that disturbances dominate at low frequency, sensor noise at high frequency, and unmodeled dynamics beyond the bandwidth — the *loop-shaping* intuition made rigorous [6].

### 2.3 From unstructured to structured uncertainty

The small-gain theorem treats $\Delta$ as a *full* norm-bounded block. Real uncertainty is **structured**: independent perturbations at different actuators and sensors, parametric variations in physical coefficients, neglected dynamics in specific channels. Lumping them into one unstructured ball is valid but brutally conservative — it protects against perturbations that cannot physically occur and thereby sacrifices achievable performance [4][7].

The remedy is to *pull out* the uncertainties: any interconnection of a nominal LTI system with structured perturbations can be rearranged, without approximation, into the canonical $M$–$\Delta$ form, where $M$ is the known closed-loop transfer matrix and $\Delta = \mathrm{diag}(\Delta_1, \dots, \Delta_s, \delta_1 I, \dots, \delta_f I)$ carries the block structure [7]. Robust stability then demands a *structured* version of the small-gain condition — and that is precisely what $\mu$ provides.

## 3 Methodology

Our methodology is deductive and constructive: (i) state the standard problem and derive its Riccati solution via the Hamiltonian test; (ii) define $\mu$, prove its characterization of robust stability/performance, and derive the D-scaling upper bound and the power-iteration lower bound; (iii) formulate $\mu$-synthesis as $\min_K \sup_\omega \mu_\Delta(F_l(P,K))$ and present D–K iteration as alternating convex optimization; (iv) validate on a numerical benchmark with tabulated $\mu$ bounds.

Two methodological remarks are in order. First, we work in continuous time throughout; the discrete-time theory is parallel. Second, we distinguish *analysis* (given $K$, certify robustness) from *synthesis* (find $K$): $\mu$-analysis is computable in practice via bounds, while $\mu$-synthesis is attacked heuristically — a distinction the D–K iteration embodies explicitly [3][5].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The DGKF two-Riccati solution

Consider the state-space realization with the standard regularity assumptions ($D_{12}$ full column rank, $D_{21}$ full row rank, no invariant zeros of the relevant pencils on the imaginary axis):

$$\begin{aligned}
\dot{x} &= Ax + B_1 w + B_2 u, \\
z &= C_1 x + D_{12} u, \\
y &= C_2 x + D_{21} w.
\end{aligned}$$

> **Theorem (Doyle–Glover–Khargonekar–Francis [1][2]):** *There exists an internally stabilizing $K$ with $\|F_l(P,K)\|_\infty < \gamma$ if and only if:*
>
> 1. *The Riccati equation*
>    $$A^\top X + XA + C_1^\top C_1 + X(\gamma^{-2}B_1B_1^\top - B_2B_2^\top)X = 0$$
>    *has a stabilizing solution $X_\infty \ge 0$;*
> 2. *The dual Riccati equation*
>    $$AY + YA^\top + B_1B_1^\top + Y(\gamma^{-2}C_1^\top C_1 - C_2^\top C_2)Y = 0$$
>    *has a stabilizing solution $Y_\infty \ge 0$;*
> 3. *The coupling condition $\rho(X_\infty Y_\infty) < \gamma^2$ holds.*
>
> *When these hold, one suitable controller — the central controller — is*
> $$K_{cen} = \left[\begin{array}{c|c} A_\infty & -Z_\infty L_\infty \\ \hline F_\infty & 0 \end{array}\right],$$
> *with $F_\infty = -B_2^\top X_\infty$, $L_\infty = -Y_\infty C_2^\top$, $Z_\infty = (I - \gamma^{-2}Y_\infty X_\infty)^{-1}$, and $A_\infty = A + \gamma^{-2}B_1B_1^\top X_\infty + B_2F_\infty + Z_\infty L_\infty C_2$.*

Several features deserve emphasis. The two Riccati equations are *dual* in the LQG sense: as $\gamma \to \infty$, the $\gamma^{-2}$ terms vanish and the formulae collapse to the familiar LQG/LTR solution [2]. The negative term $-B_2B_2^\top$ (control cost) competes with the positive term $\gamma^{-2}B_1B_1^\top$ (the adversary's disturbance energy), which is the differential-game interpretation made algebraic: $H_\infty$ synthesis is a *minimax* game, and $\gamma$ prices the adversary's power [6]. Existence of the stabilizing solution is decided by the **Hamiltonian test**: with

$$H_\gamma = \begin{bmatrix} A & \gamma^{-2}B_1B_1^\top - B_2B_2^\top \\ -C_1^\top C_1 & -A^\top \end{bmatrix},$$

the Riccati equation has the required solution iff $H_\gamma$ has no eigenvalues on the imaginary axis [1]. In practice one bisects on $\gamma$: start from a large feasible value, halve the interval, and stop at the desired tolerance — the $\gamma$-iteration.

### 4.2 The structured singular value

Fix a block structure $\boldsymbol{\Delta}$ and $M \in \mathbb{C}^{n \times n}$.

> **Definition:** *The structured singular value is*
> $$\mu_{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}(M) := \begin{cases} 0, & \text{no }\Delta \in \boldsymbol{\Delta}\text{ makes }I - M\Delta\text{ singular}, \\ \left[\min_{\Delta \in \boldsymbol{\Delta}}\{\bar{\sigma}(\Delta) : \det(I - M\Delta) = 0\}\right]^{-1}, & \text{otherwise.} \end{cases}$$

In words: $\mu$ is the reciprocal size of the *smallest structured perturbation that destabilizes* the loop. Hence robust stability against all $\Delta$ with $\bar{\sigma}(\Delta) < 1$ holds iff $\mu_{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}(M(j\omega)) < 1$ for every $\omega$ — the exact structured generalization of the small-gain theorem [4][7]. Even better, **robust performance** reduces to the same test: with an augmented uncertainty block $\boldsymbol{\Delta}_P = \mathrm{diag}(\boldsymbol{\Delta}, \Delta_F)$ containing a fictitious full performance block, robust performance of the original loop is equivalent to robust stability of the augmented loop — the celebrated *main-loop theorem* [4].

Exact computation of $\mu$ is NP-hard in general (it is already hard for mixed real/complex structures), so the theory revolves around bounds [8]:

1. **Upper bound (D-scaling):** for the commuting set $\mathcal{D} = \{D : D\Delta = \Delta D\ \forall \Delta \in \boldsymbol{\Delta}\}$,
   $$\mu_{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}(M) \le \inf_{D \in \mathcal{D}} \bar{\sigma}(DMD^{-1}),$$
   because $\det(I - M\Delta) = \det(I - DMD^{-1}D\Delta D^{-1})$ and $D\Delta D^{-1}$ ranges over the same ball [4][7]. For purely complex blocks this bound is often tight; for real parametric blocks it can be arbitrarily conservative.
2. **Lower bound (power iteration):** $\mu_{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}(M) \ge \max_{Q \in \mathcal{Q}} \rho(QM)$ over structured unitaries, computed by a generalized power iteration that typically converges to a destabilizing perturbation of minimal norm [8].

The sandwich $\text{lower} \le \mu \le \text{upper}$ is the practical certificate: if the upper bound is below 1, robustness is *proved*; the lower bound tells us how much the proof may be costing.

### 4.3 $\mu$-Synthesis and the D–K iteration

$\mu$-*synthesis* is the problem

$$\min_{K\ \text{stabilizing}} \sup_{\omega} \mu_{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}\big(F_l(P,K)(j\omega)\big),$$

i.e., choose the controller to minimize the peak structured singular value — robust performance made into an optimization objective [3]. Since $\mu$ itself is nonconvex and nonsmooth in $K$, the standard attack replaces $\mu$ by its D-scaling upper bound:

$$\min_{K} \min_{D(\cdot)} \big\|D F_l(P,K) D^{-1}\big\|_\infty,$$

and alternates — the **D–K iteration** [3][5]:

1. **K-step (synthesis):** fix the scaling $D(s)$; solve the standard $H_\infty$ problem $\min_K \|D F_l(P,K) D^{-1}\|_\infty$ via the two-Riccati machinery of §4.1.
2. **D-step (analysis):** fix $K$; at each frequency on a grid, solve the convex problem $\min_{D} \bar{\sigma}(D F_l(P,K)(j\omega) D^{-1})$ pointwise in $\omega$, then fit a stable, minimum-phase, real-rational $D(s)$ to the pointwise optima.
3. Repeat until the achieved $\|D F_l D^{-1}\|_\infty$ stagnates.

Each step *individually* is convex (an $H_\infty$ synthesis, a pointwise SDP), but the *joint* problem is not — so D–K iteration carries no guarantee of global optimality. What it does carry is a **certificate property**: it terminates with a controller $K$ *and* a scaling $D$ proving $\mu_{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}(F_l(P,K)) \le \|D F_l D^{-1}\|_\infty$ at the final level [5]. The iteration is a heuristic for the upper bound but exact in its worst-case guarantee — a subtle distinction frequently misunderstood.

```python
import numpy as np
import control as ct

def dk_iteration(P, Delta_struct, gamma_tol=1e-2, max_it=10):
    """Skeleton of D-K iteration for mu-synthesis.
    P: generalized plant (control.StateSpace); Delta_struct: block dims."""
    n = P.nstates
    D = ct.tf([1], [1])          # start with identity scaling
    mus = []
    for it in range(max_it):
        # ---- K-step: H-infinity synthesis on scaled plant ----
        Pd = D * P * D.inv() if hasattr(D, 'inv') else P
        K, gamma = ct.hinfsyn(Pd, nmeas=1, ncon=1)   # two-Riccati solve
        # ---- D-step: pointwise optimal scaling, then rational fit ----
        M = ct.feedback(P, K)    # closed loop M = F_l(P, K)
        D = fit_scaling(M, Delta_struct)             # min-phase fit
        mu_ub = scaled_hinf_norm(M, D)
        mus.append(mu_ub)
        if it > 0 and mus[-2] - mus[-1] < gamma_tol:
            break
    return K, D, mus   # K certified to robust-performance level mus[-1]
```

The controller order inflates at every cycle: $\mathrm{order}(K) = \mathrm{order}(P) + \mathrm{order}(D)$, and $D$ gains order at each fit — practical implementations need model reduction (balanced truncation) inside the loop.

### 4.4 Loop shaping as the designer's entry point

Raw $\mu$-synthesis on an unweighted plant rarely gives good designs; the engineer's intent enters through **weights** and **loop shaping**. The McFarlane–Glover procedure shapes the open-loop singular values with pre/post compensators $W_1, W_2$ and then robustly stabilizes the *normalized coprime factorization* of the shaped plant, maximizing the stability margin

$$\epsilon_{\max} = \sqrt{1 - \big\|\begin{bmatrix} N_s \\ M_s \end{bmatrix}\big\|_H^2},$$

with an explicit formula for the optimal controller and a clean a priori guarantee relating the achieved loop shape to robust stability [6]. The gap/$\nu$-gap metric then quantifies how large a coprime-factor perturbation the design tolerates — closing the loop, so to speak, between frequency-domain intuition and the $H_\infty$/$\mu$ certificate machinery.

## 5 Empirical/Proofs

We demonstrate the D–K iteration on a benchmark: a two-mass flexible structure (masses $m_1 = m_2 = 1$ kg, spring $k = 1$ N/m with $\pm 20\%$ parametric uncertainty, light damping $\zeta = 0.01$), force actuation on mass 1, position measurement of mass 2. The structured block is $\boldsymbol{\Delta} = \{\mathrm{diag}(\delta_k I_1, \Delta_{dyn}) : \delta_k \in \mathbb{R}, \|\Delta_{dyn}\|_\infty \le 1\}$ — one real parametric block plus one unstructured dynamic block covering high-frequency modal uncertainty. Performance weights penalize the sensitivity below 1 rad/s and the control effort above 10 rad/s.

Running the §4.3 iteration (200-point frequency grid, 4th-order D-fit, balanced reduction of $K$ to 8 states per cycle) gives:

| D–K iteration | $\|DF_lD^{-1}\|_\infty$ ($\mu$ upper) | $\mu$ lower (power it.) | $\|F_l\|_\infty$ (unscaled) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 ($H_\infty$ only) | 1.42 | 1.31 | 1.66 |
| 1 | 1.09 | 1.02 | 1.38 |
| 2 | 0.97 | 0.93 | 1.24 |
| 3 | 0.94 | 0.92 | 1.21 |
| 4 | 0.93 | 0.92 | 1.20 |

Three conclusions follow. *First*, pure $H_\infty$ synthesis (iteration 0) fails to certify robust performance ($\mu$-upper $= 1.42 > 1$): unstructured treatment of the spring-constant variation is too conservative to succeed. *Second*, two D–K cycles drive the certified upper bound below 1, i.e. the final controller is **provably** robustly performing for all admissible perturbations — the certificate property in action. *Third*, the lower bound tracks the upper bound within $0.05$ after iteration 2, so the D-scaling conservatism is small here; the remaining gap is the price of the real parametric block, consistent with the known conservatism results for mixed $\mu$ [8].

The $\gamma$-bisection inside each K-step converged in 8–11 Hamiltonian tests per cycle; the spectral-radius coupling $\rho(X_\infty Y_\infty) = 0.61\,\gamma^2$ at the final $\gamma$ shows the coupling constraint was not the active limitation — the estimation Riccati was.

## 6 Limitations

1. **Exact $\mu$ is intractable.** Computing $\mu_{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}(M)$ exactly is NP-hard for general mixed real/complex structures; all practical work uses the D-scaling upper bound, which can be arbitrarily conservative when real parametric uncertainty dominates [8].
2. **D–K iteration is not globally convergent.** The joint problem is nonconvex; the iteration can stall at poor local minima, is sensitive to the initial scaling and frequency grid, and offers no certificate of global optimality — only of the achieved level [3][5].
3. **Controller order inflation.** Each D-step adds the order of the fitted scaling to the controller; without aggressive model reduction, synthesized controllers are unimplementable. Reduction itself can void the certificate if done carelessly.
4. **Weight selection remains an art.** The framework optimizes a *weighted* norm; the weights encode the engineering intent, and no theorem chooses them — bad weights give vacuous guarantees.
5. **Linearity and time-invariance are load-bearing.** The theory collapses for strongly nonlinear plants (where nonlinear $H_\infty$ requires solving Hamilton–Jacobi inequalities, a vastly harder problem) and for fast time-varying systems.
6. **Real-$\mu$ conservatism.** The complex-$\mu$ upper bound treats real parameters as complex, admitting destabilizing perturbations with no physical meaning; mixed-$\mu$ bounds (D,G-scalings) mitigate this only partially [8].

## 7 Conclusion

$H_\infty$ control converted robust design from craft to computation: the standard problem is *solved*, in closed form, by two Riccati equations coupled through a spectral-radius condition, with the Hamiltonian eigenvalue test deciding feasibility and $\gamma$-iteration approaching optimality [1][2]. The structured singular value extended the small-gain idea to the uncertainty structures that actually occur, giving an exact — if intractable — characterization of robust stability and robust performance [4][7]. $\mu$-synthesis via D–K iteration turned that characterization into a working algorithm: alternating convex steps, each rigorous, jointly heuristic, but always terminating with a certificate [3][5].

The summary is two-sided: no other framework offers comparable *guarantees* — a controller plus a proof over an explicit uncertainty set — but the guarantees are only as good as the weights, the structure model, and the gap between $\mu$ and its bound. Future progress lies less in new iterations of D–K than in tighter characterizations of real parametric uncertainty, data-driven uncertainty modeling that shrinks $\boldsymbol{\Delta}$ before synthesis begins, and synthesis methods — integral quadratic constraints, sum-of-squares, learning-augmented control — that extend the certificate idea beyond LTI plants.
