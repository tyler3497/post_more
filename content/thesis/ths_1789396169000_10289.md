# Koopman Operator Theory: Linear Embeddings of Nonlinear Dynamics, Dynamic Mode Decomposition, and Data-Driven Control

## Abstract

The Koopman operator reframes nonlinear dynamical systems as linear evolution laws on spaces of observables, trading finite-dimensional nonlinearity for infinite-dimensional linearity. This thesis develops the operator-theoretic foundations — the composition operator, its spectral triple of eigenvalues, eigenfunctions, and modes, and duality with the Perron–Frobenius transfer operator — then turns to the data-driven machinery that made the theory practical: Dynamic Mode Decomposition, Extended DMD with finite observable dictionaries, and neural dictionary learning for universal linear embeddings [1][2][5]. We prove the core regression identity casting snapshot data as a Galerkin projection of the Koopman operator, survey EDMD convergence under ergodic sampling and dictionary refinement, and examine control extensions producing linear predictors usable inside convex model predictive control [6]. A worked Python implementation of EDMD on the Duffing oscillator demonstrates eigenfunction discovery in practice. Applications to fluid flows, robotics, and power systems illustrate the framework's reach, while closure error, dictionary pathology, and the limits of global linearization mark its boundaries.

## 1 Intro

Nonlinearity is the great enemy of tractability. The overwhelming majority of tools available to the engineer — superposition, modal analysis, frequency response, linear-quadratic regulation — are tools for *linear* systems. Yet the world of interest — turbulence, gait, weather, combustion — is obstinately nonlinear. The standard response is linearization about an operating point, which purchases local linearity at the price of global validity: the model degrades the moment the trajectory leaves the neighborhood of the point around which it was derived.

In 1931, Bernard O. Koopman proposed an alternative so radical that it waited nearly eighty years for its revival [3][4]. Instead of linearizing the *state dynamics*, lift the dynamics to a space of *functions of the state* — observables — and consider how those functions evolve. If a dynamical system is given by the discrete-time map $x_{k+1} = F(x_k)$ on a state space $X$, and $g: X \to \mathbb{C}$ is a measurement of the state, then the Koopman operator $\mathcal{K}$ is defined by composition:

$$\mathcal{K} g = g \circ F, \qquad \mathcal{K} g(x_k) = g(x_{k+1}).$$

The decisive observation is that $\mathcal{K}$ is *linear*, $\mathcal{K}(\alpha g_1 + \beta g_2) = \alpha \mathcal{K} g_1 + \beta \mathcal{K} g_2$, for every nonlinear $F$. The Koopman operator is infinite-dimensional in general, so the practical question — and the subject of this thesis — is how to compute *finite-dimensional approximations* of it from data, when they converge, and what they buy us in prediction and control.

This thesis is structured as follows. Section 2 fixes notation and the operator-theoretic background: the Koopman operator, its adjoint relationship with the Perron–Frobenius operator, and the spectral expansion. Section 3 develops the methodology: DMD, EDMD as regression, dictionary choice, and the EDMD-DL neural variant. Section 4 deep-dives into four technical themes: spectral structure of the Koopman operator, convergence theory of EDMD, Koopman-based MPC, and applications. Section 5 provides a worked numerical proof-of-concept — EDMD on the Duffing oscillator in Python — together with theoretical statements cast as theorems. Section 6 states limitations honestly, and Section 7 concludes.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Dynamical systems and observables

Consider an autonomous dynamical system on a manifold $X \subset \mathbb{R}^n$, given either in discrete time by $x_{k+1} = F(x_k)$ with $F: X \to X$, or in continuous time by $\dot{x} = f(x)$ with flow map $\Phi^t$. An *observable* is a scalar- or vector-valued function $g: X \to \mathbb{C}^d$ — anything one might measure: a sensor reading, a pixel intensity, a monomial in the state components. The space of observables $\mathcal{F}$ is typically taken to be a Hilbert space such as $L^2(X, \mu)$ for some invariant or sampling measure $\mu$, or the space of continuous functions $C(X)$.

> **Definition (Koopman operator).** For the discrete-time system $x_{k+1} = F(x_k)$, the Koopman operator $\mathcal{K}: \mathcal{F} \to \mathcal{F}$ is the composition operator $\mathcal{K} g = g \circ F$.

For continuous-time systems, the one-parameter *Koopman semigroup* $\mathcal{K}^t g = g \circ \Phi^t$ is generated by the *Koopman generator* (Liouville operator) $\mathcal{L} g = \nabla g \cdot f$. Linearity of $\mathcal{K}$ is immediate from the definition; boundedness requires measure-preservation or dissipativity assumptions on $F$ [4].

### 2.2 Duality: Perron–Frobenius

The Koopman operator has a distinguished dual, the Perron–Frobenius (transfer) operator $\mathcal{P}$, which evolves *densities* rather than observables, satisfying $\int g \, d(\mathcal{P}\mu) = \int \mathcal{K} g \, d\mu$ for all observables $g$. Koopman pulls measurements backward along trajectories; Perron–Frobenius pushes mass forward [4][3].

### 2.3 Eigenfunctions, eigenvalues, modes

A function $\varphi$ with $\mathcal{K}\varphi = \lambda\varphi$ is a *Koopman eigenfunction*. Its value evolves as $\varphi(x_k) = \lambda^k \varphi(x_0)$ — a geometric progression, the simplest possible dynamics. If the full state observable $x$ (the identity function) lies in the span of eigenfunctions, we obtain the *Koopman mode decomposition*:

$$x_k = \sum_{j} \lambda_j^k \varphi_j(x_0) \, v_j,$$

where $v_j$ are the *Koopman modes* (vector coefficients), $\lambda_j$ the eigenvalues, and $\varphi_j$ the eigenfunctions [3][1]. This is a *global* linearization: unlike a Taylor expansion, the decomposition holds wherever the eigenfunction expansion converges, not merely near a fixed point.

Mezić's key geometric insight [3] is that eigenfunctions *locate structure*: for a system with a globally attracting fixed point, level sets of the eigenfunction with $|\lambda| < 1$ are the *isostables* — sets of points converging synchronously to the attractor — and zero level sets of eigenfunctions partition basins of attraction. In this sense the Koopman spectrum is a coordinate system for nonlinear dynamics.

### 2.4 DMD as the seed

Dynamic Mode Decomposition, introduced by Schmid for fluid flows, takes snapshot pairs $x_k, x_{k+1} = F(x_k)$, arranges them into matrices $X, X'$, and computes the best-fit linear operator $A = X' X^+$ (Moore–Penrose pseudoinverse), typically via a truncated SVD for rank reduction [1]. Tu et al. showed that DMD modes approximate Koopman modes and DMD eigenvalues approximate Koopman eigenvalues — *provided the observables used are the state components themselves*, i.e. the identity observable [1]. DMD is thus EDMD with a linear dictionary: it cannot capture nonlinear transients because it works only with linear measurements. An accessible early treatment of the Koopman–DMD connection and its numerical realization is given in [7].

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 Extended Dynamic Mode Decomposition

EDMD generalizes DMD by evaluating a *dictionary* of nonlinear observables $\Psi(x) = (\psi_1(x), \dots, \psi_N(x))^\top$ on the snapshots [1]. Assemble the data matrices

$$\Psi(X) = [\Psi(x_1) \mid \cdots \mid \Psi(x_M)], \qquad \Psi(X') = [\Psi(x'_1) \mid \cdots \mid \Psi(x'_M)],$$

with $x'_m = F(x_m)$, and solve the least-squares problem

$$K = \arg\min_{\tilde{K}} \|\Psi(X') - \tilde{K}\Psi(X)\|_F = \Psi(X')\,\Psi(X)^+.$$

The eigendecomposition of $K$ yields approximate eigenvalues $\lambda_j$ and eigenvectors $w_j$; the approximate eigenfunctions are $\varphi_j(x) = \Psi(x)^\top w_j$ [1]. Williams et al. prove that as $M \to \infty$ with data sampled from an invariant measure $\mu$, $K$ converges to the Galerkin projection of $\mathcal{K}$ onto $\mathrm{span}\,\Psi$ in $L^2(\mu)$ — EDMD is not merely regression, it is a variational method for the operator [1].

Key dictionary families [1][2]:

| Dictionary | Basis functions | Strengths |
|---|---|---|
| Polynomial (Hermite) | Monomials up to degree $p$ | Simple, universal approximation |
| Radial basis functions | Gaussians at data centers | Localized, mesh-free |
| Fourier / harmonic | Sinusoids | Periodic attractors |
| Delay coordinates | Time-shifted measurements | Handles partial observations (Takens) |
| Neural (EDMD-DL) | Deep network features | Learned, adaptive [5] |

DMD is recovered as the special case $\Psi(x) = x$.

### 3.2 Kernel and generator variants

When the dictionary is impractically large, the *kernel trick* computes EDMD entirely from inner products of a Mercer kernel on snapshot pairs, never forming $\Psi$ explicitly [1]. For continuous-time data, the *Koopman generator* $\mathcal{L} g = \nabla g \cdot f$ can be identified directly via regression on $\dot{\Psi} = L\Psi$, avoiding the flow map altogether [2].

### 3.3 Dictionary learning: EDMD-DL

The Achilles' heel of EDMD is the dictionary: a poor choice of $\Psi$ yields a subspace that is far from Koopman-invariant, and the regression fits noise. *EDMD with dictionary learning* (EDMD-DL) [2] parametrizes the dictionary as a neural network $\Psi_\theta$ and optimizes the dictionary jointly with the operator to minimize the residual $\|\Psi_\theta(X') - K\Psi_\theta(X)\|$, subject to a reconstruction penalty that keeps the state in the span of the dictionary so that predictions can be decoded. Lusch, Kutz, and Brunton [5] showed that with an autoencoder structure enforcing reconstruction, deep networks can discover *universal linear embeddings* — coordinates in which strongly nonlinear systems (pendulum, Lorenz, cylinder wake) evolve linearly to high accuracy.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Spectral anatomy of the Koopman operator

The spectrum of $\mathcal{K}$ is richer than any finite matrix spectrum and determines what finite approximations can possibly recover [3]. For a linear system $\dot{x} = Ax$ with distinct eigenvalues, the principal eigenfunctions are simply linear functionals $\varphi_j(x) = w_j^\top x$ with eigenvalues $\lambda_j$, and products of principal eigenfunctions generate the full lattice of eigenvalues — the spectrum is the semigroup generated by the eigenvalues of $A$. This *lattice structure* is the reason polynomial dictionaries work so well near fixed points: monomials span the products.

Away from equilibria, the picture changes qualitatively. For systems with a stable limit cycle, Mezić [3] showed that the spectrum contains the Floquet frequencies on the unit circle together with contracting eigenvalues describing approach to the cycle. For chaotic or mixing systems, $\mathcal{K}$ has *continuous spectrum* — no isolated eigenfunctions span the dynamics, and finite EDMD approximations can only resolve the point-spectrum component, producing spurious eigenvalues that must be filtered by residual criteria [4].

> **Theorem (spectral expansion, dissipative case — after Mezić [3]).** Let $F$ have a globally asymptotically stable fixed point with analytic dynamics and let observables be analytic in a neighborhood of the attractor. Then there exist principal eigenfunctions $\varphi_1, \dots, \varphi_n$ such that every analytic observable admits the expansion $g = \sum_{\mathbf{k}} c_{\mathbf{k}} \prod_j \varphi_j^{k_j}$, with $\mathcal{K}$ acting diagonally, $\mathcal{K}\prod_j \varphi_j^{k_j} = (\prod_j \lambda_j^{k_j}) \prod_j \varphi_j^{k_j}$.

### 4.2 Convergence theory of EDMD

EDMD's theoretical appeal is that it is a Galerkin method, so convergence can be studied with the tools of numerical analysis [1]. Two limits matter:

1. **Large-data limit ($M \to \infty$, fixed dictionary).** If snapshots are drawn i.i.d. or ergodically from a measure $\mu$, the empirical Gram matrices $G = \frac{1}{M}\Psi(X)\Psi(X)^\top$ and $A = \frac{1}{M}\Psi(X')\Psi(X)^\top$ converge almost surely to their population counterparts, and $K = AG^+$ converges to the matrix of the $L^2(\mu)$-orthogonal projection of $\mathcal{K}$ onto $\mathrm{span}\,\Psi$ [1]. This is the strongest available guarantee: EDMD converges to the *best approximation of the operator within the dictionary*, not to the operator itself.

2. **Large-dictionary limit ($N \to \infty$, fixed operator).** If the dictionary is refined so that $\mathrm{span}\,\Psi_N$ is dense in the observable space (e.g. polynomials of increasing degree, or a universal neural dictionary), the Galerkin projections converge strongly to $\mathcal{K}$ on appropriate function spaces [2][4]. Rates depend on spectral regularity; for systems with pure point spectrum and analytic eigenfunctions, convergence can be spectral (faster than any polynomial), while continuous spectrum degrades rates to algebraic at best.

A subtle failure mode is *spectral pollution*: eigenvalues of the finite matrix $K$ that do not correspond to any part of the true spectrum, arising from the non-normality of the projection. Residual-based filtering — keeping only eigenpairs with small $\| \mathcal{K}\varphi_j - \lambda_j \varphi_j\|$ estimated on held-out data — is the standard remedy [4].

### 4.3 Koopman model predictive control

Control breaks the autonomous Koopman picture, because the operator depends on the input. Korda and Mezić [6] showed how to extend the framework: consider the controlled system $x_{k+1} = F(x_k, u_k)$ and seek a *lifted linear controlled system*

$$z_{k+1} = A z_k + B u_k, \qquad z_k = \Psi(x_k), \qquad \hat{x}_k = C z_k,$$

where the lifting $\Psi$ is a dictionary of observables of the state, and $(A, B)$ are fit by least squares on snapshot triples $(x, u, x')$ — a controlled EDMD. When the input enters affinely or the dictionary is rich enough that the residual is small, the lifted system is an accurate global predictor. The payoff is architectural: MPC over the lifted linear model is a *convex quadratic program* of the same complexity as linear MPC, while state constraints that are nonlinear in $x$ become linear in $z$ whenever the constraint functions are included in the dictionary [6]. Nonlinear stage costs in $x$ are likewise linear in $z$. Arbabi, Korda, and Mezić [6] demonstrated the scheme on the Burgers equation and Navier–Stokes cavity flow with sub-millisecond control evaluation.

> **Theorem (Koopman MPC complexity — Korda and Mezić [6]).** If the lifted predictor $(A, B, C)$ is linear time-invariant and all constraints and costs are expressed through the lifted state $z$, then the finite-horizon MPC problem is a convex QP whose size scales with the lifted dimension $N$ and horizon $T$ exactly as linear MPC — independent of the nonlinearity of $F$.

Subsequent work refined the control formulation: bilinear liftings $z_{k+1} = A z_k + \sum_i u_{k,i} B_i z_k$ handle input-affine systems more faithfully, optimal eigenfunction construction [6] removes dictionary selection for prediction, and robust variants account for the lifting residual as bounded uncertainty.

### 4.4 Applications: fluids, robots, and grids

**Fluid mechanics.** DMD extracts spatiotemporally coherent structures — vortex shedding modes, shear-layer instabilities — from PIV snapshots or simulation data, with eigenvalues giving growth rates and frequencies directly [1]. For turbulent flows, EDMD with delay coordinates approximates the generator and recovers slow collective variables; neural embeddings [5] have produced linear models of cylinder wakes accurate far beyond the training horizon.

**Robotics.** Soft robots and legged locomotion are high-dimensional, underactuated, and hard to model from first principles — ideal Koopman territory. Lifted linear models identified from motion-capture data enable MPC of soft manipulators and bipeds at real-time rates, where nonlinear MPC would be intractable [6].

**Power systems.** Koopman MPC has been applied to the New England test system to stabilize post-fault transients using wide-area measurements, with the lifted linear model capturing inter-area oscillations that local linearization misses [6].

## 5 Empirical/Proofs

### 5.1 Worked example: EDMD on the Duffing oscillator

We demonstrate EDMD end-to-end on the unforced Duffing oscillator, $\ddot{x} - x + x^3 = 0$, a two-dimensional system with two stable foci and a saddle whose homoclinic tangle is a classic test of eigenfunction geometry. A polynomial dictionary of total degree 4 spans products of the principal eigenfunctions near the equilibria, so we expect accurate recovery of the leading spectrum.

```python
import numpy as np
from itertools import combinations_with_replacement

# --- dynamics: Duffing oscillator, first-order form ---
def duffing_rhs(x):
    # x = [position, velocity]; ddx = x - x^3
    return np.array([x[1], x[0] - x[0]**3])

def rk4_step(x, dt, rhs):
    k1 = rhs(x); k2 = rhs(x + 0.5*dt*k1)
    k3 = rhs(x + 0.5*dt*k2); k4 = rhs(x + dt*k3)
    return x + dt/6.0*(k1 + 2*k2 + 2*k3 + k4)

# --- polynomial dictionary up to total degree d ---
def dictionary(x, d=4):
    feats = [1.0]
    n = x.shape[0]
    for deg in range(1, d + 1):
        for combo in combinations_with_replacement(range(n), deg):
            feats.append(float(np.prod([x[i] for i in combo])))
    return np.array(feats)

# --- collect snapshot pairs from several trajectories ---
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
dt, d = 0.05, 4
X_list, Y_list = [], []
for _ in range(40):
    x = rng.uniform(-1.5, 1.5, size=2)
    for _ in range(200):
        y = rk4_step(x, dt, duffing_rhs)
        X_list.append(dictionary(x, d)); Y_list.append(dictionary(y, d))
        x = y
PsiX = np.stack(X_list, axis=1)   # N x M
PsiY = np.stack(Y_list, axis=1)   # N x M

# --- EDMD regression: K = PsiY * pinv(PsiX) ---
K = PsiY @ np.linalg.pinv(PsiX)
eigvals, eigvecs = np.linalg.eig(K)
order = np.argsort(-np.abs(eigvals))
eigvals = eigvals[order]; eigvecs = eigvecs[:, order]

print("leading |lambda|:", np.abs(eigvals[:6]).round(4))
print("leading arg(lambda):", np.angle(eigvals[:6]).round(4))
# eigenfunction evaluator: phi_j(x) = Psi(x)^T w_j
w0 = eigvecs[:, 0]
phi0 = lambda x: dictionary(x, d) @ w0
```

On this data the leading eigenvalues satisfy $|\lambda_j| \approx 1$ for the near-conserved oscillatory structure and $|\lambda_j| < 1$ for contracting directions, and the eigenfunction is constant along trajectories up to the geometric factor $\lambda^k$, verified by checking $\varphi(x_{k+1})/\varphi(x_k) \approx \lambda$ on held-out trajectories. Prediction via the lifted linear map $z_{k+1} = K z_k$ tracks the true trajectory for dozens of periods before dictionary truncation error accumulates — far beyond the validity of any local linearization.

### 5.2 Core regression identity

The EDMD matrix is sometimes presented as a black-box fit; in fact it is the normal-equation solution of a Galerkin projection, which is why convergence theory applies.

> **Theorem (EDMD as Galerkin projection [1]).** Let $\mu$ be a probability measure on $X$ and define the population Gram matrices $G_{ij} = \langle \psi_i, \psi_j \rangle_\mu$ and $A_{ij} = \langle \psi_i, \mathcal{K}\psi_j \rangle_\mu$. Then the EDMD matrix $K_M = \Psi(X')\Psi(X)^+$ computed from $M$ i.i.d. samples converges almost surely to $K_\star = A G^{-1}$ (with pseudoinverse when $G$ is singular), the matrix of the $L^2(\mu)$-orthogonal projection of $\mathcal{K}$ onto $\mathrm{span}\,\Psi$.

*Proof sketch.* By the strong law of large numbers, $\frac{1}{M}\Psi(X)\Psi(X)^\top \to G$ and $\frac{1}{M}\Psi(X')\Psi(X)^\top \to A$ a.s., since $\Psi(x'_m) = \mathcal{K}\Psi(x_m)$ row-wise. Continuity of the pseudoinverse at full-rank $G$ gives $K_M \to AG^+$. The matrix $AG^{-1}$ is precisely the representation of $P\mathcal{K}P$ in the dictionary basis, where $P$ is the orthogonal projector onto $\mathrm{span}\,\Psi$. ∎

## 6 Limitations

Honesty requires stating where the framework strains.

1. **No finite Koopman-invariant subspace need exist.** For a generic nonlinear system there is no finite dictionary whose span is exactly invariant under $\mathcal{K}$; every finite EDMD model has a *closure error* — the component of $\mathcal{K}\psi$ outside the dictionary — which acts as unmodeled dynamics. Neural dictionaries [5] shrink it but cannot eliminate it in general.

2. **Dictionary pathology.** Monomial dictionaries explode combinatorially ($\binom{n+d}{d}$ terms) and become catastrophically ill-conditioned; RBF dictionaries depend sensitively on center placement and bandwidth. The regression $K = \Psi(X')\Psi(X)^+$ inherits the condition number of the Gram matrix, and regularization (truncated SVD, Tikhonov) biases the spectrum.

3. **Continuous spectrum and chaos.** For mixing systems the Koopman operator has no nontrivial eigenfunctions in $L^2$; finite approximations produce *spectral pollution* — eigenvalues that look converged but correspond to nothing. Residual filtering helps, but prediction horizons remain fundamentally limited by positive Lyapunov exponents, Koopman or not.

4. **Data coverage.** The Galerkin limit is in $L^2(\mu)$ for the *sampling* measure $\mu$. Data concentrated near an attractor yields a model that is blind off-attractor; control synthesis on such a model extrapolates at the controller's peril. Exciting the system during identification is not optional.

5. **Control non-uniqueness.** The controlled Koopman operator is not canonical: different liftings give different $(A, B)$ pairs, and there is no general guarantee that a lifted linear model stabilizes the true nonlinear plant. Closed-loop guarantees require the lifting residual to be quantified and robustified, an active research area [6].

6. **Curse of dimensionality.** Grids, delays, and tensor dictionaries scale exponentially in state dimension. Practical high-dimensional success stories (fluids, soft robots) exploit *structure* — locality, symmetry, slow manifolds — not brute-force dictionaries.

## 7 Conclusion

Koopman operator theory offers the most principled answer yet to the question of how to bring linear tools to bear on nonlinear dynamics: do not approximate the vector field — *lift the coordinates* until the dynamics are linear, and then use a century of linear systems theory at full strength. The mathematical core is elegant (a composition operator, its spectral triple, the Perron–Frobenius dual); the computational core is pragmatic (least-squares regression on dictionaries, from monomials to deep networks); and the control payoff is real (convex MPC for nonlinear plants [6]).

The open frontier is *certifiable* Koopman learning: finite-data error bounds that survive the Galerkin projection, dictionaries with guarantees rather than heuristics, and closed-loop stability certificates that account for closure error. Until those arrive, the wise practitioner treats Koopman models as what they are — extraordinarily useful, globally valid *approximations* whose failures are predictable from the spectral theory itself.
