# Sum-of-Squares Programming and Semidefinite Relaxations: Certifying Lyapunov Stability, Barrier Certificates, and Robust Control

## Abstract

This thesis develops a unified account of **sum-of-squares (SOS) programming** and its **semidefinite relaxation** machinery as a computational framework for certifying *stability*, *safety*, and *robustness* of nonlinear and hybrid dynamical systems. From the Gram-matrix characterization of SOS polynomials — a polynomial is SOS if and only if it admits a positive semidefinite Gram matrix in the monomial basis — we show how classical Lyapunov analysis, **barrier-certificate** safety verification, and robust control synthesis reduce to convex semidefinite programs whose feasible solutions are formal certificates of system behavior. The backbone is provided by the *Positivstellensätze* of real algebraic geometry and the **Lasserre moment-SOS hierarchy** of convergent semidefinite relaxations. We develop SOS-Lyapunov synthesis, region-of-attraction estimation, and the Prajna–Jadbabaie barrier framework, then treat robust extensions and SOSTOOLS/YALMIP/MOSEK practice. We prove core representation theorems, analyze convergence and complexity, and identify the frontier: scalability, the SOS–nonnegativity gap, and numerical conditioning.

## 1 Introduction

Lyapunov's 1892 dissertation taught control theory to reason about stability through *functions*: a system's equilibrium is asymptotically stable if there exists a positive definite function whose time derivative along trajectories is negative definite [7]. The method is conceptually complete — converse Lyapunov theorems guarantee such functions exist whenever stability holds — but constructive synthesis remained elusive for nonlinear systems throughout the twentieth century. Engineers resorted to linearization, heuristic energy functions, or numerical simulation, none of which *proves* anything about the true system.

The situation changed fundamentally at the turn of the millennium. Pablo Parrilo's 2000 Caltech doctoral thesis, *Structured Semidefinite Programs and Semialgebraic Geometry Methods in Robustness and Optimization*, introduced a computational realization of Lyapunov's idea: restrict the search to *polynomial* Lyapunov candidates and *sum-of-squares* nonnegativity conditions, and the search becomes a **semidefinite program (SDP)** — a convex optimization problem solvable in polynomial time by interior-point methods [1]. The thesis exploited a deep connection, already appreciated by Shor [8] and Nesterov [9], between real algebraic geometry and convex optimization: a polynomial is a sum of squares if and only if it admits a positive semidefinite Gram matrix relative to the monomial basis.

> **Thesis statement:** Sum-of-squares programming is the unifying convex computational framework that renders Lyapunov stability, safety verification, and robust control synthesis tractable as semidefinite programs, grounded in the Positivstellensätze of real algebraic geometry, with the moment-SOS hierarchy providing principled convergence guarantees and modern solvers providing practical performance.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Semidefinite Programming

A **semidefinite program** is the minimization of a linear objective over the intersection of an affine subspace with the cone of positive semidefinite (PSD) matrices:

```
min  ⟨C, X⟩
s.t. ⟨Ai, X⟩ = bi,  i = 1, …, m
     X ⪰ 0
```

The PSD cone is *self-dual* and convex, and SDPs are solvable to arbitrary accuracy in polynomial time via primal-dual interior-point methods [7] — the computational bedrock of everything below.

### 2.2 Sum-of-Squares Polynomials and the Gram Matrix

A polynomial $p \in \mathbb{R}[x_1, \dots, x_n]$ of degree $2d$ is a **sum of squares (SOS)** if there exist polynomials $q_1, \dots, q_k$ such that

$$p(x) = \sum_{i=1}^{k} q_i(x)^2.$$

Write each $q_i(x) = a_i^T z(x)$ where $z(x)$ is the vector of all monomials in $n$ variables of degree at most $d$. Then

$$p(x) = z(x)^T \left(\sum_{i=1}^{k} a_i a_i^T\right) z(x) = z(x)^T Q z(x)$$

with $Q = \sum_i a_i a_i^T \succeq 0$. Conversely, any PSD $Q$ admits a Cholesky factorization $Q = L^T L$, producing an explicit SOS decomposition. This yields the fundamental equivalence [1][6]:

> **Theorem (Gram-matrix characterization):** A degree-$2d$ polynomial $p$ is a sum of squares if and only if there exists a symmetric matrix $Q \succeq 0$ such that $p(x) = z(x)^T Q z(x)$, where $z(x)$ is the degree-$d$ monomial basis vector.

Matching coefficients of monomials on both sides converts the SOS constraint into an SDP: the entries of $Q$ are decision variables constrained by affine equalities (coefficient matching) and the LMI $Q \succeq 0$. Crucially, SOS implies nonnegativity ($p(x) \geq 0$ everywhere), but the converse fails in general — the Motzkin polynomial $M(x,y) = 1 + x^2 y^4 + x^4 y^2 - 3x^2 y^2$ is globally nonnegative yet not SOS. This **gap** is both a theoretical limitation and, as we shall see, often a numerically irrelevant one.

### 2.3 The Positivstellensätze

The bridge from SOS to *conditional* nonnegativity — "polynomial $p$ is nonnegative on the semialgebraic set $K = \{x : g_1(x) \geq 0, \dots, g_m(x) \geq 0\}$" — is provided by the Positivstellensätze of real algebraic geometry. The three central results:

| Theorem | Representation | Requirement |
|---------|---------------|-------------|
| **Krivine–Stengle** | $p$ in the preordering of $K$ | None beyond semialgebraic |
| **Schmüdgen** | $p$ in preordering, degree-bounded form | $K$ compact |
| **Putinar** | $p = \sigma_0 + \sum_i \sigma_i g_i$, $\sigma_i$ SOS | **Archimedean** quadratic module |

> **Theorem (Putinar's Positivstellensatz [3]):** Let $K = \{x \in \mathbb{R}^n : g_1(x) \geq 0, \dots, g_m(x) \geq 0\}$ be compact and assume the quadratic module $M(g) = \{\sigma_0 + \sum_i \sigma_i g_i : \sigma_i \text{ SOS}\}$ is *Archimedean* (i.e., $N - \|x\|^2 \in M(g)$ for some $N$). Then every polynomial $p$ *strictly positive* on $K$ belongs to $M(g)$.

Putinar's theorem is the workhorse: it tells us that to prove $p > 0$ on $K$, we need only search for SOS multipliers $\sigma_i$ of some degree — and as the degree bound grows, the search is asymptotically *complete* for strictly positive polynomials. The Archimedean condition is mild in practice: adding a redundant ball constraint $R^2 - \|x\|^2 \geq 0$ forces it. The *degree* at which the representation exists, however, admits no bounds depending only on $n$ and $\deg p$ — a fundamental source of the hierarchy's practical unpredictability [10].

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 Lyapunov Synthesis as an SDP

Consider the polynomial dynamical system $\dot{x} = f(x)$, $f: \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^n$ polynomial, with equilibrium at the origin. Lyapunov's direct method requires a function $V: \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}$ satisfying:

1. **Positive definiteness:** $V(x) > 0$ for $x \neq 0$, $V(0) = 0$,
2. **Decrease:** $\dot{V}(x) = \nabla V(x) \cdot f(x) < 0$ for $x \neq 0$.

Replacing nonnegativity with SOS, we search for polynomial $V$ and a small positive definite "margin" $l_1(x)$ such that [6]:

```
Find polynomial V(x) such that:
    V(x) - l1(x)  is SOS        (positive definite)
    -∇V(x)·f(x) - l2(x) is SOS  (strict decrease)
```

Both constraints are SOS programs — hence SDPs — in the coefficients of $V$, normalized by fixing $V(0) = 0$ and $l_1, l_2$ as small SOS polynomials (e.g., $\epsilon \sum_i x_i^{2d}$). A feasible solution *is* a certificate of asymptotic stability; Tedrake's LQR-Trees work [5] extends the idea to *funneled* regions and time-varying Lyapunov functions for trajectory stabilization.

### 3.2 Region-of-Attraction Estimation

A Lyapunov function proves stability, but its *sublevel sets* prove *quantitative* stability: if $\dot{V} < 0$ on the sublevel set $\Omega_\rho = \{x : V(x) \leq \rho\}$, then $\Omega_\rho$ is an invariant **region of attraction (ROA)**. Maximizing the ROA estimate becomes a bisection problem [5][6]:

```matlab
% SOSTOOLS-style bisection for maximal verified ROA level rho
syms x1 x2 rho;
vartable = [x1, x2];
prog = sosprogram(vartable);
[prog, V] = sossosvar(prog, monomials(vartable, [0:4]));   % quartic candidate
% V positive definite, decrease on sublevel set via S-procedure multiplier
prog = sosineq(prog, V - 1e-6*(x1^2+x2^2)^2);
[prog, s] = sossosvar(prog, monomials(vartable, [0:2]));
prog = sosineq(prog, -(jacobian(V,vartable)*f) - s*(rho - V) - 1e-6*(x1^2+x2^2));
prog = sossolve(prog);   % bisection on rho externally
```

The *S-procedure multiplier* $s(x)$ (SOS) encodes the conditional constraint "$\dot{V} < 0$ whenever $V \leq \rho$" via the algebraic identity $-\dot{V} - s(\rho - V) \geq 0$, exactly the Putinar-form mechanism of Section 2.3.

### 3.3 Barrier Certificates for Safety

Barrier certificates, introduced by Prajna and Jadbabaie [4] and extended to worst-case and stochastic settings by Prajna, Jadbabaie, and Pappas, certify *safety*: given initial set $X_0$, unsafe set $X_u$, and domain $X$, a **barrier certificate** $B(x)$ satisfies:

$$B(x) \leq 0 \ \forall x \in X_0, \qquad B(x) > 0 \ \forall x \in X_u, \qquad \nabla B(x) \cdot f(x) < 0 \ \forall x \in X \text{ with } B(x) = 0.$$

> **Theorem (Prajna–Jadbabaie [4]):** If such a $B$ exists, then no trajectory starting in $X_0$ can reach $X_u$: the zero level set of $B$ is an invariant *barrier* separating them.

With semialgebraic $X_0, X_u, X$ and polynomial dynamics, each inequality reduces via the S-procedure to SOS conditions with multiplier polynomials [6]:

```
Find B(x), SOS multipliers λ0, λu, λ such that:
    -B(x)  - λ0(x)·g0(x)  is SOS        (B ≤ 0 on X0)
     B(x)  - λu(x)·gu(x) - ε is SOS     (B > 0 on Xu)
    -∇B·f  - λ(x)·B(x)     is SOS        (decrease on zero level set)
```

No reachable-set computation is required — the decisive advantage over explicit methods [4]. The stochastic extension replaces the Lie derivative condition with a *supermartingale* inequality on the generator of the diffusion, yielding rigorous upper bounds on the probability of unsafety.

### 3.4 The Lasserre Hierarchy in Practice

The Lasserre hierarchy [2] produces a sequence of SDP relaxations indexed by relaxation order $r$ (half the degree bound). At order $r$:

- **Primal (moments):** optimize over truncated moment sequences $y$ with $M_r(y) \succeq 0$ and localizing matrices $M_{r-d_i}(g_i y) \succeq 0$;
- **Dual (SOS):** find the best lower bound $\lambda$ with $f - \lambda \in M_{2r}(g)$.

The optimal values $\rho_r$ form a monotone nondecreasing sequence converging to the global optimum $f^*$ under the Archimedean condition [2][10]. *Flat truncation* — rank conditions on the moment matrices — certifies *finite* convergence and allows extraction of global minimizers [10]. Strong duality at finite relaxation orders was analyzed by Josz, Henrion, and coauthors [10], showing that duality gaps can occur but are practically manageable with the ball-constraint regularization.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The SOS–SDP Reduction in Full Detail

The reduction from SOS feasibility to SDP is worth stating precisely, since every practical implementation (SOSTOOLS, YALMIP's `sos` module, Drake's mathematical program) follows it [6]. Let $p(x) = \sum_{|\alpha| \leq 2d} p_\alpha x^\alpha$ be unknown-coefficient polynomial. The constraint "$p$ is SOS" becomes:

1. Form $z(x)$, the vector of $\binom{n+d}{d}$ monomials of degree $\leq d$;
2. Introduce symmetric $Q \in \mathbb{S}^{\binom{n+d}{d}}$, $Q \succeq 0$;
3. Impose $\langle A_\alpha, Q \rangle = p_\alpha$ for each multi-index $\alpha$, where $(A_\alpha)_{\beta,\gamma} = 1$ if $\beta + \gamma = \alpha$ else $0$.

The matrices $A_\alpha$ are 0–1 *selection matrices*. The SDP size grows as $\binom{n+d}{d}$ — this combinatorial growth is the primary scalability bottleneck (Section 6.1). *Newton-polytope* reduction and *chordal sparsity* exploitation prune the monomial basis dramatically in structured problems, and are implemented in modern toolchains [6].

### 4.2 SOS Lyapunov Theory: From Existence to Computation

The SOS Lyapunov program of Section 3.1 is sound by construction. Its *completeness* is subtler: given an asymptotically stable polynomial system, does an SOS Lyapunov function exist? The answer involves several layers:

- **Converse Lyapunov theorems** guarantee *smooth* Lyapunov functions exist, but not polynomial ones.
- For *homogeneous* systems and *exponentially* stable equilibria, Peet and coauthors showed SOS Lyapunov functions exist at sufficiently high degree via approximation of the "true" Lyapunov function by polynomials and Putinar's theorem.
- In practice, quartic or sextic candidates succeed on textbook systems (Van der Pol, damped pendulum, Lorenz slices), while pathological counterexamples (e.g., the Motzkin-type obstructions) require care.

The standard workflow alternates between *analysis* (verify a given $V$) and *synthesis* (search for $V$ jointly with a controller), the latter being bilinear and hence nonconvex — handled by *alternating* convex searches or $V$–$s$ iteration [5][6].

### 4.3 Barrier Certificates, Hybrid Automata, and Stochastic Safety

The barrier framework extends naturally to **hybrid systems** — automata with continuous flows in discrete modes and guarded transitions [4]. A barrier certificate for a hybrid system is a family $\{B_q\}$ indexed by discrete mode $q$, satisfying mode-wise barrier conditions plus *transition* conditions: $B_{q'}(x') \leq 0$ whenever a jump from $q$ to $q'$ maps $x \mapsto x'$. The S-procedure handles guards and reset maps algebraically, keeping the entire verification convex.

The *stochastic* extension [4] considers Itô diffusions $dx = f(x)\,dt + g(x)\,dW$. A stochastic barrier certificate satisfies $\mathcal{L}B(x) \leq 0$ (generator inequality) on $B(x) = 0$, and the bound $\mathbb{P}(\text{reach } X_u) \leq \frac{\sup_{X_0} (-B)_+}{\inf_{X_u} B}$ follows from the optional stopping theorem — a rare example of a *quantitative* probabilistic safety certificate computed by convex optimization.

### 4.4 Robust Control: Uncertainty as Semialgebraic Structure

Parrilo's thesis [1] already framed *robustness* as semialgebraic geometry: parametric uncertainty $\delta \in \Delta$ with $\Delta$ semialgebraic becomes additional variables constrained by polynomial inequalities. Robust stability of $\dot{x} = f(x, \delta)$ is certified by a *parameter-dependent* Lyapunov function $V(x, \delta)$ satisfying SOS conditions *uniformly* in $\delta$ via multipliers on the uncertainty description:

$$V(x,\delta) - l_1 \in M(g_\Delta), \qquad -\nabla_x V \cdot f(x,\delta) - l_2 \in M(g_\Delta).$$

This generalizes the linear $\mu$-analysis and KYP-lemma machinery to polynomial dependence — the thesis's "nonlinear extension of many LMI-based results in uncertain linear system analysis" [1] — and extends to input saturation, time delays, and switched systems.

### 4.5 Numerical Practice: SOSTOOLS, YALMIP, MOSEK

The practitioner ecosystem has three layers [6]:

| Layer | Tools | Role |
|-------|-------|------|
| Modeling | SOSTOOLS, YALMIP `sos`, SumOfSquares.jl | Symbolic SOS → SDP translation |
| Solver | MOSEK, SeDuMi, SDPT3, SCS | Interior-point / first-order SDP |
| Analysis | Drake, custom bisection loops | ROA maximization, controller alternation |

**Practical guidance** [6]: (1) **Scale the problem** — normalize states to $[-1,1]^n$, since monomial bases are catastrophically ill-conditioned; (2) **Fix the margin** — solvers only understand nonstrict LMIs, so strict inequalities need explicit $\epsilon$-margins; (3) **Bisect, don't maximize** — handle quasiconvex objectives (ROA volume) by bisection on the level $\rho$; (4) **Verify the certificate** — re-check Gram-matrix eigenvalues and residual polynomial identities independently, since round-off can fake feasibility.

## 5 Empirical Results and Proof Sketches

### 5.1 Proof Sketch: The Gram-Matrix Equivalence

($\Rightarrow$) If $p = \sum_i q_i^2$ with $q_i(x) = a_i^T z(x)$, then $p(x) = z(x)^T (\sum_i a_i a_i^T) z(x)$ and $Q = \sum_i a_i a_i^T \succeq 0$ by construction. ($\Leftarrow$) If $p(x) = z(x)^T Q z(x)$ with $Q \succeq 0$, factor $Q = L^T L$; then $p(x) = \|L z(x)\|^2 = \sum_j (l_j^T z(x))^2$, an explicit SOS decomposition. ∎

### 5.2 Proof Sketch: Soundness of SOS Lyapunov Conditions

Suppose $V - l_1$ and $-\nabla V \cdot f - l_2$ are SOS with $l_1, l_2$ positive definite. Then $V(x) \geq l_1(x) > 0$ for $x \neq 0$, and $\dot{V}(x) = \nabla V(x) \cdot f(x) \leq -l_2(x) < 0$ for $x \neq 0$. By Lyapunov's direct method, the origin is asymptotically stable; moreover every sublevel set $\{V \leq \rho\}$ contained in the domain where the decrease holds is invariant and contained in the ROA. ∎

### 5.3 Empirical Benchmarks

Representative results from the literature illustrate the method's practical reach:

- **Van der Pol oscillator (reversed time):** a quartic SOS Lyapunov function verifies a region of attraction closely approximating the true limit-cycle interior; bisection on $\rho$ converges in 6–8 SDP solves [6].
- **Damped pendulum with input saturation:** SOS synthesis with sector multipliers produces a stabilizing polynomial controller and a certified ROA strictly larger than the LQR ellipsoid [5].
- **Acrobot swing-up (4 states):** Tedrake's LQR-Trees [5] compose SOS-verified funnels covering the swing-up maneuver, each funnel an SOS certificate computed in seconds with MOSEK.
- **Barrier verification of a 2D hybrid navigation benchmark:** Prajna et al. [4] certify safety of a nonlinear system with an obstacle using a quartic barrier and the S-procedure, with zero level set cleanly separating $X_0$ from $X_u$.
- **Lasserre hierarchy on global optimization:** at relaxation orders 2–4, the hierarchy closes the gap on standard nonconvex polynomial benchmarks; flat-truncation rank conditions certify exactness and extract minimizers [2][10].

## 6 Limitations

### 6.1 Scalability: The Curse of the Monomial Basis

The SDP dimension $\binom{n+d}{d}$ grows combinatorially. A 6-state, degree-6 Lyapunov search involves $\binom{12}{6} = 924$ monomials and a $924 \times 924$ Gram matrix — roughly 400k decision variables. Interior-point methods scale as $O(N^3)$ in the worst case. Mitigations — Newton-polytope pruning, correlative/term sparsity, chordal decomposition, and DSOS/SDSOS inner approximations — extend reach at the cost of conservatism.

### 6.2 The SOS–Nonnegativity Gap

Not every nonnegative polynomial is SOS (Motzkin, Robinson, Choi forms), and not every positive polynomial on $K$ admits a *low-degree* Putinar representation — degree bounds depend on the minimum value, not just $(n, d)$ [10]. Consequently, SOS programming is *sound but incomplete* at fixed degree: failure to find a certificate proves nothing. The Lasserre hierarchy restores asymptotic completeness under Archimedean conditions [2], but "asymptotic" offers no finite-degree guarantee for any specific instance.

### 6.3 Numerical Conditioning and Strictness

Interior-point solvers handle only nonstrict LMIs, so strict Lyapunov inequalities require artificial margins that distort the feasible set. Monomial bases have exponentially bad conditioning (Vandermonde-like), causing solver failures or spurious infeasibility on poorly scaled problems. Best practice — state normalization, orthogonal polynomial bases (Chebyshev), and independent a posteriori verification of the returned certificate — is essential, not optional [6].

### 6.4 Bilinearity of Synthesis

Joint search for Lyapunov functions *and* controllers is bilinear (products of unknown $V$ coefficients with unknown controller gains), hence nonconvex. Alternating schemes ($V$-step / controller-step) converge to local optima with no global guarantees, and may stall. Convexification via *control Lyapunov function* relaxations or density-function duals helps in special cases but not in general.

## 7 Conclusion

Sum-of-squares programming has transformed Lyapunov theory from an art of clever guesses into a computational science of certificates. The chain of ideas — Parrilo's Gram-matrix SDP reduction [1], Lasserre's moment-SOS hierarchy [2], Putinar's representation theorem [3], Prajna–Jadbabaie's barrier certificates [4], and the SOSTOOLS/YALMIP/MOSEK toolchain [6] — constitutes a complete pipeline from a polynomial vector field to a *machine-checkable proof* of stability, safety, or robustness. Tedrake's robotics applications [5] demonstrate that these are not merely theoretical curiosities but engineering tools that fly on real robots.

The honest assessment is two-sided: no other framework combines the same *generality* (nonlinear, hybrid, uncertain, stochastic), *rigor* (certificates, not simulations), and *tractability* (convex optimization with mature solvers) — yet combinatorial scaling, the SOS–nonnegativity gap, and numerical fragility bound the practical envelope to moderate-dimensional systems.

The frontier is clear. Scalability via sparsity exploitation and first-order SDP methods, tighter integration with learning-based control (neural certificates *verified* by SOS), and automated degree-selection strategies will determine how far the SOS paradigm extends. Lyapunov asked for functions; a century later, semidefinite programming finally gave us a systematic way to find them.
