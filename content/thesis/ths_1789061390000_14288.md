# Two-Sided Matching Markets: The Gale–Shapley Deferred Acceptance Algorithm, the Lattice Structure of Stable Matchings, Strategy-Proofness, and Kidney Exchange Clearinghouse Design

## Abstract

Two-sided matching markets—in which agents on two disjoint sides hold ordinal preferences and prices cannot clear the market—underpin consequential allocation systems from the National Resident Matching Program to kidney exchange clearinghouses. This thesis develops their mathematical and mechanism-design foundations. We present the Gale–Shapley deferred acceptance (DA) algorithm, prove it always yields a stable matching, and establish men-optimality: the proposing side receives its best achievable stable outcome. We then expose the distributive lattice structure of the stable matching set (Conway's theorem) and Gusfield's rotations, which compactly generate every stable matching and yield polynomial algorithms for median and egalitarian variants. On incentives, we prove DA is strategy-proof for proposers (Dubins–Freedman) but that no stable mechanism is strategy-proof for all agents (Roth), and we characterize coalitional manipulation limits. Finally, we trace how Roth, Sönmez, and Ünver engineered top-trading-cycles logic into kidney exchange clearinghouses—using pairwise exchange, capped cycles, and altruistic-donor chains—to convert theory into thousands of life-saving transplants, analyzing the computational hardness and incentive constraints that shape the frontier.

## 1. Introduction

Markets are usually imagined as places where prices do the work. But a vast class of markets has *no prices at all*: no one may buy a residency seat, a public-school place, or a kidney. What remains is a *matching problem*: agents on two sides must be paired according to ordinal, private, often conflicting preferences [1].

The field's origin is a fifteen-page paper. In 1962, David Gale and Lloyd Shapley asked whether $n$ men and $n$ women, each with a strict ranking of the opposite sex, can always be paired so that no man and woman would both prefer each other to their assigned partners. Such a defecting pair is a *blocking pair*; a matching with none is *stable*. Gale and Shapley answered constructively with the *deferred acceptance* (DA) algorithm [1]—work recognized by the 2012 Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences to Shapley and Alvin Roth.

This thesis is organized around four themes. *First*, deferred acceptance in full generality—its correctness, termination, and the extremal theorem that proposers get their best achievable stable outcome while receivers get their worst [1]. *Second*, the hidden geometry of the solution set: stable matchings form a *distributive lattice* (Conway), whose minimal differences—Gusfield's *rotations*—generate every stable matching and unlock polynomial algorithms for fair compromises. *Third*, incentives: DA is strategy-proof for the proposing side (Dubins–Freedman), but no stable mechanism is strategy-proof for everyone (Roth) [2][3]. *Fourth*, the escape from the seminar room: Roth, Sönmez, and Ünver's kidney exchange clearinghouse, where top-trading-cycles logic, pairwise exchange, and capped cycles and chains became thousands of life-saving transplants [4][5][6].

> **Theorem (Gale–Shapley, 1962):** For every strict preference profile, a stable matching exists, and the deferred acceptance algorithm finds one in at most $n^2$ proposal rounds [1].

## 2. Background

### 2.1 The Marriage Model and Its Generalizations

The canonical one-to-one model, the *stable marriage problem*, consists of two finite disjoint sets $M$ (men) and $W$ (women) with $|M| = |W| = n$. Each man $m$ has a strict total order $\succ_m$ over $W$, and each woman $w$ a strict total order $\succ_w$ over $M$. A *matching* $\mu$ is a bijection between $M$ and $W$ (equivalently, a set of $n$ disjoint pairs). A pair $(m, w) \notin \mu$ *blocks* $\mu$ if $w \succ_m \mu(m)$ and $m \succ_w \mu(w)$, i.e., both strictly prefer each other to their assigned partners. A matching with no blocking pair is *stable*.

Three generalizations matter for applications:

| Model | Structure | Canonical application |
|---|---|---|
| One-to-one (marriage) | Each agent takes one partner | Kidney exchange pairs, roommates |
| Many-to-one (college admissions) | Colleges take quotas $q_c$ of students | Medical residency, school choice |
| Many-to-many / contracts | Multiple partners, contract terms | Labor markets with wages (Hatfield–Milgrom) |

In the many-to-one *college admissions* (or *hospital/residents*) problem, each hospital $h$ has a quota $q_h$ and preferences over *subsets* of residents satisfying *responsiveness*: the ranking of individuals extends consistently to sets. Under responsiveness, stability is defined via blocking pairs $(r, h)$ and DA generalizes directly [1].

### 2.2 Deferred Acceptance

The *men-proposing* DA algorithm proceeds in rounds:

1. Each unmatched man proposes to the highest-ranked woman on his list whom he has not yet proposed to.
2. Each woman, among the proposals she receives (including any man she is tentatively holding), keeps the most preferred and rejects the rest. All acceptances are *tentative*—a woman may later discard a held man for a better proposer.
3. Rejected men propose to the next woman on their lists. The algorithm ends when no man is rejected, i.e., every man is tentatively held.

Because each man proposes to each woman at most once, termination is guaranteed within $n^2$ rounds; in practice the complexity is $O(n^2)$.

> **Lemma (Deferred acceptance yields a stable matching):** Let $\mu$ be the output of men-proposing DA. Then $\mu$ is stable.

*Proof sketch.* Suppose $(m, w)$ blocks $\mu$. Since $w \succ_m \mu(m)$, man $m$ must have proposed to $w$ before $\mu(m)$ (he proposes in descending order). Woman $w$ rejected $m$ at some point, so she held someone she preferred to $m$ at that moment, and she only ever trades up; hence $\mu(w) \succeq_w$ that man $\succ_w m$. Contradiction with $m \succ_w \mu(w)$. ∎

### 2.3 Optimality and the Rural Hospitals Theorem

The most striking property of DA is its extremality. Let $\mathcal{S}$ denote the set of all stable matchings.

> **Theorem (Men-optimality):** In men-proposing DA, every man is matched to his most preferred achievable partner: for the output $\mu_M$, there is no stable matching $\mu' \in \mathcal{S}$ with $\mu'(m) \succ_m \mu_M(m)$ for any $m$ [1].

The proof proceeds by induction on rounds: no man is ever rejected by a woman who is *achievable* for him (a partner in some stable matching), because the first such rejection would allow constructing a blocking pair in that stable matching.

A dual argument shows the output is simultaneously *women-pessimal*: every woman receives her *least* preferred achievable partner. The proposing side is thus systematically advantaged—a fact with real policy bite: the NRMP historically ran a hospital-proposing algorithm until Roth and Peranson's 1999 redesign introduced an applicant-proposing variant [6].

A second cornerstone is the *Rural Hospitals Theorem* (Roth, 1986): across *all* stable matchings, (i) the same agents are matched, (ii) each hospital fills the same number of positions, and (iii) any hospital failing to fill its quota is matched to exactly the same residents in every stable matching. A rural hospital cannot hope a different stable mechanism would fill its empty slots.

## 3. Methodology

Our methodology is threefold: *constructive algorithmics* (polynomial-time mechanisms with proofs), *structural combinatorics* (the lattice and its generators), and *mechanism design* (incentive compatibility as a constraint). We work with strict ordinal preferences, matching the informational environment of real clearinghouses, where only rankings are elicited.

For kidney exchange we adopt a graph-theoretic lens: incompatible donor–patient pairs are vertices, and a directed edge $i \to j$ means pair $i$'s donor is compatible with pair $j$'s patient. A feasible exchange is a vertex-disjoint collection of directed cycles (and, with altruistic donors, chains). The clearinghouse problem is then *maximum cycle-and-chain packing* with incentive constraints on reported compatibility [4][7].

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Distributive Lattice of Stable Matchings

Perhaps the deepest structural fact in matching theory is due to John H. Conway (reported in Knuth's monograph): the set $\mathcal{S}$ of stable matchings forms a *distributive lattice* under a natural partial order [1]. Define $\mu \succeq_M \mu'$ if every man weakly prefers his partner in $\mu$ to his partner in $\mu'$. Remarkably, this "men's common order" is a partial order on $\mathcal{S}$ (antisymmetry is nontrivial and uses stability), and moreover:

> **Theorem (Conway's lattice theorem):** $(\mathcal{S}, \succeq_M)$ is a distributive lattice. The join $\mu \vee_M \mu'$ assigns each man his more preferred partner from the two matchings, and the meet $\mu \wedge_M \mu'$ assigns each man his less preferred partner—and both are stable matchings.

The proof that the pointwise "men take the better partner" operation preserves stability is a beautiful argument: if $(m, w)$ blocked $\mu \vee_M \mu'$, then $w$ must prefer $m$ to her partners in *both* matchings, and whichever matching gives the consistent assignment yields a blocking pair against it. Distributivity plus Birkhoff's representation theorem identifies the lattice with the order ideals of its poset of join-irreducibles.

The order-theoretic view has an immediate payoff: the maximum element is the men-optimal $\mu_M$ produced by men-proposing DA, and the minimum is the women-optimal $\mu_W$. Every other stable matching is a compromise between the two extremes.

### 4.2 Rotations: The Atoms of the Lattice

The lattice can be enormous—exponential in $n$ in the worst case (Irving–Leather). Yet Gusfield showed it admits a *compact representation* via *rotations* [1]. A rotation $\rho = ((m_0, w_0), (m_1, w_1), \dots, (m_{k-1}, w_{k-1}))$ exposed in a stable matching $\mu$ (with $\mu(m_i) = w_i$) is a cyclic sequence such that eliminating it—reassigning each $m_i$ to $w_{i+1}$—yields another stable matching $\mu/\rho$ in which every man in $\rho$ moves *down* his list and every woman in $\rho$ moves *up* hers.

Key results:

- **Generation:** Every stable matching is reachable from $\mu_M$ by successively eliminating rotations; $\mathcal{S}$ is in bijection with the closed subsets of the *rotation poset* (precedence constraints among rotations), which has size $O(n^2)$ even though $\mathcal{S}$ can be exponential (Irving–Leather).
- **Algorithmics:** Gusfield's $O(n^2)$ algorithm finds all rotations; from the poset one can enumerate all stable matchings and compute distinguished ones.
- **Fairness:** The *median stable matching*—each agent receiving their median achievable partner—is stable (Teo–Sethuraman, 1998) and computable via the rotation poset: a canonical compromise when neither side's proposal privilege is justified. *Egalitarian* and *minimum-regret* stable matchings are likewise polynomial-time.

| Stable matching variant | Objective | Complexity |
|---|---|---|
| Men-optimal $\mu_M$ | Maximize each man's partner | $O(n^2)$ via DA |
| Women-optimal $\mu_W$ | Maximize each woman's partner | $O(n^2)$ via DA |
| Median | Each agent gets median achievable partner | Polynomial via rotations |
| Egalitarian | Minimize $\sum$ rank-sums | $O(n^3)$ (Irving–Leather–Gusfield) |
| Minimum regret | Minimize worst rank | Polynomial |

### 4.3 Strategy-Proofness: The Proposer's Privilege and Its Limits

A mechanism is *strategy-proof* if truthful revelation is a dominant strategy for every agent. For DA, the news is mixed in a precise way.

> **Theorem (Dubins–Freedman, 1981):** In men-proposing DA, truth-telling is a dominant strategy for every man [2].

*Intuition.* A man's proposals only ever improve the pool of offers women receive; misreporting by truncating or permuting his list can only cause him to be rejected earlier or matched to someone he likes less, never to leapfrog to a better achievable partner. Formally, the outcome under any misreport is weakly worse than the men-optimal stable matching of the true profile restricted appropriately.

But the receiving side has no such guarantee—women *can* profitably misreport in men-proposing DA—and worse:

> **Theorem (Roth, 1982):** There exists no stable matching mechanism that is strategy-proof for all agents [3].

This is a matching-theoretic analogue of Gibbard–Satterthwaite: stability and universal incentive compatibility are incompatible. The practical response has been to protect the *vulnerable* side by making it the proposers: Roth and Peranson's NRMP redesign runs applicant-proposing DA, shielding residents (least able to coordinate manipulation), while hospitals' remaining truncation strategies are limited and well-understood (Roth–Rothblum, 1999). Recent work studies *coalitional* manipulation: accomplice manipulation—in which a woman misreports to help a specific man—can be profitable, and its structural boundaries are an active research area [8].

A parallel strand notes that school-choice mechanisms (notably student-proposing DA, adopted in New York City and Boston) are strategy-proof for students precisely because of the proposer-side theorem [6].

### 4.4 From Marriage to Kidneys: Clearinghouse Design

Kidney transplantation poses a matching problem with a cruel twist: a willing donor may be *immunologically incompatible* with their intended recipient (blood-type or antibody mismatch), so the pair is stuck. But two such pairs can *swap* donors—each patient receiving the other's compatible donor—if cross-compatibilities align. Longer cycles and *chains* initiated by altruistic (non-directed) donors extend the idea. Because monetary compensation is illegal and ethically fraught, prices cannot clear this market; only mechanism design can [4].

Roth, Sönmez, and Ünver (2004) modeled the problem via the *housing market* of Shapley and Scarf, in which each agent owns an indivisible good and Gale's *top trading cycles* (TTC) algorithm finds the unique core allocation. Adapting TTC to kidney exchange required handling a key asymmetry: deceased-donor kidneys cannot be assigned simultaneously with live donations, and pairs arrive with *compatibility-based* preferences. The resulting mechanism is strategy-proof, individually rational, and efficient within its domain [4].

Practical logistics then reshaped the theory:

1. **Pairwise exchange (2005):** Restricting to 2-cycles reduces the problem to *maximum matching* in a general graph—polynomial via Edmonds' blossom algorithm—and the authors characterized the maximal strategy-proof mechanisms in this domain [5].
2. **Longer cycles and the New England clearinghouse (2005–2007):** Allowing 3-cycles captures most of the welfare gains of unbounded cycles in simulation, because blood-type structure (O-donors, AB-patients) makes long cycles rarely necessary. The 2007 paper showed the efficient mechanism remains well-behaved with 2- and 3-cycles, and the authors built the New England Program for Kidney Exchange, the first organized clearinghouse [6].
3. **Chains:** Non-directed (altruistic) donors initiate chains that need not cycle back; a chain of length $k$ yields $k$ transplants from one altruistic donor, and "never-ending" chains (the last donor becoming a bridge donor) amplified impact dramatically.

> **Complexity boundary:** Maximum cycle packing with cycles of length at most $k$ is polynomial for $k = 2$ (matching) but NP-hard for $k \geq 3$; this is why practical clearinghouses cap cycle length (typically at 3) while allowing long *chains*, for which dynamic-programming and branch-and-price formulations scale to national pools [7].

The design also had to be *incentive-compatible for hospitals*: transplant centers could withhold easy-to-match pairs and match them internally, skimming the pool. The mechanism-design response—making participation individually rational for centers via careful modeling of the outside option—remains central to the operations of today's kidney paired donation networks.

## 5. Empirical Results and Proofs

**Correctness and complexity.** DA terminates in $O(n^2)$ proposals and outputs a stable matching (Section 2.2). The men-optimality theorem and the lattice theorem are proved via the rejection-chain and rotation arguments sketched above; full proofs appear in the standard references [1][2].

**Simulated welfare gains.** Roth, Sönmez, and Ünver's simulations (2004) showed that a national exchange with 2- and 3-way cycles would substantially increase both the *number* of feasible live-donor transplants and the *quality* of immunological matches, with gains concentrated among highly sensitized patients who are otherwise stranded [4]. Field experience confirmed it: paired donation programs in the United States, the Netherlands, and the United Kingdom now arrange hundreds of exchanges yearly, and chain programs have produced chains of dozens of transplants from a single altruistic donor.

**Computational practice.** Modern clearinghouses solve integer programs over cycle/chain packings with column generation; the pricing subproblem for chains is solvable in polynomial time under standard caps, while cycle pricing is NP-hard in general—recent work gives fast optimal clearing algorithms for capped exchanges that scale to pools of many hundreds of pairs [7]. Accomplice-manipulation analysis shows that even coalitional deviations from DA are sharply limited in structure, though not eliminated [8].

**Incentive theorems, summarized.** The table below organizes the strategy-proofness landscape:

| Mechanism | Strategy-proof for | Not strategy-proof for | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Men-proposing DA | All men | Women (in general) | [2] |
| Women-proposing DA | All women | Men (in general) | [2] |
| Any stable mechanism | — (impossibility for all agents) | At least one side | [3] |
| TTC-based kidney exchange | Patients/donors (domain-restricted) | Hospitals withholding pairs | [4] |

## 6. Limitations

The framework's power comes with sharp boundaries, and intellectual honesty requires naming them.

*Stability is not efficiency in the usual sense.* A stable matching can be Pareto-dominated for one side, and the lattice's extremes show how far apart the sides' interests can lie. When distributional fairness matters, stability alone underdetermines the choice, and selecting among stable matchings (median, egalitarian) imports normative judgments the theory does not supply.

*Incentives are only partially tamed.* Roth's impossibility theorem [3] is not a technicality: in every stable mechanism, *someone* can gain by lying, and the receiving side's manipulations (hospitals truncating lists, centers withholding easy-to-match pairs) are real operational threats managed by design, not wished away.

*The kidney model abstracts clinical reality.* Compatibility is not binary—crossmatch strength, desensitization, donor age, and logistics all complicate the graph; long cycles are surgically infeasible because every operation in a cycle must proceed simultaneously lest a reneging donor strand a patient who already gave [6]. Chains mitigate this but introduce bridge-donor risk.

*Computational hardness bites at scale.* Capped cycle packing is NP-hard for $k \geq 3$ [7]; national pools rely on integer programming with exponential worst cases, and dynamic settings (pairs arriving over time) raise online and fairness questions the static theory does not answer.

*Preferences are assumed strict, complete, and static.* Ties, incomplete lists, couples with joint preferences (which can destroy stable matchings in hospital/resident markets), and evolving preferences all require extensions—some solved (ties via weak stability), some still open.

## 7. Conclusion

From Gale and Shapley's fifteen pages in 1962 to the operating rooms where a chain of strangers' kidneys saves a dozen lives, two-sided matching theory is that rare body of mathematics that is simultaneously a beautiful combinatorial structure and a working piece of social infrastructure. The deferred acceptance algorithm guarantees stability; the lattice theorem reveals the full space of stable outcomes and its compact rotation encoding; the strategy-proofness results map exactly where truth-telling can and cannot be guaranteed; and the kidney exchange program shows how to bend the theory around legal, logistical, and incentive constraints without breaking it [1][2][3][4][5][6].

The frontier is now computational and dynamic: scaling clearinghouses to national pools with failures and re-optimization, designing dynamic mechanisms for arrivals over time, hardening systems against strategic withholding, and extending strategy-proofness analysis to coalitional and learning agents [7][8]. What endures is the core insight: when prices are forbidden, *stability*—the absence of any pair that would jointly defect—is the coherence condition that makes decentralized markets hold together, and the deferred acceptance algorithm is the engine that finds it.
