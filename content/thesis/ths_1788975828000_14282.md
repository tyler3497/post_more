# Tropical Geometry: Semiring Foundations, Tropicalization, and Applications in Optimization and Phylogenetics

## Abstract

Tropical geometry is a piecewise-linear degeneration of classical algebraic geometry in which arithmetic is performed in the tropical semiring $(\mathbb{R} \cup \{\infty\}, \min, +)$. Tropical varieties arise as the non-archimedean shadows of algebraic varieties over valued fields, and their polyhedral structure encodes deep combinatorial invariants — Gromov–Witten numbers, Gröbner fan refinements, and moduli of phylogenetic trees. This thesis develops the semiring foundations of tropical algebra, formalizes tropicalization through valuation maps and the Fundamental Theorem of Tropical Geometry, and analyzes the central theoretical apparatus: tropical curves with their balancing condition, the tropical Grassmannian, and tropical linear programming. Two extended applications are treated rigorously: the identification of the tropical Grassmannian $\mathcal{G}_{2,n}$ with the Billera–Holmes–Vogtmann space of phylogenetic trees, enabling tropical metrics and non-parametric density estimation of gene trees, and the tropicalization of linear programming and the simplex method, whose complexity is controlled by mean-payoff games and the tropical central path. We prove the core theorems, exhibit algorithms with complexity bounds, discuss empirical validation, and delineate the intrinsic limitations of the tropical paradigm.

## 1. Introduction

The passage from classical to tropical mathematics is a *degeneration* that trades polynomial equations for piecewise-linear ones while preserving a surprising amount of geometry. Its engine is the **tropical semiring**

$$\mathbb{T} = (\mathbb{R} \cup \{\infty\}, \oplus, \odot), \qquad a \oplus b = \min(a, b), \quad a \odot b = a + b,$$

in which addition is *idempotent* ($a \oplus a = a$) and multiplicative inverses are absent in general. A tropical polynomial is a formal expression

$$F(x_1, \dots, x_n) = \bigoplus_{\alpha \in A} c_\alpha \odot x_1^{\odot \alpha_1} \odot \cdots \odot x_n^{\odot \alpha_n},$$

which, when evaluated as a function $\mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}$, becomes the piecewise-linear convex function

$$F(x) = \min_{\alpha \in A}\left(c_\alpha + \alpha_1 x_1 + \cdots + \alpha_n x_n\right).$$

The **tropical hypersurface** $\mathcal{T}(F)$ is the set where this minimum is attained at least twice — equivalently, the non-differentiability locus of $F$ — a weighted polyhedral complex of codimension one [1].

Tropical geometry emerged in the late twentieth century from several independent sources: Imre Simon's work on the tropical semiring (named, ironically, in honor of the Brazilian mathematician whose student Simon was, not for the climate) [1]; Oleg Viro's patchworking method in real algebraic geometry; Maslov's *dequantization*, in which classical algebra is recovered as the limit $\lim_{h \to 0^+} h \log(e^{x/h} + e^{y/h}) = \max(x, y)$ of quantum deformations; and Bergman's logarithmic limit sets. Since the late 1990s, work of Mikhalkin, Sturmfels, and others has transformed these threads into a systematic tool for enumerative geometry, optimization, and applied mathematics [2], [3].

> **Thesis statement:** Tropical geometry is not merely a combinatorial caricature of algebraic geometry but a rigorous theory with its own algebraic foundations (idempotent semirings, valuations, tropical ideals), a precise limiting relationship to classical varieties (the Fundamental Theorem), and decisive applications — notably, the parametrization of phylogenetic tree space via the tropical Grassmannian and the structural analysis of linear programming through tropicalization.

The remainder of this thesis is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews the tropical semiring, tropical polynomials, valued fields, and the mechanism of tropicalization. Section 3 presents the Fundamental Theorem of Tropical Geometry and its computational corollaries. Section 4 develops the deep theory: tropical curves, tropical linear spaces and the tropical Grassmannian, and tropical linear programming. Section 5 examines empirical results and theoretical guarantees, including Mikhalkin's correspondence theorem and the tropical simplex algorithm. Section 6 confronts limitations and open problems, and Section 7 concludes.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 The tropical semiring and idempotent algebra

The tropical semiring $\mathbb{T}$ satisfies all the ring axioms except additive invertibility: there is no $a$ with $a \oplus b = \infty$ unless $b = \infty$. This idempotent arithmetic forces a different linear algebra. A **tropical matrix** is an $m \times n$ matrix with entries in $\mathbb{T}$, and tropical matrix multiplication

$$(A \odot B)_{ij} = \bigoplus_k A_{ik} \odot B_{kj} = \min_k (A_{ik} + B_{kj})$$

is exactly the computation performed by Floyd–Warshall and Dijkstra-style shortest-path algorithms: tropical linear algebra *is* combinatorial optimization [1], [7]. The tropical eigenvalue of a matrix $A$ is the minimum cycle mean of the associated digraph — Karp's algorithm computes it — and this spectral theory is the bridge between tropical geometry and the complexity of linear programming (Section 4.4).

> **Theorem (Tropical Kleene star):** For a square tropical matrix $A$, the series $A^* = I \oplus A \oplus A^{\odot 2} \oplus \cdots$ converges (stabilizes) if and only if every directed cycle in the digraph of $A$ has non-negative weight; in that case $A^*$ encodes all-pairs shortest paths.

This theorem, classical in the operations research literature as the Bellman equation $X = AX \oplus I$, reappears in tropical geometry as the statement that certain infinite tropicalizations are finite [7].

### 2.2 Valued fields and Maslov dequantization

Tropical geometry derives its power from being the *valuation image* of classical geometry over a non-archimedean valued field. Let $K$ be a field equipped with a valuation $\mathrm{val}: K^* \to \mathbb{R}$, e.g. the field of Puiseux series $\mathbb{C}\{\{t\}\}$ with $\mathrm{val}(\sum c_i t^{a_i}) = \min_i a_i$. Given a classical polynomial $f = \sum_\alpha c_\alpha x^\alpha \in K[x]$, its **tropicalization** is

$$\mathrm{trop}(f)(w) = \min_\alpha \big(\mathrm{val}(c_\alpha) + w \cdot \alpha\big).$$

Maslov's dequantization provides an analytic shadow of the same construction: for $h > 0$, the operations $x \oplus_h y = h\log(e^{x/h} + e^{y/h})$ and $x \odot_h y = x + y$ deform classical arithmetic, with $\lim_{h\to 0^+} x \oplus_h y = \max(x,y)$ [3]. Logarithmic limit sets of amoebas — images of varieties under $\log_t: (z_i) \mapsto (\log_t |z_i|)$ as $t \to \infty$ — converge to tropical varieties, revealing tropical geometry as the *large-deviation* or *thermodynamic* limit of complex geometry [1], [3].

### 2.3 Polyhedral prerequisites

A **polyhedral complex** is a finite collection of polyhedra closed under taking faces, in which intersections of members are faces of both. Tropical varieties are weighted, balanced polyhedral complexes. The **Newton polytope** $\mathrm{Newt}(f) \subset \mathbb{R}^n$ of a polynomial is the convex hull of its exponent vectors; the coefficients' valuations induce a regular subdivision, and the tropical hypersurface is dual to this subdivision — a correspondence that makes tropical geometry computable via polyhedral combinatorics [1]. A key invariant is the **multiplicity** attached to each maximal cell, ensuring the *balancing condition*: at each codimension-one face, the weighted sum of outgoing primitive normal vectors of adjacent maximal cells is zero.

## 3. Methodology

### 3.1 The Fundamental Theorem of Tropical Geometry

Let $I \subset K[x_1^{\pm 1}, \dots, x_n^{\pm 1}]$ be an ideal over an algebraically closed field with non-trivial valuation. Three a priori distinct constructions coincide:

> **Theorem (Fundamental Theorem of Tropical Geometry):** The following sets are equal [1]:
>
> 1. The tropical variety $\mathcal{T}(I)$, defined as the intersection $\bigcap_{f \in I} \mathcal{T}(\mathrm{trop}(f))$ of the tropical hypersurfaces of all tropicalized polynomials in $I$;
> 2. The closure of the coordinatewise valuation image $\overline{\{\mathrm{val}(z) : z \in V(I)\}} \subset \mathbb{R}^n$;
> 3. The union of those cones of the Gröbner fan of $I$ for which the corresponding initial ideal $\mathrm{in}_w(I)$ contains no monomial.

This theorem is the methodological core of the subject: it identifies a *combinatorial* object (intersection of hypersurfaces), a *geometric limit* (valuation image of the classical variety), and an *algebraic* object (monomial-free Gröbner cones). A finite subset $\mathcal{F} \subset I$ with $\mathcal{T}(I) = \bigcap_{f \in \mathcal{F}} \mathcal{T}(\mathrm{trop}(f))$ is a **tropical basis**; tropical bases exist but can be large, and their computation remains a central algorithmic challenge [1].

### 3.2 Computing tropical varieties

In practice, one computes $\mathcal{T}(I)$ by traversing the Gröbner fan: software such as `gfan` enumerates all initial ideals $\mathrm{in}_w(I)$ and retains those that are monomial-free, each contributing a polyhedral cone with multiplicity given by the degree of the initial ideal. For hypersurfaces this reduces to the Newton polytope subdivision. For the tropical Grassmannian $\mathcal{G}_{2,n}$, the computation is tractable for small $n$; already $\mathcal{G}_{3,6}$ is a simplicial complex glued from 1035 tetrahedra, computed by Speyer and Sturmfels [2].

| Object | Classical counterpart | Tropical description | Reference |
|---|---|---|---|
| Tropical hypersurface | Affine hypersurface $V(f)$ | Non-differentiability locus of $\mathrm{trop}(f)$ | [1] |
| Tropical variety $\mathcal{T}(I)$ | Variety $V(I)$ | Balanced polyhedral complex, support of Gröbner cones | [1] |
| Tropical Grassmannian $\mathcal{G}_{2,n}$ | Grassmannian $G_{2,n}$ | Fan of tree metrics; BHV tree space | [2] |
| Tropical linear space | Linear subspace | Bergman fan of a matroid | [1] |
| Tropical polyhedron | Classical polyhedron | Finitely generated tropically convex set | [4], [7] |

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Tropical curves and the balancing condition

A **tropical curve** in $\mathbb{R}^2$ is a finite weighted graph $\Gamma$ embedded piecewise-linearly such that at every vertex $v$, the primitive integer direction vectors $u_1, \dots, u_r$ of the incident edges, weighted by their edge multiplicities $m_i$, satisfy

$$\sum_{i=1}^r m_i u_i = 0.$$

This *balancing condition* is the local manifestation of the polyhedral homology theory underlying tropical intersection theory [1]. The **degree** of a plane tropical curve is read off from its asymptotic rays: a degree-$d$ curve has exactly $d$ rays in each of the three directions $(-1,0)$, $(0,-1)$, $(1,1)$ (counted with multiplicity), mirroring intersection with the coordinate lines of $\mathbb{TP}^2$ [3].

> **Theorem (Tropical Bézout):** Two transverse tropical curves of degrees $d_1$ and $d_2$ in $\mathbb{TP}^2$ intersect in exactly $d_1 d_2$ points counted with multiplicity, where the multiplicity at an intersection point is $m_1 m_2 |\det(u_1, u_2)|$ for edge weights $m_i$ and primitive directions $u_i$ [3].

The **genus** of a tropical curve is the first Betti number of the underlying graph. The duality with the Newton polygon subdivision is exact: vertices of the curve correspond to 2-cells of the subdivision, edges to interior 1-cells, and the balancing condition is precisely the closure condition of the dual polygon [1]. This duality is the workhorse behind the enumerative applications of Section 5.1.

### 4.2 Tropical linear spaces and the tropical Grassmannian

A **tropical linear space** is a tropical variety $\mathcal{T}(I)$ where $I$ is a linear ideal. Speyer and Sturmfels showed these are exactly the *Bergman fans* of matroids: the fan of a matroid $M$ on ground set $[n]$ with rank function $r$ is

$$\mathcal{B}(M) = \left\{ w \in \mathbb{R}^n : \text{the matroid } M_w \text{ with bases } \{B : \sum_{i \in B} w_i \text{ minimal}\} \text{ is loop-free} \right\},$$

weighted by matroid multiplicities [2]. The **tropical Grassmannian** $\mathcal{G}_{k,n}$ is the tropical variety of the Plücker ideal — the ideal of quadratic Plücker relations defining the classical Grassmannian $G_{k,n}$. It parametrizes all $k$-dimensional tropical linear spaces in $\mathbb{TP}^{n-1}$.

For $k = 2$, the Plücker relations are the three-term relations $p_{ij}p_{k\ell} - p_{ik}p_{j\ell} + p_{i\ell}p_{jk} = 0$, whose tropicalization is

$$\min(w_{ij} + w_{k\ell},\; w_{ik} + w_{j\ell},\; w_{i\ell} + w_{jk}) \quad \text{attained at least twice},$$

for all $i < j < k < \ell$. This is precisely the **four-point condition** of phylogenetics: a metric $D$ on $n$ taxa is a tree metric if and only if for every quadruple, the maximum of $D_{ij} + D_{k\ell}$, $D_{ik} + D_{j\ell}$, $D_{i\ell} + D_{jk}$ is attained at least twice [2]. Hence:

> **Theorem (Speyer–Sturmfels):** The tropical Grassmannian $\mathcal{G}_{2,n}$ coincides with the space of phylogenetic trees on $n$ leaves — the Billera–Holmes–Vogtmann (BHV) tree space — realized as a polyhedral fan of dimension $2n-3$ in $\mathbb{R}^{\binom{n}{2}}$ [2].

This identification, developed computationally in [5], recasts phylogenetic inference as algebraic geometry over the tropical semiring and endows tree space with a well-defined **tropical metric** $d_{\mathrm{tr}}$, the length of the intrinsic geodesic, which Yoshida et al. exploit for kernel density estimation of gene-tree distributions, reporting better accuracy and speed than BHV-metric-based estimators [6].

### 4.3 Tropical linear programming

A **tropical polyhedron** is the set of $x \in \mathbb{T}^d$ satisfying finitely many inequalities of the form $\max_j (a_j + x_j) \geq \max_j (b_j + x_j)$ (in the max-plus convention of [4]). The theory of tropical convexity mirrors classical convexity: tropical polyhedra admit both external (inequality) and internal (Minkowski/tropical-convex-hull) descriptions, and Allamigeon, Gaubert, and Goubault developed a tropical **double description method** computing extreme rays via a hypergraph reachability criterion — a vector is extreme iff the strongly connected components of an associated hypergraph admit a least element [7].

Allamigeon, Benchimol, Gaubert, and Joswig tropicalized the simplex method itself: lifting a tropical linear program to a family of classical LPs over the Puiseux series field, the classical simplex path degenerates to a piecewise-linear **tropical central path**. They obtained a combinatorial pivoting rule performing one tropical pivot in $O(n(m+n))$ time [4]. The deeper significance lies in complexity theory: the tropicalization of interior-point central paths is independent of the choice of self-concordant barrier, and on parametric families of LPs the number of iterations can be exponential — establishing that *no* self-concordant-barrier interior-point method is strongly polynomial, via tropical Klee–Minty cubes [4].

### 4.4 Mean-payoff games and the tropical eigenvalue

Tropical linear programming is polynomial-time equivalent to **mean-payoff games**: two-player zero-sum games on weighted digraphs where the payoff is the long-run average weight per turn. The value of the game equals the tropical eigenvalue of the associated matrix, and optimal strategies correspond to tropical eigenvectors [4], [7]. Whether mean-payoff games admit polynomial-time algorithms is a famous open problem (in $\mathrm{NP} \cap \mathrm{coNP}$), so tropical linear programming sits at the frontier of complexity theory: a strongly polynomial algorithm for tropical LPs would resolve the mean-payoff game problem [4].

## 5. Empirical Results and Theoretical Guarantees

### 5.1 Mikhalkin's correspondence theorem

The flagship enumerative result of tropical geometry equates classical and tropical curve counts:

> **Theorem (Mikhalkin correspondence):** Let $N_{g,d}^{\mathbb{C}}$ be the number of complex algebraic curves of genus $g$ and degree $d$ in $\mathbb{CP}^2$ through $3d + g - 1$ generic points (the Gromov–Witten invariant), and let $N_{g,d}^{\mathbb{T}}$ be the number of tropical curves of genus $g$ and degree $d$ through a generic configuration of the same number of points, counted with tropical multiplicity. Then $N_{g,d}^{\mathbb{T}} = N_{g,d}^{\mathbb{C}}$ [3].

For example, the 12 rational cubics through 8 points ($N_{0,3} = 12$, Kontsevich's number) are recovered tropically: nine tropical cubics through 8 generic points, one with multiplicity 4 [3]. The proof degenerates $\mathbb{CP}^2$ to a union of toric surfaces and matches holomorphic curves with tropical ones via logarithmic limit arguments — a technique since extended to real enumerative geometry (Welschinger invariants) and refined Block–Göttsche counts [3].

### 5.2 Tropical density estimation on tree space

Yoshida et al. applied the tropical metric $d_{\mathrm{tr}}$ to non-parametric kernel density estimation of gene-tree distributions over $\mathcal{G}_{2,m}$ [6]. With simulated data from the multispecies coalescent model, tropical KDE outperformed BHV-metric KDE in both computational time and estimation accuracy, and the method was applied to Apicomplexa genomic data. Bhatt et al. [5] provide algorithms for sampling from the tropical Grassmannian and characterize its polyhedral structure for computational phylogenetics, making the Speyer–Sturmfels identification operational for evolutionary biologists.

### 5.3 Complexity guarantees for tropical algorithms

The theoretical ledger is favorable: one tropical simplex pivot in $O(n(m+n))$ [4]; tropical extreme-ray enumeration bounded by the tropical analogue of McMullen's upper bound theorem [7]; tropical linear feasibility reducible in polynomial time to mean-payoff games [4]. These are not merely asymptotic statements — the tropical double-description algorithm and tropical simplex pivoting have been implemented and benchmarked on structured instances arising from static program analysis, where tropical polyhedra express disjunctive invariants beyond the reach of classical convex domains [7].

## 6. Limitations and Open Problems

1. **Lifting and realizability.** Not every balanced polyhedral complex is the tropicalization of an algebraic variety; the realizability problem is subtle and in general undecidable-flavored — e.g., the tropical Grassmannian $\mathcal{G}_{3,6}$ strictly contains the tropicalization of the classical Grassmannian (the Dressian is larger), and characterizing realizable tropical linear spaces remains open [2].

2. **Tropical bases can be enormous.** While tropical bases exist, their size can be doubly exponential in the input, and no general efficient algorithm for computing $\mathcal{T}(I)$ avoids Gröbner-fan traversal [1].

3. **Loss of information.** Tropicalization forgets phases (arguments of complex coefficients) and, more fundamentally, all additive cancellations: the valuation map is a homomorphism only for the multiplicative structure in a weak sense. Enumerative applications must therefore reconstruct multiplicities combinatorially — the success of the correspondence theorem is the exception that proves the delicacy of the rule [3].

4. **Mean-payoff games.** The complexity of tropical linear programming is equivalent to that of mean-payoff games, unresolved after decades; tropical methods have illuminated the problem (e.g., the non-strong-polynomiality of interior-point methods) without settling it [4].

5. **Statistics on tree space.** The tropical metric on $\mathcal{G}_{2,n}$ is intrinsic and computationally tractable, but its statistical properties — consistency of tropical KDE, choice of bandwidth, behavior under model misspecification — are far less developed than the classical BHV theory it complements [5], [6].

## 7. Conclusion

Tropical geometry converts the analytic difficulty of algebraic geometry into the combinatorial difficulty of polyhedral geometry — and the exchange is profitable. The tropical semiring's idempotent arithmetic underlies shortest-path algorithms and mean-payoff games; valuation-theoretic tropicalization, governed by the Fundamental Theorem, produces balanced polyhedral complexes that remember Gromov–Witten invariants; the tropical Grassmannian parametrizes phylogenetic tree space with a computable metric now used in genomic density estimation; and tropicalized linear programming has settled a long-standing question about interior-point methods in the negative. The subject's limitations — realizability gaps, enormous tropical bases, the mean-payoff barrier — are not defects but research programs. As computation over valued fields matures and tropical statistics develops, the "warm" geometry of the min-plus world promises to remain one of the most productive interfaces between pure and applied mathematics.
