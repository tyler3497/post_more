# Colloidal Quantum Dots: Hot-Injection Synthesis, Auger Recombination, and the Engineering of Quantum Dot Light-Emitting Diodes

## Abstract

Colloidal quantum dots occupy a singular position at the intersection of synthetic chemistry, condensed-matter physics, and optoelectronic engineering. This thesis traces the arc from the 1993 Murray–Norris–Bawendi hot-injection synthesis [1], which first delivered nearly monodisperse CdSe nanocrystallites by separating nucleation from growth, through the effective-mass theory of quantum confinement [2][3], to the core–shell heterostructures [4] that raised photoluminescence quantum yields toward unity. We examine the central antagonist of the field, Auger recombination — the nonradiative three-carrier process whose ultrafast rates were quantified by Klimov and co-workers [5][6] — and show how it explains both single-dot fluorescence blinking and the efficiency roll-off that has limited quantum dot light-emitting diodes (QD-LEDs). Device milestones from the first polymer–nanocrystal LED [7] through monolayer electroluminescent structures [8], metal-oxide transport layers [9], and high-performance solution-processed devices [10] are analyzed, alongside the cadmium-free InP trajectory [12] and the 2023 Nobel recognition [11]. The thesis closes by assessing remaining barriers to electrically pumped colloidal lasing and stable blue emission.

---

## 1 Intro

In October 2023, the Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences awarded the Nobel Prize in Chemistry to Moungi Bawendi, Louis Brus, and Alexei Ekimov for the discovery and synthesis of quantum dots [11]. The citation honored a deceptively simple idea: shrink a semiconductor crystal to a few nanometers — below its exciton Bohr radius — and its color becomes a function of its size. A vial of CdSe nanocrystals a little under 2 nm across glows blue; let them grow past 5 nm and the same material glows red [2]. No change in composition, only in dimension.

This thesis follows the full arc of that idea. It begins with the synthetic breakthrough that made it practical — the 1993 hot-injection method of Murray, Norris, and Bawendi, which for the first time separated nucleation from growth and delivered nearly monodisperse II–VI nanocrystallites [1]. It then develops the physics of quantum confinement [2][3], the core–shell heterostructures that tamed dot surfaces [4], and the many-body villain of the story: Auger recombination, whose ultrafast rates were quantified by Klimov and co-workers [5][6]. Finally, it examines how device engineers converted these fragile emitters into quantum dot light-emitting diodes, from the first polymer composites [7] and monolayer devices [8] through robust metal-oxide architectures [9] to the high-efficiency solution-processed QD-LEDs of the modern display era [10].

Section 2 reconstructs the pre-1993 landscape and foundations, Section 3 the synthetic methodology, Section 4 four deep threads (nucleation kinetics, confinement theory, heterostructure design, device physics), Section 5 the empirical case, Section 6 the limitations, and Section 7 the open problems — stable blue emission and electrically pumped colloidal lasing — that still define the frontier.

## 2 Background

In the early 1980s, Alexei Ekimov observed size-dependent absorption in semiconductor-doped glasses [11], while Louis Brus, working with aqueous CdS colloids, derived the effective-mass description of electronic states in semiconductor clusters [3]. The physics of size-dependent gaps was clear; the materials were not: aqueous colloids were polydisperse, poorly crystalline, and plagued by surface states — beautiful in principle, unusable in practice.

The 1993 paper by Murray, Norris, and Bawendi changed the materials reality overnight [1]. By injecting organometallic precursors into hot coordinating solvent, they obtained CdE (E = S, Se, Te) nanocrystallites narrow enough to show discrete optical transitions in ensemble spectra. Alivisatos's 1996 review consolidated the new field, framing nanocrystals as an intermediate regime of matter between molecules and bulk solids [2].

The essential parameter is the *exciton Bohr radius*, a_B — the natural length scale of the bound electron–hole pair, about 5.6 nm in CdSe. When the crystal radius R falls below a_B, the exciton is *strongly confined*: its kinetic energy, scaling as 1/R², overwhelms the Coulomb attraction, and the band gap becomes a strong function of size. When R exceeds a_B, confinement is weak and the gap barely moves. This single length scale explains how one chemistry spans the visible spectrum [2][3].

Small crystals are mostly surface: a 3 nm dot has roughly half its atoms at or near the interface, where unsatisfied bonds create mid-gap trap states. Bare cores therefore luminesce weakly. The 1997 introduction of CdSe/ZnS core–shell structures — growing a wider-gap shell epitaxially on the core — passivated those traps and pushed photoluminescence quantum yields from a few percent toward 50 percent and beyond [4]. The dot was now both tunable and bright. What remained was understanding why it sometimes went dark — and that led to Auger.

## 3 Methodology

The hot-injection synthesis remains the workhorse of the field, and its choreography deserves precise description [1]. A cadmium precursor — diethylcadmium originally, later the safer cadmium oxide complexed with phosphonic acids — dissolves in hot trioctylphosphine oxide (TOPO), often blended with trioctylphosphine (TOP) and long-chain amines. A selenium precursor, typically Se dissolved in TOP, is injected rapidly into the hot solution. The procedure unfolds in four kinetic stages:

1. **Injection and burst nucleation.** Cold precursor hits solvent at roughly 350 °C; monomer supersaturation spikes above the nucleation threshold and a burst of nuclei forms within seconds.
2. **Thermal quench of nucleation.** The injection cools the flask, dropping supersaturation below the nucleation threshold while remaining above the solubility limit — no new nuclei form.
3. **Size-focused growth.** Reheating to 240–300 °C lets the existing nuclei grow by monomer addition; because smaller particles grow faster in radius than larger ones, the distribution narrows.
4. **Quenching and purification.** Aliquots are withdrawn to arrest growth at target sizes; size-selective precipitation with nonsolvents tightens the distribution further.

| Parameter | Typical window | Function |
|---|---|---|
| Injection temperature | 300 to 380 °C | Sets nucleation burst density [1] |
| Growth temperature | 240 to 300 °C | Controls growth rate and annealing |
| Ligands (TOPO, amines, carboxylates) | 1 to 4 molar equiv. | Passivation and kinetic control |
| Cd:Se precursor ratio | 1:1 to 4:1 | Tunes nucleation versus growth |
| Reaction time | seconds to hours | Selects final radius, 1 to 6 nm |

Ensembles are characterized by TEM (size, crystallinity), X-ray diffraction (phase), UV–vis absorption (the first exciton peak reads out mean size), and integrating-sphere photoluminescence quantum yields. Single-dot spectroscopy, in which individual nanocrystals are immobilized and their emission trajectories recorded, reveals the blinking dynamics invisible to ensemble averaging [6]. Device fabrication typically proceeds by spin-coating of QD inks onto charge-transport layers followed by thermal evaporation of metal cathodes; electroluminescence spectra, current–voltage–luminance curves, and external quantum efficiency versus current density constitute the standard device metrology [8][9][10].

## 4 Deep Dive

Four threads, each deep enough to sustain its own literature, interlock to explain the modern quantum dot.

### 4.1 LaMer Burst Nucleation and the Kinetics of Size Focusing

The conceptual engine of hot injection is the LaMer model of burst nucleation. Monomer concentration climbs after injection until it crosses a critical supersaturation, whereupon nucleation consumes monomers faster than they are supplied; the concentration then crashes below the nucleation threshold. Nucleation is thus confined to a brief burst, and every particle shares nearly the same birth time. What follows is pure growth — and growth is a focusing process.

The focusing argument is geometric: a shell of thickness ΔR on a sphere of radius R has volume 4πR²ΔR, so at fixed monomer flux smaller particles gain radius faster and the distribution tightens. This holds only while growth dominates dissolution. As monomers deplete, Ostwald ripening takes over — small particles dissolve to feed large ones — and the distribution *defocuses*. The skilled synthesist therefore quenches at the focusing minimum, a timing refined by later work on continuous precursor addition and heat-up methods [1].

> **Theorem (LaMer burst):** *If monomer concentration is driven above the critical nucleation threshold by rapid injection and then quenched below it by thermal equilibration, nucleation terminates while growth continues; under sustained supersaturation the size distribution narrows because radial growth rates decrease with increasing radius.* [1]

### 4.2 Quantum Confinement: The Brus Equation and Beyond

The simplest quantitative model treats the exciton as a particle in a spherical box. Brus showed that the size-dependent gap follows [3]:

> **Theorem (Brus confinement):** *For a spherical crystallite of radius R below the exciton Bohr radius, the optical gap is E_g(R) = E_g,bulk + (h²/8R²)(1/m_e + 1/m_h) − 1.8e²/(4πεR), where m_e and m_h are the carrier effective masses and the last term is the Coulomb attraction; the gap thus grows as the inverse square of the radius.* [2][3]

The three terms tell the story: bulk gap, kinetic confinement cost scaling as 1/R², Coulomb stabilization as 1/R. For CdSe, with m_e ≈ 0.13 m_0 and m_h ≈ 0.45 m_0, the kinetic term dominates below about 5 nm. The Python model below evaluates this relation across the experimentally relevant size range:

```python
import numpy as np

# Brus equation for CdSe: confinement energy vs nanocrystal radius
m0 = 9.1093837e-31      # electron mass, kg
m_e = 0.13 * m0         # electron effective mass in CdSe
m_h = 0.45 * m0         # hole effective mass in CdSe
e = 1.602176634e-19     # elementary charge, C
h = 6.62607015e-34      # Planck constant, J s
eps0 = 8.854187817e-12  # vacuum permittivity, F/m
eps_r = 10.2             # CdSe dielectric constant
E_gap_bulk = 1.74       # CdSe bulk gap at 300 K, eV

def brus_gap_eV(radius_nm):
    R = radius_nm * 1e-9
    kinetic_J = (h ** 2) / (8 * R ** 2) * (1 / m_e + 1 / m_h)
    coulomb_J = 1.8 * e ** 2 / (4 * np.pi * eps0 * eps_r * R)
    return E_gap_bulk + kinetic_J / e - coulomb_J / e

radii_nm = np.array([1.5, 2.0, 2.5, 3.0, 4.0, 6.0])
gaps_eV = brus_gap_eV(radii_nm)
for r, gap in zip(radii_nm, gaps_eV):
    wavelength_nm = 1240.0 / gap
    print(f"R = {r:.1f} nm -> E_g = {gap:.2f} eV ({wavelength_nm:.0f} nm)")
```

The model predicts the celebrated color series: near 2 nm radius the gap approaches 2.4 eV (green emission), while 4 nm pushes the gap toward 2.0 eV (deep red) — matching the measured first-absorption peaks of hot-injection CdSe ensembles [1][2]. The model has limits: effective-mass breaks down for the smallest clusters, where atomistic pseudopotential and tight-binding calculations take over, and it neglects fine structure — exchange splitting, crystal-field effects, and the dark exciton ground state [2]. Yet as a design rule — *pick the radius, pick the color* — the Brus equation has no equal.

### 4.3 Core–Shell Heterostructures and the Suppression of Auger

Passivation was only the first virtue of the shell. In a type-I heterostructure such as CdSe/ZnS, both electron and hole are confined to the core while the wider-gap shell presents a tunneling barrier to the surface, suppressing both trap-mediated nonradiative decay and photo-oxidation [4]. Type-II alignments instead separate the carriers, red-shifting emission for photovoltaics and bioimaging.

The deeper war, however, is against **Auger recombination**: the nonradiative process in which an electron–hole pair recombines and donates its energy to a third carrier, ejecting it to a hot state instead of emitting a photon. Klimov's group showed that in strongly confined dots, Auger lifetimes collapse to tens of picoseconds for biexcitons and of order one nanosecond for charged trions — orders of magnitude faster than the roughly 20 ns radiative lifetime [5]. Auger rates scale with carrier overlap, following an approximately universal volume scaling: larger dots and smoother confinement potentials slow Auger down.

This insight drove heterostructure engineering: alloyed or graded interfaces soften the confinement potential, suppressing Auger by reducing high-momentum Fourier components of the carrier wavefunctions, and thick-shelled giant dots extended multiexciton lifetimes substantially [6]. The same physics explains fluorescence intermittency: a dot that ejects a carrier becomes charged, and every subsequent exciton is born as a trion that Auger-quenches in under a nanosecond — the dot goes dark until it neutralizes. Electrochemical control of the charge state can suppress blinking entirely, confirming the mechanism [6].

### 4.4 QD-LED Device Physics: Injection, Excitons, and Roll-Off

Turning bright dots into bright diodes required solving charge injection. The first QD-LED (Colvin, Schlamp, Alivisatos, 1994) blended CdSe dots into a semiconducting polymer — modest efficiency, but electroluminescence proven [7]. The 2002 breakthrough by Coe, Woo, Bawendi, and Bulović sandwiched a *single monolayer* of dots between organic transport layers, achieving a large leap in power efficiency and demonstrating that excitons could be formed directly on dots by sequential charge injection [8].

Later architectures attacked charge imbalance and quenching: Caruge and colleagues introduced sputtered metal-oxide transport layers enabling current densities above 3.5 A per cm² [9], and Dai's 2014 inverted devices — ZnO electron-transport beneath the QD film — combined tailored core–shell dots with optimized transport to exceed 20 percent external quantum efficiency with low roll-off [10].

Efficiency roll-off at high brightness remains the signature device pathology, and Auger is again the prime suspect. Under electrical injection, dots accumulate excess charge; charged dots form trions; trions Auger-quench. The external quantum efficiency therefore droops as current density rises, following the toy model below, where the quadratic term captures the growing weight of nonradiative multicarrier decay:

```rust
// Toy model: QD-LED external quantum efficiency vs current density,
// with Auger-assisted roll-off competing against radiative recombination.
fn eqe_vs_current(j: f64) -> f64 {
    let eqe0 = 0.20;    // low-current external quantum efficiency
    let j_roll = 120.0; // roll-off current density, mA per square cm
    eqe0 / (1.0 + (j / j_roll).powi(2))
}

fn main() {
    let currents = [1.0, 10.0, 50.0, 120.0, 300.0, 1000.0];
    for j in currents {
        println!("J = {:>6.1} mA/cm^2 -> EQE = {:.3}", j, eqe_vs_current(j));
    }
}
```

Field-induced Stark separation of the wavefunctions and exciton dissociation compound the loss. Mitigation — balanced injection, Auger-suppressed heterostructures, outcoupling structures — pushed red and green QD-LEDs past 20 percent EQE; blue, whose wider gap demands deeper work functions and suffers faster Auger, still trails [10][12].

## 5 Empirical/Proofs

Four independent evidentiary pillars support the picture developed above:

1. **Size–gap correspondence.** The first exciton absorption peak tracks microscopy-measured radii along the Brus curve to within experimental error — the quantitative proof that confinement, not chemistry, sets the gap [1][2][3].
2. **Ultrafast multicarrier dynamics.** Transient-absorption spectroscopy resolves biexciton decay in tens of picoseconds and trion decay near one nanosecond, while single excitons live roughly 20 ns — the orders-of-magnitude hierarchy that makes Auger the dominant loss channel whenever a dot holds more than one excitation [5].
3. **Blinking as charging.** Single-dot trajectories show binary on/off intermittency with power-law statistics, and spectro-electrochemistry shows injected charge quenches emission via Auger while controlled potentials suppress blinking entirely — a causal identification [6].
4. **Device roll-off.** EQE-versus-current-density curves droop precisely in the regime where dots become multiply charged, and Auger-engineered heterostructures flatten the droop — closing the loop from single-dot physics to device metrics [9][10].

Taken together: the synthesis works because LaMer kinetics says it must; the colors follow because the Brus equation says they must; the dots blink and the diodes droop because Auger recombination, measured independently at single-exciton, trion, and biexciton levels, leaves no alternative.

## 6 Limitations

- **Cadmium toxicity** constrains commercialization; the field's answer, InP-based dots, reached high efficiency only in 2019 with ZnSe/ZnS shelled structures [12], and blue InP remains stubbornly inefficient — the wider gap amplifies Auger and demands transport materials with extreme energy levels.
- **Electrically pumped colloidal lasing is still unrealized**: Auger depletes gain faster than practical current densities can replenish it, though sub-single-exciton gain designs have narrowed the gap.
- **Outcoupling** caps planar devices near 20 to 30 percent extraction; photonic structuring helps but adds cost.
- **Hot injection remains a batch art** — precursor reactivity drifts and scale-up beyond grams is nontrivial.
- **Theory still leans on effective-mass approximations** that fail for ultrasmall clusters and alloyed interfaces, where ab initio methods are accurate but expensive.

## 7 Conclusion

In three decades, colloidal quantum dots traveled from polydisperse curiosities to Nobel-recognized, display-grade emitters [11]. Hot injection [1] made them uniform; confinement theory [2][3] made them predictable; core–shell engineering [4] made them bright; and the elucidation of Auger recombination [5][6] explained their intermittency and their diodes' limitations — then pointed past them.

Two challenges define the frontier: stable, efficient blue emission without cadmium — demanding simultaneous mastery of wide-gap synthesis, deep-level transport, and Auger suppression [12] — and the electrically pumped colloidal laser, which would convert the deepest understanding of multicarrier physics into its most consequential application. If history is a guide [7][8][9][10], each barrier falls to the same combination: precise synthesis, quantitative theory, and device engineering informed by both.
