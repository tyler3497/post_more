# The Post-Quantum TLS Transition: Hybrid Key Exchange, KEMTLS, and the X.509 Certificate Problem

## Abstract

The migration of the Transport Layer Security (TLS) ecosystem to post-quantum cryptography is the largest coordinated cryptographic transition in the history of the internet. This thesis examines the three load-bearing pillars of that transition: (i) hybrid key exchange, in which a classical elliptic-curve exchange is combined with a post-quantum key encapsulation mechanism such as ML-KEM so that the session remains secure if either component holds [2]; (ii) KEMTLS, an alternative TLS 1.3 handshake that replaces the server's online signature with a second KEM operation against a long-term KEM key carried in the server's certificate, circumventing the bandwidth and CPU costs of post-quantum signatures in the handshake [1][3]; and (iii) the X.509 certificate problem — the structural fact that post-quantum signatures are far larger than ECDSA or RSA signatures, so substituting ML-DSA into today's certificate chains pushes the handshake past the TCP initial congestion window and measurably degrades web latency [4]. We survey the NIST standards (FIPS 203 ML-KEM, FIPS 204 ML-DSA, FIPS 205 SLH-DSA) finalized in August 2024 [5], analyze hybrid combiners and downgrade-safety, reconstruct the KEMTLS authentication design and its formal analysis in the Tamarin prover [6], quantify the certificate-size crisis and its mitigations — certificate compression, Merkle-tree certificate schemes, and KEMTLS certificates — and close with an empirical performance synthesis and a research agenda.

## 1 Introduction

TLS is the load-bearing cryptographic protocol of the modern internet: it secures web traffic, email, APIs, and virtually all machine-to-machine communication. Its classical foundations — RSA, finite-field and elliptic-curve Diffie–Hellman, and ECDSA — are vulnerable to a sufficiently powerful quantum computer via Shor's algorithm [7]. The threat is not merely prospective. Adversaries can record encrypted traffic *today* and decrypt it once a cryptographically relevant quantum computer (CRQC) exists — the "harvest now, decrypt later" attack. For records with multi-decade confidentiality requirements, such as financial instruments, medical data, and government communications, the transition to post-quantum cryptography (PQC) is already overdue.

NIST's response, after an eight-year public competition, was the finalization of three post-quantum standards on August 13, 2024: FIPS 203 (ML-KEM, a module-lattice key encapsulation mechanism derived from CRYSTALS-Kyber), FIPS 204 (ML-DSA, a module-lattice digital signature derived from CRYSTALS-Dilithium), and FIPS 205 (SLH-DSA, a stateless hash-based signature derived from SPHINCS+) [5]. HQC, a code-based KEM, was selected in March 2025 as a backup standard. Yet standards alone do not migrate protocols. TLS 1.3 must be re-engineered around new primitives whose key material is one to two orders of magnitude larger than the classical objects they replace.

Three sub-problems dominate the engineering reality of this transition. *First*, the key-exchange problem: how to introduce ML-KEM into the TLS 1.3 handshake without betting the internet's confidentiality on a single young hardness assumption. The industry answer is the *hybrid* key exchange — combine X25519 with ML-KEM-768 and require only one component to hold [2]. *Second*, the authentication problem: TLS 1.3 authenticates servers with an online signature over the handshake transcript. Post-quantum signatures are large and slow; KEMTLS replaces that signature with a KEM against a certified long-term KEM key, achieving implicit authentication with smaller transcripts and dramatically lower server-side CPU [1][3]. *Third*, the certificate problem: the X.509 chain, SCTs, and the handshake signature together carry roughly 1.2 KB of classical authentication material; substituting ML-DSA inflates this to nearly 15 KB, blowing past TCP's initial congestion window and adding real latency to every connection [4].

This thesis treats the three problems as a single system: a transition cannot succeed by solving one in isolation.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 TLS 1.3 in One Handshake

TLS 1.3 (RFC 8446) establishes a session in one round trip (or zero, with PSK resumption). The client sends a `ClientHello` containing one or more ephemeral key shares; the server answers with `ServerHello`, its key share, its certificate chain, a `CertificateVerify` message containing a signature over the handshake transcript, and a `Finished` MAC. The signature in `CertificateVerify` proves possession of the private key matching the public key certified in the leaf certificate. Key exchange defaults to ECDH (X25519 or NIST curves) or finite-field DH; authentication is via RSA-PSS or ECDSA signatures under keys certified by X.509 chains.

The protocol's security analysis — notably the Tamarin-based model of Cremers et al. — treats these components as modular: key exchange provides forward-secret key establishment, and the transcript signature binds the session to a certified identity [6].

### 2.2 The Quantum Threat Model

Shor's algorithm reduces integer factorization and the discrete logarithm problem — the security bases of RSA, DH, ECDH, and ECDSA — from sub-exponential to polynomial time on a large fault-tolerant quantum computer. Symmetric primitives and hash functions are affected only quadratically (Grover), and remain safe with doubled key sizes. The relevant threat for TLS is therefore asymmetric: session keys established by classical key exchange lose confidentiality retroactively, while signatures lose unforgeability only against real-time attackers once a CRQC exists.

The NSA's CNSA 2.0 suite mandates migration of national security systems to PQC on a 2030–2035 schedule, and NIST's draft guidance would deprecate RSA-2048 and P-256 after 2030 and disallow them after 2035 [8].

### 2.3 The NIST Post-Quantum Standards

The finalized standards rest on three mathematical families:

| Standard | Pre-standard name | Primitive | Hardness assumption |
|---|---|---|---|
| FIPS 203 (ML-KEM) | CRYSTALS-Kyber | Key encapsulation | Module Learning With Errors |
| FIPS 204 (ML-DSA) | CRYSTALS-Dilithium | Digital signature | Module LWE + Module SIS |
| FIPS 205 (SLH-DSA) | SPHINCS+ | Digital signature | Hash-function properties only |

ML-KEM-768 offers 192-bit classical security with a 1184-byte public key and 1088-byte ciphertext; the derived 256-bit shared secret is combined into session keys. ML-DSA-44 produces 2420-byte signatures with 1312-byte public keys. SLH-DSA's signatures range from roughly 7 KB to 49 KB depending on parameter set, but its security rests solely on hash functions, making it the conservative hedge against a lattice breakthrough [5][9]. FN-DSA (FIPS 206, derived from Falcon) is in final standardization and offers ~666-byte signatures at the cost of complex floating-point implementation.

### 2.4 Key Encapsulation Mechanisms

A KEM is a triple `(KeyGen, Encaps, Decaps)`. `KeyGen` produces `(pk, sk)`; `Encaps(pk)` outputs a random shared secret `K` and a ciphertext `ct`; `Decaps(sk, ct)` recovers `K`. ML-KEM is IND-CCA2 secure under the Module-LWE assumption. KEMs are the natural vehicle for post-quantum key establishment: unlike lattice-based *encryption* schemes, their IND-CCA2 security gives TLS the strong key-exchange guarantees the analysis of TLS 1.3 expects, and their ciphertexts are compact enough to fit the handshake's flight structure.

## 3 Methodology

Our method is a systematization-of-knowledge study over the primary sources of the post-quantum TLS transition: the NIST FIPS publications and standardization reports, the IETF draft specifications for hybrid key exchange, the KEMTLS academic literature (CCS 2020, Latincrypt 2021, and the Tamarin formal analyses), and deployment reports from large operators and CA vendors. We reconstruct each mechanism from its specification, state the precise security claim each makes, and then stress-test those claims against the operational constraints of the web PKI: bandwidth budgets, middlebox behavior, and certificate lifecycle management.

We adopt the following analytical frame. A migration mechanism is evaluated on four axes:

1. **Downgrade safety**: a classical adversary must gain nothing, and a quantum adversary must gain no more than the post-quantum component permits, regardless of how the peer negotiates.
2. **Transcript economy**: every extra kilobyte in the handshake is latency on real networks; we quantify against the TCP initial congestion window (~10 segments ≈ 14.5 KB).
3. **Authentication binding**: whatever replaces the `CertificateVerify` signature must bind the session to the certified identity with equivalent explicitness, or the loss of explicitness must be formally characterized.
4. **Ecosystem fit**: mechanisms must compose with X.509 path validation, certificate transparency, and existing TLS libraries without fork-lifting the PKI.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Hybrid Key Exchange: Design and the Combiners

A hybrid key exchange runs two key-establishment algorithms in parallel and combines their outputs so that the result is secure if *either* algorithm is secure. The IETF's approach (e.g., the X25519–Kyber768 draft [2]) instantiates the TLS 1.3 `NamedGroup` as a single logical group whose `key_exchange` field is the concatenation of the two components' shares, and whose shared secret is the concatenation of the two shared secrets, fed jointly into the TLS 1.3 key schedule:

```python
# Hybrid X25519 + ML-KEM-768 (draft-tls-westerbaan-xyber768d00)
client_share  = x25519_pubkey          # 32 bytes
              + mlkem768_pubkey        # 1184 bytes   -> 1216 bytes total
server_share  = x25519_pubkey          # 32 bytes
              + mlkem768_ciphertext    # 1088 bytes   -> 1120 bytes total
shared_secret = x25519_secret          # 32 bytes
              + mlkem768_secret        # 32 bytes     -> 64 bytes total
handshake_secret = HKDF-Extract(0, shared_secret)  # TLS 1.3 key schedule
```

The combiner here is concatenation-then-KDF, a dual-PRF construction: it is secure if either input is a pseudorandom, independently-generated secret [10]. This matters operationally. If a classical break of ML-KEM were discovered — the lattice equivalent of the SIKE collapse of 2022 — the X25519 component still protects the session against classical adversaries. Conversely, a CRQC breaks X25519 but cannot touch the ML-KEM component. The combination is also negotiation-safe: the hybrid is offered as a distinct `NamedGroup`, so an active attacker cannot strip the post-quantum component without detection — any downgrade attempt changes the negotiated group, which is covered by the `Finished` MAC.

> **Theorem:** *If the key derivation function is a dual PRF and at least one of the two component key exchanges is IND-CCA-secure (for the KEM) or a secure PRF under CDH-type assumptions (for ECDH), then the hybrid shared secret is indistinguishable from random to any adversary that breaks at most one component.*

Deployed reality matches the theory. Cloudflare and Google have run hybrid X25519–Kyber/ML-KEM handshakes at scale; roughly half of Cloudflare's traffic now negotiates ML-KEM-based key exchange, adding only ~2 KB to handshakes [4]. The key-exchange half of the transition is, empirically, nearly solved.

### 4.2 KEMTLS: Authentication Without Handshake Signatures

The asymmetry that motivates KEMTLS is stark: among NIST PQC candidates, KEMs are compact while signatures are enormous. ML-KEM-768 has a 1184-byte public key and 1088-byte ciphertext; ML-DSA-44 has a 2420-byte signature. KEMTLS (Schwabe, Stebila, Wiggers; ACM CCS 2020 [1]) exploits this by removing the online signature from the handshake entirely.

In KEMTLS, the server's certificate carries a *long-term KEM public key* rather than a signature verification key. The handshake proceeds in two phases. First, an ephemeral KEM (or hybrid ECDH+KEM) establishes forward-secret keying material, exactly as in TLS 1.3. Second, the client encapsulates to the server's *certified long-term KEM key*, producing a second shared secret that only the true server can decapsulate. This achieves **implicit authentication**: the client knows that whoever completes the handshake must hold the private key corresponding to the certified public key, because only that party can derive the final session keys. The CA's signature on the certificate (offline, long-lived) still anchors trust; what disappears is the *online, per-handshake* signature.

The performance argument is decisive for operators. A size-optimized KEMTLS instantiation transmits less than half the bytes of a size-optimized post-quantum TLS 1.3 handshake, and a speed-optimized instantiation reduces server CPU cycles by nearly 90%, because KEM decapsulation is far cheaper than lattice signing [1][11]. Time-to-first-application-byte improves in the common unilateral-authentication case. A variant, KEMTLS-PDK (pre-distributed keys), lets the client encapsulate to a cached server KEM key and send application data in the first flight — the analogue of 0-RTT with implicit authentication.

Formally, KEMTLS was first analyzed in the reductionist multi-stage key exchange model [1], and subsequently in two Tamarin models: one adapted from the TLS 1.3 wire-format model of Cremers et al., and one following the multi-stage abstraction [6]. The headline result is strong: KEMTLS(-PDK) satisfies the same security properties as TLS 1.3 in the wire-format model — explicit and implicit authentication, forward secrecy, and session-key independence — with the caveat that application data sent *during* the handshake enjoys only implicit authentication and slightly weaker downgrade resilience until the handshake completes. The second, finer-grained model additionally surfaced minor flaws in the pen-and-paper proof's claims about deniability and graded forward secrecy, which were subsequently repaired — a textbook illustration of why mechanized analysis matters for handshake redesigns.

```haskell
-- KEMTLS key schedule (simplified; cf. Schwabe–Stebila–Wiggers §4)
kemtlsHandshake :: KemParams -> IO SessionKeys
kemtlsHandshake params = do
  (ekEphem, ctEphem) <- encaps (ephemPK params)   -- forward-secret KEM
  k1 <- deriveKey "kemtls stage 1" ekEphem        -- handshake traffic keys
  (ekAuth, ctAuth)   <- encaps (longTermPK params) -- implicit auth KEM
  k2 <- deriveKey "kemtls stage 2" (ekEphem <> ekAuth)
  return (handshakeKeys k1, applicationKeys k2)    -- k2 binds to cert identity
```

KEMTLS is now being prepared for IETF standardization, with implementations in multiple TLS libraries and active investigation of IoT and certificate-profile questions [12].

### 4.3 The X.509 Certificate Problem

If key exchange is nearly solved, authentication infrastructure is not. A contemporary TLS handshake transmits approximately five signatures and two public keys across the chain, SCTs, and `CertificateVerify` — about **1,248 bytes** of classical authentication material. Substituting ML-DSA-44 inflates this to **14,724 bytes**: five signatures at 2,420 bytes plus two public keys at 1,312 bytes — a ~12× increase that lands directly on top of the ~14.5 KB TCP initial congestion window, forcing extra round trips that Cloudflare's measurements show can slow handshakes by 60% or more [4]. ML-DSA-65 chains exceed 20 KB; SLH-DSA chains run to tens of kilobytes per signature.

The problem is structural. X.509 chains were designed when signatures were tens of bytes; path validation, OCSP, and CT all assume small objects. Several mitigations are in flight:

1. **Certificate compression** (RFC 8879) already trims classical chains by ~30% and is more effective on repetitive lattice structures; necessary but not sufficient.
2. **KEMTLS-style long-term KEM certificates**, which replace the leaf's signature key with a KEM key and eliminate the online signature [1].
3. **Merkle-tree certificates (MTCs)**, Google's proposal: the CA signs the Merkle root of all its issued certificates once, and each server staples a compact inclusion proof (~1–2 KB) instead of a chain — per-connection cost becomes logarithmic in issuance volume, at the cost of concentrating issuance-log power in the CA [4].
4. **Dual/hybrid certificates** carrying both a classical and a post-quantum signature, preserving verifiability by legacy validators during the transition — at double the signature cost [9].
5. **FN-DSA (FIPS 206)** as a small signature (~666 bytes) once finalized, roughly halving the chain-size problem relative to ML-DSA, though its floating-point implementation complexity gives conservative operators pause [9].

Let's Encrypt's public roadmap acknowledges the scale problem directly: the Web PKI's transition depends on browser, library, and ACME-client adoption of whichever authentication mechanism wins the standards process [8].

### 4.4 The Long Tail: Middleboxes, Constrained Devices, and Crypto-Agility

Beyond the headline numbers, the transition must survive the internet's ossified middle. Handshakes above ~16 KB trip fragmentation and reassembly bugs in middleboxes never tested with large flights; UDP-based protocols (DTLS, QUIC) face amplification limits that lattice certificates strain; embedded TLS stacks like wolfSSL must restructure static buffers to parse PQC chains at all [13]. Operationally, the winning strategy is **crypto-agility**: negotiate hybrids now, instrument negotiation telemetry, and keep the option to rotate the post-quantum component — HQC as a code-based backup KEM, SLH-DSA as the hash-based signature hedge. The lesson of SIKE's 2022 classical break is that no single young assumption should be load-bearing; the hybrid combiners of §4.1 exist precisely so the transition can absorb a future break without an emergency migration.

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

We synthesize the published measurement literature rather than re-running benchmarks; the numbers below are drawn from the cited primary sources.

| Configuration | Handshake bytes (server flight) | Server CPU vs. classical TLS 1.3 | Handshake latency delta |
|---|---|---|---|
| TLS 1.3 + X25519 + ECDSA (baseline) | ~4.5 KB | 1.0× | — |
| TLS 1.3 + hybrid X25519–ML-KEM-768, ECDSA auth | ~6.5 KB | ~1.1× | negligible (+2 KB fits initcwnd) |
| TLS 1.3 + ML-KEM-768, ML-DSA-44 chain | ~21 KB | ~3–5× (signing) | +60% (extra round trips) [4] |
| KEMTLS + Kyber512, Dilithium II | ~8.3 KB | ~0.1× (no signing) | faster first app data [1] |
| KEMTLS + SPHINCS+-128f chain | ~15+ KB | high (hash signing) | +30–45% on slow networks [11] |
| Google MTC (projected) | ~1–2 KB proof | 1.0× (verification only) | near-baseline [4] |

The KEMTLS measurements also characterize mutual authentication: signing is much more expensive than verifying for most algorithms, so total computational load roughly doubles compared with unilateral experiments. On a 30.9 ms latency network, large certificates — particularly SPHINCS+-128f instances exceeding 15 KB — add 30–45% handshake latency, since the client cannot send its request until it has received and verified the server's certificate [11].

On the proof side, the Tamarin analyses of KEMTLS give the transition its firmest formal footing: machine-checked proofs that KEMTLS(-PDK) matches TLS 1.3's security properties in the symbolic model, with precisely characterized weakenings (implicit-only authentication of early data) [6]. The hybrid combiners rest on standard dual-PRF arguments [10]. What remains unproven at scale is *compositional*: no mechanized analysis yet covers the full deployed stack — hybrid groups, MTC proofs, CT logs, and dual certificates — as one system.

## 6 Limitations

This thesis has several boundaries worth stating plainly.

1. **No new cryptanalysis.** We systematize published results; we do not evaluate the Module-LWE assumption itself. A classical or quantum breakthrough against lattice assumptions would invalidate the quantitative conclusions while leaving the hybrid *architecture* (which exists to survive exactly such a break) intact.
2. **Measurement synthesis, not replication.** Latency and CPU figures are drawn from the cited studies, whose network conditions and hardware differ; operators should re-benchmark on their own topologies.
3. **KEMTLS is not yet standardized.** Its wire format, certificate profile for long-term KEM keys, and negotiation with middleboxes remain active IETF work [12]; production deployment today would be premature.
4. **The authentication endgame is undecided.** MTCs, KEMTLS certificates, and ML-DSA X.509 chains are competing proposals with different trust models; this thesis does not pick a winner, because the standards process has not.
5. **Client authentication and mutual TLS are underexamined.** Most measurement literature, including the KEMTLS evaluations, focuses on unilateral server authentication; mutual-TLS deployments (notably in enterprise and IoT) face roughly doubled costs [11].
6. **Harvest-now-decrypt-later bounds are assumption-dependent.** The urgency argument assumes recorded traffic and eventual CRQCs; timelines for fault-tolerant quantum computers remain contested by an order of magnitude.

## 7 Conclusion

The post-quantum TLS transition decomposes into three problems of very different difficulty. **Hybrid key exchange is solved in practice**: the X25519+ML-KEM combiner is theoretically sound, cheap on the wire, and already deployed at internet scale [2][4]. **KEMTLS is the most promising answer to the handshake-signature problem**: by replacing the online signature with a KEM against a certified long-term key, it halves the transcript, nearly eliminates server signing CPU, and carries machine-checked proofs of TLS-1.3-equivalent security [1][6]. **The X.509 certificate problem is the hard core of the transition**: no standardized signature keeps a full chain within the web's bandwidth budget, and the contenders — certificate compression, dual certificates, FN-DSA, KEMTLS certificates, and Merkle-tree certificates — imply different PKI architectures with different trust assumptions [4][8].

The research agenda that follows is therefore clear: finish KEMTLS standardization and its certificate profile; resolve the authentication endgame in the Web PKI before CNSA 2.0's 2030 deprecation dates force a rushed choice; extend mechanized analysis from individual handshakes to the composed deployment stack; and keep every deployment hybrid and agile, because the only safe bet in a cryptographic transition is the ability to change the bet. The internet has roughly one decade to rebuild its trust infrastructure around mathematics that quantum computers cannot touch. The standards exist; the combiners work; the remaining work is engineering, standardization, and the will to deploy.
