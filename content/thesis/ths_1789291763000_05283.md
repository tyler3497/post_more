# Prime Editing and the Repair-Pathway Logic of Search-and-Replace Genome Engineering

## Abstract
Prime editing rewrites genomic sequence by coupling a Cas9 nickase to a reverse transcriptase and programming the edit through a prime editing guide RNA (pegRNA), achieving search-and-replace genome engineering without double-strand breaks or donor templates. Yet the enzyme is only half the story: whether the reverse-transcribed edit survives depends on a competition between endogenous DNA repair pathways — flap excision and ligation that incorporate the edited strand versus mismatch repair (MMR) that excises it. This thesis develops a repair-pathway logic of prime editing, tracing the system from the Anzalone et al. PE1/PE2 architecture through the PE3 nick-biasing strategy to PE4/PE5, which transiently inhibit MMR via dominant-negative MLH1, and onward to paired-pegRNA systems for kilobase-scale edits. Drawing on structural studies, genome-scale repair screens, and quantitative models of flap equilibrium, we argue that prime editing efficiency is best understood not as a property of the editor but as the output of a cellular decision circuit — and that future gains will come from programming that circuit rather than merely the enzyme.

---

## 1. Introduction

The central dogma of genome editing has long held that precise sequence replacement requires a double-strand break (DSB): cut the genome, then coax homology-directed repair (HDR) to copy a donor template into the lesion. Base editors broke this monopoly by chemically rewriting single bases within a deamination window, but they remain confined to transitions (and, with newer adenine editors, a limited set of transversions), unable to install insertions, deletions, or arbitrary multi-base replacements [1]. Prime editing, introduced by Anzalone et al. in 2019, proposed a third paradigm — *search-and-replace* — in which the editing machinery itself synthesizes the replacement sequence by reverse transcription directly at the target locus, with no DSB and no exogenous donor [1].

The conceptual elegance of the system belies a messy biological reality. The prime editor does not so much *write* the genome as *propose* an edit: reverse transcription generates an edited 3′ DNA flap that must then win a thermodynamic and enzymatic competition against the original unedited 5′ flap, and the resulting heteroduplex must survive the cell's mismatch surveillance before replication locks it in. Editing outcomes therefore depend as much on the host cell's repair-pathway wiring as on the editor's catalytic design. This thesis formalizes that dependence as a *repair-pathway logic*: a small set of competing reactions — flap equilibration, structure-specific excision, MMR recognition, nick-directed resynthesis — whose relative rates determine whether a given pegRNA program executes or is silently reverted.

We proceed as follows. Section 2 reconstructs the canonical prime editing mechanism and the PE1–PE5 lineage. Section 3 presents our methodological lens: modeling editing as a stochastic competition over DNA intermediates. Section 4 dissects four facets of the repair logic — flap thermodynamics, the MMR antagonism and its pharmacologic-genetic suppression, nick timing as a repair gate, and the extension to paired-pegRNA architectures. Section 5 surveys empirical results and offers a quantitative proof sketch of the MMR-inhibition gain. Section 6 examines limitations, and Section 7 concludes with a research program for repair-aware editor design.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 The prime editor and the pegRNA

A prime editor is a fusion protein: *Streptococcus pyogenes* Cas9 nickase (H840A, which nicks rather than cuts) tethered to an engineered Moloney murine leukemia virus reverse transcriptase (M-MLV RT) [1][3]. Its guide, the pegRNA, extends a standard sgRNA with a 3′ extension encoding two functional modules: the *primer binding site* (PBS, typically 10–15 nt) and the *reverse transcription template* (RTT), which carries the desired edit [1][4].

The reaction cycle proceeds in discrete steps:

1. **Search.** The spacer directs nCas9 to the protospacer adjacent to an NGG PAM, forming an R-loop that displaces the non-target (PAM-containing) strand.
2. **Nick.** nCas9 nicks the displaced non-edited strand, liberating a 3′-OH.
3. **Prime.** The liberated 3′ end anneals to the pegRNA's PBS, positioning it for extension.
4. **Reverse transcribe.** M-MLV RT copies the RTT, synthesizing a 3′ DNA flap that encodes the edit.
5. **Resolve.** The edited 3′ flap and the original unedited 5′ flap compete for annealing to the complementary strand; cellular structure-specific endonucleases (e.g., FEN1) preferentially excise 5′ flaps, and ligation seals the edited strand in, leaving a heteroduplex.
6. **Fix.** Replication or repair copies the edit onto the complementary strand, permanently installing it [2][3].

PE1 used wild-type M-MLV RT; PE2 substituted an engineered RT with improved thermostability and processivity, raising efficiency [1]. PE3 added a second sgRNA that nicks the *unedited* strand, biasing repair to use the edited strand as template; PE3b refined this by designing the second nick to match only the edited allele, so nicking occurs preferentially after the edit is installed, suppressing indel byproducts [1][3].

### 2.2 Structural insight

Cryo-electron microscopy has now resolved the prime editor–pegRNA–DNA complex in pre-initiation, initiation, elongation, and termination states [4]. These structures reveal how the RT domain engages the PBS–DNA heteroduplex and how the complex accommodates the growing 3′ flap — providing a physical basis for rational pegRNA design, including optimal PBS/RTT lengths and the placement of edits relative to the nick [4][8].

### 2.3 The pegRNA stability problem

The 3′ extension that makes prime editing programmable is also its Achilles' heel: exposed 3′ ends are substrates for cellular exonucleases. Engineered pegRNAs (epegRNAs) append structured RNA motifs such as tevopreQ1 or mpknot to the 3′ terminus, conferring exonuclease resistance and substantially improving efficiency [3][7]. A genome-scale CRISPRi screen further identified the endogenous La protein, a small RNA-binding protein, as a key determinant of prime editing — La binds and stabilizes pegRNAs, and its depletion collapses editing [5].

> **Theorem (Repair-Pathway Dominance):** *For a fixed pegRNA program, the variance in prime editing efficiency across cell types is dominated by the relative activities of antagonistic repair pathways (MMR versus flap resolution), not by editor expression or target accessibility.*

This thesis defends that claim by examining each pathway in turn.

## 3. Methodology

Our analysis synthesizes three evidence streams: (i) mechanistic literature from the 2019 discovery through 2025 optimization reviews [1][2][3][7]; (ii) structural data from cryo-EM studies of the editing complex [4]; and (iii) quantitative reasoning about the reaction network, formalized below as a stochastic competition model.

We treat prime editing as a program executed by an unreliable cellular interpreter. The editor proposes a branched intermediate — the 3′/5′ flap pair — and the cell's repair machinery resolves the branch. Each resolution pathway is modeled as a competing Poisson process with a rate constant conditioned on the intermediate's structure:

```python
# Competing-rates model of flap resolution (illustrative)
import math

def edit_probability(k_incorp, k_mmr, k_revert, t_window):
    """Probability the edited flap is incorporated before MMR excision
    or spontaneous reversion, given competing exponential rates."""
    k_total = k_incorp + k_mmr + k_revert
    # Probability incorporation wins the race within the repair window
    p_win = k_incorp / k_total
    p_in_window = 1 - math.exp(-k_total * t_window)
    return p_win * p_in_window

# Baseline PE2: MMR active, no second nick
p_pe2 = edit_probability(k_incorp=0.8, k_mmr=2.5, k_revert=1.0, t_window=2.0)
# PE5: MMR inhibited (MLH1dn) + nick-biased resynthesis
p_pe5 = edit_probability(k_incorp=3.2, k_mmr=0.3, k_revert=1.0, t_window=2.0)
print(f"PE2 ~ {p_pe2:.2f}, PE5 ~ {p_pe5:.2f}")
```

The model is deliberately coarse — real rate constants depend on edit type, sequence context, and cell cycle — but it captures the essential logic: *efficiency gains correspond to reweighting the repair competition*, whether by accelerating incorporation (PE3 nicking), decelerating excision (MMR inhibition), or both (PE5) [2][6].

To reason rigorously about the edit program itself, we can encode the pegRNA specification as a typed transformation:

```haskell
-- PegRNA program as a typed edit specification
data Edit = Subst Int Char        -- position, new base
          | Insert Int String     -- position, inserted sequence
          | Delete Int Int        -- start, length

data PegRNA = PegRNA
  { spacer :: String   -- 20 nt targeting sequence
  , pbs    :: String   -- 10-15 nt primer binding site
  , rtt    :: [Edit]   -- reverse transcription template program
  }

-- The repair interpreter: total function only if every edit
-- survives flap resolution AND mismatch repair
interpret :: RepairContext -> PegRNA -> Either Reverted Genome
interpret ctx peg = do
  flap   <- reverseTranscribe ctx peg      -- 3' edited flap
  winner <- resolveFlaps ctx flap          -- flap competition
  duplex <- ligate ctx winner
  surviveMMR ctx duplex                    -- MMR surveillance gate
```

The `Either Reverted Genome` return type is the formal content of our thesis: prime editing is a computation whose result is *reverted* unless every repair gate passes.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Flap thermodynamics: the first gate

After reverse transcription, the locus presents two redundant single-stranded flaps: the edited 3′ flap and the original 5′ flap. Their competition for annealing is an equilibrium process in which the unedited 5′ flap is typically thermodynamically favored, being perfectly complementary [2]. The cell breaks this symmetry enzymatically: 5′ flaps are preferred substrates of structure-specific endonucleases such as FEN1, which act during lagging-strand synthesis, biasing resolution toward the edited strand [2][3].

This has a direct design consequence. Longer RTTs and edits positioned far from the nick produce longer flaps whose annealing equilibrium and nuclease processing differ markedly; pegRNA design tools trained by deep transfer learning now predict efficiency from these features [8]. The flap gate is thus partially programmable — PBS length tunes priming efficiency, RTT length tunes flap stability — but its outcome is ultimately adjudicated by endogenous nucleases the editor does not control.

### 4.2 The MMR antagonism: the second gate

Even when the edited flap wins, the resulting heteroduplex contains mismatches or insertion/deletion loops that are canonical substrates of the DNA mismatch repair pathway. The MutSα (MSH2–MSH6) and MutSβ (MSH2–MSH3) sensors recognize the lesion, recruit MutLα (MLH1–PMS2), and direct excision of the newly synthesized — i.e., *edited* — strand, followed by resynthesis from the unedited template [2][6]. MMR is, in effect, an immune system against precisely the intermediate prime editing must create.

The evidence is systematic. A CRISPRi screen of 476 DNA-repair genes identified MMR components (MSH2, MSH6, MLH1, PMS2) as the strongest suppressors of prime editing: their depletion increased efficiency, while their presence reverted edits [2][6]. Notably, certain edit classes naturally evade MMR — G•C-to-C•G transversions and multi-base substitutions create heteroduplexes with clustered mismatches that MMR recognizes poorly, and these edits install at markedly higher efficiency [3]. The repair logic is legible in the data: *MMR is a sequence-dependent filter on the edit program*.

Two engineering responses followed. First, transient MMR inhibition: Chen et al. screened dominant-negative MMR variants and identified MLH1dn (lacking residues 754–756, ablating endonuclease function); co-expression with PE2 yields PE4 and with PE3 yields PE5, boosting efficiency ~7.7-fold over PE2 and ~2-fold over PE3, with PE5 additionally halving indel byproducts [2][6]. No overt microsatellite instability was observed under transient expression, though long-term mutational burden remains under investigation [6]. Second, MMR evasion by design: installing additional silent mutations near the intended edit creates a multiply-mismatched heteroduplex that escapes MMR recognition, improving efficiency without globally perturbing repair — at the cost of requiring empirical optimization per target [3].

### 4.3 Nick timing as a repair-logic gate

PE3's second nick is best understood not as "more cutting" but as *reprogramming the repair logic*. Nicking the unedited strand signals to the repair machinery that the unedited strand is the damaged one, directing long-patch resynthesis to copy the edited strand [1][3]. PE3b sharpens the logic temporally: its nicking guide matches only the edited sequence, so the biasing nick fires preferentially *after* the edit is installed, avoiding the concurrent-nick indel byproducts of PE3 [1][3].

We can express this as a temporal-logic specification over the repair computation:

```tla+
------------------------------ MODULE PrimeEdit ----------------------------
VARIABLES flap, duplex, edited

TypeOK == flap \in {"none", "3p_edited", "5p_unedited", "resolved"}
        /\ duplex \in {"none", "heteroduplex", "homoduplex_edited"}

InstallEdit == flap = "none" /\ flap' = "3p_edited"

ResolveFlaps == flap = "3p_edited" /\ flap' = "resolved"
               /\ duplex' = "heteroduplex"

\* PE3b: the biasing nick may fire only after the edit is installed
BiasNick == duplex = "heteroduplex" /\ duplex' = "homoduplex_edited"

\* Safety: the genome is never rewritten before flap resolution
Safe == [][duplex /= "homoduplex_edited" => flap /= "none"]_<<flap, duplex>>
=============================================================================
```

The PE3b design is a *guard strengthening*: by conditioning the second nick on the edited allele, it converts a race between nicking and flap resolution into an ordered sequence, eliminating the interleavings that produce indels [1].

| System | Architecture | Nick strategy | MMR handling | Reported gain |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| PE1 | nCas9–M-MLV RT (WT) | Single nick (edited strand) | None | Baseline [1] |
| PE2 | nCas9–engineered RT | Single nick | None | ~2–5× over PE1 [1] |
| PE3 | PE2 + nicking sgRNA | Dual nick (both strands) | None | Higher install, more indels [1] |
| PE3b | PE2 + edit-matched nicking sgRNA | Temporally gated nick | None | PE3 efficiency, fewer indels [1] |
| PE4 | PE2 + MLH1dn | Single nick | Transient MMR inhibition | ~7.7× over PE2 [2] |
| PE5 | PE3 + MLH1dn | Dual nick | Transient MMR inhibition | ~2× over PE3, ~2× fewer indels [2] |
| PEmax/PE4max/PE5max | Optimized architecture editor | As above | As above | Further gains in hPSCs [7] |

### 4.4 Beyond substitutions: paired pegRNAs and kilobase-scale logic

The repair-pathway logic extends gracefully to larger edits. Twin prime editing (twin-PE) deploys two pegRNAs targeting opposite strands, generating complementary edited flaps that anneal to each other — the repair machinery then resolves a fully edited duplex rather than a heteroduplex, enabling insertions and deletions of hundreds of base pairs [3][7]. Combined with site-specific recombinases, prime editing has achieved RNA-programmed insertions exceeding 5 kb in mammalian cells [3].

Conceptually, twin-PE changes the game the repair pathways are playing: by eliminating the heteroduplex intermediate, it sidesteps the MMR gate entirely and presents the cell with a substrate — a double-flap recombination intermediate — that the endogenous machinery resolves by annealing rather than surveillance [7]. The lesson generalizes: *each prime editing generation can be read as a redesign of the DNA intermediate to route around a specific repair gate*.

## 5. Empirical Results and Proofs

The quantitative record supports the repair-pathway account with unusual clarity:

1. **MMR screen causality.** The CRISPRi screen targeting 476 repair genes found MMR genes as the dominant suppressors; inhibition of MSH2, MSH6, MLH1, or PMS2 each increased editing, establishing a causal — not correlative — role [2][6].
2. **Fold-change magnitudes.** PE4 improves on PE2 by ~7.7-fold and PE5 on PE3 by ~2-fold across cell types, with the gain largest in MMR-proficient cells — exactly the signature of removing an antagonistic pathway rather than improving catalysis [2].
3. **Edit-type dependence.** Multi-base and G•C-to-C•G edits, which evade MMR, install efficiently even without MLH1dn, while single-transition edits benefit most from MMR inhibition — the predicted interaction between edit program and repair filter [3].
4. **Indel reduction.** PE5 halves indel byproducts relative to PE3, consistent with MMR's role in processing nicked intermediates into indels when the second nick fires prematurely [2][3].

> **Theorem (MMR-Inhibition Gain Bound):** *Let p₀ be baseline incorporation probability and let μ ∈ [0,1] be the fraction of heteroduplexes excised by MMR. Then transient MMR inhibition with residual activity μ′ < μ raises efficiency to at most p₀/(1−μ), with equality when flap resolution is otherwise perfect.*

*Proof sketch.* Partition editing attempts into those whose flaps resolve to heteroduplex (probability p₀) and those lost earlier. Conditional on heteroduplex formation, survival requires evading MMR (probability 1−μ). Efficiency is thus p₀(1−μ) at baseline and p₀(1−μ′) under inhibition; the ratio is (1−μ′)/(1−μ) ≤ 1/(1−μ). The bound is tight when p₀ → 1, i.e., when flap thermodynamics and nick biasing are already optimal — explaining why MMR inhibition helps PE2 (7.7×) more than PE3 (2×): PE3's nick biasing has already captured part of the available headroom [2]. ∎

## 6. Limitations

The repair-pathway logic, while explanatory, faces genuine limits. **Efficiency remains variable**: across loci, edits, and cell types, prime editing ranges from a few percent to over 70%, and pegRNA design rules — though improving via deep learning — still require empirical validation [8]. **Delivery is constrained**: the editor (~6.3 kb coding sequence plus pegRNA) exceeds single-AAV packaging limits, complicating therapeutic deployment and motivating split-intein and compact-Cas variants [3][7]. **MMR inhibition carries risk**: even transient MLH1dn expression raises the specter of elevated genome-wide mutation rates and microsatellite instability; the absence of observed instability in short-term assays does not settle the long-term safety question for clinical use [6]. **Off-target effects**, though lower than nuclease-based editing, are nonzero: pegRNA-independent off-target reverse transcription and nickase off-target binding require continued characterization [3][5]. Finally, our competing-rates model is phenomenological — true rate constants for flap nucleases and MMR excision at edited loci have not been measured *in situ*, and chromatin context, transcription, and cell-cycle phase all modulate the repair competition in ways the current formalism abstracts away [4][7].

## 7. Conclusion

Prime editing is conventionally narrated as an enzyme story — a better nickase, a tougher reverse transcriptase, a more stable pegRNA. This thesis has argued for the complementary narration: prime editing is a *repair-pathway* story, in which the editor proposes and the cell disposes. Each generational advance — PE2's engineered RT, PE3's biasing nick, PE3b's temporal gating, PE4/PE5's MMR inhibition, epegRNAs' exonuclease resistance, twin-PE's heteroduplex elimination — is legible as a move in a game against endogenous repair, reweighting a competition over DNA intermediates rather than merely accelerating catalysis.

The research program this suggests is repair-aware by construction: editors co-designed with their repair context, pegRNAs optimized against explicit models of MMR recognition, nick timing programmed as temporal logic, and safety engineered through transient, self-limiting repair modulation rather than permanent pathway ablation. If the first era of prime editing taught us to write the edit program, the next must teach us to program the interpreter. The genome is not a blank tape; it is a heavily guarded manuscript, and search-and-replace engineering succeeds exactly to the extent that it negotiates with the guards.
