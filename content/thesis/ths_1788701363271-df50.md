# Recursive Zero-Knowledge Proofs via Folding: Halo, Nova and STARK-Based Incremental Verifiable Computation, Accumulation Schemes, and Sublinear Verification for Rollups

## Abstract

Recursive zero-knowledge proofs transform verifiable computation from a one-shot primitive into a composable one: a succinct proof attests to the correctness of an entire chain of prior proofs, enabling incremental verifiable computation (IVC) whose verification cost is independent of computation depth. This thesis unifies the three traditions of recursion — the *nested-amortization* approach of Halo [2], the *folding-scheme* approach of Nova [1] and HyperNova [4], and the *transparent STARK-based* approach of Ben-Sasson et al. [3] — together with the accumulation schemes of Bünz et al. [5]. We formalize folding as an interactive reduction from checking two NP instance–witness pairs to checking one, prove that it composes into an IVC scheme with constant recursion overhead, and compare asymptotic and concrete costs: Nova's two-multiexponentiation prover, Halo's amortized logarithmic verification, and STARKs' polylogarithmic verification. We survey soundness reductions under discrete-logarithm, knowledge-of-exponent, and hash-based assumptions, derive proof-size-versus-depth scaling laws for rollups, and close with open problems in post-quantum folding, cycle-free accumulation, and the limits of sublinear verification.

## 1. Introduction

Zero-knowledge proofs of knowledge were introduced by Goldwasser, Micali, and Rackoff as interactive protocols in which a prover convinces a verifier that a statement is true while revealing nothing beyond its validity. Four decades later, succinct arguments underpin privacy-preserving cryptocurrencies and *zk-rollups*: layer-2 systems in which thousands of transactions are compressed into a single on-chain proof. Yet rollups face a structural bottleneck: as batched transactions grow, the *prover's* burden grows with it, while on-chain verification must stay cheap. Recursion resolves this: the prover produces a sequence of small proofs, each certifying that the previous proof was valid *and* that the next batch of work was performed correctly [6]. The final object the chain verifies is a single proof of constant size.

This paradigm is formalized as *incrementally verifiable computation* (IVC), introduced by Valiant, in which a prover recursively establishes the correctness of an iterated nondeterministic computation $y = F^{(\ell)}(x)$ for $\ell > 0$. The classical approach to IVC embeds a SNARK verifier *inside* the circuit being proven: each recursive step proves "I verified the previous SNARK proof and executed one step of $F$." This *naive recursion* is cryptographically expensive: SNARK verifiers rely on bilinear pairings, and arithmetizing pairing operations inside the proof system's own field triggers the *field-mismatch problem*, forcing the use of carefully constructed 2-cycles of elliptic curves [6, 1].

Two breakthroughs changed the landscape. First, **Halo** (Bowe, Grinspan, Hornby) [2] showed that recursion need not embed a full SNARK verifier: by *nested amortization* — deferring expensive verification work to a later, cheaper check with an untrusted helper — Halo achieved the first practical recursion without a trusted setup, on an ordinary cycle of 255-bit prime-order curves. Second, **Nova** (Kothapalli, Setty, Tzialla) [1] introduced *folding schemes*, weaker than SNARKs: they merely reduce checking two instances of an NP relation to checking one. Nova's folding for *relaxed* R1CS yields IVC with the smallest recursion overhead known — two group scalar multiplications in-circuit — requiring no trusted setup, no FFTs, and no pairings. **HyperNova** [4] folds customizable constraint systems with optimal prover cost (a single MSM), zero-knowledge "for free," and CycleFold instantiation over curve cycles.

Meanwhile, a parallel hash-based tradition — **STARKs** (Ben-Sasson et al.) [3] — achieves transparency and conjectured post-quantum security through the FRI interactive oracle proof and Reed–Solomon codes, with polylogarithmic verification but proofs hundreds of kilobytes long. STARKs are deployed at industrial scale (StarkNet, StarkEx), and their recursive composition powers the largest rollups in production [3]. Finally, the *accumulation* schemes of Bünz, Fisch, and Szepieniec ("Halo Infinite") [5] generalized Halo's nested amortization to *any* additive polynomial commitment scheme.

This thesis unifies these threads. We present:

1. **A formal framework** for folding schemes as interactive reductions with relaxed witnesses, and a composition theorem: any knowledge-sound folding scheme for an NP-complete relation implies an IVC scheme.
2. **A comparative analysis** of Halo's nested amortization versus Nova's folding versus STARK-based recursion, across prover time, recursion overhead, proof size, verification time, setup assumptions, and post-quantum security.
3. **An accumulation-theoretic view** in which recursion decomposes into a *deferred check* and a *decider*, explaining why accumulator schemes remove the need for algebraic cycles of elliptic curves [5].
4. **Empirical benchmarks** at rollup scale and proof-size-versus-depth scaling laws.
5. **Limitations and open problems**: the post-quantum gap in folding, witness-boundedness of relaxed R1CS, and lower bounds for IVC.

> **Theorem:** (Informal, IVC from folding.) Let $F$ be a nondeterministic step function. If there exists a public-coin knowledge-sound folding scheme for a relaxed NP relation encoding $F$'s step circuit, then there exists a public-coin IVC scheme for $F$ whose recursion overhead per step is the cost of one folding-verifier circuit, with prover work per step dominated by one fold of two instances.

The remainder of the thesis: Section 2 reviews SNARKs, STARKs, arithmetizations, curve cycles, and Valiant's IVC. Section 3 defines folding and accumulation schemes and proves the IVC composition theorem. Section 4 is the deep dive: Nova's folding protocol, Halo's nested amortization, STARK recursion, and accumulation. Section 5 derives empirical and complexity results; Section 6 discusses limitations; Section 7 concludes.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 Succinct Arguments: SNARKs and STARKs

A *succinct non-interactive argument of knowledge* (SNARK) for an NP relation $\mathcal{R}$ lets a prover knowing witness $w$ for instance $x$ produce a proof $\pi$ of size $\mathrm{poly}(\lambda, \log |C|)$, verified in time $\mathrm{poly}(\lambda, |x|, \log |C|)$. SNARKs divide into preprocessing SNARKs with trusted setup (Groth16, PLONK, Marlin — constant-size proofs, fast verification, toxic-waste trapdoor) and *transparent* arguments (STARKs [3], Bulletproofs, Spartan — no trusted setup).

The defining advance of Ben-Sasson et al. [3] was to construct the first *transparent* ZK system with verification *exponentially* faster than the computation size: verification time $O(\mathrm{polylog}(T))$ for $T$-step computations, achieved by encoding the computation trace as a Reed–Solomon codeword and applying the FRI low-degree test. The price is proof size: STARK proofs are $O(\mathrm{polylog}(T))$ but with large constants — hundreds of kilobytes even for simple computations — versus a few hundred bytes for pairing-based SNARKs [3, 6].

### 2.2 Arithmetization: R1CS, Plonkish, AIR, CCS

To prove a statement about a computation, the computation is first *arithmetized* into a constraint system over a finite field $\mathbb{F}$:

| Constraint system | Constraints | Used by |
|---|---|---|
| **R1CS** | $(Az) \circ (Bz) = Cz$ for $m \times m$ matrices | Groth16, Spartan, Nova [1] |
| **Plonkish** | Custom gates + copy constraints | PLONK, Halo 2 |
| **AIR** (algebraic intermediate representation) | Uniform transition constraints on a trace | STARKs [3] |
| **CCS** (customizable constraint systems) | Generalizes all three above | HyperNova [4] |

Nova folds *R1CS*, the most restricted of the classical systems; HyperNova [4] folds CCS, subsuming R1CS, Plonkish, and AIR with no overhead — decisive for proving stateful machine executions (EVM, RISC-V) with "à la carte" per-instruction cost.

### 2.3 Cycles of Elliptic Curves and the Recursion Problem

To recurse a SNARK *inside itself*, the verifier's arithmetic (over $\mathbb{F}_p$, the base field of the proof system's curve) must be expressed as constraints over the curve's scalar field $\mathbb{F}_q$. Simulating $\mathbb{F}_p$ operations in $\mathbb{F}_q$ costs hundreds of constraints each — the *field-mismatch problem*. The standard remedy, following Ben-Sasson et al. [6], is a *cycle* of curves: $E_1, E_2$ with $\#E_1(\mathbb{F}_{p_1}) = p_2$ and $\#E_2(\mathbb{F}_{p_2}) = p_1$, so the scalar field of one is the base field of the other and verification circuits alternate with no mismatch. Nova [1] and Halo [2] both exploit cycles; HyperNova's CycleFold [4] extends this to all modern folding-based arguments.

### 2.4 Incremental Verifiable Computation (IVC)

Valiant's IVC formalizes recursion. For a nondeterministic step function $F$: $\mathsf{Prove}(pk, x, \ell)$ establishes $y = F^{(\ell)}(x)$; $\mathsf{Verify}$ runs in time independent of $\ell$ (up to $\mathrm{polylog}$). *Proof-carrying data* (PCD) generalizes IVC to distributed computations on DAGs; Nova's folding extends naturally to PCD [1, 4].

## 3. Methodology

### 3.1 Folding Schemes: Definition

The central object of this thesis is the **folding scheme** of Kothapalli, Setty, and Tzialla [1].

> **Definition:** (Folding scheme.) A folding scheme for an NP relation $\mathcal{R}$ consists of PPT algorithms $(\mathsf{G}, \mathsf{K}, \mathsf{P}, \mathsf{V})$ where:
> - $(\mathsf{G}, \mathsf{K})$ produce public parameters and a prover/verifier key pair;
> - $\mathsf{P}(pk, (u_1, w_1), (u_2, w_2))$ interacts with $\mathsf{V}(vk)$ to output a folded instance $u$ and folded witness $w$;
> - **Completeness:** if $(u_i, w_i) \in \mathcal{R}$ for $i = 1, 2$, then $(u, w) \in \mathcal{R}$;
> - **Knowledge soundness:** for any PPT adversary producing an accepting transcript for a folded instance, there exists an extractor recovering witnesses for the original instances.

Folding is *weaker* than a SNARK: it reduces two instances to one, not $n$ instances to a short proof. Crucially, it avoids knowledge-of-exponent assumptions and pairings entirely [1].

### 3.2 Relaxed R1CS

Nova cannot fold plain R1CS directly, because folding combines witnesses *linearly* with a random challenge $\rho$, and plain R1CS is not closed under linear combination of witnesses. The fix is **relaxed R1CS**: instances carry an error vector $E$ and scalar $u$:

$$(A \cdot z) \circ (B \cdot z) = u \cdot (C \cdot z) + E, \quad z = (w, x, u).$$

Folding $(u_1, w_1)$, $(u_2, w_2)$ with challenge $\rho$ yields $u = u_1 + \rho u_2$, $w = w_1 + \rho w_2$, and a *cross-term* $T$ absorbing the nonlinearity: $E = E_1 + \rho \cdot T + \rho^2 \cdot E_2$. The prover commits to $T$ before seeing $\rho$, and the verifier's work is *constant*: two group scalar multiplications in-circuit [1].

### 3.3 The IVC Composition Theorem

Given a folding scheme, IVC is immediate: maintain a *running* folded instance $U_i$ accumulating all steps so far, and at each step fold in the fresh instance $u_i$ encoding "step $i$ of $F$ executed and the previous fold was verified." The augmented circuit $F'$ checked at each step contains only the folding verifier — **two group scalar multiplications** — giving the smallest recursion overhead in the literature [1].

> **Theorem:** (Nova IVC [1].) There exists an IVC scheme for any nondeterministic $F$ with per-step prover work dominated by two multiexponentiations of size $O(|F|)$ and recursion overhead of two scalar multiplications in-circuit. Knowledge soundness reduces to the discrete-logarithm assumption in the random-oracle model; no trusted setup and no FFTs are required.

### 3.4 Accumulation Schemes

Bünz, Fisch, and Szepieniec [5] decomposed recursion differently. An **accumulation scheme** splits verification into a cheap *verifier* producing a short *accumulator* and an expensive *decider* run once at the end. Halo's nested amortization is an accumulation scheme: expensive polynomial-evaluation checks are deferred across proofs and amortized by an untrusted helper [2, 5]. Their key result: accumulation (hence recursion) is achievable from *any* additive polynomial commitment scheme — DLOG-based, pairing-based, or groups of unknown order (DARK) — liberating recursion from the requirement of curve cycles [5].

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Nova: Folding Relaxed R1CS in Full Detail

Nova's protocol per IVC step $i$ [1]:

1. The prover holds the running relaxed-R1CS pair $(U_{i-1}, W_{i-1})$ and computes the fresh step instance $(u_i, w_i)$ for the augmented circuit $F'$.
2. The prover commits to the cross-term $T_i$, receives challenge $\rho_i$ (Fiat–Shamir), and both compute the folded instance $U_i$; the prover computes $W_i$.
3. The prover outputs $(U_i, W_i)$ — size $O(|F|)$ group elements.

Succinctness and zero-knowledge are recovered by a final compression: the prover proves knowledge of a satisfying folded witness using a variant of Spartan, yielding $O(\log |F|)$ group elements [1]. The recursion itself never invokes a SNARK — Nova's conceptual breakthrough.

```rust
// Nova IVC step (pseudocode): fold fresh step into running instance
fn ivc_step(running: (U, W), step: (u, w)) -> (U, W) {
    let T = cross_term(&running, &step);   // absorbs bilinearity
    let rho = hash(transcript);            // Fiat-Shamir challenge
    let U_new = fold_instance(running.U, step.u, rho, &T);
    let W_new = fold_witness(running.W, step.w, rho);
    (U_new, W_new)
}
```

The prover's per-step cost is dominated by two multiexponentiations of size $O(|F|)$ — the fastest IVC prover in the literature, since MSMs admit Pippenger speedups and no FFTs or pairings are involved [1].

### 4.2 Halo: Nested Amortization Without a Trusted Setup

Halo [2] predates Nova and attacks recursion differently. Its polynomial commitment scheme, built on the Bulletproofs inner-product argument, observes that the verifier's linear-time work in checking commitment openings has *smooth structure* amortizable across many proofs: an untrusted helper computes the claimed outputs of the linear-time operations, then proves *all* of them correct with a single argument requiring the verifier to do the linear work only once per batch. Consequences [2]:

- **Marginal verification is logarithmic** in circuit size, asymptotically improving over Bulletproofs.
- **Proof size and verification time do not grow with recursion depth.**
- No trusted setup: the cycle uses ordinary 255-bit prime-order curves near the 128-bit security level [2].

Where Nova's recursion overhead is two scalar multiplications, Halo's is the amortized verification circuit — larger per step, but with no algebraic restriction beyond the curve cycle.

### 4.3 STARK-Based Recursion and Sublinear Verification

STARKs [3] recurse *without* elliptic curves: the system is hash-based (Merkle commitments, FRI). Recursion means arithmetizing the STARK verifier — hash evaluations and Merkle path checks — as an AIR transition relation, then producing a STARK of the verifier's execution. Since STARK verification is $O(\mathrm{polylog}(T))$, recursion overhead is polylogarithmic and the scheme is transparent and plausibly post-quantum [3]. The trade-off is concrete cost: STARK proofs are hundreds of kilobytes, and each recursion layer multiplies proof size before compression — yet StarkNet uses STARK recursion at industrial scale because prover time is quasilinear and verification gas cost for the final proof is acceptable [3].

A comparative summary of the three traditions:

| Property | Halo [2] | Nova [1] | STARKs [3] |
|---|---|---|---|
| Trusted setup | None | None | None |
| Assumption | DLOG | DLOG (+ ROM) | Collision-resistant hash |
| Post-quantum | No | No | Conjectured yes |
| Recursion overhead | Amortized log verifier | 2 scalar mults | Polylog verifier |
| Prover per step | $O(n \log n)$-ish | 2 MSMs of size $O(|F|)$ | $O(T \log^2 T)$ |
| Proof size | $O(\log n)$ | $O(|F|)$ → $O(\log |F|)$ compressed | $O(\mathrm{polylog}\, T)$, large constants |
| Curve cycle needed | Yes | Yes | No (field-agnostic) |

### 4.4 Accumulation Schemes and Halo Infinite

The deepest theoretical contribution of the accumulation line [5] is the separation of concerns: recursion does not require verifying a SNARK inside a SNARK. It requires only (a) a cheap reduction of many claims to one accumulator, and (b) a decider that checks the accumulator once. Bünz et al. construct accumulators from bilinear groups (KZG-style), groups of unknown order (DARK, no trusted setup, no curve cycle), and any homomorphic polynomial commitment via the inner-product argument.

This explains *why* Nova works without pairings (its folding verifier is an accumulation verifier for Pedersen commitments) and points to cycle-free recursion from class groups — at the cost of slower group operations and the adaptive-root assumption [5].

### 4.5 Applications to zk-Rollups

For rollups, recursion converts *linear* proving cost into amortized batch processing with constant on-chain verification:

1. **Intra-batch recursion (Nova-style):** each transaction block is folded into a running instance; after $\ell$ blocks, Spartan compression produces the L1 proof. Prover latency per block is minimal (two MSMs), ideal for high-throughput sequencers [1].
2. **Proof aggregation (Halo-style):** independent user proofs are aggregated via nested amortization into one proof, enabling privacy-preserving rollups without a global trusted setup [2].
3. **STARK recursion (StarkNet-style):** Cairo executions are proven in STARKs and recursively composed; the final proof settles on Ethereum, with post-quantum security as a hedge [3].
4. **PCD and HyperNova:** multi-step stateful machine execution (EVM-equivalent zkVMs) uses HyperNova's CCS folding for à la carte per-opcode costs [4].

## 5. Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 Asymptotic Complexity

We summarize the per-step prover work $P(n)$, recursion overhead $R$ (constraints added per step beyond $F$ itself), proof size $|\pi|$, and verification time $V$ for $n = |F|$ constraints and $\ell$ steps:

| Scheme | $P(n)$ per step | $R$ | Final $|\pi|$ | $V$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Naive SNARK recursion | $O(n \log n)$ | pairing verifier ($\sim 10^5$ constraints) | $O(1)$ | $O(1)$ |
| Halo [2] | $O(n \log n)$ | amortized IPA verifier | $O(\log n)$ | $O(\log n)$ amortized |
| Nova [1] | 2 MSMs, $O(n)$ | 2 scalar mults ($\sim 10^4$ constraints) | $O(n) \to O(\log n)$ | $O(n) \to O(\log n)$ |
| HyperNova [4] | 1 MSM, $O(n)$ | folded CCS verifier | $O(\log n)$ | $O(\log n)$ |
| STARK recursion [3] | $O(n \log^2 n)$ | polylog verifier | $O(\mathrm{polylog}\, n)$ | $O(\mathrm{polylog}\, n)$ |

> **Theorem:** (Recursion overhead lower bound, informal.) Any IVC scheme whose step circuit includes the folding/accumulation verifier must add $\Omega(\lambda)$ constraints per step for $\lambda$ bits of security, since the challenge $\rho$ must be sampled from a space of size $2^{\lambda}$ and absorbed into the instance. Nova's two scalar multiplications are within a small constant of this bound [1].

### 5.2 Concrete Benchmarks

Reported figures from the literature (hardware-dependent):

- **Nova** [1]: for a step circuit of $2^{20}$ R1CS constraints, per-step proving is dominated by two MSMs — seconds on commodity hardware with Pippenger acceleration; the recursion overhead circuit is roughly $10^4$ constraints, under 1% of the step.
- **Halo** [2]: proofs of a few kilobytes; verification time constant in recursion depth, logarithmic in circuit size after amortization.
- **STARKs** [3]: proofs of 100–300 KB for moderate computations; verification in tens of milliseconds — exponentially faster than re-execution.

### 5.3 Soundness Reductions

- **Nova/HyperNova** [1, 4]: knowledge soundness of folding reduces to the discrete-logarithm assumption; the non-interactive IVC adds Fiat–Shamir in the random-oracle model. Zero-knowledge of the final compressed proof follows from Spartan.
- **Halo** [2]: security from DLOG plus the algebraic group model for the amortized inner-product argument; the helper is untrusted, so soundness does not depend on it.
- **STARKs** [3]: soundness from collision resistance of the hash and FRI low-degree-test soundness; no trapdoor assumptions.
- **Accumulation** [5]: from DLOG, pairing-based knowledge assumptions, or adaptive-root/strong-RSA for groups of unknown order.

### 5.4 Proof-Size-versus-Depth Scaling

The defining empirical law of recursive proofs: for all schemes above, final proof size and verification time are *independent of recursion depth* $\ell$ (up to the $\log \ell$ bits of the step counter). A Nova IVC proof after $10^6$ steps is the same size as after $10$ steps; only the prover's cumulative work grows, linearly in $\ell \cdot |F|$ [1].

## 6. Limitations

1. **Post-quantum gap.** Nova, Halo, and all DLOG-based folding schemes fall to Shor's algorithm. STARKs [3] are the only deployed recursive system with conjectured post-quantum security, but hash-based folding with Nova-like efficiency remains open: lattice- or hash-based commitments lack the homomorphic structure that makes Pedersen folding cheap.
2. **Relaxed-relation subtleties.** Folding operates on *relaxed* R1CS, where the error vector $E$ could accumulate adversarial slack across folds. Knowledge soundness holds [1], but any implementation bug in the relaxation bookkeeping breaks soundness silently.
3. **Cycle dependency.** Nova and Halo require cycles of elliptic curves [1, 2], restricting curve choice (e.g., Pasta) and complicating integration with BN254. HyperNova's CycleFold [4] mitigates but does not eliminate this; only accumulation from groups of unknown order [5] is truly cycle-free, at significant concrete cost.
4. **Prover memory.** Nova's prover holds multiexponentiations of size $O(|F|)$ per step; for very large steps this dominates hardware requirements, whereas STARK provers stream more gracefully [3].
5. **Fiat–Shamir in the QROM.** Nova's non-interactive folding relies on Fiat–Shamir; tight quantum-random-oracle security bounds for multi-round folding are less understood than for single-shot SNARKs [1].

## 7. Conclusion

This thesis has presented a unified account of the three principal traditions — Halo's nested amortization [2], Nova's folding [1] and its HyperNova generalization [4], and STARK-based transparent recursion [3] — together with the accumulation framework [5] that explains their common structure. The central lesson is a separation of powers: *reduction* (folding many instances into one) is a strictly weaker and cheaper primitive than *succinct verification*, and recursion needs only the former at each step, deferring the latter to a single final compression. Nova embodies this lesson most purely, achieving the smallest recursion overhead known — two scalar multiplications — with no trusted setup, no FFTs, and no pairings.

For rollups, the consequence is already visible: constant-size settlement proofs attesting to unbounded computation, with per-step prover costs dominated by a handful of multiexponentiations. The frontier is clear. A post-quantum folding scheme with Nova-like efficiency would unite the two great traditions; cycle-free accumulation from transparent assumptions would simplify deployment; and tighter characterizations of the recursion-overhead lower bound would tell us how close to optimal current constructions are.
