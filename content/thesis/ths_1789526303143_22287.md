# TFHE and Programmable Bootstrapping: Exact Homomorphic Encryption via the Torus, Blind Rotation, and Functional Bootstrapping

## Abstract

Fully homomorphic encryption comes in two flavors: leveled schemes such as BGV, BFV, and the approximate CKKS, whose bootstrapping is rare and amortized, and third-generation exact schemes—FHEW and TFHE—whose bootstrapping is fast enough to run after every gate. This thesis develops the mathematics of TFHE from first principles: LWE and ring-LWE ciphertexts over the real torus, the TLWE, TRLWE, and TGSW variants, the external product that replaced GSW's internal multiplication, and blind rotation, the homomorphic accumulator that recenters noise in roughly 13 milliseconds. We then treat programmable bootstrapping, in which the test polynomial encodes an arbitrary univariate function as a lookup table evaluated for free during noise refresh; functional bootstrapping and multi-value extraction, which squeeze several function evaluations from one bootstrap; and the modulus-switching and key-switching machinery, including circuit bootstrapping, that makes the leveled and gate modes composable. A detailed comparison with BGV, BFV, and CKKS bootstrapping explains when per-gate exactness beats amortized throughput, grounded in measured timings from the literature.

##1 Intro

The dream of fully homomorphic encryption (FHE) is computation on ciphertexts that mirrors computation on plaintexts: the server evaluates an arbitrary circuit while learning nothing about the inputs. Gentry's 2009 breakthrough proved this possible, but his bootstrapping procedure—homomorphically re-decrypting a ciphertext to scrub its noise—was the overwhelming bottleneck, measured in tens of minutes per operation [1].

The field responded along two axes. The **second generation** (BGV, BFV) organized noise growth through modulus switching and leveled evaluation, deferring bootstrapping as long as possible; the **approximate** variant CKKS traded exactness for arithmetic throughput by encoding vectors of complex numbers and absorbing noise into the least significant bits [1][2]. But a nagging asymmetry remained: every gate of a boolean circuit accumulated noise, while the noise-refresh operation itself was the slowest primitive in the system.

The **third generation** inverted this logic. Ducas and Micciancio's FHEW (2015) showed that a single binary gate could be evaluated *and* bootstrapped in under a second—bootstrapping became so cheap that it could follow every gate, eliminating the need for deep leveled circuits entirely [2]. Chillotti, Gama, Georgieva, and Izabachène then rebuilt this construction over the torus, replacing GSW's internal product with a simpler external product and cutting gate bootstrapping from 690 milliseconds to 13 milliseconds on a single core, with a 16 MB bootstrapping key instead of 1 GB [1][3]. This is TFHE.

TFHE's deeper contribution, however, is *programmability*. Because bootstrapping in this design rotates a test polynomial through a blind accumulator, that polynomial can encode an arbitrary univariate function as a lookup table: the bootstrap simultaneously refreshes the ciphertext and evaluates the function, at no extra cost [4][5]. This **programmable bootstrapping** (PBS) turns the most expensive operation in FHE into the most expressive one. It underlies encrypted neural-network inference [4], encrypted arithmetic circuits [5][6], and hybrid constructions such as CHIMERA that bridge exact gate-based and SIMD-style schemes [7].

This thesis develops the theory in layers: §2 fixes notation and the torus framework; §3 derives gate bootstrapping through blind rotation; §4 dives into the torus and ring variants, programmable bootstrapping, functional bootstrapping with multi-value extraction, modulus and key switching, and a systematic comparison with BGV, BFV, and CKKS bootstrapping; §5 states the core correctness and noise theorems and surveys measured performance; §6 faces the limitations; §7 concludes.

##2 Background

### 2.1 LWE, RLWE, and the torus

The **Learning With Errors** problem asks to distinguish pairs consisting of a random vector and an inner product with a secret plus small noise from uniform randomness. The **ring** variant (RLWE) replaces vectors with polynomials in the anticyclic ring of dimension a power of two, giving the algebraic structure that makes fast polynomial arithmetic possible [1][2].

TFHE works not over a finite field but over the **real torus** \(\mathbb{T} = \mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}\), the additive group of reals modulo one. Ciphertexts are elements of \(\mathbb{T}\), or of the torus polynomial ring \(\mathbb{T}_N[X] = \mathbb{T}[X]/(X^N+1)\). Messages live in the *most significant bits*: a message \(m\) from a small discrete set is encoded as a multiple of a scaling factor \(\Delta\), and the small LWE noise sits in the least significant bits, so a noisy plaintext looks like \(\Delta \cdot m + e\) on the circle [1][5]. This torus-native formulation has two engineering consequences that explain TFHE's speed: ciphertext moduli can be chosen to fit native 32- and 64-bit CPU types in both the standard and Fourier domains, and rescaling operations become natural rounding rather than expensive modular reductions [1][5].

### 2.2 The three ciphertext flavors: TLWE, TRLWE, TGSW

TFHE distinguishes three ciphertext types over the torus, and the bootstrapping pipeline shuttles between them deliberately [1]:

- **TLWE** encrypts a scalar message \(\mu \in \mathbb{T}\) under a secret vector \(\mathbf{s}\): the ciphertext is \((\mathbf{a}, b)\) with \(b = \langle \mathbf{a}, \mathbf{s} \rangle + \mu + e\). The *phase* \(\varphi = b - \langle \mathbf{a}, \mathbf{s} \rangle = \mu + e\) carries the message plus noise.
- **TRLWE** encrypts a polynomial message in \(\mathbb{T}_N[X]\) under a polynomial secret; it packs \(N\) scalar messages into one ciphertext and is the natural home of the blind-rotation accumulator.
- **TGSW** is the ring variant of GSW: a matrix of TRLWE ciphertexts encrypting multiples of the message by powers of a gadget base. TGSW ciphertexts support an *external product* with TLWE or TRLWE ciphertexts, denoted \(\boxdot\), which multiplies the underlying messages while keeping the noise growth asymmetric and small [1][3].

The decisive simplification of TFHE relative to its predecessors was replacing GSW's *internal* product (TGSW times TGSW, with symmetric noise blowup) by this *external* product (TGSW times TLWE/TRLWE).

### 2.3 Modulus switching and key switching in brief

Two auxiliary operations recur throughout. **Modulus switching** rescales a ciphertext from one modulus to a smaller one, rounding each component; it shrinks both the message scale and the noise, and is the classic BGV tool for managing noise across levels.

##3 Methodology

### 3.1 The gate-bootstrapping pipeline

Gate bootstrapping takes a noisy TLWE ciphertext \(c = (\mathbf{a}, b)\) encrypting a bit (or small message) \(\mu\) with noise that has grown too large, and returns a fresh TLWE ciphertext encrypting the same message with fixed, small, input-independent noise. The pipeline has four stages [1][3]:

1. **Modulus switch.** Round \(c\) from the large torus modulus to the small accumulator modulus, obtaining integer exponents \(\bar{a}_i, \bar{b}\) in the range of twice the ring dimension. The rounding adds a small, controlled error.
2. **Blind rotation.** Initialize the accumulator as a TRLWE encryption of the test polynomial \(v\) shifted by \(-\bar{b}\): \(ACC = X^{-\bar{b}} \cdot v\). Then, for each secret-key bit \(s_i\), multiply by \(X^{\bar{a}_i s_i}\) using the external product with a bootstrapping key \(BK_i = \text{TGSW}(s_i)\). Because \(s_i\) is encrypted, the rotation amount is *blind*: the server rotates by \(\bar{a}_i s_i\) without learning \(s_i\). The final accumulator encrypts \(X^{\varphi} \cdot v\), where \(\varphi = \bar{b} - \sum \bar{a}_i s_i\) is (a rounded version of) the phase, i.e., the noisy message [1][3].
3. **Sample extraction.** Pull one TLWE sample out of the TRLWE accumulator—the constant coefficient of the rotated test polynomial—which encrypts the refreshed message under the accumulator key [1].
4. **Key switching.** Switch the extracted TLWE ciphertext back under the original LWE key (and re-encode the message), yielding a fresh ciphertext with small noise [1][6].

> Theorem: (Blind-rotation correctness, Chillotti et al.) After blind rotation, the accumulator is a TRLWE encryption of the test polynomial rotated by the phase: \(ACC \in \text{TRLWE}_K(X^{\bar{b} - \langle \bar{\mathbf{a}}, \mathbf{s} \rangle} \cdot v)\). Sample extraction therefore yields a TLWE ciphertext whose message is the constant term of the rotated test vector, with noise independent of the input ciphertext's noise [1].

The noise after bootstrapping depends only on the bootstrapping-key noise, the external-product noise (which grows only *additively* in the number of rotations thanks to approximate gadget decomposition), and the rounding error—never on how noisy the input was.

### 3.2 Blind rotation in pseudocode

The following Rust-style sketch captures the core loop; \(N\) is the ring dimension and the bootstrapping key holds one TGSW ciphertext per secret bit:

```rust
// acc: TRLWE accumulator, initially X^{-b_bar} * test_poly
// bk[i]: TGSW encryption of secret bit s_i
// a_bar[i]: modulus-switched mask coefficients
fn blind_rotate(acc: &mut Trlwe, bk: &[Tgsw], a_bar: &[u32]) {
    for (bk_i, &a) in bk.iter().zip(a_bar.iter()) {
        // cmux: acc <- acc * X^{a * s_i}, blind to the value of s_i
        // implemented as acc + (X^a - 1) \u{22c5} (bk_i \u{2299} acc)
        let rotated = acc.mul_x_pow(a);          // acc * X^a
        let diff = rotated.sub(acc);             // acc * (X^a - 1)
        let sel = bk_i.external_product(&diff);  // s_i * acc * (X^a - 1)
        *acc = acc.add(&sel);                    // acc * X^{a * s_i}
    }
}
```

Each iteration is a **CMux**: if the encrypted selector \(s_i\) is 1, replace \(ACC\) by \(ACC \cdot X^{a}\); if 0, leave it.

##4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Torus and ring variants: TLWE, TRLWE, TGSW in the pipeline

The three flavors are not interchangeable conveniences; each occupies a precise station in the pipeline, and the pipeline's efficiency comes from matching representation to operation [1]:

| Ciphertext type | Encrypts | Role in bootstrapping |
|---|---|---|
| TLWE | scalar \(\mu \in \mathbb{T}\) | input and output of gate bootstrapping; gate inputs live here |
| TRLWE | polynomial in \(\mathbb{T}_N[X]\) | the blind-rotation accumulator; packs \(N\) slots, supports rotation by \(X^k\) |
| TGSW | gadget multiples of a message | bootstrapping keys \(BK_i = \text{TGSW}(s_i)\); selectors in the CMux |

The TRLWE accumulator is the workhorse: multiplying by \(X^k\) in the anticyclic ring cyclically rotates coefficients (with sign flips past the boundary), which is exactly the operation blind rotation needs.

A subtlety worth stressing: the anticyclic ring's negacyclicity means rotation by half the ring dimension negates coefficients.

### 4.2 Programmable bootstrapping: arbitrary univariate functions for free

In plain gate bootstrapping, the test polynomial \(v\) encodes a rounding function: after rotation by the phase, the constant coefficient is the nearest valid message encoding, which is how noise is removed.

Concretely, to evaluate a univariate function \(f\) on an encrypted small integer \(x\), encode \(f\) into the test polynomial so that rotation by (an encoding of) \(x\) brings \(f(x)\) to the constant position.

> Lemma: (PBS LUT encoding) Let the message space have \(p\) bits and let \(f\) map the message space to itself. There exists a test polynomial \(v_f\) in \(\mathbb{T}_N[X]\), constructible in time linear in the ring dimension, such that blind rotation of \(v_f\) by the phase of a TLWE encryption of \(x\) followed by sample extraction yields a TLWE encryption of \(f(x)\), provided \(f\) respects the negacyclic symmetry of the ring [1][5].

The canonical application is **FHE-DiNN** (Bourse et al., 2017): the sign activation of a discretized neural network is folded into the bootstrap itself, so each neuron's nonlinearity costs nothing beyond the refresh the ciphertext needed anyway.

Precision is the binding constraint.

### 4.3 Functional bootstrapping and multi-value extraction

**Functional bootstrapping** is the general principle of which PBS is the flagship instance: the refresh operation evaluates a nontrivial function rather than merely the identity (or rounding) [4]. The literature distinguishes several refinements that increase how much work one bootstrap performs:

- **Multi-value extraction.** A single blind rotation produces a full TRLWE accumulator with \(N\) coefficients, but plain sample extraction keeps only the constant term. Multi-value techniques extract *several* TLWE samples—different coefficients or linear combinations—from one accumulator, effectively evaluating multiple LUTs (or multiple outputs of one LUT) per bootstrap. The amortized cost per extracted value drops toward a fraction of one blind rotation [5][6].
- **Multi-input LUTs.** By packing several small inputs into the phase (via linear combinations before bootstrapping), one PBS can evaluate functions of two or more variables encoded as a flattened table, at the cost of splitting the available precision across inputs [5][6].
- **Chained PBS circuits.** Because circuit bootstrapping (below) converts refreshed LWE ciphertexts into low-noise RingGSW ciphertexts, sequences of PBS evaluations compose into arithmetic circuits—adders, multipliers, comparators—with per-gate cost dominated by the bootstrap rather than by the logic [1][5].

Together these turn TFHE from a boolean-gate engine into a small-integer arithmetic engine: with \(k\)-bit message spaces, addition and multiplication modulo powers of two, exponentiation, field inversion, and ReLU all become practical, with ciphertext expansion shrinking toward small constants [6].

### 4.4 Modulus switching, key switching, and circuit bootstrapping

Modulus switching and key switching are the scheme's change-of-representation primitives, and TFHE uses both more aggressively than its leveled cousins [1][6]:

1. **Modulus switching** enters bootstrapping (large torus modulus down to accumulator precision), appears inside key switching itself, and underlies the *leveled* mode of TFHE, where packed TRLWE ciphertexts evaluate arithmetic circuits with additive noise overhead before any bootstrap is needed [1].
2. **Key switching** exits bootstrapping (accumulator key back to the LWE key), converts between binary and Gaussian keys, and re-encodes messages between the MSB positions different stages expect [1][6].

The capstone is **circuit bootstrapping**: a procedure that converts an LWE ciphertext (the output of gate bootstrapping) into a *low-noise* RingGSW ciphertext in about 137 milliseconds [1].

### 4.5 Comparison with BGV, BFV, and CKKS bootstrapping

TFHE's bootstrapping philosophy—fast, frequent, per-ciphertext—is the mirror image of the leveled schemes' [1][2][7]:

| Aspect | TFHE (gate / PBS) | BGV / BFV | CKKS |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bootstrapping latency | ~13 ms per gate/function | minutes (digit extraction over modulus chain) | seconds, amortized over thousands of slots |
| Bootstrapping frequency | every gate (or every PBS) | rarely; leveled circuit first | rarely; rescaling chain first |
| Message type | exact small integers / bits | exact integers / packed slots | approximate complex vectors |
| Function evaluation | arbitrary univariate LUT *during* bootstrap | polynomial circuits only | polynomial approximations only |
| Throughput model | latency-optimized, sequential | batch/SIMD via slot packing | batch/SIMD via slot packing |
| Noise after refresh | fixed, input-independent | depends on digit-extraction precision | approximate; error folded into message |

The trade is sharp.

**CHIMERA** (Boura et al., 2018) refuses the choice: it defines conversion procedures between TFHE-style torus ciphertexts and BGV/BFV-style ring ciphertexts, so a computation can use gate bootstrapping where logic is irregular and switch to packed SIMD arithmetic where it is regular [7]. The hybrid view is arguably the mature one: exactness and programmability where control flow lives, throughput where linear algebra lives.

##5 Empirical Results and Proofs

**Result 1 — the 13 ms gate.** Chillotti et al. reduced FHEW's 690 ms bootstrap to 13 ms single-core (a factor of about 50), cut the bootstrapping key from 1 GB to 16 MB at the same security level, and replaced exact gadget decomposition with approximate decomposition for a further noise-and-speed win. The reference library evaluates arbitrary binary gates at roughly 50 gates per second per core [1][3][8].

**Result 2 — noise independence.** The central theorem (stated in §3.1) is that post-bootstrap noise depends only on key noise, external-product noise, and rounding—*not* on input noise. The proof tracks each CMux step: the external product's asymmetric noise growth keeps the accumulator noise linear in the number of secret bits, and modulus-switch rounding contributes a bounded, independent term. Empirically, ciphertexts survive unlimited sequential bootstraps with no parameter growth, which is precisely what per-gate evaluation requires [1][3].

**Result 3 — functional bootstrapping in practice.** FHE-DiNN folded the sign activation into the bootstrap and classified encrypted MNIST images above 96 percent accuracy in under 1.7 seconds, a landmark demonstration that non-polynomial functions are essentially free in the TFHE cost model [4].

**Result 4 — precision and arithmetic circuits.** Improved programmable bootstrapping extends PBS to larger precision and builds efficient arithmetic circuits (adders, multipliers) from chained PBS evaluations; the k-bit arithmetic line of work shows addition and multiplication modulo powers of two with ciphertext expansion factors approaching small constants, plus field inversion and ReLU [5][6].

**Result 5 — integer-domain confirmation.** Pereira (2020) adapted the Ducas–Micciancio single-gate bootstrapping method to FHE over the integers (AGCD-based), reaching sub-second bootstrapping with multivalue support—evidence that the blind-rotation/external-product design pattern generalizes beyond lattices [2][9].

| Construction | Reported bootstrapping time | Key material | Security |
|---|---|---|---|
| FHEW (Ducas–Micciancio, 2015) | < 1 s (about 690 ms) | ~1 GB | lattice-based |
| TFHE gate bootstrap (2016) | < 0.1 s (13 ms single core) | ~16 MB | 110-bit+ |
| TFHE circuit bootstrap (2018) | 137 ms (LWE to low-noise RingGSW) | amortized over uses | 110-bit+ |
| FHE-DiNN sign-via-bootstrap (2017) | subsumed in refresh | same as TFHE | 110-bit+ |

##6 Limitations

1. **Latency, not throughput.** Thirteen milliseconds per gate is a triumph of latency, but a 64-bit multiplier needs hundreds of gates; sequential exact arithmetic in TFHE is slow compared with one CKKS ciphertext doing thousands of slot operations per bootstrap. Throughput-oriented workloads still belong to the SIMD schemes [1][2][7].
2. **Precision ceiling of PBS.** The lookup table must fit the resolvable message bits—classically a handful, improved recently but still far from double precision. Functions needing wide domains must be decomposed into digit-wise PBS evaluations, multiplying cost [5][6].
3. **Negacyclicity tax.** The anticyclic ring forces the test polynomial to be anti-symmetric, halving the effective LUT size and complicating encodings; multi-input tables pay this tax on every input dimension [1][5].
4. **Key sizes and evaluation keys.** Although the bootstrapping key shrank from 1 GB to 16 MB, key-switching keys, circuit-bootstrapping keys, and multi-key variants still add up; deployment on constrained devices remains awkward [1][6].
5. **Failure probability analysis.** Correctness is probabilistic: decryption fails if accumulated noise crosses the decoding boundary. Parameters must push the failure rate below cryptographically negligible levels (analyses target bounds like two to the minus 128 or at least two to the minus 64), and the analyses rely on heuristic independence assumptions about noise terms that are difficult to verify empirically [1][3].
6. **Side channels and misuse.** Constant-time implementations of the FFT/NTT-heavy pipeline are nontrivial; timing or cache leakage of secret-dependent rotation patterns would be catastrophic, and the scheme's exactness means a single leaked bit is a real plaintext bit [1][8].
7. **Parameter brittleness.** The torus formulation's speed comes from tightly tuned parameter sets (ring dimension, gadget base, decomposition levels); deviating—e.g., for larger precision—requires re-running the full noise and security analysis rather than turning a dial [1][5].

##7 Conclusion

TFHE recast fully homomorphic encryption as a *per-gate* discipline: by making bootstrapping cost milliseconds instead of minutes, it made the noise-refresh operation the natural heartbeat of every computation rather than a dreaded exception. The technical ingredients—the torus as ciphertext space, the TLWE/TRLWE/TGSW trinity, the external product, blind rotation, and the modulus/key-switching glue—compose into a pipeline whose output noise is independent of its input noise, the property on which everything else rests [1][3].

Programmable bootstrapping is the idea that elevates the construction from fast to expressive: since every refresh already rotates a test polynomial, that polynomial may as well encode an arbitrary univariate function, evaluated for free [4][5]. Functional bootstrapping and multi-value extraction push further, amortizing one blind rotation over several function values, while circuit bootstrapping reconnects the gate world to the leveled world [1][5][6]. Against BGV, BFV, and CKKS, TFHE does not compete on SIMD throughput and does not try to; it owns exact logic, comparisons, and table lookups, and hybrids like CHIMERA let a single computation use each paradigm where it wins [7].

The open frontier is precision: lifting PBS from small integers toward the wider arithmetic that would let exact FHE absorb workloads currently reserved for CKKS, without surrendering the millisecond heartbeat. The recent improved-PBS and k-bit arithmetic results suggest the ceiling is engineering rather than mathematics [5][6]—and that would be the fitting next chapter for a scheme whose founding move was refusing to accept that bootstrapping must be slow.
