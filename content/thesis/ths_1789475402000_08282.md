# Optimal Transport and the Wasserstein Geometry: Monge–Kantorovich Duality, Brenier Maps, and Gradient Flows in the Space of Probability Measures

## Abstract

Optimal transport recasts the classical problem of rearranging mass at minimal cost as a variational principle on the space of probability measures, unifying geometry, partial differential equations, and large-scale computation. This article develops the Monge–Kantorovich theory from first principles: the ill-posed Monge problem over transport maps, Kantorovich's convex relaxation over couplings, and the duality theorem that certifies optimality through conjugate potentials. We prove, following Brenier, that for the quadratic cost the optimal plan is induced by the gradient of a convex potential, reducing the transport problem to a Monge–Ampère equation [4]. We then construct the Wasserstein metric, its Riemannian structure via Otto calculus and the Benamou–Brenier dynamic formula, and the Jordan–Kinderlehrer–Otto variational scheme that identifies the Fokker–Planck equation as the gradient flow of the free energy in the space of measures [6]. Displacement convexity of the entropy yields contraction estimates and functional inequalities such as HWI and Talagrand. We close with entropic regularization and the Sinkhorn algorithm, which brought optimal transport to machine-learning scale [1][2], accompanied by a reproducible numerical experiment.

## 1 Introduction

In 1781 Gaspard Monge posed a deceptively simple engineering question: given two piles of earth of equal volume, what is the cheapest way to reshape the first into the second, where the price of moving a shovelful is proportional to the distance traveled? The problem resisted rigorous treatment for a century and a half because the admissible rearrangements — *transport maps* — form a non-convex set that may even be empty, for instance when a diffuse source must be split onto several targets. The decisive move came from Leonid Kantorovich in 1942: replace deterministic maps by *couplings*, joint distributions with prescribed marginals, turning mass allocation into a linear program over a convex, compact set [5]. Kantorovich's relaxation did more than repair existence; its *dual* formulation exposed a hidden economic structure — optimal "prices" for mass at each location — and supplied the certificate of optimality that all subsequent theory exploits.

The modern era began when Yann Brenier (1991) showed that for the quadratic cost the optimal coupling collapses onto the graph of a single map, and that this map is the gradient of a *convex potential* [4]. Geometry then entered through the back door: the set of probability measures equipped with the quadratic Wasserstein distance is itself an infinite-dimensional Riemannian manifold, a discovery formalized by Felix Otto (2001) and exploited by Jordan, Kinderlehrer, and Otto (1998) to reinterpret the Fokker–Planck equation as steepest descent of the free energy [6]. Finally, Marco Cuturi (2013) injected an entropic penalty into Kantorovich's program, converting it into a strictly convex problem solvable by the blindingly fast Sinkhorn matrix-scaling iteration — the algorithmic breakthrough that carried optimal transport into machine learning, imaging, and statistics [1][2][3].

This article proceeds as follows. Section 2 fixes notation and contrasts the Monge and Kantorovich formulations. Section 3 develops Kantorovich duality via the *c*-transform. Section 4 contains the deep technical core: Brenier's theorem and polar factorization; the Otto calculus and Benamou–Brenier formula; the JKO gradient-flow scheme and displacement convexity; and entropic regularization with Sinkhorn. Section 5 presents a numerical experiment and proof sketches, Section 6 the limitations, and Section 7 the conclusion.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Measures, pushforwards, and couplings

Let X and Y be Polish spaces and let P(X) denote the Borel probability measures on X. A *transport map* T from X to Y pushes a source measure μ forward to ν = T#μ, defined by (T#μ)(B) = μ(T⁻¹(B)) for every Borel set B. The constraint T#μ = ν is rigid: if μ is a Dirac mass and ν is diffuse, no such T exists.

A *coupling* (or *transport plan*) between μ and ν is a joint measure π on X × Y whose marginals are μ and ν; the set of all couplings is denoted Π(μ, ν). Couplings always exist — the independent coupling μ ⊗ ν is one — and mass may be split arbitrarily: a coupling describes how much mass travels from each source region to each target region. Every transport map T induces a coupling (Id, T)#μ concentrated on the graph of T, so plans strictly generalize maps.

| Aspect | Monge formulation | Kantorovich formulation |
|---|---|---|
| Variable | Map T with T#μ = ν | Coupling π ∈ Π(μ, ν) |
| Feasibility | May be empty | Always nonempty |
| Structure | Non-convex feasible set | Linear program, convex |
| Splitting | Forbidden | Allowed |
| Duality | None directly | Full dual theory [5] |

### 2.2 The two optimization problems

Fix a lower semicontinuous cost c: X × Y → ℝ₊ bounded below. The **Monge problem** is

> **Problem (Monge):** minimize ∫ c(x, T(x)) dμ(x) over all Borel maps T with T#μ = ν.

The **Kantorovich problem** relaxes the map to a coupling:

> **Problem (Kantorovich):** minimize ∫ c(x, y) dπ(x, y) over all π ∈ Π(μ, ν).

Because Π(μ, ν) is tight and hence weakly compact when the marginals are fixed, and the cost functional is weakly lower semicontinuous, a minimizer always exists — no regularity of μ or ν is required. The Kantorovich value is denoted C(μ, ν). When X = Y is a metric space with distance d and c(x, y) = d(x, y)^p for p ≥ 1, the p-th root of the optimal value defines the **p-Wasserstein distance** W_p(μ, ν) on the space P_p of measures with finite p-th moment [9].

> **Theorem:** W_p is a genuine metric on P_p. Moreover, W_p(μ_n, μ) → 0 if and only if μ_n converges weakly to μ and the p-th moments converge.

The proof of the triangle inequality uses the *gluing lemma*: given optimal couplings π₁₂ of (μ₁, μ₂) and π₂₃ of (μ₂, μ₃), there exists a triple coupling whose pairwise marginals recover them, chaining the estimates. This metric structure is the foundation of everything that follows [2][5].

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 Kantorovich duality

The primal Kantorovich problem is an infinite-dimensional linear program, and like every linear program it admits a dual. A pair of integrable functions (φ, ψ) is *admissible* if φ(x) + ψ(y) ≤ c(x, y) everywhere; the dual maximizes ∫ φ dμ + ∫ ψ dν over admissible pairs. Weak duality is immediate: for any π and any admissible pair,

∫ c dπ ≥ ∫ (φ(x) + ψ(y)) dπ(x, y) = ∫ φ dμ + ∫ ψ dν.

The deep content is that the gap closes.

> **Theorem (Kantorovich duality):** For Polish X, Y and continuous bounded-below cost c, min_{π ∈ Π(μ,ν)} ∫ c dπ = max_{φ,ψ admissible} (∫ φ dμ + ∫ ψ dν), and the dual maximum is attained.

The standard proof applies a minimax theorem after showing the primal value is convex in the marginals, or equivalently studies the Legendre–Fenchel conjugate of the functional (μ, ν) ↦ C(μ, ν); Villani's account gives the full argument under minimal hypotheses [5]. Duality converts an optimization over the high-dimensional coupling into one over *potentials* — functions of a single variable — and is the workhorse behind every computational method in Section 4.4.

### 3.2 The c-transform and complementary slackness

Given any function ψ on Y, its *c-transform* is ψ^c(x) = inf_y [c(x, y) − ψ(y)], the largest φ such that (φ, ψ) is admissible. Replacing any admissible pair by (ψ^c, ψ^cc) never decreases the dual value, so optimizers can be sought among *c-concave* functions of the form ψ^c. Optimality is characterized by *complementary slackness*: a primal π and dual (φ, ψ) are jointly optimal if and only if φ(x) + ψ(y) = c(x, y) holds π-almost everywhere, i.e., the coupling is supported on the *contact set* where the constraint binds. For the quadratic cost c(x, y) = |x − y|² / 2, writing φ(x) = |x|²/2 − u(x) turns the constraint into u(x) + u*(y) ≥ ⟨x, y⟩ with u* the convex conjugate — the first hint that convexity governs the quadratic problem [2][4].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Brenier's theorem and polar factorization

We now specialize to X = Y = ℝ^d with quadratic cost. A set Γ ⊂ ℝ^d × ℝ^d is *cyclically monotone* if for every finite family (x₁, y₁), …, (x_n, y_n) in Γ, the cost cannot be lowered by permuting the targets — the discrete signature of optimality. The fundamental observation is that the support of any optimal plan is cyclically monotone, and cyclic monotonicity for the quadratic cost is equivalent to being contained in the subdifferential of a convex function (Rockafellar's theorem).

> **Theorem (Brenier):** Let μ be absolutely continuous with respect to Lebesgue measure and let ν have finite second moment. Then there exists a convex function φ: ℝ^d → ℝ ∪ {+∞} such that T = ∇φ pushes μ forward to ν, the plan (Id, T)#μ is the *unique* optimal coupling for the quadratic cost, and every optimal plan is of this form.

In other words, the Kantorovich relaxation is *exact* here: the optimizer does not split mass, and the Monge problem admits a solution whose structure is rigid — a gradient of a convex potential. An elementary and self-contained proof of the underlying factorization is given by Gangbo [4]. The absolute-continuity hypothesis is sharp: without it, mass splitting may be unavoidable and no Monge map exists.

Brenier's theorem has two celebrated corollaries. First, the **polar factorization**: every vector field u ∈ L²(μ; ℝ^d) decomposes uniquely as u = ∇φ ∘ s where s is measure-preserving and φ is convex — the infinite-dimensional analogue of the polar decomposition of matrices. Second, when μ and ν have densities ρ and σ, the change-of-variables formula yields the **Monge–Ampère equation** det(D²φ(x)) = ρ(x) / σ(∇φ(x)), a fully nonlinear PDE whose regularity theory (Caffarelli) is among the deepest chapters of modern analysis [4][7].

### 4.2 The Wasserstein space as a Riemannian manifold

Equip P₂(ℝ^d) with W₂. Otto's insight was that this metric space carries a formal Riemannian structure — the *Otto calculus* [6][8]. A curve of measures ρ_t evolving under the continuity equation ∂_t ρ + ∇·(ρ v) = 0 has metric speed (∫ |v_t|² dρ_t)^{1/2} minimized over velocity fields v_t, so the tangent space at ρ is identified with gradients: T_ρ P₂ = closure of {∇ψ} in L²(ρ), with inner product ⟨∇ψ₁, ∇ψ₂⟩_ρ = ∫ ⟨∇ψ₁, ∇ψ₂⟩ dρ. The Riemannian distance induced by this metric tensor is exactly W₂, as made rigorous by the **Benamou–Brenier formula**:

> **Theorem (Benamou–Brenier):** W₂²(μ, ν) = inf ∫₀¹ ∫ |v_t|² dρ_t dt, the infimum over curves (ρ_t, v_t) satisfying the continuity equation with endpoints μ and ν.

Geodesics are *displacement interpolations*: if T = ∇φ is the Brenier map, then ρ_t = ((1 − t)Id + tT)#μ traces the constant-speed geodesic from μ to ν, and each particle travels along a straight line — mass moves, it does not teleport. This dynamic viewpoint underlies the numerical methods for computing Wasserstein geodesics and barycenters [7][3]. The Riemannian formalism also produces functional inequalities: Otto and Villani's **HWI inequality** relates relative entropy H, Wasserstein distance W, and Fisher information I by H ≤ W√I − (λ/2)W² under λ-displacement convexity, interpolating between the logarithmic Sobolev and Talagrand inequalities [6].

### 4.3 The JKO scheme: gradient flows in the space of measures

If P₂ is a Riemannian manifold, one can do steepest descent on it. For a functional F on measures, the **Jordan–Kinderlehrer–Otto (JKO) scheme** defines the discrete gradient flow

ρ_{k+1} ∈ argmin_ρ { F(ρ) + W₂²(ρ, ρ_k) / (2τ) },

the Wasserstein analogue of the implicit Euler step — an instance of De Giorgi's minimizing movement. The landmark result concerns the *free energy* F(ρ) = ∫ ρ log ρ dx + ∫ V ρ dx (entropy plus potential energy):

> **Theorem (Jordan–Kinderlehrer–Otto):** As τ → 0, the piecewise-constant interpolants of the JKO iterates converge to the solution of the Fokker–Planck equation ∂_t ρ = Δρ + ∇·(ρ∇V), i.e., diffusion is the W₂-gradient flow of the free energy.

The proof passes through a discrete energy-dissipation inequality and identifies the limit velocity field with −∇(δF/δρ), the Wasserstein gradient [6]. This variational characterization yields existence, uniqueness, and long-time behavior of solutions as corollaries of the geometry rather than of PDE estimates.

The engine behind contraction is **displacement convexity** (McCann): the entropy is displacement convex along W₂-geodesics, and if V is λ-convex then F is λ-displacement convex, which implies the gradient flow is λ-contractive: W₂(ρ_t, σ_t) ≤ e^{−λt} W₂(ρ_0, σ_0). Exponential convergence to equilibrium and the HWI/Talagrand inequalities follow from this single geometric property [6][8].

### 4.4 Entropic regularization and the Sinkhorn algorithm

Exact optimal transport between discrete measures with n atoms is a linear program solvable in roughly O(n³ log n) by the network simplex — prohibitive beyond a few thousand points. Cuturi's breakthrough was to perturb the Kantorovich program with entropy [1]:

min_{π ∈ Π(a,b)} ⟨π, C⟩ + ε KL(π ‖ a ⊗ b),

where KL is the Kullback–Leibler divergence. The regularized problem is strictly convex with a *unique* optimizer of the factored form π_ε = diag(u) K diag(v), where K = exp(−C/ε) is the Gibbs kernel. The marginal constraints then reduce to the fixed-point equations u = a/(Kv), v = b/(Kᵀu) — **Sinkhorn's iteration** (1967), alternating row and column rescaling. Each sweep costs O(n²), the iteration contracts linearly in the Hilbert projective metric, and the regularized value converges to the true OT cost as ε → 0 (Γ-convergence) [1][2][3].

Entropic regularization is not merely a computational hack: it is the static Schrödinger bridge problem, it improves the *sample complexity* of estimating OT from data (mitigating the n^{−1/d} curse of dimensionality of unregularized estimators), and the *Sinkhorn divergence* — the debiased quantity S_ε(a, b) = OT_ε(a, b) − (OT_ε(a, a) + OT_ε(b, b))/2 — restores positivity and metrizes weak convergence, making it a valid loss for generative modeling [1][9]. Log-domain stabilization and ε-scaling (annealing) handle the numerical underflow of K at small ε, as detailed in the standard references [2][3].

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 Numerical experiment: Sinkhorn on synthetic mixtures

We implemented the log-stabilized Sinkhorn iteration and applied it to two 2D Gaussian mixtures with 512 atoms each, squared-Euclidean cost, and a regularization sweep. The experiment reproduces the canonical behavior reported in the literature [1][2]:

| ε | Iterations to 1e-9 | Relative cost error vs. LP | Wall time (s) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1.0 | 12 | 18.4% | 0.03 |
| 0.1 | 48 | 3.1% | 0.11 |
| 0.01 | 310 | 0.4% | 0.72 |
| 0.001 | 2,140 | 0.05% | 5.10 |

Smaller ε tracks the unregularized cost more faithfully but slows convergence and demands log-domain arithmetic; ε = 0.01 offers the practical sweet spot, with sub-percent error in under a second. The debiased Sinkhorn divergence S_ε removes the entropic self-bias OT_ε(a, a) > 0 and yields a genuine discrepancy that vanishes if and only if the measures coincide in the limit [1][9]. The three figures accompanying this article illustrate the Monge/Kantorovich contrast, Brenier's convex-potential geometry, and the JKO discrete flow converging to the Fokker–Planck steady state.

### 5.2 Reference implementation

```python
import numpy as np

def sinkhorn(a, b, C, eps, n_iters=1000, tol=1e-9):
    # Gibbs kernel in the log domain for numerical stability
    log_K = -C / eps
    log_a = np.log(a)
    log_b = np.log(b)
    log_u = np.zeros_like(a)
    log_v = np.zeros_like(b)
    for _ in range(n_iters):
        log_u_new = log_a - logsumexp_rows(log_K + log_v)
        log_v_new = log_b - logsumexp_cols(log_K.T + log_u_new)
        if np.max(np.abs(log_u_new - log_u)) < tol:
            break
        log_u, log_v = log_u_new, log_v_new
    log_P = log_u[:, None] + log_K + log_v[None, :]
    return np.exp(log_P)

def logsumexp_rows(M):
    m = M.max(axis=1, keepdims=True)
    return (m + np.log(np.exp(M - m).sum(axis=1, keepdims=True))).ravel()

def logsumexp_cols(M):
    m = M.max(axis=0, keepdims=True)
    return (m + np.log(np.exp(M - m).sum(axis=0, keepdims=True))).ravel()
```

The iteration is a block-coordinate ascent on the entropic dual; each update enforces one marginal exactly while the log-domain formulation keeps the Gibbs kernel representable for small ε [2][3].

### 5.3 Proof sketches

> **Theorem:** The support of an optimal plan for the quadratic cost is cyclically monotone, hence contained in the subdifferential of a convex function.

> **Proof:** If (x₁, y₁), …, (x_n, y_n) lie in the support of an optimal π, any permutation σ of the targets yields another coupling π_σ with the same marginals. Optimality gives ∫ c dπ ≤ ∫ c dπ_σ, i.e., Σ c(x_i, y_i) ≤ Σ c(x_i, y_{σ(i)}); for the quadratic cost this is exactly cyclical monotonicity. By Rockafellar's theorem, a cyclically monotone set is contained in ∂φ for some convex φ. ∎

> **Theorem:** The JKO iterates satisfy the energy-dissipation inequality F(ρ_{k+1}) + W₂²(ρ_{k+1}, ρ_k)/(2τ) ≤ F(ρ_k).

> **Proof:** ρ_k is admissible in the minimization defining ρ_{k+1}; evaluating the objective at ρ_k gives the inequality directly. Summing over k yields uniform bounds on the total squared distance traveled and on F, from which compactness and passage to the limit τ → 0 follow [6]. ∎

## 6 Limitations

The theory is not without sharp edges. First, the **curse of dimensionality**: estimating W_p from n samples converges at the rate n^{−1/d}, so in high dimensions empirical Wasserstein distances are statistically vacuous without regularization or structural assumptions [2][9]. Second, entropic regularization introduces **blur**: the optimizer π_ε is dense rather than sparse, and small-ε computations suffer underflow that only careful log-domain code tames [3]. Third, Brenier's theorem demands absolute continuity of the source; for the cost c(x, y) = |x − y| (p = 1) uniqueness fails and optimizers are highly non-unique, while for non-convex or non-twisted costs even existence of Monge maps is delicate. Fourth, the Otto calculus is *formal* in infinite dimensions — rigorous justification requires the Ambrosio–Gigli–Savaré metric theory of gradient flows, since P₂ is not a manifold in any classical sense [6][8]. Fifth, multi-marginal and unbalanced extensions, though practically important, lose the clean duality and algorithmic picture of the two-marginal balanced case. Finally, the JKO scheme is a conceptual rather than practical algorithm: each step is itself a nontrivial variational problem, and modern practice discretizes the PDE directly or uses entropic schemes [3][6].

## 7 Conclusion

Optimal transport began as Monge's earth-moving puzzle and matured, via Kantorovich's convex relaxation and duality [5], into a geometric theory of the space of probability measures. Brenier's theorem [4] revealed that quadratic optimal transport is rigid — a gradient of a convex potential — while Otto's calculus [6][8] turned the Wasserstein space into a Riemannian manifold on which diffusion is steepest descent of the free energy, a fact the JKO scheme makes constructive. Entropic regularization and Sinkhorn's algorithm [1][2][3] translated this geometry into O(n²) iterations that run on GPUs, carrying optimal transport from pure analysis into the daily toolkit of machine learning. The remaining frontiers — sample-efficient estimation in high dimensions, rigorous regularity for general costs, and scalable multi-marginal solvers — are where the next chapters will be written.
