# Fully Homomorphic Encryption: BGV and CKKS, Bootstrapping, and Noise Management

## Abstract

Fully homomorphic encryption (FHE) permits arbitrary computation directly on ciphertexts, enabling privacy-preserving outsourcing of computation to untrusted servers. This article presents a rigorous survey of the two dominant ring-learning-with-errors (RLWE) constructions: the Brakerski–Gentry–Vaikuntanathan (BGV) scheme, which computes exact arithmetic over finite plaintext spaces through modulus switching, and the Cheon–Kim–Kim–Song (CKKS) scheme, which computes approximate arithmetic over real and complex numbers through rescaling. We unify both schemes under a noise-budget framework that quantifies how addition, multiplication, relinearization, and key switching consume noise budget, and we dissect bootstrapping — the homomorphic evaluation of a scheme's own decryption circuit that refreshes exhausted ciphertexts — for both exact and approximate settings. Proof sketches cover modulus-switching correctness, multiplication noise bounds, and Gentry's bootstrappability theorem. We close with the practical limitations that still gate deployment: the circular-security assumption, bootstrapping cost and precision, and the parameter-selection problem.

## 1 Introduction

A *fully homomorphic encryption* (FHE) scheme is a public-key encryption scheme equipped with an evaluation procedure such that decrypting the homomorphically evaluated ciphertext yields the same result as applying the desired function to the plaintexts. The dream is old: Rivest, Adleman, and Dertouzos posed *privacy homomorphisms* in 1978 [9], but the first plausible construction arrived thirty years later, when Craig Gentry built FHE from ideal lattices and introduced *bootstrapping*, the technique of homomorphically re-encrypting a ciphertext to reduce its noise [9].

Gentry's blueprint had two stages. First, construct a *somewhat homomorphic* scheme that can evaluate circuits up to some bounded depth. Second, if that scheme can homomorphically evaluate its own decryption circuit (plus one extra operation), publish an encryption of the secret key and *bootstrap*: homomorphically decrypt the noisy ciphertext, obtaining a fresh encryption of the same plaintext with small, fixed noise. Repeating this indefinitely yields unbounded computation.

The decade that followed replaced ideal lattices with the (ring) learning-with-errors assumption. Brakerski and Vaikuntanathan built FHE directly from standard LWE [9], and Brakerski, Gentry, and Vaikuntanathan introduced the **BGV** scheme, a leveled FHE construction that avoids bootstrapping entirely through *modulus switching* [2]. Later, Gentry, Halevi, and Smart optimized bootstrapping itself, showing how to homomorphically evaluate the decryption circuit with only polylogarithmic overhead [4]. A separate line of work produced the **CKKS** scheme of Cheon, Kim, Kim, and Song, which trades exactness for the ability to compute natively on approximate real and complex numbers — the natural domain of machine learning [1].

This article unifies BGV and CKKS under a single analytical lens: the *noise budget*. Every ciphertext carries noise; every homomorphic operation grows it; when the budget is exhausted, decryption fails. BGV manages noise by shrinking the modulus (modulus switching), CKKS by shrinking the message scale (rescaling), and both ultimately rely on bootstrapping for unbounded depth. We formalize these mechanisms, quantify noise growth, sketch the key correctness proofs, and assess where the theory still falls short of practice.

## 2 Background

### The RLWE assumption

Consider the cyclotomic ring *R = Z[X] / (X^n + 1)*, with *R_q = R / qR* denoting reduction of coefficients modulo *q*. The *ring learning with errors* (RLWE) problem asks an adversary to distinguish samples of the form *(a, b = a·s + e)* in *R_q × R_q*, where *a* is uniform, *s* is a secret drawn from a narrow distribution (often ternary), and *e* is drawn from a discrete Gaussian of small width, from uniformly random pairs [9].

> **Geometric intuition:** picture the infinite lattice of all integer linear combinations of a basis. An RLWE sample *(a, b)* is a point *near* the lattice — displaced by a short error vector *e*. Without the secret *s* (the trapdoor), recovering the exact lattice point from the noisy sample is as hard as bounded-distance decoding in high dimension; with *s*, it is easy.

The lattice diagram below illustrates this decoding picture: the red point is the noisy observation, and the trapdoor holder rounds it back to the lattice.

![RLWE lattice decoding intuition](public/thesis/ths_1789368009000_02289-3.webp)

### Ciphertext anatomy

Both BGV and CKKS ciphertexts are RLWE pairs, but they embed the message differently:

- **BGV** encrypts a message *m* from a plaintext ring *R_p* (plaintext modulus *p*) as *ct = (a, b)* with *b = a·s + p·e + m* modulo *q*. The factor *p* amplifies the noise so that reduction modulo *p* strips it away at decryption [2].
- **CKKS** encrypts an *approximate* message: a complex vector is first encoded into a polynomial *μ* at scale *Δ* (a large integer), then *ct = (a, b)* with *b = a·s + e + μ* modulo *q*. Decryption returns *μ + e*, and decoding divides by *Δ*, so the noise *e* contaminates only the low-order fractional bits [1].

### A taxonomy of homomorphism

- *Somewhat homomorphic encryption (SHE)* supports circuits of bounded depth.
- *Leveled FHE* supports circuits of depth up to a parameter *L* fixed at key generation, with per-gate cost growing polynomially in *L* — no bootstrapping needed [2].
- *Pure (unbounded) FHE* supports arbitrary depth, typically achieved by bootstrapping whenever noise runs low.

### SIMD packing

Via the Chinese Remainder Theorem, the plaintext space decomposes into independent *slots* (roughly *n* for BGV, *n/2* complex slots for CKKS), so one ciphertext encrypts a whole vector processed slot-wise [3].

## 3 Methodology

Our analysis rests on a uniform *noise accounting* discipline. For a ciphertext *ct = (c_0, c_1)* encrypting *m* under secret *s*, define the *noise* as the small ring element *v = c_0 + c_1·s − m* (with the scheme-specific message embedding understood). Decryption is correct whenever a suitable norm of *v* stays below a threshold determined by the current modulus. Two norms appear in the literature: the coefficient infinity norm and the canonical-embedding norm; the latter is sub-multiplicative and gives tighter bounds for products [7].

We adopt the average-case variance-propagation approach for concrete estimates — tracking the variance of each coefficient of *v* through operations and validating against experiments — following recent work that accounts for dependencies between error terms generated under the same key [7]. Correctness means exact recovery for BGV (decryption returns precisely *m*) and bounded-error recovery for CKKS (decryption returns *m + e'* with *‖e'‖* small relative to the message magnitude).

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The BGV Scheme: Exact Arithmetic via Modulus Switching

BGV is a *leveled* scheme organized around a chain of moduli *q_0 > q_1 > … > q_{L−1}*. A fresh ciphertext lives at the top of the chain, at level 0 with modulus *q_0*. Homomorphic multiplication is the expensive operation: each multiplication consumes one level, and after *L* multiplications the modulus is too small to absorb further noise.

**Encryption and decryption.** With plaintext modulus *p* and secret *s*:

- *Encrypt(m)*: sample uniform *a*, small *e*; output *(a, a·s + p·e + m)*.
- *Decrypt(ct)*: compute *v = c_0 + c_1·s* modulo *q*, then output *v* modulo *p*. Since *v = m + p·e*, reducing modulo *p* removes the noise term exactly — provided *‖v‖∞ < q/2* [2].

**Modulus switching.** The key innovation of BGV is a noise-management operation that scales a ciphertext from modulus *q* down to a smaller modulus *q'*:

> **Theorem (informal):** Let *ct* encrypt *m* under modulus *q* with noise *v*. Define *ct'* by scaling each coefficient: *c'_i = round((q'/q)·c_i)*. Then *ct'* encrypts the same *m* under modulus *q'* with noise approximately *(q'/q)·v* plus a small rounding term. The ratio of noise to modulus — the quantity that determines correctness — is essentially preserved, while the absolute noise drops.

In practice the modulus chain is chosen so that after each multiplication, a modulus switch brings the noise back down to a fixed bound. Addition of ciphertexts at the same level adds their noises; to add ciphertexts at different levels, the higher one is switched down first.

**Multiplication and relinearization.** Multiplying *ct_1 = (a_1, b_1)* and *ct_2 = (a_2, b_2)* naively yields a degree-2 ciphertext *(d_0, d_1, d_2)* decryptable with *(1, s, s^2)*. *Relinearization* collapses this back to two components using an *evaluation key* — an encryption of *s^2* under *s* — at the cost of additional key-switching noise [2].

The noise-budget diagram below summarizes the lifecycle: fresh ciphertexts start with a comfortable budget, additions erode it linearly, multiplications quadratically, and modulus switching restores headroom by shrinking both modulus and noise.

### 4.2 The CKKS Scheme: Approximate Arithmetic via Rescaling

CKKS abandons exactness. Its message space is *C^{n/2}*: vectors of complex numbers, the native data type of signal processing and neural networks.

**Encoding.** The canonical embedding *σ: R → C^{n/2}* evaluates a polynomial at the primitive roots of unity. To encode a vector *z*, one computes *μ = round(Δ · σ^{−1}(z))*, a polynomial whose coefficients carry the message scaled up by *Δ*. Decoding applies *σ* and divides by *Δ*, rounding away the fractional error [1].

**Encryption and rescaling.** A ciphertext *(a, b = a·s + e + μ)* at modulus *q* decrypts to *μ + e*; decoding yields *z + e/Δ*. Two noise sources therefore exist: the RLWE encryption noise *e* and the encoding rounding error. Multiplication is where CKKS diverges from BGV: multiplying two ciphertexts at scale *Δ* produces a ciphertext at scale *Δ²*. *Rescaling* divides the ciphertext by *Δ* (rounding) and drops the modulus by the same factor, restoring scale *Δ* at the price of one level — exactly analogous to BGV's modulus switching, except that the *message itself* shrinks rather than the noise [1].

The semantic consequence is profound: CKKS decryption is *approximate by design*. The application must tolerate errors in the least significant bits, much like floating-point arithmetic. Variants exist that remove the LWE noise from encryption and key switching entirely, leaving only encoding and rescaling rounding as error sources, which simplifies precision analysis considerably [6].

### 4.3 Noise Dynamics: A Unified Budget Analysis

| Operation | Noise growth (BGV) | Noise growth (CKKS) | Budget cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fresh encryption | *O(p·σ·√n)* | *O(B_enc + B_enc-encode)* | 1 level of headroom |
| Homomorphic addition | *B_1 + B_2* (linear) | *B_1 + B_2* (linear) | Small |
| Homomorphic multiplication | *≈ B_1·B_2·poly(n)* before mod-switch | Scale squares; rescale restores | 1 level |
| Relinearization / key switching | *+ O(n·log q·B)* additive | *+ O(B_ks)* additive | Small |
| Modulus switch (BGV) | Noise × *q'/q*, ratio preserved | — | 1 level, restores budget |
| Rescale (CKKS) | — | Noise × *1/Δ* + rounding | 1 level, restores budget |

The following Python sketch simulates the budget lifecycle for a BGV-style chain, showing how each multiplication consumes a level while modulus switching resets the noise to its baseline:

```python
import math, random

def simulate_budget(levels, mult_noise_factor=8.0, add_noise=1.0, threshold=100.0):
    """Track noise (log scale) across a BGV modulus chain."""
    noise, budget_log = 1.0, []
    ops = ["enc"] + ["mult", "modswitch"] * (levels - 1) + ["add"] * 3
    for op in ops:
        if op == "mult":
            noise = noise * noise * mult_noise_factor  # quadratic growth
        elif op == "modswitch":
            noise = 1.0  # modulus switch restores baseline
        elif op == "add":
            noise = noise + add_noise
        ok = noise < threshold
        budget_log.append((op, math.log2(noise), ok))
    return budget_log

for op, logn, ok in simulate_budget(levels=4):
    print(f"{op:10s} log2(noise)={logn:6.2f}  decrypts={'yes' if ok else 'NO'}")
```

Accurate parameter selection demands more than these asymptotics: average-case analyses that model the *dependence* between error terms sharing a key predict real noise far more tightly than worst-case bounds, directly shrinking the required modulus chain and improving performance [7].

### 4.4 Bootstrapping: Refreshing Exhausted Ciphertexts

When the modulus chain (BGV) or the rescaling levels (CKKS) run out, *bootstrapping* refreshes the ciphertext: homomorphically evaluate the decryption circuit on the noisy ciphertext using an encrypted copy of the secret key, producing a fresh encryption of the same message with small noise. Gentry's *bootstrappability* condition states that if a somewhat homomorphic scheme can evaluate its own (augmented) decryption circuit, it can be made fully homomorphic [9].

**BGV bootstrapping pipeline.** The modern pipeline, refined by Gentry, Halevi, and Smart, proceeds in four stages [3]:

1. **Modulus switch down.** Switch the exhausted ciphertext from its large modulus to a small one (roughly *p^e* for small *e*), so the decryption formula becomes a low-degree computation.
2. **Homomorphic linear decryption.** Using the bootstrapping key (an encryption of *s*), homomorphically compute the linear part *c_0 + c_1·s*.
3. **Digit extraction / rounding.** Homomorphically evaluate the non-linear step that strips the noise: reduce modulo *p^e* and round, recovering an encryption of *m* alone. This is the expensive step, implemented via polynomial approximations of the modular-reduction map with only polylogarithmic overhead [4].
4. **Modulus switch up.** Lift the refreshed ciphertext back to a large modulus, restoring a full noise budget.

The pipeline diagram below traces a ciphertext through these four stages, from exhausted input to refreshed output.

![Bootstrapping pipeline: modulus switching, homomorphic decryption, rounding](public/thesis/ths_1789368009000_02289-1.webp)

**CKKS bootstrapping pipeline.** Approximate bootstrapping is structurally similar but replaces exact rounding with *homomorphic modular reduction* of a scaled value [5]:

1. **Modulus raise.** Lift the ciphertext to a larger modulus, interpreting the message-plus-noise as an integer.
2. **CoeffToSlot.** Homomorphically move polynomial coefficients into the SIMD slots via the linear encoding transform.
3. **Homomorphic modular reduction.** Evaluate *t ↦ t mod q* homomorphically. The standard approach approximates a scaled sine function composed with itself, exploiting double-angle identities to keep multiplicative depth low [5].
4. **SlotToCoeff.** Transform back to coefficient representation.

Each CKKS bootstrap consumes substantial depth (on the order of ten multiplicative levels for the modular reduction alone) and injects approximation error, historically limiting precision to roughly twenty bits of fixed-point accuracy — though error-variance-optimal polynomial approximations and lazy relinearization have steadily improved both precision and depth [5].

### 4.5 BGV versus CKKS: Selecting a Scheme

The choice between the schemes is a choice between exactness and expressiveness:

| Dimension | BGV | CKKS |
|---|---|---|
| Message space | Exact integers / finite fields via *R_p* | Approximate complex vectors *C^{n/2}* |
| Noise management | Modulus switching (modulus shrinks) | Rescaling (message scale shrinks) |
| Correctness | Bit-exact if budget suffices | Approximate; error in low-order bits |
| Bootstrapping | Exact digit extraction, polylog overhead [3][4] | Approximate modular reduction via sine polynomials [5] |
| Typical workloads | Integer arithmetic, comparisons, exact aggregation | ML inference, signal processing, statistics |
| Implementations | HElib, OpenFHE, Lattigo | SEAL, OpenFHE, Lattigo |

## 5 Proofs

We sketch the three foundational results; full proofs appear in the cited works.

> **Theorem (Modulus-switching correctness, BGV [2]):** Let *ct* be a ciphertext encrypting *m ∈ R_p* under modulus *q* with noise *v* satisfying *‖v‖∞ < q/2 − (p/2)·n·‖s‖∞* (up to constants). Let *q' < q* and define *ct'* by coefficient-wise scaling *c'_i = ⌊(q'/q)·c_i⌉*. Then *ct'* encrypts *m* under modulus *q'* with noise *v'* satisfying *‖v'‖∞ ≤ (q'/q)·‖v‖∞ + (p/2)·√n·‖s‖∞* roughly — i.e., the noise-to-modulus ratio is preserved up to an additive rounding term.

> *Proof sketch.* Write *c_i = (q'/q)^{−1}·c'_i + δ_i* with *|δ_i| ≤ 1/2*. The decryption identity *c_0 + c_1·s = m + p·e* (over the integers, using centered representatives) scales linearly: the left side becomes *(q'/q)(c_0 + c_1·s) + (δ_0 + δ_1·s)*, and dividing through shows the new noise equals *(q'/q)·v* plus the rounding contribution *δ_0 + δ_1·s*, whose norm is bounded by the secret's norm times √n. The modulus-*p* message is untouched because scaling commutes with the embedding. ∎

> **Theorem (Multiplication noise bound [2][7]):** Let *ct_1, ct_2* encrypt *m_1, m_2* with noises bounded by *B_1, B_2* in canonical-embedding norm. Then the tensor-product ciphertext has noise bounded by *O(B_1·B_2 + p·(B_1 + B_2)·poly(n))*. After relinearization and one modulus switch, the noise returns to *O(p·σ·poly(n))*, independent of the input noises.

> *Proof sketch.* The tensor product decrypts via *(1, s, s²)*; expanding *(m_1 + v_1)(m_2 + v_2)* gives noise *m_1·v_2 + m_2·v_1 + v_1·v_2*, and sub-multiplicativity of the canonical norm yields the quadratic bound. Relinearization replaces the *s²* term using the evaluation key, adding only the key-switching noise, which is linear in the gadget decomposition. A final modulus switch divides everything by the growth factor. ∎

> **Theorem (Bootstrappability implies FHE — Gentry [9]):** If a somewhat homomorphic scheme can homomorphically evaluate its own decryption circuit augmented with one additional NAND (or AND) gate — i.e., it is *bootstrappable* — then it can be transformed into a fully homomorphic scheme assuming circular security (that publishing an encryption of the secret key is safe).

> *Proof sketch.* Publish *Enc(s)* as the bootstrapping key. Given a ciphertext whose noise is near the decryption threshold, homomorphically evaluate *Decrypt* on it: each gate of the decryption circuit is evaluated on encrypted bits, and the scheme's homomorphism guarantees the output encrypts *Decrypt(ct) = m*. Because the evaluated circuit's depth is fixed (the decryption circuit plus one gate, which the scheme supports by bootstrappability), the output noise depends only on the scheme parameters, not on the computation history. Refreshing after every operation (or whenever needed) bounds noise forever, yielding unbounded depth. Circular security is required because the evaluator holds *Enc(s)*. ∎

A Haskell-flavored sketch captures the level-indexed structure that makes these proofs go through — ciphertexts carry their level as a type-level index, and operations that consume levels are visible in the types:

```haskell
-- Levels indexed by a type-level natural; Q 0 is the top of the chain.
data Ctxt (lvl :: Nat) = Ctxt Poly Poly

modSwitch :: Ctxt lvl -> Ctxt (lvl + 1)   -- noise ratio preserved
addCtxt   :: Ctxt lvl -> Ctxt lvl -> Ctxt lvl
multCtxt  :: EvalKey -> Ctxt lvl -> Ctxt lvl -> Ctxt (lvl + 1)

-- Bootstrapping resets the index: any level back to Q 0.
bootstrap :: BootKey -> Ctxt lvl -> Ctxt 0
```

## 6 Limitations

Despite the maturity of the theory, several obstacles separate FHE from routine deployment:

- **Circular security.** Bootstrapping publishes an encryption of the secret key. No proof reduces this *key-dependent message* security to RLWE; it remains an assumption [9].
- **Bootstrapping cost.** A single bootstrap remains orders of magnitude more expensive than the computation it refreshes. BGV bootstrapping needs deep homomorphic digit extraction [3][4]; CKKS bootstrapping consumes ten or more levels and injects approximation error [5].
- **CKKS precision.** Approximate bootstrapping historically caps precision near twenty fixed-point bits. Error-variance-optimal approximations improve this substantially, but CKKS can never offer the bit-exact guarantees of BGV [5][6].
- **Parameter selection.** Choosing the modulus chain, ring degree, and error width to simultaneously guarantee security, correctness, and performance is delicate; average-case noise analyses help but remain scheme- and library-specific [7].
- **Security model.** RLWE-based FHE is IND-CPA secure, not CCA secure: decryption oracles and malformed ciphertexts can leak the key. Applications must never expose decryption of adversarially chosen ciphertexts [8].
- **Key sizes.** Evaluation and bootstrapping keys run to tens or hundreds of megabytes, complicating deployment on constrained devices.

## 7 Conclusion

BGV and CKKS represent two complementary answers to the noise problem. BGV keeps arithmetic exact and pays for noise growth by shrinking the modulus [2]. CKKS keeps arithmetic natural for real numbers and pays by shrinking the message scale [1]. Both ascend to unbounded computation through bootstrapping: homomorphically re-running decryption to launder away accumulated noise, exactly via digit extraction [3][4] or approximately via homomorphic modular reduction [5].

The unified noise-budget viewpoint clarifies that these are not competing tricks but instances of one principle: *correctness depends on the ratio of noise to modulus, and every level of computation must be paid for in modulus bits*. The mathematics of computing on encrypted data is largely settled; the engineering of making it fast is the frontier.
