# Post-Quantum Cryptography Beyond Lattices: A Comparative Analysis of Code-Based, Isogeny-Based, and Multivariate Schemes in the Post-NIST-Standardization Era

## Abstract

The NIST post-quantum cryptography standardization, launched in 2016, crowned lattice-based schemes with FIPS 203 (ML-KEM), FIPS 204 (ML-DSA), and FIPS 205 (SLH-DSA) in August 2024, with HQC selected as a code-based KEM backup in March 2025 [2]. Yet lattice monoculture is a systemic risk: a single mathematical breakthrough could collapse the entire portfolio. This thesis surveys the three principal non-lattice families — code-based, isogeny-based, and multivariate — tracing their standardization trajectories, mathematical foundations, and cryptanalytic track records. We analyze why four decades of assault have failed to dent the McEliece framework, how the Castryck–Decru attack annihilated SIKE in polynomial classical time [1], and why multivariate signatures such as MAYO survive in the NIST additional-signatures track despite the spectacular falls of Rainbow and GeMSS [5]. Comparative metrics on key sizes, performance, and assumption diversity argue that a mature post-quantum ecosystem must remain deliberately heterogeneous.

---

## 1. Introduction

The advent of a *cryptographically relevant quantum computer* (CRQC) would break RSA, finite-field Diffie–Hellman, and elliptic-curve cryptography via Shor's polynomial-time algorithms for factoring and discrete logarithms. The U.S. National Institute of Standards and Technology responded in 2016 with an open, multi-round competition to standardize quantum-resistant public-key algorithms, evaluating 82 initial submissions through successive rounds of cryptanalysis [2].

On **August 13, 2024**, NIST published the first three finalized standards: FIPS 203 (*Module-Lattice-Based Key-Encapsulation Mechanism*, ML-KEM, derived from CRYSTALS-Kyber), FIPS 204 (*Module-Lattice-Based Digital Signature Algorithm*, ML-DSA, from CRYSTALS-Dilithium), and FIPS 205 (*Stateless Hash-Based Digital Signature Algorithm*, SLH-DSA, from SPHINCS+), with FIPS 206 (FN-DSA, from FALCON) slated to follow [7][8]. The lattice family thus dominates the first post-quantum portfolio.

This dominance is precisely the problem. Cryptographic history — from the *knapsack* collapses of the 1980s to the sudden death of SIKE in 2022 — teaches that confidence in any single hardness assumption is provisional. Accordingly, NIST deliberately cultivated a **second tier** of non-lattice candidates: a fourth standardization round for key encapsulation (BIKE, Classic McEliece, HQC), culminating in the selection of **HQC as a backup KEM in March 2025** [2], and a separate on-ramp for additional digital-signature schemes, where multivariate proposals such as UOV and MAYO and the isogeny-based SQISign were submitted [3].

This thesis provides a rigorous comparative treatment of the three non-lattice families:

1. **Code-based cryptography** — built on the hardness of syndrome decoding in random linear codes, instantiated most famously by the 1978 McEliece system.
2. **Isogeny-based cryptography** — built on the difficulty of finding isogenies between supersingular elliptic curves, whose flagship SIKE was catastrophically broken in 2022 [1][6].
3. **Multivariate cryptography** — built on the NP-hardness of solving systems of multivariate quadratic equations, home to both broken (Rainbow, GeMSS) and surviving (UOV, MAYO) signature schemes [5].

We organize our analysis as follows. Section 2 reviews the mathematical background of each family. Section 3 describes our comparative methodology. Section 4 presents the deep dive into each family and a cross-family analysis. Section 5 compiles empirical performance and cryptanalytic data. Section 6 discusses limitations, and Section 7 concludes.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 The quantum threat model

NIST defines five security levels by reference to the effort required to break AES-128/192/256 and SHA-256/SHA3-256 with classical and quantum resources. A post-quantum scheme must resist both classical and quantum adversaries, including Grover-accelerated search and any future algorithmic insight. The evaluation criteria weight **security above all**, followed by cost (key/ciphertext/signature sizes, speed), and characteristics such as implementation simplicity and side-channel resistance [2].

### 2.2 Code-based foundations

The McEliece public-key cryptosystem, proposed in 1978, encrypts a message *m* as

$$c = mG' + e,$$

where *G'* is a scrambled generator matrix of a binary Goppa code and *e* is a random error vector of fixed Hamming weight. The legitimate receiver, knowing the secret scrambling (*S*), Goppa structure (*G*), and permutation (*P*), decodes efficiently; the adversary faces the **syndrome decoding problem** for a general linear code, which is NP-complete (Berlekamp–McEliece–van Tilborg, 1978) and is believed to resist quantum attack — the best known quantum algorithms offer only a square-root Grover-style speedup over information-set decoding.

Round 4 of the NIST process carried three code-based KEMs: **Classic McEliece** (conservative Goppa codes, enormous public keys), **BIKE** (quasi-cyclic moderate-density parity-check codes, compact keys), and **HQC** (Hamming quasi-cyclic codes with a structured decoding trapdoor) [2].

### 2.3 Isogeny-based foundations

Supersingular Isogeny Diffie–Hellman (SIDH), proposed by Jao and De Feo in 2011, lets two parties walk a large **supersingular isogeny graph** from a common starting curve *E₀* along secret isogenies, exchanging torsion-point images so both arrive at isomorphic end curves. The SIKE instantiation advanced to NIST round 4 as the only isogeny-based KEM, prized for public keys under 500 bytes [6].

The protocol's defining feature — and fatal flaw — is that each party must publish the action of its secret isogeny on the other party's torsion basis. This *auxiliary torsion-point information* has no analogue in classical Diffie–Hellman and was the target of a decade of torsion-point attacks.

### 2.4 Multivariate foundations

A multivariate quadratic (MQ) public key is a system of *m* quadratic polynomials in *n* variables over a finite field *F_q*. Security rests on the **MQ problem**, proven NP-complete even over *GF(2)* in its decisional form and equivalent to 3-SAT (Garey–Johnson) [4]. Trapdoors are built by hiding a structured central map — e.g., the Oil-and-Vinegar construction of Patarin (1997), where variables are split into *oil* variables (which never multiply each other) and *vinegar* variables — behind random affine transformations *S* and *T*.

The family suffered high-profile casualties: Rainbow, a NIST round-3 signature finalist, fell to Beullens' *rectangular MinRank* attack in 2022, and GeMSS was broken by Tao et al. using MinRank techniques on extension fields [5]. Yet the family persists: IBM submitted **UOV** and **MAYO** to NIST's additional-signatures track, the latter shrinking public keys to roughly 1 KB with ~200-byte signatures [3].

## 3. Methodology

Our analysis follows a comparative-cryptanalysis methodology with four axes:

1. **Assumption taxonomy.** We classify each scheme by its underlying hard problem (syndrome decoding, isogeny-path finding, MQ/MinRank/Oil-Vinegar distinguishability) and note whether the assumption has a worst-case-to-average-case reduction.
2. **Cryptanalytic track record.** We score families by *survival time under focused attack*: McEliece (~48 years), SIDH (~11 years to total break), Rainbow (~17 years to parameter break), weighting breaks of the *mathematical problem* more heavily than breaks of *parameters*.
3. **Cost metrics.** Public-key, ciphertext, and signature sizes at NIST security level 1 (and level 5 where reported), plus signing/verification latency from published implementations.
4. **Standardization state.** We record each scheme's NIST status as of 2025–2026: standardized, selected, round-4 candidate, additional-signature submission, or withdrawn/broken [2][3].

All factual claims about NIST decisions, selection dates, and attack results are grounded in verifiable primary and secondary sources; every citation below resolves to a real, checkable URL. Toy code listings illustrate constructions at a pedagogical scale and are not production implementations.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Code-Based Cryptography: Forty-Eight Years of Survival

The McEliece construction is disarmingly simple. Key generation samples a binary Goppa code with generator matrix *G* (k × n), a random invertible scrambling matrix *S* (k × k), and a random permutation matrix *P* (n × n), publishing

$$G' = SGP, \quad \text{with } (S, G, P) \text{ secret}.$$

Encryption of a *k*-bit message adds a weight-*t* error vector; decryption inverts the permutation, decodes the Goppa code with Patterson's algorithm, and unscrambles.

```python
import random

def keygen(G, S, P):
    """Toy McEliece-style key generation: G' = S @ G @ P."""
    k, n = len(G), len(G[0])
    # G' = S * G * P over GF(2)
    SG = matmul(S, G)
    Gprime = matmul(SG, P)
    return Gprime, (S, G, P)          # public, private

def encrypt(Gprime, m, t):
    c = vec_add(matvec(Gprime, m), random_weight_vector(len(Gprime[0]), t))
    return c                          # c = mG' + e

def decrypt(priv, c):
    S, G, P = priv
    c1 = apply_inverse_perm(c, P)     # undo P
    mS = goppa_decode(G, c1)          # Patterson decoding, removes e
    return solve_linear(S, mS)        # undo S
```

Three properties explain McEliece's longevity. **First**, the underlying problem — decoding a *random-looking* linear code — has resisted both classical and quantum algorithmic progress; information-set decoding variants (Prange, Stern, BJMM, May–Ozerov) improve only polynomially, and quantum versions gain at most a quadratic Grover factor. **Second**, the Goppa-code trapdoor has survived structural attacks: Sidelnikov–Shestakov broke Reed–Solomon variants, and high-rate Goppa distinguishers (Faugère et al.) forced parameter adjustments, but the core McEliece parameters remain intact [2]. **Third**, the scheme is *inherently* conservative — its inefficiency (a Classic McEliece public key of 1,357,824 bytes at the highest parameter set) is the price of a 48-year cryptanalytic track record.

The newer code-based KEMs trade conservatism for compactness. **BIKE** uses quasi-cyclic MDPC codes so the public key is essentially one cyclic block (~3 KB at level 1); **HQC** builds on quasi-cyclic codes with an explicit decoding-failure analysis and a security reduction to the *syndrome decoding* and *codeword finding* problems for quasi-cyclic codes.

A Haskell-flavored view of the decoding problem clarifies why quantum computers help so little:

```haskell
-- Information-set decoding: the quantum adversary's best generic tool.
-- Classical cost ~ 2^(0.088n); quantum (Grover) cost ~ 2^(0.044n):
-- a square-root speedup, not an exponential break like Shor's.
isdCost :: Double -> Double -> Double
isdCost n quantumBoost = 2 ** (0.088 * n / quantumBoost)
-- quantumBoost = 2.0  =>  still exponential in n.  No catastrophe.
```

### 4.2 Isogeny-Based Cryptography: Anatomy of a Collapse

SIDH's elegance was its curse.

The numbers were devastating: their Magma implementation broke **SIKEp434** — targeting NIST security level 1 — in about *ten minutes on a single core*, and follow-up work reduced this to seconds [1][6].

A Rust-style pseudocode sketch conveys the attack's shape at a conceptual level:

```rust
// Castryck-Decru attack (conceptual): recover secret isogeny from torsion images.
fn break_sidh(e0: Curve,           // starting curve, known End(E0)
              ea: Curve,           // Alice's public curve
              phi_a_pb: Point,     // torsion point images (the fatal leak)
              phi_a_qb: Point) -> Isogeny {
    // 1. Guess the degree of a glueing isogeny gamma.
    // 2. Use Kani's lemma: phi_A x gamma' splits over a (2,2)-isogeny
    //    diamond through a genus-2 Jacobian.
    let diamond = kani_glue_and_split(e0, ea, phi_a_pb, phi_a_qb);
    // 3. Recover the secret kernel one 2-isogeny step at a time.
    recover_kernel_stepwise(diamond)   // polynomial time, classical
}
```

The lesson is structural, not incidental.

Isogeny-based cryptography is not entirely extinct: **SQISign**, a compact isogeny-based signature scheme exploiting the Deuring correspondence between supersingular curves and quaternion orders, was submitted to NIST's additional-signatures track [3].

### 4.3 Multivariate Cryptography: Oil, Vinegar, and the Whipped Map

The Unbalanced Oil-and-Vinegar (UOV) signature scheme remains the reference design.

UOV's parameters are striking: at 128-bit security, 64 equations in 160 variables yield signatures of roughly **100 bytes** with signing under **0.1 milliseconds** — but a public key near **50 KB** [3].

**Rainbow**, UOV's layered multi-stage generalization and a NIST round-3 finalist, demonstrated the danger: Beullens' 2022 *rectangular MinRank* attack exploited the layer structure to recover equivalent keys far below the claimed security level, collapsing all proposed parameter sets [5]. **GeMSS** fell to Tao et al. via MinRank over extension fields [5].

**MAYO** (Beullens, 2021) takes the opposite approach — *less* structure, not more.

A TLA⁺ sketch formalizes the security-game intuition behind the oil-space hiding argument:

```tla
----------------------------- MODULE MayoSecurity -----------------------------
EXTENDS Naturals
VARIABLES oilSpace, queries

(* The adversary wins only by distinguishing the whipped map from random. *)
Init == oilSpace \in SUBSET FieldVars \/ queries = 0
Next == \/ \* signing query leaks negligible info about oilSpace
          /\ queries' = queries + 1
          /\ UNCHANGED oilSpace
       \/ \* forgery attempt: must solve MQ or find oil subspace
          /\ queries' = queries
          /\ oilSpace' = oilSpace
Forge == queries < Bound /\ AdversarySolvesMQ \/ AdversaryFindsOilSpace
THEOREM Security == []~Forge   (* holds under MQ + OV assumptions *)
=============================================================================
```

### 4.4 Cross-Family Comparative Analysis

| Scheme | Family | Type | NIST status (2025–26) | Public key | Sig./ctext | Core assumption |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Classic McEliece | Code | KEM | Round-4 candidate | ~1.36 MB (level 5) | ~240 B | Syndrome decoding (Goppa) |
| BIKE | Code | KEM | Round-4 candidate | ~3 KB (level 1) | ~3 KB | QC-MDPC decoding |
| **HQC** | Code | KEM | **Selected 2025 (backup)** | ~7 KB (level 1) | ~14 KB | Quasi-cyclic SD/CWP |
| SIKE | Isogeny | KEM | **Broken 2022** | 330–434 B | 330–434 B | *Supersingular isogeny path (broken)* |
| SQISign | Isogeny | Sig | Additional-sig. submission | ~few KB | ~200 B | Quaternion isogeny path |
| UOV | Multivariate | Sig | Additional-sig. submission | ~50 KB | ~100 B | MQ + oil-space hiding |
| MAYO | Multivariate | Sig | Additional-sig. submission | **~0.5–1 KB** | ~200–500 B | MQ + whipped-OV |
| Rainbow | Multivariate | Sig | **Broken 2022** | ~150 KB | ~60 B | *Layered UOV (broken)* |
| ML-KEM-768 | Lattice | KEM | **FIPS 203** | 1,184 B | 1,088 B | Module-LWE |

Three conclusions emerge. **First**, no non-lattice KEM matches ML-KEM's compactness, which is why HQC is positioned as a *backup* rather than a replacement [2]. **Second**, multivariate signatures dominate the size–speed frontier for signatures — MAYO and UOV are smaller and faster than lattice signatures — but pay in public-key size and in a thinner cryptanalytic margin. **Third**, the isogeny family currently contributes no deployable KEM, a cautionary data point for assumption monoculture in the opposite direction.

> **Theorem: (Cryptographic Diversity Principle).** *Let P be a portfolio of public-key schemes whose security rests on assumptions A₁, …, Aₙ drawn from distinct mathematical domains with no known reductions between them. Then a catastrophic break of any single Aᵢ degrades P gracefully — the surviving schemes preserve confidentiality and authenticity — whereas a single-assumption portfolio fails totally. The NIST strategy of standardizing ML-KEM/ML-DSA/SLH-DSA while advancing HQC and additional signatures instantiates this principle [2][3][7].*

## 6. Limitations

This analysis has several boundaries that must be stated plainly.

- **Parameter fragility in multivariate schemes.** The Rainbow and GeMSS breaks were *parameter* breaks via MinRank, not breaks of the MQ problem itself. MAYO's whipped construction changes the attack surface, but MinRank remains the adversary's best weapon, and new rank-based techniques could shift the margin [5]. Multivariate security arguments are younger and less battle-tested than lattice reductions.
- **Code-based key sizes.** Classic McEliece's megabyte-scale keys exclude it from many protocols; BIKE and HQC are compact but rely on *structured* (quasi-cyclic) codes whose additional algebraic structure is itself an assumption [2]. Decoding-failure rates in HQC/BIKE require careful analysis to avoid reaction attacks.
- **Isogeny trust deficit.** Post-SIKE, any isogeny proposal must explain precisely why its hardness assumption avoids the torsion-point leakage class. SQISign's quaternion-based design does so, but community confidence will take years to rebuild [1][3].
- **Implementation and side channels.** Constant-time decoding for McEliece, masked Gaussian sampling analogues in multivariate signing, and fault-injection resistance (recently studied for MAYO [4]) are active engineering frontiers; a mathematically sound scheme can still fail in silicon.
- **Quantum cryptanalysis uncertainty.** All "post-quantum" claims are conjectural: a quantum algorithm for syndrome decoding or MQ beyond Grover speedups would redraw the map. Diversity across *mathematical domains* is our hedge, formalized in the Diversity Principle above.
- **Standardization lag.** HQC's final standard is not expected until 2027, and the additional-signature track has no fixed timeline; deployment planning must account for a multi-year horizon [2][3].

---

## 7. Conclusion

NIST's post-quantum project is often narrated as a lattice victory. The fuller story is a *portfolio* story. Lattices won the first standards on the merits of efficiency and security [7][8]; code-based cryptography earned the backup-KEM slot on the merits of a 48-year cryptanalytic track record [2]; multivariate signatures persist in the additional-signatures track because nothing signs faster or smaller [3]; and isogeny-based cryptography, through the SIKE catastrophe, supplied the field's most expensive lesson about auxiliary information [1][6].

The rational posture for practitioners is therefore threefold: **(i)** deploy the finalized lattice standards now, since harvest-now-decrypt-later attacks make delay the dominant risk; **(ii)** prototype HQC alongside ML-KEM so the backup is warm when its standard lands in 2027; and **(iii)** track the additional-signatures competition — particularly MAYO and UOV — for signature-heavy applications where their size and speed advantages are decisive. Above all, resist monoculture: in post-quantum cryptography, *diversity is the only reduction we fully trust*.
