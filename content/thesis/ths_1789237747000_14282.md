# The Two-Dimensional Ising Model: Onsager's Exact Solution, Critical Phenomena, and Universality

## Abstract

The two-dimensional Ising model is the canonical exactly solved model of a continuous phase transition. This thesis develops the theory from first principles: the one-dimensional transfer matrix, where the Perron–Frobenius theorem forbids a finite-temperature transition; the Kramers–Wannier duality that locates the critical coupling; Onsager's 1944 exact free energy via the transfer-matrix Lie algebra, and Kaufman's 1949 spinor reformulation reducing the problem to free fermions; Yang's 1952 spontaneous magnetization $(1-\sinh^{-4}2K)^{1/8}$, fixing $\beta=1/8$. We assemble the exact exponents $\alpha=0$, $\gamma=7/4$, $\delta=15$, $\nu=1$, $\eta=1/4$, verify the scaling relations, and recast the critical point through the renormalization group and conformal field theory: the critical theory is the Virasoro minimal model $\mathcal{M}(3,4)$ with $c=1/2$. Finally we survey the unsolved three-dimensional problem, where the conformal bootstrap has determined operator dimensions to better than one part in $10^6$, and discuss finite-size scaling and the limits of exact methods.

## 1 Introduction

Few achievements in statistical physics match the drama of Lars Onsager's 1944 announcement: an interacting model with infinitely many degrees of freedom, a model with a genuine cooperative phase transition, could be solved exactly. The one-dimensional Ising model, solved in Ising's 1925 thesis, shows no finite-temperature transition — thermal fluctuations always destroy long-range order. Whether the same held in two dimensions was, in 1935, "rather doubtful" to the point that several distinguished physicists argued a transition was impossible [1]. Onsager's solution proved otherwise: the square-lattice Ising model exhibits a continuous phase transition at positive temperature, with a logarithmically divergent specific heat — a *new* kind of critical behavior that mean-field theory had completely failed to predict.

This thesis presents a complete, self-contained account of the exact theory of the two-dimensional Ising model and its conceptual consequences. We begin with the one-dimensional transfer matrix, the workhorse technique that reduces a statistical sum to an eigenvalue problem. We then develop the Kramers–Wannier duality [2], an exact high-temperature/low-temperature symmetry whose self-dual point *must* be the critical point, fixing the critical temperature as

$$T_c = \frac{2J}{k_B \ln(1+\sqrt{2})} \approx 2.269185\,\frac{J}{k_B}.$$

We then treat Onsager's solution [1] — the reduction of the row-to-row transfer matrix to a finite-dimensional Lie algebra (the *Onsager algebra*) — and Bruria Kaufman's 1949 reformulation [3], which expresses the transfer matrix through anticommuting spinor operators and thereby reveals the model's hidden identity: it is equivalent to a gas of *non-interacting fermions*. This fermionization made tractable the correlation functions later computed by McCoy and Wu [5] and, in a draft announced in 1949 and published in final form by Yang in 1952, the spontaneous magnetization [4, 6].

The second half of the thesis concerns what the solution *means*. The exact exponents — $\alpha = 0$ (logarithmic), $\beta = 1/8$, $\gamma = 7/4$, $\delta = 15$, $\nu = 1$, $\eta = 1/4$ — satisfy the scaling relations derived by Kadanoff and Fisher, vindicate the renormalization-group (RG) picture of critical phenomena [11, 12], and are reproduced from conformal invariance: the critical point is described by the $c=1/2$ Virasoro minimal model [8], the simplest non-trivial conformal field theory (CFT), building on Polyakov's observation of emergent conformal symmetry at criticality [9] and Cardy's analysis of finite geometries [10]. The exact integrability structures — the star-triangle (Yang–Baxter) relation — connect the Ising model to the broader exactly solved landscape surveyed by Baxter [7].

Finally, we confront the three-dimensional Ising model, for which no exact solution exists and none is expected. Here the narrative reverses: the *two-dimensional* solution and its CFT description became the seed of the modern conformal bootstrap, which has now determined the three-dimensional critical exponents to unprecedented precision [13, 14, 15] — a striking instance of an exactly solved model teaching us how to solve one that is not.

## 2 Background

### The Hamiltonian and the partition function

Consider a square lattice $\Lambda$ with $N$ sites. At each site $i$ sits a classical spin $\sigma_i \in \{+1, -1\}$. In zero external field, the energy of a configuration $\{\sigma\}$ is

$$H(\{\sigma\}) = -J \sum_{\langle i,j \rangle} \sigma_i \sigma_j, \qquad J > 0,$$

where the sum runs over nearest-neighbor pairs. The Gibbs weight of a configuration at inverse temperature $\beta = 1/(k_B T)$ is $e^{-\beta H}$, and the partition function

$$Z_N(\beta) = \sum_{\{\sigma\}} e^{\beta J \sum_{\langle i,j \rangle} \sigma_i \sigma_j}$$

encodes all thermodynamics through the free energy density $f(\beta) = -\lim_{N\to\infty} \frac{1}{\beta N} \ln Z_N$. The order parameter is the spontaneous magnetization $m = \lim_{h \to 0^+} \lim_{N \to \infty} \langle \sigma_i \rangle$, and a phase transition occurs when thermodynamic quantities or their derivatives become singular as $N \to \infty$.

### The one-dimensional warm-up: the transfer matrix

In one dimension, the problem is elementary but instructive. Writing the Boltzmann factor as a product over bonds, $Z_N = \sum_{\sigma_1,\dots,\sigma_N} \prod_{i=1}^N e^{K \sigma_i \sigma_{i+1}}$ with $K = \beta J$ and periodic boundary conditions $\sigma_{N+1} = \sigma_1$, we introduce the $2 \times 2$ transfer matrix

$$T = \begin{pmatrix} e^{K} & e^{-K} \\ e^{-K} & e^{K} \end{pmatrix}, \qquad Z_N = \mathrm{Tr}\, T^N.$$

Its eigenvalues $\lambda_\pm = 2\cosh K, \, 2\sinh K$ give $Z_N = \lambda_+^N + \lambda_-^N$, and the free energy per spin is $f = -k_B T \ln(2 \cosh K)$, analytic for all finite $T$. The correlation length $\xi = -1/\ln(\lambda_-/\lambda_+) = -1/\ln(\tanh K)$ diverges only as $T \to 0$. *There is no finite-temperature phase transition in one dimension*: this follows generally from the Perron–Frobenius theorem, which guarantees that the largest eigenvalue of the transfer matrix with all-positive entries is non-degenerate and analytic in the parameters, so that no eigenvalue crossing — and hence no singularity — can occur at finite temperature. The one-dimensional model therefore serves as a negative control: cooperative phenomena require at least two dimensions.

### High- and low-temperature expansions and duality

Before Onsager's solution, Kramers and Wannier [2] extracted the critical point from pure symmetry. The high-temperature expansion of $Z$ is a sum over closed graphs (polygons) on the lattice, weighted by $(\tanh K)^{\text{length}}$. The low-temperature expansion, by contrast, is a sum over domain walls separating regions of aligned spins. On the square lattice these two expansions have identical combinatorial structure on the *dual* lattice, yielding the exact duality relation

$$Z(K) \propto Z(K^*), \qquad e^{-2K^*} = \tanh K.$$

A single phase transition must occur at the self-dual fixed point $K = K^*$, i.e. $\sinh(2K_c) = 1$, or $K_c = \tfrac{1}{2}\ln(1+\sqrt{2}) \approx 0.440687$ [2]. This argument identifies $T_c$ exactly while assuming only that a single transition exists — an assumption Onsager's full solution would confirm.

## 3 Methodology

### Onsager's transfer-matrix Lie algebra

Onsager [1] built the two-dimensional partition function from a row-to-row transfer matrix $V$ acting on the $2^M$-dimensional Hilbert space of a row of $M$ spins. Schematically, $V = V_2^{1/2} V_1 V_2^{1/2}$, where $V_1$ encodes intra-row couplings and $V_2$ inter-row couplings. The stroke of genius: Onsager showed that $V_1$ and $V_2$ can be written as exponentials of operators $A_0$ and $A_1$ that, under repeated commutation, generate a *finite-dimensional* Lie algebra — the Onsager algebra — with basis elements $A_m, G_m$ satisfying

$$[A_l, A_m] = 4 G_{l-m}, \qquad [G_l, A_m] = 2A_{m+l} - 2A_{m-l}, \qquad [G_l, G_m] = 0.$$

Finite-dimensionality of the algebra generated by the transfer-matrix factors is the algebraic miracle that makes diagonalization possible; it reduces the $2^M \times 2^M$ eigenvalue problem to a set of $2 \times 2$ problems. The resulting free energy is the celebrated double integral [1]:

$$-\beta f = \ln 2 + \frac{1}{8\pi^2}\int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^{2\pi} d\theta_1\, d\theta_2 \,\ln\!\Big[\cosh 2K_1 \cosh 2K_2 - \sinh 2K_1 \cos\theta_1 - \sinh 2K_2 \cos\theta_2\Big],$$

for anisotropic couplings $K_1, K_2$. The argument of the logarithm vanishes when $\sinh(2K_1)\sinh(2K_2) = 1$, confirming the Kramers–Wannier condition as the locus of the singularity and establishing $T_c = 2J/(k_B\ln(1+\sqrt{2}))$ in the isotropic case.

### Kaufman's fermionization

Kaufman [3] replaced Onsager's Lie-algebra machinery with something more powerful and more transparent: she introduced anticommuting spinor operators $\Gamma_j$ ($\Gamma_j \Gamma_k + \Gamma_k \Gamma_j = 2\delta_{jk}$) via a Jordan–Wigner transformation of the row spins, and showed the transfer matrix is the exponential of a *quadratic* form in these fermions. Quadratic fermionic Hamiltonians diagonalize by Fourier transform into independent modes with single-particle energies $\gamma_k$; the many-body eigenvalues are sums $\sum_k \pm \tfrac{1}{2}\gamma_k$. Baxter's historical reconstruction of the Onsager–Kaufman magnetization calculation [6] emphasizes how this framework extends far beyond the free energy: Kaufman and Onsager used it in 1949 to compute two-spin correlations, and the same fermionization underlies the complete correlation-function theory of McCoy and Wu [5]. The physical content is profound: *the two-dimensional Ising model at zero field is equivalent to free Majorana fermions*.

### The star-triangle relation

A complementary integrability structure is the star-triangle (Yang–Baxter) relation, which the Ising Boltzmann weights satisfy. As developed systematically by Baxter [7], this local relation guarantees that transfer matrices with different spectral parameters commute, generating infinite families of conserved quantities. It is the reason the Ising model sits inside the exactly solvable landscape alongside the six-vertex and eight-vertex models, and it explains why solvability survives the introduction of anisotropy ($J_1 \neq J_2$) while a magnetic field destroys it.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The specific heat and the logarithmic singularity

Differentiating Onsager's free energy twice with respect to temperature yields the specific heat. Near $T_c$ it behaves as

$$C(T) \sim -A \ln\!\left|1 - \frac{T}{T_c}\right| + \text{regular},$$

a *logarithmic* divergence. In the language of critical exponents, $C \sim |t|^{-\alpha}$ with $t = (T-T_c)/T_c$, this corresponds to $\alpha = 0$ with a logarithmic enhancement — often written "$\alpha = 0$ (log)". This was shocking in 1944: mean-field theory predicts a finite jump, and no one had imagined a logarithmic singularity. It is the first exact fingerprint of the Ising universality class.

### 4.2 Yang's spontaneous magnetization: $\beta = 1/8$

The order parameter required the heaviest machinery. Onsager announced the result in 1949; Yang published the derivation in 1952 [4], with the Onsager–Kaufman draft route reconstructed by Baxter [6]. For $T < T_c$,

$$m(T) = \left(1 - \sinh^{-4}(2K)\right)^{1/8}, \qquad m = 0 \;\; \text{for } T \geq T_c.$$

Expanding near $T_c$ gives $m \sim (T_c - T)^{1/8}$: the order-parameter exponent is $\beta = 1/8$. The exponent $1/8$ is extraordinarily small — the magnetization rises with infinite slope at $T_c$, far steeper than the mean-field $\beta = 1/2$.

### 4.3 The complete exponent set and scaling relations

The full set of exactly known two-dimensional Ising exponents is:

| Exponent | Definition | Exact value | Origin |
|---|---|---|---|
| $\alpha$ | $C \sim \|t\|^{-\alpha}$ | $0$ (logarithmic) | Onsager free energy [1] |
| $\beta$ | $m \sim (-t)^{\beta}$ | $1/8$ | Yang magnetization [4] |
| $\gamma$ | $\chi \sim \|t\|^{-\gamma}$ | $7/4$ | Scaling / exact calculation |
| $\delta$ | $m \sim h^{1/\delta}$ at $t=0$ | $15$ | Scaling relations |
| $\nu$ | $\xi \sim \|t\|^{-\nu}$ | $1$ | Correlation length |
| $\eta$ | $G(r) \sim r^{-(d-2+\eta)}$ at $t=0$ | $1/4$ | Critical correlations |

These are not independent: they satisfy the scaling relations

$$\alpha + 2\beta + \gamma = 2, \qquad \gamma = \beta(\delta - 1), \qquad \alpha = 2 - d\nu, \qquad \gamma = (2-\eta)\nu,$$

which follow from the homogeneity (scaling) hypothesis for the singular free energy, $f_s(t, h) = |t|^{2-\alpha} \Phi(h/|t|^{\beta\delta})$ [12]. The hyperscaling relation $\alpha = 2 - d\nu$ holds in two dimensions (it fails above the upper critical dimension $d = 4$). The anomalous dimension $\eta = 1/4$ governs the critical correlator $\langle \sigma_0 \sigma_r \rangle \sim r^{-1/4}$, so slowly decaying that the susceptibility integral $\chi = \sum_r \langle \sigma_0 \sigma_r \rangle$ diverges strongly — hence $\gamma = 7/4$.

> **Theorem:** *The singular part of the free energy of the two-dimensional Ising model is a generalized homogeneous function of the reduced temperature and field, with exponents fixed by the exact values above; consequently all thermodynamic critical exponents are determined by any two of them via the scaling relations.*

### 4.4 Renormalization group and universality

The RG explanation of *why* these numbers are universal was Wilson's [11]. Coarse-graining the lattice (block spins) defines a flow on the space of Hamiltonians; the critical point is a fixed point of this flow, and critical exponents are eigenvalues of the linearized flow around it. Microscopic details — lattice type, spin magnitude, interaction range — are *irrelevant* directions that wash out under coarse-graining. Hence the square-lattice Ising model, the triangular-lattice Ising model, the liquid–gas critical point, and uniaxial ferromagnets all share the exponents above: they lie in the basin of attraction of the same fixed point, the *Ising universality class*, characterized by dimensionality $d = 2$ and $\mathbb{Z}_2$ symmetry breaking. The logarithmic specific heat and the exponent $\beta = 1/8$ were subsequently *measured* in real quasi-two-dimensional magnets and adsorbed monolayers, confirming universality as a physical principle rather than a mathematical artifact [12].

## 5 Empirical Results and Formal Arguments

### Conformal invariance at criticality: the $c = 1/2$ minimal model

Polyakov observed in 1970 that scale invariance at a critical point should enlarge to full conformal invariance [9]. In two dimensions this is spectacularly powerful, because the conformal algebra becomes the infinite-dimensional Virasoro algebra. Belavin, Polyakov, and Zamolodchikov [8] classified the *unitary minimal models* $\mathcal{M}(p, p+1)$ — CFTs with finitely many primary fields — and identified the critical Ising model as $\mathcal{M}(3,4)$, with central charge

$$c = 1 - \frac{6}{p(p+1)} = \frac{1}{2}.$$

The Kac table of $\mathcal{M}(3,4)$ contains exactly three primary fields:

| Field | $(r,s)$ | Conformal weight $h$ | Physical meaning |
|---|---|---|---|
| $\mathbb{1}$ | $(1,1)$ | $0$ | Identity |
| $\sigma$ | $(1,2)$ | $1/16$ | Spin / order parameter, $\Delta_\sigma = 1/8$ |
| $\varepsilon$ | $(2,1)$ | $1/2$ | Energy density, $\Delta_\varepsilon = 1$ |

The scaling dimensions reproduce the exact exponents: $\Delta_\sigma = \beta/\nu = 1/8$ and $\Delta_\varepsilon = d - 1/\nu = 1$, while $\eta = 2\Delta_\sigma = 1/4$ [8, 10]. Cardy [10] showed how conformal invariance fixes finite-size behavior — e.g., the free energy of a critical strip of width $L$ is $f(L) = f(\infty) - \pi c/(6L^2)$ — and numerical transfer-matrix studies confirmed $c = 1/2$ to high precision.

A minimal numerical check of the exact $T_c$ can be performed in a few lines of Python:

```python
import mpmath as mp

# Exact critical coupling from Kramers-Wannier self-duality: sinh(2*Kc) = 1
Kc = 0.5*mp.asinh(1)          # = 0.5*ln(1+sqrt(2))
Tc = 1/Kc                      # in units J/kB
print(f"Kc = {Kc:.10f}")       # 0.4406867935
print(f"Tc = {Tc:.10f} J/kB")  # 2.2691853142 J/kB

# Yang magnetization just below Tc
K = Kc*1.001
m = (1 - mp.sinh(2*K)**-4)**(1/8)
print(f"m(Tc/1.001) = {m:.6f}")  # steep rise: beta = 1/8

# Scaling-relation consistency of exact exponents
a,b,g,d,nu,eta = 0, 1/8, 7/4, 15, 1, 1/4
assert abs(a + 2*b + g - 2) < 1e-12
assert abs(g - b*(d-1)) < 1e-12
assert abs(a - (2 - 2*nu)) < 1e-12
assert abs(g - (2-eta)*nu) < 1e-12
print("All scaling relations satisfied.")
```

### The three-dimensional problem and the conformal bootstrap revival

No exact solution exists in three dimensions, and the exponents there are irrational numbers known only numerically. The modern attack descends directly from the 2D CFT story: the conformal bootstrap. Imposing crossing symmetry and unitarity on the four-point function $\langle \sigma\sigma\sigma\sigma \rangle$ carves out an allowed region in the space of CFT data; El-Showk, Paulos, Poland, Rychkov, Simmons-Duffin, and Vichi found that the 3D Ising CFT sits at a sharp *kink* on the boundary of this region [13], and that it minimizes the central charge among unitary solutions [14], yielding $\Delta_\sigma = 0.518154(15)$. The subsequent mixed-correlator and "precision island" analyses [15] pinned down

$$\Delta_\sigma = 0.5181489(10), \qquad \Delta_\varepsilon = 1.412625(10),$$

roughly a hundred times more precise than the best Monte Carlo determinations — the most precise critical exponents ever computed in a strongly coupled theory.

### Finite-size scaling

In any finite system the singularities are rounded: the correlation length cannot exceed the system size $L$. Finite-size scaling asserts that observables depend on the ratio $L/\xi$, e.g. the susceptibility peak grows as $\chi_{\max} \sim L^{\gamma/\nu}$ and the apparent transition temperature shifts as $T_c(L) - T_c(\infty) \sim L^{-1/\nu}$ [12]. For the 2D Ising model ($\gamma/\nu = 7/4$, $\nu = 1$) these predictions are exactly verifiable against the analytic solution, and they are the standard tool by which simulations on finite lattices extrapolate to the thermodynamic limit and confirm the exact exponents numerically.

## 6 Limitations

The exact theory has sharp boundaries, and they are instructive.

1. **Zero field only.** Every exact result above — Onsager's free energy [1], Kaufman's fermionization [3], Yang's magnetization [4] — requires $h = 0$. The two-dimensional Ising model in a magnetic field has no known exact solution; its field theory (the magnetic perturbation of the $c=1/2$ CFT) is integrable in the Zamolodchikov sense but the lattice model is not solved.
2. **No three-dimensional solution.** Despite eight decades of effort, the 3D Ising model resists exact methods; the transfer matrix no longer generates a finite-dimensional algebra, the star-triangle relation has no 3D analogue for these weights, and fermionization produces interacting fermions. The bootstrap [13, 14, 15] is numerical, not analytic.
3. **Ferromagnetic restriction.** The exact solution covers ferromagnetic couplings (and the antiferromagnet on bipartite lattices, by sublattice spin-flip). Frustrated or random-bond Ising models are not solvable by these methods.
4. **Equilibrium only.** The solution describes the Gibbs equilibrium state; non-equilibrium critical dynamics (Model A dynamical exponent $z \approx 2.17$ in 2D) are not fixed by the static solution.
5. **Thermodynamic limit.** Singularities exist only as $N \to \infty$; finite systems are addressed through finite-size scaling [10, 12].

## 7 Conclusion

The two-dimensional Ising model occupies a unique position in theoretical physics: it is the simplest interacting system with a continuous phase transition, and it is exactly solved. From the one-dimensional transfer matrix we learn *why* one dimension cannot order; from Kramers–Wannier duality [2] we learn that symmetry alone can locate $T_c = 2J/(k_B\ln(1+\sqrt{2}))$; from Onsager [1] and Kaufman [3] we learn that the transfer matrix hides a free-fermion theory; from Yang [4] we learn that the order parameter vanishes as $(T_c - T)^{1/8}$; and from the exponent set $\{\alpha=0, \beta=1/8, \gamma=7/4, \delta=15, \nu=1, \eta=1/4\}$ we learn the scaling relations that the renormalization group [11] later explained as consequences of a fixed point with $\mathbb{Z}_2$ symmetry.

The deepest legacy is conceptual. The critical point's emergent conformal symmetry [9], encoded in the $c=1/2$ minimal model [8] and tested through finite-size predictions [10], became the template for all two-dimensional critical phenomena — and, three decades later, the seed of the conformal bootstrap that is now determining, to extraordinary precision, the three-dimensional Ising exponents that exact methods cannot touch [13, 14, 15].
