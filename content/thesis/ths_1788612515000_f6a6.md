# Continuous-Depth Latent Models: Adjoint Sensitivity, Stiff Solvers, and Liquid Time-Constant Networks for Irregularly-Sampled Dynamics

## Abstract

Deep neural networks are conventionally compositions of discrete layers, yet an influential line of work treats depth as a continuous quantity governed by a parameterized ordinary differential equation. This thesis unifies the theory of continuous-depth latent models. We begin with **Neural Ordinary Differential Equations**, in which a hidden state evolves under a learned vector field $dh/dt = f(h, t, 	heta)$ and the output comes from a black-box ODE solver [1]. We develop the **adjoint sensitivity method**, which yields constant-memory gradients by solving a second ODE backward in time [1, 5], and we analyze **stiff ODEs** and **adaptive step-size control**, the numerics that determine whether these models train at all. We extend the framework to **irregularly-sampled time series** through Latent ODEs, ODE-RNNs, and Neural Controlled Differential Equations [2, 4, 6], and close with **Liquid Time-Constant Networks**, continuous-time recurrent models whose state-dependent time constants give stable, bounded, and provably expressive dynamics [3]. Throughout we state precise theorems, give derivations, provide runnable code, and survey the empirical evidence.

## 1. Introduction

The dominant architectural paradigm of deep learning builds complex transformations by *composing a sequence of transformations* applied to a hidden state:

$$
\mathbf{h}_{t+1} = \mathbf{h}_t + f(\mathbf{h}_t, \boldsymbol{\theta}_t), \qquad t \in \{0, \dots, T\}.
$$

Residual networks, recurrent decoders, and normalizing flows all fit this template. Chen et al. [1] observed that these iterative updates are formally an **Euler discretization of a continuous transformation**, and asked the natural limiting question: *what happens as we add more layers and take smaller steps?* In the limit, one parameterizes the continuous dynamics of hidden units with an ODE specified by a neural network:

$$
\frac{d\mathbf{h}(t)}{dt} = f(\mathbf{h}(t), t, \boldsymbol{\theta}). \tag{1}
$$

The network's output is computed by handing the learned vector field to a black-box differential equation solver — a perspective shift with four profound consequences. First, continuous-depth models have **constant memory cost** during training, regardless of effective depth. Second, they **adapt their evaluation strategy** to each input, spending more function evaluations on harder examples. Third, they **trade numerical precision for speed** via solver tolerances. Fourth, they provide a principled foundation for **continuous-time latent variable models** of irregularly-sampled data, the setting where standard RNNs — which assume uniformly spaced observations — break down [2].

This thesis develops the mathematical machinery and engineering practice behind these models. In §2 we review the continuous-limit viewpoint and its precursors. In §3 we present the methodology: the adjoint sensitivity method, stiff and adaptive solvers, latent-variable constructions, and liquid time-constant dynamics. Section §4 is a four-part deep dive into each component. Section §5 presents empirical evidence and formal proofs; §6 discusses limitations; §7 concludes.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 From residual blocks to continuous transformations

The identification of ResNets with discretized ODEs rests on elementary observations. The residual update $\mathbf{h}_{t+1} = \mathbf{h}_t + f(\mathbf{h}_t, \boldsymbol{\theta}_t)$ with unit step size is exactly one explicit Euler step of the vector field $f$. Lu et al. [2017] and Haber & Ruthotto [2017] independently formalized this connection, showing that weight-tied residual networks correspond to Euler integration of a *stationary* vector field. Once depth is seen as a discretization parameter, continuous depth becomes a modeling choice rather than a fixed architectural integer.

### 2.2 The original Neural ODE construction

Chen et al. [1] define a neural ODE as the initial value problem

$$
\mathbf{h}(0) = \mathbf{h}_{\text{in}}, \qquad \frac{d\mathbf{h}(t)}{dt} = f(\mathbf{h}(t), t, \boldsymbol{\theta}), \qquad \mathbf{h}_{\text{out}} = \mathbf{h}(T),
$$

where $f$ is a neural network (in the paper, a small MLP or convolutional residual function), $t$ is concatenated as an extra input channel to preserve time-inhomogeneity, and $\mathbf{h}(T)$ is obtained with any ODE solver. Two consequences distinguish this from weight-tied ResNets: the *number of function evaluations* (NFE) is decided adaptively by the solver's error controller, and backpropagation does not unroll through the solver's steps at all.

### 2.3 Continuous normalizing flows

Applying the same idea to normalizing flows yields *continuous normalizing flows* (CNFs) [1]. The standard change-of-variables formula for a discrete bijection requires $\log \det(\partial g / \partial \mathbf{z})$, an $\mathcal{O}(D^3)$ computation. The continuous analogue — the **instantaneous change-of-variables theorem** — states:

> **Theorem:** Let $\mathbf{z}(t)$ solve $d\mathbf{z}/dt = f(\mathbf{z}(t), t)$ with log-density $p(\mathbf{z}(t))$. Then
>
> $$
> \frac{\partial \log p(\mathbf{z}(t))}{\partial t} = -\operatorname{tr}\!\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial \mathbf{z}(t)}\right).
> $$

The trace costs only $\mathcal{O}(D)$, and Hutchinson's stochastic trace estimator reduces it further — enabling maximum-likelihood training of flows without partitioning or ordering the data dimensions [1].

### 2.4 Irregular sampling: the failure mode of discrete time

Standard RNNs update hidden states only at observation times and have no notion of the *gap* between observations. The common remedy — binning the timeline into uniform intervals and imputing missing values — destroys timing information that is itself informative (e.g., measurement times in clinical data correlate with patient acuity [Lipton et al., 2016; Che et al., 2018]). Continuous-time models instead define latent states *at all times*, handling arbitrary gaps by construction [2, 4].

## 3. Methodology

### 3.1 The adjoint sensitivity method

The central methodological question of [1] is: *how does one compute gradients through an ODE solver without storing its internal trajectory?* Naively backpropagating through every solver step costs memory proportional to the number of steps and requires differentiating the solver's internal operations. The **adjoint sensitivity method** treats the solver as a black box. Let $L$ be a scalar loss depending on $\mathbf{z}(t_1)$, where $\mathbf{z}$ solves $d\mathbf{z}/dt = f(\mathbf{z}, t, \boldsymbol{\theta})$. Define the **adjoint state**

$$
\mathbf{a}(t) = \frac{\partial L}{\partial \mathbf{z}(t)}.
$$

Then the adjoint satisfies its own ODE *backward* in time:

$$
\frac{d\mathbf{a}(t)}{dt} = -\,\mathbf{a}(t)^\top \frac{\partial f(\mathbf{z}(t), t, \boldsymbol{\theta})}{\partial \mathbf{z}}, \tag{2}
$$

with terminal condition $\mathbf{a}(t_1) = \partial L / \partial \mathbf{z}(t_1)$. Gradients with respect to parameters and the initial state follow from integrating the augmented system:

$$
\frac{dL}{d\boldsymbol{\theta}} = -\int_{t_1}^{t_0} \mathbf{a}(t)^\top \frac{\partial f(\mathbf{z}(t), t, \boldsymbol{\theta})}{\partial \boldsymbol{\theta}}\, dt, \qquad \frac{dL}{d\mathbf{z}(t_0)} = \mathbf{a}(t_0). \tag{3}
$$

The procedure is: (i) solve the forward ODE and cache only $\mathbf{z}(t_0)$ and $\mathbf{z}(t_1)$; (ii) solve the augmented backward system (state, adjoint, parameter gradients) from $t_1$ to $t_0$. Because $\mathbf{z}(t)$ is *recomputed* on the backward pass rather than stored, memory cost is **$\mathcal{O}(1)$** in the number of solver steps [1].

### 3.2 Stiffness and adaptive step-size control

Adaptive solvers such as Dormand–Prince (dopri5) propose a step, estimate the local error, and accept or shrink the step according to user-specified absolute and relative tolerances. A decisive practical concern is **stiffness**: a system is stiff when the solver is forced to take tiny steps *not* because the solution varies rapidly, but because the Jacobian $\partial f / \partial \mathbf{z}$ has eigenvalues with large negative real parts, forcing explicit methods toward instability [Hairer & Wanner, 1996]. Neural vector fields can and do become stiff during training, at which point dopri5's NFE explodes. The standard remedies are:

| Strategy | Mechanism | Cost |
|---|---|---|
| Tolerances (rtol/atol) | Looser tolerances accept larger steps | Accuracy of the gradient estimate |
| Regularizing NFE | Penalty on function evaluations encourages smooth fields | Extra loss term |
| Implicit solvers (e.g. Radau, BDF) | A-stable updates tolerate stiff Jacobians | Per-step linear solve, $\mathcal{O}(D^3)$ naively |
| Adjoint seminorms [5] | Norm adapted to adjoint structure rejects fewer steps | Minor code change |

### 3.3 Latent ODEs for irregularly-sampled series

Rubanova et al. [2] build a **continuous-time latent variable model** for sequences observed at arbitrary times $\{t_i, \mathbf{x}_i\}$. An ODE-RNN encoder processes observations *backward* in time, producing a posterior $q(\mathbf{z}_{t_0} \mid \{\mathbf{x}_i, t_i\})$ over the initial latent state. The latent trajectory is then defined everywhere by

$$
\mathbf{z}(t) = \mathbf{z}_{t_0} + \int_{t_0}^{t} f(\mathbf{z}(s), s, \boldsymbol{\theta})\, ds, \tag{4}
$$

and observations are reconstructed from $\mathbf{z}(t_i)$ by a decoder. Crucially, the ODE-RNN encoder itself has continuous hidden dynamics *between* observations — hidden states evolve by $d\mathbf{h}/dt = f(\mathbf{h}, t, \boldsymbol{\theta})$ and jump via a standard GRU cell *at* observation times — so it generalizes RNNs to continuous time and can additionally model the distribution over observation times via a Poisson process [2].

### 3.4 Liquid time-constant networks

Hasani et al. [3] introduce a distinct class of continuous-time models: networks of linear first-order systems modulated by nonlinear interlinked gates,

$$
\frac{d\mathbf{x}(t)}{dt} = -\left[\frac{1}{\boldsymbol{\tau}} + f(\mathbf{x}(t), \mathbf{I}(t), t, \boldsymbol{\theta})\right] \odot \mathbf{x}(t) + f(\mathbf{x}(t), \mathbf{I}(t), t, \boldsymbol{\theta}) \odot \mathbf{A}, \tag{5}
$$

where $\boldsymbol{\tau}$ is a base time-constant vector, $\mathbf{I}(t)$ the input, and $\mathbf{A}$ a bias vector. The effective time constant $\boldsymbol{\tau}_{\text{sys}} = 1/(1/\boldsymbol{\tau} + f(\cdot))$ is *state- and input-dependent* — hence *liquid*. LTCs are shown to be stable, bounded, and strictly more expressive (by trajectory-length measures) than fixed–time-constant continuous-time RNNs and standard Neural ODEs within the same parameter budget [3].

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 The adjoint method, in full detail

Let us derive (2)–(3). Introduce a Lagrange multiplier $\mathbf{a}(t)$ and consider the Lagrangian

$$
\mathcal{L} = L(\mathbf{z}(t_1)) + \int_{t_0}^{t_1} \mathbf{a}(t)^\top \left( \dot{\mathbf{z}}(t) - f(\mathbf{z}(t), t, \boldsymbol{\theta}) \right) dt.
$$

Integrating the $\mathbf{a}^\top \dot{\mathbf{z}}$ term by parts and requiring stationarity of $\mathcal{L}$ with respect to variations of $\mathbf{z}(t)$ yields the adjoint ODE (2) together with the terminal condition $\mathbf{a}(t_1) = \partial L / \partial \mathbf{z}(t_1)$. Stationarity with respect to $\boldsymbol{\theta}$ then gives (3). Two subtleties matter in practice:

- **The adjoint dynamics are linear in $\mathbf{a}$** but inherit the *transpose* Jacobian-vector products of $f$, which automatic differentiation computes cheaply as vector-Jacobian products (VJPs) — this is why the backward pass composes well with deep learning frameworks [7].
- **Reversibility is numerical, not exact.** The backward pass recomputes $\mathbf{z}(t)$ by integrating *backward*; for stiff or chaotic systems the recomputed trajectory can diverge from the forward one, corrupting gradients. Checkpointing (storing a few intermediate states) trades a little memory for fidelity.

> **Theorem:** *(Adjoint sensitivity, Chen et al. [1].)* For the IVP in §3.1, the gradient $dL/d\boldsymbol{\theta}$ can be computed by solving a single augmented ODE backward in time with **memory cost independent of the number of forward solver steps**.

Kidger, Chen & Lyons [5] sharpen the method itself. The adjoint solve uses an adaptive solver whose step acceptance depends on an error estimate *relative to a norm* — conventionally $L^2$. Because the augmented state couples quantities of wildly different scales (state, adjoint, and parameter adjoints), the $L^2$ norm is unnecessarily stringent: steps are rejected that were in fact accurate where it matters. Replacing it with a carefully chosen **seminorm** yields a median **40% reduction in function evaluations** — roughly halving training time — across time-series, generative, and physical-control tasks [5].

```python
# Adjoint backward pass with torchdiffeq [7]
from torchdiffeq import odeint_adjoint

class ODEFunc(torch.nn.Module):
    def forward(self, t, z):
        return self.net(torch.cat([z, t.expand_as(z[:, :1])], dim=1))

zT = odeint_adjoint(ODEFunc(), z0, t_span, method='dopri5',
                    rtol=1e-4, atol=1e-6)   # O(1) memory gradients
```

### 4.2 Stiffness, tolerances, and the solver as hyperparameter

In continuous-depth models the ODE solver is not infrastructure — it is part of the model's *inductive bias*. The Dormand–Prince 5(4) pair embedded in dopri5 estimates local error via the difference between 5th- and 4th-order updates; if the error is below $\text{atol} + \text{rtol}\|z\|$, the step is accepted, else it is shrunk by a PI controller. Consequences for practitioners:

1. **Tolerances trade accuracy for compute.** Typical training uses `rtol=1e-3, atol=1e-4`; tightening them increases NFE superlinearly on stiff fields.
2. **Smooth activations help.** ReLU-based vector fields have discontinuous Jacobians that force small steps; ELU, softplus, and tanh fields train markedly faster.
3. **Stiffness is emergent.** Early in training fields are smooth, but learned fields can develop fast-decaying modes that explicit solvers resolve expensively. Implicit solvers (Radau IIA) handle stiffness at the cost of per-step Newton iterations and Jacobian factorization — viable in moderate dimensions, but rarely used at neural scale [1].

Empirically, [1] reports NFE growing with problem difficulty: MNIST models used tens of evaluations per forward pass, while CNF density estimation required hundreds — the solver's *adaptive computation* manifesting directly.

```julia
# Stiff vs non-stiff solves in Julia's DifferentialEquations.jl
using DifferentialEquations
f_stiff(u,p,t) = -1000.0u .+ 3000.0*(0.5 .- u.^2) .* sin.(t)
prob = ODEProblem(f_stiff, u0, (0.0, 1.0))
sol1 = solve(prob, Tsit5())        # explicit: thousands of steps
sol2 = solve(prob, Rodas5())       # Rosenbrock (implicit): a few dozen
```

### 4.3 Latent ODEs, ODE-RNNs, and neural CDEs for irregular data

The latent-ODE pipeline of [2] has three moving parts: (i) an **ODE-RNN encoder** running backward over the observation sequence, (ii) a **generative ODE** defining the prior dynamics, and (iii) a **decoder** mapping $\mathbf{z}(t_i)$ to observations. Training maximizes an ELBO with KL penalty between the approximate posterior over $\mathbf{z}_{t_0}$ and the prior. The Poisson-process extension adds a term modeling *when* observations occur, so the model learns that measurement timing is informative — a key advantage over imputation baselines on clinical datasets like PhysioNet [2].

Kidger et al. [4] identify a fundamental limitation of this scheme: *the solution of an ODE is determined by its initial condition*, so there is no mechanism for later observations to steer the trajectory. Their fix is the **Neural Controlled Differential Equation**

$$
\mathbf{z}(t) = \mathbf{z}(0) + \int_0^t f(\mathbf{z}(s), s, \boldsymbol{\theta}_f)\, dX(s), \tag{6}
$$

where the integral is Riemann–Stieltjes against a *control path* $X(t)$ — typically a natural cubic spline interpolating the observations. Incoming data now continuously drives the hidden state, the model is the continuous-time limit of an RNN (both GRUs and LSTMs are shown to discretize CDEs), and — unlike latent ODEs — it supports **adjoint-based backpropagation even across observation times**. Kidger et al. prove **universal approximation** for continuous paths and show Neural CDEs *subsume* alternative ODE models [4]. De Brouwer et al. [6] offer a complementary construction, GRU-ODE-Bayes, which fuses a GRU cell with a continuous ODE flow for Bayesian filtering of sporadic observations, demonstrating strong mortality prediction on MIMIC-III.

| Model | Handles gaps | Posterior over $z_0$ | Adjoints across obs. | Observation-time model |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Latent ODE [2] | ✔ | ✔ | ✘ (restart per obs.) | ✔ Poisson process |
| ODE-RNN [2] | ✔ | — | ✘ | ✔ |
| GRU-ODE-Bayes [6] | ✔ | ✔ (filtering) | ✘ | ✘ |
| Neural CDE [4] | ✔ | — (direct) | ✔ | ✘ |

### 4.4 Liquid time-constant networks: theory of liquid dynamics

LTCs [3] are motivated by the nervous systems of small species (notably *C. elegans*), where synaptic transmission makes each neuron's effective time constant depend on presynaptic activity. Equation (5) can be rearranged to expose the liquid mechanism:

$$
\frac{d\mathbf{x}}{dt} = -\frac{1}{\boldsymbol{\tau}_{\text{sys}}(t)}\odot\mathbf{x}(t) + \text{driving terms}, \qquad \boldsymbol{\tau}_{\text{sys}} = \frac{1}{1/\boldsymbol{\tau} + f(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{I}, t, \boldsymbol{\theta})}.
$$

Hasani et al. prove three foundational results [3]:

> **Theorem:** *(Boundedness.)* For bounded inputs $\mathbf{I}(t)$ and any initialization, LTC hidden states remain in a compact set determined by $\mathbf{A}$; the system cannot diverge.
>
> **Theorem:** *(Universal approximation.)* Any finite trajectory of an $n$-dimensional continuous dynamical system can be approximated by the internal state of an LTC network's hidden units plus $n$ output units.
>
> **Theorem:** *(Expressivity.)* By the *trajectory length* measure — expected arc length of latent trajectories under input perturbations — LTCs realize strictly longer, more expressive trajectories than fixed–time-constant CTRNNs and Neural ODEs with equal parameter counts.

The boundedness theorem is practically significant: whereas vanilla Neural ODEs can learn vector fields that blow up (causing solver failures mid-training), LTCs are *structurally* prevented from doing so. Subsequent work (notably Neural Circuit Policies and closed-form continuous-time models) built solver-free approximations of (5) — but the LTC formulation remains the cleanest theoretical statement of *why* input-dependent time constants help.

## 5. Empirical Results / Proofs

**MNIST and density estimation [1].** Neural ODEs match ResNet-6 accuracy on MNIST with substantially fewer parameters in community reproductions, and continuous normalizing flows trained by maximum likelihood achieve competitive bits-per-dimension on standard tabular and image benchmarks — with NFE adapting to input difficulty.

**Irregular time series [2, 4, 6].** On the PhysioNet 2012 challenge (mortality prediction from irregularly-sampled ICU data), Latent ODEs outperform GRU-D and other imputation-based RNNs; on Human Activity recognition, latent-ODE classifiers beat RNN baselines; Neural CDEs achieve state-of-the-art against ODE/RNN competitors across CharacterTrajectories, PhysioNet sepsis, and MuJoCo forecasting tasks [4]. GRU-ODE-Bayes reports competitive AUROC on MIMIC-III mortality [6].

**Adjoint efficiency [5].** The seminorm trick reduces backward-pass NFE by a median of 40% and up to 62% — directly halving wall-clock training time on several benchmarks, with only about a dozen lines of code changed.

**LTC time-series benchmarks [3].** On seven real-world datasets (including person-activity and room-occupancy prediction), LTCs outperform LSTMs, GRUs, ODE-RNNs, and CT-RNNs — often with an order of magnitude fewer neurons — and the trajectory-length analysis confirms the expressivity theorems empirically.

**Proof sketch — adjoint equations.** The derivation in §4.1 is a standard application of the Lagrange formalism; the key step is recognizing that stationarity of $\mathcal{L}$ w.r.t. $\mathbf{z}(t)$ for $t \in (t_0, t_1)$ forces the adjoint ODE, while boundary terms fix $\mathbf{a}(t_1)$. The $\mathcal{O}(1)$ memory claim follows because the backward solve recomputes $\mathbf{z}(t)$ on demand rather than caching the forward trajectory [1].

## 6. Limitations

1. **Solver cost and stiffness.** Adaptive solves are serial; NFE can balloon on stiff or chaotic fields, making wall-clock time unpredictable and sometimes worse than a fixed-depth ResNet. Implicit solvers help but scale poorly in high dimensions.
2. **Numerical gradient fidelity.** The backward pass recomputes the forward trajectory, and any divergence (common in stiff systems) silently corrupts gradients. Practitioners should monitor forward/backward state agreement.
3. **ODEs cannot represent all functions.** Vanilla Neural ODEs preserve topology — trajectories cannot cross — so they cannot represent, e.g., the map $x \mapsto -x$ in one dimension without augmentation [Dupont et al., 2019]. Augmented Neural ODEs lift the state space to recover universality.
4. **Irregular-series models still interpolate.** Neural CDEs depend on the choice of spline interpolant $X(t)$; causally-invalid (future-leaking) interpolation can inflate offline metrics, and the online variant (rectilinear control) is less expressive.
5. **LTC theory vs. practice.** The boundedness and expressivity theorems assume idealized conditions; training LTCs still requires careful solver tolerances, and closed-form successors trade exactness for speed.
6. **Benchmark fragility.** Several reported advantages are sensitive to preprocessing (imputation baselines, quantization of PhysioNet) and to solver-tolerance tuning, which is rarely ablated with the same rigor as architecture.

---

## 7. Conclusion

Continuous-depth latent models replace the discrete architectural integer of "number of layers" with the analytic machinery of differential equations. The **adjoint sensitivity method** makes this practical by rendering backpropagation memory-constant [1, 5]; **adaptive and stiff-aware solvers** make it efficient by spending computation where the vector field demands it; **Latent ODEs, ODE-RNNs, and Neural CDEs** extend the framework to the irregularly-sampled regime where discrete RNNs fail [2, 4, 6]; and **Liquid Time-Constant Networks** show that making time constants themselves a function of state yields provably stable, bounded, and more expressive continuous-time computation [3]. Together these ideas form a coherent research program: *depth as time*, trained by adjoints, evaluated by solvers, and deployed on data whose timestamps are as informative as its values. The open frontier — stiff neural fields at scale, topology-preserving expressivity, and solver-free inference — will determine whether continuous depth remains a specialized tool or becomes the default substrate of sequence modeling.
