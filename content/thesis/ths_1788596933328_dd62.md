# Coverage-Guided Hardware Fuzzing for RTL Designs: Mutational Stimulus Generation, Assertion-Driven Oracles, and Coverage Closure in TheHuzz, RFuzz, and Processor Fuzzers

## Abstract

Pre-silicon functional verification remains the dominant cost center of chip development, with most ASIC projects reporting bugs that escape to silicon and demand costly re-spins. Coverage-guided hardware fuzzing has emerged as a scalable complement to formal verification and constrained-random testing, adapting the mutational feedback loops of software fuzzers to the register-transfer level (RTL). This thesis develops a unified treatment of the field through three representative systems: **RFuzz**, which introduced FIRRTL-based instrumentation and FPGA-accelerated mux-toggle coverage; **DifuzzRTL**, which pioneered differential testing of RTL against ISA simulators with register coverage; and **TheHuzz**, which fuzzes processors at the ISA level using golden-reference models and six industrial coverage metrics. We formalize mutational stimulus generation for instruction streams, analyze assertion-driven and differential oracles, and evaluate the metrics — line, branch, toggle, FSM, expression, condition — that capture hardware-native behaviors invisible to software metrics. Drawing on empirical results — 11 bugs from TheHuzz, 17 bugs and 13 CVEs from MorFuzz, 278× convergence speedups from FPGA-native fuzzing — we characterize the path to *coverage closure* and the fundamental limits of the approach.

## 1. Introduction

The economics of hardware correctness are unforgiving. Unlike software, where a defective release can be patched over the network, a functional bug baked into silicon can only be corrected by a *re-spin* — a new tape-out costing tens of millions of dollars and months of schedule. Industry surveys have consistently reported that a majority of ASIC and FPGA projects discover functional flaws after fabrication, and that verification already consumes the largest share of engineering effort in chip programs [6]. The central technical challenge is one of scale: a modern out-of-order processor contains billions of transistors and an effectively infinite reachable state space, while the verification budget — engineer-hours, simulation cycles, and formal-verification compute — is finite.

Classical verification relies on two pillars. *Constrained-random testing* (CRT) generates stimulus from hand-written generators biased by verification engineers, and measures progress with functional and code coverage. *Formal verification* proves properties exhaustively but suffers from state-space explosion; in a head-to-head comparison reported alongside TheHuzz, the industrial formal tool Cadence JasperGold detected only a fraction of the bugs found by fuzzing, and required manually specified security properties that do not scale to large designs [1]. Information-flow tracking offers another formal route but demands manual label assignment and is prone to label pollution and false positives [1].

Coverage-guided fuzzing, the dominant paradigm in software vulnerability discovery since AFL, promises a third way: *automated, feedback-driven stimulus generation* that requires minimal manual setup and explores the design's state space guided by measured coverage. Translating this idea to hardware, however, confronts four structural obstacles:

1. **Hardware does not crash.** Software fuzzers rely on crashes, hangs, and sanitizer aborts as cheap oracles. An RTL design under simulation simply continues executing; functional incorrectness is silent unless an oracle observes it.
2. **The input abstraction is unclear.** A processor's natural input is an instruction stream with rich grammar, not an unstructured byte buffer. Naive bit-level mutation produces overwhelmingly illegal instructions.
3. **Simulation is slow.** RTL simulators execute at kilohertz to megahertz rates — six to nine orders of magnitude slower than native software execution — making throughput the binding constraint.
4. **Software coverage metrics are insufficient.** Edge coverage over a translated C++ model (e.g., Verilator output) misses hardware-native behaviors: signal toggles, floating wires, glitches in combinational logic, and FSM state/transition coverage.

This thesis argues that the last decade of hardware fuzzing research constitutes a coherent engineering discipline organized around three coupled problems: **(i)** mutational stimulus generation that respects instruction-set grammar, **(ii)** oracles that detect silent functional deviations, and **(iii)** coverage metrics and closure strategies that capture hardware semantics. We develop this argument through the lineage RFuzz → DifuzzRTL → TheHuzz and the subsequent generation of processor fuzzers (ProcessorFuzz, MorFuzz, TurboFuzz, GoldenFuzz), grounding each claim in published empirical results and grounding every factual assertion in primary sources.

The remainder of this thesis is organized as follows. Section 2 surveys the background and the evolution of hardware fuzzers. Section 3 presents the reference methodology: the closed-loop fuzzing architecture. Section 4 provides a deep dive into stimulus generation, oracles, coverage metrics, and closure. Section 5 reports empirical results and a convergence argument. Section 6 discusses limitations, and Section 7 concludes.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 RFuzz: Coverage-Directed Fuzz Testing of RTL on FPGAs

RFuzz (Laeufer et al., ICCAD 2018) was the first system to frame RTL verification explicitly as *coverage-directed test generation* driven by software-fuzzing ideas [2]. Its key architectural insight was to operate not on Verilog or VHDL directly but on the **FIRRTL** intermediate representation, a compiler IR for hardware. By expressing instrumentation as FIRRTL compiler passes, RFuzz became *language-agnostic*: any HDL that lowers to FIRRTL can be instrumented uniformly.

RFuzz introduced three compiler passes of lasting influence. First, an instrumentation pass rewires signals through the module hierarchy and exposes *coverage pins* at the top level, appending **mux-toggle coverage** circuitry that is itself FPGA-synthesizable. Second, the **MetaReset** pass resets all registers to zero, enabling fast deterministic DUT reset without modifying design semantics. Third, the **SparseMem** pass tracks and resets only memory locations written during a test, avoiding the cost of full memory re-initialization [2]. A test-harness generator then wraps the instrumented DUT, and the fuzzer communicates with the harness — running either in software simulation or on an FPGA — through shared-memory buffers, using high-bandwidth DMA to tolerate CPU–FPGA round-trip latency. Input pins are concatenated into a bit vector mapped to bytes, and AFL-style mutation functions generate new stimuli. RFuzz demonstrated the approach on TileLink SPI/I2C peripherals from the SiFive Freedom SoC platform [2].

The limitations of RFuzz, as diagnosed by subsequent work, were twofold: its coverage metric (mux toggle) captured only a narrow slice of hardware behavior, and it reported no processor bugs in its evaluation [1][6].

### 2.2 DifuzzRTL: Register Coverage and Differential Testing

DifuzzRTL (Hur et al.) advanced the field along two axes. First, it introduced **register coverage** — tracking which control registers assume new values — as a hardware-meaningful feedback signal that correlates with exploration of the design's control state. Second, and more consequentially, it introduced **differential testing against ISA simulators**: the same instruction stream is executed on the RTL DUT and on a reference ISA simulator, and divergence indicates a bug. This solved the oracle problem for processors without requiring manually written assertions.

On the RISC-V Rocket core, DifuzzRTL's register coverage achieved up to **40×** the fuzzing efficiency of RFuzz's mux coverage, with only 4.2% simulation slowdown from instrumentation (versus 15.4% for RFuzz's mux coverage) [3]. The work established the template — *mutate instruction streams, compare against a golden model, feed back hardware coverage* — that all subsequent processor fuzzers follow.

### 2.3 TheHuzz: Industrial-Strength Instruction Fuzzing

TheHuzz (Kande et al., USENIX Security 2022) is the most complete realization of the processor-fuzzing paradigm to date [1]. Its contributions are architectural:

- **ISA-level input abstraction.** TheHuzz generates *processor instructions*, the natural input of a CPU, rather than raw signal bit-vectors. Each input comprises *configuration instructions* (CIs) that establish a bare-metal environment (stack, exception handlers, cleared state) and *test instructions* (TIs) that constitute the actual fuzzing payload — typically 20 TIs per seed [1].
- **Golden-reference-model oracles.** Every instruction's architectural effect (register and memory updates) is compared against a golden reference model (GRM): **Spike** for RISC-V targets and **or1ksim** for OpenRISC targets. Any mismatch is a candidate bug.
- **Six industrial coverage metrics.** Using the commercial simulator ModelSim, TheHuzz extracts *statement, branch, expression, toggle, FSM, and condition* coverage — the same metrics used in industrial sign-off flows — without modifying the DUT. A case study on a cache controller demonstrates that toggle and expression coverage detect bugs in combinational logic that line coverage misses entirely [1].
- **AFL-style mutation with opcode mutation.** Beyond bit-flips and arithmetic mutations, TheHuzz mutates *opcode bits* to synthesize out-of-spec and illegal instruction sequences, then retains only inputs that expand coverage.

Evaluated on four open-source processors (Ariane/CVA6, mor1kx, or1200, Rocket Core) with 10 repetitions on a 32-core Xeon platform, TheHuzz found **11 bugs, 8 of them previously unknown**, each mapped to a CWE (e.g., CWE-440, CWE-1202), and demonstrated two software-exploitable vulnerabilities: a FENCE.I cache-coherence flaw in Ariane and an EPCR privilege-escalation flaw in mor1kx [1]. It achieved **1.98×** the bug-finding rate of random regression testing and **3.33×** that of DifuzzRTL (Mann-Whitney U, *p* < 0.05), at the cost of 71% runtime overhead from multi-metric coverage collection [1].

### 2.4 The Second Generation: MorFuzz, TurboFuzz, GoldenFuzz, and Hybrids

**MorFuzz** (USENIX Security 2023) attacked the *input validity* problem: naive mutation wastes cycles on illegal instructions, while purely valid instructions never stress decoders. Its **runtime instruction morphing** uses microarchitectural runtime information to generate instruction streams that are simultaneously format-valid and semantically meaningful, coupled with a **synchronizable co-simulation** framework that eliminates false mismatches arising from GRM implementation differences. On CVA6, Rocket, and BOOM, MorFuzz discovered **17 new bugs with 13 assigned CVEs**, achieving 4.4× the state coverage of DifuzzRTL and 1.6× that of the constrained generator riscv-dv [4].

**TurboFuzz** moved the *entire* fuzzing loop — mutator, DUT execution, and coverage collection — into FPGA hardware, eliminating the host–FPGA communication bottleneck that limits DifuzzRTL-style offloading. It sustains **75.12 fuzzing iterations per second** (versus 4.13 Hz for FPGA-offloaded DifuzzRTL) and reaches 35,000 coverage points in 14 seconds, a **278×** speedup over the Cascade baseline, discovering two previously unknown issues in CVA6/BOOM [5].

**GoldenFuzz** replaces hand-built mutators with a *language model* that generates instruction blocks, refined first against the GRM and then guided by VCS-collected FSM, condition, and line coverage — evidence that generative models can serve as grammar-aware stimulus engines [8]. **HyPFuzz** hybridizes fuzzing with formal verification (JasperGold), directing the fuzzer with assertion cover properties [10], while **RtlFuzzLab/HwFuzzEnv** demonstrated that sketched coverage interfaces and simplified mutation engines alone yield **6.2×–21.5×** (average 12.7×) single-thread speedups, underscoring how much performance is lost to coverage-collection overhead rather than DUT simulation itself [7].

### 2.5 The Stakes: Zenbleed

The practical significance of hardware fuzzing was demonstrated outside academia in July 2023, when Google researcher Tavis Ormandy disclosed **Zenbleed (CVE-2023-20593)**, a data-leakage vulnerability in AMD Zen 2 processors discovered *by fuzzing hardware for flaws* [9]. Under specific microarchitectural conditions, a register was not zeroed correctly, allowing unprivileged code to exfiltrate data from other processes at **≥30 KB per core per second** — a Meltdown-class impact found not by formal methods but by automated stimulus exploration [9]. Zenbleed is the existence proof that motivates this thesis: silicon bugs with software exploitability are discoverable by fuzzing, and the methodology deserves the same rigorous treatment as its software counterpart.

## 3. Methodology

We now present the reference architecture shared — with variations — by all coverage-guided hardware fuzzers studied here. The system is a closed feedback loop with five stages:

1. **Seed corpus.** A set of initial inputs. For processor fuzzers, seeds are short instruction sequences (CIs + TIs); for peripheral fuzzers (RFuzz), seeds are byte vectors mapped onto input pins. Seeds may be hand-written, extracted from ISA test suites (e.g., the RISC-V tests repository), or generated by a seed generator.
2. **Mutational stimulus generation.** A mutator transforms corpus inputs into candidate inputs using a portfolio of operators (Section 4.1).
3. **DUT execution.** The candidate input is applied to the RTL design under test, either in a software simulator (Verilator, ModelSim, Synopsys VCS, Cadence Xcelium) or on an FPGA (RFuzz, DifuzzRTL, TurboFuzz).
4. **Coverage feedback.** Instrumentation records which coverage points the input exercised. Inputs that expand coverage are retained in the corpus; others are discarded. This is the *greybox* feedback that distinguishes fuzzing from blind random testing.
5. **Oracle checking and triage.** Each execution is checked against an oracle — assertions, a golden reference model, or both. Divergences are minimized, deduplicated, and confirmed as bugs.

The loop's dynamics are governed by three design decisions — *what to mutate, how to detect wrongness, and what to measure* — which we analyze in depth next.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Mutational Stimulus Generation for Instruction Streams

The mutator is the engine of exploration, and its design must resolve a fundamental tension: the input space of a processor is *grammatical* (instructions have opcodes, register fields, immediates with validity constraints), yet the most interesting bugs lurk at the grammar's boundaries — illegal instructions, reserved encodings, and privilege-level edge cases.

**Operator portfolio.** All surveyed fuzzers draw from an AFL-inspired operator set, extended with hardware-aware operators:

- *Bit-level operators:* single/multiple bit flips, byte flips, arithmetic increment/decrement on byte and word values, insertion of "interesting" constants (`0x0`, `0xFFFFFFFF`, sign boundaries).
- *Structural operators:* block deletion, block cloning, splicing (crossover) of two parent sequences.
- *Opcode mutation* (TheHuzz): targeted mutation of opcode and funct fields to synthesize *new instruction sequences* and out-of-spec encodings, deliberately probing decoder behavior on illegal inputs [1].
- *Instruction morphing* (MorFuzz): runtime-guided mutation that preserves format validity while varying semantics, using microarchitectural feedback (e.g., which pipeline stages were exercised) to bias generation toward meaningful instruction mixes [4].
- *Havoc and dictionary:* stacked random mutations plus dictionaries of ISA-specific tokens (valid opcodes, CSR addresses, exception vectors).

The following Python sketch illustrates the structure of a grammar-aware instruction mutator of the kind used in TheHuzz-style flows:

```python
import random

class InstructionMutator:
    """AFL-style mutator over a corpus of instruction sequences."""
    def __init__(self, opcode_dict, interesting_vals):
        self.opcode_dict = opcode_dict          # legal opcode/funct3/funct7 tuples
        self.interesting = interesting_vals     # e.g. [0, 1, -1, 0xFFF, 0x80000000]
        self.ops = [self.bit_flip, self.arith, self.splice,
                    self.opcode_mutate, self.interesting_insert]

    def bit_flip(self, seq):
        insn = random.choice(seq); pos = random.randrange(32)
        return [i ^ (1 << pos) if i is insn else i for i in seq]

    def opcode_mutate(self, seq):
        """Replace opcode bits -> possibly illegal/out-of-spec encoding."""
        idx = random.randrange(len(seq))
        new = list(seq); new[idx] = (new[idx] & 0xFFFFFF80) | random.getrandbits(7)
        return new

    def splice(self, seq, other):
        cut = random.randrange(len(seq))
        return seq[:cut] + other[cut:]

    def mutate(self, seq, corpus, rounds=8):
        for _ in range(rounds):
            op = random.choice(self.ops)
            seq = op(seq, random.choice(corpus)) if op == self.splice else op(seq)
        return seq
```

**Scheduling and corpus management.** As in software fuzzing, not all seeds deserve equal attention. Fuzzers employ *power schedules* that favor small, fast, high-coverage inputs, and *corpus minimization* that retains a minimal subset covering all observed coverage points. TheHuzz's optimization stage explicitly searches over (instruction, mutation-operator) pairs to maximize coverage per unit simulation time [1].

> **Theorem (Corpus monotonicity):** Let *C<sub>t</sub>* be the set of coverage points exercised by the corpus at iteration *t*, and let the retention rule keep an input iff it exercises at least one point outside *C<sub>t</sub>*. Then *C<sub>t</sub> ⊆ C<sub>t+1</sub>* for all *t*, and the sequence converges to a fixed point *C\** determined by the mutator's reachable set.
>
> *Proof sketch.* Retention only adds inputs whose coverage is not subsumed; discarding a non-expanding input cannot remove points already covered by retained inputs. Monotonicity follows directly; convergence follows because the coverage-point universe (for a fixed instrumentation) is finite. ∎

The theorem is elementary but clarifying: greybox fuzzing is *monotone coverage ascent* over a finite lattice, and its ceiling *C\** is set entirely by the mutator's expressiveness and the oracle's precision — which is why Sections 4.1–4.3, not raw iteration count, determine a fuzzer's ultimate effectiveness.

### 4.2 Assertion-Driven Oracles and Lockstep Differential Testing

Because RTL simulations do not crash, the oracle problem dominates hardware-fuzzing design. The literature exhibits three oracle families:

| Oracle family | Mechanism | Strengths | Weaknesses |
|---|---|---|---|
| **SystemVerilog Assertions (SVA)** | Temporal properties checked during simulation | Precise, zero golden-model requirement; integrates with formal flows (HyPFuzz) | Manual effort to write; only checks specified properties |
| **Golden-reference differential** | Lockstep comparison of DUT vs ISA simulator (Spike, or1ksim) | No manual properties; catches *any* architectural deviation | Requires a trusted GRM; false positives from timing/microarchitectural differences |
| **Self-consistency / metamorphic** | Invariants (e.g., determinism under reset) | No oracle model at all | Weak; misses most functional bugs |

**Assertion-driven oracles.** Industrial practice expresses intent as SVA properties. A fuzzer can treat every assertion as a bug oracle: any input sequence that fires an assertion is a reproducer. The following SystemVerilog assertion illustrates the style — a privilege-escalation guard of the kind relevant to the mor1kx EPCR bug found by TheHuzz [1]:

```systemverilog
// EPCR must never be writable from user mode
property epcr_priv_guard;
  @(posedge clk) disable iff (!rst_n)
  (csr_we && (csr_addr == `EPCR) && (priv_mode == USER))
  |-> ##1 $stable(epcr_value);
endproperty
assert_epcr_priv : assert property (epcr_priv_guard)
  else $error("EPCR modified from user mode");
```

HyPFuzz goes further, using *cover properties* as fuzzing targets: the fuzzer is rewarded for hitting assertion cover points, hybridizing formal intent with fuzzing throughput [10].

**Lockstep differential testing.** The dominant oracle for processor fuzzers compares the DUT's architectural state against the GRM after each instruction (or basic block). TheHuzz extracts per-instruction traces — the sequence of executed instructions plus register/memory updates — from ModelSim and diffs them against Spike [1]. Three engineering problems must be solved:

1. **Synchronization.** The DUT is a cycle-accurate microarchitecture; the GRM is a functional model. Comparison must occur at *architectural commit points*, not every cycle.
2. **Implementation differences.** The GRM and DUT may legitimately differ in unspecified behavior (e.g., reset values, performance counters). MorFuzz's *state synchronization* technique explicitly aligns such differences before comparison, eliminating a major false-positive source [4].
3. **Nondeterminism.** Uninitialized memories and X-propagation must be controlled (RFuzz's MetaReset/SparseMem passes serve exactly this purpose [2]) or the differential signal drowns in noise.

When these are handled, differential testing is remarkably powerful: it converts *every* ISA-visible behavior into a checked property, which is why TheHuzz and MorFuzz could find bugs no engineer had specified.

### 4.3 Coverage Metrics: Capturing Hardware-Native Behavior

The choice of coverage metric is the single most consequential design decision in a hardware fuzzer, because the metric defines the feedback landscape the mutator climbs. Software-derived edge coverage is inadequate: it cannot observe signal transitions, multi-bit conditions, or FSM state graphs. The following table compares the metrics employed across the surveyed fuzzers:

| Metric | What it measures | Hardware behaviors captured | Used by |
|---|---|---|---|
| Line / statement | RTL source lines executed | Basic activation | TheHuzz, GoldenFuzz |
| Branch | Taken/not-taken of control branches | Control-flow divergence | TheHuzz |
| Toggle | 0→1 and 1→0 transitions on signals | Floating wires, stuck-at faults, glitch sensitivity | TheHuzz, RFuzz (mux) |
| FSM | States visited, transitions taken | Protocol/decode state machines, deadlocks | TheHuzz, GoldenFuzz |
| Expression | Sub-expression outcomes in combinational logic | Combinational bugs invisible to line coverage | TheHuzz |
| Condition | All combinations of sub-conditions | Corner cases in complex guards | TheHuzz |
| Register | Control registers assuming new values | Control-state exploration | DifuzzRTL |
| ISA-transition / control-path register | Architectural state transitions | Processor-specific control behavior | ProcessorFuzz |

TheHuzz's cache-controller case study is the canonical demonstration of *why* metric diversity matters: a bug in combinational flush logic was invisible to statement and branch coverage but immediately exposed by toggle and expression coverage, because the faulty signal transitioned without any new line executing [1]. RFuzz's mux-toggle coverage was motivated identically — multiplexers are the fundamental control primitive of RTL, and toggling their select lines exercises the datapath steering that line coverage ignores [2].

Metric richness is not free. TheHuzz's six-metric collection via ModelSim imposed **71% runtime overhead**, versus 6.9% for DifuzzRTL's single register-coverage metric [1]. This overhead/precision trade-off is a first-order design parameter: TurboFuzz and HwFuzzEnv both attack it directly, the former by moving collection into hardware, the latter by replacing heavyweight simulator coverage APIs with sketched interfaces (462×–1053× faster coverage collection on small benchmarks [7]).

### 4.4 Coverage Closure: From Growth Curves to Sign-off

*Coverage closure* is the industrial term for the endgame of verification: demonstrating that the coverage curve has saturated and that remaining holes are either unreachable or waived by review. Hardware fuzzing contributes three mechanisms to closure:

1. **Saturation detection.** The monotone corpus property (Section 4.1) implies diminishing returns; fuzzers track the coverage growth curve and declare convergence when the marginal gain per unit simulation time falls below threshold. Empirically, mutational fuzzers exhibit steep early growth followed by long tails — the regime where directed techniques pay off.
2. **Directed and hybrid escalation.** For coverage holes the mutator cannot reach, HyPFuzz-style hybrids hand hard targets to formal engines (JasperGold cover properties), while MorFuzz-style runtime guidance reshapes the input distribution toward uncovered microarchitectural regions [4][10].
3. **Throughput scaling.** Because the coverage ceiling *C\** is fixed by the mutator, the practical question is how fast it is reached. The empirical record is striking: TurboFuzz reaches 35,000 coverage points in 14 seconds where Cascade needs 3,893 seconds (**278×**) and DifuzzRTL fails within five hours [5]; RtlFuzzLab's optimized coverage/mutation pipeline yields 6.2×–21.5× single-thread speedups [7].

Closure also demands *deduplication*: thousands of failing inputs typically map to a handful of root causes. Fuzzers cluster reproducers by coverage signature and minimize them (delta-debugging instruction sequences) before human triage — a step as important as finding the bug, since verification engineers, not fuzzers, sign off.

### 4.5 Triage, Confirmation, and Security Impact Assessment

A differential mismatch is a *candidate*, not a bug. The triage pipeline minimizes the reproducer, replays it deterministically (fast reset being essential — cf. MetaReset [2]), bisects against design revisions, and finally maps the root cause to a weakness taxonomy. TheHuzz's mapping of each finding to a CWE entry (e.g., CWE-440 *Expected Behavior Violation*, CWE-1202) and its two exploit demonstrations — the Ariane FENCE.I incoherence enabling cache-based attacks and the mor1kx EPCR flaw enabling privilege escalation — exemplify the standard: a hardware fuzzer's output is only as valuable as its *exploitability analysis* [1]. MorFuzz's 13 CVEs on CVA6, Rocket, and BOOM [4] and Zenbleed's real-world impact [9] confirm that this pipeline reaches all the way from coverage point to assigned CVE.

## 5. Empirical Results and Convergence Analysis

Table 2 consolidates the headline empirical results across the surveyed systems. All figures are as reported in the primary sources; speedups are relative to each paper's own baseline.

| Fuzzer | Venue | Throughput | Bugs / CVEs | Key speedup claim |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| RFuzz | ICCAD 2018 [2] | FPGA-accelerated | — (methodology) | First FPGA CDG framework |
| DifuzzRTL | — [3] | 2.29 MHz sim (4.2% overhead) | Multiple (incl. new) | 40× vs RFuzz on Rocket |
| TheHuzz | USENIX Sec 2022 [1] | ModelSim, 71% cov. overhead | **11 bugs (8 new)** | 1.98× vs random; 3.33× vs DifuzzRTL |
| MorFuzz | USENIX Sec 2023 [4] | Co-simulation | **17 new bugs, 13 CVEs** | 4.4× state cov. vs DifuzzRTL; 1.6× vs riscv-dv |
| TurboFuzz | arXiv 2025 [5] | **75.12 Hz** full-HW loop | 2 new (C9, C10) + all known | 278× coverage convergence vs Cascade |
| RtlFuzzLab | arXiv 2025 [7] | Single-thread optimized | — (infra) | 6.2×–21.5× (avg 12.7×) |
| Zenbleed (industrial) | CVE-2023-20593 [9] | — | 1 critical CPU bug | ≥30 KB/core/s exfiltration |

Three patterns emerge. **First**, oracle quality dominates: the jump from RFuzz (no bugs reported) to DifuzzRTL/TheHuzz/MorFuzz (dozens of bugs and CVEs) coincides exactly with the adoption of differential GRM oracles. **Second**, coverage-metric fidelity dominates guidance quality: toggle/expression/FSM coverage finds bug classes that line coverage cannot [1]. **Third**, throughput dominates time-to-closure once the first two are fixed: the 278× TurboFuzz result [5] and the 12.7× RtlFuzzLab result [7] show that coverage-collection and host-communication overheads, not DUT complexity, are the binding constraint in mature flows.

> **Theorem (Feedback dominance):** For a fixed mutator *M* and coverage universe *U*, let *C\**(M, U)* be the converged corpus coverage. If *U₁ ⊆ U₂* (metric set 2 subsumes metric set 1 in distinguishing power), then *C\**(M, U₁) ≤ C\**(M, U₂)* in bug-relevant distinguishing power, with strict inequality when *U₂ \ U₁* separates a buggy from a non-buggy behavior.
>
> *Proof sketch.* Every input retained under *U₁* is retained under *U₂* only if it expands *U₂*-coverage; the corpus under *U₂* is therefore a refinement that can distinguish strictly more behaviors. TheHuzz's toggle/expression case study [1] is the empirical witness: a behavior pair indistinguishable under line coverage is separated under toggle coverage. ∎

The practical reading: invest in *metrics and oracles* before *iterations*. A fuzzer running 10× longer with line coverage alone will not find the bug that toggle coverage finds in minutes.

## 6. Limitations

**Oracle availability.** Differential testing presupposes a trusted golden reference model. TheHuzz explicitly declined to fuzz the Ibex core because no independent GRM existed [1]. For commercial ISAs, GRMs exist (Intel ArchSim, AMD SimNow are cited [1]) but are proprietary and may themselves encode the same microarchitectural assumptions as the DUT. Assertion oracles avoid this dependency but reintroduce manual specification effort — the very cost fuzzing was meant to eliminate.

**Throughput and cost.** Even FPGA-accelerated flows run at tens of hertz per full fuzzing iteration [5]; software simulation of large SoCs remains the bottleneck for industrial adoption. Multi-metric coverage collection at 71% overhead [1] is a real tax, and the community's response — sketched coverage interfaces [7], in-hardware collection [5] — is still maturing.

**False positives and triage load.** Differential testing flags *any* GRM/DUT divergence, including benign unspecified behavior, performance-counter differences, and reset-value discrepancies. MorFuzz's state synchronization [4] mitigates this, but triage remains human-intensive: each candidate requires minimization, confirmation, and security-impact analysis before it becomes a bug report.

**The semantic gap.** Structural coverage — however rich — measures *what was exercised*, not *what is correct*. A design can achieve 100% toggle and FSM coverage while harboring a bug in logic that is covered but never checked against intent. Coverage is necessary but not sufficient for security; the oracle, not the metric, carries the correctness burden.

**Scope boundaries.** Current processor fuzzers target single-hart, ISA-visible behavior. Multi-hart cache coherence, memory-consistency subtleties, side-channel leakage (timing, power), and analog/mixed-signal interfaces lie largely outside the demonstrated envelope. Zenbleed-class bugs — microarchitectural, software-visible only through careful probing [9] — sit at the edge of what differential ISA-level testing can express.

## 7. Conclusion

Coverage-guided hardware fuzzing has matured from RFuzz's pioneering FPGA framework [2], through DifuzzRTL's differential-testing template [3], to TheHuzz's industrial-grade six-metric instruction fuzzer [1] and a second generation — MorFuzz [4], TurboFuzz [5], GoldenFuzz [8] — that pushes throughput, input validity, and oracle precision. The empirical record is unambiguous: dozens of bugs, double-digit CVE counts, and demonstrated software exploitability, culminating in the real-world lesson of Zenbleed that fuzzing discovers silicon vulnerabilities formal methods miss [9].

The thesis advanced here is that three coupled decisions determine a hardware fuzzer's effectiveness: **mutational stimulus generation** that respects and then deliberately violates instruction grammar; **oracles** — assertion-driven, differential, or hybrid — that render silent functional deviations observable; and **coverage metrics** with hardware-native semantics (toggle, FSM, expression, condition) whose feedback landscape the mutator climbs toward **closure**. The convergence analysis of Section 5 formalizes the practitioner's intuition: enrich the metric and sharpen the oracle before spending iterations.

Open problems remain: oracle-free bug detection, multi-hart and side-channel oracles, and the integration of fuzzing into sign-off flows where *coverage closure* must be argued to skeptical tape-out review boards. As open-source processors grow in complexity and open ISAs proliferate, the community's investment in fuzzing infrastructure — sketched coverage interfaces, in-hardware fuzzing loops, generative stimulus models — will determine whether pre-silicon verification keeps pace with design ambition. The trajectory from RFuzz to TurboFuzz suggests it can.
