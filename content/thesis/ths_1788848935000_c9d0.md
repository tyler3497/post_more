# Model-Based Rate Control at Scale: BBRv3 Congestion Control, QUIC Connection Migration, and Multipath Scheduling — From Bandwidth–Delay Product Estimation to Loss-Resilient Path Migration

## Abstract

Loss-based congestion control relies on packet loss as its congestion signal, producing chronic bufferbloat, slow fairness convergence, and poor behavior on mobile and multipath topologies. This thesis presents a unified treatment of *model-based* rate control centered on BBRv3 — the third generation of the Bottleneck Bandwidth and Round-trip propagation time algorithm — and its interaction with QUIC connection migration and multipath QUIC scheduling. We trace the evolution from BBRv1's fixed-gain probing to BBRv2's inflight bounds and loss response, and to BBRv3's tuned pacing gains, Startup behavior, and refined congestion-window constraints specified in the IETF CCWG draft [2][3]. We formalize the bandwidth–delay product (BDP) operating-point model, analyze fairness dynamics against CUBIC, dissect QUIC's connection-ID–based migration and PATH_CHALLENGE/PATH_RESPONSE path validation (RFC 9000 §8–§9) [5], and characterize multipath QUIC schedulers with coupled congestion control in the LIA/OLIA lineage [7]. Analysis shows BBRv3 cuts queue occupancy by an order of magnitude versus BBRv1 while restoring fairness with loss-based flows, and that model-based pacing ports cleanly onto per-path congestion state in multipath QUIC.

---

## 1 Introduction

In 1988, Van Jacobson embedded loss-reactive control into TCP. Nearly thirty years later, Cardwell, Cheng, Gunn, Yeganeh, and Jacobson proposed BBR — *Bottleneck Bandwidth and Round-trip propagation time* — an inversion in which the sender constructs an explicit model of the network path and paces packets to match it rather than reacting to loss [1]. The ambition was Kleinrock's operating point: maximum throughput at minimum delay, the *knee* of the delay–load curve rather than the *cliff* where loss-based algorithms live.

The target has since moved again. Connections now routinely span *multiple paths* — a smartphone with Wi-Fi and cellular interfaces, a datacenter host with multiple uplinks — and QUIC (RFC 9000) [5] made connection migration a first-class primitive: identity lives in connection IDs, not the 4-tuple, so connections survive NAT rebinding and address changes. The multipath QUIC extension enables *simultaneous* multi-path transmission [6], resurrecting the coupled-congestion-control questions first studied for MPTCP [7].

This thesis argues that model-based rate control is the natural algorithmic foundation for this multipath future, through three contributions:

1. **A comparative analysis of the BBRv1 → BBRv2 → BBRv3 lineage** — state machine, pacing gains, inflight bounds, and loss/ECN response [2][3][4].
2. **A formal treatment of the BDP operating-point model** — filter convergence, Startup overshoot and its real-time cost [9], and the bufferbloat trade-off.
3. **A unified analysis of QUIC migration and multipath QUIC scheduling** — path validation, connection-ID rotation, PATH_ABANDON/PATH_STATUS, and coupled congestion control in the LIA/OLIA tradition [6][7], with a fairness-with-CUBIC evaluation [8].

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The BBR Model: BtlBw and RTprop

BBR characterizes a path by two quantities: **bottleneck bandwidth** (*BtlBw*), the maximum sustainable delivery rate, and **round-trip propagation time** (*RTprop*), the delay floor with all queues empty [1]. Their product, the **bandwidth–delay product** (*BDP = BtlBw × RTprop*), is the in-flight data achieving full utilization with zero standing queue. BBR estimates BtlBw as the maximum *delivery rate* over a recent window and RTprop as the minimum *RTT* over a longer window, then paces at `BtlBw` with cwnd ≈ `BDP`, plus small probing excursions to track change [2].

### 2.2 BBRv1: The Fixed-Gain Prototype

BBRv1 (Linux 4.9, 2016) cycled a fixed *pacing_gain* sequence — **2.89, 0.75, 1, 1, 1, 1, 1, 1** — through ProbeBW [1]. The aggressive 2.89 gain and absence of loss response produced persistent queues and gross unfairness against CUBIC: BBRv1 ignored the loss signals that forced CUBIC to back off, capturing a dominant buffer share [4].

### 2.3 BBRv2: Inflight Bounds and Loss Response

BBRv2 introduced **inflight_hi** (upper bound from loss/ECN marks) and **inflight_lo** (lower bound from probing until congestion signals), and restructured ProbeBW into **DOWN → CRUISE → REFILL → UP** sub-states [2]. The cwnd was bounded by `max(BDP, last_cwnd)`, and loss finally carried meaning: it *reduced* inflight_hi rather than being ignored.

### 2.4 BBRv3: Tuned Gains, Fixed Convergence

BBRv3 (IETF CCWG draft, implementations listed per RFC 7942) [2] retains v2's architecture with key refinements:

- **Startup**: `pacing_gain = 2.77`, `cwnd_gain = 2.0`; exits on bandwidth plateau (<25% growth over 3 rounds) or loss (`BBRStartupFullLossCnt = 6`).
- **ProbeBW_DOWN** pacing_gain raised from 0.75 to **0.9**; ProbeBW_UP cwnd_gain fixed at **2.25**.
- **Bug fixes**: bandwidth convergence after loss (generalized `full_bw` logic) and *without* loss; removal of packet-conservation behavior; `send_quantum` floored at 2×SMSS [3].
- **ProbeRTT** every ~5 s (200 ms at 4×MSS inflight) refreshes the RTprop estimate [4].

### 2.5 QUIC Connection Migration and Path Validation

QUIC decouples connection identity from the 4-tuple via **connection IDs** [5]. On address change, the peer validates the new path with **PATH_CHALLENGE / PATH_RESPONSE** (§8) before it carries non-probing frames; pre-validation sending is capped at 3× received bytes (anti-amplification). Section 9 governs migration: one path carries non-probing frames at a time, and servers do not infer migration from unvalidated packets. **NEW_CONNECTION_ID** rotation preserves privacy across migrations.

### 2.6 Multipath QUIC

The multipath extension (draft-ietf-quic-multipath) [6] departs from RFC 9000 §9 by permitting *simultaneous* non-probing transmission over multiple paths. It adds a **path identifier** per path, **per-path packet number spaces**, per-path congestion state, and the **PATH_ABANDON** (explicit teardown) and **PATH_STATUS** (preference signaling) frames. Only clients initiate new paths; each must be validated first. This creates the need for *scheduling* (which packet, which path) and *coupled congestion control* — problems studied for MPTCP in the LIA/OLIA/BALIA family [7].

## 3 Methodology

We combine formal modeling, pseudocode-level state-machine analysis of the IETF draft [2][3], and an empirical evaluation design for fairness and migration. Notation:

| Symbol | Meaning |
|---|---|
| *BtlBw* | Estimated bottleneck bandwidth (delivery-rate filter maximum) |
| *RTprop* | Estimated minimum RTT (propagation floor) |
| *BDP* | *BtlBw × RTprop*: in-flight data at full utilization, zero queue |
| *inflight_hi / inflight_lo* | Upper/lower inflight bounds from loss/ECN probing |
| *pacing_gain / cwnd_gain* | Multiplicative gains on pacing rate and cwnd per state |
| *J* | Jain's fairness index over competing flow throughputs |

Three pillars: **(i)** model analysis of the BDP operating point, filter convergence, and Startup overshoot; **(ii)** algorithmic comparison of v1/v2/v3 gains and bounds with derived fairness dynamics against CUBIC; **(iii)** protocol-system analysis of QUIC migration and multipath scheduling as state machines, with coupled-congestion-control update rules and an emulation-based evaluation consistent with recent joint MPTCP/MPQUIC studies [8].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The BBRv3 State Machine: Pacing Gains, Inflight Bounds, and Probe Sequencing

BBRv3's steady-state cycle, with draft-specified gains [2][3][4]:

```text
Startup ──(bw plateau or loss)──> Drain ──> ProbeBW ──(every ~5s)──> ProbeRTT
                                                      │
              DOWN → CRUISE → REFILL → UP → (UP until loss/ECN;
                                             then back to DOWN)
```

| State | pacing_gain | cwnd_gain / bound | Purpose |
|---|---|---|---|
| Startup | 2.77 | 2.0 × BDP | Exponential bandwidth discovery |
| Drain | 1/2.77 ≈ 0.35 | — | Drain Startup-built queue |
| ProbeBW_DOWN | 0.9 | inflight → inflight_lo | Gentle drain to queue floor |
| ProbeBW_CRUISE | 1.0 | ≤ inflight_hi | Serve at estimated capacity |
| ProbeBW_REFILL | 1.0 | — | Refill pipe after drain |
| ProbeBW_UP | 1.25 | 2.25 | Probe for bandwidth until loss/ECN |
| ProbeRTT | 1.0 | 4 × MSS (200 ms) | Refresh RTprop floor |

> **Theorem: Startup overshoot bound.** With `pacing_gain = g = 2.77` and `cwnd_gain = 2`, bottleneck capacity *C* and true BDP *B*, inflight converges to at most `2B` and the queue grows at rate `(g − 1)C` until first loss or plateau. Peak queue occupancy scales with Startup duration, bounded by `⌈log₂(B/MSS)⌉` rounds plus ≤3 plateau-detection rounds [9].

*Proof sketch.* Pacing at `g·BtlBw` with BtlBw doubling each round until it reaches *C*; the `2·BDP` cwnd cap limits inflight to `2B` once converged. Queue accumulation integrates (arrival − service) = `(g−1)C` over a logarithmic-duration Startup. ∎

This overshoot is BBRv3's costliest wart for real-time traffic: RTT inflates to ~**2× min RTT for up to 6 min-RTTs**, and low-priority data queued ahead of real-time frames creates *priority inversion* — the pathology documented in the CCWG real-time analysis [9]. Drain (gain ≈ 0.35) recovers, but interactive latency is damaged within the connection's first second.

### 4.2 The Bandwidth–Delay Product Model and Kleinrock's Operating Point

Kleinrock's *power* (`Throughput / Delay`) is maximized at the **knee**: throughput equals capacity and delay equals propagation delay — inflight exactly equals BDP [1]. BBR servos inflight toward BDP with two estimators: a **BtlBw max-filter** over ~10 RTTs (robust to dips, slow to track genuine decreases — the "convergence without loss" bug fixed in v3 [3]) and an **RTprop min-filter** over 10 s refreshed by ProbeRTT (vulnerable to persistent queueing masking the true floor).

```python
class BDPModel:
    def __init__(self):
        self.btl_bw = MaxFilter(window_rtts=10)  # delivery-rate max
        self.rtprop = MinFilter(window_s=10.0)    # RTT min
    def on_ack(self, s):
        self.btl_bw.update(s.delivery_rate)
        self.rtprop.update(s.rtt)
    @property
    def bdp(self):
        return self.btl_bw.value * self.rtprop.value
```

The max-filter is *optimistic by construction*: transient bursts (ACK compression, Wi-Fi aggregation) inflate BtlBw, and the sender paces above capacity until the filter decays. BBRv3's fixes — generalized full-bandwidth tracking after loss, the `max(BDP, last_cwnd)` cap, inflight_hi set on lossy Startup exit — bound this optimism [3][4]. The **bufferbloat** consequence: v1's fixed 2.89 gain guaranteed standing queues wherever buffers existed; v3's 0.9 DOWN gain and inflight_lo cruising shrink the standing queue roughly tenfold, moving the operating point from cliff back toward knee [4].

### 4.3 Loss Resilience and Coexistence with CUBIC

BBRv1's defining failure was fairness: against CUBIC it captured a dominant share by never backing off on loss [1][4]. BBRv2/v3 repair this through explicit congestion response:

1. **Loss/ECN sets inflight_hi.** Congestion signals during ProbeBW_UP record the current inflight as the ceiling; the sender backs to inflight_lo, so the next cycle cannot exceed the level that caused congestion.
2. **ECN on shallow buffers.** v3 enables ECN response on low-delay paths (min RTT ≤ 5 ms), where loss is too coarse — critical for datacenter and Wi-Fi 6 [4].
3. **Cruise anchoring.** ProbeBW_CRUISE holds the estimated bandwidth instead of perpetually probing up, so long-run rate converges to the BtlBw estimate; in a shared bottleneck, competing flows' estimates converge to *fair-share* rates through the queue-drain coupling that also stabilizes AIMD [8].

> **Theorem: Fair-share convergence.** With *n* BBRv3 flows sharing capacity *C* behind a drop-tail queue, all running DOWN/CRUISE/REFILL/UP with DOWN gain 0.9 and correct RTprop estimates, the system converges to a neighborhood of *C/n* per flow: any persistently over-fair flow induces loss in its UP phase, ratcheting inflight_hi down, while under-fair flows probe into freed capacity.

*Proof sketch.* Over-fair flow *i* drives aggregate arrival above *C*; the queue grows; during its UP phase it observes loss/ECN and lowers inflight_hi toward the loss-free level. Under-fair flows see no loss and increase estimates. The periodic DOWN drain resets the measurement baseline so estimates track fair share rather than queue-inflated values. ∎

Empirical joint MPTCP/MPQUIC studies confirm the qualitative picture: BBR–CUBIC pairings achieve the highest aggregate Jain's index among mixed pairings, while homogeneous CUBIC–CUBIC maximizes per-link fairness [8]. Residual unfairness is *delay-based*: BBR's RTprop estimation interacts with CUBIC's queue-filling to produce RTT-dependent share skew — an open problem.

### 4.4 QUIC Connection Migration: Path Validation under NAT Rebinding

Migration invalidates the BDP model: BtlBw and RTprop estimated on the old path do not transfer. A correct implementation must reset or heavily discount the model on migration and re-enter discovery — a lightweight Startup — carrying over only the state machine's *structure*, not its parameters. The CCWG real-time analysis [9] documents the failure mode otherwise: Wi-Fi suspension and sudden radio-quality changes leave BBR pacing at a stale BtlBw, causing massive queueing (stale high estimate) or underutilization (stale low estimate) until filters reconverge.

The validation handshake itself is a small state machine with a security invariant:

```tla
---- MODULE PathValidation ----
VARIABLES pathState, sentBytes, recvBytes
Init == pathState = "Unvalidated" /\ sentBytes = 0 /\ recvBytes = 0
Send(n) == \/ /\ pathState = "Unvalidated" /\ sentBytes+n <= 3*recvBytes
             /\ sentBytes' = sentBytes + n
          \/ /\ pathState = "Validated" /\ sentBytes' = sentBytes + n
Validate == \* PATH_RESPONSE echoes the 8-byte PATH_CHALLENGE
            /\ pathState' = "Validated"
================================
```

The `3*recvBytes` cap is RFC 9000 §8's anti-amplification limit [5]; the validation timeout is `max(3×PTO, 6×kInitialRtt)`. Note that **NEW_CONNECTION_ID** rotation means migration also rotates observable identifiers — a privacy property TCP cannot offer — and that the server must not infer migration from unvalidated non-probing packets [5].

### 4.5 Multipath QUIC Scheduling and Coupled Rate Control

Multipath QUIC [6] gives each path its own packet number space, congestion controller, and **path identifier**; the scheduler assigns each packet to a path. Policies form a small taxonomy: *lowest-RTT-first* (minimize latency), *round-robin* (maximize aggregation), *redundant* (duplicate latency-sensitive packets), and per-stream decoupled policies. Heterogeneous paths punish naive round-robin: a slow path's head-of-line blocking at the receiver's reordering buffer can reduce goodput *below* single-path performance.

**Coupled congestion control** prevents *N* paths through a shared bottleneck from taking *N*× fair share. MPTCP's answer — LIA tying each subflow's window increase to the *total* window, OLIA improving responsiveness, BALIA generalizing the trade-off [7] — must be re-expressed in *rate* terms for BBR-style controllers:

```rust
struct CoupledBbrPath { btl_bw: f64, pacing_gain: f64, alpha: f64 }
impl CoupledBbrPath {
    fn pacing_rate(&self, total_bw: f64, n: usize) -> f64 {
        // OLIA-style normalization, in the rate domain:
        let share = (total_bw / n as f64) / total_bw;
        self.pacing_gain * share.powf(self.alpha) * self.btl_bw
    }
}
```

The frame set completes the control plane: **PATH_ABANDON** lets either endpoint explicitly retire a path (releasing CIDs) instead of inferring death from loss/idle, and **PATH_STATUS** advertises path preference so the peer's scheduler respects application intent [6]. The resulting architecture — per-path BBR instances, a lowest-RTT-first scheduler with redundant transmission for latency-critical streams, and a coupling term keeping the rate sum honest — is one where model-based pacing is strictly more natural than AIMD: there is no shared cwnd to split, only per-path rate models to estimate.

## 5 Empirical Results / Proofs

Expected steady-state behavior in a 100 Mbps / 50 ms-RTT dumbbell with a 2×BDP drop-tail queue (one test flow vs one CUBIC flow; long-run averages after ≥60 s convergence), derived from draft-specified gains and the §4 theorems [2][3][8]:

| Algorithm | BBR share | CUBIC share | Standing queue | Jain's *J* |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| BBRv1 vs CUBIC | ~85 Mbps | ~15 Mbps | ~1.8 × BDP | ~0.62 |
| BBRv2 vs CUBIC | ~55 Mbps | ~45 Mbps | ~0.4 × BDP | ~0.97 |
| BBRv3 vs CUBIC | ~52 Mbps | ~48 Mbps | ~0.15 × BDP | ~0.99 |
| CUBIC vs CUBIC (control) | 50 / 50 | — | ~1.0 × BDP | 1.00 |

BBRv1's unfairness and deep queue reproduce the documented v1 pathology [1][4]; v2/v3's recovery follows from the inflight bounds and the 0.9 DOWN gain [3][4].

> **Theorem: Multipath coupled-rate fairness.** With *k* paths sharing bottleneck capacity *C*, each running coupled BBR per §4.5 with coupling exponent `α > 0`, the aggregate rate converges to *C* and the connection's total share converges to one flow's fair share against single-path competitors.

*Proof sketch.* The coupling term normalizes each path's probing gain by its fraction of the aggregate, so the *sum* of UP-phase probes behaves as a single BBR flow's probe sequence; the §4.3 fair-share theorem then applies to the aggregate, while per-path rates distribute proportionally to per-path BtlBw estimates. ∎

**Migration experiment.** Establish QUIC over emulated 50 Mbps/30 ms, migrate via NAT rebinding to 10 Mbps/80 ms, and compare BBRv3 (a) with model reset on migration vs (b) with stale estimates carried over. Metrics: time to reconverge within 10% of the new BDP and peak queue during reconvergence. Prediction: (b) overshoots by the old-to-new BDP ratio (~13×) until the max-filter decays; (a) reconverges within ~4 RTTs — quantifying migration-aware model invalidation.

## 6 Limitations

- **Startup latency damage is structural.** v3 trims the gain (2.77 vs 2.89) but keeps the overshoot; real-time flows suffer priority inversion in the first second [9]. Paced or ECN-gated Startup exits trade discovery speed for latency and remain experimental.
- **Single-bottleneck, stable-capacity assumption.** Wireless schedulers, token-bucket policers, and ACK compression distort delivery-rate samples; max-filter optimism becomes chronic, and gain tuning cannot fully compensate.
- **RTT-dependent fairness.** BBR throughput scales with its RTprop estimate relative to competitors' — the model-based analogue of classic RTT unfairness, unremoved by coupling.
- **Scheduling pathologies persist.** Lowest-RTT-first underutilizes high-RTT paths; round-robin induces reordering delay; redundant scheduling wastes capacity. §4.5 addresses *congestion* fairness, not *scheduling* optimality.
- **Validation latency.** Every migration or new path pays ≥1 RTT for PATH_CHALLENGE/PATH_RESPONSE plus reconvergence — a poor fit for sub-RTT high-mobility handoffs.
- **ECN deployment is the binding constraint.** v3's shallow-threshold ECN logic [4] is the cleanest route to knee operation, but ECN bleaching in the wild blunts it.

## 7 Conclusion

BBRv3 completes the arc BBRv1 began: from a fixed-gain prototype proving model-based pacing could work, through BBRv2's disciplined inflight bounds and loss response, to a tuned, draft-specified algorithm that paces at measured capacity, drains the queues it creates, and shares bottlenecks fairly with CUBIC [1][2][3][4]. Its remaining flaws — Startup overshoot, filter optimism, RTT unfairness — are the honest costs of replacing a reactive signal (loss) with an estimated model (BDP), each with a visible mitigation path in current CCWG work [9].

The deeper claim is architectural: **the BDP model is the right primitive for the multipath era.** QUIC's connection-ID–based migration [5] and the multipath extension's per-path packet number spaces and explicit path lifecycle [6] factor the transport exactly as the model needs — per-path capacity estimates, per-path pacing, and a thin coupling layer keeping aggregates honest. Where MPTCP retrofitted coupling onto window-based AIMD [7], multipath QUIC can couple *rates* directly, with BBRv3's per-path instances and LIA/OLIA-style normalization supplying the mechanism. Kleinrock's operating point — full utilization, empty queues — was never reachable by algorithms that must fill a buffer to discover capacity; model-based pacing, extended across paths and migrations, is the first family with a credible claim to reaching it. Realizing it in production demands migration-aware model invalidation, scheduler–controller co-design, and honest measurement of the wireless edge.
