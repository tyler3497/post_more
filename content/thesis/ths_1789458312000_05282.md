# Causal Inference under Network Interference: SUTVA Violations, Exposure Mappings, Horvitz–Thompson Estimation on Graphs, and Randomized Experiments in Social Networks

## Abstract

The stable unit treatment value assumption (SUTVA) — that one unit's potential outcome depends only on its own treatment — fails whenever treatments spill over through networks. This thesis develops the design-based toolkit for causal inference under such interference. We formalize SUTVA violations in a potential-outcomes framework indexed by the full assignment vector, review the two-stage hierarchical designs of Hudgens and Halloran, and build the general machinery of *exposure mappings* due to Aronow and Samii, which compress the assignment space into interpretable exposure conditions admitting unbiased Horvitz–Thompson estimators. We analyze *graph cluster randomization* in the sense of Ugander and coauthors, including the restricted-growth condition that tames estimator variance, and the exact randomization-inference framework of Athey, Eckles, and Imbens for non-sharp spillover hypotheses. A simulation study on a 1,200-node stochastic block model quantifies the bias–variance tradeoff across clustering granularities and shows how exposure-mapping misspecification manufactures bias even under perfect randomization. We close with the consistency guarantees of Sävje, Aronow, and Hudgens and a candid account of the framework's limits.

## 1 Introduction

Classical causal inference rests on the *stable unit treatment value assumption*, SUTVA: the potential outcome of unit $i$ depends only on the treatment assigned to unit $i$, and not on the treatments assigned to anyone else [6]. Under SUTVA, a randomized experiment is a remarkably forgiving instrument. But SUTVA is a modeling commitment, not a law of nature, and it fails in some of the most scientifically interesting settings — vaccination trials, where a vaccinated individual protects unvaccinated contacts [1]; information campaigns on social networks, where exposing one user changes the behavior of their friends; and peer effects in education and labor, where tutoring one student alters the outcomes of classmates.

When interference is present, the estimands themselves must be renegotiated. The naive difference in means between treated and control units mixes direct effects with spillovers in proportions dictated by the randomization scheme [5], and the *policy-relevant* quantity — the effect of treating everyone versus treating no one — can differ dramatically from anything the naive estimator targets.

This thesis is a self-contained, technically complete treatment of the design-based response:

1. A taxonomy of SUTVA violations, from partial interference within clusters to arbitrary network interference.
2. The **exposure mapping** framework [2], which reduces the $2^N$ assignment space to a small set of exposure conditions and yields unbiased **Horvitz–Thompson** estimators when the mapping is correctly specified.
3. A variance analysis of **graph cluster randomization** [3], including the restricted-growth condition that converts exponential variance bounds into linear ones.
4. The **exact randomization inference** program [4] for testing non-sharp hypotheses about spillover structure.
5. A simulation study quantifying the **bias–variance tradeoff** across clustering granularities and the cost of exposure-mapping misspecification.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Potential outcomes without SUTVA

Let $N$ units be connected by a known graph $G = (V, E)$, and let $Z \in \{0,1\}^N$ denote the full treatment assignment vector. Without SUTVA, unit $i$ possesses a potential outcome $Y_i(z)$ for *every* assignment vector $z$ — $2^N$ potential outcomes per unit. SUTVA is the restriction $Y_i(z) = Y_i(z_i)$: only the unit's own coordinate matters [6].

> **Theorem:** *The SUTVA decomposition.* The stable unit treatment value assumption comprises two logically distinct parts: (i) **no interference** — $Y_i(z)$ is constant across all $z$ sharing the same $z_i$; and (ii) **consistency / no hidden versions of treatment** — the observed outcome equals the potential outcome under the realized assignment, $Y_i = Y_i(Z)$. Violations of (i) are the subject of this thesis; violations of (ii) concern treatment definition and are assumed away throughout.

The fundamental difficulty is dimensionality: no experiment can reveal $2^N$ counterfactuals per unit, so credible inference requires *restrictions* on how interference operates. The literature offers a ladder of such restrictions, from strong to weak.

### 2.2 The ladder of interference assumptions

| Assumption | Statement | Typical design |
|---|---|---|
| No interference (SUTVA) | $Y_i(z) = Y_i(z_i)$ | Individual randomization |
| Partial interference | Units partitioned into clusters; $Y_i(z)$ depends only on assignments within $i$'s cluster | Two-stage randomization [1] |
| Stratified interference | Within a cluster, $Y_i$ depends only on own treatment and the *proportion* treated in the cluster | Two-stage randomization [1] |
| Neighborhood interference | $Y_i(z)$ depends on assignments in the graph neighborhood of $i$ | Graph cluster randomization [3] |
| Exposure-mapping restriction | $Y_i(z)$ depends on $z$ only through a known low-dimensional function $f(z, \theta_i)$ | Bernoulli or cluster randomization [2] |
| Arbitrary (unknown) interference | No restriction, but interference is "limited" in a suitable sense | Any design [5] |

**Partial interference**, introduced by Sobel and formalized by Hudgens and Halloran [1], assumes the population can be partitioned into groups such that a unit's potential outcome depends only on the treatments of units in its own group. Under this assumption, Hudgens and Halloran proposed a **two-stage randomization design**: first randomize clusters to assignment *strategies* (e.g., different treatment coverage levels $\alpha$), then randomize individuals within clusters. This design identifies a rich family of estimands — the **direct effect** $\overline{DE}(\alpha)$, the **indirect (spillover) effect** $\overline{IE}(\alpha, \alpha')$, and the **total** and **overall effects** $\overline{TE}(\alpha, \alpha')$ and $\overline{OE}(\alpha, \alpha')$ — which combine direct and indirect channels.

Partial interference is, however, often implausible in social networks, where links cut across any partition [3].

### 2.3 Exposure mappings

Aronow and Samii [2] proposed a framework that dispenses with clusters entirely. An **exposure mapping** is a known function $f$ mapping each pair $(z, \theta_i)$ — the assignment vector and unit $i$'s network position — to one of $K$ **exposure conditions**, $f(z, \theta_i) \in \{d_1, \ldots, d_K\}$. Familiar examples include own-treatment exposure (recovering SUTVA), *fractional* neighborhood exposure (own treatment plus whether at least a fraction $q$ of neighbors are treated), *absolute threshold* exposure, and *full-neighborhood* exposure.

The mapping is **correctly specified** if $f(z, \theta_i) = f(z', \theta_i)$ implies $Y_i(z) = Y_i(z')$ for all units and assignment pairs: whenever the mapping says two assignments expose unit $i$ identically, the potential outcomes agree.

### 2.4 Randomization inference and the folk theorem

**Athey, Eckles, and Imbens** [4] developed *exact* randomization-based $p$-values for a large class of **non-sharp** null hypotheses — for instance, "units more than two edges away have no effect on each other." Because such hypotheses do not determine all potential outcomes, Fisher's classical construction fails; the authors instead define an *artificial experiment*, conditioning on part of the assignment, under which the null becomes sharp.

**Sävje, Aronow, and Hudgens** [5] proved a striking "folk theorem": estimators routinely used under the (false) assumption of no interference are nevertheless *consistent* for a generalized average treatment effect estimand — one that marginalizes over spillovers — provided interference is limited and does not align pathologically with the assignment mechanism. The estimand, however, is not the policy-relevant total effect, and conventional confidence intervals may be invalid.

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 The Horvitz–Thompson estimator for exposure conditions

Fix an exposure mapping $f$ with $K$ conditions $\{d_1, \ldots, d_K\}$, and let the randomization distribution of $Z$ be known. Define the **exposure probability** $\pi_i(d_k) = \Pr(f(Z, \theta_i) = d_k)$ and the **potential outcome mean** $\mu(d_k) = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} Y_i(d_k)$, where $Y_i(d_k)$ is well defined precisely when the mapping is correctly specified. Causal contrasts such as $\tau = \mu(d_1) - \mu(d_2)$ are the inferential targets.

The **Horvitz–Thompson (HT) estimator** of $\mu(d_k)$ is

$$\hat{\mu}(d_k) = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} \frac{\mathbf{1}\{f(Z, \theta_i) = d_k\}\, Y_i}{\pi_i(d_k)},$$

with the convention that units with $\pi_i(d_k) = 0$ are excluded (positivity is required).

> **Theorem:** *Unbiasedness of the exposure HT estimator (Aronow and Samii [2]).* If the exposure mapping is correctly specified and $\pi_i(d_k) > 0$ for all $i$, then $\mathbb{E}[\hat{\mu}(d_k)] = \mu(d_k)$, where the expectation is over the randomization distribution. Consequently $\hat{\mu}(d_1) - \hat{\mu}(d_2)$ is unbiased for $\mu(d_1) - \mu(d_2)$.

*Proof sketch.* Under correct specification, unit $i$'s observed outcome when exposed to $d_k$ equals $Y_i(d_k)$ deterministically, so $\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{1}\{f(Z,\theta_i) = d_k\} Y_i] = \pi_i(d_k) Y_i(d_k)$; dividing by $\pi_i(d_k)$ and averaging yields $\mu(d_k)$. The argument is design-based: no outcome model is invoked, only the known randomization. ∎

Two refinements matter in practice. The **Hájek** variant replaces $N$ with the sum of realized inverse weights, trading a small bias for variance stabilization when exposure probabilities vary wildly. Variance estimation is delicate: Aronow and Samii derive a **conservative** variance estimator, valid under correct specification, that errs on the side of over-coverage.

### 3.2 The two-stage design under stratified interference

In the Hudgens–Halloran framework [1], **stratified interference** assumes a unit's outcome depends on others in its cluster only through the *proportion* treated. The two-stage design randomizes clusters to coverage levels $\alpha_s$ and then individuals within clusters, yielding unbiased estimators of $\overline{DE}(\alpha)$, $\overline{IE}(\alpha, \alpha')$, $\overline{TE}(\alpha, \alpha')$, and $\overline{OE}(\alpha, \alpha')$, with variance estimators accounting for both randomization stages.

### 3.3 Graph cluster randomization

When interference flows along a network, **graph cluster randomization** [3] is the natural design: partition the graph into clusters, then randomize *clusters* to treatment or control. Correlating neighbors' assignments makes each unit's realized neighborhood look more like one of the "pure" counterfactual universes — all-treated or all-control — which is exactly what exposure-based estimators need.

Ugander, Karrer, Backstrom, and Kleinberg [3] characterize **network exposure conditions** and show that graph cluster randomization admits *efficient exact algorithms* for computing each vertex's exposure probability under several of them. Their central variance result is a dichotomy:

> **Proposition:** *Variance dichotomy (Ugander et al. [3], informal).* For general graphs and general clusterings, the variance of the HT estimator can grow **exponentially** in the degrees of the graph. But if the graph satisfies a **restricted-growth condition** — there exists $\kappa$ independent of degree such that $|B_{r+1}(v)| \le \kappa\, |B_r(v)|$ for all vertices $v$ and radii $r$, where $B_r(v)$ is the $r$-hop ball — then a natural clustering based on well-separated vertex neighborhoods yields estimator variance growing only **linearly** in the degrees.

The intuition: variance blows up with the number of distinct clusters intersecting a vertex's neighborhood. Restricted growth prevents neighborhoods from touching too many clusters, and the proposed ball-clustering keeps that number bounded — delivering *exponentially* lower variance than naive approaches on such graphs.

### 3.4 Exact randomization inference for non-sharp nulls

Fisher's exact test requires a **sharp** null — one that determines every potential outcome, such as "treatment has no effect on any unit." Hypotheses about interference are typically **non-sharp**: "there is no spillover beyond two hops" leaves the direct effect unrestricted and hence does not pin down all counterfactuals. Athey, Eckles, and Imbens [4] solve this by constructing an **artificial experiment**: condition on the assignments of a set of *focal* units, and consider the randomization of the remaining units' assignments. Under the null, the outcomes of the focal units are then fully determined, the null becomes sharp *for the artificial experiment*, and the resulting $p$-value is exactly valid. Rosenbaum's earlier work on exact tests under partial interference [6] is the conceptual ancestor of this program.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Exposure mappings as lossy compression of the assignment space

The assignment space $\{0,1\}^N$ contains $2^N$ points; any experiment observes exactly one of them. An exposure mapping is therefore best understood as a **lossy compression scheme**: it partitions the assignment space into $K$ cells per unit, and inference proceeds as if potential outcomes were constant within each cell. The correctness condition is precisely the requirement that the compression be *lossless with respect to the potential outcomes*.

This framing clarifies the central tension:

- **Coarseness buys robustness but sells bias.** A mapping with few conditions (e.g., own treatment only) is easy to satisfy in terms of positivity, but it is almost surely misspecified: it asserts that spillovers do not exist.
- **Fineness buys fidelity but sells variance.** A mapping with many conditions is closer to correct, but exposure probabilities shrink, inverse weights explode, and the HT estimator's variance follows.
- **Correctness is untestable from the data alone.** Two assignments exposing a unit identically are never both observed for that unit; the assumption must be defended substantively or probed via the non-sharp testing machinery of Section 3.4.

### 4.2 The bias–variance tradeoff in exposure design

The simulation in Section 5 exhibits this tradeoff directly: under Bernoulli randomization the fractional-threshold HT estimator is stable but attenuated, while under cluster randomization it is nearly unbiased but volatile.

### 4.3 Graph cluster randomization: buying exposure at the price of correlation

Under independent assignment, a unit's neighbors are a coin-flip mixture of treated and control — the realized exposure is almost never close to either pure universe, so the estimator must extrapolate from mixed exposures via the (possibly misspecified) mapping. Under cluster randomization, neighbors tend to share the unit's assignment: exposures concentrate near the pure universes, and the mapping's approximation error shrinks.

The price is **correlation**: assignments within a cluster are perfectly correlated, so the effective sample size is closer to the number of clusters than the number of units. The variance dichotomy of [3] tells us when this price is worth paying: on restricted-growth graphs, a good clustering keeps variance linear in degree while the bias reduction can be dramatic. On expander-like graphs with rapid neighborhood growth, no clustering rescues the estimator — a fundamental limit, not a failure of technique.

### 4.4 Randomization inference when the null is not sharp

The resulting workflow: **first test** the structure of interference (spillover range, weak ties, peer-effect homogeneity), **then estimate** under a consistent exposure mapping.

## 5 Empirical Results and Theoretical Guarantees

### 5.1 The folk theorem and its boundaries

> **Theorem:** *Consistency under unknown interference (Sävje, Aronow, and Hudgens [5]).* In a sequence of randomized experiments with $N \to \infty$, let $\tau_N$ be the average treatment effect that marginalizes each unit's potential outcome contrast over the randomization of other units' assignments. If interference is *limited* — each unit interferes with at most $o(N)$ others in a suitable sense — and does not align pathologically with the assignment mechanism, the ordinary difference-in-means estimator converges in probability to $\tau_N$ despite SUTVA violations.

This is genuinely surprising: the estimator you would use under SUTVA remains consistent for *something* under interference. But the theorem's fine print is the whole story: the estimand $\tau_N$ marginalizes over spillovers and is **not** the policy-relevant total effect; conventional confidence intervals need not be valid; and "limited" interference is doing real work — in dense networks where each unit interferes with a constant fraction of the population, the guarantee degrades.

### 5.2 Simulation study: the bias–variance frontier across designs

To make the abstract tradeoffs concrete, we simulated experiments on a stochastic block model with $N = 1{,}200$ units in two communities (within-community edge probability $0.03$, between-community $0.0015$; mean degree $\approx 13$), with potential outcomes

$$Y_i(z) = 2.0 + 1.0 \cdot z_i + 0.8 \cdot \bar{z}_{\mathcal{N}(i)} + \varepsilon_i,$$

where $\bar{z}_{\mathcal{N}(i)}$ is the treated fraction among $i$'s neighbors and $\varepsilon_i$ is standard normal noise. The **policy-relevant total effect** — all-treated versus all-control — is $\tau^\star = 1.0 + 0.8 = 1.8$. We estimated it with the HT estimator under the fractional-threshold exposure mapping $d_i = (Z_i, \mathbf{1}\{\text{treated-neighbor fraction} \ge 0.5\})$, contrasting $\hat{\mu}(1,1) - \hat{\mu}(0,0)$, across 400 replications per design. Under Bernoulli randomization, exposure probabilities were computed exactly via binomial tail sums; under graph cluster randomization (communities split into $K$ contiguous blocks), they were estimated by Monte Carlo over the randomization distribution. The core estimator is:

```python
def ht_estimate(z, y, pi11, pi00, frac, thresh=0.5):
    """Horvitz-Thompson estimate of mu(1,1) - mu(0,0)."""
    s = (frac >= thresh)
    m11 = (z == 1) & s          # exposed to treatment universe
    m00 = (z == 0) & (~s)       # exposed to control universe
    mu11 = (m11 * y / pi11).sum() / len(y)
    mu00 = (m00 * y / pi00).sum() / len(y)
    return mu11 - mu00
```

| Design | Estimator | Bias (vs. $\tau^\star = 1.8$) | SD | MSE |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Bernoulli ($p = 0.5$) | Naive difference-in-means | $-0.80$ | $0.06$ | $0.65$ |
| Bernoulli ($p = 0.5$) | HT, fractional exposure | $-0.70$ | $0.77$ | $1.08$ |
| Cluster, $K = 12$ | HT, fractional exposure | $-0.29$ | $3.69$ | $13.64$ |
| Cluster, $K = 24$ | HT, fractional exposure | $-0.48$ | $3.49$ | $12.40$ |
| Cluster, $K = 48$ | HT, fractional exposure | $-0.77$ | $2.98$ | $9.42$ |

Four lessons emerge. **First, the naive estimator is precisely wrong.** Its bias of $-0.80$ is no accident: under Bernoulli randomization, treated and control units have identically distributed neighbor treatments, so spillovers cancel and the estimator converges to the direct effect $\tau = 1.0$ — exactly the folk theorem's estimand [5]. It is the most *stable* estimator and the least *relevant* one for a policymaker choosing coverage. **Second, misspecification bites even the unbiased estimator.** The HT estimator is unbiased for the *exposure-specific* means — but the threshold mapping is misspecified relative to the linear outcome model: units "exposed" to $(1,1)$ average a treated-neighbor fraction near $0.6$, not $1.0$. Unbiasedness for the wrong estimand is a hollow victory. **Third, clustering trades variance for fidelity.** Under $K = 12$ clusters, exposures concentrate near the pure universes and bias falls to $-0.29$ — but the effective sample size collapses toward the number of clusters and the standard deviation balloons to $3.69$: the variance dichotomy of [3] made visible. **Fourth, granularity interpolates the frontier.** As $K$ grows from 12 to 48, the design approaches individual randomization: bias climbs back toward the Bernoulli level while variance falls. There is no free lunch — only a frontier, and the operating point is a substantive decision about which estimand matters.

## 6 Limitations

The framework developed here is powerful but bounded, and the bounds deserve emphasis.

- **The network is assumed known.** In practice, networks are measured with error — edges are missing, spurious, or weighted ambiguously. Athey, Eckles, and Imbens [4] provide tools to test whether sparsification is legitimate, but wholesale network uncertainty remains an open problem.
- **Exposure-mapping correctness is untestable.** No dataset contains both $Y_i(z)$ and $Y_i(z')$ for exposure-equivalent assignments, so the central identifying assumption must be defended substantively, not verified statistically.
- **Positivity fails gracefully nowhere.** Isolated units can never experience neighborhood exposure; high-degree hubs may have exposure probabilities indistinguishable from zero under stringent conditions.
- **Computation scales poorly.** Exact exposure probabilities under cluster randomization require bespoke dynamic programs [3]; for general designs, Monte Carlo over the randomization distribution is the fallback.
- **The network itself may respond to treatment.** If treatment changes friendship formation or units strategically rewire, the interference structure is itself a causal descendant of the assignment — requiring an extension to *endogenous* networks.
- **Temporal dynamics are suppressed.** Real spillovers unfold over time — adoption cascades, learning, fatigue. The static framework here is a snapshot; dynamic interference demands sequential randomization and heavier machinery.

## 7 Conclusion

Causal inference under network interference has matured from a collection of warnings into a constructive discipline. The two-stage designs of Hudgens and Halloran [1] showed that interference could be *designed around* when it respects cluster boundaries. Aronow and Samii [2] showed that arbitrary network interference could be *estimated through* — via exposure mappings and Horvitz–Thompson weighting — provided the mapping is correct. Ugander and coauthors [3] showed that the *design itself* could be optimized, with graph cluster randomization and the restricted-growth condition taming otherwise exponential variance. Athey, Eckles, and Imbens [4] showed that the *structure* of interference could be *tested* exactly, even for non-sharp hypotheses. And Sävje, Aronow, and Hudgens [5] showed that even naive estimators remain consistent for a meaningful — if limited — estimand under unknown interference.

Our simulation adds a cautionary coda: unbiasedness is relative to an estimand, and the estimand is relative to a possibly misspecified exposure mapping. The bias–variance frontier across clustering granularities is not a technical nuisance but the central object of experimental design under interference. The experimenter's job is no longer merely to randomize — it is to choose, deliberately, which counterfactual universes the design can see.
