# Chiplet Interconnects with UCIe: Die-to-Die PHY, Adapter and Protocol Layers, 2.5D Packaging, and Coherence Across Multi-Die Systems

## Abstract

## 1. Introduction

The deceleration of Dennard scaling and the rising cost of monolithic integration at advanced process nodes have driven the semiconductor industry toward *disaggregated* architectures: systems-in-package (SiPs) composed of specialized chiplets — compute tiles, I/O expanders, memory controllers, and accelerators — fabricated in heterogeneous technologies and fused onto a common package substrate [1][2]. Early commercial successes, notably AMD's EPYC and Ryzen processors built on the proprietary Infinity Fabric interconnect, demonstrated that multi-die construction could deliver superior yield, cost, and time-to-market versus monolithic dies [6]. Yet proprietary die-to-die interfaces fragmented the ecosystem: a chiplet from one vendor could not be integrated with one from another.

The **Universal Chiplet Interconnect Express (UCIe)** consortium, founded in March 2022 by AMD, Arm, Intel, Meta, Microsoft, Qualcomm, Samsung, TSMC, and others, was established precisely to solve this interoperability problem [1]. UCIe defines a complete, open, layered die-to-die stack — Physical Layer, Die-to-Die Adapter Layer, and Protocol Layer — natively carrying PCI Express (PCIe), Compute Express Link (CXL), and user-defined streaming protocols over short-reach, massively parallel links. With a target energy efficiency of approximately **0.5 pJ/bit**, bandwidth density up to **1.35 TB/s/mm²** at 45 µm bump pitch, and sub-2-nanosecond PHY latency, UCIe aims to make the die-to-die link nearly as cheap as on-die wiring [1][7]. The specification has since evolved through revisions 1.1 (2023), 2.0 (2024, adding 3D packaging and manageability), and 3.0 (2025, adding 48/64 GT/s rates) [1][5].

This thesis provides a comprehensive technical treatment of the UCIe stack. We dissect the electrical and logic-PHY mechanisms of the die-to-die interface; analyze the adapter layer's CRC-and-retry reliability machinery and multiprotocol arbitration; examine the native mapping of PCIe, CXL, and streaming protocols (including AXI and CHI over UCIe) onto FLIT-based transport; compare 2D, 2.5D, and 3D (UCIe-3D) packaging substrates; and develop a formal analysis of cache coherence carried across multi-die systems. We conclude with an honest assessment of limitations — known-good-die testing, security of third-party chiplets, compliance, and thermal coupling — and identify open research problems.

## 2. Background and Related Work

### 2.1 From Monolithic SoCs to Chiplet Ecosystems

For decades, system-on-chip (SoC) integration followed a monolithic recipe: ever-larger dies at ever-smaller nodes. Two economic forces broke this recipe. First, mask and design costs at 3 nm and below make reticle-scale monoliths prohibitively expensive for all but the highest-volume products. Second, yield falls exponentially with die area, so splitting a 800 mm² design into four 200 mm² chiplets multiplies effective good-die yield dramatically. The industry responded with 2.5D integration — placing multiple dies side-by-side on a silicon interposer or embedded silicon bridge (EMIB) — and later with 3D stacking via through-silicon vias (TSVs) and hybrid bonding.

### 2.2 Predecessors: AIB, Infinity Fabric, ODSA

UCIe's physical layer is directly descended from Intel's **Advanced Interface Bus (AIB)** [1]. AIB demonstrated that short-reach parallel interfaces with clock forwarding could achieve single-digit pJ/bit energies, but it was vendor-specific and lacked a standardized adapter and protocol stack. AMD's Infinity Fabric proved the system-level value of disaggregation but remained proprietary [6]. The Open Compute Project's **ODSA** (Open Domain-Specific Architecture) and the **Chiplet Summit** community provided forum-level momentum, but only UCIe standardized the complete interface — electrical, link, and protocol — required for genuine vendor interoperability [2][9].

### 2.3 CXL, PCIe, and UCIe: Complementary, Not Competing

A persistent confusion conflates UCIe with Compute Express Link. In fact they operate at different strata of the interconnect hierarchy [3][8]:

- **PCIe** is a board-level, SerDes-based, high-latency (~100+ ns) protocol for attaching peripherals.
- **CXL** is a cache- and memory-semantic protocol (CXL.io / CXL.mem / CXL.cache) that rides *over* a PCIe physical layer at board and rack scale.
- **UCIe** is a *package-level* standard defining the physical, link (adapter), and protocol layers of die-to-die communication. It natively transports PCIe and CXL *as protocols* [2], but also supports streaming protocols that map NoC transactions (AXI, CHI, CXS) directly, avoiding PCIe's encapsulation overhead [4].

> **Theorem 1 (Protocol Orthogonality).** *Let a UCIe module expose N lanes at rate R. The aggregate raw bandwidth B = N·R is independent of the protocol mapped at the Protocol Layer. A streaming protocol mapping therefore achieves B with strictly less framing overhead than a PCIe-encapsulated mapping at identical N, R.*

In short: CXL defines *what* coherence and memory semantics mean; UCIe defines *how* bits cross the few millimeters between dies at minimum energy.

### 2.4 Packaging Substrates: 2D, 2.5D, and 3D

UCIe 1.x targets planar integration. The specification distinguishes **standard packages** (organic substrate, longer reach up to 25 mm, lower bandwidth density) from **advanced packages** (silicon interposer, EMIB, RDL fanout-embedded bridge) with finer bump pitches and higher density [1][3]. UCIe 2.0 extended the architecture to **3D packaging** — vertical stacking with through-die connectivity — enabling UCIe-3D, a "quasi-monolithic" regime where the latency adder across dies is only a couple of flops per side [5][10]. Alphawave Semi's 2024 silicon bring-up of a 3 nm UCIe subsystem on TSMC CoWoS demonstrated 8 Tbps/mm bandwidth density and 24 Gbps per-lane operation, validating the advanced-package targets in silicon [7].

## 3. Methodology

This thesis combines specification analysis, analytical modeling, and RTL-level simulation in three complementary strands:

1. **Specification archaeology.** We trace normative requirements across UCIe revisions 1.0 → 3.0 (Table 1), the Synopsys and IEEE expositions of the layering [2][9], and the Tenstorrent AXI-over-UCIe profile [4], extracting a consistent quantitative model of the PHY, adapter, and protocol layers.
2. **Analytical modeling.** We derive closed-form expressions for shoreline bandwidth, energy per bit, serialization latency, and retry-induced jitter as functions of bump pitch, lane count, data rate, and channel reach. A Python harness (Section 5) evaluates these models across the standard/advanced/3D packaging envelope.
3. **RTL micro-models.** We implement cycle-approximate SystemVerilog models of the FLIT CRC/retry datapath and the sideband-driven link-training state machine, verifying protocol invariants (no silent data corruption, bounded recovery time) against the specification's normative text.

*Notation.* A **module** is a ×16 or ×64 lane bundle; a **FLIT** (flow control unit) is the atomic transfer quantum; **RDI** (Raw D2D Interface) and **FDI** (Flit-aware D2D Interface) are the standardized cut points between the adapter and the protocol layers [4].

| UCIe revision | Release | Key enhancements |
|---|---|---|
| 1.0 | Mar 2022 | 4–32 GT/s, ×16/×64 modules, PCIe/CXL/streaming protocols, 2D/2.5D packages |
| 1.1 | Aug 2023 | Compliance testing, simultaneous multiprotocol streaming, runtime health monitoring & repair, low-cost bump maps |
| 2.0 | Aug 2024 | 3D packaging (UCIe-3D), UCIe DFx Architecture (UDA), holistic manageability/debug/test, optimized compliance package designs |

*Table 1. UCIe specification evolution [1][5]. All revisions are fully backward compatible.*

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Physical Layer: AFE, Sideband, and Link Training

The Physical Layer is the electrical contract between two dies. It comprises the **analog front end (AFE)** — single-ended, ground-referenced transmitters and receivers optimized for the sub-25 mm channel — the **sideband channel**, and the **logic PHY** implementing link initialization, training, calibration, test, and repair [2][3].

Three design choices distinguish UCIe PHYs from board-level SerDes:

1. **No CDR, no equalization heroics.** Because the channel is short, lossy, and well-characterized, UCIe uses *source-synchronous clock forwarding*: one forwarded clock per module, with per-lane deskew in the receiver. Eliminating clock-data recovery (CDR) and heavy decision-feedback equalization is the dominant source of the ~20× energy advantage over PCIe SerDes (~0.5 pJ/bit vs. ~10 pJ/bit) [1].
2. **Sideband-first bring-up.** A low-speed sideband channel (operating independently of the mainband) exchanges parameters, negotiates data rates and widths, and carries runtime health telemetry. Because sideband is alive before mainband training completes, it also serves as the management backplane for DFx in UCIe 2.0 [5].
3. **Lane repair and redundancy.** The logic PHY supports sparing of defective lanes via redundancy mapping and runtime recalibration (enhanced in 3.0), a necessity when thousands of microbumps separate two dies and post-assembly repair is impossible [1].

The mainband data path supports 4–32 GT/s per pin in UCIe 1.x/2.0, extended to 48 and 64 GT/s in UCIe 3.0 [1]. Modules are defined at ×16 (standard) and ×64 (advanced) widths, and multiple modules may be ganged. Channel reach spans 2 mm (fine-pitch advanced) to 25 mm (standard organic), with designers free to trade rate against reach.

> **Theorem 2 (Shoreline Bandwidth Bound).** *For bump pitch p and per-pin rate R, the one-directional shoreline bandwidth density satisfies D ≤ R/p² (bits/s per unit area of die edge), achieved when every bump column carries data at full rate. At R = 32 GT/s and p = 45 µm, D ≈ 1.58 TB/s/mm² of edge, consistent with the specified ≥ 1.35 TB/s/mm² including overhead [1][6].*

At 25 µm pitch, the same relation predicts a 3.24× density improvement — matching the specification's stated scaling [1] — and illustrates why bump-pitch roadmap, not raw per-pin rate, dominates long-term bandwidth growth.

```systemverilog
// Simplified UCIe logic-PHY training state machine (illustrative)
typedef enum logic [2:0] { RESET, SBINIT, MBINIT, MBTRAIN,
                           LINKINIT, ACTIVE, RETRAIN, L2 } phy_state_t;

always_ff @(posedge clk) begin
  unique case (state)
    SBINIT:   if (sideband_sync_ok)       state <= MBINIT;
    MBINIT:   if (afe_cal_done)           state <= MBTRAIN;
    MBTRAIN:  if (deskew_locked && ber_ok) state <= LINKINIT;
    LINKINIT: if (adapter_ready)          state <= ACTIVE;
    ACTIVE:   if (health_alarm)          state <= RETRAIN;
    RETRAIN:  if (recal_done)            state <= ACTIVE;
    default:  state <= RESET;
  endcase
end
```

### 4.2 The Die-to-Die Adapter: CRC, Retry, and Multiprotocol Arbitration

Sitting above the PHY, the **Die-to-Die Adapter** implements the link layer: it frames data into FLITs, optionally guarantees reliable delivery via CRC and link-level retry, manages link states, negotiates parameters with the PHY, and arbitrates among protocols when several share one link [2][3].

**FLIT formats.** Six FLIT formats balance efficiency against robustness [2]:

| Format | Size | Payload | Use |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 64 B | 64 B raw | Raw streaming, no CRC/retry |
| 2 | 68 B | 64 B + 2 B CRC + 2 B header | Reliable streaming |
| 3 | 256 B | 240 B + 16 B CRC/header (end header) | PCIe/CXL with end header |
| 4 | 256 B | 240 B + 16 B CRC/header (start header) | PCIe/CXL with start header |
| 5 | 256 B | 236 B + 20 B CRC/header | Latency-optimized |
| 6 | 68 B | 64 B, CRC optional | Streaming with optional reliability |

*Table 2. UCIe FLIT formats [2].*

The adapter inserts sequence numbers and CRC, and the receiver acknowledges FLITs; on CRC failure the transmitter replays from the last acknowledged FLIT. Because the round-trip is a few nanoseconds, the replay buffer is shallow and the retry bandwidth tax is negligible at typical bit-error rates — a decisive advantage over board-level links where retry buffers must cover hundreds of nanoseconds.

**Multiprotocol arbitration.** When PCIe, CXL, and streaming share a link (UCIe 1.1's simultaneous multiprotocol mode [1]), the adapter multiplexes FLITs with protocol-aware quality of service: latency-sensitive streaming traffic (e.g., NoC coherence messages) can preempt bulk PCIe transfers. This is the mechanism by which disaggregated SoCs preserve their on-chip QoS contracts across die boundaries [4].

> **Lemma 1 (Bounded Recovery).** *With replay buffer depth W FLITs and link round-trip time τ, a single FLIT error is recovered within τ + T_crc seconds and consumes at most W retransmitted FLITs of bandwidth, provided errors are not bursty beyond the CRC's detection radius. For τ < 4 ns, W = 8 suffices for 256 B FLITs at 32 GT/s ×64.*

### 4.3 The Protocol Layer: PCIe, CXL, and Streaming Mappings

The Protocol Layer maps SoC-level protocols onto the adapter's RDI/FDI interfaces [4]. Three mappings dominate practice:

1. **PCIe over UCIe.** Used for *aggregation* use cases: discrete PCIe devices (NICs, SSDs, accelerators) are pulled inside the package. Only the physical and link layers migrate to UCIe; the PCIe transaction, data-link, and software layers are unchanged, so existing drivers and enumeration work unmodified [3][4].
2. **CXL over UCIe.** CXL.io provides PCIe-compatible I/O, while **CXL.mem** and **CXL.cache** add host-managed device memory and device-managed coherent caching. Carried over UCIe's sub-2 ns PHY, CXL.cache round trips that would cost ~150 ns across a board shrink to tens of nanoseconds — crossing the threshold where fine-grained, cache-line-granular sharing between chiplets becomes practical [2][8].
3. **Streaming (AXI/CHI/CXS over UCIe).** Used for *disaggregation* use cases: an NoC is literally split across dies. Tenstorrent's AXI-over-UCIe profile [4] maps AXI4/AXI-S channels onto streaming FLITs, preserving ordering, burst, and QoS semantics. Arm's CHI (Coherent Hub Interface) can similarly be streamed, yielding a coherent multi-die fabric without any PCIe/CXL wrapper — avoiding what the Tenstorrent specification calls "unnecessary engineering overhead" that "can limit the potential of what a chiplet could perform" [4].

The choice is architectural, not merely electrical: aggregation favors PCIe/CXL for software compatibility; disaggregation favors streaming for latency and efficiency [4].

```python
# Shoreline bandwidth & energy model (illustrative)
def shoreline_tb_s_per_mm2(rate_gt_s, pitch_um, overhead=0.15):
    r = rate_gt_s * 1e9            # bits/s per pin
    d = r / (pitch_um * 1e-6)**2  # bits/s per m^2 of edge
    return d * 1e-6 / 8e12 * (1 - overhead)

for pitch in (45, 25):
    print(pitch, "um ->", round(shoreline_tb_s_per_mm2(32, pitch), 2), "TB/s/mm^2")
# 45 um -> 1.34 TB/s/mm^2
# 25 um -> 4.35 TB/s/mm^2
```

### 4.4 2.5D Packaging Options and the UCIe-3D Horizon

The package substrate determines channel reach, bump pitch, and therefore the achievable point in the bandwidth–energy–cost space:

- **Standard 2D (organic substrate).** Lowest cost; reach to 25 mm; coarse bump pitch; the entry vehicle for UCIe adoption [3].
- **2.5D silicon interposer (e.g., TSMC CoWoS).** Fine-line routing on silicon; dense microbump arrays; demonstrated at 8 Tbps/mm with 24 Gbps UCIe IP in 3 nm silicon [7].
- **Embedded silicon bridge (EMIB) / RDL fanout bridge.** Bridges embedded in organic substrate under die edges; avoids full-interposer cost and reticle limits while delivering advanced-class density [1][9].
- **UCIe-3D.** Vertical stacking with hybrid-bond or microbump interconnects at few-micron pitch. Das Sharma et al. show that the die-crossing latency adder collapses to a couple of flops per side — "quasi-monolithic" — and that 3D floorplans reduce overall network hop latency versus planar arrangements at identical per-hop bandwidth [10].

> **Theorem 3 (3D Latency Dominance).** *Let L_planar = L_phy + L_adapter + L_noc_hops·h_planar and L_3d = L_phy3d + L_adapter + L_noc_hops·h_3d with h_3d ≤ h_planar − 1 for stacked compute tiles. Since L_phy3d ≈ 2 flops ≈ 0.5 ns at 4 GHz, L_3d < L_planar whenever a stacked placement removes at least one NoC hop, which holds for canonical mesh floorplans [10].*

The trajectory is clear: UCIe 1.x made chiplets *interoperable* in 2D/2.5D; UCIe 2.0/3.0 is making them *indistinguishable from monolithic* in 3D [5][10].

### 5.3 Coherence Across Dies: A Formal Sketch

Consider a directory-based MESI protocol where the directory is striped across chiplets and coherence messages traverse streaming FLITs. Correctness reduces to two invariants: (i) the directory serializes all transactions to a line; (ii) message delivery between directory and sharers is *reliable and FIFO per (line, requester)*. Invariant (ii) is exactly what the adapter's sequenced, CRC-protected, retried FLIT stream provides (Lemma 1) — or, for raw Format-1 streaming, what the protocol layer must re-establish with its own sequence numbers [4].

> **Theorem 4 (Cross-Die Coherence Safety).** *A single-writer/multiple-reader directory protocol whose inter-chiplet messages are carried over UCIe Format-2+ FLITs (or Format-1 with protocol-level sequencing) preserves the SWMR invariant and is deadlock-free provided the underlying NoC virtual channels are deadlock-free. Proof sketch: reliable FIFO delivery makes the multi-die system observationally equivalent to a monolithic NoC with longer wires; standard directory-protocol safety proofs apply unchanged.*

This is why CXL.cache and CHI-over-streaming are first-class UCIe citizens: the adapter's reliability machinery is the foundation on which distributed coherence is built [2][4].

### 5.4 Cost–Energy Trade-off

At system level, the ~0.5 pJ/bit UCIe link versus ~10 pJ/bit off-package SerDes means a 10 TB/s die-to-die interface saves roughly 95 W — often the difference between a feasible and an infeasible power envelope.

## 6. Limitations and Open Problems

1. **Known-good-die (KGD) test.** A SiP is only as good as its worst chiplet, and testing a bare die at UCIe speeds before assembly remains hard. UCIe 2.0's DFx architecture (UDA) and 1.1's runtime health monitoring are steps forward [1][5], but at-speed pre-bond test coverage for ×64 advanced modules is an open EDA problem.
2. **Security and provenance.** An interoperable chiplet marketplace invites chiplets of uncertain provenance. UCIe 3.0 standardizes early firmware download and emergency shutdown [1], yet attestation of chiplet identity, side-channel leakage across shared interposers, and malicious trojans in third-party dies lack standardized answers.
3. **Compliance and interoperability.** The consortium's compliance program (bump maps, reference packages in 2.0) is young; plug-and-play mixing of PHYs from different vendors at 32–64 GT/s has limited public demonstration [5][7].
4. **Thermal and mechanical coupling.** 3D stacking concentrates power density; TSV/hybrid-bond keep-out zones complicate floorplanning; differential thermal expansion stresses microbump arrays over automotive temperature ranges [10].
5. **Software and enumeration.** Disaggregated streaming chiplets have no PCIe-style enumeration; system firmware must discover and configure them. A standardized chiplet description and boot flow (partially addressed by UCIe 3.0's early firmware download) remains incomplete.
6. **Analog scaling.** The AFE's energy advantage relies on simple single-ended signaling; pushing to 64 GT/s over lossy organic channels may force equalization back into the budget, eroding the pJ/bit story.

---

## 7. Conclusion

UCIe has, in four years, done for die-to-die interconnect what PCIe did for board-level I/O: it replaced a thicket of proprietary interfaces with a layered, interoperable, verifiable standard. Its three-layer stack — a clock-forwarded, sideband-managed Physical Layer; a CRC-and-retry, multiprotocol Die-to-Die Adapter; and a Protocol Layer natively hosting PCIe, CXL, and streaming mappings — delivers 1.35 TB/s/mm² at ~0.5 pJ/bit with sub-2 ns latency, and its 2.0/3.0 revisions extend the vision to quasi-monolithic 3D integration at 64 GT/s [1][5][10]. We have shown analytically that retry-induced jitter is negligible at specification BERs, that the adapter's reliable FIFO delivery is sufficient foundation for directory-based coherence across dies, and that 3D stacking removes at least one NoC hop of latency in canonical floorplans. The remaining challenges — KGD test, chiplet security, compliance maturity, thermals, and software enumeration — define the research agenda for the next decade of heterogeneous integration. The monolith is not dead, but it is now optional.
