# Stratospheric Aerosol Injection: Radiative Physics, Termination Shock, and the Governance of Solar Geoengineering

## Abstract

Stratospheric aerosol injection (SAI) is the most studied solar radiation modification technique: the deliberate injection of reflective aerosol into the lower stratosphere to offset greenhouse gas warming. This thesis synthesizes the radiative physics of sulfate and engineered solid aerosols, the microphysical and chemical constraints that bound injection efficiency, and the climate-system consequences revealed by the Geoengineering Model Intercomparison Project (GeoMIP) and the Geoengineering Large Ensemble (GLENS). We analyze the multi-objective feedback control architecture developed by MacMartin, Kravitz, and collaborators, which tailors injection latitudes to manage the global mean temperature, the interhemispheric gradient, and the equator-to-pole gradient simultaneously. We quantify termination shock — the rapid rebound of temperature following abrupt cessation — documented across eleven climate models, and show why sustained deployment creates a commitment analogous to the emissions problem it purports to solve. Finally, we examine the governance landscape, from the 2021 National Academies report to proposals for an international non-use agreement, and the suspended SCoPEx field experiment. SAI emerges as physically capable of fast cooling, yet bound by diminishing radiative returns, asymmetric hydrological side effects, and profound governance risk.

##1 Intro

The climate intervention landscape changed permanently in 2006, when Paul Crutzen — a Nobel laureate in atmospheric chemistry — published a calculation in *Climatic Change* arguing that injecting sulfur into the stratosphere to enhance planetary albedo deserved serious scientific attention [1]. The proposal was deliberately framed as a hedge against policy failure: a "contribution to resolve a policy dilemma," in the paper's own title. Crutzen estimated that roughly 5.3 billion kilograms of sulfur per year could offset the radiative forcing of a doubled carbon dioxide atmosphere, and he closed by noting that the best outcome would be emissions reductions sufficient to make the experiment unnecessary — a wish he himself rated as "pious."

Two decades later, SAI has become the centerpiece of the solar geoengineering research program. It is the only intervention for which the atmosphere has already run a full-scale, unplanned experiment: the 1991 eruption of Mount Pinatubo injected roughly 20 million tonnes of sulfur dioxide into the tropical stratosphere, producing a sulfate aerosol veil that cooled the global surface by about half a degree Celsius for over a year, and measurable effects for roughly three [1]. This natural analogue both grounds the physical plausibility of SAI and disciplines the most inflated claims about it, because the eruption also revealed the aerosol veil's limitations: transient lifetime, ozone depletion, stratospheric heating, and altered precipitation patterns.

The contemporary research program has moved far beyond the single-latitude thought experiment. Three intellectual strands now define the field. **Radiative physics and microphysics** determine how much cooling each tonne of injected precursor actually buys, and why that price rises with the injection rate. **Control theory** — applied by Doug MacMartin, Ben Kravitz, and collaborators — treats the planet as a system with multiple climate objectives and multiple injection-latitude actuators, asking what combinations of targets are jointly achievable [3][7]. **Governance theory** confronts the uncomfortable corollary of success: a technology that cools fast, must be maintained for as long as carbon accumulates, and can produce catastrophic rebound if interrupted, is a political artifact as much as a physical one [5][6].

This thesis proceeds as follows. Section 2 surveys the radiative and chemical physics of stratospheric aerosols. Section 3 describes the multi-objective feedback control methodology. Section 4 examines four deep questions: microphysical efficiency limits, ozone and dynamical side effects, the physics of termination shock, and governance. Section 5 reports the empirical record from GeoMIP and GLENS. Section 6 maps the principal limitations, and Section 7 concludes.

##2 Background

### The radiative mechanism

The physics of SAI is disarmingly simple and its consequences are not. Sulfur dioxide injected at altitudes of 20 to 25 kilometers oxidizes over weeks to sulfuric acid vapor, which nucleates and condenses into a persistent layer of sulfate aerosol droplets roughly half a micron in diameter [3]. Particles of this size scatter incoming solar radiation in the Mie regime with high efficiency while absorbing comparatively little terrestrial longwave radiation. The net effect is a reduction in absorbed shortwave flux — a negative radiative forcing on the order of −2 watts per square meter for the benchmark scenarios that would roughly offset a doubling of preindustrial CO2 [2].

The advantage of the stratosphere over the troposphere is dynamical persistence. Tropospheric sulfate aerosols — the familiar cooling agent of industrial pollution — are scavenged by precipitation within days to weeks. Stratospheric particles, residing above the weather, sediment slowly and are removed on timescales of one to two years, giving a small annual injection a large integrated radiative effect. This persistence is precisely what makes SAI an efficient climate lever and precisely what makes it a commitment device: the lever works only while it is held.

### The volcanic analogue

Mount Pinatubo's eruption remains the field's Rosetta Stone. The stratospheric sulfate veil reduced global-mean surface temperature by approximately 0.5 degrees Celsius, enhanced diffuse sunlight at the surface, measurably reduced global column ozone by about 2.5 percent, and warmed the tropical lower stratosphere by several degrees through aerosol absorption of longwave radiation [1]. Volcanic analogues also demonstrate the scaling problem at the heart of SAI: Pinatubo injected its sulfur in a single pulse, while SAI requires a sustained, precisely maintained injection rate for decades to centuries — a regime of continuous perturbation with no natural precedent.

### Aerosol chemistry and the ozone constraint

The most salient direct risk of sulfate-based SAI is stratospheric ozone loss [2]. Sulfuric acid aerosol accelerates ozone destruction through heterogeneous chemistry: reactions such as HCl plus ClONO2 proceeding on cold aqueous aerosol surfaces shift halogens from reservoir species into catalytically active compounds, while hydrolysis of N2O5 alters the nitrogen oxide budget. Model studies of the benchmark −2 watt-per-square-meter forcing find average column ozone reductions of 1 to 13 percent, with the largest relative losses in the polar spring [2]. Because anthropogenic chlorine is declining under the Montreal Protocol, future ozone losses from a given sulfate loading will be smaller than Pinatubo's, but a multi-decade sulfate commitment still prolongs the recovery of the ozone hole by decades [1].

This chemistry is the primary motivation for **engineered solid aerosols**. David Keith and Debra Weisenstein proposed injecting alkaline metal salts — for example, calcite or alumina particles — whose surfaces scavenge the acids that drive ozone loss, enabling radiative forcing with minimal stratospheric heating and potentially even ozone restoration [2]. The concept remains theoretically elegant but experimentally unproven: solid particles may aggregate, sediment differently, and interact with stratospheric water vapor in ways that sulfate's extensive volcanic record constrains far better.

### From solar dimming to designed experiments

The Geoengineering Model Intercomparison Project standardized early SAI simulations, most of them using the computationally cheap trick of simply reducing the solar constant — the so-called G1 through G4 experiments [4]. These idealized runs established the field's foundational results: global-mean temperature can be restored, but precipitation and the hydrological cycle cannot be simultaneously corrected, because solar forcing and greenhouse forcing have different vertical and latitudinal structures.

##3 Methodology

### The control-theoretic formulation

The methodological core of modern SAI research is the inversion of the traditional simulation question. Rather than injecting a fixed amount and observing the outcome, the "design experiment" approach specifies climate objectives first and lets a feedback controller compute the injection schedule required to meet them [7]. Formally, define the global-mean surface temperature T0, the interhemispheric temperature gradient T1, and the equator-to-pole gradient T2, each relative to a reference climate. Injection occurs at four latitudes (30S, 15S, 15N, 30N), producing aerosol optical depth modes l0, l1, l2 analogous to the temperature decomposition. A proportional-integral controller adjusts the annual injection at each latitude from the observed errors:

> **Theorem:** *Let the linearized climate response map injection-rate adjustments to the three temperature objectives with full row rank. Then a proportional-integral feedback controller with sufficiently small gains stabilizes the system about the target state, rejecting both model bias and interannual variability, provided the actuation constraints admit a feasible solution.*

##4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Microphysics: why the radiative price of cooling rises with dose

The relationship between injected sulfur mass and realized radiative forcing is **sublinear**, and the reasons are microphysical. As the injection rate increases, particle number concentrations rise, and coagulation accelerates: small, optically efficient particles collide and merge into larger droplets that scatter sunlight less efficiently per unit mass and sediment out faster. Model studies consistently find that the forcing per unit injection falls as the annual injection climbs toward the tens of teragrams demanded by high-emissions scenarios [8].

### 4.3 Termination shock: the physics of rebound



> **Theorem:** *For a climate with equilibrium sensitivity S and aerosol veil lifetime tau of order one year, termination of an injection maintaining forcing F produces an initial warming rate approximately F divided by (S times the thermal inertia timescale), which exceeds the concurrent greenhouse-driven warming rate whenever the masked forcing exceeds the forcing accumulated during the veil's own decay time. Since the veil decays in years while CO2 accumulates over centuries, this condition holds for any sustained, large-scale deployment.*



### 4.4 Governance: from the National Academies to the non-use movement





1. **Coordinated research governance** — the NASEM model: transparent, internationally inclusive research with staged decision gates. Fragile to geopolitical fracture.
2. **Unilateral deployment** — a single state or coalition, facing climate damages, begins injection. Technically feasible with existing aircraft; politically explosive; creates termination-shock hostage dynamics.
3. **Prohibitory regime** — a non-use norm or treaty. Plausibly effective against states, weakly effective against non-state actors, and in tension with the research needed to justify it.

##5 Empirical Results and Proofs

### The GeoMIP record

The Geoengineering Model Intercomparison Project remains the empirical backbone of SAI assessment. Its key experiments and findings:

- **G1 (instant 4xCO2, solar dimming to balance):** global-mean temperature restored, but precipitation reduced by roughly 4 percent relative to the control — the canonical demonstration that temperature and hydrology cannot be jointly restored with a single global knob [4].
- **G2 (1 percent per year CO2 ramp, solar dimming for 50 years, then termination):** eleven models, unanimous rapid post-termination warming; consensus on faster high-latitude and land warming; disagreement on regional precipitation patterns and on net primary productivity [4].
- **G3/G4 (RCP4.5 with gradual or fixed sulfate injection):** fixed-injection experiments showing that aerosol-based SAI reproduces the qualitative temperature response of solar dimming but adds ozone and heating effects that dimming cannot capture.

### The GLENS design experiments

The GLENS ensemble — twenty members of CESM1-WACCM with interactive chemistry and a feedback controller — delivered the field's most policy-relevant empirical result: simultaneous control of T0, T1, and T2 is achievable with four-latitude SO2 injection, holding all three near 2020 levels through 2100 under RCP8.5 [7]. The cost: injection rising to approximately 50 teragrams of SO2 per year by 2099. The same ensemble documented the stratospheric dynamical response — polar vortex changes, Quasi-Biennial Oscillation disruption, ozone feedbacks — establishing that the side-effect profile of realistic injection differs qualitatively from solar-dimming proxies.

### Altitude and efficiency experiments

Comparing high-altitude (23–25 km) and low-altitude (19–20 km) injection strategies under identical climate objectives showed that higher injection is more radiatively efficient per unit mass but produces larger stratospheric heating and stronger dynamical perturbations — a direct empirical mapping of the efficiency-versus-side-effect tradeoff surface [7].

### A formal bound on termination rate

Let delta_T(t) be the global-mean temperature anomaly, F_mask the masked radiative forcing at termination, tau the veil lifetime (~1.5 years), and C the effective ocean heat capacity. Immediately after termination, the aerosol forcing decays as F(t) ≈ F_mask times e to the power of negative t over tau, while the greenhouse forcing continues its slow climb. The initial warming rate is bounded below by:

```
d(delta_T)/dt |_{t=0+}  ≥  (F_mask / λ) × (1/τ_eff)  −  dF_GHG/dt / λ
```

where λ is the climate feedback parameter and τ_eff the effective thermal response time. For F_mask of several watts per square meter — the magnitude of a serious deployment — the first term dominates, yielding post-termination warming rates of order **0.2 to 0.5 degrees Celsius per year**, several times the maximum sustained rates of the underlying greenhouse trajectory [4][5]. This is not a model artifact; it is an energy-balance necessity. Any sustained veil that meaningfully offsets warming, when removed, converts stored ocean heat uptake into surface warming at the veil's decay rate.

A minimal numerical demonstration of the rebound dynamics, using a two-box energy balance model, illustrates the asymmetry between the slow greenhouse climb and the fast veil collapse:

```rust
/// Two-box energy balance model with terminating aerosol veil.
struct Climate {
    t_mixed: f64,   // mixed-layer temperature anomaly (K)
    t_deep: f64,    // deep-ocean temperature anomaly (K)
    c_mixed: f64,   // mixed-layer heat capacity (W yr m^-2 K^-1)
    c_deep: f64,    // deep-ocean heat capacity
    lambda: f64,    // feedback parameter (W m^-2 K^-1)
    gamma: f64,     // inter-box exchange coefficient
}

impl Climate {
    fn step(&mut self, f_ghg: f64, f_aero: f64, dt: f64) {
        let forcing = f_ghg + f_aero;
        let d_mixed = (forcing - self.lambda * self.t_mixed
            - self.gamma * (self.t_mixed - self.t_deep)) / self.c_mixed;
        let d_deep = self.gamma * (self.t_mixed - self.t_deep) / self.c_deep;
        self.t_mixed += d_mixed * dt;
        self.t_deep += d_deep * dt;
    }
}

fn main() {
    let mut c = Climate { t_mixed: 0.0, t_deep: 0.0,
        c_mixed: 8.0, c_deep: 100.0, lambda: 1.2, gamma: 0.7 };
    let dt = 0.1;
    // 50 years of masked warming: GHG forcing ramps to 4 W/m^2,
    // aerosol veil holds net forcing near zero.
    for year in 0..500 {
        let f_ghg = 4.0 * (year as f64) / 500.0;
        c.step(f_ghg, -f_ghg, dt);
    }
    // Termination: veil decays with ~1.5 yr lifetime.
    let mut veil = -4.0;
    for _ in 0..100 {
        veil *= (-dt / 1.5_f64).exp();
        c.step(4.0, veil, dt);
    }
    println!("post-termination anomaly: {:.2} K", c.t_mixed);
}
```

Running this model reproduces the qualitative GeoMIP result: decades of suppressed warming followed by a rebound whose peak decadal rate exceeds anything in the ramp phase — the signature of termination shock [4].

##6 Limitations

**Model structural uncertainty.** The GeoMIP consensus on temperature rebound is strong, but agreement collapses for regional precipitation, net primary productivity, and monsoon responses [4]. The models share structural assumptions about aerosol microphysics that the SCoPEx-class experiments were meant to test — and that testing has not happened.

**Microphysical extrapolation.** Coagulation, sedimentation, and particle-size evolution at sustained injection rates of tens of teragrams per year are extrapolated from volcanic pulses that lasted months, not decades. The sublinearity of forcing with injection rate is robust in sign but uncertain in magnitude — the difference between a viable and an unviable program at high forcing [8].

**The solar-dimming proxy problem.** Much of the foundational literature, including the G2 termination experiments, used reduced solar constant rather than interactive aerosol [4]. Interactive-aerosol runs show additional stratospheric heating and dynamical effects, so dimming-based risk estimates are lower bounds.

**Governance intractability.** The control theory assumes a single rational controller with stable objectives across decades. The real system has ~200 sovereign controllers with divergent objectives, no enforcement mechanism, and the demonstrated ability to halt even sub-scale experiments [6]. Termination shock converts this institutional fragmentation into a physical weapon: the threat to stop injecting is the threat to impose rapid warming.

**Irreversibility of commitment.** SAI does nothing to the carbon stock. Every year of deployment under continued emissions deepens the masked forcing and raises the termination penalty — a ratchet that the 2021 Academies report flagged as the technology's most distinctive hazard [5][6].

**Moral hazard.** Empirical evidence on whether SAI research weakens emissions mitigation is mixed, but the political economy is clear: a visible "plan B" alters the bargaining equilibrium of climate negotiations, and not in the direction of ambition.

##7 Conclusion

Stratospheric aerosol injection is the rare climate intervention that is simultaneously physically plausible, quantitatively understood in its first-order effects, and politically alarming in its second-order ones. The radiative physics works: a maintained stratospheric veil of sulfate or engineered aerosol cools the surface quickly, at a forcing per unit mass that volcanic history constrains and interactive-chemistry models reproduce [1][2][3]. The control theory works: multi-objective feedback controllers can hold global-mean temperature, the interhemispheric gradient, and the equator-to-pole gradient near reference levels, within a design space bounded by the geometry of non-negative aerosol optical depth [7][8].

But the three great objections survive every refinement. The efficiency of injection declines with dose, so the price of offsetting high emissions rises superlinearly. The hydrological cycle cannot be jointly restored with temperature, and regional precipitation — the variable that determines agricultural viability — is the least certain and least controllable output [4][8]. And termination shock converts the program's greatest strength, its speed, into its greatest danger: a sustained veil is a stored warming debt that comes due at the veil's decay rate, in years rather than centuries, if the program is ever interrupted [4][5].

Crutzen's 2006 paper ended with a plea that emissions reductions make the experiment unnecessary, and a fear that they would not [1]. The subsequent two decades of research have sharpened both horns of his dilemma: we now know SAI could cool the planet, and we now know more precisely what it would cost — in ozone, in monsoons, in governance, and in the hostage logic of termination. The responsible reading of the evidence is the one the National Academies adopted: research the physics and the governance in parallel, govern the research before it scales, and never mistake the ability to mask warming for the ability to manage it [6].
