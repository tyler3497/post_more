# The Neural Tangent Kernel and the Lazy–Rich Divide: Infinite-Width Dynamics, Feature Learning, and Finite-Width Corrections

## Abstract

Modern deep learning theory is organized around a fundamental dichotomy between the *lazy regime*, in which wide networks train as fixed kernels, and the *rich regime*, in which internal representations actively evolve. This thesis develops the mathematics of the *Neural Tangent Kernel* introduced by Jacot, Gabriel, and Hongler [1], proves its constancy in the infinite-width limit, and derives the kernel gradient flow governing function-space training. We contrast this with the *lazy-training* formalism of Chizat and Bach [2], which traces laziness to scaling rather than over-parameterization, and the *Tensor Programs* dichotomy of Yang and Hu [3], which shows infinite-width limits admit either kernel dynamics or feature learning — never both. We then analyze the large-learning-rate *catapult mechanism* of Lewkowycz et al. [4], where gradient descent escapes laziness by transiently destabilizing the NTK, and discuss finite-width corrections in which NTK evolution enables feature learning, closing with empirical evidence and open problems.

## 1 Introduction

A central mystery of deep learning is why gradient descent on a highly non-convex objective reliably finds solutions that generalize. Early theories appealed to the non-convexity itself — the complexity of the loss landscape, the implicit regularization of stochastic gradient descent, or the dynamics of representation learning. The *Neural Tangent Kernel* (NTK) program, initiated by Jacot, Gabriel, and Hongler in 2018 [1], offered a strikingly different perspective: in the limit of infinite width, a neural network trained by gradient descent behaves exactly like a *linear model* in a fixed feature space, and the non-convex parameter-space dynamics become convex kernel gradient descent in function space.

This linearization is not merely an approximation — it is asymptotically exact. Lee et al. [5] showed that wide networks of any depth evolve as linear models under gradient descent, with predictions converging to those of a Gaussian process posterior. The theory explained several phenomena at once: why sufficiently wide networks converge to global minima, why early stopping is effective, and why the training dynamics decompose along the eigenfunctions of a data-dependent kernel.

Yet the NTK theory also produced a puzzle. If wide networks are just kernel machines, why do real neural networks outperform classical kernels on representation-dependent tasks like vision and language modeling? Chizat and Bach [2] gave the conceptual answer: the lazy behavior is *not* an inevitable consequence of over-parameterization, but of an implicit choice of scaling. Yang and Hu [3] proved the sharp dichotomy: any natural infinite-width scaling either yields kernel gradient descent or allows feature learning, never both simultaneously. And Lewkowycz et al. [4] demonstrated that large learning rates push networks out of the lazy regime entirely, via the *catapult mechanism*, where the NTK itself undergoes transient instability and permanent restructuring.

This thesis synthesizes these threads into a unified account. Our contributions are:

1. A self-contained derivation of the NTK, its infinite-width constancy, and the associated kernel gradient flow with closed-form least-squares solutions.
2. A comparative analysis of the lazy and rich regimes, formalized through scaling arguments (Chizat–Bach) and the Tensor Programs dichotomy (Yang–Hu), including the maximal update parameterization (μP).
3. An exposition of the catapult mechanism and finite-width corrections, where NTK evolution is the *mechanism* of feature learning.
4. A critical discussion of limitations: kernel lower bounds, the gap between lazy and practical performance, and open questions.

> **Theorem:** In the infinite-width limit under the NTK parameterization, gradient flow on the squared loss reduces to kernel gradient descent with a time-independent kernel, converging exponentially fast at a rate determined by the smallest positive eigenvalue of the limiting NTK Gram matrix [1].

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Networks as differentiable programs

Consider a feedforward network $f(x; \theta): \mathbb{R}^{d} \to \mathbb{R}$ with parameters $\theta \in \mathbb{R}^{P}$. Under gradient flow with learning rate $\eta$,

$$\frac{d\theta}{dt} = -\eta \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}(\theta),$$

where $\mathcal{L}(\theta) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \ell(f(x_i; \theta), y_i)$ is the empirical risk. By the chain rule, the *function-space* dynamics are

$$\frac{df(x; \theta_t)}{dt} = \left\langle \nabla_\theta f(x; \theta_t), \frac{d\theta_t}{dt} \right\rangle = -\eta \sum_{i=1}^{n} \Theta_t(x, x_i)\, \ell'(f(x_i; \theta_t), y_i),$$

where the **empirical neural tangent kernel** is defined as [1]

$$\Theta_t(x, x') = \left\langle \nabla_\theta f(x; \theta_t), \nabla_\theta f(x'; \theta_t) \right\rangle.$$

This object is the heart of the theory: it measures how an infinitesimal parameter update changes the function's output at different inputs, coupling the dynamics across the training set.

### 2.2 Gaussian process limit at initialization

A prerequisite result, due to Neal (1996) and extended by Lee et al. (2018) and Matthews et al. (2018), states that a randomly initialized network converges in distribution to a Gaussian process as hidden widths tend to infinity. If $W^{(\ell)} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma_w^2 / n_{\ell-1})$ and biases $b^{(\ell)} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma_b^2)$, then for fixed inputs the preactivations at layer $\ell$ are jointly Gaussian with covariance given recursively by

$$\Sigma^{(\ell)}(x, x') = \sigma_w^2\, \mathbb{E}_{(u,v) \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \Sigma^{(\ell-1)})}[\phi(u)\phi(v)] + \sigma_b^2.$$

This is the *NNGP kernel*. The NTK theory asks: does an analogous deterministic limit govern the *gradients* — and therefore the training dynamics?

| Limit | Object | Behavior under training |
|---|---|---|
| NNGP ($\Sigma$) | Function prior at init | Static (describes initialization only) |
| NTK ($\Theta$) | Gradient inner products | Static at infinite width; evolves at finite width |
| Mean-field | Particle distribution over neurons | Evolves via PDE (rich regime) |
| μP limit | Maximal feature learning | Features evolve; kernel evolves |

### 2.3 Parameterization conventions

The scaling of weights and learning rates with width determines the limit. In the **NTK parameterization**, each layer computes $h^{(\ell)} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{n_{\ell-1}}} W^{(\ell)} a^{(\ell-1)}$ with $W^{(\ell)}_{ij} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$; the $1/\sqrt{n}$ factor keeps forward variances $O(1)$ and, crucially, makes parameter gradients $O(1/\sqrt{n})$ per coordinate so that the summed kernel contribution is $O(1)$. In the **standard parameterization** (LeCun/He init), the same scaling appears inside the weight variance. The choice matters enormously for the *training* limit, as Yang and Hu showed [3].

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 Derivation of the NTK limit

Fix a depth-$L$ network in NTK parameterization. The empirical NTK decomposes across layers:

$$\hat{\Theta}^{(L)}_t(x, x') = \sum_{\ell=1}^{L} \left\langle \nabla_{W^{(\ell)}} f(x; \theta_t), \nabla_{W^{(\ell)}} f(x'; \theta_t) \right\rangle + \text{(bias terms)}.$$

Each term is a sum of $n_{\ell-1} \times n_\ell$ weakly correlated random variables. As $\min_\ell n_\ell \to \infty$, the law of large numbers drives each layer's contribution to a deterministic limit. Jacot et al. [1] proved the recursive characterization:

$$\Theta^{(1)}(x, x') = \Sigma^{(1)}(x, x'), \qquad \Theta^{(\ell)}(x, x') = \Theta^{(\ell-1)}(x, x') \cdot \dot{\Sigma}^{(\ell)}(x, x') + \Sigma^{(\ell)}(x, x'),$$

where $\dot{\Sigma}^{(\ell)}(x, x') = \sigma_w^2\, \mathbb{E}[\dot{\phi}(u)\dot{\phi}(v)]$ is the derivative covariance. The first term propagates gradient information from deeper layers; the second term, $\Sigma^{(\ell)}$, is the fresh contribution of layer $\ell$ itself.

### 3.2 Constancy during training

The deeper result of [1] is that the empirical NTK *stays constant during training* in the infinite-width limit. The argument: under gradient flow with $O(1)$ learning rates, each parameter moves only $O(1/\sqrt{n})$ over the entire training trajectory, because the total change in any coordinate is bounded by the integrated gradient, which scales as $1/\sqrt{n}$. Hence

$$\sup_{t \geq 0} \|\hat{\Theta}_t - \Theta\| \xrightarrow{p} 0 \quad \text{as } n \to \infty,$$

uniformly over compact training horizons (extended to infinite horizon under positive-definiteness by later work [5]). Parameters barely move, yet the *function* moves substantially — this is the defining signature of the lazy regime.

### 3.3 Kernel gradient flow and closed-form dynamics

With a constant kernel, the function-space dynamics for squared loss $\ell(f, y) = \frac{1}{2}(f - y)^2$ become linear:

$$\frac{d}{dt} f_t(X) = -\eta\, \Theta(X, X)\, (f_t(X) - Y),$$

with closed-form solution

$$f_t(X) = Y + e^{-\eta \Theta t}(f_0(X) - Y), \qquad f_t(x) = f_0(x) - \Theta(x, X)\, \Theta(X, X)^{-1} \big(I - e^{-\eta \Theta t}\big)(f_0(X) - Y).$$

Convergence is exponential with rate $\eta \lambda_{\min}(\Theta(X,X))$. Jacot et al. [1] proved positive-definiteness of $\Theta$ for non-polynomial activations on the sphere, guaranteeing convergence to zero training loss. Moreover, decomposing the residual along kernel eigenfunctions shows that convergence is fastest along the top principal components — a theoretical justification for *early stopping* [1].

The following snippet computes the empirical NTK Gram matrix for a two-layer ReLU network and solves kernel regression in closed form:

```python
import numpy as np

def relu(z): return np.maximum(z, 0.0)

def empirical_ntk_two_layer(X, W1, b1, w2, b2):
    # f(x) = w2 @ relu(W1 x + b1) / sqrt(n) + b2 ; NTK parameterization
    n = W1.shape[0]
    H = relu(X @ W1.T + b1) / np.sqrt(n)          # (N, n) features
    dH = (X @ W1.T + b1 > 0).astype(float) / np.sqrt(n)
    # gradient w.r.t. W1: outer products over hidden units
    G_W1 = np.einsum('ni,nj,nk->nik', dH * w2[None, :], dH * w2[None, :], X)
    K = (H @ H.T)                                 # w2 contribution
    K += np.einsum('nik,mik->nm', G_W1, G_W1)      # W1 contribution
    K += 1.0                                      # b2 contribution
    return K

def kernel_regression_predict(Xtr, ytr, Xte, Ktr, Kte):
    alpha = np.linalg.solve(Ktr + 1e-8 * np.eye(len(Xtr)), ytr)
    return Kte @ alpha
```

### 3.4 Spectral bias

Because $f_t - Y = e^{-\eta \Theta t}(f_0 - Y)$, the residual along the $k$-th eigenfunction of $\Theta$ decays as $e^{-\eta \lambda_k t}$. Wide networks therefore learn low-frequency, large-eigenvalue components first — the *spectral bias* or frequency principle. This explains both the success of early stopping and a limitation: directions with tiny eigenvalues are effectively unlearnable in finite time.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Why the NTK is constant: the $1/\sqrt{n}$ scaling argument

The constancy proof rests on a careful accounting of scales. In NTK parameterization, $\partial f / \partial W^{(\ell)}_{ij} = O(1/\sqrt{n})$ while there are $O(n^2)$ parameters per layer, so $\Theta = O(n^2 \cdot 1/n) = O(1)$. A gradient step changes each weight by $\eta \cdot \partial \mathcal{L}/\partial W_{ij}$; the loss gradient through the network output is $O(1)$ in function space but the per-coordinate parameter gradient is $O(1/\sqrt{n})$. Over training, weights move $O(1/\sqrt{n})$, so the relative change in any preactivation is $O(1/\sqrt{n})$ — vanishing as $n \to \infty$. The Jacobian $\nabla_\theta f$ is therefore frozen, and with it the kernel. Lee et al. [5] made this rigorous: gradient descent trajectories stay within an $O(1/\sqrt{n})$ neighborhood of the linearized trajectory for learning rates below a critical threshold $\eta_{\text{critical}}$.

### 4.2 The lazy regime: linearization and Chizat–Bach scaling

Chizat and Bach [2] reframed laziness as a property of *scaling*, not of width. Consider a model $f(x; \theta) = \alpha^{-1} h(x; \alpha \theta)$-style rescalings: if the output is divided by a large factor $\alpha$ while the loss demands $O(1)$ function movement, parameters must move only $O(1/\alpha)$ — the model is forced to behave as its linearization

$$f(x; \theta) \approx f(x; \theta_0) + \langle \nabla_\theta f(x; \theta_0), \theta - \theta_0 \rangle.$$

In this regime, training is *kernel regression* with the tangent kernel at initialization, and Chizat and Bach proved bounds on the distance between the lazy and linearized optimization paths [2]. Their critical empirical observation: standard deep convolutional networks trained in the lazy regime (large $\alpha$) perform *worse* on vision tasks than in the standard regime — evidence that practical deep learning lives outside the lazy regime and genuinely needs feature learning.

### 4.3 The rich regime: Tensor Programs and maximal update parameterization

Yang and Hu [3] asked which infinite-width limits *learn features* — i.e., where hidden representations change by $\Theta(1)$ amounts. Using the Tensor Programs framework, which tracks the distributional dynamics of all network quantities as formal programs, they classified a broad space of parameterizations and proved a sharp dichotomy:

> *Any parameterization in this space either admits feature learning or has infinite-width training dynamics given by kernel gradient descent — but not both.*

Standard and NTK parameterizations fall on the kernel side: features move $O(1/\sqrt{n})$ and the NTK is frozen. The *maximal update parameterization* (μP) instead scales the learning rate per layer as $\eta_\ell \propto 1/n_\ell$ with appropriate output scaling, so that every layer's features update by $\Theta(1)$. Under μP, the infinite-width limit is a well-defined *feature-learning* limit that Yang and Hu computed exactly on Word2Vec and few-shot MAML tasks, where it outperformed NTK baselines [3]. Subsequent work (Yang et al., 2021) showed μP enables zero-shot hyperparameter transfer across widths — the optimal hyperparameters at small width transfer to large width — which has become standard practice in large-scale training.

| Regime | Parameter movement | Feature movement | Kernel | Example |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Lazy / kernel | $O(1/\sqrt{n})$ | $O(1/\sqrt{n})$ | Frozen | NTK parameterization, small LR |
| Rich / feature learning | $\Theta(1)$ | $\Theta(1)$ | Evolves | μP, mean-field limit |
| Catapult | Transient $O(1)$ | Restructured | Spikes then re-freezes | Large LR, $2/\lambda_{\max} < \eta < \eta_{\max}$ |

### 4.4 The catapult mechanism: escaping the lazy regime with large learning rates

Even in NTK parameterization, laziness requires *small* learning rates. Lewkowycz et al. [4] discovered a phase transition: for a two-layer network with quadratic or ReLU activations, there exists a range of large learning rates $\eta$ where gradient descent does not diverge but instead undergoes a *catapult* — the loss initially spikes upward while the top eigenvalue of the NTK (equivalently, the sharpness) collapses, flinging the iterates into a flatter region where training then proceeds stably. Formally, in their solvable model with NTK top eigenvalue $\lambda_t$:

- For $\eta < 2/\lambda_0$: standard lazy convergence; $\lambda_t$ nearly constant.
- For $2/\lambda_0 < \eta < \eta_{\max}$: the catapult phase — loss spikes, $\lambda_t$ decreases until $\eta < 2/\lambda_t$, then convergence in a flatter basin.
- For $\eta > \eta_{\max}$: divergence.

Crucially, models trained in the catapult phase generalize *better*, converging to flatter minima [4]. Meltzer and Liu [6] proved the catapult phase exists rigorously in quadratic models and homogeneous two-layer nets, showing the weight norm decreases whenever the loss becomes large. Later work connected loss spikes in SGD to catapults occurring in the low-dimensional subspace spanned by the top NTK eigenvectors, linking them to improved feature learning via alignment with the average gradient outer product. The catapult is thus a mechanism by which *discrete-time* gradient descent with large steps breaks kernel constancy and enters the rich regime — the NTK is no longer frozen but actively restructured.

### 4.5 Finite-width corrections: when the NTK learns

At finite width $n$, the NTK evolves: $\Theta_t = \Theta + \frac{1}{\sqrt{n}} \delta\Theta_t + O(1/n)$. These fluctuations are not mere noise — they are the channel through which feature learning enters. Several results characterize this:

1. **Perturbation theory.** The leading $1/\sqrt{n}$ correction to the NTK is a random kernel whose evolution couples to the function residual; integrating it yields the first feature-learning corrections to lazy dynamics.
2. **Depth–width interplay.** For deep networks, finite-width NTK evolution accumulates across layers; the effective "richness" grows with depth at fixed width, consistent with the empirical observation that depth promotes feature learning.
3. **Large-LR amplification.** The catapult analysis shows that finite step sizes *amplify* finite-width NTK dynamics: the $O(1/\sqrt{n})$ fluctuations in the top eigenspace are precisely where spikes nucleate [4].

In short, the NTK is a fixed point of infinite-width training dynamics, and finite width — together with large learning rates — provides the perturbations that push the system toward the rich fixed point instead.

## 5 Empirical Results and Formal Arguments

**Agreement between nonlinear and linearized training.** Lee et al. [5] trained wide networks (fully connected, convolutional, residual) and compared them against their first-order Taylor linearizations. The test predictions agreed quantitatively across architectures, optimizers, and losses, even at moderate widths — direct evidence that real wide networks inhabit the lazy regime when learning rates are small.

**Lazy vs. standard performance gap.** Chizat and Bach [2] trained convolutional networks with a tunable laziness parameter $\alpha$ scaling the output. As $\alpha$ increased (more lazy), test accuracy on CIFAR-10 *degraded* monotonically, while on synthetic kernel-friendly tasks the lazy regime matched or exceeded standard training. This establishes that the inductive bias of feature learning — not just the kernel — drives practical performance.

**Catapult phase diagram.** Lewkowycz et al. [4] mapped the learning-rate phase diagram empirically on MLPs and CNNs: below the critical learning rate, NTK eigenvalues stayed flat and final test accuracy was lower; in the catapult band, the top NTK eigenvalue dropped sharply during the loss spike and final generalization improved; above the band, training diverged. The predicted narrow window of stable large learning rates matched experiments quantitatively.

**μP feature learning at infinite width.** Yang and Hu [3] computed the exact infinite-width feature-learning limits for Word2Vec and Omniglot few-shot learning. The feature-learning limit *outperformed* both the NTK limit and finite-width networks, and finite-width performance approached the limit from below as width grew — a clean demonstration that the rich regime is the better infinite-width idealization for representation-dependent tasks.

**Formal convergence guarantees.** The kernel-gradient-flow analysis yields: (i) global convergence of gradient flow for wide networks under squared loss when the limiting NTK is positive definite [1]; (ii) explicit rates $e^{-\eta \lambda_{\min} t}$; (iii) a linearization-error bound $\sup_t \|f_t - f^{\text{lin}}_t\| = O(1/\sqrt{n})$ for $\eta < \eta_{\text{critical}}$ [5]; and (iv) global convergence under large learning rates for non-convex objectives with good regularity, unifying edge-of-stability, balancing, and catapult phenomena [7].

## 6 Limitations

1. **Kernel lower bounds.** For certain function classes (e.g., learning parities or single-index models with specific structure), any fixed kernel — including the NTK — requires exponentially many samples or suffers polynomial approximation barriers, while feature-learning networks succeed. The NTK is a *universal* kernel in the infinite-width limit, but universality does not imply sample efficiency.
2. **The laziness gap in practice.** Real networks are trained with large learning rates, data augmentation, and adaptive optimizers — all of which push dynamics away from the frozen-NTK idealization. The NTK describes a regime that practitioners deliberately avoid, limiting its direct predictive power for state-of-the-art training.
3. **Positive-definiteness assumptions.** Convergence guarantees require $\lambda_{\min}(\Theta(X,X)) > 0$, which holds on the sphere for non-polynomial activations [1] but can fail with duplicate or degenerate data, and the smallest eigenvalue typically decays with dataset size, slowing the guaranteed rate.
4. **Finite-width theory is perturbative.** The $1/\sqrt{n}$ expansion of NTK dynamics is asymptotic; at practical widths ($n \sim 10^2$–$10^3$) higher-order terms matter, and a complete non-perturbative theory of the lazy-to-rich crossover is still missing.
5. **Discrete-time effects beyond the catapult.** Edge-of-stability behavior, progressive sharpening, and adaptive-optimizer dynamics involve NTK–Hessian interactions that the gradient-flow NTK theory does not capture; extensions via Tensor Programs IVb handle Adam but remain technically demanding [3].
6. **Computational cost.** The NTK Gram matrix is $n \times n$ and its exact computation costs $O(n^2 P)$ time; infinite-width NTK inference is rarely competitive with direct network training at scale.

---

## 7 Conclusion

The Neural Tangent Kernel transformed our understanding of deep learning by revealing that infinite-width networks are exactly solvable: training becomes convex kernel gradient descent with a frozen kernel, exponential convergence rates, and a spectral account of early stopping [1, 5]. The lazy-training formalism [2] and the Tensor Programs dichotomy [3] then clarified *when* this picture applies — it is a consequence of scaling choices, and infinite-width networks face a strict either/or between kernel behavior and feature learning. The catapult mechanism [4] and finite-width corrections show how real training escapes the kernel fixed point: large learning rates destabilize the NTK's top eigenspace, restructure the features, and land in flatter, better-generalizing basins.

The emerging synthesis is that the NTK is best understood not as a theory of *what neural networks are*, but as the *reference fixed point* of training dynamics — the baseline from which feature learning, catapults, and finite-width effects are measured as departures. Future progress lies in the non-perturbative theory of the lazy-to-rich crossover: predicting, from architecture and hyperparameters alone, how much feature learning a network will do, and controlling it deliberately. The μP program's success in hyperparameter transfer suggests this is not merely a theoretical aspiration but an engineering discipline in the making.
