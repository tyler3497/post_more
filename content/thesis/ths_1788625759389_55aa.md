# Proofs of Space and Time: A Cryptographic and Economic Analysis of Chia Network's Storage-Based Consensus

## Abstract

Proof of Work secures Bitcoin at the cost of prodigious, ongoing energy expenditure. Proofs of Space (PoSpace) propose an alternative foundation for Nakamoto consensus: security rooted in committed disk capacity rather than continuous computation. This thesis presents a comprehensive cryptographic and economic analysis of Chia Network's proof-of-space-and-time consensus, the first large-scale deployment of PoSpace in a permissionless blockchain. We dissect the Beyond-Hellman plot construction underlying the chiapos library — seven nested tables of 2^k entries whose 64-value proofs resist Hellman-type time-memory trade-offs — and trace the full lifecycle from the four plotting phases through challenge-response farming. We formalize the roles of the plot filter, proof quality, signage points, and timelord-operated verifiable delay functions, which together enforce a strict chain cadence. An economic model contrasts farming with mining, quantifying the shift from operational to capital expenditure and the resulting energy profile. Finally, we survey the adversarial landscape — replotting, grinding, compressed and rented plots, and pooling centralization — and evaluate Chia's mitigations, including the forthcoming proof-of-space format with reduced minimum k.

---

## 1 Introduction

Bitcoin's proof of work (PoW) solved Sybil-resistant consensus in open networks by binding security to the continuous expenditure of energy. A decade of deployment has produced industrial mining, geographic concentration near cheap power, and a planetary-scale energy footprint. Alternatives have followed two paths: *proof of stake*, replacing physical resources with financial ones, and *useful work*, attempting to redirect computation toward valuable tasks — a goal that founders on defining "useful" in a permissionless setting [1].

Proofs of Space (PoSpace), introduced by Dziembowski, Faust, Kolmogorov, and Pietrzak, chart a third course [2]. A prover *commits* persistent storage during a one-time initialization called *plotting*, then answers random challenges by reading from that storage. Because the marginal cost of answering is a handful of disk seeks, steady-state energy can be orders of magnitude below a PoW chain with comparable security. The Chia Network, founded by BitTorrent creator Bram Cohen, is the first production blockchain on this idea, combining proofs of space with *proofs of time* — verifiable delay functions (VDFs) enforcing strict temporal ordering of blocks [1].

This thesis contributes: (1) a precise exposition of the Beyond-Hellman plot construction and its four plotting phases [4]; (2) a formal account of farming — challenges, plot filter, proof quality, signage points, difficulty adjustment [1][5]; (3) analysis of the proof-of-time layer and its role against grinding and replotting [1][6]; (4) an economic comparison of farming and mining [7][8]; (5) a security survey of replotting, grinding, compressed-plot, and rental attacks plus the pooling protocol, with evaluation of current and planned mitigations [1][9].

## 2 Background

### 2.1 From proof of work to proof of space

Nakamoto consensus requires block-production rights to be scarce and unforgeable. PoW manufactures scarcity from computation: a valid header demands an expected 2^d hash evaluations at difficulty d. Early storage-bound proposals observed that disk capacity is a *stock*, not a *flow* — once provisioned, it persists without further expenditure. This is the economic fulcrum of PoSpace: security derives from an asset the farmer already owns, not energy the miner must continuously buy.

### 2.2 Graph pebbling and function inversion

The first rigorous PoSpace constructions (Ateniese et al.; Dziembowski et al.) used *graph pebbling* — labeling a DAG with a hash function and opening random labels with Merkle proofs [2]. Theoretically sound but heavy, they saw little deployment. A simpler approach builds PoSpace from *inverting a random function*: store a large input-output table, invert random outputs on challenge. The obstacle is Hellman's time-memory trade-off: any permutation over N elements inverts in time T with S bits of tables whenever S · T ≈ N, letting a prover store far less than N and recompute on the fly [4][10].

> **Theorem (Hellman, 1980).** *For any permutation f over a domain of size N, an inversion algorithm exists using S bits of precomputed tables and T online evaluations with S · T ∈ O(N); for arbitrary functions, S · T² ∈ O(N²) suffices.*

Abusalah, Alwen, Cohen, Khilko, Pietrzak, and Reyzin broke this barrier at ASIACRYPT 2017 [4][10]. Their insight: construct a function that *cannot be efficiently evaluated forward*, though its full table computes in quasilinear time. Defining h(x) = g(x, x′) with f(x′) = π(f(x)) for random g, random permutation f, and fixed-point-free π, evaluation itself requires inverting f — so Hellman's attack, which assumes cheap forward evaluation, does not apply. They prove that for any constant k, the nested construction forces S^k · T ∈ Ω(N^k): compressing storage by any constant factor demands exponential online time [4].

> **Lemma (Beyond-Hellman lower bound, informal).** *For the k-times nested inversion function over a domain of size N, any adversary with S bits of auxiliary storage and T oracle queries succeeds non-negligibly only if S^k · T ∈ Ω(N^k).*

Chia instantiates this with k = 6 nestings — seven tables — the deepest practical parameterization [5].

### 2.3 Verifiable delay functions

A verifiable delay function (VDF) needs a prescribed number of *sequential* steps to evaluate but verifies exponentially faster. Chia's *proof of time* uses VDFs run by *timelords* to produce an unforgeable public clock pacing the chain [1]. VDF evaluation cannot be parallelized, neutralizing faster hardware and underpinning the anti-replotting and anti-grinding arguments of Sections 4.5 and 6.

## 3 Methodology

This thesis uses a specification-driven analytical methodology:

- **Primary source analysis.** Protocol reconstructed from the Chia Green Paper [1], the chiapos specification [4], and official documentation [2][5][7].
- **Parameter verification.** Every quantitative claim traced to a primary source and cross-checked — e.g., the 1/512 plot-filter pass rate against the 9.375-second signage cadence yields the documented ~80-minute mean inter-access time per plot.
- **Adversarial modeling.** Attack taxonomy systematized from the Green Paper's threat model (§1.8.3 on replotting) and the precursor paper's grinding analysis [6], evaluated against deployed and planned mitigations [9].
- **Economic modeling.** Farming versus mining compared through capital-versus-operational-expenditure, using one-time plotting cost and near-zero marginal farming cost as structural inputs [7][8].
- **Executable illustration.** Closed-form expectations (proofs per challenge, win probability versus netspace share) derived and demonstrated in Python.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Beyond-Hellman plot construction: seven tables and four phases

A Chia *plot* is a pseudorandom file sized by security parameter k:

$$\text{space}(k) = 780 \cdot k \cdot 2^{k-10}\ \text{bytes},$$

with protocol minimum k = 32 (≈101.4 GiB), accounting for ~98% of network space, and maximum k = 59 (keeping intermediates in 64 bits) [2][4][5].

The plot holds **seven tables** T₁…T₇ of 2^k entries each. T₁ stores pairs of *x-values* (integers in [0, 2^k)); each Tᵢ entry (i > 1) stores two pointers into Tᵢ₋₁, matched when their x-values satisfy the Beyond-Hellman arithmetic relation and recorded as a *line point*. A valid proof is **64 x-values** with the required nested matching; proof *quality* derives from just 2 of them [2].

Plotting proceeds in four phases in chiapos [4]:

| Phase | Operation |
|---|---|
| 1. Forward propagation | Compute matching pairs table by table from the seed |
| 2. Backpropagation | Discard entries that cannot complete a full proof |
| 3. Compression | Sort, deduplicate, and pack surviving entries |
| 4. C-table & park construction | Build sorted index tables C₁, C₂, C₃ with grouped *parks* of line points |

The layout optimizes the proving direction: C₂ is sorted for binary search on a challenge-derived target, and parks group the line points needed to walk a candidate proof back through the tables [4]. A k32 plot takes ~5 minutes on high-end hardware or several hours on commodity machines — but is created exactly once and farmed for years [2].

> **Lemma (Plot uncompressibility, informal).** *The tables hold pseudorandom data no adversary can meaningfully compress: any substantially smaller representation forces the prover into the Beyond-Hellman bound, i.e., exponential online recomputation per challenge [4][5].*

The minimum k = 32 is itself a security parameter: with small k, an attacker could *regenerate an entire plot inside the proving window*, answering challenges by plotting on the fly [4].

### 4.2 Challenges, the plot filter, proof quality, and signage points

Timelords gossip a *signage point* every **9.375 seconds**, each serving as a farming challenge [1]. The *plot filter* admits a plot only if

$$\text{trunc}_9\big(H(\text{plot\_id} \,\|\, \text{challenge} \,\|\, \text{sp})\big) = 0,$$

excluding 511 of 512 plots per challenge — a 512× I/O reduction that "hurts everyone equally (except for replotting attackers), and is therefore fair" [2]. Each plot is thus accessed only once per 512 × 9.375 s ≈ **80 minutes** in expectation, so petabyte farms of HDDs behind a Raspberry Pi 4 keep up [2].

Passing plots run *proof retrieval*, in time independent of k [4]:

1. Derive `target` from the challenge; binary-search C₂ for the last entry below it.
2. Read corresponding C₁ entries and a *park* from C₃.
3. Find indices where the table-7 function value equals the target.
4. Walk matched line points back through the tables, recovering each candidate proof's 64 x-values in canonical order.
5. Compute the *quality string* and test it against the difficulty threshold.

Quality deterministically decides wins: probability scales with the farmer's share of network space, and quality is *not grindable* — a fixed function of plot and challenge, improvable by no extra computation [1][2]. Difficulty retargets every **4,608 blocks** (~24 h) to hold **32 blocks per 10 minutes** network-wide, the space-based analogue of Bitcoin's 2016-block retarget [8].

### 4.3 Timelords, VDFs, and the proof of time

Space alone cannot order blocks: without a clock, proofs could be precomputed for future challenges or history rewritten cheaply. Timelords — permissionless specialized nodes — continuously evaluate **three VDFs** (challenge chain, reward chain, infused challenge chain), gossiping each signage point's output every 9.375 s [1].

A winning farmer builds a block (transactions in the *foliage block*, plus the proof); if it reaches the fastest timelord in time, it is *infused* 28.125–37.5 s after the signage point. VDF outputs are deterministic and sequentially forced, so every node verifies the delay elapsed — no farmer runs ahead of the timelord, and faster plot scanning buys nothing [1][8]. Timelording is deliberately thankless — slower timelords just recompute gossiped values — yet a standby set is wanted in case the fastest fails [1], a centralization pressure revisited in Section 6.

> **Theorem (VDF pacing, informal).** *Absent a VDF speedup exceeding the infusion slack, no farmer can produce a valid block for a signage point before its VDF output is public, nor backdate a block to an earlier one [1].*

### 4.4 Farming versus mining: an economic comparison

Mining converts *operational expenditure* (electricity, continuously) into consensus weight; farming converts *capital expenditure* (disks, once). Plotting is mining-like in intensity but one-time; farming thereafter is near-free [7].

| Dimension | PoW mining | PoSpace farming |
|---|---|---|
| Scarce resource | Hash rate (flow) | Committed disk (stock) |
| Marginal cost per attempt | Electricity for 2^d hashes | Disk seeks every ~80 min/plot |
| Hardware lifecycle | ASIC obsolescence in years | HDDs farm 5–10+ years; plots never expire |
| Geographic pressure | Toward cheapest power | Toward cheapest storage; diffuse |
| Entry hardware | Specialized ASICs | Any PC; minimum a Pi 4 + 101.4 GiB [2] |
| Difficulty retarget | 2016 blocks (~2 weeks) | 4608 blocks (~24 h) [8] |

A petabyte farm draws on the order of hundreds of watts while contesting every challenge through the filter [2][7] — versus megawatts of continuous hashing for equivalent PoW security. This is the "beyond-useful-work" motivation made concrete: rather than subsidizing consensus with useful tasks, PoSpace prices a resource whose idle cost is intrinsically near zero [1].

The pooling protocol preserves decentralization structurally: farmers join pools via a *plot NFT* binding farmer key to pool contract puzzle hash, but the *farmer* — not the pool — assembles the block and selects transactions, sharply limiting operator power versus PoW pools [2][8].

### 4.5 Adversarial analysis: grinding, replotting, compression, and pooling

**Replotting.** Green Paper §1.8.3 notes space's asymmetry with stake: given a challenge, a farmer can *inefficiently* mint many proofs by repeatedly plotting fresh and extracting one proof each [1]. Defenses layer: challenges arrive every 9.375 s, making inter-challenge replotting infeasible; the filter must not be too aggressive, since aggressive filtering aids the replotter; and k = 32 keeps plotting far slower than the proving window [1][4].

**Grinding.** In the precursor design, an attacker generated multiple *foliage blocks* with perturbed timestamps before a difficulty adjustment, deriving distinct difficulty parameters per fork and grinding VDF challenges in parallel to outrun the honest chain [6]. The deployed adjustment derives difficulty from *infused VDF outputs* rather than farmer-chosen timestamps, closing the vector [1].

**Compressed and rented plots.** The live threat: GPUs farming nominal-space plots stored compressed, recomputing missing tables on the fly, or rented compute simulating space. Chia's December-2024 new proof-of-space format resists both, even against future GPUs — bit-dropping quickly costs as much as grinding full plots; K28 plots stay viable; the plot ID filter starts at 256 with scheduled reductions and headroom to tighten two further orders of magnitude (toward 1) should SSDs ever dominate [9].

**Filter trade-off.** The filter cuts honest I/O 512× but also the *effective* space per challenge, easing replotting — the Green Paper is explicit it "cannot be chosen too aggressively" [1].

**Dynamic availability.** A result cited as [BP22] in the Green Paper proves *no* pure-PoSpace longest-chain protocol is secure under dynamic availability; Chia escapes via VDF-based proof of time — a participation-independent global clock [1].

**Pooling.** Because the winning farmer assembles the block, pools cannot censor or selfish-mine on farmers' behalf, and farmers switch pools without replotting [2][8].

### 5.1 Parameter summary

| Difficulty retarget | 4608 blocks (~24 h); target 32 blocks/10 min | [8] |
| k32 netspace share | ≈98% | [5] |

### 5.2 Expected proofs and win probability

Per signage point, each plot independently passes the filter with probability 1/512; passing plots beat the difficulty-tuned quality threshold at a network-calibrated rate. A farmer's win probability is thus ≈ (n/N) per block interval — linear in space share, the fairness property PoSpace promises:

```python
import hashlib

def proof_quality(plot_id: bytes, challenge: bytes, sp_hash: bytes) -> int:
    """Deterministic quality: fixed for (plot, challenge); not grindable."""
    return int.from_bytes(hashlib.sha256(plot_id + challenge + sp_hash).digest(), "big")

def passes_plot_filter(plot_id: bytes, challenge: bytes, sp_hash: bytes) -> bool:
    """9 leading zero bits -> 1/512 plots eligible per signage point."""
    h = hashlib.sha256(plot_id + challenge + sp_hash).digest()
    return h[0] == 0 and (h[1] >> 7) == 0

def expected_wins_per_day(n_plots: int, net_plots: int) -> float:
    sps_per_day = 86400 / 9.375
    return sps_per_day * 0.5 * (n_plots / net_plots)  # 32 blocks / 64 SPs per 10 min

print(expected_wins_per_day(10_000, 200_000_000))  # ~0.23 blocks/day
```

### 5.3 Security reduction sketch

> **Theorem (PoSpace security, informal).** *Under the Beyond-Hellman bound S^k · T ∈ Ω(N^k) [4][10] and VDF sequentiality [1], an adversary with β < 1/2 of network space and no VDF speedup beyond infusion slack overtakes the honest chain with probability negligible in confirmation depth.*

*Proof sketch.* Challenges bind to VDF outputs the adversary can neither predict nor accelerate. Per challenge, adversarial plots pass the filter and prove at the same per-space rate as honest plots (quality is ungrindable), so block production is Poisson with rate ∝ space. With β < 1/2 the adversary's chain grows strictly slower in expectation; Chernoff over the confirmation window gives negligible fork probability. Replotting is bounded by the 9.375 s cadence versus multi-minute minimum plotting at k = 32 [1][4]. ∎

### 5.4 Energy profile

Plotting amortizes over multi-year plot lifetimes; farming is sporadic reads. Steady-state draw is disk idle power — single-digit watts per HDD — so a 1 PiB farm (≈10,000 k32 plots) runs in the low hundreds of watts while contesting every challenge [2][7]. Equivalent PoW security demands continuous hashing at full network difficulty: orders of magnitude more power, forever. PoSpace is not free — disk capex and plotting energy are real — but its *marginal* energy per unit of consensus participation approaches the thermodynamic floor of data retention [1][8].

## 6 Limitations

**Timelord centralization.** VDF racing is winner-take-all: only the fastest timelord's output matters, and slower ones merely recompute gossiped values — "seemingly no point" in participating [1]. A compromised fastest timelord could degrade liveness, with only voluntary standby redundancy as backstop.

**The compression arms race.** The new format raises compressed/rented-plot costs parametrically (filter thresholds, minimum k), not absolutely. A discontinuous GPU/FPGA leap could erode the margin faster than scheduled filter reductions compensate — an accepted monitoring burden [9].

**Replotting residual.** Anti-replotting is quantitative: challenge frequency versus plotting time. Plotting fell from hours to ~5 minutes for k32 on high-end hardware [2], directly tightening the margin — minimum-k and filter parameters must be revisited as hardware evolves [1][4].

**Storage externalities.** PoSpace demand concentrates on HDD/SSD markets; the 2021 launch visibly distorted drive pricing, undercutting the "use existing hardware" narrative [8].

**Dynamic availability.** Per [BP22], security under fluctuating participation rests entirely on the VDF layer; a catastrophic timelord outage with fallback to pure PoSpace would reopen long-range replotting [1].

**Weak finality.** Farmers costlessly extend multiple forks with the same plots; Chia relies on the VDF-infused heaviest-chain rule and checkpointing, not slashing — weaker finality than BFT protocols.

---

## 7 Conclusion

Chia's proof of space and time is the most ambitious attempt to ground Nakamoto consensus in a *stock* resource rather than a *flow*. Its core — the Beyond-Hellman nested construction with S^k · T ∈ Ω(N^k) — turns Hellman's trade-off from fatal attack into tuned parameter, and the four-phase chiapos pipeline makes it plottable once on ordinary hardware and farmable for years [4][5][10]. Around it sits a balanced architecture: the 1/512 plot filter enabling Pi-class HDD farming, the ungrindable quality lottery, the 4,608-block retarget, and the timelord VDF chain pacing signage points at 9.375 s and defeating grinding and replotting [1][2][8].

Economically, consensus cost shifts from opex to capex, collapsing steady-state energy toward the cost of data retention — the "beyond useful work" vision realized [1][7]. Adversarially, the system has survived contact with reality: compression and rental attacks arrived as predicted, and the new format answers parametrically rather than by emergency redesign [9].

Open problems remain — timelord decentralization, compression-margin stability, dynamic availability with VDFs — but as an existence proof that blockchains need not burn energy to stay secure, the experiment has succeeded. The general lesson: *what* a protocol prices — computation, capital, or capacity — determines both its security model and its physical footprint.
