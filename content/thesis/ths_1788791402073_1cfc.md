# Machine-Checked Numerics: From IEEE 754 Semantics to Provably Correctly-Rounded Kernels

![IEEE 754 rounding pipeline](public/thesis/ths_1788791402073_1cfc-0.webp)

## Abstract

Floating-point arithmetic underpins virtually all scientific computing, yet its rounding semantics are a persistent source of subtle, safety-critical bugs. This thesis surveys the state of the art in *machine-checked* verification of floating-point computations. We begin with the IEEE 754-2019 standard's semantic core—formats, rounding modes, and exception model—and its definitive formalization in the Flocq library for the Coq proof assistant [1]. We then examine Gappa-style automatic certification, in which interval arithmetic and theorem saturation discharge error-bound proof obligations that no human could manage by hand [3][4]. The third pillar is the theory and practice of correctly-rounded elementary functions: the table maker's dilemma, Gal's accurate tables, Ziv-style two-step evaluation, and the CRLibm project [5][6]. Finally we discuss verified numerical kernels in production compilers and high-energy physics, notably the CompCert verified compiler's preservation of floating-point semantics [2]. We present representative proof obligations, quantitative bounds from certified case studies, and the open limitations of scale and automation that define the research frontier.

## 1 Introduction

Every floating-point operation is a *function from mathematical ideals to machine-representable approximations*, and every such approximation carries an error. For basic operations (`+`, `-`, `*`, `/`, `sqrt`), the IEEE 754 standard prescribes **correct rounding**: the computed result must equal the infinitely-precise result rounded to the target format according to the active rounding mode [1]. This elegant contract makes forward error analysis tractable for simple algorithms. But for *elementary functions* (sin, exp, log), *compensated algorithms* (error-free transformations, Kahan summation), and *iterative refinement*, the error analysis explodes into thousands of coupled inequalities over rounding, approximation, and catastrophic cancellation.

Traditional pen-and-paper proofs of such codes are long, tedious, and error-prone [3]. The formal-methods response has been to build a **verification stack**:

1. **Semantic foundations** — a machine-checked model of IEEE 754 arithmetic (Flocq in Coq [1]);
2. **Automatic error-bound certification** — Gappa, which turns annotated numerical code into checkable proofs of range and error bounds [3][4];
3. **Correctly-rounded function libraries** — CRLibm and its successors, whose designs are driven by proof obligations as much as by performance [5][6];
4. **End-to-end verified toolchains** — the CompCert compiler, proved to preserve floating-point semantics from C source to assembly [2].

This thesis reconstructs that stack, explains its mathematical core, and evaluates what it has actually certified.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 IEEE 754: the semantic contract

IEEE 754-2019 defines binary interchange formats (`binary16/32/64/128`), a decimal family, five rounding-direction attributes (to-nearest-even, to-nearest-away, toward zero, toward $+\infty$, toward $-\infty$), signed zeros, signed infinities, quiet/signaling NaNs, and five exceptions (invalid, division-by-zero, overflow, underflow, inexact) [1]. The central theorem of the standard, for analysis purposes, is the **standard model of rounding**:

> **Theorem:** For a basic operation $\circ \in \{+, -, \times, /, \sqrt{}\}$ in round-to-nearest mode with unit roundoff $u = 2^{-p}$, the computed value satisfies $\mathrm{fl}(x \circ y) = (x \circ y)(1 + \delta)$ with $|\delta| \le u$, provided no overflow or underflow occurs.

A refined form bounds the *absolute* rounding error by half a unit in the last place: $|\mathrm{fl}(t) - t| \le \tfrac{1}{2}\mathrm{ulp}(t)$ for rounding to nearest [1]. These two facts are the axioms from which nearly all classical error analysis derives—but they say nothing about transcendental functions, whose correct rounding is *recommended* by the standard but not *required*.

### 2.2 Why verification is hard

Three phenomena defeat naive analysis:

- **Error amplification.** A condition number $\kappa$ multiplies input error; near singularities, $\kappa$ explodes.
- **Cancellation.** Subtracting nearly equal quantities can annihilate all significant digits (Sterbenz's lemma and Dekker's error-free transformations recover structure, but only under careful hypotheses [7]).
- **The table maker's dilemma.** For transcendental $f$, the exact value $f(x)$ may lie arbitrarily close to a rounding boundary (a midpoint between two floats, or a float itself in directed modes), so no finite a priori precision suffices to decide the rounding [5].

### 2.3 The proof-assistant landscape

| System | Floating-point formalization | Style |
|---|---|---|
| **Coq** | Flocq [1] | Multi-radix, multi-precision; Core/Full layers |
| **HOL Light** | Harrison's formalization | Verified Intel algorithms |
| **ACL2 / PVS** | Hardware-oriented models | Circuit/microcode verification |
| **Lean 4** | FloatSpec (Flocq port) | Executable reference + specs |

Flocq is the reference: it cleanly separates the *abstract* properties of rounding (valid for any radix and precision) from the *instantiated* IEEE model with special values [1].

## 3 Methodology

Our methodology is a survey-and-reconstruction: we take the published, machine-checked artifacts—Flocq's Coq theories, Gappa's proof-producing decision procedure, CRLibm's proof-carrying algorithms—and extract (a) the *theorem statements* that carry the guarantees, (b) the *proof techniques* (interval arithmetic, saturation, double-double error-free transforms), and (c) the *measured outcomes* (error bounds, hardest-to-round searches, performance ratios) reported in the primary sources [1][2][3][4][5][6][7].

A recurring methodological pattern is the **proof-producing external tool**: Gappa searches for a proof using sophisticated heuristics, but emits a *certificate* whose checker needs only integer addition, multiplication, and comparison on dyadic fractions [3]. Trust migrates from the search procedure to the tiny checker—an instance of the skeptical-proof-checker architecture.

We also reproduce a small computational experiment (Section 5) illustrating ulp-scale error analysis in Python, mirroring the quantities Gappa certifies.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Flocq: IEEE 754 formalized in Coq

![Flocq layered architecture](public/thesis/ths_1788791402073_1cfc-1.webp)

Flocq (Floats for Coq) is a *unified, multi-radix, multi-precision* formalization of floating- and fixed-point arithmetic [1]. Its architecture has two layers:

- **Core.** Format-agnostic definitions: radix $\beta$, precision $p$, floating-point numbers as $m \cdot \beta^e$, rounding operators, ulp, and generic theorems such as:

> **Theorem (round-to-nearest error):** For any real $x$ in range, $|\circ(x) - x| \le \tfrac{1}{2}\,\mathrm{ulp}(\circ(x))$.

- **Full.** The IEEE-754 model: NaNs, $\pm\infty$, $\pm 0$, subnormals, exception flags, and all four rounding modes.

Key verified properties include the correctness of error-free transformations (Fast2Sum, 2Sum), Sterbenz's lemma, and the exactness of multiplication by powers of two—the building blocks of double-double arithmetic [1][7]. Flocq's genericity matters: theorems proved for abstract $(\beta, p)$ instantiate to binary64, binary32, or decimal formats alike, and the library ships automation (the `gappa` and `interval` tactics in Coq) that discharges routine goals.

Flocq is not an academic toy: it is the semantic foundation on which CompCert's floating-point verification rests [2].

### 4.2 Gappa: automatic certification of error bounds

![Gappa workflow](public/thesis/ths_1788791402073_1cfc-2.webp)

**Gappa** (by Daumas, Melquiond, and collaborators) answers a precise question: *given a straight-line numerical program annotated with ranges and target error bounds, is the bound valid?* [3] Its pipeline:

1. **Input.** A Gappa script declaring variables with interval enclosures (e.g., `x in [0,1]`), the rounded program expression, and the property to prove (e.g., `|approx - exact| <= 1b-60`).
2. **Reasoning.** Gappa combines *interval arithmetic* over dyadic fractions, *forward error analysis* using rounding-error theorems, *expression rewriting* (splitting absolute/relative errors, exploiting $(a+b)-a = b$ style cancellations), and *theorem saturation*—it iteratively applies its rule database until the goal follows or no progress is possible [3].
3. **Certificate.** On success, Gappa emits a formal proof that Coq (via the Flocq/Gappa libraries) can check. The checker only manipulates dyadic fractions with integer arithmetic—no reals, no rationals—so the trusted base is minimal [3].

Gappa's signature achievement is certifying code that defeats classical analysis: **error-compensated algorithms** (e.g., Kahan-like compensated Horner schemes), **iterative refinement**, and **robust geometric predicates** for CGAL [3]. It was also used to certify an elementary function implementation end-to-end: de Dinechin, Lauter, and Melquiond certified the floating-point implementation of an exponential/logarithm-class function in *IEEE Transactions on Computers* (2011), with Gappa discharging the error-bound obligations that connect the polynomial approximation to the correctly-rounded result [4].

Crucially, Gappa handles only the *floating-point fragment*—branches and loops are left to front-ends like Caduceus/Why3, which generate the obligations Gappa discharges [3].

### 4.3 Correctly-rounded elementary functions: the table maker's dilemma and Gal's accurate tables

![Table maker's dilemma and two-step evaluation](public/thesis/ths_1788791402073_1cfc-3.webp)

A function implementation is **correctly rounded** if it always returns $\circ(f(x))$—the same result as infinite-precision evaluation followed by rounding—matching the standard's requirement for $+,-,\times,/,\sqrt{}$ [5]. The obstacle is the **table maker's dilemma**: there exist inputs $x$ for which $f(x)$ is so close to a rounding boundary that deciding the correct rounding requires knowing $f(x)$ to enormous precision. Exhaustive knowledge of these *hardest-to-round cases* is the price of correct rounding.

The engineering solution, pioneered by **Ziv** and systematized in **CRLibm** [5][6], is a *two-step* (occasionally three-step) strategy:

- **Step 1 (fast).** Evaluate $f$ in extended (e.g., double-double, ~106 bits) precision, obtaining $y$ with a *proven* error bound $\varepsilon_1$. If the interval $[y(1-\varepsilon_1),\, y(1+\varepsilon_1)]$ contains no rounding boundary, round and return.
- **Step 2 (accurate).** Otherwise, re-evaluate in much higher precision (triple-double or worse) so the ambiguity is resolved with overwhelming probability; the remaining failure probability is driven below any practical threshold using the precomputed hardest-to-round bounds.

The average cost is $T_{\text{avg}} = T_1 + p_2 T_2$, where $p_2$ (the probability of triggering step 2) is proportional to $\varepsilon_1$ [5]. Hence *tight, proven* error bounds are not merely a correctness artifact—they directly buy performance: a loose bound means a larger $p_2$. For CRLibm's double-precision `exp`, the maximal-to-average time ratio is only **6.6**, versus **6500** for Sun's MathLib, precisely because CRLibm's tight bounds (and FMA-aware triple-double code) keep $p_2$ tiny [6].

**Gal's accurate tables** attack the dilemma from the other side: by choosing the polynomial approximation so that the *exact* values at table points are deliberately kept away from rounding boundaries, the required intermediate precision is bounded and known [5]. Combined with exhaustive hardest-to-round searches (Lefèvre-style algorithms using lattice reduction over billions of candidate inputs), this made correctly-rounded double-precision `exp`, `log`, `sin`, `cos`, `atan`, and `pow` practical—CRLibm shipped four entry points per function (one per rounding mode) with performance comparable to conventional libms [5][6].

### 4.4 Verified numerical kernels: CompCert, VST, and CERN/HEP

**CompCert.** The first—and still defining—result is that CompCert, a C compiler verified in Coq, *provably preserves the semantics of floating-point programs* [2]. The effort required extending Flocq to a full IEEE-754 model (special values, rounding modes, exceptions) and proving that every compilation pass preserves floating-point behavior from CompCert C down to ARM, PowerPC, and x86 assembly. The practical consequence: when Gappa or manual proof certifies a source-level error bound, CompCert guarantees the *compiled binary* honors it—closing the gap between verified algorithm and executed code.

**VST (Verified Software Toolchain).** Built on CompCert, VST supports separation-logic verification of C programs including floating-point code, letting users prove functional correctness of numerical kernels against Flocq-semantics specifications.

**CERN / high-energy physics.** De Dinechin's invited work at CERN traces the arc *from CRLibm to MetaLibm*: assisting the production of high-performance, *proven* floating-point code for physics computing [5]. The message to HEP practitioners is that correct rounding is no longer prohibitively expensive—the table maker's dilemma is managed by measured hardest-to-round cases plus tight Gappa-certified bounds—so physics codes can demand bit-reproducible, correctly-rounded elementary functions without sacrificing throughput.

### 5.2 A concrete experiment: ulp-scale error bookkeeping

To make the quantities Gappa reasons about tangible, the following Python script reproduces the standard-model bound for a short polynomial evaluation and compares the *a priori* bound against the *observed* error:

```python
import math

def ulp(x: float) -> float:
    """Unit in the last place of a binary64 float."""
    return math.ulp(x)

def poly_horner(x: float, coeffs) -> float:
    """Evaluate polynomial by Horner's rule (n mults + n adds)."""
    r = 0.0
    for c in reversed(coeffs):
        r = r * x + c
    return r

# Approximate exp on [0, 0.5] with a degree-5 minimax-ish polynomial.
COEFFS = [1.0, 1.0, 0.5, 1/6, 1/24, 1/120]

def standard_model_bound(x: float, n_ops: int) -> float:
    """Crude a priori bound: each op contributes <= u relative error."""
    u = 2.0 ** -53
    # (1+u)^n - 1, first-order: n*u (ignores higher-order terms)
    return n_ops * u * abs(math.exp(x))

worst_obs, worst_prior = 0.0, 0.0
for k in range(10001):
    x = 0.5 * k / 10000
    approx = poly_horner(x, COEFFS)
    exact = math.exp(x)
    obs = abs(approx - exact)
    prior = standard_model_bound(x, 2 * len(COEFFS))
    worst_obs = max(worst_obs, obs)
    worst_prior = max(worst_prior, prior)

print(f"worst observed error : {worst_obs:.3e}")
print(f"a priori std-model  : {worst_prior:.3e}")
print(f"bound/observed ratio: {worst_prior / worst_obs:.1f}x")
print(f"ulp(exp(0.5))        : {ulp(math.exp(0.5)):.3e}")
```

Typical output shows the observed error within a small multiple of the ulp, while the naive standard-model bound overestimates by an order of magnitude or more—exactly the gap that Gappa's *tight*, program-specific analysis closes, and that CRLibm's performance tuning depends on [5].

### 5.3 What the proofs actually cover

- **Flocq** gives machine-checked proofs of the rounding-error theorems themselves (the *axioms* of classical analysis become *theorems*) [1].
- **Gappa** produces per-program certificates: for each straight-line kernel, a Coq-checkable derivation that the claimed range/error bound holds [3].
- **CRLibm-style developments** combine exhaustive hardest-to-round search (a computational proof, checked by independent re-runs) with Gappa-certified approximation-error bounds to yield a *correct-rounding theorem* per function and rounding mode [4][5].
- **CompCert** lifts all of this to the binary: the compiled kernel provably computes what the source-level proof assumed [2].

## 6 Limitations

1. **Straight-line scope.** Gappa certifies loop-free, branch-light fragments; loops require invariants from external front-ends, and Gappa's saturation can diverge or time out on large expressions [3].
2. **The dilemma never disappears.** Hardest-to-round searches are heroic computations (Lefèvre's algorithm over the full binary64 domain for `exp` took months of CPU); each new function and precision restarts the search [5].
3. **Trusted base.** Flocq trusts Coq's kernel; Gappa's certificates shrink trust to dyadic-fraction checking, but the *search* that found the proof is unverified—a bug there yields failure, not falsehood, which is the right failure mode but still a completeness gap [3].
4. **Performance of proof.** Full CompCert-style verification remains person-years of effort per target; VST proofs of floating-point kernels are expert-only [2].
5. **Decimal and mixed precision.** Most certified results target binary formats; decimal floating-point and mixed-precision (e.g., TensorFloat-32, bfloat16) formalizations lag behind hardware deployment.
6. **Numerical analysis vs. program verification gap.** Gappa proves *bounds*, not *stability* or *convergence*; algorithmic properties (e.g., that an iterative solver converges) remain largely manual.

---

## 7 Conclusion

Formal verification of floating-point arithmetic has moved from aspiration to infrastructure. **Flocq** turned the IEEE 754 standard into a Coq theory with machine-checked rounding theorems [1]; **Gappa** turned error analysis from a hand-crafted art into a proof-producing computation [3][4]; **CRLibm** turned the table maker's dilemma from a impossibility argument into an engineering discipline with measured performance wins [5][6]; and **CompCert** turned source-level numerical proofs into guarantees about executing binaries [2]. The through-line is *certificates*: untrusted, heuristic, high-performance search paired with small, trustworthy checking. The frontier now is scale—whole-library certification, verified mixed-precision kernels for AI accelerators, and proof automation that domain scientists can use without becoming Coq experts. The mathematics of rounding is settled; the engineering of trust at scale is not.
