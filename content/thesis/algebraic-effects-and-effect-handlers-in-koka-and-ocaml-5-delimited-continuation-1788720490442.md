# Algebraic Effects and Effect Handlers in Koka and OCaml 5: Delimited Continuations, One-Shot Semantics, and Performance Against Monadic Transformers

## Abstract

Algebraic effects and effect handlers unify exceptions, state, iterators, and async programming under one abstraction: operations are performed algebraically, and handlers interpret them by manipulating delimited continuations. This thesis compares two production-grade realizations: **Koka**, which pairs algebraic effects with row-polymorphic effect typing and type-directed selective CPS compilation, and **OCaml 5**, which retrofits handlers onto a mainstream language using heap-allocated fibers and one-shot continuations. We formalize the handler reduction rule in both systems, contrast Koka's multi-shot resumption with OCaml's linear continuation discipline, and derive cost models against the classical monad transformer stack. Synthesizing published evidence — including the PLDI 2021 study reporting sub-1% average overhead for fiber-enabled OCaml across 54 real workloads — we conclude algebraic effects are a viable high-performance substrate for structured control flow. Remaining limitations center on multi-shot expressiveness, resource safety under non-linear use, and the static-versus-dynamic typing trade-off.

## 1 Introduction

The history of programming language design can be read as a history of *control*. From the unstructured `goto` to structured loops, exceptions, coroutines, iterators, generators, and `async`/`await`, each advance has been an attempt to make non-local control flow safe, composable, and efficient. **Algebraic effects and effect handlers** represent arguably the most general such advance to date: a single mechanism from which exceptions, state, backtracking, concurrency, and asynchronous I/O can all be derived as user-defined libraries rather than built-in language features [1][7].

The intellectual lineage runs from Plotkin and Power's algebraic theory of computational effects, through Plotkin and Pretnar's *handling algebraic effects* [1], to a flowering of practical languages: Eff [1], Frank [6], Multicore OCaml, and Koka [2][3]. Two of these deserve particular attention because they have crossed the threshold from research artifact to engineered reality:

1. **Koka** (Daan Leijen, Microsoft Research) — a function-oriented language with Hindley–Milner-style inference over *row-polymorphic effect types* [2], and a type-directed compilation pipeline that selectively applies CPS translation only where effects demand it [3]. Koka demonstrates that algebraic effects can be *compiled* efficiently to standard runtime platforms (JavaScript, .NET, C) rather than merely interpreted.
2. **OCaml 5** — which retrofitted effect handlers and a multicore runtime onto one of the oldest industrial functional languages without breaking backward compatibility [4][5]. Its design commits to **one-shot continuations** implemented as heap-allocated **fibers**, trading the theoretical generality of multi-shot continuations for predictable performance and a sound interaction with resources and exceptions.

The central questions of this thesis are threefold. First, how do the semantics of delimited continuation capture and resumption differ between Koka's handler model and OCaml 5's fiber model, and what is lost — and gained — by the one-shot restriction? Second, what are the true performance characteristics of these implementations relative to the incumbent technology they displace, the **monad transformer stack**? Third, where do the remaining fundamental limitations lie?

Our contributions: a unified formal presentation of the handler reduction rule in both systems; a cost model comparing handler dispatch against monad transformer plumbing; a critical synthesis of the published empirical evidence [3][4]; and an honest accounting of limitations.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Algebraic theories of effects

An *algebraic effect* is described by a signature of operations with arities. The canonical example is mutable state:

```haskell
-- Algebraic signature of state (Haskell-style notation)
data State s k = Get (s -> k) | Put s (() -> k)
```

A program *performs* operations; a *handler* gives them meaning by interpreting each operation clause, receiving the operation's arguments **and the delimited continuation** — the captured remainder of the computation up to the handler boundary. Plotkin and Pretnar's foundational treatment [1] defines handling as a fold over the free algebra generated by the signature:

> **Theorem (Adequacy of handling, informal):** *For a well-typed handler `h` and computation `c`, handling `c` with `h` is equivalent to interpreting `c` in the algebra defined by `h`'s operation clauses, with the continuation bound in each clause denoting the recursive interpretation of the captured context.*

The reduction rule at the heart of every implementation is (in the style of Leijen [3]):

```
handle_h (H[op(v)]) ⟶ e[x ↦ v, resume ↦ λy. handle_h (H[y])]
    where (op(x) → e) ∈ h
```

Here `H` is the *capture context* — the delimited continuation up to the nearest enclosing handler `h` for that operation. The continuation is re-wrapped in the handler, which is what makes handlers *deep* by default: resuming continues under the same handler.

### 2.2 Delimited continuations and the one-shot restriction

Delimited continuations generalize `call/cc` by capturing only a *segment* of the stack, delimited by a prompt or handler frame. The critical semantic axis is **resumption multiplicity**:

| Discipline | Resume `k` zero times | Resume once | Resume multiple times |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Multi-shot** | abort (exceptions) | standard | backtracking, probabilistic programming |
| **One-shot (linear)** | abort | standard | *runtime error / undefined* |

OCaml 5 enforces one-shot semantics: a continuation object, once resumed via `Effect.Deep.continue` (or discontinued via `discontinue`), is invalidated; a second resume raises `Invalid_argument` [4]. The rationale is both *semantic* (linear use composes soundly with exception-based resource cleanup such as `Fun.protect` / `unwind-protect` idioms) and *performance-driven* (multi-shot resumption of a real call stack requires copying the stack segment on every resume).

Koka, by contrast, implements handlers via a CPS translation in which the continuation is an ordinary heap-allocated closure; resuming it multiple times is semantically well-defined, and idioms such as *ambiguity* (non-deterministic choice) depend on exactly this multi-shot capability [2][3]:

In Koka, a non-determinism handler resumes the captured continuation once per branch — the canonical use of multi-shot resumption [2].

### 2.3 The incumbent: monad transformers

Before effect handlers, the standard functional answer to composing effects was the **monad transformer stack** (e.g., `ReaderT r (StateT s (ExceptT e IO))`). Transformers suffer three well-documented pathologies:

1. **Quadratic plumbing**: each layer of the stack re-wraps the layers below; `lift` calls multiply, and the well-known *n² instances* problem complicates abstraction.
2. **Order dependence**: `StateT s (ExceptT e m)` and `ExceptT e (StateT s m)` have observably different semantics (does state roll back on exception?), forcing the programmer to commit to a semantics via stack order.
3. **Static closed world**: the stack is fixed at compile time; adding an effect requires restructuring, and effect rows cannot be inferred.

Algebraic effects address all three: operations are performed without knowledge of the handler stack (no `lift`), handler nesting order gives local, lexically apparent semantics, and effect rows support inference and polymorphism [2][6].

## 3 Methodology

Our method is *comparative and evidence-driven*, combining three modes of analysis:

1. **Semantic reconstruction.** We present the handling semantics of Koka and OCaml 5 in a common notation, isolating where the designs diverge. Primary sources are [2][3][4].

2. **Asymptotic cost modeling.** We derive per-operation dispatch costs for (a) Koka's type-directed selective CPS, (b) OCaml 5's fiber switch, and (c) monad transformer layering, parameterized by handler-stack depth *d*, operation count *n*, and resumption multiplicity *r*. The models are calibrated against published measurements rather than invented numbers.

3. **Empirical synthesis.** We critically report the benchmark evidence in [4] (54 real OCaml programs: Coq, Menhir, Irmin, and others) and [5] (multicore runtime overheads), and Leijen's compilation benchmarks [3]. We do not re-run benchmarks; instead we analyze what the published data do and do not establish, with attention to threats to validity.

Out of scope: effect systems without handlers, dynamically-typed handler embeddings, and OCaml 5's parallelism except where it interacts with the fiber design.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Row-polymorphic effect types in Koka

Koka's signature innovation is to treat the *effect* of a function as a first-class, inferable part of its type, using **extensible rows with scoped labels** [2][3]. A function type has the shape `τ₁ → ε τ₂`, where `ε` is an effect row such as `<exn, div | r>` — the operations `exn` and `div` plus a polymorphic tail `r`:

![Row-polymorphic effect types](/thesis/ths_1788719437422_9af6-1.webp)

```rust
// Koka-style signatures (Rust-like pseudocode for illustration)
fn map(f: fn(a) -> <e> b, xs: List<a>) -> <e> List<b>
fn traverse(f: fn(a) -> <exn|e> b, xs: List<a>) -> <exn|e> List<b>
```

Three properties make this design unusually practical:

- **Effect polymorphism for free.** `map` works over any effect `e` — no `lift`, no re-stacking.
- **Duplicate labels for precise elimination.** Handling removes exactly one label occurrence, giving *sound and complete* inference [3] without effect pollution.
- **Totality checking.** Koka verifies the top-level row is empty (`total`), guaranteeing all effects are handled — a static guarantee OCaml's dynamic effects cannot provide [2][4].

Inferred rows can grow verbose in large programs, though Leijen reports the approach scales in practice [3]; Kammar and Pretnar further showed that no value restriction is needed for algebraic effects and handlers (JFP 2017).

### 4.2 Type-directed selective CPS compilation

Naive implementations of effect handlers compile *everything* to continuation-passing style, destroying the performance of effect-free code. Leijen's POPL 2017 contribution [3] is a compilation scheme that uses the effect types themselves to decide what to transform:

- `total` (empty-row) functions compile to **direct style** — no CPS overhead.
- Effectful functions compile to CPS, but only along effectful paths; the translation is *selective*, guided by inferred rows.
- The direct operational semantics and the CPS translation are proved coherent.

The type system thus doubles as a whole-program effect analysis driving code generation; benchmarks in [3] show competitive code on JS, .NET, and JVM targets, and the later `libhandler` C backend extends the same ideas to a GC-free setting.

> **Theorem (Selective CPS coherence, after Leijen [3]):** *If `Γ ⊢ e : τ | ε` and the selective CPS translation maps `e` to `⟦e⟧`, then evaluation of `⟦e⟧` in the target coincides with the direct operational semantics of `e` up to the handler reduction rule; total subterms incur zero translation overhead.*

### 4.3 OCaml 5: fibers, one-shot continuations, and the retrofitting constraint

OCaml 5 faced a constraint Koka never had: **twenty-five years of existing code** — debuggers, profilers, C bindings, and exception idioms — that had to keep working [4]. The implementation strategy:

- **Fibers as heap-allocated stack segments.** Each effect handler (and each domain) runs on a *fiber*: a heap-allocated stack chunk with its own exception pointer and GC roots. Performing an effect unwinds to the handler by switching the stack pointer to the fiber's parent — a constant-time operation.
- **First-class continuations as heap objects.** The continuation handed to an effect clause is a small GC-managed object pointing at the suspended fiber. `continue k v` resumes the fiber; `discontinue k exn` resumes it by raising.
- **One-shot enforcement.** Because resuming reuses the *actual stack segment* rather than a copy, resuming twice would corrupt the stack. The runtime invalidates the continuation after first use. This is not merely an optimization — it is what makes the design *sound* with respect to OCaml's resource-cleanup idioms, which are exception-based and assume linear control flow [4].

The deep-handler syntax added in OCaml 5.3 illustrates the surface language:

```ocaml
(* OCaml 5.3 deep handler syntax *)
match f () with
| x -> x
| effect (Ask x), k -> Effect.Deep.continue k (lookup x)
```

Crucially, OCaml's effects are **dynamically typed** (the `Effect` module uses extensible variant types and `Obj`-level tricks under the hood); there is no static effect row, no totality check, and performing an unhandled effect raises `Unhandled` at runtime, discontinuing the continuation with an exception [4]. This is the mirror image of Koka's static discipline: OCaml buys backward compatibility and simplicity at the cost of static guarantees.

### 4.4 Deep versus shallow handlers, and the resumption-mode spectrum

Both languages default to **deep handlers** (the handler is re-installed around the continuation, so resumed code remains handled). **Shallow handlers** — where resumption continues *outside* the handler — are expressible in Koka via explicit re-handling and in OCaml via `Effect.Shallow`. Shallow handlers are the more primitive notion (deep handlers can be macro-expressed with shallow ones plus recursion), and they matter for implementing features like tail-resumptive operations efficiently.

Koka further refines the design space with *declared resumption modes* per operation clause [3]:

| Mode | Keyword | Semantics |
|---|---|---|
| Tail-resumptive | `fun` | Always resumes exactly once with the clause value; compiled without capturing the continuation |
| General | `ctl` / `control` | Captures the continuation; explicit `resume` |
| Final | `final ctl` | Never resumes (aborts) |

The `fun` mode is a significant optimization: operations like `ask` (reader) or `get` (state) that always resume in tail position need no continuation capture at all, reducing them to essentially a checked function call. This is Koka's answer to the objection that handlers are inherently heavier than direct implementations — for the common case, they need not be.

### 4.5 Cost model: handlers versus monad transformers

Let *d* be the handler/transformer stack depth, *n* the number of performed operations, and *r* the resumption multiplicity (*r* = 1 for linear use). Per-operation costs:

| Mechanism | Dispatch cost per operation | Resumption cost | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Koka `fun` (tail-resumptive) | *O(1)*, no capture | *O(1)* | Equivalent to a function call; no CPS penalty |
| Koka `ctl` (general, CPS) | *O(d)* handler search + closure alloc | *O(1)* per resume; *O(r)* total | Multi-shot: continuation is a reusable closure |
| OCaml 5 fiber switch | *O(1)* stack-pointer switch | *O(1)*; *r* must equal 1 | One-shot: no copy, but no re-resume |
| Monad transformer stack | *O(d)* `lift` plumbing per operation | *O(d)* per bind | Cost paid *everywhere*, not just at operations |

The structural difference is decisive. In a transformer stack, the *O(d)* plumbing tax is paid on **every bind** throughout the program, including effect-irrelevant code paths, and the stack depth is a global property of the program. In handler systems, dispatch cost is paid **only at `perform` sites**; pure code runs at full speed — in Koka provably so (total functions compile to direct style [3]), in OCaml empirically so (sub-1% average overhead on handler-agnostic code [4]).

![Handlers versus monad transformer stacks](/thesis/ths_1788719437422_9af6-3.webp)

A concrete consequence: adding a new effect to a transformer program requires re-stacking (changing *d* for all downstream code and re-verifying `lift` placement), while adding an effect to a handler program is *local* — perform the operation where needed, handle it where appropriate, and the row system (Koka) or dynamic dispatch (OCaml) absorbs it. This is the compositionality argument that motivated the entire line of work [1][6].

## 5 Empirical Evaluation / Proofs

### 5.1 What the PLDI 2021 study establishes

Sivaramakrishnan et al. [4] evaluate the retrofitted OCaml runtime on **54 real-world OCaml workloads** — verification tools (Coq, Alt-Ergo), parsers (Menhir), storage engines (Irmin), bioinformatics kernels, and numerical simulations — comparing stock OCaml against the fiber-enabled multicore runtime. The headline result: the geometric mean overhead across all 54 programs is **less than 1%** [4]. Thirty-two of the 54 programs show ≤5% overhead; only 8 exceed 10%.

This is a strong result with a precise meaning: *enabling the fiber runtime does not measurably slow down programs that do not use effects*. The mechanism is the **red zone** optimization — a 16-word stack red zone that elides most stack-overflow checks — plus the fact that fiber switching only occurs at `perform` sites.

Reported costs are honest [4][5]: ~19% larger text section from stack-overflow checks; slower exceptions (the exception-pointer register is repurposed, and fiber stacks relocate on growth — exception-heavy `game_of_life` regresses ~20%); and `perform`/`continue` round-trips that are cheap but not free.

### 5.2 What Koka's compilation results establish

Leijen [3] demonstrates end-to-end that row-typed algebraic effects admit *efficient compilation to stock platforms* — the selective CPS translation produces code competitive with hand-written direct-style code for total fragments, and the effectful fragments pay only for the handlers they use. The follow-on C backend (`libhandler`) further shows that the runtime requirements are minimal: no GC support needed, continuations as explicit stack segments in C.

### 5.3 Semantic proof obligations and their status

The metatheory is in good shape along the main axis:

- **Type soundness** for row-typed handlers is established for Koka's core calculus [3], including the duplicate-label discipline for precise effect elimination.
- **Coherence of selective CPS** with the direct operational semantics is proved in [3].
- **Expressive equivalence** results connect handlers to delimited control: Forster et al. [7] show that effect handlers, monadic reflection, and delimited control are mutually macro-expressible (with caveats about typing), placing handlers precisely in the landscape of control operators.
- **OCaml's soundness argument** is operational rather than type-theoretic: linear (one-shot) continuation use is enforced dynamically, and `discontinue` preserves the exception-based resource-cleanup invariants of legacy code [4]. There is no static effect system, so "soundness" here means *the runtime never corrupts the stack and legacy invariants hold* — a weaker but pragmatically sufficient guarantee.

### 5.4 Threats to validity

1. The PLDI 2021 benchmarks measure programs that *do not use* effect handlers; they bound the *retrofitting tax*, not handler dispatch cost under load. Handler-intensive workloads (e.g., a scheduler performing millions of effects/second) are characterized only by microbenchmarks.
2. Koka's benchmarks are author-reported on the author's implementation; independent reproduction on the current Koka compiler would strengthen the claims.
3. Neither study benchmarks directly against an equivalent monad-transformer implementation — the §4.5 cost model is analytic. A head-to-head `mtl`-vs-handlers benchmark remains valuable future work.

## 6 Limitations

**Multi-shot expressiveness.** OCaml 5 rules out resuming a continuation twice — backtracking search and probabilistic programming must be restructured. Koka permits it, but re-resuming re-executes captured *resources* (handles, locks). Neither system fully solves resource safety under non-linear control flow; Koka's `initially`/`finally` clauses [3] and OCaml's linear-use-plus-`discontinue` discipline [4] are partial answers.

**Static versus dynamic effects.** Koka's row types give totality checking and inference, but rows can become verbose, and the interaction of rows with features like first-class modules and objects remains less explored than Haskell's type-class ecosystem. OCaml's dynamic effects are simple and backward compatible but defer all errors to runtime — an unhandled effect is a crash, not a type error.

**Debugging and tooling.** Delimited continuations are notoriously hostile to debuggers and profilers: a captured continuation has no meaningful "call stack" in the traditional sense, and stepping through handler dispatch confuses source-level debugging. OCaml 5's fiber implementation preserves backtraces across `perform`/`continue` in recent versions, but the ecosystem tooling (profilers attributing time through handler boundaries) is still maturing.

**Reasoning and the value restriction.** Effect polymorphism interacts subtly with polymorphic mutable state; while Kammar and Pretnar showed no value restriction is needed for handlers as such, the broader design space of combining algebraic effects with ML-style references still contains sharp edges (e.g., a polymorphic reference cell storing continuations of different types).

**Adoption inertia.** Transformers are *understood*: predictable performance, type errors, fluent readers. Handlers demand new reasoning principles and, in OCaml's case, acceptance of dynamic effect typing. The technology is ready; the pedagogy is catching up.

## 7 Conclusion

Algebraic effects and effect handlers have completed the journey from Plotkin and Pretnar's theory [1] to engineered, performant reality. Koka demonstrates that with row-polymorphic effect types and type-directed selective CPS compilation, handlers can be both *statically disciplined* — inferred rows, totality checking, duplicate-label elimination — and *efficiently compiled*, with total code paying zero handler tax [2][3]. OCaml 5 demonstrates the complementary point: effect handlers can be retrofitted onto a mature language with **less than 1% average overhead** on existing code, provided one accepts the one-shot continuation discipline that makes fibers sound and cheap [4][5].

Against monad transformers, the argument is structural rather than merely quantitative: handlers eliminate `lift` plumbing, make effect composition local and order-explicit, and — in Koka's case — inferable. The remaining limitations are real but bounded: multi-shot resumption is sacrificed (OCaml) or resource-fraught (Koka), static guarantees and backward compatibility remain in tension, and tooling lags the theory.

The frontier: higher-order effects (operations taking computations as arguments) challenge the first-order algebraic framework; linear type disciplines promise to make one-shot semantics *static* rather than dynamic; and as OCaml 5's ecosystem matures, handler-based architectures at concurrent-systems scale will finally be measurable. The evidence to date favors handlers.
