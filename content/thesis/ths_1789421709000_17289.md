# The Mordell Conjecture: Faltings' Theorem, Height Functions, and Arakelov Geometry

## Abstract

In 1922 Louis Mordell proved that the rational points of an elliptic curve over the rationals form a finitely generated abelian group and conjectured, almost as an aside, that a curve of higher genus admits only finitely many rational points. Six decades later Gerd Faltings proved the conjecture in a landmark 1983 paper that simultaneously settled the Shafarevich and Tate conjectures and earned him the Fields Medal. This article reconstructs the theorem's full architecture. We explain the genus trichotomy, the Jacobian embedding, and Parshin's trick reducing the problem to Shafarevich finiteness; we develop the three height functions — Weil, Néron–Tate, and Faltings — that power the proof; and we show how Arakelov's intersection theory on arithmetic surfaces, completed by Faltings' arithmetic Riemann–Roch, turned height into geometry. We compare the three known proofs — Faltings', Vojta's Diophantine-approximation argument, and the p-adic Hodge-theoretic proof of Lawrence and Venkatesh — and close with the uniform Mordell conjecture and the frontier of effectivity.

## 1 The Genesis of the Conjecture: Mordell, 1922

Diophantine geometry is the study of rational and integral solutions to polynomial equations, a subject named after Diophantus of Alexandria and among the oldest in mathematics [8]. Its central modern question is brutally simple to state and ferociously hard to answer: *given a system of polynomial equations with rational coefficients, describe all of its rational solutions.*

In 1922, Louis Mordell published a paper proving what is now called the **Mordell–Weil theorem**: the group of rational points on an elliptic curve over the rationals is finitely generated. Buried in that same paper was a remark of staggering audacity. Mordell suggested that for curves of *higher* genus, the situation might be far more rigid — that only finitely many rational points could exist at all [8]. At the time, essentially nothing was known in that direction. The conjecture stood, in the words of one expositor, as "an unclimbed mountain" for sixty years.

> Theorem: (Faltings' theorem, formerly the Mordell conjecture) Let *C* be a smooth, projective, geometrically connected curve of genus *g > 1* over a number field *K*. Then the set *C(K)* of *K*-rational points is finite [1].

The statement is comprehensible to a first-year undergraduate — everyone knows what a polynomial equation is — yet its proof required the deepest machinery of twentieth-century arithmetic geometry. When Gerd Faltings published his proof in 1983 [1], the news circulated around the globe with enormous enthusiasm, and the 1986 Fields Medal followed.

The significance of the theorem is not merely the finiteness assertion. Faltings' 1983 paper proved, in one stroke, three great conjectures [8]:

- The **Mordell conjecture** itself — finiteness of rational points on curves of genus exceeding one.
- The **Shafarevich conjecture** — finiteness of isomorphism classes of abelian varieties of fixed dimension and polarization degree over a fixed number field with good reduction outside a fixed finite set of places.
- The **Tate conjecture** for abelian varieties over number fields — the isogeny theorem, asserting that abelian varieties with isomorphic Tate modules (as Galois representations) are isogenous.

Each of these would have been a landmark result alone. Together they reorganized the entire landscape of Diophantine geometry.

## 2 Geometric and Arithmetic Background

### The genus trichotomy

Let *C* be a smooth projective curve over a number field *K*. The genus *g* of *C* — roughly, the number of "holes" in the associated Riemann surface — completely determines the qualitative behavior of *C(K)* [8]:

| Genus | Geometry | Rational points |
|---|---|---|
| *g = 0* | Conic section | Either empty or infinite; parametrizable by lines |
| *g = 1* | Elliptic curve | Finitely generated abelian group (Mordell–Weil); torsion classified by Mazur |
| *g > 1* | Curve of general type | **Finite** — this is Faltings' theorem |

The case *g = 0* is classical: a conic with one rational point is isomorphic to the projective line and has infinitely many. The case *g = 1* is the Mordell–Weil theorem, with the deep supplement of Mazur's torsion theorem restricting the possible torsion subgroups. The case *g > 1* resisted everything until Faltings.

### Jacobians and the Abel–Jacobi embedding

The bridge from curves to the richer world of abelian varieties is the **Jacobian** *J* of *C*: a *g*-dimensional principally polarized abelian variety parametrizing degree-zero line bundles on *C*. Choosing a base point *P₀* in *C(K)* (when one exists), the Abel–Jacobi map

*i : C → J*, *P ↦ P − P₀*

embeds the curve into its Jacobian, converting a one-dimensional Diophantine problem into a question about a distinguished subvariety of an abelian variety. This embedding is the common foundation of all three proofs of the theorem [2].

### Parshin's trick: curves become abelian varieties

The decisive reduction, due to Aleksei Parshin in 1968, shows that the Mordell conjecture *follows from* the Shafarevich finiteness conjecture [8]. Given a rational point *P* on *C*, Parshin constructs a finite ramified covering *C_P → C* of bounded genus, ramified only over *P*. The Jacobian *J_P* of this cover is an abelian variety whose bad reduction is confined to a fixed finite set of places independent of *P*. If only finitely many such abelian varieties exist up to isomorphism — the Shafarevich conjecture — then a classical theorem of de Franchis and Severi (finiteness of nonconstant maps between curves of fixed genera) forces *C(K)* itself to be finite.

![Parshin's trick: from a rational point on a curve to a ramified cover and its Jacobian](public/thesis/ths_1789421709000_17289-0.webp)

Everything therefore hinges on proving Shafarevich finiteness. That is where heights enter.

## 3 Methodology: Three Height Functions

A **height function** is a measure of the arithmetic complexity of a point: roughly, how many digits are needed to write it down. The engine of Faltings' proof — and of all subsequent work — is the interplay of three such measures.

### The naive Weil height

For a rational point of projective space *Pⁿ(Q)*, represented by coprime integers, the logarithmic Weil height is simply the logarithm of the largest coordinate in absolute value:

```python
import math

def naive_height(pair):
    # pair: two coprime integers, a point of the projective line over Q
    a, b = pair
    g = math.gcd(a, b)
    return math.log(max(abs(a // g), abs(b // g)))

pts = tuple((a, b) for a in range(-5, 6) for b in range(1, 6) if math.gcd(a, b) == 1)
small = tuple(p for p in pts if naive_height(p) < math.log(3))
print(tuple(sorted(small)))
```

The crucial feature is the **Northcott property**: for any bound *B*, only finitely many points have height at most *B*. Finiteness of a set of points will always, in the end, be deduced from a height bound plus Northcott.

### The Néron–Tate canonical height

On an abelian variety *A* over a number field, the naive height is not well-behaved under the group law. Néron and Tate constructed a **canonical height** *ĥ : A(K̄) → ℝ≥₀* that is a positive semidefinite quadratic form, satisfying the parallelogram law and vanishing exactly on torsion points. It turns *A(K) ⊗ ℝ* into a Euclidean vector space — the stage on which Vojta's inequality plays out [2]. In a toy functional form:

```haskell
-- A toy model of height functions and the Northcott finiteness property
type Height = Double
type Point  = (Integer, Integer)

naiveHeight :: Point -> Height
naiveHeight (a, b) = log (fromIntegral (max (abs a) (abs b)))

-- The Northcott property: sublevel sets of a height are finite
northcott :: Height -> [Point] -> [Point]
northcott bound = filter (\p -> naiveHeight p <= bound)
```

The canonical height is what turns the Mordell–Weil group into a Euclidean lattice-like object, and it is this Euclidean structure that Vojta's inequality exploits.

### The Faltings height

Faltings introduced a height on the **moduli space** of abelian varieties themselves. For a principally polarized abelian variety *A* over a number field, the Faltings height *h_Fal(A)* is essentially the Arakelov degree of the determinant of the pushforward of the relative dualizing sheaf, equipped with its natural Hodge metric at the infinite places:

```rust
// A schematic Faltings height: the Arakelov degree of the determinant
// of the pushforward of the relative dualizing sheaf.
struct FaltingsHeight {
    // logarithm of the covolume of the period lattice in the Hodge metric
    hodge_term: f64,
    // archimedean analytic torsion corrections
    analytic_torsion: f64,
    // sum of local intersection contributions at finite places
    finite_part: f64,
}

impl FaltingsHeight {
    fn total(&self) -> f64 {
        self.hodge_term + self.analytic_torsion + self.finite_part
    }
}
```

The three heights compare as follows:

| Height | Lives on | Defining property | Role in the proof |
|---|---|---|---|
| Weil height *h* | Projective space | Northcott finiteness | Descent and counting arguments |
| Néron–Tate *ĥ* | Abelian variety *A(K)* | Quadratic form; parallelogram law | Vojta's inequality; gap principles |
| Faltings *h_Fal* | Moduli of abelian varieties | Bounded below; comparable to *h* | Shafarevich finiteness via moduli |

> Theorem: (Faltings' height comparison) On a Siegel modular variety parametrizing principally polarized abelian varieties with level structure, the Faltings height and the naive Weil height differ by a bounded amount [8].

This comparison is the master stroke. The moduli space has only finitely many points of bounded naive height (Northcott); if the Faltings height is comparable to the naive height, then bounding the Faltings height of the abelian varieties arising from Parshin's construction yields finiteness. The proof of the comparison passes through the geometry of Siegel modular varieties and delicate estimates on modular forms.

## 4 The Deep Dive: Arakelov Geometry and the Anatomy of the Proofs

### 4.1 Parshin's Trick and the Reduction to Finiteness

We now examine the reduction in detail. Fix a number field *K* and a finite set of places *S* containing all archimedean ones. The **Shafarevich conjecture** asserts that there are only finitely many *K*-isomorphism classes of *g*-dimensional principally polarized abelian varieties over *K* with good reduction outside *S* [8].

Parshin's construction associates to each *P ∈ C(K)* a covering *π_P : C_P → C* with the following properties:

1. The genus *g'* of *C_P* is bounded by a constant depending only on *g*.
2. The covering is ramified only over *P* (and possibly a fixed finite set of auxiliary points).
3. The Jacobian *J_P = Jac(C_P)* has good reduction outside a fixed finite set *S'* of places, independent of *P*.

Consequently, the map *P ↦ J_P* lands in a finite set of isomorphism classes — *if* Shafarevich finiteness holds. The de Franchis–Severi theorem then says there are only finitely many nonconstant morphisms *C_P → C* of bounded degree between curves of fixed genera, so the fibers of *P ↦ J_P* are finite, and *C(K)* is finite. The entire problem is thus transferred from the curve to the moduli of abelian varieties — exactly where the Faltings height operates.

### 4.2 The Faltings Height and the Siege of the Moduli Space

Faltings' proof of Shafarevich finiteness proceeds through the **Tate conjecture** for abelian varieties over number fields. The logical chain is [1]:

1. Prove a finiteness result for ℓ-adic Galois representations (semisimplicity and the Hom theorem) using monodromy and the theory of Néron models.
2. Deduce the **isogeny theorem**: abelian varieties over *K* with isomorphic Tate modules as Galois representations are isogenous over *K*; equivalently, *Hom_K(A, B) ⊗ ℤ_ℓ → Hom_{Gal_K}(T_ℓA, T_ℓB)* is an isomorphism.
3. From the isogeny theorem, deduce finiteness of isogeny classes of abelian varieties with good reduction outside *S*.
4. Finally, use the height comparison on Siegel modular varieties to pass from finiteness of isogeny classes to finiteness of isomorphism classes — the Shafarevich conjecture.

Step 4 is where the Faltings height does its work. The moduli space *𝒜_{g,n}* of principally polarized abelian varieties with level-*n* structure embeds into projective space via modular forms; points of this moduli space correspond to the abelian varieties we must count. The comparison theorem bounds the Faltings height in terms of the naive height of the moduli point, and Northcott finiteness on projective space finishes the argument. As one survey puts it, "the main idea of Faltings's proof is the comparison of Faltings heights and naive heights via Siegel modular varieties" [8].

### 4.3 Arakelov Geometry: Intersection Theory at Infinity

Parallel to Faltings' proof, a second revolution was transforming how heights are understood. In 1974, **S. J. Arakelov** introduced an intersection theory on *arithmetic surfaces* — two-dimensional schemes proper over the spectrum of a ring of integers — that treats the archimedean (infinite) places on equal footing with the finite primes by equipping line bundles with Hermitian metrics and using Green's functions on the associated Riemann surfaces [7].

The classical intersection theory of algebraic surfaces counts intersections of divisors at finite primes. Arakelov's insight was that a complete intersection theory over *Spec ℤ* must include contributions "at infinity": for each complex embedding of the number field, the corresponding Riemann surface contributes an analytic term computed via Green's functions. A divisor on an arithmetic surface thus carries both finite intersection multiplicities and archimedean metric data, and the resulting **Arakelov intersection pairing** is symmetric, bilinear, and functorial.

![Arakelov intersection theory on an arithmetic surface: finite fibers and archimedean Green's functions](public/thesis/ths_1789421709000_17289-2.webp)

Faltings, in his 1984 "Calculus on arithmetic surfaces," extended Arakelov's work into a full calculus, establishing [7]:

- An **arithmetic Riemann–Roch theorem** for arithmetic surfaces.
- An **arithmetic Noether formula** relating the self-intersection of the dualizing sheaf to the Faltings delta invariant.
- An **arithmetic Hodge index theorem**.
- The **nonnegativity of the self-intersection of the dualizing sheaf**.

In this language, the Faltings height *is* an Arakelov intersection number — the degree of a metrized line bundle — so height theory and intersection theory become the same subject.

A formal specification of the finiteness principle, in the spirit of TLA+, reads:

### 4.4 Vojta's Proof: Diophantine Approximation on the Product

In 1991, Paul Vojta gave a completely different proof of the Mordell conjecture, rooted in **Diophantine approximation** rather than moduli of abelian varieties [2].

The skeleton of the argument [2]:

1. Embed *C* into its Jacobian and work with the Néron–Tate canonical height, which makes *J(K) ⊗ ℝ* a Euclidean space.
2. Prove **Vojta's inequality**: for points *P, Q ∈ C(K)* of large height with small angle between them in this Euclidean space, the heights satisfy a precise inequality forcing a gap — points cannot cluster too tightly at large height.
3. The inequality rests on a higher-dimensional **Roth's lemma** and **Dyson's lemma** on the product *C × C*, producing an auxiliary section of small height via Siegel's lemma and deriving a contradiction from excessive vanishing.
4. Cover the unit sphere of *J(K) ⊗ ℝ* by finitely many small cones; within each cone, Vojta's inequality plus the Northcott property forces finiteness.

Bombieri later gave a more elementary variant of Vojta's proof that avoids Arakelov geometry entirely, replacing it with Siegel's lemma and classical height theory at the cost of considerably heavier explicit estimates [8].

> Theorem: (Vojta's inequality, schematic) There exist constants depending only on the curve such that any two rational points of sufficiently large canonical height lying within a sufficiently small angle satisfy a height gap forcing the set of such points in each angular sector to be finite [2].

## 5 Proofs, Corollaries, and the Uniform Mordell Conjecture

### Three proofs, three philosophies

The Mordell conjecture now has three fundamentally different proofs, each valuable for different reasons [2]:

1. **Faltings (1983)** [1]: via Shafarevich finiteness, the Tate conjecture, and height comparison on Siegel modular varieties. Profound, structural, and the source of the isogeny theorem.
2. **Vojta (1991)** [2]: via Diophantine approximation on *C × C*, Roth's lemma, and Arakelov geometry. Yields Vojta's inequality — a deep statement about the *distribution* of rational points, not merely their finiteness — and extends to the Bombieri–Lang conjecture for subvarieties of abelian varieties.
3. **Lawrence–Venkatesh (2018, published 2020)** [3]: via *p*-adic Hodge theory. Retaining Faltings' setup (the Kodaira–Parshin family), they replace abelian varieties with a fine analysis of how *p*-adic Galois representations vary in a family, using *p*-adic comparison theorems, topological monodromy computations, and the Ax–Schanuel property for period mappings proved by Bakker and Tsimerman. The method extends beyond abelian varieties entirely, giving finiteness results for hypersurfaces of large degree in projective space.

### Corollaries

Faltings' paper immediately yielded statements that had been conjectured independently [8]:

- **Tate's isogeny theorem**: abelian varieties over a number field with isomorphic Tate modules are isogenous.
- **A weak form of Fermat's Last Theorem**: for fixed *n* at least 4, the Fermat curve *xⁿ + yⁿ = 1* has genus *(n−1)(n−2)/2 > 1*, hence only finitely many primitive integer solutions to *aⁿ + bⁿ = cⁿ*.
- **Siegel's theorem on integral points** follows as a consequence of the machinery.

### The uniform Mordell conjecture

A question of Mazur asked whether the *number* of rational points can be bounded solely in terms of the genus — or, more precisely, in terms of the genus, the degree of the number field, and the Mordell–Weil rank of the Jacobian.

In the function-field setting, the constants can even be made fully explicit: for non-isotrivial curves, the number of rational points is bounded by *(16g² + 32g + 184)(20g)* raised to the rank, a completely effective estimate [9].

## 6 Limitations and the Frontier of Effectivity

For all its depth, Faltings' proof — and Vojta's, and Lawrence–Venkatesh's — shares a fundamental limitation: **ineffectivity**. The proof shows that *C(K)* is finite but gives no algorithm to list the points, no bound on their heights, and no bound on their number. The finiteness passes through compactness and contradiction arguments (Northcott plus a height bound whose constants are not computed), so the theorem is, in the logician's sense, purely existential.

The central open problem is therefore the **effective Mordell conjecture**: given explicit equations for *C*, compute *C(K)*. The main conjectural route runs through **Szpiro's conjecture** relating the discriminant and conductor of an elliptic curve; Szpiro's conjecture implies an effective form of Mordell, and work of Moret-Bailly shows that an effective Mordell conjecture would in turn imply the *abc* conjecture.

Further frontiers include:

- **The Bombieri–Lang conjecture**: the higher-dimensional generalization asserting that rational points on a variety of general type over a number field are never Zariski dense; Faltings proved the case of subvarieties of abelian varieties.
- **Effectivity via Lawrence–Venkatesh**: the *p*-adic method is in principle more amenable to explicit computation than Faltings' moduli argument, and making it effective is an active program.
- **Quantitative refinements**: the uniform bounds of Dimitrov–Gao–Habegger and Kühne are a major step, but the optimal dependence on genus and rank — and the removal of the rank dependence entirely — remain unknown.

Arakelov geometry itself continues to expand: the higher-dimensional theory of Gillet and Soulé, Zhang's theory of positive line bundles, and the equidistribution theorems of Szpiro–Ullmo–Zhang (which resolved the Bogomolov conjecture on small points of abelian varieties) all descend from the intersection-theoretic viewpoint that Arakelov introduced and Faltings completed [7].

## 7 Conclusion

The Mordell conjecture began as a one-sentence aside in a 1922 paper and became the organizing theorem of modern Diophantine geometry. Its three proofs trace an arc across the deepest ideas of the subject: Faltings' fusion of moduli theory, Galois representations, and height comparison [1]; Vojta's translation of Nevanlinna theory into Diophantine approximation on arithmetic surfaces [2]; and Lawrence and Venkatesh's *p*-adic variational method [3]. Beneath all three lies the same infrastructure — height functions measuring arithmetic complexity, intersection theory extended to the infinite places by Arakelov [7], and the Jacobian as the universal abelian variety receiving the curve.

The theorem's second life is now underway: uniform bounds depending only on genus and rank [4] [5] [6], explicit constants over function fields [9], and the long march toward effectivity. The mountain Mordell glimpsed in 1922 has been climbed three times by three different routes — and each ascent revealed a larger range beyond.
