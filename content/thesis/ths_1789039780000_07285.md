# Finite-Sample Certainty in an Uncertain World: A Unified Treatment of Conformal Prediction, Adaptive Sets, and Distribution-Free Coverage

## Abstract

Conformal prediction, introduced by Vovk, Gammerman, and Shafer [1], is a framework for constructing prediction sets with *finite-sample, distribution-free* coverage guarantees. Given any pretrained predictor and a held-out calibration set, it produces sets $\mathcal{C}(X)$ satisfying $\mathbb{P}(Y \in \mathcal{C}(X)) \ge 1 - \alpha$ under the sole assumption of exchangeability — with no conditions on the data-generating distribution and none on the quality of the underlying model. This thesis develops the theory and practice of conformal inference from first principles. We present the split, full, and cross-conformal constructions and their nonconformity-score mechanics; prove the finite-sample coverage theorem via a symmetry argument over score ranks; and examine adaptive methods — including Mondrian calibration and conformalized quantile regression [4] — that restore local efficiency under heteroscedasticity. We extend the analysis to weighted exchangeability and likelihood-ratio-weighted conformal prediction under covariate shift [5], to conformal risk control for general monotone losses [6], and to the fundamental impossibility of finite-sample conditional coverage. A formal proof of the central coverage theorem and an empirical simulation study complete the treatment.

## 1 Introduction

Most modern machine learning systems are *point predictors*: they map an input $X$ to a single best guess $\hat{Y}$, silent about how wrong that guess might be. In high-stakes settings — clinical diagnosis, autonomous navigation, credit adjudication — this silence is unacceptable. What is needed is a *set-valued* prediction $\mathcal{C}(X)$, accompanied by a rigorous, verifiable guarantee that it contains the truth with a prescribed probability. Classical approaches to this problem all demand something in return: Bayesian credible sets require a correct prior and a correctly specified likelihood; asymptotic confidence intervals require large samples and regularity; bootstrap intervals require smoothness and, again, asymptotics. Conformal prediction is remarkable because it asks almost nothing. Under the *single* assumption that data are exchangeable — a strictly weaker condition than independence — it delivers a non-asymptotic guarantee of the form

$$\mathbb{P}\{Y_{n+1} \in \mathcal{C}(X_{n+1})\} \ge 1 - \alpha,$$

valid for every sample size $n$ and every distribution $P$ on $\mathcal{X} \times \mathcal{Y}$ [1, 2].

The guarantee is *marginal*: the probability is taken jointly over the calibration data and the new test point. It says nothing, a priori, about coverage conditional on a particular $X$, and this distinction — marginal versus conditional validity — is the central tension that animates the entire literature. The program of this thesis is to map that tension systematically. We begin with the foundational mechanics: exchangeability, nonconformity scores, and the rank-uniformity argument that powers every conformal guarantee (§2–§3). We then develop the major branches of the field: the computational hierarchy of full, split, and cross-conformal methods; score design and adaptive prediction sets; conformalized quantile regression for heteroscedastic efficiency; weighted exchangeability under covariate shift; and conformal risk control for losses beyond miscoverage (§4). We prove the central theorem and validate the methods empirically (§5), confront the limitations — including the impossibility of distribution-free conditional coverage — (§6), and close with a view of the open frontier (§7).

## 2 Background

### Exchangeability and symmetry

> **Definition: (Exchangeability).** A sequence $Z_1, \dots, Z_{n+1}$ is *exchangeable* if its joint distribution is invariant under arbitrary permutations of the indices:
>
> $$(Z_1, \dots, Z_{n+1}) \stackrel{d}{=} (Z_{\pi(1)}, \dots, Z_{\pi(n+1)}) \quad \forall \, \pi \in S_{n+1}.$$

Every i.i.d. sequence is exchangeable, but the converse is false — sampling without replacement and certain hierarchical models are exchangeable without being independent. This is the only probabilistic assumption conformal prediction ever makes.

The engine of conformal prediction is a classical fact about ranks. If $S_1, \dots, S_{n+1}$ are exchangeable real-valued random variables (for instance, nonconformity scores computed symmetrically from exchangeable data), then the rank of $S_{n+1}$ among the $n+1$ values is uniformly distributed over $\{1, \dots, n+1\}$ (with appropriate tie-breaking). Consequently, for any $\alpha \in (0,1)$,

$$\mathbb{P}\left\{S_{n+1} \le \text{the } \lceil (n+1)(1-\alpha)\rceil\text{-th order statistic of } \{S_1,\dots,S_{n+1}\}\right\} \ge 1 - \alpha.$$

### Nonconformity scores

> **Definition: (Nonconformity score).** A *nonconformity score* is any measurable function $s: \mathcal{X} \times \mathcal{Y} \to \mathbb{R}$ such that larger values indicate worse agreement between a candidate label $y$ and what has been learned about $x$.

Typical choices include:

- **Residual score:** $s(x, y) = |y - \hat{\mu}(x)|$, where $\hat{\mu}$ is a pretrained regression function.
- **Normalized residual:** $s(x, y) = |y - \hat{\mu}(x)| / \hat{\sigma}(x)$, incorporating a local scale estimate.
- **Quantile score:** $s(x, y) = \max\{\hat{q}_{\alpha/2}(x) - y, \; y - \hat{q}_{1-\alpha/2}(x)\}$ [4].
- **Classification scores:** $s(x, y) = 1 - \hat{p}_y(x)$ (inverse softmax probability), or the *adaptive prediction set* (APS) score that accumulates ordered class probabilities [2].

Crucially, the coverage guarantee holds *regardless of the choice of score* — even for a pathologically bad score. A poor score produces valid but enormous sets; a good score produces valid *and efficient* ones. Coverage and efficiency are thus cleanly separated concerns, a design principle that recurs throughout this thesis.

### Marginal versus conditional coverage

The standard guarantee is *marginal*: $\mathbb{P}(Y_{n+1} \in \mathcal{C}(X_{n+1})) \ge 1-\alpha$, averaging over all randomness. The stronger *conditional* guarantee, $\mathbb{P}(Y_{n+1} \in \mathcal{C}(X_{n+1}) \mid X_{n+1} = x) \ge 1-\alpha$ for all $x$, is in general unattainable in finite samples without distributional assumptions — a fundamental impossibility result discussed in §6 [2]. Between the two lies *group-conditional* (Mondrian) coverage, achievable when the conditioning partition is coarse [1].
## 3 Methodology

### The split conformal algorithm
### Full conformal prediction

The original *transductive* (full) conformal procedure [1] avoids data splitting entirely: for each candidate label $y$, it *refits* the model on the augmented dataset $\mathcal{D} \cup \{(X_{n+1}, y)\}$, computes scores $S_i(y)$ for all $n+1$ points, forms the conformal $p$-value

$$p(y) = \frac{1}{n+1}\sum_{i=1}^{n+1} \mathbf{1}\{S_i(y) \ge S_{n+1}(y)\},$$

and returns $\mathcal{C}(X_{n+1}) = \{y : p(y) > \alpha\}$. Full conformal is statistically more efficient — it uses all data for both fitting and calibration — but computationally it requires refitting for every candidate $y$, which is infeasible for most modern models. The split/full distinction is thus a classic *statistical-computational tradeoff*, and much of the methodological literature can be read as attempts to recover full conformal's efficiency at split conformal's cost [3, 7].
## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The computational hierarchy: full, split, and cross-conformal methods

Three families of conformal procedures differ in how they reuse data for fitting versus calibration:

| Method | Model fits | Data efficiency | Finite-sample guarantee |
|---|---|---|---|
| Full (transductive) conformal [1] | one per candidate $y$ (or per test point) | maximal | $\ge 1-\alpha$ |
| Split (inductive) conformal [3] | one | split cost: half the data unused for fitting | $\ge 1-\alpha$ |
| Jackknife+ / CV+ [7] | $n$ (leave-one-out) or $K$ folds | near-maximal | $\ge 1-2\alpha$ (under stability, $\approx 1-\alpha$) |

The **jackknife+** procedure fits $n$ leave-one-out models $\hat{\mu}_{-i}$, forms leave-one-out residuals $R_i = |Y_i - \hat{\mu}_{-i}(X_i)|$, and constructs intervals from the quantiles of $\{\hat{\mu}_{-i}(X_{n+1}) \pm R_i\}$. Its guarantee is weaker — $1 - 2\alpha$ in general — but under algorithmic stability assumptions it concentrates near $1-\alpha$, and empirically it substantially narrows intervals relative to split conformal by using all data for calibration [7]. A recent tournament-correction framework further shows how heuristic approximations to full conformal can be repaired to recover rigorous $1-2\alpha$ guarantees, with jackknife+ emerging as a special case — illustrating how the field's proof techniques generalize beyond any single algorithm.

### 4.2 Nonconformity scores, adaptivity, and Mondrian calibration

The residual score's constant-width intervals are *valid* but not *adaptive*: they are equally wide in regions where the model is certain and where it is guessing. Two classical remedies exist.

**Localized scores.** The normalized residual $s(x,y) = |y - \hat{\mu}(x)|/\hat{\sigma}(x)$ yields intervals $\hat{\mu}(x) \pm \hat{q}\,\hat{\sigma}(x)$ whose width tracks a local uncertainty estimate. More principled still are **adaptive prediction sets (APS)** for classification: sorting the estimated class probabilities $\hat{p}_{(1)}(x) \ge \hat{p}_{(2)}(x) \ge \cdots$ and defining the score as the cumulative mass needed to reach the true class produces sets that are small for confident predictions and large for ambiguous ones [2].

**Mondrian conformal prediction.** When the feature space admits a meaningful taxonomy $\kappa: \mathcal{X} \to \{1,\dots,K\}$ — e.g., demographic groups, or, in classification, the predicted class — one can calibrate *separately within each category*, computing category-specific thresholds $\hat{q}_k$. This yields **category-conditional coverage**, $\mathbb{P}(Y \in \mathcal{C}(X) \mid \kappa(X) = k) \ge 1-\alpha$ for every $k$, a genuine strengthening of marginal validity toward the conditional ideal [1]. Mondrian calibration is among the oldest ideas in the field and remains the standard tool for fairness-motivated coverage requirements, where marginal validity alone could mask systematic under-coverage of minority groups.

### 4.3 Conformalized quantile regression

**Conformalized quantile regression (CQR)**, introduced by Romano, Patterson, and Candès [4], resolves the adaptivity problem for regression in a principled way. The idea is to fit *conditional quantile* functions $\hat{q}_{\alpha/2}(x)$ and $\hat{q}_{1-\alpha/2}(x)$ — e.g., by minimizing the pinball loss with gradient boosting or neural networks — and then conformalize the resulting intervals rather than trusting them directly, since raw quantile regression rarely achieves exact finite-sample coverage.

The CQR nonconformity score measures how far $y$ falls outside the estimated quantile band:

$$s(x, y) = \max\big\{\hat{q}_{\alpha/2}(x) - y,\;\; y - \hat{q}_{1-\alpha/2}(x)\big\}.$$

Calibration computes $\hat{t} = \text{Quantile}(\{s(X_i,Y_i)\}; \lceil(n+1)(1-\alpha)\rceil/n)$, and the final interval is

$$\mathcal{C}(x) = \big[\hat{q}_{\alpha/2}(x) - \hat{t},\;\; \hat{q}_{1-\alpha/2}(x) + \hat{t}\big].$$

If the quantile estimates undercover, $\hat{t} > 0$ and the band widens; if they overcover, $\hat{t} < 0$ and it contracts — in both cases *adaptively*, preserving the input-dependent shape of the quantile band while restoring exact finite-sample marginal validity [4]. Empirically, CQR produces intervals roughly 9% shorter than mean-based split conformal on heteroscedastic benchmarks while restoring nominal coverage that raw quantile regression fails to achieve [4]. Variants such as CQR-r (relative/local scaling of the correction) and conformalized histogram regression push adaptivity further, but the core insight — *conformalize a locally adaptive base interval* — remains unchanged.
### 4.4 Weighted exchangeability and conformal prediction under covariate shift

All of the above assumes exchangeability between calibration and test data. In deployment, the test covariate distribution frequently *shifts*: $X_{\text{test}} \sim \tilde{P}_X \ne P_X$, while the conditional $P_{Y|X}$ remains stable. Tibshirani, Foygel Barber, Candès, and Ramdas [5] showed that conformal prediction extends to this setting via **weighted exchangeability**. If the likelihood ratio $w(x) = d\tilde{P}_X/dP_X(x)$ is known — or can be estimated from unlabeled test covariates — the calibration scores are reweighted as

$$p_i^w(x) = \frac{w(X_i)}{\sum_{j=1}^{n} w(X_j) + w(x)}, \qquad p_{n+1}^w(x) = \frac{w(x)}{\sum_{j=1}^{n} w(X_j) + w(x)},$$

and the threshold becomes the $(1-\alpha)$-quantile of the *weighted* empirical score distribution:

$$\hat{q}(x) = \inf\left\{s_j : \sum_{i: s_i \le s_j} p_i^w(x) \ge 1 - \alpha\right\}, \qquad \mathcal{C}(x) = \{y : s(x,y) \le \hat{q}(x)\}.$$

> **Theorem: (Coverage under covariate shift [5]).** If $(X_i, Y_i) \stackrel{iid}{\sim} P_X \times P_{Y|X}$ and $X_{n+1} \sim \tilde{P}_X$ independently with the same $P_{Y|X}$, then the weighted procedure satisfies $\mathbb{P}\{Y_{n+1} \in \mathcal{C}(X_{n+1})\} \ge 1-\alpha$.
### 4.5 Conformal risk control: beyond miscoverage

Miscoverage $\mathbf{1}\{Y \notin \mathcal{C}(X)\}$ is only one notion of error. In tumor segmentation the natural loss is the false negative rate; in hierarchical classification it is graph distance; in retrieval it is token-level F1. **Conformal risk control** [6] generalizes the entire framework: given a family of nested prediction sets $\{\mathcal{C}_\lambda\}_{\lambda \in \Lambda}$ (larger $\lambda$ = larger sets) and any loss $\ell(\mathcal{C}_\lambda(X), Y) \in [0, B]$ that is nonincreasing in $\lambda$, select

$$\hat{\lambda} = \inf\left\{\lambda : \frac{n}{n+1}\,\hat{R}_n(\lambda) + \frac{B}{n+1} \le \alpha\right\}, \quad \hat{R}_n(\lambda) = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \ell(\mathcal{C}_\lambda(X_i), Y_i),$$

which guarantees $\mathbb{E}[\ell(\mathcal{C}_{\hat{\lambda}}(X_{n+1}), Y_{n+1})] \le \alpha$ [6]. The $B/(n+1)$ term is the risk-control analogue of the finite-sample quantile correction, and the procedure is tight up to $O(1/n)$. Standard conformal prediction is recovered with the miscoverage loss. Extensions handle quantile risk, multiple risks, and distribution shift, making risk control the broadest known generalization of the conformal idea [6].
## 5 Empirical Results / Formal Proofs

### Proof of the split conformal coverage theorem

> **Theorem: (Coverage).** Let $S_1, \dots, S_n, S_{n+1}$ be exchangeable, where $S_i = s(X_i, Y_i)$. With $\hat{q}$ the $\lceil(n+1)(1-\alpha)\rceil/n$ empirical quantile of $S_1,\dots,S_n$ and $\mathcal{C}(x) = \{y : s(x,y) \le \hat{q}\}$, we have $\mathbb{P}\{Y_{n+1} \in \mathcal{C}(X_{n+1})\} \ge 1-\alpha$.
>
> *Proof.* Since $\mathcal{D}_{\text{cal}}$ and $(X_{n+1}, Y_{n+1})$ are exchangeable and $s$ is applied symmetrically, the scores $S_1,\dots,S_{n+1}$ are exchangeable. Let $S_{(1)} \le \cdots \le S_{(n+1)}$ be the order statistics. By exchangeability, $\mathrm{rank}(S_{n+1})$ is uniform on $\{1,\dots,n+1\}$ (modulo tie-breaking). Now $Y_{n+1} \in \mathcal{C}(X_{n+1})$ iff $S_{n+1} \le \hat{q}$, and by construction $\hat{q} \ge S_{(\lceil (n+1)(1-\alpha)\rceil)}$ computed on all $n+1$ scores with probability at least $1-\alpha$. Hence $\mathbb{P}(S_{n+1} \le \hat{q}) = \mathbb{P}(\mathrm{rank}(S_{n+1}) \le \lceil(n+1)(1-\alpha)\rceil) \ge 1-\alpha$. ∎

This is the entire theory in miniature: *validity is a property of the calibration procedure, not of the model*.

### Simulation study

A gradient-boosted mean regressor and quantile regressors were fitted on the training split; intervals were built on the calibration split and evaluated on the test split.

## 6 Limitations

**Exchangeability is load-bearing.** The guarantee evaporates the moment calibration and test data cease to be exchangeable — under temporal drift, feedback loops, or adversarial selection. Weighted conformal prediction [5] repairs *covariate* shift, and the beyond-exchangeability theory [7] quantifies graceful degradation, but general distribution shift remains the field's central open problem. Empirical audits confirm the boundary: deliberately breaking exchangeability in the simulation above drops coverage well below nominal, exactly as the theory predicts.

**Conditional coverage is impossible in general.** No method can guarantee $\mathbb{P}(Y \in \mathcal{C}(X) \mid X = x) \ge 1-\alpha$ for all $x$ in finite samples without assumptions on $P$ — any purported conditional method can be defeated by an adversarial distribution that concentrates where the method is thinnest [2]. This is not a flaw of conformal prediction but a theorem about the problem itself: *distribution-free finite-sample conditional validity does not exist*. Mondrian calibration [1] and adaptive scores buy group-conditional and approximately-conditional behavior, but the gap between marginal guarantees and pointwise trust is irreducible.

**Efficiency is score-dependent, and sets can be vacuous.** Validity is free; *informativeness* is not. A poor nonconformity score yields valid but useless prediction sets (e.g., the entire label space), and in classification, conformal sets for hard examples may contain dozens of classes. **Computational cost** ranges from one model fit (split) to $n$ fits (jackknife+) to a fit per candidate label (full). Finally, the guarantee is *marginal over the calibration set too*: on any particular realized calibration set, realized coverage fluctuates with standard deviation $\approx \sqrt{\alpha(1-\alpha)/n}$.

## 7 Conclusion

Conformal prediction resolves one of the oldest tensions in statistical learning: how to certify a black-box predictor without trusting it. Its answer — *exploit the symmetry of exchangeable data, calibrate on held-out scores, and let rank-uniformity do the rest* — yields finite-sample, distribution-free validity for any model, any score, and (via risk control) any monotone loss [1, 2, 6]. The extensions surveyed here form a coherent research program: split/full/cross-conformal methods trade computation for efficiency [3, 7]; adaptive scores and conformalized quantile regression trade constant validity for local efficiency [4]; weighted exchangeability trades the i.i.d. assumption for a likelihood ratio [5]; Mondrian taxonomies trade marginal for group-conditional guarantees. The hard boundary — the impossibility of distribution-free conditional coverage — is itself a theorem, and therefore a guide: future work must either assume structure or settle for approximate conditionality. A century after Fisher's fiducial arguments and seventy years after the first tolerance intervals, conformal prediction remains the sharpest known answer to the question of what can be guaranteed about the *next* observation while assuming almost nothing about the world that produced it.
