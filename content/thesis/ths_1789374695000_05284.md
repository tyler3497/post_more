# Secure Multiparty Computation Against Dishonest Majorities: Garbled Circuits, Oblivious Transfer, and the SPDZ Protocol Family

## Abstract

Secure multiparty computation (MPC) enables a set of mutually distrustful parties to evaluate an agreed function of their private inputs while revealing nothing beyond the prescribed output. The dishonest-majority setting — in which *all but one* party may deviate arbitrarily from the protocol — is the strongest corruption model, and the hardest to make practical. This article traces the theoretical and engineering arc from feasibility to performance. We develop Yao's garbled circuits with the point-and-permute optimization, show how 1-out-of-2 oblivious transfer and the IKNP extension construction reduce expensive public-key operations to a small seed cost, and contrast the GMW boolean-circuit paradigm with arithmetic-circuit approaches. The centerpiece is the SPDZ protocol family: Beaver multiplication triples combined with information-theoretic message authentication codes split computation into a slow input-independent preprocessing phase and a fast, unconditionally secure online phase. We survey modern preprocessing (MASCOT, Overdrive), the universal composability framework used to define dishonest-majority security, and private machine-learning inference as the flagship application driving current research.

## 1 Intro

In 1982, Andrew Yao asked how two millionaires could determine who is richer without revealing their wealth [1] — inaugurating *secure multiparty computation* (MPC), in which *n* parties jointly compute a public function of private inputs while leaking nothing else. MPC is now central to privacy-preserving machine learning, confidential benchmarking, private auctions, and threshold cryptography.

Difficulty depends on the adversary model: *semi-honest* adversaries follow the protocol but mine their transcript, while *malicious* ones deviate arbitrarily. Orthogonal is the *corruption threshold*: an *honest majority* assumes fewer than half corrupt, a *dishonest majority* up to *n − 1* of *n*. Dishonest-majority security is the gold standard — no trust assumption about who is compromised — but it rules out information-theoretic techniques, requires computational assumptions, and forfeits fairness and guaranteed output delivery.

This article surveys the three pillars of dishonest-majority MPC: Yao's garbled circuits with point-and-permute [1]; oblivious transfer and the IKNP extension that made large-scale garbling practical [2]; and the SPDZ family, which pairs Beaver triples with information-theoretic MACs for malicious security with a lightweight online phase [3], supported by modern preprocessing (MASCOT [4], Overdrive) and unified in MP-SPDZ [5]. Security claims are anchored in the universal composability framework [6].

## 2 Background

### The real/ideal paradigm

Security is defined against an *ideal world* in which parties send inputs to an incorruptible trusted party that computes the function and returns the output. A protocol is *secure* if for every real adversary there exists an *ideal-world simulator* producing an indistinguishable transcript; hence the adversary learns nothing beyond the output and cannot influence the computation beyond the corrupt parties' inputs.

> **Theorem (Composition).** If a protocol securely realizes an ideal functionality in the standalone model, it remains secure when run concurrently with arbitrary other protocols *only* under stronger notions such as universal composability [6]. Standalone simulation does not compose in general.

### Adversary models and corruption thresholds

- **Semi-honest (honest-but-curious):** corrupt parties follow the protocol but pool their views to infer honest parties' inputs.
- **Covert:** a malicious adversary that wishes to cheat but fears detection; security is parameterized by a deterrence probability.
- **Malicious:** corrupt parties deviate arbitrarily, send malformed messages, and collude; up to *n − 1* of *n* parties may be corrupt in the dishonest-majority setting.
- **Static vs. adaptive:** corruption is fixed before the protocol starts (static) or may occur mid-execution (adaptive). This article focuses on static corruption, the standard assumption in the SPDZ line of work.

### Secret sharing

Secret sharing splits a value into *shares* distributed among parties so that only authorized subsets can reconstruct it. Two schemes dominate MPC:

1. **Additive sharing:** a value *x* in a finite field is shared as random summands *x_1, …, x_n* with *x = x_1 + ⋯ + x_n*. Any *n − 1* shares reveal nothing; all *n* are needed to reconstruct. This is the workhorse of dishonest-majority protocols.
2. **Shamir sharing:** a degree-*t* polynomial with *x* as its constant term yields shares *x_i = p(i)*; any *t + 1* shares reconstruct *x*. This requires an honest majority (*t < n/2*) and is therefore excluded from the dishonest-majority setting, but it underlies the BGW protocol often contrasted with the approaches here.

We write *⟨x⟩* for an additive sharing of *x*, and *⟨x⟩_i* for party *P_i*'s share.

### Oblivious transfer

*1-out-of-2 oblivious transfer* (OT) is a two-party functionality: the sender inputs two strings *(m_0, m_1)*, the receiver inputs a choice bit *b* and learns *m_b*, while the sender learns nothing about *b* and the receiver learns nothing about *m_{1−b}*. OT is *complete* for MPC — given OT, any efficiently computable function can be securely evaluated — making OT cost the central efficiency metric of the field [2].

## 3 Methodology

Our analysis compares protocols along four axes:

1. **Security model:** semi-honest vs. malicious, and the corruption threshold. We emphasize malicious security with dishonest majority, proved in the UC framework [6] so that protocols compose safely with preprocessing subprotocols and higher-level applications.
2. **Circuit model:** *boolean* circuits (gates AND, XOR, NOT) vs. *arithmetic* circuits over a finite field (gates addition and multiplication). Boolean circuits suit bitwise operations and comparisons; arithmetic circuits suit integer and fixed-point computation such as statistics and neural-network inference.
3. **Cost metrics:** total communication, round complexity, and computational work — distinguishing expensive *public-key* from cheap *symmetric-key* operations, the basis of *OT extension*, which converts a few base OTs into arbitrarily many effective ones [2].
4. **The preprocessing model:** an input-independent *offline phase* (expensive, run before inputs are known) feeding a fast *online phase*. Beaver's triple technique [3] is canonical: random multiplication triples generated offline make online multiplication nearly free.

We present security arguments as simulation sketches and state the key theorems precisely, citing full proofs. Performance figures are drawn from the original protocol papers and the MP-SPDZ implementation framework [3][4][5].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Garbled Circuits and Point-and-Permute

Yao's protocol [1] solves two-party computation of a boolean circuit *C* in a constant number of rounds. One party, the *garbler*, assigns to every wire *w* two random *labels* — symmetric keys *k_w^0* and *k_w^1* representing the bit values 0 and 1. For each gate *g* with input wires *a, b* and output wire *c*, the garbler produces four *doubly encrypted* ciphertexts, one per input combination:

- *Enc_{k_a^0, k_b^0}(k_c^{g(0,0)})*, *Enc_{k_a^0, k_b^1}(k_c^{g(0,1)})*, *Enc_{k_a^1, k_b^0}(k_c^{g(1,0)})*, *Enc_{k_a^1, k_b^1}(k_c^{g(1,1)})*

The ciphertext order is randomly permuted so position reveals nothing about the truth table. The *evaluator* obtains exactly one label per own input wire via oblivious transfer — learning *k_w^b* without revealing *b* — and decrypts gate by gate until output labels are decoded. Fresh random labels mean the evaluator learns nothing about the garbler's inputs or intermediate values.

The naive scheme requires the evaluator to attempt decryption of all four ciphertexts per gate. The *point-and-permute* optimization appends a random *select bit* *p_w* to each wire label; the permuted select bits of the two input labels directly index the correct ciphertext, so evaluation performs exactly one decryption per gate. Combined with *row reduction* (dropping one ciphertext by fixing an output label) and the *half-gates* construction (two ciphertexts per AND gate), modern garbling costs roughly two symmetric-key operations per AND gate and has zero cost for XOR gates under the free-XOR technique [1].

The following sketch captures the evaluator's per-gate logic with point-and-permute:

```python
def eval_garbled_gate(ciphertexts, label_a, label_b):
    # label = (key, permute_bit); ciphertexts indexed by (pa, pb)
    pa = label_a[1]          # select bit of wire a
    pb = label_b[1]          # select bit of wire b
    ct = ciphertexts[(pa, pb)]
    out_label = decrypt(label_a[0], label_b[0], ct)
    return out_label         # evaluator learns nothing about the bit it encodes
```

Yao's protocol is natively semi-honest; a malicious garbler could garble a different circuit than agreed. The classical *cut-and-choose* countermeasure multiplies cost by a statistical security parameter, motivating more efficient malicious two-party protocols — but for semi-honest security, garbled circuits remain essentially round-optimal [1].

### 4.2 Oblivious Transfer and OT Extension

Every garbled-circuit evaluation consumes one OT per input bit of the evaluator, so OT throughput directly determines garbling performance. Public-key OT constructions (based on RSA, Diffie–Hellman, or lattice assumptions) cost milliseconds per instance — far too slow for circuits with millions of input bits.

The breakthrough of Ishai, Kilian, Nissim, and Petrank (IKNP) [2] was to show that a small number *κ* of *base OTs* — where *κ* is the computational security parameter — can be *extended* into any polynomial number *m* of OTs using only symmetric-key primitives. The construction arranges the extension as a matrix: the receiver's choice bits select rows of a pseudorandom matrix, and the sender's base-OT secrets mask the columns, so that each column yields one effective OT. Concretely, *κ* base OTs plus *O(mκ)* hash evaluations produce *m* OTs, amortizing the public-key cost to essentially zero. A circuit requiring a million OTs needs only about 128 public-key operations.

Later work hardened extension against malicious receivers via consistency checks on the choice-bit matrix (notably KOS), at modest overhead [4]. *Correlated* and *random* OT variants reduce communication further and are the exact flavors consumed by modern preprocessing; silent OT extension has since pushed marginal cost below one bit per instance, but IKNP remains the conceptual foundation [2].

### 4.3 The GMW Protocol and Secret-Sharing-Based MPC

While Yao garbles the whole circuit at once, the Goldreich–Micali–Wigderson (GMW) protocol evaluates it gate by gate over secret shares. Each party holds an additive (XOR) share of every wire value. XOR gates are evaluated *locally* — shares of *x ⊕ y* are just the XOR of the shares — while each AND gate requires one 1-out-of-2 OT per pair of parties: the parties use OT to compute shares of the cross terms *x_i · y_j* without revealing their shares. The protocol needs one round per multiplicative layer of the circuit, so round complexity scales with circuit depth.

GMW established the *secret-sharing paradigm* of all later dishonest-majority protocols: keep every intermediate value secret-shared, evaluate linear gates locally, and spend interaction only on nonlinear gates. It is secure against any number of semi-honest corruptions; upgrading it to malicious security via the classical GMW *compiler* (zero-knowledge proofs and commitments) is costly — precisely the gap SPDZ fills with a far more efficient mechanism [3].

| Protocol | Corruption | Circuit type | Costly operation | Rounds |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Yao's garbled circuits [1] | 1 of 2, semi-honest | Boolean | Symmetric-key per AND gate | Constant |
| GMW | *n − 1* of *n*, semi-honest | Boolean | OT per AND gate | Depth of circuit |
| SPDZ [3] | *n − 1* of *n*, malicious | Arithmetic | Public-key (offline only) | Depth of circuit |
| MASCOT [4] | *n − 1* of *n*, malicious | Arithmetic | Symmetric-key (offline) | Depth of circuit |

### 4.4 Beaver Triples and the SPDZ Online/Offline Paradigm

Beaver's 1991 *circuit randomization* technique is the single most influential idea in practical MPC. A *Beaver triple* (or *multiplication triple*) is a triple of secret-shared values *(⟨a⟩, ⟨b⟩, ⟨c⟩)* with *a, b* uniformly random and *c = a · b*. Given such a triple, parties holding *⟨x⟩* and *⟨y⟩* can multiply with only two *openings* (broadcast reconstructions):

1. Open *d = x − a* and *e = y − b* (these masks are uniform, so they leak nothing).
2. Compute *⟨z⟩ = ⟨c⟩ + d·⟨b⟩ + e·⟨a⟩ + d·e*, which equals *⟨x·y⟩* since *(a+d)(b+e) = ab + db + ea + de*.

Addition gates and multiplication by constants are local on additive shares, so the entire online phase reduces to communication of two field elements per multiplication gate — with *no cryptography at all* in the online phase.

The SPDZ protocol of Damgård, Pastro, Smart, and Zakarias [3] secures this blueprint against a malicious dishonest majority using *information-theoretic MACs*. Every shared value *⟨x⟩* is accompanied by shares of a MAC *γ(x) = α · x*, where the global MAC key *α* is itself secret-shared and never reconstructed, so any deviation is caught in a single batched MAC check, keeping the online phase fast. The offline phase generates triples and MACs using somewhat homomorphic encryption with distributed decryption and zero-knowledge proofs of plaintext knowledge.

> **Theorem (SPDZ online phase [3]).** Conditioned on correct preprocessing, the SPDZ online protocol UC-realizes the arithmetic-circuit functionality against a static malicious adversary corrupting up to *n − 1* parties, with information-theoretic security. Each multiplication costs two openings and the total per-party work is linear in the circuit size.

The security argument is a simulation: the simulator extracts corrupt inputs from the opened masks (using the known triple randomness it chose in the ideal preprocessing), and any inconsistent MAC causes an abort indistinguishable from the real protocol's abort. Because the MAC key is information-theoretic, even an unbounded adversary cannot forge tags — the computational assumptions live entirely in the offline phase [3].

A compact Rust-style sketch of the online multiplication step:

```rust
// shares held by party i; (a, b, c) is a Beaver triple, c = a*b
fn beaver_multiply(xi: Fp, yi: Fp, ai: Fp, bi: Fp, ci: Fp) -> Fp {
    let di = open(xi - ai);          // broadcast d = x - a
    let ei = open(yi - bi);          // broadcast e = y - b
    // z = c + d*b + e*a + d*e  ==  x*y
    ci + di * bi + ei * ai + di * ei
}
```

The architectural split — heavy public-key preprocessing feeding a symmetric-key-only online phase — is what makes dishonest-majority MPC practical rather than merely feasible.

### 4.5 Modern Preprocessing: MASCOT and Overdrive

The original SPDZ offline phase [3] used somewhat homomorphic encryption (SHE) with expensive zero-knowledge proofs per ciphertext, making preprocessing the bottleneck. Two lines of work attacked it.

**MASCOT** [4] replaced public-key machinery with *oblivious transfer*: correlated OT yields shares of products of private field elements, and KOS-style consistency checks lift semi-honest extension to malicious security at under 6× the semi-honest cost. Authenticated triples are assembled from pairwise products plus MACs, all with symmetric-key primitives — over 200× faster than SHE-based preprocessing, demonstrated for up to five parties [4].

**Overdrive** kept SHE but made it *practical*: a BGV-type cryptosystem with better packing, the faster TopGear zero-knowledge proof of plaintext knowledge, and tuned parameters cut SHE-based triple generation by orders of magnitude [5]. MASCOT wins on computation, Overdrive on communication in high-latency networks; both ship in MP-SPDZ, making preprocessing a deployment-time choice [5].

Defining what "secure preprocessing" even means requires care: the offline phase must remain secure when composed with the online phase and with arbitrary concurrent sessions. This is exactly what the UC framework [6] provides. Modern proofs model preprocessing as an ideal functionality *F_Prep* delivering authenticated triples, prove the online phase secure in the *F_Prep*-hybrid model, and prove each preprocessing protocol realizes *F_Prep* — the UC composition theorem then yields end-to-end security [4][6].

## 5 Empirical/Proofs

### Security theorems

The central theoretical claim of the SPDZ line is that malicious dishonest-majority security need not slow down the online phase:

> **Theorem (Malicious security with free online phase).** The SPDZ online protocol, given authenticated Beaver triples, securely evaluates any arithmetic circuit against a static malicious adversary corrupting any strict subset of parties, with abort. The online phase uses only field additions and broadcasts; its computational security is information-theoretic [3].

> **Proof sketch.** The simulator runs the adversary against simulated honest parties, choosing the triple randomness itself. When the adversary opens masks *d, e*, the simulator extracts effective inputs as *x = d + a*, *y = e + b* and forwards them to the ideal functionality. Output shares are then programmed to match the ideal output. If any MAC check fails, both real and ideal executions abort. Indistinguishability follows because masks are uniformly random and MACs are information-theoretically unforgeable without the shared key *α* [3].

For garbled circuits, Lindell and Pinkas gave the first complete simulation proof of Yao's semi-honest protocol [1]: the simulator for a corrupt evaluator garbles a circuit that always outputs the ideal result, and indistinguishability reduces to the security of the underlying encryption scheme; the simulator for a corrupt garbler simply runs the OT simulator. Malicious security for garbling is obtained via cut-and-choose, whose proof shows that cheating in more than a negligible fraction of circuits is caught with overwhelming probability [1].

### Measured performance

Concrete numbers from the protocol literature (all for 128-bit computational security unless noted):

1. **Original SPDZ [3]:** with 3 parties over 64-bit fields, online multiplication at ~0.05 ms per gate with total online work linear in *n*; preprocessing ~13 ms per triple — already 2–3 orders of magnitude faster than prior dishonest-majority protocols.
2. **MASCOT [4]:** OT-based preprocessing improved triple-generation throughput by more than 200× over SHE-based SPDZ, with malicious security at under 6× the cost of the semi-honest baseline, demonstrated for up to 5 parties.
3. **MP-SPDZ [5]:** benchmarks dozens of protocol variants on identical circuits, with online phases sustaining tens of thousands of multiplications per second per party on commodity hardware.
4. **OT extension [2]:** IKNP reduces the amortized public-key cost of OT to *κ* base OTs regardless of the number of extended OTs; modern implementations extend millions of OTs per second per core, making OT effectively a symmetric-key-cost primitive.

These figures explain why dishonest-majority MPC crossed from theory to deployment: the online phase — the part that touches real data under latency constraints — is now cheap enough for interactive applications, while the heavy lifting is pushed to an input-independent offline phase that can run overnight [3][5].

## 6 Limitations

**No fairness or guaranteed output.** A classical result (Cleve) shows that with a dishonest majority, fairness — either everyone gets the output or no one does — is impossible for general functions without setup assumptions. SPDZ-family protocols therefore provide *security with abort*: a corrupt party can always deprive honest parties of the output. Applications must either tolerate aborts or add identifiable-abort and financial-penalty mechanisms.

**Preprocessing remains expensive.** The online phase is cheap, but generating millions of authenticated triples still costs real communication and computation. MASCOT's OT-based approach is communication-heavy in wide-area networks; SHE-based Overdrive is computation-heavy. Silent preprocessing from pseudorandom correlation generators promises to shrink this cost dramatically, but maliciously secure constructions remain an active research area [5].

**Setup assumptions for UC security.** UC-secure OT and MPC are impossible in the plain model without setup; deployments assume a common reference string or random oracle, and the hybrid-model proofs in [4][6] must be instantiated carefully.

**Arithmetic vs. boolean mismatch.** SPDZ excels at arithmetic circuits over large fields, but comparisons, bit decompositions, and other bitwise operations are costly in that representation. Mixed-circuit techniques (e.g., BMR-SPDZ hybrids converting between garbled boolean and authenticated arithmetic sharings) exist but add conversion overhead [5].

**Private ML constraints.** Neural-network inference needs fixed-point arithmetic, truncation, and non-linear activations — none native to field arithmetic. Protocols emulate them with probabilistic truncation and piecewise-linear approximations, introducing numerical error that must not degrade model accuracy, while the communication volume of large models strains even optimized preprocessing [5].

**Side channels and implementation risk.** Proofs assume correct implementations; deployments face timing side channels in OT extension, randomness failures in triple generation, and the gap between a UC proof and its code. MP-SPDZ mitigates this with a single audited codebase, but protocol agility is itself a risk surface [5].

## 7 Conclusion

Dishonest-majority MPC has traveled from Yao's 1986 feasibility result to a practical engineering discipline. Garbled circuits with point-and-permute remain the definitive constant-round solution for two-party boolean computation [1]; oblivious transfer extension turned OT from the bottleneck into a near-free resource [2]; and the SPDZ family showed that malicious security against any number of corruptions is compatible with a fast, unconditionally secure online phase, by authenticating Beaver triples with information-theoretic MACs and pushing all public-key work into preprocessing [3]. Modern preprocessing engines — MASCOT's OT-based triples [4] and Overdrive's rehabilitated homomorphic approach — together with the unifying MP-SPDZ framework [5], have made these protocols deployable, all under security definitions grounded in universal composability [6].

The frontier is *application-driven*: private ML at real-model scale, silent preprocessing to remove the offline bottleneck, and stronger guarantees like identifiable abort. The foundations surveyed here — garbling, OT, triples, MACs, UC composition — are the stable core of that next generation.
