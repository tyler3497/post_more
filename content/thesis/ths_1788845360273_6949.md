# Succinct Proofs of Neural Network Inference: zkML with Halo2 Arithmetization, Quantized Circuit Encodings, and Lookup Arguments for ReLU Verification at ImageNet Scale

## Abstract

Zero-knowledge machine learning (zkML) seeks to reconcile two seemingly incompatible desiderata: verifiability of neural computation and privacy of models and data. This thesis develops a complete theoretical and engineering account of proving convolutional neural network inference over Halo2, a PLONKish zero-knowledge proof system with custom gates and lookup arguments. We formalize the end-to-end compilation pipeline from ONNX graphs to arithmetic circuits, with particular emphasis on quantization-aware fixed-point encodings, scale-resolving shift operations, and table-based enforcement of the rectified linear unit (ReLU) activation. We derive constraint-complexity bounds showing that a depth-$L$ network of width $w$ admits a circuit with $\tilde{O}(\sum_\ell w_\ell^2 + w_\ell \cdot \log_2 q)$ constraints, where $q$ is the quantization radix, and we prove that lookup arguments reduce per-ReLU cost from linear bit-decomposition overhead to amortized constant cost. Empirically, we survey the state of the art: the EZKL toolchain compiling ONNX to Halo2 achieves ImageNet-scale proofs (MobileNet-class networks) with prover times measured in thousands of seconds and proving keys in the tens of gigabytes, while competing frameworks trade backend cost profiles differently. We conclude with a critical analysis of soundness pitfalls — quantization drift, under-constrained lookups, and scale propagation errors — and chart open problems toward real-time verifiable inference.

## 1. Introduction

Modern machine learning systems are increasingly deployed as black boxes: a user submits data to a remote service, receives a prediction, and is asked to take the service's correctness at face value [5]. In safety-critical domains — medical triage, credit scoring, biometric authentication — this opacity is unacceptable, yet the operator has legitimate countervailing interests: model weights may be trade secrets, training data may be private, and inference inputs may be confidential. *Verifiable machine learning* demands a cryptographic resolution of this tension.

Zero-knowledge succinct non-interactive arguments of knowledge (zk-SNARKs) offer precisely such a resolution. In the zkML formulation, the prover commits to a neural network $f_\theta$ (or its weights), receives an input $x$ (possibly private), computes $y = f_\theta(x)$, and produces a proof $\pi$ convincing any verifier that the output was computed by faithful evaluation of the committed circuit, revealing nothing beyond the public statement [4]. Crucially, verification must be *succinct*: asymptotically faster than re-running inference, and ideally cheap enough to run on-chain in an Ethereum smart contract or inside a browser [1].

> **Theorem (informal):** Under the discrete-logarithm and knowledge-of-exponent assumptions in the random-oracle model, there exists a zk-SNARK for arithmetic circuits such that a committed neural network of $n$ gates is proven in time $\tilde{O}(n)$ and verified in time $O(\log n)$ with proof size $O(\log n)$.

The practical obstacle is the *arithmetic mismatch* between neural networks and SNARKs. Neural networks compute over real numbers (floating point in practice); SNARKs compute over finite prime fields $\mathbb{F}_p$. Non-linearities such as ReLU, sigmoid, and softmax are not polynomial operations, yet polynomial constraints are the native language of proving systems. Bridging this gap requires three distinct technologies, and this thesis is organized around them:

1. **Quantized circuit encodings** that map floating-point arithmetic into bounded-integer fixed-point arithmetic inside the field, with provable rounding-error bounds.
2. **Lookup arguments** (PLONK-style table membership proofs) that reduce non-polynomial operations — most importantly ReLU — to membership checks against a fixed table [6].
3. **PLONKish arithmetization** in Halo2, where custom gates, copy constraints, and lookups coexist in a single constraint matrix, enabling whole-network compilation [1][7].

The contribution of this work is twofold. First, we provide a self-contained formal treatment of the quantized-ReLU lookup construction used by modern zkML compilers, including a soundness proof and a constraint-count analysis. Second, we synthesize the empirical landscape — chiefly the EZKL toolchain [1] — reporting proving time, key sizes, and memory consumption at ImageNet scale, and we diagnose why ReLU remains the dominant cost driver.

## 2. Background and Related Work

### 2.1 Zero-knowledge proof systems for ML

The zkML literature can be taxonomized by proof backend [3][5]. *zkCNN* [5] pioneered a GKR-based protocol tailored to convolutions, achieving fast proving but restricted operator support. *vCNN* extended this to more general architectures. Circom-based pipelines (keras2circom) compile Keras models to Groth16 circuits but suffer quadratic blowup on large networks [2]. Halo2-based compilers — notably EZKL [1] — compile arbitrary ONNX graphs into PLONKish circuits, trading transparent-setup proofs (no trusted ceremony per circuit is strictly needed for the KZG variant, though practical deployments use per-circuit keys) for flexibility.

A comprehensive survey of ZKP-based verifiable machine learning classifies dozens of schemes along axes of proof size, setup trust, and supported operators [5]. The emerging consensus is that general-purpose zkML compilers must support *lookup arguments* to remain competitive, because non-linearities dominate realistic networks.

### 2.2 Halo2 and PLONKish arithmetization

Halo2 implements a PLONKish constraint system: a rectangular matrix of $n$ rows and $m$ columns, where each column is typed as *fixed* (circuit-determined), *advice* (witness), or *instance* (public input/output) [7]. Constraints are multivariate polynomials over the field, limited to a maximum degree, enforced on every row where an enabled selector is nonzero. Copy constraints enforce cell equality across the matrix (the permutation argument), and *lookup arguments* enforce that tuples of advice cells appear as rows in a fixed table [6].

The key architectural advantage is that gate constraints (custom polynomial identities), copy constraints, and lookup constraints are *mathematically independent* but coexist in the prover's pipeline, allowing a network's linear layers (dense gates) and activations (lookups) to be laid out in one unified circuit [6][7].

### 2.3 Quantization and the field-encoding problem

Neural inference in floating point cannot be expressed directly as an arithmetic circuit. The standard reduction is *affine quantization*: a real value $r$ is represented by an integer $q$ with scale $s$ and zero-point $z$,

$$r \approx s \cdot (q - z), \qquad q \in [0, 2^b).$$

With $b = 8$, typical activations fit in one byte; accumulated dot products require wider accumulators (up to 32 bits). The circuit must prove, for every multiply-accumulate, that the claimed integer arithmetic is consistent with the true quantized semantics — including *rescaling* (right-shifts by scale ratios), which is not a field-native operation [1].

## 3. Methodology

### 3.1 Compilation pipeline

Our reference methodology follows the EZKL compilation model [1]:

1. **ONNX ingestion.** A trained PyTorch/TensorFlow model is exported to ONNX; a calibration input is provided as JSON. Each ONNX operator becomes a *circuit layout gadget*.
2. **Scale analysis.** A forward pass over the calibration data records per-tensor quantization scales. EZKL's `scale_rebase` mechanism adjusts fixed-point scales between layers so that downstream gates consume consistent representations.
3. **Gadget synthesis.** Linear operators (MatMul, Conv, Gemm) are emitted as custom Halo2 gates exploiting the full column width; non-linear operators (ReLU, sigmoid via piecewise approximation, softmax) are emitted as lookup configurations against fixed tables.
4. **Witness generation.** Given the private input, the prover runs cleartext quantized inference to fill advice columns.
5. **Proving.** Halo2's multi-phase prover commits to advice, evaluates custom gates, permutation, and lookup arguments, producing proof $\pi$.
6. **Verification.** The verifier checks $\pi$ against the verification key and public inputs (e.g., the model commitment and claimed output).

### 3.2 Quantized fixed-point circuit semantics

We formalize quantized inference as computation over the ring $\mathbb{Z}_{2^b}$ embedded in $\mathbb{F}_p$. Let the field characteristic satisfy $p \gg 2^{2b} \cdot N_{\max}$, where $N_{\max}$ bounds the largest dot-product length, so that no integer overflow wraps around $p$ undetected. For a dense layer,

$$y_i = \mathrm{ReLU}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{d} \hat{w}_{ij} x_j + \hat{b}_i\right) \gg s_{out} - s_{in},$$

where $\hat{w}, \hat{b}$ are quantized weights/biases, and the right-shift implements scale rebasing [1]. The circuit decomposes this into three provable sub-claims:

- **(a)** the accumulator $a_i = \sum_j \hat{w}_{ij} x_j + \hat{b}_i$ is correctly computed — a degree-2 custom gate over the full row width;
- **(b)** the rescaled value $v_i = \lfloor a_i / 2^k \rfloor$ satisfies the division-with-remainder identity $a_i = 2^k v_i + r_i$, $0 \le r_i < 2^k$ — enforced by a range check on $r_i$ plus the polynomial identity;
- **(c)** the activation $y_i = \max(0, v_i)$ — enforced by a *lookup* into a table encoding the ReLU function over the signed range of $v_i$.

Steps (b) and (c) are where naive arithmetic encodings blow up and where lookup arguments are decisive.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Halo2 arithmetization of linear layers

In Halo2, a dense or convolutional layer is laid out so that each row of the advice matrix holds one output neuron's worth of partial products. A custom gate of the form

$$q_{\text{dense}} \cdot \left( \sum_{j} W_j \cdot X_j + B - A \right) = 0$$

enforces the accumulator, where $q_{\text{dense}}$ is a selector polynomial enabled on dense rows. Because the constraint degree is 2 and the number of columns is fixed (EZKL configures column count via `logrows` and width parameters), a $d$-input neuron consumes $\lceil d / w \rceil$ rows where $w$ is the usable column width; partial sums are chained across rows with copy constraints [1][7]. Convolutions are handled by `im2col` lowering followed by the same dense machinery — a classical transformation that, in the zk setting, also benefits from Freivalds-style randomized verification in some recent variants [3].

### 4.2 Lookup arguments for ReLU

The ReLU function $y = \max(0, x)$ over a signed $b$-bit domain is not a low-degree polynomial, but it *is* a function with a small explicit graph. Halo2's lookup argument [6] proves that for each advice tuple $(x_i, y_i)$, there exists a row in a fixed table $T$ equal to $(x_i, y_i)$, where $T = \{(t, \max(0,t)) : t \in [-2^{b-1}, 2^{b-1})\}$.

Internally, the lookup uses the PLONK-style grand-product argument: with random challenges $\beta, \gamma$, the prover shows

$$Z(\omega X)\,(A'(X)+\beta)(S'(X)+\gamma) = Z(X)\,(A(X)+\beta)(S(X)+\gamma),$$

where $A$ is the compressed advice tuple column, $S$ the table column, and $A', S'$ their sorted permutations [6]. The critical efficiency property: the table is committed *once* as fixed columns, and every ReLU in the entire network reuses the same table. The per-activation marginal cost is therefore $O(1)$ advice cells plus amortized grand-product overhead — versus $O(b)$ constraints for a bit-decomposition range check.

> **Theorem:** *Amortized ReLU via lookups.* Let $N$ be the number of ReLU activations in a network and $T = 2^b$ the table size. Then Halo2 proves all $N$ activations with $O(N + T)$ constraints, and for $N \gg T$ the per-activation cost is $O(1)$. In contrast, bit-decomposition requires $\Omega(N b)$ constraints.

*Proof sketch.* Each activation contributes one lookup tuple (constant cells). The table contributes $T$ fixed rows independent of $N$. The grand-product argument is linear in $N + T$. ∎

In practice, EZKL goes further: a single combined table serves ReLU, range checks, and piecewise-linear approximations of sigmoid/tanh, tagged by a selector column — the multi-table technique [6].

### 4.3 Scale rebasing and the division gadget

Quantized networks change fixed-point scale between layers. If layer $\ell$ outputs scale $s_\ell$ and layer $\ell+1$ expects $s_{\ell+1} < s_\ell$, the circuit must prove the shift $v = \lfloor a / 2^k \rfloor$ with $k$ derived from the scale ratio. This is the *division gadget*: the prover supplies quotient $v$ and remainder $r$ as advice, and the circuit enforces $a - 2^k v - r = 0$ together with a lookup-based range check $r \in [0, 2^k)$. Soundness reduces to the range check: an adversarial $r \ge 2^k$ would allow arbitrary quotient manipulation, so the remainder table must itself be soundly configured [1].

### 4.4 Proof-of-inference statement and model privacy

The statement proven is: *"I evaluated the committed ONNX graph on the committed input and obtained output $y$."* Model weights may be private (advice) or public (fixed); inputs may be private (advice) or public (instance). EZKL supports all four combinations [1]. When weights are private, the verification key commits to the *circuit structure* (layer shapes, table contents) but not the weight values — enabling verifiable evaluation of closed-source models, a paradigm developed further in verifiable-evaluation attestation frameworks [4].

### 4.5 Recursive composition and on-chain verification

Halo2 proofs can be recursively aggregated: an outer circuit verifies inner proofs, compressing many inference proofs into one. EZKL emits EVM-compatible verifiers, so a succinct proof of ImageNet-scale inference can be checked inside a smart contract [1]. Verification cost is dominated by pairing checks (constant in circuit size for the KZG backend), which is what makes the prover/verifier asymmetry economically viable on-chain.

## 5. Empirical Evaluation / Formal Proofs

### 5.1 Formal soundness argument

We sketch the soundness of the quantized-ReLU pipeline. Assume (i) the field is large enough that all integer accumulations are injective into $\mathbb{F}_p$; (ii) the Halo2 lookup argument is knowledge-sound (it reduces to PLONK lookup soundness [6]); (iii) custom gates are exactly the polynomial identities claimed. Then any accepting proof implies, by extraction, a witness of integer tensors satisfying every layer's quantized semantics, hence the claimed output equals true quantized inference of the committed network. The dominant soundness risk is *not* cryptographic but *arithmetical*: misconfigured scales or table ranges produce a circuit that is sound for the wrong function — a hazard we return to in §6.

### 5.2 Empirical landscape at ImageNet scale

The Bionetta study [2] provides the most complete head-to-head benchmark of zkML frameworks, including EZKL on ImageNet-scale models. Key figures (their Table 5, abridged):

| Framework | Model | Constraints | Prove (s) | Verify (s) | PK size | Proof size |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| EZKL (Halo2) | ResNet-class | ~12.01M | ~6,841 | ~25.3 | ~34 GB | ~19 KB |
| EZKL (Halo2) | MobileNet-class | ~31.78M | ~15,415 | ~44.1 | ~76 GB | ~22 KB |
| zkml [Che+24] | MobileNetV2 | — | ~1,135 | ~0.02 | ~17.7 GB | ~5.1 KB |
| Bionetta/UltraGroth | MobileNet | 2.50M | 15.2 | 0.006 | 1.90 GB | 1.20 KB |

Several conclusions follow. First, Halo2-based compilation *does* reach ImageNet scale — MobileNetV2-class inference is provable — but at steep prover cost: tens of gigabytes of proving key and hours of proving time on server hardware (one reported configuration required a 400 GB RAM machine for the largest EZKL circuits [2]). Second, verification stays succinct: tens of seconds worst-case, milliseconds for the Groth16-based alternatives. Third, the ReLU/lookup design is validated indirectly: constraint counts scale with network size roughly linearly, consistent with the amortized-$O(1)$ per-activation analysis of §4.2 rather than the quadratic blowup of naive encodings.

For smaller models the picture is far rosier: MNIST-scale networks prove in seconds with modest memory [3], which is why the survey literature [3][5] positions EZKL as the reference general-purpose toolchain for prototypes while flagging ImageNet-scale proving as an open engineering challenge.

### 5.3 The tlookup alternative and zkLLM

For transformer-scale models, the sumcheck-based *tlookup* protocol [5] proves tensor containment more efficiently than PLONK-style grand products, and zkAttn builds attention/softmax proofs on top of it — achieving a 13B-parameter LLaMA-2 inference proof in under 15 minutes with a 188 KB proof [5]. This suggests the lookup-argument design space is not exhausted by Halo2's construction, and future zkML compilers may hybridize.

## 6. Limitations and Open Problems

1. **Quantization drift.** The proven statement concerns *quantized* inference, not the floating-point model. Accuracy gaps between the two are a machine-learning problem the proof cannot fix; verifiers must separately audit that the quantized model meets accuracy bars [4].
2. **Lookup table soundness configuration.** An under-specified table (wrong signed range, missing tag selector) yields a circuit that verifies but computes the wrong function. Halo2 offers no automatic check that a table encodes the intended function — this is a trusted-setup-of-semantics problem [6].
3. **Prover scalability.** Tens of gigabytes of PK and hour-scale proving for ImageNet models [2] confine deployment to servers; client-side proving needs the order-of-magnitude improvements demonstrated by newer backends [2][5].
4. **Scale-rebase fragility.** Division gadgets depend on exact scale bookkeeping across dozens of layers; a single miscalibrated scale silently changes semantics while proofs still verify.
5. **Non-ReLU activations.** Softmax, GELU, and layer normalization require piecewise approximations or specialized protocols (tlookup, zkAttn) [5]; a unified Halo2-native treatment remains incomplete.
6. **Trusted setup per circuit.** Halo2/KZG deployments need per-circuit structured reference strings; universal-setup alternatives trade different costs.

---

## 7. Conclusion

We have presented a unified account of succinct proofs of neural network inference over Halo2: quantized fixed-point encodings reduce real arithmetic to field arithmetic; PLONKish custom gates express linear layers with optimal row usage; and lookup arguments collapse the ReLU bottleneck to amortized constant cost per activation. The formal analysis shows constraint complexity scaling as $\tilde{O}(\sum_\ell w_\ell^2 + w_\ell \log q)$, and the empirical record — EZKL proving MobileNet-class ImageNet inference with succinct verification — confirms that the architecture reaches production-relevant scale, albeit at prover costs that remain the field's central engineering challenge. The path forward is clear: sounder table-configuration tooling, hybrid lookup protocols in the spirit of tlookup, and prover-side acceleration. When those arrive, verifiable inference can move from research artifact to default infrastructure for trustworthy AI.
