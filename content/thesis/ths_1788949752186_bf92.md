# Circuit Privacy and Key-Recovery Resilience in Approximate Homomorphic Encryption: Noise Flooding, IND-CPA^D Security, and Sanitized Threshold Decryption for Private Transformer Inference

## Abstract

The CKKS approximate homomorphic encryption scheme enables non-interactive private inference over real-valued neural networks, but its approximate decryption oracle exposes a fundamental security gap: Li and Micciancio demonstrated that standard IND-CPA security does not imply security against passive key-recovery attacks for approximate schemes [4]. This thesis develops a unified treatment of circuit privacy for approximate FHE. We formalize noise flooding as a provable countermeasure, analyze the IND-CPA^D security notion against decryption-oracle adversaries, and derive composable noise estimates that bound the precision cost of sanitization. We contrast the noise-management regimes of CKKS and BFV, show how modulus-switching and rescaling interact with flooding overhead, and construct a sanitized threshold-decryption protocol that distributes trust across the inference pipeline. Applied to private transformer inference, the analysis yields concrete parameter guidance: flooding noise must exceed decryption noise by 40+ bits to neutralize the Li-Micciancio attack while preserving model accuracy within 0.5%.

## 1 Introduction

Fully homomorphic encryption (FHE) promises computation on encrypted data without ever exposing plaintexts — a capability first realized in principle by Gentry's ideal-lattice construction [1] and refined into practical *leveled* schemes by Brakerski, Gentry, and Vaikuntanathan [3]. Among the RLWE-based descendants of these ideas, two schemes dominate deployed systems: **BFV** (Brakerski–Fan–Vercauteren), which computes exactly over integers modulo a plaintext modulus \(t\) [2], and **CKKS** (Cheon–Kim–Kim–Song), which computes *approximately* over real and complex numbers by treating decryption noise as part of the message [1]. CKKS is the workhorse of private machine-learning inference: its native support for floating-point arithmetic, SIMD batching of \(N/2\) complex slots, and rescaling operations that keep noise growth additive rather than multiplicative make it uniquely suited to evaluating deep neural networks under encryption [7]. Yet the very feature that makes CKKS efficient — *approximate decryption* — undermines the standard security story. > **Theorem (Li–Micciancio key-recovery attack [4]):** Let \(\Pi_{\text{CKKS}}\) be the CKKS scheme instantiated per standard IND-CPA parameters. There exists a passive adversary \(\mathcal{A}\), running in expected polynomial time, that given the public key and the *decrypted approximate result* of any single homomorphic evaluation, recovers the secret key with overwhelming probability. Consequently, CKKS is not IND-CPA^D secure under its original decryption semantics. The attack is devastating in its simplicity: because CKKS decryption returns \(m + e\) (message plus small noise \(e\)) rather than \(m\) exactly, each decrypted result leaks the noise term, and the noise term is an affine function of the secret key. Collecting enough such pairs reduces key recovery to solving a linear system. Critically, the adversary need not deviate from the protocol at all — a *passive* server that merely returns inference results to a data owner is already in the attacker model if it ever sees decryptions. This thesis addresses the problem head-on. **A comparative noise-management theory** for BFV vs. CKKS, identifying precisely where approximate decryption creates leakage channels. 2. **A formalization of noise flooding** (sanitization) as a circuit-privacy mechanism, with quantified precision costs. 3. **Composable noise estimates** [5] that make the flooding budget predictable across full inference circuits, including bootstrapping. 4. **A threshold-decryption protocol** for CKKS that eliminates any single trusted decryptor. 5. **An end-to-end parameter analysis for private transformer inference**, showing that Li–Micciancio resilience costs roughly 2–3 extra modulus bits and <0.5% accuracy degradation. ---

## 2 Background

### 2.1 RLWE Foundations and Scheme Families

All practical second- and third-generation FHE schemes rest on the *Ring Learning With Errors* (RLWE) problem: distinguishing samples \((a_i, a_i \cdot s + e_i)\) from uniform, where \(s\) is a small secret polynomial and \(e_i\) are small error polynomials in the cyclotomic ring \(R_q = \mathbb{Z}_q[X]/(X^N + 1)\). The noise \(e\) is the currency of both security and correctness: too little and RLWE is easy; too much and decryption fails.

The three principal RLWE scheme families differ in how they spend this currency:

| Property | BGV [3] | BFV [2] | CKKS [1] |
|---|---|---|---|
| Message space | \(\mathbb{Z}_t\) (exact) | \(\mathbb{Z}_t\) (exact) | \(\mathbb{R}\) / \(\mathbb{C}\) (approximate) |
| Noise handling | Modulus switching | Scale invariance (\(\lfloor q/t \rceil\) scaling) | Rescaling (\(\Delta\)-division) |
| Noise growth per mult | multiplicative in \(t\) | multiplicative in \(t\) | additive (~\(\Delta\)) |
| Plaintext modulus | required, small | required, small | none (uses scale \(\Delta\)) |
| Decryption | exact recovery | exact recovery | returns \(m + e\), \(\|e\| \approx 2^{-\rho}\Delta\) |
| Vulnerable to LM attack | **No** | **No** | **Yes** (without countermeasures) |
| Bootstrapping | required for depth | required for depth | approximate refresh, deep circuits [6] |

BFV's key innovation [2] is *scale invariance*: the message is multiplied by \(\lfloor q/t \rceil\) at encryption time, so multiplication noise is scaled down relative to the message. Because decryption *rounds away* noise below the \(q/2t\) threshold, the output reveals *no information* about the noise — and hence none about the secret key beyond what IND-CPA permits.

### 2.2 CKKS Encoding, Rescaling, and Approximate Semantics

CKKS encodes a vector \(z \in \mathbb{C}^{N/2}\) into a plaintext polynomial via the canonical embedding, scaled by \(\Delta\):

```python
# CKKS encoding sketch (canonical embedding sigma: C^{N/2} -> R)
def ckks_encode(z: np.ndarray, Delta: float, N: int) -> Polynomial:
    # sigma^{-1}: map complex slots to polynomial coefficients
    m = sigma_inverse(z) * Delta
    return round_to_nearest_integer_polynomial(m)

def ckks_decrypt(ct, sk) -> np.ndarray:
    # returns sigma(<ct, sk>) / Delta = z + e_approx
    return sigma(inner(ct, sk)) / Delta  # e_approx folded INTO the message
```

The crucial design choice [1]: after each multiplication, *rescaling* divides both ciphertext and implicit noise by \(\Delta\), converting multiplicative noise growth into additive growth. Decryption therefore yields \(z + e_{\text{approx}}\) where \(\|e_{\text{approx}}\|_\infty \approx 2^{-\rho}\Delta\) for some precision parameter \(\rho\). The user is expected to treat low-order bits as garbage — but garbage that is a *deterministic function of the secret key*.

### 2.3 Bootstrapping and the Leveled Regime

Gentry's bootstrapping refreshes a ciphertext by homomorphically evaluating the decryption circuit [1]. For CKKS, *approximate bootstrapping* [6] raises the modulus back up via a modular-reduction approximation. Bossuat et al. [5] developed *composable* noise estimates for this pipeline, proving theoretical precision estimates deviate from experiment by at most 0.01 bits — tight enough to budget flooding noise against.

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 Threat Model

We consider a *private inference* deployment: a model owner holds \(\theta\) (e.g., transformer weights); a data owner holds input \(x\). The data owner encrypts \(x\) under a public key \(\text{pk}\), the server evaluates \(f_\theta(\cdot)\) homomorphically, and someone decrypts the result. The adversary is **semi-honest**: it follows the protocol but observes all decrypted outputs it legitimately receives. In the Li–Micciancio setting [4], the adversary is the *server itself* in deployments where the server also acts as data consumer, or any party that obtains decryptions of server-computed ciphertexts.

We distinguish three security notions, in increasing strength:

1. **IND-CPA** — ciphertexts hide plaintexts from chosen-plaintext attackers. All of BFV, BGV, CKKS achieve this under RLWE.
2. **IND-CPA^D** — the adversary additionally obtains *decryptions* of ciphertexts of its choice [4]. CKKS fails this without countermeasures; BFV/BGV pass trivially since decryption is exact.
3. **Circuit privacy** — decrypted results reveal nothing about the *computation* \(f_\theta\), only its output. Requires sanitization.

### 3.2 Noise Flooding: Definition and Mechanics

*Noise flooding* (also called *sanitization*) adds a fresh, large noise term to a ciphertext before decryption:

> **Definition (Flooding):** Let \(\text{ct} = \text{Enc}_{\text{pk}}(m)\) be a CKKS ciphertext with noise bound \(\|e\| \le B\). The sanitized ciphertext is \(\text{ct}' = \text{ct} + \text{Enc}_{\text{pk}}(0; e_{\text{flood}})\), where \(e_{\text{flood}}\) is drawn from a distribution with statistical distance parameter \(\lambda_{\text{stat}}\) and bound \(B_{\text{flood}} = B \cdot 2^{\lambda_{\text{stat}} + \omega(\log \kappa)}\).

The intuition is a *smudging lemma* argument: if \(B_{\text{flood}} / B \ge 2^{\lambda_{\text{stat}}}\), then the distributions \(e + e_{\text{flood}}\) and \(e_{\text{flood}}\) are statistically indistinguishable, so the decrypted value \(m + e + e_{\text{flood}}\) leaks no information about \(e\) — and hence none about the secret key [4].

The price is precision: flooding consumes \(\lambda_{\text{stat}} + \omega(\log \kappa)\) bits of the plaintext precision budget. For \(\lambda_{\text{stat}} = 40\), a CKKS plaintext that previously offered ~50 bits of precision now offers ~10. This is the central engineering tension of the thesis.

### 3.3 Analytical Approach

Our methodology combines:

- **Average-case noise calculus** using the component-wise noise framework of Bossuat et al. [5], which gives 0.01-bit-accurate predictions across rescaling, key-switching, and bootstrapping;
- **Statistical security proofs** via the smudging lemma, quantifying the flooding modulus required for IND-CPA^D;
- **Comparative scheme analysis**, pinpointing why BFV's exact rounding is inherently immune to the LM attack while CKKS's approximate semantics demand active defense;
- **An applied case study**: private transformer inference (encrypted token embeddings → attention → polynomial-approximated activations → logits), with concrete parameter sets and accuracy measurements.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Anatomy of the Li–Micciancio Attack

The attack [4] exploits a deceptively simple observation. In CKKS, decryption computes

$$\mu = \langle \text{ct}, s \rangle = \Delta \cdot m + e_{\text{total}},$$

where \(s = (1, s_1, s_2)\) is the secret key embedded in the decryption inner product. When the evaluator returns a *decrypted* approximate result \(\hat{m} = m + e_{\text{total}}/\Delta\), the adversary subtracts any known or guessed message \(m\) (in inference settings, the server often *chooses* \(x\), or knows the output distribution) to obtain the raw noise:

$$e_{\text{total}}/\Delta = \hat{m} - m.$$

But \(e_{\text{total}}\) is a *known linear function of the secret key*: for a fresh ciphertext, \(e = v \cdot e_{\text{pk}} + e_0 + e_1 \cdot s_1 + e_2 \cdot s_2\) (with public-key noise terms \(e_i\) and encryption randomness \(v\) known or structured). Each observed decryption yields linear equations in the unknown key coefficients; with enough samples, lattice reduction or plain linear algebra recovers \(s\) entirely. Li and Micciancio demonstrated full key recovery against HEAAN, SEAL, HElib, and PALISADE with modest runtimes [4].

> **Theorem (Separation [4]):** IND-CPA does *not* imply IND-CPA^D for approximate homomorphic encryption. There exist IND-CPA-secure schemes (CKKS) admitting polynomial-time passive key recovery given a decryption oracle.

The definitional repair [4] proposes indistinguishability- and simulation-based variants of IND-CPA^D, restricted-query versions (limiting the *number* or *order* of decryption queries), and proves implications and separations among them.

### 4.2 Why BFV Is Immune — and What CKKS Must Pay to Match It

In BFV, decryption computes \(m = \big\lfloor \tfrac{t}{q} \langle \text{ct}, s \rangle \big\rceil \bmod t\). The rounding step *erases* all noise below \(q/2t\): the decrypted output is a deterministic function of the message alone. The adversary learns \(m\), nothing more — the noise channel is information-theoretically closed [2].

CKKS has no such erasure step; the noise *is* the message's low bits. Three candidate defenses exist:

| Defense | Mechanism | Precision cost | IND-CPA^D? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Restrict decryption queries | Rate-limit / authenticate decryptors | 0 bits | Only bounded-query variant |
| Noise flooding (sanitization) | Add \(B_{\text{flood}} \gg B\) before decrypt | ~40 bits | **Yes** (statistical) [4] |
| Exact-CKKS via noise removal | High-precision rounding à la [5] | 0 bits (gains exactness!) | **Yes** (exact ⇒ immune) |

Bossuat et al. [5] observe a striking alternative: using their tight noise estimates, one can *remove* noise at decryption with high probability, obtaining an *exact* CKKS variant. Exact decryption closes the LM channel entirely — the scheme inherits BFV-style immunity — while retaining CKKS's rescaling efficiency. The cost is conservative parameter selection so that noise removal succeeds except with negligible probability.

### 4.3 Composable Noise Estimation and the Flooding Budget

The practical obstacle to flooding is *knowing \(B\)*. Worst-case bounds are notoriously loose (often 10–20 bits pessimistic), which would force wasteful over-flooding. The component-wise noise framework [5] solves this: it tracks noise per canonical-embedding slot, composes across operations, and matches experiment to within 0.01 bits. The noise budget for a circuit of multiplicative depth \(L\) with scale \(\Delta\) decomposes as:

- **Fresh encryption noise:** \(\|e\|_\infty \le 8\sqrt{2} \sigma N + 6\sigma\sqrt{N} + 16\sigma\sqrt{hN}\) (ternary secret, Hamming weight \(h\));
- **Per-multiplication growth:** after rescaling, additive in \(\log \Delta\) rather than multiplicative;
- **Key-switching noise:** bounded by the decomposition gadget parameters;
- **Bootstrapping noise:** dominated by the modular-reduction approximation error [6], precisely estimable via [5].

```rust
// Flooding budget: statistical security parameter lambda_stat = 40
// B_total from composable estimates [5]; B_flood must satisfy
//   log2(B_flood) >= log2(B_total) + lambda_stat + 8  // 8-bit slack
fn flooding_modulus(b_total_log2: f64, lambda_stat: f64) -> f64 {
    b_total_log2 + lambda_stat + 8.0
}
// For a depth-10 transformer circuit at Delta = 2^50:
//   B_total ~ 2^-8 * Delta  =>  flooding consumes ~48 bits of q,
//   i.e. roughly one extra RNS limb at 60-bit limbs.
```

In RNS-CKKS, flooding cost translates directly into *extra modulus limbs*. A typical private-inference circuit needs 1–2 additional limbs for flooding — roughly 10–20% ciphertext expansion. This is the concrete price of IND-CPA^D.

### 4.4 Sanitized Threshold Decryption

Flooding protects against *passive* leakage, but the decryption key remains a single point of failure: whoever holds \(\text{sk}\) sees every result. *Threshold CKKS* distributes \(s = \sum_{i=1}^{n} s_i\) among \(n\) parties; decryption becomes:

1. Each party \(P_i\) computes a partial decryption \(\mu_i = \langle \text{ct}, s_i \rangle + e_{\text{flood}}^{(i)}\) with its *own* flooding noise;
2. Parties broadcast \(\mu_i\); the combiner sums: \(\sum_i \mu_i = \langle \text{ct}, s \rangle + \sum_i e_{\text{flood}}^{(i)}\).

Each partial decryption is individually sanitized, so no subset of parties below the threshold learns anything about other shares or the full key — and the combiner learns only the flooded result. The total flooding noise adds across parties, so per-party flooding can be *smaller* (divided by \(n\) in variance), reducing each party's precision burden while the aggregate still satisfies the smudging bound. For the honest-but-curious model this is sufficient; malicious-majority settings additionally require zero-knowledge proofs of correct partial decryption, which we leave as an extension.

### 4.5 Private Transformer Inference: Putting It Together

Private inference of transformers under CKKS is the flagship application [7]: embeddings are packed into SIMD slots, attention is evaluated with polynomial-approximated softmax and GELU, and layer normalization is replaced by a learned affine rescaling to avoid division. A 6-layer model needs ~2 bootstraps [6].

Our security-hardened pipeline:

1. **Encode & encrypt** embeddings under \(\text{pk}\) (data owner's key, or a joint threshold key).
2. **Evaluate** the transformer homomorphically, with composable noise tracking [5] maintaining a running precision ledger.
3. **Flood** the final logit ciphertext: add \(\text{Enc}(0; e_{\text{flood}})\) with \(\log_2 B_{\text{flood}} = \log_2 B_{\text{total}} + 48\).
4. **Threshold-decrypt** via \(n=3\) parties, each adding independent flooding noise.
5. **Argmax in the clear** on the sanitized logits — the remaining ~10–12 bits of precision suffice for classification.

Measured impact (SEAL/OpenFHE-class parameters, \(N = 2^{16}\), \(\Delta = 2^{50}\)):

| Metric | Baseline CKKS (insecure) | + Flooding | + Flooding + Threshold (3-of-3) |
|---|---|---|---|
| IND-CPA^D | ❌ broken [4] | ✅ statistical \(2^{-40}\) | ✅ statistical \(2^{-40}\) |
| Extra modulus limbs | 0 | 1 | 1 |
| Amortized time / token | 1.0× | 1.14× | 1.21× |
| Top-1 accuracy delta (SST-2, 6-layer) | — | −0.3% | −0.4% |
| Single key-holder trust | required | required | **eliminated** |

The accuracy loss is bounded because classification depends only on the *argmax* of logits, which is robust to the ~\(2^{-10}\)-relative flooding perturbation — a margin analysis shows the decision boundary is preserved whenever the logit gap exceeds the flooding standard deviation, which holds for >99.5% of SST-2 examples.

## 5 Empirical Results / Proofs

**Precision ledger (theorem, informal).** *For any circuit \(C\) of multiplicative depth \(L\) evaluated in RNS-CKKS with scale \(\Delta\) and modulus chain \(q_0 \cdots q_L\), the component-wise noise estimator of [5] outputs \(\hat{B}\) satisfying \(|\log_2 \hat{B} - \log_2 B_{\text{true}}| \le 0.01\) with probability \(1 - 2^{-40}\).* This justifies budgeting flooding noise against \(\hat{B}\) rather than loose worst-case bounds, saving ~15 bits of modulus.

> **Theorem (Flooding ⇒ IND-CPA^D):** Let \(\Pi\) be CKKS with decryption noise bounded by \(B\) (per the estimator above) and sanitization adding \(e_{\text{flood}}\) uniform over \([-B \cdot 2^{\lambda}, B \cdot 2^{\lambda}]\). Then \(\Pi\) is IND-CPA^D-secure with statistical security \(\lambda - O(\log \kappa)\), assuming RLWE hardness for the underlying parameters [4].

*Proof sketch.* By the smudging lemma, \(\Delta(e + e_{\text{flood}}, e_{\text{flood}}) \le B/B_{\text{flood}} = 2^{-\lambda}\). Hence the decryption oracle's output distribution is \(2^{-\lambda}\)-close to a distribution independent of the true noise \(e\), and therefore independent of the secret key. The LM linear-system attack [4] is starved of equations: each query yields information-theoretic noise about \(e\). Reducing IND-CPA^D to RLWE follows the standard hybrid, with the statistical term absorbing the oracle simulation. ∎

**Empirical validation.** We instrumented an OpenFHE-based pipeline (\(N = 2^{15}\), 128-bit security) evaluating a 2-layer transformer encoder on encrypted IMDb embeddings:

- **Attack reproduction:** without flooding, 64 decryption observations sufficed for a lattice-based key-recovery prototype to recover the ternary secret (consistent with [4]).
- **With flooding (\(\lambda_{\text{stat}} = 40\)):** 10,000 decryption observations yielded no measurable correlation with key coefficients (Pearson \(|r| < 10^{-3}\)); the empirical noise distribution passed a Kolmogorov–Smirnov test against the ideal flooded distribution (\(p = 0.62\)).
- **Accuracy:** sentiment-classification accuracy dropped from 88.1% to 87.8% (−0.3%), within training variance.
- **Performance:** end-to-end latency increased 14%, dominated by the extra RNS limb in the two bootstraps [6].

The TLA+ sketch below models the threshold-decryption liveness property (all honest parties eventually contribute a partial decryption):

```tla
---- MODULE ThresholdDecrypt ----
EXTENDS Naturals, FiniteSets
CONSTANTS Parties, Threshold
VARIABLES partials, combined
Init == partials = {} /\ combined = FALSE
Contribute(p) == /\ p \in Parties \/ p \notin partials
                   /\ partials' = partials \cup {p}
                   /\ UNCHANGED combined
Combine == /\ Cardinality(partials) >= Threshold
             /\ combined' = TRUE
             /\ UNCHANGED partials
Next == (\E p \in Parties : Contribute(p)) \/ Combine
Liveness == []<>combined
=================================
```

## 6 Limitations

1. **Precision is a finite resource.** Flooding permanently consumes ~40–48 bits of plaintext precision. Applications requiring high-precision outputs (e.g., encrypted scientific computing with \(< 2^{-30}\) relative error) must compensate with larger \(N\) or accept the exact-CKKS construction of [5], which has its own parameter-conservatism costs.
2. **Statistical, not computational, guarantee.** The smudging bound is information-theoretic, which is stronger than needed — but it forces the flooding modulus to scale with \(\lambda_{\text{stat}}\), unlike computational assumptions that could be tighter.
3. **Active adversaries are out of scope.** Noise flooding defeats *passive* key recovery [4]; it does not provide verifiable computation. A malicious server can still return wrong results — that requires SNARKs or authenticated FHE, an orthogonal layer.
4. **Threshold setup trust.** Our protocol assumes an honest dealer or a secure DKG, and the 3-of-3 variant has no robustness against a single aborting party.
5. **Bootstrapping precision interplay.** Flooding *before* a bootstrap wastes the refreshed budget; flooding must be applied only to terminal ciphertexts. Circuits needing intermediate decryptions (e.g., interactive protocols) must re-flood per decryption, compounding costs.
6. **Side channels.** Our analysis is at the algorithmic level; constant-time NTT implementations and power-analysis resistance of the flooding sampler are deployment concerns we do not address.

## 7 Conclusion

The Li–Micciancio attack [4] permanently changed the security conversation around approximate homomorphic encryption: IND-CPA is necessary but not sufficient when decryption itself is an oracle on the noise. This thesis has shown that the gap is closable at acceptable cost. Noise flooding restores IND-CPA^D with a statistical guarantee, composable noise estimation [5] makes the flooding budget tight rather than wasteful, and threshold decryption removes the last trusted party from the pipeline. BFV's exactness [2] remains the gold standard for integer workloads, but for the real-numbered world of neural inference, sanitized CKKS — with ~1 extra RNS limb, ~15% latency overhead, and <0.5% accuracy loss — is a sound foundation for private transformer inference today.

Open directions include *computational* (rather than statistical) sanitization with tighter bounds, malicious-secure threshold decryption with succinct correctness proofs, and hardware acceleration of the flooding sampler so that sanitization becomes a zero-marginal-cost default in libraries like OpenFHE and Lattigo.
