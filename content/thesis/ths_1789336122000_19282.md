# Unclonable Cryptography: Quantum Money, Copy-Protection, and One-Shot Signatures

## Abstract

This thesis develops a unified treatment of **unclonable cryptography**: the family of quantum cryptographic primitives whose security rests on the impossibility of copying unknown quantum states. Beginning with Wiesner's 1970s conjugate-coding proposal for **quantum money** [1], we trace the lineage through the no-cloning theorem [2], the hidden-subspace public-key money of Aaronson and Christiano [3], and Zhandry's *quantum lightning* and group-action constructions [4][5]. We then turn to **quantum copy-protection**, formalized by Aaronson in 2009 [6], covering point functions, pseudorandom functions, and compute-and-compare programs [7][8][9]. Next we analyze **one-shot signatures** (Amos, Georgiou, Kiayias, Zhandry) [10] and the recent standard-model constructions of Shmueli and Zhandry [11], then **unclonable encryption** [12][13], **monogamy-of-entanglement** proof techniques, and the **impossibility results** of Ananth and La Placa [14] that circumscribe what can be copy-protected. We conclude with open problems, oracle separations, and a research agenda for the next decade.

## 1. Introduction

Classical cryptography assumes *information can be copied*: keys, signatures, ciphertexts, and programs are bit strings, duplicable at negligible cost. This copyability is what makes piracy, key leakage, and counterfeiting endemic — and what makes unforgeable money and unpiratable software classically impossible [6].

Quantum mechanics removes this assumption at the root: the **no-cloning theorem** [2] forbids copying an arbitrary unknown quantum state by any linear operation. Unknown states can be consumed, measured, and transmitted, but not duplicated. *Unclonable cryptography* converts this physical restriction into cryptographic functionality that is provably impossible classically.

> **Thesis:** Unclonability is not a single primitive but a design principle. It yields quantum money, quantum lightning, copy-protected software, one-shot signatures, unclonable encryption, and single-decryptor encryption — and it does so through a small set of recurring techniques: conjugate coding, subspace and coset states, monogamy-of-entanglement games, and indistinguishability obfuscation in the standard model.

Section 2 reviews conjugate coding, no-cloning, and monogamy of entanglement; Section 3 fixes the definitional framework; Section 4 develops money, lightning, copy-protection, one-shot signatures, and unclonable encryption; Section 5 surveys proof techniques; Section 6 records limitations and impossibility results; Section 7 concludes with open problems.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 Conjugate Coding and Wiesner's Quantum Money

In the late 1960s, Stephen Wiesner conceived **quantum money** [1]: banknotes of qubits prepared in randomly chosen bases, each paired with a classical serial number recorded by the issuing bank. Each qubit encodes one classical bit in one of two conjugate bases — rectilinear or diagonal — and the bank stores, per serial number, the encoded bits and the secret basis choices.

Verification is private-key: the bank measures each qubit in its recorded basis. A counterfeiter guessing bases introduces a detectable error with probability at least one quarter per qubit, so forging *n*-qubit notes succeeds with probability at most (3/4)^n. Wiesner's idea predated the no-cloning theorem by over a decade and remained unpublished for years, eventually appearing in *SIGACT News* in 1983 [1].

### 2.2 The No-Cloning Theorem

Wootters and Zurek, and independently Dieks, proved in 1982 that no unitary operation can clone an arbitrary unknown quantum state [2]:

> **Theorem:** There exists no unitary operator *U* acting on a Hilbert space *H* tensored with an ancilla such that *U*(|psi> tensored with |0>) = |psi> tensored with |psi> for all |psi> in *H*.

*Proof sketch.* Suppose such a *U* existed. Linearity forces *U* to map the superposition (|0> + |1>)/sqrt(2) tensored with |0> to (|00> + |11>)/sqrt(2), but cloning demands ((|0> + |1>)/sqrt(2)) tensored with itself — a contradiction.

Crucially, the theorem covers only *unknown* states: known orthogonal states copy perfectly, and approximate cloning achieves bounded fidelity. Unclonable cryptography must therefore ensure the relevant states are unknown and non-orthogonal, and that approximate cloning does not suffice for the adversary.

### 2.3 Public-Key Quantum Money and the Minischeme Framework

Wiesner's scheme is *private-key*: only the bank, which holds the secret basis table, can verify. A far more ambitious goal is **public-key quantum money**, where anyone can verify a note's validity using only public information, yet no efficient counterfeiter can produce two notes that both pass verification. Public-key money cannot be information-theoretically secure — an unbounded adversary could search for a state the public verifier accepts — so security must rest on computational assumptions [3].

Aaronson and Christiano introduced the *minischeme* abstraction [3]: the bank issues a single note (serial number *s*, state rho_s) with no secret state retained, and security requires that no counterfeiter given one valid note can produce two (possibly entangled) states that both pass verification. Any full public-key scheme reduces to a secure minischeme plus a classical digital signature scheme binding serial numbers.

### 2.4 Monogamy of Entanglement

A recurring proof technique across unclonable cryptography is the **monogamy of entanglement** (MoE): roughly, if Alice shares strong correlations with Bob, she cannot simultaneously share equally strong correlations with Charlie. Formalized as *MoE games*, this yields quantitative bounds: two isolated adversaries, Bob and Charlie, cannot both extract the same secret from a single quantum register. Broadbent and Lord used MoE games to analyze unclonable encryption [12]; the hidden-coset framework extended MoE to *coset states* to obtain copy-protection of pseudorandom functions [9]; and recent interactive variants power unclonable encryption with interaction [13].

## 3. Methodology

We adopt a definitional methodology common to the literature. Each primitive is specified by a tuple of algorithms (Setup/KeyGen, Mint/Protect/Gen, Verify/Eval) and a security *game* between a challenger and a quantum polynomial-time (QPT) adversary. Security is *unclonability-flavored*: the adversary receives one legitimate quantum object and wins only if it produces *two* objects that both satisfy the honest acceptance predicate. This two-for-one structure is the unifying motif of the field.

Our technical exposition follows three methodological pillars:

1. **Oracle-first design.** We first build primitives relative to classical or quantum oracles, where query lower bounds (the inner-product adversary method, one-way-to-hiding) give clean proofs [3][6], then discuss de-oraclization via iO or LWE.
2. **Reduction to monogamy.** We reduce unclonability to monogamy-of-entanglement games, turning a cloning adversary into players violating a known MoE bound [12][13].
3. **Impossibility mapping.** We pair each feasibility result with its impossibility counterpart — notably Ananth and La Placa [14] — to delineate the boundary of the possible.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Quantum Money: From Wiesner to Hidden Subspaces to Group Actions

**Hidden-subspace money.** Aaronson and Christiano's breakthrough [3] constructs public-key quantum money relative to a classical oracle. A banknote is the *subspace state* |A>, the uniform superposition over all vectors of a random subspace *A* of dimension *n*/2 inside the vector space over the binary field of dimension *n*. The serial number provides membership oracles for *A* and its orthogonal complement *A*-perp. Verification — membership oracle for *A*, quantum Fourier transform, membership oracle for *A*-perp, second Fourier transform — provably computes a rank-one projection onto |A>. Security rests on a new query lower bound — the **inner-product adversary method** — showing that cloning |A> requires exponentially many oracle queries. Their proposed de-oraclization — obfuscating membership with low-degree polynomials vanishing on *A* — was later broken in polynomial quantum time via Zariski tangent-space computation [15].

**Quantum lightning.** Zhandry [4] defined **quantum lightning**: a strengthening of public-key money in which *no* procedure — not even honest minting — can produce two valid notes sharing a serial number except with negligible probability. Combined with a classical blockchain, lightning yields decentralized payments with unbounded throughput and no trusted mint [16]. Zhandry gave the first construction from the multi-collision resistance of random degree-2 polynomial systems, and a win-win theorem: either hash functions satisfy strong new security notions, or they imply quantum money [4].

**From assumptions.** Later work based quantum money on standard assumptions: quantum lightning from multi-collision resistance [4], and — most significantly — public-key money and lightning from **abelian group actions** (instantiable from elliptic-curve isogenies) with security in the generic group-action model [5]. A 2024 duality result extended this to non-abelian group actions under a direct computational assumption [17].

| Scheme | Year | Setting | Assumption / Model |
|---|---|---|---|
| Wiesner [1] | 1970s/1983 | Private-key | Information-theoretic (conjugate coding) |
| Aaronson–Christiano [3] | 2012 | Public-key (oracle) | Classical oracle; polynomial obfuscation (broken [15]) |
| Zhandry lightning [4] | 2019 | Public-key lightning | Multi-collision resistance of degree-2 systems |
| Zhandry group actions [5] | 2023/2024 | Public-key / lightning | Generic group-action model + isogenies |
| Noise-tolerant PK money | 2024 | Public-key (oracle) | Classical oracle + quantum error correction |

### 4.2 Copy-Protection: Software That Cannot Be Pirated

Aaronson formalized **quantum copy-protection** in 2009 [6]: given a Boolean function *f*, produce a quantum state rho_f that allows evaluating *f* on any input, but from which no efficient adversary can produce two states that both usefully compute *f*. Classically this is impossible — any classical program string can be copied — so copy-protection is a *separating* example of quantum advantage in cryptography.

Security is defined by the **pirate game**: the pirate receives one copy-protected program, splits it into a bipartite state for freeloaders Bob and Charlie, and both must evaluate correctly on random challenge inputs — succeeding with probability significantly better than trivial guessing must be infeasible.

Progress came in stages:

- **Point functions.** Aaronson [6] gave oracle constructions for copy-protecting point functions relative to a quantum oracle, plus candidate explicit schemes built from random stabilizer states.
- **Compute-and-compare programs.** The QROM construction of arXiv:2009.13865 [7] gave the first copy-protection scheme with provable security in a standard cryptographic model, for compute-and-compare programs — programs that output 1 exactly when *f(x) = y* — and simultaneously established secure software leasing and a precise relationship between unclonable encryption and copy-protection.
- **Pseudorandom functions.** The hidden-coset result of Coladangelo, Liu, Liu, and Zhandry (Crypto 2021) [9] copy-protects *PRFs* from iO + LWE using coset states — subspace states masked by a quantum one-time pad — with security from a new *direct-product hardness* and strong monogamy property of coset states. A PRF key compactly represents a random function, so this is nearly copy-protection of arbitrary unlearnable functionality.
- **Classical oracle constructions.** "New approaches for quantum copy-protection" [8] showed that *any* unlearnable program can be copy-protected relative to a *classical* oracle, improving Aaronson's original quantum-oracle result, and that watermarkable programs admit copy-detection.

### 4.3 One-Shot Signatures: Keys That Self-Destruct

**One-shot signatures (OSS)** [10] are among the most striking unclonable primitives: setup produces a classical verification key *vk* and a *quantum* signing key |sk>; signing *one* chosen message measures and thereby destroys |sk>, making a second signature under *vk* impossible even for the honest signer. Classically this is trivially impossible — a classical key can be backed up before use.

Applications include blockchainless cryptocurrency [16], quantum money with classical communication, and non-interactive certifiable min-entropy [10]. The original construction was justified only relative to a classical oracle, and its proof was later found to contain a fatal bug, leaving even oracle-model OSS open for several years. This was resolved in 2025: Shmueli and Zhandry gave the first *standard-model* OSS assuming sub-exponential iO and LWE, plus the first unconditionally secure construction relative to a classical oracle, introducing *permutable pseudorandom permutations* as the key technical tool [11]. A subsequent work gave a simpler and more efficient OSS scheme with a clean analysis of the Shmueli–Zhandry distinguisher [18].

```python
# Conceptual sketch of the one-shot signature security game (AGKZ20)
def oss_security_game(adversary, security_param):
    crs = Setup(security_param)          # classical common reference string
    vk, sk_quantum = KeyGen(crs)        # vk classical, sk_quantum quantum
    m1, sig1, m2, sig2 = adversary(crs, vk, sk_quantum)
    # Adversary wins only if BOTH signatures verify under the same vk
    # and the messages are distinct:
    return (m1 != m2
            and Verify(crs, vk, m1, sig1)
            and Verify(crs, vk, m2, sig2))
# Security: Pr[game returns True] <= negl(security_param) for all QPT adversaries.
# Classically this game is trivially winnable by copying sk; quantumly,
# signing consumes sk_quantum via measurement.
```

### 4.4 Unclonable Encryption and Monogamy of Entanglement

Broadbent and Lord introduced **unclonable encryption** [12]: encryption of a classical message such that two cooperating-but-isolated adversaries, Bob and Charlie, cannot *both* recover the plaintext — even after the secret key is revealed to them. Their construction combines Wiesner's conjugate coding with a quantum-secure PRF modeled as an oracle, proved via the one-way-to-hiding lemma and monogamy-of-entanglement bounds. Follow-up work strengthened the notion to *unclonable indistinguishability*, gave constructions from minimal assumptions (post-quantum one-way functions for private-key; post-quantum PKE for public-key), established lower bounds including a 0.75^n cloning attack against the original scheme, and showed that strong unclonable encryption implies copy-protection of point functions [19]. Interactive variants — where the receiver must interact with the sender to decrypt — achieve information-theoretic security via a strengthened MoE property of coset states in which Charlie also learns Bob's answer [13].

The conceptual payoff is a web of implications among unclonable primitives:

1. One-shot signatures imply quantum money, quantum lightning, and non-collapsing hash functions [11][18].
2. Strong unclonable encryption implies copy-protection of point functions [19].
3. Copy-protection of balanced binary functions implies unclonable decryptors with CPA security, liftable to CCA2 with signatures [20].
4. Quantum lightning plus a classical blockchain yields decentralized payments [16].

-- Type-level sketch of the unclonability interface shared by these primitives

## 5. Empirical Results and Proofs

Lacking fault-tolerant quantum hardware, the field's "empirical results" are theorem statements with quantitative bounds plus the cryptanalytic record. We summarize the principal results.

> **Theorem:** (Aaronson–Christiano [3]) Relative to classical membership oracles for *A* and *A*-perp, any quantum counterfeiter that maps |A> to a state from which two verifiers both accept must make 2^{Omega(n)} oracle queries, where *n* is the security parameter.

> **Theorem:** (Zhandry [4]) Assuming the multi-collision resistance of random degree-2 polynomial systems, there exists a quantum lightning scheme: no QPT adversary, given the public parameters, can output two valid bolts with the same serial number except with negligible probability.

> **Theorem:** (Coladangelo–Liu–Liu–Zhandry, hidden cosets [9]) Assuming post-quantum iO and LWE, there exists a copy-protection scheme for pseudorandom functions secure against malicious pirates in the standard model.

> **Theorem:** (Shmueli–Zhandry [11]) Assuming sub-exponential iO and LWE, one-shot signatures exist in the standard model; unconditionally, they exist relative to a classical oracle.

> **Theorem:** (Ananth–La Placa [14]) There exist unlearnable function families that cannot be copy-protected in the plain model, even approximately. Hence general copy-protection requires oracles, idealized models, or computational assumptions beyond the function's unlearnability.

The cryptanalytic ledger is equally instructive and counts as the field's empirical feedback loop:

- **Broken:** the Aaronson–Christiano polynomial instantiation [15]; Zhandry's 2017 multivariate-hash money, clonable via its own verification circuit [15]; the original AGKZ one-shot signature proof, which contained a fatal bug [11].
- **Standing:** Wiesner's private-key money remains information-theoretically secure against non-adaptive attacks (adaptive "online attacks" were addressed by Aaronson–Christiano's private-key variants [3]); the hidden-subspace *oracle* scheme's exponential query lower bound is unbroken; group-action money stands in the generic group-action model [5]; standard-model OSS from iO+LWE is the current frontier [11].

Security bounds at a glance:

| Primitive | Best known bound | Model | Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| Subspace money [3] | 2^{-Omega(n)} for poly queries | Classical oracle | Secure (oracle); instantiation broken [15] |
| Quantum lightning [4] | negl(lambda) | Standard model (MCR) | Standing |
| Group-action money [5] | negl(lambda) | Generic group-action model | Standing |
| Copy-protection of PRFs [9] | negl(lambda) | Standard model (iO + LWE) | Standing |
| One-shot signatures [11] | negl(lambda) | Standard model (iO + LWE) | Standing (2025) |
| Unclonable encryption [12] | Adversary success <= 0.85^n (one-time) | QROM / oracle | Improved to semantic security [19] |
| General copy-protection [14] | Impossible, even approximate | Plain model | Impossibility |

## 6. Limitations

1. **Oracles and idealized models.** Many feasibility results live relative to oracles [3][6][8][10]. De-oraclization via iO yields only asymptotic candidates: current iO is astronomically inefficient, and LWE-based subspace-hiding instantiations remain theoretical.
2. **Broken instantiations.** Plausible explicit schemes have fallen to cryptanalysis — the Aaronson–Christiano polynomial system and Zhandry's multivariate scheme [15] — and unclonable schemes have received far less scrutiny than lattice-based PKE, so each candidate must be treated as provisional.
3. **Impossibility boundaries.** Ananth and La Placa [14] show copy-protection cannot cover all unlearnable functions in the plain model. Positive results must thread the corridor between learnability (trivially re-learnable) and unprotectability.
4. **Quantum hardware.** All these primitives need long-lived quantum memory and quantum state transmission. Noise-tolerant constructions are a first step, but fault-tolerant quantum money remains decades away.
5. **Definitional subtleties.** Security games differ on freeloader communication, challenge distributions, and approximate correctness. The literature's definitions are still stabilizing — the 2025 OSS repairs are evidence [11][18].
6. **No-cloning is not enough.** The adversary also receives verification oracles and public parameters, which can leak enough structure to clone, as the tangent-space attack shows [15]. Arguing that public information is useless for cloning is the most fragile step in each proof.

## 7. Conclusion

Unclonable cryptography turns a physical law — no-cloning — into functionalities provably impossible classically: uncounterfeitable money, unpiratable software, single-use signatures, and unsplittable encryption. From Wiesner's banknotes [1] through hidden subspaces [3], quantum lightning [4], group actions [5], copy-protected PRFs [9], and standard-model one-shot signatures [11], the field has moved from oracle-model feasibility toward standard-model constructions from iO and LWE.

Three directions define the frontier: *assumption minimization* (LWE alone, without iO?), *cryptanalysis* of candidate explicit constructions [15], and *physical realization* via noise tolerance and long-lived quantum memory. The no-cloning theorem guarantees that nature will not copy our secrets; it remains our task to build systems worthy of that guarantee.
