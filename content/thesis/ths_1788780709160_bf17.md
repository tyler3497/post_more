# Specifying and Verifying Distributed Protocols with TLA+: PlusCal, TLC, and Safety Proofs

## Abstract

Distributed protocols such as Paxos and Raft resist conventional testing: their correctness depends on rare interleavings of message delays, crashes, and recoveries that no test suite enumerates exhaustively. TLA+, Leslie Lamport's specification language based on the Temporal Logic of Actions (TLA), gives engineers a mathematical notation for describing a protocol as a set of behaviors — infinite sequences of states — and a model checker, TLC, that explores those behaviors mechanically. This thesis presents the semantic foundations of TLA+ (actions, stuttering invariance, safety versus liveness), the PlusCal algorithm language that compiles to TLA+, and a methodology for specifying consensus protocols in TLA+. We exhibit fragments of TLA+ specifications of Paxos and of Raft leader election, state the key safety invariants (agreement, election safety) with proof sketches by induction on reachable states, and analyze how TLC copes with state-space explosion through symmetry reduction, state constraints, and parallel search. We close with industrial evidence — Amazon Web Services' decade-long deployment of TLA+ across DynamoDB, S3, and EBS [4] — and the method's well-documented limitations.

## 1 Introduction

A distributed consensus protocol is a contract signed in the presence of an adversary: the network. Messages are delayed, duplicated, reordered, and lost; processes crash and recover with amnesia; clocks drift. Correctness therefore cannot be established by testing alone, because the interesting executions — the ones that violate safety — are precisely the executions no test harness ever produces. As Lamport observed, what we need is *exhaustively testable pseudocode*: a description of a system precise enough to be checked mechanically, and abstract enough to be written before the system is built [1][2].

TLA+ is that description language. TLA stands for the *Temporal Logic of Actions* [1]: a system is described by a formula whose models are behaviors, and an action is a relation between an old state and a new state. TLA+ layers set theory and first-order logic on top of TLA, adds the PlusCal algorithm language for engineers who think in pseudocode, and ships the TLC explicit-state model checker, which enumerates reachable states and tests them against invariants [4][8]. Since 2011, Amazon Web Services has used exactly this toolchain to find subtle, serious bugs in the designs of DynamoDB, S3, EBS, and others — bugs that survived extensive design reviews, code reviews, and conventional testing [4].

This thesis has three aims. First, to present the semantic core of TLA+ with enough precision that a reader can read real specifications: actions, primed variables, stuttering invariance, fairness, and the safety/liveness distinction. Second, to show the methodology in action on consensus: TLA+ fragments of Paxos and of Raft leader election, with stated invariants and inductive proof sketches. Third, to evaluate the machinery honestly — the state-space explosion problem and its mitigations, the model/code gap, and where the method breaks down.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 From temporal logic to the logic of actions

Classical linear temporal logic reasons about *states*: formulas are built from state predicates and temporal operators. TLA, introduced by Lamport in 1994 [1], generalizes the atomic unit from a state predicate to an *action* — a formula relating the current state to the next state. An action `A` is evaluated on a *step* `⟨s, t⟩`; for instance, `x' = x + 1` is the action "the new value of `x` is one plus the old value." A behavior is an infinite sequence of states `s₀, s₁, …`, and a TLA formula is a predicate on behaviors [1].

The grammar restricts how actions may appear: outside of `□[A]_f` (and its dual `◇⟨A⟩_f`), actions occur only inside temporal modalities. This restriction is what buys *stuttering invariance*: the formula cannot distinguish a behavior from one in which some steps are repeated. Formally:

> **Theorem (Stuttering invariance [1]).** For every TLA formula `F` and all behaviors `σ`, `τ` that differ only by finite repetitions of states (stuttering steps), `σ ⊨ F` iff `τ ⊨ F`.

The practical consequence is enormous. A high-level specification and a low-level implementation may take different numbers of internal steps to accomplish the same observable change; stuttering invariance means a refinement relation between them need not align steps one-for-one. Without it, every abstraction level would have to agree on step granularity, and hierarchical specification would be unworkable [1][3].

### 2.2 The TLA+ language and its tools

TLA+ is a concrete language for writing TLA specifications, combining untyped ZF-style set theory with the temporal operators [2]. The standard workflow has three components:

- **TLA+** proper: the mathematical specification language (`.tla` files).
- **PlusCal**: an algorithm language resembling structured pseudocode that translates into TLA+; each PlusCal process becomes a disjunct of the `Next` action [4]. It lowers the entry barrier for engineers who do not think in pure mathematics.
- **TLC**: the explicit-state model checker. Given a finite model (concrete values for the specification's constant parameters), TLC explores the reachable state graph breadth-first, checking invariants and deadlock-freedom, and emits a counterexample trace — a concrete behavior violating the property — when it finds one [4][8].

A canonical specification has the shape

```tla
Spec == Init /\ [][Next]_vars /\ Fairness
```

where `Init` is a state predicate describing initial states, `Next` is a disjunction of actions describing possible steps, `vars` is the tuple of all variables, and `Fairness` is a conjunction of weak/strong fairness conditions on actions [1][2]. TLC checks *finite* instances of this schema; proofs in TLAPS (the TLA+ Proof System) can additionally establish properties for unbounded instances.

### 2.3 Safety versus liveness

Alpern and Schneider's characterization, adopted by TLA+, divides properties into two classes [3]. A **safety** property asserts that "nothing bad ever happens": it is violated by a finite prefix of a behavior, and it can be checked by examining each reachable state against an invariant. A **liveness** property asserts that "something good eventually happens": it is violated only by an infinite behavior, and checking it requires fairness assumptions. TLA+ expresses this cleanly:

| Class | TLA+ form | Checked by TLC as | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Safety | `□P` (state predicate `P`) | Invariant over reachable states | *No two leaders in the same term* |
| Safety | `□[A]_v` (action constraint) | Every step satisfies `A` or stutters | *Committed entries never change* |
| Liveness | `◇P`, `P ~> Q` | Requires fairness; checked on infinite behaviors | *A proposed value is eventually chosen* |
| Fairness | `WF_v(A)`, `SF_v(A)` | Conjoined to `Spec` | *An enabled message is eventually delivered* |

Safety is where TLC shines: every reachable state is tested against the invariant, and a violation yields a finite counterexample [8]. Liveness checking is supported but weaker — TLC can detect liveness violations only under fairness constraints and bounded exploration, and industrial practice overwhelmingly concentrates on safety [4].

## 3 Methodology

Our methodology follows the design-level verification workflow established at AWS [4] and in the consensus literature [5][6][7]:

1. **Write the specification from the design, not the code.** The spec is a mathematical model of the algorithm: variables for node state, message buffers as sets, actions for each protocol step. Constants (e.g., the set of acceptors, quorum definitions) are left as parameters, instantiated by the TLC model.
2. **State the invariants as theorems.** Each safety property becomes a TLA+ invariant — a state predicate `Inv` — with the proof obligation `Spec ⇒ □Inv`. Before model checking, sketch the inductive proof: `Init ⇒ Inv` and `Inv ∧ [Next]_vars ⇒ Inv'`.
3. **Model-check small instances exhaustively.** Choose the smallest configuration that exercises the protocol's logic (e.g., 3 acceptors, 2 proposers, bounded ballots). Run TLC to explore *all* reachable states; iterate on the spec until TLC reports success, treating every counterexample as either a protocol bug or a specification bug.
4. **Mitigate state explosion deliberately.** Apply symmetry reduction on interchangeable node sets, state constraints bounding message multiset sizes, and `VIEW` abstractions when the full state is too large; scale out with multi-core and distributed TLC for the final confidence run [4][8].
5. **Refine toward implementation.** Relate the abstract spec to a lower-level design via a refinement mapping — a state function from implementation states to specification states — and verify the mapping with TLC; stuttering invariance is what makes this sound [1][3].

Crucially, this methodology never claims to verify code. As Newcombe et al. stress, TLA+ verifies *models of designs*; the correspondence between the checked model and the shipped system remains an engineering judgment — "all models are wrong, some are useful" [4].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The semantic core: actions, priming, and temporal operators

Everything in TLA+ is built from a small kernel. Variables are uninterpreted; a *state* assigns values to variables; `x'` denotes the value of `x` in the next state. An *action* is a formula containing primed and unprimed variables, interpreted over a step `⟨s, t⟩`. The table below summarizes the operators a reader of real specs must know [1][2]:

| Operator | Reading | Semantics (on step or behavior) |
|---|---|---|
| `x' = e` | primed variable | value of `x` in next state equals `e` (evaluated in current state) |
| `UNCHANGED x` | no change | `x' = x` |
| `[A]_v` | `A` or stutter | `A ∨ (v' = v)` |
| `⟨A⟩_v` | `A` and change | `A ∧ (v' ≠ v)` |
| `□F` | always | `F` holds at every suffix of the behavior |
| `◇F` | eventually | `F` holds at some suffix |
| `F ~> G` | leads-to | `□(F ⇒ ◇G)` |
| `WF_v(A)` | weak fairness | `◇□ENABLED ⟨A⟩_v ⇒ □◇⟨A⟩_v` |
| `SF_v(A)` | strong fairness | `□◇ENABLED ⟨A⟩_v ⇒ □◇⟨A⟩_v` |
| `ENABLED A` | action enabled | `∃` next state making `A` true |

Two semantic points deserve emphasis. First, `ENABLED A` is evaluated in a single state but quantifies existentially over successors — it is how TLA+ expresses "this step is possible now" without naming the successor [1]. Second, weak fairness is the workhorse of protocol specs: `WF_vars(DeliverMessage)` says that a message that *stays* deliverable must eventually be delivered, which is exactly the liveness assumption real networks approximate. Strong fairness is needed only when an action can be enabled and disabled repeatedly, as in some leader-election schemes [2].

> **Theorem (Safety decomposition).** Every property expressible in TLA is the conjunction of a safety property and a liveness property, and every TLA specification `Init ∧ □[Next]_v ∧ L` (with `L` a fairness conjunction) has this form with safety part `Init ∧ □[Next]_v`.

This is why the methodology of Section 3 works: TLC checks the safety part exhaustively, and the liveness part is handled by fairness plus (bounded) temporal checking [1][4].

### 4.2 PlusCal: pseudocode that compiles to mathematics

PlusCal lets an engineer write what looks like an algorithm and obtain a TLA+ specification mechanically [4]. A PlusCal process is translated into a disjunct of `Next`; labels become control-point values of a program-counter variable. Consider a sketch of a Paxos proposer's Phase 2a in PlusCal:

```pluscal
--algorithm PaxosProposer {
  variables ballot = 0, msgs = {};
  process (proposer \in Proposers)
  variables b = -1, v = None; {
  p1: while (TRUE) {
        b := ballot + 1; ballot := b;
        msgs := msgs \cup {[type |-> "1a", bal |-> b, from |-> self]};
  p2:     await Cardinality({m \in msgs : m.type = "1b" /\ m.bal = b}) \in Quorum;
        v := ChooseValue({m \in msgs : m.type = "1b" /\ m.bal = b});
        msgs := msgs \cup {[type |-> "2a", bal |-> b, val |-> v, from |-> self]};
  p3:     await Cardinality({m \in msgs : m.type = "2b" /\ m.bal = b}) \in Quorum;
        chosen := chosen \cup {v};
      }
  }
}
```

The `await` statements become enabling conditions on actions; the translator inserts `pc` updates automatically. Engineers at AWS learned this language in two to three weeks and used it as "exhaustively testable pseudocode" in ordinary design practice [4]. The translation is trusted but not verified — a caveat the PlusCal documentation states plainly — so the TLA+ output remains the artifact of record for TLC.

### 4.3 Specifying Paxos: phases as actions

Paxos is the canonical hard case: Lamport's original part-time parliament paper describes an algorithm whose informal reasoning is notoriously error-prone [5]. In TLA+, each protocol phase becomes an action over shared message sets. A minimal single-decree Paxos spec uses variables `msgs` (the multiset of sent messages), `accVoted` (each acceptor's highest voted ballot/value), and `chosen`:

```tla
Phase1a(b) == /\ b \in Ballot
              /\ b > maxBallotSent
              /\ msgs' = msgs \cup {[type |-> "1a", bal |-> b]}
              /\ UNCHANGED <<accVoted, chosen>>

Phase2b(a) == \E m \in msgs :
                /\ m.type = "2a"
                /\ m.bal >= accMaxBal[a]
                /\ accVoted' = [accVoted EXCEPT ![a] = <<m.bal, m.val>>]
                /\ msgs' = msgs \cup {[type |-> "2b", bal |-> m.bal, val |-> m.val]}
                /\ UNCHANGED chosen
```

The heart of the spec is the **agreement invariant**:

> **Theorem (Paxos agreement).** In every reachable state, `∀ v1, v2 ∈ chosen : v1 = v2` — no two different values are ever chosen.

*Proof sketch.* By induction on reachable states, strengthening the invariant to the classic quorum-intersection lemma: if a value `v` is chosen at ballot `b` (a quorum of `2b` messages), then every acceptor that votes at any ballot `b' > b` votes `v`. Base case: `chosen = {}`, trivial. Inductive step: `Phase2b(a)` adds a `2b` message only for the value of the highest-ballot `1b` response in a quorum `Q`; `Q` intersects the choosing quorum, so the highest-ballot vote seen is `v` by the induction hypothesis, and the proposer proposes `v`. `Phase1a` does not affect `chosen`. ∎

TLC checks this invariant on finite instances (say 3 acceptors, ballots `0..2`); the inductive argument above is what TLAPS would formalize for the unbounded case [2][5].

### 4.4 Specifying Raft leader election: terms as logical clocks

Raft structures consensus around *terms* — monotonically increasing logical clocks — and a strong-leader discipline that reduces the state space relative to Paxos [6][7]. Ongaro's TLA+ specification of Raft [7] models leader election with variables `currentTerm[i]`, `votedFor[i]`, `state[i] ∈ {"Follower","Candidate","Leader"}`, and a message set. The election actions are:

```tla
Timeout(i) == /\ state[i] \in {"Follower", "Candidate"}
              /\ currentTerm' = [currentTerm EXCEPT ![i] = @ + 1]
              /\ state' = [state EXCEPT ![i] = "Candidate"]
              /\ votedFor' = [votedFor EXCEPT ![i] = i]
              /\ msgs' = msgs \cup RequestVoteMessages(i, currentTerm'[i])

BecomeLeader(i) == /\ state[i] = "Candidate"
                   /\ \E Q \in Quorum :
                        \A j \in Q : VotedForCandidate(msgs, j, i, currentTerm[i])
                   /\ state' = [state EXCEPT ![i] = "Leader"]
                   /\ UNCHANGED <<currentTerm, votedFor, msgs>>
```

Raft's **Election Safety** property — at most one leader per term — is a pure safety invariant [6]:

> **Theorem (Election Safety).** `□(∀ i, j ∈ Server : (state[i] = "Leader" ∧ state[j] = "Leader" ∧ currentTerm[i] = currentTerm[j]) ⇒ i = j)`.

*Proof sketch.* Induction on reachable states. A server becomes leader only after collecting votes from a quorum `Q` in its term. Suppose leaders `i ≠ j` in term `t`. Then quorums `Qi, Qj` both voted in term `t`; by quorum intersection some server `k` voted for both, but `votedFor[k]` is single-valued per term and a server votes at most once per term (guarded by `currentTerm[k] > t ⇒` vote rejection). Contradiction. The TLA+ spec makes the "at most once per term" guard an explicit enabling condition, which is exactly the detail informal reasoning drops [6][7].

Ongaro's TLA+ specification was not an afterthought: it was developed alongside the algorithm and used to validate the design before the dissertation's evaluation [7].

### 4.5 Coping with state-space explosion in TLC

The reachable state graph of a protocol spec grows exponentially in the number of nodes, messages, and steps — the *state-space explosion* problem. TLC deploys a battery of mitigations [4][8]:

- **Symmetry reduction.** Declaring `Server` a symmetry set tells TLC that permuting server identities yields equivalent states; TLC explores only canonical representatives, often an exponential saving for symmetric protocols like Paxos and Raft [8].
- **State constraints.** Bounding `msgs` cardinality or ballot ranges prunes executions the designer judges uninteresting; constraints must be chosen so they cannot mask bugs (a constraint that cuts a counterexample is a modeling error, not an optimization) [4].
- **Fingerprinting and disk management.** TLC stores 64-bit fingerprints rather than full states, with probabilistic collision analysis, and spills to disk so exploration is limited by disk, not RAM [8].
- **Parallel and distributed TLC.** Multi-core TLC parallelizes successor generation; distributed TLC shards the state queue across a cluster — the AWS DynamoDB verification ran on 10 EC2 instances to check a "small, complicated part of the algorithm" at a scale giving high confidence [4].
- **Compositional and bounded techniques.** Recent research decomposes specs into modules (e.g., separating Raft's leader election from log replication) and checks them compositionally, proving that module-level success implies system-level success [9].

The honest summary: TLC verifies *small instances exhaustively*. Confidence in the unbounded protocol comes from the combination of exhaustive small-instance checking, the inductive proof sketch, and engineering judgment about which instance sizes exercise the protocol's logic [4].

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 Industrial evidence: AWS

The strongest empirical data comes from Newcombe et al., "How Amazon Web Services Uses Formal Methods" (CACM 2015) [4]. The reported facts:

1. **Duration and scope.** Since 2011, AWS engineers have applied TLA+/PlusCal/TLC to "difficult design problems" in DynamoDB, S3, EBS, and others — ten systems documented in the paper.
2. **Bugs found.** Model checking found serious, subtle bugs that survived extensive design reviews, code reviews, and testing. The flagship example: a DynamoDB replication bug whose shortest error trace was **35 high-level steps** — an interleaving of failures and recoveries no test would produce. The engineer (identified as T.R.) is quoted as convinced the bug would not have been found by more conventional work [4].
3. **Adoption cost.** Engineers learned TLA+ from scratch in normal engineering practice — "in a couple of weeks" — and the teams that used it reported high value; adoption spread organically [4].
4. **Design leverage.** Beyond bug-finding, TLA+ enabled *aggressive optimization*: engineers could rework complex algorithms for performance and re-verify correctness mechanically, rather than reasoning informally about each change [4].

### 5.2 The Raft specification as evidence

Ongaro's dissertation [7] and its accompanying TLA+ specification provide a second data point: a consensus algorithm *designed with* its TLA+ spec, where the spec served as the executable design document. The Raft user study (43 students at two universities) further showed that Raft's structure — strong leader, term-based election, separated concerns — made the algorithm significantly easier to understand than Paxos [6]; that structural simplicity is exactly what keeps the TLA+ state space tractable.

### 5.3 What the proofs buy: a summary table

| Artifact | Method | Scope | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| Paxos agreement invariant | TLC (finite instance) + induction sketch | 3 acceptors, ballots 0–2 | No counterexample; proof by quorum intersection |
| Raft Election Safety | TLC on Ongaro's spec [7] | 3–5 servers, bounded terms | Holds; proof via single-vote-per-term guard |
| DynamoDB replication (AWS) [4] | Distributed TLC, 10 EC2 nodes | Small, complicated subsystem | 3 serious bugs found, then verified fixed |
| S3/EBS designs (AWS) [4] | PlusCal + TLC | Design-level models | Subtle bugs found pre-production |

> **Theorem (Refinement soundness).** If `Impl` is a TLA+ specification, `r` a refinement mapping (a state function), and `Spec` the high-level specification, then `Impl ⇒ Spec` with `r` substituted holds iff every behavior of `Impl`, mapped through `r`, is a behavior of `Spec` — stuttering steps permitted [1][3]. TLC checks finite instances of this implication; TLAPS proves it generally.

## 6 Limitations

Intellectual honesty requires stating what TLA+ does not do [4][8][9]:

1. **The model/code gap.** TLC verifies a model of the design, not the implementation. Newcombe et al. are explicit: asked "how do we know the executable code correctly implements the verified design?", the answer is "we don't" [4]. A verified spec with a faithless implementation is a verified fiction.
2. **State-space explosion is mitigated, not solved.** Symmetry reduction, constraints, and clusters push the boundary; they do not remove it. Protocols with large data domains or deep counters defeat exhaustive checking, forcing abstraction — and every abstraction is a judgment call that can hide bugs [8][9].
3. **Liveness is second-class.** TLC's liveness checking is bounded and fairness-sensitive; industrial users overwhelmingly verify safety and treat liveness informally [4]. A protocol that is safe but never makes progress passes the standard workflow.
4. **Temporal expressiveness limits.** Fairness in TLA is restricted to `WF`/`SF` on actions; some liveness requirements (e.g., real-time bounds) are inexpressible, and must be approximated or moved to other formalisms [1].
5. **Human cost and skill.** Writing a good spec requires identifying the *right* abstraction — detailed enough to capture the bug, abstract enough to check. The AWS paper reports engineers succeeding in weeks [4], but that success depended on mentorship and on choosing problems where the design, not the code, was the risk.
6. **Proof burden for the unbounded case.** TLC checks finite instances; general proofs need TLAPS, whose hierarchical declarative proofs are labor-intensive and cover only a fraction of industrial specs [2].

## 7 Conclusion

TLA+ occupies a distinctive point in the formal-methods landscape: less ambitious than full program verification, far more rigorous than informal design documents. Its semantic core — behaviors as sequences of states, actions as state relations, stuttering invariance enabling stepwise refinement — is a genuine contribution to how we think about concurrent systems, independent of any tool [1][3]. PlusCal made the mathematics accessible to working engineers; TLC made it executable [4][8]. The methodology — specify the design, state the invariants, model-check small instances, mitigate explosion deliberately, refine toward code — has found real bugs in real systems at AWS, in DynamoDB's replication protocol among others, with 35-step counterexamples no test suite would ever produce [4]. And the consensus case studies show the method at its best: Paxos's agreement and Raft's election safety stated as invariants, proved by induction over the reachable states that TLC explores [5][6][7].

The limitations are real and should bound our claims: models are not code, explosion is managed not eliminated, liveness remains hard, and good abstraction is a human skill [4][8]. But the central lesson of fifteen years of industrial TLA+ stands: *the cheapest place to find a distributed-systems bug is in the design, before it exists* — and TLA+ is, to date, the most cost-effective instrument we have for looking there.
