# Neural Population Coding under Noise Correlations: Tuning Curves, Fisher Information, and Optimal Decoding

## Abstract

How do networks of noisy neurons represent continuous sensory variables with high fidelity? This thesis develops the mathematical theory of population coding from the single-neuron tuning curve to the asymptotic performance of optimal decoders operating under correlated noise. We derive the linear Fisher information *I(θ) = f′(θ)ᵀ Σ(θ)⁻¹ f′(θ)* for a population of *N* neurons with tuning curves *f* and noise covariance *Σ*, establish its link to the Cramér–Rao bound, and analyze how maximum-likelihood and Bayesian estimators approach this bound as population size grows. The central thread is the role of noise correlations: we prove the sign-rule theorem that governs when correlations help or hurt coding, show that optimal codes sit on the boundary of the space of allowed correlations, and demonstrate that differential correlations — noise components proportional to *f′(θ)f′(θ)ᵀ* — impose a hard ceiling on information that no increase in neuron count can lift. We evaluate experimental evidence from retina and cortex, including scaling studies of Fisher information in large populations, against the theory.

##1 Introduction

Sensory cortex represents continuous variables — orientation of an edge, direction of motion, position of an animal — with populations of neurons that are individually noisy and collectively variable. A single neuron's response fluctuates from trial to trial even for a fixed stimulus, yet the animal's perceptual judgments are remarkably precise, often far exceeding what any individual neuron could support. Understanding how fidelity emerges from noisy populations is one of the central mathematical problems of systems neuroscience [1][3].

Two quantities anchor the theory. The *tuning curve* *fᵢ(θ)* describes how the mean response of neuron *i* depends on the stimulus *θ*, and the *noise covariance* *Σ(θ)* describes the trial-to-trial cofluctuations around those means. From these two objects, nearly everything of interest can be derived: the maximum precision any unbiased decoder can achieve, given by the *Fisher information* and the Cramér–Rao bound, and the structure of the optimal decoders — maximum likelihood and Bayesian — that attain it.

For decades the default analytical move was to assume independent noise, under which information grows linearly with the number of neurons and precision improves as the square root of population size. Real populations violate this assumption: noise correlations, typically positive and of order 0.1–0.2 in cortex, are ubiquitous [1][7]. Their effect on coding is subtle and has been the subject of a twenty-year theoretical program. Small limited-range correlations degrade accuracy in homogeneous populations; heterogeneity can rescue or even reverse the effect [7]; a general *sign rule* relates the sign of noise correlations to the sign of signal correlations [1][2]; and a particular family of correlations — *differential correlations* — limits information in a way that no other correlation structure does [3][5].

This thesis synthesizes these results into a single mathematical narrative. Section 2 fixes notation and background; Section 3 develops the methodology of Fisher information and optimal estimation; Section 4 is the deep dive into the geometry of correlated population codes; Section 5 confronts the theory with experimental data and sketches the key proofs; Section 6 states the limitations; and Section 7 concludes.

##2 Background

### Tuning curves and Poisson-like variability

The tuning curve *fᵢ(θ)* is the mean spike count (or firing rate) of neuron *i* as a function of a one-dimensional circular stimulus *θ*, such as the orientation of a grating. In primary visual cortex, orientation tuning is well described by a bell-shaped function — often a circular Gaussian — with three parameters: a *preferred orientation* *θᵢ* where the response peaks, a *gain* *Aᵢ* setting the peak amplitude, and a *width* *σ* setting selectivity [3][9].

Crucially, the response is not deterministic. For a fixed *θ*, the spike count *rᵢ* varies across trials with variance roughly proportional to the mean — *Poisson-like* variability with Fano factor near one in many areas, though often exceeding one in cortex [1][9]. We therefore model the population response vector **r** = (*r₁, …, r_N*) as drawn from a distribution with mean **f**(*θ*) and covariance *Σ*(*θ*), keeping the Gaussian approximation

> **Model.** **r** | *θ* ~ 𝒩(**f**(*θ*), *Σ*(*θ*)),

which is justified for large spike counts by the central limit theorem and is exact enough for the local analysis of fine discrimination [3][8].

### Signal correlations versus noise correlations

For a pair of neurons, two distinct correlation measures arise [1]:

- **Signal correlation**: the correlation, across stimuli, of the two tuning curves. Neurons with overlapping tuning have positive signal correlation; neurons with opponent preferences have negative signal correlation.
- **Noise correlation**: the correlation, across trials at fixed stimulus, of the two neurons' residual fluctuations. This is the off-diagonal structure of *Σ*(*θ*).

The relationship between these two objects — whether noise and signal correlations have the *same* or *opposite* signs — turns out to control whether correlations help or hurt coding, a fact formalized by the sign rule of Section 4 [1][2].

### A taxonomy of decoders

Decoding maps an observed response **r** back to a stimulus estimate *θ̂*(**r**). The principal estimators are:

| Decoder | Definition | Properties |
|---|---|---|
| Population vector | *θ̂* ∝ Σᵢ *rᵢ* **u**(*θᵢ*) | Simple, biologically plausible, generally suboptimal |
| Optimal linear estimator (OLE) | *θ̂* = **w**ᵀ**r** + *b*, **w** minimizing MSE | Best linear decoder; closed form via *Σ*⁻¹ |
| Maximum likelihood (MLE) | *θ̂* = argmax_θ log *p*(**r**\|*θ*) | Asymptotically efficient; attains the Cramér–Rao bound |
| Bayesian (MAP / posterior mean) | posterior *p*(*θ*\|**r**) ∝ *p*(**r**\|*θ*)*p*(*θ*) | Incorporates priors; optimal under squared error |

For independent Poisson neurons, the log-likelihood is *linear* in the spike counts — log *p*(**r**|*θ*) = Σᵢ *rᵢ* log *fᵢ*(*θ*) − Σᵢ *fᵢ*(*θ*) + const — so the maximum-likelihood decoder reduces to a linear readout whose weights are the log tuning curves [9]. This elegant fact is the entry point to the theory below.

##3 Methodology

### The linear Fisher information

Consider estimating *θ* from **r** ~ 𝒩(**f**(*θ*), *Σ*(*θ*)). The Fisher information measures how sensitively the response distribution changes with *θ*. Expanding the log-likelihood to second order and taking the expectation yields the *linear* Fisher information [3]:

> **Definition.** *I*(*θ*) = **f**′(*θ*)ᵀ *Σ*(*θ*)⁻¹ **f**′(*θ*),

where **f**′(*θ*) is the vector of tuning-curve derivatives. The full Gaussian Fisher information contains an additional term from stimulus-dependent covariance, but for Poisson-like noise the covariance's stimulus dependence is second-order, and the linear term dominates the analysis [3][7].

Two special cases illuminate the formula:

1. **Independent noise**, *Σ* = diag(*σᵢ²*): *I*(*θ*) = Σᵢ (*fᵢ*′(*θ*))² / *σᵢ*². Information adds across neurons — the foundation of the folk intuition that bigger populations are always better.
2. **Poisson-like noise**, *σᵢ²* = *fᵢ*(*θ*): *I*(*θ*) = Σᵢ (*fᵢ*′(*θ*))² / *fᵢ*(*θ*). Neurons contribute most where their tuning curves are steepest, not where they fire most — the flanks of the tuning curve, not the peak, carry the information.

### The Cramér–Rao bound and asymptotic efficiency

> **Theorem (Cramér–Rao).** For any unbiased estimator *θ̂* of *θ*,
>
> Var(*θ̂*) ≥ 1 / *I*(*θ*).

No decoder can beat the inverse Fisher information. The maximum-likelihood estimator is *asymptotically efficient*: as the effective sample size (neurons, or time) grows, its variance converges to the bound [8][9]. The Bayesian posterior mean inherits this efficiency under mild regularity, with the prior's influence washing out as *N* grows. This is why Fisher information, though defined through a local linear approximation, is the central currency of population-coding theory — it is simultaneously a property of the encoder and the performance limit of the best decoder.

### Simulation protocol

Our numerical experiments follow a standard protocol [7]: circular Gaussian tuning curves with uniformly spaced preferred orientations, gain *A*, and width *σ*; Poisson-like variances; and a noise correlation matrix with *limited-range* structure — correlation decaying with the distance between preferred orientations. We compute *I*(*θ*) exactly by matrix inversion and compare independent, correlated, and differentially correlated populations as *N* grows. The accompanying code in Section 5 implements this protocol.

##4 The Geometry of Population Information under Correlated Noise

### Tuning Geometry and the Fisher Information Decomposition

The Fisher information is a quadratic form: it measures the squared length of the *signal direction* **f**′(*θ*) in the metric defined by the inverse noise covariance *Σ*⁻¹. Geometrically, each stimulus *θ* defines a point **f**(*θ*) on the *neural manifold* — the curve traced by the mean population response — and **f**′(*θ*) is its tangent. Noise defines an ellipsoid around each point; information is high when the noise ellipsoid is thin along the tangent direction [3].

When the noise covariance itself depends on the stimulus, the full Fisher information for the Gaussian model splits into two terms [7]:

- *J_mean* = **f**′ᵀ *Σ*⁻¹ **f**′, information from the stimulus dependence of the mean response;
- *J_cov* = (1/2) Tr[(*Σ*⁻¹ *Σ*′)²], information from the stimulus dependence of the covariance.

In homogeneous populations (identical tuning curves, stimulus-independent variances), *J_cov* dominates at large *N*; in heterogeneous populations with diverse gains and shapes — the biologically realistic case — *J_mean* carries most of the information [7]. Heterogeneity is thus not a nuisance but a coding resource: it breaks the symmetries that allow noise to masquerade as signal.

Tuning width itself can be optimized for Fisher information, with an interior optimum balancing the number of informative neurons against each neuron's steepness [8]. But there is a subtle trap:

> **Caveat (Berens et al.).** The folk theorem that Fisher information accurately predicts coding quality at large *N* holds only if tuning width is *not* re-optimized as the population grows. Optimizing width for Fisher information makes the tuning curves narrow with *N*, the linear approximation underlying the Cramér–Rao link degrades, and the bound is no longer tight even asymptotically [8].

Fisher information is a *local* quantity — it governs fine discrimination.

### The Sign Rule and the Correlation-Polytope Boundary

The most celebrated organizing principle of correlated coding is the *sign rule* [1][2].

> **Theorem (Sign rule; Averbeck, Latham & Pouget; Hu et al.).** If noise and signal correlations have *opposite* signs for every pair (*cᵢⱼ* · *sᵢⱼ* < 0), then coding performance — measured by linear Fisher information, OLE precision, or mutual information — is *always* enhanced relative to independent noise. The converse fails: same-sign correlations can also help [1][2].

The intuition is geometric: opposite-sign noise pushes the noise ellipsoid *away* from the signal direction, thinning variability precisely where it would be confused with stimulus changes.

Two refinements deepen the picture.

Second, heterogeneity again matters: in populations of diversely tuned neurons, the detrimental effect of limited-range correlations is non-monotonic in correlation strength and depends on population size, unlike the monotone degradation seen in homogeneous populations [7].

### Differential Correlations as Information-Limiting Noise

The deepest result of the modern theory identifies the *only* correlation structure that fundamentally caps information.

> **Decomposition.** *Σ* = *Σ₀* + *I∞*⁻¹ **f**′**f**′ᵀ,

where the second term — the *differential correlations* — is proportional to the outer product of the tuning-curve derivatives.

> **Theorem (Moreno-Bote et al.).** *I_N*⁻¹ = *I_{0,N}*⁻¹ + *I∞*⁻¹,

where *I_{0,N}* is the information from the non-limiting component *Σ₀*.

The geometric meaning is vivid: differential correlations are trial-to-trial fluctuations that move the population response *along the neural manifold's tangent* — exactly as a small change in the stimulus would.

Three properties make differential correlations scientifically treacherous and fascinating:

1. **They can be arbitrarily small yet decisive.** Because they act along the one informative dimension, differential correlations far below the resolution of correlation measurements can still saturate information [3][5].
2. **They hide inside ordinary correlations.** In homogeneous populations, limited-range correlations *contain* differential correlations, which is why information saturates there; in heterogeneous populations, limited-range correlations need not contain them, and information can grow linearly [3][7]. The general theory thus subsumes the earlier, apparently contradictory findings.
3. **They leave a scaling signature.** Kafashan and colleagues recorded nearly 500 neurons in mouse visual cortex and showed that Fisher information follows the predicted *I_N*⁻¹ = (*cN*)⁻¹ + *I∞*⁻¹ law, saturating as *N* grows — with roughly 10% of the neurons carrying essentially all the available information [5].

Independent experimental support comes from choice-correlation analysis: the correlation between single-neuron fluctuations and the animal's behavioral report in vestibular areas matches the pattern predicted by differential correlations [3].

### Optimal Decoding: From Maximum Likelihood to Bayes

The encoder sets the information; the decoder must extract it.

The Bayesian decoder forms the full posterior *p*(*θ*|**r**) ∝ *p*(**r**|*θ*)*p*(*θ*).

A practical caveat: estimating Fisher information or training the optimal decoder from finite data is treacherous precisely because of differential correlations.

##5 Empirical Results and Proofs

### Analytical results

We collect the theory's sharp predictions:

| Prediction | Statement | Status |
|---|---|---|
| Sign rule | Opposite-sign noise/signal correlations always help | Theorem [1][2] |
| Boundary optimum | Optimal correlations lie on the feasible polytope's boundary | Theorem; tested in retina [2][4] |
| Saturation | Differential correlations cap information at *I∞* | Theorem [3]; scaling confirmed in mouse V1 [5] |
| Heterogeneity rescue | Diverse tuning restores linear scaling under limited-range correlations | Simulation + analysis [7] |
| Width caveat | Fisher-optimal tuning widths break asymptotic tightness | Theory + simulation [8] |
| Retinal decoding | Coupled models recover ~10% more information than independent ones | Experiment [6] |

### Proof sketch: information saturation

Starting from *Σ* = *Σ₀* + *I∞*⁻¹ **f**′**f**′ᵀ, the Sherman–Morrison identity gives

*Σ*⁻¹ = *Σ₀*⁻¹ − (*Σ₀*⁻¹**f**′**f**′ᵀ*Σ₀*⁻¹) / (*I∞* + **f**′ᵀ*Σ₀*⁻¹**f**′).

Left- and right-multiplying by **f**′ᵀ and **f**′ and writing *I_{0,N}* = **f**′ᵀ*Σ₀*⁻¹**f**′ yields *I_N* = *I_{0,N}* − *I_{0,N}*²/(*I∞* + *I_{0,N}*), i.e. *I_N*⁻¹ = *I_{0,N}*⁻¹ + *I∞*⁻¹ [5]. As *N* → ∞ with *I_{0,N}* = *cN*, the first term vanishes and *I_N* → *I∞*. ∎

### Numerical demonstration

The following Python code implements the Section 3 protocol and exhibits both the linear scaling of the independent population and the saturation induced by differential correlations:

```python
import numpy as np

def circular_tuning(theta, pref, width, gain):
    """Circular Gaussian tuning curves."""
    return gain * np.exp((np.cos(theta - pref) - 1.0) / width**2)

def linear_fisher(theta, pref, width, gain, rho):
    """Linear Fisher information with limited-range correlations."""
    f = circular_tuning(theta, pref, width, gain)
    eps = 1e-4
    fp = (circular_tuning(theta + eps, pref, width, gain)
          - circular_tuning(theta - eps, pref, width, gain)) / (2 * eps)
    var = f + 1e-6                      # Poisson-like variance
    D = np.diag(np.sqrt(var))
    d = np.abs(pref[:, None] - pref[None, :])
    d = np.minimum(d, 2 * np.pi - d)    # circular distance
    C = (1 - rho) * np.eye(len(pref)) + rho * np.exp(-(d / 0.6) ** 2)
    Sigma = D @ C @ D
    return float(fp @ np.linalg.inv(Sigma) @ fp)

def fisher_differential(N, c, I_inf):
    """Sherman-Morrison saturation: 1/I_N = 1/(cN) + 1/I_inf."""
    return 1.0 / (1.0 / (c * N) + 1.0 / I_inf)

# Example: N = 200 neurons, gain 40 Hz, width 0.5 rad
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
pref = rng.uniform(-np.pi, np.pi, 200)
I_indep = linear_fisher(0.3, pref, 0.5, 40.0, rho=0.0)
I_corr = linear_fisher(0.3, pref, 0.5, 40.0, rho=0.2)
print(f"independent: {I_indep:.1f}   correlated: {I_corr:.1f}")
for N in (50, 200, 1000, 5000):
    print(N, f"{fisher_differential(N, c=0.5, I_inf=40.0):.1f}")
```

Typical output shows the independent information growing with *N* while the differentially correlated population asymptotes near *I∞* = 40 — the ceiling no decoder can breach.

### Experimental evidence

- **Retina.** Pillow and colleagues fit coupled generalized linear models to 27 simultaneously recorded ganglion cells and decoded a white-noise stimulus; the coupled model, which captures noise correlations, recovered roughly 10% more information than the uncoupled model, and spike-history effects added further gains [6].
- **Cortex.** Adaptation reduces noise correlations in macaque V1 while increasing Fisher information; Ecker and colleagues mapped how heterogeneity modulates these effects across population sizes [7]. Kafashan and colleagues' large-scale recordings provide the most direct evidence that information-limiting correlations shape cortical codes at scale [5].
- **Behavior.** The link from information to behavior passes through the decoder the animal actually uses; choice-probability analyses in vestibular cortex reveal the signature of differential correlations in the animal's own reports [3].

##6 Limitations

The theory developed here is powerful but bounded, and its boundaries deserve emphasis.

1. **Fisher information is local.** It governs fine discrimination near a stimulus value. Coarse discrimination, detection, and the full Bayesian mean-squared error can disagree with Fisher-based rankings, especially when tuning widths are optimized or when noise correlations reshape the likelihood's global geometry [8].
2. **Asymptotics can fail.** The Cramér–Rao link relies on the linear approximation of the log-likelihood improving with *N*. With Fisher-optimal tuning or strong correlations, convergence can be slow or absent, and the bound may never be tight [8].
3. **The Gaussian–Poisson idealization.** Real spike trains show overdispersion, bursting, and history dependence; cortical Fano factors routinely exceed one, and non-Poisson variability changes both the information and the optimal decoder [1][6].
4. **Correlations are not stationary.** Attention, adaptation, learning, and behavioral state modulate noise correlations on fast timescales, so the *Σ* that matters is the one present during the behavior, not the one measured under anesthesia or passive viewing [7].
5. **Estimation is hard.** Differential correlations can be orders of magnitude smaller than the correlations we can reliably measure; Fisher information estimated from finite trials is biased, and cross-validated decoder bounds are tight only with abundant data [3].
6. **The decoder is unknown.** Information is a property of the code, but behavior reflects the animal's actual decoder, which may be suboptimal, mismatched to the correlation structure, or optimized for a different task entirely [3][9].

##7 Conclusion

The mathematics of population coding delivers a coherent story. Tuning curves set the signal geometry; Poisson-like variability sets the noise scale; and the Fisher information — the squared length of the signal direction in the inverse-noise metric — sets the precision limit that maximum-likelihood and Bayesian decoders asymptotically attain. Noise correlations enter not as a uniform penalty but as a structured perturbation of this geometry: the sign rule tells us when they help, the boundary theorem tells us where optima live, heterogeneity tells us when their harm can be undone, and differential correlations identify the one component that no amount of neural hardware can overcome.

The practical moral for the analysis of large-scale recordings is twofold. First, measure the *right* thing: raw correlation coefficients are a poor proxy for coding impact, and the component of the covariance along **f**′**f**′ᵀ — however small — is what limits information. Second, scale matters: the questions that correlations pose cannot be settled in pairs or dozens of neurons; they are answered, if at all, in the hundreds and thousands now accessible to modern recording [5]. The theory reviewed here provides the scaffolding on which those answers will hang: a complete, quantitative account of how noisy neurons conspire to represent the world.
