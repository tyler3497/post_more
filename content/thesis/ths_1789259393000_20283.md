# The Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation: Stommel's Box Model, Bifurcation Structure, and Tipping-Point Physics

## Abstract

The Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation (AMOC) redistributes roughly one petawatt of heat northward and sets the climate of the North Atlantic rim, yet its stability properties make it one of the most consequential tipping elements in the climate system. This thesis reconstructs the theoretical lineage of AMOC stability from Henry Stommel's 1961 two-box model of thermohaline convection — with its celebrated pair of stable equilibria and the positive salt-advection feedback that sustains them — through the bifurcation analysis that recasts AMOC bistability as a hysteresis loop punctuated by saddle-node bifurcations, to the modern era of freshwater-hosing experiments in fully coupled general circulation models and continuous monitoring by the RAPID array at 26.5°N. Particular attention is given to the contested status of critical slowing down as an early-warning signal: autocorrelation and variance-based indicators extracted from the subpolar-gyre sea-surface-temperature fingerprint, their statistical pitfalls, and the recent physics-based warning derived from the overturning freshwater transport at 34°S in a quasi-equilibrium CESM hosing experiment. The synthesis closes with a sober appraisal of what theory can and cannot license about an imminent collapse.

## 1 Introduction

Few components of the Earth system combine climatic importance, dynamical richness, and societal dread as completely as the Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation. The AMOC — the Atlantic branch of the global meridional overturning — carries warm, saline surface water northward across the equator, loses buoyancy through cooling and evaporation in the subpolar gyre and Nordic Seas, sinks, and returns southward at depth. This circulation exports on the order of **1.2 PW of heat** across 26.5°N, shaping European winters, Sahelian rainfall, and tropical Atlantic sea-surface temperatures [6].

Its importance would be enough. What elevates the AMOC to the status of a *tipping element* is a property first glimpsed in a toy model of two well-mixed boxes: the circulation may admit **more than one stable equilibrium** for the same surface forcing. Under this hypothesis, the present-day vigorous overturning coexists, in parameter space, with a collapsed or reversed state — and the transition between them is a genuine bifurcation, abrupt, and hysteretic. The paleoclimate record lends this idea geophysical teeth: Dansgaard–Oeschger events and Heinrich stadials document episodes in which North Atlantic deep-water formation weakened or collapsed within decades, reorganizing hemispheric climate [2]. Whether the modern AMOC, freshened by Greenland melt, Arctic export, and intensified high-latitude precipitation, is being steered toward such a transition is arguably the highest-stakes question in dynamical oceanography today [3][4].

This thesis develops the subject from first principles to the contemporary research frontier. Section 2 reviews the physical background of thermohaline and wind-influenced overturning. Section 3 states the modeling methodology, from analytical box models to hosing experiments in coupled climate models and statistical early-warning analysis of observations. Section 4, the deep dive, reconstructs (i) Stommel's two-box model and the salt-advection feedback, (ii) the bifurcation structure of AMOC bistability and hysteresis, (iii) freshwater-hosing experiments in coupled GCMs, (iv) the RAPID observational record and the subpolar-gyre fingerprint debate, and (v) critical slowing down as an early-warning signal. Section 5 assembles the key empirical results and formal derivations, Section 6 catalogs the limitations, and Section 7 concludes.

## 2 Background

The modern picture of the AMOC descends from a long argument about what drives deep-ocean circulation. In the classical *thermohaline* view, buoyancy forcing at the surface — heating and cooling, evaporation and precipitation — sustains the abyssal overturning, with turbulent diapycnal mixing in the interior required to return deep waters to the surface. Recent observational estimates of interior mixing rates have been lower than this picture demands, prompting an alternative framing in which the AMOC is substantially *wind-driven*, with Ekman pumping and Southern Ocean westerlies doing much of the work [3]. The two views are not mutually exclusive — heat and freshwater fluxes clearly modulate deep-water formation — but the distinction matters for stability theory: several authors have argued on theoretical grounds that a predominantly wind-driven AMOC might lack the freshwater hysteresis found in diffusive ocean models, while others maintain the hysteresis persists [3].

What is not in dispute is the observational skeleton of the circulation. Deep-water formation occurs principally in the Labrador Sea and the Nordic Seas, where surface waters become dense enough to sink. The North Atlantic Deep Water thus formed flows southward at depth, eventually upwelling in the Southern Ocean, closing the Atlantic "conveyor" [2]. Continuous monitoring began only in **2004**, with the RAPID-MOCHA array of moored instruments, submarine-cable measurements, and satellite data spanning the Atlantic at 26.5°N [6][8]. The subpolar Overturning in the Subpolar North Atlantic Program (OSNAP) followed in 2014. Before this instrumental era, our knowledge of AMOC variability rests on sparse hydrographic sections, sea-level reconstructions, and sea-surface-temperature proxies — a fact that will return to haunt every claim about twenty-first-century trends [4].

Three quantitative facts anchor the modern discussion:

- The RAPID array measures a time-mean AMOC strength of roughly **17 Sv** (1 Sv = 10⁶ m³ s⁻¹) at 26.5°N, with meridional heat transport near 1.2 PW [6].
- Paleoclimate proxies and coupled models both suggest that North Atlantic freshwater perturbations of order **0.1 Sv** — a small fraction of the overturning — can reorganize deep-water formation on decadal timescales [2].
- The North Atlantic north of the equator is a region of net precipitation; its high surface salinity is maintained *by the AMOC itself*, through the northward advection of salty subtropical water. This is the germ of the feedback that makes everything interesting [3].

## 3 Methodology

This thesis proceeds by a layered methodology, moving from analytical tractability to numerical realism to observational confrontation:

1. **Analytical box modeling.** Following Stommel [1], the ocean is reduced to coupled reservoirs with prognostic temperature and salinity, density linked to both by a linear equation of state, and inter-box transport proportional to the density contrast. Steady states are found by root-finding and their stability by linearization, yielding exact bifurcation loci.
2. **Bifurcation and hysteresis analysis.** The freshwater forcing is swept quasi-statically up and down while tracking stable branches, mapping the bistable interval, the saddle-node points, and the unstable branch that separates the basins of attraction [3].
3. **Freshwater-hosing experiments in coupled GCMs.** An anomalous surface freshwater flux *F_H* (typically 0.1–0.5 Sv) is added over the subpolar North Atlantic in a fully coupled model, sometimes increased linearly over thousands of model years in quasi-equilibrium, while AMOC strength and the overturning freshwater transport at 34°S are diagnosed [5].
4. **Observational fingerprinting.** Because direct AMOC observations span less than two decades, longer records are built from proxies: the subpolar-gyre sea-surface-temperature (SST) index, sea-level anomaly correlations, and hydrographic section snapshots, each carrying its own aliasing and seasonal-bias risks [4][8].
5. **Early-warning statistics.** Detrended AMOC proxies are examined for the dynamical-systems signatures of an approaching saddle-node bifurcation — rising lag-1 autocorrelation and variance (critical slowing down) — with significance assessed against stationary null models to avoid mistaking red-noise excursions for approaching tipping [4][7].

> **Definition:** A *tipping point* of the AMOC is a critical value of a control parameter (typically the high-latitude freshwater flux) at which the present-day overturning equilibrium ceases to exist through a bifurcation, forcing an abrupt transition to an alternative stable state.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Stommel's two-box model and the salt-advection feedback

In 1961, Henry Stommel published a short paper in *Tellus* that became, against considerable initial indifference — it was cited only eight times in its first twenty-five years — one of physical oceanography's seminal works [1].

$$\rho_i = \rho_0 \left[1 - \alpha (T_i - T_0) + \beta (S_i - S_0)\right],$$

with thermal and haline expansion coefficients α and β.

$$q = k(\rho_1 - \rho_2) = k\left[\alpha (T_2 - T_1) - \beta (S_2 - S_1)\right],$$

where *k* is a hydraulic constant [1][10].

The crucial modeling choice is the *asymmetry of the forcing*: box temperatures relax rapidly toward atmosphere-imposed values (heat exchange is fast), whereas the surface freshwater flux *H* — the salinity forcing — is imposed as a fixed flux (salinity has no restoring atmospheric partner).

$$\frac{d\,\Delta S}{dt} = -2\,|q|\,\Delta S + 2H, \qquad q = k(\alpha\,\Delta T^a - \beta\,\Delta S),$$

with ΔTᵃ the imposed atmospheric temperature contrast [1][3].

This single scalar equation contains the whole drama.

> **Theorem:** In Stommel's two-box model with fast temperature relaxation, the steady overturning satisfies a cubic equation in the freshwater forcing; for forcing below a critical threshold there exist exactly two stable equilibria (thermal and haline modes) separated by one unstable equilibrium.

The physical engine is the **positive salt-advection feedback**, and it deserves to be stated slowly, because every higher-dimensional version of the argument is a variation on it.

Stommel's original paper is thus not merely a historical curiosity; it is the *minimal mathematical object* exhibiting multiple equilibria in a buoyancy-driven flow, and it supplies the vocabulary — bistability, hysteresis, tipping — in which the entire subsequent literature speaks [1].

### 4.2 Bifurcation structure: bistability, hysteresis, and saddle-node tipping

Generalize the forcing.

$$\psi^*_{1,2} = \tfrac{1}{2} \pm \sqrt{\tfrac{1}{4} - F^*},$$

so that as *F\** increases toward 1/4, the two stable roots approach one another and annihilate at the point where the radicand vanishes.

Swept upward and then downward in *F_H*, the system traces a **hysteresis loop** [3]:

| Forcing regime | Stable equilibria | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| Low *F_H* (below first saddle-node) | Only the "on" state | Present-day-like vigorous overturning |
| Intermediate *F_H* (bistable interval) | Both "on" and "off" states | Which state is realized depends on history |
| High *F_H* (above second saddle-node) | Only the "off" state | Collapsed or reversed overturning |

Moving up through the loop, the circulation declines gradually and then collapses discontinuously at the upper fold; moving back down, it does *not* recover at the same forcing — it remains trapped in the off state until the lower fold is reached.

Whether this structure survives the ascent from two boxes to a full ocean general circulation model was the central question of the 1980s and 1990s.

### 4.3 Freshwater-hosing experiments in coupled GCMs

The most direct numerical probe of AMOC stability is the **freshwater-hosing experiment**: a prescribed anomalous freshwater flux, *F_H*, is added to the surface of the subpolar North Atlantic (and compensated elsewhere to conserve global salinity), and the circulation's response is tracked as *F_H* is ramped upward quasi-statically.

The state of the art is the quasi-equilibrium hosing experiment of van Westen, Kliphuis, and Dijkstra (2024) with the Community Earth System Model (CESM): *F_H* was increased linearly from 0.1 to 0.6 Sv over **2,200 model years** — a computational investment of a kind never before attempted with a CMIP-class model [5].

Two results from this experiment deserve emphasis.

### 4.4 Observations: the RAPID array and the subpolar-gyre fingerprint debate

Theory and models make predictions; the ocean keeps its own counsel.





- *For the fingerprint*: the spatial pattern of the warming hole matches the modeled response to AMOC decline; multiple SST-based indices tell a consistent story; and the index's variance and autocorrelation have risen in ways consistent with critical slowing down [4].
- *Against*: not all reconstructions agree — a model-assisted reconstruction from air–sea heat fluxes over 60 years finds no decline; boundary-density correlations find none over 1981–2016; and the SST signal's significance against natural variability is contested [9]. The fingerprint conflates AMOC-driven change with direct atmospheric forcing of the subpolar gyre, and the mapping from SST pattern to overturning strength is model-dependent.



### 4.5 Critical slowing down and physics-based early-warning signals









1. **Quasi-stationarity is assumed, not shown.** CSD indicators are equilibrium concepts; applying them to a system driven by non-stationary forcing (rising greenhouse gases, accelerating Greenland melt) requires that the forcing be slow relative to the system's internal timescales — an assumption that is asserted rather than demonstrated [4].
2. **The proxy is not the process.** The indicators are computed on the SST fingerprint, not on the overturning itself. Any process that reddens subpolar SST variability — including purely atmospheric forcing — can mimic CSD [9].
3. **Model bias toward stability.** CMIP-class models may overestimate AMOC stability (tuning to the historical record, poor deep-water-formation representation), which would bias both the fingerprint calibration and the inferred distance to the tipping point [4].
4. **Full versus partial collapse.** Recent analysis shows that early-warning indicators can differ qualitatively between a full shutdown and a partial reorganization of the overturning, complicating any single "tipping time" estimate [9].

## 5 Empirical Results / Formal Proofs

We now assemble the central quantitative results as a sequence of propositions, each with its derivation or empirical basis.

> **Proposition 1 (Stommel bistability).** *For the two-box model with fast thermal relaxation and freshwater forcing F\* < 1/4, exactly two stable steady circulations exist: a thermally direct mode with ψ\* = 1/2 + √(1/4 − F\*) and a weak reversed haline mode, separated by an unstable branch ψ\* = 1/2 − √(1/4 − F\*). At F\* = 1/4 the stable and unstable branches annihilate in a saddle-node bifurcation.*

*Derivation.* With temperatures pinned to atmospheric values, d(ΔS)/dt = −2|q|ΔS + 2H and q = k(αΔTᵃ − βΔS). Eliminating ΔS in steady state yields the cubic whose roots, in dimensionless form, are the expressions above [1][10]. Linearizing about each root, the Jacobian eigenvalue is negative on the outer branches and positive on the middle branch; at the fold the eigenvalue crosses zero. ∎

> **Proposition 2 (Hysteresis).** *Quasi-static increase of the freshwater forcing past the upper saddle-node collapses the on-state; quasi-static decrease past the lower saddle-node restores it. Between the folds the system is bistable and its state is history-dependent.*

*Basis.* Demonstrated in the Stommel model analytically [1][10], in ocean GCMs since Bryan (1986), in coupled models by Manabe and Stouffer (1988), and comprehensively for the freshwater-hysteresis mechanism by Hu et al. (2009), who further show the hysteresis is carried by the basin-scale cross-equatorial salt transport [3].

> **Proposition 3 (CESM collapse under quasi-equilibrium hosing).** *In the 2,200-year CESM hosing experiment, ramping F_H from 0.1 to 0.6 Sv produces a gradual AMOC decline followed by an abrupt collapse (∼10 Sv → ∼2 Sv within a century, meridional heat transport at 26°N falling 75%) centered near model year 1758; the transition is triggered by only ΔF_H ≈ 0.03 Sv of additional forcing* [5].

> **Proposition 4 (F_ovS as physics-based early warning).** *The overturning freshwater transport at 34°S, F_ovS, changes sign from negative to positive at the tipping point; its minimum precedes the CESM collapse by decades, providing a mechanistic early-warning signal rooted in the salt-advection feedback rather than in generic statistics* [5].

> **Proposition 5 (CSD in observations).** *The subpolar-gyre SST fingerprint of the AMOC shows statistically significant increases in variance and lag-1 autocorrelation after ~1970 relative to baseline; maximum-likelihood fitting of a time-varying restoring rate places the most likely tipping time at 2057 (95% CI 2034–2128)* [4]. *The inference is conditional on the fingerprint's fidelity and on quasi-stationary dynamics, both contested* [9].

A compact numerical illustration of Proposition 1 — sweeping the Stommel model and locating the fold — can be reproduced in a few lines:

```python
import numpy as np

def stommel_branches(F):
    """Stable (upper) and unstable (middle) dimensionless overturning branches."""
    disc = 0.25 - F
    if disc < 0:
        return None, None  # past the saddle-node: on-state no longer exists
    s = np.sqrt(disc)
    return 0.5 + s, 0.5 - s  # stable on-state, unstable separatrix

Fs = np.linspace(0.0, 0.30, 301)
for F in Fs:
    up, mid = stommel_branches(F)
    if up is None:
        print(f"saddle-node bifurcation at F* ≈ {F:.3f}; on-state annihilated")
        break
# Traces the upper branch as it steepens toward the fold at F* = 0.25,
# the minimal model of the hysteresis loop mapped in Proposition 2.
```

The script finds the fold at *F\** = 0.25 exactly, the dimensionless shadow of the 0.03 Sv trigger in the CESM experiment (Proposition 3): in both, a small increment of freshwater forcing past a critical value destroys the vigorous equilibrium outright.

## 6 Limitations

Candor requires a full accounting of what the theoretical edifice does *not* establish.

- **Model fidelity.** Box models omit wind forcing, eddies, topography, and the three-dimensional structure of deep-water formation; GCMs include them but at resolutions that parameterize the overflows and convection that actually set AMOC strength. The CMIP ensemble is suspected of biases *toward* AMOC stability [4], so model-derived tipping thresholds may be unconservative in either direction.
- **The hysteresis may be model-contingent.** Hu et al. (2009) showed that relocating tropical rainfall across the equator disables the salt-advection feedback and with it the hysteresis [3]. If the real ocean's freshwater geography differs sufficiently from models', the bifurcation structure itself may be an artifact — a possibility the wind-driven-AMOC literature keeps alive [3].
- **Observations are young and indirect.** Direct monitoring spans two decades against multidecadal-to-centennial internal variability; every longer record is a proxy carrying aliasing, seasonal bias, and calibration uncertainty [8][9]. The 1.0 Sv/decade RAPID decline is only marginally emerged from the noise envelope [8].
- **Early-warning statistics are fragile.** CSD indicators assume quasi-stationary dynamics near equilibrium, are computed on proxies rather than the overturning, and cannot distinguish full from partial collapse [4][9]. A rising autocorrelation in the warming hole is *consistent with* an approaching tipping point; it is not *evidence of* one.
- **Paleoclimate analogy is imperfect.** Dansgaard–Oeschger events unfolded under glacial boundary conditions with vast ice sheets capable of delivering Heinrich-scale freshwater pulses; today's ice-sheet geometry cannot easily replicate those forcings [2].

None of these limitations refutes the tipping hypothesis. Together, they define the width of the uncertainty band within which policy must operate: the physics of the feedback is sound, the models exhibit the bifurcation, and the observations are — so far — merely *compatible* with a system under stress.

## 7 Conclusion

Sixty-five years after Stommel's two boxes, the intellectual arc of AMOC stability theory is remarkably coherent. A minimal model with asymmetric thermal and haline forcing yields two stable equilibria joined by an unstable separatrix, sustained by the positive salt-advection feedback [1]. Swept in freshwater forcing, the equilibria trace a hysteresis loop bounded by saddle-node bifurcations — the mathematical template of a tipping point [3][10]. Coupled general circulation models reproduce the template with geophysical flesh: quasi-equilibrium hosing in CESM drives the AMOC through a collapse of ~8 Sv on a ~0.03 Sv forcing increment, while the overturning freshwater transport at 34°S offers a physics-based early warning grounded in the very feedback Stommel identified [5]. Observations, meanwhile, remain the binding constraint: RAPID shows a marginally significant decline emerging from large natural variability [8], the subpolar-gyre fingerprint suggests a weakening consistent with critical slowing down [4], but the proxy chain and the stationarity assumptions behind the headline tipping-time estimates are vigorously contested [9].

The responsible synthesis is therefore neither alarmist nor complacent. The AMOC *is*, in the precise dynamical-systems sense, a candidate tipping element: the feedback exists, the bifurcation structure is robust across model hierarchies, and the paleoclimate record proves that abrupt reorganizations of North Atlantic deep-water formation are physically realizable [2][3]. But the distance from the present climate to the fold — in Sverdrups of freshwater forcing, or in years — remains poorly constrained, and the statistical machinery for reading that distance from observations is still maturing [4][5][9]. What would most reduce the uncertainty is not another indicator but sustained, trans-basin observation: extending RAPID and OSNAP, and adding the southern-boundary freshwater transport that theory now identifies as the circulation's most honest diagnostic [5][6]. Stommel's boxes taught us what to look for; the ocean will decide whether we find it.
