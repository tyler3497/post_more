# Quantum Low-Density Parity-Check Codes: From Expander Foundations to Constant-Overhead Fault Tolerance

## Abstract

Fault-tolerant quantum computation has long been dominated by the surface code, whose two-dimensional locality exacts a punitive price: physical qubit counts grow quadratically with distance, so the encoding rate vanishes asymptotically. Quantum low-density parity-check (qLDPC) codes shatter this barrier, offering asymptotically *good* families with constant rate $k/n = \Theta(1)$ and linear distance $d = \Theta(n)$, while every stabilizer check remains constant-weight. This thesis traces the arc from classical LDPC and expander codes through the hypergraph product, the lifted product of Panteleev and Kalachev, and the quantum Tanner codes of Leverrier and Zémor, to the recent landmark establishing constant-overhead fault-tolerant quantum computation under general circuit noise. We examine *single-shot* error correction, which compresses syndrome history into one noisy measurement round; the small-set-flip and belief-propagation decoders; and numerical threshold evidence ($0.2\%$–$0.8\%$ pseudo-thresholds under circuit-level noise). We close by charting the remaining roadblocks — long-range connectivity, fault-tolerant logical gates, and magic-state distillation — between theory and a scalable qLDPC processor [1][2][5][12].

## 1 Introduction

The threshold theorem for fault-tolerant quantum computation states that an arbitrarily long quantum computation can be performed reliably provided the physical error rate lies below some constant threshold. For two decades, the quantitative form of this theorem was dismal: concatenating the $[[7,1,3]]$ Steane code demands a space overhead polynomial in the target computation length, and even the topological surface code — the practical workhorse of the field — encodes $k$ logical qubits into $\Theta(k d^2)$ physical qubits, where $d$ is the code distance. Suppressing the logical error rate to $\epsilon$ requires $d = \Theta(\log(1/\epsilon))$, so the overhead grows polylogarithmically with the inverse error target [8]. No fundamental law mandates this waste: the quantum capacity of the depolarizing channel is positive, and Shannon-theoretic reasoning suggests a constant encoding rate should suffice. The obstruction was purely constructive — until quantum LDPC codes arrived.

Classical LDPC codes, invented by Gallager, achieve capacity-approaching performance with linear-time belief-propagation decoding [11]. Their quantum analogs languished because the CSS construction needs *two* sparse parity-check matrices $H_X, H_Z$ with orthogonal rows, $H_X H_Z^T = 0$. Early families (hypergraph products) delivered constant rate but sublinear distance. The breakthrough decade began with quantum *expander* codes (linear distance, vanishing rate), and culminated in 2022 with the lifted product codes of Panteleev and Kalachev and the quantum Tanner codes of Leverrier and Zémor [1] — both $[[n, \Theta(n), \Theta(n)]]$ with constant-weight checks.

> **Theorem (Leverrier–Zémor, 2022):** There exist families of quantum Tanner codes with parameters $[[n, \kappa n, \delta n]]$ for positive constants $\kappa, \delta$, where every stabilizer generator has weight bounded by a constant independent of $n$ [1].

Good codes alone do not buy fault tolerance. Gottesman's program showed that constant-overhead fault tolerance is *possible* with qLDPC codes, contingent on efficient decoding and fault-tolerant gadgets — a program recently completed for quantum Tanner codes under general noise [2].

This thesis presents the full stack: classical foundations, quantum constructions, single-shot decoding theory, threshold theorems, decoding algorithms, numerical evidence, limitations, and the road ahead.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Classical LDPC codes and expander codes

A binary linear code is *low-density parity-check* if it admits a parity-check matrix $H$ whose rows and columns have bounded weight: each check involves $O(1)$ bits and each bit participates in $O(1)$ checks. The associated **Tanner graph** is the bipartite graph with variable nodes (bits) on one side and check nodes (parity constraints) on the other; an edge connects a bit to each check containing it. Sparsity of $H$ becomes bounded degree in the Tanner graph.

Gallager's analysis hinted at the role of *expansion*: if every small set of variable nodes has many check neighbors, a simple bit-flipping decoder corrects a constant fraction of errors. Sipser and Spielman made this rigorous: a $(\gamma, \delta)$-expander bipartite graph yields a code of rate $\geq 1 - c/d$ and distance $\geq \delta n$, decoded greedily in linear time. Expansion converts *local* progress (flipping bits to reduce syndrome weight) into *global* guarantees.

*Belief propagation* (BP) generalizes flipping to soft information: variable and check nodes exchange log-likelihood-ratio messages along Tanner-graph edges, iteratively refining posterior beliefs. Its quantum adaptations are the practical decoding workhorse for qLDPC codes [11].

### 2.2 Stabilizer and CSS codes

A quantum stabilizer code on $n$ qubits is the joint $+1$ eigenspace of an abelian subgroup of the Pauli group. A **CSS code** is specified by two classical binary codes $C_X, C_Z$ with $C_X^\perp \subseteq C_Z$ (equivalently $H_X H_Z^T = 0$), encoding $k = \dim C_X + \dim C_Z - n$ logical qubits with distance $d = \min(d_X, d_Z)$. Quantum LDPC means both $H_X$ and $H_Z$ are sparse — every stabilizer generator acts on $O(1)$ qubits and every qubit participates in $O(1)$ generators.

The orthogonality constraint is the crux: two random sparse matrices are almost surely *not* orthogonal, so quantum LDPC constructions must manufacture commutativity by design. The product constructions below solve exactly this problem.

### 2.3 The surface code baseline

The surface code is a qLDPC code with parameters $[[2d^2, 2, d]]$ (rotated variant): constant check weight, but rate $k/n = \Theta(1/d^2) \to 0$. Its virtues are geometric locality (nearest-neighbor checks on a 2D grid) and a high circuit-level threshold $\approx 0.7\%$–$1\%$ with minimum-weight perfect matching decoding. The cost is overhead: a distance-$27$ logical qubit needs $\approx 1500$ physical qubits, and a useful computation needs millions. Every improvement in rate that qLDPC codes offer is measured against this baseline — and recent qLDPC constructions now match the surface code's threshold while encoding many more logical qubits per block [8].

## 3 Methodology

Our analysis follows the constructive pipeline from classical ingredients to fault-tolerant gadgets:

1. **Code construction.** We present the hypergraph product (warm-up), the lifted product (Panteleev–Kalachev), and quantum Tanner codes (Leverrier–Zémor), tracking how each enforces $H_X H_Z^T = 0$ while preserving sparsity and expansion [1][7][12].
2. **Single-shot decoding theory.** We formalize *linear confinement* — the property that low-weight syndromes imply low-weight errors modulo stabilizers — and show it yields single-shot decoders with sustainable thresholds under local stochastic noise [3].
3. **Threshold analysis.** We distinguish three noise models of increasing realism: *code capacity* (perfect syndromes), *phenomenological* (noisy syndromes), and *circuit-level* (errors inside the extraction circuit). We state the constant-overhead threshold theorem for quantum Tanner codes under general noise [2].
4. **Decoder survey.** We compare the small-set-flip decoder (provable, parallelizable), BP+OSD (practical, high-threshold), and fault-tolerant BP over space–time Tanner graphs [1][4][9].
5. **Numerical evidence.** We report published memory-experiment thresholds for explicit finite-size codes — bivariate bicycle (BB) codes, mirror codes, tricycle codes, and small quantum Tanner instances [5][6][7][8].

Throughout, "overhead" means the ratio of physical to logical qubits (space) times the slowdown factor (time); *constant overhead* means this ratio is bounded independently of the computation length.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Hypergraph products: the first constant-rate family

Given classical parity-check matrices $H_1 \in \mathbb{F}_2^{r_1 \times n_1}$ and $H_2 \in \mathbb{F}_2^{r_2 \times n_2}$, the **hypergraph product** defines a CSS code with

$$H_X = \begin{pmatrix} H_1 \otimes I_{n_2} & I_{r_1} \otimes H_2^T \end{pmatrix}, \qquad H_Z = \begin{pmatrix} I_{n_1} \otimes H_2 & H_1^T \otimes I_{r_2} \end{pmatrix}.$$

The Kronecker structure guarantees $H_X H_Z^T = 0$ *algebraically* — no search needed — and sparsity is inherited: check weights multiply by at most the classical degrees. If the classical codes have parameters $[n_i, k_i, d_i]$, the quantum code achieves $[[n_1 n_2 + r_1 r_2,\ k_1 k_2,\ \min(d_1, d_2)]]$. Taking both classical codes to be good LDPC families gives *constant rate* $\kappa > 0$.

The catch is distance: $d = \min(d_1, d_2) = O(\sqrt{n})$ for the natural parameter choices, because the quantum length is quadratic in the classical length. This "square-root barrier" stood for a decade; circuit-level simulations of hypergraph product codes show thresholds of $0.60\%$–$0.84\%$ with cavity-mediated non-local gates [12]. Breaking it required lifts or entirely new complexes.

| Construction | Parameters $[[n,k,d]]$ | Check weight | Rate | Key idea |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Surface code | $[[2d^2, 2, d]]$ | 4 | $\to 0$ | 2D topological locality |
| Hypergraph product | $[[n, \Theta(n), \Theta(\sqrt{n})]]$ | $O(1)$ | $\Theta(1)$ | Kronecker product of classical LDPC [12] |
| Quantum expander | $[[n, \Theta(1), \Theta(n)]]$ | $O(1)$ | $\to 0$ | Expander + small-set-flip [1] |
| Lifted product | $[[n, \Theta(n), \Theta(n)]]$ | $O(1)$ | $\Theta(1)$ | Base-matrix lift over group algebra [7] |
| Quantum Tanner [1] | $[[n, \Theta(n), \Theta(n)]]$ | $O(1)$ | $\Theta(1)$ | Tanner complex on square complex |

### 4.2 Lifted products: breaking the square-root barrier

Panteleev and Kalachev's **lifted product** starts from a *base matrix* $A$ over the group algebra $\mathbb{F}_2[G]$ of a finite group $G$ (typically cyclic or abelian). The lift replaces each group-algebra entry by the corresponding $|G| \times |G|$ permutation matrix, producing binary matrices $\tilde{A}$ of size multiplied by $|G|$. The quantum code is then a twisted product of $\tilde{A}$ with its transpose.

Why does this beat the hypergraph product? The base matrix can be chosen so that the *base code* already has linear distance relative to its small size, and the lift preserves expansion of an associated Tanner graph built from the group's Cayley structure. The framework yields families with $k = \Theta(n)$, $d = \Theta(n)$, and constant check weight — explicit small instances such as the $[[144, 12, 12]]$ bivariate bicycle code (a lifted product over $C_6 \times C_6$) remain among the most hardware-relevant codes today [7][8].

```python
# Sketch: lifted product over a cyclic group algebra F2[C_l x C_m]
# Base matrix entry = group-ring element; lift = block matrix of cyclic shifts.
import numpy as np

def lift(base_poly, l, m):
    """base_poly: dict {(i,j): coeff} representing an element of F2[C_l x C_m].
       Returns the (l*m) x (l*m) binary lift matrix (circulant blocks)."""
    n = l * m
    M = np.zeros((n, n), dtype=np.uint8)
    for (a, b), c in base_poly.items():
        if c:
            for x in range(l):
                for y in range(m):
                    M[x*m + y, ((x+a) % l)*m + ((y+b) % m)] ^= 1
    return M
```

### 4.3 Quantum Tanner codes: expansion on square complexes

Leverrier and Zémor took a different route. Their **quantum Tanner codes** are built on a *square complex*: a 2D combinatorial object whose vertices, edges, and faces carry a Tanner-like local code structure. Start with a group $G$ and two generating sets $A, B$; the Cayley graphs $\mathrm{Cay}(G, A)$ and $\mathrm{Cay}(G, B)$ form the "horizontal" and "vertical" skeletons, and faces correspond to commuting squares $ag = gb$. On each vertex, place a small *local* classical code (a tensor-like "robust" code); the global CSS code glues these local views together [1].

The magic ingredient is *robustness* of the local codes combined with *expansion* of the underlying graphs: if the local codes are sufficiently robust (random tensor codes suffice) and the Cayley graphs are expanders (e.g., Ramanujan graphs from $PSL(2, q)$), the global quantum code has linear distance. The decoder mirrors the construction: a parallel *mismatch* decomposition in which each vertex locally decodes its view, with expansion guaranteeing global convergence in *logarithmic* parallel time [1]. This parallelizability is essential: while the classical decoder runs, fresh errors accumulate, so decoding must outpace the physical clock. Explicit small instances of quantum Tanner codes have now been constructed and benchmarked, showing competitive pseudo-thresholds against surface codes of similar distance under phenomenological and circuit-level noise [5].

### 4.4 Single-shot error correction

Conventional fault-tolerant QEC repeats syndrome measurement $\Theta(d)$ times to build a reliable *history*, because a single noisy syndrome round is untrustworthy. For the surface code this multiplies the time overhead by $d$. **Single-shot** error correction collapses this to *one* noisy syndrome round: the code's redundancy is rich enough that measurement errors can be corrected *together with* data errors, using a decoder that treats the syndrome itself as noisy.

The formal engine is **linear confinement**: a code family has $(\gamma, \alpha)$-linear confinement if every error $E$ of weight $\leq \gamma n$ produces a syndrome of weight $\geq \alpha |E|$ modulo stabilizers. Confinement implies small syndromes cannot hide large errors, so one noisy syndrome round already localizes the damage. For any qLDPC family with linear confinement, a single-shot decoder with a *sustainable threshold* against local stochastic syndrome-data noise exists; moreover, small-set-flip-type decoders imply linear confinement as a corollary [3].

> **Theorem (Single-shot threshold, informal):** For a qLDPC family with linear confinement, there exists a threshold $p^* > 0$ such that below $p^*$, one round of noisy syndrome extraction followed by a constant-depth classical decoder reduces the residual error weight by a constant factor, with failure probability $\exp(-\mathrm{poly}(n))$ [2][3].

Gu, Pattison, and Tang proved single-shot error correction for quantum Tanner codes under *general* circuit noise, with a *constant-depth* classical decoder — the key lemma behind their constant-overhead theorem [2]. Single-shot is what makes the time overhead *constant*: without it, the $\Theta(d) = \Theta(n)$ repeated rounds would destroy the asymptotic advantage. Recent tricycle codes push this further, enabling constant-depth *single-shot preparation of logical magic states* with thresholds above $0.5\%$ under circuit-level noise [6].

### 4.5 Constant-overhead fault tolerance: the threshold theorem

Gottesman's program established that *if* good qLDPC codes admit efficient decoders and fault-tolerant gate gadgets, then fault-tolerant quantum computation needs only constant space overhead — a program recently completed for quantum Tanner codes under general noise [2]:

1. **Fault-tolerant error correction:** single-shot EC with general circuit noise reduces the stabilizer-reduced error weight $sn \to s'n$ ($s' < s$) in *constant time* — one constant-depth syndrome circuit plus a constant-depth classical decoder [2].
2. **Fault-tolerant gates:** transversal and code-deformation gadgets implement a universal gate set with constant overhead; Tanner color codes on expanding simplicial complexes even provide transversal non-Clifford phase gates [10].
3. **Threshold:** below a constant physical error rate, the logical error per gadget is $\exp(-\Omega(n))$, so scaling $n$ (not concatenating) drives the computation-length overhead to a constant [2].

The upshot: an arbitrarily long quantum computation on $k$ logical qubits can be executed with $O(k)$ physical qubits and $O(1)$ time slowdown per gate — exponentially better space scaling than the surface code's $\Theta(k \log^2(1/\epsilon))$. The catch, discussed in §6, is that the constants and the connectivity demands are formidable.

## 5 Empirical Evaluation and Proofs

### 5.1 Proof sketches

**Linear distance of quantum Tanner codes (idea).** Suppose a $Z$-error $E$ has weight $< \delta n$ but trivial syndrome. Its *local views* at vertices are codewords of the local codes; robustness of the local tensor codes forces each nonzero local view to have large weight, while expansion of the Cayley graphs bounds how many vertices can share the support of $E$. Choosing the robustness parameter against the expansion constant yields a contradiction unless $E$ is a stabilizer — hence $d_Z \geq \delta n$ [1].

**Small-set-flip decoding (idea).** The decoder maintains a candidate correction $F$; while $\sigma(E + F)$ is nonzero, it finds a *small* set $S$ of qubits ($|S| \leq c$) whose flip strictly decreases $|\sigma|$. Expansion guarantees such an $S$ exists below the decoding radius. Each step reduces syndrome weight by $\geq 1$, so decoding terminates in $O(n)$ sequential steps — or $O(\log n)$ parallel steps over disjoint sets. Codes admitting such local greedy decoders possess linear confinement, closing the loop to single-shot thresholds [1][3].

**Single-shot threshold (idea).** Under $p$-local stochastic noise, both data and syndrome bits are corrupted. Linear confinement turns the noisy syndrome into a *metasyndrome* problem: decode the syndrome noise first (a classical LDPC instance on the *syndrome code*), then decode data errors with the cleaned syndrome. Below threshold, a union bound over minimal-weight failure configurations gives exponentially small failure probability in $\mathrm{poly}(n)$; iterating the constant-time EC gadget gives a *sustainable* threshold for arbitrarily many cycles [2][3].

### 5.2 Numerical thresholds

Theory gives existence; simulations give numbers. Representative published results under *circuit-level* noise (depolarizing gates, measurement and idle errors):

| Code | Parameters | Decoder | Threshold / pseudo-threshold |
|---|---|---|---|
| Rotated surface | $[[2d^2, 2, d]]$ | MWPM / UF | $\approx 0.7\%$–$1.0\%$ |
| BB code | $[[144, 12, 12]]$ | BP-OSD | $\approx 0.7\%$ [8] |
| Coset LDPC (w=6) [7] | $[[96, 8, 10]]$ | BP-OSD | $\approx 0.65\%$ |
| Tricycle [6] | various | BP-OSD / MLE | $> 0.5\%$ |
| Mirror (w=6) [8] | $[[60, 4, 10]]$ etc. | BP-OSD | $\approx 0.2\%$ |
| Quantum Tanner (small) [5] | $[[144, \cdot, \cdot]]$-scale | BP | competitive pseudo-thresholds |
| Hypergraph product [12] | various | BP | $0.60\%$–$0.84\%$ |

Two observations: (i) qLDPC thresholds now *match or approach* the surface code's under identical noise models — the historical threshold gap has closed; (ii) the win is in *rate*: the $[[144, 12, 12]]$ BB code encodes 12 logical qubits where a distance-12 surface code encodes 1–2, a $\sim 6\times$ space saving that compounds with distance [8].

### 5.3 Decoder landscape

- **Small-set-flip:** provable linear-time (logarithmic parallel) decoding up to a constant error fraction; the workhorse behind the *theoretical* single-shot and threshold results [1][3].
- **BP + OSD (ordered statistics decoding):** the practical champion — belief propagation produces soft marginals, OSD post-processes the most likely error; achieves the best finite-size thresholds but with superlinear classical cost. Notably, an order-of-magnitude gap remains between BP+OSD and the exact (exponential) most-likely-error decoder on tricycle codes, motivating better practical decoders [6].
- **Fault-tolerant BP (FTBP):** runs BP on a *space–time* Tanner graph spanning multiple noisy syndrome rounds, with probabilistic error consolidation to tame degeneracy and an adaptive sliding window for long error events; reports $0.4\%$–$0.87\%$ thresholds on topological families and extends naturally to qLDPC [4].
- **LEAD / ensemble decoders:** local ensemble-assisted parallel decoding frameworks tailored to quantum Tanner codes, exploiting their local vertex structure for throughput [9].

The central tension: provable decoders (small-set-flip) have weaker thresholds; high-threshold decoders (BP-OSD) lack proofs and strain real-time budgets. Closing this gap — a *provable, parallel, high-threshold* decoder — is the field's main algorithmic open problem.

## 6 Limitations

1. **Long-range connectivity.** Good qLDPC codes are *not* geometrically local: checks couple distant qubits. Realizing them needs long-range gates — available in trapped-ion, photonic, and reconfigurable neutral-atom arrays (where atoms are physically shuttled), but essentially absent in fixed superconducting grids [3]. The constant-overhead theorem assumes this connectivity; without it, routing overhead can erase the advantage.
2. **Constants matter.** Asymptotic goodness hides constants: the proven thresholds are far below simulated ones, the required block sizes for the rate advantage to materialize are $n \gtrsim 10^3$–$10^4$, and the classical decoding throughput (syndrome volume per QEC cycle) is a serious systems challenge — near-memory processing architectures are being explored to meet it [11].
3. **Fault-tolerant logic beyond memory.** Most threshold results concern quantum *memory*. Universal computation needs fault-tolerant gates and magic-state distillation at *constant* overhead too. Progress exists — transversal gates on Tanner color codes [10], single-shot magic-state preparation in tricycle codes [6] — but a complete low-overhead universal gate set for one good code family is still being assembled.
4. **Syndrome extraction costs.** Constant-weight checks still need fault-tolerant measurement circuits; a single ancilla per check risks hook errors, while verified multi-ancilla extraction adds qubit and depth costs that headline thresholds often assume away [12].
5. **Decoder latency.** BP-OSD's classical runtime can exceed the QEC cycle budget at scale; the provable small-set-flip decoder is parallel but lower-threshold. Real-time decoding at MHz syndrome rates for $10^4$-qubit blocks remains unsolved [4][11].

## 7 Conclusion

Quantum LDPC codes have completed a remarkable journey: from Gallager's sparse graphs and Sipser–Spielman expanders, through the hypergraph product's constant rate and the expander codes' linear distance, to the asymptotically good lifted-product and quantum Tanner families of 2022 — and finally to a full constant-overhead fault-tolerance theorem under general circuit noise [1][2]. Single-shot error correction collapses the time overhead to a constant; linear confinement and small-set-flip decoding supply the proofs; and finite-size instances (BB, mirror, tricycle, small Tanner codes) now post circuit-level thresholds competitive with the surface code while encoding an order of magnitude more logical qubits per physical qubit [5][6][7][8].

The surface code will likely remain the pragmatic choice for the first generations of fault-tolerant devices, where 2D locality is king. But the asymptotic verdict is in: *constant overhead is achievable*, and the road to it runs through quantum LDPC codes. The remaining work is constructive and concrete — long-range hardware, real-time decoders, and universal gate gadgets — the kind of problems that yield to engineering once the theory says "possible."
