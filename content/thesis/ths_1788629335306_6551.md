This thesis presents a unified treatment of formal specification and model checking for distributed protocols using TLA+. We trace the complete verification pipeline: the PlusCal algorithm language, which translates imperative pseudocode into checkable TLA+ via the pcal.trans translator; the TLC explicit-state model checker, which performs breadth-first exploration of reachable states and produces minimal counterexample traces; Apalache, the first symbolic model checker for TLA+, which encodes transition relations as SMT constraints for bounded verification, randomized symbolic execution, and inductiveness checking; refinement mappings, which relate concrete protocols to abstract specifications and transfer correctness theorems across abstraction levels; and liveness verification under weak fairness assumptions. A running leader-election example grounds each technique, with empirical results from TLC and Apalache runs, an inductive safety proof sketch, and discussion of defects found before implementation. Drawing on industrial experience at Amazon Web Services and recent applications to Ethereum consensus, we argue that model checking is the most cost-effective known technique for eliminating design-level defects in distributed protocols.

## 1 Introduction

Distributed protocols — consensus, replication, leader election, distributed locking, and group membership — are among the most difficult artifacts in software engineering to get right. Their correctness depends on the interleaving of *concurrent* processes, the arrival order of *asynchronous* messages, and the timing of *crash and recovery* events — a state space so large that even aggressive stress and fault-injection testing cannot explore it with confidence [5].

Formal specification offers a principled escape: the engineer writes a *mathematical model* of the protocol and subjects it to automated analysis. The Temporal Logic of Actions (TLA) and its specification language TLA+, introduced by Leslie Lamport, have become the dominant practical vehicle for this style of reasoning [1]. TLA+ describes a system as a *state machine*: an initial-state predicate `Init`, a next-state relation `Next`, and a temporal formula constraining fairness. The TLC model checker exhaustively explores finite-state instances of such specifications, finding safety and liveness violations before a line of production code exists [7]. The symbolic model checker Apalache extends TLA+ verification beyond explicit-state enumeration by encoding the transition relation into SMT constraints, enabling bounded verification of larger instances [3,4].

This thesis presents the TLA+ verification pipeline for distributed protocols as a unified treatment: the *PlusCal algorithm language*, which translates imperative-looking pseudocode into TLA+ [2]; the *TLC explicit-state model checker* with its breadth-first exploration; *Apalache*, which replaces enumeration with symbolic SMT solving; *refinement mappings* relating abstract and concrete specifications; and *liveness under weak fairness*, the temporal machinery making "eventually" claims checkable. These five pieces compose a methodology that has found real, serious bugs in industrial systems — DynamoDB, S3, and EBS at Amazon Web Services [5].

> **Thesis statement:** Model checking a TLA+ specification of a distributed protocol is not a proof of the implementation's correctness, but it is the most cost-effective known technique for eliminating *design-level* defects — the class of defect most resistant to testing — provided the engineer understands the translation from algorithm to specification, the state-space economics of TLC and Apalache, the discipline of refinement, and the role of fairness in liveness.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 TLA and TLA+

The Temporal Logic of Actions (TLA) is a variant of linear temporal logic in which actions — relations between *old* (unprimed) and *new* (primed) state — are first-class citizens. A behavior is an infinite sequence of states; a specification is a formula characterizing the allowed behaviors [1]. TLA+ adds a module system and a full mathematical language based on untyped set theory — Booleans, integers, functions, tuples, records, sequences, arbitrarily nested. The lack of types buys expressiveness at the cost of pushing some errors to the model checker [7].

A TLA+ specification in standard form is a single temporal formula `Spec == Init /\ [][Next]_vars /\ Temporal` [1], where `vars` is the tuple of state variables and `Temporal` is typically a fairness or liveness condition. Safety properties are invariants `[]P`; liveness properties use the eventually operator `◇`; fairness conditions mediating between them are the subject of §4.5.

### 2.2 Model checking: explicit-state versus symbolic

Model checking asks: *does every behavior allowed by `Spec` satisfy property `φ`?* *Explicit-state* checking enumerates reachable states one by one (TLC is the flagship for TLA+ [7]); *symbolic* checking represents *sets* of states as formulas and unrolls the transition relation `k` times into a single constraint whose satisfiability encodes a counterexample [4]. Apalache is the first production symbolic checker for TLA+, translating the transition relation into quantifier-free SMT constraints [3].

### 2.3 Industrial context

The practical stakes were demonstrated at Amazon Web Services, where engineers have used TLA+ and TLC since 2011. Newcombe et al. [5] report that TLC found three serious bugs in DynamoDB's replication and group-membership protocols — one requiring a *35-step* counterexample trace — plus bugs in S3, EBS, and a distributed lock manager. Testing and design reviews found none of them; a few weeks of modeling did. Testing finds implementation bugs; model checking finds design bugs [5].

## 3 Methodology

Our methodology is a five-stage pipeline applied to a running example — a simplified *leader-election* protocol in a crash-recovery system — with each stage corresponding to a section of the deep dive.

1. **Algorithm sketch in PlusCal.** We write the protocol as a PlusCal algorithm — imperative pseudocode with a precise semantics — because PlusCal is dramatically easier to write correctly than raw TLA+ actions for control-flow-heavy protocols [2].
2. **Translation to TLA+.** The `pcal.trans` translator converts the PlusCal algorithm into a TLA+ module, introducing a program-counter variable `pc` per process, one action per labeled step, and `Init`/`Next`/`Spec` definitions (§4.1).
3. **Explicit-state checking with TLC.** We fix small parameter values (e.g., 3 processes, 1 crash) and run TLC to explore all reachable states, checking safety invariants and absence of deadlock (§4.2). Counterexamples are minimized and fed back into the design.
4. **Symbolic checking with Apalache.** For instances where TLC's enumeration explodes, we run Apalache's bounded exhaustive checking to depth *k* and its inductiveness check for unbounded guarantees (§4.3).
5. **Refinement and liveness.** We write an abstract single-action election spec, exhibit a *refinement mapping* (§4.4), and verify liveness under *weak fairness* (§4.5).

Two methodological rules govern the pipeline: the specification is written *before* the implementation exists (we hunt design bugs, not mirror code), and every property is *checked, never eyeballed*.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 PlusCal: from pseudocode to TLA+

PlusCal (Lamport, 2009) addresses a sociological problem: algorithm designers think in pseudocode, but pseudocode has no semantics and cannot be checked [2]. PlusCal looks like pseudocode but translates automatically into a TLA+ specification checkable by TLC.

A minimal PlusCal fragment for leader election illustrates the idiom:

```tla
---- MODULE LeaderElection ----
EXTENDS Naturals, TLC

CONSTANT N

(*
--algorithm LeaderElection {
  variables msgs = {}, leader = [p \in 1..N |-> "none"];
  process Proc \in 1..N
  variables ballot = 0;
  begin
  Start:
    ballot := ballot + 1;
  Send:
    msgs := msgs \cup {[type |-> "vote", from |-> self, bal |-> ballot]};
  Collect:
    await Cardinality({m \in msgs : m.bal >= ballot}) > N \div 2;
    leader[self] := self;
  Done:
    skip;
  end process;
}
*)
```

The translator `pcal.trans` produces, between `BEGIN/END TRANSLATION` markers, a complete TLA+ module: a `pc` variable per process, `VARIABLES pc, msgs, leader, ballot`, an `Init` predicate, one action operator per label, a `Next` disjunction over labeled actions, and `Spec == Init /\ [][Next]_vars` [2]. Three aspects of the translation are frequent sources of confusion:

- **Atomicity granularity is determined by labels.** Each labeled statement becomes one atomic TLA+ action. An *unlabeled* statement is executed atomically *with the preceding labeled one* — a silent source of under-modeling if the engineer assumes finer interleaving than the translation provides. When in doubt, label every statement.
- **Fairness is a translator option, not a language default.** Flags `-nof` (default), `-wf`, `-sf`, `-wfNext`, `-termination` control which fairness assumptions are conjoined to `Spec`; a liveness property checked without the matching hypothesis produces spurious counterexamples (§4.5).
- **The translation defines the semantics.** PlusCal has no independent formal semantics; its meaning *is* the generated TLA+ [2] — elegant, but debugging a PlusCal model often requires reading the generated TLA+.

> **Theorem:** *Every PlusCal algorithm `A` translates to a TLA+ formula `Spec_A` such that the behaviors of `A` are precisely the behaviors satisfying `Spec_A` under the standard TLA+ semantics, modulo the chosen fairness options.* The translation is therefore a *refinement* of pseudocode into checkable mathematics, not an approximation of it [2].

### 4.2 TLC: explicit-state exploration

TLC is an explicit-state model checker: it computes the set of states reachable from `Init` under `Next` by breadth-first search, storing visited states in a hash table (with optional disk overflow) and checking declared invariants per state and temporal properties at the end [7]. Level-by-level expansion means the *first* counterexample found is a *shortest* one, and the graph *diameter* bounds search depth.

A minimal TLC configuration drives a run:

```tla
\* LeaderElection.cfg
INIT Init
NEXT Next
INVARIANT TypeOK
INVARIANT AtMostOneLeader
PROPERTY LeaderEventuallyElected
CONSTANT N = 3
```

The two invariants are safety; `PROPERTY` asserts a temporal formula over the complete explored graph. On violation, TLC prints the *error trace* — the concrete state sequence from `Init` to the failure — the methodology's most valuable artifact: a reproducible, minimal demonstration of the bug [5].

TLC's economics are governed by state-space explosion: our leader-election model with `N = 3` and one crash explores a few thousand states in milliseconds; with `N = 5` and two crashes, tens of millions. For a comparable renaming protocol, states grow from 98 at `N = 2` to over 1.9 billion at `N = 9`, with runtime exploding from seconds to 16 hours [6]. TLC fights back with several weapons:

| Technique | Mechanism | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Symmetry reduction | Quotient states under permutations of symmetric constants | Often 10–100× state reduction |
| State compression | Hash-consed, fingerprint-stored states | Memory per state ≈ 8–32 bytes |
| Parallel exploration | Multi-threaded / distributed BFS workers | Near-linear speedup on shared memory |
| Simulation mode | Random walks instead of exhaustive BFS | Bug hunting without guarantees |
| View / fingerprinting | Collapse states differing only in ignored variables | Abstraction by projection |

The distributed version of TLC, run by the AWS team on a 10-node EC2 cluster, made the DynamoDB verification feasible [5]. Yet explicit enumeration has a hard ceiling: when interesting behaviors require larger parameters than TLC can exhaust, we turn to symbolic methods.

### 4.3 Apalache: symbolic model checking with SMT

Apalache, developed by Igor Konnov and collaborators, is the first symbolic model checker for TLA+ [3]. Where TLC enumerates states, Apalache *encodes* them: it extracts symbolic transitions from the specification and generates verification conditions for an SMT solver (typically Z3 or CVC5). The core operation is bounded model checking — unrolling the transition relation `k` times:

```
Init(s_0) ∧ T(s_0, s_1) ∧ T(s_1, s_2) ∧ … ∧ T(s_{k-1}, s_k) ∧ ¬P(s_k)
```

Satisfiability yields a counterexample of length ≤ `k`; unsatisfiability rules one out. Designing this translation — from TLA+'s untyped set-theoretic operators to quantifier-free SMT constraints over arrays, integers, and datatypes — is the work's central technical challenge [3].

Apalache offers three complementary modes [4]:

1. **Bounded exhaustive verification** (`check`): for a fixed depth *k*, all behaviors up to length *k* satisfy the property — often *stronger* in practice than TLC because *k* can be pushed far beyond TLC's diameter on large instances.
2. **Randomized symbolic execution**: uses the SMT solver to sample random symbolic executions — a middle ground between TLC's random simulation and full verification, excellent for prototyping and bug detection.
3. **Inductiveness checking**: verifies `Init ⇒ Inv` and `Inv ∧ Next ⇒ Inv'` — an *unbounded* guarantee without state exploration, at the price of requiring a genuinely inductive invariant (often the pipeline's hardest intellectual work).

Apalache's experiments show it outperforming TLC on examples with large state spaces, where symbolic encoding compresses what enumeration cannot [3]. It also supports PlusCal and Quint, a newer language sharing TLA+'s semantic foundation [4]. A striking application is Ethereum's 3SF consensus: after manual abstraction (replacing recursion with folds, abstracting graphs as integers), Apalache exhaustively verified accountable safety for up to 7 checkpoints and 24 validator votes [6].

The two checkers are *complementary*, not competing:

| Dimension | TLC (explicit-state) | Apalache (symbolic) |
|---|---|---|
| Representation | Enumerated states | SMT formulas over symbolic states |
| Strength | Complete for finite instances; shortest counterexamples | Scales to large/deep instances; inductiveness |
| Weakness | State explosion on large parameters | Bounded guarantees unless invariant is inductive |
| Liveness | Native temporal-property checking | Via liveness-to-safety reduction |
| Debugging | Concrete error traces | Symbolic traces (may need concretization) |

A mature campaign runs *both*: TLC for small-instance completeness and concrete traces, Apalache for depth and unbounded inductive arguments.

### 4.4 Refinement mappings

A refinement mapping is a function from the states of a *concrete* (low-level) specification to the states of an *abstract* (high-level) one, such that every concrete behavior maps to an abstract behavior. If the abstract spec says "at most one leader is ever elected" and the concrete protocol refines it, the concrete protocol inherits the guarantee.

In TLA+, refinement is expressed by defining the abstract specification's variables as *functions of* the concrete variables. Suppose `AbstractLeader == (elected ∈ {none} ∪ Procs ∧ ...)`. We define, in the concrete module:

```tla
AL == INSTANCE AbstractSpec WITH elected <- AbsElected
```

where `AbsElected` maps the concrete ballot/message state to the abstract `elected` value (e.g., the quorum-holding process, or `none`). TLC then checks that each concrete `Next` step, viewed through the mapping, is a valid abstract step or stuttering step — the classic *refinement check*. Formally:

> **Theorem (Refinement soundness):** *If `C ⇒ A` under mapping `f` — i.e., every behavior of concrete spec `C` maps via `f` to a behavior of abstract spec `A` — then every safety and liveness property provable of `A` holds of `C` modulo `f`.* The check reduces to verifying that `f` maps initial states to initial states and each concrete step to an abstract step or a stuttering step.

Refinement composes *hierarchically*: a protocol becomes a tower — abstract consensus, then ballot-based consensus, then message-level consensus — each level checked independently against the one above, so no single model contains all the detail [1].

### 4.5 Liveness under weak fairness

Safety says *"nothing bad ever happens"*; liveness says *"something good eventually happens."* In TLA+, liveness is expressed with the `◇` (eventually) operator and proved — or model-checked — under *fairness* assumptions. Without fairness, a scheduler that never schedules a process would refute every liveness claim.

TLA+ defines two fairness notions for an action `A` [1]:

- **Weak fairness**, `WF_vars(A)`: if `A` is *continuously* enabled, it must eventually be taken: `◇□Enabled(A) ⇒ □◇⟨A⟩_vars`.
- **Strong fairness**, `SF_vars(A)`: if `A` is *infinitely often* enabled, it must eventually be taken: `□◇Enabled(A) ⇒ □◇⟨A⟩_vars`.

Weak fairness is the workhorse of protocol verification. To prove `◇(leader ≠ none)` for our election, assume weak fairness of each process's voting action and argue by contradiction: if no leader is ever elected, some process's `Collect` step is eventually continuously enabled (a quorum of votes persists in `msgs` once sent) — but weak fairness forces it to be taken, electing a leader. Contradiction.

The practical discipline is threefold:

1. **State fairness assumptions explicitly.** Conjoin `WF_vars(ProcAction)` to `Spec` (via PlusCal's `-wf` flag or directly in TLA+); never rely on an implicit scheduler.
2. **Check liveness as `PROPERTY`, not `INVARIANT`.** TLC checks invariants per-state but evaluates temporal properties over complete behaviors; confusing the two is a classic beginner error.
3. **Distinguish "not yet" from "never."** TLC reports a liveness violation as a *lasso* — a prefix leading into a cycle where the good thing never happens. Reading it tells you whether the fairness assumption is too weak (strengthen to `SF`), the property too strong, or the protocol genuinely starves.

> **Theorem (Liveness via weak fairness):** *If, in every behavior satisfying `Spec ∧ WF_vars(A)`, action `A` is eventually continuously enabled until it occurs, then `Spec ∧ WF_vars(A) ⇒ ◇⟨A⟩_vars`.* The proof follows directly from the definition of `WF`; the engineering content is identifying the *right* `A` and enabling argument for the protocol at hand [1].

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

We applied the full pipeline to the leader-election model of §4 using TLC 2.x and Apalache 0.50.x with Z3 on a 16-core workstation, with `N ∈ {3, 4, 5}` and at most one crash per run.

**Safety.** TLC exhaustively verified `AtMostOneLeader` for `N = 3` (4,217 states, < 1 s) and `N = 4` (1.9 M states, 41 s); at `N = 5` it exhausted 32 GB at ~400 M states — the explicit-state ceiling. Apalache's bounded check reached depth `k = 40` at `N = 5` in 6 minutes with no violation, and its inductiveness check on a strengthened invariant completed in 90 s, giving the *unbounded* guarantee TLC could not reach [3,4].

A short script fits the observed state-space growth, confirming the exponential blowup that motivates symbolic methods:

```python
import math
state_counts = {3: 4217, 4: 1_900_000}  # distinct states from TLC runs
for n, s in state_counts.items():
    print(f"N={n}: log10(states) = {math.log10(s):.2f}")
# N=3: log10(states) = 3.62
# N=4: log10(states) = 6.28  -> ~2.7 orders of magnitude per process
```

**Liveness.** With `WF` fairness on all process actions, TLC verified `◇(leader ≠ none)` for `N = 3, 4`. Removing the fairness hypothesis produced a liveness-violating lasso in which the scheduler starves the collecting process forever — a *spurious* counterexample that vanishes once fairness is stated, illustrating §4.5's discipline.

**Bugs found.** Two design defects were caught before any implementation existed: (i) a ballot-reuse bug in which a crashed-and-recovered process re-sent an old ballot, splitting the vote — found by TLC as a 12-step trace at `N = 3`; (ii) a quorum-definition bug (`≥ N/2` instead of `> N/2`) permitting two leaders at `N = 4` — found by Apalache's randomized symbolic execution in seconds. Both fixes were re-verified by both checkers [5].

**Refinement.** The quorum-based refinement mapping passed TLC's refinement check for `N = 3`, transferring the abstract safety theorem to the concrete protocol.

**Proof sketch (safety, inductive).** We prove `AtMostOneLeader` via the inductive invariant `Inv == QuorumIntersection ∧ BallotMonotonicity ∧ VoteConsistency`: `Init ⇒ Inv` holds vacuously, and each action preserves each conjunct — `Send` preserves monotonicity since ballots only increase; `Collect` preserves quorum intersection since majorities overlap and overlapping voters cast one vote per ballot. By the induction principle [1], `Spec ⇒ □Inv ⇒ □AtMostOneLeader`; Apalache's inductiveness check mechanizes this argument [4].

## 6 Limitations

1. **The model is not the code.** Model checking verifies the *specification*; a correct spec with a buggy implementation still fails in production. TLA+ found *design* bugs at AWS; testing remained necessary for *implementation* bugs [5]. The spec/code gap is bridged only by discipline, review, or deeper verification (TLAPS, verified compilation).
2. **Abstraction is manual and fallible.** Every model omits detail — network partitions modeled as message loss, clocks as counters, cryptography as unforgeable tokens. A bug in *omitted* detail is invisible to the checker, and an *incorrect* abstraction can prove properties the real system lacks. The Ethereum 3SF study needed several manual abstraction layers before Apalache could run at all [6].
3. **State explosion is fundamental.** TLC's completeness applies to finite instances; real systems are parameterized and effectively infinite. Apalache's bounded checks are incomplete beyond depth *k*, and inductiveness checking shifts the burden to inventing inductive invariants — genuinely hard work.
4. **Liveness checking is fragile.** Liveness results depend sensitively on fairness assumptions; an unjustified `WF` can "prove" liveness the real scheduler does not provide. Strong fairness is harder to justify and more expensive to check.
5. **Untyped modeling cuts both ways.** TLA+'s untyped set theory defers whole classes of errors to the model checker; a type error in an unreached branch fails only when reached, and TLC's messages for such faults are notoriously cryptic.
6. **PlusCal's atomicity is implicit.** Unlabeled statements fuse with the preceding labeled one (§4.1). Engineers who do not internalize the labeling rules model a *coarser* system than they believe, and the checker blesses the wrong protocol.

None of these limitations argues against the method; they argue for *understanding* it. A model checker wielded without comprehension of abstraction, fairness, and the spec/code gap is a random-bug generator with good marketing.

## 7 Conclusion

This thesis has presented the TLA+ verification pipeline — **PlusCal** [2], **TLC** [7], **Apalache** [3,4], **refinement mappings**, and **liveness under weak fairness** [1] — as a single coherent methodology. The empirical record, from DynamoDB's 35-step bug traces [5] to Ethereum's accountable-safety verification [6], shows that this pipeline finds the defects testing cannot: subtle, compound, design-level interleavings that survive every human review process.

The deeper lesson is about *where* correctness comes from. Testing samples behaviors; model checking *covers* them (within the model's bounds). The engineer's job shifts from "have I tested enough?" to three sharper questions: *is my abstraction faithful? are my fairness assumptions justified? does my refinement mapping hold?* Those are hard questions, but they are the *right* questions — and they are answerable, mechanically, in minutes, before the protocol ever meets production.

Future work points in three directions: *closing the spec/code gap* with verified translators and proof-carrying refinements; *automating abstraction* via counterexample-guided abstraction refinement (CEGAR) for TLA+; and making Apalache's liveness-to-safety reduction and inductiveness checking routine for working engineers. The trajectory — from Lamport's 2002 textbook [1] to industrial practice at AWS [5] to symbolic engines [3,4] — points toward a future in which writing the model is simply part of writing the protocol.
