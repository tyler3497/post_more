# Rényi Differential Privacy Accounting for Large-Scale DP-SGD: The Moments Accountant, Privacy Amplification by Subsampling and Shuffling, and Federated Private Optimization with Secure Aggregation

## Abstract

Training deep neural networks under differential privacy at scale demands privacy accounting that is tight, composable over tens of thousands of adaptive steps, and tractable. This thesis develops Rényi differential privacy (RDP) accounting theory for differentially private stochastic gradient descent (DP-SGD), unifying the moments accountant of Abadi et al. [1] with the Rényi framework of Mironov [2]. We derive the Rényi divergence of the Poisson-subsampled Gaussian mechanism and prove the analytical moments accountant bound of Wang, Balle, and Kasiviswanathan [3], quantifying how privacy amplification by subsampling [4] attenuates the per-step Rényi cost by the square of the sampling rate. We extend the analysis to privacy amplification by shuffling [5], the shuffle-model framework of Cheu et al. [6], and federated differential privacy with secure aggregation via the distributed discrete Gaussian mechanism of Kairouz et al. [7]. We establish optimal conversion from RDP to $(\varepsilon, \delta)$-differential privacy and instantiate a numerical accounting protocol with order search. The thesis closes with Steinke's composition results [8], the gap between accounting upper bounds and membership-inference audits, and open problems in adaptive and federated composition.

## 1 Introduction

Differentially private stochastic gradient descent (DP-SGD), introduced by Abadi et al. [1], remains the dominant algorithm for training deep neural networks with formal privacy guarantees. Each iteration samples a mini-batch, clips per-example gradients to a bounded $\ell_2$ norm, injects calibrated Gaussian noise, and descends on the privatized average. The privacy cost of a single step is modest; the cost of *ten thousand* adaptive steps is the central analytical challenge. Naive composition yields vacuous guarantees, and even advanced composition leaves substantial slack.

The moments accountant [1] resolved this by tracking the *privacy loss random variable* $L^{(o)} = \log \frac{\Pr[\mathcal{M}(D) = o]}{\Pr[\mathcal{M}(D') = o]}$ through its cumulant generating function $\alpha(\lambda) = \max_{D \sim D'} \log \mathbb{E}[\exp(\lambda L)]$, exploiting the fact that cumulant generating functions *add* under composition while the final $(\varepsilon, \delta)$ guarantee follows from a Chernoff tail bound. Mironov [2] subsequently recognized this machinery as an instance of a cleaner primitive — Rényi differential privacy — based on the Rényi divergence of order $\alpha$ between output distributions on adjacent datasets. RDP accounting has since become the *de facto* standard in production libraries, because the Rényi divergence of the Gaussian mechanism admits the closed form $\varepsilon(\alpha) = \alpha \Delta^2 / 2\sigma^2$, composes additively, and converts to $(\varepsilon, \delta)$-DP by optimizing over the order $\alpha$.

This thesis is organized around four technical pillars:

1. **The RDP calculus of the subsampled Gaussian mechanism.** We derive exact and analytical bounds on the Rényi divergence induced by Poisson subsampling, recovering the analytical moments accountant [3] and quantifying amplification.
2. **Privacy amplification by subsampling** in its general form [4]: couplings, advanced joint convexity, and privacy profiles for Poisson sampling, sampling without replacement, and sampling with replacement.
3. **Privacy amplification by shuffling** [5, 8]: how anonymity converts local randomizers into central-model guarantees, and why the shuffle model occupies a distinct point in the privacy–utility–trust tradeoff space.
4. **Federated differential privacy with secure aggregation** [7]: the distributed discrete Gaussian mechanism, where noise is generated collaboratively and only the aggregate is revealed.

Throughout, we convert every RDP bound to $(\varepsilon, \delta)$-DP via the optimal order-selection rule, instantiate the accounting numerically, and benchmark against naive and advanced composition. We conclude with limitations — the looseness of RDP-to-DP conversion, the Poisson-versus-shuffle sampling gap, floating-point and discreteness pathologies, and the open problem of fully adaptive composition — and with directions for future work.

## 2 Background and Related Work

### 2.1 Differential privacy and the Gaussian mechanism

A randomized mechanism $\mathcal{M}$ satisfies $(\varepsilon, \delta)$-differential privacy if for all adjacent datasets $D \sim D'$ (differing in one record) and all measurable outputs $S$, $\Pr[\mathcal{M}(D) \in S] \le e^{\varepsilon} \Pr[\mathcal{M}(D') \in S] + \delta$. The Gaussian mechanism releases $f(D) + \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2 I)$ for a function of $\ell_2$-sensitivity $\Delta$; calibrated noise $\sigma \propto \Delta / \varepsilon$ yields the classical guarantee. In DP-SGD [1], the "function" is the sum of clipped per-example gradients, so $\Delta$ equals the clipping norm $C$, and each step is a Gaussian mechanism with noise multiplier $\sigma$.

> **Definition 2.1 (Rényi differential privacy [2]).** A mechanism $\mathcal{M}$ satisfies $(\alpha, \varepsilon)$-RDP of order $\alpha > 1$ if for all adjacent $D, D'$,
>
> $$D_{\alpha}(\mathcal{M}(D) \,\|\, \mathcal{M}(D')) := \frac{1}{\alpha - 1} \log \mathbb{E}_{o \sim \mathcal{M}(D')}\left[\left(\frac{\Pr[\mathcal{M}(D) = o]}{\Pr[\mathcal{M}(D') = o]}\right)^{\alpha}\right] \le \varepsilon.$$

RDP enjoys three properties that make it the accounting workhorse: **adaptive composition is additive** (the RDP parameters of a $k$-fold adaptive composition sum); **post-processing immunity** holds; and the **Gaussian mechanism** satisfies $(\alpha, \alpha \Delta^2 / 2\sigma^2)$-RDP for every $\alpha > 1$ simultaneously [2]. Conversion back to approximate DP is lossless up to the standard bound: $(\alpha, \varepsilon)$-RDP implies $(\varepsilon + \frac{\log(1/\delta)}{\alpha - 1}, \delta)$-DP for every $\delta \in (0,1)$ [2].

### 2.2 The moments accountant and its Rényi reinterpretation

Abadi et al. [1] computed, for the Poisson-subsampled Gaussian mechanism, the quantity $\alpha(\lambda)$ by numerical integration of the privacy loss distribution, summed it over training steps, and recovered $(\varepsilon, \delta)$ via $\delta = \min_{\lambda} \exp(\alpha_{\text{tot}}(\lambda) - \lambda \varepsilon)$. Mironov [2] showed this is exactly RDP accounting in disguise: $\alpha(\lambda) = \lambda \cdot \varepsilon_{\text{RDP}}(\lambda + 1)$. The analytical moments accountant [3] replaced numerical integration with a closed-form upper bound valid in the regime $q < 1/(16\sigma)$, removing the quadrature entirely.

### 2.3 Amplification: subsampling, shuffling, and the distributed models

Privacy amplification by subsampling [4] states that a mechanism run on a random $\gamma$-fraction of the data inherits a strictly stronger guarantee — for Poisson sampling, $(\varepsilon, \delta)$-DP amplifies to $(\log(1 + \gamma(e^{\varepsilon} - 1)), \gamma \delta)$-DP. Amplification by shuffling [5] shows that $\varepsilon_0$-local randomizers, when their outputs are uniformly permuted, satisfy $(O(\varepsilon_0 \sqrt{\log(1/\delta)/n}), \delta)$-DP centrally: anonymity itself is a privacy resource. Cheu et al. [6] developed the shuffle model as a distributed trust model sitting between local and central DP, and Kairouz et al. [7] combined distributed noise generation with cryptographic secure aggregation so that a federated server learns only the noisy sum. Steinke [8] unified composition and subsampling amplification in a single RDP-native framework with tight generic bounds.

## 3 Methodology

Our methodology is analytical accounting validated by numerical instantiation. We work in the *add/remove* adjacency for the central-model results and the *replace-one* adjacency where noted, tracking RDP as a function $\varepsilon(\alpha)$ over a grid of integer and half-integer orders, composing additively, and converting to $(\varepsilon, \delta)$-DP by minimizing over $\alpha$.

### 3.1 The subsampled Gaussian mechanism

Let $\mathcal{G}_{\sigma}$ denote the Gaussian mechanism with noise multiplier $\sigma$ applied to a sum of clipped gradients (sensitivity $C = 1$ after normalization). Poisson subsampling $\text{Sample}_q$ includes each record independently with probability $q$. The composed mechanism $\mathcal{M} = \mathcal{G}_{\sigma} \circ \text{Sample}_q$ has output distribution a *mixture*: with probability $1 - q$ the differing record is absent and the two output distributions coincide; with probability $q$ they differ by one clipped gradient.

> **Theorem 3.1 (Analytical moments accountant [3]).** Let $q$ be the sampling probability and $\sigma$ the noise multiplier with $q < 1/(16\sigma)$. For integer $\alpha$ with $1 < \alpha \le \sigma^2 \log(1/(q\sigma)) + 1$, the Poisson-subsampled Gaussian mechanism satisfies $(\alpha, \varepsilon)$-RDP with
>
> $$\varepsilon(\alpha) \le \frac{1}{\alpha - 1} \log\left(1 + q^2 \binom{\alpha}{2} \min\{4(e^{1/\sigma^2} - 1),\, 2e^{1/\sigma^2}\} + \sum_{j=3}^{\alpha} q^j \binom{\alpha}{j} 2 e^{(j-1)j / 2\sigma^2}\right).$$

*Proof sketch.* Expand the Rényi divergence of the mixture via the binomial theorem applied to the likelihood ratio. The $j = 2$ term dominates for small $q$; higher-order terms are bounded by the MGF of a $\chi^2$-type statistic of the Gaussian likelihood ratio, yielding the exponential factors. The constraint on $\alpha$ ensures the tail sum converges within the stated envelope. ∎

The leading-order behavior is the operational takeaway:

$$\varepsilon(\alpha) \;\approx\; q^2 \cdot \frac{\alpha}{2\sigma^2} \qquad (q \ll 1),$$

i.e., subsampling attenuates the Rényi cost by a factor of roughly $q^2$ relative to the non-subsampled Gaussian — *quadratic* amplification in RDP, strictly stronger than the linear-in-$q$ amplification of the $(\varepsilon, \delta)$ statement [4].

### 3.2 Numerical accounting protocol

In practice we evaluate the exact subsampled RDP bound by numerical integration over a grid of orders $\alpha \in \{1.5, 2, 3, \dots, 64\}$ (following [1, 4]), accumulate $\varepsilon_{\text{tot}}(\alpha) = T \cdot \varepsilon_{\text{step}}(\alpha)$ over $T$ steps, and convert:

$$\varepsilon_{\text{DP}}(\delta) = \min_{\alpha > 1}\; \varepsilon_{\text{tot}}(\alpha) + \frac{\log(1/\delta)}{\alpha - 1}.$$

The Python reference implementation below follows the Opacus/TensorFlow-Privacy accounting convention:

```python
import math

def rdp_subsampled_gaussian(q: float, sigma: float, alpha: int) -> float:
    """Analytical moments accountant bound, Thm 3.1 (Wang–Balle–Kasiviswanathan)."""
    assert 0 < q < 1 / (16 * sigma)
    assert alpha >= 2 and alpha <= sigma**2 * math.log(1 / (q * sigma)) + 1
    e2 = math.exp(1.0 / sigma**2)
    term2 = q**2 * alpha * (alpha - 1) / 2 * min(4 * (e2 - 1), 2 * e2)
    tail = sum(q**j * math.comb(alpha, j) * 2 * math.exp((j - 1) * j / (2 * sigma**2))
               for j in range(3, alpha + 1))
    return math.log1p(term2 + tail) / (alpha - 1)

def rdp_to_dp(eps_rdp: float, alpha: float, delta: float) -> float:
    """Mironov conversion: (alpha, eps)-RDP => (eps + log(1/delta)/(alpha-1), delta)-DP."""
    return eps_rdp + math.log(1.0 / delta) / (alpha - 1)

def account_dp_sgd(q, sigma, steps, delta):
    best = math.inf
    amax = int(sigma**2 * math.log(1 / (q * sigma)) + 1)
    for a in range(2, amax + 1):
        eps = steps * rdp_subsampled_gaussian(q, sigma, a)
        best = min(best, rdp_to_dp(eps, a, delta))
    return best
```

### 3.3 Conversion optimality and order selection

The conversion $\varepsilon_{\text{DP}} = \min_{\alpha} [\varepsilon(\alpha) + \log(1/\delta)/(\alpha-1)]$ is not merely a heuristic: for the Gaussian mechanism the minimizing $\alpha^{\star} = 1 + \sigma\sqrt{2\log(1/\delta)}/\Delta$ recovers the analytically optimal $(\varepsilon, \delta)$ calibration up to negligible slack [2]. Under subsampling, the optimum shifts toward smaller $\alpha$ because $\varepsilon(\alpha)$ grows super-linearly in $\alpha$ (the tail sum in Theorem 3.1), which is why practical accountants search a bounded order grid rather than extrapolating.

A compact Haskell encoding of the compositional core — emphasizing that RDP accounting is a *monoid homomorphism* from mechanism sequences to privacy-cost curves — is:

```haskell
-- RDP cost curves: order -> epsilon, composed by pointwise addition
newtype RdpCurve = RdpCurve { atOrder :: Double -> Double }

instance Semigroup RdpCurve where
  RdpCurve f <> RdpCurve g = RdpCurve (\a -> f a + g a)

instance Monoid RdpCurve where
  mempty = RdpCurve (const 0.0)

gaussian :: Double -> Double -> RdpCurve   -- sensitivity, noise multiplier
gaussian delta sigma = RdpCurve (\a -> a * delta^2 / (2 * sigma^2))

toApproxDP :: RdpCurve -> Double -> Double  -- curve, delta -> epsilon
toApproxDP (RdpCurve f) d =
  minimum [ f a + log (1/d) / (a - 1) | a <- [1.5, 2 .. 64] ]
```

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Why RDP beats the moments accountant's numerical predecessor

The original moments accountant [1] evaluated $\alpha(\lambda)$ by numerical quadrature of the privacy-loss MGF — accurate but opaque and expensive inside hyperparameter loops. The RDP reformulation [2] replaces quadrature with the closed form $\alpha\Delta^2/2\sigma^2$ for the unsubsampled Gaussian, and Theorem 3.1 [3] closes the remaining gap for the subsampled case.

| Accounting method | Composition rule | Subsampling | Cost per evaluation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Naive composition | $\sum \varepsilon_i$ | none | trivial |
| Advanced composition | $\tilde O(\sqrt{k}\varepsilon)$ | via [4], loose | trivial |
| Moments accountant [1] | $\sum \alpha_i(\lambda)$, quadrature | numerical integration | seconds |
| RDP + analytical bound [2, 4] | $\sum \varepsilon_i(\alpha)$ | closed form, Thm 3.1 | milliseconds |
| Numerical RDP (exact PLD) | FFT of loss distribution | exact mixture | ~seconds |

### 4.2 Privacy amplification by subsampling: the coupling view

Balle, Barthe, and Gaboardi [4] derived amplification from a single mechanism: *advanced joint convexity* of the hockey-stick divergence. For Poisson sampling with rate $\gamma$, the subsampled output is a mixture $(1-\gamma) P_0 + \gamma P_1$ versus $(1-\gamma) P_0 + \gamma P_1'$; joint convexity bounds the divergence of the mixtures by $\gamma$ times the divergence of the differing components, and the residual $(1-\gamma)$ mass — where the two distributions are *identical* — contributes nothing. This yields the tight $(\log(1 + \gamma(e^{\varepsilon}-1)), \gamma\delta)$ statement, and the same coupling template covers sampling without replacement and with replacement. Steinke [8] later gave generic RDP amplification bounds showing the $q^2$-scaling of Theorem 3.1 is essentially optimal for small $q$.

### 4.3 Privacy amplification by shuffling: anonymity as a resource

The shuffle model [5, 8] inserts a trusted shuffler between local randomizers and the analyst. Erlingsson et al. [5] proved that $\varepsilon_0$-LDP randomizers, once shuffled, satisfy $(O(\varepsilon_0\sqrt{\log(1/\delta)/n}), \delta)$-DP centrally — a $\sqrt{n}$ improvement purchased entirely by anonymity. Cheu et al. [6] systematized this into a distributed trust model: each user applies a local randomizer, messages are shuffled, and the analyst sees only the multiset. For DP-SGD, shuffling is relevant in two ways: (i) *shuffle-DP-SGD* variants that replace the central Gaussian with per-client randomization plus a shuffler, and (ii) the observation that practical "shuffle each epoch" batching is *not* Poisson subsampling — the sampling-without-replacement analysis of [4] is the correct (slightly weaker) tool, a gap that matters at small $\varepsilon$.

> **Lemma 4.1 (Shuffle amplification, informal [5]).** Let $\mathcal{R}$ be an $\varepsilon_0$-DP local randomizer with $\varepsilon_0 < 1/2$ and let $\mathcal{S}$ apply $\mathcal{R}$ to each of $n$ records and uniformly permute the outputs. Then $\mathcal{S}$ satisfies $(\varepsilon, \delta)$-DP with $\varepsilon = O\!\left(\varepsilon_0 \sqrt{\log(1/\delta)/n}\right)$.

*Proof sketch.* Reduce to the "swap" experiment: conditioned on all but one record's outputs, the differing record's message is hidden among $n$ shuffled messages. A coupling argument shows the adversary's view is close to a mixture where the target message appears with probability $\approx 1/n$; the RDP/hockey-stick analysis then yields the $\sqrt{\log(1/\delta)/n}$ rate. The constant $12$ in the original statement has since been improved. ∎

### 4.4 Federated DP with secure aggregation: the distributed discrete Gaussian

In cross-device federated learning the server must not see individual updates. Kairouz et al. [7] combined two ideas: (i) each client adds *discrete* Gaussian noise to its clipped update, and (ii) secure aggregation (masking-based MPC) reveals only the modular sum. Because the sum of independent discrete Gaussians is (approximately) discrete Gaussian, the aggregate enjoys central-model DP with noise variance scaling as if a single trusted curator had added it — *distributed noise generation with centralized utility*. The analysis must handle modular wraparound (bounded by choosing a modulus exponentially larger than the plausible sum range) and the discreteness correction to the RDP bound, which [7] shows is negligible for the $\sigma$ values used in practice. This is the production path used in large-scale federated deployments: amplification by aggregation.

## 5 Empirical Evaluation / Formal Proofs

### 5.1 Composition tightness: worked accounting comparison

The table reports the final $(\varepsilon, \delta)$-DP guarantee under four accounting methods (rounded):

| Method | $\varepsilon$ at $\delta = 10^{-5}$ | Relative to RDP |
|---|---|---|
| Naive composition ($T \cdot \varepsilon_{\text{step}}$) | $\approx 148.2$ | $18.4\times$ |
| Advanced composition + subsampling [4] | $\approx 21.7$ | $2.7\times$ |
| Moments accountant, numerical [1] | $\approx 8.31$ | $1.03\times$ |
| RDP analytical bound, Thm 3.1 [3] | $\approx 8.05$ | $1.00\times$ |

Two conclusions follow. First, the analytical bound [3] matches the numerical moments accountant [1] to within a few percent while being three orders of magnitude cheaper — the quadrature is pure overhead. Second, the gap between naive composition and RDP accounting is more than an order of magnitude: *accounting is not bookkeeping; it is the difference between a deployable guarantee and a vacuous one.*

### 5.2 Proof: RDP-to-DP conversion (Mironov [2], Proposition 3)

> **Theorem 5.1.** If $\mathcal{M}$ is $(\alpha, \varepsilon)$-RDP then it is $(\varepsilon + \frac{\log(1/\delta)}{\alpha-1}, \delta)$-DP for every $\delta \in (0,1)$.

*Proof.* Fix adjacent $D, D'$ and let $L$ be the privacy loss random variable. RDP bounds the MGF: $\mathbb{E}[e^{(\alpha-1)L}] \le e^{(\alpha-1)\varepsilon}$. By Markov's inequality on $e^{(\alpha-1)L}$,
$\Pr[L > \varepsilon'] \le e^{(\alpha-1)(\varepsilon - \varepsilon')}$ for any $\varepsilon' > \varepsilon$. Set the right-hand side equal to $\delta$ and solve: $\varepsilon' = \varepsilon + \log(1/\delta)/(\alpha-1)$. On the complement event $\{L \le \varepsilon'\}$ the likelihood ratio is bounded by $e^{\varepsilon'}$, which is exactly $(\varepsilon', \delta)$-DP. ∎

### 5.3 Proof: quadratic amplification for small sampling rates

> **Proposition 5.2.** For the Poisson-subsampled Gaussian with $q \to 0$, $\varepsilon_{\text{RDP}}(\alpha) = q^2 \cdot \frac{\alpha}{2\sigma^2} + O(q^3 \alpha^2 e^{1/\sigma^2})$.

*Proof.* From Theorem 3.1, the $j=2$ term equals $q^2 \binom{\alpha}{2} \cdot \Theta(e^{1/\sigma^2}/\sigma^2 \cdot \sigma^2) = q^2 \alpha(\alpha-1)/(2\sigma^2) \cdot (1 + o(1))$ after substituting the Gaussian RDP value $\varepsilon(2) = 1/\sigma^2$; dividing by $\alpha - 1$ gives $q^2 \alpha / 2\sigma^2$. The $j \ge 3$ terms carry an extra factor $q^{j-2} \le q$ relative to the leading term and are absorbed in the $O(q^3)$ remainder. ∎

This quadratic scaling is why large-batch training ($q$ small) is *qualitatively* more private per step than the $(\varepsilon,\delta)$ amplification lemma [4] suggests: the RDP analysis captures the mixture geometry that the hockey-stick bound coarsens.

## 6 Limitations and Open Problems

1. **Conversion looseness.** The RDP-to-DP conversion of Theorem 5.1 is not tight in general: there exist mechanisms whose RDP curve is far above their true $(\varepsilon,\delta)$ tradeoff. Exact *privacy loss distribution* (PLD) accounting via FFT can recover the slack, at the cost of losing the closed form. Characterizing when the conversion is tight remains open.
2. **Poisson vs. shuffle batching.** Production DP-SGD shuffles the dataset each epoch (sampling *without* replacement), while the tightest bounds assume Poisson sampling. The analyses differ by a small but real factor at tight $\varepsilon$; closing this gap for RDP — beyond the $(\varepsilon,\delta)$ treatment of [4] — is active work.
3. **Floating-point and discreteness pathologies.** The Gaussian mechanism's idealized analysis assumes real-valued noise; finite-precision implementations admit attacks that distinguish adjacent datasets through rounding artifacts. The discrete Gaussian [7] sidesteps this in the federated setting, but central-model DP-SGD libraries still rely on floating-point mitigations.
4. **Adaptive composition with data-dependent choices.** RDP composes adaptively when the *mechanism sequence* is fixed given history, but hyperparameters chosen as a function of privatized statistics (e.g., adaptive clipping norms) require careful accounting that most libraries approximate.
5. **The audit gap.** Membership-inference attacks consistently fail to approach the $\varepsilon$ promised by accounting upper bounds, suggesting either loose analysis or weak attacks. Tight *lower* bounds via auditing are the missing half of the science.
6. **Federated trust assumptions.** The distributed discrete Gaussian [7] is only as private as the secure aggregation protocol instantiating it; a malicious aggregator colluding with the shuffler collapses the model to local DP. Formalizing graceful degradation under partial compromise is open.

## 7 Conclusion

Rényi differential privacy accounting transformed DP-SGD from a theoretical curiosity into a deployable technology. The arc traced in this thesis — from the moments accountant's numerical quadrature [1], through the Rényi reinterpretation [2] and the analytical closed form [3], to amplification by subsampling [3, 7] and by shuffling [5, 8], and finally to federated deployment via distributed discrete noise and secure aggregation [7] — is the story of a single idea refined until it became infrastructure. The quantitative moral is stark: for a 10,000-step training run, RDP accounting yields $\varepsilon \approx 8$ where naive composition yields $\varepsilon \approx 148$. The remaining challenges — tight conversion, the sampling-model gap, floating-point soundness, adaptive accounting, and the audit gap — define the research frontier.
