# Neural Radiance Fields and 3D Gaussian Splatting: Volumetric Rendering Equations and Differentiable Rasterization

## Abstract

Neural Radiance Fields (NeRF) [1] and 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) [5] are the two dominant paradigms for photorealistic novel view synthesis from posed photographs. NeRF represents a scene as a continuous five-dimensional function that maps a spatial coordinate and a viewing direction to emitted radiance and volume density, implemented by a multilayer perceptron, and renders pixels by numerically integrating the classical volume rendering equation along camera rays. 3D Gaussian Splatting replaces the implicit network with an explicit set of anisotropic 3D Gaussian primitives colored by spherical harmonics, and replaces ray marching with a differentiable tile-based rasterizer that depth-sorts and alpha-composites splats in screen space. This thesis derives both formulations from first principles: the Beer–Lambert transmittance model and its quadrature approximation, the spectral-bias analysis that motivates sinusoidal positional encoding [2], the Jacobian projection of 3D covariances to 2D screen space, and the exact gradient flow through front-to-back compositing. We compare asymptotic complexity, report quantitative benchmarks on the Mip-NeRF360 dataset [3], and characterize the failure modes of each approach, including aliasing, unbounded memory growth, and view-extrapolation artifacts.

---

## 1. Introduction

Novel view synthesis — rendering a scene from camera viewpoints never observed during capture — is one of the oldest ambitions of computer vision. Classical image-based rendering attacked it with light fields, layered depth images, and multiplane images: discrete, memory-hungry structures that interpolated between photographs but struggled with view-dependent effects such as specular highlights. In 2020, Mildenhall et al. showed that a scene could be represented as a *continuous volumetric function* approximated by a single multilayer perceptron (MLP), optimized end-to-end by nothing more than the photometric error between rendered and observed pixels [1].

Two properties made NeRF a landmark. First, its rendering step — numerical integration of the classical volume rendering equation along camera rays — is analytically differentiable, so gradients flow from pixel error back into the weights of the scene function. Second, its cost was punishing: rendering one pixel required hundreds of MLP evaluations, and training a single scene consumed one to two days on a high-end GPU.

In 2023, Kerbl et al. took a different path [5]. 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) discards the neural network entirely, representing the scene as an explicit set of anisotropic 3D Gaussian primitives initialized from structure-from-motion points, and discards ray marching in favor of a differentiable tile-based rasterizer. The result matches or exceeds NeRF-class quality while rendering at hundreds of frames per second.

This thesis derives both paradigms from first principles. We begin with the emission–absorption model of radiative transfer and its quadrature approximation, analyze the spectral bias that makes positional encoding necessary, derive the covariance projection that maps 3D Gaussians to screen space, and compute the exact gradient flow through front-to-back alpha compositing.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 Classical volume rendering

Long before neural networks entered the picture, graphics had settled the mathematics of participating media. Kajiya and Von Herzen's 1984 treatment of the rendering equation for volumes models light transport through a medium by two competing processes: *emission* of radiance by the medium itself and *absorption* (extinction) along the path. Neglecting scattering, the radiance observed along a ray r(t) = o + td over the interval [t_n, t_f] obeys

C(r) = ∫_{t_n}^{t_f} T(t) σ(r(t)) c(r(t), d) dt,

where σ is the volume density (extinction coefficient), c is the emitted color, d is the ray direction, and T(t) = exp(−∫_{t_n}^{t} σ(r(s)) ds) is the *transmittance* — the probability that a photon emitted at t reaches the camera unabsorbed. This is the Beer–Lambert law in integral form, and it is the equation both NeRF and 3DGS discretize.

For real-time graphics, the integral was traditionally approximated by front-to-back compositing of discrete slabs, an idea whose lineage runs through Westover's splatting and Zwicker's elliptical weighted average (EWA) splatting. 3DGS is, in a precise sense, the differentiable, learned descendant of EWA splatting.

### 2.2 Implicit neural scene representations

NeRF sits in a short lineage of learned scene functions. DeepVoxels learned persistent 3D voxel features; Scene Representation Networks (SRNs) learned a continuous mapping from 3D points to features decoded by a ray marcher. NeRF's decisive simplification was to learn *radiance directly*: the network outputs color and density, and classical volume rendering does the rest. No 3D supervision is required; multi-view consistency emerges from the photometric loss. The network F_θ : (x, d) ↦ (c, σ) is therefore a learned plenoptic function, and training is the inversion of the rendering operator: given images, recover the volumetric function whose renderings reproduce them.

## 3. Methodology

### 3.1 NeRF: the continuous formulation

NeRF models the scene as F_θ(x, d) = (c, σ), where x ∈ ℝ³ is position, d ∈ 𝕊² is viewing direction, c ∈ ℝ³ is RGB radiance, and σ ∈ ℝ⁺ is volume density. The network is an MLP with eight fully connected layers of 256 units and ReLU activations, with a skip connection concatenating the input to the fifth layer's activations [1]. Density σ is predicted from position alone, while color c is predicted from a 256-dimensional feature *together with* the viewing direction — enforcing view-independent geometry with view-dependent appearance.

Rendering a pixel proceeds in four steps:

1. **Ray casting.** For camera center o and pixel direction d, define r(t) = o + td for t ∈ [t_n, t_f].
2. **Stratified sampling.** Partition [t_n, t_f] into N evenly spaced bins and draw one uniform sample per bin, t_i ∼ U[t_n + (i−1)δ, t_n + iδ].
3. **Network evaluation.** Evaluate F_θ at each (r(t_i), d) to obtain (c_i, σ_i).
4. **Quadrature.** Approximate the rendering integral by Ĉ(r) = Σ_{i=1}^{N} T_i α_i c_i, with α_i = 1 − exp(−σ_i δ_i) and T_i = exp(−Σ_{j<i} σ_j δ_j).

Training minimizes the squared photometric error Σ_r ‖Ĉ(r) − C_gt(r)‖² over random ray batches (4096 rays per iteration), using Adam with learning rate decaying exponentially from 5×10⁻⁴ to 5×10⁻⁵.

### 3.2 3DGS: the explicit formulation

3D Gaussian Splatting represents the scene as a set of N anisotropic 3D Gaussians. Each primitive i stores a mean μ_i ∈ ℝ³, a covariance Σ_i ∈ ℝ³ˣ³, an opacity o_i ∈ [0, 1], and view-dependent color as spherical harmonic (SH) coefficients. Its spatial influence is G_i(x) = exp(−½ (x − μ_i)ᵀ Σ_i⁻¹ (x − μ_i)).

To keep Σ_i positive semi-definite under gradient descent, it is never stored directly but factorized as Σ = R S Sᵀ Rᵀ, where R is a rotation matrix (stored as a normalized quaternion) and S = diag(s) is a diagonal scale matrix with s = exp(ŝ) enforced positive via the exponential.

Rendering projects each Gaussian to screen space. Given world-to-camera matrix W and the Jacobian J of the projective mapping at μ, the projected 2D covariance is Σ' = J W Σ Wᵀ Jᵀ [5]. The rasterizer then processes 16×16-pixel tiles: Gaussians intersecting a tile are depth-sorted by a GPU radix sort, and each pixel blends the sorted splats front-to-back:

C = Σ_i c_i α_i Π_{j<i} (1 − α_j),

where α_i = o_i · G'_i(p) is the opacity modulated by the projected 2D Gaussian evaluated at pixel p, and c_i is the SH color evaluated for the viewing direction. Both the projection and the compositing are differentiable, so the entire pipeline — means, covariances, opacities, SH coefficients — is optimized by gradient descent on L = (1 − λ) L_1 + λ L_D-SSIM with λ = 0.2 [5].

Interleaved with optimization is *adaptive density control*: every 100 iterations, low-opacity or oversized Gaussians are pruned; Gaussians with large view-space positional gradients are cloned if small (under-reconstruction) or split into two smaller Gaussians if large (over-reconstruction). This grows the representation exactly where the photometric error demands detail.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 The volume rendering equation and its quadrature

We now derive the discrete compositing rule from the continuous model. Differentiating the transmittance definition gives dT/dt = −σ(t) T(t) with T(t_n) = 1, the differential form of Beer–Lambert absorption. Assume σ and c are piecewise constant on intervals [t_i, t_{i+1}] with widths δ_i. On interval i, transmittance decays as exp(−σ_i δ_i), so the fraction of light *absorbed and re-emitted* within the interval is α_i = 1 − exp(−σ_i δ_i). Light emitted in interval i is attenuated by all preceding intervals, giving the accumulated transmittance T_i = Π_{j<i} (1 − α_j) = exp(−Σ_{j<i} σ_j δ_j). The discrete rendering equation follows: Ĉ = Σ_i T_i α_i c_i. The reference implementation below makes the gradient path explicit — every operation is a differentiable tensor op, which is why the photometric loss can supervise geometry it never observes directly:

```python
import torch

def volume_render(sigmas, colors, deltas):
    # sigmas: (N,) densities, colors: (N,3), deltas: (N,) interval widths
    alphas = 1.0 - torch.exp(-sigmas * deltas)          # (N,)
    shifted = torch.cat([torch.zeros(1, device=sigmas.device),
                         sigmas[:-1] * deltas[:-1]])
    trans = torch.exp(-torch.cumsum(shifted, dim=0))    # (N,) transmittance
    weights = trans * alphas                            # (N,) compositing weights
    rgb = torch.sum(weights[:, None] * colors, dim=0)   # (3,)
    depth = torch.sum(weights * torch.cumsum(deltas, dim=0))
    return rgb, weights, depth
```

### 4.2 Positional encoding: taming spectral bias

A plain MLP fed with raw coordinates (x, y, z) fails catastrophically at representing fine detail. The reason is *spectral bias*: neural networks with standard initializations learn low-frequency functions exponentially faster than high-frequency ones, a fact formalized through the neural tangent kernel [2]. NeRF's remedy is to lift the input into a high-dimensional Fourier feature space before the MLP:

γ(p) = (sin(2⁰πp), cos(2⁰πp), …, sin(2^{L−1}πp), cos(2^{L−1}πp)).

NeRF uses L = 10 for position (mapping 3 → 60 dimensions) and L = 4 for direction. Tancik et al. showed this is a principled kernel-shaping operation: the encoding defines a stationary kernel whose bandwidth is controlled by the frequency spectrum, trading off underfitting against high-frequency noise [2].

```python
import torch

def positional_encoding(p, L):
    # p: (..., D) coordinates normalized to [-1, 1]
    freqs = 2.0 ** torch.arange(L, device=p.device) * torch.pi  # (L,)
    angles = p[..., None] * freqs                                # (..., D, L)
    return torch.cat([torch.sin(angles), torch.cos(angles)], dim=-1)
```

### 4.3 Hierarchical sampling and coarse-to-fine density estimation

Uniform stratified sampling wastes most network evaluations in empty space. NeRF's hierarchical procedure trains two networks: a *coarse* network evaluated at N_c = 64 samples produces weights w_i = T_i α_i, which (normalized) form a piecewise-constant probability density along the ray; *fine* samples (N_f = 128) are then drawn by inverse transform sampling from this distribution, concentrating evaluations near surfaces. The final color uses all N_c + N_f samples — importance sampling where the proposal distribution is learned jointly with the integrand. Mip-NeRF [3] later replaced rays with cones and integrated positional encodings over conical frustums, eliminating aliasing at the cost of more elaborate encoding math.

### 4.4 From rays to splats: anisotropic Gaussians and EWA projection

3DGS abandons the network but keeps the compositing equation. Its central mathematical object is the projected covariance. A 3D Gaussian viewed through a projective camera does not project to a Gaussian in closed form, but Zwicker et al. showed that a local affine approximation — the Jacobian J of the projective map at the Gaussian's center — yields an excellent approximation known as EWA splatting. With W the viewing transformation,

Σ' = J W Σ Wᵀ Jᵀ,

and dropping the third row and column gives the 2×2 screen-space covariance. View-dependent color uses real spherical harmonics to degree 3 (16 coefficients per channel, 48 floats per Gaussian):

c(d) = Σ_{l=0}^{3} Σ_{m=−l}^{l} k_l^m Y_l^m(d),

where the SH basis functions Y_l^m are evaluated for the view direction d. Degree 3 captures low-frequency specular lobes — glossy highlights — while remaining compact.

```python
import torch

def covariance_from_scale_rot(scales, quats):
    # scales: (N,3) log-space, quats: (N,4) unnormalized quaternions
    s = torch.exp(scales)
    q = quats / quats.norm(dim=-1, keepdim=True)
    w, x, y, z = q.unbind(-1)
    R = torch.stack([
        1-2*(y*y+z*z),   2*(x*y-z*w),   2*(x*z+y*w),
          2*(x*y+z*w), 1-2*(x*x+z*z),   2*(y*z-x*w),
          2*(x*z-y*w),   2*(y*z+x*w), 1-2*(x*x+y*y)], dim=-1).reshape(-1,3,3)
    S = torch.diag_embed(s)
    return R @ S @ S.transpose(-1,-2) @ R.transpose(-1,-2)  # (N,3,3) PSD
```

### 4.5 Differentiable tile-based rasterization and adaptive density control

The rasterizer is where 3DGS earns its speed. After frustum culling, each Gaussian is assigned to the 16×16-pixel tiles its projected 2σ ellipse overlaps; (tile_id, depth) keys are sorted once with a GPU radix sort, producing per-tile lists ordered front-to-back. Each tile is then processed by a CUDA block that walks its list, accumulating color with the same compositing equation as NeRF's quadrature — but now the "samples" are explicit primitives rather than ray-march points, and each pixel touches only the Gaussians overlapping its tile.

> **Theorem:** *Gradient flow through front-to-back compositing.* Let C = Σ_i c_i α_i Π_{j<i}(1 − α_j). Then ∂C/∂α_i = (c_i − B_i) Π_{j<i}(1 − α_j), where B_i is the background color accumulated behind splat i, normalized by (1 − α_i). Occluded splats are automatically starved of gradient, and the backward pass has the same per-pixel complexity as the forward pass.

Adaptive density control closes the loop. Under-reconstructed regions (small Gaussians with large view-space positional gradients) are *cloned*: the Gaussian is duplicated and shifted along the gradient. Over-reconstructed regions (large Gaussians with large gradients) are *split* into two smaller ones with scale divided by 1.6. Gaussians with opacity below ε_α or excessive world-space extent are *pruned*. Densification runs every 100 iterations until iteration 15,000, after which the set is frozen and only parameters refine.

## 5. Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 Benchmark protocol

The Mip-NeRF360 dataset [3] is the standard benchmark: nine real scenes (five outdoor, four indoor) with every eighth image held out for testing. Metrics are PSNR, SSIM, and LPIPS. The table below reports approximate mean PSNR values as published in Kerbl et al. [5]:

| Method | Representation | Mean PSNR (dB) | Training time | Render speed |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| NeRF [1] | MLP, 5D plenoptic | ≈ 27.1 | 1–2 days | ~0.05 FPS |
| Mip-NeRF360 [3] | MLP, cone-traced | ≈ 29.2 | ~2 days | non-real-time |
| Instant-NGP [4] | Hash grid + tiny MLP | ≈ 26.4 | ~5 min | ~10 FPS |
| Plenoxels [6] | Sparse voxel SH grid | ≈ 23.6 | ~12 min | ~15 FPS |
| 3DGS, 7k iters [5] | 3D Gaussians + rasterizer | ≈ 25.6 | ~6 min | 100+ FPS @1080p |
| 3DGS, 30k iters [5] | 3D Gaussians + rasterizer | ≈ 27.2 | ~40–51 min | 100+ FPS @1080p |

Three conclusions follow. First, 3DGS at 30k iterations slightly exceeds Mip-NeRF360 — the previous quality champion — while training in under an hour instead of days and rendering three orders of magnitude faster. Second, even the 7k-iteration checkpoint surpasses Plenoxels, demonstrating rapid convergence to a useful solution. Third, the speed is structural: tile-based rasterization touches only relevant primitives per pixel, whereas NeRF evaluates hundreds of MLP layers per ray regardless of scene content.

### 5.2 Ablations and convergence

Kerbl et al. ablate the key components [5]. Removing spherical harmonics (diffuse-only color) costs roughly 1 dB PSNR on scenes with glossy surfaces. Disabling adaptive density control — optimizing only the initial SfM points — collapses quality by several dB, proving that densification, not the rasterizer alone, carries the representation's expressive power. The opacity reset heuristic measurably reduces floater artifacts. Convergence is rapid: most of the final PSNR is reached within the first third of training, with remaining iterations refining high-frequency texture.

## 6. Limitations

Neither paradigm is a solved problem. NeRF's limitations are computational and representational: training and rendering remain far from interactive without acceleration structures; the implicit field entangles geometry, material, and lighting, making editing and relighting difficult; and extrapolation to distant viewpoints produces cloudy artifacts, since the network learns view *interpolation* rather than true 3D understanding.

3DGS trades these for a different set of weaknesses. Memory grows with scene complexity — large scenes require millions of Gaussians, each carrying 59 floats, totaling hundreds of megabytes. The discrete primitive set can produce *popping* artifacts when depth ordering flips between adjacent views. True volumetric effects (smoke, translucency) are poorly modeled by opaque-ish ellipsoids, and thin structures may be missed entirely if no SfM point seeds them. Aliasing under minification was a notable early flaw, later addressed by Mip-Splatting's low-pass filtering [7]. Finally, both methods assume static scenes with known camera poses; dynamic scenes, deformable objects, and pose-free optimization remain active research frontiers addressed by frameworks such as Nerfstudio [8].

---

## 7. Conclusion

NeRF and 3D Gaussian Splatting are two discretizations of the same continuous physics. NeRF discretizes the *integral*: it keeps the emission–absorption model exact in the limit and learns the integrand with a neural network, paying for generality with hundreds of MLP evaluations per pixel. 3DGS discretizes the *medium*: it approximates the scene with explicit Gaussian primitives and evaluates the same compositing equation in screen space, paying for speed with memory and aliasing artifacts. The mathematics that connects them — transmittance, alpha compositing, differentiable rendering — is classical; what changed is our ability to optimize through it at scale.

The trajectory from days-long training to real-time rendering in three years suggests the field is converging on a practical substrate for captured 3D content. The remaining challenges — relighting, dynamics, compactness, and true volumetric phenomena — will likely require hybridizing the two paradigms: neural fields for what should be continuous and learned, explicit primitives for what should be fast and editable. The rendering equation, indifferent to our representations, will remain the meeting point.
