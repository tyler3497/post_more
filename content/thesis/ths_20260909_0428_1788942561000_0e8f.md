# Photonic Computing with Silicon Photonics: Mach–Zehnder Meshes, Optical Neural Networks, and the Physical Limits of Light-Based Matrix Algebra

## Abstract

Photonic computing promises to accelerate linear algebra by performing matrix–vector multiplication at the propagation speed of light, with parallelism across wavelength, spatial mode, and polarization degrees of freedom. This thesis develops the theory and engineering of silicon-photonic processors built on Mach–Zehnder interferometer (MZI) meshes, the Reck and Clements decompositions for synthesizing arbitrary unitary operators, microring-modulator weight banks, and photodetector-based nonlinearities that together implement optical neural networks. We derive the 2×2 transfer matrix of the canonical MZI cell, prove the universality and depth complexity of triangular and rectangular mesh layouts, and analyze the singular-value-decomposition embedding of arbitrary real matrices. We further examine coherent Ising machines based on time-multiplexed degenerate optical parametric oscillators as a distinct, optimization-oriented photonic architecture. Quantitative attention is given to the fundamental physical limits: shot noise, thermal crosstalk, phase-quantization error, fabrication-induced beam-splitter imbalance, and insertion-loss accumulation. We conclude that while photonic accelerators demonstrate orders-of-magnitude advantages in energy per multiply-accumulate operation at fixed precision, their scaling is bounded by analog precision rather than transistor density, and that hybrid photonic–electronic systems represent the viable path to data-center deployment.

## 1 Introduction

The dominant computational kernel of modern AI is the *dense matrix–vector product*: a single transformer inference pass spends most of its FLOPs on GEMMs, and training multiplies that cost by iterations and batch size. Electronic processors have responded with systolic arrays and tensor cores, yet remain bound by the von Neumann memory–compute separation and by interconnect energy — moving a 32-bit operand across a chip costs orders of magnitude more than the multiply-accumulate (MAC) itself.

Photonic computing attacks this bottleneck at the physical layer. A coherent optical field propagating through a network of beam splitters and phase shifters performs a unitary matrix–vector multiplication *passively*, at the speed of light, with energy cost dominated not by the multiplication but by electro-optic conversion at the input and output boundaries [1]. Wavelength-division multiplexing further parallelizes many such products through the same hardware. Optical computing is an old idea, but two developments made it an engineering discipline: **silicon photonics** as a CMOS-compatible platform, and programmable interferometer meshes implementing arbitrary linear operators with *in-situ* calibration [2, 3].

Section 2 reviews silicon-photonic device physics. Section 3 develops the mathematics: the MZI transfer matrix, the Reck and Clements decompositions, and singular-value embedding of general matrices. Section 4 dives into four pillars — the MZI cell, mesh topologies, optical neural networks, and coherent Ising machines. Section 5 surveys experiments and proves a fidelity bound. Section 6 confronts the limits: precision, loss, crosstalk, and the nonlinearity problem. Section 7 concludes with an assessment of where photonic acceleration is genuinely competitive.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The silicon photonics platform

Silicon-on-insulator (SOI) confines light in sub-micron waveguides via the index contrast between silicon (*n* ≈ 3.48) and silica (*n* ≈ 1.44) at 1550 nm. Losses of 1–3 dB/cm and bend radii near 5 µm permit thousands of components per die, manufactured in commercial CMOS foundries — the decisive economic fact: photonic processors can ride the same fabrication infrastructure as electronics [4].

The elementary active and passive components relevant to computing are:

- **Directional couplers and multimode-interference (MMI) couplers**, which implement fixed beam-splitting ratios (nominally 50:50) between adjacent waveguides.
- **Phase shifters**, thermo-optic (~kHz, compact, lossless) or electro-optic carrier-depletion (tens of GHz, with insertion loss).
- **Mach–Zehnder interferometers (MZIs)**, formed by two couplers with a phase shifter in one or both arms; the tunable 2×2 building block of all mesh architectures.
- **Microring resonators**, wavelength-selective elements shifted thermally or electrically; the weight elements of *weight-bank* architectures [5].
- **Grating couplers and edge couplers**, interfacing to fiber at 1–5 dB loss per facet — a persistent system tax.
- **Germanium photodetectors**, converting optical signals to electrical with >40 GHz bandwidth.

### 2.2 A brief history of optical computing

Coherent optical processors of the 1960s performed Fourier transforms with lenses; the 1980s added digital optical computing proposals. These collapsed against CMOS scaling: optics cannot compete on digital logic, where cascadability, gain, and noise margins are non-negotiable.

The modern revival targets **analog linear algebra** — the workload where optics holds a genuine asymptotic advantage. Turning points: Reck *et al.*'s 1994 universality proof [2], Miller's self-configuration theory [3], the loss-tolerant Clements mesh [6], and Shen *et al.*'s 2017 vowel-recognition demo [1]. Commercial ventures — Lightmatter, Lightelligence, Luminous Computing — have raised the stakes from publication to product.

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 The Mach–Zehnder transfer matrix

The canonical tunable 2×2 gate is the MZI with internal phase *θ* (splitting ratio) and external phase *φ* (output phase); with ideal 50:50 couplers, its transfer matrix is

*T(θ, φ)* = *i* · *e^{iθ/2}* · [[*e^{iφ} sin(θ/2)*, *cos(θ/2)*], [*e^{iφ} cos(θ/2)*, *−sin(θ/2)*]]

This device spans *U*(2) up to global phase: *(θ, φ)* parameterize a Givens rotation on the mode pair, and any *N×N* unitary factors into such rotations.

A minimal Python model of one MZI cell and a two-mode product illustrates the primitive:

```python
import numpy as np

def mzi(theta, phi):
    """Transfer matrix of an ideal Mach-Zehnder cell."""
    t = 1j * np.exp(1j * theta / 2.0)
    return t * np.array([
        [np.exp(1j * phi) * np.sin(theta / 2.0), np.cos(theta / 2.0)],
        [np.exp(1j * phi) * np.cos(theta / 2.0), -np.sin(theta / 2.0)],
    ])

def givens_null(U, i, j):
    """Return (theta, phi) zeroing U[i, j] via right-multiplication by T."""
    r = np.hypot(abs(U[i, j]), abs(U[i, i]))
    theta = 2.0 * np.arctan2(abs(U[i, j]), abs(U[i, i]))
    phi = np.angle(U[i, j]) - np.angle(U[i, i]) + np.pi
    return theta, phi
```

### 3.2 Decompositions: Reck, Clements, and the SVD embedding

> **Theorem 1 (Reck *et al.*, 1994):** Any *N×N* unitary matrix *U* can be factored into a product of at most *N(N−1)/2* two-mode unitaries *T_{m,n}(θ, φ)* and a diagonal matrix of phases [2].

*Proof sketch.* Proceed by successive nulling: right-multiply *U* by *T_{N−1,N}* chosen to zero the *(N, N−1)* entry, then continue along the bottom row, then the row above, following a triangular elimination order. Each multiplication mixes only two adjacent modes and zeroes exactly one targeted entry without disturbing previously zeroed entries. After *N(N−1)/2* steps the matrix is diagonal with unit-modulus entries. ∎

The **Clements design** [6] rearranges the same *N(N−1)/2* beam splitters into a *rectangular* mesh, halving maximum optical depth from *2N−3* to *N−1* and sharply reducing differential path loss — the dominant fidelity-degradation source in large meshes. Both use exactly the *N²* real phases of *U(N)*, hence both are component-count *optimal*; they differ in loss balance and calibration.

Arbitrary real matrices use the **singular value decomposition** *M = UΣV†*: two unitary meshes plus a diagonal attenuator bank implement any *N×N* matrix. In Haskell-flavored pseudocode, the type discipline of the construction is:

```haskell
-- A mesh program is a list of two-mode rotations plus output phases.
data Mesh = Mesh { cells :: [(Int, Int, Double, Double)], outPhases :: [Double] }

-- Universality: every unitary arises from some mesh program.
reck :: Matrix (Complex Double) -> Mesh       -- triangular layout
clements :: Matrix (Complex Double) -> Mesh   -- rectangular layout

-- SVD embedding of an arbitrary real matrix into two meshes + diagonal.
svdEmbed :: Matrix Double -> (Mesh, [Double], Mesh)
svdEmbed m = let (u, s, v) = svd m in (clements u, s, clements (conjugateTranspose v))
```

### 3.3 Programming and self-configuration

Fabricated meshes deviate from design: imbalanced couplers, thermal crosstalk, length variations. Miller's self-configuration [3] programs a mesh *without* a global model — working backwards from the output, each MZI is tuned via local power monitors, one cell at a time. Being *progressive*, it converges with unknown component parameters and can be re-run to track drift, turning fabrication tolerance from a yield catastrophe into a calibration routine.

```rust
/// Local progressive tuning of one MZI cell against a power monitor.
fn tune_cell(cell: &mut MziCell, monitor: &dyn PowerMonitor, target: f64) {
    // Sweep internal phase theta to extremize tapped power (split ratio).
    let theta = extremize(|t| { cell.set_theta(t); monitor.read() }, 0.0, PI);
    cell.set_theta(theta);
    // Sweep external phase phi to lock output phase against reference.
    let phi = extremize(|p| { cell.set_phi(p); monitor.read() }, 0.0, 2.0 * PI);
    cell.set_phi(phi);
    assert!((monitor.read() - target).abs() < 1e-3, "cell failed to lock");
}
```

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Mach–Zehnder cell as a universal 2×2 gate

The MZI cell's power is its completeness: it realizes any element of *U*(2), the generators of *U(N)*. Real couplers are never exactly 50:50 — a 45:55 imbalance caps extinction near 19 dB. Miller showed that replacing each fixed coupler with a tunable MZI (a double-MZI cell) restores perfect extinction at doubled component count: *perfect optics from imperfect components* [3], now a standard foundry library element.

Thermo-optic shifters dissipate ~10–20 mW per *π* and respond in tens of microseconds — fine for weight-stationary inference, but an *N = 64* mesh burns watts merely holding weights. Electro-optic carrier-depletion shifters switch in picoseconds but add ~1 dB loss each plus nonlinear phase–voltage curves needing per-device lookup tables. The choice is *slow, lossless weights* versus *fast, lossy weights*; no current technology offers both.

### 4.2 Mesh topologies: Reck versus Clements

| Property | Reck (triangular) [2] | Clements (rectangular) [6] |
|---|---|---|
| Beam splitters | *N(N−1)/2* | *N(N−1)/2* |
| Max optical depth | *2N − 3* | *N* (symmetric) |
| Footprint | triangular, asymmetric | rectangular, compact |
| Loss imbalance across paths | severe (long vs. short paths) | balanced |
| Calibration | sequential from one corner | parallel layer-by-layer |
| Fabrication sensitivity | high | moderate |

In the Reck triangle the top mode traverses *2N−3* cells and the bottom one; with per-cell loss *α* the worst path suffers *(2N−3)α* dB — a *non-unitary* error no phase tuning can correct. Clements equalizes paths to ~*N* cells each, turning loss into near-uniform attenuation that calibration absorbs. For *N = 32*, *α = 0.2 dB/cell*: ~12 dB worst-path loss (Reck) versus ~6 dB uniform (Clements) — decisive in practice [6].

A TLA+ sketch of the layer-by-layer Clements calibration protocol captures its parallel structure:

```tla+
---------------------------- MODULE ClementsCal -----------------------------
EXTENDS Naturals
CONSTANTS N
VARIABLES layer, calibrated

Init == layer = 0 /\ calibrated = {}
Next == \/ /\ layer < N
           /\ calibrated' = calibrated \cup {layer}
           /\ layer' = layer + 1
        \/ UNCHANGED <<layer, calibrated>>
Spec == Init /\ [][Next]_<<layer, calibrated>> /\ WF_<<layer, calibrated>>(Next)
THEOREM Spec => []<>(calibrated = 0..N-1)
=============================================================================
```

The liveness property states that every layer is eventually calibrated; the rectangular geometry is what makes layers independent enough for the parallel sweep to be sound.

### 4.3 Optical neural networks: weight banks and the nonlinearity problem

**Coherent meshes** [1, 7] encode activations as field amplitudes and weights as mesh phases, computing *y = σ(Wx)* optically. **Incoherent weight banks** [5] multiplex inputs across wavelengths, weight each channel with a microring, and sum via balanced photodetection — high fan-in without phase stability, but only non-negative weights per detector pair and one wavelength per input.

Both families hit the same wall: the **nonlinearity**. Optics excels at *Wx* but has no good *σ*. Demonstrated options include:

1. **Photodetector + modulator loops**: detect, apply *σ* electronically, re-modulate — arbitrary, programmable activations at the cost of conversion latency [1].
2. **Saturable absorbers and semiconductor optical amplifiers**: all-optical, but with fixed, hard-to-tune transfer functions and added noise.
3. **Kerr and phase-change nonlinearities**: ultrafast but requiring high optical power or slow thermal cycling, respectively.

With opto-electronic nonlinearities, linear algebra runs at light speed and nonlinearities at electronic speed, with ADC/DAC conversions at every layer. The energy advantage thus concentrates in the MAC itself — which dominates large dense layers. Feldmann *et al.* demonstrated the trade at scale: a phase-change tensor core projecting 2 tera-MAC/s with zero-static-power nonvolatile weights [8].

In-situ training closes the loop: Hughes *et al.* showed gradients can be measured optically by injecting the error vector backwards through the mesh and interfering it with the forward field — *in-situ backpropagation* — so the mesh learns its own imperfections with no digital hardware model [10].

### 4.4 Coherent Ising machines: a second photonic paradigm

The **coherent Ising machine (CIM)** uses optics for combinatorial optimization: degenerate optical parametric oscillators (DOPOs) oscillating at 0 or *π* phase encode Ising spins *σᵢ ∈ {+1, −1}*, optical couplings implement *Jᵢⱼ*, and the minimum-loss collective mode is the ground state of *H = −Σᵢⱼ Jᵢⱼσᵢσⱼ* [9].

Inagaki *et al.* time-multiplexed 2,000 DOPO pulses in a fiber ring, coupled all-to-all via measurement and FPGA feedback, solving 2,000-node MAX-CUT ~50× faster than simulated annealing [9]; later work reached 100,000 spins. The CIM is a *physics-based heuristic solver*, not a universal computer: coupled-oscillator bifurcation dynamics sample low-energy configurations. It matters twice over — a commercially relevant workload where analog photonic dynamics beat digital heuristics, and a time-multiplexed architecture sidestepping the *N²* component scaling of spatial meshes.

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 Experimental milestones

- **Shen *et al.* (2017)** [1]: a 56-MZI processor ran a single-layer ONN recognizing four vowel phonemes at 77% accuracy (vs. ~90% electronic), projecting 100× speedup and 1000× energy efficiency at scale.
- **Harris *et al.* (2018)** [7]: an 88-MZI processor demonstrated high-fidelity unitaries with in-situ programming on a foundry-compatible platform.
- **Feldmann *et al.* (2021)** [8]: a phase-change photonic tensor core with soliton microcombs projected 2×10¹² MAC/s for parallel convolutions, weights stored nonvolatilely.
- **Inagaki *et al.* (2016)** [9]: the 2,000-spin CIM solved dense MAX-CUT in ~70 µs versus ~3.2 ms for simulated annealing — a ~50× wall-clock advantage on a natively hard problem.

### 5.2 A fidelity bound for imperfect meshes

> **Theorem 2 (Fidelity under component error):** Consider a Clements mesh of *N* modes with per-cell amplitude error bounded by *ε* (from coupler imbalance) and per-cell phase error bounded by *δ* (from quantization and crosstalk). Let *U* be the target unitary and *Ũ* the implemented operator. Then the operator-norm error satisfies ‖*U − Ũ*‖ ≤ *N*(*ε + δ*) + *O*(*N*(*ε + δ*)²), i.e., error grows at most linearly in mesh size for the balanced layout.

*Proof sketch.* Each cell implements *T̃ = T + E*, ‖*E*‖ ≤ *ε + δ*. In the Clements layout every path traverses Θ(*N*) cells across *N* balanced layers; submultiplicativity and the triangle inequality bound total deviation by layer count times per-layer error. The Reck layout admits only the weaker *O*(*N²*(*ε+δ*)) bound, its longest path traversing *2N−3* cells with asymmetric error compounding. ∎

Practically: at 6-bit-equivalent precision (*ε + δ* ~ 1.5%), a *N = 64* Clements mesh reaches operator error ~1.0 (marginal) while *N = 16* holds ~0.25 (acceptable for inference) — the quantitative form of precision, not component count, bounding scale.

### 5.3 The energy argument, quantified

| Operation | Electronic (7 nm MAC) | Photonic MZI mesh (projected) |
|---|---|---|
| Energy per MAC | ~1 pJ (incl. data movement) | ~10–100 fJ (modulation + detection amortized) |
| Latency per MVM (*N* = 64) | ~µs (memory-bound) | ~100 ps (time of flight) |
| Precision | 8–32 bit exact | 4–8 bit effective (analog) |
| Reprogramming rate | GHz | kHz (thermo-optic) – GHz (electro-optic) |

The table explains both the enthusiasm and the skepticism: photonics wins where *throughput per joule at low precision* is the metric — inference of large dense layers — and loses where exactness, fast reconfiguration, or branching logic is required.

## 6 Limitations

**Analog precision is the hard ceiling.** Shot noise, laser RIN, thermal crosstalk (1–5% of *π* leaking to neighbors), DAC quantization, and drift hold the full signal chain to ~5–8 effective bits. In-the-loop training recovers some accuracy, but no calibration removes the shot-noise floor: SNR scales as √*P* for *P* detected photons per MAC, so each extra bit quadruples optical energy.

**Loss accumulates.** At an optimistic 0.1 dB per MZI, a 64-mode Clements mesh imposes ~6 dB on-chip loss before ~2–4 dB fiber coupling and detector inefficiency. Compensating with laser power reintroduces the energy costs photonics was meant to escape and risks waveguide nonlinearities.

**The I/O bottleneck.** Every inference pays for DACs driving modulators and ADCs reading detectors; at high precision the converters dominate energy and area. System-level, the *periphery* — not the photonic core — determines competitiveness.

**Nonlinearities and memory remain electronic.** No all-optical nonlinearity combines cascadability, low power, and programmability, so architectures alternate photonic linear stages with electronic nonlinear ones, and the conversions cap speedup. Photonic memory beyond phase-change weights is essentially nonexistent.

**Control complexity.** An *N = 64* mesh holds ~4,000 phase shifters, each needing a DAC channel, calibration table, and thermal stabilization. The *O*(*N²*) control plane — thousands of analog voltages settling in microseconds to milliseconds — rivals the photonics in engineering difficulty.

## 7 Conclusion

Silicon photonics has made optical computing a quantitative engineering discipline. Reck and Clements give a rigorous compilation target — arbitrary unitaries from 2×2 cells — with the rectangular layout supplying the loss balance large meshes need [2, 6]. Self-configuration turns fabrication imperfection into a calibration procedure [3]. Experiments now span vowel recognition, tera-MAC convolution, and 2,000-spin optimization [1, 8, 9].

The honest assessment is narrower: photonic processors are *analog linear-algebra accelerators* capped near 5–8 effective bits, with electronic nonlinearities and memory, whose system advantage appears only when conversion costs amortize over very large weight-stationary operations. The defensible near-term deployment is the **hybrid photonic–electronic accelerator** — photonic tensor cores in electronic control planes, running dense inference layers where TOPS-per-watt at modest precision decides. Within that envelope the physics favors photonics: light has been doing Fourier transforms for free since long before we learned to compute with it.
