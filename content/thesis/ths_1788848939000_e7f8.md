# Single-Server Private Information Retrieval at Scale: SealPIR, OnionPIR, MulPIR, and the Communication–Computation Frontier

## Abstract

Private information retrieval (PIR) allows a client to download a record from a server-held database without revealing *which* record was requested. Single-server PIR was long a theoretical curiosity until SealPIR (Angel et al., IEEE S&P 2018) [1] combined the BFV somewhat-homomorphic cryptosystem with query compression, shrinking uploads by up to 274×, and MulPIR (Ali et al., 2019) [2] demonstrated full-recursion somewhat-homomorphic PIR with communication rate 4/9 — roughly a 440× rate improvement over SealPIR's small-entry regime. OnionPIR (Mughees, Chen, Ren, ASIACRYPT 2021) [3] then layered fully homomorphic encryption to shrink *responses*, holding the request at 64 KB from a million to a billion entries while SealPIR's climbs toward 1 MB. This thesis reconstructs the lineage — PIR definitions, BFV arithmetic, hypercube encoding, query expansion, FHE onion-folding, MulPIR's packing and recursion — surveys benchmarked throughputs (SealPIR ≈ 105 MB/s, OnionPIR ≈ 149 MB/s, MulPIR ≈ 69 MB/s, Spiral ≈ 1.5 GB/s per core [4][5]), analyzes the mandatory linear scan, and examines applications: private media streaming, contact discovery, safe browsing, and certificate transparency.

## 1 Introduction

Suppose a dissident wishes to read a single article from a news archive, a user wants to check a password against a breach database, or a messaging client must discover which contacts are registered — *without the server learning what was looked up*. This is the *private information retrieval* (PIR) problem, introduced by Chor, Kushilevitz, Goldreich, and Sudan in 1995 [6]. In single-server PIR — computational PIR, where privacy rests on cryptographic hardness rather than non-colluding replicas — the server provably must touch the *entire* database for every query [6]: otherwise it would learn that untouched regions were not of interest. PIR is therefore *intrinsically memory-bound*, and for thirty years the question was not whether the linear scan could be avoided but whether it could be made cheap enough to matter.

The lattice-based PIR renaissance began with XPIR (Aguilar-Melchor et al., 2016) [7], which showed ring-LWE homomorphic encryption could evaluate the PIR dot product at tens of megabytes per second on commodity CPUs. SealPIR [1] then attacked communication: the XPIR query was linear in the database size, and SealPIR showed how a *compressed* query could be homomorphically *expanded* server-side using automorphism tricks, achieving up to 274× query shrinkage. MulPIR [2] took recursion — Stern's and Kushilevitz–Ostrovsky's classic dimension-folding idea — and made it actually implementable with somewhat homomorphic encryption, flipping the bottleneck: tiny responses (rate 4/9) at the price of large queries (tens of megabytes). OnionPIR [3] applied *fully* homomorphic layering, peeling one FHE "onion layer" per database dimension, so that response size became independent of the entry count and request size stayed flat at 64 KB across six orders of magnitude of database growth. Spiral [5] pushed throughput to 1.5 GB/s per core with 14 KB queries, and stateful variants (SimplePIR, DoublePIR, YPIR) approach memory bandwidth itself.

This thesis is a deep technical treatment of this lineage. Section 2 fixes definitions: correctness, privacy, communication rate, server work. Section 3 develops the methodology — the BFV cryptosystem, hypercube encoding, query expansion, and FHE folding — in enough detail to reimplement. Section 4 dives into SealPIR, OnionPIR, MulPIR, and the tradeoff frontier with head-to-head numbers. Section 5 states the key theorems and empirical results. Section 6 catalogs limitations honestly. Section 7 concludes with the remaining research agenda.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The PIR problem

Let the server hold a database $DB = (DB[0], \dots, DB[N-1])$ of $N$ records, each of $\ell$ bits. A single-server PIR scheme is a triple of algorithms $(\mathsf{Query}, \mathsf{Answer}, \mathsf{Reconstruct})$ (plus parameter setup) with the following interface:

- $\mathsf{Query}(i) \to (q, st)$: on target index $i \in [N]$, the client produces a query $q$ and secret state $st$.
- $\mathsf{Answer}(DB, q) \to a$: the server computes a deterministic function of the whole database and the query.
- $\mathsf{Reconstruct}(st, a) \to DB[i]$: the client recovers the desired record.

**Correctness** requires recovery of $DB[i]$ with overwhelming probability. **Privacy** (computational) requires $\mathsf{Query}(i)$ and $\mathsf{Query}(j)$ to be indistinguishable for any $i, j$ — under RLWE in all schemes treated here.

Two cost metrics dominate:

1. **Communication**: $|q| + |a|$ bits, or the *rate* $\rho = \ell / (|q|+|a|)$ — the fraction of downloaded bits carrying payload.
2. **Server computation**: homomorphic operations per query, necessarily $\Omega(N \cdot \ell)$ in the worst case.

> **Theorem:** (Chor et al., 1995; refined by Beimel et al.) Any single-server PIR protocol with information-theoretic privacy requires $\Omega(N)$ communication. Computational PIR circumvents this bound under cryptographic assumptions, achieving $o(N)$ communication [6][8].

The communication lower bound landscape is subtle: Kushilevitz and Ostrovsky showed $n - o(n)$ is achievable with quadratic residuosity; Gentry and Ramzan achieved *optimal* rate $1 - o(1)$ under the $\Phi$-hiding assumption. The lattice schemes trade rate for post-quantum security and CPU efficiency.

### 2.2 BFV: the workhorse cryptosystem

All three schemes in this thesis build on the Brakerski/Fan–Vercauteren (BFV) somewhat-homomorphic scheme over the ring $R_q = \mathbb{Z}_q[x]/(x^n+1)$, $n$ a power of two (typically $n \in \{2048, 4096, 8192\}$), with plaintext modulus $t$ and ciphertext modulus $q$ [9]. A BFV ciphertext encrypting $m \in R_t$ is a pair $(c_0, c_1) \in R_q^2$ with $c_0 + c_1 \cdot s = \Delta \cdot m + e \pmod q$ ($\Delta = \lfloor q/t \rfloor$) for secret $s$ and small noise $e$. BFV supports **addition** (noise adds), **multiplication** (noise multiplies — expensive, needs relinearization keys), and **rotation/automorphism** $\mathsf{Rotate}(ct, k)$ permuting slots nearly for free (with Galois keys).

The *expansion factor* $F$ maps a $\zeta$-bit plaintext to an $F$-bit ciphertext; for BFV with $n = 4096$ and $\log q \approx 100$, $F$ is in the hundreds. Kushilevitz–Ostrovsky-style analysis gives communication $\approx N^{1/d} \cdot F^{d-1}$ for $d$-dimensional recursion — the quantity every scheme here minimizes.

### 2.3 The homomorphic dot product

The beating heart of lattice PIR: the client encrypts a *selection vector* $e_i$ (1 at position $i$, 0 elsewhere); the server computes $a = \sum_j \mathsf{Enc}(e_i[j]) \;\boxdot\; DB[j]$, where $\boxdot$ is the cheap plaintext–ciphertext multiplication. The sum homomorphically accumulates exactly $DB[i]$. The naïve query is $N$ ciphertexts — and *every* optimization in this thesis is a scheme for shrinking, structuring, or folding that selection vector.

```python
# Client                                  # Server
q = BFV.Enc(selection_vector(i))  # --->  a = sum(plain_mult(q[j], DB[j])
st = secret_key                   # <---      for j in range(N))  # homomorphic dot product
record = BFV.Dec(st, a)  # == DB[i]
```

### 2.4 Dimension folding (recursion)

Stern's idea, refined by Kushilevitz–Ostrovsky: arrange the $N$ records as a $d$-dimensional hypercube of side $m = N^{1/d}$. The client sends $d$ selection vectors of length $m$; the server folds dimensions one at a time, each fold multiplying ciphertext count by $F$. Communication drops from $N \cdot F$ to roughly $d \cdot m \cdot F + F^{d-1}$ response ciphertexts [8]. SealPIR uses $d = 2$; MulPIR pushes $d$ large (full recursion); OnionPIR folds with FHE so the response term stays small.

## 3 Methodology

Our methodology is reconstructive and comparative: we re-derive each protocol's core mechanism from the primary sources [1][2][3][5], normalize their reported benchmarks onto common axes (request size, response size, rate, per-core throughput), and verify parameter claims against independent surveys [4][10]. In PIR *no number is meaningful without its regime*, so for each scheme we recover the entry-size and database-size regime behind each headline figure.

Concretely, for each scheme we specify the **encoding** (hypercube dimension $d$, record packing into plaintext slots, moduli $(n, \log q, t)$), the **query** (what the client encrypts, how the server expands or folds it), and derive **costs**: upload $= f(N, d, F)$, download $= g(N, d, F, \ell)$, server work $= h(N, \ell)$ in modular multiplications — cross-checked against [4] and [10] (SealPIR ≈105 MB/s, FastPIR ≈186 MB/s, MulPIR ≈69 MB/s, OnionPIR ≈149 MB/s, Spiral ≈1516 MB/s per core).

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 SealPIR: compressed queries over the BFV hypercube

**The problem SealPIR solved.** XPIR's query contained a number of ciphertexts proportional to the database size — for a database of $2^{20}$ entries, the query alone was tens of megabytes, dwarfing the response. SealPIR (Angel, Chen, Laine, Setty, S&P 2018) [1] introduced two techniques that made computational PIR practical for the first time.

**Technique 1: query compression.** Instead of sending the full selection vector (one ciphertext per hypercube row), the client packs the *entire* selection vector into the coefficients of a *single* plaintext polynomial and encrypts it once. The server then runs an **expansion** procedure: using Galois automorphisms $x \mapsto x^{2k+1}$ on the RLWE ciphertext, it homomorphically extracts each coefficient into its own ciphertext, recovering the full selection vector. Expansion costs the server modest computation while the client uploads up to 274× fewer bytes [1]: SealPIR's request is ≈ 32–64 KB where XPIR's was megabytes.

**Technique 2: probabilistic batch codes (PBCs).** For multi-query workloads — the same client issuing many queries, as in Pung's private messaging — SealPIR encodes the database with PBCs so a batch of $b$ queries is answered with amortized server work: up to 40× speedup, and 36× network reduction with 3× throughput gain in the Pung port [1].

**Parameters and performance.** With $n = 2048$ or $4096$, SealPIR answers queries over a million-record database with ≈ 32 KB upload and ≈ 105 MB/s per-core throughput [4]. Its *rate*, however, is poor for small entries — roughly 1/1000 in the 288-byte regime [2] — because the response carries $F^{d-1}$ large ciphertexts regardless of payload size.

> **Theorem:** (Angel et al. [1], informal) Under RLWE, SealPIR's expanded query is computationally indistinguishable from the encryption of any other selection vector; the server learns nothing about the target index beyond the database size.

### 4.2 OnionPIR: response efficiency via layered FHE

**The problem OnionPIR solved.** SealPIR's *request* was small, but its *response* grew with the database: past ~4 million entries SealPIR's request climbs steeply — 64 KB at 16M entries, ~1 MB at a billion [3]. OnionPIR (Mughees, Chen, Ren, ASIACRYPT 2021) [3] attacked response size with *layered fully homomorphic encryption*.

**The onion technique.** The database is again a $d$-dimensional hypercube. The client encrypts its per-dimension selection vectors under FHE but *nests* the encryptions: the query for dimension 2 is encrypted under a key whose decryption is itself performed homomorphically inside the outer layer — like onion routing, each server-side folding step "peels" one layer. After the first dimension is folded, the server homomorphically decrypts the inner query (a bootstrapping-like step using the encrypted secret key) and proceeds to the next dimension. Because each fold's output is re-encrypted under the next layer rather than accumulating $F$ blowup per dimension, the response stays compact: its size depends on the *entry* size, not the *entry count*.

**Quantitative behavior.** For one million entries, OnionPIR's request is 64 KB — about 2× SealPIR's 32 KB — but it *remains* 64 KB up to **one billion** entries, growing only logarithmically thereafter, while SealPIR's climbs to ~1 MB [3]. OnionPIR is "response efficient": the property that matters when the client is download-constrained (mobile) but tolerates a slightly larger upload.

**Stateful PIR integration.** OnionPIR's second contribution folds in the *stateful* PIR paradigm of Patel et al.: a client that downloads a one-time hint (a function of the database) can subsequently issue much cheaper queries. This prefigures SimplePIR/DoublePIR [11], where the hint is a linear function of the database and per-query server work approaches raw memory bandwidth.

### 4.3 MulPIR: full recursion and the 4/9 rate

**The problem MulPIR solved.** Ali, Lepoint, Patel, Raykova, Schoppmann, Seth, and Yeo (2019) [2] asked what the *optimal* communication–computation tradeoff looks like, and whether recursion could be pushed to its limit. MulPIR uses somewhat homomorphic encryption "in a new way" — applying dimension-folding recursion *fully* ($d$ large) rather than stopping at $d = 2$ like SealPIR.

**Packing and compression.** MulPIR's key insight: with enough recursion depth, the response term $F^{d-1}$ — SealPIR's albatross — is driven down by packing many database bits into each response ciphertext. The Appendix A variant achieves communication **rate 4/9 ≈ 0.44**: 44% of all bits exchanged carry payload, versus SealPIR's ~1/1000 in the small-entry regime — a ~440× improvement [2]. Server work drops to ≈ 1.8 modular multiplications per database byte (with 50–60-bit $q$), *fewer* operations per byte than SealPIR's ~20 CPU cycles per byte.

**The price.** MulPIR's queries are *large* — 26–198 MB — because deep recursion needs many per-dimension selection ciphertexts, and the 4/9 rate requires **large entries (≥ 100 KB)** for packing to amortize ciphertext overhead [2]. For small-entry databases (DNS records, contact discovery), SealPIR remains the right tool; for large-entry databases (images, video chunks, documents), MulPIR dominates the response side. The paper also improves Gentry–Ramzan PIR, and gives sparse-database PIR, multi-query batching, and symmetric PIR [2].

> **Theorem:** (Ali et al. [2], informal) MulPIR with full $d$-dimensional recursion achieves communication $N^{1/d} \cdot F + o(\cdot)$-type scaling with rate approaching 4/9 for large entries, under RLWE; the Gentry–Ramzan variant retains optimal $1 - o(1)$ rate under $\Phi$-hiding with reduced computation.

### 4.4 The communication–computation frontier

The three schemes trace a Pareto frontier, not a ranking. Normalizing the benchmark data [2][4][10]:

| Scheme | Request (1M entries) | Response regime | Rate | Server throughput/core | Best entry regime |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| SealPIR [1] | ~32 KB | $F^{d-1}$ cts, grows w/ $N$ | ~1/1000 (small entries) | ~105 MB/s | Small–medium (288 B) |
| FastPIR [12] | ~32 KB | compressed response | higher than SealPIR | ~186 MB/s | Small–medium |
| MulPIR [2] | 26–198 MB | tiny (rate 4/9) | **0.44** (large entries) | ~69 MB/s | Large (≥ 100 KB) |
| OnionPIR [3] | 64 KB (flat to 1B entries) | small, $\log N$ scaling | $\log N$ comm. | ~149 MB/s | Medium–large |
| Spiral [5] | ~14 KB | small | high | ~1516 MB/s | Short records |

Three structural observations:

1. **Upload vs. download asymmetry.** SealPIR minimizes upload; MulPIR minimizes download (rate); OnionPIR keeps *both* sublinear with the flattest growth. The right choice is determined by the client's link asymmetry — a mobile client on a metered downlink wants OnionPIR or MulPIR; a client with constrained uplink wants SealPIR.
2. **Entry size is the hidden parameter.** SealPIR's rate collapses for small $\ell$ because ciphertext overhead dominates; MulPIR's rate requires large $\ell$ for packing to amortize. Spiral explicitly splits the regimes: 2.17 GB/s for short records, 13.14 GB/s for long records with higher communication [4].
3. **Computation is memory-bound, not crypto-bound.** Modern schemes (SimplePIR, YPIR, HintlessPIR) push server throughput to 83–86% of raw memory bandwidth [10] by replacing heavy homomorphic multiplications with linear operations over the database. The linear scan is fundamental; the frontier is about how little *extra* work each scanned byte costs.

### 4.5 Applications: private media streaming and contact discovery

PIR left the laboratory through two flagship applications:

**Private media streaming.** Popcorn (Gupta et al.) [13] used PIR so clients could stream video without revealing viewing habits — the large-entry regime, since multi-megabyte video chunks dwarf ciphertext overhead: an honest-but-curious CDN learns nothing about which content is popular with whom, defeating a powerful traffic-analysis vector.

**Private contact discovery.** Messaging apps must map a user's address book to registered users without uploading the address book in the clear. Signal's deployed solution uses SGX enclaves with remote attestation [14] — a trusted-hardware trust model. The PIR-based alternative (e.g., the PoPETs 2018 construction [15] using DPF-based PIR over cuckoo tables) replaces hardware trust with cryptography: the server holds a cuckoo hash table of registered identifiers, and the client PIR-queries each contact's bin. Security is then *incomparable* to SGX — no hardware trust, but the server performs $O(N)$ work per discovery and lattice parameters must be fixed before the database size is known [15]. Related deployments include **safe browsing** (private blocklist lookup), **compromised-credential checking**, **certificate transparency** monitoring, and **metadata-private messaging** [10].

## 5 Empirical Results / Proofs

**Security theorems.** All three schemes reduce query privacy to RLWE (hence post-quantum, unlike $\Phi$-hiding or DCR-based alternatives): SealPIR [1] inherits IND-CPA security of BFV, with expansion a public deterministic function of the compressed query adding no leakage; OnionPIR [3] composes layered FHE security by a hybrid argument over independently keyed layers; MulPIR [2] uses somewhat-homomorphic security with noise flooding so the response leaks nothing beyond the record.

**Benchmarked results** (per-core server throughput, from [4]): SealPIR **105 MB/s**; FastPIR **186 MB/s**; MulPIR **69 MB/s**; OnionPIR **149 MB/s**; Spiral **1516 MB/s**. SealPIR query compression: **up to 274×** vs. XPIR [1]; PBC batching: **up to 40×** amortized speedup [1]. OnionPIR request: **64 KB flat** from 1M to 1B entries vs. SealPIR's growth to ~1 MB [3]. MulPIR rate: **4/9 ≈ 0.44** vs. SealPIR ≈ 1/1000 — a **~440×** rate improvement in the large-entry regime [2].

**Scaling law.** Across all lattice schemes, per-query server work is $\Theta(N \cdot \ell / B)$ where $B$ is effective memory bandwidth; the constant ranges from ~1 (SimplePIR-class, near bandwidth) to ~20 cycles/byte (SealPIR) [2][10]. Doubling the database doubles latency — there is no sublinear server; preprocessing and client state only shift the constant.

## 6 Limitations

1. **The linear scan is mandatory.** Lower bounds [6] force the server to touch every record per query. A billion-entry database at 1.5 GB/s still costs ~10+ seconds per query per core — fine for occasional lookups, prohibitive for interactive search. Stateful hints (SimplePIR: 362 MB hint for 8 GB DB [10]) amortize this only for repeat clients.
2. **Fixed lattice parameters vs. dynamic databases.** $n$, $q$, $t$ must be chosen before the database size is known; growing past the parameter envelope requires re-encoding, and variable-length records need padding to a maximum size [15].
3. **MulPIR's query size.** 26–198 MB uploads suit fiber, not mobile uplinks — MulPIR is effectively a datacenter-to-datacenter protocol [2].
4. **Noise management.** Deep recursion (MulPIR) and homomorphic decryption (OnionPIR) consume noise budget aggressively, forcing conservative parameters and larger ciphertexts. A mis-set modulus chain breaks correctness silently — decryption yields garbage, not an error.
5. **No integrity by default.** A malicious server can return a wrong record undetected; verifiable variants add cost.
6. **Side channels.** Memory access is data-independent by construction, but NTT timing variations and traffic analysis of *when* queries arrive sit outside the formal model.

---

## 7 Conclusion

SealPIR, OnionPIR, and MulPIR are not three competitors but three answers to one question — *where should the cost of privacy go?* SealPIR put it on the server's expansion computation to shrink the client's upload; MulPIR put it on the client's upload to maximize the communication rate for large entries; OnionPIR layered FHE to flatten both curves across six orders of magnitude of database growth. Together with Spiral's throughput engineering and the stateful-PIR program now approaching memory bandwidth, they have moved single-server PIR from "prohibitively expensive" to "deployable with eyes open": private media streaming, contact discovery, safe browsing, and breach-alerting are all within reach of the current frontier.

The open problems are now systems problems as much as cryptographic ones: dynamic databases under fixed lattice parameters, malicious-server verifiability at near-zero marginal cost, and tighter post-quantum parameter selection. From XPIR's megabyte queries to Spiral's 14 KB ones, the trajectory suggests the next decade belongs to whoever makes privacy's tax invisible.
