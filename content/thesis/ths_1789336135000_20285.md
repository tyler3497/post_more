# Certified Robustness of Neural Networks: Randomized Smoothing, Interval Bound Propagation, and the Geometry of Adversarial Perturbations

## Abstract

Adversarial examples — inputs perturbed by imperceptible distortions that flip a neural network's prediction — exposed a gap between empirical robustness and provable guarantees. This thesis surveys certified defenses that close that gap with mathematical rigor. We develop the three pillars of certification: randomized smoothing, which turns any base classifier into a provably robust smoothed classifier via the Neyman–Pearson lemma and yields the tight Gaussian certificate of Cohen et al.; deterministic propagation methods, including interval bound propagation and its refinement CROWN, which propagate linear bounds layer by layer through the network; and Lipschitz-constant arguments that translate geometric margins into certified radii. We formalize the projected gradient descent attack as the empirical foil these methods must dominate, analyze the robustness–accuracy tradeoff through the lens of noise variance and feature representations, and give a self-contained Monte Carlo certification algorithm. The article concludes with the remaining frontiers: scaling verifiers to billion-parameter models and closing the gap between probabilistic and deterministic certificates.

---

## 1 Intro

Adversarial examples were first documented by Szegedy et al., who showed that imperceptible perturbations — crafted by box-constrained optimization — could force state-of-the-art networks to misclassify with high confidence [1]. The discovery upended the view of neural networks as smooth interpolators and launched a decade-long arms race between attacks and defenses. Early defenses — distillation, gradient masking, input preprocessing — were systematically dismantled by adaptive attacks, most notably the projected gradient descent (PGD) procedure of Madry et al., which frames adversarial training as robust optimization over a perturbation ball measured in an l_p norm [2]. Empirical robustness, it turned out, is a claim about the attacks tried so far, not about the attacks that exist.

Certified robustness answers a harder question: can we *prove*, for a given input, that no perturbation within some radius changes the prediction? This thesis surveys the three mathematical pillars of that program: **randomized smoothing**, which converts any classifier into one with a tight, probabilistic l_2 certificate [3]; **interval bound propagation** and its refinement **CROWN**, deterministic verifiers that propagate linear relaxations through the network [4][5]; and **Lipschitz-based** arguments that translate geometric margins into certified radii [7]. Along the way we formalize PGD as the empirical benchmark every certificate must dominate, and we confront the robustness–accuracy tradeoff that [6] showed may be intrinsic to the learning problem itself.

## 2 Background

*The adversarial formulation.* Consider a classifier f mapping inputs in R^d to a finite label set Y, trained on data drawn from a distribution D. An adversarial example for a labeled input (x, y) is a perturbation delta with norm at most epsilon such that f(x + delta) differs from y, where epsilon is small enough that the perturbed input is semantically identical to the original. The canonical attack solves the constrained maximization of the loss over the perturbation ball, and PGD — iterated projected gradient ascent with random restarts — is the strongest first-order realization of this optimization [2]. Madry et al. further proposed *adversarial training*: solving the min-max problem of minimizing expected loss under the worst-case perturbation inside the ball, with the inner maximization approximated by PGD — an approach that remains the empirical state of the art [2].

*Two kinds of guarantee.* Certified defenses differ from empirical ones in what they promise. A **deterministic certificate** states that f(x + delta) equals f(x) for *every* perturbation delta in the ball — a logical universal quantifier, verified by analyzing the network's structure [4][5]. A **probabilistic certificate** states the same conclusion with failure probability at most alpha, typically by sampling [3]. Both are strictly stronger than the statement that PGD failed to find an attack, because they quantify over all attacks, known and unknown.

*Why certification is hard.* Exact verification of ReLU networks is NP-complete in general, and the perturbation set is continuous and high-dimensional. Any tractable verifier must therefore *relax* the problem: over-approximate the set of reachable activations, as deterministic methods do, or smooth the classifier so its statistics become analyzable, as randomized methods do. Every certificate is thus a negotiation between tightness and tractability.

## 3 Methodology

*Randomized smoothing.* Given a base classifier f and noise scale sigma > 0, define the *smoothed classifier* g(x) as the argmax over classes c of the probability that f(x + delta) equals c, where delta is drawn from an isotropic Gaussian with variance sigma squared. Cohen et al. proved that if the top class c_A is returned with probability at least p_A and every other class with probability at most p_B, then g is constant within the l_2 ball of radius R = (sigma/2)(Phi^{-1}(p_A) − Phi^{-1}(p_B)), where Phi^{-1} is the standard Gaussian inverse CDF — and that this radius is *tight*: no larger radius can be certified from these probabilities alone [3]. When p_A exceeds one half, the formula simplifies to R = sigma·Phi^{-1}(p_A). The proof applies the Neyman–Pearson lemma to the hypothesis test distinguishing the Gaussian centered at x from the Gaussian centered at the perturbed point, showing the worst-case base classifier is a half-space [3]. Earlier versions of smoothing derived from differential privacy [8] gave looser, non-tight guarantees.

*Interval bound propagation.* IBP over-approximates each layer's output range with an interval — in prose, a lower and upper bound pair. For an affine layer z = Wx + b with input bounds l and u, the output bounds are l' = W_+ l + W_- u + b and u' = W_+ u + W_- l + b, where W_+ and W_- are the positive and negative parts of W; monotone activations such as ReLU propagate elementwise by clipping [4]. A robustness property holds if the lower bound of the true class's logit exceeds the upper bounds of all others. Gowal et al. made this a *training* objective, minimizing a loss that blends natural accuracy with the worst-case specification loss computed from the propagated bounds, and achieved the strongest verified accuracy of its era on MNIST [4].

*CROWN and linear relaxations.* CROWN generalizes IBP by bounding each activation with two *linear* functions rather than constants: for a ReLU with pre-activation bounds l < 0 < u, the lower bound is alpha·z for a tunable slope alpha between zero and one, and the upper bound is the chord (u/(u−l))(z − l) [5]. Propagating these linear bounds backward from the output to the input yields global linear lower and upper envelopes of the network, strictly tighter than interval bounds while remaining cheap to compute [5].

*Lipschitz certificates.* If the margin function m(x), defined as the true class score minus the best runner-up score, is L-Lipschitz, then the prediction is certifiably constant within radius m(x)/(sqrt(2)·L). Salman et al. showed that randomized smoothing itself induces Lipschitz continuity: the smoothed score functions have Lipschitz constant sqrt(2/(pi·sigma^2)), yielding a certificate directly from the margin [7]. This unifies the probabilistic and geometric viewpoints.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Why the Neyman–Pearson certificate is tight

The tightness argument is the intellectual core of [3]. Fix the class probabilities under the noise distribution centered at x. An adversary choosing a perturbed center x' wants to minimize the probability that the base classifier returns the top class under noise centered at x'. By the Neyman–Pearson lemma, among all decision regions with fixed mass p_A under the Gaussian centered at x, the one minimizing mass under the Gaussian centered at x' is a half-space — the likelihood-ratio test between the two Gaussians. The induced robustness radius is therefore *exactly* what the formula states; no analysis using only the pair (p_A, p_B) can certify more [3]. This is why the earlier differential-privacy-flavored analyses [8] left slack on the table: they bounded a quantity that was already pinned down.

> **Theorem (Tight smoothing certificate, Cohen et al. [3]).** Let g be the smoothed classifier built from base classifier f with noise scale sigma. If the probability that f returns class c_A under Gaussian noise centered at x is at least p_A, and the probability of every other class is at most p_B, then g(x + delta) = c_A for all perturbations delta with l_2 norm strictly less than (sigma/2)(Phi^{-1}(p_A) − Phi^{-1}(p_B)).

### 4.2 Certifying in practice: Monte Carlo with Clopper–Pearson bounds

The probabilities p_A and p_B are unknown, so Cohen et al. estimate them by sampling [3]. The procedure has two phases. First, *predict*: draw n0 samples (typically 100), take the majority class, and run a binomial test to confirm it is the true argmax with confidence 1 − alpha. Second, *certify*: draw n samples (typically 100,000), count agreements n_A, and compute the Clopper–Pearson lower bound p-bar_A at confidence 1 − alpha. If p-bar_A exceeds one half, return radius sigma·Phi^{-1}(p-bar_A); otherwise *abstain*. Abstention is not a bug — it is the honest output of a statistical certificate. The reference implementation is compact:

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.stats import norm, binomtest

def predict(base, x, sigma, n0=100):
    noise = np.random.randn(n0, *x.shape) * sigma
    votes = [base(x + d) for d in noise]
    classes, counts = np.unique(votes, return_counts=True)
    return classes[np.argmax(counts)]

def certify(base, x, sigma, n0=100, n=100_000, alpha=0.001):
    c_hat = predict(base, x, sigma, n0)
    noise = np.random.randn(n, *x.shape) * sigma
    nA = sum(1 for d in noise if base(x + d) == c_hat)
    lo = binomtest(nA, n, alternative="greater") \
             .proportion_confint(confidence_level=1 - alpha)[0]
    if lo > 0.5:
        return c_hat, sigma * norm.ppf(lo)
    return c_hat, 0.0  # abstain: no certificate issued
```

A subtlety: the certificate holds *with probability at least 1 − alpha over the sampling randomness*, for each certified input. Certifying an entire test set of size m therefore demands a union-bound or per-input accounting of alpha — a cost that deterministic methods avoid [3][4].

### 4.3 IBP versus CROWN: the precision–cost frontier

IBP's bounds are the cheapest possible — two matrix multiplications per layer — but they compound with depth, since each layer's interval widens. CROWN's linear envelopes track the network's geometry far more faithfully, at roughly twice the cost of a backward pass. Empirically, CROWN-based verifiers certify substantially larger radii on the same networks [5], and hybrid CROWN-IBP training narrows the train–verify gap by optimizing the same bounds used at certification time. The lesson generalizes: a verifier is only as useful as the tightness its training objective can exploit [4][5]. The following Rust sketch shows the IBP affine step — the workhorse both methods build on:

```rust
pub struct Interval { pub lo: f64, pub hi: f64 }

pub fn affine_ibp(w: &[Vec<f64>], b: &[f64], input: &[Interval]) -> Vec<Interval> {
    w.iter().zip(b.iter()).map(|(row, &bias)| {
        let (mut lo, mut hi) = (bias, bias);
        for (j, &wij) in row.iter().enumerate() {
            let (a, c) = if wij >= 0.0 {
                (input[j].lo, input[j].hi)
            } else {
                (input[j].hi, input[j].lo)
            };
            lo += wij * a;
            hi += wij * c;
        }
        Interval { lo, hi }
    }).collect()
}
```

### 4.4 The geometry of the decision boundary

Why do tiny perturbations flip predictions at all? Tsipras et al. argue the answer is *features*: standard training exploits any correlation with the label, including brittle, high-frequency patterns that humans ignore [6]. Robust models, forced to discard these non-robust features, learn representations whose gradients align with salient, human-meaningful structure — which is why adversarially trained networks yield strikingly interpretable gradient visualizations [6]. Geometrically, the l_2 certificate of [3] carves a provably pure ball around each input, while the boundary itself remains a crumpled, high-curvature hypersurface; smoothing does not flatten the boundary so much as it votes over it. This also explains a persistent empirical fact: l_infinity-certified radii lag far behind l_2 ones, because the geometry of the noise distribution and the geometry of the threat model must match — Gaussian noise buys l_2 guarantees, while l_1 or l_infinity require Laplace or uniform noise [3][8].

### 4.5 Training for certifiability

A base classifier trained on clean data performs poorly under Gaussian noise, so Cohen et al. train with Gaussian augmentation — and Salman et al. go further, showing that *adversarial training of the smoothed classifier*, attacking the expected prediction under noise rather than the base prediction, substantially raises certified accuracy [7]. Gowal et al. make the parallel point deterministically: minimizing the IBP specification loss during training produces networks whose bounds are tight enough to verify [4]. In both paradigms, the network must be *taught* to be verifiable; post-hoc certification of an arbitrary network certifies almost nothing.

## 5 Empirical/Proofs

The headline numbers anchor the theory. On ImageNet, Cohen et al. report a smoothed ResNet-50 with 49% certified top-1 accuracy under l_2 perturbations of radius 0.5 — the first certified defense demonstrated at ImageNet scale, where deterministic verifiers remain computationally infeasible [3]. On CIFAR-10, the adversarially trained smoothed classifiers of [7] dominate the certified-accuracy frontier across radii, and on MNIST the IBP-trained models of [4] reached verified robust accuracy that held the deterministic record at publication.

| Method | Guarantee | Norm | Scales to | Key cost |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Randomized smoothing [3] | Probabilistic, tight | l_2 (Gaussian) | ImageNet | ~100k forward passes per input |
| PixelDP [8] | Probabilistic, loose | l_1 / l_2 | CIFAR-scale | Similar sampling cost |
| IBP training [4] | Deterministic | l_infinity | Medium CNNs | 2x forward-pass cost |
| CROWN [5] | Deterministic, tighter | l_infinity / l_2 | Medium CNNs | Backward-bound pass |
| Lipschitz margin [7] | Deterministic | Any, via L | Small nets | Estimating L |

The table's pattern is the field's central tension: the methods that scale, like smoothing, are probabilistic and norm-restricted, while the methods that give logical certainty, like IBP and CROWN, stall at medium scale [3][4][5]. Certified accuracy curves consistently peak at intermediate noise or regularization strength: too little sigma and the radius collapses; too much and clean accuracy — the p_A that feeds the certificate — collapses with it [3][7].

## 6 Limitations

First, **norm mismatch**: real adversaries are not confined to l_2 balls, and Gaussian smoothing provably buys only l_2 guarantees (Laplace smoothing only l_1) [3][8]. Certified l_infinity robustness at scale remains largely open. Second, **the sampling tax**: one hundred thousand forward passes per certified input makes per-query certification expensive, and the union bound over a test set dilutes the confidence budget. Third, **deterministic verifiers do not scale**: IBP bounds degrade with depth, and while CROWN's relaxations are tighter, neither can touch billion-parameter models [4][5]. Fourth, **certificates assume the threat model**: they say nothing about distribution shift, backdoors, or adaptive attacks that query the smoothed classifier's sampling procedure itself. Finally, [6] warns that some of the robustness–accuracy tradeoff may be *intrinsic* rather than an artifact of current methods — a provable tension in even simple data models, suggesting certificates may always cost accuracy.

---

## 7 Conclusion

Certified robustness transformed adversarial defense from an empirical arms race into a mathematical discipline. Randomized smoothing showed that *any* classifier can be wrapped in a tight, provable l_2 guarantee [3]; interval propagation and CROWN showed that networks can be built — and trained — to carry their own proofs [4][5]; and the Lipschitz perspective unified both under geometry [7]. Yet the certificates remain narrow in norm, costly in compute, and haunted by the tradeoff of [6]. The next decade's problem is not a tighter bound on the same networks but verifiers — and training objectives — that scale to the models the world actually deploys.
