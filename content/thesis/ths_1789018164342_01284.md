# Arithmetizing the Ethereum Virtual Machine: Constraint Systems, State Proofs, and the Proving Economics of the zkEVM

## Abstract

The zero-knowledge Ethereum Virtual Machine (zkEVM) produces succinct cryptographic proofs that batches of Ethereum transactions executed correctly — every opcode, storage access, and balance update — without re-execution. This thesis gives a unified account of how the EVM is *arithmetized* into polynomial constraint systems. We analyze four production architectures — Polygon zkEVM's PIL/STARK ROM with recursive compression [2][3], Scroll's two-layer halo2-KZG system [4][5], Taiko's type-1 multi-prover design [10][11], and zkSync Era's Boojum circuits [12][13] — across opcode gadgets, state and storage proofs, Keccak-256 arithmetization, lookup arguments, and recursion with aggregation. We show that Keccak and state-proof machinery dominate proving cost, that lookups tame EVM bit-twiddling, and that recursion converts prover latency into verifier succinctness. Measured parameters — including Scroll's 116-column EVM circuit at $2^{18}$ rows per megagas — ground a cost model relating equivalence type to proving time. We conclude with the fundamental limits: the EVM was never designed for arithmetization, and that design debt is paid in constraints.

## 1. Introduction

Ethereum's execution layer is defined by the Ethereum Virtual Machine, a quasi-Turing-complete, stack-based, 256-bit-word machine specified in the Yellow Paper. Every full node re-executes every transaction; consensus *is* re-execution. Zero-knowledge rollups replace this $O(n)$ verification with a single succinct proof: a prover executes a batch of transactions off-chain and convinces Ethereum that the resulting state root is correct. The catch is that the statement being proven — *"this EVM execution trace is valid"* — must be expressed in the language of the proof system: polynomial equations over a finite field.

That translation, *arithmetization*, is the central engineering and mathematical challenge of the zkEVM. The EVM is arguably the most hostile mainstream virtual machine to arithmetize: 256-bit words in a $\sim$254-bit scalar field, pervasive bitwise operations, Keccak-256 hashing at every state access, dynamic jumps, gas metering with quadratic memory costs, and a Merkle–Patricia state trie whose every node hash must be re-derived inside the circuit. One industry estimate holds that interpreting EVM semantics inside a zkVM imposes up to an **800× overhead** on proving time relative to native execution [14] — a figure that motivated proposals to eventually replace the EVM with a proving-friendly ISA such as RISC-V [14].

This thesis makes four contributions. **First**, we systematize the constraint-system architectures of the four leading zkEVM efforts, mapping Buterin's equivalence taxonomy (Types 1–4) [1] onto arithmetization choices. **Second**, we dissect the three dominant cost centers — opcode gadgets with their stack/memory/storage buses, Merkle–Patricia state and storage proofs, and Keccak-256 arithmetization — with quantitative circuit parameters where public. **Third**, we explain the two great cost-management technologies of the field: *lookup arguments* (plookup [8], PlonKup [9], halo2 lookups [4]) and *recursive proof composition* (STARK→SNARK chains [3], two-layer aggregation [5]). **Fourth**, we assemble a proving-cost model across equivalence types and identify the fundamental limits of the approach.

> **Theorem (Thesis claim, informal).** *For any fixed equivalence type, the proving cost of a zkEVM is dominated by (i) Keccak-256 evaluations, (ii) state-trie path verification, and (iii) the per-opcode gadget overhead of the execution trace — in that order — and each is mitigated by a distinct technique: dedicated hash circuits, commitment-friendly state trees, and lookup arguments respectively.*

The remainder of this thesis proceeds as follows. §2 reviews the EVM, proof systems, and the type taxonomy. §3 describes the shared proving pipeline. §4 performs the deep technical dive. §5 presents empirical parameters and a security sketch. §6 discusses limitations, and §7 concludes.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 The EVM as a proving target

The EVM is a stack machine with a 1024-element stack of 256-bit words, volatile byte-addressable memory, persistent per-account storage ($2^{256}$ slots of 256-bit words), read-only calldata, and an append-only log journal. Roughly 140 opcodes manipulate these resources under a gas schedule: each opcode costs gas, memory expansion is quadratic in the highest touched word, and storage writes are priced to reflect state growth. Execution is *dynamic*: `JUMP` targets are computed at runtime (constrained only to valid `JUMPDEST` positions), call frames nest arbitrarily, and precompiled contracts implement cryptography (ECRECOVER, SHA-256, MODEXP, elliptic-curve pairings) natively.

Three properties make the EVM expensive to arithmetize. **Word size**: 256-bit words overflow the $\approx$254-bit BN254/BLS12-381 scalar fields, forcing limb decomposition with range checks. **Bitwise opcodes** (`AND`, `XOR`, `SHL`, `BYTE`): bitwise logic has no native arithmetic expression and must be proven bit-by-bit or via lookup tables. **Keccak-256**: the state trie, transaction trie, receipts, contract addresses, and `EXTCODEHASH` all hash with Keccak-256, whose 24-round bitwise permutation (§4.3) is the single most expensive primitive to constrain.

### 2.2 Proof systems and arithmetizations

A zkEVM ultimately compiles EVM semantics into one of three arithmetization families:

- **R1CS** (rank-1 constraint systems): constraints of the form $\langle a, w\rangle \cdot \langle b, w\rangle = \langle c, w\rangle$. Used with Groth16/PLONK backends; expressive but rigid.
- **AIR** (algebraic intermediate representation): uniform transition constraints $T(s_i, s_{i+1}) = 0$ over an execution trace — the native language of STARKs and of Polygon's PIL (Polynomial Identity Language).
- **PLONKish**: the halo2 model [4] — a rectangular matrix of *fixed*, *advice*, and *instance* columns with custom gates (arbitrary low-degree polynomial constraints per row, with rotations to neighboring rows), equality (copy) constraints, and *lookup arguments* [4]. Scroll, Taiko's circuit efforts, and zkSync's Boojum all live in this world.

Polynomial commitments split along two lines: **KZG** (pairing-based, constant-size proofs, requires a trusted setup) and **FRI** (hash-based, transparent setup, larger proofs). The choice propagates through the entire architecture: KZG proofs verify cheaply on Ethereum (pairing precompiles), while FRI-based STARK proofs are typically *recursed into* a SNARK before touching L1.

### 2.3 The equivalence taxonomy

Buterin's August 2022 taxonomy [1] classifies zkEVMs by how faithfully they reproduce Ethereum:

| Type | Definition | Representative | Proving burden |
|------|-----------|----------------|----------------|
| **Type 1** | Fully Ethereum-equivalent: identical state trees, hashes, precompiles, gas | Taiko (target) | Highest — Keccak MPT, all precompiles |
| **Type 2** | Fully EVM-equivalent, minor deviations (e.g., gas schedule tweaks) to ease proving | Scroll (target) | High — EVM semantics preserved |
| **Type 2.5** | EVM-equivalent except gas costs | — (transitional) | High |
| **Type 3** | Almost EVM-equivalent; drops the hardest corners | Polygon zkEVM (launch), Scroll (launch) | Medium — surgery on pain points |
| **Type 4** | High-level-language equivalent: compiles Solidity/Yul to a ZK-friendly ISA | zkSync Era | Lowest — new VM, new state tree |

The taxonomy is really a *cost function*: each step down buys proving efficiency by spending compatibility.

## 3. Methodology

Despite their differences, all four systems implement the same five-stage pipeline (Figure 1):

![Figure 1: The canonical zkEVM proving pipeline — witness generation, arithmetization, proof, recursion/aggregation, L1 verification.](ths_1789018164342_01284-0.webp)

1. **Witness generation (trace extraction).** An instrumented EVM executor runs the transaction batch and records an *execution trace*: every opcode step with its stack/memory/storage operands, plus auxiliary data (memory contents, trie nodes touched, Keccak preimages). Scroll's `bus-mapping` crate parses Geth debug traces into structured witness inputs for the EVM and state circuits [6]; Polygon's ROM executor interprets a JSON *ROM program* (`rom.json`) against `zkevm.pil` to emit committed and constant polynomials [2].
2. **Constraint assignment.** The trace is laid into the arithmetization's grid — AIR rows for Polygon's STARK, PLONKish rows/columns for Scroll/halo2 and zkSync/Boojum — and every gadget's polynomial identities are evaluated.
3. **Proving.** The backend (FRI-based STARK, or KZG-based halo2/PLONK) commits to the trace polynomials and produces the proof.
4. **Recursion and aggregation.** The large first-layer proof is itself verified *inside* a second circuit, compressing many batch proofs into one succinct proof (Polygon's STARK→circom→STARK chain [3]; Scroll's aggregation layer [5]; zkSync's recursion layer [13]).
5. **L1 verification.** The final SNARK is verified by an Ethereum contract against the previous and new state roots.

Our method is comparative analysis of public specifications, audit reports, and measured parameters [2][3][5][6][7], reconstructing the constraint-level design decisions and their cost implications. Where implementations are evolving, we mark claims as architectural rather than empirical.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Opcode constraint circuits: stack, memory, and storage buses

The core of every zkEVM is the **EVM circuit**: a constraint system whose satisfying assignments are exactly the valid execution traces of EVM bytecode. The standard construction decomposes execution into three *buses* — ordered logs of reads and writes: the **stack bus** (push/pop with stack-pointer bookkeeping), the **memory bus** (reads/writes with expansion-cost accounting), and the **storage bus** (`SLOAD`/`SSTORE` with cold/warm tracking per EIP-2929).

Each bus is constrained independently (e.g., "every pop matches a prior push at the same height"), and the buses are *stitched together* by the execution trace: row $i$ of the EVM circuit represents one execution state, and copy constraints (in PLONKish) or permutation arguments tie the operand cells of row $i$ to the corresponding bus entries. This separation is what makes the circuit modular: adding an opcode means adding one *execution gadget*, not rewriting the machine.

In Scroll's halo2 implementation, every gadget is an `ExecutionGadget` parameterized by an `ExecutionState`, and a `SameContextGadget` performs a lookup into a fixed `ResponsibleOpcode` table enforcing that the (state, opcode) pair is legal [6]:

```rust
// Illustrative sketch of Scroll-style execution-gadget wiring (halo2 API, simplified)
impl<F: Field> ExecutionGadget<F> for AddGadget<F> {
    const NAME: &'static str = "ADD";
    const EXECUTION_STATE: ExecutionState = ExecutionState::ADD;
    fn configure(cb: &mut ConstraintBuilder<F>) -> Self {
        let opcode = cb.query_cell();
        // (state, opcode) must appear in the fixed ResponsibleOpcode table
        cb.add_lookup("Responsible opcode lookup", Lookup::Fixed {
            tag: FixedTableTag::ResponsibleOpcode.expr(),
            values: vec![
                cb.execution_state().as_u64().expr(),
                opcode.expr(),
                0.expr(),
            ],
        });
        let a = cb.query_cell(); // stack top
        let b = cb.query_cell(); // stack second
        // ADD semantics over 256-bit words, with wraparound:
        let (sum, _) = cb.query_word32_add(a.expr(), b.expr());
        cb.stack_push(sum.expr()); // constrained push
        Self { .. }
    }
}
```

Because the scalar field cannot hold 256-bit words natively, arithmetic opcodes decompose words into limbs (e.g., eight 32-bit limbs) with **range checks** — themselves usually implemented as lookups into a $2^8$ or $2^{16}$ byte table. The Trail of Bits audits of Scroll's circuits document how subtle this is: missing range checks on `MulAdd` chip limbs were rated *critical*, since an unconstrained carry limb lets a malicious prover satisfy $a \cdot b + c = d$ for arbitrary incorrect values [6]. Under-constraining is the existential bug class of zkEVM development, and every production team has shipped instances of it.

Polygon takes a different route: the entire zkEVM is a **ROM program**. `zkevm.pil` declares the state-machine polynomials and transition identities, and `rom.json` is a fixed program the executor interprets to fill the trace [2]. Proving an opcode then means proving the ROM interpreter stepped correctly — uniform AIR constraints, at the price of interpretation overhead. Dynamic jumps, gas accounting, and exceptional halts (`REVERT`, out-of-gas) become ROM subroutines rather than bespoke gadgets.

> **Theorem (Opcode-gadget soundness, folk).** *If (i) for every execution state the active gadget enforces the opcode's Yellow-Paper transition relation on its operand cells, (ii) the stack, memory, and storage buses are internally consistent (every read matches a preceding write), and (iii) copy constraints bind each step's operands to the bus entries, then any satisfying assignment of the EVM circuit decodes to a valid EVM execution trace. Completeness follows by assigning the honest executor's trace.*

The practical cost driver is *rows per opcode*: simple stack opcodes cost a handful of rows, while `SSTORE` or `CREATE` cost orders of magnitude more. Scroll budgets **$2^{18}$ rows per megagas** for its EVM circuit [5] — the fundamental unit of zkEVM capacity planning.

### 4.2 State and storage proofs: arithmetizing the Merkle–Patricia trie


Two mitigations dominate practice. **First, separation of concerns**: the *state circuit* (or MPT circuit) is decoupled from the EVM circuit. The EVM circuit emits state accesses as bus entries; the state circuit independently proves each access against pre/post roots; a final public-input check asserts root continuity across the batch. Scroll's architecture makes this explicit with dedicated state, MPT, and *super* circuits that aggregate the sub-circuits [6].


Taiko, as a Type 1 design, cannot take this shortcut: full Ethereum equivalence means the Keccak-MPT must be proven as-is, which is precisely why Type 1 proving is the most expensive and why Taiko's pragmatic path layers SGX and zkVM (SP1/RISC0) proofs beneath the eventual pure-zk tier [10][11].

Storage proofs add a second trie descent (account → storage root → slot), doubling the hashing. The key optimization is *proof batching across the block*: trie nodes shared by many accesses are proven once, and the state circuit processes the block's accesses as a sorted batch — an idea that composes naturally with lookup-based RW tables (§4.4).

![Figure 3: Merkle–Patricia state trie with account and storage proof paths arithmetized in-circuit.](ths_1789018164342_01284-2.webp)

### 4.3 Keccak arithmetization: the dominant cost

Keccak-256 is the inescapable tax of Ethereum compatibility. The permutation Keccak-*f*[1600] operates on a $5 \times 5 \times 64$ state for 24 rounds; each round applies $\theta, \rho, \pi, \chi, \iota$, of which $\chi$ is the nonlinear step:

$$\chi:\quad a[x][y] \;\leftarrow\; a[x][y] \oplus \big(\lnot a[x{+}1][y] \land a[x{+}2][y]\big).$$

In an arithmetic circuit, every XOR/AND/NOT is a bitwise operation on 64-bit lanes. The standard arithmetization decomposes each lane into bits, evaluates $\chi$ as polynomial identities over bits, and re-composes — tens of thousands of constraints per compression, versus a few hundred for a STARK-friendly hash like Poseidon. There is no clever escape: Keccak was designed for hardware, not for fields.


```python
# Illustrative: why Keccak-chi is expensive — per-bit constraints for one lane
def chi_lane_constraints(a_bits, b_bits, c_bits):
    # a[x][y] ^= (~a[x+1][y]) & a[x+2][y], for 64 bits of lane x
    cons = []
    for i in range(64):
        # booleanity: b_i in {0,1} — one constraint per bit, or a table lookup
        cons.append(f"b{i} * (1 - b{i}) == 0")
        cons.append(f"c{i} * (1 - c{i}) == 0")
        # chi output bit: out_i = a_i + (1 - b_i) * c_i - 2*a_i*(1-b_i)*c_i
        # (XOR expressed arithmetically; degree 3 — needs custom gate or aux vars)
        cons.append(f"out{i} == a{i} + nbc{i} - 2*a{i}*nbc{i}")
    return cons  # ~192 constraints per lane per round; x25 lanes x24 rounds
```

### 4.4 Lookup arguments, recursion, and aggregation

**Lookup arguments** are the great enabling technology of modern zkEVMs. Plookup [8] proves that every value of a committed polynomial $f$ over a subgroup $H$ appears in a table $t$, via a grand-product argument over a sorted concatenation:

> **Theorem (Plookup [8], informal).** *Let $s$ be the sorted merge of the wire values $f|_H$ and the table $t$. Then $F(\beta,\gamma) = G(\beta,\gamma)$ as formal polynomials iff every wire value occurs in the table (and $s$ is correctly sorted). Three auxiliary commitments suffice.*

PlonKup [9] integrates this into PLONK; halo2 defines its own lookup argument over the same idea [4]. In zkEVMs, lookups replace what would otherwise be mountains of bit-constraints:

- **Range checks**: every limb decomposition looks up bytes in a $2^8$ table (or 16-bit chunks in $2^{16}$).
- **Fixed tables**: the `ResponsibleOpcode` table (§4.1), EIP-2929 warm/cold access-cost tables, precompile address tables.
- **RW tables**: read-write consistency of memory and storage can be enforced by sorting accesses and checking adjacent pairs — a lookup-flavored argument that avoids per-access Merkle proofs inside the EVM circuit.
- **Keccak glue**: the EVM circuit looks up (input, output) pairs in the Keccak circuit's table rather than re-proving hashes.

Scroll's EVM circuit uses **50 lookup tables** alongside its 2496 custom gates [5] — a quantitative signature of how central lookups are.

**Recursion and aggregation** solve the *verifier* side. A batch proof is enormous; verifying it directly on L1 is infeasible. Instead, the proof is verified *inside another circuit*, compressing it stepwise. **Polygon** chains STARK → `c12a` compression → `recursion1/2/f`, converting STARK→circom/R1CS (S2C) and back to STARK (C2S) until a final L1-verifiable SNARK [2][3]. **Scroll** uses two halo2-KZG layers — layer 1 with Poseidon commitments for speed, layer 2 aggregating the sub-circuit proofs with Keccak commitments for L1 verification [5]; the aggregation circuit is deliberately tiny (23 columns, 1 custom gate, $2^{25}$ rows). **zkSync/Boojum** uses base-layer circuits feeding a recursion layer, with inter-circuit communication through queue gadgets whose rolling-hash commitments prove FIFO consistency [12][13]. Aggregation also amortizes L1 gas: one verification covers an entire batch.

### 5.3 Security sketch

> **Theorem (zkEVM soundness decomposition).** *A zkEVM is sound if (a) each sub-circuit is sound for its relation (opcode semantics, trie paths, Keccak), (b) public inputs chain sub-circuits consistently (state roots, batch hashes), and (c) the recursion/aggregation layer is knowledge-sound for the inner verification relation. Under the soundness of the backend (FRI/KZG) and collision-resistance of the hashes, a verifying L1 proof implies the claimed post-state root is the correct result of executing the batch.*

The historical record validates the decomposition's fragility: Trail of Bits' Scroll audits found under-constrained gadgets (unconstrained opcodes enabling trace hijacking; missing range checks in `MulAdd`) [6], and Spearbit's Polygon review flagged missing binary enforcement on selector keys in recursion circuits [2]. In practice, zkEVM soundness is established more by audit and differential testing against Geth than by machine-checked proof — a gap the field acknowledges.

## 6. Limitations

1. **Latency vs. slot time.** Even GPU-accelerated, proving a full block takes minutes [5] against Ethereum's 12-second slots. zkEVMs finalize *asynchronously*; real-time proving remains out of reach without hardware orders of magnitude faster (the bet behind ZK ASIC programs [7]).
2. **The compatibility–cost frontier is fundamental.** Type 1 must arithmetize Keccak-MPT and every precompile; each type step down exists precisely because some Ethereum feature proved too expensive. There is no known arithmetization that makes Type 1 as cheap as Type 4 — the taxonomy [1] is a lower-bound landscape, not just engineering status.
3. **Trusted setups and upgrade risk.** KZG-based systems inherit per-circuit trusted-setup ceremonies; FRI-based systems pay in proof size and recursion complexity. Both face the same governance problem: a soundness bug in a deployed verifier is a consensus bug, and upgrading requires coordinated action.
4. **Auditability gap.** The constraint systems are among the largest ever written; complete formal verification of a production zkEVM is beyond current tooling. The audit findings in [6] suggest under-constraining will remain the dominant vulnerability class.
5. **Economic centralization.** Proving at these scales demands datacenter GPUs; the prover market centralizes even as verification stays trustless. Taiko's multi-prover tiers [10] and based sequencing are partial answers, not complete ones.

## 7. Conclusion

Arithmetizing the EVM means confronting, in polynomial form, every design decision Ethereum made without zero-knowledge proofs in mind: 256-bit words, bitwise opcodes, Keccak-256 everywhere, and a hexary Merkle–Patricia state trie. The four production architectures surveyed here — Polygon's PIL/STARK ROM with recursive compression [2][3], Scroll's two-layer halo2-KZG system [4][5], Taiko's type-1 multi-prover stack [10][11], and zkSync's Boojum circuit layers [12][13] — share one pipeline (trace → constrain → prove → recurse → verify) and one lesson: **the cost concentrates in Keccak, state proofs, and opcode dispatch, and each has its dedicated remedy** — hash circuits, commitment-friendly tries, and lookup arguments. Recursion then converts the resulting large proofs into L1-verifiable succinctness. The open problems are no longer purely cryptographic: real-time proving, formal verification of million-constraint systems, and prover decentralization will decide whether the zkEVM remains a rollup technology or becomes, as Buterin envisioned [1], the way Ethereum itself is verified.
