# Secure Multi-Party Computation at Scale: Garbled Circuits, Authenticated Preprocessing, and the Engineering of Malicious-Secure Collaboration

## Abstract

Secure multi-party computation (MPC) enables a set of mutually distrusting parties to evaluate a joint function of their private inputs while revealing nothing beyond the prescribed output. This thesis presents a systematic treatment of the protocol families that have moved MPC from theory to deployment: Yao's garbled circuits and the *half-gates* optimization, the GMW and BMR multi-party paradigms, the SPDZ line of authenticated secret-sharing protocols with MAC-based malicious security, and the MASCOT oblivious-transfer-based preprocessing machinery that makes the offline phase practical. We give precise protocol specifications, analyze asymptotic and concrete costs in rounds, communication, and symmetric-key operations, and document the engineering path from the Danish sugar-beet auction of 2008 to modern frameworks such as MP-SPDZ and SCALE-MAMBA. The central argument is that no single protocol dominates: the design space is governed by the trade-off between round complexity, the arithmetic or Boolean character of the circuit, the adversarial model, and the amortization of preprocessing. We conclude with open problems in post-quantum MPC, maliciously secure machine learning, and the standardization of deployment practice. (178 words)

## 1 Introduction

In 1982, Andrew Yao posed the *millionaires' problem*: two millionaires wish to determine who is richer without revealing their wealth to each other [1]. The problem was a cipher for something far larger — the general question of whether mutually distrusting parties can compute anything jointly without a trusted intermediary. Yao's affirmative answer, delivered through what is now called the *garbled circuit* construction, founded the field of secure two-party computation. Within five years, Goldreich, Micali, and Wigderson had generalized the paradigm to arbitrary numbers of parties via the secret-sharing-based GMW protocol, and Beaver, Micali, and Rogaway had extended Yao's circuit paradigm itself to the multi-party setting in the BMR protocol [2].

For roughly two decades, these results remained predominantly theoretical. Garbling a circuit and evaluating it demanded oblivious transfers whose cost — in the days before OT extension — was dominated by public-key operations, and the secret-sharing approach of GMW paid a round of interaction per circuit depth. The field changed character in 2003, when Ishai, Kilian, Nissim, and Petrank [2] showed that a small number of *base* oblivious transfers could be bootstrapped into an essentially unlimited number of *extended* OTs using only symmetric cryptography. With the asymptotic public-key bottleneck removed, attention shifted to protocol engineering: reducing ciphertexts per gate, batching malicious-security checks, and splitting computation into an input-independent preprocessing phase and a fast online phase.

The preprocessing paradigm culminated in SPDZ, the protocol of Damgård, Pastro, Smart, and Zakarias, which achieves malicious security against a dishonest majority — the strongest standard adversarial model — for arithmetic circuits over large prime fields, with an online phase whose cost per multiplication is essentially two field-element openings [6]. The original SPDZ preprocessing relied on somewhat-homomorphic encryption and was expensive; MASCOT [4] replaced it with oblivious-transfer-based triple generation that was over two hundred times faster than prior arithmetic-MPC preprocessing, at less than six times the cost of semi-honest security. Frameworks such as MP-SPDZ and SCALE-MAMBA [10] have since industrialized this stack, offering a dozen interoperable protocols behind a single compiler.

This thesis is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews the background: security models, oblivious transfer and its extension, and circuit models. Section 3 describes our methodology for comparing protocols on a common cost basis. Section 4 is the deep dive, covering garbling optimizations, GMW versus BMR, the SPDZ authenticated-sharing machinery, OT extension internals, and the framework and deployment landscape. Section 5 develops the empirical record and proof sketches. Section 6 examines limitations, and Section 7 concludes.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Security models

MPC protocols are analyzed against two canonical adversary classes. A *semi-honest* (honest-but-curious) adversary follows the protocol specification exactly but attempts to infer additional information from the transcript. A *malicious* (active) adversary may deviate arbitrarily: sending malformed messages, aborting, or substituting inputs. Security is formalized through the real/ideal paradigm [1]: a protocol is secure if, for every real-world adversary, there exists an ideal-world simulator interacting with a trusted functionality such that the two views are computationally indistinguishable. Malicious security against a *dishonest majority* — any $n-1$ of $n$ parties corrupt — is the gold standard, and it is the model targeted by SPDZ and MASCOT [4][6].

> **Theorem (Composition):** If a protocol $\pi$ securely realizes functionality $\mathcal{F}$ in the $\mathcal{G}$-hybrid model, and $\rho$ securely realizes $\mathcal{G}$, then $\pi^\rho$ securely realizes $\mathcal{F}$.

This composition theorem underlies all modular MPC design.

### 2.2 Oblivious transfer and OT extension

*1-out-of-2 oblivious transfer* (OT) is the workhorse of practically all efficient MPC. The sender holds messages $m_0, m_1$; the receiver holds a choice bit $b$ and learns $m_b$ while the sender learns nothing about $b$ and the receiver learns nothing about $m_{1-b}$. Yao's garbling consumes one OT per input bit of the evaluator, and GMW consumes correlated OTs per AND gate. The IKNP extension [2] reduces $m$ OTs to $k$ base OTs (with $k$ the security parameter, e.g. 128) plus $O(m)$ hash evaluations: the receiver builds a matrix whose rows encode its choices, the parties transpose roles via base OTs, and a correlation-robust hash $H$ masks the sender's messages. Security rests on $H$ being a *correlation-robust* function — a strictly weaker assumption than the random oracle in the semi-honest setting, though care is required under active attack.

### 2.3 Circuit models and cost metrics

Functions are expressed either as *Boolean circuits* (gates AND, XOR, NOT) or as *arithmetic circuits* over a field $\mathbb{F}_p$ (gates $+$, $\times$). Boolean circuits suit comparisons, hashing, and AES; arithmetic circuits suit statistics, linear algebra, and machine learning, where addition is *free* (local) under additive secret sharing and only multiplications cost interaction. We compare protocols on four axes:

| Metric | Symbol | Typical unit |
|---|---|---|
| Round complexity | $R$ | interaction rounds |
| Communication | $C$ | bits / field elements per gate |
| Symmetric operations | $S$ | AES / hash calls per gate |
| Preprocessing cost | $P$ | offline work amortized per gate |

## 3 Methodology

Our comparison methodology is analytic rather than benchmark-driven, because published benchmarks across frameworks differ in network conditions, threading, and field choices. We proceed in three steps.

1. **Canonical cost accounting.** For each protocol we derive, from the primary sources [1][2][3][4][5][6][10], the per-gate communication and round costs in the standard parameters: security parameter $\kappa = 128$, field $\mathbb{F}_p$ with $\log_2 p \approx 128$ for arithmetic protocols.
2. **Adversary-normalized comparison.** Semi-honest and malicious variants are compared separately; we record the *maliciousness overhead factor* — the ratio of malicious to semi-honest cost — since it is the quantity protocol designers optimize (MASCOT reports under $6\times$ [3]).
3. **Deployment-case validation.** Asymptotic claims are checked against the two canonical field deployments — the Danish sugar-beet auction [6][7][8] and the Boston gender wage-gap study — and against the protocol matrices shipped by MP-SPDZ [9], which expose the engineering cost of each choice.

We deliberately exclude fully-homomorphic-encryption-based MPC and information-theoretic honest-majority protocols (BGW/CCD), except as points of contrast, to keep the comparison within the dishonest-majority computational setting where garbling and SPDZ compete.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Yao's Garbled Circuits and the Half-Gates Optimization

Yao's protocol [1] involves two parties: the *garbler* $P_0$, who encrypts the Boolean circuit, and the *evaluator* $P_1$, who decrypts exactly one path through it. For every wire $w$, the garbler samples two random $\kappa$-bit *labels* $k_w^0, k_w^1$, indistinguishable to the evaluator. For each gate $g$ with input wires $a, b$ and output wire $c$, the garbler produces a *garbled table*: four doubly-encrypted entries, one per input combination, each encrypting the corresponding output label. The evaluator, holding one label per input wire (its own obtained via OT, the garbler's sent directly), decrypts exactly one row and proceeds. Output authenticity follows because labels are random: the evaluator cannot forge a label it never received.

The classic construction transmits 4 ciphertexts per AND gate. *Row reduction* (Naor–Pinkas–Sumner) eliminates one row by fixing an output label as a function of the others, yielding 3. *Free-XOR* (Kolesnikov–Schneider) makes XOR gates costless in communication by deriving all wire labels from a global secret offset $\Delta$: $k_w^1 = k_w^0 \oplus \Delta$, so XOR evaluation is a local XOR of labels. The culmination is the *half-gates* construction of Zahur, Rosulek, and Evans, which garbles an AND gate with exactly **2 ciphertexts** — provably optimal for the standard garbling model — by splitting the AND into a "garbler half gate" and an "evaluator half gate," each costing one ciphertext [3]. Modern multi-party garbling (MYao) ports row reduction and half gates to the BMR setting, substantially improving online efficiency when the party count is large [3].

```python
# Conceptual sketch: garbling one AND gate with point-and-permute
def garble_and(ka0, ka1, kb0, kb1, kc0, kc1, delta):
    table = []
    for a_bit, ka in [(0, ka0), (1, ka1)]:
        for b_bit, kb in [(0, kb0), (1, kb1)]:
            out = kc0 if (a_bit & b_bit) == 0 else kc1
            # permute bit = lsb of label; evaluator decrypts the row
            # matching the permute bits of its two input labels
            ct = enc(ka, enc(kb, out))
            table.append((lsb(ka), lsb(kb), ct))
    return table  # evaluator tries the single consistent row
```

### 4.2 GMW versus BMR: Secret Sharing Against Joint Garbling

GMW [2] evaluates the circuit gate by gate under the invariant that the parties hold an XOR (or additive) secret sharing of every wire value. XOR gates are free — each party XORs its shares locally — while each AND gate requires the parties to multiply shared bits, which is reduced to oblivious transfer: for parties $P_i, P_j$ holding shares $x_i, x_j$ and $y_i, y_j$ of the inputs, the cross terms $x_i y_j$ are computed with one OT each. GMW therefore has **$O(\text{depth})$ rounds** but low per-gate communication, and it generalizes naturally to $n$ parties with arbitrary Boolean circuits.

BMR [2] takes the opposite approach: it generalizes Yao's garbling itself to $n$ parties. All parties jointly garble the circuit — a task achievable with a simple semi-honest MPC among the garblers — after which every party holds the appropriate input labels and evaluates the garbled circuit *locally*. The result is **constant-round** evaluation regardless of circuit depth, at the price of a heavier garbling phase: each party contributes key material to every gate, so communication scales with $n^2$ in the naive construction. The choice is architectural:

| Protocol | Parties | Rounds | Cost driver | Ideal workload |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Yao | 2 | $O(1)$ | 2 ct / AND (half gates) | 2-party Boolean, low latency |
| GMW | $n \geq 2$ | $O(\text{depth})$ | OT per AND gate | deep circuits, cheap network rounds OK |
| BMR | $n \geq 2$ | $O(1)$ | joint garbling, $\sim n^2$ factor | wide/shallow circuits, high-latency links |

The MYao line of work [3] shows that the BMR garbling phase can inherit the half-gate and row-reduction optimizations, narrowing the gap to semi-honest BMR and making multi-party garbling competitive for large $n$ in the online phase.

### 4.3 SPDZ and MASCOT: Authenticated Preprocessing for Malicious Security

Where garbling targets Boolean circuits, the SPDZ family targets *arithmetic* circuits with malicious, dishonest-majority security. The core invention is the *authenticated share*. Fix a global MAC key $\alpha \in \mathbb{F}_p$, itself secret-shared so that no party knows it. A value $x$ is represented as

$$\llbracket x \rrbracket = (\langle x \rangle, \langle m_x \rangle), \qquad m_x = \alpha \cdot x,$$

where $\langle \cdot \rangle$ denotes additive sharing. Addition is local on both components; multiplication consumes a *Beaver triple* $(\llbracket a \rrbracket, \llbracket b \rrbracket, \llbracket c \rrbracket)$ with $c = ab$, generated in a preprocessing phase:

```rust
// Beaver multiplication, online phase (per party i, shares a_i,b_i,c_i)
let d_i = x_i - a_i;            // masked input share
let e_i = y_i - b_i;
let d = open(d_i); let e = open(e_i);   // two broadcasts; MAC-checked
let z_i = c_i + d * b_i + e * a_i + (if i == 0 { d * e } else { 0 });
// z_i is an additive share of x*y; MAC share updates linearly
```

Because the adversary does not know $\alpha$, forging a MAC on a modified share succeeds with probability at most $1/p$ per check; the protocol batches verification of all opened values into a single random linear combination $\sigma = \sum_i r_i(\gamma(y_i) - \alpha y_i)$ and aborts unless $\sum \sigma_i = 0$ [6]. Crucially, SPDZ detects cheating but does *not* identify the cheater or recover — *abort* security, not guaranteed output delivery.

The original SPDZ preprocessing used somewhat-homomorphic encryption per multiplication — asymptotically fine, concretely painful. **MASCOT** (Keller–Orsini–Scholl, 2016) replaced it with OT-based triple generation: multiplications are performed through an arithmetic view of oblivious transfer with consistency checks, achieving malicious security at *under six times* the cost of the semi-honest baseline and improving on prior arithmetic-MPC preprocessing runtimes by *more than two hundred times* with parallelism and vectorization [4]. Subsequent preprocessing protocols — Overdrive, LowGear, HighGear, TopGear zero-knowledge proofs [10] — trade communication against computation, but MASCOT remains the reference OT-based design.

> **Theorem (SPDZ online soundness, informal):** Conditioned on the preprocessing producing valid authenticated triples, any malicious adversary that alters a share during the online phase causes the final MAC check to fail except with probability at most $2^{-\kappa} + q/p$, where $q$ is the number of opened values.

### 4.4 Oblivious Transfer Extension as the Computational Engine

Both the garbling and SPDZ/MASCOT stacks bottom out in OT extension [2]. The IKNP construction works as follows: the receiver samples a random $m \times k$ binary matrix $T$ and choice vector $\mathbf{r} \in \{0,1\}^m$; for each column $j$, the parties execute one base OT in which the sender inputs $(s_j, s_j \oplus \mathbf{q}_j)$ and the receiver's choice determines whether column $j$ of the transmitted matrix equals $T^j$ or $T^j \oplus \mathbf{r}$. The sender then masks its $i$-th message pair with $H(i, \mathbf{q}_i)$ and $H(i, \mathbf{q}_i \oplus \mathbf{s})$, and the receiver — knowing $\mathbf{r}$ — can unmask exactly the message matching $r_i$. The $k$ base OTs (public-key) are thus amortized over $m$ extended OTs (symmetric-key).

IKNP's original analysis requires $H$ to be correlation-robust; malicious-receiver variants add consistency checks on the matrix rows, as in MASCOT's correlated-OT subprotocol [4]. In practice, OT extension throughput — millions of OTs per second on commodity hardware — is what makes GMW's per-AND-gate OT affordable and what keeps MASCOT's triple factory fed.

```tla
---- MODULE MACCheck ----
\* Abstract specification of the batched SPDZ MAC check.
VARIABLES shares, macs, alphaShares, opened
Check == \* all parties commit to sigma_i, then open; abort unless sum = 0
  LET sigma == [i \in Parties |-> macs[i] - alphaShares[i] * opened]
  IN  Sum(sigma) = 0
\* Safety: a tampered share alters macs[i] inconsistently with alpha*opened,
\* so Check fails; alpha is never reconstructed, hence unforgeable.
====
```

### 4.5 From Theory to Practice: Frameworks and Deployments

**MP-SPDZ** (Keller) is the reference implementation, exposing a protocol matrix behind a Python-like compiler: `mascot-party.x` for malicious dishonest-majority arithmetic [4], `semi-party.x` for the semi-honest baseline, `spdz2k-party.x` for rings $\mathbb{Z}_{2^k}$, `lowgear`/`highgear` for SHE-based preprocessing with TopGear proofs, and `tinier`/`tiny` for the binary domain — with *daBits* bridging arithmetic and Boolean worlds [10]. **SCALE-MAMBA**, developed at KU Leuven and Bristol, provides the complementary full-stack deployment (circuit compilation through the Bristol fashion, preprocessing, and online execution) used in several applied pilots.

Two deployments anchor this history. In **January 2008**, the Danish sugar-beet auction became the first large-scale real-world MPC deployment: roughly 1,200 farmers submitted secret supply/demand bids, and a three-server MPC computed the market-clearing price — the price equalizing aggregate supply and demand — without revealing any individual bid, in about half an hour of computation [7][8][9]. In **2015–2016**, Boston University's Hariri Institute (Bestavros, Lapets, and students) built an MPC-based aggregation platform for the Boston Women's Workforce Council's 100% Talent Compact: employers masked male and female pay totals with large random offsets split between BU's server and an independent party, enabling computation of the aggregate gender pay gap — reported as 77 cents on the dollar across 112,600 employees — without any company's payroll ever being exposed [11][12]. Both cases share a moral: MPC succeeds in deployment where *trust is scarcest*, not where computation is heaviest.

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

The quantitative record, drawn from the primary papers, is summarized below ($\kappa = 128$; field elements $\approx 128$ bits).

| Protocol / optimization | Per-AND/multiplication cost | Rounds | Adversary |
|---|---|---|---|
| Yao, classic garbling | 4 ct ($\approx 512$ B) | $O(1)$ | semi-honest |
| Yao + row reduction | 3 ct | $O(1)$ | semi-honest |
| Yao + half gates | **2 ct** (optimal) | $O(1)$ | semi-honest |
| GMW | $\approx 2$ OT-equivalents | $O(\text{depth})$ | semi-honest |
| BMR / MYao | joint garbling ($\sim n^2$ factor) | $O(1)$ | semi-honest |
| SPDZ online | 2 openings ($\approx 2$ field elements) | $O(\text{depth})$ | malicious, $n-1$ corrupt |
| MASCOT preprocessing | $<6\times$ semi-honest OT cost | amortized | malicious, $n-1$ corrupt |

Two proof sketches illustrate the security arguments. **(i) Yao privacy [1]:** the simulator, given only the output, garbles a circuit that always outputs the prescribed result: it samples random labels for all wires and programs each garbled table to decrypt to the (simulated) correct output label regardless of input labels. Because real and simulated labels are identically distributed random strings, and the evaluator's OT-obtained labels reveal nothing about the garbler's inputs by OT security, the views are indistinguishable. **(ii) SPDZ MAC unforgeability [6]:** the MAC key $\alpha$ is uniformly random and secret-shared; an adversary modifying its share of $x$ to $x + \delta$ must produce a MAC share consistent with $\alpha(x+\delta)$, i.e., guess $\alpha \cdot \delta$ — impossible except with probability $1/p$ per attempt, since $\alpha$ is information-theoretically hidden. The batched check preserves this bound via the Schwartz–Zippel-style random linear combination.

The MASCOT paper's headline empirical claim — preprocessing over $200\times$ faster than SHE-based SPDZ preprocessing for up to five parties, at $<6\times$ semi-honest cost [4] — has been independently reproduced in the MP-SPDZ benchmarking suite [10], which further shows the crossover where OT-based preprocessing (MASCOT) beats SHE-based preprocessing (LowGear/HighGear) moving in MASCOT's favor as party count grows, because OT extension parallelizes near-linearly while SHE ciphertext operations do not.

## 6 Limitations

The results above must be read against persistent limitations. **First**, malicious security in the dishonest-majority setting buys *abort*, not fairness or guaranteed output: a corrupt party can always learn the output and then refuse to let others learn it. **Second**, the preprocessing model assumes an offline phase whose total work typically exceeds the online phase by an order of magnitude; for one-shot computations, preprocessing cannot be amortized and semi-honest garbling may win outright. **Third**, OT extension's security proofs are delicate: the correlation-robustness assumption has been revisited repeatedly, and several deployed OT-extension variants required post-hoc patches against selective-failure and related-key attacks — a reminder that the symmetric-key layer deserves the same scrutiny as the protocol layer [2][4]. **Fourth**, circuit compilation remains a bottleneck: converting programs with data-dependent loops or floating point into efficient Boolean or arithmetic circuits (via CBMC-GC, HyCC, or MP-SPDZ's compiler) is still expert work, and naive compilation can inflate AND counts by orders of magnitude. **Fifth**, all protocols surveyed assume classical hardness (OT from DDH/QR/LWE); a cryptographically relevant quantum computer breaks the base OTs, and post-quantum MPC at MASCOT's efficiency is an open engineering problem. **Finally**, deployment experience [8][11] shows the binding constraint is rarely cryptography: it is key management across organizations, auditability of the software stack, and the legal framing of "computation without custody."

## 7 Conclusion

Secure multi-party computation has completed the journey from Yao's 1986 thought experiment to production software. The intellectual arc is clear: *garbling* made two-party Boolean computation constant-round; *GMW and BMR* offered the two canonical multi-party generalizations — secret-shared gate-by-gate evaluation versus joint garbling; *OT extension* removed the public-key bottleneck that had kept all of them impractical; and *SPDZ with MASCOT preprocessing* married malicious, dishonest-majority security to an online phase cheap enough to deploy. The Danish sugar-beet auction [7][8][9] and the Boston wage-gap study [11][12] demonstrate that the technology's comparative advantage lies in computations where no party may be trusted with the inputs — auctions, benchmarks, and equity audits.

The research frontier now lies in three directions. First, *post-quantum MPC*: OT extension from lattice assumptions exists in theory but has not been engineered to IKNP's throughput. Second, *maliciously secure machine learning*: frameworks like CrypTen have shown the demand, but malicious training at scale remains out of reach. Third, *standardization*: the field needs audited, interoperable implementations with the deployment discipline — reproducible builds, formal specifications of the OT layer, transparent preprocessing ceremonies — that turned TLS from a protocol into infrastructure. The mathematics of computing on secrets is settled; the engineering of trusting the computation is not.
