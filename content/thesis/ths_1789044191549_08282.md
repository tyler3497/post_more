# Certifying Mathematical Truth: Dependent Type Theory, Elaboration, Tactics, and Metaprogramming in Lean 4 and the Mathlib Ecosystem

## Abstract

We present a systematic study of the Lean 4 interactive theorem prover and its surrounding ecosystem as an instrument for the machine-checked formalization of mathematics. Beginning from dependent type theory — the logical foundation in which propositions are types and proofs are programs — we trace how Lean 4's elaborator converts human-readable surface syntax into kernel-checkable proof terms through metavariable-driven bidirectional type checking, postponed unification constraints, and integrated typeclass inference. We analyze the tactic framework and the metaprogramming monad stack (*CoreM*, *MetaM*, *TermElabM*, *TacticM*) that makes the implementation language and the object language coincide, so that user-defined automation is ordinary Lean code rather than an external plugin API. We examine Mathlib, the community-maintained mathematical library, as a case study in large-scale collaborative formalization, and review hammer-style automation such as Lean-auto that bridges dependent type theory with first-order solvers [2]. We close with the soundness architecture — a small trusted kernel plus explicit axiom auditing — and the limitations that remain.

## 1 Introduction

Mathematics aspires to a standard of rigor in which every claim follows from stated assumptions by explicit rules of inference. In practice, published proofs elide steps the author judges obvious, and the history of the subject records episodes in which such elisions concealed genuine errors. *Interactive theorem proving* (ITP) proposes a technological answer: proofs are developed inside a software system whose kernel independently re-checks every inference, so that a completed development is a machine-verifiable certificate of correctness [1].

Lean 4, designed by Leonardo de Moura and Sebastian Ullrich, occupies a distinctive position among proof assistants [1]. Unlike systems whose automation lives in a separate implementation language, Lean 4 is *self-hosted*: the language in which mathematicians write definitions and proofs is the same language in which the elaborator, the tactic framework, and user extensions are written. Automation that once required modifying the prover's source tree becomes ordinary library code — distributed, versioned, and reviewed like any other contribution.

The stakes are visible in Mathlib, the community library of formalized mathematics built on Lean 4 [3]: among the largest coherent bodies of machine-checked mathematics ever assembled, developed *monolithically* so that every commit is re-checked in full. Understanding how this is possible requires understanding the full pipeline — the type theory, the elaborator, the tactics, the metaprogramming layer, and the social-technical apparatus of the library itself.

> **Principle (de Bruijn criterion).** The correctness of a Lean 4 development depends only on the correctness of the small kernel type checker, never on the elaborator, the tactics, or the metaprograms that produced the proof term.

Section 2 recalls the logical background; Section 3 describes our method; Section 4 examines the type-theoretic core, elaboration, tactics, metaprogramming, and Mathlib in depth; Section 5 reports empirical observations; Section 6 discusses limitations; and Section 7 concludes.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Propositions as types

The logical foundation of Lean is *dependent type theory*, organized around the Curry–Howard correspondence: propositions are types, and proofs are programs inhabiting those types [7]. Implication *P → Q* is the function type from proofs of *P* to proofs of *Q*; universal quantification *∀ x : α, P x* is the *dependent* function type *(x : α) → P x*, whose codomain varies with its argument. To prove a theorem is to construct a term; to check a proof is to type-check a term. Proof checking thereby inherits the decidability of type checking — precisely what makes the de Bruijn criterion attainable.

The specific theory belongs to the tradition of the *Calculus of Constructions* of Coquand and Huet [5], extended with inductive types in the style of the *Calculus of Inductive Constructions*. Three mechanisms carry most of the expressive load: **dependent function types (Π-types)**, generalizing both functions and universal quantification; **inductive families** (`Nat`, `List α`, the equality type `Eq`), whose automatically derived recursors encode induction principles with computation by *iota-reduction*; and a **universe hierarchy** (`Type u : Type (u+1)`, with impredicative `Prop` at the bottom) that stratifies types to avoid Girard's paradox.

### 2.2 Definitional versus propositional equality

Lean distinguishes two equalities, and the distinction drives much of the system's design. *Definitional* equality holds when two terms are equal by computation: β-reduction, δ-unfolding of definitions, ι-reduction of recursors, ζ-reduction of let-bindings, and η-expansion. The kernel decides it by normalization. *Propositional* equality is the inductive family `Eq`, whose proofs may require genuine argument. The tactic `rfl` succeeds exactly when two sides are definitionally equal; everything else demands proof terms.

Two further features matter. Lean has *definitional proof irrelevance*: any two proofs of the same `Prop` are judgmentally equal, keeping proof terms from bloating computation. And propositional reasoning rests on three axioms beyond the core theory (Table 1), each auditable per-theorem via `#print axioms` — a transparency mechanism with no analogue in informal mathematics.

| Axiom | Statement | Role |
|---|---|---|
| `propext` | *(a ↔ b) → a = b* | Propositional extensionality |
| `Quot.sound` | Quotient construction is sound | Yields function extensionality `funext` |
| `Classical.choice` | Indefinite description | Noncomputable choice; classical reasoning |

*Table 1: The three axioms extending Lean's core theory. Developments avoiding `Classical.choice` remain fully constructive and computable.*

## 3 Methodology

This study proceeds by *architectural analysis*: we dissect the Lean 4 pipeline stage by stage — surface syntax, elaboration, tactic execution, kernel certification — and characterize the contract each stage offers its neighbors. Primary sources are the system description of de Moura and Ullrich [1], the community metaprogramming reference [6], the Lean-auto translation paper [2], the Mathlib project description [3], and the earlier metaprogramming framework paper [4], supplemented by the type-theoretic literature [5][7]. Claims about elaborator behavior were cross-checked against the community metaprogramming documentation and worked examples [6]; empirical observations in Section 5 draw on documented milestones including the Mathlib corpus [3] and the machine-checked irrationality of ζ(3) with an explicit prime number theorem error term [9].

Two disciplines govern the analysis. First, we distinguish *trusted* components (the kernel, the axiom set) from *untrusted but checked* components (elaborators, tactics, macros), per the de Bruijn criterion. Second, we treat automation claims empirically: a tactic is evaluated by what it discharges in practice — the standard applied by the Lean-auto authors on the Mathlib4 benchmark [2].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Type-Theoretic Core: Universes, Inductive Families, and the Kernel Contract

At the bottom of the stack sits the *kernel*, a small, deliberately austere type checker. Everything above it — parser, elaborator, every tactic ever written — is untrusted: the kernel re-checks every declaration and proof term before admitting it to the environment. This is what makes metaprogramming *safe*: a buggy tactic cannot introduce a false theorem, because the kernel rejects the ill-typed proof term. At worst, a tactic bug is a crash or a failed proof, never an unsoundness.

The kernel's expression language is minimal. In Lean 4's implementation it is the `Expr` datatype, whose constructors are exactly the term formers of the theory [6]:

```lean
inductive Expr where
  | bvar   : Nat → Expr                   -- bound variable (de Bruijn index)
  | fvar   : FVarId → Expr                -- free variable (local context)
  | mvar   : MVarId → Expr                -- metavariable (elaboration hole)
  | sort   : Level → Expr                 -- Type u, Prop
  | const  : Name → List Level → Expr     -- global constant
  | app    : Expr → Expr → Expr           -- application
  | lam    : Name → Expr → Expr → BinderInfo → Expr
  | forallE: Name → Expr → Expr → BinderInfo → Expr  -- Π-type
  | lit    : Literal → Expr
  | mdata  : MData → Expr → Expr
  | proj   : Name → Nat → Expr → Expr     -- structure projection
```

Bound variables use *de Bruijn indices*, eliminating α-equivalence concerns. Metavariables are first-class: elaboration builds terms with holes and solves them by unification, and the kernel never sees an unsolved hole. Universe polymorphism is explicit — `const` carries a list of levels — giving an infinite hierarchy at the cost of occasional opaque *universe constraint* errors.

The kernel's definitional equality is the engine of small-scale automation. When a user writes `rfl : 2 + 2 = 4`, the kernel decides by ι-reduction that `2 + 2` and `4` coincide. The `decide` tactic generalizes this: for a decidable proposition it evaluates the decision procedure by kernel reduction and closes the goal with the resulting proof. This *proof by reflection* — turning computation into proof — is dependent type theory's signature move, and Lean 4's compiler-backed reduction makes it practical at scales that would choke a purely interpreted kernel.

### 4.2 Elaboration: From Surface Syntax to Kernel Terms

If the kernel is the system's conscience, the *elaborator* is its interpreter: surface Lean supports implicit arguments, typeclass arguments, coercions, overloading, pattern matching, and tactic blocks, none of which exist in the core theory. Elaboration compiles this rich surface into bare `Expr` trees, and it is arguably Lean 4's most sophisticated component [1][6].

Its central device is the *metavariable with postponement*. Elaboration is bidirectional: given an expected type, information flows inward; where information is missing, a metavariable `?m` is created and *constraints* recorded. Constraints that cannot yet be solved are *postponed* rather than failed — when later information assigns the metavariable, postponed constraints are revisited in a propagator-like loop. In `ident 5 : Nat` with `ident {α : Type} (x : α) : α`, the implicit `?α` is created, unified with `Nat` from the expected type flowing inward, and the postponed constraint on the argument then resolves. This is what makes heavy implicit-argument inference feel effortless — and what makes elaboration failures occasionally inscrutable, since the error surfaces far from the constraint that caused it.

Three elaboration-time mechanisms deserve emphasis:

1. **Typeclass inference as constraint solving.** Class arguments (e.g., `[Add α]`) become *synthetic opaque* metavariables discharged by a dedicated Prolog-style search after ordinary unification. Because resolution runs inside elaboration, notations like `a + b` resolve instances during type checking itself. Pathological instance sets can still cause exponential blowup — the classic performance cliff of large Mathlib developments.
2. **Coercions.** When `t : α` fails to unify against expected `β`, registered coercions `α → β` are inserted silently. Chains like `ℕ → ℤ → ℚ → ℝ → ℂ` are among Mathlib's most load-bearing automation — and most delicate, since a misplaced coercion sends elaboration down a wrong path with a confusing error.
3. **The monad stack.** Elaboration is organized as layered monads [6]: `CoreM` (environment, options) → `MetaM` (metavariables, unification) → `TermElabM` (expected types, implicits) → `TacticM` (goals). Each layer is available to user metaprograms, and since a `by` block is just a term whose elaborator runs tactics to fill a metavariable, the boundary between "programming" and "proving" dissolves at the implementation level.

### 4.3 Tactics: Backward Proof as Metavariable Assignment

A Lean 4 tactic proof is term elaboration with a twist: the *goal* is a metavariable whose type is the proposition to be proved, and each tactic is a `TacticM` program that partially assigns it. `intro h` assigns `?m : P → Q` to `fun h => ?m'`; `exact e` assigns the goal to the elaborated term `e`; `apply f` unifies the goal with `f`'s conclusion, generating new goals for the premises. When no goals remain, the kernel re-checks the complete term. Tactics cannot lie — they can only fail.

Tactics compose through a combinator language — sequential (`;`), alternation (`<;>`), backtracking (`first | t | u`), repetition (`repeat`) — made semantically clean by the `MonadBacktrack` class: `MetaM`, `TermElabM`, and `TacticM` provide `saveState`/`restoreState` over the environment, metavariable context, and goals, so a failing tactic restores the exact prior state as if it had never run [6].

The working arsenal stratifies by power and predictability: **structural** tactics (`intro`, `exact`, `apply`, `cases`, `induction`) mirror the theory's inference rules; **rewriting** tactics (`rw`, and `simp` — a conditional rewriting engine with ordered rule sets and arithmetic preprocessing, Mathlib's workhorse); **decision procedures** (`decide`, `omega` for linear integer arithmetic, `ring` for commutative-ring normalization), complete on their fragments; and **search-based** automation (`aesop`'s best-first search, and hammer interfaces). Lean-auto [2] implements the first general-purpose hammer for Lean 4: it translates dependent-type-theoretic goals to first-order logic, dispatches them to external ATPs, and reconstructs kernel-checked proof terms. Its authors prove soundness of the translation and report it solving more Mathlib4 benchmark problems than existing tools [2] — evidence that the long-standing DTT-to-FOL bottleneck is now substantially cleared.

| Tactic class | Examples | Completeness | Trust basis |
|---|---|---|---|
| Structural | `intro`, `exact`, `apply` | N/A (user-guided) | Kernel re-check |
| Simplification | `simp`, `rw` | Heuristic | Kernel re-check |
| Decision procedure | `decide`, `omega`, `ring` | Complete on fragment | Kernel re-check |
| Search / hammer | `aesop`, Lean-auto [2] | Heuristic | Kernel re-check |

*Table 2: Every tactic class bottoms out in kernel-checked proof terms; automation affects only which proofs are found, never their validity.*

### 4.4 Metaprogramming: The System Programming Itself

Lean 4's most radical proposition is that *there is no metaprogramming API distinct from the language*: macros, elaborators, and tactics are Lean programs, compiled by the Lean compiler, manipulating Lean's own `Syntax` and `Expr` types [4][6]. Extending the prover is library development. The extension pipeline has three tiers:

1. **Macros** (`MacroM`: syntax → syntax) declare new surface syntax expanded before elaboration, with automatic hygiene via macro scopes. `do`-notation and much of Mathlib's notation are macros — purely syntactic, hence predictable.
2. **Elaborators** (`elab` rules in `TermElabM`/`CommandElabM`) run arbitrary elaboration code: custom commands, type-directed notations, whole DSLs.
3. **Tactics** (`TacticM`) with full access to goals, unification, and the environment.

A miniature `rfl` shows how little machinery tactic authoring requires [6]:

```lean
open Lean Elab Tactic

elab "my_rfl" : tactic => do
  let goal ← getMainGoal
  goal.withContext do
    let t ← goal.getType
    match t with
    | Expr.app (Expr.app (Expr.app (.const ``Eq _) _) a) b =>
        if ← isDefEq a b then
          goal.assign (← mkAppM ``Eq.refl #[a])
        else throwTacticEx `my_rfl goal "sides are not defeq"
    | _ => throwTacticEx `my_rfl goal "not an equality"
```

Pattern matching on `Expr`, definitional-equality testing via `isDefEq`, proof-term construction, goal assignment — the complete loop in a dozen lines, in the same language as the mathematics. The safety story is the whole point: *metaprograms are untrusted*. A macro may generate ill-typed syntax; the elaborator rejects it. A tactic may assign a bogus proof; the kernel rejects it. The trusted base is the kernel plus the axioms — everything else, including code from thousands of Mathlib contributors, is checked output. That is what makes crowdsourced, machine-checked mathematics socially scalable: contributors need not be trusted, only their artifacts.

### 4.5 Mathlib: Formalized Mathematics at Industrial Scale

Mathlib is the existence proof that the architecture scales [3]: a *monolithic*, community-maintained library for Lean 4 — monolithic in the deliberate sense that all of mathematics lives in one repository, one namespace hierarchy, one build, so any result may freely use any other. It exceeds a million lines of Lean, developed by hundreds of contributors under continuous integration that re-checks the entire library on every change.

## 5 Empirical Results / Proofs

**Observation 1 (Soundness in practice).** The de Bruijn architecture delivers its central promise: no false theorem has been admitted through a tactic or elaborator bug surviving kernel checking, because kernel checking is *independent* of those components. Soundness incidents in ITP history instead involve the trusted base itself — a far smaller attack surface, and one that `#print axioms` makes auditable per-theorem.

**Observation 2 (Automation is now measurable).** Lean-auto [2] gives the first rigorous benchmark of hammer-style automation for Lean 4: a provably sound DTT-to-FOL translation, evaluated on Mathlib4 problems, outperforming prior Lean 4 automation. This moves "tactic power" from folklore to measurement — and premise selection over Mathlib's million-line corpus is the binding constraint on human formalizers.

**Observation 3 (Scale is achieved).** Mathlib's monolithic, CI-gated model sustains growth that no centrally managed formalization project has matched [3]. Lake-based incremental builds, automation-friendly style, and blueprint-driven parallelization constitute a reproducible *method* for large formalization.

**Observation 4 (The reflection dividend).** Proof by `decide` and verified decision procedures (`omega`, `ring`) show that identifying computation with proof is a daily workhorse, not a curiosity: routine goals are discharged by evaluation, with the kernel's definitional equality as sole arbiter.

> **Theorem (Elaboration soundness, informal).** *If the elaborator produces a closed term `e` and the kernel accepts `e : T`, then `e` is well-typed in the core theory — regardless of which macros, tactics, or metaprograms participated in its construction.*

This is enforced rather than proved about the implementation: kernel acceptance is the only admission criterion, which is what justifies treating the entire metaprogramming ecosystem as untrusted.
## 6 Limitations

1. **Classical opacity.** Most Mathlib mathematics uses `Classical.choice`, rendering definitions noncomputable — a classical existence proof cannot in general be *run* to extract a witness. The system supports sequestering classical reasoning but does not enforce it.
2. **Elaboration unpredictability.** Postponement, typeclass search, and coercions interact in ways difficult to predict or debug; errors report the *symptom* (a failed unification in a postponed constraint) rather than the *cause* (the ambiguous notation three lines up).
3. **Automation gaps.** Despite Lean-auto [2], Lean 4 automation still trails Isabelle/HOL's Sledgehammer in premise selection over very large libraries, and `simp`'s power is bought with sensitivity to lemma-set curation.
4. **Proof-term scale.** Elaborated proof terms from heavy automation can be enormous, occasionally stressing the kernel's evaluator; compact certificate formats remain an active concern.
5. **Trust ends at the kernel — but correctness does not.** A definition may formalize the wrong concept (the *specification gap*); a `sorry`-free development may rest on mis-stated axioms; the compiler and pretty-printer are untrusted. Machine-checked means correct *relative to the formal statement* — and the statement remains a human artifact needing review.
6. **Cost of entry.** The learning curve is steep and formalization remains roughly an order of magnitude slower than informal writing for most practitioners, concentrating the effort in communities that value certification over speed.

## 7 Conclusion

Lean 4 achieves a long-anticipated convergence: a *single language* serving as programming language, proof assistant, tactic framework, and metaprogramming platform, grounded in a dependent type theory whose kernel is small enough to trust and whose elaborator is powerful enough to make formalization humane [1][4]. Metavariable-driven bidirectional elaboration, the tactic monad stack with principled backtracking, and the everything-is-a-Lean-program metaprogramming model make automation into library code — reviewed, versioned, composable. Mathlib shows this scales to the largest collaborative formalization effort in history [3], and hammer interfaces such as Lean-auto show the automation frontier still advancing [2].

The deeper significance is not any single checked theorem but a *new medium* for mathematics: definitions and proofs as machine-queryable, refactorable, permanently re-checkable artifacts. The limitations — classical opacity, elaboration inscrutability, the specification gap, the cost of entry — define the research program. If the last decade built the instrument, the next will determine how much of mathematics learns to play it.
