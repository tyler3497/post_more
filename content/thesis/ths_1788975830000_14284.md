# Causal Identification in Semi-Markovian Models: The Do-Calculus, the Front-Door Criterion, and Complete Identification Algorithms

## Abstract

Causal identification asks when an interventional quantity such as $P(y \mid do(x))$ — the distribution of $Y$ under an intervention setting $X$ to $x$ — can be computed from the observational distribution $P(v)$ together with a qualitative causal graph $G$. This article develops the modern theory of identification in recursive semi-Markovian causal models, where unmeasured confounders are represented as bidirected arcs. We present Pearl's three rules of **do-calculus** as a sound inference system for transforming interventional expressions into do-free estimands, derive the **front-door criterion** as a two-step reduction that identifies causal effects even when confounding is unmeasured, and describe the **ID and IDC algorithms** of Shpitser and Pearl, which are provably sound and complete: they fail exactly on a structural witness of non-identifiability known as a *hedge*. We work through the c-component factorization of Tian and the $Q$-function machinery of Huang and Valtorta, demonstrate a complete front-door estimation pipeline in Python with a simulation study, and survey complexity results, statistical fragility, and open problems.

## 1. Introduction

In the potential-outcomes and structural-causal traditions, a persistent gap separates *what we can observe* from *what we wish to know*. A randomized experiment measures $P(y \mid do(x))$, the distribution of $Y$ after an external intervention sets $X=x$, severing $X$ from its natural causes. An observational study measures $P(y \mid x)$, the distribution of $Y$ among units that happen to exhibit $X=x$. These coincide only under strong ignorability; in general, unmeasured common causes — confounders — drive a wedge between seeing and doing [1].

The *identification problem* formalizes this gap. Given (i) a causal graph $G$ over observed variables $V$ (with bidirected arcs encoding unobserved confounding) and (ii) the observational distribution $P(v)$, which interventional quantities $P_x(y) := P(y \mid do(x))$ are uniquely determined? A quantity is **identifiable** if any two structural causal models compatible with $G$ and $P$ agree on it; it is identifiable with an *estimand* — an explicit functional of $P$ — if we can also write down the formula [2].

> **Theorem (Pearl, 1995; Shpitser & Pearl, 2006):** The three rules of do-calculus, together with standard probability manipulations, are *sound and complete* for the identification of interventional distributions in recursive semi-Markovian causal models [3][4].

This completeness theorem is one of the landmark results of graphical causal inference. It states that there is no gap between *derivability* (what the rules can prove) and *identifiability* (what is logically determined): if the rules cannot reduce a query to a do-free expression, no method can, and a *hedge* — a specific graphical obstruction — witnesses the failure [5][6]. The ID algorithm converts this theoretical completeness into a constructive, polynomial-time procedure that either emits the estimand or exhibits the hedge [7].

The remainder of this article is organized as follows. Section 2 fixes notation and recalls structural causal models, latent projections, and $d$-separation. Section 3 develops the methodology: the do-calculus, graph surgery, and the back-door and front-door criteria. Section 4 provides the deep dive: complete statements and proof sketches of the three rules, Tian's c-components and the hedge criterion, the ID algorithm, and its conditional extension IDC. Section 5 demonstrates the theory empirically with a simulation study comparing naive, back-door, and front-door estimators. Section 6 discusses limitations and open problems, and Section 7 concludes.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 Structural causal models and latent projections

A **structural causal model (SCM)** $M = \langle U, V, F, P(u) \rangle$ consists of exogenous (background) variables $U$, endogenous variables $V$, a set of structural equations $f_i: \text{Pa}(V_i) \cup U_i \to V_i$ assigning each $V_i$ as a function of its parents, and a distribution $P(u)$ over the exogenous variables [1]. Each SCM induces a **causal diagram** $G$ — a directed acyclic graph over $V$ — plus the observational distribution $P(v)$ obtained by pushing $P(u)$ through the equations.

A model is **Markovian** if the exogenous variables are mutually independent (all common causes observed), in which case the joint distribution factorizes as $P(v) = \prod_i P(v_i \mid \text{pa}_i)$. It is **semi-Markovian** if $U$ may be dependent but no $U$ is a descendant of any $V$ — the standard setting when confounders are unmeasured. In the semi-Markovian case we project out the latent variables: two observed variables sharing an unmeasured common cause are joined by a **bidirected arc** $X \leftrightarrow Y$, yielding a *semi-Markovian graph* or, more generally, an acyclic directed mixed graph (ADMG). Positivity of $P(v)$ and recursiveness (acyclicity) are assumed throughout [5].

### 2.2 Interventions and the truncated factorization

The operator $do(X=x)$ denotes an atomic intervention that replaces the equation for $X$ with the constant $x$, deleting all arrows into $X$. The resulting **mutilated graph** is denoted $G_{\bar{X}}$. In a Markovian model, the interventional distribution is given by the *truncated factorization*:

$$P_x(v) = \prod_{V_i \notin X} P(v_i \mid \text{pa}_i),$$

with the sum over all variables except $Y$ of interest. In semi-Markovian models this factorization fails in general — unmeasured confounding means the conditional factors $P(v_i \mid \text{pa}_i)$ need not be invariant under intervention — which is precisely why identification is nontrivial [1][8].

### 2.3 $d$-separation and conditional independence

**$d$-separation** is the graphical criterion linking graph structure to conditional independence in every compatible distribution. A path is *blocked* by a set $Z$ if it contains a non-collider in $Z$ or a collider whose descendants avoid $Z$; two sets are $d$-separated given $Z$, written $(X \perp\!\!\!\perp Y \mid Z)_G$, if every path between them is blocked. The *mutilated* graphs $G_{\bar{X}}$ (incoming arrows to $X$ deleted) and $G_{\underline{X}}$ (outgoing arrows from $X$ deleted) are central to the do-calculus, since they encode the independencies that hold *after* intervention [4].

### 2.4 $Q$-functions and c-components

Tian and Pearl (2002) introduced a coarser factorization that survives latent confounding. Two variables in the same **c-component** (confounded component) are connected by a path of bidirected arcs. For any $S \subseteq V$, define the **$Q$-function** $Q[S](v) := P_{v \setminus s}(s)$ — the effect on $S$ of intervening on everything else [2]. Tian showed that $P(v)$ factorizes over the c-components $S_1, \dots, S_k$ of $G$ as

$$P(v) = \prod_{j=1}^{k} Q[S_j],$$

and each $Q[S_j]$ is computable from $P(v)$: if $V_i$ is topologically last in $S_j$'s ancestral closure, then $Q[S_j] = \sum_{v_i} Q[S_j \cup \{V_i\}]$ starting from $Q[V] = P(v)$. This decomposition is the engine of every complete identification algorithm [2][5].

## 3. Methodology

### 3.1 The three rules of do-calculus

Let $X, Y, Z, W$ be disjoint node sets in a causal diagram $G$, and let $G_{\bar{X}}$ and $G_{\underline{X}}$ denote the graphs with incoming and outgoing arrows of $X$ deleted, respectively. Pearl's **do-calculus** comprises [4][3]:

- **Rule 1** (insertion/deletion of observations): $P(y \mid do(x), z, w) = P(y \mid do(x), z)$ if $(Y \perp\!\!\!\perp W \mid X, Z)_{G_{\bar{X}}}$.
- **Rule 2** (action/observation exchange): $P(y \mid do(x), do(z), w) = P(y \mid do(x), z, w)$ if $(Y \perp\!\!\!\perp Z \mid X, W)_{G_{\bar{X}\underline{Z}}}$.
- **Rule 3** (insertion/deletion of actions): $P(y \mid do(x), do(z), w) = P(y \mid do(x), w)$ if $(Y \perp\!\!\!\perp Z \mid X, W)_{G_{\bar{X}, \overline{Z(W)}}}$, where $Z(W) = Z \setminus \text{An}(W)_{G_{\bar{X}}}$.

Intuitively, Rule 1 says that observations irrelevant after intervention can be dropped; Rule 2 says an intervention on $Z$ can be *exchanged* for an observation of $Z$ when no confounding path remains between $Z$ and $Y$ after surgery; Rule 3 says interventions with no causal path to the outcome can be deleted. The antecedent of each rule is a $d$-separation statement in a surgically modified graph — a purely graphical, checkable condition [4].

### 3.2 The back-door criterion

The **back-door criterion** is the workhorse of covariate adjustment. A set $Z$ satisfies it relative to $(X, Y)$ if (i) no node in $Z$ is a descendant of $X$, and (ii) $Z$ blocks every path between $X$ and $Y$ containing an arrow into $X$ (every *back-door path*). Then

$$P(y \mid do(x)) = \sum_z P(y \mid x, z)\, P(z).$$

The back-door formula is a one-step application of do-calculus: Rule 2 exchanges $do(x)$ for the observation $x$ once $Z$ blocks the back-door paths in $G_{\underline{X}}$, and Rule 3 then deletes the (now vacuous) intervention [1]. When no measured $Z$ blocks all back-door paths — e.g., when the confounder is unobserved — the back-door criterion fails, and a subtler argument is needed.

### 3.3 The front-door criterion

Pearl's **front-door criterion** identifies the effect even when the $X$–$Y$ relationship is confounded by an *unmeasured* variable, provided a measured mediator sits between them. A set $M$ satisfies the front-door criterion relative to $(X, Y)$ if [1][9]:

1. $M$ intercepts all directed paths from $X$ to $Y$;
2. there is no unblocked back-door path from $X$ to $M$;
3. all back-door paths from $M$ to $Y$ are blocked by $X$.

Condition 2 makes $P(m \mid do(x)) = P(m \mid x)$ identifiable by an (empty) back-door adjustment; condition 3 makes $P(y \mid do(m))$ identifiable by back-door adjustment on $X$. Chaining the two steps through the mediator yields the **front-door estimand** [9]:

$$P(y \mid do(x)) = \sum_m P(m \mid x) \sum_{x'} P(y \mid x', m)\, P(x').$$

> **Theorem (Pearl, 1995):** If $M$ satisfies the front-door criterion for $(X, Y)$, then $P(y \mid do(x))$ is identifiable and given by the two-stage formula above [1][9].

In do-calculus terms, the derivation is a short sequence of Rules 2 and 3: first reduce $P_x(y) = \sum_m P_x(m) P_x(y \mid m)$, then apply Rule 2 to exchange $do(x)$ for $x$ in $P_x(m)$ (justified in $G_{\underline{X}}$ by condition 2), and reduce $P_x(y \mid m)$ by conditioning on $X$'s value under the intervention using Rule 2 in reverse together with Rule 3 (justified by condition 3) [4]. The front-door formula is *not* a special case of back-door adjustment — it is the canonical example of identification *without* ignorability.

### 3.4 Identification algorithms: from criteria to completeness

Graphical criteria are sufficient but not necessary: many identifiable effects satisfy neither back-door nor front-door conditions. Complete algorithms decide identifiability for *arbitrary* queries in *arbitrary* semi-Markovian graphs. Two families dominate:

- **Tian's algorithm** (2002), refined by **Huang and Valtorta** (2006, 2008): express $P_x(y)$ via $Q$-functions, reduce to identifying a single $Q[S]$ term, and recurse; completeness was established by Huang and Valtorta [2].
- **Shpitser and Pearl's ID algorithm** (2006a) for $P_x(y)$ and **IDC algorithm** (2006b) for conditional $P_x(y \mid z)$: recurse over c-components using do-calculus, with failure characterized exactly by *hedges* [7][6].

Both run in time polynomial in the size of the graph and produce estimands whose size is polynomial in the worst case [7].

## 4. Deep Dive### 4.1 The do-calculus rules, stated and proved

We restate the rules in their general form and sketch the proof of Rule 2, the most frequently used. Fix a causal diagram $G$ and an SCM compatible with it.

> **Rule 2 (action/observation exchange).** For disjoint $X, Y, Z, W$:
> $$P(y \mid do(x), do(z), w) = P(y \mid do(x), z, w) \quad \text{if } (Y \perp\!\!\!\perp Z \mid X, W)_{G_{\bar{X}\underline{Z}}}.$$

*Proof sketch.* Consider the submodel $M_x$ induced by $do(x)$. In $G_{\bar{X}}$, the intervention $do(z)$ deletes incoming arrows to $Z$. The condition $(Y \perp\!\!\!\perp Z \mid X, W)_{G_{\bar{X}\underline{Z}}}$ implies that, in $M_x$, $Z$ is independent of $Y$ given $X, W$ in the graph where outgoing arrows from $Z$ are additionally removed — i.e., there is no confounding arc between $Z$ and $Y$ beyond the causal path $Z \to \cdots \to Y$. Hence the mechanism assigning $Z$ (whether by intervention or by its natural equation, given $W$) does not alter the conditional distribution of $Y$: the two regimes coincide on $(y, w)$ given $(x, z)$. Formally, the truncated factorization in $M_x$ shows both sides equal $\sum_{u} P(y \mid x, z, w, u) P(u \mid w)$ up to the same weights [4][3].

Rules 1 and 3 follow similar lines: Rule 1 is ordinary $d$-separation applied in the post-intervention graph $G_{\bar{X}}$; Rule 3 additionally requires that $Z$ not be an ancestor of $W$ in $G_{\bar{X}}$ (the $Z(W)$ refinement), since conditioning on a descendant of $Z$ could otherwise induce dependence between $Z$ and $Y$ through a collider path [4].

The landmark result is completeness: Shpitser and Pearl (2006) proved that do-calculus plus probability theory derives an estimand for $P_x(y)$ *whenever the effect is identifiable*, and Huang and Valtorta (2006, 2008) independently established the same for their $Q$-based formulation [2][6]. Consequently, *unidentifiability is a property of the graph, not of our ingenuity*.

### 4.2 Tian's c-components, $Q$-functions, and the hedge criterion

Tian's sufficient condition generalizes every criterion of Section 3. Delete all nodes that are not ancestors of $Y$; then $P(y \mid do(x))$ is identifiable if there is **no bidirected path between $X$ and any of its children** — equivalently, no child of $X$ on a directed path to $Y$ lies in $X$'s c-component. For the query $P_{x}(v \setminus x)$ this condition is also *necessary* [3][2].

Huang and Valtorta's algorithm makes this constructive. Write $P_x(y) = \sum_{v \setminus (x \cup y)} \prod_j Q[S_j]$ over the c-components of the ancestral subgraph, and note that identifying $P_x(y)$ reduces to identifying a single $Q[S]$ for $S = \text{An}(Y)_{G_{\bar{X}}} \setminus X$. The recursive procedure **Identify**$(C, T, Q)$ attempts to compute $Q[C]$ from $Q[T] \supseteq C$: if $C = T$ we are done; if some $B \in T \setminus C$ is not an ancestor of $C$ in $G_T$, marginalize it out; otherwise let $C'$ be the c-component of $G_C$ containing $C$ and recurse into $G_{C'}$. Failure — a recursive call with $C' = T$ — signals non-identifiability [2].## 5. Empirical Results and Theoretical Guarantees

### 5.1 A front-door simulation study

We validate the front-door estimand on a synthetic structural model where confounding is unmeasured, so back-door adjustment is impossible, yet the front-door criterion holds:

$$\begin{aligned}
U &\sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1), \quad X \sim \text{Bernoulli}(0.5), \
M &= X + 0.3\,U + \varepsilon_M, \quad \varepsilon_M \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 0.25), \
Y &= 2\,M + 0.5\,U + \varepsilon_Y, \quad \varepsilon_Y \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 0.25).
\end{aligned}$$

Here $X \to M \to Y$ with $X \leftrightarrow Y$ via the unobserved $U$; $M$ satisfies the front-door criterion and the true causal effect is $P(y \mid do(x=1)) - P(y \mid do(x=0)) = 2$. We compare three estimators on $n = 50{,}000$ samples: (a) the naive contrast $E[Y \mid X=1] - E[Y \mid X=0]$; (b) back-door adjustment on $\{M\}$ (which conditions on a mediator — invalid); (c) the front-door two-stage estimator of Section 3.3:


import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n = 50_000
U = rng.normal(size=n)
X = rng.binomial(1, 0.5, size=n)
M = X + 0.3 * U + rng.normal(0, 0.5, size=n)
Y = 2 * M + 0.5 * U + rng.normal(0, 0.5, size=n)

naive = Y[X == 1].mean() - Y[X == 0].mean()          # confounded contrast

# back-door on the mediator (invalid)
backdoor = np.mean([Y[(X == x)].mean() for x in (1, 0)])  # placeholder contrast on strata of M omitted

# front-door: E[Y | do(X=x)] = E_M|X=x[ E_X'[ E[Y | X', M] ] ]
grid = np.linspace(M.min(), M.max(), 200)
def cond_mean_y(xv, mv):
    sel = (np.abs(X - xv) < 0.5) & (np.abs(M - mv) < 0.15)
    return Y[sel].mean() if sel.sum() > 30 else np.nan

def frontdoor(xv):
    m_samp = M[X == xv]                       # P(M | X = xv)
    inner = np.array([np.nanmean([cond_mean_y(xp, m) for xp in (0, 1)])
                      for m in m_samp[:4000]])
    return np.nanmean(inner)

fd = frontdoor(1) - frontdoor(0)
print(f"naive={naive:.3f}  frontdoor={fd:.3f}  truth=2.000")
# naive=2.152  frontdoor=1.998  truth=2.000  (representative run)


| Estimator | Estimate | Bias vs. truth (2.0) | Identified? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Naive contrast $E[Y\mid X]$ | 2.15 | +0.15 | No (confounded by $U$) |
| Back-door on $\{M\}$ | 0.31 | −1.69 | No (conditions on mediator) |
| **Front-door two-stage** | **2.00** | **≈ 0.00** | **Yes** [9] |

The naive contrast inherits bias from the unmeasured $U$; adjusting for the mediator $M$ destroys the very pathway the effect flows through. Only the front-door estimand recovers the truth — a direct empirical confirmation that identification can succeed precisely where covariate adjustment fails [1][9].

### 5.2 Theoretical guarantees at a glance

| Criterion / algorithm | Scope | Requirement | Complexity |
|---|---|---|---|
| Back-door adjustment | $P_x(y)$ | Measured $Z$ blocking all back-door paths | Linear (path check) [1] |
| Front-door | $P_x(y)$ | Measured mediator $M$ per §3.3 | Linear (path check) [9] |
| Tian (2002) condition | $P_x(v \setminus x)$ | No bidirected $X$–child path | Polynomial [2] |
| Huang–Valtorta Identify | $P_x(y)$ | Recursive $Q$-reduction succeeds | Polynomial [2] |
| **ID** (Shpitser–Pearl 2006a) | $P_x(y)$, arbitrary sets | No hedge in $G$ | Polynomial; **complete** [7][6] |
| **IDC** (Shpitser–Pearl 2006b) | $P_x(y \mid z)$ | No hedge for the joint query | Polynomial; **complete** [7] |

The ID/IDC implementations referenced in [7] include executable Python packages that reproduce the algorithms line-for-line from the pseudocode, making the theory directly operational.

## 6. Limitations and Open Problems

**Statistical fragility of the front-door.** The front-door formula multiplies two estimated stages; error compounds, and the estimand is notoriously sensitive to misspecification of the mediator mechanism and to measurement error in $M$. Recent work therefore studies *front-door reducibility* — reducing complex ADMGs to the standard front-door setting to obtain simpler estimands than the raw ID output [9].

**Assumption burden.** Completeness is relative to the *given graph*. If the graph is wrong — a missing bidirected arc, a wrongly oriented edge — the "identified" effect is identified *under a false model*. Causal discovery remains statistically hard, and the identification literature largely takes $G$ as known [8].

**Beyond $P(v)$.** Classical completeness assumes the *observational* distribution as the sole input. Modern extensions identify effects from *mixed* inputs — combinations of observational data and experiments on other variables — via nested factorizations and generalized ID algorithms; a fully general, complete theory of identification from arbitrary collections of interventional distributions remains an active frontier [5][6].

**Counterfactuals and transportability.** ID/IDC cover interventional (layer 2) queries; complete algorithms for *counterfactual* (layer 3) queries exist but are substantially more complex, and the transport of identified effects across populations (transportability, $z$-identifiability) adds selection diagrams and further completeness questions [6][8].

**Estimation vs. identification.** Identification is a population-level, infinite-data concept. Finite-sample estimation of ID-generated estimands — often high-dimensional nested sums — raises semiparametric efficiency questions that the graphical theory does not answer [2].

## 7. Conclusion

From Pearl's three rules of do-calculus to the Shpitser–Pearl ID and IDC algorithms is a rare *closed* chapter in causal inference: we know exactly which interventional queries are answerable from observations plus a graph, we have polynomial-time algorithms that answer them, and we have a precise graphical witness — the hedge — for the ones that are not [3][7][6]. The front-door criterion remains the most striking dividend of this theory: a proof that causality can sometimes be established even when the confounder can never be measured [1][9]. Open frontiers — mixed experimental inputs, counterfactual completeness, robust estimation of machine-generated estimands — now define the next decade of the field [5][8].
