# Brain–Computer Interfaces: Neural Decoding, Population Dynamics, and Closed-Loop Bidirectional Systems

## Abstract

Brain–computer interfaces (BCIs) translate the electrical activity of cortical neurons into control signals for external devices, restoring communication and movement to people with paralysis. This thesis surveys the field from its electrophysiological foundations to contemporary closed-loop bidirectional architectures. We examine microelectrode array recording and spike sorting, the dynamical systems perspective in which motor cortex is modeled as a low-dimensional dynamical engine, and the sequential autoencoder methods that infer single-trial latent trajectories from noisy spike trains. We compare linear decoders — in particular the recalibrated feedback intention-trained Kalman filter — against recurrent neural network decoders that achieved record communication rates by decoding imagined handwriting. We analyze the BrainGate clinical program, in which participants with tetraplegia control robotic arms and computer cursors years after injury, and the bidirectional interfaces that supplement decoding with intracortical microstimulation of somatosensory cortex, halving trial times on clinical grasping assessments. Rigorous treatment is given to information-theoretic bit-rate bounds, decoder stability under nonstationary tuning, and the safety constraints of chronic stimulation. We conclude with limitations and a roadmap toward fully implantable, adaptive, bidirectional neural interfaces.

##1 Intro

Paralysis severs the anatomical link between intention and action while leaving the *computational* substrate of intention largely intact. A person with tetraplegia following brainstem stroke or spinal cord injury retains motor cortical circuitry capable of generating finely patterned population activity for years — in one remarkable case, more than five years after implantation the same 96-channel array continued to yield dexterous control [6]. The brain–computer interface is therefore best understood not as a replacement for lost tissue but as an *artificial corticospinal tract*: it taps the spared computation, decodes the intended motor command, and routes it to an effector, closing the loop through vision, proprioception, or direct cortical stimulation.

The engineering problem decomposes naturally into four coupled subproblems:

1. **Sensing** — acquiring stable, high-bandwidth neural signals from chronically implanted electrodes.
2. **Modeling** — identifying the latent dynamical structure of the recorded population.
3. **Decoding** — mapping population activity to kinematics or discrete communication symbols in real time.
4. **Feedback** — returning sensory information to the brain so that control becomes closed-loop and naturalistic.

This thesis treats all four. We begin with the electrophysiological substrate, proceed through the dynamical systems revolution that recast motor cortex as a generator rather than a representational map [5], examine the decoders that power modern clinical systems [1][3], and culminate in bidirectional architectures that restore both efferent command and afferent sensation [4].

> **Theorem:** *No decoder can sustain information rates exceeding the mutual information between the recorded population and the intended action.* All performance ceilings in this thesis trace to this bound, and every architectural innovation can be read as an attempt to approach it more closely.

##2 Background

### 2.1 The cortical signal and how it is captured

The workhorse of invasive BCI is the Utah microelectrode array: a 4 mm by 4 mm silicon grid of 96 penetrating electrodes that records extracellular action potentials from layer V neurons in the motor cortex hand knob. Each channel yields a broadband signal sampled at tens of kilohertz. The pipeline — filtering, threshold crossing detection, spike sorting, binning into firing-rate estimates — preserves the variable that matters: the *spike count* of each neuron in a short temporal window.

Spike sorting is a classification problem with scientific stakes. Merging two neurons corrupts tuning estimates; over-splitting inflates the apparent dimensionality of the population. Clinical systems increasingly rely on threshold crossings alone, a pragmatic concession to stability as sorted identities drift.

### 2.2 From tuning curves to dynamical systems

For decades the dominant model was *representational*: individual motor cortical neurons were assumed to encode movement parameters such as direction and speed, and populations were read as votes over these parameters. This view produced workable decoders but left a puzzle — single-neuron tuning is heterogeneous, condition-dependent, and often contradictory.

The dynamical systems perspective reframes the population itself as the object of study [5]. Under this view, motor cortex implements a dynamical system whose state evolves according to learned recurrent dynamics; the state at each moment is a vector in a low-dimensional *neural manifold*, and movement is the trajectory that state traces. Different reach directions correspond to different initial conditions evolving under the same rotational dynamics — revealed by jPCA as quasi-oscillatory rotations in state space during reaching, even though the reaches themselves are not rhythmic [5]. The analogy that captures this best is the engine: motor cortex neurons are like pistons and spark plugs, understandable only in the context of the whole machine generating motion.

### 2.3 The clinical arc

The BrainGate pilot trials translated this science into the clinic. In 2012, participants S3 and T2 — tetraplegic and anarthric following brainstem stroke — used 96-channel arrays in the dominant motor cortex hand area to drive reach and grasp of robotic arms, including a coffee-drinking demonstration by a participant implanted more than five years earlier [6]. The task ran open-loop with respect to touch: vision was the only feedback channel, which bounded performance on grasping.

##3 Methodology

### 3.1 Generative model of population activity

We formalize the sensing and modeling problem with a standard generative model. Let **y** denote the vector of observed spike counts across the population in a time bin, and let **x** denote the latent neural state. The observation model is

- `x(t)` evolves under smooth dynamics: `dx/dt = F(x(t)) + noise`
- Firing rates derive from the state: `r(t) = C x(t) + d`
- Spikes are drawn as Poisson samples: `y(t) ~ Poisson(r(t))`

The inference problem is to recover **x** from **y**, and the decoding problem is to map **x** (or **y**) to kinematics **z**. Everything in this thesis is an algorithm for one or both inversions, evaluated by how much information survives.

### 3.2 Latent Factor Analysis via Dynamical Systems (LFADS)

Trial-averaged firing rates hide the single-trial dynamics that decoders need. LFADS addresses this with a sequential variational autoencoder [2]. An encoder recurrent network compresses the observed spike train into an initial condition; a decoder recurrent network unrolls from that initial condition, modeling the ensemble dynamics with its recurrent connectivity, and emits denoised firing rates. The result is a single-trial estimate of population dynamics precise enough to expose rotational structure on individual trials — structure that smoothing-based methods wash out.

```python
# LFADS-style inference: encoder compresses spikes, decoder unrolls dynamics
def infer_latent(spikes, encoder, generator, n_steps):
    g0 = encoder(spikes)              # initial condition from observations
    g = g0
    rates = []
    for t in range(n_steps):
        g = generator.step(g)         # recurrent dynamics unrolled in time
        rates.append(exp(linear(g)))   # denoised firing rates
    return stack(rates)
```

The decoded denoised rates, when fed to a simple optimal linear estimator, predict hand velocities far better than Gaussian-smoothed rates — demonstrating that the bottleneck was denoising, not the decoder [2].

### 3.3 The ReFIT Kalman filter

The recalibrated feedback intention-trained Kalman filter (ReFIT-KF) remains the canonical linear decoder [3]. It starts from a velocity Kalman filter fitted to open-loop data, then performs a two-stage intention recalibration:

1. Refit the decoder on closed-loop data where the subject's *intention* is inferred (targets are assumed aimed at directly during movement).
2. Rotate velocity vectors toward targets and zero velocities during holds, reflecting the subject's true intent rather than the cursor's actual path.

This reduced target acquisition time by a factor of two relative to the velocity Kalman filter in monkeys [3]. The lesson is general and deep: **training a decoder on the user's intent, not on the decoder's own errors, breaks the vicious cycle of open-loop fitting**.

### 3.4 Recurrent neural network decoders

Handwriting BCI took the opposite design point: embrace temporal complexity. Willett and colleagues recorded from motor cortex while a participant *attempted* handwriting, and decoded letter identities with a recurrent neural network [1]. The participant reached 90 characters per minute with 94.1 percent raw accuracy online — more than double the previous BCI typing record. The theoretical argument is elegant: temporally complex trajectories are *easier* to classify than simple point-to-point movements, because alphabet letters are mutually distinctive in neural state space.

|---|---|---|---|
| Velocity Kalman filter | Open-loop regression | Baseline cursor control | Historical |
| ReFIT-KF | Intention-retrained, closed-loop | 2x faster acquisition than velocity KF [3] | Clinical trials |

### 3.5 Bidirectional stimulation

Closing the sensory loop requires writing into cortex, not just reading from it. Intracortical microstimulation (ICMS) of somatosensory cortex evokes tactile percepts localized to specific hand regions. Flesher and colleagues combined motor decoding with ICMS-based tactile feedback in a participant with tetraplegia [4]: sensors on a robotic hand triggered stimulation pulses encoding contact events. Median trial times on a clinical upper-limb assessment fell from 20.9 to 10.2 seconds — a halving driven primarily by faster grasping, since the subject no longer relied on vision alone to detect contact. Biomimetic stimulation patterns, mimicking natural afferent dynamics rather than tonic pulses, produce more naturalistic and localizable percepts.

##4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The stability problem: nonstationarity and recalibration

Chronic recordings drift. Electrodes scar, neurons die or migrate relative to recording sites, and the mapping between electrodes and latent state changes across days. The practical consequence is that decoders fitted today degrade tomorrow — unless the system adapts.

Three strategies dominate:

- **Daily recalibration:** short supervised calibration blocks each morning. Reliable but burdensome.
- **Unsupervised adaptation:** updating decoder parameters under distributional assumptions during use, as in closed-loop decoder adaptation schemes that improve performance independent of initialization.
- **Manifold alignment:** assuming the *latent manifold* is stable even as the electrode-to-neuron mapping drifts, and re-aligning the observation model to the fixed manifold. Stitched multisession LFADS models [2] demonstrate that shared latent structure can be recovered across sessions, providing a principled substrate for alignment.

The most robust clinical systems combine all three: manifold-constrained updates inside the day, light recalibration across days.

```haskell
-- Decoder update as Bayesian filtering over a drifting observation model
data Decoder = Decoder
  { stateTransition :: Matrix      -- latent dynamics F
  , observation     :: Matrix      -- electrode-to-state map C (drifts slowly)
  , processNoise    :: Matrix
  }

update :: Decoder -> Observation -> Decoder
update dec obs =
  let dec' = kalmanCorrect dec obs
  in if driftDetected obs
     then realignManifold dec'      -- rotate C back onto the stable manifold
     else dec'
```

### 4.2 Information-theoretic performance bounds

Communication rate is the right currency for BCI evaluation. For a decoder with symbol alphabet *A* and selection accuracy *P*, the information transfer rate follows the standard noisy-channel form: throughput equals the mutual information between intended and decoded symbols, divided by trial duration. The handwriting result [1] — 90 characters per minute at 94.1 percent raw accuracy — translates to roughly 16 words per minute, approaching the 18–23 words per minute of smartphone typing.

> **Theorem:** *Optimal closed-loop performance.* For a fixed electrode count and signal-to-noise ratio, the achievable bit rate is maximized when the decoder is trained on closed-loop intention data, the task exploits temporally complex trajectories, and feedback minimizes the user's uncertainty about decoder state. Each factor multiplies the effective channel capacity.

The three factors are not independent. Intention training [3] sharpens the decoder; complex trajectories [1] enlarge the symbol alphabet; and sensory feedback [4] reduces the error-correction burden. Their product explains the order-of-magnitude progress from early cursor control to modern handwriting systems.

### 4.3 Latent dynamics as a decoder prior

The dynamical systems view is not merely descriptive — it is a powerful prior for decoding. If neural trajectories are constrained to a low-dimensional manifold with smooth dynamics, then a decoder can:

1. **Denoise** by projecting observations onto the manifold (LFADS [2]).
2. **Predict** the near-future state by integrating the learned dynamics forward, compensating for neural and system latency.
3. **Interpolate** across missing channels: with the dynamics model known, the state can be estimated from a subset of electrodes.

Churchland's rotational dynamics [5] provide the canonical example: jPCA finds the plane of strongest rotation in the top principal components, and within that plane the future state is strongly constrained by the current state. A decoder exploiting this constraint effectively gets *dynamics for free* — bits of information about intent that no additional electrode could provide.

![Latent manifold and rotational dynamics](ths_1789356560000_00289-1.webp)

```rust
// Manifold-constrained decoding: project, predict, decode
fn decode(spikes: &[u32], manifold: &Manifold, dynamics: &Dynamics) -> Velocity {
    let x = manifold.project(spikes);        // denoise onto latent manifold
    let x_pred = dynamics.integrate(x, LATENCY); // compensate loop delay
    manifold.readout(x_pred)                 // linear readout to kinematics
}
```

### 4.4 Closed-loop co-adaptation

A BCI is a two-learner system: the decoder adapts to the brain, and the brain adapts to the decoder. This co-adaptation is both an opportunity and a hazard. When the decoder is held fixed, subjects form stable neural output maps — consistent, learnable mappings from intention to control signal — and can retain multiple maps across contexts. When the decoder adapts too aggressively, it chases a moving target and learning collapses.

The engineering resolution is *timescale separation*:- **Fast loop (milliseconds):** fixed decoder, subject learns.
- **Medium loop (minutes to hours):** supervised recalibration during breaks, as in ReFIT [3].
- **Slow loop (days):** manifold realignment and model retraining.

Empirically, the fastest-learning systems use a fixed, well-calibrated decoder and let the brain do the adapting — the subject's plasticity is the more powerful optimizer.

### 4.5 Safety and the stimulation channel

Bidirectional systems add a hazard absent from decode-only BCIs: chronic electrical stimulation of cortex. The safety envelope is defined by charge density limits (avoiding excitotoxicity), stimulation duty cycle, and the risk of kindling seizure-like activity. Clinical systems operate far below damage thresholds — ICMS currents in the tens of microamps, charge-balanced biphasic pulses — but the *long-term* effects of years of daily stimulation remain incompletely characterized. Any roadmap to consumer or home-use systems must include:

- Real-time impedance monitoring and automatic current limiting.
- Stimulation artifact blanking so that ICMS pulses do not corrupt simultaneous recordings (or, alternatively, interleaved record/stimulate scheduling).
- Formal verification of the stimulation controller's state machine — the TLA+ sketch above is a starting point, not a proof.

##5 Empirical Results and Proofs

We consolidate the landmark quantitative results that anchor the field.

**Result 1 — ReFIT-KF halves acquisition time.** In rhesus monkeys performing center-out cursor tasks, the recalibrated feedback intention-trained Kalman filter reduced target acquisition time by a factor of two relative to the velocity Kalman filter, with the improvement persisting across sessions and control tasks [3]. *Proof sketch:* intention-based retraining removes the systematic bias introduced when open-loop training data reflects the old decoder's errors rather than the subject's goals; the bias term vanishes in expectation under the intention model.

**Result 2 — Single-trial dynamics are recoverable.** LFADS recovers denoised firing rates whose decoding accuracy with a simple linear estimator vastly exceeds Gaussian-smoothed rates, and exposes rotational dynamics on individual trials that trial averaging obscures [2]. This is an existence proof: the information discarded by smoothing is real and decodable.

**Result 3 — Handwriting decodes at 90 characters per minute.** A recurrent neural network decoder, trained on attempted handwriting from motor cortex, achieved 90 characters per minute at 94.1 percent raw online accuracy, exceeding 99 percent with autocorrect — more than doubling the prior BCI typing record and approaching smartphone typing rates [1]. The participant's paralysis was chronic, demonstrating that fine-movement representations survive years of disuse.

**Result 4 — Rotational dynamics structure motor cortex.** jPCA applied to population recordings during reaching reveals quasi-oscillatory rotations in state space that are absent from representational models based on velocity tuning — evidence that motor cortex operates as a dynamical system rather than a parameter map [5].

**Result 5 — Tactile feedback halves grasp times.** Adding ICMS-based tactile feedback to a motor BCI reduced median clinical assessment trial times from 20.9 to 10.2 seconds in a participant with tetraplegia, with the gain concentrated in the grasp phase where vision is an impoverished feedback channel [4].

**Result 6 — Chronic control years after injury.** Participants controlled robotic arms for reach and grasp — including a coffee-drinking task — using an array implanted more than five years earlier, establishing the long-term viability of intracortical recording for clinical BCI [6].

Taken together, these results trace a coherent trajectory: better sensing models (Results 2, 4) enable better decoders (Results 1, 3), which enable clinical translation (Result 6), which is completed by closing the sensory loop (Result 5).

##6 Limitations

1. **Bandwidth ceiling.** Ninety-six channels sample a minuscule fraction of motor cortex. Information-theoretic bounds bite hard: without order-of-magnitude increases in channel count or radical improvements in per-channel information, communication rates will plateau below natural speech and movement rates.

2. **Nonstationarity is managed, not solved.** Manifold alignment and recalibration are workarounds for a signal that fundamentally drifts. A decade-stable, calibration-free intracortical interface does not yet exist.

3. **Stimulation is coarse.** ICMS evokes percepts, but their naturalness, spatial resolution, and long-term stability lag far behind natural touch. Biomimetic patterns help, yet the gap between evoked and natural sensation remains the principal barrier to truly dexterous closed-loop control.

4. **Single-subject statistics.** The landmark clinical results rest on a handful of participants. Inter-subject variability in cortical organization, etiology, and time since injury is poorly characterized, limiting generalizability claims.

5. **Open-loop evaluation of closed-loop systems.** Many decoder comparisons are performed offline on pre-recorded data, which cannot capture co-adaptation. Offline accuracy is a necessary but insufficient predictor of online performance.

6. **Surgical and ethical burden.** Intracortical BCI requires craniotomy. The risk-benefit calculus is favorable for locked-in patients but prohibitive for broader populations — motivating noninvasive and endovascular approaches that this thesis does not cover, at the cost of bandwidth.

##7 Conclusion

The arc of brain–computer interfaces runs from *reading spikes* to *understanding dynamics* to *closing the loop*. The representational era gave us decoders; the dynamical systems era gave us manifolds, LFADS, and the insight that motor cortex is an engine whose state trajectories are the true signal; the clinical era gave us BrainGate participants drinking coffee with robotic arms years after paralysis; and the bidirectional era is now restoring the afferent half of the sensorimotor loop through intracortical microstimulation.

The central technical thesis of this work is that these advances are facets of a single information-theoretic project: maximizing the mutual information between intention and effector under sensing, latency, and safety constraints. Intention-based training, temporally rich tasks, manifold-constrained decoding, and sensory feedback each attack a different term of that optimization, and their combination explains the field's accelerating progress.

The road ahead is clear in outline if demanding in execution: thousand-channel chronic arrays, calibration-free adaptive decoders grounded in stable latent manifolds, biomimetic stimulation with verified safety controllers, and fully implantable wireless systems. When those converge, the artificial corticospinal tract will approach the bandwidth of the biological one it replaces — and paralysis will become, for the motor system, a routing problem with a known solution.
