# Type-Theoretic Certainty: Formalized Mathematics in Lean 4, from Dependent Types to Machine-Checked Proofs of the Four Color Theorem and the Kepler Conjecture

## Abstract

Machine-checked proof has crossed from logic seminar into mathematical production. This article surveys formalized mathematics through Lean 4 and its library Mathlib — a dependent type theory with quotient types and classical axioms, whose tactic framework and elaborator let mathematicians write proofs a small trusted kernel independently checks. We trace the theoretical substrate, the Curry-Howard correspondence identifying propositions with types and proofs with programs, and the de Bruijn criterion that confines trust to a few thousand lines of kernel code. Two landmark verifications anchor the narrative: Georges Gonthier's complete formalization of the Four Color Theorem in Coq, which tamed a 633-configuration computational case analysis, and the Flyspeck project's machine verification of the Kepler conjecture in HOL Light and Isabelle, closing a 400-year-old problem with half a million lines of formal text. We examine Mathlib's monolithic, continuously integrated library design, its blueprint methodology, and its sorry-free axiom audits, and we report modern milestones — the Feit-Thompson theorem, the irrationality of Apéry's constant, the Ramanujan–Nagell equation — and Lean4Lean, an independent typechecker for Lean written in Lean itself.

---

## 1 Introduction

The formalization of mathematics — the encoding of definitions, theorems, and proofs in a language in which every inference is mechanically verifiable — has undergone a transformation in the last three decades. What began as the province of logicians, with systems like Automath and Mizar, has become a technology that settles century-old disputes and reorganizes how large mathematical collaborations operate [6]. The engine of this change is the *interactive theorem prover*: a program that pairs a small, trusted logical kernel with an elaborator and tactic language generous enough that working mathematicians can actually write proofs in it.

The stakes are not merely philosophical. In 1998, Thomas Hales announced a proof of the Kepler conjecture on optimal sphere packing, first posed in 1611. The resulting 300-page argument, threaded through some 180,000 lines of custom computer code, took twelve referees four years to review — and they emerged only 99 percent certain [3]. Hales's response was the Flyspeck project: re-derive the entire proof inside proof assistants whose kernels are small enough to audit, and let the machine certify what human referees could not. Completed in 2014 and published in 2017, the formal proof ran to roughly half a million lines of formal text checked by HOL Light and Isabelle [3][4].

A decade earlier, Georges Gonthier had completed a fully machine-checked proof of the Four Color Theorem in Coq, based on the Robertson–Seymour–Sanders–Thomas reducibility argument with its 633 unavoidable configurations [1][2]. And in 2012, Gonthier's team finished a six-year formalization of the Feit-Thompson odd order theorem — some 170,000 lines of Coq — demonstrating that even deep structural group theory could be captured mechanically [7].

The current center of gravity, however, has shifted to Lean. Lean 4, designed by Leonardo de Moura, combines a dependent type theory with quotient types and classical reasoning, a metaprogramming framework in which tactics are ordinary Lean programs, and Mathlib: a single, monolithic, continuously integrated library of formalized mathematics with hundreds of contributors. Recent Lean formalizations — Beukers's proof of the irrationality of Apéry's constant [8], the Ramanujan–Nagell theorem on the exponential Diophantine equation [9] — show the system absorbing analytic number theory at a pace that would have been unthinkable a decade ago. Meanwhile Lean4Lean provides the first complete independent typechecker for Lean written in Lean itself, a step toward a verified kernel [5].

This article is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews the logical background: the Curry-Howard correspondence, the landscape of proof assistants, and the de Bruijn criterion. Section 3 describes the methodology of modern formalization: tactics, elaboration, blueprints, and axiom auditing. Section 4 deep-dives into dependent type theory, the Four Color formalization, Flyspeck, and Mathlib. Section 5 surveys empirical milestones, Section 6 the limitations, and Section 7 concludes.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The Curry-Howard correspondence

The conceptual keystone of type-theoretic proof assistants is the Curry-Howard correspondence: propositions are types, proofs are programs, and proof checking is type checking. Under this identification, implication `A → B` is the function type, conjunction is the product type, and a proof of `∀ x : α, P x` is a dependent function mapping each `x` to evidence for `P x`. To *prove* is to *construct a term*; to *verify* is to *typecheck*.

This correspondence is not a metaphor but a design principle with teeth. Because proof terms are ordinary data, they can be computed with: proof by reflection — deciding a proposition by evaluating a verified decision procedure — becomes a legitimate and extremely powerful proof method. Gonthier's Four Color proof exploits this relentlessly: the 633-case reducibility check is not 633 hand-written arguments but a single boolean computation whose correctness the kernel reduces to definitional equality [1].

### 2.2 The proof assistant landscape

| System | Logic | Kernel style | Landmark result |
|---|---|---|---|
| Coq | Calculus of Inductive Constructions | Small kernel in OCaml | Four Color Theorem [1][2]; Feit-Thompson [7] |
| HOL Light | Classical higher-order logic | ~400-line ML kernel | Kepler conjecture, Flyspeck [3][4] |
| Isabelle/HOL | Classical higher-order logic | LCF-style kernel | Kepler conjecture, Flyspeck [4]; seL4 |
| Lean 4 | Dependent type theory with quotients and choice | Small kernel; independent checker in Lean4Lean [5] | Mathlib; Ramanujan–Nagell [9] |

Two architectural philosophies are visible. The *LCF tradition* (HOL Light, Isabelle) keeps the kernel microscopic — every theorem must pass through a handful of primitive inference rules — and builds automation outside it. The *type-theoretic tradition* (Coq, Lean) admits dependent types, inductive families, and computation inside the logic itself, so definitional unfolding can discharge proof obligations silently. Both satisfy the de Bruijn criterion.

### 2.3 The de Bruijn criterion

> Theorem (de Bruijn criterion, informal): *A proof assistant is trustworthy if the correctness of any proof it accepts depends only on the correctness of a small, independently auditable kernel.*

In practice this means the elaborator — the million-line component that parses tactic scripts, infers implicit arguments, unifies metavariables, and synthesizes proof terms — is *untrusted*. Whatever it produces, the kernel re-checks from scratch. A bug in a tactic can only fail to find a proof; it cannot manufacture a false one, because the kernel would reject the resulting term. Lean4Lean sharpens this guarantee: it is the first complete external typechecker for Lean 4 besides the reference C++ implementation, written in Lean itself, competitive in performance (20 to 50 percent slower), and capable of verifying all of Mathlib — opening the door to proving metatheorems *about* the kernel inside the system [5].

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 Tactic-driven proof development

No mathematician writes raw proof terms. Instead, proofs are developed *tactically*: the user issues commands that transform the current goal into simpler subgoals, and the system assembles the corresponding proof term behind the scenes. A typical Lean 4 proof has the following shape:

```lean
theorem nat_add_comm (m n : Nat) : m + n = n + m := by
  induction m with
  | zero => simp
  | succ k ih => rw [Nat.succ_add, Nat.add_succ, ih]
```

Here `induction` splits the goal by cases on `m`, `simp` discharges the base case by simplification, and `rw` rewrites with lemmas. Each tactic is a metaprogram — in Lean 4, literally a Lean program running in the `TacticM` monad — with access to the full elaborator. Crucially, tactics may fail, loop, or produce garbage; none of that threatens soundness, because the kernel checks the final term. This division of labor — *clever, untrusted search* plus *dumb, trusted checking* — is what makes large formalizations feasible.

### 3.2 Elaboration and the trusted base

Elaboration bridges human-friendly surface syntax and kernel terms: it inserts implicit arguments, resolves overloaded notation, elaborates pattern matching into recursors, and unifies metavariables. Because elaboration is untrusted, its output must typecheck. The disciplined workflow therefore includes an *axiom audit*: Lean's `#print axioms` command lists every axiom a theorem depends on. A theorem that should be constructive must not secretly depend on the axiom of choice; a `sorry`-free, audit-clean development is the gold standard, and Mathlib enforces it in continuous integration.

### 3.3 Blueprints and the social machinery

Large formalizations are software projects as much as mathematics. The community has converged on a methodology:

1. **Blueprint**: a LaTeX document stating every definition, lemma, and theorem with dependency edges, often rendered as an interactive graph (as in the Ramanujan–Nagell project [9]).
2. **Modular formalization**: contributors claim blueprint nodes and formalize them independently against stated interfaces.
3. **Continuous integration**: every pull request rebuilds the library; Mathlib's CI checks that all proofs still elaborate and that no `sorry` remains.
4. **Refactoring toward generality**: lemmas are generalized after the fact — a specific computation becomes an instance of an abstract algebraic structure — because the library rewards reuse.

This workflow explains Mathlib's explosive growth: a decentralized community of hundreds of authors contributing to one coherent, always-compiling library, rather than scattered incompatible developments.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Dependent type theory: the logical substrate

Lean 4's logic is a dependent type theory — the Calculus of Inductive Constructions extended with quotient types — augmented by three classical axioms: propositional extensionality, function extensionality, and the axiom of choice. Dependent types mean types may depend on values: `Vec α n`, the type of length-`n` vectors over `α`, is a type *indexed by* the natural number `n`. This lets specifications live inside types: a sorting function can be given the type `∀ l : List α, Sorted (sort l)`, so that *typechecking the implementation proves it correct*.

Universe polymorphism organizes types into a hierarchy `Type 0 : Type 1 : Type 2 : …`, avoiding Girard's paradox while letting definitions work uniformly across levels. Inductive types — naturals, lists, trees — come with automatically generated recursors, and the kernel's definitional equality includes computation: `2 + 2` and `4` are equal *by definition*, so a proof of `2 + 2 = 4` is the single term `rfl`. Quotient types, the one extension beyond the pure Calculus of Inductive Constructions, let mathematicians form `ℝ` as Cauchy sequences modulo equivalence, or a group as a set modulo a congruence — indispensable for everyday mathematics.

The three classical axioms are quarantined rather than hidden: they are available, their use is tracked by `#print axioms`, and large parts of Mathlib remain axiom-free or choice-free by construction. This explicit bookkeeping is a distinctive strength of the Lean methodology.

### 4.2 The Four Color Theorem in Coq

> Theorem (Four Color, Gonthier [1]): *Every planar map is four-colorable — formalized as: the vertices of any planar finite graph admit a proper coloring with four colors.*

The Four Color Theorem states that any map drawn in the plane can be colored with four colors so that adjacent regions differ. Appel and Haken's 1976 proof, refined by Robertson, Sanders, Seymour, and Thomas, reduces the problem to checking that a set of 633 configurations is *reducible* — that no minimal counterexample can contain any of them — plus an unavoidability argument showing every planar triangulation contains one [1].

Gonthier's Coq formalization, completed in 2005 and fully checked by Coq version 7.3.1, confronted two challenges [1][2]. First, the *statement* itself had to be formalized: planarity, hypermaps, and combinatorial embeddings were developed from scratch in the Calculus of Inductive Constructions, using Coq's dependent types to encode the combinatorial structure of maps. Second, the *computation*: the 633 reducibility checks, which in the original proof were performed by custom C programs, were reimplemented as verified Coq computations. Because Coq's kernel evaluates terms during typechecking, each check reduced to verifying that a boolean function returned `true` — proof by reflection at industrial scale.

The payoff was epistemological. The Appel–Haken proof had always carried a residue of doubt: who checks the checkers? Gonthier's account made the answer precise — a small, auditable kernel checks everything, including the programs that check the cases — and his 2008 *Notices of the AMS* exposition remains the clearest statement of what formal proof buys mathematics [1].

### 4.3 The Kepler conjecture and Flyspeck

> Theorem (Kepler, Hales et al. [3]): *No packing of congruent balls in three-dimensional Euclidean space has density exceeding that of the face-centered cubic packing.*

First asserted by Kepler in 1611, the conjecture resisted proof for nearly four centuries. Hales's 1998 proof combined classical geometry with massive computation: roughly 100,000 linear programs in up to a few hundred variables, derived from a hybrid Voronoi–Delaunay decomposition and a delicate "score" function bounding local density [4]. When twelve referees spent four years and could certify only 99 percent confidence, Hales launched Flyspeck — *Formal Proof of Kepler* — in 2003, aiming to remove the residual uncertainty by machine [3].

Flyspeck's architecture differed from Gonthier's. Rather than one system, it used *two* proof assistants — HOL Light and Isabelle — so that independent kernels cross-checked the development. The formal text ultimately ran to about half a million lines, covering tame graph enumeration, nonlinear inequality verification by interval arithmetic, and the full linear programming certificates. In August 2014 the checkers reported success; the formal proof was published in 2017 with DOI 10.1017/fmp.2017.1 [3].

Flyspeck demonstrated something the Four Color formalization had only suggested: that *no* proof, however computational, lies beyond mechanical certification in principle. It also exposed the cost — eleven years of work by a large team — which is precisely the cost curve Lean and Mathlib are engineered to bend.

### 4.4 Mathlib and the Lean 4 renaissance

Mathlib is the largest single coherent body of formalized mathematics ever assembled: hundreds of thousands of theorems spanning algebra, topology, analysis, number theory, and category theory, in one repository, with every commit verified by continuous integration. Its design decisions are sociological as much as technical:

- **Monolithic, not federated.** One library, one set of conventions, one build. Contributors extend shared abstractions rather than forking private ones.
- **Generalize aggressively.** A lemma about natural numbers is restated for monoids, then for categories, as soon as the proof permits; the library's algebraic hierarchy (`Monoid`, `Group`, `Ring`, `Field`, …) is the skeleton everything hangs on.
- **Tactic infrastructure as library.** Tools like `simp` (conditional rewriting with a huge lemma database), `omega` (linear arithmetic), and `aesop` (proof search) are developed alongside the mathematics and tested against it.
- **Classical by default, constructive by audit.** Classical reasoning is available everywhere, but `#print axioms` tracks dependence, so constructive fragments are identifiable.

Lean 4 itself was rewritten from Lean 3 with metaprogramming as a first-class citizen: tactics, elaborators, and even domain-specific languages are written in Lean, compiled efficiently, and documented as ordinary code. The result is a system where automation and mathematics co-evolve — each hard formalization, from the irrationality of ζ(3) [8] to the Ramanujan–Nagell theorem [9], deposits reusable automation back into the library.

## 5 Empirical Results / Proofs

| Result | System | Scale / method | Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| Four Color Theorem | Coq | ~60,000 lines; 633 configurations by reflection | Complete [1][2] |
| Feit-Thompson odd order theorem | Coq / SSReflect | ~170,000 lines; six years | Complete [7] |
| Kepler conjecture | HOL Light + Isabelle | ~500,000 lines; interval arithmetic, LP certificates | Complete [3][4] |
| Irrationality of ζ(3) | Lean 4 / Mathlib | Beukers's integral method | Complete [8] |
| Ramanujan–Nagell theorem | Lean 4 / Mathlib | Class number of the quadratic field Q(√−7) | Complete [9] |
| Lean4Lean external checker | Lean 4 | Verifies all of Mathlib; 20–50% slower than reference | Complete [5] |

Several patterns emerge. First, *reflection scales*: wherever a proof contains a large finite computation — 633 configurations, thousands of tame graphs, interval subdivisions — encoding the check as a verified computation inside the logic beats hand-writing cases by orders of magnitude [1][3]. Second, *libraries compound*: the Feit-Thompson project formalized a substantial textbook of finite group theory along the way, and each Lean analytic-number-theory project enriches Mathlib's shared infrastructure [7][8]. Third, *independent checking is now routine*: Flyspeck's dual-kernel cross-check anticipated Lean4Lean's independent typechecker, which guards the entire Mathlib corpus against kernel bugs [4][5].

The Lean analytic program deserves emphasis. The irrationality of ζ(3) — Apéry's constant — was formalized following Beukers's streamlined integral proof, closing a gap where Lean had lagged Isabelle in analysis [8]. The Ramanujan–Nagell theorem, characterizing the integer solutions of the exponential Diophantine equation x² + 7 = 2ⁿ as exactly the five known pairs, required computing the class number and unit group of the ring of integers of Q(√−7) — serious algebraic number theory, machine-checked end to end, with an interactive dependency graph and blueprint [9]. These are not toy examples; they are research-level mathematics whose formalizations other systems took years longer to reach, if at all.

## 6 Limitations

The program is not without honest limits.

**The kernel is small, not verified.** The de Bruijn criterion confines trust to the kernel, but the kernel itself — tens of thousands of lines of C++ in Lean's case — is *audited*, not *proved correct*. Lean4Lean narrows this gap by providing an independent checker written in Lean, with initial steps toward formalizing the type theory itself, but a fully verified kernel remains future work [5]. A soundness bug in the kernel would be catastrophic precisely because everything rests on it.

**Elaboration is powerful and fragile.** Implicit argument inference, typeclass resolution, and tactic metaprograms form a large, untrusted, occasionally unpredictable surface. Proofs that elaborate today may need repair after elaborator changes; Mathlib's CI absorbs this cost, but it is real, and debugging a failing `simp` call across a million-line library demands expertise.

**The formalization gap.** A machine-checked proof certifies a *formal statement*, and the correspondence between the formal statement and the intended informal theorem is itself informal. Definitions can be wrong in ways the kernel cannot see — a misformalized "planar graph" would make the Four Color formalization vacuous. Gonthier and Hales both invested heavily in validating statements against the literature, but the gap cannot be closed by computation alone [1][3].

**Classical axioms and constructivity.** Lean's quotient types and choice axiom buy enormous convenience at the price of computational content: from a classical existence proof, no algorithm can be extracted. For pure mathematics this is irrelevant; for program verification it matters, and the community tracks axiom dependence explicitly to manage it.

**Cost and expertise.** Even with modern automation, formalization remains labor-intensive — the oft-quoted *de Bruijn factor* of roughly four means a formal proof is about four times as long as its informal counterpart. Until automation improves by another order of magnitude, large-scale formalization will remain a specialist activity.

## 7 Conclusion

From Gonthier's 633 reflected computations in Coq [1], through Flyspeck's half-million-line dual-kernel verification of the Kepler conjecture [3][4], to Mathlib's continuously integrated library and Lean4Lean's self-hosted typechecker [5], formalized mathematics has matured from a logical curiosity into an engineering discipline. The methodology is now clear: dependent types to say exactly what is meant, tactics to search for proofs at human scale, a small kernel to check everything, blueprints to organize collaboration, and axiom audits to keep the foundations honest.

The trajectory points toward a near future in which every significant theorem ships with a machine-checkable certificate — not replacing human understanding, but underwriting it. The remaining work is quantitative rather than qualitative: better automation to shrink the de Bruijn factor, verified kernels to close the trust gap, and enough library coverage that formalization becomes the path of least resistance. The century of the informal proof is ending; the century of the checked proof has begun.
