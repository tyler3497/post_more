# DAG-Based Byzantine Consensus: Narwhal, Bullshark, and the Leaderless Revolution in Distributed Ledgers

## Abstract

The decades-old consensus architecture in which a single rotating leader both disseminates and orders transactions has become the principal bottleneck of Byzantine fault-tolerant (BFT) distributed ledgers. This thesis analyzes the directed acyclic graph (DAG) paradigm that separates *transaction dissemination* from *transaction ordering*, beginning with the Narwhal mempool protocol and its co-designed consensus layers — Tusk, Bullshark, and Mysticeti. We formalize how certifying a DAG of causal histories once, via reliable broadcast, lets consensus order merely cryptographic hashes rather than payloads, reducing amortized communication to $O(n)$ per transaction and decoupling throughput from the commit path. We compare the asynchronous *zero-message overhead* approach of Tusk [1], the partially synchronous fast path of Bullshark which commits in two round-trips during synchrony [2], and the uncertified-DAG design of Mysticeti-C that reaches the three-message-round latency lower bound [4]. Drawing on published WAN evaluations — Narwhal-HotStuff at 170,000 tx/sec versus 1,800 tx/sec for HotStuff alone [1], and Mysticeti-C's 400 ms p50 latency on 106 validators [4] — we assess where the paradigm stands, identify its remaining costs, and sketch the open problems defining the next decade of leaderless consensus.

## 1. Introduction

For forty years, agreement in the presence of Byzantine faults has been engineered the same way: a distinguished leader proposes, everyone else votes, and if the leader stalls, a view change expels it. Practical Byzantine Fault Tolerance (PBFT) [5], Zyzzyva, Tendermint, and HotStuff [6] all share this monolithic anatomy — the leader sits *in the critical path of data dissemination*. Every transaction must pass through the leader's proposal before it can be committed, which caps throughput at what a single machine can broadcast and makes the leader a permanent liveness target for the adversary.

The DAG-based revolution begins with a deceptively simple question: *what if ordering and dissemination were different protocols?* The mempool — that neglected, operational component every blockchain runs — is promoted from plumbing to the foundation of consensus. Validators continuously build a shared directed acyclic graph of *causal histories*: each vertex contains transaction batches and references $2f+1$ vertices from the previous round, forming an ever-growing DAG that every honest validator eventually replicates in full. Consensus then becomes an act of *interpretation*: a deterministic rule, executed locally by each validator over its own copy of the DAG, selects an ordered sequence of leaders whose causal histories yield the total order of transactions. No votes are exchanged beyond DAG construction itself — the votes are *inferred* from the graph's edges.

This thesis develops the DAG paradigm in full technical depth. Section 2 reviews the classical leader-based lineage and its failure modes. Section 3 presents the core methodology: the Narwhal separation, certified DAG construction, and wave-based commit rules. Section 4 dives into four instantiations — Narwhal's primary-worker architecture, Tusk's zero-message-overhead asynchronous consensus, Bullshark's fast path for partial synchrony, and Mysticeti's uncertified DAG pushing toward the latency lower bound. Section 5 surveys the empirical results and the theoretical guarantees (safety, liveness, fairness, complexity). Section 6 confronts the limitations. Section 7 concludes with open problems.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 The Leader Bottleneck

Classical BFT consensus inherits its structure from Lamport, Shostak, and Pease's original formulation: $n = 3f + 1$ replicas tolerate $f$ Byzantine faults, and a quorum of $2f+1$ is needed for safety. PBFT introduced the pre-prepare/prepare/commit triad with a stable leader [5]; HotStuff linearized the protocol to $O(n)$ communication per view via threshold signatures and pipelined phases, becoming the dominant partially synchronous design [6].

Yet every leader-based protocol shares two structural weaknesses:

1. **Dissemination in the critical path.** The leader must receive, batch, and broadcast *all* transactions. Throughput is bounded by one machine's ingress and egress bandwidth, and the leader's batching decisions serialize what could be parallel work.
2. **View changes are liveness cliffs.** When a leader is faulty or the network is slow, the protocol stalls while replicas time out, elect a successor, and re-propose. During asynchrony (before GST), partially synchronous protocols lose liveness entirely.

Empirically, this was the state of play circa 2021: HotStuff on a WAN achieved roughly **1,800 tx/sec at 1-second latency** with 10-50 validators [1] — three orders of magnitude below what the underlying networks could carry.

### 2.2 Asynchronous Precursors

The asynchronous school — HoneyBadgerBFT and its descendants — refused to rely on timing assumptions at all, but paid for it in communication complexity and latency. DAG-Rider, presented in the "All You Need is DAG" framework of Keidar et al. [3], took a decisive step: it showed that a DAG built by reliable broadcast, combined with a global perfect coin, suffices for *asynchronous* Byzantine atomic broadcast with expected $O(1)$ rounds of latency per commit. DAG-Rider's insight is the direct ancestor of everything this thesis covers: *the DAG is not just a transport; it is the consensus substrate itself.*

> **Theorem (DAG-Rider, informal):** In an asynchronous network with $n = 3f+1$ parties, a DAG in which each vertex references $2f+1$ vertices of the previous round, interpreted with wave-based commit rules and a common coin, implements Byzantine atomic broadcast with expected constant latency and $O(n^2)$ amortized communication per vertex [3].

The practical gap remained: DAG-Rider disseminated full payloads through the DAG and assumed unbounded memory. Narwhal closed it.

## 3. Methodology

### 3.1 The Narwhal Separation of Concerns

Narwhal's foundational methodological move is architectural: split consensus into two layers that run concurrently [1]:

- **The mempool layer (Narwhal proper)** guarantees *reliable dissemination and storage of causal histories*. Validators continuously advance a DAG: each round, a validator creates a vertex (a small header containing hashes of transaction batches and $2f+1$ references to the previous round's vertices), reliably broadcasts it, and waits until it has collected $2f+1$ certified vertices of the current round before advancing. Certification — collecting $2f+1$ signatures on a vertex — prevents equivocation and guarantees *causal completeness*: any certified vertex's entire causal history is eventually retrievable by every honest party.
- **The consensus layer** observes the DAG and totally orders it. It need only agree on *which leaders to commit*, because each leader's causal history is already fixed and retrievable. Leaders are tiny (hashes and signatures), so the consensus protocol communicates almost nothing — in the extreme case of Tusk, *zero additional messages* [1].

Formally, define round $r$ of the DAG as the set of certified vertices $\{v_{i,r}\}$ created by validators $i \in [n]$. Each vertex $v_{i,r}$ carries $\text{parents}(v_{i,r})$, a set of $2f+1$ references to round $r-1$ vertices. The *causal history* $H(v)$ is the transitive closure of parent references. The fundamental agreement property Narwhal provides is:

> **Lemma (Causal agreement):** If an honest validator holds certified vertex $v$, every honest validator eventually holds the identical causal history $H(v)$ [1].

Since honest validators agree on every causal history, agreement on a *sequence of committed leaders* immediately yields agreement on the *sequence of transactions*: order each leader's newly covered causal history deterministically.

### 3.2 Wave-Based Commit Rules

Following DAG-Rider [3], rounds are grouped into **waves** of 4 rounds. Each wave elects (in retrospect) a leader vertex — the first round's vertex from a randomly chosen validator. A leader *commits* when it gathers sufficient support: at least $f+1$ vertices in a later round of the wave have paths to it. The commit rule is evaluated locally and deterministically:

1. Process waves in order; for each wave, determine its leader via the shared coin.
2. If the leader satisfies the commit rule (enough causal support), commit it and totally order its causal history minus already-committed portions.
3. Otherwise, skip the leader — its transactions will be picked up by a later committed leader's causal history (this is where *fairness* comes from: no honest vertex is ever orphaned).

Because the coin is only revealed after the wave's vertices are fixed, the adversary cannot target the leader in advance. This is how DAG protocols circumvent the FLP impossibility [3] while keeping the common case fast.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Narwhal's Primary-Worker Architecture and Certified DAG

Narwhal's engineering contribution is as important as its theory. Each validator is split into a **primary** and multiple **workers** [1]:

- *Workers* receive client transactions, batch them (e.g., 500 KB batches), and disseminate batches to all other workers via best-effort broadcast, returning only content hashes to the primary.
- The *primary* builds the DAG of headers — each vertex references batch hashes, never payloads — and runs the certification protocol.

This achieves two things simultaneously. First, throughput scales *horizontally*: adding workers increases dissemination capacity linearly, and the evaluation showed 600,000 tx/sec with additional workers and no latency increase [1]. Second, consensus messages stay tiny: committing orders hashes, and the actual payloads are fetched from any $f+1$ honest holders. The mempool is no longer a dumb queue; it is a *certified, causally-ordered data availability layer* that consensus consumes for free.

The cost is certification overhead: every vertex requires a round of signature collection ($2f+1$ signatures) before it enters the DAG. For $n=50$ validators this is 50 signature verifications per vertex per validator — manageable, but it is the price of the strong causal-completeness guarantee.

### 4.2 Tusk: Zero-Message-Overhead Asynchronous Consensus

Tusk is Narwhal's consensus companion for the fully asynchronous setting, and its headline property is startling: **it adds zero messages beyond DAG construction** [1]. The protocol is pure interpretation — validators run the wave/commit logic locally over the DAG Narwhal already builds.

How is this possible? In DAG-Rider-style protocols, the "votes" for a leader are not messages but *edges*: a vertex in round $r+1$ that references the leader's vertex constitutes a vote. The commit rule — $f+1$ (in Tusk's formulation, sufficient quorum support) later-round vertices with paths to the leader — is evaluated from the graph alone. Randomness comes from a threshold-coin revealed retrospectively per wave. No prepare/commit phases, no view changes, no extra round trips exist anywhere in the design.

Tusk's guarantees are those of asynchronous Byzantine atomic broadcast: safety always, liveness with probability 1, expected constant-round latency. Empirically it delivered **~140,000–160,000 tx/sec at 3–4 seconds latency** — roughly 20× the throughput of prior asynchronous state of the art [1]. Its honest limitation, which motivated Bullshark, is *quantifiable fairness under synchrony*: Tusk garbage-collects old DAG rounds, and while garbage collection keeps memory bounded, the fairness guarantees during synchronous periods are weaker than what a purpose-built partially synchronous protocol can offer [2].

### 4.3 Bullshark: Fast Paths Under Partial Synchrony

Bullshark asks the natural follow-up: if the network is *usually* synchronous (the standard partial-synchrony assumption with an unknown GST), why pay asynchronous prices in the common case? [2]

Bullshark preserves the full DAG-Rider/Tusk machinery as a **fallback path** — asynchronous worst-case liveness is retained — and adds a **fast path** exploiting synchrony:

- **Two leader types per wave.** Each 4-round wave designates a *steady-state leader* (deterministic round-robin, first round) and a *fallback leader* (chosen retrospectively by the coin, also anchored at the wave's first round), plus a second steady-state leader in round 3 of the wave for pipelining [2].
- **Fast commit rule.** During synchrony, a steady-state leader commits when $2f+1$ vertices of the *immediately following round* reference it — just **2 round-trips between commits**, a 75% latency reduction versus DAG-Rider's 8 [2]. Under asynchrony, the protocol falls back to the 6-round-trip expected latency of the DAG-Rider core.
- **No view changes.** Leader failures do not trigger a synchronization protocol: the DAG's causal structure already encodes who voted for whom, so every validator can locally determine which leader type the wave committed. A missing steady-state leader simply causes the wave to resolve through the fallback path.
- **Garbage collection with fairness after GST.** Bullshark answers an open question from prior work by showing how to bound memory while preserving quantifiable fairness once the network stabilizes [2].

The result is the first partially synchronous DAG-BFT protocol with optimal amortized communication ($O(n)$ per transaction) and post-quantum-safe assumptions that is genuinely optimized for the common case — and it ships in production: Sui's consensus layer ran Bullshark before migrating to Mysticeti [4].

### 4.4 Mysticeti and the Uncertified DAG Frontier

Mysticeti pushes the paradigm to its logical extreme by questioning Narwhal's most expensive assumption: *certification itself* [4]. In Mysticeti-C, validators broadcast blocks **without** collecting $2f+1$ signatures first — an *uncertified DAG*. Blocks are proposed every round on a threshold-clock schedule, and a novel commit rule lets **every block commit without delay**, achieving the **3-message-round latency lower bound** for Byzantine consensus, with multiple leaders per round for pipelining.

The trade-offs are sharp and instructive:

| Design | Certification | Commit latency (good case) | Throughput (WAN) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Narwhal-HotStuff [1] | Yes ($2f+1$ sigs) | ~2.5 s | ~170,000 tx/s |
| Tusk [1] | Yes | ~3–4 s | ~140,000–160,000 tx/s |
| Bullshark [2] | Yes | 2 round-trips | comparable to Narwhal |
| Mysticeti-C [4] | **No** | **3 message rounds (~400 ms p50)** | >50,000 tx/s |

Mysticeti-C is the first Byzantine protocol to reach 0.5 s WAN commit latency at state-of-the-art throughput [4], and its deployment on Sui cut p50 latency from 1.9 s (Bullshark) to 400 ms across 106 independent validators [4]. Mysticeti-FPC extends the design with a fast commit path for asset transfers woven directly into the DAG, minimizing signatures and messages relative to prior fast-path protocols [4].

The uncertified DAG's risk is denial of service: without certification, Byzantine validators can equivocate cheaply and flood the DAG with junk. Subsequent work (e.g., Adelie [4, follow-on]) adds active Byzantine-behavior detection and validator cut-off on top of the Mysticeti commit rules — evidence that the frontier has moved from *latency* to *robustness under adversarial load*.

## 5. Empirical Results and Theoretical Guarantees

**Throughput decoupling.** The central empirical claim of the paradigm is verified: Narwhal-HotStuff sustains **170,000 tx/sec at 2.5 s latency** where HotStuff alone manages 1,800 tx/sec [1] — a ~95× improvement from architecture alone. Workers scale throughput linearly to 600,000 tx/sec [1].

**Latency ladder.** Each generation has halved the good-case cost: DAG-Rider's ~8 round-trips → Bullshark's 2 → Mysticeti-C's 3 message rounds (the proven lower bound) [2][3][4]. Production numbers track the theory: Sui's Bullshark→Mysticeti migration cut p50 finality 1.9 s → 400 ms [4].

**Guarantees.** Across the family, the theorems compose cleanly:

> **Theorem (Safety):** No two honest validators commit conflicting transaction orders. Follows from causal agreement on the DAG plus deterministic interpretation of committed leaders [1][2][4].

> **Theorem (Liveness):** Tusk commits with probability 1 under asynchrony [1]; Bullshark commits within bounded time after GST and retains asynchronous worst-case liveness via its fallback path [2]; Mysticeti-C is live under partial synchrony [4].

> **Theorem (Fairness):** Every honest validator's vertex is eventually included in the committed order — either directly by a commit rule or via a later leader's causal history. Bullshark strengthens this to quantifiable fairness after GST with bounded memory [2].

**Complexity.** Amortized communication is $O(n)$ per transaction (optimal), versus $O(n^2)$ for naive all-to-all dissemination [2]. Consensus-layer messages are $O(1)$ per vertex in Tusk (literally zero beyond the DAG) [1].

## 6. Limitations and Open Problems

**Certification overhead vs. DoS exposure.** The family spans a genuine dilemma: Narwhal's certification buys causal completeness and DoS resistance at the cost of a signature-collection round per vertex; Mysticeti's uncertified DAG buys the latency lower bound at the cost of exposing the DAG to equivocation floods. No current design gets both properties cheaply — hybrid or adaptive certification is an open problem.

**Memory and garbage collection.** Early DAG protocols assumed unbounded memory to preserve fairness. Tusk and Bullshark introduced garbage collection, but the interaction between GC depth, fairness under asynchrony, and adversarial DAG growth remains only partially characterized [2].

**Fairness during asynchrony.** Bullshark guarantees quantifiable fairness only *after GST*; Tusk's fairness under sustained asynchrony is weaker. A protocol with strong, quantifiable fairness in fully asynchronous executions and bounded memory is still missing.

**Leaderless is not egalitarian.** "Leaderless" is something of a misnomer: every protocol here still elects leaders per wave — the innovation is that leadership is *interpreted from the DAG* rather than *exercised over the network*. The steady-state/fallback distinction in Bullshark [2] and multi-leader pipelining in Mysticeti [4] show the leader concept mutating rather than disappearing.

**Practical gaps.** Production deployments still wrestle with DAG synchronization under churn, epoch-change coordination, and the engineering complexity of the primary-worker split. Formal verification of the full stack (Narwhal + Bullshark/Mysticeti in Rust) lags the paper proofs.

## 7. Conclusion

The DAG-based revolution has rewritten the consensus playbook in five years. Narwhal showed that the mempool could be a certified, causally-ordered substrate and that consensus could be reduced to interpreting it [1]; Tusk showed that interpretation can cost *zero additional messages* [1]; Bullshark showed that partial synchrony buys a 2-round-trip fast path without surrendering asynchronous liveness [2]; Mysticeti showed that dropping certification reaches the latency lower bound at production scale [4]. Together they demonstrate that the leader bottleneck was never fundamental — it was an architectural choice, and better architectures exist.

The paradigm's next decade will be defined not by raw latency, which has hit its theoretical floor, but by *robustness*: adaptive certification, bounded-memory fairness under asynchrony, DoS-resistant uncertified DAGs, and verified implementations. The DAG is now the consensus; the work ahead is making it unbreakable. The SoK systematization of Wang et al. [7] remains the best map of where this family sits in the thirty-year history of DAG-based ledgers — and of how far it has yet to go.
