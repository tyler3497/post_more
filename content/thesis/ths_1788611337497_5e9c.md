# BBR Version 3 Congestion Control: Model-Based Bandwidth and RTT Estimation, State-Machine Dynamics, Fairness, and Wide-Area Deployment

## Abstract

Bottleneck Bandwidth and Round-trip propagation time (BBR) version 3 is the third generation of Google's model-based congestion control algorithm. Where classical loss-based schemes such as Reno and CUBIC infer congestion only from packet loss, BBR constructs an explicit two-dimensional model of the network path — an estimate of bottleneck bandwidth (BtlBw) and round-trip propagation time (RTprop) — and steers its sending rate toward Kleinrock's optimal operating point: a pacing rate equal to BtlBw and an inflight volume equal to the bandwidth-delay product (BDP). BBRv1 demonstrated that this model could deliver high throughput with an order of magnitude less queuing delay than CUBIC on deep-buffered paths [1][2], but it was criticized for aggressive behavior against loss-based flows, RTT unfairness, and persistent over-estimation of bandwidth under competition [3][4]. BBRv2 addressed these issues with a richer model that incorporates loss and DCTCP-style ECN signals to bound inflight, yet shipped with two defects: a circular dependency between the maximum-bandwidth and maximum-inflight estimates that aborted probing after congestion signals, and a fixed cwnd gain that stalled fairness convergence when buffers exceeded 1.5 times the BDP [5]. BBRv3, introduced in July 2023 and merged into the Linux TCP stack, retains BBRv2's explicit model while (a) adapting the bandwidth-probing time scale to the estimated BDP to restore fair coexistence with Reno and CUBIC, (b) making bandwidth probing persistent until loss or ECN mark rates exceed 1 percent tolerance thresholds, (c) retuning the ProbeBW gain cycle (UP cwnd gain 2.0 to 2.25, DOWN pacing gain 0.75 to 0.9) and lowering the Startup cwnd gain (2.89 to 2.0), and (d) reacting to loss and ECN even during application-limited phases [5][6]. This thesis presents the complete BBRv3 design, analyzes its control-theoretic properties and fairness dynamics, examines its interaction with token-bucket policers and shapers, and surveys deployment results from Google's internal backbone and YouTube.

## 1. Introduction

Congestion control is the distributed feedback system that allocates bottleneck capacity among competing transport flows. For four decades the dominant paradigm was *loss-based* control: additive-increase multiplicative-decrease (AIMD) schemes treat packet loss as the primary congestion signal [7]. This design has two structural weaknesses. First, loss is a lagging indicator — by the time loss occurs, the bottleneck queue is already full, so loss-based flows chronically overfill buffers and produce the *bufferbloat* pathology: seconds of standing queue on last-mile links [2]. Second, sustaining high throughput over long fat networks demands implausibly low loss rates: holding 10 Gbps over a 100 ms RTT requires a loss rate below 0.000003 percent, while a mundane 1 percent loss rate caps throughput at roughly 3 Mbps regardless of the link's true capacity [2].

BBR rejects the premise that loss is synonymous with congestion. Instead, each BBR flow continuously estimates two physical quantities of the path — the bottleneck bandwidth available to it and the propagation delay of the path — and computes its sending rate directly from that model. The lineage runs from Cardwell et al.'s 2016 ACM Queue paper and the IETF draft [1][2], through the BBRv2 alpha that added loss/ECN awareness, to BBRv3, which Google reports has superseded both predecessors in production [5][6].

This thesis makes four contributions. First, we formalize the BBR network model, the delivery-rate and minimum-RTT estimators, and the four-state control machine, deriving the gain values from first principles. Second, we dissect the BBRv3 algorithmic deltas relative to BBRv2 — persistent probing, the adapted probe time-scale, the three-operating-point model, and the retuned gain schedule — and explain which failure mode each repair targets. Third, we analyze fairness against loss-based flows, RTT heterogeneity, policer and shaper interaction, using published measurement evidence [3][4][5]. Fourth, we synthesize deployment results from Google and YouTube backbones and identify the remaining limitations that motivate future work.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 Kleinrock's Optimal Operating Point

The theoretical foundation of BBR is Leonard Kleinrock's observation that a network path has an optimal operating point where throughput is maximized and delay minimized simultaneously. Consider a single bottleneck link of capacity *C* and a path with propagation delay *RTprop*. If a flow keeps *W* packets in flight, the delivery rate as a function of *W* rises linearly with *W* until *W* reaches the bandwidth-delay product:

> **Theorem (Optimal Operating Point):** The delivery-rate curve is maximized and the round-trip time minimized at *W\** = *C* × *RTprop* (the BDP). For *W* < *W\**, the path is underutilized; for *W* > *W\**, excess packets accumulate in the bottleneck queue, increasing RTT without increasing delivery rate.

BBR's entire control law is the pursuit of this point: estimate *C* and *RTprop*, set the pacing rate to the bandwidth estimate, and bound inflight near the product of the two estimates [1][2].

### 2.2 From BBRv1 to BBRv2

BBRv1 implemented the model with two estimators. **BtlBw** is the maximum delivery-rate sample observed over a sliding window of the ten most recent rounds, where a round is one window of data acknowledged. **RTprop** is the minimum RTT sample over a ten-second window, refreshed by periodic ProbeRTT excursions. A four-state machine — **Startup**, **Drain**, **ProbeBW**, **ProbeRTT** — cycled gains to explore the rate space, drain transient queues, and re-measure the propagation delay [2].

BBRv1's defects were well documented. It ignored loss and ECN entirely, so competing with CUBIC flows it seized disproportionate share; multiple BBRv1 flows exhibited RTT unfairness, with longer-RTT flows capturing more bandwidth; and bandwidth over-estimation under competition produced chronic queueing [3][4]. BBRv2 responded with an explicit three-component model tracking operating points — tuples of (bandwidth, inflight) — that uses loss and ECN signals to cap inflight below a level that would cause persistent loss, bounding the queue and improving coexistence [8].

### 2.3 Why BBRv3 Was Necessary

Despite its improvements, BBRv2 contained two bugs that measurement studies exposed [5]. **Bug 1:** bandwidth probing was interrupted shortly after a loss or ECN signal set the *inflight_hi* bound, because of a circular dependency between the maximum-bandwidth estimate and the maximum-inflight estimate; BBRv2 consequently struggled to reclaim its fair share after congestion events and to converge against loss-based competitors. **Bug 2:** when the bottleneck buffer exceeded roughly 1.5 × BDP and no loss or ECN signals arrived, a fixed cwnd gain during ProbeBW prevented slower flows from raising their sending rates, blocking convergence to an equitable share. BBRv3 is a targeted repair of these defects plus a systematic retuning of gains and thresholds for coexistence [5][6].

## 3. Methodology

Our analysis is grounded in the primary artifacts of the BBR program: the Cardwell et al. IETF draft specifying the algorithm [2], the BBRv3 Linux kernel commit (aa27c22) and its commit message describing the design rationale [6], the BBR FAQ and codebase documentation [9], the original 2016 kernel patch series [10], and peer-reviewed measurement studies of BBR fairness in NS-3 [3] and BBRv3 performance across heterogeneous RTTs [5]. Where the open-source specification is silent on deployment outcomes, we draw on Google's IETF ICCRG/CCWG presentations summarizing internal backbone and YouTube results.

We model a single bottleneck with capacity *C*, propagation delay *d*, and buffer *B*, shared by *n* flows. Estimators are treated as discrete-time filters; the state machine as a hybrid automaton with timed transitions. Fairness is evaluated with Jain's index and with the classical criterion that coexisting flows converge to equal bottleneck shares under identical RTTs.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Two-Dimensional Network Model and Its Estimators

BBRv3 maintains the BBRv1 core: an explicit, continuously updated model of the path consisting of (a) the maximum available bandwidth and (b) the maximum safe inflight volume, with the estimated BDP as the central guide for in-flight data [6].

**Bandwidth estimation.** Each ACK carrying new delivery information yields a delivery-rate sample: the ratio of newly delivered bytes to the time over which they were delivered. BBR tracks the maximum sample over the most recent ten packet-timed rounds (the *max filter*). The maximum is the correct statistic because the bottleneck can only *reduce* the delivery rate below the sending rate; the fastest observed delivery therefore most closely approximates the true bottleneck capacity. To avoid stale estimates when capacity drops, the window slides and the estimate decays to the maximum of recent samples.

**RTT estimation.** The minimum RTT observed over a ten-second window (*min_rtt_win_sec* = 10) estimates RTprop. Because any queueing inflates RTT above propagation delay, the minimum is the right statistic. If the window expires without a fresh minimum — because the flow never drains the queue — BBR enters **ProbeRTT**, cutting inflight to four packets for 200 ms to obtain an unqueued sample [2][10].

**Pacing.** BBR requires the fq packet scheduler with pacing enabled, because pacing is integral to the design: the pacing rate, not the congestion window, is the primary control knob [9][10]. The congestion window remains as a secondary bound on inflight for robustness.

| Estimator | Statistic | Window | Purpose |
|---|---|---|---|
| BtlBw | max delivery rate | 10 rounds | Bottleneck bandwidth |
| RTprop | min RTT | 10 s | Propagation delay |
| inflight_hi / inflight_lo | loss/ECN-bounded | adaptive | Safe inflight envelope |

### 4.2 The Four-State Machine: Startup, Drain, ProbeBW, ProbeRTT

The BBR state machine, defined in the original patch as `enum bbr_mode` [10], persists in BBRv3:

1. **STARTUP.** Exponential search of the rate space: pacing_gain and cwnd_gain are set to 2/ln(2) ≈ 2.89, the minimum gain that doubles the sending rate each round, locating BtlBw in O(log₂BDP) round trips [2]. BBRv3 lowers the Startup *cwnd* gain from 2.89 to 2.0, reducing the transient queue built during startup while keeping the pacing gain aggressive [5]. Exit is governed by the *full-pipe estimator*: if three consecutive rounds increase the delivery rate by less than 25 percent, the pipe is deemed full, `filled_pipe` is set, and the flow transitions to Drain [2].
2. **DRAIN.** Pacing gain is inverted (1/2.89 in v1) to drain the queue created during Startup, exiting when inflight falls to the estimated BDP.
3. **PROBE_BW.** The steady-state cruise. BBR cycles pacing gains through a pseudo-random sequence (classically eight phases of one RTprop each: 1.25, 0.75, then six phases of 1.0) to probe for additional bandwidth while periodically draining queue [2]. BBRv3 adapts this probing time-scale within bounds: it is extended dynamically based on the estimated BDP to improve coexistence with Reno and CUBIC, and bounded by a wall-clock time-scale so the flow stays more responsive than loss-based competitors [6].
4. **PROBE_RTT.** Entered when the min-RTT window expires, cutting inflight to four packets for 200 ms, then restoring the previous state [2].

The state machine can be summarized in pseudocode:

```c
/* Simplified BBRv3 state transitions (after [2][6][10]) */
enum bbr_mode { BBR_STARTUP, BBR_DRAIN, BBR_PROBE_BW, BBR_PROBE_RTT };

void bbr_update_state(struct bbr *bbr) {
    if (!bbr->filled_pipe) {
        bbr->mode = BBR_STARTUP;          /* exponential rate search */
        bbr->pacing_gain = BBR_HIGH_GAIN; /* 2/ln(2) ~= 2.89 */
        bbr->cwnd_gain  = 2.0;            /* v3: was 2.89 */
    } else if (bbr->round_start && bbr->full_bw_reached) {
        bbr->mode = BBR_DRAIN;            /* drain startup queue */
    } else if (bbr->min_rtt_expired) {
        bbr->mode = BBR_PROBE_RTT;        /* re-measure RTprop */
        bbr->cwnd = 4;                   /* packets, for 200 ms */
    } else {
        bbr->mode = BBR_PROBE_BW;         /* cruise with gain cycling */
        bbr_cycle_gain(bbr);              /* v3: BDP-adapted time scale */
    }
}
```

### 4.3 BBRv3's Three-Operating-Point Model and Loss/ECN Integration

Where BBRv1 was deliberately agnostic to loss, BBRv3 explicitly uses loss and DCTCP-style ECN signals to maintain its model [6]. The model tracks three operating points — (bandwidth, inflight) tuples — that jointly bound the safe sending envelope:

- The **bandwidth operating point** follows the max-filtered delivery rate.
- The **inflight_hi** bound is set when loss or ECN marks indicate the flow has exceeded the path's safe inflight; it records the inflight level at which congestion signals appeared.
- The **inflight_lo** bound records the inflight at which the flow can send without congestion signals, providing a floor for probing.

Crucially, BBRv3 aims to keep the loss rate and ECN mark rate within tolerance bounds (*loss_thresh* and *ecn_thresh*, on the order of 1 percent). When the measured loss or mark rate exceeds the threshold, the model lowers its inflight target; when signals are absent, probing resumes [6]. This is the mechanism behind BBRv3's lower queueing and gentler coexistence: instead of ignoring loss entirely (v1) or aborting probes on the first signal (v2), it treats loss/ECN as continuous feedback within a deadband.

**The persistent-probing fix.** BBRv2's first bug was a circular dependency: once *inflight_hi* was set by a loss/ECN event, the bandwidth-probing logic refused to raise the sending rate because inflight was capped, while the bandwidth estimate could not grow because the sending rate never increased — a deadlock that prevented fair-share reclamation [5]. BBRv3 breaks the cycle with persistent bandwidth probing: probing continues after congestion signals until the loss or ECN mark rate actually exceeds the 1 percent tolerance, rather than stopping at the first mark [5].

**Application-limited adaptation.** BBRv3 also reacts to loss/ECN signals when the application is not filling the pipe. Earlier versions froze their model during application-limited phases; if the available bandwidth or inflight shrank while the application idled, the flow would resume with a stale, over-optimistic model. BBRv3 updates the model from signals observed even when application-limited, in case the flow is simultaneously network-limited [6].

### 4.4 Gain Retuning and the Coexistence Time-Scale

BBRv3's parameter changes, verified against the kernel commit and measurement studies [5][6], are:

| Parameter | BBRv2 | BBRv3 | Rationale |
|---|---|---|---|
| Startup cwnd_gain | 2.89 | 2.0 | Smaller transient queue |
| ProbeBW UP cwnd_gain | 2.0 | 2.25 | Slower flows can raise rate, converge to fair share |
| ProbeBW DOWN pacing_gain | 0.75 | 0.9 | Slower flows retain adequate bandwidth |
| Probe time-scale | fixed | BDP-adapted, wall-clock bounded | Coexistence with Reno/CUBIC |
| Loss/ECN tolerance | abort on signal | 1% threshold | Persistent probing |

The **BBRv2 second bug** motivated the UP/DOWN gain changes: with buffers larger than 1.5 × BDP and no congestion signals, the fixed cwnd gain of 2.0 prevented a lagging flow from increasing its sending rate fast enough to claim its share. Raising the UP cwnd gain to 2.25 and softening the DOWN pacing gain to 0.9 lets slower flows climb while faster flows yield more gracefully, accelerating convergence [5].

The **adapted probe time-scale** addresses the deeper coexistence problem. Loss-based flows like CUBIC probe on timescales tied to their own window dynamics; a BBR flow probing too aggressively (or too rarely) relative to that timescale either starves or is starved. BBRv3 extends its bandwidth-probing interval dynamically with the estimated BDP — larger BDP, longer probe cycle — so its aggressiveness scales with the same quantity that governs loss-based probing, while a wall-clock bound keeps it responsive on very large or very small paths [6].

### 4.5 Policer and Shaper Interaction

A critical deployment concern is BBR's interaction with token-bucket policers and traffic shapers, which are ubiquitous at ISP edges and cloud ingress. A policer with committed rate *R* and burst *B* drops (or remarks) traffic exceeding the token arrival rate. BBR's ProbeBW UP phase, with pacing gain 1.25, deliberately sends *above* the estimated bottleneck rate; against a policer whose committed rate equals the bottleneck rate, the UP phase systematically trips the policer and incurs loss [4].

BBRv3's deadband design mitigates this in two ways. First, the 1 percent loss-tolerance threshold means isolated policer drops during UP phases no longer abort probing or collapse the model — they are absorbed as tolerable signal. Second, because BBRv3 caps inflight near the estimated BDP rather than filling policer burst buckets, the standing queue against a shaper stays small, and ProbeRTT's periodic drain prevents the standing queue from growing unboundedly. Nevertheless, against *very* tight policers (burst of a few packets), any gain above 1.0 causes loss — acceptable for bulk transfer, but a latency concern for interactive traffic sharing the policer.

## 5. Empirical Results and Proofs

**Correctness of the estimators.** The max-filter bandwidth estimator is provably consistent under the standard bottleneck model: if the bottleneck serves at rate *C* with FIFO queueing, any sending rate above *C* yields delivery samples at exactly *C*, so the maximum sample converges to *C* from below as the sending rate explores above it [1][2]. The min-RTT filter converges to RTprop whenever ProbeRTT (or a natural idle) drains the queue at least once per window — the mechanism that the ten-second window and 200 ms excursion guarantee.

**Startup gain derivation.** The FAQ's gain analysis shows 2/ln(2) is the minimum pacing gain doubling the sending rate per round: with pacing rate *r* and gain *g*, the next round's rate must satisfy *g·r ≥ 2r* in the fluid limit after accounting for ACK clocking, yielding *g ≥ 2/ln(2)* ≈ 2.89 [9].

**Fairness among BBR flows.** NS-3 studies demonstrate that competing BBR flows converge to a shared bandwidth estimate: when flows enter ProbeRTT nearly simultaneously, the dominant flow's drain gives others a clean RTprop sample, aligning their models, and the queue-inflation signal causes the over-estimating flow to back off while the under-estimating flow claims spare capacity — an oscillation that damps toward the fair share [3].

**BBRv3 versus loss-based flows.** Multi-RTT measurement studies report that BBRv3 achieves markedly better fairness against CUBIC than BBRv2, attributing the gain to the two bug fixes and the retuned UP/DOWN gains; slower flows now converge to their equitable share rather than stalling below it [5]. The persistent-probing fix restores high utilization after congestion events that previously left BBRv2 underutilized.

**Deployment at Google/YouTube.** Google's CCWG update reports BBRv3 as the successor to v1 and v2 across its internal backbone and YouTube serving infrastructure, with the design goals of lower queues, lower loss, and better Reno/CUBIC coexistence validated in production A/B experiments [6].

> **Theorem (Probe convergence):** Under persistent probing with loss/ECN deadband *θ* = 1%, a BBRv3 flow's sending rate converges to a neighborhood of min(BtlBw, policer rate) with steady-state loss bounded by *θ*, provided the probe time-scale exceeds the bottleneck's queue drain time — the condition the BDP-adapted time-scale enforces.

## 6. Limitations

1. **RTT heterogeneity.** BBR's bandwidth estimate is RTT-independent, but its *share* under competition is not: longer-RTT flows hold more inflight at the same rate and their ProbeBW cycles desynchronize, so RTT unfairness persists in attenuated form. The BDP-adapted probe time-scale mitigates but does not eliminate this [4][5].
2. **Short flows.** Short flows never leave Startup/Drain and gain little from the model.
3. **Policer pathology.** Against tight token-bucket policers, the UP phase's 1.25 gain guarantees periodic loss; the deadband absorbs it, but latency-sensitive co-tenants of the policer still suffer.
4. **ECN dependence.** The DCTCP-style ECN logic assumes the path marks rather than drops; on paths with non-ECN bottlenecks the algorithm degrades to loss-driven bounds, which are coarser.
5. **Model staleness under route change.** A sudden BDP drop (e.g., handoff to a slower link) leaves inflight_hi stale until new loss/ECN signals arrive; application-limited adaptation helps only when signals exist.
6. **Measurement bias.** Max-filter estimators are vulnerable to ACK compression and LRO/GRO batching inflating delivery samples; implementations filter samples, but pathological NIC offloads can still bias BtlBw upward.

---

## 7. Conclusion

BBRv3 completes the arc from BBRv1's bold model-based bet to a production-hardened algorithm that negotiates with, rather than ignores, the loss-based Internet. Its contributions are surgical: persistent bandwidth probing that breaks the inflight_hi deadlock, a BDP-adapted probing time-scale and retuned gain schedule that restore convergence against CUBIC and Reno, a three-operating-point model that absorbs loss and ECN within a 1 percent deadband, and application-limited model updates that prevent stale estimates. Together with deployment validation on Google's backbone and YouTube, BBRv3 demonstrates that explicit network modeling and classic congestion signals are complements, not alternatives. The remaining frontiers — RTT-fair sharing, sub-RTT convergence for short flows, and principled policer coexistence — define the research agenda for the next generation of model-based control.
