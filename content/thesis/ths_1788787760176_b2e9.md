# io_uring and the Modern Linux Async I/O Subsystem: Submission/Completion Rings, Fixed Files and Buffers, Multishot Operations, and SQPOLL/IOPOLL for Million-IOPS Storage and Networking

## Abstract

Since its introduction in Linux 5.1, io_uring has displaced three decades of asynchronous I/O plumbing on Linux: readiness-based event notification (epoll), the constrained asynchronous block interface (libaio/AIO), and the one-syscall-per-operation tax that once bounded storage and network servers well below a million IOPS per core. This thesis presents a rigorous account of the modern io_uring subsystem as it stands in the 6.x kernels: its shared-memory submission and completion ring architecture, the fixed-file and registered-buffer mechanisms that collapse per-request kernel overhead, the multishot operations that convert re-arm loops into persistent kernel-side requests, and the SQPOLL and IOPOLL modes that remove the system call from the hot path entirely. We derive an analytical cost model of per-operation latency, calibrate it against published measurements from database-engine integrations and kernel-busy-poll configurations, and identify precisely where order-of-magnitude gains materialize. We close with a candid analysis of the subsystem's limits: security surface, kernel-version fragmentation, and workloads where io_uring buys nothing.

---

## 1. Introduction

For most of the history of Unix, asynchronous I/O was an embarrassment of partial solutions. BSD gave us `select(2)` and `poll(2)` with their linear scan over descriptor sets; Linux countered with `epoll`, a scalable readiness interface that nevertheless required a separate syscall to *learn* that I/O was possible and yet another to *perform* it [1]. For storage, Linux offered POSIX AIO — a glibc emulation layered over threads — and later `libaio`, whose `io_submit` path was in practice restricted to `O_DIRECT` block I/O and never achieved true asynchrony for buffered files. The consequence was a fractured landscape: network servers spoke `epoll`, storage engines spoke `libaio`, and hybrid systems paid the complexity of both, with syscall rates scaling linearly in request rates.

`io_uring`, merged in Linux 5.1 under the stewardship of Jens Axboe, is the first Linux interface to unify *all* of this under one completion-based, batch-oriented, shared-memory API [1]. Its premise is disarmingly simple: the application and the kernel communicate through two lock-free ring buffers mapped into the application's address space — a submission queue (SQ) carrying requests and a completion queue (CQ) carrying results. Batching is not an optimization layered on top; it is the *primitive*. Dozens of operations can be enqueued with ordinary memory stores and committed with a single `io_uring_enter(2)` — or with no syscall at all.

This thesis examines the subsystem at the level of mechanism. Section 2 reconstructs the historical pathologies that motivated the design. Section 3 formalizes the architectural model: rings, entries, and the three execution paths a request may take. Section 4, the deep dive, dissects the four pillars named in the title — ring machinery and memory ordering, the fixed-file/registered-buffer zero-copy contract, multishot persistent requests, and the SQPOLL/IOPOLL polling modes. Section 5 develops an analytical throughput model calibrated against published empirical studies, including a recent PVLDB analysis of io_uring in high-performance database engines [5]. Section 6 confronts the limitations, and Section 7 concludes.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 The readiness tax

Classical event-driven servers on Linux are built on *readiness* notification. `epoll_wait(2)` reports that a socket *may* be readable; the application then issues `recv(2)`, which may still return `EAGAIN` under edge-triggered semantics, may copy through several layers, and always costs a full syscall round trip. On a keep-alive HTTP workload, the hot path is therefore `epoll_wait → recvmsg → sendmsg`: three syscalls per request, each carrying roughly 100–200 ns of entry/exit overhead plus the collateral damage of Spectre/Meltdown mitigations, TLB pressure, and context-switch bookkeeping [2]. At one million requests per second, the syscall substrate alone consumes multiple cores before a single byte of application logic runs.

### 2.2 The storage half of the problem

`libaio` addressed none of this for networking, and for storage only the `O_DIRECT` case, punting buffered I/O to threads. It could not batch submit-and-reap into one call, could not express operation *dependencies* (write-then-fsync), and exposed no syscall-free completion polling; database engines built bespoke thread pools to compensate [5].

### 2.3 The completion-based turn

`io_uring` inverts the model. Instead of asking the kernel *whether* work can proceed, the application *tells* the kernel what work to do and learns of completion afterward. This completion-based discipline, standard in Windows IOCP and Solaris ports for decades, eliminates the readiness round trip entirely and — crucially — makes batching natural: the SQ is just memory, so submitting *N* operations costs *N* stores plus one commit. Table 1 summarizes the generational shift.

| Interface | Model | Syscalls per op (steady state) | Unified net + storage | True async buffered I/O |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| `select`/`poll` | readiness, O(n) scan | 2+ | no | no |
| `epoll` | readiness, O(1) interest | 2–3 | no | no |
| `libaio` | completion | 1–2 | no | no |
| `io_uring` (batched) | completion, shared rings | ≈ 1/B (B = batch size) | **yes** | **yes** |
| `io_uring` (SQPOLL + multishot) | completion, kernel-polled | **≈ 0** | yes | yes |

> **Theorem (Syscall amortization).** Let *B* be the number of SQEs committed per `io_uring_enter(2)` and *R* the request rate. The application's syscall rate is bounded by *R/B*, independent of the number of open descriptors. With `IORING_SETUP_SQPOLL`, the bound tightens to zero in steady state.
>
> *Proof sketch.* SQEs are plain stores into mapped memory; the commit is a single tail-index update plus, in the non-SQPOLL case, one enter call that both submits pending entries and harvests completions. Under SQPOLL a kernel thread observes the tail autonomously, so no application syscall is required to move work. ∎

## 3. Methodology

Our analysis follows an *architectural-model* methodology: we reconstruct the subsystem from its public ABI contracts (the `io_uring_setup(2)`, `io_uring_enter(2)`, and `io_uring_register(2)` man pages and liburing sources [3]), from the design documents published by its principal author [2], and from peer-reviewed empirical studies of its behavior in production-grade engines [5][6]. Where absolute numbers appear, they are either quoted from these sources or derived from the analytical cost model of Section 5, clearly labeled as modeled estimates rather than new measurements.

### 3.1 Ring establishment

A ring is created by `io_uring_setup(entries, &params)`, which returns a file descriptor representing the instance. The `params` structure negotiates queue depths, feature flags (`IORING_FEAT_*`), and the offsets at which the SQ/CQ metadata and arrays are `mmap`'d into userspace [3]. Three memory regions result:

1. **The SQ ring**: an array of indices plus head/tail counters. The application writes a 32-bit index into the slot at `tail & mask`.
2. **The SQE array**: `struct io_uring_sqe`, 64 bytes each, holding opcode, flags, file reference, address/length, and user data.
3. **The CQ ring**: an array of `struct io_uring_cqe` (16 bytes: `user_data`, `res`, `flags`) plus head/tail counters written by the kernel.

All coordination is through the head/tail indexes with acquire/release ordering; liburing wraps the barriers in `io_uring_smp_store_release` and friends. No locks cross the user/kernel boundary.

### 3.2 The three execution paths

A submitted request traverses one of three paths, a taxonomy made explicit only recently by observability work that reconstructed the request lifecycle from kernel tracepoints [6]:

- **(a) Inline completion.** The operation completes synchronously during submission (e.g., a cached buffered read). The CQE is posted immediately; latency is a handful of microseconds with no thread involvement.
- **(b) Poll-retry path.** For sockets and pipes, the request parks on the file's wait queue via the kernel's internal poll mechanism (`IORING_FEAT_FAST_POLL`, 5.7+). No thread is consumed while waiting — the decisive improvement over punting everything to workers.
- **(c) io-wq worker fallback.** Genuinely blocking work (uncached buffered I/O on older kernels, filesystem operations without nowait support) is offloaded to the io_uring worker pool. This path is the classic hidden source of tail latency and motivates `O_DIRECT` plus `IOPOLL` for latency-sensitive storage [6].

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Ring Machinery: Submission Paths and Memory Ordering

The submission fast path is worth examining instruction by instruction, because every cycle here is multiplied by the request rate. `io_uring_get_sqe` returns a pointer into the mapped SQE array — no syscall, no allocation. The application fills the 64-byte entry: `opcode` selects among 70+ operations (reads, writes, `send`/`recv`, `accept`, `openat`, `futex` waits, even `madvise`), `flags` carries modifiers such as `IOSQE_FIXED_FILE` or `IOSQE_IO_LINK`, `fd` names the target, and `user_data` is an opaque 64-bit tag echoed back in the CQE for demultiplexing.

Committing is a tail bump. If and only if the kernel must be woken — the SQ was empty and no SQPOLL thread exists — the application issues `io_uring_enter` with `IORING_ENTER_GETEVENTS`, which simultaneously submits and waits for completions, collapsing the old submit-then-reap pair into one call [3].

| SQE field | Size | Role |
|---|---|---|
| `opcode` | 1 B | Operation selector (`IORING_OP_READ`, `IORING_OP_RECV`, …) |
| `flags` | 1 B | `IOSQE_FIXED_FILE`, `IOSQE_IO_LINK`, `IOSQE_ASYNC`, … |
| `ioprio` | 2 B | I/O priority hints |
| `fd` | 4 B | Target fd, or fixed-file index |
| `addr` / `len` | 8+4 B | Buffer pointer or registered-buffer index + length |
| `user_data` | 8 B | Opaque tag returned in the CQE |
| `personality` | 2 B | Credential selection for multi-tenant rings |

Memory ordering is the subtle contract: the kernel must observe SQE *contents* before the tail advance, the application CQE *contents* before the head advance — release/acquire pairs on shared indexes. The `IORING_FEAT_SUBMIT_STABLE` flag (5.5+) guarantees that once the kernel consumes an SQE, the application may reuse the entry [3].

### 4.2 Fixed Files and Registered Buffers: The Zero-Copy Contract

Two per-request costs dominate at high IOPS: resolving the file descriptor to a `struct file` (`fget`/`fput` with atomic refcount traffic) and mapping the user buffer (page pinning, `get_user_pages`, TLB and MMU-notifier overhead). io_uring lets both be paid *once*, at setup, rather than per operation.

**Fixed files.** `io_uring_register(fd, IORING_REGISTER_FILES, fds, nr)` installs an array of file descriptors into the ring; subsequent SQEs reference them by *index* with `IOSQE_FIXED_FILE` set [3]. The kernel resolves each file exactly once, at registration. The payoff is twofold: the atomic refcount dance disappears from the hot path, and — critically — the SQPOLL thread can safely use the files without the submitting task's file table, since references are held by the ring itself.

**Registered buffers.** `IORING_REGISTER_BUFFERS` pins and maps a set of user buffers once. `IORING_OP_READ_FIXED` / `WRITE_FIXED` then address buffers by index. For networking, the *provided buffers* mechanism (`IORING_OP_PROVIDE_BUFFERS`, and its successor, buffer rings) goes further: the application donates a pool of buffers, and the kernel *selects* one per receive, reporting the chosen buffer's ID in the CQE's `flags` (`IORING_CQE_F_BUFFER`). The application never names a destination buffer for receives at all — the kernel picks, fills, and reports, and the application recycles the ID. This is the foundation of the multishot receive pipeline in Section 4.3 [4].

The zero-copy data path that results — DMA from NVMe into pre-pinned pages, or socket skbs into pre-registered buffers, with no per-request `fget` and no page-table walk — is what lets a single core drive millions of small IOPS. A Rust-flavored sketch of the registration contract:

```rust
// One-time setup: pay fget + page-pinning ONCE, not per request.
ring.register_files(&sockets)?;          // -> fixed-file table
ring.register_buffers(&recv_pool)?;      // -> pinned buffer table

// Hot path: SQE references indexes, never raw fds or pointers.
let sqe = ring.next_sqe();
sqe.prep_read_fixed(fixed_idx(3), buf_idx(17), len, offset);
sqe.set_flags(libc::IOSQE_FIXED_FILE);
// ... commit tail; no enter() needed under SQPOLL
```

> **Design principle.** *Registration converts per-request dynamic costs into per-ring static costs.* Any overhead that can be hoisted out of the request path — fd lookup, page pinning, even buffer selection — eventually is, and each hoisting is gated behind a feature flag the application can probe with `IORING_FEAT_*` or `io_uring_probe` [3].

### 4.3 Multishot Operations: Persistent Requests

In the singleshot discipline, every completion obliges the application to re-arm: accept one connection, post a CQE, submit a fresh accept SQE. At high connection churn this re-arm loop is pure overhead — a round trip through the ring merely to express *keep doing what you were doing*. Multishot operations collapse the loop into a single persistent request that emits one CQE per event until explicitly cancelled [2][4].

The flagship instances, all stabilized across the 5.19–6.x window:

- **`IORING_OP_ACCEPT` with `IORING_ACCEPT_MULTISHOT`** (5.19): one SQE yields a CQE per incoming connection, each carrying the new fd, until the request is cancelled. The accept storm — thousands of SYNs arriving in a burst — is drained by the kernel without userspace re-arming [2].
- **`IORING_OP_RECV` / `RECVMSG` multishot** (6.0/6.1): one SQE per socket yields a CQE per datagram or stream segment, drawing destination buffers from the provided-buffer pool and reporting buffer IDs via `IORING_CQE_F_BUFFER`. The kernel even retries inline when more data is already queued, up to a fairness cap (currently 32 inline retries before requeueing, to avoid starving other connections) [4].
- **Multishot send with zerocopy notifications**: `IORING_OP_SEND_ZC` posts a second CQE when the kernel has finished with the buffer — the missing piece for true zero-copy transmit lifecycle management.

Each completion except the last carries `IORING_CQE_F_MORE` in its flags, telling the application the request remains armed; the terminal CQE (on cancel or error) omits it. The state machine is therefore: `SUBMITTED → ARMED ⇄ (EVENT → CQE[F_MORE]) → DONE`.

```c
// One accept SQE serves the listener for the lifetime of the server.
struct io_uring_sqe *sqe = io_uring_get_sqe(&ring);
io_uring_prep_multishot_accept(sqe, listen_fd, &addr, &addrlen, 0);
io_uring_sqe_set_data(sqe, tag_accept);
io_uring_submit(&ring);

// ... each CQE: res = new fd (or -ECANCELED when torn down),
// flags & IORING_CQE_F_MORE  =>  still armed, no re-submit needed.
```

Quantitatively, multishot removes one SQE submission and one re-arm decision per event; kernel-side inline retry converts socket-buffer bursts into batched CQEs with no additional userspace action [4].

### 4.4 SQPOLL and IOPOLL: Escaping the Syscall Boundary

Two flags, set at `io_uring_setup` time, attack the last remaining per-batch cost — the `io_uring_enter` syscall itself [3].

**`IORING_SETUP_SQPOLL`** spawns a kernel thread per ring that busy-polls the SQ tail. The application submits by writing SQEs and bumping the tail in shared memory; the poller picks them up within microseconds, executes them, and posts CQEs — all with zero application syscalls in steady state. The trade is explicit: the poller burns a CPU core (mitigated by the `sq_thread_idle` timeout), and early kernels required fixed files for SQPOLL (relaxed by `IORING_FEAT_SQPOLL_NONFIXED`, 5.11+). Where a dedicated core is affordable — NVMe benchmarking, packet pumps — SQPOLL is the difference between 1M and 3M+ IOPS.

**`IORING_SETUP_IOPOLL`** is the storage-side dual: for `O_DIRECT` block I/O, the application itself polls the CQ (`io_uring_enter` with `min_complete = 0` returns immediately with whatever is done, or spins until the time slice expires), and the kernel polls the device completion queue rather than waiting for interrupts [3]. Interrupt coalescing and IRQ affinity tuning vanish from the picture; in exchange, the polling core is surrendered to the device. IOPOLL is meaningless for sockets and buffered files — it is a contract with the block layer.

The two modes compose: SQPOLL on the submission side plus IOPOLL on the completion side yields a ring in which the application touches only shared memory, and the kernel touches only the device. Table 2 sketches the per-operation latency budget this unlocks.

| Configuration | Submit path | Complete path | Modeled p50/op | CPU cost |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| `read(2)` loop | syscall + copy | syscall | ~2.5 µs | 1 core ≈ 400K IOPS |
| io_uring batched (B=32) | 1 enter / 32 ops | shared-mem reap | ~0.6 µs | 1 core ≈ 1.6M IOPS |
| io_uring SQPOLL + fixed + multishot | shared-mem only | shared-mem only | ~0.3 µs | 1 core + 1 poller ≈ 3M+ IOPS |
| io_uring IOPOLL, O_DIRECT NVMe | shared-mem | device poll | device-bound | 1 core + device poll |

*Table 2: analytical latency budget per 4 KiB operation; IOPS figures are modeled estimates from the cost model, not benchmark claims.*

## 5. Empirical Evaluation

We evaluate the architecture along two axes: a first-principles cost model of the submission path, and calibration against published empirical studies.

### 5.1 The cost model

Let *c_s* be syscall entry/exit (≈ 150 ns), *c_f* the per-request `fget`/`fput` pair, *c_m* the buffer-mapping cost, and *c_x* execution proper. Classical `pread(2)` pays *c_s + c_f + c_m + c_x*; batched io_uring (*B* = 32) with fixed files and registered buffers pays *c_s/B + c_x*, and SQPOLL drives *c_s → 0*. The model predicts the literature's step function: ≈2–4× from batching, another ≈2–3× from SQPOLL where submission overhead dominated [2][5].

### 5.2 Calibration against published studies

A 2026 PVLDB study found *naive* substitution of io_uring yielded little; the wins came from registered buffers, batching, and the unified completion model — applying these guidelines to PostgreSQL's io_uring backend gave 14% speedup [5]. The ring is not magic, merely an *opportunity to delete terms* from the per-operation cost equation.

The `uringscope` work reconstructed the full request lifecycle from eBPF tracepoints at 0.7–9.9% aggregate overhead on NVMe workloads, confirming production-affordable introspection [6]; its taxonomy independently validates the three-path model of Section 3.2.

### 5.3 Where the model says *not* to bother

For large sequential transfers the device term *c_x* dwarfs everything else; deleting *c_s + c_f + c_m* changes nothing measurable. For sub-thousand-IOPS control planes, the engineering cost of ring management exceeds any latency saving. The model's honest prediction — corroborated by [5] — is that io_uring's advantage concentrates in the high-IOPS, small-operation regime: NVMe random I/O, connection-churned networking, and multiplexed mixed workloads.

## 6. Limitations

**Kernel-version fragmentation.** io_uring is a moving target: the opcode set grew from 7 in 5.1 to dozens by 6.x, multishot accept arrived in 5.19, multishot recv in 6.x, and each advance is gated behind `IORING_FEAT_*` flags or `io_uring_probe` [3]. Applications must write version-adaptive code or pin a minimum kernel — a real tax on portability that epoll never imposed.

**Security surface.** A ring is a standing kernel-execution engine fed by shared memory; malformed-SQE exploits drove several 2022–2024 CVEs and led distributions to restrict unprivileged io_uring. Mitigations (`IORING_SETUP_SINGLE_ISSUER`, `IORING_SETUP_NO_SQARRAY`, seccomp) exist, but the surface is structurally larger than syscall-per-op designs.

**The io-wq tail.** Anything that cannot complete inline or via poll-retry falls back to worker threads, reintroducing scheduling jitter precisely where determinism was purchased. Buffered filesystem I/O without nowait support is the canonical victim; the remedy — `O_DIRECT` plus application-level caching — re-imposes on userspace the complexity io_uring promised to absorb [6].

**SQPOLL's core tax and fairness.** The polling thread consumes a full core even when lightly loaded (modulo the idle timeout), and aggressive multishot inline retries can starve sibling connections — the reason the kernel caps inline multishot retries and requeues [4]. Throughput purchased with polling is throughput denominated in cores.

**Cognitive load.** Rings, barriers, buffer-ring recycling, `CQE_F_MORE`/`CQE_F_BUFFER` flag decoding, and version-gated features compose into one of the steepest learning curves in systems programming. liburing helps; it does not make the memory-ordering contract disappear.

---

## 7. Conclusion

io_uring is best understood not as a faster `read(2)` but as a *restructuring of the user/kernel contract*: requests become shared-memory data, batching becomes the default, completions replace readiness, and — through fixed files, registered buffers, multishot, SQPOLL, and IOPOLL — every per-request cost can be hoisted, amortized, or eliminated. The gains arrive in discrete, engineerable steps, each roughly doubling small-operation throughput until the device itself is the bound.

The empirical literature corroborates the mechanism while disciplining the hype: the wins are real but *conditional* on using the advanced features deliberately, as the PVLDB database study's 14% PostgreSQL gain demonstrates [5], and the lifecycle is now observable cheaply enough to verify in production [6]. For the workloads that defined its design — million-IOPS NVMe storage and million-request networking on a handful of cores — io_uring has quietly become the only rational substrate on Linux. The remaining work is consolidation: stabilizing the ABI surface, hardening the security story, and making the extraordinary performance ordinary to achieve.
