# Time Crystals and Floquet Phases of Matter: Discrete Time-Translation Symmetry Breaking in Periodically Driven Quantum Systems

## Abstract

We present a theoretical and experimental account of *discrete time crystals* (DTCs) — non-equilibrium phases of periodically driven (Floquet) quantum matter in which discrete time-translation symmetry is spontaneously broken and observables oscillate at an integer submultiple of the drive frequency. We review how many-body localization (MBL) stabilizes π-paired Floquet eigenstates and thus *eigenstate order* throughout the spectrum [2][3], and how high-frequency driving opens a *prethermal* route to time-crystalline behavior without disorder [4][6]. We analyze the kicked-Ising Floquet unitary realized in trapped-ion experiments [1], the subharmonic and Edwards–Anderson diagnostics certifying the phase, and the eigenstate-order demonstration on the Sycamore quantum processor [5]. We situate the DTC among Floquet topological phases, derive the heating bounds on prethermal realizations, and confront open challenges: the contested stability of MBL [8], finite-size verification of eigenstate order, and Floquet heating limits.

## 1 Introduction

The classification of phases of matter is an organizing triumph of modern physics. Time crystals represent its most radical recent enlargement: a phase ordered *in time itself*, spontaneously breaking the translation symmetry of the clock that drives it.

The story begins with a 2012 proposal that a quantum ground state could support persistent periodic motion. This *continuous* time crystal was swiftly ruled out: no-go theorems established that long-range spatiotemporal order cannot exist in the ground state or Gibbs state of a local Hamiltonian. For a time, the field appeared closed.

The decisive turn came from the *non-equilibrium* setting. A periodically driven system — H(t + T) = H(t) — possesses only a *discrete* time-translation symmetry, and no equilibrium argument forbids its spontaneous breaking [2][7]. If interactions and disorder prevent the drive from heating the system to infinite temperature — most famously via many-body localization — then stroboscopic dynamics can lock into a response of period nT. The oscillation is *rigid*: its frequency stays pinned at ω/n as drive parameters are detuned, just as a spatial crystal's lattice constant resists smooth deformation. This is the discrete time crystal.

Since the 2016 theoretical proposals [2][3], DTCs have been realized in trapped ions [1], nitrogen-vacancy centers in diamond [7], and on a superconducting quantum processor with explicit tests of eigenstate order [5]. Parallel work revealed a second mechanism, *prethermalization*, enabling time-crystalline order in clean systems for times exponential in the drive frequency [4][6]. The DTC has become a laboratory for non-equilibrium statistical mechanics: eigenstate thermalization, localization, and phases away from equilibrium.

Section 2 develops the Floquet and MBL background. Section 3 presents the kicked-Ising methodology and its diagnostics. Section 4 dives into π-pairing and eigenstate order, MBL versus prethermal mechanisms, Floquet topological neighbors, and the rigidity and melting transition. Section 5 surveys experiments and proofs; Section 6 confronts limitations; Section 7 concludes.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Floquet theory and quasienergies

For H(t) = H(t + T), evolution over one period defines the *Floquet unitary* U_F = 𝒯 exp(−i∫_0^T H(t)dt), with eigenstates U_F|φ_α⟩ = e^{−iε_α T}|φ_α⟩. The quasienergies ε_α are defined modulo ω = 2π/T. Two facts dominate: energy is conserved only modulo ω, so generic interacting Floquet systems heat to infinite temperature; and quasienergy spectra admit *π-pairing* — eigenstates whose quasienergies differ by exactly π/T — the sharp signature of discrete time-translation symmetry breaking [2][3].

> **Theorem:** (Equilibrium no-go) *No local, time-independent Hamiltonian with short-range interactions admits a ground state or thermal Gibbs state with long-range order in both space and time. Spontaneous breaking of continuous time-translation symmetry is forbidden in equilibrium.*

The Watanabe–Oshikawa formulation rests on cluster properties of equilibrium states [7]. Its loophole is its premise: it says nothing about *driven* systems, where no equilibrium state exists.

### 2.2 Many-body localization as a stabilizer

If Floquet heating is the enemy, many-body localization is the shield. MBL systems — disordered interacting chains — fail to thermalize: eigenstates violate the eigenstate thermalization hypothesis, retain memory of initial conditions, and are labeled by quasi-local integrals of motion (*l-bits*). Crucially, MBL is a property of the *entire spectrum*.

The 2016 breakthrough [2][3] combined these ingredients: a disordered, interacting Floquet chain can be simultaneously many-body localized and ordered in *every* Floquet eigenstate. This *eigenstate order* — the non-equilibrium generalization of a phase — underlies the DTC: each eigenstate is a macroscopic cat superposition whose branches are exchanged each period, yielding π-paired quasienergies.

### 2.3 Taxonomy of dynamical phases

| Phase | Symmetry broken | Stabilizer | Lifetime | Platform |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| MBL discrete time crystal | ℤ → nℤ | Many-body localization | Infinite (ideal) | Trapped ions [1], Sycamore [5] |
| Prethermal DTC | ℤ → nℤ | High-frequency drive | exp(O(ω)) | Trapped ions [4], NV ensembles |
| Dissipative time crystal | ℤ or U(1) | Drive–dissipation balance | Steady state | BEC–cavity systems [7] |
| Floquet topological phase | None (SPT) | Localization / gap | Infinite (ideal) | Cold atoms, photonics |

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 The kicked Floquet–Ising model

The canonical DTC model is the disordered, periodically kicked Ising chain. One period has two stages — an imperfect global spin flip, then evolution under disordered Ising interactions [2][3]:

U_F = exp(−i t_2 H_int) · exp(−i t_1 H_flip),
H_flip = (π/2 − ε) Σ_i σ^x_i,
H_int = Σ_{i<j} J_{ij} σ^z_i σ^z_j + Σ_i h^z_i σ^z_i.

Here ε is the deviation from a perfect π-pulse — the imperfection that tests *rigidity* — J_{ij} are Ising couplings, and h^z_i ∈ [−W, W] are random fields driving MBL. For ε ≠ 0 the response would naively dephase, unless interactions synchronize the chain and lock the subharmonic response. The (J, ε) phase diagram shows a sharp boundary: increasing J drives a transition from a trivial Floquet paramagnet into the DTC, identified by a Fourier peak *pinned* at ω/2 whose height (not position) varies with ε [3].

The Python sketch below implements exact diagonalization of a small instance and extracts the subharmonic diagnostic behind the phase diagrams of [3]:

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.linalg import expm

def floquet_unitary(L, eps, J, W, rng):
    sx = np.array([[0, 1], [1, 0]]); sz = np.array([[1, 0], [0, -1]])
    I = np.eye(2)
    km = lambda ops: np.linalg.multi_dot([])  # placeholder
    def kron_many(ops):
        M = ops[0]
        for o in ops[1:]: M = np.kron(M, o)
        return M
    H_flip = sum(kron_many([sx if i == j else I for j in range(L)])
                 for i in range(L)) * (np.pi/2 - eps)
    h = rng.uniform(-W, W, L)
    H_int = sum(h[i] * kron_many([sz if j == i else I for j in range(L)])
                for i in range(L))
    H_int += sum(J * kron_many([sz if k in (i, (i+1) % L) else I
                                for k in range(L)]) for i in range(L))
    return expm(-1j * H_int) @ expm(-1j * H_flip)

def subharmonic_peak(L=10, eps=0.1, n_periods=200):
    rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
    UF = floquet_unitary(L, eps, 1.0, 5.0, rng)
    psi = np.zeros(2**L); psi[0] = 1.0
    sz1 = np.kron(np.diag([1., -1.]), np.eye(2**(L-1)))
    mag = []
    for _ in range(n_periods):
        psi = UF @ psi
        mag.append(np.vdot(psi, sz1 @ psi).real)
    spec = np.abs(np.fft.rfft(mag - np.mean(mag)))**2
    return np.fft.rfftfreq(len(mag)), spec  # DTC: peak pinned at 1/2
```

For L ≳ 20, exact diagonalization gives way to matrix-product-state evolution or quantum Monte Carlo for the effective Hamiltonian [4].

### 3.2 The three-stage experimental protocol

The trapped-ion realization [1] uses a refined three-stage cycle on 10 ^171Yb^+ ions:

1. **Spin flip:** near-π rotation R_x(π − 2ε), ε ∈ [0, 0.15];
2. **Interactions:** long-range Ising couplings J_{ij} ≈ J_0/|i−j|^α (α ≈ 1.5) via spin-dependent optical dipole forces;
3. **Disorder:** site-resolved Stark shifts with W·t_3 = π.

The observable is the stroboscopic magnetization ⟨σ^z_i(nT)⟩. The DTC fingerprint: a Fourier peak at ν_tc = 1/(2T) surviving ~100 periods, *pinned* as ε varies, collapsing without interactions — proving rigidity is collective [1].

### 3.3 Diagnostics of eigenstate order

Three diagnostics, in increasing stringency, certify a DTC:

1. **Subharmonic response:** persistent oscillations at ω/n from a symmetry-breaking initial state.
2. **Rigidity:** the Fourier peak position is locked against drive detuning; only its height varies.
3. **Eigenstate order:** the response occurs for *generic* initial states — equivalently, π-pairing of the full quasienergy spectrum, probed via the Edwards–Anderson parameter χ_SG = (1/L)Σ_{i≠j}⟨σ^z_iσ^z_j⟩² [5].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 π-pairing and Floquet eigenstate structure

The sharpest characterization of the MBL DTC is spectral. In the idealized limit ε → 0, W → ∞, U_F² ≈ 𝟙 on the l-bit basis while U_F acts as the dressed global spin-flip parity P. Each l-bit configuration pairs with its flipped partner, and the cat superpositions |φ_±⟩ = (|{τ_i}⟩ ± P|{τ_i}⟩)/√2 are eigenstates with quasienergies split by exactly π/T [2]. A product initial state overlaps both members of many doublets, giving ⟨σ^z_i(nT)⟩ ≈ (−1)^n m_i — period doubling from *every* eigenstate. Deviations from π-pairing are exponentially small in system size, e^{−O(L)} [2].

> **Theorem:** (π-spectral pairing) *In the MBL DTC, Floquet quasienergies form pairs (ε_α, ε_α + π/T) up to e^{−O(L)} corrections. Equivalently, 𝒰U_F𝒰† = P·e^{−iDT} with [D,P] = 0 and P² = 𝟙 an emergent ℤ_2 symmetry.*

The emergent P explains rigidity: perturbations merely renormalize the dressed flip, while the ℤ_2 algebra is protected against smooth deformations [2][3].

### 4.2 MBL-enabled versus prethermal time crystals

Two distinct mechanisms stabilize DTCs. The *MBL DTC* [2][3] needs strong disorder and is eigenstate-ordered with infinite ideal lifetime. The *prethermal DTC* [4][6] needs no disorder: high-frequency driving (ω ≫ local scales) suppresses heating exponentially, so the system first relaxes to the Gibbs state of an effective Hamiltonian D and heats only on τ* ∼ exp(c·ω/J). If D spontaneously breaks ℤ_2 and the initial state lies in D's ordered phase, stroboscopic observables oscillate at ω/2 until τ*. The 2021 ion-trap experiment [4] measured τ_pre and τ* independently and mapped this dynamical phase boundary, corroborated by quantum Monte Carlo of D's equilibrium transition. Clean-chain numerics [6] show the complementary diagram: weak interactions give a symmetric phase, intermediate interactions with strong driving give the prethermal DTC, strong interactions thermalize — large driving *protects* the phase.

| Feature | MBL DTC | Prethermal DTC |
|---|---|---|
| Disorder required | Yes (strong) | No |
| Lifetime | Infinite (ideal) | τ* ∼ exp(O(ω/J)) |
| Initial-state dependence | None (eigenstate order) | Must lie in D's ordered phase |
| Dimensionality | Proven in 1D; contested in 2D [8] | Works in any dimension |

### 4.3 Floquet topological neighbors

The DTC is one citizen of the Floquet-phase zoo — phases classified by the topology and symmetry of U_F rather than a static Hamiltonian, e.g. Floquet SPTs and the anomalous Floquet–Anderson insulator, whose chiral edge modes wind around the quasienergy zone boundary with no static analog. The DTC's π-pairing is itself topological: the emergent ℤ_2 symmetry P cannot be deformed away without closing the π-gap. In Floquet-SPT language, the DTC is a phase whose *micromotion* carries symmetry-breaking order while the stroboscopic effective Hamiltonian may be trivial — which is why static-effective-Hamiltonian analyses miss it. Driving MBL SPTs can further pump between topological sectors each period, a frontier where time-crystalline and topological order intertwine [7].

### 4.4 Rigidity, criticality, and melting

Varying ε and J maps a dynamical phase diagram analogous to a ferromagnet in a transverse field [3]. At fixed disorder, small ε and sufficient J give the DTC; increasing ε destroys π-pairing at a critical ε_c(J), beyond which lies a trivial Floquet paramagnet. Finite-size scaling of the subharmonic peak height and its disorder variance indicates a *continuous* dynamical transition, in the same conceptual family as the melting of a spatial crystal — with drive imperfection playing the role of temperature [3]. The Sycamore experiment located this transition via finite-size scaling of the Edwards–Anderson parameter [5].

Rigidity — not mere subharmonicity — is the defining property. A parametrically driven pendulum also oscillates at ω/2, but its frequency tracks the drive. The distinction, in functional form:

```haskell
-- Parametric resonance: response tracks the drive detuning
parametricResponse :: Double -> Double -> Double
parametricResponse w detuning = w / 2 + detuning

-- Time crystal: response frequency is PINNED against detuning
dtcResponse :: Double -> Double -> Double
dtcResponse w _detuning = w / 2

-- Rigidity: zero susceptibility of the response frequency
rigid :: (Double -> Double -> Double) -> Double -> Bool
rigid f w = f w 0.05 == f w (-0.05)
```

This pinning is the temporal analog of a spatial crystal's fixed lattice constant [3].

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 Trapped ions: the first observation

In March 2017 the Monroe group reported the first DTC in 10 ^171Yb^+ ions [1], with programmable disorder (W·t_3 = π) and tunable long-range interactions (α ≈ 1.5). Magnetization oscillated at half the drive frequency for 100+ periods. Three controls were decisive: the peak stayed pinned at 1/(2T) as ε varied to 15%; its height grew with J_0 and vanished without interactions; spins synchronized across the chain only with interactions present. Published alongside the diamond NV-center realization [7], it established the DTC as a broad phase.

### 5.2 Prethermal DTC: order without disorder

Kyprianidis *et al.* [4] removed disorder entirely in a 25-ion chain. They measured τ_pre (approach to the effective-Hamiltonian ensemble) and τ* (heating to infinite temperature) separately, finding τ* growing exponentially with drive frequency while τ_pre stayed fixed — a parametrically large prethermal window. States in D's ordered phase showed period doubling up to τ*; states in D's disordered phase lost it by τ_pre — a measured prethermal phase boundary confirmed by quantum Monte Carlo [4].

### 5.3 Eigenstate order on a quantum processor

The most stringent test came on Sycamore in 2022 [5]. Using *quantum typicality* — random highly-entangled states revealing typical spectral behavior — Mi *et al.* probed over a million effective initial states in milliseconds. A Loschmidt-echo protocol separated intrinsic thermalization from extrinsic decoherence, and finite-size scaling of χ_SG located the DTC-to-thermal transition: period doubling for *generic* initial states, the first direct observation of eigenstate-ordered MBL-DTC physics [5].

### 5.4 What is rigorously established

**Three pillars:** **(i)** the Else–Bauer–Nayak framework [2] proves stable π-paired eigenstate order via convergent quasi-local unitaries; **(ii)** Yao *et al.* [3] establish rigidity and criticality with quantitative trapped-ion phase boundaries; **(iii)** prethermalization theorems bound heating as τ* ≥ exp(cω/J), giving the prethermal DTC [4][6] a rigorous lifetime guarantee.

## 6 Limitations

**The MBL controversy.** The MBL-DTC assumes many-body localization survives in the thermodynamic limit — now under siege. Khemani, Moessner, and Sondhi [8] showed the models of [3] do *not* realize an MBL DTC in the quoted regimes: generic states decay rapidly, and polarized-state oscillations reflect static transverse-field-Ising physics, not eigenstate order. Broader numerics suggest MBL may fail in 2D and for long-range interactions — the experimental regimes. If so, the "infinite-lifetime" DTC is strictly a long-lived prethermal phenomenon. The Sycamore finite-size analysis [5] is the strongest counter-evidence, but the thermodynamic limit stays open.

**Heating as a fundamental bound.** Floquet heating is inexorable; the prethermal lifetime, though exponential in ω/J, is finite, and higher ω collides with bandwidth limits and off-resonant excitations. There is no free lunch — only a parametrically large window.

**The verification problem.** Certifying eigenstate order means probing the *entire* spectrum — exponentially hard. All experiments sample polynomially many (or typical) states and extrapolate [5]. Finite-size scaling mitigates but does not eliminate the concern.

**Decoherence.** Dephasing and control errors mimic thermalization; the echo protocol of [5] separates them only partially. Dissipative time crystals [7] evade heating via continuous energy throughput — but are then driven steady states, not isolated phases.

## 7 Conclusion

The discrete time crystal has matured from a loophole in a no-go theorem into the first experimentally certified phase of matter existing *only* out of equilibrium. Its insights — spontaneous breaking of discrete time-translation symmetry, π-spectral pairing as eigenstate order, MBL and prethermalization as distinct stabilizers, and rigidity as the defining diagnostic — have reshaped non-equilibrium physics.

What remains: resolving the MBL controversy [8], where larger processors running the typicality protocols of [5] are the natural arbiters; exploring Floquet-topological hybrids with subharmonic edge modes; and extending dissipative and continuous time crystals into open and classical systems. Wilczek's perpetual-motion vision stays forbidden — but its non-equilibrium heir proved more interesting: a new chapter in the classification of matter, written in the language of time itself.
