# Exactly-Once Stream Processing at Planetary Scale: Event-Time Watermarks, Stateful Checkpointing, and the Flink–Kafka–Beam Architecture

## Abstract

Delivering **exactly-once semantics** for unbounded event streams across thousands of machines is one of the defining achievements of modern data infrastructure. This thesis presents a comprehensive treatment of the mechanisms that make exactly-once stream processing possible at planetary scale: *event-time watermarks* for reasoning about temporal completeness in out-of-order streams, *asynchronous barrier snapshotting* for consistent distributed state, and *two-phase commit* protocols that extend transactional guarantees end-to-end across systems such as Apache Kafka. We analyze the Flink checkpointing architecture introduced by Carbone et al. [1], the Dataflow model of Akidau et al. [2], and the comparative watermark semantics of Flink and Cloud Dataflow [3]. The thesis derives formal properties of watermark propagation and snapshot consistency, examines the engineering of incremental and unaligned checkpoints, and evaluates empirical throughput, latency, and recovery measurements from production-scale deployments. We close with a discussion of remaining limitations — including the correctness–latency–cost trilemma, the limits of transactional sinks, and open research directions in deterministic replay and disaggregated state.

---

## 1. Introduction

The shift from batch analytics to continuous stream processing is not a fashion but a structural response to how data is produced: unbounded, unordered, and global in origin [2]. Web logs, mobile telemetry, sensor networks, and financial order flows never terminate, and consumers demand answers *while* the data is arriving — dashboards, fraud alerts, recommendation features, and billing aggregates that must reflect the most recent state of the world without sacrificing correctness.

The central difficulty is the tension between **correctness**, **latency**, and **cost** — quantities that cannot be optimized simultaneously. Akidau et al. formalized this *trilemma* in the Dataflow model [2], arguing that practitioners must abandon the batch-era assumption of eventual completeness and instead adopt principled abstractions — *event time*, *processing time*, *windows*, *triggers*, and *watermarks* — for navigating the tradeoff.

This thesis examines how the trilemma is resolved in practice by three converging architectural families:

- **Apache Flink** [1], whose *asynchronous barrier snapshotting* (ABS) algorithm provides exactly-once state consistency with low latency overhead, inspired by the classical Chandy–Lamport distributed snapshot protocol [4];
- **Apache Kafka** [5], whose *transactions* and *idempotent producers* extend transactional guarantees across the source–sink boundary, enabling *end-to-end* exactly-once pipelines; and
- **Apache Beam** [6], whose unified programming model and portability layer abstract watermarks, triggering, and state behind a single SDK, allowing the same pipeline to execute on Flink, Dataflow, or Spark with comparable semantics.

Our contribution is threefold. *First*, we give a unified formal treatment of watermarks and checkpoint barriers as two instances of the same idea — markers that flow through a dataflow graph to establish a global, consistent cut. *Second*, we analyze the Flink–Kafka two-phase commit architecture and prove (informally but rigorously) why it achieves exactly-once end-to-end semantics despite asynchronous failures. *Third*, we survey empirical evidence from large-scale deployments and identify where the current generation of systems falls short, proposing directions for future research.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 Streaming, windows, and the two time domains

A *stream* is an unbounded sequence of timestamped elements. Classical batch systems pretend the sequence is finite and wait for its end; streaming systems cannot afford that luxury. The Dataflow model [2] therefore distinguishes two domains of time:

- **Event time** — the time at which an event occurred in the world (e.g., a purchase completed at 11:30:00). Event time is an intrinsic property of the data and never changes.
- **Processing time** — the time at which the system observes the event at some point in the pipeline (e.g., 11:35:12). Processing time varies across operators and across retries.

The gap between them — *time-domain skew*, the default condition of distributed streaming [3] — forces every system to answer: *when is a window of events complete enough to compute?*

**Windowing** assigns each element to one or more finite intervals so that aggregates can be computed. The standard taxonomy includes:

| Window type | Definition | Completeness signal |
|---|---|---|
| Tumbling | Fixed, non-overlapping intervals | Watermark passes window end |
| Sliding | Fixed intervals, fixed slide < size | Watermark passes window end |
| Session | Activity-bounded intervals with gap timeout | Inactivity + watermark |
| Global | Single window covering all event time | Watermark = ∞ or custom trigger |

Since unbounded streams have no natural end, windows on event time must be closed by *progress indicators* rather than by stream termination. This is the role of the **watermark**.

### 2.2 Watermarks: reasoning about completeness

A *watermark* with timestamp *t* flowing through a stream asserts that **no element with event time earlier than *t* will be observed downstream** [3]. Formally, if `W(t)` is observed at an operator, the operator may safely treat the stream prefix `(-∞, t)` as complete for triggering purposes. Watermarks therefore convert the unknowable ("have we seen everything?") into a tractable heuristic, acknowledging that the assertion is only as good as the source's knowledge of upstream lateness.

Watermark *propagation* obeys a simple rule: an operator emits `W(min)` over the current watermarks of all input partitions. A single slow partition — a *straggler* — can therefore stall the watermark for an entire pipeline, which is why idleness detection and per-partition strategies are critical in production [3].

Elements arriving with event time *below* the current watermark are **late data**, which systems drop, accumulate as refinements, or side-output for offline reprocessing [2][6].

> **Theorem:** *(Watermark-induced completeness).* In a watermarked dataflow where every source's watermark is a correct lower bound on future event times, and every operator forwards the minimum of its input watermarks, any window whose end timestamp precedes the current watermark contains exactly the set of non-late elements assigned to it. *Proof sketch.* Correctness of source watermarks implies no element with `t < W` remains upstream of any source. By induction on the operator graph, minimum-forwarding preserves the invariant that the operator has observed every element with `t < W` on all inputs. A window ending at `e < W` therefore cannot receive further elements except via the late-data path. ∎

### 2.3 Delivery semantics: at-most, at-least, exactly-once

Stream processors distinguish three levels of delivery guarantees:

| Guarantee | Failure behavior | Use case |
|---|---|---|
| **At-most-once** | Events may be lost; no correctness guarantee | Low-value telemetry, sampling |
| **At-least-once** | Events may be duplicated; never lost | Log forwarding, metrics |
| **Exactly-once** | Result equals failure-free execution | Billing, fraud, ML features |

The subtlety is that *exactly-once* never means the transport layer delivers each byte once — that is physically impossible under failures. Rather, it means the *observable state and outputs* of the pipeline are indistinguishable from a failure-free run [1]. This is achieved by making state recovery atomic (checkpoints) and making sink writes atomic (transactions) [5].

### 2.4 The Chandy–Lamport lineage

Flink's checkpointing is explicitly *inspired by the Chandy–Lamport distributed snapshot algorithm* [1][4]: a *marker* message flows along all channels, and each process records its local state upon first receipt. Flink's checkpoint *barriers* are the streaming analogue of these markers, and *barrier alignment* the analogue of channel-state recording.

## 3. Methodology

### 3.1 Asynchronous barrier snapshotting (ABS)

The core fault-tolerance mechanism of Apache Flink is the *Asynchronous Barrier Snapshotting* algorithm, presented by Carbone et al. [1]. ABS adapts Chandy–Lamport to the streaming setting:

1. The **checkpoint coordinator** (in the JobManager) periodically injects a special record — the *checkpoint barrier* — into every source, for checkpoint epoch *n*.
2. Barriers flow *in-band* with the data stream from sources to sinks.
3. When an operator instance receives a barrier on **all** of its input channels, it snapshots its operator state to durable storage (e.g., HDFS or cloud object storage), stores its position in the input streams, and forwards the barrier downstream.
4. If barriers arrive at different times on different inputs, the operator performs **barrier alignment**: it buffers records from the faster inputs until the barrier arrives on the slower one. This guarantees the snapshot contains exactly the records from epochs `< n` and none from epochs `≥ n`.
5. When all sinks acknowledge the barrier, checkpoint *n* is **complete** and may be used for recovery.

Crucially, snapshotting is *asynchronous*: writing state to durable storage happens in the background while the operator continues processing. This yields the headline properties of ABS — low latency impact, low space cost, and linear scalability even with frequent snapshots [1].

> **Theorem:** *(Snapshot consistency).* ABS produces a consistent global snapshot: the recorded operator states correspond to a cut in which no record crosses from a later epoch into an earlier epoch's state. *Proof sketch.* Barrier alignment ensures that each operator's snapshot is taken at a moment when, for every input channel, all records before the barrier have been consumed and no record after the barrier has been. The union of these per-operator cuts therefore forms a consistent cut in the Chandy–Lamport sense: any record's effects are either entirely inside the snapshot or entirely outside it. ∎

A relaxed mode — **unaligned checkpoints** [1] — disables alignment and includes post-barrier records in the snapshot, replaying them from input logs on recovery: *at-least-once* with near-zero latency impact, trading guarantee strength for speed.

### 3.2 Watermark generation strategies

Watermark correctness depends on the source's model of upstream lateness. Two strategies dominate [3]:

- **Periodic watermarks**: the source emits `W = maxObservedEventTime − boundedOutOfOrderness` on a timer (e.g., every 200 ms). The bound encodes domain knowledge: "events are at most *k* seconds late." Larger *k* increases latency; smaller *k* increases late data.
- **Punctuated (per-record) watermarks**: special records in the source stream carry the watermark explicitly, allowing precise control (used by Beam's `withAllowedLateness` triggers [6]).

In Python pseudocode, the periodic strategy is:

```python
class BoundedOutOfOrdernessGenerator:
    def __init__(self, max_out_of_orderness_ms: int):
        self.max_lateness = max_out_of_orderness_ms
        self.max_event_time = -10**18

    def on_event(self, event) -> None:
        self.max_event_time = max(self.max_event_time, event.event_time)

    def current_watermark(self) -> int:
        # heuristic: nothing earlier than (max - lateness) is still in flight
        return self.max_event_time - self.max_lateness
```

### 3.3 End-to-end exactly-once: the two-phase commit sink

Internal state recovery alone does not yield *end-to-end* exactly-once: a sink that writes to Kafka or a database could emit duplicates when a checkpoint commits, the job fails *before* the sink commits, and recovery replays the same records. The standard solution, implemented in Flink's `TwoPhaseCommitSinkFunction` and the Kafka sink, is to wrap sink writes in a **two-phase commit (2PC) protocol** scoped to the checkpoint epoch [5]:

1. **Pre-commit** (triggered by the checkpoint barrier): the sink flushes all buffered records for epoch *n* to the external system's transaction, but does not commit. Kafka sources simultaneously record their consumed offsets.
2. **Commit**: once checkpoint *n* is globally complete, the coordinator instructs all sinks to commit transaction *n* (Kafka: `commitTransaction`). If the checkpoint aborts (timeout or failure), all sinks abort their transactions (`abortTransaction`).

Because Kafka transactions are atomic — a consumer with `isolation.level=read_committed` either sees all of transaction *n* or none of it — the externally visible output of the pipeline matches exactly the failure-free execution, even across restarts. The price is that output latency is coupled to the checkpoint interval: results become visible only after checkpoint completion [5].

### 3.4 Beam's unified model

Apache Beam [6] factors the same ideas into a portable programming model — **windowing**, **watermarks**, **triggers**, and **accumulation mode** — executed by *runners* (Flink, Dataflow, Spark, Samza) that translate Beam's `DoFn` state and timer APIs into runner-native backends. A pipeline written once inherits each runner's exactly-once machinery; Beam is the open-source embodiment of the Dataflow model [2].

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Watermark algebra: merging, idleness, and per-key watermarks

Watermark propagation is a *monoid* over the lattice of timestamps with minimum as the combine operation. In Haskell notation:

```haskell
newtype Watermark = W { unW :: Timestamp }
  deriving (Eq, Ord)

instance Semigroup Watermark where
  W a <> W b = W (min a b)   -- operator input merging

instance Monoid Watermark where
  mempty = W maxBound         -- no inputs yet: unbounded
```

This algebraic view explains three production phenomena:

2. **Keyed watermarks.** Coarse per-partition watermarks over-approximate lateness when keys have heterogeneous arrival patterns. Fine-grained, per-key watermark generation (e.g., Wu et al.'s keyed watermarks for Flink) tightens the bound and reduces late data for skewed workloads.
3. **Idle sources and `Long.MIN_VALUE`.** A source that has never emitted a watermark contributes the lattice bottom, freezing the pipeline — a classic debugging trap that idleness detection also addresses.

Watermarks are, moreover, not only triggers: they are the system's *garbage collection* signal. Flink's state backends use the watermark to evict expired window state and retire old timers; Dataflow uses it to bound persistent-state retention [3]. A watermark that advances too slowly leaks memory; one advancing too fast creates late data.

### 4.2 Checkpoint barriers under backpressure: aligned vs. unaligned

Barrier alignment is the dominant source of checkpoint-induced latency. Consider an operator with two inputs, *A* (fast) and *B* (slow). With alignment, the operator must buffer all of *A*'s post-barrier records until *B*'s barrier arrives. Under sustained backpressure this buffer can grow to gigabytes, and the checkpoint itself is delayed — which delays the 2PC commit of sink transactions and therefore delays output visibility. The phenomenon is self-reinforcing: slow checkpoints make recovery points stale, which makes the next failure more expensive.

 The tradeoff matrix:

| Mode | Guarantee | Latency impact | State size |
|---|---|---|---|
| Aligned | Exactly-once | Barrier-wait backpressure | Small |
| Unaligned | At-least-once (or exactly-once with idempotent replay) | ~Zero | Larger (in-flight data) |

Production guidance that has emerged: use aligned checkpoints when sink transactions demand exactness (billing); use unaligned checkpoints when sub-second latency dominates (alerting, real-time bidding) and the sink is idempotent or transactional. In effect, alignment trades barrier-wait backpressure for smaller state, while unaligned mode captures in-flight channel buffers instead of waiting — at-least-once or exactly-once via idempotent replay with near-zero latency impact.

### 4.3 End-to-end exactly-once across Kafka: the full protocol

Let us now prove the headline claim. Consider a pipeline: Kafka source → Flink operators → Kafka sink, with checkpointing interval *T* and transactional sink [5].

> **Theorem:** *(End-to-end exactly-once).* If (i) the Kafka source records consumed offsets in each checkpoint, (ii) the sink pre-commits exactly the records of epoch *n* on barrier *n* and commits only after checkpoint *n* completes, and (iii) downstream consumers read with `read_committed` isolation, then the externally visible output stream equals the failure-free output stream. *Proof.* Consider any failure. Recovery restores operator state and source offsets from the latest complete checkpoint *n\**. All records with offset `≤ offset(n*)` have either been committed (their transactions committed before the failure) or aborted (pre-committed but checkpoint never completed). By Kafka transaction atomicity, aborted transactions are invisible to `read_committed` consumers. Records with offset `> offset(n*)` are replayed exactly once through deterministic operators and land in a fresh transaction, which commits iff its checkpoint completes. Hence every input record contributes to exactly one committed output transaction. ∎

The proof's assumptions are worth naming, because each is a real production failure mode:

- **Deterministic operators.** Non-deterministic functions (random IDs, wall-clock reads) break the "replay equals original" argument. Flink requires operators to be deterministic for exactly-once; randomness must be keyed and seeded so replays reproduce it exactly:

```rust
fn process_element(ctx: &mut Ctx, key: u64, elem: &Event) {
    // deterministic: recovery replays the same values
    let mut rng = SeededRng::seed_from_u64(ctx.epoch ^ key);
    let event_id: u128 = rng.random();
    ctx.state.update(key, elem, event_id);
}
```
- **Idempotent or transactional sinks.** Sinks without transactions (plain HTTP, files) cannot participate in 2PC; Flink offers *at-least-once* or *idempotent-write* fallbacks there [5].
- **Checkpoint completion liveness.** If checkpoints never complete (perpetual backpressure), transactions never commit and output stalls — a liveness failure, not a safety failure.

Kafka's side of the contract rests on the *transactional idempotent producer* (KIP-98): each producer receives a `ProducerId` and monotonically increasing sequence numbers per partition, so broker-side deduplication makes retries safe, and the transaction coordinator's `WriteTxnMarkers` make multi-partition writes atomic [5].

### 4.4 Stateful operators at scale: key groups, incremental snapshots, and rescaling

Exactly-once would be a curiosity if it only worked for small state. Production pipelines hold terabytes: per-user session state, ML feature stores, join buffers. Three mechanisms make this tractable [1]:

1. **Keyed state partitioning.** Flink partitions keyed state into *key groups* — indivisible units assigned to subtasks, serving as the currency of both checkpointing and rescaling [1].
2. **Incremental checkpoints.** The RocksDB state backend writes only SST files changed since the last checkpoint, sharing the rest by reference — order-of-magnitude reductions in checkpoint duration for slowly-changing state [8].
3. **Rescalable state.** Because snapshots capture key groups plus upstream offsets, a job can be restarted with different parallelism: the coordinator redistributes key groups across the new subtask set. The same mechanism enables savepoint-based migration and blue/green deployments without downtime. [7]

The cost model is instructive. Let *S* be total state size, *δ* the per-epoch change fraction, *B* the durable-storage bandwidth, and *T* the checkpoint interval. Full checkpoints cost `S/B` per epoch; incremental checkpoints cost `δS/B`. When `δ ≪ 1` (typical for session windows and feature stores), incremental snapshots reduce checkpoint load by 10–100× at the price of slower recovery (more files to reassemble) and background compaction — again, the trilemma, now between checkpoint cost, recovery time, and operational complexity.

## 5. Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 Throughput and latency: what the numbers say

Published benchmarks and operator reports give a consistent picture of the overhead of exactly-once machinery:

| Configuration | Throughput | p99 latency | Source class |
|---|---|---|---|
| At-least-once, no checkpointing | ~10⁷ events/s/node | < 10 ms | Baseline |
| Aligned checkpoints, 1 GB state | ~0.9 × baseline | + checkpoint interval | Flink operator reports |
| Unaligned checkpoints, 1 GB state | ~0.98 × baseline | ~baseline | Flink 1.11+ releases |
| End-to-end exactly-once (Kafka 2PC) | ~0.7–0.8 × baseline | checkpoint interval + commit | Flink + Kafka deployments |

Watermark-induced latency is the second term. With bounded-out-of-orderness *k*, event-time windows close *k* after their end by construction; production values of *k* range from seconds (datacenter telemetry) to days (mobile analytics with offline devices). The watermark paper's comparative evaluation of Flink and Dataflow shows that both engines implement the same *semantics* with starkly different *mechanisms* — Flink's in-band watermark records versus Dataflow's watermark tracking in the shuffle service — with comparable correctness and differing operational profiles [3].

### 5.2 Recovery: time-to-consistency after failure

Recovery time decomposes into detection + restart + state download + catch-up replay. With incremental checkpoints, state download is proportional to *changed* state rather than total state, and catch-up replay is bounded by Kafka retention and consumer throughput — typically minutes total. The key operational metric is **time-to-consistency** after failure; checkpoint intervals of 1–5 minutes keep it in the single-digit-minute range for most deployments [1][5].

### 5.3 Formal results summarized

The thesis has established three formal properties:

1. **Watermark completeness** (§2.2): under correct source watermarks and minimum-forwarding, closed windows contain exactly their non-late elements.
2. **Snapshot consistency** (§3.1): ABS barriers produce a Chandy–Lamport-consistent cut.
3. **End-to-end exactly-once** (§4.3): checkpoint-scoped 2PC with transactional Kafka yields failure-free-equivalent output.

Together they compose: (2) makes internal state recoverable, (3) extends the guarantee across the sink boundary, and (1) makes temporal semantics well-defined — subject to the determinism and liveness assumptions of §4.3.

## 6. Limitations

**The watermark correctness assumption is uncheckable.** A watermark is a *claim* by the source that no earlier events remain; the system cannot verify it. Clock skew, wedged producers, and silent partition failures all produce watermarks that lie, and the failure mode is silent data loss (late data dropped) or silent staleness (watermark frozen). Idleness detection and monitoring of watermark lag are operational necessities, not optional extras [3].

**Exactly-once is scoped to the pipeline, not the world.** The 2PC proof assumes deterministic operators and transactional sinks. Real pipelines call external APIs, write to non-transactional stores, and generate non-deterministic IDs. Each such boundary degrades the guarantee to at-least-once with idempotency — which is why experienced practitioners design sinks to be *idempotent by construction* (upserts keyed by event ID) rather than relying on transactions alone [5].

**The trilemma is not resolved; it is parameterized.** Shorter checkpoint intervals reduce output latency and recovery time but increase load on durable storage and the coordinator. Larger watermark lateness bounds reduce late data but increase window-closing latency. Incremental snapshots cut checkpoint cost but complicate recovery. Every deployment is a point in this space, and the "right" point shifts with workload, state size, and SLA [2].

**Ordering guarantees are per-partition.** Kafka orders within a partition, not across them; Flink preserves per-channel order, not global order. Applications needing global order must repartition to a single key — or accept the ordering that watermarks provide.

## 7. Conclusion

Exactly-once stream processing at planetary scale rests on three ideas that are, at bottom, one idea: *markers flowing through a dataflow graph that establish what is complete and what is consistent*. Watermarks assert temporal completeness; checkpoint barriers assert state consistency; transaction markers assert output atomicity. The Flink–Kafka–Beam architecture shows how these markers compose into a system that processes tens of millions of events per second, recovers from machine failures in minutes, and produces results indistinguishable from a failure-free execution.

The deeper lesson is the one the Dataflow model taught [2]: streaming is not batch with smaller batches. Unbounded, out-of-order data demands that we *name* our assumptions — about lateness, about determinism, about what "complete" means — and then build systems that honor exactly those assumptions and no more. Watermarks, barriers, and two-phase commits are the vocabulary in which those assumptions are expressed.

Open directions remain. Deterministic replay at the language level (eliminating the determinism assumption by construction), disaggregated state that separates compute from storage for near-instant recovery, and formal verification of the ABS and 2PC protocols in proof assistants are all active frontiers. The trilemma will not disappear, but each generation of systems gives practitioners a sharper set of knobs with which to navigate it.
