# Density Functional Theory: Kohn–Sham Equations, Exchange–Correlation Functionals, and Ab Initio Materials Design

## Abstract

Density functional theory (DFT) is the dominant first-principles framework for predicting the electronic structure and thermodynamic stability of matter from the Schrödinger equation alone. This thesis traces DFT from the Hohenberg–Kohn theorems (1964), which establish the ground-state electron density as the fundamental variable, through the Kohn–Sham construction (1965), which maps the interacting many-body problem onto a fictitious non-interacting system solved self-consistently. We systematically develop the hierarchy of exchange–correlation approximations — local density, generalized gradient, meta-GGA, and hybrid functionals — using Perdew's Jacob's ladder as an organizing principle, and derive the plane-wave pseudopotential and projector augmented-wave machinery that renders crystalline solids computationally tractable. Beyond-semilocal corrections including DFT+U, range-separated hybrids, and the GW quasiparticle approximation are analyzed for the band-gap problem. Benchmarks against experimental lattice parameters, formation enthalpies, and band gaps quantify accuracy; the Materials Project demonstrates DFT at genome scale. We conclude by confronting the theory's persistent failures — strong correlation, dispersion, self-interaction — and chart routes toward systematically improvable functionals.

## 1 Introduction

The quantum-mechanical many-electron problem is, in its raw form, intractable. The time-independent Schrödinger equation for \(N\) electrons,

> **Theorem (Exponential wall):** The exact ground-state wavefunction \(\Psi(\mathbf{r}_1, \sigma_1, \dots, \mathbf{r}_N, \sigma_N)\) is a function of \(3N\) spatial coordinates. For a modest \(N = 100\), storage alone demands on the order of \(10^{200}\) numbers — more than the number of atoms in the observable universe. Direct solution is not merely difficult; it is *forbidden by combinatorics*.

Approximate wavefunction methods — Hartree–Fock, coupled cluster, full configuration interaction — trade this complexity against controllable error, but their computational cost scales steeply: \(O(N^5)\) to \(O(N^7)\) and beyond. For the extended systems of condensed-matter physics, containing effectively \(10^{23}\) electrons, an entirely different strategy is required.

Density functional theory provides that strategy. Rather than computing the \(3N\)-dimensional wavefunction, DFT asserts that *the entire ground state of an interacting electron system is encoded in a single three-dimensional object*: the ground-state electron density \(n(\mathbf{r})\). Every observable — total energy, forces on nuclei, magnetic moments, phonon frequencies — is, in principle, a functional of this density. The revolution this enabled cannot be overstated: it converted quantum many-body physics into an optimization problem over a scalar field, one whose cost scales approximately as \(O(N^3)\), and which is sufficiently robust that millions of distinct materials have been computed and archived in high-throughput databases [9].

We begin with the two foundational theorems of Hohenberg and Kohn [1], formalize the variational principle through Levy's constrained search [3], derive the Kohn–Sham auxiliary system [2], climb the ladder of exchange–correlation approximations from LDA through meta-GGAs to hybrids [4][6], and examine the numerical machinery — plane waves, pseudopotentials, projector augmented-wave methods [8] — that underpins solid-state codes. We then quantify where the theory succeeds and where it fails, and describe how ab initio materials design operates at scale today.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The many-electron Hamiltonian

Within the Born–Oppenheimer approximation the electronic Hamiltonian in atomic units reads

\[ \hat{H} = -\sum_i \frac{\nabla_i^2}{2} + \sum_{i<j} \frac{1}{|\mathbf{r}_i - \mathbf{r}_j|} + \sum_i v_{\text{ext}}(\mathbf{r}_i) \]

where \(\hat{T}\) is the kinetic energy, \(\hat{V}_{ee}\) the electron–electron Coulomb interaction, and \(v_{\text{ext}}\) the external potential due to the nuclei. The first two terms are *universal*; only \(v_{\text{ext}}\) varies between systems. That all complexity of chemistry resides in the external potential is the seed from which DFT grows.

### 2.2 Thomas–Fermi theory: the prehistory

The idea of using the density as the fundamental variable predates DFT by nearly four decades. The Thomas–Fermi model (1927) wrote the kinetic energy as a local functional of the density,

\[ T_{\text{TF}}[n] = C_F \int n(\mathbf{r})^{5/3}\, d^3r, \qquad C_F = \frac{3}{10}(3\pi^2)^{2/3}, \]

treating electrons locally as a uniform gas. Combined with classical electrostatics it produced a variational principle for the density. Yet Thomas–Fermi theory cannot bind molecules (the Teller no-binding theorem, 1962) and ignores shell structure entirely. The failure is instructive — it is the kinetic-energy functional that fails, not the density-variable program itself. The resolution arrived in two stages: Hohenberg and Kohn proved existence and variationality [1]; Kohn and Sham solved the kinetic-energy problem exactly for a reference system [2].

### 2.3 Hartree and Hartree–Fock: the orbital prelude

The Hartree method (1928) replaced each electron's interaction with the mean field of all others, and Hartree–Fock (1930) added antisymmetrization and the exchange integral. These are *orbital theories*: single-particle wavefunctions \(\phi_i\) make the problem manageable. Kohn–Sham DFT inherits the orbital picture while shifting the approximation target from the wavefunction to the exchange–correlation energy — a far smaller, more forgiving quantity [10].

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 The Hohenberg–Kohn theorems

> **Theorem 1 (Hohenberg–Kohn I):** For any system of interacting electrons in an external potential \(v_{\text{ext}}(\mathbf{r})\), the potential is determined — up to an irrelevant additive constant — *uniquely* by the ground-state density \(n(\mathbf{r})\).

Assume two potentials \(v\) and \(v'\) differing by more than a constant yield the *same* ground-state density \(n\). The variational principle then gives \(E_0 + E'_0 < E'_0 + E_0\) — a contradiction [1]. Since \(n\) determines \(v_{\text{ext}}\), which determines \(\hat{H}\), which determines \(\Psi_0\), *every ground-state observable is a functional of the density*.

> **Theorem 2 (Hohenberg–Kohn II):** There exists a universal functional \(F[n] = T[n] + V_{ee}[n]\), independent of the external potential, such that the total energy functional \(E[n] = F[n] + \int v_{\text{ext}}(\mathbf{r}) n(\mathbf{r})\, d^3r\) attains its minimum at the true ground-state density, and this minimum is the ground-state energy.

The universality of \(F[n]\) is profound: one functional governs atoms, molecules, and solids alike. The theorems, however, are *existence results*. They give no recipe for \(F[n]\). Moreover, the original proof requires non-degenerate ground states and *v*-representability — the assumption that every admissible density is the ground-state density of some potential. Levy's constrained-search formulation [3] removes both restrictions elegantly:

\[ F[n] = \min_{\Psi \to n} \langle \Psi | \hat{T} + \hat{V}_{ee} | \Psi \rangle \]

where the search runs over all antisymmetric \(N\)-electron wavefunctions yielding the prescribed density \(n\). This extends \(F\) to all *N*-representable densities and handles degeneracy. The universality is preserved; computing \(F[n]\) exactly remains as hard as solving the many-body problem itself.

### 3.2 The Kohn–Sham construction

Kohn and Sham's masterstroke was to abandon the direct approximation of \(T[n]\) and instead partition the universal functional [2]:

\[ E[n] = T_s[n] + \int v_{\text{ext}} n \, d^3r + E_H[n] + E_{xc}[n] \]

where

- \(T_s[n]\) is the kinetic energy of a *fictitious non-interacting system* of orbitals \(\phi_i\) that reproduces the density \(n(\mathbf{r}) = \sum_{i=1}^{N} |\phi_i(\mathbf{r})|^2\). This term, which carries the lion's share of the true kinetic energy, is computed *exactly* from the orbitals: \(T_s = \sum_i \langle \phi_i | -\nabla^2/2 | \phi_i \rangle\).
- \(E_H[n] = \frac{1}{2}\iint n(\mathbf{r})n(\mathbf{r}')/|\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}'|\, d^3r\, d^3r'\) is the classical Hartree repulsion.
- \(E_{xc}[n]\) is the **exchange–correlation energy** — everything else: \(E_{xc} = (T - T_s) + (V_{ee} - E_H)\), the kinetic-energy correction plus the non-classical electron–electron interaction.

The minimizing orbitals satisfy the **Kohn–Sham equations**:

> **Theorem (Kohn–Sham equations):** The minimizing orbitals satisfy single-particle Schrödinger equations
>
> \[ \left[ -\frac{\nabla^2}{2} + v_{\text{ext}}(\mathbf{r}) + v_H(\mathbf{r}) + v_{xc}(\mathbf{r}) \right] \phi_i(\mathbf{r}) = \varepsilon_i \phi_i(\mathbf{r}) \]
>
> with \(v_H(\mathbf{r}) = \int n(\mathbf{r}')/|\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}'|\, d^3r'\) and \(v_{xc}(\mathbf{r}) = \delta E_{xc}[n]/\delta n(\mathbf{r})\), solved self-consistently with \(n(\mathbf{r}) = \sum_{i}^{N} |\phi_i(\mathbf{r})|^2\).

Practical DFT thus reduces to two tasks: approximating \(E_{xc}[n]\) and solving the SCF problem efficiently.

### 3.3 The self-consistent field cycle

A typical SCF iteration proceeds as follows:

1. **Guess** an initial density \(n^{(0)}\) (e.g., superposition of atomic densities).
2. **Construct** the effective potential \(v_{\text{eff}}^{(k)} = v_{\text{ext}} + v_H[n^{(k)}] + v_{xc}[n^{(k)}]\).
3. **Diagonalize** the Kohn–Sham Hamiltonian to obtain orbitals \(\{\phi_i^{(k)}\}\) and eigenvalues \(\{\varepsilon_i^{(k)}\}\).
4. **Build** the output density \(n_{\text{out}}^{(k)}(\mathbf{r}) = \sum_i f_i |\phi_i^{(k)}(\mathbf{r})|^2\) with Fermi occupations \(f_i\).
5. **Mix** output and input densities (Pulay/Broyden) to form \(n^{(k+1)}\); stop when the density and energy are converged, else return to step 2.

A minimal illustration in Python (using exact diagonalization of a model Hamiltonian) captures the fixed-point structure:

```python
import numpy as np

def scf_loop(H_core, eri_contraction, n_elec, max_iter=100, tol=1e-8, mix=0.5):
    """Self-consistent field iteration for a model Kohn-Sham problem."""
    n_basis = H_core.shape[0]
    P = np.zeros((n_basis, n_basis))  # initial guess: zero density
    E_old = 0.0
    for it in range(max_iter):
        J, K, v_xc = eri_contraction(P)
        F = H_core + J + v_xc          # KS Fock matrix (K folded into v_xc)
        eps, C = np.linalg.eigh(F)
        occ = C[:, :n_elec // 2]       # closed-shell aufbau
        P_new = 2.0 * occ @ occ.T
        E = np.trace(P_new @ (H_core + 0.5 * (J + v_xc)))
        dE, dP = abs(E - E_old), np.linalg.norm(P_new - P)
        print(f"iter {it:3d}  E = {E:16.10f}  dE = {dE:.2e}  dP = {dP:.2e}")
        P = mix * P_new + (1 - mix) * P  # linear density mixing
        if dE < tol and it > 0:
            return E, P, eps
        E_old = E
    raise RuntimeError("SCF did not converge")
```

In production codes the mixing is far more sophisticated (Pulay's DIIS, Kerker-preconditioned Broyden) and the diagonalization uses iterative eigensolvers (Davidson, RMM-DIIS), but the logical skeleton above is universal.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Jacob's Ladder: the hierarchy of exchange–correlation functionals

The exchange–correlation energy is the *only* approximated quantity in Kohn–Sham DFT, and forty years of functional development can be organized on Perdew's **Jacob's ladder** [5], where each rung adds a new ingredient to \(E_{xc}\) and ascends toward the heaven of chemical accuracy:

| Rung | Name | Ingredients | Example | Typical atomization error |
|------|------|-------------|---------|---------------------------|
| 1 | Hartree | \(E_{xc} = 0\) | — | ~10 eV (no binding correction) |
| 2 | LDA/LSDA | \(n(\mathbf{r})\) | PW92 | ~1.3 eV, overbinding |
| 3 | GGA | \(n, \nabla n\) | PBE [4] | ~0.4 eV |
| 4 | meta-GGA | \(n, \nabla n, \tau\) | SCAN [6], TPSS | ~0.2 eV |
| 5 | Hybrid | + exact exchange | PBE0, HSE06 | ~0.15 eV |

**Rung 2 — the local density approximation (LDA).** The LDA replaces the true exchange–correlation energy density at each point by that of a uniform electron gas of the local density:

\[ E_{xc}^{\text{LDA}}[n] = \int n(\mathbf{r})\, \epsilon_{xc}^{\text{unif}}(n(\mathbf{r}))\, d^3r \]

where \(\epsilon_x^{\text{unif}} = -\tfrac{3}{4}(3n/\pi)^{1/3}\) is known exactly (Dirac, 1930) and \(\epsilon_c^{\text{unif}}\) is parametrized from quantum Monte Carlo data (Ceperley–Alder, parametrized by Perdew–Wang). LDA is exact for the uniform gas and surprisingly serviceable elsewhere, but it *systematically overbinds*: lattice constants are underestimated by 1–3% and atomization energies overestimated.

**Rung 3 — generalized gradient approximations.** GGAs incorporate the density gradient through the dimensionless reduced gradient \(s = |\nabla n|/(2(3\pi^2)^{1/3} n^{4/3})\), writing \(E_{xc}^{\text{GGA}} = \int n\, \epsilon_x^{\text{unif}} F_{xc}(n, s)\, d^3r\). The PBE functional [4] is the landmark: it is *non-empirical* — all its parameters fixed by exact constraints (the Lieb–Oxford bound, correct uniform scaling, linear response of the uniform gas) — yet it became the workhorse of materials science. PBE corrects LDA's overbinding and yields lattice constants accurate to ~1% and formation energies to ~0.2 eV/atom, at essentially LDA cost.

**Rung 4 — meta-GGAs.** Adding the positive-definite kinetic-energy density \(\tau(\mathbf{r}) = \tfrac{1}{2}\sum_i |\nabla \phi_i(\mathbf{r})|^2\) lets a functional distinguish *bond types*: the dimensionless ratio \(\alpha = (\tau - \tau_W)/\tau_{\text{unif}}\) (with \(\tau_W\) the von Weizsäcker single-orbital limit) is \(\alpha \approx 0\) for covalent single bonds, \(\alpha \approx 1\) for metallic regions, and \(\alpha \gg 1\) for weak noncovalent overlap. SCAN [6] — strongly constrained and appropriately normed — satisfies *all 17 known exact constraints* a meta-GGA can satisfy, achieving hybrid-like accuracy for diversely bonded systems at near-GGA cost.

**Rung 5 — hybrids.** Hybrid functionals admix a fraction \(a\) of *exact* (Hartree–Fock) exchange computed from the Kohn–Sham orbitals:

\[ E_{xc}^{\text{hyb}} = a E_x^{\text{exact}} + (1-a) E_x^{\text{GGA}} + E_c^{\text{GGA}} \]

The rationale is the *adiabatic connection*: the true \(E_{xc}\) interpolates between the non-interacting (exact exchange) and fully interacting limits, and perturbation theory suggests \(a \approx 1/4\) (PBE0). Screened hybrids such as HSE06 retain exact exchange only at short range — recovering accurate semiconductor band gaps at manageable cost for solids.

### 4.2 Plane waves, pseudopotentials, and the PAW method

For periodic crystals, the Kohn–Sham orbitals obey Bloch's theorem, \(\phi_{n\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{r}) = e^{i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{r}} u_{n\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{r})\), and the periodic part \(u_{n\mathbf{k}}\) expands naturally in **plane waves**,

\[ u_{n\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{r}) = \sum_{|\mathbf{G}|^2/2 < E_{\text{cut}}} c_{n\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{G})\, e^{i\mathbf{G}\cdot\mathbf{r}}, \]

truncated at a cutoff energy \(E_{\text{cut}}\). Plane waves are unbiased and systematically improvable, and make the Hartree term trivial via FFTs; their weakness is the violent core-electron oscillations near nuclei, which would demand enormous cutoffs.

The **pseudopotential** approximation replaces the nucleus plus core electrons with a smooth effective potential reproducing valence scattering beyond a cutoff radius \(r_c\), keeping pseudo-wavefunctions nodeless. The **projector augmented-wave (PAW) method** [8] goes further: a linear transformation \(|\Psi\rangle = \mathcal{T}|\tilde{\Psi}\rangle\) reconstructs the true all-electron wavefunctions from the smooth pseudo-wavefunctions, recovering all-electron accuracy (core-level shifts, electric field gradients) at pseudopotential cost. PAW is the engine inside VASP, GPAW, and ABINIT — and hence inside most of the world's materials computations.

A converged calculation typically uses plane waves with \(E_{\text{cut}} \approx\) 400–800 eV for PAW-PBE, Monkhorst–Pack k-point meshes (dense with Fermi smearing for metals), and analytic Hellmann–Feynman forces plus stress for structural relaxation and *ab initio* molecular dynamics [10]. The canonical scaling is \(O(N^3)\), dominated by orthogonalization; linear-scaling \(O(N)\) methods exist via localized orbitals and density-matrix truncation.

### 4.3 Beyond semilocal approximations: DFT+U, hybrids, and GW

Semilocal functionals fail qualitatively for **strongly correlated** materials — Mott insulators such as NiO or FeO, where PBE predicts a metal. The failure is the *self-interaction error*: an electron spuriously repels itself through the Hartree term, over-delocalizing \(d\) and \(f\) orbitals. **DFT+U** adds a Hubbard-like on-site penalty [7],

\[ E_{\text{DFT+U}} = E_{\text{DFT}} + \frac{U_{\text{eff}}}{2} \sum_{m,\sigma} \left(n_{m\sigma} - n_{m\sigma}^2\right), \]

which drives integer occupation of localized orbitals and opens gaps in transition-metal oxides. With \(U_{\text{eff}} \approx\) 3–6 eV, oxidation energies of transition-metal oxides — catastrophically wrong in PBE — become chemically useful [7]. The price is empiricism, though linear-response and cRPA schemes can compute \(U\) ab initio.

The **band-gap problem** is deeper. The Kohn–Sham gap \(\varepsilon_{\text{LUMO}} - \varepsilon_{\text{HOMO}}\) is *not* the fundamental gap \(I - A\); they differ by the derivative discontinuity \(\Delta_{xc}\) of the exact functional, which no semilocal functional possesses. Hence PBE underestimates gaps by 30–50% (silicon: 0.6 eV vs. experiment 1.17 eV). Range-separated hybrids (HSE06: 1.2 eV) and the **GW approximation** — quasiparticle energies from \(\Sigma = iGW\) — restore quantitative gaps (silicon GW: ~1.2 eV) at \(O(N^4)\) cost, making GW the gold standard for band structures.

### 4.4 Dispersion and the long-range tail

Semilocal functionals are blind to **van der Waals dispersion**: the \(-C_6/R^6\) attraction arises from correlated fluctuations at long range, invisible to any functional of the local density and its gradients. Two remedies dominate: pairwise corrections (Grimme's D3/D4), adding damped \(-C_6/R^6\) terms at negligible cost, and nonlocal van der Waals density functionals (vdW-DF family), which evaluate a double integral over the density with a model response kernel. Dispersion corrections are mandatory for molecular crystals, layered materials, and surface adsorption — anywhere weak bonds determine the physics.

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 Formal exactness and its boundaries

The Hohenberg–Kohn theorems are *mathematically proven* statements about the exact functional [1][3]: if \(E_{xc}[n]\) were known exactly, Kohn–Sham DFT would yield exact ground-state energies and densities. Every practical limitation of DFT is therefore a limitation of the *approximate* \(E_{xc}\), never of the framework. This clean separation of exact scaffolding from approximate content is unique among electronic-structure methods and is the reason DFT can be improved systematically: better functionals, better results, same equations.

### 5.2 Benchmarks against experiment

| Property | LDA | PBE [4] | SCAN [6] | HSE06 | Experiment |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Lattice constant MAE (Å) | 0.06–0.09 (under) | 0.04–0.06 (over) | 0.02–0.03 | 0.02 | — |
| Cohesive energy MAE (eV/atom) | 0.5–0.7 | 0.2–0.3 | 0.1–0.2 | 0.1 | — |
| Formation enthalpy MAE (eV/atom) | 0.3–0.4 | 0.15–0.25 | 0.10–0.15 | 0.1 | — |
| Si band gap (eV) | 0.5 | 0.6 | 0.8 | 1.2 | 1.17 |
| Atomization energy MAE (eV) | 1.3 | 0.4 | 0.2 | 0.15 | — |

PBE lattice constants are accurate to roughly 1%, sufficient to predict crystal structures *de novo*, and formation enthalpies at the 0.1–0.2 eV/atom level enable convex-hull phase-stability analysis — a criterion that has predicted thousands of real materials. The band-gap column exposes the systematic semilocal failure and its hybrid/GW remedy: a missing derivative discontinuity \(\Delta_{xc}\), not a failure of DFT itself.

### 5.3 DFT at genome scale: the Materials Project

The ultimate empirical validation of DFT is operational: an entire field now *designs materials computationally before synthesizing them*. The Materials Project [9] has computed, with standardized PBE(+U) protocols, the structures, formation energies, elastic tensors, and band structures of well over 150,000 inorganic compounds, all openly queryable. Its convex-hull phase diagrams guide synthesis: compounds predicted near the hull are prioritized for experimental realization, and its battery-electrode screening identified cathode candidates later confirmed in the laboratory [9]. When a theory's predictions are trusted to allocate millions of dollars of synthesis effort, it has passed from mathematics into engineering.

## 6 Limitations

DFT's limitations are well-characterized — a theory whose errors are *systematic* can be corrected:

1. **Strong correlation.** Semilocal functionals cannot describe Mott insulators, heavy-fermion systems, or multireference bond dissociation. DFT+U and hybrids patch the symptoms; DFT+DMFT addresses the disease at far greater cost.
2. **Self-interaction error.** Spurious Hartree self-repulsion over-delocalizes electrons, underestimates reaction barriers, and misplaces charge-transfer excitations. Perdew–Zunger correction exists but is orbital-dependent and cumbersome.
3. **The band-gap problem.** Semilocal Kohn–Sham gaps underestimate fundamental gaps by roughly a factor of two; the missing derivative discontinuity \(\Delta_{xc}\) is a property no semilocal functional can possess.
4. **Dispersion interactions.** Without explicit corrections (§4.4), semilocal DFT misses van der Waals binding entirely — catastrophic for supramolecular chemistry and layered materials.
5. **Excited states and dynamics.** Ground-state DFT is a ground-state theory. TDDFT extends it via the Runge–Gross theorem, but adiabatic approximations fail for charge-transfer and Rydberg excitations, double excitations, and conical intersections.
6. **No guaranteed monotonic improvement.** Climbing Jacob's ladder offers no guarantee — the fifth rung can underperform the third for specific properties (e.g., some hybrids worsen metallic lattice constants).
7. **Computational scaling.** Canonical \(O(N^3)\) scaling with a large prefactor limits routine calculations to a few hundred atoms; thousand-atom systems need linear-scaling implementations with their own approximations.

These limitations define the active research frontier: machine-learned functionals trained on exact constraints and high-level data, the random phase approximation as a fifth-rung correlation treatment, and embedding theories that graft wavefunction accuracy onto DFT scalability.

## 7 Conclusion

In six decades, density functional theory has traveled from two short papers in *Physical Review* [1][2] to the computational backbone of chemistry, physics, and materials science. The arc is clean: Hohenberg and Kohn proved that the density suffices; Levy made the variational principle rigorous; Kohn and Sham made the kinetic energy computable and isolated all ignorance into a single, small, refinable object — the exchange–correlation energy. The subsequent half-century has been the progressive refinement of that object, from the uniform-gas local approximation through PBE's constraint satisfaction [4] to SCAN's seventeen exact constraints [6] and the screened exact exchange of modern hybrids.

The practical consequence is a theory that predicts crystal structures to 1%, formation energies to 0.1 eV/atom, and — with GW corrections — band gaps to 0.1 eV, cheaply enough to evaluate millions of compounds and thereby invert the materials-discovery pipeline from experiment-led to computation-led [9]. Its failures are understood, bounded, and actively shrinking. If the twentieth century belonged to the wavefunction, the twenty-first — so far — belongs to the density.
