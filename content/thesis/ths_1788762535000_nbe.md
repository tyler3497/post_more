# Type-Directed Partial Evaluation and Normalization by Evaluation: From Two-Level Languages to Dependent Types and Certified Compilation

## Abstract

Normalization by evaluation (NbE) and type-directed partial evaluation (TDPE) are two faces of a single deep idea: semantics can be inverted. Where classical normalization proceeds by syntactic rewriting, NbE evaluates an open term into a semantic domain and then reifies the semantic value back into a normal-form term, guided at every step by the type. Danvy's TDPE extends this architecture to staged, two-level languages, where binding-time annotations separate static from dynamic computation and the very same reify/reflect coercions drive offline and online program specialization. This thesis develops the unified theory: the two-level lambda calculus and binding-time analysis; NbE for the simply typed lambda calculus with its reflect/reify construction and Kripke-style logical relations; the extension to dependent types, where normalization proofs yield canonicity and decidability of conversion; and the deployment of these algorithms in certified compilers and proof assistants such as Coq, Agda, and Lean, where efficient strong reduction is a trust-critical kernel operation.

---

## 1 Introduction

Computing *normal forms* is among the oldest problems in computation. In the untyped lambda calculus, normalization by rewriting is fragile: strategies diverge, confluence must be proved, efficiency is elusive. In *typed* calculi every well-typed term normalizes — yet *how* to compute normal forms efficiently, and prove it correct, stayed open for decades after Church and Tait.

In 1991, Berger and Schwichtenberg proposed a radical alternative [1]: instead of rewriting terms, *evaluate* them in a semantic domain, then *quote* the value back into syntax via the *inverse of the evaluation functional*. Host-language evaluation performs all beta reductions at once, so the algorithm is strikingly efficient, and type-guided quotation yields eta-expansion for free. This is *normalization by evaluation* (NbE). Five years later, Danvy recognized NbE as the computational content of *partial evaluation* [2, 3]: TDPE reformulates specialization as NbE over a *two-level* language, where the static level is evaluated away, the dynamic level is reconstructed as residual code, and the type dictates how the levels interact.

This thesis unifies these developments: (1) the **two-level lambda calculus** and **binding-time analysis** behind TDPE; (2) **NbE for STLC**, with its reflect/reify construction and logical-relations correctness; (3) **NbE for dependent types**, scaling to normalization proofs, canonicity, and decidable conversion; (4) **applications in certified compilers and proof assistants**, where NbE is now trust-critical kernel infrastructure. The central claim: TDPE and NbE are the *same* technique through different lenses — reify and reflect are the fundamental operations, and the type system is the conductor.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Partial evaluation and binding times

Partial evaluation (Futamura, Ershov, Jones et al. [11]) specializes a program with respect to part of its input: given a static/dynamic input division, it produces a *residual program* computing the same result faster. The Futamura projections show partial evaluation is the engine of compiler generation itself.

A *binding-time analysis* (BTA) annotates each operation as static (performed during specialization) or dynamic (residualized into code). *Offline* PE runs BTA as a separate phase; *online* PE decides binding times during specialization. The annotated program is then interpreted by a *two-level* evaluator: static constructs are evaluated, dynamic ones reconstructed as syntax.

### 2.2 The normalization problem

For STLC, *normalization* states that every well-typed term has a normal form. Traditional proofs use Tait's *logical relations*: a type-indexed predicate on terms, defined at base types syntactically and at function types by closure under application, shown to contain every well-typed term [4]. Normalization *algorithms* traditionally rewrite — contracting redexes per some strategy — requiring explicit substitution machinery and the full apparatus of rewriting theory (confluence, standardization). NbE sidesteps all of this by delegating reduction to the *meta-language* evaluator.

### 2.3 Danvy's synthesis

Danvy's key observation: the two-level evaluator of offline partial evaluation *is* NbE [2]. The static level is semantic evaluation; the dynamic level is reification of values into residual syntax; the binding-time coercions are precisely NbE's *reflect* and *reify*, indexed by type. Advances thus transfer both ways — coproducts, delimited control, and dependent types flow into TDPE, while online specialization and let-insertion flow back into NbE.

---

## 3 Methodology

Our methodology is *semantic and constructive*: normalization and specialization functions are defined by structural recursion over types and terms (Haskell/OCaml here; Agda/Coq in the surveyed formalizations), and correctness is established by *logical relations* — type-indexed predicates relating syntax and semantics — never by rewriting arguments.

The development proceeds in three stages: (1) **two-level staging** — the two-level lambda calculus, binding-time discipline, and Danvy's type-indexed reify/reflect coercions, with offline and online TDPE variants; (2) **NbE for STLC** — the semantic domain, evaluation, and reflect/reify, with *soundness* and *completeness* via a Kripke logical relation; (3) **dependent types** — the glued-model generalization, universes and large eliminations, following Abel's normalization proof for Martin-Löf type theory [6] and its decidability corollaries.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Type-directed partial evaluation and the two-level lambda calculus

The two-level lambda calculus tags every constructor with its binding time — *static* (overlined) or *dynamic*. Thus `λ̅x. t` is a static abstraction (eliminated during specialization), `λx. t` a dynamic one (reconstructed into the residual program), `t₁ @̅ t₂` static application (performed at specialization time), and `t₁ @ t₂` dynamic application (residualized as code). A *binding-time analysis* produces such annotated terms; its correctness criterion is *congruence*: erasing annotations and evaluating must agree with evaluating the original term. In Danvy's formulation [3], BTA is a type-directed translation, and the annotated calculus enjoys subject reduction with a strongly normalizing static fragment.

The heart of TDPE is a pair of *type-indexed* coercions, with `Exp` the type of residual expressions:

- **reify** `↓_τ : ⟦τ⟧ → Exp` — semantic (static) value to residual code;
- **reflect** `↑_τ : Exp → ⟦τ⟧` — residual code to a semantic value that builds larger code fragments when used.

At base type `↓` is the identity on code and `↑` embeds residual expressions as values; at function type the definitions are mutually recursive:

```haskell
-- Semantic domain: static values are host functions,
-- dynamic values are residual expressions
data Sem = SInt Int | SFun (Sem -> Sem) | DExp Exp

reify :: Ty -> Sem -> Exp
reify TInt  (SInt n) = Lit n
reify TInt  (DExp e) = e
reify (TArr s t) (SFun f) = Lam (\x -> reify t (f (reflect s (Var x))))
reify (TArr s t) (DExp e) = e  -- eta-expand via reflect below

reflect :: Ty -> Exp -> Sem
reflect TInt  e = DExp e
reflect (TArr s t) e = SFun (\v -> reflect t (App e (reify s v)))
```

The choreography: reifying a function *applies it to a reflected fresh variable*, forcing static computation now while residualizing dynamic parts; reflecting code *wraps it in a host function* that builds application nodes when applied. This is NbE with the semantic domain stratified into static and dynamic layers — "evaluation" performs specialization, "reification" emits the residual program [2].

> **Theorem (TDPE correctness, Danvy):** Let `t` be a well-typed two-level term. Then `↓(⟦t⟧)` is a well-typed residual term, and erasing annotations commutes with evaluation: specializing and then running the residual program yields the same result as running the original program.

Danvy's *online* TDPE [3] makes binding-time decisions during specialization: a dynamic conditional with a static test can still be reduced, and *let-insertion* avoids the code duplication that plagues naive specialization. Implemented in Scheme with quasiquote/unquote as the two levels, TDPE becomes a *macro-expansion* discipline — staging as typed, hygienic code generation.

| Aspect | Offline PE | Online TDPE |
|---|---|---|
| Binding times | Fixed by prior BTA | Decided during specialization |
| Termination | Guaranteed by BTA | Requires care (memoization, well-foundedness) |
| Precision | Limited by BTA analysis | Maximally precise, context-sensitive |
| Implementation | Two phases | Single interleaved phase |
| NbE reading | Evaluation in a two-level model | Reify/reflect with dynamic tests |

### 4.2 Normalization by evaluation for the simply typed lambda calculus

Stripped of staging, TDPE reduces to NbE. Berger and Schwichtenberg's canonical presentation [1], refined by Altenkirch, Hofmann, and Streicher, interprets typed syntax into a semantic domain defined by recursion on types:

```haskell
data Ty   = Base | Arr Ty Ty

data Tm   = Var Name | Lam Name Tm | App Tm Tm

data Val  = VAbs (Val -> Val)   -- semantic functions (host closures)
          | VNe Ne              -- neutral (stuck) terms

data Ne   = NVar Name | NApp Ne Val

-- Evaluation: compositional interpretation into the domain
eval :: Env -> Tm -> Val
eval env (Var x)   = lookup x env
eval env (Lam x t) = VAbs (\v -> eval (extend x v env) t)
eval env (App f a) = apply (eval env f) (eval env a)
  where apply (VAbs g) v = g v
        apply (VNe n)  v = VNe (NApp n v)

-- Reification: quote a semantic value back to a normal term
reify :: Ty -> Val -> Tm
reify Base       (VNe n)  = quoteNe n
reify (Arr s t)  f        = Lam x (reify t (apply f (reflect s (NVar x))))
  where x = fresh

-- Reflection: embed a neutral term as a semantic value
reflect :: Ty -> Ne -> Val
reflect Base       n = VNe n
reflect (Arr s t)  n = VAbs (\v -> reflect t (NApp n (reify s v)))

-- Normalization: evaluate, then reify
norm :: Ty -> Tm -> Tm
norm ty t = reify ty (eval empty t)
```

Several points deserve emphasis. **Host-language reuse**: beta reduction is performed by the *meta-language's* function application, so NbE inherits the host evaluator's efficiency and correctness. **Type-directed eta-expansion**: `reify (Arr s t)` applies the semantic function to a reflected fresh variable and reifies under a binder, producing *eta-long beta-normal forms* with no rewriting. **Open terms**: free variables become neutral terms — computations *stuck* on an unknown — and the domain is generalized to a *presheaf* over contexts (Kripke semantics) so fresh variables can be introduced during reification [5, 8]. **No rewriting theory**: correctness uses a logical relation between syntax and semantics, never reduction sequences — Streicher's "reduction-free" normalization proof [9].

> **Theorem (Soundness of NbE):** For every well-typed term `Γ ⊢ t : τ`, the normal form `norm τ t` satisfies `Γ ⊢ norm τ t ≡ t : τ` in the beta-eta equational theory.

> **Theorem (Completeness of NbE):** If `Γ ⊢ t ≡ u : τ` then `norm τ t = norm τ u` syntactically. Hence NbE *decides* beta-eta convertibility for STLC.

Extensions abound: Altenkirch et al. extended NbE to *strong sum types*, where commuting conversions demand a monadic semantic domain [4]; Abel and Sattler treated *call-by-push-value* and polarized calculi, showing evaluation order is reflected in monad placement [5]; and connections with *delimited control* (shift/reset) reveal classical principles as control operators in the normalizer.

### 4.3 NbE for dependent types: normalization proofs, canonicity, decidability

The true stress test of NbE is *dependent type theory*, where types contain terms and conversion must be decided during type checking. Here NbE is not merely an algorithm but the engine of a *normalization proof* with sweeping metatheoretic consequences: *canonicity* (every closed term of natural-number type computes to a numeral), *decidability of conversion*, *decidability of type checking*, and *consistency*.

The generalization is conceptually direct but technically demanding: the domain must model a *type theory* — dependent functions `Π(x:A). B`, universes, inductive families. Abel's approach [6] interprets types as *presheaves* with a realizability-like logical relation — a *glued* model pairing every semantic type with a syntactic *quote* function. Reflect/reify become *quote* and *unquote*: at `Π(x:A). B`, quoting a semantic function `f` yields `λx. q (f (u x))`, eta-expansion where the fresh variable is unquoted at the domain, applied, and the result quoted at the substituted codomain — with type dependency tracked semantically. Universes become codes for semantic types, with large eliminations handled by interpreting codes as type formers. The fundamental lemma relates every well-typed term to its denotation; instantiating at closed terms yields normalization, and a *canonicity* argument shows closed normal forms of inductive type are canonical constructors [7].

> **Theorem (Normalization for MLTT, Abel):** Every well-typed term of Martin-Löf type theory with universes and inductive families has a normal form computable by NbE, and definitional equality is decidable.

A landmark consequence is decidability of conversion *internalized* in type theory itself: Abel et al. showed conversion for a fragment of type theory is decidable *within* type theory, with direct implications for proof-assistant kernel design [6]. Coquand's streamlined presentation of canonicity and normalization [7] emphasizes that NbE's semantic proof avoids rewriting bureaucracy — no confluence, no standardization, no explicit substitutions in the metatheory.

### 4.4 Applications in certified compilers and proof assistants

NbE has crossed the boundary from metatheory into *systems that people trust with their proofs*.

**Coq.** Grégoire and Leroy demonstrated a *compiled* implementation of strong reduction — NbE compiled to native code — with order-of-magnitude speedups on conversion-heavy developments; Boespflug, Dénès, and Grégoire's "full reduction at full throttle" pushed further with locally nameless open terms. Modern Coq's `native_compute` and `vm_compute` are the industrial descendants of the Berger–Schwichtenberg idea: evaluate in a fast substrate, quote back, let the type direct the quotation.

**Agda.** Agda's type checker is built around an NbE-style evaluator: terms become *values* (closures and neutrals), conversion is checked by comparing values modulo eta-expansion, and quotation happens only when a normal form must be displayed — the architecture that lets Agda's unification scale.

**Isabelle/HOL and Lean.** Aehlig, Haftmann, and Nipkow compiled NbE into ML for Isabelle, achieving native-speed normalization near the trusted kernel. Lean 4's kernel adopts the same evaluate-then-compare discipline for definitional unfolding.

**Certified compilation.** TDPE provides the *staging* story for verified compilation: a compiler is a partial evaluator specializing an interpreter (the first Futamura projection), and the type-indexed coercions ensure residual code is well-typed *by construction* — binding-time annotations become a certificate that static computation was fully discharged.

The practical lesson is uniform: *let the type do the work*. Reify and reflect are small, but indexed by types they compose into normalizers, specializers, and conversion checkers whose correctness proofs are local, modular, and mechanizable.

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

**Correctness theorems.** The argument factors into three lemmas: *reflect/reify inversion* (`reify_τ ∘ reflect_τ` is the identity on neutrals); the *fundamental lemma* (every well-typed term is related to its denotation); and *adequacy* (the relation at observable type implies convertibility). Soundness and completeness follow as corollaries. These proofs have been fully mechanized — in Agda by Kovács [10] and in Coq by several authors — and are notably shorter than rewriting-based proofs, since no substitution lemmas about reduction sequences are needed.

**Performance.** NbE's empirical claim is efficiency through host-language reuse:

| System | Technique | Reported effect |
|---|---|---|
| Minlog (Berger/Schwichtenberg) | Scheme-hosted NbE | First practical strong normalizer for extracted programs [1] |
| Coq kernel (Grégoire–Leroy) | Compiled strong reduction | Order-of-magnitude speedup on conversion tests |
| Coq `native_compute` | NbE via OCaml compilation | Near-native evaluation inside proofs |
| Isabelle/HOL (Aehlig et al.) | Compiled NbE | Native-speed normalization in the trusted base |
| Agda | Value-based conversion | Scales to large dependently typed developments |

**TDPE case studies.** Danvy's online TDPE [3] demonstrated interpreter specialization with *operand-probing primitive operators* — e.g., multiplication that reassociates and folds static products inside dynamic contexts and correctly handles *binding-time upset* (dynamic times static zero yielding static zero) — showing the coercions gracefully handle anomalies that defeat naive two-level evaluators.

**Metatheoretic payoffs.** The dependent-types program has delivered normalization for MLTT with universes [6], canonicity [7], decidable conversion and type checking, and a pathway to *self-certifying* kernels — each previously requiring formidable rewriting-theoretic arguments, now shorter, modular, and readily formalized.

> **Theorem (Decidability of conversion):** In the type theories covered by the NbE normalization proof, the relation `Γ ⊢ t ≡ u : A` is decidable: compute `norm(t)` and `norm(u)` and compare syntactically.

## 6 Limitations

Intellectual honesty requires acknowledging where the reify/reflect architecture strains.

**Coproducts and commuting conversions.** *Sum types* break the naive scheme: a neutral of sum type stuck under case analysis cannot be reified without deciding the unknowable injection. The fixes — monadic reflection [4], CPS semantics, delimited control — complicate domain and proof considerably, and Abel and Sattler's polarized/cbpv analysis [5] shows evaluation order must be made explicit to recover principled normal forms.

**Effects and control.** Pure NbE assumes a total, effect-free meta-language; state, exceptions, or general recursion demand step-indexing or domain theory, and the elegant proofs get much heavier. Online TDPE faces the dual problem: specialization *termination* is no longer by construction and must be enforced externally (memoization, binding-time improvements, fuel).

**Dependent types: the cost of gluing.** The glued-model proofs [6, 7] are clean but intricate: Russell vs. Tarski universes, cumulativity, large eliminations, and inductive families each add bureaucracy, and real proof assistants' definitional extensions (Coq's guard condition, Agda's sized types, Lean's quotients) each demand bespoke extensions of the normalization argument.

**Performance cliffs.** NbE's efficiency rides on the host evaluator; pathological terms can still blow up normal-form size exponentially (inherent to normalization). Quotation can dominate runtime and naive closures leak space, so production systems invest in representation engineering (locally nameless, sharing, machine integers) that the clean theory omits.

**Staging vs. typing.** TDPE is only as good as its binding-time analysis: poor annotations residualize too much (slow code) or attempt too much statically (non-termination, code explosion). Automatic *binding-time improvement* remains partly heuristic.

## 7 Conclusion

From Berger and Schwichtenberg's inverse of the evaluation functional [1], through Danvy's recognition that partial evaluation *is* NbE in a two-level language [2, 3], to modern normalization proofs for dependent type theory [6, 7] deployed in the kernels of Coq, Agda, and Isabelle, one architectural idea has proven astonishingly fertile: **evaluate into semantics, then let the type guide the quotation back to syntax**.

The reify/reflect pair is the atom of this architecture. Indexed by types it yields normalizers; stratified into two levels it yields specializers; glued over a presheaf model it yields normalization for dependent types and decidability of conversion. The type is not a static check but the *conductor* of computation. Open frontiers remain — NbE for effects and linearity, fully mechanized proofs covering production type theories, TDPE-based certified compilers with checkable staging certificates — but the semantic turn in normalization has moved from surprising trick to standard architecture, and type-directed quotation now sits inside the trusted computing base of modern formal mathematics.
