# A Unified Treatment of Optimal Control: Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman Dynamic Programming, Pontryagin's Maximum Principle, and Receding-Horizon Model Predictive Control

## Abstract

This treatise develops a unified exposition of the three foundational paradigms of deterministic optimal control: Richard Bellman's dynamic programming and the associated Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman (HJB) partial differential equation, Pontryagin's maximum principle with its Hamiltonian boundary-value structure, and receding-horizon model predictive control (MPC) as a computationally tractable synthesis of the two. We show how each framework answers the same question—characterizing optimal feedback for nonlinear dynamical systems subject to state and input constraints—from complementary vantage points: global sufficient conditions through the value function, local necessary conditions through costate dynamics, and online finite-horizon optimization with closed-loop stability guarantees. Central technical developments include the viscosity solution theory of Crandall and Lions, which restores well-posedness to the generically nonsmooth HJB equation; the needle-variation derivation of the maximum principle and its geometric interpretation; and the terminal-cost/terminal-set machinery that endows constrained MPC with recursive feasibility and asymptotic stability. A numerical study on minimum-time and linear–quadratic benchmarks quantifies the trade-offs among the three approaches, and we close with open problems at the interface of learning-based control and rigorous optimality.

## 1. Introduction

Optimal control theory asks a deceptively simple question: given a dynamical system whose evolution we can influence, how should we choose the influence—the *control*—so as to extremize a performance criterion over a prescribed horizon? The answers that have emerged since the 1950s form one of the great intellectual edifices of applied mathematics, with consequences spanning aerospace guidance, process control, robotics, economics, and, increasingly, machine learning.

Three pillars support the modern theory. The first is **dynamic programming**, introduced by Richard Bellman in the 1950s [1]. Its central object is the *value function*—the optimal cost-to-go from any state—and its engine is the *principle of optimality*, which reduces global optimization to a recursive functional equation. In continuous time this becomes the Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman PDE, whose smooth solutions furnish optimal feedback laws in closed form.

The second pillar is **Pontryagin's maximum principle** [2], developed by Lev Pontryagin and his school at the Steklov Institute [2]. Where dynamic programming is *global* and *sufficient*, the maximum principle is *local* and *necessary*: along any optimal trajectory there exists a costate trajectory such that the Hamiltonian is maximized pointwise over admissible controls, yielding a two-point boundary-value problem that characterizes extremals without certifying global optimality.

The third pillar is **model predictive control** (MPC), also known as receding-horizon control [5, 6]. MPC sidesteps the curse of dimensionality that afflicts dynamic programming and the initialization fragility of indirect methods by solving, at each sampling instant, a *finite-horizon* open-loop optimal control problem from the current measured state, applying only the first control move, and repeating. For two decades this was regarded as a heuristic; the landmark analysis of Mayne, Rawlings, Rao, and Scokaert [5] showed that, with suitable terminal ingredients, receding-horizon control inherits recursive feasibility, constraint satisfaction, and asymptotic stability [5].

This thesis presents these three frameworks not as rivals but as three projections of a single geometric reality. Section 2 fixes notation and the canonical problem class. Section 3 describes our comparative methodology. Section 4 develops each pillar in depth, including the viscosity solution theory that reconciles the HJB equation with nonsmooth value functions. Section 5 reports numerical experiments benchmarking the approaches on canonical problems. Sections 6 and 7 discuss limitations and conclusions.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 The Bolza problem

We consider the finite-horizon *Bolza problem*. Let the state evolve on ℝⁿ according to

> *ẋ(t) = f(x(t), u(t)), x(0) = x₀,*

with control *u(t) ∈ U ⊂ ℝᵐ*, where *U* is compact and *f* is continuous and locally Lipschitz in *x*. Admissible controls are taken to be measurable functions. The cost functional is

> *J(x₀, u(·)) = Ψ(x(T)) + ∫₀ᵀ L(x(t), u(t)) dt,*

where *L : ℝⁿ × U → ℝ* is the running cost and *Ψ : ℝⁿ → ℝ* the terminal cost. The objective is to find an admissible *u∗* minimizing *J*. Variants include free final time (the *minimum-time problem*, *L ≡ 1*), state constraints *x(t) ∈ X*, and infinite-horizon discounted or average-cost formulations.

### 2.2 The value function and the Hamiltonian

The **value function** is defined as

> *V(t, x) = inf_{u(·) admissible} { Ψ(x(T)) + ∫_tᵀ L(x(s), u(s)) ds },*

the optimal cost-to-go from state *x* at time *t*. Under mild hypotheses *V* is continuous, though generically *not* differentiable—a fact with profound consequences discussed in §4.2.

The **(control) Hamiltonian** couples dynamics and cost through a costate vector *λ ∈ ℝⁿ*:

> *H(x, λ, u) = L(x, u) + λᵀ f(x, u).*

The **minimized Hamiltonian** is *H∗(x, λ) = min_{u∈U} H(x, λ, u)*. Both recur throughout: Pontryagin's costate is formally *∂V/∂x*, and the MPC stage cost is the integrand the value function accumulates.

### 2.3 Standing assumptions

Throughout we assume:

1. *f* is continuous, locally Lipschitz in *x* uniformly in *u*, with at most linear growth;
2. *L* and *Ψ* are continuous, with *L* bounded below and coercive in the control where needed for existence;
3. the control set *U* is nonempty and compact;
4. for MPC, the system is stabilizable and the constraint sets are closed.

Existence follows from Filippov-type arguments under convexity of the velocity set; without convexity, *relaxed* controls restore it—a theme revisited in §6.

## 3. Methodology

Our treatment follows a **tripartite comparative methodology**:

- **Analytic derivation.** Optimality conditions for each framework are derived from first principles, making the logical dependencies among the three explicit.
- **Regularity analysis.** We track where smoothness is used and what replaces it: viscosity solutions, nonsmooth extensions, and set-theoretic terminal ingredients.
- **Computational benchmarking.** We implement grid-based value iteration, indirect shooting, and nonlinear MPC on minimum-time and linear–quadratic benchmarks, measuring achieved cost, constraint satisfaction, and wall-clock time.

The methodology is deliberately *constructive*: every theorem admits an algorithmic counterpart, exercised in §5.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman Equation and Dynamic Programming

Bellman's principle of optimality yields the **dynamic programming principle** (DPP): for any *0 ≤ t < t+δ ≤ T*,

> *V(t, x) = inf_{u(·)} { ∫_t^{t+δ} L ds + V(t+δ, x(t+δ)) }.*

Dividing by *δ* and letting *δ → 0*, under the hypothesis that *V* is *C¹*, one obtains the **Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman equation**

> *−∂_t V(t,x) = min_{u∈U} { L(x,u) + ∇_x V(t,x)·f(x,u) },   V(T,x) = Ψ(x).*

Equivalently, *−V_t + H∗(x, ∇V) = 0*, where *H∗* is the minimized Hamiltonian. This is a first-order, fully nonlinear PDE of Hamilton–Jacobi type.

> **Theorem:** (Verification theorem.) *Suppose V ∈ C¹ satisfies the HJB equation with terminal condition Ψ, and suppose u∗(t,x) attains the pointwise minimum in the Hamiltonian for each (t,x). Then the feedback control u∗ is optimal, and V coincides with the value function.*

The verification theorem is the crown jewel of the sufficient side: *any* smooth solution certifies global optimality. Its practical weaknesses—generic nonsmoothness of *V* and the *curse of dimensionality* of grid-based PDE solvers—motivated viscosity solution theory (§4.2) and approximate dynamic programming.

In discrete time the same ideas take the familiar form of the **Bellman recursion**, implemented below in Haskell as a pure function—a notation that makes the fixed-point character of dynamic programming transparent:

```haskell
-- States, actions and costs
type State  = [Double]
type Action = [Double]
type Cost   = Double

-- One Bellman backup: the optimal cost-to-go satisfies v = T v
bellman :: (State -> Cost)          -- current value estimate v
        -> (State -> [Action])      -- admissible actions
        -> (State -> Action -> State) -- transition model
        -> (State -> Action -> Cost)  -- stage cost
        -> Double                     -- discount factor
        -> State -> Cost
bellman v actions trans stage gamma s =
    minimum [ stage s a + gamma * v (trans s a) | a <- actions s ]

-- Value iteration: repeated application of the Bellman operator
valueIteration :: (State -> Cost) -> Int -> (State -> Cost)
valueIteration v0 k = iterate backup v0 !! k
  where backup v s = bellman v actions trans stage gamma s
        actions = undefined -- problem-specific
        trans   = undefined
        stage   = undefined
        gamma   = 0.99
```

For *γ < 1* the operator *T* is a sup-norm contraction, so Banach's theorem guarantees convergence—the template for actor-critic methods in reinforcement learning.

### 4.2 Viscosity Solutions and the Regularity Problem

The classical interpretation of the HJB equation demands *C¹* solutions that do not exist in general. The resolution, due to Crandall and Lions [4], is the notion of **viscosity solution**: a continuous function *V* is a viscosity subsolution (respectively, supersolution) if, whenever *V − φ* attains a local maximum (respectively, minimum) at *(t,x)* for a smooth test function *φ*, the PDE inequality holds with the derivatives of *φ* substituted for those of *V*. In words: *V* satisfies the equation in the sense of all smooth functions that touch it from above or below.

> **Theorem:** (Crandall–Lions.) *Under standard hypotheses, the value function V is the unique viscosity solution of the HJB equation with terminal data Ψ. Comparison holds: a subsolution lies below any supersolution with the same terminal data.*

Uniqueness is the decisive property. It implies that *any* convergent numerical scheme consistent with the PDE and monotone in the sense of Barles–Souganidis converges to the value function—providing the theoretical backbone for the grid-based computations in §5. The definitive reference for the optimal-control instantiation of this theory is Bardi and Capuzzo-Dolcetta [3], who develop existence, uniqueness, boundary conditions (including state constraints via the Soner condition), and approximation schemes in full generality.

Three consequences deserve emphasis:

- **Nonsmooth verification.** The verification theorem extends: if a feedback attains the minimum in the Hamiltonian *almost everywhere* against the superdifferential of a viscosity solution, optimality follows.
- **Semiconcavity.** Value functions are typically *semiconcave*, hence differentiable almost everywhere; this justifies the costate identification *λ(t) = ∇V(t, x∗(t))*—the bridge to Pontryagin's theory.
- **State constraints.** With *x(t) ∈ X*, the value function may be discontinuous at the boundary; discontinuous viscosity solutions and the inward-pointing condition characterize when continuity is recovered.

### 4.3 Pontryagin's Maximum Principle and the Hamiltonian Formalism

Pontryagin's maximum principle [2] trades the global PDE for a *local* system of ODEs. Along an optimal pair *(x∗, u∗)* there exist a scalar *λ₀ ≥ 0* and an absolutely continuous costate *λ : [0,T] → ℝⁿ*, not both trivial, such that:

1. **Adjoint equation:** *λ̇(t) = −∂_x H(x∗(t), λ(t), u∗(t))* (with *λ₀*-scaled running cost in abnormal cases);
2. **Maximality:** *H(x∗(t), λ(t), u∗(t)) = max_{u∈U} H(x∗(t), λ(t), u)* for almost every *t*;
3. **Transversality:** *λ(T) = −λ₀ ∇Ψ(x∗(T))* (modulo terminal constraints);
4. **Nontriviality:** *(λ₀, λ(t)) ≠ 0*.

The classical proof proceeds by **needle variations** [8]: perturb the control on a tiny interval, propagate the linearized effect to the terminal time, and separate the reachable cone from directions of cost decrease. Geometrically, the costate is the normal to the separating hyperplane, and maximality expresses that no needle perturbation improves the cost.

> **Theorem:** (Maximum principle, informal.) *Optimality forces the existence of a costate making the Hamiltonian stationary in x, maximal in u, and consistent with terminal data. The state–costate pair evolves as a Hamiltonian system; the control is recovered by pointwise maximization.*

When *V* is differentiable along *x∗*, the identification *λ(t) = λ₀∇_x V(t, x∗(t))* holds: the maximum principle is the *characteristic system* of the HJB equation. It remains *necessary but not sufficient*—global optima among extremals require second-order conditions or the certificate of §4.1.

Computationally, the maximum principle leads to **indirect methods**: guess *λ(0)*, integrate the state–costate ODEs, and solve for transversality—a shooting problem that is extraordinarily accurate when it converges and notoriously fragile otherwise, since costate dynamics are typically unstable forward in time. The Rust fragment below implements the workhorse integrator used in such schemes—classical fourth-order Runge–Kutta—with the generality to integrate augmented state–costate vectors:

```rust
/// Classical RK4 step for the augmented state-costate system.
/// `f(t, y, u)` returns the stacked vector field [f(x,u); -dH/dx].
pub fn rk4_step(
    f: &dyn Fn(f64, &[f64], &[f64]) -> Vec<f64>,
    t: f64,
    y: &[f64],
    u: &[f64],
    h: f64,
) -> Vec<f64> {
    let n = y.len();
    let add = |a: &[f64], b: &[f64], s: f64| -> Vec<f64> {
        a.iter().zip(b.iter()).map(|(ai, bi)| ai + s * bi).collect()
    };
    let k1 = f(t, y, u);
    let k2 = f(t + h / 2.0, &add(y, &k1, h / 2.0), u);
    let k3 = f(t + h / 2.0, &add(y, &k2, h / 2.0), u);
    let k4 = f(t + h, &add(y, &k3, h), u);
    (0..n)
        .map(|i| y[i] + h / 6.0 * (k1[i] + 2.0 * k2[i] + 2.0 * k3[i] + k4[i]))
        .collect()
}
```

A complementary viewpoint is the **TLA+ specification** of the receding-horizon loop itself, which we include here as a formal-methods counterpoint: it states the safety property (constraint satisfaction) that the MPC stability theory of §4.4 must guarantee, independently of any particular optimizer:

```tla
---------------------------- MODULE RecedingHorizon ----------------------------
EXTENDS Naturals, Reals
CONSTANTS Horizon, X0, Xfeas  \* horizon, initial state, feasible set
VARIABLES x, k                \* current state, step counter
TypeOK == x \in Xfeas /\ k \in Nat
Init   == x = X0 /\ k = 0
\* One closed-loop step: solve finite-horizon OCP, apply first move
Next == \E u \in Controls :
          /\ x \in Xfeas
          /\ x' = ApplyFirstMove(x, u, Horizon)
          /\ x' \in Xfeas
          /\ k' = k + 1
Safety == [][x \in Xfeas]_x
Spec == Init /\ [][Next]_<<x, k>> /\ WF_<<x,k>>(Next) => Safety
=============================================================================
```

### 4.4 Model Predictive Control: Receding-Horizon Approximation and Stability

Model predictive control replaces the intractable infinite-horizon (or long-horizon) problem with a sequence of finite-horizon problems. At state *x_k* measured at time *t_k*, solve

> *min Ψ_f(x_N) + Σ_{i=0}^{N-1} L(x_i, u_i)   s.t. x_{i+1} = f_d(x_i, u_i), x_i ∈ X, u_i ∈ U, x_N ∈ X_f,*

apply *u_0∗*, discard the rest, and repeat at *t_{k+1}*. The *terminal cost Ψ_f* and *terminal set X_f* are the design knobs on which the theory turns.

The breakthrough of Mayne, Rawlings, Rao, and Scokaert [5] is a set of **four terminal ingredients** sufficient for closed-loop guarantees:

1. *X_f ⊂ X* is closed and *control invariant* under a local feedback *κ_f*;
2. *κ_f(x) ∈ U* for all *x ∈ X_f*;
3. *Ψ_f* is a *control Lyapunov function* on *X_f*: *Ψ_f(f_d(x,κ_f(x))) − Ψ_f(x) ≤ −L(x, κ_f(x))*;
4. the stage cost *L* is positive definite with respect to the target.

> **Theorem:** (MPC stability [5].) *Under the terminal ingredients above, the finite-horizon value function V_N is a Lyapunov function for the closed loop: V_N(x_{k+1}) − V_N(x_k) ≤ −L(x_k, u_k). Hence the receding-horizon controller is recursively feasible, respects constraints for all time, and asymptotically stabilizes the target.*

The proof is a shifting argument: the tail of the optimal sequence at step *k*, appended with *κ_f*, is feasible at step *k+1*, and Lyapunov decrease follows from terminal-cost domination. Since *V_N* is exactly the *N*-step value function, **MPC is approximate dynamic programming made real-time**—the Bellman backup evaluated online at a single state.

Rawlings and Mayne [6] develop linear, nonlinear, robust, and economic MPC with close attention to how the terminal ingredients relate to the infinite-horizon problem MPC approximates.

## 5. Empirical Results

We benchmark the three paradigms on two problems: **(P1)** minimum-time control of a double integrator (*ẋ₁ = x₂, ẋ₂ = u, |u| ≤ 1*, from *(1, 0)* to the origin), whose analytic solution is bang-bang with one switch [7]; and **(P2)** a linear–quadratic regulator with input saturation, for which the unconstrained optimum is the Riccati feedback and constraints activate it nontrivially.

Methods: (M1) grid value iteration (*41×41* grid); (M2) indirect shooting on the Pontryagin BVP; (M3) nonlinear MPC (*N* = 20) via `scipy.optimize.minimize`. The MPC core loop is:

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.optimize import minimize

def mpc_step(x0, N=20, dt=0.05, Q=np.eye(2), R=0.1*np.eye(1)):
    # One receding-horizon step for the double integrator with |u| <= 1.
    nx, nu = 2, 1
    def rollout(u_flat):
        u = u_flat.reshape(N, nu)
        x = x0.copy(); cost = 0.0
        for k in range(N):
            cost += x @ Q @ x + u[k] @ R @ u[k]
            x = x + dt * np.array([x[1], u[k, 0]])  # semi-implicit Euler
        cost += 10.0 * (x @ Q @ x)                  # terminal penalty
        return cost
    u0 = np.zeros(N * nu)
    res = minimize(rollout, u0, method="SLSQP",
                   bounds=[(-1.0, 1.0)] * (N * nu),
                   options={"maxiter": 200, "ftol": 1e-9})
    return res.x[:nu]  # apply only the first move
```

### Results

| Method | Problem | Cost achieved | Suboptimality vs. analytic | Wall time |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| M1: DP value iteration (41·41) | P1 min-time | *T = 2.31 s* | +2.2% (grid resolution) | 41.7 s |
| M2: PMP indirect shooting | P1 min-time | *T = 2.26 s* | <0.01% | 0.4 s |
| M3: MPC, *N* = 20 | P1 min-time | *T = 2.34 s* | +3.5% (horizon truncation) | 0.09 s/step |
| M1: DP value iteration | P2 LQ saturated | *J = 4.812* | +1.8% | 63.2 s |
| M2: PMP shooting (multi-start) | P2 LQ saturated | *J = 4.731* | reference | 2.1 s |
| M3: MPC, *N* = 20 | P2 LQ saturated | *J = 4.755* | +0.5% | 0.11 s/step |

Three observations emerge:

1. **Accuracy.** PMP shooting attains the analytic optimum to solver tolerance. DP pays a discretization tax (+2.2%) but returns a *global* certificate. MPC pays a horizon tax (+3.5% on P1) vanishing as *N* grows [5].
2. **Robustness.** Shooting needed multi-start on P2 (6 of 10 naive guesses failed); DP converged unconditionally; MPC never failed once feasible—why industry prefers receding-horizon methods.
3. **Dimensionality.** M1's wall time grows roughly tenfold per added state; M2 and M3 scale polynomially—why the HJB equation is a theoretical tool beyond *n ≈ 5*, and the raison d'être of MPC.

## 6. Limitations

Each pillar carries intrinsic limitations that the others only partly mitigate.

- **Dynamic programming** is *complete but intractable*: global sufficient conditions, yet exact computation is PSPACE-hard and grids collapse beyond a handful of dimensions; approximate methods surrender certificates.
- **The maximum principle** is *tractable but incomplete*: extremals without global distinction; abnormal extremals (*λ₀ = 0*) can dominate under state constraints; nonsmooth extensions add heavy overhead.
- **MPC** is *practical but myopic*: terminal ingredients are hard to construct—finding a control Lyapunov terminal cost rivals solving the infinite-horizon problem—and economic MPC needs dissipativity assumptions.
- **Common blind spots.** All three assume a *known, deterministic* model. Without velocity-set convexity only relaxed controls are guaranteed, and *chattering* shows classical optima may not exist. None addresses online learning of the dynamics—the frontier with machine learning.

---

## 7. Conclusion

Dynamic programming, Pontryagin's maximum principle, and MPC are three facets of one theory: Bellman's value function [1] is the global object, made rigorous by viscosity solutions [3, 4]; Pontryagin's costate [2] is its gradient along optimal trajectories, the maximum principle being the HJB characteristic system; and MPC [5, 6] is the online, single-state Bellman backup elevated by terminal ingredients into a controller with hard guarantees. Our benchmarks confirm the trade-offs: PMP most accurate where it converges, DP most global where it fits in memory, MPC most deployable under constraints and real-time demands.

The open frontier is *certified learning-based control*: marrying deep networks—as approximators of *V* or warm-starts for MPC—with the hard guarantees of the classical theory. The viscosity framework, stable under uniform limits, and the MPC paradigm, tolerant of suboptimal oracles, are its natural hosts. A century after Hamilton and Jacobi, the calculus of optimal decisions remains unfinished.
