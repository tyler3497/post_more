# Analog In-Memory Computing with Phase-Change Memory Crossbars: Kirchhoff-Law Matrix-Vector Multiplication, Conductance Drift Compensation, and Noise-Aware Training for DNN Inference

## Abstract

Analog in-memory computing (AIMC) with phase-change memory (PCM) crossbars promises to collapse the von Neumann memory–processor boundary by executing deep neural network (DNN) inference where the weights physically reside. By encoding synaptic weights as device conductances and applying input activations as voltages, an entire matrix–vector multiplication (MVM) is evaluated in a single time step through Kirchhoff's current law. This thesis develops the full stack of this paradigm: the device physics of chalcogenide PCM, the 1T1R differential crossbar architecture that suppresses sneak paths, and the non-idealities that corrupt analog computation — principally conductance drift, programming variability, and read noise. We formalize global drift compensation (GDC), analyze iterative program-and-verify schemes, and present noise-aware training as the mechanism by which iso-accuracy inference is achieved on prototype hardware. Drawing on IBM's HERMES 64-core chip and computational-PCM inference experiments, we quantify throughput (up to 63.1 TOPS) and efficiency (up to 9.76 TOPS/W), derive error bounds under drift, and close with an honest accounting of the physical limits separating laboratory prototypes from general-purpose analog accelerators.

## 1 Introduction

For seven decades, general-purpose computing has obeyed the von Neumann template: a physically separate memory and processing unit, connected by a bandwidth-limited, energy-hungry bus. This arrangement — the *memory wall* — has survived because both halves scaled together under Dennard and Moore. In the deep-learning era it has not. A single ResNet-50 inference moves hundreds of megabytes of weights across the bus; a large-language-model forward pass moves tens of gigabytes. Data movement, not arithmetic, now dominates both latency and energy: moving a 32-bit operand from off-chip DRAM costs on the order of **10⁴ times** the energy of a 32-bit multiply–accumulate in a modern CMOS datapath. The arithmetic is cheap; the commute is ruinous.

Analog in-memory computing attacks the problem at its root by refusing the commute altogether [1][3]. If a memory device's *conductance* can serve as a stored weight and an applied *voltage* can serve as an input activation, then Ohm's law computes their product locally, and Kirchhoff's current law sums products along a wire. A two-dimensional crossbar of resistive devices therefore evaluates

$$
I_j = \sum_{i=1}^{N} V_i \, G_{ij}
$$

for every output column *j* simultaneously — a full matrix–vector multiplication (MVM) in effectively **O(1) time**, independent of matrix dimensions, with the dominant energy cost residing in the peripheral analog-to-digital converters rather than the computation itself. Among candidate devices, phase-change memory (PCM) stands out: it is a mature, CMOS-back-end-integrable technology with demonstrated million-device arrays, genuinely analog multilevel programmability, and retention measured in years [2][4].

Yet analog computation trades one set of difficulties for another. PCM devices *drift*: the amorphous phase structurally relaxes, so conductance decays as a power law in time, and it decays fastest precisely in the high-resistance states used to encode information [2][7]. Programming is stochastic, reads are noisy, devices vary across the array, and interconnect resistance distorts the idealized Kirchhoff summation. This thesis is a rigorous treatment of that trade: it presents the crossbar MVM primitive, models the device physics that perturb it, derives compensation and training techniques that restore accuracy, and evaluates the resulting systems against software baselines with real, published hardware numbers.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The von Neumann bottleneck and the case for AIMC

Digital DNN accelerators — GPUs, TPUs, and systolic-array NPUs — improve throughput by increasing parallelism and data reuse, but they remain prisoners of the memory hierarchy. Systolic arrays, for example, keep weights stationary in on-chip SRAM and stream activations through the array; this amortizes weight movement but cannot eliminate it, and SRAM cells are large (~150 F²), volatile, and limited in density. AIMC replaces the SRAM with a dense non-volatile analog cell and replaces the digital multiply–accumulate with physical laws. The distinction is qualitative: in a digital accelerator, memory *supplies* data to the compute unit; in AIMC, memory *is* the compute unit.

> **Theorem:** *(O(1) MVM in an ideal crossbar.)* In an ideal resistive crossbar with negligible wire resistance, zero sneak paths, and linear devices, applying voltages $V_1, \ldots, V_N$ to the $N$ rows produces column currents $I_j = \sum_i V_i G_{ij}$ whose evaluation time is independent of $N$ and the number of columns $M$. The total energy is dominated by the peripheral conversion circuitry, not the $\mathcal{O}(NM)$ arithmetic.

The idealization is load-bearing, and much of this thesis is concerned with quantifying how far real hardware departs from it.

### 2.2 Phase-change memory device physics

A PCM cell stores information in the structural phase of a chalcogenide alloy — most commonly germanium-antimony-telluride (Ge₂Sb₂Te₅, "GST"), frequently doped to tune crystallization kinetics [4][6]. The material is sandwiched between electrodes; a short, high-current *RESET* pulse melts a volume of material that quenches into the **amorphous** phase (high resistance, low conductance), while a longer, lower-current *SET* pulse anneals it back to the **crystalline** phase (low resistance, high conductance). Partial SET pulses crystallize partial volumes, yielding a continuum of intermediate conductance states — the analog programmability that makes PCM an artificial synapse [1].

Modern arrays integrate devices in a *key-hole* geometry with ~20 nm bottom-electrode radii, with one million devices organized as 512 wordlines × 2048 bitlines in the IBM inference prototype [2]. Crucially for inference, PCM is *backend-of-line* integrable: the memory layers are deposited after the CMOS logic is fabricated, allowing AIMC cores to be embedded alongside digital units in a standard 14 nm process — the HERMES chip demonstrated exactly this [1].

### 2.3 The 1T1R differential cell and array organization

A bare passive crossbar suffers from *sneak paths*: current flows not only through the selected cell but through unintended parallel routes, corrupting the readout. The industry-standard remedy is the **1T1R cell** — one access transistor in series with each resistive device — which electrically isolates unselected cells during programming and reading [6]. To represent signed synaptic weights with unipolar (non-negative) conductances, the standard construction is the **differential pair**: each weight $w$ is encoded as the difference of two conductances, $w \propto G^+ - G^-$, so that potentiation increases $G^+$ with $G^-$ fixed and depression increases $G^-$ with $G^+$ fixed [3][6]. The column pair's differential current then directly encodes the signed MVM output, and common-mode drift partially cancels — a first, passive line of defense against conductance drift.

### 2.4 Conductance drift: the central non-ideality

The amorphous phase of GST is not in thermodynamic equilibrium; it undergoes *structural relaxation*, and the conductance of a programmed state decays approximately as a power law in time [2][7]:

$$
G(t) = G_0 \left(\frac{t}{t_0}\right)^{-\nu},
$$

where $\nu$ is the drift coefficient (typically $0.05$–$0.15$ at room temperature) and — critically — $\nu$ is *state-dependent*: it is larger for higher-resistance (more amorphous) states. Resistance drift therefore corrupts different weights at different rates, progressively distorting the weight matrix. Left uncompensated, this produces a classification-accuracy degradation of order 10% over hours-to-days at room temperature in unmitigated SNN/ANN demonstrations [8]. Understanding, modeling, and compensating this effect is the core engineering challenge of PCM-based AIMC.

## 3 Methodology

Our analysis follows a three-layer methodology mirroring how real AIMC systems are actually built and validated [2]:

1. **Device-to-circuit modeling.** We model the crossbar as a conductance matrix subject to (a) state-dependent drift $G(t) = G_0 (t/t_0)^{-\nu(G_0)}$, (b) Gaussian programming variability with device-dependent standard deviation, (c) 1/*f*-like read noise, and (d) parasitic wire resistance (IR drop). This produces a statistical MVM model used for all subsequent analysis.
2. **Compensation and programming schemes.** We formalize *global drift compensation* (GDC), which estimates a single array-wide drift factor $\hat{\alpha}$ from periodically re-read calibration columns and applies the inverse gain in the digital domain; iterative *program-and-verify* (P&V), which converges device conductances to target tolerances; and *projected PCM* devices, whose parallel metal-nitride segment decouples read current from the drifting amorphous volume [5].
3. **Hardware-aware training and hardware validation.** We describe *noise-aware training* — injecting the measured device noise distributions into the forward pass during training — and validate the entire stack against published hardware results: the 1M-device prototype inference experiments of Joshi *et al.* [2] and the 64-core HERMES chip of Le Gallo *et al.* [1], which demonstrated near-software-equivalent accuracy on ResNet and LSTM workloads at 63.1 TOPS and 9.76 TOPS/W in low-precision read mode.

All quantitative claims in Section 5 are drawn from these published measurements or derived from the drift model under stated assumptions; no simulated result is presented as measured hardware data.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Kirchhoff-Law Matrix-Vector Multiplication in the 1T1R Crossbar

The computational primitive deserves a precise circuit-level statement. Consider an $N \times M$ 1T1R array. During the *read/compute phase*, each selected wordline turns on its access transistors, a read voltage $V_i$ (small enough to avoid disturbing the programmed state) is applied to row $i$, and each device $(i,j)$ contributes a branch current $V_i G_{ij}$ onto column $j$. By Kirchhoff's current law, the column node sums these contributions:

$$
I_j = \sum_{i=1}^{N} V_i G_{ij} + \varepsilon_j, \qquad j = 1,\ldots,M,
$$

where $\varepsilon_j$ aggregates all non-idealities. In the ideal limit ($\varepsilon_j = 0$), this is exactly the matrix–vector product $\mathbf{I} = \mathbf{G}^\top \mathbf{V}$, computed in parallel across all $M$ columns in the settling time of the column circuitry — typically tens of nanoseconds.

Several architectural choices determine how cleanly this ideal is approached:

- **Differential weight encoding.** Since $G_{ij} \ge 0$ physically, signed weights use paired columns with conductances $G_{ij}^+, G_{ij}^-$ and the digital periphery computes $I_j^+ - I_j^- \propto \sum_i V_i (G_{ij}^+ - G_{ij}^-) \approx \sum_i V_i w_{ij}$ [3][6]. The subtraction also rejects common-mode drift and common-mode read noise — a property we exploit in Section 4.2.
- **Input encoding and ADC resolution.** The HERMES chip supports 8-bit input/output MVMs, using pulse-width or amplitude encoding of $V_i$ and integrating ADCs on the columns [1]. The chip implements two read modes: a *four-phase* (high-precision) mode achieving 16.1 TOPS at 2.48 TOPS/W, and a *one-phase* (low-precision) mode achieving 63.1 TOPS at 9.76 TOPS/W — directly illustrating the precision–efficiency trade that governs AIMC design.
- **Wire resistance and IR drop.** Real bitlines have finite resistance; the voltage at cell $(i,j)$ is $V_i - \Delta V_{ij}$ where $\Delta V_{ij}$ depends on the cumulative upstream current. This makes the effective matrix input-dependent and is the dominant spatial non-ideality in large arrays. Mitigations include Kelvin sensing, segmented arrays, and — most powerfully — absorbing the residual error into noise-aware training (Section 4.4).
- **Sneak-path suppression.** The 1T1R access transistor guarantees that unselected cells present an open circuit, eliminating sneak paths during both programming and computation at the cost of one transistor per cell [6]. This is why PCM AIMC arrays are 1T1R rather than passive crossbars: density is traded for determinism.

| Design parameter | Typical value (prototype) | Governing trade-off |
|---|---|---|
| Array size | 512 WL × 2048 BL (1M devices) [2] | Larger arrays amortize ADC cost but worsen IR drop |
| Cell structure | 1T1R, differential pair | Signed weights, sneak-path immunity vs. area |
| Conductance levels | 4–16 distinguishable states | Precision vs. programming time/energy |
| Read voltage | ~0.1–0.3 V | Signal-to-noise vs. read disturb |
| ADC resolution | 8-bit (HERMES I/O) [1] | Output fidelity vs. peripheral energy/area |
| Programming | Iterative P&V, ~µs per pulse | Accuracy vs. write latency and endurance |

### 4.2 Conductance Drift Physics and Compensation

Drift is not a defect of a particular fabrication run; it is *thermodynamics*. The melt-quenched amorphous dome exists in a high-free-energy glassy state and relaxes toward the ideal glass, annihilating the defect states that mediate conduction. The phenomenological power law $G(t) = G_0(t/t_0)^{-\nu}$ captures this well over many decades of time, but the state dependence of $\nu$ — larger for more amorphous, higher-resistance states — is what makes drift pernicious for DNNs: it does not merely scale the weight matrix, it *reshapes* it [2][7].

> **Theorem:** *(Drift-induced MVM error bound.)* Let the target weight matrix be $\mathbf{W}$ with $\|\mathbf{W}\|_\infty \le W_{\max}$, encoded differentially with conductance range $[G_{\min}, G_{\max}]$, and let each device drift with state-dependent coefficient $\nu(G_0) \in [\nu_{\min}, \nu_{\max}]$. Then the relative drift-induced error of the MVM output after time $t$ satisfies $\|\Delta \mathbf{I}\|_\infty / \|\mathbf{I}\|_\infty \le \big((t/t_0)^{-\nu_{\min}} - (t/t_0)^{-\nu_{\max}}\big) + \big(1 - (t/t_0)^{-\bar{\nu}}\big)$, where the first term is the *differential* distortion (uncompensatable by global scaling) and the second is the *common-mode* decay (compensatable).

This decomposition motivates the standard compensation hierarchy:

1. **Differential encoding (passive).** The common-mode component of drift cancels in $G^+ - G^-$ to first order, roughly halving the effective drift without any circuitry [3].
2. **Global drift compensation (GDC).** A small set of $L$ calibration columns is programmed to known conductances $G_{mn}(t_0)$. Periodically — during array idle time — the calibration currents $I_m$ are re-read under a fixed $V_{\text{cal}}$, and the drift factor is estimated directly from device data [2]:
$$
\hat{\alpha} = \frac{\sum_{m=1}^{L} I_m}{V_{\text{cal}} \sum_{n,m} G_{mn}(t_0)}, \qquad \text{outputs scaled by } 1/\hat{\alpha}.
$$
GDC requires no model of $\nu$, no timestamps, and no retraining; the scaling is linear and can be fused with batch normalization in the digital periphery. In the Joshi *et al.* experiments, GDC restored iso-accuracy on CIFAR-10/ResNet workloads over the full drift horizon [2].
3. **Projected PCM (device-level).** A parallel metallic projection segment carries the read current around the drifting amorphous volume, decoupling the read conductance from structural relaxation while the write path still passes through the phase-change material [5]. This attacks the differential-distortion term that GDC cannot remove, at the cost of added process complexity.
4. **Periodic refresh / reprogramming.** For the longest horizons, weights can simply be re-programmed, resetting drift at $t_0$ at the cost of write energy and endurance — acceptable when inference dominates the duty cycle.

### 4.3 Iterative Program-and-Verify

Programming a PCM device to an analog target is inherently stochastic: identical SET pulses produce a distribution of conductance outcomes due to thermal and nucleation randomness. The remedy is *iterative program-and-verify* (P&V): apply a pulse, read the conductance, compare against the target window, and repeat with adjusted pulse parameters until the device lands inside tolerance [2][4]:

```python
def program_and_verify(device, G_target, tol=0.05, max_iter=20):
    """Iteratively program a PCM cell to G_target within relative tolerance."""
    for k in range(max_iter):
        G = device.read()                      # non-destructive read
        err = (G - G_target) / G_target
        if abs(err) <= tol:
            return True, k                     # converged
        # polarity-selective correction: partial SET to raise G,
        # partial RESET to lower G (RESET is coarser: prefer small SET steps)
        pulse = select_pulse(err, k)           # amplitude/duration schedule
        device.apply(pulse)
    return False, max_iter                     # failed to converge
```

Two practical observations dominate P&V design. First, *SET is finer than RESET*: crystallization proceeds gradually with successive partial SET pulses, whereas RESET (melt-quench) is abrupt and coarse — so the standard strategy programs from the amorphous side and creeps upward with SET pulses, using RESET only for gross correction. Second, P&V is a *write-time* cost paid once per weight deployment, while drift and read noise are *inference-time* costs paid forever; the tolerance should therefore be tightened only to the point where programming error is subdominant to drift over the intended deployment horizon. Over-tightening P&V wastes write energy and endurance for no inference benefit — a quantitative instance of the general principle that in AIMC, every precision budget must be allocated end-to-end.

### 4.4 Noise-Aware Training: Teaching the Network Its Hardware

Even with perfect programming and perfect drift compensation, the analog MVM is noisy: programming leaves residual error, reads add thermal and 1/*f* noise, and IR drop adds input-dependent distortion. The breakthrough insight — demonstrated conclusively by Joshi *et al.* [2] — is that these non-idealities should not be fought purely in hardware; they should be *taught* to the network. In noise-aware (hardware-aware) training, the forward pass used during gradient computation injects noise sampled from the measured device distributions:

$$
\tilde{w}_{ij} = w_{ij} + \delta_{ij}, \qquad \delta_{ij} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma_{\text{prog}}^2 + \sigma_{\text{read}}^2),
$$

with additional drift-noise terms and weight clipping (e.g., clip scale $\alpha = 2.5$ with $L_2$ decay $10^{-4}$ in the published experiments). The network then learns weight configurations that are *robust* to the hardware's statistics — flat minima in the loss landscape where small weight perturbations do not change predictions. The result was striking: a ResNet-32 trained with additive noise and deployed on the 1M-device PCM prototype achieved iso-accuracy versus software and maintained it under drift with GDC applied [2]. Noise-aware training is arguably the single most important algorithmic contribution to practical AIMC: it converts irreducible device physics from an accuracy tax into a regularization signal.

### 4.5 Mixed-Precision and the Division of Labor

A subtle but crucial architectural lesson comes from the *mixed-precision* approach [4]: not every operation belongs in the analog domain. Weight-stationary MVMs — the $O(N^2)$ bulk of DNN inference — run on the PCM crossbar, while the $O(N)$ operations that demand precision (weight updates, normalization statistics, activation functions in some designs) run in digital logic, with the HERMES chip integrating both plus an on-chip communication network [1]. The mixed-precision prototype (500 × 2000 crossbar) matched the accuracy of a 4-bit FPGA implementation while consuming **80× less energy** [4]. The design principle generalizes: *analog for the dense linear algebra, digital for everything else*. AIMC is not a replacement for digital computing; it is a domain-specific accelerator for the operation that dominates modern AI workloads.

## 5 Empirical Results / Proofs

We now consolidate the published hardware evidence into a single quantitative picture.

**Result 1 — Iso-accuracy inference on a 1M-device prototype (Joshi *et al.*, Nature Communications 2020) [2].** Using a 512 × 2048 1T1R PCM array with differential weight encoding, iterative program-and-verify, noise-aware training, and global drift compensation, the authors deployed CNN and ResNet-class networks and measured 93.7% CIFAR-10 accuracy (ResNet-32) against a 93.9% software baseline — a gap of 0.2 percentage points, within run-to-run training variance. Drift was characterized over extended time and compensated by GDC with calibration columns read during idle periods; the compensation required no per-device model and no retraining.

**Result 2 — End-to-end multicore AIMC (Le Gallo *et al.*, Nature Electronics 2023; HERMES) [1].** A 64-core chip in 14 nm CMOS with backend-integrated PCM demonstrated near-software-equivalent inference accuracy on ResNet and LSTM networks with *all* weight-layer and activation-function computations executed on chip. For 8-bit I/O MVMs: 16.1 TOPS at 2.48 TOPS/W (four-phase high-precision read) and 63.1 TOPS at 9.76 TOPS/W (one-phase low-precision read). This is the existence proof that AIMC scales from single-array prototypes to a complete inference SoC.

**Result 3 — Mixed-precision energy advantage (Le Gallo *et al.*, Nature Electronics 2018) [4].** The 1M-device mixed-precision prototype achieved accuracy comparable to a 4-bit FPGA implementation at 80× lower energy consumption, establishing the order-of-magnitude efficiency claim that motivates the entire field.

**Result 4 — Drift model validation.** The power-law drift model with state-dependent $\nu$ was validated against array-scale measurements; GDC's $\hat{\alpha}$ estimator tracked the common-mode component across the full experimental horizon [2][7].

**Result 5 — Training with analog memory (Ambrogio *et al.*, Nature 2018).** Extending beyond inference, equivalent-accuracy *training* was demonstrated using analog memory with a two-device synapse architecture, showing that the noise-aware philosophy generalizes from deployment to learning [4].

Taken together, these results support a precise claim — not that AIMC is a mature technology, but that the *accuracy* barrier has been experimentally cleared: with differential encoding, program-and-verify, noise-aware training, and global drift compensation, PCM crossbars perform DNN inference at software-equivalent accuracy with order-of-magnitude energy advantages.

## 6 Limitations

Intellectual honesty requires stating plainly what these results do *not* establish.

1. **The differential-distortion floor.** GDC compensates only common-mode drift. State-dependent drift coefficients leave a residual differential distortion that grows with time and with the spread of programmed conductance values; projected PCM mitigates it but adds process complexity [5][7]. For very long deployment horizons without refresh, this term — not noise, not IR drop — is the fundamental accuracy limit.
2. **Endurance and write asymmetry.** PCM endurance ($\sim$10⁶–10⁹ cycles depending on device and pulse scheme) is ample for inference-dominated workloads but constrains frequent reprogramming and on-chip training; RESET pulses require melt currents that dominate the write energy budget [6].
3. **Peripheral overhead.** The ADCs, DACs, and digital periphery that make the analog core usable consume a large fraction of total chip energy and area; the 9.76 TOPS/W figure is a system measurement that already includes this, but scaling to larger models increases the conversion burden linearly with I/O bandwidth [1].
4. **IR drop at scale.** Wire resistance effects grow with array dimensions; the largest demonstrated arrays remain at the 1M-device scale, and multi-megabyte models require tiling across many cores with attendant communication costs — precisely the problem HERMES's on-chip network begins to address [1].
5. **Generality.** All iso-accuracy demonstrations to date target inference on vision and sequence models. The mixed-precision and noise-aware techniques have not been shown to generalize to arbitrary workloads (e.g., attention-heavy transformers at scale), and on-chip training remains a research frontier rather than a product reality.
6. **Variability and yield.** Device-to-device variability in drift coefficients, programming response, and stuck-at faults requires redundancy and remapping strategies at manufacturing scale that are not yet standardized.

---

## 7 Conclusion

Analog in-memory computing with phase-change memory crossbars rests on an elegant physical observation — that Ohm's law multiplies and Kirchhoff's law adds — and on a decade of unglamorous engineering that makes the observation usable. The 1T1R differential crossbar turns the MVM into a single-step physical process; the power-law drift model quantifies its principal corruption; global drift compensation, projected devices, and iterative program-and-verify contain it; and noise-aware training converts the residual non-idealities from an accuracy tax into a regularization signal the network learns to absorb. The hardware record — iso-accuracy ResNet inference on a million-device prototype [2], a 64-core 14 nm SoC at 63.1 TOPS and 9.76 TOPS/W [1], and an 80× energy advantage over digital equivalents [4] — demonstrates that the accuracy barrier has fallen. What remains is the scaling barrier: larger arrays, longer drift horizons, peripheral efficiency, and generality beyond the demonstrated workloads. If those yield, the von Neumann memory wall — the defining constraint of the deep-learning era — will have been breached not by moving data faster, but by refusing to move it at all.
