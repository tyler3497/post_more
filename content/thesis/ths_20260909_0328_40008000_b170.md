# Modern Web Search at Scale: Inverted Indexes, Learning-to-Rank, and the Systems Engineering of Billion-Query Engines

## Abstract

Modern web search engines answer on the order of a hundred thousand queries per second over indexes of tens of billions of documents, holding tail latencies to a few hundred milliseconds. This thesis develops the full stack that makes this possible: the inverted index as a compressed, randomly accessible data structure with SIMD-accelerated codecs such as PForDelta and Elias–Fano; exact top-k retrieval via dynamic pruning algorithms including WAND, MaxScore, and Block-Max WAND; the probabilistic ranking heritage of BM25 and its generalization in learning-to-rank, culminating in LambdaMART gradient-boosted tree ensembles; and the neural era of cross-encoders, dual-encoder dense retrieval, ColBERT late interaction, and approximate nearest-neighbor search. We analyze the serving substrate — document sharding, scatter–gather fan-out, replicated state, and tail-latency control — descended from the cluster architecture of early Google [2]. Empirical evidence from TREC, MS MARCO, and published systems studies quantifies the central trade-offs. The treatment closes with limitations: index freshness, vocabulary mismatch, evaluation bias, and the energy and storage costs of dense models.

---

## 1. Introduction

The web search engine is arguably the most demanding information system ever deployed: a single query must sift tens of billions of documents, score the survivors with models that took months to train, and return an answer before a human notices the wait. The founding blueprint, Brin and Page's anatomy of Google [1], already identified the essential decomposition — crawl, index, serve — but its numbers (tens of millions of documents, a single cluster) are quaint beside today's: roughly **10^5 queries per second** at peak, **tens of billions of documents** and petabytes of postings, with **hundreds of milliseconds of tail latency** per query [2].

No single algorithm spans that gap. Instead, modern search is a *cascade* of stages, each shrinking the candidate set cheaply enough that the next, more expensive stage can afford accuracy: crawling and ingestion; compressed sharded indexing; first-pass retrieval with dynamic pruning; cascaded ranking (BM25 [4], gradient-boosted ensembles [8], neural re-rankers [10]); and distributed serving under strict latency SLOs.

> **Thesis statement.** *Billion-query search is won not by any single ranking function but by the joint optimization of compression, pruning, cascaded learned rankers, and distributed serving — each stage buying the latency and quality budget the next stage spends.*

This thesis is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews inverted indexes, ranking families, and evaluation. Section 3 develops cascade design, index compression, exact top-k pruning, and tail-latency-aware serving. Section 4 treats compressed posting codecs, WAND-family pruning, LambdaMART, and neural ranking in depth. Section 5 presents empirical results; Sections 6 and 7 discuss limitations and conclude.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 The inverted index

The inverted index is the central data structure of text search: a dictionary mapping each term to a **posting list** of the documents containing it, each posting carrying the document identifier plus payload such as term frequency and word positions [3].

> **Definition 1 (Posting list).** *For a term t, the posting list L(t) is the sorted sequence of document identifiers containing t, each annotated with within-document statistics (term frequency, positions, field information).*

Because identifiers are sorted, they are stored as *d-gaps* — differences between successive identifiers — which are small integers and highly compressible. Term frequencies follow a Zipf distribution, so the longest posting lists dominate both index size and query cost. Practical indexes partition postings into blocks with **skip pointers**, permitting jumps over document stretches that cannot contribute to the ranking.

Two partitioning strategies dominate distributed indexes. In **document sharding**, each machine holds a complete index over a disjoint document subset; a query is broadcast and partial top-k lists merged. In **term partitioning**, each machine holds the posting lists of a term subset. Document sharding is the near-universal choice in web search: it balances load naturally and localizes scoring [2].

### 2.2 Ranking families

Ranking functions have evolved through four generations:

- **Vector-space models**: cosine similarity between tf–idf weighted query and document vectors — the baseline everything displaced.
- **Probabilistic models**: the Probability Ranking Principle led to **BM25** [4], saturating term-frequency influence and normalizing length via *k1* and *b*.
- **Learning-to-rank**: ranking as supervised learning over labeled pairs, optimized for listwise metrics such as NDCG, culminating in LambdaMART [8].
- **Neural ranking**: contextual encoders scoring query–document pairs — cross-encoders, dual-encoder dense retrieval [9], late-interaction models [10].

### 2.3 Evaluation and test collections

Progress is measured on shared benchmarks. The TREC *ad hoc* tracks established pooling-based judgments with MAP and NDCG; **MS MARCO** provided million-scale query logs and popularized MRR@10 and Recall@1000. A recurring theme is the gap between offline metrics and online outcomes: click logs are abundant but position-biased, while human judgments are unbiased but scarce.

## 3. Methodology

### 3.1 Cascade methodology

The core methodological move of production search is the *retrieval cascade*: the problem is factored so no single ranker must be both cheap and accurate.

1. A **boolean prefilter** (conjunctions, phrase constraints, tiered indexes) selects candidates cheaply.
2. **First-pass scoring** with BM25 plus light learned features reduces millions of candidates to thousands.
3. **Second-pass re-ranking** applies the expensive models — deep ensembles, cross-encoders — to the survivors.

Each stage is tuned so its output recall exceeds what the next stage needs, within its latency budget — cascade design as *budget allocation*.

### 3.2 Index construction methodology

Building the index is a batch dataflow problem: crawled documents pass through parsing, tokenization, link extraction, and spam classification, and token streams are inverted into posting lists. Two concerns dominate: **compression**, so the working set fits in RAM, and **freshness**. A static compressed index cannot absorb a news article in seconds, so production systems are *tiered*: a large static index rebuilt periodically plus small dynamic indexes merged at query time, often via log-structured merge trees.

Document identifier assignment is a consequential optimization: nearby identifiers for similar documents (e.g., URL-lexicographic ordering clusters a host's pages) shrink d-gaps and improve compression — an NP-hard problem addressed with heuristics [6].

### 3.3 Exact top-k pruning methodology

The methodology is *upper-bound pruning*: maintain a threshold *θ* equal to the current k-th best score, and skip any document whose provable score upper bound falls below *θ*. WAND [5] uses per-term upper bounds and pivot selection, MaxScore splits terms into essential and non-essential sets, and Block-Max WAND [11] refines bounds to the block level — all *safe*, returning exactly the top-*k* exhaustive scoring would.

### 3.4 Serving methodology

At serving time the query fans out to hundreds or thousands of shards, each returning its local top-*k*. Tail latency is the enemy: with fan-out *F*, the query completes when the *slowest* shard responds. Countermeasures are replication, hedged requests, and deadline-aware cancellation of stragglers — pioneered in the Google cluster architecture [2] and its Bigtable substrate [3].

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 The inverted index as a compressed data structure

A posting list is a sorted integer sequence, and integer-sequence compression trades three quantities: **bits per posting**, **decode speed**, and **random access / skipping**.

- *Bit-level codes* (Elias-γ, Elias-δ, Golomb) encode each gap individually: excellent compression, slow bit-by-bit decoding.
- *Byte-aligned codes* (variable-byte, as in Lucene) trade some compression for much faster decoding.
- *Block codes* choose a bit-width per block. **PForDelta** [6] fits most values in the width and stores outliers ("exceptions") separately.
- *SIMD codecs* decode multiple integers per vector instruction — under one CPU cycle per integer [7] — shifting the bottleneck to memory bandwidth.
- *Elias–Fano* (partitioned) sits near the information-theoretic lower bound with *O(1)* random access to the *i*-th element: a skip pointer becomes a direct seek.

Skip lists are typically two-level: posting blocks of ~128 postings, each annotated with its maximum document identifier and maximum term contribution — the block-max metadata Block-Max WAND exploits for pruning (Section 4.2).

| Codec | Bits/posting (typical) | Decode speed | Random access | Skip support |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Uncompressed 32-bit | 32 | trivial | yes | manual |
| Elias-γ / Elias-δ | 5–9 | slow (bit ops) | no | auxiliary |
| Variable-byte | 8–12 | fast | no | auxiliary |
| PForDelta [6] | 6–9 | very fast | block-level | block skips |
| Simple-16 / word-aligned | 7–10 | fast | no | block skips |
| Elias–Fano (partitioned) | 7–10 | fast | *O(1)* | direct seek |
| SIMD-BP128 / FastPFor [7] | 7–9 | <1 cycle/int | block-level | block skips |

The following Python sketch implements BM25 scoring over posting lists, the canonical first-pass scorer:

```python
import math

def bm25_idf(doc_freq, num_docs):
    # Robertson-Sparck Jones IDF with smoothing
    return math.log(1.0 + (num_docs - doc_freq + 0.5) / (doc_freq + 0.5))

def bm25_score(term_freq, doc_len, avg_len, idf, k1=1.2, b=0.75):
    # tf saturation: occurrences help with diminishing returns
    tf_component = term_freq * (k1 + 1.0) / (term_freq + k1 * (1.0 - b + b * doc_len / avg_len))
    return idf * tf_component

def score_document(query_terms, postings, doc_id, doc_len, avg_len, num_docs):
    total = 0.0
    for term in query_terms:
        plist = postings.get(term)
        if plist is None:
            continue
        for pid, tf in plist:  # toy list of (doc_id, term_freq) pairs
            if pid == doc_id:
                total += bm25_score(tf, doc_len, avg_len, bm25_idf(len(plist), num_docs))
    return total
```

The two free parameters have stable optima across collections (*k1* ≈ 1.2, *b* ≈ 0.75) — the robustness behind BM25's decades-long tenure as the default first-pass ranker [4].

### 4.2 Dynamic pruning: WAND, MaxScore, and Block-Max WAND

Given a query with per-term upper bounds *UB(t)* (the maximum score any document can obtain from term *t*), **WAND** [5] maintains a threshold *θ* — the current k-th best score — and one cursor per posting list. It sorts terms by cursor position, accumulates upper bounds until the sum exceeds *θ*, and identifies a *pivot* term: every document before the pivot's cursor provably cannot reach *θ* and is skipped. The pivot document is fully scored; entering the top-*k* raises *θ* and sharpens pruning.

> **Theorem 1 (WAND safety [5]).** *WAND evaluates exactly the set of documents that can enter the top-k under the scoring function, and returns precisely the top-k documents in score order. Skipped documents are provably incapable of exceeding the final threshold.*

**MaxScore** sorts terms by decreasing upper bound and splits them into *essential* terms (whose bounds alone can exceed *θ*) and *non-essential* terms, aborting a document's scoring early once its partial score plus remaining bounds cannot reach *θ*.

**Block-Max WAND** [11] fuses the two ideas at block granularity: each posting block stores its block-maximum term contribution, yielding tighter bounds than the global term bound, with pivot logic operating over blocks. On long queries — where many terms each contribute weakly — it roughly halves the scored postings relative to plain WAND.

```python
def heap_min(h):
    return min(s for s, _d in h)

def wand_topk(postings, upper_bounds, k):
    # postings: dict term -> sorted list of (doc_id, partial_score)
    # upper_bounds: dict term -> max achievable contribution of that term
    cursors = {t: 0 for t in postings}
    topk = []          # scored candidates so far
    threshold = 0.0

    def cursor_doc(term):
        i = cursors[term]
        if i < len(postings[term]):
            doc, _score = postings[term][i]
            return doc
        return float('inf')

    while True:
        ordered = sorted(postings, key=cursor_doc)  # terms by cursor position
        first_term = next(iter(ordered))
        if cursor_doc(first_term) == float('inf'):
            break
        # accumulate upper bounds until the pivot exceeds the threshold
        acc, pivot = 0.0, None
        for term in ordered:
            acc += upper_bounds[term]
            if acc > threshold:
                pivot = term
                break
        if pivot is None:
            break  # nothing left can beat the threshold
        pivot_doc = cursor_doc(pivot)
        if cursor_doc(first_term) == pivot_doc:
            # all cursors agree: fully score the pivot document
            s = 0.0
            for t in postings:
                i = cursors[t]
                d, sc = postings[t][i]
                if d == pivot_doc:
                    s += sc
            push_or_replace(topk, k, (s, pivot_doc))
            threshold = heap_min(topk) if len(topk) == k else 0.0
            advance_all_to(postings, cursors, pivot_doc + 1)
        else:
            advance_all_to(postings, cursors, pivot_doc)  # skip to pivot
    return sorted(topk, reverse=True)
```

In practice these algorithms run document-at-a-time over compressed blocks, with the threshold shared across shards only after local top-*k* lists are computed — keeping pruning safe under sharding.

### 4.3 Learning-to-rank: LambdaMART

BM25 scores terms; **learning-to-rank** scores *query–document pairs* using hundreds of engineered features — BM25 variants per field (title, body, anchor text, URL), PageRank-style static scores, click-derived features, proximity features, and freshness signals. The learning problem is listwise: optimize NDCG, a discontinuous, rank-based metric, rather than per-document error.

**LambdaRank** solved the differentiability problem with *lambdas*: for a document pair, the gradient is the RankNet pairwise gradient scaled by the NDCG change that swapping the pair would induce. **LambdaMART** [8] fits gradient-boosted regression trees to these lambdas each boosting iteration, accumulating them into the ranking function. The combination won the Yahoo! Learning to Rank Challenge and remained the production workhorse for over a decade.

Production imposes its own constraints: ensembles must score thousands of candidates within milliseconds, so models stay shallow with early-exit tree evaluation, and feature computation is budgeted per cascade stage. Training uses distributed boosting over billions of logged impressions with debiasing — clicks conflate relevance with position, so counterfactual and interleaving methods separate the two.

### 4.4 Neural ranking: cross-encoders, dense retrieval, and late interaction

Pretrained transformers raised the quality ceiling — and the cost floor. A **cross-encoder** concatenates query and document for a full BERT forward pass; superb quality, prohibitive latency for retrieval, so cross-encoders live in the final re-ranking stage.

**Dense retrieval** restores sublinear search by factorizing encoding: a dual encoder maps queries and documents independently into one vector space, and retrieval becomes **approximate nearest-neighbor (ANN)** search — HNSW graphs, IVF indexes, product quantization — over precomputed vectors [9]. The price is the *single-vector bottleneck*: one embedding loses the fine-grained term matching BM25 does effortlessly.

**ColBERT** [10] breaks the bottleneck with *late interaction*: queries and documents are each encoded as *bags of contextualized token embeddings*, indexed offline, and scored by MaxSim — each query token takes its maximum similarity over document tokens, summed over the query. Documents are still pre-encoded, but matching is token-level again; residual compression and centroid pruning (PLAID) keep the token index tractable, delivering near-cross-encoder effectiveness at retrieval latency.

| Model family | Paradigm | Query latency | Index cost | Effectiveness trend |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| BM25 [4] | lexical, unsupervised | microseconds | postings only | strong baseline |
| LambdaMART [8] | LTR over hand features | ~ms per 1k docs | features + trees | production standard |
| Cross-encoder | full pair attention | ~10ms per pair | none (rerank only) | quality ceiling |
| Dual encoder / DPR [9] | single-vector ANN | ~ms via HNSW | 1 vector/doc | fast, bottlenecked |
| ColBERT [10] | late interaction | ~10–50ms | tokens × dim/doc | near-ceiling at speed |

Hybrid systems are the pragmatic endpoint: sparse (BM25) and dense scores are fused, because lexical precision and semantic recall fail on *different* queries.

## 5. Empirical Results and Analysis

### 5.1 Compression and decoding

On TREC-style collections, bit-level codes reach ~5–9 bits per posting but decode at tens of millions of integers per second; PForDelta-family and SIMD codecs [6][7] land at 6–9 bits per posting while decoding at *billions* of integers per second. The index working set therefore fits in RAM, and query-time decoding is rarely the bottleneck; memory bandwidth is. Partitioned Elias–Fano pays a small space premium for *O(1)* random access, simplifying skip-heavy query plans.

### 5.2 Pruning effectiveness

Dynamic pruning wins where it matters: long queries over skewed term distributions. On TREC Terabyte-style workloads, WAND [5] evaluates 2–4× fewer postings than exhaustive disjunctive scoring, and Block-Max WAND [11] roughly halves WAND's remaining work on many-term queries. Because pruning is *safe*, these speedups cost zero effectiveness — the top-*k* is bit-identical to exhaustive scoring.

### 5.3 Ranking quality

On MS MARCO passage ranking, MRR@10 tells the generational story: BM25-class baselines near 0.19; dual-encoder dense retrievers [9] near 0.31; ColBERT-style late interaction [10] near 0.36; cross-encoder re-rankers defining the ceiling near 0.39. Each quality step costs roughly an order of magnitude in per-document compute — the quantitative justification for the cascade.

### 5.4 Serving at scale

The cluster-architecture studies [2][3] report invariants that still hold: per-query work is dominated by the slowest shard, so operators provision for p99 latency; replication factors of 2–3 absorb failures and smooth load; hedged requests cut tail latency at modest cost. A production query typically budgets ~50–150 ms for retrieval and first-pass ranking across the shard fleet, reserving the rest of a few-hundred-millisecond SLO for neural re-ranking.

## 6. Limitations

**Index freshness.** The compression that makes the static index economical also makes it immutable. Real-time tiers handle breaking news, but merging static and dynamic results complicates scoring consistency — there is always a freshness gap, and spam and SEO manipulation exploit exactly the fastest-changing documents.

**Vocabulary mismatch.** Lexical retrieval fails when queries and documents use different words for the same concept; dense retrieval mitigates this but introduces failure modes on rare entities, numbers, and exact-match intents (codes, part numbers, quoted phrases). Hybrid fusion helps on average while doubling infrastructure.

**Evaluation bias.** Offline metrics on pooled judgments under-reward novel good answers the pool never saw; click logs reflect presentation bias. The field still lacks an evaluation protocol that is simultaneously unbiased, cheap, and sensitive — so production decisions rest on slow, expensive online experiments.

**Cost of dense models.** A ColBERT-style token index can be an order of magnitude larger than the postings it augments, and GPU serving for re-ranking dominates per-query energy. At 10^5 queries per second, milliseconds of accelerator time per query become datacenter-scale power draw — while tail queries, the majority of *distinct* queries, remain where learned rankers are weakest.

---

## 7. Conclusion

Web search at billion-query scale rests on four interlocking technologies. **Compressed inverted indexes** with SIMD codecs and quasi-succinct structures keep petabytes of postings in RAM and randomly accessible [6][7]. **Dynamic pruning** — WAND, MaxScore, Block-Max WAND — makes exact top-*k* retrieval affordable [5][11]. **Learning-to-rank**, from BM25's probabilistic foundations [4] through LambdaMART [8], converts behavioral and content signals into NDCG-optimized orderings. **Neural ranking** — cross-encoders, dense dual encoders, ColBERT late interaction [9][10] — raised the quality ceiling and forced the invention of ANN infrastructure. Underneath, sharded, replicated, tail-latency-controlled serving [2][3] turns these algorithms into a service with a latency SLO.

Open directions: learned index structures co-designed with the ranker; retrieval integrated with generative models, where the index becomes a knowledge source rather than a destination; evaluation methodology that keeps pace with models trained on the very signals used to judge them; and the economics of doing all of this, a hundred thousand times per second, within a power budget the planet can afford.
