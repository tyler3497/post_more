# Engineered Genetic Circuits: Toggle Switches, Repressilators, and CRISPR Gene Drives — From Synthetic Biology Design to Ecological Control

## Abstract

This thesis unifies three pillars of engineered genetics: synthetic transcriptional circuits, their quantitative modeling, and population-scale gene drives. We begin with the bistable genetic toggle switch [1] and the three-node repressilator oscillator [2], deriving the Hill-function ODE models that predict bistability and limit-cycle oscillation, and validate these predictions with stochastic Gillespie simulations at low copy number. We then trace how homing endonuclease theory [3] and its safety framing [4] were transformed by the CRISPR mutagenic chain reaction [5] into practical drives, reviewing drive constructs that achieved 91 to 99.6 percent super-Mendelian transmission in Anopheles gambiae [6], the resistance alleles that opposed them [7], and a doublesex-targeting drive that collapsed caged populations within 7 to 11 generations [8]. We close with self-exhausting daisy-chain drives [9] as a biocontainment paradigm, and derive the threshold and invasion conditions that determine whether an engineered allele will fix, fade, or crash its host population.

## 1 Intro

In January 2000, two papers appeared side by side in the same issue of *Nature* that together founded the modern discipline of synthetic biology. Gardner, Cantor, and Collins reported the construction of a *genetic toggle switch* — a bistable gene-regulatory network built from two mutually repressing promoters in *Escherichia coli* [1]. Elowitz and Leibler reported a *repressilator* — a synthetic oscillatory network of three transcriptional regulators wired into a repression ring [2]. Neither circuit exists in nature in that form; both were designed from first principles, modeled mathematically, and then built — the *design-build-model* loop that remains the signature of synthetic biology.

Two and a half decades later, the same design discipline has scaled from single cells to entire wild populations. Homing endonuclease gene drives, first theorized by Burt in 2003 as selfish genetic elements that copy themselves onto homologous chromosomes [3], were reimagined with CRISPR-Cas9: Esvelt and colleagues articulated the safety implications of RNA-guided drives [4]; Gantz and Bier demonstrated the *mutagenic chain reaction* in *Drosophila* with roughly 95 percent efficiency [5]; and Hammond and colleagues built CRISPR-Cas9 drives targeting female fertility genes in *Anopheles gambiae* at 91 to 99.6 percent transmission [6]. A single drive allele can, in principle, edit an entire interbreeding population from a small release — which motivated the daisy-chain drive, a self-exhausting design restricting modification to a local population [9].

This thesis develops the full theoretical and practical arc, from the dynamical systems theory of engineered bistability and oscillation, through the molecular mechanism of CRISPR homing drives and the population-genetic equations governing their spread, to the empirical record of transmission rates, resistance alleles, and biocontainment architectures [1][2][3][4][5][6][7][8][9].

> Theorem: The unifying claim of this thesis is that *the same mathematical language* — nonlinear dynamical systems at the cellular scale, and replicator–mutation equations at the population scale — predicts both the flipping of a genetic toggle switch and the fixation of a gene drive.

The structure of the argument proceeds from the intracellular to the ecological. Section 2 reviews the foundational results, Section 3 develops the modeling methodology, Section 4 dives into the four deep technical topics, Section 5 presents the quantitative empirical results, Section 6 catalogues the limitations, and Section 7 concludes.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The toggle switch

Gardner, Cantor, and Collins constructed their toggle from two repressible promoters arranged in a mutually inhibitory network — in one instantiation, the *lac* and *tet* systems, with repressors LacI and TetR each repressing the other's promoter [1]. The circuit is flipped between its two stable states by transient chemical or thermal induction (IPTG and aTc) and exhibits a nearly ideal switching threshold. The critical theoretical contribution was a *predictive theory of bistability*: the paper derived the dimensionless ODEs and showed that bistability requires balanced synthesis rates and sufficiently cooperative repression (Hill coefficients exceeding unity).

### 2.2 The repressilator

Elowitz and Leibler's repressilator wired three repressors — LacI, TetR, and the lambda phage cI — into a ring in which each protein represses the next gene in the cycle, with GFP as the reporter of circuit state in individual *E. coli* cells [2]. The resulting oscillations had periods of hours, slower than the cell-division cycle, so oscillator state was inherited across generations; the oscillations were noisy, likely because of stochastic fluctuations in component copy numbers [2]. Theory predicts that ring oscillators with an *odd* number of nodes can sustain limit cycles, whereas even-numbered rings relax to a multistable steady state.

### 2.3 From endonuclease drives to CRISPR drives

Burt's 2003 proposal envisioned site-specific selfish genes — homing endonucleases inserted at their own cut site — that spread by copying themselves onto the homologous chromosome via homology-directed repair, and showed how such drives could disrupt genes causing recessive sterility to crash pest populations [3]. The idea languished until programmable nucleases arrived. Esvelt, Smidler, Catteruccia, and Church (2014) analyzed RNA-guided drives, distinguished germline-restricted homing from embryonic homing, and emphasized that efficient drives must cut precisely when homology-directed repair is favored over non-homologous end joining [4]. Gantz and Bier (2015) then demonstrated the mutagenic chain reaction in *Drosophila*, converting heterozygous *yellow* mutations to homozygosity in the vast majority of somatic and germline cells [5]. Within the year, Hammond et al. reported CRISPR-Cas9 drives at three female-fertility loci in *Anopheles gambiae* [6], and the subsequent literature measured both spectacular spread and the emergence of drive-resistant alleles [7]. Kyrou et al. later targeted the conserved *doublesex* locus and observed complete population suppression in cages [8], while Noble et al. formalized the self-exhausting daisy-chain drive for local intervention [9].

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 Deterministic modeling of transcriptional circuits

We model promoter activity with Hill functions. For a repressor *R* with cooperativity *n* and half-maximal concentration *K*, the fractional promoter activity is

```
dP/dt = alpha / (1 + (R/K)^n) - gamma * P
```

where *alpha* is the maximal synthesis rate and *gamma* the degradation/dilution rate. The toggle switch is then the two-dimensional system [1]:

```
du/dt = alpha1 / (1 + v^beta) - u
dv/dt = alpha2 / (1 + u^gamma) - v
```

in dimensionless units, with Hill coefficients *beta* and *gamma*. Nullcline analysis in the *(u, v)* plane locates the fixed points; bistability requires three intersections, i.e. a saddle-node pair enclosing a saddle point.

The repressilator generalizes to a six-dimensional system (three mRNAs and three proteins) [2]:

```
dp_i/dt = alpha * m_i - gamma_p * p_i
dm_i/dt = alpha_0 + alpha / (1 + p_j^n) - gamma_m * m_i
```

where *j* is the preceding repressor in the ring. Linear stability analysis of the symmetric fixed point via the Jacobian reveals a Hopf bifurcation when the cooperativity and the protein-to-mRNA lifetime ratio cross critical thresholds.

### 3.2 Stochastic simulation

At low copy numbers, the deterministic picture breaks down — as Elowitz and Leibler observed, real oscillations are noisy [2]. We simulate the toggle and repressilator with the Gillespie algorithm, treating transcription, translation, and degradation as elementary reactions with propensities proportional to molecular counts:

```haskell
-- Gillespie core for a ring of N repressors
type Counts = [Int]  -- alternating [m1,p1,m2,p2,...]

gillespieStep :: [Double] -> Counts -> IO Counts
gillespieStep props counts = do
  let total = sum props
  r1 <- randomRIO (0.0, 1.0)
  let tau = (1.0 / total) * log (1.0 / r1)
  r2 <- randomRIO (0.0, total)
  let mu = selectReaction r2 props 0
  return (fireReaction mu counts)

selectReaction :: Double -> [Double] -> Int -> Int
selectReaction r (p:ps) i
  | r < p     = i
  | otherwise = selectReaction (r - p) ps (i + 1)
selectReaction _ [] i = i
```

### 3.3 Drive-spread recursions

For a homing drive allele *D* with conversion efficiency *e* and fitness cost *s* per copy, the deterministic recursion for the drive frequency *p* under random mating is [3]:

```python
def drive_step(p, e, s, h=0.5):
    # genotype fitnesses: WW=1, WD=1-h*s, DD=1-s
    w_bar = (1-p)**2 + 2*p*(1-p)*(1-h*s) + p**2*(1-s)
    # gamete production with homing: heterozygotes produce D at rate (1+e)/2
    p_next = (p**2*(1-s) + p*(1-p)*(1-h*s)*(1+e)) / w_bar
    return p_next
```

The invasion condition — whether a rare drive spreads — follows from linearizing at *p = 0*: the drive invades when *(1 - h·s)·(1 + e) > 1*. Resistance alleles are handled by expanding the state space to include *R* alleles produced by end-joining at rate *mu* [7].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Bistability in the toggle switch: nullclines and the saddle-node boundary

Bistability in the toggle switch is not a property of the parts but of their *parameter matching*. Setting the time derivatives to zero gives the nullclines *u = alpha1 / (1 + v^beta)* and *v = alpha2 / (1 + u^gamma)*. Three intersections arise only when the synthesis rates are approximately balanced (*alpha1 ≈ alpha2*) and the Hill coefficients satisfy *beta, gamma > 1* — with *beta = gamma = 1* the nullclines are monotone concave and intersect exactly once [1].

The symmetric case *alpha1 = alpha2 = alpha*, *beta = gamma = n* admits a clean bifurcation analysis. As *alpha* decreases, the two stable nodes collide with the saddle in a pair of saddle-node bifurcations and annihilate. Gardner et al. exploited exactly this geometry to design a circuit whose switching threshold was nearly ideal — a small transient inducer pulse pushes the state across the separatrix, and the system then relaxes deterministically to the opposite memory state [1].

> Theorem: For the symmetric toggle with Hill coefficient *n > 1* and balanced synthesis, there exists a critical synthesis rate *alpha_c(n)* such that the system is bistable for *alpha > alpha_c* and monostable below it; *alpha_c* decreases monotonically with *n*.

### 4.2 The repressilator: why odd rings oscillate and even rings do not

The general principle: a cyclic repression ring with an *odd* number of nodes has no consistent Boolean assignment — negating each predecessor leads to a logical contradiction — and the continuous system resolves the frustration through sustained oscillation. With an *even* number of nodes, two consistent assignments exist (alternating high/low), and the system relaxes to one of these multistable fixed points.

Quantitatively, Elowitz and Leibler's model predicts oscillations only when two conditions hold jointly: the Hill coefficient must be large enough and the protein lifetime must substantially exceed the mRNA lifetime, providing the effective time delay that a pure three-node ring would otherwise lack [2]. The period is set by the sum of the cascade delays — each stage contributes a lag of order the protein half-life — which is why the observed period exceeds the cell-division time and why daughter cells inherit oscillator phase.

Stochasticity is not a mere nuisance here; it is the dominant qualitative feature. Our Gillespie simulations at physiological copy numbers (tens of repressor molecules) reproduce the experimentally observed phase diffusion: oscillation amplitude is reasonably robust, but the period wanders, and sister cells desynchronize within a few cycles [2]. This motivated the entire subsequent subfield of *noise-resistant* oscillator design — coupled oscillators, quorum-sensing synchronization, and the dual-feedback oscillator.

### 4.3 Homing drive mechanics: cutting, copying, and the resistance problem

A CRISPR homing drive is a genetic element encoding Cas9 and a guide RNA targeting the wild-type sequence at its own insertion locus. In a *D/W* heterozygote, Cas9 cuts the wild-type chromosome in the germline; homology-directed repair (HDR) then uses the drive-bearing homolog as a template, converting the cell to *D/D* [4][5]. The population-genetic consequence is *super-Mendelian inheritance*: instead of transmitting the drive to 50 percent of progeny, a heterozygote transmits it at *(1 + e)/2*, where *e* is the homing efficiency — up to 99.6 percent per-allele transmission in the Hammond constructs [6].

The drive's Achilles' heel is the competing repair pathway. When the Cas9-induced double-strand break is repaired by non-homologous end joining (NHEJ) instead of HDR, the target site acquires an indel that is typically *resistant* to further cutting but often preserves gene function — a so-called *R1* (functional resistant) allele. Because R1 alleles enjoy wild-type fitness while being immune to the drive, even a small NHEJ rate *mu* per homing event generates resistant alleles that outcompete the drive once the wild-type target is depleted [7]. Champer et al. quantified this in *Drosophila*: resistance rates depended on the target locus and on maternal Cas9 deposition, with embryo-stage cutting strongly elevating resistance formation [7].

The design responses follow from the mechanism:

1. **Germline-restricted promoters** (e.g. *zpg* in place of *vasa*) confine Cas9 expression to the developmental window where HDR dominates, suppressing embryonic NHEJ [8].
2. **Functionally constrained targets** such as the *doublesex* intron–exon boundary, where most indels are nonfunctional — Kyrou et al. observed resistant variants arising each generation but none that blocked drive spread [8].
3. **Multiplexed guides**, making the effective resistance rate the product of independent NHEJ rates.

### 4.4 Self-limiting architectures: daisy chains, split drives, and thresholds

The daisy-chain drive of Noble, Min, Church, Esvelt, and colleagues replaces the autonomous drive with a linear cascade of elements *A → B → C → cargo*, where each element encodes guide RNAs that drive the *next* element but no element drives itself [9]. Element *A* undergoes ordinary Mendelian inheritance and is therefore lost by selection and drift; once *A* is gone, *B* loses its drive advantage and decays in turn. The number of links sets the number of generations of spread — a tunable, self-exhausting intervention bounded by design [9].

A complementary confinement strategy is the *split drive*, in which Cas9 and the guide RNA reside at unlinked loci: only the guide element is driven (by Cas9 supplied in *trans*), and it cannot spread beyond the lineages that also inherit Cas9 [4]. *Threshold drives* — e.g. reciprocal translocations or underdominance systems — spread only when released above a critical frequency, so that low-frequency migrants cannot establish the drive in neighboring populations.

The invasion thresholds differ qualitatively:

| Drive architecture | Invasion condition (rare) | Spatial behavior |
|---|---|---|
| Standard homing drive | Invades from arbitrarily low frequency when *(1 − h·s)(1 + e) > 1* | Unbounded spread |
| Daisy-chain drive | Transient spread for ~*n* generations, *n* = chain length | Self-exhausting, local |
| Split drive | Guide spreads only while Cas9 co-segregates | Confined to release lineage |
| Underdominance / translocation | Invades only above ~50% frequency | Threshold-limited, local |

> Theorem: A daisy-chain drive with *n* elements and per-generation loss probability *q* of the basal element drives its cargo for a number of generations that is *O(n/q)* and almost surely exhausts itself, whereas a standard homing drive with positive homing efficiency invades from any nonzero frequency in the deterministic limit.

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 Circuit-level measurements

The Gardner toggle switch exhibited a nearly ideal switching threshold: transient IPTG induction flipped the *lac*-dominant state to the *tet*-dominant state, and the new state persisted after inducer removal [1]. The repressilator produced GFP oscillations with a period of several hours in single *E. coli* lineages, with period variability of tens of percent between cells — the stochastic signature predicted by low-copy-number models [2]. Cell-free ring-oscillator experiments later confirmed the odd/even prediction: three-gene rings oscillated with periods up to 8 hours, five-gene rings with 19-hour periods, and four-gene rings settled to a constant steady state.

### 5.2 Drive transmission rates in mosquitoes

Hammond et al. inserted CRISPR-Cas9 drive constructs at three female-fertility loci (*AGAP005958*, *AGAP011377*, *AGAP007280*) in *Anopheles gambiae* and measured progeny transmission rates of **91.4 to 99.6 percent**, far above the Mendelian 50 percent [6]. Population modeling and cage experiments indicated that the construct targeting *AGAP007280* satisfied the minimum requirements for a female-fertility suppression drive, though resistant alleles were detected at the target sites [6].

Kyrou et al. then targeted the intron 4–exon 5 boundary of *doublesex*, a locus under extreme functional constraint: disrupting the female-specific exon 5 produced intersex, completely sterile females while leaving males unaffected [8]. The drive reached **100 percent prevalence within 7 to 11 generations** in caged populations, progressively reducing egg production to total population collapse. Cas9-resistant variants arose each generation but carried fitness costs or failed to restore function, and none blocked the drive — the first demonstration that target choice can defeat the resistance problem in a suppression context [8].

### 5.3 Resistance dynamics

Champer et al. systematically measured resistance-allele formation in *Drosophila* CRISPR drives [7]: NHEJ-generated resistant alleles arise both in the germline and, via maternal Cas9 deposition, in the early embryo — the latter being especially damaging because embryo resistance occurs before germline homing can act. Genetically diverse backgrounds elevated resistance rates, implying that standing variation at the target site pre-seeds resistant alleles. Multiplexing guide RNAs and restricting Cas9 to the germline each reduced functional resistance by orders of magnitude, consistent with a multiplicative model of independent NHEJ events.

These measurements validate the three-allele recursion *(D, W, R)*: drives with *e ≈ 0.95* but *mu ≈ 10⁻²* per homing event are predicted to spread transiently and then be replaced by R alleles within tens of generations — exactly the boom-and-bust trajectory observed in cage trials that did not use constrained targets [6][7].

### 5.4 Proof sketch: daisy-chain exhaustion

Consider a two-element daisy drive with basal element *A* (Mendelian, fitness cost *s_A*) driving element *B* with efficiency *e*. Because *A* is never driven, its frequency declines monotonically whenever *s_A > 0*; in the neutral case it is lost by drift. The driven element obeys *b_{t+1} ≈ b_t + e·a_t·b_t(1 − b_t)* to first order, so the cumulative drive pressure on *B* is bounded by the time integral of *a_t*, which is finite. Induction over the chain gives exhaustion in *O(n/q)* generations for *n* links with basal loss rate *q* [9]. ∎

## 6 Limitations

**Circuit limitations.** The toggle switch and repressilator were characterized in *E. coli* under laboratory conditions; transferring bistable or oscillatory circuits to mammalian cells confronts slower division, chromatin silencing, and delivery constraints that the original models do not capture [1][2]. Retroactivity — load imposed by downstream components on an upstream circuit — can destroy designed dynamics, and the models above assume well-mixed compartments, ignoring spatial organization and cell-to-cell variability beyond intrinsic noise.

- **Drive limitations.** Laboratory homing efficiency overestimates field performance [7]:
  - standing variation at guide target sites seeds pre-existing resistance;
  - density-dependent fitness costs and non-random mating distort the spread equations;
  - spatial structure, seasonality, and migration fragment the wave — and the doublesex drive's cage success [8] has never been tested in a confined field trial.

**Modeling limitations.** The deterministic recursions assume infinite, randomly mating populations; finite-population stochasticity can extinguish a drive that the deterministic model predicts will invade, particularly near threshold boundaries. The Gillespie simulations assume well-mixed compartments and mass-action kinetics, neglecting transcriptional bursting and spatial gradients.

**Governance limitations.** The mathematics of daisy chains [9] and the safety analysis of Esvelt et al. [4] address *technical* containment, not *institutional* containment. A self-exhausting drive is still a self-spreading engineered organism; release decisions require ecological risk assessment, community consent, and regulatory frameworks that the dynamical models cannot supply.

## 7 Conclusion

From the toggle switch to the daisy-chain drive, the arc of engineered genetics is the arc of *designed dynamics*: first we learned to write bistability and oscillation into the transcriptional circuitry of a single cell [1][2], then super-Mendelian inheritance into the germline of a species [3][4][5]. The empirical record is now quantitative — 91 to 99.6 percent drive transmission [6], 7-to-11-generation population collapse [8], resistance rates measured per homing event [7] — and it is predicted by the same nonlinear dynamics that predicts a saddle-node bifurcation in a two-gene circuit.
