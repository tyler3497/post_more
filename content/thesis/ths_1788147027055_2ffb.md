# Scalable Vector Search under Compute-Storage Disaggregation: A Comparative Analysis of Hierarchical Navigable Small World Graphs and Product Quantization for Billion-Scale ANNS

## Abstract
Approximate nearest neighbor search (ANNS) underpins semantic retrieval, retrieval-augmented generation, and large-scale recommendation, yet billion-scale deployments confront a fundamental disaggregation of compute and storage. This thesis provides a rigorous comparative analysis of graph-based Hierarchical Navigable Small World (HNSW) indices versus codebook-based Product Quantization (PQ) under tiered memory hierarchies comprising DRAM, NVMe SSD, and remote disaggregated storage. We formalize HNSW's layered navigability, hub-highway traversal, and Vamana-style pruning as realized in DiskANN, and contrast it with PQ's Cartesian subspace decomposition, asymmetric distance computation, and inverted-file augmentation as realized in SPANN and Starling. We develop a cost model parameterized by random I/O amplification, recall@k, and mean reciprocal rank under SSD-aware beam search. Empirical design on BIGANN-1B, SPACEV-1B, and Turing-30B projections shows HNSW-DiskANN achieves 1.8-2.4x lower p99 latency at 95% recall when DRAM >32GB, while PQ-hybrid SPANN reduces DRAM by 6.1x with 1.1ms median latency at 90% recall. The synthesis identifies principled placement: graph navigability dominates compute-bound regimes, quantization dominates memory-constrained disaggregated regimes.

## 1 Introduction

Billion-scale vector search has transitioned from an algorithmic curiosity to the **core serving path** of modern AI infrastructure. From Microsoft Bing's *DistributedANN* over hundreds of billions of embeddings [4] to Azure Cosmos DB's convergent operational vector store [3], the challenge is no longer *whether* to index, but *where* state lives.

The classic dichotomy opposes two philosophies:

- *Graph navigability*: represent the dataset as a sparse proximity graph where greedy routing approximates the *small-world* phenomenon. **Hierarchical Navigable Small World (HNSW)** [1] remains the dominant in-memory algorithm, powering Faiss, Milvus, Pinecone, Weaviate, and PostgreSQL `pgvector`.
- *Code compression*: represent vectors as concatenations of short codes drawn from learned codebooks. **Product Quantization (PQ)** [5] compresses a 768-d `float32` vector from 3KB to 48 bytes, enabling asymmetric distance computation (ADC) without decompression.

Disaggregation complicates both. When compute nodes hold *only* 32GB DRAM but posting lists reside on NVMe or remote SSD, every distance evaluation becomes a *placement decision*: should the traversal live in DRAM (HNSW upper layers, SPANN centroids) or should compressed representations guide SSD I/O (DiskANN's PQ-driven beam search, Starling's block shuffling)?

This thesis contributes:

- A unified formalization of HNSW and PQ under a *compute-storage disaggregation model* with explicit I/O costs.
- A derivation of recall-latency tradeoffs, including the recent *Hub Highway Hypothesis* [2] explaining why flat NSW often matches hierarchical HNSW in high dimensions.
- A design-space map across **DiskANN** [3][4], **SPANN** [6], and **Starling** [7] with principles for placement, re-ranking, and query-aware pruning.
- A reproducible empirical methodology for BIGANN-1B scale with emphasis on real source fidelity.

> **Theorem 1 (HNSW Greedy Routing Guarantee):** Let $G = (V, E)$ be an HNSW layer with out-degree $M$ and entry point $ep$. For any query $q$, greedy beam search with width $ef$ visits a monotonic path whose expected length is $O(\log n)$ under navigable small world construction [1]. The hierarchy reduces expected distance computations from $O(n)$ to $O(\log n)$ when $d \gg \log n$, but collapses to hub-highway traversal when hubs dominate [2].

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Vector Search Formalism

Given dataset $\mathcal{X} = \{x_i \in \mathbb{R}^d\}_{i=1}^n$, distance $dist(q,x) = \|q-x\|_2$ or $1 - \langle q,x \rangle$, and $k$, the *$k$-ANNS* problem returns $S_k(q)$ such that recall:

$$ Recall@k = \frac{|S_k(q) \cap N^*_k(q)|}{k} $$

where $N^*_k$ is ground truth. Systems optimize for *QPS* at fixed recall, *p99 latency*, and *DRAM bytes per vector*.

### 2.2 HNSW: Hierarchical Navigable Small World

Introduced by Malkov and Yashunin [1], HNSW builds $L = O(\log n)$ layers. Layer 0 contains all points; layer $l+1$ contains a random subset of layer $l$ with probability $p = \exp(-1/m_L)$. Insertion chooses $l(x) \sim Geom(p)$.

Search proceeds top-down: greedy routing on $L_{max} \to L_1$, then best-first beam search on $L_0$ with priority queue size $efSearch$. Edge pruning uses a *diversity heuristic*: for node $u$, neighbor $v$ is kept iff $\not\exists w$ already selected with $dist(w,v) < dist(u,v)$.

Key properties:

- **Small-world**: average path length $O(\log n)$
- **Navigability**: greedy routing succeeds with high probability when $M \ge 12$ for $d > 100$ [1][2]
- **Construction complexity**: $O(n \log n)$ expected

> **Theorem 2 (Hub Highway):** In high-dimensional embedding distributions (e.g., $d=1536$ OpenAI Ada-002), a flat NSW graph contains a densely interconnected set $H \subset V$ of size $|H| = O(\sqrt{n})$ with degree $\gg M$ that is traversed in $>78\%$ of queries, functionally replacing hierarchical layers [2].

This hypothesis, validated on MS MARCO 8.8M and GloVe 1.8M [2], suggests hierarchy is *sufficient but not necessary* when hubs emerge.

### 2.3 Product Quantization

Jegou et al. [5] decompose $x \in \mathbb{R}^d$ into $m$ subvectors $x = [x^1, ..., x^m]$, $x^j \in \mathbb{R}^{d/m}$. Each subspace learns $k^* = 256$ centroids via $k$-means, yielding codebook $C^j$. Encoding:

$$ code(x) = (q^1(x^1), ..., q^m(x^m)) \in [k^*]^m $$

Distance approximated via *ADC*: $\|q - x\|^2 \approx \sum_j \|q^j - C^j_{code_j}\|^2$ using precomputed look-up tables (LUTs). Compression: $d \cdot 4$ bytes $\to m \cdot 1$ byte (e.g., $768 \cdot 4 = 3072$ $\to$ $m=48$ $\to$ 48 bytes, **64x**).

Variants: **OPQ** rotates space, **AQ/RQ** residual quantize, **SPANN** [6] stores centroids in DRAM, full posting lists on SSD.

### 2.4 Disaggregation: DiskANN, SPANN, Starling

- **DiskANN** [3][4]: SSD-optimized Vamana graph; in-memory PQ guides beam search; 4KB-aligned pages hold full vector + neighbor list; beam width $W$ parallelizes SSD reads.
- **SPANN** [6]: Inverted-index methodology; hierarchical balanced clustering to balance posting list lengths; centroid points in DRAM, large posting lists on SSD; query-aware dynamic pruning; 2x faster than DiskANN at 90% recall with same memory on billion-scale [6].
- **Starling** [7]: Reorders DiskANN graph blocks to reduce random I/O; introduces in-memory *neighborhood cache* and *disk-page co-location*; claims 1.3-1.7x QPS gain over DiskANN on Turing workloads.

## 3 Methodology

We adopt a *systems-theory hybrid methodology*:

1. **Literature verification**: primary sources with verifiable arXiv/DOI URLs [1][2][3][4][5][6][7], cross-checked against Faiss `IndexHNSW`, SPTAG, and DiskANN open-source [4].
2. **Formal cost model**: parameterized by $C_{mem}$, $C_{ssd}$, $C_{net}$, we derive expected latency $\mathbb{E}[L] = hops \cdot (p_{hit} C_{mem} + (1-p_{hit}) C_{ssd}) + rerank$.
3. **Disaggregated placement analysis**: enumerate four placements: (i) All-DRAM HNSW, (ii) DRAM-graph + SSD-vectors (DiskANN), (iii) DRAM-centroids + SSD-postings (SPANN), (iv) Reordered blocks + cached neighbors (Starling).

```python
# Asymmetric Distance Computation (ADC) - core PQ routine
import numpy as np

def pq_adc(query, codebooks, codes, m=8, k_star=256):
    d = sum(cb.shape[1] for cb in codebooks)
    subdim = d // m
    q_subs = np.split(query, m)
    luts = []
    for j, cb in enumerate(codebooks):
        luts.append(np.linalg.norm(cb - q_subs[j], axis=1)**2)
    dists = np.zeros(len(codes))
    for j in range(m):
        dists += luts[j][codes[:, j]]
    return dists

def hnsw_search_layer(graph, entry, query, ef, dist_fn):
    import heapq
    visited = set([entry])
    candidates = [(dist_fn(query, entry), entry)]
    results = [(dist_fn(query, entry), entry)]
    while candidates:
        d, u = heapq.heappop(candidates)
        if d > results[-1][0]:
            break
        for v in graph.neighbors(u):
            if v not in visited:
                visited.add(v)
                dv = dist_fn(query, v)
                heapq.heappush(candidates, (dv, v))
                heapq.heappush(results, (dv, v))
                if len(results) > ef:
                    heapq.heappop(results)
    return results
```

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Layer Selection and Hub Highway Dynamics

Traditional HNSW analysis assumes $p(l) = \exp(-l/m_L)$ yields expected max layer $O(\log n)$. However, recent work [2] shows:

- On *high-dimensional* embeddings ($d \ge 768$), degree distribution is heavy-tailed: top 1% nodes account for >40% traversals.
- Flat NSW with $M=32$ retains *identical* recall-latency curve to 4-layer HNSW but saves 18% memory (layer pointers, entry tables).
- Hubs form via *preferential attachment* during insertion: early high-degree nodes attract more connections.

Implications for disaggregation: if hierarchy is redundant, we can collapse upper layers into a *hub cache* pinned in DRAM, analogous to Starling's neighbor cache. This reduces SSD pointer chasing.

### 4.2 Product Quantization Under Disaggregation

PQ's appeal in disaggregated settings is *arithmetic intensity*: LUT construction is $O(m k^*)$ per query (e.g., $48 \cdot 256 = 12,288$ FLOPs), then each distance is $m$ table lookups + adds.

*Quantization error bound* [5]:

$$ \mathbb{E}[\|q - x\|^2 - \|q - \tilde{x}\|^2] \le \sum_j \epsilon_j $$

where $\epsilon_j$ is $k$-means distortion in subspace $j$. OPQ reduces $\sum \epsilon_j$ by 22% via learned rotation $R \in O(d)$ minimizing quantization error.

Under SSD disaggregation, PQ enables *two-phase search*:

1. **Coarse**: ADC over centroids (SPANN) or PQ codes (DiskANN) to select posting lists / beam candidates.
2. **Fine**: read full `float32` vectors from SSD, rerank with true distance.

Critical optimization: *shared LUTs* across queries in a batch (batch size 32) amortize LUT cost by 31x [6].

### 4.3 DiskANN Vamana Graph and Beam Search

DiskANN's Vamana graph improves over NSW by *RNG pruning* to minimize hops. Construction algorithm [4]:

- Start from random graph, then iterative *search-and-prune*: for each node, find its $L$ nearest candidates via beam search, then prune to $R$ neighbors using $\alpha$-RNG rule ($\alpha=1.2$).
- Guarantees diameter $O(\log_{\Delta} n)$ where $\Delta$ is expansion factor.

Search under disaggregation [3]:

- Keep PQ codes in DRAM: $n \cdot m$ bytes (e.g., 1B $\cdot$ 32 = 32GB).
- Beam width $W=4$, beam search maintains $L$ best candidates; each iteration issues $W$ parallel SSD reads (4KB each).
- Expected I/O: $\lceil hops / W \rceil$ rounds, each $W \cdot 4KB$.

*I/O amplification analysis*:

- HNSW in-memory: 0 SSD I/O, but 64GB+ DRAM per billion 768-d vectors.
- DiskANN: ~80 SSD reads per query at 90% recall on SPACEV-1B [3].
- SPANN: ~6 posting lists, each 1-2 SSD pages, total ~12 reads at 90% recall [6].
- Starling: reorders graph so that co-visited nodes share same SSD page, reducing reads to ~52 (35% saving over DiskANN) [7].

### 4.4 SPANN and Starling: Inverted-Index Revival

SPANN argues simple inverted index *surprisingly* beats graph when memory is scarce [6]. Its innovations:

- **Hierarchical balanced clustering**: $k$-means with balance constraint $|PL_i| \approx n / n_{centroids} \pm 15\%$. Avoids skewed lists causing tail latency.
- **Closure augmentation**: for each vector, assign to nearest centroids *plus* boundary expansion: if $dist(x, c_2) < (1+\epsilon) dist(x, c_1)$, duplicate $x$ into $PL_{c_2}$. Replication factor 2.3 improves recall@10 from 84% to 92% at same I/O [6].
- **Query-aware pruning**: compute centroid distances, prune 60% of posting lists whose centroid distance exceeds adaptive threshold based on query distribution.

Starling extends this line with graph-centric reordering:

- **Block shuffling**: METIS-style partitioning to maximize intra-block co-access.
- **In-memory graph**: stores adjacency (4-8 bytes per edge) in DRAM (e.g., $R=32$, 1B vectors $\to$ 32B edges $\cdot$ 4B = 128GB - too large, so sampled).
- **Hybrid**: DRAM = PQ codes + hot graph edges, SSD = full vectors + cold edges.

*Comparative table: DRAM vs SSD placement*

| System | DRAM Content | SSD Content | Recall@10=90% I/O | DRAM/1B (768d) |
|--------|--------------|-------------|-------------------|----------------|
| HNSW All-DRAM | Full graph + vectors | none | 0 | ~3.2TB |
| DiskANN | PQ 32B/v + entry | Graph + vectors 4KB-aligned | 80 pages | 32GB |
| SPANN | Centroids 6B/v (10% centroids) | Posting lists | 12 pages | 10GB |

### 4.5 Theoretical Tradeoff: Graph vs Quantization

> **Theorem 3 (Graph-Quantization Lower Bound):** Under disaggregated memory with DRAM budget $B < n \cdot d \cdot 4$, any ANNS algorithm achieving recall $1-\delta$ must incur $\Omega(\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{\log(B/(m k^*))})$ SSD I/Os if using PQ, vs $\Omega(\frac{\log n}{\log M})$ hops if using HNSW, where $M$ is out-degree [1][5][6].

*Proof sketch*: reduction to noisy decision tree; graph hops correspond to edge expansion, PQ to codebook lookup depth. Formalization uses *volume argument* from [1] Thm 3.2 and PQ rate-distortion from [5] Eq. 12.

Practical rule: when $B / n < 16$ bytes/vector, quantization dominates (SPANN regime); when $B / n > 32$ bytes/vector, graph navigability dominates (DiskANN regime). Between 16-32 bytes, *hybrid* (Starling's hot-edge cache) Pareto-optimal.

### 5.3 Proof of I/O Optimality for Balanced Clustering

> **Theorem 4 (Balanced Posting Lists Minimize Tail Latency):** Given query distribution $Q$, let posting list lengths $\ell_i$ with $\sum \ell_i = n \cdot r$ ($r$ replication). For uniform random queries accessing $t$ lists, expected I/O $\mathbb{E}[IO] \propto \max_i \ell_i$ if lists accessed proportionally to size. Minimizing $\max_i \ell_i$ subject to $\sum \ell_i$ constant yields $\ell_i = n r / k$ (balanced). SPANN's hierarchical balanced $k$-means approximates this optimum within 1.15x [6].

*Proof*: by convexity and Lagrange multipliers; skew increases p99 because long lists cause head-of-line blocking on SSD.

## 6 Limitations

1. **Non-stationary embeddings**: All analyses assume static $\mathcal{X}$. In production, embeddings evolve as models update (e.g., Ada-002 $\to$ Ada-003). DiskANN's *Fresh-DiskANN* [4] supports inserts/deletes with 3.2x build slowdown but no formal guarantee on graph quality after 10% churn. SPANN's closure augmentation requires periodic re-clustering ($O(n \log n)$).

2. **Distance metric brittleness**: HNSW theory assumes metric satisfying triangle inequality; inner-product search violates this. Normalization tricks ($\|x\|=1$) mitigate but degrade hub formation [2]. PQ ADC error grows with anisotropic distributions (e.g., contrastive LLM embeddings with high $\ell_2$ norm variance).

3. **Disaggregated network tail**: Remote SSD via Azure Blob/NVMe-oF introduces 0.5-2ms extra per round-trip; DiskANN's $W=4$ parallel reads amplify tail if one replica straggles. No formal tail-tolerant beam search exists.

4. **Build cost**: Vamana construction for 1B vectors requires 64 machines $\times$ 12 hours (768 machine-hours) [4]. Carbon cost and $\approx$ $8k Azure cost per rebuild limit freshness.

5. **Recall definition**: Recall@k ignores ranking quality; *MRR* and *NDCG* may favor PQ (which preserves global distance) over graph (which preserves local connectivity). Evaluation should include ranking metrics.

6. **Hardware heterogeneity**: Results on `L32s_v3` NVMe may not transfer to `EBS io2` (AWS) with 64K IOPS cap; SPANN's sequential reads benefit more from high IOPS than DiskANN's random 4KB.

## 7 Conclusion

Graph navigability and code compression represent *dual* strategies for scaling ANNS under disaggregation: HNSW exploits *topology* (short paths via hubs), PQ exploits *algebra* (subspace factorization). Under tight DRAM budgets (<16B/v), quantization-first designs like SPANN achieve superior QPS-per-dollar by minimizing random SSD I/O through balanced inverted lists and query-aware pruning. Under moderate budgets (32-64B/v), DiskANN's PQ-guided graph traversal offers lower p99 latency and simpler deployment, while Starling's block reordering bridges the gap by caching hot edges.

The Hub Highway Hypothesis [2] suggests future work should *pin hubs* rather than layers, unifying HNSW and SPANN: treat hubs as centroids, maintain their posting lists as dense SSD pages, and use flat NSW for residual routing. Such a *hub-centric disaggregated index* would inherit SPANN's memory efficiency and HNSW's navigability, potentially achieving sub-millisecond p99 at 95% recall on billion-scale with 16GB DRAM.

Future directions include learned block reordering via GNNs, end-to-end differentiable quantization (e.g., VQ-VAE) replacing $k$-means, and co-design with RDMA-based disaggregated memory (CXL). The convergence of operational databases (Cosmos DB [3]) and specialized vector stores suggests the winning architecture will be *inside* the database, not beside it.
