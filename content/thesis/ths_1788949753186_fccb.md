# Closing the Verification Gap: Unifying TLA+ Model Checking, Linearizability Analysis, and Jepsen-Style Fault Injection for Distributed Consensus Correctness

## Abstract

Distributed consensus protocols such as Paxos and Raft are published alongside mechanically checked safety arguments, yet production systems built upon them routinely exhibit linearizability violations under faults. This thesis argues that the discrepancy is structural: *protocol-level verification, history-level checking, and system-level fault injection each target a different formal object*, and no single layer establishes end-to-end client-observable correctness. We develop a unified verification pipeline that closes this gap. At the base, TLA\+ specifications and model checking establish state-machine safety for consensus cores. In the middle, algorithmic linearizability checkers enforce the Herlihy–Wing condition on recorded execution histories, decomposing the NP-complete checking problem through P-compositionality. At the top, Jepsen-style fault injection adversarially completes the fault model that model checking necessarily abstracts. We analyze Raft's leader-lease and read-index optimizations — whose correctness depends on linearization points *outside* the model-checked envelope — present the LeaseGuard theorem proving linearizable reads over committed prefixes, and demonstrate how the etcd/Consul Jepsen analyses caught violations in systems whose consensus cores were formally verified. A composition theorem shows that the layered pipeline preserves linearizability across abstraction boundaries.

## 1 Introduction

Few results in distributed systems are as celebrated as the formal verification of consensus. The Raft specification [2] ships with a TLA\+ model and a machine-checked proof of the *Log Completeness Property*; IronFleet [3] extends this discipline to full implementations, proving both safety and liveness in Dafny. And yet the empirical record is sobering: the Jepsen project has documented linearizability violations in etcd, Consul, and numerous other systems — many of them sitting atop consensus protocols that were themselves formally verified [4][6].

This thesis contends that these outcomes are not contradictory but *complementary*, and that the contradiction dissolves once we recognize three distinct formal objects of verification:

1. **The protocol state machine** — a TLA\+ specification whose behaviors are checked against safety invariants (e.g., *State Machine Safety*, *Leader Completeness*).
2. **The execution history** — the record of client invocations and responses, judged linearizable or not against a sequential specification [1].
3. **The deployed system** — code, clocks, networks, and disks, whose behavior under faults must be elicited adversarially.

Each layer answers a question the others cannot ask. TLA\+ asks *does the protocol preserve agreement over all interleavings of an abstracted fault model?* Linearizability checking asks *does this concrete execution admit a legal sequential explanation?* Jepsen-style testing asks *does the system preserve its guarantees when the network itself is adversarial?* Our contribution is a **refinement-based pipeline** that composes these answers into a single end-to-end argument, and a precise characterization of where the layers' guarantees terminate — most notably, at the *linearization points* of read operations, which lie outside every published Raft TLA\+ specification.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Linearizability

Herlihy and Wing define correctness for concurrent objects through *histories*: sequences of invocation and response events [1]. A history $H$ is **linearizable** if there exists a legal sequential history $S$ such that:

- **L1 (equivalence):** $complete(H')$ is equivalent to $S$ for some extension $H'$ of $H$;
- **L2 (real-time order):** if operation $m_0$ precedes $m_1$ in $H$ (the response of $m_0$ precedes the invocation of $m_1$), then $m_0$ precedes $m_1$ in $S$.

> **Theorem: (Locality, Herlihy–Wing [1]).** A history $H$ is linearizable if and only if, for every object $x$, the subhistory $H|x$ is linearizable.

Locality is what makes linearizability the standard correctness condition for distributed key-value stores: each key can be reasoned about independently, and violations are attributable to specific operations. The checking problem — *does this history admit a linearization?* — is **NP-complete** in general [4], which motivates the algorithmic and decompositional techniques of Section 4.3.

### 2.2 Consensus and Replicated State Machines

Consensus protocols solve *agreement*: a set of replicas must converge on a single sequence of commands despite crash faults and asynchrony. Raft [2] achieves this through leader election and log replication with two decisive safety properties:

| Property | Statement |
|---|---|
| **Log Matching** | If two logs contain an entry with the same index and term, the logs are identical in all preceding entries. |
| **Leader Completeness** | If a log entry is committed in some term, it appears in the log of the leader of every higher-numbered term. |
| **State Machine Safety** | If a server has applied a log entry at index $i$ to its state machine, no other server applies a different command at $i$. |

The published Raft formal specification (roughly 400 lines of TLA\+) makes these properties mechanically precise and proves Log Completeness via the TLA\+ proof system [2]. Crucially, however, the specification abstracts *clients, clocks, leases, read paths, and retry logic* — precisely the machinery that determines whether the system is linearizable from the client's perspective.

### 2.3 TLA\+ and Model Checking

TLA\+ (Temporal Logic of Actions) specifies systems as state machines over untyped set theory; the TLC model checker exhaustively explores finite instances. Its strength is *protocol* verification: subtle interleavings of elections, replications, and crashes are explored mechanically, producing counterexample traces when invariants break. Its weakness is *finitude and abstraction*: TLC cannot model unbounded executions, real time, or the specification–implementation gap that IronFleet [3] closes with Dafny refinement proofs.

## 3 Methodology

We construct a three-layer verification pipeline in which each layer's output becomes the next layer's assumption, linked by refinement relations:

1. **Layer 1 — Protocol verification.** Write a TLA\+ specification of the consensus core; model-check safety invariants (Leader Completeness, State Machine Safety) with TLC; mechanize key proofs in TLAPS. The verified artifact is a *refinement contract*: any implementation behavior must refine the specification.
2. **Layer 2 — History verification.** Instrument the implementation to record invocation/response histories during tests. Check linearizability with a Porcupine-class checker [5], exploiting P-compositionality [4] to decompose histories by key and check them in parallel.
3. **Layer 3 — Adversarial fault injection.** Run the deployed system under a Jepsen-style nemesis [6] that injects partitions, clock skew, process kills, and disk faults while a workload generator records histories. The verified artifact is *absence of known violations under an adversarial fault schedule*.

```tla
------------------------------ MODULE RaftCore ------------------------------
EXTENDS Naturals, FiniteSets, Sequences

CONSTANTS Servers, Quorums
VARIABLES currentTerm, votedFor, log, commitIndex, state

TypeOK ==
  /\ currentTerm \in [Servers -> Nat]
  /\ state \in [Servers -> {"Follower", "Candidate", "Leader"}]
  /\ commitIndex \in [Servers -> Nat]

\* Safety invariant: no two leaders in the same term (Election Safety)
ElectionSafety ==
  \A s, t \in Servers :
    (state[s] = "Leader" /\ state[t] = "Leader"
     /\ currentTerm[s] = currentTerm[t]) => s = t

\* Safety invariant: State Machine Safety
StateMachineSafety ==
  \A s, t \in Servers, i \in Nat :
    (i <= commitIndex[s] /\ i <= commitIndex[t])
      => log[s][i] = log[t][i]
================================================================================
```

The pipeline is deliberately *heterogeneous*: each layer uses the formalism suited to its object (temporal logic for protocols, history-checking algorithms for executions, empirical adversarial testing for deployments), and refinement arguments compose their conclusions. The following sections develop each layer and then prove that their composition yields end-to-end linearizability.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 TLA\+ Specifications of Raft and the Boundary of the Model-Checked Envelope

The Raft TLA\+ specification models servers, terms, logs, and message passing at a level of abstraction that makes TLC exploration tractable. Within this envelope, the mechanically proven properties are *agreement* properties: they constrain the *order of committed commands*, not the *timing of client observations*.

Consider what the specification omits. Clients are absent: there is no notion of a write returning to a caller, of a retry after timeout, or of a read served from a leader's state machine. Leases are absent: the timed assumptions underlying lease-based reads cannot be expressed in untimed TLA\+. Deduplication is absent: the serial-number mechanism that makes retried writes idempotent (Section 8 of [2]) lives in implementation prose, not in the model. Each omission is individually defensible — abstraction is the price of tractable model checking — but collectively they define a **verification boundary**: everything outside the envelope is verified, if at all, by other means. The central claim of this thesis is that linearizability lives almost entirely outside that boundary.

### 4.2 From Consensus to Linearizability: The Linearization-Point Gap

State Machine Safety guarantees that all replicas apply the same commands in the same order. Linearizability demands more: every operation must *appear* to take effect atomically at some instant — its **linearization point** — between invocation and response. For writes in Raft, the linearization point is identifiable: the moment the entry commits on a majority. For *reads*, there is no such moment in the consensus core at all.

The canonical failure is the *stale read from a deposed leader*. A leader partitioned from its cluster retains its state machine but loses the ability to commit; if it serves reads from local state, a client can observe a value older than one returned by a completed write — a textbook linearizability violation, despite perfect State Machine Safety. The Raft dissertation proposes three remediations [2]:

- **Read via log:** route reads through the log as no-op entries (correct, adds a commit round-trip);
- **ReadIndex:** the leader confirms leadership with a heartbeat round to a quorum before serving the read;
- **Leases:** the leader assumes leadership for a bounded duration via clock-synchronized leases.

The LeaseGuard analysis [7] formalizes exactly what these mechanisms must guarantee:

> **Theorem: (Linearizable Reads over Committed Prefixes [7]).** Let $\mathcal{L}$ be the set of servers that are leader at some moment, and let $i_{\max}$ be the greatest commit index over $\mathcal{L}$. Any read $r$ observes the effects of all commands $c_i$ for all $i \le i_{\max}$, for the value of $i_{\max}$ at some moment between $r$'s invocation and acknowledgment.

The proof proceeds by case analysis over the read's leader relative to the committing leader, showing each case reduces to observing a committed prefix. The theorem's significance for our pipeline is architectural: it identifies the *linearization point* of a lease-guarded read as the instant the leader's commit index is confirmed current — an event involving *clocks*, which no untimed TLA\+ specification can capture. Verification must therefore move up the stack.

```rust
// ReadIndex: linearizable reads without log round-trips (sketch)
fn linearizable_read(&self, key: &Key) -> Result<Value, Error> {
    let read_index = self.confirm_leadership()?; // heartbeat quorum round
    // linearization point: instant quorum confirms this server is leader
    self.wait_applied(read_index)?;             // apply all <= read_index
    Ok(self.state_machine.get(key))             // read reflects committed prefix
}
```

### 4.3 Algorithmic Linearizability Checking: Taming NP-Completeness

Given a recorded history, deciding linearizability is NP-complete [4]: in the worst case the checker must search an exponential space of candidate linearizations. Practical checkers survive through *structure exploitation*. The key theoretical advance is **P-compositionality** [4]:

> **Theorem: (P-Compositionality [4]).** Histories can be partitioned along a *conflict relation* $P$ such that the history is linearizable iff every $P$-partition is linearizable, and each partition can be checked independently.

For key-value workloads, partitioning by key reduces an intractable monolithic check to many small ones — each a search over the operations touching a single key — which compose by the Locality theorem [1]. Porcupine [5] implements this strategy in Go with additional engineering: it encodes the check as a search with aggressive pruning, memoization over abstract states, and partition-level parallelism. A typical Jepsen run produces histories of $10^4$–$10^5$ operations; P-compositionality makes checking them routine.

### 4.4 Jepsen-Style Fault Injection as Adversarial Model Completion

Model checking explores the interleavings of an *abstracted* fault model: message loss, duplication, reordering, crashes. Real deployments fail through faults outside that abstraction — asymmetric partitions, clock jumps, disk corruption, and the insidious *gray* failures where a node is neither up nor down. Jepsen-style testing [6] closes this by treating the fault model as *empirical and adversarial*: a **nemesis** process injects faults on a schedule designed to break invariants, while a workload generator records the resulting history for linearizability checking.

The methodology's power is best illustrated by its canonical results. Kingsbury's analyses of etcd and Consul [6] demonstrated stale reads and lost updates in systems whose consensus substrates were well understood — the violations lived in *client interaction protocols, session handling, and read paths*, i.e., precisely the components outside the model-checked envelope identified in Section 4.1. The nemesis completes the fault model that TLC abstracts; the checker from Section 4.3 then judges the elicited histories with algorithmic rigor. Neither component suffices alone: fault injection without a formal checker reduces to eyeballing logs, and checking without adversarial fault injection judges only benign histories.

```haskell
-- Nemesis schedule: adversarial fault program (schematic)
data Fault = Partition [Node] [Node] | Kill Node | ClockSkew Node Seconds | DiskStall Node

nemesis :: Schedule -> IO ()
nemesis sched = forM_ sched $ \(t, fault) -> do
  threadDelay t
  inject fault        -- the fault model TLC cannot express
  threadDelay healT
  heal fault
```

### 4.5 Composition: The Layered Correctness Theorem

We now state the composition result that justifies the pipeline. Let $Impl$ be an implementation, $Spec$ its TLA\+ specification, $\mathcal{H}$ the set of histories elicited under a nemesis schedule $N$, and $\sqsubseteq$ the refinement relation.

> **Theorem: (Layered Linearizability).** Suppose (i) every behavior of $Impl$ refines $Spec$ ($Impl \sqsubseteq Spec$), (ii) $Spec$ implies State Machine Safety and Leader Completeness, (iii) the implementation's read path satisfies the LeaseGuard condition (every read observes a committed prefix current within its interval), and (iv) every history $h \in \mathcal{H}$ elicited under nemesis $N$ is judged linearizable by a sound checker. Then every $h \in \mathcal{H}$ is linearizable, and any violation of end-to-end linearizability in $Impl$ is attributable to a component outside (i)–(iii) — i.e., to client retry semantics, clock assumptions, or faults outside $N$'s schedule.

*Proof sketch.* By (i) and (ii), committed writes form a single total order consistent across replicas. By (iii) and the LeaseGuard theorem [7], each read linearizes at the instant its observed prefix is confirmed current, which lies within its invocation–response interval. Writes linearize at commit. The resulting per-operation linearization points induce a total order respecting real-time precedence (any operation completing before another begins linearizes earlier, since commit indices and confirmed prefixes are monotone), and the induced sequential history is legal for the register specification. Soundness of the checker (iv) then confirms that the elicited histories admit such orders; any unelicited violation must therefore arise from assumptions outside the verified envelope. ∎

The theorem's value is as much *diagnostic* as *certificatory*: it tells the engineer exactly where to look when a violation appears. A checker-reported stale read with a valid lease implies a clock fault outside the nemesis schedule; a violation under partitions implicates the read path's leadership confirmation.

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 What Each Layer Catches: A Comparative Record

| Layer | Formal object | Representative finding | Caught by other layers? |
|---|---|---|---|
| TLA\+ / TLC | Protocol interleavings | Log replication edge cases in Raft spec [2] | No — invisible to history checkers (no client model) |
| IronFleet/Dafny [3] | Implementation refinement | Packet encode/decode disagreements | Partially — Jepsen might never elicit the path |
| Porcupine/Knossos [4][5] | Recorded histories | Stale reads, lost updates under partitions | No — needs adversarial elicitation |
| Jepsen nemesis [6] | Deployed system under faults | etcd/Consul consistency violations [6] | No — TLC's fault model excludes gray failures |
| LeaseGuard proof [7] | Read-path linearization | Lease expiry races in Raft read optimization | No — outside all executable checkers |

### 5.2 Checker Performance Under P-Compositionality

The practical feasibility of Layer 2 rests on decomposition. Representative figures from the literature [4][5]:

- **Monolithic backtracking** on a 5,000-operation single-key history: timeout (>1h).
- **P-compositional checking** on a 50,000-operation multi-key history: minutes, scaling near-linearly with partition count.
- **Porcupine** on Jepsen-scale etcd histories ($\sim 10^5$ ops): seconds to minutes per partition set, dominated by the largest single-key partition.

The complexity remains exponential in the *worst case within a partition* — P-compositionality does not change the complexity class — but key-partitioned workloads exhibit small conflict windows in practice, which is why the approach succeeds empirically.

### 5.3 Proof Obligations Discharged

Within this thesis we have established: (a) the formal boundary of the model-checked envelope for Raft-style specifications (Section 4.1); (b) the reduction of linearizable reads to the LeaseGuard prefix condition with its case-analysis proof (Section 4.2, after [7]); (c) the soundness-and-decomposition argument for history checking via P-compositionality and Locality (Section 4.3, after [1][4]); and (d) the Layered Linearizability composition theorem with proof sketch (Section 4.5). Together these constitute a *complete argument skeleton*: each premise is either mechanically checked, algorithmically decided, or empirically tested.

## 6 Limitations

**Finitude of model checking.** TLC explores bounded instances; the step from "holds for 3 servers, 3 terms" to "holds generally" rests on the TLAPS proof or on small-scope hypotheses. Neither is a substitute for unbounded verification, and both leave the implementation's read path unmodeled.

**Incompleteness of history checking.** A checker judges only elicited histories. The nemesis schedule is necessarily finite and heuristic; absence of violations under schedule $N$ says nothing about faults outside $N$. Gray failures — the most dangerous class — are precisely those hardest to schedule.

**Clock dependence.** Lease-based linearizable reads assume bounded clock drift, an assumption no model checker, history checker, or fault injector can *verify* — only monitor.

**Refinement gap.** IronFleet-style proofs [3] close the specification–implementation gap but at substantial proof cost (tens of thousands of lines of Dafny for a key-value store). Most production systems will never pay it, which is why Layers 2 and 3 — testing, not proof — remain the pragmatic backstop.

**Client semantics.** Linearizability is defined over client-observable histories. Retry storms, duplicate suppression, and session semantics live in client libraries that the pipeline treats as trusted; a correct server with a broken client SDK is still observably non-linearizable.

---

## 7 Conclusion

Formal verification of consensus has sometimes been presented — by its enthusiasts and its skeptics alike — as settling the correctness question for replicated systems. It does not. What TLA\+ specifications and their proofs settle is *agreement*: that replicas converge on one order of commands. *Linearizability* — the property clients actually observe — depends on linearization points that live in read paths, leases, clocks, and retry logic, outside every model-checked envelope. This thesis has shown that the gap is closable, not by a single grand formalism, but by a disciplined composition: protocol verification establishes the ordered log, algorithmic history checking enforces linearizability on elicited executions, adversarial fault injection elicits the executions worth checking, and refinement arguments — mechanized where economical, as in IronFleet [3] — bind the layers together. The Layered Linearizability theorem makes the composition precise and, equally importantly, makes its *residue* explicit: whatever the pipeline cannot see is exactly the set of assumptions the next bug will exploit. The history of Jepsen analyses [6] suggests that treating that residue as a first-class verification artifact — scheduled, monitored, and re-examined — is the difference between a verified protocol and a correct system.
