# Fiat-Shamir With Aborts and Trapdoor Sampling: CRYSTALS-Dilithium, Falcon, and the Module-LWE Security Foundation

## Abstract

Post-quantum standardization has converged on lattice-based signatures as the workhorse of future authentication infrastructure. This paper studies the two lattice signature families selected by NIST — CRYSTALS-Dilithium, standardized as ML-DSA in FIPS 204, and Falcon, standardized as FN-DSA in FIPS 206 — through the lens of their shared module-lattice foundation and their sharply divergent signing paradigms. Dilithium instantiates Fiat–Shamir with aborts over module lattices, replacing trapdoor sampling with rejection sampling over uniform masks and grounding security in the Module-LWE and Module-SIS problems. Falcon instantiates the Gentry–Peikert–Vaikuntanathan hash-and-sign framework over NTRU lattices, drawing discrete Gaussian preimages with a secret trapdoor basis via the Ducas–Prest fast Fourier sampler on an LDL decomposition tree. We compare the schemes on hardness assumptions, sampler machinery, sizes, performance, and implementability, and situate both within the module-LWE hardness landscape stretching from Regev's worst-case reductions through ring and module variants. The analysis clarifies when ML-DSA's simplicity and constant-time friendliness outweigh FN-DSA's compactness — and why floating-point Gaussian sampling remains the central implementation risk of the latter.

##1 Intro

Shor's algorithm renders RSA, Diffie–Hellman, and elliptic-curve signatures vulnerable to a sufficiently large quantum computer, and the migration of authentication infrastructure cannot wait for such a machine to exist: adversaries can harvest ciphertexts and signed artifacts today and exploit them later. NIST's post-quantum cryptography standardization, launched in 2016, therefore selected not one but two lattice-based signature schemes for complementary roles. The primary standard is CRYSTALS-Dilithium, published as the Module-Lattice-Based Digital Signature Algorithm (ML-DSA) in FIPS 204 [3], derived from the design of Ducas, Kiltz, Lepoint, Lyubashevsky, Schwabe, Seiler, and Stehlé [1]. The compact alternative is Falcon, published as FN-DSA in FIPS 206, built by Fouque, Hoffstein, Kirchner, Lyubashevsky, Pornin, Prest, Ricosset, Seiler, Whyte, and Zhang over NTRU lattices [2].

What makes this pairing instructive is that the two schemes agree on the *hardness* foundation — module-structured lattice problems — yet disagree completely on the *signing* mechanism. Dilithium is a Schnorr-like Fiat–Shamir signature with aborts: the signer masks the secret with a uniformly random polynomial, hashes to obtain a sparse challenge, and restarts the signing attempt whenever the candidate response would leak secret information [5]. No trapdoor is ever used, and no Gaussian sampling is required. Falcon follows the older hash-and-sign lineage of Gentry, Peikert, and Vaikuntanathan: the signer hashes the message to a target point and uses a short secret basis of an NTRU lattice to sample a nearby lattice point from a discrete Gaussian distribution, a procedure that demands high-precision floating-point arithmetic [2].

This paper gives a unified account of both designs. Section 2 recalls the lattice problems and signature paradigms on which they rest. Section 3 describes our comparative methodology. Section 4 analyzes Dilithium's abort-based Fiat–Shamir construction, Falcon's NTRU trapdoor sampler, the module-LWE hardness landscape underwriting both, and the sampler design space that separates them. Section 5 states the security theorems and compares concrete parameters and sizes. Section 6 discusses limitations — side channels, structural assumptions, and proof tightness — and Section 7 concludes with guidance on when each standard is the right choice.

##2 Background

A *lattice* is a discrete additive subgroup of Euclidean space, usually presented as the integer span of a basis. Its algorithmic hardness underpins two families of average-case problems. The **Short Integer Solution (SIS)** problem, introduced by Ajtai, asks for a short nonzero vector in the kernel of a random matrix; it yields collision-resistant hashing and one-way functions. The **Learning With Errors (LWE)** problem, introduced by Regev, asks to distinguish noisy linear equations from uniform ones, and admits a quantum reduction from worst-case approximate shortest-vector problems on general lattices to average-case LWE [4]. This worst-case-to-average-case connection is the crown jewel of lattice cryptography: breaking a random instance is as hard as approximating short vectors in *every* lattice of the class.

Structured variants trade some generality for dramatic efficiency. **Ring-LWE** (Lyubashevsky, Peikert, Regev) works over the ring of integers of a cyclotomic number field, with hardness reduced from worst-case problems on ideal lattices [4]. **Module-LWE** and **Module-SIS** generalize further to modules of rank greater than one over such rings — introduced in the BGV line of work and given worst-case hardness foundations by Langlois and Stehlé — occupying a middle ground: Ring-LWE is the rank-one case, plain LWE the large-rank unstructured case, and module variants let designers tune the rank as a security–efficiency dial [6].

Two signature paradigms dominate lattice constructions. The **Fiat–Shamir** paradigm converts a three-move identification protocol into a signature by replacing the verifier's challenge with a hash of the commitment and message. For lattices, the naive analogue leaks the secret through the response distribution, so Lyubashevsky introduced **Fiat–Shamir with aborts**: the signer draws the masking randomness from a wide enough distribution and *restarts* whenever the response would fall outside a safe region, making the output statistically independent of the secret [5][7]. The **hash-and-sign** paradigm of Gentry, Peikert, and Vaikuntanathan instead equips the signer with a trapdoor — a short basis of the lattice — hashes the message to a target, and samples a lattice vector close to the target from a discrete Gaussian, so that signatures reveal nothing about the trapdoor. NTRU lattices, defined by Hoffstein, Pipher, and Silverman from the relation *h = g · f⁻¹ mod q*, provide the compact structured setting in which Falcon instantiates this framework [2].

##3 Methodology

This study is a comparative design analysis rather than a new construction or implementation effort. We evaluate Dilithium and Falcon along four axes:

1. **Hardness assumptions** — which module-lattice problems each scheme reduces to, and the quality of the worst-case-to-average-case reductions behind them.
2. **Sampler machinery** — how each scheme produces signature randomness without leaking the secret key: uniform rejection sampling versus discrete Gaussian trapdoor sampling.
3. **Concrete costs** — public-key and signature sizes, computational profile, and arithmetic requirements (integer NTT versus floating-point FFT) as specified in the primary sources.
4. **Implementability** — constant-time feasibility, side-channel surface, and determinism.

Security is discussed in the standard notions: strong existential unforgeability under chosen-message attack (SUF-CMA), proved in the (quantum) random-oracle model. All parameter figures are taken from the scheme specifications and the FIPS documents [1][2][3]; we perform no independent benchmarking, so the "empirical" content of Section 5 consists of the published parameter sets and the security theorems that relate them to the underlying problems. Notation follows the literature: *R_q* denotes the ring of integers modulo *q* in the 256th or 512th cyclotomic quotient, *S_η* the set of polynomials with coefficients bounded by *η*, and *B_τ* the set of polynomials with exactly *τ* nonzero coefficients in {−1, +1}.

##4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 CRYSTALS-Dilithium: Fiat–Shamir With Aborts Over Module Lattices

Dilithium is, in the authors' own description, a "Schnorr-like" lattice signature [1]. Key generation samples a random public matrix *A* over *R_q* (from a short seed), short secret vectors *s_1*, *s_2* with coefficients in *S_η*, and publishes *t = A s_1 + s_2* — a Module-LWE instance. Signing proceeds as follows: sample a uniform mask *y* with coefficients between −(γ_1 − 1) and γ_1 − 1; compute *w = A y* and its high bits *w_1*; hash to a sparse challenge *c* in *B_τ*; form the candidate response *z = y + c s_1*; then **abort** — discard *z* and restart — if the infinity norm of *z* reaches γ_1 − β, or if the low bits of *A y − c s_2* are too large. The surviving *z*, together with a small *hint* vector that lets the verifier recover the high bits despite the compression of *t*, forms the signature [1][3].

The abort step is the entire security trick. Because *y* is uniform over a box much wider than the shift *c s_1*, conditioning on acceptance makes *z* uniform over a box independent of the secret — rejection sampling as a leakage firewall [5][7]. Two further design choices matter. First, Dilithium samples *uniformly*, never from a discrete Gaussian, so implementations need no constant-time Gaussian sampler and no floating-point arithmetic — a deliberate departure from earlier Fiat–Shamir lattice signatures [1]. Second, signing is *deterministic*: the mask *y* is derived from a secret seed and the message, following Kiltz, Lyubashevsky, and Seiler's observation that reusing masks across identical messages tightens the reduction in the quantum random-oracle model [1].

Security rests on two module problems: Module-LWE (recovering *s_1* from *t*) and a self-target variant of Module-SIS (forging a valid *(z, c, h)* triple implies finding a short preimage relation). The scheme operates over *R_q* with *n = 256* and *q = 8,380,417 = 2^23 − 2^13 + 1*, a prime with *q ≡ 1 mod 512* chosen so that the Number Theoretic Transform gives fast polynomial multiplication, and security is scaled simply by the module dimensions *(k, ℓ)* [1][3].

### 4.2 Falcon: Hash-Then-Sign Over NTRU Lattices

Falcon instantiates the GPV hash-and-sign framework over NTRU lattices. Key generation finds small polynomials *f, g, F, G* in *R = Z[x]/(x^n + 1)* satisfying the **NTRU equation** *fG − gF = q mod φ*, where *φ = x^n + 1*. The public key is *h = g · f^{−1} mod q*; the secret key is the short basis formed by *(f, g)* and *(F, G)*, which generates the same lattice as the "long" public basis but enables trapdoor sampling [2].

Signing hashes the message and a random salt to a target polynomial *c*, then uses the secret basis to sample a pair *(s_1, s_2)* from a discrete Gaussian over the lattice coset *s_1 + s_2 h = c*, outputting the salt and the short *s_2* (the verifier reconstructs *s_1*). The sampler is the heart of Falcon: a randomized, ring-adapted variant of Babai's nearest-plane algorithm due to Ducas and Prest, whose recursion mirrors the Fast Fourier Transform — hence "fast Fourier sampling." It operates on the LDL decomposition of the Gram matrix of the secret basis in the Fourier domain, walking a binary **Falcon tree** from root to leaves and drawing one-dimensional discrete Gaussians at each node [2].

Two numerical constraints govern correctness and security. The secret basis must satisfy a Gram–Schmidt norm bound (below 1.17√q in the FIPS 206 profile), which guarantees that the LDL tree leaves carry enough precision for the sampler to hit the target distribution. The Gaussian width σ must be large enough that samples leak nothing about the secret basis — the analysis of Prest shows σ bounded by a small multiple of the Gram–Schmidt norm suffices for the NIST query bounds — yet small enough to keep signatures short [2]. All of this runs in floating point: key generation, signing, and the LDL decomposition need doubles, which is precisely what makes Falcon compact and precisely what makes it hard to implement in constant time.

### 4.3 The Module-LWE Landscape: A Tunable Hardness Dial

Dilithium and Falcon both live in the module-lattice world, but at different points of it. It helps to see the full spectrum of structured LWE assumptions [6]:

| Assumption | Algebraic object | Key-size behavior | Used by |
|---|---|---|---|
| Plain LWE | *Z_q^n*, no structure | Quadratic in dimension | Early schemes, FHE |
| Module-LWE | Rank-*k* module over *R_q* | Linear in rank | **Dilithium / ML-DSA** |
| Ring-LWE | Rank-1 module (ideal) | Compact | Early ideal-lattice schemes |
| NTRU | Rank-2 module with extra relation | Very compact | **Falcon / FN-DSA** |

The hardness story is a chain of reductions. Regev gave a quantum reduction from worst-case GapSVP to average-case LWE; Peikert and others later gave classical variants under stronger parameter regimes. Lyubashevsky, Peikert, and Regev extended the paradigm to rings, reducing worst-case ideal-lattice problems to Ring-LWE [4]. Langlois and Stehlé then proved worst-case-to-average-case reductions for *module* lattices, so Module-LWE and Module-SIS inherit the same style of guarantee with the module rank as a free parameter [6]. Dilithium exploits this directly: its module dimensions *(k, ℓ)* = (4,4), (6,5), (8,7) select NIST levels 2, 3, and 5 while the ring stays fixed, so one optimized NTT implementation serves all levels [1][3].

Falcon's assumption is narrower. The NTRU lattice is a rank-2 module lattice with the additional structure of the NTRU equation, and its hardness is less completely reduced to worst-case problems than Module-LWE is — cryptanalysis targets the NTRU problem and the Ring-SIS analogue directly. In practice this is a conservative-but-comfortable position: NTRU has survived since 1998, and Falcon's parameters (*n* = 512 or 1024, *q* = 12289) are set against the best known lattice attacks with wide margins [2]. The philosophical difference is nevertheless real: Dilithium's security story is "break my scheme and you solve Module-LWE/SIS," while Falcon's is "break my scheme and you solve the NTRU problem that has resisted attack for decades."

### 4.4 Samplers: Uniform Rejection Versus Discrete Gaussian

The deepest engineering divide between the schemes is how they randomize.

Falcon's Gaussian sampler produces shorter signatures because a discrete Gaussian concentrates mass near zero, so the response *s* is as short as information-theoretically possible for the required security.

##5 Empirical/Proofs

The security claims of both schemes are theorems, not heuristics. We state their informal content; full proofs appear in the cited works.

> **Theorem (Fiat–Shamir with aborts, informal [1][5]).** If Module-LWE and (self-target) Module-SIS are hard for the chosen parameters, then the Dilithium signing construction is strongly existentially unforgeable under chosen-message attack in the (quantum) random-oracle model. Every forgery yields either an MLWE distinguisher or a short solution to the MSIS instance defined by the public key.

> **Theorem (GPV hash-and-sign over NTRU, informal [2]).** If the NTRU problem and Ring-SIS are hard, and the Ducas–Prest sampler outputs are statistically close to the ideal discrete Gaussian over the coset, then Falcon is strongly existentially unforgeable under chosen-message attack in the random-oracle model. Signatures are distributed independently of the trapdoor basis.

The concrete parameter sets, taken from the specifications, make the trade-offs tangible [1][2][3]:

| Scheme | NIST level | Public key (bytes) | Signature (bytes) | Arithmetic |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| ML-DSA-44 | 2 | 1,312 | 2,420 | Integer NTT |
| ML-DSA-65 | 3 | 1,952 | 3,293 | Integer NTT |
| ML-DSA-87 | 5 | 2,592 | 4,595 | Integer NTT |
| Falcon-512 | 1 | 897 | 666 | Floating-point FFT |
| Falcon-1024 | 5 | 1,793 | 1,280 | Floating-point FFT |

At comparable strength, Falcon-512 signatures (666 bytes) are roughly one quarter the size of ML-DSA-44 signatures (2,420 bytes), while ML-DSA avoids floating point entirely and signs and verifies in well under a millisecond on modern CPUs with simple constant-time code [1][2][9]. The following toy model illustrates the rejection-sampling economics that Dilithium relies on:

```python
import random, math

def trial_acceptance(gamma1: int, beta: int, dim: int, trials: int = 200000) -> float:
    """Estimate P(||z||_inf < gamma1 - beta) for z = y + shift,
    y uniform in [-(gamma1-1), gamma1-1], shift bounded by beta."""
    width = 2 * gamma1 - 1
    safe = 2 * (gamma1 - beta) - 1
    # per-coordinate acceptance ~ safe/width; independence across dim
    p = (safe / width) ** dim
    ok = sum(1 for _ in range(trials)
             if all(random.random() < safe / width for _ in range(dim)))
    return ok / trials

for gamma1, beta in [(131072, 78), (524288, 196)]:
    print(gamma1, beta, round(trial_acceptance(gamma1, beta, 8), 3))
```

The simulation confirms the design intuition: widening γ_1 relative to the shift bound β drives the per-attempt acceptance probability toward one, at the direct cost of larger *z* and hence larger signatures — the quantitative form of the compactness-versus-simplicity trade [1][7].

```haskell
-- NTRU key relation, declarative view (Falcon key generation target)
-- Find small f, g, F, G in R = Z[x]/(x^n + 1) such that:
--   f * G - g * F == q        (NTRU equation, mod x^n + 1)
--   h = g * invMod f  (mod q) (public key)
-- Security needs: ||(f,g)|| small, Gram-Schmidt norm of basis < 1.17 * sqrt q
ntruKeyRelation :: (Poly, Poly, Poly, Poly) -> Integer -> Poly -> Bool
ntruKeyRelation (f, g, bigF, bigG) q phi =
  polyMod (f .* bigG .-. g .* bigF) phi == constPoly q
```

Deployment evidence aligns with the analysis: ML-DSA is positioned as the default general-purpose signature for firmware, software, and PKI use [3][8], while Falcon's compactness makes it attractive wherever transmitted bytes are the binding constraint — certificate chains, embedded update channels, and bandwidth-sensitive handshakes — provided the implementer can rely on a constant-time, audited floating-point pipeline [2][9].

##6 Limitations

Neither scheme is free of caveats, and a PhD-level treatment should name them plainly.

**Falcon's floating point is a genuine liability.** The security proof requires the sampler's output distribution to be statistically close to the ideal Gaussian; the implementation approximates this with double-precision FFTs and an LDL tree whose leaves must carry enough precision. Validating that approximation, and making it constant-time across platforms with different floating-point units, is far harder than validating Dilithium's integer code — and Falcon is unusable on targets without an FPU [2]. Side-channel history here is unforgiving: power and timing analysis of Gaussian samplers has repeatedly recovered secrets in lattice implementations, which is why the FIPS 206 profile pins down the Gram–Schmidt bound and sampler precision explicitly.

**Dilithium pays in bandwidth.** Signatures of 2.4 to 4.6 kB are large next to Falcon's 666 bytes or ECDSA's 64 bytes; for protocols that transmit many signatures (blockchains, large certificate chains), this is a real cost [1][3]. Deterministic signing, while good for tight proofs, concentrates risk on the secret seed: a fault attack that perturbs one signing run can, in principle, leak the mask and hence the secret — randomized variants exist but loosen the QROM reduction [1].

**Structural assumptions deserve humility.** Module-LWE enjoys worst-case reductions, but the *module* and *ideal* structure is still more algebraic than plain LWE, and the history of lattice cryptanalysis includes surprises for highly structured rings. Falcon's NTRU assumption is older but less reducible. Neither concern is actionable today, but both counsel crypto-agility: standards, not eternal truths.

**Proof tightness is imperfect.** The Fiat–Shamir-with-aborts reduction loses factors related to the number of signing queries and the abort probability; concrete parameters are therefore set conservatively against the best known attacks rather than derived tightly from the theorems [1][5].

##7 Conclusion

Dilithium and Falcon represent the two mature answers to the question of how to sign with lattices. Dilithium — ML-DSA — shows how far the Fiat–Shamir-with-aborts idea can be pushed: no trapdoors, no Gaussians, no floating point, just uniform masks, sparse challenges, and the discipline to restart when a candidate response might leak. Its security reduces cleanly to Module-LWE and Module-SIS, and its integer-only, constant-time-friendly implementation profile makes it the right default for general-purpose post-quantum authentication [1][3]. Falcon — FN-DSA — shows the payoff of the older hash-and-sign lineage refined to its limit: an NTRU trapdoor, the Ducas–Prest fast Fourier sampler, and signatures under 700 bytes at NIST level 1, at the cost of a floating-point pipeline that demands expert implementation [2].

The module-LWE landscape explains why both can coexist: module rank is a dial, and the two schemes simply sit at different settings of the structure–compactness trade, with Dilithium at the reducible, integer-arithmetic end and Falcon at the compact, trapdoor-sampling end [4][6]. For practitioners the guidance is simple: choose ML-DSA unless transmitted signature size is the binding constraint, in which case choose FN-DSA only with a vetted constant-time implementation. For researchers the open problems are sharper proofs with less query loss, float-free compact samplers in the spirit of recent follow-ups, and continued cryptanalysis of module-structured assumptions — the quiet work on which the post-quantum transition depends.
