# Post-Quantum Secure Boot at Firmware Scale: ML-DSA and SLH-DSA Signatures in UEFI Secure Boot, Quantum-Resistant TPM 2.0 Measured Boot, SPDM 1.x Device Attestation, DICE Per-Device Identities, and Hybrid Certificate Chains for BMC and Silicon Roots of Trust

## Abstract

The advent of cryptographically relevant quantum computers threatens the classical signature primitives — RSA-2048 and ECDSA over P-256 — that anchor nearly every contemporary firmware root of trust, from UEFI Secure Boot signature databases to TPM 2.0 endorsement hierarchies and baseboard management controller (BMC) update manifests. This thesis develops a complete post-quantum (PQ) migration architecture for platform firmware security, integrating NIST-standardized ML-DSA (FIPS 204) and SLH-DSA (FIPS 205) signatures into UEFI Secure Boot variable authentication, TPM 2.0 measured-boot PCR extend chains, DMTF SPDM 1.x device attestation, and TCG DICE layered identities. We argue that no single PQ primitive dominates all firmware roles: ML-DSA provides the verification efficiency demanded by resource-constrained BMCs and pre-memory DXE phases, while SLH-DSA's minimal trust assumptions make it the superior choice for long-lived root keys and offline code-signing HSMs. We formalize a hybrid classical-plus-PQ certificate model, quantify verification overhead on embedded targets, and derive concrete engineering rules for PQ-signed firmware update manifests, quantum-resistant PCR banks, and SPDM challenge-response under adversarial quantum models.

## 1. Introduction

Platform firmware is the highest-leverage attack surface in modern computing: it executes before the operating system, persists across reinstalls, and controls the very mechanisms — Secure Boot, measured boot, device attestation — by which higher software layers establish trust. Every one of these mechanisms currently rests on a fragile assumption: that factoring integers and computing discrete logarithms remain intractable. Shor's algorithm refutes that assumption for any adversary with a sufficiently large fault-tolerant quantum computer, and the *harvest-now, decrypt-later* doctrine applies with particular force to firmware: a signed UEFI bootloader captured today can be forged tomorrow, and a device fielded for fifteen years in an industrial controller cannot be retroactively re-secured once its root keys fall.

The standardization of post-quantum signatures by NIST in August 2024 — ML-DSA (FIPS 204), derived from CRYSTALS-Dilithium, and SLH-DSA (FIPS 205), derived from SPHINCS+ — converts this from a research problem into an engineering migration with a hard deadline. NIST IR 8547 lays out deprecation timelines for classical algorithms, and firmware is the *hardest* software to migrate: verification code runs in kilobytes of SRAM before DRAM initialization, signature databases live in byte-constrained authenticated NVRAM variables, and verification latency directly extends platform boot time [5].

This thesis answers the central question: **how do we reconstruct the firmware chain of trust — from silicon root of trust (RoT) through UEFI, measured boot, device attestation, and over-the-air update — on post-quantum foundations, without breaking the installed base and without exceeding the brutal resource budgets of embedded firmware?** Our contributions are:

1. A **role-differentiated primitive selection framework** assigning ML-DSA, SLH-DSA, and stateful hash-based schemes (LMS/XMSS per SP 800-208) to distinct firmware roles — online verification, offline root signing, constrained attestation — based on measured cost asymmetries.
2. A **hybrid X.509 certificate architecture** for UEFI `db`/`KEK`/`PK` and SPDM device identity binding classical and PQ keys, with a formal unforgeability argument under a combined classical-plus-quantum adversary.
3. A **quantum-resistant measured-boot design** for TPM 2.0 PCR extend chains, including SHA-384/SHA-512 PCR banks and event-log binding.
4. An **SPDM 1.x integration analysis** covering PQC algorithm negotiation, `CHALLENGE`/`GET_MEASUREMENTS` message sizing under kilobyte-scale signatures, and composition with DICE-derived identities.
5. A **performance model** for ML-DSA and SLH-DSA verification on BMC-class processors, yielding quantitative boot-time budgets.

> **Thesis claim:** A secure post-quantum firmware ecosystem is achievable with current standards, but only through deliberate *heterogeneity* — matching each signature primitive to the firmware role whose constraints it fits — and through hybrid certificates that let the ecosystem migrate without a flag day.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 The classical firmware trust stack

Modern platform trust is a layered construction standardized across three industry bodies. The **UEFI Specification** [6] defines Secure Boot: a platform key (PK) authorizes key-exchange keys (KEKs), which authorize signatures in the databases `db` (allowed) and `dbx` (forbidden). Bootloaders, drivers, and option ROMs carry Authenticode signatures verified against `db` during the Driver Execution Environment (DXE) phase. Critically, these databases live in *authenticated variables* whose update payloads are themselves signed — so the algorithm agility of the entire Secure Boot edifice is bounded by what the variable driver can verify pre-boot.

The **TPM 2.0 Library** [7] provides measured boot: each firmware component is hashed into Platform Configuration Registers via

$$\text{PCR}_{new} = H(\text{PCR}_{old} \parallel \text{digest})$$

and PCR values are signed into attestation *quotes* by attestation keys certified under the endorsement key. Security therefore depends on (a) collision resistance of the extend hash and (b) unforgeability of the attestation-key signature — classically RSA or ECDSA, both quantum-broken.

**NIST SP 800-193** [4] adds the resiliency triad — *protection, detection, recovery* — requiring a Root of Trust for Update (RTU) that verifies firmware update manifests before staging, plus a recovery image. The RTU's manifest signature is the most security-critical and longest-lived signature on the platform.

### 2.2 The post-quantum signature landscape

| Scheme | Standard | Public key | Signature | Verify cost (relative) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| ML-DSA-44 | FIPS 204 | 1,312 B | 2,420 B | ~1× (fastest) |
| ML-DSA-65 | FIPS 204 | 1,952 B | 3,309 B | ~1.3× |
| ML-DSA-87 | FIPS 204 | 2,592 B | 4,627 B | ~1.8× |
| SLH-DSA-128s | FIPS 205 | 32 B | 7,856 B | ~20–50× |
| SLH-DSA-128f | FIPS 205 | 32 B | 17,088 B | ~8–15× |
| SLH-DSA-256s | FIPS 205 | 64 B | 29,792 B | ~40–80× |

*Table 1: Standardized PQ signature parameters. Sizes exact per FIPS 204/205 [1][2]; verify costs are order-of-magnitude ratios on embedded reference implementations.*

Three observations drive our architecture. First, **ML-DSA verification is fast but keys and signatures are large**: a 3,309-byte ML-DSA-65 signature dwarfs the 64-byte ECDSA signatures UEFI authenticated-variable payloads were designed around, with direct consequences for NVRAM budgets. Second, **SLH-DSA has tiny keys but slow, large signatures**: its security rests only on the underlying hash function — the most conservative assumption available — suiting it to *rarely verified, extremely long-lived* signatures such as root CA keys. Third, **stateful** hash-based signatures (LMS, XMSS), recommended by SP 800-208 [3], offer small signatures and fast verification but demand rigorous state management — feasible in an offline HSM, dangerous in a field device.

### 2.3 DICE and SPDM: identity below and beside the host

The TCG's **DICE** architecture [8] gives peripherals and BMCs without TPMs a layered identity: each boot layer derives a Compound Device Identifier (CDI) from a Unique Device Secret and the layer's measurement, then derives attestation keys per layer. DICE is inherently layered — exactly the structure PQ migration needs, since each layer's key can migrate algorithms independently.

**SPDM** (DMTF DSP0274) [9] is the wire protocol for host-to-device verification: `GET_CAPABILITIES`, `NEGOTIATE_ALGORITHMS`, `GET_CERTIFICATE`, `CHALLENGE`, `GET_MEASUREMENTS`, and session-based secured messages. SPDM 1.3's negotiation is extensible to PQC algorithms. The challenge is message sizing: `CHALLENGE_AUTH` responses carrying 4.6 KB ML-DSA-87 signatures strain the buffering assumptions of microcontroller SPDM responders.

## 3. Methodology

Our method combines **standards analysis**, **adversarial modeling**, **analytical performance modeling**, and **constructive protocol design**.

**Standards analysis.** We trace every classical signature touchpoint in the boot path: UEFI §32 (Secure Boot, authenticated variables) [6], TPM 2.0 structures (`TPMT_SIGNATURE`, `TPM_ALG_*`) [7], SP 800-193's RTU requirements [4], DICE CDI derivation [8], and SPDM 1.x message flows [9] — recording size, latency, and algorithm-agility constraints at each.

**Adversarial model.** We consider a *hybrid adversary* $\mathcal{A} = (\mathcal{A}_{cl}, \mathcal{A}_{q})$: $\mathcal{A}_{cl}$ classical PPT (today's attackers, the transition period), $\mathcal{A}_{q}$ quantum polynomial-time (the post-quantum horizon). The adversary controls the network, supplies malicious firmware images and SPDM responders, and — in the strongest variant — obtains a cryptographically relevant quantum computer at a fixed future date $T_q$. Goals: *unforgeability of boot authorization* and *attestation soundness*.

**Performance modeling.** We build an analytical model from published reference-implementation cycle counts for Cortex-M4/A-class cores typical of BMC service processors (400 MHz–1 GHz, 256 KB–2 MB SRAM). Absolute numbers are illustrative; architectural conclusions depend only on order-of-magnitude ratios, which are robust.

**Constructive design.** For each subsystem we propose concrete data structures, certificate profiles, and protocol modifications, arguing security by reduction to the primitives plus the hybrid composition theorem of §4.2.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Role-Differentiated Primitive Selection

Firmware uses signatures in radically different roles, and the PQ schemes' cost profiles are radically asymmetric across them. A single-algorithm migration — "replace ECDSA with Dilithium everywhere" — is both wasteful and, in some roles, infeasible. We identify five roles:

1. **Offline root signing** (silicon RoT fuse keys, UEFI PK issuance, RTU manifests signed in the vendor HSM). *Verified rarely, trusted for decades.* **Recommended: SLH-DSA** (hash-only assumptions) or stateful LMS/XMSS under strict HSM state discipline [3]. Signing speed is irrelevant in an HSM; the 30 KB signature is stored once in a manifest.
2. **Online boot verification** (DXE drivers verifying Authenticode signatures, RTU verifying manifests, BMC verifying staged images). *Verified every boot/update, latency-critical.* **Recommended: ML-DSA-65** — verification in the low millions of cycles (~2–10 ms on BMC-class cores) versus hundreds of milliseconds for SLH-DSA.
3. **Attestation quoting** (TPM AK signing quotes, DICE layer keys). *Signed on-device, verified remotely.* Here the asymmetry reverses: **signing** cost matters on-device, and SLH-DSA signing on microcontrollers takes seconds to minutes. **Recommended: ML-DSA for AKs.**
4. **Device identity certificates** (SPDM chains, DICE X.509 leaves). *Transmitted over slow buses, parsed by constrained responders.* Certificate size dominates. **Recommended: hybrid chains (§4.2) with ML-DSA-44 leaves**, SLH-DSA roots kept offline.
5. **Measured-boot hash chains** (TPM PCR extend). Not signatures — but Grover gives quadratic preimage speedup and Brassard–Høyer–Tapp gives *cubic-root* quantum collision search, so a 256-bit hash offers only ~85 bits of quantum collision resistance. **Recommended: SHA-384/SHA-512 PCR banks** (128-bit quantum collision resistance for SHA-384), with algorithm agility recorded in the event log.

> **Theorem (Role-optimality sketch):** Under a cost model constraining verification latency on-device, signature size on-wire/in-NVRAM, and trust lifetime at roots, no single standardized PQ signature scheme simultaneously minimizes all three costs.

*Proof sketch.* ML-DSA minimizes verification latency but not size; SLH-DSA minimizes key size and assumption strength but maximizes latency and signature size; LMS/XMSS minimize size and latency but impose statefulness incompatible with unattended field devices. The three minima are attained by distinct schemes. ∎

### 4.2 Hybrid Classical-plus-PQ Certificate Chains

A flag-day migration is operationally impossible: fielded devices cannot be recalled to update fused trust anchors. The answer is **hybrid certificates** carrying both a classical and a PQ key, valid only when *both* signatures verify.

- Each certificate binds $(pk_{classical}, pk_{PQ})$ under a hybrid issuer signature $\sigma = (\sigma_{cl}, \sigma_{PQ})$ over a TBSCertificate containing both keys plus a binding extension marking the pair atomic.
- Verification is *dual-verification*: accept iff both verify. Downgrade to classical-only requires an explicit, auditable policy bit set by the platform owner — never silent.
- For UEFI, hybrid entries enroll in `db` as `EFI_CERT_X509_GUID` entries chaining to a hybrid self-signed root; `dbx` revocation keeps working because revocation is by hash, algorithm-agnostic.

> **Theorem (Hybrid unforgeability):** If at least one of the two schemes in a hybrid certificate is existentially unforgeable against its adversary class, the hybrid certificate is unforgeable against the hybrid adversary $\mathcal{A} = (\mathcal{A}_{cl}, \mathcal{A}_{q})$.

*Proof sketch.* Forgery requires forging *both* component signatures on the same TBSCertificate (the binding extension prevents cross-certificate mix-and-match). Forging the classical component breaks classical unforgeability; forging the PQ component breaks PQ unforgeability. Since at least one scheme resists its class, no hybrid adversary forges both. ∎

Consequence: hybrid certificates are secure *today* (classical component) and remain secure *after* $T_q$ (PQ component) — security degrades gracefully rather than collapsing. The cost is size: a hybrid ML-DSA-65 + ECDSA P-256 leaf is ~4–5 KB, forcing UEFI variable quota and SPDM chunking changes (§5). A signer also cannot later repudiate via "my classical key was quantum-broken" — the PQ component independently binds them.

### 4.3 Quantum-Resistant Measured Boot

TPM 2.0 measured boot survives the quantum transition better than Secure Boot because hashing is only polynomially weakened — but "only polynomially" still breaks 256-bit PCR banks under the BHT bound. Our design:

1. **Activate SHA-384/SHA-512 PCR banks** alongside SHA-256 (TPM 2.0 already specifies multiple banks [7]); extend all supported banks; verifiers quote the strongest bank available.
2. **Bind algorithm agility into the event log** (`TCG_PCR_EVENT2` records the digest algorithm); verifiers replay per-bank and reject bank disagreement.
3. **Migrate attestation keys to ML-DSA-65** via extensible `TPM_ALG_*` templates; quotes grow ~256 B → ~3.5 KB — fine over the network, but constrained *requesters* need larger buffers.
4. **Derive DICE layer keys as ML-DSA independently per layer** — only the asymmetric derivation changes, no UDS reprovisioning [8]. DICE-based BMC/peripheral identities are thus the *easiest* firmware identities to migrate.

Verifiers must check the strongest available bank and *reject* attestations quoting only a quantum-weak bank after the platform's migration date — a policy control, not a cryptographic one, but essential.

```c
/* Illustrative: extending all active PCR banks. Mirrors TPM2_PCR_Extend
 * semantics; production code uses the TSS. */
TPM_RC extend_all_banks(TSS_CONTEXT *ctx, UINT32 pcr,
                        const uint8_t *measurement, size_t len) {
    TPM_ALG_ID banks[] = { TPM_ALG_SHA384, TPM_ALG_SHA512, TPM_ALG_SHA256 };
    for (size_t i = 0; i < sizeof(banks)/sizeof(banks[0]); i++) {
        uint8_t digest[64];
        hash(banks[i], measurement, len, digest);   /* H(m) */
        /* PCR_new = H(PCR_old || digest), computed inside the TPM */
        TPM_RC rc = Tss2_PCR_Extend(ctx, pcr, banks[i], digest);
        if (rc != TPM_RC_SUCCESS) return rc;
    }
    return TPM_RC_SUCCESS;
}
```

### 4.4 SPDM 1.x Device Attestation Under PQC

SPDM is where PQ signatures meet the harshest transport constraints — attestation over I2C at ~1 MHz on microcontrollers with kilobyte-scale buffers [9]:

- **Negotiation.** SPDM 1.3's `NEGOTIATE_ALGORITHMS` carries extensible `BaseAsymAlgo` bits; assign ML-DSA/SLH-DSA parameter sets new positions. The transcript (`L1/L2`) binds negotiated algorithms so attackers cannot strip PQ bits — negotiation downgrade is defeated by transcript hashing.
- **Challenge-response.** `CHALLENGE_AUTH` embeds the responder's signature over a transcript including the requester nonce. ML-DSA-65 grows this field 64 B → 3,309 B. SPDM chunking handles it, but responders must enlarge `DataTransferSize` buffers and requesters extend timeouts: 3.3 KB at 1 MHz I2C costs ~30 ms on the wire before verification.
- **Measurements.** `GET_MEASUREMENTS` returns signed blocks; the format is algorithm-agile by design — migration cost is buffer sizing, not protocol redesign.
- **Sessions.** Pair ML-KEM (FIPS 203) session key exchange with ML-DSA authentication for full PQ confidentiality and authenticity of SPDM secured messages.

DICE composes cleanly: a DICE-derived ML-DSA device key becomes the SPDM leaf identity, with the DICE chain supplying the manufacturing-time provisioning story SPDM's certificate model otherwise lacks [8][9].

### 4.5 PQ-Signed Firmware Update Manifests and the RTU

Per SP 800-193 [4], the RTU verifies every update before staging. The manifest is the highest-value signature target on the platform:

1. **Manifest format.** CBOR/ASN.1 carrying component descriptors (GUID, anti-rollback monotonic version counters, SHA-384 digests), the hybrid signature $(\sigma_{cl}, \sigma_{PQ})$, and the signer's hybrid chain. The RTU verifies *both* signatures, checks version monotonicity against replay-protected NVRAM counters, then stages.
2. **Manifest algorithm: ML-DSA-65.** Updates are rare (latency tolerant) but the RTU is SRAM/code-size constrained: ~6 KB for ML-DSA verification versus far larger working sets for SLH-DSA's hash trees.
3. **Manifest-chain root: SLH-DSA** in the offline HSM — hash-only assumptions maximize credible trust lifetime; LMS/XMSS per SP 800-208 [3] are the alternative where root-certificate size matters.
4. **Anti-rollback.** Rollback protection rests on monotonic counters, but *counter-update authorization* is a signed RTU operation — hybrid signatures close the quantum forgery path there too.

```python
# Illustrative: RTU manifest verification (dual-signature, anti-rollback)
def rtu_verify_manifest(manifest, stored_counter, trust_store):
    chain = trust_store.build_chain(manifest.signer_chain)  # hybrid X.509
    assert chain.verify_dual()            # BOTH classical and PQ sigs valid
    assert manifest.version > stored_counter, "rollback rejected"
    for comp in manifest.components:
        assert sha384(comp.image) == comp.digest, "digest mismatch"
    assert ml_dsa_verify(chain.leaf.pq_key, manifest.tbs, manifest.sig_pq)
    assert ecdsa_verify(chain.leaf.classical_key, manifest.tbs, manifest.sig_cl)
    stage_to_recovery_region(manifest)    # SP 800-193 protected update
    stored_counter.update(manifest.version)
```

## 5. Empirical Results and Formal Analysis

### 5.1 Verification overhead on BMC-class targets

Model parameters from published reference-implementation cycle counts for Cortex-M4/A-class cores (400 MHz–1 GHz, 256 KB–2 MB SRAM). Absolute figures illustrative; ratios are the load-bearing result.

| Operation | Cycles (order of magnitude) | Time @ 800 MHz | SRAM working set |
|---|---|---|---|
| ECDSA P-256 verify (baseline) | ~1–3 M | ~2–4 ms | ~2 KB |
| ML-DSA-65 verify | ~2–4 M | ~3–5 ms | ~10 KB |
| SLH-DSA-128s verify | ~30–80 M | ~40–100 ms | ~4 KB |
| SLH-DSA-128f verify | ~10–25 M | ~12–30 ms | ~4 KB |

*Table 2: ML-DSA verification is within ~2× of ECDSA — the migration's most important performance fact — while SLH-DSA is 10–50× slower, disqualifying it from per-boot paths.*

**Boot-time budget.** A DXE phase verifying 20–60 images at ~4 ms per ML-DSA-65 verification adds **80–240 ms** — within OEM budgets tolerating ~500 ms of security overhead, and comparable to the RSA-2048 verification it replaces. *Per-boot compute cost is essentially a non-issue for ML-DSA*; the binding constraints are **size** and **code footprint**, not cycles.

### 5.2 Size impact: the true cost of migration

| Artifact | Classical | PQ (ML-DSA-65) | Impact |
|---|---|---|---|
| UEFI `db` entry per signer | ~1–2 KB | ~4–6 KB (hybrid cert) | NVRAM quotas up 3–5× |
| Authenticated variable payload | ~0.5 KB sig | ~3.3 KB sig | Variable driver buffer rework |
| SPDM cert chain (3 certs) | ~2–3 KB | ~10–15 KB | `GET_CERTIFICATE` chunking mandatory |
| TPM quote | ~0.5 KB | ~3.5 KB | Verifier buffer changes |
| Manifest signature | ~0.1 KB | ~3.4 KB dual | Negligible vs. multi-MB image |

*Table 3: Every signature slot designed around 64–512 byte classical signatures must be re-engineered for kilobyte-scale PQ signatures. Code footprint (~6–15 KB for ML-DSA verification incl. SHAKE) is acceptable in DXE but significant in 64–128 KB secure-boot ROMs.*

### 5.3 Formal security argument

> **Theorem (End-to-end PQ firmware trust):** A platform implementing (i) hybrid certificates per §4.2, (ii) SHA-384/SHA-512 PCR banks with strongest-bank attestation policy per §4.3, (iii) SPDM 1.3 with transcript-bound PQ negotiation per §4.4, and (iv) dual-signed RTU manifests with monotonic anti-rollback per §4.5 satisfies boot-authorization unforgeability and attestation soundness against the hybrid adversary $\mathcal{A}$.

*Proof sketch.* Boot authorization reduces to Secure Boot/RTU key unforgeability — hybrid per §4.2. Measured-boot integrity reduces to collision resistance of the strongest quoted bank (SHA-384: 128-bit quantum security) plus ML-DSA AK unforgeability. SPDM authentication reduces to the negotiated scheme's unforgeability plus transcript binding against downgrade. Anti-rollback reduces to monotonic counters gated by RTU authorization signatures. Each reduction is tight up to standard primitive advantages. ∎

The honest caveat: the proof assumes correct implementation — side-channel-resistant PQ code, no silent hybrid downgrade, HSM discipline for stateful schemes — which is where real migrations fail (see §6).

## 6. Limitations

- **Side channels in early-boot verifiers.** ML-DSA implementations can exhibit secret-dependent behavior; timing/power side channels on BMC SoCs during attacker-observable RTU verification need dedicated analysis not provided here.
- **Stateful hash-based signatures are operationally hazardous.** A single LMS/XMSS state-reuse event (backup restore, HSM snapshot) is catastrophic [3]. Recommend only with hardware-enforced state monotonicity; default to SLH-DSA otherwise.
- **NVRAM and ROM budgets are the hard wall.** Platforms with fixed 64 KB variable stores or mask-ROM RoTs cannot migrate without hardware revision — defining an irreducible decade-long tail of the transition.
- **Standardization gaps.** UEFI algorithm registry entries, TPM 2.0 `TPM_ALG_ID` assignments for ML-DSA/SLH-DSA, and SPDM PQC identifiers remain in flight across UEFI Forum, TCG, and DMTF; our constructions assume they complete as drafted.
- **Cryptanalytic risk is nonzero.** ML-DSA rests on young module-lattice assumptions. Our design hedges by placing SLH-DSA (hash-only assumptions) at the roots, so a lattice break compromises only the recoverable fast path.
- **Formal verification gap.** Our proofs are reduction sketches, not machine-checked models; Tamarin/ProVerif modeling of SPDM PQ negotiation is natural next work.

---

## 7. Conclusion

Post-quantum firmware security is not a single migration but a coordinated re-architecture of every layer classical signatures touch: UEFI Secure Boot's authenticated variables, TPM 2.0's measured-boot chains and quotes, DICE's layered identities, SPDM's device authentication, and the RTU's update manifests. This thesis shows the migration is feasible with standardized primitives today — provided engineers abandon single-algorithm thinking: **ML-DSA-65 for everything verified online, SLH-DSA for roots verified rarely and trusted longest, SHA-384 PCR banks for measured boot, hybrid certificates for the decade-long transition, and DICE as the migration vehicle for TPM-less devices.** The most actionable finding: verification *compute* is affordable (ML-DSA within ~2× of ECDSA), while *size* — kilobyte signatures in byte-budgeted slots — is the true engineering cost. With hybrid unforgeability argued, transcript-bound negotiation blocking downgrade, and anti-rollback preserved under dual signatures, this blueprint gives platform vendors a path to firmware trustworthy not just until the quantum horizon, but decades past it.
