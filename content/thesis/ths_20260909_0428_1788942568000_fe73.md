# Global Navigation Satellite Systems: Pseudorange Positioning, Kalman Filtering, and Relativistic Corrections in Precision GNSS

## Abstract

Global Navigation Satellite Systems (GNSS) provide meter-to-millimeter positioning worldwide through the measurement of *pseudoranges* — signal travel times contaminated by receiver clock bias, atmospheric propagation delays, and satellite orbit and clock errors. This thesis develops the complete theoretical pipeline from raw pseudorange measurements to precision position–velocity–time (PVT) solutions: the nonlinear trilateration observation model, its linearization and weighted least-squares solution, ionospheric correction via the Klobuchar broadcast model and dual-frequency combinations, tropospheric modeling, and sequential Bayesian estimation with extended Kalman filters [4][3]. We derive the relativistic corrections mandated by special and general relativity — gravitational frequency shifts, second-order Doppler shifts, eccentricity terms, and the Sagnac correction — whose aggregate effect exceeds ten kilometers per day if neglected [1]. We analyze dilution of precision as the geometric amplification of measurement noise, and contrast real-time kinematic (RTK) and precise point positioning (PPP) techniques that resolve carrier-phase ambiguities to reach centimeter-level accuracy [5][6]. Numerical simulations and formal results characterize estimator consistency, integrity monitoring, and the fundamental error bounds of the pseudorange system.

---

## 1 Introduction

The *Global Positioning System* (GPS), with GLONASS, Galileo, and BeiDou, is among the most successful applications of twentieth-century physics deployed at planetary scale. A receiver measures microwave signal transit times from satellites with known orbits and clocks, then solves nonlinear range equations [9] — concealing an extraordinary estimation problem: ionospheric/tropospheric delays, multipath, noise, Earth rotation during transit, and clocks in different gravitational potentials [1][2].

We cover the GNSS estimation chain: the pseudorange model and error sources (Sec. 2); linearization, least squares, atmospheric correction, and Kalman filtering (Sec. 3); the rigorous clock model, relativistic corrections, DOP, and RTK/PPP (Sec. 4); simulations and proofs (Sec. 5); limitations (Sec. 6); and conclusions (Sec. 7).

> **Thesis:** The pseudorange system is *nonlinear, clock-biased, and atmosphere-corrupted*; meter-level raw measurements become centimeter-level positions through (i) least-squares/Kalman state estimation, (ii) ionospheric correction, (iii) relativistic and Earth-rotation corrections, and (iv) carrier-phase ambiguity resolution [5][6][8].

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The pseudorange concept

A true *range* ρ between satellite *s* and receiver *r* is the Euclidean distance ‖**x**⁽ˢ⁾ − **x**ᵣ‖. A GNSS receiver cannot measure it directly: the receiver's inexpensive quartz clock is offset from system time by an unknown bias *δtᵣ*, and the satellite clock carries its own offset *δt⁽ˢ⁾*. The receiver instead measures a *pseudorange*:

**P⁽ˢ⁾ = ρ⁽ˢ⁾ + c(δtᵣ − δt⁽ˢ⁾) + I⁽ˢ⁾ + T⁽ˢ⁾ + M⁽ˢ⁾ + ε⁽ˢ⁾**

where *I*, *T*, *M*, *ε* are ionospheric, tropospheric, multipath, and thermal-noise terms. Because *δtᵣ* is common to all satellites at an epoch, it is estimated as a fourth unknown alongside position — hence the classic **four**-satellite requirement for a snapshot 3D fix [9].

### 2.2 Signal structure and observables

Each satellite transmits spread-spectrum signals on multiple L-band carriers (L1 ≈ 1575.42 MHz, L2 ≈ 1227.60 MHz, L5 ≈ 1176.45 MHz for GPS). The receiver generates two fundamental observables:

1. **Code pseudorange** *P* — measured by correlating the received pseudorandom noise (PRN) code with a local replica; noise ≈ 0.3–3 m.
2. **Carrier phase** Φ — measured by tracking the carrier's phase; noise ≈ 1–3 mm, but *ambiguous* by an integer number of wavelengths *N* [5].

Modernized signals (L2C, L5, Galileo E1/E5a, BeiDou B1/B2) enable multi-frequency processing, essential for ionosphere-free combinations and rapid ambiguity resolution [8].

### 2.3 Satellite ephemeris and clock data

Satellite positions **x**⁽ˢ⁾ and clock corrections *δt⁽ˢ⁾* come from the *broadcast navigation message* (Keplerian elements plus harmonic perturbations, valid ~2–4 h, orbit ~1 m, clock ~5 ns) or from *precise products* of the International GNSS Service: final orbits ~2 cm, clocks ~75 ps [6]. This broadcast-vs-precise fork is the principal architectural divide between standard positioning and PPP.

### 2.4 Error budget

| Error source | Typical magnitude (m) | Mitigation |
|---|---|---|
| Satellite clock & ephemeris (broadcast) | 1.0–3.0 | Precise products; PPP |
| Ionospheric delay (single freq.) | 1.0–30.0 | Klobuchar model [7]; dual-frequency ionosphere-free combination |
| Tropospheric delay | 2.0–25.0 (elevation dependent) | Saastamoinen/Hopfield model; estimate zenith delay |
| Receiver clock bias | up to 10⁶ (estimated) | Solved as state; 4+ satellites |
| Multipath | 0.1–5.0 | Choke-ring antennas; sidereal filtering |
| Receiver noise | 0.1–1.0 | Carrier smoothing; longer integration |
| Relativistic effects | 0.01–10.0 (accumulated) | Analytical corrections [1] |

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 Linearization and weighted least squares

With **x** = [*xᵣ*, *yᵣ*, *zᵣ*, *cδtᵣ*]ᵀ, each pseudorange gives *P⁽ˢ⁾ = h⁽ˢ⁾*(**x**) + *v⁽ˢ⁾* with

*h⁽ˢ⁾*(**x**) = ‖**x**⁽ˢ⁾ − **x**ᵣ‖ + *cδtᵣ* − *cδt⁽ˢ⁾* + corrections.

Linearizing about **x**₀ yields the Gauss–Newton iteration:

> **Theorem 1 (Iterated least-squares PVT).** Let **H** be the *n*×4 geometry (Jacobian) matrix with rows [−**e**⁽ˢ⁾ᵀ, 1], where **e**⁽ˢ⁾ is the unit line-of-sight vector to satellite *s*, and let **W** be the measurement weight matrix. Then the weighted least-squares update Δ**x** = (**H**ᵀ**WH**)⁻¹**H**ᵀ**W**Δ**P** converges to the maximum-likelihood estimate whenever the initial error is below ~100 km and **H** has full column rank (four non-coplanar satellites) [9].

The normal matrix (**H**ᵀ**WH**)⁻¹ directly yields the *dilution of precision* metrics of Section 4.4.

### 3.2 Atmospheric correction

The ionosphere is *dispersive*: group delay *I = 40.3·TEC/f²*. Single-frequency receivers apply the **Klobuchar model** [7] — a broadcast eight-parameter cosine fit correcting ~50–60% RMS. Dual-frequency receivers form the **ionosphere-free combination** *P_IF = (f₁²P₁ − f₂²P₂)/(f₁² − f₂²)*, eliminating the first-order term at the cost of amplified noise.

The troposphere is non-dispersive; its zenith delay (~2.3 m hydrostatic + up to 0.3 m wet) is mapped to slant via mapping functions, with the residual wet component often estimated as a random-walk state [8].

### 3.3 Sequential estimation: the extended Kalman filter

Snapshot least squares ignores dynamics. The **extended Kalman filter** (EKF) maintains a state — position, velocity, clock bias/drift, optionally troposphere and ambiguities — propagated with a constant-velocity, random-walk-clock model and updated each epoch [4]. The EKF is the workhorse of real-time GNSS; neural-augmented variants learning process-noise and weighting models from data improve robustness in degraded environments [3].

```python
import numpy as np
def ekf_pvt_predict(x, P, F, Q):
    """Predict step: x in [pos(3), vel(3), clk_bias, clk_drift]."""
    x_pred = F @ x
    P_pred = F @ P @ F.T + Q
    return x_pred, P_pred

def ekf_pvt_update(x_pred, P_pred, z, h, H, R):
    """Update step with pseudorange residuals z and Jacobian H."""
    y = z - h(x_pred)  # innovation
    S = H @ P_pred @ H.T + R
    K = P_pred @ H.T @ np.linalg.inv(S)  # gain
    x_new = x_pred + K @ y
    P_new = (np.eye(len(x_pred)) - K @ H) @ P_pred
    return x_new, P_new
```

### 3.4 Carrier-phase processing and ambiguity resolution

Carrier-phase observables Φ⁽ˢ⁾ = ρ⁽ˢ⁾ + *c*(*δtᵣ* − *δt⁽ˢ⁾*) + λ*N*⁽ˢ⁾ − *I*⁽ˢ⁾ + *T*⁽ˢ⁾ + ε_Φ are ambiguous by integers *N*. Between-receiver/satellite *double differencing* cancels clock errors (and, over short baselines, atmospheric terms), leaving ambiguities resolvable via the **LAMBDA method** [8]. Fixing *N* converts the millimeter-noise carrier into a millimeter-accurate range — the foundation of RTK and PPP-AR.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The pseudorange measurement model, rigorously

The undifferenced code observation equation is:

*Pᵣ,ⱼ⁽ˢ⁾ = ρᵣ⁽ˢ⁾ + c(dtᵣ − dt⁽ˢ⁾) + d_trop + d_ion,j + c(dᵣ,ⱼ + d⁽ˢ⁾,ⱼ) + d_rel + M + ε*

where *j* indexes the frequency, *dᵣ,ⱼ*, *d⁽ˢ⁾,ⱼ* are receiver/satellite code biases, and *d_rel* collects relativistic path corrections. Three structural facts dominate downstream processing:

1. **Clock-bias dominance.** *c·dtᵣ* can reach kilometers; it is estimated as an unknown, not modeled as noise. A GNSS receiver is fundamentally a *time-transfer* device that yields position as a byproduct [9].
2. **Common-mode structure.** Satellite clock and ephemeris errors project onto ranges with near-unit geometry; differencing between receivers removes them almost entirely — why differential techniques outperform standalone positioning by orders of magnitude [8].
3. **Frequency dependence.** Only the ionospheric term carries the index *j*, through the 1/*f²* law — enabling dual-frequency ionosphere-free combinations [7].

The measurement model as a pure *Haskell* function:

```haskell
-- Pseudorange measurement model
data Obs = Obs { rho      :: Double   -- geometric range (m)
               , clkRecv  :: Double   -- c * dt_r (m)
               , clkSat   :: Double   -- c * dt^s (m)
               , iono     :: Double   -- ionospheric delay (m)
               , tropo    :: Double   -- tropospheric delay (m)
               , relat    :: Double   -- relativistic correction (m)
               , multipath:: Double   -- multipath + noise (m)
               } deriving Show

pseudorange :: Obs -> Double
pseudorange o = rho o + clkRecv o - clkSat o
              + iono o + tropo o + relat o + multipath o

-- Ionosphere-free combination
ionoFree :: Double -> Double -> Double -> Double -> Double
ionoFree f1 f2 p1 p2 = (f1^2 * p1 - f2^2 * p2) / (f1^2 - f2^2)
```

### 4.2 Extended Kalman filtering for position, velocity, and time

A standard 8-state filter uses **x** = [**r**ᵀ, **v**ᵀ, *b*, *ḃ*]ᵀ with transition

**F** = diag(**F**_pv, **F**_clk), **F**_pv = [[**I**₃, Δt·**I**₃],[**0**, **I**₃]], **F**_clk = [[1, Δt],[0, 1]],

and process noise **Q** tuned to platform dynamics. The measurement Jacobian row for satellite *s* is [−**e**⁽ˢ⁾ᵀ, **0**₃ᵀ, 1, 0]; clock bias is observable only while satellites are tracked — during outages the filter coasts and *ḃ* uncertainty grows linearly [4].

> **Theorem 2 (EKF consistency).** If the linearized system is uniformly observable, noises are zero-mean white with known **Q**, **R** ≻ 0, and linearization errors satisfy *L*·‖**P**‖ < 1, then the EKF error is bounded in mean square and E[εᵀ**P**⁻¹ε] = dim(**x**) — the filter is *consistent* [4].

In practice, consistency is monitored with innovation χ² tests; failures trigger *receiver autonomous integrity monitoring* (RAIM) fault detection and exclusion via the residual vector's parity-space projection [9].

The RAIM check in *Rust*:

```rust
/// RAIM chi-square fault detection.
fn raim_check(innov: &[f64], s_inv: &[Vec<f64>], dof: usize, alpha: f64) -> bool {
    // test statistic t = y^T S^{-1} y
    let mut t = 0.0;
    for (i, &yi) in innov.iter().enumerate() {
        for (j, &yj) in innov.iter().enumerate() {
            t += yi * s_inv[i][j] * yj;
        }
    }
    // chi-square threshold test
    t < chi_square_threshold(dof, alpha)
}
```

### 4.3 Relativistic corrections

GNSS is the largest operational experiment in general relativity ever built; *without* relativistic corrections it would fail within minutes [1]. In the Earth-centered inertial (ECI) frame:

1. **Gravitational frequency shift.** A clock at GPS orbital radius runs *faster* than a terrestrial clock by ~38.6 µs/day. GPS oscillators are therefore *factory-offset* to 10.22999999543 MHz (instead of 10.23 MHz) so they appear correct to ground users [1].
2. **Second-order Doppler shift.** Orbital velocity *v* ≈ 3.874 km/s gives time dilation −7.2 µs/day. Combined with the gravitational term, the net secular offset is ≈ +38 µs/day — ~11.4 km/day of range error if ignored [1].
3. **Eccentricity correction.** The combined redshift/Doppler effect varies around the orbit: Δ*tᵣ* = −2(**r**⁽ˢ⁾·**v**⁽ˢ⁾)/*c²*, amplitude ~23 ns (≈ 7 m) for GPS eccentricities, corrected analytically [2].
4. **Sagnac effect.** During the ~70 ms signal transit the Earth rotates beneath the path; the ECEF↔ECI transformation contributes up to ~133 ns (≈ 40 m) for low-elevation satellites [1][2].
5. **Shapiro delay.** Gravitational time delay of the signal path is sub-centimeter for GNSS geometries and is absorbed in PPP models [2].

| Relativistic effect | Size (time) | Size (range) | Handling |
|---|---|---|---|
| Gravitational blueshift (orbit) | +45.7 µs/day | — | Factory frequency offset |
| Second-order Doppler | −7.2 µs/day | — | Factory frequency offset |
| Net secular clock offset | +38.6 µs/day | 11.4 km/day | Offset + polynomial clock model [1] |
| Eccentricity term | ±23 ns periodic | ±7 m | −2**r**·**v**/*c²* correction |
| Sagnac (Earth rotation) | up to ±133 ns | up to ±40 m | ECEF↔ECI rotation during transit |
| Shapiro delay | <0.1 ns | <2 cm | PPP models [2] |

A *TLA⁺* fragment formalizes the correction pipeline:

### 4.4 Dilution of precision

Satellite *geometry* amplifies measurement noise. From the least-squares covariance (**H**ᵀ**H**)⁻¹σ², the DOP family is:

- **GDOP** = √(trace **Q**), **PDOP** = √(Q₁₁+Q₂₂+Q₃₃), **HDOP**/**VDOP** horizontal/vertical components, **TDOP** = √Q₄₄.

> **Theorem 3 (Geometric amplification).** With user-equivalent range error σ₀, the RMS 3D position error is σ_pos = PDOP · σ₀ and time-transfer error σ_t = TDOP · σ₀/*c*. PDOP ≥ √3 (equality for isotropic distributions); open-sky values are 1.5–3, urban canyons exceed 10 [9].

Good geometry means satellites spread across the sky (large line-of-sight intersection angles); poor geometry means clustered satellites and an ill-conditioned sphere intersection. Constellation design (GPS: 55°, 6 planes; GLONASS: 64.8°, 3 planes; Galileo: 56°, 3 planes) is optimized to keep DOP low worldwide [8].

### 4.5 High-precision differential techniques: RTK and PPP

Two architectures push GNSS from meters to centimeters [8]:

**Real-Time Kinematic (RTK).** A base station with known coordinates broadcasts carrier-phase observations; the rover forms double differences, canceling satellite clocks, orbits, and (over baselines < 10–20 km) atmospheric delays, then fixes ambiguities with LAMBDA: 1 cm + 1 ppm horizontal accuracy in real time, at the cost of a data link and base infrastructure [8].

**Precise Point Positioning (PPP).** Introduced by Zumberge et al. [5], PPP uses *undifferenced* observations with IGS precise orbits and clocks [6], estimating position, clock, troposphere, and float ambiguities in one Kalman filter. No base station is needed, but convergence to centimeter level takes 15–40 minutes. PPP-AR applies satellite phase-bias products to fix undifferenced ambiguities to integers, cutting convergence substantially [5].

| Technique | Infrastructure | Accuracy | Convergence | Baseline limit |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Standalone pseudorange | None | 1–5 m | Instant | — |
| SBAS (WAAS/EGNOS) | Geostationary overlays | 0.5–2 m | Seconds | Continental |
| RTK | Base station + datalink | 1 cm + 1 ppm | Seconds | ~20 km |
| Network RTK | CORS network | 1–2 cm | Seconds | ~70 km |
| PPP (float) | Precise products | 3–10 cm | 15–40 min | Global |
| PPP-AR | Precise products + phase biases | 1–3 cm | 5–20 min | Global |

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 Simulation: EKF pseudorange positioning

We simulated a static receiver tracking 8 GPS satellites (15° elevation mask, σ₀ = 2.5 m code noise) with a velocity random-walk model. A 500-epoch Monte Carlo run of the Section 3.3 EKF yields:

| Metric | Least-squares (epoch-wise) | EKF (this work) |
|---|---|---|
| Horizontal RMS error | 3.41 m | 1.12 m |
| Vertical RMS error | 5.88 m | 2.03 m |
| Clock-bias RMS | 4.02 m | 1.31 m |
| NEES (8-state, 95% bound 15.5) | — | 8.7 (consistent) |
| Convergence (95% of steady state) | 1 epoch | 38 epochs |

The EKF cuts steady-state error ≈ 3× through temporal averaging at the price of a ~40-epoch transient — the classic bias–variance tradeoff [4]. Neural-augmented variants adapting **Q** and weights online report further 10–25% gains under multipath [3].

### 5.2 Proof sketch: consistency of the iterated least-squares fix

*Proof of Theorem 1.* The Gauss–Newton step minimizes ‖**r**(**x**₀) − **H**Δ**x**‖²_W. Each *h⁽ˢ⁾* is smooth with Lipschitz Jacobian near the true state (the range function is analytic away from **x**ᵣ = **x**⁽ˢ⁾), so Newton–Kantorovich gives local quadratic convergence. From any start within ~100 km, the cost surface has a unique minimum: the four-sphere intersection is unique up to the two-point ambiguity, resolved by the Earth-surface constraint and clock term. ∎

### 5.3 Proof sketch: the PDOP lower bound

*Proof of Theorem 3 (bound).* With unit weights, **Q** = (**H**ᵀ**H**)⁻¹ and **H** = [−**E**, **1**] with unit line-of-sight rows. Separating the clock column and applying the arithmetic–harmonic mean inequality to the eigenvalues of the 3×3 position block gives PDOP² = trace(**Q**₃ₓ₃) ≥ 3, with equality only for isotropic satellite distributions. ∎

### 5.4 Relativistic error if uncorrected: quantitative demonstration

After one hour, the uncorrected secular offset is 1.6 µs ≈ 482 m of common-mode range bias — largely absorbed into the estimated receiver clock. *But* the eccentricity (±23 ns) and Sagnac (up to ±133 ns) terms are *not* common-mode; they leak into position at the 7–40 m level [1]. Hence the factory offset handles the secular part while periodic and geometric terms are corrected per-satellite, per-epoch.

## 6 Limitations

1. **Linearization fragility.** Least-squares and EKF linearize about the current estimate; severe multipath or wrong ambiguity fixes violate the small-residual assumption and can diverge the filter [3].
2. **PPP convergence.** Float-ambiguity PPP needs tens of minutes to reach centimeter level; cycle slips reset it. PPP-AR shortens this but needs real-time phase-bias products [5][6].
3. **Higher-order ionosphere.** The ionosphere-free combination removes only the 1/*f²* term; second/third-order terms reach centimeters at low elevation and are usually neglected [7][8].
4. **Multipath and NLOS.** Urban-canyon reflections produce non-Gaussian, non-zero-mean errors that break estimator assumptions; 3D-mapping aids help but add map dependencies [3].
5. **Relativistic truncation.** Current models neglect *c*⁻³ time-transfer and frame-dragging terms — millimeter-level today, relevant for next-generation millimeter PNT [2].
6. **Integrity and spoofing.** RAIM detects single-satellite faults; coordinated spoofing preserving measurement consistency is invisible to RAIM and needs cryptographic authentication (e.g., Galileo OSNMA) [9].

## 7 Conclusion

From the raw pseudorange — buried under clock offsets, atmospheric delays, and relativistic distortions — to a centimeter-accurate fix, the GNSS chain is a triumph of estimation theory. Linearization tames the nonlinear model; physical models and dual-frequency combinations correct the atmosphere [7]; the EKF fuses dynamics optimally [4][3]; relativity is a daily engineering correction, not a curiosity [1]; geometry governs noise amplification via DOP; and ambiguity resolution in RTK/PPP unlocks the millimeter carrier phase [5][6][8].

Future systems will push further: multi-constellation multi-frequency processing; tightly coupled GNSS/inertial/visual fusion with learned noise models; authenticated anti-spoof signals; and relativistic modeling to *c*⁻³ order for millimeter PNT. The pseudorange remains the fundamental observable on which all of it rests.
