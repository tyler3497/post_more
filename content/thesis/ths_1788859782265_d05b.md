# On the Formal Foundations of Distributed Rate Limiting: Token Buckets, Leaky Buckets, GCRA Virtual Scheduling, Sliding Windows, and Gossip-Coordinated Quotas

## Abstract

## 1 Introduction

Every production networked system eventually confronts a fundamental asymmetry: demand is unbounded and bursty, while service capacity is finite and provisioned in advance. *Rate limiting* is the engineering discipline that mediates this asymmetry, transforming an uncontrolled arrival process into a shaped one that a backend can honor. Its modern branches extend to the planetary scale: Cloudflare's edge, Envoy's service-mesh rate-limit service, and multi-region API gateways that must enforce a single global quota without a single global bottleneck.

Despite its ubiquity, rate limiting is frequently treated as folklore — a menu of "pick one" algorithms with only qualitative comparisons. The distinctions between a *token bucket* and a *leaky bucket* are blurred in practitioner writing; the GCRA is cited but rarely derived; and sliding-window variants are deployed without analysis of their approximation error. This thesis aims to repair that deficit. Its contributions are:

1. **A unified formalism.** We model every classical limiter as an instance of a single admission predicate over arrival-time sequences, and prove that the token bucket, the leaky bucket as a meter, and GCRA virtual scheduling are pairwise equivalent under a parameter transformation.
2. **Closed-form results.** Burst bounds, worst-case memory, and approximation-error bounds for sliding-window counters versus sliding-window logs.
3. **A distributed extension.** Quota sharding by consistent hashing, atomic single-RTT enforcement in Redis via Lua and `redis-cell`'s `CL.THROTTLE`, and a gossip-based reconciliation protocol for multi-region quotas with a derived convergence bound.

The remainder is organized as follows: Section 2 reconstructs the classical single-node algorithms; Section 3 develops the shared mathematical methodology; Section 4 gives the deep technical treatment — equivalences, window algorithms, GCRA, distributed enforcement, and gossip reconciliation; Section 5 evaluates empirically and proves complexity bounds; Section 6 discusses limitations; Section 7 concludes.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The traffic-policing problem

Consider a stream of requests arriving at times $t_1 \le t_2 \le \cdots$. A *policer* is a function $P$ mapping each arrival to a binary decision, *admit* or *shed*, such that admitted traffic satisfies a negotiated contract. The standard contract is the $(\sigma, \rho)$ envelope of network calculus: over any interval $[s, t]$, admitted arrivals are bounded by

$$A(s,t) \le \rho \cdot (t - s) + \sigma,$$

where $\rho$ is the sustained rate and $\sigma$ the burst tolerance. Virtually every classical limiter is a mechanism for enforcing some $(\sigma, \rho)$ envelope, and the families differ in how much state they keep, whether they shape or merely police, and how they behave at window boundaries.

### 2.2 Token bucket

The token bucket holds at most $b$ tokens; tokens accrue at rate $r$; each admitted request consumes one token. An arrival at time $t$ is admitted iff the bucket is non-empty. With continuous replenishment, the token count evolves as

$$B(t) = \min\big(b,\; B(t^-) + r \cdot (t - t_{\text{last}}) - \mathbf{1}_{\text{admit}}\big).$$

The bucket admits bursts of size up to $b$ instantaneously and sustains rate $r$ thereafter — precisely the $(\sigma=b, \rho=r)$ envelope. Tokens are commonly credited lazily (on arrival) rather than by a background timer, making the algorithm $O(1)$ time and $O(1)$ state per key [5].

### 2.3 Leaky bucket: meter versus queue

The literature's most persistent confusion concerns the leaky bucket. There are two distinct objects sharing the name [2]:

- **Leaky bucket as a meter (policer).** A finite-capacity bucket whose real-valued content drains at one unit per time unit and increases by an increment per conforming arrival; an arrival conforms iff the content does not exceed a limit. This is the GCRA's continuous-state formulation.
- **Leaky bucket as a queue (shaper).** Arrivals enter a FIFO queue drained at a constant rate; overflow is shed. This variant *smooths* traffic, emitting at exactly the drain rate, but introduces unbounded queueing delay under overload.

The distinction matters: the meter decides conformance and sheds; the queue reshapes and delays. Production API rate limiting almost always wants the meter.

### 2.4 GCRA: the ATM heritage

The Generic Cell Rate Algorithm, standardized by the ATM Forum [1] and ITU-T I.371 [2], is given in two provably equivalent forms:

- **Virtual scheduling.** Maintain a *theoretical arrival time* (TAT). For arrival $k$ at time $t_a(k)$: if $t_a(k) < \text{TAT} - L$, the cell is non-conforming; else it conforms and $\text{TAT} \gets \max(t_a(k), \text{TAT}) + I$, where $I$ is the increment (inverse rate) and $L$ the limit (tolerance).
- **Continuous-state leaky bucket.** Maintain bucket content $X$ and last-compliance time; drain continuously; admit iff content plus increment stays within the limit.

Redis's `redis-cell` module implements exactly this GCRA as `CL.THROTTLE`, returning remaining quota and retry-after in a single command [6].

### 2.5 Windowed counters

- **Fixed window.** Partition time into windows of length $W$; count per window; shed when the count exceeds $N$. Suffers the *boundary burst*: $2N$ arrivals can be admitted across two adjacent windows within an arbitrarily small interval [5].
- **Sliding window log.** Store every arrival timestamp in a sorted set; on arrival, evict entries older than $W$ and admit iff the remaining count is below $N$. Exact, but $O(N)$ memory per key.
- **Sliding window counter.** Keep two fixed-window counters (current and previous) and estimate the sliding count as $c_{\text{prev}} \cdot (1 - \tau) + c_{\text{curr}}$, where $\tau$ is the fractional position within the current window. $O(1)$ memory with bounded approximation error — reportedly the design Cloudflare employs internally [7].

## 3 Methodology

Our method is analytic rather than experimental: we formalize each limiter as a *deterministic admission predicate* over arrival sequences, derive its envelope, and compare families by three metrics — **precision** (how tightly the enforced envelope matches the target), **state complexity** (bytes per key, asymptotic in the rate), and **coordination cost** (round trips and shared state required per decision in a distributed deployment).

For distributed analysis we adopt the standard model: $m$ edge nodes, each seeing a sub-stream of arrivals; a global contract $(N, W)$ or $(\sigma, \rho)$ that the *union* of admitted traffic must satisfy; communication between nodes that is asynchronous with bounded staleness $\Delta$. We distinguish *strong* enforcement (the global contract is never violated) from *probabilistic/best-effort* enforcement (violations bounded with high probability or in expectation).

> **Theorem:** *Envelope characterization.* If a limiter admits arrival $t_k$ only when a state variable $s_k$ satisfies $s_k \le \sigma$ and updates $s_{k+1} = f(s_k, t_{k+1} - t_k)$ with $f$ monotone in the inter-arrival gap, then admitted traffic satisfies a $(\sigma, \rho)$ envelope with $\rho$ determined by the drift of $f$. *Proof sketch.* Induction on arrivals: each admission consumes at most $\sigma$ of "budget" and idle time replenishes it at rate $\rho$; summing over any interval telescopes to the envelope bound.

All code in this thesis is Python 3 for clarity; production deployments typically use Lua inside Redis or Go/Rust at the edge for the same logic.

```python
from dataclasses import dataclass

@dataclass
class TokenBucket:
    rate: float      # tokens per second (rho)
    burst: float     # bucket capacity (sigma)
    tokens: float = 0.0
    last: float = 0.0

    def admit(self, now: float) -> bool:
        # Lazy replenishment: O(1) time, O(1) state.
        self.tokens = min(self.burst,
                          self.tokens + self.rate * max(0.0, now - self.last))
        self.last = now
        if self.tokens >= 1.0:
            self.tokens -= 1.0
            return True
        return False
```

Note the `max(0.0, ...)` clamp: non-monotonic clock steps (NTP corrections, leap seconds) would otherwise manufacture or destroy tokens — a real production failure mode [8].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Equivalence of token bucket, leaky bucket as a meter, and GCRA

The three classical "bucket" algorithms are not three algorithms at all. We prove they are one algorithm in three costumes.

> **Theorem:** *Bucket equivalence.* The token bucket with parameters $(r, b)$, the continuous-state leaky bucket meter with increment $I = 1/r$ and limit $L = b/r$, and GCRA virtual scheduling with the same $(I, L)$ admit exactly the same arrival sequences. *Proof sketch.* Map token-bucket tokens $B(t)$ to leaky-bucket content $X(t) = b - B(t)$; replenishment of tokens at rate $r$ becomes drainage of content at rate $r$, and the capacity cap $b$ becomes the content floor $0$. For GCRA, set $\text{TAT}$ such that $\text{TAT} - t$ equals the time until the bucket refills to admit one more arrival; the conformance test $t_a(k) \ge \text{TAT} - L$ is then algebraically identical to the content test $X + I \le L$. The virtual-scheduling update $\text{TAT} \gets \max(t_a(k), \text{TAT}) + I$ mirrors lazy token replenishment.

The practical corollary: `redis-cell`'s `CL.THROTTLE` reports token-bucket state computed through GCRA arithmetic [6]. Choosing among the three is a matter of which state variable is most convenient to store, not which traffic is admitted.

The leaky bucket *as a queue* is the genuine outlier: it delays rather than sheds, converting a policing problem into a queueing one with delay bound $b/r$ and requiring buffer for $b$ requests.

### 4.2 Windowed algorithms: exactness, error, and the boundary pathology

Fixed-window counting is the simplest distributed-friendly limiter — a single `INCR` plus `EXPIRE` per window — but its pathology is severe. An adversary (or merely an unlucky phase alignment) can place $N$ arrivals at the end of window $k$ and $N$ more at the start of window $k+1$, admitting $2N$ requests within $\epsilon$ seconds against a nominal $N$-per-$W$ contract [5]. The effective burst is doubled; for abuse prevention this is often unacceptable.

The sliding-window log eliminates the pathology exactly: it answers "how many arrivals in $(t - W, t]$?" from stored timestamps. Its cost is $O(N)$ memory per key and a sorted-set range deletion per decision — fine for hundreds of keys, ruinous for millions of distinct IPs.

The sliding-window counter splits the difference. With previous-window count $c_p$ and current-window count $c_c$ at fractional offset $\tau \in [0, 1)$:

$$\hat{c}(t) = c_p \cdot (1 - \tau) + c_c.$$

> **Theorem:** *Approximation bound.* The estimator $\hat{c}(t)$ satisfies $|\hat{c}(t) - c(t)| \le \min(c_p, c_c) \le N$, where $c(t)$ is the true sliding count; in particular the relative error vanishes as $N$ grows and the estimate is exact at window boundaries ($\tau = 0$). *Proof sketch.* The true count weights previous-window arrivals by their actual overlap with $(t-W, t]$; the estimator replaces each arrival's true indicator with the uniform weight $(1-\tau)$. The discrepancy is bounded by the number of previous-window arrivals that fall outside the true overlap, at most $\min(c_p, c_c)$.

| Algorithm | Memory per key | Precision | Boundary burst |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fixed window | $O(1)$ counter | Exact within window | Up to $2N$ |
| Sliding window log | $O(N)$ timestamps | Exact | None |
| Sliding window counter | $O(1)$ (two counters) | Approximate, bounded error | None (estimated) |
| Token bucket / GCRA | $O(1)$ (two floats) | Exact vs. $(\sigma,\rho)$ envelope | Bounded by $\sigma$ |

In Redis, the sliding log maps to `ZREMRANGEBYSCORE` + `ZADD` + `ZCARD` in a `MULTI` block [9]; the fixed and sliding-counter variants map to `INCR`/`GET` pipelines. The choice is thus a three-way trade among memory, precision, and round trips.

### 4.3 GCRA virtual scheduling, derived in full

Virtual scheduling deserves its reputation as the most elegant limiter. The entire algorithm is one comparison and one assignment. Let $I$ be the nominal inter-arrival time (inverse of the sustained rate) and $L$ the limit (jitter/burst tolerance). State: a single timestamp, TAT.

```
on arrival at time t:
    if t < TAT - L:      # too early even with tolerance
        return SHED
    TAT = max(t, TAT) + I
    return ADMIT
```

The first arrival initializes $\text{TAT} = t$. Observe what the two branches do: if the arrival is *late* ($t \ge \text{TAT}$), the schedule re-anchors to the actual arrival — idle time is forgiven, not banked beyond $L$. If the arrival is *early but within tolerance*, the schedule extends from the theoretical time, spacing future admissions exactly $I$ apart. The limit $L$ therefore plays precisely the role of token-bucket burst depth: with $L = (b-1)\cdot I$, up to $b$ back-to-back arrivals conform.

This is why GCRA is the natural fit for Redis: one float of state per key, one Lua script, atomic under concurrency, and `CL.THROTTLE` additionally returns the retry-after as $\text{TAT} - L - t$ [6]. Its weakness is expressiveness — it enforces a single $(\sigma, \rho)$ pair, whereas real APIs want tiered limits (e.g., 100/min *and* 10,000/day), requiring stacked GCRA instances where a request must conform to all of them [1].

### 4.4 Distributed enforcement: sharding, atomicity, and the Envoy model

A single Redis is a single point of contention. Production systems distribute enforcement along two axes.

**Consistent-hash quota sharding.** Partition the key space across $m$ Redis shards by consistent hashing (or Redis Cluster's 16,384 hash slots with gossip-based membership [10]). Each key's limiter state lives on exactly one shard, so decisions remain single-RTT atomic Lua executions. The trade: a *global* quota (e.g., 10,000 QPS across all regions) cannot be enforced exactly by independent per-shard limiters without over-admission by up to a factor of $m$ in the worst case.

**Static partition with dynamic re-weighting.** Split the global quota into per-region slices ($C_{\text{global}} = \sum_i C_i$), enforce locally at $<1$ms, and periodically rebalance unused quota between regions [8]. This preserves the hard invariant — the sum of slices never exceeds the global cap — while recovering utilization when traffic is imbalanced.

**Envoy's rate-limit service (RLS).** Envoy externalizes the decision: sidecars send `RateLimitRequest`s over gRPC to a dedicated RLS cluster, which holds the canonical counters (commonly Redis-backed) and returns `OK` / `OVER_LIMIT` with `current_limit` descriptors [11]. Local Envoy token-bucket filters handle the fast path; the global RLS handles cross-instance coordination. The architecture cleanly separates *mechanism* (per-node admission) from *policy state* (central counters), at the cost of an added network hop on the request path — typically mitigated by caching verdicts for short TTLs.

```python
# Atomic sliding-window admission in Redis via Lua: one RTT, no races.
LUA = """
local key, now, window, limit = KEYS[1], tonumber(ARGV[1]),
                                tonumber(ARGV[2]), tonumber(ARGV[3])
redis.call('ZREMRANGEBYSCORE', key, 0, now - window)
local n = redis.call('ZCARD', key)
if n < limit then
    redis.call('ZADD', key, now, now .. ':' .. math.random())
    redis.call('PEXPIRE', key, window)
    return {1, limit - n - 1}   -- admitted, remaining
end
return {0, 0}                   -- shed
"""
```

### 4.5 Gossip-coordinated quotas: eventual consistency for rate limits

When regions cannot share a synchronous store — or when the coordination latency would dominate — *epidemic gossip* reconciles quota consumption in the background. Each node maintains a local token bucket for its quota slice and a PN-Counter CRDT (grow-only positive and negative component counters) recording consumed tokens; every $T_g$ (typically 200–500 ms), nodes exchange digests with random peers and merge by component-wise maximum [8][12].

> **Theorem:** *Gossip convergence bound.* With $m$ nodes, fan-out $f$, and per-round success probability $p$, a quota-consumption update reaches all nodes within $O(\log m)$ rounds with high probability; the maximum global over-admission during the convergence window is bounded by $(m - 1) \cdot r \cdot T_{\text{conv}}$, where $r$ is the per-node rate and $T_{\text{conv}}$ the convergence time. *Proof sketch.* Standard epidemic analysis: the infected set grows by factor $(1 + fp)$ per round until saturation, giving logarithmic spread. During the $T_{\text{conv}}$ window, each of the $m-1$ uninformed nodes may admit up to its full local rate against stale state, yielding the bound.

The design space then offers a clean choice, summarized in Section 5. Gossip coordination is *not* a way to get strong global guarantees cheaply — it is a way to trade a bounded, quantifiable amount of over-admission for the elimination of cross-region coordination from the request path.

## 5 Empirical Results / Proofs

We consolidate the analytic results as complexity claims, each with a proof sketch, and report representative measured behavior from the literature.

**Result 1 — State complexity.** Token bucket, GCRA (either formulation), fixed window, and sliding-window counter are $O(1)$ state per key (a constant number of machine words); the sliding-window log is $\Theta(N)$ per key where $N$ is the window limit. *Proof.* Inspection of the state variables: two floats/timestamps versus one timestamp per admitted arrival.

**Result 2 — Decision cost.** All single-node algorithms decide in $O(1)$ amortized time; the sliding-window log pays $O(\log N)$ for sorted-set maintenance. With Redis Lua, every variant executes in a single RTT.

**Result 3 — Precision hierarchy.** Fixed window can over-admit by $2\times$ at boundaries; sliding-window counter error is bounded by $\min(c_p, c_c)$ (Theorem, §4.2); sliding log, token bucket, and GCRA are exact with respect to their contracts. Measured deployments report the sliding counter's relative error below 1% for typical window sizes [7].

**Result 4 — Distributed over-admission.** With $m$ independent shards each enforcing limit $N$, worst-case global over-admission is $m \cdot N$ against a nominal $N$ global contract; static partitioning reduces this to zero while sacrificing at most the imbalance fraction of utilization; gossip reconciliation bounds it by $(m-1) \cdot r \cdot T_{\text{conv}}$ (§4.5). Redis Cluster's hash-slot scheme keeps per-key atomicity while scaling membership gossip at a fixed ~2 KB/s per node [10].

**Result 5 — Coordination latency.** Envoy RLS adds one gRPC RTT to the decision path (sub-millisecond within a region); local token-bucket pre-filtering absorbs the common case. Gossip reconciliation removes coordination from the request path entirely at the cost of the convergence window above.

| Architecture | Global exactness | Request-path cost | Partition behavior |
|---|---|---|---|
| Single Redis + Lua | Exact | 1 RTT | Total outage if Redis fails |
| Sharded Redis (hash slots) | Per-key exact, global approximate | 1 RTT | Degraded per shard |
| Static quota partition | Exact (by construction) | Local only | Independent operation |
| Envoy global RLS | Exact | 1 gRPC RTT (cached) | Fail-open or fail-closed (policy) |
| Gossip reconciliation | Bounded over-admission | Local only | Independent operation |

## 6 Limitations

## 7 Conclusion

Rate limiting, viewed formally, is a smaller field than its folklore suggests: the token bucket, the leaky bucket as a meter, and GCRA virtual scheduling are one algorithm; windowed counters form a precision–memory–cost ladder with the sliding-window counter as the practical sweet spot; and distributed enforcement is a choice among exact-but-centralized, partitioned-but-rigid, and gossip-reconciled-but-approximate designs, each with a quantifiable over-admission bound. The practitioner rule that falls out of the theory is concrete: GCRA arithmetic for per-key precision, sliding-window counters where boundary bursts matter, static partition plus gossip reconciliation across regions — and always clamp your clock deltas.
