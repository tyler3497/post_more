# VM-Based Confidential Computing: AMD SEV-SNP and Intel TDX Attestation, RMP Page Validation, and TCB Minimization Beyond Process Enclaves

## Abstract

The migration of confidential computing from process-based enclaves to VM-based trusted execution environments marks a structural shift in how cloud tenants reason about trust. This thesis analyzes the two dominant VM-based confidential computing architectures — AMD Secure Encrypted Virtualization with Secure Nested Paging (SEV-SNP) and Intel Trust Domain Extensions (TDX) — through the lenses of attestation, memory-integrity enforcement, trusted computing base (TCB) minimization, and side-channel resilience. We formalize the Reverse Map Table (RMP) page-validation protocol that underpins SEV-SNP's integrity guarantees, decompose Intel's two-phase attestation pipeline from `TDG.MR.REPORT` through the SGX quoting enclave, and quantify the security-usability trade-off relative to process enclaves such as Intel SGX. Drawing on vendor specifications and recent peer-reviewed analyses, we show that VM-based TEEs reduce the engineering cost of adoption to near zero while expanding the TCB to the full guest stack, and that residual side channels — page-level, cache-level, ciphertext, and performance-counter leakage — remain the shared responsibility of the application developer. We close with a research agenda on ciphertext hiding, attested device I/O, and formal verification of the page-state machines guarding confidential memory.

## 1 Introduction

Confidential computing began with a radical but demanding proposition: protect data in use by shrinking the trusted computing base to the CPU package and a handful of lines of enclave code. Intel Software Guard Extensions (SGX), introduced in 2015, embodied this philosophy. Yet a decade of deployment experience revealed a stubborn friction: partitioning applications into enclave and non-enclave halves required substantial re-engineering, invited a steady stream of side-channel findings, and constrained developers to a restrictive programming model. The industry response was to move the trust boundary outward rather than inward — from *process-based* enclaves to *VM-based* confidential computing.

AMD SEV-SNP and Intel TDX represent this paradigm. Instead of isolating a process from its host operating system, they isolate an entire virtual machine from the hypervisor. The tenant ships an unmodified guest OS and application stack; the hardware encrypts guest memory with a key the hypervisor cannot access and enforces memory-integrity policy in silicon. The promise is "lift and shift" confidentiality: any workload that runs in a VM runs, unmodified, in a confidential VM.

This thesis examines that promise critically: VM-based TEEs solve real problems — adoption friction, VM-scale memory encryption — but they reintroduce a full operating system into the TCB and inherit side channels enclave architectures partially suppressed. Our contributions are threefold. First, we provide a precise architectural account of SEV-SNP's Reverse Map Table and its page-validation protocol, the mechanism that closes the memory-remapping attacks that plagued earlier SEV generations. Second, we decompose Intel TDX's attestation pipeline, showing how it reuses the SGX quoting infrastructure to convert a locally MAC-protected `TDREPORT` into a remotely verifiable quote. Third, we synthesize the empirical security literature — ciphertext side channels, interrupt-injection attacks, performance-counter surveillance — into a comparative evaluation of both architectures against process enclaves, and we identify the open problems that must be solved before VM-based confidential computing can claim defense in depth.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 From process enclaves to confidential VMs

Intel SGX isolates regions of application memory, called enclaves, from all other software including the operating system and hypervisor. Memory encryption in SGX is paired with integrity and freshness protection via a Merkle tree over the Enclave Page Cache, at the cost of a limited protected-memory footprint (historically 128 MB of EPC) and a programming model that partitions trust at the function-call boundary. AMD's original SEV took the opposite approach: encrypt each VM's memory with a per-VM key (the VEK), managed by the on-die AMD Secure Processor (AMD-SP), while trusting the hypervisor to manage page tables honestly. SEV-ES added encrypted and integrity-protected register state (the VMSA) on VM exits, closing a direct register-inspection channel.

Both early designs left a gaping hole: the hypervisor retained full control over the *mapping* of guest memory. Attacks such as SEVered demonstrated that a malicious hypervisor could remap guest-physical pages, replay ciphertext, and exfiltrate secrets without ever touching the encryption keys — because encryption without integrity of the address translation is insufficient. Software-only integrity was judged infeasible at acceptable cost, motivating hardware integrity in the page-walk path.

### 2.2 SEV-SNP: the Reverse Map Table

SEV-SNP, introduced with AMD EPYC "Milan" (3rd generation) in 2021, adds a hardware structure called the Reverse Map Table. The RMP is indexed by system physical address and records, for every 4 KiB page, which guest (identified by ASID) owns it, whether it is private or shared, its VMPL permissions, and — critically — a *Validated* bit. On every nested-page-table walk and every guest access, the CPU consults the RMP and raises a fault if the mapping disagrees with the recorded ownership. Because only the guest can set the Validated bit (via the `PVALIDATE` instruction) and the hypervisor cannot restore it once cleared, the hypervisor loses the ability to silently remap, alias, or replay guest-private pages. SNP also introduces VM privilege levels (VMPL0–VMPL3), enabling a privileged Secure VM Service Module (SVSM) to run above the guest OS, and supports in-guest attestation requests through the `SNP_GUEST_REQUEST` interface to the AMD-SP.

### 2.3 Intel TDX: trust domains

Intel TDX, shipping on 4th-generation Xeon Scalable ("Sapphire Rapids") processors, introduces *trust domains* (TDs): hardware-isolated VMs whose private memory is encrypted with Intel Total Memory Encryption–Multi-Key (TME-MK) using a per-TD private key ID (HKID). Two nested paging structures separate private from shared memory — a secure EPT for private pages and a shared EPT for VMM-shared pages — with the guest-physical address's shared bit selecting between them. A CPU-attested software module, the TDX Module (itself measured into `MRSEAM` at load by the SEAM loader), manages TD lifecycle: build, measurement, and teardown. At launch, the module records the TD's initial contents into the `MRTD` measurement register; at runtime, the guest can extend four Runtime Measurement Registers (`RTMR[0..3]`) in a TPM-PCR-like fashion:

```
RTMR[i] = SHA384(RTMR[i] || x)
```

Attestation proceeds in two phases: the guest requests a `TDREPORT` via `TDG.MR.REPORT`, and an SGX quoting enclave verifies the report's MAC with `EVERIFYREPORT2` and countersigns it as a remotely verifiable quote.

### 2.4 Threat model

Both architectures assume a *malicious hypervisor* with full control over scheduling, interrupts, device emulation, and (on AMD) visibility into guest ciphertext, plus a physically present adversary limited by the memory-encryption boundary. Neither architecture claims to defeat microarchitectural side channels (cache timing, speculative execution) or physical interposer attacks outright; these are mitigated partially in hardware and otherwise delegated to software hardening. The verifier's job — checking attestation evidence — is assumed to run on a trusted machine with access to the vendor's root of trust.

## 3 Methodology

This thesis is an *architectural analysis* grounded in primary vendor specifications and the peer-reviewed security literature, rather than a new empirical measurement campaign. Our method has four components.

First, **specification analysis**: we trace the normative behavior of both architectures from Intel's TDX Module specifications [1][2] and AMD's SEV-SNP and SEV-TIO documentation [3], extracting the exact protocols for attestation, page-state transitions, and measurement. Second, **protocol formalization**: we restate the RMP page-validation protocol and the TDX two-phase attestation pipeline as state machines with explicit security invariants, enabling precise claims about what each mechanism does and does not guarantee. Third, **comparative evaluation**: we contrast VM-based TEEs with process enclaves along four axes — TCB size, adoption cost, attestation semantics, and side-channel exposure — using a qualitative scoring informed by published CVE analyses [7]. Fourth, **literature synthesis**: we integrate recent attack research — CipherLeaks and its generalization to ciphertext side channels [4], the Heckler interrupt-injection attack [5], and the SNPeek side-channel toolkit with its cross-platform mitigation survey [6] — to characterize the residual attack surface and the division of responsibility between vendor and application developer.

Where the literature reports measurements (e.g., CVE counts, covert-channel bandwidths, single-stepping resolution), we reproduce them with attribution; all quantitative claims in Section 5 are drawn from these published results.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The RMP page-validation protocol

The RMP is the linchpin of SEV-SNP's integrity story. Conceptually, it inverts the nested page table: while the NPT maps guest-physical addresses (GPA) to system-physical addresses (SPA) under hypervisor control, the RMP maps SPA back to the owning guest and its expected GPA, under hardware control. Every guest memory access triggers an RMP check alongside the NPT walk; disagreement raises an RMP page fault (`#PF` with an RMP error code) rather than silently serving the wrong page.

The lifecycle of a private page is governed by a small set of instructions and firmware calls:

1. **Allocation**: the hypervisor assigns a page to the guest via `RMPUPDATE`, recording the ASID, expected GPA, and page size (4 KiB or 2 MiB, the latter via `PSMASH` from a validated 2 MiB page).
2. **Validation**: the guest executes `PVALIDATE` on the page, setting the Validated bit. Only now may the guest safely use the page.
3. **Transition to shared**: either party may request conversion to shared (hypervisor-owned) state, which clears validation.
4. **Reclaim**: the hypervisor may reclaim the page, but any modification of the RMP entry clears the Validated bit irreversibly from the hypervisor's perspective — subsequent guest access faults, alerting the guest to tampering.

> **Theorem (RMP remap resistance):** Let page *p* be private to guest *G* with `Validated(p) = 1` and recorded mapping *(ASID_G, GPA_g)*. Then any hypervisor action that changes the NPT mapping of *GPA_g* to a different *SPA*, or that modifies the RMP entry of *p*, causes either an RMP fault on the next guest access to *p* or permanent loss of the Validated bit. In neither case can the hypervisor substitute attacker-chosen plaintext for the guest's data without detection.

*Proof sketch.* The CPU's page-walk hardware checks the RMP entry indexed by the resolved SPA against the accessing ASID and GPA. A remapped NPT entry resolves to an SPA whose RMP entry records a different owner or GPA, failing the check. Direct RMP modification via `RMPUPDATE` clears Validated, and the hypervisor lacks any instruction to set it; the guest's next access faults. ∎

This closes the SEVered-class remapping attacks at the hardware level. However, the theorem's scope is deliberately narrow: it says nothing about *ciphertext observability*. Because SEV-SNP encrypts each 16-byte block deterministically at a fixed physical address (AES-XTS-like with a physical-address tweak), a hypervisor that cannot remap pages can still *read* ciphertext and observe which blocks change — the ciphertext side channel analyzed in [4] and [6]. Integrity of mapping is necessary but not sufficient for confidentiality of access patterns.

### 4.2 Intel TDX attestation: from TDREPORT to quote

TDX attestation is a two-phase protocol that deliberately reuses the SGX attestation infrastructure rather than inventing a parallel PKI [1][2].

**Phase 1 — local report.** Inside the TD, software invokes `TDCALL(TDG.MR.REPORT)`, supplying a 64-byte `REPORTDATA` value (typically a hash of an ephemeral public key, binding the quote to a secure channel). The TDX Module constructs a `TDREPORT_STRUCT` containing:

- `TDINFO`: the TD's measurements (`MRTD` from build, `RTMR[0..3]` runtime extensions) and initial configuration locked at `TDH.MR.FINALIZE` (`MRCONFIGID`, `MROWNER`, `MROWNERCONFIG`, attributes);
- `TEE_TCB_INFO`: the TDX Module's own measurements (`MRSEAM`, `MRSEAMSIGNER`) and TCB SVN levels;
- a MAC over the structure computed with a CPU-resident HMAC key.

The MAC key is accessible only to the CPU; the report is therefore integrity-protected but *not remotely verifiable* — it can only be checked on the same platform.

**Phase 2 — quoting.** An SGX quoting enclave (QE), signed by Intel and running on the same host outside the TD, receives the `TDREPORT` and verifies its MAC with the new `ENCLU[EVERIFYREPORT2]` leaf. If verification passes, the QE embeds the TD's measurements into a quote and signs it with its attestation key, certified through the Intel Provisioning Certification Service (PCS) chain. The verifier then checks the certificate chain, the TCB SVN levels against revocation data, and the measurements against expected values.

The design reuses one PKI, one revocation infrastructure, and one verifier ecosystem for both enclaves and trust domains. Its cost is a dependency — TDX attestation inherits the SGX TCB, including the quoting enclave and the platform's SGX provisioning state, and TD migration invalidates locally generated reports because the HMAC key is platform-bound [2].

### 4.3 TCB minimization: what "minimal" means at VM scale

A persistent misconception holds that VM-based TEEs "minimize" the TCB the way SGX does. They do not; they *relocate* it. The table below compares the trust assumptions:

| Component | Intel SGX | AMD SEV-SNP | Intel TDX |
|---|---|---|---|
| CPU package & microcode | Trusted | Trusted | Trusted |
| Firmware RoT (AMD-SP / SEAM loader, ME) | Trusted | Trusted | Trusted |
| Attestation signer (QE / VCEK chain) | Trusted | Trusted | Trusted |
| Hypervisor / VMM | Untrusted | Untrusted | Untrusted |
| Host OS | Untrusted | Untrusted | Untrusted |
| Guest OS kernel | **N/A (not in TCB)** | **Trusted** | **Trusted** |
| Guest userspace | Partially trusted (enclave code only) | **Trusted** | **Trusted** |
| Device emulation | Untrusted | Untrusted (virtio; SEV-TIO extends trust to devices [3]) | Untrusted |

The VM-based TCB is therefore strictly larger than the enclave TCB: it includes the entire guest kernel, drivers, and runtime. TCB *minimization* in this context means something more modest — minimizing the *host-side* TCB (the hypervisor is excluded) and minimizing *new* trusted components (the TDX Module, the SVSM) — while accepting the guest stack as trusted by construction. VMPLs and the SVSM [3] allow tenants to claw back some granularity by placing security services (vTPM, key management) at VMPL0 above a less-trusted guest OS, but the default posture trusts the whole VM.

This has a direct consequence for vulnerability accounting: an empirical CVE survey found 49 CVEs touching AMD SEV(-ES/SNP) versus 9 for Intel TDX (up to June 2024), with 81% being host-to-guest attacks and over half rooted in firmware [7]. A larger TCB is a larger patch surface, and the TCB version fields in attestation reports (`CURRENT_TCB`, `REPORTED_TCB`, launch TCB) exist precisely so verifiers can enforce freshness policy.

### 4.4 Side-channel defenses and their limits

The malicious-hypervisor adversary retains powerful *observation* channels even when it cannot tamper with memory. The literature identifies four families, surveyed systematically with per-platform mitigation status in [6]:

1. **Page-level (controlled channel).** The hypervisor observes guest page faults — which pages are touched, in what order. Unmitigated on all platforms; vendors classify it out of scope, pushing mitigation (oblivious memory layouts) to developers.
2. **Cache-level.** Prime-and-probe or eviction-based observation of shared caches. Unmitigated; same delegation to software.
3. **Ciphertext visibility.** Unique to AMD's design: the hypervisor can read guest ciphertext [4]. Because encryption is deterministic per physical address, changes in ciphertext reveal changes in plaintext at 16-byte granularity — enough to break constant-time RSA/ECDSA via the VMSA page (CipherLeaks) and, generalized, to attack arbitrary guest memory. Intel TDX and ARM CCA deny the hypervisor access to private-page ciphertext entirely, so they are not vulnerable [6]. AMD's planned remedy is *ciphertext hiding* on Zen 5-class parts [6].
4. **Performance counters (HPC).** Hundreds of counter events are exposed to the hypervisor; combined with APIC-based single-stepping, attackers achieve instruction-level tracing of victim VMs (CounterSEVeillance). Partially mitigated: TDX supports trusted PMC virtualization, and AMD plans PMC virtualization for Zen 5 [6].

Active *injection* attacks complement passive observation. Heckler [5] showed that a malicious hypervisor can inject interrupts (e.g., `int 0x80`) into both SEV-SNP and TDX guests, hijacking control flow of security-sensitive code paths; the demonstrated defenses are guest-kernel patches that detect externally injected interrupts by inspecting the APIC page (TDX) or the interrupted instruction stream (SEV-SNP, since AMD also permits VMCB-based injection). Physical interposer attacks ("Battering RAM," "Wiretap") further show that deterministic memory encryption without freshness enables replay and dictionary attacks against both vendors' designs when the adversary has hardware access — a reminder that the threat model explicitly scopes out physical attackers with interposers.

The pattern is consistent: vendors harden the *tamper* channels in silicon (RMP, secure EPT, interrupt filtering) while declaring *observation* channels the application developer's problem. Confidential VMs thus provide a strong integrity floor with a confidentiality ceiling set by software discipline.

## 5 Empirical Evaluation and Proofs

We evaluate the security posture of both architectures against the claims in their specifications, using published measurements rather than new experiments.

**Attestation soundness.** The TDX two-phase protocol reduces remote attestation security to (a) the unforgeability of the CPU-resident report MAC and (b) the SGX quoting PKI. No published work breaks the MAC binding; the protocol's weakest documented link is operational — stale TCB and advisory enforcement by verifiers, and report replay across migration boundaries [2]. SEV-SNP's attestation binds launch measurement, VMPL, policy, `REPORT_DATA`, and TCB versions in a report signed by the VCEK (chip-unique, certified via AMD's KDS chain through ARK/ASK). Verification is fail-closed over this chain in production deployments.

**RMP integrity guarantees.** The page-state machine has been validated at scale in the Linux KVM implementation (RMP table management, `RMPUPDATE`/`PSMASH`/`PVALIDATE` handling, and RMP-fault delivery merged upstream). No published attack remaps or aliases a *validated* private page without triggering a fault — the precise class SEV-SNP was built to defeat. The residual, demonstrated bypasses operate *around* the RMP rather than through it: ciphertext observation [4], interrupt injection [5], and performance-counter tracing [6].

**Side-channel quantification.** SNPeek's evaluation on production SEV-SNP hardware uncovered previously unnoticed leaks in representative privacy workloads (private information retrieval, private heavy hitters, Wasm UDFs), including a covert channel exfiltrating data at 497 kbit/s, and guided low-overhead mitigations via oblivious memory and differential privacy [6]. CounterSEVeillance demonstrated single-instruction-resolution tracing on fully patched systems. These results bound what "confidential" can mean in practice: integrity is hardware-enforced; confidentiality of access patterns is not.

**CVE accounting.** The survey in [7] (49 AMD vs. 9 Intel CVEs, 81% host-to-guest) should be read as a maturity signal, not a safety ranking — SEV shipped years earlier and its firmware-heavy design (54% of CVEs in firmware) presents the larger patch surface. The trend it supports is architectural: VM-based TEEs concentrate risk in platform firmware and the guest stack, whereas enclave TEEs concentrated it in microcode and the SDK.

## 6 Limitations

This analysis has several boundaries that future work should address. First, it is *specification- and literature-based*; we performed no independent hardware measurements, so microarchitectural behaviors that vendors document opaquely (e.g., exact PMC exposure per stepping) are taken on the literature's authority. Second, the threat model excludes physical interposer attacks and supply-chain compromise of the firmware RoT — both demonstrated in practice — which bounds our positive claims to the remote-software-adversary setting. Third, TCB versioning and revocation policy, arguably the most operationally fragile part of attestation, are treated descriptively; a formal analysis of verifier policy languages remains open. Fourth, emerging extensions — SEV-TIO's device attestation [3], TDX Connect, live migration of TDs — change the trust boundary (devices, migration agents) and are covered only in passing. Finally, our comparison with SGX inherits the literature's x86 focus; ARM CCA's realm model and RISC-V's CoVE may shift the trade-offs, particularly around physical-address-space separation and delegated attestation.

---

## 7 Conclusion

VM-based confidential computing is best understood not as a stronger SGX but as a different point on the security-usability frontier. AMD SEV-SNP's Reverse Map Table and Intel TDX's secure-EPT plus module-mediated lifecycle both achieve the core goal — a malicious hypervisor can no longer silently tamper with guest memory — and both offer attestation that binds a VM's initial state to hardware-rooted keys. The price is a TCB that includes the entire guest stack and a residual side-channel surface that vendors explicitly delegate to application developers. The empirical record is unambiguous: ciphertext visibility, performance-counter tracing, page-fault observation, and interrupt injection all remain viable against production systems, mitigated only partially by planned hardware features such as ciphertext hiding and PMC virtualization.

Three research directions follow. First, *formal verification* of the RMP and secure-EPT page-state machines would turn today's proof sketches into machine-checked guarantees. Second, *attested I/O* — binding device identity into the same attestation evidence as the VM — is necessary before confidential VMs can safely consume accelerators. Third, the community needs *principled, low-overhead software mitigations* for observation channels, in the spirit of SNPeek's oblivious-memory guidance, so that "lift and shift" does not silently mean "leak and shift." Until then, confidential VMs deliver integrity you can verify and confidentiality you must still earn.
