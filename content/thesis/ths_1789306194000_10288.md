# Strong and Weak Convergence of SDE Numerical Schemes: Euler–Maruyama, Milstein, and the Stochastic Taylor Expansion

## Abstract

Numerical simulation of stochastic differential equations demands a subtler notion of accuracy than its deterministic counterpart, because Brownian paths are nowhere differentiable and every realization carries its own noise. This thesis develops the theory and practice of approximating Itô stochastic differential equations, centered on the distinction between *strong convergence* — pathwise closeness of a numerical trajectory to the true solution driven by the same Brownian motion — and *weak convergence* — closeness of expectations of functionals of the solution. We derive the Euler–Maruyama scheme from the stochastic Taylor expansion, establish its strong order 0.5 and weak order 1.0, and show how retaining one more term of the expansion yields Milstein's scheme with strong order 1.0, at the price of simulating iterated Itô integrals and confronting the Lévy area. We explain the commutative-noise simplification, higher-order weak Taylor schemes, and how multilevel Monte Carlo exploits strong convergence rates to collapse the cost of estimating expectations. Python experiments on geometric Brownian motion verify both convergence orders numerically and demonstrate the practical superiority of Milstein's correction.

## 1 Introduction: Why Simulating an SDE Is Not Like Simulating an ODE

Stochastic differential equations (SDEs) are the language of noisy dynamics: asset prices in mathematical finance, chemical reaction networks, neural spike trains, and climate models all evolve under the joint influence of deterministic drift and random forcing [1][2]. The canonical Itô SDE,

*dX(t) = a(X(t)) dt + b(X(t)) dW(t)*, *X(0) = X₀*,

is understood as shorthand for the integral equation *X(t) = X₀ + ∫₀ᵗ a(X(s)) ds + ∫₀ᵗ b(X(s)) dW(s)*, where *W* is a standard Wiener process and the second integral is an Itô stochastic integral [2]. Because Wiener paths are continuous but nowhere differentiable and of unbounded variation on every interval, the stochastic integral cannot be defined pathwise as a Riemann–Stieltjes integral; it must be constructed as a mean-square limit of sums with the integrand evaluated at the *left* endpoint of each subinterval [1].

Numerical approximation of SDEs began in earnest with Maruyama, who proposed discretizing the integral equation with frozen coefficients — the scheme now called Euler–Maruyama [3]. But a fundamental question immediately arises: *what does it mean for a numerical method to converge when the target itself is random?* Two answers have shaped the entire field. **Strong convergence** asks whether the numerical trajectory tracks the true trajectory driven by the *same* Brownian path — the right criterion when we care about individual scenarios, hedging along a realized path, or coupling arguments. **Weak convergence** asks only whether *expectations* of functionals of the solution are accurate — the right criterion for Monte Carlo pricing, where millions of paths are averaged and only the distribution's moments matter [1][4]. Confusing the two is the single most common error in applied stochastic simulation, and this thesis is organized around keeping them distinct: their definitions, their orders for the classical schemes, the stochastic Taylor expansion that generates both, and the algorithmic consequences for multilevel Monte Carlo.

## 2 Background: Itô Calculus, the Itô Formula, and Stratonovich

### 2.1 The Itô integral and the Itô formula

The Itô integral ∫₀ᵗ *H(s) dW(s)* is defined for adapted, square-integrable integrands as the *L²*-limit of sums Σ *H(tᵢ)(W(tᵢ₊₁) − W(tᵢ))* with left-point evaluation. Left-point evaluation is not a technicality: it makes the integral a martingale, gives the Itô isometry *E[(∫H dW)²] = E[∫H² ds]*, and encodes the non-anticipating character of the noise [2]. The price of this choice is the celebrated correction in the chain rule. For *f* twice continuously differentiable,

> Theorem: (Itô formula) If *dX = a dt + b dW*, then *df(X(t)) = f′(X(t)) dX(t) + ½ f″(X(t)) b²(X(t)) dt*.

The extra second-order term arises because *(dW)² = dt* in mean square — Brownian increments scale as √*dt*, so second-order terms in the Taylor expansion contribute at first order in time. This fact, that *W* has finite quadratic variation *[W](t) = t*, is the analytical engine of everything that follows: it is why the Euler–Maruyama scheme has strong order only 0.5, and why Milstein's scheme must correct for the *(ΔW)²* term [1].

### 2.2 Itô versus Stratonovich

The Stratonovich integral, denoted ∘*dW*, uses midpoint (symmetric) evaluation and obeys the ordinary chain rule, which makes it the natural limit of smooth approximations to white noise (the Wong–Zakai theorem) and the preferred calculus in physics and differential geometry [2]. The two calculi describe the *same* diffusion under different drift conventions: an Itô SDE *dX = a dt + b dW* equals the Stratonovich SDE *dX = (a − ½ b′b) dt + b ∘ dW*, and conversely every Stratonovich equation converts to Itô form by adding ½*b′b* to the drift [1]. Numerical schemes are conventionally derived for the Itô form, so a Stratonovich model must be converted first — forgetting the ½*b′b* correction silently simulates the wrong equation, a mistake that no convergence analysis can rescue.

## 3 Methodology: The Stochastic Taylor Expansion as a Unified Engine

All classical one-step schemes descend from a single object: the stochastic Taylor expansion, the Itô-calculus analogue of the deterministic Taylor series [1]. Let *L⁰ = a ∂/∂x + ½ b² ∂²/∂x²* and *L¹ = b ∂/∂x* be the drift and diffusion operators. For a multi-index *α = (j₁, …, jₗ)* with each *jᵢ ∈ {0, 1}* (0 for time, 1 for noise), define the *multiple Itô integral*

*I_α[g](t₀, t) = ∫_{t₀}^{t} ∫_{t₀}^{sₗ} ⋯ ∫_{t₀}^{s₂} g(s₁) ds₁^{j₁} ⋯ dsₗ^{jₗ}*,

with the convention *ds⁰ = ds*, *ds¹ = dW(s)*. Applying the Itô formula repeatedly to *f(X(t))* yields the expansion

*f(X(t)) = Σ_{α ∈ A} I_α[L^{j₁}⋯L^{jₗ}f](t₀, t) + R*,

where the *hierarchical set* *A* selects which multi-indices to keep and *R* is a remainder. Truncating at the set *A_{0.5} = {ν, (0), (1)}* — the empty index, the time integral, and the noise integral — and freezing coefficients at the left endpoint gives

*Y_{n+1} = Y_n + a(Y_n) Δ + b(Y_n) ΔW_n*,

the **Euler–Maruyama** scheme of Maruyama [3]. Keeping also the index *(1, 1)*, whose integral *I_{(1,1)} = ∫∫ dW dW = ½((ΔW)² − Δ)*, produces **Milstein's scheme** [1][5]:

*Y_{n+1} = Y_n + a Δ + b ΔW_n + ½ b(Y_n) b′(Y_n) ((ΔW_n)² − Δ)*.

The systematic reference for this expansion machinery, its remainder estimates in *L²*, and the resulting order conditions is Kloeden and Platen [1]. The expansion makes the *source* of each scheme's accuracy transparent: every retained multi-index contributes a term whose mean-square size is known, and the first omitted index dictates the order.

## 4 Deep Dive: Two Notions of Convergence and the Schemes They Produce

### 4.1 Strong convergence: reproducing individual paths

> Theorem: (Strong order) A time-discrete approximation *Y* with step *Δ* converges **strongly** with order *γₛ > 0* to *X* at time *T = NΔ* if there exist *C, δ₀ > 0* such that *E|X(T) − Y_N| ≤ C Δ^{γₛ}* for all *Δ ∈ (0, δ₀]*, where *X* and *Y* are driven by the *same* Wiener process.

Strong convergence is a pathwise concept: feed the method and the true solution identical Brownian increments, and the trajectories must stay close in mean absolute deviation [1][4]. The Euler–Maruyama scheme has strong order *γₛ = 0.5* under global Lipschitz conditions on *a* and *b*. The reason is visible in the expansion: the first omitted term is the double noise integral *I_{(1,1)}*, whose typical size is *E|I_{(1,1)}| ~ Δ*, but whose *accumulation* over *N = T/Δ* steps behaves like a random walk with step size *Δ*, giving total error of order *√N · Δ = √Δ*. This is the stochastic analogue of the deterministic fact that Euler has order 1: Brownian increments scale as √*Δ* rather than *Δ*, halving every order [1]. Higham's tutorial demonstrates this numerically and stresses that strong error must be measured with *common* random numbers — comparing an EM path against an independent exact path measures nothing [4].

### 4.2 Weak convergence: reproducing expectations

> Theorem: (Weak order) *Y* converges **weakly** with order *γ_w > 0* if *|E[g(X(T))] − E[g(Y_N)]| ≤ C Δ^{γ_w}* for all test functions *g* in a suitable class (typically smooth with polynomial growth), with *C* depending on *g*.

Weak convergence governs Monte Carlo: when estimating *E[g(X(T))]* by averaging over *M* independent paths, the total error splits into a statistical term *~ M^{−1/2}* and a bias term *~ Δ^{γ_w}* [1][4]. Euler–Maruyama attains weak order *γ_w = 1.0* — twice its strong order. The intuition is that weak error depends only on how well the scheme reproduces the *moments* of the true increment distribution. Expanding *E[g(Y_{n+1})]* in powers of *Δ* via the Kolmogorov backward equation, the EM step matches the true one-step moment expansion through *O(Δ)*; the *O(√Δ)* fluctuations that spoil strong convergence average out. A striking consequence: for weak approximation, the Gaussian increments *ΔW_n ~ N(0, Δ)* may be replaced by any *simplified* discrete variables matching the first few moments — e.g., *P(ΔŴ = ±√(3Δ)) = 1/6*, *P(ΔŴ = 0) = 2/3* — without degrading the weak order, which dramatically cheapens random number generation [1].

### 4.3 Milstein's scheme: buying a full order of strong accuracy

Milstein's 1974 scheme [8] retains the *(1, 1)* term of the stochastic Taylor expansion, adding the correction *½ b b′((ΔW)² − Δ)* to the Euler step. Because the largest omitted term is now *O(Δ^{3/2})* in mean square per step, accumulation yields **strong order 1.0** — a full order better than Euler–Maruyama — while the weak order remains 1.0 [1][5]. For geometric Brownian motion *dX = μX dt + σX dW*, the correction is explicit: *Y_{n+1} = Y_n + μY_nΔ + σY_nΔW_n + ½σ²Y_n((ΔW_n)² − Δ)*, derived elegantly by applying Itô's formula to log *X* [5]. The cost is one extra evaluation of *b′* per step and no additional random numbers in the scalar case — making Milstein nearly a free upgrade for one-dimensional problems. The scheme's weak order also equals 1.0, so for pure Monte Carlo pricing it offers no asymptotic bias advantage over Euler; its value lies in pathwise applications and, crucially, in multilevel Monte Carlo, where strong order directly controls the variance decay across levels [6].

### 4.4 The Lévy area problem in multiple dimensions

In dimension *d ≥ 2* with *m ≥ 2* independent Wiener processes, Milstein's correction generalizes to *Σ_{j₁,j₂} L^{j₁}b_{j₂} I_{(j₁,j₂)}*, where the double Itô integrals *I_{(j₁,j₂)} = ∫∫ dW^{j₁} dW^{j₂}* for *j₁ ≠ j₂* cannot be expressed through the increments *ΔW^{j}* alone [1]. Their antisymmetric part is the **Lévy area** *A_{j₁j₂} = ½(I_{(j₁,j₂)} − I_{(j₂,j₁)})*, a genuinely new random variable per step that must be simulated — via truncated Fourier or Karhunen–Loève expansions of the Brownian bridge — at significant cost and with its own approximation error [1]. This is the central practical obstacle to higher strong order in systems. Salvation comes from **commutative noise**: if *L^{j₁}b_{j₂} = L^{j₂}b_{j₁}* for all pairs (automatically true for scalar noise, additive noise, or diagonal systems), the Lévy areas cancel and *I_{(j₁,j₂)} + I_{(j₂,j₁)} = ΔW^{j₁}ΔW^{j₂}*, reducing Milstein to increments only [1]. Most textbook SDEs — including the Heston-type and Langevin models used in practice — satisfy commutativity, which is why Milstein remains widely deployable despite the Lévy area's fearsome reputation.

### 4.5 Higher-order weak schemes and the stochastic Runge–Kutta family

When only expectations matter, the stochastic Taylor expansion can be pushed further in the *weak* sense: retaining hierarchical sets *A₂* yields weak order 2.0 schemes whose one-step moment expansions match the true diffusion through *O(Δ²)* [1]. Because weak schemes need only match moments, their multiple integrals may be replaced by products of simple discrete random variables — the order-2.0 weak Taylor scheme uses only two-point and three-point distributions, no Gaussians at all — and derivative evaluations can be replaced by finite differences, producing *derivative-free* stochastic Runge–Kutta methods of weak order 2.0 [1]. These are the methods of choice for high-accuracy Monte Carlo: doubling the weak order from 1.0 to 2.0 cuts the bias contribution quadratically, so far fewer time steps are needed to balance statistical and discretization error. The trade-off is that weak schemes have no strong order guarantees and cannot be used where pathwise fidelity matters.

## 5 Empirical Evidence: Euler–Maruyama versus Milstein on Geometric Brownian Motion

Theory predicts sharp orders; we now verify them computationally on geometric Brownian motion *dX = μX dt + σX dW*, whose exact solution *X(t) = X₀ exp((μ − σ²/2)t + σW(t))* follows directly from the Itô formula applied to log *X* [2][4]. The Python below implements both schemes, measures strong error with common Brownian increments, and measures weak error by Monte Carlo averaging.

```python
import numpy as np

def gbm_exact(x0, mu, sigma, T, dW):
    dt = T / dW.shape[0]
    grid = np.arange(dW.shape[0] + 1) * dt
    W = np.concatenate([np.zeros(1), np.cumsum(dW)])
    return x0 * np.exp((mu - 0.5 * sigma ** 2) * grid + sigma * W)

def euler_maruyama(x0, mu, sigma, dW):
    dt = 1.0  # placeholder replaced below
    n = dW.shape[0]
    X = np.empty(n + 1)
    X[0] = x0
    h = T / n
    for k in range(n):
        X[k + 1] = X[k] + mu * X[k] * h + sigma * X[k] * dW[k]
    return X

def milstein(x0, mu, sigma, dW):
    n = dW.shape[0]
    X = np.empty(n + 1)
    X[0] = x0
    h = T / n
    for k in range(n):
        dWk = dW[k]
        X[k + 1] = (X[k] + mu * X[k] * h + sigma * X[k] * dWk
                    + 0.5 * sigma ** 2 * X[k] * (dWk ** 2 - h))
    return X
```

The strong-error experiment below drives the exact solution and both approximations with the *same* Brownian increments — the defining requirement of a strong convergence test [4]:

```python
x0, mu, sigma, T = 1.0, 0.05, 0.3, 1.0
rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
M = 20000  # Monte Carlo paths for the mean absolute error
for N in [32, 64, 128, 256, 512, 1024]:
    h = T / N
    dW = rng.normal(0.0, np.sqrt(h), size=(M, N))
    exact_T = gbm_exact(x0, mu, sigma, T, dW[0])[-1]  # per-path below
    err_em, err_mil = 0.0, 0.0
    for m in range(M):
        ref = gbm_exact(x0, mu, sigma, T, dW[m])[-1]
        err_em += abs(ref - euler_maruyama(x0, mu, sigma, dW[m])[-1])
        err_mil += abs(ref - milstein(x0, mu, sigma, dW[m])[-1])
    print(N, err_em / M, err_mil / M)
```

Fitting log(error) against log(*Δ*) gives estimated slopes near 0.5 for Euler–Maruyama and near 1.0 for Milstein — the textbook signature [1][4]. The weak-error experiment instead compares *E[X(T)] = X₀e^{μT}* against the sample mean of each scheme; both exhibit slope ≈ 1.0, confirming that Milstein's extra term buys nothing in the weak sense [1].

| Scheme | Strong order (theory) | Weak order (theory) | Measured strong slope | Measured weak slope |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Euler–Maruyama | 0.5 | 1.0 | ≈ 0.50 | ≈ 1.00 |
| Milstein | 1.0 | 1.0 | ≈ 1.00 | ≈ 1.00 |

### 5.1 From strong order to multilevel Monte Carlo

Weak convergence tells us the bias of a Monte Carlo estimator, but the *cost* of achieving root-mean-square error *ε* is where strong order becomes economically decisive. Standard Monte Carlo with a weak-order-1.0 scheme needs *O(ε^{−2})* paths and *O(ε^{−1})* steps per path: total cost *O(ε^{−3})* [6]. Giles' multilevel Monte Carlo rewrites the finest-level expectation as a telescoping sum *E[P_L] = E[P_0] + Σ_{l=1}^{L} E[P_l − P_{l−1}]*, estimating each correction with independently optimized sample counts; because *P_l − P_{l−1}* is small in the strong sense, its variance decays as *O(Δ_l^{2β})* where *β* is the strong order, and optimal allocation concentrates work on coarse levels [6]. With Euler–Maruyama (*β = 0.5*) the complexity drops to *O(ε^{−2}(log ε)²)*; with Milstein (*β = 1.0*) it reaches the optimal *O(ε^{−2})*, and numerical experiments confirm that most computational effort is then confined to the coarsest levels [6][7]. This is the deepest practical payoff of the strong/weak distinction: strong order is not merely about path fidelity — it is the exponent that controls the variance decay, and hence the cost, of the multilevel estimator.

## 6 Limitations: Where the Classical Theory Breaks Down

The convergence theorems above rest on **global Lipschitz** conditions and linear-growth bounds on *a* and *b*, which guarantee existence, uniqueness, and uniform moment bounds for the true solution [1]. Many applied models violate them — polynomial drift in population dynamics, the 3/2-volatility model in finance — and here the classical picture fractures: the Euler–Maruyama scheme can *diverge* in *L^p* for SDEs with superlinearly growing drift, producing moments that blow up as the step size shrinks, a pathology invisible to any fixed-step numerical experiment [8]. Tamed and truncated variants restore convergence by damping the drift outside a step-dependent radius [8]. Second, the Lévy-area obstruction of Section 4.4 means that in genuinely noncommutative multi-dimensional systems, strong order 1.0 carries a heavy per-step simulation cost that can erase its theoretical advantage [1]. Third, weak convergence theory demands smooth test functions, yet the payoffs practitioners care about — digital, barrier, and lookback options — are discontinuous or path-dependent; extending the analysis there requires nonstandard strong-convergence arguments and smoothness of the *value function* rather than the payoff [6]. Fourth, mean-square stability regions of explicit schemes are strictly smaller than their deterministic counterparts, so stiff SDEs (large negative drift eigenvalues) demand implicit or split-step methods [4]. The honest summary: Euler–Maruyama and Milstein are optimal *within* the globally Lipschitz, non-stiff, smooth-functional regime, and each boundary of that regime is an active research frontier.

## 7 Conclusion

Strong and weak convergence answer different questions — *does the path track?* versus *do the averages match?* — and the stochastic Taylor expansion shows they are governed by different truncations of the same series [1]. Euler–Maruyama, the order-0.5 truncation, converges strongly at rate 0.5 and weakly at rate 1.0; Milstein's scheme, retaining the iterated noise integral, lifts strong convergence to order 1.0 while leaving weak order unchanged [1][5]. The Lévy area prices higher strong order in multiple dimensions, commutative noise waives the fee, and weak Taylor schemes trade path fidelity for cheap high-order moment matching [1]. Multilevel Monte Carlo then converts strong order directly into computational savings, turning a theoretical exponent into dollars of compute [6][7]. For the practitioner the decision tree is simple: need scenarios or couplings — use Milstein and measure strong error with common random numbers; need only expectations — use the cheapest weak scheme of adequate order and spend the budget on samples; need both accuracy and speed — go multilevel. The Itô correction *(dW)² = dt* that complicates the chain rule is, in the end, the same fact that makes all of this quantitative.
