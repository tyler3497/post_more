# Authenticated Dictionaries from Patricia Tries to Verkle Tries: Vector Commitments and Stateless Validation

## Abstract

An authenticated dictionary lets a client holding only a short digest verify individual key–value pairs served by an untrusted prover. For a decade, Ethereum's state has been the largest deployed authenticated dictionary: a hexary Merkle–Patricia trie whose inclusion witnesses grow with tree depth and the number of touched accounts, reaching tens of megabytes per block and making stateless validation infeasible. This thesis traces the route from Merkle hashing through vector commitments to Verkle tries, where each internal node is a *vector commitment* over its children rather than a *hash* of them. We formalize the Catalano–Fiore primitive of position binding and conciseness, derive Kuszmaul's bandwidth–computation tradeoff of O(kn) construction for O(log_k n) proofs, examine polynomial commitments — from Kate–Zaverucha–Goldberg pairings to inner-product arguments — as concrete instantiations, and dissect Ethereum's EIP-6800 design: 256-ary width, Pedersen commitments over the Bandersnatch curve, and stem-and-suffix key embedding, cutting average witnesses to roughly 200 bytes per account. We survey benchmark evidence for stateless clients, sketch the security reductions, and isolate the open problems that remain: prover cost, setup assumptions, and post-quantum migration.

## 1. Introduction

The authenticated dictionary is one of the oldest problems in applied cryptography. A prover commits to a map `K -> V` with a short digest `d`; a verifier holding only `d` must be able to confirm that the pair `(k, v)` it receives from an untrusted party genuinely belongs to the committed map, or to confirm that a key is *absent*. Merkle trees solved the integrity half of this problem in the 1970s, and Patricia tries — bitwise tries with path compression — made the structure a practical dictionary for sparse key spaces. Ethereum combined the two into the Merkle–Patricia trie (MPT), the authenticated data structure underpinning every account balance, contract store, and transaction receipt in the network's state.

Yet the MPT's success exposed a structural ceiling. An inclusion proof in a hexary MPT must ship every sibling hash along the path from leaf to root, so witness size scales as the product of the block's state accesses, the branching factor, and the tree depth. When Kuszmaul introduced Verkle trees in 2018, he observed that the sibling data is not intrinsic to authentication at all: it is an artifact of using hash functions at internal nodes [1]. Replace the hash with a *vector commitment* — a primitive with constant-size position openings, formalized by Catalano and Fiore [2] — and the siblings vanish from the witness. The parent commits to all `k` children at once, and a single constant-size opening certifies any one of them.

This thesis reconstructs that intellectual arc end to end. We begin with the formal definition of vector commitments and the two great instantiation families — pairing-based polynomial commitments [3] and discrete-logarithm inner-product arguments — then show how Kuszmaul's construction turns them into a tree with `O(kn)` build time and `O(log_k n)` proof size. We then descend into Ethereum's concrete realization, EIP-6800, which pairs 256-ary branching with Pedersen commitments over the Bandersnatch curve and a stem-and-suffix embedding of the entire state into a single key space [4][5]. Finally we examine what all of this is *for*: stateless clients that validate blocks from witnesses alone, evaluated against binary-Merkle alternatives with SNARK compression [6].

## 2. Background

### Authenticated dictionaries before vector commitments

A Merkle tree authenticates an ordered list of `n` leaves with membership proofs of size `O(log_2 n)`: the verifier recomputes hashes up the path using one sibling per level and compares against the root. The Patricia trie adds dictionary semantics — keys are bit strings, and nodes branch on key prefixes — plus *extension nodes* that compress runs of single-child paths so that sparse key spaces do not drown in empty branches. Ethereum's MPT is hexary (16-way) rather than binary, trading wider proofs for shallower depth: each nibble of the key selects one of 16 children.

The MPT's economics, however, are unforgiving at scale. EIP-6800's motivation section quantifies the problem precisely: a witness touching a single account averages close to 3 kB in the hexary trie, and under a 15-million-gas block with 6,000 worst-case accesses the aggregate witness approaches 18 MB — far beyond what can be gossiped through a peer-to-peer network inside a 12-second slot [4]. The dominant term is `O(T · B · k · log_k N)`, where `T` is transactions per block, `B` the node size, `k` the arity, and `N` the account count: the witness is *linear in the branching factor* because every sibling must travel the wire [6].

### Vector commitments, concisely

Catalano and Fiore defined a vector commitment (VC) as a commitment to an ordered sequence `(m_1, ..., m_q)` such that the commitment string and any position opening are *concise* — independent of `q` — while *position binding* guarantees no adversary can open the same position to two different values [2]. Their paper gives two realizations, from RSA and from the computational Diffie–Hellman assumption in bilinear groups, and applies them to verifiable databases with efficient updates, updatable zero-knowledge databases, and universal dynamic accumulators.

Polynomial commitments are the special case that matters most for Verkle trees. Kate, Zaverucha, and Goldberg showed that a polynomial `p(x)` can be committed as a *single group element* `C = g^{p(τ)}` for secret `τ`, with evaluation proofs of constant size: to prove `p(z) = v`, the prover commits to the quotient `q(x) = (p(x) − v)/(x − z)` as `π = g^{q(τ)}`, and the verifier checks one pairing equation [3]:

```
e(C − v·G, G2) = e(π, τ·G2 − z·G2)
```

The price is a *structured reference string* — the "powers of τ" — produced by a trusted ceremony. The inner-product-argument (IPA) family avoids the trusted setup entirely at the cost of logarithmic proof size and linear verification, a tradeoff Ethereum's designers ultimately preferred for state [5].

![KZG polynomial commitment: commit, prove, verify](public/thesis/ths_1789007479491_22287-1.webp)
*The Kate–Zaverucha–Goldberg pipeline: commit to the polynomial as one group element, open an evaluation with a quotient commitment, verify with a single pairing equation.*

### The stateless-client imperative

A *stateless client* validates blocks without storing the state trie: it receives each block together with a *witness* containing the pre-state values and the proofs needed to check them, executes the transactions, and verifies that the resulting post-state root matches the header. The witness is the bandwidth price of statelessness, and under the MPT that price is denominated in megabytes. Everything that follows — Verkle trees, wider tries, SNARK-compressed witnesses — is an attempt to redenominate it in kilobytes [6].

## 3. Methodology

Our analysis proceeds in three layers.

**Layer 1 — Primitive.** We take the Catalano–Fiore syntax `(Setup, Commit, Open, Verify)` as the axiomatic base [2]: a vector commitment scheme is *correct* if honest openings verify, *position-binding* if no PPT adversary opens one position to two values, and *concise* if commitment and opening sizes are `O(1)` in the vector length. We instantiate it twice — with KZG pairings for the theory [3], and with Pedersen commitments plus IPA-style aggregation for the Ethereum design [5] — and keep the two instantiations cleanly separated, since their trust assumptions differ.

**Layer 2 — Construction.** Following Kuszmaul, we build the Verkle tree exactly as a Merkle tree but with the internal hash replaced by a VC [1]: leaves are grouped in chunks of `k`, each chunk becomes a vector commitment `C_i` with position openings for its members, and the process recurses over the commitments until a single root commitment remains. Membership proofs concatenate one constant-size opening per level, yielding `O(log_k n)` proof size, while construction touches each element once per level of VC computation, yielding `O(kn)` build time.

**Layer 3 — Instantiation and measurement.** We map the abstract construction onto EIP-6800's concrete choices — 256-ary nodes, Bandersnatch Pedersen basis, the `stem || suffix` key layout, and the extension-and-suffix subtree — and evaluate the design against published benchmark evidence: Kuszmaul's original measurements and recent stateless-client comparisons of Verkle witnesses against binary Merkle trees with SNARK compression [4][6]. Security is argued by reduction: position binding of the tree reduces to position binding of the underlying VC, level by level.

```haskell
-- The Catalano–Fiore vector commitment interface, abstractly
data VC v = VC
  { vcSetup  :: SecurityParam -> IO PublicParams
  , vcCommit :: PublicParams -> Vector v -> IO (Commitment, OpenState)
  , vcOpen   :: OpenState -> Position -> IO (v, OpeningProof)
  , vcVerify :: PublicParams -> Commitment -> Position -> v -> OpeningProof -> Bool
  }
-- Conciseness: |Commitment|, |OpeningProof| = O(1) in |Vector v|
-- Position binding: no adversary opens one Position to two distinct v.
```

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 The authenticated dictionary, formalized

An authenticated dictionary scheme is a tuple `(Gen, Put, Get, Prove, Verify)`: `Put` updates the digest, `Prove(k)` returns an inclusion or exclusion proof, and `Verify(d, k, v, π)` accepts iff `(k, v)` is consistent with the committed map. *Key addressing* and *authentication* are orthogonal concerns — and the Verkle insight is that only the second one needs changing.

> **Theorem:** Let `VC` be a position-binding, concise vector commitment scheme. Then the `k`-ary Verkle tree built over `VC` is a secure authenticated dictionary with proof size `O(log_k n)` and update time `O(k · log_k n)`.

*Proof sketch.* Induction on tree height. Position binding at each internal node means the adversary cannot substitute a child commitment; conciseness means each level contributes `O(1)` to the proof. An update recomputes the `k`-wide commitment on the path, one level at a time. ∎

The theorem's real content is what it *omits*: no sibling data appears anywhere. In a `k`-ary Merkle tree, the proof carries `k − 1` siblings per level — `O(k log_k n)` — which is why widening a Merkle tree makes proofs *larger*, not smaller. The vector commitment breaks that coupling [1].

### 4.2 Kuszmaul's tradeoff, quantified

The complexity table from the original paper remains the clearest statement of the design space [1]:

| Scheme | Construct | Update | Proof size |
|---|---|---|---|
| Binary Merkle tree | `O(n)` | `O(log_2 n)` | `O(log_2 n)` |
| `k`-ary Merkle tree | `O(n)` | `O(k log_k n)` | `O(k log_k n)` |
| Vector commitment (flat) | `O(n²)` | `O(n)` | `O(1)` |
| `k`-ary Verkle tree | `O(kn)` | `O(k log_k n)` | `O(log_k n)` |

The flat VC gives constant proofs but quadratic construction — unusable at blockchain scale. The Verkle tree interpolates: `k` becomes a knob trading prover work against bandwidth, and the bandwidth win is *depth-independent*, a factor of `log_2 k` over binary Merkle proofs. Kuszmaul's measurements on trees of `2^14` leaves made the tradeoff concrete: at `k = 1024`, a 10× bandwidth reduction cost 110.1 ms per leaf to construct; at `k = 32`, a 5× reduction cost 8.4 ms per leaf [1]. His conclusion — bandwidth costs far more than computation in these applications — underpins the entire statelessness program.

### 4.3 From KZG to the IPA: choosing the inner commitment

KZG is the theorist's ideal VC: 48-byte commitments and proofs on BLS12-381 with constant-time verification [3]. But the structured reference string is a governance problem for a base-layer protocol: whoever knows `τ` can forge openings, so the ceremony must be trusted and its toxic waste verifiably destroyed.

Ethereum's designers chose differently. EIP-6800 specifies each internal node as a **Pedersen vector commitment** over the Bandersnatch curve:

```python
# EIP-6800 core: a width-256 Pedersen commitment over Bandersnatch
VERKLE_NODE_WIDTH = 256  # len(PEDERSEN_BASIS)

def compute_commitment_root(children):
    acc = bandersnatch.Z          # identity point
    for generator, child in zip(PEDERSEN_BASIS, children):
        acc = bandersnatch.add(acc, bandersnatch.mul(generator, child))
    return acc                    # C = Σ child_i · G_i
```

Pedersen commitments need no secret: the basis `G_i` is a fixed, verifiable set of curve points, so there is no toxic waste and no ceremony. Position openings and multiproofs are then handled with inner-product arguments, whose proofs are logarithmic rather than constant but require no trusted setup [5].

### 4.4 EIP-6800: the Verkle tree as Ethereum's state

The EIP replaces the two-layer MPT with a *single* 32-byte-key/32-byte-value Verkle tree [4], resting on three design decisions:

1. **Width 256.** Nodes commit to 256 children, so a 32-byte key addresses depth-4 paths with room to spare; the tree is dramatically shallower than the hexary MPT. 2. **Stem-and-suffix keys.** Each 32-byte key splits into a 31-byte *stem* shared by up to 256 related values and a 1-byte *suffix*. Values sharing a stem live under an *extension-and-suffix tree* of two width-256 commitments over the 512 half-values, so related state clusters inside one multiproof. 3. Account headers pack `version`, `code_size`, `nonce`, and `balance` into one 32-byte leaf; contract bytecode is chunked into 31-byte slices; storage slots near the account sit adjacent to it, while the rest scatter under `MAIN_STORAGE_OFFSET = 256^31` [4]. The embedding is engineered so that the values a transaction touches together *prove* together. Notably, the EIP currently carries **stagnant** status: after the hard fork it describes, the Verkle tree would absorb all new state while the MPT freezes [4]. Whether the network completes that migration or pivots to SNARK-compressed binary trees remains an open governance question [6]. ### 4.5 Witnesses, multiproofs, and the stateless execution model

The payoff of the vector-commitment design appears in the witness. Where the MPT witness repeats sibling hashes per access, the Verkle witness carries, per internal node, only the commitments on the accessed paths — and then a **multiproof** compresses all of the block's openings into one: the IPA's homomorphic structure lets the prover aggregate hundreds of position openings across many nodes into a single logarithmic-size argument. The EIP's headline number follows: ~200 bytes per account in the average case, versus ~3 kB under the MPT [4].

![Stateless validation: block plus witness, no local state](public/thesis/ths_1789007479491_22287-2.webp)
*A stateless node verifies an execution witness — values plus multiproof — against the state root, executing the block with no local state database.*

The stateless execution model is then straightforward: the block producer assembles the witness, validators verify the multiproof against the parent state root, execute the transactions against the witnessed pre-state, and check the resulting root against the header. Recent benchmarking work consolidates this picture and compares it against the main alternative — a unified binary Merkle tree whose `O(T)`-sized witnesses are achieved with constant-size SNARKs — finding the Verkle path more practical for proving and verification latency today [6].

## 5. Empirical Results and Proofs

**Asymptotics, proved.** Kuszmaul's analysis establishes the table in §4.2 by a direct accounting argument [1]: each of the `log_k n` levels contributes one constant-size VC opening to the proof (`O(log_k n)` total), and building the tree computes one `k`-wide VC per internal node over `n` leaves (`O(kn)` total). The bandwidth reduction factor over binary Merkle proofs is exactly `log_2 k`, independent of depth — the paper's central quantitative claim.

**Original measurements.** On `2^14`-leaf trees, `k = 1024` delivered the predicted 10× bandwidth reduction at 110.1 ms average construction time per leaf; `k = 32` delivered 5× at 8.4 ms per leaf [1]. Because asymptotics already dominate at that scale, the author argues these figures are representative of larger trees — a claim Ethereum's designers implicitly accepted when they fixed `k = 256` (an 8× reduction over binary proofs at moderate prover cost).

**Ethereum-scale estimates.** EIP-6800's motivation section supplies the numbers that justify the migration: ~3 kB average (up to ~9 kB worst-case) per account access in the hexary MPT, ~18 MB worst-case block witnesses, against ~200 bytes per account under the Verkle design — roughly a 15× average-case improvement before multiproof aggregation [4]. The same document notes that the witness-gas schedule must price the residual worst case (adversarial addresses sharing long key prefixes), since the tree's security is average-case over key distribution [5].

**Comparative benchmarks.** Oberst et al. benchmark Verkle witnesses against binary Merkle trees with SNARK-compressed witnesses for the stateless-client use case, consolidating the theoretical estimates (`O(TBk log_k N)` naive witness complexity) with implementation measurements, and report Verkle trees as the more practical option for proving and verification latency [6]. This is the evidence base on which the current roadmap debate — Verkle migration versus SNARK-compressed binary trees — actually rests.

**Security reduction sketch.** Position binding of the full tree reduces to position binding of one VC invocation: any adversary that opens a tree position `(path, value)` to a second value `value' ≠ value` must, at the first level where the two openings diverge, break the binding of that level's commitment. With Pedersen commitments this is the discrete-logarithm assumption on Bandersnatch; the IPA multiproof adds only a soundness error negligible in the challenge space [2][5]. The reduction is tight in the number of levels — `log_k n`, hence small.

## 6. Limitations and Open Problems

**Prover cost is real.** `O(kn)` construction with `k = 256` means every state update performs hundreds of elliptic-curve scalar multiplications along the path. Block producers — who must rebuild witnesses for every block — absorb this cost continuously, and the multiscalar multiplications set a floor on proving latency. Kuszmaul's 8.4 ms/leaf at `k = 32` was measured on 2018 hardware with a CDH-based VC; production Pedersen/IPA proving at `k = 256` is heavier per leaf, and the community still lacks a published, reproducible end-to-end benchmark of witness *generation* (as opposed to verification) at mainnet state sizes.

**Assumptions are not minimal.** The deployed design rests on the discrete logarithm problem in the Bandersnatch group. This is a standard, well-studied assumption — but it is not post-quantum, and unlike hash-based Merkle proofs it cannot be swapped out without changing the tree's algebraic structure. A migration to lattice- or hash-based vector commitments would change proof sizes from constant to logarithmic-in-dimension and is an open research problem with no deployment-ready candidate.

**Worst-case witnesses and grinding.** The ~200-byte average witness assumes keys are well distributed. An adversary can grind addresses sharing long prefixes, forcing deeper shared paths and larger witnesses; the gas schedule must price this [5]. The analysis of optimal grinding strategies against the 31-byte stem space remains incomplete.

**Governance and the roadmap.** EIP-6800's stagnant status reflects a genuine strategic fork: continue the multi-phase Verkle migration, or leapfrog to binary Merkle trees with SNARK-compressed witnesses [6]. The two paths share the stateless-client goal but differ in cryptographic assumptions, client complexity, and migration risk. Until the network commits, client teams must maintain both code paths.

**Updates and the flat-VC alternative.** The tree exists because flat vector commitments cost `O(n²)` to build [1]; but subvector commitments with optimal opening complexity are an active research area, and a future VC with `O(n log n)` construction and updatable openings could collapse the tree entirely. The tree is an engineering compromise around the prover complexity of 2013-era VCs — it may not be the final one.

---

## 7. Conclusion

The Verkle tree is best understood not as a new data structure but as a *substitution*: take the Merkle tree, replace the hash at each internal node with a vector commitment, and watch the siblings — and with them, the bandwidth bottleneck of stateless validation — disappear from the witness. Kuszmaul's 2018 paper supplied the asymptotics and the first measurements [1]; Catalano and Fiore supplied the primitive's definition and security notions [2]; Kate, Zaverucha, and Goldberg supplied the pairing-based instantiation that showed constant-size openings were practical [3]; and Ethereum's EIP-6800 translated the whole stack into a concrete state design — 256-ary Pedersen commitments over Bandersnatch, stem-and-suffix key embedding, multiproof-aggregated witnesses at ~200 bytes per account [4][5].

Whether that design ships as specified or yields to SNARK-compressed binary trees [6], the conceptual debt is settled: authenticated dictionaries no longer need to choose between hash-based efficiency and commitment-based succinctness. The vector commitment lets a trie be both. The remaining work — faster provers, tighter worst-case analysis, post-quantum instantiations, and the governance decision itself — is engineering and research built on a foundation that is now, for the first time, fully specified.
