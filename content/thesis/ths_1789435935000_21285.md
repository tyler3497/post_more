# Incompressible Quantum Fluids in Two Dimensions: Laughlin States, Composite Fermions, and the Topological Invariants of the Fractional Quantum Hall Effect

## Abstract

The fractional quantum Hall effect is the paradigmatic setting in which strong correlations, topology, and emergent fractionalization meet. In a two-dimensional electron gas subject to a perpendicular magnetic field so strong that the kinetic energy is quenched into degenerate Landau levels, the Coulomb interaction selects ground states that are incompressible quantum fluids with fractionally quantized Hall conductance. This thesis develops the subject from the Landau-level problem and the integer effect, whose quantization is protected by the TKNN invariant and the first Chern number, through Laughlin's 1983 trial wavefunction and its celebrated plasma analogy, to the fractionally charged quasiparticles that carry anyonic exchange statistics. It then presents Jain's composite-fermion construction, which reorganizes the observed hierarchy of filling fractions into the integer quantum Hall effect of emergent particles formed by binding electrons to vortices, and Wen's chiral Luttinger liquid theory of the gapless edge. Experimental signatures — the quantized plateaus, direct shot-noise measurements of fractional charge, and interferometric probes of braiding — are surveyed, and the limitations of the current picture, including the unresolved non-Abelian candidates at half filling, are assessed.

##1 Introduction

When von Klitzing, Dorda, and Pepper discovered in 1980 that the Hall conductance of a two-dimensional electron gas is quantized in integer multiples of *e²/h* to parts in a billion, condensed matter physics acquired its first precision topological phenomenon. The *integer* quantum Hall effect (IQHE) could be understood within a single-particle picture: Landau levels, disorder-induced localization, and the topological TKNN invariant explained both the plateaus and their astonishing accuracy. Two years later, Tsui, Störmer, and Gossard pushed to higher magnetic fields and cleaner samples and found plateaus at *fractional* fillings — *ν* = 1/3, 2/3, 2/5, and eventually dozens more. The single-particle picture had no answer: within a partially filled Landau level, the kinetic energy is entirely quenched, every configuration of electrons is degenerate, and the entire physics is dictated by the Coulomb interaction. Perturbation theory fails completely, and the ground state must be found among the exponentially many degenerate configurations of a strongly correlated liquid.

Robert Laughlin's 1983 proposal changed the terms of the problem. Rather than diagonalizing the interaction, he wrote down a variational wavefunction whose analytic structure encodes the correlations directly: every electron sees every other electron as a zero of order *m*, the wavefunction is the unique densest zero-energy state of a short-range model interaction, and the resulting fluid is incompressible at filling *ν* = 1/*m*. Its excitations carry fractional charge *e/m* and obey fractional exchange statistics — the first theoretical prediction of *anyons*. Jain subsequently showed that the bewildering observed hierarchy of fractions, from 1/3 to 2/5, 3/7, 4/9, and their particle-hole conjugates, is the integer quantum Hall effect of *composite fermions*, bound states of electrons and quantized vortices. What remains of the electron's charge is fractionalized; what remains of the magnetic field is partially cancelled; and the strongly coupled problem becomes a weakly coupled one.

This thesis is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews Landau levels and the integer effect with its Chern-number topology. Section 3 describes the wavefunction-first methodology, centered on the plasma analogy. Section 4 develops the deep structure in four parts: the Laughlin state, its anyonic excitations, the composite-fermion construction, and the chiral edge. Section 5 surveys the experimental record, Section 6 the limitations, and Section 7 the conclusions.

##2 Background

### Landau levels

Consider non-relativistic electrons of charge *−e* and mass *m\** confined to a plane in a uniform perpendicular field **B** = *B* **ẑ**. In the Landau gauge **A** = (0, *Bx*, 0) the single-particle Hamiltonian is

$$H = \frac{1}{2m^*} \left( p_x^2 + (p_y + eBx)^2 \right),$$

and the spectrum collapses into Landau levels with energies *Eₙ* = *ħωc*(*n* + 1/2), where *ωc* = *eB/m\** is the cyclotron frequency [2]. The characteristic length is the magnetic length

$$\ell_B = \sqrt{\frac{\hbar}{eB}},$$

which at *B* = 10 T is about 8 nm. Each Landau level contains one state per flux quantum *φ₀* = *h/e* threading the sample, so the degeneracy per unit area is *n_B* = *eB/h* = 1/(2*πℓ_B²*). The dimensionless filling factor

$$\nu = \frac{n}{n_B} = \frac{nh}{eB}$$

counts filled levels, with *n* the electron density.

> **Theorem:** In a clean system, the density of states of a two-dimensional electron gas in a magnetic field is a sequence of delta functions at the Landau energies, each with weight *eB/h* per unit area. Disorder broadens each level into a band of extended states near its center flanked by localized states in the tails; as the chemical potential sweeps through the localized regions, the Hall conductance stays pinned while the longitudinal conductance vanishes.

This is the mechanism of the integer plateaus: localized states contribute no current, and each filled band of extended states contributes exactly one conductance quantum *e²/h* [2].

### The integer effect and the TKNN invariant

The quantization *σ_xy* = *Ne²/h* is far too precise to be an accident of band structure, and Thouless, Kohmoto, Nightingale, and den Nijs (TKNN) showed it is topological. Averaging the Kubo formula over the two fluxes threading a toroidal sample, the Hall conductivity becomes an integral of the Berry curvature *F_xy* over a parameter torus:

$$\sigma_{xy} = -\frac{e^2}{h} \, C, \qquad C = \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{T}^2} d^2\theta \, \mathcal{F}_{xy}.$$

The integer *C ∈ ℤ* is the first Chern number of the occupied-band bundle; it cannot change without closing the bulk gap, which is why the plateaus survive disorder, sample geometry, and microscopic detail [7]. Equivalently, the chiral edge modes of a finite sample carry the current, each contributing *e²/h* with a chirality fixed by the field direction — the bulk-boundary correspondence in its earliest incarnation [2].

The lattice Chern number can be computed numerically with the Fukui–Hatsugai–Suzuki discretization of the Berry flux, here for the Hofstadter model at flux *p/q* per plaquette:

```python
import numpy as np

def hofstadter_ham(kx, ky, p, q, t=1.0):
    """q x q Hofstadter Hamiltonian, flux p/q per plaquette (Landau gauge)."""
    H = np.zeros((q, q), dtype=complex)
    for m in range(q):
        H[m, m] = -2*t*np.cos(ky - 2*np.pi*p*m/q)
        H[m, (m+1) % q] = -t*np.exp(1j*kx)
        H[(m+1) % q, m] = -t*np.exp(-1j*kx)
    return H

def chern_number(p, q, filled, nk=30):
    """Lattice Chern number of `filled` bands (Fukui-Hatsugai-Suzuki)."""
    ks = np.linspace(0, 2*np.pi, nk, endpoint=False)
    vecs = np.empty((nk, nk, q, filled), dtype=complex)
    for ix, kx in enumerate(ks):
        for iy, ky in enumerate(ks):
            _, U = np.linalg.eigh(hofstadter_ham(kx, ky, p, q))
            vecs[ix, iy] = U[:, :filled]
    F = np.zeros((nk, nk))
    for ix in range(nk):
        for iy in range(nk):
            jx, jy = (ix+1) % nk, (iy+1) % nk
            def link(a, b, c, d):
                M = vecs[a, b].conj().T @ vecs[c, d]
                return np.linalg.det(M) / abs(np.linalg.det(M))
            F[ix, iy] = np.angle(link(ix,iy,jx,iy)*link(jx,iy,jx,jy)
                                *link(jx,jy,ix,jy)*link(ix,jy,ix,iy))
    return round(F.sum() / (2*np.pi))
```

The integer effect is thus a solved problem of *non-interacting* topology. The fractional effect begins where this picture ends: at *ν* = 1/3 the lowest Landau level is one-third full, the single-particle gap is absent, and the Chern-number argument has nothing to quantize. The gap — measured in the tens of kelvin in the best samples — is generated entirely by interactions.

##3 Methodology

The methodology of fractional quantum Hall physics is wavefunction-first. Because the lowest Landau level (LLL) consists of functions of the form *f(z)e^{−|z|²/4ℓ_B²}* with *f* analytic in the complex coordinate *z* = *x* + *iy*, any many-body state in the LLL is an analytic polynomial times a Gaussian. This severe restriction turns out to be a gift: one can *guess* the ground state by its analytic structure, and the guesses are spectacularly good.

The three pillars of the method are:

1. **Trial wavefunctions.** Laughlin's and Jain's wavefunctions are written down from symmetry and analyticity, not derived. Their credibility comes from overlaps exceeding 0.99 with exact-diagonalization ground states of the Coulomb interaction on spheres and tori for accessible sizes, and from their being exact ground states of model short-range (pseudopotential) Hamiltonians [1][7].
2. **The plasma analogy.** The squared modulus of a trial wavefunction is reinterpreted as the Boltzmann weight of a classical two-dimensional plasma. Screening in the classical problem proves uniformity, incompressibility, and fractional charge in the quantum one [1][2].
3. **Effective field theory.** Chern–Simons and chiral-boson theories capture the universal long-wavelength data — Hall conductance, fractional charge, braiding phases, edge exponents — that no microscopic detail can alter, and they organize states into a topological classification [6][9].

Numerical checks proceed by Monte Carlo evaluation of the trial states (the plasma analogy makes them samplable) and exact diagonalization in the LLL for small electron numbers, where the model Hamiltonians are defined through Haldane's pseudopotentials *V_m*, the interaction energy of a pair with relative angular momentum *m* [7].

##4 The Architecture of the Fractionalized Fluid

### The Laughlin Wavefunction and the Plasma Analogy

Laughlin's proposal for filling *ν* = 1/*m*, with *m* odd, is

$$\Psi_m(\{z_i\}) = \prod_{i<j}(z_i - z_j)^m \, e^{-\sum_i |z_i|^2 / 4\ell_B^2}.$$

Every pair of electrons vanishes to order *m* as they approach — for *m* = 3 the wavefunction has a *triple* zero at coincidence, powerfully suppressing the short-range Coulomb repulsion — while the Gaussian confines the droplet. Antisymmetry demands *m* odd for electrons; even *m* gives the bosonic analogues. The state is the exact, unique, densest zero-energy ground state of the model interaction with *V₁ = V₃ = ... = V_{m−2} > 0* and all higher pseudopotentials zero [1][7].

The plasma analogy converts this analytic object into classical statistical mechanics. Writing *|Ψ_m|² = e^{−βΦ}* with *β* = 2*m* gives

$$\Phi = -\sum_{i<j} \ln|z_i - z_j| + \frac{1}{4m\ell_B^2}\sum_i |z_i|^2,$$

the potential energy of a two-dimensional one-component plasma: particles repelling with the 2D Coulomb (logarithmic) interaction in a uniform neutralizing background. A classical plasma *screens*: the mobile charges arrange themselves to neutralize the background on average, so the electron density is uniform,

$$n = \frac{1}{2\pi m \ell_B^2}, \qquad \nu = \frac{nh}{eB} = \frac{1}{m},$$

exactly the claimed filling [2]. Because the plasma has a finite screening length, density fluctuations cost a finite energy — the quantum fluid is *incompressible*, with a bulk gap. The analogy also explains why the precise form of the short-range repulsion hardly matters: any plasma with coupling in the liquid regime screens the same way, which is the microscopic root of universality.

### Fractionally Charged Quasiparticles and Anyonic Statistics

The elementary excitations are defects in the plasma. Inserting a thin solenoid at *z₀* and adiabatically threading one flux quantum multiplies the wavefunction by *∏ᵢ(zᵢ − z₀)*, pushing electrons away from *z₀* — a *quasihole*. In the plasma picture this is an inserted impurity of charge +1, which the plasma screens by accumulating a compensating electron deficit. Since each plasma particle carries charge *m* (in the natural units of the analogy), perfect screening expels charge *1/m*: the quasihole carries electric charge *+e/m*, a fraction of the electron's charge [1][2]. The *quasielectron*, obtained by removing flux, carries *−e/m*.

Fractional charge forces fractional statistics. Exchanging two quasiholes of the *ν* = 1/*m* Laughlin state accumulates a Berry phase

$$\theta = \frac{\pi}{m},$$

neither bosonic (0) nor fermionic (*π*) — the excitations are *anyons*, a possibility unique to two dimensions where particle exchange is governed by the braid group rather than the permutation group [2][7]. Braiding one quasihole around another gives twice this phase, 2*π/m*. This is not a gauge artifact: it is the Berry phase of the many-body wavefunction under adiabatic transport, and it is as physical as the fractional charge itself. The modern understanding is that the Laughlin states are the simplest examples of *topological order* — gapped phases with no local order parameter, characterized instead by ground-state degeneracy on the torus (an *m*-fold degeneracy at *ν* = 1/*m*) and by the braiding data of their anyons [6].

### Jain's Composite Fermions and the Jain Sequences

The observed fractions form striking sequences — 1/3, 2/5, 3/7, 4/9, ... and 2/3, 3/5, 4/7, ... — converging on *ν* = 1/2, where no plateau appears and the system is a compressible metal. Jain's composite-fermion (CF) theory explains all of them at once [4][5]. The idea: bind each electron to *2p* quantized vortices (flux quanta). The resulting *composite fermion* sees a reduced effective field

$$B^* = B - 2p\, n\phi_0,$$

because the attached flux partially cancels the external field. At electron filling *ν*, the composite fermions fill

$$\nu^* = \frac{\nu}{1 - 2p\nu}$$

effective Landau levels (Λ levels). When *ν\** is an integer *n*, the composite fermions exhibit the *integer* quantum Hall effect — an incompressible state with a gap — which translates back to the electron fractions

$$\nu = \frac{n}{2pn \pm 1}.$$

For *p* = 1 this yields the principal sequences 1/3, 2/5, 3/7, ... (positive effective field) and 2/3, 3/5, 4/7, ... (negative effective field), precisely the observed hierarchy [4][7]. At *ν* = 1/2 the effective field vanishes and the composite fermions form a Fermi sea — the Halperin–Lee–Read metal — explaining the absence of a plateau and predicting the observed Fermi wavevector through surface-acoustic-wave and magnetic-focusing experiments [7][8].

Microscopically, Jain's wavefunctions are

$$\Psi_{\nu} = \mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{LLL}} \prod_{i<j}(z_i - z_j)^{2p} \, \Phi_{\pm n}(\{z_i\}),$$

the Jastrow factor attaching *2p* vortices to the Slater determinant *Φ_{±n}* of *n* filled Landau levels, projected back into the lowest Landau level [4]. These trial states achieve overlaps with exact ground states that are essentially perfect for the accessible sizes, and the construction unifies the Laughlin states (*n* = 1) with the entire hierarchy. The CF theory and the older Haldane–Halperin hierarchy describe the same universality classes but posit distinct microscopic mechanisms — a distinction experiments have been able to probe [5]. A recent line of work has put the CF mapping on firmer footing, deriving the electron–CF correspondence as a unitary relationship emerging from interactions within a single Landau level, without uncontrolled projection by hand [6].

| Sequence (*p* = 1) | Fractions *ν* | CF filling *ν\** | Observed gap trend |
|---|---|---|---|
| *n*/(2*n*+1) | 1/3, 2/5, 3/7, 4/9 | +1, +2, +3, +4 | Decreasing with *n* |
| *n*/(2*n*−1) | 2/3, 3/5, 4/7, 5/9 | −1, −2, −3, −4 | Decreasing with *n* |
| Half filling | 1/2 | ∞ (Fermi sea) | Gapless, compressible |

### Edge States and the Chiral Luttinger Liquid

A finite droplet of fractional quantum Hall fluid has a gapless edge, and the edge is where the topological order meets the laboratory. Because the bulk is incompressible, low-energy deformations live at the boundary; because time reversal is broken, they propagate in only one direction — a *chiral* edge. Wen showed that the edge of the *ν* = 1/*m* Laughlin state is a chiral Luttinger liquid: a single branch of bosonic density waves with the effective action

$$S = \frac{m}{4\pi}\int dx\,dt\, \partial_x\phi\,(\partial_t - v\partial_x)\phi,$$

where the electron operator is the vertex *e^{imφ}* and the quasihole operator is *e^{iφ}* [9]. The integer *m* in the action is the same *m* as in the bulk wavefunction — bulk topological order dictates the edge theory completely.

The consequences are sharp and testable.

##5 Empirical Results and Proofs

The experimental record of the fractional quantum Hall effect is a fifty-year dialogue between quantization and correlation.

- **Quantized plateaus.** The Hall resistance *R_H* = *h/(νe²)* is quantized at fractions including 1/3 (3*h/e²* ≈ 77.4 kΩ), 2/5, 3/7, 2/3, 3/5, 4/7, 5/2, and more than a hundred others in the cleanest GaAs heterostructures, with the longitudinal resistance vanishing on each plateau. The pattern of observed fractions follows the Jain sequences with uncanny fidelity, and the measured activation gaps scale as expected for composite-fermion Λ-level spacings, shrinking as *n* grows [7][8].
- **Direct observation of fractional charge.** The decisive experiment partitioned a current through a quantum point contact at *ν* = 1/3 and measured the shot noise *S_I* = 2*qI_B* of the backscattered current. The noise was proportional to charge *q* = *e*/3, not *e* — the current is carried by Laughlin quasiparticles, exactly as predicted [3]. Independent measurements confirmed the result, and at *ν* = 2/5 the weak-backscattering charge *e*/5 was observed, crossing over toward *e* as the constriction is pinched and quasiparticles bunch [8][9].
- **Statistics and interferometry.** Fabry–Pérot interferometers, in which edge quasiparticles encircle localized bulk quasiparticles, show interference phases consistent with the predicted anyonic braiding, including the 2*π*/3 phase at *ν* = 1/3 — a direct probe of the fractional exchange statistics derived in Section 4 [7].
- **Composite-fermion Fermi sea.** At *ν* = 1/2, surface acoustic waves propagate with anomalies revealing a Fermi wavevector *k_F* = √(4*πn*) of composite fermions at zero effective field, and magnetic focusing experiments image their cyclotron orbits — the emergent Fermi liquid predicted by the CF theory [7][8].
- **Topological quantization.** The integer plateaus remain quantized to metrological precision via the TKNN Chern invariant, and the fractional plateaus inherit a parallel rigidity: the fractional Hall conductance *σ_xy* = *νe²/h* is protected by the bulk gap and the topological order of the fluid [2][7].

Taken together — the sequences, the *e*/3 noise, the braiding phases, the CF Fermi sea — the evidence is that the fractional quantum Hall fluid is precisely the correlated, fractionally charged, anyonic liquid described by the Laughlin and Jain wavefunctions.

##6 Limitations

The picture is triumphant but incomplete, and the gaps are where the field now lives.

1. **The half-filled second Landau level.** At *ν* = 5/2 the system is gapped but fits no Jain sequence; the leading candidates (Moore–Read Pfaffian, anti-Pfaffian, particle-hole-symmetric Pfaffian) host *non-Abelian* anyons, whose braiding would enable topological quantum computation. Thermal Hall measurements have not settled the pairing channel, and the issue remains the most important open problem in the field.
2. **Microscopic rigor.** The composite-fermion mapping, for all its empirical success, long lacked a controlled derivation; the LLL projection in Jain's wavefunctions is uncontrolled, and the precise sense in which the CF theory and the hierarchy theory coincide is subtle [5][6]. Recent unitary-mapping results improve the situation but do not close it [6].
3. **Quasielectron asymmetry.** The plasma analogy applies cleanly to quasiholes but not to quasielectrons, whose wavefunctions break the clustering properties of the ground state; the neutral excitations and the precise gap values remain harder to compute than the charged ones [7].
4. **Disorder and edges.** Real samples have disorder, edge reconstruction, and smooth confinement, all of which complicate the clean chiral-Luttinger-liquid predictions; measured tunneling exponents sometimes deviate from the universal values, and separating bulk from edge physics requires care [9].
5. **Scope.** This account is restricted to Abelian states in GaAs-like systems; graphene, bilayer systems, and fractional Chern insulators extend the phenomenology in directions barely touched here.

##7 Conclusion

The fractional quantum Hall effect demonstrates that the electron is not the end of the story: in two dimensions, under a strong magnetic field, the true low-energy particles carry fractions of the electron's charge and exchange with fractions of the fermionic phase. Laughlin's wavefunction showed how to write down such a fluid; the plasma analogy showed why it is uniform and incompressible; Jain's composite fermions showed that the entire observed hierarchy is the integer effect in disguise; and Wen's edge theory showed where to look. The TKNN invariant protects the integer plateaus, while topological order — ground-state degeneracy, fractional charge, anyonic braiding — protects the fractional ones. Shot-noise measurements of *e*/3 and interferometric measurements of braiding phases have moved these ideas from mathematics to metrology. What remains — the non-Abelian candidate at 5/2, a fully controlled microscopic derivation, the extension to new materials — ensures that the quantum Hall fluid will continue to be the laboratory in which our understanding of correlated topological matter is forged.
