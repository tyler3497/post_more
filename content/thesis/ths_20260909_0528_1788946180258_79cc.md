# Pulsar Timing Arrays and the Nanohertz Gravitational-Wave Background: Correlated Residuals, the Hellings–Downs Curve, and Supermassive Black Hole Binaries

## Abstract

Pulsar timing arrays (PTAs) have become a gravitational-wave observatory for the nanohertz band — wavelengths of light-years, unreachable by terrestrial interferometers. The 2023 data releases from NANOGrav, the European, Parkes, and Chinese collaborations reported correlated common-spectrum signals consistent with a stochastic gravitational-wave background (GWB): the NANOGrav 15-year data set favors Hellings–Downs angular correlations over uncorrelated common noise with Bayes factors of 200–1000, inferring a characteristic strain amplitude of $2.4^{+0.7}_{-0.6}\times 10^{-15}$ at one cycle per year [1]. This thesis develops the machinery behind that claim: millisecond pulsars as cosmic clocks, the timing model and residual structure, power-law noise modeling (red spin noise, chromatic dispersion-measure variations), the Hellings–Downs overlap reduction function as the decisive GW discriminant, the Bayesian hierarchical and optimal-statistic analyses behind the evidence, the astrophysical interpretation as a cosmic population of inspiralling supermassive black hole binaries with spectral index $\gamma=13/3$, and competing explanations — cosmic strings, first-order phase transitions, and scalar-induced primordial backgrounds — closing with the statistical limitations and the IPTA/SKA roadmap.

## 1 Introduction

Gravitational-wave astronomy has, since 2015, been a high-frequency science: ground-based interferometers probe the audio band, tens of hertz to kilohertz, where stellar-mass compact binaries merge. But the gravitational-wave sky spans roughly twenty decades in frequency, and the loudest astrophysical signals of all — inspiralling supermassive black hole binaries (SMBHBs) of $10^8$–$10^{10}\,M_\odot$ — radiate at nanohertz frequencies, with orbital periods of years to decades [9]. No Earth-bound apparatus can reach this band; only a Galaxy-scale detector can. The pulsar timing array is precisely such an instrument: millisecond pulsars across kiloparsec distances, whose pulse trains serve as fiducial clocks against which long-wavelength gravitational waves imprint correlated deviations in pulse times of arrival (TOAs) [10].

The idea traces to the late 1970s. Sazhin (1978) and Detweiler (1979) first formulated how a passing gravitational wave modulates the observed spin frequency of a pulsar, treating the pulsar–Earth baseline as a free-mass antenna arm [3]. The decisive theoretical step came in 1983, when Hellings and Downs computed the expected angular correlation of timing residuals between pulsar pairs for an isotropic stochastic background — the Hellings–Downs curve, a quadrupolar signature unique to gravitational waves that remains the "smoking gun" of any PTA detection claim [2]. Millisecond pulsars, discovered in 1982, supplied the clocks: rotational stabilities rivaling atomic standards over year-long baselines made microsecond-level timing practical, and by the mid-2000s regional consortia — Parkes PTA, European PTA, NANOGrav — had formalized into coordinated experiments under the IPTA [9].

For decades, PTAs produced only upper limits, each ruling out increasingly optimistic SMBHB background models. The NANOGrav 12.5-year analysis (2020) found a common-spectrum red process but no significant inter-pulsar correlations. In June 2023, the NANOGrav 15-year data set, EPTA DR2 (combined with InPTA), the PPTA, and the Chinese PTA each reported evidence for a common signal with Hellings–Downs–consistent correlations [1][5].

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Millisecond pulsars as clocks

A millisecond pulsar is a rapidly rotating neutron star ($P \sim 1.5$–$30\,\mathrm{ms}$) whose beamed radio emission sweeps across Earth once per rotation. The best-timed MSPs exhibit fractional rotational stability $\sigma_y \sim 10^{-15}$ over years, comparable to the finest terrestrial frequency standards [3]. Template-matching of folded pulse profiles achieves TOA uncertainties of tens to hundreds of nanoseconds at gigahertz frequencies.

The *timing model* predicts every TOA from a deterministic ephemeris: the pulsar's spin frequency and derivatives, astrometric position and proper motion, parallax, binary Keplerian and post-Keplerian parameters, and the dispersive delay of the ionized interstellar medium (ISM). The difference between observed and predicted TOAs — the *timing residual* $r(t) = t_{\rm obs} - t_{\rm model}$ — is the primary observable. After fitting the timing model, residuals of a perfect clock in quiet spacetime would be white noise; everything else — spin irregularities, ISM propagation effects, solar-system ephemeris errors, clock errors, and gravitational waves — lives in the residual time series and its covariance [10].

### 2.2 The gravitational-wave imprint

A gravitational wave propagating between pulsar and Earth perturbs the null geodesic of the radio pulses. Detweiler's (1979) result expresses the fractional Doppler shift of the observed pulse frequency as [3]

$$
z(t) \equiv \frac{\nu_0 - \nu(t)}{\nu_0} =
\frac{(\alpha^2-\beta^2)(h_+^E - h_+^P) + 2\alpha\beta\,(h_\times^E - h_\times^P)}{2(1+\gamma)},
$$

where $(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)$ are the direction cosines of the pulsar and $h_{+,\times}^{E,P}$ are the wave amplitudes at Earth ($E$) and at the pulsar ($P$). The timing residual is the integral of this redshift, $r(t) = \int^t z(t')\,dt'$. Two features are critical. First, the **Earth term** $h^{E}$ is common to all pulsars — a passing wave shakes our end of every antenna arm simultaneously — while the **pulsar terms** $h^{P}$ are uncorrelated between pulsars separated by hundreds of parsecs, because the waves sampled at emission left each pulsar thousands of years apart. Second, only the correlated Earth term survives cross-correlation between pulsars, which is what makes the Hellings–Downs analysis possible at all.

> **Theorem:** *For an isotropic, unpolarized stochastic gravitational-wave background in general relativity, the expected cross-correlation of timing residuals between pulsars $a$ and $b$ depends only on their angular separation $\zeta_{ab}$ on the sky, through the Hellings–Downs overlap reduction function $\Gamma_{ab}(\zeta_{ab})$ [4].*

### 2.3 The Hellings–Downs curve

For an isotropic background of uncorrelated sources with random phases, the sky- and polarization-averaged product of two pulsars' responses to a plane wave yields the analytic curve [2]

$$
\Gamma_{ab}(\zeta) = \frac{1}{2} - \frac{1-\cos\zeta}{4} + \frac{3}{2}\left(\frac{1-\cos\zeta}{2}\right)\ln\!\left(\frac{1-\cos\zeta}{2}\right), \qquad a \neq b,
$$

with $\Gamma_{aa} = 1$. The curve equals $+1/2$ at zero separation, crosses zero near $\zeta \approx 50^\circ$, reaches a minimum near $90^\circ$, and returns to $-1/4$ for antipodal pulsars — a quadrupolar pattern reflecting the spin-2 nature of the metric perturbation. Its variance for realistic pulsar geometries has been computed in detail, confirming that scatter about the curve is expected and quantifiable [4].

Crucially, the Hellings–Downs pattern is *specific* to gravitational waves. A monopolar correlation would indicate clock errors; a dipolar one would betray solar-system ephemeris errors. No terrestrial or astrophysical systematic produces a quadrupolar sky correlation. Hence the detection logic of every PTA: first find a common-spectrum red process, then demand that the cross-correlations follow $\Gamma_{ab}$ [5].

### 2.4 The supermassive black hole binary background

For circular binaries evolving purely by GW emission, Phinney's theorem gives the characteristic strain spectrum

$$
h_c(f) = A_{\rm GWB}\left(\frac{f}{f_{\rm yr}}\right)^{-2/3},
$$

with $f_{\rm yr} = 1\,\mathrm{yr}^{-1}$, which translates to a timing-residual power spectral density $P(f) \propto f^{-\gamma}$ with spectral index $\gamma = 13/3 \approx 4.33$ [6]. Eccentricity, environmental coupling, and finite-source discreteness modify this slope, generally hardening it at low frequencies — a diagnostic handle discussed in §5.

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 Data and timing pipelines

Modern PTA data sets are multi-telescope, multi-frequency campaigns spanning 15–25 years. The NANOGrav 15-year release times 67 MSPs with Arecibo and the Green Bank Telescope; EPTA DR2 times 25 MSPs over 24.7 years across five European telescopes, with a 10.3-year high-quality subset from modern backends, combined with InPTA's first release for ten pulsars [1][5]. Multi-frequency TOAs (typically 300 MHz–3 GHz) are essential: the ISM dispersive delay scales as $\nu^{-2}$, separating chromatic propagation effects from achromatic gravitational signals.

### 3.2 The noise model

Every PTA likelihood is a hierarchical model of TOA residuals with layered noise components:

| Component | Character | Typical parameterization |
|---|---|---|
| White noise | Uncorrelated in time | EFAC (scale), EQUAD (additive), ECORR (correlated across simultaneous sub-bands) |
| Achromatic red noise | Correlated, spin irregularities | Power law $P(f) = A_{\rm red}^2 (f/f_{\rm yr})^{-\gamma_{\rm red}}/12\pi^2 f^3$ |
| DM variations | Chromatic ($\propto \nu^{-2}$), ISM turbulence | Power law in dispersion measure, $P_{\rm DM}(f)$ |
| Common red process | Shared spectrum across pulsars | Power law with Hellings–Downs spatial correlations |

White-noise parameters (EFAC, EQUAD, ECORR) are typically fixed from single-pulsar analyses; red noise and DM variations are modeled as Gaussian processes with power-law spectra, sampled jointly with the timing model [5]. A cautionary example: per-pulsar red-noise amplitudes inferred in isolation are covariant with the common process, so modern analyses fit individual and common terms simultaneously, and Bayesian model comparison decides between noise-only, uncorrelated common red noise (CURN), and Hellings–Downs–correlated GWB hypotheses [1].

### 3.3 Bayesian and frequentist detection statistics

The flagship analysis is Bayesian: the marginalized likelihood over timing-model, noise, and GWB parameters yields Bayes factors between competing correlation structures — $B_{\rm HD/CURN}$ for Hellings–Downs versus uncorrelated common red noise, and $B_{\rm HD/noise}$ against no common process at all. Because Bayes factors depend on prior choices, a second, frequentist pillar is standard: the *optimal statistic*, a noise-weighted sum of inter-pulsar cross-correlations,

$$
\hat{A}^2 = \frac{\sum_{a<b} r_a^T C_a^{-1} \tilde{S}_{ab} C_b^{-1} r_b}{\sum_{a<b} \mathrm{tr}\!\left[C_a^{-1} \tilde{S}_{ab} C_b^{-1} \tilde{S}_{ba}\right]},
$$

where $\tilde{S}_{ab} = \Gamma_{ab} S(f)$ encodes the HD pattern times the common spectrum, evaluated with noise covariances $C_a$ fixed at their maximum-likelihood values. Its signal-to-noise ratio is calibrated against a null distribution built by *phase-shifting* or *sky-scrambling* the data — destroying true inter-pulsar correlations while preserving each pulsar's autocorrelation — yielding $p$-values independent of the Bayesian priors [1].

### 3.4 Spectral characterization

The common process is fit as a power law, or as a free spectrum (independent amplitude per frequency bin), to test consistency with $\gamma = 13/3$. Deviations carry physics: a low-frequency turnover suggests environmental coupling (stellar/gas-driven hardening) or eccentricity; excess power in narrow bins hints at individual bright binaries; a blue-tilted or peaked spectrum would favor cosmological sources [6][7]. A search pipeline for *continuous waves* — deterministic sinusoids from single nearby SMBHBs — runs in parallel, since a loud individual binary contributes both to the stochastic background and as a resolvable source [5].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Anatomy of a timing residual: separating signal from plasma

Consider a single pulsar timed at two frequencies. The raw residual contains: (i) achromatic components — spin noise and any GW signal — identical at both frequencies; (ii) chromatic DM variations, scaling as $\nu^{-2}$ and tracking the line-of-sight electron column through the turbulent ISM; and (iii) white measurement noise. The decomposition is illustrated conceptually by the following generative model used in simulation studies:

```python
import numpy as np

def simulate_residuals(t, freqs, A_gwb=2.4e-15, gamma=13/3,
                       A_red=1e-14, gamma_red=3.0, dm_rms=1e-7):
    """Toy PTA residual generator: GWB + red noise + DM variations."""
    f = np.fft.rfftfreq(len(t), d=np.median(np.diff(t)))
    f[0] = f[1]
    # common GWB spectrum (characteristic strain -> residual PSD)
    P_gwb = A_gwb**2 / (12*np.pi**2) * (f/3.17e-8)**(-gamma) / f**3
    # achromatic spin red noise
    P_red = A_red**2 / (12*np.pi**2) * (f/3.17e-8)**(-gamma_red) / f**3
    def gp(P):
        w = np.random.randn(len(f)) + 1j*np.random.randn(len(f))
        return np.fft.irfft(np.sqrt(P) * w / np.sqrt(2*np.diff(t).mean()), len(t))
    r = {}
    gwb, red = gp(P_gwb), gp(P_red)          # achromatic: same at all freqs
    for nu in freqs:
        dm = dm_rms * np.cumsum(np.random.randn(len(t)))  # chromatic
        r[nu] = gwb + red + (4.15e3/dm_rms)*(nu/1e9)**-2 * dm/np.std(dm)*dm_rms
    return r
```

The lesson is quantitative: for the inferred GWB amplitude, the signal is *subdominant* to intrinsic red noise and DM variations in most pulsars at most frequencies. Detection is never about seeing the signal in one pulsar — it is about the *coherent cross-pulsar structure* surviving after per-pulsar noise is modeled out. Noise-model misspecification is thus the dominant systematic: unmodeled chromatic components can leak into the common process and bias $\gamma$ away from $13/3$ [7].

### 4.2 The Hellings–Downs curve as a hypothesis test

In practice the HD curve is not fit as a curve through binned points — binning loses information and invites over-interpretation. Instead, $\Gamma_{ab}$ enters the likelihood directly as the correlation structure of the common process. The analysis then compares three nested hypotheses: (H0) independent red noise per pulsar; (H1) a common red process with *no* spatial correlations (CURN); (H2) a common red process with Hellings–Downs correlations. The jump H0→H1 measures evidence for *something* common; the jump H1→H2 measures evidence that the something is *gravitational waves*.

This two-step structure matters because H1 is already strongly favored in all 2023 data sets — the common process is real in the sense of being shared — while H1→H2 is the weaker, genuinely GW-specific step. NANOGrav finds $B_{\rm H1/H0} > 10^{14}$ but $B_{\rm H2/H1} \sim 200$–$1000$ depending on spectral modeling [1]; EPTA's full 24.7-year data set gives only marginal H1→H2 evidence (Bayes factor $\approx 4$, false-alarm probability 4%), while its 10.3-year modern-backend subset reaches a Bayes factor of 60 with $\sim 3\sigma$ significance [5]. The frequentist optimal statistic tells the same story: NANOGrav's HD-correlated SNR is $5 \pm 1$ with $p = 5\times10^{-5}$–$1.9\times10^{-4}$ ($\approx 3.5$–$4\sigma$) under sky-scramble nulls [1].

> **Theorem:** *A monopolar correlation signature indicates clock-reference errors and a dipolar signature indicates solar-system ephemeris errors; only a quadrupolar (Hellings–Downs) angular dependence is consistent with a transverse-traceless metric perturbation, making it the unique discriminant of a gravitational-wave origin [2][9].*

### 4.3 The supermassive black hole binary population

If the signal is astrophysical, its amplitude encodes the cosmic merger history of massive galaxies. Population synthesis converts galaxy stellar-mass functions, pair fractions, merger timescales, and black hole–host scaling relations into a predicted $A_{\rm GWB}$. The NANOGrav amplitude, $2.4^{+0.7}_{-0.6}\times 10^{-15}$, sits at the *upper* end of pre-2023 predictions — high but not excluded — and its relatively steep spectrum has been used to constrain binary hardening timescales and the $M_{\rm BH}$–$\sigma$ relation [6][11]. Improved re-analyses of the EPTA data show that most of the apparent tension with $\gamma = 13/3$ traces to excess power in just two frequency bins ($\sim 1.3\times10^{-8}$ and $2.9\times10^{-8}$ Hz), with the remainder of the spectrum consistent with the canonical slope [7].

A striking corollary: the brightest few binaries may be individually resolvable. EPTA DR2 contains a candidate continuous-wave feature at 4–5 nHz, though "GWB + continuous wave" versus "GWB only" gives a Bayes factor of just 0.7 — the data cannot yet decide whether the feature is a single binary or a fluctuation of the background [5]. Anisotropy searches currently find no significant large-scale-structure–correlated component, consistent with an approximately isotropic background at current sensitivity [11].

### 4.4 New-physics alternatives

The data are consistent with SMBHBs, but the nanohertz band is a discovery regime for early-Universe physics. Bayesian model comparisons consider three leading cosmological sources [6][8]:

1. **Cosmic strings.** A network of strings with tension $G\mu \sim 10^{-11}$–$10^{-10}$ generates a broad, nearly flat $\Omega_{\rm GW}(f)$ plateau from loop decay that can match the observed amplitude; the 2023 data place the tightest PTA constraints on $G\mu$ to date.
2. **First-order phase transitions.** A strong transition near the QCD scale ($T \sim 100$ MeV) produces sound-wave and turbulence-sourced GWs peaking in the nanohertz band; fitting requires substantial supercooling or non-standard equations of state.
3. **Scalar-induced gravitational waves.** An enhancement of primordial curvature perturbations at small scales sources second-order tensor modes; the steep $\Omega_{\rm GW} \propto f^{1.3\text{–}2.4}$ slope favored by the 15-year data has renewed interest in this channel, with implications for primordial black hole abundances [8].

Current Bayes factors between astrophysical and cosmological models are of order 10–100 in either direction under specific priors — intriguing but not decisive [6]. The tie-breakers will be spectral shape measured over more frequency bins, anisotropy, and non-Gaussian statistics of the background.

## 5 Empirical Results / Formal Analysis

The consolidated empirical picture as of the 2023 data releases is as follows. A common-spectrum red process is detected by all four major PTAs with high significance. Its inter-pulsar correlations are consistent with the Hellings–Downs curve at $3$–$4\sigma$ in NANOGrav's 15-year data set and at $\gtrsim 3\sigma$ in EPTA's modern-backend subset [1][5]. Under the fiducial $f^{-2/3}$ SMBHB spectrum, the inferred amplitude is $A_{\rm GWB} = 2.4^{+0.7}_{-0.6}\times 10^{-15}$ at $f_{\rm yr}$, with the free-spectrum analysis showing the power-law index consistent with $\gamma = 13/3$ within uncertainties, modulo the two-bin excess noted above [1][7].

Formally, the claim rests on a *consilience of statistics*: Bayesian evidence ratios from independent software pipelines, frequentist optimal-statistic SNRs calibrated against sky-scramble nulls, and free-form correlation fits that recover a quadrupolar shape compatible with $\Gamma_{ab}$ [1][4][5]. The agreement of four independent collaborations — using different telescopes, pulsars, and analysis codes — is itself a powerful systematic control.

A compact summary of the headline numbers:

| Data set | Pulsars / span | HD evidence | Amplitude $A_{\rm GWB}$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| NANOGrav 15-yr | 67 / 15 yr | BF 200–1000; SNR $5\pm1$ | $2.4^{+0.7}_{-0.6}\times10^{-15}$ |
| EPTA DR2 full | 25 / 24.7 yr | BF $\approx 4$ (marginal) | consistent, spectrum uncertain |
| EPTA DR2 new (10.3 yr) | 25 / 10.3 yr | BF $\approx 60$; $\sim 3\sigma$ | consistent |
| PPTA / CPTA | 18–57 / $\sim$18 yr | supporting | consistent |

All amplitudes quoted at $f_{\rm yr} = 1\,\mathrm{yr}^{-1}$ under an $h_c \propto f^{-2/3}$ model [1][5].

## 6 Limitations

The evidence, while historic, remains at the *emerging* rather than *definitive* stage, and several limitations temper the interpretation.

**Statistical maturity.** The GW-specific step (HD correlations over uncorrelated common noise) stands at $3$–$4\sigma$, below the conventional $5\sigma$ discovery threshold used in particle physics and adopted informally by the PTA community [2]. Bayes factors in the hundreds are strong but prior-sensitive; the spread of 200–1000 across spectral modeling choices in NANOGrav's own analysis illustrates the residual model dependence [1].

**Noise-model systematics.** The GWB inference is only as reliable as the per-pulsar noise models it marginalizes over. Dispersion-measure variations, scattering, jitter, and band-dependent systematics are all partially covariant with a common red process. The EPTA experience is instructive: tension of the inferred spectral index with $13/3$ shrinks substantially under improved DM Gaussian-process modeling [7].

**Astrophysical uncertainties.** Mapping $A_{\rm GWB}$ to SMBHB demographics requires the galaxy merger rate, black hole–host relations at high redshift, and sub-parsec hardening efficiency — each uncertain at factors of several. The observed amplitude, at the high end of pre-2023 predictions, may signal efficient mergers, over-massive black holes, or an additional cosmological contribution [6].

**Spectral resolution.** With 15-year baselines, only the lowest $\sim 10$–$14$ Fourier frequencies carry signal. Distinguishing a $13/3$ SMBHB slope from a cosmic-string plateau or a peaked phase-transition spectrum needs longer baselines and more pulsars — precisely what the IPTA's combined data sets and the SKA will deliver [9].

## 7 Conclusion

Pulsar timing arrays have crossed a threshold: from placing upper limits on the nanohertz sky to characterizing a correlated signal within it. The chain of inference — millisecond pulsars as clocks [3], timing residuals as the observable, hierarchical noise modeling that isolates a common red process, and the Hellings–Downs angular correlation as the uniquely gravitational-wave discriminant [2][4] — has produced a coherent, multi-collaboration picture of a stochastic background with amplitude $\sim 2\times 10^{-15}$ and a spectrum broadly consistent with inspiralling supermassive black hole binaries [1][5][6].

What remains is the transition from *evidence* to *detection* and from *detection* to *astrophysics*. The IPTA's third data release will sharpen the HD correlation measurement toward $5\sigma$; the Square Kilometre Array, timing hundreds of MSPs, should resolve the background's spectral shape, detect its anisotropy, and pick out individual continuous-wave binaries [9]. Whether the background proves to be purely the murmur of merging supermassive black holes or carries an imprint of cosmic strings, phase transitions, or primordial fluctuations, the Hellings–Downs curve has done its historic work: it turned a Galaxy full of clocks into a gravitational-wave telescope [8][10].
