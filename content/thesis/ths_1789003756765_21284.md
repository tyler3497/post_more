# Concentration of Measure in High Dimensions: Log-Sobolev Inequalities, Transportation-Cost Bounds, and Dimension-Free Phenomena

## Abstract

The concentration of measure phenomenon — that Lipschitz functions on large metric measure spaces are essentially constant, with fluctuations decaying exponentially or Gaussianly — is a central idea of modern probability, geometry, and functional analysis. Originating in Lévy's isoperimetric work on high-dimensional spheres and crystallized by Milman in asymptotic geometric analysis, it is encoded analytically by three families of functional inequalities: the **logarithmic Sobolev inequality (LSI)** of Gross, the **Poincaré (spectral gap) inequality**, and **transportation-cost inequalities** of Talagrand type. This thesis gives a unified account of these tools: the Bakry–Émery curvature criterion, Herbst's entropic argument, the Otto–Villani theorem linking LSI to the quadratic transportation inequality T₂, and Cattiaux–Guillin's result that T₂ is strictly weaker than LSI. We prove Herbst's argument in full, derive dimension-free Gaussian concentration for the standard Gaussian measure, analyze the isoperimetric origins on the sphere Sⁿ, treat concentration in product spaces via Talagrand's convex distance inequality, and close with limitations: sharpness of constants, the Poincaré–LSI gap, heavy tails, and open problems in non-smooth spaces.

**Keywords:** concentration of measure, logarithmic Sobolev inequality, Poincaré inequality, Talagrand transportation inequality, Bakry–Émery criterion, Herbst argument, isoperimetry, dimension-free bounds.

## 1. Introduction

Let $(X, d, \mu)$ be a metric space equipped with a Borel probability measure. For a set $A \subset X$ and $r > 0$, denote by $A_r = \{x \in X : d(x, A) < r\}$ the $r$-neighborhood of $A$. The *concentration function* of $(X, d, \mu)$ is defined by

$$\alpha(r) = \sup_{A : \mu(A) \geq 1/2} \big(1 - \mu(A_r)\big), \qquad r > 0.$$

Intuitively, $\alpha(r)$ measures the worst-case mass lying at distance at least $r$ from a "typical" half-mass set. A space exhibits the *concentration of measure phenomenon* when $\alpha(r)$ decays exponentially or Gaussianly in $r$, with constants *independent of dimension* [1].

In 1919, Lévy observed that on the Euclidean sphere $S^n$ with normalized rotation-invariant measure, any 1-Lipschitz function is nearly constant: it concentrates around its median with Gaussian tails whose variance shrinks like $1/n$ [2]. In the early 1970s, V. Milman seized upon this phenomenon as the key geometric insight behind Dvoretzky's theorem on almost-spherical sections of high-dimensional convex bodies, launching *asymptotic geometric analysis* [1]. In parallel, a functional-analytic program emerged: Gross's logarithmic Sobolev inequality (1975) [4], the Bakry–Émery curvature criterion (1985), and Talagrand's transportation-cost inequality (1996) [5] — all encoding concentration as consequences of entropy and energy dissipation.

> **Theorem (Lévy, 1919, modern formulation):** Let $\sigma_n$ be the normalized uniform measure on the unit sphere $S^n \subset \mathbb{R}^{n+1}$, and let $f : S^n \to \mathbb{R}$ be continuous with modulus of continuity $\omega$. If $m$ is a median of $f$, then for every $\eta > 0$,
> $$\sigma_n\big(|f - m| \geq \eta\big) \leq 2 \exp\left(-\frac{(n-1)\eta^2}{2\,\omega(\eta)^2}\right).$$

In particular, for 1-Lipschitz $f$ the deviation probability decays like $\exp(-cn\eta^2)$: *concentration improves as dimension grows*. This counter-intuitive sharpening — the larger the space, the tighter the concentration — is the signature of the phenomenon.

This thesis is organized around a central question: **which functional inequalities imply concentration, how do they relate to one another, and what is the price of dimension-independence?** We pursue three complementary viewpoints — isoperimetric (Lévy–Gromov–Milman spherical concentration), entropic/functional (log-Sobolev, Poincaré, Herbst, Bakry–Émery), and transport-theoretic (Talagrand's $T_2/T_1$, Otto–Villani, Marton) — unified by *tensorization*: all of these inequalities are preserved under product measures with *dimension-independent* constants, which is precisely why they capture high-dimensional concentration.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 The concentration function and Lipschitz concentration

If $F : X \to \mathbb{R}$ is 1-Lipschitz and $m$ is a median of $F$, setting $A = \{F \leq m\}$ gives $A_r \supset \{F < m + r\}$, hence $\mu(F \geq m + r) \leq \alpha(r)$; applied to $-F$ as well,

$$\mu(|F - m| \geq r) \leq 2\alpha(r), \qquad r > 0. \tag{2.1}$$

Thus the concentration function controls *all* Lipschitz observables simultaneously: on a concentrating space, every smooth macroscopic quantity is nearly deterministic — the analytic heart of the statistical-mechanics slogan that macroscopic observables of large systems do not fluctuate.

### 2.2 Entropy, Fisher information, and the log-Sobolev inequality

Let $\mu$ be a probability measure on $\mathbb{R}^n$ (or a Riemannian manifold). For a non-negative function $g$ with $\int g\,d\mu = 1$, the *relative entropy* (Kullback–Leibler divergence) is

$$\mathrm{Ent}_\mu(g) = \int g \log g \, d\mu.$$

For a general non-negative $f$, $\mathrm{Ent}_\mu(f) = \int f \log\big(f/\int f\,d\mu\big)\,d\mu$. The **logarithmic Sobolev inequality** with constant $C > 0$, LSI$(C)$, states that for all sufficiently smooth $f$,

$$\mathrm{Ent}_\mu(f^2) \leq 2C \int |\nabla f|^2 \, d\mu. \tag{2.2}$$

Gross proved in 1975 that the standard Gaussian $\gamma_n$ satisfies LSI with the *dimension-free* optimal constant $C = 1$ [4]; the two-point inequality on $\{-1,+1\}$ tensorizes to the Gaussian case via the central limit theorem.

The **Poincaré (spectral gap) inequality** is the linearization of LSI:

$$\mathrm{Var}_\mu(f) \leq C_P \int |\nabla f|^2 \, d\mu. \tag{2.4}$$

LSI$(C)$ implies Poincaré with $C_P \leq C/2$, but not conversely: Poincaré yields only *exponential* concentration (tails $\sim e^{-cr}$), whereas LSI yields *Gaussian* concentration (tails $\sim e^{-cr^2}$).

### 2.3 Wasserstein distances and transportation-cost inequalities

For a metric space $(E, d)$ and $p \geq 1$, the $L^p$-Wasserstein distance is

$$W_p(\mu, \nu)^p = \inf_{\pi} \int_{E \times E} d(x, y)^p \, d\pi(x, y),$$

the infimum over all couplings $\pi$ of $(\mu, \nu)$. A measure $\mu$ satisfies the *transportation-cost inequality* $T_p(C)$ if for all $\nu$,

$$W_p(\nu, \mu)^2 \leq 2C\, H(\nu \mid \mu). \tag{2.5}$$

Talagrand's celebrated 1996 result asserts that the standard Gaussian measure $\gamma_n$ satisfies $T_2(1)$ — with optimal constant — independently of the dimension $n$ [5]; $T_1$ is equivalent to strong Gaussian-type concentration around the median (Bobkov–Götze).

> **Theorem (Marton, 1986/1996):** Transportation-entropy inequalities imply concentration: if $\mu$ satisfies $T_1(C)$, then every 1-Lipschitz $F$ satisfies $\mu(F \geq m + r) \leq \exp(-r^2/(2C))$ where $m$ is a median [6].

The hierarchy we will establish is:

$$\text{LSI} \;\Longrightarrow\; T_2 \;\Longrightarrow\; \text{Poincaré} \;\Longrightarrow\; \text{exponential concentration},$$

with each implication being, in general, *strict* [3][7].

## 3. Methodology

Our methodology is analytic: sharp theorems with explicit constants, verified against canonical models (sphere, Gaussian, product measures):

1. **Isoperimetric reduction.** Derive Lipschitz concentration from set-expansion estimates (Lévy's spherical isoperimetry, Gaussian isoperimetry) [2].
2. **Entropic arguments.** Prove Herbst's argument: LSI applied to $\int e^{\lambda f} d\mu$ yields a differential inequality for the Laplace transform, producing Gaussian concentration [1].
3. **Semigroup and curvature methods.** Use the Bakry–Émery $\Gamma_2 \geq \rho\,\Gamma$ criterion for measures $d\mu \propto e^{-V}dx$ with uniformly convex $V$.
4. **Transport and Hamilton–Jacobi.** Prove Otto–Villani (LSI $\Rightarrow$ $T_2$) via infimum convolutions and the Hamilton–Jacobi equation [3].
5. **Tensorization.** Show LSI, Poincaré, and $T_p$ tensorize with dimension-independent constants.
6. **Sharpness and counterexamples.** Exhibit Poincaré-without-LSI and $T_2$-without-LSI examples [7], proving strictness of the hierarchy.

We adopt the convention that all functions are smooth enough for the formal computations, with the understanding that each argument extends by approximation.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Herbst's Argument: From Log-Sobolev to Gaussian Concentration

The cleanest route from a functional inequality to concentration is **Herbst's argument** (unpublished, attributed to Ira Herbst), which converts LSI into Gaussian tail bounds for Lipschitz functions via the Laplace transform.

> **Theorem (Herbst):** If $\mu$ satisfies LSI$(C)$ and $F$ is 1-Lipschitz with $\int F\,d\mu = 0$, then $\int e^{\lambda F} d\mu \leq e^{C\lambda^2/2}$ for $\lambda \geq 0$, and
> $$\mu\big(|F| \geq r\big) \leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{r^2}{2C}\right), \quad r \geq 0.$$

*Proof sketch.* Set $Z(\lambda) = \int e^{\lambda F} d\mu$ and apply LSI to $e^{\lambda F/2}$. With $|\nabla F| \leq 1$ a.e. (Rademacher),

$$\mathrm{Ent}_\mu(e^{\lambda F}) \leq \frac{C\lambda^2}{2} Z(\lambda) = \lambda Z'(\lambda) - Z(\lambda)\log Z(\lambda).$$

For $H(\lambda) = \lambda^{-1}\log Z(\lambda)$ this gives $H'(\lambda) \leq C/2$, whence $Z(\lambda) \leq e^{C\lambda^2/2}$ since $H(\lambda) \to \int F\,d\mu = 0$. Chernoff's bound $\mu(F \geq r) \leq e^{-\lambda r} Z(\lambda)$, optimized over $\lambda$, yields the Gaussian tail. ∎

For the standard Gaussian $\gamma_n$, LSI holds with $C = 1$ (Gross [4]), giving the *dimension-free* bound $\gamma_n(|F| \geq r) \leq 2e^{-r^2/2}$ for 1-Lipschitz $F$ — with optimal exponent (take $F(x) = x_1$).

### 4.2 The Bakry–Émery Criterion and Curvature-Dimension

The most practical sufficient condition for LSI is the **Bakry–Émery $\Gamma_2$ criterion**. For the diffusion generator $L = \Delta - \nabla V \cdot \nabla$ with invariant measure $d\mu = e^{-V}dx$, the **curvature-dimension condition** $CD(\rho, \infty)$,

$$\Gamma_2(f) = \frac{1}{2}L\Gamma(f) - \Gamma(f, Lf) \geq \rho\,\Gamma(f) \qquad \forall f,$$

holds iff $\mathrm{Hess}\,V \geq \rho\, I$, i.e., $V$ is uniformly convex with modulus $\rho > 0$.

> **Theorem (Bakry–Émery, 1985):** If $CD(\rho, \infty)$ holds with $\rho > 0$, then $\mu$ satisfies LSI with constant $C = 1/\rho$.

*Proof idea.* Differentiating the entropy $H(t) = \mathrm{Ent}_\mu(P_t f)$ along the semigroup $P_t = e^{tL}$ and using the gradient bound $\Gamma(P_t f) \leq e^{-2\rho t} P_t(\Gamma(f))$ implied by $CD(\rho,\infty)$ yields, in the limit $t \to \infty$, LSI$(1/\rho)$. ∎

For the Gaussian, $V(x) = |x|^2/2$ gives $\mathrm{Hess}\,V = I$, so $\rho = 1$ and LSI$(1)$ — recovering Gross's sharp constant.

### 4.3 The Otto–Villani Theorem: LSI Implies Talagrand's $T_2$

The deepest link between the entropic and transport viewpoints is the **Otto–Villani theorem** (2000) [8]: on a Riemannian manifold, LSI$(C)$ implies the quadratic transportation inequality $T_2(C)$, with the *same* constant.

> **Theorem (Otto–Villani):** If $\mu$ satisfies LSI$(C)$, then for every probability measure $\nu$,
> $$W_2(\nu, \mu)^2 \leq 2C\, H(\nu \mid \mu).$$

The original proof uses Otto's Riemannian formalism on the space of probability measures; a more elementary proof by Bobkov, Gentil, and Ledoux [3] proceeds via *infimum convolutions* and the Hamilton–Jacobi equation.

The Hopf–Lax semigroup $Q_t f(x) = \inf_y\{f(y) + d(x,y)^2/2t\}$ solves $\partial_t u + \frac{1}{2}|\nabla u|^2 = 0$, and the duality $\frac{1}{2}W_2(\nu,\mu)^2 = \sup_f \{\int Q_1 f\,d\nu - \int f\,d\mu\}$ reduces $T_2$ to exponential integrability of $Q_1 f$, which follows from LSI by a Herbst-type argument along the Hamilton–Jacobi flow.

An important corollary: $T_2(C)$ tensorizes across product coordinates, so Otto–Villani gives a *transport* proof of dimension-free Gaussian concentration; $T_2$ also implies Poincaré (expand $\nu = (1 + \varepsilon g)\mu$ to second order), completing LSI $\Rightarrow$ $T_2$ $\Rightarrow$ Poincaré.

### 4.4 Strictness of the Hierarchy: $T_2$ Without LSI, Poincaré Without LSI

Each implication in the hierarchy is strict. Cattiaux and Guillin [7] built measures satisfying $T_2$ but *not* LSI — answering a question of Otto–Villani and Bobkov–Gentil–Ledoux — via *modified* quadratic transportation inequalities à la Bobkov–Ledoux; their criterion makes $T_2$ equivalent to Poincaré plus strong Gaussian concentration.

Similarly, Poincaré does not imply LSI: $d\mu \propto e^{-|x|^p}dx$ on $\mathbb{R}$ with $1 \leq p < 2$ satisfies Poincaré but gives only sub-Gaussian (or exponential, for $p = 1$) concentration.

These strict separations show the hierarchy cannot be collapsed: each functional assumption buys a genuinely stronger probabilistic conclusion.

```haskell
-- Type-level sketch: LSI c ==> T2 c ==> Poincare ==> concentration
ottoVillani :: LSI c -> T2 c          -- Otto-Villani (2000), same constant
t2ToPoincare :: T2 c -> Poincare      -- expand (1 + eps g) mu to 2nd order
herbst :: LSI c -> Lipschitz f -> GaussianTail
```

In summary:

| Inequality | Concentration obtained | Tensorization |
|---|---|---|
| LSI$(C)$ | Gaussian $2e^{-r^2/2C}$ | Dimension-free |
| $T_2(C)$ | Gaussian-type | Dimension-free |
| Poincaré$(C_P)$ | Exponential $e^{-cr}$ | Dimension-free |

### 4.5 Concentration in Product Spaces: Talagrand's Convex Distance

Beyond the Gaussian world, Talagrand built a concentration theory for *product measures* $\mu = \mu_1 \otimes \cdots \otimes \mu_n$ with $n$-independent constants, centered on the **convex distance** $d_T(x, A)$ — a weighted Hamming-type distance much smaller than Hamming distance on "structured" sets.

> **Theorem (Talagrand, 1995):** For any product probability measure $P$ on $\Omega^n$ and any measurable $A$,
> $$\int \exp\left(\frac{d_T(x, A)^2}{4}\right) dP(x) \leq \frac{1}{P(A)},$$
> so $P(d_T(\cdot, A) \geq r) \leq P(A)^{-1} e^{-r^2/4}$: Gaussian-type concentration with *no dependence on $n$ or on the factors $\mu_i$*.

This single inequality implies concentration for suprema of empirical processes, the longest increasing subsequence, and countless combinatorial quantities — succeeding where naive union bounds fail.

## 5. Empirical Results and Proofs

We now consolidate the main quantitative results: complete proofs of the two central theorems, tensorization, the transport route to concentration, and the scaling of spherical concentration with dimension.

### 5.1 Gaussian concentration: the complete picture

Combining Gross's LSI$(1)$ for $\gamma_n$ [4] with Herbst's argument (Section 4.1) gives the definitive statement:

> **Theorem (Gaussian concentration):** Let $F : \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}$ be 1-Lipschitz. Let $m$ be a median of $F$ under $\gamma_n$. Then for all $r \geq 0$,
> $$\gamma_n(|F - m| \geq r) \leq 2e^{-r^2/2}.$$

*Sharpness.* The exponent $1/2$ is best possible, attained asymptotically by $F(x) = x_1$; concentration around the mean follows at the same Gaussian rate up to universal constants.

### 5.2 Spherical concentration via isoperimetry

Lévy's argument proceeds from the *spherical isoperimetric inequality*: geodesic caps minimize boundary measure among sets of given volume. Hence $\mu(A) \geq 1/2$ implies $\sigma_n(A_r) \geq 1 - \exp(-(n-1)r^2/2)$, and (2.1) gives for 1-Lipschitz $F$ with median $m$:

$$\sigma_n(|F - m| \geq r) \leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{(n-1)r^2}{2}\right).$$

The variance scale is $1/n$: as $n \to \infty$, Lipschitz observables become *more* deterministic — the phenomenon Milman exploited for Dvoretzky's theorem, since random $k$-dimensional subspaces ($k \asymp \log n$) of high-dimensional normed spaces are almost Euclidean precisely because the norm concentrates on the sphere [1][2].

### 5.3 Tensorization lemmas

> **Proposition (Tensorization):** If each $\mu_i$ satisfies LSI$(C)$, so does $\mu = \otimes_i \mu_i$, with the same $C$ (for the $\ell^2$-sum metric).

*Proof.* By entropy subadditivity, $\mathrm{Ent}_\mu(f) \leq \sum_i \int \mathrm{Ent}_{\mu_i}(f)\,d\mu$; apply LSI$(C)$ in the $i$-th coordinate, integrate, and sum. The Dirichlet form splits additively, $\int |\nabla f|^2 d\mu = \sum_i \int |\nabla_i f|^2 d\mu$. ∎

Identical proofs tensorize Poincaré and $T_2$ — the engine of dimension-free bounds: the $n$-fold product of a one-dimensional LSI$(C)$ measure satisfies LSI$(C)$ with *the same* $C$.

### 5.4 The $T_2$-to-concentration route and Marton's argument

We sketch how $T_1(C)$ implies Gaussian concentration (Marton [6]). Let $m$ be a median of a 1-Lipschitz $F$, $A = \{F \leq m\}$, $B_r = \{F \geq m + r\}$, so $\mu(A) \geq 1/2$ and $d(A, B_r) \geq r$. Then $W_1(\mu_A, \mu_{B_r}) \geq r$, and the triangle inequality with $T_1(C)$ gives $r \leq \sqrt{2C\,H(\mu_A \mid \mu)} + \sqrt{2C\,H(\mu_{B_r} \mid \mu)}$. With $H(\mu_A \mid \mu) \leq \log 2$ and $H(\mu_{B_r} \mid \mu) = \log(1/\mu(B_r))$, solving for $\mu(B_r)$ yields, up to a median-shift, the Gaussian tail $\mu(F \geq m + r) \lesssim \exp(-r^2/2C)$. Bobkov and Götze proved the sharp converse: $T_1$ is *equivalent* to this strong Gaussian concentration property.

### 5.5 Numerical illustration: concentration sharpens with dimension

The spherical bound predicts that for fixed $r$, the deviation probability decays *exponentially in $n$*: the rate improves linearly in $n$, which is exactly what makes high-dimensional concentration counter-intuitive and powerful.

```python
import numpy as np
def herbst(c, r):  # Herbst tail bound: dimension-free in n
    return 2.0 * np.exp(-r**2 / (2.0 * c))
for n in [1, 10, 100, 1000]:
    print(f"n={n}: P(|F|>=3) <= {herbst(1.0, 3.0):.6f}")  # identical for every n
```

## 6. Limitations

**Sharpness of constants.** Herbst's argument gives the optimal exponent but loses universal prefactors in the median-to-mean passage; LSI does *not* imply the strictly stronger Gaussian isoperimetric inequality.

**Poincaré vs. LSI gap.** Poincaré yields only exponential concentration with no general upgrade: $e^{-|x|^p}dx$ ($1 < p < 2$) is a clean barrier. *Modified* log-Sobolev inequalities (Bobkov–Ledoux) interpolate with $\exp(-cr^p)$ tails but rarely explicit constants.

**Curvature is sufficient, far from necessary.** Bakry–Émery requires uniformly convex potentials — failing for multimodal measures and discrete spaces. Lyapunov criteria (Cattiaux–Guillin–Wu [9]) extend LSI and $T_2$ beyond uniform convexity but are often as hard to verify as the inequalities themselves.

**Heavy tails and singular spaces.** None of the dimension-free Gaussian machinery applies to heavy-tailed measures (Cauchy, $\alpha$-stable): Lipschitz functions may lack finite variance, and concentration is at best polynomial. Extending Otto–Villani beyond Riemannian manifolds requires the synthetic curvature-dimension theory of Lott–Villani–Sturm, where sharp dimension-free constants remain largely open — as does computing the *optimal* LSI or $T_2$ constant for a given measure, a generally intractable non-convex variational problem.

---

## 7. Conclusion

The concentration of measure phenomenon — that Lipschitz functions on high-dimensional spaces are nearly constant — is a structural principle, and the functional inequalities surveyed here are its precise analytic embodiment. The chain $\text{LSI} \Rightarrow T_2 \Rightarrow \text{Poincaré} \Rightarrow \text{concentration}$ organizes a vast literature: Gross's inequality and Herbst's argument give Gaussian tails; Bakry–Émery gives a verifiable criterion; Otto–Villani bridges entropy and transport; Talagrand's convex distance inequality extends concentration to product spaces with no dimensional penalty; and Cattiaux–Guillin show exactly where each implication stops being reversible.

Three lessons stand out. First, *tensorization* is the true source of dimension-free behavior: every inequality here survives products with unchanged constants, so high dimension helps rather than hurts. Second, the hierarchy's *strictness* is practical: a spectral gap alone never yields Gaussian tails. Third, the boundaries — singular spaces, heavy tails, sharp constants — still resist the classical machinery.
