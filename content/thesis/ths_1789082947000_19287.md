# Neural Population Coding: Fisher Information, Noise Correlations, and Optimal Decoding in Cortical Circuits

## Abstract

How does a population of noisy cortical neurons collectively encode the continuous features of the world—orientation, direction of motion, position in space? This thesis develops the quantitative theory of **neural population coding**: the mapping from high-dimensional, stochastic neural activity to scalar stimulus estimates. We begin with the encoding formalism—tuning curves, noise distributions, and the Cramér–Rao bound—and derive the linear Fisher information as the canonical measure of coding fidelity [1]. The heart of the work is the modern understanding of **noise correlations**: shared trial-to-trial variability that cannot be averaged away and that, in a precise and generic sense, saturates the information carried by arbitrarily large populations [2][3]. We prove the decomposition of the noise covariance into information-limiting and information-free components, show why differential correlations are the universal bottleneck, and analyze the optimal linear and nonlinear decoders that attain the Cramér–Rao bound [4][7]. Empirical evidence from macaque visual cortex, hippocampal ensembles, and large-scale Neuropixels recordings is reviewed [5][8][9], and we close with the open problems that keep the field honest: nonlinearity, nonstationarity, and the gap between information available and information used.

## 1. Introduction

Cortical neurons are noisy: the same oriented grating evokes wildly fluctuating spike counts across repeated presentations [5]. Yet perception is stable and precise—human observers discriminate orientation differences of a few degrees. The resolution of this paradox is **population coding**: the *joint* activity of hundreds of neurons, pooled and read out downstream, achieves precision no single neuron possesses.

This thesis provides a self-contained, mathematically rigorous account of how populations of neurons encode continuous stimuli, how much information they can carry, and what limits them. Three questions organize everything:

1. **Encoding**: How do the mean response and its variability jointly represent a stimulus parameter?
2. **Information**: How much can be learned about the stimulus from the population response, and how does this scale with population size?
3. **Decoding**: What is the optimal rule for recovering the stimulus from a noisy population vector, and can biology implement it?

The answers form one of the most satisfying success stories of theoretical neuroscience. The encoding model of *tuning curves plus noise* [7] leads, via classical estimation theory, to the **Fisher information** as the measure of coding fidelity. *Noise correlations*—shared variability between neurons—first clarified and then overturned naïve intuitions about averaging [6]. And the theory of **information-limiting correlations** gave the story its modern, definitive form: a hard ceiling on information whose geometry can be written down exactly [1].

> **Thesis:** For any neural population with noise covariance Σ and mean tuning f(s), the optimal decoding precision is bounded by the linear Fisher information I(s) = f′(s)ᵀΣ⁻¹f′(s). Information-limiting correlations—covariance components proportional to f′(s)f′(s)ᵀ—force I(s) to saturate as population size grows, regardless of heterogeneity, and only the magnitude of this component determines the asymptotic coding limit.

The rest of this document is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews the encoding formalism and estimation theory. Section 3 describes the methodology: analytical derivations, controlled simulations, and the statistical estimators used on real data. Section 4 is the deep dive: differential correlations, scaling laws, the geometry of optimal decoders, and efficient coding. Section 5 presents empirical evidence and a key theorem. Section 6 confronts the limitations honestly. Section 7 concludes.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 Encoding: tuning curves and noise models

Consider a scalar stimulus *s* (say, the orientation of a grating) and a population of *N* neurons. The mean response of neuron *i* to stimulus *s* is its **tuning curve** *fᵢ(s)*. For orientation, tuning is approximately periodic and bell-shaped; for motion direction, it is often modeled as a von Mises or cosine function [5]. The actual spike count vector on a single trial is **r** = (r₁, …, r_N)ᵀ, drawn from some conditional distribution *p*(**r**|*s*). The simplest model is Poisson-like: each neuron fires independently with rate *fᵢ(s)*,

p(rᵢ|s) = e^(−fᵢ(s)) fᵢ(s)^(rᵢ) / rᵢ!

in which the variance equals the mean. Real cortical data are over-dispersed relative to Poisson (Fano factors of 1–2), but the qualitative theory is robust to this choice [3].

The crucial fact, established in the seminal experiments of Zohary, Shadlen, and Newsome [5][6], is that the variability of different neurons is **not independent**. Neurons with similar tuning share trial-to-trial fluctuations; the correlation of spike counts across repeated identical stimuli—*noise correlations*—typically averages 0.01–0.2 in cortex [5]. These correlations are positive, weak, and widespread, and they change the entire scaling story.

### 2.2 Estimation theory and the Cramér–Rao bound

Decoding is estimation: from the observed vector **r**, produce an estimate *ŝ*(**r**) of the stimulus. Any unbiased estimator has variance bounded below by the inverse Fisher information [1]:

> **Theorem (Cramér–Rao):** For an unbiased estimator *ŝ* of *s*, Var(ŝ) ≥ 1/I(s), where the Fisher information is
>
> I(s) = E[(∂/∂s log p(**r**|s))²] = −E[∂²/∂s² log p(**r**|s)].

The Fisher information is a *local* measure: it quantifies discriminability of *s* versus *s* + *ds* for infinitesimal *ds*. For the multivariate Gaussian model **r**|*s* ∼ 𝒩(**f**(s), Σ(s)), it takes the closed form

I(s) = **f**′(s)ᵀ Σ(s)⁻¹ **f**′(s) + ½ Tr[Σ⁻¹ Σ′ Σ⁻¹ Σ′],

and when the covariance varies slowly with *s* (the standard assumption), the first term—the **linear Fisher information**—dominates:

I_lin(s) = **f**′(s)ᵀ Σ⁻¹ **f**′(s).   [1][3]

This single formula is the workhorse of the entire field. If neurons are independent, Σ is diagonal, and I_lin grows linearly with *N*: the independent population gains √N in precision, as naïve averaging suggests. But once Σ has off-diagonal structure, the inverse Σ⁻¹ redistributes weight, and the scaling can be anything from supralinear to saturated.

### 2.3 Decoding: from the center of mass to the optimal linear estimator

The brain must implement some decoding rule. The simplest is the **population vector** or center-of-mass readout; more principled is the **optimal linear estimator** (OLE), which minimizes mean squared error among linear functions of **r**. Seung and Sompolinsky [7] showed that for large, well-distributed populations, a linear readout can achieve near-optimal performance, and that the maximum-likelihood estimator asymptotically attains the Cramér–Rao bound. Bayesian population-coding frameworks [4] extended this to the posterior setting, showing that with flat priors the posterior mean approaches the efficient estimator, and that the shape of the tuning curves determines the geometry of the posterior.

A central and subtle point [4]: an optimal decoder *must know* the noise covariance Σ to weight neurons correctly. A decoder that assumes independence when correlations exist pays an information penalty; a decoder matched to the true Σ extracts everything extractable.

## 3. Methodology

Our analysis rests on three methodological pillars: (i) exact analytical results for the Gaussian and Poisson encoding models; (ii) Monte Carlo simulations of tuning-curve populations with structured covariance, used to verify scaling laws and estimator convergence; and (iii) the bias-corrected estimation procedures developed for finite experimental data [3].

For the simulations, each neuron *i* has a preferred stimulus φᵢ uniformly tiling the circle, with von Mises tuning fᵢ(s) = R₀ + R₁ exp(κ cos(s − φᵢ)), and the noise covariance Σ is constructed with tunable structure (independent, limited-range, or differential) while remaining positive definite. Linear Fisher information is computed by direct inversion; the optimal linear estimator is solved in closed form. The Haskell fragment below expresses the estimation problem in a typed encoding-model DSL, and the Python fragment measures how I_lin scales with *N* under different correlation structures.

```haskell
-- A minimal typed model of a population code.
data Tuning  = VonMises { baseline :: Double, gain :: Double, kappa :: Double }
data Corr    = Independent | LimitedRange Double | Differential Double

-- Encoding model: stimulus -> mean vector and covariance.
encode :: [Double] -> Tuning -> Corr -> Double -> ([Double], [[Double]])
encode prefs t c s = (means, cov)
  where
    means = [ baseline t + gain t * exp (kappa t * cos (s - phi))
            | phi <- prefs ]
    cov   = case c of
      Independent        -> diag (means)            -- Poisson-like variance
      LimitedRange a     -> [ [ sqrt (mi*mj) * a ^ dist i j | (j,mj) <- zip [0..] means ]
                           | (i,mi) <- zip [0..] means ]
      Differential eps   -> addRank1 (diag means) (map f' means) eps
```

```python
import numpy as np

def linear_fisher(fprime, Sigma):
    """I_lin = f'^T Sigma^{-1} f' — the central quantity of this thesis."""
    return float(fprime @ np.linalg.solve(Sigma, fprime))

def build_sigma(f, corr_strength, mode, N):
    D = np.diag(np.maximum(f, 1e-3))          # Poisson-like variances
    if mode == "independent":
        return D
    i = np.arange(N)
    dist = np.minimum(np.abs(i[:, None] - i[None, :]), N - np.abs(i[:, None] - i[None, :]))
    C = corr_strength * np.exp(-dist / (N / 8))   # limited-range profile
    S = np.sqrt(np.outer(f, f)) * C
    np.fill_diagonal(S, f)
    return S

# Sweep population size; watch I_lin saturate under differential correlations.
Ns = [50, 100, 200, 400, 800, 1600]
for mode in ["independent", "limited-range", "differential"]:
    infos = []
    for N in Ns:
        f, fp = tuning_population(N)          # means and derivatives at s0
        Sig = build_sigma(f, 0.2, mode, N)
        if mode == "differential":
            Sig = Sig + 1e-3 * np.outer(fp, fp)   # the limiting component
        infos.append(linear_fisher(fp, Sig))
    print(mode, [round(v, 1) for v in infos])
```

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 The geometry of Fisher information in correlated populations

The linear Fisher information I_lin = **f**′ᵀΣ⁻¹**f**′ has a crisp geometric meaning. The vector **f**′(s) is the *signal direction*: the direction in activity space along which the population mean moves when the stimulus changes. The covariance Σ describes an ellipsoid of noise around that mean. Fisher information is the squared signal length measured in the *whitened* metric—signal projected onto the inverse noise.

This geometry immediately explains why *some* correlations help and *some* hurt [6]. Decompose any noise vector into a component along **f**′ and an orthogonal component. Noise *orthogonal* to the signal direction is harmless: the decoder can project it away, and with enough neurons it averages out. Noise *along* the signal direction is catastrophic: it moves the population response exactly as a stimulus change would, and no amount of averaging can distinguish the two. This is the intuition behind **differential correlations** [1].

Formally, any covariance can be decomposed as

Σ = Σ₀ + ε **f**′**f**′ᵀ,

where Σ₀ is the "harmless" part and ε**f**′**f**′ᵀ is the **information-limiting component**. The Sherman–Morrison formula then yields the celebrated decomposition [1][4]:

> **Theorem (Information-limiting decomposition):** If Σ = Σ₀ + ε **f**′**f**′ᵀ, then
>
> 1/I = 1/I₀ + ε,
>
> where I = **f**′ᵀΣ⁻¹**f**′ and I₀ = **f**′ᵀΣ₀⁻¹**f**′.

The proof is a two-line application of Sherman–Morrison, but the consequence is profound: *no matter how large I₀ grows with N, the total information I saturates at 1/ε*. Information cannot exceed the inverse variance of the component of noise that masquerades as signal. And—critically—this holds for *any* population, homogeneous or heterogeneous, Gaussian or not (for the linear Fisher information) [2][3].

### 4.2 Noise correlations: measurement, structure, and mechanism

Empirically, noise correlations in cortex are [5]:

- **Weak but positive**: mean pairwise values of 0.01–0.2, depending on area, layer, distance, and behavioral state.
- **Structured**: neurons with similar tuning (positive *signal* correlations) tend to have stronger *noise* correlations; this structure—*limited-range correlations*—was the original candidate mechanism for saturation.
- **State-dependent**: attention, adaptation, and learning modulate correlation magnitude and structure [5][6]. In *homogeneous* populations (identical tuning curves, just shifted), limited-range correlations contain a differential component, and information saturates—this was known since the 1990s. In *heterogeneous* populations (varying amplitudes, widths, baselines—as in most brain areas), Shamir and Sompolinsky showed that limited-range correlations need *not* saturate information: information can again grow with *N* [3]. ### 4.3 Scaling laws: from linear growth to the 1/ε ceiling

The practical content of the theory is a set of scaling laws summarized below.

| Correlation structure | Population | I_lin scaling with N | Asymptote |
|---|---|---|---|
| Independent | any | ∝ N | unbounded |
| Limited-range, homogeneous tuning | uniform | saturates | finite |
| Limited-range, heterogeneous tuning | varied | ∝ N (slope < independent) | unbounded |
| Differential (ε > 0) | any | I₀(N) grows, I → 1/ε | **1/ε** |

The last row is the universal result [1].

A final quantitative subtlety [3]: the differential component ε is extremely hard to estimate from finite data, because it is a projection of Σ onto the rank-one direction **f**′**f**′ᵀ, and covariance estimates from limited trials are noisy in exactly the ways that matter.

### 4.4 Optimal decoding: the matched filter and its biological plausibility

Given the encoding model, what is the optimal decoder?

**w**_opt ∝ Σ⁻¹**f**′,

i.e., each neuron's vote is weighted by the signal direction whitened by the inverse noise covariance [7].

But can cortex implement Σ⁻¹**f**′?

1. **Learning**: readout weights can be learned by error-driven plasticity if the learning rule has access to the right error signal; whether biological plasticity approximates the inverse-covariance computation is unresolved [4].
2. **Mismatch cost**: a decoder that ignores correlations (Σ̂ = diag) suffers an information loss that can be computed exactly; in practice, the loss is often modest when correlations are weak, which may explain why simple readouts work [6].
3. **Nonlinearity**: the optimal decoder for non-Gaussian noise or coarse discrimination is generally nonlinear. Extensions of the information-limiting framework to nonlinear statistics [4] show that the same 1/ε bound applies to the covariance of the *relevant nonlinear features*—neither more neurons nor richer decoders can escape it.

### 4.5 Efficient coding: why the code looks the way it does

The final piece of the deep dive asks *why* tuning curves and correlations have the observed form.

- **Decorrelation as a design principle**: since shared noise along the signal direction is the enemy, circuits should actively *decorrelate* responses. Indeed, retinal and early visual processing exhibit strong decorrelation mechanisms, and Ecker et al. showed that asynchronous cortical states carry more information precisely because correlations drop [3][5].
- **Matching the code to the decoder**: information is only useful if it can be read out. An elegant formulation represents *probability distributions* (not point estimates) in population activity, with fidelity governed by the same Fisher-information geometry [4].

*In short*: cortex sits at a compromise—correlated enough to reflect shared inputs and recurrent dynamics, decorrelated enough to keep the information-limiting component small, and read out by decoders at least approximately matched to the true noise structure.

## 5. Empirical Results / Proofs

### 5.1 Theorem: universality of the information limit

We now state and prove the central result in full generality [1].

> **Theorem (Universal information limit):** Let **r**|*s* be a population response with mean **f**(s) and covariance Σ(s) = Σ₀(s) + ε(s)**f**′(s)**f**′(s)ᵀ, with Σ₀ positive definite. Then the linear Fisher information satisfies
>
> I_lin(s) = 1 / (1/I₀(s) + ε(s)) ≤ 1/ε(s),
>
> where I₀(s) = **f**′(s)ᵀΣ₀⁻¹**f**′(s). In particular, lim sup_{N→∞} I_lin(s) ≤ 1/ε(s) irrespective of tuning heterogeneity, and equality holds in the limit of vanishing non-limiting noise.

*Proof.* By the Sherman–Morrison formula,

(Σ₀ + ε**f**′**f**′ᵀ)⁻¹ = Σ₀⁻¹ − (ε Σ₀⁻¹**f**′**f**′ᵀΣ₀⁻¹)/(1 + ε**f**′ᵀΣ₀⁻¹**f**′).

Contracting with **f**′ on both sides gives I = I₀ − εI₀²/(1 + εI₀) = I₀/(1 + εI₀), and inverting yields 1/I = 1/I₀ + ε. ∎

Two remarks. First, the proof uses *nothing* about the tuning curves beyond the existence of **f**′—this is why heterogeneity cannot rescue the population [1]. Second, the bound is on *linear* Fisher information; for the full Fisher information including the covariance-derivative term, additional structure can in principle help, but the linear component is the one accessible to linear readouts and is the standard measure in the experimental literature [3].

### 5.2 Empirical evidence

The theoretical prediction—that information saturates with population size in real cortex—has been tested with increasing sophistication:

1. **Macaque MT and V1**: early work found that pooling correlated MT neurons yielded diminishing returns, motivating the entire research program [5][6]. Modern re-analyses with bias-corrected estimators confirm sublinear information growth [3].
2. **Mouse visual cortex (Neuropixels)**: Kafashan et al. [8] recorded thousands of neurons and showed that the scaling of Fisher information follows the predicted I_N⁻¹ = I_{0,N}⁻¹ + I_∞⁻¹ law with finite I_∞—the cleanest population-level signature of information-limiting correlations to date.
3. **Hippocampus**: spatial decoding error in CA1 ensembles asymptotes to a nonzero floor as ensemble size grows; shuffling trials (which destroys noise correlations while preserving tuning) removes the floor [9].
4. **Perceptual correspondence**: Rumyantsev et al. placed fundamental bounds on the fidelity of sensory cortical coding and showed that behavioral thresholds are consistent with a finite, correlation-limited information budget [4].

A persistent puzzle, worth stating plainly: some studies report neural information *exceeding* perceptual performance by large factors, while others find approximate equality [3]. The resolution likely involves distinguishing the information *available* in a population from the information *used* by the animal's actual decoder—a theme we revisit in the limitations.

## 6. Limitations

Intellectual honesty requires enumerating what the theory does *not* do.

1. **Fisher information is local.** It measures fine discrimination and is blind to global errors—catastrophic confusions between distant stimuli and perceptual illusions all lie outside its scope [3].
2. **The brain may not decode optimally—or at all.** Fisher information bounds the *best possible* estimator; the animal's actual decoder may be far from optimal, and in many natural behaviors the brain never explicitly estimates the stimulus parameter. Information *available* ≠ information *used* [4][6].
3. **Nonstationarity.** Tuning curves drift over days; correlations change with attention, arousal, and learning [5]. Extending the theory to nonstationary codes is largely open.
4. **Beyond second order.** The entire framework is built on means and covariances. Higher-order correlations (synchrony, precise spike timing patterns) can carry information invisible to I_lin, and work on nonlinear population codes is only beginning to map this territory [4].
5. **Estimability.** The differential component ε is devilishly hard to estimate from finite data, and naive estimates of I_lin from small trial counts are wildly biased upward [3]. Every empirical claim in this area must be read with the estimator's bias in mind.
6. **Mechanistic gap.** We know the *statistical* origin of information-limiting correlations (shared upstream variability through nonlinear tuning [2]), but the full *circuit* story—how specific cell types, layers, and recurrent motifs contribute to ε—remains incomplete.

## 7. Conclusion

The theory of neural population coding has converged on a remarkably clean picture. A population encodes a stimulus in its mean activity **f**(s); the precision of any unbiased readout is bounded by the Fisher information I_lin = **f**′ᵀΣ⁻¹**f**′; and the noise covariance generically contains an information-limiting component ε**f**′**f**′ᵀ that caps information at 1/ε no matter how many neurons participate [1]. This result is universal across tuning heterogeneity, has a constructive origin in shared upstream variability [2], and bears clear empirical signatures in cortex and hippocampus [8][9].

The frontier has moved from *whether* information saturates to *how much* and *what for*: quantifying ε in behaving animals, understanding the circuit mechanisms that set it, extending the theory to nonlinear and nonstationary codes, and—most importantly—connecting the information available in a population to the information the animal actually uses [4][6].
