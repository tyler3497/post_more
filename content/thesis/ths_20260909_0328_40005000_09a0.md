# The Lambda Calculus at Scale: Explicit Substitutions, Optimal Reduction, and the Geometry of Interaction

## Abstract

The untyped lambda calculus is the smallest universal programming language, yet evaluating its terms efficiently remains one of the deepest problems in theoretical computer science. This thesis traces the full arc from Church and Rosser's confluence theorem [1] to the modern machinery that makes evaluation practical: de Bruijn's nameless representation, explicit substitution calculi such as λσ [7], Krivine's abstract machine [6], Lévy's theory of redex families and optimal sharing, Lamping's sharing-graph algorithm [4] with its oracle problem [9], and Girard's geometry of interaction [5], which recasts execution as token travel along the paths of a proof net. We compare reduction strategies and abstract machines, dissect why naive substitution causes exponential blowups, explain the distant beta rule of the linear substitution calculus, and survey the cost-model crises that optimal reduction exposed. The treatment is mathematically dense but self-contained, aimed at readers with graduate-level exposure to rewriting theory and programming language semantics.

---

## 1. Introduction

When Church introduced the lambda calculus in the 1930s, evaluation meant a mathematician's hand performing β-reductions on paper. Ninety years later, the same calculus — as Haskell's Core language, as compiler intermediate representation, as the logical substrate of proof assistants — runs at industrial scale. The gap between elegant theory and engineering reality is bridged by a remarkable body of work on *how* reduction should be performed: which redex to contract next, how to represent the delayed substitutions that contraction creates, and how to avoid repeating work already done.

Three pillars structure this investigation. **Confluence and strategy** [1][2] guarantee that evaluation order is a question of efficiency rather than correctness: Church–Rosser ensures all terminating reduction paths agree, while standardization singles out leftmost-outermost reduction as complete. **Explicit substitutions** [7] internalize the meta-level operation of substitution into the object language, turning an informal implementation trick into a syntactic theory. **Optimal reduction** — Lévy's redex families realized by Lamping's sharing graphs [3][4] — asks the deepest question: what is the *minimum* number of β-steps any evaluator must perform, and can a machine achieve it? Girard's geometry of interaction [5] reframes the enterprise as geometry — execution as the circulation of a token through a fixed graph — connecting lambda calculus to linear logic and the abstract machines of Danvy and colleagues [10].

> **Thesis statement.** *The efficient evaluation of lambda terms is not a matter of choosing the right reduction order, but of managing three interacting resources — work (β-steps), sharing (duplication control), and bookkeeping (administrative overhead) — and the history of explicit substitutions, optimal reduction, and the geometry of interaction is the history of discovering that no single mechanism optimizes all three at once.*

## 2. Background

### 2.1 Syntax, β-reduction, and the Church–Rosser theorem

The pure untyped lambda calculus has a famously minimal syntax:

```
t ::= x | λx.t | t t
```

β-reduction is the single computational rule: *(λx.M) N →β M[x := N]* with capture-avoiding substitution, applied in any context — so reduction is nondeterministic.

> **Theorem (Church–Rosser [1]).** *If M →\*β N₁ and M →\*β N₂, then there exists P with N₁ →\*β P and N₂ →\*β P.*

Confluence implies unique normal forms — but not *whether* a sequence reaches one: *(λx.y) Ω* has normal form *y*, yet only the outermost-first strategy finds it.

The standard proof of confluence, due to Tait and Martin-Löf, introduces *parallel reduction* ⇉, which contracts any set of redexes simultaneously and enjoys the diamond property; →\*β, its transitive closure, is hence confluent [2].

### 2.2 The standardization theorem and complete strategies

The **standardization theorem** (Curry–Feys, 1958) states that any reduction sequence can be reorganized into a *standard* one — head reductions first, left-to-right — ending at the same term. Its corollary justifies *normal order* (leftmost-outermost) reduction: if a normal form exists, normal order finds it. Weak head reduction — stopping at a head λ — models the *weak* strategies of implementations. Krivine's machine [6] implements weak head call-by-name; the SECD, CEK, and STG machines implement call-by-value and lazy variants [10].

### 2.3 de Bruijn indices and the substitution problem

Named variables are a constant source of pain: capture-avoiding substitution needs α-renaming, freshness side conditions, and the Barendregt variable convention — an informal discipline that resists mechanization. In 1972, de Bruijn replaced variable *names* with *indices* counting binders between an occurrence and its binder [12]: *λx.λy.x y* becomes *λλ.1 0*. Substitution becomes arithmetic — shifting and lifting indices — and α-equivalence collapses to syntactic equality. His notation underlies virtually every modern explicit-substitution calculus and proof assistant kernel.

De Bruijn exposed a deeper issue: substitution is *not* a primitive but a meta-operation, and naive β-reduction copies the argument *N* once per occurrence of *x* in *M* — a potential exponential blowup. Implementations therefore *delay* substitution in an environment, and the theory of **explicit substitutions** makes this delay a first-class, analyzable object [7].

## 3. Methodology

### 3.1 From meta-substitution to object-substitution

The methodological move of the explicit-substitution program is to extend the syntax with a substitution constructor:

```
t ::= x | λx.t | t t | t[x := u]        (λx-style)
```

or, in the de Bruijn-based λσ calculus of Abadi, Cardelli, Curien, and Lévy [7]:

```
t ::= 1 | t t | λt | t[s]      s ::= id | ↑ | t·s | s∘s
```

Here *t[s]* applies the explicit substitution *s* to *t*, and substitutions compose. The β-rule becomes a *creation* rule *(λa)b → a[b·id]*, and *propagation* rules push the substitution through the term: *(a b)[s] → a[s] b[s]*, *(λa)[s] → λ(a[1·(s∘↑)])*, *1[a·s] → a*, *(t[s])[s′] → t[s∘s′]*. Substitution is thus decomposed into small, local, first-order rewriting steps — what an implementation does, now subject to proof.

### 3.2 The two litmus tests: confluence and preservation of strong normalization

Every explicit-substitution calculus is judged by two properties. **Confluence on open terms** asks whether the extended rewriting system is confluent even when terms contain free variables and suspended substitutions. **Preservation of strong normalization (PSN)** asks whether a pure term that is strongly normalizing under β remains strongly normalizing in the extended calculus — i.e., the administrative rules introduce no new infinite reductions.

These properties are fragile. The original λσ is confluent on closed terms, but Melliès showed in 1995 that typed λ-calculi with explicit substitutions can lose strong normalization. Calculi such as λυ recover PSN by decomposing substitution more finely, at the price of confluence on open terms. The modern consensus, Accattoli and Kesner's **linear substitution calculus** (LSC), uses a *distant* β-rule — *(λx.t) L u → t[x←u] L* where *L* lists explicit substitutions — needing no commuting rules at all, recovering confluence, PSN, and a clean connection to proof nets.

### 3.3 Abstract machines as compiled strategies

An abstract machine compiles a reduction strategy into a deterministic transition system over *states* — code, environment, stack. Danvy's methodology [10] shows machines are *derived*, not invented: closure conversion, CPS transformation, and defunctionalization of continuations turn a compositional evaluator into a machine — Krivine's from call-by-name, the CEK from call-by-value.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Lévy's redex families: what "optimal" means

In 1978, Jean-Jacques Lévy asked a disarmingly simple question: when a redex is *copied* by reduction, are the copies "the same" redex? Consider *(λx.x x)((λy.y) z)*. Contracting the outer redex duplicates the inner redex *(λy.y) z* into two copies. Intuitively those copies share a common origin and *should* be reduced together — doing the same work twice is wasteful.

Lévy formalized this through the **family relation** [3], with three equivalent definitions: the **zigzag** relation (copies modulo permutation of independent steps, closed under symmetry and transitivity); **extraction** (two redexes share a family iff erasing causally irrelevant reductions yields the same creation history); and **labels** (a labeled λ-calculus where redexes with the same label form a family).

> **Definition (Lévy-optimal reduction).** *A reduction sequence is optimal if it never contracts two redexes of the same family in separate steps — every family is contracted at most once, in a single parallel family step.*

> **Theorem (Lévy [3]).** *For every term, all complete family reductions have the same length, and this length is minimal among all complete reductions.*

The number of families contracted is a lower bound no evaluator can beat; whether a machine can *achieve* it without drowning in bookkeeping is the next question.

### 4.2 Lamping's sharing graphs and the oracle problem

John Lamping's 1990 POPL paper [4] gave the first concrete algorithm for Lévy-optimal reduction, based on **sharing graphs**: term graphs in which sharing is explicit. The key nodes are **fan-in / fan-out (multiplexers)**, which delimit a shared subgraph, and **brackets** (croissants and square brackets), Lamping's bookkeeping nodes that track the *scope* of each share so overlapping shares can be disentangled.

Beta reduction and duplication become local, *O(1)* graph-rewriting rules. The profound difficulty — the **oracle problem** — is that when two fan nodes meet, the algorithm must decide whether they are matching delimiters of the *same* share (annihilate) or belong to *different* overlapping shares (duplicate past each other). Lamping's brackets solve this correctly, but at a cost:

> **Theorem (Lawall–Mairson [9]).** *There exist terms whose optimal β-reduction needs only Θ(n) family steps, but for which Lamping's bookkeeping performs Ω(2ⁿ) administrative steps.*

Asperti and Mairson later showed the cost of a single parallel β-step is not bounded by any elementary recursive function — though Asperti's 2017 survey argued the pathology lives in the *terms*, whose Lévy labels genuinely grow non-elementarily. For terms typable in Elementary Affine Logic, the abstract algorithm alone runs in polynomial time. Modern interaction-net runtimes (notably Taelin's HVM) deliberately *omit* the oracle and thereby share computations even *inside* lambda bodies — something GHC cannot do. Gonthier, Abadi, and Lévy's 1992 reformulation replaced brackets with *levels*: nodes carry integer tags, and fans annihilate only at equal levels — a geometric bridge to Girard's world.

### 4.3 The geometry of interaction: execution as token travel

Girard's **geometry of interaction** (GoI) [5], born from linear logic, proposes that the dynamics of cut-elimination — and hence of β-reduction — can be captured *without rewriting the proof at all*. Instead, a fixed graph (a proof net) is traversed by a token, and the token's path *is* the computation.

The algebraic core is the **execution formula**. To each proof net one associates a matrix *M* of paths through the cut-free part (entries in Girard's *dynamic algebra*) and a partial permutation σ recording the cuts; then

> *EX(σ, M) = (1 − σ²)(Σᵢ M(σM)ⁱ)(1 − σ²)*

is invariant under cut-elimination in the multiplicative fragment [5]. The infinite sum resolves the feedback equation: paths bouncing through cuts arbitrarily many times are all accounted for, algebraically rather than operationally.

For the λ-calculus, the GoI yields **token machines** (Mackie; Danos–Regnier): a term compiles to a graph, a token enters at the root and walks it, pushed by a stack of pending questions — like a Krivine machine whose environment has been flattened into geometry. GoI explains *why* sharing graphs work: fan nodes incarnate linear logic's exponential modalities, and the oracle problem is the operational shadow of nesting exponential boxes. Traced monoidal categories (Joyal–Street–Verity) provide the chassis: the execution formula *is* a categorical trace.

### 4.4 Machines and strategies

| Strategy | Redex selection | Finds normal form if one exists? | Sharing of work |
|---|---|---|---|
| Normal order | Leftmost-outermost | Yes (complete) | None |
| Applicative order | Leftmost-innermost | No | None |
| Call-by-name (weak) | Head redex only | Head normal forms only | None |
| Call-by-value (weak) | Values first | Values only | None |
| Call-by-need (weak) | Demanded redexes, memoized | Head normal forms only | Memoized thunks |
| Lévy-optimal | Whole redex families | Yes | Maximal (families) |

The **Krivine machine** [6] is the purest expression of call-by-name. A state is a triple *(code, environment, stack)* of closures; three transitions suffice:

```haskell
-- Krivine abstract machine (call-by-name, weak head reduction)
data Term = Var Int | Lam Term | App Term Term
type Env  = [Closure]
data Closure = Clo Term Env

step :: (Term, Env, [Closure]) -> Maybe (Term, Env, [Closure])
step (Var n, env, stack)       = Just (t, env', stack)
  where Clo t env' = nth env n            -- lookup the nth closure
step (Lam body, env, arg:rest) = Just (body, arg : env, rest)  -- Grab
step (App f a, env, stack)     = Just (f, env, Clo a env : stack)  -- Push
step _                         = Nothing  -- weak head normal form
```

Environments are exactly *delayed explicit substitutions* — the machine is λσ compiled to transitions, correct by a simulation argument [6][10]. Landin's **SECD** (1964) is the ancestor of them all; Felleisen and Friedman's **CEK** makes continuations explicit; Peyton Jones's **Spineless Tagless G-machine** [11] powers Haskell, where GHC Core is a call-by-need explicit-substitution calculus engineered for production.

### 4.5 Call-by-need: memoization as semantics

**Call-by-need** evaluates each argument at most once and memoizes the result. Ariola and Felleisen gave it a rigorous foundation [8]: their calculus extends call-by-name evaluation contexts so evaluation can *enter* a binding, updating it in place when a demanded thunk reduces to a value. Launchbury's natural semantics made this operational: thunks are heap-allocated, forced on demand, and *updated* with their values.

A tiny de Bruijn evaluator shows how substitution, environments, and weak strategies fit together:

```python
# Weak call-by-name evaluator, de Bruijn terms, explicit environment
# Term = ('var', n) | ('lam', body) | ('app', f, a); Env = list of thunks

def nth(env, n):
    for thunk in env:
        if n == 1:
            return thunk
        n -= 1
    raise LookupError("unbound index")

def whnf(term, env):
    while True:
        match term:
            case ('app', f, a):
                term, env = f, env + [(a, env)]   # push argument as thunk
            case ('lam', body):
                return ('closure', term, env)    # weak: stop at lambda
            case ('var', n):                     # variable: force its thunk
                t2, e2 = nth(env, n)
                term, env = t2, e2
```

Add an update step on forcing and this becomes call-by-need. The calculi enabling this analysis compare as follows:

| Calculus | Substitution form | Confluent (open terms) | PSN | Distinguishing feature |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| λσ (Abadi et al. [7]) | *t[s]*, de Bruijn | No | No | Substitutions compose: *s ∘ s′* |
| λx | *t[x:=u]*, named | Yes | Yes | Simple, close to implementations |
| λυ | fine-grained | No | Yes | Decomposed propagation steps |
| LSC (Accattoli–Kesner) | *t[x←u]*, at a distance | Yes | Yes | Distant β-rule, no commuting rules |

## 5. Formal Results and Proofs

### 5.1 Confluence via parallel reduction (Tait–Martin-Löf)

> **Theorem (Church–Rosser [1]).** *→\*β is confluent.*

> **Lemma (Substitution for parallel reduction).** *If M ⇉ M′ and N ⇉ N′, then M[x := N] ⇉ M′[x := N′].*

*Proof sketch.* Define parallel reduction *M ⇉ N* with the crucial rule: if *M ⇉ M′* and *N ⇉ N′* then *(λx.M)N ⇉ M′[x := N′]*. Show (i) *→β ⊆ ⇉ ⊆ →\*β*, so *⇉\* = →\*β*, and (ii) ⇉ has the diamond property, via the substitution lemma above proved by induction on derivations. The diamond lifts to the transitive closure by a strip argument [2]. ∎

### 5.2 Standardization and completeness of normal order

> **Theorem (Standardization).** *If M →\*β N, there is a standard reduction sequence from M to N.*

Any sequence reorders into head-reductions-first order (Mitschke; or via Lévy's labels). Corollary used by every lazy language: *normal order reaches the normal form whenever one exists*. Krivine's machine inherits the weak form: it reaches the weak head normal form exactly when the strategy does [6].

### 5.3 Lévy's optimality theorem

> **Theorem (Lévy [3]).** *All complete family reductions of a term have equal length, and no complete reduction is shorter.*

*Proof sketch.* Each family step contracts precisely the redexes carrying one label, and labels are never duplicated by family steps; hence a complete family reduction contracts each label class exactly once, while any ordinary reduction must contract every label class at least once. ∎

### 5.4 Simulation: LSC into sharing graphs

The linear substitution calculus simulates in Lamping's sharing graphs with constant-factor overhead per *β*-step. The administrative steps — bracket propagation, fan annihilation — are exactly the bookkeeping Lawall and Mairson showed can explode [9]: the β-count is minimal, but the machine-step count is unbounded by any function of it.

### 5.5 Preservation results and their fragility

The λσ calculus is confluent on closed terms and correctly implements β [7], but fails PSN: Melliès exhibited a simply-typed term whose explicit-substitution reductions diverge, because propagation rules can loop against each other through *(t[s])[s′] → t[s∘s′]*. The LSC avoids this structurally — no propagation rules, only *creation* (distant β) and *linear substitution* — enjoying both confluence and PSN; its proof-net reading makes the invariant visible: exponential boxes are never duplicated, only entered.

## 6. Limitations

**The oracle is unavoidable in the worst case.** Lawall and Mairson's exponential bookkeeping [9] is no artifact: any correct implementation of Lévy-optimal reduction must solve fan-pairing, genuinely requiring non-elementary work on adversarial terms. Asperti's defense — the complexity lives in the terms — is plausible but unproven; the polynomial-overhead conjecture remains open.

**Optimality counts the wrong thing.** Lévy-optimality minimizes β-family steps while ignoring machine steps. A sharing-graph evaluator may do exponentially more bookkeeping per family step than a Krivine machine does per weak step; optimal reduction's practical advantage appears only for *strong* reduction, as in proof assistants.

**Explicit substitutions are not free.** Every ES calculus pays in administrative rules; PSN failures show the rules can even introduce divergence. Real compilers (GHC Core) use *ad hoc* let-floating rather than any published calculus — theory guides, but engineering diverges.

**The geometry of interaction is hard to compute with.** The execution formula's infinite sum is a beautiful invariant but not an algorithm; token machines make it operational at the price of potentially exponential path exploration. GoI has been more fruitful as *semantics* than as evaluation technology.

**Cost models remain unsettled.** The invariance thesis demands polynomial mutual simulation of reasonable models; the β-step cost model violates it in both directions. Accattoli's *reasonable* cost models — counting machine steps of designed abstract machines — are the best current answer, conceding that "the" cost of a λ-term is strategy-relative.

## 7. Conclusion

From Church and Rosser's confluence proof [1] to Girard's execution formula [5], the story of lambda calculus at scale is the story of making the invisible visible. Substitution was invisible — a meta-operation — until explicit-substitution calculi [7] made it syntax. Duplication was invisible — hidden inside β-contraction — until Lévy's families [3] named it and Lamping's graphs [4] drew it. Evaluation order was invisible until abstract machines [6][10] compiled it into transitions and call-by-need [8] memoized it into semantics. And the dynamics of proof was invisible until the geometry of interaction rendered it as the path of a token through a fixed graph.

No single system optimizes everything: the Krivine machine shares nothing, Lamping's graphs drown in bookkeeping, the GoI computes nothing directly. The practical synthesis — environments with lazy thunks, distant β-rules, selective sharing — is what production languages actually run. The open frontier is a cost model both *reasonable* and *faithful*, and an optimality theory counting machine steps rather than β-families.
