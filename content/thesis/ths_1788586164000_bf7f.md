This thesis characterizes the cryptographic data path of TLS 1.3 and QUIC at line rates approaching 400 Gbps. We analyze AES-GCM and ChaCha20-Poly1305 record protection through the lens of SIMD vectorization, presenting the interleaved AES-NI plus carry-less multiplication (PCLMULQDQ) pipeline that sustains roughly one byte per cycle on modern cores, and the AVX-512 vectorized GHASH constructions that extend throughput beyond 100 Gbps per core. We examine kernel TLS (kTLS) offload and its zero-copy send semantics, QUIC packet number encryption and header protection as defined in RFC 9001, and the role of hardware accelerators including Intel QuickAssist Technology (QAT) and AWS Nitro cryptographic offload in moving both record encryption and handshake asymmetric operations off the host CPU. Finally, we quantify the overhead of hybrid post-quantum key exchange (X25519 combined with ML-KEM-768) on TLS 1.3 handshake latency, message expansion, and 0-RTT replay exposure, and derive throughput and latency models validated against published benchmark data. Our results identify the precise bottleneck transition from compute-bound AES rounds to memory-bandwidth-bound record framing above 200 Gbps.

# Hardware-Accelerated TLS 1.3 and QUIC Record Protection at 400Gbps

## Abstract

Transport encryption at 400 Gbps line rate imposes cryptographic throughput demands that exceed the capacity of naive software implementations by more than an order of magnitude. This thesis characterizes the cryptographic data path of TLS 1.3 [1] and QUIC [2][3] at such rates. We analyze AES-GCM [4] and ChaCha20-Poly1305 [5] record protection through SIMD vectorization, presenting the interleaved AES-NI plus carry-less multiplication (PCLMULQDQ) pipeline sustaining approximately one byte per cycle per core, and AVX-512 vectorized GHASH constructions exceeding 100 Gbps per core. We examine kernel TLS (kTLS) offload with zero-copy send semantics, QUIC packet number encryption and header protection per RFC 9001, and hardware accelerators (Intel QAT, AWS Nitro) that relocate record encryption and handshake asymmetric operations off host CPUs. Finally, we quantify hybrid post-quantum key exchange overhead (X25519 + ML-KEM-768 [6]) on handshake latency and 0-RTT replay risk, deriving throughput models validated against published benchmarks. Results locate the bottleneck transition from compute-bound AES rounds to memory-bandwidth-bound framing above 200 Gbps.

## 1. Introduction

Modern datacenter fabrics deploy 400-gigabit Ethernet as standard spine-leaf interconnect, and every byte crossing those links in multi-tenant environments is expected to be encrypted in transit. TLS 1.3 removed legacy cipher suites and mandates authenticated encryption with associated data (AEAD); QUIC integrates TLS 1.3's handshake while redefining the record layer entirely, encrypting packet numbers and masking header bits to defeat ossification. The cryptographic cost model therefore splits into two regimes:

- **The data path**: per-record AEAD encryption/decryption, executed billions of times per second at 400 Gbps. For 1500-byte records, 400 Gbps demands roughly 33 million AEAD operations per second *per direction* — before accounting for the smaller records typical of RPC-heavy workloads, which inflate the operation rate dramatically.
- **The control path**: handshake asymmetric cryptography (key exchange, signatures), executed per connection. Post-quantum migration multiplies key and ciphertext sizes by one to two orders of magnitude, stressing both latency budgets and middlebox compatibility.

This thesis unifies these regimes. Section 2 reviews AEAD constructions and the TLS 1.3 / QUIC protection layers. Section 3 describes our measurement and modeling methodology. Section 4 presents the deep technical analysis across five subsections: SIMD AES-GCM pipelines, kTLS offload, QUIC packet protection, hardware accelerators, and hybrid post-quantum handshakes with 0-RTT analysis. Section 5 derives and validates quantitative models; Section 6 discusses limitations; Section 7 concludes.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 AEAD primitives in TLS 1.3

TLS 1.3 (RFC 8446) [1] permits exactly five cipher suites, three built on AES-GCM variants and two on ChaCha20-Poly1305:

| Suite | KDF | AEAD | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| `TLS_AES_128_GCM_SHA256` | HKDF-SHA256 | AES-128-GCM | Dominant in datacenters |
| `TLS_AES_256_GCM_SHA384` | HKDF-SHA384 | AES-256-GCM | 14 rounds vs 10 |
| `TLS_CHACHA20_POLY1305_SHA256` | HKDF-SHA256 | ChaCha20-Poly1305 | Mobile / no-AES-NI |
| `TLS_AES_128_CCM_SHA256` | HKDF-SHA256 | AES-128-CCM | Constrained IoT |
| `TLS_AES_128_CCM_8_SHA256` | HKDF-SHA256 | AES-128-CCM-8 | Truncated tag |

AES-GCM [4] combines counter-mode encryption with GHASH, a universal hash over GF(2^128) defined by the polynomial X^128 + X^7 + X^2 + X + 1. Authentication of associated data and ciphertext reduces to polynomial evaluation, which admits carry-less multiplication acceleration. ChaCha20-Poly1305 [5] replaces the block cipher with the ChaCha20 stream cipher and authentication with the Poly1305 one-time authenticator over the prime field 2^130 - 5; it is constant-time friendly and fast on platforms lacking AES hardware.

> Theorem: (GCM security bound, McGrew–Viega [4]). For AES-GCM with a 96-bit nonce, the advantage of any adversary making q encryption queries totaling sigma blocks is bounded by approximately sigma^2 / 2^129 plus the PRP advantage against AES, provided nonces never repeat.

The nonce-misuse catastrophe — nonce repetition destroys both confidentiality and authenticity in GCM — motivates TLS 1.3's deterministic nonce construction: the 96-bit nonce is formed by XORing the 64-bit sequence number into the `iv` derived from the traffic secret, guaranteeing uniqueness per key [1].

### 2.2 QUIC packet protection

QUIC (RFC 9000) [2] replaces the TLS record layer with its own packet protection (RFC 9001) [3]:

1. **Payload encryption** uses the negotiated AEAD with a nonce derived from the packet number and IV, identical in spirit to TLS 1.3's construction.
2. **Packet number encryption**: a sample of ciphertext (16 bytes) feeds AES-ECB (or ChaCha20, or AES-CTR) to produce a 5-byte mask; the packet number bytes and the header's reserved/key-phase bits are XORed with this mask. This hides packet numbers from observers, defeating transport ossification and complicating traffic analysis.
3. **Header protection** thus applies *after* payload encryption and must be removed *before* payload decryption on receipt — a strict ordering constraint that shapes hardware offload designs.

### 2.3 The 400 Gbps arithmetic

At 400 Gbps with 1500-byte frames, a link carries ~33.3M packets/s. Each QUIC packet needs one AEAD operation plus one header-protection mask derivation (an additional AES block encryption). TLS records multiplex multiple application messages per record but add framing copies. The per-core budget at 3 GHz is therefore brutally tight:

- 33.3M ops/s ÷ 16 cores ≈ 2.08M ops/s/core → ~1440 cycles per packet per core.
- AES-128-GCM on ~1500 bytes at 1 byte/cycle ≈ 1500 cycles — already *over* budget on a single core without vectorization or offload.

> Observation: Software AES-GCM at ~1 cycle/byte cannot sustain 400 Gbps on fewer than ~24 cores even under ideal conditions; real deployments require vectorized GHASH, kTLS zero-copy, or hardware offload.

## 3. Methodology

Our analysis combines three methods:

1. **Algorithmic cost modeling.** We count AES rounds, carry-less multiplications, and memory touches per record as functions of record length L, deriving cycles-per-byte (cpb) models for scalar, AES-NI+PCLMULQDQ, AVX2, and AVX-512 implementations following the optimization lineage of Gueron–Krasnov [7] and subsequent vectorized GHASH work.
2. **System-level accounting.** We model the kernel/user data path: `sendmsg`/`sendfile` copies, TLS framing overhead, kTLS offload semantics (Linux `setsockopt(TCP_ULP, "tls")`), and QUIC's userspace packetization costs, to locate the compute-to-bandwidth bottleneck crossover.
3. **Literature-calibrated validation.** Throughput and latency figures are cross-checked against published benchmarks: OpenSSL speed results, Intel QAT performance briefs, Cloudflare and Google post-quantum handshake experiments, and the TLS 1.3 / QUIC RFC specifications themselves. We report *ranges* rather than single-machine numbers, since absolute throughput varies with microarchitecture.

We assume AES-NI and PCLMULQDQ availability (universal on server CPUs since ~2013), AVX-512 on recent Xeon-class parts, and TLS 1.3 with 16 KiB maximum record size. Side-channel countermeasures (constant-time table-free AES) are treated as mandatory, not optional.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Interleaved AES-NI + PCLMULQDQ AES-GCM pipelines

The canonical high-performance AES-GCM software construction, due to Gueron and Krasnov [7], exploits two independent execution resources:

- **AES-NI** (`AESENC`/`AESENCLAST`) executes one AES round per instruction with data-independent timing, retiring roughly one round per cycle throughput on modern ports.
- **PCLMULQDQ** performs 64x64-to-128-bit carry-less multiplication, the primitive for GF(2^128) multiplication in GHASH.

The key insight is *decoupling*: counter-mode encryption and GHASH authentication are independent until the final tag computation. An optimized implementation processes 4–8 blocks in flight:

```c
/* Interleaved AES-CTR x4 + aggregated GHASH reduction (schematic) */
__m128i ctr0 = _mm_add_epi64(nonce_blk, _mm_set_epi64x(0, 0));
__m128i ctr1 = _mm_add_epi64(nonce_blk, _mm_set_epi64x(0, 1));
__m128i ctr2 = _mm_add_epi64(nonce_blk, _mm_set_epi64x(0, 2));
__m128i ctr3 = _mm_add_epi64(nonce_blk, _mm_set_epi64x(0, 3));
for (r = 0; r < 9; r++) {          /* 9 full rounds, AES-128 */
    ctr0 = _mm_aesenc_si128(ctr0, rk[r]);
    ctr1 = _mm_aesenc_si128(ctr1, rk[r]);
    ctr2 = _mm_aesenc_si128(ctr2, rk[r]);
    ctr3 = _mm_aesenc_si128(ctr3, rk[r]);
    /* GHASH multiply of previously produced ciphertext words
       issues on port 5 (PCLMUL) while AES uses port 0/1 */
    acc = ghash_mul_accumulate(acc, ctxt_word, H_powers);
}
```

Because `AESENC` and `PCLMULQDQ` dispatch to different execution ports, the GHASH multiplications *hide under* the AES rounds: the marginal cost of authentication approaches zero for large messages, and total throughput converges to the AES-CTR rate of ~0.6–1.0 cycles/byte. The GHASH reduction itself uses the reflected-polynomial trick: precompute H^2, H^3, H^4 (powers of the hash subkey) and aggregate four blocks per reduction step, cutting the number of expensive modular reductions by 4x.

**AVX-512 vectorization** extends this further. VAES instructions process four AES blocks per instruction (`vaesenc` on 512-bit registers), and `VPCLMULQDQ` performs two carry-less multiplications per instruction. Reported implementations exceed 0.3 cpb for AES-128-GCM on Ice Lake-class cores — over 100 Gbps per core on large records [8]. The practical ceiling then becomes load/store bandwidth: at 0.3 cpb and 3 GHz, a core demands ~10 GB/s of memory traffic just for plaintext/ciphertext streaming, brushing against per-core L2/L3 bandwidth limits.

### 4.2 Kernel TLS (kTLS) offload and zero-copy semantics

kTLS (Linux `TCP_ULP`, since 4.13) moves the TLS record layer into the kernel [10]. The application performs the handshake in userspace, then installs the traffic keys via `setsockopt(SOL_TLS, TLS_TX, ...)`; subsequent `send()` calls pass *plaintext*, and the kernel performs framing, nonce assignment, and AES-GCM encryption during TCP segmentation.

The performance wins are structural:

1. **Zero-copy transmit.** With `MSG_ZEROCOPY` or `sendfile`, plaintext pages are encrypted in place during DMA to the NIC — the classic double copy (user-to-kernel-to-NIC) collapses.
2. **Record framing in the segmentation path.** The kernel already walks SKBs for TSO/GRO; performing AEAD there amortizes per-record overhead and enables NIC TLS offload (`ethtool` `tls-hw-tx-offload`), where the NIC itself executes AES-GCM per TCP segment descriptor.
3. **Reduced context switches and TLB pressure** from avoiding userspace crypto libraries on the hot path.

Limitations are equally structural: kTLS supports only a subset of cipher suites (AES-GCM, ChaCha20-Poly1305 on newer kernels, *not* CCM), only TLS 1.2/1.3 record formats, and — critically — **not QUIC**, which lives in userspace over UDP by design. Receive-side kTLS (`TLS_RX`) decrypts in `strparser` but cannot easily zero-copy into application buffers without `splice`-style plumbing, so most deployments use kTLS for *transmit* offload only. Key installation also widens the kernel attack surface: traffic secrets reside in kernel memory, and defects in the TLS ULP would compromise all connections on the host.

### 4.3 QUIC packet number encryption and header protection

RFC 9001 [3] defines QUIC's two-stage protection precisely because QUIC encrypts *more* than TLS does. After payload AEAD encryption with packet-number-derived nonces, header protection computes:

```
mask = AES-ECB(key=hp_key, sample)[0..5]     # sample = 16 ciphertext bytes
protected_pn    = packet_number XOR mask[1..]
protected_flags = header_byte XOR (mask[0] & 0x0f)
```

Three consequences follow:

1. **An extra AES invocation per packet.** AES-ECB on one block is cheap (tens of cycles with AES-NI) but it is *latency*-critical: the receiver cannot even parse the packet number — and thus cannot derive the payload nonce — until header protection is removed. This serializes the pipeline: sample-to-ECB-to-unmask-to-nonce-to-AEAD-decrypt. Hardware offload engines must implement this exact ordering.
2. **ChaCha20-based header protection** (QUIC v1 permits ChaCha20 with a 32-byte key and 16-byte sample as counter/nonce input) avoids AES entirely on non-AES-NI platforms, at the cost of a full ChaCha20 block function per packet.
3. **Ossification resistance as a security property.** By encrypting packet numbers, QUIC denies middleboxes the version/sequence visibility that ossified TCP; any offload device must therefore be a *willing endpoint participant* (holding `hp_key`), not a transparent middlebox — a deliberate architectural constraint.

For 400 Gbps QUIC termination, the per-packet ECB/AES block plus AEAD on small (~1200 byte, to avoid IP fragmentation) datagrams pushes the operation rate to ~41.7M packets/s. Scaling to 400 Gbps therefore requires RSS-style flow sharding across dozens of cores *or* NIC inline crypto offload for QUIC, where vendor support is emerging but not yet uniform [11].

### 4.4 Hardware crypto accelerators: Intel QAT and AWS Nitro

When host cores are saturated, cryptography migrates to dedicated silicon:

**Intel QuickAssist Technology (QAT).** QAT (on-chip since Ice Lake-SP, discrete 4xxx series cards) exposes symmetric crypto (AES-GCM up to ~100+ Gbps per device), public-key acceleration (RSA, ECDSA, ECDH), and compression through a request/queue-pair DMA interface [12]. The programming model is asynchronous: the driver submits crypto *jobs* with source/destination buffers, and completions arrive via polling or interrupts. Implications:

- *Throughput vs. latency trade-off.* QAT excels at bulk AES-GCM (large buffers, deep queues) but per-job submission overhead (doorbell, DMA descriptor, ~1–2 us) makes it uneconomical for small records — the crossover sits around 4–16 KiB per op depending on the platform.
- *Handshake offload.* QAT's PKE engine accelerates RSA-2048 signing (~50k+ ops/s) and ECDH, directly lifting TLS handshake rates for certificate-heavy servers.
- *TLS record offload in software stacks.* OpenSSL's QAT engine/provider routes `EVP_aes_128_gcm` to hardware; frameworks like SPDK and VPP integrate QAT for inline IPsec/TLS.

**AWS Nitro.** Nitro cards offload the hypervisor data path, and the Nitro crypto story centers on *s2n-tls* with AWS-LC acceleration plus Nitro's hardware AES engines for EBS and network encryption. For TLS specifically, Graviton3/4 cores include AES/SHA/PMULL instructions delivering competitive per-core AEAD rates [9], while Nitro offloads the *underlay* (VPC encapsulation encryption) so guest vCPUs spend cycles only on application TLS. The emerging pattern is *layered offload*: underlay crypto in Nitro silicon, overlay TLS on accelerated host cores — with SmartNIC-class devices (AWS Nitro v5, NVIDIA BlueField) beginning to offer inline TLS/QUIC record offload at 400G.

| Accelerator | Bulk AES-GCM | Handshake PKE | Small-record efficiency | QUIC hp |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| AVX-512 (host core) | ~100 Gbps/core | — | Excellent | Full (software) |
| Intel QAT 4xxx | ~100–400 Gbps/device | RSA/ECDH/ECDSA | Poor below ~4 KiB | Not native |
| kTLS + NIC TLS offload | Line rate (400G) | — | Good (TSO-sized) | No (TCP only) |
| Nitro/SmartNIC inline | Up to 400G | Limited | Good | Emerging |

### 4.5 Hybrid post-quantum key exchange and 0-RTT replay

NIST's 2024 standardization of ML-KEM (FIPS 203) [6] triggered production hybrid deployments combining classical and post-quantum KEMs, e.g. `X25519MLKEM768` in Chrome and Cloudflare's stack [13]. The hybrid construction concatenates shared secrets: `ss = X25519_ss || ML-KEM_ss`, then feeds the TLS 1.3 key schedule unchanged. Security holds if *either* component is unbroken — the standard hybrid rationale [14].

**Overhead anatomy** (ML-KEM-768: public key 1184 B, ciphertext 1088 B):

1. *Message expansion.* ClientHello `key_share` grows from 32 B (X25519) to ~1216 B; ServerHello's share similarly. Total handshake flight inflation is ~2.3 KiB — enough to push ClientHello past typical MTUs, forcing TCP segmentation or QUIC multi-datagram coalescing, and risking middlebox fragmentation bugs (the same class that plagued early TLS 1.3 deployment).
2. *CPU cost.* ML-KEM-768 keygen/encaps/decaps run ~50–200k cycles in optimized AVX2 implementations — comparable to or cheaper than an ECDSA-P256 sign, so handshake *rate* impact is modest (~5–15% in Cloudflare's measurements [13]). The dominant cost is *latency*: larger flights interact with TCP slow-start and QUIC amplification limits (server may not send more than 3x received bytes before address validation), adding round trips on lossy paths.
3. *QAT/Nitro implications.* PKE accelerators lack ML-KEM support in current silicon generations; hybrid handshakes therefore fall back to host AVX2/AVX-512 software, partially negating handshake-offload investments until PQC-capable hardware ships.

**0-RTT replay risk.** TLS 1.3 0-RTT (early data) is replayable by network attackers *by design* — the server cannot distinguish a replayed 0-RTT flight from a fresh one without additional state [1]. Post-quantum hybrids do not fix this; they change its *economics*: larger 0-RTT flights are cheaper to capture and replay verbatim, and QUIC's address-validation tokens only prove return-routability, not freshness. Mitigations remain:

- Single-use ticket/STEK rotation with short lifetimes;
- Server-side replay caches (Bloom filters over 0-RTT anti-replay windows);
- Restricting 0-RTT to idempotent, safe operations (the RFC 8446 Section 8 guidance), enforced at the application layer.

## 5. Empirical Results and Formal Analysis

### 5.1 Throughput model

Per-core AEAD throughput as a function of record length L (bytes) follows an Amdahl-like form:

```
T(L) = L / (C_fixed + L * cpb_stream)     bytes/second
```

where C_fixed ≈ 200–500 cycles covers nonce setup, tag handling, and function-call overhead, and cpb_stream is the asymptotic streaming cost:

| Implementation | cpb_stream (AES-128-GCM) | 1500 B record, per-core |
|---|---|---|
| Scalar (no AES-NI) | ~20–30 | ~0.3 Gbps |
| AES-NI + PCLMULQDQ interleaved [7] | ~0.6–1.0 | ~15–25 Gbps |
| AVX2 4x interleave | ~0.5–0.8 | ~20–30 Gbps |
| AVX-512 VAES+VPCLMULQDQ [8] | ~0.25–0.4 | ~60–100 Gbps |
| QAT bulk offload [12] | — | ~100–400 Gbps/device |

Cores required for 400 Gbps (1500 B records, both directions ≈ 800 Gbps crypto): AVX-512 host software needs ~8–14 cores; AES-NI-only needs ~32–54 cores — before framing, copies, and stack overhead, which field experience suggests inflates by 1.5–2x. Hence the industry migration to kTLS/NIC inline offload for the transmit path.

### 5.2 Bottleneck crossover analysis

Total per-byte cost decomposes as C_total = C_crypto + C_framing + C_copy. With AVX-512, C_crypto ≈ 0.3 cpb; a single 64-byte cache-line copy costs ~0.04 cpb amortized, but TLS's *two* copies (user-to-kernel, kernel-to-NIC descriptor) plus record-header writes and SKB metadata manipulation contribute ~0.5–1.0 cpb of non-crypto overhead. **Above ~200 Gbps, the data path is memory-bandwidth-bound, not crypto-bound** — which is exactly why kTLS zero-copy and NIC TLS offload (eliminating C_copy and kernel framing) outperform even AVX-512 software at 400G.

### 5.3 Handshake latency model

Hybrid handshake added latency decomposes as:

```
Δt_hybrid = Δt_flight_bytes / goodput  +  Δt_kem_cpu
```

- Δt_flight: ~2.3 KiB extra at 10 Mbps effective goodput ≈ 1.8 ms — negligible on broadband, significant on constrained links.
- Δt_kem: ML-KEM-768 decaps ~0.05 ms on AVX2 — negligible.

Measured handshake-rate impact of X25519MLKEM768 vs X25519 alone: **under 10% degradation** in server handshakes/s in Cloudflare's fleet data [13], dominated by the extra keygen/encaps rather than bandwidth.

## 6. Limitations

1. **QUIC offload immaturity.** Unlike TCP+TLS (kTLS, NIC TLS offload), QUIC's userspace packet protection and header-protection ordering lack standardized kernel or NIC offload APIs; 400G QUIC termination today means sharding across many cores.
2. **QAT small-record penalty.** Sub-4 KiB operations often run *slower* on QAT than on AVX-512 host cores due to submission latency; naive offload-everything policies regress tail latency.
3. **kTLS cipher and protocol coverage.** No QUIC, no CCM, receive-side zero-copy limitations, and kernel-resident traffic secrets expand the trusted computing base.
4. **PQC hardware gap.** No shipping QAT/Nitro generation accelerates ML-KEM; hybrid handshakes are host-CPU-bound until PQC silicon arrives (expected 2026–2028 vendor roadmaps).
5. **0-RTT replay is fundamental.** No KEM choice — classical or post-quantum — removes early-data replayability; mitigations are operational (single-use tickets, replay caches, idempotency discipline), not cryptographic.
6. **Model calibration.** Absolute Gbps figures vary ±30% across microarchitectures, compiler flags, and record-size mixes; our crossover conclusions are robust to this variance but precise capacity planning requires on-target benchmarking.
---

## 7. Conclusion

Sustaining 400 Gbps of TLS 1.3 and QUIC record protection is achievable, but no single technique suffices across the workload space. Interleaved AES-NI+PCLMULQDQ pipelines and AVX-512 VAES/VPCLMULQDQ constructions push host-core AES-GCM past 60–100 Gbps per core, enough for 400G with modest core counts on large records — yet the bottleneck migrates from crypto to memory bandwidth above ~200 Gbps, where kTLS zero-copy transmit and NIC inline TLS offload become necessary. QUIC's packet number encryption and header protection add a latency-critical serial AES block per packet and, lacking mature offload APIs, remain a many-core software problem at 400G. Hardware accelerators (QAT, Nitro/SmartNIC) deliver line-rate bulk crypto but penalize small records and currently offer no post-quantum handshake acceleration. Hybrid X25519+ML-KEM-768 key exchange adds ~2.3 KiB of flight expansion and under 10% handshake-rate degradation — an acceptable price for harvest-now-decrypt-later resistance — while 0-RTT replay risk persists orthogonally and demands operational mitigation. The coherent 400G architecture that emerges is *layered*: AVX-512 software or QAT for bulk, kTLS/NIC offload for transmit, flow-sharded userspace QUIC, and hybrid handshakes in software until PQC silicon ships.
