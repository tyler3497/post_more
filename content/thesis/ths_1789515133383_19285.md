# Secure Multi-Party Computation: Garbled Circuits, the GMW Protocol, and the SPDZ Framework

## Abstract

Secure multi-party computation (MPC) allows a set of mutually distrustful parties to evaluate an agreed function over their private inputs while revealing nothing beyond the prescribed output. This thesis presents a unified technical treatment of the field's foundational protocol families and their modern synthesis. We begin with the *ideal/real simulation paradigm* and Canetti's universal composability framework, the formal yardsticks against which every MPC protocol is measured. We then reconstruct Yao's garbled circuits — including point-and-permute and the free-XOR optimization — and show how oblivious transfer delivers evaluator input privacy, with the Naor–Pinkas protocol and the IKNP OT extension converting a handful of public-key oblivious transfers into millions of symmetric-key ones. We develop the GMW protocol over secret sharing, introduce Beaver triples and the *preprocessing model* that decouples input-independent correlation from input-dependent computation, and culminate in the SPDZ framework: active security against a dishonest majority via information-theoretic message authentication codes and an offline/online architecture. We close with threshold fully homomorphic encryption approaches, proof sketches, a comparative performance analysis, and open limitations.

## 1 Introduction

In 1982 Andrew Yao posed the *millionaires' problem*: two millionaires wish to determine who is richer without disclosing their actual wealth to each other [7]. This deceptively simple question inaugurated the field of **secure multi-party computation (MPC)**, whose ambition is far grander: *any* efficiently computable function of *n* private inputs can be evaluated by *n* mutually distrustful parties so that no coalition of corrupt parties learns anything beyond what its own inputs and the prescribed output imply.

The feasibility of MPC was settled in the late 1980s by a sequence of landmark results. Yao presented the first constant-round protocol for two-party computation via *garbled circuits* [6], while Goldreich, Micali, and Wigderson (GMW) showed that oblivious transfer suffices for secure computation among any number of parties [7]. Ben-Or, Goldwasser, and Wigderson (BGW) complemented this with an information-theoretic protocol based on Shamir secret sharing secure against a minority of corrupt parties [7]. These constructions established the field's central thesis: *privacy need not be sacrificed for collaboration*.

The modern motivation is intensely practical: private set intersection powers contact discovery in messaging applications; threshold signing secures cryptocurrency custody; and collaborative analytics over medical or financial data demands computation without data pooling [7]. Each application stresses different axes — round complexity, communication, preprocessing cost, and adversarial strength — and each axis is best served by a different protocol family.

This thesis develops three pillars of MPC in depth:

1. **Yao's garbled circuits**, the constant-round workhorse of two-party computation, together with its two most consequential optimizations: *point-and-permute* and *free-XOR* [2].
2. **Oblivious transfer**, the protocol primitive that Kilian proved *complete* for MPC — sufficient, on its own, to build secure computation for any function — including the Naor–Pinkas construction and the IKNP *OT extension* that made million-gate garbled circuits practical [3].
3. **The GMW protocol**, the secret-sharing paradigm for the multi-party setting, its refinement through Beaver's *multiplication triples* [4], and its modern incarnation in the **SPDZ framework**, which achieves *active* security against a *dishonest majority* using information-theoretic MACs in a preprocessing model [1].

We conclude with **threshold fully homomorphic encryption (FHE)**-based MPC, which collapses interaction to a near-minimum at the cost of heavier cryptography [8].

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The Ideal/Real Simulation Paradigm

MPC security is not defined by a list of forbidden attacks but by comparison with an *ideal world*. In the ideal world, all parties send their inputs to an incorruptible trusted party implementing the *ideal functionality* $\mathcal{F}$, which computes the function and returns each party its prescribed output. In the real world, parties run a protocol $\pi$ over a network controlled by an adversary. A protocol **securely realizes** $\mathcal{F}$ if for every real-world adversary $\mathcal{A}$ there exists an *ideal-world simulator* $\mathcal{S}$ such that no efficient distinguisher can tell the two worlds apart:

> **Definition (Simulation security, informal):** $\pi$ securely realizes $\mathcal{F}$ if for every probabilistic polynomial-time adversary $\mathcal{A}$ attacking $\pi$ there exists a probabilistic polynomial-time simulator $\mathcal{S}$ attacking the ideal process such that $\mathrm{REAL}_{\pi,\mathcal{A}} \approx_c \mathrm{IDEAL}_{\mathcal{F},\mathcal{S}}$.

The simulator is given only what the corrupt parties legitimately learn — their inputs and outputs — and must fabricate a view indistinguishable from the real protocol transcript. This single definition elegantly captures *privacy* (the transcript leaks nothing extra), *correctness* (honest parties' outputs match $\mathcal{F}$), and *input independence*.

**Universal composability (UC)** strengthens the paradigm: Canetti's framework introduces an *environment* $\mathcal{Z}$ that interacts with the adversary throughout the execution, and a protocol is UC-secure if simulation succeeds against this stronger distinguisher [7]. The payoff is the **UC composition theorem**: a UC-secure protocol remains secure when run concurrently with arbitrary other protocols — a property the basic stand-alone definition does not guarantee.

### 2.2 Adversary Models

The literature is organized by two orthogonal axes — *behavior* and *corruption threshold*:

| Adversary | Behavior | Typical threshold |
|---|---|---|
| Semi-honest (honest-but-curious) | Follows the protocol; analyzes its view | $t < n$ (any minority/majority) |
| Malicious (active) | Deviates arbitrarily | $t < n/2$ (BGW/GMW) or $t < n$ (SPDZ) |
| Covert | Cheats only if unlikely to be caught | $t < n$ |

Security against a *dishonest majority* ($t = n-1$ corruptions) is the strongest standard notion and the regime of SPDZ [1]. A fundamental limitation: with a dishonest majority, *fairness* (either everyone learns the output or no one does) and *guaranteed output delivery* are impossible in general — the adversary can always abort after learning the output. Protocols therefore typically guarantee *security with abort* [7].

### 2.3 Secret Sharing

A $(t,n)$-threshold secret sharing scheme splits a secret $s$ into $n$ shares such that any $t$ shares reconstruct $s$ while fewer than $t$ reveal nothing. Two schemes dominate MPC:

- **Additive sharing** over a field $\mathbb{F}$: party $P_i$ holds $x_i$ with $\sum_i x_i = x$. Reconstruction requires all shares; it is $n$-out-of-$n$.
- **Shamir sharing**: shares are points $(i, p(i))$ on a random degree-$(t-1)$ polynomial $p$ with $p(0) = s$; reconstruction is Lagrange interpolation at zero.

Both schemes are *linear*, so additions and scalar multiplications are local; multiplication of shared values is the hard operation (Section 4.3).

### 2.4 Oblivious Transfer

In 1-out-of-2 oblivious transfer ($\binom{2}{1}$-OT), a sender holds messages $m_0, m_1$ and a receiver holds a choice bit $b$; the receiver learns $m_b$ and nothing about $m_{1-b}$, while the sender learns nothing about $b$. OT is *complete* for MPC: Kilian showed that OT alone suffices to construct secure computation for any function [7]. This completeness is why OT extension (Section 4.2) is arguably the single most impactful efficiency result in applied MPC.

## 3 Methodology

All protocol families in this thesis share a common skeleton:

1. **Circuit representation.** The target function $f$ is expressed as a circuit — *boolean* (AND/XOR gates) for Yao/GMW-style protocols, or *arithmetic* (addition/multiplication gates over a field) for SPDZ-style protocols [1][7].
2. **Input sharing.** Each party's input is distributed in a privacy-preserving encoding: garbled labels (Yao), XOR-shares (GMW), or additive/MAC-authenticated shares (SPDZ).
3. **Gate-by-gate evaluation.** Parties jointly evaluate the circuit, gate by gate, maintaining the invariant that intermediate wire values stay hidden while the computation progresses correctly.
4. **Output reconstruction.** Final shares or labels are opened or decoded to reveal $f(x_1, \dots, x_n)$.

A second, cross-cutting methodological idea is the **preprocessing (offline/online) model**. Input-*independent* correlated randomness — Beaver triples, authenticated shares, garbled circuits themselves — is generated in an *offline phase* before inputs are known; the *online phase*, executed once inputs arrive, uses only cheap symmetric operations [1][4].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Yao's Garbled Circuits: Point-and-Permute and Free-XOR

A **garbling scheme** is a tuple of algorithms $(\mathrm{Gb}, \mathrm{En}, \mathrm{Ev}, \mathrm{De})$ formalized by Bellare, Hoang, and Rogaway [6]:

- $\mathrm{Gb}(1^\lambda, f) \to (F, e, d)$: garbles circuit $f$ into garbled circuit $F$, encoding information $e$, decoding information $d$.
- $\mathrm{En}(e, x) \to X$: encodes input $x$ into garbled input $X$.
- $\mathrm{Ev}(F, X) \to Y$: evaluates to garbled output $Y$.
- $\mathrm{De}(d, Y) \to y$: decodes to plaintext output $y$.

The core gadget is the **garbled gate**. For a binary gate $g$ with input wires $a, b$ and output wire $c$, the garbler samples two random $\lambda$-bit *labels* per wire, $W^0_w, W^1_w$, and publishes four doubly-encrypted ciphertexts — one per combination of input bits:

$$c_{i,j} = \mathrm{Enc}_{W^i_a}\big(\mathrm{Enc}_{W^j_b}(W^{g(i,j)}_c)\big), \qquad i,j \in \{0,1\}.$$

The evaluator, holding exactly one label per input wire, can decrypt exactly one row and learns the corresponding output label — but never the underlying bit. Yao's two-party protocol then runs as follows: the garbler sends $F$ and her own garbled input; the evaluator obtains his garbled input labels via oblivious transfer (so the garbler does not learn his input bits); the evaluator computes $\mathrm{Ev}(F, X)$ gate by gate in topological order; and the parties decode the output [6].

Two optimizations transformed garbled circuits from a theoretical curiosity into a practical tool:

**Point-and-permute.** Each label carries a random *select bit* (permutation bit) $p_w$, with $p^b_w$ indicating which table row to decrypt. The evaluator uses the select bits — not trial decryption — to locate the single decryptable row, eliminating decryption failures and the need for verifiable decryption [6]. The garbled table is then permuted according to these bits, so row position leaks nothing about the underlying truth values.

**Free-XOR.** Kolesnikov and Schneider observed that if all wire labels satisfy a global invariant $W^1_w = W^0_w \oplus \Delta$ for a secret global offset $\Delta$, then XOR gates require *no garbled table at all*: the evaluator simply XORs the input labels, since $(W^a_u) \oplus (W^b_v) = W^{a \oplus b}_w$ follows algebraically from the invariant [2]. XOR gates become free — zero communication, zero cryptographic operations — typically cutting garbled-circuit size by a factor of 2 to 4 on real circuits.

The free-XOR trick is subtle to prove secure. The global $\Delta$ correlates all wire labels, so the hash function used to encrypt gate rows must remain secure under *correlated* inputs. Choi et al. showed that correlation robustness alone does *not* suffice: some form of **circular security** is required, formalized as *circular 2-correlation robustness* of the hash function [5]. This is a rare case where a wildly successful practical optimization demanded a genuinely new assumption to be placed on rigorous footing.

> **Theorem (Free-XOR security, informal [5]):** The free-XOR garbling technique is secure when instantiated with a circular 2-correlation robust hash function. Correlation robustness alone is insufficient.

Further refinements such as *garbled row reduction* and *half-gates* have reduced the per-AND-gate cost to two ciphertexts [6].

The following Python sketch illustrates the garbled-AND construction with point-and-permute select bits, using a hash-based dual-key cipher:

```python
import hashlib, os

def enc(key_a, key_b, tweak, msg):
    h = hashlib.sha256(key_a + key_b + tweak).digest()
    return bytes(x ^ y for x, y in zip(h[:len(msg)], msg))

def garble_and(labels):
    # labels[w] = (label0, label1, select_bit); select bits random
    (a0, a1, pa), (b0, b1, pb), (c0, c1, pc) = labels
    table = {}
    for i, (ai, pi) in enumerate([(a0, pa), (a1, pa ^ 1)]):
        for j, (bj, pj) in enumerate([(b0, pb), (b1, pb ^ 1)]):
            out = (c1, pc ^ 1) if (i and j) else (c0, pc)
            row = (pi << 1) | pj          # permuted position
            table[row] = enc(ai, bj, b"gate", out[0] + bytes([out[1]]))
    return table  # evaluator decrypts the single row indexed by select bits
```

### 4.2 Oblivious Transfer: Naor–Pinkas and IKNP OT Extension

Yao's protocol needs one $\binom{2}{1}$-OT per evaluator input bit — and GMW-style protocols need one OT per AND gate.

The breakthrough for scale was **OT extension** by Ishai, Kilian, Nissim, and Petrank (IKNP) [3].

1. The parties perform $k$ base OTs with reversed roles: the OT sender becomes the extension receiver and vice versa.
2. The extension receiver samples two $m$-bit strings and transfers them through the base OTs according to a secret choice vector $s \in \{0,1\}^k$.
3. Both parties expand the resulting correlated randomness with a pseudorandom generator and combine it with the receiver's $m$ choice bits; each extended OT then costs roughly two hash evaluations and a few bits of communication [3].

The quantitative impact is dramatic: millions of OTs at an amortized cost of a handful of symmetric-key operations each.

### 4.3 The GMW Protocol: Secret Sharing and Beaver Triples

The GMW protocol evaluates a boolean circuit among $n$ parties using **XOR secret sharing**: each wire value $v$ is shared as $v = v_1 \oplus \cdots \oplus v_n$, with party $P_i$ holding $v_i$.

GMW's original AND-gate protocol uses OT: for each AND gate with input shares held across parties, the parties run OTs so that each party obtains an additive share of the product without learning the other party's share [7].

**Beaver triples** [4] provide an alternative, preprocessing-friendly route to the same end. A *multiplication triple* is a triple of secret-shared random values $([a], [b], [c])$ with $c = a \cdot b$. Given such a triple, parties multiply shared values $[x]$ and $[y]$ as follows:

1. Locally compute shares of $d = x - a$ and $e = y - b$, then *open* (reconstruct) $d$ and $e$ publicly. Since $a, b$ are uniformly random and independent of $x, y$, the opened masks leak nothing.
2. Each party locally computes its share of the product via the identity
$$x \cdot y = (a + d)(b + e) = c + d \cdot b + e \cdot a + d \cdot e,$$
using its shares $[a], [b], [c]$ and the public $d, e$.

One triple is consumed per multiplication, and the online phase needs only two openings — no public-key operations at all.

### 4.4 SPDZ: Information-Theoretic MACs and the Preprocessing Model

SPDZ (Damgård–Pastro–Smart–Zakarias) [1] lifts the preprocessing model to **active security against a dishonest majority** — up to $n-1$ corrupt parties — for arithmetic circuits.

The online phase mirrors the Beaver-triple protocol, but every opened value is *checked* before use:

- **Linear operations** (addition, multiplication by public constants) are performed locally on both the value shares and the MAC shares, exploiting the homomorphism $\gamma(x+y) = \gamma(x) + \gamma(y)$.
- **Multiplication** consumes an *authenticated* Beaver triple $(\langle a \rangle, \langle b \rangle, \langle c \rangle)$: parties open $d = x - a$ and $e = y - b$, then form $\langle z \rangle$ via Beaver's identity applied to both shares and MACs.
- **MAC check.** Before outputs are revealed, the parties batch-verify: they commit to random linear combinations of all opened values and their MACs, open the combination, and confirm the MAC relation holds [1].

> **Theorem (SPDZ unforgeability, informal [1]):** In the SPDZ online phase, any deviation by the adversary that modifies a share is detected by the MAC check except with probability at most $2^{-s}$, where $s$ is the statistical security parameter. The MAC key $\alpha$ itself is information-theoretically hidden from any coalition of $n-1$ parties.

The **offline phase** of SPDZ is where the heavy cryptography lives: authenticated triples are generated using *somewhat homomorphic encryption* (SHE), with zero-knowledge proofs and a *sacrificing* step that verifies triples in pairs [1].

### 4.5 Threshold FHE-Based MPC

A different route to low interaction uses **threshold fully homomorphic encryption**.

| Approach | Interaction | Assumption | Adversary |
|---|---|---|---|
| Garbled circuits (Yao) | Constant rounds (2PC) | One-way functions / circular correlation robustness | Semi-honest (malicious w/ cut-and-choose) |
| GMW + OT extension | $O(\text{depth})$ rounds | OT + symmetric crypto | Semi-honest |
| SPDZ | $O(\text{depth})$ rounds online | SHE/OT preprocessing | Malicious, dishonest majority |
| Threshold FHE | Near-non-interactive | LWE | Semi-honest / malicious variants |

Threshold FHE minimizes communication and interaction but concentrates computational cost in homomorphic evaluation and distributed key generation — the mirror image of SPDZ's cheap online phase [8].

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

### Proof sketch: selective security of Yao's garbled circuits

The standard proof [6] constructs a simulator $\mathcal{S}$ that, given only the circuit $f$ and the output $y = f(x)$, produces a fake garbled circuit indistinguishable from the real one. $\mathcal{S}$ samples random labels for every wire exactly as the honest garbler would, but garbles each gate so that *all four rows encrypt the output label corresponding to the true output bit*: for a gate whose output on the evaluated input is $v$, every row encrypts $W^v_c$. It then programs the decoding table $d$ so the evaluator's labels decode to the correct output bits $y$. Because the evaluator can decrypt exactly one row per gate (by the CPA security of the underlying encryption, or correlation robustness with point-and-permute), its view consists of random labels plus one decryptable row per gate — identically distributed to the real view up to the semantic security of the encryption. The OT-hybrid model handles the evaluator's input labels.

### Proof sketch: GMW composition and Beaver correctness

GMW's security follows from a hybrid argument over circuit layers: each AND gate's OT subprotocol is replaced, one gate at a time, by its ideal functionality, with OT security guaranteeing indistinguishability at each step [7]. Beaver's trick is *perfectly* correct by the algebraic identity of Section 4.3, and perfectly private because the opened masks $d, e$ are one-time-padded by uniform randomness [4].

### Quantitative comparison

Concrete costs (order-of-magnitude, 128-bit security):

| Protocol | Offline cost per mult/AND | Online communication per mult/AND | Rounds |
|---|---|---|---|
| Yao + free-XOR + half-gates | 2 ciphertexts per AND | 2 ciphertexts per AND | Constant (2PC) |
| GMW + IKNP extension | $\approx$ 2 hashes per AND | $O(n)$ bits per AND | $O(\text{depth})$ |
| SPDZ | SHE triple generation (heavy) | 2 field openings + MAC check amortized | $O(\text{depth})$ |
| Threshold FHE | Distributed keygen (heavy) | $O(n)$ ciphertexts total | Near-constant |

The pattern is unmistakable: preprocessing converts public-key work into symmetric-key work [1][3].

## 6 Limitations

**Fairness and guaranteed output delivery.** With a dishonest majority, a corrupt party can learn the output and abort before honest parties do; Cleve's impossibility result shows this is inherent for general functions without additional setup [7]. SPDZ and its relatives therefore guarantee only *security with abort* [1].

**Preprocessing dominates real cost.** The SPDZ offline phase — SHE-based triple generation with sacrificing — is orders of magnitude more expensive than the online phase it serves [1]. OT-based successors (MASCOT) and silent-preprocessing techniques narrow the gap, but generating maliciously secure correlated randomness remains the central bottleneck of dishonest-majority MPC.

**Circuit blowup.** Garbled circuits expand every AND gate into multiple $\lambda$-bit ciphertexts; large circuits demand gigabytes of transfer and memory. Free-XOR and half-gates mitigate but do not eliminate the expansion [2][5].

**Assumption fragility.** Free-XOR's reliance on circular correlation robustness is a non-standard assumption whose exact strength was debated for years [5]. Each efficiency gain in MPC has historically been purchased with a stronger or less standard assumption.

## 7 Conclusion

Secure multi-party computation has traveled from Yao's millionaires to production systems in four decades, and the journey is visible in the protocol families themselves. **Garbled circuits** taught us that *encryption can evaluate*: a circuit can be scrambled so thoroughly that evaluation reveals nothing but the output, with point-and-permute and free-XOR [2] paring the cost to its essentials — at the price of subtle assumptions about circular security [5]. **Oblivious transfer** proved to be the field's universal joint: complete for MPC in theory [7], and, through the IKNP extension [3], cheap enough in practice to underwrite million-gate computations. The **GMW protocol** showed that secret sharing plus interaction suffices for any number of parties, and **Beaver triples** [4] revealed the deeper structure — that multiplication can be preprocessed, splitting every protocol into an input-independent offline phase and a lightweight online phase. **SPDZ** [1] brought this architecture to its strongest natural conclusion: active security against a dishonest majority, authenticated by information-theoretic MACs under a key no party ever holds. **Threshold FHE** [8] offers the complementary extreme of minimal interaction.
