# Fine-Grained Complexity: The Strong Exponential Time Hypothesis, the Orthogonal Vectors Conjecture, and Conditional Lower Bounds

## Abstract

Classical complexity theory classifies problems as tractable or intractable, but says little about *how fast* a polynomial-time algorithm must run. Fine-grained complexity repairs this gap by proving conditional lower bounds *inside* P and inside exponential time: assuming conjectures such as the Strong Exponential Time Hypothesis (SETH), no algorithm for edit distance, longest common subsequence, or dynamic time warping runs in strongly subquadratic time, and no algorithm for all-pairs shortest paths runs in truly subcubic time. This thesis develops the fine-grained complexity program from its foundations in the Exponential Time Hypothesis of Impagliazzo and Paturi [1], through the SETH-to-Orthogonal-Vectors reduction of Williams (as exposited in [7]), the string-algorithm lower bounds of Backurs and Indyk [2], Abboud, Vassilevska Williams, and Weimann [3], and Bringmann and Künnemann [4], to the survey architecture of Vassilevska Williams [5] and the nondeterministic barrier results of Carmosino et al. [6]. We give the reduction machinery in full detail, map the three-pillar SETH/3SUM/APSP landscape, and close with a critical appraisal of the program's barriers and limitations.

## 1 Introduction

The great dichotomy of modern complexity theory — polynomial time versus NP-completeness — is, for the working algorithm designer, frustratingly coarse. Knowing that a problem is in P says nothing about whether the textbook algorithm is optimal. Edit distance has a classic $O(n^2)$ dynamic-programming algorithm [2]; the all-pairs shortest paths (APSP) problem has the $O(n^3)$ Floyd–Warshall algorithm; yet for decades no one could explain *why* faster algorithms remained elusive. NP-hardness gives no leverage at all: the problems are not NP-hard.

Fine-grained complexity supplies this missing explanatory layer [5], via a precision-engineered refinement of the classical NP-completeness method:

1. **Select core conjectures** on exact running times: SETH, 3SUM, and APSP.
2. **Define fine-grained reductions** — with overhead so small that a speedup for the target transfers to a speedup for the source, contradicting the conjecture [5].
3. **Propagate lower bounds** across dozens of problems, building equivalence classes in which either all or none admit faster algorithms.

> **Theorem:** (Fine-grained reduction schema.) If problem $A$ reduces to problem $B$ via a *fine-grained* reduction and $B$ is solvable in time $T_B(n)$, then $A$ is solvable in time $T_A(n) \approx T_B(O(n))$. Hence a lower bound on $A$ yields a lower bound on $B$.

Section 2 formalizes ETH and SETH [1]; Section 3 describes the reduction methodology; Section 4 gives the deep dive (OV hub, sequence-alignment bounds, APSP and 3SUM pillars, the hardness-in-P landscape); Section 5 proves the central theorems; Section 6 examines limitations; Section 7 concludes.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 From P vs NP to exact running times

For each $k \geq 3$, define $s_k = \inf\{\delta : k\text{-SAT} \text{ is solvable in } O(2^{\delta n})\}$. Impagliazzo and Paturi [1] then define:

> **Definition (ETH):** The *Exponential Time Hypothesis* asserts that $s_3 > 0$: 3-SAT admits no $2^{o(n)}$-time algorithm.
>
> **Definition (SETH):** The *Strong Exponential Time Hypothesis* asserts that $\lim_{k\to\infty} s_k = 1$: for every $\varepsilon > 0$ there is some $k$ such that $k$-SAT requires $2^{(1-\varepsilon)n}$ time.

SETH is strictly stronger than ETH and far stronger than P ≠ NP. It asserts that *exhaustive search is essentially optimal* for satisfiability, modulo polynomial factors. Two structural facts underpin its use:

- **The sparsification lemma** [1]: for every $\varepsilon > 0$ and $k$, a $k$-CNF formula on $n$ variables can be rewritten, in $O(2^{\varepsilon n})$ time, as the OR of at most $2^{\varepsilon n}$ formulas each with $O(n)$ clauses. This is why ETH-based reductions can target instances with linearly many clauses, and why the sequence $s_k$ is monotone and the limit $s_\infty$ well defined.
- **Infinitely-often separation**: if ETH holds, $s_k$ increases infinitely often, with $s_k \leq s_\infty(1-\alpha/k)$ for some constant $\alpha$ [1]; SETH kills the remaining gap by asserting the limit is $1$.

### 2.2 The Orthogonal Vectors problem

The problem that mediates between SETH and the algorithmic world is *Orthogonal Vectors* (OV): given two sets $A, B \subseteq \{0,1\}^d$ each of size $n$, determine whether there exist $a \in A$, $b \in B$ with $\langle a, b \rangle = 0$ (inner product over the integers). The naive algorithm checks all pairs in $O(n^2 d)$ time. Ryan Williams proved that OV is the SETH-hard *quadratic* problem *par excellence*: a $O(n^{2-\varepsilon})$-time OV algorithm would yield a $O(2^{(1-\delta)n})$-time CNF-SAT algorithm, refuting SETH [7]. The reduction splits the $n$ variables of a SAT instance into two halves and enumerates assignments, encoding clause satisfaction as vector coordinates so that an orthogonal pair exists exactly when the two half-assignments combine to a satisfying assignment (Section 5 gives the construction in full).

The *Orthogonal Vectors Conjecture* (OVC) sharpens this: it asserts that for every $\varepsilon > 0$ there exists $c$ such that OV in dimension $d = c \log n$ has no $O(n^{2-\varepsilon})$-time algorithm. Note that OVC is not independent of SETH — it is *implied* by SETH. Its value is as a *convenient intermediate hypothesis*: reductions from OV are far easier than from SAT, so dozens of lower bounds are stated "under OVC" while ultimately resting on SETH [5][7].

### 2.3 The 3SUM and APSP conjectures

Two further pillars organize the landscape [5]:

- **3SUM conjecture:** detecting a zero-sum triple among $n$ numbers requires $n^{2-o(1)}$ time.
- **APSP conjecture:** all-pairs shortest paths on $n$ vertices requires $n^{3-o(1)}$ time.

Each conjecture anchors an *equivalence class*: APSP is equivalent under subcubic reductions to min-plus product, graph diameter, and others — all truly subcubic or none are [5]. The program produces webs of equivalent hardness, not isolated bounds.

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 Fine-grained reductions

The classical polynomial-time reduction is far too blunt for exact running times: a quadratic blowup in the input size trivially destroys a quadratic lower bound. Fine-grained reductions therefore impose two constraints [5]:

1. The reduction runs in time substantially *faster* than the conjectured lower bound (typically $\tilde{O}(n)$ or $O(n \log^c n)$).
2. The instance-size blowup is at most quasi-linear: $n$ elements map to $O(n \log^c n)$ elements.

> **Definition:** A $(\alpha, \beta)$-*fine-grained reduction* from $A$ to $B$ maps instances of size $n$ to instances of size $O(n^\beta)$ in time $O(n^\alpha)$ with $\alpha < $ the conjectured exponent of $A$.

If $B$ then admits an algorithm beating $A$'s conjectured bound, composition violates the conjecture — the contrapositive is the conditional lower bound.

### 3.2 The split-and-list technique

The engine of the SETH→OV reduction is *split-and-list*, also called meet-in-the-middle [7]. Given a CNF formula $\phi$ on $n$ variables:

- Partition variables into $X_1, X_2$ of $n/2$ each.
- Enumerate all $2^{n/2}$ partial assignments to each half as vector lists $A, B$.
- For each clause $C_j$ and each partial assignment, emit coordinates recording whether the assignment "threatens" the clause from its side, in such a way that the pair $(a, b)$ is orthogonal exactly when $a \cup b$ satisfies every clause.

The dimension required is $O(m \log m)$, and $m = O(n)$ after sparsification, so $d = O(n \log n)$; with more careful gadgetry the dimension is reduced to $O(\log n)$ [7]. The crucial quantitative accounting is:

| Quantity | Value |
|---|---|
| SAT instance | $n$ variables, $m = O(n)$ clauses (post-sparsification) [1] |
| Vector count per side | $N = 2^{n/2}$ |
| Vector dimension | $d = O(n \log n)$ |
| OV instance size | $\Theta(N \cdot d)$ |

An $O(N^{2-\varepsilon})$ OV algorithm then refutes SETH. All string-algorithm bounds below compose further fine-grained reductions *from OV* — hence OV is the "hub" of the SETH lower-bound network [5].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 SETH and the Exponential Time Hypothesis family

ETH asserts only $s_3 > 0$ — no *subexponential* 3-SAT algorithm — yet already implies tight $2^{\Omega(n)}$ lower bounds for many NP-hard problems via sparsification [1]. SETH pins the exact growth rate, denying any constant-factor improvement in the exponent's base for large clause width. Cygan et al. showed SETH is *equivalent* to exhaustive-search optimality for a battery of standard NP-complete problems (independent set, dominating set, vertex cover, ...): it speaks about the whole NP-hardness ecosystem, not just SAT [5].

Whether the same statement holds for *general* CNF-SAT rather than $k$-CNF is open: the sparsification lemma applies to fixed $k$ but has no known analogue for general CNF (Santhanam and Srinivasan gave evidence such a sparsification may be impossible). SETH's power in fine-grained reductions derives precisely from the sparsification lemma [1]; the reductions below would all fail without it.

### 4.2 Orthogonal Vectors: the hub conjecture

The Orthogonal Vectors conjecture sits at the center of the SETH-based lower-bound network for reasons both technical and sociological. Technically, OV is *the* canonical quadratic problem whose hardness follows from SETH by a clean split-and-list reduction [7]; every quadratic lower bound below is either proved from OV directly or from a chain OV → X → Y where each link is a fine-grained reduction. Williams' construction (exposited in [7]) is worth appreciating in detail because its gadget pattern — enumerate half-assignments, encode clause constraints as coordinate-wise orthogonality, align threshold distances to a target value — is *exactly* the template reused by Backurs and Indyk [2], Abboud et al. [3], and Bringmann and Künnemann [4].

*Why dimension matters.* The refined construction achieves dimension $d = \Theta(\log n)$ via efficient clause-satisfaction gadgets [7]; it is this form of OVC that the string reductions of Section 4.3 target.

### 4.3 Hardness of sequence similarity: edit distance, LCS, and dynamic time warping

The most celebrated victories of fine-grained complexity concern the *sequence-similarity measures* used across computational biology and NLP:

- **Edit distance** (Levenshtein distance). The textbook $O(n^2)$ dynamic program stood for decades; the best known exact algorithm (Masek–Paterson) runs in $O(n^2/\log^2 n)$, still nearly quadratic. Backurs and Indyk proved that a *strongly subquadratic* $O(n^{2-\delta})$ exact algorithm would refute SETH [2]. Their reduction embeds OV instances into strings: the strings are built from vector gadgets arranged so that the edit distance attains a threshold *exactly* when an orthogonal pair exists. The construction must carefully handle deletions and substitutions so that no "cheating" alignment achieves the threshold without encoding orthogonality — the technical heart of the paper [2].
- **Longest common subsequence (LCS).** Abboud, Backurs, and Vassilevska Williams established matching SETH-hardness for LCS and a suite of other sequence-similarity measures (local alignment, edit distance variants, Fréchet distance), and Bringmann and Künnemann extended this to *dynamic time warping* (DTW) [2][4]. The result is a remarkable universality: virtually every classical sequence-comparison problem is SETH-hard to improve upon.
- **Multivariate refinement.** Bringmann and Künnemann [4] characterized LCS complexity across all standard parameters ($n, m, L, \delta, \Delta$, alphabet size, $M, d$): under SETH the optimal time is $(n + \min\{d, \delta\Delta, \delta m\})^{1 \pm o(1)}$, matching the three known algorithms — a tight landscape explaining why parameterized improvements stalled after 1990 [4].

Abboud, Vassilevska Williams, and Weimann [3] extended the program to *local* alignment, showing that strongly subquadratic local alignment would simultaneously yield breakthrough algorithms for 3SUM, CNF-SAT, and max-weight 4-clique — tying the SETH, 3SUM, and APSP pillars together through a single problem.

### 4.4 The APSP conjecture and subcubic equivalences

The APSP conjecture — all-pairs shortest paths needs $n^{3-o(1)}$ time — anchors the *cubic* side [5]. Its signature achievement is the *subcubic equivalence class*: APSP, min-plus matrix multiplication, replacement paths, and graph diameter are all equivalent, in that a truly subcubic ($n^{3-\varepsilon}$) algorithm for any one yields one for all. This explains why fifty years of work never broke $n^3/\mathrm{polylog}(n)$, and why the same barrier besets problems that look nothing like shortest paths.

The APSP pillar interacts with SETH as well: the *diameter* of an unweighted graph admits SETH-based quadratic lower bounds via OV reductions, so diameter sits at the intersection of the quadratic and cubic networks [5].

### 4.5 The 3SUM conjecture and "hardness in P"

The 3SUM conjecture — that finding a zero-sum triple needs $n^{2-o(1)}$ time (Pătrașcu, SODA 2010) — is a second quadratic pillar alongside SETH/OV [5], anchoring dozens of 3SUM-hard geometry problems. The SETH–3SUM relationship is *open*: neither implies the other. The program tolerates this plurality of independent conjectures, while some problems (e.g., local alignment) are hard under all of them simultaneously [3].

The slogan **"hardness in P"** [5] captures the achievement: a systematic, NP-hardness-like theory for problems *inside* polynomial time — core conjectures, fine-grained reductions, equivalence classes — since extended to dynamic problems, approximation, and parameterized complexity.

### 4.6 Barriers: why SETH is hard to prove or refute

SETH is a statement about *all* $k$-SAT algorithms — an extremely strong claim.

1. **Unconditional lower-bound obstacles.** Like P vs NP, SETH resists all current lower-bound techniques; a proof would require technology far beyond the state of the art, while a refutation would require genuinely new algorithmic ideas.
2. **The NSETH barrier.** Carmosino et al. [6] showed that refuting the *nondeterministic* SETH would imply new circuit lower bounds (separating E$^{\text{NP}}$ from subexponential-size circuits), and that proving *non-reducibility* between SETH-hard problems *requires* NSETH. This is the fine-grained analogue of the algebrization barrier: the program's own reduction structure is conditionally constrained [6].
3. **The magnification gap.** Proving *any* superpolynomial SAT lower bound is far out of reach, so SETH must be assumed, not derived; its justification is the failure of decades of algorithmic effort — the evidence the program systematizes rather than replaces [5].

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

We now give the central reduction in full: **SETH ⇒ OVC** (Williams' theorem, following the exposition in [7]).

> **Theorem (Williams):** Assume SETH. Then for every $\varepsilon > 0$ there exists $c$ such that Orthogonal Vectors on $n$ vectors in dimension $d = c \log n$ requires $\Omega(n^{2-\varepsilon})$ time. Equivalently: if OV is solvable in $O(n^{2-\varepsilon})$ time, then for some $\delta > 0$, CNF-SAT on $n$ variables is solvable in $O(2^{(1-\delta)n})$ time, refuting SETH.

*Proof sketch.* Fix $\varepsilon_0 > 0$ from SETH: there exists $k$ such that $k$-SAT on $n$ variables needs $2^{(1-\varepsilon_0)n}$ time. Let $\phi$ be a $k$-CNF formula with $n$ variables and $m$ clauses; by the sparsification lemma [1] we may assume $m = O(n)$ at the cost of a $2^{\gamma n}$ factor for arbitrarily small $\gamma > 0$.

Split the variables into halves $X_1, X_2$ of size $n/2$. Let $A$ be the set of all $2^{n/2}$ assignments to $X_1$ and $B$ the set of all $2^{n/2}$ assignments to $X_2$; $|A| = |B| = N = 2^{n/2}$. For each clause $C_j$ and each $a \in A$, define a block of coordinates recording which *sub-pattern* of the clause's variables in $X_1$ the assignment $a$ realizes; similarly for $b \in B$ and $X_2$. The standard gadget (see [7] for the $O(\log n)$-dimensional refinement) ensures:

$$\langle a, b \rangle = 0 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad a \cup b \text{ satisfies every clause of } \phi.$$

For each clause, $a$'s coordinates mark partial assignments to $C_j \cap X_1$ failing to satisfy $C_j$ regardless of $X_2$, and $b$'s mark the complementary patterns; orthogonality across all clause-blocks is equivalent to joint satisfaction. The construction runs in $\tilde{O}(N)$ time, with dimension refined to $O(\log n)$ in the exposited form [7].

Now suppose OV is solvable in time $O(N^{2-\varepsilon}) = O(2^{(1-\varepsilon/2)n})$. Composing the reduction with the OV algorithm solves $k$-SAT in time $\tilde{O}(2^{n/2}) + O(2^{(1-\varepsilon/2)n}) = O(2^{(1-\delta)n})$ for $\delta = \varepsilon/2 - o(1)$, contradicting the SETH choice of $k$. ∎

> **Corollary (Edit distance):** If edit distance on length-$n$ strings is solvable in $O(n^{2-\delta})$ time, then SETH is false [2].

Backurs and Indyk compose the above with a *fine-grained reduction from OV to edit distance* [2]: strings assembled from vector gadgets $G(a)$ over a constant alphabet, with separator blocks forcing alignments to respect gadget boundaries, attain a threshold distance exactly when an orthogonal pair exists. The $\tilde{O}(N)$-time reduction composed with a subquadratic edit-distance algorithm solves SAT in $2^{(1-\delta)n}$ time.

> **Corollary (LCS and DTW):** Strongly subquadratic algorithms for LCS or dynamic time warping refute SETH [2][4].

The same gadget philosophy applies, with alignment scoring replacing edit operations; Bringmann and Künnemann's multivariate analysis [4] shows the bounds are tight against the known parameterized algorithms across the full parameter space, and Abboud et al. [3] extend the phenomenon to local alignment with simultaneous consequences for 3SUM and APSP-class problems.

```python
# Quantitative schema of a fine-grained reduction chain (illustrative)
# SAT(n vars) --split-and-list--> OV(N=2^{n/2} vecs) --gadgets--> EditDistance(len ~ N)
#
# If EditDistance in O(n^{2-delta}):
#   OV in O((2^{n/2})^{2-delta}) = O(2^{(1-delta/2)n})
#   => k-SAT in O(2^{(1-delta')n}) for large k  =>  SETH false
def composed_exponent(delta):
    return 1 - delta / 2   # exponent of 2^n for the resulting SAT algorithm
```

*Evidence for the conjectures.* Every known exact algorithm for edit distance, LCS, DTW, and APSP sits within polylogarithmic or $n^{o(1)}$ factors of its conditional lower bound [2][4][5]; no counterexample has emerged in a decade of intensive effort — the strongest evidence available for conjectures of this strength.

## 6 Limitations

The fine-grained complexity program, for all its explanatory power, has structural limitations that a PhD-level treatment must confront honestly:

1. **Conditionality is inherited, not discharged.** Every lower bound in Sections 4–5 rests on SETH, 3SUM, or APSP. If SETH is false, the entire quadratic network collapses simultaneously — dozens of theorems would be vacated by a single algorithmic breakthrough. The program converts *one* hard conjecture into many theorems; it does not reduce the total conjectural mass. Critics note that this creates a monoculture risk: the field's lower-bound technology is hostage to the truth of SETH in a way classical NP-hardness, anchored in the far more robust P ≠ NP, is not.

2. **The conjectures may be false, and falsifiability is asymmetric.** A faster edit-distance algorithm would refute SETH — but the *absence* of such an algorithm, however prolonged, never confirms it. Worse, SETH asserts something about *all* $k$ simultaneously; a refutation at any fixed $k$ kills the hypothesis, making it an unusually fragile conjecture to build an edifice on. The NSETH analysis [6] deepens this worry: the *structure* of the reduction network itself depends on unproven nondeterministic hypotheses.

3. **Fine-grained reductions are brittle.** The gadget constructions ([2][3][4]) are extraordinarily delicate — one misaligned threshold invalidates the reduction, and several published bounds have needed gadget corrections. The methodology rewards intricate engineering over conceptual insight, and the proofs are hard to audit.

4. **Limited scope.** The equivalence classes [5] group problems by running-time exponent alone, which can feel ad hoc next to NP-completeness' natural decision structure. The program is also silent on *average-case* complexity, *approximation* (where many SETH-hard problems become easy), and *quantum* algorithms.

5. **The barrier results cut both ways.** Carmosino et al. [6] show that certain natural strengthenings of the program (non-reducibility between SETH-hard problems) *require* NSETH — meaning the program cannot even fully map its own internal structure without additional unproven assumptions. This is a genuine foundational limitation, not a technical inconvenience.

## 7 Conclusion

Fine-grained complexity has given algorithm design its first systematic theory of *exact* running times. From the sparsification lemma and the $s_k$ hierarchy of Impagliazzo and Paturi [1], through Williams' split-and-list reduction from SETH to Orthogonal Vectors [7], to the quadratic lower bounds for edit distance [2], LCS and dynamic time warping [4], local alignment's simultaneous assault on all three pillars [3], the architectural vision of Vassilevska Williams [5], and the nondeterministic barriers of Carmosino et al. [6], the program has explained decades of algorithmic stagnation with a precision that classical complexity theory could never achieve.

Yet the program's greatest strength — converting one conjecture into a web of tight lower bounds — is also its defining vulnerability: the bounds are only as credible as SETH, 3SUM, and APSP themselves. As *explanatory* technology, fine-grained complexity is unmatched — it tells us exactly why our algorithms are stuck and which single breakthrough would unstick them all; as *foundational* technology it is a promissory note, and the central open problem is finding independent evidence — or refutations — for its pillars.
