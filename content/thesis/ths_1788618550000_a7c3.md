# Copy-on-Write Filesystems Reconsidered: Btree Topology, Erasure Coding, and Scalable fsck in bcachefs

## Abstract

bcachefs is a copy-on-write (CoW) filesystem for Linux, authored by Kent Overstreet and merged into mainline with kernel 6.7, that rethinks the relationship between a filesystem and its underlying search structures. Rather than layering extent trees, inode tables, allocators, and snapshot machinery as independent subsystems, bcachefs collapses *every* piece of filesystem state — extents, inodes, dirents, xattrs, allocation metadata, backpointers, and snapshot graphs — into roughly twenty-eight B+-trees managed by a single transactional key–value engine [1][2]. Its btrees use unusually large 256 KiB nodes with log-structured interiors, a three-dimensional sort key (*bpos* = inode, offset, snapshot), and snapshot versioning embedded directly in the key space rather than cloned tree roots [2][3]. Writes are erasure-coded through a stripe abstraction that avoids the classical RAID write hole, journaled with device-replicated write-ahead logging, and recovered by a dependency-tracked system of roughly fifty fsck passes rather than a single monolithic checker [1][4]. This thesis presents a detailed technical analysis of the bcachefs design: the topology of its btree family, the mechanics of its copy-on-write transactions, its stripe-based erasure coding geometry, its journal and superblock recovery flow, and the recovery-pass architecture that makes its offline checking scale to tens of terabytes. It concludes with a comparative assessment against ZFS and Btrfs, the two systems whose design space bcachefs most directly inhabits.

## 1 Introduction

Modern copy-on-write filesystems share a family resemblance: a forest of B-trees maps logical structure to physical storage, updates are applied by cloning rather than overwriting, and crash consistency is achieved through write-ahead logging or atomic root updates. Yet within this family, the engineering choices diverge sharply, and those divergences determine the failure modes a filesystem can survive. ZFS builds its Merkle-tree world around the *uberblock* and a slab-based allocator; Btrfs builds around per-inode extent trees, a chunk allocator, and root cloning for snapshots. bcachefs takes a third path: *one* transactional B-tree engine, one key space, one journal, and one recovery framework serving all twenty-eight btrees uniformly [1][7].

This uniformity is the central thesis of the design, and it is worth taking seriously as a research result in its own right. In classical filesystems, each metadata subsystem brings its own on-disk format, its own locking discipline, and its own recovery logic, and the interactions between them — quota accounting versus allocation, reflink sharing versus snapshot deletion, backpointers versus device removal — become the dominant source of bugs. bcachefs instead assigns each subsystem a btree (or several) and routes every mutation through the same transaction, locking, and journaling path. The *alloc* btree, for example, stores one key per bucket tracking data type, dirty sectors, and generation number; the *backpointers* btree stores reverse mappings from physical locations to the keys that reference them; the *snapshots* btree stores the parent/child/sibling links of the snapshot tree [1]. Because all of these are btrees, a single change to the btree implementation — six-phase locking, log-structured node interiors, checksummed bsets — upgrades every subsystem simultaneously.

Three additional design decisions merit close study. First, snapshots are implemented not by cloning a CoW tree (the Btrfs approach) but by *adding a version number to filesystem objects*: the snapshot identifier is a coordinate in every key, so creating a snapshot is O(1) and snapshots scale to the millions on test virtual machines without degradation [2][3]. Second, erasure coding is expressed through a *stripes* btree that records stripe geometry, enabling RAID-6-style layouts that the designers claim avoid both the RAID write hole and I/O fragmentation, paired with a bucket allocator and copying garbage collector [2][1]. Third, crash recovery and offline checking are organized as roughly fifty small, dependency-tracked recovery passes — `check_extents`, `check_allocations`, `check_btree_backpointers`, `journal_replay` — each reading btrees rather than scanning raw devices, which is the key to fsck scalability on large arrays [4][7].

The remainder of this thesis proceeds as follows. Section 2 reviews the background: the CoW filesystem lineage from ZFS through Btrfs, the RAID write-hole problem, and the bcache caching layer from which bcachefs descends. Section 3 describes the analytical methodology. Section 4 — the deep dive — examines the btree topology and key format, CoW transaction semantics, erasure coding geometry, the journal/superblock recovery flow with compression and checksumming, and the recovery-pass architecture. Section 5 reports empirical observations from public fsck traces and documented behavior. Section 6 discusses limitations, including erasure coding maturity, the copying garbage collector's reserve requirements, and the project's turbulent relationship with mainline Linux. Section 7 concludes.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The copy-on-write lineage

Copy-on-write filesystems trace their modern lineage to ZFS (2005), which demonstrated that checksumming every block, storing the checksums in parent pointers up to a single *uberblock*, and never overwriting live data yields self-healing storage [5]. Btrfs (2007) brought CoW into Linux with a different decomposition: B-trees for everything, copy-on-write tree nodes, snapshot-by-root-cloning, and a chunk allocator mapping logical to physical extents. Both systems validated the core thesis — that CoW simplifies crash consistency by making the on-disk state always a consistent rooted structure — and both exposed the costs: ZFS's complexity and CDDL licensing, which prevents full in-kernel integration on Linux [5]; Btrfs's RAID5/6 write-hole and fragmentation pathologies, and the well-known instability of its RAID implementations.

bcachefs originates from a different starting point. Kent Overstreet announced it in 2015 as the successor to *bcache*, the block-layer caching system already in the kernel, with which bcachefs shares roughly 80% of its code; the block-layer cache functionality itself was removed in December 2021 as the filesystem matured [2]. The design brief was explicit: the important features of ZFS (integrated volume management, checksumming, RAID, snapshots) with the performance of ext4 or XFS, all-GPL, and — critically — more reliable than Btrfs [5][3]. It entered mainline Linux in kernel 6.7 (January 2024) after a submission process involving some 2,500 fine-grained patches [4][5].

### 2.2 The RAID write hole and allocator design

The *write hole* is the classical failure of parity RAID: if a crash interrupts a read-modify-write cycle, parity becomes inconsistent with data, and the array cannot distinguish which is correct. Traditional fixes (write-ahead intent logs, journaling) add latency or complexity. bcachefs approaches the problem at the allocator: a *bucket-based allocator* writes full buckets and tracks per-bucket state in the *alloc* btree, so parity updates and data updates land in a single CoW transaction recorded in the journal. The CoW property — nothing is ever overwritten in place — eliminates the torn-write ambiguity that creates the write hole, and the bucket allocator's copy garbage collector reclaims fragmented buckets in the background [2]. The design reserves a configurable fraction of space (default `gc_reserve_percent = 8`) for copy-GC headroom [1].

### 2.3 B-trees at scale: node size and interior structure

Most filesystems use small B-tree nodes (4–16 KiB) because nodes must be rewritten on update. bcachefs defaults to **256 KiB nodes** — an order of magnitude larger — and can afford to because nodes are *internally log-structured*: updates append to a log-structured *bset* within the node rather than rewriting it, forming a hybrid data structure that reduces write amplification [1][2]. Nodes are cached in a dedicated btree cache (not the page cache), and the locking discipline uses *six-locks* — shared/intent/exclusive phases — with the invariant that btree locks are never held during I/O, allowing multi-node operations without write-lock contention [7].

Claims about on-disk formats, defaults, and recovery behavior are grounded in these sources and cross-checked where two independent sources agree. No new code was written and no new measurements were taken; the "empirical" section analyzes publicly reported fsck traces and documented scalability claims, interpreting them through the design model of Section 4. The analysis assumes familiarity with B+-tree indexing, RAID-6 erasure coding, and Linux block-layer concepts.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Btree topology and the *bpos* key space

At the core of bcachefs is the claim that a filesystem is a transactional key–value store. Every piece of state lives in one of roughly twenty-eight B+-trees (twenty-nine in recent metadata versions, adding *stripes* and reconcile bookkeeping) [1][7]. There are no separate inode tables, bitmap allocators, or per-inode extent trees. The catalog divides into functional groups:

| Group | Btrees | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| Core data | `extents`, `inodes`, `dirents`, `xattrs`, `reflink` | File offset → physical mappings; inode metadata; name → inode; extended attributes; shared extent pointers for reflinked data |
| Allocation | `alloc`, `freespace`, `need_discard`, `bucket_gens`, `backpointers` | One key per bucket (type, dirty sectors, generation); free-space index by size/location; buckets awaiting TRIM; generation numbers for stale-pointer detection; reverse physical → key mappings |
| Snapshots | `subvolumes`, `snapshots`, `snapshot_trees`, `subvolume_children`, `deleted_inodes` | Subvolume metadata; snapshot parent/child/sibling links; roots of snapshot trees; subvolume parent–child relations; inodes pending deletion |
| Stripes / misc | `stripes`, `accounting`, `lrus`, `quota`, `rebalance_work` | Erasure-coding stripe geometry; usage accounting; LRU lists; quota tracking; background rebalance state |

All keys share a single three-dimensional coordinate, the *bpos*:

```rust
/// The universal sort key of the bcachefs key-value store [7].
struct bpos {
    inode:    u64,  // inode number (or device/bucket for alloc-space keys)
    offset:   u64,  // file offset, bucket offset, or hashed name
    snapshot: u32,  // snapshot version: the CoW versioning coordinate
}
```

The *snapshot* coordinate is the pivotal innovation. In Btrfs, a snapshot clones a tree root; every snapshot is a root pointer and sharing is implicit in the CoW tree. In bcachefs, each key carries its snapshot version, and snapshot creation inserts a new version node into the *snapshots* btree — an O(1) metadata operation. Different snapshots of the same file hold *different keys in the same btree* [1]. Overstreet has reported a million snapshots on test VMs without scalability problems [3], which follows directly from the design: snapshot count affects key-space density, not tree depth or root fan-out.

The *backpointers* btree deserves emphasis because it inverts the usual metadata direction. Each entry maps a physical bucket location back to the btree key(s) referencing it. This makes device removal, scrubbing, and data migration *queries* rather than *scans*: to evacuate a device, walk its backpointers and rewrite the referencing keys [1]. Bucket generations (`bucket_gens`) provide a cheap stale-pointer check — comparing a pointer's recorded generation against the bucket's current generation — without reading the full *alloc* key [1].

> **Theorem:** In a btree-indexed CoW filesystem with per-key snapshot versioning, the marginal cost of creating a snapshot is O(1) in both time and metadata space, and snapshot deletion cost is proportional to the number of keys uniquely owned by the deleted version — independent of total snapshot count.

*Proof sketch.* Creation inserts a constant number of keys into the `snapshots` btree (parent/child/sibling links) [1]; no data or metadata keys are touched. Deletion must walk keys whose snapshot coordinate equals the deleted version and either drop them or reassign ownership, which touches exactly the uniquely-owned key set. Tree depth is unaffected because versions share the same key space. ∎

### 4.2 Copy-on-write semantics and transactions

Copy-on-write in bcachefs is not a file-level feature but a *transactional invariant*: no btree node and no data bucket is ever modified in place. Every mutation — a file write, a rename, a bucket allocation — is expressed as a set of key insertions/deletions applied atomically by a btree transaction. The transaction protocol uses six-phase locking with *intent* locks, which allow a transaction to reserve the nodes it will touch without holding exclusive locks across I/O; the hard rule is that **btree locks are never held during I/O** [7]. This is what makes multi-node, multi-btree transactions (e.g., allocating buckets, writing extents, updating backpointers and accounting in one atomic step) tractable without deadlock.

Reflink and deduplication-style sharing fall out of the same machinery. The *reflink* btree holds shared extent pointers with reference counts: copying a file with `cp --reflink` inserts new `extents` keys pointing at the same physical data and bumps the reflink refcount, without copying any data. The following Python sketch illustrates the versioned-key logic that makes snapshot and reflink sharing safe:

```python
def cow_write(extents_btree, reflink_btree, key, new_data_ptr):
    """Copy-on-write update of an extent key (simplified model)."""
    old_ptr = extents_btree[key]
    if reflink_btree.refcount(old_ptr) > 1:
        # Shared: decrement old refcount, do NOT overwrite in place
        reflink_btree.decref(old_ptr)
        extents_btree[key] = new_data_ptr   # new key version, same bpos
    else:
        extents_btree[key] = new_data_ptr   # exclusive: still CoW at btree level
    # btree nodes themselves are never rewritten; updates append to bsets
```

Because the allocator hands out whole buckets and the journal records the transaction, a crash can only ever reveal the pre-transaction or post-transaction state — the property that kills the RAID write hole. Full-disk encryption (ChaCha20/Poly1305) [2] and per-extent compression compose with CoW naturally: compression is applied per *extent* rather than per 4 KiB page, which yields better ratios [7], with a maximum encoded extent size of 256 KiB (`encoded_extent_max`) [1].

### 4.3 Erasure coding and stripe geometry

Erasure coding in bcachefs is organized around the *stripes* btree. When erasure coding is enabled, data extents are grouped into *stripes*: sets of blocks spread across devices, of which some hold data and some hold parity. The documented ceiling is RAID-6 — `data_replicas` notes that erasure coding "currently caps this at 3, RAID6" [1], i.e., data plus two parity shards, tolerating two concurrent device failures. Each stripe's geometry (member devices, block offsets, parity rotation) is recorded as a key in the `stripes` btree, and extent pointers reference stripe membership rather than raw device offsets.

The geometry matters for two classic failure modes:

1. **The write hole.** Because stripes are written by CoW transactions — new data and new parity land in fresh buckets and the stripe key is updated atomically — there is no read-modify-write window in which a crash leaves parity inconsistent. Recovery replays the journal and either sees the complete new stripe or the complete old one [1][2].
2. **I/O fragmentation.** The bucket allocator writes sequentially into buckets and the copying garbage collector compacts live data, so parity shards and data shards remain co-located in allocation order rather than scattered by partial overwrites [2].

A candid note is required: in the documented options reference, the `erasure_code` switch is annotated **"DO NOT USE YET"** [1] — erasure coding was, at the time of that documentation, the least mature major subsystem. Its architecture (stripe btree + CoW transactions + bucket allocation) is sound, but production hardening lagged replication. This is a characteristic bcachefs pattern: the design is complete before the implementation is trusted.

### 4.4 Compression, checksumming, and the journal

Every data and metadata write in bcachefs carries a checksum, defaulting to CRC-32C for both (`data_checksum`, `metadata_checksum`), with 64-bit checksum options available [1][2]. Reads retry up to `checksum_err_retry_nr = 3` times on checksum failure before reporting an error [1]. Compression is selectable per-filesystem and per-inode (`compression`, `background_compression`): LZ4, gzip, and Zstandard are supported [1][2], applied per extent up to `encoded_extent_max = 256 KiB`.

The journal is a write-ahead log replicated across devices according to `metadata_replicas` (default 1): if one device's journal is unreadable, recovery proceeds from the other copies [1]. Btree nodes are written lazily; the journal is the source of truth for recent transactions. The on-disk story therefore has three layers:

- **Superblock**: replicated per device, identifies the filesystem, journal locations, and the recovery passes required on next mount.
- **Journal**: ordered transaction records, checksummed, replicated per `metadata_replicas`.
- **Btrees**: lazily checkpointed; on mount, journal replay brings them current.

Mount-time recovery reads the journal (`journal read done, replaying entries N–M`), runs the required passes, and only then goes read-write [6]. The superblock's required-pass list (e.g., `check_topology`, `check_subvols`, `check_extents`, `check_dirents`) is itself versioned: metadata versions 0.10–1.39 (sixty versions) support forward upgrades with downgrade tables [7].

A C-like sketch of the extent pointer format conveys the density of per-pointer metadata:

```c
/* Simplified extent pointer: physical location + metadata [1]. */
struct bch_extent_ptr {
    u64 offset;      /* device bucket offset */
    u8  dev;         /* device index */
    u8  gen;         /* bucket generation: stale-pointer detection */
    u8  cached:1;    /* cached vs. durable */
    /* followed by optional CRC / compression / stripe fields */
};
```

### 4.5 Scalable fsck: the recovery-pass architecture

Traditional `fsck` scales poorly because it is a single program that must hold the whole filesystem's invariants in one pass over raw blocks. bcachefs decomposes checking into **~50 small recovery passes with dependency tracking** [7], each operating on btrees rather than scanning devices. Observed pass names from real fsck traces include:

| Pass | Function |
|---|---|
| `accounting_read`, `alloc_read`, `stripes_read`, `snapshots_read` | Load core btrees into memory |
| `check_topology` | Verify btree root/child pointer consistency |
| `check_allocations`, `check_alloc_info` | Bucket state vs. allocator expectations |
| `check_btree_backpointers`, `check_extents_to_backpointers` | Forward ↔ reverse mapping agreement |
| `check_extents`, `check_inodes`, `check_dirents`, `check_xattrs` | Per-btree key validity |
| `check_snapshots`, `check_snapshot_trees`, `delete_dead_snapshots` | Snapshot graph integrity |
| `journal_replay` | Apply outstanding transactions |
| `bucket_gens_init`, `check_alloc_to_lru_refs` | Generation tables, LRU references |

A real in-kernel offline fsck trace shows the pattern: passes complete in seconds (`check_extents... done (0 seconds)`), with dependency injection — `delete_dead_interior_snapshots` *forces* `check_snapshots` to run, and `btree_bitmap_gc` is scheduled when accounting diverges [6]. The userspace `bcachefs fsck` and the in-kernel offline fsck share this pass machinery [6][8]. Scalability follows from the architecture: each pass is a btree iteration (logarithmic seeks, sequential leaf scans), passes run only when the superblock flags them, and the ~540 hierarchical error codes give precise, typed diagnostics rather than generic corruption flags [7].

## 5 Empirical Results

Public traces corroborate the design claims at three levels. **Snapshot scalability:** Overstreet's report of one million snapshots on test VMs without problems [3] matches the O(1)-creation analysis of Section 4.1 — the cost model has no term in snapshot count. **fsck behavior:** the verbatim trace in [6] shows a full recovery sequence — `accounting_read`, `alloc_read`, `stripes_read`, `snapshots_read`, `check_allocations`, `journal_replay`, `check_btree_backpointers`, `check_extents_to_backpointers`, and the snapshot passes — completing a multi-hundred-gigabyte filesystem check in seconds per pass, with passes dynamically scheduled (`scheduling recovery pass btree_bitmap_gc`) when invariants diverge. **Allocator behavior:** the trace's `246M btree buckets and 2.25G marked in bitmap` line shows the allocator's bucket accounting operating at fine granularity, consistent with the bucket-based design [6].

The mainline history is itself empirical data about process, not just code. The 2,500-patch submission [4], the January 2024 merge in kernel 6.7 [5], the subsequent conflict with Linus Torvalds over rc-phase development practices, the August 2025 reclassification to "Externally maintained," and the removal of bcachefs code beginning with kernel 6.18 [2] together describe a filesystem whose technical design outpaced its institutional integration. The filesystem remains available as an external module [2].

## 6 Limitations

1. **Erasure coding maturity.** The architecture is complete and the write-hole argument is sound, but the `erasure_code` option was long marked "DO NOT USE YET" [1]; production users should treat EC as experimental and prefer replication (`data_replicas`) for durability.
2. **Copy-GC reserve tax.** The bucket allocator requires headroom for the copying garbage collector (`gc_reserve_percent`, default 8%) [1]; on nearly-full filesystems this is a real capacity cost, and GC write amplification under sustained random-write workloads remains the design's principal performance risk.
3. **Snapshot deletion complexity.** Snapshot *creation* is O(1), but deletion walks uniquely-owned keys, and real-world traces show edge cases (empty interior snapshot nodes, orphaned subvolume references) requiring dedicated recovery passes (`delete_dead_interior_snapshots`, `delete_dead_snapshots`) [6].
4. **Ecosystem and support.** Removal from mainline beginning with kernel 6.18 [2] means distribution support, testing breadth, and reviewer attention now depend on the external module and its community; tooling such as Snapper integration remains Btrfs-centric [5].
5. **Checksum strength default.** CRC-32C is fast but not cryptographic; silent-corruption detection relies on replication/EC to *repair*, and single-device single-copy configurations can detect but not heal.

---

## 7 Conclusion

bcachefs is best understood not as a collection of features but as a single architectural bet: that a filesystem should be a transactional key–value store over B+-trees, and that every subsystem — extents, inodes, allocation, backpointers, snapshots, stripes — should be a tenant of that store rather than a bespoke mechanism. From this bet flow the design's distinctive results: 256 KiB log-structured btree nodes that make large nodes affordable; a three-dimensional *bpos* key space that makes snapshots O(1); a stripes btree that expresses erasure coding as ordinary key–value state inside CoW transactions, eliminating the RAID write hole by construction; a replicated journal with lazy btree checkpointing; and a fifty-pass, dependency-tracked recovery architecture that turns fsck from a monolithic scan into a set of btree queries. The limitations are real — erasure coding maturity, the copy-GC reserve, snapshot-deletion edge cases, and the project's exit from mainline Linux — but they are limitations of implementation and institution, not of the architecture. As an existence proof that uniform btree topology can carry the full weight of a modern CoW filesystem, bcachefs is a significant entry in the design literature, and its key-space versioning of snapshots in particular deserves wider adoption.
