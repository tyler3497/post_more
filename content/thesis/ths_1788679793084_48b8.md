# Automatic Differentiation: From Dual Numbers to Compiler-Level Gradient Synthesis

## Abstract

Automatic differentiation (AD) is the algorithmic engine behind modern machine learning, scientific computing, and optimal control. This thesis presents a unified, mathematically precise treatment of the two classical modes of automatic differentiation: *forward mode*, realized algebraically through dual numbers, and *reverse mode*, realized operationally through Wengert lists and tapes. We formalize both modes on the computational graph of an elementary decomposition, prove the fundamental complexity theorem — a single reverse sweep computes an entire gradient row of the Jacobian at cost bounded by a small constant multiple of the primal — and dissect the two dominant implementation paradigms, operator overloading versus source transformation. The memory-compute tradeoff inherent in reverse mode is addressed through checkpointing, culminating in Griewank and Walther's optimal Revolve schedule with its logarithmic growth guarantees. Higher-order differentiation, hyper-dual numbers, and nested adjoints are developed next. Finally, we analyze two modern realizations: JAX's composable function transformations (`grad`, `jit`, `vmap`, `pmap`) and Enzyme's source-transformation AD operating directly on optimized LLVM IR.

## 1. Introduction

Numerical differentiation by finite differences is unstable; symbolic differentiation suffers expression swell. Automatic differentiation occupies the exact middle ground: it evaluates *exact* derivatives (to machine precision) at a point by applying the chain rule mechanically to the sequence of elementary operations executed by a program. Given a differentiable function *f*: ℝⁿ → ℝᵐ implemented in code, AD computes either Jacobian–vector products (forward mode) or vector–Jacobian products (reverse mode) without ever forming a closed-form derivative [1].

The significance of AD is hard to overstate. Backpropagation in neural networks is simply reverse-mode AD applied to a scalar-valued composition [2]. Adjoint methods in PDE-constrained optimization, sensitivity analysis in computational finance, and gradient-based inference in probabilistic programming all reduce to the same two-mode calculus. Yet the gap between the textbook chain rule and an industrial implementation is enormous: tapes, checkpoint schedules, activity analysis, and interaction with optimizing compilers determine whether gradients are obtained in minutes or in weeks.

This thesis contributes a self-contained exposition that connects the algebraic foundation (dual numbers), the operational machinery (Wengert lists), the implementation design space (overloading vs. transformation), the scheduling theory (Revolve checkpointing), and the modern compiler-level systems (JAX and Enzyme). Our central organizing result is the *reverse-mode complexity theorem*: for *f*: ℝⁿ → ℝ with primal cost *C(f)*, the full gradient ∇*f* is computable in time *O(C(f))* and memory *O(C(f))* [3].

## 2. Background

### 2.1 Computational graphs and elementary decompositions

Any straight-line numeric program computing *f* can be decomposed into a sequence of *elementary operations* φ₁, …, φₗ — additions, multiplications, `sin`, `exp`, and the like — applied to *intermediate variables* *v*₁, …, *v*ₗ with inputs *x* = (*x*₁, …, *x*ₙ) = (*v*₁₋ₙ, …, *v*₀) and outputs *y* = (*y*₁, …, *y*ₘ):

$$v_i = \varphi_i(v_{j} : j \prec i), \qquad y_k = v_{l-m+k}.$$

The directed acyclic graph (DAG) recording the dependence *j* ≺ *i* is the *computational graph*. Its topological order is exactly the program's execution order. Differentiability of each φᵢ implies differentiability of *f* by the multivariate chain rule.

### 2.2 The Jacobian as a product of elementary Jacobians

Writing *f* = φₗ ∘ ⋯ ∘ φ₁ locally, the Jacobian factors as *J(f)* = *J*ₗ⋯*J*₁. Forward mode accumulates this product left-to-right (columns: tangents), reverse mode right-to-left (rows: adjoints). This duality — *Jv* versus *wᵀJ* — is the entire story of AD in one line.

### 2.3 Historical notes

Forward accumulation was formalized by Wengert in 1964 [3]; reverse accumulation was discovered independently by Linnainmaa (1970), Werbos (1974), and others. Griewank's monograph [4] remains the canonical mathematical reference, and the Baydin–Pearlmutter–Radul–Siskind survey [2] maps the territory through the machine-learning lens.

## 3. Methodology

Our methodology is threefold. **(i) Algebraic:** we ground forward mode in the ring of dual numbers ℝ[ε]/(ε²), making derivative propagation a consequence of arithmetic rather than bookkeeping. **(ii) Operational:** we define reverse mode as a two-pass algorithm over an explicit *tape* (Wengert list) recording every elementary operation and its primal values, with a formal complexity analysis in the straight-line RAM model. **(iii) Systems:** we compare implementation strategies — operator overloading (PyTorch-style define-by-run), source transformation (Tapenade/OpenAD-style define-then-run), tracing-based transformation (JAX), and IR-level transformation (Enzyme) — against uniform cost metrics, and analyze the optimal checkpointing schedule of Revolve.

We assume throughout that each elementary operation has unit cost and that elementary partials ∂φᵢ/∂*v*ⱼ are available in closed form.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Forward mode via dual numbers

A *dual number* has the form *a* + *b*ε with ε² = 0, introduced by Clifford in 1873. Arithmetic extends by truncation:

$$(a + b\varepsilon)(c + d\varepsilon) = ac + (ad + bc)\varepsilon, \qquad \sin(a+b\varepsilon) = \sin a + b\cos a \cdot \varepsilon.$$

> **Theorem (Dual-number AD):** Let *f* be a composition of differentiable elementary operations. Evaluating the program with dual-number arithmetic at *x* + ε·ẋ yields *f(x)* + ε·(*J(x)*ẋ), where ẋ ∈ ℝⁿ is the *tangent* (seed) vector.

*Proof sketch.* By induction on the elementary sequence: each operation's dual arithmetic exactly implements the chain rule term ∂φᵢ/∂*v* · v̇. ∎

Concretely, one forward pass with tangent seed *e*ⱼ computes column *j* of the Jacobian. The cost is *O(n · C(f))* for the full Jacobian — cheap when *n* is small, prohibitive when *n* ≫ *m* (the neural-network regime).

A minimal Python implementation illustrates the algebra:

```python
class Dual:
    def __init__(self, val, der=0.0):
        self.val, self.der = val, der
    def __mul__(self, other):
        o = other if isinstance(other, Dual) else Dual(other)
        return Dual(self.val * o.val, self.der * o.val + self.val * o.der)
    def __add__(self, other):
        o = other if isinstance(other, Dual) else Dual(other)
        return Dual(self.val + o.val, self.der + o.der)

def f(x1, x2):                      # f = x1*x2 + sin(x1)
    return x1 * x2 + Dual.sin(x1)

x1, x2 = Dual(2.0, 1.0), Dual(3.0, 0.0)   # tangent e1
out = f(x1, x2)
print(out.val, out.der)             # f(2,3) and ∂f/∂x1 = x2 + cos(x1)
```

Forward mode is *non-invasive to memory*: it needs no tape, only the tangent vector carried alongside primals. This makes it the natural choice for *f*: ℝⁿ → ℝᵐ with *n* ≪ *m*, e.g. parameter sensitivities of ODE solutions.

### 4.2 Reverse mode: Wengert lists, tapes, and the complexity theorem

Reverse mode splits differentiation into two phases. The **forward (primal) sweep** executes the program, appending each elementary operation — its inputs, its operator, and its computed value — to a *tape*, also called a *Wengert list* after Wengert's 1964 formulation (which was in fact forward accumulation; the tape itself is the shared artifact) [1][3]. The **reverse (adjoint) sweep** walks the tape backwards, propagating *adjoints* v̄ᵢ = ∂*y*/∂*v*ᵢ:

$$\bar{v}_i = \sum_{j: i \prec j} \bar{v}_j \frac{\partial \varphi_j}{\partial v_i}, \qquad \bar{y} = 1.$$

A canonical example on *y* = *x*₁*x*₂ + sin *x*₁, with tape *v*₁ = *x*₁, *v*₂ = *x*₂, *v*₃ = *v*₁*v*₂, *v*₄ = sin *v*₁, *v*₅ = *v*₃ + *v*₄:

| Reverse step | Update | Result at *x* = (2, 3) |
|---|---|---|
| seed | v̄₅ = 1 | 1 |
| *v*₅ = *v*₃ + *v*₄ | v̄₃ += v̄₅·1, v̄₄ += v̄₅·1 | v̄₃ = 1, v̄₄ = 1 |
| *v*₄ = sin *v*₁ | v̄₁ += v̄₄·cos *v*₁ | v̄₁ = cos 2 |
| *v*₃ = *v*₁*v*₂ | v̄₁ += v̄₃·*v*₂, v̄₂ += v̄₃·*v*₁ | v̄₁ = 3 + cos 2, v̄₂ = 2 |

The adjoints of the inputs are exactly the gradient components [1]. In Haskell, the essence is a fold over a reversed instruction list:

```haskell
-- tape: list of (output index, operator, input indices, partials)
reverseSweep :: Tape -> Adjoints -> Adjoints
reverseSweep tape adj = foldr propagate adj (reverse tape)
  where propagate (i, _, js, partials) a =
          foldl (\acc (j, p) -> addTo acc j (a!i * p)) a (zip js partials)
```

> **Theorem (Reverse-mode complexity):** For *f*: ℝⁿ → ℝ with primal cost *C(f)*, one reverse sweep computes the full gradient ∇*f(x)* in time ≤ *ω·C(f)* for a small constant *ω* (typically 2–5), using memory *O(C(f))* for the tape. The bound is independent of *n*.

*Proof sketch.* Each elementary operation φᵢ contributes its adjoint update v̄ⱼ += v̄ᵢ·∂φᵢ/∂*v*ⱼ — a constant number of multiply-adds per edge of the computational graph. Summing over all edges gives *O(|E|)* = *O(C(f))*. Memory stores one primal value per tape entry: *O(C(f))*. ∎

This *n*-independence is why deep learning is possible at all: a billion-parameter loss gradient costs a few multiples of one forward evaluation, not a billion. The price is the tape: storing every intermediate of a 1000-layer network is the memory wall that motivates §4.3's checkpointing.

*Fan-out and accumulation.* When a variable feeds *k* consumers, its adjoint accumulates *k* contributions (v̄₁ above received two). This is the "accumulation" in reverse accumulation, and it is exact — no approximation enters.

### 4.3 Implementation paradigms and the memory wall: checkpointing and Revolve

**Operator overloading vs. source transformation.** The two classical implementation strategies differ in *when* the computational graph is materialized [5][6]:

| Dimension | Operator overloading (OO) | Source transformation (ST) |
|---|---|---|
| Mechanism | Numeric type overloaded; tape recorded at runtime | Compiler parses source, emits differentiated code |
| Examples | ADOL-C, PyTorch autograd, CoDiPack | Tapenade, OpenAD, Enzyme (at IR level) |
| Control flow | Natural (tape reflects executed path) | Requires static analysis of branches/loops |
| Overhead | Per-operation dispatch + tape allocation | Near-zero at runtime; cost at compile time |
| Optimization | Limited (tape is opaque to compiler) | Full (differentiated code is optimizable) |

OO tools are unbeatable for legacy code: replace `double` with an active type and the tape builds itself. ST tools win on performance because the adjoint program is ordinary compiled code subject to inlining, vectorization, and dead-code elimination. A hybrid strategy — OO tape for orchestration, ST (e.g. Enzyme) for hot kernels — captures both [6].

**Checkpointing.** For long computations (time-stepping loops, deep unrolled networks), the tape grows linearly with steps *T*, exceeding memory. *Checkpointing* stores only a subset of states and recomputes forward segments during the reverse sweep — trading compute for memory [4][7]. The naive uniform schedule with *c* checkpoints costs *O(T²/c)* recomputation; the optimal schedule does far better.

> **Theorem (Griewank–Walther, Revolve):** With *c* checkpoint slots and *T* time steps, the optimal binomial checkpointing schedule (*Revolve*) achieves reverse-mode differentiation with temporal complexity *O(T log T)* and spatial complexity *O(c log T)* — logarithmic growth in both, the best possible up to constants [7][8].

The Revolve algorithm solves the combinatorial scheduling problem exactly: given *c* checkpoints and a step budget, it computes the maximal *T* reachable (a binomial coefficient expression) and the placement achieving it. Intuitively, checkpoints are placed at binomially-spaced intervals so that each recomputation segment is itself optimally checkpointed — a recursive, fractal structure. Modern implementations such as Checkpointing.jl expose Revolve schedules that interoperate with both Zygote-style OO AD and Enzyme [9]. For adaptive or unknown *T*, *online* checkpointing algorithms (Stumm–Walther) extend the theory [10].

### 4.4 Higher-order derivatives and JAX's composable transformations

**Higher-order AD.** Differentiating an AD-generated derivative yields Hessians and beyond. Options include:

1. *Nested forward-over-reverse*: forward-mode dual numbers applied to a reverse-mode gradient — computes Hessian–vector products *Hv* in *O(C(f))* without forming *H*.
2. *Hyper-dual numbers* (*a* + *b*ε₁ + *c*ε₂ + *d*ε₁ε₂, ε₁² = ε₂² = 0): a single evaluation yields *f*, ∇*f*·ẋ, and second-order mixed terms exactly, with no subtractive cancellation [11].
3. *Taylor-mode AD*: propagate truncated Taylor series through the graph, computing higher derivatives in *O(d²)* per operation for degree *d* rather than the *O(d!)* of naive nesting.

Reverse-over-reverse is notably treacherous: differentiating a tape-building program requires differentiating the tape machinery itself, which is why most systems implement it via source-level transformation rather than OO nesting.

**JAX: transformations as functions.** JAX (Frostig, Johnson, Maclaurin, Leary et al.) reframes AD as *composable function transformations* over a pure functional subset of Python + NumPy [12]. Tracing a Python function produces a *jaxpr* — a typed, first-order intermediate representation in ANF/SSA form, morally a Wengert list. Transformations then rewrite jaxprs:

```python
from jax import grad, jit, vmap
grad_fn   = jit(grad(loss))                        # compiled reverse-mode gradient
perex     = jit(vmap(grad_fn, in_axes=(None, 0, 0)))  # per-example grads, vectorized
```

Composability is the key property: `grad(grad(f))` differentiates the transposed-jaxpr transformation itself; `jit(vmap(grad(f)))` fuses, vectorizes, and compiles in one pipeline, lowering through XLA to GPU/TPU. Because `grad` is just a jaxpr→jaxpr rewrite, nesting is principled rather than accidental. Limitations follow from tracing: data-dependent control flow must go through `lax.cond`/`lax.scan`, and side effects are forbidden — purity is the price of transformability.

### 4.5 Enzyme: AD after optimization, at the LLVM level

Enzyme (Moses & Churavy, 2020) performs *source-transformation-style AD on optimized LLVM IR* [13]. Rather than parsing C++/Fortran/Julia/Rust source or overloading operators, Enzyme runs as an LLVM compiler plugin: it takes the optimizer's output IR, performs *activity analysis* (which values actually influence the differentiated outputs) and *type analysis*, then synthesizes the adjoint IR — including a differentiated *shadow* memory model and a tape realized as ordinary LLVM values.

The decisive advantage is *ordering*: differentiating *after* LLVM's optimization pipeline means the adjoint is generated from simplified, inlined, vectorized IR rather than from naive source structure. On Microsoft's ADBench suite this yields a **4.2× geometric-mean speedup** over differentiating pre-optimization IR, with state-of-the-art absolute performance [13]. Because any language with an LLVM frontend (C, C++, Fortran, Julia, Rust, Swift, MLIR dialects) lowers to the same IR, Enzyme is language-agnostic — it differentiates "foreign code" without rewrites, and integrates with PyTorch/TensorFlow to let ML frameworks call differentiated C/Fortran kernels directly [13][14]. Extensions cover GPU kernels (SC'21) and parallel paradigms — OpenMP, MPI, Julia tasks — via compiler augmentation (SC'22) [15].

Enzyme's tape is implicit in the generated IR: checkpoints, caching of primal values, and recomputation decisions are made by the synthesis algorithm with the full power of LLVM analyses behind it — a modern answer to the memory wall of §4.3.

## 5. Empirical Results and Proofs

**Complexity comparison.** For *f*: ℝⁿ → ℝᵐ with primal cost *C*:

| Method | Time (full Jacobian) | Memory | Exact? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Finite differences | *O(n·C)* | *O(1)* | No (truncation + roundoff) |
| Forward mode | *O(n·C)* | *O(1)* extra | Yes |
| Reverse mode | *O(m·C)* | *O(C)* tape | Yes |
| Reverse + Revolve(*c*) | *O(C·T log T)*, *c* slots | *O(c log T)* | Yes |
| Symbolic differentiation | *O(C)* eval, expression swell | Expression-size dependent | Yes |

The table is the practical decision procedure: scalar losses with many parameters (*n* ≫ *m* = 1) demand reverse mode — the backpropagation theorem in full generality [2].

**Empirical anchors.** (i) Enzyme's 4.2× geomean speedup of post-optimization over pre-optimization AD on ADBench, reaching parity with or beating hand-written and OO baselines [13]. (ii) Revolve's binomial schedule is provably optimal for the fixed-step checkpointing model; implementations confirm the logarithmic memory/compute scaling on PDE time-stepping benchmarks with hundreds of steps and single-digit checkpoint slots [7][9]. (iii) JAX's `jit(grad(f))` routinely compiles reverse-mode gradients of neural-network losses to XLA executables within small constant factors of hand-tuned frameworks, demonstrating that transformation-based AD composes with industrial compilers [12].

**Proof sketch — optimality of binomial checkpointing.** Griewank and Walther show that with *c* checkpoints, the maximum number of steps reversible with *t* forward evaluations satisfies the recurrence *T(c, t)* = *T(c−1, t−1)* + *T(c, t−1)* + 1, whose solution is binomial; any schedule is bounded by this recurrence, and Revolve attains it [7]. Hence no checkpointing scheme with *c* slots can asymptotically beat Revolve's *O(log T)* overhead.

## 6. Limitations and Open Problems

1. **Nonsmoothness.** ReLU, absolute value, and control-flow-dependent kinks make *f* nondifferentiable on measure-zero sets; AD returns an arbitrary subgradient element. Lexicographic differentiation and conservative Jacobians are active remedies, but no tape-based system handles them transparently.
2. **Memory wall persistence.** Revolve is optimal for straight-line step sequences, but arbitrary DAGs need the NP-hard-adjacent optimal checkpointing of arbitrary computation graphs; recent shortest-path formulations on accessibility graphs extend optimality to linear and then arbitrary graphs at *O(|V|²|E| + |V|³ log |V|)* solver cost [16].
3. **Higher-order reverse.** Reverse-over-reverse remains poorly supported in OO systems; transformation-based systems (JAX, Enzyme) handle it but with code-size blowup and fragile activity analysis at second order.
4. **Language coverage vs. analyzability.** Enzyme requires statically analyzable IR: `dlopen`'d code, exceptions, and inline assembly defeat it [14]. JAX requires functional purity. A unified AD that tolerates effects, exceptions, and dynamic dispatch without giving up transformation power is unsolved.
5. **Probabilistic and discrete structure.** Differentiating through samplers, sorting, and combinatorial solvers demands relaxations (Gumbel-softmax, perturbed optimizers) outside classical AD's scope.

## 7. Conclusion

Automatic differentiation is best understood as a single idea — the chain rule applied to an explicit computational graph — realized through a spectrum of engineering choices. Dual numbers make forward mode *algebra*; Wengert lists make reverse mode *data*; the reverse-mode complexity theorem explains why backpropagation scales; checkpointing theory (Revolve) explains how it fits in memory; and modern systems show the two viable endpoints of the design space — JAX's pure functional transformations over traced jaxprs, and Enzyme's post-optimization synthesis over LLVM IR. The field's trajectory is clear: AD is migrating downward into compilers, where activity analysis, optimization ordering, and checkpoint scheduling compound. The remaining frontier is upward: nonsmooth, higher-order, and effectful programs await a differentiation theory as clean as the one Wengert began in 1964.
