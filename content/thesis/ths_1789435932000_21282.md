# From Interactive Proofs to Succinct Arguments: The Sumcheck Protocol, Polynomial Commitments, and the Permutation Arithmetization of PLONK

## Abstract

Zero-knowledge succinct non-interactive arguments of knowledge (zk-SNARKs) have advanced from per-circuit trusted setups to universal, updatable, and fully transparent constructions. This article traces the lineage from interactive proof systems to modern succinct arguments through three pillars: the sumcheck protocol, which compresses claims about sums over exponentially large hypercubes into logarithmic-round interactions with a linear-time prover; polynomial commitment schemes, which bind the prover to hidden polynomials while enabling compact evaluation proofs, instantiated by KZG, inner-product, and FRI-based designs; and the PLONK arithmetization, which replaces per-circuit rank-1 constraint systems with a universal constraint language built from gate identities and a grand-product permutation argument. We analyze the Fiat–Shamir transform that compiles these interactive protocols into non-interactive arguments in the random-oracle model, and we compare Groth16, PLONK, and STARKs along setup assumptions, proof size, prover complexity, and cryptographic hardness. The discussion closes with applications to zk-rollups and verifiable computation, together with the principal limitations that define current research frontiers.

##1 Introduction

The demand for **verifiable computation** — outsourcing heavy computation to untrusted machines while retaining cryptographic certainty that the result is correct — has grown from a theoretical curiosity into the backbone of blockchain scaling. A *succinct zero-knowledge argument* lets a prover convince a verifier that some computation was performed correctly, with a proof that is short (polylogarithmic or constant in the computation size), fast to check, and reveals nothing beyond the claim's truth.

Three ideas, each born in a different decade, conspire to make this possible:

1. **The sumcheck protocol** (1992), which turns claims about sums over exponentially large domains into interactive conversations of only logarithmically many rounds [1].
2. **Polynomial commitment schemes** (2010), which let a prover bind to an entire polynomial with a single group element and later prove any of its evaluations with compact, checkable evidence [5][6].
3. **PLONK arithmetization** (2019), which replaces the rigid rank-1 constraint systems of earlier SNARKs with a flexible, universal constraint language whose wiring is enforced by a permutation argument over Lagrange bases [3].

Together with the **Fiat–Shamir transform**, which replaces interactive verifier randomness with a cryptographic hash, these components yield the modern non-interactive proof systems deployed in zk-rollups today [8]. This article explains each component with mathematical precision, shows how they compose, compares the dominant instantiations — *Groth16*, *PLONK*, and *STARKs* — and assesses their limitations.

---

##2 Background

### Interactive proofs and their descendants

The story begins with interactive proof systems — a powerful prover in randomized conversation with a polynomial-time verifier — and the celebrated result that interaction plus randomness captures exactly *PSPACE*. The field then pursued two diverging goals:

- **Reducing interaction.** Can the conversation be collapsed to a single message? This led to *non-interactive* arguments.
- **Reducing communication.** Can the total proof be shorter than the computation itself? This is *succinctness*, achieved by Kilian's argument systems built on probabilistically checkable proofs (PCPs): a long PCP is compressed by committing to it with a hash tree, so the verifier checks only a few randomly sampled locations.

Modern succinct arguments crystallize this evolution into a layered architecture:

| Layer | Role | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Arithmetization** | Encode the computation as algebraic constraints | R1CS, PLONKish, AIR |
| **Polynomial IOP** | Interactive protocol where the prover sends oracle access to polynomials | Sumcheck-based PIOPs, PLONK protocol [3][7] |
| **Polynomial commitment** | Replace oracle access with binding commitments and opening proofs | KZG, IPA/Bulletproofs, FRI [5][6] |
| **Compiler** | Make the protocol non-interactive | Fiat–Shamir in the random-oracle model |

This four-layer decomposition is the analytical backbone of the field: every major system — Groth16, PLONK, STARKs — is one choice per row, plus a security analysis of how the choices compose. Recent work has even shown that sumcheck-based schemes compiled in this way satisfy *non-malleability* properties essential for distributed deployments [2].

### Zero-knowledge and knowledge soundness

Two properties distinguish zero-knowledge arguments from plain succinct ones. **Knowledge soundness** requires that a convincing prover must *know* a witness, formalized through an extractor. **Zero-knowledge** requires the verifier learn nothing beyond the statement's truth, formalized through a simulator. Both the commitment scheme and the Fiat–Shamir compiler must preserve these properties — naive compilation destroys zero-knowledge unless blinding is designed in from the start.

---

##3 Methodology

This article proceeds by **constructive decomposition**. Rather than surveying systems as monoliths, we isolate each of the four layers, develop it with full mathematical detail, and then show how the layers compose:

1. We derive the sumcheck protocol from first principles and prove its soundness bound, treating it as the canonical *polynomial interactive oracle proof*.
2. We present polynomial commitment schemes as a standalone primitive with a formal syntax (setup, commit, open, verify) and compare the KZG, inner-product-argument, and FRI-based constructions along a uniform axis of assumptions and asymptotics.
3. We reconstruct the PLONK constraint system gate by gate — selector polynomials, wire polynomials, the gate identity, and the grand-product permutation argument — showing how arbitrary circuits are expressed without per-circuit setup.
4. We analyze the Fiat–Shamir transform in the random-oracle model, stating precisely which security properties survive compilation and which require additional machinery.

For the comparison we use the standard dimensions of trusted setup, proof size, prover time, verifier time, and hardness assumptions [8], all asymptotic in the circuit size *n*.

##4 The Machinery of Succinct Zero-Knowledge Arguments

### 4.1 The Sumcheck Protocol: Collapsing Exponential Sums into Logarithmic Rounds

The sumcheck protocol is the engine room of modern succinct arguments. It addresses the following problem: given a multivariate polynomial *g* over a finite field, with *v* variables and total degree *d*, and a claimed value *H*, convince the verifier that the sum of *g* over the entire Boolean hypercube equals *H*:

$$H = \sum_{b \in \{0,1\}^v} g(b)$$

The hypercube contains *2^v* points — exponentially many — yet the protocol completes in only *v* rounds.

**The round structure.** In round *i*, the prover sends the univariate polynomial formed by binding the first *i − 1* variables to the verifier's earlier challenges and summing over the remaining ones:

$$g_i(X_i) = \sum_{b \in \{0,1\}^{v-i}} g(r_1, \ldots, r_{i-1}, X_i, b)$$

The verifier checks that *g_i(0) + g_i(1)* equals the previous round's claimed value, then samples a fresh random challenge *r_i* and continues. After *v* rounds, the verifier evaluates *g* at a single point *(r_1, ..., r_v)* itself and checks consistency.

> **Theorem:** (Sumcheck soundness). If the claimed sum *H* is incorrect, then any (even unbounded) cheating prover succeeds with probability at most *v·d / |F|*, where *|F|* is the field size.

Soundness follows by induction: a lying round forces the next univariate polynomial to differ from the honest one, so they agree at the random challenge with probability at most *d / |F|* by Schwartz–Zippel; union-bounding over *v* rounds gives the result.

**Why the prover is fast.** The naive prover would evaluate *g* over the whole hypercube in every round, but a classic optimization restructures the computation so each round scans the remaining table once: *O(2^v)* total field operations, linear in the number of summands. Modern refinements keep most multiplications in a small base field when the protocol runs over an extension field, cutting expensive operations by orders of magnitude [1] — which is why sumcheck underlies the fastest known SNARK provers, including Spartan and HyperPlonk [7].

A minimal illustration of the round logic in Python:

```python
def sumcheck_round(poly_vals, claimed_sum, challenge):
    # poly_vals: evaluations of g over remaining hypercube, ordered by bit
    # returns the univariate g_i as coefficients (constant, linear) for multilinear g
    half = len(poly_vals) // 2
    g_at_0 = sum(poly_vals[:half])
    g_at_1 = sum(poly_vals[half:])
    assert g_at_0 + g_at_1 == claimed_sum, "prover inconsistent"
    # fold: interpolate the next table along the challenged variable
    folded = [(1 - challenge) * a + challenge * b
              for a, b in zip(poly_vals[:half], poly_vals[half:])]
    # verifier's next claim is g_i(challenge), checked next round
    next_claim = g_at_0 + challenge * (g_at_1 - g_at_0)
    return folded, next_claim
```

Each round halves the table and binds one variable to the verifier's challenge; after *v* rounds a single field element remains, checked against one direct evaluation of *g*.

### 4.2 Polynomial Commitment Schemes: From KZG to FRI

A *polynomial commitment scheme* (PCS) binds a prover to a polynomial *f* with a short digest *C*, later proving *f(z) = y* for any claimed point without revealing *f*. The requirements are **binding** and **evaluation binding** (no commitment opens to two polynomials, or two values at one point); hiding variants add zero-knowledge.

**KZG commitments** [5] work over a pairing-friendly elliptic curve. A trusted setup publishes powers of a secret *τ* in the exponent: *τ^0·G, τ^1·G, ..., τ^d·G*. To commit to *f*, the prover computes *C = f(τ)·G* using these public parameters — possible without knowing *τ*. To prove *f(z) = y*, the prover computes the *quotient polynomial* *q(x) = (f(x) − y) / (x − z)*, which is a genuine polynomial exactly when the claim is true, and sends *π = q(τ)·G*. The verifier checks a single pairing equation:

$$e(C - y \cdot G_1,\ G_2) = e(\pi,\ \tau \cdot G_2 - z \cdot G_2)$$

> **Theorem:** (KZG evaluation binding). Under the *t-strong Diffie–Hellman* assumption, no efficient adversary can produce a commitment and two valid opening proofs for distinct values at the same point.

KZG gives constant-size commitments and constant-size opening proofs, at the price of a trusted setup and pairing-friendly curves that are not post-quantum secure. Batching techniques extend KZG so that a single group element can open many polynomials at one point, or one polynomial at many points, keeping verifier work nearly constant [6].

**Inner-product-argument (IPA) commitments**, as in Bulletproofs, eliminate the trusted setup entirely — security reduces to the discrete logarithm assumption — at the cost of logarithmic-size proofs and a linear-time verifier. **FRI-based commitments**, the foundation of STARKs, are also transparent and additionally believed post-quantum secure, resting only on hash functions, but proofs are larger (tens to hundreds of kilobytes).

| Scheme | Setup | Proof size | Assumption | Post-quantum |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **KZG** [5] | Trusted (universal, updatable) | O(1) | t-SDH / pairings | No |
| **IPA / Bulletproofs** | Transparent | O(log n) | Discrete log | No |
| **FRI (STARKs)** | Transparent | O(log² n) | Collision-resistant hash | Believed yes |

The commitment scheme is the most consequential design decision in a proof system: it simultaneously determines the setup ceremony, the asymptotic proof size, and the post-quantum outlook.

A sketch of the KZG opening in Rust-like pseudocode:

```rust
// prover side: open commitment `com` of f at point z with claimed value y
fn kzg_open(f: &Poly, z: Field, y: Field, srs: &Srs) -> G1 {
    // quotient q(x) = (f(x) - y) / (x - z); exists iff f(z) == y
    let q = (f.clone() - Poly::constant(y)) / Poly::from_roots(&[z]);
    srs.commit_poly(&q)  // returns q(tau) * G1 using the SRS
}

// verifier side
fn kzg_verify(com: G1, z: Field, y: Field, pi: G1, vk: &Vk) -> bool {
    // e(com - y*G1, G2) == e(pi, tau*G2 - z*G2)
    pairing(&(com - G1::generator() * y), &vk.g2)
        == pairing(&pi, &(vk.tau_g2 - G2::generator() * z))
}
```

### 4.3 PLONK Arithmetization: Gate Identities and the Grand-Product Permutation Argument

Earlier SNARKs such as Groth16 encode computations as *rank-1 constraint systems* (R1CS), requiring a fresh trusted setup per circuit. **PLONK** [3] instead defines a *universal* constraint system: one setup supports all circuits up to a size bound, and circuits are described by two kinds of polynomial identities.

**Gate identities.** Fix a multiplicative subgroup *H* of the field, one gate per element. The prover supplies wire values *a, b, c* as polynomials over *H*, and the circuit designer supplies *selector polynomials* *q_M, q_L, q_R, q_O, q_C* encoding each gate's operation:

$$q_M(X) \cdot a(X) b(X) + q_L(X) \cdot a(X) + q_R(X) \cdot b(X) + q_O(X) \cdot c(X) + q_C(X) = 0 \quad \forall X \in H$$

*q_M = 1* gives a multiplication gate; *q_L = q_R = 1, q_O = −1* gives addition; constants and boolean constraints fall out similarly. *Custom gates* generalize the identity with higher-degree terms and more wires, letting complex operations occupy a single gate — the key idea behind TurboPlonk and UltraPlonk.

**Copy constraints via the permutation argument.** Gate identities alone do not wire gates together. PLONK encodes wiring as a *permutation σ* over wire positions, and the prover shows the wire assignment is constant on each cycle of *σ*. This is enforced by a *grand-product argument*: with a random challenge *β* and *γ*, the prover builds an accumulator polynomial *z* satisfying

$$z(\omega X) = z(X) \cdot \frac{a(X) + \beta X + \gamma}{a(X) + \beta \sigma(X) + \gamma}} \cdots$$

where the product runs over the wire polynomials and *ω* generates *H*. If and only if the wire values respect the permutation, the telescoping product closes with *z* vanishing appropriately on *H*.

> **Lemma:** (Permutation product identity). Let *σ* be a permutation of the wire positions. Then the wire assignment respects every copy constraint of *σ* if and only if the two grand products — over positions and over permuted positions — are equal as multisets, which the accumulator identity checks with overwhelming probability over random *β, γ*.

Gate and permutation identities are combined into a single *quotient polynomial t(X)* divisible by the subgroup's vanishing polynomial *Z_H(X)*. The prover commits to all wire and accumulator polynomials, and the verifier's entire check reduces to a handful of polynomial evaluations at one random point, each verified through the commitment scheme. The PlonKup extension shows the same grand-product machinery yields *lookup arguments* (plookup): each query is shown to appear in a precomputed table, the technique that makes zkEVM designs practical [4].

### 4.4 The Fiat–Shamir Transform and Random-Oracle Security

So far everything is *interactive*: the verifier's challenges must be unpredictable to the prover. The **Fiat–Shamir transform** compiles any public-coin protocol into a non-interactive one by deriving each challenge as a hash of the transcript prefix:

```
challenge_i = H(transcript_prefix_i)
```

The prover computes the entire proof alone, hashing each of its messages to obtain the challenges the verifier *would* have sent; the verifier recomputes the same hashes and checks consistency. In the **random-oracle model** — where the hash is treated as a truly random function — this compilation preserves soundness: a cheating prover that fools the non-interactive verifier can be rewound to fool the interactive one, because each hash query fixes a challenge before the prover commits to the next message.

Three caveats:

1. **The transform is heuristic.** Security is proved in the random-oracle model, but real hashes are not random oracles, and contrived protocols secure interactively can break after Fiat–Shamir. Standard protocols like PLONK are nonetheless widely accepted under this heuristic.
2. **Zero-knowledge must be designed in.** Fiat–Shamir preserves zero-knowledge only if the underlying protocol is *honest-verifier* zero-knowledge *and* the simulator can program the oracle — in practice, protocols add random blinding factors to every committed polynomial.
3. **Weak Fiat–Shamir is dangerous.** Hashing only part of the transcript (omitting the statement or prior commitments) has broken real deployments; the transform must absorb the *entire* prefix, including public inputs.

The compilation of polynomial IOPs through commitments and Fiat–Shamir is exactly the modern SNARK construction paradigm, and recent analyses confirm that sumcheck-based instantiations inherit strong properties such as simulation extractability — the non-malleability needed when proofs are posted to adversarial networks [2].

##5 Empirical Results and Proofs

### Asymptotic comparison of Groth16, PLONK, and STARKs

The table below compares the three dominant families on the dimensions that drive deployment decisions. All complexities are asymptotic in the circuit or trace size *n*.

| Dimension | Groth16 | PLONK | STARKs |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Trusted setup** | Per-circuit (toxic waste per program) | Universal, updatable (one ceremony for all circuits) | None (transparent) |
| **Proof size** | O(1): 3 group elements | O(1): constant group elements | O(log² n): tens–hundreds of KB |
| **Prover time** | O(n log n) | O(n log n) | O(n log² n) |
| **Verifier time** | O(1) + public-input term | O(1) + public-input term | O(log² n) |
| **Core assumption** | Knowledge-of-exponent + pairings | Knowledge-of-exponent + pairings (KZG) | Collision-resistant hashing |
| **Post-quantum** | No | No (with KZG) | Believed yes |
| **Custom gates** | No (R1CS only) | Yes (Turbo/UltraPlonk) [7] | Yes (AIR) |
| **Recursion** | Expensive (pairing-unfriendly cycles) | Moderate | Natural (hash-based) |

**Groth16** remains the proof-size champion — three group elements and the fastest verifier — but every program change demands a new per-circuit ceremony. **PLONK** trades a few extra group elements for a universal setup and a far more expressive constraint language, and HyperPlonk makes its prover linear-time with high-degree custom gates [7]. **STARKs** pay larger proofs for transparency and post-quantum security, the right trade when no trusted party may exist.

### Complexity-theoretic results

> **Theorem:** (Succinctness of the compiled protocols). Compiling the PLONK polynomial IOP with a KZG commitment scheme and the Fiat–Shamir transform yields a non-interactive argument with *O(1)*-size proofs and *O(1)*-time verification (plus a term linear in the public input size), under the *t*-strong Diffie–Hellman assumption in the random-oracle model [3][5].

> **Theorem:** (Transparent succinctness). Compiling a sumcheck-based polynomial IOP with a FRI commitment scheme yields proofs of size *O(log² n)* and verification time *O(log² n)* under only collision-resistant hashing — no trusted setup and no number-theoretic assumptions [8].

### Empirical observations

Benchmarking confirms the asymptotics while exposing constant-factor realities [8]: prover time is dominated by multi-scalar multiplications and NTTs in pairing-based systems and by Merkle hashing in STARKs, with GPU acceleration narrowing the gap. Recent sumcheck prover optimizations attack the dominant cost directly, keeping expensive extension-field arithmetic minimal when committed values live in a small base field [1].

##6 Limitations

1. **Trusted setups remain a trust bottleneck.** KZG-based systems (Groth16, PLONK) need a structured reference string whose trapdoor must be destroyed. Universal updatable ceremonies mitigate but do not eliminate the assumption: a single honest participant suffices, but *verifying* that claim is a social, not mathematical, process.
2. **No post-quantum security for pairing-based SNARKs.** Shor's algorithm breaks the assumptions underlying Groth16, PLONK-with-KZG, and IPA commitments. Only hash-based systems (STARKs, FRI) are believed quantum-resistant — at the cost of larger proofs.
3. **The random-oracle gap.** Fiat–Shamir security proofs live in the random-oracle model. While no practical attack on standard-model instantiations of PLONK or STARKs is known, the heuristic is unproven and cannot be proven in full generality.
4. **Prover resource intensity.** Provers need gigabytes of RAM for large circuits; memory, not time, is often the binding constraint, scaling with the SRS size.
5. **Arithmetization overhead.** Compiling real programs into constraints is lossy: one EVM opcode can expand into thousands of gates or rows, which is why lookup arguments [4] and custom gates [7] remain active research.
6. **Soundness error composition.** Each layer contributes soundness error — sumcheck's *v·d/|F|*, the commitment's computational binding, Fiat–Shamir's forking bounds — and the union over all of them must stay negligible, forcing large fields and conservative parameters.

##7 Conclusion

The modern succinct argument composes four decades of ideas: interactive proofs showed randomness plus interaction can verify arbitrary computation; the sumcheck protocol collapsed exponential sums into logarithmic conversations with linear-time provers [1]; polynomial commitments turned those conversations into compact, checkable objects [5][6]; PLONK's permutation arithmetization made the constraint language universal and expressive [3][4][7]; and the Fiat–Shamir transform made the assembly non-interactive. The Groth16–PLONK–STARK space is best understood as one tension — *trusted setup and tiny proofs* versus *transparency and quantum resistance* — with the commitment scheme as the dial. As zk-rollups move toward general-purpose virtual machines, pressure is shifting from proof size to prover efficiency and arithmetization expressiveness, and the sumcheck-plus-commitment paradigm, refined by recent work on non-malleability and small-characteristic optimization [1][2], is where the frontier lies.
