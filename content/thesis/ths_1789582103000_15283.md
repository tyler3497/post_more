# The Hasse Principle and the Brauer–Manin Obstruction: Rational Points on Cubic Surfaces

## Abstract

The question of whether a smooth projective variety over a number field possesses a rational point admits a necessary condition: points must exist over every completion of the field. For quadrics this condition is also sufficient, by the Hasse–Minkowski theorem, but for cubic surfaces it fails — smooth cubic surfaces over the rationals can be everywhere locally soluble yet devoid of rational points, as first shown by Swinnerton-Dyer in 1962 and refined by Mordell, Cassels, Guy, Birch, and Swinnerton-Dyer. This article explains these failures through the cohomological mechanism introduced by Manin in 1971: the Brauer–Manin obstruction, which pairs the adelic points of a variety with its Brauer group via local class field theory. For a smooth cubic surface the algebraic Brauer group is governed by the Galois action on the 27 lines, a combinatorial problem in the Weyl group of type E6, and the resulting classes of order two and three account for every known counterexample. We survey the computational machinery, the persistence theorem of Viray, and the Colliot-Thélène–Sansuc conjecture that the obstruction is exhaustive.

##1 Intro

The *local–global principle*, often called the *Hasse principle*, asks whether solubility over every completion of a number field forces solubility over the field itself. Precisely: if $X$ is a projective variety over a number field $k$, and $X(k_v) \neq \emptyset$ for every place $v$ of $k$ (including the archimedean ones), does it follow that $X(k) \neq \emptyset$? For quadrics the answer is the celebrated **Hasse–Minkowski theorem**: a quadratic form over a number field represents zero nontrivially over $k$ if and only if it does so over every completion $k_v$. The proof is a triumph of class field theory, and for decades it encouraged the hope that the same pattern held for varieties of higher degree.

That hope was misplaced. In 1951, Selmer exhibited the diagonal plane cubic curve

$$3x^3 + 4y^3 + 5z^3 = 0$$

which has points over $\mathbf{R}$ and over every $\mathbf{Q}_p$ but no rational point [7]. For surfaces, the first counterexample was constructed by Swinnerton-Dyer in 1962: a smooth cubic surface over $\mathbf{Q}$ with points everywhere locally and none globally. Mordell produced an infinite series generalizing Swinnerton-Dyer's example, and Cassels and Guy found the strikingly simple diagonal example

$$5x^3 + 12y^3 + 9z^3 + 10w^3 = 0,$$

again everywhere locally soluble but without a rational point [3]. Around 1970, Manin observed that these scattered counterexamples shared a common cohomological origin [6]. The mechanism he uncovered — the *Brauer–Manin obstruction* — has since become the central tool for understanding rational points on Fano varieties, and cubic surfaces are its original and still most instructive testing ground.

> **Theorem:** *(Hasse–Minkowski)* Let $q$ be a quadratic form over a number field $k$. Then $q$ represents zero nontrivially over $k$ if and only if it represents zero nontrivially over every completion $k_v$. No such statement holds for cubic forms: counterexamples exist already among smooth cubic surfaces over $\mathbf{Q}$ [1][3][6].

The stakes of the subject were raised by Colliot-Thélène and Sansuc, who conjectured that the Brauer–Manin obstruction is in fact the *only* obstruction to the Hasse principle for geometrically rational surfaces — and, in Colliot-Thélène's broader formulation, for all smooth projective geometrically rationally connected varieties [6][2]. For cubic surfaces this conjecture remains open, but it has accumulated remarkable supporting evidence, both computational and theoretical, surveyed below.

##2 Background

### The adelic picture

Let $k$ be a number field and $\mathbf{A}_k$ its ring of adeles, the restricted product of the completions $k_v$ over all places $v$ of $k$. For a smooth projective variety $X$ over $k$, the set of *adelic points* $X(\mathbf{A}_k)$ is the product of the local point sets $X(k_v)$. The diagonal embedding $k \hookrightarrow \mathbf{A}_k$ induces an inclusion $X(k) \hookrightarrow X(\mathbf{A}_k)$, and the Hasse principle is exactly the assertion that nonemptiness of $X(\mathbf{A}_k)$ implies nonemptiness of $X(k)$. We write $X(\mathbf{A}_k)^{\mathrm{Br}}$ for the *Brauer–Manin set*, an intermediate subset

$$X(k) \;\subseteq\; X(\mathbf{A}_k)^{\mathrm{Br}} \;\subseteq\; X(\mathbf{A}_k),$$

defined cohomologically. If $X(\mathbf{A}_k)^{\mathrm{Br}} = \emptyset$ while $X(\mathbf{A}_k) \neq \emptyset$, we say there is a *Brauer–Manin obstruction to the Hasse principle*; if the inclusions are strict, there is an obstruction to *weak approximation* [3][6].

### The Brauer group and the pairing

The *Grothendieck–Brauer group* $\mathrm{Br}(X) = H^2_{\acute{e}t}(X, \mathbf{G}_m)_{\mathrm{tors}}$ classifies Azumaya algebras on $X$ up to Morita equivalence. For each place $v$, local class field theory provides the *invariant map* $\mathrm{inv}_v \colon \mathrm{Br}(k_v) \xrightarrow{\sim} \mathbf{Q}/\mathbf{Z}$, an isomorphism at non-archimedean places and an injection onto the subgroup of order two at real places. Given a class $\mathcal{A} \in \mathrm{Br}(X)$ and an adelic point $(x_v)$, one evaluates the pullback of $\mathcal{A}$ to each local point and sums the invariants:

$$\langle (x_v), \mathcal{A} \rangle \;=\; \sum_v \mathrm{inv}_v\big(\mathcal{A}(x_v)\big) \;\in\; \mathbf{Q}/\mathbf{Z}.$$

The Brauer–Manin set is the common kernel of all such pairings as $\mathcal{A}$ ranges over $\mathrm{Br}(X)$.

> **Theorem:** *(Manin)* Let $X$ be smooth and projective over the number field $k$, and let $\mathcal{A} \in \mathrm{Br}(X)$. Then every rational point $x \in X(k)$ satisfies $\sum_v \mathrm{inv}_v(\mathcal{A}(x_v)) = 0$, where $(x_v)$ is the adelic point induced by $x$. Consequently $X(k) \subseteq X(\mathbf{A}_k)^{\mathrm{Br}}$ [3][6].

The proof is the global reciprocity law of class field theory: the sum of the local invariants of any class in $\mathrm{Br}(k)$ vanishes, and evaluating $\mathcal{A}$ at a $k$-rational point factors through $\mathrm{Br}(k)$. Only the quotient $\mathrm{Br}(X)/\pi^*\mathrm{Br}(k)$ matters, where $\pi \colon X \to \mathrm{Spec}\,k$ is the structure morphism, since classes pulled back from the base pair trivially by reciprocity [3].

### Algebraic versus transcendental classes

There is a natural filtration $\mathrm{Br}_0(X) \subseteq \mathrm{Br}_1(X) \subseteq \mathrm{Br}(X)$, where $\mathrm{Br}_1(X) = \ker(\mathrm{Br}(X) \to \mathrm{Br}(\overline{X}))$ is the *algebraic* Brauer group. For a smooth projective geometrically rational variety, $\mathrm{Br}(\overline{X}) = 0$, so every class is algebraic, and the Hochschild–Serre spectral sequence yields

$$\mathrm{Br}(X)/\pi^*\mathrm{Br}(k) \;\cong\; H^1\big(k, \mathrm{Pic}(\overline{X})\big),$$

a finite abelian group computable from the Galois action on the Picard lattice [3][6]. For a smooth cubic surface $S$ over $\overline{k}$, the surface is the blow-up of $\mathbf{P}^2$ at six points in general position, so $\mathrm{Pic}(\overline{S}) \cong \mathbf{Z}^7$ with an intersection form of signature (1, 6); its orthogonal complement to the canonical class is the root lattice of type $E_6$. The celebrated *27 lines* on $S$ generate this Picard group, and the Galois action factors through the Weyl group $W(E_6)$ of order 51,840. The computation of $H^1(k, \mathrm{Pic}(\overline{S}))$ is therefore a finite combinatorial problem — albeit a demanding one — in the orbit structure of the 27 lines [1][3].

For K3 surfaces the situation is genuinely harder: $\mathrm{Br}(\overline{X})$ need not vanish, and transcendental classes can obstruct rational points, a phenomenon explored in van Luijk's work relating cubic points on cubic curves to the Brauer–Manin obstruction on K3 surfaces [7].

##3 Methodology

Computing a Brauer–Manin obstruction on a smooth cubic surface $S$ over $\mathbf{Q}$ proceeds through a pipeline that is now largely algorithmic, though several steps remain computationally demanding [1][3]:

1. **Determine the Galois action on the 27 lines.** Compute the splitting field of the 27 lines, or at least its Galois group as a subgroup of $W(E_6)$, and enumerate the orbits. From the orbit data, compute $H^1(\mathbf{Q}, \mathrm{Pic}(\overline{S}))$, which enumerates the algebraic Brauer classes modulo constants.
2. **Identify a splitting field.** Find a finite extension $L/\mathbf{Q}$ over which a *sixer* — six pairwise skew lines — is Galois-invariant; over such $L$ the relative Brauer group vanishes, and restriction maps reduce the computation to cyclic subextensions. Classical configurations (double-sixes, Steiner trihedra, triplets of sixers) dictate whether the resulting class has order 2 or 3 [3].
3. **Write down an explicit Azumaya algebra.** A class in $H^1(\mathbf{Q}, \mathrm{Pic}(\overline{S}))$ is represented by a divisor $D$ with norm $N_{L/\mathbf{Q}}(D)$ principal; an explicit rational function $f$ with divisor equal to that norm yields a cyclic algebra, or, for classes of order 2, a quaternion algebra $(f, g)$. This is the hardest step: finding the rational function amounts to finding a rational point on a norm equation, and no fully general algorithm is known [3][6].
4. **Evaluate local invariants.** For each place $v$, compute $\mathrm{inv}_v(\mathcal{A}(x_v))$ as $x_v$ ranges over $S(\mathbf{Q}_v)$. By continuity of the evaluation map, the invariant is constant on each connected component in the $v$-adic topology, and for all but finitely many $v$ it vanishes identically. Order-2 classes are evaluated via the Hilbert symbol; order-3 classes via norm-residue symbols. The geometry of the reduction of $S$ mod $v$ sharply constrains the possible values [3].
5. **Apply reciprocity.** Sum the local constants over all places. If the sum is nonzero on every adelic point, then $S(\mathbf{A}_\mathbf{Q})^{\mathrm{Br}} = \emptyset$: a Brauer–Manin obstruction to the Hasse principle [3].

##4 Deep Dive

### The Brauer group of a smooth cubic surface

The entire algebraic Brauer group of a smooth cubic surface is encoded in the Galois module structure of the 27 lines. Manin's fundamental computation shows that $\mathrm{Br}(S)/\pi^*\mathrm{Br}(k)$ is annihilated by 6, and in fact every class has order dividing 2 or 3; the group is a product of copies of $\mathbf{Z}/2$ and $\mathbf{Z}/3$ whose ranks are read off from the orbit decomposition [3]. Two classical line configurations produce the two kinds of classes:

- **Order-2 classes** arise from *double-sixes*: pairs of sixers $\{a_1,\dots,a_6\}$, $\{b_1,\dots,b_6\}$ with $a_i$ meeting $b_j$ for $i \neq j$ and skew to $b_i$. A Galois-invariant double-six yields a quaternion algebra, typically presented as a cyclic algebra of degree 2 built from a rational function whose divisor is the difference of the two sixers.
- **Order-3 classes** arise from *triplets* of sixers (Steiner trihedra): three sixers permuted cyclically by Galois. The associated Brauer class is a cyclic algebra of degree 3, and the norm equation it encodes is precisely the shape appearing in the Birch–Swinnerton-Dyer family of norm-form surfaces [1][3].

Among the conjugacy classes of subgroups of $W(E_6)$ that produce order-3 classes, exactly nine admit a cyclic cubic splitting field, and for a surface with a Galois-invariant triplet every splitting field contains one of the three natural ones — a surprisingly rigid statement proved by inspecting the subgroup lattice [3].

### The pairing and reciprocity in practice

For a quaternion class $\mathcal{A} = (f, g)$ of order 2, the local evaluation at a $v$-adic point $x_v$ is the Hilbert symbol $(f(x_v), g(x_v))_v \in \{0, 1/2\} \subseteq \mathbf{Q}/\mathbf{Z}$. The evaluation map $\mathrm{ev}_v \colon \mathrm{Br}(S) \times S(\mathbf{Q}_v) \to \mathbf{Q}/\mathbf{Z}$ is continuous in the second variable, so the invariant is locally constant on $S(\mathbf{Q}_v)$; for all but finitely many places it vanishes identically [3]. The classical counterexamples are explained by a uniform pattern:

> **Theorem:** *(Manin)* Suppose $\mathcal{A} \in \mathrm{Br}(S)$ has order 2 and that $\mathrm{inv}_v(\mathcal{A}(x_v))$ is constant (independent of $x_v$) for every place $v$, with the constants summing to a nonzero value in $\mathbf{Q}/\mathbf{Z}$. Then $S(\mathbf{A}_\mathbf{Q})^{\mathrm{Br}} = \emptyset$: there is a Brauer–Manin obstruction to the Hasse principle [3][6].

Bright's analysis of the evaluation map shows how sharply the answer depends on the geometry of the reduction: for a suitably generic cubic surface whose reduction at some prime is a cone, the local evaluation is surjective onto the possible invariants and no Brauer–Manin obstruction can occur — a result extending Colliot-Thélène, Kanevsky, and Sansuc [3]. This is why the *special* Galois action on the 27 lines is the true source of the obstruction: the arithmetic is controlled by the geometry of the line configuration.

### The Colliot-Thélène–Sansuc conjecture and persistence

The boldest conjecture in the subject asserts that the Brauer–Manin obstruction is *exhaustive* for rational surfaces:

> **Conjecture:** *(Colliot-Thélène–Sansuc)* Let $S$ be a smooth projective geometrically rational surface over a number field $k$. If $S(\mathbf{A}_k)^{\mathrm{Br}} \neq \emptyset$, then $S(k) \neq \emptyset$ [1][6].

Colliot-Thélène further conjectured that the same holds for all smooth projective geometrically rationally connected varieties [2]. West's computational verification of the conjecture for infinitely many Birch–Swinnerton-Dyer norm-form surfaces provides the strongest direct evidence in the cubic-surface case [1].

A striking recent theorem of Viray gives the conjecture a sharper form for cubic surfaces [2]:

> **Theorem:** *(Viray)* Let $X$ be a cubic surface over a global field $k$. A Brauer–Manin obstruction to the existence of $k$-points persists over every extension $L/k$ whose degree is coprime to 3. Hence the Colliot-Thélène–Sansuc conjecture for cubic surfaces implies that $X$ has a $k$-rational point if and only if $X$ admits a zero-cycle of degree 1.

The zero-cycle statement is a special case of a conjecture of Cassels and Swinnerton-Dyer, so the sufficiency of the Brauer–Manin obstruction is tied to one of the oldest open problems about cubic surfaces [2].

### Beyond the algebraic: transcendental classes and integral points

Because $\mathrm{Br}(\overline{S}) = 0$ for a smooth cubic surface, all obstructions are algebraic — the transcendental Brauer group vanishes. This is a special feature of rational surfaces; for K3 surfaces, transcendental classes genuinely obstruct, and van Luijk's thesis exhibits the phenomenon by linking Galois cubic points on diagonal cubic curves to the Brauer–Manin obstruction on the associated Kummer K3 surfaces [7].

The *integral* Hasse principle — for affine models, asking for integral rather than rational points — is strictly harder. Colliot-Thélène, Kanevsky, and Sansuc constructed the first infinite families of integral counterexamples, and only recently did Lyczak, Mitankin, and Uppal produce the first counterexamples to the integral Hasse principle for *affine diagonal* cubic surfaces, together with quantitative distribution results: failures are sparse but precisely counted, with $B^2/\log B \ll N(B) \ll B^2(\log B)^6$ for the number of counterexamples of height at most $B$ [4]. Their surfaces all possess rational points, so the integral obstruction is genuinely new, not inherited from a rational failure — and some of it is explained by transcendental Brauer elements [4][6].

##5 Empirical/Proofs

The computational record for cubic surfaces is unusually strong. Manin's original "informal algorithm" for producing the Brauer class from a Galois-invariant sixer or triplet has been implemented in computer algebra systems: given the 27 lines with explicit equations, one computes the Galois group as a subgroup of $W(E_6)$, extracts a class in $H^1(G, \mathrm{Pic})$, finds a splitting field $L$, and solves the resulting norm equation for the rational function representing the cyclic or quaternion algebra [1][3]. West's thesis work applied exactly this pipeline to the Birch–Swinnerton-Dyer norm-form family




1. *Local solubility* is checked place by place, typically by exhibiting explicit local points or by Hensel lifting from smooth mod-$p$ points.
2. *The Brauer class* is produced from the line configuration as above; its order (2 or 3) is read from the Galois orbits.
3. *Local invariants* are computed: at the finitely many "bad" places the evaluation map is determined by analyzing the reduction and, for quaternion classes, by computing Hilbert symbols on explicit local points [3].
4. *Reciprocity* finishes the argument: the invariants are constant on each $S(\mathbf{Q}_v)$ and sum to $1/2$ (order-2 case) or $\pm 1/3$ (order-3 case), so no adelic point can satisfy the global reciprocity constraint, and $S(\mathbf{A}_\mathbf{Q})^{\mathrm{Br}} = \emptyset$.

##6 Limitations

The Brauer–Manin obstruction is not a universal solvent, and cubic surfaces, for all their tractability, expose several hard boundaries:

- **The sufficiency conjecture is open.** For cubic surfaces over number fields, it is not known whether $S(\mathbf{A}_k)^{\mathrm{Br}} \neq \emptyset$ always implies $S(k) \neq \emptyset$; Viray's persistence theorem reduces this to the Cassels–Swinnerton-Dyer zero-cycle conjecture, which is equally open [2]. In full generality, Skorobogatov showed the Brauer–Manin obstruction is *not* the only obstruction (his bielliptic surface examples), so the phenomenon is special to rationally connected varieties [6].
- **Explicit Azumaya algebras are hard.** The step from a cohomology class in $H^1(k, \mathrm{Pic})$ to an explicit rational function or quaternion algebra requires solving a norm equation over the splitting field — a task for which no general algorithm exists, and which in practice limits computations to surfaces with small splitting fields [3][6].
- **Transcendental phenomena appear next door.** Already for K3 surfaces — and for affine models of cubic surfaces in the integral setting — transcendental Brauer classes and obstructions beyond the Brauer–Manin set (étale Brauer–Manin, descent obstructions) play a role [4][7].
- **Weak approximation is finer than the Hasse principle.** Even when rational points exist, they may fail to be dense in the Brauer–Manin set; the obstruction explains failures of weak approximation on cubic surfaces that satisfy the Hasse principle, and the distribution of the failure across places is delicate [3][6].
- **Statistical ignorance persists.** Browning's theorem singles out cubic surfaces in $\mathbf{P}^3$ as the one Fano family where the density of rational points is not understood — the local-to-global question remains open for a typical cubic surface [5].

---

##7 Conclusion

The story of rational points on cubic surfaces is the story of the local–global principle growing up. The Hasse–Minkowski theorem for quadrics suggested a universe in which local solubility always lifts; Selmer, Swinnerton-Dyer, Mordell, Cassels, Guy, Birch, and Swinnerton-Dyer showed that cubic surfaces live outside it; and Manin supplied the cohomological language — the Brauer group, the adelic pairing, and global reciprocity — that turns a zoo of counterexamples into instances of a single mechanism [3][6]. For smooth cubic surfaces the theory is as complete as anywhere in arithmetic geometry: the obstruction group is finite and computable from the 27 lines, the pairing is explicit via Hilbert and norm-residue symbols, and computational campaigns have verified the Colliot-Thélène–Sansuc conjecture on infinite families [1]. Yet the central question — whether the Brauer–Manin obstruction is the *only* obstruction — remains open, tethered by Viray's persistence theorem to the classical conjecture on zero-cycles of degree one [2]. Until it is settled, the cubic surface will remain what it has been since 1962: the simplest variety on which the Hasse principle fails, and the sharpest probe of why.
