# The Replication Crisis and the Credibility Revolution: P-Hacking, Researcher Degrees of Freedom, and the Statistical Reformation of Science

## Abstract

Beginning in 2011, a cascade of failed replications, statistical exposés, and high-profile fraud cases forced the empirical sciences into an epistemological reckoning now known as the *replication crisis*. This thesis traces the crisis from its theoretical foundations — John Ioannidis's 2005 model of the positive predictive value of research findings [1] and Simmons, Nelson, and Simonsohn's 2011 demonstration that undisclosed analytic flexibility makes false positives trivially easy to manufacture [2] — through the empirical verdicts of the Reproducibility Project: Psychology and the Many Labs consortia [4, 9], to the ongoing *credibility revolution*: preregistration [6], multiverse and specification-curve analysis [5, 7], and Bayesian reforms of statistical practice [3]. Drawing on nine primary sources and large-scale replication evidence across psychology, economics, and cancer biology, I argue that the crisis was never principally a story of fraud. It was a story of incentive structures interacting with a misunderstood statistical machinery — and the reforms it catalyzed constitute the most significant methodological advance in empirical science in half a century.

## 1 Intro

On March 9, 2011, the *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology* published a paper reporting that Cornell undergraduates could *feel the future* — that they exhibited extrasensory perception in nine experiments with a combined significance level that its author, Daryl Bem, presented as compelling [3]. The paper passed peer review at one of psychology's flagship journals. Within months, three independent replication teams reported failure. The ESP paper did not survive; but the questions it raised did: if peer review at a top journal could not filter an implausible claim supported by *p* < 0.05, what exactly was the filter *for*?

The Bem episode detonated in a minefield that was already laid. In the same year, Diederik Stapel — one of Europe's most decorated social psychologists — was exposed as having fabricated entire datasets, and Joseph Simmons, Leif Nelson, and Uri Simonsohn published "False-Positive Psychology," showing that no fraud at all was needed to produce Bem-like results: ordinary, ubiquitous, and largely unconscious analytic flexibility was sufficient [2, 8].

What followed was the **replication crisis**: a decade-long audit of the empirical literature, and then the **credibility revolution**: preregistration, open data, registered reports, multiverse analysis, and a Bayesian reformation of statistical practice. This thesis is organized as follows. Section 2 reconstructs the theoretical machinery of the crisis — Ioannidis's positive predictive value model, the researcher-degrees-of-freedom argument, and the garden of forking paths. Section 3 describes the methodology of the large-scale replication evidence. Section 4 is a deep dive into the five pillars of the debate: p-hacking, publication bias, preregistration, multiverse analysis, and Bayesian alternatives. Section 5 reports the empirical results. Section 6 confronts the limitations of both the crisis narrative and its proposed cures. Section 7 concludes.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The null-hypothesis significance testing machinery

The dominant inferential ritual of twentieth-century empirical science is null-hypothesis significance testing (NHST). A researcher posits a sharp point null hypothesis *H₀* (typically "zero effect, zero systematic error"), computes a test statistic, and rejects *H₀* when the *p*-value falls below a conventional threshold, almost always α = 0.05. Rejection is then narrated as "discovery."

Two features of this machinery matter for everything that follows. First, the *p*-value is routinely misinterpreted: it is **not** the probability that the null hypothesis is true, nor the probability that the finding is false — it is the probability of data at least as extreme *assuming the null is true*. Second, as Cohen (1994) and others argued decades before the crisis, the "nil hypothesis" of exactly zero effect is almost never literally true: two treatments are always different to *some* decimal place [1]. The machinery thus tests a hypothesis nobody believes against a threshold whose evidential meaning is widely misunderstood.

### 2.2 Ioannidis's positive predictive value model

In 2005, John Ioannidis asked a question that sounds almost theological: *what is the probability that a published research finding is true?* [1] Framing the literature as a screening process, he derived the **positive predictive value** (PPV) of a claimed finding:

> **Theorem (Ioannidis, 2005).** Let *R* be the pre-study odds that a tested relationship is true, α the Type I error rate, and β the Type II error rate. Then the probability that a claimed "statistically significant" finding is true is
>
> PPV = (1 − β)·R / (R − β·R + α).
>
> Adding a bias parameter *u* — the fraction of analyses that would not have been findings but are reported as such due to design and reporting flexibility — drives PPV lower still.

The corollaries are devastating and purely mathematical. A finding is less likely to be true when studies are *smaller*, when effect sizes are *smaller*, when *more* relationships are tested with *less* pre-selection, when there is *greater flexibility* in designs and analyses, and when fields are "hot" with many teams chasing significance [1]. The model predicts that in low-power, high-flexibility fields, most published findings *must* be false — not because scientists are dishonest, but because the screening process itself is the problem.

### 2.3 Researcher degrees of freedom

Simmons, Nelson, and Simonsohn (2011) supplied the mechanism [2]. The culprit, they argued, is **researcher degrees of freedom**: the myriad ambiguous analytic decisions — whether to collect more data, which observations to exclude, which conditions to combine, which covariates to include, which measures to report — that are typically made *after* seeing the data, guided by the desire for *p* ≤ 0.05. Through simulations, they showed that four commonplace flexibilities together push the actual false-positive rate from the nominal 5% to **above 60%**. To prove the point was not merely theoretical, they ran real experiments "demonstrating" the absurd: that listening to the Beatles' "When I'm Sixty-Four" makes people *chronologically younger* — statistically significant, peer-reviewable, and entirely artifactual.

The problem, they stressed, is *not* malicious intent. It is the combination of (a) genuine ambiguity about the right analysis and (b) motivated reasoning: researchers, like all humans, resolve ambiguity in favor of the conclusions they desire [2]. Their remedy was disclosure-based: a simple 21-word addition to every methods section ("We report how we determined our sample size, all data exclusions... all manipulations, and all measures in the study"), plus six concrete author requirements and four reviewer guidelines — minimal burden, maximal transparency.

### 2.4 The garden of forking paths

Gelman and Loken (2014) generalized the argument beyond deliberate choice [3]. Even when a researcher performs only *one* analysis, that analysis is contingent on the data: had the data looked different, a different exclusion rule or a different model would have been "the obvious choice." This is the **garden of forking paths** — data-dependent analysis that inflates Type I error without any conscious p-hacking at all. Their canonical examples (ovulation and voting, ESP, the "beautiful parents have more daughters" claim) showed how forking paths produce publishable false positives from researchers acting in complete good faith.

### 2.5 How prevalent are these practices?

John, Loewenstein, and Prelec (2012) surveyed over 2,000 psychologists using incentivized truth-telling elicitation [8]. The results were sobering: large fractions admitted to practices such as failing to report all dependent measures, deciding whether to collect more data after checking for significance, and selectively reporting studies that "worked." Combining three estimation methods, they concluded that some questionable practices "may constitute the prevailing research norm" [8]. The crisis, in other words, was not a few bad apples — it was the barrel.

The following simulation, in the spirit of Simmons et al. [2], makes the inflation concrete. Pure noise data; the analyst tries up to *k* plausible variants and reports the best:

```python
import numpy as np
from math import erf, sqrt

def false_positive_rate(n_sims=20000, n=30, alpha=0.05, k_choices=4, seed=1789252165):
    """Nominal alpha vs. actual false-positive rate under k analysis choices."""
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    hits = 0
    for _ in range(n_sims):
        p_min = 1.0
        for _c in range(k_choices):
            x = rng.normal(size=n)          # pure noise: no true effect
            t = x.mean() / (x.std(ddof=1) / sqrt(n))
            p_two_sided = 2 * (1 - 0.5 * (1 + erf(abs(t) / sqrt(2))))
            p_min = min(p_min, p_two_sided)
        hits += (p_min < alpha)
    return hits / n_sims

for k in [1, 2, 4, 8]:
    print(f"k={k}: empirical false-positive rate = {false_positive_rate(k_choices=k):.3f}")
```

With *k* = 1 the rate is ≈ 0.05, as advertised. With *k* = 4 it roughly quadruples; with *k* = 8 it approaches 0.35 — and this is before publication bias selects, from thousands of such analyses across the field, only the significant ones for print.

> **Theorem (P-hacking inflation bound).** If an analyst selects the minimum *p*-value from *k* independent candidate tests and reports significance when minᵢ pᵢ ≤ α, then under the global null, P(false rejection) = 1 − (1 − α)ᵏ. At α = 0.05 and *k* = 18, this exceeds 0.60.

## 3 Methodology

The theoretical arguments of Section 2 made *predictions*: if the literature is inflated by degrees of freedom and publication bias, then direct, high-powered, preregistered replications of published findings should succeed at rates far below the literature's near-100% significance rate. Testing that prediction required a new methodology: **large-scale, collaborative, preregistered replication**.

The landmark was the **Reproducibility Project: Psychology** (RP:P) [4]. The Open Science Collaboration replicated 100 experimental and correlational studies from three top journals (*Psychological Science*, *JPSP*, *JEP: Learning, Memory, and Cognition*), using high-powered designs and original materials where available, with protocols and analysis plans specified in advance. Replication success was assessed by multiple criteria: statistical significance in the same direction, whether the original effect size fell within the replication's 95% confidence interval, and subjective ratings of whether the replication "replicated" the original.

The **Many Labs** consortia extended the design across settings: Many Labs 2 (Klein et al., 2018) administered preregistered, peer-reviewed protocols for 28 classic and contemporary findings to 125 samples comprising 15,305 participants across 36 countries and territories — testing not just *whether* effects replicate but whether replicability *varies* across samples and settings [9]. Parallel projects exported the methodology: the Social Sciences Replication Project (Camerer et al., 2018; 21 studies in *Nature* and *Science*), an economics replication project (Camerer et al., 2016), and the Reproducibility Project: Cancer Biology (Errington et al., 2021), which attempted 50 experiments from 23 high-impact preclinical papers.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The anatomy of p-hacking

P-hacking is not one practice but a portfolio. The table below catalogues the principal researcher degrees of freedom identified across [2, 3, 8]:

| Degree of freedom | Typical manifestation | Effect on Type I error |
|---|---|---|
| Optional stopping | Peeking at data; stopping when *p* < 0.05 | Severe inflation |
| Selective exclusion | Dropping "outliers" post hoc | Moderate–severe |
| Covariate shopping | Trying control sets until significance | Moderate |
| Measure selection | Reporting only the DV that "worked" | Severe |
| Condition merging | Combining/contrasting cells post hoc | Moderate |
| Subgroup analysis | Reporting significant subgroups only | Severe |
| HARKing | Presenting post hoc hypotheses as a priori | Inflates apparent confirmation |

Two refinements matter.

### 4.2 Publication bias and the decline effect

Even perfectly honest analyses produce a distorted literature if only significant results are published.

The signature of publication bias is the **decline effect**: effect sizes shrink as evidence accumulates.

### 4.3 Preregistration and the prediction–postdiction distinction

Nosek, Ebersole, DeHaven, and Mellor (2018) diagnosed the core pathology as a confusion of **prediction** and **postdiction** [6].

Preregistration does not forbid exploration; it *labels* it.

Evidence suggests the mechanism works: preregistered studies and registered reports publish null results at far higher rates than the traditional literature, consistent with the claim that much of the historical significance rate was manufactured by flexibility and selection.

### 4.4 Multiverse analysis and specification curves

Preregistration constrains *future* flexibility, but what of the vast published record — and of analyses where many choices are genuinely defensible?

Simonsohn, Simmons, and Nelson (2020) formalized and extended the idea as **specification-curve analysis** [7]: (1) enumerate all theoretically justified, statistically valid, non-redundant specifications; (2) display the full curve of estimates so readers can see which decisions are consequential; (3) conduct *joint* inference across specifications via permutation-based tests of the aggregate evidence.

The multiverse reframes the question.

### 4.5 Bayesian alternatives and the reformation of thresholds

A parallel reform movement targets the inferential framework itself [3].

1. **Bayes factors**, which quantify relative evidence for competing models rather than rejecting a straw-man null;
2. **Hierarchical and regularized modeling**, which shrink noisy estimates toward plausible values and directly combat Type M error;
3. **Posterior predictive checking**, which asks whether a model can reproduce the data's salient features;
4. **Redefined significance thresholds** (e.g., the proposal to move the default α from 0.05 to 0.005 for novel claims), reducing the false-positive rate at the cost of power — a trade-off Ioannidis's model makes explicit [1].

Gelman and colleagues further advocate **design analysis** and **retrodictive checks**: evaluating, before and after a study, the probability of sign and magnitude errors given plausible effect sizes [3].

## 5 Empirical Results

The large-scale replication projects delivered the empirical verdict summarized below.

| Project | Domain | Replicated / Total | Headline statistic |
|---|---|---|---|
| Reproducibility Project: Psychology [4] | Psychology | 36–47 / 100 | 97% of originals significant vs. 36% of replications; replication effects half the magnitude |
| Many Labs 1 | Psychology | 11 / 13 (85%) | High replication for pre-selected robust effects |
| Many Labs 2 [9] | Psychology (28 findings, 125 samples, 15,305 participants) | 15 / 28 (54%) | Median *d*: 0.60 original → 0.15 replication; 32% of effects reversed sign |
| Many Labs 3 | Psychology | 3 / 10 (30%) | Preregistered, peer-reviewed protocols |
| Social Sciences Replication Project | *Science*/*Nature* social science | 12–14 / 21 (57–67%) | Higher replication for stronger original evidence |
| Economics Replication Project | Experimental economics | 11 / 18 (61%) | — |
| RPP: Cancer Biology | Preclinical cancer biology | 39 / 97 (40%) | Of positive original effects replicated |
| Experimental philosophy | Philosophy | 31 / 40 (78%) | Highest replication rate observed |

Several patterns emerge. First, **replication rates cluster around one-third to two-thirds**, far below the literature's ~97% significance rate [4] — exactly the gap Ioannidis's model predicts for low-power, high-flexibility fields [1]. Second, **effect sizes shrink dramatically**: the RP:P halving [4] and the Many Labs 2 median collapse from *d* = 0.60 to *d* = 0.15 [9] are the winner's curse made visible. Third, **strength of original evidence predicts replication success** better than features of the replication teams [4] — a finding with a sharp moral: the information about credibility was in the original statistics all along, had anyone read them skeptically. Fourth, heterogeneity across settings is real but modest: in Many Labs 2, only one effect showed more than slight heterogeneity (τ > 0.20), and little was attributable to lab-versus-online administration or WEIRD versus non-WEIRD samples [9] — undermining the convenient excuse that "hidden moderators" explain every failure.

Critics countered. Gilbert, King, Pettigrew, and Wilson (2016) argued the RP:P contained statistical errors and that its data were consistent with *high* reproducibility — a dispute that itself illustrated how researcher degrees of freedom afflict even meta-science. Others noted that "failed replication" is ambiguous between "the effect does not exist" and "the effect exists but the replication was infelicitous" — the direct-versus-conceptual replication debate. These objections are addressed in Section 6.

## 6 Limitations

The crisis narrative, and each proposed cure, has genuine limitations.

**Limits of the evidence.** Replication "failure" is not falsification. A non-significant replication may reflect low fidelity, hidden moderators, or simply the original effect being smaller than assumed — absence of evidence is not evidence of absence. The RP:P's sampling (three journals, one publication year) and its operationalization of "replication" were contested [4]. Moreover, large-scale replications select *published, significant* findings — the most inflated stratum of the literature — so population-level false-discovery rates are likely lower than headline replication rates suggest.

**Limits of preregistration.** Preregistration constrains *confirmatory* analysis but cannot eliminate forking paths in data processing, cannot fix underpowered designs, and is awkward for exploratory, qualitative, or secondary-data research [6]. Worse, it can create a false sense of security: a preregistered analysis of a bad design is still a bad design. Compliance is also imperfect — vague preregistrations preserve flexibility while wearing the badge of rigor.

**Limits of multiverse analysis.** The multiverse replaces one arbitrary choice (which specification to report) with another (which specifications count as "reasonable") — and the boundaries of the multiverse are themselves a researcher degree of freedom [5].

**Limits of Bayesian reform.** Bayesian methods require priors, and priors are themselves degrees of freedom — arguably *more* flexible than α = 0.05, since they are harder for readers to audit. Bayes factors are sensitive to prior specification in ways that can reproduce the very fragility they were meant to cure. And none of the statistical reforms address the **incentive structure** — publish-or-perish, novelty premiums, and funding lotteries — that made the garden of forking paths worth wandering in the first place.

**The overcorrection risk.** There is a growing concern that the credibility revolution's rhetoric — "most findings are false" [1] — has eroded public trust in science beyond what the evidence warrants, and that hyper-skeptical norms (demanding preregistration for everything, treating exploration as suspect) may slow discovery. The crisis was real; the cure must be proportionate.

## 7 Conclusion

The replication crisis was empirical science's encounter with its own reflection. Ioannidis (2005) showed mathematically that low-power, high-flexibility, significance-filtered literatures *must* be largely false [1]. Simmons, Nelson, and Simonsohn (2011) showed mechanistically how easily false positives are manufactured without fraud [2]. Gelman and Loken (2014) showed that the problem runs deeper than intention, into the contingency of analysis on data itself [3]. The large-scale replications then measured the damage: roughly a third to two-thirds of headline findings surviving direct retest, with effect sizes collapsing toward — and sometimes across — zero [4, 9].

The credibility revolution is the response: preregistration to separate prediction from postdiction [6]; multiverse and specification-curve analysis to map robustness across the space of defensible analyses [5, 7]; Bayesian and regularized methods to replace dichotomous decisions with calibrated uncertainty [3]; and open data, open code, and registered reports to make the entire pipeline auditable. None of these is sufficient alone — each has the limitations catalogued in Section 6 — but jointly they represent a genuine reformation of statistical practice.

Science's credibility never resided in any single study's *p*-value; it resides in the *system's* capacity to detect and correct its own errors. The replication crisis was that capacity working as designed — painfully, publicly, and a decade late. The credibility revolution is the attempt to make it work faster, cheaper, and *before* publication rather than after. Whether it succeeds will be decided not by statisticians but by incentives: until the rewards of science favor being right over being first, the garden of forking paths will keep its gardeners.
