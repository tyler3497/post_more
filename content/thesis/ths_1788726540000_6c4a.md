# Secure Aggregation for Federated Learning: Mask-Based Protocols, Dropout Resilience, and Malicious-Server Threat Models

## Abstract

Federated learning keeps training data on client devices and shares only model updates, yet those updates themselves leak private information through gradient inversion and membership inference attacks. *Secure aggregation* closes this gap cryptographically: the server learns the *sum* of client updates and nothing else. This thesis develops the full protocol landscape, beginning with federated averaging as the insecure baseline and the Bonawitz et al. (CCS 2017) masking protocol — pairwise Diffie-Hellman-derived masks that cancel on summation, plus a self-mask and Shamir secret sharing of seeds for dropout recovery. We then analyze Turbo-Aggregate's multi-group circular construction, which breaks the quadratic aggregation barrier to achieve O(N log N) overhead with 50% dropout tolerance, and survey the malicious-server threat model: consistency attacks, selective aggregation, and Sybil injection, with mitigations from verifiable aggregation to distributed trust. Finally, we characterize the interplay between secure aggregation and differential privacy — orthogonal mechanisms whose composition yields the strongest practical guarantees. Protocol specifications, security arguments, complexity analyses, and empirical comparisons are presented throughout, grounded in the primary literature.

## 1 Introduction

Federated learning (FL), introduced by McMahan et al., trains shared models by averaging locally-computed updates from decentralized clients, cutting communication 10–100× versus synchronized SGD while keeping raw data on device [2]. But "data stays on device" is not a privacy guarantee: a long line of work shows that *gradients are data* — model updates can be inverted to reconstruct training samples, and membership inference can determine whether a particular record participated [5]. The honest-but-curious aggregation server, which follows the protocol faithfully while inspecting everything it sees, is therefore a realistic and potent adversary.

*Secure aggregation* (SecAgg) is the cryptographic answer: a protocol by which *n* clients contribute vectors **x**₁…**x**ₙ and the server learns only Σ**x**ᵢ, learning nothing about any individual **x**ᵢ beyond what the sum reveals [1]. The seminal Bonawitz et al. protocol (CCS 2017) made this practical for mobile fleets via pairwise masking; subsequent work — SecAgg+, Turbo-Aggregate, LightSecAgg, Flamingo — attacked its quadratic communication cost and its fragility under client dropout [3][4]. This thesis contributes:

1. **A unified specification** of mask-based secure aggregation from first principles, with the pairwise-mask cancellation argument and dropout-recovery construction made explicit.
2. **A complexity-theoretic comparison** of the protocol family, centered on Turbo-Aggregate's multi-group circular strategy and its O(N log N) barrier-breaking result [3].
3. **A threat-model taxonomy** extending beyond honest-but-curious to malicious servers, with concrete attacks and mitigations.
4. **A compositional analysis** of secure aggregation with differential privacy, showing how the two mechanisms address orthogonal leakage channels [7].

Section 2 sets the background; Section 3 describes methodology; Section 4 is the deep dive; Section 5 gives empirical and theoretical results; Section 6 discusses limitations; Section 7 concludes.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Federated averaging: the insecure baseline

In federated averaging (FedAvg), each round the server broadcasts the global model **w**ₜ; a subset of clients performs local SGD and returns updates Δᵢ = **w**ᵢ − **w**ₜ; the server computes **w**ₜ₊₁ = **w**ₜ + Σ(nᵢ/N)Δᵢ [2]. The protocol is correct and communication-efficient, but every Δᵢ is visible to the server in the clear. Gradient inversion attacks reconstruct recognizable images and text from these updates alone [5] — the motivating vulnerability for everything that follows.

### 2.2 Threat models

We distinguish three server behaviors:

- **Honest-but-curious (semi-honest):** follows the protocol exactly, but records all messages and attempts inference. The Bonawitz protocol targets this adversary [1].
- **Malicious (active):** deviates arbitrarily — sends inconsistent models to different clients, injects Sybil clients, aborts selectively. Requires verifiable or distributed-trust mitigations (Section 4.4).
- **Colluding subsets:** up to *T* clients may collude with the server, pooling their masks/seeds. Protocols parameterize privacy by the collusion threshold they tolerate.

### 2.3 Cryptographic building blocks

Three primitives recur: **Diffie-Hellman key agreement** (each client pair derives a shared seed without server mediation); **pseudorandom generators** expanding seeds into vector-valued masks; and **Shamir (*t*, *n*) secret sharing**, splitting each seed into shares such that any *t* reconstruct it but *t*−1 reveal nothing — the engine of dropout resilience.

## 3 Methodology

This thesis is a *protocol-analytic* study with three components:

1. **Formal specification.** We present each protocol in a common notation (clients *U*, vectors over a finite field 𝔽_q, pairwise seeds *s*ᵢⱼ, self-seeds *b*ᵢ), enabling direct complexity comparison.
2. **Security argument sketches.** For each construction we state the precise leakage claim (e.g., "the server's view is simulatable given only the sum and the dropout set") and identify the assumption it rests on (DDH, honest majority, threshold).
3. **Empirical grounding.** We report the published EC2 measurements of Turbo-Aggregate (up to 40× speedup over prior art at *N* = 200) and the complexity table of the protocol family, and we describe a reproducible simulation methodology for dropout sweeps [3][6].

All protocol claims are cross-referenced to the primary papers; no security claim is made that the cited literature does not support.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Bonawitz et al. Protocol: Pairwise Masks that Cancel

The CCS 2017 protocol [1] proceeds in four rounds. Setup: each client *i* generates a DH keypair and broadcasts its public key through the server. Then:

1. **Pairwise seed agreement.** Clients *i* < *j* compute a shared seed *s*ᵢⱼ = DH(*sk*ᵢ, *pk*ⱼ) = DH(*sk*ⱼ, *pk*ᵢ) and expand it via a PRG into a mask vector **p**ᵢⱼ ∈ 𝔽_q^d.
2. **Masked upload.** Client *i* additionally samples a self-mask **b**ᵢ (for dropout handling, below) and uploads
**y**ᵢ = **x**ᵢ + **b**ᵢ + Σ_{j<i} **p**ⱼᵢ − Σ_{j>i} **p**ᵢⱼ.
3. **Dropout declaration.** The server announces the survivor set *U*₁ (clients whose uploads arrived) and the dropout set *D*.
4. **Unmasking.** Surviving clients reveal secret shares enabling the server to reconstruct: (a) the self-masks **b**ᵢ of *dropped* clients, and (b) the pairwise seeds *s*ᵢⱼ for pairs where one endpoint dropped. The server computes Σ_{i∈U₁} **y**ᵢ, subtracts reconstructed masks of dropouts, and the pairwise masks of survivors cancel pairwise: each **p**ᵢⱼ was added by one endpoint and subtracted by the other. What remains is exactly Σ_{i∈U₁} **x**ᵢ.

> **Theorem: Mask cancellation (Bonawitz et al.).** *If all surviving clients follow the protocol and fewer than the secret-sharing threshold collude with the server, the server's view after unmasking is identically distributed to its view given only Σ_{i∈U₁} **x**ᵢ and the sets U₁, D. Individual **x**ᵢ are information-theoretically hidden beyond the sum.*

The elegant core is that privacy needs no heavyweight MPC: addition in a finite field plus pairwise-agreed randomness suffices, because the randomness is *structured to vanish* under summation. The cost is the protocol's famous quadratic: each of *N* clients performs *O(N)* key agreements, and the server routes *O(N²)* messages [4].

```python
# Masked upload, client i (finite field arithmetic mod q)
y = (x + self_mask) % q
for j in range(N):
    if j == i: continue
    p = prg(dh_shared_seed(i, j), dim)   # pairwise mask vector
    y = (y + p) % q if j < i else (y - p) % q
upload(y)  # server sees only y; masks cancel in the sum
```

### 4.2 Dropout Resilience via Secret Sharing

Mobile clients drop — batteries die, tunnels collapse. A naive masking scheme fails catastrophically here: if client *k* drops after others masked against it, its pairwise masks **p**ᵢₖ never cancel, corrupting the sum. The protocol's answer is to *share the seeds before they are needed* [1]:

- Each client Shamir-shares every pairwise seed *s*ᵢⱼ and its self-seed *b*ᵢ among all clients (threshold *t*).
- After the dropout set *D* is declared, survivors release shares for: self-seeds of *dropped* clients (to remove their **b**ₖ contributions) and pairwise seeds touching *dropped* clients (to remove uncancelled **p**ᵢₖ).
- Crucially, shares are *never* released for both a client's self-seed and its pairwise seeds simultaneously in a way that would unmask a survivor: the server must commit to the dropout set first, and the threshold *t* is chosen so that fewer than *t* colluding clients cannot reconstruct anything alone.

This yields robustness to dropouts at the price of two extra communication rounds and the *O(N²)* share distribution — the dominant cost the next generation of protocols attacks.

### 4.3 Turbo-Aggregate: Breaking the Quadratic Barrier

So et al. observed that the quadratic cost is structural to *all-pairs* masking, and proposed restructuring aggregation itself [3]. Turbo-Aggregate has three components:

1. **Multi-group circular aggregation.** The *N* users are randomly partitioned into *L* groups. Aggregation proceeds in stages: users in group ℓ aggregate the running sum passed from group ℓ−1 together with their own masked models, then pass the result to group ℓ+1. Each user interacts only with neighboring groups, cutting per-user communication from *O(N)* to *O(N/L + L)* — optimized to *O(log N)* per user, *O(N log N)* total.
2. **Additive secret sharing for privacy.** Instead of pairwise DH masks, each user additively shares its model with randomness structured to cancel upon aggregation — an information-theoretic (not merely computational) privacy guarantee against up to *T = N/2* colluding users.
3. **Lagrange-coded redundancy for dropouts.** The inter-group messages are encoded with Lagrange polynomials, injecting redundancy so the aggregate can be *decoded* despite up to 50% user dropout — dropout recovery via coding theory rather than interactive secret-share revelation.

> **Theorem: Turbo-Aggregate guarantees (So et al.).** *Turbo-Aggregate achieves secure aggregation overhead C = O(N log N) (versus O(N²)), tolerates dropout rates up to p = 0.5, and protects each user's update information-theoretically against any coalition of up to N/2 users [3].*

Experimentally, on Amazon EC2 with *N* = 200 users, total running time grows almost linearly in *N* with up to **40× speedup** over the prior state of the art [3]. The protocol family comparison:

| Protocol | Per-user comm. | Server work | Dropout tolerance | Privacy basis |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Bonawitz et al. (SecAgg) [1] | O(N + d) | O(N² + Nd) | threshold *t* | computational (DDH) |
| SecAgg+ | O(log N + d) | O(N log N) | threshold *t* | computational |
| Turbo-Aggregate [3] | O(log N + d) | O(N log N) | 50% | information-theoretic |
| LightSecAgg | O(d) | O(Nd) | threshold *t* | computational |
| One-shot (OPA) [4] | O(d), 1 round | O(Nd) | committee threshold | computational |

where *d* is model dimension and *N* the client count.

### 4.4 Malicious Servers: Attacks and Mitigations

The honest-but-curious model is optimistic. A *malicious* server can:

- **Run consistency attacks:** send different global models to different clients, then difference the aggregates to isolate a victim's update. Defense: clients gossip or verify model consistency via commitments before uploading.
- **Selectively aggregate:** claim a large cohort participated but aggregate only the victim's update (a "Sybil-augmented isolation" attack), learning it exactly. Defense: *threshold attestation* — clients require proof (e.g., a signed participant list or bulletin-board transcript) that at least *k* independent updates entered the sum.
- **Inject Sybils:** spawn fake clients whose masks it knows, shrinking the effective anonymity set. Defense: authenticated enrollment with rate-limited identities, plus distributed-trust variants (Flamingo-style decryptor committees) where no single server holds unmasking capability [4].
- **Deviate in unmasking:** request shares for survivors' seeds under false dropout claims. Defense: verifiable secret sharing and binding the dropout declaration to an auditable transcript — a single malicious client deviating from secret sharing can already deny reconstruction, and there is no in-protocol way to identify the attacker, so accountability must come from identifiable-abort extensions [5].

The trajectory of the field is clear: from trusting the server for correctness (Bonawitz), to verifying its behavior (verifiable aggregation), to removing the single trusted aggregator entirely (multi-server and committee designs) [4][7].

### 4.5 Differential Privacy Interplay

Secure aggregation and differential privacy (DP) are *orthogonal*: SecAgg hides individual updates from the server *during* aggregation; DP bounds what the *released aggregate* reveals about any individual *after* aggregation [7]. Their composition is strictly stronger than either alone:

- **Distributed DP:** clients add discrete Gaussian noise shares that sum to the target noise distribution; SecAgg ensures the server sees only the noisy sum, so no trusted curator is needed for central-DP guarantees.
- **Sensitivity control:** DP's noise scale depends on update norms, so clients clip updates to bound *C* before masking — clipping and masking commute cleanly since both operate in the finite field.
- **Privacy accounting:** Rényi DP composition tracks the (ε, δ) budget across rounds; SecAgg contributes no additional privacy loss because its leakage is exactly the sum, which DP already accounts for.

> **Theorem: Composition (informal).** *If each client's update is clipped to norm C, masked under a secure aggregation protocol leaking only the sum, and the sum is perturbed to satisfy (ε, δ)-DP, then the released model satisfies (ε, δ)-DP against any adversary observing all protocol transcripts — including a malicious server colluding with fewer than the masking threshold of clients.*

In practice, DP noise slightly degrades model utility while SecAgg costs only computation/communication — the standard deployment pairs them, accepting small accuracy loss for defense-in-depth [6][7].

## 5 Empirical Results and Theoretical Guarantees

### 5.1 Complexity-theoretic summary

The central theoretical result of the modern literature is the barrier break: secure aggregation *need not* cost Ω(N²). Turbo-Aggregate's O(N log N) construction with information-theoretic privacy and 50% dropout tolerance is the existence proof [3]; one-shot protocols (OPA) further reduce *round* complexity to a single client message per iteration at the cost of committee-sized cryptographic overhead scaling with model dimension [4].

### 5.2 Published measurements

So et al.'s EC2 evaluation remains the reference empirical anchor [3]:

| N (users) | SecAgg-family runtime | Turbo-Aggregate runtime | Speedup |
|---|---|---|---|
| 50 | ~baseline | ~baseline | ~4× |
| 100 | grows quadratically | near-linear | ~15× |
| 200 | — (prohibitive) | near-linear | up to 40× |

Dropout sweeps confirm graceful degradation: reconstruction succeeds through 50% random dropout, with runtime dominated by decoding (Lagrange interpolation) rather than communication at high dropout rates. Model-size sweeps show the expected linear scaling in *d*, and bandwidth sweeps confirm the protocol is compute-bound (coding operations) rather than network-bound above ~10 Mbps per client [3].

### 5.3 Reproducible evaluation methodology

For independent verification we specify a simulation harness: (a) *N* ∈ {50, 100, 200, 500} simulated clients with Dirichlet-partitioned non-IID data; (b) dropout injected as independent Bernoulli(*p*), *p* ∈ {0, 0.1, 0.3, 0.5}; (c) collusion simulated by pooling up to *T* seeds with the aggregator and testing sum-only leakage via a mutual-information probe; (d) metrics: wall-clock per round, bytes per client, reconstruction success rate, and final model accuracy versus plaintext FedAvg. The harness treats each protocol as a black-box aggregator behind a common interface, making the complexity table above directly falsifiable.

## 6 Limitations

**The sum itself leaks.** Secure aggregation hides individuals *behind the sum*, but the sum of a small cohort — or repeated sums across rounds with known participation patterns — still enables reconstruction. DP composition (Section 4.5) is the principled fix, at a utility cost.

**Quadratic setup lingers.** Even sub-quadratic protocols pay setup costs: key distribution, group partitioning via bias-resistant randomness, and committee election are non-trivial distributed protocols in their own right [3][4].

**Synchrony assumptions.** Dropout declarations, threshold reconstruction, and group staging all assume loose synchrony. Fully asynchronous, permissionless settings (blockchain-adjacent FL) remain largely open.

**Malicious clients.** A single client deviating from secret sharing can deny reconstruction with no in-protocol attribution [5]; Byzantine-robust *aggregation* (robust mean estimation) is a separate, complementary problem.

**Finite-field quantization.** Operating in 𝔽_q requires quantizing float updates, introducing quantization error that interacts with DP noise and clipping bias — a three-way tradeoff rarely analyzed jointly.

**Post-quantum exposure.** DH-based pairwise masking falls to quantum adversaries; lattice-based replacements exist but inflate the already-heavy per-client cost.

## 7 Conclusion

Secure aggregation transforms federated learning's privacy story from "trust the server with your gradients" to "the server provably learns only the sum." The Bonawitz protocol showed it could be practical with nothing more exotic than Diffie-Hellman and finite-field addition [1]; Turbo-Aggregate showed it could scale, breaking the quadratic barrier with multi-group circular aggregation and Lagrange-coded dropout recovery [3]; and the malicious-server literature is now dismantling the last trusted component — the aggregator itself. Composed with differential privacy, mask-based aggregation gives the strongest deployable guarantee in the field: per-round (ε, δ)-DP against adversaries who see everything except individual updates, because individual updates are never visible at all. The open problems — asynchrony, malicious-client accountability, post-quantum masking, and the quantization-DP-clipping trilemma — define the next decade of private distributed learning.
