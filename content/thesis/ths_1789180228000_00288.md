# High-Radix Interconnection Networks for Exascale Systems: Dragonfly and Dragonfly+ Topologies, Slim-Fly Variants, and Deadlock-Free Adaptive Routing

## Abstract

As exascale systems scale beyond hundreds of thousands of endpoints, the interconnection network has become the dominant determinant of application performance, acquisition cost, and power consumption. This thesis develops a comprehensive account of **high-radix interconnection networks**, tracing the evolution from fat-tree and flattened-butterfly designs to the *Dragonfly* topology of Kim, Dally, Scott, and Abts [1], its commercial successor *Dragonfly+* [8], and the diameter-two *Slim Fly* of Besta and Hoefler [2]. We formalize the technology-driven cost model that motivates high-radix routers, derive the radix and global-channel budgets of the Dragonfly group abstraction, and contrast its diameter-three routing profile against the near-Moore-bound degree-diameter efficiency of Slim Fly. We present adaptive routing — Valiant load balancing, Universal Globally-Adaptive Load-balanced (UGAL) routing, and progressive adaptive routing — and prove **deadlock freedom** through virtual-channel allocation grounded in the channel-dependency graph framework [7]. Empirical evidence from the Cray Aries Cascade system [5], Mellanox Dragonfly+ deployments [8], and the first real-world Slim Fly installation [3] quantifies the reported cost and latency advantages. We close with open problems: per-packet adaptivity at million-node scale, job-placement coupling, and topology-aware collectives.

---

## 1 Introduction

The interconnection network of a modern supercomputer is at once its nervous system and its most expensive single subsystem. In exascale machines the network accounts for a large fraction of acquisition cost, a dominant share of dynamic power dissipation, and — through tail latency — the effective scalability of tightly coupled applications. Because transistor scaling continues to increase router pin bandwidth faster than it reduces SerDes energy per bit, network architects have steadily migrated from *low-radix* routers (a few dozen ports) toward *high-radix* routers with hundreds of ports [1]. This migration is not cosmetic: it reshapes which topologies are cost-optimal.

A high-radix router concentrates a great deal of bandwidth in one device, which suggests building networks whose diameter is small — ideally two or three hops — so that packets traverse few switching stages. But high-radix networks have a catch: their channels must be longer. Because optical and even copper cables dominate network cost (often exceeding the cost of the routers themselves), the number of long *global* cables must be minimized to realize an efficient network [1]. This single observation is the technology-driven pivot of the Dragonfly topology: use a **group of high-radix routers as a virtual router** with an enormous effective radix, so that each minimally routed packet traverses at most **one global channel**.

The thesis that follows makes four contributions:

1. A formalization of the high-radix cost model and the Dragonfly group abstraction, including radix accounting and the reduction of global channels relative to flattened butterflies and folded Clos networks.
2. A comparative analysis of the Dragonfly, Dragonfly+, fat-tree, and Slim Fly families with respect to diameter, degree-diameter efficiency, bisection bandwidth, and physical realizability.
3. A rigorous exposition of adaptive routing — from Valiant randomization to UGAL congestion sensing and progressive adaptive routing — with deadlock-freedom proofs via virtual-channel allocation and escape channels.
4. A survey of empirical results from simulation and production deployments, identifying quantitative performance and cost outcomes and remaining research gaps.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Technology trends and the case for high radix

Let a router have radix $k$ (ports) and per-port bandwidth $b$. The *pin bandwidth* $k \cdot b$ of a chip grows with technology generation, and the marginal cost of an additional on-chip port is small relative to the fixed cost of the device, packaging, and SerDes. Consequently, as observed by Kim et al., it is increasingly attractive to spend the available pin bandwidth on **more ports rather than faster ports** [1]. A router with $k = 64$ ports at 25 Gb/s delivers the same aggregate bandwidth as one with $k = 16$ at 100 Gb/s, but the former enables networks with dramatically fewer switching stages.

> **Theorem (Degree–diameter trade-off).** *For a regular topology of maximum degree $\Delta$ and diameter $D$, the number of nodes $N$ is bounded by the Moore bound*
> $$
> N \le 1 + \Delta \sum_{i=0}^{D-1} (\Delta-1)^i \,,
> $$
> *so increasing router radix permits exponential reductions in diameter for fixed $N$.*

This bound underlies the entire high-radix program: doubling radix roughly squares the addressable system size at fixed diameter.

### 2.2 Fat-trees and the universal network

The *fat-tree* introduced by Leiserson is a rooted tree in which link capacity grows toward the root, eliminating the bisection bottleneck of ordinary trees [6]. Leiserson proved that fat-trees are **volume-universal**: a fat-tree of volume $V$ can simulate any other network of comparable volume with only polylogarithmic slowdown. Folded Clos networks — the multistage interconnection network (MIN) form of fat-trees — remain the dominant datacenter topology because they provide *full bisection bandwidth* and simple deterministic routing.

However, universal comes at a price. A non-blocking fat-tree of $N$ nodes requires $\Theta(N \log N)$ switch ports and $\Theta(N \log N)$ cables, with diameter $\Theta(\log N)$. For $N \ge 16{,}384$, Kim et al. showed the Dragonfly costs **52% less** than a folded Clos at equal bisection, precisely because the Clos must pay for $\log N$ stages of switches and the long cables between them [1].

### 2.3 Deadlock, virtual channels, and the channel-dependency graph

The foundational theory of routing correctness is due to Dally and Seitz and is canonically presented by Dally and Towles [7]. A *routing function* $R$ maps the current channel and destination to a set of admissible next channels. Let $C$ be the set of channels; the **channel-dependency graph** $G_R$ has vertex set $C$ and a directed edge $c_1 \to c_2$ iff $R$ may route a packet from $c_1$ to $c_2$.

> **Theorem (Dally–Seitz).** *A routing function $R$ is deadlock-free iff its channel-dependency graph $G_R$ is acyclic.*

**Virtual channels** (VCs) — logically separate buffers sharing one physical channel — are the primary instrument for enforcing acyclicity. By partitioning each physical channel into VCs $v_0, v_1, \dots$ and constraining packets to move between VC classes in a monotone order, cycles in $G_R$ are eliminated. An **escape channel** is a distinguished VC (typically $v_0$) whose routing sub-function is deadlock-free on its own; packets may descend into the escape set but never climb back, guaranteeing that at least one packet always makes progress.

## 3 Methodology

Our analysis follows three complementary lines of inquiry.

1. **Analytic modeling.** We derive radix budgets, group counts, diameter, and worst-case channel loads for the Dragonfly and Slim Fly families from their graph-theoretic definitions. Cost comparisons use the *cable-dominated* cost model of Kim et al., in which the number of global (long) cables is the first-order term [1].
2. **Routing theory.** We formulate minimal and non-minimal routing on Dragonfly as multi-stage path-selection problems, present the UGAL decision rule with its bias parameter and credit-round-trip-latency congestion signal, and prove deadlock freedom via VC-class assignment following Dally and Towles [7].
3. **Empirical synthesis.** We aggregate published simulation and deployment studies — the Cray Cascade (Aries) dragonfly installation [5], the Mellanox Dragonfly+ deployments (Niagara, Cygnus, CSC) [8], and the first physical Slim Fly cluster with its multipath routing architecture [3] — to quantify cost, latency, power, and throughput outcomes under realistic HPC workloads.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Dragonfly topology: groups as virtual routers

The Dragonfly organizes $N$ terminals into **groups**. A group contains $a$ routers fully connected by short *local* channels (an all-to-all intra-group topology). Each router has $p$ terminal ports, $a-1$ local ports, and $h$ *global* ports connecting to routers in other groups via long optical channels. The router radix is therefore

$$
k = p + (a - 1) + h\,,
$$

and a group contributes $a \cdot h$ global channels. With $g$ groups, the global channels form a complete graph $K_g$ between groups, so $g \le a h + 1$. Total terminals: $N = a \cdot p \cdot g$ [1].

**Minimal routing** on the Dragonfly proceeds in three steps: (i) route within the source group from the source router to the router holding the global link to the destination group (a local hop, $l$); (ii) traverse the single global channel ($g$); (iii) route within the destination group to the destination router (local hop, $l$). The minimal path is thus $l$–$g$–$l$: **at most three router traversals and exactly one global channel** — a diameter of three with the expensive resource used sparingly. Because each packet crosses only one long cable, Kim et al. report cost reductions of **20% versus a flattened butterfly** and **52% versus a folded Clos** for systems with $\ge 16$K nodes [1].

### 4.2 Dragonfly+: commercializing the idea at InfiniBand scale

**Dragonfly+** is the Mellanox/NVIDIA InfiniBand realization of the two-level dragonfly concept, deployed in production systems including the *Niagara* supercomputer at the University of Toronto (1,500 nodes, 60,000 cores, EDR InfiniBand) and the CSC and Tsukuba installations [8]. Whereas the original Dragonfly paper is a technology-driven proposal, Dragonfly+ solves the practical problems of building and expanding such systems: the topology permits **system expansion at zero cabling cost** — new leaf and spine switches can be added within racks while global (inter-group) connectivity and bisection bandwidth are preserved without recabling long cables, a property that fat-trees and Aries-style dragonflies cannot match without port reservation or rewiring [8].

The practical lesson of Dragonfly+ is that *expandability* and *in-network computing* (e.g., SHARP aggregation offload) are first-class design objectives alongside diameter and cost — a theme we return to in §6.

### 4.3 Slim Fly: approaching the Moore bound

Slim Fly, introduced by Besta and Hoefler, attacks the degree–diameter problem directly: it constructs router graphs that approximate the **Moore bound**, the theoretical maximum number of nodes for given degree and diameter [2]. Key properties:

- **Diameter two.** Any router reaches any other in at most two hops, versus three for Dragonfly and $2\log k$ for fat-trees.
- **~2× radix efficiency.** For a given router radix, Slim Fly connects nearly twice as many nodes as Dragonfly-derived designs because it wastes no ports on redundant structural symmetry.
- **Cost and power.** Construction cost and consumed power are reported as **25–30% lower** than Clos/fat-tree, with **~50% lower latency** [2].

The first real-world Slim Fly installation (200 servers, InfiniBand) with a topology-agnostic **multipath routing architecture** was demonstrated at NSDI '24 [3]: it achieved performance comparable to or better than non-blocking fat-trees at lower cost, validating that low-diameter networks are not merely theoretical optima but deployable systems.

| Topology | Diameter | Min. router radix for $N$ nodes | Relative cost | Full bisection? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Fat-tree (folded Clos) | $2\log_2 k$ | moderate | baseline (1.0×) | yes |
| Flattened butterfly | $\le 3$–$4$ | high | −20% vs Clos | near |
| Dragonfly | 3 ($l$–$g$–$l$) | high | −52% vs Clos @16K | partial |
| Dragonfly+ (IB) | 3 | high | −expandability gain | partial |

### 4.4 Adaptive routing: Valiant, UGAL, and progressive adaptivity

Minimal routing on the Dragonfly is fragile: adversarial traffic (e.g., every group sending to one other group) saturates the single global link between group pairs. The classical remedy is **Valiant's randomized routing**: send each packet first to a randomly chosen *intermediate group* $G_i$, then minimally to its destination. This transforms any traffic matrix into approximately uniform random traffic at the cost of up to **two global channels** and five steps ($l$–$g$–$l$–$g$–$l$) [1].

**UGAL** (Universal Globally-Adaptive Load-balanced routing, Singh 2005) makes this choice *adaptive* rather than oblivious. At the source, UGAL evaluates the congestion on the minimal path and on a Valiant non-minimal path, selecting

$$
\text{route} = \arg\min \, \big\{\, Q_{\min},\; Q_{\text{nm}} \,\big\},
$$

where $Q$ denotes the measured queue occupancy (or estimated delay), with a **bias parameter** $T$ favoring minimal routes:

```python
def ugal_route(src, dst, groups):
    """Universal Globally-Adaptive Load-balanced routing (Singh, 2005)."""
    q_min = queue_occupancy(minimal_path(src, dst))          # l-g-l path
    Gi = random.choice(groups)                              # Valiant intermediate
    q_nm = queue_occupancy(via_group(src, Gi, dst))          # l-g-l-g-l path
    # Bias toward minimal: non-minimal chosen only if much less congested
    if q_min <= q_nm * BIAS:                                # BIAS > 1, e.g. 2.0
        return minimal_path(src, dst)
    return via_group(src, Gi, dst)
```

Because the source router has only *indirect* knowledge of the global channels (it cannot directly observe the queue of a remote router's global port), Kim et al. introduced two congestion-sensing mechanisms: **selective virtual-channel discrimination** (using VC occupancy as a proxy for downstream congestion) and **credit round-trip latency** (measuring how long credits take to return, which grows with downstream buffering) [1]. Figure 4 (*ths_1789180228000_00288-3.webp*) diagrams the UGAL decision flow.

Subsequent work refined adaptivity further: **progressive adaptive routing (PAR)** and **virtual-channel-indirect (VCI)** schemes let each packet carry its own misrouting budget, choosing minimal or non-minimal hops *at each router* rather than only at the source, and are covered in the patent literature on dragonfly adaptive routing [9]. On-the-fly variants that re-evaluate at every hop have been shown to approach the ideal UGAL-G (global-knowledge) bound in simulation [4].

### 4.5 Deadlock-free virtual-channel allocation

Adaptive routing introduces *cycles* into the channel-dependency graph: a packet misrouted through intermediate group $G_i$ may later request a global channel that another misrouted packet holds, forming a dependency cycle. The standard cure, following Dally and Towles [7], assigns **VC classes by route phase**:

| Phase | Channels used | VC class |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Source group (local) | $l$ | VC0 |
| 2. First global channel | $g$ | VC1 |
| 3. Intermediate group (local) | $l$ | VC1 |
| 4. Second global channel | $g$ | VC2 |
| 5. Destination group (local) | $l$ | VC2 |

*Table 2: VC-class assignment for non-minimal dragonfly routing.*

> **Theorem (Dragonfly deadlock freedom).** *With the VC assignment of Table 2, every channel dependency advances from VC$_i$ to VC$_j$ with $j \ge i$, and every global-channel dependency strictly increases the VC index. Hence the channel-dependency graph is acyclic and routing is deadlock-free.*

*Proof sketch.* Local hops within a group can be ordered arbitrarily but never *decrease* the VC index; each global hop *strictly increases* it. Any directed cycle in $G_R$ would require a global hop that does not increase the index — a contradiction. Minimal routing needs only VC0/VC1 (two classes); non-minimal routing needs three [1]. For request–reply protocols (e.g., coherence traffic), the sets are doubled to prevent *protocol* deadlock — message-dependent deadlock is orthogonal to routing deadlock and must be handled separately [7].

## 5 Empirical Results and Theoretical Guarantees

**Simulation.** Cycle-accurate studies of dragonfly networks confirm the theory: minimal routing achieves near-ideal latency on uniform random traffic but collapses on adversarial patterns, while UGAL with credit-round-trip sensing recovers throughput within a few percent of the ideal global-knowledge adaptive router (UGAL-G) [1][4]. Notably, García et al. showed that on-the-fly adaptive routing *dominates* source-only UGAL under adversarial traffic with large intermediate groups, because local misrouting inside the intermediate group avoids the pathological $1/h$ local-link bottleneck [4].

**Production: Cray Cascade (Aries).** Bhatele and Kale instrumented dragonfly-based supercomputers and quantified network health and congestion under real workloads, confirming that UGAL-G-style adaptivity minimizes system-wide congestion and that job placement interacts strongly with routing policy [5]. The Cray XC series — a direct commercial descendant of the Dragonfly — deployed tens of thousands of nodes with the group hierarchy of §4.1.

**Production: Dragonfly+ (InfiniBand).** The Niagara system (1,500 nodes, EDR InfiniBand, Dragonfly+) demonstrated order-of-magnitude reductions in MPI *AllReduce* latency via in-network SHARP aggregation on the dragonfly+ fabric [8], showing that the topology composes well with collective offload.

**Production: Slim Fly.** The NSDI '24 deployment validated the 25–30% cost/power and ~50% latency advantages in a real 200-server cluster, with the multipath routing architecture proving portable across low-diameter topologies [3].

**Theoretical guarantees.** Slim Fly's near-Moore-bound construction yields the strongest asymptotic claim: for diameter $D=2$, it asymptotically maximizes the number of endpoints per router radix [2]. Dragonfly's guarantee is economic rather than extremal: at most one global hop per minimal packet, and hence minimal expenditure on long cables [1].

## 6 Limitations

1. **Adaptive routing complexity.** UGAL's indirect congestion sensing is heuristic: credit round-trip latency conflates queueing with link latency, and stale estimates cause oscillation. True global-knowledge routing is not implementable at scale, and the gap between practical UGAL and UGAL-G remains open for adversarial patterns [4].
2. **Job-placement coupling.** On dragonfly networks, throughput depends strongly on the mapping of MPI ranks to groups; randomized placement or topology-aware collectives are required to approach the theoretical bisection, and production schedulers rarely co-optimize [5].
3. **Partial bisection.** Dragonfly, Dragonfly+, and Slim Fly deliberately sacrifice full bisection bandwidth for cost. Workloads with sustained all-to-all traffic at injection rates near line rate can still saturate global links; only the folded Clos family guarantees non-blocking behavior [1][2].
4. **Expandability vs. optimality.** Dragonfly+ achieves zero-cost expansion but inherits diameter three; Slim Fly achieves diameter two but its irregular cabling complicates incremental growth [3][8].
5. **Protocol deadlock and collectives.** VC-class schemes address *routing* deadlock; request–reply and collective protocols require separate VC partitioning, doubling buffer cost [7].

## 7 Conclusion

High-radix interconnection networks represent the convergence of device physics and graph theory: as pin bandwidth grows, routers with hundreds of ports make small-diameter topologies both feasible and economical. The Dragonfly topology [1] translated this insight into a concrete, deployable design — groups of high-radix routers acting as virtual routers, one global hop per packet, and cost reductions exceeding 50% over folded Clos at scale — while UGAL adaptive routing with indirect congestion sensing solved the adversarial-traffic problem that minimal routing could not. Dragonfly+ [8] carried the idea into the InfiniBand ecosystem with expandability as a first-class objective, and Slim Fly [2][3] pushed the degree–diameter frontier to its theoretical limit with diameter-two networks validated in real deployments. Deadlock freedom throughout rests on the classical virtual-channel and channel-dependency-graph theory of Dally and Towles [7]. The remaining frontier is *co-design*: routing, job placement, and collective algorithms jointly optimized for million-node, AI-driven exascale workloads — a problem whose solution will define the next decade of interconnect architecture.
