# Processing-in-Memory at the End of the Memory Wall: Bank-Level Parallelism, Industrial DRAM-PIM Devices, and the Programming-Model Frontier of Near-Data Computation

## Abstract

Modern computing is overwhelmingly processor-centric: systems move terabytes from DRAM to the core, pay orders of magnitude more energy for data movement than for computation, and stall on bandwidth that no longer scales. Processing-in-memory (PIM) inverts this design, placing computation in or near the DRAM arrays so that workloads execute where their data already lives. This treatise develops the two canonical PIM families—*processing-using-memory*, which exploits the analog behavior of memory cells for massively parallel in-situ operations, and *processing-near-memory*, which integrates SIMD engines and general-purpose cores at the DRAM bank boundary—and grounds them in industrial reality: Samsung's HBM-PIM/FIMDRAM and SK hynix's GDDR6-AiM accelerator-in-memory products, plus the UPMEM DRAM Processing Unit architecture and its PrIM characterization [1][2]. We formalize the energy and bandwidth arguments, model bank-level parallelism as a lockstep command protocol, survey the programming-model and virtual-memory challenges that constitute the adoption frontier, and conclude that the shift from a processor-centric to a memory-centric infrastructure is the dominant architectural transition of the decade.

---

## 1. Introduction

The memory wall is not a metaphor; it is a measured, compounding divergence. Processor performance improved at roughly 60% per year in the 1980s and 1990s while DRAM latency improved at roughly 7% per year, and although the rate changed, the underlying economics never reversed: off-chip data movement now dominates both execution time and energy in data-intensive workloads [1]. A 64-bit DRAM read costs two to three orders of magnitude more energy than a 32-bit add on the core that consumes the data, so that for many machine-learning and analytics kernels the *von Neumann bottleneck* is less about latency than about the cost of feeding the machine.

Processing-in-memory responds with a disarmingly simple thesis: **stop moving data to computation, and start placing computation at the data**. The idea is not new—it descends from EXECUBE, Terasys, IRAM, DIVA, and the Active Pages lineage of the 1990s and 2000s—but three modern trends have re-armed it [1]:

1. **Applications became data-intensive.** Machine learning, graph analytics, genomics, and database scanning are dominated by memory bandwidth rather than ALU throughput.
2. **Memory technology changed.** 3D-stacked memory with logic layers (HBM), in-DRAM error correction, and the normalization of specialized DRAM standards made "intelligent" memory architecturally plausible.
3. **Vendors actually shipped it.** Samsung's HBM-PIM, SK hynix's GDDR6-AiM, and UPMEM's DPU-equipped DIMMs are commercial DRAM-PIM products, not proposals.

This treatise gives a PhD-level account of the field. Section 2 reviews the DRAM substrate that PIM exploits. Section 3 states the methodology: a two-family taxonomy plus the energy/bandwidth formalisms used throughout. Section 4 is the deep dive: DRAM internals, bank-level SIMD engines, general-purpose in-DRAM cores, and the programming stack. Section 5 presents empirical evidence from real silicon and formal protocol arguments. Section 6 is honest about limitations, and Section 7 concludes.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 The DRAM organization that matters

A DRAM chip is not a monolithic array. It is a hierarchy: *channel → rank → bank group → bank → subarray → row*, where a row activation dumps an entire page (typically 8 KB) into a row buffer of sense amplifiers. The internal bandwidth of a modern DRAM is enormous precisely because activations expose entire rows in parallel; the *pin* bandwidth visible to the host is a small, expensive fraction of it. Every PIM design in this treatise is an argument about which level of this hierarchy is the right place to inject computation.

### 2.2 The energy argument

The canonical way to quantify the memory-wall crisis is Horowitz's energy ladder. Representative figures, rounded to orders of magnitude, are shown below:

| Operation | Approximate energy |
|---|---|
| 32-bit FP16/FP32 multiply-add (near core) | ~1–10 pJ |
| 32 KB on-chip SRAM access | ~10s of pJ |
| Off-chip DRAM read (per 64-bit word) | ~1–3 nJ |
| Moving one bit off chip vs. on chip | ~100× more |

The exact numbers vary by process and design, but the ratio is the point: **data movement is the operation**, and it costs two orders of magnitude more than the arithmetic it feeds. Any architecture that eliminates the off-chip round trip for a memory-bound kernel reclaims that margin directly.

### 2.3 The bandwidth argument

Consider a matrix-vector product \(y = Ax + b\) with \(A \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}\). Arithmetic intensity is

$$AI = \frac{2mn}{8(mn + n + m)} \approx \frac{1}{4}\ \text{FLOP/byte (FP16)},$$

placing GEMV deep in the memory-bound region of any roofline. If the host pins deliver \(B_{ext}\) while the internal bank fabric can expose \(B_{int} \gg B_{ext}\) under concurrent activation, then a kernel that computes *inside* the memory can raise its roof from \(AI \cdot B_{ext}\) to \(AI \cdot B_{int}\). This is the entire bandwidth case for PIM in one inequality: **bank-level parallelism converts stranded internal bandwidth into throughput.**

## 3. Methodology

We organize the field using the taxonomy of the modern primer literature, which distinguishes two families [1]:

- **Processing-using-memory (PUM).** Exploits the *analog operational principles* of the memory itself—charge sharing on bitlines, row-buffer operations such as triple-row activation (RowClone, Ambit)—to perform massively parallel logic and bulk bitwise operations in situ, with essentially no added compute logic. The computation *is* the memory operation.
- **Processing-near-memory (PNM).** Places real compute logic *close* to the memory: in the logic layer of 3D-stacked DRAM, at the bank I/O boundary, or in the memory controller. All three industrial products studied here (HBM-PIM, GDDR6-AiM, UPMEM DPUs) are PNM designs.

Our analysis proceeds in three moves: (i) substrate mechanics—what the DRAM hierarchy actually exposes; (ii) device case studies from shipped silicon, characterized by published specifications and third-party measurement; and (iii) the cross-layer adoption stack—programming models, data placement, and coherence—treated as a research frontier rather than solved engineering. Wherever we state a protocol property, we accompany it with a machine-checkable invariant sketch in TLA+.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 The DRAM Substrate: Banks, Subarrays, and Stranded Bandwidth

A bank can have only one row open at a time, but *different banks are independent*. A DDR4/HBM device with 16–32 banks therefore has 16–32 independent row buffers—a fact the host memory controller normally exploits only one or two banks at a time, serializing the rest behind the command bus. The bank is thus the natural **unit of PIM parallelism**: attach one small compute engine per bank, issue the same command to all banks in lockstep, and the aggregate internal bandwidth scales with bank count rather than pin count.

Subarray-level parallelism goes one step further. Within a bank, independent subarrays can support concurrent row operations, which is what enables PUM techniques: copying a full row within a subarray (RowClone) costs a handful of activate/precharge cycles instead of a round trip through the host, and triple-row activation computes majority/AND/OR of three rows directly on the bitlines [1]. These primitives give bulk bitwise operations—bitmaps, encryption, database scans—throughputs that scale with the number of subarrays, not the memory bus.

> **Theorem:** *For a DRAM device with \(K\) banks each exposing internal row-buffer bandwidth \(b\), any workload whose working set fits in simultaneously-open rows and whose per-bank computation is data-parallel achieves throughput bounded by \(K \cdot b\) rather than the pin bandwidth \(B_{ext}\).*
>
> *Proof sketch.* Each bank's row buffer is an independent \(b\)-bandwidth port once its row is open. Lockstep command broadcast activates all \(K\) rows with one command-bus transaction; per-bank engines then drain their local ports concurrently with no pin traffic. Throughput is the sum of independent ports: \(K \cdot b\). ∎

The theorem's hypotheses are doing the real work—fitting the working set into open rows, and data-parallel per-bank work—and the rest of this section is about devices that engineer those hypotheses into silicon.

### 4.2 Bank-Level SIMD: HBM-PIM (FIMDRAM) and GDDR6-AiM

**Samsung HBM-PIM.** Presented at ISSCC 2021 under the internal name FIMDRAM and productized as Aquabolt-XL, Samsung's design embeds a *Programmable Computing Unit* (PCU) at the bank I/O boundary of an HBM2 die [3][4]. Each PCU runs at 300 MHz and implements a 16-wide SIMD FP16 engine; 32 PCUs per die are driven in lockstep by conventional DRAM commands, so the host memory controller needs no protocol changes—the PIM operations are encoded as standard-looking command sequences. The stack operates in two modes: *standard mode* (ordinary HBM2) and *FIM mode* (in-memory compute), selected by command. Samsung reports more than **2× system performance and over 70% energy reduction** on AI workloads relative to the same HBM2 without PIM, with four Aquabolt packages delivering about **1.2 TFLOPS** of in-memory compute [3]. A later demonstration swapped Aquabolt-XL into a standard Xilinx Alveo card with no card modifications, measuring 2.5× system performance and 62% lower energy [8]. The capacity cost is real: PCU-equipped dies hold 4 Gb versus 8 Gb, so 6 GB stacks mix four PCU dies with four plain dies—a concrete instance of the area-vs-capacity tradeoff that every PNM design pays.

**SK hynix GDDR6-AiM.** SK hynix took the same bank-parallel philosophy to GDDR6. Each AiM die integrates 16 processing units (one per bank region) with **512 GFLOPS** of BF16 MAC throughput and **512 GB/s** of internal bandwidth against only 32 GB/s of external pin bandwidth—a 16× internal/external ratio that is the device's entire value proposition [5][6]. The PUs also carry lookup-table-based activation units (sigmoid, tanh, GELU, ReLU, Leaky ReLU) so that a full transformer MLP block's non-linearities need not round-trip to the host. The ISSCC 2022 device paper reports a 1-ynm 1.25 V 8 Gb die sustaining 16 Gb/s/pin with 1 TFLOPS-class MAC throughput [5][6], and the AiMX accelerator card prototype packages 16 AiM devices with FPGAs for LLM inference, where the design is explicitly positioned as an *attention accelerator*—the GEMV-heavy decode phase of autoregressive generation being the textbook memory-bound kernel.

Both devices embody the same architectural pattern:

1. One small engine per bank (PCU or PU), wired at the bank I/O boundary.
2. Lockstep broadcast: the host issues one command; all banks execute concurrently.
3. Dual-mode operation: standard DRAM when PIM is not wanted, compute mode when it is.
4. No host-protocol redesign: adoption is a command-encoding problem, not a new standard.

### 4.3 General-Purpose In-DRAM Cores: The UPMEM Architecture

Where Samsung and SK hynix ship *fixed-function SIMD engines*, UPMEM ships *general-purpose cores*: each DRAM chip integrates DRAM Processing Units (DPUs)—in-order, multithreaded RISC cores sitting beside the DRAM arrays on a standard DDR4 DIMM [2][7]. The first-generation DPU exposes roughly 64 MB of MRAM (its DRAM main memory), 64 KB of WRAM scratchpad, and up to 24 hardware tasklets (threads), programmed in C with an SDK that handles host↔DPU transfers explicitly.

The SAFARI group's PrIM suite made UPMEM the first commercially available PIM architecture to be rigorously characterized: 16 memory-bound workloads (vector addition, GEMV, SpMV, select, unique, binary search, …) with CPU and GPU baselines, measured on 640-DPU and 2,556-DPU systems [2]. The findings are the empirical backbone of modern PIM claims:

- Workloads whose access patterns are *streaming and bank-partitionable* (GEMV, SpMV, database scans) show large speedups and energy wins over CPU baselines, because they convert directly into the \(K \cdot b\) regime of the theorem above.
- The programming model is explicit and SPMD-like: the programmer partitions data across DPUs, stages through WRAM, and orchestrates transfers—there is no transparent cache coherence with the host.
- Inter-DPU communication goes through the host; there is no direct DPU-to-DPU fabric. This is the architecture's sharpest limitation and the clearest lesson for future designs.

UPMEM therefore plays a different role than the vendor SIMD engines: it is the *existence proof* that general-purpose, C-programmable PIM can be manufactured on a commodity DRAM process and beaten into a real benchmark suite, and its characterization tells us exactly which workload shapes justify the memory-centric bet [2][7].

### 4.4 Programming Models, Data Placement, and the Virtual-Memory Problem

Hardware is the easy half. The adoption frontier is the stack above it, and it has four open problems [1]:

1. **Data placement.** PIM throughput is available only to data that already sits in the right bank/DPU. Optimal placement requires the compiler or runtime to reason about bank striping—an allocation problem the host OS memory manager was never designed to solve. A Rust sketch of the core transformation for bank-striped GEMV tiles:

```rust
/// Partition rows of A across `n_banks` PIM banks so each bank's
/// tile fits in simultaneously-open rows (cf. the Theorem in §4.1).
fn stripe_gemv_tiles(a_rows: usize, n_banks: usize, row_bytes: usize) -> Vec<(usize, usize)> {
    assert!(row_bytes <= 8192, "tile must fit one DRAM row");
    let tile = a_rows.div_ceil(n_banks);
    (0..n_banks)
        .map(|b| {
            let start = b * tile;
            let end = (start + tile).min(a_rows);
            (start, end) // rows [start, end) owned by bank b
        })
        .collect()
}
```

2. **Coherence and virtual memory.** If the host caches a line that a PIM engine also reads or writes, somebody must keep the two views consistent. Current commercial designs dodge this by making PIM regions *uncached and explicitly managed*—correct, but it abandons the transparent programming model that made caches successful.
3. **Offload granularity and cost modeling.** Not every kernel wins: compute-bound kernels gain nothing, and kernels with irregular cross-bank communication (graph traversals with poor partitioning) can lose. A compiler needs a cost model—arithmetic intensity, bank fit, transfer amortization—to decide what to offload, and no production compiler has one yet.
4. **Portability.** HBM-PIM's PCU ISA, AiM's PU command set, and UPMEM's DPU C dialect are three different targets. Without a portable intermediate representation for near-data kernels, every PIM program is a device driver.

## 5. Empirical Results and Formal Arguments

### 5.1 Measured results from real silicon

| System | Device | Reported result |
|---|---|---|
| Samsung HBM-PIM (Aquabolt-XL, ISSCC'21) | HBM2 + per-bank PCU | >2× system perf, >70% energy reduction on AI workloads [3] |
| Aquabolt-XL in Xilinx Alveo (Hot Chips 33) | Drop-in HBM2 replacement | 2.5× system perf, 62% energy reduction [8] |
| SK hynix GDDR6-AiM (ISSCC'22) | GDDR6 + 16 PUs/die | 512 GFLOPS, 512 GB/s internal vs 32 GB/s external [5][6] |
| UPMEM + PrIM suite (2,556 DPUs) | DDR4 DIMM + DPU cores | Large speedups on GEMV/SpMV/scan vs CPU; inter-DPU via host [2] |

The pattern across all four rows is identical: kernels with low arithmetic intensity and streaming access win; the win is proportional to the internal/external bandwidth ratio; energy follows data movement.

### 5.2 A formal protocol sketch: lockstep all-bank dispatch

The bank-parallel pattern of §4.2 rests on a protocol obligation: *a PIM command must reach every participating bank in the same command window, and no bank may interleave a standard-mode access with a PIM-mode command stream.* We state this as a TLA+ invariant over a minimal model:

```tla
---- MODULE AllBankPIM ----
EXTENDS Naturals, FiniteSets
CONSTANTS Banks, CmdBus
VARIABLES mode, issued, done

TypeOK ==
    /\ mode \in [Banks -> {"std", "pim"}]
    /\ issued \in SUBSET CmdBus

(* Safety: no bank executes a PIM command while in standard mode. *)
ModeSafety == \A b \in Banks :
    \A c \in issued : (c.op = "pim") => (mode[b] = "pim")

(* Liveness intent: lockstep broadcast eventually completes on all banks. *)
LockstepCompletes == \A b \in Banks : <>(done[b] = TRUE)

Init ==
    /\ mode = [b \in Banks |-> "std"]
    /\ issued = {}
    /\ done = [b \in Banks |-> FALSE]

EnterPIM ==
    /\ mode' = [b \in Banks |-> "pim"]
    /\ UNCHANGED <<issued, done>>

Broadcast(c) ==
    /\ c.op = "pim" => \A b \in Banks : mode[b] = "pim"
    /\ issued' = issued \cup {c}
    /\ UNCHANGED <<mode, done>>

Next == EnterPIM \/ \E c \in CmdBus : Broadcast(c)
Spec == Init /\ [][Next]_<<mode, issued, done>>
===============================
```

`ModeSafety` is the property that makes dual-mode devices (HBM-PIM's standard/FIM modes) composable with an unmodified host controller: the controller need only guarantee that the mode-switch command precedes the broadcast, which is exactly what Samsung's command-encoding approach implements [4]. Model-checking this spec for small bank counts is trivial; the research value is that it pins down the *contract* between the host memory controller and the PIM substrate—contracts of this kind are currently implicit in vendor documentation.

### 5.3 The energy theorem, quantified

> **Theorem (Data-movement dominance):** *Let \(E_c\) be the energy of one MAC and \(E_m\) the energy of fetching its operands off chip. For FP16 GEMV-class kernels on contemporary systems, \(E_m / E_c \gtrsim 100\). Any PIM design that eliminates the off-chip fetch for a fraction \(f\) of operands reduces kernel energy by a factor approaching \(1/(1-f)\) in the memory-bound limit.*
>
> *Proof sketch.* Total energy \(E = N(E_c + E_m)\). Eliminating fetches for fraction \(f\) gives \(E' = N(E_c + (1-f)E_m)\). As \(E_m/E_c \to \infty\), \(E/E' \to 1/(1-f)\). ∎

This is why vendor claims of "70% energy reduction" [3] and "80% reduction in data-movement power" [6] are mutually consistent and unsurprising: they are the same theorem with \(f \approx 0.7\)–\(0.8\).

## 6. Limitations

An honest account must state what PIM does not solve:

1. **Capacity and area tax.** PCUs and PUs consume die area; Samsung's PCU dies hold half the capacity of plain dies [4]. Every PNM design trades memory density for compute density, and the tradeoff only improves with 3D stacking if logic-layer area stays cheap.
2. **No inter-bank fabric.** UPMEM DPUs cannot talk to each other directly; reductions and shuffles serialize through the host [2]. Workloads needing fine-grained cross-bank communication are poor fits.
3. **Thermal and power delivery.** Activating all banks concurrently—the very source of the throughput theorem—draws peak power inside a package designed for staggered activation. Power-delivery network design for all-bank operation is an under-published constraint.
4. **The software cliff.** Explicit data placement, uncached PIM regions, and three mutually incompatible ISAs mean that PIM today is programmable only by experts. Until compilers acquire bank-aware cost models and a portable near-data IR, adoption will be confined to hand-tuned AI kernels.
5. **Security surface.** In-memory compute engines are new privileged actors on the memory bus; RowHammer-style disturbance and malicious PIM kernels are essentially unstudied threat models.

## 7. Conclusion

Processing-in-memory has crossed the line from proposal to product. The technical content of that crossing is narrow and precise: *bank-level parallelism converts stranded internal DRAM bandwidth into throughput for data-parallel, memory-bound kernels*, and three independent industrial designs—Samsung's HBM-PIM, SK hynix's GDDR6-AiM, and UPMEM's DPU DIMMs—implement exactly this pattern with measured 2×-class performance and 60–80% energy wins [2][3][5][8].

The remaining work is not in the DRAM array; it is in the stack. Data placement, coherence with host caches, compiler cost models for offload decisions, and a portable near-data programming interface constitute the research program that will decide whether PIM becomes infrastructure or remains a collection of impressive demos [1]. The deepest claim of the primer literature stands: the largest adoption challenge is the shift from a processor-centric to a memory-centric mindset—and once that shift is made, the energy and bandwidth economics make the outcome nearly inevitable [1].
