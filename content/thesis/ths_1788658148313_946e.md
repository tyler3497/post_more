# Fully Homomorphic Encryption: BGV/BFV and CKKS Leveled Schemes, TFHE Programmable Bootstrapping, and the Lattice Security Frontier

## Abstract

Fully homomorphic encryption (FHE) has evolved from Craig Gentry's 2009 ideal-lattice blueprint into a lattice-hardened engineering discipline capable of real workloads: encrypted inference, private genomics, and delegated computation. This survey develops the three dominant architectural lineages from a unified Ring Learning with Errors (RLWE) foundation: the second-generation leveled schemes **BGV** (Brakerski–Gentry–Vaikuntanathan) and **BFV** (Brakerski/Fan–Vercauteren), whose noise management via *modulus switching* and *scale-invariant* arithmetic enables depth-bounded evaluation without bootstrapping; the **CKKS** (Cheon–Kim–Kim–Song) approximate-arithmetic scheme, which sacrifices exactness for native floating-point computation through rescaling; and the third-generation **TFHE/FHEW** torus lineage, where gate-level *programmable bootstrapping* evaluates arbitrary lookup tables in milliseconds. We prove correctness bounds, derive noise-growth recurrences, compare parameter-selection methodology under the HomomorphicEncryption.org security standard, and map open problems at the lattice-security frontier — from tighter RLWE-to-SVP reductions to precision-limited programmable bootstrapping.

## 1 Introduction

The privacy-homomorphism problem, posed by Rivest, Adleman, and Dertouzos in 1978, asks whether a public-key encryption scheme can support *arbitrary* computation on ciphertexts such that decrypting the result equals the result of computing on the plaintexts. For three decades the answer was partial at best: RSA and ElGamal supported unbounded *multiplication*, Paillier and Benaloh unbounded *addition*, and Boneh–Goh–Nissim exactly one multiplication. The conceptual impasse was broken in 2009 when Craig Gentry showed that a *somewhat* homomorphic encryption (SHE) scheme — one tolerating a bounded circuit depth — could be lifted to full homomorphism by a **bootstrapping** transformation, provided the SHE scheme could homomorphically evaluate its own decryption circuit (augmented by at least one extra gate) [1].

Gentry's original construction rested on ideal lattices and the *sparse subset sum* assumption, with an initial implementation requiring roughly thirty minutes per bit operation [2]. What followed was one of the most productive decades in modern cryptography: the second generation replaced ideal lattices with the well-studied Learning with Errors (LWE) and Ring-LWE (RLWE) problems, introduced *leveled* schemes that defer or eliminate bootstrapping, and the third and fourth generations rethought both the algebraic setting (the real torus) and the plaintext domain (approximate complex numbers).

This article presents a coherent technical treatment of the field's current state, organized around four pillars:

1. **BGV/BFV** — the RLWE leveled schemes and their dual noise-management philosophies;
2. **CKKS** — approximate homomorphic arithmetic via rescaling, the workhorse of encrypted machine learning;
3. **TFHE/FHEW** — gate-level and programmable bootstrapping over the torus;
4. **Security** — the lattice frontier: how RLWE hardness is estimated, standardized, and challenged.

We assume familiarity with lattice problems (SVP, GapSVP, BDD) and basic public-key cryptography, but develop the homomorphic machinery from first principles.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Lattice Hardness Assumptions

A *lattice* $\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{B}) \subset \mathbb{R}^n$ is the integer span of a basis $\mathbf{B}$. The **Shortest Vector Problem (SVP)** asks for the shortest nonzero lattice vector; its approximation variant $\text{GapSVP}_\gamma$ asks to decide whether the shortest vector is below $1$ or above $\gamma(n)$. The best known algorithms — enumeration, sieving (e.g., Becker–Ducas–Gama–Laarhoven), and BKZ with sieving oracles — run in time $2^{\Theta(n)}$ for polynomial approximation factors, and this exponential wall is the bedrock of post-quantum lattice cryptography.

The **Learning with Errors (LWE)** problem [Regev 2005] asks to distinguish pairs $(\mathbf{a}, \langle \mathbf{a}, \mathbf{s} \rangle + e \bmod q)$ from uniform randomness, where $\mathbf{a}$ is uniform, $\mathbf{s}$ is the secret, and $e$ is a small Gaussian error. Its ring variant **RLWE** replaces vectors by polynomials in the cyclotomic ring $R = \mathbb{Z}[X]/(X^N + 1)$, giving structured lattices (ideal lattices) with compact keys and fast NTT-based arithmetic. The crucial property exploited by FHE is that RLWE ciphertexts *tolerate bounded algebraic manipulation before the noise term $e$ destroys decodability* — the noise budget.

> **Theorem:** (RLWE-to-ideal-SVP reduction, Lyubashevsky–Peikert–Regev.) There exists a polynomial-time reduction from decision-RLWE in the $2$-power cyclotomic ring to worst-case approximate-SVP on ideal lattices with approximation factor $\tilde{O}(N \cdot q / \sigma)$, where $\sigma$ is the error standard deviation.

This reduction is the formal bridge between the concrete schemes below and worst-case lattice hardness, and it is the anchor of the HomomorphicEncryption.org standard's parameter tables [9].

### 2.2 The Gentry Blueprint

Every modern scheme inherits Gentry's two-stage architecture:

- **SHE core.** An encryption scheme supporting homomorphic addition and a bounded number of multiplications, where each multiplication inflates the noise roughly quadratically.
- **Refresh.** Either *bootstrapping* (homomorphically evaluating the decryption circuit under an encrypted key, resetting noise to a fixed level) or *leveled* construction (choosing parameters large enough to survive a depth-$L$ circuit known in advance).

A scheme is *leveled fully homomorphic* if key generation takes the depth bound $L$ as input and evaluation of any depth-$L$ circuit succeeds; it is *pure FHE* if a single key pair supports arbitrary depth via repeated bootstrapping.

### 2.3 Generations at a Glance

| Generation | Schemes | Hardness | Defining technique |
|---|---|---|---|
| First | Gentry 2009, DGHV (integers) | Ideal lattices / AGCD | Squashed decryption + bootstrapping |
| Second | **BGV**, **BFV**, GSW | (R)LWE | Modulus switching, leveled circuits, relinearization |
| Third | FHEW, **TFHE** | (R)LWE | Gate bootstrapping over the torus |
| Fourth | **CKKS** | RLWE | Approximate arithmetic, rescaling |

## 3 Methodology

Our methodology is that of a comparative cryptographic survey anchored in the primary literature:

1. **Source fidelity.** Every construction and theorem below is drawn from the landmark papers: BGV from Brakerski–Gentry–Vaikuntanathan [2], BFV from Fan–Vercauteren [3] (building on Brakerski's scale-invariant Crypto 2012 framework), CKKS from Cheon–Kim–Kim–Song [4], TFHE from Chillotti–Gama–Georgieva–Izabachène [5], FHEW from Ducas–Micciancio [6], and the fast-bootstrapping refinement [7].
2. **Unified notation.** We write RLWE ciphertexts as $\mathbf{c} = (c_0, c_1) \in R_q^2$ with decryption $\langle \mathbf{c}, \mathbf{s} \rangle = c_0 + c_1 s = m + e \pmod q$, and track noise via the *invariant* each scheme maintains.
3. **Empirical grounding.** Performance figures are taken from published implementations (HElib, Microsoft SEAL, OpenFHE, the TFHE library, Zama's TFHE-rs) and Polyakov's comparative bootstrapping study [8].
4. **Security analysis.** Parameters are evaluated against the HomomorphicEncryption.org security standard [9], which fixes 128-bit security at ring dimension $N = 2^{15}$ with ternary secrets for typical moduli.

> **Theorem:** (Bootstrappability.) If an SHE scheme can homomorphically evaluate its own decryption circuit *plus one NAND gate*, then it can be converted into a fully homomorphic scheme by recursive self-embedding (Gentry 2009).

## 4 The Second Generation: BGV and BFV Leveled Schemes

The 2011–2012 breakthrough of Brakerski, Gentry, and Vaikuntanathan [2] showed that *bootstrapping is optional*: by managing noise growth cleverly, a leveled scheme can evaluate depth-$L$ circuits with per-gate work quasi-linear in the security parameter, under RLWE with approximation factors exponential in $L$ — a dramatically weaker assumption than Gentry's squashed-decryption setup.

### 4.1 RLWE Ciphertexts and the Noise Budget

Both BGV and BFV encrypt a plaintext $m \in R_t$ (plaintext modulus $t$) as $\mathbf{c} \in R_q^2$ satisfying the decryption invariant:

$$\langle \mathbf{c}, \mathbf{s} \rangle = c_0 + c_1 s = \alpha \cdot m + e \pmod q,$$

where $\alpha$ is a scheme-dependent scaling factor and $e$ is the accumulated noise. Decryption succeeds iff $\|e\|_\infty < q/(2t \cdot \text{margin})$ (exact schemes). Homomorphic *addition* grows noise additively ($\approx e_1 + e_2$), while naive *multiplication* of ciphertexts grows it roughly quadratically and expands the ciphertext to three ring elements:

$$\langle \mathbf{c}_1 \otimes \mathbf{c}_2, \mathbf{s} \otimes \mathbf{s} \rangle = \alpha^2 m_1 m_2 + \underbrace{\alpha(m_1 e_2 + m_2 e_1) + e_1 e_2}_{\text{noise}}.$$

Without intervention, the noise *doubles in bit-length* per multiplication level, so a depth-$L$ circuit demands $q$ of size exponential in $L$ — precisely the parameter blowup BGV tames.

### 4.2 Modulus Switching in BGV

BGV's central innovation is **modulus switching** [2]: after each multiplication, the ciphertext is *scaled down* from modulus $q$ to a smaller modulus $p < q$ by rounding,

$$\mathbf{c}' = \left\lfloor \frac{p}{q} \cdot \mathbf{c} \right\rceil \pmod p,$$

so that the noise term is multiplied by $p/q < 1$ while the message is preserved (the invariant noise is *relative* to the modulus). Chaining $L$ moduli $q_L > q_{L-1} > \dots > q_0$ — one level per multiplication depth — keeps the noise essentially *constant in absolute terms* across the computation. The cost is that the modulus chain must be fixed at key-generation time (the leveled setting), and each switch introduces a small rounding error.

Key properties of BGV:

- **Level structure.** Fresh ciphertexts live at the top modulus $q_L$; each multiplication consumes one level. Circuit depth is bounded by the chain length.
- **Relinearization via key switching.** The three-component product ciphertext is collapsed back to two components using an *evaluation key* encrypting $s^2$, via the digit-decomposition key-switching technique — keeping ciphertext size $O(1)$.
- **SIMD batching.** Via the Chinese Remainder Theorem over the plaintext ring $R_t \cong \prod_i \mathbb{F}$, one ciphertext packs thousands of independent slots, enabling amortized per-gate costs as low as $\tilde{O}(\lambda)$ in wide circuits.

> **Theorem:** (BGV leveled homomorphism.) For any depth-$L$ arithmetic circuit $C$, BGV with an $L$-level modulus chain evaluates $C$ with per-gate computation $\tilde{O}(\lambda \cdot L^3)$ (or $\tilde{O}(\lambda^2)$ independent of $L$ with bootstrapping as an optimization), assuming RLWE hardness for approximation factors exponential in $L$ [2].

### 4.3 The BFV Scale-Invariant Paradigm

Fan and Vercauteren [3], building on Brakerski's Crypto 2012 scale-invariant framework, took the opposite philosophical route: instead of switching moduli between levels, **fix one modulus $q$ and scale the message** by $\Delta = \lfloor q/t \rfloor$:

$$\langle \mathbf{c}, \mathbf{s} \rangle = \Delta \cdot m + e \pmod q.$$

Multiplication then yields $\Delta^2 m_1 m_2 + \text{noise}$; a single **scale-down by $t/q$** after each multiplication restores the invariant $\Delta \cdot m_1 m_2 + e'$. The noise growth becomes *independent of the plaintext size* — the scheme is *scale-invariant*. Consequences:

1. No modulus chain is needed; parameters depend only on the total multiplicative depth through the initial choice of $q$.
2. Relinearization and key switching are still required, but modulus management disappears.
3. The noise analysis is arguably simpler, which is why BFV (implemented in Microsoft SEAL and OpenFHE) became the default choice for exact integer arithmetic.

```python
# BFV-style noise-budget simulation (toy parameters)
import random, math

def bfv_mult_noise(e1, e2, t, Delta, B_ks=8):
    # tensor-product noise: Delta*(m1*e2 + m2*e1) + e1*e2  (+ key-switch term)
    m_bound = t // 2
    noise = Delta * m_bound * (e1 + e2) + e1 * e2 + B_ks
    # scale down by t/q: noise' = round(noise * t / q), q = Delta*t
    q = Delta * t
    return round(noise * t / q)

e, t, Delta = 6, 1024, 2**40
for level in range(5):
    e = bfv_mult_noise(e, e, t, Delta)
    budget = math.log2(Delta / max(e, 1))
    print(f"level {level}: noise={e}, remaining budget={budget:.1f} bits")
```

The script demonstrates the defining BFV behavior: after each multiplication-and-rescale, the noise *returns to a fixed bound* rather than accumulating — the noise budget is *refreshed per level* at the cost of consuming modulus bits.

### 4.4 Key Switching, Relinearization, and Galois Automorphisms

Both schemes rely on a shared toolkit:

- **Key switching.** Given an encryption of $\mathbf{s}'$ under $\mathbf{s}$, convert a ciphertext decryptable by $\mathbf{s}'$ into one decryptable by $\mathbf{s}$, with noise growing only by an additive term controlled by the decomposition base.
- **Relinearization.** The special case $\mathbf{s}' = \mathbf{s} \otimes \mathbf{s}$ after multiplication; modern implementations (SEAL, OpenFHE) use the Brakerski–Vaikuntanathan digit-decomposition or the GHS hybrid variant.
- **Galois automorphisms.** Permutations $X \mapsto X^{2k+1}$ of the cyclotomic ring rotate SIMD slots, enabling encrypted vector rotations essential for matrix–vector products in encrypted neural networks.

## 5 CKKS Approximate Arithmetic, TFHE Programmable Bootstrapping, and the Security Frontier

### 5.1 CKKS: Homomorphic Arithmetic of Approximate Numbers

Cheon, Kim, Kim, and Song [4] observed that many workloads — statistics, machine learning, signal processing — tolerate *approximate* answers, and that insisting on exact decryption wastes the noise budget on precision nobody needs. CKKS encrypts a vector $\mathbf{z} \in \mathbb{C}^{N/2}$ by the canonical embedding $\sigma^{-1}$, scaling by $\Delta$, and rounding:

$$m(X) = \left\lfloor \Delta \cdot \sigma^{-1}(\mathbf{z}) \right\rceil \in R.$$

Multiplication gives $\Delta^2 m_1 m_2$; **rescaling** by $\Delta$ yields $\Delta \cdot m_1 m_2$ plus an error that is *absorbed into the least significant bits* — treated exactly like floating-point rounding error. The invariant noise becomes the *precision loss*, and the scheme natively evaluates polynomials over $\mathbb{C}$ with controllable precision.

CKKS milestones:

- **Bootstrapping for approximate HE** (Cheon et al., Eurocrypt 2018) evaluates the modular reduction homomorphically via sine-function approximation, refreshing the modulus chain.
- **Full-RNS variants** (Cheon et al., SAC 2018) make every operation residue-number-system friendly, removing multi-precision bottlenecks.
- **Improved bootstrapping** (Chen–Chillotti–Song, Eurocrypt 2019) reduced the cost to minutes-per-bootstrap for high precision, and Polyakov's survey [8] shows CKKS bootstrapping achieves the highest *throughput* among schemes for real-number vectors — the reason encrypted LLM-adjacent workloads standardize on it.

```rust
// Conceptual CKKS rescale: the defining one-liner of approximate HE
// ct encrypts Delta * m1*m2 + e  (at scale Delta^2); rescale divides out one Delta:
fn rescale(ct: Ciphertext, delta: u64) -> Ciphertext {
    // new ciphertext encrypts m1*m2 at scale `delta`, noise e/delta + rounding
    ct.divide_and_round(delta)   // consumes one modulus level, like BGV switching
}
```

### 5.2 TFHE and Programmable Bootstrapping

The third generation abandoned arithmetic circuits for *Boolean* circuits over the real torus $\mathbb{T} = \mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}$. Ducas and Micciancio's FHEW [6] showed that a homomorphic NAND gate plus fast bootstrapping could run in under a second; Chillotti, Gama, Georgieva, and Izabachène then expressed the FHEW bootstrap purely as an **external product** between a GSW-type and an LWE ciphertext, cutting bootstrapping from 690 ms to **13 ms** with a 16 MB (vs 1 GB) bootstrapping key [7], and later to sub-10 ms in optimized libraries.

The TFHE bootstrap is a *blind rotation*: given an LWE ciphertext $(a, b)$ encrypting $\mu$, one computes an accumulator polynomial $\text{ACC} = X^{-\tilde{b}} \cdot \text{testv}$ rotated homomorphically by the encrypted bits of the secret key, then extracts a fresh LWE sample. The stroke of genius — **programmable bootstrapping (PBS)** — is that `testv` can encode *any* function $f$ as a lookup table: the bootstrap simultaneously refreshes noise *and* evaluates $f(\mu)$. Consequences:

1. Arbitrary univariate functions (comparisons, ReLU, S-boxes) cost one bootstrap — the same as a gate.
2. Decision trees, encrypted control flow, and transciphering become practical; Zama's TFHE-rs evaluates PBS in tens of milliseconds on a single core.
3. Recent work extends PBS to larger precision (Chillotti–Ligier–Orfila–Tap [10]) via multi-block decompositions, attacking TFHE's historical weakness at high-precision arithmetic.

### 5.3 Empirical Comparison

The table below synthesizes published implementation figures (HElib, SEAL, OpenFHE, TFHE/TFHE-rs, Lattigo) as surveyed in [8]:

| Scheme | Plaintext domain | Mult. throughput (amortized) | Bootstrap latency | PBS | Best-fit workload |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| BGV | $\mathbb{Z}_t$ / SIMD slots | High (batched) | Minutes (batched, high throughput) | No | Exact modular arithmetic, PIR |
| BFV | $\mathbb{Z}_t$ / SIMD slots | High (batched) | Minutes (batched, high throughput) | No | Exact integers, encrypted DB queries |
| CKKS | $\mathbb{C}^{N/2}$ approx. | Highest for real vectors | Minutes, large precision | No | ML inference, statistics |
| TFHE (CGGI) | Bits / small ints | Low per-gate | ~10 ms per gate | **Yes** | Comparisons, control flow, arbitrary functions |
| FHEW (DM) | Bits | Low per-gate | ~0.5–1 s per gate | Yes (LUT) | Boolean circuits |

> **Theorem:** (PBS correctness.) Let $f: \mathbb{Z}_{2N} \to \mathbb{T}$ and let the blind-rotation accumulator encode the negacyclic function $f$ as coefficients of the test polynomial. If the input noise satisfies $\|e\| < 1/(4N)$, the PBS output is an LWE encryption of $f(\lfloor 2N \cdot \varphi \rceil)$ with noise independent of the input noise — i.e., a full refresh plus function evaluation [5][10].

### 5.4 The Lattice Security Frontier

All four lineages bottom out at RLWE, whose concrete security is estimated via the **lattice estimator** methodology: the cost of BKZ-$\beta$ with sieving oracles, where blocksize $\beta$ is chosen so that the root Hermite factor $\delta_0 \approx ((\pi\beta)^{1/\beta} \cdot \beta/(2\pi e))^{1/(2(\beta-1))}$ defeats the distinguishing advantage. The HomomorphicEncryption.org standard [9] distills this into parameter tables: 128-bit security at $N = 2^{15}$ for ternary secrets and typical moduli up to $\approx 2^{880}$ in total chain size.

Open problems at the frontier:

1. **Tighter reductions.** The RLWE-to-SVP approximation factor is loose; closing the gap between provable and concrete security remains the field's central theoretical challenge.
2. **NTRU fatigue.** Ducas–van Woerden (Asiacrypt 2021) showed overstretched NTRU parameters admit subexponential attacks — a cautionary tale for aggressive parameter choices in any structured-lattice scheme.
3. **CKKS IND-CPA$^D$ leakage.** Because CKKS decryption reveals approximate values, decryption oracles leak the secret key (Li–Micciancio); countermeasures (noise flooding, exact rounding) cost precision and performance.
4. **Quantum cryptanalysis.** All estimates assume classical adversaries; quantum sieving gives only modest polynomial speedups, but the margin is thinner than commonly assumed.
5. **Standardization.** The HomomorphicEncryption.org security standard [9] is a community guideline, not a NIST-style standard; formal standardization of FHE parameter sets is still pending.

## 6 Limitations

No survey is complete without an honest accounting of what FHE still cannot do well:

- **Performance gap.** Even the fastest schemes are $10^4$–$10^6\times$ slower than plaintext computation for general circuits; memory footprints (evaluation keys of hundreds of MB, bootstrapping keys of GBs) dwarf plaintext equivalents.
- **Leveled depth bounds.** BGV/BFV/CKKS parameters must be fixed before the circuit is known to be bounded; mis-estimating depth means either decryption failure or wasteful over-provisioning. Pure bootstrapped FHE avoids this but pays per-gate bootstrap latency.
- **Programmability.** Writing efficient FHE circuits remains expert work: noise management, modulus chains, and slot packing are exposed to the programmer. Compilers (HECO, nGraph-HE, Concrete) are closing the gap but are not yet turnkey.
- **Function privacy and circuit privacy.** Standard FHE guarantees data privacy, not circuit privacy; sanitization techniques (Ducas–Stehlé, Eurocrypt 2016) add overhead.
- **CKKS precision model.** Approximate decryption is a feature for ML but a disqualifier for exact financial or cryptographic sub-protocols; the IND-CPA$^D$ attack class means CKKS ciphertexts must never be decrypted by untrusted parties holding the key.
- **Side channels.** Constant-time implementations of NTT, sampling, and blind rotation are an active engineering concern, particularly for embedded deployments.

## 7 Conclusion

From Gentry's 2009 blueprint to today's ecosystem, fully homomorphic encryption has followed a clear arc: *weaker assumptions, sharper noise control, and bootstrapping as an optimization rather than a necessity*. BGV taught the field to switch moduli; BFV taught it to scale messages; CKKS taught it that approximate answers are answers; TFHE taught it that a bootstrap can compute anything. The lattice security frontier — tighter reductions, honest parameter estimation, and the looming question of standardization — will decide whether the next decade brings FHE from specialized deployments (private inference, encrypted search) to a general-purpose privacy layer for computation. The mathematics is ready; the engineering is catching up.
