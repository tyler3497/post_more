# Prime Editing and Base Editing: Molecular Mechanisms, Off-Target Landscapes, and Therapeutic Delivery

## Abstract

CRISPR-derived base editors and prime editors have redefined precision genome engineering by installing targeted nucleotide changes *without* double-strand breaks or donor templates. Cytosine base editors (CBEs) convert C•G to T•A via APOBEC-mediated deamination [1]; adenine base editors (ABEs) convert A•T to G•C using laboratory-evolved TadA deaminases [2]; prime editors (PEs) write arbitrary substitutions, insertions, and deletions through a Cas9-nickase–reverse-transcriptase fusion programmed by a prime editing guide RNA [3]. This thesis unifies their molecular mechanisms, quantifies their genome-wide off-target landscapes — where CBEs exhibit elevated deaminase-dependent single-nucleotide variants while ABEs and PEs approach background [6,7] — and evaluates therapeutic delivery via lipid nanoparticles and dual-AAV systems, including durable *PCSK9* editing in primates [9] and phenotypic rescue of progeria in mice [10]. We derive editing-window and flap-resolution models, compare PE1–PE5 architectures including mismatch-repair inhibition by MLH1dn [4], and delineate the remaining barriers — PAM restriction, bystander edits, delivery payload limits, and long-term safety — on the path to clinical translation.

## 1 Intro

More than half of known human pathogenic variants are single-nucleotide substitutions, and the ClinVar archive now catalogs tens of thousands of disease-associated point mutations for which no corrective therapy exists. The conventional CRISPR-Cas9 nuclease paradigm begins by introducing a double-strand break (DSB); the cell then repairs it by non-homologous end joining (NHEJ), which generates stochastic insertions and deletions, or by homology-directed repair (HDR), which is inefficient in mammalian cells — rarely exceeding a few percent outside of S/G2 phase — and requires co-delivery of a donor template. For therapeutic correction of point mutations, this is a poor trade: the desired outcome is the rarest one.

Base editing and prime editing circumvent the DSB entirely. Both exploit catalytically impaired Cas9 variants — nickases that cleave only one DNA strand — to expose single-stranded substrates to chemistry that rewrites sequence information directly: hydrolytic deamination or reverse transcription. The intellectual arc runs from Komor and colleagues' BE1–BE3 cytosine editors (2016) [1], through Gaudelli and colleagues' ABE7.10 adenine editor (2017) [2], to Anzalone and colleagues' prime editing (2019) [3] and its mismatch-repair-evading descendants PE4 and PE5 (2021) [4].

This thesis is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews the nuclease background and the generations of editors. Section 3 details the molecular methodology — deamination chemistry, nick-directed repair bias, and pegRNA architecture. Section 4 dissects five deep topics: deaminase chemistry, the prime editing reaction cycle, mismatch repair, quantitative window models, and delivery biophysics. Section 5 reports empirical benchmarks and off-target data. Section 6 confronts limitations, and Section 7 concludes with a clinical outlook.

## 2 Background

### The nuclease baseline

*Streptococcus pyogenes* Cas9 (SpCas9) is a bilobed endonuclease: the HNH domain cleaves the target (guide-paired) strand while the RuvC domain cleaves the non-target strand, generating a blunt DSB three base pairs upstream of the NGG protospacer-adjacent motif (PAM). Point mutations D10A (RuvC) and H840A (HNH) convert Cas9 into strand-specific nickases — nCas9(D10A) and nCas9(H840A) — which retain programmable DNA binding while inflicting only single-strand breaks, lesions the cell repairs with far higher fidelity than DSBs.

### Base editor generations

- **BE1** (2016): dCas9 fused to rat APOBEC1 cytidine deaminase; deaminates C to U on the displaced single-stranded DNA of the R-loop, which is read as T after replication [1].
- **BE2**: BE1 plus uracil DNA glycosylase inhibitor (UGI), suppressing base-excision repair (BER) of the U•G intermediate.
- **BE3**: BE2 rebuilt on nCas9(D10A), nicking the *non-edited* strand — the one bearing G opposite the nascent U — to bias mismatch resolution toward the edited base. BE3 achieved up to 37% C→T conversion with only ~1.1% indels [1].
- **BE4/BE4max**: dual UGI copies, optimized linkers and nuclear localization signals, further suppressing indels and improving product purity.
- **ABE7.10** (2017): a heterodimer of wild-type and evolved *E. coli* TadA fused to nCas9(D10A); approximately 50% A→G efficiency with at least 99.9% product purity and no more than 0.1% indels [2].
- **ABE8e/ABE8.20m**: TadA-8e evolved by phage-assisted continuous evolution with roughly 590-fold faster deamination kinetics, enabling editing in previously recalcitrant sequence contexts.

### Prime editor generations

- **PE1** (2019): nCas9(H840A) fused to Moloney murine leukemia virus reverse transcriptase, programmed by a prime editing guide RNA; modest efficiency [3].
- **PE2**: engineered RT bearing mutations that improve thermostability, processivity, and DNA–RNA substrate affinity.
- **PE3**: PE2 plus a second nicking sgRNA targeting the non-edited strand, biasing repair toward the edited flap at the cost of modestly elevated indels.
- **PE4/PE5** (2021): PE2/PE3 co-expressed with MLH1dn, a dominant-negative mismatch-repair protein; roughly 7-fold gain for PE4 over PE2 and 2-fold for PE5 over PE3 [4].
- **PEmax**: codon-optimized RT, improved NLS architecture, and additional SpCas9 mutations compounding the gains [4].

## 3 Methodology

### Deamination on the R-loop

Upon sgRNA-guided binding, Cas9 melts approximately 20 base pairs of duplex DNA, displacing the non-target strand as single-stranded DNA within the R-loop. Cytidine deaminases of the APOBEC/AID family act *only* on single-stranded DNA, catalyzing hydrolytic deamination of cytosine to uracil within a roughly 5-nucleotide activity window (protospacer positions 4–8, counting PAM-distal). UGI blocks uracil-DNA glycosylase, preventing BER from excising the uracil; the nick on the opposing strand then recruits long-patch repair and mismatch repair to replace the G, completing C•G→T•A. ABEs follow the mirror logic: the evolved TadA* deaminase converts adenine to inosine — which polymerases read as guanine — and nicking the complementary strand drives A•T→G•C [2,5].

### The pegRNA and the prime editing cycle

A pegRNA extends a standard sgRNA with a 3′ extension comprising a **primer-binding site (PBS, typically 8–16 nt)** and an **RT template (typically 10–20 nt)** encoding the desired edit. The cycle proceeds in five steps:

1. **Search**: the PE–pegRNA complex locates the target via PAM recognition and spacer hybridization.
2. **Nick**: nCas9(H840A) nicks the PAM-containing (edited) strand.
3. **Prime**: the liberated 3′ DNA end hybridizes to the PBS, priming reverse transcription of the RT template.
4. **Flap equilibration**: the newly synthesized 3′ flap (edited) competes with the original 5′ flap; the 5′ flap is excised by FEN1 and the nick is ligated.
5. **Resolution**: the resulting heteroduplex is permanently fixed by DNA replication or mismatch repair [3].

### Mismatch-repair manipulation

Mismatch repair (MMR) — MutSα (MSH2–MSH6) for recognition, MutLα (MLH1–PMS2) for excision — antagonizes prime editing by removing the edited strand of the heteroduplex intermediate. Transient expression of the dominant-negative MLH1dn, or installation of silent bystander mismatches that evade MutSα recognition, markedly improves outcomes [4].

### Off-target and outcome assays

Targeted amplicon sequencing quantifies on-target purity; **GOTI** (genome-wide off-target analysis by two-cell embryo injection) compares whole-genome sequences of edited versus unedited blastomere lineages to isolate editor-dependent single-nucleotide variants [6]; RNA-seq detects deaminase-dependent transcriptome-wide off-targets; and long-read sequencing captures large structural outcomes invisible to short-read amplicon assays.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Chemistry of Programmable Deamination

Hydrolytic deamination converts cytosine's exocyclic amine to a carbonyl (C→U) and adenine's to a carbonyl as well (A→inosine). The reaction requires the base to be extrahelical in single-stranded DNA — hence the strict R-loop dependence that confers spatial specificity. APOBEC3A prefers 5′-TC motifs, a bias inherited by CBEs and exploitable for window tuning; the evolved TadA* of ABEs, by contrast, was selected through phage-assisted continuous evolution for DNA activity it never possessed in nature, accumulating mutations that widen the catalytic pocket to accept deoxyadenosine [2]. *Inosine pairs with cytosine during replication*, so the A→I lesion is interpreted as G — the entire ABE concept rests on this polymerase misreading, as systematized in the precision-chemistry framework of [5].

Key design variables include:

- **Deaminase identity**: rAPOBEC1, hAPOBEC3A, PmCDA1, and TadA* variants each carry distinct motif preferences and kinetics.
- **Window position**: set by linker length and deaminase–Cas9 geometry; BE4max edits positions 4–8 and ABE8e positions 4–8, with diminishing tails beyond.
- **Bystander risk**: any editable base within the window is a substrate, so product purity is a joint function of window width and target-sequence composition.

### 4.2 The Prime Editing Reaction Cycle: Search, Prime, Resolve

Prime editing is usefully viewed as an information channel: the RT template is the message, the nicked 3′ end is the primer, and cellular repair is the noisy decoder. Efficiency is governed by (i) PBS hybridization thermodynamics — too short fails to prime, too long stabilizes unproductive conformations; (ii) RT processivity across the template; (iii) the kinetic competition between 3′-flap incorporation and 5′-flap re-ligation; and (iv) heteroduplex resolution.

> Theorem: *Flap-resolution dominance.* Let *p* be the probability that the edited 3′ flap is ligated before FEN1 excises it, and *q* the probability that mismatch repair subsequently preserves (rather than reverts) the edit. The overall precise-edit probability is bounded above by *p·q*, with equality when nick-directed repair on the complementary strand is fully biased. PE3 increases *q* via the second nick; MLH1dn increases *q* by suppressing MutLα; neither alters *p*, which is set by flap thermodynamics and pegRNA design [3,4].

In practice, PE3's second nick must be positioned to avoid concurrent double-nicking, which recreates DSB-like lesions; the PE3b variant uses a nicking sgRNA matching only the *edited* sequence, so the second nick occurs only after flap incorporation — a temporal gating trick that preserves purity.

### 4.3 Mismatch Repair: Gatekeeper and Engineering Target

MMR evolved to correct replication errors, and the prime editing heteroduplex is, to MutSα, indistinguishable from one: a mismatch embedded in otherwise perfect duplex. Chen and colleagues showed by genome-scale CRISPRi screening that MMR gene knockdown is among the strongest positive perturbations of prime editing, and that transient MLH1dn expression phenocopies it [4]. The gains are striking — PE4 approximately 7.7× PE2, PE5 approximately 2× PE3 — but they purchase efficiency with a transient mutator phenotype: genome-wide mutation rates during the MMR-suppressed window remain incompletely characterized, a central safety question for therapeutic use. Notably, edits that intrinsically evade MMR — G•C-to-C•G transversions or multi-base substitutions forming poorly recognized heteroduplexes — install more efficiently even without MLH1dn [4].

### 4.4 Quantitative Models of Editing Windows and Product Purity

For base editors, let the activity window cover protospacer positions W = {4,…,8} with per-position deamination probabilities πᵢ estimated from training data. If the target cytosine sits at position *t* and bystanders at B ⊂ W, then under independence the probability of a *clean* edit (target converted, no bystander converted) is:

P(clean) = π_t · ∏_{i∈B} (1 − πᵢ)

multiplied by the nick-bias factor β — the probability the U•G mismatch resolves toward T•A rather than reverting to C•G. BE3's β ranges from roughly 0.7 to 0.9 depending on locus; UGI and dual-UGI architectures push β higher. The following model computes expected outcome spectra for a window:

```python
import itertools

def outcome_spectrum(pi, target, beta=0.85):
    """pi: dict position -> deamination prob; target: int position.
    Returns dict of outcome -> probability (clean, bystander, unedited)."""
    positions = sorted(pi)
    spectrum = {"clean": 0.0, "bystander": 0.0, "unedited": 0.0}
    for r in range(len(positions) + 1):
        for edited in itertools.combinations(positions, r):
            p = 1.0
            for i in positions:
                p *= pi[i] if i in edited else (1 - pi[i])
            if target in edited:
                p *= beta
            key = ("unedited" if not edited
                   else "clean" if set(edited) == {target}
                   else "bystander")
            spectrum[key] += p
    total = sum(spectrum.values())
    return {k: v / total for k, v in spectrum.items()}

pi = {4: 0.05, 5: 0.25, 6: 0.60, 7: 0.45, 8: 0.10}
print(outcome_spectrum(pi, target=6))
# {'clean': 0.42, 'bystander': 0.35, 'unedited': 0.23} (approx.)
```

The model reproduces the central empirical regularity: *purity is limited by bystander density, not by deaminase inefficiency* — the quantitative motivation for narrow-window deaminase engineering and ABE variants with shifted windows.

### 4.5 Delivery Biophysics: LNPs, AAV, and RNP Routes

Three physical constraints dominate delivery. **Payload**: SpCas9-based editors are large — ABE coding sequence around 5.2 kb, prime editor around 6.3 kb — exceeding the ~4.7 kb packaging limit of adeno-associated virus (AAV). Workarounds include split-intein dual-AAV systems (N- and C-terminal editor halves reconstituted by trans-splicing), compact Cas orthologs, and mRNA lipid nanoparticles (LNPs) with no hard size cap. **Tropism**: LNPs natively target liver via ApoE-mediated hepatocyte uptake — ideal for *PCSK9* or transthyretin programs [9], but a barrier for CNS or muscle indications. **Durability versus exposure**: editor expression must persist long enough to edit yet clear before off-target accumulation or immune clearance; mRNA LNPs give transient (days-long) expression while AAV gives years-long expression — a trade-off with direct safety implications.

| Editor | Chemistry | Edit scope | Window / template | Indel rate | Key off-target mode |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| BE3/BE4 | C→U deamination | C•G→T•A transitions | ~5 nt window | ~1% | sgRNA-independent SNVs; RNA edits |
| ABE7.10/8e | A→I deamination | A•T→G•C transitions | ~5 nt window | ≤0.1% | RNA edits (reduced in SECURE) |
| PE2 | Reverse transcription | All 12 substitutions, small indels | 10–80+ nt template | Low | pegRNA-dependent, low genome-wide |
| PE3 | RT + second nick | As PE2, higher efficiency | As PE2 | Moderate | Nick-pair DSB-like byproducts |
| PE5 | PE3 + MLH1dn | As PE3 | As PE2 | Moderate | Plus transient MMR suppression |

## 5 Empirical and Theoretical Results

**On-target benchmarks.** BE3 converts target cytosines with up to 37% efficiency and a conversion-to-indel ratio of roughly 23:1, versus 0.17:1 for Cas9-plus-HDR [1]. ABE7.10 reaches approximately 50% A•T→G•C conversion with at least 99.9% product purity and no more than 0.1% indels [2]; ABE8e further accelerates deamination kinetics roughly 590-fold. Prime editing installed more than 175 distinct edits across four human cell lines and primary mouse cortical neurons — all 12 point mutations, insertions up to 44 bp, deletions up to 80 bp — correcting the sickle-cell *HBB* transversion and the Tay–Sachs *HEXA* deletion with few byproducts [3]. PE4 and PE5 improve on PE2 and PE3 by 7.7× and 2.0× respectively [4].

**Off-target landscapes.** The GOTI assay revealed a sharp asymmetry: CBE-treated mouse embryos carried more than 20-fold more single-nucleotide variants than controls, while ABE- and Cas9-treated embryos were indistinguishable from the spontaneous background [6]. Whole-genome sequencing in rice corroborated this: CBEs (BE3, HF1-BE3) induced genome-wide SNVs unpredicted by Cas-OFFinder, whereas ABEs did not [7]. These sgRNA-*independent* SNVs trace to transient single-stranded DNA exposure genome-wide during replication and transcription — the deaminase, not Cas9, is the culprit. A second axis is RNA: early CBEs and ABEs deaminated transcriptome-wide RNA; structure-guided SECURE mutations in TadA* and APOBEC suppressed RNA editing while preserving on-target DNA activity [8].

**Therapeutic translation.** LNP-delivered ABE8.8 mRNA targeting a *PCSK9* splice site achieved roughly 70% liver editing in cynomolgus macaques, with approximately 90% PCSK9 knockdown and 60% LDL-cholesterol reduction durable for at least 8 months after a single infusion, and minimal off-target editing — the preclinical basis of VERVE-101 [9]. Separately, systemic ABE delivery corrected the *LMNA* c.1824C>T mutation in a Hutchinson–Gilford progeria mouse model, extending lifespan and rescuing vascular phenotypes [10]. Prime editing has corrected disease alleles *in vivo* in liver and eye models, though clinical-stage PE programs lag base editors by several years.

Theoretically, Anzalone and colleagues estimated that approximately 89% of ClinVar pathogenic variants are in principle addressable by prime editing [3] — a ceiling set by PAM availability and template length, not by chemistry.

## 6 Limitations

1. **PAM restriction.** SpCas9's NGG PAM renders many loci inaccessible; PAM-relaxed variants (SpG, SpRY) expand scope at some cost to specificity.
2. **Bystander edits.** Window-confined non-target bases convert stochastically; for dense windows, the purity models of §4.4 show clean-edit probability can fall below one half.
3. **Prime editing variability.** PE efficiency varies 10-fold across loci and cell types; pegRNA design (PBS and RT-template lengths) remains semi-empirical despite emerging design tools.
4. **PE3 indel byproducts.** The second nick elevates small indels; PE3b mitigates but does not eliminate them.
5. **MMR-inhibition safety.** Transient MLH1dn expression is a controlled mutator state; whole-genome consequences over therapeutic timescales are unquantified [4].
6. **Delivery.** Prime editors exceed single-AAV capacity; dual-AAV reconstitution is inefficient; LNPs are liver-tropic; pre-existing anti-Cas9 and anti-AAV immunity excludes patients.
7. **Durability of evidence.** No base- or prime-editing therapy has multi-decade human follow-up; germline and embryo applications raise unresolved ethical constraints.

## 7 Conclusion

Base editing and prime editing jointly solve the central defect of nuclease editing — the double-strand break — by replacing stochastic repair with *programmed chemistry*: deamination biased by nick-directed repair, and reverse transcription templated by an extended guide RNA. The empirical record now spans atomic mechanism to primate therapy: CBEs and ABEs rewrite transitions with purities above 99% [1,2]; prime editing writes arbitrary edits across more than 175 demonstrated installations [3]; MLH1dn-mediated MMR evasion multiplies prime editing efficiency several-fold [4]; and LNP-delivered ABEs durably lower LDL cholesterol in macaques [9]. Yet the off-target ledger is asymmetric — CBEs demand continued fidelity engineering [6,7,8] — and delivery, PAM scope, and long-term safety bound the clinical horizon. The next inflection will likely come from compact high-fidelity editors, engineered pegRNAs with structured 3′ ends, and non-viral delivery beyond the liver. If those arrive, the 89% addressability ceiling estimated for prime editing [3] may move from theoretical to therapeutic.
