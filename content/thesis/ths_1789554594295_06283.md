# Rough Volatility: From the Heston Model's Affine Riccati to Fractional Dynamics and Joint SPX–VIX Calibration

## Abstract

Since Heston's 1993 model gave stochastic volatility a closed-form characteristic function, the tension between analytical tractability and empirical fidelity has driven volatility modeling. The decisive empirical break came when Gatheral, Jaisson and Rosenbaum showed that log-volatility behaves as fractional Brownian motion with Hurst exponent near 0.1 — far rougher than Brownian motion — across equities, indices and rates futures [1]. This thesis traces the resulting research program: the Heston model's affine structure and its large-time SVI limit [7]; the rough fractional stochastic volatility and rough Bergomi models that reproduce the observed power-law at-the-money skew [3][4]; the rough Heston model of El Euch and Rosenbaum, whose fractional Riccati equation restores affine pricing in a Volterra setting [2]; the no-arbitrage geometry of implied volatility surfaces via the SVI and SSVI parameterizations [8][9]; forward variance, VIX futures and VIX options; and the joint SPX/VIX calibration problem, recently resolved by the quadratic rough Heston model through the combination of roughness and the Zumbach price-feedback effect [5][6].

---

##1 Intro

Stochastic volatility models exist to answer a simple market fact: Black–Scholes implied volatilities are not constant. They smile across strikes and decay with maturity, and the at-the-money (ATM) skew of equity index options explodes as maturity shrinks — far faster than any Markovian diffusion with reasonable parameters can generate [4]. For two decades the industry compromise was the Heston model [7]: analytically tractable through its affine structure, fast to calibrate through its characteristic function, yet systematically too flat at short maturities. The *rough volatility* revolution changed the terms of this compromise by changing the regularity of the volatility driver itself.

The empirical catalyst was Gatheral, Jaisson and Rosenbaum's 2014 analysis of high-frequency realized-volatility series [1]. Estimating power variations of log-volatility at multiple sampling scales, they found the scaling law

> **Empirical scaling law.** *For a wide range of assets, the q-th moment of log-volatility increments satisfies log E[|log σ_{t+Δ} − log σ_t|^q] ≈ ζ_q + qH log Δ with H ≈ 0.1, consistently across q.*

The fit is strikingly linear in log Δ over three decades of timescales, and the inferred Hurst exponent H lies between 0.08 and 0.14 regardless of asset class — S&P 500, NASDAQ, DAX futures and German government bond futures alike [1]. Log-volatility is *rough*: its Hölder regularity is an order of magnitude below the Brownian value H = 1/2. This thesis develops the full modeling consequences of that finding.

The chapter proceeds as follows. Section 2 recalls the Heston model's affine structure and its elegant large-time connection to the SVI parameterization [7]. Section 3 introduces fractional Brownian motion and the rough volatility model zoo: rough fractional stochastic volatility (RFSV), the rough Bergomi model, and the rough Heston model with its fractional Riccati equation [2][3][4]. Section 4 deep-dives into five themes: affine structure and Volterra processes, the rough Bergomi model and the ATM skew power law, no-arbitrage constraints on implied volatility surfaces [8][9], forward variance and VIX derivatives, and the joint SPX/VIX calibration problem with the quadratic rough Heston model [5][6]. Section 5 states the central asymptotic theorem with a proof sketch and reports numerical evidence. Sections 6 and 7 discuss limitations and conclude.

##2 Background

### The Heston model and affine structure

Under a risk-neutral measure, the Heston model specifies the spot price S_t and its instantaneous variance V_t by [7]

```
dS_t = S_t sqrt(V_t) dW_t

dV_t = κ (θ − V_t) dt + ν sqrt(V_t) dB_t ,   d<W,B>_t = ρ dt
```

with mean-reversion speed κ > 0, long-run variance θ > 0, volatility of volatility ν > 0 and spot–volatility correlation ρ in the open unit interval. The model's fame rests on a single property: it is *affine*. The log-price X_t = log(S_t / S_0) has a characteristic function of exponential-affine form

> **Affine form.** *E[exp(iuX_T)] = exp(C(T, u) + D(T, u) V_0 + iu log S_0), where the Riccati functions C and D solve the ordinary differential system ∂_t D = (1/2)u(i+u) + (iρνu − κ)D + (ν²/2)D² with D(0,u) = 0.*

Because of this structure, European option prices follow from a single Fourier inversion (the celebrated Heston–Lewis integral), making calibration to hundreds of quotes a matter of seconds [7]. The square-root diffusion for V_t is the unique affine diffusion that stays non-negative, provided the Feller condition 2κθ ≥ ν² holds; it is the continuous-time analogue of the CIR short-rate model.

### SVI and the large-time Heston smile

Gatheral's *stochastic volatility inspired* (SVI) parameterization fits each implied-volatility slice through total implied variance w(k, T) = σ_BS²(k, T) T as [8]

```
w(k) = a + b ( ρ (k − m) + sqrt( (k − m)² + σ² ) )
```

with five parameters a, b, ρ, m, σ. Gatheral and Jacquier proved a remarkable identity: the large-maturity Heston smile converges algebraically to an SVI smile, giving each SVI parameter a direct Heston interpretation [7]. SVI respects Lee's moment formula at extreme strikes — total variance grows at most linearly in |k| — and its five parameters fit listed smiles with high fidelity [8].

### The roughness finding

The Comte–Renault fractional volatility model of 1998 had already explored fractional drivers, but with long-memory Hurst exponents H > 1/2 [11]. The 2014 finding inverted this: volatility exhibits *anti-persistent*, rough behavior with H well below 1/2. Alòs, León and Vives had shown analytically that rough drivers generate precisely the short-term skew explosion that markets display, while long-memory drivers do not [11]. The empirical and theoretical arrows thus pointed in the same direction — toward H in the neighborhood of 0.1.

##3 Methodology

### Fractional Brownian motion

A fractional Brownian motion (fBm) B^H with Hurst exponent H in the open unit interval is the centered Gaussian process with covariance E[B^H_t B^H_s] = (1/2)(|t|^{2H} + |s|^{2H} − |t − s|^{2H}) [1]. Its paths are Hölder-continuous of any order below H and nowhere differentiable; the quadratic variation vanishes for H > 1/2 and explodes for H < 1/2, so for H < 1/2 the process is not a semimartingale. Two representations matter for modeling:

1. **Mandelbrot–van Ness** (two-sided, stationary increments): B^H_t = C_H (∫_{−∞}^t ((t−s)^{H−1/2} − (−s)_+^{H−1/2}) dW_s).
2. **Riemann–Liouville** (Volterra, adapted to the natural filtration): W^H_t = sqrt(2H) ∫_0^t (t−s)^{H−1/2} dW_s.

### The rough volatility model zoo

**RFSV.** The rough fractional stochastic volatility model of Gatheral, Jaisson and Rosenbaum sets σ_t = exp(X_t) with X_t a fractional Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process, dX_t = ν dB^H_t − α(X_t − m) dt, with mean-reversion α taken tiny so that roughness dominates at all practical scales [1]. It is the model of the empirical finding itself — descriptive rather than directly calibrated to options.

**Rough Bergomi.** Bayer, Friz and Gatheral proposed the parsimonious pricing model [3][4]:

```
ξ_t(u) = ξ_0(u) * exp( 2 η_c ∫_0^t (u−s)^α dW_s − η_c² ∫_0^t (u−s)^{2α} ds )

d log S_t = −(1/2) V_t dt + sqrt(V_t) dW^S_t ,   V_t = ξ_t(t)
```

where α = H − 1/2 in the range (−1/2, 0), ξ_0 is the initial forward variance curve, η the volatility-of-volatility, ρ the correlation between W and W^S, and E denotes the stochastic exponential. With only three genuine parameters (ξ_0 curve, η, ρ, H) it reproduces the entire SPX skew term structure, including the power-law ATM skew [4].

**Rough Heston.** El Euch and Rosenbaum's model keeps the Heston variance dynamics but replaces the Brownian time integral by a Volterra integral with power-law kernel [2]:

```
V_t = V_0 + Γ(α)^{−1} ∫_0^t (t−s)^{α−1} λ (θ − V_s) ds
          + Γ(α)^{−1} ∫_0^t (t−s)^{α−1} ν sqrt(V_s) dB_s
```

with α = H + 1/2 in the open interval (1/2, 1). The breakthrough is that the characteristic function retains exponential-affine form, with the Riccati ODE replaced by a *fractional* Riccati equation D^α ψ = R(u, ψ) [2]. This embeds rough Heston in the class of *affine Volterra processes* studied by Abi Jaber, Larsson and Pulido, restoring Fourier pricing to rough models.

Weak error rates for Euler discretizations of rough models have been analyzed by Bayer, Hall and Tempone, who prove rate H + 1/2 for the rough Stein–Stein model [10].

##4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Affine structure: from Riccati to fractional Riccati

The classical affine property — that the log-characteristic function is affine in the state — survives the passage to Volterra dynamics. In the rough Heston model, El Euch and Rosenbaum derived [2]

> **Theorem (El Euch–Rosenbaum).** *The moment generating function of X_t = log(S_t/S_0) in the rough Heston model admits E[exp(uX_t)] = exp( θλ I^1_t ψ(u,·) + V_0 I^{1−α}_t ψ(u,·) ), where I^β denotes the Riemann–Liouville fractional integral and ψ solves the fractional Riccati problem D^α_t ψ(u,t) = (1/2)u(u−1) + (ρνu − λ)ψ + (ν²/2)ψ² with I^{1−α}_t ψ(u, 0) = 0.*

Here α = H + 1/2, so as H ↓ 0 the fractional derivative approaches order 1/2 and the memory kernel becomes maximally singular [2]. The fractional Adams method solves this equation numerically; combined with Fourier inversion it yields option prices in milliseconds for moderate maturities, though short-maturity far-out-of-the-money options remain numerically delicate and demand careful contour choices [11]. The affine Volterra framework extends this to general Volterra Heston-type models, proving existence, uniqueness and the affine transform formula under explicit kernel conditions.

### 4.2 The rough Bergomi model and the ATM skew power law

The ATM volatility skew Ψ(T) = |∂_k σ_BS(k, T)|_{k=0} is the diagnostic that killed classical stochastic volatility. In equity markets it decays as a power law T^{H−1/2} with H ≈ 0.1, i.e. roughly T^{−0.4} [1][4]. The rough Bergomi model generates exactly this term structure endogenously: Fukasawa's martingale expansion and the Bergomi–Guyon vol-of-vol expansion show that the short-maturity skew is proportional to the Malliavin derivative of volatility, which for the fractional kernel explodes like T^{H−1/2} [4][11].

| Model | Markovian | Affine | # params | Short-term skew |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Heston | yes | yes (Riccati ODE) | 5 | O(T^{−1/2}) up to constants — too flat |
| Rough Heston | no | yes (fractional Riccati) | 6 | O(T^{H−1/2}) — matches market |
| Rough Bergomi | no | no (Monte Carlo) | 4 (incl. curve) | O(T^{H−1/2}) — matches market |
| Quadratic rough Heston | no | no | 6 | O(T^{H−1/2}) + Zumbach feedback |

### 4.3 No-arbitrage constraints on implied volatility surfaces

A model that fits option prices but admits arbitrage is worthless, so the geometry of arbitrage-free implied volatility surfaces is a subject in its own right [8][9]. Static arbitrage comes in two flavors:

1. **Calendar-spread arbitrage** arises when total implied variance w(k, T) decreases in T for fixed log-moneyness k — equivalently, when call prices at fixed strike decrease with maturity. Absence requires ∂_T w(k, T) ≥ 0.
2. **Butterfly arbitrage** arises when the call-price function fails to be convex in strike, producing negative risk-neutral densities. Roper's theorem gives necessary and sufficient conditions on the smile in terms of the Durrleman function g(k) = (1 − k w′/(2w))² − (w′)²(1/w + 1/4) + w′′/2 ≥ 0 [9].

Fukasawa (2012) reformulated the butterfly condition elegantly: absence of butterfly arbitrage is equivalent to the Black–Scholes quantities −d_1(k) and −d_2(k) being strictly increasing functions of log-moneyness k [9]. Building on this, Martini and Mingone fully characterized the butterfly-arbitrage-free domain of the SVI parameterization, yielding simple explicit range conditions on rescaled SVI parameters and a least-squares calibration algorithm guaranteed to produce arbitrage-free smiles [9].

For the *surface*, Gatheral and Jacquier's SSVI parameterization writes w(k, T) = θ_T ψ(k φ(θ_T)) with ATM total variance θ_T, correlation ρ and a shape function φ, and proved sufficient conditions for the absence of static arbitrage [8]:

> **SSVI no-arbitrage conditions.** *∂_T θ_T ≥ 0; 0 ≤ ∂_θ(θφ(θ)) ≤ (4 − θφ(θ)(1+|ρ|))/(1+|ρ|); and θφ(θ)(1+|ρ|) < 4 for all θ > 0.*

Guo, Jacquier, Martini and Neufcourt generalized this to arbitrary ψ shapes, deriving necessary and sufficient conditions coupling ψ and φ [8].

### 4.4 Forward variance, VIX futures and VIX options

Bergomi's forward variance framework takes as primitive the curve ξ_t(u) = E^Q[V_{t+u} | F_t], the risk-neutral expectation of future instantaneous variance [3]. The VIX index is, up to discretization and jump corrections, the square root of 30-day forward variance:

```
VIX_t² ≈ (1/Δ) ∫_t^{t+Δ} ξ_t(u) du ,   Δ = 30 days
```

so VIX futures are futures on integrated forward variance and VIX options are options on the square root of a variance swap [3]. In the rough Bergomi model the forward variance curve has an explicit exponential form, and VIX_T² becomes lognormal conditional on F_T, yielding closed-form VIX futures and a tractable VIX smile [3]. Bayer, Friz and Gatheral analyzed this structure in detail, deriving VIX futures directly from the forward variance curve and probing how well the model describes SPX and VIX jointly — finding an excellent joint fit of SPX smiles and VIX smiles, with the VIX futures term structure as the binding constraint [3].

### 4.5 Joint SPX/VIX calibration: quadratic rough Heston and the Zumbach effect

Fitting SPX and VIX smiles *simultaneously* with continuous paths was long conjectured impossible (Guyon's conjecture), because the SPX skew demands strong negative spot–vol correlation while the VIX smile slopes upward, demanding positive vol-of-vol skew [5]. Gatheral, Jusselin and Rosenbaum refuted the conjecture with the *quadratic rough Heston* (QRH) model [5]:

```
V_t = a (Z_t − b)² + c

dZ_t = λ (θ(t) − Z_t) dt + ν dW^H_t        (fractional driver, H ≈ 0.1)
```

The quadratic map from the latent factor Z_t to variance creates the *Zumbach effect*: past price trends feed back into volatility, so that volatility reacts asymmetrically to the path of returns [5]. This single mechanism reconciles the two smiles — SPX skew from the rough fractional driver, VIX upward slope from the convex quadratic transformation — with one fixed parameter set. Rosenbaum and Zhang subsequently built a deep-calibration pipeline using multi-factor Markovian approximations of the fractional kernel and neural-network pricing maps, achieving VIX option prices within bid–ask spreads and an excellent SPX ATM skew fit, plus instantaneous hedging quantities [6].

The QRH model thus represents the current frontier: roughness explains the SPX surface, the Zumbach feedback explains the VIX surface, and the two are unified in one continuous-path model [5][6].

##5 Empirical Results and Proofs

### The central asymptotic theorem

> **Theorem (rough ATM skew).** *In a stochastic volatility model where volatility is driven by a Riemann–Liouville fractional Brownian motion with Hurst exponent H in the open interval (0, 1/2), the at-the-money implied volatility skew satisfies Ψ(T) = |∂_k σ_BS(k,T)|_{k=0} ~ C T^{H−1/2} as T ↓ 0, for an explicit constant C depending on the vol-of-vol, the spot–vol correlation and the forward variance curve.*

> **Proof sketch.** *Fukasawa's martingale expansion expresses the short-maturity smile through conditional expectations of volatility functionals. The skew coefficient is proportional to E[D_s σ_t], the Malliavin derivative of volatility with respect to the Brownian shock at time s. For the fractional kernel (t−s)^{H−1/2}, this derivative explodes like (t−s)^{H−1/2} as s ↑ t. Integrating over the option's life and applying the Bergomi–Guyon expansion yields the T^{H−1/2} rate; the constant C is identified from the leading term of the expansion [4][11]. For H = 1/2 the classical O(1) skew is recovered, and for H > 1/2 the skew vanishes — matching the Alòs–León–Vives dichotomy.*

### Numerical evidence

**Hurst exponent estimates** (realized-volatility scaling regressions, Gatheral–Jaisson–Rosenbaum methodology) [1]:

| Asset | Estimated H | Timescale range |
|---|---|---|
| S&P 500 | 0.14 | 1 day to 1 year |
| NASDAQ | 0.12 | 1 day to 1 year |
| DAX futures | 0.13 | 1 day to 1 year |
| Bund futures | 0.08 – 0.14 | 1 day to 1 year |

All estimates lie far below 1/2, and the scaling regressions achieve R² above 0.99 across moment orders q from 0.5 to 3 — the monofractal signature of fractional Brownian motion [1].

**Skew term-structure fit.** Calibrating the rough Bergomi model to SPX options, Bayer, Friz and Gatheral obtain H ≈ 0.07 with η ≈ 1.9 and ρ ≈ −0.9, reproducing the empirical ATM skew from one week to two years; the Heston model fit to the same data requires implausible vol-of-vol above 2.0 and still misses short maturities by several volatility points [4]. The hybrid-scheme Monte Carlo with the variance-reduction composition of McCrickerd and Pakkanen prices the full surface roughly twenty times faster than naive Monte Carlo at fixed RMSE [4].

### Reproducible simulation snippet

The following Python code simulates a rough Bergomi variance path using the Cholesky method (exact for small grids), illustrating the roughness directly:

```python
import numpy as np

def rough_bergomi_paths(n_paths=4, n_steps=252, T=1.0, H=0.07,
                        eta=1.9, rho=-0.9, xi0=0.04, seed=0):
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    dt = T / n_steps
    t = np.linspace(dt, T, n_steps)
    # Covariance of Riemann-Liouville fBm increments
    s = t[:, None]; u = t[None, :]
    cov = 0.5 * (s**(2*H) + u**(2*H) - np.abs(s-u)**(2*H))
    L = np.linalg.cholesky(cov + 1e-12*np.eye(n_steps))
    paths = []
    for _ in range(n_paths):
        z1, z2 = rng.standard_normal(n_steps), rng.standard_normal(n_steps)
        W = L @ z1                      # fractional driver
        Ws = rho*z1 + np.sqrt(1-rho**2)*z2  # spot Brownian (correlated)
        Y = np.sqrt(2*H) * np.cumsum(
            ((t[:,None]-t[None,:]).clip(min=0)**(H-0.5)) @ (np.sqrt(dt)*z1))
        logV = np.log(xi0) + eta*Y - 0.5*eta**2 * t**(2*H)
        V = np.exp(logV)
        logS = np.cumsum(-0.5*V*dt + np.sqrt(V*dt)*Ws)
        paths.append((t, V, logS))
    return paths
```

##6 Limitations

Rough volatility is not without open problems. **First**, the models are non-Markovian, so hedging and American-option pricing require either infinite-dimensional lifts or Markovian approximations (multi-factor Bergomi-style sums of Ornstein–Uhlenbeck factors), each introducing approximation error that is hard to quantify uniformly [6][11]. **Second**, the fractional Riccati equation, while affine in form, is numerically delicate: short-maturity, far-out-of-the-money pricing demands careful contour deformation in the Fourier integral, and published calibrations have occasionally been "ghost calibrations" in which numerical error and model error cancel to produce spurious fits [11]. **Third**, the empirical H ≈ 0.1 finding has been challenged on statistical grounds — measurement noise in realized volatility can bias roughness estimators downward, and some authors argue the true Hurst exponent may be higher once microstructure noise is properly accounted for [11]. **Fourth**, rough models assume continuous paths; they cannot generate the jump-like short-dated smile convexity seen around earnings or central-bank announcements without additional jump components. **Finally**, the Zumbach feedback in the quadratic rough Heston model, while empirically motivated, lacks the microstructural derivation that Hawkes-process limits provide for plain rough Heston [2][5].

##7 Conclusion

The rough volatility program has, in a decade, rewritten the foundations of stochastic volatility modeling. From the Heston model's affine Riccati structure and its SVI large-time limit [7], through the empirical discovery that log-volatility is fractional Brownian motion with H ≈ 0.1 [1], to the rough Bergomi and rough Heston models that reproduce the power-law ATM skew [2][3][4], the field has converged on a coherent picture: *volatility is rough, forward variance is the natural state variable, and affine structure survives in Volterra form*. The no-arbitrage geometry of implied volatility surfaces — SVI/SSVI parameterizations with explicit arbitrage-free domains [8][9] — provides the guardrails within which these models are calibrated. And the joint SPX/VIX calibration problem, once conjectured impossible for continuous models, has been solved by combining roughness with the Zumbach price-feedback effect in the quadratic rough Heston model [5][6]. The remaining frontier is computational: fast, provably accurate pricing and hedging under non-Markovian dynamics at production scale.
