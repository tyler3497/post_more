# Chemical Reaction Network Theory: Deficiency Theorems, Persistence, and the Global Attractor Conjecture

## Abstract

Chemical reaction network theory deduces the qualitative dynamics of mass-action systems from the combinatorial structure of the reaction graph alone, independent of the numerical rate constants. This thesis develops the deficiency framework of Horn, Jackson, and Feinberg, in which the integer δ = n − ℓ − s — the number of complexes minus the number of linkage classes minus the rank of the stoichiometric subspace — governs the possible steady-state structure. We prove the Deficiency Zero Theorem in full: a weakly reversible deficiency-zero network admits exactly one positive equilibrium per stoichiometric compatibility class for every choice of rate constants, and every equilibrium is complex balanced and asymptotically stable; a non-weakly-reversible deficiency-zero network admits no positive equilibrium at all. The Deficiency One Theorem extends this rigidity to δ = 1. We then develop persistence and permanence, siphons, and boundary equilibria as the obstructions to global convergence, and present the resolution of the Global Attractor Conjecture through Craciun's toric differential inclusions, alongside the single-linkage-class and strongly endotactic special cases. Numerical verification and applications to signaling and metabolic networks complete the account.

##1 Introduction

A chemical reaction network is among the most austere objects in applied mathematics: a finite set of species, a finite set of complexes, and a directed graph of reactions between them. From this combinatorial data, mass-action kinetics constructs a polynomial vector field on the positive orthant, with one positive rate constant per reaction. The central miracle of chemical reaction network theory is that the long-time behavior of this vector field — the number, location, and stability of its equilibria — is often forced by the graph structure alone, holding for *every* choice of rate constants [4][8]. Where most of nonlinear dynamics surrenders to parameter dependence, CRNT identifies structural invariants that are robust by construction.

The most celebrated such invariant is the *deficiency*, introduced in the manuscripts of Feinberg and of Horn and Jackson in 1972 [9]. The deficiency δ = n − ℓ − s is a nonnegative integer combining the number of complexes, the number of linkage classes, and the rank of the stoichiometric subspace. Its power lies in two theorems of exceptional scope. The Deficiency Zero Theorem states that a weakly reversible deficiency-zero network admits exactly one positive equilibrium in each stoichiometric compatibility class for all rate constants, and no positive equilibrium at all when weak reversibility fails. The Deficiency One Theorem extends this rigidity to δ = 1 [8][9].

Yet deficiency theory does not settle convergence: even when the equilibrium is unique and locally stable, trajectories might approach the boundary of the orthant, where species go extinct. The questions of *persistence* and *permanence* — whether trajectories stay uniformly away from the boundary — became the central frontier of the field. The Global Attractor Conjecture, formulated by Horn in 1974, asserts that in each compatibility class the unique complex-balanced equilibrium attracts every positive trajectory [1]. Its resolution, through Craciun's toric differential inclusions in 2015, closed the oldest open problem in the field and is the culmination of this thesis [1][4].

##2 Background

### Networks, complexes, and the reaction graph

A *chemical reaction network* is a triple {S, C, R}:

- **Species.** The set S = {S1, ..., Sn} collects the interacting entities — chemical compounds, proteins, metabolites.
- **Complexes.** A *complex* is a formal nonnegative integer combination of species, such as A + 2B, appearing on either side of a reaction arrow. C denotes the set of all complexes, with |C| = m. Each complex y is a vector in ℝ^n with nonnegative integer entries.
- **Reactions.** A *reaction* y → y′ is an ordered pair of distinct complexes, and R is the set of all reactions. The complexes and reactions form the *reaction graph*, a finite directed multigraph.

The connected components of the underlying undirected reaction graph are the *linkage classes*, counted by ℓ. The strongly connected components are the *strong linkage classes*; a strong linkage class is *terminal* when no reaction leaves it. The network is *weakly reversible* when every linkage class is a strong linkage class — equivalently, every reaction lies on a directed cycle of complexes [5][8]. Weak reversibility is strictly weaker than reversibility: individual reactions need not be reversible, only the graph must admit return paths.

### Stoichiometry and mass action

The *stoichiometric subspace* is S = span{y′ − y : y → y′ ∈ R}, of dimension s, the *rank* of the network.

Under mass-action kinetics, each reaction y → y′ proceeds at rate κ_{y→y′} x^y, where x^y = Π_i x_i^{y_i} is the mass-action monomial and κ_{y→y′} > 0 is the rate constant.

ẋ = Σ_{y→y′ ∈ R} κ_{y→y′} x^y (y′ − y),

a polynomial dynamical system on the positive orthant [4].

### The deficiency

The *deficiency* of the network is the nonnegative integer

δ = m − ℓ − s,

where m is the number of complexes, ℓ the number of linkage classes, and s the rank of the stoichiometric subspace [9].

The following table computes the deficiency for four canonical networks:

| Network | m | ℓ | s | δ | Weakly reversible |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| A ⇌ B | 2 | 1 | 1 | 0 | yes |
| E + S ⇌ ES → E + P (Michaelis–Menten) | 3 | 1 | 2 | 0 | no |
| 2A ⇌ A + B → 2B | 3 | 1 | 1 | 1 | no |
| 2A ⇌ A + B ⇌ 2B | 3 | 1 | 1 | 1 | yes |

In the Michaelis–Menten network, the complex E + P is terminal with no outgoing reaction, so weak reversibility fails even though δ = 0 — a fact whose dynamical consequences are exactly what the Deficiency Zero Theorem predicts.

##3 Methodology

Our mathematical apparatus has three pillars: the matrix formulation of the mass-action equations, the Lyapunov theory of complex-balanced systems, and the combinatorial theory of siphons.

**Matrix formulation.** The species-formation-rate function factorizes as f(x) = Y A_κ Ψ(x), where Y is the n × m matrix whose columns are the complexes, Ψ(x) ∈ ℝ^m_{>0} is the vector of mass-action monomials x^y, and A_κ is the Kirchhoff (graph Laplacian) matrix of the weighted reaction graph [4]. This factorization separates the *stoichiometric* data (Y) from the *kinetic* data (A_κ) and is the starting point of both deficiency theory and toric geometry approaches [7].

**Complex balance.** A *complex-balanced* equilibrium x* satisfies A_κ Ψ(x*) = 0: at each complex, the total inflow of complex equals the total outflow. Complex balance generalizes the detailed-balance condition of statistical physics [8]. The foundational structural result is:

> Theorem (Horn and Jackson): Suppose a mass-action system admits a complex-balanced equilibrium x̄ ∈ ℝ^n_{>0}. Then (i) no positive equilibrium fails to be complex balanced — every positive equilibrium is complex balanced; (ii) the network is weakly reversible; (iii) each positive stoichiometric compatibility class contains exactly one complex-balanced equilibrium; and (iv) each such equilibrium is locally asymptotically stable [8].

Moreover, any complex-balanced mass-action system, regardless of deficiency, is *absolutely* complex balanced: complex balance of one positive equilibrium forces complex balance of all of them [1].

**Lyapunov theory.** For a complex-balanced equilibrium x̄, the function

V_{x̄}(x) = Σ_i ( x_i (ln x_i − ln x̄_i − 1) + x̄_i )

is strictly convex on the positive orthant with its global minimum at x̄, and is strictly decreasing along every non-equilibrium trajectory — the standard Lyapunov function of chemical reaction network theory [2].

**Siphons.** A subset Σ ⊆ S of species is a *siphon* when every reaction that produces a species of Σ also consumes a species of Σ: once all species of a siphon are absent, they can never be produced again. Siphons are the combinatorial obstructions to persistence; a siphon is *critical* when no conservation law is supported within it, and networks without critical siphons are persistent [6].

**Computational methodology.** We compute deficiencies by exact rank computation over the rationals, integrate mass-action ODEs with their conservation laws to verify convergence, and use decay of the Lyapunov function V as a numerical certificate of global attraction.

##4 Deficiency Theory, Persistence, and the Global Attractor Conjecture

### 4.1 The Deficiency Zero Theorem and complex balancing

The Deficiency Zero Theorem, proved independently by Feinberg and by Horn and Jackson in 1972, is the cornerstone of CRNT [9]:

> Theorem (Deficiency Zero Theorem — Feinberg, Horn, Jackson): Let the network have deficiency zero. (a) If the network is weakly reversible, then for *every* choice of positive rate constants, the associated mass-action system admits exactly one positive equilibrium in each positive stoichiometric compatibility class; every positive equilibrium is complex balanced; and each is locally asymptotically stable. (b) If the network is not weakly reversible, then for *every* choice of positive rate constants, the system admits no positive equilibrium at all [8][9].

The two halves are equally striking.

A finer consequence of part (a) is the *robust ratio* property: at any positive steady state, the monomial ratio x^y / x^{y′} for two complexes y, y′ in the same linkage class takes the same value, namely K(y)/K(y′), where the tree constants K are computed from the reaction graph by the matrix-tree theorem [4].

The proof of part (a) combines the Horn–Jackson theorem above with an existence argument: a Brouwer fixed-point argument on the invariant polyhedron (x0 + S) ∩ ℝ^n_{≥0} produces a positive zero of f, which the Horn–Jackson theorem then promotes to a unique complex-balanced equilibrium per class [8].

### 4.2 The Deficiency One Theorem

Feinberg's Deficiency One Theorem extends this rigidity to networks of deficiency one, at the price of a condition on each linkage class [9]:

> Theorem (Deficiency One Theorem — Feinberg): Suppose δ = 1, each linkage class has deficiency at most one, and the sum of the linkage-class deficiencies equals one. Then: (1) if the network is weakly reversible, every mass-action system admits exactly one positive equilibrium in each positive stoichiometric compatibility class; (2) if the network is not weakly reversible but each linkage class contains exactly one terminal strong linkage class, the system admits no positive equilibria; (3) if each linkage class contains exactly one terminal strong linkage class, the system admits at most one positive equilibrium per positive stoichiometric compatibility class [8][9].

Consider the network 2A ⇌ A + B → 2B from the table: it has δ = 1, a single linkage class of deficiency one, and a single terminal strong linkage class {2B}.

The deficiency one theorem is sharp: at deficiency two and above, the structural theory loses control, and multistationarity, oscillations, and chaos all become possible [4].

### 4.3 Persistence, permanence, and boundary equilibria

Deficiency theory controls equilibria but says nothing about whether trajectories actually converge to them.

- **Persistence.** A mass-action system is *persistent* if every trajectory with positive initial condition stays at a positive distance from the boundary of ℝ^n_{≥0}; equivalently, no ω-limit point lies on the boundary.
- **Permanence.** A system is *permanent* if it is persistent and all trajectories are bounded — so each trajectory is eventually confined to a compact subset of the interior.

The *Persistence Conjecture* asserts that every weakly reversible mass-action system is persistent [4][8].

The combinatorial route to persistence runs through siphons.

A geometric sufficient condition is *endotacticity*: the reaction arrows point inward relative to the Newton polytope of the vector field, so trajectories cannot escape to the boundary.

### 4.4 Toric differential inclusions and the resolution of the Global Attractor Conjecture

The Global Attractor Conjecture, formulated by Horn in 1974, is the oldest and most important open problem of the field [2]:

> Conjecture (Global Attractor Conjecture — Horn): For a complex-balanced mass-action system (equivalently, a toric dynamical system), the unique positive equilibrium in each positive stoichiometric compatibility class is globally asymptotically stable relative to that class [1].

The Lyapunov function V rules out interior ω-limit points other than the equilibrium, and local asymptotic stability is classical; the entire difficulty is excluding ω-limit points on the *boundary*.

| Result | Hypothesis | Reference |
|---|---|---|
| Single linkage class | reaction graph connected | Anderson, 2011 [2] |
| Strongly endotactic | inward-pointing geometry | Gopalkrishnan–Miller–Shiu, 2013 [3] |
| Dimension ≤ 3 | stoichiometric subspace dim ≤ 3 | Craciun–Nazarov–Pantea; Pantea [4] |
| No critical siphons | combinatorial persistence | Angeli–De Leenheer–Sontag [6] |

Craciun's 2015 resolution introduced *toric differential inclusions* [1].

An immediate corollary is that all detailed-balanced mass-action systems and all deficiency-zero weakly reversible networks have the global attractor property [1].

##5 Empirical Results and Proofs

### Computational verification of deficiency

We implemented the deficiency computation δ = m − ℓ − s directly, with linkage classes found by union-find on the reaction graph and rank computed over the rationals:

```python
import numpy as np

def deficiency(stoich, complex_ids, reactions):
    parent = {c: c for c in complex_ids}
    def find(c):
        while parent[c] != c:
            parent[c] = parent[parent[c]]
            c = parent[c]
        return c
    for s, t in reactions:
        rs, rt = find(s), find(t)
        if rs != rt:
            parent[rt] = rs
    ell = len({find(c) for c in complex_ids})
    vecs = np.array([stoich[t] - stoich[s] for s, t in reactions], dtype=float)
    s_rank = int(np.linalg.matrix_rank(vecs))
    return len(complex_ids) - ell - s_rank

# Michaelis-Menten: E+S <-> ES -> E+P ; species order (E, S, ES, P)
mm = {"es": np.array([1, 1, 0, 0]), "x": np.array([0, 0, 1, 0]),
      "ep": np.array([1, 0, 0, 1])}
mm_rxns = [("es", "x"), ("x", "es"), ("x", "ep")]
print("Michaelis-Menten deficiency:", deficiency(mm, list(mm), mm_rxns))

# Deficiency-one example: 2A <-> A+B -> 2B ; species order (A, B)
d1 = {"a2": np.array([2, 0]), "ab": np.array([1, 1]), "b2": np.array([0, 2])}
d1_rxns = [("a2", "ab"), ("ab", "a2"), ("ab", "b2")]
print("Example deficiency:", deficiency(d1, list(d1), d1_rxns))
```

The program outputs deficiency 0 for the Michaelis–Menten network (m = 3, ℓ = 1, s = 2) and deficiency 1 for the second example (m = 3, ℓ = 1, s = 1), confirming the hand computations in the table of Section 2.

### Numerical experiment: global convergence in a deficiency-zero network

We simulated the weakly reversible deficiency-zero network A ⇌ B with rate constants k1 = 1.5 and k2 = 0.5 and conserved total a + b = 3. The unique positive equilibrium per class is a* = 0.75, b* = 2.25. From three initial conditions spanning the class, the Horn–Jackson Lyapunov function V decays monotonically to zero:

| Initial a(0) | V at t = 0 | V at t = 25 | a(25) − a* |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0.2 | 0.348 | < 1e-8 | < 1e-5 |
| 1.0 | 0.052 | < 1e-8 | < 1e-5 |
| 2.9 | 3.611 | < 1e-8 | < 1e-5 |

Every trajectory converges to the same equilibrium, exactly as the Deficiency Zero Theorem and the Global Attractor Conjecture jointly predict [1][8].

### Proof: the Horn–Jackson Lyapunov dissipation

The analytic core of the convergence theory is the dissipation identity for the Lyapunov function, which we prove in full:

> Proof: Let x* be complex balanced, so that for each complex y0 the common value c_{y0} = Σ_{y→y0} κ_{y→y0} (x*)^y = Σ_{y0→y′} κ_{y0→y′} (x*)^{y0} is well defined. With V(x) = Σ_i (x_i (ln(x_i/x*_i) − 1) + x*_i), we have ∇V(x) = ln x − ln x*, and hence along trajectories dV/dt = Σ_{y→y′} κ_{y→y′} x^y (y′ − y)·(ln x − ln x*). Writing φ(y) = x^y/(x*)^y, this becomes dV/dt = Σ_{y→y′} κ_{y→y′} (x*)^y φ(y) (ln φ(y′) − ln φ(y)). For each reaction, the elementary inequality ln t ≤ t − 1 gives φ(y)(ln φ(y′) − ln φ(y)) ≤ φ(y′) − φ(y). Summing, dV/dt ≤ Σ_{y→y′} κ_{y→y′} (x*)^y (φ(y′) − φ(y)) = Σ_{y0} φ(y0)(c_{y0} − c_{y0}) = 0, where the last equality is exactly complex balance. Equality holds only when φ is constant on each linkage class, i.e. ln x − ln x* ∈ S^⊥, which forces x = x* by Birch's theorem on the unique intersection of the toric manifold with the compatibility class [2][8]. ∎

Birch's theorem thus supplies uniqueness while the Lyapunov function supplies attraction from the interior; persistence — the exclusion of boundary ω-limit points — is the remaining ingredient that toric differential inclusions provide [1].

### Applications to systems biology

The parameter-free character of these theorems is precisely what systems biology needs, since in vivo rate constants are rarely known. Metabolic networks frequently contain deficiency-zero weakly reversible subnetworks, for which the theory certifies a unique globally attracting steady state in each conservation class regardless of enzyme kinetics details [4][7]. Signaling cascades such as phosphorylation cycles have been analyzed through CRNT to delineate when multistationarity is structurally impossible (low deficiency) and when it can arise (higher deficiency), guiding both model construction and experimental design [4].

##6 Limitations

Several boundaries of the theory deserve emphasis. **First**, the deficiency theorems are sharp: at deficiency two and above, structural control is lost, and multistationarity, sustained oscillations, and chaos all occur. The theorems are silent there, and one must resort to algebraic methods — resultants, Gröbner bases, and toric elimination — surveyed in [7].

**Second**, deficiency theory is a deterministic mass-action theory. Stochastic reaction networks, modeled as continuous-time Markov chains, exhibit their own stationary-distribution theory that does not reduce to the deterministic picture; the tutorial [8] develops both in parallel, and the two can disagree on extinction behavior.

**Third**, non-mass-action kinetics — Michaelis–Menten and Hill reductions, power-law kinetics with variable exponents — lie outside the classical theorems. The extended persistence and permanence conjectures for variable-rate systems remain open, as does the endotactic (non-strong) case [4][5].

**Fourth**, the toric-differential-inclusion proof of the Global Attractor Conjecture is an existence argument: the invariant regions it constructs are not explicit enough to yield quantitative convergence rates or basin estimates [1]. Numerical certification, as in Section 5, remains the practical complement.

**Finally**, computing the deficiency is easy, but deciding weak reversibility-related dynamical properties for large networks, enumerating all positive equilibria, and certifying global attraction algorithmically remain active computational challenges [7][9].

##7 Conclusion

Chemical reaction network theory shows that the reaction graph alone can determine the qualitative dynamics of a mass-action system for all rate constants. The deficiency δ = m − ℓ − s is the organizing invariant: at deficiency zero, weak reversibility is equivalent to the existence of a unique, complex-balanced, asymptotically stable equilibrium per compatibility class; at deficiency one, Feinberg's theorem preserves uniqueness or rules out equilibria according to the terminal linkage structure [8][9]. Persistence and siphon theory identify boundary extinction as the sole obstruction to global convergence [6], and Craciun's toric differential inclusions remove that obstruction, resolving Horn's 1974 Global Attractor Conjecture [1][4]. What remains — extended persistence and permanence for variable-rate systems, the endotactic case, and quantitative convergence theory — defines the current frontier of a field that turns combinatorics into dynamics.
