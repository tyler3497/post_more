# Earth System Models in the CMIP6 Era: Coupled Architecture, Cloud Parameterization, Emergent Constraints, and Tipping Points

## Abstract

**Earth system models (ESMs)** are the principal scientific instruments for projecting anthropogenic climate change, integrating atmosphere, ocean, land, cryosphere, and biogeochemical subsystems through conserving flux exchanges mediated by software couplers. The sixth phase of the Coupled Model Intercomparison Project (CMIP6) coordinated more than one hundred models from roughly fifty modeling centers around a shared experimental protocol — the DECK experiments, a historical simulation, and more than twenty endorsed Model Intercomparison Projects — run under the *Shared Socioeconomic Pathway* (SSP) scenario framework. This thesis synthesizes ESM architecture, the governing physics of dynamical cores, the parameterization paradigm that encodes unresolved processes such as cloud formation, and the principal uncertainty sources in twenty-first-century projections. We examine cloud parameterization as the dominant uncertainty source, the emergent-constraint methodology that narrows ensemble spread using observed variability, the observational evaluation infrastructure, and the representation of climate tipping points. We conclude that process-informed machine-learned closures, targeted resolution increases, and disciplined observational constraints offer the most credible path toward trustworthy projections.

## 1 Introduction

Climate change is a boundary-value problem of staggering scale: a rotating, stratified fluid envelope heated differentially by the Sun, coupled to a slowly evolving ocean, reactive biosphere, and dynamic cryosphere, forced by trace gases altered by a single species. No analytic solution exists at the scales of interest, and no controlled experiment can be performed on the system. *Earth system models* are therefore our numerical laboratories — discretizations of geophysical fluid dynamics, augmented by representations of sub-grid processes, advanced forward under prescribed external forcings.

The lineage runs from one-dimensional radiative-convective models through atmospheric GCMs, to coupled atmosphere–ocean GCMs, and finally to modern ESMs adding interactive carbon cycles, dynamic vegetation, chemistry, aerosols, and in some cases ice sheets [1]. Each step added realism at the cost of computational burden and parametric freedom. CMIP6, coordinated through the protocol of Eyring et al. [1], underpins the Sixth Assessment Report's physical science basis.

Three facts motivate this thesis. **First**, the ensemble spread of CMIP6 twenty-first-century projections remains large, with equilibrium climate sensitivity (ECS) ranging roughly from 1.8 K to over 5.6 K across models [5], a spread dominated by divergent cloud feedbacks. **Second**, methodological advances — process-resolving simulations, machine-learned parameterizations, and emergent constraints — are maturing simultaneously and may jointly narrow this spread [3][6]. **Third**, models now routinely exhibit abrupt, tipping-like behavior in components such as the Atlantic meridional overturning circulation and subtropical low clouds [4], forcing a confrontation between smooth scenario projections and possible qualitative regime shifts.

Section 2 describes the CMIP6 design and SSP scenario architecture. Section 3 formalizes coupled-model methodology. Section 4 deepens four critical topics — dynamical cores and resolution, cloud parameterization, emergent constraints, and tipping points. Sections 5–7 cover evaluation, limitations, and conclusions.

## 2 Background: From GCMs to the CMIP6 Enterprise

The modern ESM descends from the primitive-equation atmospheric models pioneered at GFDL, NCAR, and the UK Met Office, which demonstrated that the large-scale circulation emerges from radiative forcing, rotation, and stratification without empirical tuning of the circulation itself. Coupling an interactive ocean added the thermal inertia that sets the *transient* character of the climate response; adding biogeochemistry transformed the models from climate simulators into Earth system simulators, in which atmospheric CO2 becomes a prognostic variable whose evolution is co-determined by terrestrial photosynthesis, heterotrophic respiration, ocean solubility, and anthropogenic emissions.

**2.1 The CMIP6 design.**

CMIP6 is not a single experiment but a federated protocol. Its enduring core is the **DECK** (Diagnostic, Evaluation and Characterization of Klima) set plus the historical simulation [1]:

| Experiment | Description | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| `piControl` | Pre-industrial control, fixed 1850 forcing | Baseline climate, drift, internal variability |
| `AMIP` | Atmosphere-only, observed SSTs 1979–2014 | Atmospheric fidelity given observed oceans |
| `abrupt-4xCO2` | Instantaneous CO2 quadrupling | Fast adjustments, ECS estimation |
| `1pctCO2` | 1%/yr CO2 increase to quadrupling | Transient response, TCR estimation |
| `historical` | All forcings, 1850–2014 | Twentieth-century attribution, evaluation |

Around this core, more than twenty endorsed MIPs targeted specific questions — clouds (CFMIP), aerosols (AerChemMIP), the carbon cycle (C4MIP), paleoclimate (PMIP), high resolution (HighResMIP) — each requiring the DECK as common ground [1]. Common *input4MIPs* forcing datasets ensured inter-model differences reflected model structure rather than forcing discrepancies.

**2.2 The SSP scenario framework.**

Projections require boundary conditions for the future, which cannot be predicted but can be spanned. The **Shared Socioeconomic Pathways** provide reference narratives of twenty-first-century society and ecosystems *in the absence of climate policy or climate change* [2], spanning combinations of challenges to mitigation and adaptation:

| Pathway | Narrative | Mitigation challenge | Adaptation challenge |
|---|---|---|---|
| **SSP1** | Sustainability, rapid development | Low | Low |
| **SSP2** | Middle of the road | Medium | Medium |
| **SSP3** | Regional rivalry | High | High |
| **SSP4** | Inequality | Low | High |
| **SSP5** | Fossil-fueled development | High | Low |

Integrated assessment models translated SSP narratives into emissions and land-use pathways, paired with radiative-forcing targets to produce the Tier-1 ScenarioMIP experiments: **SSP1-2.6**, **SSP2-4.5**, **SSP3-7.0**, and **SSP5-8.5**, named for their 2100 forcing levels in W m⁻². The SSPs separate *socioeconomic* uncertainty from *physical* uncertainty: the same model under SSP1-2.6 and SSP5-8.5 produces dramatically different warming, and no physical-model improvement can narrow the socioeconomic component.

## 3 Methodology: The Coupled Earth System

**3.1 Component architecture and the coupler.**

An ESM factorizes the Earth system into components — **atmosphere**, **ocean**, **land surface**, **sea ice**, and increasingly **land ice**, **chemistry**, and **biogeochemistry** — each with its own grid, time step, and numerics. They exchange fluxes through a **coupler** (OASIS, ESMF, or bespoke): momentum, heat, moisture, and tracers. Conservative remapping between non-coincident grids is the coupler's central burden: energy, water, and carbon must be conserved to machine precision across component boundaries, lest spurious sources accumulate into centennial climate drift.

Coupling frequency (typically hourly to daily) mediates a trade-off between fidelity and cost; explicit coupling advances each component with lagged boundary conditions, while implicit schemes reduce *coupling shock* at added expense. The coupled integration is a partitioned differential-algebraic system whose numerical stability is nontrivial in its own right.

**3.2 Dynamical cores.**

The **dynamical core** integrates the resolved fluid equations. The atmosphere is governed by the hydrostatic or non-hydrostatic primitive equations — momentum, continuity, thermodynamic energy, and moisture conservation on a rotating sphere:

```
∂v/∂t + v·∇v = −(1/ρ)∇p − fk×v + F
∂ρ/∂t + ∇·(ρv) = 0
c_p dT/dt − (1/ρ)dp/dt = Q
```

Horizontal discretization strategies include spectral methods (now largely retired for climate), finite-volume on cubed-sphere or icosahedral grids (which avoid the polar singularity and scale efficiently), and spectral-element formulations. Vertical coordinates are hybrid sigma-pressure systems that follow terrain near the surface and flatten aloft. The ocean core integrates the Boussinesq equations with parameterizations of mesoscale eddies unless resolution permits their explicit representation.

**3.3 The parameterization paradigm.**

Grid spacings of order 100 km in the atmosphere cannot resolve convection, boundary-layer turbulence, cloud microphysics, gravity waves, or plant-canopy processes. These are **parameterized**: represented as statistical functions of the resolved state,

```
∂X̄/∂t + ... = P(X̄; θ)
```

where `X̄` is the resolved state and `θ` a vector of empirical parameters — closure hypotheses such as bulk mass-flux convection schemes, PDF-based boundary-layer cloud schemes, and two-moment microphysics. They encode physical reasoning but also structural assumptions and tunable parameters. The central challenge [3]: the parameterizations needed most are those whose statistics may fall outside the observed distribution under warming — present-day calibration does not guarantee fidelity in novel regimes.

## 4 Deep Dive: Four Pillars of Modern ESM Science

### 4.1 Dynamical cores and the resolution frontier

Resolution sets which processes are *resolved* versus *parameterized*, and hence the structural character of a model. Typical CMIP6 atmosphere grids are ~100 km; ocean grids range from 1° (parameterized eddies) to 0.25° (eddy-permitting). The computational cost of a global simulation scales brutally:

```python
# Scaling of atmospheric model cost with horizontal resolution dx
def relative_cost(dx_ref, dx_new, years=100, ensemble=30):
    # cost ~ (1/dx)^3 in space-time for fixed physics,
    # and time step must shrink with dx (CFL): ~ (1/dx)^4 total
    space_time = (dx_ref / dx_new) ** 4
    return space_time * years * ensemble

print(f"100km -> 25km cost factor: {relative_cost(100, 25):.0f}x")
# -> 100km -> 25km cost factor: 256x for the same experiment length
```

This is why HighResMIP multi-decadal simulations at 25–50 km remain hero experiments rather than ensemble standards [1]. Higher resolution demonstrably improves tropical cyclones, atmospheric blocking, ocean boundary currents, and orographic precipitation — but does not automatically reduce ECS spread, because cloud feedbacks are set by parameterized physics that resolution alone cannot fix. The emerging consensus is *targeted* resolution: eddy-resolving oceans and convection-permitting regional refinement where the payoff is largest, while the parameterization frontier is attacked with process-resolving models.

> **Theorem:** *Reynolds decomposition.* Any prognostic field φ may be decomposed into resolved and subgrid parts, φ = φ̄ + φ′, such that the evolution of the resolved field depends on the divergence of subgrid fluxes, ∇·(v′φ′)̅, which no finite-resolution integration can compute from resolved variables alone. *Corollary:* parameterization is not an engineering convenience but a mathematical necessity of discretization; the question is never whether to parameterize, but how honestly to quantify the closure error.

### 4.2 Cloud parameterization: the dominant uncertainty

Clouds are the single largest source of uncertainty in climate projections, and have been since the Charney Report era. The mechanism is the **cloud feedback**: as the planet warms, cloud amount, altitude, optical depth, and phase change, altering both reflected shortwave and emitted longwave radiation. In CMIP6, inter-model spread in cloud feedback explains most ECS spread, led by subtropical low clouds [5].

The scale gap explains the difficulty: a GCM grid cell spans ~10⁴ km² while the shallow cumulus clouds dominating subtropical cloud feedback are ~1 km across with ~30-minute lifetimes. Their collective radiative effect must be inferred from resolved temperature, humidity, stability, and subsidence. Competing schemes disagree sharply on the warming response of low-cloud cover: models whose boundary-layer mixing entrains more dry air as climate warms lose low clouds and warm more; models with different entrainment or microphysical assumptions do not.

> **Theorem:** *Cloud-controlling factors.* The top-of-atmosphere cloud radiative effect R can be decomposed as R ≈ Σᵢ (∂R/∂xᵢ)·Δxᵢ over controlling factors xᵢ (surface temperature, estimated inversion strength, subsidence, humidity). *Implication:* cloud feedback uncertainty is traceable to uncertainty in a small set of parametric sensitivities — which is precisely what makes it amenable to observational constraint, and precisely where current parameterizations diverge.

Several lines of attack are active:

1. **Large-eddy simulation (LES)** as a parameterization testbed: LES resolves boundary-layer turbulence and shallow convection at ~10–100 m over limited domains, exposing parameterization failure modes directly [3].
2. **Superparameterization**: embedding 2D cloud-resolving models in each GCM column — dramatically improved cloud statistics at enormous cost.
3. **Machine-learned closures**: neural networks trained on LES or storm-resolving simulations predict subgrid fluxes, embedded in the GCM with conservation and stability constraints [3]. Online stability and out-of-distribution generalization under warming remain open problems.
4. **Process fixes with global consequences**: CMIP6 models precipitate too readily from warm (liquid) clouds, underestimating the cloud-lifetime feedback; correcting warm-rain microphysics reduces projected warming by an amount comparable to the CMIP5-to-CMIP6 sensitivity increase. Small-scale process errors project onto planetary-scale sensitivity.

### 4.3 Emergent constraints: narrowing the spread with observations

If parameterization uncertainty is irreducible from first principles, perhaps *observations* can adjudicate among models. **Emergent constraints** exploit relationships, found across a model ensemble, between an observable quantity X (e.g., present-day variability) and an unobservable future quantity Y (e.g., ECS): Y ≈ aX + b. Measuring X in the real world then constrains Y, with uncertainty propagated through the regression.

The celebrated example is Cox et al.'s fluctuation–dissipation constraint: in CMIP5, the metric Ψ — combining temperature variance and one-year-lag autocorrelation — predicted ECS with striking fidelity, yielding ~3 K [6]. Related work constrained the **transient climate response (TCR)** to a likely range of 1.3–2.1 K (central 1.68 K), *narrower* than the raw CMIP6 ensemble, by relating TCR to post-1975 warming when the forcing signal-to-noise ratio is high [7].

> **Theorem:** *Fluctuation–dissipation.* In a system near equilibrium subject to stochastic forcing, the response to a small perturbation is determined by the statistics of unforced fluctuations. *Climate corollary:* models that fluctuate more vigorously also respond more strongly to sustained forcing — provided the fast feedbacks governing variability are those governing the forced response, an assumption to be tested, not assumed.

And test it we must, because emergent constraints have stumbled. Schlund et al. [5] re-evaluated eleven published ECS constraints and found many substantially weaker in CMIP6 — including the Cox et al. variability constraint, whose significance collapsed (p = 0.0032 → 0.54). Williamson et al. [6] traced this to a violated assumption: the derivation assumed low correlation between ECS and the internal-variability parameter, true in CMIP5 but not in CMIP6, where altered cloud feedbacks couple variability and sensitivity. The generalized relation survives, but observational estimates of the new predictor are too uncertain to yield a constraint. The lesson: emergent constraints are hypotheses about shared mechanisms, and each must survive *out-of-sample* testing on a new ensemble. Constraints that merely data-mine correlations across a few dozen models will not survive the next generation.

### 4.4 Tipping points in Earth system models

A **tipping point** is a threshold beyond which a subsystem transitions to a qualitatively different, often irreversible state via self-reinforcing feedbacks. Armstrong McKay et al. [4] reassessed the canonical tipping elements — ice sheets, AMOC, Amazon, permafrost, monsoons, coral reefs — and found risk escalates sharply above 1.5 °C: five elements already sit within the *possible* range at current warming (~1.1–1.2 °C), with central threshold estimates below 1.5 °C [4].

How do ESMs represent these dynamics? Unevenly:

- **AMOC**: ESMs show weakening under warming, but full collapse is rare in CMIP6 scenario runs. Dedicated *hosing* experiments reveal hysteresis in many models, yet the threshold is deeply uncertain (estimates span 1.4–8 °C [4]), and common North Atlantic salinity biases may artificially stabilize the simulated circulation.
- **Ice sheets**: most CMIP6 ESMs lack interactive ice-sheet dynamics; sea-level projections rely on offline models driven by ESM output. Marine ice-sheet instability is therefore absent from coupled projections — a structural blind spot of the first order [4].
- **Amazon dieback**: models with dynamic vegetation and fire can simulate forest-to-savanna transitions under severe drying, but thresholds depend on parameterized plant hydraulics, CO2 fertilization, and fire — among the least constrained components.
- **Permafrost carbon**: CMIP6 land models increasingly include permafrost soil carbon, but abrupt thaw processes (thermokarst, ice-wedge degradation) are largely absent; release is represented as gradual, likely underestimating committed emissions [4].
- **Clouds as tipping elements**: LES studies show subtropical stratocumulus decks can break up above ~1200 ppm CO2, a *global* tipping point to a hothouse state with ~8 K additional warming — a bifurcation invisible to any ESM whose boundary-layer scheme cannot represent the decoupling instability.

The uncomfortable synthesis: the models defining our projection envelopes structurally exclude or under-resolve several of the most consequential nonlinearities, making tipping points a problem of *unknown unknowns in the structural error budget*.

## 5 Empirical Evaluation and the CMIP6 Sensitivity Surprise

Trust in ESMs is earned through confrontation with observations, systematized in tools like **ESMValTool** and the PCMDI metrics package: CERES for top-of-atmosphere fluxes; AIRS and MLS for humidity profiles (used in the SU emergent constraint [5]); Argo for ocean heat content; GRACE for ice-sheet mass balance; HadCRUT and Berkeley Earth for surface temperature; ISCCP, MODIS, and CloudSat/CALIPSO for clouds. Standard metrics span mean-state biases, seasonal cycles, variability spectra, and process-oriented diagnostics such as the tropical precipitation–moisture relationship.

Despite decades of improvement, systematic biases persist: the **double ITCZ** in Pacific precipitation; the **equatorial cold tongue** extending too far west, distorting ENSO; **Southern Ocean** shortwave biases from too little supercooled liquid cloud; and divergent Arctic sea-ice loss rates. These biases matter because they implicate the same parameterized physics — convection, boundary-layer clouds, ocean mixing — that governs feedbacks. A model can reproduce twentieth-century warming for partially wrong reasons (e.g., excessive aerosol cooling compensating excessive sensitivity), which is why *process-oriented* evaluation, not just global-mean hindcast skill, is the community standard.

**5.3 The CMIP6 sensitivity increase.**

The most discussed CMIP6 result: 12 of 34 analyzed models exceed ECS of 4.5 K, and ensemble-mean TCR rose to 2.0 ± 0.4 K [7]. The increase traces to revised cloud schemes — particularly mixed-phase microphysics that removed a spurious negative phase-change feedback, unmasking stronger positive low-cloud feedbacks. Whether this is improved physics or new error compensation is debated; the warm-rain result in Section 4.2 suggests part of the increase may be artifactual. Notably, the observationally constrained TCR (1.3–2.1 K, central 1.68 K [7]) sits *below* the raw ensemble mean — the observations favor the less sensitive models.

## 6 Limitations

1. **Resolution versus ensembles.** Feasible resolutions cannot resolve deep convection or ocean eddies globally while also producing the ensembles needed for calibration, internal-variability sampling, and risk quantification [3]. Every resolution choice is a bet about where errors matter most.
2. **Model interdependence.** The ensemble is an ensemble of opportunity, not a designed sample of hypothesis space. Models share code, parameterizations, and tuning targets; effective independence is far lower than the model count, and naive multi-model means overweight shared assumptions.
3. **Parameter degeneracy.** Many parameter combinations reproduce the observed mean climate (equifinality); tuning to the twentieth century does not identify a unique parameter set, and compensating errors hide until a new forcing regime exposes them.
4. **Missing components.** Interactive ice sheets, realistic vegetation disturbance, permafrost abrupt thaw, and interactive chemistry remain absent or primitive in most CMIP6 ESMs [4]; tipping-element thresholds inherit these omissions.
5. **Emergent-constraint fragility.** Constraints can fail out of sample when mechanistic assumptions break [5][6]; they require physical derivation and independent validation, not mere correlation.
6. **Irreducible uncertainty.** Even a perfect model leaves unknowable future emissions and the chaotic realization of internal variability, which dominates regional near-term projections.

## 7 Conclusion

Earth system models are among the most ambitious computational artifacts ever constructed: coupled, conserving integrations of geophysical fluid dynamics and biogeochemistry that reproduce the observed climate's large-scale structure and its response to historical forcing with fidelity no simpler tool can match. The CMIP6 protocol [1] and the SSP framework [2] organized this enterprise into a rigorous, comparable, policy-relevant evidence base.

Yet the central scientific problem — the spread of projected warming — remains dominated by the oldest parameterization challenge: clouds. The path forward [3] is a synthesis: **process knowledge** encoded in physically structured parameterizations, **resolution** targeted where it resolves rather than merely refines, and **AI-derived closures** trained on observations and process-resolving simulations, all disciplined by **emergent constraints** [5][6][7] that must earn their keep out of sample. Tipping points [4] remind us that the projection envelope is not the risk envelope: the most consequential outcomes may lie in structural blind spots of the current generation.

The maturation of ESMs will be measured not by any single model's resolution or sensitivity, but by the community's ability to *quantify what the models cannot do* — to map the closure error, to validate constraints on new ensembles, and to represent abrupt change where the physics demands it. That is a harder and more honest standard than lower spread, and it is the standard on which trillion-dollar adaptation decisions implicitly rest.
