## Abstract

Classical text indexes—suffix trees and suffix arrays—answer substring queries in near-optimal time but consume an order of magnitude more space than the text itself, rendering them impractical for terabyte-scale corpora. This thesis develops the theory of *succinct* and *compressed* data structures that close this gap, following a single intellectual arc: from Jacobson's rank/select dictionaries that answer bitvector queries in constant time using only *n + o(n)* bits, through wavelet trees that lift these primitives to general alphabets at *nH₀ + o(n)* bits, to the FM-index, a *self-index* that replaces the text with its Burrows–Wheeler transform and counts pattern occurrences via backward search in time linear in the pattern. We present the two-level directory construction for rank, the Huffman-shaped and compressed wavelet tree, the LF-mapping that makes the BWT a navigable permutation, and the *r*-index that indexes highly repetitive collections in space proportional to the number of BWT runs. We prove the central space bounds—including the FM-index's celebrated *5nHₖ + o(n)* bits holding simultaneously for all orders *k*—and quantify the trade-offs against dynamic, large-alphabet, and repetitiveness-aware regimes.

# Succinct Data Structures for Compressed Full-Text Indexing: Rank/Select Dictionaries, Wavelet Trees, and the FM-Index

## Abstract

Classical text indexes—suffix trees and suffix arrays—answer substring queries in near-optimal time but consume an order of magnitude more space than the text itself, rendering them impractical for terabyte-scale corpora. This thesis develops the theory of *succinct* and *compressed* data structures that close this gap, following a single intellectual arc: from Jacobson's rank/select dictionaries that answer bitvector queries in constant time using only *n + o(n)* bits, through wavelet trees that lift these primitives to general alphabets at *nH₀ + o(n)* bits, to the FM-index, a *self-index* that replaces the text with its Burrows–Wheeler transform and counts pattern occurrences via backward search in time linear in the pattern. We prove the central space bounds—including the FM-index's celebrated *5nHₖ + o(n)* bits holding simultaneously for all orders *k*—and quantify the trade-offs against dynamic, large-alphabet, and repetitiveness-aware regimes.

## 1. Introduction

The tension between *space* and *functionality* is among the oldest in algorithm design. A suffix array [SA] of a text *T[1..n]* over an alphabet Σ occupies *n log n* bits—for *n = 10¹⁰* (a modest genomic database), roughly 50 bytes per character, more than fifty times the 1 byte per character of the raw text. A suffix tree is worse still, commonly requiring 15–25 bytes per character in optimized implementations. The data is useful only when it can be indexed, yet indexing it at such space blowups is often infeasible.

*Succinct data structures* resolve this tension through a precise quantitative contract. Let *I(Z)* denote the information-theoretic minimum number of bits needed to represent any object in a class *Z*. A representation of a member of *Z* is *succinct* if it uses *I(Z) + o(I(Z))* bits while supporting the natural operations in near-optimal time, and *compact* if it uses *O(I(Z))* bits. For a bitvector of length *n*, *I(Z) = n*; for a string of length *n* over an alphabet of size σ, *I(Z) = n log σ*. The ambition of the field, launched by Jacobson's 1989 FOCS paper on space-efficient static trees and graphs [1], is to make the index *fit inside the compressed text itself*—culminating in the *self-index*, a data structure that discards the original text entirely and yet supports counting, locating, and extracting arbitrary substrings.

Three ideas form the spine of this thesis:

1. **Rank/select dictionaries.** The operations *rank₁(i)* = number of 1s in *B[1..i]* and *select₁(j)* = position of the *j*-th 1 reduce tree navigation, permutations, and inverted lists to bitvector primitives answerable in *O(1)* time with *o(n)* bits of redundancy [1][6].
2. **Wavelet trees.** Grossi, Gupta, and Vitter's 2003 construction generalizes rank/select from binary to arbitrary alphabets, storing a string in *nH₀ + o(n log σ)* bits while supporting *access*, *rank*, and *select* in *O(log σ)* time [3].
3. **The FM-index.** Ferragina and Manzini's 2000/2005 breakthrough fuses the Burrows–Wheeler transform with rank/select to build a self-index of size *5nHₖ + o(n)* bits—simultaneously for every *k*—that counts occurrences of a pattern *P* in *O(|P|)* time without decompressing the text [2][4].

The remainder of this thesis is organized as follows. Section 2 fixes notation and the entropy measures that calibrate every space bound. Section 3 describes the construction methodology common to all the structures. Section 4 develops each structure in depth. Section 5 states and sketches proofs of the key space–time theorems and compares the indexes quantitatively. Section 6 discusses limitations, and Section 7 concludes.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 Empirical entropy

Let *T[1..n]* be a string over Σ with |Σ| = σ. The *zero-order empirical entropy* is

> *H₀(T) = −Σ_{c∈Σ} (n_c/n) log(n_c/n)*,

where *n_c* is the frequency of *c*. *nH₀(T)* is the minimum bits achievable by any zero-order (memoryless) compressor. The *k-th order empirical entropy* *Hₖ(T)* averages *H₀* over the substrings following each length-*k* context; *Hₖ ≤ H₀* for *k > 0*, and *Hₖ* captures the compressibility exploited by PPM- and BWT-based compressors [2]. A bound expressed in terms of *nHₖ* is *opportunistic*: the structure shrinks automatically as the text becomes more compressible.

### 2.2 The Burrows–Wheeler transform

Burrows and Wheeler's 1994 report introduced a reversible permutation of *T* with a remarkable property: characters sharing similar right-contexts become adjacent, so that the transformed string is far easier to compress with simple local encoders [4]. Form the *n × n* matrix of cyclic rotations of *T$* (with *$* a sentinel smaller than all symbols), sort the rows lexicographically, and take the last column *L = BWT(T)*. The first column *F* is simply the sorted multiset of characters.

The transform is invertible because of the *LF-mapping*: the *i*-th occurrence of character *c* in *L* corresponds to the *i*-th occurrence of *c* in *F*. Formally, with *C[c]* = number of characters in *T* strictly smaller than *c*, and *Occ(c, i)* = number of occurrences of *c* in *L[1..i]*,

> **LF-mapping:** *LF(i) = C[L[i]] + Occ(L[i], i)*.

This identity is the entire foundation of the FM-index: it lets us *walk backward through the text* using only *L* and rank queries, never the text itself [2].

## 3. Methodology

All structures in this thesis are built by a common methodology: **reduce the problem to rank/select on bitvectors, then engineer rank/select to cost *o(n)* bits**. The pipeline is:

1. **Design the query in terms of primitives.** Counting in the FM-index becomes a sequence of *Occ* (i.e., rank) queries on *BWT(T)*; wavelet tree navigation becomes a descent through per-node bitvectors.
2. **Construct the two-level directory.** Partition each bitvector into superblocks of size *ℓ = log²n* and blocks of size *s = (log n)/2*; precompute cumulative ranks at both levels plus a universal lookup table for all *2^s* block patterns (Jacobson [1]).
3. **Boost to higher-order entropy.** Apply the wavelet tree to *BWT(T)* rather than *T*: because the BWT groups equal contexts, each node's bitvector is compressible to its zero-order entropy, and the sum over nodes telescopes to *nHₖ(T)* via the compression-boosting argument [3].
4. **Sample to locate.** A self-index counts with ranks alone; to *locate* occurrences, store every *(log^{1+ε} n)*-th suffix array entry explicitly and reconstruct the rest by iterated LF-steps [2].

Steps 1–2 yield succinctness; step 3 yields high-order compression; step 4 trades a logarithmic locate penalty for discarding the text.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Rank/Select Dictionaries: Jacobson's Construction

Given a bitvector *B[1..n]*, define *rank₁(i) = |{j ≤ i : B[j] = 1}|* and *select₁(j) = min{i : rank₁(i) = j}*. These two operations are the *assembly language* of succinct data structures: Jacobson showed that a binary tree's level-order bitvector plus rank/select supports parent/child navigation, and the same primitives implement permutations, sparse sets, and inverted indexes [1].

Jacobson's construction for *rank* uses two levels of directories:

- **Superblocks.** Partition *B* into blocks of *ℓ = ⌈log² n⌉* bits. Array *R_ℓ* stores, for each superblock, the cumulative number of 1s preceding it: *(n/ℓ)* entries of *⌈log n⌉* bits each, totaling *n/log n* bits.
- **Blocks.** Subdivide each superblock into blocks of *s = ⌈(log n)/2⌉* bits. Array *R_s* stores, per block, the number of 1s *relative to its superblock start*: *(n/s)* entries of *⌈log ℓ⌉ = 2 log log n* bits, totaling *O(n log log n / log n)* bits.
- **Lookup table.** *R_p* tabulates *rank* within every possible *s*-bit pattern at every offset: *2^s · s · log s = O(√n log n log log n)* bits.

A query decomposes as *rank₁(i) = R_ℓ[⌊i/ℓ⌋] + R_s[⌊i/s⌋] + R_p[B-block][i mod s]*, three array probes: *O(1)* time. Every table is *o(n)* bits, so the total is *n + o(n)* bits [1]. Later refinements by Clark and Munro reduced *select* to *O(1)* time within the same space budget, and Golynski tightened the redundancy to *(1 + o(1))(n log log n / log n)* bits [6].

> **Theorem (Jacobson [1]).** *There exists a representation of an n-bit vector using n + O(n log log n / log n) bits that answers rank₁ in O(1) time and select₁ in O(log n) time.*

```python
# Rank query over Jacobson's two-level directory (schematic)
def rank1(B, R_l, R_s, R_p, ell, s, i):
    return (R_l[i // ell]
            + R_s[i // s]
            + R_p[block_bits(B, i // s)][i % s])
```

The construction generalizes to *compressed* bitvectors (Raman, Raman, Rao) whose space tracks the binomial coefficient *⌈log(n choose m)⌉* for *m* ones—within a constant additive term of optimal—making rank/select *entropy-aware* rather than merely succinct [6].

### 4.2 Wavelet Trees: Rank/Select on General Alphabets

Bitvectors are not enough: the BWT of a text over Σ needs *rank_c* for every *c ∈ Σ*. Grossi, Gupta, and Vitter's wavelet tree reduces the general-alphabet problem to the binary one [3]. For a string *S[1..n]* over Σ, build a binary tree whose root splits Σ into two halves Σ₀, Σ₁; store at the root a bitvector *B* with *B[i] = 0* iff *S[i] ∈ Σ₀*; recurse on the two projected subsequences. Then:

- *access(i)*: follow bits down, *O(log σ)*.
- *rank_c(i)*: descend tracking the interval, summing via rank₀/rank₁ at each level, *O(log σ)*.
- *select_c(j)*: ascend from the leaf using select₀/select₁, *O(log σ)*.

The compression miracle: if each node's bitvector is stored in compressed (e.g., RRR) form, the sum of zero-order entropies across levels telescopes, giving total space *nH₀(S) + o(n log σ)* bits [3]. A *Huffman-shaped* variant (Mäkinen–Navarro) replaces the balanced tree by the Huffman tree of the symbol distribution, so that the total stored bits equal *n(H₀ + 1)* and average query time is *O(H₀ + 1)*—proportional to entropy itself [8]. The wavelet tree is now the standard substrate of virtually every compressed index [6][8].

| Structure | Space (bits) | rank | select | access |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Jacobson bitvector [1] | *n + o(n)* | *O(1)* | *O(log n)* | *O(1)* |
| Clark–Munro bitvector | *n + o(n)* | *O(1)* | *O(1)* | *O(1)* |
| Balanced wavelet tree [3] | *nH₀ + o(n log σ)* | *O(log σ)* | *O(log σ)* | *O(log σ)* |
| Huffman-shaped wavelet tree [8] | *n(H₀+1)* | *O(H₀+1)* avg | *O(H₀+1)* avg | *O(H₀+1)* avg |
| Wavelet matrix | *nH₀ + o(n log σ)* | *O(log σ)* | *O(log σ)* | *O(log σ)* |

### 4.3 The FM-Index: Backward Search via LF-Mapping

The FM-index stores *L = BWT(T)* in a wavelet tree and answers substring queries by *backward search* [2]. To count occurrences of *P[1..p]*, maintain the suffix-array interval *[sp, ep]* of rows prefixed by the processed suffix of *P*, initialized to *[1, n]*. Processing *P* right-to-left with character *c = P[i]*:

```rust
// Backward search: one step per pattern character
fn backward_step(c: u8, sp: usize, ep: usize, C: &[usize], wt: &WaveletTree) -> (usize, usize) {
    let sp2 = C[c as usize] + wt.rank(c, sp - 1) + 1;
    let ep2 = C[c as usize] + wt.rank(c, ep);
    (sp2, ep2) // empty iff sp2 > ep2: pattern absent
}
```

Each step is two rank queries—*O(log σ)* with a wavelet tree, *O(1)* with alphabet-partitioned bitvectors—so counting costs *O(p)* rank operations and never touches *T*. Why does it work? The LF-mapping guarantees that the rows of the BWT matrix prefixed by *c·X* are exactly the LF-images of the rows prefixed by *X* whose last character is *c*; the interval arithmetic above is the rank-space form of that correspondence [2].

To *locate* occurrences (report text positions) and *extract* substrings, the FM-index samples the suffix array: store *SA[i]* explicitly for every *(log^{1+ε} n)*-th row, then recover any position by iterating LF-steps until hitting a sample—at most *log^{1+ε} n* steps of *O(1)* each. The samples add *o(n)* bits. The text itself is discarded: the index *is* the compressed text, hence a *self-index* [2].

> **Theorem (Ferragina–Manzini [2]).** *For any text T[1..n], there exists a self-index occupying at most 5nHₖ(T) + o(n) bits, simultaneously for every k ≥ 0, that counts the occurrences of P[1..p] in O(p) time and locates each occurrence in O(log^{1+ε} n) time, for any fixed ε > 0.*

The "simultaneously for every *k*" clause is the opportunistic property: the index needs no tuning parameter, yet its space tracks the best *k*-th order model of the data [2].

### 4.4 The r-Index: Indexing Highly Repetitive Collections

Empirical entropy has a blind spot: on *highly repetitive* collections—thousands of human genomes, versioned source repositories—*nHₖ* barely shrinks while the data is intuitively tiny. The right measure is *r*, the number of *runs* in *BWT(T)*: on *n/r ≈ 500–10,000* (pangenomes), *r* can be three orders of magnitude smaller than *n* [5].

The run-length FM-index (RLFM-index) of Mäkinen and Navarro compresses *L* by runs in *O(r log n)* bits and counts in *O(p)*-ish time, but locating within *O(r)* space was open for nearly a decade [5]. Gagie, Navarro, and Prezza's *r-index* closed it. The key is the *toehold lemma* of Policriti and Prezza: during backward search, the BWT interval of *P* always contains a run boundary, so storing *O(r)* suffix-array samples—at run starts/ends in *L* and the corresponding *F* positions—suffices to locate *one* occurrence per interval; the rest are recovered by a predecessor structure over the samples in *O(log log_w(n/r))* time each [5].

> **Theorem (Gagie–Navarro–Prezza [5]).** *There exists an index of O(r) words that counts occurrences of P[1..m] in O(m log log_w(σ + n/r)) time and locates each in O(log log_w(n/r)) time, where r is the number of runs in BWT(T).*

On the Pizza&Chili repetitive corpora, the r-index reaches *0.06–0.2 bits per symbol*—an order of magnitude below entropy-bounded indexes—while locating orders of magnitude faster than Lempel–Ziv-based alternatives [5]. The measure *r* has since been shown to bound, and be bounded by, the other repetitiveness measures (attractor size γ, LZ parse size *z*) up to logarithmic factors, making it the canonical yardstick of repetitiveness-aware indexing [5].

## 5. Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 The space-bound hierarchy

The following table distills the proven bounds. "Self-index" indicates the text can be discarded.

| Index | Space (bits) | Count time | Locate / occurrence | Self-index |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Suffix array | *n log n* | *O(p log n)* | *O(1)* | No |
| FM-index [2] | *5nHₖ + o(n)*, ∀k | *O(p)* | *O(log^{1+ε} n)* | Yes |
| Alphabet-friendly FM-index [2] | *nHₖ + o(n log σ)* | *O(p log log σ)* | *O(log^{1+ε} n)* | Yes |
| GGV compressed suffix array [3] | *nHₖ + o(n log log n / log_σ n)* | *O(p + log^{ε} n)* | *O(log^{ε} n)* | Yes |
| RLFM-index | *O(r log n)* | *O(p log log_w(σ+n/r))* | unbounded in *O(r)* | Yes |
| r-index [5] | *O(r)* words | *O(p log log_w(σ+n/r))* | *O(log log_w(n/r))* | Yes |

### 5.2 Proof sketch: the FM-index bound

We sketch why the wavelet tree over *BWT(T)* achieves *nHₖ* space (compression boosting [2][3]). Partition *BWT(T)* into blocks by length-*k* context (these are exactly the contiguous intervals of rows sharing a prefix). Within block *j* of length *n_j*, store the symbols' bitvectors at zero-order entropy: *n_j H₀(block_j)* bits. Summing, *Σ_j n_j H₀(block_j) = nHₖ(T)* by definition of *Hₖ*. The wavelet tree's internal bitvectors at the root and internal nodes are compressible precisely because the BWT groups equal contexts—characters inside a context interval are skewed toward few symbols. Adding the *o(n)*-bit rank/select directories per bitvector and the *o(n)*-bit SA samples yields *5nHₖ + o(n)* in the original FM-index (constant 5 from the explicit table layout), improvable to *nHₖ + o(n log σ)* with a Huffman-shaped wavelet tree [2][8]. Crucially, the *partition is never materialized*: the wavelet tree over the whole BWT attains the boosted bound implicitly, so the guarantee holds for all *k* at once.

### 5.3 Lower-bound context

Any index supporting *rank* on a length-*n* bitvector needs *n − o(n)* bits in the worst case (the *n* bits of the vector are information-theoretically necessary), so Jacobson's *n + o(n)* is optimal up to lower-order terms [1]. For full-text indexes, Demaine and López-Ortiz proved that locating with *o(n)*-bit redundancy is impossible below certain query-time thresholds, situating the FM-index's logarithmic locate penalty as essentially unavoidable [2]. For repetitive collections, *r* is bounded above by *O(z log(n/z))* where *z* is the Lempel–Ziv parse size, and the r-index's *O(r)* space is optimal up to the log factor separating *r* from the string attractor size γ [5].

## 6. Limitations

1. **Construction cost.** Building the BWT for *n = 10¹¹* requires external-memory suffix array construction (e.g., SA-IS in external memory); in-memory construction peaks at several bytes per symbol. The succinct query-time story assumes a built index [6].
2. **Dynamic updates.** The structures above are *static*. Inserting a character can reshuffle Θ(n) BWT positions. Dynamic compressed indexes exist (Chan et al.; dynamic wavelet trees) but pay *O(log n / log log n)* per operation and larger redundancy [8].
3. **Alphabet dependence.** Naive bitvector-per-symbol FM-indexes cost *O(σ)* rank time; alphabet-friendly variants (wavelet tree, Golynski et al.'s multiary structures) restore *O(log log σ)* but complicate the *o(n)* analysis [2].
4. **Cache behavior.** *o(n)*-bit directories are information-theoretically tiny but pointer-chase across *log σ* tree levels; on real hardware a Huffman-shaped wavelet tree or the wavelet matrix often beats asymptotically superior variants due to sequential access patterns [6].
5. **Repetitiveness cliff.** Entropy-bounded indexes degrade on versioned collections where *r ≪ nHₖ*; the r-index fixes this but its *O(r)*-word space still exceeds *O(z)*-bit LZ-indexes on moderately repetitive data [5].

## 7. Conclusion

From Jacobson's two-level directory to the r-index, the field has pursued one invariant: *never pay more than the data's intrinsic complexity, plus lower-order terms, for full query power*. Rank/select dictionaries made bitvectors navigable in *n + o(n)* bits [1]; wavelet trees lifted the primitive to general alphabets at entropy bounds [3]; the FM-index turned the Burrows–Wheeler transform into a self-index whose space tracks *every* order of empirical entropy simultaneously [2][4]; and the r-index extended the program to repetitive collections where entropy itself is the wrong measure [5]. The practical payoff is concrete: modern read aligners (BWA, Bowtie) search billions of DNA bases from an index smaller than the genome, and pangenome indexes fit thousands of genomes in gigabytes. The open frontier—dynamic, cache-conscious, and learned succinct structures—continues the same program: making the index disappear into the compressed data.
