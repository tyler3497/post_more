# Sieve Methods and Bounded Gaps Between Primes: The Selberg Sieve, the Bombieri–Vinogradov Theorem, and the Polymath Project

## Abstract

For two thousand years the gaps between consecutive primes resisted uniform control: the twin-prime conjecture asserts that *H_1 = lim inf (p_{n+1} − p_n)* equals 2, yet until 2013 it was not even known whether *H_1* is finite. This thesis reconstructs the sieve-theoretic machinery that changed that. We begin with the classical sieves — Eratosthenes–Legendre, Brun, and Selberg — and the parity barrier that caps what any sieve can detect. We then develop the level-of-distribution philosophy: the Bombieri–Vinogradov theorem, large-sieve inequalities, and the Elliott–Halberstam conjecture, and show how the Goldston–Pintz–Yıldırım weighting scheme converts equidistribution of primes in arithmetic progressions into small gaps. The core of the thesis is the modern revolution: Zhang's 2013 proof that *H_1* is finite via smooth-moduli dispersion estimates resting on Deligne's proof of the Riemann hypothesis over finite fields; the Maynard–Tao multidimensional sieve, which collapsed the bound from seventy million to 600; and the Polymath8 collaboration, which drove the unconditional bound to *H_1 ≤ 246* — and to 6 under a generalized Elliott–Halberstam hypothesis, provably the sieve-theoretic optimum.

## 1 Intro

The distribution of the primes is governed, at large scales, by the prime number theorem: *π(x) ~ x / log x*, so the *average* gap between consecutive primes near *x* is about *log x*. But the extremal behavior of gaps is far subtler. The twin-prime conjecture — in the general form stated by de Polignac in 1849 — asserts that the least gap occurring infinitely often,

*H_1 = lim inf_{n→∞} (p_{n+1} − p_n)*,

equals 2 [1]. For over a century it was not even known whether *H_1* is finite — whether prime gaps remain bounded infinitely often.

The weapon of choice has always been **sieve theory**: the art of estimating, from above or below, the number of integers surviving the removal of prescribed residue classes modulo small primes. Brun's 1919 combinatorial sieve proved that the sum of the reciprocals of twin primes converges — the primes thin out too quickly for naive inclusion–exclusion, and only a truncated, approximate sieve can see them [6]. Selberg's sieve of the 1940s replaced combinatorics with optimization, producing nearly optimal upper bounds — but subject to the **parity problem**, a fundamental obstruction showing that no sieve can distinguish integers with an odd number of prime factors from those with an even number [6].

A second ingredient lay half-dormant: the *level of distribution* of primes in arithmetic progressions. The Bombieri–Vinogradov theorem gives level *1/2* on average [3], while the Elliott–Halberstam conjecture predicts level *1 − ε* for every *ε > 0*. In 2005, Goldston, Pintz, and Yıldırım (GPY) fused these threads: a Selberg-style weighted sieve whose success is essentially *equivalent* to a level of distribution exceeding *1/2*, proving unconditionally that prime gaps are *o(log p_n)*, and conditionally that *H_1 ≤ 16* under Elliott–Halberstam [7].

Then came the deluge. In 2013 Yitang Zhang proved a weakened Elliott–Halberstam statement at level *1/2 + 1/584* for smooth moduli, and with it *H_1 < 7 × 10^7* — the first finite bound in history [1]. Within months, James Maynard (and independently Terence Tao) replaced the one-dimensional GPY weights by a **multidimensional** sieve, collapsing the bound to *H_1 ≤ 600* using only Bombieri–Vinogradov [2]. The Polymath8 project — a massive open collaboration — then optimized every parameter, reaching *H_1 ≤ 246* unconditionally, *H_1 ≤ 12* under Elliott–Halberstam, and *H_1 ≤ 6* under a generalized Elliott–Halberstam hypothesis, the last being provably best possible for sieve methods [4][5].

This thesis tells that story with full technical detail: the classical sieves (§2), the general weighted-sieve method (§3), the four deep ideas that made bounded gaps possible (§4), the proofs and numerical evidence (§5), and the barriers that remain (§6).

## 2 Background

### The sieve problem

A sieve problem starts with a finite sequence *A* of integers, a set of *sifting primes* *P*, and for each *p ∈ P* a set *Ω(p)* of forbidden residue classes modulo *p*. The sifted set is

*S(A, z) = { n ∈ A : n mod p ∉ Ω(p) for all p < z }*,

and the task is to estimate its cardinality. For twin primes, *A* runs over integers up to *x* with *Ω(p) = {0, −2}* [6].

- **Eratosthenes–Legendre.** Inclusion–exclusion gives the exact formula *S(A,z) = Σ_{d|P(z)} μ(d) |A_d|*, where *A_d* counts elements of *A* divisible by *d*. Writing *|A_d| = X·ω(d)/d + r_d*, the remainder accumulates over *2^{π(z)}* divisors — far too many — so the Legendre sieve is useless beyond *z* about *log x* [6].

- **Brun's combinatorial sieve.** Brun's insight (1919) was to *truncate* inclusion–exclusion — stopping after an even number of terms undercounts, after an odd number overcounts — keeping the error controlled while the main term survives. The payoff was historic: the sum of the reciprocals of twin primes converges, and the count of twin primes satisfies *π_2(x) ≪ x (log log x)^2 / (log x)^2* [6].

- **Selberg's upper-bound sieve.** Selberg replaced the *±1* coefficients of inclusion–exclusion by arbitrary real weights *λ_d* with *λ_1 = 1*, exploiting the trivial-but-magical inequality

*(Σ_{d|n} λ_d)^2 ≥ 1* whenever *n* survives sifting,

since the inner sum equals *λ_1 = 1* for such *n*. Expanding the square and minimizing the resulting quadratic form gives *S(A, z) ≤ X / J + Σ_{d < z^2} 3^{ω(d)} |r_d|*, with the sieve determinant *J = Σ_{d<z} μ^2(d)/f(d)* — the direct ancestor of every weight in the bounded-gaps story [6].

> **Lemma (Large-sieve inequality).** *For arbitrary complex coefficients a_n,*
> *Σ_{q≤Q} Σ_{(a,q)=1} |Σ_{n≤N} a_n e(an/q)|^2 ≤ (N + Q^2) Σ_{n≤N} |a_n|^2.*
> *This analytic form of the large sieve — originating with Linnik — is the engine behind the Bombieri–Vinogradov theorem [3].*

- **The parity problem.** Selberg observed a devastating limitation: sieve weights are *blind to parity*. A sieve upper bound for *π(x)* exceeds the truth by a factor asymptotic to 2, because the sieve cannot distinguish primes (one prime factor) from products of two primes. Chen's 1973 theorem — infinitely many primes *p* with *p + 2* prime or a product of two primes — represents essentially the limit of what pure sieve theory can prove about prime pairs [6].

### Equidistribution: level of distribution

For *(a, q) = 1*, write *ψ(x; q, a)* for the Chebyshev count of prime powers in the progression *a mod q*. The primes are said to have **level of distribution** *θ* if, for every *A > 0*,

*Σ_{q ≤ x^θ/(log x)^C} max_{(a,q)=1} |ψ(x; q, a) − x/φ(q)| ≪_A x/(log x)^A*

for suitable *C = C(A)*. The prime number theorem for arithmetic progressions gives any *θ < 1* for *fixed* moduli; the content is uniformity in *q*.

> **Theorem (Bombieri–Vinogradov, 1965).** *The primes have level of distribution θ = 1/2. The proof combines the large sieve with Vaughan's identity and bilinear (Type I/II) decompositions of the von Mangoldt function [3].*

The **Elliott–Halberstam conjecture** asserts level *θ = 1 − ε* for every *ε > 0* — far beyond current technology, and not even implied by the Generalized Riemann Hypothesis. Its *generalized* form (GEH) extends the claim to convolutions *α ∗ β*, which is what the multidimensional sieve actually needs [4][5].

### The GPY breakthrough

Goldston, Pintz, and Yıldırım attacked small gaps with *Selberg-style weights on prime tuples*: for admissible *H = {h_1, …, h_k}* they formed

*w_n = ( Σ_{d|P(n;H)} μ(d) (log(R/d)/log R)^{k+l} )^2*,

with *P(n;H) = ∏_i (n + h_i)*, studying *S = S_2 − ρS_1*; positivity for large *N* forces some *n* to make more than *ρ* of the shifted values prime. The error terms demand level of distribution *θ* with *R = x^{θ/2}*. GPY proved unconditionally that *lim inf (p_{n+1} − p_n)/log p_n = 0*, and that *θ > 1/2* implies *bounded* gaps — with *H_1 ≤ 16* under full Elliott–Halberstam [7]. Crucially, Soundararajan showed the converse for these weights: the GPY method *cannot* succeed at *θ = 1/2*; it intrinsically requires a level exceeding one half [7]. Bounded gaps therefore demanded either a new equidistribution theorem or a new sieve.

## 3 Methodology

The modern attack on bounded gaps is a pipeline with five stages. We describe it in the Maynard–Tao form, which contains GPY and Zhang as special cases [2][4].

1. **Choose an admissible tuple.** Fix *H = {h_1, …, h_k}* admissible, of *diameter* *diam(H) = max h_i − min h_i*. Narrow tuples give small gaps; the narrowest admissible *k*-tuple has diameter *H(k)* satisfying *(1/2 + o(1)) k log k ≤ H(k) ≤ (1 + o(1)) k log k* [4]. Admissibility is a finite check, illustrated below.

2. **Build multidimensional weights.** With *R = N^{θ/2 − ε}* and a smooth *F* supported on the simplex *{x_i ≥ 0 : Σ x_i ≤ 1}*, set

   *w_n = ( Σ_{d_1,…,d_k : d_i | n+h_i} (∏ μ(d_i)) F(log d_1/log R, …, log d_k/log R) )^2*.

   For *k = 1* this is Selberg's sieve; GPY uses *F* depending only on *Σ x_i*. The *k*-dimensional freedom is Maynard's decisive innovation [2].

3. **Evaluate the main terms.** A multidimensional Selberg-sieve computation gives

   *S_1 = (1 + o(1)) · φ(W)^k/W^{k+1} · N (log R)^k I_k(F)*,

   *S_2^{(m)} = (1 + o(1)) · φ(W)^k/W^{k+1} · N (log R)^{k+1}/log N · J_k^{(m)}(F)*,

   with *I_k(F)*, *J_k^{(m)}(F)* explicit integrals of *F* over the simplex [2].

4. **Reduce to an optimization problem.** Since *S = Σ_m S_2^{(m)} − ρ log N · S_1*, positivity for large *N* follows from

   *(θ/2) · M_k > ρ, where M_k = sup_F ( Σ_m J_k^{(m)}(F) ) / I_k(F)*.

   For two primes (*ρ = 1*) at *θ = 1/2*, one needs *M_k > 4*. The analytic problem thus collapses to a *variational* problem over smooth functions on a simplex [2][4].

5. **Feed in arithmetic information.** The error terms in stage 3 are controlled by whatever level of distribution is available: Bombieri–Vinogradov (*θ = 1/2*) suffices for Maynard–Tao; Zhang's smooth-moduli theorem supplies *θ = 1/2 + 1/584* for the GPY weights [1][3].

The table below compares the four generations of the method.

| Method | Sieve weights | Level *θ* | Tuple size *k* | Bound on *H_1* |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| GPY (2005) | 1-dim Selberg, *F(Σx_i)* | *> 1/2* needed | large | *o(log p_n)*; *≤ 16* under EH |
| Zhang (2013) | smoothed GPY, smooth moduli | *1/2 + 1/584* | *3.5 × 10^6* | *< 7 × 10^7* |
| Maynard–Tao (2013) | *k*-dim Selberg | *1/2* (BV) | *105* | *≤ 600* |
| Polymath8b (2014) | *k*-dim, optimized basis | *1/2* (BV) | *50* | *≤ 246*; *≤ 6* under GEH |

Admissibility — the combinatorial gatekeeper of the whole method — is checked as follows:

```haskell
import Data.List (nub)

primes :: [Integer]
primes = sieve [2..]
  where sieve (p:xs) = p : sieve [x | x <- xs, x `mod` p /= 0]

-- A tuple is admissible iff no prime's residue classes are all covered.
admissible :: [Integer] -> Bool
admissible hs = all ok (takeWhile (<= maximum hs) primes)
  where ok p = length (nub (map (`mod` p) hs)) < fromIntegral p

main :: IO ()
main = do
  print (admissible [0, 2, 6])   -- True:  diameter 6, the GEH-optimal triple
  print (admissible [0, 2, 4])   -- False: covers every class mod 3
  print (admissible [0, 2, 6, 8, 12])  -- True: diameter 12, EH bound witness
```

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Selberg sieve and the parity barrier

Selberg's construction is best understood as *variational*: among all weight sequences with *λ_1 = 1*, the one minimizing the quadratic form *Σ_{d_1,d_2} λ_{d_1} λ_{d_2} ω(l)/l* with *l = lcm(d_1, d_2)* gives the smallest upper bound, and the minimum has the closed form *1/J* by diagonalization. The remainder is controlled when the *level* *z^2* stays within the range where the *r_d* are small on average — the level-of-distribution phenomenon of §4.2 [6].

The *parity problem* is the statement that this optimization is *intrinsically* blind: the weights *(Σ λ_d)^2* majorize the indicator of sifted integers, but they majorize almost-primes almost as well. Concretely, applying Selberg's sieve to *π(x)* yields an upper bound asymptotic to *2x/log x* — twice the truth — and *no* choice of weights can repair the factor of 2, because the sieve cannot correlate with the Liouville function. Every subsequent breakthrough can be read as a strategy for *smuggling parity information* into the weights — via progressions (GPY), dispersion (Zhang), or multidimensional concentration (Maynard–Tao).

### 4.2 Level of distribution and Zhang's breakthrough

The Bombieri–Vinogradov theorem says the primes are equidistributed modulo *q* on average for *q* up to *x^{1/2}*, and GPY needed just past *1/2*. Zhang's radical idea: *restrict the moduli to be smooth* — all prime factors below *x^δ* with *δ = 1/1168* — and prove a level *1/2 + 2δ* for *those* moduli, while smoothing the GPY sieve weights to match [1].

The proof is a *dispersion method* argument. After Cauchy–Schwarz and Poisson summation, the error reduces to trilinear character sums — the **Type I**, **Type II**, and **Type III** estimates — over moduli with a smooth factorization *q = q_1 q_2 q_3*. The deepest input is a three-variable character sum bound of Birch–Bombieri resting on Deligne's Riemann hypothesis over finite fields, which supplies the needed square-root cancellation [1][3]. With *k_0 = 3.5 × 10^6* and *l_0 = 180*, the GPY ratio computation then yields *H_1 < 7 × 10^7* — the first finite bound ever [1].

Polymath8a pushed Zhang's architecture to its limit — refined Type I/II/III estimates and the exponent *7/300* — lowering the bound to *H ≤ 4680* [3]; the GPY weights were now essentially exhausted.

### 4.3 The Maynard–Tao multidimensional sieve

Maynard's observation: the GPY weights use only the *sum* of the logarithmic divisor sizes, but the sieve sum naturally lives in *k dimensions*; letting *F* depend on the full vector gives the variational problem for *M_k* enormously more freedom. With a basis of monomials (later, piecewise-polynomial and Bessel-type bases), Maynard proved *M_105 > 4* — hence *H_1 ≤ 600* from the narrowest admissible *105*-tuple — using **only** Bombieri–Vinogradov [2]. Tao arrived independently at the same framework with slightly weaker numerics. The method also yields *H_m ≪ m^3 e^{4m}*: bounded gaps between *m*-tuples of primes for every *m*, a qualitative leap beyond GPY [2].

The key analytic point is *why* multidimensionality helps: the ratio *Σ_m J_k^{(m)}(F)/I_k(F)* measures how well the weights *concentrate* on integers where many of the *n + h_i* are simultaneously prime. One-dimensional weights spread mass thinly across the simplex diagonal; *k*-dimensional weights pile mass where several coordinates are simultaneously small — exactly the event that several shifts are prime [2][4].

### 4.4 Polymath8: optimization to 246 and the parity optimum

The Polymath8 project split into two phases. **Polymath8a** refined Zhang-type equidistribution for smooth moduli, reaching level *1/2 + 7/300* [3]. Polymath8b then abandoned Zhang's estimates — Maynard's weights needed only *θ = 1/2* — and attacked the variational problem with industrial-scale numerics: larger bases, the *"epsilon trick"* of enlarged simplex support, and optimized tuples. The result was *M_50 > 4*, giving the unconditional bound *H_1 ≤ 246* from the narrowest admissible *50*-tuple of diameter 246 [4].

Under Elliott–Halberstam (*θ → 1*), the threshold drops to *M_k > 2*, and Polymath8b obtained *H_1 ≤ 12* (Maynard had earlier reached 12 as well, improving GPY's 16) [4]. Under the **generalized** Elliott–Halberstam hypothesis, the threshold drops to the point where an admissible *triple* suffices — and every admissible triple has diameter at least 6 (e.g. *{0, 2, 6}*), yielding *H_1 ≤ 6* [4].

> **Theorem (Polymath8b parity optimality).** *The bound H_1 ≤ 6 is the best that can be obtained from purely sieve-theoretic considerations, even assuming GEH. A modified Selberg parity argument shows that sieve weights alone cannot force two primes into any shorter pattern [4].*

The project also produced *H_m ≪ m e^{(4 − 28/157)m}* and explicit bounds for *H_2* through *H_5*, plus a retrospective on which ingredients were essential [4][5]. A 2025 refinement by Stadlmann combined Bombieri–Vinogradov with newer smooth-moduli estimates to reach *H_1 ≤ 240*, the current record [7].

## 5 Empirical/Proofs

We isolate the proof-theoretic core shared by all four generations: a positivity criterion converting sieve estimates into prime patterns.

> **Theorem (GPY positivity criterion).** *Let H be admissible and w_n ≥ 0 sieve weights. Put S_1 = Σ_{N≤n≤2N} w_n and S_2 = Σ_{N≤n≤2N} (Σ_i θ(n+h_i)) w_n with θ the Chebyshev weight. If S_2 − ρ log(3N)·S_1 > 0 for arbitrarily large N, then there are infinitely many n such that at least ⌊ρ⌋ + 1 of the values n + h_i are prime.*

> **Proof.** *If at most ⌊ρ⌋ of the n + h_i were prime for every large n, each inner sum Σ_i θ(n+h_i) would be at most ⌊ρ⌋·log(3N) ≤ ρ log(3N), forcing S_2 − ρ log(3N) S_1 ≤ 0 — a contradiction. The nonnegativity of w_n is essential: it prevents cancellation from hiding a positive contribution [2][4].*

The historical collapse of the bound is summarized below.

| Year | Result | Bound on *H_1* | Key ingredient |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2005 | GPY | *o(log p_n)*; *≤ 16* under EH | Selberg-style tuple weights [7] |
| 2013 | Zhang | *< 7 × 10^7* | smooth-moduli dispersion, Deligne [1] |
| 2013 | Trudgian | *≤ 59,874,594* | optimized admissible tuple |
| 2013 | Pintz | *≤ 2,530,338* | refined GPY analysis |
| 2014 | Polymath8a | *≤ 4,680* | Zhang-type estimates to *1/2 + 7/300* [3] |
| 2013 | Maynard–Tao | *≤ 600* | multidimensional sieve, BV only [2] |
| 2014 | Polymath8b | *≤ 246*; *≤ 6* under GEH | optimized *M_k*, parity analysis [4] |
| 2025 | Stadlmann | *≤ 240* | BV plus smooth-moduli estimates [7] |

On the empirical side, the bound *246* is visible long before asymptotics take over. The following script sieves primes to five million and measures the gap distribution directly:

```python
def prime_sieve(limit: int) -> bytearray:
    """Bytearray sieve of Eratosthenes: nonzero marks prime."""
    is_prime = bytearray(b"\x01") * (limit + 1)
    is_prime[0:2] = b"\x00\x00"
    p = 2
    while p * p <= limit:
        if is_prime[p]:
            start, step = p * p, p
            is_prime[start:limit + 1:step] = b"\x00" * ((limit - start) // step + 1)
        p += 1
    return is_prime

def gap_statistics(limit: int):
    is_prime = prime_sieve(limit)
    primes = [i for i, v in enumerate(is_prime) if v]
    gaps = [b - a for a, b in zip(primes, primes[1:])]
    small = sum(1 for g in gaps if g <= 246)
    return {
        "primes_found": len(primes),
        "max_gap": max(gaps),
        "gaps_leq_246": small,
        "fraction_leq_246": small / len(gaps),
        "twin_pairs": sum(1 for g in gaps if g == 2),
    }

if __name__ == "__main__":
    for key, value in gap_statistics(5_000_000).items():
        print(f"{key}: {value}")
```

Running it gives *primes_found: 348513*, *max_gap: 154*, *fraction_leq_246: 1.0*, and *twin_pairs: 32463*: every one of the 348,512 gaps below five million is already at most 154 — far inside the proven bound of 246. The theorem's content is not that small gaps *occur* (computation sees them immediately) but that they occur *infinitely often*, which no finite computation can establish.

## 6 Limitations

- **The parity wall is absolute for sieves.** Polymath8b's modified parity argument shows *H_1 ≤ 6* is the finest conclusion any sieve argument can draw, even under GEH [4]. Twin primes (*H_1 = 2*) therefore require a genuinely *parity-breaking* idea — a method that correlates with the Möbius or Liouville function — of which none is currently known.
- **Elliott–Halberstam remains open.** Sixty years after Bombieri–Vinogradov, level *1/2* for *all* moduli is still the unconditional record; even the Generalized Riemann Hypothesis does not imply EH. Zhang's and Polymath8a's excursions past *1/2* work only for *smooth* moduli, and the exponents (*1/584*, *7/300*) are tiny [1][3].
- **GPY weights are provably stuck.** Soundararajan's analysis shows the one-dimensional GPY method *needs* *θ > 1/2* — Bombieri–Vinogradov alone can never suffice for it [7]. Maynard's multidimensionality was not a luxury but a necessity.
- **Optimization has diminishing returns.** The jump from *M_105 > 4* to *M_50 > 4* consumed Polymath8b's full numerical arsenal; the unconditional bound appears close to its sieve-theoretic floor [4][5].
- **Effectivity and uniformity.** The error terms are ineffective in the tuple parameters; the theorems guarantee infinitely many bounded gaps but locate none of them.

## 7 Conclusion

In a single decade, sieve theory traveled from *not knowing whether prime gaps are ever bounded* to a proven unconditional bound of 246 — and a conditional bound of 6 that is provably optimal for the method. The arc has a clear moral: **weights** (Selberg → GPY → Maynard–Tao) and **equidistribution** (Bombieri–Vinogradov → Zhang's dispersion → Polymath8a) are the two independent axes of progress, and bounded gaps required advancing both. The remaining step — from 6 to 2 — is not a matter of sharper estimates or bigger computers. It is blocked by the parity problem, the oldest and deepest limitation in sieve theory, and overcoming it will require mathematics that does not yet exist [4][6].
