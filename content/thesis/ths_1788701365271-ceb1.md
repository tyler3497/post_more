# Silicon Roots of Trust: Caliptra and OpenTitan — DICE Identity Hierarchies, Measured Boot Chains, SPDM Attestation, and Hardware-Anchored Supply Chain Security

## Abstract

Modern confidential computing rests on a fragile premise: that the lowest layer of silicon can be trusted, yet evaluated, by independent parties. This thesis presents a comparative architectural analysis of the two leading open silicon roots of trust — Caliptra, the reusable RTL root-of-trust block co-founded by AMD, Google, Microsoft, and NVIDIA under OCP and CHIPS Alliance stewardship, and OpenTitan, the first open-source silicon RoT to reach commercial availability under lowRISC with validated chips in hand — through the unifying formalism of TCG's Device Identifier Composition Engine (DICE). We formalize DICE's layered identity derivation (UDS → CDI trees) and measured-boot chains, map them onto Caliptra's immutable-ROM boot flow and OpenTitan's secure-boot implementation, and extend the trust perimeter across component boundaries using DMTF's Security Protocol and Data Model (SPDM) for device authentication, measurement collection, and attestation. Drawing on published specifications, RTL implementations, and cryptographic accelerator characterization data, we evaluate attestation latency, silicon cost, and supply-chain transparency, and argue that open silicon RoTs transform supply-chain security from blind trust into verifiable, evidence-based assurance.

---

## 1. Introduction

Every chain of trust has a weakest first link — historically a proprietary microcontroller, a closed firmware blob, or a TPM whose internals are protected by trade secrecy rather than mathematics. The industry's response to supply-chain attacks, from counterfeit network hardware to firmware implants in data-center servers, has been to *harden the anchor*: move the root of trust (RoT) into silicon, make its behavior deterministic, and make its design *inspectable* [8].

Two open-silicon efforts now define this frontier from complementary directions:

- **Caliptra** — a reusable, drop-in RTL IP block for a complete silicon RoT (identity, measurement, attestation, secure boot), from AMD, Google, Microsoft, and NVIDIA, developed openly under CHIPS Alliance and OCP [1].
- **OpenTitan** — a discrete, taped-out silicon RoT SoC and the first commercial open-source silicon project, built on the lowRISC Ibex RISC-V core with hardware crypto accelerators, OTP lifecycle management, and secure boot [2][8].

Both converge on the same cryptographic skeleton — the TCG's **Device Identifier Composition Engine (DICE)**, deriving layered identities from a hardware-protected *Unique Device Secret* (UDS) plus firmware measurements [4] — and both face the same boundary problem: how one chip's RoT *convinces* a verifier on another chip. The industry answer is **SPDM** (DMTF DSP0274): standardized device authentication, measurement collection, and session establishment [6].

This thesis contributes:

1. A formal treatment of DICE layered identity derivation as a *measurement-bound key-derivation tree*, with security reductions stated as theorems.
2. A stage-by-stage mapping of measured boot onto Caliptra's and OpenTitan's boot flows, identifying where each design binds measurements into attestation evidence.
3. An analysis of SPDM as the cross-component attestation transport, including its message choreography, certificate model, and DICE evidence bindings.
4. A comparative evaluation of Caliptra vs. OpenTitan across identity, measurement, attestation, renewability, and supply-chain transparency axes.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 Roots of trust: definitions and the trust chain

A *root of trust* is a set of functions, always trusted by the platform's security policy, that anchor all subsequent trust decisions. The classic decomposition distinguishes three roots:

| Root | Role | Typical realization |
|------|------|---------------------|
| **RTM** (Root of Trust for Measurement) | Measures code/data and extends results into protected registers | DICE engine, immutable ROM, TPM `Extend` |
| **RTS** (Root of Trust for Storage) | Protects keys and sealed data at rest | OTP/eFuse, wrapped key hierarchies |
| **RTR** (Root of Trust for Reporting) | Signs evidence for external verifiers | Attestation keys, embedded CA |

The *chain of trust* generalizes: if layer *i* verifies layer *i+1* before transferring control, trust transitively extends — provided verification is *measured* rather than merely asserted [2].

### 2.2 TCG DICE: identity as a function of measurement

DICE replaces static, factory-provisioned device certificates with *computed* identities. The hardware RoT holds a **Unique Device Secret (UDS)**, provisioned once (ideally by the silicon vendor in a trusted facility) and thereafter accessible only to the first mutable code (FMC) through a hardware-gated latch [4]. At reset:

1. The DICE engine measures the next firmware layer, producing a **TCB Component Identifier (TCI)** — typically a cryptographic digest of the firmware image.
2. It computes the **Compound Device Identifier**: `CDI = OWF(UDS, TCI)`, where OWF is a one-way function (in practice, HMAC or a KDF over SHA-256/SHA-512).
3. The CDI is handed to the next layer, which derives its own keys and repeats the process: `CDI_{i+1} = KDF(CDI_i, TCI_{i+1})` [4].

The consequence: **identity is a pure function of (hardware secret, firmware measurements)**. Patch vulnerable firmware and all derived keys automatically re-key — compromise is *self-healing* under update [5].

> **Theorem (DICE update re-keying):** With KDF a pseudorandom function, updating layer *k* (`TCI_k → TCI'_k`) makes every `CDI_n` (n ≥ k) pseudorandom and independent of its predecessor — invalidating all keys derived from it.

DICE also defines a certificate discipline: each layer's embedded CA issues X.509 certificates binding the next layer's attestation key to TCG extensions carrying TCI and SVN evidence, forming a manufacturer-rooted chain [10].

### 2.3 Measured boot vs. secure boot

*Secure boot* enforces policy: boot only code whose signature verifies against an authorized key. *Measured boot* records evidence: hash each stage into an append-only register before executing it, whether or not it was authorized. Caliptra and OpenTitan implement **both**, because they serve different relying parties: secure boot protects the *device owner* at boot time; measured boot produces evidence for *remote verifiers* long after boot completes [1][2].

### 2.4 SPDM: attestation across the PCIe boundary

A server is not one chip: GPUs, NICs, SSDs, and BMCs each carry firmware, each a potential implant vector. DMTF **SPDM** (DSP0274) defines how a *requester* (host CPU, BMC) authenticates a *responder* (accelerator) and collects signed measurements over PCIe DOE, MCTP, or TCP [6][7]; SPDM 1.x adds `KEY_EXCHANGE`/`PSK_EXCHANGE` to bootstrap encrypted sessions from attestation [7].

### 2.5 The open-silicon RoT landscape

| Property | Caliptra | OpenTitan |
|----------|----------|-----------|
| Form | Reusable RTL IP block integrated into host SoCs | Discrete SoC (Earl Grey taped out 2023) / embeddable (Darjeeling, Integrated) [2][9] |
| Founders / stewards | AMD, Google, Microsoft, NVIDIA; OCP + CHIPS Alliance [1] | Google, lowRISC + 9-member coalition; ETH Zurich, Nuvoton, Western Digital, G+D… [8] |
| CPU | VeeR (CHIPS Alliance RISC-V core, Apache 2.0) [1] | lowRISC Ibex RISC-V |
| Identity basis | DICE-style CDI hierarchy, IDevID/LDevID | DICE-style key hierarchy, secure OTP |
| Attestation northbound | SPDM, mailbox APIs, SoC manifest services | SPDM-capable firmware, owner-facing attestation |
| Commercial status | Spec 1.x published; industry adoption in cloud silicon | Validated chips in hand — first commercial open silicon [8] |

## 3. Methodology

This thesis is an *architectural-analytic* study, not an RTL implementation report. Our method has four components:

1. **Primary sources.** The Caliptra hardware spec and open RTL/firmware [1]; OpenTitan documentation and characterization literature [2][3]; the TCG DICE family [4][10]; DMTF SPDM DSP0274 [6] and DSP2058 [7].
2. **Formal modeling.** DICE derivation as a key-derivation tree; measured boot as an append-only measurement log bound to derived keys; SPDM as a challenge–response protocol reducing to signature unforgeability plus nonce freshness.
3. **Comparative axes.** *Identity strength*, *measurement coverage*, *attestation expressiveness*, *renewability*, and *supply-chain transparency* for each design.
4. **Threat model.** A supply-chain adversary capable of firmware substitution, and a physical adversary with interposer access at the edge [1]; decapsulation-grade invasive attacks are out of scope.

Performance figures come from peer-reviewed characterization (notably 4.3×–12.5× OpenTitan accelerator speedups [3]); Caliptra claims are specification-derived where silicon data remains under NDA.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 DICE Identity Hierarchies: From UDS to CDI Trees

DICE is a *deterministic identity tree* rooted in silicon [4]: the UDS is latched so first mutable code sees only a derived CDI, never the UDS itself:

```python
import hashlib, hmac

def kdf(secret: bytes, label: bytes, context: bytes, out_len: int = 32) -> bytes:
    """NIST SP 800-108-style counter-mode KDF over HMAC-SHA256 (simplified)."""
    out = b""
    ctr = 1
    while len(out) < out_len:
        out += hmac.new(secret, ctr.to_bytes(4, "big") + label + context,
                        hashlib.sha256).digest()
        ctr += 1
    return out[:out_len]

def dice_boot(uds: bytes, firmware_images: list[bytes]) -> list[bytes]:
    """Simulate DICE layered derivation across a boot chain."""
    cdis, prev = [], uds
    for i, fw in enumerate(firmware_images):
        tci = hashlib.sha256(fw).digest()          # TCB Component Identifier
        cdi = kdf(prev, b"CDI", tci)              # CDI_{i} = KDF(CDI_{i-1}, TCI_i)
        cdis.append(cdi)
        prev = cdi
    return cdis

# Any firmware change avalanches through every downstream identity:
uds = bytes.fromhex("aa" * 32)
chain_a = dice_boot(uds, [b"rom", b"runtime-v1", b"app"])
chain_b = dice_boot(uds, [b"rom", b"runtime-v2", b"app"])
assert chain_a[0] == chain_b[0]      # ROM layer identical
assert chain_a[1] != chain_b[1]      # runtime re-keys...
assert chain_a[2] != chain_b[2]      # ...and everything above it
print("layer-0 CDI match:", chain_a[0] == chain_b[0])
print("layer-2 CDI differs:", chain_a[2] != chain_b[2])
```

From each `CDI_i`, a layer derives two key families with domain separation [4][10]:

- **DeviceID keys** — stable across updates of that layer, for long-lived device identity (IDevID analog).
- **Alias keys** — bound to the exact firmware measurement; they change on every update, letting verifiers detect drift.

Each layer also acts as an **embedded CA**, issuing X.509 certificates for the next layer's alias key with TCG extensions carrying TCI, SVN, and serial [10] — a manufacturer-rooted path (root CA → embedded CA(s) → alias leaf) that names the exact firmware behind each signature.

> **Theorem (CDI one-wayness):** If the derivation is a PRF keyed by the secret input, no `CDI_i` leaks the UDS or any ancestor secret — even to an adversary controlling all firmware at layers ≥ i. *Proof sketch:* PRF security at each layer, with fresh context per derivation [4].

**Caliptra** instantiates this in immutable ROM: on reset it latches UDS-derived seeds, measures the runtime image in mailbox SRAM, derives the CDI, and generates the IDevID/LDevID and attestation hierarchy before jumping to runtime firmware — which can never again read the UDS [1]. **OpenTitan** mirrors this across mask ROM → ROM_EXT → owner firmware, its keymgr advancing through *Creator → OwnerIntermediate → Owner* stages, each binding the next software digest [2].

### 4.2 Measured Boot Chains: Extending Trust Layer by Layer

A measured boot chain is a *hash chain with hardware-protected head*. Each stage, before transferring control, extends a digest of the next stage into a register that supports only the extend operation `R ← H(R ∥ digest)`:

| Stage | Caliptra | OpenTitan | What is measured |
|-------|----------|-----------|------------------|
| 0 — Immutable | Caliptra ROM (mask ROM) | Mask ROM (ROM stage 1) | Nothing (trust anchor; verified by design review) |
| 1 — Mutable platform FW | Runtime FW image (signed, anti-rollback SVN) | ROM_EXT (silicon-owner signed) | FW image digest → CDI/FMC measurement |
| 2 — SoC FW | SoC manifest / image | BL0 / owner kernel | SoC FW digest, config straps, debug state |
| 3 — Runtime | Workload FW via mailbox | Owner applications | Per-component digests, TCB SVN |

Three design decisions determine the strength of such chains:

1. **Measurement scope.** Caliptra measures the full runtime image plus the SoC manifest; OpenTitan's keymgr additionally binds *lifecycle state*, so `DEV` vs. `PROD` devices derive different keys from identical firmware [2].
2. **Rollback protection.** Both bind a *security version number* (SVN) into derived keys and evidence, letting verifiers reject firmware below a policy floor.
3. **Recovery.** Caliptra updates in-field via its mailbox interface; OpenTitan via ROM_EXT/owner updates with ownership-transfer ceremonies. The §4.1 re-keying theorem makes recovery *cryptographically clean*: post-update keys are independent of pre-update keys [4].

> **Theorem (Measurement binding):** If each stage is measured by its predecessor and the alias key derives from the terminal CDI, then an alias-key signature proves the *entire measurement log* — forging evidence for F′ while running F requires breaking second-preimage resistance or extracting a layer secret.

### 4.3 SPDM Attestation: Evidence Exchange Across Component Boundaries

DICE secures the *vertical* dimension (boot layers within a device). **SPDM secures the *horizontal* dimension** (peer components across a bus) [6]. In the SPDM model, a *requester* interrogates a *responder*; the canonical choreography is:

1. `GET_VERSION` / `GET_CAPABILITIES` — negotiate SPDM version and capability flags (certificates? measurements? key exchange? mutual auth?).
2. `GET_DIGESTS` / `GET_CERTIFICATE` — retrieve the responder's certificate chain; the requester validates it to a trusted root (manufacturer CA or allow-listed leaf).
3. `CHALLENGE` — requester sends a 32-byte nonce; responder returns `CHALLENGE_AUTH`: a signature over the nonce, a measurement summary hash (`TCB` or `ALL`), and slot identifiers.
4. `GET_MEASUREMENTS` — retrieve the signed measurement block(s): firmware digests, SVN, debug state, configuration — the responder's equivalent of a TPM quote, signed by the attestation key.
5. `KEY_EXCHANGE` / `PSK_EXCHANGE` — (SPDM ≥ 1.1) establish a session with derived handshake/data secrets, enabling encrypted, integrity-protected follow-on traffic [7].

| SPDM message | Security property | DICE analog |
|--------------|-------------------|-------------|
| `CHALLENGE_AUTH` signature | Liveness + identity binding (nonce freshness) | Alias-key signature over TCI evidence |
| Measurement summary hash | Compact TCB attestation | TCI digest chain |
| Certificate chain (X.509v3) | Manufacturer-rooted trust | Embedded-CA certificate path [10] |
| Session keys (`KEY_EXCHANGE`) | Confidential channel bootstrapped from attestation | Sealing keys derived from CDI |

The TCG's *DICE Concise Evidence Binding for SPDM* encodes DICE evidence (TCI values, CDI-bound alias keys) into SPDM measurement records, letting verifiers appraise *boot-time* layering through *run-time* SPDM exchanges [4]. For Caliptra-class IP the responder runs in runtime firmware with its attestation key rooted in the DICE hierarchy — the PCIe-facing SPDM identity is *cryptographically downstream of the UDS*, not a clonable provisioned credential.

> **Theorem (SPDM liveness):** Verifying `CHALLENGE_AUTH` over a fresh 256-bit nonce accepts only evidence generated *after* the challenge — defeating replay under signature unforgeability.

One caveat: the SPDM stack itself joins the TCB — DSP2058 notes that compromise of the SPDM stack is undetectable by SPDM, with recovery out of scope [7]. The responder must therefore run *inside* the measured perimeter, not in unmeasured driver code.

### 4.4 Caliptra vs. OpenTitan: Two Architectures of Open Trust

The designs differ less in cryptography than in *packaging philosophy*:

- **Caliptra = RoT as IP.** A synthesizable block — VeeR RISC-V core, crypto accelerators, mailbox/APB interfaces, immutable ROM, key vault — integrated *inside* host SoCs (CPUs, GPUs, NICs, SSDs) [1]. Trust is *inlined* and attestation exposed via mailbox commands and SPDM; one verified RTL codebase amortized industry-wide gives cloud providers consistent APIs for heterogeneous fleets.
- **OpenTitan = RoT as chip.** Earl Grey is a complete taped-out SoC — Ibex core, crypto accelerators, OTBN, entropy source, OTP lifecycle management, alert handler, boot ROM — shipped as validated silicon [2][8]; *OpenTitan Integrated* adapts it for embedding in large SoCs with hardened trust boundaries [9].

Both publish RTL, firmware, and verification suites — Caliptra under CHIPS Alliance governance [1], OpenTitan under lowRISC — enabling independent audit up to the "foundry boundary" [5]. Both are DICE-native and SPDM-compatible. The choice between them is thus a *system-integration* choice: Caliptra minimizes per-device cost and standardizes the cloud fleet's attestation dialect; OpenTitan maximizes the inspectability of a self-contained trust anchor and has crossed the commercial-silicon threshold first [8].

They are not mutually exclusive: a Caliptra block can serve as the DICE engine inside an OpenTitan-Integrated-style subsystem, with SPDM bridging to discrete OpenTitan parts — a heterogeneous RoT fabric attested through one protocol.

### 4.5 Hardware-Anchored Supply Chain Security

Open silicon RoTs invert the economics of supply-chain security — *claims of provenance* are cheap, *evidence of integrity* expensive — as follows:

1. **Counterfeit resistance.** A clone lacks the genuine UDS, so its DICE identity cannot chain to the manufacturer CA — counterfeiting becomes key extraction, not remarking.
2. **In-transit tampering detection.** Reflashed firmware yields a *different identity*; the DICE certificate profile's TCI/SVN extensions name exactly what changed [10].
3. **Transparent manufacturing.** Open RTL and reproducible builds let third parties check the design *as specified* against *as taped out* (modulo the foundry boundary) [5][8], shrinking the trusted-manufacturing assumption to the fabrication step alone.
4. **Ownership and decommissioning.** Lifecycle and ownership-transfer flows bind firmware-authorization policy into the identity hierarchy, enabling cryptographic erasure of decommissioned devices [2].
5. **SBOM binding.** When each listed component's digest is *measured at boot* and reported via SPDM, the SBOM becomes a verifiable claim, not self-attestation.

## 5. Empirical Results and Proofs

**Cryptographic offload.** Cycle-accurate RTL characterization of OpenTitan's HMAC, AES, and OTBN offload reports **4.3×–12.5× speedups over software**, with firmware using only **16%–61% of accelerator bandwidth** — headroom for optimization [3]. This bounds attestation latency: hardware-accelerated signing keeps SPDM `CHALLENGE_AUTH`/`GET_MEASUREMENTS` in the low-millisecond regime.

**Commercial maturity.** OpenTitan's 2024 milestone — validated chips from its nine-member coalition — makes it the first commercial open-source silicon project, with production-grade verification, testing, and CI [8]. Caliptra's evidence is specification- and ecosystem-based: a published 1.x spec, open RTL/firmware under CHIPS Alliance, and cloud-silicon integration commitments [1].

**Proofs.** Three reductions from §4.1–4.3 — (i) *update re-keying*, (ii) *measurement binding*, (iii) *SPDM liveness* — yield the central formal claim:

> **Theorem (End-to-end attestation soundness):** Given (a) DICE derivation per [4], (b) every boot stage measured before execution transfer, (c) attestation keys confined to the RoT, and (d) fresh 256-bit SPDM nonces [6], a verifier checking the certificate chain, measurement signatures, and TCIs against reference manifests accepts *only* evidence from genuine hardware running the claimed firmware — under PRF security, second-preimage resistance, and signature unforgeability.

The theorem assumes correct implementation and an honest foundry — the assumptions open silicon makes *auditable* rather than eliminates (§6).

## 6. Limitations

1. **The SPDM stack is TCB.** DSP2058 warns SPDM provides no protection, detection, or recovery for its own TCB [7] — a memory-safety bug in a responder's parser silently undermines attestation, arguing for memory-safe SPDM stacks.
2. **UDS provisioning remains a trust bottleneck.** DICE's guarantees begin *after* provisioning; a compromised facility can inject known UDS values, enabling perfect clones. Ceremonies and PUF-derived secrets mitigate this, but the ceremony is process control, not mathematics [2][4].
3. **The foundry boundary.** Open RTL ends where fabrication begins; foundry trojan insertion is outside both designs' threat models, with detection relying on post-fabrication testing [5].
4. **Side channels.** DICE derivation and attestation signing are prime DPA/EMA targets. OpenTitan ships masking countermeasures in its crypto blocks; Caliptra's physical-security requirements are specified but implementation-dependent [2].
5. **Post-quantum migration.** Long-lived DICE hierarchies (5–10 year device lifetimes) need ML-DSA/SLH-DSA migration designed in now; fleets will straddle algorithms for years.
6. **Formal verification coverage.** Neither project's full RTL has end-to-end formal proofs of the DICE state machine; verification is simulation- and assertion-based. The §4–5 theorems are *paper* reductions over idealized primitives — design discipline, not silicon guarantees.

---

## 7. Conclusion

Caliptra and OpenTitan mark a phase change: the root of trust is no longer a vendor's promise but an *auditable artifact* — readable RTL, rebuildable firmware, and a DICE identity hierarchy reducing to standard assumptions. Measured boot turns booting into evidence; SPDM turns evidence into a protocol; open silicon lets third parties verify rather than believe.

The central lesson is that **identity should be a function of measurement, not of manufacture**. When keys derive from what the device *runs* rather than what it *is*, counterfeits fail attestation, tampered firmware re-keys itself out of the trust fabric, and updates become clean recovery events. The empirical record — OpenTitan's commercial silicon [8], Caliptra's standardization [1], 4.3×–12.5× crypto offload [3] — marks this as the emerging baseline for confidential computing.

Open problems remain — the foundry-boundary gap, SPDM stack hardening, post-quantum migration, and verified RTL — but the direction is set: the silicon root of trust is becoming *small, open, and provable*.
