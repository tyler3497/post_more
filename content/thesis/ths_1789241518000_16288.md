# Quantum Kernel Methods, Barren Plateaus, and the Search for Provable Quantum Advantage in Machine Learning

## Abstract
Quantum machine learning promises to exploit exponentially large Hilbert spaces, yet the conditions for genuine quantum advantage remain contested. This thesis examines quantum kernel methods — the most theoretically developed QML paradigm — through three lenses: kernel construction and estimation, the trainability barrier of barren plateaus and exponential concentration, and the search for provable advantage. We trace the path from the fidelity kernel of Havlíček et al. [1] to projected kernels that provably enlarge the geometric difference between quantum and classical models [3], and to the discrete-logarithm concept class of Liu, Arunachalam, and Temme yielding the first rigorous fault-tolerant speedup in supervised learning [2]. We analyze why naive kernels concentrate exponentially, becoming asymptotically trivial, and how local projections, structured feature maps, and physics-informed embeddings restore learnability [4,5,7]. Dequantization arguments delimit where advantage can reside. Synthesizing large-scale simulations and hardware studies [3,6,7], we conclude that provable advantage currently lives on engineered datasets, while practical advantage on natural data awaits solution of the alignment problem.

## 1 Introduction

The conjunction of quantum computing and machine learning was anticipated to be transformative: quantum processors manipulate state vectors in a $2^n$-dimensional Hilbert space, and machine learning is, at its core, the art of extracting geometric structure from high-dimensional data. The hope was that exponentially large quantum feature spaces would confer an equally exponential representational advantage.

Reality has proven more subtle. A decade of research has produced a rich taxonomy of quantum learning models, but **provable** quantum advantage has been established only for carefully constructed problem classes [2], and **practical** advantage on natural datasets remains unobserved. Two phenomena are the principal obstacles: **barren plateaus**, the exponential vanishing of gradients in variational landscapes [4], and **exponential concentration**, the collapse of quantum kernel matrices toward trivial constants as system size grows [5].

This thesis argues that quantum kernel methods provide the cleanest theoretical laboratory in which to settle these questions. Unlike variational models, kernel methods separate the quantum computation (feature embedding) from the classical optimization (convex training), sidestepping barren plateaus while inheriting the full machinery of classical learning theory — generalization bounds, the representer theorem, and model-selection theory [5]. This separation makes the advantage question sharper: *given fixed classical data, does estimating a kernel on a quantum processor permit learning some concept class that no efficient classical algorithm can learn?* The answer is a qualified yes — qualified by fault-tolerance assumptions, engineered concept classes, and the concentration phenomena that threaten naive constructions.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Classical kernel theory

Kernel methods rest on a single elegant idea: map data through a feature map $\phi: \mathcal{X} \to \mathcal{H}$ into a reproducing kernel Hilbert space (RKHS) $\mathcal{H}$, and learn a linear function there. The kernel $k(x, x') = \langle \phi(x), \phi(x') \rangle_{\mathcal{H}}$ is all the algorithm ever needs. The **representer theorem** guarantees that the minimizer of any regularized empirical risk admits the finite expansion $f(x) = \sum_{i=1}^{m} \alpha_i k(x, x_i)$, reducing infinite-dimensional optimization to a convex quadratic program over the $m \times m$ Gram matrix. Mercer's theorem decomposes the kernel into eigenfunctions of the integral operator $T_k$, whose spectrum governs model capacity and learning rates. The question QML poses is whether *quantum* feature maps unlock RKHSs with favorable spectra that classical kernels cannot efficiently emulate.

### 2.2 Quantum feature maps and the fidelity kernel

A quantum feature map encodes classical data $x \in \mathcal{X}$ into a quantum state via a parameterized unitary $U(x)$:

$$|\psi(x)\rangle = U(x)|0\rangle^{\otimes n}.$$

The **fidelity quantum kernel** is the squared overlap $\kappa(x, x') = |\langle \psi(x) | \psi(x') \rangle|^2$ [1,5].

Because $\kappa(x, x') = \langle \rho(x), \rho(x') \rangle_{\mathrm{HS}}$ for $\rho(x) = |\psi(x)\rangle\langle\psi(x)|$, $\kappa$ is a genuine positive-semidefinite kernel, so all classical kernel machinery applies directly. Havlíček et al. [1] proposed the **quantum kernel estimator**: evaluate each Gram-matrix entry on a quantum processor (via a compute–uncompute circuit or SWAP test), then train a classical SVM on the resulting matrix. Both the kernel estimator and their variational classifier were demonstrated on a two-qubit superconducting processor, marking the birth of gate-based QML as an empirical discipline.

### 2.3 Barren plateaus and cost concentration

The trainability crisis of variational QML was formalized by McClean et al. (2018): for sufficiently expressive (2-design-like) parameterized circuits, the variance of the cost gradient vanishes exponentially in the number of qubits, $\mathrm{Var}[\partial_k C] \in \mathcal{O}(2^{-n})$. Cerezo et al. [4] refined this into a **cost-function-dependent** theory: global cost functions exhibit barren plateaus at essentially all depths, while local cost functions — sums of few-body observables — remain trainable at logarithmic depth. The phenomenon is a special case of **concentration of measure**: expressive ansätze approximate Haar-random unitaries, and both costs and gradients concentrate around uninformative means [4].

### 2.4 The dequantization backdrop

Any claim of quantum advantage must survive comparison with the best classical algorithms. For QML, the relevant boundary is sharp: if a quantum kernel's feature map produces states that are classically simulable (low entanglement, Clifford-dominated, or concentrated), then the kernel admits an efficient classical estimator, and no advantage is possible [5]. Advantage therefore requires feature maps that are simultaneously (i) classically hard to simulate, (ii) slowly concentrating, and (iii) aligned with the learning task.

## 3 Methodology

This thesis is a theoretical and empirical synthesis. Our methodology has three components.

**Theoretical analysis.** We work within statistical learning theory: a concept class $\mathcal{C}$, a distribution $\mathcal{D}$, and sample complexity against target excess risk. The key complexity measure is the **geometric difference** $g$ between the quantum kernel $K_Q$ and the best classical kernel $K_C$ (Huang et al. [3]): larger $g$ means the quantum feature space contains directions inaccessible to classical kernels. We track three diagnostics for any proposed quantum kernel:

1. *Estimability*: the shot complexity of estimating each Gram entry to precision $\epsilon$ (typically $\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{-2})$ shots via sampling).
2. *Concentration rate*: how $\mathrm{Var}_{x,x'}[\kappa(x,x')]$ scales with qubit count $n$; exponential concentration $\in \mathcal{O}(2^{-n})$ signals a trivial kernel.
3. *Geometric difference*: the separation $g(K_Q, K_C)$ from the classical kernel family under consideration.

**Empirical grounding.** We synthesize results from the largest published QML simulation campaigns [3] — 30 qubits, $\sim 2$ exaflops — and from hardware-scale studies of kernel concentration on superconducting and neutral-atom platforms [7].

**Comparative evaluation.** We score each kernel family on: (a) provable separation from classical kernels, (b) resistance to exponential concentration, (c) NISQ-noise compatibility, and (d) estimation-cost scaling with dataset size.

> **Theorem:** *(Informal synthesis.)* If a quantum kernel concentrates exponentially, $\mathrm{Var}[\kappa] \in \mathcal{O}(2^{-n})$, then distinguishing it from a constant kernel needs $\Omega(2^{n/2})$ shots, and any classifier trained on it is statistically indistinguishable from one trained on trivial features. With the projected structure of §4.3, concentration is at most polynomial in $n$ and the geometric difference $g$ can grow with $n$ [3,5].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Quantum feature maps and the fidelity kernel

The expressive power of a quantum kernel is inherited from its feature map $U(x)$. Common constructions:

- **Angle encoding**: $U(x) = \bigotimes_{i=1}^{n} R_P(x_i)$ — shallow and local, but producing product states with no entanglement; efficiently simulable and typically uninteresting.
- **IQP-type embeddings**: layers of commuting diagonal gates interleaved with Hadamards [1,3] — conjectured hard to simulate classically, and the workhorse of advantage proposals.
- **Hamiltonian evolution maps**: $U(x) = e^{-i H(x) t}$ for data-dependent Hamiltonians — physically motivated and natural for quantum-native data [3].
- **Data re-uploading**: alternating encoding and variational layers — universal in the single-qubit case, but concentration-prone at scale.

The fidelity kernel's estimation is straightforward: prepare $U(x')^\dagger U(x)|0\rangle^{\otimes n}$ and measure the all-zero probability. Each entry costs one circuit evaluation (up to shot noise), so the full Gram matrix costs $\mathcal{O}(m^2)$ evaluations for $m$ training points — the quadratic bottleneck that QUACK [6] addresses.

```python
import numpy as np

def estimate_gram(feature_map, X, shots=4096):
    m = len(X)
    K = np.zeros((m, m))
    for i in range(m):
        for j in range(i, m):
            # prepare U(x_j)^dagger U(x_i) |0>^n, measure P(0...0)
            p0 = feature_map.zero_probability(X[i], X[j], shots)
            K[i, j] = K[j, i] = p0
    return K
# Shot-noise std per entry ~ sqrt(p0(1-p0)/shots). Under exponential
# concentration p0 ~ 2^{-n} off-diagonal, so resolving structure
# needs shots >> 2^n — the kernel becomes unlearnable [5].
```

### 4.2 Exponential concentration and the vanishing kernel

The most damaging theoretical result for naive QML is the **exponential concentration** of quantum kernels. Tanner, Kam, and Wang [5] provide the definitive review: for feature maps whose unitaries form approximate 2-designs, the fidelity kernel concentrates as

$$\mathrm{Var}_{x,x' \sim \mathcal{D}}[\kappa(x,x')] \in \mathcal{O}\left(2^{-n}\right),$$

and the kernel matrix satisfies $\|K - c\,\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^\top\| \to 0$ in probability for an uninformative constant $c$ — predictions on unseen data are then no better than the majority-class baseline. This is the kernel analogue of the barren plateau: the *same* concentration-of-measure phenomenon viewed through a different observable [4,5].

Three distinct mechanisms drive concentration, and distinguishing them matters for mitigation:

1. **Expressibility-induced concentration**: Haar-random-like feature maps spread each $|\psi(x)\rangle$ uniformly over Hilbert space; overlaps concentrate at $2^{-n}$ by Levy's lemma. *Mitigation*: structured, low-expressibility maps aligned to the data.
2. **Entanglement-induced concentration**: highly entangled encodings make reduced states maximally mixed. *Mitigation*: projected kernels (§4.3) that measure local reductions deliberately.
3. **Noise-induced concentration**: under local depolarizing noise, the state converges to the maximally mixed state exponentially in depth. This is the hardest barrier for NISQ hardware and motivates the concentration-free constructions of [7].

A GFM comparison of the kernel families:

| Kernel family | Concentration | Classical simulability | Provable separation | NISQ viability |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Fidelity (global, expressive map) | Exponential in $n$ | Hard (IQP-type) | Only on engineered data | Poor (noise-amplified) |
| Fidelity (local, shallow map) | Polynomial/mild | Often easy (low entanglement) | None known | Moderate |
| Projected quantum kernel | Controlled by projection rank | Hard possible | Yes, via geometric difference [3] | Moderate |
| Centroid (QUACK) [6] | Same as base kernel | Same as base kernel | None claimed | Good (linear scaling) |
| Rydberg-blockade kernel [7] | Concentration-free by design | Platform-specific | Empirical | Good (native hardware) |

### 4.3 Projected quantum kernels and the geometric difference

Huang et al. [3] introduced the **projected quantum kernel**, which replaces the global fidelity with a classical kernel applied to *local* quantum features. Concretely, for each qubit (or patch) $k$, compute the reduced density matrix $\rho_k(x) = \mathrm{Tr}_{\neq k}[|\psi(x)\rangle\langle\psi(x)|]$, and define

$$\kappa_{\mathrm{PQ}}(x, x') = \exp\!\left(-\gamma \sum_{k=1}^{n} \|\rho_k(x) - \rho_k(x')\|_2^2\right).$$

Because each $\rho_k(x)$ lives in a constant-dimensional space (a single qubit's Bloch ball), the kernel cannot concentrate exponentially in $n$; concentration is at worst polynomial, governed by the number of patches. Yet the map $x \mapsto \{\rho_k(x)\}$ can still be classically hard to compute when $U(x)$ generates volume-law entanglement — the local reductions are hard to simulate even though each is small. This combination — *local measurement of a globally hard state* — is the key insight.

The paper's central theoretical contribution is the **geometric difference** $g(K_Q, K_C)$, quantifying how much of the quantum kernel's feature space lies outside the span of classical kernel features: if $g$ is large, there *exists* a dataset on which the quantum kernel achieves small prediction error while every classical kernel in the comparison class fails. They prove projected kernels can make $g$ grow with system size, and demonstrate numerically — at 30 qubits, the largest digital QML simulation campaign published — that the projected kernel outperforms classical RBF kernels on engineered quantum datasets, with the gap *widening* with system size [3]. The same work shows projected kernels recover the discrete-logarithm separation of [2] with a simpler construction.

### 4.4 Barren plateaus in variational QML: trainability versus kernelization

Variational QML models — parameterized circuits $V(\theta)$ trained by gradient descent — face barren plateaus head-on [4]. The causes and mitigations are now well catalogued: *global cost functions* concentrate cost and gradient (mitigation: local costs, trainable at $\mathcal{O}(\log n)$ depth); *excessive expressibility* of 2-design-like ansätze (mitigation: problem-inspired ansätze with restricted dynamical Lie algebras); *entanglement and noise* inducing deterministic concentration (mitigation: shallow circuits, error mitigation); and *random initialization* landing in the plateau (mitigation: identity-block initialization, layerwise training).

There is a deep connection to kernel methods: in the infinite-width / lazy-training limit, a variational quantum model linearizes around its initialization and becomes kernel regression with the **quantum neural tangent kernel** (QNTK). Barren plateaus correspond precisely to concentration of the QNTK — when gradients vanish, the tangent features are uninformative. Kernel methods are thus the *limit* in which trainability is solved by construction: the quantum device performs only non-variational estimation, and all optimization is convex and classical [5]. The price is quadratic Gram-matrix scaling and kernel concentration — but the latter is a property of the *feature map*, addressable by design (§4.2–4.3).

The following TLA+ sketch captures the safety property that classical SVM training must satisfy regardless of how the kernel was estimated:

```tla+

```

The point of the sketch: finite-shot estimation error $\epsilon$ perturbs the Gram matrix, and training stays sound only while the perturbed matrix remains positive semidefinite — where shot noise, concentration, and optimization interact.

### 4.5 Dequantization and the classical-simulability boundary

The dequantization literature sets the outer boundary of possible advantage. If the states $|\psi(x)\rangle$ produced by a feature map have bounded entanglement (matrix-product-state representable), or if $U(x)$ is Clifford-dominated, or if the kernel concentrates so severely that only $\mathcal{O}(1)$-precision estimates are needed, then classical algorithms can estimate $\kappa(x,x')$ efficiently and the quantum device adds nothing [5]. Tensor-network dequantization of QKMs has been demonstrated explicitly: for commonly used feature maps at NISQ-accessible depths, classical surrogates match quantum kernel performance.

In short, advantage requires the intersection of four conditions — a classically hard feature map, slow concentration, large geometric difference from classical kernels, and alignment between the concept class and the quantum features [2,3,5]. On *natural* data, the alignment condition is unproven and is the central open empirical question. Only this intersection admits genuine quantum advantage — a narrow but non-empty region, as §5 shows.

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 The discrete-logarithm separation: a rigorous speedup

The landmark result is due to Liu, Arunachalam, and Temme [2], published in *Nature Physics* (2021). They construct a concept class $\mathcal{C} = \{f_s\}_{s \in \mathbb{Z}_p^*}$ over bit-strings, where the label of $x$ depends on the discrete logarithm $\log_g x$ relative to a secret shift $s$. Their theorem, informally:

> **Theorem:** *(Liu–Arunachalam–Temme [2].)* Assuming the classical hardness of the discrete logarithm problem, no efficient classical learner achieves accuracy $\tfrac12 + 1/\mathrm{poly}(n)$ on $\mathcal{C}$, while a quantum kernel method — an SVM whose kernel is estimated on a fault-tolerant quantum computer via Shor's algorithm as a subroutine — achieves accuracy $1 - o(1)$, robust to additive estimation error in the kernel entries.

The construction is ingenious: the quantum kernel effectively computes features in the "discrete-log basis," in which the classes are linearly separable intervals, while classically the data are pseudorandom. The result is *rigorous* (reduction to DLP hardness), *robust* (tolerates finite-shot kernel estimates), and uses only *classical access to data*. Its limitation is equally clear: the concept class is engineered around a cryptographic hardness assumption, and the quantum algorithm needs fault tolerance to run Shor's algorithm — a proof of *possibility*, not a blueprint for practical advantage.

### 5.2 Power of data: projected kernels at 30 qubits

Huang et al. [3] provide the most ambitious empirical study of quantum kernels to date. Using up to 30 qubits and $\sim 2$ exaflops of simulation, they validate the geometric-difference theory: on engineered datasets where the labeling function is generated by a quantum process, the projected quantum kernel achieves substantially lower prediction error than optimized classical kernels, and the gap *widens* with system size. On fashion-MNIST-derived classical data, no advantage is observed, consistent with the alignment condition of §4.5. This negative result on natural data is as informative as the positive result on quantum data — it localizes advantage to problems whose intrinsic structure is quantum-mechanical.

### 5.3 Scaling the Gram matrix: QUACK and linear-time kernels

A practical obstacle independent of concentration is the $\mathcal{O}(m^2)$ cost of Gram-matrix estimation. Tscharke et al. [6] introduce QUACK (Quantum Aligned Centroid Kernel), which computes kernel entries only between training samples and learned class centroids — an $(m \times c)$ matrix for $c$ classes — optimizing centroid positions and kernel parameters iteratively, so inference needs only $c$ circuit evaluations per test point. On simulated benchmarks including high-dimensional MNIST variants (784 features), QUACK matches full quadratic kernel methods while scaling linearly in training-set size, removing the scaling bottleneck that would otherwise dominate practical deployment.

### 5.4 Concentration-free kernels on neutral atoms

Hardware noise is the third concentration mechanism (§4.2), and recent work attacks it at the physics level: a quantum kernel implemented natively in the Rydberg blockade of neutral-atom arrays is **concentration-free by construction** — the blockaded dynamics constrain the accessible state manifold so kernel values stay well-separated with system size, on current neutral-atom processors [7]. Concentration is thus not a law of quantum kernels but a property of *unstructured* embeddings.

## 6 Limitations

Intellectual honesty requires stating plainly what the field has *not* achieved.

**Engineered versus natural data.** Every rigorous advantage result [2,3] uses concept classes constructed to embed quantum-hard structure. No peer-reviewed study shows a quantum kernel beating a well-tuned classical model on a natural, industrially relevant dataset; solving this alignment problem is the single most important open question for practical QML.

**The fault-tolerance gap.** The Liu et al. separation [2] requires running Shor's algorithm coherently, i.e., fault-tolerant quantum computation at scales far beyond current hardware. NISQ-era kernels face noise-induced concentration [4,5,7], and error mitigation carries sampling overheads that scale exponentially with circuit volume.

**Shot complexity and the estimation tax.** Even a well-designed kernel must be estimated entry-by-entry: $\mathcal{O}(m^2)$ circuit evaluations each requiring $\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{-2})$ shots. QUACK [6] reduces the scaling but adds its own optimization loop. Classical kernels, by contrast, evaluate in microseconds.

**Expressibility–trainability–simulability trilemma.** Highly expressive maps concentrate; structured maps risk classical simulability; task-aligned maps are unknown for natural data. Navigating this trilemma, rather than scaling qubit counts, is the field's central design challenge.

**Theoretical gaps.** Generalization bounds for quantum kernels are looser than classical ones; the spectral theory of quantum integral operators is nascent [5]; and the link between geometric difference and practical model selection is poorly understood.

---

## 7 Conclusion

Quantum kernel methods have matured from the two-qubit demonstrations of Havlíček et al. [1] into the most theoretically disciplined branch of quantum machine learning: first *construction* (fidelity kernels separating quantum embedding from convex training [1]), then *crisis* (exponential concentration and barren plateaus exposing naive embeddings as asymptotically trivial [4,5]), then *refinement* (projected kernels, geometric difference, centroid methods, and physics-informed embeddings showing the crisis is a property of bad design [3,6,7]) — capped by the discrete-logarithm separation proving that rigorous quantum advantage in supervised learning is a theorem, not a hope [2].

The sober assessment is this: **provable quantum advantage in machine learning exists, but it currently lives on engineered problems under fault-tolerance assumptions; practical advantage on natural data does not yet exist, and its discovery requires solving the alignment problem.** The most promising path forward combines the theoretical clarity of kernel methods (convex training, no barren plateaus, analyzable generalization) with deliberate feature-map design: local projections of globally hard states, noise-aware constructions, and linear-scaling estimation.
