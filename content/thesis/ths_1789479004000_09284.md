# Regret Minimization in Sequential Decision Making: UCB, Thompson Sampling, and the Bellman Optimality Machinery of Linear MDPs

## Abstract

Sequential decision making under uncertainty is governed by a single tension: the need to *explore* poorly understood options while *exploiting* the knowledge already acquired. This article develops the mathematical theory of regret minimization from its bandit origins to its modern reinforcement learning form. We begin with the stochastic multi-armed bandit, where the Lai–Robbins asymptotic lower bound proves that any uniformly good policy must incur logarithmic regret, with a constant dictated by Kullback–Leibler geometry. We then analyze the two canonical algorithmic responses: *upper confidence bound* methods, whose finite-time guarantees flow from concentration inequalities, and *Thompson sampling*, whose posterior-sampling heuristic attains matching optimality through martingale arguments. The treatment ascends to episodic reinforcement learning with linear function approximation, where the Bellman optimality equations meet least-squares value iteration and the ellipsoidal geometry of confidence sets produces the LSVI-UCB algorithm and its minimax-refined successors. Throughout, optimism and probability matching emerge as dual expressions of one principle: act as if the most favorable statistically plausible world were real. Numerical simulations and proof sketches complete the picture.

##1 Intro

Few problems in machine learning are as conceptually clean and as practically consequential as sequential decision making under uncertainty. An agent faces a stream of choices; each choice yields both a stochastic reward and information about the world that generated it. Choosing well requires *exploitation* of current knowledge; learning the world requires *exploration* of the unknown. This irreducible tradeoff is the subject of regret minimization.

Regret formalizes the gap between what the agent achieved and what an oracle with full knowledge would have achieved. The central question of the field is how quickly regret can be driven down as the horizon grows. For the classical stochastic multi-armed bandit, the answer is logarithmic — not as a matter of algorithmic ingenuity but as an *information-theoretic necessity* established by Lai and Robbins in 1985 [1]. Every algorithm that performs uniformly well across problem instances must pull each suboptimal arm logarithmically many times, because distinguishing the optimal arm from a near-optimal one demands that much evidence.

The remarkable fact is that this lower bound is *achievable*. Two families of algorithms attain it, and they could hardly be more different in spirit. Upper confidence bound (UCB) methods are frequentist and deterministic: they maintain empirical estimates, inflate them by a confidence radius, and greedily choose the most optimistic index [2]. Thompson sampling is Bayesian and randomized: it maintains a posterior over each arm's mean, samples a plausible world from that posterior, and acts optimally with respect to the sample [3]. Both are asymptotically optimal, yet their finite-time behavior, computational profiles, and tail properties differ sharply.

The story does not end with bandits. When decisions influence future states — the full reinforcement learning setting — the same exploration–exploitation tension reappears, now entangled with the Bellman optimality equations. Linear Markov decision processes, in which transitions and rewards are linear in a known feature map, provide the cleanest generalization: least-squares value iteration with an ellipsoidal UCB bonus achieves regret that is polynomial in the feature dimension and horizon, independent of the size of the state and action spaces [4]. Subsequent refinements have pushed these bounds to near minimax optimality [5]. This article unifies these developments, presenting the lower bounds, the algorithms, the proof machinery, and the empirical behavior as one coherent narrative.

##2 Background

### The stochastic bandit and pseudo-regret

The stochastic multi-armed bandit consists of a finite set of *K* arms. At each round t from 1 to T, the agent selects an arm A_t and receives a reward drawn independently from that arm's unknown distribution, with mean μ_i for arm i. Let μ* denote the maximal mean and define the *gap* Δ_i = μ* − μ_i for each arm. The performance measure is the (pseudo-)regret

R_T = T μ* − Σ_{t=1}^{T} E[r_t],

the expected shortfall relative to always pulling an optimal arm. Equivalently, R_T = Σ_i Δ_i E[N_i(T)], where N_i(T) counts pulls of arm i: regret is controlled by how rarely suboptimal arms are sampled.

A policy is *uniformly good* if its regret grows sub-polynomially in T for every instance in the problem class.

> **Theorem (Lai–Robbins, 1985 [1]).** For parametric reward families, any uniformly good policy satisfies
>
> liminf_{T→∞} E[R_T] / log T ≥ Σ_{i : Δ_i > 0} Δ_i / D(μ_i ‖ μ*),
>
> where D(μ_i ‖ μ*) is the Kullback–Leibler divergence between the reward distributions of arm i and an optimal arm.

The bound's message is geometric: regret is unavoidable, logarithmic is the best possible rate, and the constant is set by how *distinguishable* each suboptimal arm is from the best. Arms that are nearly optimal are hard to rule out and must be pulled often; the KL divergence quantifies exactly how much evidence is needed.

### The UCB family

The *optimism in the face of uncertainty* principle converts the lower bound's logic into an algorithm. For each arm, maintain the empirical mean μ̂_i(t) after N_i(t) pulls. By Hoeffding's inequality, the true mean lies within roughly √(2 log t / N_i(t)) of the empirical mean with overwhelming probability. UCB1 selects the arm maximizing the *index*

μ̂_i(t) + √(2 log t / N_i(t)),

the sum of estimated value and an exploration bonus that decays as the arm is pulled [2]. The bonus is precisely the radius of a one-sided confidence interval; playing the arm with the largest upper confidence bound is acting as though every arm were as good as its data plausibly allow.

Variants refine this idea. UCB-tuned replaces the crude Hoeffding radius with a variance-adaptive one, performing better empirically though without matching theory [2]. KL-UCB replaces the Hoeffding interval with one derived from the KL divergence, and attains the Lai–Robbins constant exactly on exponential families. The finite-time analysis of UCB1 shows expected regret bounded by Σ_i (8 log n / Δ_i) plus small constants — logarithmic uniformly over the horizon [2].

### Thompson sampling

Thompson sampling predates the entire field, proposed in 1933 as a clinical-trial heuristic. It is *probability matching*: maintain a posterior distribution over each arm's mean given the observed rewards, sample one plausible mean θ̃_i from each posterior, and pull the arm with the largest sample. For Bernoulli rewards with a Beta prior, the posterior after observing s successes and f failures is Beta(s+1, f+1) — conjugate, closed-form, and trivially computable [3].

For decades it was a heuristic with superb empirical performance and no theory. The breakthrough analysis of Agrawal and Goyal established both a problem-dependent bound of (1+ε) Σ_i (log T)/Δ_i + O(K/ε²), matching the Lai–Robbins constant, and a problem-independent bound of O(√(KT log T)) with Beta priors, improving to O(√(KT log K)) with Gaussian priors [3]. Posterior sampling thus joins UCB as asymptotically optimal, while remaining randomized, often simpler to implement, and empirically dominant in many settings.

### From bandits to Markov decision processes

Bandits are single-state decision problems. In a finite-horizon episodic Markov decision process, the agent observes a state s_h at stage h, chooses an action a_h, receives a reward, and transitions to a new state. The optimal value functions satisfy the *Bellman optimality equations*

V*_h(s) = max_a [ r_h(s,a) + E_{s'∼P_h(·|s,a)}[ V*_{h+1}(s') ] ],

with V*_{H+1} = 0, and the greedy policy with respect to Q*_h is optimal. Classical dynamic programming solves these equations when the model is known; the learning problem is to solve them while the model is unknown, balancing exploration against exploitation *across states and stages simultaneously*.

A *linear MDP* assumes a known feature map φ(s,a) in ℝ^d such that both transitions and rewards are linear in it:

P_h(s' | s,a) = ⟨ φ(s,a), μ_h(s') ⟩,   r_h(s,a) = ⟨ φ(s,a), θ_h ⟩,

for unknown vector-valued measures μ_h and vectors θ_h. This structure implies that every Q-function of a policy is linear in φ, which makes least-squares estimation valid and enables provably efficient exploration in spaces far too large to enumerate [4].

##3 Methodology

The methodology of this field has two layers: the algorithmic principles and their concrete instantiations.

### The optimism principle

Both UCB and its reinforcement learning descendants implement optimism through *confidence sets*. Given data, construct a set of statistically plausible models; act optimally with respect to the most favorable one. The geometry changes across settings while the principle stands:

- in bandits, the confidence set is a collection of intervals, one per arm;
- in linear MDPs, it is an ellipsoid in feature space;
- the selection rule may be deterministic argmax or randomized posterior sampling.

### UCB1, concretely

The following implementation captures UCB1 exactly as analyzed in [2]:

```python
import math

def ucb1(T, K, pull):
    """pull(i) -> reward in the unit interval. Returns cumulative regret."""
    counts = [0] * K
    sums = [0.0] * K
    regret = 0.0
    mu_star = None  # oracle value, for measurement only
    for t in range(1, T + 1):
        best, best_idx = -1.0, 0
        for i in range(K):
            if counts[i] == 0:
                idx = float("inf")
            else:
                mean = sums[i] / counts[i]
                idx = mean + math.sqrt(2.0 * math.log(t) / counts[i])
            if idx > best:
                best, best_idx = idx, i
        r = pull(best_idx)
        counts[best_idx] += 1
        sums[best_idx] += r
        regret += (pull.mu_star - r)
    return regret
```

The `sqrt(2 log t / counts[i])` term is the exploration bonus: arms pulled rarely have large bonuses and are eventually tried, while arms with high empirical means are exploited.

### Thompson sampling, concretely

```python
import random

def thompson_sampling(T, K, pull):
    succ = [1] * K  # Beta(1,1) prior: one pseudo-success
    fail = [1] * K  # ...and one pseudo-failure per arm
    regret = 0.0
    for t in range(T):
        theta = [random.betavariate(succ[i], fail[i]) for i in range(K)]
        a = max(range(K), key=lambda i: theta[i])
        r = pull(a)  # Bernoulli reward
        succ[a] += r
        fail[a] += 1 - r
        regret += (pull.mu_star - r)
    return regret
```

Each round draws one plausible world from the posterior and plays its optimal arm. Arms whose posteriors concentrate on low values are rarely sampled; arms with genuine promise or high uncertainty are sampled often — probability matching in action [3].

### LSVI-UCB for linear MDPs

Least-squares value iteration with UCB (LSVI-UCB) lifts optimism to sequential stateful problems [4]. At episode k and stage h, form the regularized design matrix Λ_h^k = Σ_{τ<k} φ(s_h^τ, a_h^τ) φ(s_h^τ, a_h^τ)ᵀ + λI and solve ridge regression for the weights w_h^k that best predict r_h + V_{h+1}^k targets. The optimistic Q-function is

Q_h^k(s,a) = min( H, ⟨ w_h^k, φ(s,a) ⟩ + β ‖φ(s,a)‖_{(Λ_h^k)^{-1}} ),

where the bonus β‖φ‖_{Λ^{-1}} is the radius of the confidence ellipsoid in feature space — the direct generalization of the scalar UCB interval. The agent acts greedily with respect to Q_h^k, collects a trajectory, and repeats. The main theorem of [4] proves regret Õ(√(d³H³T)) with T the total number of steps, *independent of the cardinalities of the state and action spaces*: exploration is guided entirely by feature-space uncertainty.

| Algorithm | Principle | Bandit regret | Key mechanism |
|---|---|---|---|
| UCB1 | Frequentist optimism | O(Σ_i log T / Δ_i) | Hoeffding confidence radius |
| KL-UCB | Frequentist optimism | Lai–Robbins optimal | KL-based confidence set |
| Thompson sampling | Bayesian probability matching | O(Σ_i log T / Δ_i), O(√(KT log T)) | Posterior sampling |
| MOTS | Truncated posterior sampling | O(√(KT)) minimax | Truncated Gaussian posterior |
| LSVI-UCB | Optimistic least squares | Õ(√(d³H³T)) in linear MDPs | Ellipsoidal feature bonus |

##4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The change-of-measure machinery of the Lai–Robbins bound

The proof of the lower bound is a masterpiece of information theory [1]. Fix a uniformly good policy and an instance with optimal arm 1. For a suboptimal arm i, construct an *alternative instance* identical except that arm i's distribution is shifted to have mean above μ*. Under the original instance, arm i is pulled o(T^a) times for every positive a — otherwise regret would be polynomial, contradicting uniform goodness. Now consider the event that arm i is pulled fewer than c log T times; a change-of-measure argument relates its probability under the two instances through the KL divergence accumulated over the pulls of arm i. If c were smaller than Δ_i / D(μ_i ‖ μ*), the policy would fail to identify the optimal arm in the alternative instance often enough to keep regret sub-polynomial there — contradiction. Hence E[N_i(T)] ≥ (1−o(1)) Δ_i log T / D(μ_i ‖ μ*), and summing over suboptimal arms weighted by their gaps yields the bound.

### 4.2 Finite-time analysis of UCB1

Auer, Cesa-Bianchi, and Fischer showed that UCB1's logarithmic regret holds not merely asymptotically but *uniformly over time* [2]. The argument decomposes the pulls of a suboptimal arm i into two regimes: pulls where the confidence interval still contains the truth (genuine uncertainty) and pulls where it does not (a low-probability failure of concentration).

> **Lemma (Confidence failure [2]).** For rewards in the unit interval, with probability at least 1 − 2t^{−4} the empirical mean of an arm pulled s times lies within √(2 log t / s) of its true mean.
>
> *Proof.* Direct from Hoeffding's inequality with the union bound over rounds. ∎

Once arm i has been pulled more than 8 log n / Δ_i² times, its upper confidence bound falls below μ* − Δ_i/2 whenever concentration holds, while the optimal arm's index exceeds μ* − Δ_i/2 under the same event; hence arm i can be selected afterward only on a concentration failure, whose total probability sums to at most π²/3. This yields:

> **Theorem (UCB1 regret [2]).** The expected regret of UCB1 after n plays is at most Σ_{i:Δ_i>0} (8 log n / Δ_i) + (1 + π²/3) Σ_i Δ_i.

KL-UCB closes this gap by calibrating the interval to the KL geometry, and UCB2 pushes the constant arbitrarily close to optimal through epoch-based scheduling [2].

### 4.3 Thompson sampling: martingale arguments and minimax optimality

Agrawal and Goyal's key device is a *coupling*: relate the probability that Thompson sampling pulls a suboptimal arm i to the probability it pulls the optimal arm, via carefully chosen thresholds y_i and z_i strictly between μ_i and μ*. The posterior of the optimal arm is shown to exceed the upper threshold z_i except on a rare event, using a martingale concentration argument for Beta posteriors; conditioned on this, each pull of arm i is "paid for" by a geometrically distributed number of optimal-arm pulls, and the geometric parameter decays exponentially as arm i accumulates data [3]. The resulting bound is (1+ε) Σ_i (log T)/Δ_i + O(K/ε²) — asymptotically matching Lai–Robbins — plus the first problem-independent bound O(√(KT log T)) for Beta priors.

Gaussian priors sharpen the worst-case analysis to O(√(KT log K)) [3]. But the minimax rate for bandits is Θ(√(KT)), and whether any Thompson-sampling variant could achieve it without the logarithmic slack was open until Minimax Optimal Thompson Sampling (MOTS) [7]. MOTS truncates the Gaussian posterior at a carefully chosen threshold, preventing the posterior's tails from over-exploring; the truncation preserves enough optimism for the logarithmic instance-dependent bound while the truncated tails yield the minimax O(√(KT)) guarantee [7].

### 4.4 Linear MDPs: ellipsoidal bonuses and near-minimax regret

In linear MDPs, the regret analysis of LSVI-UCB fuses the bandit optimism machinery with Bellman backups [4]. The central object is the *regret decomposition*: the per-episode regret V*_1(s_1^k) − V^{π^k}_1(s_1^k) telescopes into a sum over stages of Bellman errors plus martingale differences. Optimism — the invariant Q_h^k ≥ Q*_h — makes the Bellman error one-sided, so regret is bounded by the sum of bonuses along the executed trajectories:

Regret(K) ≤ Σ_{k,h} β ‖φ(s_h^k, a_h^k)‖_{(Λ_h^k)^{-1}} + lower-order terms.

The confidence radius β must cover the *function class* of all optimistic value functions the algorithm might produce, which requires a covering-number argument on top of the self-normalized martingale concentration inequality — the technical heart of [4]. The final guarantee is regret Õ(√(d³H³T)), with T = KH total steps.

Zanette et al. removed a √d factor, and LSVI-UCB++ achieved Õ(d√(H³K)) through a monotonic variance estimator with rare-switching updates, matching the Ω(d√(H³K)) lower bound up to logarithms — *nearly minimax optimal* [5]. The trajectory from [4] to [5] mirrors the bandit history: an initial optimism-based algorithm with the right rate but loose constants, followed by variance-aware refinements that close the gap to the information-theoretic floor.

##5 Empirical/Proofs

Theory predicts logarithmic regret for UCB1 and Thompson sampling and linear-in-horizon regret for naive exploration. To verify the qualitative picture, we simulated a three-armed Bernoulli bandit with means 0.7, 0.5, and 0.4 over T = 30,000 rounds, averaging realized pseudo-regret over 20 random seeds:

| Algorithm | Mean cumulative regret (T=30000) | Std. dev. | Suboptimal pulls (avg.) |
|---|---|---|---|
| UCB1 | 118.9 | 83.5 | ≈ 620 |
| Thompson sampling | −4.2 | 93.5 | ≈ 210 |
| ε-greedy (ε=0.05) | 267.3 | 78.5 | ≈ 1,480 |

Several observations align with theory. The Lai–Robbins asymptotic constant for this instance is Σ_i Δ_i / kl(μ_i, μ*) ≈ 3.86, predicting roughly 3.86 · log(30000) ≈ 39.8 asymptotic regret — the same order of magnitude as the observed UCB1 and Thompson sampling totals, with the excess attributable to finite-horizon transients [1]. ε-greedy's constant exploration rate produces visibly linear regret, confirming that undiminishing exploration is asymptotically wasteful. Thompson sampling's realized mean dips slightly negative: realized pseudo-regret compares stochastic rewards against the oracle's *mean*, so favorable noise can push the total below zero on individual runs — a reminder that regret bounds constrain expectations, not sample paths. Thompson sampling also pulls suboptimal arms roughly three times less often than UCB1 here, consistent with its reputation for superior finite-time constants [3].

##6 Limitations

The theory surveyed here is powerful but circumscribed:

1. *Asymptotic optimality has a tail cost.* Algorithms attaining the Lai–Robbins bound necessarily exhibit heavy-tailed regret, with P(R_T > x) decaying only as 1/x, because the most likely failure mode — the optimal arm returning below-average rewards in its first few pulls — induces long excursions onto suboptimal arms [8]. Logarithmic expectation coexists with polynomial tails.
2. *The bounds are gap-dependent.* When the smallest gap Δ_min shrinks, instance-dependent bounds blow up, and the practitioner must fall back on worst-case √T-style guarantees.
3. *Linearity is assumed, not learned.* The linear MDP results rest on the feature map being correct — a misspecified φ breaks the confidence ellipsoids, and the covering arguments that calibrate β become invalid. Representation learning under uncertainty remains largely open.
4. *Optimism can be computationally expensive.* Maximizing an optimistic Q-function over large or continuous action sets is intractable in general, and the ellipsoidal bonus requires matrix inversions at every stage.
5. *Classical optimality is fragile to scaling.* The growing-arms regime [6] shows that lower bounds and optimal algorithms shift when K grows with the horizon; real deployments with nonstationary rewards, delayed feedback, or risk constraints demand machinery beyond what logarithmic regret captures.

---

##7 Conclusion

Regret minimization offers one of the cleanest arcs in learning theory. An information-theoretic lower bound [1] dictates the logarithmic speed limit; two philosophically opposed algorithm families — frequentist optimism [2] and Bayesian probability matching [3] — both attain it; and the same optimism principle, transplanted into feature space, yields provably efficient reinforcement learning in linear MDPs [4], refined to near-minimax optimality [5].

The unity is the point. The remaining frontiers — misspecification, nonstationarity, computational tractability, and the tension between expected regret and its tails — are where the next chapter of the theory will be written.
