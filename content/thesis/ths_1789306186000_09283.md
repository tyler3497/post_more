# Pan-Cancer Blood Screening with Circulating Tumor DNA: Shedding Kinetics, Fragmentomics, Methylation Tissue-of-Origin Atlases, and the Positive-Predictive-Value Mathematics of Early Detection

## Abstract

Circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) is the only analyte capable of detecting multiple cancer types from a single blood draw before symptoms appear, yet its translation to screening is constrained by a quantitative reality: early-stage tumors shed roughly one mutant genome equivalent per milliliter of plasma against thousands of normal cell-free DNA fragments, while a population screening test must exceed 99% specificity or drown in false positives. This thesis develops the full mathematical and molecular framework of multi-cancer early detection (MCED). We formalize cfDNA shedding as a stochastic Poisson emission process with rapid clearance, derive the fragment-length and chromatin-footprint statistics underlying fragmentomics, pose methylation-based tissue-of-origin deconvolution as a constrained linear inverse problem, and prove the positive-predictive-value theorem governing all population screening. We review the landmark programs — DELFI genome-wide fragmentation [1][2], error-suppressed CAPP-Seq [11], CancerSEEK [10], the GRAIL atlas program [4], longitudinal PanSeer screening [12], and multimodal ensembles [3][9] — and show why specificity, not sensitivity, is the binding constraint. We close with the open problems that will decide whether liquid biopsy becomes a screening standard.

---

## 1 Introduction

Cancer mortality is dominated not by biology's complexity but by its timing: five-year survival for localized colorectal cancer exceeds 90%, while distant metastatic disease falls below 15%, a gap that is essentially a measurement problem. Conventional screening detects a handful of cancers — breast, cervical, colorectal, lung, prostate — leaving the majority of malignancies without any recommended early-detection modality. Circulating tumor DNA, the fraction of plasma cell-free DNA shed by tumor cells through apoptosis, necrosis, and active secretion, is the only biomarker class that is simultaneously **pan-cancer** (a genomic signal exists in essentially every malignancy), **tissue-informative** (methylation and fragmentation encode the cell of origin), and **quantitative** (tumor fraction tracks burden) [6][7].

The promise collides with three fundamental limits. First, an *abundance* limit: stage I tumors may contribute tumor fractions of 10⁻⁴ to 10⁻³, below the error floor of naive sequencing. Second, a *specificity* limit: clonal hematopoiesis of indeterminate potential (CHIP) — age-related somatic mosaicism in blood cells — generates cancer-like mutations in healthy individuals, and every epigenomic feature has benign confounders. Third, a *statistical* limit: at 1% aggregate cancer prevalence, a test with 99% specificity still yields more false than true positives, as Bayes' theorem dictates. This thesis treats early detection as a joint problem in **molecular biology, signal detection theory, and clinical epidemiology**, and develops each layer with mathematical precision.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The biology of cell-free DNA

In healthy plasma, cfDNA originates predominantly from apoptotic leukocytes and exists as nucleosome-protected fragments with a characteristic length distribution: a dominant mode at **~166 bp** (mononucleosome plus linker DNA), weaker modes at ~330 and ~500 bp (di- and tri-nucleosomes), and a sub-nucleosomal tail from endonuclease trimming [6]. Plasma cfDNA concentration is roughly 1–10 ng/mL, corresponding to a few hundred to a few thousand genome equivalents per milliliter. Clearance is rapid — reported half-lives range from ~16 minutes to a few hours — mediated by hepatic uptake, renal filtration, and plasma nucleases [6]. The consequence is profound for screening: a single blood draw captures a transient snapshot of a high-turnover steady state, and the signal from a 1 cm tumor (∼10⁹ cells, shedding a small fraction daily) must survive this kinetic dilution.

### 2.2 Tumor fraction and the Poisson emission model

Let *cₜ* be the steady-state number of tumor-derived genome equivalents per mL and *cₙ* the normal background. The **tumor fraction** *f = cₜ/(cₜ + cₙ)* is the master parameter of liquid biopsy. Empirical data show *f* scaling roughly with tumor volume: advanced disease reaches 1–30%, while resectable early-stage tumors typically sit at 0.01–0.1% [5][11]. Drawing *N* fragments for sequencing, the number of tumor-derived observations *K* follows *K ~ Poisson(N·f)*. For *N* = 10⁷ (deep targeted sequencing) and *f* = 10⁻⁴, *E[K]* = 1,000 — comfortable; for whole-genome 0.5× screening with *N* ≈ 1.5×10⁷ short reads spanning the genome, per-locus coverage collapses and only genome-aggregated features remain informative [3]. This is why the field split into two architectures: **targeted mutation detection** with molecular barcoding (CAPP-Seq and its descendants), and **genome-wide feature integration** (DELFI, methylation panels) [1][11].

### 2.3 From mutations to methylomes: the analyte ladder

Early ctDNA assays targeted somatic mutations — maximally specific because strong driver mutations are essentially absent from healthy tissue. But early tumors present *few* mutations per captured locus, so sensitivity per milliliter is limited, and CHIP contaminates the signal: mutations in *TP53*, *DNMT3A*, *TET2* appear in the blood of cancer-free older adults, requiring paired white-blood-cell sequencing to subtract [8]. This motivated the analyte ladder:

1. **Somatic mutations** — highest specificity per event, lowest sensitivity per mL at early stage [11].
2. **Copy-number aberrations** — detectable from shallow sequencing but require *f* ≳ 5–10% [8].
3. **Fragmentation (fragmentomics)** — tumor cfDNA is systematically shorter and shows altered nucleosome phasing; the signal aggregates across millions of fragments, working at *f* ≳ 0.1% [1][6].
4. **Methylation** — CpG methylation patterns are tissue-specific and pervasive across the genome, yielding thousands of independent informative loci per sample; methylation classifiers show the highest single-analyte sensitivity for MCED and the best tissue-of-origin prediction [4][7].
5. **End motifs and jagged ends** — cleavage signatures of tumor-associated nucleases [6].

The modern consensus is that **multimodal integration** — methylation + fragmentomics + copy number + motifs — outperforms any single analyte, because the failure modes are uncorrelated [3][9].

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 The screening decision problem

A screening test maps a plasma sample *x* to a binary call *T(x) ∈ {0,1}*. With disease prevalence *p*, sensitivity *Se = P(T=1 | D=1)* and specificity *Sp = P(T=0 | D=0)*, the quantity patients actually experience is the **positive predictive value**:

$$\mathrm{PPV} = \frac{Se \cdot p}{Se \cdot p + (1 - Sp)\cdot(1-p)}$$

At *p* = 0.01 and *Sp* = 0.99, even *Se* = 1.0 yields PPV ≈ 50%; the test's value is dominated by specificity. This is formalized below as the screening-specificity theorem. Trial design therefore prioritizes specificity estimation: the GRAIL Circulating Cell-free Genome Atlas program enrolled thousands of cancer cases and non-cancer controls to pin specificity near **99.5%** [4], and the PATHFINDER prospective study evaluated the Galleri MCED test in asymptomatic adults, reporting a cancer-signal-detection rate with a number-needed-to-screen in the hundreds [4].

### 3.2 Error-suppressed mutation detection (CAPP-Seq paradigm)

Targeted deep sequencing reaches single-molecule sensitivity through **molecular barcoding**: each input molecule receives a unique identifier before PCR, so reads sharing a barcode are collapsed into a consensus, suppressing polymerase and sequencing errors by orders of magnitude. Newman *et al.*'s CAPP-Seq (cancer personalized profiling by deep sequencing) demonstrated detection of ctDNA at allele fractions as low as ~0.0025% with 96% specificity [11]. The methodology generalizes to the following pipeline:

1. **Panel design** — recurrently mutated regions (a "selector") covering the tumor type's driver landscape.
2. **Duplex/UMI consensus** — collapse reads by unique molecular identifier; require variant support on both strands.
3. **CHIP subtraction** — matched leukocyte sequencing removes hematopoietic variants [8].
4. **Statistical calling** — per-position background error models; a sample is positive if the aggregate variant evidence exceeds a calibrated threshold [11].

### 3.3 Fragmentomics: the DELFI construction

DELFI (DNA evaluation of fragments for early interception) treats the plasma fragmentome as a signal-processing problem [1]. Partition the genome into non-overlapping 5 Mb windows (∼504 windows). In each window *w*, compute two features: the **short-to-long fragment ratio** *R_w = N_short(w)/N_long(w)* (short: 100–150 bp; long: 151–200 bp...220 bp) and the **normalized coverage** *C_w*. Healthy plasma shows stable, chromatin-correlated fragmentation; tumor plasma shows elevated *R_w* in windows with copy-number aberrations and disrupted nucleosome phasing [1][6]. A gradient-boosted ensemble over these ∼1,000 features yields the DELFI score. Because each of millions of fragments is weakly informative, the law of large numbers does the work that single mutations cannot at low *f*.

### 3.4 Methylation deconvolution as a linear inverse problem

Cell-free methylomes are mixtures. Let **y** ∈ ℝ^m be the observed methylation vector over *m* CpG clusters, and **A** ∈ ℝ^{m×k} the tissue-of-origin atlas matrix whose columns are reference methylation profiles of *k* cell types (Moss *et al.*'s 2018 atlas pioneered this construction [7]). The deconvolution problem is

$$\hat{\boldsymbol{\pi}} = \arg\min_{\boldsymbol{\pi} \ge 0,\ \sum\pi_j = 1}\ \|\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{A}\boldsymbol{\pi}\|_2^2 + \lambda\,R(\boldsymbol{\pi})$$

where **π** is the vector of tissue proportions. In cancer, the estimated tumor-tissue proportion rises above the healthy null, and the *argmax* of the anomalous component identifies the tissue of origin with reported accuracies of 61–93% depending on assay and cohort [1][4][7]. Deep-learning refinements (e.g., cfSort) replace the quadratic program with attention-based regressors but preserve the inverse-problem structure [7].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The shedding kinetics: a stochastic model of ctDNA emission

Consider a tumor of *N_c* cells, each undergoing apoptosis/necrosis at rate *λ* per day, releasing *γ* genome equivalents per dying cell into circulation, with plasma clearance rate *κ* (half-life *t_½ = ln 2/κ*). The steady-state tumor-derived concentration is

$$c_t = \frac{\lambda\,\gamma\,N_c}{\kappa\,V_p}$$

with plasma volume *V_p* ≈ 3 L. Plugging plausible values — *λ* = 10⁻³/day, *γ* = 0.5, *N_c* = 10⁹ (≈1 cm³), *κ* = 8/day (*t_½* ≈ 2 h) — gives *c_t* ≈ 20 genome equivalents per mL, i.e. *f* ≈ 1% against a background of ~2,000 genome equivalents/mL. Halve the tumor volume to 5 mm and *f* falls to ~0.1%, the regime where mutation-based detection becomes marginal and fragmentomics/methylation aggregation becomes essential [5][6]. The model also predicts **temporal noise**: because clearance is fast, *c_t* fluctuates with bursts of cell death, so longitudinal sampling (as in the PanSeer design [12]) integrates over more independent emissions than a single draw.

> **Theorem (Screening specificity bound).** For a screening population with aggregate cancer prevalence *p*, any test with PPV ≥ 1/2 must satisfy *Sp ≥ 1 − Se·p/(1−p)*. In particular, at *p* = 0.01 and *Se* = 0.7, specificity must exceed **99.3%**.
>
> *Proof sketch.* Set the PPV expression ≥ 1/2 and solve: *Se·p ≥ (1−Sp)(1−p)*, i.e. *1−Sp ≤ Se·p/(1−p)*. ∎

This inequality is the entire strategic content of MCED development: sensitivity can be mediocre, but specificity is non-negotiable [4][5].

### 4.2 Fragment-length statistics and nucleosome phasing

Apoptotic cleavage occurs preferentially in linker DNA, so fragment lengths encode chromatin structure. Tumor-derived fragments are shifted shorter — enriched in the 90–150 bp range — because tumor chromatin is more accessible and tumor-associated nucleases trim differently [1][6]. Three statistics carry the signal:

- **Size-ratio profiles** across 5 Mb windows (the DELFI feature set) [1].
- **Windowed coverage periodicity**: Fourier analysis of coverage at ~190 bp periodicity reveals nucleosome phasing; tumor plasma shows dampened amplitude at transcription start sites of highly expressed genes.
- **End-motif spectra**: the 4-mer sequences at fragment ends reflect nuclease preferences (DNASE1L3 vs. DFFB), and motif imbalance is an independent tumor marker [6].

Critically, these features require only **shallow whole-genome sequencing** (1–2×), making fragmentomics the cheapest genome-wide analyte per sample [1][2].

### 4.3 Methylation: the highest-information analyte

CpG methylation is binary at the molecule level and tissue-specific at the region level, giving MCED assays millions of informative bits. The GRAIL approach uses targeted bisulfite sequencing of hyper-/hypo-methylated differentially methylated regions, training classifiers on the CCGA cohorts spanning 50+ cancer types; reported performance centers on ~51.5% sensitivity at 99.5% specificity overall, with sensitivity rising steeply by stage [4]. Methylation also solves the **tissue-of-origin problem**: because methylation is the primary molecular memory of cell identity, the deconvolution atlas of §3.4 localizes the cancer signal to an organ, which is operationally required — a positive screen without a localization hypothesis triggers an unmanageable diagnostic odyssey [4][7]. The SPOT-MAS assay demonstrated that methylation and fragment-size features can be co-extracted from a single shallow sequencing workflow (~0.55×), reaching 72.4% sensitivity at 97.0% specificity across five cancer types with stage I sensitivity of 73.9% [3].

### 4.4 Multimodal ensembles and the CancerSEEK lesson

CancerSEEK (Cohen *et al.*, 2018) combined ctDNA mutations in 16 genes with 8 circulating protein biomarkers, detecting 69–98% of eight cancer types (ovarian to breast) at >99% specificity in a case-control cohort of 1,005 patients, and localizing the tissue of origin in a majority [10]. The lesson generalizes: each analyte fails differently — mutations fail on low shedding, methylation on benign confounders, proteins on specificity — and a calibrated ensemble exceeds every component. AlphaLiquid's cancer-signature ensemble (methylation + copy number + fragmentation) achieved 88.9% sensitivity at 95.2% specificity, more than 10 percentage points above any single-feature classifier [9]. The DELFI-plus-mutation combination likewise reached 91% sensitivity at 98% specificity [1].

The following table summarizes the landmark assay architectures and their reported operating points:

| Assay | Analytes | Cohort scale | Reported Se | Reported Sp | Key result |

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 The PPV catastrophe, numerically

The screening-specificity theorem is not abstract. A short simulation makes the operating-point trade-off concrete:

```python
import numpy as np

def ppv(se, sp, p):
    return se*p / (se*p + (1-sp)*(1-p))

prevalence = 0.01  # 1% aggregate cancer prevalence in screening population
for se, sp in [(0.70, 0.99), (0.70, 0.995), (0.51, 0.995), (0.90, 0.97)]:
    n = 100_000
    tp = int(n*prevalence*se); fp = int(n*(1-prevalence)*(1-sp))
    print(f"Se={se:.2f} Sp={sp:.3f} -> PPV={ppv(se,sp,prevalence):.3f} "
          f"({tp} true positives vs {fp} false positives per 100k screened)")
```

Output (rounded):

- Se=0.70, Sp=0.990 → PPV ≈ 0.414 (700 true vs 990 false positives)
- Se=0.70, Sp=0.995 → PPV ≈ 0.585 (700 true vs 495 false positives)
- Se=0.51, Sp=0.995 → PPV ≈ 0.507 (510 true vs 495 false positives)
- Se=0.90, Sp=0.970 → PPV ≈ 0.233 (900 true vs 2,970 false positives)

Two conclusions follow. First, the Galleri-style operating point (51% sensitivity, 99.5% specificity) is not a failure of sensitivity engineering — it is the *rational* corner solution of the PPV constraint [4][5]. Second, every half-point of specificity below 99% converts a screening program into a false-positive factory, which is why prospective trials like PATHFINDER measure the diagnostic resolution pathway, not just accuracy [4].

### 5.2 Prospective and longitudinal evidence

Case-control studies systematically overestimate sensitivity because they use clinically detected cancers with higher tumor fractions [5]. The PanSeer longitudinal study is the strongest counterweight: plasma from 123,115 initially healthy individuals in the Taizhou cohort was assayed retrospectively, and the methylation classifier detected cancer in 95% of asymptomatic individuals diagnosed within four years of the blood draw, at 96% specificity [12]. This is the closest empirical demonstration that the ctDNA signal *precedes* clinical diagnosis by years — the lead-time property screening requires.

### 5.3 Statistical calibration and the multiple-testing burden

MCED classifiers scan thousands of features; without rigorous cross-validation and independent test cohorts, performance estimates are optimistic. The credible studies share a methodology: feature selection and model training on a discovery cohort, locked model, then evaluation on held-out or geographically independent cohorts [1][2][3]. DELFI's external validation in the LUCAS lung cohorts (AUC 0.87–0.90) and SPOT-MAS's 738-case/1,550-control design exemplify this discipline [2][3]. Remaining risk concentrates in **spectrum bias** (training on symptomatic cases, deploying on asymptomatic screenees) and **pre-analytical variance** (collection tubes, time-to-processing, extraction kits), which can shift fragment-size distributions as much as early cancer does [6][8].

## 6 Limitations

1. **Sensitivity at the earliest stages remains modest.** Stage I sensitivities of 40–74% across assays [1][3][4] mean a negative test cannot rule out cancer; MCED is a complement to, not a replacement for, organ-specific screening.
2. **CHIP and benign clonal processes** confound mutation-based calls and require leukocyte-matched sequencing, doubling cost and complexity [8].
3. **Tissue-of-origin accuracy is imperfect** (61–93% depending on assay) [1][4][7]; mislocalization wastes diagnostic resources and patient trust.
4. **Lead-time and overdiagnosis bias** are unresolved: detecting indolent lesions earlier may not reduce mortality, and no completed randomized trial has yet shown mortality benefit for an MCED test [5].
5. **Equity and cost**: deep or multimodal assays at population scale must reach price points compatible with annual screening; shallow fragmentomics (~0.55–1×) is the most promising cost lever [1][3].
6. **Pre-analytical standardization** is incomplete: fragmentomics is exquisitely sensitive to handling, and no universal reference materials exist [6][8].

---

## 7 Conclusion

Early cancer detection from circulating tumor DNA is no longer a question of *whether* the signal exists — longitudinal methylation data show it precedes diagnosis by years [12], and genome-wide fragmentomics detects stage I disease from shallow sequencing [1][2]. It is a question of *operating-point engineering under the PPV constraint*: specificity above 99%, calibrated tissue-of-origin prediction, and multimodal ensembles that aggregate weak evidence across millions of fragments and thousands of CpGs [3][9]. The shedding-kinetics model of §4.1 sets the physical floor; the screening-specificity theorem sets the statistical ceiling; everything in between is assay design. The decisive experiments of the next decade are prospective: randomized trials measuring cancer mortality, not just detection rates, and health-economic analyses of the diagnostic workup that follows a positive signal [4][5]. If those trials succeed, the blood draw joins — and eventually subsumes — the fragmented organ-by-organ screening paradigm.
