# Tokamak Plasma Control: MHD Stability, Disruption Prediction, and Magnetic Confinement at Reactor Scale

## Abstract

Magnetic confinement in the tokamak rests on the axisymmetric Grad–Shafranov equilibrium, yet reactor-scale operation demands survival against kink modes, tearing modes, edge-localized modes, and disruptions that dump hundreds of megajoules and drive multi-mega-ampere halo currents into the vessel [1][2]. This thesis develops the full chain from equilibrium to catastrophe to control: the safety-factor profile *q(ψ)* and rational-surface resonances, linear and nonlinear MHD theory of ideal kinks and neoclassical tearing modes, and the empirical disruption boundary (Greenwald density, Troyon beta, *q₉₅*) against DIII-D, JET, EAST, SPARC, and ITER data [2][3]. We then treat the data-driven layer — the Fusion Recurrent Neural Network, which generalized from DIII-D training to JET plasmas, and random-forest predictors on Alcator C-Mod, DIII-D, and EAST [1][3] — and real-time control: preemptive NTM stabilization by ECCD with centimeter-accurate *q*-surface alignment [4][5] and shattered-pellet-injection mitigation for ITER and SPARC [6][7][8].

## 1 Introduction

The tokamak confines a hot plasma with a helical magnetic field: a strong toroidal field *B_φ* generated by external coils plus a poloidal field *B_θ* generated by a toroidal plasma current. The resulting field lines wind around nested toroidal flux surfaces, labeled by the poloidal flux *ψ*, on which pressure, density, and temperature are constant in equilibrium. This elegant picture is the solution of the **Grad–Shafranov equation**,

> **Theorem (Axisymmetric equilibrium):** In steady state, an axisymmetric plasma satisfies ∇*p* = **J** × **B**, and with the representation **B** = ∇*ψ* × ∇*φ* + *F*∇*φ*, the flux function obeys Δ\*ψ = −μ₀R² *dp/dψ* − *F dF/dψ*.

Here *F* = *RB_φ* is the poloidal current function and Δ\* is the Grad–Shafranov operator. Every reconstructed tokamak equilibrium — from the EFIT code used inter-shot and increasingly in real time — is a numerical solution of this equation constrained by magnetics, motional Stark effect, and kinetic measurements [5].

Equilibrium, however, is not stability. Perturbations δ**ξ** grow when the energy principle δW becomes negative, and in high-performance plasmas the free energy stored in pressure gradients and current gradients is large. The *normalized beta* β_N = β(%)·*a*(m)·*B*(T)/*I_p*(MA) is limited empirically to β_N ≲ 4*l_i* (the Troyon limit), beyond which ideal kink and ballooning modes erupt [2]. The *safety factor*,

$$q(\\psi) = \\frac{1}{2\\pi}\\oint \\frac{B_\\phi}{R B_\\theta}\\,d\\ell_{\\mathrm{pol}},$$

measures field-line pitch: where *q* = *m/n* is rational, magnetic islands (tearing modes) can reconnect and grow. Operation is therefore steered to *q₉₅* ≈ 3–4, safely above the *q* = 2 surface where the most dangerous 2/1 modes resonate [2][4].

When control fails, the plasma *disrupts*: a thermal quench (TQ) dumps the stored energy in ~1 ms, followed by a current quench (CQ) on the timescale of the vessel *L/R* time, generating halo currents that exert tens of meganewtons of force on the vessel and accelerating runaway electrons (REs) to tens of MeV [6][8]. ITER, at 15 MA and 350 MJ of thermal stored energy, can tolerate only a handful of unmitigated disruptions in its lifetime [1]. The stakes motivate the three pillars of this thesis: (i) first-principles MHD stability understanding, (ii) machine-learning disruption prediction, and (iii) real-time control and mitigation at reactor scale.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Ideal and resistive MHD

The single-fluid MHD model couples the plasma to Maxwell's equations through Ohm's law, **E** + **v** × **B** = η**J**. In the ideal limit (η → 0), magnetic flux is frozen into the plasma and reconnection is forbidden; instability then requires bending or compressing field lines, giving rise to *kinks* (global helical displacements, e.g., the *m* = 1 internal kink driving sawteeth) and *ballooning modes* (localized on the outboard bad-curvature side). With finite resistivity, field lines can reconnect at rational surfaces, forming **magnetic islands** — the tearing mode. The stability of a tearing mode is quantified by Δ′, the jump in the logarithmic derivative of the perturbed flux across the rational surface: Δ′ > 0 implies instability in classical tearing theory.

### 2.2 Neoclassical tearing modes

In high-temperature, collisionless plasmas, the bootstrap current *j_BS* — a self-generated current proportional to the pressure gradient — introduces a *neoclassical* drive. Inside an island, fast parallel transport flattens the pressure profile, locally removing bootstrap current; the resulting helical current deficit amplifies the island. The island width *w* evolves per the generalized Rutherford equation,

$$\frac{\\tau_R}{r}\\frac{dw}{dt} = \\Delta'(w) + \\Delta_{BS}(w) + \\Delta_{GGJ}(w) + \\Delta_{pol}(w) + \\Delta_{ECCD},$$

where Δ_BS is the destabilizing bootstrap term, Δ_GGJ and Δ_pol are stabilizing curvature and polarization terms, and Δ_ECCD is the control term supplied by localized current drive [4]. Crucially, the NTM is *linearly stable but nonlinearly unstable*: a seed island (from a sawtooth crash or ELM) must exceed a threshold width before growth takes over, which is precisely why active control — shrinking the island below threshold — works so well [5].

### 2.3 The empirical disruption boundary

Decades of operation have mapped the disruption space: the Greenwald density limit *n_G* = *I_p*/(π*a*²), the Troyon beta limit, low-*q* operation (*q₉₅* < 2 invites the 2/1 external kink), impurity accumulation, and locked modes (error-field penetration braking plasma rotation). The locked-mode precursor is so reliable that the *n* = 1 radial field is a standard disruption predictor input [3].

### 2.4 Reactor design points

SPARC, the high-field compact tokamak, is designed for *Q* > 2 with β_N = 1.0, *n/n_G* = 0.37, and *q₉₅* ≈ 3.4 — a conservative point with comfortable margin to the empirical limits and low drive for NTMs at the *q* = 2 surface [2]. ITER targets *Q* = 10 at 15 MA with an ECCD-based NTM suppression system and a shattered-pellet-injection disruption mitigation system (DMS) [4][6][7].

## 3 Methodology

Our analysis proceeds in three layers, mirroring the control stack of a modern tokamak.

**Layer 1 — Equilibrium and stability analysis.** We reconstruct equilibria with a Grad–Shafranov solver (EFIT-style) and compute *q(ψ)*, magnetic shear *ŝ* = (*r/q*)(*dq/dr*), and the Mercier criterion for interchange stability. Ideal stability is assessed via the energy principle; resistive stability via Δ′ from a cylindrical tearing analysis with toroidal corrections.

**Layer 2 — Data-driven prediction.** We review two paradigms: (a) the Fusion Recurrent Neural Network (FRNN), a deep architecture combining convolutional layers (spatial profile features) with LSTM recurrence (temporal evolution), trained on >10⁴ DIII-D discharges and tested on JET — the first demonstration of cross-machine generalization of a disruption predictor [1]; and (b) random-forest classifiers trained on real-time-available scalar signals on Alcator C-Mod, DIII-D, and EAST, achieving ~80–90% shot-by-shot detection rates with interpretable feature importances [3].

**Layer 3 — Control and mitigation.** We analyze the DIII-D NTM control system — real-time *q*-surface reconstruction feeding ECCD alignment to ~1 cm accuracy, with preemptive and modulated deposition strategies [4][5] — and SPI mitigation physics: pellet shattering, impurity assimilation, radiative thermal quench, and runaway-electron suppression, as modeled by the NIMROD, M3D-C1, JOREK, and DREAM codes [6][7][8].

A compact illustration of the predictor layer in code clarifies the data flow:

```python
import numpy as np

def disruption_score(signals, model, threshold=0.5):
    """Realtime disruption score from multi-channel plasma signals.
    signals: dict of 1D arrays, e.g. locked-mode amplitude, li, q95,
             radiated power fraction, density / Greenwald fraction.
    Returns (alarm: bool, score: float, sensitivity: dict)."""
    X = np.stack([signals[k] for k in sorted(signals)], axis=-1)  # (T, C)
    score = float(model.predict_proba(X[None, -64:])[0, 1])       # last 64 ms
    alarm = score > threshold
    # sensitivity: gradient of score w.r.t. each channel (interpretability)
    sens = {k: float(np.abs(model.channel_gradient(X, k)).mean())
            for k in signals}
    return alarm, score, sens

# Example alarm logic: with ~30-50 ms warning the PCS can fire SPI [1][6]
```

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Safety factor, shear, and rational surfaces

The safety factor profile is the skeleton of tokamak stability. A monotonic *q* rising from *q₀* ≈ 1 on axis to *q₉₅* ≈ 3–4 at the edge places the *q* = 1, 3/2, and 2 surfaces at distinct radii; reversed-shear profiles (minimum *q* off-axis) can stabilize turbulence via internal transport barriers but invite double-tearing modes. Magnetic shear *ŝ* enters tearing stability directly: strong shear narrows islands, while low shear near *q_min* widens the vulnerable layer. Real-time knowledge of *q(ψ)* — from magnetics plus motional Stark effect fed to a Grad–Shafranov solver at ~ms cadence — is therefore the *sensor* on which NTM control closes the loop [5]. The DIII-D system achieves *q*-surface/ECCD alignment to order 1 cm, the tolerance required to deposit driven current inside a centimeter-scale island [5].

### 4.2 Kink, tearing, and ELM dynamics

The external kink (*m* = 2, *n* = 1) is the classic low-*q* disruption trigger: as *q₉₅* → 2, the mode couples to the wall, locks, and the plasma follows. Internal kinks (*m* = 1/*n* = 1) drive the sawtooth cycle — periodic core crashes that conveniently provide seed islands for NTMs. Tearing modes at *q* = 3/2 degrade confinement by ~30% in stored energy when they appear; at *q* = 2 they are, statistically, the single most common root cause of disruptions [4]. Edge-localized modes are the price of H-mode: the edge transport barrier steepens the pressure gradient until peeling–ballooning modes erupt quasi-periodically, ejecting ~5–10% of stored energy per ELM. In ITER, unmitigated type-I ELMs would erode the tungsten divertor, so ELM suppression by resonant magnetic perturbations (RMPs) or ELM-free regimes (QH-mode, negative triangularity) is an active frontier.

> **Theorem (Newcomb's criterion):** A cylindrical plasma is ideally stable to *m* ≥ 2 kinks iff *q(a)* > *m/n* at every rational surface inside the plasma; violation at the edge invites the external kink and, at *q* = 2, the most virulent disruption precursor.

| Instability | Drive | Timescale | Disruption role |
|---|---|---|---|
| External kink 2/1 | Current gradient, low *q₉₅* | ~100 μs (ideal) | Direct trigger, wall locking |
| NTM 3/2, 2/1 | Bootstrap current deficit | ms–s (resistive) | Confinement loss; 2/1 → disruption [4] |
| Sawtooth 1/1 | Core pressure, *q₀* < 1 | ~100 ms period | Seed island source |
| Type-I ELM | Edge pressure/current | ~ms, periodic | Divertor erosion; seed for NTMs |
| Vertical displacement | Elongation κ | ~ms (with wall) | VDE → halo currents |

### 4.3 Disruption prediction with deep learning

The FRNN result is a landmark: trained on DIII-D data, it predicted JET disruptions it had never seen, closing in on the ITER requirement of 95% true positives with <3% false alarms at ~30 ms warning time [1]. Its architecture matters — CNNs extract spatial structure from profile diagnostics while LSTMs track temporal precursors — and so does its interpretability layer: per-channel *sensitivity scores* identify *why* the alarm fired (e.g., core radiation and *q₉₅* for impurity-driven events), turning a black box into actionable guidance for the plasma control system (PCS), such as which actuator to engage [1]. Complementary random-forest studies on C-Mod, DIII-D, and EAST showed that the most predictive signals differ per machine — a cautionary result for naive transfer — yet shot-by-shot detection rates of 80–90% were achievable with real-time-capable features on all three [3]. The synthesis: deep sequence models for accuracy and transfer, shallow interpretable models for real-time deployment and physics insight.

### 4.4 Real-time control: shape, NTMs, and the PCS

Modern PCS architectures (DIII-D, EAST, KSTAR) run multiple control loops at kHz rates: vertical stabilization of elongated plasmas (κ ~ 1.8), shape/position control via poloidal-field coils, density control via gas and pellets, and rotation control via neutral beams. NTM control is the flagship *physics* loop. The DIII-D system detects the island, reconstructs the *q* = 3/2 or 2 surface in real time, and steers ECCD deposition onto the island O-point — including *modulated* ECCD synchronized to island rotation, which achieves suppression with less average power, and *preemptive* ECCD that prevents mode onset in NTM-prone scenarios [4][5]. Full suppression of the 3/2 mode was obtained robustly with ~1.5 MW of ECCD, recovering the ~30% stored-energy loss and allowing β above the original onset threshold — a factor ~2 improvement in limiting beta [5]. The 2/1 mode, more demanding, was suppressed in the low-torque ITER baseline scenario by ramping the toroidal field to walk the ECCD deposition onto the *q* = 2 surface [4]. The engineering lesson is alignment: 1-cm accuracy maintained *after* the mode disappears, so the defense persists without a target to track [5].

### 4.5 Disruption mitigation at reactor scale

When prediction fails or time runs out, mitigation must convert an unmitigated disruption into a benign one. The ITER DMS baseline is **shattered pellet injection**: a cryogenic pellet (D₂ with neon) is pneumatically launched at hundreds of m/s and shattered at the vessel entrance; the shards ablate in the plasma, radiating the thermal energy uniformly (reducing localized heat loads) while the injected deuterium raises density to collisionally suppress runaway-electron avalanches [6][8]. Three-dimensional MHD codes (NIMROD, M3D-C1, JOREK) model the assimilation and the MHD response, including thermal-quench onset criteria tied to shard arrival at the *q* = 2 surface and the critical metric of toroidal radiation peaking — asymmetric radiation that can focus heat onto wall panels [8]. For SPARC, modeling shows disruption loads comparable to or exceeding ITER's in some channels (halo currents up to ~50 MN), motivating both massive material injection and a passive runaway-electron mitigation coil that stochastically deconfines REs [2][7]. The mitigation timeline is brutal: detection to injection in tens of milliseconds, thermal quench in ~1 ms, current quench in tens of ms — which is exactly why predictors like FRNN are sized to the ITER 30-ms requirement [1][6].

## 5 Empirical Results / Formal Analysis

We consolidate the quantitative record:

- **FRNN cross-machine prediction:** trained on DIII-D, tested on JET; true-positive rate approaching the ITER target of 95% with <3% false alarms and ~30 ms warning — the first reliable prediction on an unseen device [1].
- **Random-forest predictors:** 80–90% shot-by-shot detection on Alcator C-Mod, DIII-D, and EAST using only real-time-available signals; deployed in inter-shot and real-time environments on EAST and DIII-D [3].
- **NTM suppression:** 3/2 mode fully suppressed with ~1.5 MW ECCD in DIII-D; β limit improved by nearly a factor of 2 after stabilization; 2/1 preemptive stabilization demonstrated in the low-torque ITER baseline scenario; alignment maintained to ~1 cm [4][5].
- **SPARC stability margins:** design point β_N = 1.0, *n/n_G* = 0.37, *q₉₅* ≈ 3.4 with low β_p at the *q* = 2 surface — a tearing-resilient operating space — yet unmitigated halo-current forces up to 50 MN demand mitigation hardware [2].
- **SPI mitigation modeling:** multi-injector SPI increases impurity assimilation roughly with injector count while reducing toroidal radiation peaking; injection geometry (top vs. midplane) strongly affects assimilation via driven poloidal rotation [8]. Runaway-electron modeling for ITER SPI spans the full TQ-onset criterion space (early vs. late onset) to bracket RE current conversion [6].

Formally, the control problem is a race between the Rutherford growth time of the 2/1 island (tens of ms once super-threshold) and the PCS latency (detection + actuator response). The empirical record shows the race is winnable: preemptive ECCD never lets the island nucleate [4], and FRNN-class predictors buy the tens of ms that SPI needs [1][6].

## 6 Limitations

The chain has weak links that honest reactor design must confront. **Prediction transfer** remains fragile: the random-forest study showed the most predictive signals differ markedly between C-Mod, DIII-D, and EAST [3], so FRNN's DIII-D→JET success [1] does not guarantee DIII-D→ITER transfer to a burning plasma with alpha-particle physics, tungsten walls, and ELM-suppressed regimes absent from the training distribution. **ECCD power economics** are sobering: megawatts of electron-cyclotron power spent preemptively on NTM suppression are megawatts not heating the plasma, and ITER's 2/1 stabilization at reactor β_N remains to be demonstrated at full field and current [4]. **SPI assimilation physics** carries order-unity uncertainties — fragment size distributions, drift, and TQ-onset criteria bracket rather than predict RE conversion [6][8]. **Stellarators**, the main alternative, sidestep disruptions by eliminating the plasma current (no current-driven kinks or tearing modes) but pay with 3D coil complexity and neoclassical transport challenges; the Wendelstein 7-X program is the empirical counterpoint, though a reactor-scale stellarator remains unbuilt. Finally, **burning-plasma effects** — alpha-driven Alfvén eigenmodes, self-heating nonlinearity, tritium inventory constraints — lie outside the validated domain of every predictor and controller discussed here.

## 7 Conclusion

Tokamak plasma control at reactor scale is a solved-in-principle, unfinished-in-practice engineering science. The equilibrium theory (Grad–Shafranov), the stability taxonomy (kink/tearing/ELM), and the empirical operating boundaries give us a map of where not to go; deep-learning predictors (FRNN) and interpretable classifiers give us headlights, with cross-machine generalization demonstrated but not yet proven for burning plasmas [1][3]; real-time ECCD control with centimeter-accurate *q*-surface alignment gives us steering, demonstrated to full suppression of 3/2 and 2/1 NTMs including in ITER-relevant scenarios [4][5]; and shattered pellet injection gives us airbags, designed into ITER and SPARC with 3D MHD modeling guiding injector count and geometry [6][7][8]. SPARC's conservative high-field design point [2] and ITER's integrated DMS reflect the same lesson: at reactor scale, *avoidance* (stable operating point + prediction + active control) and *mitigation* (SPI + RE control) must be layered, because no single layer meets the "handful of disruptions per lifetime" requirement alone. The next decisive experiments — SPARC's first plasma at reactor-relevant field, ITER's demonstration of ECCD NTM suppression at 15 MA, and closed-loop FRNN-to-SPI control — will determine whether the control stack scales from today's machines to the burning-plasma era.
