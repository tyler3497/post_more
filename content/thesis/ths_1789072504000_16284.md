# Cost-Based Query Optimization: Selinger-Style Dynamic Programming, Cardinality Estimation, and the Frontiers of Learned Optimizers

## Abstract

Cost-based query optimization decides, for every declarative query, the physical execution plan among an exponentially large space of alternatives. This article traces the intellectual arc of the field: from Selinger et al.'s dynamic-programming framework for System R — left-deep join enumeration, *interesting orders*, and costed access paths [1] — through the empirical crisis exposed by the Join Order Benchmark, which showed that cardinality-estimation error, not enumeration or cost modeling, is the dominant cause of poor plans [3, 4], to the contemporary wave of learned optimizers that predict latency directly from experience [6, 7]. We formalize how multiplicative q-error compounds across join chains, compare classical synopses (histograms, sampling, HyperLogLog and Count-Min sketches) against deep cardinality models (Naru, DeepDB) [8, 9], and analyze enumeration frameworks from Cascades to adaptive techniques such as Eddies and LEO [2]. We conclude that the "cardinality estimation is the root of all evil" thesis is substantially correct but incomplete: robust optimization now demands error-aware enumeration, adaptive execution, and learned components whose failure modes are understood as rigorously as the dynamic programming they aim to supersede.

## 1 Introduction

The central promise of the relational model is physical data independence: a query states *what* to compute, and the system determines *how* to compute it. That determination — plan selection — is performed by the query optimizer, and for half a century the dominant design has been **cost-based optimization (CBO)**: enumerate candidate execution plans, estimate each plan's cost using statistics about the data, and execute the cheapest [1].

Three components carry the design:

1. **Cardinality estimation** — predicting the number of tuples flowing through each operator, since join and sort costs scale with input size.
2. **Plan enumeration** — searching the space of join orderings, join algorithms, and access paths without exploding combinatorially.
3. **Cost modeling** — translating estimated cardinalities and physical properties into a scalar cost (I/O + CPU + network) comparable across plans.

These components interact in a treacherous way. The optimizer searches *as if* its estimates were true, so an enumeration algorithm can only be as good as the estimates that guide it. This article examines each component, the mathematics connecting them, the celebrated experimental evidence that estimation error dominates the other two [3, 4], and the two modern escape routes: **adaptive optimization**, which corrects course during execution, and **learned optimization**, which replaces hand-built heuristics with models trained on observed latency [6, 7, 8].

## 2 Background

### 2.1 From heuristics to cost

Early systems (INGRES, early System R prototypes) optimized primarily by heuristics: push selections down, choose nested-loop joins with the smaller relation outside. Selinger et al. replaced this with a principled separation: logical rewrites transform the relational algebra expression, while physical optimization selects access paths and join methods by *cost* [1]. PostgreSQL's optimizer is a direct descendant — Selinger-style dynamic programming over join subsets, pathkeys encoding interesting orders, and a cost model parameterized by `seq_page_cost`, `random_page_cost`, and `cpu_tuple_cost`.

### 2.2 Cardinality synopses

No optimizer can afford to scan the data per plan candidate, so it maintains **synopses** — compact summaries that answer "how many tuples satisfy *p*?":

- **Histograms.** Equi-width and equi-depth histograms partition a column's domain into buckets and assume *uniformity within each bucket*. One-dimensional histograms capture single-column distributions well but combine predicates with the *attribute value independence (AVI)* assumption, which fails catastrophically on correlated columns.
- **Sampling.** Runtime or maintained samples evaluate predicates against a sample and scale up. Sampling is unbiased under mild conditions but suffers *sampling failure* on highly selective predicates: the interesting tuples may not appear in the sample at all.
- **Sketches.** *HyperLogLog* estimates distinct counts (needed for `COUNT(DISTINCT)` and join-size estimation) in kilobytes with ~1% error via harmonic-mean harmonic estimation of leading-zero hash patterns [10]. *Count-Min* sketches estimate frequencies of join keys with bounded overestimation, useful for foreign-key/primary-key join selectivity. Sketches are mergeable and streaming-friendly, but they fundamentally answer point queries, not arbitrary range predicates over correlated columns.

### 2.3 The cost model

A cost model maps an estimated plan to a scalar. Schematically, for a join operator:

> **Cost formula:** `C(P ⋈ Q) = C(P) + C(Q) + cpu_tuple_cost · |P| · selectivity_cost_join + io_cost(inner scan)`
>
> Every term depends on `|P|` and `|Q|` — the estimated cardinalities of the inputs. The cost model is a *deterministic function of its inputs*; it cannot rescue estimates that are wrong by orders of magnitude.

This observation is the crux of the modern debate: the machinery downstream of estimation is sound, so research effort has migrated toward fixing, bounding, or circumventing the estimates themselves [3, 5].

## 3 Methodology

This article adopts the methodology of a critical survey anchored in reproducible experimental evidence:

- **Canonical decomposition.** We treat the optimizer as the composition `argmin_{P ∈ plans(Q)} Cost(Estimate(P), physical(P))`, following the Leis et al. evaluation framework that *injects* controlled cardinality estimates into real optimizers to isolate the contribution of each component [3, 4].
- **The Join Order Benchmark (JOB).** JOB's 113 queries over the IMDb dataset — riddled with real-world correlations — replaced synthetic TPC-H-style workloads for join-order research and remains the standard testbed for estimation claims [3].
- **Error metric.** We quantify estimation quality with **q-error**, the multiplicative factor between estimate and truth:
  `q-error = max(est, actual) / min(est, actual)`, lower-bounded at 1 to avoid division by zero [8]. Unlike relative error, q-error penalizes underestimates of small cardinalities symmetrically.
- **Scope of claims.** Empirical statements about classical estimators reference Leis et al. [3, 4] and Moerkotte et al. [5]; statements about learned estimators reference Naru [8], DeepDB [9], and the MSCN family [10]; statements about learned optimizers reference Neo [6] and Bao [7].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Selinger-Style Dynamic Programming: Join Enumeration, Interesting Orders, Left-Deep Trees

The number of join orderings for *n* relations grows super-exponentially — *(2n−2)!/(n−1)!* bushy trees, and *n!* even restricting to linear orderings. Selinger et al.'s insight was that the problem has optimal substructure *once physical properties are tracked* [1].

**The algorithm.** For each subset *S* of tables, retain the cheapest plan for every *interesting order* — a sort order on the output that could make a downstream operator cheaper (merge-join key order, `GROUP BY` keys, `ORDER BY` keys). Build bottom-up: single-table access paths first (sequential scan, index scans), then for each *k*-table subset, extend every (*k*−1)-subset plan by joining one more table as the inner operand, keeping only non-dominated plans [1].

```python
def selinger(tables, join_graph):
    best = {}  # (frozenset, order) -> (cost, plan)
    for t in tables:
        for path in access_paths(t):          # seq scan, index scans
            for o in interesting_orders(t):
                keep_cheapest(best, ({t}, o), path)
    for k in range(2, len(tables) + 1):
        for S in subsets(tables, k):
            for T in proper_subsets(S):       # left-deep: |S \ T| == 1
                inner = S - T
                for join_algo in (nested_loop, sort_merge, hash_join):
                    for po in best_plans(T):
                        p = build_join(po, inner, join_algo)
                        keep_cheapest(best, (S, output_order(p)), p)
    return best[(frozenset(tables), required_order)]
```

**Why left-deep trees.** Restricting the inner operand to a base table keeps the search space at *O(n·2ⁿ)* subsets × orders rather than Catalan-scale bushy explosion, pipelines elegantly, and rarely loses much: for the inner/outer cost asymmetry of System R's join implementations, optimal plans are *usually* left-deep [1].

**Interesting orders** are the algorithm's masterstroke. A subplan that is *not* cheapest overall is retained if it produces a useful order — paying a small premium now to avoid an expensive sort later. This is Pareto optimization over (cost, physical properties), the ancestor of Cascades' property enforcement and PostgreSQL's pathkeys [1, 2].

### 4.2 The Cardinality Estimation Crisis: Why Estimates Are the Root of Poor Plans

Leis et al. injected *true* cardinalities into industrial optimizers and measured the counterfactual: with perfect estimates, plans were nearly optimal; with production estimators, plans were routinely orders of magnitude worse. Their findings, replicated across systems [3, 4]:

1. **All industrial estimators routinely produce large errors** — q-errors in the hundreds are common on JOB, and they concentrate in multi-join queries.
2. **The cost model matters comparatively little.** Swapping cost models barely moves the needle; swapping estimate quality moves it dramatically.
3. **Exhaustive enumeration still helps.** Dynamic programming beats greedy heuristics *despite* bad estimates, because it at least compares the same flawed currency consistently.

The mathematics of the crisis is multiplicative error propagation:

> **Theorem:** Suppose each single-table selectivity estimate has q-error at most *q*, and join selectivities are estimated by multiplying single-predicate selectivities (AVI). Then the estimated cardinality of an *n*-way join has q-error at most *qⁿ* — exponential in the number of joins.
>
> *Proof sketch.* Each estimated selectivity *ŝᵢ* satisfies *sᵢ/q ≤ ŝᵢ ≤ sᵢ·q*. The product estimate *Πŝᵢ* satisfies *Πsᵢ/qⁿ ≤ Πŝᵢ ≤ Πsᵢ·qⁿ*; base cardinalities multiply the same way. Hence the ratio between estimate and truth is bounded by *qⁿ*. ∎

With *q = 2* (a "good" per-predicate estimate) and 10 joins, worst-case q-error reaches 1024 — and real workloads add correlation, which AVI turns from a bounded error into an unbounded one. This is why practitioners summarize the situation as *"cardinality estimation is the root of all evil"* in query optimization [3].

Moerkotte, Neumann, and Steidl proposed the principled mitigation: **bound the impact of estimation errors** rather than pretending they can be eliminated. Their framework propagates *upper and lower bounds* on cardinalities through the plan and prunes plans that are not robust — a plan is chosen only if it remains near-optimal across the whole uncertainty interval [5]. This reframes optimization from point-estimate gambling to decision-making under quantified uncertainty.

### 4.3 Plan Enumeration Beyond Dynamic Programming: Cascades, Columbia, and Orca

Selinger's DP is bottom-up and join-centric. Graefe's **Cascades** framework (1995) generalized optimization to a top-down, rule-driven search over an AND-OR DAG (the *memo* structure), where transformation rules (logical rewrites), implementation rules (logical→physical), and enforcer rules (property enforcement, e.g. inserting sorts) uniformly explore alternatives with branch-and-bound pruning [2]. Key advances over Volcano/EXODUS:

- **Unified logical/physical rules** with task-based search and *promise*-ordered exploration, finding good plans early so branch-and-bound prunes aggressively.
- **Enforcers as rules**, eliminating special-cased sort insertion — the direct descendant of Selinger's interesting orders, generalized to arbitrary physical properties (partitioning, distribution).
- **Extensibility**: new operators and rules plug in without rewriting the search engine — the architecture behind SQL Server's optimizer, Greenplum's Orca, and CockroachDB's cascade-style optimizer.

Columbia demonstrated Cascades as a standalone optimizer generator, and Orca brought it to massively parallel, shared-nothing execution where *distribution* is itself an interesting property negotiated during enumeration [2].

### 4.4 Adaptive Optimization: Eddies, LEO, Rio, and Mid-Flight Reoptimization

If estimates cannot be trusted *a priori*, correct them *during* execution. Three designs define this space:

- **Eddies** (Avnur & Hellerstein) route each tuple independently through a set of physical operators, continuously reordering the effective join order based on observed selectivities and costs — continuous, fine-grained adaptivity with no optimizer re-invocation.
- **LEO** (DB2's LEarning Optimizer) compares estimated and actual cardinalities after execution and adjusts future estimates — a feedback loop that learns systematic estimator bias per query template.
- **Rio** and mid-query reoptimization checkpoints validate cardinality assumptions at pipeline breakers; when observed cardinalities diverge, the remainder of the plan is re-optimized with corrected statistics.

The common principle is *decision under uncertainty with recourse*: the optimizer need not commit to a single point estimate when it can observe ground truth mid-flight.

### 4.5 Learned Optimizers: Neo, Bao, Lero, and Deep Cardinality Models

The most radical response abandons hand-built estimation entirely and learns from *observed query latency* — the one ground truth the system always has.

**Learned cardinality estimation.** Two architectures dominate [8, 9, 10]:

- **Naru** (deep *unsupervised* cardinality estimation) trains an autoregressive density model over the table (or full outer join) by maximum likelihood, then answers selectivity queries via progressive sampling. It is data-driven — robust to workload shift — and achieves worst-case single-digit q-errors where classical methods fail by orders of magnitude [8].
- **DeepDB** learns relational sum-product networks (RSPNs) over the data, supporting both cardinality estimation and approximate query answering from one model, with cheap incremental updates relative to autoregressive retraining [9].
- **Query-driven** models (MSCN, LW-XGB/NN) regress from query featurizations to cardinalities [10]; they are accurate on the training workload but brittle under distribution shift — the same Achilles' heel as any supervised approach.

**Learned plan selection.** **Neo** replaces the cost model with a neural network that predicts plan latency from a tree-structured encoding of the plan, bootstrapped from an existing optimizer and refined by executing candidate plans and learning from the observed latency — it matched or beat commercial optimizers on JOB-family workloads [6]. **Bao** takes the pragmatic middle path: instead of replacing the optimizer, it learns (via Thompson sampling over hint sets) which *steering hints* to feed the existing optimizer, keeping the battle-tested enumeration machinery while learning the mapping from query features to good hint choices — deployable without optimizer surgery [7]. **Lero** and related work learn *comparators*: given two candidate plans, predict which runs faster, sidestepping the harder absolute-latency regression.

The through-line: classical CBO optimizes `Cost(Estimate(Q))`; learned optimizers optimize `E[Latency(Q)]` directly from data, collapsing the estimate→cost indirection that the q-error theorem exploits.

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 The Join Order Benchmark evidence

Leis et al.'s experiments remain the empirical bedrock [3, 4]:

- Across commercial and open-source systems, the **runtime spread between the best and worst plans for the same JOB query reaches two orders of magnitude** — join order dominates all other decisions.
- Injecting *true* cardinalities recovers near-optimal performance in almost every case; estimation quality, not plan-space coverage, is the binding constraint.
- Their 2018 follow-up extended the analysis to disk-based engines and confirmed the hierarchy: **estimation ≫ enumeration > cost model** in impact on end-to-end latency [4].

Representative q-error behavior on JOB-style workloads (orders of magnitude, from [3, 4, 8]):

| Estimator | Median q-error | 95th pct q-error | Max q-error |
|---|---|---|---|
| Industrial histogram-based | ~2–5 | ~10² | ~10⁴ |
| Sampling-based | ~1.5–3 | ~10–50 | ~10³ |
| Naru (autoregressive) [8] | ~1.1–1.5 | ~2–5 | <10² |
| DeepDB (RSPN) [9] | ~1.1–1.5 | ~3–8 | ~10² |

> **Theorem (robust plan dominance, after [5]):** Given cardinality intervals [*loᵢ*, *hiᵢ*] for each operator output and a monotone cost function, a plan *P* is *robust* if no alternative plan *P′* has strictly lower cost than *P* at every point in the Cartesian product of intervals. The set of robust plans is exactly the Pareto frontier over interval endpoints, and can be enumerated by DP over (cost-low, cost-high) pairs.
>
> *Implication.* Instead of gambling on point estimates, the optimizer can restrict attention to plans that are defensible across the entire uncertainty region — trading a small constant-factor regret for immunity to catastrophic q-error blowups [5].

### 5.2 Learned optimizer results

- **Neo** (PVLDB 2019): bootstrapped from PostgreSQL's optimizer, Neo's latency-predicting model achieved performance comparable to commercial optimizers and surpassed them on some JOB workloads, while adapting automatically to data skew the hand-tuned cost model mishandled [6].
- **Bao** (SIGMOD 2021): by learning hint sets with Thompson sampling, Bao improved tail latency on real workloads with minutes of training overhead and near-zero deployment risk, since the underlying optimizer still generates all candidate plans [7].
- **Naru vs. classical** (PVLDB 2020): on high-dimensional range queries, Naru achieved up to **90× better tail q-error** than the best classical and supervised baselines, and was the only estimator with worst-case single-digit multiplicative error [8].

### 5.3 Join algorithms interplay

Plan enumeration and join *algorithm* selection are coupled through cardinality: hash join dominates when the build side fits in memory (estimated!), sort-merge wins when interesting orders make sorting free, and index nested-loop wins when the outer cardinality is small. Every decision reads the same flawed estimates — which is why Leis et al. found physical-operator choice to be *downstream* of join-order quality: get the order right and the algorithm choice is forgiving; get it wrong and no algorithm saves you [3, 4].

## 6 Limitations

1. **The q-error theorem assumes AVI-style multiplication.** Real estimators correlate errors in complex ways; the bound is a worst case, not a typical case — but JOB shows the typical case is bad enough.
2. **JOB is a single, read-only, main-memory-friendly workload.** Generalization to streaming, HTAP, skewed updates, and UDF-heavy queries is under-explored; learned models in particular are evaluated on narrow benchmarks.
3. **Learned estimators pay in latency and staleness.** Naru's progressive sampling costs milliseconds per estimate — fine for analytics, fatal for OLTP planning budgets — and autoregressive retraining on updated data is expensive, whereas histograms update in seconds [8, 9].
4. **Learned optimizers lack guarantees.** Neo and Bao offer no worst-case bounds; a distribution shift can silently degrade plans with no diagnostic, unlike Moerkotte-style robust plans whose uncertainty intervals are explicit [5, 6, 7].
5. **Adaptive techniques add runtime complexity.** Eddies-style routing and mid-query reoptimization introduce scheduling overhead and complicate costing, transactions, and parallel execution — the reason most production systems adopt only LEO-style offline feedback.
6. **Interesting orders don't compose across all physical dimensions.** Distribution, partitioning, and NUMA locality multiply the property space Cascades must track, threatening the DP state explosion Selinger's design originally contained [1, 2].

---

## 7 Conclusion

Selinger et al. gave the field its enduring skeleton: dynamic programming over subsets, interesting orders as Pareto dimensions, left-deep trees as the pragmatic restriction [1]. Cascades generalized the skeleton to arbitrary physical properties and rule-driven search [2]. What neither could fix is the information-theoretic core of the problem: the optimizer must decide under uncertainty about data it has not read, and multiplicative error compounds exponentially with query complexity.

The empirical record is unambiguous that **cardinality estimation is the dominant term** in the optimization error budget [3, 4] — but the "root of all evil" framing, while useful as a rallying cry, is incomplete as a research program. Three complementary directions have emerged: *robust enumeration* against uncertainty intervals [5]; *adaptive execution* that observes ground truth mid-flight and replans; and *learned components* that replace the estimate→cost indirection with direct latency prediction [6, 7, 8, 9].

The frontier is their synthesis: learned estimators with explicit uncertainty quantification, feeding robust enumerators, inside optimizers that adapt at runtime. The next decade of query optimization will be won not by whoever estimates best on average, but by whoever *fails most gracefully* when the data surprises them.
