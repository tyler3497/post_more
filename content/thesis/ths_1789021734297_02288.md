# Matching Markets and the Deferred Acceptance Algorithm: Gale–Shapley Stability, School Choice, and Kidney Exchange

## Abstract

The deferred acceptance algorithm of Gale and Shapley (1962) is the most consequential constructive procedure in matching theory: given two finite sets of agents with strict ordinal preferences, it computes in quadratic time a matching no pair can jointly improve upon — a *stable* matching [1]. We prove existence and side-optimality of the stable outcome, characterize the **lattice** of all stable matchings with man-optimal and woman-optimal extremes, and derive the incentive frontier: truth-telling is weakly dominant for the proposing side, yet no mechanism makes it dominant for both sides [2][10]. We trace institutionalization in the National Residency Matching Program and the reconception for school choice, where priorities play the algebraic role of preferences and stability becomes the elimination of justified envy [6][9]. We cross into one-sided markets via Gale's *top trading cycles*, the engine of kidney exchange [4][5][7], and unify both traditions in Hatfield–Milgrom *matching with contracts*, where substitutes and the law of aggregate demand restore deferred acceptance guarantees in priced markets [3].

## 1 Introduction

Few papers in the social sciences have achieved what David Gale and Lloyd Shapley accomplished in seven pages of *The American Mathematical Monthly* in 1962. "College Admissions and the Stability of Marriage" introduced *two-sided matching*, proposed a *deferred acceptance* procedure, and proved a stable outcome always exists [1]. Its algorithm — or provably equivalent procedures — now assigns medical residents to hospitals across the United States, allocates public school seats in New York City, Boston, and Denver, and, through its intellectual descendants, clears thousands of kidney transplants annually [6][9][11].

A *matching market* is one in which the commodity is indivisible, monetary transfers are absent or constrained, and both sides have preferences over whom they match. The central solution concept is **stability**:

> **Definition (Blocking pair):** A pair $(m, w) \in M \times W$ *blocks* a matching $\mu$ if $m$ strictly prefers $w$ to $\mu(m)$ and $w$ strictly prefers $m$ to $\mu(w)$. A matching with no blocking pairs is *stable*.

Stability is a *no-arbitrage* condition: no two agents can jointly defect profitably. Its force is institutional. In labor markets that *unraveled* — hospitals making exploding offers to students years before graduation — the absence of stability destroyed the market [9]. Centralized clearinghouses that produce stable matchings survive; those that do not, collapse — a regularity documented across British physician markets and the American NRMP that elevates stability from curiosity to design requirement [9].

This thesis: (1) reconstructs deferred acceptance rigorously — termination, stability, side-optimality — with a complete worked example; (2) characterizes the **lattice of stable matchings**; (3) derives the **incentive frontier**: proposing-side strategy-proofness versus the Roth impossibility result; (4) traces the two great applications — *school choice* and *kidney exchange* — and the generalization to *matching with contracts*; (5) analyzes computational complexity across the family.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The two-sided model

Let $M = \{m_1, \dots, m_n\}$ and $W = \{w_1, \dots, w_n\}$ be finite disjoint sets. Each man $m$ has a strict preference order $P_m$ over $W \cup \{m\}$ — where $\{m\}$ denotes remaining unmatched — and each woman $w$ has a strict order $P_w$ over $M \cup \{w\}$. A matching $\mu: M \cup W \to M \cup W$ satisfies $\mu(m) \in W \cup \{m\}$, $\mu(w) \in M \cup \{w\}$, and $\mu(\mu(x)) = x$ for all $x$.

The **college admissions model** extends this to many-to-one: colleges have quotas $q_C$ and *responsive* preferences over student subsets — induced by ranking individuals [1][10]. Responsiveness guarantees the one-to-one results lift to hospitals-and-residents, students-and-schools settings.

### 2.2 Intellectual lineage

Three threads converge in modern matching theory: **two-sided stability** (Gale & Shapley 1962 [1]; Roth–Sotomayor; Gusfield–Irving); **one-sided exchange** — the Shapley–Scarf *housing market*, cleared by Gale's *top trading cycles* (TTC), which finds the unique competitive/core allocation under strict preferences [7][8], is strategy-proof [2], and selects the unique strict-core outcome [7]; and **market design practice** — Roth's NRMP reconstruction [9], Abdulkadiroğlu & Sönmez's school choice [6], Roth–Sönmez–Ünver's kidney exchange [4][5], unified by Hatfield & Milgrom's *matching with contracts* [3].

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 The deferred acceptance algorithm

The men-proposing DA proceeds in rounds [1]: each unmatched man proposes to the highest-ranked woman who has not rejected him; each woman *tentatively holds* her most-preferred proposer and rejects the rest (she may displace a held proposal for a better arrival); rejected men propose anew. The algorithm terminates when no man is rejected in a round, and all tentative holds become permanent. Acceptance is *deferred* until termination — the algorithm's namesake and its entire strategic content.

> **Theorem (Gale–Shapley, 1962):** The men-proposing DA algorithm terminates after finitely many rounds, and the resulting matching is stable [1].

*Proof sketch.* Termination: no man proposes to the same woman twice, so at most $n^2$ proposals occur. Stability: if $(m, w)$ blocks the final matching $\mu$, then $m$ proposed to $w$ and was rejected — yet $w$'s held partner's rank never decreases across rounds, so $w$'s final partner is at least as good as $m$, contradiction. ∎

The procedure is *proposer-optimal*:

> **Theorem (Side-optimality):** The men-proposing DA outcome $\mu_M$ is *man-optimal*: every man weakly prefers $\mu_M$ to every other stable matching. Symmetrically, the women-proposing DA outcome $\mu_W$ is woman-optimal [1][9].

*Proof sketch.* Call a woman *achievable* for $m$ if some stable matching pairs them. Suppose some man is rejected by an achievable woman during DA, and consider the *first* such rejection: man $m$ rejected by achievable woman $w$ in favor of $m'$. Some stable matching $\mu'$ pairs $(m, w)$; since $w$ prefers $m'$ to $m$, $\mu'$ must pair $m'$ with someone he prefers to $w$ (else $(m', w)$ blocks $\mu'$). But $m'$ proposed to $w$ before anyone worse, and no achievable rejection occurred before this round — so $m'$ must have been rejected by an achievable woman *earlier*, a contradiction. ∎

The following reference implementation makes the propose-and-reject dynamics explicit:

```python
def deferred_acceptance(men_prefs, women_prefs):
    """Men-proposing DA. Prefs: dict agent -> list, best first."""
    free_men = list(men_prefs.keys())
    next_proposal = {m: 0 for m in men_prefs}   # index into m's list
    held = {}                                   # woman -> man currently held
    while free_men:
        m = free_men.pop(0)
        w = men_prefs[m][next_proposal[m]]; next_proposal[m] += 1
        current = held.get(w)
        if current is None:
            held[w] = m
        elif women_prefs[w].index(m) < women_prefs[w].index(current):
            held[w] = m            # displace: acceptance was only tentative
            free_men.append(current)
        else:
            free_men.append(m)      # rejected; will propose to next choice
    return {m: w for w, m in held.items()}
```

### 3.2 A worked example

With $M = \{m_1, m_2\}$, $W = \{w_1, w_2\}$ and preferences:

| Agent | 1st | 2nd |
|---|---|---|
| $m_1$ | $w_1$ | $w_2$ |
| $m_2$ | $w_2$ | $w_1$ |
| $w_1$ | $m_2$ | $m_1$ |
| $w_2$ | $m_1$ | $m_2$ |

**Men-proposing DA:**

| Round | Proposals | Holds / rejections |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | $m_1 \to w_1$, $m_2 \to w_2$ | $w_1$ holds $m_1$; $w_2$ holds $m_2$ |
| — | no rejections | **Terminate**: $\mu_M = \{(m_1,w_1), (m_2,w_2)\}$ |

**Women-proposing DA:**

| Round | Proposals | Holds / rejections |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | $w_1 \to m_2$, $w_2 \to m_1$ | $m_2$ holds $w_1$; $m_1$ holds $w_2$ |
| — | no rejections | **Terminate**: $\mu_W = \{(m_1,w_2), (m_2,w_1)\}$ |

The two procedures deliver *different* stable matchings: every man prefers $\mu_M$ and every woman prefers $\mu_W$ — the signature of the lattice structure in Section 4.1.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The lattice of stable matchings

The most beautiful structural result in matching theory is Conway's theorem: the set of all stable matchings, ordered by the men's common preference, forms a **distributive lattice** [7][9]. For stable matchings $\mu, \nu$, the *join* $\mu \vee_M \nu$ assigns each man the *better* of his two partners and the *meet* $\mu \wedge_M \nu$ the *worse*; both are stable. The order reverses across sides: the men's join is the women's meet — the formal expression of the sides' *conflict of interest*.

> **Theorem (Conway's lattice theorem):** The stable matchings ordered by "$\mu \succeq_M \nu$ iff every man weakly prefers $\mu$ to $\nu$" form a finite distributive lattice, with greatest element the man-optimal $\mu_M$ from men-proposing DA and least element the woman-optimal $\mu_W$ [7].

Three corollaries carry practical weight. **Rural hospitals theorem** (Roth 1986): the set of *matched* agents is identical across all stable matchings, and each hospital fills the same number of positions in every stable matching [12] — changing *which* stable matching a clearinghouse selects cannot change *how many* doctors serve rural hospitals, a result that settled a policy debate about the NRMP [9][12]. **Decomposition:** the lattice factors into rotations — minimal cyclic exchanges of partners — and every stable matching is reachable from $\mu_M$ by eliminating rotations, yielding enumeration and minimum-weight stable matching algorithms. **Polarization:** the sides' interests are diametrically opposed *within* the stable set — men unanimously prefer $\mu_M$ to $\mu_W$ and women the reverse [1][9]. Stability does not reconcile the sides; it only eliminates bilateral deviations.

### 4.2 Incentives and the impossibility frontier

Deferred acceptance is not merely a computational procedure; it is a *mechanism* in which agents report preferences, and its incentive properties are sharply asymmetric [2][10]:

> **Theorem (Dubins–Freedman 1981; Roth 1982):** Under men-proposing DA, truthful revelation is a *weakly dominant strategy* for every man, and no coalition of men can jointly misreport to make every member strictly better off [10].

The intuition: a man can never gain by proposing out of order, because proposing to a less-preferred woman first only forfeits priority at his true favorite, and deferred acceptance means early proposals confer no commitment advantage. Women, however, *can* profitably manipulate men-proposing DA — for instance by truncating lists to trigger a more favorable rejection chain.

> **Theorem (Roth 1982 — impossibility):** There exists *no* stable matching mechanism that makes truth-telling a dominant strategy for *all* agents on both sides simultaneously [10].

This is matching theory's analogue of the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem: stability and universal incentive compatibility are jointly unattainable. The impossibility bites asymmetrically. In the NRMP — redesigned as an *applicant-proposing* DA — the proposing side (medical students) can safely report truthfully, which is precisely the side the designers most wanted to protect [9]. Hospitals retain theoretical manipulation opportunities, but these vanish in large markets: as size grows, the fraction of agents with any profitable deviation shrinks to zero [9].

The practical lesson is a design maxim: **run deferred acceptance with the side you most need to protect as the proposers**, and rely on market thickness to blunt the other side's incentives.

### 4.3 From two-sided matching to one-sided exchange: top trading cycles

Not all allocation problems have two strategic sides. In *school choice*, schools are passive — their "preferences" are administratively fixed *priorities* [6][10]. In *kidney exchange*, there is only one side: patient–donor pairs who own an endowment (the donor's kidney) and wish to trade. These are *one-sided* markets, governed by the Shapley–Scarf *housing market* model and cleared by Gale's **top trading cycles** (TTC) algorithm: each agent points to the owner of their most-preferred remaining object; every directed cycle executes and is removed; repeat [7][8].

> **Theorem (Shapley–Scarf 1974; Roth–Postlewaite 1977):** TTC terminates at the unique competitive allocation, coinciding with the unique strict-core allocation under strict preferences. The TTC mechanism is Pareto efficient, individually rational, and strategy-proof [2][7][8].

TTC's role in school choice is as the *efficiency* alternative to DA: Abdulkadiroğlu & Sönmez (2003) showed that a TTC variant for school choice is strategy-proof and Pareto efficient for students, whereas student-proposing DA — though it *eliminates justified envy* — can sacrifice Pareto efficiency [6]. DA Pareto-dominates *every other* envy-free mechanism, but TTC may dominate DA on pure student welfare — the central efficiency–fairness trade-off of school assignment [6].

New York City adopted student-proposing DA for high school admissions in 2003; Boston followed in 2005, replacing its manipulable *Boston mechanism* (immediate acceptance, where ranking a school first was strategically costly) after economists showed it penalized unsophisticated families [6][10]. The number of NYC students receiving none of their ranked choices fell from roughly 30,000 to 3,000 in the first year — a natural experiment in mechanism design at metropolitan scale [9].

### 4.4 Matching with contracts: substitutes and the law of aggregate demand

Hatfield & Milgrom (2005) unified the two-sided, auction, and package-bidding worlds in a single framework: **matching with contracts** [3]. A contract $x = (d, h, t)$ names a doctor $d$, a hospital $h$, and negotiable *terms* $t$ — salary, duties, schedules. Agents hold preferences over *sets* of contracts; hospitals choose via a *choice function* $C_h(X) \subseteq X$.

Stability generalizes: no hospital–doctor coalition can block with an alternative contract set. Existence is no longer free — it returns under two regularity conditions [3]:

- **Substitutes:** a contract chosen from a set remains chosen when the set shrinks — hospitals never treat contracts as complements.
- **Law of aggregate demand (LAD):** the *number* of chosen contracts is monotone in the offered set.

> **Theorem (Hatfield–Milgrom 2005):** If hospitals' choice functions satisfy substitutes and the law of aggregate demand, then (i) a stable allocation exists and is found by a doctor-proposing cumulative-offer (generalized DA) algorithm; (ii) the doctor-optimal stable mechanism is strategy-proof for doctors; and (iii) the rural hospitals theorem and lattice structure extend to this setting [3].

The substitutes condition is the precise boundary of the theory: substitutes *alone* — without the implicit *irrelevance of rejected contracts* condition — does not guarantee existence, and weaker *bilateral* and *unilateral* substitutes preserve progressively fewer conclusions [3]. Matching with contracts thus reveals deferred acceptance as the special case of a deeper phenomenon: *cumulative offer processes converge to stable outcomes whenever choice is substitutable* — the same mathematics underlying ascending package auctions.

### 4.5 Kidney exchange: cycles, chains, and computational limits

Kidney exchange is matching theory's most literal life-and-death application. A patient with a willing but incompatible donor forms an *incompatible pair*; pairs can swap donors when cross-compatibilities align, and longer cycles generalize the idea [4]. Roth, Sönmez & Ünver (2004) modeled this as a housing-market variant — patients "own" their donors' kidneys — and proposed the **top trading cycles and chains (TTCC)** mechanism: patients point to their most-preferred compatible kidney (or the waitlist option $w$), kidneys point to their paired recipients, and cycles and $w$-chains are cleared [4].

Two constraints reshape the theory for the operating room [5][11]: **simultaneity** — all transplants in a cycle must be performed at once (early programs managed at most 4–6 simultaneous operations, restricting cycles to length 2) — and **0–1 preferences**, since surgeons report that among compatible kidneys graft survival is essentially equal [5].

Under pairwise-only exchange with 0–1 preferences, the problem collapses elegantly: the compatibility digraph reduces to an undirected graph, and maximum-cardinality exchange equals *maximum matching*, solvable in polynomial time by Edmonds' blossom algorithm; Pareto efficiency coincides with maximum cardinality [11]. Priority-respecting maximum matchings exist and are incentive-compatible in the sense that pairs maximize their match probability by bringing all donors and accepting all compatible kidneys [5]. *Figure 3 (generated diagram)* renders this structure: incompatible pairs as nodes, compatibilities as directed edges, a 3-cycle, and an altruistic-donor chain.

But the general problem is hard: maximizing transplants when cycles of length $\ge 3$ are allowed is **NP-hard**, so modern programs solve large integer programs — typically cycle formulations with branch-and-price [11]. The New England Program for Kidney Exchange (2004), Johns Hopkins, and the Alliance for Paired Donation, together with the 2007 amendment to the National Organ Transplant Act, built the legal and logistical infrastructure; UNOS now runs a national pilot [11]. Simulations suggested TTCC could reduce the fraction of incompatible-pair patients left without a transplant from roughly 45% to under 10% — a welfare gain measured in lives [4].

## 5 Empirical Results and Complexity Analysis

### 5.1 Computational complexity across the family

| Problem | Algorithm | Complexity | Ref |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bipartite stable matching ($n \times n$) | Deferred acceptance | $O(n^2)$ proposals | [1] |
| Enumerating all stable matchings | Rotation poset traversal | $O(n^2 + n \cdot \|\mathcal{S}\|)$ | [7] |
| Pairwise kidney exchange | Maximum matching (Edmonds) | Polynomial | [5][11] |
| $k$-cycle kidney exchange, $k \ge 3$ | Integer programming | NP-hard | [11] |
| TTC (housing market) | Top trading cycles | $O(n^2)$ | [7][8] |
| Matching with contracts (substitutes + LAD) | Cumulative offer | $O(\|X\|^2)$ in contracts | [3] |

DA's quadratic bound is tight in the worst case, but average-case analyses show dramatically fewer rounds; the bottleneck in deployed systems is data plumbing, not the core loop [9]. The hardness cliff in kidney exchange is the family's sharpest computational fact: the *same* clearing problem flips from polynomial to NP-hard the moment simultaneity constraints relax from pairs to triples [11].

### 5.2 Field evidence

**NRMP:** the applicant-proposing redesign now matches ~40,000+ applicants annually; the rural hospitals theorem guaranteed the redesign could not alter rural staffing levels, defusing political opposition [9][12]. **School choice:** NYC's DA adoption (2003) and Boston's (2005) replaced manipulable procedures; strategy-proofness measurably leveled the playing field for families lacking strategic sophistication [6][10]. **Kidney exchange:** from the first New England exchanges to national programs clearing thousands of transplants, the TTCC lineage moved from theorem to operating room in under a decade [4][11].

### 5.3 Incentive robustness at scale

Pathologies of DA — receiving-side manipulation, efficiency loss vs. TTC — attenuate in large markets. The set of agents who can profitably misreport shrinks with market size, and the DA–TTC welfare gap narrows — which is why DA, rather than TTC, became the workhorse of large school districts despite TTC's efficiency edge [6][9].

## 6 Limitations

The deferred acceptance paradigm, for all its triumphs, carries structural limitations:

1. **Stability vs. efficiency.** Stability is not Pareto efficiency. Student-proposing DA can produce outcomes Pareto-dominated by other (unstable) matchings; the efficiency loss is the price of envy-freeness [6][10]. TTC recovers efficiency but abandons stability — in school choice, a defensible trade, since "stability" there is a fairness axiom about priorities, not a participation constraint.
2. **Couples and complementarities.** The NRMP must accommodate couples seeking co-located residencies — a *complementarity* violating substitutes that can destroy stable matchings entirely and makes existence NP-complete. Deployed heuristics carry no existence guarantee; failures are rare but real [9][3].
3. **Preference elicitation.** DA assumes complete strict rankings; real participants rank dozens of options at most, and truncation interacts subtly with quotas and lotteries [6].
4. **Kidney exchange frictions.** The polynomial results require 0–1 preferences and short cycles; real programs face blood-type imbalance, highly sensitized patients, dynamic arrival, and the NP-hard core of long-cycle optimization — addressed by engineering (integer programming, altruistic-donor chains) rather than theory alone [4][11].

---

## 7 Conclusion

Deferred acceptance began as a seven-page existence proof and became the operating system of the world's most consequential matching markets. Its durability rests on an unusual conjunction — *existence*, *optimality* for the proposing side, *tractability* in quadratic time, and *incentives* making truth-telling safe for proposers — that no rival procedure replicates [1][2][10]. The lattice theorem reveals the outcome set's hidden order; the impossibility theorem maps its boundary; TTC and matching with contracts show propose-and-reject surviving the passage from two-sided to one-sided to priced markets [3][7].
