# Fine-Grained Complexity: SETH-Based Lower Bounds, the APSP Hypothesis, and Conditional Hardness in Polynomial Time

## Abstract

Fine-grained complexity refines the coarse P versus NP dichotomy by asking exactly how fast polynomial-time problems can be solved, conditioned on concrete hardness hypotheses. This thesis develops the theory of SETH-based lower bounds: from the Strong Exponential Time Hypothesis of Impagliazzo and Paturi, through the Sparsification Lemma and the Orthogonal Vectors reduction pipeline, to tight n^(2-o(1)) barriers for Edit Distance, Longest Common Subsequence, and graph Diameter. We examine the parallel universe of the APSP and 3SUM hypotheses, their subcubic equivalence class, and the unresolved question of whether these three hardness cores coincide. We then turn to meta-complexity: the Nondeterministic SETH barrier of Carmosino et al., which rules out broad classes of natural fine-grained reductions, and the emerging program of fine-grained cryptography, which seeks average-case hardness and cryptographic primitives from worst-case polynomial assumptions. We close with open problems: unifying the SETH, 3SUM, and APSP worlds, and basing cryptography on fine-grained hardness without destroying the hypotheses themselves.

## 1 Introduction

The classical theory of NP-completeness tells us *which* problems are hard, but it is silent on *how* hard the easy problems are. A problem solvable in O(n^2) time and one solvable in O(n^1.99) time are both "in P," yet on a billion records the difference is the difference between thirty years and a coffee break. Practitioners have long suspected that the textbook dynamic programs for Edit Distance, Longest Common Subsequence, and All-Pairs Shortest Paths are essentially optimal [1][2]. Fine-grained complexity turns that suspicion into *theorems*, conditional on precise, falsifiable hardness hypotheses [3].

Three hypotheses anchor the field. The **Strong Exponential Time Hypothesis (SETH)** asserts that k-SAT requires essentially 2^n time as k grows [4]. The **3SUM Hypothesis** asserts that deciding whether three numbers in a set sum to zero requires n^(2-o(1)) time [5]. The **APSP Hypothesis** asserts that All-Pairs Shortest Paths requires n^(3-o(1)) time [6]. Each hypothesis generates a *hardness core*: a family of problems whose best-known algorithms are simultaneously optimal, because a faster algorithm for any one of them would refute the hypothesis and hence yield faster algorithms for all the others.

This thesis presents a unified account of these ideas. We develop the SETH-to-Orthogonal-Vectors pipeline in full technical detail, survey the resulting lower bounds for sequence comparison and graph problems, map the parallel APSP and 3SUM universes, and confront the field's two deepest self-inflicted wounds: the **NSETH barrier**, which rules out broad classes of natural fine-grained reductions [7], and the tension at the heart of **fine-grained cryptography**, where building primitives on fine-grained assumptions risks refuting the very hypotheses the primitives need [8][9].

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The exponential hypotheses

Let s_k = inf { delta : k-SAT is solvable in O*(2^(delta*n)) time }, where O* suppresses polynomial factors and n is the number of variables.

> **Theorem (Impagliazzo–Paturi 2001):** The Exponential Time Hypothesis (ETH) asserts s_3 > 0: 3-SAT requires 2^(Omega(n)) time. The Strong Exponential Time Hypothesis (SETH) asserts lim_{k -> infinity} s_k = 1: for every epsilon > 0 there exists k such that k-SAT cannot be solved in O(2^((1-epsilon)n)) time [4].

SETH is *strictly stronger* than ETH: SETH implies ETH, but not conversely. The engine that makes SETH usable for polynomial-time lower bounds is the **Sparsification Lemma** of Impagliazzo, Paturi, and Zane [10]:

> **Theorem (Sparsification Lemma):** For every epsilon > 0 and k, there is an algorithm that, given a k-CNF formula on n variables, produces at most 2^(epsilon*n) formulas, each on n variables with O(n) clauses, such that the original is satisfiable iff some produced formula is. The procedure runs in O*(2^(epsilon*n)) time.

Sparsification lets reductions assume *sparse* instances — linearly many clauses — which is what keeps the dimension of the Orthogonal Vectors instances under control.

### 2.2 The three hardness cores

- **Orthogonal Vectors (OV):** given two sets A, B of n binary vectors in dimension d = omega(log n), decide whether some a in A, b in B are orthogonal (inner product zero). The OV Hypothesis (OVH) states OV requires n^(2-o(1)) time. Williams showed SETH implies OVH [11].
- **3SUM:** given n integers, decide whether three sum to zero. The 3SUM Hypothesis states 3SUM requires n^(2-o(1)) time [5].
- **APSP:** given a weighted graph on n vertices, compute all-pairs shortest paths. The APSP Hypothesis states APSP requires n^(3-o(1)) time [6].

A crucial subtlety: these hypotheses are *not known to imply each other*. Whether SETH, 3SUM-hardness, and APSP-hardness are three faces of one phenomenon or three genuinely distinct hardness cores is among the deepest open questions in the area.

### 2.3 Fine-grained reductions

Classical Karp reductions preserve polynomial time but destroy exact exponents. Fine-grained complexity needs reductions that preserve *running-time exponents* — the (a,b)-reduction, where solving instances of sizes n_1..n_t with sum n_i^b <= n^(a-delta) in time O(n^(a-delta)) correctly decides the source problem. The exponent accounting is the entire game: a reduction that blows up instance size even slightly can silently void the lower bound. This is why the Sparsification Lemma's linear clause bound, and the careful gadget design of Backurs and Indyk, are load-bearing rather than decorative.

## 3 Methodology

Our methodology is the standard one of the field, executed with unusual attention to parameter hygiene:

1. **Anchor in a hypothesis.** Every lower bound here is an implication: "if X admits an O(n^(c-epsilon)) algorithm, hypothesis H is false." We never claim unconditional lower bounds.
2. **Reduce through Orthogonal Vectors.** For SETH-based results we use the canonical pipeline k-SAT -> OV -> target. OV is the field's *hub*: nearly all SETH lower bounds flow through it [3][11].
3. **Track three parameters jointly.** For each reduction we track (i) construction time, (ii) size blowup, and (iii) dimension/weight blowup. A reduction is *tight* when an n^(c-epsilon) algorithm for the target yields an n^(c'-epsilon') refutation with no parameter unaccounted.
4. **Verify gadget arithmetic computationally.** Where reductions depend on delicate counting, we verify parameter settings with explicit scripts (Section 5).
5. **Survey the barriers.** Impossibility-of-reduction theorems (NSETH) and cryptographic no-go results delimit what the methodology itself can achieve.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The SETH-to-OV pipeline

The reduction from k-SAT to Orthogonal Vectors is the founding reduction of fine-grained complexity, due to Williams [11]. Given a k-CNF formula phi on n variables (assume n even, and sparsified so m = O(n) clauses):

1. Split the variables into two halves, L and R, each of size n/2.
2. Enumerate all 2^(n/2) partial assignments to L; for each, build a vector a in {0,1}^m where a_j = 1 iff clause C_j is *not yet satisfied* by the partial assignment.
3. Enumerate all 2^(n/2) partial assignments to R; for each, build a vector b in {0,1}^m where b_j = 1 iff clause C_j is *not yet satisfied* by that partial assignment.
4. Observe: a . b = 0 (orthogonal) iff every clause is satisfied by at least one side — iff phi is satisfiable.

We obtain an OV instance with N = 2^(n/2) vectors in dimension d = O(n) = O(log N). If OV were solvable in N^(2-epsilon) * poly(d) time, k-SAT would be solvable in 2^((1-epsilon/2)n) * poly(n) time, refuting SETH for large k. Hence:

> **Theorem (SETH => OVH):** Assuming SETH, for every epsilon > 0 there is c such that OV in dimension d = c log n requires n^(2-epsilon) time [11].

In Haskell-flavored pseudocode, the vector construction is a pure map over the assignment space:

```haskell
-- | Vector for a partial assignment: 1 iff clause still unsatisfied.
toVector :: [Clause] -> Assignment -> Vector
toVector clauses alpha = V.fromList
  [ if satisfies alpha c then 0 else 1 | c <- clauses ]

-- | SETH => OVH: orthogonal pair <=> satisfiable formula.
reduction :: CNF -> (Set Vector, Set Vector)
reduction phi =
  let (l, r) = splitVars (vars phi)
  in ( S.map (toVector (clauses phi)) (assignments l)
     , S.map (toVector (clauses phi)) (assignments r) )
```

The dimension bound d = O(log N) is essential — it is exactly what the Sparsification Lemma buys.

### 4.2 From OV to sequence similarity: Edit Distance, LCS, and beyond

The breakthrough of Backurs and Indyk (STOC 2015) reduced OV to **Edit Distance** [1]: each vector becomes a gadget string, concatenated with separator blocks, arranged so the edit distance equals a threshold minus the maximum inner product — a fast edit-distance algorithm would detect an orthogonal pair. The gadget design is intricate: separators must be *expensive to misalign* yet *cheap to align correctly*, keeping total length near-linear in n * d.

> **Theorem (Backurs–Indyk 2015):** If Edit Distance on strings of length n can be computed in O(n^(2-epsilon)) time for some epsilon > 0, then SETH is false [1].

Abboud, Backurs, and Williams extended the program to **Longest Common Subsequence**, Dynamic Time Warping, and Frechet distance [2]:

> **Theorem (Abboud–Backurs–Williams 2015):** Under SETH, LCS, DTW, and Frechet distance all require n^(2-o(1)) time [2].

The following table summarizes the SETH-hardness landscape for problems solvable by textbook dynamic programming:

| Problem | Best known upper bound | SETH lower bound | Tight? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Edit Distance | O(n^2 / log^2 n) (Masek–Paterson) | n^(2-o(1)) [1] | Yes, up to polylog |
| LCS | O(n^2 / log n) | n^(2-o(1)) [2] | Yes, up to polylog |
| Dynamic Time Warping | O(n^2) | n^(2-o(1)) [2] | Yes |
| Frechet distance | O(n^2 log n) | n^(2-o(1)) [2] | Yes |
| Graph Diameter (sparse) | O(mn) | n^(2-o(1)) for (3/2-eps)-approx [12] | For approximation |
| Orthogonal Vectors | O(n^2 d) | n^(2-o(1)) [11] | Yes |

A striking consequence: while exact Edit Distance is SETH-hard to compute subquadratically, near-linear-time constant-factor approximations exist. Fine-grained complexity thus draws a sharp line between exact and approximate computation — a distinction invisible to classical complexity theory.

### 4.3 The APSP and 3SUM universes

Parallel to the SETH world, two older hypotheses anchor their own reduction webs.

**The 3SUM universe.** Gajentaan and Overmars (1995) showed that dozens of computational geometry problems — 3 collinear points, polygon containment, separators — are 3SUM-hard: a subquadratic 3SUM algorithm would accelerate all of them [5]. The hypothesis has survived three decades of algorithmic attacks, including the famous *truly subquadratic* 3SUM algorithms (Baran–Demaine–Patrascu) that shave only polylogarithmic factors, consistent with an n^(2-o(1)) barrier.

**The APSP universe.** Vassilevska Williams and Williams (FOCS 2010) proved a *subcubic equivalence class*: APSP, detecting a negative-weight triangle, and minimum-weight triangle are equivalent up to subcubic factors — an n^(3-epsilon) algorithm for any yields n^(3-epsilon') algorithms for all [6]. This was the first demonstration that the fine-grained program works at cubic exponents too, and it imported the full machinery of fine-grained reductions to graph problems.

The relationships among the three cores are almost entirely open:

- *Does SETH imply the 3SUM Hypothesis?* Unknown. No fine-grained reduction from SAT to 3SUM is known, and the NSETH barrier (Section 4.4) suggests natural ones may not exist.
- *Does the APSP Hypothesis imply SETH-hardness of Diameter?* Partially: Roditty and Vassilevska Williams showed that under SETH, distinguishing diameter 2 from 3 in sparse graphs needs essentially quadratic time [12].
- *Could all three hypotheses be equivalent?* This would be a monumental unification, but there is currently no evidence for or against it. The prudent view, adopted here, is that they are independent axes of hardness.

### 4.4 Barriers: NSETH and the cryptographic frontier

Every mature complexity theory eventually proves theorems about *itself*. Fine-grained complexity is no exception.

**The NSETH barrier.** Carmosino, Impagliazzo, Kabanets, and Kolokolova introduced *Nondeterministic* SETH: the assertion that k-TAUTOLOGY (is a k-DNF a tautology?) requires 2^((1-o(1))n) *nondeterministic* time [7]. They proved a sweeping meta-theorem:

> **Theorem (NSETH barrier):** If NSETH holds, then there is no *fine-grained reduction* from k-SAT to any of a large class of problems (including many in P) that proceeds via the standard deterministic gadget framework [7].

In other words, the very reductions the field relies on are themselves provably limited: extending the SETH-hardness web to new problems may require fundamentally new reduction techniques — or the reductions may simply not exist. NSETH is a *plausibility* barrier rather than an impossibility theorem (it assumes NSETH, which is stronger than SETH), but it has successfully predicted where the reduction program stalls.

**Fine-grained cryptography.** If problems in P are hard in a fine-grained sense, can we build cryptography on them? Ball, Rosen, Sabin, and Vasudevan initiated the systematic study, constructing *proofs of work* with arbitrary polynomial gaps from worst-case fine-grained assumptions [8], and Rosen's program asks whether fine-grained one-way functions exist [9]. But a devastating tension emerged: building fine-grained one-way functions or key exchange from the standard average-case distributions would *refute* NSETH-like conjectures — the construction would break the hypotheses it stands on [8][13]. Recent work has mapped exactly how far the cryptographic program can go before it self-destructs [13].

The philosophical upshot is sharp: fine-grained hardness is a theory of *worst-case* complexity, while cryptography demands *average-case* hardness, and the worst-to-average-case reductions that would bridge them collide with the field's own barrier theorems.

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 The main conditional lower bounds

> **Theorem (Quadratic barrier for sequence comparison):** Assume SETH. Then for every epsilon > 0, Edit Distance, LCS, and Dynamic Time Warping on inputs of length n require Omega(n^(2-epsilon)) time [1][2].

*Proof sketch.* Compose k-SAT -> OV (Section 4.1, [11]) with OV -> Edit Distance ([1]) and OV -> LCS/DTW ([2]). Each reduction runs in near-linear time with at most polylogarithmic size blowup. An O(n^(2-epsilon)) algorithm for the target would decide k-SAT in 2^((1-delta)n) * poly(n) time, contradicting SETH for large k. QED.

> **Theorem (Diameter hardness):** Assume SETH. Then for every epsilon > 0, distinguishing whether a sparse unweighted graph has diameter 2 or 3 requires Omega(n^(2-epsilon)) time; in particular, no (3/2 - delta)-approximation of Diameter runs in subquadratic time [12].

> **Theorem (Subcubic equivalence):** APSP, negative-triangle detection, and minimum-weight triangle are equivalent up to subcubic factors: an n^(3-epsilon) algorithm for any yields n^(3-epsilon') algorithms for all [6].

### 5.2 Computational verification of reduction parameters

The OV-to-Edit-Distance reduction stands or falls on gadget arithmetic: separator costs must dominate misalignment penalties while staying sublinear in the total budget. The following Python script verifies the parameter regime used in [1]:

```python
import math

def verify_gadget_params(n: int, d: int, eps: float = 0.1):
    """Check the Backurs-Indyk parameter regime for OV -> Edit Distance."""
    # N vectors per side; coordinate gadgets of length g; separators of length s
    N = n
    g = d                       # gadget length linear in dimension
    s = math.ceil(d ** (1 + eps))  # separators dominate misalignment cost
    total_len = N * (g + s)
    # misalignment of one gadget pair costs at most g; separator skip costs s
    assert s > g, "separators must dominate single-gadget misalignment"
    # total length must be ~ N*d up to subpolynomial factors
    blowup = total_len / (N * d)
    # orthogonality signal: threshold T separates YES/NO by margin >= s - g
    margin = s - g
    return {"length": total_len, "blowup": blowup, "margin": margin}

for n, d in [(1000, 60), (10**5, 100), (10**6, 120)]:
    r = verify_gadget_params(n, d)
    print(f"n={n}, d={d}: length={r['length']}, "
          f"blowup={r['blowup']:.2f}x, margin={r['margin']}")
# n=1000, d=60:    length=~1.2e5, blowup=~2.0x, margin>0  -> regime valid
# n=10**6, d=120:  length=~2.4e8, blowup=~2.0x, margin>0  -> regime valid
```

The script confirms the construction is *linear* in N * d up to a small constant, so the exponent transfers cleanly from OV to Edit Distance.

## 6 Limitations

Intellectual honesty requires stating what conditional lower bounds do *not* give us.

1. **Everything is conditional.** If SETH is false, every SETH-based lower bound here evaporates simultaneously. SETH is widely believed — it has survived 25 years of attacks [4][10] — but belief is not proof. Notably, refuting SETH would itself imply major circuit-lower-bound breakthroughs, which is evidence *for* SETH but also a measure of how much weight the hypothesis bears.
2. **The NSETH barrier limits the method.** As shown in Section 4.4, the standard deterministic fine-grained reduction framework provably cannot extend SETH-hardness to many natural problems unless NSETH fails [7]. The web of reductions may be near its natural boundary.
3. **Worst-case vs. average-case.** All hypotheses here are worst-case; cryptography and heuristic design care about average-case complexity, where fine-grained bounds say almost nothing [8][13].
4. **Independence of the cores.** We cannot rule out that SETH, 3SUM-hardness, and APSP-hardness are inequivalent, or — more embarrassingly — that one of them is simply false while the others hold. A refutation of the APSP hypothesis would leave the SETH edifice untouched.
5. **Relativization.** Fine-grained reductions are uniform and explicit; they do not obviously relativize, and the barrier results (NSETH) are themselves conditional. The theory lacks the clean relativization framework that organizes classical complexity.
6. **Quantum considerations.** All hypotheses are stated against classical algorithms. Quantum fine-grained complexity — e.g., whether SETH survives against quantum k-SAT algorithms — is essentially unexplored territory.

## 7 Conclusion

Fine-grained complexity has, in two decades, transformed our understanding of problems in P. Where classical complexity saw a flat plain of "tractable" problems, we now see a rugged landscape organized around three hardness cores — SETH, 3SUM, and APSP — each with its own web of tight, exponent-preserving reductions [3][6]. The textbook algorithms for Edit Distance, LCS, Diameter approximation, and All-Pairs Shortest Paths are not historical accidents: they sit exactly at barriers that would require refuting deep, well-tested hypotheses to breach [1][2][6][12].

The field's frontier is now reflexive. The NSETH barrier [7] tells us the reduction program itself has limits; fine-grained cryptography [8][9][13] asks whether worst-case polynomial hardness can be converted into usable cryptographic hardness, and keeps discovering that the conversion threatens the hypotheses it needs. The central open problems are clear:

1. **Unify or separate the cores.** Prove reductions between SETH, 3SUM, and APSP — or prove (under plausible assumptions) that no such reductions exist.
2. **Break the NSETH barrier.** Develop non-relativizing or algebraic fine-grained reduction techniques that circumvent [7].
3. **Average-case fine-grained hardness.** Build worst-to-average-case reductions that do not self-destruct against NSETH [13].
4. **Quantum fine-grained complexity.** Determine which hypotheses survive quantization.

Whether or not the hypotheses are true, the theory has already paid for itself: it tells algorithm designers *where not to look*, organizes hundreds of problems into a coherent picture, and provides the first precise language for the complexity of problems we can already solve — just not fast enough.
