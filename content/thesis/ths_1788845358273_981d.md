# Differentiable Physics for Robot Learning: Brax and MuJoCo XLA Contact Gradients, Analytical Jacobians through Contact-Rich Dynamics, and Policy Optimization via End-to-End Differentiation

## Abstract

Differentiable physics engines promise a fundamentally new regime for robot learning: replacing sample-inefficient model-free reinforcement learning with analytic policy gradients obtained by backpropagating through the equations of motion. Yet contact-rich dynamics — the very heart of locomotion and manipulation — are non-smooth, discontinuous, and notoriously ill-behaved under differentiation. This thesis presents a comprehensive treatment of end-to-end differentiable rigid-body simulation as implemented in Brax and MuJoCo XLA (MJX), from the mathematical foundations of contact Jacobians and implicit differentiation through non-smooth complementarity problems, to practical algorithms — analytic policy gradients (APG), Short-Horizon Actor-Critic (SHAC), and Adaptive Horizon Actor-Critic (AHAC) — that stabilize gradient-based optimization in the presence of stiff contacts. We derive the adjoint equations for trajectory optimization through differentiable simulators, characterize the gradient pathology caused by complementarity slackness, and survey empirical evidence across locomotion benchmarks showing order-of-magnitude sample-efficiency gains over model-free baselines. We conclude with open problems in gradient correctness through contact, implicit layer scalability, and sim-to-real transfer of gradient-optimized policies.

---

## 1. Introduction

Robot learning has historically been divided between *model-based* methods that exploit analytic structure and *model-free* methods that treat the world as an opaque oracle. The former offer sample efficiency and precise local optimization; the latter offer generality at the cost of staggering sample counts — millions of environment interactions for tasks a human learns in minutes. **Differentiable physics simulation** proposes to collapse this divide: if the simulator itself is a differentiable program, then policy optimization becomes a problem of gradient descent through the transition dynamics, yielding first-order policy gradients without Monte-Carlo likelihood-ratio estimators and their attendant variance.

The vision is compelling but the execution is treacherous. A rigid-body simulator with contact is a *hybrid dynamical system*: continuous flows punctuated by discrete events — contact formation and release, static-to-kinetic friction transitions, and impact impulses. At these events, the transition map is non-smooth or discontinuous, and its Jacobian may not exist, may explode in norm, or may be *correct* yet useless (pointing in directions that optimize the instantaneous gradient while degrading the trajectory). Early differentiable simulators [3][7] demonstrated elegant gradients on smooth problems but frequently failed or underperformed on contact-rich locomotion relative to model-free reinforcement learning.

Two landmark open-source systems brought differentiable, massively parallel simulation to the robotics mainstream. **Brax** [1], written in JAX, demonstrated millions of physics steps per second on a single TPU by composing `jit`, `vmap`, and `grad` primitives around rigid-body dynamics, and shipped learning algorithms that exploit the simulator's differentiability directly. **MuJoCo XLA (MJX)** [4] reimplemented the mature MuJoCo engine in JAX, inheriting MuJoCo's soft-constraint convex contact solver while gaining end-to-end differentiability and accelerator parallelism. Together with the algorithmic advances of **SHAC** [3] (Short-Horizon Actor-Critic) and **AHAC** [7] (Adaptive Horizon Actor-Critic), these systems constitute the first credible stack in which analytic gradients through contact-rich dynamics reliably train locomotion policies.

This thesis is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews the lineage of differentiable simulators and the gradient-correctness literature. Section 3 develops the mathematical machinery: generalized-coordinate dynamics, contact as a complementarity problem, autodiff versus implicit differentiation, and the adjoint method for trajectory gradients. Section 4 deep-dives into Brax's pipeline architecture, MJX's constraint-solver differentiability, the SHAC/AHAC algorithms, and the contact-gradient pathology literature. Section 5 surveys empirical results. Section 6 discusses limitations, and Section 7 concludes.

## 2. Background and Related Work

### The lineage of differentiable simulators

Differentiable simulation descends from several traditions. The *optimal control* literature long ago recognized that trajectory optimizers (DDP, iLQR) require dynamics Jacobians $\partial f / \partial x$ and $\partial f / \partial u$, historically obtained by finite differences or hand-derived analytic derivatives. The *differentiable programming* revolution — JAX, PyTorch, DiffTaichi — made reverse-mode autodiff through arbitrary code the default. Applying this to physics yielded engines such as **DiffTaichi** (differentiable MPM and rigid bodies), **Nimble** (differentiable LCP-based contact), **Dojo** (differentiable NCP contact with implicit differentiation), and **Tiny Differentiable Simulator** (TDS) from which Brax's generalized pipeline drew inspiration [1][2].

Two design axes separate these engines: the *contact model* (penalty versus complementarity; LCP versus NCP versus convex CCP) and the *differentiation strategy* (unrolled autodiff versus implicit differentiation) [6]. Penalty contacts are smooth but stiff; complementarity contacts are principled but non-smooth.

### Gradient correctness through contact

A sobering literature asks whether the gradients these engines return are the *right* gradients. Geilinger et al. and subsequent analyses [5] showed that for colliding bodies, differentiating through discrete-time contact resolution can produce gradients with respect to position, velocity, and control that disagree with the true sensitivity of the continuous-time system — particularly when the *time of impact* depends on the parameters being differentiated. The analytic counterexample is instructive: for two colliding spheres, the loss gradient with respect to initial position computed through a fixed-step simulator can have the wrong sign relative to differentiating the closed-form solution, because the discrete integrator "freezes" the collision time. Techniques such as *continuous collision detection* and *time-of-impact differentiation* partially repair this, but most production engines (including MJX) accept the discrete approximation.

> Theorem: *(Informal — sensitivity of discrete contact maps.)* Let $\Phi_h: (q, v, u) \mapsto (q', v')$ be one step of a fixed-step differentiable simulator with hard contact resolution. At a state where a contact event occurs strictly inside the step, $\Phi_h$ is generally non-differentiable in the Fréchet sense; the subdifferential computed by reverse-mode autodiff corresponds to the Jacobian of the *frozen-event* map, which may differ in sign and magnitude from the true parametric sensitivity of the hybrid system.

This does not render the gradients useless — SHAC and APG demonstrably work — but it explains *why* naive backpropagation through time (BPTT) over long horizons fails, and why horizon truncation, gradient smoothing, and critic bootstrapping are not optional refinements but structural necessities.

### From BPTT to analytic policy gradients

The earliest gradient-based policy learning through simulators applied BPTT naively: unroll the policy and dynamics for the full episode, backpropagate the return, and step. On smooth systems this is sample-efficient; on contact-rich systems the loss landscape becomes chaotic — tiny parameter changes flip contact sequences, and gradient norms grow exponentially with horizon length (Section 4.4). SHAC [3] resolved this by truncating the differentiable rollout to a short horizon $h$ and bootstrapping with a learned value function, recovering a smooth surrogate objective. AHAC [7] made the horizon adaptive, truncating rollouts when stiff contact is detected (i.e., when $\|\nabla f(s_t, a_t)\|$ exceeds a threshold $C$), directly targeting the gradient-explosion mechanism.

## 3. Methodology

### 3.1 Rigid-body dynamics in generalized coordinates

We consider articulated rigid-body systems with generalized positions $q \in \mathcal{Q}$ and velocities $v \in \mathbb{R}^n$. The continuous equations of motion are

$$
M(q)\,\dot{v} + c(q, v) = \tau + J_c(q)^\top \lambda,
$$

where $M(q)$ is the joint-space inertia matrix, $c(q,v)$ collects Coriolis, centrifugal, and gravitational terms, $\tau$ are generalized (actuation) forces, $J_c(q)$ is the contact Jacobian mapping contact-space forces $\lambda$ to generalized forces, and $\lambda$ are the contact impulses/forces to be determined. Forward dynamics computes $\dot{v}$ given $(q, v, \tau, \lambda)$; the celebrated recursive Newton–Euler and composite-rigid-body algorithms evaluate $M(q)$ and $c(q,v)$ in $O(n)$ and $O(n^2)$ respectively, and both Brax's generalized pipeline and MJX implement JAX-differentiable versions [1][4].

A single simulation step integrates these dynamics over timestep $h$:

$$
(q_{t+1}, v_{t+1}) = \mathcal{F}(q_t, v_t, a_t; \theta),
$$

where $a_t = \pi_\theta(s_t)$ is the policy output and $\theta$ collects model or policy parameters. If every operation inside $\mathcal{F}$ — kinematics, inertia computation, contact detection, constraint solve, integration — is expressed in JAX, then `jax.grad` applied to a scalar loss $\mathcal{L} = \sum_t \ell(s_t, a_t)$ yields the exact gradient of the *discretized* program via reverse-mode autodiff. This is the "free lunch" of differentiable simulation: no hand-derived adjoints are needed for first-order gradients.

### 3.2 Contact as a complementarity problem

The subtlety is entirely in $\lambda$. For hard contact with Coulomb friction, the contact forces solve a **nonlinear complementarity problem (NCP)**:

$$
0 \le \lambda_N \perp \phi(q) \ge 0, \qquad \lambda \in \mathcal{K}(\mu),
$$

where $\phi(q) \ge 0$ is the signed distance (non-penetration), $\lambda_N$ the normal impulse, and $\mathcal{K}(\mu)$ the friction cone with coefficient $\mu$. Practical solvers relax or reformulate this: MuJoCo's classic solver uses a convex **cone complementarity (CCP)** approximation solved by projected Gauss–Seidel; MJX inherits a JAX reimplementation of this soft-constraint solver [4][6]. Brax's generalized pipeline resolves contacts with a similar impulse-based scheme, while its *positional* pipeline uses position-based dynamics (PBD/XPBD) [1].

Two differentiation strategies exist for the solver:

1. **Unrolled autodiff**: differentiate through the solver's iterations directly. Simple, exact for the computed fixed point, but memory-hungry (iteration count $\times$ state size in the tape) and polluted by the solver's transient iterates rather than its converged solution.
2. **Implicit differentiation**: treat the converged solution $\lambda^*$ as implicitly defined by optimality conditions $G(\lambda^*; q, v, \tau) = 0$ and apply the implicit function theorem:

$$
\frac{d\lambda^*}{d\theta} = -\left(\frac{\partial G}{\partial \lambda}\right)^{-1} \frac{\partial G}{\partial \theta}.
$$

This requires only the final iterate and gives the gradient of the true solution rather than the iteration path [6]. In practice, MJX-style engines most often rely on unrolled autodiff through a small fixed number of solver iterations — a pragmatic compromise.

### 3.3 The adjoint (reverse-mode) view of policy gradients

For a trajectory loss $\mathcal{L}(\theta) = \sum_{t=0}^{H-1} \ell(s_t, a_t) + \ell_H(s_H)$ with $s_{t+1} = \mathcal{F}(s_t, \pi_\theta(s_t))$, the first-order policy gradient decomposes into per-step adjoints:

$$
\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial a_t} = \frac{\partial \ell}{\partial a_t} + \lambda_{t+1}^\top \frac{\partial \mathcal{F}}{\partial a_t}, \qquad
\lambda_t = \frac{\partial \ell}{\partial s_t} + \lambda_{t+1}^\top \frac{\partial \mathcal{F}}{\partial s_t} + \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial a_t}\frac{\partial \pi_\theta}{\partial s_t},
$$

with $\lambda_H = \partial \ell_H / \partial s_H$, and finally $\nabla_\theta \mathcal{L} = \sum_t \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial a_t} \frac{\partial \pi_\theta}{\partial \theta}$. This is exactly what reverse-mode autodiff computes — equivalently, a discrete adjoint method. The product $\prod_t \partial \mathcal{F} / \partial s_t$ over long horizons is where contact stiffness enters: each factor's spectral norm can greatly exceed 1 at contact events, so gradient norms explode exponentially in $H$.

### 3.4 What we compute in practice

The algorithms in Section 4.3 optimize a *truncated surrogate*:

$$
J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=0}^{h-1} \gamma^t r(s_t, a_t) + \gamma^h V_\phi(s_h)\right],
$$

differentiating through $\mathcal{F}$ only within the short window $h$ (typically 16–64 steps) and using a learned critic $V_\phi$ beyond it. The critic is trained model-free (TD-$\lambda$), so the method is a hybrid: *analytic* gradients where they are trustworthy, *learned* values where they are not. Gradient clipping, contact-aware horizon truncation (AHAC), and massive parallel sampling (thousands of environments on one accelerator) complete the recipe.

```python
# Minimal pattern: analytic policy gradient through MJX (schematic)
import jax, mujoco, mujoco.mjx as mjx

mx = mjx.put_model(model); dx = mjx.put_data(model, data)

def rollout(policy_params, key, steps):
    def step(carry, _):
        dx, key = carry
        a = policy(policy_params, dx.qpos, dx.qvel)
        dx = mjx.step(mx, dx.replace(ctrl=a))   # differentiable physics step
        return (dx, key), -jnp.sum(dx.qvel**2)  # toy reward
    (_, _), rewards = jax.lax.scan(step, (dx, key), None, length=steps)
    return rewards.sum()

grad_fn = jax.jit(jax.value_and_grad(rollout))
loss, grads = grad_fn(policy_params, key, 32)   # first-order gradient, no score function
```

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Brax: four pipelines, one JAX program

Brax's defining architectural decision is to express the *entire* simulation-learning loop — physics, policy, and optimizer — as one JIT-compiled JAX program, vectorized across environments with `vmap` and differentiated with `grad` [1]. It offers four physics pipelines behind a shared API [2]:

| Pipeline | Method | Differentiability | Best for |
|---|---|---|---|
| **MJX** | JAX reimplementation of MuJoCo (generalized coordinates, convex contact solver) | Full `grad` support | Accurate robotics, transfer to MuJoCo |
| **Generalized** | Featherstone-style generalized-coordinate dynamics, impulse contacts | Full `grad` support | Classic Brax locomotion tasks |
| **Positional** | Position-based dynamics (PBD/XPBD) | Differentiable, very stable | Fast prototyping, deformables-adjacent |
| **Spring** | Impulse/spring approximations | Differentiable, cheapest | Rapid experimentation |

The four pipelines can run side-by-side in one program, enabling transfer-learning studies aimed at closing the sim-to-real gap [2]. Brax ships PPO, SAC, ARS, and evolutionary strategies alongside **analytic policy gradients (APG)** agents that backpropagate through the physics directly [1][2], reaching *millions of simulation steps per second* on a single TPU for Ant-scale environments.

### 4.2 MJX: MuJoCo's solver, made differentiable

**MuJoCo XLA (MJX)** is a JAX reimplementation of the MuJoCo physics engine developed at Google DeepMind [4]. It reproduces MuJoCo's generalized-coordinate dynamics and its *soft-constraint* convex contact solver, but every kernel is a pure JAX function, so `mjx.step` is end-to-end differentiable and `vmap`-able across thousands of parallel scenes on GPU/TPU. The official MJX tutorial notebooks include a **differentiable physics** track that trains locomotion policies with analytical gradients automatically derived from MuJoCo's physics step (`training_apg.ipynb`) — arguably the clearest public demonstration of the paradigm [4].

The contact solver determines gradient quality. MuJoCo-family solvers treat contacts as *convex* constraints (a CCP approximation to the true NCP), solved by projected iteration. Convexity aids differentiation: the solution map is locally Lipschitz and directionally differentiable almost everywhere, so unrolled autodiff through a fixed iteration budget yields usable gradients. The survey in [6] classifies MJX as *CCP with autodiff*, versus Nimble's *LCP with implicit differentiation* and Dojo's *NCP with implicit differentiation*.

> Theorem: *(Differentiability of convex contact maps.)* For a strictly convex contact QP with a unique solution $\lambda^*(p)$ depending on parameters $p = (q, v, \tau)$, the solution map is piecewise smooth; at points where the active set is stable, $d\lambda^*/dp$ exists and equals the implicit derivative of the KKT system. Autodiff through a convergent fixed-point iteration converges to this derivative as the iteration count grows — but practical engines truncate iterations, so the returned gradient is the derivative of the *truncated* map, with bias proportional to the solver residual.

### 4.3 SHAC and AHAC: making analytic gradients work

**SHAC (Short-Horizon Actor-Critic)** [3], published at ICLR 2022, is the canonical algorithm for policy learning with parallel differentiable simulation. Its diagnosis: BPTT over full episodes produces noisy loss landscapes, exploding memory, and numerically pathological gradients; its prescription: split each episode into short windows of length $h$, backpropagate analytic first-order gradients only within a window, and bootstrap with a learned critic:

$$
\mathcal{L}_a(\theta) = -\sum_{t=0}^{h-1} \gamma^t r(s_t, a_t) - \gamma^h V_\phi(s_h), \qquad
\mathcal{L}_c(\phi) = \mathbb{E}\big[\|V_\phi(s_t) - \hat{V}_t\|^2\big],
$$

with $\hat{V}_t$ a TD-$\lambda$ target. The critic is trained from the same massively parallel rollouts, so wall-clock efficiency stays high. On the paper's six-task benchmark (Ant, Humanoid, Humanoid MTU, etc., built on a custom GPU differentiable simulator), SHAC substantially outperformed PPO and SAC in sample efficiency — often by an order of magnitude in environment steps — and matched or exceeded their asymptotic performance.

**AHAC (Adaptive Horizon Actor-Critic)** [7] refines SHAC with a simple but incisive observation: not all contacts are equally stiff, so a *fixed* horizon is a blunt instrument. AHAC rolls out until it detects stiff contact — quantified by the dynamics Jacobian norm $\|\nabla f(s_t, a_t)\| > C$ — and truncates the differentiable graph there, preventing gradient explosion at its source. On a reimplemented Hopper task, AHAC-1 with threshold $C = 500$ beat fixed-horizon SHAC ($H = 32$) in asymptotic reward; an ablation showed reward peaking when the horizon approximated the gait period ($H \approx 28$ for Ant), suggesting the optimal window is tied to the task's natural dynamical timescale [7]. The catch: adaptive truncation is asynchronous across vectorized environments, complicating the fixed-shape XLA programs that make accelerators fast — AHAC-1 is not directly applicable to parallel vectorized envs without padding or masking tricks.

### 4.4 The contact-gradient pathology, precisely

Why do long-horizon analytic gradients fail? Three mechanisms compound:

1. **Jacobian norm explosion.** At stiff contact, $\|\partial \mathcal{F} / \partial s\| \gg 1$. The adjoint recursion multiplies $H$ such factors; the gradient norm scales as $\rho^H$ for effective spectral radius $\rho > 1$. Even moderate stiffness ($\rho = 1.2$) over $H = 1000$ steps gives $10^{79}$ — pure noise after floating-point rounding.
2. **Event non-smoothness.** Contact formation/release changes the active constraint set; the transition map is only piecewise smooth. Gradients computed on one side of the kink mislead optimization on the other — the *frozen-event* bias of the Theorem in Section 2.
3. **Chaotic loss landscapes.** Contact sequences are discrete; an infinitesimal parameter change can add or remove a foot strike, discontinuously changing the return.

## 5. Empirical Evaluation / Formal Proofs

### 5.1 What the benchmarks show

Across six contact-rich tasks, SHAC reached PPO's final performance using roughly **10× fewer environment steps** on Ant and Humanoid-class tasks, where naive BPTT diverged or plateaued [3].

AHAC's ablations [7] add a second empirical leg: on Hopper, adaptive truncation at contact-stiffness threshold $C = 500$ outperformed fixed $H = 32$ SHAC, and the horizon-sensitivity curve on Ant peaked near the gait period — evidence that horizon choice should be *dynamically* matched to contact stiffness rather than fixed a priori.

### 5.2 Formal statement: short-horizon gradient error bound

We sketch the standard justification for horizon truncation. Assume the one-step dynamics $\mathcal{F}$ is $L$-Lipschitz in $s$ with $L > 1$ near contacts (stiff regime), the reward $r$ is $L_r$-Lipschitz, and the critic satisfies $\|V_\phi - V^*\|_\infty \le \epsilon$. Then the bias of the truncated analytic gradient relative to the infinite-horizon policy gradient decomposes as:

$$
\|\nabla_\theta J_h - \nabla_\theta J\| \;\le\; \underbrace{O(\gamma^h L^h)}_{\text{truncation of dynamics sensitivity}} \;+\; \underbrace{O(\gamma^h \epsilon)}_{\text{critic error at boundary}}.
$$

The first term grows exponentially in $h$ when $L > 1$ — but it is numerically uncomputable anyway; truncation replaces it with a bounded surrogate plus critic error. Empirically, $h \in [16, 64]$ hits the sweet spot for locomotion [3][7].

### 5.3 Gradient-correctness experiments

The "correct gradients" literature [5] provides the cautionary experiments: on minimal colliding-body tasks with analytic ground truth, discrete-step autodiff gradients w.r.t. initial position, velocity, and control can disagree with closed-form sensitivities — sometimes in sign. The discrepancy vanishes as $h \to 0$ or with time-of-impact-aware differentiation, confirming that the error is a *discretization artifact*, not a fundamental flaw of the approach.

## 6. Limitations and Open Problems

1. **Gradient correctness through hard contact remains unsettled.** Discrete-time autodiff through complementarity solvers returns the gradient of the *discretized, truncated* map, not the true hybrid-system sensitivity [5]. Time-of-impact differentiation and continuous collision detection help but are not standard in MJX/Brax. A production-grade *provably correct* contact gradient is still missing.
2. **Implicit differentiation at scale.** The implicit-function approach [6] requires differentiating through KKT systems that may be singular at active-set changes — costly inside XLA graphs.
3. **Adaptive horizons vs. static XLA graphs.** AHAC's per-environment truncation [7] fights the fixed-shape discipline of `vmap`/`jit`. Padding, masking, or dynamic-slice workarounds exist but erode the throughput advantage. A first-class "ragged differentiable rollout" primitive in JAX would unlock AHAC at Brax scale.
4. **Memory.** Reverse-mode through scanned rollouts stores every intermediate state; `jax.checkpoint` trades compute for memory but interacts poorly with solver iteration tapes.
5. **Sim-to-real transfer of gradient-optimized policies.** Analytic gradients can overfit simulator idiosyncrasies — including *gradient artifacts* of the contact solver — more aggressively than model-free methods, because the optimizer follows the simulator's exact local structure. Domain randomization over contact parameters (friction, restitution, solver iterations) is standard but partially negates the sample-efficiency win.
6. **Beyond locomotion.** Most successes are in locomotion and simple manipulation. Dexterous in-hand manipulation, deformable contact, and multi-body assembly — where contact graphs are dense and persistent — remain largely unconquered by pure analytic-gradient methods.

---

## 7. Conclusion

Differentiable physics has moved, in half a decade, from a curiosity to a credible pillar of robot learning. **Brax** [1][2] proved that JAX-native rigid-body simulation could saturate accelerators at millions of steps per second while remaining end-to-end differentiable; **MJX** [4] brought the same capability to the mature MuJoCo ecosystem with a convex contact solver whose gradients are well-behaved enough to train on; and **SHAC** [3] and **AHAC** [7] supplied the algorithmic insight — short, contact-aware horizons with critic bootstrapping — that tames the exponential pathology of backpropagation through stiff contact.

Looking forward, the most valuable advances will likely be infrastructural rather than algorithmic: provably correct contact gradients, ragged-horizon XLA primitives, and batched implicit-differentiation layers.
