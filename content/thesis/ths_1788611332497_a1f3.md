# Homomorphic Encryption with CKKS Bootstrapping for Private Machine Learning Inference

## Abstract

Privacy-preserving machine learning inference demands the evaluation of deep arithmetic circuits over encrypted data without ever exposing plaintexts to the computing party. The Cheon–Kim–Kim–Song (CKKS) scheme is the dominant fully homomorphic encryption (FHE) construction for this task because it natively supports *approximate arithmetic* over real and complex numbers, matching the numerical character of neural network computation. This thesis develops the complete stack for private inference with CKKS: the RNS-CKKS variant with rescaling and modulus switching, noise dynamics across multiplicative depth, the four-stage bootstrapping pipeline (ModRaise, CoeffToSlot, EvalMod, SlotToCoeff), baby-step giant-step (BSGS) acceleration of homomorphic linear transforms, direct polynomial approximation of modular reduction with error-variance minimization, and parameter selection achieving 128-bit security. These primitives are composed into an end-to-end encrypted ResNet inference pipeline over ImageNet-scale batches, with correctness and noise-bound proofs, representative performance figures, and a frank assessment of limitations.

## 1. Introduction

The outsourcing of machine learning inference to untrusted cloud infrastructure creates a fundamental tension: the client wishes to benefit from a proprietary model without revealing either the input data or, ideally, the model itself. Classical mitigations such as trusted execution environments rely on hardware assumptions; *fully homomorphic encryption* (FHE) offers a purely cryptographic resolution, permitting arbitrary computation directly on ciphertexts [1].

Most second-generation FHE schemes — BGV and BFV — operate over exact integer arithmetic. Neural networks, however, are intrinsically approximate: weights are real-valued, activations are smooth nonlinearities, and the entire training procedure tolerates and even exploits numerical noise. The CKKS scheme of Cheon, Kim, Kim, and Song [1] exploits this alignment by treating the decryption noise *as part of the approximation error*, yielding a scheme whose plaintext space is a vector of complex numbers with bounded absolute error. This design decision makes CKKS the natural substrate for encrypted deep learning.

Two obstacles stand between the scheme and practical inference. First, every homomorphic multiplication grows the ciphertext noise and consumes one level of a finite modulus chain; a ResNet-50 forward pass requires hundreds of multiplicative levels, far beyond any leveled parameterization. The answer is *bootstrapping*: homomorphically refreshing a ciphertext to restore its modulus budget. Second, the approximation error introduced by bootstrapping itself must be small enough that the neural network's accuracy does not degrade — a requirement that drove the line of work from indirect sine-based modular reduction [4] to direct optimal polynomial approximation with lazy BSGS evaluation [3].

**Contributions.** This thesis (i) gives a self-contained exposition of RNS-CKKS rescaling, modulus switching, and noise dynamics; (ii) decomposes CKKS bootstrapping into its four stages and analyzes the BSGS and double-hoisting optimizations that made it practical [2, 5]; (iii) derives parameter sets achieving 128-bit security; and (iv) constructs an end-to-end encrypted ResNet inference pipeline with proofs of correctness and noise bounds.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 The Ring Learning With Errors problem

CKKS security rests on the *Ring Learning With Errors* (RLWE) problem over the $2N$-th cyclotomic ring

$$R = \mathbb{Z}[X]/(X^N + 1), \qquad N = 2^k.$$

A secret $s \in R$ is sampled from a narrow distribution (ternary or sparse); a sample is $(a, b = a\cdot s + e) \in R_q^2$ with $a$ uniform and $e$ small Gaussian noise. Distinguishing such samples from uniform is believed hard for both classical and quantum adversaries, and the best known attacks run in time exponential in $N/\log q$ [7].

### 2.2 Canonical embedding and approximate encoding

Let $\zeta = e^{\pi i / N}$ and define the *canonical embedding*

$$\sigma: R \to \mathbb{C}^{N/2}, \qquad \sigma(m) = (m(\zeta), m(\zeta^3), \dots, m(\zeta^{2(N/2)-1})).$$

Encoding a message vector $\mathbf{z} \in \mathbb{C}^{N/2}$ at scale $\Delta$ computes

$$\mathsf{Encode}(\mathbf{z}) = \left\lfloor \Delta \cdot \sigma^{-1}(\mathbf{z}) \right\rceil \in R,$$

and decoding inverts approximately: $\mathsf{Decode}(m) = \sigma(m)/\Delta \approx \mathbf{z}$. The rounding error $\lVert \mathbf{z} - \mathsf{Decode}(\mathsf{Encode}(\mathbf{z})) \rVert_\infty \le \frac{1}{2\Delta}\lVert \sigma^{-1} \rVert$ is the *encoding error*, deliberately tolerated as part of the approximate-arithmetic model [1]. A single ciphertext thus packs $N/2$ complex *slots* processed in SIMD fashion, and the Galois automorphisms $X \mapsto X^{2j+1}$ induce cyclic *rotations* of the slot vector — the workhorse of encrypted linear algebra [5].

### 2.3 RNS-CKKS, rescaling, and modulus switching

The *residue number system* (RNS) variant of CKKS replaces a single large modulus $q$ by a chain of machine-word primes $q = \prod_{i=0}^{L} q_i$, enabling all arithmetic in 64-bit limbs. A ciphertext at level $\ell$ lives in $R_{q_\ell}^2$ with $q_\ell = \prod_{i=0}^{\ell} q_i$. After a homomorphic multiplication, the scale squares to $\Delta^2$; *rescaling* divides it back down:

> **Definition (Rescaling).** $\mathsf{RS}_{\ell \to \ell-1}(c) = \left\lfloor \tfrac{q_{\ell-1}}{q_\ell} \cdot c \right\rceil \bmod q_{\ell-1}$, mapping a ciphertext of scale $\Delta^2$ at level $\ell$ to scale $\Delta$ at level $\ell-1$, at the cost of one modulus prime and a small rounding error.

Each rescaling consumes one level; a depth-$L$ circuit needs $L+1$ primes. When levels are exhausted, *bootstrapping* must refresh the ciphertext.

### 2.4 Noise dynamics

Let a fresh ciphertext carry noise of magnitude $B_0$. Homomorphic addition adds noise; multiplication of ciphertexts with noises $e_1, e_2$ and messages bounded by $\nu$ yields noise roughly $\nu(\lVert e_1 \rVert + \lVert e_2 \rVert) + \lVert e_1 e_2 \rVert/\Delta$ after rescaling. The dominant term is linear in the message bound, which is why CKKS pipelines *normalize activations into bounded intervals* before bootstrapping: EvalMod's polynomial approximation is only valid on a bounded domain, and failure outside it is catastrophic [2, 3].

## 3. Methodology

Our methodology proceeds in four layers, each building on the last:

1. **Parameter selection.** Choose $(N, \Delta, \{q_i\}, P)$ — ring dimension, scale, modulus chain, and special key-switching modulus — satisfying 128-bit RLWE security per the HomomorphicEncryption.org standard [7], while maximizing the number of usable levels $L$ and post-bootstrap precision.
2. **Bootstrapping pipeline.** Implement the four-stage refresh: $\mathsf{ModRaise} \to \mathsf{CoeffToSlot} \to \mathsf{EvalMod} \to \mathsf{SlotToCoeff}$, with BSGS linear transforms [5] and double-hoisted key switching [2].
3. **Modular-reduction approximation.** Replace the indirect sine-based approximation [4] with the *direct* optimal polynomial approximation of the modular reduction function, evaluated by lazy BSGS to minimize both depth and error variance [3].
4. **Encrypted ResNet composition.** Map convolution, batch normalization, ReLU, and pooling onto $\{\oplus, \otimes, \mathsf{Rot}, \mathsf{Bootstrap}\}$, pack ImageNet batches across slots, and bound the end-to-end approximation error against plaintext inference.

All complexity claims are stated asymptotically in $N$ and the slot count $n \le N/2$; concrete figures are drawn from the Lattigo and OpenFHE implementations [2, 6].

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 RNS-CKKS rescaling and modulus switching in detail

In RNS-CKKS the modulus chain is typically chosen with each $q_i \approx \Delta$, so that rescaling is nearly scale-preserving. Concretely, a widely used parameterization sets $\Delta = 2^{40}$ and $q_i \approx 2^{40}$–$2^{60}$, with the top primes reserved for key switching. The *hybrid* key-switching technique decomposes the switching key over a digit size $d_{\text{num}}$, trading key size against noise.

A subtle point: rescaling error is *deterministic rounding*, not Gaussian noise, and it accumulates additively across levels. After $L$ rescalings the accumulated rounding error is bounded by $\tfrac{L}{2}\lVert \sigma^{-1} \rVert_\infty / \min q_i$ in the canonical embedding norm — negligible against the $\approx 2^{-40}$ relative precision set by $\Delta = 2^{40}$. The following Sage-style snippet computes a valid 128-bit chain:

```python
# SageMath-style parameter selection for 128-bit RNS-CKKS
N = 2**16                      # ring dimension
Delta = 2**40                  # scale: ~12 decimal digits of precision
logQ_max = 1793                # HE standard v1.1 bound for n=2^16 @128-bit [7]
primes = [60]*4 + [40]*28      # key-switch primes + level primes (bits)
assert sum(primes) <= logQ_max # total modulus budget respected
L = len([p for p in primes if p == 40]) - 1   # usable multiplicative levels
print(f"levels L={L}, post-bootstrap precision ~ {Delta.bit_length()-8} bits")
```

The tension is fundamental: larger $\Delta$ improves precision but each level's prime must satisfy $q_i \gtrsim \Delta$, so precision directly consumes the finite modulus budget. Bootstrapping breaks this deadlock by *resetting* $\ell$ to a high level at the cost of a fixed precision loss ($\approx$ 10–20 bits) per refresh [2].

### 4.2 Bootstrapping via homomorphic linear transforms

CKKS bootstrapping refreshes a level-0 ciphertext $\mathsf{ct} = (b, a)$ encrypting $m$ with $\lVert m \rVert_\infty < q_0/4$. The four stages are [4, 2]:

| Stage | Operation | Purpose | Depth cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. ModRaise | Interpret $(b, a)$ over $R_Q$, $Q \gg q_0$ | Lift to large modulus so decryption formula is nearly correct | 0 |
| 2. CoeffToSlot | Homomorphic DFT: coefficients $\to$ slots | Move noisy coefficients into SIMD slots | 1 |
| 3. EvalMod | Approximate $t \mapsto t \bmod q_0$ | Remove the $q_0$-multiple wrap-around from ModRaise | 10–12 |
| 4. SlotToCoeff | Inverse homomorphic DFT: slots $\to$ coefficients | Return to coefficient encoding at high level | 1 |

After ModRaise, the "decrypted" value is $m + q_0 \cdot I$ for an unknown small integer polynomial $I$; EvalMod must compute the modular reduction *homomorphically*, which is the entire difficulty since $t \bmod q_0$ is discontinuous [4].

The two linear transforms are matrix–vector products by (scaled) DFT matrices. Naively they need $O(N)$ rotations; the BSGS method of Halevi–Shoup [5], adapted to CKKS by Cheon et al., factors the $n \times n$ transform into baby steps and giant steps, reducing rotations and rotation keys to $O(\sqrt{n})$. Bossuat et al. [2] further observed that key switching dominates the cost and introduced *double hoisting*: the expensive $\mathsf{ModUp}$/$\mathsf{ModDown}$ decomposition of key switching is hoisted across both the baby-step and giant-step loops, roughly halving the total work and yielding a $2\times$ speedup of the linear transforms on top of BSGS.

> **Theorem (Bootstrapping correctness sketch).** Let $\mathsf{ct}$ encrypt $m$ at level $0$ with $\lVert m \rVert_\infty \le q_0/4$ and let $P_{\text{mod}}$ approximate $t \bmod q_0$ uniformly to $\epsilon$ on the relevant interval. Then the bootstrapped ciphertext encrypts $m + e_{\text{boot}}$ with $\lVert e_{\text{boot}} \rVert_\infty \le \epsilon + e_{\text{lin}}$, where $e_{\text{lin}}$ is the linear-transform rounding error, and resides at level $L_{\text{boot}} \approx L - \text{depth}(P_{\text{mod}}) - 2$. *Proof idea.* ModRaise is exact; CoeffToSlot/SlotToCoeff are unitary up to the scaling $\Delta$, contributing only rounding error; EvalMod's uniform error transfers through the approximate decryption identity $\langle \mathsf{ct}, \mathsf{sk} \rangle = m + q_0 I + e \pmod Q$. ∎

### 4.3 Polynomial approximation of modular reduction

The classical approach [4] approximates modular reduction *indirectly*: using $\sin(2\pi t / q_0)$, whose zeros coincide with the integers, composed with an arcsine-like inversion — effectively $t \bmod q_0 \approx \tfrac{q_0}{2\pi}\sin^{-1}(\sin(2\pi t/q_0))$, where the sine is approximated by Taylor/Chebyshev polynomials and sharpened by repeated squaring $\sin(2x) = 2\sin x \cos x$. This indirect route costs multiplicative depth 11–12 for high precision.

Lee et al. [3] proposed *direct* approximation: find the minimax polynomial for the modular reduction function itself on $[-Kq_0, Kq_0]$, optimal in error variance, then evaluate it with a *lazy* BSGS variant that defers relinearization and rescaling to halve the number of expensive operations. Their results: multiplicative depth **10** for modular reduction at state-of-the-art accuracy, and 93-bit precision within depth 11 — strictly dominating the indirect method in both depth and error [3]. Since EvalMod dominates bootstrapping depth, this directly increases the usable levels $L_{\text{boot}}$ after each refresh, compounding across the dozens of bootstraps in a deep network.

```python
# Conceptual lazy-BSGS evaluation of the EvalMod polynomial P of degree d
def evalmod_lazy(ct, coeffs):
    # baby steps: ct^1, ct^2, ..., ct^B  (defer rescaling)
    babies = [ct]
    for i in range(2, B+1):
        babies.append(relin_noscale(babies[-1] * ct))   # lazy: no rescale yet
    # giant steps combine babies with rescaling only at block boundaries
    acc = encrypt_zero()
    for j in range(num_blocks):
        gj = power_giant(ct, j*B, babies)
        acc = acc + gj * plain_block(coeffs, j)
    return rescale_once(relin(acc))   # single rescale per block [3]
```

### 4.4 Noise management and 128-bit security parameter selection

Noise in a bootstrapped pipeline has three sources: (i) fresh-encryption noise $B_0$; (ii) per-level rescaling rounding $\approx \sqrt{N}/q_i$; (iii) bootstrapping error $\epsilon_{\text{boot}} \approx 2^{-19}$ in the Bossuat parameterization (19.1 bits of precision) [2]. The critical derived quantity is the *failure probability*: the chance that some slot's message exceeds the EvalMod approximation interval, causing catastrophic wrap-around. Bossuat et al. give a systematic analysis bounding it at $2^{-15.58}$ for their parameters — $468\times$ smaller than prior work — by modeling the message distribution and choosing the approximation interval $K$ conservatively [2].

Security is governed by the HomomorphicEncryption.org standard [7]: for ring dimension $N = 2^{16}$ and 128-bit classical security, the total modulus bit-length must satisfy $\log_2(PQ) \lesssim 1793$. A typical split reserves $\approx 4 \times 60 = 240$ bits for the special key-switching primes and leaves $\approx 1550$ bits for the chain — e.g., 28 levels at 55 bits each. Sparse secrets ($h = 192$–$256$ nonzero coefficients) are common in practice, though Bossuat et al. notably achieve their results with *non-sparse* keys, removing a once-standard assumption [2].

| Parameter | Symbol | Typical 128-bit value | Role |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ring dimension | $N$ | $2^{16} = 65536$ | Security + $N/2$ slots |
| Scale | $\Delta$ | $2^{40}$ | $\approx 12$ digits precision |
| Level primes | $q_i$ | $\approx 2^{55}$ | One consumed per multiplication |
| Special primes | $P$ | $\approx 2^{240}$ | Key-switching budget |
| Secret weight | $h$ | 192–256 (or dense) | RLWE hardness |
| Bootstrap precision | — | $\approx 19$ bits | Per-refresh error floor [2] |
| Failure probability | — | $2^{-15.58}$ | EvalMod interval overflow [2] |

### 4.5 End-to-end encrypted ResNet inference

A ResNet forward pass decomposes into operations CKKS supports natively ($\oplus$, $\otimes$, $\mathsf{Rot}$) plus two that need adaptation: *convolution* and *ReLU*.

**Packing.** An ImageNet input ($224 \times 224 \times 3$) far exceeds $N/2 = 32768$ slots, so inference is *batched*: $B$ images are packed with each image's channels interleaved across slots, or a single image is split across multiple ciphertexts with channel packing. Throughput is reported *amortized per image*.

**Convolution via the diagonal method.** A convolution is a linear map; Gazelle's diagonal encoding writes the weight matrix's diagonals as plaintext vectors $\mathbf{d}_j$ and evaluates $\sum_j \mathbf{d}_j \odot \mathsf{Rot}(\mathsf{ct}, j)$ — one plaintext–ciphertext multiplication and one rotation per diagonal, with hoisting amortizing the rotation cost across diagonals [5]. Strided and padded convolutions are handled by plaintext masking; average pooling is a scaled sum, hence free of depth.

**Batch normalization folding.** At inference time batch norm is an affine map $x \mapsto \gamma \frac{x - \mu}{\sigma} + \beta$, which is *folded* into the preceding convolution's weights and bias offline — zero homomorphic cost.

**ReLU approximation.** ReLU is non-polynomial and must be approximated. Two families dominate: (a) low-degree *minimax* polynomials (e.g., degree-3 or composite degree-$3^2$) fitted on the activation's empirical range; (b) the square activation $x^2$, which costs a single multiplication but changes the model's semantics and requires retraining. Composite polynomials $p_2(p_1(x))$ achieve high accuracy at depth 2–4 and are the standard choice; their approximation error is absorbed into the network's noise tolerance, typically costing $< 1\%$ top-1 accuracy on ImageNet-scale models after fine-tuning.

**Residual connections** are additions — free in both depth and noise. The full ResNet-50 forward pass consumes roughly 60–100 multiplicative levels depending on the ReLU degree, mandating 3–6 bootstraps per inference at strategic *refresh points* (typically after each residual stage), each bootstrap costing on the order of tens of seconds and $\approx 19$ bits of precision [2].

## 5. Empirical Results and Proofs

> **Theorem (End-to-end error bound).** Consider a ResNet with $S$ stages, each stage consuming $d_s$ multiplicative levels and introducing polynomial-approximation error $\eta_s$ (ReLU) plus rescaling rounding $\rho_s$. With bootstrapping error $\epsilon_{\text{boot}}$ per refresh and $R$ refreshes, the decrypted logits $\hat{y}$ satisfy $\lVert \hat{y} - y \rVert_\infty \le \sum_s (\eta_s + \rho_s) \cdot \prod_{t>s} L_t + R \cdot \epsilon_{\text{boot}} \cdot L_{\text{tail}}$, where $L_t$ are the Lipschitz constants of downstream stages. *Proof.* By induction over stages: each stage is $L_t$-Lipschitz in the canonical embedding norm, rescaling/rounding errors add, and bootstrapping replaces the ciphertext with one encrypting the same message plus $\epsilon_{\text{boot}}$, which then propagates through the remaining Lipschitz stages. ∎

In practice the Lipschitz products are the binding constraint — deep networks amplify early errors — which is why refresh points are placed *after* stages with small downstream Lipschitz mass, and why ReLU approximations are fitted tightly to each layer's empirical activation range rather than globally.

Representative performance figures from the literature (amortized, 128-bit parameters, $N = 2^{16}$):

| Operation | Latency (order of magnitude) | Precision | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| One CKKS bootstrap, $n = 32768$ slots | $\approx 18$ s | 19.1 bits | [2] |
| EvalMod polynomial, direct approx. | depth 10 | 93-bit (depth 11) | [3] |
| Homomorphic linear transform ($n \times n$) | $O(\sqrt{n})$ rotations | 1 level | [5] |
| Encrypted ResNet-20 (CIFAR-10), per image amortized | minutes → seconds (batched) | $<1\%$ accuracy drop | literature consensus |
| ResNet-50 / ImageNet batch | hours wall-clock (amortized s/image) | $1$–$2\%$ top-1 drop | literature consensus |

> **Corollary (Feasibility).** Under 128-bit parameters, a ResNet-50 inference over an encrypted ImageNet batch is *computationally feasible* — wall-clock hours amortized to seconds per image — with accuracy degradation bounded by the sum of ReLU approximation error and $R \cdot \epsilon_{\text{boot}}$ propagated through the network's Lipschitz constants. The dominant cost is bootstrapping, not the linear layers. ∎

The key quantitative takeaway from Bossuat et al. [2] remains the headline number: **$14.1\times$ improvement in bootstrapped throughput** (plaintext-bits per second) over the previous best, at 128-bit security with non-sparse keys — the result that moved CKKS bootstrapping from a theoretical capability to a deployable primitive, as subsequently productized in OpenFHE [6].

## 6. Limitations

1. **Bootstrapping latency dominates.** Even at 18 seconds per refresh [2], a ResNet-50 pipeline requiring several bootstraps spends the majority of wall-clock time refreshing rather than computing. Real-time inference remains out of reach.
2. **Precision ceiling.** Each bootstrap injects $\approx 2^{-19}$ relative error; pipelines deeper than $\approx 6$–$8$ refreshes accumulate error that no ReLU fine-tuning can absorb. Ultra-deep or recurrent architectures are effectively excluded.
3. **Failure probability is nonzero.** The $2^{-15.58}$ bound [2] is per bootstrap; across thousands of inference queries the expected number of catastrophic wrap-around failures is nonzero, and detection requires expensive plaintext-side validation.
4. **Non-polynomial operators.** Max-pooling, softmax, and attention softmax have no efficient polynomial form; they are replaced by average pooling and polynomial surrogates, changing model semantics and requiring retraining.
5. **Memory footprint.** Evaluation keys (rotation keys, bootstrapping keys) for $N = 2^{16}$ run to *gigabytes*; the client must generate and transmit them, and the server must hold them resident.
6. **Security model scope.** CKKS provides IND-CPA security only; it does not hide the *circuit* being evaluated (the model architecture and, via access patterns, aspects of the weights), nor does it provide circuit privacy or verifiability of the computation [7].
7. **Approximate decryption is a feature and a liability.** The same error tolerance that enables approximate arithmetic means CKKS ciphertexts can leak a few bits of the secret key under decryption-oracle misuse — the *IND-CPA^D* concern — so decrypted results must never be fed back into adversarially chosen computations without countermeasures.

---

## 7. Conclusion

CKKS bootstrapping converts the scheme from a bounded-depth curiosity into a general engine for private real-number computation. The intellectual arc is clear: Cheon et al. [1] defined approximate homomorphic arithmetic; Cheon et al. [4] showed bootstrapping was possible via homomorphic DFT and sine-based modular reduction; Halevi–Shoup [5] and Bossuat et al. [2] made the linear transforms and key switching fast through BSGS and double hoisting; and Lee et al. [3] replaced the indirect approximation with a direct, error-variance-optimal polynomial evaluated lazily.

The engineering recipe for private inference is now stable: RNS-CKKS with $\Delta = 2^{40}$, $N = 2^{16}$, batch packing across $32768$ slots, diagonal-method convolutions, folded batch norm, composite-polynomial ReLUs, and bootstraps at stage boundaries. The remaining frontier is not feasibility but *economics*: reducing bootstrap latency by another order of magnitude, taming the gigabyte evaluation keys, and extending the precision ceiling to deeper architectures. Until then, CKKS private inference occupies a well-defined niche — high-value, latency-tolerant, batch-processed inference where data confidentiality justifies hours of computation — and within that niche it is unmatched.
