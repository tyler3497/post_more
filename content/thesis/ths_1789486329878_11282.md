# Neural Population Geometry: Tuning Manifolds, Information Bottlenecks, and Dimensionality Reduction in Cortical Coding

## Abstract

Cortical computation is enacted by populations, not single neurons. The joint activity of *N* neurons traces a point cloud in an *N*-dimensional state space whose intrinsic structure is governed by a small number *D* of latent variables, so that trial-averaged responses lie on or near a *D*-dimensional tuning manifold. This article develops three linked perspectives on that geometry. First, we examine how the shape of the manifold — its curvature, topology, and extrinsic embedding — depends on the tuning-curve structure of the population, and why linear dimension can exceed intrinsic dimension exponentially, so that PCA and related methods systematically misjudge the true complexity of neural codes. Second, we recast coding as an information bottleneck problem: neural representations must compress the sensory input while preserving predictive information about task-relevant variables, a tradeoff formalized by the information-bottleneck Lagrangian and visualized on the information plane. Third, we survey dimensionality-reduction methods as statistical inference about the manifold, from PCA and factor analysis through demixed PCA and nonlinear embeddings, and examine when each succeeds or fails. ## 1 Introduction: From Single-Neuron Tuning to Population Geometry

For much of the twentieth century, the dominant metaphor of cortical coding was the single neuron as a feature detector. In this view, inherited from Barlow's neuron doctrine, the job of the analyst is to find the stimulus that makes a cell fire and the job of the cell is to report that stimulus downstream. The picture is elegant, falsifiable, and — for large-scale population recordings — increasingly inadequate. Neurons in higher cortical areas respond to mixtures of sensory, motor, cognitive, and contextual variables; their "tuning" is broad, overlapping, and state-dependent; and the information available to a downstream reader lives not in any one cell but in the joint pattern across thousands of them [5].

The geometric program in systems neuroscience replaces the question "what does this neuron encode?" with the question "what shape does the population activity take, and what does that shape imply about computation?" Concretely, let the vector **y**(*t*) in R^N collect the activity of *N* simultaneously recorded neurons in a time bin centered at *t*. Over a recording session the population traces out a cloud of points in R^N. When the responses are driven by a time-varying *D*-dimensional latent variable **x**(*t*) — a stimulus, a motor plan, an internal estimate of location, a decision variable — the generative model takes the form **y**(*t*) = *F*(**x**(*t*)) + **ξ**(*t*), where *F* maps latent states to mean population responses and **ξ**(*t*) is residual variability [7]. Ignoring noise, the data then lie on the image of *F*, a *D*-dimensional subset of R^N that, under mild smoothness conditions, is a manifold: the tuning manifold of the population.

Three bodies of theory bear on this picture, and this article is organized around their intersection. First, the *geometry* of tuning manifolds: how the functional form of tuning curves determines curvature, topology, and the relationship between intrinsic dimension *D* and the dimension of the best-fitting linear subspace. Second, the *information bottleneck*: an information-theoretic account of what the representation is *for*, in which compression of the input is traded against prediction of task-relevant variables. Third, *dimensionality reduction*: the statistical toolkit — PCA, factor analysis, demixed PCA, nonlinear embeddings — by which we infer the manifold from finite, noisy data, and the precise sense in which each tool can succeed or fail. These are not three separate stories. The geometry constrains what any reduction method can recover; the bottleneck objective shapes which geometries are worth learning; and the reduction methods are how we test geometric and information-theoretic hypotheses against data.

## 2 Tuning Manifolds: Curvature, Topology, and the Cost of Linearity

### The state-space picture

The manifold viewpoint begins with a change of coordinates. Instead of treating a recording as *N* coupled time series, we treat each moment as a point in neural state space, with one axis per neuron. Structure in the population code becomes shape: clusters, rings, sheets, and trajectories. The orientation columns of primary visual cortex furnish the canonical example. As the orientation of a grating is swept continuously, the mean population response traces a closed one-dimensional curve — a ring — embedded in a space whose dimension equals the number of recorded units. Two stimuli in a discrimination task are then two points, or two noise-broadened distributions, on that ring, and perceptual learning can be read off as deformation of the distributions: changes in their separation, their spread, or their angular position along the manifold.

This geometric reframing matters because computation itself becomes geometry. A downstream area that must classify stimuli needs only a hyperplane that separates the relevant regions of the manifold; a recurrent circuit that must remember a continuous variable needs an attracting ring; a motor circuit that must produce many behaviors from one network needs trajectories that stay within a shared low-dimensional set [6]. The shape is the program.

### Translation-symmetric codes and exponential linear dimension

Many of the brain's most important codes share a structural property: *translation-symmetric tuning*. Each neuron has a tuning curve of a common functional form — often well approximated by a Gaussian — differing from its neighbors mainly in the location of its preferred stimulus. Orientation tuning in V1, hippocampal place fields, head-direction cells, and grid cells all belong to this family, as do less obviously spatial codes such as numerosity tuning and tuning to accumulated decision evidence [7].

De and Chaudhuri analyzed the manifolds generated by such codes and arrived at a striking, quantitative warning [7]. Consider *N* neurons with Gaussian tuning to a *D*-dimensional stimulus. The intrinsic dimension of the response manifold is *D*: the data are, in the noiseless limit, exactly parameterized by the stimulus. But the dimension of the *linear subspace* needed to capture a fixed fraction of the variance — the quantity PCA reports — grows at least exponentially in *D*. For *D* as small as three or four, capturing 80 percent of the variance can require thousands of linear dimensions. Linear dimensionality reduction therefore *dramatically overestimates* the true dimension of data drawn from these population codes, and it does so even in the limit of arbitrarily many neurons and data points and zero noise. The failure is not statistical; it is geometric. The manifold is curved, and curvature defeats planes.

The analysis also exposes an inverse relationship between linear dimension and sparsity. Sharply tuned neurons (narrow tuning width σ) produce manifolds that are *more* nonlinear: the linear dimension scales roughly as the reciprocal of the tuning width. This is intuitive once seen: narrow tuning means each neuron is informative only in a small neighborhood of stimulus space, so the population response is a sparse, highly curved object — nearly a set of coordinate axes — while broad tuning smears responses across the population and flattens the manifold toward something a plane can approximate. Adding neurons concentrated at special locations, or with sharper tuning, can only increase the linear dimension [7]. In short, the codes the brain actually uses — sparse, localized, translation-symmetric — are precisely the codes for which naive linear analysis lies most severely.

### Multiplicative tuning and product structure

A second ubiquitous structure is *multiplicative tuning*, in which the response factorizes into a product of lower-dimensional factors. The textbook case is gain modulation: attention multiplicatively scales a neuron's sensory tuning curve without changing its shape. Epoch codes modulated by task variables, and the gating of sensory responses by behavioral state, have the same product form. De and Chaudhuri show that multiplicative codes inherit the same pathology: the linear dimension grows exponentially with the intrinsic dimension, with the same Fourier-analytic mechanism — the covariance eigenspectrum is the *D*-dimensional Fourier transform of the tuning-covariance function, and the number of non-negligible eigenvalues explodes combinatorially [7].

### Noise geometry: signal, variance, and discriminability

So far we have discussed the *mean* manifold, the image of *F*. Real data are noise-broadened: trial-to-trial responses to a fixed stimulus form a distribution around the manifold, and pairwise noise correlations stretch that distribution into an ellipsoid rather than a sphere. The geometry of *discrimination* therefore involves two objects at once: the signal geometry (where the means sit) and the noise geometry (how the clouds are oriented). Classical signal-detection quantities reappear in geometric dress. The Fisher information of the population code is, locally, the squared speed at which the mean response moves along the manifold, measured in units of the noise covariance; perceptual thresholds correspond to distances along the manifold in this noise-whitened metric. Manifold shrinkage (reduced trial-to-trial spread), signal separation (increased distance between stimulus distributions), and signal rotation (repositioning of distributions along the manifold) are distinct, experimentally dissociable geometric mechanisms of learning, and they make different predictions about which linear projections will and will not change with training.

The practical moral of this section is a conditional. When the tuning manifold is nearly flat — as in the rotational dynamics of motor cortex, where a handful of linear dimensions capture most variance [6] — linear methods are superb: interpretable, tractable, and theoretically grounded. When the code is translation-symmetric, sparse, or multiplicative — as in much of sensory and hippocampal coding — linear methods report dimensions that are artifacts of curvature, and the analyst who mistakes linear dimension for intrinsic dimension will conclude the code is vastly more complex than it is [7].

## 3 Information Bottlenecks: The Compression–Prediction Tradeoff

### The bottleneck Lagrangian

If geometry describes the *shape* of the code, the information bottleneck (IB) describes its *purpose*. Let *X* be the sensory input (or, more generally, the high-dimensional variable the population observes) and *Y* the task-relevant variable to be predicted — a stimulus category, a future reward, a motor command. A representation is a (possibly stochastic) mapping from *X* to a bottleneck variable *T*, forming the Markov chain *X* → *T* → *Y*. The IB principle states that a good representation should be maximally *compressed* with respect to *X* while remaining maximally *informative* about *Y* [2]. Formally, one seeks an encoder *p*(*t*|*x*) minimizing the Lagrangian

$$\mathcal{L}[p(t|x)] = I(X;T) - \beta\, I(T;Y),$$

where *I* denotes mutual information and the Lagrange multiplier β ≥ 0 sweeps out a tradeoff curve: at small β the representation is aggressively compressed, at large β it is richly predictive. The optimal solutions trace a concave *information curve* in the plane with coordinates (*I*(*X*;*T*), *I*(*T*;*Y*)) — the information plane — and no representation can lie above it.

> **Theorem (Data Processing Inequality).** For any Markov chain *X* → *T* → *Y*, the information *T* carries about *Y* cannot exceed the information *X* carries about *Y*, nor the information *T* carries about *X*: *I*(*X*;*Y*) ≤ min(*I*(*X*;*T*), *I*(*T*;*Y*)).

The inequality is the reason the bottleneck is well-posed: information about the target can only be *lost*, never created, as it passes through successive representations.

## 4 Dimensionality Reduction as Statistical Inference

### 4.1 Linear subspaces: PCA, factor analysis, and when they work

Principal component analysis remains the workhorse of population analysis for good reasons: it is the maximum-likelihood linear latent-variable model under isotropic noise, it is computationally trivial at modern scale, and its outputs — orthonormal directions ordered by explained variance — are immediately visualizable [5].

```python
import numpy as np

def participation_ratio(eigvals):
    """Effective dimensionality of a covariance eigenspectrum."""
    lam = np.asarray(eigvals, dtype=float)
    lam = lam[lam > 0]
    return float((lam.sum() ** 2) / (lam ** 2).sum())

def variance_explained(eigvals, k):
    lam = np.sort(np.asarray(eigvals, dtype=float))[::-1]
    return float(lam[:k].sum() / lam.sum())
```

which interpolates between 1 (all variance in one direction) and *N* (variance spread evenly).

The results of Section 2 imply a research program.

The comparative summary is worth stating plainly:

| Method | Core assumption | What it buys | Characteristic failure |
|---|---|---|---|
| PCA | Data near a linear subspace | Speed, interpretability, guarantees | Curvature inflates dimension exponentially [7] |
| Factor analysis | Low-rank shared + private noise | Separates signal from independent noise | Same linearity assumption as PCA [5] |
| dPCA | Task parameters combine additively | Interpretable, demixed components | Nonlinear (e.g. multiplicative) interactions [4][8] |
| Kernel dPCA | Low-rank structure in kernel space | Demixing under nonlinear interactions | Kernel and rank selection; data hunger [8] |
| Communication subspace | Inter-area signal is low-rank | Isolates transmitted from private dynamics | Misses nonlinear or high-rank coupling [5] |
| Nonlinear embeddings | Data on a smooth manifold | Intrinsic geometry, true dimension | Sampling density; hyperparameter sensitivity [7] |

## 5 Synthesis: Efficient Coding on Manifolds Under Bottlenecks

We can now state the unified problem. A cortical population must choose an encoding — a tuning map *F*, or equivalently an encoder *p*(**t**|**x**) — that minimizes a cost with (at least) three terms: the *description cost* of the representation (metabolic energy, wiring, and, in the IB language, *I*(*X*;*T*)); the *prediction shortfall* (lost task-relevant information, *I*(*T*;*Y*), or behavioral error); and a *decodability* constraint imposed by downstream readers that can only implement certain computations, typically linear or shallow ones. Geometry is what the solution *looks like*; the bottleneck is what the solution *is for*; dimensionality reduction is how we *check*.

This synthesis makes several non-obvious predictions. First, the curvature results of Section 2 and the compression objective of Section 3 are two sides of one coin: aggressive compression of a translation-symmetric code *requires* curvature, because a flat embedding of a high-information code would need ambient dimension comparable to the linear dimension — biologically implausible — while a curved embedding packs the same information into few intrinsic dimensions at the price of extrinsic nonlinearity. Second, *communication subspaces* between areas are bottlenecks made geometric: when area A projects to area B, only the low-rank component of A's activity that predicts B's responses survives, and the rest — however high-dimensional — is private to A. Inter-areal communication is thus literally an information bottleneck with a geometric signature, measurable by targeted dimensionality reduction [5]. Third, learning rules that optimize bottleneck objectives should *produce* the observed manifolds: the ring attractors, the preserved motor modes, the demixed prefrontal components are not accidents of anatomy but fixed points of compression–prediction tradeoffs [2][6].

The typed formulation clarifies what is being optimized:

```haskell
-- The information bottleneck as a typed optimization problem.
-- An encoder maps inputs to distributions over bottleneck states.
type Encoder x t = x -> Distribution t

-- Sweeping beta traces the optimal tradeoff curve on the information plane.
ibLagrangian :: Double -> Double -> Double -> Double
ibLagrangian beta iXT iTY = iXT - beta * iTY

-- Geometry enters as a constraint: the encoder's image must be
-- realizable by tuning curves the circuit can implement.
```

Open questions abound. Do cortical circuits implement anything like the IB Lagrangian in their plasticity rules, or does bottleneck-like behavior emerge from unrelated objectives? Can we measure the information plane *in vivo* — tracking *I*(*X*;*T*) and *I*(*T*;*Y*) across learning in a single population — given the notorious difficulty of mutual-information estimation from finite spike data [9]? And can we close the loop experimentally: perturb the manifold's curvature (by manipulating tuning widths or noise correlations) and test whether decoding and behavior change as the bottleneck theory predicts? The tools reviewed here — the Fourier analysis of tuning codes, the variational bounds on information, the demixed and nonlinear embeddings — are the instruments with which those questions will be answered.

## 6 Limitations

Several caveats bound what the frameworks above can claim. *Estimation.* Mutual information between high-dimensional neural responses and stimuli is extremely difficult to estimate from finite data; naive estimators are badly biased, and the history of the IB-in-deep-learning debate shows how easily estimator artifacts masquerade as scientific findings [9]. Geometric estimators of intrinsic dimension face analogous finite-sample and noise-floor problems. *Linearity bias.* The field's default pipeline — PCA, then visualization, then interpretation — systematically overestimates dimensionality for exactly the codes the brain uses most [7]; published "dimensionality" numbers should be read as upper bounds contingent on a linearity assumption that is rarely tested. *Manifold versus mechanism.* A manifold is a description of the data, not an explanation of the circuit: many different connectivities and plasticity rules can produce the same tuning geometry, and inferring mechanism from shape alone is underdetermined. *Task-relativity.* Both the bottleneck's relevance variable *Y* and dPCA's task parameters are chosen by the experimenter; a representation that looks like pure noise under one *Y* may be an optimal code under another [3][4]. *Causality.* Nearly all the evidence reviewed here is correlational: we observe low-dimensional structure and preserved manifolds, but targeted perturbations that reshape the manifold and test the behavioral consequences remain rare. Until the geometry is manipulated, not merely measured, the claim that the brain *computes with* manifolds — rather than merely exhibiting them — stays a hypothesis.

## 7 Conclusion

The population-geometric view has moved systems neuroscience from cataloging single-neuron selectivities to characterizing the shape of collective activity, and the shape turns out to be richly structured: curved by translation-symmetric and multiplicative tuning, broadened elliptically by noise correlations, preserved across behaviors in motor cortex, and demixable into task-parameter components in prefrontal cortex [4][6][7]. The information bottleneck supplies the normative counterpart: representations are solutions to compression–prediction tradeoffs, visualizable on the information plane and approachable by layered architectures, biological or artificial [1][2][3]. Dimensionality reduction is the bridge — a set of statistical inferences, each encoding assumptions about linearity, noise, and task structure, whose failures are as informative as their successes [5][8]. The synthesis is a single optimization problem — minimize description cost, maximize relevant information, respect decodability — whose solutions are the tuning manifolds we record. If that picture is right, the deepest question in the field is no longer *what* the manifold looks like, but *why* this manifold and not another: which objective, under which constraints, makes the brain's geometry inevitable.
