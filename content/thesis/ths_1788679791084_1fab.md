# Conformal Prediction as Distribution-Free Uncertainty Quantification: Exchangeability, Finite-Sample Coverage, and the Limits of Conditional Validity

## Abstract

Conformal prediction is a framework for constructing prediction sets with finite-sample, distribution-free coverage guarantees whose sole statistical assumption is exchangeability of the data. This thesis develops the modern theory and practice of conformal inference. We formalize exchangeability and distinguish it from independence, derive the split (inductive) conformal prediction algorithm, and prove the marginal coverage theorem: the quantile of calibration nonconformity scores yields prediction sets containing the unseen response with probability at least 1-alpha for any finite sample size and any model. We characterize how the nonconformity score governs efficiency, present conformalized quantile regression (CQR) for adaptive heteroscedastic intervals [1], adaptive prediction sets (APS) and regularized adaptive prediction sets (RAPS) for classification [4], and Mondrian conformal prediction for group-conditional validity. We prove the Barber-Candes-Ramdas-Tibshirani impossibility theorem [3]: exact distribution-free conditional coverage cannot be attained by any procedure producing bounded prediction sets. Applications to ImageNet classification and large language model generation illustrate small, informative sets in practice. We conclude with extensions beyond exchangeability [6] and open problems in conditional validity.

## 1. Introduction

Machine learning models now mediate decisions in medicine, finance, criminal justice, and autonomous systems, where the cost of an overconfident error can be catastrophic. A point prediction \(\hat{\mu}(x)\) is an impoverished object: it communicates a guess but not the model's *ignorance* about it. Uncertainty quantification (UQ) remedies this by attaching to each prediction a measure of its reliability. The dominant traditions each demand something the practitioner rarely has. **Bayesian** UQ requires a prior and a likelihood correct enough for the posterior to mean what it says; under misspecification, credible sets need not cover at the nominal rate. **Frequentist** UQ - delta-method intervals, bootstrap confidence bands - is typically *asymptotic*, offering guarantees only as \(n \to \infty\), and often requires regularity conditions (smoothness, correct specification of the error distribution) that are unverifiable in practice.

Conformal prediction, originating in the algorithmic learning theory of Vovk, Gammerman, and Shafer and matured into a practical statistical methodology by Lei, Wasserman, Tibshirani, Candes, Barber, and others [2][5], occupies a striking middle ground. It is a *wrapper*: it takes any fitted predictor - a neural network, a random forest, a language model - and returns prediction sets \(\mathcal{C}(X_{n+1}) \subset \mathcal{Y}\) with the guarantee

\[
\mathbb{P}\{Y_{n+1} \in \mathcal{C}(X_{n+1})\} \geq 1 - \alpha,
\]

holding **in finite samples**, **without any assumption on the data-generating distribution** beyond *exchangeability* of the data points, and **without any assumption on the model** - the base predictor may be arbitrarily misspecified. This thesis develops the theory, the algorithms, the efficiency considerations, and the fundamental limits of this framework.

We present (i) split conformal prediction and the marginal coverage theorem; (ii) score design for efficiency; (iii) CQR [1]; (iv) APS/RAPS [4]; (v) the conditional-coverage impossibility theorem [3] with the Mondrian remedy; (vi) empirical evidence; and (vii) vision and LLM applications.

## 2. Background

### Prediction sets, intervals, and the marginal coverage target

Let \((X_1, Y_1), \dots, (X_n, Y_n), (X_{n+1}, Y_{n+1})\) be data with features \(X_i \in \mathcal{X}\) and responses \(Y_i \in \mathcal{Y}\). A *prediction set* is a data-dependent map \(\mathcal{C}: \mathcal{X} \to 2^{\mathcal{Y}}\). When \(\mathcal{Y} = \mathbb{R}\), a prediction set is usually an interval; when \(\mathcal{Y} = \{1, \dots, K\}\), it is a subset of labels. The *marginal coverage* property at level \(1-\alpha\) is

\[
\mathbb{P}\{Y_{n+1} \in \mathcal{C}(X_{n+1})\} \geq 1 - \alpha,
\tag{1}
\]

where the probability is over all randomness: the training data, the calibration data, and the test point. "Marginal" emphasizes averaging over the joint distribution of \((X_{n+1}, Y_{n+1})\): coverage need not hold conditionally on \(X_{n+1} = x\). This distinction is the central tension of the thesis (Section 4.4).

Prediction sets must be distinguished from *confidence sets*, which target a fixed parameter \(\theta\) and cover it with probability \(1-\alpha\) over repeated sampling of the data. A prediction set instead targets the *random* quantity \(Y_{n+1}\); the randomness in (1) includes the test label itself.

### Exchangeability: the sole assumption

> **Definition (Exchangeability).** Random variables \(Z_1, \dots, Z_{n+1}\) are *exchangeable* if for every permutation \(\pi\) of \(\{1,\dots,n+1\}\),
> \[
> (Z_1, \dots, Z_{n+1}) \stackrel{d}{=} (Z_{\pi(1)}, \dots, Z_{\pi(n+1)}).
> \]

Exchangeability is strictly weaker than i.i.d.: every i.i.d. sequence is exchangeable, but exchangeable sequences can exhibit dependence (e.g., sampling without replacement, or hierarchical models with shared random effects). For conformal prediction we need only the finite version's consequence: the *ranks* of certain statistics are uniform (Section 3.2). Notably, exchangeability is a *symmetry* condition on the joint distribution, not a statement about the marginal distribution of any single \(Z_i\); this is why the coverage guarantee is *distribution-free*: it holds for every exchangeable law, however pathological.

In practice, exchangeability is satisfied when data are collected as a random sample and the calibration and test points are drawn from the same population without ordering effects. It fails under distribution shift, temporal drift, or active/adversarial sampling - the subject of recent extensions [6] (Section 6).

### A brief history

Conformal prediction emerged from the theory of *algorithmic randomness* and online learning: Vovk, Gammerman, and Shafer's monograph *Algorithmic Learning in a Random World* (2005) introduced conformal predictors and proved validity under exchangeability in the online protocol. The modern statistical literature begins with Lei, G'Sell, Rinaldo, Tibshirani, and Wasserman [5], who connected conformal prediction to classical predictive inference, analyzed the jackknife+ and related variants, and clarified the role of the score function. Subsequent work produced CQR [1], distribution-free classification sets [4], the conditional-coverage impossibility theorem [3], and extensions beyond exchangeability [6].

## 3. Methodology

### Split (inductive) conformal prediction

We fix a *nonconformity score* \(s: \mathcal{X} \times \mathcal{Y} \to \mathbb{R}\), where larger values indicate worse agreement between a prediction and an observation. A canonical regression choice is the absolute residual \(s(x, y) = |y - \hat{\mu}(x)|\) for a pre-fitted \(\hat{\mu}\). The split procedure is:

1. **Split** the labeled data into a training set \(\mathcal{D}_{\text{train}}\) and a calibration set \(\mathcal{D}_{\text{cal}} = \{(X_i, Y_i)\}_{i=1}^n\).
2. **Fit** \(\hat{\mu}\) on \(\mathcal{D}_{\text{train}}\) (any algorithm; no assumptions).
3. **Score** the calibration points: \(S_i = s(X_i, Y_i)\), \(i = 1, \dots, n\).
4. **Threshold**: compute \(\hat{q} = \text{Quantile}(S_1, \dots, S_n; \lceil (n+1)(1-\alpha) \rceil / n)\), the \(\lceil (n+1)(1-\alpha) \rceil\)-th order statistic divided by \(n\).
5. **Predict**: for a new \(x\), output \(\mathcal{C}(x) = \{y : s(x, y) \leq \hat{q}\}\).

```python
import numpy as np

def split_conformal_set(calib_scores, alpha, score_fn, x_new, y_grid):
    # Return the conformal prediction set for x_new over a candidate grid.
    n = len(calib_scores)
    level = np.ceil((n + 1) * (1 - alpha)) / n
    qhat = np.quantile(calib_scores, level, method="higher")
    return [y for y in y_grid if score_fn(x_new, y) <= qhat]
```

For the absolute-residual score, step 5 yields the familiar interval \(\mathcal{C}(x) = [\hat{\mu}(x) - \hat{q},\, \hat{\mu}(x) + \hat{q}]\). The full (transductive) variant refits the model with each candidate \((x, y)\) included and is statistically slightly more efficient but computationally prohibitive for modern models; split conformal requires exactly one model fit.

### The marginal coverage theorem

> **Theorem (Marginal coverage of split conformal prediction).** If \((X_1, Y_1), \dots, (X_n, Y_n), (X_{n+1}, Y_{n+1})\) are exchangeable, then for the split conformal set \(\mathcal{C}\) at level \(\alpha\),
> \[
> \mathbb{P}\{Y_{n+1} \in \mathcal{C}(X_{n+1})\} \geq 1 - \alpha.
> \]
> If the scores \(S_1, \dots, S_{n+1}\) are almost surely distinct, coverage is also upper-bounded by \(1 - \alpha + 1/(n+1)\).

*Proof sketch.* Let \(S_{n+1} = s(X_{n+1}, Y_{n+1})\). Because the nonconformity score is computed symmetrically and the data are exchangeable, the vector \((S_1, \dots, S_{n+1})\) is exchangeable, hence the rank of \(S_{n+1}\) among the \(n+1\) scores is uniform on \(\{1, \dots, n+1\}\). Now \(Y_{n+1} \in \mathcal{C}(X_{n+1})\) iff \(S_{n+1} \leq \hat{q}\), i.e., iff the rank of \(S_{n+1}\) is at most \(\lceil (n+1)(1-\alpha) \rceil\). By rank uniformity,

\[
\mathbb{P}\{Y_{n+1} \in \mathcal{C}(X_{n+1})\} = \frac{\lceil (n+1)(1-\alpha) \rceil}{n+1} \geq 1 - \alpha,
\]

which completes the proof. ∎

Three remarks are essential. First, the proof uses *nothing* about \(\hat{\mu}\): a terrible model still yields valid coverage, merely with wide sets - validity and efficiency are cleanly separated. Second, the finite-sample correction \(\lceil (n+1)(1-\alpha) \rceil / n\) (rather than the naive \(1-\alpha\) empirical quantile) is what makes the guarantee exact rather than asymptotic; omitting it is the most common implementation error. Third, coverage is *marginal*: it averages over \(X_{n+1}\), and the theorem says nothing about \(\mathbb{P}(Y_{n+1} \in \mathcal{C}(X_{n+1}) \mid X_{n+1} = x)\).

### Nonconformity scores: the design lever

The score function is the practitioner's main design lever: validity is guaranteed for *any* score, but the *size* of \(\mathcal{C}(x)\) - its efficiency - depends entirely on the score's alignment with the true conditional uncertainty. Common choices:

| Setting | Score \(s(x, y)\) | Resulting set |
|---|---|---|
| Regression, homoscedastic | \(\lvert y - \hat{\mu}(x) \rvert\) | Fixed-width interval |
| Regression, heteroscedastic | \(\lvert y - \hat{\mu}(x) \rvert / \hat{\sigma}(x)\) | Width proportional to \(\hat{\sigma}(x)\) |
| Quantile-based (CQR) [1] | \(\max\{\hat{q}_{\alpha/2}(x) - y,\; y - \hat{q}_{1-\alpha/2}(x)\}\) | Adaptive interval |
| Classification (LAC) | \(1 - \hat{\pi}_y(x)\) | Threshold on softmax |
| Classification (APS) [4] | cumulative softmax mass up to \(y\) | Adaptive label sets |

The normalized-residual score divides by a dispersion estimate \(\hat{\sigma}(x)\) (e.g., from a mean-absolute-deviation regression), producing intervals whose width adapts to local noise - a cheap form of adaptivity that already dominates fixed-width intervals under heteroscedasticity.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Nonconformity scores, nested sets, and efficiency

A deeper viewpoint treats conformal prediction through *nested* prediction sets. A family \(\{\mathcal{F}_t(x)\}_{t \in \mathbb{R}}\) is nested if \(\mathcal{F}_t(x) \subseteq \mathcal{F}_{t'}(x)\) for \(t \leq t'\); any score induces one via \(\mathcal{F}_t(x) = \{y : s(x, y) \leq t\}\). The conformal threshold \(\hat{q}\) simply selects the nesting level calibrated to the desired coverage. Efficiency - expected set size \(\mathbb{E}|\mathcal{C}(X)|\) or interval width - is then governed by how well the nesting order matches the true conditional distribution \(P_{Y \mid X}\): the *oracle* score is (a monotone transform of) the conditional density \(p(y \mid x)\), whose superlevel sets are the smallest sets attaining each coverage level. In practice, scores are heuristics approximating this ideal: quantile-based scores (Section 4.2) approximate conditional quantiles; softmax-based scores (Section 4.3) approximate the conditional class distribution.

Score *calibration* (e.g., Platt scaling of softmax outputs) does not affect validity but can dramatically improve efficiency by making the nesting order more faithful to \(P_{Y\mid X}\) - the motivation for the regularization in RAPS.

### 4.2 Conformalized quantile regression (CQR)

Fixed-width intervals are inefficient under heteroscedasticity: they over-cover low-noise regions and under-cover high-noise ones *conditionally* (marginal validity is preserved, but the sets are uninformative). Quantile regression estimates \(\hat{q}_{\alpha/2}(x), \hat{q}_{1-\alpha/2}(x)\) by minimizing the pinball loss, producing adaptive intervals - but without any coverage guarantee in finite samples. CQR [1] conformalizes them with the score

\[
s(x, y) = \max\{\hat{q}_{\alpha/2}(x) - y,\; y - \hat{q}_{1-\alpha/2}(x)\},
\]

yielding the conformal interval

\[
\mathcal{C}(x) = [\hat{q}_{\alpha/2}(x) - \hat{Q},\; \hat{q}_{1-\alpha/2}(x) + \hat{Q}],
\]

where \(\hat{Q}\) is the conformal quantile of the calibration scores. The adjustment \(\hat{Q}\) is *signed*: if the quantile estimates under-cover, \(\hat{Q} > 0\) widens the interval; if they over-cover, \(\hat{Q} < 0\) *shrinks* it. The marginal coverage theorem applies verbatim, so CQR inherits exact finite-sample marginal validity while adapting interval width to heteroscedasticity.

Empirically, on standard regression benchmarks, CQR attains the nominal 90% marginal coverage with intervals on average substantially shorter than fixed-width conformal intervals, and its coverage is far more balanced across low- and high-noise strata [1]. 

```python
def cqr_interval(q_lo, q_hi, calib_scores, alpha):
    # Conformalized quantile regression interval for one test point.
    n = len(calib_scores)
    level = np.ceil((n + 1) * (1 - alpha)) / n
    Q = np.quantile(calib_scores, level, method="higher")
    return q_lo - Q, q_hi + Q   # Q may be negative: valid shrinkage
```

### 4.3 Adaptive prediction sets for classification: APS and RAPS

For classification with \(K\) classes, the least-ambiguous-classifier (LAC) score \(s(x, y) = 1 - \hat{\pi}_y(x)\) yields sets \(\{y : \hat{\pi}_y(x) \geq 1 - \hat{q}\}\) - valid but *non-adaptive*: easy and hard inputs receive sets governed by the same global softmax threshold, so coverage concentrates on easy examples. Adaptive prediction sets (APS) instead accumulate sorted softmax probabilities: with \(\hat{\pi}_{(1)}(x) \geq \hat{\pi}_{(2)}(x) \geq \cdots\), the score of the true label \(y\) with rank \(r\) is \(s(x, y) = \sum_{j=1}^{r} \hat{\pi}_{(j)}(x)\) (plus a randomization term for exactness). The set includes classes in decreasing softmax order until the cumulative mass exceeds \(\hat{q}\). APS sets are larger for uncertain inputs and singleton for confident ones - *adaptivity* in the classification sense.

Raw APS suffers from a long tail: tiny softmax probabilities of implausible classes accumulate, inflating sets on hard inputs. Regularized APS (RAPS) [4] adds a penalty \(\lambda \cdot (r - k_{\text{reg}})_+\) that discourages including classes beyond rank \(k_{\text{reg}}\), with \(\lambda, k_{\text{reg}}\) tuned on held-out data. On ImageNet with a ResNet-152, RAPS achieves 90% coverage with average set sizes of roughly **2-3 labels** (versus 5-10x larger for a Platt-scaling baseline), and the sets remain small on ImageNet-V2, demonstrating robustness to mild distribution shift [4]. 

### 4.4 The impossibility of distribution-free conditional coverage, and the Mondrian remedy

Marginal coverage averages over \(X_{n+1}\); practitioners often want the stronger *conditional* guarantee \(\mathbb{P}(Y_{n+1} \in \mathcal{C}(X_{n+1}) \mid X_{n+1} = x) \geq 1-\alpha\) for (almost) every \(x\). Barber, Candes, Ramdas, and Tibshirani [3] proved this is impossible in a distribution-free sense:

> **Theorem (Impossibility of conditional coverage [3]).** Let \(\mathcal{C}_n\) be any (possibly randomized) procedure satisfying exact distribution-free conditional coverage. Then for every distribution \(P\) with nonatomic \(X\)-marginal, \(\mathbb{E}[\text{leb}(\mathcal{C}_n(X_{n+1}))] = \infty\) in regression - i.e., the only conditionally valid sets are trivially, infinitely wide.

The proof is a coupling argument: conditional coverage at *every* \(x\) forces the procedure to also cover a counterfactual test point drawn independently of the training data, which can only be guaranteed by unbounded sets. The paper further quantifies the middle ground via \((1-\alpha, \delta)\)-conditional coverage - coverage \(1-\alpha\) conditional on every set \(\mathcal{X} \subseteq \mathcal{X}\) with \(P_X(\mathcal{X}) \geq \delta\) - and shows that achieving it distribution-free requires marginal coverage at the far more stringent level \(1 - \alpha\delta\), producing uselessly wide intervals

The constructive remedy is *Mondrian* (group-conditional) conformal prediction: partition \(\mathcal{X}\) into bins \(\{\mathcal{X}_k\}\) (by a protected attribute, a difficulty score, or any taxonomy) and run split conformal *separately within each bin*, i.e., compute \(\hat{q}_k\) from calibration points with \(X_i \in \mathcal{X}_k\). The coverage guarantee then holds *conditionally on bin membership*:

\[
\mathbb{P}\{Y_{n+1} \in \mathcal{C}(X_{n+1}) \mid X_{n+1} \in \mathcal{X}_k\} \geq 1 - \alpha \quad \forall k,
\]

at the cost of requiring enough calibration points per bin. Mondrian conformal prediction is the principled way to obtain conditional validity for *pre-specified* groups; the impossibility theorem says no method can do this for *all* measurable groups simultaneously.

## 5. Empirical Results and Proofs

**Empirical evidence.** We summarize representative published results (all at nominal 90% coverage, \(\alpha = 0.1\)):

| Method / dataset | Marginal coverage | Mean set size / width |
|---|---|---|
| Split conformal, absolute residual (regression benchmarks) | 0.900 +/- 0.005 | baseline width \(2\hat{q}\) |
| CQR, neural-net quantiles [1] | 0.901 +/- 0.004 | 10-20% narrower than baseline |
| LAC on ImageNet / ResNet-152 [4] | 0.900 | ~19 labels |
| APS on ImageNet / ResNet-152 [4] | 0.900 | ~4-5 labels |
| RAPS on ImageNet / ResNet-152 [4] | 0.900 | ~2-3 labels |
| RAPS on ImageNet-V2 (shifted) [4] | 0.895 | ~3 labels |

Three lessons emerge. **First**, validity is essentially exact everywhere: empirical coverage matches \(1-\alpha\) to within Monte Carlo error regardless of the model or score - the theory's signature prediction. **Second**, efficiency varies enormously with score design: moving from LAC to RAPS shrinks ImageNet sets by nearly an order of magnitude at identical coverage. **Third**, mild distribution shift (ImageNet to ImageNet-V2) degrades coverage only slightly, but the guarantee itself is void without exchangeability - a practitioner caveat, not a theorem violation.

For language models, conformal prediction enables *selective generation*: a nonconformity score over sampled completions calibrates a response set guaranteed to contain an acceptable answer with probability 1-alpha [2], with set size serving as a calibrated abstention signal.

**Proof of the coverage theorem (complete).** We restate and prove the central result in full. Let \(S_i = s(X_i, Y_i)\), \(i = 1, \dots, n+1\), with \((X_i, Y_i)\) exchangeable and \(s\) any measurable function.

> **Theorem.** \(\mathbb{P}(S_{n+1} \leq \hat{q}) \geq 1-\alpha\), where \(\hat{q}\) is the \(\lceil (n+1)(1-\alpha) \rceil / n\) empirical quantile of \(S_1, \dots, S_n\).

*Proof.* Exchangeability of the data and symmetry of \(s\) imply \((S_1, \dots, S_{n+1})\) is exchangeable. Hence, with \(R\) the rank of \(S_{n+1}\) among \(\{S_1, \dots, S_{n+1}\}\) (ties broken arbitrarily but symmetrically), \(R\) is uniform on \(\{1, \dots, n+1\}\); i.e., \(\mathbb{P}(R = r) = 1/(n+1)\) for all \(r\). Let \(k = \lceil (n+1)(1-\alpha) \rceil\). By definition of the empirical quantile with `method="higher"`, \(S_{n+1} \leq \hat{q}\) iff \(R \leq k\) (up to tie-handling that can only increase coverage). Therefore

\[
\mathbb{P}(S_{n+1} \leq \hat{q}) = \mathbb{P}(R \leq k) = \frac{k}{n+1} = \frac{\lceil (n+1)(1-\alpha) \rceil}{n+1} \geq 1 - \alpha.
\]

Since \(Y_{n+1} \in \mathcal{C}(X_{n+1}) \iff S_{n+1} \leq \hat{q}\), the claim follows. ∎

The proof's brevity is the point: the entire edifice - CQR, APS, RAPS, Mondrian - rests on rank uniformity under exchangeability, a one-paragraph argument with no distributional or model assumptions.

## 6. Limitations and Open Problems

1. **Exchangeability is load-bearing.** Under distribution shift - temporal drift, deployment-population mismatch, feedback loops - calibration and test scores are no longer exchangeable and coverage can fail arbitrarily. Weighted conformal prediction [6] restores validity under *known* covariate shift via likelihood-ratio reweighting of the empirical quantile, and "conformal prediction beyond exchangeability" [6] provides coverage bounds degrading gracefully with a measure of non-exchangeability. 

2. **Conditional coverage remains elusive.** The impossibility theorem [3] rules out exact distribution-free conditional coverage with bounded sets, and Mondrian methods only condition on pre-specified groups. *Approximate* conditional coverage - e.g., via localized or weighted conformal methods that condition softly on \(x\) - is an active frontier, but all such methods either assume smoothness of \(P_{Y\mid X}\) or lose the distribution-free property.

3. **Efficiency under model misspecification.** Validity is free, but efficiency is not: a poor \(\hat{\mu}\) or poorly chosen score yields valid yet vacuous sets (e.g., the entire label space). There is no distribution-free *optimality* theory comparable to the coverage theory; score design remains heuristic, guided by the oracle-score intuition of Section 4.1.

4. **Structured and sequential outputs.** Extending conformal guarantees to LLM generations, sets, rankings, and multi-step agent trajectories requires nonconformity scores over combinatorial spaces and raises questions about *what* coverage means when the output space itself is model-generated. Conformal language modeling and conformal abstention are promising but nascent.

5. **Computational cost of full conformal.** Split conformal needs one fit; full conformal and jackknife+ need \(O(n)\) fits, prohibitive for foundation models. Cross-conformal and out-of-bag variants reduce cost but complicate the exchangeability argument.

## 7. Conclusion

Conformal prediction delivers something almost paradoxical: *rigorous, finite-sample uncertainty quantification with a single, weak, checkable-in-principle assumption*. Exchangeability of calibration and test data - and nothing else, no model correctness, no distributional shape - suffices for exact marginal coverage via a proof that fits in a paragraph. The methodology is a clean separation of concerns: the nonconformity score controls *efficiency* (CQR for heteroscedastic regression, APS/RAPS for adaptive classification, Mondrian binning for group-conditional validity), while the conformal calibration step controls *validity* uniformly over all scores.

The limits are equally sharp. Exact conditional coverage is impossible without further assumptions [3]; exchangeability violations void the guarantee [6]; and efficiency under misspecification has no free lunch. These boundaries, precisely characterized, are what make conformal prediction a *scientific* tool rather than a heuristic: it tells the practitioner exactly what is promised, exactly what is assumed, and exactly where the promise ends.
