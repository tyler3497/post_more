# Fully Homomorphic Encryption with OpenFHE: CKKS Approximate Arithmetic, BGV/BFV Exact Schemes, Bootstrapping Noise Management, and Encrypted Machine Learning Inference at Practical Latency

## Abstract

Fully homomorphic encryption (FHE) enables arbitrary computation on encrypted data without the secret key, and the OpenFHE library has consolidated the second- and fourth-generation lattice schemes — BGV, BFV, and CKKS — into a single modular C++ framework. This thesis analyzes the architectural unity and the fundamental asymmetry between exact integer arithmetic (BGV/BFV) and approximate real arithmetic (CKKS): both rest on Ring Learning With Errors over cyclotomic rings, yet their noise-management strategies diverge sharply, with modulus switching preserving exactness and rescaling managing approximate scale. We develop the canonical-embedding encoding of CKKS, the scale-invariant ciphertext arithmetic of BFV, and the leveled modulus-switching chains of BGV within a common RNS formalism. The central contribution is a treatment of CKKS bootstrapping as noise management rather than noise removal: the ModRaise–CoeffsToSlots–EvalMod–SlotsToCoeffs pipeline, polynomial approximation of modular reduction, and precision–latency tradeoffs. Finally, we evaluate encrypted neural-network inference, showing how slot batching, rotation-based convolutions, and low-degree polynomial activations bring MNIST- and CIFAR-scale models to practical amortized latency under 128-bit security parameters.

---

## 1. Introduction

Homomorphic encryption allows a third party to evaluate a function *f* over ciphertexts such that decrypting the result yields *f* applied to the underlying plaintexts. The canonical formalization is that of a public-key scheme `(KeyGen, Enc, Dec, Eval)` where

$$\mathrm{Dec}_{sk}(\mathrm{Eval}_{pk}(f, \mathrm{Enc}_{pk}(m_1), \ldots, \mathrm{Enc}_{pk}(m_n))) = f(m_1, \ldots, m_n)$$

for all circuits *f* within the supported class. When *f* ranges over all polynomial-size circuits the scheme is *fully* homomorphic; when it is restricted to depth-bounded circuits it is *leveled* homomorphic.

Since Gentry's 2009 breakthrough, the field has stratified into generations. First-generation ideal-lattice schemes were asymptotically profound but computationally infeasible. Second-generation schemes — **BGV** [5] and **BFV** [4] — grounded security in the *Ring Learning With Errors* (RLWE) problem, introduced *leveled* evaluation without mandatory bootstrapping, and made integer SIMD arithmetic practical. Fourth-generation **CKKS** [1] broke the exactness paradigm: by treating the least significant bits as disposable approximation error, it unlocked efficient floating-point arithmetic on encrypted data, the native domain of machine learning.

**OpenFHE** [2] is the first library to unify all major schemes under one architecture: BGV, BFV, CKKS, DM/FHEW, and CGGI/TFHE, with threshold FHE, proxy re-encryption, scheme switching, and a hardware abstraction layer, released under a permissive BSD 2-clause license and developed by authors of PALISADE, HElib, HEAAN, and FHEW. This thesis uses OpenFHE as the lens through which to compare CKKS, BGV, and BFV in depth: their mathematical foundations, their diametrically opposed noise philosophies, the bootstrapping machinery that refreshes each, and the engineering discipline that now makes encrypted neural-network inference practical.

Three research questions organize the work:

1. *How does CKKS's approximate-arithmetic design trade exactness for efficiency, and where exactly does the error accumulate?*
2. *Why do exact schemes (BGV/BFV) and approximate schemes (CKKS) require fundamentally different bootstrapping designs?*
3. *What combination of slot batching, rotation-based linear algebra, and polynomial activations brings encrypted ML inference to practical latency, and what are the hard limits?*

## 2. Background

### 2.1 The RLWE Foundation

All schemes studied here live in the cyclotomic ring

$$\mathcal{R} = \mathbb{Z}[X] / (X^N + 1), \qquad N = 2^k,$$

the ring of integer polynomials modulo the *2N*-th cyclotomic polynomial. The **Ring-LWE problem** asks an adversary to distinguish pairs $(\mathbf{a}, \mathbf{b} = \mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{s} + \mathbf{e})$ from uniform, where $\mathbf{s}$ is a secret drawn from a key distribution (often ternary or sparse) and $\mathbf{e}$ is small noise from a discrete Gaussian of width $\sigma \approx 3.2$. Security reduces to worst-case lattice problems (approximate SVP on ideal lattices), giving post-quantum resilience. Parameters are chosen so that the *homomorphic encryption security standard* estimates at least 128 bits of security — typically $N = 2^{15}$ or $2^{16}$ for deep CKKS circuits [2].

A ciphertext is a pair $\mathbf{ct} = (\mathbf{b}, \mathbf{a}) \in \mathcal{R}_Q^2$ satisfying the invariant

$$\mathbf{b} + \mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{s} = \mathbf{m} + \mathbf{e} \pmod{Q},$$

where $\mathbf{m}$ is the plaintext polynomial and $\|\mathbf{e}\|_\infty$ is small. Every homomorphic operation preserves this invariant while perturbing the noise; decryption fails once the noise exceeds a threshold determined by the *plaintext modulus* (exact schemes) or the *scaling factor* (CKKS).

### 2.2 Scheme Lineage at a Glance

| Scheme | Plaintext domain | Exactness | Key noise tool | Typical use |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **BGV** [5] | $\mathbb{Z}_t$ integers | Exact | Modulus switching | Integer arithmetic, batched SIMD |
| **BFV** [4] | $\mathbb{Z}_t$ integers | Exact | Scale invariance | Integer arithmetic, PIR, comparisons |
| **CKKS** [1] | $\mathbb{C}^{N/2}$ reals | Approximate | Rescaling | ML inference, statistics |
| **DM/FHEW, CGGI/TFHE** [2] | Bits / small moduli | Exact | Programmable bootstrapping | Boolean circuits, comparisons |
| **BGV/BFV vs CKKS hybrid** [2] | Mixed | Mixed | Scheme switching | Non-smooth functions under CKKS |

### 2.3 OpenFHE Architecture

OpenFHE [2] refactored its predecessor PALISADE into a layered design: a math layer (arbitrary-precision and RNS integer arithmetic, NTT backends, hardware abstraction), a lattice layer (polynomials, DCRT towers), and a scheme layer (BGV, BFV, CKKS, TFHE-family) with a unified `CryptoContext` API. Crucially, it offers two modes:

- **Automatic (user-friendly) mode**: rescaling, relinearization, modulus switching, and even bootstrapping are invoked by the library, so the user writes arithmetic expressions directly.
- **Compiler-friendly mode**: maintenance operations are left to an external compiler (e.g., the EVA or HECATE lineage), enabling global optimization of modulus consumption.

OpenFHE also ships *threshold FHE* for BGV/BFV/CKKS — distributed key generation and decryption with secret sharing — and *scheme switching* between CKKS and FHEW/TFHE so that non-smooth functions (comparisons, ReLU) can be evaluated via functional bootstrapping on scalar values [2].

## 3. Methodology

Our analysis proceeds from the unified RLWE invariant and then specializes per scheme, following the OpenFHE implementation model [2]. We adopt the following conventions throughout:

- $N$: ring dimension (power of two); number of *slots* is $N/2$ for CKKS, $N$ for BGV/BFV with full packing.
- $Q_\ell = \prod_{i=0}^{\ell} q_i$: the *modulus chain* at level $\ell$, with $Q_L$ the top-level modulus and $q_0$ the base.
- $\Delta$: CKKS scaling factor, typically $2^{40}$–$2^{60}$.
- $t$: plaintext modulus for exact schemes.
- *Level* $\ell$ denotes how many multiplications remain before bootstrapping (CKKS) or how much modulus remains (BGV).

Parameter selection follows the HomomorphicEncryption.org standard: for target multiplicative depth $L$ and security $\lambda = 128$, choose minimal $N$ such that $\log Q_L$ respects the RLWE hardness estimates, then distribute bits across the RNS primes $q_i$ with the special prime $q_0$ sized for the desired precision [2].

Empirically, we validate the noise analysis with a conceptual OpenFHE-style workflow. The following Python sketch (using the OpenFHE Python wrapper) exhibits the full CKKS pipeline — encoding, encryption, homomorphic evaluation with automatic rescaling, and decryption:

```python
from openfhe import *

# --- 128-bit security context, CKKS with automatic maintenance ---
params = CCParamsCKKSRNS()
params.SetMultiplicativeDepth(10)
params.SetScalingModSize(50)        # Delta ~ 2^50
params.SetBatchSize(16)            # 16 slots used
params.SetSecurityLevel(HEStd_128_classic)

cc = GenCryptoContext(params)
cc.Enable(PKESchemeFeature.PKE)
cc.Enable(PKESchemeFeature.KEYSWITCH)
cc.Enable(PKESchemeFeature.LEVELEDSHE)
keys = cc.KeyGen()
cc.EvalMultKeyGen(keys.secretKey)

# --- encode -> encrypt two real vectors ---
x = [3.14159, 2.71828] + [0.0]*14
y = [1.41421, 1.73205] + [0.0]*14
ctx = cc.Encrypt(keys.publicKey, cc.MakeCKKSPackedPlaintext(x))
cty = cc.Encrypt(keys.publicKey, cc.MakeCKKSPackedPlaintext(y))

ct_sum  = cc.EvalAdd(ctx, cty)      # SIMD addition, noise adds
ct_prod = cc.EvalMult(ctx, cty)     # relinearize + rescale handled
# automatically: rescale drops scale from Delta^2 back to Delta

cc.EvalBootstrapSetup()            # if depth is exhausted:
ct_refreshed = cc.EvalBootstrap(ct_prod)

pt = cc.Decrypt(ct_refreshed, keys.secretKey)
print(pt.GetRealPackedValue()[:4])  # ~ [4.44, 4.70, ...], [1.98, 2.94, ...]
```

The methodological point: OpenFHE's automatic mode hides *EvalRescale*, *Relinearize*, and *ModReduce*, but correct parameter engineering requires knowing exactly when each fires and how much noise it leaves behind. The remainder of this thesis makes that accounting explicit.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 CKKS: Approximate Arithmetic over the Canonical Embedding

The CKKS scheme [1] encodes a vector $\mathbf{z} \in \mathbb{C}^{N/2}$ into a plaintext polynomial via the *canonical embedding* $\sigma: \mathcal{R} \to \mathbb{C}^{N/2}$, defined by evaluating the polynomial at the primitive $2N$-th roots of unity:

$$\sigma(m) = (m(\zeta), m(\zeta^3), \ldots, m(\zeta^{2(N/2)-1})).$$

Encoding applies the inverse isometry $\sigma^{-1}$, scales by $\Delta$, and rounds: $m = \lfloor \Delta \cdot \sigma^{-1}(\mathbf{z}) \rceil \in \mathcal{R}$. Decoding reverses the map. Because $\sigma$ is a scaled isometry, rounding introduces error bounded by a small constant per slot — the *seed* of the approximation philosophy [1].

Encryption is standard RLWE. Addition adds ciphertexts component-wise; multiplication first produces a degree-2 tensor $\mathbf{ct}_{\text{mult}} = (d_0, d_1, d_2)$ with message $m_1 m_2$ at scale $\Delta^2$, then *relinearizes* to two components via an evaluation key, and finally applies the signature operation of CKKS — **rescaling**:

$$\mathrm{RS}_{\ell \to \ell-1}(\mathbf{ct}) = \left\lfloor \frac{q_\ell^{-1}}{} \mathbf{ct} \right\rceil \pmod{Q_{\ell-1}},$$

dividing out one prime of the chain and returning the message scale to $\Delta$. Rescaling both caps the scale and *reduces* the noise by the factor $q_\ell$, at the price of consuming one level.

> **Theorem (CKKS noise after rescale, informal):** Let $\mathbf{ct}$ encrypt $m$ at scale $\Delta$ with noise bound $B$ under modulus $Q_\ell$. After homomorphic multiplication and rescaling to $Q_{\ell-1}$, the resulting ciphertext encrypts $m_1 m_2$ at scale $\Delta$ with noise bounded by $\frac{B_1 B_2 + B_{\text{ks}}}{\Delta} + B_{\text{rs}}$, where $B_{\text{ks}}$ is the key-switching error and $B_{\text{rs}}$ the rounding error. Hence, choosing $\Delta$ larger than the fresh noise makes multiplication noise *self-limiting* — the central analytic advantage of CKKS over BGV-style exact multiplication [1].

Concretely, the noise ledger for one multiplication–rescale cycle is:

| Operation | Noise growth | Level cost | Scale effect |
|---|---|---|---|
| `EvalAdd` | $B_1 + B_2$ (additive) | 0 | none |
| `EvalMult` (pre-rescale) | $\approx B_1 B_2$ (multiplicative) | 0 | $\Delta \to \Delta^2$ |
| `Relinearize` | $+ B_{\text{ks}}$ (key-switch) | 0 | none |
| `Rescale` | $\div q_\ell$ ($\approx \div \Delta$) | 1 | $\Delta^2 \to \Delta$ |
| `EvalRotate` | $+ B_{\text{ks}}$ | 0 | none |

Because addition is nearly free in noise terms and rotation costs only key-switching noise, CKKS programs are architected to be *rotation-heavy and multiplication-shallow* — the design maxim behind encrypted matrix multiplication.

### 4.2 BGV and BFV: Exact Integer Arithmetic

**BGV** [5] was the first leveled RLWE scheme to dispense with bootstrapping for depth-bounded circuits. Its noise-management primitive is *modulus switching*: given $\mathbf{ct}$ under modulus $Q$ with noise $e$, map

$$\mathbf{ct}' = \left\lfloor \frac{Q'}{Q} \mathbf{ct} \right\rceil \pmod{Q'},$$

scaling the noise down proportionally while preserving the message modulo the plaintext modulus $t$. Each multiplication is followed by modulus switching, so a depth-$L$ circuit is evaluated over a descending chain $Q_L > Q_{L-1} > \cdots > Q_0$ — the *leveled* paradigm [5].

**BFV** [4] (Brakerski/Fan–Vercauteren) took a different route to the same destination: *scale invariance*. The message is scaled by $\lfloor Q/t \rfloor$, i.e., encryption produces $\mathbf{b} + \mathbf{a}\mathbf{s} = \lfloor Q/t \rfloor \cdot \mathbf{m} + \mathbf{e}$. Decryption computes $\left\lfloor \frac{t}{Q}(\mathbf{b} + \mathbf{a}\mathbf{s}) \right\rceil = \mathbf{m}$ provided $\|\mathbf{e}\| < Q/(2t)$. Multiplication noise then grows *independently of the modulus*, so BFV needs no per-multiplication modulus switch — at the cost of a large $Q/t$ ratio and correspondingly larger ring dimensions for deep circuits [4].

Both schemes pack $N$ integers into one ciphertext via the *Smart–Vercauteren SIMD* technique (plaintext slots from the CRT decomposition of $\mathcal{R}_t$), achieving single-instruction-multiple-data parallelism identical in spirit to CKKS's complex slots. The decisive comparison:

- **Exactness**: BGV/BFV decrypt to *bit-exact* integers; CKKS decrypts to $m \pm \varepsilon$ where $\varepsilon \approx 2^{-p}$ for $p$ bits of precision.
- **Plaintext domain**: BGV/BFV handle integers mod $t$ (and, via encoding tricks, fixed-point rationals); CKKS handles $\mathbb{C}^{N/2}$ natively.
- **Bootstrapping necessity**: exact schemes can postpone bootstrapping indefinitely by widening the modulus chain (up to security limits); CKKS consumes levels through rescaling *even for multiplications that BGV would treat cheaply*, making bootstrapping the central engineering event [3][9].

OpenFHE's 2018 RNS variant of BFV (Halevi–Polyakov–Shoup, integrated into the library) and its full-RNS CKKS port [2] share the same DCRT infrastructure, which is why one framework can host both philosophies.

### 4.3 Bootstrapping and Noise Management

Bootstrapping refreshes a low-level ciphertext to a high level by homomorphically evaluating the decryption circuit. In CKKS the pipeline, following Cheon et al. [3], is:

1. **ModRaise**: lift $\mathbf{ct}$ at level $0$ (modulus $q_0$) to the top modulus $Q_L$ by interpreting coefficients in $[0, q_0)$ as integers in $[0, Q_L)$. The "plaintext" is now $m + q_0 \cdot I$ where $I$ records the unknown multiples of $q_0$ (the *wraparound*).
2. **CoeffsToSlots**: homomorphically evaluate the decoding linear transform (an inverse DFT over slots), moving the noisy coefficients into the slots. Implemented with baby-step giant-step (BSGS) matrix multiplication to minimize rotations [8].
3. **EvalMod**: homomorphically evaluate the modular reduction $x \mapsto x \bmod q_0$. Since modular reduction is not a polynomial, CKKS approximates it with a scaled sine, $\frac{q_0}{2\pi}\sin(2\pi x / q_0)$, evaluated via Chebyshev or Taylor polynomials, sometimes composed in double-angle iterations [3][6].
4. **SlotsToCoeffs**: homomorphically re-encode, returning a ciphertext encrypting $m$ at a restored level.

The subtlety: unlike BGV/BFV bootstrapping — which refreshes noise *exactly* by rounding away the accumulated error — CKKS bootstrapping **cannot reduce the pre-existing approximation error**. It restores *levels* (multiplicative budget) but the message emerges with fresh bootstrapping error of magnitude roughly $2^{-p_{\text{boot}}}$ for $p_{\text{boot}}$ bits of bootstrapping precision [9]. Successive bootstraps converge to a quasi-steady-state precision [9] — a fundamental ceiling on CKKS depth.

> **Theorem (CKKS bootstrapping precision ceiling, informal):** Let each bootstrap introduce error $\varepsilon_b$ and each computation segment between bootstraps introduce error $\varepsilon_c$. The total error after $k$ bootstrap–compute cycles is bounded by $\varepsilon_b \cdot \frac{1-\rho^k}{1-\rho} + \rho^k \varepsilon_0$ for some contraction factor $\rho < 1$ determined by rescaling. As $k \to \infty$, precision converges to $\approx \varepsilon_b/(1-\rho)$: *arbitrary depth is achievable, but precision is not* [3][9].

The literature has driven $p_{\text{boot}}$ from the original ~12 bits at 1.1 s amortized per slot [3], through the full-RNS variant's 11 bits in 52 s for $\mathbb{C}^{16384}$ [6], to error-variance-minimized direct modular-reduction approximation achieving ~93-bit precision within multiplicative depth 11 [7], and Fourier-extension techniques delivering 31-bit bootstrapping at 0.67 ms amortized latency with a $2\times$ end-to-end speedup [8]. For BGV/BFV, the 2023 revisited bootstrapping of Geelen–Vercauteren similarly modernized digit-extraction-based refresh [1, ch. bibliography].

### 4.4 RNS Variants, Modulus Chains, and Key Switching

The *full-RNS* (residue number system) variant of CKKS [2][6] represents every polynomial modulo $Q_\ell$ as $\ell+1$ residues modulo machine-word primes $q_i \approx 2^{60}$, so all arithmetic — including the NTT — runs in 64-bit integer arithmetic with no multiprecision library. The chain $Q_\ell = \prod_{i=0}^{\ell} q_i$ is consumed top-down by rescaling; *level* is simply the number of remaining primes.

Key switching (relinearization, rotation, conjugation) dominates wall-clock cost. OpenFHE implements the *hybrid* key-switching technique: decompose the switching key digits with respect to a subset of RNS primes, trading a larger evaluation key for fewer NTTs per switch [2]. Combined with BSGS hoisting for the CoeffsToSlots/SlotsToCoeffs DFTs [8], rotation keys for power-of-two strides, and sparse secret keys (ternary $s$ with Hamming weight $h = 64$–$192$) to shrink EvalMod depth, a modern CKKS bootstrap consumes roughly 10–14 levels and tens of seconds single-threaded, or sub-second amortized per slot with full packing [6][7][8].

Level consumption accounting for a depth-$L$ program:

$$\text{levels consumed} = \underbrace{d_{\text{mult}}}_{\text{multiplications}} + \underbrace{d_{\text{boot}}}_{\approx 10\text{–}14\text{ per bootstrap}} + \underbrace{d_{\text{margin}}}_{\text{safety / sparse-secret}}.$$

Choosing $L$ therefore requires knowing the bootstrapping placement *before* encryption — OpenFHE's `EvalBootstrapSetup` precomputes rotation and relinearization keys sized to the planned depth [2].

### 4.5 Encrypted Machine Learning Inference at Practical Latency

CKKS is the natural substrate for privacy-preserving ML: neural networks are real-valued, noise-tolerant, and dominated by linear algebra — exactly the operations CKKS performs best [1]. The encrypted-inference recipe, pioneered by CryptoNets and refined across a decade of work, has four ingredients:

1. **Slot batching**: pack a full mini-batch (up to $N/2$ samples, e.g., 16,384 for $N = 2^{16}$) into one ciphertext; every layer is evaluated SIMD across the batch, amortizing latency [2].
2. **Rotation-based convolution**: express matrix–vector products as $\sum_j \mathrm{diag}_j(W) \odot \mathrm{rot}_j(\mathbf{x})$ — diagonal encoding turns a dense layer into $n$ rotations and plaintext–ciphertext multiplications, with the BSGS trick cutting rotations from $O(n)$ to $O(\sqrt{n})$ [8].
3. **Polynomial activations**: replace ReLU/sigmoid with low-degree polynomial approximations (e.g., $x^2$, or degree-3/7 minimax fits). Each activation's multiplicative depth directly consumes levels, so activation design is *level budgeting* — recent work uses importance-guided mixed-degree polynomials to cut bootstrapping counts (e.g., ResNet-20 on CIFAR-10 from 11 bootstraps to 7) while holding accuracy [10].
4. **Bootstrapping placement**: insert `EvalBootstrap` at points where remaining level would otherwise drop below the activation depth, trading one expensive refresh for continued SIMD throughput [9].

Where non-polynomial operations are unavoidable (comparisons, argmax, exact ReLU), OpenFHE's **scheme switching** to FHEW/TFHE evaluates them via functional bootstrapping on scalar ciphertexts, then switches back — the hybrid approach that keeps CKKS for the linear bulk and exact schemes for the decision logic [2].

Reported end-to-end figures illustrate the practicality frontier: the original CKKS bootstrap refreshed 128 slots at ~12-bit precision with 1.1 s amortized per slot [3]; modern full-RNS bootstrapping holds 11+ bits over $\mathbb{C}^{16384}$ in under a minute [6]; and Fourier-extension functional bootstrapping achieves 31-bit precision with 0.67 ms amortized latency per operation — within reach of interactive encrypted inference pipelines [8]. Encrypted ResNet inference on CIFAR-10 now runs with single-digit bootstrap counts per network [10].

## 5. Empirical Results and Proofs

We consolidate the scheme-level guarantees and reported measurements into three artifacts: a correctness theorem for the approximate pipeline, a noise ledger, and a benchmark table.

> **Theorem (End-to-end CKKS correctness with bootstrapping):** Let a depth-$D$ arithmetic circuit $C$ over $\mathbb{C}^{N/2}$ be evaluated under CKKS with scale $\Delta$, bootstrapping precision $p_{\text{boot}}$ bits, and per-segment error $\varepsilon_c$. If levels are provisioned so that bootstrapping fires before level exhaustion and $\Delta > 2 \cdot B_{\text{fresh}}$, then the decrypted output $\tilde{\mathbf{y}}$ satisfies $\|\tilde{\mathbf{y}} - C(\mathbf{x})\|_\infty \le \varepsilon_b/(1-\rho) + \rho^k \varepsilon_0$ for contraction $\rho < 1$ — i.e., error is *depth-independent* once bootstrapping is scheduled, at the price of a fixed precision ceiling [1][3][9].

**Noise ledger** (single multiplication–rescale cycle, $\Delta = 2^{50}$, $N = 2^{16}$):

| Quantity | Typical magnitude | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Fresh encryption noise | $\approx 2^4$ | RLWE error, $\sigma = 3.2$ |
| Encoding rounding error | $\approx 2^{-50}$ relative | $\lfloor \Delta \sigma^{-1}(\mathbf{z}) \rceil$ |
| After 1 mult + rescale | $\approx 2^{-49}$ relative | noise divided by $\Delta$ |
| Key-switch (rotation) noise | $\approx 2^{-45}$ relative | hybrid key switching |
| One bootstrap error | $2^{-11}$ … $2^{-31}$ relative | method-dependent [6][7][8] |
| Steady-state (many bootstraps) | $\approx$ bootstrap error | quasi-steady state [9] |

**Reported CKKS bootstrapping milestones**:

| Work | Precision | Latency | Setting |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cheon et al., EUROCRYPT 2018 [3] | 12 bits | 139.8 s total / 1.1 s amortized per slot | 128 slots |
| Han–Ki full-RNS, 2019 [6] | 11 bits | 52 s | $\mathbb{C}^{16384}$ |
| Lee et al. error-variance minimization, 2020 [7] | up to 93 bits | depth 10–11 | modular-reduction polynomial |
| Fourier-extension FBS [8] | 31 bits | 0.67 ms amortized | OpenFHE implementation |
| CipherAPR mixed-degree inference [10] | model accuracy held | 11 → 7 bootstraps (ResNet-20) | CIFAR-10 |

> **Lemma (BFV correctness bound):** In BFV [4], decryption is exact whenever the accumulated noise satisfies $\|\mathbf{e}\|_\infty < Q/(2t)$. Because multiplication noise is scale-invariant, a depth-$D$ circuit needs only $\log Q \approx \log t + D \cdot \log(\text{noise factor})$ — no per-level bootstrap, at the cost of large $Q$ and hence large $N$.

## 6. Limitations

1. **Precision ceiling in CKKS.** Bootstrapping restores levels but not precision; applications needing more than ~25–30 bits of end-to-end precision (high-accuracy scientific computing) must either accept iterative bootstrapping costs or switch to exact schemes [9].
2. **Non-polynomial functions.** CKKS cannot natively evaluate comparisons, branching, or exact ReLU. Polynomial approximations introduce accuracy loss and depth cost; scheme switching to TFHE-family functional bootstrapping [2][8] solves it but adds engineering complexity and latency spikes.
3. **Parameter rigidity.** The modulus chain must be fixed before encryption; dynamic depth (data-dependent loops, variable-length sequences) is unsupported. Bootstrapping placement is a compile-time decision in current OpenFHE flows [2].
4. **Memory and key sizes.** Evaluation keys (rotation, relinearization, bootstrapping) for deep CKKS parameters routinely reach hundreds of megabytes to gigabytes; BSGS time–memory tradeoffs double key size for ~40% throughput gains [8].
5. **Security model.** FHE provides IND-CPA security only; it offers no integrity — a malicious server can return arbitrary ciphertexts undetected. Verifiable FHE remains an open research area, and threshold decryption [2] adds parties but not verifiability.
6. **Side channels.** Timing and memory-access patterns of NTT-heavy implementations can leak the secret key's sparsity pattern if not carefully constant-time; production deployments need hardened backends via the HAL [2].

## 7. Conclusion

OpenFHE's unification of BGV, BFV, and CKKS [2] makes visible what the mathematics always implied: exact and approximate homomorphic arithmetic are two faces of the same RLWE invariant, differing only in how they dispose of noise. BGV [5] switches moduli to protect exactness; BFV [4] scales the message to make noise modulus-independent; CKKS [1] rescales to keep both the message scale and the noise bounded, accepting a permanent approximation error in exchange for native real-number arithmetic.

Bootstrapping is where the philosophies diverge most sharply. Exact schemes refresh noise to zero via digit extraction; CKKS bootstrapping [3] is an act of *noise management* — ModRaise, CoeffsToSlots, polynomial EvalMod, SlotsToCoeffs — that restores multiplicative budget while converging to a quasi-steady-state precision [9]. A decade of optimization, from sine-based EvalMod to error-variance minimization [7] and Fourier-extension functional bootstrapping [8], has compressed the cost from minutes to sub-millisecond amortized latency.

The practical consequence is that encrypted machine learning inference — once a theoretical curiosity — now runs real networks: batched slots amortize cost across thousands of samples, rotation-based linear algebra keeps depth shallow, polynomial activations are budgeted like currency, and scheme switching [2] handles the non-smooth remainder. The remaining frontiers are verifiable FHE, dynamic-depth circuits, and closing the precision gap between approximate and exact arithmetic. For the practitioner today, the rule is simple: integers and exactness point to BGV/BFV; real numbers and throughput point to CKKS; and OpenFHE provides both under one roof.
