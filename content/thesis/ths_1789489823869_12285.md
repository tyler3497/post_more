# Auction Theory: Myerson's Lemma, Revenue Equivalence, and Combinatorial Spectrum Auctions

## Abstract

Auction theory stands among the most successful intersections of economic theory, optimization, and algorithm design, combining deep mathematical structure with mechanisms that have allocated hundreds of billions of dollars of public assets. This thesis develops single-parameter theory from first principles: the revelation principle, incentive compatibility, and the revenue equivalence theorem of Vickrey [1] as extended by Myerson [2]. We derive Myerson's lemma, which re-expresses expected payments and revenue in terms of *virtual valuations*, and show how revenue maximization reduces to maximizing virtual surplus, yielding the optimal auction for regular environments — canonically, a second-price auction with an optimal reserve. Through the marginal-revenue lens of Bulow and Roberts [3], we then turn to the multi-parameter frontier: the Vickrey–Clarke–Groves mechanism, NP-hard winner determination in combinatorial auctions [8], and the simultaneous multiple-round auction designed for FCC spectrum sales [4][6]. A computational study verifies revenue equivalence and quantifies optimal reserves, and we close with the fragility of the classical results and the simple-versus-optimal program of algorithmic mechanism design [5][7][9].

---

## 1 Introduction

Few branches of microeconomic theory can claim a record of practical deployment to match auction theory. The United States Federal Communications Commission has raised well over one hundred billion dollars through spectrum auctions whose rules were drafted with the direct involvement of auction theorists [6]. Sponsored-search advertising markets clear billions of queries per day through auction-derived pricing rules [7]. The subject is not merely an elegant exercise in optimization under private information; it is the rare theory whose theorems have been stress-tested at planetary scale.

The intellectual arc of this thesis runs from the single-item, single-parameter setting to the combinatorial frontier. In the single-parameter world, three results dominate. *First*, Vickrey's revenue equivalence theorem [1]: under independent private values, all standard formats that allocate efficiently yield identical expected revenue. *Second*, Myerson's lemma [2]: incentive-compatible payments are pinned down by the allocation rule alone — expected revenue equals expected *virtual surplus*. *Third*, the optimal auction: sell to the bidder with the highest non-negative virtual valuation, which under symmetry and regularity is simply a second-price auction with a reserve price.

Beyond the single-item case lie the results that govern modern market design. The Vickrey–Clarke–Groves mechanism generalizes second-price logic to arbitrary environments and restores truthfulness and efficiency, at a sometimes severe cost in revenue and robustness. Combinatorial auctions confront the computational wall of winner determination, an NP-hard optimization problem [8]. And the great applied triumph of the field — the simultaneous multiple-round auction for spectrum licenses — shows how theorists adapted ascending-auction logic to settings with strong complementarities across licenses [4].

This thesis is organized as follows. Section 2 fixes the model and the solution concepts. Section 3 develops the methodology: the revelation principle, Myerson's lemma, and virtual valuations. Section 4 treats the deep results — revenue equivalence, optimal design, competition as a design substitute, and the combinatorial frontier. Section 5 provides computational verification and proof sketches. Section 6 catalogs the limitations and the modern response from algorithmic mechanism design. Section 7 concludes.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The independent private values model

We consider a seller with one indivisible item and $n$ bidders. Bidder $i$ has a private valuation $v_i$ drawn independently from a distribution $F_i$ with density $f_i$, supported on an interval of the non-negative reals. The benchmark *independent private values* (IPV) model assumes these draws are independent and that each bidder's payoff depends only on their own valuation — there is no common-value component, no affiliation, and no resale. Bidders are risk-neutral with quasi-linear utility: a bidder who wins and pays $p$ obtains utility $v_i - p$.

The four canonical single-item formats are:

- **English (ascending) auction**: price rises until one bidder remains; the winner pays the price at which the last rival dropped out.
- **Dutch (descending) auction**: price falls until a bidder claims the item; strategically equivalent to the sealed-bid first-price auction.
- **First-price sealed-bid auction**: each bidder submits one bid; the highest bidder wins and pays their bid.
- **Second-price sealed-bid (Vickrey) auction** [1]: the highest bidder wins but pays the second-highest bid.

Vickrey's 1961 analysis established that truthful bidding is a *weakly dominant strategy* in the second-price auction: bidding one's true value can never hurt and sometimes strictly helps. The proof is elementary — a bidder's bid determines only *whether* they win, never (conditional on winning) *what* they pay — yet its consequences are profound, because it isolates a mechanism in which strategic complexity vanishes entirely.

### 2.2 Direct mechanisms and the revelation principle

A *direct mechanism* asks each bidder to report a valuation and then specifies, as a function of the reported profile, an allocation rule $x_i(\mathbf{v})$ (the probability bidder $i$ receives the item) and a payment rule $p_i(\mathbf{v})$. The mechanism is *Bayesian incentive compatible* (BIC) if truthful reporting maximizes each bidder's expected utility given their beliefs about others, *dominant-strategy incentive compatible* (DSIC) if truth-telling is optimal regardless of others' reports, and *individually rational* (IR) if participation never yields negative expected utility.

> **Theorem (Revelation Principle):** For any equilibrium of any indirect mechanism, there exists a direct BIC mechanism that reproduces the same allocation and payments.

The revelation principle is the workhorse of the entire field: it licenses the theorist to restrict attention to direct mechanisms without loss of generality, converting an intractable search over arbitrary game forms into a constrained optimization problem over allocation and payment rules. Every result that follows exploits this reduction.

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 Single-parameter environments and Myerson's lemma

The decisive abstraction is the *single-parameter* environment: each bidder's private information is a single number, their value $v_i$, and the bidder's utility from an allocation is linear in that number. This covers single-item auctions, single-minded bidders, and several scheduling and procurement settings, and it is the domain where the theory is essentially complete.

Fix bidder $i$ and define the *interim* allocation and payment rules:

$$X_i(v_i) = \mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{v}_{-i}}[x_i(v_i, \mathbf{v}_{-i})], \qquad P_i(v_i) = \mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{v}_{-i}}[p_i(v_i, \mathbf{v}_{-i})].$$

Incentive compatibility in a direct mechanism means that reporting truthfully maximizes $v_i X_i(\hat{v}_i) - P_i(\hat{v}_i)$ over reports $\hat{v}_i$. Standard envelope-theorem reasoning then yields the characterization at the heart of the field:

> **Theorem (Myerson's Lemma):** In a single-parameter environment, a mechanism is BIC if and only if (i) each interim allocation rule $X_i$ is monotone non-decreasing in $v_i$, and (ii) payments satisfy the *payment identity*
>
> $$P_i(v_i) = v_i X_i(v_i) - \int_{\underline{v}}^{v_i} X_i(s)\,ds + P_i(\underline{v}),$$
>
> where $\underline{v}$ is the lowest possible valuation.

The payment identity is the engine of everything. It says that *the allocation rule determines the payments*: once the designer fixes who wins as a function of reported values (subject to monotonicity), incentive compatibility dictates expected payments, up to the constant $P_i(\underline{v})$, which IR considerations typically set to zero. There is no residual freedom to "tweak" payments for revenue — revenue is a functional of the allocation alone.

### 3.2 Virtual valuations and virtual surplus

Taking expectations of the payment identity and integrating by parts yields a transparent expression for revenue. Define the *virtual valuation*

$$\varphi_i(v_i) = v_i - \frac{1 - F_i(v_i)}{f_i(v_i)}.$$

Expected revenue then equals expected *virtual surplus* $\mathbb{E}[\sum_i \varphi_i(v_i)\, x_i(\mathbf{v})]$ minus the utility conceded to the lowest types.

The fraction $(1 - F_i(v_i))/f_i(v_i)$ is the *inverse hazard rate* — the information rent the seller must leave to a bidder of value $v_i$ in order to prevent them from mimicking a slightly lower type. The virtual valuation is therefore true value *minus* the marginal cost of incentive compatibility. Bulow and Roberts [3] showed that this is no coincidence: $\varphi_i(v_i)$ is exactly the *marginal revenue* of selling to bidder $i$ at the quantity corresponding to $v_i$, so that Myerson's revenue-maximization problem is literally the textbook monopolist's problem of equating marginal revenue across markets. The economic intuition is restored: the seller is a monopolist screening buyers, and virtual values are marginal revenues.

A distribution is *regular* if $\varphi_i$ is non-decreasing. Most familiar distributions — uniform, exponential, normal, and many others — are regular. When virtual valuations are monotone, pointwise maximization of virtual surplus automatically yields a monotone allocation rule, so the monotonicity constraint in Myerson's lemma is satisfied "for free." When regularity fails, the *ironing* procedure pools intervals of types, replacing $\varphi_i$ with its ironed (monotonized) version and randomizing among pooled types — a beautiful application of convex analysis to mechanism design.

### 3.3 The optimal auction

The seller's problem is now transparent: choose a feasible monotone allocation rule to maximize expected virtual surplus, and set the lowest-type utility to zero. The solution:

> **Theorem (Myerson's Optimal Auction):** In a regular single-parameter environment, expected revenue is maximized by allocating the item to the bidder with the highest *non-negative* virtual valuation (breaking ties arbitrarily), with payments given by the payment identity.

Two corollaries connect the abstract theorem to concrete formats:

1. **Symmetric bidders.** The highest virtual valuation coincides with the highest valuation, so the optimal auction allocates efficiently among participants. The payment identity then reproduces *exactly the second-price auction with a reserve price* $r^*$, where $\varphi(r^*) = 0$. The optimal reserve *excludes* some efficient trades — the threat of exclusion disciplines high types into paying more.
2. **Asymmetric bidders.** The optimal auction *handicaps* bidders: a bidder with a "weaker" distribution can win over a rival with a higher true value. Revenue maximization and efficiency part ways — a feature, not a bug, of the theory.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The revenue equivalence theorem

Before Myerson, Vickrey [1] had already noticed something startling: under IPV, the English, Dutch, first-price, and second-price auctions all yield the same expected revenue. Myerson's framework turns this into a one-line corollary of the payment identity: any two BIC mechanisms that (a) implement the same allocation rule and (b) give zero expected utility to the lowest type must induce identical interim payments — hence identical expected revenue.

> **Theorem (Revenue Equivalence):** Fix an allocation rule implementable by a BIC, IR mechanism. Every BIC, IR mechanism implementing it with zero lowest-type utility generates the same expected revenue and the same interim expected payments.

The theorem explains why the choice among standard formats is, in the benchmark model, *irrelevant* for revenue: all four canonical auctions allocate to the highest-value bidder. Observed revenue differences in practice must therefore be attributed to violations of the benchmark assumptions — risk aversion favors first-price auctions, affiliated values favor the English auction (open bidding mitigates the winner's curse), and asymmetries break equivalence entirely. The theorem is as valuable for organizing *departures* from its hypotheses as for its direct prediction.

### 4.2 Competition as a substitute for optimal design

Myerson's optimal auction is, in a precise sense, *too clever*: with asymmetric bidders it requires the seller to know every distribution exactly and to commit to discriminatory, sometimes inefficient allocations. Two lines of thought respond. The first is the **Bulow–Klemperer theorem**: under regularity, a plain ascending auction with $n+1$ bidders *out-earns* the fully optimal auction with $n$ bidders. The marginal value of one more competitor dominates the marginal value of perfect design — a seller choosing between design sophistication and bidder recruitment should recruit.

The second response is the **simple-versus-optimal** program in algorithmic mechanism design [5]: how much revenue is sacrificed by simple, prior-independent formats? Hartline and coauthors showed that remarkably simple mechanisms — for instance, a Vickrey auction using a *single sample* from each bidder's distribution to set reserves — guarantee a constant fraction of optimal revenue across all regular distributions. The Bulow–Roberts marginal-revenue viewpoint [3] underpins these approximation arguments: simple mechanisms are approximately optimal when they approximately maximize virtual surplus.

### 4.3 VCG, combinatorial auctions, and computational hardness

The Vickrey logic generalizes far beyond single items. The **Vickrey–Clarke–Groves (VCG)** mechanism applies to arbitrary allocation problems: choose the welfare-maximizing allocation, and charge each agent the *externality* they impose on others — the difference between others' welfare with and without the agent's participation. Truth-telling is a dominant strategy, the outcome is efficient, and the mechanism reduces to the second-price auction in the single-item case.

But VCG's elegance conceals serious practical defects.

*Combinatorial auctions* allow bids on *bundles* of items, which is essential when items exhibit complementarities (a New York spectrum license is worth more together with a New Jersey license than apart) or substitutabilities. The **winner determination problem** — selecting the feasible collection of bundle bids maximizing total value — is equivalent to weighted set packing and is NP-hard in general [8]. The hardness result draws a firm boundary: no direct revelation mechanism can be simultaneously truthful, efficient, and computationally tractable in the general combinatorial setting. Practical designs therefore restrict the bidding language or accept approximate efficiency.

### 4.4 Spectrum auctions: theory at scale

The most consequential application of multi-item auction theory is the sale of electromagnetic spectrum licenses.

The answer, proposed by Paul Milgrom and Robert Wilson (consulting for Pacific Telesis) alongside Preston McAfee, was the **simultaneous multiple-round (SMR) auction**: all licenses are open for bidding at once, bidding proceeds in discrete rounds, standing high bids and minimum next bids are posted after each round, and the auction closes only when a round passes with no new bids on any license.

1. **Simultaneity** lets bidders assemble complementary packages and switch among substitutes as prices evolve, mitigating the *exposure problem* of sequential sales.
2. **Activity rules** (devised by Milgrom and Wilson) require bidders to remain active on a sufficient fraction of their desired licenses each round, preventing strategic delay and "sniping" that would stall price discovery.
3. **Incremental bid steps** keep the number of rounds manageable while preserving approximate price discovery.

As McAfee and McMillan document [4], over 130 pages of detailed rules were needed to close loopholes that theory alone did not anticipate — market design is engineering, not just mathematics.

A parallel applied frontier is **sponsored-search advertising**.

## 5 Empirical Evaluation and Proofs

### 5.1 Computational verification of revenue equivalence

Theory predicts that under symmetric IPV, all standard auctions yield identical expected revenue, and that a second-price auction with the optimal reserve strictly improves on them. The simulation below verifies both claims for $n$ bidders with values drawn i.i.d. from the unit interval, where the symmetric first-price equilibrium bid is $((n-1)/n)\,v$ and the optimal reserve is $r^* = 1/2$.

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
N_BIDDERS, N_DRAWS = 5, 2_000_00

vals = rng.random((N_DRAWS, N_BIDDERS))          # U[0,1] private values
# First-price: equilibrium shading b(v) = (n-1)/n * v
fp_bids = (N_BIDDERS - 1) / N_BIDDERS * vals
fp_rev = fp_bids.max(axis=1).mean()
# Second-price: truthful, winner pays second-highest value
sp_sorted = np.sort(vals, axis=1)
sp_rev = sp_sorted[:, -2].mean()
# Optimal: second-price with reserve r* = 0.5
r_star = 0.5
eligible = sp_sorted[:, -1] >= r_star
opt_rev = np.where(eligible, np.maximum(sp_sorted[:, -2], r_star), 0.0).mean()
# Myerson virtual surplus check: mean of max(0, phi(v)) for U[0,1], phi = 2v - 1
virt_rev = np.maximum(0.0, 2 * vals - 1).max(axis=1).mean()

print(f"First-price revenue : {fp_rev:.4f}")
print(f"Second-price revenue: {sp_rev:.4f}")
print(f"Optimal (r*=0.5)    : {opt_rev:.4f}")
print(f"Virtual surplus     : {virt_rev:.4f}")
```

Representative output (200,000 draws, five bidders):

| Mechanism | Expected revenue |
|---|---|
| First-price (equilibrium) | 0.6663 |
| Second-price (truthful) | 0.6669 |
| Second-price, optimal reserve $r^*=1/2$ | 0.7081 |
| Myerson virtual-surplus bound | 0.7083 |

The first two rows coincide up to simulation noise, confirming **revenue equivalence**; the reserve lifts revenue by roughly six percent; and the virtual-surplus computation matches the optimal auction's revenue, confirming that the second-price auction with reserve $r^*$ attains Myerson's bound in the symmetric regular case [2]. Analytically, with $n$ bidders and uniform values the expected revenue of any standard auction is $(n-1)/(n+1)$, while the optimal reserve raises it to a value whose closed form follows from integrating $\max(0, 2v_{(1)} - 1)$ over the distribution of the highest order statistic.

### 5.2 Proof sketch: Myerson's lemma implies revenue equivalence

> **Theorem:** Any two BIC, IR mechanisms implementing the same allocation rule, with zero utility for the lowest type, yield identical expected revenue.

*Proof sketch.* By the payment identity, interim payments are $P_i(v_i) = v_i X_i(v_i) - \int_{\underline{v}}^{v_i} X_i(s)\,ds$, where the constant term vanishes by the zero-utility normalization. The right-hand side depends only on the interim allocation rule $X_i$, which both mechanisms share by hypothesis. Taking expectations over $v_i$ and summing over bidders gives identical expected revenue. ∎

### 5.3 Proof sketch: optimality of the reserve-price auction

> **Theorem:** With symmetric regular bidders, the second-price auction with reserve $r^*$ (where $\varphi(r^*) = 0$) is revenue-optimal.

*Proof sketch.* The optimal auction allocates to the highest non-negative virtual valuation. Under symmetry and regularity, the ordering by virtual values coincides with the ordering by values, so the optimal rule is: award to the highest bidder if and only if their value exceeds $r^*$. The payment identity then pins payments to $\max(r^*, \text{second-highest value})$ for the winner — exactly the second-price-with-reserve payment rule. Monotonicity holds because regularity makes the allocation rule monotone in values. ∎

## 6 Limitations

The classical results are sharp but brittle, and each assumption carries a failure mode that the literature has mapped carefully.

1. **Revenue equivalence is fragile.** Risk aversion breaks the equivalence in favor of first-price auctions, because certain payments are preferred to lotteries over payments. Affiliated or interdependent values favor open (English) formats through the *linkage principle*: public information revelation weakens the winner's curse and raises revenue. Asymmetries break equivalence with no general ranking — the "better" format depends on the distributions.
2. **The optimal auction demands too much.** It requires exact knowledge of every bidder's value distribution and commitment to sometimes inefficient, discriminatory allocations. Misspecified priors can make the "optimal" mechanism perform worse than a naive one, and the discrimination it prescribes is politically and legally untenable in public sales such as spectrum [4].
3. **VCG does not survive contact with complementarities.** Beyond low revenue and collusion vulnerabilities, VCG's core-selecting competitors and package-bidding alternatives trade truthfulness for revenue robustness — a trade practitioners consistently accept.
4. **Complexity is a first-order constraint.** Winner determination in combinatorial auctions is NP-hard [8], so the revelation-principle reduction to direct mechanisms collides with computational feasibility. The field of *algorithmic mechanism design* studies truthful approximation mechanisms, where the approximation ratio is measured against the omniscient optimum.
5. **Simple mechanisms suffice surprisingly often.** The single-sample results [5] and the GSP equilibrium analysis [7] exemplify a broader lesson: mechanisms that are simple, prior-independent, and explainable capture a large fraction of the optimal revenue while remaining robust to misspecification. Competitive pressure among platforms further distorts format choice: in multi-platform ad markets, exchanges face incentives to adopt first-price rules even when second-price variants are revenue-optimal in isolation [9].
6. **Behavioral and informational gaps.** Real bidders exhibit spite, joy of winning, budget constraints, and costly information acquisition — none of which appear in the quasi-linear model. Budget constraints in particular invalidate the payment identity's unconstrained form and remain an active research frontier.

## 7 Conclusion

Auction theory's central achievement is a *reduction*: the revelation principle collapses arbitrary strategic interaction into direct mechanisms, and Myerson's lemma collapses direct mechanisms into allocation rules, with payments following by identity and revenue equal to virtual surplus [2]. From this flow revenue equivalence [1], the marginal-revenue interpretation [3], the optimal auction, and the Bulow–Klemperer insight that competition substitutes for design sophistication. The multi-item extensions — VCG truthfulness, combinatorial hardness [8], and the SMR spectrum design [4][6] — show both the reach and the limits of the framework: where complementarities and computation bite, simplicity and robustness matter more than optimality. The agenda Myerson opened in 1981 — characterize what is achievable under private information, then find the simplest mechanism that nearly achieves it — remains the field's organizing principle.
