# Earliest Eligible Virtual Deadline First: The Linux EEVDF Scheduler, Latency-Nice, and the sched_ext Framework for BPF-Programmable CPU Scheduling

## Abstract

This thesis examines the two most consequential changes to Linux CPU scheduling in a decade: the replacement of the Completely Fair Scheduler's selection discipline with Earliest Eligible Virtual Deadline First (EEVDF), merged in Linux 6.6, and the introduction of `sched_ext`, a BPF-programmable scheduling class merged in Linux 6.12. EEVDF, first described by Stoica and Abdel-Wahab in 1995, recasts proportional-share scheduling as a lag-bounded virtual-deadline discipline: each entity carries a lag value measuring deviation from ideal weighted service, only entities with non-negative lag are *eligible*, and the eligible entity with the earliest virtual deadline is dispatched. This yields stronger fairness bounds than vruntime-ordered CFS and a principled latency knob — *latency-nice* — that prioritizes short-slice tasks without abandoning fairness. `sched_ext` exposes enqueue, dispatch, and load-balancing decisions to verified BPF programs with automatic fallback to the in-kernel policy. We formalize EEVDF's eligibility and deadline mechanics, analyze the latency-nice interface, dissect the sched_ext ops table and dispatch-queue model, and survey reported empirical results, including learning-based BPF schedulers and the contested cost of strict eligibility.

## 1 Introduction

CPU scheduling in a general-purpose operating system must reconcile objectives that are in genuine tension: *fairness* (no task starved), *latency* (interactive tasks respond promptly), *throughput* (batch workloads progress efficiently), and *programmability* (administrators can encode domain knowledge). For nearly two decades, Linux addressed these objectives with a single monolithic answer: the Completely Fair Scheduler (CFS), introduced by Ingo Molnár in 2007, which orders runnable entities in a red–black tree by *virtual runtime* (vruntime) and always dispatches the leftmost, least-served entity. CFS was a landmark: it replaced heuristic timeslice accounting with a fluid-model approximation of Generalized Processor Sharing, and its tunables (`sched_latency_ns`, `sched_min_granularity_ns`) became the de facto levers of interactive performance.

Yet CFS's vruntime discipline has a structural blind spot. Vruntime ordering guarantees long-run proportional fairness but says nothing about *when* a task receives service within a scheduling horizon. A latency-sensitive task that wakes after a long sleep can find itself behind a wall of vruntime-identical competitors, and the task's only recourse is the blunt instrument of wakeup preemption. The result, documented across years of scheduler mailing-list discussion, is a pattern of ad hoc interactivity fixes layered onto a fairness model that was never designed to reason about deadlines.

Two coordinated developments have now broken this stalemate. First, Peter Zijlstra's 2023 proposal to replace CFS's selection discipline with **EEVDF — Earliest Eligible Virtual Deadline First** — was merged for Linux 6.6 [2][3]. EEVDF keeps the vruntime machinery but adds two concepts borrowed from packet fair queueing: *lag*, the signed deviation between a task's ideal and actual service, and *eligibility*, the requirement that lag be non-negative before a task may be selected. Selection then proceeds by earliest *virtual deadline*, which naturally prioritizes tasks requesting short slices — precisely the latency-sensitive ones. Second, Tejun Heo and David Vernet's **sched_ext** framework, merged for Linux 6.12, inserts a new scheduling class whose decisions are implemented in BPF, with verified safety and automatic fallback to the built-in policy [5][6]. Together, these changes move Linux from *one scheduler fits all* to a regime where fairness has deadline semantics and policy itself is programmable.

This thesis makes three contributions. First, it gives a precise, self-contained account of EEVDF's eligibility and virtual-deadline mechanics, including the lag-decay rule for sleeping tasks and the latency-nice interface. Second, it dissects sched_ext's architecture — the ops table, dispatch queues, and the safety envelope that makes BPF scheduling deployable. Third, it surveys the empirical record: reported tail-latency improvements, the controversial eligibility trade-off, and early BPF schedulers such as latency-aware and machine-learning-driven variants.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 From GPS to packet fair queueing

The theoretical ancestor of modern fair schedulers is *Generalized Processor Sharing* (GPS), the fluid model in which each backlogged flow *i* receives service at rate proportional to its weight *wᵢ*. Real systems cannot serve infinitesimally; packet fair-queueing algorithms *emulate* GPS. Weighted Fair Queueing (WFQ, Demers–Keshav–Shenker, 1989) tracks per-flow virtual time and serves the packet with the smallest virtual finish time. Stoica and Abdel-Wahab's **EEVDF** (1995) generalized this idea by introducing an explicit *eligibility* condition: a flow is eligible only when its *lag* — the difference between the service it would have received under GPS and the service it actually received — is non-negative, and among eligible flows the one with the earliest *virtual deadline* is served [1]. The elegance of EEVDF is that eligibility bounds how far any flow can fall behind, while deadlines order service to meet latency objectives; the same mechanism that enforces fairness also expresses urgency.

> **Theorem:** (Stoica–Abdel-Wahab lag bound, paraphrased). For a set of backlogged flows scheduled by EEVDF, the lag of every flow is bounded at all times: no flow can receive more than a bounded amount of excess service, and no eligible flow is delayed beyond the virtual-deadline ordering. In particular, over any interval in which a flow is continuously backlogged, the difference between its GPS-ideal service and its actual service is bounded by a constant depending only on the maximum packet (quantum) size, independent of the number of competing flows [1].

### 2.2 CFS: vruntime and its limits

CFS implements weighted fair queueing over tasks rather than packets. Each scheduling entity accumulates vruntime, defined as actual runtime scaled inversely by weight:

$$\text{vruntime}_i(t) = \text{vruntime}_i(0) + \int_0^t \frac{W_0}{w_i(\tau)}\, d\tau$$

where *W₀* is the weight of nice-0. Entities are ordered in a red–black tree keyed by vruntime; `pick_next_task_fair()` selects the leftmost. Newly woken tasks are placed at `min_vruntime` (with a small initial bonus), and preemption is driven by wakeup granularity checks. This design is simple, provably fair in the long run, and cheap. Its weakness is *temporal*: nothing in the vruntime ordering encodes when service should occur. Interactive tasks under load can suffer wakeup delays because the tree cannot distinguish "owed service that is urgent" from "owed service that can wait." Zijlstra's EEVDF proposal explicitly targeted this gap, motivated in part by reports that CFS's interactivity under mixed workloads had regressed relative to expectations [3][4].

### 2.3 The road to programmable scheduling

Parallel to the EEVDF work, a separate line of development asked why the scheduling *policy* must be fixed in the kernel at all. The BPF verifier changes the calculus: because BPF programs are statically verified for termination and memory safety, a scheduling policy expressed in BPF can be loaded at runtime without trusting its author. Heo's sched_ext proposal (first posted late 2022) exploits exactly this — the kernel retains mechanism while BPF supplies policy [5][6].

## 3 Methodology

This thesis is a synthesis and analysis study. Our method has four components:

1. **Primary-source analysis.** We ground the EEVDF description in the kernel documentation (`Documentation/scheduler/sched-eevdf.rst`) [2], the EEVDF and latency-nice patch series on the kernel mailing lists [4], and the sched_ext core documentation and header definitions (`include/linux/sched/ext.h`, `kernel/sched/ext.c`) [6]. Claims about interfaces (flag names, ranges, file paths) are drawn from these sources, not from secondary summaries.
2. **Literature triangulation.** We cross-check kernel behavior against LWN's contemporaneous reporting on both the EEVDF merge [3] and the sched_ext merge trajectory [5][7], which capture design rationale and community debate absent from the code.
3. **Analytical reconstruction.** We restate EEVDF's eligibility and deadline rules in the notation of the original 1995 paper [1] and show how the kernel's vruntime-based implementation approximates them, making the fairness–latency trade-off explicit.
4. **Empirical survey.** We collect reported benchmark outcomes — tail-latency measurements on the scheduler lists, the EEVDF-vs-CFS latency comparisons circulated during review [4], and published results from BPF scheduler experiments [8] — and assess them with attention to methodology and generality, rather than presenting new measurements.

We do not claim hard real-time properties for either mechanism: EEVDF deadlines are *soft* (no admission control), and sched_ext schedulers are best-effort policies with a safety fallback.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Lag, eligibility, and the virtual deadline

The conceptual core of EEVDF is the *lag* of a scheduling entity. Let *Sᵢ(t₁, t₂)* be the service flow *i* would receive under ideal weighted fair queueing over *[t₁, t₂]*, and *sᵢ(t₁, t₂)* the service it actually received. The lag is

$$\text{lag}_i(t) = S_i(0,t) - s_i(0,t).$$

Positive lag means the entity is *owed* service; negative lag means it has *consumed* service beyond its fair share. EEVDF's first rule is **eligibility**: only entities with `lag ≥ 0` may be selected. This is a strict strengthening of CFS, where every runnable entity is always selectable. Eligibility is what bounds unfairness: a task that has over-consumed is simply not a candidate until the virtual clock catches up.

The second rule is the **virtual deadline**. When an entity becomes eligible, it is assigned a virtual deadline computed from the current virtual time plus a term proportional to the slice it requests and inversely proportional to its weight:

$$\text{VD}_i = V(t) + \frac{q_i}{w_i}\cdot W_0,$$

where *qᵢ* is the entity's requested time quantum and *V(t)* the system virtual time. The scheduler dispatches the eligible entity with the smallest VD. The crucial consequence: **a task requesting a short slice receives an early deadline and is therefore prioritized**, even against heavier tasks that are equally "owed" service. Latency sensitivity falls out of the fairness mechanism itself rather than being bolted on via wakeup heuristics.

In the kernel implementation, this machinery is layered onto the existing vruntime infrastructure rather than replacing it. Entities retain `vruntime`; the runqueue tree is ordered to reflect deadlines of eligible entities, and `update_entity_lag()` maintains the lag value as service accrues. The following pseudocode captures the selection discipline (simplified from `kernel/sched/fair.c`):

```c
/* Simplified EEVDF selection discipline (cf. kernel/sched/fair.c) */
struct sched_entity *pick_eevdf(struct cfs_rq *cfs_rq)
{
	struct sched_entity *se, *best = NULL;
	u64 earliest_deadline = U64_MAX;

	/* rbtree ordered by (eligible) virtual deadline */
	rbtree_postorder_for_each_entry(se, &cfs_rq->tasks_timeline, run_node) {
		if (entity_lag(se) < 0)
			continue;              /* not eligible: over-consumed */
		if (se->deadline < earliest_deadline) {
			earliest_deadline = se->deadline;
			best = se;
		}
	}
	return best; /* NULL only if every entity has negative lag */
}
```

Two subtleties deserve emphasis. First, **lag decay for sleeping tasks**. A task that sleeps with negative lag (it over-consumed before sleeping) would, under a naive rule, wake ineligible and be penalized for past behavior indefinitely. The kernel instead applies a *decay*: a sleeping task remains logically on the runqueue (deferred dequeue) while its lag decays as a function of advancing virtual time, so long sleepers eventually reset toward eligibility [2]. This closes an obvious gaming vector — briefly sleeping to shed negative lag — because the decay is paced by the virtual clock, not by the act of sleeping.

Second, **preemption is deadline-driven**. A newly eligible entity whose virtual deadline is earlier than the current task's triggers preemption, which is why short-slice interactive tasks see improved wakeup behavior: their deadlines are *structurally* early, not merely boosted by a heuristic.

| Dimension | CFS (vruntime) | EEVDF (lag + deadline) |
|---|---|---|
| Selection key | Minimum vruntime | Earliest virtual deadline among eligible |
| Eligibility | All runnable entities | Only `lag ≥ 0` |
| Latency mechanism | Wakeup preemption heuristics, granularity tunables | Short slices ⇒ early deadlines (structural) |
| Sleep handling | Placement at `min_vruntime` | Lag decay via deferred dequeue |
| Per-task latency knob | None (nice only affects weight) | Latency-nice + requested slice |
| Fairness bound | Long-run proportional | Bounded lag (per-interval) [1] |

### 4.2 Latency-nice and per-task slice control

EEVDF's deadline rule creates a new degree of freedom: if a task can request a *shorter slice*, it earns an *earlier deadline*. The kernel exposes this through **latency-nice**, a per-task attribute in the range **[-20, 19]** — mirroring the familiar nice range — settable via the extended `sched_setattr(2)` interface and, for groups, through the cgroup `cpu.latency.nice` file [4]. A negative latency-nice value marks a task as latency-sensitive: its requested slice shrinks and its virtual deadlines are correspondingly rushed. A positive value does the reverse, voluntarily deferring the task in exchange for longer, more cache-friendly quanta.

```c
/* Requesting latency-sensitive scheduling (conceptual) */
struct sched_attr attr = {
	.size = sizeof(attr),
	.sched_policy = SCHED_NORMAL,
	/* latency-nice in [-20, 19]: negative => latency-sensitive */
	.sched_latency_nice = -10,   /* via extended sched_attr */
};
sched_setattr(0 /* self */, &attr, 0);
```

The EEVDF series additionally allows tasks to request a *specific* time slice through the same `sched_setattr()` interface, giving latency-critical applications (audio pipelines, game loops, input handlers) direct control over the *qᵢ* term in their deadline computation [2]. This is a qualitative change from CFS, where the only per-task lever was weight: weight changes *how much* CPU a task gets, while latency-nice and slice requests change *when* it gets it, without disturbing the proportional-share invariant. The cgroup interface (`cpu.latency.nice`) further lets administrators bound the latency-nice of a group, preventing a single aggressive task from monopolizing early deadlines within a shared hierarchy [4].

### 4.3 The sched_ext architecture: mechanism in the kernel, policy in BPF

sched_ext introduces a new scheduling class positioned in the class hierarchy as `stop > deadline > rt > ext > fair > idle`, i.e., above the fair (EEVDF/CFS) class but below realtime [6]. A BPF program implementing a scheduler is loaded from userspace and registers a `struct sched_ext_ops` table of callbacks. The kernel core (`kernel/sched/ext.c`) retains all mechanism — context switching, migration, timers, watchdog — and invokes the BPF callbacks at decision points. If no BPF scheduler is loaded, tasks in the `SCHED_EXT` policy are scheduled as if `SCHED_NORMAL`; once a scheduler loads (without `SCX_OPS_SWITCH_PARTIAL`), it takes over all tasks below realtime priority [5].

The ops table is the architectural contract:

| Callback | Role |
|---|---|
| `select_cpu` | Choose a CPU for a waking task |
| `enqueue` | Task became runnable; assign to a dispatch queue |
| `dispatch` | Fill a CPU's local queue from dispatch queues |
| `running` / `stopping` | Task start/stop notifications (accounting) |
| `yield` | Task voluntarily yielded |
| `cpu_acquire` / `cpu_release` | CPU ownership transitions |
| `init` / `exit` | Scheduler lifecycle |

```c
/* Sketch of the sched_ext ops table (cf. include/linux/sched/ext.h) */
struct sched_ext_ops {
	int  (*select_cpu)(struct task_struct *p, s32 prev_cpu, u64 wake_flags);
	void (*enqueue)(struct task_struct *p, u64 enq_flags);
	void (*dispatch)(s32 cpu, struct task_struct *prev);
	void (*running)(struct task_struct *p);
	void (*stopping)(struct task_struct *p, bool runnable);
	void (*yield)(struct task_struct *p);
	/* ... cpu_acquire/release, init/exit, cgroup hooks ... */
	u64  timeout;   /* watchdog: max ns a task may wait */
	const char *name;
};
```

Dispatch is organized around **dispatch queues (DSQs)**. A BPF scheduler places tasks into DSQs with `scx_bpf_dispatch()` (FIFO) or `scx_bpf_dispatch_vtime()` (priority-ordered by a scheduler-chosen virtual time); built-in DSQs include the global `SCX_DSQ_GLOBAL` and per-CPU local DSQs consumed automatically [6]. The BPF program never manipulates runqueues directly; it only classifies tasks into queues, and the core drains them. The safety envelope has three layers: the BPF verifier guarantees the scheduler program itself cannot crash the kernel; a **watchdog** detects tasks stalled beyond the scheduler's declared timeout and aborts the BPF scheduler, falling back to the built-in fair class; and operator escape hatches (debug dumps, SysRq sequences) restore default scheduling even under pathological policies [6].

The example schedulers shipped in `tools/sched_ext/` demonstrate the range: `scx_simple` (minimal global FIFO), `scx_qmap` (multi-level feedback), `scx_lavd` (latency-aware, targeting consistent frame rates for games), `scx_bpfland` (minimizing response latency), `scx_rusty` (hybrid BPF/user-space), and `scx_layered`/`scx_p2dq` exploring topology-aware placement [7].

### 4.4 Writing BPF schedulers: a minimal example

A complete (if naive) BPF scheduler needs only two callbacks: enqueue tasks into the global DSQ and let the core dispatch them.

```bpf
/* Minimal sched_ext scheduler: global FIFO (cf. tools/sched_ext/) */
SEC("struct_ops/simple_ops")
struct sched_ext_ops simple_ops = {
	.enqueue  = (void *)simple_enqueue,
	.dispatch = (void *)simple_dispatch,
	.name     = "simple_fifo",
};

void BPF_STRUCT_OPS(simple_enqueue, struct task_struct *p, u64 enq_flags)
{
	/* FIFO into the global dispatch queue; core drains it. */
	scx_bpf_dispatch(p, SCX_DSQ_GLOBAL, SCX_SLICE_DFL, enq_flags);
}

void BPF_STRUCT_OPS(simple_dispatch, s32 cpu, struct task_struct *prev)
{
	/* No custom DSQ used: nothing to do; local+global DSQs
	 * are consumed automatically by the core. */
}
```

Real schedulers are more ambitious. `scx_lavd` computes per-task *latency criticality* from runtime statistics and biases dispatch order and CPU selection accordingly, reporting improved frame-time consistency for games on the Steam Deck [7]. A particularly striking demonstration of the framework's expressiveness is a learning-based sched_ext scheduler that performs load balancing with a small neural network evaluated in BPF (using fixed-point arithmetic, since floating point is forbidden in the kernel): on a kernel-compilation benchmark it reduced build time by **10%** versus EEVDF while cutting task migrations by **77%**, with the model trained in userspace, shipped to the kernel in a BPF map, and continuously re-trained on observed outcomes [8]. Whether or not one endorses ML in the scheduling hot path, the result proves the platform claim: policies that would previously have required a kernel fork can now be prototyped, measured, and deployed as signed BPF objects.

A Python-level model of the deadline computation builds intuition for how slice length reshapes ordering without touching weights:

```python
# Illustrative EEVDF deadline model (not kernel code)
W0 = 1024  # nice-0 weight

def virtual_deadline(vtime, requested_slice_ns, weight=W0):
    """VD = V + (q_i / w_i) * W0. Shorter slice -> earlier deadline."""
    return vtime + (requested_slice_ns * W0) // weight

tasks = [("batch", 6_000_000), ("audio", 750_000), ("game", 1_500_000)]
for name, q in sorted(tasks, key=lambda t: virtual_deadline(0, t[1])):
    print(f"{name:6s} slice={q/1e6:4.2f}ms -> VD={virtual_deadline(0, q)}")
# audio (0.75ms) < game (1.5ms) < batch (6ms): latency order from slices alone
```

## 5 Empirical Results and Formal Arguments

**Formal footing.** EEVDF's headline guarantee is the bounded-lag property of [1]: unlike vruntime ordering, which bounds only long-run proportions, eligibility ensures that at *every* instant no task's deviation from its ideal service exceeds a fixed bound. A second formal consequence concerns wakeup latency: because a waking interactive task with a short requested slice receives a deadline earlier than the incumbents' (whose deadlines were computed from longer slices), the task is *provably* dispatched ahead of any eligible competitor with a later deadline — the latency improvement is a theorem of the ordering, not an emergent tuning artifact.

> **Theorem:** (Deadline-ordered wakeup). In EEVDF, let task *p* wake at virtual time *V* with requested slice *q_p* and weight *w_p*, and let every currently eligible competitor *c* have virtual deadline VD_c ≥ *V + (q_c/w_c)·W₀*. If *q_p/w_p < min_c(q_c/w_c)* and *lag_p ≥ 0*, then *p* is dispatched before all such competitors. Hence, reducing a task's requested slice (e.g., via negative latency-nice) *monotonically* improves its worst-case dispatch precedence among eligible tasks.

**Reported measurements.** During EEVDF review, scheduler developers circulated latency comparisons that both motivated and complicated the merge. On a 2-core input-latency workload, CFS measured **168 ms** average latency; EEVDF *with* the eligibility check regressed to **206 ms**, while EEVDF *without* eligibility improved to **143 ms**, and EEVDF without eligibility plus a larger 6 ms base slice reached **104 ms** [4]. These numbers are workload-specific and were reported on a mailing list rather than in a controlled study, but they crystallize the central empirical tension: the eligibility check — the very mechanism that gives EEVDF its fairness bound — can *increase* context-switching and hurt latency on small systems, because ineligible tasks must wait even when the CPU would otherwise idle-switch cheaply. The merged kernel retains eligibility, and subsequent work (including fixes from reviewers) narrowed the gap, but the episode is a reminder that stronger invariants have measurable costs.

| Experiment (as reported) | Configuration | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Input latency, 2-core (lkml) [4] | CFS | 168 ms avg |
| Input latency, 2-core (lkml) [4] | EEVDF, eligibility on, 1.5 ms slice | 206 ms avg (regression) |
| Input latency, 2-core (lkml) [4] | EEVDF, no eligibility, 1.5 ms slice | 143 ms avg |
| Input latency, 2-core (lkml) [4] | EEVDF, no eligibility, 6 ms slice | 104 ms avg |
| Kernel build + ML load balancer (LWN) [8] | BPF scheduler vs EEVDF | −10% build time, −77% migrations |
| Gaming frame consistency (LWN) [7] | `scx_lavd` on Steam Deck-class HW | Improved frame-time stability (qualitative) |

For sched_ext, the empirical story is one of *existence proofs*: production experiments at Meta and Google, Valve's interest for Steam Deck game scheduling, and Ubuntu's consideration of shipping it [5] collectively demonstrate that BPF schedulers survive contact with real fleets. The framework's value proposition was never that a BPF scheduler beats EEVDF on every workload — it is that workload-specific policies can be *tried at all* without forking the kernel.

## 6 Limitations

**EEVDF's eligibility cost.** As the measurements above show, strict eligibility can increase preemption and context-switch rates on latency-sensitive small systems. The kernel community continues to debate refinements — eligibility relaxation heuristics and reviewer-proposed fixes — and the trade-off between the lag bound and dispatch agility remains incompletely characterized across topologies [4].

**Lag-decay gaming.** The deferred-dequeue decay rule paces lag forgiveness by the virtual clock, but adversarial or merely pathological workloads (e.g., tasks alternating sleep and burst in resonance with the decay rate) have not been exhaustively analyzed. The fairness bound of [1] assumes the packet-network model; its translation to the kernel's vruntime approximation, with hierarchical groups and bandwidth control, weakens the cleanest form of the guarantee.

**Soft deadlines only.** EEVDF's virtual deadlines carry no admission control and no runtime enforcement. Applications needing hard guarantees must still use `SCHED_DEADLINE`, whose constant-bandwidth-server semantics are a different mechanism entirely; conflating the two "deadline" schedulers is a common and consequential error.

**sched_ext's single global scheduler.** Only one BPF scheduler may be loaded system-wide; per-workload policies on shared machines remain future work, though sub-scheduler proposals (attaching schedulers to cgroups) are under active development [7].

**API instability.** The `scx_bpf_*` kfuncs and ops table carry *no stability guarantee* across kernel versions [6]. Scheduler authors must track the kernel, which limits sched_ext's appeal for out-of-tree distribution — a deliberate trade-off to keep the core evolvable.

**Verifier and overhead constraints.** BPF schedulers run in contexts with strict limits (no floating point, bounded loops, limited stack); expressive policies must be split across BPF and userspace (as in `scx_rusty`), adding IPC latency to the decision path. The dispatch path itself adds indirection relative to the in-kernel fair class.

---

## 7 Conclusion

EEVDF and sched_ext are complementary answers to the same question: how should an operating system schedule when workloads are too diverse for one policy? EEVDF strengthens the *default* policy, replacing vruntime ordering with a lag-bounded virtual-deadline discipline in which latency sensitivity is expressed structurally — through shorter requested slices and latency-nice — rather than through heuristics fighting the fairness model.

sched_ext, meanwhile, concedes that no default policy suffices and makes the *policy* a loadable, verifiable, fallible-safe artifact. Open problems — lag-decay robustness, per-cgroup sub-schedulers, API stabilization, and deadline-driven preemption across NUMA topologies — define the research frontier. The deeper lesson is architectural: by separating *eligibility* from *ordering* (EEVDF) and *mechanism* from *policy* (sched_ext), Linux has made its scheduler both more principled and more programmable than at any point in its history.
