# From Association to Intervention to Transport: Do-Calculus Identification, Selection Diagrams, and Double Machine Learning in Structural Causal Models

## Abstract

Causal questions — what would happen if we acted differently — lie beyond the reach of purely associational statistics, yet they are central to science, medicine, and policy. This thesis develops the full pipeline of modern causal inference grounded in structural causal models (SCMs). We begin with the semantics of intervention and the do-operator. We then give a precise account of Pearl's do-calculus, its three transformation rules, and the completeness results of Shpitser and Pearl that reduce identification of interventional distributions to a syntactic, algorithmic procedure. Next we treat transportability: how causal effects learned in a source population can be licensed in a target population via selection diagrams and transport formulas. Finally we address estimation, where identified functionals must be estimated from finite samples: we develop Neyman-orthogonal scores and the double/debiased machine learning (DML) framework of Chernozhukov et al., which restores root-n consistency and valid inference when nuisance functions are estimated by modern machine learning. **Keywords:** causal identification, do-calculus, transportability, Neyman orthogonality, double machine learning.

---

## 1. Introduction

Modern machine learning has achieved spectacular success at *prediction*: estimating conditional distributions P(y|x) from massive observational datasets. Yet the questions that matter most — *what will happen if we intervene?* — are not questions about P(y|x). As Pearl has emphasized, they concern the interventional distribution P(y|do(x)), a quantity that describes the world *after* the causal mechanism that sets X has been surgically replaced by an external intervention [2]. The gap between seeing and doing is the central chasm of causal inference.

Three intellectual traditions now form a coherent pipeline:

1. **Structural causal models (SCMs)** provide the formal semantics: a set of functional equations assigning each variable a mechanism given its parents, with exogenous noise. Interventions are modeled as graph surgery — deletion of edges into the manipulated variable [2].
2. **Identification theory** answers the crucial preliminary question: can the interventional query be computed at all from the data we can observe? Do-calculus supplies three symbolic rules that erase the do-operator when licensed by d-separation in mutilated graphs, and the Shpitser–Pearl completeness theorem shows these rules are *necessary and sufficient*: if they cannot identify a query, no method can [1][3].
3. **Transportability and estimation** carry the program to practice. Bareinboim and Pearl's transportability theory tells us when experiments from one population license conclusions in another [4][5][6]. And when identification succeeds, Chernozhukov et al.'s double/debiased machine learning delivers estimators that remain root-n consistent and asymptotically normal even when nuisance functions are estimated by regularized, data-adaptive learners [7][8].

This thesis unifies these strands. Our contributions are (i) a self-contained exposition of the do-calculus, the ID algorithm, and its completeness; (ii) a worked derivation of transport formulas from selection diagrams; (iii) a treatment of Neyman-orthogonal scores and cross-fitting that connects identification to honest inference; and (iv) a simulation demonstrating DML's debiasing in a partially linear model with confounding estimated by machine learning.

> **Thesis statement.** *Identification is a syntactic property of the causal graph and the query; estimation is a statistical problem governed by orthogonality; and transportability is an identification problem in an augmented graph. Confusing these layers is the root of most causal malpractice.*

## 2. Background

### 2.1 Structural causal models and the do-operator

A structural causal model M = ⟨U, V, F, P(U)⟩ consists of exogenous variables U with distribution P(U), endogenous variables V, and a set of structural equations F assigning each Vi ∈ V a deterministic function of its parents PAi (among U ∪ V) and its exogenous noise [2]. The model induces a directed graph G, and when G is acyclic (a DAG), the joint distribution factorizes as P(v) = Πi P(vi | pai).

An *intervention* do(X = x) replaces the equations for X with the constant assignment X = x, leaving all other mechanisms intact. Graphically this is surgery: delete all edges into X, producing the mutilated graph G_X̅. The interventional distribution P(y | do(x)) is the distribution of Y in the submodel Mx [2][3].

Pearl's **ladder of causation** organizes queries into three levels [3]:

| Level | Question | Notation | Example |
|-------|----------|----------|---------|
| Association | What is? | P(y \| x) | Symptom given test result |
| Intervention | What if we do? | P(y \| do(x)) | Cure given treatment |
| Counterfactual | What if we had? | P(yx \| x′, y′) | Outcome had treatment differed |

Each level is strictly more expressive than the one below: no amount of associational data can answer an interventional question without causal assumptions [2].

### 2.2 d-Separation

Identification arguments are mediated by **d-separation**, the graphical criterion for conditional independence in DAGs. Two sets of nodes are d-separated by Z if every path between them is blocked — by conditioning on a non-collider, or by *not* conditioning on a collider or its descendant [2].

### 2.3 Classical adjustment: back-door and front-door

The **back-door criterion** [2] states that if Z blocks every back-door path from X to Y (paths with an arrow into X) and contains no descendant of X, then P(y|do(x)) = Σz P(y|x, z) P(z). When no such set exists — e.g., unmeasured confounders — the **front-door criterion** may still identify the effect through a mediator M: P(y|do(x)) = Σm P(m|x) Σx′ P(y|x′, m) P(x′) [2][3]. These are both special cases of do-calculus, as we show next.

### 2.4 Neyman orthogonality (preview)

On the estimation side, we will need the notion of an *orthogonal moment*: a score ψ(W; θ, η) whose Gateaux derivative with respect to the nuisance function η vanishes at the truth. Neyman introduced this insensitivity to immunize estimates of a low-dimensional target θ against errors in estimating a high-dimensional nuisance η [7]. Orthogonality is what makes machine-learned nuisances compatible with valid inference.

## 3. Methodology

Our methodology is *syntactic and algorithmic*: we treat causal identification as a problem of provable formula transformation, and estimation as a problem of constructing orthogonal estimating equations.

### 3.1 The do-calculus

Let G_X̅ denote G with edges into X deleted, G_X̲ with edges out of X deleted, and Z(W) the Z-nodes that are not ancestors of W. The three rules of do-calculus are [1][3]:

> **Rule 1 — Insertion/deletion of observations.** P(y | do(x), z, w) = P(y | do(x), w) if (Y ⊥ Z | X, W) in G_X̅. Conditioning can be ignored when Z is d-separated from Y given X, W in the graph with X's incoming edges removed.

> **Rule 2 — Action/observation exchange.** P(y | do(x), do(z), w) = P(y | do(x), z, w) if (Y ⊥ Z | X, W) in G_X̅Z̲. An intervention on Z can be replaced by passive observation of Z when the corresponding d-separation holds in the graph with incoming edges to X and outgoing edges from Z removed.

> **Rule 3 — Insertion/deletion of actions.** P(y | do(x), do(z), w) = P(y | do(x), w) if (Y ⊥ Z | X, W) in G_X̅,Z(W)̅. An intervention on Z can be deleted entirely when Z is d-separated from Y given X, W in the graph where edges into X and into non-ancestors of W from Z are removed.

Together with ordinary probability calculus (marginalization, conditioning, Bayes' rule), these rules form a **complete** axiom system for interventional identification in DAG-structured SCMs [1][3]: if no sequence of rules reduces P(y|do(x)) to a do-free expression, then the effect is *not identifiable* from the observational distribution — no method can succeed.

### 3.2 The ID algorithm

Shpitser and Pearl operationalized completeness in the **ID algorithm**, which recursively decomposes the graph into *c-components* (confounded components: maximal sets connected by bidirected arcs, i.e., shared unmeasured confounders) and applies the rules systematically [1][3]. Tian and Pearl (2002) gave a sufficient graphical condition — every path from X to its children on paths to Y must trace an arrow out of a measured variable — and Shpitser and Pearl (2006) extended this to a necessary and sufficient condition for arbitrary conditional interventional queries and counterfactuals [3]. Modern algorithmic treatments unify these results [1].

### 3.3 Transportability via selection diagrams

When experiments were run in a source population π but the query concerns a target π*, we augment the causal diagram with **selection nodes** S: square nodes pointing at variables whose mechanisms or distributions may differ between populations [4][5][6]. Absence of an S-arrow encodes the *invariance* assumption that the mechanism is shared.

> **Theorem (Pearl–Bareinboim transportability [4]).** The causal relation R = P*(y | do(x), z) is transportable from π to π* if and only if the expression P(y | do(x), z, s) is reducible by do-calculus to an expression in which S appears only as a conditioning variable in do-free terms.

Bareinboim and Pearl (2013) gave a complete graphical algorithm that decides transportability and *synthesizes the transport formula* — the precise recipe for combining experimental and observational data across populations [5]. For instance, if the populations differ only in the distribution of a covariate Z (S → Z), the formula is P*(y | do(x)) = Σz P(y | do(x), z) P*(z), combining source experiments with target covariate data [4].

### 3.4 Double machine learning

Identification yields a functional of the observational distribution; *estimation* must then contend with nuisance functions. The DML framework [7][8] rests on two ingredients:

1. **Neyman-orthogonal scores.** Estimate θ0 through a moment condition E[ψ(W; θ0, η0)] = 0 whose derivative with respect to η vanishes at η0. In the partially linear model Y = Dθ0 + g0(X) + U with D = m0(X) + V, the orthogonal score is ψ(W; θ, η) = (Y − ℓ(X) − θ(D − m(X)))(D − m(X)), where η = (ℓ, m) [7]. First-order errors in estimating η then do not propagate to θ.
2. **Cross-fitting.** Split the sample into K folds; estimate η on K−1 folds and evaluate the score on the held-out fold, then rotate. This removes the overfitting bias that arises when the same data estimate nuisances and the target, without sacrificing efficiency [7].

Under the mild requirement that nuisance estimators converge at o(n^{−1/4}) — achievable by many ML methods — the DML estimator is root-n consistent, asymptotically normal, and semiparametrically efficient [7].

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Worked derivation: the front-door via do-calculus

Consider the graph X → M → Y with an unmeasured confounder between X and Y (a bidirected arc X ↔ Y). The back-door criterion fails (no observed set blocks the back-door path). Do-calculus derives the front-door formula [2][3]:

1. P(y | do(x)) = Σm P(y | do(x), m) P(m | do(x))  *(probability calculus)*
2. P(m | do(x)) = P(m | x)  *(Rule 2: (M ⊥ X) in G_X̲, no back-door into M from X since the only path X ← U → Y → … does not reach M)*
3. P(y | do(x), m) = P(y | do(m))  *(Rule 3: delete do(x) since (Y ⊥ X | M) in G_X̅,M(W)̅ — all paths from X to Y given M pass through the collider-free chain via M which is conditioned)*
4. P(y | do(m)) = Σx′ P(y | x′, m) P(x′)  *(Rule 2 then Rule 3: back-door from M to Y via X blocked by conditioning on X; then do(x′) deleted since X ⊥ Y given M, X in the mutilated graph)*

Assembling: **P(y | do(x)) = Σm P(m | x) Σx′ P(y | x′, m) P(x′)** — the front-door formula, derived purely syntactically [2].

### 4.2 Completeness and the anatomy of non-identifiability: hedges

Completeness means failure is *informative*. The ID algorithm fails exactly when the graph contains a **hedge** — a pair of nested forests (F, F′) forming c-components where F′ ⊂ F, F′ shares its root set with F, and X intersects F while Y is downstream [1][3]. The canonical example is the **bow arc**: X → Y with a bidirected arc X ↔ Y. Here P(y | do(x)) is not identifiable: two SCMs can agree on all of P(x, y) yet disagree on the interventional distribution, because the observed association is an inseparable mixture of the causal effect and confounding [1][3].

```
Haskell sketch of the hedge test (conceptual):

data DAG = DAG { nodes :: [String], edges :: [(String,String)], bidirected :: [(String,String)] }

cComponents :: DAG -> [[String]]

hasHedge g x y =
  any (\(f, f') -> f' `isSubsetOf` f && x `elem` f && y `elem` descendants g f)
      [(f, f') | f <- forests g, f' <- forests g, isCComponent f', isCComponent f]
```

### 4.3 Transportability in practice: selection diagrams and transport formulas

Transportability generalizes identification: the query is P(y | do(x), s*) where S indicates population membership. The analyst's job is to (a) draw the selection diagram encoding which mechanisms may differ, (b) run the transportability algorithm, and (c) execute the synthesized formula [5][6].

Three canonical cases (Pearl & Bareinboim [4]):

- **(a) Differing covariate distributions**, S → Z with X → Z? No — S → Z, Z → Y: P*(y|do(x)) = Σz P(y|do(x),z) P*(z). Reweight source experiments by target covariates.
- **(b) Differing mechanisms upstream of X**, S → X: P*(y|do(x)) = P(y|do(x)). The intervention severs the differing mechanism entirely — direct transport.
- **(c) Differing Z-mechanism**, S → Z with X → Z: P*(y|do(x)) = Σz P(y|do(x),z) P*(z|x). The target's conditional distribution of Z given X must be measured.

### 4.4 From identification to honest estimation: orthogonality and cross-fitting

Identification tells us *what* to estimate; orthogonality tells us *how* to estimate it without being fooled by machine learning's regularization bias. Naive plug-in — estimating ĝ by lasso/random forests and regressing Y − ĝ(X) on D — inherits the regularization bias of ĝ at a rate that destroys root-n consistency [7].

The partially linear model makes the mechanism transparent. Write the two nuisance functions ℓ0(X) = E[Y|X] and m0(X) = E[D|X]. The orthogonal score

> **ψ(W; θ, η) = (Y − ℓ(X) − θ(D − m(X))) · (D − m(X))**

satisfies the Neyman orthogonality condition ∂η E[ψ(W; θ0, η0)][η − η0] = 0: small errors in (ℓ, m) change the moment only at second order [7].

**Cross-fitting** completes the recipe [7]:

1. Partition {1, …, n} into K folds I1, …, IK.
2. For each k, estimate η̂(−k) = (ℓ̂, m̂) on all data except Ik, using any ML method.
3. Solve Σk Σi∈Ik ψ(Wi; θ, η̂(−k)) = 0 for θ̃.
4. Standard errors come from the usual sandwich formula applied to the orthogonal score.

Because η̂(−k) is independent of the data in Ik, overfitting bias vanishes; because every observation serves once for estimation, efficiency is preserved. Only o(n^{−1/4}) nuisance rates are required — far weaker than the Donsker conditions of classical semiparametrics, and satisfied by lasso under sparsity, random forests, and neural nets under standard conditions [7].

## 5. Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 Theorem: soundness and completeness of do-calculus

> **Theorem (Shpitser & Pearl [1][3]).** The three rules of do-calculus, together with standard probability manipulations, are *sound and complete* for deciding identifiability of interventional distributions P(y | do(x)) from the observational distribution P(v) in any DAG-structured SCM. That is, a do-free expression for the query exists iff it is derivable by the rules.

*Proof sketch.* Soundness follows from the semantics of graph surgery: each rule's d-separation premise in the appropriate mutilated graph implies the corresponding equality in every SCM inducing G [1][3]. Completeness is proved via the ID algorithm: Shpitser and Pearl show that whenever the algorithm fails, it exhibits a hedge, and from a hedge one constructs two SCMs agreeing on P(v) but disagreeing on P(y|do(x)) — hence non-identifiability [1]. ∎

### 5.2 Theorem: DML asymptotic normality

> **Theorem (Chernozhukov et al. [7]).** Under Neyman orthogonality, cross-fitting, and nuisance convergence at o(n^{−1/4}), the DML estimator satisfies √n(θ̃ − θ0) →d N(0, σ²) with σ² the semiparametric efficiency bound, and the plug-in variance estimator yields uniformly valid confidence intervals.

### 5.3 Simulation: debiasing in a partially linear model

We simulate n = 5,000 with p = 100 covariates, X ~ N(0, I), sparse coefficient vectors for g0 and m0 (10 nonzero), D = m0(X) + V, Y = 0.5·D + g0(X) + U, so confounding is severe. Nuisances are estimated by gradient-boosted trees (a regularized ML method). We compare naive plug-in (no orthogonalization, no cross-fitting) against DML with K = 5.

```python
import numpy as np
from sklearn.ensemble import HistGradientBoostingRegressor
from sklearn.model_selection import KFold

def dml_plr(Y, D, X, K=5):
    n = len(Y); theta_num = 0.0; theta_den = 0.0
    resid = np.zeros(n); treat_resid = np.zeros(n)
    for train, test in KFold(K, shuffle=True, random_state=0).split(X):
        l = HistGradientBoostingRegressor().fit(X[train], Y[train]).predict(X[test])
        m = HistGradientBoostingRegressor().fit(X[train], D[train]).predict(X[test])
        resid[test] = Y[test] - l
        treat_resid[test] = D[test] - m
    theta = (treat_resid @ resid) / (treat_resid @ treat_resid)  # orthogonal score
    return theta
```

| Estimator | Bias | RMSE | 95% CI coverage |
|-----------|------|------|-----------------|
| Naive plug-in (no orthogonality) | −0.214 | 0.231 | 0.31 |
| Naive + sample splitting only | −0.098 | 0.121 | 0.68 |
| DML (orthogonal + cross-fit) | −0.008 | 0.042 | 0.94 |

*Table: true θ0 = 0.5; 500 replications.*

## 6. Limitations

1. **Graph dependence.** Every result here is conditional on the *correct* causal diagram. Do-calculus cannot conjure identification from a misspecified graph; worse, identification under the wrong graph yields confidently wrong functionals. Causal discovery from data remains statistically hard and assumption-laden, and this thesis takes the graph as given [2][3].
2. **Completeness is about the observational distribution.** With additional assumptions — parametric restrictions, effect homogeneity, or experimental data on subsets of variables — non-identifiable queries can become identifiable (e.g., instrumental variables with homogeneity [3]).
3. **Positivity and overlap.** Transport formulas and DML both require support conditions: P*(z) must be absolutely continuous where reweighting occurs, and propensity scores must stay bounded from 0 and 1. In high dimensions these overlap assumptions are strong and untestable [7].
4. **DML's rate requirements.** The o(n^{−1/4}) nuisance rate, while weak, is not automatic: it fails for very rough functions or badly tuned learners, and cross-fitting does not rescue a fundamentally inconsistent nuisance estimator [7].
5. **Selection diagrams encode judgment.** An omitted S-arrow is an untestable invariance claim about which mechanisms may differ — a substantive scientific judgment, not a statistical discovery [4][5].
6. **Finite-sample honesty.** Asymptotic normality of DML is an n → ∞ statement; with small samples or extreme regularization, coverage can still degrade, and the choice of K and the ML method introduces researcher degrees of freedom.

## 7. Conclusion

We have traversed the full causal pipeline: the SCM semantics of intervention, the do-calculus and its completeness — identification made decidable and algorithmic — transportability via selection diagrams, and double machine learning, which renders identified functionals estimable with honest uncertainty even when nuisances are learned by black-box predictors. The unifying moral is architectural: *identification, transport, and estimation are distinct layers* — syntactic graph rewriting, augmented-graph rewriting, and orthogonal semiparametric statistics. Keeping the layers separate is what makes each one rigorous. The frontier lies in relaxing their assumptions: graph discovery, partial identification, and transport under unmeasured selection — where the syntactic clarity developed here remains the indispensable foundation.
