# Post-Quantum TLS 1.3: Hybrid Key Exchange with ML-KEM and X25519, KEMTLS Authentication, and Migration Economics for Internet-Scale Deployment

## Abstract

The arrival of cryptographically relevant quantum computers threatens the public-key primitives underpinning Transport Layer Security: Shor's algorithm reduces ECDHE and RSA key exchange to polynomial time, exposing decades of *harvest-now, decrypt-later* traffic to retroactive compromise. NIST's standardization of **ML-KEM** (FIPS 203, August 2024) [1], derived from CRYSTALS-Kyber and grounded in the Module Learning-with-Errors (M-LWE) problem, provides the first production-grade post-quantum key encapsulation mechanism. This thesis examines the complete migration path for TLS 1.3 (RFC 8446) [2] from X25519 ECDHE to quantum-resistant handshakes. We analyze the **X25519MLKEM768** hybrid named group in the IETF's draft-ietf-tls-ecdhe-mlkem [3], the **KEMTLS** authentication paradigm that replaces post-quantum signatures with KEM-based implicit authentication [4], and the migration economics measured in Google's and Cloudflare's Internet-scale deployments (CECPQ2, 2024–2025) [5]. We quantify handshake-size amplification (32 bytes → 1,216 bytes client key share), formalize the hybrid combiner security argument, and model the cost function of fleet-wide migration including middlebox ossification, certificate-chain inflation, and session-resumption amortization. We conclude with a phased doctrine: hybrid key exchange first, KEMTLS authentication for bandwidth-critical domains, pure post-quantum only after middlebox telemetry confirms readiness.

## 1 Introduction

Transport Layer Security 1.3, standardized in RFC 8446 [2], secures roughly 60–70% of all Internet traffic and depends for its forward secrecy on the hardness of the elliptic-curve discrete logarithm problem (ECDLP) over curves such as X25519. A fault-tolerant quantum computer running Shor's algorithm [6] would solve ECDLP in polynomial time, collapsing both the confidentiality of ephemeral key exchange and the authenticity of RSA/ECDSA certificate signatures. Unlike classical attacks, this threat has a *retroactive* character: an adversary who records encrypted traffic today can decrypt it years from now once a cryptographically relevant quantum computer (CRQC) exists. Intelligence agencies and standards bodies therefore treat migration as urgent *before* Q-day, a position codified in the U.S. National Security Memorandum on quantum computing (NSM-10, 2022) and in CNSA 2.0's 2030 deadlines for national-security systems.

NIST's post-quantum cryptography standardization project, launched in 2016, concluded its first round in August 2024 with three finalized standards: FIPS 203 (ML-KEM) [1], FIPS 204 (ML-DSA), and FIPS 205 (SLH-DSA). ML-KEM is the workhorse of the transition: a module-lattice KEM with compact keys (ML-KEM-768: public key 1,184 bytes, ciphertext 1,088 bytes) [7] and microsecond-scale operations on modern CPUs, dramatically more efficient than pre-quantum-failure-era alternatives such as Classic McEliece (public keys > 260 kB).

> **Theorem: Harvest-now-decrypt-later imperative.** Let Π be TLS 1.3 with ECDHE key exchange over X25519 and let A_q be a quantum adversary capable of solving ECDLP in time T_q after a delay δ. Any ciphertext transcript C recorded at time t remains confidential only until t + δ. Therefore, for data with confidentiality lifetime L > δ, post-quantum key exchange must be deployed *before* t + δ − L, not at T_q.

This thesis makes five contributions:

1. **A self-contained exposition of ML-KEM internals** — key generation, the Fujisaki–Okamoto (FO) transform achieving IND-CCA2 security, and Number Theoretic Transform (NTT) polynomial arithmetic in the ring R_q = ℤ_q[x]/(x^256 + 1) with q = 3329.
2. **A formal treatment of hybrid key exchange in TLS 1.3** — the concatenation combiner, its security proof sketch, and the exact wire-format and key-schedule integration of the X25519MLKEM768 named group (codepoint 0x11EC) from draft-ietf-tls-ecdhe-mlkem [3].
3. **A comparative analysis of KEMTLS versus PQTLS** — showing how KEMTLS authenticates the server via long-term KEM keys, eliminating post-quantum signatures (which inflate chains by 5–35 kB [8]) at the cost of an additional round trip, with KEMTLS-PDK recovering 1-RTT under pre-distributed keys [9].
4. **An empirical synthesis of deployment telemetry** — Cloudflare's origin-facing ML-KEM deployment and Google's Chrome CECPQ2 experiment [5], quantifying handshake amplification, middlebox failure modes, and the observed per-kilobyte latency penalty (~1.5% median per 1 kB [8]).
5. **A migration-economics model** — a cost function over handshake bytes, CPU cycles, certificate PKI re-issuance, and session-resumption amortization, yielding a phased deployment doctrine for Internet-scale fleets.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 TLS 1.3 handshake anatomy

RFC 8446 [2] restructured the TLS handshake around a 1-RTT ephemeral Diffie–Hellman core. The client sends a `ClientHello` containing one or more `KeyShareEntry` values — ephemeral public keys for its guessed named groups. The server selects a group, returns its `KeyShare`, and the shared secret `ZZ` is fed into the HKDF-based **key schedule**:

```
              0
              |
              v
    PSK ->  HKDF-Extract = Early Secret
              |
              v
        Derive-Secret(., "ext binder" | "c e traffic", "")
              |
              v
   (EC)DHE -> HKDF-Extract = Handshake Secret
              |
              v
   ...
```

In TLS 1.3 the key schedule derives traffic secrets via `HKDF-Expand-Label`, binding each stage to the running transcript hash. A hybrid group feeds a *combined* shared secret into `HKDF-Extract` where the `(EC)DHE` input is the concatenation of the two components' secrets.

| Handshake message | Classical size (X25519) | Hybrid X25519MLKEM768 |
|---|---|---|
| ClientHello key_share | 32 bytes | 32 + 1,184 = **1,216 bytes** |
| ServerHello key_share | 32 bytes | 1,184 bytes (server KEM share per draft) |
| Server ciphertext (key share response) | 32 bytes | 32 + 1,088 = **1,120 bytes** (X25519 ct + ML-KEM ct) |
| Certificate chain | ~3–5 kB | unchanged (classical sigs) or 8–35 kB (PQ sigs) |

*Table 1: Wire-size amplification of the X25519MLKEM768 hybrid group. Sizes from draft-ietf-tls-ecdhe-mlkem [3] and Cloudflare measurements [5].*

### 2.2 Module Learning-with-Errors

ML-KEM's security reduces to the **Module-LWE** problem: given a random matrix **A** ∈ R_q^{k×k} over the cyclotomic ring R_q = ℤ_q[x]/(x^256 + 1) with q = 3329, distinguish samples (**A**, **A·s** + **e**) from uniform, where **s**, **e** are small-coefficient secret and error vectors. The module rank k ∈ {2, 3, 4} yields ML-KEM-512, -768, -1024, mapping to NIST security categories 1, 3, and 5 respectively (equivalent, per FIPS 203, to AES-128, AES-192, and AES-256) [1][7]. NIST recommends ML-KEM-768 as the default parameter set.

The FO transform converts the IND-CPA public-key encryption scheme into an IND-CCA2 KEM: encapsulation re-encrypts deterministically from hashed randomness, and decapsulation implicitly rejects ciphertexts that fail re-encryption comparison. This yields active security against chosen-ciphertext attackers on the network.

### 2.3 Hybrid design framework

IETF's draft-ietf-tls-hybrid-design [10] defines terminology now adopted across the PQUIP working group: *traditional* algorithms (ECDH, ECDSA — widely deployed), *next-generation* algorithms (ML-KEM, ML-DSA — not yet ubiquitous), and *hybrid* key exchange as the simultaneous use of two or more algorithms whose outputs are combined so that the final key is secure if **at least one** component remains unbroken. This conservative posture hedges against both quantum cryptanalysis *and* the possibility that young lattice schemes suffer a classical break (a non-trivial risk, as the breaks of Rainbow, GeMSS, and SIDH demonstrated [5]).

## 3 Methodology

We proceed as a *synthetic-empirical* study: we derive ML-KEM's primitives from FIPS 203 [1] and the CRYSTALS-Kyber specification, reconstruct the TLS 1.3 key-schedule modifications from draft-ietf-tls-ecdhe-mlkem [3] and draft-ietf-tls-hybrid-design [10], and synthesize performance data from published microbenchmarks of NTT lattice arithmetic (including Cloudflare's ops/sec table for X25519, ML-KEM-768, and the hybrid [5]), the KEMTLS project's handshake-size tables at NIST level III [8], and deployment experiments — Chrome's CECPQ2 rollout beginning in Chrome 116 (2023) [11] and Cloudflare's 2024–2025 origin-facing ML-KEM migration [5].

Security arguments are presented as proof sketches in the random-oracle model, following the hybrid combiner literature and the KEMTLS security analysis (CCS 2020) [4].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 ML-KEM internals: NTT arithmetic, FO transform, and parameter geometry

ML-KEM key generation samples a 256-bit seed ρ, expands it via SHAKE-128 (XOF) into the public matrix **Â** in the NTT domain, samples secret **s** and error **e** from centered binomial distributions B_η, and computes the public key as the compressed pair (ρ, **t** = **Â** ∘ **ŝ** + **ê**). Encapsulation samples randomness m, derives (K̄, r) = G(m ∥ H(pk)), computes ciphertext **c** = Compress(Enc(pk, m; r)), and outputs the shared secret K = KDF(K̄ ∥ H(c)).

The performance of the entire scheme rests on the **Number Theoretic Transform**: multiplication in R_q of degree-256 polynomials is performed as point-wise multiplication in the NTT domain,

```python
# Pseudocode: ML-KEM-768 matrix-vector multiplication core (k=3)
def kem_encaps_internal(A_hat, t_hat, m, coins):
    r = sample_binomial(coins, eta=2)          # small error vector
    r_hat = ntt(r)                             # forward NTT, 256-pt
    u = intt(ntt_mul(A_hat.T, r_hat)) + e1     # u = A^T r + e1
    v = intt(ntt_mul(t_hat.T, r_hat)) + e2 + decompress(m)
    return compress(u), compress(v)             # ciphertext (c1, c2)
```

The modulus q = 3329 = 13 · 2⁸ + 1 admits a primitive 256th root of unity, enabling a *fully splitting* NTT without padding. The FO re-encryption check doubles encapsulation work on decapsulation, but the whole KEM remains ~2–3× the cost of X25519 in CPU time while dominating it in *bandwidth* (1,184-byte public keys versus 32-byte curve points) [5].

> **Theorem: IND-CCA2 of ML-KEM.** In the random-oracle model, the Fujisaki–Okamoto transform applied to an IND-CPA-secure module-LWE PKE with correctness error δ yields a KEM with IND-CCA2 advantage bounded by q_RO · δ + Adv^M-LWE, where q_RO is the number of random-oracle queries. ML-KEM-768's decryption-failure probability 2^−164 renders the q_RO · δ term cryptographically negligible.

### 4.2 Hybrid key exchange in TLS 1.3: the X25519MLKEM768 named group

The IETF draft [3] specifies three hybrid groups — **X25519MLKEM768** (0x11EC), **SecP256r1MLKEM768** (0x11EB), and **SecP384r1MLKEM1024** — constructed per the concatenation combiner of draft-ietf-tls-hybrid-design [10]. The client's `KeyShareEntry.key_exchange` carries `x25519_pubkey ∥ mlkem768_pubkey`; the server responds with its ephemeral X25519 share concatenated with the ML-KEM **ciphertext** c (encapsulated to the client's ML-KEM public key); the combined shared secret is:

```rust
// Hybrid shared-secret derivation (concatenation combiner)
fn hybrid_secret(x25519_ss: &[u8; 32], mlkem_ss: &[u8; 32]) -> [u8; 64] {
    let mut ikm = [0u8; 64];
    ikm[..32].copy_from_slice(x25519_ss);   // traditional component first
    ikm[32..].copy_from_slice(mlkem_ss);    // next-gen component second
    ikm
}
// TLS 1.3 key schedule: HKDF-Extract(salt=0, IKM=hybrid_secret) -> Handshake Secret
```

> **Theorem: Hybrid combiner security.** Let KEM₁ be X25519-ECDH and KEM₂ be ML-KEM-768, and let the hybrid secret be Z = Z₁ ∥ Z₂ fed into HKDF-Extract modeled as a dual-PRF. Then Adv^hybrid(A) ≤ Adv^KEM₁(B₁) + Adv^KEM₂(B₂): breaking the hybrid requires breaking *both* components. In particular, a future quantum break of ML-KEM-768 leaves classical X25519 security intact, and a classical lattice break leaves the X25519 component's security against classical adversaries intact.

Operationally, CECPQ2 taught the industry that the dominant cost is not CPU but **middlebox ossification**: middleboxes with hard-coded ClientHello size limits dropped the ~1.2 kB key shares, and Chrome added a `PostQuantumKeyAgreementEnabled` enterprise policy as an escape hatch [11]. Cloudflare's origin-facing deployment confirmed that with modern stacks the CPU overhead is "hard to notice in practice" — the ~1 kB handshake growth being the real constraint [5].

### 4.3 KEMTLS: authentication without signatures

Post-quantum *signatures* are the migration's ugly sibling. A Dilithium3 (ML-DSA-65) signature is 3,293 bytes with a 1,952-byte public key; SPHINCS+ signatures reach 8–50 kB; certificate chains inflate accordingly — the KEMTLS project's measurements show level-III PQTLS handshakes reaching 8–27 kB of public-key material versus ~850 bytes classically [8].

**KEMTLS** (CCS 2020; Schwabe et al. [4], IETF draft [12]) eliminates signatures entirely. The server's long-term identity is a KEM public key in its certificate; authentication is *implicit*:

1. Client encapsulates to the server's long-term KEM public key, producing ciphertext ct_S and secret K_S.
2. K_S is mixed into the handshake key schedule; only the holder of the long-term secret key can decapsulate and derive matching traffic keys.
3. The Finished MAC under the derived keys constitutes implicit authentication — the client is convinced it speaks to the certificate holder without any signature ever being computed or transmitted.

```
Client                                            Server
------                                            ------
ClientHello + KEM key_share ──────────────────►
                                    ◄────────── ServerHello + KEM ciphertext
[derive handshake keys]
                                    ◄────────── EncryptedExtensions + Certificate
                                                (long-term KEM pk, NO signature)
Client encaps → ct_auth ──────────────────────►
[Finished verifies implicit auth]               [Server decaps → Finished]
```

Because ML-KEM-768 ciphertexts (1,088 bytes) are far smaller than ML-DSA signatures, KEMTLS reduces both bandwidth and server CPU. Its cost is an extra round trip for full authentication — recovered by **KEMTLS-PDK**, which uses pre-distributed server public keys (cached, pinned, or provisioned) to collapse back to 1-RTT [9].

### 4.4 Migration economics: a cost model for Internet-scale fleets

We model total migration cost for a fleet serving N handshakes/day as:

*C_total = N · (Δbytes · c_byte + Δcycles · c_cycle) + C_pki + C_middlebox + C_risk*

where:

- **Δbytes** ≈ 1,184 bytes (client) + 1,120 bytes (server) for X25519MLKEM768 vs. X25519 [3][5]; at Internet scale this is petabytes of additional ingress monthly for hyperscalers, but negligible per-connection.
- **Δcycles**: negative-to-neutral — ML-KEM-768 encapsulation is *faster* than X25519 scalar multiplication on AVX2 (31k vs 17k ops/sec client-side per Cloudflare [5]); the hybrid pays both, roughly doubling key-exchange CPU, still microseconds.
- **C_pki**: certificate re-issuance dominates if PQ signatures are adopted; hybrid-key-exchange-only migration (classical signatures retained) defers this entirely — the strongest economic argument for the phased approach.
- **C_middlebox**: the empirically dominant term — CECPQ2's middlebox failures required vendor engagement and policy escape hatches [11].

### 4.5 Formal verification and implementation assurance

For TLS integration, TLA+ specifications of the hybrid key schedule assert the critical invariant:

```tla
---- MODULE HybridKeySchedule ----
EXTENDS Naturals, Sequences
VARIABLES earlySecret, handshakeSecret, transcript
Combine(ss1, ss2) == ss1 \o ss2        \* concatenation combiner
Next == /\ handshakeSecret' = HKDF_Extract(earlySecret, Combine(x25519_ss, mlkem_ss))
        /\ transcript' = transcript \o Hash(handshakeMessages)
Invariant == \A t \in transcript : HashBound(t)   \* transcript binding preserved
====
```

The invariant that matters — *every derived secret is bound to the full transcript including both key shares* — prevents downgrade attacks in which a middlebox strips the ML-KEM component: TLS 1.3's transcript binding covers the negotiated group list, so stripping a share invalidates the Finished MAC.

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 Size and performance synthesis

Consolidating Cloudflare [5], the KEMTLS measurement project [8], and FIPS 203 [1]:

| Primitive (level III) | Public key | Ciphertext / signature | Client ops/sec | Server ops/sec |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| X25519 (classical) | 32 B | 32 B | ~17,000 | ~17,000 |
| ML-KEM-768 | 1,184 B | 1,088 B | ~31,000 | ~70,000 |
| X25519MLKEM768 hybrid | 1,216 B | 1,120 B | ~11,000 | ~14,000 |
| ML-DSA-65 (Dilithium3) | 1,952 B | 3,293 B (sig) | sign ~5,000 | verify ~15,000 |
| Falcon-1024 | 1,793 B | 1,280 B (sig) | sign slow | verify fast |
| SPHINCS+-192f | 48 B | 35,664 B (sig) | slow | slow |

*Table 2: Level-III post-quantum primitive sizes and throughput. ML-KEM-768 alone outperforms X25519 per-operation; the hybrid roughly halves throughput while remaining in the microsecond regime. Signature schemes are 3–30× larger per authentication event.*

### 5.2 Handshake-level consequences

A full PQTLS handshake at level III (Kyber-768 key exchange + Dilithium3 certificates) transmits ~10.8 kB of public-key material in the leaf handshake alone, ~16 kB including the intermediate CA — versus ~848 bytes classically [8]. This crosses TCP's initial congestion window for full chains, adding round trips on lossy paths. KEMTLS with the same KEM transmits roughly the *key-exchange* bytes only, avoiding the signature blowup entirely — the decisive quantitative argument of [4].

### 5.3 Security proof sketches

**Hybrid key exchange.** In the random-oracle model with HKDF-Extract as a dual-PRF, the concatenation combiner is a *robust* combiner: for any PPT adversary A against the hybrid KEM, there exist reductions B₁, B₂ with Adv^hybrid(A) ≤ Adv^X25519(B₁) + Adv^ML-KEM(B₂) [10]. Downgrade resistance follows from TLS 1.3 transcript binding: the negotiated group list is hashed into every derived secret, so stripping the ML-KEM share invalidates the Finished MAC.

**KEMTLS authentication.** The CCS 2020 analysis [4] proves KEMTLS secure in a multi-stage key-exchange model: implicit authentication follows from binding the server's long-term KEM secret to the session key, and forward secrecy from the ephemeral KEM exchange. The proof assumes IND-CCA2 security of the long-term KEM — precisely ML-KEM's guarantee.

> **Theorem: Phased-migration security.** A fleet running hybrid X25519MLKEM768 key exchange with classical ECDSA authentication is quantum-safe for *confidentiality* against harvest-now-decrypt-later, while authentication remains classically secure. Upgrading authentication to KEMTLS or ML-DSA later does not retroactively weaken recorded sessions, because session keys depend only on the (already quantum-safe) key exchange. Hence key-exchange-first migration is *lossless* with respect to future authentication upgrades.

## 6 Limitations

1. **Middlebox ossification.** The largest deployment risk is not cryptography but infrastructure: hard-coded size limits in middleboxes, load balancers, and DPI equipment. CECPQ2 encountered real failures [11]; any fleet migration requires telemetry-gated rollout and enterprise opt-outs.
2. **Authentication gap.** Hybrid key exchange protects confidentiality, but classical certificate signatures remain quantum-vulnerable: a CRQC could forge certificates and mount active man-in-the-middle attacks. Full quantum safety requires PQ signatures or KEMTLS, with their bandwidth and PKI costs.
3. **Cryptanalytic youth.** Module-LWE has ~20 years of study versus ~40 for ECDLP; the SIDH break (2022) is a cautionary tale for isogeny schemes, though lattice schemes have survived far more scrutiny. The hybrid design is explicitly the hedge [5][10].
4. **Side channels.** ML-KEM's NTT, binomial sampling, and FO re-encryption are sensitive to timing and power analysis; constant-time implementations are mandatory, and masked hardware implementations remain an active research area. FIPS 203's implicit-rejection decapsulation must not leak *which* ciphertexts failed.
5. **Post-quantum signatures in the web PKI.** Certificate-chain inflation (8–35 kB) interacts badly with TCP slow start, QUIC amplification limits, and DNS (for DANE/TLSA records); this remains the least-solved engineering problem in the migration [8].

## 7 Conclusion

The migration of TLS 1.3 to post-quantum security is no longer a research question but an engineering program with a standardized primitive, specified wire formats, measured costs, and field telemetry. **ML-KEM-768**, standardized in FIPS 203 [1], delivers IND-CCA2 key encapsulation from Module-LWE at microsecond speeds with ~1.1 kB keys. The **X25519MLKEM768** hybrid group [3] provides a cryptographically conservative, wire-compatible upgrade path: security reduces to the stronger of its two components, and field data from Google and Cloudflare [5][11] shows the cost is ~1.2 kB of handshake growth with negligible CPU impact. **KEMTLS** [4] solves the harder authentication problem by replacing post-quantum signatures with KEM-based implicit authentication, cutting handshake bytes by an order of magnitude relative to PQTLS at the price of protocol change and — without pre-distributed keys — an extra round trip.

The economically rational doctrine is *phased*: (i) deploy hybrid key exchange fleet-wide now, amortized by session resumption, to neutralize harvest-now-decrypt-later; (ii) gate on middlebox telemetry with enterprise opt-outs; (iii) migrate authentication to KEMTLS or ML-DSA certificates in latency-tolerant and high-assurance domains first; (iv) reserve pure-PQ operation for the post-2030 horizon. The cryptography is ready. The remaining work is measurement, PKI logistics, and the unglamorous labor of upgrading the Internet's middle.
