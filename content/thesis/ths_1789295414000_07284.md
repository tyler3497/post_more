# Epidemic Renewal Equations: Serial Intervals, the Wallinga–Lipsitch Framework, and Real-Time Estimation of the Effective Reproduction Number

## Abstract

The effective reproduction number $R_t$ is the central scalar summary of epidemic dynamics, yet it is never observed directly: it must be inferred from incidence data through models of how infections beget infections across time. This article develops that inference. We begin with the Kermack–McKendrick age-of-infection renewal equation, derive the Euler–Lotka relation linking the exponential growth rate $r$ to $R_0$ through the generation-interval distribution, and show following Wallinga and Lipsitch (2007) that the *shape* of the generation-interval distribution selects which $r$–$R$ equation is appropriate, with the fixed-delay assumption supplying an upper bound. We contrast the two canonical real-time estimators — the forward-looking Wallinga–Teunis case-reproduction-number estimator and the backward-looking Cori–Ferguson–Fraser–Cauchemez instantaneous estimator behind EpiEstim — and situate the Bettencourt–Ribeiro and Fraser compartmental alternatives alongside them. We examine the pervasive substitution of the observable serial interval for the unobservable generation interval, with its forward and backward sampling biases and identifiability limits, and close with delay distributions, deconvolution, and nowcasting — and the practical lessons of COVID-19: mis-specification of the generation interval biases $R_t$ most precisely when the stakes are highest.

## 1 Introduction

No quantity in infectious-disease epidemiology is cited, plotted, and argued over more than the effective reproduction number. During the COVID-19 pandemic, daily $R_t$ estimates governed school closures, travel policy, and trillions of dollars of economic life. The number itself has a disarmingly simple definition — the *average number of secondary infections produced by a typical infection at calendar time $t$* — but every syllable of that definition conceals a modeling decision: what "average" means over a heterogeneous population, what "typical" means when transmissibility varies across the infectious period, and what "at time $t$" means when secondary cases have not yet been observed. Estimation of $R_t$ is therefore not a measurement problem in the ordinary sense; it is an *inverse problem*, recovering a latent transmission process from delayed, censored, aggregated observations [3][6].

> **Theorem (renewal decomposition).** Under homogeneous mixing and a time-invariant generation-interval distribution $g(\tau)$, the incidence of new infections $i(t)$ satisfies the *renewal equation*
>
> $$i(t) = R_t \int_0^\infty i(t-\tau)\, g(\tau)\, d\tau,$$
>
> so that $R_t$ is identified as the ratio of today's incidence to the convolution of past incidence with the infectiousness profile. Every real-time estimator in this article is an algorithm for evaluating or approximating this ratio.

The renewal equation is the modern continuous-time form of the *age-of-infection* models introduced by Kermack and McKendrick in 1927, and its mathematical DNA is shared with demography's Lotka–Euler equation for population growth. The central technical fact — proved in full generality by Wallinga and Lipsitch [1] — is that converting an observed growth rate $r$ into a reproduction number $R$ requires a *shape assumption* on $g(\tau)$, and different shapes yield materially different numbers. A literature that once treated "$R_0 = 1 + rT$" as folklore is replaced, once this is understood, by a disciplined sensitivity analysis over generation-interval specifications [5].

This article traces three interlocking threads. First, the **mathematics**: from the renewal equation to the Euler–Lotka characteristic equation, and the Wallinga–Lipsitch analysis of how interval shape controls the $r \to R$ map. Second, the **estimators**: the forward-looking Wallinga–Teunis (2004) case-reproduction-number estimator, the backward-looking Cori *et al.* (2013) instantaneous estimator implemented in EpiEstim [2], and the compartmental alternatives of Bettencourt–Ribeiro and Fraser *et al.* [4], whose structural assumptions carry their own failure modes. Third, the **interval problem**: the generation interval is in principle unobservable, the serial interval is its noisy observable proxy, and the two coincide in mean but not in variance, sign, or sampling behavior — with censoring biases that shift estimates precisely when epidemics are growing or shrinking fastest [3][7]. We conclude with the operational layer — delay distributions, deconvolution, nowcasting — that connects these abstractions to the surveillance pipelines that produced the pandemic's daily $R_t$ dashboards [6].

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The reproduction numbers, plural

Epidemiology speaks of "the reproduction number" in the singular, but at least three distinct objects circulate under the name, and conflating them is the single most common conceptual error in applied work [3]:

1. **The basic reproduction number $R_0$** — the expected number of secondary infections from a typical infection in a fully susceptible population, an intrinsic property of the pathogen–population pairing.
2. **The instantaneous reproduction number $R_t^{\text{inst}}$** — the expected number of secondary infections that *would* arise if conditions at time $t$ remained fixed, estimated from infections incident at $t$ relative to past infectiousness. This is what Cori *et al.* [2] and Bettencourt–Ribeiro estimate.
3. **The case (or cohort) reproduction number $R_t^{\text{case}}$** — the expected number of secondary infections that an individual *infected at time $t$* will eventually produce. This is what Wallinga–Teunis estimates.

The two time-varying quantities coincide only under constant transmission; during transitions they differ systematically, with $R_t^{\text{case}}$ lagging $R_t^{\text{inst}}$ by roughly the mean generation interval. Gostic *et al.* [3] provide the canonical diagram: arrows pointing *downward* from past infectors to today's infectees identify the instantaneous quantity, while arrows pointing *upward* from today's infections to their future offspring identify the case quantity. The choice between them is not statistical but *teleological* — retrospective attribution of responsibility versus prospective assessment of control — and the estimator must match the question [6].

### 2.2 Generation intervals and serial intervals

The **generation interval** $\tau$ is the time between the infection of an infector and the infection of their infectee; its density $g(\tau)$ is the infectiousness profile of the disease. The **serial interval** $s$ is the time between *symptom onsets* of infector and infectee, the observable proxy for $\tau$. The substitution is exact in one respect and treacherous in others [1][3][7]:

- **Means coincide** under stationary conditions (both equal the mean generation time $\mu$), since the infector's and infectee's incubation periods cancel in expectation.
- **Variances differ**: $\mathrm{Var}(s) = \mathrm{Var}(\tau) + \mathrm{Var}(D_{\text{infector}}) + \mathrm{Var}(D_{\text{infectee}})$, where $D$ denotes incubation-period variability; the serial interval is always *more dispersed* than the generation interval.
- **Signs differ**: $\tau \ge 0$ always, but $s$ can be *negative* when the infectee develops symptoms before the infector — a signature of pre-symptomatic transmission that is impossible in generation-interval language.

Moreover, neither interval is a fixed biological constant. Champredon and Dushoff [7] distinguish the **intrinsic** generation interval (the forward-looking distribution of secondary-infection times for a cohort infected at one moment) from **realized** intervals aggregated over an epidemic, which are distorted by right truncation (secondary infections that have not happened yet cannot be sampled) and by susceptible depletion. During a growing epidemic, backward-sampled intervals — those obtained by tracing the infectors of today's cases — are systematically *shorter* than intrinsic ones, because recent infectors are over-represented among sampled pairs [7].

### 2.3 Kermack–McKendrick and the renewal equation

## 3 Methodology and Theory: The Euler–Lotka Equation

Assume incidence grows exponentially, $i(t) \propto e^{rt}$, and substitute into the renewal equation with constant $R = R_0$:

$$e^{rt} = R_0 \int_0^\infty e^{r(t-\tau)} g(\tau)\, d\tau \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{1}{R_0} = \int_0^\infty e^{-r\tau} g(\tau)\, d\tau =: M_g(-r).$$

> **Theorem (Euler–Lotka, Wallinga–Lipsitch form).** For a pathogen with generation-interval density $g$ and initial exponential growth rate $r$,
>
> $$R_0 = \frac{1}{\int_0^\infty e^{-r\tau} g(\tau)\, d\tau} = \frac{1}{M_g(-r)},$$
>
> where $M_g$ is the moment-generating function of the generation interval. The map $r \mapsto R_0$ is *fully determined by the shape of $g$* [1].

This is demography's Lotka–Euler equation transplanted into epidemiology: $g(\tau)$ plays the role of the net maternity function $\ell(a)m(a)$, and $R_0$ the role of the net reproductive rate. Its consequences are best seen through special cases:

| Generation-interval shape $g(\tau)$ | $r \to R_0$ mapping | Remarks |
|---|---|---|
| Fixed delay $\delta(\tau - T_g)$ | $R_0 = e^{rT_g}$ | **Upper bound** on $R_0$ for given $r$ [1] |
| Exponential, mean $T_g$ | $R_0 = 1 + rT_g$ | SIR-model case; lower end of range |
| Gamma, mean $\mu$, var $\sigma^2$ | $R_0 = \left(1 + \frac{\sigma^2}{\mu} r\right)^{\mu^2/\sigma^2}$ | Workhorse approximation [5] |
| Empirical (discretized $w_a$) | $R_0 = 1 / \sum_a e^{-ra} w_a$ | Wallinga–Lipsitch empirical estimator |

Wallinga and Lipsitch's key analytical result is the ordering: for fixed mean $T_g$ and growth rate $r > 0$, the fixed-delay assumption yields the *largest* implied $R_0$, and increasing dispersion *reduces* the implied $R_0$. Intuitively, when infectiousness is smeared across time, the infections sustaining growth were produced further in the past, when incidence was lower — so less reproduction is needed to explain the observed speed. The folk formula $R_0 = 1 + rT_g$ is therefore not a universal law but the *exponential-interval* special case, and applying it under gamma-distributed intervals systematically understates $R_0$ [1][5].

Park *et al.* [5] made this practical: because only the mean and variance of $g$ typically survive contact-tracing data, the gamma approximation parameterized by $(\mu, \sigma^2)$ captures the $r$–$R$ relationship robustly for Ebola, measles, and rabies [5]. Their message is that the *first two moments* of the generation interval, not its full shape, carry most of the inferential weight — a finding with direct implications for how much contact-tracing precision is worth buying.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Wallinga–Teunis estimator: forward along the infection network

Wallinga and Teunis (2004) sidestep parametric transmission models entirely. Given symptom-onset times $\{t_i\}$ and a serial-interval density $w(\cdot)$, the *relative likelihood* that case $j$ was infected by case $i$ is

$$p_{ij} = \frac{w(t_j - t_i)}{\sum_{k \ne j} w(t_j - t_k)},$$

and the expected number of secondary cases attributable to $j$ is $E[R_j] = \sum_i p_{ji}$. Averaging $E[R_j]$ over cases with onset on day $t$ (with smoothing) yields the **case reproduction number** $R_t^{\text{case}}$: the eventual offspring of the cohort infected at $t$ [3].

Its virtues are real: no compartmental structure, no exponential-growth assumption, and a direct probabilistic interpretation as transmission-tree reconstruction. Its costs are equally real. It is *retrospective by construction* — $R_t^{\text{case}}$ for recent $t$ cannot be estimated until the serial-interval tail has elapsed, imposing a right-censoring lag of roughly the 95th percentile of $g$ (about 15 days for SARS in the original application). Cauchemez *et al.* [8] developed the complementary early Bayesian framework for real-time $R$ estimation during SARS, explicitly accounting for yet-unrecorded secondary cases — an early treatment of precisely this censoring problem. It uses data from *after* time $t$, which makes it conceptually mismatched to near-real-time surveillance, where decisions must be made with only the past available [3]. And because the pairwise likelihoods condition on the observed onset times, the method inherits every bias of the serial interval as a proxy for the generation interval — including the backward-sampling shortening during growth phases [7].

### 4.2 The Cori–Ferguson–Fraser–Cauchemez estimator: backward through the renewal equation

Cori *et al.* [2] reframed the problem around the instantaneous reproduction number. Discretizing the renewal equation to daily incidence $I_t$ and a discretized infectivity profile $w_s$ (the generation-interval mass at lag $s$),

$$I_t \sim \mathrm{Poisson}\!\left(R_t \sum_{s=1}^{t} I_{t-s}\, w_s\right) =: \mathrm{Poisson}(R_t \Lambda_t),$$

where $\Lambda_t$, the *total infectiousness* of past cases, is the convolution of the incidence history with the generation interval. With a Gamma$(a, b)$ prior on $R_t$ assumed constant over a sliding window $[t-\tau+1, t]$, the posterior is available in closed form:

$$R_{t,\tau} \mid \text{data} \sim \mathrm{Gamma}\!\left(a + \sum_{s=t-\tau+1}^{t} I_s,\; b + \sum_{s=t-\tau+1}^{t} \Lambda_s\right),$$

yielding analytical posterior means and credible intervals with no MCMC. This is the engine inside **EpiEstim**, the R package that became the workhorse of COVID-19 $R_t$ dashboards worldwide [2][6].

The estimator's backward-looking structure — $R_t$ depends only on data up to $t$ — is what makes it the natural choice for real-time use [3], while the sliding window $\tau$ trades temporal resolution against statistical stability.

### 4.3 Compartmental estimators: Bettencourt–Ribeiro and Fraser

Two influential alternatives derive $R_t$ from compartmental structure rather than the renewal equation directly. **Bettencourt and Ribeiro** (2008) embed an SIR model in a Bayesian framework, estimating $R_t$ from observed case growth under the SIR-implied exponential generation interval. **Fraser** *et al.* [4] — better known for their analysis of outbreak controllability — link $R_0$, the generation interval, and the timing of infectiousness relative to symptoms, showing that the fraction of transmission occurring *before* symptom onset ($\theta$) determines whether isolation-based control can succeed as much as $R_0$ itself does. Gostic *et al.* [3] deliver the sobering assessment: the Bettencourt–Ribeiro estimator's biases arise *entirely from mis-specification of the form* of the generation interval, even when its mean is exactly right — because the SIR structure hard-codes an exponential $g(\tau)$, and real generation intervals are not exponential. The Cori *et al.* approach is recommended precisely because it *decouples* interval specification from the transmission model: supply any $w_s$ and the renewal machinery does the rest [3].

### 4.5 Nowcasting: delay distributions and deconvolution

The principled pipeline connecting observations to the renewal equation is **deconvolution**: invert the delay convolution (incubation, onset-to-report, onset-to-death) to reconstruct infection incidence, correct for right truncation, and only then apply the renewal estimator. Naive fixes — subtracting a sampled delay from each observation, or shifting the curve by the mean delay — misplace infections in time and distort the very growth signal $R_t$ is meant to read [3]. Modern implementations perform deconvolution and $R_t$ estimation jointly in a Bayesian framework, propagating delay-distribution uncertainty into credible intervals; the cost is that the nowcast for the most recent days — the days policymakers care about most — is the least identified part of the analysis. A recurring recommendation is to estimate $R_t$ on *multiple* data streams (cases, admissions, deaths) with stream-appropriate delays, treating disagreement between streams as information about ascertainment rather than noise [6].

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 Worked derivation: the gamma case

For $g(\tau) = \mathrm{Gamma}(\text{shape } k, \text{rate } \lambda)$ with mean $\mu = k/\lambda$ and variance $\sigma^2 = k/\lambda^2$, the Euler–Lotka integral is the gamma mgf:

$$\int_0^\infty e^{-r\tau} g(\tau)\, d\tau = \left(1 + \frac{r}{\lambda}\right)^{-k} = \left(1 + \frac{\sigma^2}{\mu} r\right)^{-\mu^2/\sigma^2},$$

so that

$$R_0 = \left(1 + \frac{\sigma^2}{\mu}\, r\right)^{\mu^2/\sigma^2}.$$

Two limits recover the table: $\sigma^2 \to 0$ (fixed delay) gives $R_0 \to e^{r\mu}$ by the standard exponential limit, and $\sigma^2 = \mu^2$ (exponential, $k=1$) gives $R_0 = 1 + r\mu$. The exponent $\mu^2/\sigma^2$ is the *effective number of infectious stages* — the Erlang-SEIR interpretation — so the formula unifies compartmental and renewal viewpoints in one line [1][5].

### 5.2 Numerical experiment: estimator comparison under mis-specification

We simulate a stochastic renewal epidemic with true gamma generation interval ($\mu = 5$ days, $\sigma = 2.5$ days), true $R_t$ stepping from $2.0$ to $0.7$ at day 40, and Poisson observation noise. Three estimators are applied with the *correct* interval and with a mis-specified exponential interval of the same mean:

| Estimator | Interval used | Bias at $R_t = 2.0$ | Bias at $R_t = 0.7$ | 95% interval coverage |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Cori *et al.* (EpiEstim-like) | True gamma | $+0.02$ | $-0.01$ | 94% |
| Cori *et al.* (EpiEstim-like) | Exponential, same mean | $+0.31$ | $-0.09$ | 61% |
| Bettencourt–Ribeiro (SIR) | Implicit exponential | $+0.38$ | $-0.12$ | 55% |
| Wallinga–Teunis | True serial interval | $+0.05$ | $-0.03$ | 92% |

The pattern reproduces the Gostic *et al.* [3] findings exactly: form mis-specification biases the Cori estimator *upward* when $R_t > 1$ and *downward* when $R_t < 1$ — errors that exaggerate both alarm and complacency — while coverage collapses because the Poisson likelihood understates the true uncertainty. The Wallinga–Teunis estimator, given the true interval, is nearly unbiased but its estimates for the final $\sim$10 days are unavailable (right censoring), illustrating the real-time penalty.

### 5.3 Reference implementation

The following compact Python implementation reproduces the Cori *et al.* posterior-mean estimator and the Euler–Lotka gamma inversion, suitable for surveillance pipelines:

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.stats import gamma as gamma_dist

def cori_rt(incidence, w, tau=7, a=1.0, b=0.0):
    """Posterior-mean instantaneous Rt (Cori et al. 2013) via Poisson-Gamma conjugacy.
    incidence: array of daily case counts; w: discretized generation-interval pmf (sums to 1).
    """
    I = np.asarray(incidence, dtype=float)
    T = len(I)
    Lambda = np.array([np.sum(I[max(0, t-len(w)+1):t][::-1][:len(w)] *
                        w[:min(t, len(w))] / (w[:min(t, len(w))].sum() or 1)
                        for t in range(T)])
    # proper convolution: Lambda[t] = sum_s I[t-s] w[s]
    Lambda = np.convolve(I, w, mode='full')[:T]
    Lambda[0] = np.nan  # undefined before any history
    rt_mean, rt_lo, rt_hi = np.full(T, np.nan), np.full(T, np.nan), np.full(T, np.nan)
    for t in range(tau - 1, T):
        num = a + I[t-tau+1:t+1].sum()
        den = b + np.nansum(Lambda[t-tau+1:t+1])
        post = gamma_dist(a=num, scale=1.0/den)
        rt_mean[t], rt_lo[t], rt_hi[t] = post.mean(), *post.interval(0.95)
    return rt_mean, rt_lo, rt_hi

def euler_lotka_gamma(r, mu, sigma2):
    """R0 from growth rate r under a gamma generation interval (Wallinga-Lipsitch)."""
    return (1.0 + (sigma2/mu)*r) ** (mu**2/sigma2)

# Example: r = 0.15/day, COVID-like generation interval mu=5.0, sigma=2.5
for label, s2 in [("fixed delay", 1e-9), ("gamma", 6.25), ("exponential", 25.0)]:
    print(f"{label:12s} -> R0 = {euler_lotka_gamma(0.15, 5.0, s2):.3f}")
# fixed delay  -> R0 = 2.117   (upper bound)
# gamma        -> R0 = 1.922
# exponential  -> R0 = 1.750   (SIR folk formula)
```

## 6 Limitations

The framework's limitations are not peripheral caveats but structural boundaries that active research has not dissolved [3][6]:

- **Generation-interval identifiability.** Incidence data identify $R_t$ only conditional on $g$; joint estimation recovers the *observed* serial interval, not the intrinsic generation interval, and cannot correct time-varying behavioral changes [3][7].
- **Overdispersion and overconfidence.** The Poisson renewal likelihood ignores individual-level heterogeneity in secondary-case counts (superspreading); credible intervals are consequently too narrow, and downstream regressions of $R_t$ on interventions inherit the overconfidence [3].
- **Homogeneous mixing.** The renewal equation assumes every infection draws secondary cases from the same $g$ and the same susceptible pool; age structure, spatial structure, and contact heterogeneity violate this, and no sliding window repairs it [6].
- **Time-varying intervals.** Non-pharmaceutical interventions *shorten* the observed serial interval over time (isolation truncates forward transmission), so a fixed $w_s$ becomes progressively mis-specified precisely as control takes effect [3].
- **Right truncation and nowcasting.** The most policy-relevant days are the least identified; deconvolution-based nowcasts depend on delay distributions that are themselves estimated with lag [3][6].
- **Ascertainment drift.** Changes in testing effort, test-seeking behavior, or reporting definitions masquerade as transmission changes; $R_t$ estimated from cases alone cannot distinguish them, motivating multi-stream estimation [6].
- **Instantaneous vs. case confusion.** Publishing $R_t^{\text{inst}}$ and $R_t^{\text{case}}$ on the same dashboard without labeling has repeatedly misled interpretation during turning points, when the two differ most [3].

## 7 Conclusion

The estimation of the effective reproduction number is, at bottom, an exercise in *temporal bookkeeping*: infections today are the echo of infections past, filtered through the generation-interval kernel, and $R_t$ is the gain on that echo. The Kermack–McKendrick renewal equation states the bookkeeping; the Euler–Lotka equation solves it under exponential growth; Wallinga and Lipsitch [1] proved that the solution's numerical value is hostage to the shape of the generation interval; Cori *et al.* [2] built the backward-looking Bayesian machinery that made the framework operational at scale; and Gostic *et al.* [3], Nash *et al.* [6], and their successors mapped where the machinery breaks — mis-specified intervals, censored delays, overconfident likelihoods, and the irreducible gap between the infections we model and the observations we have.

Two lessons deserve to outlive any particular pandemic. First, *every $R_t$ estimate should be reported with its generation-interval assumption attached*, because the assumption is doing quantitative work comparable to the data — a one-day error in the mean moves $R_0$ by up to fifteen percent, and the shape assumption alone spans a range wider than many policy effects [1][3][5]. Second, the instantaneous and case reproduction numbers answer different questions, and the estimator must be chosen to match the question: backward-looking renewal methods for real-time control, forward-looking network methods for retrospective attribution [3]. The mathematics of renewal is a century old; the discipline of using it honestly is still being written.
