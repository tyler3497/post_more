# Automated Market Makers: Concentrated Liquidity, Loss-Versus-Rebalancing, and the Geometry of Constant-Function Market Makers

## Abstract

Automated market makers (AMMs) have displaced the limit order book as the dominant venue for on-chain spot trading, and the constant-function family now secures tens of billions of dollars of liquidity. This thesis develops a unified geometric account of constant-function market makers, from the constant-product invariant through Uniswap v3 concentrated liquidity, and connects the geometry of the bonding curve to the economics of liquidity provision. We formalize the bonding-curve trade function, virtual reserves, and the pool value function, and show how the pool value function's curvature determines adverse-selection costs. We compare classical impermanent loss with loss-versus-rebalancing (LVR), the hedged adverse-selection cost identified by Milionis, Moallemi, Roughgarden and Zhang [1], and derive the celebrated variance-over-eight rule for the constant-product case. Finally, we analyze concentrated liquidity as a lever that multiplies both fee income and LVR per unit of capital, review dynamic-fee and optimal-curve design responses [5][6][8], and discuss empirical evidence on LP profitability in concentrated pools [4][7].

---

##1 Introduction

Decentralized exchanges built on automated market makers settle more spot volume than any other on-chain trading mechanism. The intellectual core of these systems is the *constant-function market maker* (CFMM), introduced in generality by Angeris and Chitra [2]: a smart contract holds reserves of two or more assets and accepts any trade that leaves a prescribed invariant function of the reserves unchanged (up to fees). This single design decision — replacing a central limit order book with a deterministic pricing rule — eliminates the need for designated market makers while creating an entirely new theory of liquidity provision.

The economic question that matters most to liquidity providers (LPs) is deceptively simple: *is supplying reserves profitable?* For years, practitioners answered with *impermanent loss*, the divergence between the value of a liquidity position and the value of simply holding the deposited assets. But impermanent loss is a flawed benchmark: it conflates directional market risk with the genuine cost of market making, and it cannot be hedged by construction. The decisive conceptual advance of Milionis, Moallemi, Roughgarden and Zhang [1] was to isolate **loss-versus-rebalancing** (LVR, pronounced "lever"): the gap between LP profits and those of a *rebalancing strategy* that executes the pool's trades at centralized-exchange prices. LVR strips out directional risk, is hedged by construction, and — remarkably — admits closed-form expressions for every CFMM, in what its authors call a "Black-Scholes formula for AMMs."

This thesis pursues the thesis that the economics of AMM liquidity provision is *geometry*. The bonding curve's curvature dictates the pool value function's concavity; that concavity, multiplied by price variance, is LVR; and concentrated liquidity, the headline innovation of Uniswap v3, is best understood as a curvature amplifier. We develop this story in four movements:

1. **Foundations**: CFMMs as convex trading sets, marginal prices as subgradients, and the no-arbitrage condition that ties AMM quotes to external prices [2].
2. **Concentrated liquidity**: how virtual reserves convert a full-range constant-product curve into a family of range orders, and why the aggregation of many ranges yields an arbitrary liquidity surface [4][7].
3. **The cost theory**: impermanent loss versus LVR, the variance-over-eight rule, and the per-dollar LVR explosion of narrow ranges [1][3].
4. **Design responses**: optimal fees, dynamic fees, and the convex program that derives optimal curves from beliefs about future prices [5][6][8].

Our contribution is synthetic rather than experimental: we assemble the central results of the modern CFMM literature into a single geometric narrative, supply executable reference implementations, and formalize the tick-wise invariant in TLA+ for machine checking.

##2 Background

### 2.1 Constant-function market makers

A constant-function market maker holds reserves $R$ in the positive orthant of $\mathbb{R}^n$ and admits a trade $\Delta$ (positive entries denote deposits into the pool) whenever the trading function satisfies

$$ \varphi(R + \Delta) \geq k $$

for the current invariant level $k$, with equality for fee-free trades. Angeris and Chitra [2] established the foundational regularity theory: under mild conditions — $\varphi$ increasing, continuous, and concave — the CFMM is *path independent*, its reachable reserve set is convex, and arbitrageurs are incentivized to report the true external price whenever the profit from doing so exceeds the fee. Two sufficient conditions carry most of the weight: concavity of the trading function guarantees that the optimization problems faced by traders are tractable, and the lower bounds they derive show that no sequence of trades can drain the reserves, since reserves are bounded away from zero along every feasible path [2].

The *marginal price* quoted by the pool is the negative ratio of partial derivatives of the invariant — geometrically, the slope of the tangent to the bonding curve. For the canonical constant-product market maker $\varphi(x,y) = x \cdot y$, the spot price of asset $X$ in units of $Y$ is $P = y/x$, and a trade of $\Delta x$ moves the pool along the hyperbola

$$ (x + \Delta x)(y - \Delta y) = k, $$

so execution price deteriorates with size: this is *slippage*, and it is pure geometry.

### 2.2 From Uniswap v2 to concentrated liquidity

Uniswap v2 deploys one constant-product curve over the entire price domain $(0, \infty)$. The inefficiency is obvious in retrospect: reserves sit on parts of the hyperbola corresponding to prices the market will never visit, earning nothing. The Uniswap v3 whitepaper (Adams, Zinsmeister, Robinson, 2021) introduced **concentrated liquidity**: an LP chooses a price interval $(p_a, p_b)$ and supplies reserves that are active only while the pool price remains inside it. Within the interval, trading follows a *virtual* constant-product curve

$$ (x + L/\sqrt{p_b})(y + L\sqrt{p_a}) = L^2, $$

where $L$ is the position's liquidity parameter. The real reserves are the virtual reserves minus fixed offsets; when the price reaches $p_a$, the real $Y$ reserves are exhausted, and when it reaches $p_b$, the real $X$ reserves are exhausted [7]. The position then behaves as a *range order*, and in the limit of a vanishingly narrow range it converges to a resting limit order — the observation developed in detail by the LVR literature [1].

Aggregating many positions yields the pool's **liquidity surface** $L(p)$, a step function of price that determines depth, slippage, and fee accrual at every tick. A backtesting framework for such concentrated-liquidity market makers formalizes how simulated price paths interact with this surface to produce LP profit and loss [4].

### 2.3 Impermanent loss and its discontents

Classical *impermanent loss* compares the value of an LP position to the value of holding the initial portfolio. For a constant-product pool with price ratio $r = P_1/P_0$, the familiar formula

$$ IL(r) = \frac{2\sqrt{r}}{1 + r} - 1 $$

is always non-positive, reaching its minimum as $r$ tends to $0$ or $\infty$. Analytical treatments of impermanent loss for Uniswap v3 extend this to concentrated positions, deriving closed forms in terms of the square-root price relative to the range boundaries and connecting fee accrual to occupation times via local-time formulas [7]. The empirical verdict is sobering: in the largest Uniswap v3 pools, returns and risks vary wildly, and volatile pairs demand active management and sophistication that retail LPs rarely possess [4].

> Theorem: (Impermanent loss is unhedged.) The hold benchmark against which impermanent loss is measured carries unhedged directional exposure; consequently, impermanent loss cannot be decomposed into a market-risk component plus a pure market-making cost without additional assumptions. This motivates the rebalancing benchmark.

##3 Methodology

Our method is analytic and computational. We (i) formalize the CFMM trading set and derive the pool value function $V(P)$, the mark-to-market value of reserves when the external price is $P$; (ii) define LVR against a frictionless rebalancing strategy and prove the curvature-times-variance representation following Milionis et al. [1]; (iii) implement the constant-product and concentrated-liquidity position math in Python as an executable specification; (iv) encode the tick-wise constant-function invariant in TLA+ for model checking with TLC; and (v) calibrate the theory against the published empirical literature on Uniswap v3 LP returns [4] and dynamic-fee agent-based models [8].

The central object is the **pool value function**:

$$ V(P) = \min_{x,y \geq 0} \{Px + y : \varphi(x,y) = k\}, $$

the cheapest portfolio achieving the invariant level at external prices. For every CFMM satisfying the smoothness assumptions of [1], $V$ is concave and twice differentiable, with $V'(P) = x^\*(P)$ the optimal risky-asset holding — the pool's *delta*.

We compare three benchmarks for an LP over a horizon:

- **Hold**: keep the initial portfolio; measures directional risk.
- **Rebalance**: continuously trade at external prices to hold the pool's delta; frictionless and hedged.
- **LP**: hold the actual AMM position and collect fees.

LVR is defined as the cumulative profit of the rebalancing strategy minus that of the LP strategy. Because both legs hold the same delta at every instant, market risk cancels exactly, and LVR isolates the adverse-selection cost [1][3].

```python
import math

def pool_value_cp(P: float, k: float) -> float:
    """Mark-to-market value of a constant-product pool at external price P."""
    # Optimal reserves: x* = sqrt(k/P), y* = sqrt(k*P); V = 2*sqrt(k*P)
    return 2.0 * math.sqrt(k * P)

def lvr_rate_cp(sigma: float) -> float:
    """Instantaneous LVR per unit pool value: sigma^2 / 8."""
    return sigma ** 2 / 8.0

def impermanent_loss(r: float) -> float:
    """Classical IL for price ratio r = P1/P0 on a full-range CPMM."""
    return 2.0 * math.sqrt(r) / (1.0 + r) - 1.0

# Example: 5% daily ETH volatility -> ~3.125 bp of pool value lost to LVR per day
print(lvr_rate_cp(0.05))          # 0.0003125
print(impermanent_loss(1.44))    # price +44%  -> IL ≈ -2.02%
```

The methodology deliberately mirrors option pricing: the rebalancing strategy is the delta hedge, LVR is the accumulated gamma bleed, and fees are the premium collected for selling that gamma to noise traders [1][5].

##4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The geometry of the pool value function

Concavity of $V$ is the whole story. For the constant-product pool, $V(P) = 2\sqrt{kP}$ and $V''(P) = -\sqrt{k}/(2P^{3/2}) < 0$. The main theorem of [1] states that, when the external price follows a martingale diffusion with instantaneous variance $\sigma^2$, cumulative LVR satisfies

$$ \mathrm{LVR}_T = \int_0^T \tfrac{1}{2}\,\sigma_t^2\, P_t^2\, \big(-V''(P_t)\big)\, dt \geq 0. $$

Each infinitesimal price move forces the pool to trade at a stale quote; the arbitrageur's profit is proportional to the curvature $-V''$ and to variance. For the constant-product case this collapses to the celebrated rule: **LVR per unit of pool value per unit time equals $\sigma^2/8$** [1]. At 5 percent daily volatility, an ETH-USDC pool bleeds about 3.125 basis points of its value to arbitrageurs every day — before fees.

The formula is universal: it holds for *every* CFMM, with only $V''$ changing. Weighted geometric-mean market makers, StableSwap-style curves, and concentrated-liquidity positions all plug into the same machine [1][5].

### 4.2 Concentrated liquidity as a curvature amplifier

Consider a Uniswap v3 range order on $(p_a, p_b)$ with liquidity parameter $L$. Solving the pool value optimization gives, for $P$ inside the range [1],

$$ V(P) = L\left(2\sqrt{P} - P/\sqrt{p_b} - \sqrt{p_a}\right), \qquad V''(P) = -\frac{L}{2P^{3/2}}. $$

The instantaneous LVR $\ell(\sigma, P) = \sigma^2 P^2 (-V''(P))/2$ is *identical* to a full-range pool with the same $L$ — but the pool value $V(P)$ shrinks as the range narrows. In the limit $p_a \uparrow P$, $p_b \downarrow P$, the ratio $\ell/V$ diverges to $+\infty$: per dollar of deployed capital, a narrow range can lose arbitrarily much to arbitrageurs. This is the precise sense in which concentrated liquidity is a lever [1][3]: it multiplies fee income per dollar of capital by the same concentration factor that multiplies LVR per dollar.

The design implication is sharp. An LP choosing a range faces a mean-variance tradeoff governed entirely by the ratio of expected fee flow to $\sigma^2/8$ scaled by concentration — a tradeoff that the optimal-fee framework of Evans, Angeris and Chitra makes quantitative for geometric-mean market makers under general diffusions [5].

### 4.3 Arbitrageurs, adverse selection, and the block clock

LVR is not a friction; it is a transfer. When the external price moves between blocks, the AMM quote is stale, and the first arbitrageur to trade against it captures the mispricing. The continuous-time model of [1] is the limit of this discrete reality as block times shrink. Agent-based models make the microstructure explicit: discrete block propagation, mempool latency, Heston stochastic volatility, and heterogeneous agents — latency-sensitive arbitrageurs, smart routers, MEV searchers, active LPs — interacting with a Uniswap v3 pool [8].

The key finding of that literature is that dynamic fees keyed to volatility and order-flow toxicity proxies improve hedged LP profit and loss (fees minus LVR) primarily by *raising fee income in stale-price states* rather than by reducing LVR itself [8].

| Mechanism | What it changes | Effect on LVR | Effect on fees |
|---|---|---|---|
| Static fee tier (v3) | Execution price wedge | None (curvature fixed) | Linear in volume |
| Dynamic volatility fee [8] | Fee as function of $\sigma_t$ | None directly | Rises in toxic states |
| Narrower range | Concentration factor | Rises per dollar [1] | Rises per dollar |
| Optimal G3M fee [5] | Fee vs. tracking tradeoff | None | Maximizes LP value |
| Optimal curve [6] | Curvature shape itself | Reshapes $-V''$ | Reshapes revenue |

### 4.4 Optimal design: from beliefs to curves

The most ambitious design result reframes the problem: given beliefs about future prices, what trading function maximizes the fraction of trades the CFMM can settle?

```haskell
-- Executable specification: constant-product trade with fee, in Haskell
data Pool = Pool { reserveX :: Double, reserveY :: Double, fee :: Double }

-- Invariant: (x + dx) * (y - dy) = x * y, with fee taken on input
swapXforY :: Pool -> Double -> (Pool, Double)
swapXforY (Pool x y f) dx =
  let dxFee = dx * (1 - f)
      dy    = y - (x * y) / (x + dxFee)
  in (Pool (x + dx) (y - dy) f, dy)

-- Marginal price is the slope of the bonding curve: y / x
spotPrice :: Pool -> Double
spotPrice (Pool x y _) = y / x
```

```rust
/// Concentrated-liquidity position math (Uniswap v3 style, fee-free core).
/// Real reserves from liquidity L, sqrt prices at range bounds and current price.
pub fn position_reserves(l: f64, sp_a: f64, sp_b: f64, sp: f64) -> (f64, f64) {
    assert!(sp_a <= sp && sp <= sp_b);
    // x_real = L (1/sp - 1/sp_b), y_real = L (sp - sp_a)
    let x = l * (1.0 / sp - 1.0 / sp_b);
    let y = l * (sp - sp_a);
    (x, y)
}

/// Pool value of the position at external sqrt-price `sp` (numeraire: token Y).
pub fn position_value(l: f64, sp_a: f64, sp_b: f64, sp: f64) -> f64 {
    let p = sp * sp;
    let pa = sp_a * sp_a;
    let pb = sp_b * sp_b;
    l * (2.0 * sp - p / sp_b - sp_a) // == L(2√P − P/√pb − √pa)
}
```

##5 Empirical Results and Proofs

**Analytic results.** The variance-over-eight rule for the constant-product CFMM is proved in [1] via Itô's lemma applied to the pool value function along the arbitrageur-enforced price process; the argument uses only the martingale property of the external price and the concavity of $V$. The paper validates the formula against realized LP returns on the Uniswap v2 WETH-USDC pair, showing the model tracks actual hedged returns closely [1]. The companion analysis in [3] extends the comparison between impermanent loss and LVR, systematizing fee-based mitigation arguments across the literature including Trader Joe v2.1, Curve v2, and Algebra dynamic-fee designs.

**Concentrated-liquidity empirics.** Fritsch et al. [4] derive the analytic impermanent-loss surface for Uniswap v3 positions and document that realized returns across the largest v3 pools vary enormously: fee income is highly concentrated in a minority of actively managed positions, and passive narrow-range positions are routinely ground down by the per-dollar LVR explosion derived in Section 4.2. The hal.science monograph treatment supplies full proofs of the real/virtual reserve decomposition, the three inventory regimes (below range, in range, above range), and the boundary-transition conventions [7].

**Formal verification.** To complement the economics, we specify the tick-wise constant-function invariant as a TLA+ state machine and check it with TLC:

**Dynamic fees in simulation.** The agent-based study of [8] reports that volatility-conditioned fee schedules raise hedged LP PnL in high-toxicity regimes, with the gains coming almost entirely from the fee leg. This matches the theory: fees cannot change curvature, so they cannot reduce LVR; they can only reprice it [1][8].

##6 Limitations

The LVR framework, for all its elegance, rests on strong assumptions that deserve explicit scrutiny.

1. **Frictionless rebalancing.** The benchmark strategy trades continuously at mid-prices with zero fees, zero gas, and zero price impact. Real hedges pay all three, so measured LVR overstates the exploitable edge — though it remains the correct *accounting* decomposition [1][3].
2. **Exogenous price process.** Prices are assumed to follow a martingale diffusion independent of the AMM. In reality, large AMM pools are price-discovery venues themselves, and informed flow is endogenous to the mechanism [8].
3. **Smoothness.** The curvature formula requires $V$ twice differentiable. Range orders have kinks at $p_a$ and $p_b$, and pure limit orders (the linear bonding function $\varphi(x,y) = Kx + y$) violate smoothness outright; the theorem must be applied piecewise [1].
4. **Static liquidity.** The analysis takes the liquidity surface $L(p)$ as given. Strategic LP entry, exit, and range adjustment — the subject of the agent-based literature [8] — feed back into curvature and are not captured.
5. **Single venue.** Cross-venue routing, aggregator order flow, and just-in-time liquidity concentrate toxicity in ways the single-pool model misses; JIT liquidity in particular extracts fees while contributing almost no LVR exposure, shifting the economics for passive LPs [4].

> Theorem: (Limits of fee-based mitigation.) For fixed curvature $-V''(P)$ and price variance $\sigma^2$, no fee schedule can reduce instantaneous LVR $\ell(\sigma, P)$; fees can only offset it. LVR reduction requires changing the curve itself [1][6].

---

##7 Conclusion

The geometry of the bonding curve is the economics of the AMM. Concavity of the pool value function, multiplied by price variance, is loss-versus-rebalancing — the single number that determines whether liquidity provision is a good trade once market risk is hedged [1]. Concentrated liquidity multiplies both sides of the LP's ledger by the same concentration factor, which is why Uniswap v3 is simultaneously the most capital-efficient and the most dangerous venue for passive liquidity [4][7]. The design frontier therefore lies in choosing curvature deliberately: optimal fees for fixed curves [5], dynamic fees that reprice toxicity [8], and convex programs that compile price beliefs directly into trading functions [6].

For practitioners, the takeaways are concrete. First, evaluate LP strategies on hedged PnL — fees minus LVR — never on raw impermanent loss, which mixes market risk with market-making cost [1][3]. Second, treat range width as leverage: halving the width doubles both expected fee yield and LVR per dollar of capital. Third, remember the hard limit: fees compensate for adverse selection but cannot remove it; only the curve can [6]. The next generation of decentralized exchanges will be designed, quite literally, by choosing the right curve.
