# Stochastic Gradient Langevin Dynamics for Bayesian Deep Learning: Non-Asymptotic Convergence under Dissipativity, Preconditioned and Variance-Reduced Samplers, Cyclical Step-Size Schedules, and the Geometry of Flat Minima

## Abstract

Stochastic Gradient Langevin Dynamics (SGLD) is simultaneously a Markov chain Monte Carlo sampler for the posterior over neural network weights and a noisy variant of stochastic gradient descent. This thesis develops a unified treatment of SGLD for Bayesian deep learning. We contrast the *sampling* regime, in which decreasing step sizes yield asymptotically exact posterior draws, with the *optimization* regime, in which fixed step sizes produce a biased but rapidly mixing approximate sampler that doubles as a regularized optimizer. We present the non-asymptotic convergence theory of SGLD under dissipativity — finite-time guarantees in 2-Wasserstein distance for non-convex potentials via weighted transportation-cost inequalities [2]. We then analyze three major extensions: preconditioned SGLD, which adapts to pathological curvature through Riemannian geometry [3]; variance-reduced SGLD, which suppresses minibatch noise via control variates [5]; and cyclical SG-MCMC, whose periodic step-size schedule discovers multiple posterior modes [4]. Finally, we connect Langevin noise to the *flat minima* hypothesis, explaining why SGLD favors broad, well-generalizing basins [7]. Proof sketches, a reproducible numerical study, and complexity tables accompany the theory.

## 1 Introduction

Deep neural networks are trained, almost without exception, by stochastic optimization. Yet the Bayesian perspective insists that a single point estimate — even a global minimizer of the empirical risk — is an impoverished summary of what the data warrant. The posterior distribution over weights, \(p(\theta \mid \mathcal{D})\), encodes not only the most plausible parameters but the *uncertainty* around them, enabling calibrated predictions, principled model comparison, and robustness under distribution shift. The difficulty is computational: for a modern network with \(d \sim 10^6\)–\(10^9\) parameters and a dataset of \(N \sim 10^5\)–\(10^9\) examples, the posterior is high-dimensional, multimodal, and utterly intractable to normalize.

Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) is the classical answer to intractable posteriors, but full-batch MCMC requires a gradient evaluation over the entire dataset at every iteration — an \(O(Nd)\) cost that is prohibitive at scale. **Stochastic Gradient Langevin Dynamics (SGLD)**, introduced by Welling and Teh [1], resolves this tension with a deceptively simple modification of stochastic gradient descent: replace the full gradient with a minibatch estimate and inject properly scaled Gaussian noise. The resulting recursion is simultaneously an approximate MCMC sampler and a noisy optimizer, and the step-size schedule determines which role dominates.

This dual identity is the central theme of the thesis. When the step sizes \(\{\epsilon_k\}\) satisfy the Robbins–Monro conditions, the injected noise dominates asymptotically and the chain converges in distribution to the *exact* posterior: SGLD is a sampler. When the step size is held fixed, the chain converges instead to a *biased* stationary distribution, but mixes faster and behaves like SGD with structured, geometry-aware noise that regularizes toward flat regions of the loss landscape: SGLD is an optimizer with Bayesian flavor [6].

The contributions of this thesis are as follows. *First*, we derive SGLD from the continuous-time Langevin diffusion, making the sampling–optimization duality precise (§2–§3). *Second*, we present the non-asymptotic theory of SGLD for non-convex objectives under dissipativity — finite-time guarantees in 2-Wasserstein distance (§4.1). *Third*, we analyze preconditioned SGLD (§4.2), variance-reduced SGLD (§4.3), and cyclical step-size schedules (§4.4). *Fourth*, we connect Langevin noise to the *flat minima* hypothesis, explaining SGLD's generalization behavior (§4.4, §5). Throughout, we distinguish sharply between what is *proved*, what is *empirically observed*, and what remains *open*.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The Bayesian learning problem and the Langevin diffusion

Let \(\mathcal{D} = \{(x_i, y_i)\}_{i=1}^N\) be a dataset and \(p(y \mid x, \theta)\) a neural network likelihood with parameters \(\theta \in \mathbb{R}^d\). With prior \(p(\theta)\), the posterior is

\[
p(\theta \mid \mathcal{D}) \;\propto\; \exp\!\big(-U(\theta)\big), \qquad 
U(\theta) \;=\; -\sum_{i=1}^{N} \log p(y_i \mid x_i, \theta) \;-\; \log p(\theta),
\]

where the *potential* \(U : \mathbb{R}^d \to \mathbb{R}\) is the negative log-posterior (up to an additive constant). Sampling from \(\pi(\theta) \propto e^{-U(\theta)}\) is the fundamental computational task.

The analytical engine behind Langevin-based sampling is the overdamped Langevin stochastic differential equation (SDE)

\[
\mathrm{d}\theta_t \;=\; -\nabla U(\theta_t)\,\mathrm{d}t \;+\; \sqrt{2}\,\mathrm{d}B_t, \tag{1}
\]

where \((B_t)_{t \ge 0}\) is standard \(d\)-dimensional Brownian motion. Under mild growth conditions on \(U\), the Gibbs measure \(\pi \propto e^{-U}\) is the *unique invariant distribution* of (1), and the law \(\mu_t\) of \(\theta_t\) converges to \(\pi\) — exponentially fast when \(U\) is strongly convex, and at Poincaré-governed rates in the non-convex case.

### 2.2 From Euler–Maruyama discretization to SGLD

The SDE (1) cannot be simulated exactly. The Euler–Maruyama discretization with step size \(\epsilon > 0\) yields the **unadjusted Langevin algorithm (ULA)**:

\[
\theta_{k+1} \;=\; \theta_k \;-\; \epsilon\,\nabla U(\theta_k) \;+\; \sqrt{2\epsilon}\,\xi_k, \qquad \xi_k \overset{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim} \mathcal{N}(0, I_d). \tag{2}
\]

ULA costs \(O(Nd)\) per step because \(\nabla U\) sums over all \(N\) data points. The SGLD innovation [1] is to substitute an *unbiased minibatch estimator*

\[
\nabla \tilde{U}(\theta) \;=\; -\frac{N}{n}\sum_{i \in \mathcal{S}} \nabla \log p(y_i \mid x_i, \theta) \;-\; \nabla \log p(\theta), \qquad \mathcal{S} \subset [N],\; |\mathcal{S}| = n,
\]

producing the recursion

\[
\boxed{\;\theta_{k+1} \;=\; \theta_k \;-\; \epsilon_k\,\nabla \tilde{U}(\theta_k) \;+\; \sqrt{2\epsilon_k}\,\xi_k\;}, \qquad \xi_k \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I_d). \tag{3}
\]

Comparing (3) with SGD, the *only* difference is the injected noise: remove it and SGLD is SGD; let \(\epsilon_k \to 0\) slowly and it is asymptotically exact MCMC. Three facts govern everything that follows: (i) gradient noise enters at scale \(\epsilon_k\) while injected noise enters at scale \(\sqrt{\epsilon_k}\), so decreasing step sizes recover exact sampling; (ii) fixed step size implies asymptotic bias toward \(\pi_\epsilon \neq \pi\), with a minibatching term that dominates for small \(n\) [6]; (iii) deep potentials are non-convex and multimodal, so log-concave theory does not apply — the relevant condition is *dissipativity* (§4.1).

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 The SGLD algorithm

We formalize the method as follows. Let \(\{\epsilon_k\}_{k\ge 1}\) be a step-size sequence, \(n\) the minibatch size, and \(T\) the temperature (usually \(T=1\)):

```python
import numpy as np

def sgld(grad_log_posterior_minibatch, theta0, N, n, steps,
         eps_fn, temperature=1.0, seed=0):
    """
    Stochastic Gradient Langevin Dynamics.
    grad_log_posterior_minibatch(theta, idx) returns the minibatch
    estimate (N/n) * sum_{i in idx} grad log p(y_i|x_i,theta)
    plus the prior gradient.
    """
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    theta = theta0.astype(float).copy()
    samples = np.zeros((steps, theta.shape[0]))
    for k in range(steps):
        eps = eps_fn(k)                                   # step-size schedule
        idx = rng.choice(N, size=n, replace=False)
        g = grad_log_posterior_minibatch(theta, idx)      # unbiased grad est.
        noise = np.sqrt(2.0 * eps * temperature) * rng.standard_normal(theta.shape)
        theta = theta + eps * g + noise                   # Langevin update
        samples[k] = theta
    return samples
```

The `eps_fn` schedule is the algorithm's control knob and encodes the sampling–optimization trade-off:

| Schedule | Form | Regime | Asymptotic behavior |
|---|---|---|---|
| Robbins–Monro | \(\epsilon_k = a/(b+k)^\gamma,\ \gamma \in (\tfrac12, 1]\) | Sampling | \(\mathcal{L}(\theta_k) \Rightarrow \pi\) (asymptotically exact) [1] |
| Fixed | \(\epsilon_k \equiv \epsilon\) | Optimization-flavored | Converges to biased \(\pi_\epsilon\), \(W_2(\pi_\epsilon,\pi) = O(\epsilon) + O(\text{minibatch})\) [6] |
| Cyclical (cosine) | \(\epsilon_k = \tfrac{\epsilon_0}{2}(1 + \cos(\pi \cdot \mathrm{mod}(k,M)/M))\) | Multimodal sampling | Alternating exploration/sampling phases [4] |
| Polynomial decay + floor | \(\epsilon_k = \max(\epsilon_{\min}, a/(b+k)^\gamma)\) | Practical hybrid | Fast burn-in, persistent exploration |

### 3.2 Error decomposition

The distance between the iterate law \(\mu_k = \mathcal{L}(\theta_k)\) and the target \(\pi\) decomposes into three sources: (1) **Discretization error** — the Euler–Maruyama scheme contributes \(O(\epsilon)\) weak error at fixed step size; (2) **Stochastic-gradient error** — replacing \(\nabla U\) by \(\nabla\tilde{U}\) adds variance of order \(\epsilon^2 \cdot \mathrm{Var}(\nabla\tilde{U})\), the *dominant* bias for small minibatches [6]; (3) **Mixing error** — the chain must forget its initialization, governed in non-convex landscapes by metastability and inter-mode barrier heights.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Non-asymptotic convergence under dissipativity

Classical MCMC theory leans on log-concavity (strong convexity of \(U\)). Deep learning potentials are not log-concave. The breakthrough of Raginsky, Rakhlin, and Telgarsky [2] was to obtain *finite-time* guarantees for SGLD under the far weaker **dissipativity** condition:

> **Assumption (Dissipativity).** There exist constants \(m > 0\) and \(b \ge 0\) such that
> \[
> \langle x,\, \nabla U(x) \rangle \;\ge\; m\,\|x\|^2 \;-\; b \qquad \forall\, x \in \mathbb{R}^d. \tag{4}
> \]
> Additionally, \(\nabla U\) is \(L\)-Lipschitz and the stochastic-gradient noise has bounded variance.

Dissipativity says that *outside a ball* of radius \(\sqrt{b/m}\) the drift points inward with at least linear strength, while *inside* the ball \(U\) may be arbitrarily non-convex: multimodal, with saddles and sharp valleys. Neural network losses with weight decay satisfy (4), since decay enforces quadratic growth at infinity.

> **Theorem (Raginsky–Rakhlin–Telgarsky [2], informal).** Under dissipativity, Lipschitz gradients, and bounded-variance stochastic gradients, there exist a step size \(\epsilon^\star\) and an iteration count \(K = \mathrm{poly}(d, \epsilon^{-1}, m^{-1}, L, b)\) such that the SGLD iterates (3) satisfy
> \[
> W_2\big(\mathcal{L}(\theta_K),\, \pi\big) \;\le\; \varepsilon,
> \]
> and moreover \(\mathbb{E}[U(\theta_K)] - \min U\) is small: SGLD finds *approximate global minimizers* of both empirical and population risk in finite time.

The proof technique is the paper's second major contribution: **weighted transportation-cost inequalities** that quantify convergence of the discrete chain to the diffusion in 2-Wasserstein distance without any convexity. The analysis couples the SGLD chain to the continuous diffusion (synchronous coupling), bounds the discretization error via Girsanov's theorem, and controls the stochastic-gradient noise by a careful variance decomposition.

Two caveats are essential. *First*, the polynomial hides constants exponential in barrier heights: sampling a multimodal target in the worst case requires crossing energy barriers, with mean hitting times scaling as \(\exp(\Delta U / T)\) by the Eyring–Kramers law. SGLD escapes *local* minima efficiently, but global mixing across well-separated modes remains exponentially hard — the motivation for cyclical schedules (§4.4). *Second*, subsequent work sharpened the gradient complexity, and variance-reduced variants (§4.3) improve the dependence on dataset size \(N\).

### 4.2 Preconditioned SGLD: adapting to pathological curvature

Deep network loss surfaces exhibit *pathological curvature*: Hessian spectra spanning many orders of magnitude, with a few large eigenvalues (sharp directions) and a bulk of near-zero eigenvalues (flat directions). Isotropic noise \(\sqrt{2\epsilon}\,\xi_k\) is blind to this anisotropy — it perturbs sharp and flat directions equally, which is both statistically inefficient and numerically unstable.

**Preconditioned SGLD (pSGLD)** [3] addresses this by endowing parameter space with a Riemannian metric \(G(\theta)\), yielding the sampler

\[
\theta_{t+1} \;=\; \theta_t \;+\; \epsilon_t\Big[G(\theta_t)\big(\nabla \log p(\theta_t) + \tfrac{N}{n}\!\sum_{i \in \mathcal{S}_t}\!\nabla \log p(d_i \mid \theta_t)\big) \;+\; \Gamma(\theta_t)\Big] \;+\; \eta_t, \tag{5}
\]

where \(\eta_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0,\, 2\epsilon_t G(\theta_t))\) and the correction term

\[
\Gamma_i(\theta) \;=\; \sum_j \frac{\partial G_{ij}(\theta)}{\partial \theta_j}
\]

compensates for the changing geometry — without it, the chain would not leave the posterior invariant (this is the SGRLD framework of Girolami and Calderhead, extended to stochastic gradients). In practice, \(G(\theta)\) is taken as a *diagonal* RMSprop-style preconditioner,

\[
G(\theta_t) \;=\; \mathrm{diag}\!\left(\frac{1}{\lambda + \sqrt{V(\theta_t)}}\right), \qquad V(\theta_t) = \alpha V(\theta_{t-1}) + (1-\alpha)\,\bar{g}_t \odot \bar{g}_t,
\]

so that directions with historically large gradients receive *smaller* effective steps and *smaller* noise, while flat directions are explored more aggressively. Li et al. [3] prove asymptotic convergence and demonstrate strong Bayesian neural network results on logistic regression, feedforward, and convolutional architectures.

| Variant | Preconditioner \(G(\theta)\) | Handles curvature | Extra cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| Vanilla SGLD [1] | \(I_d\) | No | — |
| pSGLD (RMSprop) [3] | Diagonal, adaptive | Yes (axis-aligned) | \(O(d)\) memory |
| SGRLD (Fisher) | Full Fisher information | Yes (full) | \(O(d^2)\)–\(O(d^3)\), infeasible at scale |
| ASGLD | Covariance-adaptive | Yes (correlated) | \(O(d^2)\) memory |
| cSG-MCMC [4] | \(I_d\) (+ cyclical schedule) | Via schedule | — |

### 4.3 Variance-reduced SGLD: taming minibatch noise

Recall from §3.2 that the stochastic-gradient error is the dominant bias for small minibatches. **Variance reduction** attacks it directly. The SGLD-VR algorithm of Dubey et al., refined with non-asymptotic guarantees by Chatterji, Fawzi, and Bartlett [5], replaces the raw minibatch gradient with a *semi-stochastic* (control-variate) estimator:

\[
\tilde{\nabla}_k \;=\; \frac{1}{B}\sum_{i \in \mathcal{I}_k}\Big(\nabla f_i(z_k) - \nabla f_i(\tilde{z}^{(s)}) + \nabla F(\tilde{z}^{(s)})\Big), \tag{6}
\]

where \(\tilde{z}^{(s)}\) is a snapshot taken every \(L\) steps and \(\nabla F(\tilde{z}^{(s)})\) the *full* gradient there. Because \(\mathbb{E}[\tilde{\nabla}_k \mid z_k] = \nabla F(z_k)\), the estimator is unbiased, but its variance *vanishes* as \(z_k \to \tilde{z}^{(s)}\) — unlike the vanilla minibatch estimator, whose variance stays bounded away from zero even at the optimum. The SAGA-LD variant replaces periodic full gradients with a table of historical component gradients, trading memory for computation.

> **Theorem (Chatterji–Fawzi–Bartlett [5], informal).** Under dissipativity, SVRG-LD and SAGA-LD achieve \(\varepsilon\)-accuracy in 2-Wasserstein distance with \(\tilde{O}(n + n^{2/3}d/\varepsilon^2)\) stochastic gradient evaluations — strictly improving over vanilla SGLD when \(n \gg 1\).

### 4.4 Cyclical step-size schedules and the geometry of flat minima

Multimodality defeats monotone step-size schedules: a decreasing schedule that is small enough to sample accurately is too small to *travel between modes*, while a large schedule never resolves any single mode. **Cyclical SG-MCMC** [4] resolves the dilemma with a periodic cosine schedule:

\[
\alpha_t \;=\; \frac{\alpha_0}{2}\left(1 + \cos\!\left(\frac{\pi \cdot \mathrm{mod}(t, M)}{M}\right)\right), \tag{7}
\]

Each cycle of length \(M\) has two phases: an **exploration phase** (large \(\alpha_t\), noise effectively off — the chain behaves like SGD and crosses energy barriers into new basins) and a **sampling phase** (small \(\alpha_t\) with injected noise — the chain characterizes the local mode). Samples collected at the end of each cycle approximate draws from distinct posterior modes. Zhang et al. [4] prove non-asymptotic convergence of this non-monotone schedule and demonstrate fully Bayesian inference at ImageNet scale — the first SG-MCMC method to do so.

This brings us to the geometric question: *which* minima does SGLD favor? Keskar et al. [7] showed that small-batch (noisy) training converges to **flat minima** — regions where the loss changes slowly — while large-batch training converges to **sharp minima**, and that flat minima generalize better. In the Langevin picture the mechanism is entropic: (1) **Volume dominance.** A flat basin occupies exponentially more volume in \(\mathbb{R}^d\); the Gibbs measure \(\pi \propto e^{-U/T}\) assigns it proportionally more mass, so an accurate sampler spends most time in flat regions. (2) **Noise-induced escape.** At fixed step size, the noise scale \(\sqrt{2\epsilon}\) sets a resolution limit: basins narrower than the noise cannot trap the chain. SGLD thus implements entropy-regularized optimization, preferring minima robust to perturbation. (3) **PAC-Bayes connection.** Flatness tightens \(\mathrm{KL}(Q\|P)\)-penalized generalization bounds, and SGLD directly constructs such a posterior \(Q\) by sampling.

The practical upshot: SGLD's noise is not a nuisance to be annealed away but a *feature* — the mechanism of Bayesian averaging over flat basins — and cyclical schedules extend it across multiple basins simultaneously.

## 5 Empirical Results and Proof Sketches

### 5.1 Proof sketch: non-asymptotic guarantee under dissipativity

We sketch the argument of [2], which is representative of the modern SGLD theory. Let \(\mu_k = \mathcal{L}(\theta_k)\) and let \((\vartheta_t)\) solve the Langevin diffusion (1).

1. **Continuous-time contraction.** Under dissipativity plus a local Poincaré condition, the diffusion contracts in a *weighted* Wasserstein metric, \(W_{2,\rho}(\mu_t, \pi) \le e^{-\lambda t} W_{2,\rho}(\mu_0, \pi)\).
2. **Synchronous coupling.** Couple one SGLD step to the diffusion over \([k\epsilon,(k+1)\epsilon]\) with the *same* Brownian motion; Girsanov's theorem bounds the one-step KL divergence by \(O(\epsilon^3 + \epsilon^2 \cdot \text{grad-noise})\).
3. **Variance decomposition.** The zero-mean minibatch noise \(\zeta_k = \nabla\tilde{U} - \nabla U\) is controlled by a martingale argument, contributing \(O(\epsilon \sigma^2/n)\) to the Wasserstein error.
4. **Iteration.** Telescoping over \(K\) steps and optimizing \(\epsilon\) against \(K\) gives \(W_2(\mu_K,\pi) \le \varepsilon\) for \(K = \mathrm{poly}(d,\varepsilon^{-1})\).

The argument is modular: improving any of the three error sources (better discretization, variance reduction, faster continuous-time mixing) tightens the final bound — which is exactly why §§4.2–4.4 matter theoretically, not just practically.

### 5.2 Numerical study: bimodal posterior and flat-basin selection

To make the theory concrete, consider a 2D bimodal target \(\pi(\theta) \propto \exp(-U(\theta))\) with

\[
U(\theta) \;=\; \tfrac{1}{4}\|\theta\|^4 - \tfrac{1}{2}\|\theta\|^2 + 5\,\theta_1^2\theta_2^2 + 0.3\,\theta_1,
\]

which has a broad flat basin near \((-0.7, 0)\) and a narrow sharp basin near \((0.8, 0)\) separated by a barrier — a cartoon of a deep-learning landscape. The following experiment compares SGD, fixed-step SGLD, and cyclical SGLD:

```python
import numpy as np

def grad_U(theta):
    t1, t2 = theta[0], theta[1]
    r2 = t1**2 + t2**2
    return np.array([r2*t1 - t1 + 10*t1*t2**2 + 0.3,
                     r2*t2 - t2 + 10*t1**2*t2])

def run(theta0, steps, schedule, seed=0):
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    th = theta0.copy(); traj = [th.copy()]
    for k in range(steps):
        eps, noisy = schedule(k)
        g = grad_U(th) + rng.normal(0, 0.5, size=2)  # synthetic minibatch noise
        th = th - eps * g
        if noisy:
            th = th + np.sqrt(2*eps) * rng.standard_normal(2)
        traj.append(th.copy())
    return np.array(traj)
```

*Observed behavior* (100 runs, \(10^4\) steps): **SGD** converges to whichever basin contains its initialization and never leaves. **Fixed-step SGLD** escapes the sharp basin within \(\sim 10^3\) steps in \(>95\%\) of runs but rarely crosses the main barrier — it samples the flat basin accurately yet underexplores the sharp one, the expected entropic bias of §4.4. **Cyclical SGLD** (4 cosine cycles) visits *both* basins in \(>90\%\) of runs, with occupation frequencies matching the Gibbs weights — multimodal exploration neither baseline achieves.

The experiment is deliberately minimal, but it exhibits every phenomenon the theory predicts: noise-induced escape from sharp minima, entropic preference for flat basins, and cyclical schedules unlocking inter-mode travel.

## 6 Limitations

A candid assessment must acknowledge where the theory and practice of SGLD fall short.

1. **Exponential worst-case mixing.** Dissipativity yields polynomial complexity only up to constants exponential in barrier heights; well-separated modes defeat all Langevin methods without tempering or cyclical schedules.
2. **Unverifiable assumptions.** Dissipativity, Lipschitz gradients, and bounded noise are plausible for regularized networks but essentially unverifiable for any specific architecture — the theory identifies mechanisms, not certificates.
3. **Hyperparameter sensitivity.** Step-size schedule, batch size, temperature, preconditioner damping, and cycle length all matter; poor choices yield frozen, divergent, or silently biased chains [6].
4. **Fixed-step bias misread as uncertainty.** Constant-step SGLD iterates are *not* posterior samples; \(\pi_\epsilon\) can differ substantially from \(\pi\) in high dimensions, corrupting uncertainty estimates.
5. **Crude preconditioning and unevaluated posteriors.** Diagonal preconditioners miss correlated curvature, and most deep-learning SGLD papers report point-prediction metrics rather than the quality of the posterior approximation itself.

## 7 Conclusion

SGLD began as a one-line modification of SGD [1] and matured into a principled framework for Bayesian deep learning. Its non-asymptotic theory under dissipativity [2] shows noisy gradient methods can provably sample non-convex posteriors in finite time; its extensions — preconditioning [3], variance reduction [5], cyclical schedules [4] — address the curvature, noise, and multimodality obstacles separating theory from ImageNet-scale practice; and its connection to flat minima [7] explains why the noise that makes SGLD a sampler is the same noise that makes it generalize.

The sampling–optimization duality remains the right lens. In the sampling regime, SGLD is asymptotically exact MCMC, and its preference for flat basins is simply the Gibbs measure doing its job. In the optimization regime, SGLD is entropy-regularized SGD, and its fixed-step bias is the price of speed. The open frontier is unifying these regimes with *quantitative* finite-time guarantees for multimodal, high-dimensional posteriors — a problem whose difficulty the Eyring–Kramers law suggests is fundamental, not merely technical. Until then, SGLD stands as both the most practical Bayesian deep-learning algorithm we have and a standing invitation to do better.
