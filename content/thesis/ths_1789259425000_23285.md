# Predictive Coding and the Free-Energy Principle: Hierarchical Bayesian Inference, Precision-Weighted Prediction Errors, and Active Inference

## Abstract

Predictive coding proposes that the brain is a *hierarchical Bayesian inference engine*: rather than passively registering sensory input, each cortical level generates top-down predictions of the activity below, and only the unpredicted residual — the **prediction error** — propagates upward to revise beliefs. The free-energy principle extends this architecture into a unified theory of perception, action, and learning, showing that minimizing variational free energy is equivalent to performing approximate Bayesian inference under a generative model of the world. This survey traces the theory from its computational origins in Rao and Ballard's 1999 model of visual cortex, through the formal apparatus of precision-weighted prediction errors, canonical microcircuit implementations, and active inference, to modern machine-learning results linking predictive coding to backpropagation. We review empirical evidence from mismatch negativity, repetition suppression, and laminar electrophysiology, and confront the framework's sharpest critiques: the falsifiability objection, the dark-room problem, and the adaptation-versus-prediction debate. The brain, we conclude, is best understood not as a camera but as a *prediction machine* — though one whose Bayesian machinery remains only partially mapped.

## 1 Introduction

The classical view of sensory processing is feedforward and bottom-up: photons strike the retina, features are extracted in V1, assembled into objects in inferotemporal cortex, and finally handed to cognition [1]. On this picture, perception is *construction from data* — the world writes upon a blank cortical slate. Yet a persistent puzzle haunts this account: the cortex is dominated by **feedback** connections. In primate visual cortex, descending projections outnumber ascending ones roughly ten to one, a numerical asymmetry the feedforward story cannot explain.

Predictive coding inverts the classical direction. Proposed in its modern computational form by Mumford and realized by Rao and Ballard [1], the theory holds that feedback pathways carry *predictions* of lower-level activity, while feedforward pathways carry only the *residual errors* between prediction and actual activity. Perception is then not data-driven assembly but **hypothesis testing**: the brain entertains a generative model of the hidden causes of sensation, and updates it in proportion to the precision-weighted surprise it encounters.

The **free-energy principle** (FEP), developed by Karl Friston [2], radicalizes this idea. It claims that any self-organizing biological system must minimize a quantity called *variational free energy*, which bounds the surprise (negative log-evidence) of its sensory states. Perception minimizes free energy by updating beliefs; action minimizes it by changing the world; learning minimizes it by updating model parameters. Because free energy is *always* minimized by approximate Bayesian inference, the principle promises something extraordinary: a **unified account of action, perception, and learning** — and, in its most ambitious formulations, a physics of sentience itself.

Andy Clark's influential review popularized the vision of brains as *"prediction machines"* — hierarchical cascades that match incoming sensory input with top-down expectation [3]. But ambition invites scrutiny. This survey examines the mathematical core, the neural plausibility arguments, the empirical record, and the objections that threaten to deflate the program.

## 2 Background

The intellectual ancestry of predictive coding runs deeper than its 1999 christening. Three lineages converge on it.

**Helmholtz and unconscious inference.** Hermann von Helmholtz argued in the 19th century that perception is *unconscious inference*: the visual system infers the most likely distal causes of proximal stimulation, effectively inverting an implicit model of how scenes generate images. Predictive coding is Helmholtz's idea made algorithmic — the "inversion" is performed by gradient descent on prediction error rather than by explicit probabilistic computation.

**Barlow's efficient coding.** Horace Barlow's redundancy-reduction hypothesis held that sensory systems recode their inputs to minimize redundancy, since neural bandwidth is expensive. Predictive coding is a concrete implementation: if a signal is perfectly predictable from context, transmitting it wastes capacity; transmitting only the *residual* — the part that could not be predicted — achieves efficient coding automatically.

**Bayesian brain hypothesis.** By the 1990s, psychophysicists had shown that human perception behaves like an optimal Bayesian estimator: cues are combined in proportion to their reliability, and priors bias perception in measurable ways. The question was *how* neural tissue could implement Bayes. Rao and Ballard supplied the first compelling computational answer [1]: a hierarchical network in which feedback connections carry predictions of lower-level neural activities and feedforward connections carry residual errors.

> Theorem: *Under a hierarchical Gaussian generative model, gradient descent on the sum of squared precision-weighted prediction errors is exactly variational Bayesian inference under the Laplace approximation.*

The mathematics was subsequently generalized by Friston, who showed that the same scheme minimizes **variational free energy** — a bound on surprise — and hence implements approximate Bayesian inference for perception, learning, and model selection alike [2]. The framework was then mapped onto cortical microanatomy by Bastos and colleagues [4], extended to action as *active inference*, and, in a striking recent development, shown to approximate the backpropagation algorithm used to train deep neural networks [5].

## 3 Methodology

This survey synthesizes the primary theoretical papers [1][2][4][5], major review literature [3][7], and empirical studies of prediction-error responses in human electrophysiology [6][8]. Our exposition follows the standard mathematical formulation of hierarchical predictive coding under Gaussian assumptions, then examines each claimed neural correspondence and each line of evidence in turn.

### 3.1 The hierarchical generative model

Consider a hierarchy of levels indexed *i* = 0, …, *L*, where level 0 is the sensory input *s* (e.g., retinal activity) and level *L* encodes abstract causes. At each level, hidden states *v⁽ⁱ⁾* generate predictions of the level below through a (possibly nonlinear) function *g*:

*s = g⁽⁰⁾(v⁽¹⁾) + z⁽⁰⁾;   v⁽ⁱ⁾ = g⁽ⁱ⁾(v⁽ⁱ⁺¹⁾) + z⁽ⁱ⁾*

where the *z⁽ⁱ⁾* are Gaussian fluctuations with precision (inverse covariance) Π⁽ⁱ⁾. The brain's task is to invert this model: infer the hidden states *v* given sensation *s*. Exact inversion is intractable, so the brain minimizes **variational free energy** *F*, which under the Laplace approximation reduces (up to constants) to the sum of precision-weighted squared prediction errors plus a complexity penalty:

*F ≈ Σᵢ ε⁽ⁱ⁾ᵀ Π⁽ⁱ⁾ ε⁽ⁱ⁾ + ln|Π⁽ⁱ⁾| + D_KL(q‖p)*

where the prediction error at level *i* is *ε⁽ⁱ⁾ = v⁽ⁱ⁾ − g⁽ⁱ⁾(v⁽ⁱ⁺¹⁾)*. Perception becomes **gradient descent on prediction error**: beliefs *μ⁽ⁱ⁾* (the brain's estimates of *v⁽ⁱ⁾*) evolve to explain away the errors, while learning updates the parameters of *g* on slower timescales — and model selection (e.g., attention switching, structure learning) minimizes *F* over model space.

### 3.2 A minimal implementation

The core inference loop can be written in a few lines. The following Python sketch implements one level of a predictive-coding hierarchy performing gradient descent on precision-weighted prediction error:

```python
import numpy as np

def predictive_coding_step(mu, s, W, Pi, eta=0.01):
    """One gradient-descent step of predictive coding inference.
    mu : current belief about hidden causes (level i+1)
    s  : activity at the level below (level i)
    W  : generative weights mapping mu -> prediction of s
    Pi : precision (inverse variance) of the prediction error
    """
    prediction = W @ mu                      # top-down prediction g(mu)
    error = s - prediction                   # bottom-up prediction error epsilon
    dmu = eta * (W.T @ (Pi @ error))         # ascend accuracy gradient
    mu_new = mu + dmu                        # belief update explains away error
    free_energy = 0.5 * error.T @ (Pi @ error)
    return mu_new, error, free_energy

# Simulate: infer a hidden cause from noisy sensation
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
W_true = rng.normal(size=(8, 4))
mu_true = rng.normal(size=4)
s = W_true @ mu_true + 0.1 * rng.normal(size=8)
mu, Pi = np.zeros(4), np.eye(8) * 100.0      # precise sensory channel
for t in range(200):
    mu, err, F = predictive_coding_step(mu, s, W_true, Pi)
print("final free energy:", round(float(F), 4), "| residual norm:", round(float(np.linalg.norm(err)), 4))
```

Two features of this loop are worth emphasizing. First, the update uses only **locally available quantities**: the error, the precision, and the weights — no global backpropagated signal. Second, the precision Π acts as a *gain control* on the error: imprecise channels contribute little to belief updating. This gain-control role is the computational entry point for attention, as we shall see.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Variational Free Energy: The Accuracy–Complexity Trade-off

Variational free energy is the workhorse of the entire framework, and it deserves a precise statement. Let *s* denote sensory data, *ϑ* hidden causes, and *q(ϑ)* the brain's approximate posterior (the "recognition density"). Then:

*F(s, q) = E_q[−ln p(s, ϑ)] − H[q] = −ln p(s) + D_KL(q(ϑ) ‖ p(ϑ|s)) ≥ −ln p(s)*

Free energy is an **upper bound on surprise** (−ln *p(s)*, the negative log model evidence). Minimizing *F* with respect to *q* therefore does two things at once: it makes the approximate posterior *q* close to the true posterior (minimizing the KL divergence — *perception*), and it maximizes model evidence (minimizing surprise — the system's imperative to occupy unsurprising states). Rearranged, *F* = **complexity** − **accuracy**, where complexity is *D_KL(q‖p(ϑ))* (how far beliefs move from priors) and accuracy is *E_q[ln p(s|ϑ)]* (how well beliefs explain data). Every inference is thus a trade-off: fit the data, but do not overfit — the Bayesian Occam's razor, computed by neurons [2].

This decomposition explains why the framework claims such generality. Perceptual inference minimizes *F* over beliefs; learning minimizes it over parameters; attention minimizes it over precisions; and action — in active inference — minimizes *expected* free energy over policies. One functional, four timescales, one principle.

### 4.2 Precision-Weighted Prediction Errors and Attention

Raw prediction error is not enough; the brain must decide *which* errors to trust. A tiny error on a reliable channel should drive learning, while a large error on a noisy channel should be ignored. **Precision** (inverse variance) provides the weighting, and its optimization is the theory's account of *attention* [7]. Attention is not a spotlight added to perception — it *is* the gain control on prediction-error units, implemented by neuromodulatory systems:

| Neuromodulator | Computational role | Proposed neural target |
|---|---|---|
| Acetylcholine | Increases **sensory precision** (gain of ascending errors) | Superficial pyramidal cells, L2/3 |
| Dopamine | Encodes **prior precision** (confidence in predictions) | Deep pyramidal cells, L5/6 |
| Noradrenaline | Signals **volatility** (expected uncertainty) | Global gain modulation |
| Serotonin | Encodes **aversive** or opponent precision | Subcortical–cortical loops |

This mapping makes testable predictions: cholinergic enhancement should amplify early sensory responses to unpredicted stimuli, while dopaminergic manipulations should alter the weight of prior expectations. Both predictions have received empirical support in pharmacological EEG and computational psychiatry, where aberrant precision-weighting is proposed to underlie **schizophrenia** (excessively precise prediction errors → hallucinations) and **autism** (overly precise sensory channels → weak priors) [7].

### 4.3 Active Inference and Expected Free Energy

Perception alone cannot keep an organism alive; it must *act*. Active inference extends the free-energy principle from belief updating to behavior. Instead of minimizing present free energy, the agent selects **policies** (action sequences) that minimize *expected* free energy *G* — the free energy the agent expects to encounter in the future:

*G(π) = E_q[ln q(ϑ|π) − ln p(s, ϑ|π)] ≈ −(epistemic value) − (pragmatic value)*

Expected free energy decomposes into two interpretable terms: **epistemic value** (expected information gain — the drive to resolve uncertainty, i.e., curiosity and exploration) and **pragmatic value** (expected log-preference — the drive to realize preferred outcomes, i.e., exploitation). Crucially, there is no separate reward signal: "goals" are encoded as *prior preferences* over sensory states, and action fulfills predictions about preferred sensations. Reaching for a cup is recast as the brain predicting the proprioceptive consequences of grasping and then minimizing the resulting prediction error through spinal reflex arcs — perception and action become two sides of one inferential coin [2][3].

This dissolves the classical perception–action divide but introduces its own puzzles, notably the **dark-room problem**: if organisms minimize surprise, why do they not seek dark, silent rooms where sensation is perfectly predictable? The standard reply is that prior preferences — shaped by evolution and learning — assign high probability to *interesting* states (a well-fed, socially embedded organism), so minimizing expected surprise means seeking the states the phenotype expects, not sensory deprivation. Critics find this reply dangerously close to tautology, as discussed in §6.

### 4.4 Canonical Microcircuits: Neural Implementations

A theory of cortical computation must answer to cortical anatomy. Bastos and colleagues [4] derived the canonical microcircuit for predictive coding by asking which neuronal populations could implement the message-passing implied by the mathematics — and found a striking correspondence with the known laminar organization of cortex:

- **Superficial pyramidal cells (layers 2/3)** encode *prediction errors* and project feedforward (ascending) to higher areas — consistent with the observed origin of feedforward projections in supragranular layers.
- **Deep pyramidal cells (layers 5/6)** encode *predictions* (the conditional expectations *μ*) and project feedback (descending) — consistent with infragranular origins of feedback projections.
- **Inhibitory interneurons** implement the subtraction that computes errors, and their gain modulation implements precision-weighting.
- **Spectral asymmetries** fall out naturally: prediction errors propagate in the **gamma band** (30–100 Hz, bottom-up), predictions in the **beta band** (13–30 Hz, top-down) — a dissociation supported by laminar recordings and Granger-causality analyses.

Earlier, Mumford had already argued that descending deep pathways transmit "templates" fitted to lower-level data, with residuals ascending superficially — the same architecture without the name [7]. Shipp's detailed review further mapped prediction and error units onto specific cell types and laminar targets, arguing that the cortex's repeating columnar module *is* a predictive-coding circuit [7]. The correspondence is elegant; whether it is *true* — whether identified superficial cells really compute ε and deep cells μ — remains the theory's most consequential open empirical question.

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

The framework's empirical case rests on several converging lines of evidence, each mapping a predicted computational signature onto observed neural phenomena.

**Mismatch negativity (MMN).** The MMN is an event-related potential elicited when a rare "deviant" stimulus violates a regularity established by repeated "standards" — peaking 150–200 ms post-stimulus in auditory cortex. Under predictive coding, the MMN *is* a precision-weighted prediction error: standards build a precise top-down prediction that explains away ascending input (repetition suppression), while deviants generate a large error signal [6]. Dynamic causal modeling of MMN data has shown that responses to deviants are best explained by changes in both intrinsic and extrinsic connectivity consistent with prediction-error propagation through a frontotemporal hierarchy [6]. The oddball paradigm thus functions as a direct experimental probe of the theory's core variable.

**Repetition suppression.** Repeated presentation of the same stimulus attenuates neural responses across fMRI, EEG, and single-unit recordings. Predictive coding explains this as *explaining away*: as top-down predictions sharpen, the residual error shrinks. Alternative accounts invoke neural fatigue or sharpening, but predictive-coding models uniquely predict the observed *context dependence* of suppression — suppression is reduced when repetitions are unexpected, exactly as precision-weighting requires.

**Extra-classical receptive field effects.** Rao and Ballard's original triumph [1]: their hierarchical network, trained on natural images, spontaneously developed simple-cell-like receptive fields *and* reproduced end-stopping — the suppression of a V1 neuron's response when a bar extends beyond its classical receptive field. On the classical feedforward view, end-stopping required ad hoc lateral circuitry; in predictive coding, it falls out of feedback predictions "explaining away" the predictable surround.

**The backpropagation equivalence.** Perhaps the most startling recent result is computational rather than neural. Whittington and Bogacz proved that a predictive-coding network performing supervised learning with purely local Hebbian plasticity converges, under certain parameter regimes, to the weight updates of the error-backpropagation algorithm [5]. This was subsequently generalized to arbitrary computation graphs [7]. The result is doubly significant: it answers the long-standing objection that the brain cannot implement backprop (no weight transport, no global error signal), and it positions predictive coding as a biologically plausible substrate for deep learning — now an active research program in machine learning proper.

**Model comparison.** Formal Bayesian model comparison of dynamic causal models fitted to electrophysiological data has repeatedly favored predictive-coding architectures over non-hierarchical alternatives in MMN and repetition paradigms [6]. These are not mere curve fits: the models are penalized for complexity via the same free-energy bound the theory proposes the brain itself optimizes.

## 6 Limitations

The framework's ambition is also its vulnerability. Four critiques cut deepest.

**Falsifiability.** The most persistent objection — articulated forcefully in the critical literature — is that the free-energy principle is *too* accommodating. If every behavior can be redescribed as minimizing some free energy under some generative model, the principle risks becoming unfalsifiable: a mathematical tautology rather than an empirical theory. Defenders reply that specific *process theories* (particular generative models, particular precision mappings) make falsifiable predictions, and that the principle is a *normative* framework like thermodynamics, not a mechanistic hypothesis. Whether this defense succeeds depends on whether the field converges on process models precise enough to fail.

**The dark-room problem.** If organisms minimize surprise, why explore, play, or take risks? The active-inference answer — expected free energy includes epistemic value, and prior preferences favor phenotype-typical states — is formally coherent but empirically thin: prior preferences are typically *fitted* to the behavior they explain, raising the specter of circularity. Until preferences are independently constrained, the dark-room reply remains a promissory note.

**Adaptation versus prediction.** May's 2021 challenge to the predictive-coding account of MMN argues that stimulus-specific adaptation in recurrent networks can reproduce MMN-like responses without any explicit prediction machinery [8]. If adaptation suffices, the MMN evidence — the theory's flagship empirical pillar — is ambiguous. Proponents counter with paradigms (omission responses, where the brain responds to an *absent* expected stimulus) that adaptation cannot easily explain, but the debate is live and quantitative model comparison has not yet settled it.

**Implementational specificity.** The canonical microcircuit [4] is, by its authors' admission, a *perspective* — a consistency argument, not a direct observation. Laminar-resolved recordings in humans remain scarce; the mapping of ε onto superficial and μ onto deep pyramidal cells is supported by spectral and anatomical asymmetries but not by the kind of cell-type-specific causal evidence that would be decisive. Moreover, the theory's mathematics assumes Gaussianity, mean-field factorizations, and Laplace approximations whose validity in real cortical dynamics is largely untested. As one review cautions, the framework's "remarkable correspondence" with anatomy may reflect the flexibility of the mathematics as much as the structure of the brain [7].

## 7 Conclusion

Predictive coding and the free-energy principle offer the most ambitious unifying framework in contemporary neuroscience: perception as Bayesian inference, attention as precision-weighting, action as active inference, and learning as free-energy minimization — all implemented in the laminar microcircuitry of cortex, and all formally continuous with the learning algorithms of modern AI [1][2][3][4][5]. Its empirical footprint — from end-stopping to mismatch negativity to the backprop equivalence — is genuinely impressive and growing.

Yet the gap between *normative principle* and *mechanistic process theory* remains the program's central tension. The free-energy principle tells us what the brain *must* do if it is to resist disorder; predictive coding tells us one way it *might* do it. Whether the cortex actually implements variational inference on precision-weighted prediction errors — or merely behaves *as if* it does in the regimes we have probed — is an empirical question that will be settled by laminar physiology, causal perturbation, and quantitative model comparison, not by mathematical elegance.

The brain is not a camera. Whether it is a Bayesian inference engine — and what kind of engine, exactly — remains the deepest open question in theoretical neuroscience. The prediction, at least, is precise enough to be wrong. That is no small achievement.
