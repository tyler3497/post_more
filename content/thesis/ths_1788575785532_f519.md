# Convergence, Continuity, and Demand Paging: A Comparative Study of Live Migration Techniques for Virtual Machines and Containers

## Abstract

Live migration — the transfer of a running execution context across physical hosts with negligible perceptible downtime — is a foundational capability of modern cloud infrastructure, enabling fault management, load balancing, and non-disruptive maintenance. This thesis surveys and compares the principal memory-migration strategies: pre-copy, post-copy, and hybrid schemes, with attention to the convergence condition that governs whether an iterative pre-copy phase can terminate at all. It then extends the analysis to container live migration via CRIU checkpoint/restore, covering soft-dirty page tracking, pre-dump iterations, the page-server architecture, and userfaultfd-driven post-copy (lazy) restore with the lazy-pages daemon. We examine hardware- and transport-level accelerators — RDMA, multi-FD parallel page transfer, and post-copy preemption — and the orchestration layer exemplified by KubeVirt's live migration of VirtualMachineInstances in Kubernetes. Formal models of downtime and total migration time are derived, key convergence theorems are stated, and empirical results from the literature (downtimes under 165 ms for pre-copy workloads, sub-second lazy restore independent of memory footprint) are reconciled with theory. Limitations — source-host dependence in post-copy, RDMA cost and network topology constraints, and container state that cannot be checkpointed — are treated honestly, and open research directions are identified.

## 1. Introduction

The ability to relocate a running computation from one physical host to another without terminating it is among the most consequential operations in contemporary systems infrastructure. First industrialized in VMware's VMotion and systematized in the academic literature by Clark *et al.* in their seminal NSDI 2005 paper on Xen [1], **live migration** underpins three pillars of datacenter operation: *transparent fault management* (evacuating a host whose hardware is failing), *load balancing* (smoothing utilization across a fleet), and *maintenance without service interruption* (patching hypervisors while workloads keep running).

At its core, live migration is a race between two rates: the guest dirties memory at rate $R$ (pages/s) while the migrator ships pages at throughput $B$ (pages/s). If $B > R$ comfortably, iterative transfer converges; otherwise the migration churns or ends with a catastrophic pause. Every migration design is a different answer to one question: **how do we bound the stop-the-world downtime while keeping total migration time and network cost tractable?**

This thesis compares **pre-copy**, **post-copy**, and **hybrid** schemes on equal footing, derives the convergence condition separating viable pre-copy from pathological cases, and follows the field's second act — *container* migration — where CRIU (Checkpoint/Restore In Userspace) and Linux `userfaultfd` play the roles once played by shadow page tables and hypervisor dirty bitmaps. We also examine transport acceleration (RDMA, multifd, post-copy preemption) and orchestration (KubeVirt on Kubernetes).

Contributions: (1) a formal pre-copy convergence model with theorem and proof sketch; (2) an operational account of CRIU's pre-dump, page-server, and lazy-restore pipeline; (3) a comparison of post-copy fault mechanics in QEMU vs. CRIU's lazy-pages daemon; (4) candid limitations and open problems.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 Virtual machines and the memory-transfer problem

A VM comprises CPU state, memory, and storage. CPU/device state is small (kilobytes to megabytes) and can be shipped during a brief pause; memory dominates. The naive *stop-and-copy* suspends the VM and ships all $M$ pages: downtime equals $M/B$ — 32 seconds for a 512 MB VM at 128 Mbit/s [1], unacceptable for interactive services.

The industry-standard remedy is **pre-copy** [1]: while the VM keeps running on the source, memory is copied in rounds. Round 0 ships all $M$ pages; in each subsequent round $i$, only pages dirtied during round $i-1$ are re-shipped. Dirty pages are tracked by the hypervisor — in Xen via *shadow page tables* that trap writes, in KVM via the hardware dirty bit in EPT/NPT entries or the `KVM_GET_DIRTY_LOG` ioctl. When the set of remaining dirty pages is small enough (the *writable working set*, WWS, of Clark *et al.*), the VM is briefly suspended and the residue plus CPU state transferred. Downtime collapses from tens of seconds to tens or hundreds of milliseconds.

### 2.2 The convergence condition

Pre-copy is not free. Each round re-sends pages, so total network traffic is a multiple of $M$. Worse, it may not converge. Let $r_i$ be the number of pages dirtied during round $i$ and let round $i$ take time $t_i = r_i / B$ to ship (with $r_0 = M$). The recurrence is:

$$r_{i+1} = R t_i = R r_i / B = r_i (R/B)$$

where $R$ is the (assumed steady) dirty rate. If $R/B < 1$, the sequence contracts geometrically and terminates after $O(\log M)$ rounds; if $R/B \ge 1$, rounds never shrink — the migration churns until aborted or forced into a catastrophic stop-and-copy. This inequality is the *convergence condition*; nearly all pre-copy engineering exists to cope with it.

> **Theorem (Pre-copy convergence).** Let $M$ be the guest memory size in pages, $R$ the sustained page-dirty rate, $B$ the migration throughput in pages per unit time, and $D_{max}$ the downtime budget. If $R < B$, then pre-copy terminates with final-round size $r_n < D_{max} B$ after $n \le \lceil \log(M / (D_{max} B)) / \log(B/R) \rceil$ rounds, and total transferred data is bounded by $M B / (B - R)$. If $R \ge B$, no finite number of rounds reduces the dirty set.

*Proof sketch.* With constant $R$, $r_i = M(R/B)^i$; the choice of $n$ with $r_n/B \le D_{max}$ gives the bound, and $\sum_i r_i = MB/(B-R)$. If $R \ge B$, $r_{i+1} \ge r_i$ monotonically.

In practice $R$ is bursty: Clark *et al.* measured the WWS of SPEC CINT2000, kernel compiles, OLTP, SPECweb99, and a Quake 3 server, finding most workloads migratable but `gap` (consistently high dirty rate) pathological [1]. Migration policy must *observe* $R$ online and adapt.

### 2.3 Post-copy: trading downtime for dependency

Hines, Deshpande, and Gopalan (VEE 2009) proposed the mirror image: **post-copy** [8]. Suspend the VM *first*, ship only CPU/device state, resume on the destination immediately, then pull pages on demand — faulted pages fetched from the source as the guest touches them, the rest streamed in the background. Total time and traffic are *lower* than pre-copy (each page sent at most once), and downtime is minimal. The price: (i) every first touch of a missing page costs a network round trip (a *fault storm* after resume), and (ii) the source must stay alive until the last page lands — source failure in this window loses the VM.

### 2.4 Containers and CRIU

Containers share the host kernel, so "migration" means checkpointing a *process tree* — memory, files, sockets, namespaces, cgroups — and restoring it elsewhere. **CRIU** freezes the tree (freezer cgroup), injects *parasite code* into victims' address spaces to dump externally-unreadable state, and serializes everything into image files [3]. For *live* migration, CRIU mirrors pre-copy with **pre-dump** iterations using the soft-dirty bit (or `PAGEMAP_SCAN`), dumping only pages dirtied since the last pre-dump to shrink the final frozen window [4]. For post-copy semantics, `--lazy-pages` dumps *without* writing pages; on restore, the **lazy-pages daemon** registers the address space with `userfaultfd` and injects pages on demand via `UFFDIO_COPY`, fetched from the source's **page server** [5][6].

## 3. Methodology

Our method is comparative-analytic: we reconstruct each technique's operational semantics from primary sources [1][2][3][4][5][7] and evaluate against a common metric set:

| Metric | Definition | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| **Downtime** | Wall-clock interval the guest is fully suspended | Directly visible to clients; SLA-bound |
| **Total migration time** | Start of migration to guest fully resident on destination | Bounds the maintenance window |
| **Network traffic** | Total bytes shipped, as multiple of $M$ | Cost; congestion imposed on the fabric |
| **Performance degradation** | Guest slowdown during/after migration | Throughput and tail-latency impact |
| **Failure exposure** | Window in which source failure loses state | Reliability engineering |
| **Convergence guarantee** | Whether termination is assured | Operational predictability |

We model pre-copy analytically, describe post-copy and CRIU mechanisms operationally with reference to actual code paths, and reconcile published measurements with the models (*analytic replication*). Scope: storage migration is excluded (shared storage assumed [1]); the network is a single bottleneck link of capacity $B$, except where RDMA changes the picture (Section 4.4).

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Pre-copy engineering: WWS, dynamic rate limiting, and stopping rules

Clark *et al.*'s Xen implementation [1] introduced two refinements that remain the template for pre-copy systems:

1. **Writable working set (WWS) tracking.** Repeatedly-dirtied pages are identified as the WWS and deferred to the final stop-and-copy round, converting the geometric tail from hot-page churn into a bounded one-shot transfer. The WWS is estimated by sampling the dirty bitmap at 50 ms granularity over an 8 s window.
2. **Dynamic rate limiting.** The transfer rate for round $i+1$ tracks the dirty rate observed in round $i$: more bandwidth when the guest dirties faster (up to a ceiling), backoff when quiet. Their SPECweb99 experiment — 800 MB VM, 350 clients — migrated with **165 ms downtime** and 1.20x overhead, the limiter visibly tracking the dirty rate [1].

Modern QEMU/KVM exposes `max-bandwidth`, `downtime-limit`, and auto-converge via `migrate-set-parameters` — the latter *throttles guest vCPUs* when $R$ approaches $B$, manufacturing $R < B$ by degrading the guest when the theorem's precondition fails. Stopping rules — dirty-page threshold, iteration caps, diminishing-returns detection — are heuristic approximations of the bound under non-stationary $R$.

### 4.2 Post-copy in QEMU: userfaultfd, the fault thread, and preemption

In QEMU's post-copy [2], the destination maps guest RAM and registers it with **userfaultfd** (`UFFDIO_REGISTER`). A guest access to a missing page traps; the kernel parks the faulting vCPU and delivers a `uffd_msg` to QEMU's **fault thread** (`postcopy_ram_fault_thread`). The thread requests the page from the source; on arrival, `UFFDIO_COPY` installs it and wakes the vCPU, while a background stream ships remaining pages in address order.

Two refinements deserve attention:

- **Post-copy preemption** (QEMU 8.0+) [2]: fault-requested pages travel on a *separate preempt channel* instead of queueing behind the bulk stream — without it, latency-sensitive faults serialize behind megabytes of cold pages.
- **Huge pages and userfaultfd constraints**: works on `tmpfs`/`/dev/shm` and `hugetlbfs`, though the kernel lacks `MADV_DONTNEED` equivalents for hugetlbfs in some configurations [2].

The vhost-user integration extends the pattern to external processes (e.g., DPDK): the client passes its own userfaultfd plus an address-mapping table to QEMU, which services the client's faults [2].

Downtime is the CPU/device-state transfer (milliseconds); total time is about $M/B$ plus fault overhead; traffic is about $M$ (optimal). Degradation is a *spike*: after resume, hot pages are missing, so latency balloons until the working set materializes. Hines *et al.* showed post-copy wins decisively on write-intensive workloads — lower traffic, time, and *eviction time* — exactly where pre-copy's convergence condition is weakest [8].

### 4.3 CRIU: pre-dump, page server, and lazy restore for containers

CRIU decomposes container live migration into stages paralleling the VM story [3][4]:

**Pre-dump (pre-copy for processes).** `criu pre-dump -t PID` uses the soft-dirty bit to dump only pages changed since the previous dump, *without long freezes*. Repeated pre-dumps shrink the final dump's delta — pre-dump optimization is framed as minimizing frozen time [4], the container analogue of downtime.

**Page server.** During migration, pages stream to a `criu page-server` on the destination rather than to disk images, avoiding disk I/O on both ends [4] — the network data plane of CRIU migration.

**Lazy restore (post-copy for processes).** `criu dump --lazy-pages` checkpoints everything *except* memory; on the destination, `criu restore` recreates the address-space layout (VMAs from `mm-*.img`, file mappings re-`mmap`ed) and registers regions with `userfaultfd`. The **lazy-pages daemon** services `UFFD_EVENT_PAGEFAULT`: fetching each faulted page from the page server and injecting it via `UFFDIO_COPY`/`UFFDIO_ZEROPAGE`, after which the kernel resumes the thread [5]. Non-cooperative guests that `fork()`/`mremap()`/`munmap()` during lazy restore are handled via `UFFD_FEATURE_EVENT_FORK/REMAP/REMOVE` notifications [5].

> **Theorem (Lazy-restore downtime bound).** For a container of memory size $M$ restored with `--lazy-pages`, the initial downtime is $O(S)$ where $S$ is the size of non-memory state (VMAs, file descriptors, namespaces), *independent of $M$*. Formally, $T_{down} = T_{map} + T_{ufd} + T_{resume}$, each bounded by metadata size, not by memory footprint.

Hence lazy restore resumes "in milliseconds, regardless of total memory size" [5] — post-copy's structural advantage, now for processes — at the same cost: **source dependence** until all pages land, plus post-restore latency jitter.

### 4.4 Transport acceleration: RDMA, multifd, and the bandwidth lever

Recall the convergence theorem: everything hinges on $B$ relative to $R$. Raising $B$ is the most direct lever, and two mechanisms do it:

- **RDMA-assisted migration.** QEMU supports RDMA (`rdma:` URIs over Infiniband/RoCE). Kernel-bypass, zero-copy shipment raises effective $B$ by an order of magnitude and slashes CPU overhead — widening the $R < B$ regime to otherwise unmigratable workloads and shrinking $M/B$ proportionally.
- **Multifd (parallel page transfer).** QEMU's `multifd` stripes transfer across multiple connections/threads. With `xbzrle` compression (shipping only page *deltas* — a WWS analogue), it attacks both $B$ and bytes-per-page.

Throughput accounting in code:

```rust
// Migration feasibility check: the convergence condition in code
fn migration_feasible(m_pages: u64, dirty_rate_pps: f64, bw_pps: f64,
                      downtime_budget_s: f64) -> Result<u32, &'static str> {
    if dirty_rate_pps >= bw_pps {
        return Err("divergent: dirty rate exceeds migration throughput");
    }
    let ratio = dirty_rate_pps / bw_pps;
    // rounds n s.t. m_pages * ratio^n / bw_pps <= downtime_budget_s
    let n = ((m_pages as f64 / (downtime_budget_s * bw_pps)).ln()
             / (1.0 / ratio).ln()).ceil() as u32;
    Ok(n)
}
```

The table below summarizes the transport options:

| Transport | Effective $B$ | CPU cost | Requirements | Best for |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| TCP (single fd) | Baseline | High (kernel stack, copies) | None | Default, heterogeneous fabrics |
| TCP multifd + xbzrle | 2-8x baseline | Medium (parallel compress) | QEMU 3.x+, `multifd` cap | High-dirty-rate pre-copy |
| RDMA | 10x+ on IB/RoCE | Near zero (kernel bypass) | Infiniband/RoCE fabric, `rdma:` URI | Large-memory, latency-sensitive guests |
| Post-copy + preempt channel | Same $B$, lower fault latency | Low | QEMU 8.0+, `postcopy-preempt` cap | Tail-latency-sensitive post-copy |

### 4.5 Hybrid migration and orchestration: KubeVirt

**Hybrid** schemes sequence the strategies: pre-copy ships the bulk while the guest runs, then post-copy handles the hot residue — bounding traffic and downtime. QEMU enables `postcopy-ram` *during* pre-copy (`migrate-start-postcopy`); hybrid-copy algorithms add *prediction* (e.g., Markov models of page dirtiness) to decide which pages to defer [9]. The hybrid is the engineer's answer to the theorem: pre-copy where it converges, post-copy where it does not.

**KubeVirt** turns live migration into Kubernetes-native declarative API [7]: a `VirtualMachineInstanceMigration` object (or `virtctl migrate <vmi>`) migrates the `virt-launcher` pod's QEMU process to another node. Key concerns:

- **Migratability gating**: the VMI's `LiveMigratable` condition is computed at start, largely from volume access modes — only `ReadWriteMany` volumes permit migration [7].
- **Eviction-driven migration**: `evictionStrategy: LiveMigrate` plus a PodDisruptionBudget makes `kubectl drain` migrate rather than kill workloads [7].
- **Progress and timeout policy**: `completionTimeoutPerGiB` and `progressTimeout` bound migration runtime and stalls — operational encodings of the convergence concern; a non-converging migration is cancelled, not left to churn.
- **Decentralized (cross-cluster) migration** pairs source/target migration resources across clusters [7].

KubeVirt closes the loop opened by Clark *et al.*: migration becomes a reconciled control-plane object with timeouts, conditions, and disruption budgets.

## 5. Empirical Results and Formal Analysis

**Pre-copy (Xen [1]).** 512 MB VM: stop-and-copy downtime of 32 s at 128 Mbit/s; one pre-copy round cuts this 4x in the worst case (SPECweb startup), 16x+ typically (kernel compile, OLTP). Headline: 800 MB VM, SPECweb99, 350 clients — **165 ms downtime**, 1.20x traffic [1]. Exactly the theorem's prediction for $R/B \ll 1$: downtime $\approx r_n/B$, traffic $\approx M(1+\epsilon)$.

**Post-copy (Hines *et al.* [8]).** For write-intensive workloads, post-copy beats pre-copy on traffic, total time, and *eviction time* — the regime where pre-copy diverges. Downtime is near-instantaneous. Debit: responsiveness degrades post-resume until the working set faults in, and a memory-pressured destination inflates eviction time ~6.3x for *both* techniques [8] — the receiver's memory subsystem is part of $B$.

**CRIU lazy restore [5].** Downtime is metadata-bound (milliseconds, independent of $M$); cost moves to post-restore tail latency. The page server keeps disk out of the hot path. Pre-dumps give containers the pre-copy analogue with identical convergence structure.

**Analytic synthesis.** Let $T_{pre}(n) = \sum_{i=0}^{n} r_i/B + r_{n+1}/B_{stop}$, $T_{post} = S/B + M/B + F L_{fault}$ ($S$ = CPU/device state, $F$ = faulted pages, $L_{fault}$ = per-fault RTT). Pre-copy minimizes $F$ at traffic cost $1/(1-R/B)$; post-copy minimizes traffic (~1.0x) at cost $F L_{fault}$ in the post-resume window. Hybrid minimizes $\max(T_{down})$ under a traffic budget. No scheme dominates on all six Section-3 metrics — the choice is a Pareto point in $(R, B, L_{fault})$.

## 6. Limitations

1. **Post-copy's source dependence.** Until the last page lands, the source is a single point of failure; a crash means unrecoverable guest loss. Pre-copy's dual property — the destination holds only a stale replica until cutover — makes source failure harmless. Hybrid shortens but does not eliminate the window.
2. **Convergence is workload-adversarial.** Sustained $R \ge B$ (write-storm databases, HPC stencils) defeats pre-copy. Auto-converge throttling, xbzrle, and RDMA buy headroom but cost performance or money. There is no free convergence.
3. **CRIU cannot checkpoint everything.** Device states, non-repairable connections, cross-namespace SysV IPC, in-flight RDMA queue pairs — such containers are simply not migratable, often discovered empirically [3].
4. **userfaultfd privilege and portability.** Unprivileged use needs the `vm.unprivileged_userfaultfd` sysctl (libvirt enables it on demand); huge-page guests face hugetlbfs kernel gaps [2].
5. **RDMA's deployment tax.** End-to-end lossless fabric (InfiniBand/RoCE with PFC/ECN) rules RDMA out in many cloud and edge settings, where multifd-over-TCP is the ceiling.
6. **Security surface.** Migration streams carry raw guest memory. QEMU's TLS migration is opt-in; unencrypted channels — or CRIU's page server — on shared fabrics are catastrophic exposures.
7. **KubeVirt's preconditions.** `ReadWriteMany` storage, no device passthrough, bounded per-node migration parallelism — each narrows migratability in practice [7].

## 7. Conclusion

Two decades after Clark *et al.* hit 60–165 ms downtimes with Xen pre-copy [1], the central tension is unchanged: *downtime, total time, traffic, and failure exposure cannot be minimized simultaneously*, and $R/B$ decides which techniques are viable. Pre-copy stays the default — safe, graceful degradation; post-copy wins where pre-copy diverges, at the price of a fragile window and a fault storm; hybrids and CRIU's lazy restore extend the ideas to processes. RDMA, multifd, and preempt channels move the frontier by raising $B$; KubeVirt moves it operationally by making migration declarative, timeout-bounded, and eviction-integrated.

Open directions: learned dirty-page predictors beating the WWS heuristic, erasure-coded post-copy to soften source dependence, CRIU support for un-checkpointable state (RDMA, GPU contexts), and machine-checked verification of the migration protocols — the fault-thread/background-stream/guest concurrency is subtle enough to deserve it. The migration problem is not solved; it has been promoted from hypervisor feature to distributed-systems discipline.
