# Equality Saturation and E-Graphs: From Congruence Closure to Superoptimization

## Abstract

Equality saturation is an approach to program optimization and synthesis that sidesteps the *phase-ordering problem* of classical compilers: rather than destructively rewriting a program one optimization at a time, it accumulates equalities between program fragments in a compact data structure — the **e-graph** — until no new equivalences can be derived, and only then extracts the best program according to a global cost model. E-graphs descend from the congruence closure algorithms of Nelson and Oppen [3] and of Downey, Sethi, and Tarjan, which decide the quantifier-free theory of equality with uninterpreted functions in near-linear time. This article traces that lineage: from congruence closure decision procedures, through Tate et al.'s equality saturation framework for Java optimization and translation validation [1], to Willsey et al.'s *egg* library [2], whose deferred rebuilding and e-class analyses made equality saturation fast and extensible. We develop the core machinery — e-graph representation and invariants, the rebuild algorithm, e-matching modulo equivalence, rewrite application, and cost-driven extraction — and examine applications from floating-point accuracy improvement to superoptimization, closing with complexity results, soundness arguments, and fundamental limitations.

---

## 1. Introduction

Classical optimizing compilers are organized as pipelines of *passes*: constant folding, inlining, loop unrolling, common subexpression elimination, and dozens more, each destructively transforming the intermediate representation before handing it to the next. This architecture suffers from two intertwined pathologies. First, the **phase-ordering problem**: the final code quality depends sensitively on the order in which passes run, because each pass can enable or disable opportunities for later ones, and no universally good order exists. Second, **local profitability heuristics**: each pass decides whether to fire based only on local information, unable to account for transformations that later passes might perform [1].

Equality saturation proposes a radical restructuring. Optimizations are recast as *equality analyses* that **add** equality information to a shared intermediate representation instead of replacing code. The optimizer repeatedly applies these analyses, inferring equivalences between program fragments until the representation is *saturated* — closed under all applicable equalities — at which point it compactly encodes a vast space of equivalent programs. A single global *profitability heuristic* then selects the final program. Ordering disappears as a concern because nothing is ever destroyed; heuristics become global because they choose among fully explored alternatives [1].

The data structure that makes this feasible is the **e-graph** (equivalence graph). An e-graph represents a *congruence relation* — an equivalence relation closed under function application — over a set of terms, using maximal structural sharing so that exponentially many terms occupy polynomial space. E-graphs were developed in the late 1970s for automated theorem proving [3], but equality saturation repurposes them as the substrate for rewrite-driven optimization and synthesis [2].

This article develops the subject from first principles to the research frontier: background on congruence closure and rewriting (§2), the e-graph and saturation loop (§3), a deep dive into invariants, rebuilding, e-matching, and extraction (§4), empirical results and soundness (§5), and fundamental limitations (§6).

## 2. Background

### 2.1 Congruence closure: deciding equality

The quantifier-free theory of equality with uninterpreted function symbols asks whether a formula such as

$$x = y \;\land\; f(x) = a \;\land\; f(y) \neq a$$

is satisfiable. In 1980, Nelson and Oppen showed that this reduces to computing the **congruence closure** of a relation on a term graph: the smallest congruence relation containing a given set of equalities [3]. Their algorithm represents each subterm as a vertex, merges vertices connected by asserted equalities using union-find, and propagates *congruence*: if $f(s_1,\dots,s_n)$ and $f(t_1,\dots,t_n)$ are vertices with each $s_i$ equivalent to $t_i$, the two applications are congruent and must be merged. Downey, Sethi, and Tarjan gave an $O(n \log n)$-average-time variant using hash tables keyed on the equivalence classes of successors, framing congruence closure as a generalization of common subexpression elimination [3]. Nieuwenhuis and Oliveras later extended the framework to produce checkable proofs of each derived equality [4] — a capability that foreshadows translation validation (§5).

> **Definition (Congruence relation).** An equivalence relation $\cong$ on terms is a *congruence* if $s_i \cong t_i$ for all $i$ implies $f(s_1,\dots,s_n) \cong f(t_1,\dots,t_n)$ for every function symbol $f$.

The congruence closure of a set of ground equalities $E$ is the smallest congruence containing $E$. Deciding $E \models s = t$ then amounts to checking whether $s$ and $t$ land in the same block of the closure — exactly the query an e-graph answers structurally.

### 2.2 Term rewriting: confluence, termination, completion

*Term rewriting* studies directed equalities $l \to r$ applied by substituting instances of $l$ with instances of $r$. A rewrite system is **terminating** if no infinite rewrite sequence exists, and **confluent** (Church–Rosser) if divergent rewrite paths can always be rejoined — in which case every term has a unique normal form. Knuth–Bendix completion attempts to transform an equational theory into a confluent, terminating system by orienting equations and resolving *critical pairs*.

Classical rewriting is *destructive*: applying $l \to r$ discards the redex. This is precisely what forces compilers into the phase-ordering trap — choosing *which* rewrite to apply *when* determines the outcome, and a wrong choice is irreversible. Equality saturation keeps both sides: applying a rewrite *adds* the instantiated right-hand side to the e-graph and *unions* it with the matched e-class, so the e-graph monotonically accumulates the deductive closure of the rewrite system instead of committing to a single rewrite path [1][2].

## 3. Methodology

### 3.1 The e-graph

An **e-graph** compactly represents many terms and an equivalence relation over them. Its two kinds of nodes are:

- **E-nodes**: operator applications $f(c_1, \dots, c_n)$, where each child $c_i$ is an *e-class id* rather than a concrete subterm. An e-node is *hash-consed*: structurally identical e-nodes are stored once.
- **E-classes**: sets of e-nodes (and transitively, the terms they represent) known to be equivalent. Each e-class has a canonical id maintained by a union-find structure.

A term $t$ is *represented* by an e-class $c$ if $t$ can be built by choosing, at each position, any e-node of the class named there. Because choices multiply, an e-graph with $n$ e-classes can represent $2^{\Omega(n)}$ distinct terms [2].

Two invariants define a well-formed e-graph:

1. **Hash-cons invariant**: no two e-nodes in the graph are structurally identical *up to canonical e-class ids* (the e-node map is injective).
2. **Congruence invariant**: if two e-nodes $f(c_1,\dots,c_n)$ and $f(d_1,\dots,d_n)$ satisfy $c_i \equiv d_i$ for all $i$, they reside in the same e-class.

Together these say the e-graph is exactly the congruence closure of the equalities asserted so far.

### 3.2 The equality saturation loop

Equality saturation iterates two phases [2]:

```python
def equality_saturation(expr, rewrites):
    egraph = initial_egraph(expr)
    while not egraph.is_saturated_or_timeout():
        matches = []
        # read-only phase: invariants hold, search all matches
        for rw in rewrites:
            for (subst, eclass) in egraph.ematch(rw.lhs):
                matches.append((rw, subst, eclass))
        # write-only phase: add terms, union classes
        for (rw, subst, eclass) in matches:
            eclass2 = egraph.add(rw.rhs.subst(subst))
            egraph.merge(eclass, eclass2)
        # restore invariants once per iteration
        egraph.rebuild()
    return egraph.extract_best()
```

Three operations carry the weight:

- **`ematch`** (e-matching): finds all substitutions $\sigma$ such that the pattern $l\sigma$ is represented in some e-class, *modulo the equivalences already in the graph*.
- **`merge`**: unions two e-classes (union-find), recording the union on a worklist for later congruence propagation.
- **`rebuild`**: restores the hash-cons and congruence invariants after a batch of merges, amortizing the cost of invariant maintenance (§4.2).

### 3.3 Extraction

Once saturated (or timed out), the e-graph encodes the full space of equivalent programs. **Extraction** selects the best term according to a *cost function* $k$, typically computed bottom-up: the cost of an e-node is a function of its operator and the costs of its children, and the cost of an e-class is the minimum over its e-nodes. A simple greedy fixed-point computes near-optimal terms for *local* (compositional) cost functions; *e-class analyses* (§4.5) generalize this to dataflow-like properties computed over the e-graph [2].

In Rust, the *egg* library [2][6] expresses this workflow directly:

```rust
use egg::{define_language, rewrite, EGraph, Id, RecExpr, Runner};

define_language! {
    enum Arith {
        Num(i32),
        "+" = Add([Id; 2]),
        "*" = Mul([Id; 2]),
    }
}

let rules = &[
    rewrite!("commute-mul"; "(* ?a ?b)" => "(* ?b ?a)"),
    rewrite!("distribute";  "(* ?a (+ ?b ?c))" => "(+ (* ?a ?b) (* ?a ?c))"),
    rewrite!("fold-add";     "(+ (Num ?a) (Num ?b))" => "(Num (+ ?a ?b))"),
];

let expr: RecExpr<Arith> = "(+ (* 2 x) (* 3 x))".parse().unwrap();
let runner = Runner::default().with_expr(&expr).run(rules);
// runner.egraph now represents (+ (* 2 x) (* 3 x)) = (* 5 x) = ...
```

After saturation, the e-class of the input contains `(* 5 x)`, and extraction with a cost model penalizing multiplications yields the strength-reduced form.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 E-Graph Representation and Invariants

Concretely, an e-graph is three structures in lockstep:

1. A **union-find** over e-class ids with path compression and union by rank, giving amortized inverse-Ackermann `find`.
2. An **e-node map** (hash-cons table) from canonical e-nodes $f(\text{find}(c_1),\dots,\text{find}(c_n))$ to the e-class containing them — enforcing the hash-cons invariant.
3. For each e-class, the set of **parent** e-nodes that reference it, enabling upward congruence propagation: when two classes merge, only their parents can become newly congruent.

In Haskell-flavored pseudocode, the shape is:

```haskell
type EClassId = Int
data ENode    = ENode Symbol [EClassId]        deriving (Eq, Ord, Show)
data EClass   = EClass
  { enodes  :: Set ENode                        -- equivalent operators
  , parents :: Set (ENode, EClassId)             -- upward uses
  , payload :: AnalysisData                      -- e-class analysis state
  }
```

> **Theorem (Representation adequacy).** *In an e-graph satisfying the hash-cons and congruence invariants, two ground terms are represented in the same e-class iff they are equal in the congruence closure of the asserted equalities.*

The parent sets are the subtle part: they make congruence closure *incremental*. Merging classes $c \equiv d$ only requires re-examining e-nodes that mention $c$ or $d$ — typically a small fraction of the graph — rather than re-scanning everything [2][3].

### 4.2 Congruence Closure and Deferred Rebuilding

Traditional e-graph implementations restore the congruence invariant **eagerly**: after every union, they immediately re-canonicalize affected e-nodes and merge newly congruent pairs. In equality saturation this is pathological. A single saturation iteration performs thousands of merges; eager repair re-canonicalizes the same e-nodes repeatedly as their classes merge again and again, yielding quadratic behavior on saturation workloads [2].

*egg*'s answer is **rebuilding**: defer all invariant restoration to a single pass at the end of each iteration [2][5]:

```
rebuild():
    worklist = all classes touched by merges this iteration (deduplicated)
    while worklist non-empty:
        c = pop(worklist)
        for (n, p) in parents(c):          # n = f(..., c, ...)
            n' = canonicalize(n)            # re-resolve child class ids
            if n' already in enode-map:
                merge(p, enode-map[n'])    # congruent: union, enqueue
                # (deduplication: each class enters the worklist once)
            else:
                repair enode-map entry
```

Because each e-class is processed at most once per rebuild, rebuilding is *amortized near-linear* in the size of the change — an asymptotic improvement over eager repair. The read phase of e-matching then always runs against a fully canonical graph, which is also what makes *egg*'s saturation loop independent of rewrite ordering [2].

The TLA⁺ essence of the deferred-repair discipline is a classic *stabilization* pattern:

```tla
--------------------------- MODULE Rebuild ---------------------------
EXTENDS Integers, FiniteSets
VARIABLES parent, dirty
Merge(c, d) ==  \* union c, d; mark affected classes dirty
    /\ parent' = [x \in DOMAIN parent |->
                    IF parent[x] \in {parent[c], parent[d]}
                    THEN parent[c] ELSE parent[x]]
    /\ dirty'  = dirty \cup {parent[c], parent[d]}
RebuildStep == \* repair one dirty class, possibly dirtying parents
    /\ dirty /= {}
    /\ \E c \in dirty : dirty' = (dirty \ {c}) \cup ParentsOf(c)
    /\ UNCHANGED parent
Spec == Merge \/ RebuildStep  \* interleavings all converge to closure
=============================================================================
```

Every interleaving of merges and repair steps converges to the same congruence closure; deferring repair merely chooses a coarser, cheaper interleaving.

### 4.3 E-Matching: Pattern Matching Modulo Equivalence

**E-matching** generalizes syntactic pattern matching: given a pattern $p$ with variables, find all pairs $(\sigma, c)$ such that $p\sigma$ is *represented* in e-class $c$. The "modulo equivalence" clause is the point — where syntactic matching would find one occurrence of `(+ a b)`, e-matching finds every e-class containing *any* term equal to `(+ a b)`, including `(+ b a)` if commutativity has been learned.

The standard algorithm compiles the pattern into a join plan over the e-graph's relations and backtracks:

1. Match the pattern's root e-node against candidate e-nodes (indexed by function symbol).
2. Recursively bind variables to e-classes, requiring consistent bindings.
3. Non-linear patterns (repeated variables) add *equality join* constraints between e-classes.

Worst-case e-matching is exponential in pattern size, but two engineering facts save it in practice: patterns are small (rewrite left-hand sides), and the e-node symbol index makes the top-down search highly selective. Modern implementations compile patterns to relational joins — *relational e-matching* — evaluated with worst-case-optimal join algorithms, a major follow-on advance to the *egg* line of work [2][5].

### 4.4 The Saturation Loop and Rewrite Application

Rewrite application in equality saturation is *non-destructive and unordered*. Applying $l \to r$ under substitution $\sigma$ at e-class $c$:

1. `add(rσ)` — insert the instantiated right-hand side (hash-consing shares structure automatically);
2. `merge(c, class(rσ))` — assert the equality.

Crucially, the left-hand side is *not removed*. This monotonicity yields the framework's central metatheorem:

> **Theorem (Soundness of equality saturation).** *If every rewrite rule $l \to r$ is valid ($\models l = r$ under all substitutions), then every term represented in the e-graph after any number of saturation iterations is semantically equal to the initial term.*

*Proof sketch.* By induction on iterations. `add` introduces terms; `merge` only unions classes when justified by a valid rule instance or by congruence from previously justified unions. Representation adequacy (§4.1) lifts class membership to semantic equality. ∎

Note what the theorem does *not* say: it guarantees no completeness. Saturation may time out long before deriving an optimal equivalent — the e-graph then represents a *subset* of the true equivalence class, and extraction returns the best term *found*, not the best term *possible*.

*egg* further splits each iteration into a **read phase** (all e-matching, invariants intact) and a **write phase** (all adds and merges, invariants deferred), then a single `rebuild()`. Besides enabling deferred rebuilding, this makes the algorithm *invariant to rewrite order*: unlike traditional saturation, where rules later in the list observe the effects of earlier rules within the same iteration, *egg*'s matches all see the same pre-iteration graph [2].

### 4.5 Extraction: From Equivalence to Optimality

Extraction turns the e-graph from a *proof object* into an *optimizer*. Given a cost function on terms, the classic algorithm is a bottom-up dynamic program: repeatedly set

$$\text{cost}(c) = \min_{f(c_1,\dots,c_n) \in c} k\big(f, \text{cost}(c_1), \dots, \text{cost}(c_n)\big)$$

until fixpoint, then reconstruct the minimizing term. For *local* cost functions (node cost depends only on its operator and children's costs — e.g., AST size or weighted instruction count), this greedy pass is optimal *among tree-shaped terms*.

**E-class analyses** [2] generalize extraction into a programmable framework. An analysis associates each e-class with data from a join-semilattice (e.g., constant values, free variables, estimated floating-point error) plus two hooks: `make` (compute data for a new e-node from children's data) and `join`/`modify` (merge data when classes union). Analyses power *conditional rewrites* — rules that fire only when analysis data satisfies a predicate, such as constant folding `(+ (Num a) (Num b)) → (Num (a+b))` or range-based strength reduction. The Herbie floating-point improver, rebuilt on *egg*, uses analyses to track error bounds and drove the reported 3000× simplification speedup [2].

Optimal extraction is harder than it looks: with sharing-aware (DAG) cost models, choosing the cheapest representative is **NP-hard** in general (optimal code generation from expression DAGs is the classical analogue), so production systems use the greedy fixed-point, ILP encodings, or extraction-time search [2][5].

## 5. Empirical Results and Proofs

**Complexity.** Asymptotics motivating the modern implementation:

| Operation | Eager repair | Deferred rebuilding (*egg*) |
|-----------|--------------|------------------------------|
| Union-find merge | $\alpha(n)$ amortized | $\alpha(n)$ amortized |
| Congruence repair per iteration | $O(m \cdot u)$ worst-case ($u$ = unions) | $O(m \log^* m)$-ish amortized in practice |
| E-matching (pattern size $p$) | $O(n^p)$ worst-case | same (algorithmic, not implementation) |
| Extraction (local costs) | $O(n)$ per fixpoint round | $O(n)$ per fixpoint round |

The rebuilding row is the headline result of [2]: on saturation workloads, deferring repair converts repeated quadratic re-canonicalization into a near-linear amortized pass, an *asymptotic* speedup that grows with problem size — confirmed empirically across *egg*'s case studies [2][5].

**Measured results.** Three numbers anchor the practical claims [1][2]:

- *Herbie* (floating-point accuracy): replacing the Racket-based simplifier's core loop with *egg*-style batched rewriting and rebuilding made the simplification backend **over 3000× faster** on Herbie's ~500-benchmark suite, collapsing simplification from 98% of total runtime to under 5% [2].
- *Peggy* (Java via E-PEGs): equality saturation discovered intricate optimizations (including ones composing inlining, loop transforms, and arithmetic identities) that phase-ordered pipelines missed, and validated a realistic optimizer by translation — checking the saturated e-graph for equivalence between unoptimized and optimized code [1].
- *Spores*/vectorization and CAD decompilation case studies in [2][5] show the same engine, unchanged, delivering state-of-the-art results across domains — the payoff of extensibility via e-class analyses rather than ad-hoc e-graph surgery.

**Proofs and guarantees.** Beyond the soundness theorem (§4.4), two proof-theoretic properties matter. First, *proof production*: congruence closure can emit a checkable derivation for each derived equality [4], so an e-graph optimizer can in principle certify every optimization. Second, *translation validation*: because the e-graph represents both the input program and all its optimized versions, validating a *foreign* compiler reduces to inserting both programs into one e-graph and saturating — if they land in one e-class, the compilation was correct [1].

**Superoptimization.** The limiting case of equality saturation is *superoptimization*: saturate a space of candidate programs and extract the cheapest under a cost model. Denali, the goal-directed superoptimizer, pioneered e-graph-backed search over loop-free instruction sequences with theorem-proving equivalence checks; equality saturation generalizes the idea by making the *search itself* deductive — rewrites propose candidates, congruence shares them compactly, extraction picks the winner. Stochastic superoptimizers sample the space randomly; equality saturation explores it *exhaustively up to the rewrite theory*, trading stochastic incompleteness for deductive closure bounded only by time [1][2].

## 6. Limitations

Equality saturation is not a panacea; its failure modes are structural:

1. **Non-termination of saturation.** Rewrite systems like associativity–commutativity generate infinite equivalence classes in principle; in practice the e-graph grows until timeout. Saturation is reached only for well-behaved theories, and "saturated or timeout" is the honest loop condition [2].
2. **E-graph bloat.** Each iteration can add exponentially many e-nodes in the worst case. Memory, not time, is usually the binding constraint; practitioners cap iterations or schedule rules to prioritize productive rewrites [5].
3. **E-matching cost.** Non-linear patterns and associative-commutative theories stress the matcher; naive backtracking can dominate runtime. Relational e-matching mitigates but does not eliminate the worst case [2].
4. **Extraction optimality gap.** Greedy extraction is optimal only for local, tree-shaped cost models. Real cost models (register pressure, instruction scheduling, DAG sharing) are non-local, and optimal extraction is NP-hard — the global heuristic chooses among *represented* programs, which may exclude the true optimum [2][5].
5. **Rewrite authoring burden.** Soundness is only as good as the rules: an invalid rewrite silently poisons every downstream result. Conditional rewrites via e-class analyses help (predicates can encode side conditions), but verifying rule sets remains manual [2].
6. **Proof gaps.** While congruence closure can be proof-producing [4], most saturation engines discard derivations for speed; reconstructing *why* two terms became equal (for certification or debugging) requires additional machinery.

---

## 7. Conclusion

From Nelson and Oppen's congruence closure [3] to Tate et al.'s equality saturation [1] to Willsey et al.'s *egg* [2], the trajectory is one of *progressive de-constraining*: first decide equality efficiently, then saturate program spaces with it, then engineer the saturation loop until it is fast and flexible enough for production optimizers. The e-graph's core trick has not changed in forty years: represent a congruence relation with maximal sharing, and exponential spaces become tractable. What changed is what we ask of it — from verifying a single equality to enumerating every optimization a compiler could ever apply, and extracting the best one. Superoptimization, once a heroic per-program search, becomes a disciplined consequence of saturating the right rewrite theory. The open frontiers — relational e-matching at scale, certified extraction, theories beyond ground equality — suggest the e-graph's second half-century will be as productive as its first.
