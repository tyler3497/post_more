# Prime Editing: pegRNA Architecture, Reverse-Transcriptase-Mediated Edit Incorporation, and Genome-Wide Off-Target Profiling

## Abstract

Prime editing (PE) is a genome-editing technology that writes arbitrary edits — all twelve base-to-base conversions, insertions up to 44 bp, and deletions up to 80 bp — into a specified locus without double-strand breaks (DSBs) or donor DNA [1]. The system fuses a Cas9(H840A) nickase to engineered M-MLV reverse transcriptase and uses a prime editing guide RNA (pegRNA) whose 3′ extension encodes a primer-binding site (PBS) and a reverse-transcription template (RTT) carrying the edit. After nicking, the 3′ DNA flap anneals to the PBS and RT extends it along the RTT; flap equilibration plus mismatch-repair-mediated heteroduplex resolution then installs the edit on both strands [2][3]. Iterations PE2 (pentamutant RT), PE3/PE3b (strategic second nick), and PE5 (MLH1dn-mediated mismatch-repair inhibition) raised efficiencies while preserving specificity. Genome-wide profiling via Digenome-seq, GUIDE-seq, CIRCLE-seq/CHANGE-seq, and the GOTI embryo assay indicates far fewer off-target DSBs and single-nucleotide variants than Cas9 nuclease editing [5][6][7], though residual indels and incomplete repair evasion remain active targets. This thesis formalizes pegRNA architecture, flap-equilibration thermodynamics, and off-target nomination strategies.

## 1 Introduction

The CRISPR-Cas9 nuclease revolutionized genome editing by enabling programmable DNA cleavage, yet its dependence on double-strand breaks (DSBs) carries intrinsic liabilities: error-prone non-homologous end joining (NHEJ), unpredictable indel spectra, large-scale chromosomal rearrangements, and cytotoxicity from p53 activation [3]. Base editors (CBEs, ABEs) eliminated the DSB requirement but were initially confined to four transition mutations within a narrow activity window [1]. A more general chemistry was required — one capable of writing arbitrary sequences directly into the genome with the precision of a word processor.

Prime editing, reported by Anzalone *et al.* in 2019, supplies that chemistry [1]. It couples two molecular machines with complementary capabilities: a Cas9 nickase that generates a programmable 3′ DNA primer, and a reverse transcriptase that extends that primer using an RNA template physically tethered to the targeting guide. The result is a *search-and-replace* operation in which the edit is specified entirely within the pegRNA sequence — no donor DNA, no DSB, no homology-directed repair dependency.

The generality is striking. From a single scaffold, PE installs transversions and transitions alike (all 12 possible single-base conversions), corrects frameshifts via programmed indels, and supports multiplexed combinatorial edits [4]. Yet the technology's sophistication is also its challenge: pegRNA design lives in a high-dimensional parameter space (spacer length, PBS length, RTT length, edit position, nicking-guide offset), flap resolution is governed by competing thermodynamic equilibria and enzymatic degradation kinetics, and specificity must be validated at genome scale rather than assumed from the absence of DSBs [5][6].

This thesis unifies the three pillars of the title: pegRNA architecture and design rules, the stepwise RT-mediated incorporation chemistry, and a critical comparison of genome-wide off-target nomination assays.

> **Theorem:** *For any target sequence edit encodable in the RTT — substitution, insertion, or deletion — prime editing achieves installation through a DSB-free pathway, with product purity determined by the ratio of edited 3′ flap incorporation to unedited 5′ flap retention after mismatch-repair resolution.*

## 2 Background

### 2.1 From Cas9 nucleases to nickase-based editors

Wild-type *Streptococcus pyogenes* Cas9 (SpCas9) uses two nuclease domains — HNH and RuvC — to cleave the target and non-target DNA strands respectively, three nucleotides upstream of the NGG PAM [3]. The H840A mutation inactivates the HNH domain, yielding a nickase (nCas9) that cleaves only the non-target strand. Because single-strand nicks are repaired with high fidelity by the cell, nCas9 alone is essentially non-mutagenic; its power lies in *directing* effector domains to a strand-specific primer [1].

This principle underlies base editors (deaminase–nCas9 fusions) and, crucially, prime editors: an M-MLV reverse transcriptase fused to nCas9(H840A) [1][2]. The nick is not the edit — it is the *invitation* for the RT to write one.

### 2.2 Reverse transcription as an editing chemistry

Reverse transcriptases polymerize DNA from an RNA template, initiating from a 3′-OH primer. In prime editing, the nicked genomic 3′ end serves as the primer and the pegRNA 3′ extension as the template. M-MLV RT was selected for its moderate processivity and engineerability; the PE1 prototype used wild-type M-MLV RT, while PE2 employs an engineered pentamutant (D200N/L603W/T330P/T306K/W313F) with improved thermostability, processivity, DNA–RNA substrate affinity, and attenuated RNase H activity — yielding 1.6- to 5.1-fold efficiency gains and compatibility with shorter PBS sequences [1][3].

### 2.3 The mismatch-repair bottleneck

A critical insight, elaborated by Chen *et al.* (2021) and exploited in PE5, is that the post-extension heteroduplex (edited 3′ flap annealed to unedited complementary strand) is a canonical mismatch-repair (MMR) substrate [4][6]. Active MMR can excise the edit-bearing strand and restore the original sequence, capping efficiency. PE5 co-expresses a dominant-negative MLH1 (MLH1dn) fragment to transiently suppress MMR, and PE5max pairs this with optimized editor architecture and engineered pegRNAs (epegRNAs) to reach high efficiencies in mammalian embryos with minimal off-target SNVs [6].

### 2.4 Scope of editable lesions

The following taxonomy organizes what PE can install, with empirically reported limits [1][4]:

| Edit class | Typical range | Representative applications |
|---|---|---|
| Point substitutions | all 12 conversions | pathogenic SNV correction (e.g., sickle-cell *HBB* locus) |
| Small insertions | 1–44 bp | epitope tags, loxP sites |
| Small deletions | 1–80 bp | exon skipping, frameshift repair |
| Combinatorial | substitution + indel | multiplexed allele engineering |
| Large edits (twinPE) | >100 bp (paired pegRNAs) | gene-sized insertions via recombinase pairing |

## 3 Methodology

Our analysis proceeds on three complementary tracks: (i) a formal model of pegRNA architecture with quantitative design heuristics drawn from the primary literature; (ii) a mechanistic, step-resolved derivation of RT-mediated edit incorporation with thermodynamic and kinetic reasoning; and (iii) a comparative evaluation of genome-wide off-target nomination assays, scored on sensitivity, biological context, input requirements, and validation burden [7][8].

### 3.1 Analytical framework

We treat prime editing as a pipeline of conditional probabilities. Let $E$ denote successful biallelic edit installation. Then, to first order,

$$P(E) = P(\text{bind}) \cdot P(\text{nick} \mid \text{bind}) \cdot P(\text{prime} \mid \text{nick}) \cdot P(\text{extend} \mid \text{prime}) \cdot P(\text{flap}_{3'} \mid \text{extend}) \cdot P(\text{resolve} \mid \text{flap}_{3'}),$$

where the terms correspond respectively to pegRNA–target binding, H840A nicking, PBS hybridization of the nicked 3′ end, RT extension along the RTT, incorporation of the edited 3′ flap over the displaced 5′ flap, and MMR-mediated heteroduplex resolution favoring the edit. Each term is tunable: $P(\text{prime})$ by PBS length and GC content; $P(\text{extend})$ by RTT length and RT processivity (PE1 vs. PE2); $P(\text{resolve})$ by nicking-guide strategy (PE3/PE3b) and MMR status (PE5) [1][4][6].

### 3.2 Literature corpus and design rules

We synthesized design heuristics from the primary reports and reviews [1][2][3][4]:

1. **Spacer:** 17–20 nt protospacer, PAM-proximal nick positioned so the edit lies within ~30 nt of the nick on the PAM-containing strand.
2. **PBS:** typically 8–17 nt; longer PBS stabilizes priming but excessively long PBS can reduce specificity; PE2 tolerates shorter PBS than PE1.
3. **RTT:** length set by edit position plus a 3′ homology arm (commonly 10–16 nt downstream of the edit) to anchor the flap.
4. **Nicking sgRNA (PE3/PE3b):** offset +40 to +90 nt from the pegRNA nick on the opposite strand; PE3b designs the nicking guide against the *edited* sequence so it nicks only after edit installation, suppressing indels ~13-fold relative to PE3 [1].

A reference pegRNA-design routine is sketched in Section 4.4.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 pegRNA Architecture: A Four-Module RNA Cassette

The pegRNA is a single RNA molecule with four functional modules arranged 5′→3′:

```
5′ — [ spacer (17–20 nt) ] — [ Cas9 scaffold (~80 nt) ] — [ RTT (edit template) ] — [ PBS (8–17 nt) ] — 3′
```

- **Spacer.** Identical in function to a standard sgRNA protospacer: it programs nCas9 binding and positions the H840A nick on the PAM-containing (non-target) strand, 3 nt upstream of the PAM [1][2].
- **Scaffold.** The conserved tracrRNA-derived hairpin structure required for Cas9 loading and R-loop formation.
- **Reverse-transcription template (RTT).** The information payload. It encodes the desired sequence change flanked by homology: the RTT is the reverse complement of the *intended edited* DNA strand, so RT extension writes the edit directly into the nascent 3′ flap. RTT length must accommodate both the edit and sufficient downstream homology for stable flap annealing [4].
- **Primer-binding site (PBS).** Complementary to the nicked 3′ DNA end. PBS hybridization converts the nick into a primed substrate. The PBS–DNA duplex melting temperature $T_m$ is the dominant determinant of $P(\text{prime})$: too short and priming fails; too long and off-target priming or pegRNA misfolding increases [1][3].

The 3′ extension is the locus of most engineering: 3′-terminal RNA is vulnerable to exonucleolytic degradation, motivating engineered pegRNAs (epegRNAs) that append structured RNA motifs (e.g., tevopreQ1 pseudoknot) to the 3′ end, improving stability and editing efficiency — a key component of the PE5max system [6].

> **Theorem:** *pegRNA activity is maximized when the PBS $T_m$ lies in a Goldilocks window — sufficient for stable priming at 37 °C yet short enough to preserve on-target discrimination — and the RTT provides ≥10 nt of post-edit homology to anchor the edited flap.*

### 4.2 Reverse-Transcriptase-Mediated Edit Incorporation: The Six-Step Chemistry

**Step 1 — Targeting and R-loop formation.** The nCas9(H840A)–M-MLV RT fusion, loaded with pegRNA, binds the target via spacer–protospacer pairing and PAM recognition, forming an R-loop that exposes the non-target strand [1][2].

**Step 2 — Nicking.** H840A nicks the PAM-containing strand 3 nt upstream of the PAM, liberating a 3′-OH end.

**Step 3 — PBS hybridization.** The nicked 3′ flap anneals to the PBS at the pegRNA 3′ extension, forming a primer–template heteroduplex. This is the commitment step; its equilibrium constant $K_{\text{prime}} = \exp(-\Delta G_{\text{PBS}}/RT)$ sets the priming probability.

**Step 4 — Reverse transcription.** M-MLV RT extends the 3′-OH along the RTT, polymerizing DNA complementary to the edit template. The pentamutant RT in PE2 improves processivity and template affinity, directly increasing $P(\text{extend})$ [1][3].

**Step 5 — Flap equilibration and excision.** Extension yields two competing flaps: the edited 3′ flap and the original unedited 5′ flap. Although the perfectly complementary 5′ flap is thermodynamically favored to re-anneal, cellular 5′→3′ exonucleases (notably FEN1, active during lagging-strand synthesis) preferentially degrade displaced 5′ flaps, biasing incorporation toward the edited 3′ flap [3]. The 3′ flap is then ligated.

**Step 6 — Heteroduplex resolution.** The product is a heteroduplex: one edited strand, one original strand, resolved via replication or mismatch repair. Because MMR directed at the edited strand *erases* the edit, transient MMR inhibition (MLH1dn in PE5/PE5max) shifts resolution toward edit retention [6].

The PE3 strategy adds a second nick on the *unedited* strand, directing MMR to replace the unedited strand using the edited strand as template — a ~3-fold efficiency gain over PE2 at the cost of elevated indels when the two nicks are processed as a DSB [1]. PE3b makes the second nicking guide specific to the *edited* sequence, so the second nick occurs only after successful edit installation, cutting indels ~13-fold versus PE3 [1].

### 4.3 RT Engineering Trajectory: PE1 → PE2 → PE5max

| System | RT variant | Key addition | Reported gain |
|---|---|---|---|
| PE1 | wild-type M-MLV RT | baseline fusion | reference |
| PE2 | pentamutant (D200N/L603W/T330P/T306K/W313F) | thermostability, processivity, ↓RNase H | 1.6–5.1× point-mutation efficiency [1] |
| PE3 | PE2 + nicking sgRNA | MMR redirection via second nick | ~3× over PE2 [1] |
| PE3b | PE3 with edit-specific nicking guide | indel suppression | ~13× fewer indels than PE3 [1] |
| PE5/PE5max | PE3 + MLH1dn, epegRNAs, optimized architecture | MMR inhibition, pegRNA stability | highest reported efficiencies; embryo-validated [6] |

An additional specificity refinement replaces nCas9(H840A) with a "genuine" nickase carrying H840A+N863A: whereas H840A occasionally generates DSBs, the double mutant essentially eliminates DSB formation at both on- and off-target sites while retaining prime-editing activity [5].

### 4.4 Computational pegRNA Design

Design reduces to constrained sequence optimization. The following reference implementation captures the core rules of Section 3.2:

```python
def design_pegrna(target_seq: str, pam: str, edit: dict,
                  pbs_len: int = 13, rtt_homology: int = 12) -> dict:
    """Sketch of pegRNA 3'-extension design.

    target_seq : protospacer + PAM context (5'->3', PAM-containing strand)
    edit       : {'pos': int, 'ref': str, 'alt': str}  # 0-based in target_seq
    """
    nick_site = len(target_seq) - 6  # 3 nt upstream of NGG PAM
    # PBS: complement of sequence immediately upstream of nick (3' flap)
    pbs = reverse_complement(target_seq[nick_site - pbs_len: nick_site])
    # RTT: reverse complement of edited strand, from nick through edit + homology
    edited = target_seq[:edit['pos']] + edit['alt'] + target_seq[edit['pos'] + len(edit['ref']):]
    rtt_span = edited[nick_site: edit['pos'] + rtt_homology]
    rtt = reverse_complement(rtt_span)
    assert 8 <= len(pbs) <= 17, "PBS outside workable range"
    assert edit['pos'] - nick_site <= 30, "edit too far from nick"
    return {"pbs": pbs, "rtt": rtt,
            "extension_3prime": rtt + pbs}  # note: RTT is 5' of PBS in pegRNA

def reverse_complement(s: str) -> str:
    return s.translate(str.maketrans("ATGC", "TACG"))[::-1]
```

Production tools (e.g., multicrispr, PrimeDesign) extend this sketch with off-target scoring, secondary-structure filtering of the 3′ extension, and PE3b nicking-guide selection [9].

## 5 Empirical Results / Theoretical Guarantees

### 5.1 Efficiency landmarks

- Anzalone *et al.* reported PE1/PE2-mediated installation of all 12 base conversions in HEK293T cells, with PE2 improving point-mutation efficiency 1.6–5.1× over PE1 and supporting insertions up to 44 bp and deletions up to 80 bp [1].
- PE3's second nick raised efficiency ~3-fold over PE2; PE3b preserved that gain while reducing indels ~13-fold relative to PE3 in human cell lines [1].
- PE5max (PEmax + MLH1dn + epegRNA + nicking sgRNA) achieved 7.59–100% on-target editing across tested loci in mouse embryos — the highest embryo efficiencies reported for prime editing — while whole-genome sequencing detected only background-level SNVs [6].

### 5.2 Specificity: what genome-wide profiling actually shows

Because prime editing avoids DSBs, conventional DSB-capture assays must be interpreted carefully: a nickase-based system can still produce rare DSBs (e.g., via H840A infidelity or paired PE3 nicks), and RT-mediated events can in principle occur at off-target nicks. The assay landscape [7][8]:

| Assay | Class | Principle | Sensitivity / cost profile | PE-relevant caveat |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Digenome-seq | biochemical (in vitro) | WGS of Cas9-digested naked DNA; uniform read ends mark cleavage | ~0.1% allele frequency; requires 400–500M reads | no chromatin context; over-nominates |
| DIG-seq | biochemical | Digenome-seq on cell-free chromatin | higher accuracy than Digenome-seq | technically demanding |
| CIRCLE-seq | biochemical | circularized DNA + in vitro cleavage, both break ends sequenced | detects low-frequency breaks; both-sided reads | naked DNA; high background |
| CHANGE-seq | biochemical | CIRCLE-seq with tagmentation circularization | less input DNA, faster | same chromatin caveat |
| GUIDE-seq | cellular | dsODN tag integration at DSBs in living cells | reflects true cellular activity; misses rare sites | requires DSBs — blind to pure nick/RT events |
| GOTI | in vivo (embryo) | paired tdTomato± blastomere WGS | detects SNVs/indels genome-wide in vivo | low throughput; embryo microinjection |

Empirical findings:

1. **Digenome-seq with genuine nickases.** At *HEK4*, *RUNX1*, and *EMX1*, wild-type Cas9 produced 148–454 DSB sites; nCas9(H840A) produced 8–23; nCas9(H840A+N863A) produced only 2–3 — indistinguishable from untreated controls (2.0 ± 0.0). The N863A addition therefore nearly eliminates off-target DSB formation while preserving prime-editing function [5].
2. **GOTI in embryos.** PE5max-injected embryos showed 5–18 SNVs per embryo — comparable to Cre-only controls, unique per embryo, non-recurrent, and absent from predicted off-target sites — indicating no detectable genome-wide off-target SNVs at GOTI sensitivity [6].
3. **Cellular assays.** PE systems produce small numbers of on-target indels (significant at some loci, undetectable at others), consistent with flap-processing errors rather than DSB-driven NHEJ [6].

> **Theorem:** *No single assay is sufficient for prime-editing specificity claims: biochemical assays (CIRCLE-seq/CHANGE-seq/Digenome-seq) bound the worst-case off-target landscape with high sensitivity but no chromatin context, while cellular (GUIDE-seq) and in vivo (GOTI) assays measure realized events at lower sensitivity. A defensible claim requires at least one assay from each class.*

### 5.3 Theoretical guarantees and their limits

Prime editing's specificity advantage rests on a *triple-match requirement*: productive off-target editing demands (i) spacer–protospacer pairing sufficient for nicking, (ii) PBS hybridization of the off-target nicked end, and (iii) RTT-templated extension that survives flap competition. If per-step off-target probabilities are $p_1, p_2, p_3 \ll 1$, the joint probability scales as their product — multiplicatively safer than Cas9's single-match requirement. This is the formal basis for PE's observed low off-target profile [1][6]. The guarantee weakens, however, when $p_2$ is large (promiscuous PBS priming at repetitive loci) or when PE3's second nick converts paired nicks into a *bona fide* DSB — precisely the failure mode PE3b and genuine nickases were engineered to close [1][5].

## 6 Limitations

1. **Efficiency variability across loci.** Editing rates vary dramatically with chromatin context, sequence composition, and cell type; some loci remain stubbornly below 5% even with PE5max [4][6].
2. **pegRNA design is still heuristic.** PBS/RTT length optima are locus-dependent, and current rules (Section 3.2) are empirical rather than derived from a predictive biophysical model; failed designs are common and debugging is laborious [1][4].
3. **3′ extension instability.** Unprotected pegRNA 3′ ends are exonuclease substrates; epegRNA motifs mitigate but do not eliminate degradation, and structured appendages can perturb folding [6].
4. **Residual indels.** PE3's second nick can yield DSB-like lesions; PE3b and genuine nickases reduce but do not provably eliminate them, and on-target indel byproducts persist at low frequency [1][5][6].
5. **MMR inhibition trade-offs.** Transient MLH1dn expression raises the theoretical risk of genome-wide mutation accumulation during the inhibition window; long-term safety data in therapeutic contexts are absent [6].
6. **Delivery.** The PE fusion (~6.3 kb coding sequence plus pegRNA) exceeds AAV packaging limits, complicating in vivo delivery; split-intein and mRNA-LNP strategies are investigational [4].
7. **Assay blind spots.** GUIDE-seq cannot detect pure nick/RT off-target events that never become DSBs; biochemical assays over-nominate; GOTI is low-throughput [7][8].

## 7 Conclusion

Prime editing reframes genome editing as *information transfer* rather than *damage and repair*: a nickase generates a primer, a reverse transcriptase writes a programmed sequence from an RNA template, and cellular flap-processing and mismatch-repair machinery resolve the product. The pegRNA's four-module architecture — spacer, scaffold, RTT, PBS — compresses targeting and templating into a single molecule, while the PE1→PE2→PE3/PE3b→PE5max trajectory demonstrates how each mechanistic bottleneck (RT processivity, heteroduplex resolution, MMR erasure, pegRNA stability) can be addressed by principled engineering [1][5][6].

Genome-wide profiling supports a cautiously optimistic specificity verdict: Digenome-seq shows genuine nickases produce essentially background-level DSBs [5], and GOTI whole-genome sequencing finds no detectable off-target SNVs for PE5max in embryos [6] — qualitatively superior to Cas9 nuclease editing. Yet the technology is not yet a turnkey therapeutic platform. Locus-dependent efficiency, heuristic pegRNA design, delivery constraints, and the assay-coverage problem (Section 5.2) define the current research frontier.

The path forward is clear: predictive biophysical models of PBS/RTT thermodynamics to replace trial-and-error design, high-throughput pegRNA screening, nickase variants with zero DSB leakage, and standardized multi-assay specificity dossiers combining biochemical nomination with cellular and in vivo validation [7][8]. If these mature, prime editing's promise — correcting the vast majority of known pathogenic variants without breaking DNA — moves from demonstration to medicine.
