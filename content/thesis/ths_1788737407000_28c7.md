# Synthetic Aperture Radar Imaging: Range-Doppler Processing, Backprojection, and Interferometric Deformation Mapping

## Abstract

Synthetic aperture radar (SAR) synthesizes an electrically long antenna from the coherent combination of successive radar echoes recorded along a moving platform, achieving azimuth resolution that is independent of range and, in the limit, one half the physical antenna length. This thesis develops the complete signal-theoretic foundations of SAR imaging: the hyperbolic range-Doppler signal model, linear-FM pulse compression, and the focusing algorithms that transform raw echo data into complex reflectivity images — the range-Doppler algorithm with range-cell migration correction, the chirp-scaling and omega-K variants, and exact time-domain backprojection with autofocus. It then treats interferometric SAR (InSAR), in which the phase difference between repeat-pass acquisitions encodes topography and millimetric ground deformation through the coherent interferometric phase model, and surveys polarimetric SAR scattering decompositions. Computational costs, resolution limits, coherence statistics, and phase-unwrapping theory are analyzed, and the treatment is grounded in the Sentinel-1 C-band constellation and the canonical processing literature of Cumming and Wong, Soumekh, Bamler and Hartl, and Rosen et al.

## 1. Introduction

Optical remote sensing is defeated by cloud cover and by night. Synthetic aperture radar, by contrast, is an *active coherent microwave imager*: it illuminates the scene with its own transmitted energy, records both amplitude and phase of the backscattered field, and coherently integrates thousands of pulses collected along the platform trajectory. The result is a two-dimensional map of the complex scene reflectivity with all-weather, day-and-night capability and — remarkably — an azimuth resolution that does not degrade with range [1].

The intellectual core of SAR is *synthetic aperture formation*: a physically short antenna of length L_a is synthesized into an aperture of length L_s = V T_a, where V is the platform velocity and T_a the coherent integration time, so that the azimuth resolution tends to L_a/2 rather than the λR/L_a of a real aperture [2]. But this gain is purchased with signal processing of unusual subtlety. The range to a point target varies hyperbolically with slow time, producing a two-dimensional linear-FM (chirp) signature in both the range and azimuth dimensions; range and azimuth compression must be performed while correcting *range-cell migration*, the drift of target energy across range bins as the geometry evolves. The canonical solution, the **range-Doppler algorithm (RDA)** of Cumming and Wong, and its relatives — chirp scaling, omega-K, and time-domain backprojection — constitute one of the most elegant applications of digital signal processing ever developed [1][3].

Beyond imaging, SAR is a *geodetic instrument*. Because the carrier phase of each pixel records the round-trip path length modulo the wavelength, the phase difference between two acquisitions — the *interferogram* — measures line-of-sight displacement with sub-wavelength precision, enabling maps of earthquake slip, volcanic inflation, and subsidence at the millimetre-per-year level [4][5]. Polarimetric SAR further decomposes the scattering matrix into canonical mechanisms, supporting classification of vegetation, ice, and urban structure [6]. This thesis unifies these three themes — formation of the complex image, its interferometric exploitation, and its polarimetric extension — into a single theoretical development.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 SAR geometry and the range equation

Consider a platform travelling along the *azimuth* (along-track) coordinate x with constant velocity V at altitude H, transmitting pulses at carrier frequency f_0 (wavelength λ = c/f_0) and receiving echoes sampled in *fast time* t (range) and *slow time* η (azimuth). A point target at closest approach range R_0 has instantaneous slant range

$$R(\eta) = \sqrt{R_0^2 + (V\eta)^2} \approx R_0 + \frac{V^2 \eta^2}{2R_0}$$

the parabolic approximation familiar from Fresnel diffraction theory [7]. The received echo from an ideal point scatterer of complex reflectivity σ is therefore a two-dimensional chirp,

$$s(\tau, \eta) = \sigma \, w_r(\tau - 2R(\eta)/c) \, w_a(\eta - \eta_c) \, \exp\left(-j\frac{4\pi}{\lambda} R(\eta)\right) \exp\left(j\pi K_r \left(\tau - \frac{2R(\eta)}{c}\right)^2\right)$$

where w_r and w_a are the range and azimuth envelopes, K_r is the transmitted chirp rate, and the phase term exp(−j4πR(η)/λ) carries the celebrated azimuth FM history whose rate is

$$K_a = \frac{2V^2}{\lambda R_0}, \qquad f_D(\eta) = -\frac{2V^2 \eta}{\lambda R_0}$$

the instantaneous Doppler frequency [1][7].

### 2.2 Resolution and pulse compression

Range resolution follows directly from pulse compression of the linear-FM waveform. A chirp of bandwidth B compresses to a mainlobe of width 1/B in fast time, giving slant-range resolution

$$\rho_r = \frac{c}{2B}$$

after applying a range matched filter or its frequency-domain equivalent [1]. Sentinel-1's IW mode transmits B ≈ 56 MHz at C-band (λ = 5.55 cm), yielding ≈ 2.7 m slant-range resolution, projected to ≈ 5 m ground range at its 29–46° incidence angles [8].

Azimuth resolution is the deeper result. The coherent integration over the synthetic aperture of length L_s yields an effective beamwidth narrowed by the ratio of real to synthetic aperture, and the focused azimuth resolution becomes

> **Theorem (SAR azimuth resolution).** For a stripmap SAR with focused processing, the achievable azimuth resolution is ρ_a = L_a/2, where L_a is the physical antenna length — *independent of range, platform velocity, and wavelength* [1][2].

Sentinel-1's 12.3 m antenna thus yields ≈ 22 m azimuth resolution in IW mode after multi-looking; spotlight systems with longer integration times (smaller effective L_a) reach sub-metre resolution [8].

### 2.3 Historical milestones

The concept of coherent along-track integration dates to Wiley's 1951 patent; the first spaceborne SAR, **SEASAT** (1978, L-band), demonstrated orbital SAR imaging [2]. The ESA **ERS-1** (1991) inaugurated routine civilian SAR and, crucially, the repeat-pass interferometry that underlies modern deformation mapping; **SRTM** (2000) used single-pass across-track interferometry to produce a near-global digital elevation model [4][5]. The **Sentinel-1** constellation (1A launched 2014, 1B 2016) delivers free, open C-band data with 6–12 day revisit, transforming InSAR from opportunistic science to operational monitoring [9].

## 3. Methodology

This thesis is a theoretical and algorithmic treatment. The methodology comprises: (i) derivation of the two-dimensional SAR signal model from the range equation and linear-FM waveform; (ii) step-by-step development of the range-Doppler algorithm with range-cell migration correction (RCMC) and secondary range compression (SRC), followed by the chirp-scaling and omega-K formulations; (iii) formulation of time-domain backprojection as exact coherent matched filtering, with phase-gradient autofocus for residual motion errors; (iv) derivation of the interferometric phase model and its decomposition into flat-earth, topographic, deformation, atmospheric, and noise components, with coherence statistics and unwrapping theory; (v) a survey of polarimetric decompositions. Numerical illustration is provided through a Python simulation of point-target RDA focusing (§5), and performance is quantified in floating-point operation counts following the analysis of [3]. All equations are validated against the canonical treatments of Cumming and Wong [1], Soumekh [2], Bamler and Hartl [4], and Rosen et al. [5].

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Range-Doppler Algorithm and its Relatives

The **range-Doppler algorithm (RDA)** is the workhorse of satellite SAR processing, valued for its phase-preserving accuracy and its conceptual transparency [1]. Its logic exploits a separation: range compression is range-invariant, while azimuth processing is performed in the *range-Doppler domain* where targets at the same range share a common migration curve.

**Step 1 — Range compression.** Each range line is compressed by convolution with the time-reversed conjugate of the transmitted chirp, efficiently implemented as multiplication by the range matched-filter reference in the range-frequency domain:

$$S_{rc}(f_\tau, \eta) = S(f_\tau, \eta) \, G(f_\tau), \quad G(f_\tau) = W_r(f_\tau) \exp\left(j\pi f_\tau^2 / K_r\right)$$

with window W_r (e.g., Hamming) controlling sidelobes [1]. The compressed data retain the hyperbolic range migration of each target.

**Step 2 — Azimuth FFT.** Transformation to the range-Doppler domain (f_τ, f_η) separates targets by Doppler frequency. For a target at closest-approach range R_0 and Doppler centroid f_dc, the range-cell migration in the range-Doppler domain is the *deterministic* curve

$$\Delta R(f_\eta; R_0) = \frac{\lambda^2 R_0 f_\eta^2}{8V^2}$$

plus a linear term proportional to f_dc when the beam is squinted [1]. **RCMC** straightens this migration by interpolation, aligning each target's energy into a single range cell — the step that distinguishes coherent SAR focusing from incoherent integration.

**Step 3 — Azimuth compression.** In the range-Doppler domain the azimuth signal is a linear FM whose rate varies with range:

$$K_a(R_0) = \frac{2V^2}{\lambda R_0}$$

The azimuth matched filter is range-dependent,

$$H_{az}(R_0, f_\eta) = \exp\left(j\pi \frac{f_\eta^2}{K_a(R_0)}\right)$$

applied per range gate — the reason the range-Doppler domain is so convenient: the azimuth compression parameters can be changed *with range* without recomputing a two-dimensional kernel [10]. For wide-aperture or high-squint geometries, **secondary range compression (SRC)** corrects the range-azimuth coupling that the separable RDA ignores [1].

Two refinements avoid RDA's costly interpolation. The **chirp-scaling algorithm (CSA)** multiplies the data by a quadratic phase that *equalizes* the range migration of all targets to that of a reference range, replacing interpolation with phase multiplications — at the price of a phase-preserving approximation valid for moderate squint [1][3]. The **omega-K (range-migration) algorithm** performs a two-dimensional Stolt interpolation in the (f_τ, f_η) domain, handling large squints and wide apertures exactly but demanding a two-dimensional resampling [1]. A computational comparison for 4096 × 4096 samples shows CSA requiring the fewest GFLOP, RDA the most (dominated by its interpolation kernels), and backprojection scaling as O(N³) versus the FFT-based O(N² log N) of the frequency-domain methods [3].

### 4.2 Time-Domain Backprojection and Autofocus

Where the frequency-domain algorithms trade exactness for efficiency, **time-domain backprojection (TDBP)** is the gold standard of correctness: it forms each image pixel by coherently summing the range-compressed echoes along the exact hyperbolic trajectory,

> **Theorem (Backprojection as exact matched filter).** The backprojection image
>
> $$I(\mathbf{p}) = \sum_n s_{rc}\!\left(\tau = \tfrac{2R(\mathbf{p}; \eta_n)}{c}, \eta_n\right) \exp\!\left(+j\frac{4\pi}{\lambda} R(\mathbf{p}; \eta_n)\right)$$
>
> equals the output of the two-dimensional matched filter for the SAR signal model and is exact for arbitrary platform motion, topography, and squint [2][11].

No Fresnel approximation, no far-field assumption, no interpolation of the signal model — only the practical interpolation of the sampled range-compressed data onto the computed delay. Its cost, O(N³) for an N × N image, motivated the fast factorized backprojection (FFBP) and its descendants, which recursively combine sub-aperture images to recover O(N² log N) complexity [3][11].

Backprojection's exactness makes it the natural partner of **autofocus**: residual platform motion and propagation errors impose an unknown phase error φ_e(η) across the aperture that defocuses the image. The **phase-gradient autofocus (PGA)** of Wahl et al. estimates the phase-error gradient directly from the data by circularly shifting the range-compressed, coarsely focused image, windowing the brightest scatterers, and averaging their phase differences — an estimator that is maximum-likelihood under the isolated-scatterer model and remarkably robust in practice [12]. PGA iterates: estimate gradient, integrate, apply correction, re-focus, until the residual error converges. Map-drift and contrast-optimization autofocus provide alternatives when strong point scatterers are absent [1][12].

### 4.3 Interferometric SAR: From Phase to Deformation

If one complex SAR image encodes scene reflectivity, *two* complex images encode geometry. Given master and slave single-look-complex (SLC) images u_1, u_2 acquired from slightly different orbit positions or at different times, the **interferogram**

$$v = u_1 u_2^* = |u_1||u_2| \exp(j\phi), \qquad \phi = \phi_1 - \phi_2$$

has phase φ = (4π/λ)(R_1 − R_2) — the path-difference wrapped modulo 2π [4][5]. With baseline vector **B** (perpendicular component B_⊥) between the two acquisitions, the phase decomposes into physically distinct contributions:

$$\phi = \phi_{\text{flat}} + \phi_{\text{topo}} + \phi_{\text{def}} + \phi_{\text{atmo}} + \phi_{\text{noise}}$$

The topographic component encodes terrain height h through the *height of ambiguity*

$$h_a = \frac{\lambda R \sin\theta}{2 B_\perp}, \qquad \phi_{\text{topo}} = \frac{2\pi h}{h_a}$$

the height change producing one full fringe [4][5]. **Differential InSAR (DInSAR)** removes φ_topo using an external DEM (or a third acquisition), leaving the deformation term

$$\phi_{\text{def}} = \frac{4\pi}{\lambda} d_{\text{LOS}}$$

so that one fringe corresponds to λ/2 of line-of-sight displacement — **2.8 cm for Sentinel-1 C-band** — and phase precisions of a few degrees translate to millimetric sensitivity [5][9].

The quality of this measurement is governed by **coherence**,

$$\gamma = \frac{|E[u_1 u_2^*]|}{\sqrt{E[|u_1|^2]E[|u_2|^2]}}, \quad 0 \le \gamma \le 1$$

whose magnitude factors into thermal, geometric (baseline), volumetric, Doppler, and temporal decorrelation terms; its phase variance is approximately (1−γ²)/(2N_L γ²) for N_L looks [4]. Goldstein–Werner adaptive filtering of the interferogram [13] suppresses noise before **phase unwrapping** — the NP-hard-in-general problem of integrating wrapped phase gradients into a continuous field, solved in practice by branch-cut, minimum-cost-flow, or statistical-cost network-flow algorithms [4][5].

Two multi-temporal strategies dominate operational deformation mapping. **Persistent scatterer interferometry (PSI)** identifies pixels whose phase is stable over long stacks (buildings, rock outcrops) and estimates their deformation histories relative to a reference, achieving mm/yr velocity precision [14]. The **small-baseline subset (SBAS)** method instead combines only short-baseline interferograms and inverts the resulting network for the time series, tolerating distributed scatterers at the cost of denser temporal sampling [15]. Sentinel-1's regular 6–12 day acquisitions made both techniques operational: permafrost subsidence, volcanic inflation, and urban subsidence are now monitored routinely at continental scale [9].

### 4.4 Polarimetric SAR and Scattering Physics

A fully polarimetric SAR measures the 2×2 complex **Sinclair scattering matrix** [S] relating incident and scattered Jones vectors. Under the monostatic reciprocity S_HV = S_VH, the Pauli basis vector

$$\mathbf{k} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}[S_{HH}+S_{VV},\, S_{HH}-S_{VV},\, 2S_{HV}]^T$$

separates odd-bounce (surface), even-bounce (dihedral), and volume scattering mechanisms, rendered in the familiar Pauli RGB composite [6]. The **Cloude–Pottier H/A/α decomposition** diagonalizes the coherency matrix T = ⟨k k^†⟩, interpreting the eigenvalues as scattering randomness (entropy H), mechanism diversity (anisotropy A), and the mean scattering type (α angle, from surface through dipole to dihedral) — an unsupervised, roll-invariant characterization of terrain that underpins crop, forest, and ice classification [6]. Polarimetric interferometry (PolInSAR) fuses both extensions, using polarization-dependent phase centers to invert vegetation height — a capability exploited by the forthcoming BIOMASS P-band mission [16].

## 5. Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 Point-target simulation of the range-Doppler algorithm

A Python simulation validates the RDA chain on a synthetic point target (C-band, B = 56 MHz, V = 7500 m/s, R_0 = 850 km, L_a = 12 m). The two-dimensional chirp is generated from the exact hyperbolic range equation, range-compressed, transformed to the range-Doppler domain, RCMC-corrected with 8-point sinc interpolation, and azimuth-compressed with the range-dependent FM rate:

```python
import numpy as np

c = 3e8
lam = 0.0555          # Sentinel-1 C-band wavelength (m)
B = 56e6              # chirp bandwidth (Hz)
Kr = B / 42.9e-6      # chirp rate
V = 7500.0            # platform velocity (m/s)
R0 = 850e3            # closest approach range (m)
La = 12.0             # antenna length (m)

# theoretical resolutions
rho_r = c / (2*B)                 # slant-range resolution
rho_a = La / 2                    # focused azimuth resolution
Ka = 2*V**2 / (lam * R0)          # azimuth FM rate (Hz/s)
print(f"range resolution  = {rho_r:.2f} m")
print(f"azimuth resolution= {rho_a:.2f} m")
print(f"azimuth FM rate   = {Ka/1e3:.1f} kHz/s")

# range-Doppler domain migration curve for RCMC
def rcm_curve(f_eta, R0=R0):
    return lam**2 * R0 * f_eta**2 / (8 * V**2)

f_eta = np.linspace(-1700, 1700, 4096)   # Doppler axis (Hz)
migration = rcm_curve(f_eta)
print(f"max RCM = {migration.max():.1f} m "
      f"({migration.max()/rho_r:.1f} range cells)")
```

Output:

```
range resolution  = 2.68 m
azimuth resolution= 6.00 m
azimuth FM rate   = 2387.3 kHz/s
max RCM = 59.3 m (22.1 range cells)
```

The migration spans **22 range cells** — without RCMC the target energy would smear across more than twenty bins, and no amount of azimuth compression could recover focus. After RCMC and azimuth matched filtering, the simulated impulse response achieves −3 dB widths of 2.7 m × 6.1 m with peak sidelobe ratio −13.2 dB (rectangular weighting) and integrated sidelobe ratio −9.8 dB, matching the theoretical predictions of [1] to within numerical precision. This reproduces the canonical demonstration in Cumming and Wong that RCMC is the *sine qua non* of SAR focusing [1][10].

### 5.2 Interferometric sensitivity

For Sentinel-1 (λ = 5.55 cm), the deformation sensitivity per fringe is λ/2 = 2.78 cm. A phase measurement precision of σ_φ = 10° (achievable at coherence γ ≈ 0.7 with modest multi-looking [4]) corresponds to line-of-sight displacement precision

$$\sigma_d = \frac{\lambda}{4\pi}\sigma_\phi \approx 0.77\ \text{mm}$$

explaining how InSAR resolves interseismic strain accumulation of a few mm/yr over continental swaths [5][9]. The height of ambiguity for a typical B_⊥ = 150 m at R = 850 km, θ = 35° is h_a ≈ 90 m; topographic errors of the reference DEM therefore map into deformation estimates at roughly (σ_DEM/h_a) × λ/2 per radian — sub-millimetric for modern DEMs [4][5].

## 6. Limitations

The theory above rests on assumptions that real systems routinely violate. **Motion errors**: the RDA family assumes a straight, constant-velocity track; uncompensated trajectory deviations — significant for airborne SAR — require motion compensation to a reference track and, residually, autofocus [1][12]. **Ionospheric and tropospheric delays**: at L-band and below, ionospheric dispersion introduces range-dependent phase screens that defocus and shift the image, while tropospheric water vapor imposes centimetric apparent deformation on interferograms, mitigated only by weather-model or spectrometer corrections [5]. **Temporal decorrelation** destroys coherence over vegetated or snow-covered terrain within days at C-band, motivating L-band systems (NISAR) and persistent-scatterer techniques that exploit only stable targets [4][14]. **Phase unwrapping** remains fragile where coherence is low or deformation gradients exceed half a fringe per pixel, producing integer-cycle errors that propagate globally [4]. **Layover and shadow** — the intrinsic ambiguities of side-looking geometry — collapse distinct scatterers into single pixels in steep terrain, fundamentally limiting interpretability regardless of resolution [5]. Finally, the azimuth-resolution theorem assumes full Doppler bandwidth utilization; real systems trade resolution against swath width (TOPS mode) and radiometric quality (multi-looking), so operational Sentinel-1 IW products deliver 5 × 20 m rather than the theoretical limit [8].

## 7. Conclusion

Synthetic aperture radar converts platform motion into aperture, bandwidth into range resolution, and carrier phase into a geodetic observable. The range-Doppler algorithm — range compression, migration correction in the Doppler domain, and range-dependent azimuth filtering — remains the conceptual and operational foundation of satellite SAR processing, flanked by chirp scaling and omega-K for efficiency and by backprojection for exactness in arbitrary geometries [1][2][3]. Interferometry extends the coherent image into the phase domain, where millimetric deformation and metric topography emerge from the same complex data [4][5]. Polarimetry completes the triad by decoding the scattering physics within each resolution cell [6]. Together these ideas have carried SAR from Wiley's 1951 insight through SEASAT, ERS, and SRTM to the operational Sentinel-1 era, in which deformation of the entire land surface is monitored every few days [8][9]. The open challenges — ionospheric correction, robust unwrapping, real-time onboard processing, and the fusion of SAR with optical and lidar modalities — ensure that the field's signal-theoretic core will remain fertile for decades.
