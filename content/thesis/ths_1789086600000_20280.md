# Byzantine Fault Tolerance in Partial Synchrony: PBFT, HotStuff, and Tendermint

## Abstract

This thesis develops a comparative study of Byzantine fault-tolerant state machine replication in the *partially synchronous* model, centered on three landmark protocols: Practical Byzantine Fault Tolerance (PBFT), HotStuff, and Tendermint. Beginning from the classical foundations — the FLP impossibility theorem for asynchronous consensus and the Byzantine Generals formulation with its tight 3f+1 bound — we show how partial synchrony, characterized by an unknown Global Stabilization Time (GST) after which message delays are bounded, makes deterministic agreement possible. We dissect PBFT's three-phase preprepare/prepare/commit pipeline and its intricate view-change machinery, HotStuff's linear-communication quorum-certificate framework with its three-chain commit rule and pipelined chaining, and Tendermint's gossip-based round structure with per-round proposer rotation and vote locking. Safety and liveness arguments, asymptotic and reported empirical performance, and a unified complexity comparison reveal how threshold signatures and leader rotation drove failover costs from quadratic to linear. We close with the field's persistent limitations: the 3f+1 fault ceiling, post-GST-only liveness, cryptographic trust assumptions, and the latency costs of chained pipelines.

## 1. Introduction

Consensus — getting a set of processes to agree on a single value despite failures — is the central problem of distributed computing. When failures are *Byzantine*, a faulty process may deviate arbitrarily: sending contradictory messages, colluding with other faulty processes, or withholding information strategically. This model captures the realities of modern infrastructure: compromised machines, adversarial software faults, and open networks of mutually distrustful participants [1].

Byzantine fault tolerance (BFT) underpins replicated databases, coordination services, and blockchain systems that must order transactions among parties that do not trust one another. The decisive question is not whether BFT consensus is possible but *how it can be made practical* in networks that are neither fully synchronous nor fully asynchronous.

The natural model is *partial synchrony*, introduced by Dwork, Lynch, and Stockmeyer [2]: there exists an unknown Global Stabilization Time (GST) after which message delivery becomes timely, though delays are unbounded before it. Partial synchrony rescues deterministic consensus from the FLP impossibility result [1] while remaining honest about wide-area networking. Nearly all deployed BFT systems live in this model.

This thesis compares the three protocol families that define the field:

- **PBFT** [3], Castro and Liskov's 1999 protocol that first made BFT practical, with its three-phase normal case and elaborate view-change protocol.
- **HotStuff** [4], Yin et al.'s 2019 protocol achieving *linear* communication and *responsiveness* simultaneously, via quorum certificates and chained pipelining.
- **Tendermint** [5], Buchman, Kwon, and Milosevic's gossip-based protocol modernizing the classical results for blockchains, with per-round proposer rotation and vote locking.

We reconstruct the theoretical foundations, give a phase-by-phase exposition of each protocol sufficient to verify its safety argument, present a unified analysis of complexity, latency, and liveness, and survey reported empirical results and remaining limitations.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 The Byzantine Generals Problem and the 3f+1 Bound

The Byzantine fault model originates with Lamport, Shostak, and Pease [6]. Generals must agree on a plan; some are traitors sending arbitrary messages. In the *oral messages* model (unauthenticated), they proved a sharp bound:

> **Theorem (Lamport–Shostak–Pease [6]):** No solution to the Byzantine Generals Problem exists with fewer than 3f+1 generals when up to f may be traitors, assuming oral messages.

With only 3f generals, traitors can partition loyal generals into two groups of f and feed them contradictory information that neither group can disambiguate. With *authenticated* messages — unforgeable signatures, the setting of all three protocols here — the 3f+1 bound still governs deterministic agreement in (partially) asynchronous systems, as the quorum-intersection arguments of Section 5 show [1][6]. All three protocols tolerate up to f Byzantine faults among n = 3f+1 replicas: the optimal resilience for their models.

### 2.2 The FLP Impossibility Result

The deepest classical obstacle is the Fischer–Lynch–Paterson theorem [1]:

> **Theorem (FLP [1]):** No deterministic protocol can guarantee consensus in a fully asynchronous system if even a single process may crash.

The proof is a *bivalency argument*: with no bound on message delay, a slow process is indistinguishable from a crashed one, so the adversarial scheduler can defer every decisive step and keep the execution bivalent forever.

Two escapes exist. **Randomization** circumvents FLP with probabilistic termination; PBFT, HotStuff, and Tendermint are deterministic and instead take the second route: **weakening asynchrony** to *partial synchrony* [2]. After GST, timeouts become meaningful, so a silent process can be declared faulty. Crucially, *safety* must hold in all executions (even before GST); *liveness* is required only after GST.

### 2.3 Partial Synchrony and the DLS Framework

Dwork, Lynch, and Stockmeyer formalized partial synchrony in 1988 and exhibited consensus protocols for it [2]. They distinguished timing assumptions — bounds on message delay and relative process speeds — that may hold always, eventually, or never. The practically relevant combination, unknown bounds holding *eventually*, is exactly the GST model. DLS proved consensus solvable here and introduced the *rotating coordinator* paradigm and the *locking* discipline that Tendermint later refined [2][5].

### 2.4 State Machine Replication and Quorum Systems

All three protocols implement *state machine replication* (SMR): correct replicas start from the same state and apply the same totally ordered sequence of requests. SMR reduces to *atomic broadcast*, which reduces to a sequence of consensus instances.

The analytical engine is the *quorum intersection* property. With n = 3f+1 and quorums of 2f+1, any two quorums overlap in at least f+1 replicas, of which at least one is honest:

> **Theorem (Honest Quorum Intersection):** In a system of n = 3f+1 replicas with at most f Byzantine faults, any two 2f+1 quorums share at least one correct replica.

This single honest witness carries *prepared* values across views and rounds: a value that gathered a quorum cannot be silently abandoned, because every future quorum contains a correct replica that remembers it. The argument underlies PBFT's view-change safety, HotStuff's three-chain rule, and Tendermint's locking mechanism alike [3][4][5].

## 3. Methodology

This is a comparative analytical study grounded in the primary literature, with five components:

1. **Primary-source reconstruction** from the canonical papers: PBFT [3], HotStuff [4], Tendermint [5], plus the Byzantine Generals [6], FLP [1], and partial-synchrony [2] foundations.
2. **Unified phase analysis** in a common vocabulary of phases and certificates — PBFT's prepare phase, HotStuff's generic quorum certificate, and Tendermint's polka are instances of the same quorum-certification idea.
3. **Complexity accounting**: messages and authenticators in the normal case and during leader failover, separating all-to-all broadcast (quadratic) from leader-collected threshold-signature aggregation (linear).
4. **Safety and liveness proof sketches** following the originals, in enough detail to be checkable.
5. **Empirical grounding** in the papers' reported measurements: PBFT's replicated NFS [3], HotStuff's 100+-replica deployment against BFT-SMaRt [4], and pipelined-HotStuff performance studies [7].

We claim no novel protocol: the contribution is systematic comparison, placing the designs in one framework so the technical advances appear as answers to well-posed subproblems.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 PBFT: The Three-Phase Core and the View-Change Machine

PBFT [3] was the first BFT protocol with acceptable real-system performance and remains the reference design. It runs among n = 3f+1 replicas with one *primary*; clients collect f+1 matching replies.

**Normal case.** A request traverses three phases:

1. **Pre-prepare.** The primary assigns sequence number s in view v and multicasts the proposal.
2. **Prepare.** Each backup multicasts a prepare vote on acceptance. A replica is *prepared* when it holds the pre-prepare plus 2f matching prepares — 2f+1 votes for the same proposal.
3. **Commit.** Replicas multicast commit votes; with 2f+1 matching commits the request is *committed* and executed in sequence-number order.

The prepare phase stops a faulty primary from equivocating: two conflicting proposals cannot both gather 2f+1 votes, by intersection. The commit phase ensures a committed value is known to a full quorum, so any later view-change quorum intersects it and inherits it [3].

**View change.** On suspected primary failure (request timeouts), replicas multicast `VIEW-CHANGE` messages carrying their latest stable checkpoint and prepared-but-uncommitted requests; the new primary (v mod n) collects 2f+1 of them and issues `NEW-VIEW`, re-proposing prepared requests. The view-change protocol is effectively a consensus protocol of its own, O(n²) in the worst case, and its correctness — no committed value is ever lost — is the heart of the paper [3]. Checkpoints with watermarks bound log growth and enable state transfer to slow replicas.

The cost structure: O(n²) messages per request in the normal case (all-to-all broadcast) and O(n²) or worse for view change. For small replica groups this was fine — the authors' replicated NFS ran only about 3% slower than unreplicated NFS [3] — but quadratic failover became the scaling bottleneck HotStuff would attack [4].

### 4.2 HotStuff: Linearity, Responsiveness, and the Three-Chain Rule

HotStuff [4] is built around one primitive: the *quorum certificate* (QC), an aggregation of 2f+1 signed votes that is constant-sized via threshold signatures. Replicas send partial signatures to the *leader*, which combines them — a star pattern costing O(n) authenticators per phase instead of O(n²).

**The three-phase discipline.** Each view, under a designated leader:

1. **Prepare.** The leader proposes a block extending the highest QC known; replicas vote if it extends their locked branch.
2. **Pre-commit.** The leader forms the prepareQC; replicas vote, and on seeing it they *lock* on that branch.
3. **Commit.** A second QC forms; replicas vote again.
4. **Decide.** A third QC lets replicas commit and execute.

The *three-chain rule* governs safety:

> **Theorem (HotStuff three-chain commit [4]):** A block is committed only when three QCs chain consecutively, each extending the previous. Then no conflicting block can ever be committed.

After the precommitQC, 2f+1 replicas are locked, so any future leader's proposal must extend an equally fresh QC (intersection), and conflicting branches can never assemble their own three-chain. The extra phase relative to PBFT's two buys *responsiveness*: the leader never waits for the maximum delay δ, advancing at *actual* network speed since each phase needs only n−f votes [4].

The replica voting discipline can be stated compactly:

```tla+
\* HotStuff replica vote rule (simplified)
Vote(block) ==
  /\ Extends(block, highQC)            \* proposal builds on freshest QC
  /\ block.view > lockedView => Extends(block, lockedQC)
  /\ lockedView' = Max(lockedView, block.prepareQC.view)
  /\ SendVote(leader, PartialSign(block))
```

**Chained HotStuff.** The paper's most influential idea is *pipelining*: one generic voting round simultaneously serves as the prepare of one block, the pre-commit of its parent, and the commit of its grandparent. Steady-state throughput reaches one committed block per round-trip under a correct leader, with a single uniform round type [4][7].

**Pacemaker and rotating leaders.** View changes are linear: on timeout, replicas send their highest QC to the next leader (O(n)), which simply proposes extending it — no PBFT-style log reconciliation. A *pacemaker* synchronizes views after GST via timeout certificates [4].

### 4.3 Tendermint: Gossip Rounds, Proposer Rotation, and Vote Locking

Tendermint [5] modernizes the DLS rotating-coordinator paradigm [2] over a peer-to-peer *gossip* layer, targeting blockchain deployments with large, dispersed validator sets. Every height proceeds in *rounds*, each with its own proposer chosen by weighted round-robin (by stake) — *even when the previous proposer was correct*.

**Round structure.** Each round has three steps:

1. **Propose.** The proposer broadcasts its block (or re-proposes its locked value, if locked).
2. **Prevote.** Validators gossip prevotes. Collecting prevotes from more than 2/3 of voting power for a value — a *polka* — causes a validator to *lock* on it, recording the round.
3. **Precommit.** Validators gossip precommits; more than 2/3 precommits *commit* the value at that height.

The *locking rule* is the safety engine: a locked validator prevotes only its locked value (or nil) in later rounds, unlocking only on a polka for a different value in a *later* round. After GST, the paper's termination argument shows some round has a correct proposer accepted by all correct validators, driving the system to a univalent configuration in which all decide [5].

**Gossip and termination.** Communication is epidemic rather than all-to-all: O(n²) worst case but robust, with no separate view-change subprotocol. Tendermint's central contribution is folding proposer replacement into ordinary rounds with growing timeouts, guaranteeing that after GST some round is long enough for a correct proposer's three steps to complete [5].

**Rotation by design.** Proposers rotate every round regardless of faults, so no failure detector is needed — rotation *is* the recovery. Every validator regularly proposes (reward fairness for proof-of-stake), at the cost of latency: three gossip steps per height in the best case, with gossip's constant factors exceeding direct broadcast [5].

### 4.4 Comparative Analysis: Complexity, Latency, and Liveness

| Dimension | PBFT [3] | HotStuff [4] | Tendermint [5] |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fault threshold | 3f+1, f Byzantine | 3f+1, f Byzantine | 3f+1 voting power, f Byzantine |
| Timing model | Partial synchrony | Partial synchrony | Partial synchrony |
| Normal-case phases | 3 (pre-prepare, prepare, commit) | 3 votes + decide (chained: 1 round) | 3 steps (propose, prevote, precommit) |
| Normal-case communication | O(n²) | O(n) | O(n²) via gossip |
| Leader failover cost | O(n²)–O(n³) view change | O(n) | O(n²), folded into rounds |
| Responsiveness | Yes | Yes (optimistic) | Round timeouts grow |
| Leader policy | Stable until suspected | Stable or rotating; pacemaker | Rotating every round |
| Signature aggregation | No | Threshold signatures (QCs) | No |
| Commit rule | 2f+1 commits | Three-chain of QCs | >2/3 precommits + locking |

Three observations emerge. *First*, HotStuff alone has linear cost in both the normal case and failover; leader-collected threshold-signature QCs are now the standard scalable-BFT blueprint [4][8]. *Second*, PBFT and Tendermint both pay quadratic costs for opposite reasons — all-to-all broadcast versus gossip — and gossip trades constant-factor overhead for simplicity and DoS resilience [5]. *Third*, the commit rules are one pattern: a value becomes irreversible once a quorum has *locked* on it in a way future quorums must respect, differing only in the lock's representation (prepared predicates, generic QCs, polka locks) [3][4][5].

Fast-HotStuff [8] later cut HotStuff to two phases while hardening the pipeline against forking attacks — the three-chain rule is elegant but not the final word on the latency–robustness frontier.

## 5. Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 Safety Arguments

**PBFT.** Core invariant: if a request is committed at some correct replica in view v, it is prepared in every later view's `NEW-VIEW`. By induction over views, using honest intersection twice — the 2f+1 commit quorum and the 2f+1 view-change quorum share a correct replica holding the prepared certificate — the new primary must re-propose it. No two correct replicas commit differently for the same sequence number [3].

**HotStuff.** If a block is committed (three chained QCs), every QC of equal or greater view extends its branch: the 2f+1 locked replicas refuse conflicting branches, and any future QC must include a locked replica's vote, cast only for that branch [4].

**Tendermint.** If a correct validator decides v, over 1/3 of *correct* validators are locked on v; a future polka for a conflicting value would need a locked validator's vote, which the locking rule forbids absent a later-round polka — a circularity the paper's lemmas resolve by showing the first conflicting polka cannot exist [5].

### 5.2 Liveness After GST

All three guarantee progress only after GST [2]. The arguments share a shape: after GST, timeouts exceed δ, so eventually a view/round has a correct leader whose messages arrive in time. PBFT's view change then installs a correct primary [3]; HotStuff's pacemaker synchronizes views onto a correct leader forming QCs at network speed [4]; Tendermint's growing round timeouts eventually yield a round long enough for a correct proposer's three steps [5].

### 5.3 Reported Measurements

- **PBFT** [3]: a BFT NFS implementation showed roughly 3% slowdown versus unreplicated NFS in LAN experiments; latency was dominated by cryptography (then RSA/MACs), not protocol structure.
- **HotStuff** [4]: a 100+-replica deployment achieved throughput and latency comparable to BFT-SMaRt while making leader failover linear instead of quadratic — linearity, not raw throughput, being the scaling enabler.
- **Pipelined HotStuff** [7]: follow-up studies quantify throughput under rotating leaders and degradation under forking attacks.

These are existence proofs — practical BFT at 100+ replicas — not head-to-head benchmarks, since hardware and workloads differ across studies.

### 5.4 The 3f+1 Bound Revisited

For deterministic Byzantine agreement in partial synchrony with authentication, n ≥ 3f+1 is necessary: with n ≤ 3f, the adversary splits correct replicas into two groups that each, with the faulty replicas, form a quorum, and drives them to decide differently — the argument of [6] adapts directly. All three protocols meet the bound; none can tolerate more faults per replica [1][6][3][4][5].

## 6. Limitations

**The 3f+1 ceiling.** Tolerating f Byzantine faults needs 3f+1 replicas — 3× overhead versus 2f+1 for crash faults. This is a hard floor of the deterministic partial-synchrony model, not a protocol artifact [6].

**Liveness only after GST.** Before GST, or under sustained asynchrony or targeted delay attacks, the protocols guarantee safety but may stall. An adversary selectively delaying messages can keep triggering view changes without commitment — a liveness attack no timeout tuning fully eliminates, since δ is unknown [1][2].

**Cryptographic trust.** All three assume unforgeable signatures and a PKI; HotStuff adds threshold signatures requiring a distributed key-generation ceremony. A broken signature scheme or compromised PKI voids the safety proofs silently [3][4].

**Leader-centric vulnerabilities.** PBFT's stable primary is a standing DoS target; HotStuff's pacemaker timeouts can be manipulated; Tendermint's every-round rotation guarantees faulty proposers regular wasted turns when f is large [3][4][5].

**Pipelining hazards.** Chained HotStuff admits forking attacks by Byzantine leaders; Fast-HotStuff [8] answers with a two-chain rule plus fork evidence, at the cost of unhappy-path overhead.

**Gossip costs.** Tendermint's gossip has worse constant factors than direct all-to-all; for small, well-connected groups it is pure overhead [5].

**Incentive gaps.** None addresses reward fairness, censorship by faulty-leader coalitions, or long-range attacks in proof-of-stake settings — dominant deployment concerns outside the safety formalism [5].

**Implementation complexity.** PBFT's view change, HotStuff's pacemaker, and Tendermint's lock/unlock rules are notoriously subtle; production code has repeatedly found safety-critical bugs in exactly these components. The gap between pen-and-paper proof and code remains the field's most dangerous chasm.

---

## 7. Conclusion

PBFT, HotStuff, and Tendermint trace a clear arc through partial synchrony. PBFT [3] proved BFT could be *practical*, paying with quadratic communication and a formidable view-change protocol. HotStuff [4] showed linearity and responsiveness could coexist, replacing log reconciliation with quorum certificates and chaining — the modern template for scalable BFT. Tendermint [5] took the DLS rotating coordinator [2] to its conclusion — a new proposer every round, gossip for robustness, locking for safety — becoming the consensus engine of a generation of blockchains.

Beneath the differences lies one mechanism: quorum intersection. Every safety argument here — prepared certificates surviving view change, the three-chain rule, polka locks — is the honest-overlap theorem of Section 2.4 in different clothes. The protocols differ in how they materialize quorums, when they rotate leaders, and what they optimize.

The frontier moves on — two-phase variants [8], pipelining analyses [7], DAG-based protocols — but all still answer to FLP [1] and Lamport's 3f+1 [6]. Those bounds are not going anywhere; the art of BFT is building the best possible protocol inside them.
