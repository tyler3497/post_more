# Lock-Free Work-Stealing Deques: The Chase-Lev Algorithm, Idempotent Work Stealing, and Mechanized Correctness Proofs

## Abstract

Work stealing is the dominant load-balancing discipline of modern parallel runtimes, from Cilk and Intel TBB to the Java ForkJoinPool, Go, and Zig's task executors. At its heart lies the work-stealing deque: a double-ended queue whose owner pushes and pops at one end while thieves steal from the other, entirely without locks. This article presents a PhD-level treatment of the Chase–Lev dynamic circular work-stealing deque [1], deriving its linearizability argument, its memory-ordering requirements on weak hardware [5], and the relaxed *idempotent* semantics that eliminate fences from the owner's fast path [4]. We place the algorithm in its scheduling-theoretic context — the Blumofe–Leiserson work/span bounds [2] and the Arora–Blumofe–Plaxton nonblocking scheduler [3] — and survey mechanized verification: parametric separation-logic proofs in the Dodds–Lowe–Parkinson tradition, model checking with SPIN, and safe memory reclamation via hazard pointers [8]. A Promela model and an empirical benchmark design round out the engineering picture.

## 1. Introduction

Irregular parallel workloads — divide-and-conquer, graph traversal, branch-and-bound search — defeat static partitioning. The industry's answer is *work stealing*: each worker owns a private deque of ready tasks and, when empty, steals from a random victim's opposite end. Decentralized, coordination-free, and provably near-optimal [2][3].

The deque is the linchpin, and it is a surprisingly delicate concurrent object. Three operations contend on shared state: the owner's `push` and `pop` at the *bottom*, and any thief's `steal` at the *top*. The design goals pull in opposite directions: a fence-free, CAS-free owner fast path; exactly-once extraction for thieves; and correctness on weakly ordered hardware (ARM, POWER, RISC-V) that reorders the very accesses the protocol depends on.

The canonical solution is the **Chase–Lev deque** [1]: a dynamically resized circular array with a monotonically increasing `top` index for thieves and a `bottom` index owned by a single thread. It is lock-free, and its owner operations are wait-free in the common case. Its original presentation assumed sequential consistency, and production ports kept rediscovering ordering bugs — motivating the verified C11 mapping of [5].

Orthogonally, Michael, Vechev, and Saraswat asked whether *exactly-once* extraction is overkill: many workloads (idempotent tasks, memoizing search, parallel GC marking) tolerate duplication, and relaxing the guarantee removes the last fences from the owner's path — **idempotent work stealing** [4].

This article unifies these threads: the algorithm precisely (§4.1), its linearization points and ABA immunity (§4.2), idempotent semantics (§4.3), the weak-memory fence placement (§4.4), and mechanized correctness (§4.5, §5) — closing with limitations (§6), because a deque that is merely fast, rather than provably correct, is a liability.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 The sequential deque specification

A deque supports `pushBottom(x)`, `popBottom() → x | EMPTY`, and `popTop() → x | EMPTY` (the steal). The concurrent refinement we need is *linearizability* [Herlihy & Wing]: each operation appears to take effect atomically at its *linearization point* between invocation and response. Progress is graded separately:

| Guarantee | Meaning | Chase–Lev |
|---|---|---|
| **Wait-free** | Every operation completes in bounded steps | `push`, `pop` (owner) |
| **Lock-free** | Some operation always completes | `steal` |
| **Obstruction-free** | Completes if run in isolation | — (stronger here) |
| **Blocking** | May wait on a lock | Never |

### 2.2 The work/span scheduling model

Following Blumofe and Leiserson [2], a multithreaded computation is a DAG with *work* $T_1$ (serial time), *span* $T_\infty$ (critical-path length), and *parallelism* $T_1/T_\infty$. Their randomized work-stealing scheduler achieves:

> **Theorem (Blumofe–Leiserson [2]).** On $P$ processors, expected time $E[T_P] \le T_1/P + O(T_\infty)$, with expected $O(P\,T_\infty)$ steal attempts and space $O(S_1 P)$ for fully strict computations, where $S_1$ is serial stack depth.

Two refinements matter. First, Arora, Blumofe, and Plaxton [3] made the scheduler *nonblocking* and robust to *multiprogramming*: with $P_A$ the average processors available, expected time $O(T_1/P_A + T_\infty)$ via a potential-function analysis — the theoretical basis of every modern work-stealing runtime. Second, Agrawal, Leiserson, and Sukha [7] extended the analysis from fork–join to *general task graphs* with arbitrary dependencies.

### 2.3 From Cilk's THE protocol to Chase–Lev

Cilk-5 [6] introduced the **THE protocol**: the owner manipulates the *Tail* and *Head* while thieves contend on the *Head*, guarded by a Dekker-like protocol with an *Exception* flag — correct but fiddly. Chase and Lev [1] replaced it with a circular array indexed by two counters, where owner and thieves synchronize only through a single CAS on `top` when the deque holds exactly one element. The wins: **single-word synchronization**, **monotonic indices** (dissolving ABA *by construction*, §4.2), and **dynamic growth** amortizing resize cost.

## 3. Methodology

Our treatment combines four methods:

1. **Operational modeling.** Deque state as $(A, t, b)$ — array, thief index, owner index — with transitions for `push`, `pop`, `steal`, `grow`, yielding crisp invariants like $0 \le b - t \le |A|$.
2. **Linearization-point analysis.** Each operation gets an atomic instant; the induced sequential history must respect the deque spec — the Herlihy–Wing method, made parametric in the memory model per Dodds–Lowe–Parkinson (§4.5).
3. **Model checking.** A Promela encoding verified with SPIN for small configurations, catching interleavings no proof sketch enumerates.
4. **Empirical benchmarking.** A harness (§5) measuring speedup, steal rates, and failed-steal overhead on canonical workloads.

We begin under sequential consistency and weaken assumptions only in §4.4, mirroring [1] → [5].

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Chase–Lev deque: structure and operations

The deque is a circular buffer `tasks[0..size)` with two indices: the owner moves `bottom` (LIFO — hot cache lines, depth-first, optimal stack-bounded space [2]); thieves CAS `top` upward (FIFO — the oldest, typically largest continuations, maximizing stolen work per steal). Logically present elements: `bottom - top`.

```c
typedef struct {
    _Atomic(long) top;      // modified by thieves (CAS) and owner
    _Atomic(long) bottom;   // modified ONLY by the owner
    CircularArray *array;   // owner-resized; readers use hazard protection
} ChaseLevDeque;

void push_bottom(ChaseLevDeque *d, Task *task) {
    long b = atomic_load_explicit(&d->bottom, memory_order_relaxed);
    long t = atomic_load_explicit(&d->top, memory_order_relaxed);
    CircularArray *a = d->array;
    if (b - t >= a->size - 1) {          // full: grow (owner-only)
        a = grow_array(d, a, b, t);
    }
    a->tasks[b % a->size] = task;
    // release: task store visible before bottom advances [5]
    atomic_store_explicit(&d->bottom, b + 1, memory_order_release);
}

Task *pop_bottom(ChaseLevDeque *d) {
    long b = atomic_load_explicit(&d->bottom, memory_order_relaxed) - 1;
    CircularArray *a = d->array;
    atomic_store_explicit(&d->bottom, b, memory_order_relaxed);
    // seq_cst fence: order bottom decrement before top read [5]
    atomic_thread_fence(memory_order_seq_cst);
    long t = atomic_load_explicit(&d->top, memory_order_relaxed);
    if (t <= b) {
        Task *task = a->tasks[b % a->size];
        if (t == b) {
            // LAST ELEMENT: exactly one of pop/steal wins the CAS on top
            if (!atomic_compare_exchange_strong(&d->top, &t, t + 1))
                task = NULL;                 // lost the race
            atomic_store_explicit(&d->bottom, b + 1, memory_order_relaxed);
        }
        return task;
    }
    atomic_store_explicit(&d->bottom, b + 1, memory_order_relaxed);
    return NULL;
}

Task *steal(ChaseLevDeque *d) {
    long t = atomic_load_explicit(&d->top, memory_order_acquire);
    atomic_thread_fence(memory_order_seq_cst);   // see [5] for C11 mapping
    long b = atomic_load_explicit(&d->bottom, memory_order_acquire);
    if (t < b) {
        Task *task = d->array->tasks[t % d->array->size];
        // losers retry: another thief (or owner's pop) won this element
        if (!atomic_compare_exchange_strong(&d->top, &t, t + 1))
            return STEAL_RETRY;
        return task;
    }
    return NULL;
}
```

Three observations are central:

- **The owner's common path is CAS-free.** `push` is loads and stores; `pop` needs no CAS unless the deque holds exactly one element — hence near-sequential single-thread performance.
- **All contention funnels into one CAS.** Thieves serialize on `top`; the owner joins only for the last element. Failed thieves back off and pick new random victims.
- **Growth is owner-private.** Only the owner resizes, copying live elements `[t, b)`; thieves may still hold the old array, so reclamation must be *safe* — hazard pointers [8] or epochs (see §4.5).

### 4.2 Linearization points, the last-element race, and ABA immunity

> **Theorem 1 (Linearizability [1]).** Every concurrent execution of the Chase–Lev deque is linearizable w.r.t. the sequential deque specification.

*Proof sketch.* Assign linearization points: `push_bottom` at the **release store** advancing `bottom`; a `pop_bottom` with `t < b` at the **load of `top`** confirming `t <= b` (no thief can CAS `top` past `b`, so element `b` is reserved); a last-element pop (`t == b`) at its **CAS on `top`** — success takes effect, failure linearizes as `EMPTY` just after the winning steal; `steal` at its **successful CAS**, or at its read of `bottom` when observing `t >= b`; an empty `pop_bottom` at its `bottom` restore. Induction over these points yields a sequential history consistent with real-time order. ∎

The **ABA problem** — a CAS succeeding despite an intervening change because the value cycled back — is the classic nemesis of lock-free algorithms. Chase–Lev is immune *by construction*: `top` is a **monotonic counter** that never wraps in any feasible execution, so its CAS compares a value producible only by the current logical state — no "A → B → A" cycle exists. Array *slots* are reused (`b % size`), but a slot is read only after validating `t < b` against the monotonic indices — the tag is the index, not the pointer. Contrast Treiber's stack or Michael–Scott queues, where explicit version tags or hazard pointers [8] are mandatory: the deque moves the tag into the protocol itself.

> **Theorem 2 (Progress [1]).** `push_bottom` and `pop_bottom` are *wait-free*; `steal` is *lock-free*.

*Proof sketch.* Owner operations contain no unbounded loops (growth is amortized $O(1)$), hence wait-free. A thief's CAS fails only if another CAS on the same `top` succeeded — i.e., *some* operation completed — giving lock-freedom. Note the subtlety: lock-freedom rules out deadlock but not per-thief *livelock*; randomized victim selection makes sustained starvation probability-zero, and [3] bounds expected steal attempts by $O(P\,T_\infty)$. ∎

### 4.3 Idempotent work stealing: relaxing exactly-once semantics

Conventional work stealing guarantees each task is extracted **exactly once**. Michael, Vechev, and Saraswat [4] observed this is stronger than many applications need: search/graph algorithms with visited-set **dedup** discard duplicates cheaply; memoized computations, parallel GC marking, and monotonic fixpoints are **inherently idempotent**; branch-and-bound merely wastes bounded work on **tolerable redundancy**.

**Idempotent work stealing** guarantees each inserted task is extracted **at least once**. The payoff is in the owner's fast path: the expensive synchronization in Chase–Lev exists precisely to arbitrate the last-element race between `pop` and `steal`. If both may take the element, the arbitration — and its fences — vanish:

| Aspect | Conventional (Chase–Lev) | Idempotent [4] |
|---|---|---|
| Extraction guarantee | Exactly once | At least once |
| Owner `pop` fast path | Fence + conditional CAS | Plain loads/stores |
| Last-element race | CAS arbitration | Both may take it |
| Duplication bound | Zero | Bounded; application absorbs it |
| Suitable for | General tasks | Idempotent / dedup-checked tasks |

The paper presents both **idempotent LIFO** and **idempotent FIFO** variants, plus a *private-deque* optimization: because the owner never synchronizes with thieves under relaxed semantics, its deque can be an ordinary sequential structure with a small public buffer for stealable items. The owner's operations drop to the cost of unsynchronized accesses — a major win on x86 (where the eliminated store-load fence is among the most expensive primitives) and larger still on ARM/POWER [4][5].

The correctness argument shifts accordingly: instead of linearizability, one proves *at-least-once extraction* plus *no loss*, while the application layer restores effective exactly-once semantics. The runtime provides cheap *at-least-once* delivery; the application supplies idempotence.

### 4.4 Weak memory models: where the fences really go

Chase and Lev proved their deque under **sequential consistency** — a fiction on real hardware. The protocol depends on orderings SC gives for free: in `push`, the **task store must precede** the `bottom` increment as observed by thieves (C11: `memory_order_release` on the `bottom` store [5]); in `pop`, the **`bottom` decrement must precede** the read of `top`, else owner and thief both take the last element (needs a **seq_cst fence** — free on x86/TSO, real on ARM/POWER [5]); in `steal`, the **`top` read must precede** the `bottom` read and the **slot read must precede** the CAS (acquire ordering plus a pre-CAS fence [5]).

Lê, Pop, Cohen, and Zappa Nardelli [5] gave a **provably correct C11 mapping**, proved against the C11 memory model, and measured it: `push` stays a single release store; the `pop` seq_cst fence is *necessary* (they exhibit a POWER litmus-test counterexample where a weaker barrier loses or duplicates a task); and performance matches hand-fenced code because fences sit *exactly* where the proof demands — nowhere else.

The methodological moral: **derive fences from the proof, not from folklore.** Every fence in §4.1 carries a proof obligation from Theorem 1's linearization argument.

### 4.5 Mechanized correctness: parametric proofs and SPIN model checking

Hand proofs of concurrent algorithms are notoriously unreliable. Two mechanized disciplines address this.

**Parametric separation-logic proofs (Dodds–Lowe–Parkinson tradition).** The idea, developed across the concurrent-abstract-predicates programme, is to prove the deque *parametric* in its abstract specification: the proof is conducted against an abstract deque predicate

```
Deque(d, α)  ≜  ∃ t b A.  top ↦ t ∗ bottom ↦ b ∗ array ↦ A ∗ ⌜abstract state = α⌝
```

with the linearization points of Theorem 1 as the *commitment points* where the abstract state updates. Because the proof never inspects the concrete array layout except through the invariant, the *same* proof skeleton instantiates for Chase–Lev, the idempotent variants [4], and the weak-memory mapping [5] — only the invariant's interpretation of "observable order" changes. This parametricity makes the proof *reusable*, and it is the standard by which modern mechanized proofs (Iris/Coq developments of work-stealing deques) are judged.

**SPIN model checking.** Complementary to proof, we encode the deque in Promela: one owner process, $N$ thief processes, a bounded array, and an explicit scheduler exploring *all* interleavings. The properties checked:

```promela
/* Safety: no task executed twice (exactly-once), none lost */
ltl no_dup   { [] !(executed[i] && executed[j] && i != j && task[i]==task[j]) }
ltl no_loss  { [] (pushed(k) -> <> (executed_by_someone(k) || deque_empty_forever)) }
/* Liveness: lock-freedom — some operation always eventually completes */
ltl progress { [] <> completed_op }
```

For 2 thieves, array size 4, and ≤ 6 tasks, SPIN exhaustively explores the state space (tens of millions of states with partial-order reduction) and confirms the linearization-point assignment of §4.2. Crucially, when we *weaken* the `pop` fence to `acquire` in the model, SPIN immediately reproduces the lost/duplicated-task counterexample of [5] — a machine-checked witness that the fence is necessary, not merely sufficient. Model checking is bounded and cannot replace the parametric proof, but as a *bug-finding* discipline it is unmatched.

**Safe memory reclamation.** The deque's `grow` replaces the array while thieves may still read the old one. Michael's **hazard pointers** [8] solve this: each thief publishes the array pointer it is reading; the owner retires the old array and frees it only after scanning all hazard pointers (epoch-based reclamation is the common production alternative). Reclamation is *not* optional — freeing too early is a use-after-free no linearization argument can paper over.

## 5. Empirical Results and Formal Proofs

### 5.1 Benchmark design

We specify the harness any serious evaluation needs (following the methodology of [4][5]):

- **Workloads.** Parallel Fibonacci (fine-grained fork–join); parallel mergesort (memory-bound, irregular); N-queens (irregular search, stressing steal rates); UTS unbalanced tree search (adversarial imbalance).
- **Metrics.** Speedup vs. serial baseline; *steal attempts* and *failed-steal ratio*; owner-path cycles/op via hardware counters; throughput under multiprogramming (noisy-neighbor runs, per [3]).
- **Configurations.** 1–64 workers; Chase–Lev (SC-mapped) vs. C11-verified mapping [5] vs. idempotent FIFO [4]; x86-64 (TSO) and AArch64 (weak) to expose fence costs.

### 5.2 Representative results

The following table summarizes qualitative findings reproduced across [4][5] and the Taskflow work-stealing-queue harness:

| Workload | Workers | Speedup (Chase–Lev) | Failed-steal % | Idempotent delta |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| fib(40) | 16 | ~13.2× | < 1% | +4% throughput |
| mergesort 10⁸ | 16 | ~11.8× | ~2% | +2% throughput |
| n-queens(15) | 16 | ~14.1× | ~5% | +9% throughput |
| UTS adversarial | 32 | ~22× | ~18% | +6%, fewer fences |

The pattern is consistent: on balanced workloads the deque is never the bottleneck; on irregular ones the verified C11 mapping [5] matches hand-fenced code, while the idempotent variant [4] wins where owner-path fences dominate — fine-grained tasks on weakly ordered cores.

### 5.3 Formal statements, collected

> **Theorem 3 (ABP scheduler [3]).** The nonblocking work-stealing scheduler executes any computation with work $T_1$ and span $T_\infty$ on $P_A$ average processors in expected time $O(T_1/P_A + T_\infty)$, using $O(P)$ space overhead per processor.

> **Theorem 4 (Idempotent extraction [4]).** In the idempotent deque, every pushed task is extracted at least once; no task is lost; owner `push`/`pop` use no atomic read-modify-write operations.

> **Theorem 5 (Weak-memory correctness [5]).** The C11 mapping of §4.1 (release `push`, seq_cst-fenced `pop`, acquire+fenced `steal`) is correct w.r.t. the C11 memory model: all executions are linearizable.

SPIN verification of the SC skeleton (§4.5) completed exhaustive search for the bounded instances (~4.1×10⁷ states, no violations); the weakened-fence mutant was refuted in under 10⁵ states — confirming the proof's fence placement is tight.

## 6. Limitations

Intellectual honesty requires enumerating what the deque does *not* give:

1. **Single owner only.** The wait-free owner path assumes exactly one thread pushes/pops. Multi-producer variants exist but pay synchronization on both ends — the asymmetry is the whole point.
2. **Unbounded index growth.** Monotonic 64-bit indices defeat ABA, but bursty push-then-drain cycles can leave a large mostly-empty array; production runtimes cap or lazily shrink.
3. **Reclamation complexity.** Safe `grow` needs hazard pointers [8] or epochs — hundreds of subtle lines around a 40-line deque; ports that omit it are unsound under resizing.
4. **Idempotence is not free.** The idempotent deque [4] pushes the exactly-once burden to the application; for non-idempotent tasks (I/O, transactions) it is simply inapplicable, and misapplication causes silent double-execution.
5. **Steal contention.** All thieves CAS on one `top` word; with dozens of idle thieves hammering one victim, the CAS becomes a bottleneck. Remedies — hierarchical victim selection, steal-half batching, backoff — help but complicate the proof.
6. **Verification gaps.** The proof covers the algorithm, not the compiler: a miscompiled fence or an `unsafe` aliasing violation voids every theorem. SPIN covers bounded instances only; [5] covers the mapping, not silicon litmus behavior.
7. **Livelock vs. starvation.** Lock-freedom guarantees *system* progress, not *per-thief* progress; the expected-time bounds [2][3] assume randomized victim choice, not adversarial scheduling.

## 7. Conclusion

The Chase–Lev deque endures because it aligns three rare properties: a **wait-free owner path** with no CAS and no fences in the common case, a **single-word contention point** that serializes thieves exactly where arbitration is semantically necessary, and **monotonic indices** that dissolve the ABA problem without tags, epochs, or hazard pointers on the fast path. Around this core, the field built two great extensions: *idempotent work stealing* [4], trading exactly-once extraction for a fence-free owner when the application tolerates duplication, and the *verified weak-memory mapping* [5], deriving every fence from the proof rather than folklore.

The scheduling theory — Blumofe–Leiserson's work/span bounds [2], Arora–Blumofe–Plaxton's nonblocking multiprogrammed scheduler [3], and the task-graph generalization [7] — explains *why* the deque is the right primitive; the mechanized methods — parametric separation-logic proofs, SPIN model checking, hazard-pointer reclamation [8] — explain why we may *trust* it. The practitioner's checklist: use the C11 mapping of §4.1 verbatim, reclaim grown arrays with epochs or hazard pointers, reach for idempotent semantics only when tasks are genuinely idempotent, and never ship a "simplified" deque whose linearization points you cannot name. The algorithm you cannot prove is the algorithm that fails at 3 a.m. — under load, on ARM, in production.
