# Congestion Control Theory: BBR's Model-Based Rate Control, CUBIC's Cubic Window Growth, and the Bufferbloat Dilemma

## Abstract

Congestion control is the distributed algorithm by which the Internet divides finite bandwidth among competing flows without centralized coordination. This thesis examines the two dominant modern paradigms — loss-based control, epitomized by CUBIC's time-dependent cubic window function, and model-based rate control, embodied by Google's BBR, which estimates bottleneck bandwidth and round-trip propagation time to operate at Kleinrock's optimal point — against the pathology that motivated both: bufferbloat, the excess buffering that converts queue occupancy into seconds of latency. We derive the control-theoretic foundations of each scheme, trace the delay-based lineage from TCP Vegas through FAST TCP, analyze active queue management remedies including CoDel and FQ-CoDel, and evaluate empirical evidence from Internet-scale deployment and independent measurement studies. We prove the key stability and convergence properties, quantify the fairness trade-offs addressed by BBRv2 and BBRv3, and conclude that the future of congestion control lies not in any single endpoint algorithm but in the co-design of endpoints and queues.

## 1 Introduction

The Internet is, at its core, an exercise in *distributed optimization without a controller*.

Three conceptual eras structure the field.

Hovering over all three eras is **bufferbloat**: the discovery, popularized by Jim Gettys and Kathleen Nichols, that cheap memory has filled the network edge with oversized buffers, so that loss-based controllers fill queues for *seconds* before seeing any signal [6].

This thesis proceeds as follows.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Jacobson's Algorithms and the Loss-Based Tradition

Before 1988, TCP had no congestion control: senders injected packets at the rate the receiver's advertised window allowed, and the Internet experienced its first *congestion collapse*.

But AIMD has a scaling disease.

$$
T \approx \frac{C \cdot MSS}{RTT \sqrt{p}}
$$

where `p` is the loss rate and `C ≈ 1.22`.

### 2.2 High-Speed Variants and BIC

The 2000s produced a zoo of high-speed variants — HSTCP, STCP, H-TCP, and most influentially **BIC-TCP**, which used binary search to locate available capacity.

### 2.3 The Delay-Based Lineage

**TCP Vegas** (Brakmo and Peterson, 1994) observed that waiting for loss is waiting too long: queueing delay rises *before* loss occurs [3]. Vegas computes, once per RTT:

- `Expected = cwnd / BaseRTT` (throughput if the path were empty)
- `Actual = cwnd / RTT` (measured throughput)
- `Diff = (Expected − Actual) × BaseRTT` (estimated backlog in packets)

If `Diff < α`, increase cwnd linearly; if `Diff > β`, decrease it; otherwise hold steady.

**FAST TCP** [4] generalized this idea into a full control-theoretic framework: each flow adjusts its window to maintain `α` packets queued, solving a distributed utility-maximization problem whose equilibrium is weighted proportionally fair. FAST demonstrated that delay-based control can be stable, scalable, and fair — but deployment revealed fragilities: delay signals are polluted by reverse-path queueing, route changes, and competing loss-based flows that fill the very queues delay-based flows are trying to keep empty.

### 2.4 Bufferbloat and the Queue as Adversary

Gettys and Nichols' bufferbloat work [6] identified a structural shift: as memory became cheap, vendors provisioned *enormous* buffers at the network edge — home gateways, cable modems, cellular base stations — often holding seconds of packets.

> **Theorem (The Standing-Queue Trap):** *A loss-based AIMD controller sharing a bottleneck with buffer `B ≫ C \times RTT` converges to a steady state in which the queue holds `Θ(B)` packets and the end-to-end delay exceeds `RTprop` by `Θ(B/C)`, while no congestion signal is generated until the buffer overflows. Delay, not throughput, absorbs the entire cost of the excess buffer.*

The remedy has two halves: endpoints that do not require loss to detect congestion, and queues that signal congestion *before* they grow — active queue management [6][7].

## 3 Methodology

We model the network path as a single bottleneck link with capacity `C` (packets/s) and propagation delay `RTprop`, preceded by a buffer of size `B`.

$$
RTT(t) = RTprop + \frac{Q(t)}{C}
$$

where `Q(t)` is the instantaneous queue occupancy.

**Kleinrock's optimal operating point** is the foundation of all model-based control: maximum throughput and minimum delay are achieved simultaneously when `I = C × RTprop` — exactly one bandwidth-delay product in flight — and `Q = 0`. Sending less wastes capacity; sending more gains nothing in throughput (the bottleneck still serves at `C`) and adds `Q/C` seconds of pure delay. Every congestion control algorithm can be understood as a strategy for locating and tracking this point under uncertainty about `C` and `RTprop`.

We evaluate algorithms against four criteria:

1. **Efficiency**: fraction of bottleneck capacity utilized at equilibrium.
2. **Delay**: steady-state queueing delay, `Q/C`.
3. **Fairness**: Jain's fairness index `J = (Σxᵢ)² / (n · Σxᵢ²)` across `n` competing flows, plus RTT-fairness (flows with different RTTs) and inter-protocol fairness (coexistence of different algorithms).
4. **Convergence**: time to reach equilibrium after a change in capacity, RTT, or flow population.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 CUBIC: The Cubic Window Growth Function

CUBIC's central insight is that the congestion window should be a function of *time since the last loss*, not of the RTT.

Let `W_max` be the window at the last loss event.

$$
W(t) = C (t - K)^3 + W_{max}, \quad K = \sqrt[3]{\frac{W_{max}(1-\beta)}{C}}
$$

with `C = 0.4`.

- The **plateau** around `W_max` keeps the window near the known-good operating point for most of the congestion epoch, maximizing utilization and stability — most window samples cluster near `W_max`, avoiding the burst losses that convex-only schemes trigger at the saturation point [2].
- The **concave approach** means that in small-BDP networks, where the multiplicative decrease leaves the window near the plateau, growth is slow — CUBIC behaves like standard TCP there (the *TCP-friendly region*, where CUBIC explicitly caps itself at Reno's window).
- The **convex probe** means that on large-BDP paths, CUBIC aggressively seeks newly available bandwidth, solving the Mathis scaling disease.

Because growth depends on elapsed time rather than ACK clocking, two CUBIC flows with different RTTs grow at the same rate — dramatically improving RTT-fairness over Reno, whose per-RTT increments inherently favor short-RTT flows.

```python
# Simplified CUBIC window update (per ACK), after Ha, Rhee & Xu [2]
BETA = 0.7   # multiplicative decrease factor
C = 0.4      # cubic scaling constant

def cubic_update(w_max, t, origin_point):
    """Return cwnd t seconds after the last loss event."""
    K = ((w_max * (1.0 - BETA)) / C) ** (1.0 / 3.0)
    # time to return to w_max
    return C * (t - K) ** 3 + w_max

def on_loss(cwnd):
    w_max = cwnd
    cwnd = BETA * cwnd          # gentler than Reno's 0.5
    epoch_start = now()
    return w_max, cwnd, epoch_start
```

The protocol's weakness is structural: it is still *loss-driven*, so on a bloated buffer it fills the queue to overflow before reacting — the standing-queue trap of Section 2.4.

### 4.2 BBR: Model-Based Rate Control

BBR [5] abandons the window-as-state paradigm entirely.

- **Pacing rate** = `BtlBw × pacing_gain` — how fast to inject packets (pacing spreads transmissions smoothly across the RTT, eliminating bursts).
- **Congestion window** = `BtlBw × RTprop × cwnd_gain` — the cap on data in flight, set to a small multiple of the estimated BDP.

The estimates are maintained with *windowed* filters robust to noise:

- `BtlBw` = maximum delivery rate (`delivered / Δt`) observed over the last 10 RTTs. The maximum is the right statistic: the bottleneck can only *under*-deliver when the sender under-sends, so the max sample is the least biased estimate of true capacity.
- `RTprop` = minimum RTT observed over the last 10 seconds. The minimum is the right statistic: propagation delay is the *floor* of the RTT distribution; anything above it is queueing.

These two filters are BBR's conceptual core: they convert noisy per-ACK measurements into a coherent path model, and every control decision flows from the model rather than from loss events.

The **state machine** cycles through four phases:

| State | Purpose | pacing_gain | Exit condition |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Startup** | Rapidly discover BtlBw, like slow start | 2.89 (`2/ln 2`) | Bandwidth growth < 25% for 3 RTTs |
| **Drain** | Empty the queue built during Startup | 0.75 | In-flight ≤ BDP |
| **ProbeBW** | Steady state; probe for more/less bandwidth | cycling 1.25, 0.75, 1, 1, 1, 1, 1, 1 | gain cycle period (8 RTTs) |
| **ProbeRTT** | Re-measure RTprop | 1.0, cwnd = 4 packets | 200 ms or 1 RTT, every 10 s |

In **ProbeBW**, the 8-phase gain cycle is BBR's answer to the fundamental exploration problem: gains above 1.0 probe for additional capacity, gains below 1.0 drain any queue the probe created, and the cycle averages to 1.0 so long-term throughput matches the estimated bottleneck rate.

> **Theorem (BBR Operating Point):** *If the estimates satisfy `BtlBw = C` and `RTprop = D`, then in ProbeBW with average `pacing_gain = 1` and `cwnd_gain = 2`, BBR converges to in-flight `I ∈ [C·D, 2C·D]`, i.e., queueing delay bounded by one `RTprop`, independent of the buffer size `B`. The standing-queue trap is structurally avoided: delay no longer grows with `B`.*

This is BBR's decisive break from the loss-based tradition: on a bloated buffer, CUBIC converges to seconds of delay; BBR converges to a small multiple of the propagation delay, because its target (the estimated BDP) does not depend on the buffer at all [5].

### 4.3 Bufferbloat and Active Queue Management: CoDel and FQ-CoDel

BBR fixes the endpoint, but the queue itself can be fixed too — and must be, for the billions of loss-based flows that will not upgrade.

1. **Measure sojourn time, not queue length.** The delay each packet actually experiences is the user-visible quantity and is independent of link rate; byte-counts are not comparable across a 1 Mbps DSL line and a 10 Gbps data-center link.
2. **Track the *local minimum* queue delay**, following Jacobson's 2006 insight. A *good* queue absorbs bursts and drains within an RTT; a *bad* queue — the standing queue of bufferbloat — persists across RTTs. The minimum delay over a recent interval distinguishes them robustly.
3. **Keep one state variable**, not a window of samples: how long the minimum has been above or below the target.

The control law is parameterless in practice: `target = 5 ms` standing queue delay, `interval = 100 ms` (order of a worst-case RTT).

**FQ-CoDel** [7] (RFC 8290) combines CoDel with *flow queueing*: packets are hashed by 5-tuple into (by default 1024) separate queues, each running CoDel independently, served by a deficit-round-robin scheduler that prioritizes *new* (sparse) flows. This solves CoDel's weakness against unresponsive or aggressive flows — a single bulk flow can no longer hold the shared queue hostage — and gives interactive flows (DNS, gaming, video calls) dramatically lower delay without any configuration. It is now the default qdisc in many Linux distributions and OpenWrt, arguably the single most deployed bufferbloat remedy in existence.

The relationship between AQM and congestion control is complementary, not competitive: AQM bounds the damage any endpoint algorithm can do, while model-based endpoints like BBR reduce the need for AQM to intervene at all.

### 4.4 The Delay-Based Lineage: Vegas, FAST TCP, and Hybrids

Vegas's `Diff`-based controller [3] was the first proof that delay signals could drive a complete congestion control loop: retransmission timers from fine-grained RTT estimates, congestion avoidance from backlog estimation, and even a modified slow start that paces growth against measured queueing.

$$
w \leftarrow w + \gamma \left( \alpha - x \cdot q \right)
$$

(where `x` is throughput and `q` queueing delay) converges to an equilibrium maintaining exactly `α` packets queued per flow — a *weighted proportionally fair* allocation provably stable in the absence of feedback delay.

The lineage's practical problem is *signal corruption*: queueing delay is polluted by reverse-path congestion, delayed ACKs, and — fatally — by competing loss-based flows.

**TCP Illinois** deserves mention as the pragmatic hybrid: it uses loss as the *primary* signal (direction of window change) and queueing delay as the *secondary* signal (pace of change), achieving high-speed scalability without abandoning the loss signal the Internet's queues actually generate.

### 4.5 Fairness Dynamics: BBR Against the World

BBRv1's deployment at Google and YouTube produced striking headline numbers — but independent measurement quickly found fairness pathologies.

**BBRv2** (2019) redesigned the algorithm around explicit congestion signals: it adds loss and ECN reaction, bounds inflight between `inflight_lo` and `inflight_hi` (with `inflight_hi` capping the queue a flow may maintain), and replaces the fixed gain cycle with adaptive Cruise/Refill/Up/Down phases. Evaluations on the Dropbox edge network showed BBRv2 eliminating BBRv1's massive retransmissions on shallow buffers, reducing inflight variance, and improving inter-protocol fairness with CUBIC [8]. **BBRv3** (specified in the 2024 IETF draft) continues this trajectory: gentler Startup, refined loss/ECN thresholds, and better coexistence with loss-based flows under fair-queueing AQMs [5].

The pattern is instructive: BBR began as a pure model-based design and has been progressively *re-coupled* to the congestion signals (loss, ECN, delay variation) it originally sought to transcend — because a congestion controller does not operate on an idealized path but in an ecosystem of competing algorithms, and ecosystem fitness requires speaking the ecosystem's signaling language.

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 Deployment Evidence

- **Google's BBR rollout** (YouTube, google.com): reported throughput gains of 4% on average and up to 14% on high-loss paths, with retransmission rates dropping by an order of magnitude on lossy links; on shallow-buffered bottlenecks BBR's gains over CUBIC were largest, precisely where loss-based control wastes capacity on underfilled buffers [5].
- **CUBIC at Internet scale**: as the Linux default since 2006, CUBIC carries a large fraction of global TCP traffic; its time-based growth is credited with stable high utilization on long-fat pipes where Reno's recovery times stretched to minutes [2].
- **Dropbox edge evaluation of BBRv2**: fewer unacked packets in flight than BBRv1 and slightly fewer than CUBIC; RTT-vs-inflight heatmaps showed BBRv1's inflight scaling with RTT (the unfairness mechanism) eliminated in BBRv2; BBRv2 connections spent far less time receive-window-limited [8].
- **FQ-CoDel in the field**: OpenWrt and major Linux distributions ship FQ-CoDel as default; bufferbloat.net community measurements show reductions of loaded latency from seconds to tens of milliseconds on bloated edge links with no per-link tuning [7].
- **RTT-fairness studies** (FaiRTT vs BBRv2, arXiv 2024): average throughput ratio between competing elephant/mice flows 1.44 for BBRv2 vs 1.04 for the delay-corrected FaiRTT; Jain's fairness index averaging 0.95 for BBRv2, peaking at 0.99 for the corrected scheme — quantifying both the problem and the headroom [8].

### 5.2 Proof Sketches

> **Theorem (CUBIC stability):** *The cubic window function `W(t) = C(t−K)³ + W_max` is Lipschitz continuous with constant bounded by `3C(T−K)²` over any epoch of length `T`, and its growth rate is minimal at the inflection point `t = K` (i.e., at `W_max`). Hence window increments are smallest precisely where utilization is highest, suppressing the burst synchronization that convex-only increase functions induce at the saturation point.*

*Proof sketch.* Differentiating, `W'(t) = 3C(t−K)² ≥ 0` with `W'(K) = 0`. The second derivative `W''(t) = 6C(t−K)` changes sign at `K`: concave before (decrements of the growth rate as the window approaches the known-feasible `W_max`, avoiding overshoot bursts), convex after (accelerating exploration of unknown capacity). The plateau `|W(t) − W_max| < ε` occupies a constant fraction of each epoch independent of `W_max`, so the stationary distribution of the window concentrates near the efficient point — the stochastic-ordering result of Cai et al. cited in [2]. □

> **Theorem (FAST TCP equilibrium fairness):** *The FAST TCP update `w ← w + γ(α − xq)` converges to the unique equilibrium in which each flow maintains `α_s` packets in the bottleneck queue and the rate allocation maximizes `Σ_s α_s · log x_s` — weighted proportional fairness — provided feedback delay is bounded and `γ` is sufficiently small.*

*Proof sketch.* At equilibrium `xq = α`, so `x = α/q` for all flows sharing queueing delay `q`; rates are proportional to `α_s`. The update is a gradient ascent on the strictly concave dual of the network utility maximization problem; standard Lyapunov analysis with the KL-divergence Lyapunov function gives global asymptotic stability for small step size [4]. □

> **Theorem (CoDel delay bound):** *Under CoDel with target `τ` and interval `ι`, any persistent queue's minimum sojourn delay is driven below `τ` within `O(ι · √N)` of the onset of `N` consecutive drops, independent of link rate — because the control law acts on measured delay, the same `(τ, ι) = (5ms, 100ms)` parameters bound standing-queue delay on links from kilobits to gigabits per second.*

*Proof sketch.* The drop schedule `t_{n+1} = t_n + ι/√n` produces a drop rate `λ ∝ √n/ι`; by the Mathis relation the bottlenecked flow's throughput falls as `1/√λ`, i.e., linearly in the drop count, guaranteeing the queue drains regardless of `C` [6]. □

## 6 Limitations

No congestion control algorithm escapes fundamental trade-offs; each generation's strengths are purchased with specific weaknesses.

1. **BBRv1's fairness deficits.** RTT unfairness (queue proportional to `min_rtt`), poor coexistence with loss-based flows on FIFO queues (BBRv1's probes can starve CUBIC, or be starved by it, depending on buffer depth), and heavy retransmissions on shallow buffers — the very regime BBR was meant to serve. BBRv2/v3 mitigate these by reintroducing loss/ECN signals, at the cost of some of v1's elegant simplicity [8].
2. **BBR's measurement fragility.** `RTprop` as a 10-second minimum is corrupted by route changes, cellular handoffs, and Wi-Fi aggregation delays; `BtlBw` as a 10-RTT maximum is inflated by ACK compression and stretched by application-limited periods. BBRv3's refined filters acknowledge that *model-based* does not mean *measurement-robust*.
3. **Pacing dependence.** BBR requires packet pacing (Linux `fq` qdisc); without it, bursts recreate the queueing the model assumes away, and performance degrades sharply [5]. This is a deployment tax CUBIC does not pay.
4. **CUBIC's structural delay-indifference.** CUBIC will fill any buffer it is given. On bloated edge links it is not merely suboptimal but actively harmful to interactive traffic sharing the queue — the central finding of the bufferbloat work [6].
5. **Delay-based signals remain fragile.** Vegas and FAST flows are starved by loss-based competitors on shared FIFO queues; no endpoint algorithm can fix an adversarial queue, which is why FQ-CoDel's flow isolation [7] is arguably as important as any endpoint innovation of the last two decades.
6. **The fairness pluralism problem.** Max-min fairness, proportional fairness, and RTT-fairness are mutually incompatible in general topologies; every algorithm implicitly chooses, and the Internet runs all of them simultaneously. Convergence proofs assume homogeneous populations that do not exist.

## 7 Conclusion

Four decades of congestion control trace a clear arc: from *reacting to loss* (Jacobson, Reno, CUBIC) to *anticipating delay* (Vegas, FAST TCP) to *modeling the path* (BBR) — with the queue itself, through CoDel and FQ-CoDel, finally enlisted as an active participant rather than a passive victim. Each step replaced a coarser signal with a finer model of the same underlying reality: Kleinrock's operating point, one BDP in flight and an empty queue, toward which every scheme is, knowingly or not, steering.

The deepest lesson is architectural, not algorithmic. The bufferbloat episode proved that endpoint intelligence cannot compensate for pathological queues; the BBRv2/v3 evolution proved that model-based elegance cannot ignore the signaling ecosystem it deploys into. The durable advances — CUBIC's time-based growth, BBR's delivery-rate estimation, CoDel's sojourn-time control law, FQ-CoDel's flow isolation — all share one property: they measure the quantity they actually care about (time, rate, delay, per-flow state) rather than a proxy for it. Future progress will likely come less from new window-update rules than from richer signals: ECN with accurate marking, per-flow queueing as the default, and models that treat the path as the dynamic, multi-tenant system it is. The congestion control problem is not solved; it has merely become honest about what it is: distributed optimization under radical uncertainty, where the only stable strategy is to keep measuring.
