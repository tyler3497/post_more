# Succinct Zero-Knowledge Arguments: PLONK Arithmetization, KZG Commitments, and FRI-Based STARKs

## Abstract

Succinct zero-knowledge arguments compress claims about vast computations into proofs that verify in milliseconds, underwriting privacy-preserving rollups, shielded transactions, and verifiable machine learning. This thesis unifies the three pillars of the modern succinct-proof landscape: PLONK arithmetization, which compiles arbitrary circuits into a universal constraint system over Lagrange bases with permutation arguments; Kate-Zaverucha-Goldberg (KZG) polynomial commitments, which bind evaluations under the powers-of-tau trusted setup and collapse openings to a single pairing check; and the FRI interactive oracle proof of proximity, which powers transparent STARKs through Reed-Solomon folding under hash-only assumptions. We derive PLONK's selector-and-permutation constraint algebra and its five-round protocol, prove KZG's evaluation binding under the t-strong Diffie-Hellman assumption, and analyze FRI's linear-time prover and logarithmic verifier. A quantitative taxonomy contrasts SNARKs and STARKs across setup trust, proof size, prover cost, and post-quantum security, closing with production measurements from zkEVMs and the trade-offs governing protocol selection.

## 1. Introduction

*Succinctness* is the defining promise of modern zero-knowledge cryptography: a prover convinces a verifier of a claim about an arbitrarily large computation while the proof remains small and the verifier's work grows *sublinearly* — ideally polylogarithmically — in the size of the computation [5]. What began with interactive proofs in the 1980s, and passed through the PCP theorem, Kilian's succinct arguments, and the first practical pairing-based SNARKs, has bifurcated in the last decade into two flourishing families.

The first family is **preprocessing SNARKs with universal setups**, epitomized by PLONK [1]. Where Groth16 required a fresh, circuit-specific trusted ceremony for every program, PLONK introduced a *universal and updatable* structured reference string (SRS): one ceremony supports every circuit up to a bounded size, and participants can join the ceremony after the fact to refresh its security. This was the engineering breakthrough that made SNARKs deployable at industrial scale.

The second family is **transparent arguments**, epitomized by STARKs [5]. By replacing algebraic trusted setups with symmetric cryptography — collision-resistant hashing and the FRI proximity test [4] — STARKs eliminate toxic waste entirely and inherit plausible post-quantum security, at the price of larger proofs.

This thesis makes four contributions:

1. A complete derivation of **PLONK arithmetization**: selectors, wire polynomials, the permutation (grand-product) argument, and the quotient polynomial that compresses all constraints into one divisibility claim [1].
2. A self-contained treatment of **KZG polynomial commitments** — setup, commit, open, verify — with the multi-point opening extensions that shrink PLONK proofs further [2][3].
3. A rigorous account of **FRI** and its embedding in STARK arithmetization (AIR/ALI), including its linear-prover / logarithmic-verifier complexity profile [4][5].
4. A **quantitative taxonomy** of SNARKs versus STARKs grounded in published measurements, and an honest inventory of each approach's limitations.

## 2. Background

We work over a prime field \(\mathbb{F}\) and an arithmetic circuit \(C\) with \(n\) gates. The relation of interest is *circuit satisfiability*: does there exist a wire assignment (witness) consistent with every gate and the public inputs?

Two arithmetizations dominate practice. **R1CS** (rank-1 constraint systems) expresses each constraint as \((a \cdot w) \circ (b \cdot w) = (c \cdot w)\); it is the native language of Groth16 and the zkEVMs that inherit its lineage. **Plonkish arithmetization** generalizes this: each row of a constraint matrix carries one gate, wire values are committed as polynomial evaluations over a multiplicative subgroup \(H = \{1, \omega, \dots, \omega^{n-1}\}\), and equality of wires across rows is enforced by a *permutation argument* rather than by wiring matrices [1]. Halo2's "plonkish" framework extends this with custom gates, rotations, and lookup tables, and is the proving system behind Zcash's Orchard shielded pool [9].

A **polynomial interactive oracle proof (PIOP)** cleanly separates arithmetization from cryptography: the prover sends *oracles* (black-box polynomials), the verifier queries them at random points, and a polynomial commitment scheme then compiles the PIOP into a non-interactive argument via Fiat–Shamir [1]. PLONK is a PIOP compiled with KZG; STARKs are IOPs compiled with FRI plus Merkle-tree commitments.

Three mathematical tools recur throughout:

- **Pairings** \(e: \mathbb{G}_1 \times \mathbb{G}_2 \to \mathbb{G}_T\), bilinear maps enabling one equation to check polynomial identities "in the exponent."
- The **Schwartz–Zippel lemma**: a nonzero degree-\(d\) polynomial over \(\mathbb{F}\) vanishes at a random point with probability at most \(d/|\mathbb{F}|\), which is why checking an identity at one random point suffices.
- **Trusted-setup ceremonies**: multi-party computations generating powers \([s^i]\) of a secret \(s\) (the "toxic waste") that must be destroyed; *updatable* setups let late joiners refresh security without restarting [1].

| System | Arithmetization | Setup | Commitment | Proof size | Verifier |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Groth16 | R1CS | Per-circuit trusted | KZG | ~200 B | 3 pairings |
| Sonic | R1CS-like | Universal, updatable | KZG variant | ~1 KB | ~pairings |
| PLONK | Plonkish | Universal, updatable | KZG | ~400 B | ~2 pairings + O(1) [7] |
| Halo2 | Plonkish + lookups | Transparent | IPA | ~1–2 KB | O(√n) group ops [9] |
| STARK | AIR | Transparent | FRI + Merkle | 50–200 KB | polylog hashes [5][8] |
| Bulletproofs | R1CS | Transparent | IPA | O(log n) | O(n) |

## 3. Methodology

Our method is *comparative analytic cryptography*: we reconstruct each construction from primary sources [1][2][3][4][5], verify every complexity and security claim against the published theorems, and cross-check engineering numbers against independent implementations and production reports [7][8][9][10]. We adopt the following conventions:

- \(\mathbb{F}\) is a prime field with a large smooth multiplicative subgroup \(H\), \(|H| = n\), generator \(\omega\).
- \([x]_1, [x]_2\) denote scalar multiples in \(\mathbb{G}_1, \mathbb{G}_2\).
- \(Z_H(X) = X^n - 1\) is the vanishing polynomial of \(H\); \(L_1\) is the first Lagrange basis polynomial.
- Security is analyzed in the **algebraic group model** for KZG-based arguments (binding reduces to \(t\)-SDH) and in the **random-oracle model** with collision-resistant hashing for FRI-based arguments [2][4].
- Benchmark numbers are reported only where a citable source states them; qualitative claims ("minutes-scale proving") are marked as such.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 PLONK Arithmetization: Selectors, Permutations, and the Quotient Polynomial

PLONK arithmetizes a circuit as follows [1]. Each gate \(i \in H\) consumes left, right, and output wire values, interpolated into polynomials \(a(X), b(X), c(X)\). Five *selector* polynomials — precomputed per circuit and committed in preprocessing — choose the gate's behavior:

$$q_L(X)\,a(X) + q_R(X)\,b(X) + q_M(X)\,a(X)b(X) + q_O(X)\,c(X) + q_C(X) = 0 \quad \forall X \in H$$

An addition gate sets \(q_L = q_R = 1\), \(q_O = -1\); a multiplication gate sets \(q_M = 1\), \(q_O = -1\); constants and custom gates are linear combinations of the same selectors. UltraPlonk extends the selector set and adds lookup tables, but the core identity is unchanged [6].

Wire *consistency* — the claim that two wire positions carry the same value — is enforced by a permutation \(\sigma\) over the \(3n\) wire positions and a grand-product accumulator \(z(X)\). With \(\sigma_1, \sigma_2, \sigma_3\) the permutation polynomials on each wire domain:

> **Theorem (PLONK permutation identity).** The wires are \(\sigma\)-consistent iff there exists \(z\) with \(z(1) = 1\) satisfying
> $$(a(X)+\beta X+\gamma)(b(X)+\beta k_1 X+\gamma)(c(X)+\beta k_2 X+\gamma)\,z(X)$$
> $$= (a(X)+\beta\sigma_1(X)+\gamma)(b(X)+\beta\sigma_2(X)+\gamma)(c(X)+\beta\sigma_3(X)+\gamma)\,z(\omega X)$$
> for random challenges \(\beta, \gamma\), where \(k_1, k_2\) are coset separators [1].

The prover then forms a single **quotient polynomial**

$$t(X) = \frac{\text{gate}(X) + \alpha\,\text{perm}(X) + \alpha^2 L_1(X)(z(X)-1)}{Z_H(X)},$$

whose existence (i.e., the vanishing of the numerator on \(H\)) is equivalent to *all* constraints holding. Because \(t\) has degree \(< 2n+5\), the prover splits it into three chunks \(t_{lo}, t_{mid}, t_{hi}\) and commits to each. The protocol runs in **five rounds**:

1. Commit to wire polynomials \([a]_1, [b]_1, [c]_1\).
2. Receive \(\beta, \gamma\); commit to permutation accumulator \([z]_1\).
3. Receive \(\alpha\); compute and commit to \([t_{lo}]_1, [t_{mid}]_1, [t_{hi}]_1\).
4. Receive evaluation point \(\zeta\); reveal wire, permutation, and quotient evaluations.
5. Batch all opening claims into one KZG multi-opening proof \([W_\zeta]_1, [W_{\zeta\omega}]_1\) [3].

The verifier recomputes a constant number of pairings on the batched opening. Concretely, a PLONK proof is on the order of **400 bytes** and verifies in roughly **6 milliseconds** on commodity hardware, with prover work dominated by \(O(n \log n)\) FFTs and multi-scalar multiplications [7].

```python
# Illustrative: PLONK quotient polynomial over a subgroup H (educational pseudocode)
def plonk_quotient(a, b, c, qL, qR, qM, qO, qC, z, beta, gamma, alpha, sigma_polys, H):
    n = len(H)
    ZH = lambda x: x**n - 1
    gate = [qL[i]*a[i] + qR[i]*b[i] + qM[i]*a[i]*b[i] + qO[i]*c[i] + qC[i]
            for i in range(n)]
    k1, k2 = 7, 13  # coset separators, distinct from H
    perm = []
    for i in range(n):
        x = H[i]
        lhs = (a[i]+beta*x+gamma)*(b[i]+beta*k1*x+gamma)*(c[i]+beta*k2*x+gamma)*z[i]
        rhs = (a[i]+beta*sigma_polys[0][i]+gamma)*(b[i]+beta*sigma_polys[1][i]+gamma) \
              *(c[i]+beta*sigma_polys[2][i]+gamma)*z[(i+1) % n]
        perm.append(lhs - rhs)
    copy_ok = [(z[(i+1) % n] if i == 0 else z[i]) for i in range(n)]
    # t(X) = (gate + alpha*perm + alpha^2 * L1*(z-1)) / Z_H, interpolated
    return [ (gate[i] + alpha*perm[i] + alpha**2 * (1 if i == 0 else 0) * (z[i]-1)) / ZH(H[i])
             for i in range(n) ]
```

### 4.2 KZG Commitments and the Powers-of-Tau Trusted Setup

Every pairing-based SNARK above rests on the Kate–Zaverucha–Goldberg polynomial commitment scheme [2]. **Setup** samples secret \(s \in \mathbb{F}\) and publishes the SRS \(\{[s^i]_1\}_{i=0}^{d}, \{[s^i]_2\}_{i=0}^{d}\); \(s\) is the toxic waste. **Commit** to \(p(X) = \sum p_i X^i\) as \(C = [p(s)]_1 = \sum p_i\,[s^i]_1\). **Open** an evaluation \(p(z) = y\) via the quotient \(q(X) = (p(X)-y)/(X-z)\), sending \(\pi = [q(s)]_1\). **Verify** checks

> **Theorem (KZG verification).** \(e(C - [y]_1,\, [1]_2) = e(\pi,\, [s]_2 - [z]_2)\) holds iff \(p(z) = y\), under the \(t\)-strong Diffie–Hellman assumption [2].

*Proof sketch.* Bilinearity gives \(e([p(s)]_1-[y]_1,[1]_2) = e(g_1,g_2)^{p(s)-y}\) and \(e([q(s)]_1,[s]_2-[z]_2) = e(g_1,g_2)^{q(s)(s-z)}\). Equality of the exponents is exactly \(p(s)-y = q(s)(s-z)\), the defining relation of \(q\). Binding follows because forging an opening for \(y' \neq y\) yields a \(t\)-SDH solution [2]. ∎

Three properties make KZG dominant in practice: commitments are **constant-size** (one group element, ~48 bytes on BLS12-381 [8]); openings are constant-size with constant-time verification; and the scheme supports **batching** — Boneh, Drake, Fisch, and Gabizon showed a single group element can open multiple polynomials at multiple points, which PLONK uses to collapse its entire round-5 opening phase into two group elements at the cost of extra \(\mathbb{G}_2\) operations [3]. The trusted setup is KZG's Achilles' heel, mitigated in deployment by large multi-party ceremonies (Ethereum's KZG ceremony for EIP-4844 blobs drew six-figure participation) and by updatable SRS designs in the Sonic/PLONK lineage [1][8].

```rust
// Illustrative KZG opening check (educational pseudocode; curve ops abstracted)
fn kzg_verify(commitment: G1, z: Fr, y: Fr, proof: G1, srs_g2: &[G2]) -> bool {
    // e(C - [y]_1, [1]_2) == e(pi, [s]_2 - [z]_2)
    let lhs = pairing(commitment - G1::generator() * y, srs_g2[0]);
    let rhs = pairing(proof, srs_g2[1] - G2::generator() * z);
    lhs == rhs
}
```

### 4.3 FRI: Folding Reed–Solomon Proximity into STARKs

FRI (Ben-Sasson, Bentov, Horesh, Riabzev [4]) answers a different question: given oracle access to a function \(f_0: L_0 \to \mathbb{F}\) on a domain of size \(N\), is \(f_0\) *close* to a Reed–Solomon codeword — i.e., the evaluation table of a polynomial of degree \(< K\)? This is an *interactive oracle proof of proximity* (IOPP), and it is the commitment layer of every STARK [5].

The core is the **fold operator**. Writing \(f(x) = f_{even}(x^2) + x\,f_{odd}(x^2)\),

$$\mathrm{Fold}\_\alpha(f)(x) = f_{even}(x) + \alpha\,f_{odd}(x) = \frac{f(x)+f(-x)}{2} + \alpha\,\frac{f(x)-f(-x)}{2x}.$$

If \(f\) has degree \(< K\), then \(\mathrm{Fold}\_\alpha(f)\) has degree \(< K/2\) on the halved domain \(L_1 = \{x^2 : x \in L_0\}\) — with overwhelming probability over random \(\alpha\), *only if* \(f\) was already low-degree. This gives the **commit phase**: the prover Merkle-commits to \(f_0\), receives \(\alpha_0\), commits to \(f_1 = \mathrm{Fold}\_{\alpha_0}(f_0)\), and iterates \(\log K\) times down to a constant. In the **query phase**, the verifier samples random \(x_0 \in L_0\), opens the two preimages at each layer, and checks the fold relation locally along the whole chain [4].

> **Theorem (FRI complexity [4]).** For blocklength \(N\): the interactive prover uses fewer than \(6N\) arithmetic operations; the verifier uses at most \(21\log N\) operations, \(2\log N\) queries, and \(\log N\) rounds; words \(\delta\)-far from the code are rejected with probability at least \(\min\{\delta(1-o(1)), \delta_0\}\) for a constant \(\delta_0\).

STARKs wrap FRI in two arithmetization layers [5]: **AIR** (Algebraic Intermediate Representation) encodes a computation as a *trace* — a table whose rows are consecutive machine states — plus polynomial transition constraints; **ALI** (Algebraic Linking IOP) composes the trace, constraint, and composition polynomials into one object whose low-degree-ness FRI attests. This trace-based view is why STARKs excel at uniform, repeated computation (VM execution, hashing) and why they demand fields with large power-of-two subgroups — hence STARK-friendly fields like Goldilocks, \(p = 2^{64} - 2^{32} + 1\). Proofs are *polylogarithmic*, \(O(\log^2 n)\), concretely **50–200 KB** [8]: orders of magnitude larger than KZG proofs, but with *no trusted setup* and *no algebraic assumptions* for a quantum adversary to break.

### 4.4 SNARK versus STARK: A Quantitative Taxonomy

| Dimension | KZG-based SNARK (PLONK) | FRI-based STARK |
|---|---|---|
| Setup | Universal SRS; trusted, updatable [1] | Fully transparent; public randomness [5] |
| Assumption | \(t\)-SDH / pairing-friendly curves | Collision-resistant hashing |
| Proof size | O(1): ~400 B [7] | O(log² n): 50–200 KB [8] |
| Verifier | ~2 pairings, ~6 ms [7] | Polylog hashes, ~10s of ms |
| Prover | O(n log n), MSM-dominated | O(n log n), hash-dominated |
| Post-quantum | No (Shor breaks pairings) | Plausibly yes [5] |
| Recursion | Cheap: constant-size proofs compose | Costly: large proofs recurse |
| Arithmetization | Plonkish circuits, custom gates, lookups [6][9] | AIR traces, uniform computation |

The table explains the industry's split: *verification-cost-sensitive* settings (on-chain verifiers, light clients) favor SNARKs; *trust-minimized, quantum-cautious* settings favor STARKs. Note the asymmetry in recursion — folding-friendly algebraic commitments compose gracefully, while FRI's lack of homomorphism forces STARK systems to recurse by proving "this proof verifies," which is why production STARK stacks (StarkWare's Stwo, Polygon's Plonky3) invest heavily in recursion-optimized circuits [8].

## 5. Empirical Results / Proofs

**PLONK in practice.** Independent implementations confirm the theoretical profile: proofs of roughly 400 bytes (≈ 9 \(\mathbb{G}_1\) elements plus field elements) verifying in about 6 ms, with prover time scaling quasilinearly and dominated by multi-scalar multiplications — the target of ongoing GPU-acceleration work [7]. The plookup/UltraPlonk extension, reconciled with PLONK in PlonKup [6], replaces expensive bit-decomposition subcircuits with table lookups, cutting constraint counts for range checks and elliptic-curve operations that dominate zkEVM circuits.

**STARK in practice.** The original STARK paper reported the first *implemented* transparent system whose verification scales exponentially faster than the data it attests to, demonstrated on a DNA profile-matching workload with minutes-scale proving and millisecond-scale verification [5]. Modern realizations (Stwo, Plonky3, R0VM) push proving into seconds for millions of cycles, though proof sizes remain in the tens-to-hundreds of kilobytes [8].

**zkEVM deployments.** Production rollups quantify the SNARK advantage at the verifier. zkSync Era's protocol-level polynomial aggregation saves roughly **700,000 gas per batch**; Polygon zkEVM's off-chain Montgomery batch inverses save roughly **200,000 gas** by avoiding on-chain IFFT work [10].

**Halo2 and transparent recursion.** Zcash's Halo2 demonstrates the middle path: Plonkish arithmetization with the *transparent* inner-product argument (IPA) instead of KZG, deployed in the Orchard shielded pool since 2022, with the Pallas/Vesta curve cycle enabling efficient recursive composition without any ceremony [9].

**Security reductions.** PLONK's knowledge soundness reduces to the evaluation binding of KZG (hence \(t\)-SDH) in the algebraic group model, plus Fiat–Shamir soundness in the random-oracle model [1][2]. FRI's soundness is information-theoretic given the hash: the proximity-gap analysis shows each folding round preserves distance from the code with only constant loss, so \(\log K\) rounds amplify a constant per-round rejection probability into a negligible cheating probability [4]. The two reductions compose cleanly because FRI makes no algebraic assumptions that a quantum adversary could exploit — the precise content of STARK's post-quantum claim [5].

## 6. Limitations

No construction escapes trade-offs; the honest ledger is as follows.

1. **Trusted setup.** PLONK's universality softens but does not remove the ceremony: at least one participant must honestly destroy their contribution, and the SRS must be regenerated (or extended) as circuit sizes grow [1][8].
2. **Quantum vulnerability.** KZG, Groth16, and PLONK all die under Shor's algorithm. Hybrid "best of both" designs remain an open research problem [8].
3. **FRI proof size.** Polylogarithmic is not constant: 50–200 KB proofs are prohibitive for calldata-constrained L1 verification, which is why STARK rollups typically wrap their proofs in a final SNARK [8].
4. **Prover economics.** Both families are \(O(n \log n)\); the constants differ (MSMs vs. hashing), and memory for FFTs/Merkle trees, not just time, gates deployment on consumer hardware [7].
5. **Arithmetization gap.** AIR demands uniform traces; Plonkish demands circuit thinking. General-purpose programs still pay a 10–100× overhead versus native execution, and the gap is a compiler problem as much as a cryptography problem [9][10].
6. **Recursion asymmetry.** FRI commitments lack the algebraic homomorphism that makes KZG aggregation cheap, so STARK recursion is intrinsically heavier — an active frontier [8].

## 7. Conclusion

PLONK and STARKs are best understood not as rivals but as complementary answers to one question: *what are you willing to trust?* Trust a one-time ceremony and the hardness of discrete logarithms, and PLONK with KZG delivers constant-size proofs, millisecond verification, and cheap recursion — the workhorse of today's rollups [1][2][10]. Trust only hash functions, and FRI-based STARKs deliver transparency and plausible post-quantum security at the cost of larger proofs [4][5]. The field's trajectory — lookup arguments [6], multi-point batching [3], transparent Plonkish systems [9], and STARK-to-SNARK wrapping — points toward hybrid architectures that spend each assumption exactly where it buys the most. The succinct-argument revolution is no longer about whether verification can be sublinear; it is about *which* sublinearity, at *what* price, under *whose* trust.
