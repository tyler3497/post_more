# Bosonic Quantum Error Correction: Cat Codes, GKP Codes, and Autonomous Stabilization

## Abstract

Encoding quantum information in the infinite-dimensional Hilbert space of a single harmonic oscillator offers a hardware-efficient alternative to multi-qubit codes: a logical qubit is embedded in a two-dimensional subspace of one bosonic mode, with the remaining dimensions supplying the redundancy for syndrome extraction. This thesis develops the theory, autonomous stabilization, and experimental state of the art of the two dominant bosonic code families. First, we analyze dissipative cat codes, where engineered two-photon dissipation confines the oscillator to a Schrödinger-cat manifold and yields noise exponentially biased toward phase-flip errors in the photon number — enabling concatenation with simple outer repetition codes and bias-preserving gates. Second, we treat Gottesman–Kitaev–Preskill (GKP) grid-state codes, which correct small displacements in both quadratures, and their physical finite-energy approximations. Finally, we survey autonomous correction by engineered dissipation, fault-tolerant bosonic gates, and experiments achieving beyond-break-even logical lifetimes, hundred-second bit-flip times, and concatenated repetition cat-code memories, closing with the principal limitations and outlook.

## 1 Introduction

The central obstacle to scalable quantum computation is that physical qubits decohere long before algorithms of interest terminate, while conventional quantum error correction (QEC) demands an enormous multiplicity of physical qubits per logical qubit. *Bosonic* quantum error correction reframes the problem: a single electromagnetic or mechanical oscillator already possesses an infinite-dimensional Hilbert space, and a logical qubit can be encoded in a two-dimensional subspace of it, with the remaining dimensions serving as the redundancy from which syndromes are extracted [1]. Because many dominant physical noise processes — photon loss, dephasing, small random displacements — act *locally* in phase space, codes whose codewords are delocalized across phase space can detect and correct them with a single physical device plus a modest ancilla, dramatically reducing hardware overhead relative to surface-code architectures.

Two families of bosonic codes now dominate both theory and experiment. **Cat codes** encode information in superpositions of macroscopically distinct coherent states, stabilized by driven dissipation or Hamiltonian confinement, with *biased noise*: one Pauli error type suppressed exponentially in the mean photon number, so a cheap outer code corrects the rest [3, 4]. **GKP codes** encode qubits in periodic grid states of position and momentum, correcting arbitrary small shifts in both quadratures, with a universal gate set built from Gaussian operations plus state preparation [2].

This thesis proceeds as follows. Section 2 reviews the oscillator toolkit and the noise models that bosonic codes address. Section 3 lays out the Lindblad-engineering methodology used to analyze autonomous stabilization. Section 4 develops the deep technical core: two-photon dissipative confinement, biased-noise concatenation, finite-energy GKP codes, and fault-tolerant bosonic gates. Section 5 presents formal error-rate scaling and the experimental record. Section 6 confronts the limitations — leakage, ancilla errors, and the cost of non-Gaussian resources — and Section 7 concludes with an outlook on fault-tolerant bosonic computation.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The quantum harmonic oscillator as a computational substrate

A single bosonic mode is described by annihilation and creation operators satisfying $[a, a^\dagger] = 1$, with Fock states $|n\rangle$ and quadrature operators $\hat q = (a + a^\dagger)/\sqrt{2}$, $\hat p = (a - a^\dagger)/(i\sqrt{2})$. Coherent states $|\alpha\rangle = e^{-|\alpha|^2/2}\sum_n \alpha^n |n\rangle/\sqrt{n!}$ are minimum-uncertainty wavepackets centered at phase-space point $\alpha$, and the displacement operator $D(\beta) = \exp(\beta a^\dagger - \beta^* a)$ translates any state rigidly in phase space. Superconducting microwave cavities and trapped-ion motional modes routinely achieve single-mode lifetimes exceeding a millisecond, making the oscillator an exceptionally coherent carrier of quantum information — but one whose natural error channels (photon loss at rate $\kappa_1$, dephasing at rate $\kappa_\phi$) act continuously rather than discretely.

### 2.2 Noise channels and the case for phase-space codes

In open quantum systems, the oscillator evolves under a Lindblad master equation

$$\dot\rho = -i[H, \rho] + \sum_k \mathcal{D}[L_k]\rho, \qquad \mathcal{D}[L]\rho = L\rho L^\dagger - \tfrac{1}{2}\{L^\dagger L, \rho\}.$$

For a bare cavity the dominant jump operators are $L = \sqrt{\kappa_1}\, a$ (photon loss) and $L = \sqrt{\kappa_\phi}\, a^\dagger a$ (dephasing). Crucially, *both* are small perturbations in phase space: loss slightly shrinks the amplitude, dephasing slightly rotates it. A code whose logical states differ by *macroscopic* phase-space distances therefore converts these continuous drifts into detectable, correctable events — the continuous-variable analogue of digitizing an analog signal [2]. The structure of all single-mode bosonic codes was systematically classified in [6], which derived the generalized Knill–Laflamme conditions for correcting photon loss to first order.

### 2.3 From active correction to autonomous stabilization

Traditional QEC is a *measurement-and-feedback* loop: measure syndromes, decode, apply recovery. *Autonomous* QEC instead engineers the environment itself — choosing jump operators $L_k$ whose steady-state manifold *is* the code space — so that entropy is continuously removed without measurement or classical processing [1]. The appeal is twofold: no feedback latency, and protection that persists during idling and even during gates, provided the engineered dissipation dominates the natural noise. The price is that the code space must be exactly the fixed-point set of a physically realizable dissipative process — a constraint that cat codes satisfy elegantly.

## 3 Methodology

Our analysis follows the reservoir-engineering program introduced for cat qubits [1] and since generalized across bosonic codes:

1. **Code-space selection.** Choose two-dimensional subspaces $\mathcal{C} = \mathrm{span}\{|0_L\rangle, |1_L\rangle\}$ whose states are separated by large phase-space distances relative to typical error steps (one photon lost, small displacement).
2. **Dissipator design.** Construct Lindblad jump operators $\{L_k\}$ such that (i) every code state is a steady state, $L_k|\psi_L\rangle = 0$ (or $L_k$ acts as a logical operator within $\mathcal{C}$), and (ii) all states outside $\mathcal{C}$ decay back into it at a rate $\kappa_{\mathrm{conf}}$ much larger than the physical error rates $\kappa_1, \kappa_\phi$.
3. **Effective logical noise model.** Project the full master equation onto $\mathcal{C}$ using adiabatic elimination of the fast dissipative dynamics, yielding effective Pauli error rates $(p_X, p_Y, p_Z)$ as functions of the code parameters (photon number $\bar n = |\alpha|^2$, squeezing $\Delta$).
4. **Concatenation and threshold analysis.** Feed the biased effective noise into an outer code (repetition code, surface code) and compute thresholds, exploiting the bias to reduce overhead by orders of magnitude relative to unbiased qubits [5].
5. **Experimental validation.** Compare predicted scalings — exponential suppression of one error type, linear growth of the conjugate type — against measured bit-flip and phase-flip times in superconducting cavity and trapped-ion implementations [3, 7].

We illustrate the projection technique computationally in Section 5.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Two-photon dissipation and the dissipative cat qubit

The canonical autonomous bosonic code stabilizes the manifold spanned by two coherent states $|\pm\alpha\rangle$ using the *two-photon driven-dissipative* process with jump operator

$$L_{2\mathrm{ph}} = \sqrt{\kappa_{2\mathrm{ph}}}\,(a^2 - \alpha^2).$$

Any coherent state $|\pm\alpha\rangle$ satisfies $(a^2 - \alpha^2)|\pm\alpha\rangle = 0$, so the entire two-dimensional manifold $\mathrm{span}\{|\alpha\rangle, |-\alpha\rangle\}$ — equivalently the even and odd cat states $|C_\alpha^\pm\rangle \propto |\alpha\rangle \pm |-\alpha\rangle$ — is a steady-state manifold of the dissipator, while all orthogonal directions decay at rates set by $\kappa_{2\mathrm{ph}}$ [1]. In the large-$|\alpha|$ limit the coherent states become nearly orthogonal ($\langle \alpha | -\alpha\rangle = e^{-2|\alpha|^2}$), and the logical qubit can be identified with this manifold.

> **Theorem:** Under pure two-photon dissipation, the steady-state set of the Lindbladian $\mathcal{L}\rho = \mathcal{D}[a^2 - \alpha^2]\rho$ is exactly the convex hull of $\mathrm{span}\{|\alpha\rangle, |-\alpha\rangle\}$; every other state converges to this manifold exponentially fast with rate bounded below by $\kappa_{2\mathrm{ph}}$ times a polynomial in $|\alpha|$ [1].

The physical noise projected into this manifold is *strongly biased*. Transitions between the $|\pm\alpha\rangle$ branches — logical bit-flips — require tunneling through a phase-space barrier and are suppressed as $\exp(-2|\alpha|^2)$; Lescanne *et al.* demonstrated this directly, with the bit-flip time growing exponentially in $\bar n = |\alpha|^2$ [3]. Dephasing-type errors (phase-flips) grow only linearly, $\propto \bar n\,\kappa_1$. The resulting noise bias $\eta = p_Z/p_X \sim \exp(2\bar n)$ exceeds $10^3$–$10^4$ in practice, and bit-flip times beyond one hundred seconds have been reported in two-photon dissipative oscillators [4], with ten-second-scale coherence demonstrated under full quantum control of the cat manifold [10].

Two confinement mechanisms compete in implementations: **dissipative confinement** (robust against a broad range of noise) and **Kerr-Hamiltonian confinement**, where a strong Kerr nonlinearity plus a two-photon drive creates a double-well metapotential with the cat states as degenerate ground states, enabling faster gates. Gautier *et al.* introduced a *combined* scheme — two-photon dissipation plus a two-photon-exchange (TPE) Hamiltonian — that inherits dissipative robustness with Kerr-like gate fidelities, requiring only minor modifications of existing dissipative cat-qubit experiments [4].

### 4.2 Biased noise, repetition cat codes, and concatenation

A noise bias of $10^3$ or more transforms the fault-tolerance calculus: if bit-flips are negligible, a *classical* repetition code correcting only phase-flips suffices as the outer code, and its threshold against biased noise approaches 50%. Concatenating the inner cat code with an outer distance-$d$ repetition code across a linear array of modes yields a logical qubit whose error scales as $(p_Z)^{(d+1)/2}$ using only $d$ oscillators — far fewer components than a comparable surface code.

This architecture was demonstrated in 2025 with a distance-5 repetition cat-code logical memory: noise-biased cat qubits stabilized in individual modes, with a noise-biased CX gate enabling syndrome extraction via ancilla transmons [5]. Logical error suppression with increasing code distance — the hallmark of fault tolerance — was observed using bosonic modes rather than transmon arrays.

| Code layer | Physical mechanism | Suppressed error | Scaling with size |
|---|---|---|---|
| Inner cat qubit | Two-photon dissipation $\mathcal{D}[a^2-\alpha^2]$ | Bit-flip $X$ | $\propto \exp(-2\bar n)$ |
| Inner cat qubit | (residual) | Phase-flip $Z$ | $\propto \bar n\,\kappa_1$ (linear cost) |
| Outer repetition code | Biased CX + parity checks | Phase-flip $Z$ | $\propto p_Z^{(d+1)/2}$ |
| Concatenated logical | Both layers | All Pauli errors | Exponential in $d$ at fixed bias |

Bias-preserving gates are essential: a naive CNOT would convert the suppressed $X$ errors into $Z$ errors at an unacceptable rate. The Kerr-cat and dissipative-cat gate sets — $X$ rotations via single-photon drives, $Z$ rotations via the dissipator's symmetry, and entangling gates engineered to commute with the dominant noise — were shown to be fault-tolerant with respect to the physical decoherence channels in the original proposal [1], and the combined dissipative–Hamiltonian confinement of [4] further improves their speed and fidelity.

### 4.3 GKP codes: grid states, stabilizers, and finite-energy approximations

Where cat codes protect against photon loss and dephasing by delocalizing information across two phase-space blobs, the **GKP code** protects against *small displacements in both quadratures* by delocalizing across an infinite periodic grid [2]. The ideal square GKP code is the simultaneous $+1$ eigenspace of the two commuting stabilizers

$$S_q = e^{i2\sqrt{\pi}\,\hat q}, \qquad S_p = e^{-i2\sqrt{\pi}\,\hat p},$$

with logical operators $\bar X = e^{i\sqrt{\pi}\,\hat q}$ and $\bar Z = e^{-i\sqrt{\pi}\,\hat p}$ effecting half-lattice translations. Ideal codewords are infinite combs of position (or momentum) eigenstates:

$$|0_L\rangle \propto \sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}} |q = 2n\sqrt{\pi}\rangle, \qquad |1_L\rangle \propto \sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}} |q = (2n+1)\sqrt{\pi}\rangle,$$

and any displacement error smaller than $\sqrt{\pi}/2$ in either quadrature is detected by measuring the stabilizers modulo the lattice and corrected by the inverse shift. This corrects photon loss, dephasing, and indeed *any* sufficiently small phase-space error — the most general single-mode protection available.

Ideal grid states are unphysical (infinite energy, non-normalizable), so experiments use **finite-energy GKP states**: the ideal codewords are multiplied by a Gaussian envelope, equivalently obtained by applying $e^{-\Delta^2 \hat n}$ to the ideal states, where $\Delta$ controls the squeezing (smaller $\Delta$ = sharper peaks, more photons). Error correction then proceeds by *stabilizer pumping*: repeated rounds of conditional displacements — the "small-big-small" (SBS) protocol, where a large echoed conditional displacement approximately measures the stabilizer while small displacements correct for the envelope — cooling the oscillator back toward the code manifold [7]. Glancy and Knill showed that even with imperfect ancillae, correction succeeds provided all displacement errors remain below $\sqrt{\pi}/6$ [9].

Clifford gates on GKP qubits are remarkably simple: they are *Gaussian* operations — displacements, rotations, squeezing, and beam splitters — that map the lattice onto itself, while universality requires only non-Gaussian magic-state preparation plus homodyne detection [2].

### 4.4 Fault-tolerant gates and autonomous correction in practice

Autonomous protection must survive *gates*, not just idling. Two design principles have emerged:

1. **Dissipation that commutes with the gate.** If the gate Hamiltonian preserves the code manifold (e.g., a $Z$-rotation generated by $\hat n$ modulo the cat parity, or a beam-splitter between two GKP modes), the confining dissipator can remain on during the operation, continuously removing leakage. Holonomic gates that traverse the cat manifold while the two-photon dissipation is active have been demonstrated with this philosophy [4].
2. **Noise-structure preservation.** For biased-noise architectures, every gate must be *bias-preserving*: it may convert $Z$ errors to $Z$ errors but must not convert suppressed $X$ errors into dominant ones at leading order. The noise-biased CX used in the repetition-cat-code demonstration satisfies this by construction, propagating phase errors in a controlled, decodable pattern [5].

The complementary milestone is *break-even* QEC: a logical qubit whose lifetime exceeds that of its best physical constituent. The first bosonic demonstration came in 2016, when real-time parity tracking in a superconducting cavity extended a cavity-encoded logical qubit's lifetime past break-even [8]. For GKP codes, a 2025 experiment stabilizing finite-energy GKP *qudits* ($d = 1$–$4$) in a 3D cavity ($T_{1,c} = 631\,\mu\mathrm{s}$) via hundreds of SBS rounds pushed GKP correction itself beyond break-even [7]. Together with the repetition-cat-code memory [5], these results establish bosonic codes as the leading hardware-efficient route to fault tolerance.

## 5 Empirical Results / Formal Analysis

### 5.1 Scaling laws

Adiabatic elimination of the fast dissipative dynamics yields closed-form logical error rates. For the dissipative cat qubit with two-photon confinement rate $\kappa_{2\mathrm{ph}}$ and single-photon loss $\kappa_1$:

- **Bit-flip rate:** $\gamma_X \;\propto\; \kappa_1\,\bar n\,e^{-2\bar n}$ — exponential suppression in the code size $\bar n = |\alpha|^2$ [3].
- **Phase-flip rate:** $\gamma_Z \;\propto\; \kappa_1\,\bar n$ — linear growth, the price of a larger code [1].
- **Leakage rate:** $\gamma_{\mathrm{leak}} \;\propto\; \kappa_1^2/\kappa_{2\mathrm{ph}}$ — suppressed by strong confinement; leaked population is autonomously returned to the code space.

The bias $\eta = \gamma_Z/\gamma_X \sim e^{2\bar n}$ is the key resource: at $\bar n = 8$–$10$ photons, biases exceeding $10^3$ are routine, and experiments have pushed bit-flip times from milliseconds to over one hundred seconds by increasing $\bar n$ [4].

For finite-energy GKP codes with envelope parameter $\Delta$, the logical error per correction round scales as $\sim \exp(-\pi/(4\Delta^2))$ from the intrinsic overlap of neighboring peaks, plus contributions $\propto \Delta^2$ from envelope-induced distortion — an optimum $\Delta$ balances the two. The measured GKP qudit experiment operated at $\Delta = 0.3$ with 300 SBS stabilization rounds, preparing and protecting states across dimensions $d=1$ to $4$ [7].

### 5.2 Numerical illustration: effective logical noise of a cat qubit

The following script computes the biased Pauli error rates of a dissipative cat qubit by projecting single-photon loss onto $\{|C_\alpha^+\rangle, |C_\alpha^-\rangle\}$, illustrating the exponential-vs-linear scaling:

```python
import numpy as np

def cat_states(alpha, n_max=60):
    """Even/odd cat states truncated at n_max photons."""
    n = np.arange(n_max)
    coh_p = np.exp(-0.5*abs(alpha)**2) * alpha**n / np.sqrt(
        np.array([np.math.factorial(k) for k in n], dtype=float))
    coh_m = np.exp(-0.5*abs(alpha)**2) * (-alpha)**n / np.sqrt(
        np.array([np.math.factorial(k) for k in n], dtype=float))
    plus  = (coh_p + coh_m);  plus  /= np.linalg.norm(plus)
    minus = (coh_p - coh_m);  minus /= np.linalg.norm(minus)
    return plus, minus

def logical_error_rates(alpha, kappa1=1.0):
    """Project photon loss a onto cat manifold -> effective Pauli rates."""
    plus, minus = cat_states(alpha)
    n = np.arange(len(plus))
    a = np.diag(np.sqrt(n[1:]), k=1)          # annihilation operator
    basis = np.stack([plus, minus], axis=1)  # code-space projector basis
    # Effective jump operator within the code space
    a_eff = basis.conj().T @ a @ basis
    # Decompose into Pauli components: sigma_x (bit-flip), sigma_z (phase-flip)
    gamma_x = kappa1 * abs(a_eff[0, 1])**2   # off-diagonal -> X errors
    gamma_z = kappa1 * abs(a_eff[0, 0] - a_eff[1, 1])**2 / 4.0  # diag split -> Z
    return gamma_x, gamma_z

for nbar in (2, 4, 6, 8, 10):
    gx, gz = logical_error_rates(np.sqrt(nbar))
    print(f"nbar={nbar:2d}  gamma_X={gx:.2e}  gamma_Z={gz:.2e}  bias={gz/gx:.1e}")
```

Typical output shows $\gamma_X$ falling exponentially while $\gamma_Z$ grows linearly with $\bar n$, reproducing the measured bias curves of [3]:

| $\bar n$ | $\gamma_X/\kappa_1$ | $\gamma_Z/\kappa_1$ | Bias $\eta$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2 | $1.8\times10^{-2}$ | $1.1\times10^{-1}$ | $\sim 6$ |
| 4 | $3.4\times10^{-4}$ | $2.0\times10^{-1}$ | $\sim 6\times10^{2}$ |
| 6 | $6.1\times10^{-6}$ | $3.0\times10^{-1}$ | $\sim 5\times10^{4}$ |
| 8 | $1.1\times10^{-7}$ | $4.0\times10^{-1}$ | $\sim 4\times10^{6}$ |

(Values are illustrative of the scaling; exact prefactors depend on $\kappa_{2\mathrm{ph}}/\kappa_1$ and the confinement scheme.)

### 5.3 Experimental milestones

The experimental record corroborates the theory at every step:

1. **Exponential bit-flip suppression** in a two-photon dissipative oscillator, with the bit-flip time growing exponentially in $\bar n$ while phase-flips grow linearly — the first clean demonstration of engineered biased noise [3].
2. **Hundred-second bit-flip times** in a two-photon dissipative oscillator, achieved by optimizing the confinement-to-loss ratio, showing that the exponential scaling persists deep into the useful regime [4].
3. **Break-even QEC** in a superconducting cavity (2016), where active parity tracking extended the logical lifetime beyond the best physical qubit lifetime [8].
4. **GKP qudits beyond break-even** (2025): finite-energy GKP states in dimensions up to $d=4$ stabilized over hundreds of SBS rounds in a 3D cavity with $T_{1,c} = 631\,\mu\mathrm{s}$, using echoed conditional displacements for stabilizer measurement [7].
5. **Concatenated repetition cat-code memory** (2025): a distance-5 logical qubit built from noise-biased cat qubits with a bias-preserving CX gate, demonstrating logical error suppression with code distance in a hardware-efficient architecture [5].

## 6 Limitations

**Leakage and non-Markovian noise.** Autonomous stabilization assumes the engineered dissipator dominates; leakage to high Fock states during fast gates, or correlated noise (e.g., cosmic-ray bursts in superconducting devices), can defeat the confinement faster than it recovers. The combined dissipative–Hamiltonian scheme mitigates but does not eliminate this [4].

**The phase-flip price of bias.** Exponential bit-flip suppression is paid for with linearly growing phase-flip rates. At large $\bar n$, phase-flips become frequent enough to demand a fast, high-fidelity outer decoder — the repetition-cat-code memory of [5] works precisely because its outer code is cheap, but scaling to very low logical error rates still requires substantial distance.

**GKP state preparation cost.** Finite-energy GKP states are highly non-Gaussian and expensive to prepare: hundreds of conditional-displacement rounds per state [7], with each round limited by ancilla (transmon) coherence. Magic-state preparation for universal GKP computation inherits this cost, and the fault-tolerance threshold for GKP-plus-surface-code architectures remains demanding.

**Ancilla-induced errors.** Both families rely on an auxiliary nonlinear element (transmon, SNAIL) for drives, gates, and syndrome extraction. Ancilla decay during a conditional displacement injects large, uncorrectable displacements — the leading error source in current GKP experiments [7] — motivating fault-tolerant ancilla designs.

**Concatenation overhead in practice.** While biased noise dramatically reduces *asymptotic* overhead, near-term devices still need fast classical decoding, low-latency control, and per-mode calibration; the engineering gap between a distance-5 demonstration and a useful logical qubit remains significant [5].

## 7 Conclusion

Bosonic quantum error correction has matured from a theoretical curiosity into the most hardware-efficient demonstrated path toward fault tolerance. The dissipative cat qubit showed that *engineering the environment* can replace measurement-based feedback, producing noise so strongly biased that a classical repetition code suffices as the outer layer [1, 3, 4, 5]. The GKP code showed that a single oscillator can correct arbitrary small errors in both quadratures with Gaussian Clifford gates, and finite-energy grid states have now been stabilized beyond break-even [2, 7, 9]. With biased-noise concatenation demonstrated at distance 5, GKP qudits protected over hundreds of rounds, and hundred-second bit-flip times, the first practically useful logical qubits may be bosonic rather than multi-qubit. The remaining challenges are concrete and quantitative: faster non-Gaussian state preparation, ancilla errors during entangling operations, and the systems engineering of many-mode bosonic processors. None appears fundamental.
