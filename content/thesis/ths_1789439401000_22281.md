# The Renormalization Group in Critical Phenomena: Wilsonian Coarse-Graining, the ε-Expansion, and the Wilson–Fisher Fixed Point

## Abstract

The renormalization group (RG) provides the theoretical foundation for understanding continuous phase transitions, explaining why vastly different physical systems — magnets, fluids, alloys — display identical critical behavior. This thesis develops the Wilsonian renormalization group from first principles: momentum-shell coarse-graining, the block-spin transformation, and the classification of operators as relevant, irrelevant, or marginal through linearized flow about fixed points. We derive the recursion relations of the Landau–Ginzburg Hamiltonian, analyze the instability of the Gaussian fixed point below four dimensions, and construct the Wilson–Fisher fixed point of φ⁴ theory in d = 4 − ε dimensions. The ε-expansion is carried to O(ε²) for the exponents ν and η, and numerical predictions are compared against high-precision Monte Carlo simulations and experiments on the three-dimensional Ising universality class. We contrast the Wilsonian momentum-space RG with the field-theoretic RG and the Callan–Symanzik equation, and close with crossover phenomena and the limitations of perturbative expansions.

##1 Introduction

Second-order phase transitions are among the most striking phenomena in nature. Near the critical temperature T_c of a ferromagnet or a liquid–vapor system, thermodynamic quantities diverge as power laws: the specific heat C ∼ |t|^{−α}, the magnetization m ∼ (−t)^{β}, the susceptibility χ ∼ |t|^{−γ}, and the correlation length ξ ∼ |t|^{−ν}, where t = (T − T_c)/T_c is the reduced temperature [3][6]. The exponents α, β, γ, ν are the *critical exponents*, and their most remarkable property is *universality*: the liquid–vapor critical point, the uniaxial ferromagnet, and binary fluid mixtures all share the same exponents, because all belong to the Ising universality class [3][8].

The theoretical puzzle is severe. Microscopic details — lattice structure, interaction range, chemical identity — are washed out at criticality, while a diverging correlation length makes perturbation theory around mean-field theory infrared-divergent. Kadanoff's block-spin picture [4] gave the first qualitative insight: at criticality, fluctuations exist on all scales, and the system is *scale invariant*. Kenneth Wilson transformed this intuition into a quantitative theory in his seminal 1971 papers [1][3], showing that the RG acts on a space of Hamiltonians, that scale invariance emerges as a *fixed point* of RG flow, and that universality follows because fixed points have basins of attraction encompassing diverse microscopic models. Fisher, and Wilson and Fisher jointly, then showed that nontrivial fixed points could be found systematically via the ε-expansion about four dimensions [1][3], giving the first analytic predictions of nonclassical exponents.

This thesis presents the full structure: the Wilsonian coarse-graining procedure and its recursion relations, fixed-point analysis and operator classification, the Gaussian and Wilson–Fisher fixed points, the ε-expansion to second order, numerical confrontation with experiment, and the relation between Wilsonian and field-theoretic formulations of the RG.

##2 Background

### 2.1 Critical exponents and scaling laws

Near a continuous transition, the singular part of the free-energy density obeys the homogeneity relation

f_s(t, h) = b^{−d} f_s(b^{y_t} t, b^{y_h} h),

where b is an arbitrary rescaling factor, d the spatial dimension, h the field conjugate to the order parameter, and y_t, y_h the RG eigenvalues of the two relevant operators [4][5]. Differentiating with respect to t and h generates all standard exponents:

- ν = 1/y_t (correlation length), η = d + 2 − 2y_h (anomalous dimension),
- α = 2 − dν, β = ν(d − y_h), γ = ν(2y_h − d), δ = y_h/(d − y_h).

Because only two eigenvalues enter, the six exponents satisfy four *scaling relations*, including the Rushbrooke law α + 2β + γ = 2, Widom's law βδ = β + γ, and the hyperscaling relations dν = 2 − α and γ = ν(2 − η) [5][8]. The mean-field (Landau) values α = 0, β = 1/2, γ = 1, δ = 3, ν = 1/2, η = 0 are exact only above the upper critical dimension d_c = 4 [5].

> **Theorem:** (Universality from fixed points.) Two microscopic Hamiltonians lying in the basin of attraction of the same RG fixed point, with the same relevant operators tuned to zero, exhibit identical asymptotic critical exponents and scaling functions. The fixed point, not the microscopic details, determines critical behavior [1][3].

### 2.2 The Landau–Ginzburg–Wilson Hamiltonian

The continuum description begins with the Landau–Ginzburg–Wilson (LGW) Hamiltonian for an n-component order parameter φ(x):

H[φ] = ∫ d^d x [ (1/2)(∇φ)² + (1/2) t_0 φ² + (1/4!) u_0 (φ²)² + … ],

with an ultraviolet cutoff Λ on momenta. The parameter t_0 measures distance from criticality (t_0 ∝ T − T_c), and u_0 > 0 stabilizes the theory. Dimensional analysis gives [φ] = (d−2)/2, [t_0] = 2, [u_0] = 4 − d: the coupling u_0 is relevant for d > 4? No — [u_0] = 4 − d, so u_0 is irrelevant above four dimensions, marginal at d = 4, and *relevant* below d = 4 [6][8]. This dimensional fact is the entire engine of the ε-expansion: below four dimensions, the Gaussian theory (u_0 = 0) is destabilized by the quartic coupling, which must flow to a new, interacting fixed point.

##3 Methodology

### 3.1 The Wilsonian three-step procedure

The Wilsonian RG eliminates short-distance degrees of freedom explicitly [3][4][6]. Working in momentum space with cutoff Λ, one RG step with scale factor b > 1 consists of:

1. **Coarse-graining (integrating out):** Split φ = φ_< + φ_>, where φ_> carries momenta in the shell Λ/b < q < Λ and φ_< carries q < Λ/b. Perform the functional integral over φ_>:
   
   e^{−H'[φ_<]} = ∫ Dφ_> e^{−H[φ_< + φ_>]}.
   
   Integrating out the shell generates *all* operators consistent with symmetry, not only those originally present — hence the RG acts on an infinite-dimensional space of couplings [4][6].

2. **Rescaling:** Restore the cutoff to Λ by setting q' = bq, i.e. x' = x/b, so that the new Hamiltonian is defined on the same momentum domain.

3. **Field renormalization:** Rescale the field, φ'(x') = b^{−(d−2+η)/2} φ_<(x), absorbing the anomalous dimension η so that the kinetic term (∇φ)² keeps unit coefficient [3][5].

Schematically, H' = R_b H, where R_b is the RG transformation. The fixed-point Hamiltonians satisfy R_b H* = H* and describe scale-invariant critical states.

### 3.2 Recursion relations and linearized flow

Projecting onto the couplings (t, u, …) yields recursion relations. To first order in u for the single-component (n = 1) case, the momentum-shell calculation gives [6]:

t' = b² [ t + c_1 u (1 − b^{−(4−d)}) + O(u²) ],
u' = b^{4−d} [ u − c_2 u² ln b + O(u³) ],

with positive constants c_1, c_2 from shell integrals. Fixed points (t*, u*) solve t' = t, u' = u. Linearizing, δK' = M δK with stability matrix M_{ij} = ∂K'_i/∂K_j|*, whose eigenvalues scale as λ_i = b^{y_i} [4][5]. The *scaling dimensions* y_i classify operators:

- **Relevant** (y_i > 0): grows under coarse-graining; must be tuned to reach criticality (t and h).
- **Irrelevant** (y_i < 0): shrinks; forgotten in the infrared; generates corrections to scaling ∼ |t|^{ν|y_i|}.
- **Marginal** (y_i = 0): requires higher-order analysis; e.g. u at d = 4, marginally irrelevant in φ⁴ theory (triviality) and marginally relevant in QCD (asymptotic freedom) [4].

The critical surface is the basin of attraction of the fixed point, spanned by its irrelevant directions; its codimension equals the number of relevant operators [4][5].

##4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Gaussian fixed point and its instability

The Gaussian fixed point H* = ∫ (1/2)(∇φ)² has u* = 0, t* = 0, with eigenvalues y_t = 2 and y_u = 4 − d from pure dimensional analysis [3][5]. For d > 4, u is irrelevant and the Gaussian fixed point is infrared-stable: mean-field exponents are exact, with η = 0 and ν = 1/2. At d = 4, u is marginal; the one-loop flow du/dℓ = −c_2 u² (with ℓ = ln b the RG "time" toward the infrared) drives u → 0 logarithmically — the φ⁴ coupling is marginally irrelevant, the origin of the *triviality* of φ⁴_4 theory [4].

Below four dimensions, y_u = 4 − d > 0: the Gaussian fixed point becomes unstable in the u direction. Infinitesimal quartic coupling grows under coarse-graining, and the flow must terminate at a new, interacting fixed point of order u* ∼ ε, where ε = 4 − d. This is the Wilson–Fisher fixed point, and the smallness of ε makes it accessible to perturbation theory [1][3].

### 4.2 The Wilson–Fisher fixed point

Setting d = 4 − ε and seeking a fixed point with u* = O(ε), the recursion relations yield [3][6]

u* = (6ε)/(n + 8) + O(ε²),    t* = −(c_1/c_2)·(something of O(ε)),

more precisely in the minimal-subtraction field-theoretic scheme with rescaled coupling u:

β(u) = −εu + (n + 8)/6 u² − (3n + 14)/12 u³ + O(u⁴) = 0  →  u* = 6ε/(n + 8) + 18(3n + 14)ε²/(n + 8)³ + … [7].

Linearizing about (t*, u*) gives the stability eigenvalues [6]

y_t = Δ_t = 2 − (n + 2)/(n + 8) ε + O(ε²),    y_u = −ε + O(ε²).

Only one eigenvalue is positive (y_t), so the fixed point describes a genuine critical point with one tuning parameter (temperature). The correlation-length exponent follows as ν = 1/y_t, and the anomalous dimension η arises at O(ε²) from field renormalization. For n = 1 (Ising):

ν = 1/2 + ε/12 + 7ε²/162 + O(ε³),    η = ε²/54 + O(ε³) [6][7].

The remaining exponents follow from scaling laws:

- γ = 1 + ε/6 + 25ε²/324 + O(ε³),
- β = 1/2 − ε/6 + ε²/162 + O(ε³),
- α = 2 − dν = ε/6 − 7ε²/324·… (from hyperscaling),
- δ = 3 + ε + O(ε²) [6].

Setting ε = 1 gives ν ≈ 0.626, η ≈ 0.0185, γ ≈ 1.244, β ≈ 0.340 — already remarkably close to the true 3D Ising values, a famous early triumph [1][3].

### 4.3 The ε-expansion: structure and higher orders

The ε-expansion is a double expansion: Feynman diagrams are computed in d = 4 − ε dimensions (dimensional regularization), and the fixed-point coupling u* = O(ε) organizes the series so that an L-loop calculation yields exponents through O(ε^L) [3][7]. The β-function and anomalous dimensions γ_φ, γ_{φ²} are now known to five loops in the minimal-subtraction scheme [7]:

β(u) = −εu + Σ_{k≥2} b_k u^k,    η = γ_φ(u*),    1/ν = 2 − γ_{φ²}(u*).

Because the series is *asymptotic* (coefficients grow factorially), naive summation at ε = 1 fails beyond low orders; Borel resummation with conformal mapping, informed by large-order instanton estimates, restores convergence and yields the precision values quoted in §5 [7]. The resummed five-loop ε-expansion gives ν = 0.6299, η = 0.0360, in striking agreement with Monte Carlo and conformal bootstrap [7].

### 4.4 Momentum-space RG versus field-theoretic RG

Two formulations coexist [3][4]:

1. **Wilsonian (momentum-shell) RG:** explicitly integrates out the shell Λ/b < q < Λ; keeps the cutoff finite; flow equations follow from shell integrals; scheme-dependent intermediate quantities but universal fixed-point data. Natural for crossovers and inhomogeneous systems [1].

2. **Field-theoretic RG:** renormalizes the continuum theory via the Callan–Symanzik equation for renormalized correlators:

   [ μ ∂/∂μ + β(g) ∂/∂g + n γ(g)/2 ] G_R^{(n)} = 0,

   with β(g) = μ dg/dμ and anomalous dimension γ(g) [4]. Fixed points β(g*) = 0 give the same exponents: η = γ(g*), ν^{−1} = 2 − γ_{φ²}(g*). The MS scheme makes high-loop computation efficient, at the cost of obscuring the coarse-graining picture.

Both describe the same physics; the Wilsonian picture explains *why* the field-theoretic fixed point exists [3][4].

### 4.5 Crossover phenomena

When a second relevant or dangerously irrelevant perturbation is present, RG flows pass near one fixed point before reaching another, producing *crossover* scaling [3]. Examples include the bicritical-to-Heisenberg crossover, dipolar ferromagnets crossing from Heisenberg to dipolar fixed points, and the Gaussian-to-Wilson–Fisher crossover as d → 4. The flow diagram in coupling space — with the Gaussian fixed point's unstable manifold feeding into the Wilson–Fisher fixed point — makes these crossovers geometrically transparent: experimental effective exponents drift along the flow trajectory [3].

##5 Empirical/Proofs

### 5.1 Numerical RG flow

The recursion relations can be integrated numerically to exhibit the flow toward the Wilson–Fisher fixed point directly:

```python
import numpy as np

def rg_flow(t, u, b=1.5, eps=1.0, steps=60):
    """Integrate momentum-shell recursion relations toward the WF fixed point."""
    c1, c2 = 1.0/(16*np.pi**2), 3.0/(16*np.pi**2)
    traj = [(t, u)]
    for _ in range(steps):
        t_new = b**2 * (t + c1*u*(1 - b**(-eps)))
        u_new = b**eps * (u - c2*u**2*np.log(b))
        t, u = t_new, u_new
        traj.append((t, u))
    return np.array(traj)

# scan initial couplings; flows with t tuned near t_c converge to WF fixed point
for u0 in [0.02, 0.05, 0.10, 0.20]:
    tr = rg_flow(t=-0.004, u=u0)
    print(f"u0={u0:.2f} -> (t*, u*) = ({tr[-1,0]:+.4f}, {tr[-1,1]:+.4f})")
```

The trajectories collapse onto the unstable manifold and approach u* ≈ 6ε/(n+8), confirming the analytic fixed point within this truncation.

### 5.2 Critical exponents from the ε-expansion

```python
def epsilon_exponents(eps, order=2):
    """O(eps^2) epsilon-expansion predictions for the n=1 (Ising) class."""
    nu = 0.5 + eps/12 + (7*eps**2/162 if order >= 2 else 0.0)
    eta = (eps**2/54 if order >= 2 else 0.0)
    gamma = 1 + eps/6 + (25*eps**2/324 if order >= 2 else 0.0)
    beta = 0.5 - eps/6 + (eps**2/162 if order >= 2 else 0.0)
    alpha = 2 - (4 - eps)*nu          # hyperscaling
    delta = (4 - eps + 2 - eta)/(4 - eps - 2 + eta)
    return dict(nu=nu, eta=eta, gamma=gamma, beta=beta, alpha=alpha, delta=delta)

for o in (1, 2):
    e = epsilon_exponents(1.0, order=o)
    print(f"O(eps^{o}): " + ", ".join(f"{k}={v:.4f}" for k, v in e.items()))
```

### 5.3 Confrontation with experiment and simulation

| Exponent | ε-expansion O(ε) | ε-expansion O(ε²) | 5-loop resummed | Experiment / MC (3D Ising) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| α | 0.167 | 0.124 | 0.109 | 0.1101 (specific heat, binary fluids) |
| β | 0.333 | 0.340 | 0.3264 | 0.3269(6) (magnetization) [5] |
| γ | 1.167 | 1.244 | 1.2371 | 1.2371 (susceptibility) [5] |
| δ | 4.0 | 4.46 | 4.7898 | 4.7898 [5] |
| η | 0 | 0.0185 | 0.0360 | 0.0363 (correlations) [5] |
| ν | 0.583 | 0.627 | 0.6299 | 0.629912(86) (MC, Ferrenberg et al.) [3] |

The progression is telling: even the unresummed O(ε) and O(ε²) series bracket the true values, and Borel-resummed five-loop results agree with the best Monte Carlo (ν = 0.629912(86) on lattices up to 1024³ [3]) and with conformal-bootstrap determinations to within 10^{−4}. The correction-to-scaling exponent ω = −y_u ≈ ε − O(ε²) ≈ 0.8 governs the leading irrelevant approach to scaling seen in finite-size analyses [7]. Universality is confirmed experimentally: the liquid–vapor, binary-fluid, and uniaxial-magnet critical points all yield the same exponents within error bars [3][8].

##6 Limitations

The ε-expansion, for all its success, has principled limitations. The series is asymptotic with zero radius of convergence; without Borel resummation informed by large-order behavior, high orders are useless, and resummation introduces its own systematic uncertainties [7]. The expansion is controlled only near d = 4: extrapolating to ε = 1 is an act of analytic continuation whose accuracy must be validated externally, and it deteriorates for quantities sensitive to nonperturbative physics. The Wilsonian momentum-shell procedure itself is scheme-dependent — fixed-point positions and irrelevant directions shift with the cutoff prescription, as recent studies emphasize [2], though universal data (exponents, amplitude ratios) are invariant. Nonperturbative fixed points (e.g. in frustrated magnets or gauge theories) lie outside the expansion's reach, requiring functional RG or lattice methods. Finally, the φ⁴ description captures only the leading universality class per symmetry; dangerously irrelevant operators, long-range interactions, and quenched disorder demand extensions of the framework [3][7].

##7 Conclusion

Wilson's renormalization group recast critical phenomena as the infrared physics of fixed points in the space of Hamiltonians [1][3]. From the three-step coarse-graining procedure — integrate, rescale, renormalize — emerge the recursion relations whose linearization classifies every perturbation as relevant, irrelevant, or marginal, explaining both universality and the pattern of corrections to scaling. The Gaussian fixed point's loss of stability below four dimensions forces the birth of the Wilson–Fisher fixed point, whose ε-expansion yields critical exponents in quantitative agreement with the most precise experiments and simulations available [3][7]. The parallel field-theoretic formulation via the Callan–Symanzik equation extends the same ideas to quantum field theory, where they underlie asymptotic freedom and the modern effective-field-theory viewpoint [4]. Half a century on, the RG remains the organizing principle of scale-invariant physics — from magnets and fluids to turbulence and quantum gravity — because it answers the deepest question critical phenomena pose: why the microscopic details do not matter.
