# The Succinctness Frontier: Polynomial Commitments and the Evolution from Groth16 to zk-STARKs

## Abstract

Zero-knowledge succinct arguments have evolved from the circuit-specific pairing constructions of the early 2010s into a diverse ecosystem spanning universal SNARKs, transparent STARKs, and hash-based post-quantum designs. This article argues that the prover–verifier complexity frontier of the entire field is governed by a single primitive: the *polynomial commitment scheme*. We develop the R1CS-to-QAP pipeline underlying Pinocchio [6] and Groth16 [1], the permutation-based arithmetization of PLONK [2], and the AIR/FRI architecture of zk-STARKs [3], and show how each reduces succinctness to commitments: KZG's constant-size pairing openings [4], inner-product arguments' logarithmic transparent proofs, and FRI's hash-based proximity tests. Synthesizing asymptotic bounds [8] with implementation benchmarks [7], we quantify the trade space — from 128-byte proofs with millisecond verification to post-quantum transparency at tens of kilobytes — and identify the open frontiers: linear-time provers, quantum-secure aggregation, and recursion-friendly verifier circuits.

## 1 Introduction

A *zero-knowledge succinct non-interactive argument of knowledge* (zk-SNARK) lets a prover convince a verifier that it knows a witness *w* satisfying a public relation *R(x, w) = 1*, without revealing *w*, in a single message whose size is polylogarithmic in the size of the computation. The formal properties are *completeness* (honest provers convince), *knowledge soundness* (an extractor recovers *w* from any convincing prover), and *zero-knowledge* (the proof leaks nothing beyond the truth of the statement). *Succinctness* demands that proof size and verification time be sublinear — ideally polylogarithmic — in the circuit size *n*, while the prover remains quasilinear, *O(n log n)*.

The history of the field is the history of how that succinctness is purchased. Groth16 [1] buys it with pairings and a circuit-specific trusted setup, achieving the theoretical minimum: three group elements and three pairings. PLONK [2] buys it with a universal, updatable structured reference string and a permutation argument, trading a modest constant factor for generality and recursion. zk-STARKs [3] refuse the trusted setup entirely, buying succinctness with collision-resistant hashing and interactive oracle proofs, and pay for transparency with proofs one to two orders of magnitude larger.

This article's central thesis is that *all three families are the same object wearing different polynomial commitment schemes*. Once a computation is arithmetized into polynomial constraints, the prover commits to the polynomials, and the verifier's work collapses to checking a handful of evaluations. KZG commitments [4] give constant-size openings under pairing assumptions; inner-product arguments (IPAs) give logarithmic openings under the discrete-logarithm assumption with no setup; FRI gives hash-based openings with no setup and conjectured post-quantum security. The batched-opening techniques of Boneh et al. [5] then explain how modern systems keep verification constant even as circuits grow. We make this thesis precise through asymptotics [8] and concrete benchmarks [7].

## 2 Background

**Arithmetization.** The SNARK pipeline begins by compiling a program into constraints over a finite field 𝔽. A *rank-1 constraint system* (R1CS) is a triple of *m × (n+1)* matrices *A, B, C* such that a witness vector *w* (with *w₀ = 1*) satisfies *(Aw) ∘ (Bw) = Cw*, where ∘ is the Hadamard product — every multiplication gate becomes one constraint, with linear gates folded in for free. Pinocchio [6] showed that R1CS converts to a *quadratic arithmetic program* (QAP) by Lagrange interpolation: for each constraint row *rᵢ*, one builds polynomials *Aⱼ, Bⱼ, Cⱼ* with *Aⱼ(rᵢ) = Aᵢⱼ*, and the R1CS is satisfiable if and only if

> **Theorem:** *A(x)B(x) − C(x) = H(x)Z(x)* for *A(x) = Σ wⱼAⱼ(x)* (and analogously *B, C*), where *Z(x) = ∏ᵢ (x − rᵢ)* is the vanishing polynomial of the constraint domain and *H(x)* is a degree-*(m−2)* quotient polynomial. The divisibility check reduces *m* constraints to a single polynomial identity.

The prover's expensive step is computing *H(x)* via FFTs in *O(m log m)*; the verifier never sees the polynomials themselves, only commitments to them.

**Interactive oracle proofs and arithmetization alternatives.** The IOP model generalizes PCPs: the prover sends oracles (functions the verifier can query at a few positions), and the verifier is sublinear. STARK arithmetization uses an *algebraic intermediate representation* (AIR): the computation is an execution trace — a table of field elements over time — constrained by polynomial transition rules. The trace is interpolated into polynomials, Reed–Solomon encoded (a blowup by the inverse rate *ρ⁻¹*), and constraint satisfaction becomes a claim that a certain *composition polynomial* has low degree — exactly what FRI tests [3].

**Cryptographic assumptions, ranked by strength.** Pairing-based schemes assume variants of the *t*-strong Diffie–Hellman (*t*-SDH) or knowledge-of-exponent assumptions in bilinear groups; discrete-log schemes assume only the hardness of discrete logarithms (still broken by Shor's algorithm); hash-based schemes assume only collision resistance of the hash (conjectured quantum-resistant, in the random-oracle model). Transparency — the absence of a trusted setup — is only achievable under the latter two.

## 3 Methodology

Our analysis proceeds in three layers. First, *asymptotic accounting*: for each polynomial commitment scheme we tabulate commitment size, evaluation-proof size, prover time, verifier time, public-parameter size, and setup assumptions, following the comparison taxonomy of [8]. Second, *system-level compilation*: we trace how Groth16 [1], PLONK [2], and STARKs [3] each instantiate the commit-then-open pattern, counting group elements, pairings, and hash queries at each step. Third, *empirical grounding*: we synthesize published implementation benchmarks from the ZK/SEC polynomial-commitment survey [7] — prover-side commitment throughput across laptop, server, and mobile hardware — to calibrate the asymptotic constants that determine real deployment choices. To make the FRI folding mechanics concrete, we include a small executable Python model of one FRI round over a prime field.

## 4 Polynomial Commitments and the Succinct-Argument Frontier

### 4.1 KZG: Constant-Size Commitments from Pairings

The Kate–Zaverucha–Goldberg scheme [4] remains the gold standard for compactness. The trusted setup samples a secret *τ* and publishes the structured reference string *{τⁱG₁}ᵢ₌₀ᵈ, {τⁱG₂}ᵢ₌₀ᵈ* (destroying *τ* afterward). To commit to *f(x) = Σ fᵢxⁱ*, the prover computes *C = Σ fᵢ (τⁱG₁) = f(τ)G₁* — a single group element regardless of degree. To open at *z* with claimed value *y*, the prover computes the quotient *q(x) = (f(x) − y)/(x − z)* and sends *π = q(τ)G₁*; the verifier checks

*e(C − yG₁, G₂) = e(π, τG₂ − zG₂)*.

The equation holds exactly when *f(z) = y*, because *(f(τ) − y) = q(τ)(τ − z)* in the exponent. Evaluation binding reduces to the *t*-SDH assumption [4]. KZG is additively homomorphic, which is what makes batching possible: Boneh, Drake, Fisch, and Gabizon [5] showed that a *single* group element can serve as the opening proof for multiple polynomials evaluated at different arbitrary point sets, plugging directly into PLONK to shrink proof size and prover time at the cost of extra 𝔾₂ operations for the verifier.

### 4.2 Inner-Product Arguments and the Transparent Logarithm

Bulletproof-style inner-product arguments trade KZG's constant openings for logarithmic ones in exchange for *transparency*: the public parameters are just random group elements, requiring no trusted ceremony. The prover commits to the coefficient vector of *f* as a Pedersen-style vector commitment and then recursively halves the problem — folding the two halves with a verifier challenge — until a single scalar remains. The result: commitment size 1 group element, proof size *2 log d* group elements, prover *O(d)*, verifier *O(d)*, under the plain discrete-logarithm assumption [8].

The Dory construction compresses IPA verification to *6 log d* group operations with a two-tier matrix commitment, and the Dew family pushes the transparent frontier further: Dew++ achieves a *constant* proof of *10* unknown-order-group elements plus *24* field elements, with *O(1)* public parameters and no trusted setup at all [8]. These schemes dominate the design space where a trusted ceremony is politically or technically unacceptable but pairings are unavailable — at the cost of larger constants than KZG.

### 4.3 FRI: Hash-Based Proximity and Post-Quantum Transparency

The Fast Reed–Solomon Interactive Oracle Proof of Proximity (FRI) takes a radically different route: the commitment to a function *f₀* on a domain *L₀* is simply the Merkle root of its evaluations. The verifier sends a random folding challenge *β₀*; both parties fold to *f₁(x²) = (f₀(x) + f₀(−x))/2 + β₀·(f₀(x) − f₀(−x))/(2x)* on the halved domain *L₁ = {x² : x ∈ L₀}*, which preserves low-degreeness while halving the degree. After *log n* rounds the prover sends a constant polynomial, and the verifier spot-checks *λ* random paths through the Merkle trees, rejecting on any inconsistency. The key soundness fact is a proximity gap:

> **Theorem:** If *f₀* is *δ*-far from the Reed–Solomon code *RS[L₀, ρ]*, then with high probability over the folding challenges, some folded function *fᵢ* is far from *RS[Lᵢ, ρ]*, and a single inconsistent query path exposes the prover. Repeating *λ* queries drives soundness error to *2⁻λ* with proof size *O(λ log n)* hashes.

FRI needs no structured parameters (*O(1)* setup), rests only on hash collision resistance — hence its conjectured post-quantum security — and its prover is dominated by hashing and field arithmetic rather than multi-scalar multiplications. The price is proof size: *λ log n* hashes rather than one or two group elements [3][8].

```python
# One FRI folding round over a prime field (toy parameters)
P = 12289  # 12288 = 2^12 * 3, so 2-power subgroups exist
def fold(evals, beta, inv2=(P + 1) // 2):
    # evals: values of f on a coset closed under x -> -x, paired as (x, -x)
    n = len(evals)
    out = []
    for i in range(n // 2):
        fx, fnx = evals[2 * i], evals[2 * i + 1]
        even = (fx + fnx) * inv2 % P
        odd = (fx - fnx) * inv2 % P
        out.append((even + beta * odd) % P)  # f'(x^2) halves the degree
    return out
```

### 4.4 Compiling Arithmetizations: Groth16, PLONK, and AIR

**Groth16** [1] is the minimal SNARK. From the QAP, the trusted setup samples toxic waste *α, β, γ, δ, τ* and publishes proving/verification keys of size *O(n)*. The prover computes *A = α + Σ wᵢAᵢ(τ) + rδ*, *B*, *C* (two in 𝔾₁, one in 𝔾₂) and the verifier checks a single equation with three pairings, linear only in the number of public inputs. Proof size is three group elements — about 128 bytes on BLS12-381 — and verification takes milliseconds. But the CRS is *circuit-specific*: changing one gate requires a new ceremony, and knowledge soundness holds only in the generic group model.

**PLONK** [2] replaces the QAP with a leaner arithmetization. Each gate is a constraint *q_L·a + q_R·b + q_O·c + q_M·a·b + q_C = 0* over wire values interpolated as polynomials *a(x), b(x), c(x)*, with selector polynomials encoding the gate type; *copy constraints* (the same wire feeding two gates) are enforced by a *permutation argument* — a grand-product identity with random challenges that proves the wire assignment respects a prescribed permutation. The SRS is *universal* (powers of a secret, independent of the circuit) and *updatable* (participants can sequentially contribute randomness). With KZG commitments, a PLONK proof is a constant number of group elements and evaluations (single-digit kilobytes), verified with two pairings after batching [5]. Crucially, PLONK is commitment-agnostic: swapping KZG for FRI yields a transparent, plausibly post-quantum variant at larger proof sizes [7].

**zk-STARKs** [3] complete the spectrum. AIR arithmetization plus FRI low-degree testing gives a system with no trusted setup, no elliptic curves, and no number-theoretic assumptions beyond hashing. The asymptotics are *prover O(T log T)*, *proof O(log² T)*, *verifier polylog(T)* — verified *exponentially faster* than re-execution for large computations [3]. Concretely, proofs land in the 45–200 KB range: roughly 100–1000× larger than Groth16, buying transparency and quantum resistance.

## 5 Empirical Results and Formal Proofs

The following table synthesizes the asymptotic comparison of polynomial commitment schemes from [8] (d = polynomial degree, GP = pairing group, G = prime-order group, GU = unknown-order group, λ = security parameter):

| Scheme | Trusted setup | |pp| | |commit| | |proof| |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| KZG [4] | ✗ | O(d) GP | 1 GP | 1 GP |
| Bootle et al. (IPA) | ✓ transparent | O(√d) G | 1 G | O(√d) G |
| Bulletproofs (IPA) | ✓ transparent | O(d) G | 1 G | 2 log d G |
| FRI [3] | ✓ transparent | O(1) | 1 hash | λ log₂ d hashes |
| Dory | ✓ transparent | O(d) GP | 1 GP | 6 log d GP |
| Dew++ | ✓ transparent | O(1) GU | 1 GU | 10 GU + 24 𝔽_λ |

Compiling these into full systems gives the frontier:

| System | Proof size | Verification | Setup | Assumption |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Groth16 [1] | 3 GE (~128 B) | 3 pairings + O(m) | circuit-specific | generic group / KoE |
| PLONK + KZG [2][5] | ~1–2 KB | 2 pairings | universal, updatable | t-SDH |
| PLONK + FRI [2][7] | ~10–50 KB | O(λ log n) hashes | transparent | hash CR |
| zk-STARK [3] | 45–200 KB | polylog(T) hashes | transparent | hash CR (PQ) |
| Bulletproofs [8] | O(log n) | O(n) | transparent | discrete log |

Implementation benchmarks sharpen the constants. The ZK/SEC survey's prover-side commitment throughput measurements (Remco Bloemen's benchmark suite) show FRI-based implementations (Winterfell, Plonky2) sustaining ~10⁷ field elements/second on an Apple M1 Max — roughly **10×** the throughput of the best MSM-based KZG/IPA implementations at moderate polynomial sizes — because hashing and field arithmetic are cache-friendly while multi-scalar multiplications are memory-bandwidth bound. On a 96-core server the MSM schemes parallelize well and the gap shrinks to ~2–3×, and on mobile hardware (A15) FRI's advantage widens again under memory constraints [7]. This is why client-side proving (e.g., in-browser or mobile wallets) increasingly favors FRI-based systems despite their larger proofs: the prover is the bottleneck, not the wire.

Two formal anchors underwrite the comparison. First, KZG *evaluation binding*: any prover producing a commitment *C* and two valid openings *(z, y, π), (z, y′, π′)* with *y ≠ y′* yields a *t*-SDH break, since the verification equations subtract to *(y − y′)·e(G₁, G₂) = e(π − π′, (τ − z)G₂)*, exposing *1/(τ − z)* in the exponent [4]. Second, the FRI proximity-gap theorem above: soundness error decays exponentially in the number of query repetitions *λ*, independent of the computation size [3].

## 6 Limitations

The frontier is not free of sharp edges. **Trusted setups remain a trust bottleneck**: Groth16's circuit-specific ceremony means every circuit upgrade re-runs a multi-party computation, and a single surviving toxic-waste holder can forge proofs undetectably [1]. PLONK's universal SRS mitigates this — one ceremony serves all circuits — but still assumes at least one honest participant [2]. **STARK prover memory** is the practical ceiling: Reed–Solomon blowup (typically 4–8× at rate 1/4–1/8) plus Merkle trees make the prover RAM-hungry, often the binding constraint before asymptotics matter [3][7]. **FRI proof sizes**, while polylogarithmic, are concretely large for small computations — a regime where Groth16's 128 bytes dominate — and FRI's conjectured post-quantum security, while plausible, lacks the decades of cryptanalysis behind lattice or hash-signature assumptions. **Recursive composition** (proving verification inside a circuit, the engine of incrementally verifiable computation) favors pairing-based systems with algebraic verifiers; hash-heavy FRI verifiers are expensive to recurse, though folding schemes are closing the gap. Finally, **quantum vulnerability** divides the field cleanly: everything resting on pairings or discrete logarithms — Groth16, PLONK+KZG, Dory, Bulletproofs — falls to Shor's algorithm, while only the FRI/hash family plausibly survives [3][8].

## 7 Conclusion

The succinct-argument landscape — Groth16's three group elements, PLONK's universal permutation argument, STARK's transparent polylogarithmic verification — is unified by a single design decision: *which polynomial commitment scheme compiles the arithmetization*. KZG [4] buys minimal proofs and millisecond verification with pairings and a trusted setup; inner-product arguments buy transparency at logarithmic size under discrete logarithms; FRI buys transparency *and* conjectured post-quantum security at the cost of kilobyte-scale proofs [3][8]. Batched openings [5] and updatable universal setups [2] have softened the historical trade-offs, and benchmarks [7] show the concrete crossover points depend as much on hardware — MSM bandwidth versus hash throughput — as on asymptotics. The open frontier is now threefold: *linear-time provers* that remove the FFT bottleneck, *quantum-secure aggregation* that compresses many proofs into one without number theory, and *recursion-friendly verifiers* that make incrementally verifiable computation practical across all three families. Whichever commitment scheme wins each sub-frontier will define the next generation of succinct arguments.
