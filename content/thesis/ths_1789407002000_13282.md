# Supersymmetry: the Super-Poincaré Algebra, the Hierarchy Problem, MSSM Phenomenology, and Collider Searches

## Abstract

Supersymmetry (SUSY) is the unique non-trivial extension of Poincaré symmetry in relativistic quantum field theory, proven by the Haag–Łopuszański–Sohnius theorem of 1975 [2]. By adjoining fermionic generators to the Poincaré algebra, SUSY pairs every bosonic degree of freedom with a fermionic partner and vice versa, yielding a *super-Poincaré* algebra whose anticommutators close on spacetime translations. This thesis develops the full architecture of supersymmetric particle physics: the Coleman–Mandula no-go theorem and its fermionic loophole, superspace and superfield technology, the non-renormalization theorems, and the cancellation of quadratic divergences that stabilizes the electroweak scale. We construct the Minimal Supersymmetric Standard Model — its superpotential, R-parity, and soft-breaking sector — and demonstrate gauge-coupling unification under one-loop renormalization group evolution. We review the Higgs mass, the little-hierarchy problem, neutralino dark matter, and the mediation mechanisms of supersymmetry breaking: gravity, gauge, and anomaly mediation. Finally we survey collider searches from LEP and the Tevatron to the full LHC Run 2 dataset, summarize exclusion limits on gluinos, squarks, stops, and electroweakinos, and discuss what the null results imply for natural SUSY, split SUSY, and future colliders.

## 1 Introduction

Of all proposed extensions of the Standard Model (SM), supersymmetry stands apart for a *structural* reason: it is, in a precise mathematical sense, the only consistent way to extend the spacetime symmetries of a relativistic quantum field theory beyond the Poincaré group and internal symmetries. This uniqueness was established in 1975 by Haag, Łopuszański, and Sohnius [2].

The physical stakes are high. The Standard Model contains an elementary scalar, the Higgs boson discovered in 2012, whose mass is quadratically sensitive to the highest energy scales in physics. In the absence of a protective symmetry, quantum corrections should drag the Higgs mass toward the Planck scale, roughly seventeen orders of magnitude above its measured value of 125 GeV. Supersymmetry solves this *hierarchy problem* by a boson–fermion cancellation: for every loop diagram that shifts the Higgs mass upward, there is a superpartner diagram shifting it downward by precisely the same amount [1].

Below: background (Section 2), methodology with the super-Poincaré algebra and an RG code (Section 3), the deep dive on the MSSM, unification, dark matter, and mediation (Section 4), collider results (Section 5), limitations (Section 6), and conclusions (Section 7).

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The Coleman–Mandula theorem

In 1967, Coleman and Mandula proved a no-go theorem of striking generality [3]: in a relativistic quantum field theory with a non-trivial S-matrix, the maximal symmetry group of the S-matrix is the *direct product* of the Poincaré group with an internal symmetry group. Any attempt to combine internal and spacetime symmetries in a non-trivial way — with bosonic generators transforming in non-trivial representations of the Lorentz group while carrying internal quantum numbers — forces the S-matrix to be trivial. The theorem assumes that all symmetry generators are bosonic, i.e. they satisfy commutation relations.

> **Theorem:** *(Coleman–Mandula, 1967.)* In any relativistic quantum field theory with a non-trivial interacting S-matrix, the symmetry algebra is the direct sum of the Poincaré algebra and an internal symmetry algebra. No non-trivial mixing between spacetime and internal symmetries is possible with bosonic generators [3].

### 2.2 The graded loophole: Haag–Łopuszański–Sohnius

The Wess–Zumino model of 1974 was the first interacting four-dimensional supersymmetric field theory. The definitive classification came from Haag, Łopuszański, and Sohnius in 1975 [2]:

> **Theorem:** *(Haag–Łopuszański–Sohnius, 1975.)* The most general graded Lie algebra of symmetries of a four-dimensional S-matrix with non-vanishing masses consists of the Poincaré algebra, internal symmetry generators, N fermionic supersymmetry generators Q, and central charges arising as anticommutators of the fermionic charges. In the massless limit, the fermionic charges may additionally generate the conformal group [2].

The crucial move is to allow generators with *fermionic statistics* — operators that anticommute rather than commute. The supercharges Q transform as spinors under the Lorentz group but are singlets under internal symmetries, evading Coleman–Mandula because the theorem's proof fails for graded algebras. The anticommutator of two supercharges closes on the momentum operator, the generator of spacetime translations:

$$\{Q_\alpha, \bar{Q}_{\dot{\beta}}\} = 2\,\sigma^\mu_{\alpha\dot{\beta}}\,P_\mu$$

This relation is the heart of supersymmetry: supersymmetry is, quite literally, the "square root" of spacetime translations, as illustrated in Figure 1.

### 2.3 Early history and the MSSM

Following the Wess–Zumino model, the application of supersymmetry to particle physics proper began with the work of Dimopoulos and Georgi in 1981, who constructed a softly broken supersymmetric SU(5) grand unified theory and showed that low-energy supersymmetry raises the unification scale and the predicted proton lifetime into agreement with experiment [1]. The Minimal Supersymmetric Standard Model emerged from the combined efforts of many authors in the early 1980s and was systematized in Martin's "Supersymmetry Primer" [1], the standard pedagogical reference. A parallel theoretical pillar is the non-renormalization theorem, which guarantees that the superpotential parameters receive no infinite — and, with certain regularizations, no finite — renormalization at any order in perturbation theory [1]:

> **Theorem:** *(Non-renormalization.)* In a supersymmetric gauge theory, the superpotential is not renormalized in perturbation theory: all ultraviolet divergences can be absorbed into wavefunction renormalization. Holomorphic quantities such as the superpotential and the gauge kinetic function are protected [1].

This theorem is what makes the supersymmetric solution to the hierarchy problem *technically natural*: once a small ratio of scales is arranged at tree level, radiative corrections cannot spoil it.

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 The super-Poincaré algebra and supermultiplets

The N = 1 super-Poincaré algebra in four dimensions adds two-component Weyl spinor supercharges Q to the generators P (translations), M (Lorentz transformations), and T (internal symmetries). The graded algebra reads:

- $[P_\mu, Q_\alpha] = 0$ — supercharges commute with translations
- $[M_{\mu\nu}, Q_\alpha]$ — Q transforms as a spinor
- $\{Q_\alpha, Q_\beta\} = 0$, $\{Q_\alpha, \bar{Q}_{\dot{\beta}}\} = 2\,\sigma^\mu_{\alpha\dot{\beta}}\,P_\mu$

An immediate and profound consequence: because $P^2$ commutes with all generators, particles in the same *supermultiplet* have identical mass in unbroken supersymmetry. The simplest multiplets are the **chiral supermultiplet** (one complex scalar, one Weyl fermion, one auxiliary field F) and the **vector supermultiplet** (one gauge boson, one Weyl gaugino, one auxiliary field D). This exact boson–fermion balance within each multiplet is the algebraic origin of the divergence cancellations discussed in Section 4.1.

### 3.2 Superspace and superfields

Supersymmetry is most elegantly formulated in *superspace*, ordinary spacetime augmented by two-component Grassmann (anticommuting) coordinates theta and theta-bar. A *superfield* is a function of superspace coordinates whose component expansion contains the fields of a full supermultiplet [1]. Supersymmetry transformations act as translations in the Grassmann directions, making invariance manifest.

The MSSM is built from chiral superfields for matter and Higgs fields, and vector superfields for the gauge bosons and gauginos. Interactions are encoded in the *superpotential* W, a holomorphic function of chiral superfields:

$$W_{\text{MSSM}} = y_u\,Q\,H_u\,\bar{u} + y_d\,Q\,H_d\,\bar{d} + y_e\,L\,H_d\,\bar{e} + \mu\,H_u\,H_d$$

Holomorphy is a powerful restriction: it forbids the conjugate Higgs couplings that the SM uses for down-type fermions, which is why the MSSM requires *two* Higgs doublets, $H_u$ and $H_d$.

### 3.3 Soft supersymmetry breaking

Exact supersymmetry predicts degenerate superpartners, which are excluded: we have not observed a spin-0 electron at 511 keV. Supersymmetry must be broken, but the breaking must be *soft* — introducing only operators of mass dimension less than four — so that the cancellation of quadratic divergences survives [1]. The soft Lagrangian contains scalar mass terms $m_0^2\,\phi^*\phi$, gaugino masses $(1/2)\,M_a\,\lambda^a \lambda^a$ for bino, wino, and gluino, trilinear A-terms $A_{ijk}\,y_{ijk}\,\phi_i\phi_j\phi_k$, and the bilinear B-term $B\mu\,H_u H_d$. The origin of these soft terms is addressed by *mediation mechanisms* (Section 4.6): gravity mediation, gauge mediation, and anomaly mediation each predict distinctive patterns among the soft parameters.

### 3.4 Renormalization group methodology

A key quantitative tool is the renormalization group evolution of the gauge couplings. At one loop, the inverse fine-structure constants evolve linearly with the logarithm of the energy scale:

$$\frac{d}{dt}\,\alpha_i^{-1} = -\frac{b_i}{2\pi}, \qquad t = \ln(\mu/\mu_0)$$

with MSSM beta-function coefficients $(b_1, b_2, b_3) = (33/5, 1, -3)$ versus SM coefficients $(41/10, -19/6, -7)$. The Python code below implements this running (Figure 4):

```python
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

# One-loop beta-function coefficients (b1, b2, b3)
B_SM = np.array([41/10, -19/6, -7.0])
B_MSSM = np.array([33/5, 1.0, -3.0])

# Measured couplings at M_Z, in SU(5) normalization for alpha_1
MZ = 91.1876
alpha1_inv = 59.01
alpha2_inv = 29.57
alpha3_inv = 8.44
ALPHA0 = np.array([alpha1_inv, alpha2_inv, alpha3_inv])

def run_couplings(beta, t):
    # analytic one-loop solution for inverse couplings
    return ALPHA0[:, None] - np.outer(beta, t) / (2 * np.pi)

t = np.linspace(0, np.log(2e16 / MZ), 400)
mu = MZ * np.exp(t)
sm = run_couplings(B_SM, t)
mssm = run_couplings(B_MSSM, t)

for idx, lab in enumerate(["alpha1", "alpha2", "alpha3"]):
    plt.plot(np.log10(mu), sm[idx], ls="--", label=f"SM {lab}")
    plt.plot(np.log10(mu), mssm[idx], ls="-", label=f"MSSM {lab}")
plt.xlabel("log10(mu / GeV)")
plt.ylabel("alpha_i^{-1}")
plt.legend()
plt.savefig("rge_unification.png")
```

With MSSM beta functions the three couplings meet at approximately $2 \times 10^{16}$ GeV; with SM beta functions they merely cross pairwise — the quantitative core of the unification argument [1].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The hierarchy problem and quadratic-divergence cancellation

The hierarchy problem is the question of why the electroweak scale ($v \approx 246$ GeV) is so much smaller than the Planck scale ($M_{\text{Pl}} \approx 10^{19}$ GeV), and — more sharply — why quantum corrections do not drag the Higgs mass up to the ultraviolet cutoff. A Dirac fermion of mass $m_f$ with Yukawa coupling $\lambda_f$ contributes to the Higgs mass parameter:

$$\Delta m_H^2 \supset -\frac{|\lambda_f|^2}{8\pi^2}\,\Lambda^2 + \text{logarithmic terms}$$

where $\Lambda$ is the ultraviolet cutoff. A complex scalar with quartic coupling $\lambda_S$ contributes with the *opposite* sign:

$$\Delta m_H^2 \supset +\frac{\lambda_S}{16\pi^2}\,\Lambda^2 + \text{logarithmic terms}$$

In a supersymmetric theory, every fermion has *two* scalar partners (one per chirality), and supersymmetry relates the couplings: $\lambda_S = |\lambda_f|^2$. The quadratic divergences cancel exactly, diagram by diagram, as shown in Figure 2 [1]. The dominant cancellation in the SM context is between the top quark loop and the two stop squark loops, which is why the stops play the starring role in naturalness arguments.

The cancellation leaves a finite remainder proportional to the superpartner mass splitting:

$$\Delta m_H^2 \sim \frac{3 y_t^2}{8\pi^2}\,(m_{\tilde{t}_1}^2 + m_{\tilde{t}_2}^2 - 2 m_t^2)\,\ln\frac{\Lambda}{m_{\tilde{t}}}$$

This is the origin of the naturalness bound: if the stops are much heavier than a TeV, the logarithm times the mass splitting reintroduces fine-tuning — the *little-hierarchy problem* discussed in Section 4.4.

### 4.2 The MSSM: field content, R-parity, and the superpartner spectrum

The MSSM pairs every SM particle with a superpartner of opposite spin-statistics (Figure 3):

| SM particle | Spin | Superpartner | Spin | R-parity |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Quarks $q$ | 1/2 | Squarks $\tilde{q}$ | 0 | $-1$ |
| Leptons $\ell$ | 1/2 | Sleptons $\tilde{\ell}$ | 0 | $-1$ |
| Gluon $g$ | 1 | Gluino $\tilde{g}$ | 1/2 | $-1$ |
| W boson | 1 | Wino $\tilde{W}$ | 1/2 | $-1$ |
| B boson | 1 | Bino $\tilde{B}$ | 1/2 | $-1$ |
| Higgs $h$ | 0 | Higgsinos $\tilde{H}$ | 1/2 | $-1$ |
| Photon, Z, W, h (mixed) | — | Neutralinos $\tilde{\chi}^0_{1..4}$, charginos $\tilde{\chi}^\pm_{1,2}$ | 1/2 | $-1$ |

The neutralinos — mixtures of bino, neutral wino, and neutral Higgsinos — and charginos — mixtures of charged wino and charged Higgsinos — are mass eigenstates after electroweak symmetry breaking. *R-parity* is a discrete $Z_2$ symmetry defined by $P_R = (-1)^{3(B-L)+2s}$: SM particles carry $P_R = +1$ and superpartners $P_R = -1$ [1]. Its consequences are far-reaching: sparticles are produced in pairs at colliders; each sparticle decays through cascades to lighter sparticles; and the lightest supersymmetric particle (LSP) is absolutely stable, escaping the detector as missing transverse energy and providing an excellent dark matter candidate.

R-parity also forbids the renormalizable baryon- and lepton-number-violating superpotential terms that would otherwise mediate catastrophically rapid proton decay.

### 4.3 Gauge-coupling unification

Precision measurements at LEP established the gauge couplings at the Z pole with per-mille accuracy. Evolving them upward with SM beta functions, the three couplings approach each other but fail to meet at a single point. With the MSSM beta functions — the additional Higgs doublet and the full superpartner spectrum contributing above the SUSY threshold — the couplings unify at $M_{\text{GUT}} \approx 2 \times 10^{16}$ GeV with remarkable precision [1]. This is widely regarded as the strongest circumstantial evidence for low-energy supersymmetry: it was predicted *before* the precision LEP measurements, notably by Dimopoulos, Raby, and Wilczek, and confirmed afterward. Unification at $2 \times 10^{16}$ GeV also suppresses proton decay mediated by GUT gauge bosons to lifetimes consistent with Super-Kamiokande bounds.

### 4.4 The Higgs mass, the little-hierarchy problem, and model variants

In the MSSM the quartic Higgs coupling is not a free parameter: it is fixed by the electroweak gauge couplings, giving a tree-level upper bound $m_h \le M_Z\,|\cos 2\beta| \le 91$ GeV. The observed 125 GeV Higgs mass must therefore be generated radiatively, dominantly by top–stop loops:

$$\Delta m_h^2 \approx \frac{3 m_t^4}{4\pi^2 v^2}\,\ln\frac{m_{\tilde{t}_1} m_{\tilde{t}_2}}{m_t^2} + \text{threshold corrections}$$

Reaching 125 GeV requires either multi-TeV stops or near-maximal stop mixing ($|X_t| \approx \sqrt{6}\,M_S$). Both options reintroduce fine-tuning of the electroweak scale at the percent level or worse — the *little-hierarchy problem*. This tension has driven several influential departures from the minimal framework [6][8]: **natural SUSY** keeps Higgsinos, stops, and the left-handed sbottom light while decoupling the first two generations; **split supersymmetry** [8] abandons naturalness, keeping scalars ultra-heavy while gauginos and Higgsinos stay light, preserving unification and dark matter; **gauge-mediated supersymmetry breaking** [7] generates flavor-universal soft terms with a gravitino LSP and photon-plus-missing-energy signatures; and the **NMSSM** adds a singlet superfield, raising the tree-level Higgs mass through a new quartic coupling.

### 4.5 Neutralino dark matter

With conserved R-parity, the lightest neutralino $\tilde{\chi}^0_1$ is stable, weakly interacting, and massive — a prototypical WIMP. Its thermal relic density is set by freeze-out:

$$\Omega_{\tilde{\chi}} h^2 \approx \frac{0.1\,\text{pb}}{\langle \sigma v \rangle}$$

and for weak-scale masses and electroweak-strength annihilation cross sections, this naturally yields the observed dark matter abundance — the celebrated *WIMP miracle* [1]. Viable regions of MSSM parameter space include the bulk region, the coannihilation strip, the Higgs funnel, and the focus-point region with a mixed bino–Higgsino LSP. Direct-detection experiments (XENON, LZ) now probe deep into the parameter space, while indirect detection constrains annihilation in dwarf galaxies. In split SUSY [8] and GMSB [7] the dark matter story changes qualitatively — gravitino or axino LSPs, or non-thermal production from heavy gravitino decays.

### 4.6 Mediation of supersymmetry breaking

Supersymmetry breaking is assumed to occur in a *hidden sector* and be communicated to the visible MSSM by messenger interactions [1]:

1. **Gravity mediation (mSUGRA/CMSSM):** Planck-suppressed operators generate universal soft terms at the GUT scale, parameterized by $m_0$, $m_{1/2}$, $A_0$, $\tan\beta$, and the sign of $\mu$.
2. **Gauge mediation** [7]: messenger fields charged under the SM gauge group transmit breaking through loop diagrams. Soft masses are proportional to gauge couplings squared, automatically flavor-diagonal. The gravitino is the LSP, and the NLSP decays produce photons or leptons plus missing energy.
3. **Anomaly mediation:** soft terms arise from the super-Weyl anomaly, predicting a distinctive gaugino mass pattern and a nearly degenerate wino LSP/NLSP pair with a long-lived chargino signature.

Each scheme leaves fingerprints in the sparticle spectrum and in collider signatures.

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 LEP and Tevatron era

LEP (1989–2000) searched for sparticles in electron–positron collisions up to 209 GeV, setting model-independent lower bounds: chargino masses above approximately 103.5 GeV, sleptons above roughly 100 GeV, and stops above about 95 GeV depending on mixing [1][4]. The Tevatron excluded gluinos below roughly 300–400 GeV in mSUGRA scenarios. No excess was observed at either machine.

### 5.2 LHC Run 1 and Run 2

The LHC program transformed the landscape. The ATLAS collaboration's comprehensive Run 2 summary, using up to 140 fb$^{-1}$ of 13 TeV proton–proton collisions, reports no significant excess over SM expectations across jets plus missing energy, same-sign dileptons, multileptons, photons, heavy flavor, and long-lived-particle signatures [4]. Representative 95% confidence-level exclusion limits from simplified models are compiled below [4][5][6]:

| Sparticle | Simplified-model assumption | Approx. exclusion (95% CL) |
|---|---|---|
| Gluino $\tilde{g}$ | $\tilde{g} \to q\bar{q}\tilde{\chi}^0_1$, massless LSP | $\sim 2.3$–$2.4$ TeV |
| Light-flavor squarks $\tilde{q}$ | Eightfold-degenerate, $\tilde{q} \to q\tilde{\chi}^0_1$ | $\sim 1.8$–$2.0$ TeV |
| Stop $\tilde{t}_1$ | $\tilde{t}_1 \to t\tilde{\chi}^0_1$, massless LSP | $\sim 1.2$–$1.3$ TeV |
| Sbottom $\tilde{b}_1$ | $\tilde{b}_1 \to b\tilde{\chi}^0_1$, massless LSP | $\sim 1.2$–$1.3$ TeV |
| Electroweakinos $\tilde{\chi}^\pm_1\tilde{\chi}^0_2$ | Via WZ, massless LSP | $\sim 0.6$–$1.0$ TeV |
| Sleptons $\tilde{\ell}$ | Direct pair production, massless LSP | $\sim 0.5$–$0.7$ TeV |
| Gluino (R-hadron, long-lived) | Stable charged massive particle | $\sim 1.9$–$2.3$ TeV |
| Electroweakinos (compressed) | Small mass splittings, soft leptons | $\sim 0.2$–$0.4$ TeV |

### 5.3 Interpretation: what the null results prove — and do not prove

The LHC null results prove that *minimal, natural, R-parity-conserving supersymmetry with a light spectrum and simple decays* is excluded. They do not exclude supersymmetry as a principle: compressed spectra, R-parity violation, long-lived sparticles, and heavy-scalar scenarios like split SUSY [8] remain viable and in some cases barely probed. The PDG's comprehensive review documents hundreds of individual analyses and the steady expansion of excluded regions with luminosity [5]. The lesson of the LHC era is not that supersymmetry is dead, but that if it exists, it is heavier, more compressed, or more baroque than the pre-LHC paradigm assumed.

## 6 Limitations

Supersymmetry's difficulties must be stated plainly. **First, the fine-tuning problem has returned in new form:** the LHC limits push stops and gluinos into the multi-TeV regime, implying percent-level (or worse) tuning of the electroweak scale in the MSSM — the very tuning SUSY was invented to eliminate [6]. **Second, the flavor and CP problems:** generic soft terms generate excessive flavor-changing neutral currents and electric dipole moments; viable models require unexplained flavor universality or alignment. **Third, the $\mu$ problem:** the superpotential parameter $\mu$ must lie at the weak scale for correct electroweak breaking, yet nothing in the MSSM explains why. **Fourth, proton decay:** dimension-five operators in SUSY GUTs threaten the proton lifetime unless the colored Higgsino sector is carefully arranged. **Fifth, cosmological gravitino and moduli problems:** in gravity mediation, late-decaying gravitinos can spoil Big Bang nucleosynthesis. **Sixth, and most damning empirically:** despite half a century of theoretical development and an enormous experimental program, no superpartner has been observed [4][5].

Defenders respond that these are problems of *specific models*, not of the symmetry principle. Critics respond that a theory whose central prediction has been falsified in its minimal form is losing its claim to be the unique answer. The adjudicator must be experiment.

---

## 7 Conclusion

Supersymmetry remains the deepest structural idea in particle physics beyond the Standard Model: the Haag–Łopuszański–Sohnius theorem [2] guarantees that it is the *only* consistent graded extension of spacetime symmetry, and its consequences — the cancellation of quadratic divergences, the non-renormalization theorems, gauge-coupling unification, and a natural dark matter candidate — are too striking to be accidents. The MSSM and its mediation variants [1][7][8] provide a complete, calculable framework tested with extraordinary thoroughness at LEP, the Tevatron, and the LHC [4][5].

The experimental verdict so far is a null result that has reshaped the field: simple, natural, weak-scale supersymmetry is excluded, and attention has shifted to compressed spectra, R-parity violation, long-lived signatures, split spectra, and non-minimal models [6][8]. Whether supersymmetry is realized in nature will be decided by the High-Luminosity LHC — which will extend gluino sensitivity toward 3 TeV — and ultimately by a future 100 TeV hadron collider. If superpartners are found, the super-Poincaré algebra will be recognized as the completion of Einstein's spacetime symmetries; if not, the theorem will stand as a monument to the difference between mathematical beauty and physical truth.
