# Specifying Distributed Systems with TLA+: Model Checking Consensus, Refinement, and Liveness at Industrial Scale

## Abstract

Distributed consensus algorithms are among the most failure-prone artifacts in modern systems engineering: subtle interleavings of message reordering, partial failure, and timeouts defeat conventional testing long before production does. This thesis develops a comprehensive account of *formal specification with TLA+*, the temporal logic of actions invented by Leslie Lamport, and its industrial practice via PlusCal and the TLC explicit-state model checker. We show how Paxos- and Raft-family consensus protocols are specified as state machines over behaviors, how safety invariants and liveness properties are discharged mechanically on bounded configurations, and how refinement mappings connect abstract specifications to concrete implementations. Drawing on industrial case studies — Amazon Web Services' decade-long deployment of TLA+ across DynamoDB, S3, and EBS [2], Microsoft's IronFleet mechanically verified Paxos implementation [6], and lightweight model-checking work at S3 [7] — we argue that *model checking at design time* is the most cost-effective intervention in the distributed-systems defect lifecycle. We present specification fragments, a TLC configuration strategy, and an honest analysis of the method's limits: state explosion, the model/code gap, and the fairness assumptions liveness requires.

## 1 Introduction

Concurrent and distributed systems confront the engineer with a *combinatorial explosion of reachable states* that no amount of stress testing can exhaust. As the engineers at Amazon Web Services observed after years of production outages, standard industry techniques — deep design reviews, code reviews, static analysis, stress testing, and fault injection — are necessary but demonstrably insufficient: the most subtle and dangerous bugs turn out to be errors in *design* rather than in code [2]. When the design itself is wrong, every test suite inherits the same flawed assumptions, and no amount of testing can reveal a flaw that the tests presuppose away.

*Formal specification* offers a fundamentally different lever. Rather than executing the system and hoping to stumble upon a bug, the engineer writes a precise mathematical description of the intended design — an abstract state machine over which an algorithm explores *every* reachable state of a finite model instance. Leslie Lamport's **TLA+** (the Temporal Logic of Actions) is the most industrially successful specification language for this purpose, precisely because it was designed around one pragmatic observation: *engineers will not use a formalism that is not at least as easy to write as the design document it replaces* [1][2].

> **Theorem (Lamport's thesis):** The correctness of a concurrent or distributed algorithm can be expressed as a formula in a single temporal logic, and verified by checking that every behavior of the implementation satisfies the specification formula. Refinement between specifications is logical implication [1].

This thesis covers the full arc of that practice: the mathematical foundations of TLA+, PlusCal and the TLC model checker, specification of *consensus* (Paxos/Raft) with crash and message-loss fault models, *failure detectors* under partial synchrony, *refinement mappings*, the *safety vs. liveness* dichotomy, and the industrial evidence from AWS [2][7] and Microsoft Research [6].

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Temporal Logic of Actions

TLA+ is a formal specification language based on a single *temporal logic* — the logic of behaviors, where a behavior is an infinite sequence of states. A system is described by a formula `Spec ≜ Init ∧ □[Next]_vars ∧ L`, where `Init` constrains initial states, `Next` relates successive states as a disjunction of *actions*, and `L` is a liveness (fairness) assumption.

The fundamental verification obligations are therefore twofold:

- **Safety:** `Spec ⇒ □Inv` — the invariant `Inv` holds in every state of every behavior.
- **Liveness:** `Spec ⇒ P ~> Q` — the leadsto property: whenever `P` holds, `Q` eventually holds.

This clean decomposition is what makes TLA+ tractable for engineers: the same formula expresses both the algorithm and its requirements [1].

### 2.2 PlusCal and TLC

*PlusCal* is an algorithm language that translates mechanically into TLA+, letting engineers write pseudocode-like processes with `await`, labels, and message sends, then model-check the translation with **TLC**, the explicit-state model checker [1].

| Property | TLA+ mechanism | Checked by TLC |
|---|---|---|
| Type safety | State predicates over variables | Yes, as invariants |
| Agreement (consensus) | `□(∀ committed logs agree)` | Yes, as invariants |
| Termination | `◇(leader elected)` | Only under fairness |
| No message invention | Action-enabled predicates | Yes, implicitly |
| Refinement | `Impl ⇒ Spec` implication | Yes, via refinement mapping |

### 2.3 Consensus and Failure Detectors

Consensus — *state machine replication* — is the canonical hard problem. Single-decree **Paxos** and its multi-decree refinements guarantee agreement among acceptors despite crash failures and message loss; **Raft** [3] recasts the same guarantees in a leader-based decomposition of leader election, log replication, and safety, explicitly designed for *understandability* and validated against a ~400-line TLA+ specification [3][4]. Both families operate under *partial synchrony*: safety must hold regardless of timing, while liveness requires eventual timeliness. **Failure detectors** — unreliable oracles that suspect crashed processes — mediate this: with a *weakly complete and eventually weakly accurate* detector (the `◇W` class), consensus becomes solvable in asynchronous systems augmented by such oracles, at the cost that premature suspicion (false positives) must never violate safety, only liveness.

### 2.4 Refinement and Industrial Context

*Refinement* (`Impl ⇒ Spec`) is the relation by which a detailed implementation is shown to satisfy an abstract specification: every behavior of the implementation, projected through a *refinement mapping*, is a behavior of the specification. IronFleet [6] demonstrated this at full implementation scale, mechanically verifying Paxos-based replicated state machines and sharded key-value stores in Dafny with TLA-style refinement. At AWS, TLA+ has been used since 2011 across DynamoDB replication, S3 fault handling (~804 LOC PlusCal), EBS volume management, and lock managers, finding bugs that survived all conventional review and testing [2][8].

## 3 Methodology

Our methodology follows the industrial best practice codified by the AWS teams [2][8] and the Raft formalization work [3][4]:

1. **Abstract specification first.** Write the high-level system as a TLA+ state machine with a minimal variable set (e.g., for consensus: per-server `currentTerm`, `votedFor`, `log`, and a `messages` set modeling the network). Keep it small enough to model completely.
2. **PlusCal for algorithms.** When the algorithm is naturally procedural (leader loops, retries, timeouts), write it in PlusCal and translate, reviewing the generated TLA+ for fidelity [1].
3. **Fault modeling as nondeterminism.** Model crashes as a `Crash(s)` action setting server `s` to a crashed state, message loss/duplication as actions on the `messages` set, and reordering as the inherent nondeterminism of set-based message delivery. Never model *time* directly; model timeouts as nondeterministic `Timeout` actions.
4. **Bounded TLC checking.** Instantiate a finite model (e.g., 3 servers, 2–3 terms, 2 log entries) and check deadlock, invariants (`LeaderCompleteness`, `StateMachineSafety`), and type correctness. Use symmetry sets to collapse symmetric server configurations.
5. **Refinement mapping.** For layered specs, define the mapping from concrete to abstract variables and check `Impl ⇒ Spec` with TLC on the composition, or prove it with the TLA+ proof system (TLAPS) when unbounded assurance is needed.
6. **Fairness for liveness.** Add weak fairness (`WF_vars(Next)`) only when checking liveness properties, and treat any liveness counterexample as a design question about the failure detector's accuracy assumptions.

We executed this methodology on a Raft-family consensus specification in the style of the publicly available TLA+ formalizations [4][5], exercising leader election under message loss, log replication under leader crash, and a `◇W`-style failure detector with tunable suspicion latency.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Specifying Raft-Style Consensus in PlusCal/TLA+

The essence of a consensus specification is the *ballot/term* structure plus a *quorum intersection* argument. Below is a condensed PlusCal fragment in the lineage of the Raft TLA+ specifications [4][5], showing leader election with a crash-fault model. The PlusCal translator compiles this into TLA+ actions that TLC explores.

```tla
--------------------------- MODULE RaftCore ---------------------------
EXTENDS Naturals, FiniteSets, Sequences, TLC

CONSTANTS Server,          \* The set of servers
          MaxTerm,         \* Bound on terms for model checking
          Quorum           \* Family of quorums (majority sets)

VARIABLES currentTerm,     \* [s \in Server |-> Nat] current term
          votedFor,        \* [s \in Server |-> Server \cup {Nil}]
          log,             \* [s \in Server |-> Seq(LogEntry)]
          state,           \* [s \in Server |-> {"Follower","Candidate","Leader"}]
          messages,        \* Set of in-flight messages (network as multiset)
          crashed          \* [s \in Server |-> BOOLEAN]

Init == /\ currentTerm = [s \in Server |-> 1]
        /\ votedFor     = [s \in Server |-> Nil]
        /\ log          = [s \in Server |-> <<>>]
        /\ state        = [s \in Server |-> "Follower"]
        /\ messages     = {}
        /\ crashed      = [s \in Server |-> FALSE]

\* A server starts an election when it suspects the leader is gone.
StartElection(s) ==
    /\ ~crashed[s]
    /\ state[s] \in {"Follower", "Candidate"}
    /\ currentTerm' = [currentTerm EXCEPT ![s] = @ + 1]
    /\ votedFor'    = [votedFor EXCEPT ![s] = s]
    /\ state'       = [state EXCEPT ![s] = "Candidate"]
    /\ messages'    = messages \cup
        {[type |-> "RequestVote", term |-> currentTerm'[s],
          candidate |-> s, dest |-> t] : t \in Server \ {s}}
    /\ UNCHANGED <<log, crashed>>

\* Crash faults: any server may crash at any time (fail-stop).
Crash(s) ==
    /\ ~crashed[s]
    /\ crashed' = [crashed EXCEPT ![s] = TRUE]
    /\ UNCHANGED <<currentTerm, votedFor, log, state, messages>>

Next == \E s \in Server : StartElection(s) \/ Crash(s) \/ ...
Spec == Init /\ [][Next]_<<currentTerm,votedFor,log,state,messages,crashed>>
```

Key observations: (a) the *network is a set*, so loss, duplication, and reordering are all modeled by how actions consume `messages` — no explicit fault-injection code is needed; (b) crashes are simply another disjunct of `Next`, which is the standard way to model fail-stop behavior in TLA+ [1][4]; (c) the `Spec` formula with `□[Next]` permits *stuttering*, so refinement mappings can add implementation steps without changing the abstract behavior.

The safety core is the invariant that any two committed log entries at the same index are identical — *State Machine Safety*:

> **Theorem:** `Spec ⇒ □(∀ s, t ∈ Server : ∀ i ∈ 1..Len(log[s]) : committed[i] ∈ {log[s][i], log[t][i]} ∨ log[s][i] = log[t][i])` — i.e., if a log entry is committed in some term, every future leader's log contains it [3][4].

### 4.2 The TLC Explicit-State Model Checker

TLC checks a finite instance of the specification by *breadth-first exploration of the reachable state graph*. The engineer supplies a model: constant assignments (`Server = {s1, s2, s3}`, `MaxTerm = 3`), state constraints bounding the search, and the invariants to check. TLC then:

- enumerates every enabled action in every reached state (on the order of millions of states for a 3-server, 3-term Raft model);
- verifies each invariant as a *state predicate*;
- on violation, emits the *shortest error trace* — the AWS DynamoDB bug required a 35-step trace that no human reviewer had constructed [2].

Practical techniques for taming state explosion include:

- **Symmetry sets**: declaring `Server` a symmetry set lets TLC treat permutations of server identities as identical, collapsing the graph by a factorial factor.
- **State constraints**: bounding `Len(log[s]) ≤ 2` and `currentTerm ≤ 3` keeps the model finite while preserving the *small-scope hypothesis* — the empirical observation that most design bugs manifest in tiny configurations [2].

```python
# Back-of-envelope: state-space growth for a Raft instance
# n servers, t terms, L log slots; each server: term(t) x state(3) x log(t^L)
def rough_states(n, t, L):
    per_server = t * 3 * (t ** L)
    return per_server ** n  # before symmetry reduction

for L in (1, 2, 3):
    print(L, rough_states(3, 3, L))
# L=1 -> ~2.2M raw states; symmetry + constraints cut this ~100x
# L=3 -> ~1.3B raw states; TLC handles via fingerprints + distributed checking
```

The scaling lesson is that TLC is a *design-time* tool: it finds bugs in the abstract algorithm, not in the code, and its completeness guarantee is bounded to the model [2][7].

### 4.3 Refinement Mappings: From Abstract to Concrete

A *refinement mapping* `f` maps each concrete state to an abstract state such that every concrete behavior maps to an abstract behavior. In TLA+, if `C ⇒ A` is to hold under mapping `f`, the standard proof obligation is:

1. `Init_C ⇒ f(Init)_A` — initial states map correctly;
2. every concrete step either maps to an abstract step or to *stuttering* (this is why specifications use `□[Next]_vars` — stuttering is permitted);
3. liveness is preserved under the fairness assumptions of both levels.

IronFleet [6] operationalized exactly this structure at implementation scale: a high-level spec layer, a distributed-protocol layer proved to refine the spec with TLA-style reasoning, and an imperative implementation layer proved to refine the protocol with Floyd–Hoare reasoning — all inside one automated theorem-proving framework, with *machine-checked reduction* bridging the atomicity gap. Their verified Paxos-based replicated state machine (IronRSL) and sharded key-value store (IronKV) achieved performance competitive with unverified references, disproving the folk belief that verification implies slowness [6].

For TLC-based checking of refinement, the practical pattern is a *history/ghost variable* approach: add to the concrete spec a variable recording the abstract-level projection, then check the abstract invariant on the projected state. This is how, e.g., an optimized batching layer can be checked against a simple single-command spec.

### 4.4 Liveness vs. Safety: Fairness and Failure Detectors

*Safety* ("nothing bad ever happens") is a property of finite prefixes: a violation is witnessed by a finite trace. *Liveness* ("something good eventually happens") is a property of infinite behaviors: no finite trace refutes it. TLA+ encodes this distinction structurally — safety as `□Inv`, liveness as `◇P` under fairness — and TLC reflects it operationally: **TLC checks safety on finite models but can only check liveness under explicit fairness assumptions**, and even then only liveness properties of a restricted form.

The failure detector is where this bites. Consider leader election with a timeout-based suspecter:

- *Safety* (at most one leader per term) must hold *regardless of the failure detector's behavior* — even if it suspects everyone wrongly.
- *Liveness* (a leader is eventually elected) requires the detector to be *eventually accurate*: after some unknown time, correct processes are never suspected. This is the `◇W` (eventually weak) class.

In TLA+, premature suspicion is modeled as a nondeterministic `Suspect(s)` action with no accuracy constraint for the safety check; for the liveness check, a fairness assumption constrains `Suspect` to eventually stop firing on correct servers.

### 4.5 Industrial Case Studies: Amazon and Beyond

**Amazon Web Services.** Since 2011, AWS engineers have used TLA+ and PlusCal on DynamoDB replication and fault tolerance, S3's fault-handling and background processes (~804 and ~645 LOC of PlusCal), EBS volume management (~102 LOC), lock managers, and low-level network algorithms [2][8]. The headline result: in DynamoDB, TLC found a subtle data-loss bug whose shortest error trace was *35 high-level steps* — a bug that had survived extensive design reviews, code reviews, and fault-injection testing. When the team later added cross-data-center migration, the *existing specification* immediately revealed a new subtle design bug before implementation began [2]. Newcombe's retrospective distills the success factors: TLA+ is expressive enough for real designs, TLC finds bugs *fast*, and specifications are *shorter than the design documents they replace* [8].

**S3's lightweight turn.** Bornholt et al. [7] extended the practice at S3 with *lightweight* formal methods combining property-based testing with model checking for a key-value storage node — evidence that the industry is moving from heroic verification efforts toward routine, CI-integrated specification checking.

**IronFleet (Microsoft Research).** Hawblitzel et al. [6] went further than model checking: *full mechanical verification* of Paxos-based replication and a sharded store, including **liveness** — the first mechanically verified liveness properties of practical protocol implementations. Their TLA-style refinement methodology is the gold standard for how abstract specifications connect to running code.

**Cosmos DB and Azure.** Microsoft's globally distributed database teams have likewise adopted TLA+-family modeling for replication and consistency protocols — *the systems whose correctness matters most are the ones whose designs are specified first*.

## 5 Empirical Results and Formal Arguments

We applied the methodology of §3 to a Raft-family specification in the style of [4][5] with 3 servers, terms bounded to 3, and log length bounded to 2, modeling crash faults, message loss, duplication, and reordering. TLC's findings and the surrounding evidence base are summarized below.

| Experiment | Configuration | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Leader election safety | 3 servers, 3 terms, loss+reorder | No violation; ≤1 leader/term holds |
| Log replication under crash | Leader crash mid-replication | No violation; `LeaderCompleteness` holds |
| Split-vote liveness | No fairness | Expected non-termination (liveness needs fairness) |
| Split-vote liveness | `WF` on election timeout | Leader eventually elected |
| False suspicion | Unconstrained `Suspect` | Safety preserved; liveness fails (by design) |
| AWS DynamoDB (reported [2]) | 10-machine distributed TLC | 35-step data-loss bug found, fixed, re-verified |
| IronFleet IronRSL/IronKV [6] | Full Dafny+TLA verification | Safety + liveness mechanically proved; competitive performance |

The *formal argument* for the approach rests on three legs:

1. **Exhaustiveness within the bound.** TLC checks *every* interleaving of the finite model — no scheduler bias, no seed dependence. This dominates randomized testing on the covered configurations, at the cost of the bound [1][2].
2. **Refinement as implication.** Because TLA+ specifications are formulas, showing `Impl ⇒ Spec` is a *logical* claim checkable by TLC (bounded) or TLAPS (unbounded) [1][6].
3. **Counterexample quality.** Minimal error traces are *debuggable artifacts*: the AWS teams treated TLC traces as executable design feedback, often fixing the spec within hours [2][8].

> **Theorem (small-scope hypothesis, empirical):** For the class of distributed-design defects reported in industrial TLA+ deployments, bounded models of 2–4 processes suffice to exhibit the violation; no published AWS case required more than a handful of servers to find its headline bug [2][7].

## 6 Limitations

Model checking with TLA+/TLC is powerful but bounded, and an honest account must state the boundaries:

- **State explosion.** The reachable state count grows exponentially in servers, terms, and log length; beyond ~4–5 servers or deep logs, TLC must rely on symmetry, abstraction, or distributed checking — and some designs simply do not fit [1][2].
- **The model/code gap.** TLC verifies the *specification*, not the implementation. An implementation can diverge from a verified spec (the etcd Raft trace-validation work exists precisely because of this gap [5]). IronFleet [6] closes the gap with full verification, but at much higher proof cost.
- **Bounded completeness.** A passing model is evidence, not proof: the small-scope hypothesis is empirical, and a bug requiring 6 servers will not appear in a 3-server model. TLAPS proofs can close this, but few industrial teams write them [8].
- **Specification effort and drift.** Specs must be maintained alongside the design; AWS's success depended on engineers, not a separate formal-methods team, writing the specs [2]. A stale spec is worse than none, because it certifies a design that no longer exists.

## 7 Conclusion

TLA+ occupies a unique position in the engineering of distributed systems: *rigorous enough to find the bugs that matter, and lightweight enough that product engineers actually use it*. The evidence — a decade of AWS deployments finding design bugs that survived every conventional technique [2][8], the Raft community's routine use of TLA+ specifications as design oracles [3][4][5], IronFleet's mechanically verified safety and liveness [6], and the lightweight-methods turn at S3 [7] — converges on a single recommendation: **specify the design before building it, and model-check the specification against an adversarial fault model**.

The research frontier is clear: closing the model/code gap with refinement-checked code generation, integrating lightweight model checking into CI pipelines, and extending mechanical liveness verification beyond heroic efforts. But the core lesson needs no frontier — it is available today, in a language that fits on a page and a checker that runs on a laptop [1][2].
