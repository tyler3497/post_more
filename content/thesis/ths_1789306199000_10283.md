# Superposition Revisited: The Kolmogorov–Arnold Representation Theorem, Learnable Spline Networks, and Neural Operators for High-Dimensional Problems

## Abstract

In 1957, Andrey Kolmogorov proved that every continuous function of *n* variables admits an exact representation as a superposition of continuous functions of a single variable — a result that resolved the continuous form of Hilbert's thirteenth problem and stunned the mathematical world. For decades it remained a curiosity — its inner functions too pathological to compute — until Liu and colleagues revived it in 2024 as Kolmogorov–Arnold Networks (KANs), placing learnable univariate spline activations on network edges rather than fixed activations on nodes. This thesis develops a unified account of superposition-based learning: the classical theorem and its refinements, the precise reasons the original form is unusable for machine learning, the spline-parameterized KAN construction and its scaling behavior relative to multilayer perceptrons, and the parallel development of neural operators — DeepONet and the Fourier Neural Operator — for learning maps between function spaces in high-dimensional partial differential equations. We argue that the theorem's true legacy is not a formula to be implemented literally, but a compositional inductive bias: high-dimensional structure is best attacked by learned one-dimensional primitives composed in depth.

## 1 The Ghost of Hilbert's Thirteenth

In 1900, David Hilbert asked whether the solution of the general seventh-degree equation could be expressed using only continuous functions of two variables. The question — the thirteenth in his famous list — encoded a deep intuition: that multivariate complexity might resist compression into low-dimensional primitives. For half a century, the answer appeared to be negative. Then, in 1957, the twenty-year-old Vladimir Arnold showed that every continuous function of three variables is representable by superpositions of continuous functions of two variables [2], and months later his mentor Andrey Kolmogorov demolished the problem entirely: **every** continuous function of *n* variables is a superposition of continuous functions of **one** variable and addition [1].

The result was, in Kolmogorov's own understated phrasing, "unexpected." It implies that the apparent complexity of high-dimensional continuous functions is, in a precise topological sense, illusory: all the multivariate structure can be folded into *1 + n(2n+1)* carefully chosen univariate functions. Yet the theorem slept. No numerical analyst of the 1960s could compute with it; the inner functions it guarantees are monstrous — nowhere differentiable, fractal objects whose construction proceeds by an intricate limiting argument over partitions of the cube. When neural networks rose in the 1980s, Robert Hecht-Nielsen reformulated the theorem as a three-layer network existence result [3], and Federico Girosi and Tomaso Poggio promptly declared the whole enterprise irrelevant to learning [4].

This thesis argues that the obituaries were premature but the skeptics had a point. The original theorem cannot be used directly — and understanding *why* is the key to everything that followed. The modern revival, through Kolmogorov–Arnold Networks [5, 6] and the parallel theory of neural operators [7, 8], succeeds precisely by abandoning the literal formula and retaining its spirit: **learn smooth univariate primitives, compose them deeply, and let optimization discover the superposition the theorem only promises to exist.**

## 2 The Representation Theorem and Its Discontents

### 2.1 The theorem

> **Theorem (Kolmogorov, 1957):** Let *f* be continuous on the *n*-dimensional unit cube. Then there exist continuous univariate functions *φ_{q,p}* (unit interval to the reals) and *Φ_q : ℝ → ℝ* such that
>
> *f(x₁, …, xₙ) = Σ_{q=0}^{2n} Φ_q( Σ_{p=1}^{n} φ_{q,p}(x_p) ).*
>
> The representation uses exactly *2n+1* outer functions and *n(2n+1)* inner functions — a shallow, fixed-size decomposition of any continuous multivariate map [1].

The proof is non-constructive in the practical sense: the inner functions *φ_{q,p}* are built by a delicate limiting procedure over increasingly fine partitions of the cube, and they inherit none of the regularity of *f*. Subsequent refinements sharpened the form without taming it: Lorentz collapsed the outer functions to a **single** function composed with the inner sums, while Vitushkin and Khenkin mapped the fine structure of admissible superpositions [2].

### 2.2 Why the theorem resisted computation

Four intertwined pathologies block any literal implementation:

1. **Fractal inner functions.** The *φ_{q,p}* guaranteed by the proof are typically nowhere differentiable, with Hölder regularity decaying as dimension grows. No finite basis — splines, polynomials, wavelets — approximates them at a useful rate.
2. **No smoothness inheritance.** A *C^∞* target *f* may require inner functions of essentially zero regularity. The decomposition actively *destroys* the structure that numerical methods exploit.
3. **Shallowness and rigidity.** The two-layer, *2n+1*-term form admits no depth, no width tuning, and no adaptation to the function's actual compositional structure.
4. **Non-identifiability.** The representing functions are wildly non-unique and unstable: microscopic perturbations of *f* can force macroscopic changes in the *φ_{q,p}*, making them unlearnable from finite data by any stable procedure.

Girosi and Poggio's 1989 critique [4] made the machine-learning case explicit: even granted existence, the representation offers no approximation scheme, no generalization control, and no training algorithm. Hecht-Nielsen's network reformulation [3] — a three-layer network with *2n+1* hidden units and exotic activations — changed the language but not the substance. The theorem was, for learning purposes, a beautiful dead end.

| Framework | Object approximated | Guarantee | Constructive? | Learnable? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Kolmogorov–Arnold (1957) | Continuous *f* on the unit cube | Exact, finite superposition | No | No |
| Cybenko UAT (1989) | Continuous *f* on compacts | Arbitrary accuracy, one hidden layer | No | In principle |
| Chen & Chen operator UAT (1993) | Continuous operators *G : U → V* | Arbitrary accuracy, shallow net | No | In principle |
| KAN (2024) | Smooth/compositional *f* | Empirical scaling laws | Yes (splines) | Yes |
| DeepONet / FNO | PDE solution operators | Universal approximation + benchmarks | Yes | Yes |

### 2.3 The lesson the skeptics missed

The critics were right about the formula and wrong about the moral. What the theorem really says is that **multivariate continuity has no intrinsic dimensionality**: all the action can be pushed into univariate functions composed with addition. The failure was assuming those univariate functions must be the pathological ones of the existence proof. If instead we *learn* smooth univariate functions from data — splines, say — and allow arbitrary depth, we keep the compositional inductive bias while discarding the pathology. That is exactly the move Liu et al. made [5].

## 3 Methodology: From Fixed Superposition to Learnable Splines

### 3.1 The KAN construction

A Kolmogorov–Arnold Network inverts the MLP convention. In an MLP, edges carry scalar weights and nodes apply fixed nonlinearities:

- MLP: *x_{l+1,j} = σ( Σᵢ w_{ji} x_{l,i} + b_j )*, with *σ* fixed (ReLU, tanh, …).

In a KAN, edges carry **learnable univariate functions** and nodes merely sum [5]:

- KAN: *x_{l+1,j} = Σᵢ φ_{l,j,i}( x_{l,i} )*, with each *φ* a trainable B-spline plus a residual basis function.

Concretely, each edge function is parameterized as

*φ(x) = w_b · b(x) + w_s · Σᵢ cᵢ Bᵢ(x)*,

where *b(x) = silu(x)* is a fixed residual basis, *Bᵢ* are B-splines of order *k* over a grid of *G* intervals, and the coefficients *cᵢ* are learned. The grid can be **refined** during training — coarse-to-fine fitting that adapts resolution to the function's local complexity. Sparsification penalties prune edges, and the surviving univariate functions can be symbolically regressed into closed-form expressions, yielding the interpretability that motivated the architecture [5, 6].

The following minimal implementation captures the core idea — a spline edge layer with a refinable grid:

```python
import numpy as np

class SplineEdge:
    """A learnable univariate function on a single KAN edge."""
    def __init__(self, grid_size=5, spline_order=3, x_min=-1.0, x_max=1.0):
        self.k, self.G = spline_order, grid_size
        self.knots = np.linspace(x_min, x_max, grid_size + 1)
        # coefficients for B-spline basis + residual weights
        self.c = np.random.randn(grid_size + spline_order) * 0.1
        self.wb, self.ws = 1.0, 1.0

    def _bspline_basis(self, x):
        """Cox-de Boor recursion evaluated on the knot vector."""
        x = np.atleast_1d(x)
        n = len(self.knots) - self.k - 1
        B = np.zeros((len(x), n + self.k))
        # order-0 indicators
        for i in range(n + self.k):
            lo = self.knots[min(i, len(self.knots)-1)]
            hi = self.knots[min(i+1, len(self.knots)-1)]
            B[:, i] = ((x >= lo) & (x < hi)).astype(float)
        # elevate
        for p in range(1, self.k + 1):
            Bnew = np.zeros_like(B)
            for i in range(B.shape[1] - p):
                d1 = self.knots[min(i+p, len(self.knots)-1)] - self.knots[i]
                d2 = self.knots[min(i+p+1, len(self.knots)-1)] - self.knots[i+1]
                t1 = (x - self.knots[i]) / d1 if d1 > 0 else 0.0
                t2 = (self.knots[min(i+p+1, len(self.knots)-1)] - x) / d2 if d2 > 0 else 0.0
                Bnew[:, i] = t1 * B[:, i] + t2 * B[:, i+1]
            B = Bnew
        return B[:, :len(self.c)]

    def __call__(self, x):
        basis = 1.0 / (1.0 + np.exp(-x)) * x   # silu residual basis
        return self.wb * basis + self.ws * (self._bspline_basis(x) @ self.c)

    def refine_grid(self, factor=2):
        """Double grid resolution, interpolating coefficients (multigrid-style)."""
        old_knots = self.knots
        self.knots = np.linspace(old_knots[0], old_knots[-1],
                                 (len(old_knots)-1) * factor + 1)
        self.G *= factor
        self.c = np.interp(
            np.linspace(0, 1, len(self.knots) + self.k - 1),
            np.linspace(0, 1, len(self.c)), self.c)

class KANLayer:
    """One KAN layer: every input-output pair gets its own SplineEdge."""
    def __init__(self, n_in, n_out, grid_size=5):
        self.edges = [[SplineEdge(grid_size) for _ in range(n_in)]
                      for _ in range(n_out)]
    def forward(self, x):
        # x: (n_in,) vector -> (n_out,) vector; nodes sum, edges transform
        return np.array([sum(e(xi) for e, xi in zip(row, x))
                         for row in self.edges])
```

The `refine_grid` method is the algorithmic heart of the KAN training story: start coarse, fit, refine, and continue — so that spline knots concentrate where the target function demands them [5].

### 3.2 Training, sparsification, and symbolification

KANs are trained with standard gradient methods plus an *ℓ₁*-style penalty on edge-function magnitudes, driving the network toward sparse superpositions. After training, each surviving *φ* is a smooth univariate curve that can be **plotted and inspected** — a qualitative leap over MLP weight matrices. Liu et al. then fit symbolic expressions (sin, exp, polynomials) to these curves, recovering closed-form laws from data in physics and mathematics benchmarks [5]. The KAN 2.0 extension introduces multiplication nodes (MultKAN) and tighter science integration, extending the framework beyond pure addition-of-univariates [6].

## 4 Deep Dive: Scaling, Structure, and Operators

### 4.1 Splines, grids, and approximation rates

The choice of B-splines is not cosmetic. For a univariate *C^m* function, order-*k* splines on a grid of size *G* achieve approximation error *O(G^{-min(k+1, m)})* — the classical optimal rate — whereas a ReLU MLP's piecewise-linear pieces must be *learned into position* by moving kink hyperplanes through high-dimensional space. Because each KAN edge is intrinsically one-dimensional, grid refinement acts locally and predictably: doubling the grid quarters the error for smooth targets, independent of the ambient dimension *n*. This is the precise sense in which KANs dodge the curse of dimensionality — not for arbitrary continuous functions (for which no method can), but for functions whose complexity lives in low-dimensional or compositional structure.

### 4.2 Compositional structure and spectral bias

Many functions of scientific interest are *compositional*: *f(x) = h(g₁(x), …, g_m(x))* with each *gᵢ* depending on few variables. MLPs with fixed activations struggle with such structure because of **spectral bias** — the well-documented tendency of gradient-trained networks to fit low-frequency components first, requiring exponentially many neurons to resolve high-frequency or sharply compositional features. KANs attack this from two sides. First, splines are *local* basis functions: a high-frequency wiggle in one region does not perturb the fit elsewhere, unlike global sinusoidal or polynomial features. Second, depth in a KAN composes univariate functions directly, so a compositional target maps onto a compositional architecture with parameter counts scaling in the *intrinsic* rather than ambient dimension. Liu et al. report neural scaling laws of the form *test loss ∝ N^{-α}* with steeper *α* for KANs than MLPs on structured tasks [5] — exactly the signature of an architecture matched to its problem class.

The compositional viewpoint can be stated as a typed program, which also clarifies why depth helps:

```haskell
-- A KAN as a typed composition of univariate edges (sketch)
type EdgeFunc = Double -> Double          -- one learned spline phi
type KANLayer = [[EdgeFunc]]              -- edges, not weights
type KAN      = [KANLayer]

forward :: KAN -> [Double] -> [Double]
forward []     xs = xs
forward (l:ls) xs = forward ls [ sum [ e x | (e, x) <- zip row xs ] | row <- l ]

-- Classical K-A theorem: exactly two such layers, 2n+1 middle units.
-- KAN generalization: arbitrary depth, learned smooth edges.
```

### 4.3 KAN vs MLP: a controlled comparison

To make the comparison concrete, consider fitting *f(x, y) = exp(sin(πx) + y²)* on the square *[-1,1]×[-1,1]* — smooth, compositional, and exactly the kind of target where spline edges should shine. A compact experiment (numpy, Adam, identical parameter budgets) illustrates the methodology used in the literature:

```python
import numpy as np

def target(x, y):
    return np.exp(np.sin(np.pi * x) + y ** 2)

# --- MLP baseline: fixed tanh on nodes, linear weights on edges ---
def mlp_forward(params, X):
    W1, b1, W2, b2 = params
    H = np.tanh(X @ W1 + b1)
    return (H @ W2 + b2).ravel()

# --- KAN: learnable spline edges (SplineEdge from Section 3), sums on nodes ---
kan = KANLayer(n_in=2, n_out=1, grid_size=8)

# training loop sketch: MSE + L1 edge penalty, with grid refinement at epoch 500

#     adam_step(grads)

```

The reported pattern across such benchmarks [5] is consistent: at matched parameter counts, KANs reach lower test error on compositional targets and — critically — their learned edge functions *visualize* as recognizable sin/exp/parabola shapes, while the MLP's knowledge remains distributed across inscrutable weight matrices. On unstructured or non-smooth targets the advantage reverses — see Section 6.

### 4.4 Neural operators: from functions to maps between function spaces

Solving a parametric PDE means learning not one function but an **operator** *G* mapping input functions (coefficients, boundary data, forcings) to solution functions. Chen and Chen's universal approximation theorem for operators showed shallow networks could do this in principle; DeepONet made it practical [7]. Its architecture is disarmingly simple and directly relevant to the superposition theme:

- The **branch net** encodes the input function *u* sampled at *m* fixed sensor points into coefficients *(b₁, …, b_p)*.
- The **trunk net** encodes the query coordinates *y* into basis functions *(t₁, …, t_p)*.
- The output is the inner product *G(u)(y) ≈ Σ_{k=1}^{p} b_k(u) · t_k(y)* — a learned, nonlinear generalization of a spectral expansion.

The Fourier Neural Operator takes a different route: it parameterizes the integral kernel of the operator **directly in Fourier space**, alternating pointwise nonlinearities with global convolutions implemented via FFT [8]. On Burgers' equation, Darcy flow, and Navier–Stokes, FNO demonstrated zero-shot super-resolution — training on coarse grids and evaluating on fine ones — and speedups of up to three orders of magnitude over classical solvers [8]. Where KANs decompose *functions* into univariate primitives, neural operators decompose *operators* into learned basis expansions; both are heirs of Kolmogorov's insight that infinite-dimensional objects admit finite structured representations.

### 4.5 Physics-informed learning in high dimensions

Physics-informed neural networks (PINNs) minimize PDE residuals at collocation points, but suffer spectral bias and optimization pathologies that worsen with dimension. Two remedies connect to our theme. **Physics-informed DeepONets** add the PDE residual to the operator loss, making the operator self-supervised — no precomputed solution data required — at the cost of harder training dynamics [7]. **KAN-based PDE solvers** replace MLP backbones with spline edges, exploiting the observation that PDE solutions are typically smooth and locally structured — precisely the regime where splines excel. The emerging division of labor: neural operators learn *families* of solutions across parameter space, while KAN-style primitives supply local approximation power inside each solve. Neither defeats the curse of dimensionality in full generality — Barron-space analysis shows the curse is fundamental for arbitrary functions — but both exploit the compositional and smooth structure that real physics provides.

## 5 Empirical Evidence and Theoretical Guarantees

The empirical case for KANs rests on three pillars reported by Liu et al. [5]:

1. **Accuracy at matched size.** Across regression tasks from mathematics and physics — including discovering symbolic relations like Kepler's third law from data — KANs achieve lower test RMSE than MLPs with comparable parameter counts, with the gap widening on compositional targets.
2. **Scaling exponents.** Fitted neural scaling laws show KAN test loss decaying faster with parameters than MLPs on structured problems, consistent with spline approximation theory beating the generic Barron rate when the target is compositional [5].
3. **Interpretability pipeline.** Sparsification followed by symbolic regression recovers human-readable formulas; the network becomes a hypothesis generator rather than a black box [5, 6].

For operators, the evidence is equally striking: DeepONet's 16-application study demonstrated a single architecture learning linear and nonlinear operators across dynamical systems and PDEs [7], while FNO's turbulent-flow results with zero-shot super-resolution advanced data-driven PDE surrogates [8].

> **Theorem (informal synthesis):** No finite smooth architecture can uniformly defeat the curse of dimensionality for arbitrary continuous functions — the pathological inner functions of the Kolmogorov–Arnold theorem are the obstruction made concrete. But restricted to compositional, smooth, or operator-structured classes, architectures whose primitives match the structure (learnable univariate splines; branch–trunk expansions; Fourier kernels) achieve rates governed by *intrinsic* rather than ambient dimension.

This is the precise sense in which the 1957 theorem finally pays off: not as an algorithm, but as a **design principle** — push complexity into learned low-dimensional primitives and compose them.

## 6 Limitations

Honesty requires stating where the revival strains.

1. **Training cost.** Spline evaluation is more expensive than matrix multiplication; KANs train slower per step than MLPs, and the advantage in parameter efficiency does not always translate to wall-clock efficiency [5].
2. **Smoothness dependence.** The entire construction assumes the target's structure is captured by *smooth* univariate functions. On non-smooth, high-frequency, or unstructured data — including several computer-vision benchmarks — KANs have been reported to generalize worse than standard architectures, and smoothness regularization becomes necessary.
3. **The pathology gap.** The original theorem's inner functions are unlearnable *in principle* from finite data; KANs do not implement the theorem but a smooth surrogate of it. There is no theorem guaranteeing that a smooth superposition exists for an arbitrary continuous target — indeed, the fractal constructions suggest the opposite.
4. **Operator data hunger.** DeepONet and FNO need large datasets of input–output function pairs (or expensive physics-informed training); their guarantees are approximation-theoretic, not statistical, and out-of-distribution generalization across function space remains poorly understood [7, 8].
5. **Optimization fragility.** Physics-informed variants of both KANs and DeepONets exhibit the stiff, multi-scale loss landscapes characteristic of PDE-constrained optimization, requiring careful weighting and curriculum strategies.

## 7 Conclusion

Seventy years after Kolmogorov's bombshell, the representation theorem has finally found its computational afterlife — not in the form its author proved, but in the architecture it inspired. The pathologies that made the original formula unusable — fractal inner functions, destroyed smoothness, rigid shallowness — are precisely what modern methods engineer around: KANs learn smooth spline edges instead of inheriting fractal ones [5, 6], and neural operators lift the superposition idea from functions to operators [7, 8]. The enduring lesson is compositional: high-dimensional learning succeeds when architectures mirror the low-dimensional structure of their targets.

Open problems abound. A statistical learning theory for KANs — generalization bounds in terms of spline grids and compositional depth — is nascent. Hybrid architectures embedding KAN edges inside neural operators, or operator-valued KANs, are largely unexplored. And the deepest question remains open: *which* function classes admit smooth Kolmogorov–Arnold-type decompositions at all? Answering it would turn a 1957 existence proof into a characterization of learnability itself.
