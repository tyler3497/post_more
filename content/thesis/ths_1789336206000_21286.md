# The Classification of Finite Simple Groups: The Monster, the Sporadic Groups, and the Revisionist Program

## Abstract

The Classification of Finite Simple Groups (CFSG) is the largest theorem in the history of pure mathematics: it asserts that every finite simple group is isomorphic to a cyclic group of prime order, an alternating group of degree at least five, a group of Lie type, or one of twenty-six sporadic groups, the largest of which is the Fischer–Griess Monster of order approximately eight times ten to the fifty-third [1][2]. The proof spans tens of thousands of pages across several hundred journal articles by roughly a hundred authors, published between 1955 and 2004, with its final gap — the quasithin case — closed by Aschbacher and Smith only in 2004 [8]. This article surveys the statement of the theorem and the architecture of its proof, the classical families and the sporadic constellation, the Monster's construction via the Griess algebra and its moonshine connection to modular functions, and the Gorenstein–Lyons–Solomon revisionist program that seeks to produce a coherent second-generation proof in a single multivolume series [9][10].

## 1 Introduction

There is a sense in which the integers are assembled from primes, and every theorem about integers eventually leans against the fundamental theorem of arithmetic. Finite group theory possesses an analogous atomic hypothesis: the Jordan–Hölder theorem guarantees that every finite group admits a composition series whose simple factors — the *composition factors* — are unique up to isomorphism and ordering. A group is *simple* when its only normal subgroups are itself and the trivial group, so the simple groups are the atoms of finite group theory. Which atoms exist is therefore the load-bearing question of the subject.

The Classification of Finite Simple Groups answers that question in a single, sweeping theorem [1][2]:

> **Theorem (Classification of Finite Simple Groups).** Every finite simple group is isomorphic to one of the following:
> 1. a cyclic group $C_p$ of prime order $p$;
> 2. an alternating group $A_n$ of degree $n \geq 5$;
> 3. a simple group of Lie type;
> 4. one of the twenty-six sporadic simple groups.

The statement is almost offensively brief. Its proof is the longest in the history of mathematics: an estimated ten to fifteen thousand pages of published argument, spread over some five hundred journal articles, written by more than a hundred authors, produced across five decades [8][10]. No single mathematician alive understands the whole proof; the person who came closest was Daniel Gorenstein, who died in 1992 and who had functioned as the coach of the entire enterprise [10].

The last three decades of the subject have therefore been dominated by a second-order project: not the classification itself, but its *revision*. The Gorenstein–Lyons–Solomon program — the *revisionist program* — aims to extract from the scattered original literature a single coherent, self-contained, simplified proof, published as a series of AMS monographs [9]. Gorenstein himself warned that if the proof were allowed to "become lost to the living world of mathematics, buried deep within the dusty pages of forgotten journals," the most monumental theorem ever proved might quietly cease to be understood at all [8].

This article surveys the theorem from its foundations to its frontiers: the three infinite families and the sporadic constellation they fail to contain; the Monster, its construction by Griess, and its uncanny relationship with the modular $j$-function known as *monstrous moonshine*; and the revisionist program that seeks to render the whole edifice comprehensible again.

## 2 Background

The dream of classifying the simple groups is nearly as old as group theory itself. In the nineteenth century, Émile Mathieu discovered, in the context of multiply transitive permutation groups, the first five *sporadic* simple groups — the Mathieu groups $M_{11}, M_{12}, M_{22}, M_{23}, M_{24}$ [10]. $M_{24}$ is the automorphism group of the extended binary Golay code.

Two developments turned a dream into a program. The first was Richard Brauer's address to the 1954 International Congress of Mathematicians, which laid out the strategy of *local analysis*: to understand a finite simple group, study the centralizers of its involutions and the $p$-local subgroups attached to them [8]. The second was the 1962–1963 theorem of Walter Feit and John Thompson that every group of odd order is solvable — a 255-page paper whose corollary is that every nonabelian finite simple group has even order and hence contains an involution [2].

When Gorenstein announced in 1983 that the classification was complete, he was premature: the proof of the *quasithin* case turned out to be flawed, and it took Aschbacher and Smith seven years and two further volumes to repair it. In 2004 Aschbacher could finally write that "the main theorem closes the last gap in the original proof, so (for the moment) the classification theorem can be regarded as a theorem" [8][10].

## 3 Methodology

The proof of the CFSG is not a proof in the ordinary sense of a single sustained argument; it is a *proof by exhaustive case analysis*, organized as a vast decision tree whose leaves are individual families of groups. Its methodology can be summarized in four interlocking strategies.

**Local analysis and involution centralizers.** By the Feit–Thompson theorem, every nonabelian finite simple group $G$ contains an involution $t$, and the structure of the centralizer $C_G(t)$ constrains $G$ severely. The *signalizer functor method* and the *$B$-conjecture* machinery reconstruct global structure from this local data [1][2].

**The trichotomy.** For a minimal counterexample to the classification — a nonabelian simple group not known to be on the list — one examines the *$2$-local* structure. The analysis divides into three regimes: groups of *characteristic $2$ type*, in which the generalized Fitting subgroup of every $2$-local is a $2$-group; groups of *odd type* or *component type*, in which some involution centralizer has a nonabelian simple component; and the *quasithin* boundary region, where the $2$-local subgroups are "thin" enough to resist the standard machinery. Each regime is then subdivided by the isomorphism type of the components appearing in involution centralizers [1][8].

**Generation and recognition.** In each case the strategy is to show that a minimal counterexample must contain a *standard component* — a quasisimple subgroup normalized but not centralized by an involution — and then to identify the ambient group via a *recognition theorem*: if $G$ contains subgroups isomorphic to those of a known simple group $K$ in the right configuration, then $G \cong K$. The Curtis–Tits theorem for groups of Lie type and the various *uniqueness theorems* for sporadics are the workhorses here [9].

**Computational verification.** Several steps of the modern proof lean on machine computation: the construction and uniqueness of the Monster depend on calculations in the 196884-dimensional Griess algebra now feasible only with specialized software such as the *mmgroup* package [3], and character tables and maximal subgroups of the sporadics are verified against the ATLAS of Finite Groups [5].

The content of the theorem is precisely that this case tree has no surviving branches: every branch terminates in a known family [1][9].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Classical Families: Cyclic, Alternating, and Lie Type

The cyclic groups of prime order are the abelian simple groups. The alternating groups $A_n$ for $n \geq 5$ are simple, and their maximal subgroups were described by O'Nan, Scott, Aschbacher, and Liebeck–Praeger–Saxl in the celebrated O'Nan–Scott theorem [2].

The bulk of the classification lies in the *groups of Lie type*: the finite analogues of the simple Lie groups, constructed from a root system $\Phi$ and a finite field $\mathbb{F}_q$ with $q = p^f$ elements.

| Family | Notation | Root system | Typical order |
|---|---|---|---|
| Projective special linear | $A_{n-1}(q) = PSL_n(q)$ | $A_{n-1}$ | $q^{n(n-1)/2} \prod_{i=2}^{n}(q^i - 1)/d$ |
| Projective special unitary | ${}^2A_{n-1}(q) = PSU_n(q)$ | $A_{n-1}$ (twisted) | $q^{n(n-1)/2} \prod_{i=2}^{n}(q^i - (-1)^i)/d$ |
| Symplectic | $C_n(q) = PSp_{2n}(q)$ | $C_n$ | $q^{n^2} \prod_{i=1}^{n}(q^{2i}-1)/d$ |
| Orthogonal (odd dim.) | $B_n(q)$ | $B_n$ | $q^{n^2} \prod_{i=1}^{n}(q^{2i}-1)/d$ |
| Orthogonal (even dim.) | $D_n(q), {}^2D_n(q)$ | $D_n$ | $q^{n(n-1)}(q^n \mp 1)\prod_{i=1}^{n-1}(q^{2i}-1)/d$ |
| Exceptional untwisted | $E_6(q), E_7(q), E_8(q), F_4(q), G_2(q)$ | $E_6, E_7, E_8, F_4, G_2$ | e.g. $\lvert E_8(q)\rvert = q^{120}\prod_{d}(q^d-1)$ |
| Exceptional twisted | ${}^2E_6(q), {}^3D_4(q)$ | $E_6, D_4$ | via twisted Weyl group orders |
| Suzuki / Ree | ${}^2B_2(q), {}^2G_2(q), {}^2F_4(q)$ | $B_2, G_2, F_4$ | $q$ an odd power of $2$ or $3$ |

Here $d$ is a small integer (a divisor of $n$ or similar) accounting for the center. Counting the twisted families separately, there are sixteen infinite families of groups of Lie type, plus the Tits group ${}^2F_4(2)'$ — the derived subgroup of the Ree group over the field of two elements — which is sometimes counted as a twenty-seventh sporadic group [1][2]. The order formulas are all instances of a single formula: $q^N \prod_i (q^{d_i} - 1)$ (with signs for twisted cases), where $N$ is the number of positive roots and the $d_i$ are the degrees of the fundamental invariants of the Weyl group.

### 4.2 The Sporadic Constellation: From Mathieu to Fischer

If the groups of Lie type are the expected citizens of the simple-group universe, the twenty-six *sporadic* groups are its anomalies: simple groups that belong to no infinite family and whose existence was, in several cases, predicted from numerical evidence before any construction existed. They fall into loose generations [2][8]:

- **The Mathieu groups** ($M_{11}, M_{12}, M_{22}, M_{23}, M_{24}$): discovered by Émile Mathieu in the 1860s as multiply transitive permutation groups; $M_{24}$ is the automorphism group of the extended binary Golay code and acts as the symmetry group of the Steiner system $S(5,8,24)$. Mathieu's groups were the only known sporadics for a century.
- **The Leech lattice groups** ($Co_1, Co_2, Co_3, HS, McL, Suz$): Conway's three groups are the automorphism groups of the Leech lattice $\Lambda_{24}$ and its stabilizers; the Higman–Sims, McLaughlin, and Suzuki groups arise as rank-three permutation groups related to the same geometry [8].
- **The Fischer groups** ($Fi_{22}, Fi_{23}, Fi_{24}'$) and the **Monster's court**: Fischer's groups are generated by *$3$-transpositions*; the Baby Monster $\mathbb{B}$ (constructed by Leon and Sims in 1977) is the centralizer-of-involution shadow of the Monster; the Thompson group $F_3$, the Harada–Norton group $HN$, and the Held group $He$ are further members of the Monster's paraclique.
- **The Janko groups** ($J_1, J_2, J_3, J_4$): Janko's $J_1$, found in 1965, was the first new sporadic in a century; $J_4$ was predicted from involution-centralizer analysis and constructed by computer — an early triumph of computational group theory. Griess dubbed the twenty sporadics that are subquotients of the Monster the *happy family* and the remaining six ($J_1, J_3, J_4, O'N, Ru, Ly$) the *pariahs* [6].

The O'Nan group $O'N$, one of the pariahs, has recently acquired its own moonshine: Duncan, Mertens, and Ono established arithmetic moonshine phenomena for $O'N$, and hence for its subgroup $J_1$, showing that even the groups exiled from the Monster's family participate in modular mysteries [7].

### 4.3 The Monster and the Griess Algebra

The largest sporadic group is the Fischer–Griess Monster $\mathbb{M}$, of order

$$\lvert \mathbb{M} \rvert = 808017424794512875886459904961710757005754368000000000 \approx 8 \times 10^{53},$$

which factors as $2^{46} \cdot 3^{20} \cdot 5^9 \cdot 7^6 \cdot 11^2 \cdot 13^3 \cdot 17 \cdot 19 \cdot 23 \cdot 29 \cdot 31 \cdot 41 \cdot 47 \cdot 59 \cdot 71$ [3]. Griess constructed it in 1981 as the automorphism group of a remarkable algebraic structure: a commutative, nonassociative algebra of dimension $196884$, now called the *Griess algebra* [3][4].

The Griess algebra $\mathcal{B}$ is built from the Leech lattice $\Lambda_{24}$. Its underlying vector space decomposes, as a representation of the Conway group $Co_1$, into pieces derived from $\Lambda_{24} \otimes \mathbb{R}$ and its symmetric square, plus a small exceptional summand; the algebra product is defined by explicit lattice formulas, and the Monster is precisely the group of algebra automorphisms [3][5]. Höhn and Seysen's 2025 work gives what they describe as the first self-contained computation of the Monster's order, using the *mmgroup* software package and counting arguments for certain idempotents of the Griess algebra called *axes* [3]. The Monster has exactly two conjugacy classes of involutions, and its smallest nontrivial complex representation has dimension $196883$ — a number that will return, with uncanny insistence, in the next section [3][4].

### 4.4 Monstrous Moonshine and Vertex Operator Algebras

In 1978 John McKay noticed a numerical coincidence that ought to have been meaningless: $196884 = 196883 + 1$, where the left side is the first nontrivial Fourier coefficient of the normalized modular $j$-function, $j(\tau) = q^{-1} + 744 + 196884q + 21493760q^2 + \cdots$ with $q = e^{2\pi i \tau}$, and the right side is one plus the dimension of the Monster's smallest nontrivial irreducible representation [4][6]. Thompson extended the observation to further coefficients, and Conway and Norton formulated, in 1979, the *monstrous moonshine conjectures*: for each of the 194 conjugacy classes of $\mathbb{M}$, there exists a genus-zero subgroup $\Gamma_g \subset PSL_2(\mathbb{R})$ commensurable with the modular group, such that the *McKay–Thompson series*

$$T_g(\tau) = \sum_{n \geq 0} \operatorname{Tr}(g \mid V_n)\, q^{\,n-1}$$

is the *Hauptmodul* — the normalized generator of the function field — of the genus-zero curve $\mathbb{H}/\Gamma_g$ [4][6].

> **Conjecture (Conway–Norton, 1979).** There exists an infinite-dimensional graded representation $V^\natural = \bigoplus_{n \geq 0} V_n$ of the Monster such that each McKay–Thompson series $T_g$ is a Hauptmodul for a genus-zero group.

Frenkel, Lepowsky, and Meurman constructed a candidate $V^\natural$ in 1988: the *moonshine module*, a vertex operator algebra of central charge $24$ whose automorphism group is exactly $\mathbb{M}$ and whose graded dimension is $j - 744$ [4]. Borcherds proved the conjecture in 1992 via the *Monster Lie algebra* and its denominator identity [4][6]:

$$p^{-1}\prod_{m > 0,\, n \in \mathbb{Z}} (1 - p^m q^n)^{c(mn)} = j(\sigma) - j(\tau),$$

where the $c(n)$ are the Fourier coefficients of $j$ [4][6]. Borcherds received the Fields Medal in 1998 in part for this work. The moonshine does not stop with the Monster: Norton's *generalized moonshine* conjectures (1987), proved by Carnahan in 2016, extend the phenomenon to commuting pairs in $\mathbb{M}$, and *umbral moonshine* (conjectured 2010, proved 2015) attaches mock modular forms to the Mathieu group $M_{24}$ [6].

### 4.5 The Revisionist Program: Gorenstein–Lyons–Solomon

The original proof of the CFSG is a victim of its own success: it exists, but it exists as ten to fifteen thousand pages of journal literature that no one person has ever read in full, with dependencies, gaps, and redundancies accumulated over five decades [8][10]. The *revisionist program* of Gorenstein, Lyons, and Solomon — launched after Gorenstein's 1972 Chicago seminars and pursued in earnest following his death in 1992 — aims to replace this sprawling corpus with a single, coherent, self-contained *second-generation proof*, published as a series of monographs in the AMS *Mathematical Surveys and Monographs* series [9].

The revision is not a mere reorganization. It restructures the proof around a cleaner case division, replaces ad hoc arguments with uniform methods (notably a systematic treatment of the *generic* case for groups of Lie type of large rank), and incorporates the Aschbacher–Smith quasithin work from the start rather than as a post hoc patch [9]. Six volumes appeared between 1994 and 2005, covering the foundations and the special odd case; subsequent volumes treat the generic case — the identification of the alternating groups and the large-rank groups of Lie type — with the projected series running to roughly twelve volumes and three to four thousand pages [9][10].

## 5 Empirical Evidence and Proofs

For a theorem of this magnitude, "empirical evidence" means *computational corroboration* of its most delicate constructions and *independent verification* of its most surprising consequences.

1. **The Monster's order, computed.** Höhn and Seysen's 2025 computation determines $\lvert \mathbb{M} \rvert$ by direct calculation in the Griess algebra using the *mmgroup* package — described as the first self-contained computation of the Monster's order — and establishes that $\mathbb{M}$ is the full automorphism group of both the Griess algebra and the moonshine module $V^\natural$ [3].
2. **The ATLAS and its representations.** The *ATLAS of Finite Groups* (Conway, Curtis, Norton, Parker, Wilson, 1985) and the online *ATLAS of Finite Group Representations* provide character tables, maximal subgroups, and explicit matrix and permutation representations for the sporadics, against which theoretical claims can be checked mechanically [5][8].
3. **Moonshine verified coefficient by coefficient.** Before Borcherds's proof, the Conway–Norton conjectures were supported by massive character computations matching McKay–Thompson coefficients to Hauptmoduln for all 194 classes — a computational experiment on a heroic scale that the proof then explained [4][6].
5. **Downstream theorems as consistency checks.** Hundreds of results proved *assuming* the classification — the Schreier conjecture on outer automorphism groups of simple groups, the classification of finite doubly transitive permutation groups, the restricted Burnside problem's group-theoretic consequences — form a vast web of mutual consistency; a missing simple group would have broken many of them [1][2].

The deepest "proof" on offer, of course, is the classification proof itself, now complete modulo the ongoing revision. What the computations supply is something the original literature could not: *reproducible* certainty about the objects the proof claims to classify.

## 6 Limitations

The classification theorem, for all its triumph, carries limitations that the mathematical community has never fully resolved.

**No surveyable proof exists.** The original proof's length — tens of thousands of pages by over a hundred authors — means that no living mathematician comprehends it in full, and large portions survive only in the original journal articles, some of which contain errors that were repaired informally or not at all [8][10].

**The revision is unfinished.** The Gorenstein–Lyons–Solomon series, projected at roughly twelve volumes, had published six by 2005, and the remaining volumes have appeared only slowly since [9][10]. Until the revision is complete, the "second-generation proof" remains a promise rather than a document.

**Dependence on computation.** Several load-bearing steps — the construction and uniqueness of the Monster, the $J_4$ existence proof, the Höhn–Seysen order computation — rely on machine calculations that cannot be checked by hand [3]. This is not a defect unique to group theory, but it concentrates here more heavily than anywhere else in pure mathematics, and it raises the question of what it means to *understand* a proof one can only *run*.

**Scope boundaries.** The theorem covers *finite* simple groups only. The classification of infinite simple groups remains wide open, and even for finite groups, the *extension problem* — how simple groups combine to form arbitrary finite groups — is, in full generality, considered inaccessible [8]. Knowing the atoms has not finished chemistry.

## 7 Conclusion

The Classification of Finite Simple Groups stands as the largest collaborative theorem in the history of mathematics: a fifty-year, hundred-author, ten-thousand-page argument that the atoms of finite symmetry come in exactly four kinds — the cyclic groups of prime order, the alternating groups, the groups of Lie type, and twenty-six sporadic exceptions crowned by the Monster [1][2][8]. Its consequences — from the Schreier conjecture to monstrous moonshine — continue to ramify through algebra, number theory, and mathematical physics [4][6].

Yet the theorem's very scale has made its *preservation* a mathematical problem in its own right. The revisionist program of Gorenstein, Lyons, and Solomon is an attempt to ensure that the classification remains part of "the living world of mathematics" rather than a monument buried in forgotten journals [8][9][10]. Whether the second-generation proof will be completed, and whether moonshine will finally explain *why* the Monster exists, are the open questions the next generation of group theorists inherits. The periodic table of symmetry has been written; learning to read it is the work that remains.
