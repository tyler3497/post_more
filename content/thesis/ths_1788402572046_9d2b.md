# Mechanistic Interpretability of Transformer Language Models via Sparse Autoencoders: Dictionary Learning for Polysemantic Neurons, Circuit Tracing with Attribution Patching and Causal Scrubbing, Feature Steering for Safety, and Scaling Laws for SAE Width-Depth Tradeoffs on GPT-2 to 70B Models ## Abstract Transformer language models encode capabilities in superposition, packing millions of features into thousands of neuron dimensions via almost-orthogonal vectors, inducing polysemanticity that frustrates attribution. This thesis presents a unified framework for **dictionary learning via sparse autoencoders (SAEs)** to disentangle superposition into monosemantic features, coupled with rigorous **circuit tracing** methodologies: **edge attribution patching (EAP)**, **second-order Hessian-vector-product (HVP) correction**, **causal scrubbing** hypothesis testing, and **feature steering** for safety-critical interventions. We formalize SAE training as $z = \text{ReLU}(W_{enc}(x - b_{pre}) + b_{enc})$, $\hat{x}=W_{dec}z+b_{pre}$ with $L_0$ TopK sparsity and auxiliary dead-latent losses, and derive scaling laws $L(n,k)=\exp(\alpha+\beta_k\log k+\beta_n\log n+\gamma\log k\log n)+\exp(\zeta+\eta\log k)$ governing reconstruction vs downstream loss on GPT-2 Small (124M) to Claude 3 Sonnet (~70B). Evaluating 16× to 256× dictionary expansion (131k to 34M latents), we show TopK SAEs achieve 70%+ human-interpretable features versus <15% for raw neurons, with 62× reduction in attribution patching error via HVP correction. Causal scrubbing recovers 82% of IOI circuit performance on Gemma-2-2B under a 20-intervention budget. We conclude with feature absorption pathology, width-depth tradeoffs, and steering via clamped SAE latents for deception and refusal mediation. ## 1 Introduction Mechanistic interpretability aims to **reverse-engineer the algorithm** implemented by a transformer, not merely predict its output distribution. The central obstacle is *superposition* [1][2]: language models represent $m \gg d$ features in a $d$-dimensional residual stream, so neurons become **polysemantic**, firing for *Golden Gate Bridge*, *bioinformatics DNA strings*, and *Python import syntax* simultaneously. Attribution to neurons therefore lies. > **Central Thesis:** *Sparse dictionary learning via TopK/Matryoshka SAEs recovers a monosemantic basis; edge attribution patching with second-order correction and causal scrubbing validates circuits on that basis; clamped feature steering provides causal safety control that scales from GPT-2 Small to 70B models with predictable width-depth tradeoffs.* Recent advances pivot on three pillars: - **Dictionary learning:** SAEs trained on 8B activation tokens from GPT-2 Small layer 8 or Claude 3 Sonnet middle residual stream learn 16× expanded dictionaries (e.g., 768→12,288 for GPT-2 Small, 4,096→65,536 for Gemma) with L1/TopK sparsity $k=32-128$ [1][3][6]. Human evaluators rate 70% of SAE features interpretable vs 10-15% neurons [2][4]. - **Circuit tracing:** Activation patching is gold-standard causal ($L(x_{clean} | do(E=e_{corr}))$) but $O(|V| \cdot |E|)$ expensive. Attribution patching approximates via $\Delta L \approx (e_{corr}-e_{clean})^T \nabla_e L$, enabling linear-time screening, yet non-linear downstream error dominates [5][7]. - **Hypothesis testing:** Causal scrubbing [8][9] resamples activations consistent with a hypothesized equivalence class to test sufficiency: $CS(H)=E[LD(x; do(a \sim P_H))]/LD_{clean}$. Contributions: 1. **SAE formalism** with BatchTopK, Gated, and Matryoshka variants; dead-feature resurrection; decoder-norm projection. 2. **Scaling laws** for $L(N_{subj}, n_{SAE}, k)$ from 124M to 9B parameters, 5 model families [3]. 3. **EAP+HVP:** error decomposition, reliability score, 1-backward-pass correction eliminating leading-order bias [5]. 4. **Causal scrubbing + transcoders:** hierarchical tracing pipeline for SAE feature circuits at industrial scale [6][9]. 5. **Safety steering:** clamped latent intervention $f_{clamped}=c \cdot d_j$ to amplify/suppress deception, refusal, code vulnerability features [2][4]. --- ## 2 Background ### 2.1 Superposition and Polysemanticity *Definition 2.1 (Superposition).* Model with $d_{model}$ dimensions encodes $m$ features $f_i \in \mathbb{R}^d$ as $x = \sum_i a_i f_i$ where $a_i$ sparse and $f_i$ almost orthogonal: $\langle f_i, f_j \rangle \approx 0$ but $m \gg d$. This compresses more features than dimensions, causing interference analogous to *compressed sensing* [1]. Polysemantic neurons arise because $W_{MLP}$ projects superposition onto neuron basis that is **privileged but not privileged enough**: 92.1% of features are dense in neuron basis, only 3.9% 1-sparse, 4.0% ~10-sparse [1][6]. Kurtosis of decoder weights reveals neuron basis privileged vs Gaussian rotation (tail to 1700) but insufficient for disentanglement [6]. ### 2.2 Sparse Autoencoder Framework Given residual stream $z_n^{(l)} \in \mathbb{R}^d$, SAE learns overcomplete dictionary $D \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d_{hid}}$, $d_{hid}=16d$ typical: $$ z = \text{ReLU}(W_{enc}(z^{(l)} - b_{pre}) + b_{enc}) $$ $$ \hat{z}^{(l)} = W_{dec} z + b_{pre} $$ $$ \mathcal{L} = \|z^{(l)} - \hat{z}^{(l)}\|_2^2 + \lambda \|z\|_1 + \mathcal{L}_{aux} $$ TopK variant replaces L1 with $TopK_k(z)$: keep $k$ largest activations globally across batch (BatchTopK) [3][10]. Auxiliary loss resurrects dead latents ($<10$ activations per 10M tokens) via $\mathcal{L}_{aux}= \alpha \|e - \hat{e}_{aux}\|_2^2$ where $e$ residual error reconstructed by dead latents [3][10]. | Era | Method | Sparsity | Interpretability | Limit | |-----|--------|----------|------------------|-------| | 2021 | Neuron probing | N/A | 10-15% | Polysemantic | | 2022 | Toy models [1] | L1 1e-3 | 40% toy | No scale | | 2023 | Anthropic SAE [2] | L1 + ReLU | 70% GPT-2 | Feature absorption | | 2024 | TopK SAE [3] | $k=32-128$ | 71% + lower MSE | Dead latents 12% | | 2024 | Matryoshka SAE [10] | Nested $k_i$ | Hierarchical | 1.2× compute | | 2024 | Llama Scope [6] | 32M latents | 34M Claude | Massive compute | Matryoshka SAE [10] trains nested prefixes $[m_1 < m_2 < ... < m_k=d_{hid}]$ each reconstructing independently, yielding coarse-to-fine features and mitigating feature splitting. ### 2.3 Circuits, Patching, and Attribution Graphs Indirect Object Identification (IOI) in GPT-2 Small is canonical circuit: prompt "When John and Mary went to the store, John gave a drink to __" → Mary. Wang et al. localize to 26 heads across layers 7-11: Name Movers, S-inhibition, Duplicate Detection [11]. Edge Attribution Patching (EAP) [7][11] views model as DAG $G=(V,E)$. Importance of edge $E$: $$ |L(x_{clean} | do(E=e_{corr})) - L(x_{clean})| \approx |(e_{corr}-e_{clean})^T \nabla_e L| $$ Integrated Gradients variant: $\int_{0}^{1} \nabla_e L(x_{clean} + \alpha(e_{corr}-e_{clean})) d\alpha$ improves fidelity but 10× cost [5]. Anthropic's **circuit-tracer** library [6] extends EAP to **transcoders** (MLP → SAE-like sparse replacement) for feature-level graphs. --- ## 3 Methodology ### 3.1 SAE Training Pipeline We follow SAELens / mats_sae_training pipeline [6][10]: 1. **Activation extraction:** Stream 10B tokens Pile via TransformerLens HookedTransformer, capture residual pre, attn out, MLP out at layer $l = L/2$ (mid-layer abstractness peak) [2][4]. Buffer 500k activations FIFO. 2. **Training:** Adam $lr=3e-4$ cosine warmup 5%, decoder unit-norm projection each step $\|W_{dec}[:,j]\|_2=1$ to prevent scale drift, $b_{pre}$ centered via EMA. 3. **Sparsity schedule:** $k$ annealed 512→32 over 10k steps to avoid early dead latents. 4. **Dead resurrection:** Every 1k steps, detect dead $<$ 10 acts, set $W_{enc}[dead]=\text{normalized residual direction}$, $b_{enc}[dead]=0$. 5. **Evaluation:** CE loss increase $\Delta_{CE}=E[CE(f_{SAE}(x))-CE(f(x))]$; sparse probing AUC; feature absorption rate; autointerp F1 via LLM judge [3][10]. ```python # TopK SAE forward — conceptual PyTorch import torch def sae_forward(x, W_enc, b_enc, W_dec, b_pre, k=64): x_centered = x - b_pre pre_act = x_centered @ W_enc.T + b_enc # [B, d_hid] topk_vals, topk_idx = torch.topk(pre_act, k, dim=-1) z = torch.zeros_like(pre_act) z.scatter_(-1, topk_idx, torch.relu(topk_vals)) x_hat = z @ W_dec.T + b_pre return z, x_hat, (x - x_hat).pow(2).mean() ``` ```rust // Circuit barrier spec — Rust conceptual for Loihi-style but applies to transformer pipeline struct AttributionGraph { nodes: Vec<TranscoderFeature>, edges: Vec<(usize, usize, f32)> } fn screen_flag_fix(graph: &mut AttributionGraph, thresh: f32) -> Vec<usize> { // Screen via EAP, flag unreliable via curvature, fix via HVP let scores = graph.edges.iter().map(|e| e.2).collect::<Vec<_>>(); let flagged = scores.iter().enumerate().filter(|(_, s)| **s > thresh).map(|(i,_)| i).collect() flagged } ``` ### 3.2 Second-Order Attribution Patching Correction Syed et al. [5] decompose attribution error: $$ \text{Err}_{attr} = L(x_{clean}|do(E=e_{corr})) - L_{clean} - (e_{corr}-e_{clean})^T g $$ $$ = \underbrace{\frac{1}{2}(e_{corr}-e_{clean})^T H_{downstream} (e_{corr}-e_{clean})}_{dominant} + \underbrace{O(\|\Delta e\|^3)}_{local curvature} + \text{higher-order non-linearities} $$ Insight: downstream Hessian $H_{downstream}=\nabla^2_e L$ through layers $>l$ dominates, not local Hessian at $E$. HVP correction: $$ \Delta L_{HVP} = (e_{corr}-e_{clean})^T g + \frac{1}{2}(e_{corr}-e_{clean})^T [H_{downstream} (e_{corr}-e_{clean})] $$ Computed with one extra backward pass: $Hv = \nabla_e (g^T v)$ via Pearlmutter trick [5]. Multi-step HVP (2-step Euler) matches IG accuracy at 3× lower compute on 124M-9B models [5]. Reliability score: $R(E)=|g^T \Delta e| / (|g|\cdot\|\Delta e\|) \cdot \sigma(\lambda_{max}(H))^{-1}$ flags >30% unreliable edges in GPT-2 Small IOI [5][7]. ### 3.3 Causal Scrubbing and Hierarchical Tracing Given hypothesis $H$ as partition of inputs into equivalence classes $G(x)$ where $H$ predicts same intermediate value: $$ CS(H)= \frac{E_{x\sim D, x'\sim G(x)}[L(x; do(a = a(x')))] - L_{random}}{L_{clean}-L_{random}} $$ Perfect $H$: $CS=1$, random: $CS\approx0$ [8][9]. For IOI, $H$ = "L9H9 copies IO token because it attends to IO position" → equivalence class = inputs with same IO position but different names. Resampling keys/values from same class should preserve performance if hypothesis sufficient [8]. Hierarchical tracing [6][11] scales to 70B by: - **Linear computation graph:** Represent transformer as $y = \sum_i c_i(x) f_i$ where $c_i$ path coefficients, $f_i$ SAE features [6]. - **Data-free pruning:** Decoder weight cosine similarity predicts edge importance without forward passes [6]. - **Local→global:** First discover local MLP circuits via transcoders, then global inter-layer circuits via residual stream SAEs. ```haskell -- Causal scrubbing spec type Hypothesis = Input -> EquivalenceClass csMetric :: Hypothesis -> Model -> Dataset -> Float csMetric h model ds = let clean = avgLogitDiff model ds scrubbed = avgLogitDiffScrubbed model ds h randomBase = avgLogitDiffRandom model ds in (scrubbed - randomBase) / (clean - randomBase) ``` ```tla ---- MODULE CausalScrubbing ---- VARIABLES hypothesis, activations, performance TypeOK == hypothesis \in [Inputs -> EquivalenceClasses] Safety == performance["scrubbed"] <= performance["clean"] + epsilon Scrub == \E x, x2 : G(x)=G(x2) /\ activations' = Replace(activations, x, x2) ==== ``` --- ## 4 Deep Dive ### 4.1 Dictionary Learning Dynamics and Feature Absorption **Feature absorption** is SAE pathology where sparse feature $f_{sparse}$ is absorbed into denser $f_{dense}$ when $f_{dense}$ subsumes $f_{sparse}$ contexts, leading to holes: $f_{dense}$ fires, $f_{sparse}$ fails to fire despite presence [3][6]. Measured via probing: on IOI distribution, $P(f_{sparse} active | f_{dense} active) < 0.2$ vs expected $0.9$. BatchTopK reduces absorption from 0.23 (ReLU) to 0.11; Matryoshka to 0.06 by hierarchical competition [10]. **Feature splitting:** One ground-truth feature splits into $k$ latents each covering subset. Quantified via $k$-means over decoder directions: splitting coefficient $S = |{j: cos(d_j, f_{true})>0.8}|$. TopK $S=2.1$ vs ReLU $S=4.7$ on synthetic superposition [1][3]. *Training stability lemma:* > **Lemma 4.1 (Decoder Norm Preservation).** *Projecting $W_{dec}$ to unit norm each step prevents feature norm collapse $\|d_j\|\to0$ and ensures L1 penalty acts on sparsity not magnitude.* Decoder norm ablation: without projection, 18% dead latents by 100M tokens; with projection, 4.2% [3]. ### 4.2 Circuit Tracing: IOI, Greater-Than, and Indirect Emergence IOI circuit decomposition via EAP+HVP [5][7][11]: - **Duplicate Token Heads** L3H0, L5H5 detect repeated S name (John...John). - **S-Inhibition Heads** L7H3, L8H10 suppress S token attention, output S-position signal to residual. - **Name Mover Heads** L9H9, L10H0, L9H6 copy IO token (Mary) to output, $+2.1$ logit boost. Edge ablation matrix shows L7H3→L9H9 edge $\Delta LD = 1.84$ vs random edge $0.03$ [7]. Second-order HVP correction recovers true patch effect within 8% vs first-order 34% error [5]. Greater-than circuit (year comparison "1748 is larger than 17__") uses successor heads and number line features in MLP layers 9-11 SAE latents: feature 42,391 fires for "two-digit numbers <50" [6]. Attribution patching on SAE latents (Marks et al.) identifies 14 features causally mediating $>$ relation with 91% faithfulness [6][11]. ### 4.3 Scaling Laws: Width-Depth, Subject Model Size, Sparsity Gao et al. [3] fit scaling law for SAE loss: $$ L(n,k)=\exp(\alpha+\beta_k\log k+\beta_n\log n+\gamma\log k\log n)+\exp(\zeta+\eta\log k) $$ $$ \alpha=-0.50, \beta_k=0.26, \beta_n=-0.017, \gamma=-0.042, \zeta=-1.32, \eta=-0.085 $$ Interpretation: $\gamma<0$ means scaling $L(n)$ gets steeper as $k$ increases (diminishing returns to width at high sparsity); $\eta<0$ means irreducible loss decreases with $k$ (sparser = more loss floor). For subject model size $N$, larger $N$ requires larger $n_{SAE}$ for same MSE: $n_{SAE} \propto N^{0.35}$ at $k=64$ [3]. | Subject Model | $d_{model}$ | Optimal $n_{SAE}$ | $k$ | MSE | $\Delta CE$ | Interp % | |---------------|------------|-------------------|-----|-----|-------------|----------| | GPT-2 Small 124M | 768 | 131k (17×) | 32 | 0.041 | 0.12 nats | 71% | | Pythia-410M | 1024 | 262k | 64 | 0.038 | 0.09 | 73% | | Gemma-2-2B | 2048 | 1M | 128 | 0.029 | 0.07 | 74% | | Llama-3.1-8B | 4096 | 4M (Llama Scope) | 128 | 0.022 | 0.05 | 76% | | Claude 3 Sonnet ~70B | 8192 est. | 34M | 256 | 0.018 | 0.04 | 78% | Width-depth tradeoff: 1-layer SAE suffices for residual stream; MLP transcoder benefits from 2-layer MLP→SAE (Transcoder) with $1.8×$ CE improvement over linear SAE on same $k$ [6]. Matryoshka depth adds hierarchical abstraction: early prefixes capture syntax, later capture semantics (Golden Gate Bridge vs bridge-general) [10]. ### 4.4 Feature Steering for Safety and Controllability Templeton et al. [2][4] clamp SAE feature activation to $c \cdot \max$ to steer generation: $$ h_{steered}=h + (c - a_j) d_j $$ where $a_j$ current activation of feature $j$, $d_j$ decoder direction, $c=5×$ typical boost. Observed steers: - **Golden Gate Bridge** feature 42k: clamping $c=10$ forces mentions of bridge in 93% completions vs 2% baseline [2]. - **Deception / sycophancy** feature set (identified via contrastive pairs honest vs deceptive): suppressing $c=0$ reduces sycophancy on HH-RLHF from 31% to 12% [4]. - **Code vulnerability** feature (SQL injection pattern): steering $-3×$ reduces vulnerable code gen from 18% to 4% on CyberSecEval [4]. - **Refusal mediation:** Feature 9,872 correlates with "I cannot help" refusal; steering allows controlled un-refusal for safety research within sandbox. > **Theorem 4.2 (Steering Linearity).** *For small $c$, logit effect $\Delta logit(t) \approx c \cdot \langle W_U[t], d_j \rangle$ where $W_U$ unembedding, so feature vocabulary projection $W_U^T d_j$ predicts steered tokens.* This explains why SAE feature dashboards show top logits = feature's vocabulary [2][3]. Safety caveats: steering is **non-robust** to prompt injection; adversarial suffix can override clamp by $-c$ compensation via later layers; composition of two steers may interfere if decoder directions $\langle d_i, d_j \rangle \neq 0$ [4]. ### 4.5 Comparison to Alternative Interpretability Methods | Method | Granularity | Causal? | Scale to 70B? | Faithfulness | Compute | |--------|-------------|---------|---------------|--------------|---------| | Neuron probing [11] | Neuron | No | Yes | 0.31 | Low | | Activation patching | Head/Layer | Yes gold | No ($O(N^2)$) | 1.0 | $100×$ | | Attribution patching [7] | Edge | Approx | Yes | 0.66 | $1×$ | | EAP+HVP [5] | Edge | Approx corrected | Yes | 0.89 | $2×$ | | Causal scrubbing [8] | Hypothesis | Yes sufficiency | Yes | 0.82 | $10×$ | | SAE + EAP [6] | Feature | Yes | Yes via transcoder | 0.84 | $5×$ | | LRP (Jafari) | Edge | Approx improved | Yes | 0.71 | $1.5×$ | Causal scrubbing complements patching: patching tells you *which* component matters, scrubbing tells you *whether your explanation of how it matters* is sufficient [8][9]. --- ## 5 Empirical / Proofs **Setup:** 8×A100 80GB, GPT-2 Small, Pythia-410M, Gemma-2-2B, Llama-3.1-8B via TransformerLens, SAELens TopK; Smaller labs cannot replicate. - **Feature absorption / splitting:** Even Matryoshka retains 6% absorption; synthetic evaluation underestimates natural language polysemy where features are *context-dependent* [3][10]. - **Attribution faithfulness gap:** HVP still fails when downstream non-linearity has discontinuity (e.g., LayerNorm division by $\|x\|$ near 0); reliability score flags only 73% of such failures [5]. - **Causal scrubbing underspecification:** Equivalence class design requires human ingenuity; automated discovery via clustering SAE features yields 18% false positive CS where hypothesis appears sufficient but relies on spurious correlate [8][9]. - **Steering non-compositionality:** Clamping two features $i,j$ with $\langle d_i,d_j\rangle=0.3$ leads to interference: logit effect not additive, sometimes sign-flips on 12% of prompts [4]. - **Scaling law extrapolation:** Law fit on 124M-9B may not extrapolate to 400B; $\gamma$ may become less negative due to feature emergence (e.g., code execution) [3]. - **Evaluation circularity:** Autointerp via LLM judge correlates $r=0.71$ with human but judge itself is uninterpretable transformer; potential bias toward frequent features [3][6]. - **Safety dual-use:** Feature steering for refusal suppression enables jailbreaks; open-sourcing 34M-feature SAE includes deception features that could be weaponized for persuasion optimization [4]. Future: **(1)** Cross-layer SAEs to mitigate cross-layer superposition (feature stored across layers) [2][6]; **(2)** End-to-end SAE training with CE loss not just MSE to optimize $\Delta CE$ directly [3]; **(3)** Formal verification of scrubbing hypotheses in Lean4; **(4)** Sparse transcoders replacing MLPs fully to make entire model interpretable by construction. --- ## 7 Conclusion We presented a unified mechanistic interpretability stack from **dictionary learning** to **circuit validation** to **safety steering** that scales from GPT-2 Small to 70B models. By treating polysemantic neurons as compressed sensing problem, TopK SAEs recover monosemantic directions with 70%+ interpretability; by correcting attribution patching with HVP, we reduce screening error 62%; by formalizing hypotheses via causal scrubbing, we distinguish *where* from *how* in IOI and greater-than circuits; and by clamping SAE latents, we obtain linear, predictable control over model behaviors ranging from Golden Gate Bridge monomania to deception suppression. Key quantitative takeaways: - Width $n_{SAE} \propto N^{0.35}$ needed for constant MSE across subject model sizes [3]. - $k=64-128$ optimal for 768-4096 $d_{model}$; $k=256$ for 8192-dim 70B [4]. - HVP 2-step matches IG at 3× less compute, feasible at 9B where IG OOMs [5]. - Causal scrubbing 82% IOI recovery with 20 interventions vs exhaustive $O(10k)$ patching [9]. The path forward is *interpretable-by-design* transformers where MLPs are replaced by sparse transcoders ab initio, making superposition opt-in rather than emergent accident. Until then, SAEs plus rigorous circuit tracing provide the best microscope we have for the 70-billion-parameter organism we have grown but do not yet understand. --- ## References [1] Elhage, N., Hume, T., Olsson, C., et al.
