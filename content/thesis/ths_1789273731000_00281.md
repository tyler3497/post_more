# Spin Glasses: Replica Symmetry Breaking, the Sherrington–Kirkpatrick Model, and Ultrametric Landscapes

## Abstract

Spin glasses — magnetic alloys with frozen-in random interactions — forced statistical physics to abandon the notion that a phase of matter is specified by a handful of order parameters. This thesis develops the mean-field theory of the Sherrington–Kirkpatrick (SK) model, the canonical solvable model introduced in 1975 [1], whose quenched disorder produces frustration: no spin configuration can satisfy all couplings at once. The naive replica-symmetric solution is shown to be unstable, following de Almeida and Thouless [10], yielding the notorious low-temperature negative entropy. We reconstruct Parisi's resolution — replica symmetry breaking [3][4] — in which the order parameter is promoted from a number to a function *q(x)* and equilibrium states acquire a hierarchical, *ultrametric* organization. We trace the rigorous vindication of this construction: Guerra's interpolation bound [6], Talagrand's 2006 proof of the Parisi formula [7], and Panchenko's proof of ultrametricity [9].

##1 Introduction

Few episodes in statistical physics compare in drama with the spin-glass story. In 1975, Sherrington and Kirkpatrick proposed a model of infinite-range random interactions that they declared *solvable* — in the precise sense that the quenched free energy density could be computed exactly [1]. Within a few years the "solution" had visibly disintegrated: it produced negative entropy at low temperature, and its saddle point was shown to be locally unstable almost everywhere in the putative ordered phase [10]. The model was simultaneously exactly solvable and provably unsolved. It remained so for a quarter century, until the 2006 proof that a scheme of staggering formal audacity — Parisi's replica symmetry breaking — was in fact exact [7].

Spin glasses are real materials: dilute magnetic alloys such as CuMn or AuFe in which a few percent of magnetic impurities sit at random positions in a nonmagnetic host. Their susceptibility shows a sharp cusp at a freezing temperature *T_g*, yet no Bragg peaks appear in neutron diffraction: the spins freeze, but they freeze into no periodic pattern [11]. The two ingredients responsible for this behavior are *quenched randomness* — the impurity positions and couplings are frozen on experimental timescales — and *frustration*: around a plaquette of spins with mixed ferromagnetic and antiferromagnetic bonds, no single assignment of orientations can make every bond happy. The Edwards–Anderson model of 1975 captured these ingredients on a finite-dimensional lattice [2], but the model proved analytically intractable. Sherrington and Kirkpatrick replaced the short-range interactions with infinite-range Gaussian couplings scaled so as to keep the energy extensive — a mean-field spin glass [1].

> **Theorem (Replica-symmetric pathology):** The replica-symmetric saddle point of the SK free energy predicts strictly negative entropy at sufficiently low temperature and is unstable against replicon fluctuations below the de Almeida–Thouless line [10].

> **Sketch:** Under the replica-symmetric ansatz *Q_ab = q* for *a ≠ b*, the free energy per spin is extremized by a self-consistent *q* satisfying *q = ∫ Dz tanh²(β(J√q z + h))*. At *h = 0* this yields *S(T) < 0* for *T < T₀ ≈ 0.8J*, contradicting the third law. Expanding the replica action about the RS saddle, the replicon eigenvalue *λ_R = 1 − β²J² ∫ Dz sech⁴(β(J√q z + h))* turns negative in the glassy phase, proving local instability □.

This thesis is organized as follows. §2 reviews the physical background and the SK construction. §3 presents the replica and cavity methodology. §4 develops the deep theory: the replica trick, Parisi's hierarchical ansatz, ultrametricity, and the probabilistic reformulation through Ruelle cascades and the Ghirlanda–Guerra identities. §5 assembles the empirical and rigorous evidence — experiments, simulations, and the chain of mathematical results from Guerra–Toninelli through Talagrand to Panchenko. §6 states what remains open, notably the finite-dimensional problem. §7 concludes.

##2 Background

### Disorder, frustration, and the Edwards–Anderson model

A spin glass combines two features. First, *quenched disorder*: the couplings *J_ij* are random variables fixed at the moment the material is made; thermodynamic averages are taken with the disorder held fixed, and physical observables are averaged over the disorder distribution *after* the logarithm of the partition function is taken — the *quenched* average *(1/N)𝔼 log Z_N*, in contrast to the annealed average *(1/N) log 𝔼 Z_N*, which is generically wrong at low temperature because *log 𝔼 Z_N ≥ 𝔼 log Z_N* by Jensen's inequality. Second, *frustration*: a triangle of spins with antiferromagnetic bonds admits no fully satisfied configuration; the ground-state manifold is massively degenerate even in the clean limit [2].

Edwards and Anderson proposed the first order parameter for the glassy phase: the mean squared local magnetization *q_EA = lim_{N→∞} (1/N) Σ_i ⟨σ_i⟩²*, which becomes nonzero at freezing while the uniform magnetization stays zero [2]. Their model, *H = −Σ_{⟨ij⟩} J_ij σ_i σ_j* on a lattice, is still unsolved in finite dimensions — it is, in a precise sense, computationally intractable [11].

### The Sherrington–Kirkpatrick Hamiltonian

Sherrington and Kirkpatrick sacrificed locality for solvability. For *N* Ising spins *σ = (σ₁,…,σ_N) ∈ {−1,+1}^N* and i.i.d. standard Gaussian couplings *g_ij*, the SK Hamiltonian is

$$H_N(\sigma) = -\frac{\beta}{\sqrt{N}} \sum_{i<j} g_{ij}\, \sigma_i \sigma_j \qquad\text{(inverse temperature } \beta \text{ absorbed)},$$

and the quenched free energy density is *F_N(β, h) = (1/N) 𝔼 log Σ_σ exp(−H_N(σ) + h Σ_i σ_i)*. The Gaussian process *H_N* is fully determined by its covariance, which depends on two configurations only through their *overlap* *R_{1,2} = (1/N) σ¹ · σ²*:

$$\mathbb{E}\,H_N(\sigma^1) H_N(\sigma^2) = \frac{N}{2}\, R_{1,2}^2 - \tfrac{1}{2}.$$

That single identity explains why overlaps — not spins — are the protagonists of the SK theory [8]. The diagonal-replica construction, the Parisi functional, and the ultrametric tree are all consequences of taking *R_{1,2}* seriously as the macroscopic observable.

### The RS solution and its collapse

Sherrington and Kirkpatrick solved the model by the replica trick (see §3) under the assumption that the replica overlap matrix *Q_ab* is invariant under permutation of replicas: *Q_ab = q* for *a ≠ b*. The resulting phase diagram has a paramagnet (*q = 0*) above *T_c = J* and a spin glass (*q > 0*) below [1]. Two pathologies soon emerged:

1. **Negative entropy.** At low temperature the RS entropy becomes negative — impossible for a classical spin model — signaling that the saddle point cannot be the true extremum [10].
2. **Replicon instability.** de Almeida and Thouless computed the stability spectrum of the RS saddle and found the replicon eigenvalue negative throughout the ordered phase at *h = 0* and below a critical field (the AT line) at finite *h* [10]. The symmetric saddle is a saddle, not a maximum.

Parisi's 1979 proposal — an *infinite number of order parameters* [3] — was the way out.

##3 Methodology

### The replica trick

The quenched average *𝔼 log Z* is analytically inaccessible by direct means, because the logarithm obstructs the Gaussian average. The replica method circumvents this through the identity

$$\mathbb{E} \log Z_N = \lim_{n \to 0} \frac{\mathbb{E}\, Z_N^n - 1}{n},$$

where for integer *n* the *replicated* partition function *Z_N^n = Σ_{σ¹,…,σⁿ} 𝔼 exp(−β Σ_a H_N(σᵃ))* factorizes over sites after the Gaussian disorder average, at the price of coupling the replicas through the overlap matrix

$$Q_{ab} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N \sigma_i^a \sigma_i^b, \qquad Q_{aa} = 1.$$

For integer *n ≥ 1*, the replicated free energy is an *n(n−1)/2*-dimensional saddle-point problem. The audacious move — due to Parisi — is to compute the extremum for integer *n*, find a *pattern* in the optimal *Q* matrix, and then *analytically continue* that pattern to *n → 0*, where a maximum over the *n → 0* "dimension" becomes a *minimum* [3][4][5].

### The cavity method

A parallel physical derivation, due to Mézard, Parisi, and Virasoro, adds one spin to an *N*-spin system and tracks the effective field — the *cavity method* [5], ancestor of belief and survey propagation.

### Rigorization strategy

The mathematical proof that the replica construction is exact proceeded in three stages [7][8][9]:

| Stage | Result | Technique |
|---|---|---|
| Existence of the limit | *lim_{N→∞} F_N* exists | Guerra–Toninelli interpolation, superadditivity |
| Upper bound | *limsup F_N ≤ 𝒫* (Parisi functional) | Guerra's interpolation [6] |
| Lower bound | *liminf F_N ≥ 𝒫* | Talagrand's constrained replicas [7] |
| Structure | Overlaps are ultrametric | Panchenko [9], Ghirlanda–Guerra identities |

The following minimal Python script illustrates the replica-symmetric self-consistency that must be abandoned — the correct fixed point (full RSB) replaces the scalar *q* by a function:

```python
import numpy as np
from math import erf, sqrt, pi

def Dz(f, z):
    return np.exp(-z**2/2)/sqrt(2*pi) * f(z)

# Replica-symmetric fixed point: q = E[tanh^2(beta*sqrt(q)*Z)]
def rs_fixed_point(beta, n=200):
    z = np.linspace(-8, 8, n)
    q = 0.5
    for _ in range(200):
        q = Dz(lambda z: np.tanh(beta*np.sqrt(q)*z)**2, z).sum()*(16/(n-1))
    return q

for T in (1.5, 1.0, 0.7, 0.4):
    print(T, rs_fixed_point(1.0/T))
```

At *T < 1* the nontrivial solution *q > 0* exists but, per [10], is unstable: the physical theory lives in the full RSB hierarchy described next.

##4 Deep Dive

### The Replica Trick and the Failure of Replica Symmetry

The replica-symmetric ansatz treats all distinct replica pairs identically: *Q_ab = q(1 − δ_ab)*. Physically this asserts that any two equilibrium configurations drawn independently from the Gibbs measure have the same typical overlap — i.e., that there is *one* pure state (up to global spin flip). The AT instability [10] proves this picture inconsistent: thermal fluctuations *within* the putative single state see a landscape that is itself rough on all scales. The system breaks into many *pure states* (ergodic components), and the overlap *R_{1,2}* becomes a nontrivial *random variable* even in the thermodynamic limit — a phenomenon called *non-self-averaging* of the overlap [5].

Parisi's first step (1979) was a one-step RSB ansatz: replicas are grouped into *n/m₁* blocks of size *m₁*, with *Q_ab = q₀* across blocks and *Q_ab = q₁ > q₀* within blocks [3]. The entropy pathology persisted at one step; iterating to *k* steps yields a *piecewise constant* order-parameter function, and in the *k → ∞* limit the full RSB object emerges: a *continuous nondecreasing function q(x)* on *[0,1]*, the cumulative distribution of overlaps. Symbolically, if *P(q)* is the limiting law of *R_{1,2}*,

$$x(q) = \int_{-\infty}^{q} P(q')\, dq', \qquad q(x) \text{ its inverse}.$$

A delta function *P = δ(q − q_EA)* is replica symmetry; anything broader is RSB.

### Parisi's Hierarchical Ansatz: the Order Function *q(x)* and the Parisi PDE

In the *k*-RSB scheme the Parisi functional for the free energy is built from a backward recursion. In the continuous limit, define *Φ(t, x)* solving the *Parisi PDE* for *t ∈ [0,1]*:

$$\partial_t \Phi + \frac{\xi''(t)}{2}\left(\partial_{xx}\Phi + x(t)\,(\partial_x \Phi)^2\right) = 0, \qquad \Phi(1, x) = \log 2\cosh(\beta h + x),$$

where *ξ(q) = β²q²/2* encodes the SK covariance and *x(t)* is the order function (conventionally *x(q)* is the CDF, *q(x)* its inverse). The limiting free energy is then

$$\mathcal{P}(\xi, h) = \inf_{x} \left\{ \Phi_{x}(0, h) - \frac{1}{2}\int_0^1 q\,\xi''(q)\, x(q)\, dq \right\},$$

the *Parisi formula*: free energy as an infimum over order functions of a nonlinear PDE value minus an entropic correction [6][7]. The infimum is attained at a unique minimizer — the *Parisi measure* — whose atoms count the RSB steps (continuous *x(q)* means *full* RSB). The Crisanti–Sommers functional offers an equivalent variational form [8].

The Haskell sketch below captures the discrete *k*-RSB hierarchy as a type — each level refines the overlap resolution:

```haskell
-- k-step replica symmetry breaking hierarchy
data RSB = RS [Level]              -- levels l=0..k, outermost first
data Level = Level
  { blockSize :: Double            -- m_l: Parisi block parameter
  , overlap   :: Double            -- q_l: intra-block overlap
  } deriving Show

-- full RSB: continuous order function q(x) on [0,1]
type OrderFunction = Double -> Double

parisi1RSB :: RSB
parisi1RSB = RS [Level 0.5 0.2, Level 1.0 0.9]
```

### Ultrametric Geometry of the State Space

The most striking prediction of the Parisi solution is *geometric*. Draw three configurations independently from the Gibbs measure and let *q₁₂, q₁₃, q₂₃* be their overlaps. Ultrametricity asserts that the two largest of the three overlaps are equal with probability one — equivalently, overlaps satisfy the *strong triangle inequality* *d(x,z) ≤ max(d(x,y), d(y,z))* for the distance *d = 1 − q*. The pure states thus organize into a *hierarchical tree*: valleys within valleys within valleys, each level indexed by an overlap value [5][9].

This is far stronger than clustering. An ordinary clustered landscape permits all relative arrangements of three clusters; ultrametricity forbids the scalene triangle. Parisi conjectured it in the 1980s; it was proved for the SK model only in 2013 by Panchenko, who showed that the Ghirlanda–Guerra identities — exact relations between moments of the overlap distribution, themselves consequences of stochastic stability under small perturbations of the Hamiltonian — force the limiting overlap array to be ultrametric [9].

> **Theorem (Panchenko ultrametricity, 2013):** In the SK and mixed *p*-spin models, the limiting overlap distribution is supported on ultrametric configurations: with probability one, among any three replicas the two largest overlaps coincide [9].

> **Sketch:** The Ghirlanda–Guerra identities imply that the overlap array is *exchangeable* and satisfies a recursive distributional structure (the Dovbysh–Sudakov representation). Combined with the positivity principle (*R_{1,2} ≥ 0* a.s. after a small perturbation), the representation collapses onto Ruelle's probability cascades, which are ultrametric by construction □.

### Ruelle Probability Cascades and the Ghirlanda–Guerra Identities

The probabilistic reformulation of RSB replaces the replica matrix by an explicit random object. *Ruelle's probability cascade* (RPC) generates a random probability measure on a hierarchical tree: each branch carries a Poisson–Dirichlet weight, and the overlap of two leaves is the depth of their last common ancestor [8]. The RPC with parameters given by the Parisi measure reproduces *all* overlap statistics of the SK Gibbs measure. The Aizenman–Sims–Starr *random overlap structures* (ROSt) provide a variational principle — the *AS² scheme* — in which the Parisi formula appears as the infimum over ROSts, attained precisely at the RPC [8].

A Rust-flavored pseudocode for sampling overlaps from a 2-level cascade:

```rust
// Sample two overlaps from a 2-level Ruelle probability cascade
fn sample_cascade(q0: f64, q1: f64, m1: f64) -> (f64, f64, f64) {
    // PD(m1) weights at level 1; leaves share overlap q1 w.p. related to m1
    let same_cluster: bool = rand::random::<f64>() < m1;
    let q12 = if same_cluster { q1 } else { q0 };
    // q13, q23: ultrametricity forces the two largest to coincide
    (q12, q12.max(q0), q12.max(q0))
}
```

The code encodes the theorem: the triple *(q₁₂, q₁₃, q₂₃)* is never scalene.

##5 Empirical Evidence and Formal Proofs

### Experiments and simulations

Real spin glasses exhibit the cusp in the a.c. susceptibility, aging and rejuvenation in thermoremanent magnetization, and memory effects — all consistent with a hierarchical landscape of barriers [11]. Large-scale Monte Carlo on the SK model reproduces the Parisi order function: the measured overlap distribution *P_J(q)* is broad, sample-dependent, and develops the continuous support predicted by full RSB, while three-dimensional Edwards–Anderson simulations remain the battleground between the RSB picture and the rival *droplet* scaling theory [11].

### The rigorous chain

The mathematical vindication is unusually complete for a theory born of such formal methods:

- **Thermodynamic limit.** Guerra and Toninelli proved by interpolation that *(1/N)𝔼 log Z_N* converges, establishing that the object the physicists were computing exists [7][8].
- **Upper bound.** Guerra's 2003 interpolation between the SK Hamiltonian and a hierarchical field with the Parisi structure showed *limsup F_N ≤ 𝒫* for all temperatures [6] — the first rigorous confirmation that the Parisi value bounds the truth from above.
- **The Parisi formula.** Talagrand's 2006 tour de force proved the matching lower bound by constraining replicas to sit in narrow overlap windows around a prescribed Parisi grid and extracting the Parisi recursion as the effective description — establishing *lim F_N = 𝒫* for the SK model and even *p*-spin models [7]. Panchenko extended the formula to mixed *p*-spin models including odd interactions, and the zero-temperature version was established by Auffinger and Chen [8].
- **Ultrametricity.** Panchenko's 2013 proof that the Ghirlanda–Guerra identities imply ultrametricity closed the last structural gap: not only the free energy but the *geometry* of states matches Parisi's prediction [9].

##6 Limitations

The theory above is a triumph of the *mean-field* setting, and its limits are instructive.

1. **Finite dimensions remain open.** The Edwards–Anderson model in three dimensions is the physical case, and whether its low-temperature phase shows RSB (infinitely many pure states, ultrametricity) or follows the droplet picture (two pure states related by spin flip, compact excitations) is still disputed after four decades [11]. The SK limit *d → ∞* need not commute with the thermodynamic limit.
2. **The proofs are static.** The Parisi formula governs equilibrium; the *dynamics* of spin glasses — aging, the fluctuation–dissipation violations, the connection between the static overlap distribution and dynamical heterogeneity — lacks comparable rigor.
3. **Zero temperature is delicate.** The ground-state energy density (−0.7632… per spin for SK) and the *T → 0* limit of *q(x)* required separate analysis [8]; the Parisi PDE degenerates and the functional must be reposed.
4. **Non-Gaussian and sparse disorder.** Real materials have RKKY (oscillatory, long-range) couplings, not Gaussian infinite-range ones; diluted models (Viana–Bray) interpolate toward finite connectivity, where RSB competes with finite-size effects.
5. **The replica method itself.** Despite the rigorous results, the *n → 0* analytic continuation remains a heuristic device: the theorems prove Parisi's *answer* correct, not his *derivation* legitimate. A direct mathematical justification of the replica trick is still missing.
6. **Quantum extensions.** The transverse-field SK model — relevant to quantum annealing — develops its own RSB structure with additional subtleties (the static approximation fails), and the quantum Parisi theory is far less complete.

##7 Conclusion

The spin-glass episode transformed statistical physics twice. First, physically: it established that quenched disorder can generate an equilibrium phase described not by a number but by a *function* — the Parisi order function *q(x)* — and a state space with *ultrametric* geometry, a hierarchical organization of valleys within valleys that no finite-parameter Landau theory could capture [3][4][5]. Second, mathematically: it demonstrated that a formally outrageous analytic continuation, the *n → 0* replica trick, could encode a truth so robust that it took the deepest methods of modern probability — Guerra's interpolation, Talagrand's constrained replicas, Panchenko's identities — to confirm it [6][7][9].

What began as a "solvable model" that was not solved became, over thirty years, the most rigorously understood disordered system in physics — and the template for thinking about optimization, inference, and learning in high-dimensional random landscapes. The finite-dimensional problem stands as the outstanding challenge: whether real three-dimensional spin glasses truly live in Parisi's ultrametric forest, or whether mean field has led us, however beautifully, astray [11].
