# Rough Volatility: The Microstructure of Option Prices, Rough Bergomi Models, and the Geometry of the Implied Volatility Surface

## Abstract

Rough volatility postulates that instantaneous volatility is driven by a fractional Brownian motion with Hurst exponent H well below one half, typically estimated near 0.1 in equity markets. This single regularity parameter reshapes the geometry of the implied volatility surface: it generates the power-law at-the-money skew, S_T proportional to T^{H-1/2}, that classical Markovian models cannot reproduce at the short end. We develop the mathematical architecture of rough volatility — the rough Bergomi model, the rough Heston model, and their Volterra formulations — trace their microstructure foundations in nearly unstable Hawkes order flow, and examine large-deviation geometry of the volatility surface. We survey the estimation evidence for H near 0.1, the multiscaling refinements, and the deep-learning calibration techniques that have made rough models operationally viable. We close with the emerging empirical critique: the pure power-law skew is too steep at one week and too persistent at three years, motivating path-dependent and two-factor alternatives. The result is a panoramic, technically precise account of a decade-long revolution in stochastic volatility modeling.

## 1 Introduction

For most of the history of quantitative finance, stochastic volatility lived in the comfortable world of semimartingales. The Heston model drove variance with a square-root diffusion; the Bergomi family drove the forward variance curve with Ornstein-Uhlenbeck factors. These models are Markovian, their sample paths are Holder-continuous of order one half up to logarithmic corrections, and — as a mathematical consequence — the at-the-money implied volatility skew they generate remains bounded as maturity shrinks to zero. The market disagrees. Since at least the work of Alos, Leon and Vives, it has been known that the equity at-the-money skew explodes like a power law T^{-alpha} with alpha near 0.4 as expiry T approaches zero, a rate that no diffusive stochastic volatility model can produce [3][9].

The *rough volatility* program, launched in its modern form by Gatheral, Jaisson and Rosenbaum, resolves the tension by demoting the regularity of volatility itself [1]. If the logarithm of instantaneous volatility behaves as a fractional Brownian motion with Hurst exponent H well below one half — empirically estimated near 0.1 — then volatility paths are *rougher* than Brownian motion, the Malliavin derivative of volatility explodes near the diagonal, and the skew inherits the explosion at precisely the observed rate, S_T proportional to T^{H-1/2} [2][3]. The same fractional kernel simultaneously reproduces the slow power-law decay of volatility autocorrelations observed in high-frequency data, unifying two stylized facts that classical models treat separately [1].

This thesis develops the full architecture: the rough Bergomi and rough Heston specifications and their Volterra geometry; the microstructure derivation of roughness from nearly unstable Hawkes order flow; the large-deviation description of the surface's wings; the econometric evidence for H near 0.1 and its multiscaling refinements; the numerical machinery — the hybrid simulation scheme and deep-learning calibration — that made rough models deployable; and the sobering recent evidence that the pure rough paradigm, for all its parsimony, is misspecified in the way it bends the smile [8].

## 2 Background

Classical stochastic volatility begins with Heston: under the pricing measure, dS_t / S_t = sqrt(V_t) dW_t with variance following a mean-reverting square-root diffusion, and the Bergomi models instead parametrize the forward variance curve xi_t(T) = E[V_T | F_t] and drive it with one or two Ornstein-Uhlenbeck factors. In every classical specification the driving noise is Brownian, and the implied skew at short maturities converges to a finite limit. The empirical term structure of the SPX at-the-money skew tells a different story: on a log-log plot against maturity, the skew is nearly a straight line with slope approximately -0.4 over maturities from days to months — a genuine power law, not a saturating exponential [2][8].

Fractional Brownian motion B^H with Hurst index H in the open unit interval is the centered Gaussian process with covariance (1/2)(s^{2H} + t^{2H} - |t-s|^{2H}). Its paths are Holder-continuous of any order below H. For H below one half the process is *rough*: rougher than Brownian motion, with negatively correlated increments and a spectral density that piles mass at high frequencies. The Volterra (Riemann-Liouville) representation writes the rough driver as a stochastic convolution,

Y_t = integral from 0 to t of (t-s)^{H-1/2} dW_s,

with a kernel that explodes at s = t when H is below one half. The explosion is integrable — the kernel is locally square-integrable precisely when H is positive — but it destroys the semimartingale property and with it the Markov property: Y_t depends on the entire past of W, a non-Markovian memory structure [2][9].

The microstructure story gives roughness a physical origin. Order flow in modern electronic markets arrives as a self-exciting point process well described by a Hawkes process; Jaisson and Rosenbaum showed that a sequence of *nearly unstable* Hawkes processes — with branching ratio tending to one at a calibrated speed — converges to an integrated rough volatility process, and the associated price converges to a rough Heston model [5]. In this limit, the no-arbitrage price process inherits roughness from the endogeneity of order flow: volatility is rough because trading activity is. The fractional kernel is therefore not an arbitrary modeling choice but the scaling limit of a micro-founded feedback loop.

## 3 Methodology

The rough Bergomi (rBergomi) model of Bayer, Friz and Gatheral is the workhorse specification [2]:

- dS_t / S_t = sqrt(v_t) dZ_t
- v_t = xi_0(t) exp(eta Y_t - (1/2) eta^2 t^{2H})
- Y_t = integral from 0 to t of (t-s)^{H-1/2} dW_s

with Z and W Brownian motions of correlation rho, xi_0(t) the initial forward variance curve, eta the volatility of volatility, and H in (0, 1/2) the roughness parameter. The exponential (Wick) form guarantees positivity and makes the forward variance curve a martingale by construction. A first-order expansion in eta yields the celebrated skew formula

S_T approx rho eta / (2(H + 1/2)(H + 3/2)) * T^{H-1/2},

so that the skew explodes at the short end with exponent H - 1/2 and decays as a power law at the long end, the *same* parameter H controlling both regimes [2].

The rough Heston model replaces the exponential with a square-root Volterra structure [4]:

V_t = V_0 + integral K(t-s) lambda (theta - V_s) ds + integral K(t-s) nu sqrt(V_s) dB_s,

with kernel K(u) = u^{H-1/2} / Gamma(H + 1/2). Its great analytical advantage is the fractional Riccati equation: the characteristic function of log S_T is available in closed form up to the solution of a deterministic fractional differential equation, enabling Fourier pricing at a cost comparable to classical Heston [4]. Both models are unified by the Volterra point of view: volatility is a convolution of a singular kernel against a driving noise, and the kernel's singularity at zero is the geometric source of everything that follows.

Simulation proceeds by discretizing the Volterra integral. Naive Cholesky factorization of the fractional Brownian covariance matrix costs O(n^3) and is exact but prohibitive; the hybrid scheme of Bennedsen, Lunde and Pakkanen approximates the kernel by a power function near the singularity and a Riemann sum elsewhere, achieving near-exact weak convergence at O(n log n) cost when combined with FFT-based convolution [9]. Calibration, historically the bottleneck — Monte Carlo pricing of each surface is far too slow for iterative optimization — was unlocked by the two-step deep-learning approach of Bayer, Horvath, Muguruza, Stemper and Tomas: first learn the pricing map from parameters to implied volatilities with a neural network trained on simulated data, then run classical optimizers against the fast surrogate [6].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Volterra kernel and the geometry of roughness

The kernel K(t) = t^{H-1/2} is the entire story in miniature. For H below one half it is unbounded at the origin, so the most recent innovations dW_s receive overwhelming weight: volatility *remembers the immediate past intensely and the distant past faintly*, the opposite of the exponential kernel's gentle forgetting. The Holder exponent of the resulting paths is H, meaning the graph of a simulated path has box-counting dimension 2 - H, strictly larger than the 3/2 of Brownian motion — the paths are geometrically *thicker* at fine scales. This fractal thickness is visible to the naked eye in any simulation panel: rough paths look jagged at every magnification, a self-similarity inherited from the scaling property that Y_{ct} is equal in law to c^H Y_t [2][9].

The singularity also governs the Malliavin derivative. Formally differentiating the Volterra representation gives D_s Y_t = (t-s)^{H-1/2} on the event s <= t, so the derivative explodes as s approaches t from below. Fukasawa's analysis shows that the short-end implied volatility skew is, to leading order, a functional of precisely this derivative: the skew explodes if and only if the Malliavin derivative of volatility is singular at the diagonal [3]. Roughness is therefore not a cosmetic path property; it is the *unique* mechanism, within the Gaussian stochastic volatility class, that transmits microscopic path irregularity into a macroscopic, tradeable object — the slope of the smile.

### 4.2 From Hawkes order flow to rough Heston

Markets do not trade in continuous time; they print orders. Let N be a Hawkes process with intensity lambda_t = mu + integral from 0 to t of phi(t-s) dN_s, where the kernel phi encodes self-excitation: each trade begets further trades. In the *nearly unstable* regime the L1 norm of phi approaches one, so the process sits at the edge of criticality — each order generates on average nearly one child order, and clusters of activity cascade across time scales. Jaisson and Rosenbaum proved that under a suitable rescaling, the integrated intensity converges to a fractional integral of a Brownian motion, and the tick-by-tick price converges to a rough Heston-type limit [5].

This derivation matters for two reasons. *First*, it *explains* roughness rather than postulating it: the exponent H is tied to the tail of the Hawkes kernel, linking an estimable microstructure quantity (the decay of order-flow autocorrelation) to the Hurst parameter of the macroscopic volatility model. *Second*, it disciplines the modeling: any rough specification that cannot be obtained as a scaling limit of a reasonable order-flow model should be treated with suspicion. The Hawkes foundation also explains the observed *multiscaling* — the finding that log-volatility increments are not perfectly monofractal but exhibit a spectrum of scaling exponents [7] — since real order flow is a superposition of agents operating at heterogeneous horizons.

### 4.3 Large deviations and the wings of the surface

The at-the-money skew describes the smile's slope at the center; the *wings* — deep out-of-the-money puts and calls — are governed by large deviations. For rough volatility, the small-time large-deviation principle gives a rate function expressed through the reproducing kernel Hilbert space of the fractional driver, and the implied volatility smile satisfies short-maturity asymptotics in which the rate function encodes the energy of the most likely rough path producing a given log-strike [9]. The geometry is striking: the rate function inherits the fractional scaling, so the wings decay as a power of log-strike with an exponent determined by H, producing the famously *steep* short-dated wings that traders associate with crash risk.

Practically, this means the model prices far-tail options — the domain of variance swaps, VIX derivatives, and tail hedges — with a parsimony classical models cannot match: one parameter H shapes the center slope, the wing curvature, and the autocorrelation decay simultaneously. The joint SPX-VIX calibration problem exposes the limits of this parsimony, however; matching both the equity smile and VIX options typically forces rho toward -1 and H toward zero, a tension formalized in recent joint-calibration studies [10].

### 4.4 Deep learning and the calibration bottleneck

A model that cannot be calibrated cannot be traded. For rough Bergomi, every pricing evaluation requires Monte Carlo simulation of a non-Markovian Volterra process — minutes per surface, hopeless for iterative least-squares over hundreds of quotes. The deep-calibration program inverts the workflow:

1. Simulate a large training set of parameter-surface pairs *offline* with the hybrid scheme.
2. Train a neural network to approximate the pricing map mu -> sigma_IV(mu).
3. Calibrate by minimizing the distance between market quotes and the network's output with Levenberg-Marquardt iterations [6].

Because the network is differentiable, Jacobians come essentially for free, and the full-surface calibration runs in milliseconds. Subsequent refinements replaced fixed grids with random-grid pointwise training, removing interpolation error, and extended the approach to the rough Heston model where the fractional Riccati solver serves as a fast benchmark [6]. The methodology generalizes beyond roughness: it is now the standard template for any model whose pricing map is expensive but simulable, from rough volatility to signature-based models.

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

The empirical case for roughness rests on three pillars. *First*, the scaling of log-volatility increments. Using power-variation estimators on high-frequency realized-volatility proxies across thousands of equities, Gatheral, Jaisson and Rosenbaum estimated the smoothness of log-volatility at approximately 0.1, decisively below the one-half of Brownian motion; Fukasawa, Takabatake and Westphal, using refined estimators robust to microstructure noise, found volatility to be *even rougher* than the early estimates suggested [1][7]. *Second*, the ATM skew term structure. Fitting the power law S_T approx A T^{-alpha} to SPX skews yields alpha near 0.4, i.e. H near 0.1, consistently across a decade of data [2][8]. *Third*, the autocorrelation of volatility proxies decays as a power law tau^{2H-1}, matching the fractional kernel's prediction with the same H [1].

> Theorem: (Fukasawa). Suppose instantaneous volatility is driven by a fractional Brownian motion with Hurst index H in (0, 1/2), with a non-degenerate leverage correlation. Then the at-the-money implied volatility skew satisfies S_T = c T^{H-1/2} (1 + o(1)) as T -> 0 for an explicit constant c, and the rate T^{H-1/2} is unattainable by any stochastic volatility model whose volatility has Holder regularity exceeding one half [3].

The proof proceeds by martingale expansion: the implied volatility is expanded in the volatility-of-volatility parameter, the skew emerges as a conditional expectation involving the Malliavin derivative D_s sigma_t, and the fractional kernel's singularity (t-s)^{H-1/2} integrates to the claimed rate. The argument is sharp: it shows simultaneously that rough models *must* produce the explosion and that regular models *cannot* [3][9].

```python
import numpy as np

def rbergomi_paths(n_paths, n_steps, T, H, eta, rho, xi0):
    dt = T / n_steps
    t = np.linspace(dt, T, n_steps)
    # Volterra kernel of the Riemann-Liouville fBm; singular at the diagonal
    K = np.subtract.outer(t, t)
    K = np.where(K > 0, K ** (H - 0.5), 0.0)
    L = np.linalg.cholesky(K @ K.T * dt + 1e-12 * np.eye(n_steps))
    Z = np.random.randn(n_steps, n_paths)
    Y = L @ Z * np.sqrt(dt)
    drift = 0.5 * eta ** 2 * t ** (2 * H)
    v = xi0 * np.exp(eta * Y - drift[:, None])
    dW = np.random.randn(n_steps, n_paths) * np.sqrt(dt)
    dZ = rho * dW + np.sqrt(1 - rho ** 2) * np.random.randn(n_steps, n_paths) * np.sqrt(dt)
    logS = np.cumsum(np.sqrt(v) * dZ - 0.5 * v * dt, axis=0)
    return np.vstack((np.zeros(n_paths), logS)), v
```

```rust
/// Power-law Volterra kernel; singular at tau = 0 when h < 0.5.
fn volterra_kernel(h: f64, tau: f64) -> f64 {
    assert!(h > 0.0 && h < 0.5, "roughness regime required");
    tau.powf(h - 0.5)
}

/// Hybrid-scheme weight: exact power law near the singularity,
/// Riemann sum in the regular region.
fn hybrid_weight(h: f64, tau: f64, cutoff: f64) -> f64 {
    if tau < cutoff { volterra_kernel(h, tau) } else { tau.powf(h - 0.5) }
}
```

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

The empirical case for roughness rests on three pillars. *First*, the scaling of log-volatility increments. Using power-variation estimators on high-frequency realized-volatility proxies across thousands of equities, Gatheral, Jaisson and Rosenbaum estimated the smoothness of log-volatility at approximately 0.1, decisively below the one-half of Brownian motion; Fukasawa, Takabatake and Westphal, using refined estimators robust to microstructure noise, found volatility to be *even rougher* than the early estimates suggested [1][7]. *Second*, the ATM skew term structure. Fitting the power law S_T approx A T^{-alpha} to SPX skews yields alpha near 0.4, i.e. H near 0.1, consistently across a decade of data [2][8]. *Third*, the autocorrelation of volatility proxies decays as a power law tau^{2H-1}, matching the fractional kernel's prediction with the same H [1].

> Theorem: (Fukasawa). Suppose instantaneous volatility is driven by a fractional Brownian motion with Hurst index H in (0, 1/2), with a non-degenerate leverage correlation. Then the at-the-money implied volatility skew satisfies S_T = c T^{H-1/2} (1 + o(1)) as T -> 0 for an explicit constant c, and the rate T^{H-1/2} is unattainable by any stochastic volatility model whose volatility has Holder regularity exceeding one half [3].

The proof proceeds by martingale expansion: the implied volatility is expanded in the volatility-of-volatility parameter, the skew emerges as a conditional expectation involving the Malliavin derivative D_s sigma_t, and the fractional kernel's singularity (t-s)^{H-1/2} integrates to the claimed rate. The argument is sharp: it shows simultaneously that rough models *must* produce the explosion and that regular models *cannot* [3][9].

```python
import numpy as np

def rbergomi_paths(n_paths, n_steps, T, H, eta, rho, xi0):
    dt = T / n_steps
    t = np.linspace(dt, T, n_steps)
    # Volterra kernel of the Riemann-Liouville fBm; singular at the diagonal
    K = np.subtract.outer(t, t)
    K = np.where(K > 0, K ** (H - 0.5), 0.0)
    L = np.linalg.cholesky(K @ K.T * dt + 1e-12 * np.eye(n_steps))
    Z = np.random.randn(n_steps, n_paths)
    Y = L @ Z * np.sqrt(dt)
    drift = 0.5 * eta ** 2 * t ** (2 * H)
    v = xi0 * np.exp(eta * Y - drift[:, None])
    dW = np.random.randn(n_steps, n_paths) * np.sqrt(dt)
    dZ = rho * dW + np.sqrt(1 - rho ** 2) * np.random.randn(n_steps, n_paths) * np.sqrt(dt)
    logS = np.cumsum(np.sqrt(v) * dZ - 0.5 * v * dt, axis=0)
    return np.vstack((np.zeros(n_paths), logS)), v
```

```rust
fn volterra_kernel(h: f64, tau: f64) -> f64 {

/// Riemann sum in the regular region.
fn hybrid_weight(h: f64, tau: f64, cutoff: f64) -> f64 {
    if tau < cutoff { volterra_kernel(h, tau) } else { tau.powf(h - 0.5) }
```

## 6 Limitations

The rough paradigm's parsimony is also its vulnerability. A comprehensive 2011-2022 SPX study compared rough Bergomi against its Markovian and path-dependent cousins and delivered an uncomfortable verdict: the rough model's skew increases *too fast* at the short end and decays *too slowly* at the long end; a single power law cannot fit the whole term structure, and calibrations routinely demand a *negative* Hurst exponent at long maturities — outside the model's admissible range [8]. The leverage correlation rho saturates near -1, a known structural pathology of the exponential rBergomi form. For short maturities the one-factor Markovian Bergomi model with the same number of parameters fits better; for long maturities an under-parametrized two-factor Markovian model dominates outright [8].

The computational burden remains real: non-Markovianity rules out PDE methods, Monte Carlo is expensive, and the neural surrogates that fix calibration introduce their own approximation risk [6]. The microstructure derivation, while elegant, assumes a single Hawkes kernel tail; real order flow is multi-scale, and the monofractal assumption is rejected by multifractal tests on several datasets [7]. Finally, the joint SPX-VIX problem — pricing equity and volatility derivatives consistently — stretches every rough specification to its breaking point, with parameters pushed to degenerate corners [10]. The emerging consensus is not that roughness is wrong but that it is *incomplete*: the data seem to want roughness at the short end grafted onto richer, possibly path-dependent or multi-factor, dynamics at longer horizons [8][9].

## 7 Conclusion

Rough volatility redefined what a stochastic volatility model is allowed to be: not a diffusion with smooth coefficients, but a Volterra process whose singular kernel encodes, in a single exponent H, the jaggedness of volatility paths, the explosion of the short-dated skew, the slow decay of volatility memory, and — via the Hawkes limit — the endogeneity of market microstructure [1][2][5]. Its mathematical contributions are durable regardless of the empirical debate: the fractional Riccati machinery of rough Heston, the Malliavin characterization of the skew, the large-deviation geometry of the wings, and the deep-learning calibration template have all entered the permanent toolkit [3][4][6]. The open frontier is synthesis — models that preserve the rough short end while accommodating the market's refusal to follow a single power law across the full surface [8]. Whether the resolution is path-dependent Bergomi, multi-factor Markovian lifts, or genuinely multifractal drivers, the rough decade has permanently raised the bar: any serious volatility model must now answer for the geometry of the smile at every scale.
