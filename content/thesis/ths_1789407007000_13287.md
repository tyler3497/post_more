# Two-Sided Matching Markets: Gale–Shapley Deferred Acceptance, the Core, and the NRMP Residency Match

## Abstract

Two-sided matching markets allocate indivisible goods where prices cannot clear the market: medical residents to hospitals, children to public schools, donor kidneys to patients. This thesis develops the theory and engineering of such markets. We prove that a stable matching always exists via the Gale–Shapley deferred acceptance algorithm (1962) [1], characterize the distributive lattice of stable matchings with its proposer-optimal and receiver-optimal extremes, and show the coincidence of the core with the set of stable matchings in the Shapley–Scarf housing model [2]. We prove strategy-proofness for the proposing side [3] and Roth's impossibility result: no stable mechanism is strategy-proof for both sides. The theory is then engineered into practice: the redesign of the National Resident Matching Program [8], the replacement of the manipulable Boston mechanism by deferred acceptance in school choice [6], and the top-trading-cycles machinery behind large-scale kidney exchange [7]. Complexity bounds, the rural hospitals theorem, and the boundary case of couples with complementary preferences complete the analysis.

## 1 Introduction

Most of economic theory presumes that prices mediate exchange. Yet some of the most consequential allocations in modern life are made **without prices at all**: which medical resident trains at which hospital, which child attends which public school, whose donated kidney saves whose life. These are **two-sided matching markets** — environments in which agents on two sides of a market hold preferences over the other side, and the outcome is a *matching*: an assignment pairing agents across the divide [4].

The intellectual origin of the field is a 1962 paper by David Gale and Lloyd Shapley, *College Admissions and the Stability of Marriage* [1]. Gale and Shapley asked a deceptively simple question: given strict preferences on both sides, can agents always be paired so that **no unmatched pair would both prefer each other to their assigned partners**? A matching with no such *blocking pair* is called **stable**, and stability turns out to be the load-bearing concept of the entire discipline. An unstable matching is fragile — the blocking pair has both the incentive and, typically, the means to defect — while a stable matching is self-enforcing.

Gale and Shapley's answer was affirmative and constructive: the **deferred acceptance algorithm** always produces a stable matching, in time quadratic in the number of agents. Half a century later, the 2012 Nobel Prize in Economic Sciences was awarded to Lloyd Shapley and Alvin Roth precisely for the theory of stable allocations and the practice of market design that grew from this seed [8].

This thesis develops the subject as a unified whole:

1. **Existence and structure.** The deferred acceptance algorithm and the lattice structure of the set of stable matchings.
2. **Incentives.** Strategy-proofness for the proposing side, and the sharp impossibility theorem bounding what no mechanism can achieve.
3. **The core.** The cooperative-game solution concept and its coincidence with stable matchings in exchange economies [2].
4. **Engineering.** How the theory was deployed — and where it broke — in the NRMP residency match, school choice, and kidney exchange.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The marriage model

Let \(M = \{m_1, \dots, m_n\}\) and \(W = \{w_1, \dots, w_n\}\) be two disjoint finite sets of agents. Each agent holds a **strict preference ordering** over the agents on the other side. A **matching** \(\mu\) is a bijection pairing each agent with exactly one partner on the other side. A pair \((m, w)\) **blocks** \(\mu\) if \(m\) prefers \(w\) to \(\mu(m)\) and \(w\) prefers \(m\) to \(\mu(w)\). A matching with no blocking pair is **stable** [1].

The model extends naturally. In the **college admissions problem**, colleges have quotas and *responsive* preferences over sets of students: a college's ranking of individual students extends to groups in the natural way, preferring to replace a worse student with a better one whenever capacity allows [4]. In the **hospital–resident problem**, residents are the proposing side and hospitals hold multiple positions. The same stability definition governs all these variants.

> **Definition (Stability).** A matching \(\mu\) is *stable* if it is individually rational — no agent prefers being unmatched to her assigned partner — and admits no blocking pair.

### 2.2 Why stability matters

Roth's empirical work [5] provides the decisive argument: across dozens of entry-level professional labor markets, **clearinghouses that produce stable matchings succeed, and those that produce unstable matchings fail**. Markets with unstable mechanisms unravel — participants contract early, outside the system, to avoid being trapped in an allocation someone can block. Stability is thus not merely a normative desideratum; it is a *positive* predictor of whether a centralized market survives at all.

### 2.3 Cooperative foundations: the core

In cooperative game theory, the **core** is the set of allocations no coalition can improve upon by seceding and reallocating among themselves. Shapley and Scarf [2] studied the simplest exchange economy — the *housing market*, in which each agent owns one indivisible house and seeks to trade — and showed that the core is always nonempty, characterized by the **top trading cycles** algorithm. The connection to matching is profound: in the marriage model, the core *coincides* with the set of stable matchings. A blocking pair is precisely a two-agent coalition that can do better on its own.

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 The deferred acceptance algorithm

The algorithm proceeds in rounds. One side proposes; the other side disposes — but only provisionally.

**Man-proposing deferred acceptance** [1]:

1. Each unengaged man proposes to the most preferred woman to whom he has not yet proposed.
2. Each woman *tentatively* accepts ("holds") her most preferred proposer so far and rejects the rest.
3. Rejected men propose again in the next round. Engagements are provisional until a round passes with no new proposals.

The key insight is the word *deferred*: no acceptance is final until the algorithm terminates, so a woman can always "trade up" to a better proposer who arrives later. Termination is guaranteed because each man proposes to each woman at most once.

> **Theorem (Gale–Shapley existence theorem).** For every profile of strict preferences, the deferred acceptance algorithm terminates in at most \(n^2\) proposals and produces a stable matching [1].

*Proof sketch.* Suppose \((m, w)\) blocks the output \(\mu\). Since \(m\) prefers \(w\) to \(\mu(m)\), \(m\) must have proposed to \(w\) before \(\mu(m)\) and been rejected — meaning \(w\) held someone she preferred to \(m\) at that moment, and her held partner can only improve over time. Hence \(w\) cannot prefer \(m\) to \(\mu(w)\), a contradiction. ∎

### 3.2 A working implementation

The algorithm is simple enough to implement exactly, which we do below for verification:

```python
def gale_shapley(proposers_prefs, receivers_prefs):
    """Man-proposing deferred acceptance.

    proposers_prefs: dict proposer -> list of receivers, most preferred first.
    receivers_prefs: dict receiver -> list of proposers, most preferred first.
    Returns dict proposer -> receiver (the proposer-optimal stable matching).
    """
    free = list(proposers_prefs.keys())
    next_idx = {p: 0 for p in proposers_prefs}
    engaged = {}  # receiver -> proposer (tentative)
    while free:
        p = free.pop(0)
        prefs = proposers_prefs[p]
        r = prefs[next_idx[p]]      # next receiver not yet proposed to
        next_idx[p] += 1
        if r not in engaged:
            engaged[r] = p
        else:
            q = engaged[r]
            rank = receivers_prefs[r]
            if rank.index(p) < rank.index(q):
                engaged[r] = p      # receiver trades up
                free.append(q)      # jilted proposer returns to pool
            else:
                free.append(p)
    return {p: r for r, p in engaged.items()}


def is_stable(match, proposers_prefs, receivers_prefs):
    inv = {r: p for p, r in match.items()}
    for p, plist in proposers_prefs.items():
        for r in plist[:plist.index(match[p])]:  # receivers p prefers to partner
            q = inv[r]
            if receivers_prefs[r].index(p) < receivers_prefs[r].index(q):
                return False, (p, r)  # blocking pair
    return True, None


# Demo: three men, three women
mp = {'m1': ['w2', 'w1', 'w3'],
      'm2': ['w1', 'w2', 'w3'],
      'm3': ['w1', 'w2', 'w3']}
wp = {'w1': ['m2', 'm3', 'm1'],
      'w2': ['m1', 'm2', 'm3'],
      'w3': ['m1', 'm2', 'm3']}
mu = gale_shapley(mp, wp)
print(mu)                       # {'m1': 'w2', 'm2': 'w1', 'm3': 'w3'}
print(is_stable(mu, mp, wp))     # (True, None) -- no blocking pair
```

The demo confirms the theory: the proposing side's first choices are honored whenever receivers agree, and the output admits no blocking pair.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The lattice of stable matchings and proposer optimality

The set of stable matchings is not a shapeless cloud — it carries rich algebraic structure. Define a partial order: \(\mu \succeq_M \mu'\) if every man weakly prefers his partner under \(\mu\) to his partner under \(\mu'\). Under this order, the set of stable matchings forms a **finite distributive lattice** [4]. The join of two stable matchings gives each man the better of his two partners; the meet gives each woman the better of hers — and both operations preserve stability.

The lattice has two distinguished extremes:

- \(\mu_M\), the **man-optimal** stable matching: every man does weakly better here than in any other stable matching.
- \(\mu_W\), the **woman-optimal** stable matching, defined symmetrically.

The deferred acceptance algorithm, when men propose, outputs exactly \(\mu_M\) — a fact with sharp incentive consequences. The proposing side is systematically favored: no stable mechanism can do better for the proposers, and the receiving side gets its *worst* stable outcome. This **polarization** is why the choice of proposing side is the single most consequential design decision in any real clearinghouse — it is what moved the NRMP from program-proposing to applicant-proposing.

### 4.2 Strategy-proofness and the impossibility frontier

A mechanism is **strategy-proof** if reporting true preferences is a dominant strategy: no agent can ever gain by lying. Roth proved the central positive result [3]:

> **Theorem (Roth, 1982).** The proposer-proposing deferred acceptance mechanism is strategy-proof for the proposing side. No proposer can ever obtain a better partner by misreporting preferences [3].

The intuition runs through the lattice: a man who truncates or permutes his list can only shrink the set of stable matchings computed from reported preferences, and the man-optimal matching of a shrunken lattice cannot beat the true man-optimal outcome.

But the frontier is hard. Roth also proved the matching impossibility theorem [3]:

> **Theorem (Roth's impossibility).** There exists **no** mechanism that always produces a stable matching and is strategy-proof for *all* agents on *both* sides [3].

In other words, stability and universal incentive-compatibility are jointly unattainable. Every stable mechanism is manipulable by *some* agent in *some* market. The design question is therefore never "can we make everyone truthful?" but "*which side* do we protect?" — and in labor and school markets, the answer is invariably the side that must be induced to participate voluntarily: the applicants and students.

### 4.3 The core, the housing market, and Shapley–Scarf

Consider the **housing market** of Shapley and Scarf [2]: \(n\) agents, each endowed with one indivisible house, each with strict preferences over all houses. The **top trading cycles (TTC)** algorithm proceeds by having each agent point to the owner of her favorite remaining house; the resulting directed graph always contains a cycle, the agents in each cycle trade, and the process repeats. Shapley and Scarf proved the outcome lies in the **core** — no coalition can reallocate its own endowments to make all its members strictly better off — and Roth later showed this core allocation is unique and that TTC is strategy-proof [3].

The marriage model is the two-sided analogue: the core of the associated cooperative game coincides exactly with the set of stable matchings. A blocking pair is a two-person coalition that blocks the core allocation. This coincidence unifies the non-cooperative algorithmic view (deferred acceptance) with the cooperative view (the core): stability *is* core membership, and Gale–Shapley *is* a core-selection algorithm.

TTC's strategy-proofness plus Pareto efficiency made it the natural engine for markets where one side holds endowments rather than preferences — which is precisely the structure of **kidney exchange**, where each patient–donor pair "owns" a kidney and compatibility, not preference intensity, governs trade [7].

### 4.4 From theory to clearinghouses: NRMP, school choice, kidney exchange

**The NRMP residency match.** Before 1952, the U.S. market for medical interns unraveled catastrophically: hospitals made exploding offers to students years before graduation. The 1952 clearinghouse (the Stalknaker–Mullen algorithm) restored order, and Roth [5] showed it was an early, independently discovered instance of deferred acceptance — explaining its five-decade survival where unstable clearinghouses collapsed. But two pressures accumulated: the algorithm was *program-proposing*, favoring hospitals, and **couples** — pairs of residents seeking two positions in the same city — introduced complementarities that can destroy the existence of stable matchings entirely.

In 1995 Roth was asked to direct a redesign; with Elliott Peranson he produced an **applicant-proposing** algorithm with a sequential instability-chaining procedure to accommodate couples [8]. The NRMP adopted it in 1997 (first used for the 1998 match). Empirically, the switch of proposing sides barely moved outcomes — on the order of one resident in a thousand changed programs — because real preference profiles admit few stable matchings. But the *incentive* properties changed decisively: with applicants proposing, no applicant can profit from misreporting her rank-order list.

**School choice.** Boston historically used the **Boston mechanism**: first choices are assigned first and permanently, so ranking a popular school first is costly if you fail — strategists rank "safe" schools first, punishing honest families. Abdulkadiroğlu and Sönmez [6] observed that school priorities have the formal structure of school preferences, so two-sided mechanisms apply; they showed the student-proposing DA mechanism eliminates *justified envy* (the school-choice analogue of stability), is strategy-proof for students, and Pareto-dominates every other envy-free matching. Boston adopted DA in 2005; New York City's high-school match had adopted it in 2003–04.

| Property | Boston mechanism | Student-proposing DA |
|---|---|---|
| Truth-telling safe for families | No — gaming required | Yes (strategy-proof) [6] |
| Eliminates justified envy | No | Yes |
| Rewards strategic sophistication | Yes (inequitable) | No |
| Adopted | Boston (pre-2005) | Boston 2005, NYC 2003 |

**Kidney exchange.** A patient with a willing but immunologically incompatible donor can swap donors with another such pair. Roth, Sönmez, and Ünver [7] modeled this as a housing-market problem and designed a generalized TTC mechanism — the TTCC mechanism — handling both cycles of pairs and *chains* initiated by non-directed (altruistic) donors or by pairs accepting priority on the deceased-donor waitlist. Their simulations showed large welfare gains from scaling beyond two-way swaps, and the design became the template for the New England Program for Kidney Exchange and, eventually, national kidney paired-donation clearinghouses.

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 The rural hospitals theorem

A striking structural result constrains every stable matching simultaneously:

> **Theorem (Rural hospitals).** The set of agents left unmatched is identical across *all* stable matchings; moreover, any hospital that fails to fill its quota in one stable matching fills exactly the same positions in every stable matching [5].

No mechanism, however clever, can place more residents in rural hospitals than the stable matchings allow — the shortfall is a property of preferences, not of market design. Policy aimed at rural staffing must change preferences (e.g., through subsidies), not algorithms.

### 5.2 How many stable matchings?

For random preferences with \(n\) agents per side, the expected number of stable matchings grows roughly like \(e^{-1} n \ln n\), while adversarial instances admit exponentially many; counting them is **#P-complete** [4]. Yet finding the two extreme points of the lattice costs only \(O(n^2)\) proposals. The lattice can be exponentially large while its endpoints remain trivially computable — a rare and fortunate complexity profile.

### 5.3 Large-market evidence

The NRMP matches tens of thousands of residents yearly; the applicant-proposing redesign changed roughly one match in a thousand, confirming the theoretical prediction that the lattice collapses when one side's preferences are nearly aligned [8]. In school choice, New York City's DA-based high-school match places all but a few thousand students annually and — crucially — students now report true preferences, giving administrators honest demand data for planning school closures and expansions [6]. Kidney exchange clearinghouses now routinely arrange three-way and longer exchanges plus non-simultaneous chains, with thousands of transplants attributable to the mechanism [7].

### 5.4 Couples: the boundary of the theory

Couples seeking geographically co-located positions hold preferences over *pairs* of positions, violating the responsiveness condition that guarantees existence. Stable matchings may not exist, and deciding existence is **NP-complete** [4]. The Roth–Peranson instability-chaining algorithm [8] resolves instabilities sequentially and succeeds in practice — couples are a small fraction of the market — but the complexity result marks a genuine boundary: complementarities break the elegant structure of Sections 4.1–4.3, and no general existence theorem can be recovered.

## 6 Limitations

The theory's power rests on assumptions that real markets only approximate.

- **Strict, complete, static preferences.** Real rank-order lists are truncated (applicants rank a subset), and indifferences are broken arbitrarily; truncation is itself a strategic act, though Roth and Rothblum showed limited truncation is safe under DA.
- **No transfers.** Wages and prices are excluded by design. Where compensation is flexible, the Kelso–Crawford auction framework or Hatfield–Milgrom matching with contracts generalizes stability — but at the cost of simplicity.
- **One-shot static matching.** Markets with arrivals over time (kidney exchange pools, school waitlists) need dynamic mechanisms; batching cadence becomes a design variable trading off match quality against waiting time.
- **Couples and complementarities.** As shown in Section 5.4, existence fails and computation becomes intractable; practical success relies on couples being rare.
- **Equity versus efficiency.** DA's student-optimal stable matching can be Pareto-dominated for students by an unstable matching [6]; strategy-proofness is purchased with a measurable efficiency cost, and the Boston mechanism's defenders historically pointed to exactly this trade-off.

## 7 Conclusion

Two-sided matching theory is the rare body of economic thought that traveled intact from blackboard to bureaucracy. The logical chain is unusually tight: a stability concept motivated by the fragility of handshake agreements [1]; a constructive algorithm with optimal incentive properties for one side [3]; a sharp impossibility result delineating what no mechanism can do [3]; a cooperative foundation in the core [2]; and a series of engineered clearinghouses — for residents [8], schoolchildren [6], and kidney patients [7] — that succeed exactly where the theory says they should and fail exactly where it says they must.

The open frontiers are where the classical assumptions break: complementarities from couples, dynamic arrivals, and the integration of monetary transfers without destroying incentive properties. But the core lesson endures. When prices cannot do the allocative work — because they are forbidden, repugnant, or simply absent — *stability* is the property that lets a market hold together, and *deferred acceptance* is the procedure that finds it. Gale and Shapley's 1962 algorithm remains, more than six decades on, the most successful piece of applied game theory ever deployed.
