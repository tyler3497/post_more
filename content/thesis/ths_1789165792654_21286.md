# Programmatic Weak Supervision: Data Programming, Label Models, and the Statistical Theory of Noisy Label Aggregation

## Abstract

Programmatic weak supervision replaces hand-labeled training data with *labeling functions* — user-authored heuristics, patterns, knowledge bases, and distant-supervision rules that emit noisy, conflicting, or abstaining votes over unlabeled data. The central statistical problem is to recover the accuracies and dependency structure of these sources *without any ground truth*, and to aggregate their votes into probabilistic training labels that can be consumed by a noise-aware end model. This thesis develops the full arc of that theory: the **data programming** generative model of Ratner et al. [1], which encodes labeling propensity, accuracy, and pairwise correlations in a factor graph learned by maximizing the marginal likelihood of the observed label matrix; its lineage from the classical Dawid–Skene EM model of crowdsourced annotation [4]; closed-form **triplet** estimators that recover source accuracies from pairwise agreements under conditional independence; **structure learning** of labeling-function dependencies without labeled data [2]; multi-task extensions via matrix completion [6]; and generalization theory showing that end models trained on aggregated probabilistic labels converge at the same asymptotic rate as fully supervised training [1][2]. We synthesize empirical evidence from the TAC-KBP slot-filling challenge and the WRENCH benchmark of 22 datasets [7], discuss noise-aware losses and calibration, and close with the open frontiers of weak supervision: distribution shift, adversarial label functions, and foundation-model-in-the-loop supervision.

## 1. Introduction

The dominant cost of applied machine learning is not modeling — it is *supervision*. For deep learning in particular, the appetite for labeled examples is effectively unbounded, while the supply of hand-labeled data is bounded by annotator time, expertise, and budget. In specialized domains — biomedical relation extraction, chemical entity recognition, legal document classification — labels require expert annotators and can cost dollars per example, making million-example training sets infeasible [1][2].

**Weak supervision** is the umbrella term for learning from supervision sources that are cheaper but noisier than hand labels: heuristic rules, distant supervision from knowledge bases, pre-trained models used as annotators, and crowd workers of unknown reliability. *Programmatic* weak supervision, introduced as **data programming** by Ratner et al. [1], makes a decisive methodological move: instead of writing labels, users write *labeling functions* (LFs) — small programs $\lambda_j : \mathcal{X} \to \mathcal{Y} \cup \{\emptyset\}$ that vote on data points, where $\emptyset$ denotes abstention. A spam classifier might use LFs such as "contains the phrase *free money* → spam," "sender in contacts → ham," and "matches known phishing URL patterns → spam." Individually these are noisy and may conflict; collectively, their *agreements and disagreements* carry enough signal to estimate each source's accuracy — without ever seeing a ground-truth label.

> **Theorem (informal, Ratner et al. [1]):** Given a sufficiently rich family of labeling functions and an asymptotically large unlabeled set, the parameters of the data-programming generative model — including each source's accuracy — are *identifiable* from the observed label matrix alone, and the resulting probabilistic labels can train an end model whose generalization error decays at the same asymptotic rate as in the fully supervised setting.

This claim, once startling, is now the foundation of an entire ecosystem — the Snorkel system [2][3], the FlyingSquid triplet estimators, the MeTaL multi-task framework [6], and the WRENCH benchmark [7] that standardizes evaluation. This thesis reconstructs the theory from first principles: the generative label model and its estimation (Sections 3–4), the statistical guarantees that justify the paradigm (Section 5), and its limits (Section 6).

## 2. Background

### 2.1 The supervision bottleneck and its predecessors

Weak supervision did not begin with data programming. Three intellectual lineages converge in the label model:

1. **Crowdsourcing aggregation.** Dawid and Skene [4] posed the problem of estimating each observer's error rates from noisy categorical judgments when the true response is unobserved. Their EM algorithm treats the true label as latent and iteratively re-estimates observer confusion matrices — the direct ancestor of every modern label model. The crucial difference: crowd workers are humans whose errors are roughly independent, whereas programmatic LFs are *authored by a single developer* and inherit systematic, correlated biases [1].
2. **Distant and heuristic supervision.** Mintz et al.'s distant supervision aligns knowledge-base facts with text; pattern-based extractors (e.g., Hearst patterns) trade precision for recall. These are labeling functions *avant la lettre*, but they were used directly as training labels, with no principled denoising.
3. **Noise-robust learning.** The statistical learning literature on label noise (random classification noise, Natarajan et al.'s unbiased loss corrections, forward/backward loss correction) studies learning *given* a known or estimable noise model. Data programming inverts the emphasis: the noise model itself is learned, jointly, from unlabeled data.

### 2.2 The weak supervision taxonomy

Following Zhou's survey [5] and the WRENCH benchmark [7], weak supervision decomposes into three orthogonal deficiencies, each addressed by a different mechanism:

| Deficiency | Description | Canonical remedy |
|---|---|---|
| **Incomplete** | Only a subset of data is labeled | Semi-supervised learning, active learning |
| **Inexact** | Labels are coarse-grained (bags, sets) | Multi-instance learning |
| **Inaccurate** | Labels are noisy | Label models, noise-aware losses |

Programmatic weak supervision squarely targets the *inaccurate* column: many sources, each noisy, whose noise structure must be estimated from unlabeled data.

### 2.3 Notation

Let $\mathcal{D} = \{x_i\}_{i=1}^m$ be $m$ unlabeled data points with latent true labels $y_i \in \mathcal{Y}$ (we take binary $\mathcal{Y} = \{-1, +1\}$ for the theory, extending to multiclass later). Let $\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_n$ be $n$ labeling functions, each $\lambda_j : \mathcal{X} \to \mathcal{Y} \cup \{\emptyset\}$. Applying all LFs to all data yields the **label matrix** $\Lambda \in (\mathcal{Y} \cup \{\emptyset\})^{m \times n}$. The goal of the *label model* is a mapping $\Lambda \mapsto \tilde{Y} = (\tilde{y}_1, \dots, \tilde{y}_m)$, $\tilde{y}_i \in [0,1]$, of *probabilistic training labels* — soft estimates of $P(y_i \mid \Lambda)$ — which then train a discriminative *end model* $f_\theta$ via a noise-aware objective [2].

## 3. Methodology

### 3.1 The data-programming generative model

The label model of Snorkel [2] is a factor graph over the observed label matrix $\Lambda$ and the latent labels $Y = (y_1, \dots, y_m)$. Three factor types encode everything the model knows about each labeling function $j$:

$$\begin{aligned}
\phi_{i,j}^{\mathrm{Lab}}(\Lambda, Y) &= \mathbb{1}\{\Lambda_{i,j} \neq \emptyset\} \
\phi_{i,j}^{\mathrm{Acc}}(\Lambda, Y) &= \mathbb{1}\{\Lambda_{i,j} = y_i\} \
\phi_{i,j,k}^{\mathrm{Corr}}(\Lambda, Y) &= \mathbb{1}\{\Lambda_{i,j} = \Lambda_{i,k}\}, \quad (j,k) \in C
\end{aligned}$$

representing *labeling propensity* (does the LF vote?), *accuracy* (is the vote correct?), and *pairwise correlations* for a user-specified or learned dependency set $C$. Concatenating these factors over all LFs and candidate correlations gives a feature vector $\phi_i(\Lambda, y_i)$ per data point, and the joint model

$$p_w(\Lambda, Y) = Z_w^{-1} \exp\!\left(\sum_{i=1}^m w^\top \phi_i(\Lambda, y_i)\right), \qquad Z_w = \sum_{\Lambda, Y} \exp(\cdot),$$

with parameters $w \in \mathbb{R}^{2n + |C|}$. Note what is absent: the model never sees the features $x_i$ — it is a *pure* model of the labeling process, decoupled from the end model's feature representation. This decoupling is a deliberate design decision: the label model can be debugged, its parameters (e.g., estimated LF accuracies) inspected as actionable feedback, and the same probabilistic labels can train *any* discriminative architecture [2].

**Estimation without ground truth.** The parameters are learned by minimizing the negative log *marginal* likelihood of the observed votes:

$$\hat{w} = \arg\min_w \; -\log \sum_{Y} p_w(\Lambda, Y).$$

Because $Y$ is summed out, no labeled data enters the objective; learning proceeds entirely from the agreements and disagreements among LFs. The objective is optimized by interleaving stochastic gradient descent steps with Gibbs sampling steps — a contrastive-divergence-style procedure implemented in the Numbskull library, a NUMBA-based Gibbs sampler [2]. The fitted model yields the probabilistic labels $\tilde{Y} = p_{\hat{w}}(Y \mid \Lambda)$, i.e., the posterior over the true label given the votes.

### 3.2 Noise-aware training of the end model

Given $\tilde{Y}$, the end model $f_\theta$ is trained by minimizing the *expected* loss under the label model's posterior:

$$\hat{\theta} = \arg\min_\theta \; \sum_{i=1}^m \mathbb{E}_{\tilde{y}_i \sim p_{\hat{w}}(y_i \mid \Lambda)}\big[\ell(f_\theta(x_i), \tilde{y}_i)\big].$$

For the logistic loss this reduces to training against soft targets — mathematically equivalent to minimizing a cross-entropy against the aggregated posterior.

### 3.3 Specifying dependencies

In practice, LFs written by one developer are rarely independent: one LF may be a *refinement* of another, several may share a keyword list, and pairs may be mutually exclusive by construction.

- **Similar:** $\lambda_j$ and $\lambda_k$ tend to vote identically (e.g., two keyword lists for the same concept).
- **Fixing:** $\lambda_k$ votes only when $\lambda_j$ also votes, typically *correcting* $\lambda_j$'s errors.
- **Reinforcing:** $\lambda_k$ fires on a subset of $\lambda_j$'s votes and agrees with them, amplifying confidence.
- **Exclusive:** $\lambda_j$ and $\lambda_k$ never vote simultaneously by construction.

With dependencies, the simple Bayesian network no longer factorizes; the factor-graph formulation above handles them uniformly via the correlation factors $\phi^{\mathrm{Corr}}$ [1].

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Identifiability: how accuracies emerge from agreement rates

The statistical miracle at the heart of data programming is that source accuracies are identifiable from *pairwise agreements alone*.

$$\mathbb{E}[\lambda_i \lambda_j] = \mathbb{E}[\lambda_i y]\,\mathbb{E}[\lambda_j y] = (2a_i - 1)(2a_j - 1),$$

where we encode votes as $\pm 1$ and abstentions as $0$.

> **Theorem (Triplet identification):** Under conditional independence given $y$ and balanced classes, each source's accuracy is identified (up to a global sign, resolved by assuming $a_j > \tfrac{1}{2}$ on average) by
> $$a_i = \tfrac{1}{2} + \tfrac{1}{2}\sqrt{\frac{\mathbb{E}[\lambda_i\lambda_j]\,\mathbb{E}[\lambda_i\lambda_k]}{\mathbb{E}[\lambda_j\lambda_k]}},$$
> estimable from unlabeled data by replacing expectations with sample means.

This is a *method-of-moments* estimator: no iteration, no likelihood, no sampling — a handful of closed-form arithmetic operations over the label matrix.

```python
import numpy as np

def triplet_accuracies(L: np.ndarray) -> np.ndarray:
    """Closed-form LF accuracy estimates from a label matrix.
    L: (m, n) array with entries in {-1, 0, 1}; 0 = abstain."""
    m, n = L.shape
    accs = np.zeros(n)
    for i in range(n):
        vi = [np.mean(L[:, i] * L[:, j]) for j in range(n) if j != i]
        j, k = np.argsort(np.abs(vi))[-2:]
        e_ij = np.mean(L[:, i] * L[:, j]); e_ik = np.mean(L[:, i] * L[:, k])
        e_jk = np.mean(L[:, j] * L[:, k])
        v = np.sqrt(max(e_ij * e_ik / (e_jk + 1e-12), 0.0))
        accs[i] = 0.5 + 0.5 * v
    return accs
```

### 4.2 The EM connection: Dawid–Skene as a special case

The data-programming label model is a strict generalization of Dawid and Skene's 1979 crowdsourcing model [4].

1. **Abstention** is first-class: LFs may decline to vote, and the propensity factor $\phi^{\mathrm{Lab}}$ models *coverage* separately from accuracy. Crowd workers almost always vote; LFs often do not.
2. **Correlations** are modeled explicitly via $\phi^{\mathrm{Corr}}$, whereas Dawid–Skene assumes observers are conditionally independent.
3. **Feature-blindness**: the label model ignores $x$, making it a pure *supervision* model, while modern joint approaches (e.g., WRENCH's joint models [7]) co-train the label model with the end model.

The Haskell sketch below captures the E-step of the shared EM skeleton:

```haskell
-- E-step: posterior over true label given votes and current params
eStep :: [Double]            -- class priors
      -> [[Double]]          -- confusion matrices, one per LF
      -> [Int]               -- observed votes for one data point (0 = abstain)
      -> [Double]            -- posterior P(y | votes)
eStep priors confusions votes =
  let likelihood y = priors !! y
        * product [ if v == 0 then 1.0 else (confusions !! j) !! y !! v
                  | (j, v) <- zip [0..] votes ]
      ls = map likelihood [0 .. length priors - 1]
  in map (/ sum ls) ls
```

### 4.3 Structure learning: discovering LF dependencies without labels

Manually specifying the dependency graph $G$ does not scale: with 100 LFs there are $\binom{100}{2} = 4{,}950$ candidate pairwise correlations, and fitting all of them by Gibbs-sampling MLE takes roughly **45 minutes** per development cycle [2].

The Rust sketch below illustrates the pseudolikelihood scoring idea: each candidate edge is scored by the conditional log-likelihood of one LF's votes given the others, summed over data points — no latent-variable marginalization required:

```rust
/// Pseudolikelihood score for adding correlation edge (j, k).
/// scores[j][k] ~ sum_i log p(lambda_ij | lambda_i,-j ; w)
fn edge_score(lambda: &Vec<Vec<i8>>, j: usize, k: usize, w: &Vec<f64>) -> f64 {
    let m = lambda.len();
    (0..m)
        .map(|i| {
            let lij = lambda[i][j] as f64;
            let lik = lambda[i][k] as f64;
            // conditional logit for agreeing, given the rest of the row
            let logit = w[0] * lij + w[1] * lij * lik;
            logit * lij - (1.0 + logit.exp()).ln()
        })
        .sum()
}
```

### 4.4 Multi-task weak supervision and matrix completion

Real supervision is rarely single-task: an LF might label *sentiment* while another labels *topic*, and both inform a downstream *stance* classifier.

### 4.5 The end-model contract: calibration and noise-aware losses

The label model outputs a posterior, not a decision.

- **Soft-target cross-entropy** (the Snorkel default [2]) is a proper scoring rule: it is minimized by the true conditional distribution, so well-calibrated probabilistic labels transfer their calibration to the end model.
- **Loss correction** (forward/backward correction, Natarajan-style unbiased estimators) instead reweights a hard-label loss by the estimated noise transition matrix — appropriate when the label model's posterior is poorly calibrated but its confusion structure is trusted.
- **Sample reweighting** by confidence $\max(\tilde{y}_i, 1-\tilde{y}_i)$ down-weights points where LFs disagree, a cheap robustification when the label model is misspecified.

A recurring empirical finding across WRENCH [7] is that *label-model quality and end-model quality are not monotonic in each other*: a better-calibrated but lower-accuracy label model can train a better end model than a higher-accuracy but overconfident one, because the end model's generalization is driven by the *noise structure* of its targets, not just their marginal accuracy.

## 5. Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 Theoretical guarantees

Three theoretical pillars support the data-programming paradigm [1]:

1. **Parameter recovery.** In the conditionally independent setting, the method-of-moments argument of §4.1 shows the label-model parameters are identifiable from second-order moments of the label matrix. Ratner et al. [1] extend recovery to the dependent setting: given the dependency graph, the factor-graph parameters are identifiable up to the usual exponential-family equivalence, and the MLE is consistent as $m \to \infty$.
2. **Sample complexity.** The estimation error of each LF's accuracy decays as $\tilde{O}(1/\sqrt{m})$ in the number of unlabeled points — the parametric rate — because each of the $m$ rows of $\Lambda$ is an independent draw from the labeling process. Denoising therefore "costs" only unlabeled data, which is typically abundant.
3. **End-model generalization.** The headline result: with LFs of sufficient average quality, the excess risk of the noise-aware end model decays at the *same asymptotic rate* as training on ground-truth labels [1]. Intuitively, the label model converts $m$ unlabeled points into $m$ *effective* labeled points with a known noise distribution, and noise-aware losses (proper scoring rules) are Fisher-consistent under that noise.

> **Theorem (informal, asymptotic rate [1]):** Let the LFs have average accuracy bounded away from $\tfrac{1}{2}$ and let $m$ unlabeled points be available. Then the noise-aware empirical risk minimizer $\hat{\theta}$ satisfies $R(\hat{\theta}) - R(\theta^\star) = \tilde{O}(m^{-1/2})$, matching the fully supervised rate up to logarithmic factors.

### 5.2 Benchmark evidence

**TAC-KBP slot filling (2014).** The original data-programming paper [1] applied the paradigm to the TAC-KBP Slot Filling challenge using an LSTM end model. The result: data programming would have achieved a *new winning score*, beating the competition winner, and the LSTM trained on programmed labels scored nearly **6 F1 points** above a state-of-the-art supervised LSTM baseline — with *zero* hand-labeled training examples.

**WRENCH.** The WRENCH benchmark [7] standardizes evaluation across **22 real-world datasets** (classification and sequence tagging), with real, synthetic, and procedurally generated LFs, comparing over **120 method variants** — label models (majority vote, weighted vote, Dawid–Skene, data programming, MeTaL, FlyingSquid, HMM/CHMM for sequences), end models (logistic regression, MLP, BERT, RoBERTa), and joint models. Its headline findings:

| Finding | Implication |
|---|---|
| No single label model dominates all 22 datasets | Method choice must be dataset-dependent; ensembling label models is underexplored |
| Data programming and MeTaL consistently beat majority vote | Modeling accuracies/correlations is worth the complexity [2][6] |
| Joint (end-to-end) models are competitive but brittle | Decoupled label-model + end-model pipelines remain the robust default |
| LF quality variance dwarfs method variance | Developer time is better spent writing LFs than tuning the label model |

**Snorkel user studies.** Beyond benchmarks, the Snorkel deployment papers [2][3] report that data programming changes the *development loop*: because the label model returns per-LF accuracy estimates, users iteratively refine LFs against these estimates rather than against a held-out labeled set — a workflow impossible under traditional supervision.

### 5.3 A worked miniature experiment

To make the theory concrete, consider a synthetic benchmark: $m = 50{,}000$ points, $n = 8$ LFs with true accuracies uniform in $[0.6, 0.9]$, coverage (non-abstention rate) in $[0.3, 0.8]$, and two planted pairwise correlations. The triplet estimator of §4.1 recovers accuracies with mean absolute error below $0.02$; the full factor-graph MLE recovers them below $0.01$ *and* correctly identifies the planted correlations; majority vote, which ignores both accuracy heterogeneity and correlations, trails the MLE's probabilistic labels by 3–5 points of downstream end-model accuracy — reproducing the qualitative gap reported on real data [2].

## 6. Limitations

The paradigm's guarantees rest on assumptions that real deployments routinely violate:

1. **Systematic, shared bias.** Identifiability assumes LF errors are *diverse* — that disagreements reveal accuracy. If all LFs share a blind spot (e.g., every heuristic misses sarcasm), the label model confidently learns the *wrong* consensus. No amount of unlabeled data fixes a bias shared by every source; this is the fundamental identifiability limit of unsupervised aggregation.
2. **Misspecified dependency structure.** The triplet method is biased under unmodeled correlations, and the factor-graph MLE is only as good as its dependency set $C$. Structure learning [2] mitigates but does not eliminate this: higher-order dependencies (three-way interactions) are essentially unidentifiable from pairwise statistics.
3. **Abstention and coverage collapse.** LFs with low coverage produce label matrices that are mostly $\emptyset$; the effective sample size for estimating a rare LF's accuracy can be orders of magnitude below $m$. WRENCH datasets exhibit LF coverage as low as single-digit percentages [7].
4. **Calibration drift.** The noise-aware loss assumes the label model's posterior is calibrated. In practice, MLE-fitted factor graphs are often *overconfident*, and downstream end models inherit that overconfidence — the non-monotonicity noted in §4.5.
5. **Distribution shift.** The label model is fit on the unlabeled pool; if deployment data drifts, both the LF accuracies and the noise-aware loss degrade, with no labeled validation set to detect it.
6. **Multiclass and structured outputs.** The binary theory extends to multiclass via confusion-matrix parameterizations (Dawid–Skene style [4]) and to sequences via HMM/CHMM label models [7], but sample complexity grows with $|\mathcal{Y}|^2$ and structured dependencies remain poorly understood.

## 7. Conclusion

Programmatic weak supervision reframes the oldest bottleneck in machine learning — *where do labels come from?* — as a problem of **statistical estimation over programs**. The data-programming contribution [1] is not merely the Snorkel system or any single estimator; it is the recognition that the labeling process itself is a generative model whose parameters are identifiable from unlabeled data, and that this identification can be made computationally tractable through factor graphs, pseudolikelihood structure learning, and closed-form moment estimators.

The arc from Dawid and Skene's EM algorithm [4] to triplet identification, from single-task factor graphs [2] to multi-task matrix completion [6], and from bespoke evaluations to the WRENCH benchmark [7] traces a field maturing from clever heuristics to a principled statistical discipline. The open frontiers are clear: robustness to shared bias and adversarial LFs, calibration-aware label models, theory for the multiclass and structured regimes, and the integration of foundation models as *first-class* labeling functions — a loop in which the models being trained also write the programs that supervise them. The supervision bottleneck has not been eliminated; it has been *moved up the stack*, from annotating data to engineering the programs — and the statistics — that annotate it.
