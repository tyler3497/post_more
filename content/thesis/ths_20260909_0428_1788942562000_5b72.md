# Computational Origami: the Huzita–Hatori Axioms, Rigid Foldability, and Algorithmic Design of Deployable Structures

## Abstract

Computational origami treats paper folding as a formal system: a crease pattern is a planar graph, a folding is a piecewise-isometric map, and the central algorithmic question is which patterns are realizable and at what cost. This thesis develops the field from axioms to engineering. We present the seven Huzita–Hatori axioms of constructible folding, prove the Kawasaki and Maekawa theorems governing local flat-foldability of vertices, and analyze rigid foldability of degree-4 vertices via their one-dimensional configuration spaces. We examine the Miura-ori as the canonical rigid-foldable tessellation, survey inverse-design algorithms for crease-pattern synthesis, and establish the complexity landscape: global flat foldability is NP-hard, yet the same local-global gap renders flat origami Turing complete. Finally, we connect theory to deployable structures—solar arrays, stents, and mechanical metamaterials—showing how folding kinematics impose scale-independent mechanical behavior. Origami emerges not merely as art but as a rigorous computational and physical theory constraining real deployable systems.

## 1 Introduction

The transformation of a flat sheet into a three-dimensional object is among the oldest algorithmic processes known to humanity, yet its mathematical formalization is barely three decades old. Computational origami asks: *given a pattern of creases drawn on a sheet, what shapes can it be folded into, and how hard is it to decide?* The answer weaves together combinatorics, differential geometry, group theory, and complexity theory, and it terminates in space-deployable hardware [8].

The modern era begins with the axiomatization of folding itself. Just as Euclid's postulates define straightedge-and-compass construction, the **Huzita–Hatori axioms** define *fold-and-point* construction: paper can trisect the angle and double the cube, both impossible with straightedge and compass. Above this foundation sits **flat foldability**: Kawasaki's and Maekawa's theorems give complete local characterizations of which single vertices fold flat, while the global problem—deciding whether an entire crease pattern folds flat without self-intersection—was shown to be NP-hard by Bern and Hayes in 1996 [2].

Yet rigidity introduces a third, dynamical layer. In **rigid origami**, faces are constrained to remain rigid planar panels while creases act as hinges. A degree-4 vertex generically admits a one-degree-of-freedom motion, governed by elegant algebraic relations between its fold angles. Tessellated periodically, such vertices yield the Miura-ori—a structure that packs flat with extraordinary efficiency and whose kinematics dominate its mechanical response at *every* scale [5].

This thesis argues that these three layers—axiomatic, combinatorial, and mechanical—form a single coherent computational theory, developed as follows. Section 2 reviews the geometric model of folding and the Huzita–Hatori axioms. Section 3 describes the methodology: configuration-space analysis, loop-closure constraints, and simulation by constrained optimization. Section 4 develops five deep dives: the axiomatic theory, local flat-foldability, rigid-foldable vertex kinematics, the Miura-ori tessellation, and algorithmic inverse design. Section 5 presents the complexity theorems and their empirical consequences, followed by limitations and conclusions.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The geometric model of folding

A **folded state** is a continuous piecewise-isometric map $f: P \to \mathbb{R}^3$ that is an isometry on each face (each connected component of $P \setminus E$) and bends only along creases. For flat origami the codomain restricts to $\mathbb{R}^2$ and the analysis adds a **layer ordering**: for every pair of points $x, y$ whose images coincide, a function $\lambda_f(x, y) \in \{-1, +1\}$ records which lies above the other, capturing the self-avoidance of real paper [1].

The model distinguishes three regimes of increasing restrictiveness: *simple folds* (the pattern is folded in a sequence of single-crease operations), *flat foldability* (the pattern maps into the plane with no self-intersection, respecting a consistent layer ordering), and *rigid foldability* (every face remains a rigid planar panel throughout a continuous folding motion; only dihedral angles change).

Degree-2 vertices are trivial and degree-3 vertices cannot fold rigidly, making the **degree-4 vertex** the atomic unit of rigid origami.

### 2.2 The Huzita–Hatori axioms

In the 1980s, Humiaki Huzita formalized and Koshiro Hatori completed a set of axioms describing elementary fold operations. Each axiom asserts the existence of a *unique* fold (or finite set of folds) achieving a given alignment of points and lines:

| # | Axiom | Construction achieved |
|---|-------|----------------------|
| O1 | Fold through two given points $P_1, P_2$ | Fold along line $P_1P_2$ |
| O2 | Superpose $P_1$ onto $P_2$ | Perpendicular bisector of $P_1P_2$ |
| O3 | Superpose line $L_1$ onto $L_2$ | Angle bisector of $L_1, L_2$ |
| O4 | Fold through $P_1$ perpendicular to $L_1$ | Perpendicular through $P_1$ |
| O5 | Place $P_1$ onto $L_1$ through $P_2$ | Tangent from $P_2$ to parabola with focus $P_1$, directrix $L_1$ |
| O6 | Place $P_1$ onto $L_1$ and $P_2$ onto $L_2$ | Common tangent to two parabolas |
| O7 | Fold through $P_1$ placing $P_2$ onto $L_1$ | Tangent from $P_1$ to parabola with focus $P_2$, directrix $L_1$ |

![The seven Huzita–Hatori axioms](/thesis/ths_20260909_0428_1788942562000_5b72-0.webp)

*Figure 1: The seven Huzita–Hatori axioms (O1–O7), each illustrated as a fold operation aligning points and lines.*

Axiom O6 is decisive: placing $P_1$ on $L_1$ and $P_2$ on $L_2$ simultaneously is the common tangent to two parabolas, reducing to a *cubic* equation. The **origami numbers**—lengths constructible by iterated folding—are therefore closed under cube roots, permitting angle trisection and cube doubling, both impossible with straightedge and compass [2].

### 2.3 Local flat-foldability: Kawasaki and Maekawa

For a vertex of degree $2n$ with consecutive sector angles $\theta_1, \dots, \theta_{2n}$:

> **Theorem 1 (Kawasaki, 1989):** *A single vertex folds flat if and only if the alternating sum of its sector angles vanishes:*
> $$\sum_{i=1}^{n} \theta_{2i-1} - \sum_{i=1}^{n} \theta_{2i} = 0.$$
> *Equivalently, $\theta_1 + \theta_3 + \cdots = \theta_2 + \theta_4 + \cdots = \pi$.*

> **Theorem 2 (Maekawa, 1980; Justin, 1986):** *At any flat-foldable vertex, the numbers of mountain ($M$) and valley ($V$) creases satisfy $|M - V| = 2$.*

Kawasaki's theorem is checkable in linear time per vertex, yet far from sufficient globally. Maekawa's theorem implies that a flat-foldable degree-4 vertex has exactly three creases of one parity and one of the other, a fact structuring the entire kinematics of rigid folding.

## 3 Methodology

The methodology of computational origami is best understood as a pipeline from discrete geometry to numerical simulation to hardware.

1. **Combinatorial modeling.** The crease pattern is encoded as a planar graph with sector angles and a mountain–valley assignment $\mu: E \to \{+1, -1\}$; local feasibility is tested via Kawasaki's and Maekawa's conditions in $O(|V|)$ time.
2. **Loop-closure formulation.** Around each interior vertex with fold angles $\rho_i$ and sector angles $\theta_i$, rigid foldability demands that the product of rotation matrices around the vertex equal the identity:
   $$\mathbf{F}(\boldsymbol{\rho}) = \chi_{1,2}\chi_{2,3}\cdots\chi_{n,1} = \mathbf{I}_3,$$
   where each $\chi_{i,i+1}$ composes the fold rotation about crease $i$ with the sector rotation $\theta_i$ [3]. Stacking these over all vertices yields a nonlinear system $\mathbf{c}(\mathbf{x}) = \mathbf{0}$ in vertex coordinates $\mathbf{x}$, solved by Newton–Raphson from a known valid configuration.
3. **Simulation by constrained projection.** Following Tachi's rigid-origami simulator, the configuration is represented by crease angles and the folding trajectory is computed by projecting infinitesimal motions onto the constraint manifold, triangulating polygons and adjusting crease lines to maintain flexibility [3].
4. **Inverse design.** The forward question (pattern → folded state) is inverted: given a target surface or motion, synthesize a crease pattern satisfying developability, flat-foldability, and rigidity constraints via optimization on the constraint manifold [4].
5. **Physical realization.** Kinematic designs become hinged-panel metamaterials, with crease stiffness, panel thickness, and actuation as perturbations of the ideal model [5].

We formalize the vertex-constraint model in Haskell, demonstrate the Kawasaki check in Python, encode a TLA+ specification of flat-foldable layer orderings, and sketch the Newton solver in Rust in the sections that follow.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Axiomatic folding and the arithmetic of paper

The seven axioms define a constructive geometry strictly more powerful than Euclid's. Axiom O6 requires the common tangent to two parabolas, hence the general cubic. The field of origami numbers is thus the smallest subfield of $\mathbb{R}$ containing $\mathbb{Q}$ and closed under square and cube roots: angle trisection is a single axiom application (O6), cube doubling a short folding sequence, both impossible with straightedge and compass.

A functional encoding of the axiom system captures its constructive character:```haskell
-- Elementary fold operations in the Huzita-Hatori calculus
data Point = P Double Double
data Line  = L Double Double Double   -- ax + by + c = 0

data Axiom
  = O1 Point Point              -- fold through P1, P2
  | O2 Point Point              -- superpose P1 onto P2
  | O3 Line Line                -- superpose L1 onto L2
  | O4 Point Line               -- fold through P1, perpendicular to L1
  | O5 Point Line Point         -- place P1 on L1 through P2
  | O6 Point Line Point Line    -- place P1 on L1 and P2 on L2
  | O7 Point Point Line         -- fold through P1, place P2 on L1

foldCrease :: Axiom -> [Line]
foldCrease = solveAlgebraic . encode
  where
    encode (O6 p1 l1 p2 l2) = commonTangent (parabola p1 l1) (parabola p2 l2)
    encode ax = direct ax
```

The crucial fact is that every axiom is *locally verifiable*: checking a purported fold is a constant-degree algebraic computation. This locality is exactly what the complexity results of Section 5 exploit—and shatter at the global level.

### 4.2 Local flat-foldability: the degree-4 vertex in detail

Consider a degree-4 vertex with sector angles $\alpha_1, \alpha_2, \alpha_3, \alpha_4$ in cyclic order. Kawasaki's theorem reduces to $\alpha_1 + \alpha_3 = \alpha_2 + \alpha_4 = \pi$: *opposite* sector angles must sum to $\pi$. Maekawa's theorem forces a $3$–$1$ mountain–valley split. Combined, they rigidly constrain the combinatorial type: up to symmetry only two flat-foldable mountain–valley assignments exist for a generic degree-4 vertex, and the minority crease must bound the unique largest sector-angle pair.

![Degree-4 vertex theorems](/thesis/ths_20260909_0428_1788942562000_5b72-2.webp)

*Figure 2: A flat-foldable degree-4 vertex. Kawasaki's theorem requires $\alpha_1 + \alpha_3 = \alpha_2 + \alpha_4 = 180^\circ$; Maekawa's theorem forces a 3–1 mountain–valley split.*

The local check admits a direct implementation:

```python
from math import isclose, pi

def kawasaki_holds(sectors: list[float]) -> bool:
    """Alternating sector sums must agree (radians)."""
    if len(sectors) % 2 != 0:
        return False
    even = sum(sectors[0::2])
    return isclose(even, sum(sectors[1::2]), abs_tol=1e-9) and isclose(even, pi, abs_tol=1e-9)

def maekawa_holds(mv: list[int]) -> bool:
    """|#mountains - #valleys| == 2, with +1=mountain, -1=valley."""
    return abs(sum(mv)) == 2

def locally_flat_foldable(sectors, mv) -> bool:
    return (len(sectors) == len(mv)
            and kawasaki_holds(sectors)
            and maekawa_holds(mv))
```

> **Corollary 1:** *Every flat-foldable degree-4 vertex is developable ($\sum \alpha_i = 2\pi$) and satisfies $\alpha_i < \pi$ for all $i$; no sector may exceed a half-plane.*

The local picture is therefore completely understood. The drama of the subject lies in what happens when vertices are coupled.

### 4.3 Rigid foldability and the configuration space of degree-4 vertices

In rigid origami, faces are rigid panels and only fold angles $\rho_i \in (-\pi, \pi)$ vary. For a degree-4 vertex the loop-closure condition $\mathbf{F}(\boldsymbol{\rho}) = \mathbf{I}_3$ imposes three independent scalar constraints on four variables, leaving a **one-dimensional configuration space**—a single degree of freedom. This is the mathematical reason the Miura-ori and its relatives deploy with one synchronized motion.

For symmetric vertices ($\alpha_1 = \alpha_3$, $\alpha_2 = \alpha_4$), opposite creases carry *equal* fold angles and a single parameter determines the motion—a fact exploited by every rigid-foldable tessellation.

The global simulation stacks one loop-closure system per vertex plus planarity constraints per panel. Tachi's formulation treats vertex coordinates $\mathbf{x}$ as unknowns with constraints for developability, flat-foldability, panel planarity, and boundary conditions, solved as $\mathbf{c}(\mathbf{x}) = \mathbf{0}$ via Newton–Raphson from a regular initial guess and explored by perturbation along the kernel of the Jacobian [4]:```rust
/// Newton-Raphson solve of c(x)=0 via Moore-Penrose updates.
fn solve_rigid_origami(
    mut x: DVector<f64>,
    c: &dyn Fn(&DVector<f64>) -> DVector<f64>,
    jac: &dyn Fn(&DVector<f64>) -> DMatrix<f64>,
) -> Result<DVector<f64>, &'static str> {
    for _ in 0..64 {
        let r = c(&x);
        if r.norm() < 1e-10 { return Ok(x); }
        let dx = -jac(&x).pseudo_inverse(1e-12)? * r;
        x += dx;
        if dx.norm() < 1e-12 { return Ok(x); }
    }
    Err("no convergence: pattern may not be rigid-foldable")
}
```

> **Theorem 3 (Rigid-foldable degree-4 vertex):** *A developable degree-4 vertex admits a nontrivial rigid folding motion if and only if opposite sector angles are not both equal to $\pi/2$ in the degenerate flat case; generically the motion space is a smooth one-dimensional manifold parameterized by any single fold angle.*

### 4.4 The Miura-ori: a canonical rigid-foldable tessellation

The Miura-ori, invented by Koryo Miura in 1970 as a method for packaging large membranes in space, tessellates the plane with congruent parallelograms arranged so that every interior vertex is a symmetric degree-4 vertex with a consistent mountain–valley assignment [5]. Its virtues are extraordinary:

- **Single degree of freedom**: one synchronized deployment motion.
- **Flat-packability**: deployed-to-stowed area ratio scales with cell count.
- **Negative Poisson's ratio**: a purely kinematic auxetic effect [5].
- **Scale independence**: kinematics dominate mechanics from paper to architecture [7].

![Miura-ori crease pattern](/thesis/ths_20260909_0428_1788942562000_5b72-1.webp)

*Figure 3: The Miura-ori crease pattern with mountain (solid) and valley (dashed) assignment and the periodic parallelogram unit cell parameterized by angle $\beta$.*

Parametrizing one unit cell by parallelogram side lengths $a, b$, acute angle $\beta$, and dihedral fold angle $\theta$, the outer dimensions are
$$H = a \sin\theta \sin\beta, \qquad S = b\,\frac{\cos\theta \tan\beta}{\sqrt{1 + \cos^2\theta \tan^2\beta}}, \qquad L = a\sqrt{1 - \sin^2\theta \sin^2\beta},$$
from which the negative in-plane Poisson's ratio follows by differentiation [5]. Replacing the parallelogram with arbitrary quadrilaterals satisfying the vertex conditions yields Tachi's generalized Miura-ori tessellations—freeform rigid-foldable surfaces and deployable cylinders [4].

### 4.5 Algorithmic origami design: the inverse problem

Forward simulation answers "what does this pattern do?"; **algorithmic origami design** answers "which pattern produces this shape?" The canonical inverse problem, solved by Tachi's *Origamizer*, is: given an arbitrary polyhedral surface, compute a crease pattern on a single sheet that folds into it without cuts. The approach lays out the surface's polygons, introduces *tucking molecules* absorbing excess material, and enforces validity via a two-stage mapping [4].

The design space has three branches: *tessellation design* (sector angles satisfying Kawasaki + Maekawa everywhere, then loop-closure for a target folded state); *shape approximation* (tuck-fold arbitrary polyhedral surfaces via Origamizer); and *motion design* (prescribe a deployment path and solve for crease geometry containing it as a one-DOF branch).

We can specify the correctness condition for flat-foldable layer orderings in TLA+:

```tla+
----------------------------- MODULE FlatFold -----------------------------
EXTENDS Naturals, FiniteSets
CONSTANTS Creases, MV          \* creases and mountain-valley assignment
VARIABLES lambda               \* lambda[x][y] in {-1,0,1}: layer ordering

TypeOK == lambda \in [Creases \times Creases -> {-1,0,1}]
Antisym == \A x,y \in Creases : lambda[x][y] = -lambda[y][x]
NoCycle == \A x,y,z \in Creases :   \* no cyclic above-below triple
  ~ (lambda[x][y] = 1 /\ lambda[y][z] = 1 /\ lambda[z][x] = 1)
Spec == TypeOK /\ Antisym /\ NoCycle
=============================================================================
```

> **Theorem 4 (Design completeness, informal):** *For every orientable polyhedral surface there exists a crease pattern on a square sheet whose rigid folding realizes it; the construction is algorithmic but the pattern size may be exponential in the surface complexity [4].*

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 The complexity landscape

> **Theorem 5 (Bern–Hayes, 1996):** *Deciding whether a given crease pattern with a specified mountain–valley assignment folds flat without self-intersection is NP-hard [2].*

The proof reduces from planar 3-SAT: variables become pleats whose layer ordering encodes truth values, clauses become gadgets constraining those orderings. Every *local* condition is easy—Kawasaki's theorem is linear-time per vertex—so the hardness is purely emergent: layer orderings must be simultaneously consistent across the whole pattern, and candidate orderings grow exponentially. Akitaya et al. (2016) later repaired a subtle gap in the original proof, strengthening the result to NP-completeness under a rigorous definition of the folded state.

| Level | Question | Complexity |
|-------|----------|------------|
| Local | Is this vertex flat-foldable? (Kawasaki/Maekawa) | $O(n)$ per vertex |
| Global | Is this crease pattern flat-foldable? | NP-complete [2] |
| Universal | What can flat origami compute? | **Turing complete** [1] |

### 5.2 From hardness to universality

The NP-hardness of flat foldability might suggest computational *intractability*; the deepest recent result shows *universality*:

> **Theorem 6 (Hull–Zakharevich, 2023):** *Flat origami with optional creases—creases permitted to fold or remain flat subject to global constraints—is Turing complete. The construction simulates Rule 110, a one-dimensional cellular automaton proven universal by Cook (2004) [1].*

The proof builds origami logic gates: a pleat encodes a bit by its layer ordering, crease gadgets implement NAND, and optional creases act as conditional wires forced by neighboring gadgets. Rule 110 spaceships tile the plane periodically; finite perturbations of the tiling encode a computation whose halting corresponds to the pattern folding flat [8].

This yields a profound unity: the *same* local-global gap that makes flat foldability NP-hard is the computational resource that makes origami universal. Hardness is not a defect of the model; it is its power.### 5.3 Empirical validation: mechanics and metamaterials

Theory predicts scale-independent, kinematics-dominated mechanics for rigid-foldable tessellations; experiment confirms it. Schenk and Guest measured a negative in-plane Poisson's ratio in Miura-ori shell and cellular structures matching the kinematic prediction of Section 4.4, with out-of-plane bending showing the opposite sign [5]. Wei et al. derived the geometric mechanics of periodic pleated origami from a continuum plate theory with folding energy: effective moduli are governed by crease geometry rather than base material, with experiments on paper and polymer models collapsing onto the theoretical curves [6].

## 6 Limitations

1. **Zero thickness.** The rigid-origami model assumes ideal zero-thickness panels. Real deployable structures—solar arrays, stents, architectural roofs—have finite thickness, and thickness accommodation (tapered panels, offset hinges) breaks exact rigid foldability. No general analogue of Theorem 3 exists for thick panels.
2. **Ideal hinges.** Creases are modeled as perfect revolute joints. Real creases have bending stiffness, plastic deformation, and fatigue; the analysis predicts *kinematic* branches but not which branch a physical sheet selects under load, nor bifurcation under imperfect symmetry.
3. **NP-hardness is worst-case.** Theorem 5 does not imply *practical* patterns are hard: Miura-ori, Huffman grids, and most engineered tessellations admit polynomial-time folding sequences. Average-case and parameterized complexity remain largely open.
4. **Turing completeness requires optionality.** Theorem 6 needs *optional* creases decided globally—a nonstandard model. Whether fixed crease patterns can compute is open, and the Rule 110 simulation demands astronomically large patterns for modest computations.
5. **Simulation convergence.** Newton-based solvers converge only from good initial guesses near the constraint manifold; near singular (bifurcation) configurations where the Jacobian loses rank, the methods of [3, 4] can stall or jump branches.
6. **Material and scale effects.** Scale-independence holds for kinematics, not for strength, actuation energy, or deployment dynamics. Gravity, hinge friction, and aerodynamic loads dominate at architectural scale and are absent from the geometric model.

## 7 Conclusion

Computational origami has matured from folding puzzles into a rigorous science of *programmable geometry*. The Huzita–Hatori axioms fix the atomic operations; Kawasaki's and Maekawa's theorems completely characterize local flat folding; loop-closure analysis of degree-4 vertices explains rigid-origami motion; the Miura-ori shows that one degree of freedom, correctly tessellated, can package a solar array; and the complexity theorems reveal that the gap between local checkability and global consistency is the source of computational universality.

Three insights unify the subject. First, *locality fails gracefully*: every obstruction—NP-hardness, Turing completeness, deployment bifurcation—arises from coupling individually trivial vertices. Second, *kinematics dominates mechanics*: for rigid-foldable patterns, geometry dictates behavior across scales, making the crease pattern a genuine engineering design variable. Third, *design is inversion*: the hardest problems run the forward map backward, from desired shape or motion to the pattern that realizes it.

The open frontier is thickness, dynamics, and control: thick-panel kinematics, self-folding active materials, and algorithmic synthesis of patterns with prescribed multi-stable energy landscapes. Paper taught us the theorems; engineering will teach us their limits.
