# Certified Concurrent Mark-Sweep Garbage Collection: Snapshot-at-the-Beginning Barriers, Linear Capability Proofs, and Pause-Time Bounds in Low-Latency Runtimes

## Abstract

Low-latency runtimes demand garbage collection pauses measured in hundreds of microseconds, yet the collectors achieving them rest on the subtlest correctness arguments in systems programming. This thesis presents a certified concurrent mark-sweep collector built around *snapshot-at-the-beginning* (SATB) write barriers, the Dijkstra–Lamport–Martin–Scholten–Steffens tri-color abstraction, and a machine-checked proof encoding the collector's safety invariant in a Steel-style linear separation logic. We formalize heap mutation during marking as an interleaving of mutator stores and collector scans, derive the barrier obligations preserving the tri-color invariant, and prove that SATB soundly over-approximates reachability while admitting a quantitative bound on collector-induced pause time. The proof uses *linear capabilities*—exclusive ownership tokens for heap cells and color predicates—transferred between mutator and collector threads, enabling local reasoning about a globally concurrent algorithm. We decompose worst-case pause time into root-scanning, remembered-set draining, and handshake components, yielding a bound parameterized by stack depth, SATB buffer occupancy, and barrier cost, validated on a prototype runtime. The result is a collector whose safety argument is a mechanically dischargable artifact, not a paper proof.

## 1 Introduction

Garbage collection is a *contract* between a runtime and its mutators: the runtime promises that any object still reachable through live references will never be reclaimed. In stop-the-world collectors this contract is honored in a frozen heap. In *concurrent* collectors—those interleaving marking with mutator execution to meet latency targets—it must be honored under adversarial interleavings of stores and scans, so pause times decide fitness for latency-sensitive deployment [5].

The central difficulty is the *lost-object problem*. While the marker traverses the object graph, a mutator may install a pointer into an already-scanned region, severing the only path by which the marker could have discovered a live object. Without countermeasures, the collector frees a reachable object and the program fails catastrophically. The canonical answer is the *tri-color abstraction* [9]: objects are white (unscanned, presumed garbage), gray (reachable, awaiting scan), or black (reachable, scanned), and correctness reduces to the invariant that **no black object references a white object**. Write barriers—small fragments executed on every heap store—enforce the invariant under mutation, in two classical disciplines: *Dijkstra-style insertion barriers* and *Yuasa-style deletion (SATB) barriers* [4, 7].

This thesis unifies three strands usually treated separately. First, we give a precise semantics of SATB barriers, including their *float* (garbage retained because live at mark-start), and characterize where the conservatism pays for its simplicity. Second, we formalize the collector in a linear-capability discipline inspired by Mezzo and Steel [8], in which the tri-color invariant becomes a *spatial* property: coloring a cell black transfers an exclusive capability to the collector, and mutator stores require capabilities that force barrier execution. Third, we certify the design with verification conditions in the style of Hawblitzel and Petrank's Boogie/Z3-verified collectors [1] and rely-guarantee concurrent GC verification [2], and we derive a *closed-form pause-time bound* predicting worst-case latency from measurable parameters.

Our contributions are:

1. A rigorous formulation of the SATB correctness argument as an over-approximation theorem over the *mark-start snapshot graph*, with an explicit characterization of float.
2. A linear-capability encoding of the tri-color invariant in which barrier soundness follows from capability conservation, supporting local, thread-modular reasoning.
3. A machine-checkable proof skeleton (BoogiePL/Z3 verification conditions plus TLA+ liveness sketches) following the automated-verification methodology of [1, 2, 6].
4. A quantitative pause-time model decomposing stop-the-world phases into root scanning, SATB-buffer draining, and handshake costs, validated on a prototype with sub-millisecond pauses.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The Tri-Color Abstraction

Dijkstra, Lamport, Martin, Scholten, and Steffens introduced on-the-fly garbage collection in 1978 [9]. Their algorithm colors every heap object white, gray, or black and proves that, if the invariant *no black object points to a white object* holds whenever the marker is idle, then every white object at scan completion is genuinely unreachable. Roots start gray; the marker repeatedly extracts a gray object, grays its white children, and blackens it. Termination with an empty gray set yields a *certificate*: black objects are closed under reachability, so remaining white objects are garbage. The abstraction's strength is its independence from any particular interleaving; its weakness is that the invariant must survive concurrent mutation.

### 2.2 Write Barriers: Insertion, Deletion, and the Hybrid

When the mutator executes `A.f := B` during marking, two things can break the invariant. If `A` is black and `B` is white, the new edge black→white violates it directly. If `A` previously pointed to white `C` and the write destroys that edge while `C` is reachable only through `A`, then `C` becomes *invisible* to the marker—no syntactic violation, yet `C` vanishes from the closure the marker will compute.

Two barrier disciplines answer these hazards:

- **Dijkstra (insertion) barrier.** On installing `B` into `A`, gray the target: `if white(B) then shade(B)`. This prevents black→white edges at creation, admits no float, but costs proportional to the *write rate*.
- **Yuasa (deletion, SATB) barrier.** On overwriting the field, record the *old* value: `satb_enqueue(old(A.f))`. Everything live at mark-start stays live through the cycle, so the correctness argument is a clean over-approximation. Cost is bounded by the *deletion rate* rather than the write rate, and the analysis is simpler [4, 7].

Go 1.8 introduced a *hybrid* barrier performing Yuasa-style deletion recording together with Dijkstra-style insertion shading, eliminating stack re-scanning and the last large stop-the-world phase in Go's collector [5]. OpenJDK's G1 uses SATB with a pre-write barrier enqueuing overwritten values into per-thread buffers drained during remark, with TAMS (top-at-mark-start) regions treating above-TAMS allocations as implicitly live [3].

### 2.3 Certified Garbage Collection

Mechanized certification of *practical* collectors began with Hawblitzel and Petrank, who verified x86 assembly mark-sweep and Cheney copying collectors with Boogie and Z3, demonstrating competitive performance on C# benchmarks [1]. Later work added rely-guarantee verification of a concurrent collector [2] and a mechanically verified mark-sweep collector for OCaml [6]. In parallel, separation logic brought local reasoning to pointer programs, and the *Steel* project embeds a concurrent separation logic in F* for proof-oriented programming of lock-free, thread-modular code—the exact shape of a concurrent marker [8].

## 3 Methodology

**Heap model.** The heap is a finite map from addresses to objects, each a finite map from fields to values (addresses or scalars), plus a color in {white, gray, black}. Mutators perform atomic single-field stores and loads; the collector performs atomic color transitions and field reads. Interleavings are arbitrary, subject only to the barrier protocol.

**Barrier semantics.** The SATB pre-write barrier is a ghost-enqueuing operation:

```haskell
-- SATB barrier: executed atomically before the store
satbBarrier :: Heap -> Addr -> Field -> Value -> IO ()
satbBarrier h a f new =
  when markingInProgress $ do
    let old = loadField h a f
    when (isPointer old && old /= null) (enqueue satbBuffer old)
    storeField h a f new
```

The deletion-barrier contract is *exactly* this: no store may destroy a reference without the old referent first entering the SATB buffer, which the collector re-scans at remark time.

**Linear-capability encoding.** We encode colors as *capabilities* in a Steel-style concurrent separation logic [8]. Each heap cell `c` carries an exclusive token `cap(c)`; the predicate `white(c)`, `gray(c)`, `black(c)` is a spatial assertion consuming that token. Coloring a cell black *transfers* its capability to the collector, and the tri-color invariant becomes the spatial formula:

> **Theorem:** *At every program point, the separating conjunction of all cell capabilities satisfies `∀ c. black(c) → ¬∃ d. (white(d) ∧ pointsTo(c,d))`.*

Because capabilities are linear (neither duplicable nor droppable), a mutator cannot hold a store capability for `A.f` without discharging the barrier obligation, and the collector cannot free a cell without acquiring its capability through sweep. Thread-modular reasoning follows from the frame rule: each thread's footprint is its held capabilities, and the invariant is preserved by construction.

**Verification pipeline.** Following [1], we write the collector in low-level pseudo-assembly with pre/postconditions and loop invariants in BoogiePL; Z3 discharges verification conditions for sequential sweep and buffer draining. Following [2], the concurrent marking loop is verified under rely-guarantee: the mutator's guarantee is the barrier protocol; the collector's rely is exactly that protocol. A TLA+ model of the abstract algorithm checks liveness (marking termination) under weak fairness, and pause-time bounds are derived analytically and corroborated by measurement.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Tri-Color Invariant Under Adversarial Interleavings

The invariant *no black→white edges* is deceptively simple; its fragility appears only when mutation and marking interleave:

*Hazard A — hidden deletion.* Objects `A` (gray, about to be scanned) and `B` (black, scanned) both reference white object `C`. The mutator deletes both edges and installs a fresh edge `B.f := C` *after* `B` was scanned. If the barrier misses the deletion, `C` is white and reachable only from scanned objects, so the marker never visits it: `C` is wrongly freed. The SATB barrier catches this by enqueuing `C` at deletion time; the insertion barrier catches it by re-graying at installation.

*Hazard B — the race through gray.* `A` (gray) points to white `C`; the marker dequeues `A`, scans it, and blackens it. *Concurrently*, the mutator executes `A.f := null`. If the store lands after the scan of `A.f` with no barrier, nothing visibly violates the invariant—yet `C`, reachable only via `A.f`, is lost. SATB's deletion record saves `C`.

The deep point is that the invariant is a *temporal* property over interleavings, not a state predicate. Our capability encoding captures this by making color transitions *linear*: graying a cell consumes the white token and produces the gray token, so no thread can observe a cell in two colors at once, and the barrier is the unique rule manufacturing gray tokens for targets of mutator stores.

### 4.2 SATB Semantics: The Mark-Start Snapshot as Over-Approximation

Yuasa's insight [4] was to trade precision for a principled over-approximation. Define the *snapshot graph* `G₀` as the reference graph at the instant marking begins. The SATB collector guarantees:

> **Theorem:** *The set of objects marked by an SATB collection is a superset of the objects reachable in `G₀`.*

*Proof sketch.* Induction over the marking wavefront. Roots of `G₀` are marked at the base. Any path of length k+1 ends with an edge `x → y` present in `G₀`. Either the edge still exists when `x` is scanned (so the scan marks `y`), or it was deleted—in which case the deletion barrier enqueued `y` (or `y` was already marked). Either way `y` enters the closure. ∎

The price is *float*: objects dying mid-cycle but live at mark-start are retained until the next cycle. If the mutator's death rate is δ objects/second and marking lasts T seconds, expected float is δ·T objects—typically a few percent of the heap [3]. This trade—*provably simple correctness at bounded float cost*—is why G1, ZGC, and Go's hybrid barrier all build on deletion recording.

```rust
#[inline(always)]
pub fn satb_write_barrier(slot: *mut *mut Object, new_val: *mut Object) {
    // Fast path: marking not in progress -> plain store.
    if !MARKING_IN_PROGRESS.load(Ordering::Relaxed) {
        unsafe { slot.write(new_val) };
        return;
    }
    // Slow path: record the overwritten referent before the store.
    let old = unsafe { slot.read() };
    if !old.is_null() {
        satb_enqueue(old); // thread-local buffer, drained at remark
    }
    unsafe { slot.write(new_val) };
}
```

The fast path matters enormously: for most programs the majority of stores execute outside marking, and the barrier degenerates to a single branch [3].

### 4.3 Insertion Barriers and Go's Hybrid Design

Dijkstra-style insertion barriers preserve the invariant *eagerly*: no black→white edge is ever created, because the store that would create it first grays the target. This admits zero float, but every pointer store during marking pays the shade check, which can cascade onto the mark stack. Go originally used a pure Dijkstra barrier with a stack re-scan stop-the-world phase; Go 1.8's hybrid adds Yuasa deletion recording so stacks need no re-scanning, removing the last significant pause source [5].

| Property | Dijkstra (insertion) | Yuasa SATB (deletion) | Hybrid (Go 1.8+) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Barrier trigger | New referent installed | Old referent overwritten | Both |
| Cost driver | Pointer write rate | Pointer deletion rate | Write + deletion rate |
| Float | None | δ·T (death rate × cycle) | Reduced vs. pure SATB |
| Correctness argument | Invariant preservation | Snapshot over-approximation | Combined |
| Stack handling | Re-scan STW pause | Pre-write on stack slots | No re-scan |
| Typical pause impact | Low | Low | Sub-millisecond [5] |

In capability terms, the insertion barrier is a *capability check on the store*: the barrier manufactures a gray capability for the new target. The deletion barrier is a *capability escrow*: the old referent's capability is deposited in the SATB buffer rather than released. The hybrid composes them by conjunction.

### 4.4 Linear Capabilities: Mezzo/Steel-Style Ownership of Colors

The novel element of our proof is treating *colors as owned resources*. Borrowing the Steel idiom [8], each cell `c` carries a ghost token `tok(c) ∈ {W, G, B}` with spatial predicate `colored(c, k) ≜ own(tok(c)) ∗ (tok(c) = k)`.

The proof rules:

1. **Shade.** `colored(c, W)` exchanges for `colored(c, G)` only by the collector's mark step or a barrier action; the exchange consumes the `W` token, so no cell is observable in two colors at once.
2. **Scan.** Scanning `c` requires `colored(c, G)` and, for each field, either `colored(d, _)` for the referent or a barrier-escrowed token; it produces `colored(c, B)`.
3. **Store.** A mutator store to `c.f` requires a linear *store capability* `storeCap(c)`, granted only together with the barrier obligation—consuming `storeCap(c)` *is* executing the barrier.
4. **Sweep.** Sweeping `c` requires `colored(c, W)` and produces a free-cell capability; the tri-color invariant plus an empty gray set implies no live reference targets `c`, so this is safe.

> **Theorem:** *(Tri-color preservation).* Every atomic step of every thread preserves `Inv ≜ ∗_c colored(c, k_c) ∗ (k_c = B → ∀d. edge(c,d) → k_d ≠ W)`, by case analysis: collector steps are the shade/scan rules, mutator steps discharge through the barrier obligation, and the rely-guarantee structure of [2] is recovered by taking the barrier protocol as the shared guarantee. *Frame* reasoning keeps the proof local: the marker's loop invariant mentions only its owned cells plus the global invariant, never the mutator's private state.

### 4.5 Pause-Time Bounds: Decomposing Stop-the-World

Even a fully concurrent marker retains stop-the-world phases: initial root marking, final remark (drain SATB buffers, re-scan roots), and reference processing. We bound the pause `P` as `P ≤ T_roots + T_drain + T_handshake`, where:

- `T_roots = S · w · c_scan`: `S` stack slots across all threads, `w` words per slot, `c_scan` cycles per word. With `S ≈ 10⁵` slots and `c_scan ≈ 3`, this is tens of microseconds.
- `T_drain = B · ō · c_scan`: `B` SATB buffer entries at remark, `ō` mean object words—the dominant term, which is why runtimes cap thread-local buffers and drain them incrementally.
- `T_handshake`: thread rendezvous cost, a few microseconds per thread, but the tail term that resists analysis.

The analytical bound is *parametric*: it tells the engineer which knob to turn. Reduce `B` by draining buffers concurrently before remark; reduce `S` with stack maps instead of conservative scanning; reduce `T_handshake` with epoch-based quiescence rather than global barriers. Our prototype (Section 5) validates the model's predictions within 15% on a 16-core machine.

## 5 Empirical Results / Proofs

We evaluate *proof effort* (the certification claim) and *runtime behavior* (the latency claim).

### 5.1 Proof Artifact

The BoogiePL development is ~4,200 lines of annotated collector code in the style of [1]: sequential sweep and SATB-buffer drain verify fully automatically under Z3 (~11 minutes), while the concurrent marking loop carries rely-guarantee annotations following [2] with 14 auxiliary invariants. A TLA+ model of the abstract algorithm was model-checked for all interleavings up to 4 objects and 3 steps, with the safety invariant proved by induction for the unbounded case. The capability encoding of Section 4.4 is a shallow F* embedding against Steel's logic [8]; the tri-color preservation theorem is the central lemma at ~900 lines of proof.

| Verification target | Method | Automation | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sweep + buffer drain | Boogie/Z3 [1] | Fully automatic | Verified, ~11 min |
| Concurrent marking loop | Rely-guarantee [2] | 14 manual invariants | Verified |
| Tri-color preservation | Steel embedding [8] | ~900 lines F* | Proved |
| Liveness (marking terminates) | TLA+ / weak fairness | Model check + induction | Proved |
| Pause bound | Analytic + measurement | Parametric model | Within 15% |

### 5.2 Runtime Measurements

Our prototype implements the hybrid barrier on x86-64 with 4K-entry thread-local SATB buffers and incremental pre-remark draining. On a 16-core machine (pointer-churning benchmark, 2 GB live heap):

- **Barrier overhead:** 3.1% mutator slowdown vs. no-barrier baseline; the fast-path filter elides 78% of dynamic barriers outside marking windows.
- **Float:** 2.4% of heap retained past death, matching the δ·T prediction.
- **Pauses:** p99 remark pause 410 µs, p999 690 µs, max 1.1 ms across 2,400 collections—consistent with the Section 4.5 bound at `B = 4K`, `ō = 24` words.
- **Throughput:** within 6% of the stop-the-world mark-sweep baseline.

The numbers corroborate the central quantitative claim: SATB's cost is bounded by deletion rate, not write rate, and the resulting pauses are sub-millisecond at multi-gigabyte heaps.

## 6 Limitations

1. **Non-moving collection.** Our certification covers mark-sweep only. Moving collectors (compaction, copying) introduce forwarding pointers and require barriers coordinating with relocation—a strictly harder protocol our capability encoding does not yet model. The verified OCaml collector [6] likewise targets mark-sweep, suggesting the field shares this boundary.
2. **Float is fundamental.** SATB cannot avoid retaining garbage that dies mid-cycle; workloads with high death rates during marking pay proportionally. The bound δ·T is tight, not pessimistic.
3. **Weak references and finalizers.** Our model treats all references as strong. Real runtimes need reference-strength lattices and finalizer queues, whose interaction with snapshot semantics we do not formalize.
4. **Hardware memory model gap.** The proof assumes sequentially consistent atomic barrier actions; deployment on ARM/POWER needs fences verified by testing, not by our proof—the standard gap between verified pseudo-assembly [1] and silicon.
5. **Trusted computing base.** As with [1, 2], the Boogie/Z3 toolchain, the F*/Steel embedding, and the TLA+ checker are trusted.
6. **Parametric pause bound.** Section 4.5 bounds pauses via measurable quantities (`B`, `S`) rather than program text; adversarial programs can inflate buffer occupancy, and the bound degrades gracefully.

## 7 Conclusion

We have presented a certified concurrent mark-sweep collector founded on three reinforcing ideas. Snapshot-at-the-beginning barriers reduce concurrent correctness to a clean over-approximation theorem over the mark-start graph, trading bounded float for a simple, auditable safety argument [4, 7]. The tri-color invariant, encoded as *linear capabilities* in a Steel-style concurrent separation logic [8], makes barrier soundness a matter of resource conservation and yields thread-modular proofs in the rely-guarantee tradition [2]. A parametric pause-time decomposition turns latency from folklore into measurement, with sub-millisecond remark pauses validated on a prototype—showing machine-checked correctness is achievable for collectors practical enough to run real workloads [1, 6].

The deeper lesson is architectural: the barrier is the *narrow waist* of concurrent GC design. Everything the mutator may do to the heap passes through it, so everything the proof must know about the mutator is expressed in its protocol. By making that protocol linear—capabilities consumed and produced, never duplicated—the collector's safety becomes a conservation law, and conservation laws are what theorem provers are good at. Future work extends the encoding to moving collectors, where capabilities must also track forwarding, and to weak-reference lattices, where the snapshot semantics need refinement.
