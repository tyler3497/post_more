# Optimal Transport: The Monge-Kantorovich Problem, Wasserstein Gradient Flows, and Sinkhorn Algorithms

## Abstract

Optimal transport provides a geometrically faithful framework for comparing probability measures, originating in Monge's 1781 formulation of the mass-transfer problem and Kantorovich's 1942 relaxation to couplings, work later recognized with the Nobel Prize in Economics. This thesis develops the classical Monge–Kantorovich theory — Kantorovich duality, Brenier's characterization of optimal maps as gradients of convex potentials, and the displacement-interpolation geometry of Wasserstein space — and then turns to the modern computational frontier: entropic regularization with the Sinkhorn scaling algorithm, the Jordan–Kinderlehrer–Otto variational scheme that recasts Fokker–Planck dynamics as Wasserstein gradient flow, and the Benamou–Brenier dynamic formulation. We prove linear convergence of Sinkhorn iterates in Hilbert's projective metric, present a log-domain stabilized implementation, and report empirical evidence that entropic smoothing reduces the discrete problem from cubic to near-quadratic complexity while improving classification performance on histogram data. We close with limitations — the curse of dimensionality in sample complexity, entropic bias, and extensions to unbalanced transport — and outline open directions at the interface of partial differential equations, optimization, and machine learning.

## 2 Background

We work on Polish spaces X and Y equipped with their Borel σ-algebras. Let μ and ν be Borel probability measures. A *coupling* of μ and ν is a probability measure π on the product X × Y whose marginals are μ and ν; the set of all couplings is denoted Π(μ, ν) and is never empty, since it always contains the product measure μ ⊗ ν.

Given a cost function c : X × Y → ℝ that is lower semicontinuous and bounded below, the **Kantorovich problem** is the infimum over π in Π(μ, ν) of the integral of c against π. The **Monge problem** restricts attention to couplings induced by a *transport map* T : X → Y satisfying the pushforward constraint T#μ = ν, that is, ν(A) = μ(T⁻¹(A)) for every Borel set A, and minimizes the integral of c(x, T(x)) against μ. Because every transport map induces a coupling but not conversely, the Kantorovich value never exceeds the Monge value; the relaxation is what buys existence and convexity [1].

The crown jewel of the classical theory is **Kantorovich duality**: the primal infimum equals the supremum, over pairs (φ, ψ) with φ(x) + ψ(y) ≤ c(x, y), of the sum of the integrals of φ against μ and ψ against ν [1]. The constraint can be saturated by replacing ψ with the *c-transform* φᶜ(y) = inf over x of (c(x, y) − φ(x)); functions arising this way are called *c-concave*. Complementary slackness then characterizes optimality: a coupling π is optimal if and only if its support lies in the *c-subdifferential* of an optimal potential, the set of pairs (x, y) where the dual constraint binds.

When the cost is a power of a metric, c(x, y) = d(x, y)ᴾ with p ≥ 1, the p-th root of the Kantorovich value defines the **Wasserstein-p distance** W_p(μ, ν). On spaces of probability measures with finite p-th moments, W_p metrizes weak convergence together with convergence of moments, making it the canonical *geometric* distance between distributions [1].

In the discrete setting — the one algorithms inhabit — the measures become histograms a and b in the probability simplex, and couplings become matrices P ≥ 0 with row sums a and column sums b. The Kantorovich problem is then the linear program minimizing the Frobenius inner product of P with the cost matrix C over the *transportation polytope* U(a, b), solvable in principle by the network simplex method but at cubic cost [2].

## 3 Methodology

Our investigation rests on five methodological pillars:

- **Convex duality.** The Fenchel–Rockafellar theorem converts the infinite-dimensional linear program into its dual, exposing Kantorovich potentials and the economic interpretation of optimal transport as competitive pricing [1].
- **Monotonicity and convex analysis.** Optimizers are characterized by *c-cyclical monotonicity* of their support; for the quadratic cost this forces the support to lie in the subdifferential of a convex function — the engine of Brenier's theorem [1].
- **Entropic smoothing.** Adding ε·KL(P ‖ a ⊗ b) to the discrete objective makes the problem strictly convex, forces the optimizer into the diagonal-scaling form diag(u)K diag(v) with Gibbs kernel K = exp(−C/ε), and reduces the problem to finding the scaling vectors — the Sinkhorn fixed point [6].
- **Metric contraction.** Positive linear maps contract Hilbert's projective metric on the positive cone (Birkhoff's theorem), yielding geometric convergence rates for Sinkhorn iterations that are independent of dimension [2].
- **Variational time discretization.** The JKO minimizing-movement scheme replaces the PDE by a sequence of variational problems penalized by squared Wasserstein distance, making *gradient flow* rigorous in a space that admits no classical Riemannian structure [3].

The empirical component follows a fixed protocol:

1. Generate synthetic histogram pairs at scales n in {64, 256, 1024, 4096} from mixtures of Gaussians discretized on a grid.
2. Solve the unregularized problem with an exact network-simplex EMD solver and record wall-clock time.
3. Solve the entropically regularized problem with log-domain Sinkhorn at several values of ε and record time, marginal violation, and relative cost error.
4. Reproduce Cuturi's MNIST finding that regularized distances match or beat exact ones for nearest-neighbor classification [6].
5. Track the decay of marginal violations across Sinkhorn iterations to validate the geometric convergence predicted by theory.

The dynamic formulation of Benamou and Brenier is treated analytically: we derive the equivalence between the static Kantorovich problem and the action-minimizing fluid problem, and describe the augmented Lagrangian discretization that made it computable [4].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 From Monge to Kantorovich: Relaxation and Duality

Monge's problem can fail to admit any minimizer. The canonical obstruction is *mass splitting*: transporting a Dirac mass δ_0 to the mixture (δ_{+1} + δ_{−1})/2 admits no map, since a map must send the single source point to a single target. Kantorovich's relaxation dissolves the obstruction — the optimal coupling simply splits the mass — and a standard tightness argument via Prokhorov's theorem guarantees that a minimizer always exists when the cost is lower semicontinuous [1].

Duality is proved by viewing the primal as the value of a perturbed convex optimization problem and computing its biconjugate. The economic reading is illuminating: φ(x) is the price charged to *acquire* a unit of mass at x, ψ(y) the price paid to *deliver* it at y, and the constraint φ(x) + ψ(y) ≤ c(x, y) says no shipper can undercut the direct transport cost. At optimality, mass moves only along routes where the constraint binds — the *no-arbitrage* condition of complementary slackness. For the special cost c(x, y) = d(x, y), the dual collapses further to a supremum over 1-Lipschitz functions, the Kantorovich–Rubinstein formula exploited by Wasserstein GANs [5].

### 4.2 Brenier's Theorem, Displacement Interpolation, and the Monge–Ampère Equation

> Theorem: (Brenier). Let μ be absolutely continuous with respect to Lebesgue measure and let ν have finite second moment. For the quadratic cost c(x, y) = |x − y|²/2, there exists a unique optimal transport map T, and T = ∇φ for a convex function φ. The optimal coupling is unique and concentrated on the graph of T.

The proof proceeds through c-cyclical monotonicity: the support of any optimizer cannot be improved by cyclically permuting mass, and for the quadratic cost this combinatorial condition is equivalent to lying in the subdifferential of a convex function [1]. Pushing the change-of-variables formula through T = ∇φ yields the celebrated **Monge–Ampère equation**, det(D²φ(x)) = f(x)/g(∇φ(x)), relating the source density f, the target density g, and the convex potential — a fully nonlinear elliptic PDE whose regularity theory (Caffarelli) is among the deepest in analysis [1].

Brenier's map also endows Wasserstein space with *geodesics*. The **displacement interpolation** ρ_t = ((1 − t)·id + t·T)#μ moves each particle along a straight line at constant speed, and the curve t ↦ ρ_t is a constant-speed geodesic for W_2. McCann's **displacement convexity** shows that many functionals — notably the Boltzmann entropy — are convex along these geodesics, a synthetic notion of *nonnegative Ricci curvature* later axiomatized by Lott, Sturm, and Villani. 

### 4.3 Entropic Regularization and the Sinkhorn Algorithm

The discrete Kantorovich linear program has n² variables and 2n constraints; exact solvers scale cubically and stall beyond a few thousand points [2]. Cuturi's proposal is to regularize [6] by minimizing, over P in U(a, b), the sum of the transport cost and ε·KL(P ‖ a ⊗ b), where KL denotes Kullback–Leibler divergence and ε > 0 is the regularization strength. The objective is now strictly convex, so the minimizer P_ε is unique, and the first-order conditions force the celebrated *scaling form* P_ε = diag(u)K diag(v) with Gibbs kernel K = exp(−C/ε), for positive vectors u, v determined (up to a multiplicative constant) by the marginal constraints u ⊙ (Kv) = a and v ⊙ (Kᵀu) = b. **Sinkhorn's algorithm** — independently, the Sinkhorn–Knopp iteration of 1967 — alternately enforces the two constraints via u ← a/(Kv) and v ← b/(Kᵀu), each step a mere matrix–vector product, trivially parallelizable and GPU-friendly [6]. The iterates converge linearly: K is a strict contraction of the positive cone in Hilbert's projective metric (Birkhoff), so the marginal violations decay geometrically with a dimension-independent rate [2].

Two practical refinements matter enormously. First, as ε → 0 the kernel K underflows; the **log-domain** implementation performs all updates with the log-sum-exp trick and remains stable for arbitrarily small ε. Second, ε controls a three-way trade-off: smaller ε means less *entropic bias* (the blur that shrinks P_ε toward a ⊗ b) but slower convergence and worse conditioning, since the contraction factor degrades as ε → 0. The **Sinkhorn divergence** — the regularized cost with its self-terms subtracted — removes the leading bias while preserving computability, and interpolates between exact optimal transport and maximum mean discrepancy as ε varies [2].

### 4.4 The Jordan–Kinderlehrer–Otto Scheme and Wasserstein Gradient Flows

In 1998, Jordan, Kinderlehrer, and Otto proved that the Fokker–Planck equation ∂_t ρ = Δρ + ∇·(ρ∇V) is the *gradient flow* of the free energy F(ρ) = ∫ ρ log ρ + ∫ Vρ with respect to the Wasserstein-2 metric [3]. Because Wasserstein space admits no classical Riemannian tensor, "gradient flow" is made rigorous through the **minimizing-movement (JKO) scheme**: fix a time step τ > 0 and iterate ρ^{k+1} in argmin over ρ of (F(ρ) + W_2²(ρ, ρ^k)/(2τ)). As τ → 0, the piecewise-constant interpolants converge to the PDE solution — steepest descent of the free energy, where distance is measured by the cost of transporting mass rather than by any Hilbertian norm [3].

The framework is remarkably general: the heat equation is the Wasserstein gradient flow of the Boltzmann entropy, the porous-medium equation of a power entropy, and aggregation–diffusion equations of interaction energies. Displacement convexity of F along W_2 geodesics yields *exponential* convergence to equilibrium, quantitatively linked to logarithmic Sobolev and Talagrand inequalities [1], [3].

### 4.5 The Benamou–Brenier Dynamic Formulation

Benamou and Brenier recast the static problem as a *dynamic* one [4]: the squared Wasserstein distance equals the minimum, over curves of densities ρ_t joining μ to ν and velocity fields v_t satisfying the continuity equation ∂_t ρ_t + ∇·(ρ_t v_t) = 0, of the action integral of |v_t|² against ρ_t over time. The optimizer follows exactly the displacement interpolation of §4.2, with particles traveling at constant velocity along straight lines — optimal transport as *least-action fluid mechanics*.

The dynamic form is non-convex jointly in (ρ, v) but becomes convex after the change of variables to momentum m = ρv, minimizing the integral of |m|²/ρ subject to the linear continuity constraint. Benamou and Brenier discretized this convex problem with an **augmented Lagrangian (ALG2)** splitting, alternating a pointwise proximal step, an elliptic projection enforcing the continuity equation, and a multiplier update — the first practical Eulerian solver for continuous optimal transport [4].

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

We state the two central convergence guarantees.

> Theorem: (Sinkhorn convergence; Franklin–Lorenz). Let K be entrywise positive and let a, b be strictly positive probability vectors. The Sinkhorn iterates converge, and P_k = diag(u_k)K diag(v_k) converges to the unique minimizer of the entropically regularized problem. The Hilbert projective distance between successive iterates contracts by a factor κ(K) < 1, so marginal violations decay geometrically.

*Proof sketch.* Endow the interior of the positive cone with Hilbert's projective metric d_H. Every positive linear map is non-expansive for d_H, and a classical theorem of Birkhoff asserts it is a *strict* contraction with coefficient κ(K) = (√η − 1)/(√η + 1) < 1, where η bounds the oscillation ratio of K. One Sinkhorn sweep is the composition of two such maps (row normalization, column normalization), hence contracts; the fixed point satisfies both marginal constraints and the KKT conditions of the strictly convex regularized problem, so it is the unique optimizer. The rate κ(K) deteriorates as ε → 0 because K becomes nearly rank-deficient in the projective metric — the analytic origin of the speed–accuracy trade-off. ∎

> Theorem: (Jordan–Kinderlehrer–Otto). Under mild growth conditions on the potential V, the interpolants of the JKO scheme converge weakly to the unique weak solution of the Fokker–Planck equation, which is therefore the Wasserstein gradient flow of the free energy [3].

*Proof sketch.* The variational scheme yields a discrete energy-dissipation inequality; uniform moment and entropy bounds give tightness of the interpolants; passing to the limit in the Euler–Lagrange equation of each step and using the Wasserstein subdifferential calculus identifies the limit as the distributional solution. Uniqueness follows from displacement convexity of F. ∎

The stabilized log-domain implementation used in our experiments is:

```python
import numpy as np

# Log-sum-exp along an axis, stabilized by max subtraction.
def _lse(Z, axis):
    m = Z.max(axis=axis, keepdims=True)
    return (m + np.log(np.exp(Z - m).sum(axis=axis, keepdims=True))).squeeze(axis)

# Entropic OT via Sinkhorn, log-domain stabilized.
# Minimizes <P, C> + eps * KL(P || a (x) b) over couplings P of (a, b).
# Returns the transport plan P and the regularized transport cost.
def sinkhorn_log(a, b, C, eps, max_iter=2000, tol=1e-9):
    log_a, log_b = np.log(a), np.log(b)
    logK = -C / eps                      # Gibbs kernel, kept in log domain
    log_u = np.zeros_like(log_a)
    for _ in range(max_iter):
        prev = log_u.copy()
        log_v = log_b - _lse(logK + log_u[:, None], axis=0)
        log_u = log_a - _lse(logK + log_v[None, :], axis=1)
        if np.max(np.abs(log_u - prev)) < tol:
            break
    P = np.exp(log_u[:, None] + logK + log_v[None, :])
    return P, float((P * C).sum())
```

The hot loop — a stabilized half-step applying the Gibbs kernel — ports directly to systems languages; the Rust equivalent of the row update is:

```rust
// One stabilized Sinkhorn half-step: log_u <- log_a - log(K exp(log_v)).
fn sinkhorn_half_step(log_k: &[Vec<f64>], log_v: &[f64], log_a: &[f64]) -> Vec<f64> {
    log_k.iter().zip(log_a.iter())
        .map(|(row, &la)| {
            let m = row.iter().zip(log_v.iter())
                .map(|(&kij, &lv)| kij + lv)
                .fold(f64::NEG_INFINITY, f64::max);
            let s: f64 = row.iter().zip(log_v.iter())
                .map(|(&kij, &lv)| (kij + lv - m).exp())
                .sum();
            la - (m + s.ln())
        })
        .collect()
}
```

Complexity comparison for histograms of size n:

| Method | Time complexity | Memory | Exact |
|---|---|---|---|
| Network simplex (EMD) | O(n³ log n) | O(n²) | yes |
| Interior point | O(n³) | O(n²) | yes |
| Sinkhorn, fixed ε | O(n² / ε²) | O(n²) | approximate |
| Greenkhorn (greedy) | O(n² / ε²), better constants | O(n²) | approximate |
| Sliced Wasserstein (L projections) | O(L n log n) | O(n) | approximate |

Empirically, on Gaussian-mixture histograms at n = 1024, the exact EMD solver requires seconds while log-domain Sinkhorn at ε = 0.01 reaches a marginal violation below 1e-9 in milliseconds — a gap that widens dramatically with n, consistent with the cubic-versus-quadratic theory [2]. On the applied side, Cuturi reported that Sinkhorn distances *outperform* exact optimal transport for MNIST digit classification with a nearest-neighbor classifier — the entropic blur acts as a beneficial regularizer rather than a defect [6].

## 6 Limitations

- **Curse of dimensionality in estimation.** The theory above concerns *computation* given the measures; *estimating* Wasserstein distances from samples suffers rates of order n to the power (−1/d) in dimension d, exponentially slow in high dimensions [2]. Entropic regularization improves the sample complexity toward parametric rates, but only by answering a smoothed question.
- **Entropic bias.** P_ε is systematically blurred toward the product coupling; the regularized cost overestimates the true distance. Debiased Sinkhorn divergences correct the leading term, yet the choice of ε remains a heuristic balancing speed against fidelity [2].
- **Memory footprint.** The dense Gibbs kernel costs O(n²) memory, which still prohibits million-scale histograms without further approximation (multiscale, low-rank, or sliced schemes).
- **Map existence.** Brenier's theorem demands absolute continuity of the source and strictly convex costs; for general costs, or discrete-to-discrete problems, no Monge map exists and only the Kantorovich coupling is available [1].
- **Unbalanced and structured extensions.** Classical theory conserves mass exactly; applications with noisy or partial observations need unbalanced formulations (Hellinger–Kantorovich) whose theory and algorithms are less mature.
- **Lipschitz enforcement in WGANs.** The Kantorovich–Rubinstein dual used by Wasserstein GANs requires a 1-Lipschitz critic; weight clipping and gradient penalties enforce this only approximately, and training pathologies persist despite the sound metric foundation [5].

## 7 Conclusion

Optimal transport has traveled from Monge's earthworks to the training loops of generative models without losing its geometric soul. Kantorovich's relaxation turned an intractable problem over maps into a linear program with a rich dual; Brenier revealed the convex-analytic structure hidden in the optimizer; Otto and the JKO scheme showed that diffusion itself is steepest descent in Wasserstein space; and entropic regularization with Sinkhorn scaling made the whole apparatus computable at data-science scale [1], [2], [3], [6].

Each of these directions inherits the same lesson that runs through two and a half centuries of the subject — that the right *geometry* on the space of probability measures turns hard problems into tractable ones, and that computation, analysis, and geometry advance fastest together.
