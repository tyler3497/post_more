# Differential Privacy at Scale: Rényi Accounting, Shuffle Amplification, and DP-SGD from Theory to Deployed Systems

## Abstract

Differential privacy (DP) has matured from a theoretical framework for statistical databases into the standard formalism for privacy-preserving machine learning at industrial scale. This article develops the full technical stack behind modern DP training: the classical (ε, δ)-DP definition and sensitivity-calibrated noise mechanisms [1], the Rényi DP (RDP) and concentrated DP relaxations that enable tight composition accounting [3], the moments accountant of Abadi et al. that made private deep learning practical [2], privacy amplification by random shuffling in the shuffle model that bridges local and central DP [4][5], and DP-SGD — differentially private stochastic gradient descent with per-example clipping and Gaussian noise — as implemented in production libraries [9]. We derive the subsampled-Gaussian RDP profile, prove the composition and conversion theorems that underpin modern accountants, and analyze the asymptotic O(ε₀√(log(1/δ)/n)) amplification achievable by shuffling ε₀-locally private reports. Empirical evidence from deployed systems — Apple's local-DP telemetry [8] and TensorFlow Privacy's DP-SGD MNIST benchmarks [9] — grounds the theory, and we close with a systematic audit methodology and the hard limits of what DP can promise.

---

## 1. Introduction

Modern machine learning systems are trained on data that is profoundly personal: keystrokes, location traces, medical images, private messages. A model that memorizes its training set — and large neural networks *do* memorize, as demonstrated by extraction attacks on language models — becomes a liability. Differential privacy [1] offers the only known *quantitative, composable, and worst-case* guarantee against such leakage: the distribution of any output changes only negligibly when a single individual's record is added to or removed from the training data.

The definition is deceptively simple, but two decades of research have revealed that *computing* the guarantee in practice — over thousands of adaptive training steps, heterogeneous mechanisms, and distributed deployments — is the central engineering challenge. Three developments transformed DP from a theorist's instrument into a production tool:

1. **Rényi differential privacy (RDP)** [3] and zero-concentrated DP (zCDP), which reframe privacy loss as a Rényi divergence and compose by *addition* rather than by the loose strong-composition theorem.
2. **The moments accountant** [2], which tracks the cumulant generating function of the privacy-loss random variable to obtain near-tight bounds for iterative Gaussian mechanisms, making private deep learning feasible at single-digit ε.
3. **The shuffle model** [4][5], which shows that randomly permuting locally-private reports amplifies privacy by a √n factor — converting expensive local guarantees into cheap central ones without any trusted aggregator.

This article unifies these threads. We begin with the classical foundations, develop the RDP formalism and its subsampled variant in full mathematical detail, analyze shuffle amplification with its asymptotically optimal bounds, dissect DP-SGD step by step, and finally discuss *auditing* — empirically verifying that implemented systems satisfy their claimed bounds — and the fundamental limitations of the framework.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 The classical definition

Two datasets $D, D'$ are *neighboring* (written $D \sim D'$) if they differ in at most one record. A randomized mechanism $\mathcal{M}: \mathbb{D} \to \mathcal{Y}$ satisfies **$(\varepsilon, \delta)$-differential privacy** if for all neighboring $D \sim D'$ and all measurable $S \subseteq \mathcal{Y}$:

$$
\Pr[\mathcal{M}(D) \in S] \le e^{\varepsilon}\, \Pr[\mathcal{M}(D') \in S] + \delta.
$$

The parameter $\varepsilon$ bounds the multiplicative leakage; $\delta$ is a small additive failure probability, conventionally taken cryptographically small (e.g., $10^{-5}$ or $1/n^{1.1}$ where $n$ is the dataset size) [1]. When $\delta = 0$ we speak of *pure* DP.

> **Theorem: Post-processing.** If $\mathcal{M}$ is $(\varepsilon, \delta)$-DP and $f$ is any randomized mapping, then $f \circ \mathcal{M}$ is $(\varepsilon, \delta)$-DP. Privacy cannot be destroyed by computation alone.

The workhorse mechanisms calibrate noise to *sensitivity*. For a function $f: \mathbb{D} \to \mathbb{R}^d$, the $\ell_p$-sensitivity is $\Delta_p f = \max_{D \sim D'} \|f(D) - f(D')\|_p$. The **Laplace mechanism** adds $\mathrm{Lap}(\Delta_1 f / \varepsilon)$ noise per coordinate to achieve $(\varepsilon, 0)$-DP; the **Gaussian mechanism** adds $\mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2)$ with

$$
\sigma = \frac{\sqrt{2\ln(1.25/\delta)} \cdot \Delta_2 f}{\varepsilon} \qquad (\varepsilon < 1)
$$

to achieve $(\varepsilon, \delta)$-DP [1]. The Gaussian mechanism is the foundation of DP-SGD because its $\ell_2$ geometry matches gradient clipping.

### 2.2 The composition problem

Training a neural network applies thousands of noisy gradient steps. The **basic composition theorem** says that $k$ adaptively chosen $(\varepsilon, \delta)$-DP mechanisms compose to $(k\varepsilon, k\delta)$-DP — vacuous after a few hundred steps. The *advanced composition theorem* improves this to $\tilde{O}(\sqrt{k}\varepsilon)$ [1], but still leaves slack that destroys utility: the moments-accountant line of work [2] demonstrated that mechanism-specific accounting — tracking the *exact* privacy-loss distribution of the Gaussian mechanism rather than worst-casing over all $(\varepsilon, \delta)$-DP mechanisms — recovers the constants needed for practical deep learning.

### 2.3 Local, central, and shuffle models

In the **central (curator) model**, a trusted party holds raw data and releases noisy statistics. In the **local model**, each user randomizes their own record before transmission; the server never sees raw data, but error scales as $\sqrt{n}$ worse. The **shuffle model** [4] interposes an *anonymizing shuffler*: users apply local randomizers, a trusted shuffler strips identifiers and applies a uniform random permutation $\pi$, and the analyst observes only the multiset of reports. The anonymity destroys linkage between users and messages, which amplifies privacy — the subject of Section 4.2.

## 3. Methodology

Our methodology is analytic and constructive. We (i) formalize RDP and its conversion to $(\varepsilon, \delta)$-DP, proving composition and conversion theorems; (ii) derive the subsampled-Gaussian RDP profile used by DP-SGD accounting, following the analytic moments-accountant program [6]; (iii) present the shuffle-amplification analysis of [4][5] with its optimal $\varepsilon_0$-dependence; (iv) specify DP-SGD as a concrete algorithm with per-example clipping and give its reference implementation structure [9]; and (v) describe empirical auditing — turning the guarantee into a falsifiable claim via membership-inference experiments. Throughout, we track *both* the theoretical bound and the constants that determine production feasibility.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Rényi Differential Privacy and Composition Accounting

The Rényi divergence of order $\alpha > 1$ between distributions $P, Q$ is

$$
D_\alpha(P \,\|\, Q) := \frac{1}{\alpha - 1} \log \mathbb{E}_{y \sim Q}\left[\left(\frac{p(y)}{q(y)}\right)^{\alpha}\right],
$$

with $D_1$ defined by continuity as the KL divergence and $D_\infty$ as the max log-likelihood ratio [3]. A mechanism $\mathcal{M}$ satisfies **$(\alpha, \varepsilon)$-RDP** if for all neighboring $D \sim D'$, $D_\alpha(\mathcal{M}(D) \,\|\, \mathcal{M}(D')) \le \varepsilon$. A mechanism typically satisfies a *family* of guarantees $\{\varepsilon(\alpha)\}_{\alpha > 1}$ called its **RDP profile**; the entire profile — not any single point — is the operative accounting object.

> **Theorem: Adaptive composition of RDP** [3, Prop. 1]. If $\mathcal{M}_1$ satisfies $(\alpha, \varepsilon_1)$-RDP and $\mathcal{M}_2(\cdot, \mathcal{M}_1(\cdot))$ satisfies $(\alpha, \varepsilon_2)$-RDP, then the composition satisfies $(\alpha, \varepsilon_1 + \varepsilon_2)$-RDP. *Privacy loss adds linearly in RDP — no $\sqrt{k}$ factor, no heterogeneity restriction.*

> **Theorem: RDP to DP conversion** [3, Prop. 3]. If $\mathcal{M}$ satisfies $(\alpha, \varepsilon)$-RDP, then it satisfies
>
> $$
> \left(\varepsilon + \frac{\log(1/\delta)}{\alpha - 1},\; \delta\right)\text{-DP} \qquad \forall\, \delta \in (0,1).
> $$
>
> In practice one optimizes over the integer (or real) orders $\alpha$ to minimize the right-hand side for the target $\delta$.

The Gaussian mechanism has an especially clean RDP profile: adding $\mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2)$ to a $\Delta_2$-sensitive query satisfies $(\alpha, \alpha \Delta_2^2 / 2\sigma^2)$-RDP [3]. The **moments accountant** [2] is precisely this idea operationalized: it tracks the cumulant generating function $K_{\mathcal{M}}(\lambda) = \log \mathbb{E}[e^{\lambda L}]$ of the privacy-loss random variable $L = \log(\mathcal{M}(D)(\theta)/\mathcal{M}(D')(\theta))$, which for integer $\lambda$ coincides with the RDP profile up to reparameterization, then converts to $(\varepsilon, \delta)$-DP via the tail bound $\delta = \min_\lambda e^{K(\lambda) - \lambda \varepsilon}$. Wang et al. [6] extended this to the *subsampled* setting — the crucial refinement for stochastic training.

**Amplification by subsampling.** When each step samples a batch with Poisson sampling rate $\gamma = B/n$, the per-step guarantee improves. Classically, an $(\varepsilon, \delta)$-DP mechanism under subsampling with rate $\gamma$ becomes $(\log(1 + \gamma(e^{\varepsilon} - 1)), \gamma \delta)$-DP — roughly $O(\gamma \varepsilon)$ for small $\varepsilon$ [6]. In the RDP calculus this amplification is baked into the subsampled-Gaussian profile: each order-$\alpha$ term is a mixture of binomial-weighted Gaussian RDP terms, computable in closed form [6]. The practical consequence is enormous: sampling 256 examples from 60,000 ($\gamma \approx 0.0043$) suppresses per-step leakage to nearly nothing, and RDP composition keeps the *cumulative* bound tight across epochs.

| Accounting method | Composition rule for $k$ steps | DP-SGD $\varepsilon$ (MNIST, $\delta=10^{-5}$) |
|---|---|---|
| Basic composition | $\varepsilon_g \approx k\varepsilon$ | vacuous ($> 10^2$) |
| Advanced composition | $\varepsilon_g \approx \sqrt{2k\ln(1/\delta')}\varepsilon$ | $\sim 20$–$50$ |
| **Moments accountant / RDP** [2][6] | optimize over $\alpha$ of $\sum \varepsilon_\alpha(\alpha) + \frac{\log(1/\delta)}{\alpha-1}$ | **$\approx 3.01$** (60 epochs, $\sigma=1.1$) [9] |

The characteristic-function (privacy-loss distribution) formalism [7] later showed that the full PLD convolution gives the *exact* composed guarantee, with RDP conversion within a small constant factor of optimal — confirming that RDP accounting is nearly lossless in practice.

### 4.2 Privacy Amplification by Shuffling

The shuffle model asks: if $n$ users each apply an $\varepsilon_0$-*locally* DP randomizer $\mathcal{R}$, and an intermediary shuffler applies a uniform random permutation $\pi$ before the analyst sees the messages, what *central* DP guarantee results? Erlingsson et al. [4] proved that any permutation-invariant algorithm satisfying $\varepsilon_0$-LDP satisfies

$$
\left(O\left(\varepsilon_0\sqrt{\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{n}}\right),\; \delta\right)\text{-central DP},
$$

establishing that anonymity *amplifies* privacy by a $\sqrt{n}$ factor. Intuitively, the adversary observing a shuffled multiset cannot link any single report to its originator; each user's signal "hides among the clones" [5].

Feldman, McMillan, and Talwar [5] gave a simpler, nearly optimal analysis with the asymptotically tight dependence on $\varepsilon_0$:

$$
\varepsilon = O\left((1 - e^{-\varepsilon_0})\sqrt{\frac{e^{\varepsilon_0}\log(1/\delta)}{n}}\right).
$$

They further showed their analysis yields RDP guarantees for the shuffled mechanism and implies the first asymptotically optimal analysis of noisy SGD under sampling *without* replacement [5] — closing a long-standing gap, since practical DP-SGD shuffles epochs rather than Poisson-sampling.

The shuffle model is not merely theoretical. Apple's deployment collects locally-randomized telemetry (emoji usage, QuickType lexicon statistics) with data privatized *on device*, transmitted once daily over an encrypted channel with identifiers discarded [8]. When such reports pass through an anonymizing layer — as Erlingsson et al. noted, "several LDP-based industrial deployments may have much lower privacy cost than their advertised $\varepsilon$ would indicate — at least if reports are anonymized" [4] — the shuffle analysis shows the *effective central* $\varepsilon$ is dramatically smaller than the local $\varepsilon_0$. This is the mechanism by which industry achieves usable accuracy with honest privacy parameters.

### 4.3 DP-SGD: Mechanics of Private Gradient Descent

DP-SGD modifies mini-batch SGD at exactly one point: the gradient aggregation [2]. Given loss $\ell(\theta; x)$, dataset of size $n$, clipping norm $C$, noise multiplier $\sigma$, learning rate $\eta$, and sampling rate $\gamma = L/n$:

```python
# DP-SGD, one training step (conceptual; cf. TensorFlow Privacy [9])
def dp_sgd_step(theta, batch, C, sigma, eta):
    grads = [autodiff_grad(loss, theta, x_i) for x_i in batch]
    # 1. Per-example clipping: bound ell_2 sensitivity to C
    clipped = [g / max(1.0, l2_norm(g) / C) for g in grads]
    # 2. Aggregate and add calibrated Gaussian noise
    noisy_grad = (sum(clipped) + gaussian(0, (sigma * C) ** 2, dim)) / len(batch)
    # 3. Descent step (post-processing: privacy-preserving)
    theta = theta - eta * noisy_grad
    # 4. RDP accountant: accrue one subsampled-Gaussian step
    accountant.step(noise_multiplier=sigma, sample_rate=len(batch) / N)
    return theta
```

Three design points deserve emphasis.

**Per-example clipping is non-negotiable.** Without clipping, a single example's gradient norm is unbounded, so sensitivity is infinite and no finite noise suffices. Clipping each $g_i$ to $\ell_2$-norm $C$ bounds the sensitivity of the batch sum by $C$; adding $\mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2 C^2 I)$ then yields a subsampled Gaussian mechanism whose RDP profile [6] feeds the accountant. Note the subtlety: clipping *biases* the gradient estimator — examples with large gradients are systematically down-weighted — which is one reason DP-SGD degrades on heavy-tailed data and large models.

**The noise multiplier $\sigma$ and clipping norm $C$ interact.** Increasing $C$ reduces clipping bias but scales up the noise standard deviation $\sigma C$ proportionally; the *privacy* cost depends only on $\sigma$ and the sampling rate, not on $C$. In practice one tunes $C$ to the median gradient norm and spends the privacy budget on $\sigma$ [9].

**Microbatching makes it feasible.** Materializing per-example gradients naïvely multiplies memory by the batch size; production libraries compute clipped per-example gradients via vectorized map operations (Opacus) or microbatch splitting (TensorFlow Privacy) [9]. The reference TensorFlow Privacy tutorial reports $\varepsilon = 3.01$ at $\delta = 10^{-5}$ for 60 epochs on MNIST with $\sigma = 1.1$, $C = 1.0$, batch 256 — alongside 96.6% accuracy, versus 99.0% non-private [9]. The privacy-utility gap narrows as datasets grow: sampling rate $\gamma$ shrinks, amplification strengthens, and the same $\sigma$ buys a smaller $\varepsilon$.

### 4.4 Auditing Differential Privacy Guarantees

A claimed $(\varepsilon, \delta)$ bound is a *mathematical* object; an implementation may violate it through clipping bugs, incorrect noise calibration, data-dependent preprocessing, or PRNG flaws. **Privacy auditing** treats the guarantee as falsifiable: construct an adversary (typically a membership-inference attack) and *lower-bound* the empirical privacy loss. If the audited lower bound exceeds the claimed $\varepsilon$, the implementation is wrong.

The audit methodology follows a hypothesis-testing frame. The auditor crafts neighboring datasets $D, D'$ differing in one canary record, trains many models on each, and measures the attacker's true-positive/false-positive tradeoff; the achieved advantage converts to a lower bound on $\varepsilon$ via the DP characterization of hypothesis testing. Modern one-run audits inject many canaries into a single training run and use the shuffle of canary inclusion to parallelize the experiment. A healthy audit satisfies:

$$
\varepsilon_{\text{audit (lower bound)}} \;\lesssim\; \varepsilon_{\text{claimed}},
$$

with the gap quantifying how much of the theoretical bound is *realized* versus merely *provable*. Tight audits of DP-SGD show the moments-accountant bound is nearly tight — the accountant's $\varepsilon$ is not a loose overestimate. Auditing is therefore the bridge between the theorems of Sections 4.1–4.3 and the deployments of Section 5: it is how a production team gains justified confidence that their accountant code, clipping code, and noise code jointly deliver the published parameters.

## 5. Empirical Results and Proofs

**Theorem (Subsampled Gaussian RDP profile) [6].** One DP-SGD step with Poisson sampling rate $\gamma$, clipping norm $C$, and noise multiplier $\sigma$ satisfies $(\alpha, \varepsilon_\alpha)$-RDP for all integers $\alpha \ge 2$, where $\varepsilon_\alpha(\alpha)$ is given by the analytic bound

$$
\varepsilon_\alpha(\alpha) \le \frac{1}{\alpha-1}\log\left((1-\gamma)^{\alpha-1}(\alpha\gamma - \gamma + 1) + \sum_{j=2}^{\alpha} \binom{\alpha}{j}(1-\gamma)^{\alpha-j}\gamma^j e^{(j-1)j/2\sigma^2}\right).
$$

*Proof sketch.* Condition on the subsampling event: with probability $(1-\gamma)^j\binom{\alpha}{j}\gamma^{\alpha-j}$-type mixture weights, the distinguishing record participates in $j$ of the $\alpha$ replicated steps; the non-participating mass contributes divergence $0$, while each participating step contributes the Gaussian RDP term $(j-1)j/2\sigma^2$. The closed form follows from the binomial theorem applied to the RDP of the mixture [6]. ∎

Composing $T$ steps adds the profiles ($\varepsilon_\alpha \gets T \cdot \varepsilon_\alpha$ per step by Theorem 4.1), and conversion optimizes $\min_\alpha [T\varepsilon_\alpha(\alpha) + \log(1/\delta)/(\alpha-1)]$. Numerically, with $\sigma = 1.1$, $\gamma = 256/60000$, $T = 60 \cdot 60000/256$ steps, $\delta = 10^{-5}$, the optimum sits at $\alpha \approx 32$ and yields $\varepsilon \approx 3.01$ [9].

**Empirical results in production:**

- **Apple, iOS telemetry** [8]: local-DP collection of emoji frequencies and QuickType lexicon statistics. Private Count Mean Sketch and Hadamard Count Mean Sketch protocols send a *single privatized bit* per user; the learned lexicon includes slang (*wbu*, *idc*), trending terms, and locale-specific emoji distributions — demonstrating that meaningful population statistics survive strict per-event local DP.
- **TensorFlow Privacy, MNIST** [9]: DP-SGD with default parameters reaches 96.6% accuracy at $\varepsilon = 3.01$ ($\delta = 10^{-5}$), 97.0% at $\varepsilon = 7.10$ — a smooth, predictable privacy-utility frontier.
- **Census-scale deployments** use the same accounting stack (RDP/zCDP) to allocate a global privacy budget across thousands of interdependent queries, proving the formalism scales to nation-scale data releases.

## 6. Limitations

1. **$\varepsilon$ is not a security parameter.** A guarantee of $\varepsilon = 3$ permits the likelihood ratio of an adversary's posterior to shift by $e^3 \approx 20\times$; the operational meaning of any particular $\varepsilon$ remains contested, and conversions from RDP loosen the bound further [7].
2. **Clipping bias and scale.** DP-SGD's per-example clipping interacts poorly with large models and heavy-tailed gradients; utility degrades superlinearly with parameter count at fixed $\varepsilon$, and optimal hyperparameter transfer from non-private training is unreliable.
3. **The shuffle model needs a trusted shuffler.** Amplification evaporates if the shuffler colludes with the analyst or fails to strip metadata; deploying it requires either trusted hardware or cryptographic mixnets, both of which are active research areas [4].
4. **Accounting assumes correct implementation.** The tightest accountant is worthless if noise is miscalibrated, preprocessing is data-dependent, or the PRNG is weak — hence auditing (Section 4.4) is mandatory, not optional.
5. **Composition over heterogeneous releases.** RDP composes heterogeneous mechanisms cleanly, but tracking a *global* budget across an organization — models, dashboards, ad-hoc analyses — remains an unsolved systems problem; the accountant is local to a pipeline.
6. **Fairness and disparate impact.** Noise harms underrepresented subgroups disproportionately; DP-SGD can widen accuracy gaps between majority and minority classes, a tension with fairness objectives that privacy accounting alone cannot resolve.

---

## 7. Conclusion

Differential privacy at scale rests on three pillars developed over the past decade: **Rényi accounting**, which turned composition from a $\sqrt{k}$-blowup into exact additive bookkeeping over divergence profiles [3][6]; **shuffle amplification**, which converts the expensive local model into a cheap central one through anonymity alone [4][5]; and **DP-SGD**, which reduced private deep learning to two code changes — per-example clipping and Gaussian noise — wrapped in a rigorous accountant [2][9]. Deployed systems at Apple and in open-source libraries demonstrate that these are not paper techniques: they ship, they compose, and they can be audited. The remaining frontier is not tighter $\varepsilon$ constants but *systems*: global budget management, cryptographic shufflers, and privacy that coexists with fairness and utility at frontier-model scale.
