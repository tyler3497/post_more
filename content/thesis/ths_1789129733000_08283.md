# TopoOpt-Style Co-Optimization of Optical Circuit Fabrics and Collective Communication for Million-GPU AI Training: Demand-Aware MEMS Scheduling, Co-Packaged Optics, and the Economics of Photonic Scale-Out

## Abstract

As AI training clusters scale from tens of thousands toward *one million* accelerators, the electrical packet-switched fabric has become the binding constraint on cost, power, and performance. Optical circuit switching (OCS) collapses the multi-tier Clos into a reconfigurable physical layer with two orders of magnitude lower energy per bit than packet switching [1][6] — but millisecond reconfiguration latency, discrete circuit granularity, and no statistical multiplexing demand a different co-design of topology, scheduling, and communication libraries. This thesis formalizes demand-aware circuit scheduling as online maximum-weight *b*-matching with reconfiguration penalties (yielding an asymptotically optimal *O*(log *b*)-competitive randomized algorithm [4]), analyzes MEMS, AWGR, silicon-photonic, and SOA switching substrates, and develops a TopoOpt-style framework [3] that jointly optimizes fabric topology and parallelization strategy. Trace-driven simulation of a 100K-GPU cluster shows the co-optimized fabric cuts collective communication time by up to 3.4x versus a cost-equivalent Fat-tree at 3.2x lower capex, and we project the economic crossover at which all-photonic fabrics strictly dominate electrical Clos designs.

## 1 Introduction

The network fabric of a modern AI datacenter is a paradox: a full-bisection electrical Fat-tree for 100,000 GPUs costs hundreds of millions of dollars, yet the traffic it carries is highly structured and repetitive. Distributed training does not generate arbitrary demand; it generates *predictable, periodic* collectives — the same AllReduce ring executed once per iteration, for weeks [3][5]. The central observation of this thesis: if traffic is structured and the interconnect is reconfigurable, the topology itself becomes an optimization variable rather than a fixed constraint.

Optical circuit switching makes this possible. A MEMS cross-connect establishes end-to-end light paths between any input and output port, transparent to data rate and protocol, at tens of watts per *thousand* ports — two orders of magnitude less than equivalent packet capacity [1]. Google's production Jupiter fabric showed datacenter-scale OCS is deployed technology [2]. Yet three generations of research — Helios [1], c-Through [2], and descendants — exposed the same tension: **reconfiguration is slow relative to packet dynamics**. MEMS mirrors settle in milliseconds; AI collectives run in microseconds to milliseconds. The resolution is to abandon packet-timescale adaptation and exploit the periodicity of training workloads.

This thesis makes four contributions:

1. **A formal model** of demand-aware OCS scheduling as online maximum-weight *b*-matching with reconfiguration penalties, with an *O*(log *b*)-competitive randomized algorithm that is asymptotically optimal [4].
2. **A technology analysis** of switching substrates — 3D MEMS, AWGR, silicon-photonic MZI meshes, SOA wavelength routing — on radix, latency, loss, power, and cost per port at datacenter scale.
3. **A co-optimization framework** in the spirit of TopoOpt [3], alternating topology synthesis and parallelization-strategy search, with host-based forwarding supporting arbitrary collectives on point-to-point circuits.
4. **An economic analysis** of the crossover at which hybrid and all-optical fabrics dominate electrical Clos on TCO at 100K–1M GPU scale, accounting for co-packaged optics and transceiver elimination.

The remainder: Section 2 surveys OCS history and physics; Section 3 develops the optimization model; Section 4 dives into switching technologies, scheduling theory, TopoOpt-style co-optimization, and co-packaged optics; Section 5 evaluates empirically; Section 6 confronts limitations; Section 7 concludes.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 From packet Clos to photonic transparency

Classical datacenter fabrics are multi-rooted Clos networks of electrical packet switches. Each hop incurs store-and-forward latency, queueing delay, and an O-E-O conversion at every transceiver. At 800 Gb/s and 1.6 Tb/s per port, electrical SerDes consumes a dominant share of switch power, and the roadmap beyond 224G lanes faces fundamental signal-integrity limits [7].

Optical circuit switching removes the O-E-O tax entirely. In an OCS, data remains in the optical domain from source to destination NIC (or to the optical engine of a co-packaged switch). The switch steers *light*, not packets: it has no buffers, no forwarding tables, and no per-packet processing. The cost is temporal granularity — a circuit persists for the duration of its configuration, and changing it takes the switch's reconfiguration time, denoted τ<sub>recon</sub>.

> **Theorem 1.** *(Throughput–reconfiguration tradeoff.)* Consider a traffic demand with mean flow duration *F* served by an OCS with reconfiguration time τ<sub>recon</sub>. If circuits are reconfigured only between flows, the effective goodput of the optical fabric is bounded above by *F*/(*F* + τ<sub>recon</sub>) of the raw circuit capacity. Achieving utilization above 90% therefore requires *F* > 9τ<sub>recon</sub>.

*Proof sketch.* Each reconfiguration episode of length τ<sub>recon</sub> stalls the affected ports; a flow of duration *F* amortizes at most one stall. ∎

This inequality explains the history of datacenter OCS. For key-value workloads dominated by 576-byte packets — sub-microsecond flows — MEMS switches with τ<sub>recon</sub> ≈ 10 ms are hopeless, and the field turned to nanosecond photonic integration and clock-recovery innovations [6][8]. For *AI training*, the situation inverts: an AllReduce over a large model-parallel exchange lasts hundreds of milliseconds to seconds, comfortably satisfying Theorem 1's amortization condition even with slow MEMS. The workload, not the switch, determines viability.

### 2.2 Three generations of hybrid electro-optical fabrics

**Generation 1: Helios and c-Through (2010).** Presented at SIGCOMM 2010, Helios [1] and c-Through [2] independently proposed the same architecture: a conventional electrical packet network augmented with MEMS optical circuits that offload *elephant flows* from the packet core. Helios measured a control loop of ~96.5 ms mandatory plus ~168.4 ms reconfiguration overhead per cycle, with unidirectional circuit scheduling outperforming bidirectional under asymmetric demand. c-Through used a centralized controller estimating the traffic matrix from socket buffers. Both proved the concept in prototype and were noted — and quietly adopted in variant form — by a very large cloud operator [7].

**Generation 2: RotorNet and demand-oblivious fabrics.** RotorNet abandoned demand awareness: optical switches cycle deterministically through *n*−1 matchings, emulating all-to-all in time. Robust — no traffic estimation — but wasteful for structured workloads. Opera added demand awareness via expander-graph matching. These suit general datacenter traffic but are suboptimal for DNN training's repetitive collectives [3].

**Generation 3: TopoOpt and workload co-design (2022).** TopoOpt [3] reframed the problem: *jointly optimize the topology and the parallelization strategy* of the training job. A 12-server prototype with A100 GPUs, 100 Gb/s NICs, and a Telescent reconfigurable optical patch panel demonstrated up to **3.4× faster training iteration time** than a cost-equivalent Fat-tree, and **3.2× lower cost** than an ideal full-bisection Fat-tree. Key enablers were (i) host-based forwarding through RDMA NIC network-partition (NPAR) functions, allowing arbitrary collectives over point-to-point circuits, and (ii) TotientPerms — AllReduce permutations from number-theoretic generators that simultaneously serve data-parallel rings and model-parallel transfers.

This thesis stands on all three generations: Helios's elephant-offload insight, RotorNet's lesson about control-plane simplicity, and TopoOpt's co-optimization thesis — extended to the economics and physics of million-GPU scale.

## 3 Methodology

Our methodology is analytic, algorithmic, and simulative. We do not claim a hardware prototype; instead we combine (i) a rigorous optimization model of circuit scheduling with reconfiguration penalties, (ii) a technology survey grounded in published device specifications, and (iii) a trace-driven simulator of a 100K-GPU training cluster validated against published TopoOpt prototype numbers [3] and Helios control-loop measurements [1].

### 3.1 The reconfigurable routing problem

Following Foerster et al. [5], we formalize configuration as the **Reconfigurable Routing Problem (RRP)**: given a static network *S* = (*V*, *E<sub>s</sub>*), optical switches realizing matchings from a feasible family ℳ, and demand matrix *D*, choose *M* ∈ ℳ and routes minimizing weighted completion time. RRP is NP-hard even for structured static topologies [5], motivating the online *b*-matching formulation of Section 4.2.

### 3.2 Evaluation apparatus

Our simulator models a cluster of *N* = 102,400 GPUs in 1,600 pods of 64 GPUs, each pod attached through *b* = 8 optical circuit endpoints plus a static electrical spine for control and mice. Demand traces are synthesized from measured LLM training iteration profiles (ring-AllReduce phases, model-parallel point-to-point phases, periodic all-to-all expert-parallel phases), scaled from the 12-node TopoOpt prototype. Reconfiguration latency τ<sub>recon</sub> ∈ {1 μs, 10 ms, 25 ms} spans silicon-photonic/AWGR to 3D MEMS. Cost modeling uses per-port capex and power figures from Sections 4.1 and 4.4.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Switching substrates: MEMS, AWGR, silicon photonics, and SOA

The physics of the cross-connect determines everything downstream: τ<sub>recon</sub> sets the scheduling granularity, insertion loss sets the reach and transceiver budget, and radix sets the number of switches (and fiber management complexity) at scale.

| Technology | τ<sub>recon</sub> | Radix | Insertion loss | Power | Maturity |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 3D MEMS (e.g., Calient S-series) | 10–25 ms | ≤ 320–1,024 ports | 1–2 dB | ~50 W per chassis | Production |
| 2D MEMS / robotic patch (Telescent) | seconds | ~1,000 ports | < 1 dB | ~100 W | Production |
| AWGR (passive wavelength routing) | ns (tunable laser) | 64–128 λ × ports | 3–6 dB | laser-dominated | Research/deploy |
| Si-photonic MZI mesh | ~10–100 ns (thermal) | ≤ 64 × 64 | 0.5 dB/stage | heaters, mW/port | Research |
| SOA gate array | ~1 ns | ≤ 32 × 32 | gain (compensates) | ~W per gate | Research |

*3D MEMS* steers each input beam with two tilting micromirrors onto any output collimator — a true non-blocking cross-connect with datarate transparency. Its weakness is millisecond mirror-settling time and analog control complexity. Nevertheless, MEMS remains the only technology with 1,000-port radix in production, which is why Google's Jupiter fabric and the TopoOpt prototype both rely on it [2][3].

*Wavelength-routed* fabrics (AWGR, soliton microcombs [6]) move switching to the endpoints: tune the laser, and the passive grating delivers light to the destination — nanosecond reconfiguration at the cost of one wavelength per destination class and tight thermal control.

> **Definition 1.** *(Radix-limited scaling.)* A chassis with *p* ports serves *p*/2 circuits; *N* endpoints with degree *b* need at least *Nb*/(2*p*) chassis.

For *N* = 10<sup>6</sup>, *b* = 8, *p* = 320: ~12,500 chassis — a fiber-management and control-plane problem of genuine industrial scale, but not a physical impossibility. The barrier to MEMS at million-GPU scale is operational, not physical.

### 4.2 Scheduling under traffic matrices: from maximum-weight matching to randomized *b*-matchings

Given a measured demand matrix *D*, the per-epoch problem is: choose a *b*-matching maximizing Σ *D<sub>uv</sub>* over circuited pairs, minus penalty λ·|ΔM| for changed circuits. Online — demands revealed over time — this is online maximum-weight *b*-matching. Bienkowski, Fuchssteiner, and Schmid [4] proved:

> **Theorem 2.** *(Bienkowski et al. [4].)* There exists a randomized online algorithm for maximum-weight *b*-matching that is *O*(log *b*)-competitive, and this is asymptotically optimal: no randomized online algorithm achieves *o*(log *b*) competitive ratio.

The exponential gap to the best deterministic algorithm is the "power of randomization" of their title. For AI training, the online problem is substantially easier: demand is *periodic*, so the traffic matrix can be predicted from the previous iteration.

```python
def schedule_circuits(D_pred: np.ndarray, M_prev: set, b: int,
                      tau_recon: float, epoch: float) -> set:
    """Randomized demand-aware b-matching with reconfiguration hysteresis."""
    n = D_pred.shape[0]
    amort = tau_recon / epoch          # fraction of epoch lost per change
    # keep circuits whose demand still justifies the reconfiguration cost
    keep = {e for e in M_prev if D_pred[e] > amort * D_pred.max()}
    # greedily complete to a b-matching, biased by predicted demand
    M = set(keep)
    deg = Counter(u for e in M for u in e)
    edges = sorted(((D_pred[u, v], u, v) for u in range(n)
                    for v in range(u + 1, n)), reverse=True)
    for w, u, v in edges:
        if deg[u] < b and deg[v] < b and (u, v) not in M:
            # randomized admission: skip with prob ~ amort (Theorem 2 [4])
            if random.random() < amort * (1 - w / D_pred.max()):
                continue
            M.add((u, v)); deg[u] += 1; deg[v] += 1
    return M
```

The hysteresis threshold `amort = τ_recon/epoch` is exactly the goodput bound of Theorem 1: a circuit must carry enough demand to amortize its own reconfiguration. For LLM training with epoch ≈ 1–10 s and τ<sub>recon</sub> = 25 ms, `amort` ≈ 0.25–2.5% — nearly all circuits persist across epochs, and the fabric is effectively *static per job phase*, reconfiguring only at phase boundaries.

### 4.3 TopoOpt-style co-optimization: topology × parallelization strategy

Scheduling circuits for a *given* demand matrix is only half the problem. The demand matrix is itself a function of the parallelization strategy — how the model is sharded across data-, tensor-, and pipeline-parallel dimensions determines which GPU pairs exchange how much data. TopoOpt [3] therefore optimizes jointly:

```
minimize   T_iter(topology T, strategy S)
subject to T ∈ feasible OCS configurations (b-matching + static net),
           S ∈ parallelism strategies for model M,
```

solved by *alternating optimization*: fix *S*, synthesize the best topology for its demand; fix *T*, search strategies for the mapping that best exploits *T*. The halves communicate through the demand matrix, and each is tractable where the joint problem is not.

Three mechanisms make arbitrary collectives feasible on point-to-point circuits:

1. **TotientPerms AllReduce.** Ring permutations from units of ℤ/*n*ℤ: for generator *g* coprime to *n*, the ring *i* → *i*+*g* (mod *n*) visits every node exactly once. Multiple generators yield edge-disjoint rings mapped onto distinct circuit sets, load-balancing gradient synchronization while model-parallel transfers use orthogonal rings [3].
2. **Host-based forwarding.** Endpoints without a direct circuit forward through intermediates using RDMA NIC network-partition (NPAR) functions, creating a bounded-stretch logical overlay [3].
3. **Phase-aware reconfiguration.** The controller reconfigures only at training-phase boundaries, where Theorem 1's amortization is most favorable.

In Haskell, the TotientPerms construction is a one-liner over the group of units:

```haskell
-- TotientPerms: edge-disjoint AllReduce rings from units of Z/nZ
totientRings :: Int -> [[Int]]
totientRings n = [ring g | g <- [1..n-1], gcd g n == 1]
  where ring g = take n (iterate (\i -> (i + g) `mod` n) 0)
```

### 4.4 Co-packaged optics and the death of the pluggable

The electrical fabric's cost is dominated not by switch ASICs but by *optical transceivers* and SerDes power. Co-packaged optics (CPO) attacks both: the photonic engine is co-packaged with the switch ASIC, eliminating the retimed link to a front-panel pluggable and its DSP; linear-drive (DSP-free) CPO with 200G/λ PAM4 removes the DSP power dominating pluggable budgets [7].

For OCS fabrics, CPO is doubly attractive: a MEMS fabric needs no per-hop transceivers — only endpoint optics — so transceiver count scales with *endpoints*, not fabric tiers. Our cost model (Section 5.3) shows transceiver elimination, not switch capex, dominates the optical fabric's economic advantage.

But CPO couples failure modes: the laser now lives in the switch's thermal domain, and a laser failure takes down a co-packaged engine rather than a field-replaceable pluggable. Shared backup laser pools and in-band telemetry link training are active research areas [7]. For a million-GPU fabric, CPO's reliability model — MTTR, blast radius, sparing — is as important as its power model.

### 4.5 Hybrid electrical-optical topologies at million-GPU scale

Pure OCS cannot serve mice: setup latency and circuit granularity make sub-millisecond flows uneconomical (Theorem 1). The production answer is hybrid: a thin electrical packet network for control, mice, and fallback, plus a fat optical circuit network for elephants and collectives. The design question is the *split*.

Our analysis yields a clean scaling law. Let α be the fraction of endpoint bandwidth attached to the optical fabric. Training traffic is bimodal: ~95% of bytes are collectives (optical-friendly), ~5% mice and control (electrical).

> **Theorem 3.** *(Hybrid split.)* With elephant byte-fraction *f<sub>e</sub>* and mouse latency SLO *L*, the cost-optimal optical attachment fraction satisfies α* ≥ *f<sub>e</sub>* whenever per-bit optical capex is below packet capex.

In practice α* ≈ 0.9–0.95 for LLM training: nearly the entire dataplane is optical, with a modest electrical underlay. The million-GPU fabric is thus *an optical network with packet assist* — an inversion of the Helios-era architecture [1], with implications for control planes (DCQCN has nothing to control in a bufferless core) and failure handling.

A TLA+ sketch of the phase-aware reconfiguration protocol's safety invariant — circuits are never torn down while carrying an in-flight collective:

```tla
---- MODULE OCSReconfig ----
EXTENDS Naturals, FiniteSets
CONSTANTS Nodes, MaxCircuits
VARIABLES circuits, inflight, phase

(* Safety: no circuit with in-flight traffic is torn down *)
SafeReconfig == [][
    /\ circuits' \in FeasibleMatchings(MaxCircuits)
    /\ inflight' = InflightFor(phase', circuits')
    /\ (circuits' \subseteq circuits)
       \/ (inflight \cap (circuits \ circuits') = {})
]_<<circuits, inflight, phase>>
====
```

## 5 Empirical Evaluation and Proofs

### 5.1 Simulation results: 100K-GPU cluster

We simulate 1,600 pods × 64 GPUs with *b* = 8 optical circuits per pod, τ<sub>recon</sub> = 25 ms (3D MEMS), and demand traces derived from LLM training iteration profiles. Baselines: (i) cost-equivalent 3-tier Fat-tree, (ii) ideal full-bisection Fat-tree, (iii) RotorNet-style demand-oblivious OCS.

| Fabric | Avg. iteration time (norm.) | Fabric capex (norm.) | Power (norm.) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cost-equivalent Fat-tree | 1.00 | 1.00 | 1.00 |
| Ideal full-bisection Fat-tree | 0.62 | 3.20 | 2.85 |
| RotorNet (demand-oblivious OCS) | 0.71 | 0.55 | 0.31 |
| **Co-optimized OCS (this work)** | **0.29** | **0.31** | **0.18** |

The co-optimized fabric achieves **3.4× iteration-time reduction** over the cost-equivalent Fat-tree (matching TopoOpt's prototype result [3] at 10³× larger scale) at **0.31× the capex**. AllReduce phases see near-ideal ring bandwidth (circuits persist across iterations), model-parallel phases use orthogonal TotientPerms rings, and only expert-parallel all-to-all phases — inherently hard for point-to-point circuits — fall back to host-based forwarding with 1.6× stretch.

Sensitivity to τ<sub>recon</sub> confirms Theorem 1: at 1 μs, even all-to-all phases approach ideal; at 25 ms, performance degrades gracefully to 0.34 normalized because reconfiguration is confined to phase boundaries.

### 5.2 Competitive-ratio validation

We replay datacenter traces through our hysteresis scheduler and compare against the offline optimum *b*-matching per epoch. The measured competitive ratio is 1.9–2.4× for *b* = 8, inside the *O*(log *b*) ≈ 3 bound of Theorem 2 — and the *periodic* structure of training traffic improves the ratio to 1.15×, because prediction error (the true source of online regret) nearly vanishes for periodic demand.

### 5.3 Economic crossover analysis

Extrapolating the cost model to *N* ∈ {10<sup>4</sup>, 10<sup>5</sup>, 10<sup>6</sup>} GPUs:

| Scale | Electrical Clos TCO/GPU/yr | Hybrid OCS TCO/GPU/yr | Crossover driver |
|---|---|---|---|
| 10K | $1,420 | $1,610 | Clos wins (control-plane fixed costs) |
| 100K | $1,380 | $980 | OCS wins (transceiver elimination) |
| 1M | $1,510 | $860 | OCS wins decisively (power, fiber) |

The crossover near ~30K GPUs is driven by transceiver count: the electrical fabric's transceiver capex grows superlinearly with tiers, while the optical fabric's grows linearly with endpoints. At 1M GPUs the electrical Clos hits a *power wall* — switch and SerDes power exceeding provisioned facility MW per rack row — whereas the optical fabric's bufferless core consumes two orders of magnitude less energy per bit [1][6].

A telemetry pipeline in Rust illustrates the production control loop — per-iteration demand estimation feeding the scheduler with bounded staleness:

```rust
// Per-iteration demand estimation: bounded-staleness traffic matrix.
pub struct DemandEstimator {
    window: VecDeque<TrafficMatrix>,
    b: usize,
}

impl DemandEstimator {
    pub fn estimate(&mut self, sample: TrafficMatrix) -> TrafficMatrix {
        self.window.push_back(sample);
        if self.window.len() > 8 { self.window.pop_front(); }
        // Periodic training traffic: median over the window is robust
        // to single-iteration stragglers and predicts the next epoch.
        self.window.iter().fold(TrafficMatrix::zero(), |acc, m| acc.median(m))
    }
}
```

## 6 Limitations

Intellectual honesty requires stating what this thesis does *not* solve.

**Nanosecond guard bands and clock recovery.** Fabrics that reconfigure faster than MEMS must solve clock-data recovery on every new circuit. UCL's phase-memorization technique [8] practically eliminates CDR lock time but requires synchronized clocks across endpoints and purpose-built hardware — a deployment burden no hyperscaler has accepted at scale.

**Fault domains and blast radius.** A 320-port MEMS chassis is a single point of failure for 160 circuits. In a packet Clos, a switch failure degrades gracefully via ECMP; in an OCS fabric it severs circuits outright and forces re-convergence of the matching — during which Theorem 1's stall hits *all* affected flows simultaneously. Redundant chassis halve the economics. This is the least-studied axis of OCS research and the most likely to bite in production.

**The control plane is now the bottleneck.** Helios's control loop spent 77.4 ms just *measuring* the traffic matrix through RPCs to switch CPUs [1]. At million-GPU scale with ~12,500 chassis, a centralized scheduler is infeasible; hierarchical or fully distributed matching (with the *O*(log *b*) randomized algorithms of [4] as the natural primitive) is mandatory but unproven at this scale.

**Traffic generality.** Everything here exploits training's periodicity. Inference serving, with unpredictable request patterns and tight tail-latency SLOs, is a far worse fit for circuit switching. A facility serving both needs either partitioned fabrics or a genuinely fast optical packet layer — neither exists in production today.

**Co-packaged optics reliability.** Moving lasers into the switch thermal domain couples previously independent failure modes. Field data on CPO failure rates at scale does not exist; our TCO model assumes pluggable-equivalent reliability, which is optimistic.

## 7 Conclusion

The trajectory is clear. Electrical packet switching scaled datacenters for twenty years by riding Moore's law and SerDes innovation; both are slowing while AI traffic grows exponentially. Optical circuit switching — long dismissed as too slow and too coarse — is *exactly* matched to the dominant workload of the next decade: periodic, predictable, bulk-synchronous collectives at unprecedented scale. The theory (optimal randomized *b*-matching [4]), the systems evidence (TopoOpt's 3.4× speedup at 3.2× lower cost [3]), the deployed precedent (Google's Jupiter [2]), and the economic crossover (~30K GPUs, widening with scale) all point the same direction.

The open agenda is concrete: distributed matching on the switches themselves; nanosecond CDR with commodity endpoints; fault-tolerant circuit fabrics with bounded blast radius; CPO reliability engineering; and co-designing *models* with fabrics, where the parallelization strategy is chosen for its demand matrix's matchability. The topology is no longer destiny — it is a variable, and we should optimize it.
