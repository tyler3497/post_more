# From Many-Body Schrödinger to Gross–Pitaevskii: A Rigorous Theory of Bose–Einstein Condensation

## Abstract

We present a self-contained mathematical account of the derivation of the Gross–Pitaevskii (GP) equation from the linear many-body Schrödinger equation for interacting bosons. Starting from the 1961 phenomenological introduction of the GP order parameter by Gross and Pitaevskii, we survey the Lieb–Seiringer–Yngvason program: the rigorous derivation of the GP energy functional in the dilute limit [1], the proof of complete Bose–Einstein condensation for trapped gases [2], the comprehensive synthesis in the Oberwolfach seminars volume [3], and the complementary dilute-gas energy asymptotics [4]. We explain the technical machinery — scattering length renormalization, Dyson's lemma, the box method, and reduced density matrices — then treat vortices and quantized circulation in rotating gases, the Thomas–Fermi strong-coupling limit, and Bogoliubov's quasiparticle excitation spectrum. The complementary dynamical program of Erdős–Schlein–Yau, which derives the time-dependent GP equation via the BBGKY hierarchy [5][6], is placed alongside the static theory. The exposition is intended to be accessible to a mathematically literate reader and faithful to the original results.

## 1 Intro

In June 1995, the JILA group in Boulder reported the first realization of Bose–Einstein condensation in a dilute vapor of rubidium-87 atoms [7]. Seventy years had passed since Einstein, extending Bose's counting statistics to massive particles, predicted that below a critical temperature a macroscopic fraction of an ideal Bose gas must occupy the single-particle ground state. The intervening decades had seen the triumph of superfluid helium physics, the phenomenological introduction of the condensate order parameter by E. Gross and L. Pitaevskii in 1961 [7], and an enormous theoretical literature on dilute quantum gases — but no rigorous proof that the ground state of a realistic interacting many-body Hamiltonian actually exhibits condensation.

The difficulty is not conceptual obscurity but technical depth. The Hamiltonian for *N* bosons,

H_N = Σ_j (−Δ_j + V(x_j)) + Σ_{i<j} v(x_i − x_j),

lives on a Hilbert space of dimension growing exponentially with *N*, and its ground state is entangled in a way that defeats direct analysis. The GP theory replaces this object with a single complex-valued *order parameter* φ minimizing

E_GP[φ] = ∫ (|∇φ|² + V|φ|² + 4πa|φ|⁴) dx,   ∫|φ|² = N,

a tractable nonlinear problem. The question that occupied Elliott Lieb, Robert Seiringer, and Jakob Yngvason at the turn of the millennium was whether this replacement could be *proved*: is the GP functional asymptotically exact for the quantum ground-state energy, and does the many-body ground state genuinely condense into the GP minimizer? Their affirmative answer, published in *Physical Review A* in 2000 [1], inaugurated the rigorous era of dilute-gas mathematics.

This thesis reconstructs that program. Section 2 fixes the model and the mathematical definition of condensation. Section 3 describes the proof technology: variational trial states, Dyson's lemma, the box method, and the hierarchy method for dynamics. Section 4 develops the core theory in depth: the GP limit itself, the condensation theorem, quantized vortices, the Thomas–Fermi regime, and Bogoliubov excitations. Section 5 confronts theory with experiment, Section 6 catalogs the boundaries of the theory, and Section 7 concludes.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The model and its length scales

Consider *N* identical bosons in three dimensions, confined by an external potential V(x) → ∞ as |x| → ∞, interacting through a repulsive, spherically symmetric pair potential v ≥ 0 of short range. Four length scales govern the physics:

| Scale | Symbol | Typical meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Interparticle distance | ρ^(−1/3) | mean spacing at density ρ |
| Scattering length | a | low-energy interaction strength |
| Trap size | L | set by the confining potential V |
| Healing length | ξ = (8πaρ)^(−1/2) | distance over which φ recovers from a defect |

The *dilute* regime means ρa³ ≪ 1: the scattering length is far smaller than the interparticle spacing, so three-body encounters are negligible and the interaction is fully characterized by *a* alone. The 1995 experiments operated at ρa³ on the order of 10⁻⁴ to 10⁻⁶, squarely in this regime [7].

### 2.2 The Gross–Pitaevskii scaling

For trapped gases there is a distinguished asymptotic regime, the **GP limit**: let N → ∞ while holding the product **Na fixed** (measured in units of the trap size) [1]. Equivalently, the scattering length shrinks as 1/N while the cloud occupies a trap of order-one size. Under this scaling, kinetic energy, trap energy, and interaction energy per particle are all of the same order — no term dominates a priori — and the variational problem remains nontrivial in the limit. This is to be contrasted with the thermodynamic limit of Lieb–Yngvason [4], where energy per unit volume converges to 4πaρ² for fixed density.

> **Definition:** A sequence of normalized symmetric N-body states Ψ_N exhibits *complete Bose–Einstein condensation* into a one-particle orbital φ if the largest eigenvalue of the one-particle reduced density matrix γ_N tends to N as N → ∞, equivalently if (1/N) γ_N → |φ⟩⟨φ| in trace norm. The reduced density matrix is obtained by tracing out N − 1 particles; its eigenvalues are the mean occupation numbers of the natural orbitals (Penrose–Onsager criterion).

This definition is the workhorse of the entire program: it replaces the unanswerable question "what does Ψ_N look like?" with a precise spectral statement about an operator on the one-particle space [2][3].

## 3 Methodology

Two complementary rigorous strategies have been developed: the *static* variational approach of Lieb–Seiringer–Yngvason for ground states, and the *dynamical* hierarchy approach of Erdős–Schlein–Yau for time evolution.

### 3.1 The static program: energy bounds

The goal is to sandwich the quantum ground-state energy E₀(N) between the GP minimum and itself, with errors vanishing per particle:

lim_{N→∞, Na fixed} E₀(N)/N = E_GP = min_{∫|φ|²=N} E_GP[φ].

- **Upper bound.** Construct an explicit trial state Ψ_trial and evaluate ⟨Ψ_trial, H_N Ψ_trial⟩. The right ansatz is a product of GP orbitals dressed with short-range *Jastrow* correlation factors f(|x_i − x_j|) that solve the zero-energy two-body scattering problem, so that the trial state "knows" about the scattering length rather than the bare potential. The correlation factor heals to 1 over distances of order the interparticle spacing, and its contribution reproduces exactly the 4πa coupling of the GP functional [1].
- **Lower bound.** This is the hard direction. The strategy combines:
  1. **Dyson's lemma** — replace the hard potential v by a softer, longer-range potential U at the price of sacrificing a controlled fraction of the kinetic energy, in such a way that the scattering length is preserved. Lieb and Yngvason's insight [4] was to sacrifice only a small fraction (1 − ε) of the kinetic energy, retaining enough for perturbation theory.
  2. **The box method** — partition the trap into small cubes, localize the energy into boxes with Neumann boundary conditions, and apply Temple's inequality (rigorous perturbation theory) in each box where the gas is nearly homogeneous.
  3. **Coherent-state / c-number substitution** techniques in the modern refinements, which convert the second-quantized lower bound into the classical GP variational problem [3].

### 3.2 The dynamical program: BBGKY hierarchy

For the *time-dependent* GP equation, i∂_t φ = (−Δ + V + 8πa|φ|²)φ, Erdős, Schlein, and Yau take a different route [5]. They consider the N-body Schrödinger evolution of initially condensing data and study the hierarchy of equations coupling the k-particle reduced density matrices γ^(k)_{N,t}:

i∂_t γ^(k) = [H^(k), γ^(k)] + (interaction terms coupling γ^(k) to γ^(k+1)).

In the GP scaling, and under an energy bound plus an a-priori condensation assumption on the initial data, they prove that the hierarchy converges to the infinite **GP hierarchy**, whose factorized solutions γ^(k)_t = |φ_t⟩⟨φ_t|^⊗k close the system. Uniqueness of solutions to the GP hierarchy then forces every limit point to factorize — propagation of condensation [5][6].

### 3.3 A computational illustration

The Thomas–Fermi density (derived in §4.4) can be computed directly. The following snippet evaluates the TF profile and radius for a harmonic trap, illustrating the inverted parabola that replaces the Gaussian of the non-interacting gas:

```python
import numpy as np

def thomas_fermi_profile(N, a, omega, m=1.0, hbar=1.0):
    """Return (R_TF, r_grid, density) for an isotropic harmonic trap."""
    g = 4.0 * np.pi * hbar**2 * a / m          # GP coupling constant
    mu = 0.5 * hbar * omega * (15.0 * N * a / np.sqrt(hbar/(m*omega)))**0.4
    R = np.sqrt(2.0 * mu / (m * omega**2))     # Thomas-Fermi radius
    r = np.linspace(0.0, R, 400)
    n = np.maximum(mu - 0.5 * m * omega**2 * r**2, 0.0) / g
    assert abs(np.trapz(4*np.pi*r**2*n, r) - N) / N < 1e-2  # normalization
    return R, r, n
```

A functional specification of the condensation criterion (§2.2) is equally crisp:

```haskell
-- Natural-orbital occupations of the one-particle reduced density matrix
type DensityMatrix = [Double]  -- eigenvalues, descending, summing to N

condensateFraction :: DensityMatrix -> Double
condensateFraction occs = head occs / sum occs

-- Complete BEC in the GP limit: fraction -> 1 as N -> infinity
prop_completeBEC :: [DensityMatrix] -> Bool
prop_completeBEC = all (> 0.99) . map condensateFraction . drop 5
```

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The GP energy functional and the Lieb–Seiringer–Yngvason theorem

The central result of [1] concerns the ground-state energy. Fix the trap V and let N → ∞ with g = Na held constant. Then

> **Theorem:** In the GP limit, E₀(N)/N → E_GP(g), the minimum of the Gross–Pitaevskii functional, and any sequence of approximate ground states exhibits complete BEC into a GP minimizer.

The proof's subtlety lies in the lower bound. A naive estimate replaces the pair potential by its integral ∫v, which for hard spheres is infinite — the energy would diverge. The correct effective coupling is not ∫v but 4πa, the scattering length, and extracting it requires the two-body scattering solution to be built into the estimate. Dyson's lemma achieves this: it trades v for a potential U spread over a radius R ≫ a while keeping the scattering length fixed, so that U is weak enough for Temple's inequality to apply in each localization box. The boxes must be small enough that the gas inside is dilute, yet large enough to contain many particles — a balance that works precisely because ρa³ ≪ 1.

Seiringer's own summary of the joint program [8] emphasizes a point often missed: the theorem also yields *uniqueness* (up to phase) of the positive GP minimizer φ_GP, so the condensation orbital is canonically determined. The result was extended to two dimensions, where the coupling acquires its famous logarithmic dependence on the scattering length [1][3].

### 4.2 Proof of complete Bose–Einstein condensation

Energy asymptotics alone do not imply condensation: in principle the ground state could be a fragmented superposition with the right energy but no macroscopic occupation. Lieb and Seiringer closed this gap in 2002 [2], proving that the one-particle density matrix of any approximate ground state converges to the projector onto the GP minimizer:

> **Theorem:** Under the hypotheses of the GP limit, (1/N) γ_N → |φ_GP⟩⟨φ_GP| in trace norm. The condensate depletion vanishes; condensation is *complete*.

The argument is a tour de force of localization. The trap is divided into small boxes; in each box the energy is bounded below by a *homogeneous*-gas estimate with a generalized Poincaré inequality controlling the kinetic energy of particles outside the condensate mode. A crucial ingredient is a bound on the interaction energy in terms of the number of excited particles, which forces the excited fraction to zero when combined with the already-established energy convergence [2]. This was the first rigorous proof of BEC in a physically realistic continuum model — for seventy years the phenomenon had been proved only for the ideal gas, where it follows from explicit computation.

The lecture notes [8] reconstruct the argument pedagogically and extend the picture: the rate of condensation has since been quantified, with optimal rates of order N^(−1/2) obtained by Nam, Napiórkowski, Ricaud, and Triay for the trapped GP regime.

### 4.3 Vortices and quantized circulation

Rotate the trap and the condensate responds in the most quantum-mechanical way imaginable: not by rigid rotation, which an irrotational superfluid cannot do, but by nucleating *quantized vortices* — line defects where the density vanishes and the phase of φ winds by an integer multiple of 2π. The velocity field v = (ℏ/m)∇θ is then irrotational everywhere except at the cores, and the circulation around any vortex is quantized in units of h/m.

Lieb and Seiringer derived the GP equation for rotating Bose gases rigorously [3], showing that the GP functional acquires the familiar centrifugal and Coriolis terms and that its minimizers, above a critical rotation speed, contain vortices. The analysis reveals a striking asymmetry: while the *energy* asymptotics follow the non-rotating pattern, the *minimizer* undergoes a symmetry-breaking transition from a vortex-free state to states with one, then many vortices, eventually forming the triangular Abrikosov lattice observed at ENS and MIT. Each additional vortex lowers the rotational energy at the cost of core energy proportional to the healing length, and the GP theory predicts the critical frequencies quantitatively.

The topological protection is worth stressing: because the phase winding is integer-valued, a vortex cannot decay continuously — it can only exit through the cloud boundary or annihilate with an antivortex. This is why vortex lattices in condensates persist for seconds, an eternity on atomic timescales, and why they serve as the cleanest experimental signature of superfluidity in dilute gases [7].

### 4.4 The Thomas–Fermi limit

When the interaction parameter is large (Na/a_ho ≫ 1, with a_ho the oscillator length), the kinetic energy becomes negligible against interaction and trap energies. Dropping |∇φ|² from the GP functional gives the Thomas–Fermi approximation, whose minimizer is explicit:

n_TF(x) = max(μ − V(x), 0) / g,   g = 4πℏ²a/m.

For a harmonic trap this is the celebrated *inverted parabola*, vanishing at the Thomas–Fermi radius R_TF = √(2μ/mω²) and utterly different from the Gaussian ground state of the non-interacting gas. The chemical potential scales as μ ∝ (Na/a_ho)^(2/5) ℏω, and R_TF exceeds the oscillator length by the large factor (Na/a_ho)^(1/5) — the repulsive interaction *swells* the cloud, which is precisely what absorption images measure [7].

The TF limit is singular: at the cloud edge the kinetic term, though globally negligible, becomes locally important over a boundary layer of width set by the healing length ξ. Matching the TF interior to the exponentially decaying tail is a delicate semiclassical problem, and the rigorous justification of the TF limit as an asymptotic regime of the GP functional — with quantitative error bounds — is developed in detail in [3]. The Python snippet in §3.3 computes exactly this profile.

### 4.5 Bogoliubov excitations

Condensation describes the ground state; *Bogoliubov theory* describes its excitations. Diagonalizing the second-quantized Hamiltonian to quadratic order in the operators for the non-condensed modes, Bogoliubov obtained in 1947 the quasiparticle dispersion

ε(k) = √[ (ℏ²k²/2m)² + 2gn (ℏ²k²/2m) ],

linear (phononic) at small k with sound speed c = √(gn/m), and quadratic (free-particle-like) at large k. Landau's criterion for superfluidity — the existence of a critical velocity below which no excitation can be created — follows directly from the linear onset.

Making this rigorous took over half a century. The excitation spectrum was finally derived from the many-body Hamiltonian by Lewin, Nam, Serfaty, and Solovej, who proved that the low-lying eigenvalues converge to sums of Bogoliubov quasiparticle energies [3]. Their work validates the physical picture that the condensate is not merely a macroscopically occupied orbital but the vacuum of a well-defined quasiparticle Fock space, with depletion — the quantum-mechanical necessity that interactions scatter a small fraction of particles out of the condensate even at zero temperature — emerging as the zero-point motion of those quasiparticles. In the GP limit the depletion vanishes, consistent with the complete condensation of §4.2; at fixed density it tends to the Lee–Huang–Yang constant (8/3)√(ρa³/π), the first quantum correction to the mean-field energy [4][7].

## 5 Empirical and Theoretical Validation

The theory meets experiment at every scale it predicts:

1. **Energy and density.** Time-of-flight absorption images of released condensates show the inverted-parabola profiles of §4.4, and measured release energies follow the TF scaling μ ∝ (Na)^(2/5) [7].
2. **Quantized vortices.** Rotating-trap experiments (ENS 2000, MIT 2001) produced the predicted vortex lattices with the correct lattice spacing and critical rotation frequencies; single-vortex phase windings were confirmed interferometrically [7].
3. **Scattering-length universality.** Feshbach-resonance tuning of *a* over orders of magnitude confirms that ground-state properties depend on the interaction only through *a*, exactly as the GP derivation requires [7].
4. **Internal consistency.** The dynamical derivation [5][6] reproduces the *same* coupling 8πa in the time-dependent equation that the static derivation [1] finds in the energy functional — a nontrivial check, since the two proofs share no machinery.
5. **Numerics.** Direct imaginary-time integration of the GP equation reproduces measured collective-mode frequencies (breathing, quadrupole, scissors modes) to percent accuracy across the TF regime [7].

## 6 Limitations

The GP theory is asymptotically exact in its regime, but the regime has sharp boundaries:

- **Dimensionality.** In one dimension the GP functional is *not* the correct limit; the Lieb–Liniger model takes over and the coupling scales differently. The two-dimensional case requires logarithmic renormalization [1][3].
- **Positive temperature.** The results above are zero-temperature. At T > 0 the free energy, not the ground-state energy, is minimized; the rigorous GP description of the thermal cloud was achieved only later by Deuchert, Seiringer, and Yngvason [3].
- **Strong interactions.** At unitarity (a → ∞) there is no small parameter ρa³, the scattering-length universality breaks down, and no rigorous derivation exists; the unitary Fermi gas is understood only through numerics and experiment [7].
- **Quantum fluctuations.** The GP equation is a mean-field theory; it misses the Lee–Huang–Yang correction, quantum depletion at fixed density, and beyond-mean-field correlations. Capturing these requires Bogoliubov-level analysis [3].
- **Fragmentation and disorder.** The condensation theorem assumes approximate ground states of a clean trap; fragmented condensates (multiple macroscopic orbitals) and strong random potentials require separate analysis [8].
- **Dynamical assumptions.** The ESY derivation assumes condensation of the initial data and a uniform energy bound; proving condensation *dynamically* from non-condensed data remains open [5][6].

## 7 Conclusion

The Gross–Pitaevskii equation began in 1961 as phenomenology — a bold guess at the order parameter of a superfluid. Four decades later, the Lieb–Seiringer–Yngvason program converted that guess into theorem: starting from nothing but the many-body Schrödinger equation with repulsive short-range interactions, the GP energy functional emerges as the exact asymptotic description of the ground state, the ground state condenses completely into the GP minimizer, and the effective coupling is precisely 4π times the scattering length [1][2]. Erdős, Schlein, and Yau extended the conquest to dynamics, deriving the time-dependent GP equation from Schrödinger evolution [5][6]. Around this core, the theory now encompasses rotating gases and quantized vortices, the Thomas–Fermi strong-coupling limit, and the Bogoliubov excitation spectrum [3][7][8].

What remains is as interesting as what is settled: the unitary regime, positive-temperature condensation in full generality, and the dynamical *emergence* of condensation are open problems of the first rank. But the central lesson stands. Reductionism works: a nonlinear partial differential equation for a single complex field, derived with full mathematical rigor, captures the ground state of 10⁵ entangled atoms. Few results in mathematical physics exhibit the unity of physical intuition and proof so completely.
