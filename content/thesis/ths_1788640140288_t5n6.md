# From Cytron to Briggs: SSA Construction, Φ-Placement via Dominance Frontiers, and Graph-Coloring Register Allocation

## Abstract

This thesis unifies two foundational pillars of modern optimizing compilers: the construction of static single assignment (SSA) form via dominance frontiers, introduced by Cytron, Ferrante, Rosen, Wegman, and Zadeck in 1991 [1], and global register allocation by graph coloring, pioneered by Chaitin and colleagues in 1981–1982 [2] and refined into its industrial form by Briggs [3]. We give a rigorous treatment of the dominance-frontier characterization of φ-placement, culminating in the iterated-join theorem *J*⁺(*S*) = *DF*⁺(*S*); survey the spectrum from minimal through pruned to semi-pruned SSA and the subtleties of SSA destruction, including the lost-copy and swap problems and conventional SSA form; develop Chaitin's simplify–select allocator and Briggs's optimistic coloring; analyze iterated register coalescing in the George–Appel formulation [4]; and contrast the entire coloring family with the linear-scan allocator of Poletto and Sarkar [5]. We close the loop with the chordal-graph characterization of SSA interference graphs and the complexity boundary between coloring and spilling [6, 7], arguing that the Cytron-to-Briggs pipeline remains the conceptual backbone of production compilers from GCC to LLVM.

## 1. Introduction

A compiler backend faces a deceptively simple question: given a procedure with an unbounded set of *virtual* variables and a machine offering only *K* physical registers, assign each virtual to a register or to memory such that no two simultaneously live values share a register, while minimizing the cost of the memory traffic (spills) and register-to-register moves introduced along the way. This is *register allocation*, and it is NP-complete in essentially every interesting formulation [7]. The practical answer compilers have converged upon over four decades is *graph coloring*: build an *interference graph* whose vertices are live ranges and whose edges join ranges live at the same program point, then color the graph with *K* colors [2].

Coloring, however, is only half the story. The precision of the interference graph — and therefore the quality of the allocation — depends on the intermediate representation on which liveness is computed. *Static single assignment form*, in which every variable is textually defined exactly once and control-flow merges are reconciled by φ-functions, makes def–use chains explicit and renders many dataflow facts trivially available [1]. Cytron et al.'s 1991 algorithm turned SSA from a research curiosity into a practical IR by showing that φ-functions need be placed only at the *iterated dominance frontiers* of definition sites, computable in near-linear time. Briggs's 1992 dissertation [3] then industrialized Chaitin's allocator with *optimistic coloring*, and George and Appel's iterated register coalescing [4] tamed the copy instructions that SSA and calling conventions introduce. Finally, Poletto and Sarkar's linear scan [5] offered a radically simpler alternative for just-in-time compilers, trading a measured amount of code quality for dramatic compile-time speed.

This thesis reconstructs that intellectual arc end to end. Our contributions are: (i) a self-contained, proof-oriented exposition of dominance frontiers and the iterated-join characterization of φ-placement; (ii) a taxonomy of SSA flavors — minimal, pruned, semi-pruned — and a precise account of SSA *destruction*, including conventional SSA form; (iii) a comparative analysis of Chaitin-style, Briggs-style, iterated-coalescing, and linear-scan allocators with their complexity and empirical profiles; and (iv) a discussion of the chordal-graph structure of SSA interference graphs, which reframes the boundary between the polynomial and the NP-complete in register allocation [6].

## 2. Background and Related Work

**Dominators and flow graphs.** A node *d* *dominates* a node *n* if every path from the entry to *n* passes through *d*; *d* strictly dominates *n* if additionally *d* ≠ *n*. The immediate-dominator relation forms the *dominator tree*. Lengauer and Tarjan's 1979 near-linear algorithm made dominator computation cheap enough to be a standard compiler pass, and Cytron et al. built directly upon it [1].

**Liveness and interference.** A variable is *live* at a program point if its current value may be read on some path to the exit before being overwritten. Two live ranges *interfere* if they are simultaneously live at any point; the interference graph records exactly these conflicts. Chaitin et al. showed in 1981 that register allocation for straight-line-free code is equivalent to coloring this graph, and hence NP-complete [2, 7].

**The coloring lineage.** Chaitin's 1982 allocator introduced the *simplify–select* discipline with spill heuristics based on spill cost divided by degree [2]. Chow and Hennessy proposed *priority-based coloring* (1990), which splits live ranges by priority before coloring. Bernstein et al. (1989) attacked *spill-code minimization* directly. Briggs's dissertation [3] and the Briggs–Cooper–Torczon improvements (TOPLAS 1994) introduced *optimistic coloring* and *rematerialization*, substantially reducing spill traffic. George and Appel's *iterated register coalescing* (1996) [4] interleaved coalescing with simplification to eliminate copies without degrading colorability, while Park and Moon's *optimistic coalescing* (1998, 2004) pushed aggressiveness further.

**SSA and its discontents.** Beyond Cytron et al., Briggs et al. (1998) gave *practical improvements* to SSA construction and destruction, introducing *semi-pruned* SSA. Sreedhar, Gao, and Lee (1999) formalized SSA *destruction* via conventional SSA form (CSSA), diagnosing the lost-copy and swap problems; Boissinot et al. (2008) revisited the translation with correctness proofs. Hack, Grund, and Wagner (2006) discovered that *strict* SSA programs have *chordal* interference graphs, making coloring polynomial — while spilling remains NP-complete, a boundary later sharpened by Bouchez, Darte, and Rastello [7]. Appel and George (2001) used integer programming to study *optimal* spilling, refuting Briggs's conjecture that SSA-induced live-range splits are the right granularity for spilling [6].

## 3. Methodology

This thesis proceeds by *formal reconstruction*: we state the central definitions exactly as in the primary sources, present the algorithms as executable pseudocode, prove or sketch the key theorems, and analyze asymptotic complexity. Empirical claims are drawn from the measurement sections of the cited works themselves — we report, rather than reproduce, published numbers (e.g., Poletto and Sarkar's benchmark comparisons [5]), and we mark clearly where a claim is a theorem versus a measurement.

Our scope is deliberately the *global* (intraprocedural) allocator over an imperative IR. We exclude instruction scheduling interactions, interprocedural allocation, and vector-register pressure models, noting in §6 where these omissions bite. Notation: *K* denotes the number of available registers (colors); *G = (V, E)* the interference graph; *deg(v)* the current degree of *v*; *DF(x)* the dominance frontier of node *x*.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Dominance Frontiers and the Iterated Join Theorem

The *dominance frontier* of a node *x* is the set of nodes where *x*'s dominance stops — precisely, the join points that *x* reaches but does not dominate:

> **Definition (Dominance frontier [1]).** *DF(x) = { y | x dominates a predecessor of y, and x does not strictly dominate y }.*

The mapping extends to sets of nodes by union, *DF(S) = ⋃_{x∈S} DF(x)*, and the *iterated* dominance frontier *DF*⁺(*S*) is the limit of the increasing sequence *DF*₁ = *DF(S)*, *DF*_{i+1} = *DF(S ∪ DF*ᵢ*)*.

Cytron et al. relate frontiers to *join sets*. Given a set *S* of CFG nodes, the join set *J(S)* contains every node *z* that is the *first* common node of two paths starting at two *distinct* nodes of *S*. The iterated join *J*⁺(*S*) is defined analogously as a limit. The paper's central result is:

> **Theorem (Iterated join = iterated dominance frontier [1]).** *For any set S of CFG nodes, J⁺(S) = DF⁺(S).*

*Why it matters.* If *S* is the set of blocks containing assignments to a variable *v*, then φ-functions for *v* are required *exactly* at *J*⁺(*S*) — the merge points reachable from two distinct definitions. The theorem therefore reduces φ-placement to computing *DF*⁺ over assignment blocks, which Cytron et al. compute with a bottom-up pass over the dominator tree using *local* and *up* contributions:

```c
/* Cytron et al.: dominance frontier computation [1] */
for (x in bottom_up(dominator_tree)) {
    DF[x] = {};
    for (y in successors(x))          /* DF_local */
        if (idom(y) != x) DF[x] |= {y};
    for (z in children(x))            /* DF_up */
        for (y in DF[z])
            if (idom(y) != x) DF[x] |= {y};
}
```

φ-placement itself is a worklist algorithm over *DF*⁺, and *renaming* is a preorder walk of the dominator tree maintaining one stack of reaching definitions per variable:

```c
/* φ-placement: S = assignment blocks of variable v */
W = worklist(S); placed = {};
while (W nonempty) {
    x = pop(W);
    for (y in DF[x])
        if (y not in placed) {
            insert "v = φ(v, v, ...)" at entry of y;
            placed |= {y};
            if (y not in S) push(W, y);   /* φ is itself a definition */
        }
}
```

*Renaming* then walks the dominator tree: on entering a block, each use is rewritten to the top of its variable's stack and each definition pushes a fresh subscripted name; φ-operands of successors are filled from the stacks; on exit the pushes are popped. Straight-line renaming is linear; the whole construction is *O(n + DF)* where *DF* is the size of the frontier relation — though Bilardi and Pingali later showed *DF* can be quadratic in pathological (even sparse) CFGs, motivating lock-step algorithms.

### 4.2 Minimal, Pruned, and Semi-Pruned SSA — and Getting Out Again

Cytron's algorithm builds *minimal* SSA: a φ-function appears wherever the iterated-join criterion demands one, even if the φ is dead (its result never used) or its operands are all the same value. Three flavors refine this:

| Flavor | φ-placement rule | Cost | Drawback |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Minimal** (Cytron et al. [1]) | *DF*⁺ of all assignment blocks | Cheapest | Dead and redundant φs |
| **Pruned** | Minimal, restricted to variables live across the join | Needs full liveness | Liveness analysis cost |
| **Semi-pruned** (Briggs et al. 1998) | Minimal, restricted to *global* names (upward-exposed uses) | Near-minimal cost | Conservative: keeps some dead φs |

*Semi-pruned* SSA is the pragmatic sweet spot: it processes only blocks in *A_orig* (blocks containing original, non-φ definitions) and inserts φs only for variables that are *global* — used before being defined somewhere in the procedure — which approximates liveness without a dataflow fixpoint.

**SSA destruction** is where theory meets the machine. φ-functions are not executable; they must be replaced by copies on incoming edges. Two hazards arise, identified by Sreedhar, Gao, and Lee (1999):

1. **The lost-copy problem.** Naively placing a copy for a φ-operand on a critical edge (an edge from a multi-successor block to a multi-predecessor block) can cause the copy's result to be overwritten — or never executed — along some path. Splitting critical edges is the standard cure.
2. **The swap problem.** Parallel φs such as `x1 = φ(x2); x2 = φ(x1)` in the same block cannot be sequentialized into ordinary copies without a temporary or an explicit swap.

The remedy is *conventional SSA form* (CSSA): φ-related variables are first given disjoint live ranges (via copies), so that each φ becomes a set of *parallel copies* on predecessor edges, sequentializable without loss [Sreedhar et al.]. Boissinot et al. (2008) later gave provably correct, copy-minimal out-of-SSA algorithms, distinguishing CSSA from *transformed* SSA (TSSA) where φs carry parallel-copy semantics directly.

### 4.3 Chaitin–Briggs Graph Coloring and Optimistic Spilling

Chaitin's allocator [2] operates in four phases:

1. **Build** the interference graph: for each definition, add edges to all variables live afterward (with Chaitin's special handling of copy instructions so that coalescing candidates are not needlessly connected).
2. **Simplify**: repeatedly remove a node of degree *< K*, pushing it on a stack. If no such node exists, choose a *spill candidate* minimizing estimated spill cost divided by current degree, remove it, and continue.
3. **Select**: pop nodes and assign the lowest available color; spilled nodes receive no color.
4. **Spill code**: insert loads/stores for spilled live ranges and *iterate* the whole algorithm, since spilling changes the graph.

```c
/* Chaitin simplify–select with Briggs optimistic spilling [2,3] */
stack = [];
loop {
    n = node with degree < K;
    if (n == none) {
        /* Briggs: do NOT commit to spilling yet — push an */
        /* optimistic candidate and decide during select   */
        n = argmin(spill_cost(v) / degree(v));
        mark_optimistic(n);
    }
    push(stack, n); remove n from G;
    if (G empty) break;
}
while (stack nonempty) {
    n = pop(stack);
    c = lowest color unused by colored neighbors of n;
    if (c exists) color[n] = c;
    else spill(n);            /* optimistic spill confirmed only here */
}
```

Briggs's key insight [3] was that Chaitin *over-spills*: a node removed as a spill candidate during simplify can often still be colored during select, because its high-degree neighbors may themselves receive the same color or be spilled. *Optimistic coloring* therefore defers the spill decision to select time. Combined with the Briggs–Cooper–Torczon improvements — *rematerialization* (recomputing cheap values instead of reloading them), better spill-cost estimation, and interference-graph construction refinements (Cooper–Simpson) — this remains the template for production coloring allocators, including GCC's and LLVM's greedy allocator heritage.

### 4.4 Iterated Register Coalescing and the Linear-Scan Alternative

Copy instructions — from φ-translation, calling conventions, and instruction lowering — bloat the interference graph and the final code. *Coalescing* merges the source and destination of a copy into one live range, deleting the move. Chaitin's *conservative* coalescing merged only when the combined node had degree *< K*; it was safe but timid.

George and Appel's *iterated register coalescing* [4] interleaves coalescing with simplification so that each enables the other. Nodes and moves migrate among worklists — *simplify*, *freeze*, *spill* for nodes; *worklistMoves*, *activeMoves*, *frozenMoves*, *coalescedMoves*, *constrainedMoves* for moves — under strict invariants (every move lives in exactly one set). A move *(u, v)* is coalesced only if it passes the **Briggs criterion** — the combined node has fewer than *K* neighbors of *significant* degree (≥ *K*) — or the more aggressive **George criterion**: every neighbor *t* of *u* either already interferes with *v* or has degree *< K*. When coalescing stalls, moves are *frozen* (abandoned) to unblock simplification. The result eliminates the vast majority of copies while provably never turning a colorable graph uncolorable under the Briggs criterion.

**Linear scan** (Poletto and Sarkar [5]) abandons graphs entirely. Each live range becomes a linear *interval* [start, end] over a numbered instruction stream; allocation is a single sweep:

```c
/* Poletto–Sarkar linear scan (spilling elided) [5] */
active = {};
for (i in intervals sorted by increasing start) {
    expire_old_intervals(i);          /* free registers of ended intervals */
    if (no free register) spill(i);   /* or split interval */
    else { reg[i] = free_register(); insert i into active sorted by end; }
}
```

Because intervals are processed once in order and *active* is kept sorted by end point, the algorithm is dramatically faster than coloring — *several times faster* than even a fast graph-coloring allocator in Poletto and Sarkar's measurements — while producing code *within 12%* of an aggressive iterated-coalescing allocator on all but two of their benchmarks [5]. The original formulation neither splits intervals nor models lifetime holes; Traub, Holloway, and Smith's *second-chance binpacking* (1998) and later SSA-based linear scans (e.g., in HotSpot and V8) recovered much of the gap.

## 5. Empirical Results and Proofs

**Theorems.** Three results anchor the theory:

> **Theorem (Cytron et al. [1]).** *The set of blocks requiring φ-functions for a variable defined in blocks S is exactly DF⁺(S) = J⁺(S).* Hence φ-placement needs no search beyond the iterated dominance frontier.

> **Theorem (Hack, Grund, Wagner).** *The interference graph of a strict SSA program is chordal.* Chordal graphs admit a perfect elimination ordering (via maximum-cardinality search), so greedy coloring is optimal and polynomial — *register assignment* under SSA is easy.

> **Theorem (Spill complexity [6, 7]).** *Optimal spilling remains NP-complete even for SSA programs.* Coloring is polynomial under SSA; choosing *what* to spill is not. This is the precise complexity boundary of the field.

**Measurements from the literature.** Poletto and Sarkar [5] report linear scan compiling several times faster than graph coloring while staying within 12% of its code quality (all but two benchmarks) — the quantitative basis for linear scan's adoption in JIT compilers. Briggs [3] demonstrates that optimistic coloring measurably reduces dynamic spill operations versus Chaitin's eager spilling, because many would-be spill candidates turn out colorable at select time. George and Appel [4] show iterated coalescing removing the overwhelming majority of register-to-register moves introduced by calling conventions and SSA translation, with no colorability regressions under the Briggs criterion. Appel and George [6] used integer programming to compute *truly optimal* spill placements for CISC machines, and in doing so refuted Briggs's conjecture that the live-range splits induced by SSA are the appropriate granularity for spilling decisions — optimal spill regions cut across SSA live ranges.

| Allocator | Time complexity | Code quality | Natural habitat |
|---|---|---|---|
| Chaitin simplify–select [2] | Quadratic-ish (iterated) | Good baseline | 1980s–90s AOT compilers |
| Briggs optimistic [3] | Same order, fewer iterations | Better (fewer spills) | GCC lineage |
| Iterated coalescing [4] | Higher (worklist machine) | Best of coloring family | High-opt AOT (LLVM greedy) |
| Linear scan [5] | Near-linear | Within ~12% of coloring | JITs (HotSpot, V8, Jikes) |
| Chordal/SSA coloring | Polynomial (MCS + greedy) | Optimal *coloring* | Research, LLVM fast RA ideas |

## 6. Limitations and Threats to Validity

**Frontier size.** Cytron et al. note that *|DF|* can be *Θ(n²)* even for sparse, structured CFGs; the elegant *O(|V| + |E| + |DF|)* bound degrades in practice, which is why Bilardi–Pingali lock-step and later *pruned* algorithms matter for huge procedures.

**Liveness precision.** Pruned SSA's promise depends on precise liveness; semi-pruned SSA's global-name approximation can retain dead φs, and every retained φ is a copy after destruction. CSSA translation itself inserts copies that coalescing must then remove — a round trip whose cost is real.

**Heuristic, not optimal.** Graph coloring allocators are heuristics for an NP-complete problem: spill choices, color choices, and coalescing orders are all greedy. Optimal-spilling studies [6] show the gap is genuine, though usually small.

**Linear scan's blind spots.** The original algorithm [5] cannot split intervals and ignores lifetime holes, so a long-lived variable can pin a register across a call-heavy region; later variants fix this at some compile-time cost.

**SSA strictness.** The chordal-coloring result requires *strict* SSA (each use dominated by its definition, φs placed per the iterated-join rule); real compilers routinely violate strictness during transformation, and destruction reintroduces the copies the theory assumed away.

**Empirical scope.** The headline numbers above are *reported* measurements from their original papers' benchmark suites (largely SPEC-era C/Fortran); they do not automatically transfer to modern managed-language workloads or to register-rich vector ISAs.

---

## 7. Conclusion

From Cytron's dominance frontiers to Briggs's optimistic coloring runs a single coherent idea: *make data flow explicit, then exploit its structure*. The iterated-join theorem tells us exactly where φ-functions must go; semi-pruned SSA and CSSA make that placement affordable and reversible; Chaitin's simplify–select, Briggs's optimism, and George–Appel's iterated coalescing turn interference into allocation; linear scan shows how much of that machinery a JIT can discard; and the chordal characterization of SSA interference graphs draws the exact line — coloring polynomial, spilling NP-complete — that no allocator can cross, only approach. Four decades on, every production compiler still walks the path this literature paved: frontiers in, colors out.
