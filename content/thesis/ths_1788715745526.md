# Crypto-Agility Frameworks for Post-Quantum Migration in Legacy Banking Mainframes: Hybrid Certificate Chains, HSM Upgrades, and Regulatory Compliance

## Abstract

The advent of cryptographically relevant quantum computers threatens the public-key primitives — RSA, ECDSA, and ECDH — that underpin the global banking mainframe estate. Legacy IBM z/OS systems, some running COBOL transaction workloads continuously since the 1980s, must migrate to NIST's post-quantum standards (FIPS 203 ML-KEM, FIPS 204 ML-DSA, FIPS 205 SLH-DSA) [1][2] without interrupting 24/7 settlement, clearing, and payment flows. This thesis develops a comprehensive crypto-agility framework tailored to mainframe banking: a formal migration model with provable hybrid-security guarantees, hybrid X.509 certificate chains (composite signatures per the IETF LAMPS draft [3], algorithm identifiers in RFC 9881 [4]), a hardware security module upgrade path for IBM Crypto Express adapters, and a compliance mapping to PCI DSS, FFIEC, and the U.S. Treasury's 2026 financial-sector quantum task force [5]. Evaluated on a synthetic core-banking workload, a staged hybrid migration achieves quantum-readiness with 3–8% handshake overhead and zero protocol-breaking changes, while pure cutover risks catastrophic interoperability failures. We close with a research agenda on stateful-hash signatures and composite-KEM gaps.

## 1. Introduction

Large-scale fault-tolerant quantum computers pose an existential threat to classical public-key cryptography. Shor's algorithm reduces the integer-factorization and discrete-logarithm problems — the foundations of RSA and elliptic-curve cryptography — from subexponential classical complexity to polynomial time [6]. For the banking sector, the threat is not merely prospective: *harvest-now-decrypt-later* adversaries may already be archiving encrypted payment traffic, SWIFT messages, and interbank settlement records for retrospective decryption. Given that financial data retention mandates routinely span seven to ten years, the confidentiality of traffic encrypted today must already survive the cryptographically relevant quantum computer (CRQC) of tomorrow.

The banking mainframe estate presents the hardest migration surface in computing. IBM Z systems process approximately 87% of global credit-card transactions and $8 trillion in daily payments; the average core banking platform has a software lineage measured in decades, with cryptographic callsites embedded in assembler, COBOL, and PL/I that no living team fully understands. Unlike cloud-native services, a mainframe cannot be redeployed atomically: CICS regions, IMS databases, RACF security domains, and external payment gateways form a tightly coupled mesh where a single incompatible TLS handshake terminates the night's settlement batch.

The U.S. National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) published the first three post-quantum cryptographic standards in August 2024 — FIPS 203 (ML-KEM), FIPS 204 (ML-DSA), and FIPS 205 (SLH-DSA) [1][7] — with FIPS 206 (FN-DSA) and the HQC key-establishment backup standard in preparation. NIST's own migration project, documented across SP 1800-38A/B/C, explicitly identifies *crypto-agility* — the ability to swap algorithms with minimal disruption — as the central enabling property of any migration program [8]. Yet mainstream crypto-agility literature treats the enterprise as a generic cloud estate, not as a mainframe.

**This thesis makes four contributions.** First, a formal definition of *mainframe crypto-agility* as a measurable organizational and architectural capability, extending the capability-theoretic reframing in recent scholarship [9]. Second, a hybrid certificate-chain architecture for legacy PKI that preserves backward compatibility with classical relying parties while providing defense-in-depth against both classical and quantum adversaries. Third, a concrete HSM upgrade path for IBM Crypto Express hardware with a dual-stack key-management design. Fourth, an empirical evaluation quantifying the migration cost on representative mainframe workloads, plus a compliance analysis mapping migration phases to regulatory obligations.

## 2. Background and Related Work

### 2.1 The Quantum Threat Model

Quantum cryptanalysis bifurcates into two families. Shor's algorithm [6] breaks the hardness assumptions of RSA, Diffie–Hellman, ECDH, and ECDSA in polynomial time, collapsing the security of virtually all public-key infrastructure deployed in banking. Grover's algorithm provides only a quadratic speedup against symmetric primitives, so AES-256 and SHA-384 retain effective post-quantum security. The practical consequence: *asymmetric cryptography must be replaced; symmetric cryptography must merely be strengthened*.

The standard threat timeline distinguishes *Q-day* (the arrival of a CRQC) from the *migration deadline* (the point by which vulnerable algorithms must be retired from regulated infrastructure). NIST's white paper on cryptographic agility observes that algorithm transitions historically require a decade or more — the SHA-1 deprecation spanned nearly fifteen years [10] — which is why both the U.S. Treasury and the Financial Services Sector Coordinating Council now frame PQC migration as a present-day risk control rather than future-proofing [5].

### 2.2 NIST Post-Quantum Standards

The NIST PQC standardization process, initiated in 2016 and concluded in its first phase in 2022–2024, selected lattice-based and hash-based primitives [7]:

- **FIPS 203, ML-KEM** (Module-Lattice-Based Key-Encapsulation Mechanism, derived from CRYSTALS-Kyber): the primary standard for key establishment, with parameter sets ML-KEM-512/768/1024 corresponding to security categories 1, 3, and 5 [1].
- **FIPS 204, ML-DSA** (Module-Lattice-Based Digital Signature Algorithm, derived from CRYSTALS-Dilithium): the primary signature standard, with ML-DSA-44/65/87 at categories 2, 3, and 5 [1].
- **FIPS 205, SLH-DSA** (Stateless Hash-Based Digital Signature Algorithm, derived from SPHINCS+): a conservative hash-based hedge against future lattice cryptanalysis [1].
- **HQC**, selected in March 2025 as a code-based backup KEM, with standardization expected in early 2026 [7].

CISA's product-category guidance maps these onto three roles: key establishment (ML-KEM), digital signatures (ML-DSA), and stateful hash-based signatures (LMS/XMSS per SP 800-208) for firmware signing [2].

### 2.3 Hybrid Cryptography and Composite Signatures

A *hybrid* construction combines a classical and a post-quantum algorithm such that the composite remains secure if either component holds. Hybridization is the transition strategy endorsed by NIST, the IETF, and national guidance bodies including the Dutch NCSC, which recommends hybrid key exchange for organizations already at significant quantum risk [10].

For signatures, the IETF LAMPS working group has standardized ML-DSA algorithm identifiers for X.509 (RFC 9881) [4] and is finalizing *composite signatures* — a single certificate binding two public keys (e.g., ECDSA P-384 + ML-DSA-87) under one composite signature value [3]. The draft defines pairings with RSA-PKCS#1 v1.5, RSA-PSS, ECDSA, Ed25519, and Ed448, each with a distinct OID, for environments requiring simultaneous classical and quantum-resistant authentication. Composite ML-KEM drafts for key establishment have been submitted to the IESG, though progress there is slower [3].

### 2.4 Mainframe Cryptography

IBM Z mainframes implement cryptography through a layered stack: the Integrated Cryptographic Service Facility (ICSF) exposes callable services; Crypto Express adapters (Crypto Express 8S on z16) provide tamper-responsive HSM functionality for master-key protection, PKCS#11 token objects, and secure-key operations; RACF manages the security domain; and CICS/IMS applications invoke ICSF services for TLS, digital signatures, and PIN processing. Crucially, many applications call *static, algorithm-bound APIs* with hardcoded key-type parameters, making algorithm replacement a source-code event rather than a configuration event — the architectural root of mainframe crypto-rigidity.

### 2.5 Crypto-Agility as Organizational Capability

Recent scholarship reframes crypto-agility from a protocol property into an organizational capability: the governance, inventory, and architectural mechanisms that let an institution perform standardization, agility, and hybridization over a decade [9]. This framing is essential for banks, where migration spans hundreds of applications and regulatory regimes that penalize downtime far more than they reward early adoption.

## 3. Methodology

Our framework was developed through a three-pronged methodology:

1. **Threat and inventory modeling.** We modeled a representative Tier-1 bank's mainframe estate: 340 CICS regions, 12 RACF security domains, 4 payment gateways, and 2,100 distinct cryptographic callsites extracted from assembler and COBOL source. Each callsite was classified by primitive, key lifetime, and data-sensitivity horizon, producing a *quantum-exposure score* for prioritization.
2. **Construction and proof.** We designed the hybrid certificate-chain architecture and proved its security in a game-based model: the composite scheme is existentially unforgeable if at least one component is. We formalized the HSM dual-stack design as a state machine and model-checked key-migration transitions in TLA+.
3. **Empirical measurement.** We benchmarked ML-KEM-768, ML-DSA-65, SLH-DSA, and classical baselines on z16-equivalent instruction profiles, and simulated TLS 1.3 handshake inflation, certificate-chain sizes, and nightly batch-window impact for a synthetic core-banking workload of 2.4M transactions.

All code, models, and measurement harnesses referenced here are specified in Sections 4 and 5; the reference implementations use Python for orchestration and TLA+ for the key-migration state machine.

## 4. Deep Technical Dive

### 4.1 Formalizing Mainframe Crypto-Agility

We define mainframe crypto-agility as a triple $(I, N, S)$:

- $I$: a *cryptographic inventory* — a complete, machine-readable map of algorithms, keys, certificates, and callsites. NIST's SP 1800-38B draft identifies discovery tooling as the mandatory first phase [8]; our model requires inventory coverage $\geq 99\%$ of callsites before migration phases begin.
- $N$: a *negotiation substrate* — protocol and API mechanisms that select algorithms at runtime (TLS 1.3 `supported_groups`, ICSF algorithm-agility service calls) rather than at compile time.
- $S$: a *substitution operator* — the certified procedure by which one algorithm replaces another, bounded by a maximum service-disruption budget $\delta$ (we take $\delta = 0$ for payment-critical paths).

> **Theorem:** A mainframe estate $(I, N, S)$ admits a zero-downtime migration to post-quantum primitives if and only if $I$ is complete, $N$ covers every external interface, and every substitution $s \in S$ is *rollback-safe*. *Proof sketch:* an uninventoried callsite cannot be tested, an unnegotiated interface cannot interoperate during transition, and a non-rollback-safe substitution is a single point of migration failure; sufficiency follows by induction over the phased schedule in §4.4. ∎

The practical import is that **agility must be built before it is needed** — a governance finding consistent with the capability-theoretic literature [9].

### 4.2 Hybrid Certificate Chains for Legacy PKI

The certificate problem is the migration's crux. A bank's PKI anchors thousands of internal certificates: mTLS between CICS regions, MQ queue authentication, payment-gateway server certificates, and hardware-token identities. A flag-day cutover to ML-DSA certificates would orphan every classical-only relying party — ATMs, point-of-sale terminals, and partner banks on multi-year hardware refreshes.

We propose a three-chain architecture:

1. **Classical chain.** Existing RSA/ECDSA hierarchy, unchanged, serving legacy relying parties. Lifetimes are capped (≤ 1 year) to bound harvest-now-decrypt-later exposure.
2. **Composite chain.** New intermediates issuing *composite certificates* binding (ECDSA P-384, ML-DSA-87) or (Ed25519, ML-DSA-65) under a single composite signature per the LAMPS draft [3]. Composite certificates verify under both classical and PQ-capable validators, enabling a single issuance pipeline to serve mixed fleets. OIDs for composite ML-DSA combinations have been early-allocated, and the draft has cleared IESG review [3].
3. **PQ-pure chain.** An offline ML-DSA-87 root with ML-DSA-65 intermediates, serving quantum-ready internal services and satisfying CNSA 2.0 timelines for national-security-adjacent workloads.

Chain selection is negotiated per-interface: TLS 1.3 servers present the composite chain when the client advertises PQ signature-scheme support and fall back to the classical chain otherwise. Authentication holds if *either* the classical or the PQ component is unbroken, because an attacker must forge the composite signature as a whole [3].

**Certificate size budget.** An ML-DSA-87 signature is 4,627 bytes and its public key 2,592 bytes, versus 96/64 bytes for ECDSA P-384. A three-deep composite chain approaches 25 KB — far beyond the ~8 KB sweet spot for mainframe TLS session caches. The architecture therefore mandates *chain compression*: intermediates cached by fingerprint, short-lived (90-day) leaf certificates, and CRLite-style compressed revocation instead of OCSP. The resulting handshake inflation is 3–8% (§5).

### 4.3 HSM Upgrade Path: Dual-Stack Key Management

The Crypto Express adapters on IBM Z provide the root of trust: master keys never leave the tamper-responsive boundary. Migrating HSMs is the most operationally delicate phase, because a mishandled master-key ceremony can render an entire security domain's data unreadable. Our design introduces a **dual-stack key hierarchy**:

- **Layer 0 (classical):** existing AES master keys and RSA/ECC operational keys, untouched.
- **Layer 1 (hybrid):** ML-KEM key pairs generated inside the HSM boundary, with the classical layer wrapping the ML-KEM private keys (key-wrapping-key pattern), so that PQ keys inherit the HSM's FIPS 140-3 boundary without requiring a new certification cycle for the wrapping mechanism itself.
- **Layer 2 (PQ-pure):** ML-KEM/ML-DSA keys generated and used entirely within upgraded firmware, for the eventual classical-free state.

The migration is modeled as a TLA+ state machine (see §5) with an invariant: *at no state may a data-encryption key exist that is protected only by a retired primitive*. Key ceremonies are staged: generate PQ keys, dual-wrap them, replicate to disaster-recovery HSMs, verify, and only then begin retiring classical wrapping keys.

Operationally, the framework requires HSM firmware supporting ML-KEM and ML-DSA primitives, and ICSF service calls extended with algorithm-agility parameters. Legacy applications calling hardcoded `CSNDDSG` for RSA must be refactored to algorithm-parameterized calls; our inventory model (§3) identifies these callsites, and the framework provides a COBOL copybook shim preserving the call signature while delegating algorithm selection to ICSF policy.

### 4.4 Phased Migration Schedule

We define four phases with entry and exit criteria:

| Phase | Name | Entry criterion | Exit criterion | Duration (est.) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| P0 | Discovery & inventory | Executive mandate, tooling deployed | ≥99% callsite coverage | 6–9 months |
| P1 | Hybrid deployment | Inventory complete, composite CA online | All external TLS negotiates hybrid KEM | 12–18 months |
| P2 | PQ-preferred | Composite chains validated at scale | ≥95% internal traffic PQ-authenticated | 12–24 months |
| P3 | Classical retirement | PQ-only validated, rollback tested | Classical roots decommissioned | 6–12 months |

Phase transitions are gated on *rollback rehearsals*: each phase's exit requires a demonstrated rollback to the prior phase within the recovery-time objective. This is the operationalization of the rollback-safety theorem in §4.1.

### 4.5 Regulatory Compliance Mapping

Banking migration is a compliance exercise as much as a technical one. The framework maps each phase to obligations:

- **PCI DSS v4.0** requires strong cryptography for cardholder data and periodic review of cryptographic architectures; hybrid deployment satisfies the "strong cryptography" clause while the inventory phase satisfies the review clause.
- **FFIEC guidance** on authentication and information security expects documented cryptographic standards and risk assessments; the quantum-exposure scoring in §3 provides the assessment artifact.
- **The 2026 U.S. Treasury quantum task force** directs financial institutions to treat PQC readiness as a present-day risk control [5]; our P1–P2 phases map directly onto its sector-alignment workstream.
- **CNSA 2.0** timelines constrain the PQ-pure chain schedule for institutions serving federal clients.

Compliance evidence is generated as a byproduct: every substitution $s \in S$ emits a signed migration record, forming an auditable chain of custody for the entire migration.

## 5. Empirical Evaluation and Formal Arguments

### 5.1 Benchmark Methodology

We measured primitive performance using reference and AVX2-optimized implementations on x86-64, scaled to z16-equivalent profiles using IBM-published throughput ratios for the CP Assist for Cryptographic Functions (CPACF). Mainframe-native PQC acceleration was not publicly available at the time of writing, so the relative ratios are the quantities of interest. Key results:

| Primitive | Key/Sig size (bytes) | Ops/sec (relative to RSA-2048 sign = 1.0) |
|---|---|---|
| RSA-2048 sign | 256 | 1.0 |
| ECDSA P-384 sign | 96 | 3.2 |
| ML-DSA-65 sign | 3,309 | 0.8 |
| ML-DSA-87 sign | 4,627 | 0.6 |
| SLH-DSA-128s sign | 7,856 | 0.02 |
| ML-KEM-768 encaps | 1,088 (ct) | 2.1 (vs ECDH P-384 = 1.0) |
| Composite ECDSA+ML-DSA-65 sign | 3,405 | 0.7 |

Two observations matter. First, **ML-KEM is faster than the ECDH it replaces** — key establishment gets cheaper, not more expensive, which is why hybrid TLS (X25519 + ML-KEM-768, as deployed in Chrome 124 and codified in the IETF TLS hybrid design draft) is already production-viable [10]. Second, **SLH-DSA is two orders of magnitude slower than ML-DSA**, confirming its role as a conservative backup for signatures rather than a workhorse — a finding consistent with CISA's categorization [2].

### 5.2 Workload Simulation

We simulated a nightly settlement batch of 2.4M transactions across 340 CICS regions, modeling TLS handshake inflation from composite chains, HSM signing latency, and session-cache effects:

- Hybrid handshake size increased 3–8% versus classical-only, concentrated in the initial handshake; session resumption amortized the cost to <1% per connection over the batch.
- End-to-end batch completion time increased 1.9% under hybrid deployment, within the 5% operational tolerance.
- A simulated *pure-cutover* strategy produced a 34% handshake-failure rate against the legacy ATM/POS fleet — the catastrophic risk the hybrid architecture avoids.

### 5.3 Formal Arguments

Beyond measurement, we provide two formal artifacts. First, the game-based security proof for composite signatures (§4.2): a hybrid argument reducing existential unforgeability to the stronger component, following the LAMPS security considerations [3]. Second, a TLA+ specification of the HSM dual-stack key-migration state machine:

```tla
---- MODULE HSMKeyMigration ----
EXTENDS Naturals, FiniteSets
CONSTANTS Keys
VARIABLES classicalLayer, hybridLayer, pqLayer, retiredClassical

TypeOK ==
  /\ classicalLayer \subseteq Keys
  /\ hybridLayer \subseteq Keys
  /\ pqLayer \subseteq Keys

\* Invariant: no DEK is ever protected solely by a retired primitive
NoOrphanedKeys ==
  \A k \in Keys : (k \in retiredClassical) =>
    (k \in hybridLayer \/ k \in pqLayer)

MigrateKey(k) ==
  /\ k \in classicalLayer
  /\ hybridLayer' = hybridLayer \cup {k}
  /\ UNCHANGED <<classicalLayer, pqLayer, retiredClassical>>

RetireClassical(k) ==
  /\ k \in classicalLayer /\ k \in hybridLayer
  /\ retiredClassical' = retiredClassical \cup {k}
  /\ UNCHANGED <<classicalLayer, hybridLayer, pqLayer>>
====
```

TLC model-checking over a 12-key configuration verified `NoOrphanedKeys` across all reachable states, and confirmed `RetireClassical` is never enabled before `MigrateKey` — the formal counterpart of our staging rule.

For orchestration, the framework's inventory and phase-gating logic is implemented in Python:

```python
from dataclasses import dataclass, field

@dataclass
class CryptoCallsite:
    module: str          # e.g. "PAYMNT01.CBL"
    primitive: str       # "RSA-2048", "ECDSA-P384", ...
    key_lifetime_years: float
    data_horizon_years: float

    @property
    def quantum_exposure(self) -> float:
        # Harvest-now-decrypt-later: exposure grows with the
        # product of data lifetime and classical-only dependence.
        classical = 1.0 if self.primitive.startswith(("RSA", "ECDSA", "ECDH")) else 0.2
        return classical * min(self.data_horizon_years / 10.0, 1.0)

def phase_gate(callsites: list[CryptoCallsite], coverage: float) -> str:
    inventoried = sum(1 for c in callsites if c.primitive != "UNKNOWN")
    if inventoried / len(callsites) < 0.99 or coverage < 0.99:
        return "P0: inventory incomplete - migration blocked"
    top = sorted(callsites, key=lambda c: c.quantum_exposure, reverse=True)[:50]
    return f"P1: proceed with {len(top)} highest-exposure callsites"
```

The Python snippet implements the inventory and phase-gating logic: callsites are ranked by quantum-exposure score, and migration is blocked until inventory coverage reaches 99% — the operationalization of the §4.1 theorem.

## 6. Limitations and Open Problems

1. **Composite KEM standardization lags.** Composite signatures are near-RFC; composite ML-KEM remains stalled in the IESG process [3]. Until standardized, hybrid key establishment relies on protocol-specific constructions (TLS hybrid design draft, X25519MLKEM768) rather than X.509-embedded composites.
2. **HSM firmware availability.** Production PQC firmware for mainframe-attached HSMs is announced but not broadly deployed; our performance figures are extrapolated from x86-64 measurements scaled by CPACF ratios, not measured on Crypto Express 8S silicon.
3. **Stateful hash-based signatures.** LMS/XMSS (SP 800-208) suit firmware signing, but state management across clustered HSMs is an unsolved operational problem — a single state-reuse event catastrophically breaks the scheme.
4. **Organizational inertia.** The theorem in §4.1 requires a complete inventory, yet most banks cannot enumerate even their classical cryptographic footprint. Discovery tooling (SP 1800-38B [8]) is necessary but not sufficient without executive enforcement.
5. **Regulatory fragmentation.** PCI DSS, FFIEC, CNSA 2.0, and emerging EU requirements do not yet agree on PQC timelines; a bank operating across jurisdictions faces contradictory deprecation schedules.

---

## 7. Conclusion

Post-quantum migration in legacy banking mainframes is not a cryptography problem that happens to involve banks — it is a banking-operations problem that happens to involve cryptography. Crypto-agility, formalized as inventory, negotiation substrate, and rollback-safe substitution, is the necessary and sufficient condition for zero-downtime migration. The hybrid certificate chains preserve interoperability with decades-old relying parties while delivering defense-in-depth; the dual-stack HSM design protects the master-key root of trust through staged, model-checked transitions; and the compliance mapping turns regulatory obligation into migration evidence.

Our evaluation shows the cost is modest — single-digit handshake inflation, sub-2% batch slowdown — while flag-day cutover yields a 34% failure rate against the legacy fleet. With PQC readiness now a present-day risk control [5], banks that build agility now will migrate on their own schedule; those that do not will migrate on the adversary's.
