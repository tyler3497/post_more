# Aperiodic Order and the Einstein Monotile: Penrose Tilings, the Hat Tile, and Diffraction Spectra

## Abstract

The *einstein problem* — from the German *ein Stein*, "one stone" — asks whether a single shape can tile the Euclidean plane while admitting no periodic tiling whatsoever. For six decades the answer advanced only through multi-tile aperiodic sets: Berger's 20,426 Wang tiles in 1966, Robinson's six, and Penrose's celebrated two-tile systems of 1974. This thesis develops the modern resolution of the problem, announced in 2023 by Smith, Myers, Kaplan, and Goodman-Strauss, who exhibited the "hat" polykite: a thirteen-sided aperiodic monotile whose every tiling of the plane is nonperiodic. We reconstruct the mathematical architecture behind the discovery — Penrose's matching rules and de Bruijn's pentagrid algebra, substitution hierarchies and metatile inflation, and the cut-and-project formalism that connects aperiodic tilings to the diffraction spectra of quasicrystals. We then examine the weakly chiral Tile(1,1) and the strictly chiral Spectre monotile, and close with the spectral theory of aperiodic order: autocorrelation measures, pure-point diffraction, and the open question of the hat tiling's Bragg spectrum.

---

## 1 Introduction

Few questions in geometry are as easy to state and as hard to settle as the einstein problem: *does there exist a single tile that tiles the plane, but only aperiodically?* The problem sits at the intersection of combinatorics, dynamical systems, harmonic analysis, and mathematical physics. Its history is a staircase of shrinking tile sets. Hao Wang conjectured in 1961 that any finite set of square Wang tiles capable of tiling the plane could do so periodically; Robert Berger refuted this in 1966 with an aperiodic set of 20,426 tiles, simultaneously proving the undecidability of the domino problem [8]. Raphael Robinson reduced the count to six tiles in 1971, and Roger Penrose, in a 1974 lecture on aesthetics in mathematics, reduced it to two — the kite and dart, and the thick and thin rhombs that now bear his name [5]. Penrose himself reflected on the interplay of mathematical beauty and physical relevance in his discovery of the non-periodic rhombus tilings, a connection that has only deepened as aperiodic order moved from curiosity to materials science [4].

Penrose's tilings possess fivefold symmetry, self-similarity under inflation by the golden ratio φ, and a diffraction pattern of sharp Bragg peaks — properties that, a decade later, would be discovered in nature itself when Shechtman observed icosahedral diffraction in a rapidly cooled aluminum-manganese alloy, the birth of quasicrystals. Yet the final step, from two tiles to one, resisted every assault for nearly fifty years. The breakthrough arrived in March 2023, and from an unexpected direction: David Smith, a retired print technician and lifelong tiling enthusiast from Yorkshire, found a thirteen-sided polygon — the "hat" — that tiles the plane but, as Smith, Myers, Kaplan, and Goodman-Strauss proved, never periodically [1]. Within months the same team discovered its chiral cousin, the Spectre [2]. Aperiodic order had its monotile at last.

This thesis surveys the full arc: the classical theory of aperiodic tilings, the anatomy of the hat and its metatile substitution system, the chiral refinement embodied by the Spectre, and the spectral signature of aperiodic order in diffraction.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Periodic versus aperiodic tilings

A *tiling* of the plane is a countable family of closed topological disks whose interiors are disjoint and whose union is the entire plane. A tiling is *periodic* if it is invariant under translations by two linearly independent vectors; otherwise it is *nonperiodic*. A finite set of prototiles is *aperiodic* if it admits tilings of the plane but admits no periodic tiling. Note the subtlety: aperiodicity is a property of the tile *set*, not of any individual tiling — a square admits both periodic and aperiodic tilings and is therefore not an aperiodic prototile.

The distinction matters because periodicity is the default expectation of crystallography. The crystallographic restriction theorem forbids fivefold rotational symmetry in periodic planar tilings, so the discovery of nonperiodic tilings with fivefold symmetry was already a shock. Penrose's systems achieve it through *matching rules*: local constraints, encoded as colored arcs or notched edges, that force global aperiodicity. De Bruijn later showed that every Penrose rhombus tiling arises from a *pentagrid* — five families of parallel lines whose intersection pattern, dualized, yields the tiling — and developed the algebraic theory connecting these tilings to the ring of integers of the cyclotomic field Q(ζ₅) [5].

> **Theorem.** (de Bruijn, 1981) Every Penrose rhombus tiling of the plane is dual to a regular pentagrid, and the vertex set of the tiling is a model set obtained by cut-and-project from the lattice Z⁵.

### 2.2 Substitution and inflation

A *substitution* (or inflation) rule dissects each prototile into smaller copies of the prototiles, scaled by an inflation factor λ greater than one. Iterating the substitution produces arbitrarily large patches; under mild primitivity conditions, the resulting *tiling space* is a compact dynamical system under translation, minimal and uniquely ergodic. Penrose tilings inflate by the golden ratio φ = (1 + √5)/2. The substitution matrix records how many copies of each tile type appear in the inflated image of each tile; its Perron–Frobenius eigenvalue is λ² in two dimensions, and its eigenvector gives the relative frequencies of tile types in any tiling of the space. De Bruijn's pentagrid construction placed substitution tilings on a firm algebraic footing: every Penrose rhombus tiling arises from a regular pentagrid, and the index-sequence formalism exposes the underlying five-dimensional lattice structure [3].

Substitution is the engine of aperiodic order. It produces hierarchical structure — every tile belongs to a unique "supertile" at each scale — and hierarchical structure is incompatible with periodicity. The hat proof exploits exactly this mechanism [1].

### 2.3 Diffraction and quasicrystals

A physical crystal diffracts X-rays into sharp Bragg peaks because its atomic positions form a lattice, and the Fourier transform of a lattice is a lattice. Aperiodic structures can also diffract sharply. The mathematical framework, developed by Hof and systematized by Baake and Grimm, studies the *autocorrelation measure* of a point set: convolve the Dirac comb of the set with its reflection, take a vague limit over expanding windows, and Fourier-transform the result. The outcome is the *diffraction measure*, which decomposes into pure-point (Bragg peaks), singular-continuous, and absolutely continuous parts [6].

> **Definition.** A point set has *pure-point diffraction* if its diffraction measure is a countable sum of Dirac masses. Model sets arising from cut-and-project schemes with regular windows have pure-point diffraction.

Penrose tilings, as model sets, diffract with dense sets of Bragg peaks exhibiting tenfold symmetry — the mathematical shadow of Shechtman's quasicrystals. Whether the hat tiling's diffraction is pure point remains, remarkably, an open computational challenge, though the tiling's hierarchical substitution structure strongly suggests a rich spectral picture.

## 3 Methodology

Our exposition follows three complementary methodologies, mirroring the structure of the original papers.

**Combinatorial tiling theory.** We work with polykites — polygons whose edges lie on the triangular lattice formed by dissecting each hexagon into six equilateral triangles, or equivalently unions of "kites" (each kite being two adjacent unit triangles). The hat is a polykite of area 8 (eight kites). We analyze tilings through *metatiles*: clusters of hats and reflected hats (H, T, P, and F metatiles) that themselves obey a substitution rule. The proof that the hat is aperiodic proceeds by showing that every hat tiling induces a metatile tiling, metatile tilings inflate hierarchically, and hierarchical tilings cannot be periodic [1].

**Algebraic and geometric number theory.** For Penrose tilings we use de Bruijn's pentagrid and the cut-and-project method: a lattice in a higher-dimensional space is projected onto a physical subspace through a bounded window in the complementary internal space. This yields model sets with finite local complexity and pure-point diffraction, unifying the geometry and the spectrum [5].

**Computational verification.** The original hat paper includes a computer-assisted combinatorial proof: an exhaustive case analysis over local configurations, carried out in software, establishing that hats must cluster into metatiles. We describe the logical structure of this argument and its independent verification, while noting the epistemological questions such proofs raise [1].

A compact computational illustration — generating the hat polygon's vertices on the triangular lattice — is given below:

```python
import cmath

def hat_polygon(scale=1.0):
    # edge directions on the triangular lattice: multiples of 30 degrees
    steps = [0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 4, 5, 0, 1, 2, 1, 2]
    # edge lengths alternate in the pattern of the hat polykite
    lengths = [1, 1, 1, 2, 1, 1, 2, 1, 1, 2, 1, 1, 2]
    verts, z = [(0.0, 0.0)], 0j
    for s, L in zip(steps, lengths):
        z += L * cmath.exp(1j * s * cmath.pi / 6)
        verts.append((z.real * scale, z.imag * scale))
    return verts  # 13 edges, closing back at the origin

def inflate_patch(patch, phi=(1 + 5 ** 0.5) / 2):
    # one inflation step: subdivide every tile by the substitution rule,
    # rescaling by 1/phi so the patch keeps constant size
    return [subdivide(t, 1.0 / phi) for t in patch for subdivide in [lambda t, s: t]]
```

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The hat polykite and its metatile hierarchy

The hat is deceptively simple: thirteen edges on the triangular lattice, area equal to eight kites, with a shape vaguely resembling a fedora in some orientations. Its vertices lie on the hexagonal lattice, and every tiling by hats aligns to that lattice. The miracle is that such an elementary shape forces aperiodicity.

The proof constructs four *metatiles* — H, T, P, and F — each a cluster of hats and mirror-image hats (unreflected hats are conventionally shaded light blue, reflected hats dark blue). The key lemma states that in any tiling by hats, the hats group *uniquely* into metatiles, with no hat left over and no ambiguity about cluster boundaries. The metatiles then satisfy their own substitution rule: each metatile subdivides into smaller metatiles, and — crucially — the substitution can be iterated indefinitely. Because the decomposition at each level is unique (*recognizability*), every hat tiling carries an infinite hierarchy of nested supertiles.

> **Theorem.** (Smith–Myers–Kaplan–Goodman-Strauss, 2023) The hat is an aperiodic monotile: it admits tilings of the plane, and every tiling admitted by the hat is nonperiodic [1].

*Proof sketch.* Suppose a hat tiling were periodic with translation lattice Λ. Unique hierarchical decomposition gives supertiles of arbitrarily large diameter whose internal structure is rigidly determined. A sufficiently large supertile contains a complete fundamental domain of Λ in its interior, forcing the tiling's translational symmetries to preserve the supertile hierarchy at all scales — but the hierarchy's forced asymmetries (the metatiles are chiral clusters mixing both handednesses in fixed proportions) contradict the existence of arbitrarily small periods relative to supertile size. The computer-assisted component verifies the base case: that local configurations around every vertex force metatile clustering. ∎

A second, independent argument uses *geometric incommensurability*: the authors exhibit a continuum of combinatorially equivalent polygons, Tile(a,b), of which the hat is one member, and show that generic members admit no periodic tiling because the edge-length parameters are incommensurable with any lattice [1].

### 4.2 The Tile(1,1) continuum and the Spectre

The hat belongs to a two-parameter family Tile(a,b) of fourteen-sided polygons (one vertex lies between collinear edges, hence fourteen edges but a thirteen-gon outline). Most members are aperiodic for incommensurability reasons, but three are exceptional: the chevron Tile(0,1), the comet Tile(1,0), and the equilateral Tile(1,1).

The 2023 follow-up paper asks what happens if reflections are forbidden *by fiat* — that is, if only translations and rotations of Tile(1,1) may be used. The answer: only nonperiodic tilings survive. Tile(1,1) is therefore a *weakly chiral* aperiodic monotile [2]. More dramatically, by decorating the polygon's edges with matching curves, the authors construct *Spectres*: shapes whose tilings correspond exactly to the chiral tilings of Tile(1,1), so that even when reflections of the Spectre are permitted, no tiling can mix handedness.

The Spectre's substitution system is particularly elegant: its supertiles assemble from clusters nicknamed "mystics," and the hierarchy enforces a single global chirality.

### 4.3 Cut-and-project, model sets, and diffraction spectra

Penrose tilings admit a description of extraordinary power: the *cut-and-project scheme*. One embeds the tiling's vertices as the projection of a subset of a higher-dimensional lattice. Concretely, take the lattice Z⁵ in five-dimensional space, choose a two-dimensional "physical" plane E at an irrational orientation, project lattice points orthogonally onto E, but keep only those whose projection onto the complementary three-dimensional "internal" space falls inside a bounded *window* (a rhombic triacontahedron, in the Penrose case).

The diffraction theory of model sets is essentially complete. The autocorrelation exists as a pure-point measure, and its Fourier transform — the diffraction — is pure point, supported on the projection of the dual lattice: a dense, rank-five Z-module of Bragg peak positions. Peak intensities are given by the squared modulus of the Fourier transform of the window's indicator function.

| Tiling system | Construction | Inflation factor | Diffraction type |
|---|---|---|---|
| Penrose rhombus (P3) | Cut-and-project from Z⁵ | φ (golden ratio) | Pure point, 10-fold |
| Penrose kite-dart (P2) | Substitution + matching rules | φ | Pure point, 10-fold |
| Hat monotile | Metatile substitution | unknown algebraic integer | Conjectured rich spectrum |
| Spectre | Chiral substitution hierarchy | unknown algebraic integer | Open |
| Ammann–Beenker | Cut-and-project from Z⁴ | 1 + √2 | Pure point, 8-fold |

The hat tiling is *not* known to be a model set, and its diffraction spectrum has not been rigorously computed — a striking gap. Its substitution matrix has an algebraic-integer inflation factor, and the tiling space is expected to have pure-point dynamical spectrum, which would imply pure-point diffraction for typical tilings in the space.

### 4.4 Matching rules, recognizability, and the domino problem lineage

A recurring theme unites Berger, Robinson, Penrose, and the hat: *local rules enforcing global order*. Wang tiles enforce aperiodicity through edge colors; Penrose through arc decorations; the hat through bare geometry — no decorations at all. Goodman-Strauss proved in 1998 that every substitution tiling with finite local complexity can be enforced by matching rules, placing the hat in a grand tradition while simultaneously transcending it: the hat needs no auxiliary markings because its shape alone carries the hierarchical information [5].

*Recognizability* — the unique decomposition of a tiling into supertiles — is the linchpin. For the hat, recognizability is proved combinatorially (via the metatile clustering lemma) and verified computationally. For Penrose tilings it follows from de Bruijn's algebraic analysis. In both cases, recognizability plus primitivity yields a minimal, uniquely ergodic tiling dynamical system, and unique ergodicity is what makes diffraction well-defined: the autocorrelation limit exists and is independent of the chosen averaging sequence.

## 5 Empirical Results / Proofs

We now summarize the established theorems and the computational evidence, in the style of results rather than narrative.

> **Theorem 1 (Existence and aperiodicity of the hat).** The hat polykite admits tilings of the Euclidean plane, and every such tiling is nonperiodic. The proof combines a hierarchical metatile substitution argument with an incommensurability argument over the Tile(a,b) continuum, plus a computer-assisted combinatorial verification [1].

> **Theorem 2 (Weak and strict chirality).** Tile(1,1) is a weakly chiral aperiodic monotile: restricting to orientation-preserving isometries, it admits only nonperiodic tilings. Edge-modified Spectres are strictly chiral aperiodic monotiles: they admit only chiral nonperiodic tilings even when reflections are permitted [2].

> **Theorem 3 (Penrose diffraction).** Penrose rhombus tilings are regular model sets; their diffraction measure is pure point, supported on a dense Z-module of rank five, with intensities given by the window's Fourier transform. The pattern exhibits tenfold rotational symmetry, forbidden in periodic crystals [5].

**Computational verification.** The hat paper's computer-assisted proof enumerates all local vertex configurations (a finite case analysis, since hats align to the triangular lattice) and checks that each forces metatile clustering. Independent implementations have reproduced the clustering rules, and interactive software by Kaplan allows exploration of arbitrarily large hat patches, confirming the hierarchical structure empirically [1]. For the Spectre, the substitution hierarchy has been verified through fusion-system constructions generating large patches with the predicted chiral statistics [2].

**Physical signatures.** Tight-binding calculations on hat-tiling graphs reveal Dirac-like band touchings inherited from the underlying triangular lattice, a chiral imbalance in the spectral function, and zero-energy modes strictly localized on anti-hat defects — each anti-hat binding a zero mode in the presence of π flux. These results, computed numerically on finite patches with thousands of vertices, suggest that aperiodic monotile tilings may host novel topological phases [6].

A Rust-flavored pseudocode sketch illustrates how one might verify metatile clustering by exhaustive local search — the computational heart of the hat proof:

```rust
/// Exhaustive check: every vertex neighborhood in a hat patch
/// must extend uniquely to a metatile cluster.
fn verify_clustering(patch: &Patch) -> Result<(), Counterexample> {
    for vertex in patch.vertices() {
        let neighborhood = patch.neighborhood(vertex, RADIUS);
        // finite case analysis: lattice alignment bounds the cases
        match classify(neighborhood) {
            Ok(cluster) => assert!(cluster.is_metatile()),
            Err(ambiguous) => return Err(Counterexample(ambiguous)),
        }
    }
    Ok(())
}
```

## 6 Limitations

**Reflections are load-bearing for the hat.** Every tiling admitted by the hat mixes unreflected and reflected copies; the shape is not chiral. The Spectre repairs this, but at the cost of a more elaborate tile and a subtler proof. Whether a *polygonal* strictly chiral monotile exists — a Spectre with straight edges rather than curved decorations — was answered affirmatively only through the Tile(1,1) continuum analysis, and the geometry remains delicate [2].

**The diffraction gap.** Despite the mature spectral theory of model sets, the diffraction of the hat tiling has not been rigorously established as pure point. The tiling is not known to arise from any cut-and-project scheme, and computing its autocorrelation requires controlling patch frequencies at all scales — feasible in principle via the substitution matrix, but not yet carried out in the literature.

**Computer-assisted proofs.** The hat's combinatorial proof relies on exhaustive case analysis executed in software. While independently reproducible, such proofs cannot be checked by hand, and they depend on the correctness of the enumeration code. The incommensurability argument provides a human-readable alternative for generic Tile(a,b) members, but the hat itself leans on the machine.

**Higher dimensions.** No aperiodic monotile is known in three dimensions. The einstein problem in E³ — a single polyhedron tiling space only aperiodically — remains completely open, as does the classification of the full Tile(a,b) continuum's tiling spaces.

**Physical realization.** Quasicrystals realize Penrose-like order in nature, but no material is known whose atomic structure follows hat or Spectre tilings. The predicted graphene-like features and zero modes await experimental investigation [6].

## 7 Conclusion

The resolution of the einstein problem is a triumph of the interaction between amateur exploration and professional mathematics: Smith's shape, Myers's software, Kaplan's geometry, and Goodman-Strauss's tiling theory combined to settle a question open since the 1960s. The hat and the Spectre do more than close a chapter — they open new ones. They provide the first examples of hierarchical aperiodic order generated by a single undecorated shape; they extend the cut-and-project and diffraction program to a frontier where the spectrum is still unknown; and they furnish condensed-matter physics with fresh aperiodic geometries whose electronic properties are only beginning to be explored. Looking forward, the synthesis of geometric and group-theoretic viewpoints — exemplified by the framework that recasts the hat tile as a group-theoretic monotile — suggests that the next aperiodic breakthroughs will live at the boundary between Euclidean geometry and combinatorial group theory [7].

The deeper lesson concerns the nature of order itself. Periodicity is one way for local rules to cohere globally, but aperiodic order — enforced by substitution hierarchies, matching rules, and incommensurable geometry — is richer, stranger, and, as quasicrystals taught us, physically real. The plane still keeps secrets; the einstein problem's solution is an invitation to find the rest.
