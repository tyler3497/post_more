# Hardware Trojans and Supply-Chain Security: Split Manufacturing, Logic Locking, and Post-Silicon Trust Verification

## Abstract

The globalization of the semiconductor supply chain — fabless design houses, third-party IP vendors, offshore foundries, distributed assembly and test — has enlarged the attack surface for *hardware Trojans*: stealthy, malicious circuit modifications inserted by untrusted parties. This thesis unifies Trojan threat modeling, pre-silicon obfuscation, and post-silicon trust verification. We present the Tehranipoor–Koushanfar Trojan taxonomy along its five classification axes [1]; analyze *split manufacturing*, which partitions front-end and back-end fabrication across untrusted and trusted facilities [2]; and trace *logic locking* from EPIC key-gates [3][4] through the SAT-attack arms race and its point-function defenses [5][6][11]. We then examine post-silicon verification — side-channel fingerprinting, path-delay analysis, machine-learning and golden-chip-free detection [7][21][22][24] — and derive formal bounds on attacker advantage under the oracle-guided threat model. Our central claim: no single countermeasure suffices. Credible assurance demands layered defense spanning design obfuscation, manufacturing partitioning, and statistical post-silicon attestation, with key management and physical probing treated as first-class design constraints.

## 1. Introduction

A single system-on-chip may integrate third-party IP from vendors on three continents, be synthesized with commercial EDA tools, taped out to a foundry under another jurisdiction, and assembled and tested by further contractors [9]. Each handoff is a trust boundary where an adversary may introduce a **hardware Trojan**: a deliberate, malicious modification preserving nominal functionality while hiding a triggerable payload — information leakage, denial of service, or silent functional corruption [1].

Unlike software malware, a hardware Trojan is *baked into silicon*: it cannot be patched, it survives reinstallation of every software layer, and it undermines the root of trust beneath all higher-level security. The seminal survey of Tehranipoor and Koushanfar formalized the threat and its detection landscape [1], catalyzing research spanning Trojan benchmarks (Trust-Hub) [10], design-for-trust methodologies, and a rich countermeasure literature.

Below: **§2** threat model and taxonomy; **§3** methodology; **§4** split manufacturing, logic locking, post-silicon verification; **§5** results and proofs; **§6** limitations; **§7** synthesis and roadmap.

> **Thesis claim.** Layered application of (a) split manufacturing, (b) SAT-resilient logic locking with corruption-bounded point functions, and (c) golden-chip-free post-silicon attestation reduces the probability of an undetected, exploitable Trojan insertion to negligible levels at quantifiable cost — but only when key management and physical-attack surfaces are first-class design constraints.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 The Untrusted-Foundry Threat Model

We adopt the standard threat model of the hardware-security literature [1][3][6]. The **designer** (IP owner) is trusted and produces the netlist. The **foundry** is untrusted: receiving the complete layout (GDSII), it can reverse-engineer the netlist, insert Trojan gates, overproduce ("overbuilding"), or clone the design. The **test facility** may also be untrusted. A **trusted entity** — the design house or a secure activation facility — holds the secret key unlocking logic-locked designs post-fabrication. The end user is assumed to possess an activated chip usable as an *oracle*: the attacker applies inputs and observes correct outputs [5].

This model is deliberately strong on the attacker side: the foundry adversary has full knowledge of the design flow, layout access, and mask-modification capability. The field's question is not whether such an adversary *exists* — leading-edge fabrication economics virtually guarantee offshore outsourcing — but what *provable, measurable* guarantees survive such an adversary.

### 2.2 Hardware Trojan Taxonomy

Tehranipoor and Koushanfar's taxonomy [1] classifies Trojans along five axes:

| Axis | Categories | Example |
|------|-----------|---------|
| **Insertion phase** | Specification, design, fabrication, test, assembly, in-field | Foundry-inserted dopant-level Trojan |
| **Abstraction level** | System, development environment, register-transfer, gate, transistor, physical | Gate-level rare-node trigger |
| **Activation** | Always-on; triggered (internal/external, digital/analog, time- or sensor-based) | Counter reaching 2^32 cycles |
| **Effects** | Change functionality, leak information, degrade performance, deny service | Key leakage via side channel |
| **Location** | Processor, memory, I/O, power supply, clock grid | Ring-oscillator payload in clock tree |

Two properties make Trojans exceptionally hard to detect. First, the **trigger** is a rare event — a 64-bit counter value, a conjunction of low-controllability nodes — so test patterns never activate it. Second, the **payload** is tiny: dozens of gates in a billion-transistor SoC contribute negligible area and power, defeating naive side-channel comparison [1][7].

The Trust-Hub initiative [10] provides the community's standard benchmark suite: Trojan-inserted ISCAS variants with documented trigger/payload structures, enabling reproducible evaluation — ending an era in which every detection paper evaluated against its own Trojans.

### 2.3 From Obfuscation to Provable Locking

Early countermeasures relied on *obscurity*: camouflaged gates, dummy contacts, layout tricks. The EPIC proposal (Ending Piracy of Integrated Circuits) of Roy, Koushanfar, and Markov [3] was the first systematic logic-encryption scheme: XOR/XNOR key-gates (and key-controlled flip-flops) driven by a secret key programmed post-fabrication. Without the key, the foundry sees only an obfuscated netlist; with it, the design house activates genuine functionality — simultaneously addressing overproduction, IP piracy, and Trojan insertion, since an attacker cannot meaningfully modify a circuit whose true function is unknown.

The subsequent decade was a cryptanalytic arms race: the SAT attack [5] broke virtually all pre-2015 locking in minutes; point-function defenses (SARLock [11], Anti-SAT [12], TTLock/SFLL [13]) restored SAT-resiliency at the cost of corruptibility; approximate (AppSAT [6]) and structural attacks eroded those defenses in turn (Section 4.3).

## 3. Methodology

Our methodology combines **threat modeling**, **comparative countermeasure analysis**, and **formal/statistical argumentation**:

1. **Adversary formalization.** Attacker capabilities per countermeasure: for split manufacturing, the attacker observes the FEOL layout and seeks BEOL connectivity (the *proximity attack*); for logic locking, the attacker holds the locked netlist plus an oracle chip and seeks the key or an approximately-correct key; for post-silicon verification, the defender distinguishes Trojan-free from Trojan-infected populations by measurement.

2. **Security metrics.** Locking is evaluated on *output corruptibility*, *SAT-resiliency* (DIPs required — ideally exponential in key size), and *removal/bypass resiliency* [6][14]. Split manufacturing is evaluated on *proximity-attack success rate*; detection on true/false-positive rates and the validity of the *golden-chip* assumption.

3. **Empirical grounding.** Quantitative results come from the cited primary literature on ISCAS-85/89 and ITC-99 benchmarks and Trust-Hub Trojan benchmarks [10]. Section 5 separates reproduced literature results from our own analytic contributions (proof sketches, statistical models); we fabricate no measurements.

4. **Scope discipline.** This thesis treats digital CMOS Trojans and countermeasures. Analog/RF Trojans, EDA-tool compromise, and firmware-level threats are out of scope (Section 6).

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Trojan Insertion Mechanics and the Trigger–Payload Architecture

A hardware Trojan decomposes into a **trigger circuit** and a **payload circuit** [1]: the trigger monitors rare internal nodes and fires only on a specific, improbable condition; the payload then executes the malicious effect. A canonical *time-bomb* Trojan:

```verilog
module trojan_skeleton (
    input  wire        clk,
    input  wire        rst_n,
    input  wire [63:0] secret_key,
    output wire        leak_enable
);
    reg [31:0] counter;
    wire trigger;
    // TRIGGER: fires once after 2^32 cycles -- never during test
    always @(posedge clk or negedge rst_n)
        if (!rst_n)       counter <= 32'h0;
        else if (!trigger) counter <= counter + 1'b1;
    assign trigger = (counter == 32'hFFFFFFFF);
    // PAYLOAD: exfiltrate key bits via a modulated side channel
    assign leak_enable = trigger;
endmodule
```

Three observations follow. *First*, trigger rarity is quantifiable: if the trigger condition has probability *p* per cycle under the test distribution, a test suite of *T* cycles misses it with probability ≈ *e^(−pT)* — for *p = 2^−32* and any feasible *T*, functional detection is hopeless. *Second*, the payload's parametric footprint scales with gate count, which the attacker minimizes; "analog" Trojans exploit sub-threshold effects with a handful of transistors, pushing the footprint below process-variation noise [8]. *Third*, fabrication-time insertion is hardest to counter: the foundry controls the masks and can implement *dopant-level* Trojans that alter threshold voltages without changing the drawn layout, defeating optical reverse-engineering [1].

Detection therefore bifurcates into **pre-silicon** approaches (deny the attacker design understanding — split manufacturing, logic locking) and **post-silicon** approaches (detect modification after fabrication — side-channel analysis, imaging, attestation).

### 4.2 Split Manufacturing: Denying the Foundry the Full Netlist

**Principle.** An *untrusted*, advanced foundry fabricates the front-end-of-line (FEOL) — transistors and lower metal layers — while a *trusted* facility completes the back-end-of-line (BEOL): the upper metal layers determining how transistors are wired [2]. The untrusted foundry sees only a "sea of gates" with dangling wires; recovering the true netlist without BEOL connectivity is a combinatorial matching problem.

The canonical flow, due to Imeson et al. (USENIX Security 2013) [2]: after place-and-route the design is split at metal layer *M_k*; the untrusted foundry fabricates transistors and lower interconnect with open vias at the split layer; the trusted facility deposits the remaining metal, realizing true connectivity, then assembles and tests.

**Security argument and its limits.** Security rests on defeating the *proximity heuristic*: EDA tools place connected cells near each other, so an attacker guesses BEOL connections from FEOL cell proximity — a *proximity attack* recovering a large fraction of missing wires [14]. Defenses include **wire lifting** (routing selected nets entirely in trusted BEOL layers, at area cost) [15], **placement perturbation**, and **concerted wire lifting**, shown to reduce proximity-attack success dramatically [15].

The central practical obstacle, identified in a systematic obfuscation survey [14]: *the foundry business model does not contemplate unfinished wafers*. Persuading a leading-edge foundry to ship FEOL-only wafers to a third party is a logistical and contractual challenge as much as a technical one. The **Split-Chip** variant [14] answers with a *vertical* cut: security-critical control modules at a trusted (older-technology) facility, performance-critical datapaths at the untrusted advanced node, dice integrated via 2.5D interposers or 3D stacking.

> **Theorem (informal, proximity bound):** With *W* lifted wires, the mutual information between the FEOL observation and the true BEOL netlist decreases in *W*. As *W* covers all security-critical nets, attacker advantage tends to random guessing — at BEOL routing-congestion cost superlinear in *W*.

In short: split manufacturing converts a *confidentiality* problem into a *combinatorial* one, with heuristic guarantees and primarily economic deployment friction.

### 4.3 Logic Locking and the SAT-Attack Arms Race

**EPIC and the key-gate paradigm.** Logic locking inserts key-controlled gates — typically XOR/XNOR — such that the circuit computes correctly only under the secret key [3]:

```verilog
// Key-gate insertion (EPIC-style): correct output iff key matches
module locked_and (
    input  wire a, b,
    input  wire k,          // secret key bit, programmed post-fab
    output wire y
);
    wire w;
    assign w = a & b;
    assign y = w ^ k;      // k=0: correct; k=1: inverted
endmodule
```

At scale, hundreds of key-gates are distributed across the netlist to maximize *corruptibility* and resist structural removal [4].

**The SAT attack.** Subramanyan, Ray, and Malik's 2015 result [5] was the field's watershed. Modeling the locked circuit as *C(X, K, Y)* and an activated chip as oracle *C_O(X)*, the attack iteratively asks a SAT solver for a *distinguishing input pattern* (DIP) — an input on which two candidate keys disagree — evaluates the oracle, and adds the constraint *C(X_d, K, Y_d)*, pruning inconsistent keys until no DIP exists. The surviving key is functionally correct.

> **Theorem (SAT-attack correctness [5]):** The DIP loop terminates with a key *K** satisfying *C_L(X, K*) = C_O(X)* for all inputs. Iteration counts are empirically small (tens to hundreds) for pre-2015 locking, yielding key recovery in minutes — forcing a rethinking of what "secure locking" means.

**Point-function defenses and their discontents.** The response was *SAT-resilient* locking based on point functions (SARLock [11], Anti-SAT [12], TTLock/SFLL [13]): each DIP eliminates only a *single* wrong key, forcing *2^|K|* iterations. The price is **low corruptibility** — a wrong key corrupts only a tiny fraction of outputs — so the defense must be *composed* with traditional high-corruption locking.

This composition was attacked in turn. **AppSAT** [6] observed that exact key recovery is unnecessary: SAT solving plus random testing finds an *approximately correct* key — defeating 68 of 71 point-function-defended benchmarks with error approaching *1/2^n*. **Bypass attacks** [16] exploit low corruptibility directly: patch the few corrupted patterns and use any wrong key. **Removal attacks** structurally excise the point-function block [14]. The **SMT attack** [17] generalized the formulation to richer theories, and oracle-less attacks removed the oracle assumption entirely.

The current frontier [14] — *cyclic locking*, *routing-based locking*, *provably secure* constructions under restricted models — coexists with a sobering critical literature: Engels, Hoffmann, and Paar [18] and Rahman et al. [19] show deployment violates the model's assumptions (scan chains leak the key, probing extracts it [20], key management is unsolved). The meta-lesson: *cryptographic-style locking guarantees hold only inside threat models that fabrication and test realities routinely escape*.

### 4.4 Post-Silicon Trust Verification

When pre-silicon obfuscation is insufficient or undeployable, **post-silicon verification** asks: given fabricated chips, can we detect that a Trojan was inserted? The fundamental difficulty is the *reference problem*: detection requires a notion of "correct" behavior or signature, and the attacker controls fabrication.

**Side-channel fingerprinting.** Agrawal et al. [7] pioneered *IC fingerprinting*: compare measured side-channel parameters (transient power, EM emanation) against expected signatures. Sensitivity is pure signal-to-noise: Trojan power *P_T* against process-variation noise *σ_PV* must exceed a threshold set by the target false-positive rate — demanding repeated measurements and differential techniques like power-supply calibration [21].

**Delay-based detection.** Jin and Makris [22] proposed *path-delay fingerprinting*: Trojans on functional paths alter delays measurably, revealing insertions — including some dopant-level Trojans invisible to imaging — when compared against simulation-derived models.

**Machine learning and golden-chip-free detection.** Supervised classifiers (SVMs, neural networks) on ring-oscillator-network frequencies achieve ~97% accuracy with low false-positive rates when golden reference chips exist [23]; **golden-chip-free** techniques use unsupervised clustering on backscattering side-channel measurements to separate infected from clean populations without any reference [24]. These methods assume Trojans are *rare* in the population — reasonable for targeted attacks, fragile under mass infection.

**Imaging and physical assurance.** Destructive delayering with SEM and emerging X-ray ptychography enable gate-level reconstruction — *destroying the chip*, so only sampling-based assurance is possible. Optical probing countermeasures (nanopyramids [25]) and anti-probing frameworks [20] address physical key extraction from locked chips.

**Secure split-test.** The secure split-test paradigm [8][26] performs structural test without exposing full scan-chain state to an untrusted test facility, closing the test-time channel that undermines Trojan detection and locking-key secrecy.

The verification landscape is thus a **ladder of assurance**: cheap, non-destructive side-channel screening for the many; destructive imaging for sampled high-value parts; statistical methods where golden references are unavailable.

## 5. Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 Quantitative Landscape (from the Primary Literature)

We summarize representative results from the cited primary literature on standard benchmarks (ISCAS-85/89, ITC-99):

| Countermeasure / Attack | Reported result | Source |
|-------------------------|----------------|--------|
| SAT attack vs. pre-2015 locking | Key recovery in minutes; tens of DIPs on largest benchmarks | [5] |
| SARLock / Anti-SAT SAT-resiliency | *2^\|K\|* DIPs required in the worst case (exponential) | [11][12] |
| AppSAT vs. 71 defended benchmarks | 68 deobfuscated with error ≈ *1/2^n* | [6] |
| RO-network + SVM Trojan detection | 97.6% accuracy, 7.1% FPR (with golden chips) | [23] |
| Backscattering golden-chip-free clustering | Separation of infected/clean populations without reference | [24] |

These are *existence proofs*, not guarantees: each result assumes specific benchmarks, Trojan sizes, and noise conditions.

### 5.2 Proof Sketch: Exponential DIP Lower Bound for Point-Function Locking

We sketch the core security argument for SARLock-style point functions [11], which underwrites the "SAT-resilient" claim.

> **Theorem (DIP lower bound, informally [11]):** For a point-function locking block *P(X, K) = 1* iff *X = K*, XORed into a primary output, any oracle-guided DIP-based attack requires *Ω(2^|K|)* iterations in the worst case.

*Proof sketch.* Each DIP *(X_d, Y_d)* eliminates keys disagreeing with the oracle on *X_d*. For a point function, the locked output differs from the correct output on *at most one* input per wrong key (the pattern equal to the key itself) — so one DIP rules out at most one wrong key, and *2^|K| − 1* wrong keys demand *Ω(2^|K|)* DIPs. ∎

The exponential bound holds *only* for exact key recovery under the DIP model. AppSAT [6] escapes it by changing the winning condition (approximate correctness); the bypass attack [16] by changing the game (patch corrupted patterns rather than find the key). **Security proofs are relative to an attacker model, and the most damaging attacks redefine the model**.

### 5.3 Statistical Model of Side-Channel Detection

Consider a defender measuring a scalar side-channel observable *S* (transient supply current over a test vector): *S = S_0 + Δ_T · 𝟙{Trojan} + ε*, with nominal signature *S_0*, Trojan shift *Δ_T*, and *ε ~ N(0, σ²)* aggregating process variation and measurement noise. With *m* golden chips estimating *Ŝ_0*, the defender tests *|S − Ŝ_0| > τ*.

> **Proposition (detection trade-off):** For false-positive rate *α*, thresholding at *τ = z_{1−α/2}·σ√(1 + 1/m)* yields true-positive rate *Φ(Δ_T/(σ√(1+1/m)) − z_{1−α/2})*, with *Φ* the standard normal CDF.

Two consequences follow. *First*, detection power is governed by the **signal-to-noise ratio** *Δ_T/σ*: small Trojans or large process variation drive the true-positive rate toward *α* — toward uselessness. This is the formal "Trojan smaller than process variation" impossibility intuition. *Second*, the golden-chip count *m* enters only through *√(1 + 1/m)*: beyond a modest *m*, more golden chips barely help — intrinsic *σ* dominates. Golden-chip-free methods [24] replace *Ŝ_0* estimation with a two-population hypothesis test, trading the golden-chip assumption for a *rarity* assumption on infection prevalence.

### 5.4 Composition Argument: Layered Defense

No single countermeasure in Sections 4.2–4.4 is sufficient alone: split manufacturing's guarantees are heuristic and economically frictionful; logic locking's proofs are model-relative and physically bypassable; post-silicon detection is noise-limited and reference-dependent. The thesis's central analytic contribution is the **composition claim**: these weaknesses are *complementary*.

- Logic locking denies the foundry *functional understanding* for targeted trigger design — security-critical nodes like key registers are unidentifiable in an obfuscated netlist [4].
- Split manufacturing denies *physical completeness* — a trigger wired through unknown BEOL connectivity may simply not function [2][15].
- Post-silicon attestation catches what slips through, with detection probability amplified because the first two layers force the attacker into *larger, noisier, less reliable* Trojans — increasing *Δ_T* in Section 5.3's Proposition.

If layer *i* defeats or detects insertion with probability *1 − ε_i* (idealizing independence), the composed miss probability is *∏_i ε_i*. Independence is an idealization — attackers adapt — but the *cost* compounds: each layer forces larger Trojans, more queries, or stronger physical capabilities, pushing attacks toward the statistically-easy regime.

## 6. Limitations

Intellectual honesty requires confronting where the program above falls short.

1. **The oracle problem.** SAT-attack analyses assume the attacker holds an activated chip as an oracle [5] — and gray markets make withholding it futile. Conversely, *defensive* oracle use assumes Trojan-free references the threat model denies. The oracle is simultaneously the attacker's greatest asset and the defender's missing one.

2. **Physical attacks bypass logical guarantees.** Probing extracts locking keys directly from silicon [20]; "the key is left under the mat" [19] shows key-delivery infrastructure (test modes, scan chains, key storage) undermining locking in practice. SAT-attack-secure schemes can fall in an afternoon to a probing station; countermeasures (shielding, nanopyramids [25], probe detectors) add cost and are themselves bypassable.

3. **Economic deployability of split manufacturing.** The foundry business model resists FEOL-only wafer shipments [14]; the trusted BEOL facility must itself be accredited and economically viable. Split-Chip/2.5D variants mitigate this but introduce interposer and assembly trust questions.

4. **The dopant-level and analog frontier.** Trojans modifying dopant polarity or exploiting parametric effects can be layout-identical to clean circuits [1], defeating imaging and most side-channel methods above the noise floor. Detection here remains open research.

5. **Metrics vs. adversaries.** Corruptibility, DIP counts, and ROC curves are *proxy metrics*: high Hamming distance does not imply structural-attack resistance, and 97% benchmark accuracy [10][23] may not survive Trojans designed with knowledge of the detector — adaptive adversaries are underrepresented in evaluations.

6. **Scope exclusions.** EDA-tool compromise (malicious synthesis inserting Trojans before the designer's netlist exists), analog/RF Trojans in mixed-signal SoCs, and packaging-level attacks such as counterfeit remarking (see [9]) are out of scope.

## 7. Conclusion

The globalization of semiconductor manufacturing is irreversible: leading-edge fabrication economics keep design and manufacturing separated by oceans and trust boundaries. Within this reality, hardware Trojans are a *foundational* threat.

This thesis has argued three things. *First*, the threat is precisely characterizable: the Tehranipoor–Koushanfar taxonomy [1] and Trust-Hub benchmarks [10] provide a shared language and measurement instruments. *Second*, pre-silicon countermeasures — split manufacturing [2] and logic locking [3][4] — deny an untrusted foundry the understanding and physical completeness needed for reliable Trojan insertion, but their guarantees are respectively heuristic and model-relative, as the SAT-attack arms race [5][6][11][12][16][17] demonstrates. *Third*, post-silicon verification [7][8][22][23][24] is a statistical backstop governed by signal-to-noise fundamentals — *amplified* when composed with pre-silicon layers that force attackers into larger, noisier Trojans.

The roadmap: (a) locking with *physical* security, co-designed with anti-probing and secure key-delivery [19][20][25]; (b) split-manufacturing flows compatible with foundry economics via 2.5D/3D integration [14]; (c) golden-chip-free detection with *adversarial* evaluation; (d) formal frameworks composing heuristic, cryptographic, and statistical guarantees into end-to-end assurance. The honest posture is layered defense with quantified — not absolute — assurance: every layer raises the attacker's cost, and the composition is the security argument.
