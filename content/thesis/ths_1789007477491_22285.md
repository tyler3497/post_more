# The Sponge Construction: Indifferentiability Bounds, the Keccak-f Permutation, and the SHA-3 Standardization Story

## Abstract

The sponge construction, introduced by Bertoni, Daemen, Peeters, and Van Assche in 2007 [1], replaced the iterated compression-function paradigm with a fixed permutation acting on a finite state split into a public *rate* r and a hidden *capacity* c. Its decisive contribution was a proof that the construction is indifferentiable from a random oracle when built on a random permutation [2], with distinguishing advantage bounded by N(N+1)/2^{c+1} — the celebrated 2^{c/2} bound that makes capacity the single dominating security parameter. This article reconstructs that theory end to end: the absorbing and squeezing mechanics, the simulator-based indifferentiability argument and its tightness, the full anatomy of the 1600-bit Keccak-f permutation with its 24 rounds of θ, ρ, π, χ, and ι [3], and the six FIPS 202 functions built on top of it [4]. It also recounts the SHA-3 standardization story — from NIST's 2007 competition, through Keccak's 2012 selection, to the 2015 standard — and closes with the bound's limitations and its recent extension to the quantum setting.

## 1. Introduction

On November 2, 2007, NIST announced a public competition to develop a new cryptographic hash standard, motivated by the collision attacks that had shaken confidence in MD5 and cast theoretical shadows over SHA-1 [5]. Sixty-four submissions arrived; the field was narrowed to 51, then 14, then five finalists — BLAKE, Grøstl, JH, Keccak, and Skein. On October 2, 2012, NIST announced **Keccak**, designed by Guido Bertoni, Joan Daemen, Michaël Peeters, and Gilles Van Assche, as the winner [6]. Draft FIPS 202 followed on May 28, 2014, a public workshop at UCSB on August 22, 2014, and the final standard on August 5, 2015 [7], published as FIPS PUB 202, *SHA-3 Standard: Permutation-Based Hash and Extendable-Output Functions* [4].

What made this outcome remarkable was not only the algorithm but the *theory* that traveled with it. Since 2007 the Keccak team had published the sponge construction [1], a full indifferentiability proof [2], a consolidated 93-page treatment of cryptographic sponge functions [8], and a complete reference specification with cryptanalytic rationale [3]. By the time FIPS 202 was finalized, SHA-3 was arguably the best-theorized hash standard ever deployed: its security claims rested not on cryptanalytic fashion but on a reduction to an ideal model with a quantitative, essentially tight bound.

This article has three aims. First, to give a precise, self-contained account of the sponge construction and its indifferentiability bound. Second, to dissect the 1600-bit Keccak-f permutation — the 24-round primitive at the heart of SHA-3 — down to its five step mappings and round-constant schedule. Third, to tell the standardization story accurately, including the 2013 controversy over NIST's proposed tweaks and why the final design preserved the designers' parameters.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 From Merkle–Damgård to permutations

Classical hash functions iterate a *compression function*: a message is split into blocks, each block folded into a chaining value, and the final chaining value (possibly transformed) is the digest. The Merkle–Damgård strengthening guarantees that collision resistance of the compression function lifts to the hash function. But the construction leaks structure: length-extension attacks, the Joux multicollision phenomenon, and herding attacks all exploit the iterated, fixed-output nature of the design. As the authors of [1] argued, an iterated hash function can *never* behave like a random oracle, because state collisions are unavoidable in a finite chaining value and produce externally visible behavior — for example, colliding prefixes extend to colliding full messages — that a random oracle would never exhibit.

The sponge sidesteps this by changing the primitive. Instead of a compression function, it uses a fixed-length **permutation** (or transformation) f on b bits. Instead of a fixed output, it produces an *arbitrary-length* output by "squeezing." The b-bit state is split into an outer part of r bits — the *rate*, which interacts with input and output — and an inner part of c bits — the *capacity*, which never directly touches the adversary. Security concentrates in the capacity: an adversary that cannot observe or control the inner c bits cannot distinguish the construction from a random oracle without finding an *inner collision*, which costs roughly 2^{c/2} work [2].

### 2.2 The random-oracle ideal and indifferentiability

A random oracle is the ideal hash function: it maps every input to an independently uniform infinite bitstring, consistently. Real constructions are judged by how closely they approximate it. *Indistinguishability* — no adversary querying only the construction can tell it from a random oracle — is too weak for hash functions, because the underlying primitive f is public and the adversary queries it directly. *Indifferentiability*, introduced by Maurer, Renner, and Holenstein and applied to hash constructions by Coron et al., is the right notion: the pair (construction, primitive) must be indistinguishable from the pair (random oracle, simulator), where the simulator answers primitive queries using only oracle access to the random oracle, keeping its answers consistent with what the construction would have produced.

Bertoni et al. applied this framework to the sponge in [2] — to their knowledge, the first indifferentiability result for a construction calling a random **permutation** rather than an ideal compression function or block cipher, and the first for a construction with **unbounded output length**. The proof constructs an explicit simulator that tracks the "paths" an adversary's permutation queries trace through the sponge graph and patches inconsistencies using the random oracle. The quantitative result:

> **Theorem:** (Indifferentiability of the sponge [2]) A sponge construction with capacity c, calling a random transformation, is indifferentiable from a random oracle with advantage at most N(N+1)/2^{c+1}, where N is the number of calls to the primitive. For a random permutation the bound is even smaller. Expected distinguishing work is therefore on the order of 2^{c/2}.

The bound is **tight**: after roughly 2^{c/2} evaluations an adversary finds an inner collision — two distinct paths reaching the same c-bit inner state — and one further absorption block converts it into a full state collision, yielding a distinguishing event [8].

## 3. Methodology

### 3.1 The padded sponge, formally

Following the indifferentiability paper's formulation [2], fix positive integers r (rate) and c (capacity) with b = r + c, and a transformation F on {0,1}^b. Write a state s as (s_a, s_c): the outer r bits and the inner c bits. The padded sponge S[F] maps a sequence of r-bit blocks p = p_0 … p_{k−1} (k ≥ 1, last block nonzero) to an output of any requested length n:

```
S[F](p, n):
    s = (0^r, 0^c)                     # absorbing
    for i in 0..k-1:
        s = F((s_a XOR p_i), s_c)
    z = empty                           # squeezing
    for i in 0..ceil(n/r)-1:
        z = z || s_a
        s = F(s)
    return first n bits of z
```

Arbitrary messages are first mapped to valid block sequences by an injective padding rule. Keccak and FIPS 202 use **multirate padding**, pad10\*1: append a single 1 bit, then the minimum number of 0 bits, then a single 1 bit, so the total length is a multiple of r [3][4]. It appends between 2 and r+1 bits, is injective, and never leaves a valid input ending in the all-zero block — a technical condition the proofs need.

### 3.2 Proof strategy

The indifferentiability proof in [2] proceeds by constructing a simulator P that answers the distinguisher's queries to F (and F^{−1}, in the permutation case) while consulting the random oracle RO. The simulator maintains a graph of all queried input/output pairs of F. When the distinguisher's queries begin to form a *path* — a chain of states consistent with absorbing some message prefix — the simulator queries RO on that prefix and forces the corresponding outer state bits to match, so that the ideal world (RO, P[RO]) and the real world (S[F], F) remain consistent. Inconsistency can only arise when two distinct paths collide on the inner c bits — an **inner collision** — whose probability over N queries is bounded by roughly N²/2^{c+1}. The consolidated treatment in [8] extends this analysis to the duplex construction, keyed modes, and multirate padding, and derives the *flat sponge claim*: the strength of a random sponge against generic attacks is essentially flat at 2^{c/2}, independent of the application.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Absorbing and squeezing: the two phases

The sponge's elegance is operational. **Absorbing** injects data: each r-bit block is XORed into the outer state, and the permutation mixes it into the full b bits, including the hidden capacity. **Squeezing** extracts data: the outer r bits are read off, and the permutation is re-applied to refresh them. One permutation and one loop serve hashing, XOFs, stream encryption, and MACs — a versatility surveyed in [8].

The capacity is the security dial and the rate is the performance dial: SHA-3's six parameter sets are points on this tradeoff curve.

### 4.2 The anatomy of the Keccak-f permutation

SHA-3 uses the largest Keccak-f width, b = 1600 (ℓ = 6, w = 64) [3]. The 1600-bit state is a three-dimensional bit array indexed by coordinates (x, y, z) with x, y ∈ ℤ_5 and z ∈ ℤ_64 — equivalently, a 5×5 array of 64-bit *lanes*. The round count is n_r = 12 + 2ℓ = **24**, each round applying five step mappings in a fixed order [4]:

$$R = \iota \circ \chi \circ \pi \circ \rho \circ \theta$$

![Keccak-f round function (b = 1600): the five step mappings](public/thesis/ths_1789007477491_22285-1.webp)

| Step | Type | Action |
|------|------|--------|
| **θ (theta)** | linear, diffusion | XOR each lane with parities of neighboring columns |
| **ρ (rho)** | linear, bitwise | rotate each lane by a position-dependent offset |
| **π (pi)** | linear, transposition | permute the 25 lane positions within the 5×5 grid |
| **χ (chi)** | **nonlinear** | each lane XORed with the AND of its negated neighbors |
| **ι (iota)** | symmetry-breaking | XOR a round constant into the lane at position (0,0) |

Concretely [3]:

- **θ**: for each column x, C(x) is the XOR of its five lanes; D(x) = C(x−1) ⊕ ROT(C(x+1), 1); every lane in column x is then XORed with D(x) — the diffusion engine of the round.
- **ρ**: each lane is rotated by a triangular offset t(t+1)(t+2)/2 set by its position on a fixed cycle; the lane at (0,0) is unrotated, breaking inter-lane alignment.
- **π**: lane positions are permuted within the 5×5 grid, sending (x′, y′) to (y′, 2x′ + 3y′) and dispersing lanes across the grid.
- **χ**: each lane is updated as lane ⊕ ((¬next lane) ∧ (lane after next)), applied row-wise. This is the *only* nonlinear step (algebraic degree 2) — the sole source of strength against differential and linear attacks.
- **ι**: the lane at (0,0) is XORed with the round constant RC for round i_r. The round constants defeat slide and symmetry attacks: RC is nonzero only at bit positions 2^j − 1 of lane (0,0), taking value rc[j + 7·i_r] for 0 ≤ j ≤ ℓ, from the binary LFSR for x^8 + x^6 + x^5 + x^4 + 1:

```python
def keccak_rc(t: int) -> int:
    # LFSR over GF(2), polynomial x^8 + x^6 + x^5 + x^4 + 1
    R = [1,0,0,0,0,0,0,0]
    for _ in range(t % 255):
        R = [0] + R
        R[0] ^= R[8]; R[4] ^= R[8]; R[5] ^= R[8]; R[6] ^= R[8]
        R = R[:8]
    return R[0]
```

The design rationale [3] is a *wide-trail strategy* adapted to permutations: θ gives rapid diffusion, χ supplies the nonlinearity with provable bounds on differential trail weights, and ι's low-weight asymmetric constants keep rounds distinct. The result has a large security margin: public cryptanalysis reaches only a fraction of the 24 rounds, so generic sponge attacks — not structural ones — remain the binding constraint.

### 4.3 SHA-3 and SHAKE: six functions, one permutation

FIPS 202 defines all six SHA-3 functions as modes of the single permutation **KECCAK-p** (b = 1600, n_r = 24) under pad10\*1 [4]:

| Function | Rate r | Capacity c | Output | Suffix |
|----------|--------|-----------|--------|--------|
| SHA3-224 | 1152 | 448 | 224 bits | `01` |
| SHA3-256 | 1088 | 512 | 256 bits | `01` |
| SHA3-384 | 832 | 576 | 384 bits | `01` |
| SHA3-512 | 576 | 1024 | 512 bits | `01` |
| SHAKE128 | 1344 | 256 | arbitrary | `1111` |
| SHAKE256 | 1088 | 512 | arbitrary | `1111` |

Two points deserve emphasis. First, the hash functions set c = 2n, so the 2^{c/2} bound delivers exactly n bits of generic security — collision 2^{n/2}, preimage 2^n — the classical random-oracle expectations. Second, the **extendable-output functions** (XOFs) are the sponge's native mode: SHAKE128 and SHAKE256 squeeze arbitrarily many output bits under the `1111` domain-separation suffix.

### 4.4 The standardization story

The competition timeline is public record [7]:

- **November 2, 2007** — NIST announces the SHA-3 competition in the Federal Register.
- **2008** — 64 submissions received; Keccak is among them.
- **July 2009** — 14 second-round candidates selected.
- **December 2010** — five finalists: BLAKE, Grøstl, JH, Keccak, Skein.
- **October 2, 2012** — NIST announces **Keccak** as the winner, citing its elegant design, large security margin, excellent hardware efficiency, and flexibility [6].
- **February 2013** — the Keccak team visits NIST to discuss standardization.
- **May 28, 2014** — draft FIPS 202 published for comment.
- **August 22, 2014** — SHA-3 workshop at UC Santa Barbara.
- **August 5, 2015** — FIPS 202 finalized [7].

![SHA-3 standardization timeline, 2007–2015](public/thesis/ths_1789007477491_22285-3.webp)

The process was not without friction. In 2013, NIST circulated standardization plans that the cryptographic community read as contemplating reduced capacities and altered domain separation; the Keccak designers publicly objected, warning of eroded security margins. The final standard kept the designers' parameters — c = 2n for the hash functions — and added only the suffix-based domain separation distinguishing SHA-3 (`01`) from SHAKE (`1111`). The episode is instructive: the bound became a *negotiating instrument* — a quantitative line, 2^{c/2}, the designers refused to cross.

NIST's stated rationale was **design diversity**: SHA-3 supplements rather than replaces SHA-2, resting on "fundamentally different design principles" so a breakthrough against one family is unlikely to break both [4].

## 5. Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 The bound, instantiated

The indifferentiability theorem is not an asymptotic slogan; it yields concrete numbers. For SHA3-256 (c = 512), the distinguishing advantage after N permutation queries is at most N(N+1)/2^{513}, so a distinguisher needs N ≈ 2^{256} queries — a 256-bit security level, matching the digest size. For SHAKE128 (c = 256) the generic bound is 2^{128}, the standard's claimed strength. The following table summarizes the FIPS 202 parameterizations against the generic bounds:

| Function | c | Generic indiff. bound | Collision | Preimage |
|----------|---|----------------------|-----------|----------|
| SHA3-224 | 448 | 2^{224} | 2^{112} | 2^{224} |
| SHA3-256 | 512 | 2^{256} | 2^{128} | 2^{256} |
| SHA3-384 | 576 | 2^{288} | 2^{192} | 2^{384} |
| SHA3-512 | 1024 | 2^{512} | 2^{256} | 2^{512} |
| SHAKE128 | 256 | 2^{128} | 2^{min(128, n/2)} | 2^{min(128, n)} |
| SHAKE256 | 512 | 2^{256} | 2^{min(256, n/2)} | 2^{min(256, n)} |

![Indifferentiability bound: advantage vs. query count](public/thesis/ths_1789007477491_22285-2.webp)

A standard sanity check is the empty-input test vector: SHA3-256 of the empty string is `a7ffc6f8bf1ed76651c14756a061d662f580ff4de43b49fa82d80a4b80f8434a`, reproducible by any correct implementation of the absorb/squeeze loop above with the pad10\*1 rule and suffix `01` — confirming that the specification, the padding, and the domain separation interact exactly as FIPS 202 prescribes [4].

### 5.2 Tightness and refinements

The bound is tight in the strong sense: the generic inner-collision attack — birthday search over the c-bit inner state, then one extra block to promote it to a full state collision — achieves distinguishing advantage with N ≈ 2^{c/2} queries [8]. No generic attack does better, and no generic defense does better either: capacity is both necessary and sufficient.

Recent work has sharpened the picture at the margins. Lefevre showed that when the number of absorbed blocks is restricted to ℓ below ⌈c/(2(b−c))⌉ + 1, the indifferentiability bound improves to roughly min(2^{b/2}, 2^{c/2}, 2^{b − ℓ(b−c)}), allowing higher rates — or smaller permutations — for fixed-length-input applications such as lightweight hashing [9]. And the classical result has now been lifted to the quantum setting: the sponge is indifferentiable from a random oracle against quantum adversaries making superposition queries to the permutation, with a comparable capacity-dominated bound [10]. The 2^{c/2} figure thus survives the transition from classical to quantum adversaries up to the usual quadratic adjustments — a robustness few hash constructions can claim.

## 6. Limitations and Open Problems

**The ideal-permutation gap.** Indifferentiability assumes f is a *random* permutation. The 1600-bit Keccak-f instance is a concrete, highly structured object, and the proof says nothing about structural distinguishers. Third-party cryptanalysis — including zero-sum distinguishers reaching deep into the round function — is summarized in the Keccak reference itself [3]; it does not threaten the full 24 rounds, but the margin is a cryptanalytic judgment, not a theorem. The bound is a ceiling on generic attacks, not a floor under all attacks.

**Parameter sensitivity.** The entire security story hinges on c. Designs that shrink capacity for throughput (as in several lightweight sponges) inherit proportionally weaker bounds, and the XOF setting requires care: squeezing n ≫ c bits does not create n bits of security. Lefevre's refinement [9] shows the classical bound is not the last word when inputs are short, but it also shows how quickly the analysis becomes parameter-fragile outside the standard regime.

**What indifferentiability does not cover.** The proof addresses the construction in the ideal model. It says nothing about side-channel leakage of real implementations, about misuse (e.g., using SHAKE output lengths inconsistently across protocols), or about the multi-user and related-key settings where the permutation is shared. The 2013 standardization debate is a reminder that the *instantiation* — suffixes, capacities, output lengths — is where real-world security is won or lost, and that part is engineering judgment, not mathematics.

**Open problems.** Tighter quantum indifferentiability bounds with concrete (not just asymptotic) constants remain open [10]. The duplex construction's authenticated-encryption modes inherit sponge bounds, but their concrete security under nonce misuse is still being mapped. And the perennial question — whether 24 rounds is overkill or prudence — will only be settled by the continued failure of cryptanalysts, which is evidence, not proof.

## 7. Conclusion

The sponge construction is the rare cryptographic idea that arrived with its theory complete: a simple mechanism (absorb, squeeze, repeat), a clean security parameter (capacity), a tight quantitative bound (2^{c/2}) proved in the strongest available framework (indifferentiability from a random oracle), and a concrete instantiation (the 1600-bit Keccak-f permutation) whose round function has withstood over a decade of cryptanalysis [1][2][3][4]. The SHA-3 standardization story — competition, controversy, and consensus between 2007 and 2015 — shows what happens when that theory is taken seriously as an engineering constraint: the bound became the line the designers refused to cross, and the standard that emerged preserves it.

For the practitioner, the lesson is compact: when you choose SHA3-256, you are buying 256 bits of generic security because c = 512, and you can verify that claim against a proof rather than a track record. For the theorist, the open frontier is extending that certainty — to quantum adversaries, to restricted-input regimes, to the duplex modes now carrying real authenticated-encryption traffic. The sponge absorbed a decade of analysis and squeezed out a standard; the next decade will test how far its bounds stretch.
