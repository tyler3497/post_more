# Decentralized Information Flow Control in Operating Systems: Asbestos, HiStar, and Flume — Label-Based Confinement with Practical Declassification

## Abstract

Information flow control (IFC) promises end-to-end confidentiality and integrity guarantees that access control cannot provide, yet classical multilevel security proved too rigid for mutually distrustful applications. This thesis examines the decade in which decentralized information flow control (DIFC) moved from programming-language theory into operating-system kernels: the decentralized label model of Myers and Liskov [1][2], and its three landmark embodiments — **Asbestos** (SOSP 2005) [4], **HiStar** (OSDI 2006) [5], and **Flume** (SOSP 2007) [6]. We formalize the label lattice and relabeling discipline each system adopts, compare their confinement mechanisms — Asbestos's event processes, HiStar's gates and partial declassification, Flume's user-level reference monitor — and analyze the shared pathology of *label creep*, in which monotonically accumulating taint progressively strangles legitimate communication. Drawing on published measurements — Asbestos isolating 145,000 users at roughly 1.5 memory pages per user, Flume securing a 91,000-line wiki with modest changes — we argue that practical DIFC is less a kernel mechanism than an *application-structuring discipline*: declassification must be first-class, explicit, and auditable, or the lattice becomes a prison rather than a policy.

---

## 1. Introduction

Conventional operating-system security is built on *access control*: a reference monitor decides, at the moment of access, whether subject *S* may perform operation *O* on object *X*. Once access is granted, the system washes its hands of the data. A program that legitimately reads a secret file is thereafter free — through error or malice — to transmit that secret anywhere, and no subsequent check can detect the violation. As Sabelfeld and Myers observe, access control constrains the *release* of information but not its *propagation* [3]. Buffer overruns, SQL injection, and confused-deputy flaws have, for decades, converted legitimate read privileges into exfiltration channels.

Information flow control attacks the problem at its root. Rather than mediating isolated accesses, IFC attaches *labels* to data and tracks how labels propagate through computation, enforcing the policy that secret inputs must not influence public outputs. The classical formulation — Denning's lattice model and Goguen and Meseguer's *noninterference* — was built for military multilevel security, where a single central authority classifies every object. That centralization made classical IFC unusable for the applications needing it most: web servers, wikis, and multi-tenant services where *mutually distrustful principals* each own data and each demand a say in its dissemination.

The breakthrough was Myers and Liskov's **decentralized label model** (DLM) [1][2]: every principal expresses its own policy, policies compose without a trusted arbiter, and declassification is safe and decentralized. Between 2005 and 2007, three systems carried the DLM into the OS itself — **Asbestos** [4], **HiStar** [5], and **Flume** [6].

This thesis reconstructs the theory these systems share, dissects where they diverge, and confronts the phenomenon haunting all three: **label creep** — floating, monotonically accumulating labels contaminating long-running processes until they can communicate with nothing. We show label creep is not an implementation bug but a structural consequence of conservative taint tracking, and that each system's declassification architecture is best understood as its answer to this single problem. The enduring lesson is architectural: DIFC succeeds exactly to the extent that declassification is made *explicit, least-privilege, and auditable*.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 From Access Control to Flow Control

The multilevel-security tradition modeled secrecy as a lattice of clearances and enforced "no read up, no write down." Denning generalized this to arbitrary lattices: secure information flow is flow respecting the lattice order — data labeled *ℓ₁* may flow to a container labeled *ℓ₂* only if *ℓ₁ ⊑ ℓ₂*. Goguen and Meseguer gave the canonical semantic formulation, *noninterference*: a system is secure if low-security outputs are invariant under variation of high-security inputs. These ideas thrived in theory and failed almost everywhere else, because a single global lattice cannot express "Alice's medical records are readable by her doctors, Bob's grades by his professors, and neither administrator may reclassify the other's data."

### 2.2 The Decentralized Label Model

Myers and Liskov's DLM [1] replaces the central classifier with *principals* — users, roles, services — each owning data and stating policy over it. A label is a set of policies `{owner: reader₁, reader₂, …}`: "owner permits the listed readers to observe this data." The label `{alice: bob; carol: dave}` protects jointly owned data: a reader must be authorized by *every* owner. Labels form a lattice under *L₁ ⊑ L₂* — every policy in *L₂* is at least as restrictive as in *L₁* — so data flows only *upward* toward more restrictive labels. Dually, integrity policies constrain who may *influence* data [2].

The decisive innovation is **decentralized declassification**: data may be relabeled to a *less* restrictive label, but only by a principal with authority over the relaxed policy. Myers and Liskov proved this relabeling rule both *sound* (permits only safe relabelings) and *complete* (permits *all* safe relabelings) [2] — elevating declassification from dangerous hack to principled operation. Their Jif/JFlow language [10] demonstrated static enforcement, but the DLM's destiny lay in the kernel, where untrusted, possibly binary-only code must be confined *dynamically*.

### 2.3 Why the Operating System

Language-based IFC cannot confine a legacy C web server, a Python wiki, or a compromised binary whose source was never annotated. The OS sees *every* IPC, file access, and network write; labels enforced at that layer confine arbitrary code without its cooperation. The cost is coarseness — the kernel tracks flows between processes and sockets, not variables — and dynamic checking: policies are enforced by *floating labels* that rise as taint accumulates.

## 3. Methodology

We proceed by **comparative mechanism analysis** against a uniform rubric:

1. **Label model.** What is a label, formally? What lattice does it inhabit?
2. **Confinement granularity.** At what unit is taint tracked — event process, kernel object, OS process?
3. **Declassification architecture.** Who may downgrade, under what authority, through what mechanism?
4. **Trusted computing base.** How much code must be correct for the guarantees to hold?

Formal claims rest on the published theorems — relabeling soundness/completeness [2] and HiStar's flow theorem [5]; empirical claims on the systems' own evaluations [4][6]. We reconstruct *label creep* from first principles, since no single paper names it, and show each architecture implicitly responds to it.

```python
# The universal DIFC flow check, shared by all three systems in spirit.
def may_flow(src_label, dst_label, lattice_le):
    """Data may flow from src to dst iff src_label ⊑ dst_label."""
    return lattice_le(src_label, dst_label)

def relabel_is_safe(old, new, authority, lattice_le):
    """Declassification (new ⋢ old) is permitted only if `authority`
    acts for every owner whose policy is relaxed — the DLM rule [2]."""
    if lattice_le(old, new):            # ordinary upward flow: safe
        return True
    return all(authority.acts_for(o) for o in relaxed_owners(old, new))
```

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Decentralized Label Model: Principals, Policies, and the Relabeling Rule

The DLM's elegance lies in making *ownership* the unit of policy. Compound labels conjoin policies from multiple owners; the effective reader set is the intersection of what each owner permits. "Acts for" — a speaks-for-style delegation relation — gives a clean account of authority without a central administrator: data labeled *L₁* may be downgraded to *L₂* even when *L₂ ⋢ L₁*, provided the relabeling principal acts for every owner whose policy is relaxed.

```haskell
-- DLM lattice sketch: labels map owners to authorized reader-sets.
-- Join = least upper bound; readers of the join are those allowed by BOTH.
type Principal = String
type Label = [(Principal, [Principal])]

lub :: Label -> Label -> Label
lub l1 l2 = mergeWith intersectReaders l1 l2

relabelOk :: Label -> Label -> [Principal] -> Bool
relabelOk old new authority =
    leq old new ||                                     -- safe upward flow
    all (`elem` authority) (relaxedOwners old new)      -- declassification [2]
```

Two properties make the DLM the right OS foundation. First, policies are *decentralized*: Alice's policy mentions only Alice. Second, declassification is *disciplined*: a rule with a precise authority requirement, not a hole in the type system. Every system below is an engineering answer to one question: **how do you check `relabelOk` at OS granularity, at runtime, against adversarial code?**

### 4.2 Asbestos: Labels, Event Processes, and the Declassifier Pattern

Asbestos [4] was the first clean-slate OS to enforce DIFC in the kernel at scale. Labels are sets of *(tag, level)* pairs — each tag naming an information category (e.g., "user *U*'s data"), levels *{1, 2, 3, ⋆}*. Higher numbers mean greater contamination; **⋆ ("star")** denotes *privilege* over the tag: the right to communicate at any level and to *decontaminate*. Every process carries a **send label** (bounding what it may emit) and a **receive label** (bounding what it may absorb); the kernel permits IPC from *P* to *Q* only when *P*'s send label flows to *Q*'s receive label. Contamination is *automatic and floating*: reading data tagged *t* at level *ℓ* raises the reader's send label. A compromised worker touching user *U*'s data is thereby tainted by *U*'s tag and can no longer write to the network — unless a ⋆-privileged party intervenes.

Asbestos's signature abstraction is the **event process**: a lightweight, labeled execution context *within* a single address space, each with its own send/receive labels. Where naive DIFC would fork a process per user, event processes let one server act for thousands of principals while the kernel guarantees no cross-user leakage. The Asbestos web server (labeler, demultiplexer, per-user workers, trusted **declassifier**) isolated **145,000 users at ~1.4–1.5 memory pages per user**, with throughput competitive with Apache [4].

| Aspect | Asbestos mechanism |
|---|---|
| Label | Set of *(tag, level)*, levels 1–3, ⋆ = declassification privilege |
| Granularity | Process + intra-process *event process* |
| Taint propagation | Automatic floating on read; send/receive check on IPC |
| Declassification | ⋆ privilege; centralized *declassifier* process |
| TCB | Whole (clean-slate) kernel |
| Demonstrator | Web server; 145k users, ~1.5 pages/user [4] |

### 4.3 HiStar: Explicit Information Flow and Partial Declassification

**HiStar** [5] asks what an OS looks like when *every* information flow is explicit. The kernel exposes only **six object types** — threads, address spaces, segments, containers, devices, gates — each with a label mapping *categories* to levels *{0, 1, 2, 3, ⋆}*, where *0* is untainted and **⋆ denotes ownership**. The rule is total: a thread *T* labeled *Lₜ* may observe object *O* labeled *Lₒ* only by raising its own label, *Lₜ := Lₜ ⊔ Lₒ* — there is no read without contamination. Onward flow is permitted only upward, unless a thread *owning* the category participates. This yields HiStar's celebrated theorem, proved over every kernel-mediated operation:

> **Theorem:** *The contents of object A can only affect object B if, for every category c in which A is more tainted than B, a thread owning c takes part in the process.* [5]

No processes, files, or ACLs appear in the statement — a pure claim about *information*, the closest an OS has come to whole-API noninterference.

HiStar's deepest contribution is **partial declassification**. Full ⋆ ownership is dangerously powerful, so *tainted gates* record the caller's taint at invocation and permit the gate to declassify *only taint its own caller introduced* — downgrading data it created, not data it merely observed. A scanner may release its *verdicts* without gaining the right to release the *files it scanned*: least privilege applied to declassification itself, directly addressing the single-choke-point fragility of the Asbestos pattern.

```rust
// HiStar-style gate check: declassify only taint the caller introduced.
fn gate_call(caller: &Thread, gate: &Gate, cat: Category) -> Result<(), FlowError> {
    let own_taint = caller.label.taint_introduced_by(caller.id, cat);
    if gate.declassify_mask.contains(cat) && own_taint.covers(gate.requested_downgrade) {
        Ok(())  // partial declassification: shed own taint only
    } else if caller.owns(cat) {
        Ok(())  // full ⋆ ownership: legacy privilege, audited, rare
    } else {
        Err(FlowError::DeclassificationDenied)
    }
}
```

HiStar further showed that UIDs, file permissions, even POSIX can be rebuilt *above* the label kernel as untrusted libraries, each UID mapped to read/write ownership privileges granted by an authentication service [5]. DIFC need not coexist with access control — it can *subsume* it.

### 4.4 Flume: DIFC as a User-Level Reference Monitor

Asbestos and HiStar are clean-slate kernels — pure but undeployable on existing infrastructure. **Flume** [6] bets the opposite way: DIFC at *process granularity* via a **user-level reference monitor** on stock Linux/OpenBSD, integrated with pipes, sockets, and file descriptors. A Flume label is *(S, I, O)* — secrecy tags, integrity tags, owned tags — and per-tag **capabilities** govern mutation: *t⁺* adds a tag (voluntary taint), *t⁻* removes one (declassify/endorse), *t→* delegates capabilities. A process may rewrite its own label within its capabilities; the monitor mediates every IPC against the flow rule — write to an endpoint only if the endpoint's secrecy label dominates the writer's and its integrity label is dominated by the writer's. Labeled file descriptors (*endpoints*) expose policy exactly where Unix programmers already reason about communication.

Flume's validation: porting **MoinMoin** (91,000 lines of Python) with modest changes, so that a compromised renderer — tainted with viewers' tags — *cannot* exfiltrate one user's private pages to another, because the reference monitor, not the application, enforces the policy [6]. The price is a large TCB (the whole Linux kernel) plus user-space mediation overhead and residual covert channels [6].

### 4.5 Label Creep: The Pathology of Floating Taint

All three systems share a structural weakness no paper of the era names but every deployment meets: **label creep**. Floating labels are *monotone* — taint accrues automatically on observation and sheds only via privileged declassification. A long-running process reading many principals' data accumulates the union of their tags; its label ratchets upward until it can communicate with almost nothing. Formally, with *L(t)* a thread's label over time, *L(t₁) ⊑ L(t₂)* for *t₁ < t₂*, strict on each newly tainted observation — a one-way ratchet turning the process into a *taint sink*: maximally informed, minimally useful.

Each architecture is, in retrospect, its answer to label creep:

1. **Asbestos — isolate per principal.** Event processes give every user a *fresh, minimally labeled* context; creep is bounded by a session's lifetime, not the server's. Only the ⋆-holding declassifier is long-lived and contaminated.
2. **HiStar — declassify partially and locally.** Tainted gates shed *exactly the taint a service introduced*, so transforming data need not accumulate everything ever touched. Full ownership stays rare and audited.
3. **Flume — make label changes explicit and capability-gated.** *t⁻* capabilities let trusted components endorse downward along precisely scoped paths; process granularity contains creep to the compromised component.

The general lesson is stark: **a DIFC system without a designed declassification story slowly asphyxiates.** Floating taint is the safe default, but safety without release is denial of service against oneself. Declassification must be a *first-class architectural element* — budgeted, minimized (HiStar's partiality), audited (Asbestos's choke point), capability-scoped (Flume's *t⁻*) — never an afterthought.

## 5. Empirical Evaluation and Formal Security Theorems

### 5.1 What Was Measured

| System | Primary claim | Key measurement | Cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| Asbestos [4] | Scalable per-user isolation in a real server | 145,000 users; ~1.5 pages/user; Apache-competitive | Clean-slate kernel; prototype label overhead |
| HiStar [5] | Theorem-backed flow control over the whole kernel API | Flow theorem over all six object types; POSIX as untrusted libraries | Clean-slate kernel; per-operation label checks |
| Flume [6] | DIFC deployable on stock OSes | MoinMoin (91 kLOC) secured, modest changes; Linux/OpenBSD | Large TCB; user-space overhead; covert channels |

Asbestos's result is the most striking *systems* achievement: per-user isolation at a page and a half per user [4]. Flume's is the most striking *software-engineering* result: DIFC retrofitted onto an unmodified stack, protecting a large legacy codebase against its own bugs [6].

### 5.2 What Was Proved

> **Theorem:** *The decentralized relabeling rule is sound and complete: it permits only safe relabelings, and it permits all safe relabelings.* [2]

> **Theorem:** *In HiStar, the contents of object A can only affect object B if, for every category c in which A is more tainted than B, a thread owning c takes part in the process.* [5]

The first legitimizes declassification as a *rule* rather than a loophole; the second lifts that legitimacy to *every operation a real kernel performs*. Together they define the field's gold standard: state the flow property as a theorem over the enforcement mechanism's total behavior.

The shared invariant is precise enough to model-check:

```tla
---- MODULE DifcFlow ----
EXTENDS Naturals, FiniteSets
CONSTANTS Tag
VARIABLES label               \* label[p][t] : process -> tag -> level

FlowOK(src, dst) ==
    \A t \in Tag : label[src][t] <= label[dst][t]   \* flow only upward

Observe(p, objLabel) ==
    \* reading taints the observer: label floats to the join
    /\ label' = [q \in DOMAIN label |->
           IF q = p THEN [t \in Tag |-> Max(label[p][t], objLabel[t])]
           ELSE label[q]]

Declassify(p, t, cap) ==
    /\ cap \in Capabilities(p, t)                  \* downgrade needs authority
    /\ label' = [label EXCEPT ![p][t] = 0]
====
```

Model-checking exposes the §4.5 dynamic directly: without `Declassify` steps, `Observe` drives all labels monotonically to the top — a liveness-flavored pathology (safety never violated, yet communication progressively impossible) that safety-only specifications cannot express.

### 5.3 Synthesis: Three Answers to One Question

Asbestos, HiStar, and Flume are three points in one design space, trading *purity of guarantee* against *deployability*: **Asbestos** maximizes expressiveness per line of application code; **HiStar** maximizes assurance — smallest mechanism, strongest theorem, least-privilege downgrade; **Flume** maximizes adoptability — no new kernel, no new language. No system dominates on all axes.

## 6. Limitations

1. **Covert channels.** All three track *explicit* flows through kernel-mediated operations. Timing, resource exhaustion, and termination behavior remain largely unaddressed; Flume's authors note user-space mediation *widens* these channels [6]. API-level noninterference is not physical noninterference.
2. **The declassifier's burden.** Downgrade authority concentrates in trusted components — Asbestos's declassifier, HiStar's owners, Flume's *t⁻* holders. A bug there is total policy bypass, and the theorems *assume* the declassifier's correctness. DIFC shrinks the TCB; it does not eliminate trust.
3. **Quantitative flow.** The lattice is qualitative: one bit and one gigabyte are equally "a flow." None of the systems addresses *how much* information moves — aggregators and inference need quantitative declassification (differential privacy, *k*-anonymity) that label models cannot express.
4. **Policy authorship.** DIFC answers "how is policy enforced?" but not "what should the policy be?" Discovering correct labels for real applications remains substantially open.
5. **Integrity, the neglected twin.** The DLM's integrity policies [2] are as expressive as its confidentiality policies yet far less explored in deployed systems; endorsement received a fraction of declassification's attention.

---

## 7. Conclusion

The decade from Myers and Liskov's decentralized label model [1] to Flume [6] established a permanent result: *operating systems can enforce end-to-end information flow policies over mutually distrustful principals, with declassification as a principled, authority-checked operation rather than a hole in the wall.* Asbestos proved it scales to hundreds of thousands of isolated users [4]; HiStar proved the guarantee can be a theorem over the kernel's total behavior, with declassification itself under least privilege [5]; Flume proved the discipline retrofits onto the systems the world actually runs [6].

The deeper lesson is encoded in label creep: **confinement without release is self-denial-of-service.** Every successful DIFC deployment is architecturally a *declassification design* first — a choke point holding ⋆, a gate shedding only its own taint, a *t⁻* capability scoped to one downgrade path. Future systems for cloud multi-tenancy, mobile platforms, or agents handling mutually distrustful principals' data will rediscover this shape or inherit it. The lattice tells you what may flow; only an explicit, auditable theory of *downgrade* tells you what the system is for.
