# Score-Based Diffusion Models: Denoising Score Matching, the Probability Flow ODE, and Exact Likelihood Computation

## Abstract

We present a unified, mathematically rigorous treatment of score-based diffusion models, the family of generative models that synthesize data from noise by learning and integrating the gradient field of log-probability densities. Beginning with Hyvärinen's score matching principle and Vincent's denoising score matching reformulation, we derive the continuous-time stochastic differential equation framework of Song et al., covering the variance-preserving, variance-exploding, and sub-variance-preserving forward processes, Anderson's reverse-time stochastic differential equation, and the probability flow ordinary differential equation that shares every marginal distribution of the reverse diffusion. We analyze predictor-corrector sampling, exact likelihood computation through the Hutchinson stochastic trace estimator and the instantaneous change of variables, and the structural connections of score-based diffusion to Schrödinger bridges and flow matching. The article concludes with a proof of the denoising score matching identity, a summary of empirical scaling evidence from the literature, and a discussion of limitations and open problems.

## 1 Introduction

Generative modeling asks a deceptively simple question: given samples from an unknown distribution $p_{\text{data}}$ over $\mathbb{R}^d$, how do we produce new samples that are statistically indistinguishable from the data? Classical answers are unsatisfying: maximum likelihood demands a normalized density, which is intractable in high dimensions; implicit models such as GANs sidestep densities entirely but surrender likelihood evaluation and training stability; energy-based models embrace unnormalized densities but require poorly-mixing Markov chain Monte Carlo for both training and sampling.

Score-based models occupy a striking middle ground. They model not the density itself but its *score function* $\nabla_x \log p(x)$, the gradient of the log-density with respect to the data. Because the normalization constant vanishes under differentiation, the score is well-defined for unnormalized models. Because the score is a vector field, it can be learned by regression — and once learned, it can be plugged into stochastic dynamics (Langevin dynamics, reverse-time diffusions) to generate samples, or into ordinary differential equations to evaluate exact likelihoods. This article traces the full arc: from the statistical estimation theory of score matching [1][2], through the neural score networks of the deep learning era [3][4], to the continuous-time synthesis of Song et al. [5] and the modern relatives that extend it [8][9].

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The score function and the Fisher divergence

Let $p$ be a differentiable density on $\mathbb{R}^d$. Its *score* is the vector field $s(x) := \nabla_x \log p(x)$, well-defined even for unnormalized densities. The natural distance between a model score $s_\theta$ and the data score is the **Fisher divergence**

$$
J(\theta) := \tfrac{1}{2}\,\mathbb{E}_{x \sim p_{\text{data}}}\big[\|s_\theta(x) - \nabla_x \log p_{\text{data}}(x)\|^2\big].
$$

Minimizing $J$ directly is impossible because the data score is unknown. The classical escape route is integration by parts.

### 2.2 Implicit score matching

Hyvärinen showed [1] that under mild regularity conditions (the density and its derivatives vanish sufficiently fast at infinity), the Fisher divergence is equivalent — up to a constant independent of $\theta$ — to an objective that involves only samples and model derivatives:

> **Theorem (Implicit score matching, Hyvärinen 2005):** If $p_{\text{data}}$ and $s_\theta$ satisfy the boundary conditions of [1], then
> $$
> J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim p_{\text{data}}}\left[\operatorname{tr}\big(\nabla_x s_\theta(x)\big) + \tfrac{1}{2}\|s_\theta(x)\|^2\right] + C,
> $$
> where $C$ does not depend on $\theta$ and $\nabla_x s_\theta$ denotes the Jacobian of the score network.

The empirical average over training data gives a consistent estimator, but the Hessian trace term costs $d$ backward passes — prohibitive for images. This bottleneck is what made the denoising reformulation so consequential.

### 2.3 Denoising score matching

Vincent's key insight [2] was to match the score of a *perturbed* data distribution rather than the data distribution itself. Fix a noise kernel $q_\sigma(\tilde{x} \mid x)$, typically Gaussian $\mathcal{N}(\tilde{x}; x, \sigma^2 I)$, and consider the smoothed density $q_\sigma(\tilde{x}) = \int q_\sigma(\tilde{x} \mid x)\,p_{\text{data}}(x)\,dx$. Then the Fisher divergence between $s_\theta$ and $\nabla \log q_\sigma$ has the following miraculous equivalent form:

> **Theorem (Denoising score matching, Vincent 2011):**
> $$
> \tfrac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{x} \sim q_\sigma}\|s_\theta(\tilde{x}) - \nabla_{\tilde{x}} \log q_\sigma(\tilde{x})\|^2 = \tfrac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}_{x \sim p_{\text{data}}}\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{x} \sim q_\sigma(\cdot \mid x)}\|s_\theta(\tilde{x}) - \nabla_{\tilde{x}} \log q_\sigma(\tilde{x} \mid x)\|^2 + C'.
> $$

For Gaussian corruption, $\nabla_{\tilde{x}} \log q_\sigma(\tilde{x} \mid x) = -(\tilde{x} - x)/\sigma^2$ is known in closed form, so the right-hand side is a plain regression problem: predict the scaled noise that corrupted each data point. No Hessians, no trace, just supervised denoising. The proof — a few lines of algebra expanding the quadratic and applying Bayes' rule — is given in full in Section 5. As $\sigma \to 0$, the smoothed score converges to the true data score, while for finite $\sigma$ the objective regularizes the estimation problem in low-density regions where the raw score is hard to learn.

### 2.4 From denoising autoencoders to score networks

Vincent originally framed this in the language of denoising autoencoders [2]: a DAE trained to reconstruct clean inputs from corrupted ones implicitly learns the score of an energy-based model tied to a Parzen window estimate of the data. Song and Ermon's *Noise Conditional Score Networks* [3] estimate scores at a ladder of noise scales with a single conditioned network and sample by annealed Langevin dynamics, while Ho et al.'s DDPMs [4] learn to reverse a discrete noising chain with a variational objective that is, up to weighting, denoising score matching on a particular noise schedule. The SDE framework of [5] unifies all of these under one continuous-time umbrella, which we develop next.

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 The forward diffusion as an SDE

Song et al. [5] replace the discrete noising chain of [4] with a continuous-time diffusion

$$
dx = f(x, t)\,dt + g(t)\,dw, \qquad t \in (0, T),
$$

where $w$ is a standard Wiener process, $f: \mathbb{R}^d \times (0,T) \to \mathbb{R}^d$ is the drift coefficient, and $g: (0,T) \to \mathbb{R}$ is the diffusion coefficient. Given $x(0) \sim p_0 = p_{\text{data}}$, the process induces a family of marginal densities $\{p_t\}_{t \in (0,T)}$ whose evolution is governed by the Fokker–Planck equation. Three concrete SDEs, each with a closed-form Gaussian transition kernel $p(x_t \mid x_0)$, cover essentially all practical use:

| SDE | Forward dynamics | Transition kernel $p(x_t \mid x_0)$ |
|---|---|---|
| **VP** (variance preserving) | $dx = -\tfrac{1}{2}\beta(t)x\,dt + \sqrt{\beta(t)}\,dw$ | $\mathcal{N}\big(x_t; x_0 e^{-\frac{1}{2}\int_0^t \beta}, (1 - e^{-\int_0^t \beta})I\big)$ |
| **VE** (variance exploding) | $dx = \sqrt{\tfrac{d}{dt}\sigma^2(t)}\,dw$ | $\mathcal{N}\big(x_t; x_0, (\sigma^2(t) - \sigma^2(0))I\big)$ |
| **sub-VP** | $dx = -\tfrac{1}{2}\beta(t)x\,dt + \sqrt{\beta(t)(1 - e^{-2\int_0^t \beta})}\,dw$ | $\mathcal{N}\big(x_t; x_0 e^{-\frac{1}{2}\int_0^t \beta}, (1 - e^{-\int_0^t \beta})^2 I\big)$ |

The VP SDE is the continuous-time limit of the DDPM noising chain [4], and VE is the continuous-time limit of noise-conditional score networks [3]. The sub-VP SDE was introduced in [5] to bound the variance below that of VP, which empirically improves likelihoods. In all cases the terminal marginal $p_T$ is approximately a simple prior (a Gaussian), so that sampling can start from pure noise.

### 3.2 The continuous-time denoising score matching objective

We train a time-dependent score network $s_\theta(x, t)$ to estimate $\nabla_x \log p_t(x)$ for all $t$ simultaneously, minimizing

$$
\mathcal{L}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{t \sim \mathcal{U}(0,T)}\left[\lambda(t)\,\mathbb{E}_{x_0 \sim p_0}\mathbb{E}_{x_t \sim p(x_t \mid x_0)}\left\|s_\theta(x_t, t) - \nabla_{x_t} \log p(x_t \mid x_0)\right\|^2\right],
$$

where $\lambda: (0,T) \to \mathbb{R}_{>0}$ is a positive weighting function. Because each $p(x_t \mid x_0)$ is Gaussian with a known mean and covariance, its score is available analytically — for VP it is $-(x_t - \mu_t x_0)/\sigma_t^2$ — so the objective reduces to a weighted denoising regression at every noise level. The weighting $\lambda(t)$ matters: common choices include $\lambda(t) = \sigma_t^2$ (recovering the DDPM weighting) or likelihood-based weightings that emphasize small $t$ where the score carries the most information about fine data structure.

### 3.3 Anderson's reverse-time SDE

The mathematical engine of sampling is time reversal of diffusions. Anderson proved [6] that under regularity conditions, the time-reversed process $\bar{x}(t) := x(T - t)$ is itself a diffusion, with drift corrected by the score:

> **Theorem (Anderson 1982):** Let $dx = f(x,t)\,dt + g(t)\,dw$ induce marginals $p_t$ with $p_t > 0$ smooth. Then the reverse-time process satisfies
> $$
> dx = \big[f(x, t) - g(t)^2 \nabla_x \log p_t(x)\big]\,dt + g(t)\,d\bar{w},
> $$
> where $\bar{w}$ is a Wiener process running backward in time, and the marginals of the reverse process coincide with $\{p_t\}$.

Plugging the learned score into this formula yields a generative model: sample $x_T \sim p_T$, integrate the reverse SDE back to $t = 0$, and obtain an approximate sample from $p_0$.

### 3.4 The probability flow ODE

A remarkable consequence of the Fokker–Planck equation is that the *same marginals* $\{p_t\}$ are produced by a deterministic ordinary differential equation — the **probability flow ODE** [5]:

$$
\frac{dx}{dt} = f(x, t) - \tfrac{1}{2} g(t)^2 \nabla_x \log p_t(x).
$$

The intuition: the diffusive spreading of the SDE is exactly canceled by contracting along the score direction, so the ODE's drift $f - \tfrac{1}{2} g^2 \nabla \log p_t$ transports probability mass identically. The probability flow ODE is therefore a *neural ODE*: it defines an invertible, deterministic map between the prior and the data distribution. This buys two decisive capabilities: (i) exact likelihood computation by integrating the instantaneous change of variables along the trajectory, and (ii) efficient sampling with adaptive black-box ODE solvers that take larger steps than stochastic integrators in smooth regimes.

### 3.5 Predictor-corrector sampling

Numerical SDE solvers introduce discretization error, and the learned score is imperfect. Song et al. [5] introduced a **predictor-corrector** framework: each reverse-time step first advances with a numerical SDE solver (the *predictor*, e.g., Euler–Maruyama or a reverse-diffusion step), then runs a few iterations of a score-based MCMC method (the *corrector*, typically Langevin dynamics targeting $p_t$):

1. **Predictor:** $x \leftarrow x + \big[f - g^2 s_\theta\big]\Delta t + g\sqrt{|\Delta t|}\,z$, $z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)$.
2. **Corrector (M steps):** $x \leftarrow x + \tfrac{\delta}{2} s_\theta(x, t) + \sqrt{\delta}\,z$, with step size $\delta$ set from a target signal-to-noise ratio $r$.

The corrector drives the sample back toward the true marginal $p_t$, compensating for both discretization and score-estimation error. Empirically, predictor-corrector schemes dominate either ingredient alone on image benchmarks [5].

### 3.6 Exact likelihoods via the probability flow ODE

For the probability flow ODE $dx/dt = \tilde{f}_\theta(x, t)$, the log-density evolves by the **instantaneous change of variables**

$$
\frac{d \log p_t(x(t))}{dt} = -\operatorname{tr}\left(\nabla_x \tilde{f}_\theta(x(t), t)\right),
$$

so that $\log p_0(x_0) = \log p_T(x_T) + \int_0^T \operatorname{tr}(\nabla_x \tilde{f}_\theta)\,dt$ along the ODE trajectory. The Jacobian trace is estimated without $O(d^2)$ cost using the **Hutchinson stochastic trace estimator** [7]: for $\epsilon$ with $\mathbb{E}[\epsilon] = 0$ and $\operatorname{Cov}(\epsilon) = I$,

$$
\operatorname{tr}(A) = \mathbb{E}_\epsilon[\epsilon^\top A \epsilon],
$$

which converts one trace into one vector–Jacobian product $\epsilon^\top \nabla_x \tilde{f}_\theta$, computed by a single backward-mode autodiff pass. Rademacher or standard Gaussian probes are the usual choices. The resulting estimator is unbiased, and combined with an adaptive ODE solver (e.g., Dormand–Prince) it yields bits-per-dimension values competitive with the best autoregressive likelihood models [5], a striking result for a model class whose training never mentions likelihoods. A minimal implementation:

```python
import torch

def log_prob_via_flow(x0, score_net, f, g, T, n_probe=1):
    """Exact-ish log-likelihood of x0 under the probability flow ODE."""
    def drift(x, t):
        return f(x, t) - 0.5 * g(t) ** 2 * score_net(x, t)

    logp = log_prior(x0)  # log density of p_T
    x, t = x0, torch.zeros(1)
    # Dormand-Prince with adjoint omitted for clarity
    for x, t, dt in ode_steps(x, t, T):
        eps = torch.randint_like(x, 2) * 2 - 1  # Rademacher probe
        # vector-Jacobian product eps^T d(drift)/dx
        _, vjp = torch.autograd.functional.vjp(lambda z: drift(z, t), x, eps)
        div_est = (eps * vjp).sum() / n_probe  # Hutchinson trace estimate
        logp = logp + div_est * dt
        x = x + drift(x, t) * dt
    return logp
```

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Fokker–Planck argument behind the probability flow ODE

Why does a deterministic ODE reproduce the marginals of a stochastic diffusion? Write the forward SDE in reverse time with learned score $s_\theta$ in place of $\nabla \log p_t$. Its Fokker–Planck equation for the density $q_t$ is

$$
\partial_t q_t = -\nabla \cdot \big((f - g^2 s_\theta)\, q_t\big) + \tfrac{1}{2} g^2 \Delta q_t,
$$

where the Laplacian term is the diffusive spreading and the divergence term is probability transport. Now consider the ODE $dx/dt = f - \tfrac{1}{2} g^2 s_\theta$. Its continuity equation is

$$
\partial_t q_t = -\nabla \cdot \big((f - \tfrac{1}{2} g^2 s_\theta)\, q_t\big).
$$

Subtracting, the ODE and SDE evolutions agree whenever $s_\theta = \nabla \log q_t$ — i.e., when the score network is perfect. The disagreement under imperfect scores is controlled by the score-matching error, which is why the DSM objective is the right training target for *both* the SDE and ODE samplers.

### 4.2 Score parameterization and the noise-prediction identity

There is a deep equivalence between score prediction and noise prediction. For a Gaussian transition kernel $p(x_t \mid x_0) = \mathcal{N}(x_t; \mu_t x_0, \sigma_t^2 I)$,

$$
\nabla_{x_t} \log p(x_t \mid x_0) = -\frac{x_t - \mu_t x_0}{\sigma_t^2} = -\frac{\epsilon}{\sigma_t}, \qquad \epsilon := \frac{x_t - \mu_t x_0}{\sigma_t} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I).
$$

Hence learning the score $s_\theta(x_t, t)$ is algebraically identical to learning a noise predictor $\epsilon_\theta(x_t, t)$ with $s_\theta = -\epsilon_\theta / \sigma_t$ — the DDPM parameterization of [4] is denoising score matching in disguise. This is why the same network architecture (a U-Net with time embeddings) serves both DDPMs and NCSNs: the tasks are the same regression problem under different algebraic clothing.

### 4.3 Predictor-corrector sampling and the geometry of the reverse process

The reverse drift has two components: the forward drift $f$ (often contractive) and the score correction $-g^2 \nabla \log p_t$, which pushes uphill on the log-density toward regions of high probability. Early in the reverse process the marginals are nearly Gaussian and the score is a simple linear pull toward the mean; late in the process the score field becomes intricate, with sharp ridges along the data manifold. Corrector steps let the sample relax onto the current marginal before the predictor advances, which is why predictor-corrector schemes win on sample quality [5].

### 4.4 Exact likelihoods: the Hutchinson estimator and its variance

The Hutchinson estimator is unbiased but noisy, and its variance determines how many probes are needed for a stable likelihood. For a Rademacher probe, $\operatorname{Var}[\epsilon^\top A \epsilon] = 2(\|A\|_F^2 - \sum_i A_{ii}^2)$; the variance is small when $A$ is diagonally dominant and large when it has heavy off-diagonal mass. The Jacobian $\nabla_x \tilde{f}_\theta$ of the probability flow drift inherits the structure of the score network's Jacobian, which for convolutional architectures is block-structured with local support — empirically favorable for Hutchinson estimation [7]. Likelihood evaluation thus costs solver-steps times probes in vector–Jacobian products — hundreds of network evaluations per image — and single-sample likelihoods carry Monte Carlo noise even though the expectation is exact.

### 4.5 Schrödinger bridges and flow matching

The SDE framework has two important relatives.

**Flow matching** [8] takes a different route: it trains a neural ODE directly, regressing a learned vector field $v_\theta(x, t)$ onto a *conditional* vector field $u_t(x \mid x_0)$ that generates a prescribed probability path $p_t(x \mid x_0)$ between noise and data. The conditional flow matching loss

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\text{CFM}} = \mathbb{E}_{t, x_0, x \sim p_t(\cdot \mid x_0)}\|v_\theta(x, t) - u_t(x \mid x_0)\|^2
$$

has the same gradient as the intractable marginal objective, by exactly the algebraic mechanism of Section 2.3.

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 Proof of the denoising score matching identity

We prove the theorem stated in Section 2.3, following [2] and standard lecture treatments. Expand the left-hand objective:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\tfrac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{x} \sim q_\sigma}\|s_\theta(\tilde{x}) - \nabla \log q_\sigma(\tilde{x})\|^2
&= \tfrac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{x} \sim q_\sigma}\|s_\theta(\tilde{x})\|^2 - \mathbb{E}_{\tilde{x} \sim q_\sigma}\big[s_\theta(\tilde{x})^\top \nabla \log q_\sigma(\tilde{x})\big] + C_1.
\end{aligned}
$$

The cross term is the only nontrivial piece. Writing the expectation as an integral and using $\nabla \log q_\sigma = \nabla q_\sigma / q_\sigma$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{x} \sim q_\sigma}\big[s_\theta(\tilde{x})^\top \nabla \log q_\sigma(\tilde{x})\big]
&= \int s_\theta(\tilde{x})^\top \nabla q_\sigma(\tilde{x})\,d\tilde{x} \\
&= \int s_\theta(\tilde{x})^\top \nabla_{\tilde{x}} \left(\int q_\sigma(\tilde{x} \mid x)\,p_{\text{data}}(x)\,dx\right) d\tilde{x} \\
&= \int\!\!\int s_\theta(\tilde{x})^\top \nabla_{\tilde{x}} q_\sigma(\tilde{x} \mid x)\,p_{\text{data}}(x)\,dx\,d\tilde{x} \\
&= \mathbb{E}_{x \sim p_{\text{data}}}\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{x} \sim q_\sigma(\cdot \mid x)}\big[s_\theta(\tilde{x})^\top \nabla_{\tilde{x}} \log q_\sigma(\tilde{x} \mid x)\big].
\end{aligned}
$$

Substituting back and completing the square with the $\theta$-independent term $\tfrac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}\|\nabla \log q_\sigma(\tilde{x} \mid x)\|^2$ yields the right-hand side of the theorem. ∎ The argument needs only Fubini's theorem and differentiability of the kernel — no integration by parts, no boundary conditions, and no Hessian. This is the sense in which denoising score matching is the computationally tractable face of the same Fisher divergence.

### 5.2 Empirical scaling evidence

The empirical record of [5] established the SDE framework as state of the art. On CIFAR-10, the VP-SDE with NCSN++ and predictor-corrector sampling reached Inception score **9.89** and FID **2.20**, surpassing contemporary GANs, while the probability flow ODE achieved **2.99 bits/dim**, competitive with autoregressive models; the same framework produced the first high-fidelity $1024 \times 1024$ FFHQ samples from a score-based model. The sub-VP SDE improved likelihoods over VP at matched sample quality, and ablations attributed the gains over [3] and [4] to the continuous-time DSM objective with likelihood weighting rather than any single architectural choice.

## 6 Limitations

1. **Score estimation in low-density regions.** The Fisher divergence weights errors by $p_{\text{data}}$ (or $p_t$), so the learned score can be arbitrarily wrong where data is absent. During reverse sampling the trajectory may visit such regions, and score errors there cause visible artifacts. Multi-scale noising [3] mitigates this by construction — every point is near data at large $\sigma$ — but does not eliminate it.
2. **The manifold hypothesis and dimensionality.** If data lies on a low-dimensional manifold, the score normal to the manifold is undefined in the limit of zero noise and enormous in practice; score networks must learn extremely steep gradients near the manifold, which is statistically and numerically hard.
3. **Cost of likelihoods.** Exact likelihoods via the probability flow ODE require hundreds of score evaluations per sample (solver steps times Hutchinson probes), far exceeding the cost of a single forward pass in an autoregressive model.
4. **Sampling speed.** Even with predictor-corrector and ODE solvers, score-based models need tens to hundreds of sequential network evaluations per sample — orders of magnitude slower than a GAN generator — though distillation and flow matching [8] are closing the gap.
5. **Regularity assumptions.** Anderson's reversal [6] and the Fokker–Planck argument of Section 4.1 require smooth, strictly positive densities; real data distributions (discrete tokens, bounded pixel ranges) violate these, and practitioners rely on the smoothing effect of the forward process to paper over the gap.
6. **Mode coverage is not guaranteed.** Like all likelihood-adjacent objectives, score matching can in principle cover all modes, but finite capacity and optimization dynamics can still drop modes, and the objective offers no certificate of coverage.

---

## 7 Conclusion

Score-based diffusion models unify two decades of ideas — Hyvärinen's integration-by-parts trick [1], Vincent's denoising reformulation [2], annealed Langevin sampling [3], discrete diffusion chains [4] — into a single continuous-time framework [5] in which generation is time reversal of a noising diffusion and the score is the only object that must be learned. Its crown jewels are Anderson's reverse-time SDE [6], the probability flow ODE with exact likelihoods via the Hutchinson estimator [7], and predictor-corrector samplers. Its modern extensions — Schrödinger bridges [9] that close the prior gap in finite time, and flow matching [8] that generalizes the probability path — suggest the SDE formulation is the first mature instance of a broader principle: *generative modeling as regression onto a vector field, integrated in time*.
