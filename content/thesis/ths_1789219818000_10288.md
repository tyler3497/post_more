# Threshold Schnorr Signatures at Scale: FROST, MuSig2, and ROAST — Round Optimality, Concurrent Security, and Asynchronous Robustness

## Abstract

Threshold signatures distribute the power of a single signing key across *n* parties so that any *t* of them can jointly emit a compact, standards-compliant signature while fewer than *t* cannot forge — the cryptographic backbone of custody, federated sidechains, and decentralized key management. Schnorr's linear algebra makes them uniquely elegant there. This thesis reconstructs the modern theory of threshold Schnorr signatures: the round-optimal **FROST** construction and its per-session binding-factor defense against concurrent forgery; the **MuSig2** key-aggregation multisignature specified in BIP-327 that made two-round Bitcoin Taproot signing practical; and the **ROAST** wrapper that restores guaranteed output under asynchronous networks and disruptive signers. We explain the **ROS problem** whose polynomial-time solution destroyed two decades of supposedly secure two-round Schnorr schemes, and the meta-reduction of Drijvers et al. that proved no algebraic reduction could have saved them. We contrast Schnorr's linearity with the MPC-heavy threshold-ECDSA lineage (GG18, CGGMP21), survey 2025 breakthroughs on adaptive security, and ground the theory in IETF RFC 9591 and the Liquid federation's 67-of-100 deployment.

## 1 Introduction

A signing key is a single point of catastrophic failure. Threshold cryptography answers with Shamir's secret sharing [8] and the Desmedt–Frankel formalism: split the private key *x* into shares *s₁,…,sₙ* so that any *t* can sign while any *t−1* reveal nothing.

Yet threshold *ECDSA* resisted simplicity for years: *s = k⁻¹(m + rx)* intertwines inversion and multiplication of secrets, forcing the Gennaro–Goldfeder, Lindell, and CGGMP21 lineage [7] into Paillier encryption, multiplicative-to-additive conversion, range proofs, and offline presigning.

Schnorr signatures, by contrast, are *linear*. A signature *(R, z)* under public key *X = gˣ* satisfies *gᶻ = R · Xᶜ* with challenge *c = H(X, R, m)*, where the response *z = r + cx* is a linear function of the nonce *r* and the key *x*. If shares *sᵢ* of the key and shares *(dᵢ, eᵢ)* of the nonce are distributed, partial responses *zᵢ* combine by linear aggregation into a valid group signature. This linearity is what makes threshold Schnorr dramatically simpler — and what, paradoxically, made its security proofs so treacherous.

The treachery lived in *concurrency*: protocols unforgeable under sequential sessions collapsed when an adversary interleaved many sessions adaptively. In 2019, Drijvers et al. [6] proved via meta-reduction that no algebraic reduction could prove any known two-round multisignature scheme secure under the DL or OMDL assumptions — and exhibited practical subexponential attacks. In 2021, Benhamouda et al. [5] sharpened the blade: a *polynomial-time* algorithm for the ROS problem whenever the concurrent session count ℓ exceeds log *p*, breaking Schnorr blind signatures, CoSi, two-round MuSig, GJKR threshold signatures, and the original FROST preprocessing variant itself.

Out of this wreckage came the modern consensus. **FROST** (Komlo–Goldberg, 2020) [1] added per-session *binding factors*: two-round signing — one-round with preprocessing — with identifiable aborts and OMDL-based proofs. **MuSig2** (Nick–Ruffing–Seurin, 2021) [3] solved the multisignature problem: two rounds, key aggregation defeating rogue-key attacks, preprocessing of all but one round, security under OMDL in the ROM + AGM. **ROAST** (Ruffing et al., 2022) [4] wrapped FROST in a scheduler guaranteeing *t* honest signers finish even when *n − t* are malicious and the network is asynchronous. FROST became IETF **RFC 9591** [2]; MuSig2 became Bitcoin **BIP-327** [11]; ROAST entered production in Blockstream's Liquid federation.

**§2** fixes notation and security definitions; **§3** reconstructs FROST and its binding-factor defense; **§4** dives into MuSig2's nonce aggregation, the ROS attack, ROAST's robustness argument, the threshold-ECDSA contrast, and 2025's adaptive-security breakthroughs; **§5** sketches proofs and reports empirical performance; **§6** maps the remaining limitations.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The Schnorr Identification Paradigm

Fix a cyclic group 𝔾 = ⟨*g*⟩ of prime order *q* in which the discrete logarithm problem (DLP) is hard. Key generation samples *x ←$ ℤ_q* and publishes *X = gˣ*. The Schnorr signature of message *m* is:

```
r ←$ ℤ_q          R := g^r
c := H(X, R, m)   z := r + c·x
output (R, z)
```

Verification checks *gᶻ = R · Xᶜ*. EUF-CMA security reduces to the DLP in the random oracle model via the *forking lemma*: rewind the adversary, change the oracle's answer at the forgery point, and extract the discrete log from two signatures sharing *R* [1].

> **Security Definition: Threshold Unforgeability (TS-SUF-CMA).**
> A *(t, n)*-threshold signature scheme is *unforgeable* if no PPT adversary corrupting at most *t−1* signers can produce a valid signature on a message *m* for which fewer than *t* honest signers ever participated in a signing session. The strongest variants demand security under *concurrent* sessions and *adaptive* corruptions; *static* corruption (corrupted set fixed before key generation) is the weakest model.

### 2.2 Shamir Sharing and Distributed Key Generation

Shamir's *(t, n)*-threshold scheme [8] samples a random polynomial *f* of degree *t−1* with *f(0) = x* and gives party *Pᵢ* the share *sᵢ = f(i)*. Any *t* shares reconstruct *x* via Lagrange interpolation, *x = Σᵢ λᵢ·sᵢ*, *linear in the shares* — so any linear function of *x*, including Schnorr's response *z = r + cx*, can be computed from linear combinations of the shares without ever reassembling *x*.

Key generation must be distributed. Pedersen's DKG has each party deal Shamir shares of a random contribution, with Feldman VSS commitments so misbehaving dealers are disqualified; the group key is *X = Π Xᵢ*. Gennaro–Jarecki–Krawczyk–Rabin (GJKR) strengthened this against rushing adversaries, and FROST deployments use a GJKR-style DKG [2].

### 2.3 Security Models: ROM, AGM, OMDL

Three idealizations recur in every proof below:

- **Random Oracle Model (ROM):** the hash *H* is a public random function, enabling the forking lemma.
- **Algebraic Group Model (AGM):** every group element the adversary outputs carries its *representation* as a linear combination of previously seen elements — formalizing "the adversary must know the discrete logs it uses," which rewinding-based reductions need.
- **One-More Discrete Logarithm (OMDL):** the adversary gets random group elements *R₁,…,Rℓ* and a discrete-log oracle usable at most *ℓ−1* times, and must output all ℓ discrete logs. Threshold Schnorr reductions target OMDL because each signing session hands the adversary one fresh nonce commitment and expects one response back — precisely a one-more game [3][6].

> **Theorem (informal): FROST TS-SUF-CMA.**
> FROST with preprocessing is unforgeable in the ROM under OMDL, against a static adversary corrupting *t−1* signers with polynomially many concurrent sessions [1]. *This holds only because of the binding factor of §3.3 — the original variant without it fell to the ROS attack [5].*

## 3 Methodology: The FROST Construction

### 3.1 Protocol Rounds

FROST signing proceeds in two phases per participant set *S* of size *t*. In the **commitment (preprocessing) phase**, each *Pᵢ* samples nonce pairs *(dᵢ, eᵢ) ←$ ℤ_q²* and publishes *(Dᵢ, Eᵢ) = (g^{dᵢ}, g^{eᵢ})* — message-independent, so it can run arbitrarily far in advance. In the **signing phase**, given message *m* and commitment list *B = {(i, Dᵢ, Eᵢ)}ᵢ∈S*, each participant computes:

```
ρᵢ := H₁(i, m, B)              # binding value: ties i's nonces to THIS session
R  := Πᵢ (Dᵢ · Eᵢ^ρᵢ)          # aggregate nonce commitment
c  := H₂(R, X, m)              # challenge
zᵢ := dᵢ + eᵢ·ρᵢ + λᵢ·sᵢ·c     # partial response (λᵢ: Lagrange coefficient for S)
```

An untrusted aggregator collects the *zᵢ*, verifies each share via *g^{zᵢ} = DᵢEᵢ^{ρᵢ} · Yᵢ^{λᵢc}*, and outputs *z = Σ zᵢ*. Verification is ordinary Schnorr: *gᶻ = R·Xᶜ* — the threshold machinery is invisible to verifiers.

### 3.2 Why Linearity Suffices — and Where It Almost Failed

Observe: *Σ zᵢ = r + cx* for nonce *r = Σ(dᵢ + ρᵢeᵢ)* and key *x = Σ λᵢsᵢ*. No party ever learns *r* or *x*; reconstruction happens only in the exponent, at verification time.

The subtlety is that the adversary, corrupting *t−1* parties, *chooses its commitments after seeing the honest parties' commitments* in each concurrent session. In the original FROST variant, *R* was a plain sum of all *(Dᵢ, Eᵢ)* pairs with no per-participant binding. An adversary could then solve the ROS problem across concurrent sessions: choose its commitments as linear combinations of honest commitments so that challenges satisfy a planted linear relation, then combine the honest partial responses into a forgery for a message never signed [5].

### 3.3 The Binding-Factor Defense

FROST's fix is conceptually minimal and cryptographically decisive. The binding value *ρᵢ = H₁(i, m, B)* commits each participant's *entire* nonce pair *(Dᵢ, Eᵢ)* to the triple *(i, m, B)* — the participant's identity, the message, and the full commitment list. The adversary can no longer choose *(D, E)* after seeing *B* and still control its contribution *Rᵢ = D·E^{ρᵢ}*, because *ρᵢ* itself depends on *B*; different messages or signer sets yield independent binding values, so partial responses cannot be transplanted across sessions. And because *ρᵢ* is public, anyone can check *g^{zᵢ} = DᵢEᵢ^{ρᵢ}·Yᵢ^{λᵢc}* per share — a participant submitting an invalid *zᵢ* is *identified* and excluded from future sessions.

In the security proof, the reduction simulates honest parties' nonces as OMDL challenge elements and answers signing queries using its single DL oracle query per session; the binding factor guarantees the reduction can *extract* — via forking on *H₁* and *H₂* — without the adversary's concurrent sessions collapsing the simulation [1].

### 3.4 From Two Rounds to One

With preprocessing, the commitment phase vanishes from the critical path: participants fetch pre-published *(Dᵢ, Eᵢ)* pairs and run a single signing round. FROST-Interactive (two rounds, no preprocessing) restricts concurrency for a tighter reduction; general FROST permits unbounded concurrency under OMDL. RFC 9591 standardizes both modes with five ciphersuites (Ed25519, secp256k1, P-256, ristretto255, Ed448) [2].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 MuSig2: Key Aggregation and the Rogue-Key Problem

Threshold signatures split one key among many holders; *multisignatures* go the other direction — *n* independent key holders, each with their own *Xᵢ*, jointly produce one signature on one message. The naive aggregation *X̃ = Π Xᵢ* is broken by the **rogue-key attack**: an adversary seeing honest *X₁,…,X_{n−1}* sets *Xₙ = g^{xₙ}·(Π_{i<n} Xᵢ)^{-1}*, making *X̃ = g^{xₙ}*. MuSig defeated this with **key aggregation**: *aᵢ = H_agg(L, Xᵢ)* over the key multiset *L*, so *X̃ = Π Xᵢ^{aᵢ}* and the adversary cannot cancel terms it does not know.

MuSig2 [3] kept key aggregation and solved the round problem with two rounds: **round 1** exchanges *ν* nonce commitments per signer (typically *ν = 4*); **round 2** computes nonce coefficients *b₁ = 1*, *bⱼ = H_non(agg-nonce, X̃, m)* for *j ≥ 2*, effective nonce *R̃ = Σ_j b_j·(Σᵢ R_{i,j})*, challenge *c = H_sig(X̃, R̃, m)*, and each responds *zᵢ = Σ_j (r_{i,j}·b_j) + c·aᵢ·xᵢ* — an ordinary Schnorr signature under *X̃*.

The multi-nonce trick is the insight: with *ν ≥ 2* nonces and message-dependent coefficients *bⱼ*, the Drijvers meta-reduction no longer applies — the adversary's ROS-style linear algebra must satisfy *ν* coupled random-oracle equations instead of one. Security holds under OMDL in the ROM + AGM (efficient variant), or plain ROM with slightly larger *ν*. Round 1 is message-independent and precomputable, so MuSig2 needs only one online round and composes safely with concurrent sessions — the property Drijvers et al. showed was impossible for all prior two-round schemes [6]. Bitcoin standardized it as **BIP-327** [11].

| Scheme | Rounds (online) | Signature size | Concurrency-safe | Key aggregation | Assumption |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| MuSig (MPSW19) | 3 | 1 Schnorr sig | yes | yes | OMDL, ROM |
| mBCJ [6] | 2 | >2× Schnorr | yes | no | DL, ROM |
| MuSig2 [3] | 2 (1 w/ preproc.) | 1 Schnorr sig | yes | yes | OMDL, ROM+AGM |
| FROST [1] | 2 (1 w/ preproc.) | 1 Schnorr sig | yes | n/a (threshold) | OMDL, ROM |
| CoSi | 2 | 1 Schnorr sig | **no** (ROS-broken) | yes | — |
| BLS threshold (Boldyreva) | **1 (non-interactive)** | 1 group elem | yes | n/a | Gap-DH (pairings) |

### 4.2 The ROS Catastrophe

The ROS problem — *Random inhomogeneities in an Overdetermined Solvable system of linear equations* — asks: given oracle *H_ros: {0,1}* → ℤ_p^ℓ*, find *ℓ+1* vectors *vᵢ* and coefficients *c* with *H_ros(vᵢ) = ⟨c, vᵢ⟩* for all *i*. Schnorr introduced it in 1996 as the hardness backbone of blind signatures; for 25 years it looked plausibly hard.

Benhamouda et al. [5] showed it is *easy*: polynomial time whenever *ℓ > log p*, and subexponential for any *ℓ* via Wagner's generalized-birthday algorithm. The attack on two-round Schnorr protocols works as follows. In each of *ℓ* concurrent sessions, the adversary receives honest nonce commitments, then adaptively chooses its own commitments and the messages so that the *ℓ* challenge equations become a linear system in the honest nonces. Solving ROS yields coefficients letting the adversary combine the *ℓ* honest responses *z⁽ᵏ⁾ = r⁽ᵏ⁾ + c⁽ᵏ⁾x* into a valid signature on a message the honest parties never signed. The paper demonstrated practical breaks of Schnorr blind signatures, CoSi, two-round MuSig, GJKR threshold signatures, and the *original* FROST preprocessing variant; RFC 9591's security-considerations section documents exactly why the standardized FROST's binding factor defeats the technique [2][5].

> **Proof (sketch): ROS ⇒ concurrent forgery.**
> Across *ℓ > log p* sessions the adversary adaptively chooses its commitments so the challenge vector satisfies a relation the ROS solver can exploit. The solver returns weights *wₖ* with *Σ wₖc⁽ᵏ⁾ = c\**, and *(R\*, Σ wₖz⁽ᵏ⁾)* verifies under the group key by linearity of *z⁽ᵏ⁾ = r⁽ᵏ⁾ + c⁽ᵏ⁾x*. ∎

The moral is methodological: *sequential-session proofs of Schnorr-like protocols prove almost nothing about deployed systems*. Concurrency is the default; the security model must assume it.

### 4.3 ROAST: Robustness Under Asynchrony

FROST guarantees *unforgeability* and *identifiable abort*: a cheating signer is caught and excluded. It does *not* guarantee the honest signers ever obtain a signature — one disruptive participant who sends garbage (or nothing) in the signing round forces an abort, and in an asynchronous network the coordinator cannot distinguish "malicious" from "slow." For a federated wallet, liveness failures are as fatal as forgeries.

**ROAST** [4] is a wrapper, not a new scheme. Given any threshold scheme that is (i) *semi-interactive*, (ii) equipped with *identifiable aborts*, and (iii) unforgeable under concurrent sessions — FROST satisfies all three — ROAST's coordinator repeatedly starts signing sessions with *t* responsive signers. Because aborts are identifiable, every failed session *provably eliminates at least one malicious signer*, so at most *n − t + 1* sessions are needed and the network may be fully asynchronous. The wrapper preserves unforgeability and adds *robustness*: *t* honest signers always terminate with a valid signature [4].

Blockstream's Liquid federation deployment — 67-of-100 across continents — signs within seconds even with 33 actively malicious signers [4].

### 4.4 The Threshold-ECDSA Contrast

Why did Schnorr get a clean story while ECDSA needed a decade of MPC research?

- **Schnorr:** *z = r + c·x* — linear in nonce and key; shares combine by addition.
- **ECDSA:** *s = k⁻¹(m + r·x)* — requires *inversion* and *multiplication* of shared secrets. The CGGMP21 lineage handles this with Paillier-encrypted multiplicative-to-additive share conversion, range proofs, and multi-round presigning — UC-secure with identifiable aborts [7], but with 2048-bit Paillier moduli and presign communication quadratic in *n*.

The comparison is not merely aesthetic. Threshold ECDSA remains indispensable wherever the *verifier* is fixed — Bitcoin and Ethereum script only understands ECDSA — because CGGMP21's output is byte-identical to a single-party ECDSA signature [7]. Threshold Schnorr dominates wherever the system can choose its signature scheme: greenfield custody, federations, and Taproot-era Bitcoin.

### 4.5 Adaptive Security: The 2025 Breakthroughs

The results above assume *static* corruption; real adversaries corrupt adaptively, compromising a signer *after* observing traffic. Proving FROST adaptively secure resisted years of effort: the simulator must produce the corrupted party's internal state *after* the fact, consistent with published commitments — seemingly requiring equivocation.

Two 2025 results cracked this.

## 5 Empirical Results and Proof Sketches

**Proof sketch: FROST TS-SUF-CMA (static, concurrent).** The reduction ℛ receives OMDL challenges *(D*, E*)* and a DL oracle usable *q_s − 1* times for *q_s* signing queries. ℛ embeds the challenges as one honest party's commitments in every session, programming *H₁, H₂* as random oracles, and spends one DL query per signing query. The binding factor ensures distinct sessions yield distinct *ρᵢ* and hence distinct effective nonces, so one oracle call covers exactly one session. A forgery plus a forking-lemma rewind on *H₂* then extracts the key. The ROS attack is what happens when *H₁* is absent: the adversary correlates sessions so one oracle query cannot cover them independently [1][5].

**Performance.** Per-signer cost: a constant number of multiexponentiations plus *O(t)* field operations for Lagrange coefficients; communication is two group elements per participant per round [2]:

| Configuration | Signing latency (LAN) | Coordinator work |
|---|---|---|
| 2-of-3, FROST Ed25519 | < 5 ms | *O(t)* multiexps |
| 5-of-7, FROST secp256k1 | < 20 ms | *O(t)* multiexps |
| 67-of-100, FROST + ROAST (WAN, 33 malicious) | seconds | *O(n)* sessions worst-case |

Drijvers et al.'s mBCJ experiment remains the large-scale reference point: 16,384 signers collaboratively signing in ~2 seconds [6].

**Deployment evidence.** Beyond Liquid's federation [4], FROST ships in the `secp256k1-frost` module with RFC 9591 test vectors [2] and in Zcash's `frost` Rust crates; MuSig2 is live in Bitcoin Taproot wallets via BIP-327 [11].

## 6 Limitations

**Identifiable abort ≠ guaranteed output.** FROST alone lets one malicious signer deny service indefinitely — identifiable, but the honest quorum never finishes without ROAST's scheduler. Robustness is a wrapper property, not a signature property [4].

**Static vs. adaptive corruption.** The base FROST proof assumes the corrupted set is fixed before key generation [1]. The 2025 adaptive results invoke the new LDVR assumption or modify the scheme [9]; a plain-ROM, OMDL-based adaptive proof of unmodified FROST is open — the most security-relevant gap for high-value custody.

**DKG trust assumptions.** FROST's signing security assumes key generation produced a correct sharing; Pedersen DKG is secure only against static adversaries, and GJKR-style DKG closes the rushing hole at the cost of extra rounds.

**Quantum vulnerability.** Everything here rests on the discrete logarithm problem, which Shor's algorithm breaks. The NIST post-quantum standards (ML-DSA, SLH-DSA) have no comparably mature threshold story; threshold lattice signatures remain an active research frontier.

**Verifier lock-in.** Where verifiers cannot change (legacy ECDSA systems), Schnorr's elegance is unavailable and the CGGMP21 machinery [7] remains mandatory.

**Side channels and implementation risk.** Nonce reuse across sessions is catastrophic (two responses under one nonce reveal the share), and preprocessing state must ensure commitments are never reused. The ROS history shows the failure mode is silent — protocols looked secure until the attack arrived.

## 7 Conclusion

*Linearity* made the protocols simple; *concurrency* made them fragile; the *binding factor* repaired them; *identifiable aborts* made failures attributable; *ROAST* converted attributability into liveness; and the 2025 adaptive-security results are converting static guarantees into realistic ones. Each step was forced by an attack or impossibility result — Drijvers's meta-reduction, the ROS solver — not by abstract optimization.

The practical upshot is a rare alignment: the scheme with the cleanest theory (two rounds, one Schnorr-sized signature, transparent verification) is also the scheme with the best deployments (RFC 9591, BIP-327, Liquid's 67-of-100 federation). Open problems remain — adaptive security in the plain ROM, post-quantum threshold signatures, lighter asynchronous DKG.
