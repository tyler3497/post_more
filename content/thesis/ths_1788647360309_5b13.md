# Stable Matching and School Choice: The Gale-Shapley Mechanism, Strategy-Proofness, and the Limits of Two-Sided Market Design

## Abstract

Two-sided matching markets — hospitals and residents, students and schools, patients and donors — allocate agents on both sides who hold preferences over one another, without monetary transfers. This thesis develops the theory of stable matching from its origin in the Gale–Shapley deferred-acceptance algorithm of 1962 through its modern deployments in centralized school choice and kidney exchange. We establish that a stable matching always exists and can be found in quadratic time, that the set of stable matchings carries a distributive lattice structure with a proposer-optimal extreme, and that the student-proposing variant is strategy-proof for the proposing side. We then confront the mechanism-design frontier: no stable mechanism is fully strategy-proof, a boundary sharpened by Roth's impossibility results, and stability can fail altogether in markets with couples. Finally, we survey how the theory succeeded in practice — the adoption of deferred acceptance in New York and Boston public schools, top trading cycles in kidney exchange, and modern frontiers including large-market approximate strategy-proofness and refugee resettlement under multidimensional capacity constraints.

---

## 1 Introduction

The problem of matching agents who have preferences over one another, without the mediating instrument of prices, is among the oldest in economic design. Who studies at which school, which resident works at which hospital, which patient receives a kidney from which donor — these allocations shape lives, yet resist the price mechanism for ethical reasons or because the "goods" are people with their own agency. The decisive theoretical breakthrough came from a short, almost non-technical paper: in 1962, David Gale and Lloyd Shapley published "College Admissions and the Stability of Marriage" in the *American Mathematical Monthly*, proposing both a solution concept — *stability* — and a constructive proof that such a solution always exists [1].

The subsequent six decades transformed this existence proof into a design paradigm. The deferred-acceptance (DA) algorithm of Gale and Shapley now clears the United States National Resident Matching Program (NRMP), assigns students to public schools in New York, Boston, and dozens of other cities, and its intellectual descendants — top trading cycles (TTC) and priority-based exchange mechanisms — organize national kidney exchange programs and pilot refugee resettlement systems.

This thesis proceeds as follows. Section 2 fixes the model and the stability concept. Section 3 describes our proof-driven methodology. Section 4 develops five deep-dives: (a) the marriage model and the lattice of stable matchings; (b) the deferred-acceptance algorithm — correctness, complexity, and the proposing side; (c) incentives, the impossibility theorem, and the rural hospitals theorem; (d) school choice in practice: the Boston mechanism's failures, DA adoption, and the DA–TTC tradeoff; and (e) frontiers — couples, large markets, kidney exchange, and refugee resettlement. Section 5 collects empirical results and proofs; Section 6 maps limitations and open problems; Section 7 concludes.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The marriage and college-admissions models

Let *M* be a finite set of *proposers* (men, students, doctors) and *W* a finite set of *receivers* (women, schools, hospitals), with *M ∩ W = ∅*. Each agent *a* has a strict preference relation *P_a* over the agents on the other side plus the option of remaining unmatched, denoted *∅*. The profile *P = (P_a)* summarizes preferences. In the **marriage model**, each agent is matched to at most one partner; in the **college-admissions model**, each college *c ∈ W* has a quota *q_c ≥ 1* and responsive preferences over subsets of students [1].

A **matching** *μ* pairs agents (or leaves them unmatched) such that *μ(m) = w* iff *m ∈ μ(w)*. A matching is **individually rational** if no agent prefers being unmatched to its assignment. A pair *(m, w)* is a **blocking pair** for *μ* if *m* prefers *w* to *μ(m)* and *w* prefers *m* to its current partner(s) — i.e., *w* finds *m* acceptable and either has unfilled capacity or ranks *m* above a currently matched student. A matching is **stable** if it is individually rational and admits no blocking pair [1][2].

> **Theorem:** (Gale–Shapley 1962) For every profile of strict preferences, the set of stable matchings is nonempty. When preferences are strict, there exists for each side of the market a stable matching that is *optimal* for that side: every agent on that side likes the optimal stable matching at least as well as any other stable matching [1].

### 2.2 The deferred-acceptance algorithm

The proof is constructive. In the **deferred-acceptance algorithm** with *M* proposing:

1. Each unmatched proposer *m ∈ M* proposes to its most-preferred receiver not yet proposed to.
2. Each receiver *w ∈ W* tentatively holds the most-preferred proposals up to its quota and rejects the rest.
3. Rejected proposers propose to their next choice; held proposals may later be displaced.
4. The algorithm ends when no rejected proposer has a further option; all held proposals are finalized [1][2].

The hallmark is that acceptances are *deferred*: a receiver never commits early, which is precisely what buys stability.

### 2.3 School choice as matching

Abdulkadiroğlu and Sönmez (2003) observed that school choice is a special case of two-sided matching: students are strategic agents with preferences over schools, while school "priorities" (walk zones, sibling status, lottery numbers) have the same formal structure as college preferences but carry no strategic content — schools are objects, not players. The stability axiom translates into **elimination of justified envy**: no student should prefer a school that either has a vacant seat or admitted a lower-priority student [3]. Because only students are strategic, student-proposing DA is *strategy-proof* for students — the fact that drove its adoption by real school districts.

## 4 Deep Dive

### (a) The Marriage Model: Stability, the Lattice, and Proposer-Optimality

Beyond existence, the set of stable matchings *S(P)* has striking algebraic structure. Conway, as cited by Knuth, showed that under strict preferences *S(P)* forms a **distributive lattice** under the unanimous order for men *≥_M*, dual to the lattice under *≥_W*: the matching giving each man the better of two partners (each woman the worse) is itself stable [2]. A corollary is the *polarity of interests*: *μ ≥_M ν* iff *ν ≥_W μ* — uniform gains for one side hurt the other.

At the extremes sit two distinguished matchings: the **man-optimal** (*M*-optimal) matching *μ^M*, produced by men-proposing DA, in which *every* man gets his best achievable partner across all stable matchings, and the **woman-optimal** matching *μ^W*, produced by women-proposing DA. The lattice is exactly the terrain over which the two sides' interests trade off, and the choice of proposing side is the choice of whose welfare is prioritized.

### (b) The Deferred-Acceptance Algorithm: Correctness, Complexity, and the Choice of Proposing Side

The correctness proof of DA rests on two lemmas. First, *no proposer is ever rejected by an achievable partner*: if *w* rejects *m*, then *w* holds proposals from agents it prefers to *m*, each of whom can only end up with a partner it likes at least as well as *w* — so *w* is unachievable for *m* in any stable matching. Second, the final matching is stable: if *(m, w)* blocked the output, *m* must have proposed to *w* (since it prefers *w* to its assignment), and *w* must have rejected *m*, contradicting the first lemma [1][2].

Complexity is quadratic: each proposer proposes to each receiver at most once, so proposals are bounded by *|M| · |W|* — **O(n²)** for one-to-one, **O(|students| · |schools|)** for many-to-one admissions. The reference implementation below illustrates the mechanism and its cost:

```python
def deferred_acceptance(proposers, receivers, pref_p, pref_r, quota):
    """Student-proposing DA. pref_p[m]: receivers in preference order.
    pref_r[w]: proposers in priority order. Returns dict w -> held list."""
    rank = {w: {m: i for i, m in enumerate(pref_r[w])} for w in receivers}
    next_idx = {m: 0 for m in proposers}
    held = {w: [] for w in receivers}   # tentatively held proposals
    rejected = list(proposers)
    while rejected:
        m = rejected.pop()
        if next_idx[m] >= len(pref_p[m]):
            continue                    # m stays unmatched
        w = pref_p[m][next_idx[m]]
        next_idx[m] += 1
        held[w].append(m)
        held[w].sort(key=lambda x: rank[w][x])
        for loser in held[w][quota[w]:]:  # deferred: rejections are tentative
            rejected.append(loser)
        held[w] = held[w][:quota[w]]
    return held
```

*The choice of proposing side is the single most consequential design decision.* Student-proposing DA yields the student-optimal stable matching: every student gets her best stable outcome, while schools get their worst [1][3]. The NRMP ran for decades with hospitals proposing (hospital-optimal outcomes) before the 1997–98 redesign switched to applicant-proposing, because the proposing side captures the distributional advantage [2][9].

### (c) Incentives: Strategy-Proofness, the Impossibility Theorem, and the Rural Hospitals Theorem

A mechanism is **strategy-proof** if reporting true preferences is a dominant strategy. Matching theory delivers a sharp split:

- *The good news.* Men-proposing DA is strategy-proof **for the proposing side** (Dubins–Freedman 1981; Roth 1982). In school choice, where only students are strategic, student-proposing DA is strategy-proof outright [3].
- *The bad news.* No stable mechanism is strategy-proof for **both** sides (Roth 1982). In many-to-one admissions, no stable mechanism is college-strategy-proof when colleges have multiple seats [6]; receivers can also manipulate by misreporting *capacity*.

> **Theorem:** (Roth 1982, impossibility) There exists no mechanism that, for every preference profile, selects a stable matching and makes truthful preference revelation a dominant strategy for every agent on both sides of the market [6].

The **rural hospitals theorem** (Roth 1984, 1986; special case McVitie–Wilson 1970) constrains how much outcomes can vary across stable matchings despite the lattice structure:

> **Theorem:** (Rural Hospitals, Roth 1986) In every stable matching of a college-admissions market with responsive preferences, the *same students are matched*, and the *same number of positions* are filled at every college. Moreover, a college that does not fill its quota is matched with the *same set* of students in all stable matchings [6].

The theorem's name reflects its motivation: no choice of stable mechanism can change *how many* positions a rural hospital fills — only *which* residents fill them. In the one-to-one marriage model it reduces to the classic result that the set of unmatched agents is invariant across all stable matchings.

### (d) School Choice in Practice: The Boston Mechanism's Failures, NYC and Boston DA Adoption, and the TTC–DA Tradeoff

The **Boston mechanism** (immediate acceptance) processes applications in rounds where acceptances are *permanent*: students apply to their first choice; schools admit up to capacity by priority; rejected students apply to their second choice next round — but popular schools may already be filled by students who ranked them first [3][9]. A student who lists a reach school first and is rejected can lose her safe second choice to a lower-priority student who listed it first. The mechanism is *not* strategy-proof: it punishes honest rank-order lists and rewards sophisticated gaming, systematically disadvantaging less-informed families [3][4].

| Property | Boston mechanism | Deferred acceptance (student-proposing) | Top trading cycles |
|---|---|---|---|
| Strategy-proof for students | No — manipulation can help | **Yes** | **Yes** |
| Eliminates justified envy | No | **Yes** (stable) | No |
| Pareto efficient (students) | No (in equilibrium) | Not always | **Yes** |
| Acceptance timing | Immediate (round-by-round) | Deferred to the end | Cycles cleared iteratively |
| Who benefits | Sophisticated strategists | Honest, high-priority students | Students with tradable priority |

Theoretical critique had direct policy consequences. In **2005, Boston replaced the Boston mechanism with student-proposing DA** [9], after Abdulkadiroğlu and Sönmez presented alternatives to Boston Public Schools. New York City had moved earlier: in **2003, NYC adopted DA** for its non-selective high schools, replacing a decentralized system in which students could receive multiple offers and schools over-admitted strategically. The documented effect was dramatic: the number of students receiving no offer from any ranked school fell from roughly **30,000 in 2003 to about 3,000 in 2004** [9].

The remaining design question is the **DA–TTC tradeoff**. TTC lets students trade their priority positions at schools in cycles, producing an outcome that is Pareto efficient for students and strategy-proof, but that may *violate* no-justified-envy [3]. DA eliminates justified envy and Pareto-dominates every other envy-free matching, but is not fully Pareto efficient. No mechanism has all three properties; every major district to date has chosen DA [3][9].

### (e) Frontiers: Matching with Couples, Large-Market Strategy-Proofness, Kidney Exchange, and Refugee Resettlement

**Matching with couples.** The NRMP must accommodate married couples who submit joint rank-order lists over *pairs* of positions — preferences with complementarities that break the substitutability on which existence rests. Roth (1984) showed stable matchings may not exist with couples, and deciding existence is NP-complete (Rønn 1990). Klaus and Klijn showed that when couples' preferences are *responsive* (unilateral improvements for one partner never hurt the couple), DA recovers a stable matching — but slight deviations from responsiveness can destroy existence [7]. The Roth–Peranson NRMP redesign (1999) handles couples with a heuristic DA variant that has consistently found matchings stable with respect to reported preferences [2][7].

**Large-market strategy-proofness.** Empirically, manipulability of stable mechanisms appears rare in large markets — Roth and Peranson's (1999) simulations of the NRMP suggested the mechanism was "very close to strategy-proof" in practice [2]. Azevedo and Budish (2019) formalized this as **strategy-proofness in the large (SP-L)**: truthful reporting becomes *approximately* optimal as the market grows, under full-support distributions of opponent reports [6]. With a continuum of students, stable matchings correspond to supply-and-demand equilibria with unique cutoffs — the core *converges* and strategic incentives attenuate [6].

**Kidney exchange.** When a willing donor is immunologically incompatible with her intended recipient, pairs of incompatible patient–donor pairs can swap donors. Roth, Sönmez, and Ünver (2004) modeled this as a housing market with existing tenants and proposed the **top trading cycles and chains (TTCC)** mechanism: patients point to their most-preferred compatible kidney (or the waiting-list option *w*), kidneys point to their paired patients, and cycles — plus chains initiated by altruistic non-directed donors — are cleared iteratively [5]. In simulations, the mechanism reduced the fraction of patients with willing donors who remain untransplanted from roughly **45% to under 10%** [5]. The design went live as the New England Program for Kidney Exchange (NEPKE) in 2004, followed by a 2007 amendment to the National Organ Transplant Act and a U.S. national pilot in 2010 [5].

**Refugee resettlement.** The newest frontier extends matching to *multidimensional knapsack* constraints: families have sizes across services (school places, medical needs, housing), and localities have multidimensional capacities. Delacrétaz, Kominers, and Teytelboym (2023) show standard stability may not exist here and is computationally intractable when it does; they introduce **weak envy-freeness** and a **Knapsack Deferred Acceptance (KDA)** algorithm that finds a family-optimal weakly envy-free matching — but KDA is manipulable, and they prove no family-optimal, weakly envy-free, strategy-proof mechanism exists [8]. A **Threshold KDA** variant recovers strategy-proofness at an efficiency cost, and simulations on HIAS resettlement data suggest preference-based mechanisms can substantially improve match efficiency while respecting community priorities [8].

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

We collect the quantitative and proof-theoretic record in one place.

**Deployed-mechanism outcomes.** New York City's 2003 adoption of DA for non-selective high schools reduced unmatched students from ~30,000 to ~3,000 in a single year [9]. Boston's 2005 switch from the Boston mechanism to DA was followed by the mechanism's spread to Denver, New Orleans, Washington D.C., Newark, and Indianapolis [9]. In kidney exchange, the TTCC mechanism's simulation gains (45% → under 10% untransplanted) preceded real programs that have since facilitated tens of thousands of transplants [5]. The NRMP's Roth–Peranson redesign has consistently produced matchings stable with respect to reported preferences even with 5–10% couples [2][7].

**Proof sketch: proposer-optimality of DA.** Call a receiver *w* *achievable* for *m* if some stable matching pairs them. We show no proposer is ever rejected by an achievable partner: suppose *w* is the first receiver to reject an achievable proposer *m*, holding *m'* whom *w* prefers. Some stable *μ* pairs *m* with *w*; *w* prefers *m'* to *m*, and *m'* — never before rejected by an achievable partner — prefers *w* to his partner in *μ*. Then *(m', w)* blocks *μ*, a contradiction. Every proposer thus ends with his best achievable partner — the *M*-optimal stable matching [1][2].

**Proof sketch: the rural hospitals theorem (one-to-one case).** McVitie and Wilson (1970) proved the special case: all stable matchings match the same sets of agents. Suppose *μ* and *μ'* are stable and some man *m₀* is matched in *μ* but unmatched in *μ'*. Since *μ'* is stable and *m₀* prefers any acceptable partner to being unmatched, *m₀*'s partner *w₁ = μ(m₀)* must prefer her *μ'*-partner *m₁* to *m₀* (else *(m₀, w₁)* blocks *μ'*). Since *μ* is stable, *m₁* must prefer his *μ*-partner *w₂* to *w₁* (else *(m₁, w₁)* blocks *μ*). Chaining this argument alternately through *μ'* and *μ* preferences produces an infinite sequence of distinct agents — impossible in a finite market. The contradiction establishes invariance of the matched set; Roth's generalization extends the argument to quotas via responsive preferences [6].

**Large-market asymptotics.** Kojima and Pathak (2009) showed that as the market grows with a fixed number of schools, the set of stable matchings collapses and the college-optimal stable mechanism becomes approximately strategy-proof for students — the theoretical counterpart to Roth and Peranson's empirical near-strategy-proofness [6]. Azevedo and Budish's SP-L theorem generalizes this: any mechanism whose outcomes depend only on "price-like" statistics of the report distribution is approximately strategy-proof in large markets [6].

## 6 Limitations and Open Problems

The theory's limits are as instructive as its successes.

1. **Couples and complementarities.** Existence fails with general couple preferences, and checking existence is NP-complete. The NRMP's success relies on heuristics and favorable market structure; a principled, strategy-proof, stable mechanism for couples markets remains out of reach [7].
2. **Efficiency vs. fairness.** In school choice, no mechanism is simultaneously strategy-proof, Pareto efficient, and envy-free. Districts have chosen DA's fairness, but the efficiency loss relative to TTC is real and only partially quantified [3].
3. **Receiving-side incentives.** Colleges and hospitals can manipulate stable mechanisms via preference misreports and capacity withholding; strategy-proofness in the large offers asymptotic comfort, not finite-market guarantees [6].
4. **Complex constraints.** Refugee resettlement shows that multidimensional capacities break the DA toolkit: stability may not exist, KDA is manipulable, and the efficiency–incentives tradeoff is provable, not merely conjectural [8].
5. **Open questions** (Roth 2008) include: mechanisms stable *and* efficient under indifferences, strategic behavior of couples in the NRMP, dynamic matching with arrivals, and integrating machine-learned priorities (e.g., employment-likelihood scores for refugees) with incentive guarantees [2][8].

---

## 7 Conclusion

From a four-page note on marriage in 1962, stable matching grew into the operating system of some of society's most consequential allocation systems [1]. Its arc is unusually complete: a solution concept (stability), a constructive algorithm (deferred acceptance), a structural theory (the lattice and the rural hospitals theorem), a precise map of incentive limits, and successful translations into practice — the NRMP, New York and Boston school choice, kidney exchange [2][3][5][9]. Yet the frontiers remain hard: couples break existence, capacity constraints break strategy-proofness, and multidimensional resettlement problems force new tradeoffs between efficiency and incentives [7][8]. The enduring lesson is methodological: when the designer's desiderata are stated exactly — stability, strategy-proofness, efficiency — the mathematics tells us which combinations are achievable, and policy can choose its tradeoff with eyes open.
