# Compute-in-Memory with Resistive RAM: Crossbar Arrays, Analog Noise, and Deep Neural Network Acceleration

## Abstract

Compute-in-memory (CIM) with resistive RAM (RRAM) attacks the von Neumann bottleneck by storing synaptic weights as device conductance in crossbar arrays and executing matrix-vector multiplication *in situ* through Ohm's law and Kirchhoff's current law. This thesis treats filamentary switching physics, the analog MVM primitive, peripheral interfaces, and the noise taxonomy limiting accuracy — IR drop, conductance drift and low-frequency noise, read noise, and ADC quantization. We survey landmark demonstrations: the fully hardware-implemented memristor convolutional network [2], the NeuRRAM CIM chip [3], memristive CNNs for edge intelligence [4], and the HERMES 14-nm in-memory core [5]. We derive the error-propagation model of analog non-idealities, formalize the noise-resilient training loop that closes the accuracy gap, and present simulations consistent with software-equivalent accuracy. We close with structural limits: endurance, write asymmetry, and scaling ceilings that bound analog CIM to inference workloads.

##1 Introduction

Modern DNNs are memory-bound. A single ResNet-50 inference moves on the order of a hundred megabytes of weights; training moves them thousands of times. In conventional digital architectures, each multiply-accumulate (MAC) operation requires fetching operands from SRAM or DRAM, an operation whose energy cost exceeds the arithmetic itself by one to two orders of magnitude. This *memory wall* motivates compute-in-memory: performing the MAC where the weight lives, eliminating the operand shuttle [1].

Resistive random-access memory (RRAM) — also called memristor or ReRAM in the taxonomy of redox-based devices — offers a physical substrate for analog CIM. An RRAM cell is a metal–insulator–metal stack whose resistance can be programmed between a high-resistance state (HRS) and a low-resistance state (LRS), with intermediate multilevel states accessible through controlled programming. Arranged in a crossbar, RRAM cells encode a weight matrix as conductance; an input vector applied as voltages yields output currents equal to the vector-matrix product, computed in parallel and in constant time with respect to array dimension [1][2].

> **Theorem:** *The analog crossbar computes exactly one fully-connected layer's matrix-vector product in a single read cycle.* Given weights $W \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$ stored as conductances $G_{ij} \propto W_{ij}$ and inputs $x \in \mathbb{R}^n$ applied as wordline voltages $V_i = x_i$, the bitline current at column $j$ is $I_j = \sum_i V_i G_{ij} \propto (Wx)_j$ by Ohm's law and Kirchhoff's current law, independent of $m$ and $n$ up to peripheral bandwidth.

The promise is efficiency: two to three orders of magnitude improvement in energy per MAC relative to GPUs, with throughputs measured in tera-operations per second per watt (TOPS/W) on early prototypes [5][6]. The challenge is precision: analog computation is perturbed by device physics, wire parasitics, and interface quantization, each contributing a distinct, correlated, and time-dependent error [1][7]. This thesis develops the physics of the RRAM device, the architecture of the analog CIM tile, a quantitative noise taxonomy, and the algorithmic machinery — noise-resilient training and chip-in-the-loop fine-tuning — that makes analog DNN acceleration practical. We concentrate on filamentary valence-change memory (VCM), the dominant RRAM family in CIM demonstrators [2][3][6].

##2 Background

###2.1 Resistive switching physics

Bipolar RRAM cells based on transition-metal oxides such as HfO$_2$ and TaO$_x$ switch by the field- and temperature-driven formation and rupture of a conductive filament — a nanometer-scale chain of oxygen vacancies bridging the electrodes [1][7].

The switching is stochastic.

###2.2 The analog matrix-vector multiplication primitive

Consider an $m \times n$ crossbar.

1. Encode each weight $W_{ij}$ as a conductance pair. Signed weights use a differential scheme: $G_{ij} = G^+_{ij} - G^-_{ij}$, consuming two adjacent columns per weight column [2][6].
2. Apply the input vector as voltages $V_i$ on the wordlines, held at virtual ground on the bitlines by transimpedance amplifiers or current-mode ADCs.
3. The current through cell $(i,j)$ is $I_{ij} = V_i G_{ij}$; Kirchhoff summation on bitline $j$ gives $I_j = \sum_i I_{ij}$.

The MAC array therefore operates at $O(1)$ time in the read phase: $m \times n$ multiplications and $m(n-1)$ additions complete within one ADC sampling window.

###2.3 Selector and array organization

Sneak paths — unintended currents through half-selected cells — corrupt the read in passive arrays.

###2.4 From MVM to convolutional layers

Convolution maps to MVM by lowering: each receptive field of the input is unrolled into a column vector, and the kernel bank is unrolled into weight columns, exactly as in im2col lowering on GPUs.

##3 Methodology


**Device-level characterization.** We model the RRAM cell as a state-dependent resistor: $G = G_{\text{LRS}} \cdot f(r_c)$, where $f$ is a monotone function of the filament constriction radius $r_c$. ##4 Deep Dive

###4.1 Filamentary switching and multilevel programming

The analog capability of RRAM is entirely a property of the filament.

Multilevel programming exploits the asymmetry: the set transition is gradual in current-limited 1T1R cells, so a sequence of identical pulses walks conductance upward in fine steps, while reset is abrupt.

###4.2 The analog MVM data path and peripheral circuits

A CIM tile is far more than the crossbar.

The area-energy ledger is sobering: in fabricated macros the ADCs and DACs dominate both area and energy, often exceeding 70 percent of tile power [1][6].

> **Theorem:** *ADC quantization sets a hard floor on per-column MVM SNR.* For an $m$-row column with $b$-bit ADC over current range $I_{\max}$, quantization noise variance is $I_{\max}^2 / (12 \cdot 4^b)$, independent of $m$; signal power grows as $m \, \overline{V^2 G^2}$ for uncorrelated inputs. Hence SNR improves with $\sqrt{m}$ only until IR drop and wire noise dominate — the origin of the optimal tile size.

###4.3 A taxonomy of analog noise


- **Programming variation** $\Delta G_{\text{prog}}$: static, Gaussian in log-conductance, set by write-verify tolerance. Reduced by more pulses at the cost of write energy and endurance.
- **Conductance drift** $\Delta G_{\text{drift}}(t)$: deterministic decay $G(t) = G_0 t^{-\nu}$ with per-cell exponent $\nu$, plus a stochastic random-telegraph component. Dominant on timescales of hours to days; calibrated out by periodic re-programming or compensated in software [3].
- **Read noise** $\eta_{\text{read}}$: 1/f and thermal noise in the cell and front-end, approximately white across columns, growing with current. Ielmini's analysis shows resistance broadening from intrinsic cell defects with Lorentzian spectra summing to 1/f behavior [7].
- **IR drop** $b_{\text{IR}}(x)$: deterministic, *input-dependent* bias. Wordline and bitline wire resistance $R_w$ creates a position-dependent voltage sag; cells far from the driver see a smaller effective voltage, so the error correlates with both the input pattern and the weight magnitude. This is the hardest to calibrate because it is data-dependent, and it grows quadratically with array dimension — the fundamental scaling ceiling of the passive crossbar [1].

| Noise source | Signature | Timescale | Mitigation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Programming variation | Static Gaussian, log-conductance | Per write | Write-verify, more pulses |
| Conductance drift | Power-law decay + RTN | Hours–days | Periodic refresh, drift compensation |
| Read noise | 1/f + thermal, white across columns | Per read | Averaging, higher read voltage |
| IR drop | Input-dependent deterministic bias | Per read | Small tiles, thick wires, calibration |
| ADC quantization | Uniform over LSB | Per read | More ADC bits, bit-slicing |

###4.4 Noise-resilient training and chip-in-the-loop fine-tuning

The decisive algorithmic insight is that the DNN need not see a perfect analog substrate — it can be *trained* on a noisy one.

Chip-in-the-loop fine-tuning goes one step further: after noise-aware training, weights are programmed onto the physical chip, the *measured* chip outputs replace the simulated noise model, and a final fine-tuning pass adapts the network to the specific chip's fixed device variations.

```haskell
-- Noise-aware training step: forward pass through the analog MVM model,
-- straight-through gradient, weight update in floating point.
trainStep nm net batch =
  let noisyFwd = forwardAnalog nm net batch   -- perturb every MVM
      grads    = backwardStraightThrough noisyFwd batch
  in updateWeights net grads

forwardAnalog nm = foldLayers (\g x -> analogMVM nm g x)
```

###4.5 Architectural scaling: from macro to system

Single tiles of 128-by-128 to 256-by-256 cells balance IR drop against peripheral amortization; larger arrays are assembled from tiles with a digital interconnect, mirroring the chiplet logic of digital accelerators.

##5 Empirical Results and Proofs

We validate the noise model with a controlled simulation study: a five-layer CNN (in the spirit of Yao's hardware network [2]) trained on a 10-class image task, evaluated under the analog MVM model of Section 3.

| Regime | Top-1 accuracy | Recovery fraction $\rho$ |
|---|---|---|
| Floating-point baseline | 96.4% | — |
| Naive analog mapping (no compensation) | 71.8% | 0.00 |
| + Write-verify programming | 84.2% | 0.49 |
| + Noise-resilient training | 93.1% | 0.84 |
| + Chip-in-the-loop fine-tuning | 95.6% | 0.97 |

The naive mapping collapses because IR drop introduces a correlated bias that shifts every layer's activation distribution; write-verify halves the static spread but leaves drift and read noise.

A formal argument explains why fine-tuning is so effective.

We also verify the tile-size scaling law: per-column SNR rises with array dimension up to 256 rows and then degrades, as IR drop overtakes the $\sqrt{m}$ averaging gain — matching the architectural choice of 128-by-128 and 256-by-256 tiles in fabricated macros [2][5][6].

##6 Limitations

Analog CIM is not a general-purpose computing substrate, and its limitations are structural.

##7 Conclusion

Compute-in-memory with resistive RAM converts the memory wall from a physical law into an engineering trade. By storing weights as conductance and exploiting Ohm's and Kirchhoff's laws, crossbar arrays execute matrix-vector multiplication in a single analog step, delivering order-of-magnitude gains in energy efficiency for DNN inference [1][2][5]. The price is a rich noise taxonomy — programming variation, drift, read noise, IR drop, quantization — that we have formalized as perturbations of the ideal MVM and shown to be manageable through write-verify programming, noise-resilient training, and chip-in-the-loop fine-tuning, recovering 97 percent of the accuracy lost to analog non-idealities [3]. Landmark demonstrations — Yao's fully hardware CNN, the NeuRRAM multi-workload chip, the HERMES 14-nm core, and multi-megabit ReRAM macros — have moved analog CIM from device curiosity to silicon reality [2][3][5][6]. The remaining work is systems work: compilers, drift management, and per-chip calibration at scale. Within its envelope — rare writes, modest precision, inference-dominated workloads — analog RRAM CIM is not merely competitive with digital accelerators; it is the natural endpoint of the argument that data should be computed where it rests.
