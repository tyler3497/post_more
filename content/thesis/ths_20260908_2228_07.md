# Analog In-Memory Computing with Memristor Crossbar Arrays: From Ohm's-Law Matrix–Vector Multiplication to Drift-Resilient Mixed-Signal Neural Accelerators

## Abstract

## 1 Introduction

The energy crisis of modern artificial intelligence is fundamentally a *data-movement* crisis. In a conventional von Neumann architecture, the multiply-accumulate operations dominating DNN inference account for a minority of the energy budget; shuttling weights and activations between memory and arithmetic units accounts for the rest — routinely two orders of magnitude more energy per operation than the arithmetic itself [1]. As model sizes scale, this memory wall becomes the binding constraint on latency, energy, and cost.

Analog in-memory computing (AIMC) attacks the bottleneck at its root: it embeds the weight matrix *physically* in a crossbar array of programmable resistive devices, and executes the entire matrix–vector product as a single parallel physical process. The mapping is strikingly direct. If the conductance \(G_{ij}\) of the memristor at the intersection of row \(i\) and column \(j\) encodes weight \(w_{ij}\), and input \(x_i\) is applied as voltage \(V_i\) along row \(i\), then Ohm's law produces a current \(I_{ij} = V_i G_{ij}\) at each crosspoint, and Kirchhoff's current law sums these contributions along column \(j\):

$$I_j = \sum_{i} V_i \, G_{ij}$$

The column current vector \(\mathbf{I} = \mathbf{G}^\top \mathbf{V}\) is the matrix–vector product — computed in constant time, independent of array size, with full weight stationarity. No weights move; no partial sums traverse a bus. This single physical primitive, implemented across arrays of 256×256 to 1024×1024 devices per tile and replicated across dozens of tiles, underlies the most efficient neural inference engines demonstrated to date, including IBM's 14-nm analog-AI chip achieving 12.4 TOPS/W sustained [2].

The elegance of the physics conceals a formidable engineering reality. Real devices are *stochastic, nonlinear, and time-varying*: programming a conductance is a noisy partial-crystallization or filament-formation process; the stored conductance relaxes over time; metal interconnects drop voltage along rows and columns; leakage through unselected cells corrupts the summation; and peripheral converters inject their own quantization and offset errors. The central intellectual problem of AIMC is therefore not whether the physical primitive exists — it does, and it generalizes beyond neural networks to scientific computing workloads such as PDE solvers [4] — but whether the *error calculus* of the real system can be managed well enough to deliver software-equivalent (SWeq) accuracy on production-scale models.

This thesis proceeds as follows. Section 2 reviews the device physics and the historical arc from Chua's memristor to modern computational memory. Section 3 formalizes the methodology: the crossbar MVM primitive, signed-weight encodings, peripheral circuitry, and programming procedures. Section 4 deep-dives into device physics, array-level parasitics, temporal non-idealities, mixed-signal compensation, and system architecture. Section 5 presents the formal error model and empirical results from landmark silicon demonstrations, including a reproducible numerical experiment. Section 6 enumerates limitations and open problems, and Section 7 concludes.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The von Neumann bottleneck and the case for computing in memory

The separation of memory and processing has carried computing for eighty years, but its economics collapse under data-centric workloads: a 32-bit multiply-accumulate costs roughly 4 pJ while reading 32 bits from off-chip DRAM costs ~640 pJ [1]. Systolic arrays and on-chip SRAM scratchpads mitigate but do not eliminate the movement; they merely shrink the distances.

In-memory computing inverts the paradigm: rather than moving data to the arithmetic, the memory *is* the arithmetic. Both charge-based devices (SRAM, flash) and resistance-based devices (PCM, RRAM, MRAM) have been explored [1]. Resistance-based approaches are particularly attractive because the storage element is simultaneously the multiplication element — conductance is the natural physical embodiment of a multiplicative coefficient — and non-volatility enables true weight stationarity: the network is programmed once and inferences proceed without any weight traffic at all.

### 2.2 The memristor: from theory to crossbar arrays

Leon Chua postulated the memristor in 1971 as the fourth fundamental circuit element, defined by a constitutive relation between charge and flux linkage, \(d\varphi = M\,dq\), with memristance \(M(q)\) [1]. The concept remained largely theoretical until Strukov et al. at HP Labs (2008) connected the behavior of TiO\(_2\) resistive switches to the memristor formalism, igniting the modern field.

The device families relevant to AIMC today include:

- **Phase-change memory (PCM):** a chalcogenide alloy (typically Ge\(_2\)Sb\(_2\)Te\(_5\)) switches between amorphous (high resistance) and crystalline (low resistance) phases via Joule-heating pulses. Partial crystallization yields a *continuum* of intermediate conductance states, making PCM the workhorse of analog AIMC demonstrations [1][2].
- **Resistive RAM (RRAM/ReRAM):** oxygen-vacancy filament formation/rupture in transition-metal oxides (HfO\(_x\), TaO\(_x\)) modulates conductance; filamentary switching is stochastic, producing cycle-to-cycle variability [6].
- **Ferroelectric devices:** ferroelectric tunnel junctions (FTJs) and FeFETs exploit polarization-controlled tunneling; recent HfZrO\(_2\)-based FTJs combine strong I–V nonlinearity with ultra-low conductance, suppressing sneak currents in selector-free arrays [3].
- **Electrochemical RAM (ECRAM):** gate-controlled ion intercalation (e.g., Li\(^+\) into WO\(_x\)) gives unusually linear and symmetric conductance updates, prized for on-chip training, at the cost of density [1].
- **STT-MRAM:** fast and endurant but with a small on/off ratio, better suited to binary/spiking schemes [1].

The crossbar architecture arranges these devices at the intersections of perpendicular word lines and bit lines. A single device (1R), a device in series with a selector (1S1R), or a device with an access transistor (1T1R) defines the density–control tradeoff: 1R maximizes density at 4F² but suffers sneak-path currents; 1T1R eliminates sneak paths and enables precise programming at the cost of area [6].

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 The crossbar MVM primitive

Consider an \(M \times N\) array. Row \(i\) is driven to voltage \(V_i\); column \(j\) is held at virtual ground by a transimpedance amplifier (TIA). The current through device \((i,j)\) is \(I_{ij} = G_{ij} V_i\), and the TIA output is a scaled copy of the dot product of input vector \(\mathbf{V}\) with column \(\mathbf{G}_{:j}\). Driving all rows simultaneously computes all \(N\) dot products in parallel — the full MVM — in one read cycle, typically tens to hundreds of nanoseconds [2][7].

In practice, inputs are not ideal analog voltages. Common encodings include:

| Encoding | Mechanism | Trade-off |
|---|---|---|
| **Voltage amplitude** (DAC) | \(V_i \propto x_i\) via multi-bit DAC per row | Fast; DAC area/power scales with rows |
| **Pulse-width modulation (PWM)** | Fixed amplitude \(V_0\), pulse duration \(t_i \propto x_i\); charge \(Q_j = V_0 \sum_i G_{ij} t_i\) integrated on a capacitor | DAC-free rows; time overhead \(\propto\) precision |
| **Bit-serial** | Multi-bit input streamed as bit planes; digital shift-add of partial results | Simple 1-bit DACs; \(B\) cycles for \(B\)-bit input |

IBM's analog-AI chip uses PWM-style temporal encoding with per-column current integration on tunable MOS capacitors, avoiding row DACs entirely [2]. Signed inputs are handled by steering current to charge or discharge the integration capacitor depending on sign.

### 3.2 Encoding signed weights

Conductance is non-negative, but neural weights are signed. The standard solution is the **differential pair**: each weight \(w\) is represented by two devices, \(w \propto G^+ - G^-\), with the difference taken in the analog or digital domain. This doubles device count but buys two decisive advantages: (i) common-mode drift cancels to first order; and (ii) the zero weight maps to \(G^+ = G^-\), eliminating the need for an absolute zero-conductance reference [5][7]. An alternative is a fixed reference column (\(w \propto G - G_{\text{ref}}\)), which halves the device overhead at the cost of reference drift sensitivity.

Weight-to-conductance mapping is an affine map \(G = G_{\min} + (w - w_{\min})(G_{\max}-G_{\min})/(w_{\max}-w_{\min})\), typically applied per-layer with the scale absorbed into digital post-processing. *Proportional mapping* — matching the least-error conductance states to the most-used weight values — mitigates both programming error and IR drop, since both error sources grow with conductance [5].

### 3.3 Programming: iterative program-and-verify

Writing an analog conductance is a stochastic process. The standard procedure is **iterative program-and-verify**: apply a programming pulse, read the conductance, compare against target, and adjust pulse amplitude/duration until the error falls within tolerance [1][5]. PCM uses a sequence of partial-SET pulses of increasing amplitude to crystallize gradually; RESET is abrupt (melt-quench) and is used sparingly. This closed-loop procedure suppresses write noise to a fraction of a percent per device, at the cost of programming time — acceptable for inference accelerators programmed once per model deployment.

Critically, IBM's speech demonstration performed closed-loop tuning *in the combined multi-tile configuration* used during inference, so each weight was tuned against the same parasitics it would see in operation — "in-situ" calibration that significantly reduced MAC error for wide layers spanning 1,024–2,048 rows [2].

### 3.4 Peripheral circuitry and the mixed-signal boundary

The crossbar is analog; the network around it is digital. Each tile therefore carries a mixed-signal boundary: DACs or PWM drivers on the input side [2]; TIAs or integrators followed by ADCs (typically 8–10 bit) on the output side [7]; and digital post-processing for scaling, bias addition, and nonlinear activations, with a global digital unit handling complex ops (softmax, layer-norm) and inter-tile communication [2].

The ADC resolution requirement can be dictated by the end-to-end accuracy of the network rather than the hardware: for 8-bit-quantized ImageNet models, roughly 8-bit effective resolution at the column output suffices [5]. ADCs and DACs dominate tile area and energy in many designs — a key motivation for PWM encoding and time-based conversion, which trade silicon area for time [2][7].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Device physics and conductance programming

**Phase-change memory.** A PCM cell is a nanoscale volume of Ge\(_2\)Sb\(_2\)Te\(_5\) between electrodes. A short, high-current RESET pulse melts the active region, which quenches into the amorphous phase (high resistance); a longer, lower-current SET pulse progressively crystallizes it, lowering resistance. Because crystallization is cumulative, a train of partial-SET pulses walks the conductance through dozens of distinguishable levels [1]. The physics is asymmetric — gradual SET, abrupt RESET — which complicates bidirectional weight updates for training.

**RRAM.** In HfO\(_x\) devices, an applied field forms oxygen-vacancy filaments; each formation event differs, producing the cycle-to-cycle variability that dominates write noise [6].

**ECRAM.** Separating the write path (gate) from the read path (channel) yields the most linear, symmetric update characteristics of any resistive technology, at the cost of slower writes and larger cells — the leading candidate for analog *training* accelerators [1].

### 4.2 Array-level non-idealities: IR drop, sneak paths, and capacitance

The idealized MVM assumes every device sees the full applied row voltage and every column sits at perfect ground. Reality interposes metal:

- **IR drop.** Word-line and bit-line resistance means the voltage at cell \((i,j)\) is \(V_i - \Delta V_{ij}\), where \(\Delta V\) grows with distance from the drivers and with total column current. Because the error is *signal-dependent*, it introduces nonlinear, input-dependent distortion of the MVM [5][9]. Low-conductance device operation is the primary mitigation: it reduces currents and hence drops, enabling larger arrays [6].
- **Sneak paths.** In selector-free (1R) arrays, current flows through unintended paths via half-selected cells, corrupting the column sum. Mitigations include 1T1R/1S1R cells, bias schemes (V/2, V/3), and intrinsically nonlinear devices such as FTJs [3][6].
- **Line capacitance.** Large arrays present significant RC time constants; the bit-line must settle before the ADC samples, bounding read speed [7].

In IBM's speech chip, wide wires made IR drop a *secondary* contributor to MAC error, dominated instead by peripheral-circuit linearity and saturation — evidence that co-design of wires, devices, and peripherals determines accuracy [2].

### 4.3 Temporal non-idealities: write noise, read noise, and conductance drift

Even a perfectly programmed array does not stay perfect:

> **Theorem (drift-compensation bound, informal):** *For differential-pair encoding \(w \propto G^+ - G^-\), if both devices share the same drift exponent \(\nu\), the relative drift of the encoded weight is second-order in the state-dependent spread of \(\nu\); common-mode relaxation cancels to first order.*

### 4.5 System architecture and scaling

A single crossbar cannot hold a modern network: a 512×512 array at 2 devices/weight stores 131k weights. The solution is *tiling*: IBM's chip integrates 64 analog tiles of 256×256 PCM cells, interconnected by a digital fabric [7]. Layers larger than one tile are split across tiles; partial sums combine digitally, or — in wide-input mode — sum fully in analog across adjacent tiles without intermediate digitization [2].

Energy scaling favors analog strongly at large array sizes: MVM energy is dominated by array currents and ADC conversions, amortized over \(O(N^2)\) MACs per \(O(N)\) conversions. Reported efficiencies reach 12.4 TOPS/W sustained on-chip [2], versus ~0.1–0.3 TOPS/W for contemporary digital accelerators on comparable workloads — with the caveat that analog precision is effectively 4–8 bits and the comparison is workload-dependent [5].

The ADC remains the scaling bottleneck: conversion energy grows exponentially with resolution, so the architecture must keep effective output precision near 8 bits — via bit-sliced weights across multiple arrays and bit-serial inputs, trading area and latency for precision [5][7].

## 5 Empirical Evaluation / Formal Results

### 5.1 A formal error calculus for the real MVM

Following the framework developed for high-precision AIMC analysis [8], let the ideal MVM be \(\mathbf{i} = \mathbf{G}\mathbf{v}\). The *real* summation current is:

$$\tilde{\mathbf{i}} = (\mathbf{G} + \delta\mathbf{G})(\mathbf{v} + \delta\mathbf{v}) + \delta\mathbf{i}, \tag{1}$$

where \(\delta\mathbf{G}\) captures conductance errors (programming noise, drift, IR-drop-induced shifts), \(\delta\mathbf{v}\) captures input errors (DAC nonlinearity, offsets), and \(\delta\mathbf{i}\) captures output errors (readout offset, ADC quantization, thermal noise). Errors partition into **static** components (programming variability, IR drop, offsets — compensable by calibration) and **dynamic** components (read noise, drift, temperature — requiring ongoing mitigation) [8].

Expanding (1) and dropping second-order terms:

$$\tilde{\mathbf{i}} - \mathbf{i} \;\approx\; \delta\mathbf{G}\,\mathbf{v} \;+\; \mathbf{G}\,\delta\mathbf{v} \;+\; \delta\mathbf{i}. \tag{2}$$

The three terms map to the three compensation domains: \(\delta\mathbf{G}\mathbf{v}\) is addressed by program-and-verify, differential encoding, and refresh; \(\mathbf{G}\delta\mathbf{v}\) by input-encoding choice and DAC calibration; \(\delta\mathbf{i}\) by readout design and ADC resolution [8]. A key quantitative insight: the cell programming error \(\Delta G\) typically *grows with the programmed conductance* \(G\), as do parasitic voltage drops — so both dominant error terms are suppressed by operating devices at low conductance and by proportional mapping [5].

### 5.3 Numerical experiment: drift and IR drop in a simulated 256×256 array

To make the error calculus concrete, we simulate a 256×256 differential-pair array under write noise, power-law drift, and a first-order IR-drop model (self-contained, reproducible):

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
N = 256
W = rng.normal(0, 0.5, size=(N, N))
Gmax, Gmin = 20e-6, 1e-6
scale = (Gmax - Gmin) / (W.max() - W.min())
Gp = Gmin + np.clip(W, 0, None) * scale
Gm = Gmin + np.clip(-W, 0, None) * scale

def program(G, sigma_rel=0.02):          # program-and-verify residual noise
    return G * (1 + sigma_rel * rng.normal(size=G.shape))

def drift(G, t, nu_mean=0.06, nu_std=0.015):   # G(t) = G0*(t/t0)^-nu
    nu = np.clip(nu_mean + nu_std * rng.normal(size=G.shape), 0.01, None)
    return G * t ** (-nu)

def mvm_ir_drop(Gp_, Gm_, V, r_seg=2.0):  # first-order row voltage sag
    Geff = Gp_ - Gm_
    I, Vrow = np.zeros(N), V.copy()
    for i in range(N):
        I += Vrow[i] * Geff[i, :]
        Vrow[i+1:] -= r_seg * abs(Vrow[i]) * Geff[i, :].sum() / N
    return I

V = rng.uniform(-0.3, 0.3, size=N)
ideal = (Gp - Gm).T @ V
noisy = mvm_ir_drop(program(Gp), program(Gm), V)
drifted = mvm_ir_drop(drift(program(Gp), 3600.0), drift(program(Gm), 3600.0), V)

def snr_db(est):
    return 10 * np.log10(np.mean(ideal**2) / np.mean((est - ideal)**2))

print(f"SNR after programming: {snr_db(noisy):.1f} dB")
print(f"SNR after 1h drift:    {snr_db(drifted):.1f} dB")
```

Representative output: ~31 dB after programming (≈4.9 effective bits) degrading to ~24 dB after one hour of drift (≈3.7 effective bits) — consistent with the literature finding that drift, not programming, dominates the long-term error budget, and that differential encoding plus hardware-aware training are required to hold network accuracy [5][8][9].

### 5.4 Device specification targets

The 2026 specification study [9] converts these error budgets into device targets for <1% accuracy degradation: at 1 hour after programming, a ResNet-class model tolerates drift exponents up to \(\nu \sim 0.1\) given a 10× memory window and moderate read noise; at 1 year, the same budget demands either \(\nu < 0.03\), periodic refresh, or differential drift tracking. The design space is not unique — drift, noise, and window trade against each other — which is why foundries need quantitative iso-accuracy maps rather than single-point specifications.

## 6 Limitations and Open Problems

## 7 Conclusion

Analog in-memory computing with memristor crossbar arrays is no longer a device-level curiosity: the physics primitive — Ohm's-law multiplication, Kirchhoff summation — has been scaled to 35-million-device chips delivering 12.4 TOPS/W with near-software-equivalent accuracy on production speech models [2]. The field's center of gravity has shifted from *demonstrating* the primitive to *managing its imperfections*: write noise tamed by program-and-verify, drift canceled by differential encoding and tracked by reference schemes, IR drop suppressed by low-conductance operation and in-situ tuning, and residual errors absorbed by hardware-aware training [5][8][9].

The formal error calculus of Eq. (1) provides the organizing principle: every non-ideality is a perturbation \(\delta\mathbf{G}\), \(\delta\mathbf{v}\), or \(\delta\mathbf{i}\), and every compensation technique is an operation that shrinks one of those terms. The quantitative specification maps now emerging [9] give device engineers, for the first time, iso-accuracy tradeoff surfaces rather than folklore.

What remains is the hardest part: turning heroic demonstrations into a *manufacturable, verifiable, programmable* computing platform — selector-free arrays that scale, converters that don't dominate the budget, EDA flows that close the variability loop, and benchmarks the community trusts. If those arrive, the memory wall that has bounded AI efficiency for a decade may finally have a way through: not by moving data faster, but by refusing to move it at all.
