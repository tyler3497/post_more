# Large-Eddy Simulation of Wind Farm Wakes and Wake Steering: Actuator-Line Turbine Models, Atmospheric Stability, Wake Meandering, and Yaw-Based Flow Control

## Abstract

Large-eddy simulation (LES) is the de facto high-fidelity instrument for resolving coupled atmospheric boundary layer and wind farm wake dynamics. This thesis analyzes wind farm aerodynamics through the LES lens: rotating actuator-line and actuator-disk turbine parametrizations, Monin–Obukhov atmospheric stability stratification, wake meandering and recovery physics, and yaw-based wake steering control validated against field campaigns. Convective conditions accelerate wake recovery, raising downwind turbine power ratios by roughly 15–20% relative to neutral stratification [2], while stable stratification suppresses vertical entrainment, producing persistent wakes stretching 20–30 rotor diameters downstream [1, 5]. Actuator-line LES coupled with structural dynamics captures isolated loading events from ambient turbulence at least as significant as those from upstream wakes [2], establishing meandering — not mean deficit — as the dominant fatigue driver. Field-validated yaw steering yields 6–15% wake-loss reductions per turbine pair, with SOWFA/FLORIS predictions matching measured gains within uncertainty [6, 7, 9]. We derive the filtered momentum equations with rotor body forces, quantify steering sensitivity to wind-direction uncertainty, and discuss implications for stability-aware layout optimization.

## 1 Introduction

Wind turbines extract momentum from the atmospheric boundary layer (ABL) and deposit it as high-turbulence, low-velocity wake flow. In a wind farm, this deposition is not a local perturbation: wakes from upstream rows propagate downstream, impinging on downstream rotors where they reduce power capture and amplify unsteady loads. Understanding and controlling these wakes is the central aerodynamic problem of wind farm design, and its economic stakes are measured in percentage points of annual energy production across gigawatt-scale assets.

Two complementary control philosophies have emerged. The first is *layout optimization*: place turbines so that wake interactions are geometrically minimized for the site's wind rose. The second is *wind farm control*, in which the control setpoints of individual turbines are coordinated — most prominently through **wake steering**, where upstream turbines are intentionally yawed away from the wind to deflect their wakes laterally, trading a modest upstream power penalty for a large downstream power gain [3, 7].

Neither approach can be evaluated with low-order tools alone. The dominant physics — tip-vortex breakdown, dynamic wake meandering, stability-dependent entrainment, and the interaction of veered inflow with rotor rotation — are inherently unsteady, three-dimensional, and turbulence-resolving. Large-eddy simulation, in which the energy-containing eddies are resolved and only the sub-grid scales are modeled, is the natural high-fidelity instrument. This thesis develops the full LES formulation of wind farm aerodynamics: the filtered Navier–Stokes equations with actuator turbine forcing, atmospheric stability through Monin–Obukhov similarity, actuator-line and actuator-disk representations, the statistics of wake meandering, the mathematics of yaw steering, validation against field measurements, and the resulting lessons for layout optimization.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The atmospheric boundary layer and turbine inflows

The inflow to a wind turbine is not a uniform stream; it is a turbulent boundary layer whose mean profile, turbulence intensity, and coherent structures are governed by surface roughness and thermal stratification. The **Obukhov length** $L$ classifies stratification:

$$\frac{1}{L} = -\frac{\kappa g \overline{w'\theta'_v}}{\bar{\theta}_v u_\ast^3}$$

where $u_\ast$ is the friction velocity, $\theta_v$ the virtual potential temperature, and $\kappa \approx 0.41$ the von Kármán constant. Positive $L$ denotes stable stratification (suppressed turbulence, shallow boundary layer, strong shear and veer); negative $L$ denotes unstable/convective conditions (buoyant production of turbulence, deep mixed layer); $|L| \to \infty$ is the neutral limit [1, 4].

In stably stratified conditions the mean wind often *veers* — rotates clockwise with height in the Northern Hemisphere — following an Ekman-spiral structure. LES studies of actuator-disk turbines embedded in veering inflow show that the wake responds by stretching from a circular into an elliptical cross-section, with wake strength, width, and lateral deflection all depending on the interaction between the meridional Ekman component and the sense of rotor rotation [5].

### 2.2 Wake anatomy: deficit, recovery, meandering

The time-averaged wake behind a turbine is characterized by a velocity deficit $\Delta U(x, r)$ that decays downstream as ambient turbulence entrains momentum from the free stream. Recovery rates are strongly stability-dependent: in convective conditions buoyancy-driven turbulence accelerates mixing, so velocity deficits stabilize after only a few turbine rows, whereas in stable conditions deficits persist much farther downstream [1].

Superposed on the mean deficit is **wake meandering**: a large-scale, low-frequency lateral and vertical oscillation of the entire wake deficit region, driven by the passage of atmospheric-scale eddies. Meandering is responsible for the *intermittent* loading experienced by downstream turbines and is now understood as the dominant contributor to wake-induced fatigue [4]. LES characterization of meandering amplitude, spectra, and probability distributions in horizontal and vertical planes, under neutral and unstable ABL conditions with actuator-line turbines, provides the statistical basis for dynamic wake models [4].

### 2.3 Wake steering

Yawing a turbine by an angle $\gamma$ relative to the inflow produces two effects: (i) the rotor's power drops approximately as $\cos^p(\gamma)$ with $p \approx 2$–3, and (ii) the wake is deflected laterally by an angle $\theta \propto \sin(\gamma)$, exposing downstream turbines to higher-velocity flow [3]. Analytical models by Jiménez, Bastankhah–Porté-Agel, Qian–Ishihara, and Shapiro progressively refined the assumed deficit shape from top-hat to Gaussian profiles and improved deflection predictions [3]. Field campaigns at commercial onshore and offshore farms, plus commercial products such as Siemens Gamesa's Wake Adapt system, have demonstrated measurable energy gains — a 14-month offshore swap test recorded +0.64% measured versus +0.65% predicted gain [3].

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 Filtered equations with actuator forcing

We solve the incompressible, Boussinesq-filtered Navier–Stokes equations:

$$\frac{\partial \bar{u}_i}{\partial t} + \bar{u}_j \frac{\partial \bar{u}_i}{\partial x_j} = -\frac{1}{\rho}\frac{\partial \bar{p}}{\partial x_i} - \frac{\partial \tau_{ij}}{\partial x_j} - 2\epsilon_{ijk}\Omega_j \bar{u}_k + g\frac{\bar{\theta}_v - \theta_{v0}}{\theta_{v0}}\delta_{i3} + f_i$$

where $\tau_{ij} = \overline{u_i u_j} - \bar{u}_i\bar{u}_j$ is the sub-grid stress, closed with a Smagorinsky or dynamic Lagrangian model, and $f_i$ is the turbine body-force density [2, 4].

### 3.2 Actuator-line turbine model

Rather than resolving blades, the **actuator line (AL)** model represents each blade as a rotating line of distributed force computed from blade-element momentum theory. The sectional lift and drag,

$$dF_L = \tfrac{1}{2}\rho V_{rel}^2 c\, C_l(\alpha)\,dr, \qquad dF_D = \tfrac{1}{2}\rho V_{rel}^2 c\, C_d(\alpha)\,dr,$$

are projected onto the LES grid with a regularized Gaussian kernel of width $\varepsilon \approx 2\Delta x$ to avoid numerical instability. AL resolves tip and root vortices and their breakdown, making it the standard choice for near-wake studies, albeit at higher grid resolution cost than the **actuator disk (AD)**, which distributes thrust uniformly over the rotor area and is preferred for farm-scale far-wake simulations [2, 4, 5].

A canonical sampling implementation in pseudo-code:

```python
import numpy as np

def actuator_line_forces(blade_pts, U_field, omega, geom, eps):
    """blade_pts: Nx3 positions; U_field: interpolated velocity function."""
    F = np.zeros((len(blade_pts), 3))
    for k, p in enumerate(blade_pts):
        U = U_field(p)                       # trilinear sample from LES grid
        V_rel = U - np.cross([0, 0, omega], p)  # rotational frame
        V2 = np.dot(V_rel, V_rel)
        alpha = attack_angle(V_rel, geom.twist[k])
        Cl, Cd = airfoil_table(alpha, geom.foil[k])
        d = 0.5 * RHO * V2 * geom.chord[k] * geom.dr
        lift = d * Cl * lift_dir(V_rel); drag = d * Cd * drag_dir(V_rel)
        F[k] = lift + drag
    return gaussian_smear(F, blade_pts, eps)  # project onto grid
```

Coupling to a structural dynamics solver (aeroelastic beam model of blades, drivetrain, and tower) yields the coupled aero-servo-elastic framework used to quantify load statistics under atmospheric and wake turbulence [2].

### 3.3 Atmospheric inflow: precursor and synthetic methods

Realistic turbulence is generated either by a **precursor** periodic-domain simulation that develops the ABL to statistical equilibrium, or by the **synthetic eddy method (SEM)**. Ground momentum and heat fluxes are imposed via Monin–Obukhov similarity functions $\phi_m(\zeta)$, $\phi_h(\zeta)$ with stability parameter $\zeta = z/L$, enabling neutral, stable, and convective regimes in the same solver [1, 4].

### 3.4 Wind direction uncertainty treatment

Field winds vary. Steady-state steering predictions are corrected by convolving the power response with the wind-direction distribution $p(\phi)$, typically Gaussian with standard deviation $\sigma_{WD}$ of a few degrees, plus measurement bias. This correction is essential: neglecting it systematically overestimates achievable gains [6, 8].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Stability effects on wake velocity and turbulence structure

Lattice-Boltzmann LES studies with actuator-line turbines under varying stability reveal a systematic pattern: time-averaged velocity deficits and turbulence-intensity distributions stabilize after several turbine rows, with *higher* velocity deficit magnitude and turbulence intensity in stable conditions than in convective ones [1]. Turbulent kinetic energy (TKE) budget analysis shows the mechanism: in convective ABL, buoyancy production $g\overline{w'\theta'_v}/\bar{\theta}_v$ dominates TKE production and rapidly mixes the wake; in stable ABL, vertical shear $-\overline{u'w'}\,d\bar{U}/dz$ dominates while buoyancy destroys TKE, so wakes decay slowly [1].

> **Theorem (Stability ordering of wake persistence).** *For fixed turbine thrust coefficient and hub-height wind speed, the far-wake centerline velocity deficit $\Delta U_c(x)$ satisfies $\Delta U_c^{stable}(x) > \Delta U_c^{neutral}(x) > \Delta U_c^{convective}(x)$ for all $x \gtrsim 5D$, with the inequality widening downstream.*

This ordering propagates directly into farm design: stable nighttime conditions, common at onshore sites, are precisely when deep-array wake losses peak.

| Stratification | Obukhov length | Dominant TKE source | Wake recovery | Veer |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Stable | $0 < L < 500\,\mathrm{m}$ | Vertical shear | Slow (wake persists $>20D$) | Strong |
| Neutral | $\|L\| \to \infty$ | Shear production | Moderate | Weak |
| Convective/unstable | $-500 < L < 0\,\mathrm{m}$ | Buoyancy | Fast (deficit stabilizes in a few rows) | Minimal |

### 4.2 Rotor rotation, veer, and wake asymmetry

The interaction between rotor swirl and a veering inflow produces a subtle but measurable asymmetry. LES with clockwise-rotating, counter-clockwise-rotating, and non-rotating actuator disks in veered versus non-veered inflow shows that wake strength, extension, width, and deflection depend on the superposition of the meridional Ekman component with the rotor's rotational sense; without veer, rotation direction only marginally modifies the wake [5]. For a yawed turbine this asymmetry compounds the intentional steering deflection, meaning steering lookup tables calibrated in neutral, non-veering conditions carry a systematic bias in stable nighttime ABL — a first-order correction for closed-loop controllers.

### 4.3 Wake meandering: statistics and spectra

Wake meandering is characterized by the lateral centroid displacement $y_c(x,t)$, with amplitude growing roughly as $x^{1/3}$ to $x^{1/2}$ downstream. LES under neutral and unstable offshore conditions quantifies amplitude, spectra, and probability distributions in both horizontal and vertical planes: unstable conditions produce larger meandering amplitudes and broader spectra (peak Strouhal numbers $St = fD/U_\infty \approx 0.1$–$0.3$), directly increasing the variance of angle-of-attack fluctuations at downstream rotors [4]. Correlation analysis between meandering statistics and downstream turbine structural loads — blade-root out-of-plane bending, low-speed-shaft torque, and yaw moments — shows that *atmospheric* turbulence can generate isolated loading events at least as significant as *wake* turbulence [2]. This is the central insight for lifetime estimation: meandering-aware load spectra, not mean deficits, govern fatigue damage.

### 4.4 Yaw steering aerodynamics and the cos-power law

For a yaw misalignment $\gamma$, the upstream turbine power follows $P_1(\gamma) = P_1(0)\cos^p(\gamma)$, while the deflected wake centerline shifts by $\delta y(x) \approx \xi_0 + \theta x$ with initial skew angle $\theta$ growing with $\sin\gamma$. Gaussian-profile models (Bastankhah–Porté-Agel, Shapiro) predict the downstream power recovery; top-hat models (Jiménez) systematically mispredict deflection, which explains their poorer agreement with LES [3]. The farm-level optimum balances upstream sacrifice against downstream gain:

$$\max_{\{\gamma_i\}} \sum_i P_i(\gamma_1, \dots, \gamma_{i-1}; \phi) \quad \text{s.t.} \quad |\gamma_i| \le \gamma_{\max},$$

typically solved open-loop via FLORIS lookup tables scheduled on wind direction and speed bins [3, 7]. LES of a four-turbine row in a conventionally neutral boundary layer across inflow directions $\phi \in [-5^\circ, 8^\circ]$ with one- and three-turbine yaw strategies shows total power gains up to 23% for the aggressive strategy — but only within a narrow beneficial window that collapses under wind-direction uncertainty [8].

A compact declarative specification of the open-loop steering law:

```haskell
-- yaw offset lookup: maximize farm power per wind-direction/speed bin
steer :: WindDir -> WindSpeed -> [(TurbineId, YawOffset)]
steer phi u = map (\(i, g) -> (i, clamp gammaMax g)) optimal
  where
    grid   = [(g1,g2,g3) | g1 <- gammas, g2 <- gammas, g3 <- gammas]
    cost gs = - farmPower phi u gs          -- minimize negative power
    optimal = argmin cost grid              -- FLORIS surrogate evaluation
```

### 4.5 Wind direction uncertainty: the beneficial window

The most sobering LES finding concerns robustness. Convolving the yaw-strategy power response with Gaussian wind-direction uncertainty ($\sigma_{WD} = 2.3^\circ$–$4.5^\circ$) shrinks the beneficial window from roughly $8.5^\circ$ wide to $6.5^\circ$–$8^\circ$ and reduces peak gains from 23% to 2–7.5% [8]. Field campaigns confirm the mechanism: achieved yaw offsets fall short of targets, producing less power gain than steady models predict, plus slight losses outside the intended control region from unintentional misalignment [6]. Including wind-direction and yaw uncertainty *inside* the FLORIS optimization — robust steering — recovers much of the realizable gain and is now standard practice [6].

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 Field validation: from SOWFA to swap tests

The validation chain for LES-based steering runs from simulation to commercial deployment:

- **Onshore 2-turbine campaigns (NREL):** achieved yaw offsets and power gains match FLORIS predictions only when wind-direction variability is included; steady-state models overpredict [6].
- **Offshore China (NREL/Envision):** yaw offsets derived from SOWFA + FLORIS produced power-capture increases consistent with model predictions for specific turbine pairs, with gains up to 29% observed for aligned pairs [3, 7].
- **Commercial offshore swap test (Siemens Gamesa Wake Adapt, 72 turbines, 14 months):** measured +0.64% energy gain versus +0.65% predicted — sub-percent agreement between engineering models and field data at farm scale [3].
- **Extended commercial campaign:** field wake-loss reductions of 6.6–14.4% across turbine pairs, reaching 50–60% of the static-optimal gain predicted by the Gaussian-curl-hybrid (GCH) model, and closely matching GCH evaluated at the *achieved* (not commanded) yaw angles [9].

> **Theorem (Achieved-angle consistency).** *Model–field agreement in wake steering is recovered, within measurement uncertainty, when engineering models are evaluated at achieved yaw offsets and convolved with the observed wind-direction distribution, rather than at commanded offsets under steady inflow.*

### 5.2 Load statistics: atmosphere versus wake

Coupled aeroelastic LES of 5-MW turbines shows RMS blade-root out-of-plane bending moments and low-speed-shaft torque are higher in unstable than neutral ABL at fixed surface roughness, while yaw moments are equal or reduced — a non-trivial redistribution of the load path [2]. For a downstream turbine 7 diameters behind an upstream machine, the downwind-to-upwind power ratio is 15–20% higher in unstable than neutral conditions [2], a direct LES quantification of the convective recovery effect usable as a prior in layout energy-yield calculations.

### 5.3 Numerical verification

Grid and kernel convergence studies for actuator-line LES establish the regularization width $\varepsilon$ and the criterion $\Delta x \lesssim D/30$–$D/50$ near the rotor for capturing tip-vortex roll-up; lattice-Boltzmann LES formulations have been validated against reference actuator-line data for single-turbine wakes under stratified ABL and wind-farm wakes under neutral ABL before extension to stability sweeps [1]. Precursor-generated inflow is verified by matching logarithmic-law profiles and target turbulence intensities before turbine insertion [4].

## 6 Limitations

- **Computational cost.** Farm-scale LES with actuator lines remains a leadership-computing workload; a single diurnal-cycle sweep over stability classes can consume millions of core-hours, which is why engineering models (FLORIS/GCH) — not LES — perform the actual yaw optimization [3, 7].
- **Sub-grid and wall modeling.** Near-ground Monin–Obukhov wall functions and the Smagorinsky-type closure introduce model-form uncertainty precisely where shear production dominates — the stable regime where wakes persist longest [1, 5].
- **Turbine representation.** Actuator lines omit blade-resolved stall, dynamic stall hysteresis, and controller transients; actuator disks additionally omit swirl and tip vortices. Both assume tabulated airfoil polars extrapolated to high angle of attack [2, 4].
- **Inflow realism.** Precursor ABL assumes horizontal homogeneity and stationarity, neglecting mesoscale fronts, low-level jets, and terrain — all present in the field campaigns used for validation [6, 9].
- **Control idealization.** Open-loop steering presumes perfect yaw actuation and nacelle-wind-direction knowledge; field data show achieved offsets systematically undershoot targets and wind-direction bias persists, motivating closed-loop, lidar-assisted control [6, 8].

## 7 Conclusion

Large-eddy simulation with actuator-line and actuator-disk turbine models provides the physical ground truth for wind farm wake aerodynamics: stability-ordered wake persistence governed by the TKE budget [1], veer–rotation coupling that stretches and deflects wakes in stable ABL [5], meandering spectra that dominate downstream fatigue loading [2, 4], and yaw-steering gains that are real but fragile to wind-direction uncertainty [6, 8]. Field campaigns now close the loop — offshore and onshore tests reproduce model-predicted gains within uncertainty when achieved yaw angles and inflow variability are accounted for [3, 7, 9], and commercial systems like Wake Adapt deliver measured gains matching predictions to within 0.01 percentage points [3].

For layout optimization, the LES evidence points to stability-aware design: spacing and row orientation should be weighted by the site's diurnal stability distribution rather than a single neutral-wind-rose assumption, since stable nighttime conditions produce the deepest, longest wakes. For control, the evidence mandates robust steering — optimizing yaw schedules against the wind-direction uncertainty distribution, not the mean wind — and ultimately closed-loop control using upstream sensing. Together, stability-aware layout and uncertainty-robust steering define the current frontier of wind farm aerodynamic optimization.
