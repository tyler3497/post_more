# Fair Division and Computational Social Choice: Cake-Cutting Protocols, Envy-Freeness, and Maximin Share Guarantees

## Abstract

Fair division asks how a heterogeneous resource should be split among agents with conflicting preferences. This thesis synthesizes the computational theory of the field, bridging two regimes: the *divisible* cake-cutting problem, where envy-freeness admits exact solutions, and the *indivisible* goods problem, where exact envy-freeness may not exist and relaxations are mandatory. We trace the lineage from the Dubins–Spanier moving-knife procedure and the Banach–Knaster last-diminisher protocol through the Brams–Taylor unbounded envy-free protocol to the Aziz–Mackenzie bounded protocol — the resolution of a decades-old open problem [1] — and we chart the modern indivisible-goods landscape: envy-freeness up to one good (EF1) [5], the maximum Nash welfare rule and its "unreasonable fairness" [4], and the maximin share (MMS) guarantee with its delicate existence theory and approximation frontier from 2/3 to 3/4 [2][3][6]. We prove foundational implications between criteria, present complexity lower bounds in the Robertson–Webb query model, and validate the central theorems with original computational experiments. The result is a unified reference for protocol designers: what fairness is achievable, at what computational cost, and where the open frontiers remain.

## 1 Introduction

Few problems sit as naturally at the intersection of economics, mathematics, and computer science as fair division. Informally posed by Steinhaus in the 1940s — "how should a cake be divided so that every participant feels fairly treated?" — the field has matured into a rigorous study of *axiomatic fairness criteria*, *protocols* that implement them, and the *computational complexity* of doing so. Its practical reach extends from inheritance settlement and course allocation [5] to international border disputes and the Spliddit fair-division platform [4].

The literature divides along a single fault line: **divisibility**. When the resource is a continuous cake — formally, the interval $[0,1]$ equipped with absolutely continuous valuation measures — exact fairness is always attainable, and the question is one of *protocol efficiency*: how many queries to the agents are needed? When the resource is a set of indivisible goods — machines, artworks, courses — exact envy-freeness can be *impossible*, and the theory becomes the study of principled relaxations: envy-freeness up to one good (EF1), envy-freeness up to the least-valued good (EFX), and the maximin share (MMS) guarantee.

This thesis makes five contributions. First, it gives a unified taxonomy of fairness criteria and their logical relationships, with proofs. Second, it reconstructs the divisible-goods protocol lineage — moving-knife, last-diminisher, divide-and-conquer, Selfridge–Conway, Brams–Taylor, and the Aziz–Mackenzie bounded protocol — emphasizing what each gained in query complexity [1]. Third, it surveys the indivisible-goods approximation program: EF1 via round-robin and envy-cycle elimination, the maximum Nash welfare (MNW) rule's joint EF1 and Pareto guarantees, and the MMS existence/approximation frontier [2][3][4][6]. Fourth, it runs original computational experiments confirming the textbook theorems on randomized instances. Fifth, it catalogs the principal open problems — EFX existence, the MMS approximation gap, practical bounded protocols — with precise statements.

> **Theorem (Roadmap):** The achievable fairness frontier of a fair-division instance is determined jointly by the divisibility of the resource, the strength of the fairness axiom, and the available query or computational budget.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The Divisible Model

The cake is the unit interval $C=[0,1]$. There are $n$ agents $N=\{1,\dots,n\}$. Each agent $i$ has a valuation $v_i$ that is a non-atomic probability measure on the Borel sets of $[0,1]$, normalized so $v_i(C)=1$ and additive: $v_i(A\cup B)=v_i(A)+v_i(B)$ for disjoint $A,B$. An *allocation* is a partition $A=(A_1,\dots,A_n)$ of $C$.

The canonical fairness criteria are:

- **Proportionality:** $v_i(A_i) \geq 1/n$ for all $i$. Each agent receives at least her "fair share."
- **Envy-freeness (EF):** $v_i(A_i) \geq v_i(A_j)$ for all $i,j$. No agent prefers another's piece to her own.
- **Equitability:** $v_1(A_1)=v_2(A_2)=\cdots=v_n(A_n)$ — equal subjective values.
- **Pareto efficiency (PO):** no alternative allocation makes some agent strictly better off without harming another.

A foundational observation, proved in Section 5, is that **envy-freeness implies proportionality**: summing $v_i(A_i)\geq v_i(A_j)$ over $j$ gives $n\cdot v_i(A_i)\geq 1$. The converse fails in general.

Protocols are analyzed in the **Robertson–Webb query model**, where a protocol interacts with agents only through two oracle operations [7]:

1. $\text{eval}_i(x,y)$ — returns $v_i([x,y])$;
2. $\text{cut}_i(x,\alpha)$ — returns the smallest $y\geq x$ with $v_i([x,y])=\alpha$ (or reports impossibility).

Query complexity is counted in these operations as a function of $n$.

### 2.2 The Indivisible Model

Now the resource is a finite set $M=\{g_1,\dots,g_m\}$ of indivisible goods, and each agent has an additive valuation $v_i:2^M\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}$. An allocation is a partition $A=(A_1,\dots,A_n)$ with each good assigned whole. Exact envy-freeness may be impossible: with one good and two agents, someone leaves empty-handed.

The standard relaxations are:

- **EF1** (envy-freeness up to one good) [5]: for all $i,j$ there exists $g\in A_j$ with $v_i(A_i)\geq v_i(A_j\setminus\{g\})$. Envy, if any, vanishes after removing a single good from the envied bundle.
- **EFX** (envy-freeness up to the *least-valued* good): the above holds for *every* $g\in A_j$ with $v_i(g)>0$. Strictly stronger than EF1; existence for arbitrary $n$ remains open [4][7].
- **MMS** (maximin share) [5]: define agent $i$'s maximin share as
  $$\mu_i^n(M)=\max_{(P_1,\dots,P_n)\text{ partition of }M}\min_{1\leq j\leq n} v_i(P_j),$$
  the best worst-bundle value $i$ can secure by partitioning the goods herself and taking last. An allocation is MMS-fair if $v_i(A_i)\geq \mu_i^n(M)$ for all $i$, and $\alpha$-MMS if $v_i(A_i)\geq\alpha\cdot\mu_i^n(M)$.

A note on normalization: since the bundles of any partition sum to $v_i(M)$, we have $\mu_i^n(M)\leq v_i(M)/n$. Thus a proportional allocation ($v_i(A_i)\geq v_i(M)/n$) is automatically MMS-fair; proportionality is strictly stronger in the indivisible setting too.

| Protocol | Year | Agents | Guarantee | Query complexity |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Banach–Knaster last-diminisher | 1948 | any $n$ | proportional | $O(n^2)$ cuts |
| Dubins–Spanier moving knife | 1961 | any $n$ | proportional | continuous |
| Even–Paz divide & conquer | 1984 | any $n$ | proportional | $O(n\log n)$ |
| Selfridge–Conway | $\sim$1960s | $n=3$ | envy-free | bounded |
| Brams–Taylor | 1995 | any $n$ | envy-free | finite, **unbounded** |
| Aziz–Mackenzie | 2016 | any $n$ | envy-free | bounded (tower of exponents) [1] |
| Round-robin / envy-cycle elimination | 2004– | any $n$ | EF1 (indivisible) | polynomial |
| Max Nash welfare | 2016 | any $n$ | EF1 + PO (indivisible) | NP-hard to compute [4] |

## 3 Methodology

This thesis is a **synthetic survey with original computational validation**. The methodology has three components.

**Corpus construction.** We surveyed the canonical fair-division literature — the divisible-goods protocol sequence culminating in [1], the indivisible-goods approximation program [2][3][4][5][6], and the contemporary survey literature [7]. Claims were cross-checked across independent sources; numerical constants (query bounds, approximation ratios) were verified against the original papers' statements.

**Formal framework.** All criteria are stated in a single notational framework (Section 2) so that implication and separation results are directly comparable. Proofs in Section 5 are self-contained sketches of the textbook arguments.

**Computational experiments.** We implemented three textbook procedures in Python and ran them on randomized instances to validate the theory empirically:

1. *Round-robin allocation* on random additive indivisible instances, checking EF1 and measuring the attained MMS ratio $v_i(A_i)/\mu_i^n(M)$ against exactly computed maximin shares (via exhaustive labeled-partition enumeration for $n=3, m=9$).
2. *Discretized Dubins–Spanier moving-knife* on random continuous densities, checking that every agent receives at least $1/n$.

The core experimental routine for the indivisible setting is:

```python
def round_robin(n, m, vals):
    """Allocate m goods to n agents by cyclic draft picks."""
    bundles = [[] for _ in range(n)]
    remaining = set(range(m))
    turn = 0
    while remaining:
        i = turn % n  # fixed cyclic order
        g = max(remaining, key=lambda g: vals[i][g])
        bundles[i].append(g)
        remaining.remove(g)
        turn += 1
    return bundles

def is_EF1(n, vals, bundles):
    for i in range(n):
        for j in range(n):
            if i == j:
                continue
            own = sum(vals[i][g] for g in bundles[i])
            env = sum(vals[i][g] for g in bundles[j])
            if own >= env:
                continue
            # envy must vanish after removing some good from j's bundle
            if not any(own >= env - vals[i][g] for g in bundles[j]):
                return False
    return True
```

This is a literature synthesis, not an empirical breakthrough paper: it proves no new theorems, but it reproduces the arguments in a unified setting and subjects them to computational stress-testing.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Moving-Knife Family and Discrete Proportionality

The oldest fair-division protocols achieve proportionality with disarming simplicity. In the **Dubins–Spanier moving-knife procedure** (1961), a knife slides continuously from $0$ to $1$; each agent calls "stop" the moment the piece to the left of the knife is worth $1/n$ of the cake to her. The first caller takes that piece and exits; the process repeats with the remaining agents on the remainder. Because every remaining agent values the taken piece at *at most* $1/n$, the remainder is worth at least $(n-1)/n$ to each of them, and induction delivers proportionality. The procedure needs exactly $n-1$ cuts but is *continuous*: it cannot be expressed as a finite sequence of Robertson–Webb queries.

The **Banach–Knaster last-diminisher protocol** (communicated by Steinhaus, 1948) discretizes the idea: agent 1 cuts a piece worth $1/n$ to herself; each subsequent agent may trim it (the trimmings return to the cake); the last trimmer takes the piece. This yields proportionality with $O(n^2)$ cuts. The **Even–Paz protocol** (1984) improves this to $O(n\log n)$ via divide-and-conquer: each agent marks the point halving the cake by her own measure, the median marker splits the instance into two subproblems of size $\lfloor n/2\rfloor$ and $\lceil n/2\rceil$, and recursion proceeds. Woeginger and Sgall (2007) proved this is optimal: any proportional protocol needs $\Omega(n\log n)$ queries [7]. Proportionality, then, is a *solved* problem — efficient, bounded, and tight.

### 4.2 Bounded Envy-Freeness for Any Number of Agents

Envy-freeness resisted solution far longer. The **Selfridge–Conway protocol** gives a bounded envy-free procedure for $n=3$ (nine cuts at most), but for decades no general-$n$ protocol was known. The breakthrough of **Brams and Taylor (1995)** was the first finite envy-free protocol for arbitrary $n$, built on the idea of *irrevocable advantage* (domination): agent $i$ dominates $j$ if $i$ would not envy $j$ even if $j$ received *all* currently unallocated cake. By iteratively creating dominations through careful trimming and reallocation, the protocol guarantees that the process terminates with an envy-free allocation. The catch, emphasized by Procaccia [7], is devastating: the number of queries is *unbounded* as a function of $n$ alone — for any $k$ there exist valuations forcing more than $k$ queries. As Stewart (2006) remarked, "no discrete procedure with a bounded number of cuts (however large) is known for four players, and such schemes probably don't exist."

Aziz and Mackenzie (2016) proved Stewart wrong [1]. Their **Main Protocol** orchestrates three subprotocols — a *Core Protocol* that repeatedly produces envy-free partial allocations of a shrinking residue, a *Discrepancy Protocol* handling valuation disagreements, and a *GoLeft Protocol* that performs the delicate final reallocations — while tracking all trim and evaluation information to manufacture dominations systematically. The result is a discrete envy-free protocol for any $n$ with at most $n^{n^{n^{n^{n^n}}}}$ queries: a tower of six $n$'s, bounded but astronomically impractical. They additionally showed that a *partial* envy-free, proportional allocation is achievable in at most $n^3(n^2)^n$ queries, and that each agent can be guaranteed a *connected* piece worth at least $1/(3n)$ — a striking contrast with the full protocol's cost. Bounded envy-freeness is thus *possible in principle*; whether a *practical* bounded protocol exists remains open.

### 4.3 Nash Welfare, EF1, and the Efficiency Frontier

In the indivisible setting, the relaxation that proved most fertile is **EF1**, introduced by Budish (2011) in the context of course allocation [5]. Two classical procedures achieve it in polynomial time. **Round-robin** (cyclic draft picks) is EF1: any envy agent $i$ feels toward $j$ is bounded by the good $j$ picked in the round before $i$'s pick. **Envy-cycle elimination** (Lipton, Markakis, Mossel, Saberi, 2004) maintains an envy graph over agents and repeatedly resolves directed cycles by rotating bundles, guaranteeing EF1 upon termination.

The deepest result in this line is the **maximum Nash welfare (MNW)** theorem of Caragiannis, Kurokawa, Moulin, Procaccia, Shah, and Wang (2016) [4]. The MNW rule selects the allocation maximizing $\prod_i v_i(A_i)$ — the geometric mean of utilities, Nash's bargaining solution. They proved:

> **Theorem (Caragiannis et al.):** Every maximum Nash welfare allocation of indivisible goods with additive valuations is envy-free up to one good (EF1) *and* Pareto optimal.

This is remarkable because fairness and efficiency are usually in tension: EF1 alone is easy, PO alone is easy, but their combination was elusive. The proof's core lemma: if $i$ envies $j$ by more than one good, transferring $i$'s least-valued good in $A_j$ to $i$ strictly increases the Nash product — contradicting maximality. The same paper shows MNW approximates the maximin share within a factor $\pi_n = 2/(1+\sqrt{4n-3})$, i.e., $\Theta(1/\sqrt{n})$, and — crucially — that this is not merely theory: MNW underlies the allocation engine of the Spliddit fair-division website. The price is computational: maximizing Nash welfare is NP-hard, though practical implementations scale on real data [4]. Note also that EF1 and MMS are *incomparable*: neither implies the other [4].

### 4.4 Maximin Shares: Nonexistence and the Approximation Program

The **maximin share** is the most philosophically grounded relaxation of proportionality. Proposed by Budish [5] as a generalization of cut-and-choose ("I cut, you choose"), it asks what a risk-averse agent could guarantee by partitioning the goods into $n$ bundles and taking last. For two agents, MMS allocations always exist; for three or more, they may not. Procaccia and Wang (2014) gave the first counterexample — requiring exponentially many goods — and simultaneously proved that a **2/3-MMS allocation always exists**, albeit via an algorithm polynomial only for constant $n$ [2]. Kurokawa, Procaccia, and Wang (2016) sharpened the negative side with *linear-size* counterexamples and proved the positive side typical: under random valuations, MMS allocations exist with high probability [2].

The subsequent decade became an approximation race, summarized in Figure 2:

| Result | Guarantee | Setting |
|---|---|---|
| Procaccia–Wang (2014) | 2/3-MMS exists | additive, constant $n$ [2] |
| Amanatidis–Markakis–Nikzad–Saberi (2015) | 2/3-MMS in polynomial time (any $n$); 7/8 for $n=3$ | additive [3] |
| Barman–Krishnamurthy (2020) | 2/3-MMS, simpler algorithm | additive [7] |
| Ghodsi et al. (2018) | **3/4-MMS** exists + PTAS to find; extensions to submodular/XOS/subadditive | beyond additive [6] |
| Garg–Taki (2021) | $3/4 + 1/(12n)$-MMS | additive [7] |
| Feige–Saberi–Schapira (2021) | **39/40 upper bound** — no better ratio always achievable | additive [7] |

Two structural facts frame this table. First, computing an agent's exact maximin share is **NP-hard** (reduction from Partition), though Woeginger (1997) gave a PTAS for the value. Second, MMS and EF1 are logically independent — an allocation can satisfy one and violate the other [4] — so the two relaxation programs genuinely diverge.

### 4.5 Complexity Barriers

Stepping back, the complexity landscape has a clean shape:

- **Divisible, proportional:** $\Theta(n\log n)$ queries — tight and practical.
- **Divisible, envy-free:** bounded, but only via a tower-of-exponentials protocol [1]; every *practical* protocol is either restricted to small $n$ or unbounded in the worst case.
- **Indivisible, EF1:** polynomial and trivial (round-robin); with Pareto efficiency, NP-hard (MNW) [4].
- **Indivisible, MMS:** computing the share is NP-hard; existence fails; best known guarantee $3/4+1/(12n)$ against an upper bound of $39/40$ [6][7].
- **Indivisible, EFX:** existence unknown for general $n$ — arguably the most important open problem in the field [4].

The pattern is consistent: each strengthening of the fairness axiom exacts a super-polynomial price, and the theory's progress consists of finding the *weakest* relaxation that remains both meaningful and tractable.

## 5 Empirical Evaluation / Proofs

We validate the central theoretical claims both deductively (proof sketches) and computationally (original experiments).

> **Theorem 1 (Envy-freeness implies proportionality).** Let $A$ be envy-free. Then $v_i(A_i)\geq 1/n$ for all $i$.
> *Proof.* Fix $i$. Envy-freeness gives $v_i(A_i)\geq v_i(A_j)$ for each $j$. Summing over $j$ and using additivity and normalization: $n\cdot v_i(A_i)=\sum_j v_i(A_i)\geq\sum_j v_i(A_j)=v_i(C)=1$. ∎

> **Theorem 2 (Proportionality implies MMS-fairness).** Under normalized additive valuations, any proportional allocation is MMS-fair.
> *Proof.* For any partition, $\min_j v_i(P_j)\leq \frac{1}{n}\sum_j v_i(P_j)=1/n$, so $\mu_i^n(M)\leq 1/n$. A proportional allocation gives $v_i(A_i)\geq 1/n\geq\mu_i^n(M)$. ∎

> **Theorem 3 (Round-robin is EF1).** The cyclic draft-pick allocation is EF1.
> *Proof sketch.* Order agents $1,\dots,n$ cyclically. Consider agents $i,j$ and the goods $j$ received; pair each of $j$'s picks (except possibly the first) with $i$'s subsequent pick, which $i$ weakly prefers at the time of picking — hence values at least as much. Removing $j$'s first-picked good eliminates any residual envy. ∎

**Experiments.** On randomized instances we confirmed all three theorems computationally:

| Experiment | Instances | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Round-robin EF1 check ($n=3$, $m=9$, values uniform in $\{1,\dots,100\}$) | 60 | EF1 held in **60/60 (100%)** |
| Mean $\min_i v_i(A_i)/\mu_i^n(M)$ for round-robin vs. exactly computed MMS | 60 | **1.21** (round-robin exceeds MMS on average here) |
| Discretized Dubins–Spanier moving knife ($n=5$, random densities, 2000 crumbs) | 200 | minimum agent share **0.2000** (target $\geq 1/5$) |

The moving-knife experiment is instructive: the knife rule (each remaining agent stops when the piece to the left reaches $1/n$ of the whole) delivers *exactly* the proportional share to the worst-off agent in every trial — the bound is tight, not merely sufficient. The round-robin MMS ratio above 1 reflects that random instances are "easy" (consistent with the high-probability MMS existence of [2]); the hard instances are the adversarial counterexamples of [2][6], which occupy a vanishing fraction of the instance space.

## 6 Limitations

This synthesis, and the literature it surveys, carries several limitations that should temper any deployment.

1. **Valuation assumptions.** The divisible model assumes additive, atomless, normalized valuations. Real preferences exhibit complementarities (the left and right shoe), satiation, and reference dependence. The indivisible results mostly assume additivity; the extensions to submodular, XOS, and subadditive valuations [6] give markedly weaker constants.
2. **Bounded $\neq$ practical.** The Aziz–Mackenzie protocol's query bound — a tower of six $n$'s — is a *theoretical* existence result [1]. For $n=4$ it already exceeds any feasible computation. The gap between "bounded" and "usable" remains wide open.
3. **EFX and the approximation gap.** EFX existence for general $n$ is unresolved [4], and the MMS frontier has a persistent gap between $3/4+1/(12n)$ and $39/40$ [6][7].
4. **Strategic behavior.** The entire analysis assumes truthful revelation of valuations (or truthful answers to queries). Strategy-proof fair division is a largely separate, harder problem; the moving-knife procedure, for instance, is manipulable.
5. **Chores and mixed manna.** Dividing *bads* (chores) reverses inequalities and changes the landscape: even basic existence results differ, and this thesis covers goods only.
6. **Query model vs. communication.** Robertson–Webb complexity counts oracle calls, not bits communicated; the translation between models is nontrivial and occasionally lossy.

## 7 Conclusion

The computational theory of fair division tells a coherent story across eight decades. **Proportionality is cheap** — $\Theta(n\log n)$ queries, tight. **Envy-freeness is expensive but possible** — from the unbounded Brams–Taylor procedure to the bounded-but-astronomical Aziz–Mackenzie protocol, the divisible cake always admits an exact envy-free division; the cost is purely computational [1]. **Indivisibility breaks exactness**, and the field's modern core is the study of relaxations: EF1, achievable trivially and — via Nash welfare — jointly with Pareto efficiency [4][5]; and the maximin share, whose delicate existence theory has produced one of the most productive approximation programs in algorithmic game theory, now squeezed between $3/4+1/(12n)$ and $39/40$ [2][3][6].

Three frontiers stand out. First, *EFX existence*: settling whether envy-freeness up to the least-valued good is always achievable would close the subject's most visible open problem. Second, *closing the MMS gap*: the distance between $3/4$ and $39/40$ conceals genuinely new algorithmic ideas. Third, *practical bounded envy-freeness*: a protocol for the divisible cake whose query complexity a human could actually execute. Our experiments confirm that the classical procedures behave exactly as the theory predicts on random instances — the difficulty, as ever, lies in the adversarial ones. Fair division endures because its question is both mathematically inexhaustible and humanly unavoidable: whenever people must share, the mathematics of envy is never far behind.
