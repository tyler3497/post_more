# Formal Verification of Distributed Protocols: TLA+, Model Checking, and Mechanized Proof for Consensus Algorithms

## Abstract

Formal verification has transformed distributed consensus from an art of hand-written argument into a discipline with machine-checked guarantees. This thesis develops a unified account of three complementary verification traditions: specification and explicit-state model checking with TLA+ and the TLC checker [1][2]; industrial lightweight formal methods, as practiced at Amazon Web Services, where TLA+ specifications exposed subtle design errors in production storage systems [6]; and full mechanized proof of running implementations through Coq-based frameworks such as Verdi [4] and Dafny-based refinement as in IronFleet [5][10], culminating in decidable-fragment methods such as Ivy [7][8] that automate inductive-invariant inference. We treat Paxos and Raft [3] as running examples, derive refinement mappings and inductive invariants for leader election and log replication, and quantify the trade-offs among automation, scale, and assurance. The central claim is that model checking finds design bugs early and cheaply, mechanized proof closes the formality gap between model and executable code, and decidable fragments supply a pragmatic middle ground between the two.

## 1 Introduction

Distributed consensus is among the most unforgiving problems in computing. A protocol must coax a collection of crash-prone processes, connected by an asynchronous network that reorders, drops, and duplicates messages, into agreeing on a single value or a single totally ordered log — and it must do so without ever violating safety, no matter how adversarially the failures interleave. Leslie Lamport's Paxos made such agreement practical, but its original presentation was famously subtle: even expert readers misjudged which steps were load-bearing, and hand-written correctness arguments routinely concealed gaps. Diego Ongaro and John Ousterhout's Raft re-engineered consensus for understandability, decomposing it into leader election, log replication, and safety, and enforcing a stronger coherency discipline that shrinks the space of states a reader must hold in mind [3]. Understandability is not correctness: both protocols still demand proof, and proof by hand remains the least reliable link in the chain.

Formal methods attack this problem at three altitudes. At the design altitude, the specifier writes a mathematical model of the protocol — typically in TLA+ [1][2] — and the TLC model checker exhaustively explores reachable states of small instances, hunting for invariant violations and liveness failures. At the industrial altitude, practiced most visibly at Amazon Web Services since 2011, engineers write TLA+ specifications of real systems and use model checking as a design tool that uncovers subtle bugs testing missed [6]. At the implementation altitude, mechanized frameworks verify the running artifact itself: Verdi proves Coq implementations of Raft correct against modular network semantics [4], IronFleet proves safety and liveness of Dafny implementations of a Paxos-based state machine and a sharded key-value store [5][10], and Ivy confines reasoning to a decidable fragment so that inductive invariants for Paxos can be inferred with machine assistance [7][8].

This thesis unifies these traditions around the verification of consensus algorithms. First, it presents the logical foundations — the temporal logic of actions, refinement mappings, inductive invariants, and fairness — with enough precision to read and write real specifications. Second, it compares the four dominant techniques — TLC model checking, TLAPS-style deductive proof, Verdi/IronFleet implementation verification, and Ivy's decidable-fragment automation — on guarantees, costs, and fault models. Third, it surveys the empirical record: which bugs were found, which proofs were completed, and what each cost. The conclusion is that they compose into a pipeline: model checking for design, decidable fragments for invariant discovery, and mechanized refinement for end-to-end assurance.

## 2 Background

**The consensus problem.** A consensus protocol drives *n* crash-prone processes to agreement. Correctness splits into safety — *agreement* (no two processes decide differently), *validity* (the decided value was proposed), *integrity* (each process decides at most once, and only on a genuinely chosen value) — and liveness — *termination* (some proposed value is eventually chosen) and *dissemination* (processes eventually learn it). The FLP result shows no deterministic protocol guarantees termination under full asynchrony with even one fault, so practical protocols assume partial synchrony, randomization, or failure detectors to recover liveness while safety stays unconditional.

**Quorums.** Nearly every consensus protocol reasons about quorums — sets of processes large enough that any two overlap. The workhorse is the majority quorum:

> Theorem: Quorum Intersection. In a system of *n* processes, any two sets each containing more than *n*/2 processes share at least one process.

*Proof sketch.* If two majorities were disjoint, their union would exceed *n* processes — a contradiction. This counting argument underwrites Paxos ballot majorities, Raft election quorums, and Byzantine view-change protocols alike, and it is among the first properties a model checker confirms and the hardest to keep inside decidable fragments [7].

**Paxos and Raft.** Paxos runs prepare/promise and propose/accept phases over numbered ballots, with the invariant that once a value is chosen at some ballot, every higher ballot proposes that same value [7][9]. Raft restructures the logic around a strong leader: log entries flow only from leader to followers, elections use randomized timeouts, and safety rests on *Election Safety* (at most one leader per term) and *Log Matching* (logs agreeing on an entry's index and term agree on all preceding entries) [3].

**TLA and TLA+.** The Temporal Logic of Actions, introduced by Lamport [2], describes systems as formulas over behaviors — infinite sequences of states. An action is a predicate over a state pair, written with unprimed variables for the current state and primed variables for the next. A specification has the canonical shape *Init ∧ □[Next]_vars ∧ Fairness*: *Init* describes initial states, *Next* is the disjunction of all atomic steps, the subscripted box permits stuttering steps that leave variables unchanged, and fairness rules out behaviors where an enabled step is forever ignored. Stuttering invariance is the deep idea: it reduces refinement — showing a low-level system implements a high-level one — to mapping steps to steps-or-stutters, without matching grain sizes. TLA+ embeds this logic in a specification language over untyped set theory [1], with the PlusCal algorithm language, the TLC model checker, and the TLAPS proof system.

**Model checking.** A model checker decides, for a finite instance of a specification, whether all reachable behaviors satisfy the stated properties. TLC performs explicit-state exploration — breadth- or depth-first enumeration with 64-bit fingerprint hashing, symmetry reduction to collapse isomorphic states, and parallel workers [1]. When a property fails, TLC emits a minimal counterexample trace, often the single most valuable artifact of a verification effort. The obstacle is *state explosion*: reachable states grow exponentially with processes, buffers, and data values, confining exhaustive checking to small scopes. The *small-scope hypothesis* — most design errors already manifest in tiny instances — is what makes this tolerable in practice [6].

**Mechanized proof.** Where model checking is bounded, deductive verification is unbounded but labor-intensive. Verdi [4] embeds distributed implementations in Coq, formalizes several network semantics, and introduces *verified system transformers* that lift a proof under an idealized fault model to a realistic one without added proof burden; its landmark is the first mechanically checked linearizability proof for Raft. IronFleet [5][10] blends TLA-style state-machine refinement with Hoare-logic verification in Dafny, discharging obligations with Z3 — and mechanically proves *liveness* of implementations, not just safety. Ivy [7][8] restricts specifications and invariants to EPR, the effectively propositional (Bernays–Schönfinkel) fragment where satisfiability is decidable via the finite-model property, and uses counterexample-guided generalization to infer inductive invariants for Paxos.

## 3 Methodology

Verifying a consensus protocol is a staged process synthesizing industrial TLA+ practice [6], the Verdi and IronFleet frameworks [4][5], and the decidable-fragment school [7][8]:

1. **Write the abstract specification.** State *what* the system must achieve before *how*: agreement, validity, and termination as temporal formulas over abstract state, omitting message formats, timers, and batching.
2. **State the correctness properties.** Safety becomes a state invariant (e.g., *Election Safety*: no two leaders in the same term); liveness becomes a temporal formula qualified by fairness assumptions (e.g., under weak fairness of the election action, a leader is eventually elected).
3. **Model-check bounded instances.** Run TLC on three-to-five-server configurations with small bounds; treat every counterexample as a design defect until proven otherwise — the AWS experience shows this step alone repays the specification effort [6].
4. **Strengthen to inductive invariants.** A property *P* is *inductive* if it holds initially and is preserved by every step; inductive properties imply the desired invariant for all reachable states, unboundedly. Finding them is the creative core of verification — Ivy automates the search within EPR [7], while TLAPS and Coq proofs develop them by hand.
5. **Refine toward the implementation.** Connect each design level to the next with a refinement mapping, down to the executable artifact. Verdi's verified system transformers [4] and IronFleet's layered Dafny refinement [5][10] are two realizations of this ladder.
6. **Audit the trusted computing base.** Enumerate every assumption: model-checker correctness, SMT-solver soundness, compiler and runtime, network shims. Bugs cluster at the verified–unverified boundary, so this inventory is part of the result.

The following table situates the major techniques along the axes that matter to a practitioner choosing among them:

| Approach | Representative tool | Proof artifact | Automation | Fault model | Typical cost |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Explicit-state model checking | TLC | Exhaustive check of bounded instance | Fully automatic | Crash, message loss/reorder | Hours to days |
| Deductive specification proof | TLAPS | Machine-checked proof of spec | Interactive | As modeled | Weeks |
| Decidable-fragment verification | Ivy | Inductive invariant in EPR | Semi-automatic | Crash | Days to weeks |
| Extracted implementation proof | Verdi / Coq | Verified OCaml system | Interactive | Crash, network faults | Person-months |
| Refinement-based implementation proof | IronFleet / Dafny | Verified binary + liveness | SMT-assisted | Crash | Person-years |
| Industrial lightweight specs | TLA+ at AWS | Checked design models | Fully automatic | Crash, concurrency | Days |

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Specifying Consensus in TLA+: A Leader-Election Fragment

A TLA+ specification of Raft-style leader election declares constants and variables, defines the initial predicate and the next-state relation as a disjunction of actions, and asserts the whole as a temporal formula:

```tla
---- MODULE RaftElection ----
EXTENDS Naturals, FiniteSets
CONSTANT Server
VARIABLE currentTerm, votedFor, state

Init ==
    /\ currentTerm = [s \in Server |-> 0]
    /\ votedFor    = [s \in Server |-> "none"]
    /\ state       = [s \in Server |-> "follower"]

BecomeCandidate(s) ==
    /\ state[s] = "follower"
    /\ state' = [state EXCEPT ![s] = "candidate"]
    /\ UNCHANGED <<currentTerm, votedFor>>

ElectionSafety ==
    \A s, t \in Server :
        (state[s] = "leader" /\ state[t] = "leader"
            /\ currentTerm[s] = currentTerm[t]) => (s = t)
```

*ElectionSafety* is a state predicate, checkable on every reachable state. The full specification adds vote-request and vote-grant actions, term updates, quorum counting, and log replication as further disjuncts of *Next*. Two features matter. First, nondeterminism is total: any enabled action may fire, so TLC explores *all* interleavings of timeouts, crashes, and deliveries. Second, the specification is deliberately unimplementable — no network, no serialization, no threads — which is exactly what makes it checkable.

### 4.2 Inside TLC: Explicit-State Model Checking

TLC's algorithm is conceptually simple. It keeps a queue of unexpanded states and a set of visited fingerprints; each state is expanded by applying every action, and each successor is tested against the invariants — and, for liveness properties, recorded in the behavior graph. The essence fits in a few lines of Python:

```python
from collections import deque

def tlc_essence(init, successors, invariant):
    seen, queue = {init}, deque([init])
    while queue:
        s = queue.popleft()
        if not invariant(s):
            return s          # counterexample state
        for t in successors(s):
            if t not in seen:  # 64-bit fingerprint in real TLC
                seen.add(t)
                queue.append(t)
    return None               # invariant holds on this instance
```

Real TLC adds symmetry reduction, multi-threaded expansion, checkpointing, and temporal liveness checking over the explored graph [1]. A Raft specification with three servers, small term bounds, and short logs is routinely exhaustible — tens of millions of states in minutes — while the *small-scope hypothesis* justifies the belief that the interesting bugs appear at these sizes [6]. When TLC reports a violation, it prints the shortest trace to the failure: engineers report that reading these traces is the fastest known way to understand a concurrency bug.

### 4.3 Refinement: From Abstract Spec to Running System

Model checking a specification proves nothing about code. *Refinement* bridges the gap: an implementation *refines* a specification when every implementation behavior, mapped through a refinement function, is a specification behavior (allowing stuttering where the implementation takes finer-grained steps). The payoff is compositional: prove the protocol once abstractly, then show each more concrete level refines the one above.

> Theorem: Refinement preserves safety. Let *I* be an implementation transition system, *S* a specification, and *f* a mapping from *I*-states to *S*-states such that every *I*-step maps to an *S*-step or a stuttering step. If *S* satisfies the state invariant *P*, then *I* satisfies *P* composed with *f*.

IronFleet realizes this ladder in Dafny: a TLA-style state machine is refined through protocol and host layers down to executable code, with obligations discharged by Z3 [5][10]. Verdi realizes it in Coq through *verified system transformers*: the developer verifies under an idealized network semantics, and a verified transformer ports implementation and proof to a realistic semantics with drops, duplication, and reordering, at zero additional proof cost [4]. Both deliver what TLC alone cannot: a theorem *about the artifact that ships*.

### 4.4 Decidable Fragments: Ivy and the EPR Approach

Interactive proof is expensive and model checking is bounded. The Ivy line [7][8] asks whether the *logic* can be restricted so verification becomes decidable without becoming useless. The answer is EPR — the effectively propositional (Bernays–Schönfinkel) fragment: first-order formulas with an *∃\*∀\** prefix over uninterpreted relations. EPR has the finite-model property — a satisfiable formula has a finite model bounded by its constants — so an SMT solver decides it.

Padon and colleagues showed that the Paxos safety argument — reliant on quantifier alternation ("for every ballot, there exists a quorum...") and cardinality reasoning about majorities — can be encoded in EPR [7]. Ivy automates invariant discovery: from a candidate invariant it queries the solver for counterexamples to induction, generalizes them into strengthening assertions, and iterates until the invariant is inductive or a genuine bug surfaces. Goel and Sakallah pushed this toward a fully automatic proof of Lamport's Paxos [8]. The price is expressiveness: general arithmetic, transitive closure, and unbounded data structures fall outside EPR — but for the agreement core of consensus, the fragment suffices.

### 4.5 Liveness: Fairness and the IronFleet Breakthrough

Safety says nothing bad happens; liveness says something good eventually does. In TLA+, liveness uses fairness operators: weak fairness *WF_v(A)* rules out behaviors where *A* stays enabled forever but never fires; strong fairness *SF_v(A)* rules out behaviors where *A* is enabled infinitely often but fires finitely often [2]. TLC checks liveness on finite models via the strongly connected components of the behavior graph — routinely used to validate Raft's claim that randomized timeouts eventually yield a stable leader.

Mechanized liveness for *implementations* was long considered out of reach: it demands reasoning about infinite executions of real code. IronFleet broke the barrier by embedding a TLA-style temporal proof method in Dafny, proving its Paxos-based state machine and lease-based sharded key-value store not only never misbehave but eventually respond to clients, under explicit fairness assumptions [5][10]. This remains the high-water mark: a machine-checked liveness proof of a practical distributed implementation.

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

Three programs of work — industrial, foundational, and automated — supply the hard evidence.

**Industrial: TLA+ at Amazon Web Services.** From 2011, AWS engineers adopted TLA+ as a design tool for the core algorithms of S3, DynamoDB, EBS, and other storage systems [6]. Engineers with no formal-methods background wrote specifications in days; TLC found subtle design errors — lost updates, incorrect failover, consistency violations under partition — that survived design reviews, code reviews, static analysis, and stress testing, several *before* any code was written.

**Foundational: Verdi and IronFleet.** Verdi's Coq development delivered the first mechanically checked linearizability proof for Raft, plus verified primary-backup replication and a key-value store, with performance competitive with unverified equivalents [4].

**Automated: Ivy and push-button Paxos.** Padon et al.

| System | Guarantee established | Technique | Scale of effort |
|---|---|---|---|
| AWS S3 / DynamoDB / EBS internals | Design-level safety, no lost updates | TLA+ with TLC | Days per system [6] |
| Raft (Verdi) | Mechanized linearizability | Coq, verified system transformers | Person-months [4] |
| Paxos RSM, sharded KV (IronFleet) | Mechanized safety *and liveness* | Dafny with Z3, layered refinement | Person-years [5][10] |
| Paxos (Ivy) | Inductive invariant in EPR | Counterexample-guided inference | Days to weeks [7][8] |
| Multi-Paxos family | Modular safety decomposition | Rely-guarantee, protocol combinators | Person-months [9] |

The table quantifies the central trade-off: automation buys speed, mechanization buys unbounded assurance, and decidable fragments meet in the middle by automating the most creative step — invariant discovery — while retaining unbounded soundness.

## 6 Limitations

Five limitations recur across all three traditions.

**State explosion bounds model checking.** TLC is exhaustive only for small instances: a handful of servers, bounded terms, short logs. Symmetry reduction and fingerprinting extend the frontier but cannot defeat exponential growth, and liveness checking — which analyzes the full behavior graph — exhausts memory sooner still. The small-scope hypothesis is empirical, not a theorem: a bug needing six servers will not surface in a five-server model.

**The formality gap.** TLC verifies a *model*, not code. A verified specification can coexist with a buggy implementation if the implementation deviates from the model in unexamined ways — message serialization, timeout arithmetic, failure-detector behavior. Mechanized frameworks close this gap only up to their trusted computing base: the Coq kernel is small, but extraction, the OCaml runtime, the operating system, and — in the Dafny line — the Z3 SMT solver are all trusted. Empirical audits of verified distributed systems have repeatedly found the residual bugs concentrated exactly at these verified–unverified boundaries, in shims and environmental assumptions.

**Liveness assumes fairness, not physics.** Mechanized liveness proofs quantify over fair schedulers and eventually-reliable networks [5][10]; they say nothing about *how soon* a response arrives. Real deployments care about tail latency under adversarial conditions, which remains outside every framework surveyed here.

**Decidable fragments restrict expression.** EPR cannot directly state general arithmetic or transitive closure, so Paxos's quorum-cardinality reasoning must be encoded with care [7]. Protocols whose correctness essentially depends on arithmetic — threshold cryptography, erasure-coded storage — resist the fragment entirely.

**Cost and expertise.** IronFleet-scale verification remains a person-year undertaking demanding rare expertise in both distributed systems and proof engineering [10]. Industrial TLA+ practice [6] shows the entry cost can be days, but only for the design level; the implementation level has no cheap route yet. And the Byzantine fault model — where faulty processes behave arbitrarily rather than merely crashing — is substantially harder than the crash model: quorum intersection arguments generalize, but mechanized proofs for Byzantine agreement remain a frontier rather than a routine.

## 7 Conclusion

Three traditions, one subject. TLA+ and TLC made consensus *design* checkable: write the mathematics, explore the state space, read the counterexample [1][2]. AWS practice proved this lightweight discipline finds real bugs at a cost measured in days [6]. Verdi's verified system transformers [4] and IronFleet's Dafny refinement with machine-checked liveness [5][10] extended the guarantee from model to running artifact, at a cost measured in person-years. Ivy's EPR automation [7][8] is compressing the most expensive step — inductive-invariant discovery — toward push-button practicality.

These methods are stages of a pipeline, not competitors: model-check the design to kill bugs when cheapest; infer inductive invariants in a decidable fragment to lift bounded confidence toward unbounded proof; refine, with machine-checked justification, down to the code that ships. Open frontiers remain — verified Byzantine agreement, quantitative liveness bounds, learning-assisted invariant synthesis — but the trajectory is clear: consensus, the problem that once defeated hand proof, is becoming the problem that machines prove.
