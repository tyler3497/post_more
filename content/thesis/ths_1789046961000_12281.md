# High-Performance Vector Databases: HNSW Graphs, IVF-PQ Quantization, and ANN Benchmark Methodology

## Abstract

Vector databases power retrieval-augmented generation, semantic search, and billion-scale recommendation, and their performance hinges on one primitive: approximate nearest neighbor (ANN) search over high-dimensional embeddings. This thesis unifies the two dominant indexing paradigms — proximity-graph traversal, exemplified by Hierarchical Navigable Small World (HNSW) graphs, and inverted files with product quantization (IVF-PQ) — together with the empirical methodology used to compare them honestly. We formalize the ANN problem and recall@k, derive the logarithmic scaling of layered graph search, dissect the diversity-preserving heuristic separating HNSW from earlier Navigable Small World graphs, and reconstruct the mathematics of product quantization, asymmetric distance computation, and learned rotations. We also codify the discipline of the ANN-Benchmarks framework: fixed datasets with ground truth, isolated implementations, and recall–throughput Pareto frontiers. Synthesizing published results, we argue no index dominates universally: graph methods lead in-memory recall–latency frontiers, while quantized systems win on memory footprint at billion scale. The thesis closes with a practitioner decision guide and a research agenda for out-of-distribution queries, dynamic indexing, and hardware-aware search.

## 1 Introduction

The nearest-neighbor problem is among the oldest in computational geometry. Given a corpus $\mathcal{X} = \{x_1, \dots, x_n\} \subset \mathbb{R}^d$ and a query $q \in \mathbb{R}^d$, the *exact* $k$-nearest neighbor ($k$-NN) problem demands the $k$ points minimizing a distance $\delta(q, x)$. For $d \lesssim 20$, spatial partitioning trees (kd-trees, ball trees) solve it efficiently. But embeddings produced by modern models live in $d = 128$–$4096$ dimensions, where the curse of dimensionality renders exact methods degenerate: Beyer *et al.* showed that in high dimensions, the contrast $\frac{\max \delta - \min \delta}{\min \delta}$ vanishes, so exhaustive scan — $O(nd)$ per query — becomes the only exact option, and it is far too slow for services answering thousands of queries per second over billions of vectors.

The field therefore embraced *approximation*. An algorithm is said to solve the $(c)$-approximate nearest neighbor problem if it returns $x$ with $\delta(q,x) \le c \cdot \delta(q, x^*)$ for true neighbor $x^*$. In practice, the community replaced worst-case approximation guarantees with the *empirical* criterion of **recall@k**: the fraction of the true top-$k$ returned, averaged over a query workload. This single move — trading theorems for benchmarks — reshaped a research area and created the modern vector-database industry.

Two families of methods won this empirical race:

1. **Graph-based search**, culminating in the Hierarchical Navigable Small World (HNSW) graph of Malkov and Yashunin [1]. A sparse proximity graph is built over the corpus; search is a greedy best-first traversal with a priority queue, starting from the sparsest layer and descending. HNSW dominates recall–latency Pareto frontiers on in-memory benchmarks and is the default index in Milvus, Weaviate, Qdrant, and pgvector's HNSW implementation.
2. **Quantization-based search**, culminating in the inverted-file-plus-asymmetric-distance-computation (IVFADC) architecture of Jégou *et al.* [2] and its industrial realization in Faiss [3][4]. Vectors are compressed 32–64× by product quantization, coarse-quantized into inverted lists, and searched with lookup-table distance estimation. IVF-PQ dominates when the index must fit on GPUs or in tight RAM budgets at billion scale.

This thesis unifies these paradigms. Section 2 formalizes the problem and metrics. Section 3 develops methodology: how layered graphs are built and searched, how product quantization encodes distances, and how benchmarks are designed so comparisons mean something. Section 4 dives deep into four technical mechanisms. Section 5 gathers empirical evidence from the literature. Section 6 is candid about limitations. Section 7 synthesizes a decision guide.

> **Thesis claim:** Graph traversal and quantized inverted indexes are not competitors to be ranked once and for all, but points on a *memory–latency–recall* Pareto surface whose optimal operating point is dictated by corpus scale, query distribution, and hardware — and the benchmark methodology that reveals the surface is itself a first-class scientific contribution.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Formal problem

Let $(\mathbb{R}^d, \delta)$ be a metric space (in practice $\delta$ is Euclidean $\ell_2$, inner product, or cosine distance). Let $\mathcal{X}$ contain $n$ vectors. A *data structure* for ANN consists of a preprocessing phase (index construction) and a query phase. The quality axes are:

- **Recall@k**: $\text{R@k} = \frac{1}{Q}\sum_{q} \frac{|R_k(q) \cap T_k(q)|}{k}$, where $T_k(q)$ are the true top-$k$ (computed by brute force offline) and $R_k(q)$ the returned set.
- **Throughput**: queries per second (QPS), usually single-threaded in canonical benchmarks [5].
- **Build cost**: index construction time and peak memory.
- **Footprint**: bytes per vector of the final index.

The consequence is methodological: ANN research is *inherently* empirical. Claims are not proved; they are plotted. An algorithm's merit is its position on the recall–QPS frontier, and any result reported without build-time and memory accounting is incomplete [5].

### 2.2 The two design languages

All practical ANN indexes decompose into two tools [4]:

| Tool | Idea | Representative |
|---|---|---|
| **Non-exhaustive search** | Partition the space; at query time, visit only promising partitions | IVF coarse quantizer, tree ensembles, layered graphs |
| **Compression** | Replace vectors by short codes; estimate distances without full vectors | Product quantization, scalar quantization, binary codes |

Graph methods are almost pure non-exhaustive search with full-precision vectors; IVF-PQ combines both. The rest of the thesis is the detailed anatomy of each.

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 How HNSW is built and searched

HNSW [1] constructs a *multi-layer* proximity graph. Each element is assigned a maximum layer $l$ drawn from an exponentially decaying distribution:

$$l = \left\lfloor \frac{-\ln(u)}{m_L} \right\rfloor, \qquad u \sim U(0,1),$$

where $m_L$ controls layer density (commonly $1/\ln M$). Layer 0 contains all $n$ points; each higher layer contains an exponentially smaller nested subset. Every layer is itself a Navigable Small World (NSW) proximity graph with bounded degree $M$.

**Insertion.** A new point descends greedily from the top layer to its assigned layer, finding approximate nearest neighbors at each level, then inserts itself at layers $0 \dots l$, connecting to at most $M$ neighbors selected by the *neighbor-selection heuristic* (Section 4.2). Bidirectional edges are added, and existing vertices prune excess connections.

**Search.** For a query $q$:

```
enter ← top-layer entry point
for layer = L … 1:            # coarse navigation
    enter ← greedy_search(q, enter, layer, ef=1)
candidates ← beam_search(q, enter, layer 0, ef)   # fine search
return top-k of candidates
```

The beam search maintains a dynamic candidate list of size `ef` (the *exploration factor*). At layer 0 the search explores `ef` nearest candidates; `efConstruction` governs build-time candidate lists, and `ef` (often `efSearch`) governs the recall–latency knob at query time [1].

### 3.2 How IVF-PQ is built and searched

IVF-PQ [2][3] combines a coarse partition with aggressive compression:

1. **Coarse quantization.** Run $k$-means ($n_{\text{list}}$ centroids, e.g. 4096–65536) over the corpus (or a sample). Each vector is assigned to its nearest centroid's inverted list.
2. **Residual product quantization.** For a vector $x$ in list $c$, compute the residual $r = x - c$ and encode it with product quantization: split $r \in \mathbb{R}^d$ into $m$ subvectors $r = (r_1, \dots, r_m)$, each quantized independently by its own $k$-means codebook of $2^b$ centroids ($b=8$ typical). The code is $m$ bytes.
3. **Search.** For query $q$: find the `nprobe` nearest coarse centroids; for each, use *asymmetric distance computation* (ADC) — precompute per-subspace lookup tables of $\|q_j - c_{j,i}\|^2$ (only $m \cdot 256$ entries), then score each candidate code by $m$ table lookups and additions. The database vectors are *never decompressed*.

Memory per vector drops from $4d$ bytes to roughly $m$ bytes — a 32× reduction for $d=128, m=8$ — at the cost of a quantization-distortion term added to every distance estimate.

### 3.3 How ANN systems are honestly benchmarked

The ANN-Benchmarks framework [5] established the methodological standard the field still follows:

- **Fixed artifacts.** Pre-generated HDF5 datasets (SIFT-1M, GIST-1M, GloVe, DEEP-1B, …) with train/test splits and precomputed top-100 ground truth.
- **Implementation, not idea.** Each algorithm ships as a Docker container; the framework benchmarks *implementations*, recognizing that SIMD layout, allocator choice, and wrapper overhead materially move the frontier.
- **The frontier is the figure.** Results are recall-vs-QPS Pareto curves; build time and index size are reported separately. A point is only meaningful relative to the envelope.

> **Methodological principle:** In ANN research, the benchmark harness is the instrument and the Pareto frontier is the measurement. An index evaluated on one dataset, one $k$, and one thread count has produced an anecdote, not a result.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Why the hierarchy gives logarithmic scaling

Plain NSW graphs [1] exhibit polylogarithmic search cost: greedy traversal from a random entry point wanders. HNSW's layering fixes this by *separating distance scales*. The argument mirrors skip lists, and Malkov and Yashunin make the analogy explicit — they note the structure admits a straightforward balanced distributed implementation precisely because of it [1].

Consider the descent: at layer $\ell$, the expected number of nodes is $n \cdot p^\ell$ for $p = e^{-m_L}$-ish decay. Greedy search at layer $\ell$ makes $O(1)$ hops in expectation before local-minimum trapping, because long-range edges at that scale jump over already-explored territory. With $L = O(\log n)$ layers, total hops scale as $O(\log n)$. Each hop costs one distance computation against at most $M$ neighbors, giving:

> **Theorem (HNSW search complexity, empirical):** Expected query cost is $O(M \cdot \log n)$ distance computations at fixed recall, with the constant controlled by `ef`. Malkov and Yashunin verify this scaling empirically to $n = 10^7$–$10^8$, showing the slope of log(time) vs. log($n$) remains near unity while NSW's bends upward [1].

The exponential layer assignment is load-bearing: uniform layer sizes would concentrate edges at one scale and recover NSW's polylog behavior. The parameter $m_L$ tunes the pyramid's steepness; the canonical $m_L = 1/\ln M$ keeps the expected overlap between adjacent layers constant.

### 4.2 The neighbor-selection heuristic: diversity beats proximity

The most subtle idea in HNSW is *which* $M$ neighbors a node keeps. The naive choice — the $M$ nearest candidates — clusters edges in the densest direction and leaves other directions disconnected, a failure mode Malkov and Yashunin document on highly clustered data [1]. The fix is a **relative neighborhood graph (RNG)**-inspired heuristic: a candidate $e$ is accepted as a neighbor of the inserted node $x$ only if it is *closer to $x$ than to any already-accepted neighbor*:

```
selected ← ∅
for e in candidates sorted by δ(x, e):
    if ∀ s ∈ selected: δ(x, e) < δ(e, s):
        selected ← selected ∪ {e}
```

Geometrically, this is a directional diversity filter: it prunes candidates "shadowed" by an existing neighbor, spreading edges across angular sectors like a miniature RNG [6]. The payoff is measurable: on clustered distributions, heuristic selection shifts the recall–QPS frontier outward by a wide margin, and it is the reason HNSW's layer-0 graph is simultaneously sparse ($M \approx 16$–$64$) and navigable. Later work (NSG [6]) pushed this idea further, explicitly pruning to an approximate monotonic relative neighborhood graph and reporting additional gains — evidence that *edge diversity, not edge count*, is the scarce resource in proximity graphs.

### 4.3 Product quantization: exponential codebooks at linear cost

Product quantization's [2] elegance is combinatorial. Quantizing $\mathbb{R}^d$ with a flat $k$-means codebook of $K$ centroids costs $O(Kd)$ memory and gives $K$ reproduction values. PQ instead writes the space as a Cartesian product of $m$ subspaces and quantizes each with $k^* = 2^b$ centroids:

- **Memory:** $m \cdot 2^b \cdot (d/m) = 2^b d$ floats — independent of $m$.
- **Reproduction values:** $(2^b)^m$ — exponential in $m$.

For $d=128, m=8, b=8$: 256 KB of codebooks generate $256^8 \approx 1.8 \times 10^{19}$ distinct reproduction vectors, each stored as an 8-byte code. The distortion decomposes additively across subspaces, so training is $m$ independent $k$-means problems on $(d/m)$-dimensional data — trivially parallel and sample-efficient.

**Asymmetric distance computation** is the second half of the trick. Given query $q$ and code $(i_1, \dots, i_m)$:

$$\hat{\delta}^2(q, x) = \sum_{j=1}^m \|q_j - c_{j,i_j}\|^2.$$

The $m \times 256$ table $T[j][i] = \|q_j - c_{j,i}\|^2$ is built once per query ($O(m \cdot 256 \cdot d/m) = O(256d)$), after which each candidate costs $m$ lookups and adds. Jégou *et al.* show ADC strictly dominates symmetric distance computation (quantizing the query too), because it leaves the query unquantized and halves one source of error [2]. On SIFT-1M with $m=8$, ADC-based IVFADC reaches recall@100 above 0.9 while scanning only a fraction of the lists — the operating point that made billion-scale search on a single machine feasible [2][3].

**Optimized PQ (OPQ)** [3] adds a learned orthonormal rotation $R$ minimizing quantization distortion before the subspace split, $\min_R \sum_x \|Rx - q(Rx)\|^2$, solved by alternating $k$-means with an Orthogonal Procrustes step. On correlated descriptors (e.g., CNN embeddings), OPQ's balanced variance allocation across subspaces yields large recall gains at fixed $m$ — a reminder that PQ's independence assumption across subspaces is its main statistical weakness.

### 4.4 The hybrid: IVFADC and the Faiss engineering stack

Neither tool alone suffices at billion scale: pure PQ still scans all $n$ codes per query ($O(nm)$ lookups — too slow at $n = 10^9$), while pure IVF with full vectors explodes memory. IVFADC [2] composes them: the coarse quantizer reduces the scan to `nprobe` lists ($\approx$ 0.1–1% of data), and PQ compresses the residual so the scanned subset fits in cache. Faiss [3][4] industrialized this composition with:

- **GPU kernels** for $k$-selection and ADC table construction, achieving the fastest known billion-scale search on 2017-era hardware [3];
- **Composable index factories** (`IVF65536_HNSW32,PQ64`), letting practitioners stack a graph-based coarse quantizer under PQ compression;
- **Additive quantization and residual codecs** in later releases for sub-byte-per-dimension regimes [4].

## 5 Empirical Evidence / Proofs

We now collect the literature's headline measurements. All figures below are reported values from the cited works, on their stated hardware; they are evidence, not guarantees.

### 5.1 HNSW's empirical dominance in-memory

Malkov and Yashunin [1] benchmark HNSW against prior art (FLANN, Annoy-era baselines, LSH variants, and flat NSW) on SIFT-1M ($d=128$) and GIST-1M ($d=960$):

- On SIFT-1M at recall@100 $\approx 0.99$, HNSW answers queries in **single-digit milliseconds** single-threaded, roughly an order of magnitude faster than the nearest competitor at equal recall [1].
- Scaling to SIFT-100M, the logarithmic regime holds: query time grows sublinearly while competitors' curves steepen — the experiment that made HNSW the default recommendation of the ANN-Benchmarks leaderboards for in-memory workloads [5].
- Memory cost is the tariff: HNSW stores full-precision vectors plus $\approx 2M$ edges per node. For $d=128, M=16$: $\sim$512 B (vectors) + $\sim$256 B (graph) per point — an order of magnitude hungrier than IVF-PQ's $\sim$16–64 B/point [1][2].

The independent survey of Li *et al.* [7] confirms the pattern across a wider algorithm zoo: graph methods (HNSW, NSG) occupy the upper-right of recall–QPS plots on million-scale data, with NSG occasionally extending the envelope further at the cost of a much heavier build [6][7].

### 5.2 IVF-PQ's memory–recall economics

Jégou *et al.* [2] validated PQ on **two billion** SIFT vectors — at 8 bytes/vector, the codes fit in 16 GB where raw vectors would need 1 TB. Key numbers (SIFT-1B, $m=8$, IVF with 8192 lists):

- Recall@100 $\approx$ 0.6–0.8 at millisecond-scale latency, with graceful degradation as `nprobe` shrinks [2].
- Johnson *et al.* [3] moved IVFADC to GPUs and reported searching **1 billion vectors in tens of milliseconds** with recall@10 above 0.9 in favorable configurations, plus the fastest exact-search kernels known at the time — the result that turned Faiss into infrastructure [3].
- The Faiss design paper [4] documents adoption as the core engine of major vector-database vendors and 30k+ GitHub stars, with the library framing every index as an explicit trade among search time, quality, memory, and training cost [4].

### 5.3 What the benchmark framework itself proved

ANN-Benchmarks [5] contributed a meta-result: *implementations* dominate *ideas*. Re-running the same nominal algorithm (HNSW) across wrappers shifts its frontier by factors of 2–5×; SIMD distance kernels, memory layout, and parameter grids matter as much as the graph theory. The framework's Docker-isolated protocol, Pareto-frontier visualization, and fixed ground-truth artifacts became the field's de facto peer review for systems claims — a methodological export arguably as influential as any single index [5].

| Index family | Recall@100 regime | QPS (SIFT-1M, 1 thread) | Bytes/vector | Build cost |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| HNSW ($M{=}16$, ef tuned) | 0.95–0.999 | $10^3$–$10^4$ | ~500–800 | minutes |
| IVF-PQ ($n_{\text{list}}{=}4096$, $m{=}8$) | 0.7–0.95 | $10^3$–$10^5$ | ~16–64 | minutes–hours |
| IVF-PQ + OPQ + rerank | 0.9–0.99 | $10^2$–$10^3$ | ~64–128 | hours |
| Flat brute force | 1.0 | $10^1$–$10^2$ | $4d$ | none |

*(Orders of magnitude synthesized from [1][2][3][5]; exact numbers depend on hardware, $k$, and parameter grids.)*

## 6 Limitations

Honesty requires enumerating where each paradigm breaks.

1. **HNSW's memory wall.** Full-precision storage plus graph edges makes HNSW roughly 10–30× hungrier than IVF-PQ. At $n = 10^9, d = 768$ (modern text embeddings), HNSW needs terabytes of RAM — the regime where IVF-PQ or disk-based graphs (DiskANN-style) take over [2][4].
2. **Build cost and dynamism.** HNSW insertion is incremental and reasonably fast, but *deletions* and *updates* are second-class: tombstoning and re-insertion degrade graph quality over time, and no production HNSW matches the update story of an LSM-backed store. IVF-PQ retraining (re-running $k$-means) is a batch operation that invalidates codes [4].
3. **Distribution shift.** Both methods are tuned on corpus statistics. Out-of-distribution queries — queries from a different embedding distribution than the corpus — collapse recall for IVF (wrong lists probed) and degrade graph navigation (entry points mislead). The literature's train/test splits usually *hide* this by sampling queries from the corpus distribution [5].
4. **The metric assumption.** PQ's additive distortion analysis and HNSW's RNG heuristic both assume Euclidean-like geometry. Maximum inner-product search (MIPS), common in recommendation, requires transformations (e.g., norm augmentation) that distort the operating characteristics [7].
5. **Benchmark myopia.** ANN-Benchmarks [5] is single-CPU, in-memory, static-corpus, and recall@k-centric. It does not measure tail latency under concurrent load, index-update throughput, filtered search (metadata predicates), or cost-per-query on cloud hardware — the quantities a vector-database buyer actually optimizes. Newer harnesses (e.g., Big-ANN-Benchmarks) extend the protocol to billion-scale and out-of-distribution tracks, but the field still under-reports the operational axes.
6. **Theoretical gaps.** HNSW's logarithmic scaling is *empirical*; no worst-case or smoothed analysis explains the constant factors or characterizes failure modes. PQ's rate–distortion analysis assumes subspace independence that real embeddings violate (hence OPQ). The field remains, in the best sense, an engineering science ahead of its theory.

## 7 Conclusion

This thesis has developed the two pillars of high-performance vector search and the methodology that keeps their comparison honest. HNSW [1] showed that a layered proximity graph with diversity-preserving edge selection achieves logarithmic-time, high-recall search that dominates in-memory frontiers. Product quantization and IVFADC [2] showed that Cartesian-structured compression with asymmetric distance computation collapses the memory footprint by orders of magnitude, making billion-scale search a single-machine problem. Faiss [3][4] turned both ideas into composable infrastructure, and ANN-Benchmarks [5] turned evaluation into a science of Pareto frontiers.

For the practitioner, the decision guide is now compact:

- **Corpus fits in RAM, latency-sensitive, high recall required** → HNSW (tune `M`, `efConstruction` at build, `efSearch` per query SLA).
- **Billion-scale, memory- or GPU-constrained, moderate recall acceptable** → IVF-PQ/OPQ (tune `nlist`, `nprobe`, `m`; add exact rerank on shortlists for the last recall points).
- **Filtered, dynamic, or distributed workloads** → compose: IVF partitions for sharding and filtering, HNSW within shards, PQ for cold tiers.

Open problems abound: learned partitioners that adapt to query distributions, theoretically grounded graph indexes, update-friendly dynamic graphs, and benchmarks that measure operational reality rather than single-threaded recall. The vector database is no longer a niche index — it is the memory system of the LLM era — and its foundations, surveyed here, remain an active frontier.
