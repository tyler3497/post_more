# Interactive Theorem Proving at Scale: Dependent Elaboration in Lean 4, Tactic Frameworks, and the Mathlib Verification Pipeline

## Abstract

Lean 4 represents the convergence of dependent type theory, practical programming-language design, and proof automation into a single system capable of verifying research-level mathematics at scale [1], [2]. This article examines the architectural components that make interactive theorem proving productive at scale: the dependent type theory of the Calculus of Inductive Constructions, with Pi and Sigma types and decidable definitional equality; the *elaborator*, which synthesizes implicit arguments and resolves typeclass instances through metavariable unification; the *tactic framework*, exposed through Lean 4's macro and tactic monads; the automation layer of the `simp` normalizer and the best-first `aesop` proof search [8]; the mathlib4 library, continuously verified by a CI pipeline [3]; and the small trusted kernel that certifies every proof term. Case studies — the *liquid tensor experiment* [5], [6], the *sphere eversion* formalization [7], and elementary results such as Fermat's little theorem — illustrate how these components interact in practice. We compare Lean 4 against Coq, Isabelle/HOL, and Agda, and discuss persistent limitations: elaboration predictability, kernel bootstrap trust, and the cost of maintaining a unified classical library.

## 1 Introduction

Interactive theorem proving has moved from verifying textbook lemmas to certifying original research mathematics. The transition was driven less by any single logical insight than by the co-design of logical foundations, elaboration machinery, tactic frameworks, and social infrastructure for libraries. Lean 4 is the most complete exemplar of this co-design: it is simultaneously a dependently typed programming language, a proof assistant, and the host of mathlib, a mathematical library exceeding a million lines of formalized mathematics [2], [3], [9].

The central architectural decision in Lean is a sharp separation between the *elaborator* — a large, sophisticated, and deliberately untrusted program that turns human-written proof scripts into proof terms — and the *kernel*, a small, isolated type-checker that certifies those terms. This separation enables aggressive automation: tactics, proof search, and macros can be arbitrarily clever because their output is always re-checked. Understanding Lean at scale means understanding this pipeline, the type theory it targets, and the engineering that keeps the library coherent.

This article proceeds as follows. Section 2 reviews the dependent type theory of Lean's Calculus of Inductive Constructions. Section 3 describes the methodology of this survey — how architectural claims about the system are grounded in the published literature and repositories. Section 4 dives into elaboration, typeclass inference, and tactic metaprogramming. Section 5 presents empirical case studies and the soundness properties they rest on. Section 6 discusses limitations, and Section 7 concludes.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Dependent Type Theory

Lean's logical foundation is a version of the Calculus of Inductive Constructions (CIC), an extension of Martin-Löf type theory with *inductive types* and a predicative hierarchy of universes [1], [4]. Two type formers are central:

- **Pi types** `Π (x : A), B x` — dependent functions, where the result type `B` may depend on the argument. Non-dependent arrows `A → B` are the special case. Under the Curry–Howard correspondence, Pi types encode universal quantification and implication.
- **Sigma types** `Σ (x : A), B x` — dependent pairs, encoding existential quantification and subset types when `B x` is a proposition.

Judgmental (definitional) equality is decided by *normalization*: two terms are definitionally equal when they reduce to a common weak head normal form under beta, delta, zeta, iota, and eta rules. Decidability of definitional equality is what makes the kernel's type-checking algorithm terminate, and it is the single most important metatheoretic property of the logic.

Inductive types are defined by their *constructors* and used through automatically generated *eliminators* (recursors), which embody dependent induction. The classic example is the natural numbers:

```lean
/-- The natural numbers, as an inductive type. -/
inductive Nat where
  | zero : Nat
  | succ : Nat → Nat
```

Every inductive definition must satisfy *strict positivity* conditions so that the eliminator is sound; the kernel enforces this at definition time.

### 2.2 The Propositions-as-Types Pipeline

In Lean, a *tactic proof* is a program that constructs a term of a proposition's type. Tactics operate on a *proof state* — a stack of goals, each a metavariable `?m : T` to be assigned a proof term — and each tactic transforms the state by refining goals, splitting them, or discharging them (Figure 2). The human writes surface syntax; the elaborator produces a fully explicit term; the kernel certifies it (Figure 1). Automation sits entirely above the kernel boundary (Figure 3).

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Elaborator: Metavariables, Unification, and Postponement

The gap between what a human writes and what the kernel checks is the elaborator's domain. Surface Lean code is *implicit*: arguments are omitted, notations are overloaded, and types are often unstated. The elaborator's job is to produce a fully explicit, fully typed term.

The central mechanism is the *metavariable*: an existential placeholder `?m : T` standing for an unknown term [1], [2]. Elaboration proceeds in layers of monads:

1. `ElabM` — the base elaboration monad with error reporting and local context.
2. `TermElabM` — elaboration of terms, creating metavariables for implicit arguments.
3. `MetaM` — the core metavariable monad: unification, typeclass synthesis, and *postponement* of constraints that cannot yet be solved.

Consider the classic example of implicit argument synthesis:

```lean
theorem add_comm (n m : Nat) : n + m = m + n := by
  induction n with
  | zero => simp [Nat.zero_add]
  | succ k ih => rw [Nat.succ_add, ih, Nat.add_succ]
```

The notation `n + m` elaborates to `HAdd.hAdd ?inst ?n ?m` where `?inst` is a typeclass argument. The elaborator unifies `?inst` against the expected type `Nat`, triggers *typeclass inference* — a Prolog-like backtracking search over declared instances — and fills in `instHAdd` for the naturals. Overloaded notation, coercions, and implicit universe levels are all handled by the same metavariable machinery.

Typeclass inference deserves special attention because it is mathlib's primary organizational device. Algebraic structures in mathlib are *typeclasses*: `Group G`, `Ring R`, `TopologicalSpace X`. When a lemma is stated for a general `Group G`, every application requires the elaborator to synthesize the instance — a process that can chain through dozens of instances (`CommGroup` → `Group` → `Monoid` …) and is cached aggressively for performance. This is how mathlib achieves both generality and usability: the same lemma `mul_assoc` applies to matrices, polynomials, and permutation groups because the elaborator finds the right instance automatically [3].

### 4.2 Tactic Metaprogramming: Macros and the Tactic Monad

Lean 4's most radical design decision is that tactics are *ordinary programs written in Lean itself* [2]. Earlier systems (Coq's Ltac, Lean 3's tactic monad in C++) kept tactic implementation in a separate language or layer. Lean 4 unifies the meta-level and the object-level: the same compiler, the same type system, and the same libraries serve both proving and programming.

Two mechanisms implement this:

- **Macros** (`macro_rules`) — syntax-to-syntax transformations, hygienic and composable, expanding new notation and proof commands into core syntax before elaboration.
- **Tactic monads** (`TacticM`, `TermElabM`) — full access to the elaborator state: goals, metavariables, the local context, and the environment. A tactic is a function `TacticM Unit` that reads the goal stack and assigns metavariables.

Because tactics are Lean programs, they can be *debugged, profiled, tested, and refactored* with ordinary software-engineering tooling. The `simp` tactic, the `ring` normalizer, and `aesop` are all implemented as Lean libraries, not as trusted C++ code. The consequence is a dramatic reduction in the trusted computing base: everything above the kernel is untrusted by construction.

### 4.3 Automation: The `simp` Normalizer and `aesop` Search

Two automation components carry most of the routine proof burden in mathlib.

**The `simp` normalizer.** `simp` rewrites a goal with a large, ordered set of *simp lemmas* — conditional rewrite rules tagged with the `@[simp]` attribute [4]. It applies them to a fixpoint, discharging side conditions recursively with the same machinery. What makes `simp` a *normalizer* rather than a mere rewriter is its confluent core: built-in procedures for beta/eta reduction, congruence closure, and decision procedures for linear arithmetic are interleaved with user lemmas, so `simp` converges on canonical forms. A single `simp` call can close goals spanning arithmetic, set manipulation, and propositional logic, precisely because the lemma database is populated by the entire library.

**`aesop`: white-box best-first search.** Whereas `simp` is deterministic rewriting, `aesop` performs *proof search* [8]. It maintains a tree of goals, expands nodes using a user-configured set of rules classified as *safe* (provability-preserving, e.g., introduction rules) or *unsafe* (backtrackable, e.g., applying a lemma that may not fit), and pursues the most promising branch with best-first prioritization. The white-box design philosophy is key: rule application is predictable and traceable, so users can diagnose why a search failed and add the missing rule. `aesop` integrates the simplifier for normalization and handles the subtle problem of *metavariables shared across goals* without naive backtracking — a genuine algorithmic contribution of the CPP 2023 paper.

Together, `simp` and `aesop` mean that routine lemmas in mathlib are often proved by one or two tactic invocations, with human effort concentrated on the proof's *structure* — induction principles, key intermediate claims, case splits — rather than bureaucratic rewriting.

### 4.4 Kernel Architecture and the Trust Boundary

The kernel is the smallest, most carefully reviewed component: roughly ten thousand lines of C++ implementing type-checking for CIC [2]. It performs exactly one task — given a fully elaborated term and its purported type, decide whether the judgment holds. It knows nothing of tactics, macros, typeclasses, or notation.

![Kernel architecture diagram](/thesis/ths_1788964289725_9e1d-2.webp)

The trust story is therefore unusually clean. *Elaboration is untrusted*: a buggy tactic can produce a wrong proof term, but the kernel will reject it, and the user sees an error rather than a false theorem. *The kernel is the entire trusted computing base*, alongside the compiler that built it and the hardware it runs on. This is the formal content of the soundness proposition that underwrites every Lean formalization:

> **Theorem:** *Elaborator soundness (proposition).* Let `E` be the elaborator and `K` the kernel. Any proof term `t` accepted by `K` with type `P` (a proposition) is a valid derivation of `P` in CIC, regardless of which tactics, macros, or automation produced `t`. Unsound metaprograms therefore manifest as *rejected* terms — user-visible errors — never as silently accepted false theorems [1], [2].

And the kernel's own decidability guarantee:

> **Theorem:** *Type-checking decidability.* If definitional equality in Lean's CIC is decidable via normalization to weak head normal form, then the kernel's `isDefEq` procedure decides `Γ ⊢ e₁ ≡ e₂`, and consequently the type-checking judgment `Γ ⊢ e : T` is decidable. Every elaborated proof term is therefore checkable by the kernel in isolation, with no dependence on elaborator state [2].

## 5 Empirical / Proofs

### 5.1 The Liquid Tensor Experiment

The most demanding stress test of Lean at scale was Peter Scholze's *liquid tensor experiment*. In late 2020, Scholze — a Fields medalist — publicly challenged the formalization community to verify a key theorem from his condensed mathematics program, writing that he found the prospect "a litmus test" for whether proof assistants could handle cutting-edge research mathematics [5]. The formalization, carried out primarily by Johan Commelin and collaborators in the `liquid_tensor_experiment` repository, was completed in mid-2022 [6].

The experiment exercised every layer described above. The mathematics required *extensive* typeclass-driven abstraction (topological algebra, homological algebra over condensed abelian groups), heavy `simp`-based normalization to manage the bureaucratic overhead of category-theoretic diagram chasing, and new library infrastructure that was upstreamed into mathlib. Its success demonstrated that the elaborator-plus-kernel architecture scales to proofs whose *statements* alone require thousands of lines of definitions — the hard part was formalizing the definitions, not checking the logic.

### 5.2 Sphere Eversion and the h-Principle

A second landmark is the formalization of *sphere eversion* — Smale's paradox, the regular homotopy turning the 2-sphere inside out — together with Gromov's h-principle machinery behind it [7]. Led by Patrick Massot and collaborators, this project formalized deep differential topology: immersions, the h-principle for open ample differential relations, and the explicit eversion construction.

Notably, the project pioneered *formalization blueprints*: a LaTeX document whose lemmas are hyperlinked to their formalized counterparts, letting mathematicians track correspondence between the informal proof and the mechanized one. This is a methodological contribution as much as a mathematical one — it addresses the *readability* gap between human proof sketches and machine-checked developments, which is one of the main costs of scale.

### 5.3 Fermat's Little Theorem: Automation at the Small Scale

At the opposite end of the spectrum, elementary results demonstrate the automation layer's maturity. Fermat's little theorem (`a^(p-1) ≡ 1 mod p` for prime `p` not dividing `a`) is formalized in mathlib's number theory library, and its proof is a short tactic script: the combinatorial core is handled by library lemmas about finite fields and cyclic groups, with `simp`, `rw`, and `field_simp` discharging the algebraic manipulation. The contrast with the liquid tensor experiment is instructive: the *same* elaborator, typeclass search, and kernel certify both a three-line number-theory proof and a multi-year research formalization.

### 5.4 Comparison of Proof Assistants

| Feature | Lean 4 | Coq | Isabelle/HOL | Agda |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Logical foundation | CIC + quotients, classical via choice | CIC (predicative, no built-in quotients) | Classical HOL (simple type theory) | Martin-Löf type theory (no Prop universe) |
| Proof style | Tactic scripts + term mode, metaprogrammed in Lean | Ltac/SSReflect scripts, Ltac2 metaprogramming | Isar structured proofs + tactics | Direct term construction, interactive holes |
| Kernel / TCB | Small C++ kernel; elaborator untrusted | Small OCaml kernel; plugins trusted less | LCF-style kernel; everything reduces to kernel inferences | Haskell type-checker as kernel |
| Elaboration | Metavariable unification + typeclass search | Canonical structures, typeclass hints | Type inference + overloading | Implicit arguments, instance search |
| Key automation | `simp` normalizer, `aesop` best-first search | `lia`, `ring`, `auto`, CoqHammer | Sledgehammer (external ATPs), `simp`, `auto` | `Auto`, reflection-based solvers |
| Metaprogramming | Tactics written in Lean 4 itself | OCaml plugins / Ltac2 | ML (Isabelle/ML) | Haskell reflection |
| Flagship library | mathlib4 (unified, classical) | Stdlib + MathComp (constructive-leaning) | Archive of Formal Proofs | Agda stdlib (small, constructive) |

The table highlights Lean 4's distinctive combination: a dependent logic *with* classical reasoning available by default, tactics implemented in the object language, and a single unified library developed under continuous verification.

### 5.5 The Mathlib Verification Pipeline

Mathlib's scale is sustained by engineering, not just logic [3], [9]. The repository is organized as a *directed acyclic graph* of modules (Figure 4): foundational files (`Logic`, `Data`) at the bottom, through `Algebra`, `Topology`, and `Analysis`, up to specialized theories like `NumberTheory`. Every pull request passes a CI pipeline — full build, linter checks (style, naming, documentation), and a bors-style merge queue — so the library is *continuously verified*: at any commit, every theorem in the library type-checks against the current kernel. This invariant is what makes a million-line library trustworthy rather than merely large.

## 6 Limitations

**Elaboration predictability.** The elaborator is a complex heuristic system — metavariable postponement, typeclass backtracking, coercion insertion — and its behavior can be *fragile*: small changes to a statement can produce dramatically different elaboration paths or inscrutable error messages. Debugging elaboration failures remains a expert skill, and the "untrusted but opaque" middle layer is the main usability cost of the architecture.

**Kernel bootstrap and trust.** The kernel is small but not verified: it is a C++ program whose correctness is established by code review and testing, not by a machine-checked proof. A fully bootstrapped, self-verified kernel (a kernel whose correctness proof is itself checked by the kernel) remains an open engineering challenge, and the compiler and hardware beneath it are trusted implicitly.

**Classical foundations and constructive tension.** Mathlib commits to classical logic (choice, excluded middle, quotients) as the default, which maximizes mathematical coverage but forfeits computational content: proofs in mathlib generally cannot be *executed*. Systems like Agda and Coq's MathComp preserve constructivity at the cost of library breadth. Whether a unified classical library or a constructive one is the better long-term bet is unresolved.

**Automation ceilings.** `simp` and `aesop` handle routine goals, but deep mathematical reasoning — inventing the right induction, the right auxiliary construction — remains human work. Machine-learning-guided premise selection and tactic prediction (e.g., Lean Copilot-style tools) are promising but not yet load-bearing in the mathlib workflow.

## 7 Conclusion

Lean 4's success at scale rests on a single architectural insight carried through consistently: *separate the untrusted intelligence from the trusted checker, and make the checker as small as possible*. Dependent type theory with decidable definitional equality gives the kernel a well-defined job; metavariable-based elaboration with typeclass inference bridges human notation and explicit terms; metaprogramming in Lean itself makes tactics first-class software; `simp` and `aesop` automate the routine; and mathlib's CI pipeline turns a million lines of mathematics into a continuously verified artifact. The liquid tensor experiment and sphere eversion show the ceiling; Fermat's little theorem shows the floor. The remaining challenges — elaboration predictability, kernel verification, and the constructive–classical tradeoff — define the next decade of interactive theorem proving.
