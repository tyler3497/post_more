# Paxos Protocols: From Single-Decree Consensus to Multi-Paxos and Practical State Machine Replication

## Abstract

This thesis traces the Paxos family of protocols from Leslie Lamport's original single-decree formulation in *The Part-Time Parliament* [1], through the streamlined exposition of *Paxos Made Simple* [2], to Multi-Paxos as a practical state machine replication engine. We present the two-phase protocol, the ballot discipline, and the quorum intersection logic with full rigor, then prove the central safety invariant: once a value is *chosen*, every subsequently chosen value is identical. We show how Multi-Paxos amortizes the prepare phase across a sequence of consensus instances to build a replicated log, analyze leader election and liveness under partial synchrony, and survey engineering refinements — Fast Paxos, Cheap Paxos, Flexible Paxos, and the hard-won operational lessons of *Paxos Made Live* [3]. We close with a careful accounting of limitations: the crash-fault (non-Byzantine) model, liveness's dependence on synchrony, reconfiguration subtleties, and the leader bottleneck — and argue that, despite alternatives such as Raft [4], Paxos remains the canonical framework for reasoning about crash-fault consensus.

## 1. Introduction

Few algorithms in computer science have achieved the paradoxical status of the Paxos consensus protocol: simultaneously the *de facto* standard for building consistent replicated services, and a notoriously difficult algorithm whose most famous exposition was disguised as a whimsical history of a fictional Greek parliament [1]. When Lamport first submitted "The Part-Time Parliament" in 1990, all three referees found it "mildly interesting, though not very important"; it was published, essentially unrevised, in 1998 — by which point real systems were already demanding exactly the guarantees it provided.

The problem Paxos solves is conceptually simple to state and surprisingly hard to solve correctly. Given a set of *processes* (servers), some of which may crash and stop, and an *asynchronous network* that may lose, duplicate, or arbitrarily delay messages — but never corrupt them — the processes must agree on a single *value*. The agreement must satisfy two properties:

- **Safety.** No two processes ever disagree: at most one value can be *chosen*, and once chosen, every process that decides must decide that value.
- **Liveness.** *Eventually*, some value is chosen and learned by a majority of processes.

The Fischer–Lynch–Paterson impossibility result tells us that no deterministic protocol can guarantee *both* safety and liveness in a fully asynchronous system with even one crash failure. Paxos's answer is to guarantee safety unconditionally, and liveness only *eventually* — specifically, once the system enters a sufficiently long period of synchrony with a single stable leader.

> **Theorem:** (Consensus Dichotomy) In an asynchronous message-passing system with at least one crash failure, no deterministic algorithm can guarantee both agreement and termination in all executions.

This thesis develops the full arc of the protocol family. We begin with the single-decree ("Basic") Paxos protocol, where the goal is agreement on one value (§4.1), prove its safety invariant from the quorum intersection property (§4.2), and show how running many instances in sequence — *Multi-Paxos* — yields a practical replicated state machine (§4.3). We then confront the engineering reality: the gap between a correct protocol and a working system is where most projects fail, a lesson catalogued with unusual honesty in "Paxos Made Live" [3].

## 2. Background

### 2.1 The State Machine Approach

The motivation for consensus comes from the *state machine approach* to fault tolerance: model a service as a deterministic state machine, replicate it on multiple servers, and ensure that all replicas execute the *same sequence of commands*. If every replica starts in the same state and applies the same deterministic commands in the same order, they all reach the same state — and the service survives the crash of any minority of replicas. Consensus is precisely the sub-problem of agreeing on the *n*-th command in that shared sequence [1].

### 2.2 The System Model

Paxos assumes a **crash-fault, asynchronous** model:

1. **Processes** can fail by *stopping* (crashing). They do not exhibit Byzantine behavior — they never lie or send contradictory messages. Each process may have **stable storage** that survives crashes.
2. **Messages** may be lost, duplicated, or delivered after arbitrary delay, but are never corrupted. A process may redeliver a message it already sent.
3. The set of processes is *fixed and known* (in the basic formulation); we return to reconfiguration in §6.
4. No bound on message delay is assumed for *safety*; for *liveness*, we assume the system eventually enters a long enough period of partial synchrony.

### 2.3 Why Two-Phase Commit Is Not Enough

The classical two-phase commit (2PC) protocol blocks if its coordinator crashes after the prepare phase: participants cannot safely abort *or* commit. Paxos's key advance is to make agreement **non-blocking** as long as a *majority* (a quorum) of participants remains alive — any process may initiate a new round at any time [1].

### 2.4 Related Protocols

- **Viewstamped Replication** (Oki & Liskov, 1988) — a primary-copy replication protocol with a similar flavor of view changes.
- **Raft** (Ongaro & Ousterhout, 2014) — a consensus algorithm explicitly designed for understandability; Howard and Mortier [4] show it is essentially Paxos restricted to leaders with up-to-date logs.
- **Zab, PBFT, HotStuff** — protocols in the primary-backup and Byzantine traditions; outside the scope of this thesis but sharing the replicated-log architecture.

## 3. Methodology

Our analysis proceeds on two complementary tracks. *First*, a formal track: we give the protocol's message handlers as executable pseudocode, state the safety invariant precisely, and derive it from the quorum intersection property with a proof sketch in Lamport's style. *Second*, an engineering track: we examine how the protocol is extended into Multi-Paxos, how leader election and ballot management are implemented in practice, and what the major industrial experience reports [3] identify as the dominant failure modes of real deployments.

**Notation.** We write:

- $N$ for the set of acceptors, $|N| = 2f+1$ tolerating $f$ crashes.
- A *quorum* $Q \subseteq N$ with $|Q| = f+1$ (a majority).
- Ballot numbers $b \in \mathbb{N}$, totally ordered (in practice, $(\text{counter}, \text{proposer id})$ compared lexicographically).

The central combinatorial fact exploited throughout:

> **Theorem:** (Quorum Intersection) Any two majorities of a $2f+1$-process set intersect in at least one process. In general, any two quorums of a quorum system intersect.

This single fact is the load-bearing wall of the entire protocol family. Every safety argument below reduces, directly or indirectly, to it.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Basic (Single-Decree) Paxos Protocol

Basic Paxos solves *single-decree consensus*: the processes agree on one value. It distinguishes three logical roles (a process may play several):

- **Proposers** suggest values by initiating *ballots*.
- **Acceptors** vote on ballots; each has stable storage recording the highest ballot it has *promised* and the highest ballot it has *accepted*, plus the accepted value.
- **Learners** discover which value was *chosen*.

The protocol has two phases, each with two sub-steps:

**Phase 1 — Prepare (the "discovery" phase):**

1. A proposer picks a fresh ballot number $b$ and sends `Prepare(b)` to a quorum of acceptors.
2. An acceptor receiving `Prepare(b)` with $b$ greater than any ballot it has promised *promises* never to accept any ballot $< b$, and replies with `Promise(b, b_a, v_a)` — the highest ballot $b_a < b$ it has accepted, and its value $v_a$ (or $\bot$ if none).

**Phase 2 — Accept (the "voting" phase):**

1. If the proposer receives `Promise` replies from a quorum, it picks a value $v$: if any reply carried an accepted value, $v$ is the value from the reply with the *highest* $b_a$; otherwise $v$ is any value (typically the client's proposed value). It sends `Accept(b, v)` to a quorum.
2. An acceptor receiving `Accept(b, v)` accepts it — recording $(b, v)$ in stable storage — unless it has promised a higher ballot, in which case it rejects (or ignores) the message. Accepted values are reported to learners.

A value is **chosen** when a quorum of acceptors has accepted the same ballot $(b, v)$.

The phase-1 rule is the protocol's subtle genius: a new proposer cannot simply overwrite history. By forcing every ballot to first *discover* what prior ballots may have already decided, and then *adopt* the most recent discovered value, the protocol guarantees that choice is sticky.



### 4.2 Quorums, Ballots, and the Safety Argument

The safety proof hinges on one invariant:

> **Theorem:** (Ballot Monotonicity / Safety Invariant) If a value $v$ is chosen in ballot $b$ (i.e., accepted by a quorum $Q$ in ballot $b$), then every ballot $b' > b$ that reaches Phase 2 proposes $v$.

> **Proof:** Consider the lowest ballot $b' > b$ that reaches Phase 2. Its Phase 1 contacted some quorum $Q'$. By quorum intersection, $Q \cap Q' \neq \varnothing$; pick an acceptor $a$ in the intersection. Acceptor $a$ accepted $(b, v)$ and promised only ballots $\geq b'$ afterward. Since $b'$ is the *lowest* ballot above $b$ reaching Phase 2, no ballot in $(b, b')$ was accepted at $a$ — so $a$'s `Promise` reply to $b'$ reports a ballot $\geq b$ with value $v$, and no reply from $Q'$ can report a ballot in $(b, b')$. Hence the maximum accepted ballot seen by $b'$ is at least $b$, and its associated value is $v$ (any ballot $> b$ reporting a different value would contradict minimality of $b'$). Therefore $b'$ proposes $v$. ∎

**Corollary.** At most one value is ever chosen; any two chosen values are equal.

Note what the proof does *not* require: it does not require that the proposer's Phase-1 and Phase-2 quorums be identical, nor that every acceptor respond, nor any timing assumption. *Safety is timing-independent.* This is why Paxos is correct in a fully asynchronous network.

**Flexible quorums.** The proof only needs $Q \cap Q' \neq \varnothing$ between *Phase-1* quorums and *Phase-2* quorums. Flexible Paxos (Howard, Malkhi, Spiegelman, 2016) observes that Phase-1 and Phase-2 quorums need not both be majorities: shrinking Phase-2 quorums while enlarging Phase-1 quorums preserves intersection while reducing common-case message fan-out.

### 4.3 Multi-Paxos and State Machine Replication

Basic Paxos decides one value. A replicated state machine needs an infinite *sequence* of values — a log. Multi-Paxos runs one instance of Basic Paxos per log *slot* (instance number $i = 1, 2, 3, \ldots$), with a crucial optimization: a stable **leader** (also called the *distinguished proposer*) executes Phase 1 once for *all* slots, then pipelines Phase 2 for individual slots.

The mechanics:

1. **Leader election.** Some process becomes leader for a ballot $b$ by completing Phase 1 (sending `Prepare(b)` for all slots, or a single batched prepare) with a quorum. While it remains the unique active leader, it skips Phase 1 for new slots.
2. **Steady state.** The leader assigns each client command to the next free slot $i$ and sends `Accept(b, i, cmd)`; acceptors accept; the value is chosen when a quorum accepts. This is **one message round-trip** per command in the common case.
3. **Failover.** If the leader crashes or is suspected, a new leader emerges with a higher ballot, re-runs Phase 1 (learning which slots are already decided, which are in flight, and filling gaps — possibly with no-ops), and resumes.

The replicated log converges because *each slot* independently satisfies the Basic Paxos safety invariant. Learners execute commands in slot order once all preceding slots are decided, yielding a linearizable command sequence.

| Aspect | Basic Paxos | Multi-Paxos (stable leader) |
|---|---|---|
| Decides | Single value | Unbounded log of slots |
| Messages per decision | 4 (2 round trips) | 2 (1 round trip, steady state) |
| Phase 1 frequency | Every ballot | Once per leadership change |
| Leader required | No (any proposer) | Yes, for efficiency |
| Throughput bottleneck | Ballot contention | Leader's network/CPU |

**Pipelining and batching.** Practical implementations pipeline Phase-2 messages across consecutive slots and batch multiple client commands per slot. The "Paxos Made Live" report [3] emphasizes that such batching is often the difference between a research prototype and a production system: with batching, their implementation sustained throughput competitive with the commercial databases of the era.

### 4.4 Leader Election, Liveness, and Practical Optimizations

**The liveness problem.** Safety is guaranteed; liveness is not. In the classic *dueling proposers* scenario, two proposers repeatedly preempt each other's ballots and no ballot ever completes — the FLP impossibility result made concrete. The standard remedy is **leader election with randomized backoff**: after a failure detector suspects the leader, exactly one process (with high probability) attempts to become the new leader, and retains leadership as long as it keeps receiving quorum acknowledgments.

**Leases and read optimization.** A stable leader can serve *reads* locally without running consensus, provided it holds a **lease** — a time-bounded guarantee that no other leader can emerge. This converts read-heavy workloads from $O(\text{quorum})$ messages to zero, at the cost of depending on loosely synchronized clocks (bounded clock drift, not synchrony of message delivery) [3].

**Checkpointing and log compaction.** Replicas periodically snapshot their state machine at slot $k$ and discard earlier log entries, so recovery and catch-up are bounded — essential for reconfiguration and for bounding recovery time.

**Disk Paxos and Cheap Paxos.** The acceptor role requires only *stable storage*, not computation: Disk Paxos runs acceptors on shared disks, letting diskless proposers drive consensus. Cheap Paxos runs the protocol with $f+1$ main acceptors plus $f$ lightweight auxiliaries that participate only during leader changes.

### 4.5 Variations and Modern Refinements

- **Fast Paxos** [6] allows clients to send proposals *directly to acceptors*, skipping the leader in the common case (2 message delays instead of 3), at the cost of larger quorums and a more complex collision-recovery protocol when concurrent proposals collide.
- **Generalized Paxos** (Lamport, 2005) exploits *commutativity*: if two commands commute (their order does not matter), they need not be ordered relative to each other at all, allowing greater concurrency for workloads like independent key writes.
- **EPaxos** (Moraru, Andersen, Kaminsky, 2013) generalizes this into a leaderless protocol where any replica can commit non-interfering commands in one round trip.
- **Vertical / Matchmaker Paxos** address *reconfiguration*: changing the acceptor set itself requires consensus on the configuration, handled by running a separate consensus instance (or matchmakers) over configurations.
- **Deconstruction results.** Boichat et al. [5] showed that Paxos's complexity can be factored: the consensus core decomposes into simpler building blocks (e.g., a weak *consensus* oracle plus a *failure detector*), clarifying exactly which assumptions buy which guarantees.

## 5. Empirical Results / Proofs

### 5.1 The Safety Proof, End to End

Section 4.2 gave the core invariant. We now close the loop:

> **Theorem:** (Agreement) If values $v_1$ and $v_2$ are both chosen (in ballots $b_1 \le b_2$), then $v_1 = v_2$.

> **Proof:** By the Ballot Monotonicity invariant, every ballot $b' > b_1$ reaching Phase 2 proposes $v_1$; in particular $b_2$ proposes $v_1$, and the value chosen in $b_2$ is the value proposed in $b_2$. Hence $v_2 = v_1$. ∎

> **Theorem:** (Validity / Non-triviality) Every chosen value was proposed by some proposer.

> **Proof:** Phase 2 proposes either the proposer's own input or a value adopted from a `Promise` reply — itself accepted in an earlier ballot, hence proposed earlier. By induction on ballot number, every proposed value traces back to some proposer's input. ∎

These two theorems plus the quorum-intersection lemma constitute the complete safety case for Basic Paxos; Multi-Paxos inherits them slot-wise.

### 5.2 Performance Characteristics

The "Paxos Made Live" deployment [3] — a fault-tolerant database built on Multi-Paxos at Google — reported the following qualitative results, which subsequent systems have replicated:

1. **Latency.** Steady-state commit costs one round trip to a quorum; wide-area deployments pay the speed of light between the leader and the farthest quorum member.
2. **Throughput.** Batching and pipelining move the bottleneck from protocol rounds to the leader's CPU and network; the Live system measured throughput competitive with contemporary commercial databases.
3. **Failover.** Leader failure detection dominates recovery time: aggressive detectors yield second-scale failover, conservative ones trade availability for stability against false suspicions.
4. **The dominant costs are not algorithmic.** The report's central finding is that *most* engineering effort went to issues the papers never mention: disk corruption handling, multi-operation transactions layered atop single-shot consensus, lease management, and snapshot protocols. The consensus core was, in their words, the easy part.

### 5.3 Paxos vs. Raft: An Empirical Postscript

Howard and Mortier [4] performed the head-to-head the field had long needed, re-expressing Paxos in Raft's own terminology. Their finding: the protocols are *nearly identical* in mechanism, differing essentially only in leader election — Raft restricts leadership to servers with up-to-date logs, while Paxos allows any server to lead (requiring the new leader to repair its log via Phase 1). The understandability gap, they argue, is largely a property of the *papers*, not the *protocols*.

## 6. Limitations

Intellectual honesty requires stating plainly what the Paxos family does *not* do:

1. **Crash faults only.** The entire safety argument assumes processes fail by stopping and never send contradictory messages. A single Byzantine participant voids every guarantee; Byzantine agreement requires fundamentally different protocols ($3f+1$ replicas, cryptographic authentication).
2. **No liveness guarantee in asynchrony.** Dueling proposers can livelock forever; liveness depends on eventual partial synchrony and a stable leader. Paxos is *safe* under asynchrony but only *live* under timing assumptions — the FLP boundary can be managed, never crossed.
3. **Reconfiguration is a second protocol.** The basic formulation assumes a fixed acceptor set. Changing membership requires deciding the new configuration via consensus itself, introducing subtleties (joint consensus, matchmakers) that are easy to get wrong [3].
4. **Leader bottleneck and failover latency.** All steady-state traffic flows through the leader; it is a throughput bottleneck and a latency single point. Failover latency is bounded below by failure-detection time, which trades off against false positives.
5. **Disk dependence.** Acceptors must durably record promises and acceptances before responding. A lost `fsync` — a disk that acknowledges writes it did not persist — breaks the safety invariant silently; the "Paxos Made Live" authors list storage correctness as a leading source of real-world incidents [3].
6. **Complexity of the full stack.** Basic Paxos is a few dozen lines; a *production* Multi-Paxos system — leader election, leases, log compaction, snapshots, reconfiguration, multi-operation transactions — is tens of thousands of lines. The protocol's reputation for difficulty comes less from the core algorithm than from this surrounding machinery, much of it under-specified in the original papers [1][2][3].

## 7. Conclusion

From the fictional parliament of Paxos [1] to the fault-tolerant databases underpinning modern cloud infrastructure [3], the Paxos protocol family has traveled from a rejected curiosity to the canonical framework for crash-fault consensus. Its core ideas — *ballots* that order competing proposals, *quorums* whose intersection forces convergence, the *phase-1 discovery* rule that makes choice sticky, and the *leader-based amortization* of Multi-Paxos — are simple enough to fit on a page and subtle enough to have occupied a generation of researchers.

The enduring lesson is twofold. *Theoretically*, Paxos shows how to extract the maximum possible guarantee from an asynchronous system: unconditional safety, with liveness recovered exactly when the network cooperates. *Practically*, it shows that a correct consensus core is necessary but nowhere near sufficient — the surrounding engineering of leases, snapshots, reconfiguration, and storage correctness is where real systems are won or lost [3].
