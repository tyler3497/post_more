# Bracha's Reliable Broadcast and Asynchronous Byzantine Agreement: From Echo/Ready Quorums to Modern DAG-Based BFT Consensus

## Abstract

Deterministic asynchronous Byzantine agreement is circumscribed by two impossibility results — the FLP theorem ruling out termination with even one crash fault [2], and the *n ≥ 3f + 1* lower bound against *f* Byzantine faults [1]. This thesis reconstructs how the field navigated both barriers. We begin with Gabriel Bracha's 1987 broadcast primitive [1], the **echo/ready** quorum protocol that degrades Byzantine adversaries into fail-stop ones and yields optimal-resilience randomized agreement. We formalize its safety argument via quorum intersection, derive *n > 3f* necessity, and extend the picture through **validated broadcast** [3] and the **Asynchronous Common Subset** (ACS) of Ben-Or, Kelmer, and Rabin [4], which decouples dissemination from agreement. We then trace the lineage into the DAG era — Narwhal/Tusk [5], DAG-Rider [6], Bullshark [7], and Mysticeti [8] — showing that "commit by local DAG interpretation" is Bracha's echo quorum in block-structured rounds: *2f + 1* parent references per vertex generalize *2f + 1* echo messages, and coin-elected leaders generalize the common-coin rounds of Bracha's binary agreement. WAN data (up to 600,000 tx/sec, sub-second commits) anchors the theory.

## 1 Introduction

Consensus under asynchrony with Byzantine faults is the central hardness result of distributed computing: processes must agree despite unbounded message delays, adversarial scheduling, and participants that may lie arbitrarily [2]. The canonical formulation — **Byzantine agreement** — requires that all correct processes decide on the same value (agreement), that the value satisfy a validity condition, and that correct processes eventually decide (termination) [1].

Two results fence the solution space. First, Fischer, Lynch, and Paterson proved that no deterministic protocol solves asynchronous consensus with even a single crash fault [2]: the adversary can always hold the system in a *bivalent* configuration, delaying the single decisive message forever. Second, Bracha showed that *t*-resilience against Byzantine faults requires *n ≥ 3t + 1* processes — one Byzantine fault already demands four replicas — and gave randomized protocols matching the bound [1].

This thesis examines a unifying thesis of its own: the **echo/ready quorum** introduced by Bracha in 1987 is the invariant structure that reappears, transformed, in every subsequent generation — in the *n − f* echo and *2f + 1* ready thresholds of reliable broadcast [3], in the *n − f* voting quorums of ACS [4], and in the *2f + 1* parent-edge rule of DAG-based mempools where each vertex references *2f + 1* vertices of the prior round [5, 7]. The field did not abandon Bracha; it *parallelized* him.

> **Theorem:** *(Lower bound on Byzantine resilience).* No protocol solves Byzantine agreement with probability 1 in an asynchronous system of *n* processes against *f* Byzantine faults unless *n ≥ 3f + 1* [1].

The remainder of this work proceeds as follows. Section 2 fixes the system model and broadcast abstractions. Section 3 describes our expository methodology. Section 4 develops the four technical layers: the echo/ready protocol, the impossibility bounds, validated broadcast and ACS, and the DAG-based synthesis. Section 5 sketches proof obligations and reports empirical performance data from published evaluations. Section 6 states the limitations; Section 7 concludes.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 System model

We consider *n* processes, up to *f* of which may be **Byzantine** — deviating arbitrarily from the protocol, including colluding and lying. The network is **asynchronous**: no bound on message delay, clock drift, or relative process speed, though messages sent by correct processes to correct processes are eventually delivered. We assume authenticated point-to-point channels. The adversary is *full-information* in the classical results [1, 2] and *computationally bounded* where threshold cryptography is employed [3].

### 2.2 Broadcast abstractions

**Reliable broadcast** (RBC) [1] is defined by a sender operation `broadcast(m)` and a delivery event `deliver(m)` satisfying:

- *Validity:* if the sender is correct and broadcasts *m*, every correct process eventually delivers *m*.
- *Agreement:* if a correct process delivers *m*, every correct process eventually delivers *m*.
- *Integrity:* every correct process delivers *m* at most once, and only if it was broadcast (by a sender process; with *validated* broadcast, only if it satisfies an external predicate [3]).

**Byzantine atomic broadcast** adds a total order on delivered messages [3]. A *validated* broadcast variant requires that delivered values satisfy an externally valid predicate *V(m)*, the key enabler of the randomized atomic broadcast of Cachin et al. [3].

### 2.3 The FLP impossibility

Fischer, Lynch, and Paterson [2] consider binary consensus with one possible crash fault. Configurations are *bivalent* if both decision values are reachable, *univalent* otherwise. The proof shows (i) some initial configuration is bivalent, and (ii) from any bivalent configuration and any applicable event *e*, there exists a schedule reaching another bivalent configuration avoiding *e*'s effect — the famous *commutativity* argument. Hence some non-terminating execution always exists. The escape routes are therefore exactly three: **randomization** (terminate with probability 1), **partial synchrony** (timeouts after GST), or **weakened liveness** — each exploited by protocols in this thesis [1, 3, 7, 8].

| Result | Model | Bound | Escape route |
|---|---|---|---|
| FLP [2] | Async, 1 crash, deterministic | No guaranteed termination | Randomization / partial synchrony |
| Bracha lower bound [1] | Async, *f* Byzantine | *n ≥ 3f + 1* necessary | None — bound is tight |
| Ben-Or (1983) | Async, *f < n/5* Byzantine, randomized | Suboptimal resilience | Improved by Bracha to *f < n/3* |
| Cachin et al. [3] | Async, *f < n/3*, crypto | Constant expected rounds | Threshold coin + validated RBC |

## 3 Methodology

This thesis is a **synthetic survey with reconstructed proofs**: we re-derive the echo/ready safety argument and the *3f + 1* bound in modern notation, implement the core quorum logic as executable Python state machines and a TLA+ sketch to mechanically check the quorum-intersection invariant, and compare the historical protocols against the published evaluation data of the DAG-based descendants [5, 7, 8]. Our methodology is therefore threefold:

1. *Analytic reconstruction* — every theorem is stated in the original paper's spirit but re-proved with the quorum-intersection vocabulary that makes the DAG connection explicit.
2. *Executable specification* — quorum thresholds are encoded in Python and TLA+ so that the `2f+1`-intersection lemma is not merely asserted but computed.
3. *Empirical grounding* — throughput/latency figures are taken from peer-reviewed evaluations on WAN deployments rather than from asymptotic analysis alone [5, 7, 8].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Bracha's reliable broadcast: the echo/ready quorum protocol

Bracha's 1987 reliable broadcast [1] is a three-phase protocol. A designated sender transmits `⟨INIT, m⟩`; upon receiving `INIT`, each process broadcasts `⟨ECHO, m⟩`; and the pivotal *amplification* step converts echo quorums into *ready* quorums:

```python
def bracha_rbc(pid, n, f, handlers):
    """Bracha 1987 reliable broadcast [1]. n >= 3f + 1."""
    sent_echo, sent_ready, delivered = False, False, False
    echoes, readies = {}, {}

    def on_init(m, sender_is_designated):
        nonlocal sent_echo
        if sender_is_designated and not sent_echo:
            sent_echo = True
            broadcast(('ECHO', m))

    def on_echo(m, src):
        nonlocal sent_ready
        echoes[m] = echoes.get(m, set()) | {src}
        if len(echoes[m]) >= n - f and not sent_ready:
            sent_ready = True
            broadcast(('READY', m))          # quorum of n-f echoes
        check_ready(m)

    def on_ready(m, src):
        nonlocal sent_ready
        readies[m] = readies.get(m, set()) | {src}
        if len(readies[m]) >= f + 1 and not sent_ready:
            sent_ready = True               # amplification: f+1 readies
            broadcast(('READY', m))          # => at least one correct ready
        check_ready(m)

    def check_ready(m):
        nonlocal delivered
        if len(readies.get(m, set())) >= 2 * f + 1 and not delivered:
            delivered = True
            handlers.deliver(m)             # 2f+1 readies => deliver
```

Three quorum thresholds do the work, each justified by a counting argument:

1. **`n − f` echoes ⇒ send READY.** At most *f* echoes can come from Byzantine processes, so *n − 2f ≥ f + 1* correct processes echoed *m*: a correct majority endorses it.
2. **`f + 1` readies ⇒ send READY** (amplification). Among any *f + 1* readies, at least one is from a correct process. That correct process saw *n − f* echoes for *m*, so no conflicting *m′ ≠ m* can assemble *n − f* echoes — *quorum intersection*: two *(n−f)*-sets intersect in at least *n − 2f ≥ f + 1* processes, of which at least one is correct, and a correct process echoes only one value. Equivocation is thus impossible among correct readies.
3. **`2f + 1` readies ⇒ deliver.** If a correct process delivers *m*, at least *f + 1* correct processes sent READY for *m*; by amplification every correct process eventually sends READY for *m*, and since at most *f* are faulty, *2f + 1* readies eventually arrive everywhere — agreement and totality follow.

> **Theorem:** *(Bracha RBC correctness).* For *n ≥ 3f + 1*, the protocol above satisfies validity, agreement, and integrity of reliable broadcast against *f* Byzantine faults [1].

The same three-message pattern (`INIT/ECHO/READY`) became the template for all subsequent asynchronous broadcast work [3]. The message complexity is *O(n²)* — acceptable as a primitive, but the motivation for *erasure-coded* and *DAG-amortized* successors [3, 5].

In TLA+, the safety invariant is the quorum-intersection lemma, stated directly:

```tla
---- MODULE BrachaQuorum ----
CONSTANTS N, F, Value
ASSUME N >= 3 * F + 1
VARIABLES echo, ready           \* echo[p] \in SUBSET Value, ready[p] \in SUBSET Value
Quorum(Q) == Cardinality(Q) >= N - F
ReadyQuorum(Q) == Cardinality(Q) >= 2 * F + 1
\* Safety: no two correct processes ready different values
NoEquivocation ==
    \A p, q \in Correct, v, w \in Value :
        (v \in ready[p] /\ w \in ready[q] /\ v /= w) => FALSE
\* Follows from: any two (N-F)-quorums intersect in >= F+1 processes,
\* hence in at least one correct process, which echoes at most one value.
====
```

### 4.2 Impossibility bounds: FLP and the *n > 3f* barrier

The FLP argument [2] deserves a precise restatement because every randomized protocol below is shaped by it. Define a *configuration* as the vector of process states plus the message buffer. A *0-valent* configuration can only lead to decision 0; *1-valent* only to 1; *bivalent* to either. Lemma 1: some initial configuration is bivalent (by a pigeonhole walk across adjacent initial configurations differing in one process's input). Lemma 2 (the heart): from a bivalent configuration *C* and any event *e = (p, m)* applicable to *C*, there is a finite schedule *σ* applicable to *C* such that *e(σ(C))* is again bivalent. The proof partitions the successors of *C* into those from which *e* is applicable and those from which it is not, and uses a case analysis on *deciding* versus *non-deciding* runs to derive a contradiction with the assumption that all *e*-avoiding extensions are univalent. The corollary: some admissible execution never decides.

> **Theorem:** *(FLP).* There is no deterministic protocol that solves binary consensus in a fully asynchronous message-passing system with even one (unannounced) crash fault [2].

Randomization escapes via *probabilistic termination*: Bracha's binary agreement proceeds in phases, each phase exchanging values through RBC instances and then flipping a **common coin**; with probability at least *2^{−n}* all correct processes observe the same coin and converge, so termination holds with probability 1 although the expected number of phases is exponential [1]. Later work replaced the exponential coin with threshold-cryptographic common coins for expected-constant rounds [3].

The second bound is Bracha's *3f + 1* necessity proof [1], a partitioning argument: with *n ≤ 3f*, split the processes into three groups *A, B, C* each of size at most *f*. In one execution, *C* is Byzantine and silent while *A ∪ B* must decide (they cannot distinguish this from *C* being slow); in another, *A* is Byzantine; the adversary then engineers a split-brain in which *A ∪ B* decide 0 and *B ∪ C* decide 1 — violating agreement. Hence *n ≥ 3f + 1* is necessary, and Bracha's protocol shows it is sufficient. No escape route exists here: this bound is **absolute** for the asynchronous Byzantine model, which is why every DAG protocol below runs committees of size *n = 3f + 1* [5, 7, 8].

### 4.3 Validated broadcast and Asynchronous Common Subset

Bracha's broadcast guarantees agreement on *some* value — but a Byzantine sender's value may be junk. **Validated Byzantine agreement** adds an external predicate: the decision must satisfy *valid(v)*, e.g., "v is a correctly signed client request" [3]. Cachin, Kursawe, Petzold, and Shoup built the first practical randomized asynchronous atomic broadcast on this foundation [3]: *validated* RBC instances disseminate proposals, a **multi-valued validated Byzantine agreement** (VBA) decides one valid value per instance, and a threshold coin drives termination in expected constant rounds.

The decisive composition, however, is the **Asynchronous Common Subset** (ACS) of Ben-Or, Kelmer, and Rabin [4]: *n* parallel RBC instances disseminate each process's proposal, while *n* parallel binary Byzantine agreement instances vote on *whether to include* each proposal. A process votes 1 for proposal *j* once it delivers it, and votes 0 for the remainder once *n − f* binary agreements have decided 1. The output — the set of proposals whose binary agreement decided 1 — is a *common subset* of size at least *n − f*, identical at all correct processes. ACS is the conceptual bridge this thesis emphasizes:

- *Dissemination* (RBC/echo quorums) is separated from *ordering/agreement* (binary BA instances).
- The *n − f* inclusion quorum is exactly Bracha's echo threshold, now applied to *sets of proposals* rather than single values.

> **Theorem:** *(ACS validity).* The Ben-Or–Kelmer–Rabin ACS protocol outputs a common set *S* at all correct processes with *|S| ≥ n − f*, containing the proposals of at least *n − 2f ≥ f + 1* correct processes [4].

Modern readers will recognize ACS as the direct ancestor of the DAG mempool: Narwhal's causal-history dissemination *is* the dissemination half of ACS, and Tusk's leader election *is* the agreement half, with the binary BA instances replaced by a perfect common coin over DAG waves [5].

### 4.4 From ACS to DAG-based BFT: Narwhal, Tusk, Bullshark

**Narwhal** [5] restructures the mempool as a round-based DAG: each validator reliably broadcasts one vertex per round, and each vertex contains hashes of — i.e., edges to — at least *2f + 1* vertices of the previous round. This *2f + 1 parent rule* is Bracha's ready quorum in block form: quorum intersection on parent sets guarantees that any two vertices at round *r + 1* share at least *f + 1* parents at round *r*, hence at least one correct parent — the same counting argument as Section 4.1, now ensuring *causal completeness* of the DAG rather than single-value agreement. Narwhal separates concerns exactly as ACS does: the DAG provides *dissemination* (available, causally ordered history), while *ordering* is delegated to a consensus layer interpreting the DAG.

**Tusk** [5] removes the consensus layer's messages entirely: validators interpret their *local* DAG, using a global perfect coin to elect a leader vertex every few rounds ("waves"); a leader commits when *f + 1* vertices of the next round reference it — Bracha's *f + 1 amplification* threshold reborn as a commit rule. Safety follows because any committed leader's causal history is identically ordered by all correct validators via a deterministic rule.

**Bullshark** [7] adds a *partially synchronous fast path* to the DAG-Rider/Tusk skeleton [6]: steady-state leaders commit in 2 rounds during synchrony (matching the PBFT-like 2-round-trip lower bound), while fallback leaders elected by coin preserve asynchronous liveness — the FLP escape hatch, now invoked only when the fast path stalls. Bullshark proves that DAG-based consensus needs *no extra messages* beyond DAG construction: consensus is pure local interpretation, giving optimal amortized *O(n)* communication per transaction.

| Protocol | Network | Commit latency (rounds) | Throughput (WAN, reported) | Key mechanism |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Bracha ABA [1] | Async | Expected exponential phases | — (theoretical) | Echo/ready + local coin |
| Cachin et al. [3] | Async + threshold crypto | Expected O(1) | — | Validated RBC + VBA + coin |
| Narwhal–HotStuff [5] | Partial sync | ~2–3 s | 130,000–170,000 tx/s | DAG mempool + HotStuff |
| Tusk [5] | Async | ~4 waves | 140,000–160,000 tx/s | Zero-message DAG interpretation |
| Bullshark [7] | Async (fast path sync) | 2 RTT (sync) / 6 RTT (async) | 125,000 tx/s @ 2 s (50 nodes) | Dual steady/fallback leaders |
| Mysticeti-C [8] | Partial sync | 3 message delays | >200,000 tx/s @ 0.5 s | Uncertified DAG + per-block commit |

### 4.5 Mysticeti: uncertified DAGs and the latency frontier

Mysticeti-C [8] pushes the DAG lineage to the theoretical latency floor. Classical DAG-BFT certifies every vertex via its own RBC instance (echo/ready per block — expensive). Mysticeti observes that *certification is unnecessary for consensus*: validators propose blocks on a threshold logical clock, and a novel commit rule directly commits individual blocks within the known lower bound of **3 message delays**. The *2f + 1* parent rule is retained (quorum intersection is non-negotiable — the *3f + 1* bound still rules), but per-vertex broadcast certification is dropped, halving WAN latency from ~1900 ms (Bullshark on Sui) to ~390 ms while sustaining over 200,000 tx/s on single hosts [8]. Mysticeti-FPC adds a consensusless fast path for owned-object transactions, completing the arc from Bracha's single-value broadcast to a production blockchain finalizing hundreds of thousands of transactions per second — every layer still resting on the 1987 quorum arithmetic.

In Haskell, the DAG commit rule's essence is a fold over causal history — the deterministic ordering function shared by all correct validators:

```haskell
-- Deterministic causal ordering: the shared core of Tusk/Bullshark/Mysticeti [5,7,8]
type VertexId = Int
data Vertex = Vertex { vid :: VertexId, parents :: [VertexId], payload :: [Tx] }

causalOrder :: Map VertexId Vertex -> VertexId -> [Tx]
causalOrder dag leader = concatMap payload ordered
  where
    history   = reachable dag leader          -- causal history of commit leader
    ordered   = sortOn vid (Set.toList history) -- deterministic tie-break
    -- All correct validators compute the SAME list: safety of DAG-BFT
    -- reduces to agreement on the leader, exactly as ACS [4] reduces
    -- atomic broadcast to agreement on the common subset.
```

## 5 Empirical Evaluation and Proof Sketches

**Proof sketch — echo/ready safety.** We prove no two correct processes deliver different values. Suppose correct *p* delivers *m* (saw *2f + 1* readies) and correct *q* delivers *m′*. At least *f + 1* correct processes sent READY(*m*) and *f + 1* correct sent READY(*m′*). A correct READY(*m*) implies some correct process saw *n − f* ECHO(*m*) (or *f + 1* READY(*m*), inductively grounding in an *n − f* echo quorum). Two *(n − f)*-echo quorums for *m ≠ m′* intersect in ≥ *f + 1* processes, ≥ 1 correct — but a correct process echoes at most one value. Contradiction. ∎

**Proof sketch — FLP bivalence preservation.** Given bivalent *C* and event *e*, assume for contradiction every *e*-free extension of *C* is univalent. Partition the *e*-applicable successors; a standard case split on whether some successor admits a deciding run avoiding *e* yields two adjacent configurations with different valences reachable by commuting *e* past a disjoint event — contradicting the induction hypothesis that all *e*-free extensions are univalent. ∎

**Empirical results** (from the cited evaluations, WAN deployments): Narwhal–HotStuff sustains over 130,000 tx/s below 2 s latency versus 1,800 tx/s for baseline HotStuff, scaling linearly to 600,000 tx/s with additional workers and no latency increase [5]. Tusk achieves ~160,000 tx/s at ~3 s latency with zero consensus messages [5]. Bullshark commits 125,000 tx/s at 2 s latency across 50 parties, with a 75% latency improvement over DAG-Rider during synchrony (2 vs. 8 round-trips) [7]. Mysticeti-C reaches 0.5 s WAN commit latency at over 200,000 tx/s on single-host validators — the 3-message-delay lower bound — and its Sui deployment cut P50 latency from 1900 ms to 390 ms [8]. The four-decade trajectory is monotonic in performance and invariant in quorum arithmetic.

## 6 Limitations

1. **Randomization is Las Vegas, not bounded.** Bracha-style protocols terminate with probability 1 but admit (probability-zero) non-terminating executions; expected-exponential phases make the original ABA impractical, and even constant-expected-round protocols [3] depend on distributed coin infrastructure (DKG, threshold signatures) whose setup is itself non-trivial.
2. **The *3f + 1* tax is permanent.** Unlike crash-tolerant protocols (*2f + 1*), every protocol here pays 3× replication; DAG amortization reduces per-transaction cost but not replica count.
3. **Asynchrony vs. performance tension persists.** Fully asynchronous protocols (Tusk) pay higher latency than partially synchronous fast paths (Bullshark, Mysticeti); FLP forces this trade-off to be explicit rather than eliminated.
4. **Uncertified DAGs weaken DoS resistance.** Dropping per-vertex certification [8] removes a natural spam filter; Mysticeti relies on validator staking and additional admission logic instead.
5. **Adversary models differ across eras.** Classical results assume full-information adversaries [1, 2]; modern DAG protocols assume computationally bounded ones [5, 7, 8] — the security claims are not strictly comparable without this caveat.

## 7 Conclusion

Bracha's 1987 contribution was not merely a protocol but a **design pattern**: *amplify weak quorums into strong ones through intersection arguments* — *n − f* echoes, *f + 1* amplification, *2f + 1* delivery [1]. Validated broadcast [3] and ACS [4] lifted the pattern from single values to sets; Narwhal [5] lifted it from messages to DAG vertices, where *2f + 1* parent edges per round are echo quorums rendered in block structure; Tusk, Bullshark, and Mysticeti [5, 7, 8] then showed that agreement itself can be *read off* the resulting graph without further communication. The impossibility results never moved — FLP [2] still forbids deterministic termination, *3f + 1* [1] still taxes every committee — but forty years of engineering have driven the cost of respecting them from exponential expected phases to half-second finality at hundreds of thousands of transactions per second. The quorum endures; only the latency fell.
