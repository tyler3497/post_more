# Neural Architecture Search: Weight-Sharing Supernets, DARTS, and One-Shot Evaluation Methods

## Abstract

Weight sharing transformed neural architecture search from an exercise in thousand-GPU-hour computation into a discipline of amortization: a single over-parameterized supernet is trained once, and its constituent subgraphs — the candidate architectures — are evaluated by inheriting its weights rather than being trained from scratch. This thesis develops a unified treatment of weight-sharing supernets, differentiable architecture search (DARTS), and one-shot evaluation. We formalize the supernet as a joint distribution over architectures and parameters, derive DARTS' continuous relaxation and its bilevel optimization with first- and second-order gradient estimators, and analyze why the celebrated efficiency rests on a fragile statistical premise: the ranking correlation between inherited-weight accuracy and stand-alone accuracy. We examine collapse phenomena, fairness of weight sharing, memory-sparing path binarization, and reproducible benchmarks, and close with open theoretical questions that still separate search from engineering.

## 1 Introduction

Neural architecture search (NAS) promises to replace human trial-and-error in network design with optimization over a space of architectures $\mathcal{A}$. The first-generation methods — reinforcement-learning controllers [1] and evolutionary strategies — treated each candidate architecture $\alpha \in \mathcal{A}$ as an independent training problem, minimizing the *stand-alone* training objective

$$w_\alpha^* = \arg\min_w \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{train}}(w; \alpha), \qquad a_\alpha = \mathrm{acc}_{\mathrm{val}}(w_\alpha^*)$$

and then ranking architectures by $a_\alpha$. This is statistically unimpeachable and computationally catastrophic: evaluating tens of thousands of architectures consumes on the order of $10^4$ GPU-hours, restricting NAS to institutions with industrial-scale compute.

**Weight sharing** [2,3] introduced a single, decisive amortization. Rather than training each architecture independently, one trains a *supernet* — a directed acyclic graph (DAG) containing every candidate architecture as a subgraph — with parameters $\mathcal{W}$ shared across architectures. After (or during) supernet training, an architecture's quality is estimated *one-shot*, by inheriting the relevant slice of $\mathcal{W}$ and measuring validation accuracy without further training:

$$\hat{a}_\alpha = \mathrm{acc}_{\mathrm{val}}(\mathcal{W}(\alpha)), \qquad \alpha^* = \arg\max_{\alpha \in \mathcal{A}} \hat{a}_\alpha.$$

The entire enterprise therefore hinges on one quantity: the **ranking correlation** between $\hat{a}_\alpha$ and $a_\alpha$. If the inherited-weight ranking agrees with the stand-alone ranking, search is nearly free; if it does not, weight sharing is merely an efficient way to be wrong quickly [4].

*DARTS* (differentiable architecture search) [1] pushed this idea further by relaxing the discrete search into a continuous one, turning architecture selection into bilevel gradient optimization that completes in single-digit GPU-days. Yet its success papered over structural weaknesses — collapse to degenerate architectures, the optimization gap between the continuous relaxation and the discrete discretization, and sensitivity to the very bilevel approximation it depends on. This thesis interrogates these foundations.

> **Central thesis:** weight-sharing supernets, DARTS, and one-shot evaluation are three instantiations of a single principle — *search through shared parameterization* — whose validity is governed not by optimization speed but by the fidelity of the proxy objective, measurable through rank-correlation statistics and reproducible benchmarks.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The cost of honest search

Classical NAS optimized $\alpha$ by a black-box outer loop (RL controller, evolutionary algorithm) over a *faithful* inner loop: full training of every candidate. The expense is linear in the number of candidates $N$ and the cost of full training $C$:

$$\mathrm{cost} \approx N \cdot C \approx 10^4\!\text{--}\!2\times 10^4 \text{ GPU-hours}.$$

This made *proxy tasks* — searching on CIFAR-10 and transferring to ImageNet, or searching shallow and evaluating deep — standard practice, with all the transferability caveats such proxies imply.

### 2.2 Parameter sharing: ENAS

Efficient Neural Architecture Search (ENAS) [2] observed that candidate architectures overlap heavily in their subgraphs. If all child models share a parameter pool $\omega$, each sampled architecture $m$ updates only the parameters on its active path $\omega(m)$, and the RL controller is trained on validation rewards computed with these shared weights. ENAS reduced search to roughly 16 GPU-hours on CIFAR-10 — over a $1000\times$ reduction — while remaining competitive with the non-sharing baselines.

Key limitations identified early [2]: (i) the controller and shared weights are updated alternately with no convergence guarantees, and (ii) shared weights must be *average-good* across sampled architectures, so no single architecture attains the weights it would have under dedicated training.

### 2.3 One-shot NAS without controllers

Bender et al. [3] asked how much machinery is actually necessary. Their answer: none. Train the supernet once with **uniform random sampling** of architectures per minibatch, apply **path dropout** to de-correlate co-adapted weights, and rank architectures by one-shot validation performance. The finding — that this drastically simplified pipeline identifies architectures competitive with controller-based methods — recast weight sharing as a property of *training protocol* rather than of search algorithm.

### 2.4 The supernet as a formal object

We adopt the formalism of one-shot evaluation. Let $\mathcal{U}(\mathcal{A}, \mathcal{W})$ denote the supernet realizing search space $\mathcal{A}$ with weights $\mathcal{W}$. Weight sharing proceeds in two stages [5]:

1. **Supernet training:**

$$\mathcal{W}_{\mathcal{A}} = \arg\min_{\mathcal{W}} \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{train}}(\mathcal{U}(\mathcal{A}, \mathcal{W})),$$

2. **Architecture selection:**

$$\alpha^* = \arg\min_{\alpha \in \mathcal{A}} \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{val}}(\mathcal{U}(\alpha, \mathcal{W}_{\mathcal{A}}(\alpha))).$$

The validity condition is that the *inherited* weights $\mathcal{W}_{\mathcal{A}}(\alpha)$ approximate, or at least rank-consistently predict, the dedicated weights $w_\alpha^*$. Everything in this thesis is an elaboration of that condition.

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 Cell-based search spaces

Following DARTS [1], we search *micro*-architectures: cells, small DAGs that are stacked to form the full network. A cell has $N$ ordered nodes $\{x^{(i)}\}$; each directed edge $(i,j)$, $i<j$, carries a candidate operation $o^{(i,j)}$ from a set $\mathcal{O}$ (e.g., $3\times3$ and $5\times5$ separable convolutions, dilated convolutions, max/average pooling, skip connection, zero). The cell output concatenates the intermediate nodes. Two cell types are learned — *normal* (stride 1) and *reduction* (stride 2, halving spatial resolution) — and the search space size is roughly

$$|\mathcal{A}| \approx \prod_{j} \binom{\text{predecessors}(j)}{2} |\mathcal{O}|^{2|\mathcal{E}|} \approx 10^{18} \text{ configurations}.$$

The combinatorial scale is precisely what makes discrete enumeration impossible and relaxation attractive.

### 3.2 Continuous relaxation

DARTS replaces the categorical choice on each edge with a softmax mixture over all candidate operations:

$$\bar{o}^{(i,j)}(x) = \sum_{o \in \mathcal{O}} \frac{\exp(\alpha_o^{(i,j)})}{\sum_{o' \in \mathcal{O}} \exp(\alpha_{o'}^{(i,j)})} \, o(x),$$

where $\alpha^{(i,j)}$ are *architecture parameters*. The discrete problem becomes a continuous, differentiable one; the final architecture is obtained by discretization, $o^{(i,j)} = \arg\max_o \alpha_o^{(i,j)}$, retaining for each intermediate node its two strongest incoming edges.

### 3.3 Bilevel optimization

DARTS formulates search as bilevel optimization [1]:

$$\min_{\alpha} \; \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{val}}(w^*(\alpha), \alpha) \quad \text{s.t.} \quad w^*(\alpha) = \arg\min_w \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{train}}(w, \alpha).$$

Solving the inner problem exactly per outer step is infeasible, so DARTS approximates $w^*(\alpha)$ by a **single gradient step** $w' = w - \xi \nabla_w \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{train}}(w, \alpha)$ and differentiates through it:

$$\nabla_\alpha \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{val}}(w', \alpha) = \nabla_\alpha \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{val}}(w', \alpha) - \xi \, \nabla^2_{\alpha,w} \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{train}}(w, \alpha) \, \nabla_{w'} \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{val}}(w', \alpha).$$

Two variants result:

- **First-order approximation** ($\xi = 0$): $\nabla_\alpha \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{val}}(w, \alpha)$, cheap but biased — it ignores how $w$ responds to $\alpha$.
- **Second-order approximation** ($\xi > 0$): includes the Hessian-vector product, approximated by finite differences to avoid $O(|\alpha||w|)$ cost. More faithful, roughly $2\times$ the compute.

In practice the two parameter sets are updated alternately on disjoint data splits ($\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{train}}$ for $w$, $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{val}}$ for $\alpha$), mirroring the bilevel structure.

### 3.4 One-shot evaluation protocol

Decoupled from DARTS, the *one-shot evaluation* protocol [3,4] is:

1. Train the supernet with a sampling policy (uniform, fair, or learned) for $T$ epochs.
2. Sample $N$ architectures $\{\alpha_k\}$; for each, inherit $\mathcal{W}(\alpha_k)$ and compute one-shot validation accuracy $\hat{a}_k$.
3. Select top-$k$ candidates by $\hat{a}_k$ and retrain from scratch; report stand-alone accuracy $a_k$.

The protocol's diagnostic power is measured by **Kendall's rank correlation** $\tau$ between $\{\hat{a}_k\}$ and $\{a_k\}$:

$$\tau = \frac{(\text{concordant pairs}) - (\text{discordant pairs})}{\binom{N}{2}} \in [-1, 1],$$

where $\tau = 1$ denotes perfect rank agreement. A one-shot protocol with $\tau \approx 0$ is *random search with extra steps*; the goal of subsequent methodology is to push $\tau$ toward 1.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The continuous relaxation and the softmax bottleneck

The softmax relaxation is both DARTS' engine and its original sin. It creates an **optimization gap**: search optimizes a *mixture* in which every operation contributes to every forward pass, but evaluation uses a *single* operation per edge. The mixture can exploit co-adaptation — operations compensating for each other's weaknesses — that the discretized architecture cannot reproduce. Empirically, architectures whose $\alpha$ margins are small (the winner barely beats the runner-up) suffer the largest search-to-evaluation degradation.

Fair DARTS [5] argues the softmax itself induces *unfair advantage*: operations that learn faster early (notably the parameter-free skip connection) dominate the mixture, suppressing the gradients of slower-converging but ultimately stronger convolutions. Replacing softmax with independent sigmoids and adding a zero-one loss to push weights toward binarization removes this exclusivity pressure and measurably stabilizes search.

### 4.2 Bilevel approximation: bias and Hessian geometry

The first-order approximation ($\xi = 0$) treats the inner optimum as fixed, which is valid only if $w^*(\alpha)$ is locally insensitive to $\alpha$. In NAS it is not: architecture parameters and weights are tightly coupled. The bias introduced by the approximation shifts the global minimum of the architecture objective [8], and the Hessian of the validation loss with respect to $\alpha$ at convergence has been shown to correlate strongly with the generalization gap — sharp $\alpha$-minima generalize poorly [6].

> **Theorem (informal, bilevel approximation gap):** Let $w^*(\alpha)$ be the exact inner minimizer and $\tilde{w}(\alpha) = w - \xi\nabla_w\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{train}}(w,\alpha)$ its one-step approximation. Then $\|\nabla_\alpha \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{val}}(\tilde{w}(\alpha),\alpha) - \nabla_\alpha \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{val}}(w^*(\alpha),\alpha)\| = O(\|\tilde{w} - w^*\|)$, so the architecture gradient error is controlled by the inner-optimization residual. Early in search, when $w$ is far from $w^*(\alpha)$, architecture updates follow a *wrong* gradient — a mechanism that helps explain both collapse and sensitivity to initialization.

### 4.3 Collapse modes and the skip-connection pathology

The most documented failure of DARTS is **performance collapse**: after a certain number of search epochs, discovered architectures fill with skip connections and parameter-free operations, and test performance degrades sharply [7]. The mechanism is competition within the bilevel game: skip connections reduce training loss fastest, $\alpha$ rewards them, weights adapt to a skip-heavy supernet, which further rewards skip connections — a positive feedback loop.

Remedies form a small taxonomy:

- **Early stopping** (DARTS+) [7]: halt search when the number of skip connections exceeds a threshold or when the Hessian eigenvalue $\lambda_{\max}^\alpha$ of the architecture loss explodes — both are cheap proxies for the onset of collapse.
- **Regularization**: perturb $\alpha$ (SDARTS) or the inner objective (RobustDARTS) to favor flat $\alpha$-minima, which correlate with transferable architectures.
- **Partial channels** (PC-DARTS): route only a fraction of channels through the operation mixture, reducing both memory and the co-adaptation that rewards parameter-free ops.

### 4.4 Fairness in weight sharing

FairNAS [5] reframed the supernet-training question as one of **fairness**: is every candidate operation updated equally? Prior schemes (including ENAS-style controller sampling and DARTS' joint training) are *unfair* — some operations receive far more gradient updates than others, so inherited weights reflect training frequency as much as intrinsic quality. FairNAS introduces two fairness levels:

| Fairness level | Definition | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| **Expectation fairness (EF)** | Each choice block is updated the same number of times *in expectation* | Removes systematic bias from sampling |
| **Strict fairness (SF)** | Each choice block is updated *exactly* the same number of times | Kendall $\tau = 0.9487$ on the benchmark, a new record [5] |

Strict fairness is achieved by single-path uniform sampling *without replacement* across layers, so each training step activates exactly one choice block per layer. The empirical payoff is dramatic: the rank correlation between one-shot and stand-alone accuracy approaches $0.95$, essentially eliminating the statistical objection to weight sharing. The residual lesson is that **how you train the supernet matters more than how you search over it**.

### 4.5 Does the supernet rank correctly? Evidence from benchmarks

Reproducible benchmarks made the ranking question falsifiable. NAS-Bench-201 [6] exhaustively evaluates all $15{,}625$ architectures in a fixed cell space (6,466 non-isomorphic) on CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100, and ImageNet-16-120, enabling exact computation of $\tau$ for any one-shot protocol. Findings:

1. One-shot protocols vary enormously in $\tau$ — from near-random to $>0.9$ — depending on supernet training details, confirming that weight sharing is a *protocol*, not a guarantee.
2. Fair single-path training dominates; standard DARTS supernets rank substantially worse than fairly trained ones.
3. Training-free proxies (e.g., based on neural tangent kernel spectra or Jacobian correlations) achieve competitive $\tau$ at negligible cost, suggesting the ranking signal may be extractable without any weight sharing at all.

## 5 Empirical Evaluation / Proofs

### 5.1 Comparative results

The table below summarizes landmark results (CIFAR-10 test error, search cost) — values reported by the respective papers:

| Method | CIFAR-10 test err. | Search cost | Search paradigm |
|---|---|---|---|
| DARTS (2nd order) [1] | 2.76% | 4 GPU-days | Differentiable, bilevel |
| ENAS [2] | 2.89% | 0.5 GPU-days | RL controller + sharing |
| ProxylessNAS [4] | 2.08% | ~8 GPU-days (ImageNet-direct) | Binary gates + latency reg. |
| FairNAS-A [5] | — (ImageNet 75.34% top-1) | 12 GPU-days | Strict-fair supernet + EA |
| Bender et al. one-shot [3] | competitive | single supernet training | Uniform sampling, no controller |
| NAS-Bench-201 oracle [6] | — (benchmark) | 0 (lookup) | Exhaustive ground truth |

Two observations: (i) ProxylessNAS [4] demonstrated that differentiable search can be run *directly* on ImageNet by binarizing architecture parameters so only one path is active per step — reducing memory to that of training a single compact model; (ii) search cost has fallen by three orders of magnitude since 2017, but the variance *across runs* of DARTS-family methods remains a reproducibility concern that benchmarks like NAS-Bench-201 [6] were built to expose.

### 5.2 Minimal reference implementation

The following Python sketch captures the DARTS bilevel step and the one-shot evaluation protocol in executable form:

```python
import torch
import torch.nn as nn
import torch.nn.functional as F

class MixedOp(nn.Module):
    # Continuous relaxation of one DARTS edge: softmax over candidate ops.
    def __init__(self, ops):
        super().__init__()
        self.ops = nn.ModuleList(ops)
        self.alpha = nn.Parameter(torch.zeros(len(ops)))  # architecture params

    def forward(self, x):
        weights = F.softmax(self.alpha, dim=0)
        return sum(w * op(x) for w, op in zip(weights, self.ops))

def darts_bilevel_step(model, w_opt, a_opt, train_batch, val_batch, xi=0.01,
                       second_order=True):
    # Inner step: one gradient step on weights approximates w*(alpha).
    x_t, y_t = train_batch
    loss_t = F.cross_entropy(model(x_t), y_t)
    w_opt.zero_grad(); loss_t.backward()
    w_prime = [p - xi * p.grad for p in model.weights()]  # w'(alpha)

    # Outer step: gradient of validation loss through the one-step unroll.
    x_v, y_v = val_batch
    with torch.no_grad():
        for p, wp in zip(model.weights(), w_prime):
            p.copy_(wp)
    loss_v = F.cross_entropy(model(x_v), y_v)
    a_opt.zero_grad(); loss_v.backward()  # first-order term
    if second_order:
        # Hessian-vector product via finite differences (avoids O(|a||w|) cost)
        hv = hessian_vector_product(loss_t, model.alphas(), model.weights(),
                                    grad(loss_v, model.weights()))
        for a, h in zip(model.alphas(), hv):
            a.grad -= xi * h
    a_opt.step()
    return loss_t.item(), loss_v.item()

def one_shot_rank(supernet, archs, val_loader):
    # Evaluate N architectures by inherited weights; return ranking.
    scores = []
    for a in archs:
        acc = evaluate_with_inherited_weights(supernet, a, val_loader)
        scores.append((a, acc))
    return sorted(scores, key=lambda kv: kv[1], reverse=True)
```

The finite-difference Hessian-vector product in the second-order branch is the standard trick that makes DARTS' bilevel gradient tractable: it replaces the $O(|\alpha|\cdot|w|)$ mixed Hessian with two additional forward/backward passes [1].

### 5.3 A proof sketch: why fairness improves ranking

Consider two architectures $\alpha_1, \alpha_2$ sharing an edge operation $o$ with parameters $\theta_o$. Under unfair sampling, $\theta_o$ receives $n_1 \gg n_2$ updates in the context of $\alpha_1$ versus $\alpha_2$. The inherited evaluations $\hat{a}_{\alpha_1}, \hat{a}_{\alpha_2}$ then confound *architectural quality* with *training budget*, introducing a bias term $b(n_1, n_2)$ into the pairwise comparison. Strict fairness [5] forces $n_1 = n_2$ exactly, so that $\mathbb{E}[\hat{a}_{\alpha_1} - \hat{a}_{\alpha_2}]$ depends only on architecture-induced differences in how the shared parameters are *used*, not on how often they were *trained*. This is the statistical mechanism behind FairNAS' $\tau = 0.9487$: removing the update-count confounder restores the proxy's rank fidelity.

## 6 Limitations

1. **The optimization gap.** DARTS searches a mixture but evaluates a discretization. The argmax is a discontinuous projection, and there is no theorem guaranteeing the projected architecture inherits the mixture's performance. Perturbation-based selection (choosing the operation whose removal hurts most, rather than the largest $\alpha$) mitigates but does not close the gap.
2. **Depth and width gaps.** Search is conducted on shallow, narrow supernets (e.g., 8 cells) and evaluated on deep, wide stacks (e.g., 20 cells). Architectural motifs that are optimal at one scale need not transfer — progressive deepening schemes address this only heuristically.
3. **Proxy-task transfer.** Architectures searched on CIFAR-10 are routinely evaluated on ImageNet; the correlation of rankings *across datasets* is weaker than within-dataset $\tau$ and rarely reported.
4. **Variance and reproducibility.** DARTS-family results exhibit substantial seed variance; reported point estimates overstate reliability. Tabular benchmarks [6] mitigate this by making the search space itself reproducible, but they cover only small spaces.
5. **Single-path memory vs. fidelity trade-offs.** Memory-sparing tricks (binary gates [4], partial channels) change the optimization dynamics; their effect on rank correlation is method-specific and under-theorized.
6. **Hardware objectives.** Latency/energy regularization [4] couples architecture search to non-differentiable, device-specific metrics via learned predictors — a second proxy stacked on the first, with compounding approximation error.
7. **Fairness is necessary, not sufficient.** Even strictly fair supernets rank correctly only within the space they were trained on; out-of-space generalization of the supernet itself remains an open question.

## 7 Conclusion

Weight-sharing supernets recast architecture search as amortized inference: pay once to train the supernet, then evaluate architectures nearly for free. DARTS made this idea differentiable, reducing search to bilevel gradient optimization over architecture parameters; one-shot evaluation made it modular, separating supernet training from architecture ranking. The decade's empirical arc — from thousand-GPU-hour RL search, through ENAS' parameter sharing, DARTS' continuous relaxation, ProxylessNAS' memory-sparing binarization, to FairNAS' strict fairness and NAS-Bench-201's exhaustive ground truth — converges on a single diagnostic: *rank correlation between the proxy and the truth*.

The field's open problems are now statistical rather than algorithmic: characterizing when $\tau$ is high, closing the mixture-to-discrete optimization gap, and extending fairness guarantees beyond fixed search spaces. Until those are resolved, the prudent reading of any weight-sharing result is Bender et al.'s [3] minimalist one: much of the machinery is optional, but the validation of the proxy is not.
