# Equality Saturation and E-Graphs: Program Optimization by Equational Rewriting — Congruence Closure, Extraction, and the egg/egglog Lineage

## Abstract

Equality saturation replaces the destructive, order-sensitive rewrite pipelines of traditional compilers with a non-destructive, equational approach: program fragments are accumulated into an *e-graph* — a compact representation of a congruence relation over terms — and optimization becomes the search for an equivalent, lower-cost representative of an equivalence class. This thesis reconstructs the intellectual lineage of the technique, from Nelson and Oppen's congruence closure decision procedures [1] and their deployment in the Simplify theorem prover [2], through Tate et al.'s original equality saturation framework [3], to the modern systems that made the approach practical: Willsey et al.'s **egg** [4] with its amortized *rebuilding* invariant restoration and *e-class analyses*, Zhang et al.'s **egglog** unification of Datalog and equality saturation [5], and the relational e-matching breakthrough that made pattern search asymptotically optimal [6]. We formalize the e-graph invariants, the saturation algorithm, and cost-driven extraction; we survey empirical results in compilers, superoptimization, and floating-point rewriting; and we assess fundamental limitations — saturation divergence, extraction complexity, and context insensitivity — together with the guided and contextual extensions addressing them [7].

---

## 1. Introduction

The history of compiler optimization is, to a first approximation, the history of the *phase ordering problem*: an optimizer applies dozens of transformation passes in some fixed sequence, each pass destructively replacing the program it receives with a putatively better one. The difficulty is that no single order is universally correct. Applying inlining before constant propagation can expose new constants; applying it before register allocation can destroy a carefully tuned allocation. A transformation that is locally unprofitable may unlock a cascade of later wins, and once a destructive pass has discarded the original program fragment, those alternatives are lost forever. Compilers respond with heuristics, fixed pipelines, and folk wisdom — engineering around a structural defect of the architecture itself.

*Equality saturation* proposes to eliminate the defect rather than manage it. The insight, articulated by Tate, Stepp, Tatlock, and Lerner [3], is disarmingly simple: optimizations should be recast as *equality analyses* that merely **add** information to a shared intermediate representation rather than replacing the program. The representation — an *e-graph* — accumulates every equivalence that any optimization discovers, so that after saturation it compactly encodes an astronomically large set of programs, all provably equivalent to the input. Choosing the final program then becomes a global, post hoc *extraction* problem: pick the cheapest representative from each equivalence class under a cost model, a decision made with complete information rather than with the myopia of a pass pipeline.

This thesis is a systematic treatment of that idea and its descendants. We begin with the logical foundations: congruence closure as a decision procedure for the quantifier-free theory of equality with uninterpreted functions, and the union-find machinery of Nelson and Oppen [1] that underlies both early automated provers and every modern e-graph. We then develop the e-graph data structure itself, its invariants, and the equality saturation loop, contrasting it carefully with destructive rewriting. The middle chapters trace the engineering arc that converted an elegant but impractical formalism into a workhorse of program optimization: the *rebuilding* technique and *e-class analyses* of **egg** [4]; the discovery that e-matching is fundamentally a relational query problem, yielding optimal generic-join e-matching [6]; and **egglog**, which collapses the distinction between Datalog-style fixpoint reasoning and equality saturation entirely [5]. We close with empirical evidence, rigorous complexity and soundness analysis, and an honest account of what the technique still cannot do — non-terminating saturation, the combinatorial extraction problem, and the lack of contextual (path-sensitive) reasoning, partially addressed by recent guided approaches [7].

## 2. Background

### 2.1 Congruence closure: deciding equality

The theory of equality with uninterpreted function symbols, denoted $T_E$, is the logical substrate of nearly all equational reasoning in program analysis. A conjunction of ground equalities and disequalities over $T_E$ is decidable, and the canonical decision procedure is *congruence closure*: given a set of terms and asserted equalities, compute the smallest congruence relation extending them, then check that no asserted disequality is violated.

Nelson and Oppen's formulation [1] reduces the problem to operations on a term DAG. Each subterm is a vertex; vertices are partitioned into equivalence classes by a union-find structure. The algorithm's heart is the congruence rule: if $f(a_1,\ldots,a_n)$ and $f(b_1,\ldots,b_n)$ are terms and $a_i \equiv b_i$ for every $i$, then the two $f$-applications are congruent and their classes must merge. Nelson and Oppen's procedure *MERGE(u, v)* unions the classes of $u$ and $v$ and then propagates: it examines predecessors (parent terms) of the merged vertices, and whenever two predecessors become congruent, it merges them recursively. Downey, Sethi, and Tarjan subsequently refined this to a near-linear $O(n \log n)$ algorithm, establishing that congruence closure is essentially as cheap as the term structure it reasons about.

> **Theorem:** (Nelson–Oppen [1]) Let $\Gamma$ be a conjunction of ground equalities over terms with uninterpreted function symbols. The congruence closure procedure terminates and reports satisfiable *iff* $\Gamma$ is satisfiable in $T_E$. Its time complexity is $O(n \log n)$ in the number of subterms.

Congruence closure became the engine room of automated theorem proving. The **Simplify** prover [2], developed at DEC/HP SRC as the proof backend for the Extended Static Checkers ESC/Java and ESC/Modula-3, combined Nelson–Oppen-style congruence closure for $T_E$ with decision procedures for linear arithmetic and other theories via the Nelson–Oppen theory-combination framework, plus a quantifier-instantiation matcher. Notably, Simplify matched quantified axioms *up to e-graph equivalence* rather than in a raw term DAG — a prescient architectural choice, since it means matching could exploit equalities the prover had already learned [2]. The lineage is direct: the e-graph of equality saturation is congruence closure's data structure, repurposed from *checking* equalities to *growing* them.

### 2.2 Destructive rewriting and the phase-ordering problem

Traditional term rewriting treats optimization as a sequence of *destructive* steps. A rewrite rule $l \to r$ is applied by finding a subterm matching $l\sigma$ and replacing it with $r\sigma$; the old term is gone. The classical vocabulary — confluence, termination, critical pairs, Knuth–Bendix completion — studies when such systems behave well, but compiler optimization lives in the badly-behaved regime: rewrite systems that are neither confluent nor terminating, applied under heuristic control.

The *phase-ordering problem* names the consequence. Consider two rewrites applicable to an expression $e$: applying rule $A$ first may destroy the redex needed by rule $B$, while applying $B$ first may destroy the redex needed by $A$. No local criterion can decide which order is right, because the *profitability* of a rewrite depends on rewrites that have not happened yet. Superoptimizers — which search exhaustively over instruction sequences equivalent to a target [2, §7.3 of related literature] — sidestep ordering by enumerating outcomes, but only for straight-line code of bounded length. A general compilation paradigm required a different idea: keep *all* the alternatives.

### 2.3 From theorem proving to optimization: Tate et al.

The 2009 POPL paper by Tate, Stepp, Tatlock, and Lerner [3] supplied that idea. Their proposal: restructure the optimizer as a set of *equality analyses* — procedures that, upon observing a program fragment, infer new equalities and record them in a common e-graph — plus a final *profitability heuristic* that extracts the best program from the saturated graph. The framework's central formal property is a kind of *monotonicity of the search space*:

> **Theorem:** (Tate et al. [3, §5.3]) If the saturation engine is run to a fixpoint, the resulting e-graph represents every program obtainable by any sequence of the given equality analyses. If stopped early, no region of the explored search space is rendered unreachable by the application of an analysis — a guarantee destructive optimizers cannot provide.

Tate et al. also demonstrated *translation validation*: because the e-graph accumulates proven equivalences, two programs (e.g., before and after an existing compiler's pipeline) can be checked for equivalence by inserting both into the same e-graph and testing whether they land in one e-class. The paper reported practical time and space overhead and the discovery of intricate optimizations beyond the reach of the baseline compiler — but the implementation was a research prototype, and a decade of engineering was needed to make equality saturation routine.

## 3. Methodology

Our method is a *reconstructive formal survey*: we re-derive the e-graph's invariants and the saturation algorithm from first principles, verify each claim against the primary literature, and synthesize the engineering results reported across the lineage into a single comparative framework. Where the literature gives pseudocode (e.g., egg's read/write-phase split [4, Fig. 5]), we reproduce it in executable Rust and reason about its invariants directly. Complexity claims are stated with the hypotheses the papers actually prove, not the stronger ones folklore sometimes asserts — in particular, we distinguish *e-matching* cost (now understood relationally [6]) from *congruence maintenance* cost, and *extraction* cost, which is NP-hard in general. Empirical numbers cited are those reported by the original authors in peer-reviewed venues, with the evaluation design of each study described so that the reader can judge their transferability.

The remainder of the thesis proceeds as follows. Section 4 gives the deep technical core: congruence closure mechanics, e-graph invariants, the saturation loop and its modern refinements, and the egglog convergence. Section 5 presents empirical results and the key theorems. Section 6 enumerates limitations honestly. Section 7 concludes.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Congruence closure as the union-find engine of the e-graph

An e-graph *is* a congruence closure data structure with an additional capability: it can *grow*. The classic use computes the congruence closure of a *fixed* set of terms under asserted equalities. Equality saturation repeatedly asserts *new* equalities — one per rewrite application — and re-closes. Everything else is engineering around the cost of re-closure.

Concretely, the e-graph maintains **e-classes** (equivalence classes, managed by union-find), **e-nodes** $(f, [c_1, \ldots, c_n])$ that point to *child e-class ids* rather than to other e-nodes — the indirection that makes sharing work — and a **hash-cons** from canonical e-nodes to their e-class, enforcing that no two classes contain the same canonical e-node.

The *congruence invariant* is: if $f(c_1,\ldots,c_n)$ and $f(d_1,\ldots,d_n)$ are e-nodes with $c_i = d_i$ for all $i$, both reside in one e-class. Restoring this invariant after merges is the cost center of saturation — the problem *rebuilding* solves (Section 4.3).

The following Rust sketch captures the core data structure; it is intentionally close to the presentation in the egg paper [4, §2–3]:

```rust
type Id = u32; // e-class identifier

#[derive(Clone, PartialEq, Eq, Hash)]
enum ENode {
    App { op: Symbol, args: Vec<Id> }, // f(c1, ..., cn)
    Leaf(Symbol),                      // constants, variables
}

struct EClass {
    id: Id,
    nodes: Vec<ENode>,     // e-nodes congruent to this class
    parents: Vec<(ENode, Id)>, // e-nodes elsewhere that reference this class
}

struct EGraph {
    unionfind: UnionFind,          // id -> canonical id
    classes: HashMap<Id, EClass>,  // canonical id -> class
    memo: HashMap<ENode, Id>,      // hash-cons: canonical e-node -> class
}
```

```rust
impl EGraph {
    /// Add a term; return its e-class. Purely additive: nothing is removed.
    fn add(&mut self, node: ENode) -> Id {
        let node = node.canonicalize(&self.unionfind);
        if let Some(&id) = self.memo.get(&node) { return id; }
        let id = self.unionfind.make_set();
        self.memo.insert(node.clone(), id);
        self.classes.insert(id, EClass::new(id, node));
        id
    }

    /// Assert equality of two e-classes and propagate congruence.
    fn merge(&mut self, a: Id, b: Id) -> Id {
        let (a, b) = (self.find(a), self.find(b));
        if a == b { return a; }
        let merged = self.unionfind.union(a, b);
        // move e-nodes, union parent lists, then re-canonicalize:
        // any two parents that are now congruent must themselves merge.
        self.repair_congruence(merged);
        merged
    }
}
```

```
This is the *rebuilding* result [4, §3]: by deferring invariant restoration to a single pass per saturation iteration, the amortized cost of congruence maintenance becomes essentially linear in the number of merges plus the number of affected parents, rather than multiplicative.

### 4.2 E-graph invariants and the saturation loop

Equality saturation alternates two phases — **e-matching** (find all rewrite applications) and **application** (add right-hand sides, merge e-classes) — then rebuilds once:

1. **Match:** for each rule $l \to r$ and each substitution $\sigma$ with $l\sigma$ represented in $G$, record the match's e-class $c$.
2. **Apply:** for each match, compute $c' = \text{add}(r\sigma)$ and $\text{merge}(c, c')$.
3. **Repeat** until fixpoint, timeout, or iteration limit.

The pivotal design decision in egg [4, Fig. 5] is to split each iteration into a *read-only phase* (all matching) and a *write-only phase* (all application), with a single `rebuild()` at the end. egg's split makes each iteration's result *independent of rule ordering*, a genuine semantic improvement, not merely a performance one.

> **Theorem:** (Willsey et al. [4, §3]) egg's phase-separated saturation is *rule-order invariant*: for a fixed iteration budget, the e-graph does not depend on rule order. The phase-ordering problem is not managed here; it is dissolved.

### 4.3 Rewriting, rebuilding, and e-class analyses: the egg contribution

The egg paper [4] contributes two mechanisms that together converted equality saturation from a prototype technique into a reusable library.

**Rebuilding.** Classical e-graphs restore the congruence invariant eagerly after each merge. Saturation workloads instead deliver merges in *bursts*, with read-only e-matching between them. Rebuilding defers all restoration to the end of each iteration, processing accumulated merges in a worklist [4, §3.2]. Conceptually, this is the recognition that *the e-graph's workload is not the theorem prover's workload*: Nelson and Oppen's setting asserts equalities once and queries the closure [1]; saturation asserts equalities continuously, and the data structure is specialized to that rhythm.

**E-class analyses.** Real optimizations need more than syntactic rewrites: constant folding needs to know an e-class *contains a constant*; floating-point rewriting needs interval bounds. egg's *e-class analyses* [4, §4] attach lattice-valued data to each e-class, maintained automatically across merges and rebuilds, enabling *conditional* rewrites whose applicability depends on analysis data. One mechanism subsumes constant folding, cost tracking, and the dynamic rewrites of earlier systems.

**E-matching as a database problem.** With congruence maintenance tamed, the dominant cost was *e-matching*. Zhang, Wang, Willsey, and Tatlock's *relational e-matching* [6] reframes the e-graph as relational tables (one per function symbol, keyed by e-class ids) and the pattern as a conjunctive query; compiling patterns to queries evaluated with worst-case-optimal *generic join* attains the AGM bound — asymptotically optimal e-matching [6]. The e-graph is not merely *like* a database; it *is* one.

### 4.4 egglog: unifying Datalog and equality saturation

The most recent architectural step, **egglog** [5], observes that equality saturation and Datalog are both *fixpoint computations over growing relations* and unifies them: relations may contain *e-classes* as values, function tables are kept congruent by construction, and rules are evaluated with semi-naïve incremental evaluation extended to maintain e-graph invariants. One engine asserts equalities, derives facts, and computes lattice joins at once; the paper's reimplementations of two motivating applications came out faster, simpler, and *with original bugs fixed* [5, §7]. The broader thesis: *congruence closure, e-matching, Datalog evaluation, and the database chase are facets of one underlying fixpoint computation* — and the practitioner no longer chooses between an e-graph library and a Datalog engine.

## 5. Empirical Results and Proofs

We now collect the principal quantitative and formal results across the lineage, distinguishing carefully between *theorems* (which hold unconditionally) and *empirical measurements* (which hold for the reported workloads).

**Formal results.**

1. *Congruence closure decidability.* Nelson–Oppen [1]: ground $T_E$ satisfiability is decidable in $O(n \log n)$. This is the soundness bedrock of every e-graph: merges only ever equate provably congruent terms.

2. *Saturation monotonicity.* Tate et al. [3]: at fixpoint, the e-graph holds every program reachable by any sequence of the equality analyses; under early stopping, no explored region becomes unreachable.

3. *Rule-order invariance.* Willsey et al. [4]: egg's read/write phase separation makes bounded saturation independent of rule ordering.

4. *Worst-case-optimal e-matching.* Zhang et al. [6]: relational e-matching with generic join attains the AGM bound for conjunctive pattern queries, hence is asymptotically optimal among all e-matching algorithms for the pattern class. This converts e-matching from a heuristic backtracking search into an algorithm with a *proof* of optimality.

**Empirical results.** The egg paper's three case studies [4, §6] reimplemented prior equality-saturation applications and matched or exceeded their results with less code and faster runtimes — a floating-point rewriter over hundreds of benchmarks, and a CAD kernel study scaled from dozens of hand-picked models to thousands of real-world ones. egglog [5, §7] made both reimplemented applications faster and simpler while fixing original bugs. Relational e-matching [6, §7] reports speedups of orders of magnitude on patterns with repeated variables. Tate et al.'s prototype [3, §8] showed practical overhead, discovered optimizations missed by a conventional optimizer, and validated translation of an existing compiler's output.

**Extraction: the one formal blemish.** Given a saturated e-graph and an additive cost function, *optimal* extraction — the minimum-cost representative per e-class — is NP-hard in general: sharing means a locally optimal choice in one class can force globally suboptimal choices elsewhere. Practical systems use greedy bottom-up extraction ($O(n)$, optimal for tree-like cost models) or exact ILP/SAT encodings for moderate graphs. Equality saturation *defers* the hard decision from pass-ordering time to extraction time, and extraction is where the combinatorial complexity now lives.

## 6. Limitations

An honest assessment must state what equality saturation cannot do, or cannot do cheaply.

**Non-termination of saturation.** For arbitrary rewrite systems, saturation need not reach a fixpoint: rules like associativity and commutativity generate infinitely many distinct (but finitely represented — the e-graph handles these via cycles) terms, and in general the e-graph can grow without bound. Practical systems impose iteration limits, node caps, or timeouts, which forfeit the fixpoint theorems of Section 5: with early stopping, the e-graph represents *some* explored subset of equivalences, and extraction quality degrades gracefully but without guarantees. Rule scheduling — deciding *which* rewrites to prioritize under a budget — becomes the new phase-ordering problem, relocated rather than eliminated. Recent *guided* equality saturation [7] attacks exactly this: learning or heuristic policies that steer saturation effort toward promising regions of the equivalence space.

**Extraction complexity.** As noted, optimal extraction is NP-hard in general. The greedy extractor is fast but can be arbitrarily suboptimal in the presence of sharing; exact ILP-based extraction is exponential in the worst case. For many cost models (e.g., simple node-count or depth), greedy extraction is adequate, but sophisticated models — register pressure, instruction scheduling interactions — remain difficult to encode faithfully.

**Context insensitivity.** The e-graph represents *unconditional* equivalences: if two terms share an e-class, they are considered interchangeable in *all* contexts. Many optimizations are *conditional* — valid only under a path condition, a type refinement, or a calling context. Encoding such conditional knowledge requires either duplicating the e-graph per context (destroying sharing) or extending the formalism. Contextual equality saturation is an active research direction, and its interaction with the relational e-matching machinery remains incompletely understood.

**Proof production and trust.** The e-graph records *that* two terms are equivalent, but a bare e-class merge does not by itself constitute a checkable proof: reconstructing the rewrite sequence witnessing an equivalence (a *proof certificate*) requires additional bookkeeping. For applications like translation validation and verified compilation, where the equivalence claim must be independently audited, proof-producing e-graphs are an important open engineering problem.

**Theory reasoning beyond $T_E$.** Congruence closure decides only the theory of equality with uninterpreted functions. Arithmetic facts, ordering constraints, and bit-vector semantics must enter through e-class analyses or external solvers, and the interaction between saturation and theory reasoning lacks the clean combination theorems (à la Nelson–Oppen) that SMT solvers enjoy. egglog's lattice-based reasoning [5] is a step toward principled combination, but a general, complete framework remains future work.

---

## 7. Conclusion

Equality saturation is best understood not as one more optimization technique but as a change of *architecture*: from pipelines of destructive passes, whose behavior depends on inscrutable ordering decisions, to a monotonic accumulation of proven equivalences in a shared e-graph, followed by a single global extraction decision made with complete information. The lineage we have traced — congruence closure [1] to Simplify [2] to Tate et al.'s framework [3] to egg's rebuilding and e-class analyses [4], relational e-matching [6], and the egglog unification [5] — is a coherent half-century arc: each step either strengthened the formal guarantees (decidability, monotonicity, rule-order invariance, matching optimality) or removed an engineering bottleneck (congruence maintenance, analysis integration, pattern search, engine fragmentation).

The remaining limitations are genuine: saturation may diverge, optimal extraction is NP-hard, equivalences are context-insensitive, and proof certificates are not yet routine. But the trajectory is clear. As guided saturation [7] learns to spend its budget where it matters, and as the Datalog–EqSat convergence deepens, the e-graph is becoming what the relational model became for data: the *lingua franca* substrate on which diverse reasoning tasks — optimization, synthesis, analysis, verification — are expressed as fixpoint computations over shared structure. The phase-ordering problem, which haunted compilers for decades, turned out not to require a better heuristic. It required a better data structure.
